## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Byrd's own underground excavation, generation after generation, eventually reaches sections far below
anything the exile community itself ever built — deeper than any tunnel the city's own records can
account for having dug.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Those deepest layers turn out to predate not just Tepenia's own arrival, but the exile era entirely —
genuine remnants of the original station's pre-exile operational period, material sealed away long
before anyone now living was born. Engineers extending the city's own tunnels occasionally break through
into a real, physical connection to Upper Earth's historical presence in Antarctica. Because these
encounters are rare and unpredictable — a tunneling crew might go a lifetime without ever hitting one —
nobody in the city has ever had to agree on what should happen when one actually occurs.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation, each time a find occurs, between
someone who wants it carefully preserved, documented, and studied, and someone who wants it cleared or
resealed quickly so expansion can continue. A's case is genuine intellectual and historical interest —
this is real material from before the exile era, potentially worth understanding on its own terms.
B's case is equally legitimate — Byrd's tunnels exist to serve a living, growing population's ongoing
need for space, and lingering over any one find risks slowing down excavation the city actually depends
on. This is a preservation-versus-expansion argument between two people who both have a real claim on
the same buried ground.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a historically-minded
excavation-crew member or researcher, someone whose curiosity about the pre-exile period runs deeper
than most residents' passing interest. Character B is plausibly a practical infrastructure or
expansion-focused figure, someone whose job is keeping the buried city's growth on schedule and who
sees each pause as a real cost measured in space the city needs and doesn't yet have. Because these
finds are rare, this conflict plausibly recurs across many different pairs of people over generations
rather than resolving once and for all with a single pair.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No formal, resourced preservation policy ever gets built — the argument instead settles, each time it
recurs, into the same practical middle: crews note and briefly document whatever they find, take what
can reasonably be taken without stopping work, then reseal the passage and keep moving. Neither side's
strongest position wins outright; a working habit forms instead, informal and unwritten, satisfying
neither the historian's full curiosity nor the planner's full urgency.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because there is no formal, centralized preservation effort — just an unwritten habit of quick
documentation before resealing — records of these finds end up scattered and inconsistent, kept however
a given crew or crew chief personally chose to keep them. There is no single place in Byrd where anyone
could go to see everything the deep sections have actually yielded.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because no centralized record exists, the deep layers' reputation as legend rather than confirmed fact
becomes self-reinforcing — even the encounters that genuinely happened are only findable by asking
around, tracking down whichever old crew or family kept their own personal notes. The less centralized
the evidence, the more thoroughly it reads as folklore even to residents who would otherwise take it
seriously if the record were easier to find.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Byrd's deepest layers remain exactly what its earliest arguments over them produced — real,
occasionally confirmed, but experienced by most residents only as a story, its legend-status now
self-sustaining, propped up by generations of scattered, uncentralized personal record-keeping rather
than any deliberate secrecy.
