## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Byrd's earliest economy could have stayed simple — one or two industries meeting the community's most
immediate needs, the way a well-connected young settlement elsewhere in Tepenia might reasonably get
away with.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A city this isolated needs real self-sufficiency across far more domains than a well-connected city
ever has to manage alone, and Byrd's economy is forced to grow into something genuinely integrated
rather than staying simple — fabrication, freight dispatch, snow and infrastructure management,
resource extraction, and aviation maintenance all developing side by side, each one needing the others
to actually function. But running five distinct, interdependent sectors at once, in a settlement still
young enough to be improvising as it goes, takes a level of internal coordination nobody designed in
advance and nobody yet knows how to provide.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a governance argument over how five newly
interdependent sectors should actually coordinate with each other. A wants a single, centralized
planning authority allocating labor and priorities across all five sectors at once — efficient in
principle, and a reasonable answer to real coordination chaos. B resists centralization, arguing that
each sector's specific, hands-on expertise — knowing the extraction ground, knowing the machinery,
knowing the roads — cannot be responsibly dictated by generalists sitting outside it, and that top-down
control risks worse decisions than the sectors coordinating directly with each other as equals. Neither
side is arguing for chaos or for tyranny; this is efficiency-through-central-control versus
accuracy-through-distributed-expertise, a genuine trade-off with no free answer.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early
city-wide organizer or planner, someone whose instinct is that five sectors pulling in different
directions without a single coordinating hand will eventually collide. Character B is plausibly a
leading figure from within one of the emerging sectors itself — resource extraction or fabrication are
both plausible candidates — someone whose authority comes from expertise a generalist simply doesn't
have, and who distrusts decisions made about their work by people who don't do it. The conflict plays
naturally as a founding-era argument over what kind of city Byrd is going to become, long before any of
its later, more formalized sector identities exist.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's position wins. Rather than settling under one centralized planning authority, the five emerging
sectors work out ongoing, direct coordination with each other as separate, self-governing bodies — no
single office dictates terms to the rest; coordination happens sector-to-sector, negotiated continuously
rather than handed down. Byrd becomes exactly the kind of place its own isolation demanded — not one
city with one industry, but several industries learning to run as one continuous system, out of sheer
necessity rather than any deliberate plan to build it that way.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because coordination now happens through ongoing sector-to-sector negotiation instead of central
command, each of the five sectors is forced to develop its own genuine internal leadership and
decision-making structure — someone capable of actually representing it in those negotiations. The
sectors don't just become economically distinct from each other; they become organizationally distinct,
each one answerable, in practice, to nobody outside itself.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because each sector now has its own self-contained internal leadership answering to no outside
authority, disputes between sectors — over how much labor or underground space one sector's expansion
should be allowed to claim from another, for instance — can no longer be settled by any single citywide
authority simply overruling both sides; there isn't one anymore. Every such dispute has to be negotiated
out directly, sector to sector, with no permanent referee — a structural condition Byrd carries forward
indefinitely once this cycle's arrangement takes hold.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Byrd's economy is now genuinely integrated exactly as its isolation demanded, but specifically as five
self-governing, independently led sectors bound together by necessity and continuous negotiation rather
than any central authority — a structural pattern with no built-in referee for the disputes it will keep
generating, laying track, centuries before anyone in the city calls it anything more formal, for exactly
the kind of internal, sector-driven complexity later eras of Byrd's history would recognize immediately.
