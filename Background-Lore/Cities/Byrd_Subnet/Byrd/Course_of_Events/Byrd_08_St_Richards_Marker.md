## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Byrd's original founding expedition makes camp on the surface before anyone knows what actually lies
beneath the snow around them — a small, temporary encampment, meant only to hold the group while the
buried station is searched for and, eventually, found.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Once the buried station is discovered and the community begins moving underground, that first surface
encampment has no obvious practical reason to remain significant at all. The city's whole future life is
about to happen below ground; leaving the surface camp to be reclaimed by snow would cost nothing
material. And yet, to some of the earliest arrivals, the idea of simply abandoning the place they stood
before they knew whether any of this would work starts to feel wrong — without anyone yet agreeing on
why, or what, if anything, should actually be done about it.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a meaning-versus-survival resource argument between
someone who wants the original encampment site preserved and marked, and someone who thinks a fragile,
newly underground community cannot afford to spend scarce early labor on anything that isn't shelter,
food, or structural safety. A's case is about memory — the uncertainty the expedition actually lived
through deserves to be remembered honestly, not quietly erased the moment it stopped mattering
practically. B's case is about survival math — every hour spent building a surface marker is an hour not
spent making the buried station genuinely habitable, in a community that does not yet have labor to
spare on anything non-essential. Neither position is sentimental or callous for its own sake; both are
weighing real, competing costs.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly one of the founding
expedition's own members, someone who was actually present at that first uncertain encampment and feels
its significance personally rather than abstractly. Character B is plausibly an early organizer or
planner responsible for allocating the fledgling community's still-desperately-thin labor supply,
someone whose job is making sure the group survives its first real winter underground. The conflict
plays naturally as two people who both care about the community's future, disagreeing about what that
future actually needs first.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The argument resolves toward a genuine compromise rather than either side's full position: residents
mark the site anyway, but with whatever spare labor the community can actually afford rather than any
dedicated investment — a modest, unglamorous memorial, built incidentally rather than prioritized.
Because of exactly how modestly and incidentally it comes together, the site settles into meaning
something specific: not a monument to triumph or discovery, but to the moment just before it, when
nobody yet knew whether the expedition would succeed at all.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because St. Richard's marker specifically commemorates uncertainty rather than triumph, it settles, over
the following generations, into a particular kind of civic ritual site — not a place for celebration or
founding-day pageantry, which needs a triumph to celebrate, but a place people visit quietly before
uncertain undertakings of their own: a new venture, a risky expedition, a hard decision nobody can yet
be sure will pay off.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the site becomes specifically associated with pre-decision uncertainty rather than general
remembrance, it ends up, centuries later, informally adopted by people across every one of Byrd's
different lines of work at moments of genuine institutional risk — a precision-critical call in the
fabrication plants, an uncertain read on unstable extraction ground, any decision nobody can yet be
certain will hold. The site becomes a shared civic touchstone specifically because doubt, unlike
triumph, is something every line of work in the city eventually has in common.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

St. Richard's marker stands as Byrd's own quiet, unusual civic touchstone — not a monument to what the
city achieved, but a place its people still visit whenever they face real uncertainty of their own, a
practice that outlived the individuals who built it for entirely practical, unglamorous reasons in the
first place.
