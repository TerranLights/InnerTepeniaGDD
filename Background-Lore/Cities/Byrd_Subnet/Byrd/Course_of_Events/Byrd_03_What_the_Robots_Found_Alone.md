## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The crossing to Byrd's coordinates is judged too dangerous to risk human lives on, given how little is
actually known about the route or what waits at the end of it. And yet the expedition still has to
happen — Byrd is never going to be found otherwise, and the exile community's need for it doesn't go
away because the risk is real.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The decision is made to send an all-robot crew alone, accepting a level of risk no human crew would
ever be assigned to take. If the crossing succeeds, the earliest, most formative memory of Byrd's own
founding — the actual moment of discovering the buried station, the first confirmation that any of this
was real — will belong entirely to robots, with no human witness present for it at all.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation over how the decision itself gets
framed, not over whether the crossing happens. A believes the choice to send robots is straightforwardly
practical — protecting an exile population that cannot easily be replaced by risking a population that,
by its own nature, can better withstand extreme cold, structural collapse, and the total absence of
rescue. B doesn't necessarily dispute the practical logic, but objects to the decision being made *about*
robots without being made *with* them — an assignment handed down rather than a choice offered. This
should avoid a simple "humans devalue robots" framing; A's reasoning can be genuinely sound and
non-malicious, and B's objection can be about consent and authorship rather than about the mission's
wisdom. The tension is over who gets to decide that a given risk is acceptable to take.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a human coordinator
from the broader exile leadership structure (Belgrano, or a Palmer City/Rothera logistics figure)
tasked with actually assembling the crossing crew, someone whose responsibility is to the whole
community's survival odds. Character B is plausibly one of the robots slated to make the crossing
itself, or an advocate speaking for the crew — a figure whose stake is immediate and personal, since
this decision determines whether the single most formative moment in the city's future founding story
happens to them as an assignment or as a choice they made for themselves. This conflict sits close to
the project's own central question of robot consciousness and autonomy, and works best played with real
weight on both sides rather than resolved as an easy villain-and-victim story.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The confrontation doesn't change whether the crew is all-robot — it changes how the mission is framed
and authorized. A concedes the point that matters to B: the crossing proceeds as the crew's own
undertaking, formally acknowledged as their choice rather than simply an order handed down. The crew
departs, drives the Arrastradoras alone across the unmapped interior, and finds the buried station —
with no human witness present for the discovery at all. Byrd's founding story carries a genuine,
distinctive weight from the start: a city whose very first moment of existence was experienced only by
the population that would go on to help build it.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because no human witnessed the discovery, and because the crossing was formally recognized as the
crew's own chosen act rather than an assignment, the surviving crew's own testimony becomes the
community's only source for the actual founding moment — there is no second, independent account to
check it against. From the very beginning, Byrd's civic history is built on the standing premise that
robot testimony is first-hand historical fact, not secondhand or corroborating evidence, simply because
there was never any other kind available.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Given robots' established long lifespans, at least one member of that original crew plausibly remains
alive in Byrd centuries later — and because the founding story has always rested entirely on robot
testimony as primary fact rather than supplementary account, that surviving crew member's word carries
an unusually direct, uncontested authority whenever the city's own origin comes up. Their memory isn't
treated as one perspective among several; it is, in a very literal sense, the only firsthand record that
has ever existed.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Byrd exists now, founded by a crew whose discovery no human ever witnessed and whose account has stood,
unchallenged and unsupplemented, as the entire basis of the city's origin story ever since. Robot
testimony was never something Byrd had to learn to trust as civic history — it was, from the very first
telling, the only kind of testimony the founding had.
