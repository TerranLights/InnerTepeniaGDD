## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Byrd's underground fabrication economy produces real, valuable output, but output nobody can reach does
the rest of Tepenia no good. Byrd's remote interior position leaves it with no easy natural route to
anywhere else — everything the city makes sits, for now, exactly where it's made.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Two overland highways get built specifically to solve that problem — Hwy 1 running north toward the
Peninsula and South America beyond it, Hwy 22 running east across the entire continent toward the Mirny
subnet. But maintaining two continent-spanning routes from one remote interior city takes enormous,
continuous investment — a cost that only becomes fully apparent once the roads have to be sustained
indefinitely, not simply built once and left alone.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone insisting Byrd
commit fully to permanently maintaining both roads, and someone warning that the ongoing cost is more
than one remote interior city can sustainably carry alone. A's position is existential: without both
roads staying open, Byrd's output is worthless to anyone but itself, and the city's entire relevance to
the rest of the Federation depends on it. B's position is a real resource argument, not
short-sightedness: a city this size, this far from support, committing indefinite labor and material to
two continent-spanning routes risks starving its own domestic needs to feed an external obligation that
might not be sustainable a generation from now. Both are reasoning correctly from the numbers they each
prioritize.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early trade or
dispatch-minded leader who sees the roads as Byrd's whole reason for mattering to the rest of Tepenia.
Character B is plausibly an internal resource planner or infrastructure-minded figure, someone whose
job is weighing the city's own finite labor and material against every external commitment being asked
of it. The conflict plays best as an internal argument within Byrd's own leadership over how much of the
city's future the roads are allowed to consume, not as an outside party pressuring Byrd to overextend.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A's position wins outright. Byrd commits to maintaining both roads indefinitely, understanding early
that its whole relevance to the rest of the Federation depends entirely on those two roads staying open
and functional — not as a negotiated compromise, but as a deliberate, unhedged bet on external relevance
over internal caution.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Byrd absorbs the full maintenance burden itself rather than sharing it, the city has to
structurally set aside a permanent, ongoing share of its own labor and output specifically for road
upkeep, forever — not a one-time construction cost but a standing claim on the city's productive
capacity that never goes away, baked into Byrd's economic planning as a fixed constant rather than a
variable expense.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because road upkeep becomes a fixed, non-negotiable share of Byrd's own labor and output rather than a
discretionary expense, later generations of the city's leadership structurally cannot treat the highways
as optional even during genuine internal hardship — any future strain on Byrd's own economy
automatically strains the roads as well, since the two have become the same line item rather than
separable choices a struggling city could otherwise trim independently of each other.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Byrd is now bound to two continent-spanning highways as a permanent, inseparable part of its own
domestic economy — Hwy 1 north, Hwy 22 east, each one costing the city a fixed, forever share of its own
productive capacity to keep open. The city's relevance to the rest of the Federation and its own
internal economic structure have become, for better and for worse, the same thing.
