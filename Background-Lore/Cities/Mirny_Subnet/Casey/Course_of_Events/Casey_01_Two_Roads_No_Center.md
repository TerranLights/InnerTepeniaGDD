## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Casey's junction position — the meeting point of Hwy 110 and Hwy 2 — draws a wider, more mixed early
wave of settlers than more geographically isolated founding sites ever see. Six or more nations arrive
in genuinely comparable numbers, with no single community large enough to hold an obvious claim to
founding it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

With no national community holding the numbers to set the settlement's tone on its own terms, the
earliest years carry a real, unresolved question nobody can simply legislate away: whose customs, whose
civic habits, whose sense of "how a settlement organizes itself" actually becomes Casey's default? The
six communities don't agree. Some want a rotating council seated by national bloc, on the reasonable
logic that every founding population elsewhere in Tepenia eventually settles into something like this.
Others want whichever group contributes most visibly to the junction's own upkeep to earn informal
precedence. A few want no group privileged at all — civic life organized purely around the practical
work of keeping the highway crossing itself running, with national identity kept entirely out of it. A
vote can't settle the question outright, since agreeing on voting procedure would itself require
agreeing whose customary procedures count.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone pushing to
formalize one nation's customary civic practices as Casey's default framework, and someone insisting
civic life organize around shared function instead, with no single group's customs privileged over any
other's. Character A shouldn't read as a power-grab — their instinct is the completely reasonable one by
the standard of literally every other Tepenian founding story, where one culture typically does end up
setting the civic tone. Character B's insistence on neutrality has to be earned, not merely contrarian —
they need a real, practical case that privileging any one of six comparably sized communities risks the
other five never fully investing in the settlement at all.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early community
representative from whichever of the six nations happens to hold a marginal numerical edge — not real
dominance, just enough of one to make formalizing that edge into precedence feel natural rather than
opportunistic. Character B is plausibly a junction-operations figure, someone whose actual job is
coordinating Hwy 110/Hwy 2 traffic and who has already watched all six communities cooperate
successfully around exactly that shared, practical work — arguing for building civic structure on top of
what demonstrably already functions, rather than importing a hierarchy none of the other five communities
asked for.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Character B's case wins out — not through a dramatic vote, but because the daily reality of running the
junction already models the exact kind of shared, function-first cooperation Character A's proposal
would disrupt. Casey's earliest civic institutions coalesce around the junction's own operational needs
instead of around any single nation's customs.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Casey's founding civic structure never designates a privileged culture, later immigration waves
arrive to a settlement with no existing "way things are done" that newcomers are expected to assimilate
into. Integration runs through demonstrated contribution to function-based civic roles rather than
through adoption of any dominant community's customs — an unusually low-friction absorption process
compared to founding cities elsewhere in Tepenia that do settle around one dominant culture.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the integration route runs through work rather than cultural assimilation, Casey's civic
leadership positions end up filled based on demonstrated junction-operations competence regardless of
national origin — a quietly meritocratic promotion pattern distinct from other Mirny subnet cities,
where leadership tracks typically run through the dominant founding culture's own institutions instead.
Delete the 2nd-order change and this pattern has nothing to select for; it depends directly on
integration having no cultural gatekeeping to route around.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Casey stands as a city whose civic character was built around function rather than founders — diffuse
in identity by design, functional in practice by necessity, with a leadership class shaped by
demonstrated competence at keeping the junction running rather than by inherited national standing. The
city has no founding myth to point to, only a working method that happened to become one.
