## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Casey's exile community inherits Australia's largest, best-equipped pre-exile station, along with
whatever paperwork and records happened to survive the transition intact — most of it administrative,
forgettable, filed away and never revisited.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Idris Wetherall, going through the inherited records for reasons that have nothing to do with
nostalgia — cataloguing what actually survived, mostly — comes across an old reference to a modest
social bar at the original station, named Splinters. It's a footnote by any reasonable measure: no
surviving photographs, no detailed description, just a name and a handful of administrative mentions
confirming it existed. Nothing about the record demands action. The easy, obvious choice is to note it
and move on to more materially useful salvage. But the name itself — and whatever it once represented to
the people who worked that station before exile — snags on something. Wetherall starts making the case,
to whoever will listen, that reviving it is worth actually doing, and finds that "worth doing" and
"worth spending the community's limited early resources on" are two different arguments he has to win
separately.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone who wants to spend
scarce founding-era resources reviving a purely social space on the strength of little more than a name
and a feeling, and someone responsible for prioritizing where those same scarce resources actually go.
This shouldn't play as sentiment versus cold bureaucracy — Character B's caution is entirely rational in
a settlement still establishing basic infrastructure, and Character A isn't naive about that; the
argument has to be that a community running purely on survival logic, with nothing built yet for its own
sake, risks becoming a settlement that never feels like anyone's home. Both positions are defensible;
the disagreement is genuinely about sequencing, not about whether the bar has value at all.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Idris Wetherall
himself, or a records-and-archives figure like him — someone whose job put the reference in front of
them in the first place, and who has no formal authority to allocate resources but real standing to make
a persuasive case. Character B is plausibly an early resource-allocation administrator, someone
responsible for the founding community's material priorities, whose skepticism comes from genuine
stewardship rather than indifference to civic morale.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Wetherall's case wins, but modestly — not a major allocation, just enough salvaged material and shared
labor, contributed voluntarily on top of people's existing work, to open something small. A modest,
informal gathering space, deliberately named after the forgotten record, opens its doors.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Splinters was built on volunteered labor and salvaged material rather than a formal municipal
allocation, nobody within the founding administration holds any official ownership or oversight of it —
it opens as, and stays, a genuinely community-run space from its very first night, with no civic
authority ever having claimed it as an official project.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because no civic authority holds formal ownership, Splinters becomes the one significant piece of
Casey's built environment residents point to as belonging to the community directly rather than to the
settlement's administration — a precedent, deliberately or not, for how future gathering spaces in Casey
get built: informally, voluntarily, and outside official channels, right up through the bar's later
explosive growth. Delete the 2nd-order change — imagine Splinters opening as a funded municipal project
instead — and this ownership-by-the-people precedent simply doesn't exist; it depends specifically on how
the bar got built, not merely on the fact that it exists.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Casey now holds a small, informal gathering space named after a footnote nobody else thought worth
acting on — modest in scale, but already, from its first night, understood by residents as theirs in a
way nothing officially built for them ever quite is. The seed of what will eventually become the city's
single most famous landmark is planted, owned by no one and everyone at once.
