## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Casey's cardinal social rule holds that route information gets shared immediately and accurately, since
lives genuinely depend on it — a rule stated plainly, understood by everyone, and treated as
non-negotiable civic baseline.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Living up to that rule consistently, across an entire career of forecasting katabatic wind windows for
travelers who have no other reliable source of that information, turns out to be a far harder standard
to actually meet than to state. Most dispatchers get it right most of the time, which is good enough to
keep the rule functioning in the aggregate — but Dispatcher Okonkwo-Hale gets it right every time, call
after call, for long enough that residents stop treating the pattern as luck and start treating it as
something closer to a guarantee. That shift matters, because travelers begin making genuinely
consequential decisions — when to leave, which route to risk — on the specific, unstated assumption that
Okonkwo-Hale's calls in particular carry more certainty than the civic rule technically promises anyone.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between Okonkwo-Hale (or a
dispatcher very much like them) and someone forced to reckon with what happens when a call, for the
first time in a long career, comes uncomfortably close to being wrong — a near-miss, a genuinely
marginal window that could have gone either way. The conflict isn't about incompetence; it's about the
gap between a reputation the whole city has quietly come to treat as infallible and the actual,
irreducible uncertainty of forecasting wind on the Antarctic Circle. The other party should be someone
with a direct stake in that gap — a traveler or fellow dispatcher who nearly paid the cost of it, or who
sees the near-miss as proof the whole city's trust has drifted somewhere no single dispatcher should
have to carry.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Dispatcher
Okonkwo-Hale themselves, confronting for the first time what their own reputation has quietly become.
Character B is plausibly a fellow dispatcher — someone who has watched colleagues start deferring to
Okonkwo-Hale's calls over their own judgment, or a traveler who took an unusual risk specifically
because "Long Odds never gets it wrong," and who now has to sit with how close that trust came to
costing something.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The near-miss doesn't break the reputation — Okonkwo-Hale's judgment holds, the window is called
correctly in the end, if by a narrower margin than usual. But the confrontation forces something into
the open that the city had never actually said out loud: "Long Odds" is not infallibility, it is one
dispatcher's exceptional skill operating under real, ordinary uncertainty, and the city has been quietly
treating those two things as the same.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the gap between reputation and reality gets named directly rather than left to fester, Casey's
dispatch service formalizes something it never needed to before — a standard practice of stating a
margin alongside every call, even Okonkwo-Hale's own, rather than letting travelers read every forecast
as an unqualified guarantee.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because margins are now stated explicitly rather than implied by a dispatcher's personal reputation,
"Long Odds" stops being read as a synonym for Okonkwo-Hale personally and starts becoming a genuine,
transferable civic standard — a level of stated precision every dispatcher is trained toward and judged
against, whether or not any of them ever quite match the original. Delete the 2nd-order change — imagine
the near-miss simply passing without any formal response — and there's no mechanism turning one person's
reputation into an institutional standard; it depends specifically on the margin-stating practice the
near-miss produced.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

"Long Odds" is no longer just a nickname for one exceptional dispatcher — it's a genuine, formalized
civic standard of forecasting precision that every Casey dispatcher trains toward, with stated margins
now built into every call, and Okonkwo-Hale's own record still the benchmark nobody else has quite
matched.
