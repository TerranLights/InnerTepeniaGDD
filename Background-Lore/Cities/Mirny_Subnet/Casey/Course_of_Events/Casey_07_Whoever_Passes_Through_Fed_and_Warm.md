## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Casey hosts an enormous, constant stream of strangers passing through — a volume of travelers that
could easily wear any city down into resentful, transactional hospitality, treating each new arrival as
one more anonymous face in an endless line.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Casey's whole civic function depends on travelers actually wanting to stop, not merely needing to — the
city's economic and social relevance rides directly on its reputation for hospitality, which means
resentment toward the endless stream of strangers isn't just an emotional cost, it's a practical threat
to the thing keeping the city relevant at all. But knowing that intellectually doesn't automatically
produce genuine warmth in practice — it's entirely possible to perform hospitality without feeling it,
and travelers can usually tell the difference. Some workers in Casey's hospitality-facing roles start
drifting toward exactly that: competent, efficient, hollow. Others resist the drift, insisting the
warmth has to be real or the whole reputation eventually collapses under its own performance.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a hospitality worker who
has, understandably, started treating the endless stream of travelers as an exhausting, faceless
obligation to be managed efficiently, and a colleague who insists on treating every single traveler as
someone worth stopping for, no matter how many thousand have already come through that week. Character
A's exhaustion is completely legitimate — nobody can sustain unlimited emotional labor for strangers
forever, and the burnout is real, not a character flaw. Character B's insistence isn't naive either;
they need a genuine, lived reason the effort still matters, not just a cheerful disposition. The
conflict should land as two valid responses to the same real cost, not competence versus laziness.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a longtime worker at
a Casey waypoint or hospitality post — someone who's been doing the job long enough to have genuinely
earned their exhaustion. Character B is plausibly a newer worker, or an older one who has found some
specific way of framing the work that keeps it from curdling — someone whose stake isn't abstract
civic pride but something concrete, like having once been a stranger somewhere far less welcoming
themselves.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two don't resolve into one side simply winning — instead, Character B's approach gets adopted more
widely, but modified by Character A's honest account of the cost: genuine warmth stays the standard, but
paired with real acknowledgment, for the first time institutionally, that sustaining it requires
deliberate rest and rotation rather than treating hospitality workers as an inexhaustible resource.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the resolution builds in acknowledgment of the cost rather than simply demanding more warmth,
Casey's hospitality-facing roles develop deliberate rotation and rest practices that other Tepenian
cities' equivalent roles don't formally build in — treating genuine hospitality as a resource that needs
active maintenance, not an infinite well.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because rotation and rest get built into the system rather than left to individual endurance, Casey's
hospitality workforce sustains its reputation for genuine warmth across far longer careers than
comparable roles in cities without the same practice — turnover in hospitality-facing jobs stays
unusually low, and workers who might have burned out elsewhere stay long enough to train the next
generation directly in what the warmth is actually supposed to feel like. Delete the 2nd-order
change — imagine the resolution had simply demanded more effort without addressing the cost — and
there's no mechanism protecting workers from the exact burnout that started the conflict; the low
turnover depends specifically on the rest-and-rotation practice, not on renewed goodwill alone.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Casey's hospitality culture stands as something genuinely sustainable rather than merely well-intentioned
— warmth that's real precisely because the city has learned, institutionally, to protect the people
providing it, keeping the "fed and warm" reputation honest across generations of workers rather than
burning through one generation's goodwill and hoping the next one matches it.
