## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Six nations arrive at Casey in comparable numbers, none dominant enough to set the civic tone alone —
a settlement evenly split enough that it could plausibly go several different directions, none of them
yet decided.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A city this evenly split could easily fracture into competing communities instead of coalescing into
one functioning whole — and for a period, that risk is genuinely live. Different neighborhoods start
forming along loosely national lines, not out of hostility but out of the ordinary human instinct to
cluster with the familiar. Casey's early leadership has to decide, deliberately, whether to let that
clustering harden into something more permanent and political, or to actively work against it. The
choice isn't obvious or costless — leaning into the clustering would give each community a clearer,
more legible identity within the city; resisting it means asking every group to accept a civic life that
privileges none of them, including their own.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a leadership figure
pushing for deliberate civic neutrality — no group's institutions or customs formally favored — and a
community organizer from one of the six founding nations who argues that neutrality, in practice, just
means nobody's specific needs get met, since "treating everyone equally" can quietly default to serving
whichever group already knows how to navigate purely functional institutions best. This is a legitimate
critique of neutrality as a governing principle, not simple factionalism — Character B isn't asking for
domination, just recognition that "no favorites" isn't automatically the same as "fair." Character A has
to make a real case for why neutrality is still worth the cost of dissatisfying everyone equally.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early civic
administrator or founding-council figure charged with actually running the settlement day to day, whose
commitment to neutrality comes from having watched (or feared) what favoring one group does to the
other five. Character B is plausibly a respected figure within one of the six national communities —
someone whose community genuinely has needs a purely function-first civic structure doesn't naturally
address (a specific language service, a specific religious or cultural practice), using that gap to
argue neutrality is being applied too bluntly.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Character A's core commitment to neutrality holds, but Character B's critique changes its
implementation — civic institutions stay formally unaligned with any single nation's customs, while
becoming more actively responsive to specific, demonstrated gaps regardless of which community raises
them. Neutrality survives, refined rather than abandoned.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because neutrality gets refined into responsiveness rather than simply defended as-is, Casey develops an
early civic habit of addressing specific community needs case by case, without ever formally codifying
which community "owns" which service — a pattern of ad hoc, function-first problem-solving that becomes
recognizable as the city's own governing style well before it's ever named as a philosophy.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because Casey's governing style becomes case-by-case responsiveness rather than fixed cultural
accommodation, the city never develops the kind of strong founding mythology most Tepenian cities lean
on — no single origin story, no dominant community's founding narrative, just an accumulated record of
problems solved as they arose. Outsiders visiting later read this as Casey having no distinct identity
at all, when it's actually the direct, visible residue of the responsiveness that resolved this
cycle's conflict. Delete the 2nd-order change — imagine neutrality had simply been defended unchanged
— and Casey would likely have developed exactly the kind of clustering-based identity it avoided; the
absence of a founding myth depends specifically on how neutrality evolved, not merely on neutrality
existing.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Casey stands defined by what it does for everyone equally rather than any one group's story — a genuine
achievement of balance, refined rather than rigid, that reads from the outside as simply lacking a
distinct identity, when it is in fact the accumulated, hard-won result of deliberately never picking a
side.
