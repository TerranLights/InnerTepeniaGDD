## The Records Wetherall Wouldn't Let Stay Buried

Splinters — Casey's massive bar and live-music venue, eventually Tepenia's largest and most famous — wasn't
an exile-generation invention. Per `Casey_Full_Extrapolation.md` Section V, the name and concept were
deliberately *rediscovered* from old pre-exile records of the real historical bar at Casey Station.

**"Idris Wetherall" *(placeholder, not developer-chosen)*** is credited with finding those records in the
first place — going through inherited Australian Antarctic Division archival material, per what's established
about his own role, not looking for anything in particular until a passing reference to the original
station's own bar caught his attention. Wetherall pushed for reviving it at genuine city-block scale, a
proposal several early civic planners initially treated as an odd use of resources for a transit city with no
obvious need for a destination-scale entertainment venue. He argued the opposite: a city defined entirely by
people passing through needed exactly one thing worth stopping for, and reviving a piece of Casey's own buried
history gave the city something to be known for that it had chosen, rather than simply inherited. The venue
that resulted eventually outgrew anything Wetherall himself had originally imagined — a minor local legend
specifically because the fame of what he'd found and pushed for became so disproportionate to the modest
archival curiosity that started it.

## The Forecast "Long Odds" Never Missed

Casey's cardinal social rule — route information shared immediately and accurately, because lives depend on
it — had, per local reputation, one dispatcher who embodied it more completely than anyone else across an
entire career.

**Dispatcher "Long Odds" "Okonkwo-Hale" *(placeholder, not developer-chosen)*** earned the nickname, per what's
established about the reputation, specifically because travelers began treating a route flagged safe by this
dispatcher as close to a sure thing regardless of how marginal the underlying conditions looked on paper —
years of forecasts that held up, one after another, building a level of trust few dispatchers anywhere in
Tepenia ever reached. The reputation traces to one particular call more than any other, per what's remembered
of the era: a wind-window forecast that every other available reading suggested was too risky to certify,
issued as safe anyway on the strength of a subtle pressure-pattern read most dispatchers wouldn't have caught.
The convoy that trusted the call crossed without incident, in conditions that closed the same route entirely
within hours of their arrival at the other end. Nobody currently working Casey's own dispatch office claims
"Long Odds" was infallible in any literal sense — simply that no forecast on record was ever confirmed wrong,
which is, in a transit city built entirely on trusting exactly this kind of judgment, close enough to the same
thing.

## What the Instruments Were Actually Watching For

Casey's inherited meteorological and katabatic-monitoring equipment — Australian Antarctic Division
infrastructure repurposed into the technical backbone of the city's own wind-warning culture — sat precisely
on the Antarctic Circle threshold, a genuinely useful observation point for the transition between full polar
conditions and milder coastal ones (`Casey_Full_Extrapolation.md` Section II).

The equipment's own value was tested directly during an early transitional-season storm system that
approached from an unusual direction, one the founding generation's own forecasting assumptions hadn't fully
accounted for. Technical staff monitoring the Circle-threshold instruments caught the pattern shift
considerably earlier than crews relying on conditions closer to the coast would have, and the warning reached
dispatch in time to hold several crews already staged for departure. The storm, when it arrived, proved
severe enough that the held crews would have been in real difficulty caught mid-route. The incident became,
within Casey's own technical staff, a specific, cited justification for why the inherited scientific
equipment mattered beyond institutional carryover — proof, early in the city's own history, that the
Circle-threshold position gave real, actionable warning time no other instrument placement in the subnet
could have provided as early.

## The Curiosity Down the Road

Wilkes Station — a genuine pre-exile American research site a few kilometers from Casey, already abandoned
since roughly 1969 in the real-world record and further buried under centuries of accumulated snow by
Tepenia's own founding era — remained a local curiosity throughout Casey's existence, never a meaningfully
active site (`Casey_Full_Extrapolation.md` Section III).

An early attempt by a group of curious residents to actually excavate down to Wilkes's own buried structures,
undertaken more out of idle interest than any serious research purpose, was abandoned after several days of
digging revealed only that the accumulated snow depth made any real excavation a considerably larger
undertaking than a casual weekend project could accomplish. The attempt is remembered, per what's passed down
about it, less for what it found — nothing, beyond confirming how deeply buried the site actually was — and
more as the specific moment Wilkes's own reputation as "a curiosity, not a destination" was settled through
direct, practical experience rather than simply assumed. Nobody in Casey's own subsequent history attempted a
serious excavation again.

## The Trucker Who Timed the Fork Wrong

The Hwy 110/Hwy 2 junction — the literal geographic and economic center of Casey — was, for most travelers,
an uneventful decision point: a fork in the road, chosen, and moved past. One specific miscalculation is
still cited, informally, as the reason Waystation Row's own dispatchers double-check a traveler's stated
destination before releasing route-condition information.

A trucker running an unfamiliar route, having requested wind-window conditions for the coastal Hwy 110 leg,
misheard or misremembered the dispatcher's own directional guidance and instead departed onto Hwy 2 toward
the deep interior — a considerably longer, harder route requiring different supplies and preparation the
trucker hadn't packed for. The error was caught at the next waystation checkpoint, well before it became
genuinely dangerous, and the trucker was redirected back with minimal loss beyond wasted time and fuel. Casey
dispatch, per what's established about the procedural change that followed, began explicitly confirming a
traveler's own stated destination against the route they were actually issued conditions for — a small,
specific fix, traced directly back to one trucker's one wrong turn at the fork the whole city was built
around.

## Fast in, Fast Out, By Design

Fast-turnover hospitality infrastructure — built for efficient, high-volume service to travelers passing
through rather than for guests staying to experience the city itself — reflected Casey's whole transit-economy
identity from the earliest planning stages, distinct from a destination city's own hospitality model.

The design philosophy was tested directly, once, by an early operator who built a waystation lodging house on
the more conventional destination-city model — comfortable, unhurried, designed to encourage a longer stay.
The business struggled from its own first season: travelers passing through Casey had no reason to linger,
and the lodging house's own slower turnover meant it served far fewer travelers overall than a genuinely
fast-turnover operation would have, in a city whose entire population moved on the same reactive, transit-
driven rhythm as everything else here. The operator redesigned the business within the year around rapid,
efficient service instead, and the revised model succeeded where the original hadn't — an early, concrete
confirmation that Casey's own economy simply didn't support hospitality built for lingering, however well
executed, the same lesson every subsequent waystation operator inherited without needing to relearn it
themselves.
