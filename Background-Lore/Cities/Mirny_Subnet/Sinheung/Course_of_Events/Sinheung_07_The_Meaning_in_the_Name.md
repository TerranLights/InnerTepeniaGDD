## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Sinheung's founding generation needs to decide, at some point, what to actually call the place they're
building — not the station they inherited, something of their own, a question left genuinely open in the
settlement's earliest years.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Choosing a name for a city secured through diplomacy rather than settled by conquest or discovery means
the name has to say something true about how the place actually came to exist — a name that pretends to
false roots, or simply imports the inherited Russian station terminology, would ring hollow against the
settlement's own actual, more complicated founding story. They settle on Sinheung (신흥) — "newly rising,"
a name that names the process itself rather than claiming false roots. But a name that honest invites its
own kind of pressure — you can't call yourselves "newly rising" forever without eventually having risen
into something — and once the name is chosen, someone within the founding generation has to actually
grapple with what happens if the city's own ambitions eventually stall, having named themselves after a
promise they might not keep.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation, in the naming discussion itself,
between someone advocating for Sinheung precisely because it's an honest, unresolved promise rather than
a settled claim, and someone more cautious, worried that naming the city after an aspiration rather than
an achievement sets an impossible, permanent standard the settlement might never actually live up to. A's
argument is about honesty; B's is about the real risk of binding an entire civic identity to a promise
that circumstance might not let them keep.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a founding-generation
figure with a more idealistic or forward-looking temperament, drawn to a name that names the process of
becoming rather than any fixed state. Character B is plausibly a more cautious, practically-minded
counterpart — not opposed to ambition, but wary of institutionalizing a name that could read, generations
later, as a broken promise if the settlement's fortunes ever genuinely falter.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A's argument wins the naming decision, but B's caution shapes how the name gets understood going forward
— the founding generation deliberately frames Sinheung not as a claim to have already arrived, but as an
ongoing commitment each future generation has to keep renewing, rather than a fixed achievement locked in
at founding. The name becomes, quietly, its own standing promise — one more reason the whole city leans so
hard into proving itself through output rather than simply resting on the claim that secured its ground.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the name was deliberately framed as an ongoing commitment rather than a settled achievement,
Sinheung's own founding-era civic rhetoric develops a distinct, forward-looking vocabulary — residents
and leaders alike speak of the city in terms of what it's still becoming, rather than what it already is,
a linguistic habit that outlives the founding generation itself.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that forward-looking vocabulary becomes ingrained civic habit, later generations of Sinheung
residents instinctively describe even genuinely major achievements — the fabrication economy's success,
the chamber-manufacturing role — not as arrivals but as the latest stage in an ongoing process, resisting
the kind of civic complacency a "we've made it" framing might otherwise invite.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sinheung carries its own name as a living, ongoing commitment rather than a settled fact — a civic
vocabulary, deliberately built into the founding naming decision itself, that keeps every subsequent
generation talking about the city in terms of what it's still becoming.
