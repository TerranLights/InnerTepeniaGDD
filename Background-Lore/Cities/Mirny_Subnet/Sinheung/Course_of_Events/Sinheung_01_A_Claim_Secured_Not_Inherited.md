## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Korea's claim to this site comes from the same Jeju-do court negotiation that allocates Shirayuki to
Japan, not from any Korean real-world station ever having stood here — a claim settled entirely at the
negotiating table, before a single Korean exile has ever set foot on the ice.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A claim settled by treaty, rather than grown organically the way most Tepenian cities' founding stories
work, means Korean exiles arrive to a place their nation had to formally negotiate for rather than simply
inherit. The founding population starts from a genuinely different footing than its cluster neighbors —
a home secured through diplomacy, not birthright. But a claim secured on paper still has to be made real
by the people actually living there — and in the earliest years, before there's anything concrete to
point to as proof the claim was worth securing, some among the founding population start to genuinely
wonder, openly, whether the diplomatic effort was actually worth what it cost, given how little the
settlement has yet produced to show for it.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a founding-generation
resident growing genuinely discouraged by how little the inherited research infrastructure has produced
so far, questioning whether the diplomatic claim was worth the cost of securing it, and another resident
who insists the claim's worth was never going to be proven quickly, and that abandoning confidence in it
this early would waste everything already invested. This is a conflict about patience under real,
justified doubt — A's discouragement is grounded in genuine, visible stagnation, not mere pessimism, and
B's insistence isn't blind optimism so much as a bet that visible proof simply takes longer than anyone
wants it to.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a founding-generation
settler, perhaps someone who personally remembers or was told directly about the Jeju-do negotiation's
own cost, frustrated that the settlement has little concrete to show after the effort that went into
securing it. Character B is plausibly another settler, or an early community leader, whose argument rests
less on evidence than on unwillingness to concede the claim was a mistake before it's genuinely been
given the chance to prove otherwise.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's argument holds the community together through the discouraged stretch — no exodus, no formal
abandonment of the settlement's own ambitions, just a population that keeps working while the doubt sits
unresolved. Proving the claim was worth securing becomes the founding generation's own unspoken task from
the very first years onward, carried forward specifically because the alternative — conceding the
diplomatic effort was wasted — was never seriously entertained as an option once voiced aloud.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the doubt was real and openly voiced rather than simply avoided, "proving the claim was worth
it" becomes something the founding population actually names and discusses among themselves, rather than
an unspoken cultural undercurrent nobody directly acknowledges — a founding generation that talked itself
through genuine discouragement leaves behind an explicit, remembered account of having done so.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that account survives as explicit, remembered civic history rather than vague sentiment, later
generations inherit not just the pressure to keep proving the claim's worth, but the actual story of the
founding generation's own doubt and how it was overcome — giving every subsequent wave of Sinheung
residents a concrete, human precedent to draw on whenever their own confidence in the city's trajectory
wavers, rather than having to work through the same discouragement from scratch each time.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sinheung's founding population has weathered its first real test of confidence in the diplomatically-
secured claim, and carries forward not just the resolve to keep proving it, but an actual remembered
account of the doubt itself — the beginning of a civic pattern that will recur, in different forms,
across the city's whole subsequent history.
