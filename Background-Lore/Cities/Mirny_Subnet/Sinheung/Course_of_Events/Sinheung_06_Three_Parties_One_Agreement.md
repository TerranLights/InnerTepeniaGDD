## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Zhongshan, Shirayuki, and Sinheung all trace back to the same Jeju-do negotiation among Japan, Korea, and
the Sinian Federation, before any of them ever occupy their allocated sites — three futures decided at
once, around the same table, by delegates who will never actually have to live with the outcome
themselves.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Arriving at adjacent physical locations doesn't automatically make three separate founding populations
act like established neighbors — each city's own exiles arrive with their own priorities, their own
immediate survival needs, and no built-in reason to treat the other two as anything more than
coincidentally nearby. The three communities settle into coexistence gradually, the same diplomatic
working relationship that divided the cluster among them at Jeju-do simply continuing once they're
actually living beside each other. But a relationship that starts as diplomacy takes real time to become
genuine neighborliness — and the first real test of whether that diplomatic habit actually translates into
practical cooperation comes when one city's own early struggle (plausibly Sinheung's own uncertain early
economic footing, per Suggestion #1) puts real pressure on whether the other two are willing to extend
practical help, not just diplomatic courtesy.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a Sinheung representative
requesting genuine practical assistance from one of the cluster's other two cities during a difficult

cluster neighbor. Character B is plausibly a counterpart administrator at Zhongshan or Shirayuki, weighing
a real diplomatic and historical obligation against their own city's own founding-era scarcity, genuinely
uncertain whether extending help is generous or simply irresponsible given their own population's needs.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B commits to a modest, specific form of help — not open-ended support, but a bounded, concrete
contribution scoped to what their own city can actually spare without real risk to their own population.
The cluster's eventual close cooperation traces back to three regional peers who'd already negotiated
directly once before, continuing a working relationship rather than building one from nothing.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the help extended was deliberately bounded and specific rather than open-ended, it sets a
concrete, workable precedent for future cluster cooperation — aid offered as a defined, limited
commitment rather than an unlimited promise neither side could actually sustain, giving the cluster's
later cooperative habits a realistic, sustainable template from the very first real test of the
relationship.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that bounded, sustainable template proved workable the first time, it becomes the standard shape
future cross-cluster cooperation actually takes, generation after generation — specific, scoped
commitments rather than vague mutual obligation, letting the three cities help each other repeatedly over
centuries without any one of them ever being asked to overextend past what they could genuinely spare.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Larsemann Hills cluster now has a proven, working model for cross-city cooperation — specific, bounded
help rather than open-ended obligation — established during Sinheung's own early struggle and carried
forward as the template for every subsequent instance of the three cities actually helping each other.
