## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Sinheung's growing fabrication economy needs a steady, reliable source of raw material to actually
function — its own site offers limited natural material of the right kind for heavy manufacturing, a
genuine structural gap in an otherwise capable industrial city.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A genuine supply relationship forms with Mirny, further down the highway network — raw materials
trucked in, worked into finished goods at Sinheung, a real economic dependency running between the two
cities. But relying on another city's supply chain means Sinheung's own industrial output is only ever
as reliable as Mirny's own ability to keep shipping — and the first time Mirny's own shipments genuinely
falter, whether from a harsh season, an infrastructure problem along the route connecting them, or
simply competing demand from elsewhere, Sinheung has to actually confront how exposed its own celebrated
industrial output really is to a supply chain it doesn't control.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a Sinheung production
manager, alarmed by a genuine shortfall in incoming material and pressing for firmer, prioritized
delivery commitments from Mirny, and a Mirny supplier who has their own city's own needs and other
existing commitments to balance, and can't simply guarantee Sinheung priority without shortchanging
someone else. This isn't bad faith on either side — Mirny's supplier isn't trying to undermine Sinheung,

national reputation now quietly depends on — it's a genuine negotiation over a real, finite resource.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Sinheung production
or logistics manager, directly responsible for keeping the fabrication lines running and personally
exposed to the consequences when incoming material falls short. Character B is plausibly a Mirny quarry
or shipping supervisor, managing genuinely competing demands on the same raw material output — including
Mirny's own established Hwy 110 construction-materials obligation and its national chamber-manufacturing
supply role (see Mirny's own Course of Events Suggestion #11, "What Feeds the Chambers") — without any
particular obligation to treat Sinheung's needs as automatically more important than anyone else's.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two negotiate a formal supply agreement rather than relying on informal goodwill — guaranteed minimum
shipments, agreed in advance, in exchange for Sinheung committing some of its own finished output back to
Mirny at preferential terms. The relationship becomes something both cities have to actively maintain,
not a one-time arrangement either side can take for granted once it's set up.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the relationship now runs on a formal, negotiated agreement rather than informal trust alone, both
cities develop actual administrative roles dedicated specifically to managing it — someone at Sinheung
whose job is tracking incoming shipments against the agreement, and a counterpart at Mirny doing the same
in reverse, where before the relationship had no dedicated oversight at all.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because those two dedicated administrative roles now exist and communicate regularly, they become the
established, standing channel the two cities default to whenever any other cross-city logistics question
comes up between them — not just raw material shipments, but anything else touching the highway corridor
connecting them, since the working relationship and the trust it built already exist and don't need to be
rebuilt from scratch for each new issue.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sinheung and Mirny now maintain a formal, actively managed supply relationship, complete with dedicated
administrative oversight on both sides — a partnership built out of a genuine shortfall scare, now
serving as the default channel for whatever else comes up between the two cities along the highway that
connects them.
