## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Vostok's founding Russian researchers label their equipment, archive sections, and facility terminology
in their own language as a simple, practical matter of course — nothing deliberate about preserving
Russian specifically, just the ordinary way any working population labels its own tools.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The population that eventually inherits the city speaks primarily English and Japanese, with no living
connection to Russian at all — an entire linguistic tradition arriving with no one left who actually
speaks the language the facility's own labels are written in. Those original labels never get formally
translated or replaced — too embedded in the facility's own working structure to bother changing. But
using inherited terminology without understanding its origin means residents end up fluent in words they
can't actually parse — and at some point, a newly-arrived researcher, genuinely curious rather than
merely functional about the vocabulary they use every day without understanding, starts actively asking
what the Russian terms they've memorized by rote actually mean, running into a wider community that
mostly shrugs and has simply never wondered.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a low-key but genuine confrontation between a
researcher determined to actually understand the inherited Russian vocabulary they use daily — pushing
for a real linguistic effort to translate and document it properly — and a more pragmatic colleague who
sees the whole project as unnecessary effort spent on words that function perfectly well as arbitrary
labels regardless of whether anyone understands their literal meaning. A's curiosity is genuine and
culturally meaningful; B's pragmatism reflects a real, reasonable question about whether the effort is
actually worth diverting attention from the research itself.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a newer researcher or a
younger, more historically curious member of Vostok's current population, bothered by the disconnect
between using a whole vocabulary and understanding none of it. Character B is plausibly a longtime
colleague, entirely comfortable with the linguistic layering as it already stands, more focused on the
work itself than on questions of etymology or heritage.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A pursues the translation project alone rather than convincing B to join, and produces something real: an
actual, if modest, glossary connecting the inherited Russian terms to their original meanings, compiled
from whatever archival material still exists. Everyday life at Vostok settles into a strange linguistic
layering — ordinary conversation in one language, technical vocabulary in another nobody currently living
there actually speaks, but now, for the first time, actually documented rather than simply used blind.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the glossary now exists as an actual document rather than remaining an unaddressed curiosity, it
becomes part of the same handoff-documentation culture already established elsewhere in Vostok's research
practice — new researchers arriving at the station are now handed the glossary alongside their other
onboarding material, whether or not they'd ever have thought to ask for one themselves.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because every new researcher now encounters the glossary as standard onboarding material, the inherited
Russian vocabulary stops being an unexamined curiosity and becomes, gradually, a genuine point of shared
institutional identity — Vostok's population, whatever their own actual first language, increasingly
treats knowing the meaning behind the terms as part of what it means to actually belong to the station's
research culture.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Vostok's linguistic layering remains exactly as strange as it always was — ordinary conversation in one
language, technical vocabulary rooted in another nobody speaks — but it's now documented, taught, and
quietly treated as a real piece of shared institutional identity rather than an unexplained inheritance.
