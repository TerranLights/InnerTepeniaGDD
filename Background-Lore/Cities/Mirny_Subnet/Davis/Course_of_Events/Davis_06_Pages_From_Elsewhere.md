## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Davis's population wants more cultural and intellectual life than its own remote, breadbasket-and-research
civic character naturally produces — a real, felt gap between what residents want from their minds and
what a city organized around growing food and running careful field science actually provides.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Building elaborate cultural institutions — libraries, lecture halls, formal literary societies — would
compete directly with resources Davis's breadbasket economy and research heritage genuinely need: labor,
growing space, research funding, all already stretched thin by the responsibility of feeding much of the
wider Federation. A young settlement can't easily justify that trade-off out loud. A quieter alternative
starts forming instead: individuals privately acquiring books from Upper Earth through whatever informal
channels exist, reading them, and passing them along. That kind of network needs real, sustained
coordination to keep functioning — someone has to track what's circulating, who has what, when
something needs to move on — and coordinating something like that inevitably means some residents end up
doing real organizational labor for a network they'd rather not draw much formal attention to, since
formal attention risks exactly the institutional resource competition the network was built to avoid.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone doing the
unglamorous, uncredited coordination work that keeps the informal book network running, and a
participant who wants to formalize it — a real library, official civic recognition, maybe even a modest

Character A's resistance to formalizing isn't secretive or paranoid; it's a genuine, reasoned judgment
that formal recognition invites exactly the resource competition and outside scrutiny the network exists
to route around. Character B's push isn't naive either — sustained volunteer coordination is real,
uncompensated labor, and formal support would lighten a burden nobody chose to carry indefinitely.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the informal
network's de facto coordinator — someone with no official title, just the person who ended up doing the
tracking and logistics because someone had to. Character B is plausibly an enthusiastic participant, maybe
someone newer to the network who doesn't yet feel the weight of what staying informal costs the person
actually running it, and who sees formalization as an obvious improvement rather than a risk.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The network stays informal — Character A's judgment holds, though not without acknowledging Character
B's point about the uncompensated labor involved. What changes instead is quieter: the coordination role
itself starts rotating between several residents rather than falling permanently on one person,
distributing the burden without formalizing the network into an institution.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because coordination rotates among several residents rather than sitting with one permanent figure or
one official institution, no single person or civic body ever becomes publicly identified as "running"
Davis's intellectual life — the network survives as a genuinely distributed, self-directed practice
rather than depending on any one individual's continued willingness to carry it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the network has no single point of failure and no formal institutional footprint, it survives
generational turnover far more reliably than a formal library dependent on continued civic funding would
— coordinators simply hand the rotation to whoever's next, and the practice outlasts any individual
resident's tenure, including its own founders'. Delete the 2nd-order change — imagine coordination had
stayed permanently with one person instead — and the network's survival would depend entirely on that
person's continued willingness and availability; its actual generational durability depends specifically
on the rotation this cycle produced.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Davis sustains a quiet, self-directed intellectual life that asks nothing from the wider city and needs
no outside recognition to keep functioning — a genuinely distributed, generationally durable practice,
private in character but never actually dependent on any one resident to survive, and echoing the same
unglamorous, self-sufficient instinct that defines everything else about Davis.
