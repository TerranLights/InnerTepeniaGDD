## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Naturalist Ratna Wirawan leads the team that works out Davis's sediment-core methodology, doing the bulk
of the foundational research herself — genuinely the driving intellectual force behind a discovery that
gives the young settlement its first real scientific identity.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The methodology's success creates an obvious, conventional next step: publish under Wirawan's own name,
the way individual scientific credit normally works, cementing her personal reputation as the
discovery's author. But doing so would mean overshadowing the whole team whose painstaking,
cross-referenced work (Suggestion #2) actually made the methodology possible — junior researchers,
technicians, and collaborators whose individual contributions were essential but far less visible than
the lead researcher's. Wirawan has to decide, and the decision isn't automatic: sole authorship is
easier, expected, and would do more for her own individual standing; collective credit is unusual, would
cost her personally, and isn't obviously owed to her under any normal scientific convention. Her own
instinct leans toward the group from the start — a relational, communally-minded upbringing where
leadership is built through trust rather than individual claim (per Davis's own Neo-Race Catalog entry
for Indonesia) makes solo credit feel less like an honor than a distortion of how the work actually
happened.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between Wirawan, leaning toward
insisting on collective credit, and a colleague or institutional figure — perhaps someone from outside
Davis, evaluating the work for wider Federation recognition — who pushes back, arguing that diffusing
credit across an entire team makes the achievement harder to recognize, publicize, and reward properly,
and that Wirawan is doing her own real contribution a disservice. This isn't generosity versus greed;
Character B's institutional logic (clear individual credit drives recognition, funding, and career
advancement) is genuinely how scientific reputation normally works, and Wirawan has to argue against a
system that isn't wrong so much as simply built around a different value than the one she wants to live
out.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Ratna Wirawan
herself. Character B is plausibly an outside evaluator or Federation science-recognition figure — someone
whose job is literally to determine how credit and recognition get formally assigned, applying a
convention that wasn't built with Davis's own collaborative methodology in mind.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Wirawan holds her position — she turns down the opportunity to claim sole authorship even though doing
so would have been easy and expected, insisting instead on crediting the group collectively for the
methodology's development.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because credit goes to the group rather than to Wirawan individually, her own specific contribution stays
less visible in the formal record than it could have been — but the collective-credit precedent itself
becomes something Davis's wider research institutions adopt going forward, formally structuring later
scientific achievements around team credit by default rather than individual authorship.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because collective credit becomes Davis's institutional default rather than a one-time personal choice,
the city's research culture develops a genuine structural advantage in retaining collaborative talent —
researchers drawn to methodologies (like the lake-sediment cross-referencing itself) that require
sustained teamwork are less likely to leave for institutions elsewhere that reward only the most visible
individual name, since Davis's own system already values what they're actually good at. Delete the
2nd-order change — imagine Wirawan's choice had stayed a one-time personal gesture rather than becoming
institutional practice — and there's no mechanism giving Davis a lasting edge in retaining collaborative
researchers; that retention advantage depends specifically on the precedent having been formalized.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Wirawan's decision becomes, quietly, a defining local story — proof that Davis's whole "enabler" ethos
(Suggestion #1) isn't just inherited from a namesake, but something its own people actively choose to
live out, now structurally built into how the city's research institutions credit their own work.
