## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Davis's whole civic and economic life already runs on one specific, trained skill: patient, careful
visual observation — reading a lake's ice, distinguishing one terrain feature from another, noticing
exactly what's changed in a growing crop or a monitored shoreline since last season. Looking closely is
simply what work looks like here.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A researcher or grower, deep into a routine survey, starts sketching what they're observing beyond what
the formal record actually requires — not a data diagram, an actual likeness, rendered because the
scene is worth looking at on its own terms, not only because it needs documenting. That impulse doesn't
compete with the working economy for time the way an unrelated hobby would; it happens *inside* the
observational work itself, on the same hours already budgeted for fieldwork. But it raises a real,
quieter question: whether spending part of a formal survey's attention on aesthetic rendering, rather
than strictly diagnostic recording, is a legitimate use of research time or a private indulgence riding
on the settlement's clock.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** not time-versus-labor (the old shape) but

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A clean separation, not a ban: formal survey records stay strictly diagnostic, but researchers and
growers are explicitly permitted — even quietly encouraged — to keep a second, personal set of sketches
alongside the official ones, on their own time within the same fieldwork session, clearly distinguished
from the formal record.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the two practices stay formally separate but happen side by side, a genuine naturalist
visual-arts tradition takes root without ever threatening the research program's own rigor — the same
people who sketch a lake's shoreline for a sediment-core survey sketch it again immediately afterward,
for no reason except that they're already looking that closely and it would feel strange to stop.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the personal sketches ride along on the same fieldwork hours as the formal survey work rather
than requiring separate dedicated time, the tradition survives and spreads without ever needing its own
institutional support — no studio, no formal teaching structure, no allocation the settlement has to
justify — passed on the same way the research methodology itself is, one worker showing another what
they've noticed. Delete the 2nd-order change — imagine the two practices had instead been kept fully
separate, personal sketching confined strictly to off-hours unconnected to fieldwork — and the tradition
would compete directly with rest and leisure time the way an ordinary hobby does, with no structural
reason to spread as reliably as it does when it rides along on work already being done.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Davis holds a genuine, quietly valued visual-arts tradition — proof that the city's quiet competence was
never purely functional to begin with. Looking carefully is something its people do for its own sake as
much as for the archive, and the line between the two was never as sharp as the settlement's early
research supervisors worried it might be.
