## 1. The Man Who Enabled Others

The earliest exile community inherits a station named for a mariner who made other men's expeditions
possible and never sought credit for his own. **But** a founding population could easily have chosen
a more celebratory civic myth to build around instead. **Therefore** Founding Administrator Wren
Kalloway deliberately models the young settlement's self-image on John King Davis's own unglamorous
competence, rather than reaching for anything grander. **But** that choice means Davis will never
market itself the way flashier founding stories let other cities do. **Therefore** "enabler, not
protagonist" becomes Davis's genuine civic character from its earliest institutional decisions
onward, not a trait that developed by accident later.

## 2. What the Lakes Remember

Davis's inherited terrain includes freshwater lakes, hypersaline basins, and landlocked marine pools,
each freezing and thawing on its own separate schedule. **But** none of that variety means much to
science without someone actually working out how to read it. **Therefore** researchers develop a
genuinely distinct methodology — paleoclimate reconstruction from layered lake sediment cores,
different bodies of water each preserving an independent record of the same shared history. **But**
cross-referencing multiple separate sediment records against each other is slower and more painstaking
than working from a single archive. **Therefore** Davis's research culture earns a real, specific
scientific identity of its own — water as archival medium, distinct from the ice-core science
practiced elsewhere in Tepenia.

## 3. Credit Declined

Naturalist Ratna Wirawan leads the team that works out the sediment-core methodology, doing the bulk
of the foundational research herself. **But** publishing under her own name alone would mean
overshadowing the whole team that helped build the method with her. **Therefore** she insists on
crediting the group collectively, turning down opportunities to claim sole authorship even when doing
so would have been easy and expected. **But** that choice means her own individual contribution stays
less visible than it could have been. **Therefore** her decision becomes, quietly, a defining local
story — proof that Davis's whole "enabler" ethos isn't just inherited from a namesake, but something
its own people actively choose to live out.

## 4. Ice on Different Clocks

Prydz Bay's main harbor handles Davis's primary shipping traffic, but freezes over fully during the
harshest stretch of winter like any other Tepenian port. **But** the city's own scattered fjord
inlets, shaped by the same varied terrain that defines everything else about Davis, don't all freeze
and thaw on the same schedule as the main bay. **Therefore** sailors and dispatchers work out, over
years of trial, which inlets stay navigable even when the harbor itself ices over — a genuine
logistical advantage tied directly to the terrain diversity everyone already takes for granted.
**But** exploiting that advantage means learning a genuinely complicated, ever-shifting map of ice
conditions. **Therefore** reading the fjords becomes one of Davis's own specialized local skills,
quietly essential and almost entirely invisible to anyone who's never had to rely on it.

## 5. Paint What You See

Davis's landscape — rock, lakes, fjords, ice sheet, all inside one city's footprint — offers a range of
terrain no other Tepenian city has to account for in the same space. **But** a working city organized
around quarries and fabrication shops has little obvious reason to prioritize documenting its own
scenery. **Therefore** a naturalist visual-arts tradition takes root anyway, residents sketching and
painting the Vestfold Hills' genuinely remarkable variety simply because it's there and worth looking
at closely. **But** a tradition like that needs real, sustained interest to survive generations of a
population more focused on industrial output. **Therefore** it persists as a modest, genuine minority
practice — never Davis's main identity, but never quite disappearing either.

## 6. Pages From Elsewhere

Davis's population wants more cultural and intellectual life than its own remote, working-city
character naturally produces. **But** building elaborate cultural institutions competes directly with
resources the mining and fabrication economy actually needs. **Therefore** residents turn to something
quieter and cheaper instead — a genuine, if informal, network for acquiring and circulating books from
Upper Earth, read privately rather than performed publicly. **But** sustaining that kind of network
takes real, ongoing coordination between people who'd rather not draw much attention to it.
**Therefore** Davis develops a quiet, self-directed intellectual life that asks nothing from the wider
city and needs no outside recognition to keep functioning.

## 7. Building With the Land, Not Against It

Most Tepenian cities engineer their architecture primarily to defend against wind. **But** Davis's own
terrain offers something most other cities don't have — genuine variety worth working with rather than
simply surviving. **Therefore** early planners site buildings around lake access and fjord inlets
deliberately, letting the terrain's own natural features shape the city's layout instead of building
uniform wind-defense everywhere. **But** that approach means less standardized construction and more
site-specific engineering than a simpler, wind-first design would require. **Therefore** Davis ends up
with a genuinely varied, terrain-responsive built environment — proof that the city's relationship
with its own remarkable landscape was collaborative from the very start, not adversarial.

## 8. The Wish Nobody Says Out Loud

Davis's dominant civic ethos genuinely doesn't seek outside attention, content with quiet competence
over recognition. **But** a minority of residents privately notice just how objectively unusual the
Vestfold Hills' terrain actually is by any Tepenian standard. **Therefore** a quiet, rarely-voiced wish
takes hold among that minority — not for fame, just for simple acknowledgment that what Davis has here
is genuinely remarkable. **But** saying so openly would cut against the city's whole dominant
character. **Therefore** the wish stays mostly private, surfacing only occasionally, in conversations
between residents who suspect they're not the only ones who feel it but rarely confirm it with each
other directly.

## 9. Thirteen Flags, No Winner

Later immigration waves bring thirteen distinct nations to Notable tier at Davis, longer than any other
Mirny subnet city's own minority-heritage list. **But** with no single non-Primary community carrying
outsized weight, none of them can simply claim a defined cultural niche within the city the way a
larger single minority might elsewhere. **Therefore** minority-heritage life at Davis stays genuinely
dispersed — small threads of many traditions woven through daily life rather than concentrated into
one visible "guest culture" the way it plays out at Zhongshan or Janbogo. **But** that dispersal means
no single minority tradition ever gets the critical mass to become a defining civic landmark on its
own. **Therefore** Davis's cultural texture stays quietly, permanently plural — present everywhere in
small pieces, dominant nowhere.

## 10. Quarries Before Questions

Davis's founding population arrives with a genuine research mission, inheriting decades of AAD
environmental study into the Vestfold Hills. **But** a growing exile population needs housing,
materials, and infrastructure the research mission alone was never built to provide. **Therefore**
mining and fabrication grow steadily alongside the research work, drawing more and more of the daily
workforce as the practical needs of an actual city outpace the founding mission's own scope. **But**
that growth means the research heritage risks becoming a smaller and smaller share of what Davis
actually does day to day. **Therefore** the founding scientific mission survives as a genuine, still-
respected minority tradition — smaller than it once was, but never abandoned, sitting alongside the
industrial majority rather than being replaced by it.

## 11. Food for Stone

*(Grounded in the corrected 2026-07-16 breadbasket/research identity — Davis's own agricultural surplus,
not the superseded mining/fabrication framing above, which remains flagged for developer decision.)*

Mirny's industrial and quarrying operation grows fast enough along Hwy 110 that informal, ad-hoc food
arrangements genuinely stop being adequate for its expanding workforce, at the same moment Davis's own
breadbasket surplus has nowhere established to go. **But** Davis's own enabler instinct risks letting
that surplus go quietly undervalued rather than genuinely, fairly traded. **Therefore** the two cities
negotiate a real, formal exchange — Davis's agricultural surplus for Mirny's materials and construction
goods. **But** a lopsided or informal arrangement would only repeat the same undervaluation Davis's own
civic character is prone to. **Therefore** it gets codified as a real, ongoing relationship instead, food
for stone flowing steadily along the same corridor connecting both cities.
