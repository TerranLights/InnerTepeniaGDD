## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Shirayuki's massive amateur music scene draws on softer, melodic Japanese folk instrumentation from its
founding population — a real, living tradition carried forward by generations of exiles and their
descendants.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The Larsemann Hills cluster's own broader multinational influences — Korean, Chinese, Russian,
Australian — surround the city on every side, impossible to ignore entirely, carried in by trade, by
cluster-wide social contact, and by the ordinary immigration that brings new residents into Shirayuki
itself. A genuine musical fusion starts emerging, blending the founding folk tradition with the subnet's
wider influences into something new. But a fusion this eclectic risks losing any distinct identity of its
own — the earliest attempts at blending traditions produce work that sounds, to some listeners, like an
unfocused mixture rather than a coherent new sound, and a real disagreement breaks out among the city's
own amateur musicians about whether the fusion is actually working or simply diluting what made the
founding tradition distinctive in the first place.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a musician committed to
the founding Japanese folk tradition in something closer to its original form, worried that blending it
too freely with outside influences will produce something unrecognizable, and a musician actively pushing
the fusion forward, convinced that a living tradition has to actually absorb its surroundings to stay
alive rather than calcifying into a museum piece. Neither side is simply right — A's worry about losing
something real is legitimate, and B's instinct that traditions which refuse to change eventually stop
being lived and start being merely preserved is equally legitimate.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an older or more
traditionally-trained musician, personally connected to the founding folk instrumentation and protective
of its specific character. Character B is plausibly a younger musician, likely someone who grew up
already surrounded by the cluster's wider multinational influences and never experienced the folk
tradition as something separate from them in the first place, for whom the fusion feels less like a
departure than simply how the music already sounds to their own ear.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The fusion keeps developing, but the disagreement itself sharpens it — B's work improves by actually
engaging with A's specific criticisms about what the earliest blended pieces lost, rather than dismissing
them. It doesn't lose its own identity — Glacier-pop becomes recognizably Shirayuki's own sound precisely
because of how naturally it blends, never quite reducible to any one of its component influences.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Glacier-pop's mature form was shaped directly by a traditionalist's own criticism rather than
developing in isolation from it, the genre ends up with an unusually explicit, articulable relationship
to its own component parts — musicians within the scene can describe, specifically, which elements come
from which tradition and why they're combined the way they are, rather than the fusion simply happening
and never being examined.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That articulable, self-aware relationship to its own influences makes Glacier-pop unusually well-suited
to being taught — new musicians arriving at Shirayuki's schools, or simply picking up an instrument for
the first time, inherit not just a sound but an actual explained tradition of *why* it sounds the way it
does, accelerating how quickly newcomers can meaningfully contribute to the genre rather than merely
imitate it.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Glacier-pop stands as Shirayuki's own defining musical fusion — not an unexamined blend but a genre with
an articulated, teachable relationship to its own founding and adopted influences, passed on to new
musicians with real explanatory depth rather than left for each new generation to rediscover blindly on
its own.
