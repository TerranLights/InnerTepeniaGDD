## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Shirayuki, Zhongshan, and Sinheung arrive at adjacent sites through related but distinct mechanisms —
Zhongshan's organic proximity confirmed by the same Jeju-do court, Shirayuki's and Sinheung's shared
diplomatic engineering, allocated to Japan and Korea respectively.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Sharing a diplomatic history doesn't automatically make three separate populations feel like neighbors
in daily practice — the relationship between them starts genuinely more formal and distant than their
eventual closeness, cordial contact between three communities who technically arrived together but
haven't yet actually lived beside each other. Proximity this close, sustained over generations, is hard
to keep purely formal forever — but nothing about formal cordiality automatically produces genuine
closeness either, and at some point the three communities' otherwise polite, arm's-length coexistence has
to actually be tested by some shared, practical problem none of them can solve entirely alone, forcing
real cooperation rather than merely diplomatic courtesy.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** less a conflict between two individuals than a
confrontation between two civic postures — a representative from one of the three cities who wants to
keep the relationship formally correct and administratively separate, since that's how the Jeju-do
allocation itself was structured, and a representative from another who pushes for genuinely shared,
informal cooperation on some practical shared problem (a resource shortfall, an infrastructure need, a
logistics bottleneck none of the three can solve using only their own city's resources). A's caution is
reasonable — the cluster's three populations have real, distinct identities worth protecting, not
blending prematurely; B's push is equally reasonable — solving a genuinely shared problem separately,
three times over, wastes effort all three cities can't really spare.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early civic
administrator from one of the three cities — quite plausibly Zhongshan, given its own established
non-prying, formally distant civic instinct — cautious about blurring boundaries between communities that
arrived at their positions through genuinely different processes. Character B is plausibly an
administrator or tradesperson from either Shirayuki or Sinheung, more comfortable with informal,
practical cooperation and less invested in preserving formal separateness for its own sake.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's practical case wins out on the specific shared problem at hand — the three cities cooperate directly
and informally to solve it, the first real instance of genuine, non-ceremonial cooperation between them.
The three communities warm gradually into the closely-entangled cluster identity later generations simply
take for granted — legal separation slowly giving way to genuine, lived closeness.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the first real cooperation succeeded on a specific, practical problem rather than through any
grand diplomatic gesture, the three cities develop an actual habit of informal, ad hoc joint
problem-solving whenever a similar shared issue arises later — no formal cluster government, but a real,
recurring pattern of the three administrations simply reaching out to each other directly when a problem
crosses city lines.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That recurring pattern of informal joint problem-solving, repeated often enough across generations,
becomes the actual mechanism — not any single treaty or formal unification decree — by which the three
cities' populations end up sharing infrastructure, culture, and eventually the tri-junction cluster
identity later generations take for granted. The formal, legal amalgamation that eventually follows
simply catches up to, and ratifies, a working relationship that had already been real for a long time.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Shirayuki, Zhongshan, and Sinheung now cooperate informally as a matter of ordinary practice whenever a
shared problem arises — a working closeness built out of repeated practical necessity rather than
top-down decree, the genuine foundation the cluster's later, formal unification will eventually put a
name to.
