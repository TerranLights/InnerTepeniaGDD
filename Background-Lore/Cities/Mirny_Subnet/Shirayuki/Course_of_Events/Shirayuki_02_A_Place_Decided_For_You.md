## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Japanese exiles arrive at the newly-allocated site to find a claim already secured, a decision already
made entirely without their input — the site, the name for it, the whole diplomatic arrangement settled
by people they've never met, years before they set foot on it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Inheriting a diplomatically-settled destination is a strange way to begin a new life — chosen for you,
not by you, with none of the ordinary sense of agency that comes from actually picking a place or fighting
for it. The earliest years carry a genuine, if quiet, adjustment period: residents settling into
inherited research infrastructure and a claim negotiated by people who never had to live with the
consequences themselves. But that quiet adjustment period doesn't resolve on its own — at some point, the
gap between "this was decided for us" and "this is actually our home" has to be closed by somebody
actually doing something with the place, rather than simply enduring it, and not every early resident
agrees on what that something should be.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between an early resident who wants
to preserve the inherited Bharati Station infrastructure largely as it was received — a kind of respect
for what already exists, and a wariness of overreaching into a place that was never really chosen —
and another early resident who wants to start actively remaking the physical space into something that
reflects the population actually living there now. This is a conflict about what "making a place your
own" actually requires: careful stewardship of what you inherited, or active transformation of it, and
reasonable people who've all lived through the same diplomatically-imposed founding can land on either
side.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early resident with
some background in the original research mission the station was built for — someone who feels a kind of
professional respect for the inherited labs and equipment, wary of altering something functional simply
out of restlessness. Character B is plausibly a resident with no particular connection to the original
research purpose — perhaps someone more artistically or socially inclined — who experiences the unaltered
infrastructure as a constant, literal reminder that the whole place was designed by and for someone else's
purpose, not theirs.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither position fully wins — the two residents' disagreement settles into an actual working practice:
alteration proceeds, but gradually and respectfully, adapting rather than replacing the inherited
buildings piece by piece as genuine need arises, rather than through any single sweeping transformation.
Living somewhere long enough changes your relationship to how you got there — what starts as an imposed
circumstance gradually becomes something residents actually inhabit on their own terms, the first step
in the city's whole long arc from decided-for to chosen.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the alteration happened gradually and piece by piece rather than all at once, no single moment
or generation gets to claim credit for "transforming" the city — the process becomes distributed across
decades, with each small adaptation building on the last, so that no resident can point to one clean
before-and-after and say the city changed *there*.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the transformation has no single defining moment, Shirayuki's own civic memory of its founding
comes to emphasize *cumulative* effort over any singular founding hero or turning point — a city that
understands its own identity as something built continuously, by generations of small individual choices,
rather than through one decisive founding act. This becomes a genuine point of local self-understanding
distinct from cities elsewhere in Tepenia that credit a single founding figure or moment.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Shirayuki now understands its own identity as something built cumulatively — an inherited research
station gradually, respectfully reshaped across generations into something its population actually
chose, with no single defining moment anyone can point to as the turning point, only a long, patient
accumulation of small decisions that eventually added up to a genuinely chosen home.
