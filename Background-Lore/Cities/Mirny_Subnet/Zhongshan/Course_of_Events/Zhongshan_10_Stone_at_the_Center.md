## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early construction at Zhongshan organizes itself around a genuine exposed bedrock outcrop, too solid and
too prominent to simply build over — a practical, physical fact of the site's own geography, nothing yet
attached to it beyond that.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A landmark that just sits there, unused, is a wasted piece of the city's own geography — residents start
gathering near it informally, at first for no particular reason beyond its convenient, central location.
But informal gathering spots either take on real meaning over time or quietly fade from use — and at some
point, as the surrounding city keeps growing and available central space becomes genuinely more valuable,
a civic planner or developer has to actually decide whether the open ground around the outcrop should stay
informally available, or be formally allocated to some more deliberate structural purpose the growing
city could plausibly use instead.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a civic planner responsible
for making efficient use of increasingly valuable central land, proposing to formally develop the space
around the outcrop for some concrete structural purpose, and a resident or informal community figure who
has watched the space's own organic gathering use grow over years and argues that its value as open,
unstructured common ground already outweighs whatever a formal development could add. A's position is
responsible urban planning under real space pressure; B's position defends something genuinely valuable
that doesn't show up on any planning document — the accumulated, informal meaning the space has already
taken on simply by staying open.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civic planner or
developer, under genuine pressure to use central land efficiently as the city grows. Character B is
plausibly a longtime resident or informal community organizer, someone who has personally witnessed the
space's gradual transformation from empty ground into something people actually value gathering at,
arguing for its preservation on grounds a formal planning process doesn't naturally account for.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's argument prevails — the space around the outcrop stays formally undeveloped, deliberately preserved as
open ground precisely because of the informal use it's already accumulated. The Standing Stone becomes
something closer to genuine civic ground — no formal designation, no official purpose, just generations of
residents who've kept meeting there long enough that its significance no longer needs explaining to anyone
who already lives in the city.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the space was deliberately preserved rather than developed, Zhongshan's civic planning process
establishes an actual, if informal, precedent: proposed development of any long-established informal
gathering space now has to reckon with the same argument that saved the ground around the Standing
Stone, rather than treating undeveloped central land as automatically available for the next planning
proposal.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that precedent exists, other informal gathering spaces that develop organically across Zhongshan
in later generations — smaller, less prominent than the Standing Stone, but genuinely valued by the
residents who use them — have a real, citable argument available to protect them too, whenever future
development pressure eventually reaches them, all tracing back to the same original argument first made
on the Standing Stone's own behalf.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Standing Stone remains Zhongshan's open, informally significant civic ground — and the precedent set
in preserving it now quietly protects other, smaller gathering spaces across the city as well, a single
planning argument that outgrew the one landmark it was originally made for.
