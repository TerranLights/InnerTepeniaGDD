## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Sinian defectors choose exile alongside robots over continued loyalty to their own nation's ideology — a
population defined, at its very founding, by an act of political rupture from the government that once
claimed them.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That choice raises an immediate question of identity — do they abandon everything associated with the
nation they're breaking from, or hold onto what they still value while rejecting what drove them out? The
question isn't abstract for long: within the founding population itself, real disagreement breaks out
almost immediately over how completely to sever from Sinian identity — some want a clean break, discarding
language, custom, and cultural marker alike as tainted by association with the regime; others insist that
what's actually being rejected is the ideology specifically, not the whole of what it means to be
Chinese. Founding Elder Mèi Sun has to actually articulate a position the fractured founding
community can rally around, rather than letting the disagreement calcify into a permanent internal
rift before the settlement has even properly begun.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation within the founding community itself,
between someone advocating for a complete break from Sinian identity — language, custom, everything —
as the only way to be certain the regime's ideology has been fully rejected, and Founding Elder Mèi
Sun, arguing for a more precise distinction between the nation's culture and its governing
ideology. This shouldn't be framed as one side being more "authentically" rejecting the regime than the
other — the clean-break advocate's position comes from real, understandable fear of anything that could
be mistaken for lingering loyalty, while Mèi's position requires the harder, more nuanced work of drawing
a line under real scrutiny from people who have every reason to be suspicious of half-measures.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a fellow founding-era
defector, someone whose own break from the Sinian Federation was personally costly enough that they want
absolutely no ambiguity left in how completely the new settlement rejects everything associated with it.
Character B is, per established canon, Founding Elder Mèi Sun, whose own articulated position
becomes the settlement's lasting political creed.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Mèi's distinction prevails and gets formally, publicly stated: they kept the name, the language, the
culture — they did not keep the regime. But drawing that line clearly means constantly explaining the
distinction to anyone who assumes rejecting the nation means rejecting everything Chinese about
themselves. "We kept the name; we did not keep them" becomes Zhongshan's founding political creed,
repeated and understood by every generation that follows.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the creed had to be actively, repeatedly explained rather than simply assumed, Zhongshan's
founding generation develops an explicit, rehearsed way of articulating the city's own political stance
to outsiders — a settled, ready answer rather than an improvised one whenever a visitor or new arrival
raises the obvious question of how a Chinese-founded exile city relates to the nation it broke from.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that rehearsed articulation exists and gets repeated across generations, it hardens, over time,
into something closer to a formal civic teaching than a defensive explanation — passed to children not
merely as a fact about the city's founding, but as an actual framework for how Zhongshan's later
generations think about cultural inheritance more broadly: keep what's genuinely valuable, discard what
was only ever imposed.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Zhongshan's whole civic identity now runs on a formally articulated, generationally taught political
creed — a founding-era disagreement resolved into stated principle, still recited and understood
centuries later as the city's own answer to a question every subsequent generation eventually asks.
