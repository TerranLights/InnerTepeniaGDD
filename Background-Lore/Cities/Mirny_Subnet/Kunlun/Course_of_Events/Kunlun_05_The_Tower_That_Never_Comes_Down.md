## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Kunlun's earliest comms infrastructure starts as purely functional — an antenna, satellite dishes,
nothing built for show, sized and placed for signal strength and sightline alone.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The structure ends up towering over everything else in the city regardless, visible from any approach
long before anything else comes into view — a simple consequence of what a working antenna at this
elevation actually has to be, not a deliberate design choice. It becomes, without anyone setting out to
build one, the closest thing Kunlun has to a civic monument — never intended to represent the city,
simply doing so by being the first and largest thing anyone sees on the approach. But treating pure
infrastructure as a symbol changes how residents relate to it, whether the engineers who maintain it
want that or not — people start attaching meaning to a structure whose only actual job is carrying a
signal, and eventually someone has to decide whether the antenna's own maintenance and future expansion
should account for that meaning at all, or stay purely engineering-driven the way it always has.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation over a planned upgrade or expansion
to the antenna array — one side wants the redesign driven purely by signal performance, whatever shape
that produces, even if it changes or diminishes the structure's now-familiar silhouette; the other wants
the civic silhouette itself preserved, treating the antenna's accidental symbolism as something now worth
protecting on its own terms. This is a conflict between two entirely legitimate readings of the same
object — one insisting the antenna was never supposed to mean anything and shouldn't now be held hostage
to sentiment it never asked for, the other insisting that once something becomes what an entire population
orients around, pretending it's still "just infrastructure" is its own kind of dishonesty.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a comms engineer
tasked with the technical expansion, frustrated at being asked to design around symbolism instead of
signal strength alone. Character B is plausibly a longer-tenured resident, possibly one of the Ice Cold
Buddhist devotees who has already folded the antenna's silhouette into observance in some quiet way, or
simply someone who arrived as a child and has never known Kunlun without that exact skyline — someone
for whom the antenna's shape isn't decoration, it's orientation, literally and psychologically.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A compromise resolves it, but a real one, not a vague gesture: the expansion proceeds, and the new
array is functionally superior, but its designers deliberately preserve the original structure's
silhouette as seen from the standard approach routes, engineering the added capacity into elements that
don't disrupt the skyline residents have spent generations orienting around.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Preserving the silhouette as a design constraint, rather than an afterthought, means Kunlun's engineers
now have a standing precedent: future expansions to the array are evaluated not just on signal
performance, but on their visual impact from the approach routes — an entirely new criterion, applied
nowhere else in the observatory's operations, that exists solely because of how this one dispute
resolved.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because visual-impact review is now a standing part of any major infrastructure change, Kunlun's engineers
start informally consulting residents — including devotees, who were never previously part of any
technical planning process — before finalizing large projects, since the residents are the ones whose
orientation and sense of place the review exists to protect. A purely engineering decision-making
process has, almost incidentally, gained its first genuine channel for lay resident input.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Kunlun's comms infrastructure keeps growing exactly as its mission requires, but every major expansion
now runs through both a technical review and an informal resident consultation the city never used to
have — proof that pure infrastructure, once it becomes a civic monument by accident, doesn't fully
return to being "just infrastructure" again, no matter how functional its original purpose remains.
