## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The rediscovered dome has no visible peak, no ridge, no silhouette to mark its own highest point the
way a mountain would — just an enormous, flat, featureless white plain that never seems to change
however far a pilgrim walks across it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The faith's whole still-forming theology treats elevation as spiritually significant, and a sacred
site needs an actual, findable location, not a vague description of "somewhere out there." Generations
of devotees undertake careful, patient surveying to determine the literal geometric high point of the
dome — far too subtle to identify by eye alone, requiring instruments, records, and real accumulated
labor across multiple pilgrim cohorts before anyone can say with confidence "here, exactly here." But
finding it turns out to be only half the problem: a summit with nothing to look at, once you actually
stand on it, risks feeling like an anticlimax rather than an arrival.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement, once the exact point is finally
located, over what — if anything — should be built there. One side wants something substantial enough
to give pilgrims a genuine sense of arrival after a grueling crossing; the other insists that building
anything impressive at all would betray the minimalism the faith is supposedly organized around. This
plays best as a real values conflict rather than a taste dispute: A is worried about the faith's own
survival (a destination that disappoints newcomers won't hold a growing community together), while B
is worried about the faith's own integrity (a monument built to impress is a monument built for the
wrong reason).

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a devotee with a
background suited to building and planning — someone practical, worried on the community's behalf
about what a first-time pilgrim actually experiences on arrival. Character B is plausibly one of the
faith's more doctrinally rigorous early devotees, the kind of figure who takes the theology's own
minimalist logic seriously enough to argue against comfort, spectacle, or reassurance when the theology
itself doesn't call for any of it.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side gets exactly what they asked for, and the compromise becomes the answer: Nyakkyo gets
built at the surveyed point deliberately unremarkable — small, low, empty — turning the absence of a
view into the entire theological point rather than a disappointment that needs explaining away. A gets
a built destination; B gets a destination that refuses to be impressive.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Nyakkyo's specific, deliberate blankness sets the template for everything built at Dome Fuji
afterward — the Robing Hall, the Waystones themselves, every later structure the community adds.
"Unremarkable by design" stops being one building's character and becomes the site's entire
architectural doctrine.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because Dome Fuji's whole built environment commits this thoroughly to visual unremarkableness, the
site develops a specific, self-reinforcing reputation among outsiders who eventually hear about it:
that Dome Fuji "has nothing to see." Rather than discouraging interest, this reputation deepens the
site's mystique — curious pilgrims and outside visitors alike are drawn precisely by the rumor that
there's supposedly nothing there to find, an absence of spectacle that becomes its own kind of draw.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Dome Fuji's entire built environment — Nyakkyo, the Robing Hall, the Waystones — now shares one
deliberate architectural language of restraint, and the site's outside reputation as a place with
"nothing to see" has become, paradoxically, one more reason people choose to come see it.
