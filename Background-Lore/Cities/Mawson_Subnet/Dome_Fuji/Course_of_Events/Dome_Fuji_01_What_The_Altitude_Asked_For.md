## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Dome Fuji Station's founding population arrives selected, trained, and psychologically prepared for
isolation and hardship in the abstract — but not for the specific toll of 3,810 meters, an effective
physiological altitude closer to 4,500. Nobody signed on expecting the mountain sickness to be this
particular, or this permanent.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Chronic mountain sickness and impaired cognitive and physical capacity turn out not to be occasional
setbacks the way orientation briefings implied — they're the baseline condition of staying at Dome
Fuji at all, present in some degree in every researcher who stays longer than a rotation or two. The
station has no choice but to build an entire adaptive culture around managing it: pacing, medication
schedules, deliberate acclimatization protocols, and, hardest of all, the frank acceptance of
permanent partial impairment as simply the cost of doing the work here. But managing a condition
nobody ever actually solves means every incoming cohort inherits the identical unsolved problem their
predecessors had, rather than a fix those predecessors worked toward and passed down.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement between someone who wants to keep
chasing an actual medical or technological fix for the altitude toll, and someone who believes the
station's finite time, staff, and research budget are better spent perfecting management rather than
gambling on a cure that may not exist. This shouldn't play as reckless-idealist versus
defeatist-bureaucrat — A's instinct is the same one that justifies the station's entire research
mission (you don't stop looking for answers just because they're hard), and B's caution is grounded in
real losses: speculative interventions at this altitude carry real risk to already-compromised bodies,
and every hour spent chasing a fix is an hour not spent keeping the current cohort functional. Both are
trying to protect the same people; they disagree about which risk is worse.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a newer arrival,
still early enough in their own altitude exposure to believe the problem is solvable if approached
correctly — a physician or researcher whose whole professional identity runs on fixing things.
Character B is plausibly a longer-tenured station figure, already visibly managing their own chronic
impairment, who has watched previous "promising" interventions fail or backfire and has made a
personal, hard-won peace with endurance as the only honest goal available. The generational gap
between them matters more than any personal animosity.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No cure arrives, and none ever will — B's position wins out not by argument but by attrition, the way
it always does at Dome Fuji. Dome Fuji Station's earliest civic identity forms entirely around
endurance without victory: competence here is measured by how well a person manages the altitude,
never by defeating it, and that standard settles into the station's culture as simple, unglamorous
fact rather than a concession anyone has to keep re-litigating.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because competence is now measured by managed endurance rather than research output or seniority,
informal standing within the station starts accruing to whoever demonstrates the steadiest, most
self-aware handling of their own condition — and, just as importantly, whoever helps newer arrivals
learn to do the same. Leadership, in practice if not on any official chart, starts quietly shifting
toward the best-adapted rather than the highest-ranked.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That informal, endurance-based status hierarchy runs alongside the station's official Federation
chain of command rather than replacing it — creating two genuinely separate systems of authority at
Dome Fuji, one on paper and one in practice, that mostly overlap but occasionally don't. A newly
assigned station chief with impeccable credentials and no real acclimatization to show for it finds
their formal authority quietly, politely outpaced by whichever long-tenured researcher the community
has already learned to actually defer to.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Dome Fuji now runs on two coexisting hierarchies — an official one assigned from outside, and a real
one earned from inside, built entirely on demonstrated endurance. The second one isn't written down
anywhere, isn't official, and outlasts every credential the first one hands out.
