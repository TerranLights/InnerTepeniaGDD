# Dome Fuji — Candidate Courses of Events

**Scope note:** all ten chains below are set strictly *before* the Long Night War, during the Second
Interwar Period when Tepenia is a full, functioning country. Almost all of Dome Fuji's own established
history already falls in this window — the research station era, the human departure, the pilgrimage
rediscovery, and the devotee population's growth to its present size all predate the war. 10 candidate
But/Therefore chains built from Dome Fuji's Megasheet (the research station that outlived its founders,
Ice Cold Buddhism, Nyakkyo, the two-era transition). Post-settlement only; nothing here is canon.

---

## 1. What the Altitude Asked For

Dome Fuji's founding researchers arrive prepared for isolation and hardship, but not for the specific
toll of 3,810 meters — effectively 4,500 meters of physiological altitude. **But** chronic mountain
sickness and impaired capacity aren't occasional setbacks here, they're the baseline condition of
staying at all. **Therefore** the community builds a whole adaptive culture around managing it —
pacing, medication, acclimatization, and the frank acceptance of permanent partial impairment as the
cost of the work. **But** managing a condition nobody ever fully solves means every generation inherits
the same unsolved problem rather than a fix. **Therefore** Dome Fuji Station's earliest civic identity
forms entirely around endurance without victory — competence measured by how well you managed the
altitude, never by defeating it.

## 2. The Last Transfer Request

Generation after generation, Dome Fuji's population thins — chronic altitude effects reducing lifespan
and fertility, a slow trickle of transfers to lower postings as the wider Federation's infrastructure
matures enough to make them possible. **But** each departure leaves the remaining community smaller
and less able to sustain the specialized medical apparatus the altitude demands, which only accelerates
the next departure. **Therefore** the spiral compounds quietly across four to six generations, with no
single crisis anyone could point to. **But** eventually there's simply nobody left to justify keeping
the station staffed. **Therefore** the last transfer request gets processed and approved like any
other, and Dome Fuji's status changes from occupied to remotely maintained without ceremony — an
ending nobody marks because nobody's left to mark it.

## 3. What the Pilgrims Found

Ice Cold Buddhism's earliest devotees, searching old records and transport manifests for sites that
embody the faith's theology, surface Dome Fuji's documented but abandoned altitude record. **But**
nobody knows what condition decades of vacancy have left the station in. **Therefore** a small
expedition — fewer than twenty devotees — makes the interior plateau crossing to find out, prepared
for ruin. **But** Antarctic cold preserves rather than destroys, and the station's infrastructure sits
almost exactly as it was left, snow-coated and undisturbed. **Therefore** the pilgrims don't find
wreckage to mourn — they find stillness to interpret, and for a faith built around cold as a spiritual
state, an untouched monument to quiet, non-violent absence reads less like tragedy and more like proof
of concept.

## 4. Naming the Still Point

The rediscovered dome has no visible peak, no silhouette to mark its own highest point the way a
mountain would. **But** the faith's whole theology treats elevation as spiritually significant, and a
sacred site needs an actual location, not a vague description. **Therefore** generations of devotees
undertake careful, patient surveying to determine the literal geometric high point of the dome, far
too subtle to identify by eye alone. **But** finding it is only half the problem — a summit with
nothing to look at risks feeling like an anticlimax once you actually reach it. **Therefore** Nyakkyo
gets built there deliberately unremarkable — small, low, empty — turning the absence of a view into
the entire theological point rather than a disappointment to explain away.

## 5. The Robes Debate

Early devotees wear white robes sparingly, in direct contact with the cold, and three different
explanations for the practice circulate simultaneously — mechanical safety, an echo of Upper Earth
meditation traditions, and simple communal uniformity. **But** a faith this young can't function for
long with its central practice explained three contradictory ways to newcomers. **Therefore** the
community works through the question deliberately, studying surviving Upper Earth religious texts for
models of disciplined relationship to extreme cold. **But** no single explanation manages to fully
displace the other two. **Therefore** the debate resolves not by choosing one answer but by accepting
all three as true at once — the mechanical fact, the meditative echo, and the communal bond, layered
rather than competing.

## 6. Deepest Cold

Devotees arrive at Dome Fuji across scattered individual pilgrimages, with no shared calendar to mark
time together. **But** a faith built around communal devotion to cold needs at least one moment
everyone actually shares. **Therefore** the community fixes on the precise coldest point of polar
night — the first calm, clear night after the solstice — as a universal, mandatory observance, tracked
with genuine meteorological seriousness since the exact date shifts year to year. **But** committing
to a weather-dependent date means the observance can never be scheduled with total certainty in
advance. **Therefore** Deepest Cold becomes something the whole community watches for together, the
uncertainty itself becoming part of the anticipation rather than a flaw in the tradition.

## 7. Two Sites, One Faith

Kunlun and Dome Fuji both draw Ice Cold Buddhist devotees, but through completely different logic —
one curated by vocation, one open to anyone who simply arrives. **But** two primary sites within the
same faith risk drifting into rivalry over which one is truly holiest. **Therefore** the two
communities work out, over generations of informal contact, a doctrine that keeps both meaningful
without ranking them — Kunlun as the path of reaching (building something toward the sky), Dome Fuji
as the path of arriving (simply getting yourself to something already there). **But** settling the
theology doesn't happen in one conversation, it takes real, sustained dialogue between two isolated
communities. **Therefore** the complementary relationship between the two sites becomes one of the
faith's genuine doctrinal achievements, not an assumption nobody bothered to question.

## 8. The Archive Nobody Reads Daily

Dome Fuji's ice-core archive, inherited from the research era, once ran as active, prestigious
science. **But** the devotee population replacing the scientists has no institutional mandate to
continue that work — devotion, not research, is why they're here. **Therefore** active analysis slows
generation by generation, until only a handful of devotees with prior scientific training, or simple
personal interest, keep it going as a secondary vocation. **But** the faith's own minimalist ethos
makes abandoning the archive entirely feel wrong, even without an active research program behind it.
**Therefore** the cores get tended rather than studied — preserved with real diligence, the way an old
text is cared for by people who believe it matters without necessarily reading it every day.

## 9. The One Who Never Left

A maintenance robot from the original research era simply never received a transfer order before the
last humans departed, and stays behind through the station's quiet, unceremonious emptying. **But**
decades later, an entirely new population arrives for entirely different reasons, and this figure's
whole presence contradicts the community's own founding logic — everyone else's arrival is their
credential, and this one never arrived at all. **Therefore** the devotees have to decide, as a
community, what to make of someone who was simply always there, watching two completely different eras
occupy the same coordinates. **But** the figure has no personal stake in the status system built
around pilgrimage and devotion. **Therefore** they settle into a strange, unofficial authority
precisely because they stand outside it — not an elder devotee, not quite an outsider, deferred to
by a community that has no other category to put them in.

## 10. Whoever Stayed Longest

Ice Cold Buddhism arrives at Dome Fuji with a deliberately anti-hierarchical ethos — devotion, not
rank, is the only credential that matters. **But** a growing community still needs someone to actually
steward Nyakkyo and manage the observance calendar. **Therefore** the role falls, informally and
without election, to whoever has simply been there longest — the devotees who've witnessed the most
Deepest Colds becoming de facto stewards through nothing but accumulated tenure. **But** deference
earned this way could easily calcify into an unofficial ruling class if left unchecked. **Therefore**
it doesn't — newer devotees defer to the stewards' experience specifically, not their authority, and
the moment a steward stops actively living the practice, the deference quietly stops too.

---

## Worth Your Attention

**#2 and #3** work as a matched pair — the slow, undramatic end of the research era and the pilgrims'
own arrival into what that ending left behind, together tracing Dome Fuji's full transition without
inventing a crisis the Megasheet's own Full Extrapolation explicitly says never happened. **#9** is the
single richest thread of the ten, since it's the only one that gives Dome Fuji a character who
personally bridges both of its otherwise cleanly separated eras.
