## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The Sayowa Junction's cooperative arrangement works in principle — Mawson, the Concordia route, and
Halley subnet interests each maintain what they use, nobody claiming the whole — but the actual
day-to-day coordination between them still has no one person or office actually running it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

As coordination needs grow more constant and technical — scheduling, resource allocation, settling
overlapping claims on the same infrastructure — it becomes clear that letting any single stakeholder
handle daily operations would simply recreate the control problem the cooperative arrangement was built
to avoid. Someone genuinely neutral has to do the job. The trouble is that "neutral" isn't a role any of
the current stakeholders can credibly claim to be.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over what should actually determine
who coordinates the junction — competence and resources, or neutrality and lack of self-interest. A's
case is genuinely practical: the largest, best-resourced stakeholder can plausibly do the job most

capable — recreates exactly the imbalance the cooperative arrangement exists to prevent.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a logistics figure
from one of the larger outside stakeholders (Mawson is the most natural fit, given its established
subnet-hub role) arguing their institution's resources make it the obvious choice to host the coordinator
function. Character B is plausibly a Sayowa-based figure — or a coalition voice for the smaller
stakeholders — arguing that Sayowa itself, having no comparable outside leverage to abuse, is the only
party every side could actually trust with the role.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The neutrality argument wins. The coordinator role goes to a figure embedded in Sayowa itself, answering
formally to no single stakeholder — not because Sayowa is the most capable candidate, but because it's
the only one none of the other parties have reason to suspect of quietly favoring itself.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the role carries real practical authority but zero formal backing — no chain of command, no
enforcement power beyond the stakeholders' continued goodwill — it becomes an ongoing target of low-level
political pressure, with every party periodically testing or lobbying the coordinator rather than any one
dramatic confrontation deciding the role's legitimacy.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Sayowa, hosting a role this exposed to sustained outside pressure generation after generation, develops
an informal internal apprenticeship for junction-coordination work — a deliberate practice of identifying
and preparing people suited to carry that weight, since a position this vulnerable can't simply be handed
to someone unready for it.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Sayowa Junction now runs on a locally-hosted, formally unbacked coordinator role whose entire
authority rests on continuously re-earned trust — and Sayowa has quietly built its own internal tradition
for cultivating the kind of person who can hold that position without buckling under it, a structural
reflection of the city's own service-oriented character now given an actual institutional shape.
