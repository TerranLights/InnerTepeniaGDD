## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

"Sayowa" descends phonetically from Shōwa, the Japanese imperial era spanning war, defeat, and
reconstruction — a name carrying real, unambiguous historical weight for the founding community that
chose it, understood by everyone in the young settlement roughly the same way.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That shared understanding doesn't survive the demographic current reshaping this whole corner of the
continent. As Chinese immigration becomes Sayowa's demographic Primary, most of the town's residents
arrive with no personal connection to Shōwa, JARE, or the specific history the name was chosen to carry
— for them it is simply the city's name, phonetically fine, historically inert. The gap widens
generation over generation until the town effectively contains two different publics living under one
name: one that hears an ordinary proper noun, and one that still hears an entire era of war and
reconstruction folded into six syllables. Nothing about the town's civic record — its signage, its
official history, its everyday use of the name — currently distinguishes between the two.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over whether the name's deeper
history deserves formal, public acknowledgment — plaques, civic education, an official account — or
whether it should stay exactly as unremarked-upon as it currently is. This isn't a
preservation-vs.-erasure story; both sides can genuinely want the history preserved, disagreeing instead
on whether "preserved" should mean "publicly explained" or "privately carried." A wants the name's
weight made visible so it can't quietly disappear as the founding community's share keeps shrinking; B
worries that formalizing a private, generational meaning into public civic material would flatten it
into a plaque nobody reads rather than protect it, and would expose something intimate to a demographic
majority that has no stake in treating it carefully.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a younger member of
the Japanese-heritage community, alarmed at how thin the transmission of meaning has already become and
wanting some institutional guarantee against losing it further. Character B is plausibly an older member
of the same community — someone who has carried the name's meaning generationally already and trusts
that same private transmission over any public civic gesture, having seen how quickly official markers
become background noise. This plays best as an intra-community disagreement, not majority vs. minority,
since the majority itself has no real stake in how the argument resolves.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither position fully wins. The name settles into carrying two genuinely different meanings
simultaneously and permanently — an ordinary, unremarkable proper noun for the demographic majority, and
a name still discussed with real, felt gravity within the Japanese-heritage community — without the city
ever adopting an official public account of the deeper history, and without the private tradition of
generational discussion ever formalizing into anything institutional either.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because no public account exists and no institutional record was created, Sayowa's official civic
material — its signage, its administrative history, anything a newcomer or visiting official might
consult — stays completely silent on what the name actually commemorates, even as the private
generational conversation continues, unrecorded, alongside it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That civic silence means the name's deeper meaning now depends entirely on person-to-person
transmission with no institutional backstop at all — a single broken generational link would genuinely
be enough to sever the connection between "Sayowa" and Shōwa forever, a fragility the earlier debate's
public-acknowledgment side had specifically tried, and failed, to guard against.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sayowa now carries its own name in two permanently separate registers — ordinary for most, weighty for a
few — with the weightier meaning surviving on nothing but unbroken personal transmission and no
institutional safeguard behind it. The town's Japanese-heritage community understands, even without
saying it aloud, that this leaves their own history exactly one broken handoff away from becoming a name
nobody left can explain.
