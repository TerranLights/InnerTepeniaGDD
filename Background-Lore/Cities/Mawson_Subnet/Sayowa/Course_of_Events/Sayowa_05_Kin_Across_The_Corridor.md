## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Sayowa and Princess Elisabeth sit closer together than any other two cities on opposite sides of a
subnet boundary in Tepenia — a proximity that exists purely as geographic fact, with no established
relationship between the two communities to match it yet.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Proximity alone doesn't settle what these two cities should be to each other, because their shared
Japanese heritage doesn't line up cleanly. Sayowa carries Japanese institutional heritage — JARE's
research tradition — without matching demographic weight, while Princess Elisabeth's own Japanese
population arrived through a completely separate "eastern approach" immigration current, co-Primary
alongside the USA. Two communities sit close enough to notice each other and share a national-origin
thread, but arrived by such different mechanisms that no obvious protocol exists for whether, or how,
they should treat each other as connected at all.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over whether Sayowa's smaller,
institutionally-rooted Japanese-heritage community and Princess Elisabeth's larger, demographically
distinct one should actively formalize a relationship, or simply be left to relate to each other however
individuals choose. A's motivation is legible and sympathetic — Sayowa's own community is small and
increasingly diluted (see Suggestion #10), and a real connection to Princess Elisabeth's larger
community offers something Sayowa can't generate on its own. B's resistance isn't rejection of kinship —
it's a defense of Princess Elisabeth's own distinct migration history against being read as simply a
larger version of Sayowa's story, when the two communities actually trace back through different eras
and different reasons for arriving.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Sayowa resident
from the JARE-descended community, drawn to Princess Elisabeth's larger Japanese population as a source
of connection and continuity their own city can't fully provide. Character B is plausibly a Princess
Elisabeth resident from the eastern-approach community, protective of their own distinct family history
and wary of an outside push to formalize a "shared Japanese identity" that would flatten two genuinely
different immigration stories into one.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither the push for formal unification nor the resistance to any connection at all wins outright.
Instead, both sides let the question resolve at the level it was actually being asked — individual
families on both sides begin maintaining contact on their own terms, without either city or community
imposing an official narrative about what that contact is supposed to mean.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Genuine, specific customs take root at the family level — shared holiday visits, small exchanges of
craft or goods, occasional intermarriage across the corridor — creating a real current of cultural
traffic between the two cities that exists entirely outside any official record, census, or
inter-city agreement.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

When formal highway logistics eventually reach the corridor, planners and traders find an existing
network of relationships and route knowledge already waiting for them — the formal infrastructure ends up
following paths and demand patterns the family networks had quietly established long before, rather than
creating the connection between the two cities from nothing.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sayowa and Princess Elisabeth are quietly, genuinely connected — not through any inter-city agreement,
but through generations of individual family relationships that outlasted the original disagreement over
whether such a connection even made sense. When the corridor is later formalized on the map, it will be
formalizing something that was already true on the ground.
