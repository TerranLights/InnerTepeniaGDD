## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

As Sayowa's junction grows more critical to the whole region's freight and communications, multiple
outside parties — Mawson, the inland Concordia route, interests from the Halley subnet — all come to
genuinely depend on it running smoothly, without any of them actually controlling it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Growing dependency is exactly what makes formal control tempting. As the junction's strategic value
becomes undeniable, the incentive for any one of its dependent parties to claim jurisdiction over it —
for efficiency, for accountability, or simply for advantage — grows right alongside that value. A serious
bid for formal control surfaces, and with it a real risk: a fight over who owns the junction could easily
disrupt the very function every party depends on, right at the moment disruption would cost the most.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone pushing to
formalize control of the junction under one stakeholder's authority, and someone defending the informal
status quo. This shouldn't read as power-grab-vs.-principled-resistance — A's case is genuinely
reasonable (an unowned, uncoordinated junction this important is a real operational risk, and someone
formally accountable for it isn't an unreasonable ask), while B's resistance isn't sentimental attachment
to informality for its own sake, but a clear-eyed read that any formal claim by one party would
immediately invite rival claims from the others, and that the arrangement's stability comes specifically
from nobody having successfully claimed it yet.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an administrator or
logistics figure from one of the dependent outside parties — Mawson, the Concordia route, or a Halley
subnet interest — pursuing formal jurisdiction that would benefit their own home institution. Character B
is plausibly a Sayowa-based figure with no comparable outside institution to answer to, arguing from
Sayowa's own established disposition toward quiet service rather than assertion — someone whose stake in
keeping things informal is genuinely about function, not personal advantage, since Sayowa itself has the
least to gain from claiming the junction outright.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The bid for formal control quietly fails — not through dramatic defeat, but because enough of the other
dependent parties recognize what B's position points out: any formal claim invites contest from everyone
else. The parties settle instead into an informal cooperative arrangement, each maintaining what it uses,
nobody claiming the whole.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because nobody formally owns the junction, nobody is formally responsible for major capital investment
in it either — large infrastructure upgrades become genuinely difficult to fund, since no single party
wants to unilaterally pay for improvements to something it doesn't control.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Sayowa itself, as the one party actually physically present day-to-day, ends up absorbing the bulk of the
junction's ongoing maintenance labor and cost by default — becoming, without ever formally claiming the
role, the junction's de facto custodian even though the cooperative arrangement was explicitly built to
avoid any single party holding that position.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Sayowa Junction runs on cooperative goodwill among parties who all depend on it and none of whom
control it — stable, functional, and genuinely fragile, its major investment needs quietly unmet and its
routine upkeep quietly absorbed by the one city with the least formal claim to it. The arrangement holds
for now because everyone still finds cooperation easier than a fight they'd all lose — but the
underinvestment it never solved is exactly the kind of gap that eventually needs someone to manage
directly.
