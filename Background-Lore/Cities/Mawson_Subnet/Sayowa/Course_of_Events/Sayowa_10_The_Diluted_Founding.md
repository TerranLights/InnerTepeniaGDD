## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Japan's exile population settles Sayowa organically, via JARE's inherited station infrastructure,
holding real founding-era demographic and institutional weight — with no diplomatic protection securing
that position the way Shirayuki's founders would later arrange for themselves at Jeju-do.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The same regional Chinese immigration current reshaping this whole corner of the continent begins
reaching Sayowa too, with nothing in place to slow it. The founding Japanese community faces a slow,
genuinely undramatic demographic erosion — no legal mechanism, no diplomatic leverage, no equivalent to
whatever arrangement might secure a founding population's position elsewhere. The dilution is visible
early enough that something could still theoretically be done about it, if the community chose to act
before its own political weight eroded any further.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement within the founding Japanese
community over whether to fight for some formal protection of their demographic position — a residency
preference, a cultural-quota petition to the fledgling Federation government — while there's still enough
standing to plausibly get one. A's push isn't paranoia; it's a clear-eyed read of a trend that, left
unaddressed, has an obvious endpoint. B's resistance isn't fatalism dressed as principle, but a genuine
practical judgment: Sayowa's organic, unremarkable JARE inheritance gives the community no comparable
leverage to what secured Shirayuki's protection elsewhere, and a fight this unwinnable would waste the
community's limited political capital rather than spend it wisely.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a community figure who
recognizes the dilution trend early and wants to act on it while the community still has enough numbers
and standing to be heard. Character B is plausibly an equally invested community figure — perhaps
someone with a more direct read on Tepenian federal politics — who judges the fight unwinnable given
Sayowa's specific circumstances, and would rather the community save its effort for something it can
actually secure.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The protective push is attempted and fails to secure anything binding — B's read of the community's
actual leverage proves correct — and the demographic dilution proceeds unchecked, concretely resulting,
generations later, in China holding clear Primary status while Japan settles into Significant tier
alongside several other nations.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The failed attempt itself becomes part of the community's own remembered history — a specific, named
effort that didn't work, rather than a vague story of passive, effortless decline. The Japanese-heritage
community remembers actively trying and losing, not simply fading without a fight.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Generations afterward, when word reaches Sayowa of Shirayuki's founders successfully securing diplomatic
protection at Jeju-do, Sayowa's own community reads that success specifically through the lens of their
own earlier failed attempt — giving Sayowa's diluted outcome a genuinely personal edge beyond pure
demographic contrast, the felt difference between a community that never tried and one that tried once
and came up short.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sayowa carries its diluted Japanese heritage not as a story of simple passive erosion, but as the living
memory of a specific attempt that came too late and lacked the leverage to succeed — a community that
tried, once, to do what Shirayuki's founders later managed elsewhere, and has carried the difference
between those two outcomes ever since.
