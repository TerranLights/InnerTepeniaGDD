## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

JARE's original research tradition and the Shōwa-era naming history persist as the smaller
Japanese-heritage community's own living inheritance, carried generationally through personal
conversation rather than any institutional record — a system that has worked, so far, without anyone
needing to formalize it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The founding population keeps diluting generation after generation, and the informal system starts
showing its limits. It becomes clear that without some deliberate intervention, real, specific knowledge
— exactly who settled here and why, what the research program actually accomplished, what the name
precisely commemorates — will be lost within a couple more generations. Oral transmission worked when
the community carrying it was large enough to absorb the occasional broken link; it can't be counted on
to keep working as that community keeps shrinking.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over how to guarantee memory doesn't
disappear — through a formal, documented, possibly public archive, or through keeping the tradition
exactly as private and informal as it's always been. A's push for documentation is a legitimate response
to a real, looming risk of total loss. B's resistance isn't indifference to that risk — it's a concern
that formalizing intimate, generational memory into a public or institutional archive would expose it to
a demographic majority with no stake in handling it carefully, and risks turning a living practice into a
static museum-piece nobody actually visits.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a younger member of
the community — a natural fit for the "Haruto" figure — alarmed at how much has already gone unrecorded
and pushing to systematize what remains before it's too late. Character B is plausibly an older member of
the same community, someone who has personally carried the tradition through private conversation for
decades and distrusts institutionalization as a form of loss disguised as preservation.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two positions meet in the middle rather than one defeating the other: a dedicated historian keeps
deliberate, careful records — the research tradition's own history, what the name commemorates, who
first settled here and why — but the record stays inside the community's own private structures rather
than becoming a city archive or public exhibit, satisfying A's need for durability without violating B's
concern about exposure.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the ledger is privately maintained rather than a public institution, its survival depends
entirely on the historian personally choosing and training a successor — there is no institutional
continuity mechanism behind it, only person-to-person handoff, generation after generation.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

The community starts treating that succession itself as seriously as the memory it protects — identifying
and quietly training a next keeper becomes its own deliberate civic practice, so the ledger's survival no
longer comes down to chance the way the original oral tradition it replaced eventually did.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sayowa's Japanese-heritage community now carries a genuinely maintained but institutionally invisible
historical record, kept alive through deliberate, consciously-managed succession rather than informal
luck. Haruto's own quiet, unglamorous recordkeeping — and the tradition of choosing who keeps it next —
is the specific, ongoing reason this heritage survives as more than a name nobody left can explain.
