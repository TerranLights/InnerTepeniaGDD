## What the Handoff Log Actually Recorded

## The Object That Took Three Rotations to Arrive

The Wall of Home — a communal display where departing crew added something representing their own home
city — built up, over generations of rotations, into a dense collage of dozens of different Tepenian cities,
since rotating crew were drawn from quite literally every city in the Federation.

One specific contribution, per what's preserved of the Wall's own accumulated history, took considerably
longer to actually appear than most — a crew member from a genuinely remote city, having promised departing
colleagues during her own tour that she'd send something representative once she returned home, took three
full rotation cycles before the promised object actually reached the station through the ordinary supply and
mail channels connecting her own city to the Pole. The delay wasn't a broken promise but simply the genuine
logistical difficulty of moving anything from her own particular city to the station at all — a difficulty
the eventual arrival made visible in a way the Wall's own more promptly added pieces never could. Crew who
placed the object once it finally arrived, per what's remembered of the occasion, treated the wait itself as
worth mentioning alongside the object — a small, specific reminder of just how far some contributions to the
Wall actually had to travel.

explaining the specific convention difference — a small, practical addition to the archive's own working
methodology, added specifically so no future crew would need to spend a full rotation solving the same
puzzle twice.

## The Sunrise Crews Stopped What They Were Doing For

The equinox brought a 360° simultaneous sunrise after six months of continuous polar night — one of the most
extraordinary visual phenomena in all of Tepenia, and the most plausible, earned timing for whatever informal
acknowledgment of St. Roald rotating crews observed at the actual site of Amundsen's own historic achievement.

One specific equinox, per what's preserved of the station's own informal observance history, coincided with a
demanding operational stretch that had kept the crew on duty continuously through the final days of polar
night — work that, by ordinary schedule, would have continued straight through the sunrise itself without
pause. The crew chief on duty that rotation, per what's remembered of the occasion, made the deliberate call
to halt non-essential operations for the specific window of the sunrise itself, judging the phenomenon worth
the schedule disruption even under real operational pressure. The crew gathered briefly to watch the sun
return simultaneously from every direction at once, then returned to the demanding work still waiting for
them. The specific decision to pause for it, made under genuine pressure not to, is remembered, within the
station's own informal culture, as exactly the kind of small, deliberate choice that kept the observance
alive across generations of otherwise purely functional rotations.
