# Port Lockroy — Candidate Courses of Events

**Scope note:** all ten chains below are set strictly *before* the Long Night War, during the Second
Interwar Period when Tepenia is a full, functioning country — no war damage, no post-war/present-day
material. Port Lockroy's entire established postal-hub arc (military installation → heritage museum →
living city → mature subnet correspondence hub) already unfolds within this window, so these chains
have unusually wide room to work with. 10 candidate But/Therefore chains built from Port Lockroy's
Megasheet (the layered architecture, Old Toby's stone wall, the postal-hub destiny). Post-settlement
only; nothing here is canon.

---

## 1. Old Toby's Guess

Old Toby Fenwick spends his days tending goats, working stone, and handing packages to the occasional
robot courier passing through — ordinary work in a small, quiet city. **But** something about the
steady rhythm of those courier visits gets him thinking past the present moment. **Therefore** he
starts musing, half to himself and half to whoever's listening, that Port Lockroy might someday become
a real hub of postal delivery. **But** he's old enough to know he won't live to see whether he's
right. **Therefore** he says it anyway, doubtful and unbothered by the doubt — an offhand guess that
turns out, over the centuries that follow, to have been exactly correct.

## 2. Never Torn Down

Port Lockroy's original 1944 Operation Tabarin buildings still stand when the exile community arrives
to make the site their home. **But** those buildings were built for a wartime watch post, not a
living city, and don't naturally suit everything a growing population actually needs. **Therefore**
residents build around and onto the original structures instead of clearing them away — new
construction incorporating the old rather than replacing it. **But** that choice means every later
generation has to actually work with an older building's constraints rather than starting clean.
**Therefore** Port Lockroy grows up visibly layered, a city that always knows exactly what it used to
be, because it never demolished the evidence.

## 3. The Wall Grows

Old Toby carves the first panels into a stone wall himself, a personal project he knows he'll never
finish alone. **But** a wall built to outlast its own builder needs other hands to actually keep
growing after he's gone. **Therefore** other residents start adding their own panels over the years,
some following his lead deliberately, others simply drawn to add something of their own to a
structure that already means something to the city. **But** a wall built by many different hands over
generations risks becoming visually incoherent, a jumble rather than a single artifact. **Therefore**
it becomes something richer instead — a genuine communal palimpsest, legible as one continuous project
even though no single person ever saw the whole of it built.

## 4. Fast Words, Slow Goods

Port Lockroy's harbor handles genuine maritime trade from its earliest years, goods moving in and out
at a steady, unhurried pace. **But** the same courier traffic that first caught Old Toby's attention
keeps growing, faster and more frequent than the harbor's own slower trade rhythm. **Therefore** the
two functions start to specialize apart from each other — the harbor staying the city's fuller,
slower exchange, the courier network sharpening into something faster and more purpose-built.
**But** a subnet this size needs both kinds of movement, not just one. **Therefore** Port Lockroy
settles into a genuine dual role, complementing Rothera's own heavier industrial-freight function with
something faster and lighter — the subnet's words moving through Port Lockroy while its materials move
through Rothera.

## 5. Living In a Museum

Port Lockroy's exile community inherits a site that was, before it was ever a home, a heritage
museum — a place whose whole pre-exile identity was preservation and display. **But** actually living
somewhere and performing its history for visitors are two different relationships to the same place.
**Therefore** residents find themselves constantly, quietly negotiating which one Port Lockroy actually
is — a city where people build real lives, or a site that happens to have people living inside its own
preserved shell. **But** neither answer fully satisfies everyone. **Therefore** the tension never fully
resolves, becoming instead a permanent, low-key thread running through the city's whole civic
character — never quite settled, never quite a real problem either.

## 6. Founders' Watch

Port Lockroy's original purpose, long before any exile ever arrived, was watching the South Atlantic
for naval activity. **But** a purpose that specific, from an era nobody living remembers firsthand,
could easily fade from civic memory once the war it was built for is centuries past. **Therefore**
residents deliberately keep it alive instead — Founders' Watch, an overnight vigil observance directly
honoring that original 1944 purpose, residents keeping literal watch as the holiday's central ritual.
**But** reenacting a wartime watch in a time of ordinary peace risks feeling like empty theater.
**Therefore** it doesn't — the vigil becomes genuinely meaningful anyway, a quiet acknowledgment that
the site's whole existence traces back to people watching for something, long before anyone lived
there at all.

## 7. The Long Memory

Old Toby's stone wall keeps growing, panel by panel, as an informal, ongoing communal habit. **But** an
informal habit risks fading if nobody ever makes it deliberate. **Therefore** the practice eventually
formalizes into an actual civic holiday — The Long Memory, its central ritual the addition of a new
panel to the wall itself, tying the observance directly to the same physical artifact that inspired
it. **But** formalizing something this personal risks turning genuine expression into obligatory
ceremony. **Therefore** the holiday keeps enough flexibility that each year's addition still feels like
a real choice, not a chore — residents adding to the wall because they still want to, not merely
because the calendar says it's time.

## 8. He Didn't Live to See It

Generations pass after Old Toby's own lifetime, the courier network he once mused about growing
steadily, one small expansion at a time. **But** no single moment ever announces that his old guess
has finally come true — it happens too gradually for anyone to mark the exact turning point.
**Therefore** by the late Second Interwar Period, Port Lockroy has genuinely become what he predicted —
the Palmer subnet's real correspondence-and-archive hub, a functioning role rather than a hopeful
guess. **But** the person who first said it out loud isn't there to see it confirmed. **Therefore**
residents who know the story tell it anyway, Old Toby's own doubtful, half-joking prediction becoming
local legend precisely because it turned out to be right, long after he could have known.

## 9. Smoke From Far Away

Old Toby smokes tobacco imported all the way from Argentina, since Antarctic hydroponics are still
genuinely rudimentary this early in the Second Interwar Period. **But** relying on imported goods for
something as ordinary as tobacco means Port Lockroy's daily life depends on trade connections reaching
well beyond its own small footprint. **Therefore** small, unremarkable imports like his tobacco become
part of the same growing web of inter-city and inter-subnet trade that eventually matures into Port
Lockroy's own postal-and-correspondence specialty — the city's future role quietly foreshadowed by
something as small as one old man's smoking habit. **But** nobody at the time thinks of it that way.
**Therefore** the connection stays invisible until much later, one more small proof that Port Lockroy's
eventual destiny was built out of ordinary daily details nobody thought were significant at the time.

## 10. A Hub Nobody Announced

Port Lockroy's postal role grows the same way most of the city's other functions do — gradually,
without any single deliberate decision to make it the city's defining identity. **But** a subnet-wide
correspondence network doesn't organize itself; someone has to keep expanding the routes, keep adding
capacity, keep the whole system actually working as demand grows. **Therefore** generations of
residents make that steady, unglamorous investment, one incremental improvement at a time, without
ever holding a ceremony to mark Port Lockroy officially becoming a hub. **But** enough accumulated
investment eventually looks, from outside, exactly like deliberate design. **Therefore** by the time
anyone thinks to ask how Port Lockroy became the subnet's postal center, the honest answer is that
nobody ever formally decided it — it simply kept growing, the same way Old Toby always quietly
suspected it would.

---

## Worth Your Attention

**#1 and #8** work as the clearest pair in the set — Old Toby's original offhand guess, and the
generation that finally sees it come true without him there to witness it, giving the Megasheet's own
central, quietly moving detail (a prophecy proven true only after its speaker's death) its full
dramatized arc. **#4 and #10** trace the postal-hub role's actual mechanics, from its earliest split
with harbor trade to its eventual, undramatic maturity into a real subnet-wide function.
