## A Panel Every Year

The Communal Stone Wall (Physical Infrastructure Attribute #2) is a genuine multi-generational artifact — a
new panel added each year, the city's own layered-history character made into something residents actively
build onto, rather than a static inherited landmark left over from an earlier era.

*(An informational sheet on the tradition itself, not a single panel.)* Each year's addition, per what's
established about the Wall's own practice, becomes a permanent, physical record of that specific year in Port
Lockroy's history — not curated or edited afterward, simply added to and left standing alongside every
previous year's own contribution. Residents describe the Wall as the closest thing the city has to a written
chronicle that everyone, not just a designated record-keeper, has a hand in — a small city's own way of
insisting that no single year gets forgotten or smoothed over, consistent with the broader civic character of
a place built around continuous accountability to its own history rather than papering over any part of it.

## What Old Toby Left Unfinished

Old Toby's Goat Pens and Workshop (attribute #7) is where the man remembered simply as **"Old Toby"
*(placeholder, not developer-chosen)*** kept a small-scale goat husbandry and stonework operation — modest,
quiet work fitting Port Lockroy's own small population and unhurried civic character.

Per Finding B, Old Toby's own workshop connects directly to the Communal Stone Wall — the stone he worked
fed the same Wall residents still add to today, and his own doubt about a prophecy he once made turns out,
per Tepenia's own established human lifespan facts, to be mathematically accurate. He worried the wall would
outlast his own ability to keep contributing to it, a fear the demographic facts of his own lifespan
ultimately confirmed rather than disproved. What makes the connection genuinely affecting, per Finding B, is
that a visitor can trace both halves of the same story in one walkable space — the workshop where the stone
was shaped, and the wall it slowly became, one panel added faithfully every year for as long as Old Toby
himself was able, and by others, unbroken, ever since.

## Building Over, Not Building Over Again

The 1944 Base Structures (attribute #1) — the original buildings from the site's own earlier wartime
intelligence-installation era, later a heritage museum before the exile generation arrived — were
incorporated into the later city rather than demolished, now the most structurally intact part of Port
Lockroy given their original heavy-duty construction.

*(An informational sheet on the palimpsest itself, not a single renovation.)* Every era of the site's own
history is physically present rather than erased and replaced — a genuine architectural palimpsest, distinct
from any Tepenian city built on a single continuous foundation. Builders from the exile generation, per what's
established about the site's own layered history, chose to work around and alongside the older structures
rather than clearing them for new construction, a deliberate continuation of the same "account for the past
rather than paper over it" civic instinct the Communal Stone Wall also embodies. The result is a city where a
resident can point to a single wall or foundation and name which era actually built it — the oldest layer
still legible underneath everything added since, rather than lost beneath it.

## What the Courier Network Actually Carried

The Reduced Postal Relay (attribute #5), a scaled-down, partly hand-managed correspondence network, and
whatever infrastructure once supported the full pre-war postal network's own larger operational scale
(attribute #6) together made Port Lockroy's own courier system a genuine functioning civic institution, not
merely a heritage artifact left over from an earlier era.

*(An informational sheet drawing on already-established background lore — discoverable, not quest-marked.)*
Port Lockroy's own courier network handled shipping for what became Calethina during the pre-war and Long
Night War era, part of the network's ordinary, unremarkable correspondence and small-goods traffic rather
than anything the network itself would have treated as unusual at the time. Postal staff working the relay,
per what's established about the network's own operational culture, moved correspondence and small parcels
across the Peninsula the same way comparable civic institutions elsewhere in Tepenia did — patient, routine
work, largely invisible to history except in the rare cases, like this one, where a name later became
significant enough for the connection to be worth noting at all.

## A Harbor Small Boats Could Actually Use

Port Lockroy Harbor (attributes #3–4) — sheltered from open ocean swells inside Goudier Island's own
protected natural harbor — gave the city a genuine maritime trade niche distinct from any open-coast
Peninsula city, small-craft trade rather than the bulk freight Marambio's and Rothera's own larger facilities
were built to handle.

*(An informational sheet on the niche itself, not a single voyage.)* Per Finding D, the Harbor's own modest
scale is a direct, checkable contrast to Marambio's Shipyards — Port Lockroy never functioned as a major DLC
3 cargo waypoint, and never needed to, since its actual economic role ran through its postal identity and
small-craft trade rather than heavy freight transit. Traders working the sheltered waters, per what's
established about the harbor's own character, valued exactly what made it unsuitable for bulk shipping — calm,
protected conditions that let small vessels operate reliably in weather that would have kept larger, more
exposed ports idle. The subnet's own established freight hierarchy — Marambio and Rothera for bulk, Port
Lockroy for letters and small goods — reflects genuine physical difference in what each harbor could actually
support, not a difference in ambition.

## Keeping Watch

*(A vignette drawing on the established "Founders' Watch" observance, GPS-only per project convention —
honoring the founding generation's own vigilant-watchfulness tradition rather than the site's real 1944
wartime purpose directly.)* Founders' Watch commemorates a founding-era civic instinct distinct from the
site's own earlier historical layers — the exile generation's deliberate choice to treat vigilance and
attentiveness to their own small, exposed settlement as a value worth formally marking, rather than simply
inheriting the site's older military watchfulness unreflectively.

Residents who observe Founders' Watch, per what's established about the tradition, describe it less as
honoring any specific historical event and more as renewing a standing civic commitment — a small city on a
small island, watching out for itself and each other, the same practical attentiveness that let the founding
generation actually establish a working settlement here in the first place. The observance sits comfortably
alongside the Communal Stone Wall and the layered architecture as one more expression of the same underlying
civic character: a city that takes its own continuity, and its own responsibility to maintain it, seriously
enough to mark formally, year after year.
