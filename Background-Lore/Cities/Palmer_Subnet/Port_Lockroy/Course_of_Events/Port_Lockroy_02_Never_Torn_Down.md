## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The original 1944 Operation Tabarin buildings are still standing when the exile community first arrives to
make the site their home — a compact set of structures built for a wartime watch post, occupied now by
people who intend to actually live here.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Buildings built to house a small wartime garrison don't naturally suit a growing civilian population —
there isn't enough of them, and what's there isn't shaped for family life, workshops, or the ordinary
accumulation of a real town. The community has to decide, fairly early and fairly practically, whether to
clear the old structures and start building on a clean footprint, or to build around and onto what's
already standing. That decision has to be made while people are still cold, still settling, still working
out what the site even is to them.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement between practical necessity and
inherited obligation, where both sides are responding to the same hard winter with opposite instincts.
Character A wants a clean slate — the old buildings are cramped, oddly shaped, and wrong for the population
now living around them, and clearing them would let the community build exactly what it actually needs
instead of constantly negotiating around someone else's wartime floor plan. Character B resists demolition
not out of sentimentality alone, but because the buildings are the one tangible thing connecting this new
population to the site's own history — tearing them down would mean the exile community's first real act
here was erasing what came before it, which feels like a bad way to start.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a builder or
practically-minded organizer focused on the community's immediate survival needs — housing, workshops,
storage — impatient with constraints that make hard work harder. Character B is plausibly someone with a
felt connection to the site's pre-exile identity, whether through the heritage-museum period specifically
or a more general instinct that a place's own history is worth keeping physically present — grounded in
the same "living memory versus heritage spectacle" tension the Megasheet identifies as Port Lockroy's real
founding fault line.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side gets a clean win. The community builds around and onto the original structures instead of
clearing them — new construction incorporating the old rather than replacing it, which costs the
practical side genuine convenience and gives the preservation side something real, but leaves both sides
living with the compromise rather than fully satisfied by it.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because new construction has to literally route around old foundations and walls it isn't allowed to
remove, Port Lockroy's street plan and building footprint come out irregular and non-grid — narrow,
winding, and intimate in a way a cleanly planned town wouldn't be, a physical shape dictated by what
couldn't be torn down rather than by any single designer's intention.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That irregular layout — not the original decision to preserve, but the specific winding shape it produced
— becomes the physical stage generations of later civic life actually happen on: the odd corners and
nooks the old-building constraints created turn into the natural gathering spots, courier waypoints, and
eventually the wall-carving site itself, simply because those are the spaces the layout happened to leave
open. A city built to a clean grid would have offered no equivalent spaces in the same places.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Port Lockroy is now a small, physically layered city with a genuinely irregular footprint — every winding
street and oddly shaped corner a direct architectural trace of the choice never to demolish what it
inherited, and already, without anyone planning it, becoming the ground later civic and communal life will
be built on top of.
