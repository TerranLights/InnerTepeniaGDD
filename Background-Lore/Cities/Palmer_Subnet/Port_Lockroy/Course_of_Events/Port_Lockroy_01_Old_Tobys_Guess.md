## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Old Toby spends his days tending goats, working stone on a wall he's already resigned to never finishing
alone, and handing parcels to whichever robot courier happens to be passing through — ordinary, settled
work in a small, quiet city that has never had much reason to think of itself as anything more than what
it visibly is.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The couriers keep coming, though — not dramatically more often, just steadily, the same unremarkable
rhythm repeating itself often enough that Old Toby starts noticing it as a pattern rather than a
coincidence. That noticing tips into speculation: half-formed at first, then something he actually says
out loud, that Port Lockroy might someday become a real hub of postal delivery, not just a stop a courier
happens to pass. The moment he says it, the arithmetic of his own age catches up with him — he's old
enough to do the rough math on how long "someday" would actually take, and old enough to know the answer
isn't in his favor.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between Old Toby, having just said
something unprovable out loud, and a contemporary who thinks he shouldn't have. This isn't a
villain-vs.-visionary setup — the pushback is reasonable on its own terms. Character A's stake is
something close to legacy: having noticed a real pattern, wanting to name it before it's too late to name
anything at all, even knowing he can't prove it. Character B's stake is protective, maybe of the
community's practical focus, maybe of Old Toby himself — a worry that staking a claim on a hundred-plus
years he'll never see makes him look foolish now, in a life that's otherwise been sensibly lived. Neither
side is wrong: the pattern is real but genuinely thin, and the caution is real but genuinely limiting.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Old Toby himself, in
the role the Megasheet already gives him — the goat-tending stonemason with an eye for slow patterns, the
same temperament that keeps him carving a wall he won't finish. Character B is plausibly a fellow elder
or working peer, someone whose own life has been organized around the harbor's steadier, provable trade
rather than a courier network's speculative future — grounded in Port Lockroy's established dual
harbor/postal identity, arguing from the harbor side's own quieter confidence.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Old Toby says it anyway. Not as a rebuttal, not with any real heat behind it — he simply repeats the guess,
in front of the very person telling him to drop it, doubtful and entirely unbothered by his own doubt. The
disagreement doesn't end in either side conceding; it ends because Old Toby was never actually arguing to
win, only refusing to take the guess back.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because he said it in front of a witness who pushed back rather than in private, the exchange itself
becomes a small story people repeat — not "Old Toby thinks," but "Old Toby said, right to \[so-and-so\]'s
face." Within his own remaining years, it hardens into a known local anecdote, attached specifically to
his name, told at gatherings whenever courier traffic happens to tick upward.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That repeated anecdote gives a small number of younger residents — children and near-adults who grow up
hearing it told — something to aim their working lives toward that didn't exist as a real option before:
choosing courier or postal-adjacent work specifically because of the story, decades before there's any
actual hub to justify the choice. It isn't yet a tradition; it's just a handful of individual decisions,
each one traceable back to having heard the anecdote told, not to the original guess alone.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Port Lockroy is still, in every visible respect, an ordinary small city — but it now carries a named local
story about its own possible future, and a small, informal thread of residents who've already started
orienting their working lives around a hub that doesn't exist yet, seeded by nothing more than an old
man's guess and the argument it survived.
