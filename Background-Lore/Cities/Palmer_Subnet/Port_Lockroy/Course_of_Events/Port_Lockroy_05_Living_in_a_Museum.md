## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The exile community inherits a site whose entire pre-exile identity was preservation and display — a
heritage museum before it was ever anyone's home, its buildings and layout already shaped by the job of
being looked at rather than lived in.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Actually living somewhere and performing its history for visitors turn out to be two different
relationships to the same place, and the community can't cleanly choose one over the other. Residents
find themselves constantly, quietly negotiating which one Port Lockroy actually is — a city where people
build ordinary lives, or a preserved site that happens to have people living inside its own shell — and
every attempt to settle the question one way leaves the other answer feeling betrayed.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement about what the site owes its own
past versus what it owes the people currently living in it. Character A wants Port Lockroy to fully
commit to being an ordinary town — resents having daily choices, building uses, or personal routines
implicitly measured against whether they honor the site's museum identity, and wants the community's
self-determination to come first. Character B isn't arguing against ordinary life so much as against
casual disregard for what the place already meant before the exiles arrived — treats the site's
heritage identity as something inherited and owed continued care, not a costume to be shed the moment it
becomes inconvenient. Both are right about something real: A about the cost of living inside someone
else's frame, B about the cost of discarding it.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a younger or more
practically minded resident, impatient with any expectation that ordinary life has to accommodate a
preserved identity that predates them. Character B is plausibly someone with a personal or inherited
connection to the site's museum period — perhaps someone who arrived already caring about its curatorial
history, or simply someone whose temperament runs toward stewardship — grounded in Port Lockroy's own
established layered-architecture culture, where the past is never simply cleared away.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side settles the question, and — unusually — that's the actual resolution: an informal,
unlegislated compromise takes hold where some parts of the city function more like ordinary living
quarters and others stay treated as something closer to commemorative space, without anyone ever
formally deciding the boundary between them. The tension doesn't get solved; it becomes a permanent,
low-key thread in the city's civic character — never quite settled, never quite a real problem either.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because nobody ever arbitrates the ambiguity, it stops being treated as an open dispute and starts being
taught, matter-of-factly, as simply how Port Lockroy is — something residents explain to their children
and to newcomers not as an unresolved argument but as a basic fact about the city's character, the same
way they'd explain the tides.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That self-aware, matter-of-fact civic narrative — "we've never quite decided what kind of place this is"
— becomes something Port Lockroy starts actively telling outsiders, couriers, and traders passing through,
as a piece of local color about the city's own identity. The story of the unresolved tension becomes, in
the telling, another small performance for visitors — feeding directly back into the museum-spectacle
side of the very question it describes, in a way that couldn't happen if the ambiguity had simply stayed
private.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Port Lockroy's founding tension between lived life and heritage performance is now fully self-aware —
an accepted, permanently unresolved piece of local identity that residents narrate to outsiders almost as
a point of civic character, deepening rather than settling the very ambiguity it describes.
