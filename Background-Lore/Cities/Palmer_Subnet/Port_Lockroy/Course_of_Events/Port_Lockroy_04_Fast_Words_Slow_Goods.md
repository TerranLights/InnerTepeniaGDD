## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Port Lockroy's sheltered harbor handles genuine maritime trade from its earliest years — goods moving in
and out at a steady, unhurried pace, the city's one clearly established economic function.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The same courier traffic that first caught Old Toby's attention keeps growing — not explosively, but
consistently, faster and more frequent than the harbor's own slower rhythm can comfortably absorb.
Dock space, labor, and civic attention that used to go entirely to maritime trade now have to be
divided, and the two functions start pulling apart from each other under that pressure: the harbor
staying what it's always been, the courier network sharpening into something faster and more
purpose-built, each one wanting more of the same limited resources the other one also needs.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a resource competition between two legitimate
economic interests, not a fight between an old guard and an upstart. Character A's livelihood and
identity run through the harbor — dock space, labor hours, and civic investment that have always gone to
proven, tangible maritime trade — and watching that steadily get divided with an unproven courier
operation feels like being asked to subsidize someone else's gamble. Character B is invested in the
courier network specifically because it's growing on its own, demand-driven momentum that the harbor
can't match and arguably shouldn't try to absorb — wants labor and space redirected toward what's
actually expanding, not toward propping up a function that's already stable. Both are defending something
real: one a proven present, the other a visible trend.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a harbor worker,
dockmaster, or maritime trader whose whole working life is organized around the harbor's established
rhythm. Character B is plausibly someone closer to the courier and postal side — a route coordinator, a
frequent courier contact, or simply a resident who's noticed the same pattern Old Toby noticed and wants
the city to invest accordingly, grounded in Port Lockroy's own established dual-function destiny.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two functions don't merge and neither absorbs the other — they specialize apart, deliberately.
Port Lockroy settles into a genuine dual role: the harbor keeps its slower, fuller maritime trade at a
reduced but real share of civic attention, while the courier network gets the resources it needs to
sharpen into something faster and lighter, positioning Port Lockroy to complement Rothera's heavier
industrial-freight function rather than compete with it — words moving through Port Lockroy, materials
moving through Rothera.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the postal function is now an explicit civic priority in its own right rather than an offshoot
of harbor trade, it gets sited on its own terms — nearer the Hwy 1 ramp connection for fast in-and-out
access, physically separate from the harbor's own waterfront footprint. Port Lockroy ends up with two
distinct commercial cores instead of one.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because those two cores are physically separate, distinct daily rhythms and small, informal identities
grow up around each one — harbor work paced to tides and cargo schedules, ramp work paced to courier
arrivals and dispatch windows. Residents start describing themselves, half-jokingly, as "harbor people"
or "ramp people," a small internal social geography that only exists because the two functions ended up
in two different places, not merely because they're two different jobs.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Port Lockroy now runs on two physically and culturally distinct commercial districts — a slower harbor
waterfront and a fast-paced courier district near the highway ramp — each with its own working rhythm and
its own small claim on the city's identity, complementing Rothera's freight role from a genuinely
specialized niche rather than competing against it.
