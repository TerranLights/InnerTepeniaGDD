## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Old Toby is aging, the courier network he once mused about still growing the same way it always has —
steadily, one small expansion at a time, with nothing dramatic enough yet to prove him definitively right.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The growth is real but stays too gradual to settle the argument either way — no single moment ever arrives
that would let anyone declare the guess confirmed or disproven, which means the disagreement Old Toby
started years earlier never gets to resolve on its own merits. As he gets older, that unresolved argument
starts to matter to him differently: less about being right in the abstract, more about whether anyone will
still remember he said it at all once he's gone.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a long-running, never-quite-resolved disagreement
between Old Toby, aging and increasingly aware he's running out of time to be proven right, and a
contemporary who has held, consistently and not unreasonably, that the guess remains unproven and
shouldn't be treated as settled fact just because a few decades of ordinary growth happened to follow it.
Character A's stake shifts, late in life, from wanting to be correct to wanting to be remembered — a
distinction the other side doesn't necessarily grant him. Character B's caution is genuinely
evidence-respecting, not stubborn contrarianism: predicting a hundred-plus years of civic development from
a handful of courier visits really was a leap, and saying so wasn't unfair.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Old Toby himself, now
older, his temperament unchanged from the goat-tending stonemason who first said the guess out loud.
Character B is plausibly the same kind of skeptical peer established in Port Lockroy's other founding
material — someone whose caution comes from genuine care about not overclaiming the future, not from any
wish to see Old Toby wrong.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The argument never gets settled between the two of them — Old Toby dies with the question still open,
neither side having conceded anything. Generations later, long after both are gone, the courier network
has genuinely matured into the Palmer subnet's real correspondence-and-archive hub — no dramatic
turning point, just an accumulation nobody was there to announce. Residents who still know the story tell
it anyway, and Old Toby's own doubtful, half-joking prediction becomes local legend precisely because it
turned out to be right, long after he could have known.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the confirmation arrives only after both original voices are gone, nobody still living can
actually attest to which side was being fair-minded at the time. The retelling starts crediting Old
Toby's foresight without qualification, while the other side's reasoning — caution grounded in genuine
uncertainty, not stubbornness — quietly drops out of the story. What was once a real, two-sided
disagreement flattens into a simpler, tidier legend about one man being right.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because civic memory now presents confident foresight as simply the correct instinct, with reasonable
skepticism written out of the story entirely, later residents facing their own genuinely uncertain civic
questions become subtly biased toward whichever guess sounds most confident or most mythic, rather than
weighing real uncertainty on its own terms — a mild, inherited overconfidence traceable specifically to
how the legend got smoothed in the retelling, not to Old Toby's original guess having been correct.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Port Lockroy carries a fully confirmed local legend — the prediction come true, its original skeptic long
forgotten along with the fairness of his own reasoning — and, quietly inherited alongside it, a civic
tendency to trust confident-sounding predictions about its own future a little more than the evidence at
the time actually warrants.
