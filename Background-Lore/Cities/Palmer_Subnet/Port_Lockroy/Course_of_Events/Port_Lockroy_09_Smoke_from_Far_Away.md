## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Old Toby smokes tobacco imported all the way from Argentina, an ordinary daily habit nobody thinks twice
about, since Antarctic hydroponics are still genuinely rudimentary this early in the Second Interwar
Period.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Relying on an imported good for something as ordinary as tobacco means Port Lockroy's daily life already
depends on trade connections reaching well beyond its own small footprint, whether anyone thinks of it
that way or not. Once that dependency gets noticed — even just by one resident, even just in passing — it
raises a genuine civic question nobody's had to answer yet: should the city work to reduce that
dependency, or is leaning into it actually the smarter long-term bet.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement about whether a small city's future
lies in reducing its dependence on the outside world or in getting good at depending on it deliberately.
Character A has likely been burned, directly or by report, by a supply disruption — a shipment delayed,
a good gone without for a stretch — and wants the city to invest in local production, hydroponics chief
among them, so ordinary daily life doesn't hinge on distant trade routes. Character B doesn't dispute the
risk, but doubts a city this small could ever meaningfully produce its own way out of it, and argues the
smarter investment is in getting genuinely good at receiving, sorting, and moving goods and information —
leaning into interdependence as a role worth mastering, rather than a weakness worth fighting.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a resident with direct
experience of scarcity or supply delay, motivated by wanting more control over daily necessities.
Character B is plausibly someone closer to the harbor or courier trade — Old Toby himself fits this role
naturally, given his own established dependence on imported tobacco and his instinct toward the postal
network's future — arguing from lived comfort with, rather than anxiety about, the city's position in a
wider network.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The self-sufficiency argument doesn't win, not through any dramatic defeat but through simple inertia — no
serious civic investment in local hydroponics or production follows, and imports like tobacco stay a normal,
unremarkable part of daily life. Nobody at the time frames this as a decision at all; the connection
between one old man's smoking habit and the city's whole trade posture stays invisible, one more small
proof that Port Lockroy's eventual destiny gets built out of ordinary daily details nobody thought were
significant at the time.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the self-sufficiency push never gains real traction, later infrastructure investment goes
disproportionately toward receiving, sorting, and storage capacity rather than toward agricultural or
production capacity — an early resource-allocation pattern, never explicitly chosen, that quietly compounds
across generations.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the city keeps investing specifically in logistics infrastructure rather than production capacity,
it ends up structurally primed, generations later, to pivot that same accumulated infrastructure toward
the specific correspondence-and-archive specialty it eventually becomes known for — not because anyone
planned it that way, but because decades of receiving-and-relay investment left logistics as the path of
least resistance once the postal role finally needed to scale.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Port Lockroy has, without any deliberate top-down plan, accumulated generations of infrastructure
investment oriented specifically toward receiving, sorting, and relaying rather than producing — quietly
laying the physical groundwork for the postal specialization the city will eventually become known for,
built out of nothing more dramatic than never having seriously chased self-sufficiency.
