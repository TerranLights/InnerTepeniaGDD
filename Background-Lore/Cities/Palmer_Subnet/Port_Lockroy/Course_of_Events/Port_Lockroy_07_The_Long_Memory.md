## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Old Toby's wall keeps growing, panel by panel, as an informal, ongoing communal habit — residents adding
to it whenever they're moved to, with no schedule, no rule, and no ceremony attached to any of it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

An informal habit like this can fade if nobody ever makes it deliberate — enough quiet years without a new
panel, and it risks becoming something people used to do rather than something the city still does.
That risk turns into an actual proposal: formalize the practice into a real civic holiday, something with
a fixed date the community can count on, rather than leaving the wall's continued growth to chance and
mood.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement about whether formalizing something
personal necessarily kills what made it personal. Character A wants durability — worried that leaving the
wall's growth entirely to spontaneous impulse means it could simply stop, and an institution with an
actual date attached is more likely to survive than a habit nobody's obligated to keep. Character B
distrusts exactly that impulse — believes the wall has only ever meant something because people added to
it when they genuinely wanted to, not on a schedule, and that turning a personal act into a mandated
annual event would replace real expression with obligatory ceremony, technically preserving the wall's
growth while hollowing out the reason anyone cared about it.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civic organizer type,
motivated by the same institutional-memory instinct behind Founders' Watch, wanting durable structures the
community can rely on. Character B is plausibly an artisan or individualist temperament closer to Old
Toby's own — someone who has personally added to the wall precisely because nobody told them to, and
distrusts anything that would change that.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A holiday does get created — The Long Memory, with panel-adding as its central ritual act — but built with
real flexibility rather than obligation: no rule about who must add a panel in a given year, or what it
must depict, just an open date when the option is there for anyone who wants to take it. Each year's
addition still feels like a real choice, not a chore.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because participation stays optional rather than mandated, it comes out uneven year to year — some years
bring many new panels, others bring few or none at all, a genuine record of how many people actually felt
moved to add something, rather than a uniform count anyone could have predicted from a fixed rule.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That unevenness becomes an unintentional historical record. Residents and, later, anyone curious enough to
look closely at the wall's panel density across different stretches start informally "reading" busier
years against quieter ones — a rough, unplanned chronicle of which years actually mattered to the
community, a use of the wall nobody designed it for and that could only exist because participation was
allowed to vary rather than being made uniform by rule.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Long Memory is now an established, deliberately flexible annual holiday, and the wall itself has
become an unintentional but genuine historical record — its uneven density across the years legible, to
anyone willing to look closely, as a rough chronicle of which years actually mattered.
