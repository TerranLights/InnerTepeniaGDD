## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Old Toby carves the first panels into a stone wall by himself, a personal project he starts already
knowing he'll never finish it alone — work paced to his own hands and his own years, nobody else involved
yet.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A wall built to outlast its own builder needs other hands to actually keep growing once he's gone, and
those hands start arriving — some residents following his example deliberately, others simply drawn to
add something of their own to a structure that's already started to mean something to the city. The
wall stops being one man's project and starts being everyone's, which raises a question nobody had to
answer while it was still just Old Toby working alone: what happens to a shared artifact once nobody's
single vision is guiding it anymore.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement about how much structure a communal
project can take before it stops being communal. Character A worries, reasonably, that a wall carved by
dozens of untrained hands over generations, with no shared plan, will end up an actual visual mess rather
than a meaningful one — wants some coordinating principle: agreed panel sizes, a review of what gets
added where, someone acting as steward of what the wall is supposed to be. Character B worries, just as
reasonably, that imposing any of that turns the wall into something curated and permitted rather than
something people simply choose to add to — that the moment someone has to ask permission, the wall stops
being the thing that made people want to add to it in the first place.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly someone who thinks of
themselves as continuing Old Toby's own intent — perhaps someone who worked alongside him, or simply
someone with a curatorial temperament, uncomfortable watching something meaningful risk becoming
incoherent. Character B is plausibly a resident with no particular claim to stewardship at all, someone
who added a panel purely on impulse and would resent being told there's now a process for that —
grounded in Port Lockroy's own culture of ordinary residents drawn to add something of their own without
being asked.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither formal curation nor total disorder wins. No rule ever gets written down, no steward is ever
appointed — but an unspoken order emerges anyway: new panels tend to land near others they relate to,
newer contributors watch how the wall already reads before adding to it, and the result becomes something
genuinely legible as one continuous project, even though no single person, not even Old Toby, ever saw
the whole of it built or decided how it should look.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the order that emerged was never written down, knowing how to add to the wall properly — where a
panel belongs, what it's reasonable to carve, how to read what's already there before contributing —
becomes something learned only by watching, an informal apprenticeship rather than a posted rule. Newcomers
figure it out the same way everyone else did: by paying attention.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That unwritten apprenticeship quietly becomes a marker of who actually belongs in Port Lockroy long-term,
as distinct from couriers, traders, and other short-term visitors passing through. Knowing the wall's
unspoken etiquette — not just knowing it exists — becomes something residents can recognize in each other
without ever naming it as a test, a form of belonging that couldn't exist if the wall's rules had simply
been written down and posted for anyone to read.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The wall is now a genuine multi-generational communal artifact, legible as one continuous project despite
never having a single steward or a single written rule — and, without anyone intending it, its unwritten
etiquette has become a quiet, informal way Port Lockroy tells its own long-term residents apart from
everyone just passing through.
