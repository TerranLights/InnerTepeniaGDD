## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Rothera's human workers settle into 8–10 hour shifts, while its robot workers run 10–12 hour shifts — two
overlapping but distinct working rhythms across the same industrial city, each population clocking in and
out on its own separate schedule.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A population running on genuinely different schedules risks splitting into two barely overlapping social
worlds — humans finishing their day while robots are still mid-shift, robots finally free while most
humans are already home or asleep. Left unaddressed, the mismatch would quietly sort Rothera's after-work
life into two separate populations occupying the same bars and pubs at different hours rather than
sharing them, with humans and robots meeting mostly at handoff rather than at rest.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over how a bar, pub, or other shared
leisure venue should actually run given two mismatched shift schedules. This isn't a conflict about
prejudice — both sides want the same integrated outcome in principle. A wants to simplify: separate
serving hours, or a venue that specializes in one population's schedule and effects (alcohol on a human
clock, glitch-coolant on a robot one), because trying to serve both populations well across mismatched
hours is genuinely harder to run. B has felt what the alternative costs firsthand — the isolation of
finishing a shift into an empty or unfamiliar room — and is willing to accept the added difficulty to keep
the two populations actually meeting each other at rest, not just at the factory gate.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a bar or pub owner, or
a shift supervisor thinking in scheduling terms, whose objection is practical rather than social — running
overlapping hours for two populations with different needs is more work, more stock to keep on hand, more
complexity for less predictable business. Character B is plausibly a worker — human or robot — who's
actually lived the mismatched-shift isolation and is advocating from that lived experience, not from
policy, for venues to stay open across the overlap regardless of the added cost.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's case wins out, venue by venue. Bars and pubs across Rothera establish a deliberate overlap-hour
practice — staggering their open and close times specifically to catch both shift populations at once,
stocking both alcohol and glitch-coolant as standard rather than specializing in one. Shared leisure time
becomes something residents actively protect, not something that happens to persist.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The practice generalizes past bars and pubs. Civic institutions more broadly — public meetings, parts and
goods distribution, city announcements — start deliberately timing themselves around the same
shift-overlap window, a "catch both populations" scheduling norm baked into how Rothera runs its public
life generally, not just its evenings.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because civic and social life keeps getting timed around the same recurring overlap window, Rothera
develops its own shared vocabulary for it — a local term for "changeover hour" that residents use casually
and newcomers have to learn, a small but genuine piece of local culture that marks who's actually settled
into the city's rhythm and who hasn't yet.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Rothera runs its whole civic and social life around a deliberately engineered overlap window between
human and robot shifts, complete with its own local vocabulary for the practice — genuinely integrated in
daily fact, not merely tolerant in principle.
