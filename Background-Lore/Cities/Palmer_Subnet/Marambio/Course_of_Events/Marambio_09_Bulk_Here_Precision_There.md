## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Marambio and Esperanza both handle real shipping traffic through the same Weddell Sea corridor, their
operations overlapping in the same waters with no settled division of labor between them — two cities,
one corridor, both competing for essentially the same cargo.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The two cities' own infrastructure and civic character point in genuinely different directions — Marambio
built for scale, per its established dual airfield-and-shipyard identity (see Suggestion #4), Esperanza
organized around its family charter with shipping as a secondary function. But as both cities' shipping
volumes grow, that difference stops being a neutral fact and becomes real competitive friction: Marambio's
scale advantages threaten to simply absorb Esperanza's smaller shipping business by underpricing it on
volume, while Esperanza's more specific, documented handling risks being dismissed by comparison as
inefficient — a real threat to the smaller city's shipping role altogether.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Rather than Marambio simply winning by scale, the two sides negotiate an actual formal division through
their respective logistics authorities: Marambio takes bulk, high-volume, undifferentiated cargo; Esperanza
retains smaller-scale, specific, documented shipments — codified as complementary specializations in a real
inter-city agreement, not left as unstated competitive territory that could shift again with the next change
in either city's fortunes.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the division was formally negotiated and codified rather than simply settling out through market
pressure, it becomes an actual charter between the two cities' logistics authorities, with enforceable
expectations on both sides — a genuine institutional relationship rather than the coincidence of two cities
happening not to compete for the same customers.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because it's a formal charter rather than informal habit, disputes over borderline cases — mid-volume,
partially documented cargo that doesn't cleanly fit either city's specialty — now have an actual arbitration
mechanism to run through between the two cities' logistics authorities, rather than reverting to informal
competition every time an ambiguous shipment arises.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Marambio and Esperanza now operate under a formal, codified shipping division — bulk through Marambio,
specific and documented cargo through Esperanza — a durable institutional arrangement that holds precisely
because it was actually negotiated and enforced, generation after generation, rather than merely assumed.
