## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Juan Carlos's own island archive grows increasingly valuable as the Federation matures, but stays physically
isolated and administratively separate from the rest of Tepenia's growing infrastructure — indispensable,
and entirely exposed on an island with no highway access and no protected interior fallback.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A young nation building its first real communications network has real reason to consolidate its most
important records somewhere more centrally protected, and the same wave of institutional capability-building
that produces the Arcanet puts the question squarely on the table: should Tepenia's founding archive stay
where it was built, or move somewhere the new national infrastructure can actually defend it? The archive's
own staff and the Federation's new infrastructure planners don't automatically agree on the answer.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a dispute between someone protective of Juan Carlos's
own stewardship of the archive it built and raised, and someone pushing for centralization as part of the
Arcanet-era infrastructure build-out. A's concern is not merely sentimental — an archive administrator who
has spent a career building the institution's careful, trusted culture (see Suggestion #7) has real reason
to doubt that a newly-built interior facility can replicate it. B's concern is equally legitimate — an
island archive with no highway access and no hardened protection is a genuine strategic liability the longer
the Federation depends on it. Neither side is being unreasonable; they're weighing stewardship against
security and landing on different answers.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Juan Carlos-based
archive administrator or senior archivist, personally invested in the institution's local culture and wary
that relocation means losing control built up over a career. Character B is plausibly a national-level
Federation planner or engineer working on the Arcanet's construction, for whom consolidating the country's
most important records centrally is simply the same infrastructure logic already producing the communications
network — redundancy and protection, not a judgment on Juan Carlos itself.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Centralization wins, but not as a wholesale seizure: around the same broader wave of institutional
capability-building that produces the Arcanet, Tepenia's scattered early records are copied — not simply
removed — and relocated to a single, better-protected central archive deeper in the continent's interior.
Moving the practical function elsewhere doesn't automatically move the meaning it carried for the city that
built it.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the transfer is a copy rather than a seizure, Juan Carlos keeps a smaller, working secondary archive
of its own — enough to keep handling the Machu Picchu Border & Customs Authority's day-to-day local business
without routing every routine question through the new inland site.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that retained secondary function stays live and active rather than purely ceremonial, it's exactly
what gives Upper Earth an actual, current reason to still treat a quiet island city as an intelligence
target centuries after the primary archive function moved inland — the ongoing local function, not any
lingering claim to being the original site, is what the eventual deliberate strike is aimed at.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Juan Carlos's national archive role formally relocates inland alongside the Arcanet's construction, losing
its literal recordkeeping primacy without losing the civic identity that role had already given it. The city
keeps a smaller, working archive of its own — a residual function that, centuries later, is exactly what
still makes it worth destroying.
