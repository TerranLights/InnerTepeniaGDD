## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The earliest settlers at newly-inherited Esperanza Base face the same question every multinational
Tepenian city eventually confronts: whose culture, whose language, whose civic template gets to define
the place they're building together.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Esperanza's founding population turns out to include people from too many different backgrounds for any
one nation to plausibly claim the answer — Argentine heritage sits alongside American, Brazilian,
Mexican, and British arrivals, none large enough on its own to set the civic tone by simple numbers.
Rather than let that stalemate settle by default into whichever group organizes fastest, the earliest
settlers land on a different basis for belonging entirely: not nationality, but sacrifice. Any human
whose family gave up everything — home, career, standing, an entire national identity — to follow robots
into exile has a claim on what the city exists to protect, regardless of where they came from. It's a
genuinely elegant answer to the abstract problem. But an abstraction this clean is also dangerously easy
to leave abstract — a fine sentiment voiced in a founding meeting that never quite translates into who
gets housing priority, whose case gets heard first when resources run thin, or what the principle
actually costs anyone to honor once it stops being free to declare.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation over how literally the sacrifice
principle should be read, forced by a real allocation dispute the abstract version of the promise can't
settle on its own. One side wants the principle honored broadly and literally — every exile who gave up
everything has a claim, full stop, regardless of age or circumstance. The other side wants it narrowed
onto something a young settlement can actually deliver on consistently. This shouldn't read as generosity
versus stinginess — the broad reading is the more faithful one to what was actually promised, and the
narrow reading is the only version of the promise a settlement this new can actually keep without it
collapsing into empty rhetoric the first time two claims compete for the same scarce resource.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a member of the
founding exile generation itself, or someone speaking for them — someone with direct, earned standing to
insist the promise cover them too, since they're the ones who actually paid its price. Character B is
plausibly an early civic organizer or proto-administrator handed the unglamorous job of running the young
settlement day to day, who has already watched the abstract principle fail to resolve a real dispute and
needs something concrete enough to defend the next time it happens.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The principle survives, but not in the shape either side first imagined it: it crystallizes specifically
around children. Protecting them — not adjudicating every exile's competing claim on the abstract promise
— becomes the literal, practical, enforceable shape the founding principle actually takes.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With protection formally narrowed onto children, the settlement needs some concrete mechanism for making
that promise real and checkable rather than another well-meant abstraction. The earliest, roughest version
of what will eventually become Esperanza's birth registry takes shape here — less designed than
improvised, the first tool anyone builds to turn "children are protected" into something with an actual,
countable form.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the registry, not the original abstract sacrifice principle, becomes the thing that actually gets
maintained and pointed to, Esperanza's civic identity starts organizing itself around a repeatable
practice — an act performed for every child born — rather than around a static founding fact anyone could
recite once and then forget. Generations later, almost nobody remembers the specific allocation dispute
that forced the narrowing; what survives, and what actually gets reproduced, is the registry itself as
civic ritual.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Esperanza's founding compact is no longer a contested abstraction — it has a concrete beneficiary
(children) and a concrete mechanism (the registry) for making itself real. The broader, more literal
reading of the original promise recedes into honored civic memory rather than active policy — the
founding exile generation is revered for what it gave up, but the promise's living, enforceable edge now
runs through their children and grandchildren instead of through them directly.
