## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early construction on Sejong's own territory happens somewhat haphazardly, each new structure added
wherever space allows, with no citywide plan yet governing where anything goes.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Once the island's one genuinely shared, neutral landmark — the combined lighthouse-and-signal spire —
stands finished and visible from every community's own footholds, builders finally have something real to
orient around. Sejong's layout starts gradually organizing itself in relation to that shared point,
boundary zones settling into place around it like spokes meeting at a hub rather than scattering
arbitrarily. But a layout this organic takes generations to fully settle into a legible shape — for a long
transitional period, the city is half-haphazard and half-radial at once, with newer construction following
the spire's logic while older structures, built before the spire even existed, sit awkwardly athwart where
a clean spoke would otherwise run.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a dispute between forward-looking city planning and
an established resident whose already-standing structure now sits in the way of a clean radial layout that
didn't exist when they built. A wants new construction to follow the spire's orienting logic consistently,
which sometimes means asking existing structures to reroute paths, reorient entries, or otherwise
accommodate a scheme invented after they were already built. B resents being made retroactively "wrong" by
a plan that came later than their own home did — their structure was perfectly reasonable when it went up,
and now it's treated as an obstacle. A's vision is genuinely good for the city's long-term legibility; B's
grievance is genuinely legitimate — nobody should lose their home to a plan invented after the fact.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a builder or early
planner (possibly connected to the same trade suggested in "Nowhere to Spread") pushing for a more coherent
citywide layout organized around the spire. Character B is plausibly a longtime resident or family whose
own holding — built well before the spire went up — sits squarely in the path of what would otherwise be
a clean spoke.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Rather than forcing existing structures to be altered or torn down, the plan bends around them instead —
new construction follows the spire's radial logic, but older "legacy" structures are grandfathered as
irregular rather than erased. The result is an organic, not perfectly geometric, radial pattern: a wheel
with real bends and detours built into it from the start.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the geometry accommodates rather than erases these legacy irregularities, Sejong's streets and
passages end up full of small odd bends, dead ends, and unexpected shortcuts around older grandfathered
buildings — a distinct, memorable, visibly lived-in spatial texture rather than a clean engineered wheel.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the layout is full of these idiosyncratic bends rather than a clean grid, it becomes genuinely
difficult for outsiders to navigate without a local guide — turning basic wayfinding into something
longtime residents take a quiet, specific pride in, and giving rise to an informal custom of residents
personally walking new arrivals through the city's shortcuts rather than simply pointing them toward a map.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sejong's built geometry radiates loosely from the lighthouse-spire at its hub, but never as a clean wheel —
a patchwork of legacy irregularities bent around by later, more organized construction, navigable by
instinct to residents and nearly opaque to outsiders, a lived-in complexity that's become a quiet source of
belonging rather than a planning failure.
