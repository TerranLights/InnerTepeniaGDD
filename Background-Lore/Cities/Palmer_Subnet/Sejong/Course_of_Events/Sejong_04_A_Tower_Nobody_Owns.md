## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

King George Island's dense harbor traffic needs reliable maritime signaling — a practical requirement
every coastal settlement on the island shares equally, and one none of them can fully solve alone.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Building that signal tower under any single national community's exclusive control risks turning a shared
necessity into a contested asset — whoever builds it, staffs it, and controls its light effectively
controls a piece of infrastructure every ship approaching the island depends on. The obvious answer is to
build it as neutral, shared infrastructure instead. But agreeing that it should be neutral doesn't answer
who actually pays for it, who supplies the labor, or who staffs it once it's built — and on an island where
"neutral" projects have a way of quietly becoming whoever-built-it's projects, that question turns out to
matter as much as the tower itself.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a dispute over construction and control that
persists even though both sides already agree the tower should be neutral in principle. A, representing
Sejong's own community (by now the island's largest and most resourced), argues Sejong should take the
lead on building it — practically, Sejong has the labor and materials to get it done fastest, and delay
serves no one. B, representing one of the smaller footholds, is wary precisely because of that resource
imbalance: infrastructure built and staffed predominantly by the island's largest community has a way of
becoming that community's asset regardless of what anyone agreed on paper. Neither side is acting in bad
faith — A's urgency is genuine and B's suspicion is grounded in exactly the kind of soft asymmetry that
doesn't need bad intentions to produce real imbalance.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an engineer or civic
organizer from Sejong's community, focused on getting a genuinely needed structure built without letting
process become an excuse for delay. Character B is plausibly a representative of a smaller foothold (a
Russian or Uruguayan community works well) with direct experience of larger neighbors' good intentions not
always translating into equal outcomes, insisting on structural safeguards rather than trusting goodwill
alone.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two sides settle on a joint-contribution model: every island community contributes labor, materials,
or funding in proportion to its size, and staffing rotates among the contributing communities rather than
defaulting permanently to whoever built the most. The tower gets built more slowly than A wanted and with
more oversight than A thought necessary, but with guarantees concrete enough that B's community signs on
willingly rather than reluctantly.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The joint-contribution model becomes the reference case every island community points back to whenever a
new piece of shared infrastructure gets proposed afterward — not because anyone declares it a template,
but because it's the one concrete precedent everyone already lived through and already trusts.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that precedent already exists and is already trusted, later joint projects get proposed and
negotiated in a fraction of the time the tower itself originally took — communities no longer have to
build the case for proportional contribution and rotating control from scratch each time, since the tower
already proved it works.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The lighthouse-and-signal spire stands over King George Island's harbor, jointly funded and jointly
staffed — visible from every community's own footholds, claimed exclusively by none, and now the island's
standing proof that shared necessity really can produce genuine cooperation, not just uneasy tolerance,
even among communities otherwise competing for the same limited ground.
