## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Years of informal, ad-hoc boundary-zone negotiation have finally produced something residents want to mark
formally — the original land-use and resource-sharing agreements that made coexistence on King George
Island physically workable in the first place.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Commemorating a practical agreement risks quietly implying that the deeper social tension it never
actually resolved — the island's real, ongoing competition for its most favorable ground, still visible in
the patchwork of shared-use arrangements residents live with every day — was settled too. A civic
committee forms to design a holiday marking the original agreements, and the design question turns out to
matter as much as the holiday itself: whether to let the celebration read as a broad, uncomplicated
triumph of unity, or to hold it deliberately narrow, honoring only the specific practical success and
nothing more.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a drafting dispute over what a civic holiday is
allowed to claim. A wants the new holiday to be a full, unambiguous celebration of harmony — good for
morale, good for how the city presents itself, a clean and inspiring founding story worth telling
children. B refuses to let it overclaim, insisting the holiday name only the specific, verifiable
achievement (the original land-use and resource-sharing agreements) and stop well short of declaring the
island's deeper social competition resolved. A's instinct isn't dishonest — cities benefit from stories
that unify them — but B's insistence on precision is what keeps the holiday from becoming a comfortable
myth nobody actually believes a generation later.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civic-image-minded
committee member or public figure, invested in giving the city a positive, morale-building story. Character
B is plausibly a boundary-zone liaison or someone still personally living inside one of the island's
unresolved shared-use arrangements (echoing the ongoing terrain competition), who knows exactly how much
still isn't settled and won't let a holiday paper over it.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The committee adopts B's narrower framing. The Neighbors' Accord is instituted as a holiday honoring the
specific historical land-use and resource-sharing agreements — deliberately not framed as a "unity" or
"harmony" holiday, and explicit about what it does and doesn't claim to celebrate.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the holiday's official framing stays narrow and factual rather than aspirational, its actual
observances — what gets said, performed, and taught to children about it — stay grounded and specific
across generations, rather than drifting into a comfortable, sanitized founding myth the way looser,
more triumphant holidays elsewhere tend to.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the holiday keeps transmitting an honest, specific memory rather than a myth, later generations of
Sejong residents grow up already knowing, matter-of-factly, that the island's coexistence was a real,
negotiated achievement rather than easy natural harmony — leaving them noticeably more clear-eyed and less
naive than residents of cities whose founding stories were flattered into simpler unity myths, when they
eventually have to negotiate disputes of their own.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Neighbors' Accord stands as Sejong's most honest civic holiday — a yearly, deliberately unglamorous
commemoration of one specific practical achievement, keeping every new generation of residents clear-eyed
about exactly what their city did, and didn't, ever fully resolve.
