## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Sejong's own Korean founding population dilutes steadily as later immigration reshapes the city's wider
demographic balance — by now, Korean residents are a small minority among the many nations sharing the
city's civic life.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The city's own name and civic identity trace directly back to King Sejong the Great's creation of
Hangul — a heritage worth preserving regardless of how small a share of the population still carries it
personally. Hangul instruction and cultural continuity persist deliberately as a result, carried forward
by dedicated educators even as the founding nation's own numbers keep shrinking relative to the growing
multinational whole. But sustaining a heritage tradition for a shrinking founding population takes real,
ongoing commitment from people increasingly outnumbered by newer arrivals — and as Sejong's municipal
government starts allocating its limited civic resources across a growing number of competing community
needs, Hangul instruction's claim on that budget stops being automatic.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a budget and standing dispute, not a hostile one — an
educator fighting to keep Hangul instruction funded and staffed as civic resources get reallocated toward
newer, larger communities' own more pressing needs, against a civic administrator who has to weigh limited
resources across many legitimate community interests and reasonably asks why one shrinking founding
population's heritage program deserves protected status other, larger communities' own programs don't get.
A's case rests on the city's own namesake and founding identity; B's case rests on plain fairness across a
city that, by this point, genuinely belongs to more than one nation's descendants. Both are right about
something the other is missing.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The administrator doesn't fund Hangul instruction as an ordinary discretionary community program — instead,
it gets granted protected status tied specifically to the city's own founding charter and namesake,
distinguishing it from any one community's private heritage program. The distinction matters: it survives
the budget dispute not because Korean residents can outvote or outspend anyone, but because the city's own
name makes the case for them.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because it's protected through the city's own charter rather than ordinary discretionary funding, Hangul
instruction becomes a small, genuinely secure institution — a dedicated school or academy with guaranteed
standing — rather than a vulnerable program that has to re-justify itself every budget cycle.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the institution is framed and funded as belonging to the whole city rather than to one shrinking
community, children from other backgrounds start attending too, out of civic curiosity and pride rather
than heritage — Hangul literacy spreads modestly beyond the founding population that originally fought to
keep it alive, becoming a point of general civic identity precisely because it was protected as civic
rather than communal.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Hangul instruction endures in Sejong not as a shrinking community's private inheritance but as a small,
secure civic institution belonging to the city itself — smaller in scale than its founding-era beginnings,
genuinely maintained, attended by a demographic wider than the population that first fought to protect it,
never treated as obsolete just because the numbers backing it have changed.
