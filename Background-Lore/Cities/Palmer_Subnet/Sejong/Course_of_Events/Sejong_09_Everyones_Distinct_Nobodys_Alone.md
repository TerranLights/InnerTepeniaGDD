## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Generations after being folded into Sejong's civic structure, each absorbed community still holds its own
food, language, and custom — but a resident of one of these communities could easily feel like a guest
inside someone else's city, tolerated rather than truly belonging, if that balance ever stops being
actively maintained.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Sejong's founding logic never organized itself around one dominant culture assimilating everyone else, so
each community is expected to retain real, visible distinctness while still participating fully in the
city's shared civic life. But holding that balance requires constant, deliberate effort from a city that
could easily default to picking a single dominant identity instead — and as generations pass and the
municipal government looks for ways to run more efficiently, a recurring pressure builds to simplify: one
shared civic calendar instead of a dozen parallel ones, one common working language instead of several,
consolidation framed as plain administrative sense rather than as anyone's cultural agenda.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a dispute over consolidation that isn't framed by
either side as an attack on identity — A, a civic administrator or pragmatic newer-generation figure, argues
in good faith that maintaining a dozen parallel languages, calendars, and customs indefinitely is genuinely
inefficient and that some shared common ground would serve the whole city better. B, a cultural keeper from
one of the absorbed communities, argues that "efficiency" consolidation is assimilation wearing better
language — that the entire reason nobody in Sejong has to feel like a guest is precisely because nobody has
ever been asked to simplify who they are for the city's convenience. A isn't a villain and B isn't
paranoid: administrative consolidation really would save resources, and it really would, in practice, erase
exactly the distinctness the city has spent generations protecting.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a municipal
administrator or budget officer, several generations removed from the original absorption, genuinely
focused on running the city well rather than on cultural politics. Character B is plausibly an elder or
cultural keeper from one of the neighborhood councils established generations earlier (a Polish or Czech
community elder works well), personally responsible for maintaining exactly the customs now being
questioned as inefficient.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The consolidation proposal is defeated, but not simply dismissed — the debate ends with an explicit,
deliberate re-affirmation: resources for maintaining each community's distinct language classes, holidays,
and neighborhood-council standing get formally re-funded as a permanent civic commitment, not a
discretionary nicety a future administration could quietly cut. The city chooses the harder, costlier path
again, this time on the record.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the commitment gets explicitly re-affirmed rather than simply defended informally, it gets written
into Sejong's own civic charter as a formal amendment — converting what had been inherited founding-era
habit into a codified obligation binding on every future administration, not just this one.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the commitment is now codified rather than merely customary, a future administrator can no longer
quietly deprioritize community programs the way earlier administrations might once have been tempted to —
any future attempt at consolidation now requires an actual charter amendment, a far higher and far more
visible bar, meaning this exact debate can recur, but never again gets settled quietly through simple
administrative discretion.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sejong keeps choosing the harder path, generation after generation, but no longer purely by informal habit
— it's written into the city's own charter now: plural without being fractured, unified without demanding
sameness, a codified promise future administrations can argue against but can no longer simply ignore.
