## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

King George Island's comparatively mild climate — by the standards of the Palmer Subnet — makes certain
stretches of ground genuinely more desirable than others: better sun exposure, less wind, easier building,
easier daily life.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

With a dozen communities sharing one small island, favorable ground is never abundant enough for everyone
to simply have their own share of it, and a real, ongoing competitive tension settles in around exactly
the terrain everyone wants most. It never erupts into anything dramatic — no single incident large enough
to demand a citywide reckoning — but it also never goes away. Different households and communities keep
pressing quiet, low-grade claims on the same handful of especially favorable sites, each testing how far a
claim can go without provoking open conflict, generation after generation.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a specific, concrete dispute over one especially
favorable plot — a sunny, wind-sheltered terrace good for building or growing food — claimed by two
parties with comparably legitimate stakes. This shouldn't be framed as one side being greedy and the other
being wronged: both parties want the same ordinary things (a better place to live, to grow food, to raise
a family) and neither has a claim clearly superior to the other's. The point of this conflict isn't to
produce a winner — it's to produce a specific, repeatable *kind* of resolution the island can live with
indefinitely, since the underlying scarcity driving it will never actually go away.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a member of Sejong's
own founding community with an ordinary, practical need for the plot (housing expansion, a small garden
plot). Character B is plausibly a member of one of the smaller absorbed footholds (an Argentine or Chilean
household works well) with an equally ordinary, equally legitimate claim — arrived early enough, or worked
the ground long enough, to have a real stake nobody can simply dismiss.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither household gets exclusive use. The dispute is resolved through a rotating or split-use arrangement
— the plot shared on a schedule, or physically divided in a way that gives neither side everything it
wanted. It's an unsatisfying resolution to both parties individually, but a stable one: nobody has to
accept defeat, and nobody gets to claim outright victory either.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Shared or rotating use, rather than exclusive award, becomes the default remedy Sejong's informal civic
apparatus reaches for whenever a comparably contested claim comes up afterward — not just over land, but
over any genuinely scarce, genuinely desirable resource two parties both have a real stake in.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because shared-use resolution becomes the standing default rather than an exception, no single family or
community ever manages to accumulate the island's most desirable ground outright, generation after
generation — a quiet, structural check on exactly the kind of spatial wealth concentration that a city
awarding contested resources outright would eventually produce.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sejong and its neighbors settle into an uneasy but functional equilibrium: the island's sunniest, most
favorable ground remains, generations later, a patchwork of shared-use and rotating-use arrangements
rather than anyone's exclusive property — real, unresolved competition for the best ground, managed well
enough that daily life and shared infrastructure keep working regardless.
