## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Sejong's exile community settles on King George Island alongside nearly a dozen other national
communities, all sharing the same small, crowded landmass. There is no surrounding territory to grow
into — whatever ground each community holds now is close to whatever ground it will ever hold.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The island simply doesn't have room for any of these communities to sprawl the way cities elsewhere in
Tepenia do, so Sejong starts building vertically instead — dense, stacked construction rather than
spreading outward into territory that doesn't exist. But building up rather than out changes the nature
of every structural decision: a new floor added to a building near a shared edge doesn't just change that
building, it changes the light, the sightlines, and the wind shadow falling on whatever sits beside it.
On a landmass this crowded, "beside it" usually means another community's own territory. Vertical growth
that would be a purely internal decision anywhere else becomes, on King George Island, a decision that
reaches past Sejong's own boundary every time.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a dispute over a specific vertical expansion —
someone in Sejong wants to add height to an existing structure near one of the island's boundary zones,
and someone from the adjacent community objects because the addition would fall across their light, their
sightline to open water, or their own future room to build. Neither side is being unreasonable: A has a
genuine, pressing need (housing pressure that has nowhere else to go but up), and B has a genuine, prior
claim (a neighbor's quiet enjoyment of ground they were there first to hold). The conflict shouldn't
resolve as one side simply winning — the island's whole later civic character depends on this kind of
dispute becoming routine and survivable rather than a one-time crisis either community remembers as a
grievance.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a builder or a young
family head from Sejong's own community, driven by ordinary housing scarcity rather than any grand
ambition. Character B is plausibly an equivalent figure from one of the smaller adjacent footholds (a
Czech, Polish, or Uruguayan household, for instance) with a comparably ordinary, comparably legitimate
stake in the ground now falling under a taller neighbor's shadow. Neither needs institutional standing yet
— this is meant to read as two households working out a problem no authority above them has solved for
them.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The dispute gets settled directly between the two households, through the same kind of improvised,
face-to-face negotiation King George Island's crowding has already been forcing on everyone — a
compromise on the building's final height and window placement, worked out without appeal to any higher
authority because none yet exists to appeal to. It isn't the first such settlement, and it won't be the
last, but it's specific enough and public enough that other builders start citing it as precedent.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Other builders planning their own vertical additions start informally consulting affected neighbors
before construction rather than after, treating the settled dispute as the expected first step rather than
a last resort — an unwritten but increasingly consistent local custom for how tall building gets done on
shared ground.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because pre-construction consultation becomes the expected custom rather than an exception, a small
class of residents starts to specialize in it — people other builders start asking to help negotiate the
neighbor conversation before breaking ground, simply because they've done it well before. Sejong doesn't
yet have a formal office for this, but it already has, in practice, a recognizable trade: people whose
actual skill is building tall without stepping on whoever happens to be standing beside them.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sejong's skyline keeps climbing, but no longer as a purely internal matter — every meaningful addition to
the built environment now carries an informal expectation of neighbor consultation behind it. Verticality
and negotiation have become, in practice, the same problem solved twice: the same crowding that forces the
city to build up is the crowding that forces it to keep talking to whoever that height falls beside.
