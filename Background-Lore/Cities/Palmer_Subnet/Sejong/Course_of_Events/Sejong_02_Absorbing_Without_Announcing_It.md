## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Several smaller national footholds dot King George Island alongside Sejong's own growing settlement —
each with its own small population, its own modest cluster of buildings, its own sense of being a
distinct community rather than a neighborhood of something larger.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

None of these smaller footholds ever grows large enough to sustain full independent city status the way
Sejong does — not from any single failure, just the plain arithmetic of a small island where one
settlement inevitably outgrows the rest. As Sejong becomes the island's largest, most organized
settlement, the practical case for these smaller communities' populations folding into its civic structure
starts to build: shared services, shared infrastructure, shared standing when dealing with the rest of the
Federation. But absorbing multiple distinct populations without simply erasing what makes each one
distinct takes real, deliberate accommodation — the kind no one on either side has actually worked out yet
when the question first gets raised in earnest.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a negotiation, not a takeover — someone from
Sejong's growing civic apparatus making the practical case that formal incorporation serves everyone's
interests (shared infrastructure, shared standing, real administrative capacity none of the smaller
footholds can build alone), against someone from one of those smaller communities who fears that "folding
in" is a polite word for disappearing. A shouldn't be cast as an annexationist and B shouldn't be cast as
irrationally stubborn: A's practical case is genuinely sound, and B's fear is genuinely earned — small
communities that get absorbed into larger ones do, historically and often, lose exactly the kind of
self-determination B is trying to protect.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early civic
organizer from Sejong's own founding community — practical-minded, focused on what the city needs to
function at scale rather than any particular cultural agenda. Character B is plausibly an elder or
respected figure from one of the smaller footholds (a Czech or Uruguayan community elder works well),
someone who remembers when their own community stood on its own and is negotiating specifically for the
terms under which it would agree to stop.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The smaller footholds agree to incorporation, but only on negotiated terms: each retains a guaranteed,
recognized neighborhood identity within Sejong's civic structure — its own name, its own customs, its own
standing — rather than being absorbed as undifferentiated population. Incorporation happens community by
community, not as one blanket annexation, each accepting comparable but separately negotiated terms.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because incorporation happened as a series of negotiated agreements rather than a single decree, Sejong's
municipal government ends up with a standing structure to formalize what those agreements actually
guaranteed — a system of neighborhood councils, one per absorbed community, each with a recognized voice in
citywide decisions that touch their own territory or customs.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the neighborhood-council system already exists once boundary-zone disputes and shared-infrastructure
questions start coming up in earnest, those later negotiations run through an established channel instead
of starting from nothing each time — councils that were created to protect each community's distinct
identity end up doubling, almost by accident, as the standing machinery Sejong's later diplomatic culture
gets built on top of.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sejong now functions as something close to a federation in miniature — one municipal government sitting
atop a half-dozen neighborhood councils, each representing a formerly independent foothold, each retaining
real, visible distinctness while sharing fully in the city's civic life. The city holds genuine internal
diversity most Tepenian cities never develop: not one population, but several, sharing one civic address
without ever fully merging into one.
