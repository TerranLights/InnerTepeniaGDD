## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The Falkland Treaty is signed, formally establishing Tepenia's exile terms. It is a legal fact, on paper,
in a room far from Anvers Island — not yet a place, not yet a population, not yet anything a person could
stand inside.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Waiting for further instruction, or for a more organized, centrally planned settlement effort, would mean
losing the momentum of the moment itself — the treaty is signed, and the people it concerns are already
here, already capable, already unwilling to spend one more day in a holding pattern. A group of robots,
human partners, and human ideological supporters simply begins building the same day the treaty is
signed. Palmer City is founded not out of careful planning but out of people who refused to wait even one
day longer than they strictly had to. That refusal solves the problem of momentum — but it creates a much
larger one: founding a city on pure momentum means every other question a settlement needs answered —
governance, resource allocation, who speaks for the community, how disputes get settled — has to get
figured out after the fact, live, with people already standing on the ground, rather than decided in
advance by anyone with the luxury of distance.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over how quickly the fledgling
settlement should impose formal structure on itself. One side sees the absence of any charter, any
governing document, any settled rule for how decisions get made, as a dangerous vacuum — the longer it
goes unaddressed, the more likely it is that whoever simply acts first, loudest, or most confidently ends
up setting precedent nobody actually agreed to. The other side sees premature formalization as its own
danger — writing rules down now, days or weeks into a settlement built entirely on momentum and refusal
to wait, means freezing in place decisions nobody has had time to actually think through, imposed on a
population that came here specifically because it wouldn't accept being told what to do by people who
weren't in the room. Neither side is wrong to worry; the conflict is genuinely between two real risks, not
between a reasonable position and an unreasonable one.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a human ideological
supporter with some organizing or political background before the exile — someone instinctively reaching
for structure because they've seen what happens to movements that don't build one in time. Character B is
plausibly a robot exile or one of the human partners who chose exile for a specific relationship rather
than an abstract cause — someone with less patience for formal process and more trust in the people
actually standing in the settlement together, wary of anything that looks like the old order's habit of
deciding things about robots' lives on paper, in rooms robots weren't in.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No charter gets written. Character B's side doesn't win an argument so much as it wins by attrition —
every attempt to convene a formal drafting process gets outpaced by the settlement's own daily needs,
and after enough weeks of ad hoc decisions actually working well enough to keep the city functioning, the
appetite for imposing structure from above quietly evaporates. Palmer City's whole later character forms
retroactively, shaped by the people already living there rather than planned in advance by anyone.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With no founding charter to point to, Palmer City's earliest civic decisions — who gets access to which
salvaged building, how disputes over shared resources get settled, what counts as acceptable conduct in a
population still relearning what a community even is — get resolved by custom and remembered precedent
instead of by written law. Whoever was present for a given decision the first time it came up becomes the
person others ask when it comes up again.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because precedent lives in people rather than in documents, a small, informal class of trusted
old-timers emerges — not elected, not appointed, simply accumulated authority through having been present
and remembered correctly often enough. Newer residents learn to bring disputes to these particular people
long before Palmer City has anything resembling a formal office to bring them to instead. This wouldn't
have happened from the founding-on-momentum decision alone; it specifically requires that the city's early
governance stayed customary rather than codified.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Palmer City runs on inherited custom and personal trust rather than any founding document, with a loose,
informally recognized class of old-timers functioning as the city's real early arbiters — moral authority
accumulated through memory and presence, not through office. The city's whole later identity, when it
eventually does take clearer shape, will be built on top of this habit of letting practice lead and
structure follow, rather than the other way around.
