## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early settlers, cataloguing the original Palmer Station buildings for anything usable, find a cache of old
jazz records left behind by whoever worked there before the exile. They are, for the moment, simply
objects — fragile, unplayed, without a player, a venue, or an established audience to make sense of them.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Records with no player, no venue, and no established audience could easily have stayed exactly what they
are — a curiosity nobody gets around to acting on, filed away with everything else salvaged and not yet
useful. Someone plays them anyway, for the whole young settlement, within the first year or two of
founding — an impromptu, informal gathering with no planning behind it beyond a working record player and
enough people willing to stop and listen. It becomes something residents want to repeat. But repeating a
single lucky discovery is its own kind of risk: a settlement this young has more urgent things to spend
its limited time and attention on than recreating a one-off good night, and there's no guarantee a second
attempt lands the same way the first one did by accident.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over whether the settlement should
treat the records as something to protect and catalogue, or something to actually use, worn grooves and
all. One side has grown attached to the records as irreplaceable founding-era objects — the only physical
trace of whoever worked the original station before them — and wants them handled carefully, played
rarely if at all, and properly archived before anything else happens to them. The other side sees the
records' entire value in being played, right now, for the people who need exactly the kind of unplanned
joy that first informal night produced — a settlement this new has more use for one more good night than
for a perfectly preserved artifact nobody gets to hear.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early
archivist-minded settler — someone who has appointed themselves, informally, the keeper of whatever the
settlement salvaged, protective of anything that can't be replaced. Character B is plausibly someone who
was actually in the room the first night the records played, who felt directly what that gathering did
for a settlement otherwise consumed by survival logistics, and wants that feeling made available again
rather than sealed behind careful preservation.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side gets a clean win, but the records keep getting played — not because Character B's argument
prevails in any formal sense, but because the demand for it simply keeps showing up, gathering after
gathering, whether or not anyone with authority sanctions it. The informal gathering becomes the seed of
"Needle Down," Palmer City's own defining musical holiday, later drawing on jazz, rockabilly, and
electroswing alike.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Needle Down grew out of sustained, unauthorized repetition rather than a single sanctioned
decision, nobody ends up formally owning it — there is no city office, no single family, no institution
that gets to call it theirs. But the original records themselves are, by this point, showing real wear,
and Character A's earlier worry about preservation stops being abstract and becomes urgent: the
settlement's cultural custodians begin the work of carefully cataloguing, copying, and safeguarding the
founding-era originals specifically so the tradition built around them can keep going without destroying
the thing it started from.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That preservation effort, undertaken specifically to protect records already worn from years of Needle
Down use, becomes the earliest working model for how Palmer City treats its own history generally —
carefully kept, actively used, never locked away purely for its own sake. The habit of active civic
archiving, rather than passive storage, traces specifically to the records needing protection *because*
they'd already become beloved, not merely to their having been found in the first place.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Palmer City has an annual, ownerless, genre-expanding musical holiday grown from grassroots repetition
rather than decree — and, because that holiday demanded it, the beginnings of a genuine civic habit of
preserving its own founding-era artifacts for continued use rather than simple storage.
