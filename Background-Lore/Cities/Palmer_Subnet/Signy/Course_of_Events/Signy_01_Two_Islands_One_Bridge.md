## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Signy's earliest settlers arrive to find their site naturally divided between two landmasses: small
Signy Island, home to the settlement's one usable shipping dock, and much larger Coronation Island
rising beyond it. The two islands sit close together, but nothing yet connects them — every crossing
between the dock and the rest of the site has to happen by boat, weather permitting.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A settlement split this way, with no reliable way to move people and goods between its two halves,
risks never becoming one functioning city at all — a dock island doing all the working and a
residential island doing all the living, with only unreliable boat traffic holding the arrangement
together. That risk becomes concrete enough to act on: leadership commits to building a bridge, and
the project goes ahead, engineered to carry residents from where most of them will actually live, on
Coronation Island's more livable ground, across to where the daily work is, on Signy Island's dock.
But choosing one bridge, rather than a fleet of small boats or several smaller crossings, means
committing the whole city's daily rhythm to a single structure. If it ever fails, there is no second
way across.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over how to solve the two-island
problem, surfacing once the bridge project is already underway or newly proposed. This shouldn't be
framed as one side wanting connection and the other wanting isolation — both sides want the city to
function as one place. The disagreement is about risk: is it better to build one single, strong,
permanent crossing that the whole city can depend on, or to preserve some redundancy — a maintained
boat crossing, a second smaller bridge, anything that means one structural failure can't cut the city
in half? A wants the decisive, unified solution; B wants insurance against exactly the vulnerability a
single bridge creates.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the bridge's own
lead builder or a founding-era administrator, someone thinking in terms of getting the city functioning
as fast and as solidly as possible, for whom a single well-built crossing is the obvious, efficient
answer. Character B is plausibly someone whose daily work already depends on the water separating the
islands — a dockworker, a boat operator, someone who has seen firsthand how unpredictable Scotia Sea
conditions can be, and who distrusts putting the city's whole daily function in one place that could
ice over, wash out, or simply fail.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The single bridge wins out — not because B's worry is dismissed, but because the city genuinely can't
spare the labor and material to build and maintain two crossings when it's still establishing itself.
The bridge goes up as the one connection, and from the day it opens it becomes, immediately, one of
Signy's most vital pieces of infrastructure: the literal, physical link holding the city's two halves
together as a single functioning whole.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the whole city now depends on one structure holding, the concession Signy actually makes to
B's original worry isn't a second crossing — it's vigilance. A standing watch-and-maintenance roster
forms around the bridge itself: residents take turns inspecting it, clearing ice and storm debris, and
reporting any sign of wear, treating the crossing as something the whole community actively looks
after rather than a piece of infrastructure the city can simply assume will hold.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That roster, born specifically out of watching over the bridge, becomes Signy's first real model for
organizing collective labor around anything the whole city depends on — a habit of thought, not just a
bridge-specific practice. When the city later has to build and sustain other pieces of infrastructure
nobody outside can help maintain, the instinct to organize a shared roster rather than leave the job to
a few specialists is already there, established, and unremarkable.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Signy functions, for the first time, as one connected city rather than two separated halves —
Coronation Island's residents crossing the bridge to Signy Island's dock and back as an ordinary daily
rhythm. The bridge itself is watched over by a standing community roster, and the habit that roster
represents — organizing shared labor around whatever infrastructure the whole city can't function
without — is now simply how Signy solves problems like this one.
