## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Signy's earliest daily rhythm follows the same wildlife cycles the surrounding waters and coastline
already run on — fishing seasons, breeding cycles, weather windows — the settlement's schedule shaped
entirely by conditions nobody in the city controls.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A growing settlement could easily decide to impose something faster and more standardized on top of
those natural rhythms instead — fixed hours, a uniform calendar, the kind of schedule a city elsewhere
might adopt simply because that's what growing cities do. That possibility becomes a live question once
Signy is established enough to actually consider it: keep pacing daily life by what the water and the
wildlife are doing, or start running the city by the clock the way busier places do.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement between imported efficiency and
local practical wisdom, one that shouldn't be played as obviously one-sided. The side pushing for a
standardized schedule has a real point: predictable hours make trade, coordination, and outside
relationships easier to manage, and an unhurried civic rhythm can genuinely look, from outside, like a
city simply falling behind. The side defending the wildlife-paced rhythm has an equally real point: the
fish and the weather don't care what a clock says, and forcing artificial schedule onto a resource that
runs on its own cycles doesn't just look impractical, it risks actively working against the very
resource the city depends on.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly someone oriented
toward Signy's outside relationships — a trade contact, a visiting administrator, or a resident who has
spent time in busier, more standardized cities and worries Signy looks unserious by comparison.
Character B is plausibly a fisher or elder whose whole working knowledge is built around the wildlife
cycles themselves, someone who can point to specific, concrete ways that ignoring those cycles wastes
labor or damages the resource the city can't survive without.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's practical case wins out. The community lets the wildlife cycles set the pace regardless of how it
looks to anyone passing through — work and rest genuinely paced by the island's own natural timing
rather than any imported urgency. When the unhurried civic rhythm reads, to visitors, like the whole
place is simply behind, Signy never bothers correcting the impression. Nobody here is in a real rush,
and residents feel no need to prove otherwise to anyone passing through.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the decision to prioritize wildlife cycles was made deliberately rather than left as informal
habit, Signy formalizes it: a civic calendar built around breeding seasons and weather windows rather
than fixed months, used for planning fishing effort, platform maintenance, and civic gatherings alike —
an actual working document, not just an unspoken cultural preference.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that calendar is now formal rather than informal, anyone doing business with Signy from outside
— trade contacts, supply ships, visiting officials — has to learn to plan around it rather than
expecting Signy to adapt to theirs. The city that looks, from outside, like it's simply behind ends up
quietly setting the terms of its own external relationships: Signy's timing, not the visitor's, is what
any real dealing with the city has to work around.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Signy runs on a formalized, wildlife-keyed civic calendar rather than any standard schedule, and the
unhurried reputation that once looked like the city was simply falling behind has become, structurally,
a source of quiet leverage — outside visitors and trade partners adapt to Signy's timing, not the other
way around, whether or not they ever notice that's what they're doing.
