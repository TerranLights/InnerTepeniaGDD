## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Signy's founding population independently develops a genuine biological-science focus studying the same
South Orkney Islands marine and terrestrial ecology the real-world BAS station once studied — a
convergence with that station's own historical mission, not an inheritance of it, since nothing of the
original program survived the centuries between its end and the 2564 founding.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A small, remote community has real limits on how much ambitious ongoing research it can sustain
indefinitely — labor, attention, and resources that could just as easily go toward fishing,
infrastructure, or anything more immediately survival-critical. Dr. Naledi van Zyl-Osei, whose own
background draws directly on Signy's uniquely South African founding-wave presence, takes up the
marine-and-terrestrial biology mission anyway, refusing to let it lapse simply because the community
around her is still establishing itself. But sustaining serious research in a city this isolated means
working almost entirely without the equipment, funding, and outside collaboration a better-connected
city's scientists could draw on.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over whether the community can
actually afford to keep funding and staffing research that doesn't yet produce anything the city can
eat or build with. This isn't science-versus-ignorance — the opposing side isn't hostile to research on
principle, it's worried about a small settlement's genuinely finite labor being spent on something
whose payoff, if any, is a long way off. Naledi's side of the argument is that abandoning the mission
now would mean losing something irreplaceable — both scientific continuity with what the founders came
from, and knowledge about the very waters the city's whole fishing economy depends on. The resolution
has to make both sides' priorities survive the compromise, not just declare one of them right.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Naledi herself, or a
role very like hers — a founding-era researcher whose case for continuing rests as much on identity and
continuity as on practical justification. Character B is plausibly a council figure or community
organizer responsible for allocating the settlement's still-scarce labor, not opposed to science itself
but accountable for making sure the fishing dock and the wind-and-hydro system get staffed before
anything more speculative does.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two sides settle into a working arrangement rather than either side simply losing: the research
continues, but reshaped to run lean and embedded directly in the community's existing daily work,
drawing its data from the fish already being caught, the waters already being worked, rather than
requiring imported equipment or dedicated survey time the city can't spare. Naledi's research develops
its own resourceful, self-reliant character — genuine science, conducted with whatever the island
itself actually provides.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the research is now built directly out of the fishing economy's own daily catch and effort
rather than run as a separate academic pursuit, it starts producing findings the fishing crews can
actually use — early patterns in fish population health, seasonal movement, and breeding cycles that
feed back into where and when the community fishes at all. What began as a concession to keep the
mission alive turns into a genuine practical partnership between Naledi's research and the crews she
once had to justify her funding against.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that practical partnership takes hold, the biological research station stops being treated as a
standalone institution that happens to sit in Signy and becomes something closer to civic
infrastructure in its own right — its findings routinely briefed into the community's fishing and
resource decisions, its continued operation no longer something each new generation has to re-argue
from scratch. The station's later confirmed status as fully intact and operational owes something
specific to this: it survived not just because nothing struck it, but because by the time anything
could have, the city had long since stopped seeing it as separable from its own survival.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Signy's biological research mission endures, generations on, not as an inherited relic kept alive out
of sentiment but as a working part of how the city manages its own waters — resourceful, self-reliant
science whose findings the fishing economy has come to depend on as much as the research depends, in
turn, on the fishing economy's daily catch.
