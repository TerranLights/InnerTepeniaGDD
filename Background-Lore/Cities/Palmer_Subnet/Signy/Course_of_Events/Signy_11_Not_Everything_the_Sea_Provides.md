# Signy — Not Everything the Sea Provides

still-unused Newfoundland outport caution: "marine self-sufficiency, while currently working, isn't

## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Signy's fishing economy runs reliably, extended out to sea by Declan Ferreira-Whitcombe's floating
platforms (chain #5), paced by wildlife cycles residents have long since stopped questioning (chain #9).
The Scotia Sea's own unusual richness has always simply provided — a foundation residents trust as close
to permanent, without ever having had real reason to test that trust.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A specific season, one of the fish populations central to Signy's own catch, declines sharply and without
warning — no storm, no accident, no human failure, just the ordinary unpredictability of a wild
ecosystem the city had quietly started taking for granted.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** not a conflict about whether the lean season is
real — both sides can see the numbers. Character A wants it treated as a genuine wake-up call: Signy's
whole civic identity assumes the sea's reliability, and a city this totally dependent on one source needs
some real contingency now, before a lean season becomes something worse. Character B isn't dismissive of
the concern, but genuinely believes this reads as ordinary fluctuation rather than a real pattern, and
that building serious contingency infrastructure for every possible lean season would spend resources and
attention the city doesn't yet have real evidence it needs.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a fishing-platform
worker or a civic planner unsettled by how completely the city's daily life depends on one unverified
assumption. Character B is plausibly a longtime fisher or elder confident in decades of the sea's own
observed reliability, wary of overreacting to a single hard season.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither full contingency infrastructure nor simple dismissal wins outright. The city adopts something
modest instead — a small stored reserve and a deliberately wider spread of species targeted across the
floating platforms, a real but bounded hedge against exactly this kind of unpredictable decline, not a
wholesale reorganization of the fishing economy.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The lean season passes; the fish populations recover on their own. But the small hedge stays in place
anyway, quietly, rather than being dismantled once the immediate worry fades — a permanent, modest
precaution absorbed into how Signy simply operates from then on.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Residents' own understanding of their self-sufficiency shifts, quietly but genuinely — from an
unexamined assumption that the sea always provides enough, to a more honest, lived understanding that it
provides enough as long as nobody assumes it always will.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Signy's marine self-sufficiency continues, still genuinely real and still the backbone of daily life —
but now carries a permanent, modest acknowledgment, built directly into how the city fishes, that the
sea's abundance was never actually guaranteed. The same confidence as before, just a quieter and more
honest version of it.
