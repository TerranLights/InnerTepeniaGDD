## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Signy's remote, gale-exposed position offers no realistic possibility of a connection to any wider
power grid — no cable is ever reaching this far south, and nothing about the city's geography is going
to change that. Whatever electricity Signy has, it will have to generate for itself.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A growing settlement still needs reliable power for its docks, its radio equipment, and its homes, so
the city commits to building a local generation system matched to its own conditions rather than
waiting on a connection that will never arrive — wind and hydro, drawing on the same near-constant
gales that make the island so exposed in the first place, rather than the sunlight a lower-latitude
settlement might lean on instead. But a system this small, this isolated, and this singular has no
outside backup if any part of it fails. There is no larger grid to fall back on if Signy's own
generation falters — whatever the wind and water provide is, functionally, all there is.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over who actually runs the system
once it exists, once the abstract engineering problem of "how do we generate power" becomes the
concrete political problem of "who is responsible for it working." One side wants the system built and
operated by a small group of trained specialists — safer, more efficient, easier to hold accountable if
something breaks. The other side wants operation and upkeep spread across the whole community — slower
to train up, but far more resilient, since the system's survival doesn't then depend on a handful of
specific people being available, healthy, or willing. Neither side is wrong about the trade-off;
they're weighing efficiency against exactly the kind of single-point fragility a system this isolated
can't actually afford.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the system's own
chief engineer or designer, someone who has just spent real effort getting the wind-and-hydro system
built correctly and understandably wants it kept in careful, expert hands going forward. Character B is
plausibly a community organizer or council figure who has watched what happens elsewhere in Signy when
critical infrastructure depends on too few people, and who argues that a system nobody outside the city
can help repair needs redundancy built into its operators, not just its hardware.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The community model wins out, but not by simply overruling the engineer — the compromise keeps trained
specialists at the technical center of the system while building a wider rotation of residents trained
in its basic upkeep around them, closer to Samsø's real-world community-ownership precedent than to a
purely expert-run utility. Maintaining the system becomes, from that point on, a genuine and ongoing
civic priority — something the whole community treats as its own responsibility to sustain, not
something it can quietly hand off to a few people and stop thinking about.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because upkeep training gets spread deliberately across the wider population rather than concentrated
in a small technical class, Signy develops something it didn't have before: a rotating civic education
program, ordinary residents cycling through basic training in how their own power system works, what to
watch for, and how to make small repairs, alongside whatever their main daily work actually is.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That broadly distributed technical literacy, once established, becomes a resource Signy can draw on
for problems that have nothing to do with the power system itself — residents who've already learned
to read a wind turbine's warning signs or troubleshoot a hydro line bring the same instincts to the
city's other exposed, high-maintenance infrastructure, whether that's the bridge, the dock, or, later,
the fishing platforms extending out into the Scotia Sea. Signy ends up with an unusually technically
capable population for its size, not because it set out to build one, but because keeping the lights on
demanded it.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Signy generates its own reliable power through a community-run wind-and-hydro system, its upkeep
genuinely shared rather than concentrated in a few hands. That shared responsibility has left the city
with a wider base of practical technical skill than a settlement this small and this isolated would
otherwise have — a resource the city hasn't yet had reason to draw on for anything beyond the power
system itself, but now quietly possesses.
