## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Robert Falcon Scott's 1902 expedition hut sits inside the city's own limits, one of the oldest surviving
human-made structures on the continent — respected by residents, but with no formal arrangement yet in
place for who actually looks after it day to day.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A structure that significant needs someone actually tending it, not just left to weather on its own — and
as minor, preventable deterioration starts becoming visible (a failing seal, an unaddressed structural
concern), it becomes clear that general civic goodwill isn't the same as anyone actually taking
responsibility. A resident who cares deeply about the hut's condition wants to take that responsibility on
personally, but Scott's civic leadership has to decide whether to formalize the role, fund it, or simply
let one dedicated resident handle it informally.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a low-heat but genuine conflict between a resident
volunteering to take personal responsibility for the hut's upkeep, and a civic administrator who wants to
formalize the role with real institutional backing — funding, defined authority, official recognition —
rather than letting something this significant depend entirely on one person's unpaid goodwill. The
volunteer isn't refusing help out of stubbornness; they may genuinely worry that formalizing the role
means civic bureaucracy second-guessing decisions about a site they already understand intimately. The
administrator isn't distrustful of the volunteer specifically; institutionalizing responsibility for
something this precious is simply sound civic practice, protecting the site from depending on any one
person's continued willingness or survival.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the figure later
remembered as, or a direct predecessor to, Keeper of Hut Point Wiremu Tane, someone with a personal,
almost proprietary attachment to the site. Character B is plausibly a civic administrator responsible for
Scott's modest but real institutional resources, wanting to protect the hut properly rather than leave its
fate to one individual's continued commitment.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A compromise settles the matter: the role goes to the volunteer, exactly as they wanted, but without the
full institutional apparatus the administrator originally proposed — informal personal commitment,
acknowledged and respected by the city, rather than a funded civic office with defined bureaucratic
authority.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The Keeper role carries real responsibility with no formal institutional backing behind it, just personal
commitment — the volunteer maintains the site between observances, making sure its condition never becomes
an afterthought, entirely on their own initiative and judgment.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the role was never institutionalized, its continuity depends entirely on someone choosing,
generation after generation, to take it up the same informal way — which means the Keeper position becomes
something Scott's civic culture has to actively notice and pass on rather than something a bureaucracy
simply reassigns, giving the role an unusual, personal weight that a formally appointed successor might
never carry in quite the same way.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Keeper position becomes one of Scott's own quietly essential civic roles — unglamorous, unpaid in any
grand sense, and absolutely necessary to keeping the city's most sacred site intact, sustained purely by
whoever chooses, each generation, to carry it forward.
