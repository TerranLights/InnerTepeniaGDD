## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Covered connections and heating-pipe conduits thread between Zukelli's buildings, sheltering movement
from the katabatic winds — built the same organic, needs-arose way as everything else in the city
(Suggestion #1), with no citywide plan behind any of it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

With no citywide plan behind any of it, finding your way through someone else's neighborhood means
genuinely not knowing where you are — a resident from one part of the tangled city, needing to reach
another, gets lost badly enough in unfamiliar covered passages that the problem stops being a private
inconvenience and becomes a neighborhood-level concern: something has to help people navigate passages
that look, to an outsider, functionally identical to each other.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a conflict between a resident advocating for a single,
citywide standardized marking system — consistent colors and symbols meaning the same thing everywhere,
learnable once and usable anywhere — and a resident from a specific neighborhood insisting on marking
their own passages their own way, resistant to outside standardization imposed on a space they consider
their own. The standardizer's logic is sound: a single system would genuinely be easier for the whole city
to learn. The neighborhood advocate's resistance is equally sound: Zukelli's neighborhoods already have
real, distinct identities, and a single imposed system would flatten some of that distinctiveness in the
name of convenience nobody in that neighborhood specifically asked for.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a resident or informal
civic figure who travels often between neighborhoods and personally feels the cost of inconsistent
marking. Character B is plausibly a longtime resident of one specific, tightly-knit neighborhood, invested
in that neighborhood's own particular identity and resistant to any citywide standardization effort.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The neighborhood advocate's resistance effectively wins — no citywide standardized system ever gets
adopted — but the standardizer's underlying concern gets addressed anyway, informally: individual
neighborhoods, aware now that inconsistency is a real problem, start each developing and maintaining their
own coherent internal marking convention, even without agreeing on a shared one between them.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Individual neighborhoods start marking their own passages — color, pattern, whatever convention a given
block happens to settle on — an organic, bottom-up system nobody mandates and everybody eventually
understands within their own territory, exactly mirroring the city's own organic growth pattern rather
than imposing order from outside it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the system stayed neighborhood-specific rather than citywide, it only actually works if you
already know somebody who can teach you to read it — no single map or key exists anywhere, which means
newcomers learn Zukelli's geography the same way they learn everything else about the city: through
relationships, not through any signage a stranger could simply follow alone.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Zukelli's neighborhoods each maintain their own coherent, informally understood marking conventions —
readable fluently by residents, meaningless decoration to anyone who hasn't been personally taught to read
them, a navigation system built entirely on relationship rather than any citywide standard.
