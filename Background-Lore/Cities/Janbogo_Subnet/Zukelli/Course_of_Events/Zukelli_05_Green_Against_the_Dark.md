## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Zukelli's engineers face the same katabatic winds and Antarctic dark every neighboring city contends with
— survival infrastructure (heat, shelter, insulation) already built and functioning, keeping the
population alive but offering nothing beyond bare function.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Pure survival infrastructure leaves the city feeling purely functional, nothing like the warmth its
hospitality culture is already building toward through food and music (Suggestions #3 and #4) — and as
that hospitality culture matures, the gap between Zukelli's warm civic life and its cold, purely
utilitarian built environment becomes something residents start actively noticing and wanting to close,
even though closing it means committing real engineering effort to something with no direct survival
justification.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a conflict between an engineer or civic advocate
proposing genuine investment in living green space — plantlines, elevated bridger-footroads, artificial
but genuinely effective — and a more resource-conscious figure arguing that a city already stretching its
engineering capacity on pure survival infrastructure can't justify continuous additional effort on
something as non-essential as decorative greenery. The advocate's case draws directly on the city's own
established hospitality-first values: warmth isn't just food and music, it can be physical too. The
resource-conscious figure's caution is legitimate: maintaining living green space against Antarctic
conditions takes real, continuous engineering effort most cities wouldn't bother with, precisely because
it's expensive to sustain indefinitely, not just to build once.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civic-minded engineer
or hospitality-culture advocate, someone who sees the same instinct behind Elisa's Room or the citywide
food culture as applicable to the built environment itself. Character B is plausibly a resource
administrator or infrastructure engineer, responsible for the city's ongoing survival-system maintenance
burden and wary of adding anything non-essential to it.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The advocate's proposal wins, but scoped and paced deliberately to satisfy the resource-conscious figure's
concern: elaborate plantlines and elevated bridger-footroads get engineered into the built environment
gradually, expanded only as the city's capacity to sustain them proves durable, rather than committed to
all at once.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Residents gain something resembling equatorial nature to walk among, engineered directly into the built
environment — artificial but genuinely effective, giving the city's physical spaces a warmth to match the
warmth its hospitality culture already offers.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the green infrastructure was built and expanded gradually, tied to proven sustainability rather
than one-time investment, maintaining it becomes a genuinely permanent, ongoing civic commitment rather
than a finished project — a standing engineering discipline the city keeps funding indefinitely, turning
basic infrastructure into something closer to a permanent, deliberate contrast with the dark just outside
the heating envelope.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Zukelli commits permanently to sustaining its engineered green spaces against Antarctic conditions — a
continuous, deliberately funded effort most cities wouldn't bother with, chosen specifically because it
matches the same hospitality-first instinct the city already built its whole civic identity around.
