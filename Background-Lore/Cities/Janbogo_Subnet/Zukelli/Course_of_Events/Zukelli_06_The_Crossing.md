## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Zukelli and Janbogo sit close enough across the same bay that visiting back and forth becomes practical
almost from the start — casual, unscheduled contact between the two populations, present from early in
both cities' histories.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Neither city ever formalizes the relationship into anything official — no treaty, no scheduled exchange,
just informal, constant contact — which leaves the relationship's continuation entirely dependent on both
populations simply continuing to choose it. As both cities grow and develop their own increasingly
distinct civic identities (Zukelli's food-and-music culture, Janbogo's hub-and-absorption character), a
real question surfaces on both sides of the bay: does the crossing still matter enough, now that both
cities have their own full, busy civic lives, to keep actively maintaining, or will it simply fade the way
unscheduled, informal relationships between busy communities often do?

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a conflict, more a difference in priority than
outright hostility, between a Zukelli resident who wants to actively invest time and civic effort in
sustaining the crossing — organizing regular visits, personally maintaining the relationship — and a
resident who sees the informal crossing as increasingly redundant now that Zukelli's own civic life
(Suggestions #3 through #5) is rich and full enough to not particularly need it. The invested resident
isn't nostalgic for its own sake; a centuries-deep informal relationship is worth actively protecting. The
skeptical resident isn't dismissive of Janbogo; they simply see the city's own limited civic energy as
better spent deepening what's already thriving at home.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Zukelli resident with
personal ties across the bay — family, friendship, or simply a habit of crossing regularly since
childhood. Character B is plausibly a resident more invested in Zukelli's own internal civic and
hospitality culture, for whom the crossing feels like effort spent elsewhere.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The invested resident's position prevails through sheer accumulated practice rather than formal argument
— enough individual residents keep choosing to cross, casually and repeatedly, that the skeptical
resident's concern about redundancy never gets enough traction to actually reduce the traffic, and the
relationship persists exactly as informally as it started.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

A genuine tradition takes hold over generations: residents crossing casually, visits blurring into each
other until it stops being clear which city is hosting which, sustained purely by individual choice rather
than any civic institution ever formalizing it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the relationship survives purely on continued individual choice rather than institutional
inertia, both cities develop a real, if largely unspoken, awareness that the crossing could stop at any
point if enough residents simply stopped choosing it — which paradoxically makes each individual crossing
feel more meaningful, not less, since everyone involved knows, even if only half-consciously, that the
relationship's entire continuation depends on people like them choosing to keep crossing that bay.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The Crossing becomes something both cities actively sustain rather than a geographic accident — proof
that proximity alone doesn't explain a relationship this warm, only the centuries of choosing to keep
crossing that bay explains it.
