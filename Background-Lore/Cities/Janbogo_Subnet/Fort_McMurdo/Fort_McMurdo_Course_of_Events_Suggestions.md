# Fort McMurdo — Candidate Courses of Events

**Scope note (revised):** all ten chains below are set strictly *before* the Long Night War, during
the Second Interwar Period when Tepenia is a full, functioning country — no war damage, no
post-war/present-day material. What this is: 10 independent candidate But/Therefore chains for how
Fort McMurdo's local culture changed over time, built using `But_Therefore_Lore_Design_Method.md`
against the city's own Megasheet (`Fort_McMurdo_Mega_Init.md`, `Fort_McMurdo_Cross_Reference_Synthesis.md`,
`Fort_McMurdo_Full_Extrapolation.md`). Per the current scope for this line of work, none of these
touch founding or where the city was established — every chain below picks up with the city already
settled and already a working community, and asks what happened to it *after* that, across its
different pre-war eras (early industrial years, and the later, formalized-capital years).

**Nothing here is canon**, none of these are ranked, and several are not mutually exclusive — they
cover different phases and facets of the same pre-war city rather than 10 competing answers to one
single question. A few build directly on proposals already sitting in `Fort_McMurdo_Full_Extrapolation.md`;
since that document is itself explicitly open to revision, treat these as candidate dramatizations of
it, not a locked sequel.

---

## 1. Accountability, Not Power

**Grounded in:** the informal→official capital transition (`Full_Extrapolation` §IX), the "McMurdo
Problem" faction, `Cross_Reference_Synthesis` Finding 3.

Fort McMurdo spends generations as the Federation's indispensable-but-unofficial coordination hub —
real influence nobody can point to on a map or in a census. **But** that very invisibility is what
makes the "McMurdo Problem" faction's resentment so hard to settle: there's no countable amount of
power to negotiate down, only a constant, uneasy sense that the city has more than its share.
**Therefore** other cities start pushing for formal recognition, less to grant Fort McMurdo new power
than to make its existing power visible and answerable. **But** the government keeps declining, since
naming the arrangement formally would also mean formally obligating itself to it. **Therefore** the
standoff persists for decades — until the Amundsen Tower's construction makes national dependency on
Fort McMurdo's output undeniable at exactly the moment the resentment peaks, and formalization finally
happens as the overdue answer to a question the city had been quietly living for generations.

## 2. The Stayed-Behinds

**Grounded in:** founding-era transient, seasonal contract labor (`Mega_Init`, "What It Feels Like").

Fort McMurdo's earliest working population rotates in and out on seasonal contracts — nobody plans to
stay, the whole labor system assumes turnover. **But** the meritocratic culture means a contract worker
who's good at the job can keep getting re-hired indefinitely, and some simply never rotate home.
**Therefore** a second population quietly forms alongside the transient one: people who've been
"seasonal" for twenty years, who never filed the paperwork to make it official because nobody ever
needed them to. **But** when Fort McMurdo's status is formally elevated and its labor force starts
getting counted and regulated properly for the first time, the stayed-behinds don't fit any existing
category — they're neither transient contractors nor recognized permanent residents. **Therefore** a
quiet, unglamorous civic fight over residency status becomes the city's first real internal politics,
splitting "old blood" (people who committed, on paper or not) from newer rotating labor that still
sees the city as a job, not a home — a fault line that shapes how the city writes its own first formal
labor charter once capital status arrives.

## 3. Across the Sound

**Grounded in:** the Calgary-style processing/extraction split (`Full_Extrapolation` §II).

In its early industrial years, Fort McMurdo tries to do everything itself — extraction and processing
both, all on Ross Island. **But** Ross Island's own volcanic basalt turns out to be a poor substitute
for what the exposed bedrock across McMurdo Sound in the Dry Valleys can actually supply.
**Therefore** raw extraction gradually relocates across the Sound, reached by seasonal sea-ice road or
by sea, while Fort McMurdo itself specializes down into pure processing and coordination — a
deliberate division of labor, not an accident. **But** that division creates a real vulnerability: two
sites now depend on each other across an unstable, seasonal crossing. **Therefore**, when the
convergence-era demand spike (see #7) strains the city's own processing capacity to its limit, the
Dry Valleys side — never dependent on the city's own infrastructure — keeps running at full pace
regardless, proving (not by luck, but by a decision made a generation earlier) that the split was
worth the risk.

## 4. The Skeleton and the Skin

**Grounded in:** the old-core-versus-new-expansion construction-standard divide (`Full_Extrapolation` §VIII,
reframed here for a pre-war setting).

The original McMurdo Station-era core is built to the old American Antarctic Program's
extreme-environment engineering standard — heavy, overbuilt, meant to survive decades of Antarctic
stress. **But** as the civilian population grows through the Interwar boom, the residential expansion
built to house it goes up fast and light, because speed and space matter more than durability when
nothing has ever actually tested the difference. **Therefore** two visibly different cities exist
inside one skyline, nobody thinking much about it — until a severe katabatic storm, the worst in a
generation, hits the newer expansion hard enough to buckle exposed structures the old core shrugs off
without incident. **But** rebuilding the expansion to the old standard would cost far more than
rebuilding it the way it always has been. **Therefore** the storm sparks a genuine, ongoing civic
argument about how the city's next growth phase should be built — light and fast, the way it's always
grown, or heavy and slow, the way a design nobody currently practices just proved right.

## 5. Two Flags, One Standard

**Grounded in:** China/USA co-Primary demographics (19.34%/21.69%) and the working-class, meritocratic
culture (`Mega_Init`, "Who Lives Here" and "What It Feels Like").

Fort McMurdo's earliest coordination stations are staffed almost evenly by Chinese and American
personnel, with neither side holding a clear majority. **But** that near-parity means early disputes
over whose engineering standards, whose administrative language, and whose scheduling conventions the
comms stations should run on have no easy majority-rules answer. **Therefore** the two blocs settle,
out of practical necessity rather than idealism, on judging people by output instead of origin — the
fastest, most reliable way to stop refighting the same argument every shift change. **But** that
compromise, adopted to end a specific dispute, outlives the dispute itself and calcifies into
something residents start to actually believe in. **Therefore** the meritocratic, "status earned by
what you could do" culture the city is known for by the Interwar's later years didn't arrive as a
founding ideal — it was a workaround for a stalemate between two nations neither willing to yield to
the other, which nobody remembers being a workaround anymore.

## 6. The Mountain Doesn't Care

**Grounded in:** Erebus's stable baseline volcanic activity and Volcanologist Dr. Hendrik Osei-Larsen's
monitoring program (`Full_Extrapolation` §§III, VI; `Mega_Init`, "What It Feels Like").

Generations of Fort McMurdo residents grow up under Erebus's permanent gas-and-ash plume, treating it
as background weather rather than a threat. **But** that easy familiarity is tested once, hard, when
the monitoring program Osei-Larsen built registers a real spike in seismic activity — not an eruption,
but close enough that evacuation planning has to move from theoretical to actual for the first time in
living memory. **Therefore** the city has to decide, under real time pressure, how much of its
famous calm is genuine competence and how much was just never having been tested. **But** the spike
recedes without an eruption, and Erebus settles back to its familiar baseline rumble. **Therefore**
the incident becomes local legend rather than local trauma — proof, retroactively, that the city's
unbothered relationship with a live volcano was earned readiness all along, not naivety, even though
everyone who lived through it privately knows how close that verdict came to going the other way.

## 7. Ash and Ice: The Convergence Boom

**Grounded in:** Fort McMurdo's industrial output feeding both Concordia's dome infrastructure and the
Amundsen Tower simultaneously (`Cross_Reference_Synthesis` Finding 1).

For most of its industrial history, Fort McMurdo supplies one major project at a time, at a pace it
can plan around. **But** the Amundsen Tower's construction and Concordia's own infrastructure boom end
up drawing on the city's output at the same time, and Fort McMurdo's processing capacity was never
sized for two national-scale projects at once. **Therefore** the city faces its first real
precedence crisis: which project's shipment goes out this month, and who gets to decide. **But**
neither the Tower's government backers nor Concordia's own administration is willing to formally
concede priority to the other, leaving Fort McMurdo's own logistics coordinators to make the call
themselves, project by project, shipment by shipment. **Therefore** the coordination stations that
were already the operational heart of the city's "de facto capital" status become something closer to
a shadow arbitration body — quietly deciding, through scheduling alone, which of Tepenia's two biggest
concurrent undertakings gets built faster.

## 8. The Huts on the Hill

**Grounded in:** Cape Evans and Cape Royds as heritage sites near the city's industrial footprint
(`Full_Extrapolation` §V, reframed here for a pre-war setting).

As Fort McMurdo's industrial footprint grows through the boom years, expansion planners eye Cape Evans
and Cape Royds — flat, useable, close ground — as the obvious next sites for extraction or storage
infrastructure. **But** a formal preservation push (never previously named in the record, proposed
here as its own event) blocks the expansion, arguing the Heroic Age huts are worth more to Fort
McMurdo's own claimed history than the square footage is worth to its industry. **Therefore** the
city's growth gets rerouted around the huts rather than through them, at real, measurable cost to
expansion efficiency the planners resented at the time. **But** that same rerouted-around geography
also keeps the huts genuinely accessible and intact through the rest of the industrial boom.
**Therefore** a decision that once looked like sentiment costing the city money turns into an
unplanned asset instead — a small, steady stream of heritage-minded visitors and researchers drawn to
two of Antarctica's oldest human structures, sitting an easy walk from an otherwise all-business city.

## 9. The Comms Charter

**Grounded in:** the coordination stations as the operational backbone of Fort McMurdo's "de facto
capital" status (`Mega_Init`, "What It Feels Like"), and the informal→formal capital transition (#1).

For generations, the dedicated comms stations that coordinate supply, extraction scheduling, and
inter-city logistics run on nothing but accumulated practice and mutual trust — no charter, no
formal authority, just a system everyone's learned to rely on. **But** as Fort McMurdo's influence
grows toward the undeniable dependency that eventually forces national capital status (#1), other
cities start asking pointed questions about who actually authorized the coordinators making these
calls in the first place. **Therefore** the city drafts something it never needed before: a formal
charter defining exactly what authority the comms stations hold and over whom. **But** writing that
charter means admitting, on paper, just how much informal power the coordinators had already been
exercising for years. **Therefore** the charter ends up less a grant of new authority and more a
belated confession of authority that was already real — a smaller, internal rehearsal for the
national-level formalization still to come.

## 10. The Unnamed Ones

**Grounded in:** the two placeholder Notable Figures and the systems they're each credited with
building (`Full_Extrapolation` §III).

Logistics Coordinator Amara Ferreira-Novak formalizes the comms-station network; Volcanologist Dr.
Hendrik Osei-Larsen builds the Erebus monitoring program — both systems so well-designed they keep
running smoothly for decades after either of them retires. **But** exactly because the systems work
so well without anyone actively minding them, the tacit judgment behind each one — why the schedule
works the way it does, what a seismic reading that's slightly off actually means versus one that's a
real warning — quietly stops being taught to anyone, since the systems themselves never seem to need
it. **Therefore** by the time of the convergence-boom precedence crisis (#7) and the near-miss
eruption scare (#6), both hitting within a few years of each other, the city discovers how much
inherited habit has replaced living expertise. **But** surviving both crises makes the gap
impossible to keep ignoring. **Therefore** a deliberate mentorship program gets built in their
aftermath — the city's first real attempt to write down not just what the systems do, but why, before
the people who still remember are gone too.

## Worth Your Attention

**#1, #3, and #4** are the most load-bearing of the ten — they take proposals already sitting in
`Full_Extrapolation` (capital timing, the Dry Valleys split, the old-core/new-expansion divide) and
give each one an actual causal mechanism instead of leaving it as a stated fact, which is the whole
point of running the Megasheet through this method rather than just reading it. **#6, #7, and #10**
form a natural late-Interwar cluster — the near-miss eruption and the convergence-boom precedence
crisis landing close together in time, with #10's succession-gap thread using both as the pressure
that finally forces the city to fix it.
