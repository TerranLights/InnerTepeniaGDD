## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

For most of its industrial history, Fort McMurdo supplies one major national project at a time, at a
pace its processing capacity can plan around — demand arriving in a manageable, sequential rhythm the
coordination stations have long since learned to schedule for.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The Amundsen Tower's construction and Concordia's own infrastructure boom end up drawing on Fort
McMurdo's output at the same time, and the city's processing capacity — even with the Dry Valleys split
(Suggestion #3) already in place — was never sized for two national-scale projects running concurrently.
The city faces its first real precedence crisis: which project's shipment goes out this month, and who
actually gets to decide. Neither the Tower's government backers nor Concordia's own administration is
willing to formally concede priority to the other, since both projects are, in their own right, too
important to accept second place.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a representative pressing
Fort McMurdo's coordination stations for Tower priority and a representative pressing for Concordia
priority, with the city's own logistics coordinators caught in between, formally answerable to neither
side's chain of command. This should read as two urgent, legitimate national priorities genuinely unable
to both go first, not as one side being greedy or the other overreaching — the Tower represents the
nation's whole energy future, Concordia's boom represents its largest single population center's
continued growth, and Fort McMurdo itself has no formal authority yet to simply rule on the matter (that
authority is exactly what Suggestion #1's capital formalization will later grant it).

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Tower-project
liaison, dispatched from the national government's own infrastructure office, carrying the weight of
the country's biggest single undertaking. Character B is plausibly a Concordia procurement or
infrastructure administrator, representing a city whose own growth can't simply pause while the Tower
gets built. Both are institutional outsiders to Fort McMurdo itself, which sharpens the bind: the actual
decision keeps landing on Fort McMurdo's own coordination-station staff, who never asked to be the ones
adjudicating between two national priorities neither of which they control.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither backer ever formally concedes, so the decision defaults, shipment by shipment, month by month,
to Fort McMurdo's own logistics coordinators — the people who actually run the schedules — since nobody
else is positioned to make the call in real time. The coordination stations start making the calls
themselves, deciding project priority through scheduling alone rather than through any formal ruling
either side would recognize as binding.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The coordination stations that were already the operational heart of the city's "de facto capital"
status become something closer to a shadow arbitration body — an authority nobody granted them on paper,
exercised constantly and consequentially, deciding which of Tepenia's two biggest concurrent
undertakings gets built faster, entirely through routine scheduling decisions nobody outside the stations
themselves ever formally reviews.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because that shadow-arbitration authority is being exercised constantly, visibly, and consequentially by
people with no formal charter behind it, it becomes the single most concrete example other cities point
to when pushing for Fort McMurdo's coordination role to be formally recognized (Suggestion #1) — not the
city's general reputation for influence, but this specific, ongoing spectacle of unelected coordinators
deciding, shipment by shipment, which national project moves faster. The convergence boom doesn't just
strain capacity; it becomes the concrete evidence the "McMurdo Problem" faction needed.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Fort McMurdo's coordination stations now openly function as a de facto arbitration body over national
infrastructure priority, an authority everyone can see being exercised and nobody has yet formally
sanctioned — the exact condition that, combined with the Tower's own completion, finally forces the
government's hand toward making the arrangement official.
