## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Fort McMurdo's earliest working population rotates in and out on seasonal extraction-and-processing
contracts. Nobody plans to stay — the whole labor system, inherited from McMurdo Station's own seasonal
staffing habits, assumes turnover as the default. A worker arrives, works the contract, rotates home.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The city's meritocratic culture — status earned by output, not origin — means a contract worker who's
simply good at the job keeps getting re-hired, contract after contract, with no one ever formally
questioning why. Some workers, quietly, never rotate home at all. A second population forms alongside
the transient one: people who've been "seasonal" for ten, fifteen, twenty years, who never filed the
paperwork to make their presence official because nobody, including the workers themselves, ever needed
them to. For as long as Fort McMurdo stays informal — indispensable but uncounted, per its own
established de facto-capital status — this costs nothing. Nobody audits residency in a city the
government itself declines to formally recognize. But the pressure that eventually forces Fort McMurdo's
capital status into the open (Suggestion #1) doesn't just formalize the city's external standing — it
brings, for the first time, a real census and a real labor registry, an apparatus built to count
precisely what used to go uncounted.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a stayed-behind worker whose
entire adult life in Fort McMurdo has no paper trail to prove it, and an administrator newly tasked with
building the city's first accurate labor registry, who needs a clean, countable category for every
resident and finds the stayed-behinds fitting none of them. This shouldn't read as cruelty versus
sympathy — the administrator isn't hostile to the stayed-behinds personally, they're accountable to a
new bureaucratic mandate that has no box for "technically seasonal, functionally permanent, twenty years
running." The stayed-behind isn't a victim of malice either; they're the predictable product of a system
that rewarded competence without ever asking anyone to formalize their place in it. The conflict is
structural, not personal: two reasonable positions colliding because the informal system that used to
absorb this contradiction quietly no longer exists.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a stayed-behind
worker — someone whose entire claim to belonging in Fort McMurdo rests on years of re-hired, re-proven
competence rather than any document, and who has genuine reason to fear that a new, formal system built
around paperwork will simply erase what informal trust once recognized. Character B is plausibly a
newly-appointed registry administrator or census officer, tasked with converting Fort McMurdo's entire
informal labor history into countable categories on a deadline, personally invested in the registry
working cleanly rather than in any particular outcome for any one worker. Either could plausibly be
human or robot; nothing in the founding population's demographics privileges one over the other.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The dispute doesn't resolve case by case — it becomes a genuine, unglamorous civic fight over residency
status itself, the city's first real internal politics. Two rough camps form: "old blood," people who
committed to Fort McMurdo whether or not they ever filed the paperwork to prove it, and newer rotating
labor that still, honestly, sees the city as a job rather than a home. The fight is settled not by a
court ruling in any one worker's favor, but by the city drafting its first formal labor charter — one
that recognizes continuous re-hire history itself, not filed residency paperwork, as the legal basis for
permanent status.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The labor charter's "continuous re-hire equals residency" standard becomes the template Fort McMurdo
reaches for every time some other newly formalized civic question needs a rule — most directly, it's the
precedent cited a generation later when the city drafts the comms-station charter defining who actually
holds authority over inter-city logistics decisions (Suggestion #9): output-based standing, proven over
time, counts for more than any originating document ever did.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because "prove it through years of demonstrated output" becomes Fort McMurdo's default answer to
questions of legitimacy across multiple unrelated civic domains — labor status, then coordination
authority — the city develops a quiet, generalized suspicion of any claim to standing that rests on
credentials or title alone rather than on a track record anyone can actually verify. New arrivals with
impressive formal qualifications but no local history find themselves, informally, on probation in a way
the charter never wrote down but everyone in the city understands.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Fort McMurdo now has a formal labor charter distinguishing "old blood" from rotating contract labor by
demonstrated history rather than paperwork — the city's first real experience translating an informal,
trust-based practice into codified civic law, a rehearsal for the far larger formalization still to come
when national capital status arrives. The old-blood/rotating-labor fault line remains a quiet, ongoing
feature of the city's internal culture, not fully resolved so much as formally acknowledged.
