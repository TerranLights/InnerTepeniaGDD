## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Fort McMurdo has spent generations as the Federation's indispensable-but-unofficial coordination hub —
real, load-bearing influence that nobody can point to on a map, an org chart, or a census. The city runs
the logistics that keep half the country moving, and holds no formal title for doing it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That very invisibility is what makes the resentment around it impossible to settle through ordinary
negotiation. The "McMurdo Problem" faction's grievance has no countable amount of power to bargain
down — only a constant, diffuse sense that the city has more influence than its official standing
accounts for. That diffuse resentment starts to organize: other cities begin pushing for Fort McMurdo's
coordination role to be formally recognized, less to grant the city something new than to make what it
already has visible and answerable. The government, for its part, keeps declining — naming the
arrangement formally would mean formally obligating itself to it.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone who wants Fort
McMurdo's informal power named and made accountable, and someone with a real stake in keeping the
arrangement exactly as unaccountable as it currently is. This shouldn't play as a simple
reformer-vs.-obstructionist morality tale — the government's refusal is structurally rational (naming
the arrangement means owning it), and the McMurdo Problem faction's grievance is real even though there's
nothing concrete to point to. A good version of this conflict makes both sides legible: A wants the
ambiguity resolved because the ambiguity itself is the injury; B wants the ambiguity preserved because
resolving it means accepting a liability nobody's currently on the hook for.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a delegate or
representative carrying another city's "McMurdo Problem" resentment — someone whose frustration is with
the *structure*, not with Fort McMurdo's people personally, since none of this is McMurdo's own fault.
Character B is plausibly either a national-government administrator (defending the government's interest
in not formally obligating itself) or a Fort McMurdo coordination-station figure who has, consciously or
not, built their own standing on the informality itself and has something real to lose if it's
formalized and audited. Either version works; whichever is chosen changes whether this cycle's conflict
reads as inter-city politics or as an internal city character study.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The standoff persists for decades with no clean resolution — until the Amundsen Tower's construction
makes the entire Federation's dependency on Fort McMurdo's output undeniable, at exactly the moment the
accumulated resentment peaks. Formalization finally happens: Fort McMurdo is granted national capital
status, not as a reward, but as the overdue, structurally forced answer to a question the city had been
quietly living inside for generations.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Formal capital status brings Fort McMurdo an entire layer of bureaucratic apparatus it never had to build
or staff while its authority ran on accumulated trust and practice alone — offices, charters, and formal
chains of accountability replacing what used to be informal habit.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That new apparatus becomes, in turn, the thing future disputes over Fort McMurdo's authority get
litigated through — arguments that used to be settled (or simply endured) through quiet, personal
negotiation between coordinators now have a formal process to run through instead, for better and for
worse.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Fort McMurdo is now the Federation's formally recognized capital — its once-invisible coordination role
fully codified, its power finally countable. But the very act of making it countable created a new kind
of friction the informal era never had: authority that used to run on trust now runs on process, and not
everyone who thrived under the old system is equally suited to the new one.
