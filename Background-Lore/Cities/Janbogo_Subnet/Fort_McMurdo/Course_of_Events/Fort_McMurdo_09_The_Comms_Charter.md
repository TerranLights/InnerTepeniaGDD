## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

For generations, the dedicated comms stations that coordinate supply, extraction scheduling, and
inter-city logistics run on nothing but accumulated practice and mutual trust — no charter, no formal
authority, just a system everyone across the subnet has learned to rely on without ever having formally
agreed to it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

As Fort McMurdo's influence grows toward the undeniable dependency that eventually forces national
capital status (Suggestion #1) — sharpened further by the coordination stations' own visible, unelected
arbitration role during the convergence boom (Suggestion #7) — other cities start asking pointed
questions about who actually authorized the coordinators making these calls in the first place. The
question isn't hypothetical anymore; multiple cities have, by now, had a shipment or a schedule decided
against their interest by a coordinator answerable to no charter at all.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between an outside city's delegate
demanding to know the legal basis for the comms stations' authority and a senior coordinator who has
been quietly exercising that authority for years, competently, without ever having had to justify it
formally. The delegate isn't wrong to ask — informal authority this consequential really does need
accountability — but the coordinator isn't a villain hoarding power either; they inherited a system built
on trust because trust was, for a long time, genuinely sufficient. The tension is between a system that
worked precisely because nobody examined it too closely, and the moment examining it becomes
unavoidable.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a delegate from
another Janbogo subnet city (or one of the cities on the losing end of a convergence-boom scheduling
decision), pressing the question on behalf of a constituency that's started to feel the coordination
stations' informal power directly. Character B is plausibly a senior comms-station coordinator, someone
who has genuinely earned their standing through the output-over-origin meritocratic culture
(Suggestion #5) and now has to defend, for the first time, an authority they never had to formally
justify before.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The city drafts something it never needed before: a formal charter defining exactly what authority the
comms stations hold and over whom, submitted for review by the other cities whose logistics the
stations coordinate — the first time Fort McMurdo puts its own operational power into writing rather than
simply exercising it.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Writing the charter means admitting, on paper, just how much informal power the coordinators had already
been exercising for years — scheduling authority, tie-breaking authority, effective veto power over
which city's shipments move first, all of it now itemized rather than left as ambient, unexamined
practice.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the charter itemizes powers nobody had previously had to name out loud, it ends up reading less
like a grant of new authority and more like a belated confession of authority that was already real — and
that written confession becomes the single clearest piece of documented evidence the "McMurdo Problem"
faction cites when pushing, not long after, for the city's coordination role to be formally recognized
at the national level (Suggestion #1): a smaller, internal rehearsal that inadvertently supplies the
paper trail for the larger formalization still to come.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Fort McMurdo now operates under its own written comms charter — its informal power converted, for the
first time, into an itemized, reviewable list — and that same document becomes Exhibit A in the national
argument still building toward making the city's whole standing official.
