## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Logistics Coordinator Amara Ferreira-Novak formalized the comms-station network; Volcanologist Dr.
Hendrik Osei-Larsen built the Erebus monitoring program — both systems so well-designed they keep running
smoothly for decades after either founder retires, requiring, on the surface, no active stewardship at
all.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Exactly because the systems work so well without anyone actively minding them, the tacit judgment behind
each one — why the schedule works the way it does, what a seismic reading that's slightly off actually
means versus one that's a genuine warning — quietly stops being taught to anyone, since the systems
themselves never seem to need it. Newer staff learn to operate the machinery of each system without ever
learning the reasoning the founders built into it. By the time of the convergence-boom precedence crisis
(Suggestion #7) and the near-miss eruption scare (Suggestion #6), landing within a few years of each
other, the city discovers, under real pressure both times, how much inherited habit has quietly replaced
living expertise.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation, surfacing in the aftermath of both
crises, between a veteran who still remembers working alongside Ferreira-Novak or Osei-Larsen directly
and a younger staffer who has only ever operated the inherited system procedurally, without the founders'
underlying reasoning. This shouldn't be framed as the old guard being right and the new guard being
careless — the younger staffer did exactly what the job asked of them, competently, using the only
training they were ever actually given; the gap is institutional, not personal. The veteran's frustration
is real, but so is the fact that nobody, veteran included, ever built a formal mechanism to pass the
tacit knowledge down before now.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a retired or
near-retired figure who trained directly under Ferreira-Novak or Osei-Larsen, someone whose own expertise
is genuinely irreplaceable and who is only now realizing how little of it ever got written down.
Character B is plausibly a current comms-station coordinator or monitoring-station technician, competent
and conscientious, whose confidence during the crises was shaken specifically by discovering gaps in
understanding they didn't know they had. The conflict is about whose responsibility the gap actually was
— and about what to do about it now that ignoring it is no longer an option.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Surviving both crises makes the gap impossible to keep ignoring: a deliberate mentorship program gets
built in their aftermath — the city's first real, formal attempt to write down not just what each system
does, but why, pairing veteran figures directly with current staff before the people who still remember
the founders' original reasoning are gone too.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Fort McMurdo's two flagship systems — comms coordination and volcanic monitoring — get their first actual
written doctrine: documented reasoning, not just documented procedure, covering the judgment calls that
used to live only in Ferreira-Novak's and Osei-Larsen's own heads and the heads of whoever they happened
to train personally.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the mentorship program is the first time the city has ever formally converted a founder's tacit
expertise into a written, teachable doctrine, it becomes the template Fort McMurdo reaches for whenever a
later formalization effort needs a model to follow — including, eventually, the comms charter
(Suggestion #9) and the national capital transition's own bureaucratic apparatus (Suggestion #1), both of
which draw on the same basic lesson this crisis first taught: informal excellence, however well it works,
eventually has to be written down before the person who built it retires or the city loses it for good.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Fort McMurdo now runs its two founding systems on documented doctrine rather than inherited habit alone,
with a standing mentorship program ensuring the next generational gap doesn't repeat itself unnoticed —
the city's hardest-earned lesson about the real cost of letting excellence run silently for too long.
