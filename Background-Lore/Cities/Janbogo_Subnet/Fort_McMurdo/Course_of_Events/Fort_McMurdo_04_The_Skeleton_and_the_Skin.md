## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The original McMurdo Station-era core is built to the old American Antarctic Program's
extreme-environment engineering standard — heavy, overbuilt, meant to survive decades of Antarctic
stress without needing to prove it against anything worse than routine weather.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

As the civilian population grows through the Interwar boom, the residential expansion built to house it
goes up fast and light, since speed and available space matter more, in the moment, than durability
nobody has any reason yet to think will actually be tested. For years, two visibly different cities
exist inside one skyline — the overbuilt old core and the fast-built new expansion — and nobody thinks
much of it, since the difference has never actually mattered in practice. Then a severe katabatic storm,
the worst in a generation, hits the newer expansion hard enough to buckle exposed structures the old
core shrugs off without incident.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation, in the storm's immediate aftermath,
between someone who wants the expansion rebuilt to the old core's heavy standard and someone who wants it
rebuilt the way it's always gone up — fast and light. This isn't simply safety versus recklessness: the
light-and-fast advocate has a real point that rebuilding heavy everywhere would cost far more than the
city, still growing and still needing housing quickly, can actually absorb, and that one severe storm in
a generation doesn't necessarily justify redesigning every future building around a worst case. The
heavy-standard advocate has an equally real point that the storm proved, concretely, which design
survives and which doesn't. Both are reasoning from real evidence; they're weighing different costs.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a structural engineer
or building-standards official, personally shaken by the storm damage and now advocating the old core's
proven approach for all future construction, possibly someone who works out of or trained within the
original core themselves. Character B is plausibly a civic planner or housing administrator responsible
for keeping pace with the city's continued population growth, who has to weigh the engineer's argument
against the practical reality that housing an expanding, working-class population can't wait for
every building to be built like a bunker. Neither needs to be right or wrong outright — the tension is
structural, between two legitimate civic priorities.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No single standard wins outright. The storm sparks a genuine, ongoing civic argument that resolves, in
practice, into a compromise: critical infrastructure and load-bearing structural spine get rebuilt to
something closer to the old core's heavier standard, while housing itself keeps going up fast and light
as it always has — a split standard rather than a single verdict, formalized into Fort McMurdo's first
real building code.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Fort McMurdo becomes, deliberately from this point forward, a city with two different engineering
standards operating side by side by design rather than by historical accident — a heavy-standard spine
threaded through a fast-built residential fabric, inspectors now checking new construction against
whichever standard actually applies to it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Having a formal, codified building standard — the city's first — becomes the administrative template Fort
McMurdo reaches for again once the convergence-era demand spike (Suggestion #7) forces rapid
infrastructure expansion to serve both the Amundsen Tower and Concordia's own boom at once: the split
compromise between speed and durability, first worked out here, is the model that shapes how the city's
later industrial buildout gets standardized without either sacrificing pace or repeating the storm's
mistake.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Fort McMurdo now builds to two deliberate standards, spine and skin, and carries the storm as its first
real lesson in taking its own infrastructure seriously before a bigger crisis forces the same question
at a much larger scale.
