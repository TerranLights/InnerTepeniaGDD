## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

As Fort McMurdo's industrial footprint grows through the boom years, expansion planners eye Cape Evans
and Cape Royds — flat, useable, close ground — as the obvious next sites for extraction or storage
infrastructure, seeing two Heroic Age expedition huts as, at most, a minor inconvenience to route around
or quietly absorb.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A formal preservation push blocks the expansion outright, arguing the huts are worth more to Fort
McMurdo's own claimed history than the square footage is worth to its industry — an argument the
planners never expected to actually win against real, immediate industrial need. The city's growth gets
rerouted around the huts rather than through them, at real, measurable cost to expansion efficiency the
planners resented at the time and kept resenting for years afterward.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between an industrial expansion
planner under real pressure to keep pace with the city's growth and a preservation advocate arguing two
century-old huts are worth protecting even at genuine cost to that growth. This shouldn't be a simple
philistine-versus-idealist story — the planner's pressure is real and their frustration legitimate (the
rerouted expansion genuinely costs the city efficiency it can't easily recover), while the preservation
advocate isn't arguing from pure sentiment either, since Fort McMurdo, a city with no formal capital
status and no obvious founding myth of its own yet, has a real structural need for something to anchor
its own claimed history to. Both are protecting something the city actually needs.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an industrial
expansion planner or infrastructure administrator, personally accountable for hitting growth targets the
rerouted geography now makes harder to reach. Character B is plausibly a heritage advocate or historian —
someone who might later be credited, or a predecessor of whoever eventually is, with formalizing Fort
McMurdo's own historical record — arguing from the position that a city built entirely on unglamorous
industrial output needs at least one thing it can point to as genuinely, verifiably its own history.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The preservation push wins: a permanent protected boundary is established around both Cape Evans and
Cape Royds, industrial development formally barred from the huts and a defined margin around them,
written into the city's own early land-use planning as a fixed, non-negotiable exception.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

That same rerouted-around geography that once looked like pure cost keeps the huts genuinely accessible
and intact through the rest of the industrial boom — reachable by a maintained corridor the expansion
was forced to build around rather than swallow, rather than becoming an isolated pocket surrounded by
active industrial ground.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the huts stay genuinely accessible rather than merely preserved-but-unreachable, a small, steady
stream of heritage-minded visitors and researchers starts making the trip specifically to see them —
easy walking distance from an otherwise all-business industrial city — turning a decision that once
looked like sentiment costing the city money into an unplanned, ongoing asset: Fort McMurdo's first taste
of drawing outside attention for something other than its industrial output.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Cape Evans and Cape Royds now sit as a protected, accessible historical enclave inside Fort McMurdo's
industrial sprawl — a small but genuine source of civic pride and outside visibility the city didn't
plan for, and a precedent, now on the books, that not every square meter of useable ground automatically
belongs to expansion.
