## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Cape Denison's real-world namesake carries a legendary, nearly-fatal expedition history behind it — the
site the exile city inherits both its name and its reputation from, known to every founding-generation
resident as more than an arbitrary label.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Living inside a name that famous means every generation of residents inherits an implicit standard to
measure up to, whether they asked for it or not — and as the founding generation gives way to their
children and grandchildren, some of whom had no direct hand in the original survival effort the name
commemorates, a real question starts to surface: does simply living here, inheriting infrastructure and
custom already built by others, actually earn the name, or does it only borrow it?

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a generational confrontation between an elder who
lived through the founding-era hardship the name commemorates, and a younger resident who feels the
weight of that inherited standard without ever having personally earned it — resentful, or at least
uneasy, at being implicitly measured against an ordeal they never faced themselves. The elder isn't
simply gatekeeping for its own sake: their insistence that the name still means something is a genuine
attempt to keep the city's hardest-won knowledge from being taken for granted. The younger resident isn't
simply ungrateful: inheriting a standard nobody actually asked you to meet is a real psychological
burden, not a character flaw.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a founding-generation
elder — someone who could plausibly be, or have worked alongside, the early structural engineers of
Suggestions #1 and #2 — whose whole sense of civic identity is tied to having personally survived what
the name commemorates. Character B is plausibly a younger, second- or third-generation resident, born
into the fused structure and its customs rather than having built either, unsure whether their comfortable
inherited life still counts as living up to anything.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The disagreement resolves not through the younger resident conceding the elder's standard, but through a
reframing both can actually accept: enduring the wind, generation after generation, stops being framed
as a one-time feat to be personally re-earned and becomes, instead, something closer to a shared
inheritance every resident is obligated to honor properly — through continued care, not repeated ordeal.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The city's whole culture of proud extremity-recitation (Suggestions #3 and #6) takes on an added,
explicit layer of meaning it didn't fully carry before — reciting the wind statistics becomes, in part, a
way of demonstrating the city still deserves the name it carries, not just describing conditions to
outsiders.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because reciting the numbers is now bound up with proving the city's worthiness of its own name, the
custom starts extending inward as well as outward — residents begin reciting the statistics to each
other, not just to newcomers, treating the ritual as an ongoing internal reaffirmation of shared identity
rather than purely an explanation aimed at outsiders who wouldn't otherwise understand.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Denison's wind-statistics recitation now serves two audiences at once — outsiders who need the
explanation, and residents who need the reaffirmation — treating ordinary survival as a legacy actively
honored rather than a hardship simply endured, with every generation renewing the claim to a name none of
them chose but all of them now carry.
