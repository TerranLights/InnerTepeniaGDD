## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Denison's founding population draws from the same broad Pacific-corridor mix that later defines Casey —
but arrives at Cape Denison with no modern station's infrastructure to inherit, only a legendary,
nearly-unsurvivable expedition's reputation and raw exposure to the worst wind in Tepenia.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That lack of inherited infrastructure means Denison's earliest civic character has to be built entirely
from scratch — every early decision about how to live, organize, and build made under direct
environmental pressure, with no borrowed institutional template to fall back on. Word of this difference
eventually travels between the two demographically similar communities, and it raises an uncomfortable
question for both: if the populations really are this alike, why do the two cities already feel, within
a generation or two, so visibly different from each other?

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** less a hostile confrontation than a genuine,
pointed disagreement between a Denison resident and a Casey resident — family or shared-origin
acquaintances, perhaps, comparing notes — over which city's early choices actually produced the better
outcome. The Denison figure takes pride in a civic character forged entirely by necessity, with nothing
handed down; the Casey figure takes equal pride in a civic character built on a stable, functioning
inheritance rather than having to improvise everything from nothing. Neither is wrong, and the
disagreement shouldn't resolve into one side conceding — it should surface that both cities' paths were
genuinely valid responses to genuinely different starting conditions.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Denison resident with
family ties to Casey — a cousin, a childhood friend from the same founding wave — giving the comparison
real personal stakes rather than abstract civic rivalry. Character B is plausibly the corresponding Casey
resident on the other end of that same relationship, equally invested in defending their own city's very
different path.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side convinces the other, and the disagreement never resolves into a clean winner — but airing it
out openly turns what could have been a quiet, nagging tension into an acknowledged, almost affectionate
running argument between the two communities, one both sides keep having precisely because neither
actually wants it settled.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Denison and Casey, despite sharing similar demographic origins, are now understood by both populations,
explicitly rather than just implicitly, as cities shaped by fundamentally different forces — one by what
it inherited, the other by what it had to survive — with residents of each actively able to articulate
the distinction rather than merely sensing it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the distinction is now something both cities can name and discuss rather than an unspoken
difference, it becomes a standing point of curiosity and comparison whenever residents of the two cities
meet — an ongoing, low-stakes cultural exchange built specifically around comparing notes on how alike
their founding populations actually were, kept alive precisely because the original question was never
fully settled.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Denison and Casey maintain a recognized, articulated divergence — shared origin, opposite formative
pressure — that neither population treats as a wound needing to close, but as a genuine, ongoing
curiosity residents of both cities keep returning to whenever paths cross.
