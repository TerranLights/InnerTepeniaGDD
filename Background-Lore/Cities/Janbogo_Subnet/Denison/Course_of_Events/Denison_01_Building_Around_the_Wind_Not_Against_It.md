## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early settlers at Cape Denison face average winds around 80 km/h and gusts over 300 km/h, close to
constant rather than episodic — a founding population that has to solve for survival against conditions
no other Tepenian city's own founding generation had to contend with at this severity.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Separate buildings, connected only occasionally the way other Antarctic settlements manage their own
severe weather, would leave residents exposed every time they had to cross open ground between them —
and at Cape Denison's wind speeds, that exposure isn't a mere inconvenience, it's a genuine, recurring
survival risk. The founding generation has to decide, early and decisively, how radical a structural
response the site actually demands, weighing a design that costs far more engineering effort against one
that's faster and cheaper but leaves real gaps in coverage.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between an engineer advocating a
fully interlinked, single-structure design and a pragmatist advocating a faster, cheaper approach — covered
walkways between otherwise separate buildings, matching what neighboring cities manage well enough with
their own less extreme conditions. Neither position is unreasonable: the pragmatist is right that a
comprehensively fused structure costs dramatically more time and labor a young settlement can't easily
spare, and the engineer is right that Cape Denison's specific wind severity genuinely exceeds what
partial measures elsewhere have ever had to survive. This is a resource-versus-risk argument between two
people who both understand the stakes.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an early structural

Zhang — pushing for total structural commitment from the outset. Character B is plausibly a
settlement organizer or resource administrator, responsible for a founding population's limited labor and
materials, wary of over-committing to an unproven, far more expensive design before anyone has confirmed

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The engineer's argument wins, but only after the pragmatist's own concerns force a leaner, more
disciplined version of the design rather than the most ambitious original proposal: the founding
generation commits to one single, aerodynamically coherent, structurally interlinked settlement — not
occasional covered walkways but a genuinely fused whole — built in careful, staged phases rather than all
at once, so the settlement's limited early labor force isn't overwhelmed trying to build everything
simultaneously.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Denison becomes, from its earliest years, a city that exists as one continuous structure — survival
demanding total commitment to the design, not a compromise — with construction proceeding in the staged
phases the original disagreement forced into the plan, each phase adding to a single, ever-more-integrated
whole rather than standing as an independent building.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the design was built in staged, load-sharing phases rather than as isolated buildings connected
afterward, every new addition has to integrate structurally with what already exists — there's no way to
simply bolt a new wing onto the settlement without it becoming genuinely load-bearing to the whole. That
structural interdependence, an unplanned side effect of how the city was phased into existence, becomes
the deep architectural root of Denison's later, deliberate civic culture of shared maintenance and mutual
structural reliance (Suggestion #7).

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Denison exists as one continuous, load-sharing structure, built through disciplined staged phases rather
than a single grand gesture — a design whose engineering necessity has already, without anyone planning
it, begun shaping how residents depend on each other structurally, long before that dependence becomes a
consciously named civic value.
