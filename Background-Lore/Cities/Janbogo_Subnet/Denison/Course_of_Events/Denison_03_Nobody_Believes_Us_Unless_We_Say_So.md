## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Denison's fused, aerodynamically smooth architecture does its job so well that the wind barely shows
itself from outside — no dramatic exposed structures straining visibly against the gusts the way a less
integrated design would. The city looks, from a distance, almost peaceful.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That very success becomes a problem the moment outsiders start arriving in real numbers. Visitors from
calmer subnet cities have no way to actually grasp how severe conditions here really are just by
looking, since the architecture that keeps residents alive is the same architecture that hides the very
thing it's protecting them from. Residents start reciting precise wind statistics to every outsider who
arrives, unprompted, simply because the buildings won't tell the story on their own — but doing this
constantly, to every new face, starts to risk sounding less like plain fact and more like boasting, and
some residents notice that risk before the habit fully calcifies into custom.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a resident who wants to
keep reciting the wind numbers to every arrival, unprompted, and a resident who worries the habit makes
Denison look provincial or boastful to a subnet full of cities that don't need to announce their own
extremity to be taken seriously. This isn't a pride-versus-shame morality tale — the second resident's
concern is reasonable civic image management, not self-hatred, and the first resident isn't wrong that
the numbers are simply true and worth knowing before you step outside unprepared. The disagreement is
about what kind of city Denison wants to present itself as, argued out through something as small and
recurring as how you greet a stranger.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an older or
longer-settled resident, someone who has lived through enough close calls with the wind that reciting
the numbers feels less like performance than basic safety courtesy. Character B is plausibly a younger
resident, or one with more regular contact with other subnet cities — trade, travel, a role that puts
them in contact with how Denison is perceived elsewhere — who has absorbed some outside self-consciousness
about the habit and wants the city to carry itself with more understatement.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No formal decision gets made — this isn't the kind of dispute a city settles by vote — but the argument
plays out often enough, in enough small everyday interactions, that the practical habit simply wins by
persistence: residents keep reciting the numbers regardless, because newcomers keep needing to hear them
before venturing outside unprepared, and safety keeps outweighing image every time the two collide.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The recitation habit hardens into genuine local custom rather than staying a matter of individual
temperament — Denison residents come to know their own wind numbers the way other cities know founding
dates, and telling them to a newcomer becomes a real, expected social ritual rather than something any
individual resident could opt out of without seeming to withhold something important.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the ritual is now genuinely expected rather than optional, residents start treating any
skepticism they encounter from outsiders not as an insult to brush off, but as an unresolved case the
custom itself is supposed to close — setting up exactly the kind of encounter where a visiting skeptic
gets proven wrong by the wind itself rather than merely told about it (Suggestion #6).

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Denison's wind-statistics recitation is now firmly established civic custom, expected of every resident
greeting every newcomer — settled as practice, but still untested against a genuinely skeptical
outsider who assumes the numbers are exaggeration rather than fact.
