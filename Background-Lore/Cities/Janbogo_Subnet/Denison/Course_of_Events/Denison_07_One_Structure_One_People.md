## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Because Denison was built in staged, load-sharing phases rather than as isolated buildings joined
afterward (Suggestion #1's 3rd-order structural legacy), every section of the city already depends on its
neighboring sections' upkeep in a way separate buildings never would — an interdependence nobody
consciously chose, simply the physical consequence of how the city was built.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

As the population grows past the founding generation who understood the structure intuitively because
they built it, newer residents start treating their own section's maintenance as a private matter — a
reasonable enough assumption in any other city, where one household's upkeep doesn't threaten anyone
else's. In Denison, it does: a neglected section doesn't just weaken locally, it transmits real
structural stress to everything load-sharing with it. A visible near-failure in one poorly-maintained
section makes that danger undeniable, and residents whose own sections depend on the neglected one start
demanding accountability.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a resident whose section's
neglect has put a load-sharing neighbor's safety at real risk, and that neighbor, demanding accountability
for a failure that was never framed, until now, as anyone's civic obligation. This shouldn't be a
simple negligent-villain story — the negligent resident may genuinely not have understood, until the
near-failure, that their own upkeep carried consequences beyond their own walls; the demanding neighbor's
anger is legitimate but risks over-correcting into a punitive civic response nobody has precedent for
yet. The real question the conflict has to resolve is what obligation structural interdependence actually
creates, and who gets to enforce it.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an ordinary resident
whose section of the structure has fallen behind on upkeep — not through malice, simply through the kind
of everyday neglect any household might commit if nobody had ever spelled out the stakes. Character B is
plausibly a neighboring resident or informal section-representative whose own family's safety was
directly endangered by the near-failure, someone with every reason to insist this never happens again but
no formal civic authority to compel it.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The dispute forces a genuinely new civic norm into being rather than staying a private grievance between
two residents: neighboring sections start organizing themselves into shared maintenance rotations,
making structural upkeep a collective, checked-on obligation rather than something any individual
resident can quietly let slide.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Civic habits form directly around the new rotation system — a real, felt sense spreading through the
city that letting your own section of the structure fail threatens everyone connected to it, and that
failing to participate in the rotation is a genuine breach of neighborly trust, not a private choice.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the rotation system makes neglect visible and collectively enforced rather than a private matter,
Denison develops an unusually strict, unusually enforced civic expectation around structural upkeep more
broadly — not because residents are naturally more disciplined than anyone else in Tepenia, but because
the maintenance-rotation precedent, once established, becomes the template the city reaches for whenever
any new form of shared civic obligation needs enforcing.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Denison's residents now maintain their structure through organized, mutually checked rotations rather
than individual discretion — a civic discipline the architecture itself made necessary, but that a single
near-failure and its aftermath turned into an actively defended norm rather than a passive habit anyone
could quietly ignore.
