## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Denison's recitation of precise wind statistics to every arriving outsider is now firmly established
civic custom (Suggestion #3) — but it has never yet been tested against a visitor who genuinely refuses
to believe it, someone convinced the numbers are simple civic bravado.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A visiting official from a calmer Janbogo subnet city arrives on ordinary subnet business, is greeted
with the customary recitation, and privately assumes it's exaggeration — the kind of civic
self-mythologizing every city indulges in about itself to some degree. The official has no intention of
being rude about it, but also no intention of pretending to be impressed by numbers they assume are
inflated, and says as much, politely but plainly, to a resident hosting them.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a Denison resident
insulted, on behalf of the whole city's honesty, by a visitor's calm dismissal of the numbers as
bravado, and the skeptical visiting official who isn't trying to give offense but genuinely doesn't
believe conditions can be as extreme as described. This should read as a collision of two reasonable
priors, not a rude outsider versus a wounded local — the official's skepticism is a rational response to
having heard plenty of other cities' own exaggerated civic pride before, and the resident's offense is a
rational response to being disbelieved about something that, to them, is simply observable fact.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an ordinary resident
assigned as the visiting official's host or guide — someone with genuine local pride but no particular
diplomatic training, for whom this dismissal lands personally. Character B is plausibly a mid-level
delegate or coordination official from Dumont d'Urville, Cape Adare, or elsewhere in the subnet, present
for practical business (trade, subnet administration) rather than tourism, whose skepticism is shaped by
long experience with other cities' own overstated reputations.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The disagreement gets settled the only way it honestly could be: the official steps outside during an
ordinary gust, expecting nothing especially dramatic, and is immediately and visibly proven wrong — the
numbers weren't exaggeration at all. The official's own astonishment, entirely unstaged, becomes the
proof no amount of local insistence could have supplied on its own.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The visitor's astonishment, retold afterward by residents who witnessed it, becomes a small piece of
local legend — an outside, initially skeptical source now on record validating the recitation tradition,
rather than the tradition resting solely on residents' own self-reported word.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the story now exists as a repeatable, third-party-validated legend rather than an isolated
incident, residents start folding it deliberately into the recitation ritual itself — telling new
arrivals not just the numbers, but the story of the official who doubted and was proven wrong, using
someone else's past skepticism as evidence built into the greeting from then on.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Denison's wind-statistics tradition now carries its own built-in proof, told alongside the numbers to
every new arrival — not because one visitor's astonishment automatically convinces the next skeptic, but
because the story gets repeated to every new arrival anyway, becoming part of the same verbal tradition
it originally validated.
