# Denison — Candidate Courses of Events

**Scope note:** all ten chains below are set strictly *before* the Long Night War, during the Second
Interwar Period when Tepenia is a full, functioning country — no war damage, no post-war/present-day
material (Denison's own eventual destruction is out of scope here). 10 candidate But/Therefore chains
built from Denison's Megasheet (the windiest permanently inhabited place on Earth, one continuous
fused structure, the loud proud culture of reciting wind statistics). Post-settlement only; nothing
here is canon.

---

## 1. Building Around the Wind, Not Against It

Early settlers at Cape Denison face average winds around 80 km/h and gusts over 300 km/h, close to
constant rather than episodic. **But** separate buildings, connected only occasionally, would leave
residents exposed every time they had to cross open ground between them. **Therefore** the founding
generation commits to something more radical than any neighboring city attempts: one single,
aerodynamically coherent, structurally interlinked settlement, not occasional covered walkways but a
genuinely fused whole. **But** building that way from the outset costs far more engineering effort
than piecemeal construction would. **Therefore** Denison becomes, from its earliest years, a city that
exists as one continuous structure — survival demanding total commitment to the design, not a
compromise.

## 2. Wei Zhang's Design

Chief Wind-Engineer Wei Zhang inherits the challenge of making Cape Denison's earliest
scattered structures actually hold together against wind nothing else in Tepenia has to withstand.
**But** existing engineering approaches elsewhere in the subnet all assume buildings can stay
functionally separate. **Therefore** she pioneers a genuinely new structural principle — load-sharing
interlinkage throughout the whole built environment, not just insulated connective tunnels.
**But** convincing a growing settlement to commit to a single, comprehensively unified structure means
overturning how every other Tepenian city builds. **Therefore** her design becomes Denison's defining
engineering achievement, the founding figure behind an approach no other city in the subnet fully
replicates.

## 3. Nobody Believes Us Unless We Say So

Denison's fused, aerodynamically smooth architecture does its job so well that the wind barely shows
itself from outside — no dramatic exposed structures straining visibly against the gusts the way a
less integrated design would. **But** that means visitors from calmer cities have no way to actually
grasp how severe conditions here really are just by looking. **Therefore** residents start reciting
precise wind statistics to every outsider who arrives, unprompted, simply because the architecture
itself won't tell the story on its own. **But** doing this constantly risks sounding like boasting
rather than plain fact. **Therefore** it hardens into genuine local custom anyway — Denison residents
know their own wind numbers the way other cities know founding dates, and telling them becomes a real
social ritual.

## 4. The Chambers Denison Built

As Denison's engineering culture matures, its technical capability draws attention from the nation's
broader industrial network. **But** manufacturing something as precise as a fabrication-synthesis
chamber for the Cradle robot-creation infrastructure requires exactly the kind of exacting,
interlinked-systems expertise Denison's whole civic identity is built around. **Therefore** Fabrication
Engineer Kenji Marchetti-Suh establishes a chamber-manufacturing operation here, giving the city a
genuine role in something touching the entire country's population growth. **But** that role demands
a level of precision failure simply isn't survivable for. **Therefore** Denison's manufacturing
standards become even more exacting than its architecture already required — a second domain where
the city's engineering pride gets tested and proven.

## 5. Close to Dumont d'Urville, Far From Everyone Else

Denison sits roughly 110km along the coast from Dumont d'Urville, genuinely close by Ross Sea coast
standards, while the stretch of Hwy 183 back toward Cape Adare runs dramatically longer. **But** two
cities that share regular contact inevitably start comparing notes on how they actually live.
**Therefore** a real, frequent relationship forms between Denison and Dumont d'Urville — trade,
visits, shared subnet business — while Cape Adare and the cities further north stay comparatively
distant, more occasional acquaintance than close neighbor. **But** that asymmetry shapes which cities
Denison's own residents think of as genuinely close to them. **Therefore** Denison's mental map of the
Janbogo subnet ends up lopsided, oriented almost entirely southward, toward the one neighbor near
enough to actually know well.

## 6. What the Wind Statistics Actually Mean

A visiting official from a calmer Janbogo subnet city hears Denison's residents recite their wind
numbers and privately assumes it's exaggeration, civic bravado. **But** stepping outside during an
ordinary gust proves the numbers weren't exaggerated at all. **Therefore** the visitor's own
astonishment, retold afterward, becomes a small piece of local legend — proof, from an outside
source, that Denison's proud recitation habit was never boasting, just accurate description nobody
else could otherwise verify. **But** one visitor's astonishment doesn't automatically convince the
next skeptic. **Therefore** the story gets repeated to every new arrival anyway, becoming part of the
same verbal tradition it originally validated.

## 7. One Structure, One People

Living inside one continuous, load-sharing structure means Denison's residents depend on each other's
maintenance and upkeep in a way separate buildings never would. **But** that interdependence isn't
something anyone consciously chose — it's just the physical consequence of how the city was built.
**Therefore** civic habits form around it anyway: shared maintenance rotations, a real sense that
letting your own section of the structure fail threatens everyone connected to it. **But** that level
of physical interdependence could easily breed resentment toward neighbors who don't pull their
weight. **Therefore** Denison develops an unusually strict, unusually enforced civic expectation
around structural upkeep — not because residents are naturally more disciplined, but because the
architecture leaves no room for anyone to opt out.

## 8. Trained by the Wind

Denison's founding population draws from the same broad Pacific-corridor mix that later defines
Casey, but arrives with no modern station's infrastructure to inherit — only a legendary,
nearly-unsurvivable expedition's reputation and raw exposure to the worst wind in Tepenia. **But**
that lack of inherited infrastructure means Denison's earliest civic character has to be built from
scratch, shaped entirely by the environment rather than borrowed from any founding institution.
**Therefore** Denison and Casey, despite sharing similar demographic origins, grow into visibly
different cities — one shaped by what it inherited, the other shaped entirely by what it had to
survive. **But** neither population set out to end up different from the other. **Therefore** the
divergence becomes a quiet point of curiosity whenever residents of the two compare notes on how
alike their founding populations actually were.

## 9. The Reputation That Traveled Ahead

Word of Cape Denison's extremity spreads across Tepenia long before most people ever have reason to
visit — the windiest permanently inhabited place on the continent becomes a kind of shorthand for
severity itself. **But** the reality inside the fused structure is, day to day, considerably more
livable than the reputation suggests. **Therefore** visitors consistently arrive braced for something
worse than what they find, and just as consistently leave surprised. **But** Denison's own residents
never bother correcting the exaggerated reputation, since undercutting it would mean giving up the
very extremity they take such open pride in. **Therefore** the gap between Denison's fearsome
reputation and its actually-functional daily life becomes something residents quietly enjoy
maintaining rather than a misunderstanding worth fixing.

## 10. A Name Worth Living Up To

Cape Denison's real-world namesake carries a legendary, nearly-fatal expedition history behind it —
the site the exile city inherits its name and reputation from. **But** living inside a name that
famous means every generation of residents inherits an implicit standard to measure up to, whether
they asked for it or not. **Therefore** enduring the wind stops being just survival and becomes, over
generations, something closer to a shared inheritance residents feel obligated to honor properly.
**But** treating ordinary survival as a legacy to live up to risks turning hardship into performance.
**Therefore** Denison's whole culture of proud extremity-recitation becomes, in part, a way of proving
the city still deserves the name it carries — not just surviving Cape Denison, but remaining worthy
of it.

---

## Worth Your Attention

**#1 and #2** are the most load-bearing — together they give Denison's single most distinctive
established fact (one continuous fused structure) an actual founding decision and a named originator,
rather than leaving it as an unexplained architectural given. **#3 and #6** work as a natural pair,
tracing the verbal wind-statistics tradition from its structural cause to a concrete incident that
validated it — the same resolution the Megasheet's own Full Extrapolation proposes, given an actual
dramatized shape.
