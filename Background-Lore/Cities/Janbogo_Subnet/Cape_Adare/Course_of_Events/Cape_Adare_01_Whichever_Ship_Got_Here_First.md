## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Cape Adare's location makes it the most accessible Ross Sea landfall from open ocean — the natural first
stop for anyone approaching this stretch of coast, regardless of where they set out from or what brought
them.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That accessibility means Cape Adare's founding population was never one organized settling group with a
shared plan — just whoever happened to reach it first, ship after ship, community after community, with
nothing in common but timing. As the settlement grows past its first improvised arrivals, it needs actual
civic institutions — some way to allocate ground, settle disputes, coordinate shared infrastructure — and
no single group among the arrivals has the numbers, the seniority, or the shared history to simply claim
the authority to build those institutions on everyone else's behalf.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between two early arrivals from
different founding communities, each with a genuinely workable proposal for how the settlement's first
civic institutions should be organized, each shaped by whatever administrative habits their own point of
origin happened to carry. Neither should have any special claim to authority — that's the whole point —
so the conflict can't be resolved by simply deferring to whoever arrived "first," since even that claim
is contested and unverifiable this early. The tension is between two reasonable, differently-shaped
proposals with no neutral tiebreaker available yet.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** two early settlers, ideally from different
national or cultural backgrounds represented among the founding arrivals (plausibly drawing from the
established USA/China co-Primary split, or from the broader Significant tier — Australia, Japan,
Indonesia, Canada, South Korea, UK), each informally organizing their own arriving group and each
proposing a different model for how shared civic decisions should get made. Neither is a designated
leader; both are simply the people other new arrivals have started listening to.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither proposal wins outright. Because nobody arrived with an established claim to lead, the two
camps — and the smaller groups that formed around each of them — end up building the settlement's first
real institutions through direct, ongoing consensus between every represented arrival group rather than
through either original proposal alone, a genuinely negotiated hybrid nobody would have designed from
scratch.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Consensus-building between represented groups becomes the settlement's default civic method rather than
a one-time compromise — every subsequent institution the growing city needs (dispute resolution,
resource allocation, shared infrastructure planning) gets built the same negotiated way, since no
precedent exists for any other approach and the first consensus process worked well enough to repeat.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because consensus-building is now the settlement's only established civic method, Cape Adare never
develops a single dominant civic voice or office the way cities founded around one organizing group
often do — a structural absence, not a deliberate ideal, that later becomes exactly the reason outside
communities eventually trust Cape Adare enough to host a genuine regional gathering of their own on its
ground: a city with no entrenched leadership of its own has nothing to gain by tilting anyone else's
business in its own favor.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Cape Adare now runs on a lived habit of consensus between its many represented founding communities, with
no single civic voice ever having taken root — a structural fact, born from pure accident of arrival
order, that quietly shapes everything the city builds afterward.
