## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

For years, St. Jules Day is observed quietly, however individual families choose to mark it — no shared
civic ceremony, just private, scattered acknowledgment of the founder's landing, varying household to
household.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A city this thoroughly shaped by its founder's own landing wants something more shared and deliberate —
and as the population grows past the founding generation who personally carried the observance's meaning,
a real question surfaces about how to mark the day going forward: something comfortable and easy to
sustain across a growing population, or something that actually asks something of participants, the way
the founder's own landing genuinely did.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement between a civic organizer proposing a
comfortable, indoor commemorative ceremony — accessible to everyone, unconstrained by weather, easy to
sustain year after year — and a resident, perhaps someone with a personal or scholarly attachment to St.
Jules's own expedition journal, insisting the observance has to happen outdoors, in the wind, to mean
anything at all. The organizer's practicality is reasonable: an indoor ceremony draws wider, more
consistent participation. The resident's insistence is equally reasonable: an observance held in comfort,
honoring a landing made under genuine hardship, risks becoming empty ritual disconnected from what it's
actually supposed to commemorate.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civic events
organizer or administrator, tasked with designing a citywide observance that needs to actually work,
logistically, for a growing population. Character B is plausibly a resident with a personal investment in
St. Jules's own history — perhaps a keeper of the surviving journal fragments, or simply someone whose
family has always marked the day with unusual seriousness.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The resident's position wins out: a public reading takes hold, held downtown, outdoors, regardless of the
wind — surviving fragments of Jules Dumont d'Urville's 1840 expedition journal read aloud to whoever
gathers, the organizer's practicality accommodated only in that the reading itself is kept short enough
that standing through it in the wind remains bearable rather than punishing.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Holding the reading outside, in the exact conditions the observance is honoring, means the reading itself
becomes a small trial every year — attendance requires a genuine, if brief, act of physical endurance
that an indoor ceremony never would have asked of participants.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because attending actually costs something — real discomfort, deliberately chosen — the observance
becomes self-selecting in a way an easy indoor ceremony never could have been: those who keep attending
year after year are doing so because the discomfort itself feels meaningful to them, which turns St.
Jules Day, over generations, into a genuine marker of civic commitment rather than a passive holiday
anyone might attend out of habit alone.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

St. Jules Day is now marked by an outdoor public reading of the founder's own journal, held in the same
wind he once faced — a deliberate discomfort residents choose year after year, precisely because standing
in the same wind St. Jules once did is the whole point, not a comfortable ceremony held indoors out of
convenience.
