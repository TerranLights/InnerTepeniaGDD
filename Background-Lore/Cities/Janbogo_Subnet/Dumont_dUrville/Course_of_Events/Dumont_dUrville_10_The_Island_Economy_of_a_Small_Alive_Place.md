## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Dumont d'Urville's population stays genuinely small by Tepenian standards, capped hard by the island's
own limited buildable ground — a ceiling nobody can simply build past, no matter how much the city might
otherwise want to grow.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A small population still needs enough economic and cultural depth to feel like a real city rather than an
outpost — and as neighboring subnet cities grow larger, better resourced, and more diversely
specialized, a real anxiety surfaces among some Dumont d'Urville residents: can a population this size
ever actually sustain a full civic and economic life, or is the island permanently doomed to feel thin
compared to what a larger city could offer?

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement between a resident who wants to push
for expanded mainland settlement or new engineering solutions to physically grow the island's usable
footprint — treating the population cap as a problem to solve rather than a fact to accept — and a
resident who argues the city should stop fighting its own scale and instead concentrate everything it has
into making the existing small core as rich and self-sufficient as possible. The expansionist's instinct
is understandable: more room means more possibility. The concentrator's instinct is equally sound: chasing
growth the geography won't actually support risks spreading the city's limited resources too thin to
sustain anything well.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an ambitious civic
planner or engineer, drawn to the same kind of large-scale problem-solving spirit that keeps the mainland
bridge question alive (Suggestion #5). Character B is plausibly a downtown proprietor or civic figure
already invested in the dense, concentrated core the city has been building toward (Suggestions #3 and
#4), for whom the answer to feeling small is depth, not expansion.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The concentrator's argument prevails — no serious push for physical expansion beyond the island's natural
limits ever gains real traction, and civic effort instead commits deliberately to deepening what the
existing footprint can sustain: a dense, concentrated downtown doing the work a much larger population
would otherwise spread across more space.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Residents lean hard into what the island can actually sustain, investing civic energy and resources into
concentration rather than expansion — every improvement, every new civic institution, gets built to
enrich the existing small core rather than to support a larger population the geography was never going
to allow.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because everything the city has gets concentrated into one small core rather than spread across a larger
footprint, the city has less room to absorb shocks than a larger, more spread-out place would — a single
crisis touching downtown touches the entire civic and economic life of the island at once, giving Dumont
d'Urville's whole civic character a quiet, ever-present awareness that the downtown's health and the
city's health are, structurally, the same question.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Dumont d'Urville's whole civic character now forms around protecting its one small, vital core — a
population that chose depth over growth, understanding, long before anything ever tests it, exactly how
little separates the downtown's wellbeing from the whole island's own.
