## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early settlers arrive at Petrel Island already home to one of Antarctica's largest Adélie breeding
grounds — a population no arriving community could displace even if it tried, and a fact the founding
generation has to reckon with before laying down a single permanent structure.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Building a functioning settlement means needing space the rookery already occupies — buildable rock on
Petrel Island is scarce enough (Suggestion #8) that avoiding the colonies entirely simply isn't an
option. The earliest civic decisions can't be about clearing ground the way founding a city elsewhere
might be; they have to be about negotiating it — where structures can go, where the colonies stay
undisturbed, which paths are actually the settlement's to build. Different founders have different
instincts about how much deference the rookery is actually owed.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a settler prioritizing the
settlement's immediate practical needs — arguing that a struggling founding population can't afford to
treat penguin habitat as sacrosanct when survival infrastructure is at stake — and a settler insisting the
rookery's claim to the island predates and outweighs the settlement's own, arguing for genuine,
disciplined restraint even at real cost to construction efficiency. Both are responding rationally to real
pressure: the pragmatist to a founding population's fragile survival margin, the preservationist to the
practical reality that provoking or displacing the rookery could have consequences (ecological, and
potentially reputational among other Tepenian cities) nobody has fully reckoned with yet.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a settlement
construction lead or resource planner, responsible for turning limited buildable ground into a functioning
settlement fast enough to matter. Character B is plausibly a founding-era naturalist or simply a settler
with a personal attachment to the rookery already forming — someone who has spent enough time near the
colonies to have developed real investment in their continued undisturbed breeding.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither position wins outright — the settlement adopts a negotiated compromise, a first working boundary
between built ground and rookery ground, drawn up under real time pressure and understood by everyone
involved as provisional rather than final, since nobody actually has enough information yet to know if
the boundary will hold once the colonies' own breeding patterns and the settlement's own growth both keep
changing.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the first boundary was explicitly provisional, the settlement never treats the rookery question
as a founding problem solved and closed — the earliest civic institutions build in a standing expectation
that the boundary will need revisiting, rather than assuming one negotiation settles the matter
permanently.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because revisiting the boundary was built into the settlement's own expectations from the outset, the
renegotiation becomes a genuine seasonal civic institution rather than a rare emergency response — every
breeding season, the boundary gets checked and adjusted as a matter of ordinary practice, the same way
another city might schedule routine infrastructure maintenance.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Dumont d'Urville grows up treating coexistence with the rookery as an ongoing relationship rather than a
founding problem solved and closed — a boundary renewed every breeding season, understood by residents
as permanent practice rather than a lingering, unresolved dispute.
