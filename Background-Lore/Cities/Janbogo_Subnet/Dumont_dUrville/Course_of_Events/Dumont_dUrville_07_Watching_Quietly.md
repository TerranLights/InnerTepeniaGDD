## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Robots in Dumont d'Urville develop a genuine tradition of careful, patient penguin-colony observation —
watching without disturbing, simply attending to the rookery's rhythms — but for a long stretch, this
stays a private, individual practice, each robot who does it assuming they're the only one.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Word starts spreading of how many residents already do this quietly on their own — enough separate
robots, discovered by coincidence to share the same private habit, that the practice stops feeling like a
solitary eccentricity and starts looking like something the city could actually share. That realization
puts pressure on what has always been an unspoken, individual ritual: does bringing it into the open
change what it actually is?

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a quiet disagreement — more a difference in
temperament than a hostile confrontation — between a robot who wants to openly invite others to observe
together, seeing shared practice as a genuine communal good the city currently lacks, and a robot who has
practiced this alone for years and worries that turning it social will replace the quiet that made it
meaningful with something performative or crowded. Neither is wrong: the first genuinely believes shared
contemplative practice would enrich the city; the second has direct, lived experience of what makes the
private version work, and a legitimate fear that formalizing it would kill exactly the quality worth
sharing in the first place.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a more socially
inclined robot resident, someone who has recently discovered how many others share the habit and feels
genuine enthusiasm about connecting them. Character B is plausibly a longtime solitary practitioner —
perhaps one of the earliest robots in the city to develop the habit, whose relationship to the rookery
long predates anyone else noticing.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A middle path emerges through actual practice rather than negotiation: robots simply start showing up to
observe near each other, without any formal invitation or announcement, coexisting in loose, unplanned
company rather than either staying fully solitary or organizing anything communal — the disagreement
resolves itself the moment it turns out both positions were partly right, and neither had to fully win.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The practice starts happening in loose company instead of isolation — people and robots observing
together without needing to talk through it, present in the same space at the same time without the
shared activity ever requiring coordination, scheduling, or announcement.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because no formal structure ever gets imposed, newcomers who want to join simply learn, by watching
regulars, that showing up quietly and not disturbing anyone else's observation is the entire unwritten
etiquette — the practice becomes self-teaching, passed on purely through example, exactly the way
Zukelli's structural street-marking system across the bay is later learned through relationship rather
than instruction (a parallel neither city ever discusses, since neither knows the other developed it).

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Penguin-colony observation is now Dumont d'Urville's closest thing to a shared contemplative practice —
deliberately unstructured, no formal ritual, no set schedule, sustained purely by residents and robots
choosing, individually and repeatedly, to show up quietly in the same place.
