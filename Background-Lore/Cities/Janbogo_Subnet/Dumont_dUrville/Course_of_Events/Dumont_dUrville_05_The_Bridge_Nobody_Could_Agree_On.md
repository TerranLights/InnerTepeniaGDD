## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

As the island's population grows, connecting reliably to the mainland becomes a real civic question —
one that has, so far, been handled through whatever seasonal improvisation works well enough to get by,
never treated as urgent enough to demand a permanent answer.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Every proposed permanent solution runs into a genuine engineering problem: a fixed structure faces real
aerodynamic flutter risk in wind this severe and real pack ice pier loads each winter; a seasonal
crossing means real unreliability during exactly the parts of the year when reliable access matters most.
As the population that depends on crossing keeps growing, the debate over what to actually build stops
being a background curiosity and becomes an urgent, recurring civic argument, with genuine engineering
proposals brought forward, scrutinized, and shelved rather than any design ever winning outright.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a technical disagreement between an engineer
championing a permanent fixed-structure solution — convinced the flutter and ice-load risks can be
engineered around given enough investment — and an engineer or civic administrator who considers any fixed
structure too costly and too failure-prone to bet the island's mainland access on, arguing for continued,
disciplined seasonal adaptation instead. This conflict should not resolve with one engineering approach
being vindicated — the whole point, per the standing developer instruction, is that the underlying
question stays genuinely open. The conflict's outcome should be about how the city handles an unresolved
technical disagreement, not about which side turns out to be right.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a structural or civil
engineer, ambitious enough to want to solve the crossing permanently and confident enough in emerging
techniques to think the risks are manageable. Character B is plausibly a more conservative engineer or a
resource-conscious civic administrator, wary of committing the island's limited capital to an unproven
permanent structure when adaptable seasonal methods, however inconvenient, have never actually failed
outright.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither engineering approach wins — the civic argument itself gets formally tabled rather than decided,
with both proposals kept on record as live options rather than either one being built or definitively
rejected, while the city commits, in the meantime, to simply doing what has always worked: ice roads in
years the ice supports them, boats in years it doesn't.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Dumont d'Urville institutionalizes seasonal adaptation as deliberate policy rather than mere stopgap
practice — dedicated civic roles and resources get assigned specifically to managing the ice-road/boat
transition each year, turning what was once ad hoc improvisation into a genuinely maintained, funded
system.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because seasonal crossing management is now a funded, dedicated civic function rather than improvisation,
the people who run it accumulate real, specialized expertise over years of practice — expertise that
itself becomes a quiet counterargument every time the permanent-bridge proposal resurfaces, since the
seasonal system now works well enough, run by people who know it intimately, that the case for replacing
it keeps getting harder to make on practical grounds, even though the underlying engineering debate is
never actually settled.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Dumont d'Urville simply keeps adapting — ice roads some years, boats in others — treating "how do we
cross" as a live, ongoing negotiation nobody expects to finally close, now run by a genuinely skilled,
dedicated seasonal-crossing corps whose accumulated expertise makes the standing debate over a permanent
bridge feel less urgent with every year it isn't resolved, without ever actually resolving it.
