## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Katabatic winds arrive with real, dangerous force and little obvious warning to residents caught
outside — a hazard the growing population, moving constantly between the shielded halls (Suggestion #3)
and their homes, simply has to accept as an ongoing risk of daily life.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A growing population moving between the halls and their homes can't simply avoid the outdoors entirely,
and outdoor katabatic fatalities keep accumulating at a rate the city can't treat as acceptable
background risk forever. A meteorologist proposes building a genuine forecasting and warning system —
but doing it right means years of careful, unglamorous data collection before the system is reliable
enough to actually trust, and a civic administration under pressure to show immediate results has to
decide whether to fund a slow, rigorous approach or push for something faster and less certain.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a conflict between a meteorologist insisting on
years of careful, rigorous data-gathering before deploying any public warning system, and a civic
administrator under real pressure — grieving families, mounting public concern — to deploy something,
anything, faster, even if it's less reliable. The administrator isn't wrong that lives are being lost in
the meantime and the pressure to act quickly is genuine; the meteorologist isn't wrong that a warning
system residents can't actually trust is worse than no system at all, since false confidence gets people
killed too.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the figure later
remembered as, or a direct predecessor to, Meteorologist Han Soo-jin, credited with formalizing
Janbogo's warning system. Character B is plausibly a civic administrator or hall coordinator, responsible
for public safety and under direct pressure from a population that wants a solution now.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A compromise emerges: an early, openly-labeled provisional warning system launches immediately, explicitly
described to residents as imperfect and improving, while the meteorologist's full rigorous data-gathering
continues in parallel — buying real safety improvement immediately without pretending the early version is
the finished, fully reliable system it isn't yet.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Residents grow accustomed to a warning system that visibly improves over time rather than arriving
finished — each new refinement publicly acknowledged as an upgrade, building genuine trust incrementally
rather than demanding trust all at once for an unproven system.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because residents watched the system improve openly rather than being asked to trust it blindly from the
start, they develop a durable, well-founded confidence in it that a system launched all at once, fully
formed, might never have earned as convincingly — that confidence becomes the specific reason outdoor
katabatic fatalities stay rare rather than routine, since residents actually heed the warnings instead of
treating them with the skepticism an untested system might have drawn.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Han Soo-jin's warning system stands as one of Janbogo's quiet, load-bearing achievements — trusted not
because it arrived perfect, but because residents watched it earn that trust incrementally, giving the
city genuine advance notice before the worst gusts arrive.
