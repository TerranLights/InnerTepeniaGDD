## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Storm damage has hit both the shipyards and the residential districts at once, and Sanay's leadership
has limited resources to go around. Nothing has yet been decided about which gets rebuilt first — both
are simply damaged, both are simply waiting.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The port's continued function determines whether the whole subnet keeps receiving South African
shipments at all — every city downstream of Sanay's docks is, in effect, waiting on this decision too,
whether they know it or not. That pressure pushes rebuilding priority toward the shipyards, fast and
first, ahead of resident comfort. But a decision with this much weight can't just be quietly assumed
into place; it has to be made and defended openly, in front of the people it affects, by someone
willing to say out loud what the city is choosing to sacrifice and why.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone representing the
immediate, human cost of delay and someone representing the subnet-wide, economic cost of it. This
shouldn't resolve as compassion losing to cold math — both positions are load-bearing. A's case is that
people are living in damaged homes right now and deserve to be told they matter as much as cargo. B's
case is that without a functioning port, there's no economy left to fund anyone's rebuild, including
the residential districts eventually — prioritizing the port isn't callousness, it's keeping the whole
system solvent long enough to get to everyone. The conflict is strongest if both characters already
half-agree with each other and are arguing mostly about sequencing and honesty, not about whether
residents matter at all.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a residential-district
representative or a community organizer speaking for cliffside neighborhoods, someone whose job is to
make sure the people living with the damage aren't simply deprioritized without a hearing. Character B
is plausibly a senior port or shipyard administrator — a figure in the same institutional lineage as the
Full_Extrapolation's proposed Foreman Ingrid Van Zylsberg — whose case rests on hard numbers about what
the subnet's supply chain actually requires and by when.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The decision goes to shipyards first, but not quietly — it's made in open session, argued in front of
residents rather than assumed behind closed doors, and defended on the record. "Function over
sentiment" stops being an abstract civic value and becomes a specific, remembered decision: the moment
Sanay chose, out loud, what it was actually willing to sacrifice for.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the decision was made openly rather than assumed, it leaves behind an actual record — a
resolution, formally adopted and formally worded, rather than just a remembered event. That resolution
becomes citable: the first time Sanay's civic government has had to put a resource-priority argument
into a document instead of just living it out in practice.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Having a citable resolution on the books means future resource disputes now have a formal precedent and
a formal channel to be argued through, rather than staying informal and unspoken the way this one might
have without the open hearing. That channel cuts both ways: it gives future arguments somewhere to go,
but it also means the "function over sentiment" priority is no longer just a civic habit — it's
enshrined policy, harder to challenge than an assumption ever was.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sanay has a formal, on-the-record priority policy — shipyards first, in writing, defended once in open
session and citable ever since. The residential districts are still waiting their turn, but now with a
documented decision behind the wait instead of a silent one, and with a precedent-and-process structure
in place for whatever comes of that wait next.
