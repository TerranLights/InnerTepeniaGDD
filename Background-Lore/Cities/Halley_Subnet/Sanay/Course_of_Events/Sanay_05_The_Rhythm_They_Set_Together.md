## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

South African freighters arrive at Sanay first. From there, goods split two ways: trucked directly to
nearby cities via Hwy 7, or trucked onward to Troll, whose airfield flies goods out to more remote parts
of Tepenia. Sanay unloads; Troll flies. Each city runs its own half of the chain.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The two cities initially schedule their own operations independently, each optimizing for itself —
freight sitting at Sanay's docks while Troll's runway capacity goes half-used, or the reverse, costing
both cities real time and money every time the two schedules happen not to line up. The waste is
frequent enough, and expensive enough, that it stops being tolerable as an ordinary cost of doing
business. Representatives from both cities are pushed toward the table to negotiate a shared,
coordinated schedule for the first time — formally tying Sanay's unloading rhythm to Troll's flight
capacity, something neither city has ever had to hand over control of before.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between two logistics figures who
both want the inefficiency solved and both fear what solving it costs them. Neither side is against
coordination in principle — the waste is real and both cities are losing money to it. The actual friction
is about surrendering unilateral control: whoever agrees to the joint schedule first is giving up the
ability to change their own city's operations without the other city's sign-off, indefinitely. Given
that Troll's airfield control is itself a contested, high-stakes question (per the Megasheet's own
established stakes), a Troll-side negotiator has a specific, legitimate reason to be wary of handing
Sanay any kind of leverage over Troll's scheduling — and a Sanay-side negotiator has an equally
legitimate reason to distrust leaving Sanay's own dock rhythm hostage to a city whose control is
unsettled.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly Sanay's dockmaster or
senior logistics coordinator, the person who feels the cost of every missed handoff directly in Sanay's
own operations. Character B is plausibly Troll's flight-scheduling chief or airfield administrator, who
has to weigh the same efficiency case against Troll's own precarious, contested position and a
reluctance to formally bind the airfield's capacity to any outside city's rhythm.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

After real friction over exactly how much control each side is giving up, the two negotiators land on a
formal shared schedule — Sanay's unloading rhythm tied directly to Troll's flight capacity. Neither city
can unilaterally change its own operations without consulting the other anymore. Sanay and Troll become
genuinely, formally interdependent: a relationship built to save both cities money that neither one can
quietly walk back once it's in place.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

A formal bilateral schedule this consequential doesn't run itself — it needs ongoing joint
administration. A standing liaison arrangement forms between the two cities' logistics staff, the first
regular, institutionalized coordination body either city has ever needed to maintain with a neighbor.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That standing liaison structure becomes, later, the specific mechanism through which Sanay has an actual
institutional voice whenever Troll's airfield control comes into question — not just a city with
material exposure to the outcome, but a city with an existing, working seat at the table, because the
seat was already built for an entirely different reason years earlier.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sanay and Troll now run on a single, jointly administered rhythm, maintained by a standing liaison
neither city originally set out to build. Sanay's stake in whatever eventually happens to Troll's
contested airfield control is no longer just economic exposure from a distance — it's institutional
participation, already in place, already assumed.
