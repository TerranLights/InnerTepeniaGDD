## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

A sustained, subnet-wide storm system — the worst in a generation — batters Sanay's port and
shipyards directly, no gentler to a bedrock-founded city than to any other. Dockside cranes, exposed
warehouse roofs, and unsheltered shipyard equipment all take the same battering wind and surge that
every other Halley subnet coastal city is taking at the same time.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Unlike a city built on the moving, potentially-calving ice shelves that Halley and Neumayer sit on,
Sanay's foundation on Vesleskarvet's rock holds even as real, visible damage piles up across the
exposed dockside infrastructure. Sanay comes out the other side of the storm hurt but still
functioning, still shipping, still working, while neighboring cities lose weeks of capacity outright.
That contrast draws outside attention fast — and the attention keeps arriving already shaped into a
story of luck: framing Sanay as though it simply got spared the worst of the storm, rather than as a
city that took the hit and kept moving through sheer structural and human effort.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone applying the
"spared/lucky" framing in good faith and someone who experiences that framing as an erasure of real,
costly work. This shouldn't play as outsider-villain versus local-hero — the "lucky exception" framing
is a natural, sympathetic way to talk about disaster survival, and whoever's using it likely means it
as a compliment, not a dismissal. The friction is that a compliment built on a wrong premise still
lands as an insult to the people who spent the storm keeping cranes upright and warehouses from
flooding. A good version of this conflict makes both sides' reasoning legible: A has no way to know
the framing costs anything, since from outside the difference between "spared" and "endured" looks
academic; B has no way to let it go, since the distinction is the whole of what the last several days
actually cost them.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a subnet-level relief
coordinator, comparative-damage assessor, or visiting reporter doing rounds across several
storm-hit Halley subnet cities, genuinely trying to give Sanay credit rather than take it away.
Character B is plausibly a dockworker foreman or shipyard supervisor who was physically present
through the worst of the storm — someone with mud and salt still on their boots when they hear their
own week described as good fortune, and who has the standing (through visible, recent, personal labor)
to say so in public without it reading as a complaint.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The city's whole civic self-image forms around correcting that assumption. It isn't a single speech
or a single decree — it's a wave of pushback from exactly the people who did the work, loud and
consistent enough that "spared" stops being a word anyone repeats twice in Sanay's presence.
"It wasn't luck, it was endurance" becomes the specific, insisted-upon correction, and the difference
between the two words becomes something residents treat as mattering enough to keep saying out loud.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Sanay came through functioning while its neighbors didn't, its docks become the de facto
rerouting point for subnet shipping during the recovery — freighters and inland trucking that would
normally have gone straight to a damaged neighbor get diverted through Sanay's still-working
infrastructure instead, on top of Sanay's own normal load.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That improvised rerouting role, run once out of simple necessity, quietly turns into a standing
expectation. Neighboring cities start assuming — without ever formally asking or crediting it — that
Sanay will absorb overflow capacity whenever the next crisis hits anyone else in the subnet, the same
uncredited-function pattern the city's civic identity already runs on, now attached to something
concrete rather than abstract.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sanay has won its argument about the storm — nobody who was there calls it luck anymore. But winning
that argument came bundled with an unspoken second job: the subnet's assumed overflow backbone during
any future disaster, a role nobody formally assigned and nobody has yet thought to formally thank the
city for holding.
