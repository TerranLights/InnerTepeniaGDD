## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early Sanay's dock economy staffs freighter-unloading crews with humans and robots working side by
side, roles assigned by whoever is genuinely best suited to a given task — nobody has formally decided
to favor either group, and nobody feels a need to. It's simply how a young, function-first port runs
when there's real work to do and not enough hands to waste time on hierarchy for its own sake.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

During a routine unloading run, a freighter's cargo shifts dangerously mid-transfer — a genuine
emergency, seconds from becoming a real disaster on the dock. A robot dockworker's split-second
judgment call is what actually prevents it: reading the shift before any human on the crew could, and
directing an immediate correction that holds the load. The human supervisor on duty, close by but not
the one who acted, is nonetheless who the official incident report credits with the save — not through
any deliberate dishonesty, but through an old, unconscious habit, carried over from Upper Earth, of
writing incident reports as though human judgment is what actually matters in a crisis.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** not a fight between someone defending robot dignity
and someone hostile to it — the supervisor who gets credited didn't ask for it and likely didn't notice
the report was wrong until it was pointed out. The real friction is between the crew (robot and human
alike, since the humans on shift saw exactly what happened too) who know the record is inaccurate, and
the slower, more institutional inertia of "that's just how the paperwork gets written," which nobody
individually chose but everyone has to actively push against to change. The strongest version of this
conflict has the credited supervisor genuinely uncomfortable once they realize what happened — not
defensive, embarrassed on the robot crewmate's behalf, which is part of what makes the push to correct
the record land as solidarity rather than accusation.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the robot dockworker
whose judgment call actually prevented the disaster, or a human crewmate who witnessed it directly and
refuses to let the report stand uncorrected. Character B is plausibly the credited human supervisor
themselves — not a villain, but the person who has to decide whether to quietly accept credit that isn't
theirs or push to have it reassigned, making them as much a protagonist of this correction as anyone
else.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The record gets corrected — not through a dramatic confrontation, but through the credited supervisor
themselves insisting on it once they understand what actually happened. The crew treats it as
unremarkable, exactly the kind of plain, function-first correction Sanay's own civic character already
runs on: not out of resentment, just a simple insistence on getting it right.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Fixing one report doesn't fix the underlying pattern. In the weeks that follow, crews start noticing
smaller versions of the same thing — incident logs, shift summaries, and informal credit all quietly
skewing human by old habit, never through malice, just through nobody having thought to check. Each one
gets corrected individually, but the correcting itself becomes its own small, recurring labor nobody
assigned anyone to do.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Rather than keep correcting incidents one at a time indefinitely, Sanay's dock leadership formally
reweights operational authority on the docks — not toward exact parity, but a deliberate, conscious tilt
calibrated specifically to counteract the exact bias the docks had just caught themselves making,
repeatedly, in the small print of their own paperwork. It isn't framed as a correction *for* robots; it's
framed as the docks finally writing down, on purpose, what had already been true in practice the whole
time whenever nobody was quietly miscrediting it.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Sanay's docks now run on a formally acknowledged tilt — robots holding slightly better positions than
their human counterparts, not as an unspoken accident of habit but as a deliberate, defended civic
choice the city can explain when asked. Generations later, once this same pattern gets generalized
project-wide as Tepenia's own default human-robot labor standard, nobody outside Sanay remembers that it
started here, with one incident report, one uncomfortable supervisor, and a crew that simply refused to
let a small inaccuracy stand.
