## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The old Novolazarevskaya core and the growing Maitri-site overflow have been functioning fine, day to
day, as one continuous urban fabric — shared infrastructure, shared labor, shared daily life — without
anyone ever formally declaring them a single city under one governing structure.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That informal coexistence starts running into problems only a single governing structure can actually
solve: resource disputes between the two areas, infrastructure planning that needs one coordinating
authority instead of two overlapping, informal ones, and disagreements over who's actually responsible
for what across a boundary nobody ever formally drew. The pressure builds until continuing to muddle
through informally is no longer sustainable, and it becomes clear that someone with real
administrative standing needs to step in and settle the question as an actual administrative fact
rather than an ongoing social drift.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** not a fight over whether to unify — the trigger's
pressure has already settled that — but over whose settlement's conventions the new unified government
inherits by default: which institutions become the model, what the combined city gets called, where
its seat of government physically sits. A, representing the older Novolazarevskaya-descended
population, favors continuity with the existing settlement's administrative habits, arguing there's no
reason to build new institutions from scratch when working ones already exist. B, representing the
Maitri-side population, argues that inheriting the older settlement's conventions wholesale isn't
neutral unification at all — it's one side quietly absorbing the other — and that genuine unification
needs newly, jointly designed institutions rather than simply adopting one side's playbook. Both have
a real point: A's practicality is genuine, but the path of least resistance always favors whoever
already has more institutional infrastructure, which is not the same thing as fairness.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an established
administrator or civic figure from the old Novolazarevskaya side, with genuine institutional
experience to offer and a practical urgency about solving the resource disputes quickly. Character B
is plausibly a community organizer or informal leader from the Maitri-side population, less
concerned with speed than with making sure the settlement is treated as a genuine partner rather than
an annex being absorbed into an existing structure — a role of the general kind already gestured at
by the civic-planner figure `Full_Extrapolation` §VIII proposes for this period, arbitrating between
exactly this pair of interests.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A planner is brought in specifically to arbitrate between A's and B's positions, and does formally
unify the two settlements' governance under real time pressure — but the urgency that forced the
question also constrains the answer. Building genuinely new joint institutions from scratch would take
too long to solve the disputes that made unification necessary in the first place, so the resulting
framework leans heavily on the Novolazarevskaya side's pre-existing administrative conventions.
Real concessions are written in for the Maitri side too — specific clauses in the new founding
charter acknowledging its distinct customs and guaranteeing it a defined voice in future governance —
but the overall shape favors one settlement's default over the other's. Lazar's most unified,
"grown together" identity carries a small, buried asymmetry from the exact moment it became official.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the unified government's core framework leans on Novolazarevskaya-side convention, the small
number of formal concessions written in for the Maitri side become the specific, named clauses
everyone points back to later whenever the asymmetry gets raised — functioning less as genuine parity
and more as visible, on-the-record proof that the drafters knew about the imbalance and made at least
a partial attempt to address it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because those concession-clauses exist, are named, and are known, they become the actual legal and
procedural mechanism later civic reformers or Maitri-descended representatives reach for whenever they
want to argue for further adjustments to the city's governance. What was meant as a one-time gesture
of balance instead becomes a permanent, reusable lever written directly into the city's own founding
charter — ensuring the asymmetry the planner tried to smooth over stays a live, arguable issue rather
than a settled one.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Lazar has a single, formally unified city government dating to the First Interwar Period, built mostly
on the older Novolazarevskaya settlement's institutional habits, with a handful of specific, named
concessions to the Maitri side written into its founding charter — concessions that, rather than
closing the asymmetry, remain the standing mechanism by which it keeps getting reopened whenever
governance questions resurface.
