# Halley — Candidate Courses of Events

**Scope note (revised):** all ten chains below are set strictly *before* the Long Night War, during
the Second Interwar Period when Tepenia is a full, functioning country — no war damage, no
post-war/present-day material. 10 candidate But/Therefore chains built from Halley's Megasheet (the
city that moves to stay ahead of the Brunt Ice Shelf's drift, its Sanay-nexus tradeoff, its total
dependency on overland resupply, Position Day). Post-settlement only; nothing here is canon.

---

## 1. Built to Outrun the Ice

Early residents face the same choice every ice-shelf settlement eventually confronts: wait for a
crisis and evacuate, or build for permanent movement from day one. **But** waiting for crisis means
losing whatever can't be moved in time. **Therefore** the founding generation commits to ski-mounted,
continuously propelled modules — an answer to the ice that never stops being applied. **But**
committing to permanent motion means the city can never again treat any single position as home.
**Therefore** "where we stand right now" becomes, for the first time anywhere in the subnet, an
ongoing civic question instead of a settled fact.

## 2. Trading the Nexus for the Name

Halley's own residents have the labor and the will to build the subnet's Arcanet relay nexus by hand.
**But** Sanay's bedrock is the only stable ground to actually put it on. **Therefore** Halley builds
it anyway, at Sanay, accepting that the subnet's technical heart will sit somewhere else. **But**
the subnet still needed a name, and Halley's was already the one everyone used. **Therefore** Halley
keeps the naming honor while Sanay quietly keeps the actual control — a trade nobody renegotiates for
generations, because nobody minds enough to ask.

## 3. No Harbor, By Design

Early planners consider building fixed docks and an airstrip, the way most coastal cities eventually
do. **But** a city whose whole architecture exists because the ground itself moves would just watch
the ice carry any fixed maritime or aviation infrastructure away from anywhere useful. **Therefore**
Halley deliberately builds neither, betting everything on overland resupply instead. **But** that bet
means total dependence on whichever neighbor currently has an open route. **Therefore** Halley's
entire food and materials security becomes hostage to Belgrano's and Sanay's own good health, a
dependency the city chose with open eyes and never found a way around.

## 4. The Record That Outlives the Old Ground

Halley's oldest positions don't linger as a visitable abandoned townsite the way a settlement that
simply relocates would normally leave behind — they're carried out to sea by the same ice that once
held them. **But** a city built on continuous motion still wants to know its own history.
**Therefore** Position Day becomes an annual, half-ceremonial survey: recording exactly where the
city stands relative to where it started. **But** as older positions finally slip past the calving
edge, the survey record becomes the only place that history still exists at all. **Therefore** losing
Position Day's archive would cost Halley something no other Tepenian city could lose the same way — a
past with no physical site left standing to visit.

## 5. Newer Legs, Older Bones

Halley's oldest modules, built before the propulsion-track system reached its current form, get less
benefit from continuous relocation than newer ones do. **But** an unusually early, unusually severe
calving event on the Brunt Ice Shelf doesn't wait for those older sections to be upgraded.
**Therefore** when the ice gives way faster than anyone predicted, the newest, most actively
maintained modules move clear in time while the oldest ones — already closer to the exposed edge,
already slower to relocate — barely escape with real damage. **But** that leaves a visibly split
city: fully mobile and functioning at one end, patched together and shaken at the other.
**Therefore** for years afterward, Halley's geography reads as a literal timeline of its own
engineering history, oldest and most vulnerable sections still furthest from full recovery.

## 6. The Plan Nobody Wrote

Halley's entire civic confidence rests on one assumption: the collective choice to keep moving will
always get made. **But** confidence built on an assumption is not the same as a plan for what happens
if the assumption stops holding. **Therefore** the city never writes a formal contingency for losing
its own relocation program — doing so would mean admitting the confidence might someday run out.
**But** a real scare — a near-failure of the propulsion system, a season where too few hands are left
to run it — eventually tests that gap directly. **Therefore** Halley survives the scare, but is left
facing, for the first time, the vulnerability its own culture had never let itself name.

## 7. The Season the Road Closed

Halley's overland resupply chain through Belgrano and Sanay runs so smoothly for so many generations
that the city never seriously tests what happens if it fails. **But** an unusually severe ice season
closes the connector road to Hwy 7 for months longer than any recorded closure before it.
**Therefore** Halley faces, for the first time since founding, a real test of its own deliberate
choice to forgo fixed docks and an airstrip — genuine shortages mounting with no alternative route to
fall back on. **But** there's no backup supply chain to switch to, precisely because the city never
built one. **Therefore** the closure becomes the moment residents finally reckon, out loud, with just
how much they'd bet on Belgrano and Sanay always having an open route — and how little margin that bet
actually left them.

## 8. The Courier Who Never Stops

Salagéa Aparast, a Belgrano native, becomes known to Halley's port the same way she's known at every
other Atlantic subnet stop — never settling, never claiming one city as hers. **But** Halley's own
residents, watching her pass through again and again, start treating her almost as one of their own
anyway. **Therefore** an offer eventually comes — stay, take up a fixed post at Halley's port instead
of running the whole circuit. **But** accepting would undo the exact continuous movement her value as
a courier depends on. **Therefore** she declines, and Halley learns something about its own
relationship to permanence by watching someone choose motion over belonging, the same choice the city
itself made generations earlier.

## 9. One Road, Two Signals

Hwy 59 is built from early on to serve two purposes at once: a physical highway, and the Arcanet
cable corridor running south to Amundsen Station. **But** maintaining both functions along the same
route means any disruption to the highway threatens Halley's communications link to the Pole too, not
just its travel route. **Therefore**, when the ice shelf drift finally forces the highway's Halley-end
anchor point to be relocated, engineers face a genuinely doubled project — rerouting the physical road
and preserving the cable's continuity together, a combination no other Tepenian highway project has
to manage. **But** splitting the two functions onto separate routes would be safer, just far more
expensive. **Therefore** Halley commits to keeping them combined, betting that one shared corridor is
worth the compounded risk — a bet that shapes how seriously the city treats every later stretch of
Hwy 59's upkeep.

## 10. The Fifth Position

Generations of continuous relocation put real distance between Halley's current position and its
founding site. **But** the founding position, sitting closer to the calving edge with every passing
year, was never actually protected — it was simply left behind. **Therefore** the day finally comes
when survey records confirm the original site has calved into the sea entirely, gone rather than
merely abandoned. **But** losing it changes nothing about Halley's day-to-day survival — the city
moved on from that ground generations ago. **Therefore** the loss lands as pure symbolic weight rather
than practical crisis: proof the city's whole philosophy was right all along, and also the quiet end
of any physical place residents could still call "where we started."

## 11. Wrong, and Then What

In the earliest founding years, before "Built to Move" is settled civic doctrine, planners keep having
to revise relocation routes on the fly — the ice never quite behaves the way surveys predict it will.
**But** an early miscalculation is worse than most: a route calculated as safe for the next relocation
cycle turns out to be wrong, forcing an emergency reroute with real modules already in motion.
**Therefore** the crew responsible, rather than treating the error as a failure to bury, does something
different — they document exactly what the surveys got wrong and exactly how the emergency reroute
actually worked, treating the correction itself as the real deliverable. **But** one good recovery
doesn't prove anything; it could simply be luck. **Therefore** when a second, unrelated miscalculation
tests the same instinct months later and the same documented-correction approach holds up again, the
idea stops being one crew's habit and becomes something residents start deliberately naming and
invoking — the actual founding moment of what later crystallizes into The Methodologists, and the first
time "rigor is whatever survives being wrong about the ground under your feet" gets said out loud rather
than simply practiced.

---

## Worth Your Attention

**#11** gives The Methodologists' own named civic philosophy an actual founding moment, distinct from
chains #1 and #6, which show the philosophy already *applied* — this is specifically the moment it
became a named, citable school of thought rather than an unspoken individual instinct, the same origin-
of-doctrine move Sanay's own chain #10 made for "Competence Without Commentary." **#4** and **#10** work as a matched pair — the archive that's Halley's only remaining history, and
the actual moment its founding ground disappears, giving that archive real, felt stakes rather than
abstract ones. **#6** is the most structurally significant on its own: it's the only thread that turns
the Full Extrapolation's most pointed finding (no contingency plan, and that absence *is* the risk)
into an actual tested event rather than a standing description.
