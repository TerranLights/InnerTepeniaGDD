## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Halley's oldest modules, built before the current propulsion-track system reached its mature form, get
measurably less benefit from continuous relocation than the newer sections do — a known, tolerated gap
in an otherwise well-functioning city, the kind of thing that gets fixed eventually, on nobody's
particular urgent timeline.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

An unusually early and unusually severe calving event on the Brunt Ice Shelf doesn't wait for that
gap to close on its own schedule. The newest, most actively maintained modules — already closer to
full operational readiness — move clear in time. The oldest sections, already slower to relocate and
already sitting nearer the exposed edge, barely escape at all, and don't escape undamaged. What's left
afterward is a visibly split city: one end fully mobile and functioning normally, the other patched
together, shaken, and now conspicuously behind.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a resource-triage argument in the immediate
aftermath, over where scarce engineering labor and materials should go first. A represents the
damaged old quarter's own interest — wanting the city's limited recovery capacity spent bringing the
older sections back to parity with the rest of Halley, on the reasoning that no resident should be left
permanently behind because of when their section happened to be built. B argues the opposite: that
finite resources spent chasing parity for structurally older, harder-to-modernize sections are
resources not spent hardening and expanding the newer fleet that actually keeps the whole city safe
going forward — and that the city's real future depends on doubling down on what already works, not on
restoring what was already falling behind before the calving event ever happened. Both positions carry
real stakes for real people; this isn't callousness against sentiment, it's two different theories of
what "protecting Halley" actually requires under scarcity.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a resident or
informal representative of the older sections themselves — someone whose home, workplace, and
community are the ones now visibly behind, arguing from lived stakes rather than abstract policy.
Character B is plausibly the city's chief propulsion engineer or a forward-looking civic planner,
someone whose job is literally keeping the whole city moving and who has to make the case that the
newer fleet's continued strength is what protects everyone, old quarter included, over the long run.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Character B's resource argument prevails: the city commits primary investment to the modern fleet,
while the old quarter receives only enough stabilization work to keep it safe, not enough to bring it
to real parity. The "living timeline" appearance the calving event left behind — modern and functioning
at one end, patched and aged at the other — becomes, by this decision, a semi-permanent feature of
Halley's geography rather than a temporary post-crisis look awaiting repair.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Left without parity investment, the old quarter's residents develop a distinct local identity of their
own over the following years — a counter-culture within Halley that treats its visible age and its
scars as something closer to a badge than a wound, a pointed contrast to the rest of the city's usual
always-moving-forward self-image.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That counter-identity, once it exists as a real community self-conception rather than just a physical
condition, becomes a genuine internal political voice. Old-quarter residents start organizing,
specifically, to demand a permanent seat in future relocation-and-investment planning — insisting that
which sections get upgraded first never again gets decided unilaterally by engineers the way it was
after the calving event, but through the same kind of open deliberation Halley already applies to its
broader relocation questions.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Halley's geography still reads, years later, as a literal timeline of its own engineering history —
newer, fully modern sections at one end, an older quarter proud of its scars at the other. What began
as an emergency triage decision has calcified into a lasting internal political fault line: a
self-aware old-quarter identity with a real, organized claim on how the city's future investment gets
decided.
