## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Halley's earliest residents face the same choice every ice-shelf settlement eventually confronts:
wait for a crisis and evacuate when it comes, or build for permanent movement from the very first
season. Nothing about the site forces the answer yet — the shelf is still moving at its ordinary
pace, and either approach could still work on paper.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The arithmetic settles it before anyone gets to debate it at length: waiting for a crisis to force
evacuation means losing whatever can't be packed and moved in the time a real crisis actually allows,
and on a shelf drifting 400–700 meters a year, that's not a hypothetical, it's a schedule. The
founding generation commits to ski-mounted, continuously propelled modules — an answer to the ice
applied permanently, every season, rather than held in reserve for the day it's needed. But that
commitment carries a cost nobody fully reckons with at the moment of deciding it: to build for
permanent motion is to give up, forever, the option of treating any one position as simply home. The
first modules are barely operational before residents start to feel the shape of what they've traded
away.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over what the propulsion system
actually *is* once it's built — a finished technical solution the city can now set down and stop
thinking about, or a standing civic responsibility that has to be actively re-chosen every time the
question of "where do we go next" comes up. This isn't reformer-vs.-obstructionist; both positions are
reasonable engineering-era instincts. A wants closure: the system works, the city should get on with
ordinary civic life instead of treating its own location as perpetually unsettled. B wants the
opposite: precisely because the system works, the temptation to stop asking "is this still the right
position, the right pace, the right direction" is the real danger — a solved problem that quietly
stops being monitored is how a moving city ends up somewhere it never chose to be.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly one of the
founding-era engineers who built the propulsion system itself — proud of the work, invested in seeing
it treated as done, tired of having the same argument restated as if the module tracks might fail
tomorrow. Character B is plausibly an early civic organizer or elder with no direct technical stake in
the system's design, whose worry isn't about the hardware at all but about what happens to a
community's judgment once it stops treating its own survival as an open question. The tension reads
best as sincere disagreement between two people who both want Halley to survive, not as competence
versus complacency.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Character B's view wins out, not by argument alone but by becoming civic practice: relocation
decisions — when to move, how far, toward what heading — get put to open, recurring collective
deliberation rather than left to stand as a settled technical matter once the modules are built. For
the first time anywhere in the subnet, "where we stand right now" becomes an ongoing civic question
instead of a settled fact, revisited on a real, recognized schedule rather than only when forced by
crisis.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The habit of relocation-debate hardens into an actual civic institution within a generation — regular,
expected gatherings where residents review the shelf's current drift, argue over pace and heading, and
formally re-affirm or revise the plan. No neighboring subnet city debates its own physical location
this way; for Halley, it becomes simply how the city governs itself.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That deliberative habit doesn't stay confined to relocation questions. A civic culture trained to
treat its own most fundamental fact — where the city physically is — as open for regular collective
renegotiation carries the same instinct into other major decisions, including, generations later, ones
that have nothing to do with propulsion tracks at all. The willingness to give up a settled answer in
favor of an actively re-chosen one becomes Halley's default posture toward big civic questions
generally, not just its relationship to the ice.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Halley now governs its own location the way no other Tepenian city governs anything physical: as a
live, recurring collective decision rather than a settled inheritance. That same deliberative, control-
loosening instinct — visible here first, in the willingness to keep the question of "home" perpetually
open — is the same civic reflex that will later let Halley give up its own Arcanet nexus without much
of a fight, when the moment comes.
