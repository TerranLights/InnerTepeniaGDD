## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Halley's entire civic confidence rests on one unexamined assumption: that the collective choice to
keep the city moving will always, generation after generation, actually get made. The system has never
failed. Nobody has had real reason to ask what happens if it someday does.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Confidence built on an assumption is not the same thing as a plan for what happens if the assumption
stops holding — and Halley has never written that plan, for a reason nobody says out loud but everyone
somehow understands: putting it into words would mean admitting the confidence might someday run out.
That gap between felt security and actual preparedness stays comfortably theoretical for generations,
right up until a real scare tests it directly — a near-failure in the propulsion system, compounded by
a season where too few trained hands are left to run it at full capacity. For the first time in living
memory, the question of what happens if the city can't move stops being unthinkable and starts being
immediate.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a conflict over whether to finally break a
long-standing cultural taboo. A, badly shaken by how close the scare actually came, wants Halley to
write a real, formal contingency plan now — something the city has never had. B resists, not out of
denial or negligence, but from a genuine, considered belief that formally naming the risk would corrode
the very confidence that has kept the city functioning and moving successfully for generations; a
written admission that the system might fail is, in B's view, its own kind of self-fulfilling danger.
This should read as a real philosophical disagreement about what keeps a fragile system stable —
honest acknowledgment of risk, or unbroken collective confidence — not a fight between someone sensible
and someone in denial.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the engineer or crew
lead who lived through the near-failure directly, hands-on, and can't unsee how close it came. Character
B is plausibly a senior civic or cultural figure — perhaps close to the Position Day tradition, or
otherwise a keeper of Halley's founding self-image — whose authority rests specifically on the
confidence A now wants to formally question.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side wins outright. No formal, city-wide contingency plan is ever adopted — B's resistance
holds at the level of official civic policy. But A and a small circle of allies begin, quietly and
without full civic authorization, keeping their own unofficial contingency notes and modest emergency
stockpiles on the side — a specific, concrete compromise that satisfies neither position completely but
lets both continue.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Over the following years, the existence of this quiet, informal contingency-keeping group becomes an
open secret rather than a real one. Publicly, Halley still says — and largely believes — that it has
no contingency plan, preserving the founding confidence intact. Privately, a growing number of people
know that a small group has been steadily preparing for exactly the scenario the city has never
officially named.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That open secret becomes, generations on, a subtle but real axis of civic stratification. Those "in
the know" about the informal contingency work — or descended from the original circle that started it
— hold a quiet, unofficial kind of standing that has nothing to do with wealth or role, and everything
to do with possessing contingency knowledge nobody else formally has. In an otherwise fairly
egalitarian city, this becomes one of the few genuinely unequal distributions of trust.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Halley still has no official contingency plan, and its founding confidence remains, on the surface,
fully intact. Underneath that surface, a small, semi-hereditary group with real, unofficial knowledge
of what to do if the city's central assumption ever fails has become an entrenched, unacknowledged
source of civic influence — the vulnerability the culture could never let itself name, quietly managed
by the very people the culture doesn't officially admit it needs.
