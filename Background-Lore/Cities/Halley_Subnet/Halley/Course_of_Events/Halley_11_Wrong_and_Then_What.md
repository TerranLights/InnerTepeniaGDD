## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

In Halley's earliest founding years, "Built to Move" is a practiced necessity, not yet a settled civic
doctrine. Planners keep having to revise relocation routes on short notice — the ice shelf's actual
behavior never quite matches what surveys predicted, and nobody has yet turned that recurring gap into
anything resembling a shared philosophy about it.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

An early miscalculation proves worse than the usual minor corrections: a route calculated as safe for the
next relocation cycle turns out to be wrong, and the error surfaces only after modules are already in
motion along it. An emergency reroute has to happen in real time, under real pressure, with no margin for
a second mistake.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** not a blame conflict, but a genuine disagreement
about what should happen *after* the emergency reroute succeeds — one figure wants the error quietly
absorbed and moved past, since the crisis is over and the crew performed well; another insists on
documenting exactly what the original survey got wrong and exactly how the correction worked, treating
the mistake itself as valuable data rather than an embarrassment to bury. Neither is wrong: A is
protecting morale and momentum in a young, still-fragile settlement; B is protecting the city's ability to
survive the *next* miscalculation, which both know is coming eventually.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a senior founding-era
administrator or crew lead, focused on keeping the settlement's confidence intact during its earliest,
most uncertain years. Character B is plausibly a surveyor or engineer directly involved in the original
miscalculation and its correction — someone with firsthand, specific knowledge of exactly what went wrong
and exactly what fixed it, unwilling to let that knowledge simply evaporate once the immediate danger
passes.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Character B's approach wins out, at least this once: the miscalculation and its correction get documented
in real detail, not buried. It's treated, at the time, as a reasonable but unremarkable precaution — good
practice, nothing more.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Months later, a second, unrelated miscalculation tests the same instinct again — a different route,
different circumstances, but the same underlying situation: survey confidence proven wrong, correction
needed fast. The crew handling it, aware of the earlier documented case, uses the same approach: treat the
error as data, document the correction, don't bury it.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the approach held up a second time, under genuinely different conditions, it stops reading as one
crew's personal habit and starts reading as something real — a repeatable, working method, not luck.
Residents start naming it explicitly, invoking it by name when a new miscalculation surfaces, rather than
simply doing what already worked without discussing why.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

What began as one crew's decision not to bury an uncomfortable error has become a named, citable civic
philosophy — the actual founding moment of what later crystallizes into The Methodologists. "Rigor is
whatever survives being wrong about the ground under your feet" is no longer just something Halley's
residents do; it's something they can now say out loud, point to, and teach the next generation directly.
