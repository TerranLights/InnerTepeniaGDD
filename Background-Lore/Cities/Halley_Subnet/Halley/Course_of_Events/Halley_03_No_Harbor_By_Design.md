## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early planners weigh the same infrastructure every other coastal Tepenian city eventually builds:
fixed shipping docks, a proper airstrip, permanent points of contact with the wider world. Nothing
about Halley's situation has ruled either one out yet — the question is still genuinely open.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The ground itself makes the case against permanence before the planners finish making it for
themselves: a city whose whole architecture exists because the ice beneath it moves would simply
watch any fixed maritime or aviation structure get carried off toward open water, a few hundred meters
at a time, year after year, until it sat somewhere no longer useful to anyone. Halley commits to
building neither, betting its entire resupply future on overland routes instead — a decision that
feels, in the moment, like clear-eyed realism rather than sacrifice. But the shape of what's actually
being risked only becomes visible afterward: total dependence not on infrastructure Halley controls,
but on whichever of Belgrano or Sanay currently happens to have an open route through to Hwy 7.
Halley's food and materials security is now, by its own deliberate choice, hostage to two other
cities' continued good health.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over whether committing fully to
the overland bet is the responsible choice or a needless gamble. A wants at least a modest hedge — a
small, periodically-rebuilt landing strip or dock, accepted as a recurring cost, purely as insurance
against the day both routes close at once. B argues that half-measures don't actually protect
anything; a structure sized to be "temporary enough to rebuild" is also too small to matter in a real
emergency, and the resources it consumes would do more good spent making the overland relationship
itself more resilient. Neither side is reckless — this is a genuine disagreement about what
"prepared" actually means for a city that can't build anything permanent.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an engineer or
planner with a risk-management bent, someone whose instinct is always to keep a fallback option alive
even at real recurring cost. Character B is plausibly a resource-realist civic leader — someone
responsible for Halley's actual, finite labor and materials budget, who has to weigh the hedge against
everything else that budget could buy, and who believes the more honest investment is in Belgrano's
and Sanay's own route health rather than in a doomed Halley-side fallback.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Character B's argument wins the resource fight: Halley commits fully, builds neither docks nor
airstrip, and formalizes total reliance on the overland connector to Hwy 7. The decision isn't framed
as a gamble accepted reluctantly — it's framed, publicly, as the only strategy actually consistent
with the city's own moving ground.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Having no fallback of its own, Halley starts actively investing outward instead of inward: rotating
residents through embedded liaison postings at Belgrano and Sanay, contributing labor and materials
toward the connector road's own upkeep, formalizing seasonal mutual-aid arrangements that give Halley
early warning — and some real influence — over route conditions it doesn't itself control.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

The rotating liaison posts create, over a few generations, a distinct class of Halley residents whose
whole career and identity is built around monitoring the health of routes that aren't Halley's own —
people who know, before almost anyone else in the city, whether this winter's resupply is secure. That
specialized knowledge becomes real, if informal, domestic influence: the liaison class ends up holding
a kind of civic authority nobody explicitly granted them, simply because they're the ones who actually
know whether the city eats.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Halley still has no docks, no airstrip, and no intention of building either. In their place stands a
network of embedded liaisons and mutual-aid ties binding the city to Belgrano's and Sanay's own
infrastructure health — and, alongside it, an internal class of route-monitoring specialists whose
quiet influence over Halley's domestic politics most residents have never quite had to reckon with
directly.
