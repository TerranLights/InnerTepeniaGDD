## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Halley's overland resupply chain through Belgrano and Sanay runs smoothly for so many consecutive
generations that the city never seriously tests what happens if it fails. The founding-era bet on
overland routes over fixed docks or an airstrip has, so far, simply worked.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

An unusually severe ice season closes the connector road to Hwy 7 for months longer than any recorded
closure before it. For the first time since founding, Halley faces a real, live test of its own
deliberate choice to forgo fixed maritime and aviation infrastructure — genuine shortages start
mounting, with no alternative route to fall back on, because the city never built one. The closure
doesn't ease; it holds, week after week, while stores run down.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a crisis-management conflict over whether to
hold the founding doctrine or break it under pressure. A wants to manage the shortage within Halley's
existing framework — organized rationing, calm coordination, trust that the route will reopen as it
always eventually has, and a refusal to panic into infrastructure the city deliberately chose not to
build. B wants to break with that doctrine right now, under real duress — pushing for an emergency
measure the city has never used, such as signaling for outside airlift assistance, even if it means
publicly admitting the no-fixed-infrastructure design has a real limit. A's caution isn't stubbornness;
panic-built infrastructure might not even arrive in time to matter. B's urgency isn't recklessness;
people are actually going hungry while the debate continues.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civic leader or
rationing coordinator, someone responsible for managing the shortage day to day and personally invested
in Halley's founding identity holding up under real pressure. Character B is plausibly someone with
direct exposure to the shortage's human cost — a logistics worker, a parent, or one of the route
liaisons Halley has stationed at Belgrano or Sanay — whose position is shaped by proximity to the
people actually going without, rather than by loyalty to civic doctrine.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's emergency proposal is never actually implemented — days before it would have been forced into
action, an emergency mutual-aid supply push from Sanay, organized through Halley's own route liaisons,
gets pushed through a secondary, previously little-used overland track and reaches the city with real
margin to spare. The immediate crisis ends without the founding doctrine being broken. But the debate
itself, conducted openly and urgently in front of the whole city, becomes exactly the public reckoning
the near-crisis demanded: residents acknowledge, collectively and out loud for the first time, just how
thin the margin actually was.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

In the reckoning's aftermath, Halley doesn't abandon its no-fixed-infrastructure doctrine — but it does
adopt something it never bothered maintaining before: a formal emergency reserve stockpile, sized
specifically to cover a worst-case closure of at least the length just survived.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Maintaining that reserve requires ongoing labor, storage space, and civic investment that previously
went toward other priorities — and becomes, over the following years, a recurring point of civic
argument in its own right. In good years, when the memory of the closure fades, some push to shrink or
repurpose the reserve, arguing the scare was a one-time fluke not worth the permanent cost; others
insist the reserve is exactly what proved its worth and must be maintained regardless of how long the
good years run.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Halley still has no docks and no airstrip, and still bets its survival on Belgrano's and Sanay's own
open routes. But it now carries a hard-won, periodically contested emergency reserve as well — proof
the city can adapt its practice without abandoning its founding philosophy, and a permanent, recurring
civic argument about exactly how much of the city's labor and space that caution is still worth.
