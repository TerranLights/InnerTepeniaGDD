## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Halley's oldest positions don't remain behind as a visitable abandoned townsite, the way a settlement
that simply relocates once would normally leave one. The same ice that once held them carries them out
to sea instead — there is no ruin to walk out to, no foundation left standing to point at.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A city built on continuous motion still wants, understandably, to know its own history — and the
absence of any physical site to visit makes that desire sharper, not weaker. Position Day begins as
the answer: an annual, half-ceremonial survey recording exactly where the city currently stands
relative to where it started, part civic ritual, part practical record-keeping. It works, and keeps
working, for generations, treated mostly as tradition. But as the oldest positions finally slip past
the calving edge one by one, lost to open water rather than simply left behind, the survey record
stops being a pleasant yearly custom and starts becoming something else: increasingly, it is the only
place Halley's own early history exists at all.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a disagreement over how much institutional
weight a beloved but lightly-run tradition deserves once its stakes have quietly changed. A wants to
keep Position Day exactly as it's always been — a warm, low-overhead yearly custom — and resists
turning it into bureaucratic overhead, worried that formalizing it would strip out the very thing that
makes people show up and care. B has done the math on what's actually being lost as older positions
calve away and wants real archival redundancy: multiple copies, secure and separate storage,
cross-referencing against other cities' records where possible. This isn't ceremony-lovers versus
bureaucrats — A's worry about killing the tradition by over-formalizing it is legitimate, and so is B's
worry that a single, un-backed-up record is one bad season away from losing Halley's entire early
history for good.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a long-standing
Position Day custodian — someone in a role close to the "Position Keeper" figure already proposed for
Halley (`Full_Extrapolation` §VIII), personally invested in the ritual's warmth and continuity. Character
B is plausibly a younger records-conscious historian or archivist, someone who has actually sat down
and worked out how many of the founding-era positions are already gone and how thin the surviving
record now is.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two positions merge rather than one defeating the other: Position Day keeps its full ceremonial
character, but the survey record itself is formally elevated — copied, and the copies distributed
across several physically separate Halley modules, specifically so that no single calving event or
structural loss can take the whole record with it.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With multiple redundant copies now genuinely cross-referenceable against each other for the first
time, patterns become visible in the record that a single unified copy never made practical to trace —
how far the city moved per era, correlated against which generation of propulsion technology was in
use at the time. An informal discipline of position-history analysis starts to take shape around the
archive, something closer to applied engineering history than ceremony.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That discipline, once it exists, becomes genuinely useful beyond Halley itself: it's the only
multi-century, continuous record anywhere in the subnet of exactly how fast and how far a moving
structure actually needs to be engineered to reliably outrun ice drift. Engineers working on
relocation or propulsion problems elsewhere in the subnet start seeking out Halley's own
position-history specialists specifically for that data — expertise the redundant archive made
possible to develop in the first place.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Position Day is now both what it always was — a warm, half-ceremonial yearly custom — and something
new: a formally protected, redundantly stored civic archive that has, almost by accident, made Halley
a quiet subnet authority on relocation-engineering data, sought out by outsiders for knowledge no other
city's history could have produced.
