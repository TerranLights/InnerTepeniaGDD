## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

The founding exile community names the city after a real Belgian princess, carrying genuine historical
weight for the generation that chose it. Nobody who lived through the founding needs the name explained to
them.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Two centuries and two dominant later immigration currents mean fewer and fewer residents have any personal
connection to Belgium at all. The name survives while its meaning thins out, generation by generation, into
the kind of fact a resident might know without feeling anything about it — a street sign, not a memory. But
the city's own small Belgian-heritage community keeps something closer to the original weight alive in
private, and as that community watches the wider city's indifference grow, some of its members start to
worry that "half-forgotten" is only one more generation away from "fully forgotten."

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a keeper of Belgian-heritage
tradition — someone organizing to preserve the founding memory more deliberately, worried that the wider
city's casual indifference amounts to a slow erasure of the very people who built it — and a pragmatic civic
figure who does not devalue that heritage but resists dedicating real civic prominence or resources to
reviving a founding-nation identity the city's actual lived demographics no longer reflect. This should not
play as sentiment vs. coldness. B's position is a defensible reading of what the city has actually become;
A's position is a defensible refusal to let that fact erase something true and real just because it's now a
minority experience.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a Belgian-heritage
community organizer, archivist, or elder, personally motivated by watching their own family's memory of the
founding thin out in their own children. Character B is plausibly a civic administrator or a figure from one
of the two now-dominant currents (American or Japanese), reasonably focused on where the city's limited
civic attention and resources are best spent given who actually lives here now.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A genuine compromise, not a full victory for either side: the Belgian-heritage community secures modest but
real civic backing — a small heritage archive and reading room, and a minor annual observance formally
placed on the city calendar alongside, but clearly smaller in scale than, Founders' Turbine and The Crossing
(Suggestion #6). The wider city is not asked to re-center its identity around Belgium; the memory is simply
given a permanent, findable home instead of being left to survive purely on private, informal effort.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because a real, findable archive now exists, the city's connection to its own name stops being a dead end.
A curious resident — or, decades later, a curious visitor — can actually go and find something concrete
about who Princess Elisabeth of Belgium was and why the city carries her name, rather than the name simply
being an unexplainable fact everyone has stopped asking about.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the archive is small, specifically Belgian-heritage-run, and — for years — the only dedicated
records office of its kind in the city, it also ends up quietly absorbing responsibility for preserving
records of the city's *other* founding-era decisions as well, simply because nobody else was archiving
anything else that early. The Belgian-heritage community becomes, almost by accident, the institutional
memory-keepers for Princess Elisabeth's whole founding period — not just their own heritage specifically.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The name "Princess Elisabeth" remains functional trivia to most residents day-to-day. But for the first
time, the city has an actual formal, accessible institution — modest in scale, but permanent — preserving
both the Belgian founding heritage and, almost incidentally, much of the city's broader founding-era
history. Private memory now has a real, findable home, rather than surviving only in individual families and
fading a little further with each generation.
