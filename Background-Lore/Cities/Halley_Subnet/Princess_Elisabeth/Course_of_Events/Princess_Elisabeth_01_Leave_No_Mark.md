## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Early exiles settle a site so exposed to katabatic winds that nothing about daily survival can ever be
passive. There is no shelter of terrain, no windbreak, no gentler stretch of ground to retreat to — every
decision about how to live here has to account for the wind first.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

The obvious answer — fossil fuel generation, the kind every other early exile settlement leans on to get
through its first hard years — turns out to be its own kind of danger here specifically. Constant resupply
runs across the same hostile, wind-scoured terrain would mean regularly gambling lives on the very exposure
the site is defined by. So the founding community commits instead to wind and solar power, zero waste
discharge, systems built to generate everything the city needs on-site, nothing trucked or shipped in. It is
the right call on paper. But a system with no outside fallback has to be actively balanced and maintained
without fail, every single day, forever — and "forever" is a much longer commitment than the founding
generation, exhausted and improvising, has fully reckoned with yet. The question of how absolute that
commitment should really be is still unsettled when the first hard winter arrives.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone who wants a modest
fossil-fuel safety margin kept in reserve — insurance against a wind lull or storm damage severe enough to
threaten lives before repairs can be made — and someone who insists that any fossil dependency at all, even
a small emergency-only allowance, reintroduces exactly the resupply vulnerability the site's exposure makes
catastrophic in the first place. This should not read as caution vs. recklessness. A's proposed margin is a
reasonable, standard practice anywhere else in Tepenia; B's objection is not paranoia but a correct read of
this particular site's specific danger. The disagreement is really about what "zero-emissions" is *for* —
A treats it as the primary policy with a survival exception carved out; B treats survival itself as the
reason the policy has to be absolute, with no exception large enough to be safe.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a founding-era logistics
or resource-allocation figure, someone whose job is keeping the exile community alive through the near term
and who is naturally drawn to hedged, conventional risk management. Character B is plausibly one of the
site's original design engineers or a philosophically committed founding leader — someone who chose this
specific, brutal site on purpose and has already done the math on what a single resupply run across open,
wind-scoured ground actually costs in risk. Neither needs to be right about everything; A's caution is
legitimate risk management, B's absolutism is a correct read of this specific terrain, and the city has to
choose between two forms of reasonable.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

B's position wins, but not as an abstract philosophical victory — it wins because the community formally
writes it into the terms the city is founded on. No fossil-fuel reserve, no exception, no emergency
allowance: a founding covenant establishing that the city runs entirely on wind, solar, and its own stored
capacity, full stop. "Leave no mark" stops being environmental policy layered onto an existing settlement
and becomes the literal, non-negotiable precondition for staying alive here at all.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With no chemical or fuel-based fallback permitted even in principle, the city has nowhere to look but its
own people for margin. Engineers respond by building an unusually rigorous, round-the-clock human
maintenance culture — overlapping monitoring teams, redundant eyes on every critical system at all hours —
because if the machines cannot be allowed a backup, the humans watching them have to become one instead.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That round-the-clock watching stops feeling like an emergency measure and calcifies into ordinary civic
practice — a culture where paying close, constant attention to the systems keeping everyone alive is simply
what living in Princess Elisabeth means, generation after generation. It is this specific habit, born from
the covenant's total inflexibility rather than from the original resupply-risk argument itself, that later
becomes the seed of the city's formal engineering-apprenticeship traditions.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Princess Elisabeth now runs on an absolute, founding-law commitment to zero fossil dependency — and on a
deeply ingrained civic habit of constant, shared, round-the-clock vigilance over the systems that make that
commitment survivable. The city has no fallback built into its infrastructure at all; what margin it has
lives entirely in its people's attention, and everyone alive here has inherited some share of the job of
providing it.
