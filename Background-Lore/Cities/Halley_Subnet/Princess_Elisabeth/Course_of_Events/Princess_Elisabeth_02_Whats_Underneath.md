## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Engineers realize early on that the surface here is too exposed to safely house any redundant backup
capacity — nothing above ground survives the wind reliably enough to trust it with the city's margin of
safety. Whatever backup the city builds, it cannot live on the surface.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Going without any backup at all leaves the city with zero margin if its primary systems ever falter, even
briefly — an unacceptable risk once the founding generation's absolute zero-fossil-fallback commitment
removes the one conventional hedge most other settlements would reach for instead. So the city commits to
something genuinely ambitious: a buried energy reserve — flywheels, battery banks, thermal storage — dug
deep enough to escape the wind entirely and tied into the same tunnels already carrying ground-connected
heating. It is a sound plan. But building anything underground on this terrain proves slower and far more
expensive than anyone budgeted for, and as the years pass and the costs climb, the project starts eating
into resources the young exile community needs for other, more immediate things.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone responsible for
allocating the exile community's still-limited resources across many pressing needs — housing, food
security, day-to-day survival — who watches the reserve project blow through every budget projection and
threaten those other necessities, and someone overseeing the reserve's construction, for whom halting
partway means a half-dug, structurally useless hole in the ground and an unresolved vulnerability worse
than never having started. Both are managing real risk to the same community; they simply disagree about
which risk is more immediate. This should not resolve as one side being obviously right — the budget
holder's caution is protecting people alive today, the engineer's insistence is protecting people who will
need the reserve someday, and the city genuinely cannot know in advance which need will turn out to matter
more.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a founding-era
resource-allocation administrator or council figure, someone who has to look the exile community's other
unmet needs in the eye every time the reserve's budget grows. Character B is plausibly the reserve project's
chief engineer or site foreman, someone who has already sunk years of their own expertise and the
community's trust into a project they believe is the single most important thing being built in the city.
The tension reads best as two forms of responsibility in genuine conflict, not stubbornness against reason.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The project is not canceled, but neither side gets an easy win: the reserve is finished, years later than
planned and having cost the young city more than any project it has ever undertaken, funded only by
repeatedly deferring other development the budget holder had originally hoped to protect. It becomes,
against every early projection, the single most expensive thing Princess Elisabeth has ever built — and the
literal foundation its later reputation for total self-sufficiency will rest on.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The extraordinary, sustained cost forces Princess Elisabeth to lean on outside help it would otherwise never
have needed — formally requesting extended material and resource assistance from neighboring Halley subnet
cities for the years the reserve consumes, and deferring several of its own other development projects
during that same stretch to keep the excavation funded.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That period of leaning on outside support becomes, over time, a quietly humbling thread in the city's own
founding memory — because it happened specifically while building the very thing that would define
Princess Elisabeth's self-sufficient reputation, the city's later pride in "needing nothing from outside"
carries, underneath it, the inconvenient fact that reaching total independence required real, acknowledged
outside help to get there. That tension in the story — never fully resolved, occasionally uncomfortable to
tell — becomes part of how the city eventually learns to talk about its own achievements to visitors.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

The buried reserve stands complete, tied into the same tunnels as the city's ground-connected heating —
Princess Elisabeth's genuine, hard-won passive margin, escaping the wind entirely. But the city's self-image
around it is more complicated than pure pride: the years of externally-facing dependency it took to build a
fully independent system are remembered too, tempering the achievement with the plain fact that it was never
built entirely alone.
