# Princess Elisabeth — Candidate Courses of Events

**Scope note (revised):** all ten chains below are set strictly *before* the Long Night War, during
the Second Interwar Period when Tepenia is a full, functioning country. Princess Elisabeth's own
established fate (destroyed, ruins, a small straggling survivor population) is entirely out of scope
here — these chains cover only the living city's earlier history, before any of that. 10 candidate
But/Therefore chains built from Princess Elisabeth's Megasheet (the zero-emissions founding
philosophy, total engineered self-sufficiency, the demographic crossroads between two roughly-equal
founding currents). Post-settlement only; nothing here is canon.

---

## 1. Leave No Mark

Early exiles settle a site so exposed to katabatic winds that nothing about daily survival can ever
be passive. **But** fossil fuel dependency would mean constant, vulnerable resupply runs across that
same hostile terrain. **Therefore** the founding community commits to wind and solar power, zero
waste discharge, systems that generate everything the city needs on-site. **But** a system with no
outside fallback has to be actively balanced and maintained without fail, every single day, forever.
**Therefore** "leave no mark" stops being environmental policy and becomes the literal precondition
for staying alive here at all.

## 2. What's Underneath

Engineers realize early on that the surface here is too exposed to safely house any redundant backup
capacity — nothing above ground survives the wind reliably enough to trust it. **But** going without
any backup leaves the city with zero margin if the primary systems ever falter, even briefly.
**Therefore** the city commits to something genuinely ambitious: a buried energy reserve —
flywheels, battery banks, thermal storage — dug deep enough to escape the wind entirely and tied into
the same tunnels already carrying ground-connected heating. **But** building anything underground on
this terrain proves slower and more expensive than anyone budgeted for. **Therefore** the reserve
takes years longer to finish than planned, becoming the single most expensive project the small city
has ever undertaken — and the one its whole later reputation for total self-sufficiency actually
depends on.

## 3. A Design Worth Copying

Princess Elisabeth's zero-emissions systems prove so effective that word of them spreads to other
Halley subnet cities weighing their own infrastructure investments. **But** the design was built
specifically for this site's unique total wind and solar exposure — nothing that simply transplants
elsewhere without real adaptation. **Therefore** Princess Elisabeth's own engineers find themselves
consulting on other cities' projects for the first time, translating a design built out of necessity
into something outsiders can actually learn from. **But** teaching it to outsiders means admitting
parts of the system were never formally documented at all — just built and maintained by feel.
**Therefore** the consulting requests force the city to systematically document its own founding
engineering achievement for the first time, ironically only once someone outside finally asks.

## 4. A Waypoint, Never a Shrine

Princess Elisabeth sits closer to Dome Fuji than any other Halley subnet city, right along the route
pilgrims traveling that distance would need to cross. **But** the city itself has no religious
tradition of its own tied to Dome Fuji or Kunlun's sacred status. **Therefore** it settles into a
modest, informal role anyway — the last practical stop before the long stretch toward the mountain,
useful without ever asking to be meaningful. **But** usefulness like that still creates real, if quiet,
connections between residents and passing pilgrims over generations. **Therefore** Princess Elisabeth
becomes a genuine, permanent fixture of Dome Fuji's own pilgrim routes — a waypoint that never once
needed to be sacred itself to matter to the people passing through it.

## 5. Two Currents Meeting in the Middle

Princess Elisabeth's unusually eastern position draws both the subnet's usual Atlantic-side American
current and a genuinely distinct Japanese current no other Halley city absorbs in comparable
strength. **But** two roughly-equal Primary populations means neither one gets to simply define the
city the way a single dominant nation would elsewhere. **Therefore** daily civic life settles into
constant, low-key negotiation between two cultural currents that never fully merge and never fully
separate either. **But** that negotiation stays practical and demographic — it never develops into
anything spiritual or ceremonial the way Dome Fuji's own crossroads status did. **Therefore** Princess
Elisabeth becomes a genuine crossroads that never once thought of itself as sacred, just as lived-in.

## 6. The Turbine and the Crossing

The founding generation treats system maintenance as the city's whole reason for existing, and marks
that fact with an actual holiday — public inspection and upkeep of the wind and solar infrastructure,
observance as the act of maintenance itself. **But** a city built by two roughly-equal cultural
currents also needs something that isn't about the machines at all. **Therefore** a second
observance forms around the exchange of food, language, and custom between the American and Japanese
communities specifically — proof that the crossroads is remembered on its own terms. **But** neither
holiday ever quite explains the other. **Therefore** Princess Elisabeth's calendar ends up holding
two separate civic hearts side by side: the engineering pride and the demographic truce, never fully
reconciled into one story.

## 7. The Ones Who Know the Tunnels

The underground reserve's access points and maintenance routes are complex enough that only a small,
specialized crew actually understands the full layout. **But** that concentration of knowledge means
real, quiet importance sits in very few hands. **Therefore** when a senior member of that crew starts
considering retirement, the city realizes just how thin its own margin of institutional knowledge
about its most important infrastructure actually is. **But** training a full replacement crew takes
years the aging specialist may not have left to give. **Therefore** Princess Elisabeth launches a
deliberate apprenticeship program, treating tunnel knowledge as a skill worth formally inheriting
rather than something the city can simply assume will always be there.

## 8. A Name Half-Forgotten

The founding exile community names the city after a real Belgian princess, carrying genuine
historical weight for the generation that chose it. **But** two centuries and two dominant later
immigration currents mean fewer and fewer residents have any personal connection to Belgium at all.
**Therefore** the name survives while its meaning thins out, generation by generation, into the kind
of fact a resident might know without feeling anything about it — a street sign, not a memory.
**But** the city's own small Belgian-heritage community keeps something closer to the original weight
alive in private. **Therefore** "Princess Elisabeth" ends up meaning two different things at once,
depending entirely on which resident you ask: forgotten trivia to most, quietly personal history to a
few.

## 9. Nothing to Fall Back On

A lesser failure — an equipment fault, a bad storm, a maintenance lapse — pushes the city's active
balancing systems to their real limit for the first time. **But** with no passive refuge anywhere in
the city's design, there's no way to simply wait the crisis out. **Therefore** residents scramble,
engineers work through the emergency in real time, and the city survives purely through frantic,
successful, active intervention. **But** surviving a close call through effort doesn't fix the
underlying vulnerability — it just proves the vulnerability is real. **Therefore** the close call
becomes local memory, a story residents tell about how thin the margin always was, and one of the
clearest arguments anyone ever needed for finally committing to a genuine buried reserve (see #2).

## 10. Margrethe's Balance

Chief Engineer Margrethe Ollivier-Tanaka, her own surname blending Belgian and Japanese heritage like
the city's demographic split, inherits responsibility for keeping the zero-emissions systems
balanced across a whole generation. **But** the job demands constant, exacting attention with
genuinely no room for error — the kind of pressure most engineers eventually burn out under.
**Therefore** she designs and formalizes a staggered, overlapping shift system specifically so no
single team ever has to carry that pressure alone for long. **But** formalizing the system turns her
own outsized personal contribution into ordinary institutional structure rather than personal
legend. **Therefore** Margrethe's own name stays known mainly within the engineering corps itself —
essential, quietly credited work, a small early instance of "leaving no mark" applied to how the
city remembers the very engineers who kept it alive.

---

## Worth Your Attention

**#2 and #9** work best read together — the near-miss that exposes the city's total dependency, and
the genuinely expensive commitment that dependency provokes, giving Princess Elisabeth's founding
"nothing to fall back on" premise an actual before-and-after shape within its own pre-war history.
**#7 and #10** form a natural pair as well: two different generations' worth of quietly uncredited
labor keeping the same total-dependency system alive, neither one ever seeking recognition for it.
