## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Troll's growing cargo fleet depends entirely on a steady fuel supply trucked in via Hwy 7 — a
dependency nobody has had reason to question, because the supply has simply always arrived.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A serious disruption elsewhere in the shipping chain leaves the airfield's fuel reserves running
dangerously low for the first time in the city's history — proof that "the supply always arrives" was
never a guarantee, just an unbroken streak. The city moves to invest in significantly expanded fuel
storage capacity, enough to weather future disruptions without grounding flights. But maintaining that
much stored fuel safely requires real, ongoing investment neither the city nor any single shipping
company wants to bear alone — the scale of the fix outstrips what either side can justify funding by
itself.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a negotiation over who pays for a shared
vulnerability that nobody caused individually. A's resistance to civic funding is reasonable — why
should ordinary city taxpayers subsidize what is, on its face, commercial infrastructure serving
shipping companies' fleets? B's insistence that government has a stake too is equally reasonable — the
whole city's prosperity now runs through the freight economy, and no single company can or should be
expected to carry a city-wide risk alone. This should read as two legitimate budget-holders each
trying not to be the one left holding an open-ended liability.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a city government
budget figure, wary of civic funds being committed to infrastructure that primarily benefits private
shipping interests. Character B is plausibly a representative of the airfield's largest commercial
users, or the Airfield Authority (#2) itself acting on their behalf, arguing that fuel security is now
inseparable from the city's own economic survival and can't be treated as a purely private commercial
concern.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The cost gets split between city government and the airfield's largest commercial users — a negotiated
shared-funding arrangement, tying Troll's own civic budget directly to the health of its freight
economy for the first time.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because civic budget is now structurally tied to freight-economy health, city government has a direct
financial incentive to actively support and protect that economy's growth going forward, rather than
remaining a neutral administrator standing outside it — a genuine shift in what the government's own
interests are, not just what it funds.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

With real skin in the game, Troll's civic government starts pushing, in its dealings with Sanay,
Belgrano, and the wider subnet, for terms favorable to Troll's own freight throughput specifically —
scheduling priority, routing preferences — rather than staying neutral in matters that used to be
purely commercial. For the first time, Troll's government becomes an active advocate for its own
economic interests in relations with neighboring cities, not merely an administrator of its own
internal affairs. Self-check: without the civic-commercial funding tie from the 2nd-order change,
government would have no particular stake in pushing these terms — the advocacy depends directly on
that entanglement.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Troll's fuel security is now genuinely robust, backed by expanded storage jointly funded by city and
commercial users. But that same funding arrangement has permanently entangled civic government's
financial interests with the freight economy's health — turning Troll's own government, for the first
time, into an active stakeholder in the city's regional standing rather than a neutral administrator
of it.
