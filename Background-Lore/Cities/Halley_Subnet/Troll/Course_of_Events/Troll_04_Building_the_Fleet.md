## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Troll's role as a freight hub creates real, growing demand for more cargo capacity than the aircraft
inherited from the original Polar Institute station can handle — the city's ambitions have outgrown
the hardware it started with.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A small city inheriting one research station's infrastructure has neither the budget nor the
justification to simply buy a full modern fleet outright. The city pursues a mixed strategy instead:
leasing capacity from outside carriers during peak shipping seasons while slowly investing in aircraft
of its own as revenue allows. But relying on leased outside capacity means Troll's freight throughput
is only ever as reliable as carriers with no particular loyalty to the city — a workable stopgap, not
a foundation Troll can build its whole economic identity on with any confidence.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a budget-and-risk argument over how far to push
fleet self-sufficiency, at real near-term cost, against a strategy that's already working well enough
to defer the harder investment. A's caution is fiscally sound — leasing is cheaper, lower-risk, and
doesn't require capital a still-modest city has trouble justifying. B's urgency is also sound —
dependency on carriers with no stake in Troll's future is a structural vulnerability that compounds
the longer it's tolerated. Neither side is wrong; they're weighing the same facts against different
time horizons.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a fiscally cautious
administrator — a city or Airfield Authority (#2) budget figure who has to answer for every unit of
capital spent and sees leasing as the responsible near-term choice. Character B is plausibly an
airfield operations figure — someone who deals directly with the unreliability of externally
scheduled capacity, watching shipments slip whenever an outside carrier's own priorities shift, and
who experiences the vulnerability as an operational reality rather than an abstract risk on a ledger.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The self-sufficiency argument wins out. Troll commits, deliberately and over years, to building its
own fleet toward genuine independence from leased capacity — precisely so the city's economic
centrality never depends on anyone else's goodwill.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Owning and maintaining a fleet requires Troll to build its own in-house aircraft maintenance and crew-
training capacity, rather than continuing to rely on outside carriers' own maintenance operations — a
genuinely new skilled trade appears in the city: local mechanics and flight crews trained up from
nothing, where before the city had no aviation-trades sector distinct from general freight labor.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That new skilled-labor sector becomes a distinct source of civic pride within Troll's broader working-
class culture — local aircrew and mechanics develop their own professional reputation for having
built the fleet's reliability essentially from scratch, a status distinguishable from the freight
hub's general prosperity precisely because it's a skill the city grew rather than money the city
simply earned.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Troll operates a growing, increasingly self-sufficient cargo fleet, backed by its own homegrown
maintenance and crew infrastructure — freight throughput no longer hostage to outside carriers'
goodwill, and a new skilled aviation-trades class has taken root within the city's working-class
identity, distinct and genuinely respected.
