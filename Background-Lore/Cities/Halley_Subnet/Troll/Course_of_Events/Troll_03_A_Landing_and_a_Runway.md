## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

For generations, St. Roald's Landing is Troll's only real civic holiday, honoring Amundsen and the
founding Polar Institute heritage — the city's single fixed point on its own civic calendar,
unquestioned because unchallenged.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

As the airfield's rising economic importance comes to define the city's public identity, the older
observance starts to feel disconnected from what residents actually live through day to day — a
holiday about a claim and a station, in a city increasingly organized around cargo manifests and
flight schedules. A newer holiday, Runway Day, starts to form around the airfield itself — its
founding, its growth, what it means to the city now. But older residents refuse to let the Landing
simply fade in Runway Day's favor, treating its quiet decline as something worth actively resisting
rather than accepting as inevitable drift.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a fight over Troll's *official* civic calendar,
not a private or informal disagreement — specifically whether St. Roald's Landing keeps its formal,
budgeted civic status or gets displaced by Runway Day as the city's primary observance. A's
enthusiasm for Runway Day isn't malicious or dismissive of the past; it reflects a real, live sense
that a city should hold civic ceremony around what it currently *is*. B's resistance isn't mere
nostalgia; it's a defense of formal recognition specifically, since a demoted-to-private Landing
would eventually mean the city no longer officially remembers something its own founding families
still consider load-bearing.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a younger civic
organizer or someone connected to the freight economy — an Airfield Authority-adjacent figure, or
simply a civic-minded resident who came of age entirely inside "Whoever Holds the Runway" Troll and
experiences the older holiday as belonging to a city that no longer quite exists. Character B is
plausibly an elder from one of the founding exile families, or a keeper of the Landing's own
observance traditions, for whom the holiday isn't symbolic sentiment but a genuinely load-bearing
piece of how the city remembers where it came from.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Troll ends up keeping both holidays, deliberately, side by side, formally written into the civic
calendar as equally official — one of the only Tepenian cities to formally commemorate both who it
used to be and what it's become, rather than letting one identity quietly replace the other.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Formally recognizing two civic holidays back-to-back on the calendar requires Troll to build
holiday-recognition infrastructure it never needed while it had only one uncontested observance —
parade routes, an events budget, a formal civic-proclamation process, all written down for the first
time rather than simply assumed from long habit.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Once that formal recognition process exists on paper, smaller, previously informal Troll traditions
start petitioning to be added to the official civic calendar too, using the same process the Landing
and Runway Day compromise created. The dual-holiday resolution, meant to settle one specific dispute,
inadvertently opens an ongoing civic argument over what else counts as "official" tradition — a door
that stays open well past the two holidays that originally forced it.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Troll formally keeps two civic holidays, backed by a real, precedent-setting recognition process — the
older heritage and the airfield identity both now officially commemorated. But the city's civic
calendar has become an ongoing site of negotiation in its own right, no longer the settled, single-
holiday fixture it used to be.
