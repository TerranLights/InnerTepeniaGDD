## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Troll's freight-hub economy generates real, above-average spending power for a working-class city — a
prosperity that has to go somewhere, and hasn't yet settled into any particular visible form.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

That prosperity never turns into ornate or extravagant display — Troll's whole civic character stays
plainly working-class regardless of what residents can now actually afford. The extra money shows up
instead in quality: better-made goods, better-made tools, a glitch-coolant culture that stays limited-
selection and strong-effect like its poorer neighbors' but is simply better crafted throughout. But
visitors used to reading prosperity as visible excess — grander buildings, showier goods — sometimes
miss Troll's wealth entirely, mistaking careful craftsmanship for modest means.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** an argument over what a city's money is actually
*for*, playing out specifically over how to spend Troll's newfound freight-economy surplus. A's case
for visible display isn't vanity — a more ostentatious civic face plausibly raises Troll's leverage and
recognition among outside trading partners and visiting delegations, the same partners Suggestion #1's
public-identity fight is also about winning over. B's case for craftsmanship isn't simple frugality
either — it's a genuine conviction that spending real value on visible status rather than on things
that work better would betray what the city actually values, dressing Troll up as something it isn't
just to impress people who were never going to stay long enough to know the difference.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an ambitious civic
booster or merchant figure who deals directly with outside trade delegations and wants Troll to look as
prosperous as it actually is, believing appearances carry real negotiating weight. Character B is
plausibly a senior tradesperson or guild-type craftsperson, someone whose working life is making things
that function well, for whom redirecting money into display goods reads as a waste of value that could
instead make everyday tools and goods genuinely better.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The craftsmanship position prevails, culturally rather than by any single formal decision — Troll's
extra money keeps showing up in quality rather than ornament, and the city's wealth stays legible
mainly to residents themselves, a quiet difference in craftsmanship rather than anything a stranger
would immediately notice.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Troll's prosperity stays illegible to outsiders, visiting trading partners and delegations
consistently underestimate the city's actual wealth and standing when negotiating — reading a plainly
working-class-looking city as poorer or less influential than its actual role in the freight economy
("Whoever Holds the Runway," Suggestion #1) would otherwise suggest.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Troll's own negotiators and Airfield Authority figures start to recognize this misreading and quietly
use it as genuine leverage — in fee disputes, contracts, and shipping terms, counterparts who assume
they're dealing with a modest, unassuming city set their expectations, and their opening offers,
accordingly. Self-check: without outsiders' underestimation from the 2nd-order change, there would be
nothing for Troll's negotiators to exploit — the leverage depends directly on that misreading existing
first, not on the original craftsmanship choice alone.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Troll's wealth remains genuinely invisible to outsiders by cultural preference — quality over display,
unchanged from before. But that same quiet illegibility has become an actual, if quietly exploited,
negotiating advantage for the city's own commercial and civic interests in dealings with the rest of
the Federation.
