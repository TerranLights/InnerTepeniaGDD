## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Belgrano's entire civic importance — the reason it matters to the rest of the Halley subnet at all —
rests on one piece of infrastructure: its runway. Unlike Sanay and Abowasa, both founded on stable
bedrock, Belgrano's runway sits on flat coastal ice. Nobody treats this as a live concern; it's simply
the ground the city was built on, the way any founding-era settlement takes its own terrain for granted.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A routine structural survey — the kind of maintenance check any working airbase runs as a matter of
course — turns up something nobody was looking for: real, measurable ice movement beneath a section of
the runway. Not a crisis yet, not visible to the naked eye, but hard instrument data confirming that the
ground under Belgrano's single most vital asset genuinely moves, in ways solid rock never would.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** not a personal rivalry, but an institutional
tension between urgency and reassurance — someone whose job is to sound the alarm loud enough to get
resources committed, and someone whose job is to keep the base's confident, function-first public bearing
intact while that response happens. Both are right: downplaying the finding risks catastrophe; overstating
it risks panic in a community whose entire self-image depends on not looking rattled. The strongest
version of this conflict has both characters privately more frightened than either lets show, since the
implications (Belgrano's runway, on some timeline, might not be permanently trustworthy ground) are
genuinely destabilizing to a city that built its whole identity on discipline and function.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly the structural engineer
or surveyor who actually found the movement — someone whose professional obligation is to report it
accurately regardless of how unwelcome the finding is. Character B is plausibly the base's ranking
officer or civic administrator, the person who has to decide how much of this becomes public knowledge
and how fast, balancing operational necessity against the base's own carefully maintained composure.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Base command commits to an urgent, resource-intensive reinforcement effort rather than either burying the
finding or letting panic spread unmanaged. Essentially the entire garage and mechanic workforce gets
pulled into the effort — the same hands-on competence culture that fixes engines and builds Arrastradoras
turned, for the first time, toward the ground itself.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

The reinforcement effort works — the immediate danger passes, the runway holds. But engineers are honest
with command about what reinforcement can and can't actually do: it can manage ice-shelf instability, it
can't eliminate it. Bedrock doesn't move. Ice does. No amount of Belgrano's own considerable technical
skill changes that underlying physical fact.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That honest acknowledgment — competence solved the immediate problem but couldn't solve the underlying
one — settles into the city's institutional memory as a permanent, low-grade watchfulness. Survey checks
that were once purely routine now carry a specific, remembered weight. Nobody talks about it constantly,
but everyone who's been at Belgrano long enough understands that the ground itself is the one thing the
city's own considerable competence can manage but never fully fix.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Belgrano carries forward its confident, function-before-comfort public bearing exactly as before — but
underneath it now sits a specific, concrete anxiety about the city's own foundation, distinct from
generic hardship or external threat. The first hard evidence of the unsettled self-image the city will
carry for the rest of its history didn't come from the war or from decline. It came from a routine survey
that found out the ground itself couldn't be fully trusted, years before anything else tested the city at
all.
