## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

An ordinary Interwar supply slowdown hits Belgrano's airbase — shipments delayed, official channels
too slow to help — the kind of shortage any working institution eventually rides out, given time.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Time is exactly what the base doesn't have. The officer running the base's maintenance operation knows
every garage crew in the outer city capable of fabricating or salvaging what's actually needed —
official Air Force personnel or not — and organizes an unofficial parts-sharing rotation across those
crews, with no new authority formally granted for doing it, simply because the work needs doing and
someone has to be the one who knows who can do it. The workaround starts producing results faster and
more reliably than the official supply chain it was only ever meant to patch around. As the shortage
drags on, the rotation stops looking like an emergency measure and starts looking like the base's
actual supply chain in every way but name.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone whose job is
literally the formal supply chain — accountable for its integrity, its paperwork, its chain of
custody — and someone whose only loyalty is to the operational outcome: parts fabricated, aircraft
flying, work done. This shouldn't play as bureaucrat-vs.-pragmatist caricature. A's objections are
professionally sound: unregulated salvage and fabrication genuinely can carry real safety and quality
risk, and "it worked so far" isn't the same as "it's safe to formalize." B's case is equally sound: the
official channel demonstrably failed the base when it mattered, and the outer-city crews demonstrably
didn't.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a logistics or
supply officer whose actual mandate is the formal channel itself — someone who isn't wrong to worry
that a proven emergency workaround, left unexamined, could become a permanent blind spot in the base's
accountability. Character B is plausibly the maintenance officer who organized the rotation in the
first place, or one of the outer-city garage crew leads who've been doing the actual work — someone
whose case rests entirely on results, not process, and who has no interest in institutional turf, only
in getting the base's aircraft flying.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The shortage ends, and the expected next step — winding the workaround down, reverting fully to the
official channel — never actually happens. Nobody formally authorizes the rotation to continue; nobody
formally shuts it down either. It simply keeps running, quietly folded into ordinary operations,
because every time someone raises the question of dismantling it, the answer is the same: it's still
working better than the thing it was supposed to be a stopgap for.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With no formal charter and no oversight structure ever built for it, the participating garage crews
start accumulating real standing within the base's operations — trusted, relied upon, effectively
indispensable — without any of the accountability infrastructure that would normally come with being
an official contractor: no formal contracts, no formal inspection process, no formal payment structure,
only reputation.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because their standing rests purely on reputation rather than paperwork, competition between garage
crews for a place in the rotation becomes real and personal — inclusion or exclusion is a genuine
economic and status stake for outer-city families, who self-organize into something like an unofficial
guild hierarchy that nobody designed and nobody has the standing to formally regulate, since there was
never a formal structure to regulate in the first place.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Belgrano's spare-parts economy is real and genuinely load-bearing — but wholly informal, a network of
garage crews whose standing depends entirely on reputation, running alongside, and sometimes ahead of,
the official supply chain, with no institution anywhere that actually owns or governs it.
