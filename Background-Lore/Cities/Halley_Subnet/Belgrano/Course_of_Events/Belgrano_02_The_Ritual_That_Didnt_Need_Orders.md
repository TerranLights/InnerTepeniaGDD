## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Every piece of Belgrano's founding character that matters to the institution — chain of command,
maintenance rotations, martial bearing — gets formally taught to newer, non-Argentine arrivals through
actual training channels: manuals, drills, assigned mentors. Mate-sharing sits outside all of it,
carried over as a family habit from home, taught to no one because it was never anyone's job to teach.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Newcomers pick the ritual up anyway — informally, faster and more completely than they absorb the
taught discipline, simply by sitting in rooms where it happens often enough that it stops being
foreign. Within a generation, non-Argentine residents are sharing mate with the same unconscious ease
as anyone raised with it at home. But that also makes it the one piece of "Belgrano culture" nobody
can point to an official source for — no manual, no mentor, no institutional record of who taught whom.
As the city's non-Argentine majority grows, some of its Argentine-heritage families start to notice how
casually, how completely, something that came from their own homes is being practiced by people with
no connection to where it came from at all.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone who wants the
ritual's proper form protected — treated as something with real etiquette and real origin that
deserves respect, not casual imitation — and someone who has taken the ritual up wholeheartedly and
resents the implication that their version of it needs anyone's permission to be genuine. This isn't a
gatekeeper-vs.-appropriator morality tale: A's protectiveness comes from a real place, watching
something specific and personal drift into generic city custom; B's ownership is equally real, built
from years of lived practice, not casual borrowing. Neither side is play-acting.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly an older
Argentine-heritage resident — a family elder, or someone whose household mate ritual has real,
particular customs attached to it (who pours, how refills work, what's rude and what isn't) — watching
those particulars flatten out into something looser as the practice spreads. Character B is plausibly
a younger, non-Argentine-heritage resident (German, British, Brazilian) who grew up sharing mate the
way everyone around them did, with no sense that it belonged to anyone in particular, and experiences
the challenge to that as a challenge to their own belonging in the city itself.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No formal etiquette ever gets adopted. A's attempt to establish "correct" practice — proper pouring
order, proper phrasing, a right way and a wrong way — never gathers enough buy-in to stick, not because
anyone rejects it outright but because the ritual is already too widely, too casually practiced for any
single version of it to become the standard. The ritual becomes, within another generation, the thing
newer residents feel most personally belongs to them — precisely because nobody ever formally handed it
to them, and nobody ever successfully claimed the authority to.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With no citywide standard ever settling, the ritual's exact form starts to vary from group to group —
different garages, maintenance crews, and neighborhoods develop their own local etiquettes for who
pours, how the gourd gets passed, how many refills before it's rude to complain.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because those local variants harden into their own small traditions, learning "the Belgrano way" of
sharing mate now depends entirely on which crew or neighborhood first showed a person how it's done —
the ritual is universal across the city, but no longer singular, and residents from different corners
of Belgrano needle each other, half-seriously, over whose version is the real one.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Mate-sharing is now practiced by nearly everyone in Belgrano and felt as personally theirs by
Argentine and non-Argentine residents alike — but not as one unified tradition. It survives as a
patchwork of neighborhood- and crew-specific variants, each unofficial, each defended with genuine,
good-natured pride as the true version, in a city that never once formally decided whose it was.
