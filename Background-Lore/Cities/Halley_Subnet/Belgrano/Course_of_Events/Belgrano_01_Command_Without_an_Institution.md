## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Among the earliest 2564 exiles who reclaimed the long-dormant Belgrano airbase are a number of former
Argentine Air Force personnel — themselves refugees, not survivors of the original 1979-era station, which
had sat empty for centuries before anyone arrived. Drawing on their own training, they establish a
military-style command structure from scratch to run the reclaimed base: maintenance rotations, martial
bearing, a clean chain of accountability — a genuinely new institution built by exiles who happened to know
this shape of organization, not an inherited one.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A civilian population starts forming around the base — families, tradespeople, the ordinary civic
mass any long-running settlement accumulates — and the chain of command has no formal jurisdiction
over any of it. Rather than let a separate civilian government grow up alongside the base, the
ranking officer extends military-style command to cover the whole settlement instead. It is treated,
at the time, as a stopgap: someone has to keep order while the base is still the only functioning
institution for hundreds of kilometers. But every later arrival — Argentine or not — now inherits Air
Force discipline as the *only* civic template Belgrano has ever offered them, with nothing else on
offer to compare it against.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone who wants to see
the "stopgap" formally undone — a separate civilian government finally chartered, now that the
settlement has clearly outgrown its excuse — and someone who has real, structural reasons to resist
that, even without personally wanting to hold onto power for its own sake. This shouldn't play as a
simple military-overreach story. The officer's side has a genuine, defensible case: nobody has ever
actually built the alternative, there's no civilian administrative class anywhere in the settlement,
and dismantling the only working structure before a replacement exists risks real chaos, not just
symbolic loss. The reform side's case is equally real: a population that has never consented to
military command, growing every year, governed by an institution that was never built to represent it.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a civilian resident
or tradesperson — someone with no military background at all, whose grievance is structural rather
than personal, and who is articulate enough to make the case that "temporary" has quietly become
"permanent" without anyone ever deciding it that way. Character B is plausibly a senior officer, not
necessarily the ranking commander himself, tasked with actually running day-to-day civil order and
acutely aware — better than any outsider — of exactly how much informal, undocumented labor holds the
settlement together. Their resistance should read as competent caution, not authoritarian instinct.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

No new civilian charter gets written. The officer's case — that no functioning alternative exists to
hand power to — carries the argument, not through any single decisive victory but through the reform
side's inability to answer the question "then who runs it, starting tomorrow?" with anything but a
plan still years from ready. Command stays extended over the whole settlement, formally unchallenged
from this point forward, "function before comfort" becoming Belgrano's baseline discipline for
everyone who arrives here from now on — not offered as a value to embrace, but inherited as simply the
only civic shape the city has ever had.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With no civilian government ever chartered, every non-operational civic matter — housing disputes,
resource allocation for families with no military standing, informal mediation between neighbors —
still has nowhere to go except through officers trained for logistics and maintenance, not governance.
The chain of command becomes the address for problems it was never designed to solve.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because those non-operational duties overload officers already stretched across their actual
operational mandate, informal delegation starts happening below the rank structure — capable
civilians, never formally commissioned, start quietly handling day-to-day civic matters on the
command's behalf, simply because someone has to and they're the ones already doing it. No formal
authority is ever granted them; the delegation exists entirely as understood practice.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Belgrano has no civilian government at all — its entire civic life still running through a chain of
command originally built only for the airbase itself — but beneath that formal structure, an
ever-growing shadow layer of capable, untitled people is quietly doing the actual work of governing a
city the command structure was never built to run.
