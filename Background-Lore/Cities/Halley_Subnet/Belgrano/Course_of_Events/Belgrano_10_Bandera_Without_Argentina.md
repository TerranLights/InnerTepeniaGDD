## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Argentina founded the base, set its entire operational character, and named the city after Manuel
Belgrano — the observance of that namesake, Bandera Day, understood at the time as straightforward
civic and national pride, honoring the population that built the place.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Later immigration — German, American, British, Brazilian — eventually outpaces the founding population
so thoroughly that Argentina ends up the lowest recognized demographic tier in the whole city. An
observance built around a specifically Argentine historical figure, honored by a population less and
less connected to Argentina every year, starts to look, to some residents, like it's quietly heading
toward irrelevance — a holiday that could easily fade the way the founding population's numbers already
have, kept alive by inertia rather than genuine meaning. Others feel the opposite: that the name, and
the discipline it represents, had already become the city's whole civic character independent of who
actually lives here now, and that the holiday's survival isn't inertia at all.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone questioning
whether it still makes sense to center the city's calendar on a specifically Argentine historical
figure when the population honoring him is barely Argentine anymore, and someone who has come to feel
real, personal ownership of Bandera Day as plain civic identity, regardless of heritage. This is the
same underlying demographic tension as Suggestion #6, applied specifically to a named holiday rather
than to "discipline" in general — worth playing that resonance deliberately if both cycles end up in
the same city history. A's case is about representational fairness and relevance; B's case is about
continuity — that changing or dropping the day would sever the city from its own origin story,
regardless of who currently "counts" toward it.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a newer-heritage
civic organizer or council figure, practical and inclusion-minded, proposing the holiday be reframed or
renamed to reflect the city's actual present demographics. Character B is plausibly a resident of any
heritage — not necessarily Argentine — who has grown up genuinely invested in Bandera Day as their own
civic identity, and experiences the reform proposal as an attempt to take something real away from
them, not a correction.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

When the question actually reaches a community forum or council session, the reform proposal doesn't
gain traction — not defeated in outrage, just outvoted by residents, Argentine-heritage and not alike,
who want to keep it. The holiday is explicitly reaffirmed, but in doing so its framing is stated plainly,
maybe for the first time, as civic pride in the name and founding story rather than as a claim about
anyone's ethnic heritage. Bandera Day persists, celebrated by a population that is, numerically, barely
Argentine at all anymore.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because Bandera Day gets explicitly reframed as civic pride rather than ethnic heritage, its actual
observance content quietly shifts over the following years — speeches and traditions emphasize the
discipline and founding-story themes ("function before comfort," the Air Force exile origin) rather
than anything distinctly Argentine beyond the flag itself, since that's the version of the holiday that
actually won the argument.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the holiday's content is now about discipline and founding-story rather than Argentine heritage
specifically, later immigrant groups with no connection to Argentina at all — and no connection to the
Air Force founding either — find it easy to participate in Bandera Day wholeheartedly, further diluting
any residual ethnic-heritage character the day still had. Within another generation, Bandera Day is
functionally indistinguishable from a general Belgrano founding-day civic holiday, its literal
connection to the historical Manuel Belgrano becoming a matter of trivia rather than lived meaning.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Bandera Day endures as one of Belgrano's two civic holidays, universally observed and genuinely felt —
but its content has quietly drifted from honoring a specific Argentine historical figure toward simply
celebrating the city's own founding discipline and character, worn smooth of ethnic specificity in
exactly the way the city's demographic reality had already outpaced the label on its own name.
