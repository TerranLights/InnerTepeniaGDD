## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Most exile communities across Tepenia let their founding institutions fade quietly into general civic
life once bare survival no longer strictly depends on them — early urgency easing, over a generation or
two, into ordinary governance with no formal seam left showing. Neumayer, still young enough to be making
this choice for the first time, hasn't yet decided whether the Alfred Wegener Institute will follow that
same path.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A city organized around rigor as near-religious civic virtue finds that kind of quiet dissolution
genuinely intolerable — letting the founding institution simply blur into general governance would mean
treating its own instruments of precision as disposable once their original emergency has passed, which is
exactly the kind of ambiguity Neumayer's whole culture exists to refuse. The pressure to formalize AWI's
permanence builds specifically because it's the one thing nobody can bring themselves to leave informal —
even though everyone administering the city already knows that maintaining a fully separate institutional
structure, indefinitely, is going to cost real and ongoing effort nobody is strictly required to spend.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between a civic administrator who
wants AWI folded into general city governance — for efficiency, to avoid duplicated bureaucracy, and out
of real concern that a split structure creates gaps and blind spots nobody's accounting for — and an
Institute loyalist who insists on formal, permanent independence regardless of the cost. This shouldn't
read as bean-counter versus true believer. A's efficiency argument is sound: two governments cost more
than one, and duplicated authority genuinely does create seams where responsibility can slip through. B's
insistence isn't sentiment either — it's a structural argument that AWI's independence is precisely what
let it do work like the original Tower schematics without political interference, and that folding it into
city hall would trade away exactly the kind of insulation that made the city's precision culture possible
in the first place.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a post-founding-
generation civic administrator, someone who never lived through the original emergency AWI answers and
sees it now mainly as an administrative inheritance. Character B is plausibly a senior Institute figure —
a scientist-administrator descended, literally or professionally, from the founding AWI staff — whose
case rests on protecting the Institute's independence as a working condition for good research, not as
inherited loyalty for its own sake.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

A formal charter — effectively a referendum on the question — enshrines AWI's institutional independence
permanently rather than letting the matter drift toward either quiet dissolution or forced merger.
Neumayer ends up governed by two formally distinct, deliberately overlapping systems: general civic
administration and a continuously preserved research institute, because letting either one fully absorb
the other never felt acceptable to enough people to actually happen.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

With its own budget, personnel, and decision authority formally separate from city hall, AWI develops its
own distinct professional track over the following generations — joining "the Institute" versus joining
"the City" becomes an early, defining civic choice for young Neumayerites, closer to choosing between two
separate professional guilds than picking a job within one government.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because career paths split this cleanly for generations, the two overlapping governments end up being
reconciled in practice mostly through personal relationships that cross the divide — friendships,
partnerships, family ties between people whose careers grew up on opposite sides of the Institute/City
line — far more than through the charter's own formal language, which was never detailed enough to settle
every jurisdictional overlap on paper.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Neumayer runs on two formally distinct, permanently overlapping governing bodies — general civic
administration and the continuously preserved AWI Institute — held together in practice less by the
charter that created them than by generations of informal relationships between people whose careers grew
up on either side of that divide.
