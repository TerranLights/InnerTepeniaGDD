## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Built on the Ekström Ice Shelf's floating, unstable ice, Neumayer faces the same foundational problem
every ice-shelf city in the Halley subnet does: ground that moves, on its own schedule, indifferent to
whatever's built on top of it. No settled civic method for handling that yet exists as more than local
habit and improvisation.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Halley, just up the coast, answers the same problem through continuous relocation and open civic debate
about when and where to move — a method some in Neumayer find genuinely persuasive, given the two cities'
shared origins and shared geography. But adopting it here would mean treating collective deliberation as
the right tool for a problem that Neumayer's own engineers increasingly believe is better solved by
instrumentation: precise measurement of exactly how the ice is moving, adjusted for continuously rather
than argued over periodically. The two approaches can't really coexist as equals — choosing one means
downgrading the other, and the city hasn't yet decided which.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between an instrumentation advocate
who wants Neumayer's response to the ice shelf's instability run entirely on hydraulic-leg engineering and
continuous measurement, and a deliberation advocate who wants the city to follow Halley's lead and treat
this as a decision the whole community should keep making together, out loud, as conditions change. This
shouldn't be simple engineer-vs-populist framing — A's position isn't contempt for other people's
judgment, it's a genuine belief that ambiguous, deliberated decisions get people killed on ground this
unstable, where waiting for consensus can cost the time a hydraulic adjustment doesn't need. B's position
isn't nostalgia or sentiment — it's a genuine belief that a foundation decision this consequential
shouldn't belong entirely to whoever controls the instruments, no matter how good the instruments are.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a lead engineer on the
hydraulic-leg architecture program itself, someone whose whole professional life is already organized
around trusting the numbers. Character B is plausibly a civic representative or founding-era administrator
with real ties to Halley — perhaps someone who came from there, or was trained there, and has seen
consensus-based relocation work firsthand, unwilling to concede that Neumayer's situation is different
enough to justify abandoning it without a fight.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The instrumentation approach wins, formally and completely: the city charters hydraulic-leg architecture
and continuous instrumentation as its official method for the ice shelf problem, not as one option among
several but as settled civic doctrine. "Measured, Not Debated" stops being an engineering slogan and
becomes something closer to a founding covenant — precision substituting for argument, formally, in a
domain the debate side had genuinely hoped to keep open.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because infrastructure decisions now run on measurement rather than vote, real authority over anything
that can be framed as a measurement question concentrates in whoever controls the instruments and their
interpretation — a Metrology Board, descended from the original AWI engineering staff, becomes the
de facto final word not just on the ice shelf specifically, but on any civic question anyone successfully
reframes as a technical one.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Because the Metrology Board's authority is real, growing, and genuinely easier to win an argument through
than open debate, civic actors start learning to translate their actual political asks — which
neighborhood gets resources first, whose relocation request takes priority — into measurable engineering
terms specifically to route them through the Board rather than through ordinary governance. Precision
becomes not just a method but a language worth learning to speak strategically.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Neumayer's civic culture now runs on data-authority far beyond the ice shelf that originally justified it.
Nearly every dispute in the city eventually gets reframed in measurable terms to be settled by the
Metrology Board rather than argued out politically — precision is trusted, exactly as intended, but that
trust can also be gamed by anyone patient enough to learn its grammar.
