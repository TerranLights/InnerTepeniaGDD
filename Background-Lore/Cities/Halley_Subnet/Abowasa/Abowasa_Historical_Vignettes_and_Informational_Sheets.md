## Two Hundred Meters, Closed

Abowasa's physical infrastructure originated as two genuinely separate station sites roughly 200 meters
apart, now fully, physically merged into one inseparable settlement (Physical Infrastructure Attribute #3).
The merger wasn't instantaneous — it happened gradually, as the exile population inheriting both sites found
increasingly little practical reason to maintain the gap between them.

The earliest connecting infrastructure was minimal — a cleared, marked path between the two clusters of
buildings, walkable but not yet a genuine architectural bridge. What actually closed the gap, per informal
accounts, was the Joint Household Kitchens (attribute #4): a shared cooking facility built roughly at the
midpoint between the two original sites specifically because neither community wanted to fully relocate to
the other's own buildings, and a genuinely neutral middle ground solved the problem without requiring either
side to concede anything. Once the Kitchens existed, the infrastructure around them filled in naturally —
storage, additional shared facilities, eventually enough connective building that the original 200-meter gap
stopped being a meaningful distance at all. Nobody currently living in Abowasa remembers the two sites as
genuinely separate; the merger completed itself generations before anyone thought to mark the moment it
finished.

## What Halley and Neumayer Actually Ask Abowasa For

The Bedrock Stability Institute (attribute #2) is a genuine regional export even in Abowasa's own damaged,
partial-priority-target state — its geological and structural-stability expertise directly applicable to the
foundation problems both Halley and Neumayer actively engineer around (Finding A).

*(An informational sheet on the consulting relationship itself, not a single visit.)* Requests from either
city follow a consistent pattern, per the Institute's own working records: specific structural questions
about ice-shelf or ice-mobility engineering that Abowasa's own bedrock-founded expertise can meaningfully
inform, even though Abowasa's own city sits on genuinely different terrain than either requesting city's
foundation. The relationship runs one direction almost entirely — Abowasa has comparatively little practical
need for Halley's or Neumayer's own ice-engineering expertise, sitting as it does on exposed rock rather than
moving or elevated ice. What Abowasa receives in return, per informal accounts among Institute staff, is less
material and more simply relational: recognition, from two much larger and more strategically significant
cities, that a small, low-priority-target settlement holds expertise neither of them can produce in-house.

## The First Winter Nobody Had to Retrofit

Abowasa's infrastructure was built with year-round-capable construction but staffed only seasonally
pre-exile — a funding decision, not a structural limitation, meaning the exile population inherited buildings
already structurally ready for permanent habitation (attributes #5–6). This is a genuinely different starting
condition than most Tepenian cities, most of which inherited stations built explicitly for temporary,
seasonal occupation and had to be substantially reworked before permanent settlement was even physically
possible.

The exile community's own first full winter, per surviving informal accounts, was notable mainly for how
little needed to be improvised. Buildings that would, in a genuinely seasonal-only station, have required
extensive emergency retrofitting before anyone could safely overwinter instead simply functioned as designed
— heating systems rated for continuous operation, structural engineering already accounting for the kind of
sustained cold-weather stress a seasonal station's own construction standards wouldn't have needed to meet.
The relative ease of that first winter, compared to what founding communities elsewhere in Tepenia faced, is
plausibly part of why the technical and scientific sector Abowasa later became known for (Finding D) had room
to grow as early and as substantially as it did — resources that would otherwise have gone into emergency
structural retrofitting were free, from the very beginning, to go somewhere else instead.

## What the Buildings Never Told You

*(An informational sheet naming a genuine gap, not resolving it — Finding B's own observation that Abowasa's
built environment tells only half its cultural story.)* The physically merged infrastructure and shared
Joint Household Kitchens are real, visible proof that Abowasa's daily culture genuinely unified where
practical necessity demanded it. What the buildings don't show, and never have, is the other established half
of the city's own cultural pattern: language and civic memory that stayed deliberately distinct even after
the physical merger completed.

No structure in Abowasa marks this distinction the way the Kitchens mark the unification — the preserved-
separate half of the city's own identity exists entirely in how people speak and what they remember, not in
anything a visitor could point to and read. A newcomer touring Abowasa's physical infrastructure alone would
reasonably conclude the two founding communities fully absorbed into one undifferentiated culture; a
newcomer only hearing how households actually speak to each other, and which specific memories get passed
down through which specific family lines, would conclude something closer to the opposite. Both readings are
correct, for different halves of the same city, and neither the architecture nor the spoken culture alone
tells the whole story.

## The Strike That Found the Labs, Not the Homes

Abowasa's status — damaged but partially operational, hit but not erased — reflects a specific, established
damage pattern distinct from how the war affected residential areas versus technical/scientific
infrastructure (Finding C). The city's own largest industry sector, technical and scientific work at roughly
a quarter of its economy, is plausibly the specific reason it took targeted rather than total-avoidance
treatment at all, small and strategically minor as the city otherwise was (Finding D).

The damage pattern itself, per what's currently established, concentrated disproportionately on
laboratory and technical infrastructure rather than residential household space — consistent with a strike
aimed at degrading a specific capability rather than a strike aimed at the city's population generally.
Households in the original merged settlement, including the Joint Household Kitchens themselves, emerged
comparatively intact; the Bedrock Stability Institute and comparable technical facilities took the brunt of
whatever damage Abowasa actually sustained. The distinction matters for how residents themselves talk about
the war — less as an attack on the city as a home, and more as an attack on the specific thing the city had
become good enough at to be worth degrading.

## What Gets Decided at the Overlook

Basen Overlook (attribute #10) is an informal gathering point looking out over the Vestfjella mountain
landscape — and, per Finding E, a strong candidate physical site for The Joint Founding, the proposed
observance belonging to neither of Abowasa's two founding communities individually, the same way the
Overlook itself belongs to no single household or original station site.

*(An informational sheet on the site's own informal civic function, not a single gathering.)* The Overlook's
own use, per residents, extends well beyond any single formal observance — it functions as Abowasa's default
informal gathering point for anything that doesn't have a more specific venue already established elsewhere
in the city, precisely because its own neutrality (belonging to the landscape rather than to either founding
site) makes it comfortable ground for exactly the kind of shared, cross-community occasion The Joint Founding
would need. Residents describe using the Overlook for decisions that feel like they should belong to the
whole city rather than any one household or institution — an informal civic role the space has apparently
settled into on its own, well before any formal holiday was ever proposed to make use of it.
