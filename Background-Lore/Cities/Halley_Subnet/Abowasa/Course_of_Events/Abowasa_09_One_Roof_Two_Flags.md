## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

A specific shared structure — a supply shed, a comms mast, a shared well — ends up sitting close enough
to both stations' original boundaries that neither community can plausibly claim it alone.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Early administrative habits — each community still keeping its own separate rules, inherited whole from
their founding nations rather than adapted to a shared settlement — turn the structure's ambiguous
ownership into a genuine dispute: who maintains it, who gets priority use, who pays for its repair.

**Character A:** *(left blank)*

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between two station-level
administrators, each defending their own community's claim to priority use of the shared structure
according to their own nation's own inherited rules. Neither side should be wrong on their own terms —
each is simply applying the only administrative framework they've ever had, without either framework
having been built with a shared structure in mind. The conflict is a genuine category mismatch, not a
personal grudge.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** two practically-minded station-level
figures — a quartermaster, facilities officer, or equivalent — one from each founding nation, mirrored
in role and temperament, each simply doing their job as their own institution defines it. The friction
should come from the structural gap (no existing rule covers joint assets) rather than from either
character being unreasonable.

---

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The two communities negotiate a genuinely joint-management arrangement for that one structure — shared
costs, shared scheduling, shared accountability — the first formal thing Abowasa's two founding
communities ever agree to run together.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because neither founding nation's own administrative tradition had a template for joint management,
the negotiators have to invent the process from scratch — a genuinely new, hybrid administrative
convention (shared paperwork, a shared decision-making rhythm) that belongs to neither nation's own
inherited system.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

That invented convention, proven once on a single shed, becomes the default template Abowasa reaches
for automatically every time a later joint asset needs managing — not because anyone deliberately
designed it as policy, but because it was already sitting there, tested and working, the next time a
similar dispute came up.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Abowasa now runs on a working joint-management tradition, applied by quiet default to shared
infrastructure across the whole settlement. The precedent set on one disputed shed, not any deliberate
civic policy, is what actually organizes how the two founding communities keep their shared arrangement
running today.
