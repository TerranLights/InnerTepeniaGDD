## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

In Abowasa's earliest post-arrival years, two adjacent, separately-run stations still need genuine
day-to-day coordination — shared supply runs, shared weather reporting, shared emergencies — despite
neither community yet thinking of "Abowasa" as one place.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Neither nation's own chain of command actually covers cooperation with the other. The coordination gap
is real and immediate — supplies need routing, emergencies need a response, someone has to make the
call — but no formal structure exists to make it, leaving the work to whoever is actually willing to
walk between the two stations and decide things nobody has authorized them to decide.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone stepping into the
unofficial coordinator role out of practical necessity, and someone within one station's own official
chain of command who is genuinely uneasy ceding real decision-making weight to a figure nobody actually
appointed. This shouldn't read as obstruction for its own sake — B's discomfort is structurally
reasonable, since an unaccountable coordinator really is a departure from either nation's own normal
command structure, even if the alternative (nothing gets coordinated at all) is clearly worse.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a practical,
get-it-done liaison type — could be from either founding nation, or even someone whose job already puts
them in contact with both stations (a supply runner, a comms operator). Character B is plausibly a
lower- or mid-tier official from one station's own institutional hierarchy, not hostile to cooperation
in principle, but professionally trained to be wary of decisions made outside proper channels — someone
whose objection comes from genuine institutional instinct, not personal resentment.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

The coordinator role proves indispensable and keeps getting used, again and again, precisely because
it's the only mechanism that actually gets joint problems solved quickly. But it is never
institutionalized or formally credited — it survives entirely on repeated practical necessity, never on
anyone's official say-so.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the role is never formalized, whoever occupies it accumulates real, load-bearing influence over
Abowasa's earliest cooperative habits without ever appearing in either nation's own official record of
the settlement's founding.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

When Abowasa's later history gets told, its own established cooperative, hybridized civic character gets
credited to the two founding cultures blending naturally over time — the actual, specific person (or
succession of people) who did the unglamorous daily work of making that early cooperation function is
simply absent from the story, an uncredited-founder gap consistent with Halley subnet's own broader
"quiet competence, no credit" civic thread.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Abowasa's cooperative civic character is now genuinely established and self-sustaining. But its own
origin story has a real blank at the center — a founding role every resident's daily life still quietly
depends on, and that nobody in the settlement's own record remembers by name.
