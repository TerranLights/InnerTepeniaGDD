## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Both inherited stations were engineered for genuine year-round occupation, but neither Finland nor
Sweden ever actually staffed them through the winter — a budget line each home government simply
never funded, not a structural limit the buildings themselves imposed. The stations have always been
capable of more than either nation asked of them.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

Exile removes the one thing that made seasonal staffing sensible in the first place: somewhere to go
home to when the season ends. With no home posting waiting on the other side of winter, the first
Antarctic winter simply has to be staffed, not as the deliberate founding of a new tradition but as
the only option anyone can see. It works — the buildings hold, the supplies stretch, the two
communities get through it — and having done it once, there is no longer a plausible reason not to do
it again the following year, and the year after that. What nobody has done, in all this, is actually
sit down and decide that Abowasa is now a permanent settlement rather than a resupplied outpost that
happens to keep getting resupplied.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a confrontation between someone who wants the
community to formally commit to permanence — building the winter medical, psychological, and supply
infrastructure an actual year-round settlement needs rather than what an occasionally-extended outpost
can improvise — and someone for whom that formal declaration is close to unbearable, because it means
officially closing the door on ever going home. Neither side is wrong. A is responding to a real,
practical danger: running permanent life on improvised, seasonal-outpost logistics eventually gets
someone hurt. B is responding to a real, human one: naming this "permanent" turns private, still-alive
hope into an admitted fact nobody has been ready to say out loud.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** Character A is plausibly a station engineer
or logistics figure — someone whose job is keeping people alive through the practical realities of an
Antarctic winter, and who sees the gap between what the settlement actually is and what it's still
administratively pretending to be. Character B is plausibly an exile elder or community leader who
still functions, in some quiet capacity, as the person holding the rest of the group's hope of eventual
return — someone for whom voting on "permanent" status would mean being the one who formally ends that
hope for everyone else, not just for themselves.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Neither side gets a clean win. B never allows the vote, the ceremony, or the formal declaration that
would make Abowasa's permanence official — that refusal holds, indefinitely. But A's practical program
gets adopted anyway, piece by piece, always under some other name: "extended contingency planning,"
"multi-year supply contracting," never "permanent settlement charter." By the time anyone might have
noticed, "seasonal outpost" has quietly become "permanent home" in every operational respect, without
a single formal decision anyone could point to as the moment it happened.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because permanence arrived through accretion rather than ceremony, Abowasa never gets the one thing
most new settlements get for free: a shared, official moment when grief for the old country was
allowed to be spoken and closed. With no citywide ritual ever marking "we are not going back," that
grief doesn't disappear — it goes underground, kept alive instead in scattered, private,
household-level customs that no two families necessarily practice the same way.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

Those private, uncoordinated household customs — a kept object, a private toast, a name spoken on a
particular date — persist for a full generation with no shared civic form at all, carried individually
rather than collectively. When a genuinely public, shared observance of the old country finally does
get proposed later on, it lands with far more weight than a simple heritage holiday would have: it
isn't inventing a memory, it's finally giving public shape to grief that individual households have
already been quietly carrying, unaddressed, since that first unceremonious winter.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

Abowasa is now permanent in every practical sense — properly staffed, properly supplied, properly
built for winter — with no founding charter, no formal declaration, and no single date anyone agrees
on as "when this became home." What the city has instead is a still-unresolved undercurrent of private,
unformalized mourning running quietly beneath its practical success, waiting for something to finally
give it a shared, public shape.
