## State of Affairs 1
###### The city as it stands before this cycle's turning point.

Abowasa is small enough, and close-knit enough, that every household already knows every other household
personally. Disputes happen the way they happen anywhere — old grievances, disagreements over resources,
debts that both sides remember differently — but nothing has yet tested whether the town actually has a
working way to resolve one when simple distance isn't an option.

---

## Trigger
###### What disrupts the above baseline.

A real, serious dispute breaks out between two households — something with no easy split-the-difference
answer, the kind of disagreement that in a larger city could simply be resolved by the two parties
avoiding each other from then on. Abowasa doesn't have that option. The shared well, the shared institute,
the shared everything means the two households will keep encountering each other constantly whether the
dispute is settled or not.

**Design notes — what kind of conflict this needs:** a dispute where both sides have a genuine, legitimate
grievance — not a misunderstanding, not a villain, a real conflict of interest or memory that doesn't
resolve cleanly in either direction. The stakes aren't really about who's right; they're about the fact
that whoever "loses" a clean verdict will have to keep living twenty meters from the other party for the
rest of their life. The strongest version of this conflict has both characters privately aware of that
constraint even while they're still angry — the dispute is real, but neither of them actually wants a
result that makes their own future unbearable.

**Design notes — what kind of character(s) this needs:** two ordinary residents, plausibly heads of
neighboring households, ideally one from each side of the founding Finnish/Swedish line (though the
dispute itself shouldn't be about that line — the point is that Abowasa's structural smallness applies
regardless of which founding community either party traces to). Neither should be a designated leader or
authority figure; the custom that emerges needs to belong to the town generally, not to whoever happened
to be in charge when it was invented.

## Culmination
###### The conflict's actual outcome — specific and concrete.

Rather than a ruling that names a winner, the community improvises a resolution built around both parties
retaining standing — a mediated settlement, witnessed by neighbors, that requires each side to concede
something real without either being made to publicly lose. It works, in the specific sense that both
households can still function afterward, still nod to each other at the well, still show up to the same
gatherings without the dispute curdling into a permanent feud.

---

## 2nd-Order Change
###### The direct, immediate structural consequence of *this specific outcome*.

Because the resolution actually worked — genuinely, not just nominally — word of exactly how it was
handled spreads through the rest of the town. It isn't written down anywhere yet; it's simply understood,
the way a first working solution to a real problem gets remembered and referenced the next time something
similar comes up.

---

## 3rd-Order Change
###### The downstream consequence of the 2nd-order change — caused by *it*, not by the original conflict.

The same structural problem — small town, no anonymity, nobody able to simply walk away from a dispute —
inevitably produces a second case, then a third. Each time, residents reach for the same shape of
resolution: a witnessed, face-saving settlement rather than a clean verdict. What started as one
household's improvised solution hardens into an actual, repeatable civic custom, eventually given its own
name — "The Level Table" — for the physical practice of sitting both disputing parties down together,
neither elevated above the other, to work it out with the whole community as witness.

---

## State of Affairs 2
###### The new baseline, feeding the next cycle.

"Competence is intimacy management" stops being just a description outsiders might apply to Abowasa's
civic character and becomes something residents can point to directly: The Level Table, a real, named,
repeatable practice for handling exactly the kind of dispute a town this small can't afford to let fester.
It isn't written into any formal code — it's simply what everyone knows to do, the same way the town knows
which shed belongs to whom.
