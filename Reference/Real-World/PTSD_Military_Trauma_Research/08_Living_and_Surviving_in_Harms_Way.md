## PART I — Understanding the Service Member (Chapters 1–5)

### Chapter 1: Introduction — frame only, no numbered findings
Sets up the book's four-part structure (Understanding the Service Member → On Being a Service Member →
Individual Intervention → Family/Community Intervention) as one continuous pre-to-post arc. No
extractable findings distinct from the chapter summaries below; skipped as pure roadmap.

### Chapter 2: The Soldier's Mind — Motivation, Mindset, and Attitude (Matthews)

**1. Positive psychology as the book's stated corrective to a pathology-only lens.** Only ~10–20% of
soldiers exposed to combat develop PTSD/depression/anxiety; the majority (>80%) do not, yet the field
has historically over-focused on the minority. The chapter's explicit purpose is to catalog what makes
the *resilient* majority resilient, not just what breaks the minority.
*Candidate application:* Explicit clinical/institutional permission for the defector to be written as
functional and admirable, not defined by pathology — directly supports the "1w2 principled reformer,
proven loyalty through action" framing already locked into his README, and is a clean citation for why
his file should center competence and choice, not damage.

**2. The 24 Peterson–Seligman character strengths, organized into 6 core moral virtues** (wisdom/
knowledge, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence). Military populations as a group
score distinctly higher than matched civilians on 13 of 24 strengths, with honesty, persistence,
bravery, and teamwork specifically elevated — a *measurably different* value hierarchy from the general
population, not just self-mythology.
*Candidate application:* A concrete vocabulary for what specifically the war "kept" in him rather than
took — useful for writing what a decade-plus later still marks him as ex-military even when he isn't
talking about the war: persistence and teamwork as visible personality furniture, not just backstory
facts.

**3. Grit as a distinct, non-aptitude-correlated predictor of surviving brutal initial training** — West
Point cadets one SD above mean grit were twice as likely to complete Cadet Basic Training as
lower-grit peers, and grit didn't correlate with aptitude at all.
*Candidate application:* A clean, citable split between "talented" and "the kind of person who does not
quit" — useful if the defector's file wants to establish he wasn't necessarily the most naturally gifted
soldier in his unit (matching his README's "young soldier, not a raw recruit, but not a hardened veteran
yet either" framing), but was disproportionately hard to break.

**4. What predicts combat coping specifically, not just training survival — HIGH VALUE.** In Matthews'
own unpublished survey of 29 Army officers rating which of the 24 strengths helped them most in an
actual combat deployment, the five highest-rated were **teamwork, honesty, courage, persistence, and
judgment** — the *same* cluster that predicts surviving brutal initial training, not a different one.
*Candidate application:* This is a strong, real answer to "what kind of soldier defects for principled
reasons." Someone whose top-rated combat-coping strengths are honesty and judgment (not just courage or
teamwork) is psychologically primed to notice and act on a moral violation in the chain of command — a
direct, citable throughline from character-strength research to a 1w2's specific breaking point. Judgment
and honesty ranking above raw bravery in real officers' self-reports is worth preserving verbatim in his
voice: he'd likely describe his defection in exactly these terms — not "I couldn't take it anymore" but
"the math stopped adding up" or "I couldn't square what I was told with what I saw."

**5. Positive-psychology interventions with measured, lasting effect.** The "three good things" exercise
(nightly reflection on three things that went well and why) and "using signature strengths in a new way"
both increased happiness and decreased depression for up to 6 months in RCTs — outperforming a control
condition and rivaling more clinical approaches, with no jargon or disclosure required.
*Candidate application:* A lightweight, dialogue-portable self-management habit for the defector — "I
made myself name three good things every night, out there. Still do it." Quiet, unclinical, and
consistent with Finding 5 in the sibling Ch.07 extraction (solution-focused self-management as
long-refined performance of competence).

**6. Courage as trainable, not fixed — the Holocaust-rescuer parallel.** Hannah, Sweeney, and Lester's
courage model treats the predisposition to act bravely as interacting with situational/social factors,
not a pure trait; a separate study found the specific psychological signature of those who rescued Jews
during the Holocaust was "social responsibility, altruistic moral reasoning, empathetic concern, and
risk-taking" — a distinct, identifiable profile from bystanders in the same circumstances.
*Candidate application:* Directly relevant to the load-bearing backstory gap. Real research on people who
took catastrophic personal risk to defy an unjust system in progress (not after the fact) converges on
this same four-trait cluster. Worth treating as a design checklist for the defector's decisive moment:
did he act because of an abstract principle (moral reasoning) or a concrete person in front of him
(empathetic concern)? Real rescuers were disproportionately the latter — principle made concrete by one
person's face, not a doctrine.

### Chapter 3: Training to the Warrior Ethos — Implications for Clinicians (Christian, Stivers, Sammons)

**7. Military culture as measurably collectivistic vs. the individualistic host culture — the load-bearing
premise of the whole chapter.** Collectivism = defining the self as part of a group, group goals ahead of
personal goals, emotional investment in the group; the chapter argues this is *the* cornerstone
distinguishing military from civilian culture, more foundational than hierarchy, values, or history.
*Candidate application:* Explains, better than "he misses his unit," why the defector is likely
structurally lonelier in Cancer than a civilian exile would be in an equivalent situation — he was
resocialized at a values level, not just a habits level, and Cancer (per his README, a narrow,
esteem-denying tolerance rather than genuine belonging) cannot replicate a small-unit collectivist
structure even at its most generous.

**8. Rank etiquette carries a real criminal-law weight, not just custom.** "Undue familiarity" between
ranks (fraternization) is a court-martial offense under the UCMJ — becoming too close to a subordinate is
literally illegal, specifically because officers must retain "the will to sacrifice their subordinates"
to remain combat-effective (citing Grossman, 1996) — over-familiarity is framed as a leadership failure
mode with lethal consequences, not merely bad form.
*Candidate application:* If the defector held any junior-leadership role, this gives a real, structural,
non-cliché reason for a specific kind of guilt: not "I killed someone" but "I was close to someone I was
supposed to keep at a professional distance, and something went wrong because of it" — or conversely,
"I kept the distance exactly as trained, and someone died who might not have if I hadn't." Either version
is a precise, citable moral-injury shape distinct from simple combat guilt.

**9. The four-stage indoctrination model (Baron, 2000) — softening-up, compliance, internalization,
consolidation — and the specific mechanism of the final stage: in-group/out-group construction via
deliberate dehumanization and deindividuation of "the enemy."** Explicitly named as psychologically
necessary to enable killing without unit-endangering hesitation, and explicitly named as reversible: when
veterans later "begin to consider the people they killed as being similar to friends or neighbors,"
they experience "immense guilt and suffering, even if it is clear they were placed in a position where
that person was attempting to kill them."
*Candidate application:* HIGH VALUE. This is close to a direct mechanism for how a 1w2 principled reformer
defects: dehumanization is trained, functional, and *deliberately constructed* — meaning it can also be
deliberately un-constructed by a single moment that re-humanizes "the enemy" mid-conflict. A scene where
the defector realizes an "Upper Earth enemy" is a person exactly like the people he's protecting —
possibly literally, if his defection involved recognizing a specific individual as someone rather than a
target — is a clinically grounded, dramatically strong candidate for the still-TBD provable wartime act.
The guilt-despite-justification detail ("even if it is clear they were trying to kill him") is important
character texture: his guilt need not require that he was wrong to act.

**10. The "existential" account of why indoctrination succeeds, beyond peer pressure or persuasion
(Baron, 2006).** Groups offering a "transcendent cause" resolve a felt insignificance that individualist
civilian culture leaves unaddressed; recruits who might have entered for pragmatic reasons (education,
pay, travel) frequently come to find deeper meaning in the values themselves.
*Candidate application:* A real mechanism for why defection from Upper Earth's military is not simple
disillusionment with an employer, but a genuine identity crisis — the same drive that made him
susceptible to finding purpose in service is the drive that, confronted with an unjust war, could not be
satisfied by quiet continued service. The 1w2's "how things ought to be done" energy (per his README) maps
directly onto this — he needed the transcendent cause to actually be worth the sacrifice, and when it
stopped being that, something in him structurally could not just comply anyway.

**11. Explicit clinical guidance embedded in-chapter, not just observation: civilian therapists
commonly misidentify "training to kill" as the central fact of military enculturation — the authors call
this "a misplaced assumption."** Teamwork, mission accomplishment, and "placement of the needs of others
over self" are named as the actual fundamental elements; willingness to sacrifice one's own life for
comrades is framed as the necessary moral precondition for accepting the responsibility of taking a life,
not a separate or lesser value.
*Candidate application:* A direct writing-discipline note, not just character material: don't write the
defector as defined by killing. Write him as defined first by the sacrifice/loyalty logic, with killing as
downstream of that — consistent with his README's "genuine service impulse" 2-wing framing.

### Chapter 4: Challenges and Threats of Deployment (Reger & Moore)

**12. Combat exposure statistics from OIF/OEF as a base-rate anchor.** 93% of deployed Army personnel
reported being shot at, 95% saw dead or seriously injured Americans, 50% handled or uncovered human
remains, and 48% were personally responsible for the death of an enemy combatant — yet only ~15–17%
screened positive for PTSD/depression/anxiety 3–6 months post-return.
*Candidate application:* Useful, citable ballast against treating "he killed people in the war" as itself
remarkable or defining — it is the statistical median experience of a deployed combatant, not an outlier
fact. What should be distinctive about the defector is not that violence happened, but the specific
*shape* of the one incident that changed his allegiance.

**13. Predeployment "facing mortality" as a distinct psychological task, historically framed as tension
between self-preservation and "soldierly ideals"** (citing Colonel Thomas Salmon, WWI-era military
psychiatry) — with the chapter's own gloss: consciously accepting the risk of death, rather than avoiding
the thought of it, is treated as the foundation of both courage and later resilience.
*Candidate application:* A clean, era-appropriate voice-note for pre-defection interiority — before he
ever considered defecting, he'd have already done this specific psychological work of making peace with
dying for Upper Earth's cause. That prior, genuine reconciliation with mortality-for-a-cause is what makes
the later discovery that the cause wasn't worth it a betrayal of *his own* completed sacrifice, not just
disappointment — a sharper, more specific wound than generic disillusionment.

**14. Environmental/physiological/cognitive/emotional stressor taxonomy** (heat, gear weight, sleep
deprivation, rules-of-engagement decision pressure under time constraints with lethal stakes, homefront
issues as the single most common emotional stressor — more common than grief over a fallen comrade).
*Candidate application:* Broadly reusable texture menu for any Upper Earth or Outer Tepenia combat
scene — concrete sensory/logistical detail (weight of gear in extreme temperature, decisions made in
seconds with incomplete information under later second-guessing) rather than abstract "war was hard."

**15. Rules-of-engagement ambiguity, retroactive judgment, and cognitive stress — HIGH VALUE for the
defining incident.** Service members routinely must judge friend from foe in seconds with incomplete
information; the chapter notes many later "regret that their use of force escalated too quickly or too
slowly" once post-incident investigation reveals information unavailable at the time, and that the mere
*threat* of a command investigation is itself a distinct, chronic stressor layered on top of the original
decision.
*Candidate application:* A precise, realistic mechanism for a defining wartime act that is morally
ambiguous even to the defector himself: he made a snap judgment call under fire, using an Upper
Earth-style engagement doctrine he later came to see as unjust — and the same instant that earned him
Cancer's grudging respect (a documented refusal, a specific person spared or specific harm prevented) may
be one he still second-guesses procedurally, distinct from whether it was *morally* right. This
combination — proud of the choice, still tactically uncertain about the execution — is more textured than
either pure guilt or pure vindication.

## PART II — On Being a Service Member (Chapters 6–9)

### Chapter 6: Military Stress — Acute, Chronic, and Traumatic (Kelly & Vogt) — compressed

Largely epidemiological/risk-factor literature that overlaps heavily with `07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_
Handbook.md`'s Findings 7–8 and 18–19 (DSM clusters, comorbidity, chronicity). Compressed to the
genuinely new material:

**17. The Yerkes–Dodson inverted-U specifically distinguishes simple vs. complex tasks under
arousal.** For simple, rote tasks, more arousal = better performance (linear). For complex,
high-stakes tasks — the example given is literally "planning how to engage an enemy" — performance
peaks at *moderate* arousal and *degrades* at both very low and very high arousal.
*Candidate application:* A precise, textbook-citable reason the defector's decisive wartime moment could
plausibly have hinged on being neither too calm nor too keyed-up — useful if his file ever wants to
specify the tactical texture of the incident (e.g., he was NOT the most adrenalized person in the
situation, and that specific calibration is why his judgment call, not someone else's, is the one that
held).

**18. "Malevolent environment" as a stressor category independent of discrete traumatic events** — poor
living conditions, unpredictable schedules, lack of privacy — contributes to PTSD risk on its own,
separate from combat exposure or perceived threat.
*Candidate application:* Reusable texture: the Long Night War's toll on him need not be reducible to
single dramatic incidents; chronic grinding deprivation is its own citable stressor category, useful for
grounding "wartime hardship" in specifics beyond combat itself.

**19. Positive appraisal of the war/deployment — finding meaning in it — measurably reduces PTSD risk**,
while negative appraisal increases it; likewise, approach-based coping (actively engaging problems) beats
avoidance-based coping for high-combat-exposure individuals specifically, though avoidance can be
adaptive at lower stress levels.
*Candidate application:* A clinical basis for treating the defector's decision to defect as itself a
form of active, approach-based coping rather than avoidance/flight — consistent with his README's "moral
rebellion, not self-preservation" framing. His act of defection can be read as the positive-appraisal,
meaning-making response to an otherwise senseless war, which is part of why it protected him from the
worse trajectories this chapter describes for peers who stayed and suppressed their doubts.

### Chapter 7: Vulnerability Factors — Raising and Lowering the Threshold for Response (Freeman & Freeman)

**20. The "threshold/trigger point" model — HIGH VALUE, broadly reusable mechanic.** Individual stress
tolerance is modeled as a 1–100 scale; a person's baseline stressor load sits some distance below their
personal trigger point, and any combination of the 18 listed **vulnerability factors** (injury, illness,
hunger, anger, fatigue, loneliness, major loss, poor problem-solving, substance use, chronic pain, poor
impulse control, new life circumstance, etc.) can cumulatively erode that buffer until an otherwise
survivable stressor causes a reaction — "the straw that breaks the camel's back," explicitly not a single
cause.
*Candidate application:* A clean, non-clinical vocabulary the defector himself might use, or a
frame the writers' room could use internally, for why he seems fine most of the time and then isn't on a
specific bad day — not because any one thing is wrong, but because several small, mundane vulnerability
factors (poor sleep, an old injury flaring, a stressful week) stacked up. This is a much more textured
mechanism than a single "trigger," and it directly supports his README's chronic-injury detail: physical
pain is explicitly listed as one of the 13-18 cumulative vulnerability factors, meaning his old wounds are
not just flavor text but a mechanical contributor to his bad days.

**21. Resilience vs. recovery, explicitly distinguished.** Resilience = not responding to a stressor at
all (staying at baseline); recovery = dropping below baseline and later returning to it. The chapter's own
metaphor: the spinal column's hard vertebrae (structure) plus soft disks (shock absorption) — remove the
disks and the structure survives but loses flexibility and becomes painful.
*Candidate application:* Useful, non-clinical in-world metaphor material — "structure without give"
as a description of a veteran who never bent and therefore now creaks. Could inform how the defector
physically holds himself (rigid posture, controlled economy of motion) as an outward correlate of this
psychological trait.

**22. Predeployment psychological screening was found to have essentially no predictive power** for
postdeployment mental health outcomes (a large British study), and even the best-performing measure
(PTSD screening) had low positive predictive value due to low base rates. The takeaway stated directly in
the chapter: outward psychological "fitness" before combat cannot forecast who breaks and who doesn't.
*Candidate application:* Real, citable grounds for writing the defector's later moral rebellion as
something nobody — not his commanders, not his fellow soldiers, arguably not even himself — could have
predicted in advance. This forecloses a "he was always going to do this" origin story in favor of "the war
itself is what revealed it," which is a stronger, less deterministic character arc.

**23. Unit cohesion specifically buffers PTSD severity, independent of combat exposure level** (Brailey
et al., 2007) — soldiers reporting stronger trust/connection with their unit had less severe PTSD symptoms
even when exposure was held constant.
*Candidate application:* Strengthens the case (with Finding 76 in the sibling Ch.07 extraction, on Junger's
"tribe" theory) that the defector's isolation in Cancer is not merely social but a specifically
protective factor he lost — he had this buffer during the war and has been missing it ever since, which is
a precise, citable account of why his baseline function might be *worse* now, structurally, than it was
in-theater, despite the war itself being over.

### Chapter 8: Scanning for Danger — Readjustment to the Noncombat Environment (Conoscenti, Vine, Papa, Litz) — compressed

Largely duplicates hypervigilance/exposure-therapy mechanisms already captured in
`07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_Handbook.md` (Findings 22–32, 49–52). Two points not covered there:

**24. Combat trauma is explicitly distinguished from other trauma types by combining life-threat with
"traumatic loss and challenges to moral beliefs resulting from various acts of omission and commission"**
(citing Kubany's 1994 guilt typology) — i.e., combat trauma is definitionally about *terror plus moral
injury together*, not one or the other.
*Candidate application:* A precise, citable justification for why the defector's file should not separate
"what scared him" from "what he did/failed to do" — real combat trauma theory treats them as one
combined phenomenon, supporting a single defining incident that is simultaneously the source of both his
fear-based symptoms (if any) and his moral-injury guilt.

**25. The extended "RJ" case vignette — a full, book-length composite case running through the chapter —
is a strong structural template for how a hypervigilant-but-functional veteran's ordinary day breaks
down**: a specific triggering environment (crowded spaces, after a marketplace bombing that killed a
close friend), physical safety behaviors (checking locks, scanning exits), a spouse who doesn't fully
understand and reads his calm as fine, a public panic episode that becomes the actual catalyst for seeking
help — and the therapist's insight that his stuck point ("I couldn't save my friend, I'm worthless") is
what's actually driving the avoidance of crowds, not the crowds themselves.
*Candidate application:* A full worked example of exactly the kind of specific, lived-in symptom picture
this project's discipline calls for instead of generic "war was hard" — worth treating as a structural
model (trigger → safety behavior → relationship strain → public breaking point → reframe) rather than
importing RJ's specific details.

### Chapter 9: Assessment and Evaluation (Freeman & Freeman) — compressed, per the developer's own
### explicit allowance for a "highly clinical assessment-instrument/psychometric-testing chapter with
### no narrative use"

This chapter is a clinician's-eye survey of psychological assessment history and instruments (Binet, the
Army Alpha/Beta tests, Rorschach, MMPI, mental status exam categories) and diagnostic/malingering
criteria — genuinely low narrative yield; compressed accordingly.

**26. The malingering-assessment checklist is a near-exact mirror of Cancer's own vetting problem —
worth flagging as a structural parallel.** Real clinical malingering assessment weighs things like:
objective findings not matching claimed severity, evasive/contradictory statements, symptom
exaggeration beyond realistic base rates, and (tellingly) nightmares that "exactly replicate the original
trauma" as a red flag, because real trauma memory is typically fragmented, not a clean narrative.
*Candidate application:* Cancer's own standard for defectors — "proven loyalty through action, not
claimed remorse" — is structurally the same problem in reverse: how does an in-world vetting process
distinguish a defector who is *genuinely* remorseful/loyal from one performing it for advantage? The
real-world checklist (does the account stay awkward and incomplete rather than too clean; do actions
predate any personal benefit; is there corroboration independent of the person's own testimony) is a
directly portable design tool for how Cancer's leadership could plausibly have satisfied itself the
defector's account was genuine — useful worldbuilding for the vetting process itself, not just the
defector's outcome.

**27. HALT (Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired) as a simple, memorable acronym** for the conditions under which
people are most likely to lose control (drawn from Alcoholics Anonymous, cited here as a suicide/relapse
prevention tool) — functionally a plain-language compression of the same 13–18-item "vulnerability
factor" list from Chapter 7 (Findings 20 and this chapter's parallel list).
*Candidate application:* A natural, non-clinical phrase the defector himself might use or have been
taught by a fellow veteran — "check your HALT" as remembered field-taught wisdom, in the same register as
the Ch.07 extraction's Finding 65 "clock" metaphor from Stress Inoculation Training.

**28. Case conceptualization must pass three tests: does it account for past behavior, explain current
behavior, and predict future behavior?**
*Candidate application:* A clean discipline-check for the defector's eventual full backstory write-up —
whatever wartime act is chosen should satisfy all three of these simultaneously (explains how he ended up
in Cancer, explains his present guardedness/competence, and predicts how he'd act under a comparable
pressure again), rather than being a single dramatic beat that doesn't generalize to his ongoing character.

### Chapter 10: Theoretical Base for Treatment of Military Personnel (Freeman & Moore) — compressed

A generic CBT-methodology chapter (assessment → conceptualization → schema-based treatment) with
substantial overlap with `07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_Handbook.md`'s CPT material (Findings 33–38). Two
points worth preserving:

**29. The Warrior's Creed, quoted in full** ("I am a Warrior and a member of a team... I will never
leave a fallen comrade... I will never accept defeat... I will never quit") — presented as the kind of
memorized, recited identity-text that becomes schema, not decoration (echoing Finding 4 in
`07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_Handbook.md`, core values as identity infrastructure).
*Candidate application:* A concrete template for a comparable in-world Upper Earth military creed the
defector would still be able to recite verbatim a decade-plus later, unprompted, under stress — and whose
specific lines ("never leave a fallen comrade," "never accept defeat") could be the literal words he
violated or upheld in the act that led to his defection, giving the incident a clean thematic hook back to
his own training.

**30. Schema-work sequencing is patient-specific: some individuals process cognitive→affective→
behavioral, others behavioral→cognitive→affective — there is no single correct order.**
*Candidate application:* Minor but useful writer's-room note: the defector's own path to his decisive
moment need not be "he thought it through, then felt it, then acted" — a behavior-first sequence (he acted
on trained reflex, and only afterward arrived at the belief that justified it) is equally realistic and
arguably more consistent with a disciplined soldier's decision-making under fire.

### Chapter 11: Core Psychotherapeutic Tasks With Returning Soldiers — A Case Conceptualization Approach
### (Meichenbaum)

**31. Meichenbaum's 9-box Case Conceptualization Model, and specifically its Box 1B military-history
intake checklist — HIGH VALUE as a direct backstory-generation tool, independent of any clinical use.**
The full predeployment/deployment/postdeployment checklist asks, among many other items: job/role in
military (noting explicitly the elevated risk of roles like body recovery/identification, bomb removal,
Special Forces, or providing medical/psychiatric care under fire); degree of leadership evident in
service; an "alive day" memory (a specific near-death date the person can name); exposure to gruesome
death and human atrocities specifically distinguished from ordinary combat exposure; death of a
particular buddy; perceptions of military leadership and the mission "then and now" (explicitly framed as
two different, comparable answers); what the person would say they *miss* about deployment; and,
strikingly, "lessons the client learned."
*Candidate application:* This is close to a ready-made worksheet for filling in the defector's still-TBD
backstory specifics in a properly structured, clinically-grounded way rather than picking one dramatic
beat in isolation. In particular: (1) "perceptions of leadership then vs. now" as two distinct answers is
a clean structure for showing his loyalty erode over time rather than snapping all at once; (2) "what he
misses about deployment" is an underused, textured question for a defector — the war took things from him
but likely also gave him something (purpose, clarity, brotherhood) he still privately misses, which is a
more honest and less simplistic portrait than pure trauma; (3) an "alive day" — one specific, nameable date
he survived something — is a strong, concrete anchor point distinct from (and possibly directly connected
to) whatever act earned him his place in Cancer.

**32. "Constructing meaning from combat experience" is explicitly named as a strengths-based treatment
target (citing Frankl, Baumeister, Park & Folkman)**, distinct from and complementary to symptom
management — treatment doesn't only reduce pathology, it actively helps a veteran build a coherent
account of why the experience mattered.
*Candidate application:* Reinforces (with Finding 64 in the sibling `07_...Clinical_Handbook.md` extraction
on "homo narrans") that the defector should be written as someone who has done real narrative work on his
own war, not just someone symptomatically managing it — his account of his own past should sound like a
person who has actively made meaning of it, which is consistent with a decade-plus of self-managed,
functional presentation.

### Chapter 12: Treatment of Anxiety Disorders (Riggs) — compressed, heavy overlap with `07_...Clinical_
### Handbook.md`

This chapter's exposure/cognitive-therapy/EMDR treatment-efficacy material is comprehensively duplicated,
often by the very same cited studies, in `07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_Handbook.md` Findings 22–48.
Compressed to what's genuinely new:

**33. "When does the clock start ticking" — a real diagnostic ambiguity specific to repeated/chronic
trauma (as opposed to single-incident trauma) with no clean offset.** The chapter's own worked example: a
soldier distressed after his first firefight, whose symptoms then compound after a later, worse incident
near the end of deployment — is he acute-PTSD-since-the-second-incident, chronic-PTSD-since-the-first, or
ASD dated from redeployment? The book states plainly there is no clean answer; diagnosis depends on which
event is treated as "the" trauma, which is itself a judgment call.
*Candidate application:* Directly useful ambiguity for the defector's file: rather than a single clean
"this was the incident," his psychological turn could plausibly have compounded across the deployment,
with the "provable wartime action" being the visible, dateable capstone of a longer erosion rather than a
bolt from nowhere — consistent with Finding 19 in Part II (positive appraisal/meaning-making as an
ongoing process) and Finding 10 in Part I (the existential mechanism of indoctrination, which likewise
implies gradual rather than instant reversal).

**34. Panic disorder vs. PTSD-linked panic, differentiated by content, not just symptoms.** People with
panic disorder fear the *attack itself* ("I'm having a heart attack," "I'm going crazy"); people with
PTSD-linked panic fear the *situation that cues it* (a reminder of the trauma). Practically identical
physiological presentation, entirely different internal narration.
*Candidate application:* A precise, small piece of voice-writing guidance — if the defector ever has a
panic-adjacent moment on-page, what he says to himself/aloud should be about the *trigger* ("get me out of
here," reference to what the space reminds him of), never about the panic symptoms themselves — a subtle
but real tell distinguishing PTSD-flavored reactivity from a generic anxiety attack.

### Chapter 13: Depression and Suicide — A Diathesis–Stress Model (Rudd)

**35. Joiner's interpersonal theory of suicide — "acquired capability" via habituation to pain and
death — HIGH VALUE, broadly reusable.** Joiner's model requires three co-occurring elements for actual
lethal action: **thwarted belongingness** (unmet need to belong), **perceived burdensomeness** (belief
one's death would relieve others), and **acquired capability** — a *learned* fearlessness of pain, injury,
and death built through repeated exposure to painful/provocative events. Combat exposure and handling the
dead (even without direct combat) are explicitly named as the most toxic acquirers of this capability;
crucially, it does not fade when the war ends — once acquired, the loss of the ordinary self-preservation
instinct persists.
*Candidate application:* A precise, non-cliché mechanism for why a veteran can be simultaneously the least
suicidal-seeming person in the room (competent, functional, admired) and structurally at higher baseline
risk than people who have never been near combat — the fear that stops most people is measurably reduced
in him permanently, independent of his current mood. Also gives a clean, real basis for reading physical
recklessness/undervaluing his own safety (a plausible companion-combat-behavior quirk — taking point,
volunteering for the dangerous job) as a lasting trace of this "acquired capability," not just bravado.
Thwarted belongingness maps directly onto Cancer's structural isolation (Finding 76, sibling extraction);
perceived burdensomeness is a strong, specific internal narration for a man whose entire social value in
Cancer is his usefulness ("the moment I stop being useful to them, what am I") rather than genuine
belonging — his README's "respected but never esteemed" status is exactly the soil this grows in.

**36. The five-theme "suicidal belief system," given as near-verbatim self-talk examples specific to
combat guilt.** Guilt ("I've taken a life," "I didn't do enough," "Why did I survive?"), shame
("worthlessness"), perceived burdensomeness ("I'm supposed to be strong and can't even get through the
day"), helplessness ("I can't fix these problems"), and poor distress tolerance ("I can't stand the way I
feel").
*Candidate application:* A ready-made, clinically precise set of internal-monologue lines — useful even
divorced from suicidality itself, as the underlying belief-shape of any veteran's private self-talk under
stress. "Why did I survive" (survivor guilt) is a particularly clean, specific line for a character whose
defining trauma may involve others not surviving what he did.

**37. Fluid Vulnerability Theory: risk is time-limited and reactivatable, not a permanent state.**
Suicidal (or by extension, any acute trauma-crisis) risk rises and resolves in episodes tied to specific
triggers, with a lowered threshold for reactivation persisting afterward — consistent with the Ch.07
"threshold" model (Finding 20) but specifically framed around episodic *recurrence* rather than steady
baseline severity.
*Candidate application:* Reinforces that the defector's bad days should read as discrete, triggered
episodes against a genuinely stable baseline, not a slow permanent decline — matching the "managed, not
cured" premise established across this folder's other extractions.

**38. Real, cited statistic: a fivefold increase in U.S. Army suicide-attempt rates since the start of the
Iraq war** (Washington Post, 2008) — presented as a genuine institutional crisis distinct from ordinary
peacetime rates, not an exaggerated media narrative.
*Candidate application:* Grounds any in-world equivalent institutional crisis (Long Night War veteran
mental health, on either side) in a real, comparably dramatic order of magnitude if the developer ever
wants a citable anchor for how severe wartime psychological cost gets treated as a systemic problem, not
an individual failing.

### Chapter 14: Substance Use, Misuse, and Abuse (Morgillo Freeman & Hurst) — compressed

Largely policy/screening/clinical-technique material (CAGE questionnaire, DoD substance-use statistics
tables, stages-of-change model, an extensive "impediments to change" scale) with limited narrative
application. One piece is exceptional and worth extracting in full:

**39. Colonel Floyd "Jim" Thompson — a full real biographical case study, and the single best real-world
precedent in this entire book for "a decorated, genuinely heroic veteran whose postwar life was
nonetheless visibly, publicly hard" — HIGH VALUE.** America's longest-held Vietnam-era POW: captured
1964, held 9 years (5 in solitary confinement), tortured and starved, survived a plane crash, broken back,
and gunshot wound before capture. A committed, well-adjusted soldier before deployment who had been a
moderate weekend drinker since his teens. Upon release in 1973: severe alcohol escalation, nightmares
every night, learned of his wife's infidelity and loss of his family during captivity, divorced 1974,
repeated failed treatment attempts, a suicide attempt by combined overdose, forced out of the service
under threat of "rehabilitation failure" designation, retired 1981, recommended for (but never awarded) the
Congressional Medal of Honor, died 2002.
*Candidate application:* Directly the strongest available real template for writing a figure who is
*genuinely* heroic by any external measure and *also* visibly, publicly struggling — the two are not in
tension, they are the same story. Specific transferable beats: (1) a formal honor recommended but withheld
or delayed is a clean, textured detail for Cancer's "esteem ceiling" — the defector's own accolade could be
similarly incomplete/denied rather than either fully honored or fully ignored; (2) the return-to-a-changed-
home-life detail (family gone, world moved on without him) is a strong, specific shape for what "coming
home" cost him beyond the war itself; (3) crucially, this is a real case where treatment *failed* — not
every veteran case study in this folder should end in successful management, and using this one as a
cautionary counter-example keeps the defector's own "decade-plus, managed" status from reading as
inevitable rather than as something he specifically achieved.

**40. The CAGE questionnaire** (Cut down, Annoyed by others, Guilty, Eye-opener) — four-question,
paper-free substance screening tool, reliable for 70-80% of cases.
*Candidate application:* Minor, but a clean example of the kind of brief, portable diagnostic shorthand a
frontier Sanctuary/Cancer medical system without full institutional infrastructure could plausibly still
carry over from the war — four blunt questions rather than a formal battery.

### Chapter 15: Sleep Disorders in Service Members (Moore & Krakow) — compressed

Sleep-medicine/clinical-technique material with heavy overlap with prazosin/nightmare findings already
captured in `07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_Handbook.md` (Finding 85). Compressed to what's new:

**41. A striking, citable real experiment: sleep deprivation degrading battlefield performance in hard
numbers.** An Army artillery battalion study (cited via Grossman's *On Combat*) split into four groups over
20 days of continuous simulated fire missions, at 7, 6, 5, and 4 hours of sleep per night respectively.
Accuracy/hit rate: **98% → 50% → 28% → 15%** of optimal — a near-total collapse in effectiveness from a
3-hour nightly sleep reduction alone, with no other stressor involved.
*Candidate application:* A precise, quotable statistic-shaped detail for any Upper Earth or Tepenian
military unit's operational doctrine around sleep discipline, and a strong textural note for the defector's
own account of a taxing deployment: not just "we were exhausted" but "we were pulling numbers a third of
what we should have been, and command knew it." Grossman's framing (this is the same author/co-author of
Chapter 16 below) treats sleep loss as a *silent, self-inflicted casualty generator* — worth carrying into
that chapter's "what breaks a unit" material.

**42. Imagery Rehearsal Therapy (IRT) for nightmares — a soldier voluntarily rewrites the ending of a
recurring nightmare and rehearses the new version daily until it displaces the original.** Demonstrated
effective even for *acute* nightmares in active-duty soldiers still serving in Iraq (Moore & Krakow, 2007),
not just years-later veterans.
*Candidate application:* A concrete, game-adaptable coping ritual the defector could describe using
himself, distinct from the "note on the fridge" (Ch.07 Finding 74) and "clock" metaphor (Ch.07 Finding 65)
already catalogued — deliberately re-authoring a recurring bad dream's ending as a nightly, still-practiced
habit, rather than a treatment he underwent once and finished.

### Chapter 16: After the Battle — Violence and the Warrior (Moore, Hopewell, & Grossman) — READ IN
### FULL, HIGHEST PRIORITY FOR THE DEFECTOR

This chapter draws heavily on Dave Grossman's *On Killing* and *On Combat* and is the single richest
chapter in the book for the companion's core gap — not because it directly describes disobeying orders
(Chapter 3's dehumanization/re-humanization mechanism, Findings 9–10, remains the strongest source for
that specific beat), but because it supplies the real psychological *mechanics of killing itself*, which
any account of "what he did in the war" has to be built on top of.

**43. The "universal human phobia" — interpersonal aggression — and warriors as the ~2% who move
toward it instead of away.** Grossman's own framing, cited directly: shown a hypothetical armed attacker
in a crowded room, up to 98% of people would freeze or flee; a handful would fight defensively; almost none
would *advance* toward the threat to protect others. The warrior is defined precisely by training that
overrides this near-universal reflex.
*Candidate application:* A precise, almost mythic vocabulary for what made the defector recruitable and
trainable as a soldier in the first place, before any of his moral arc begins — he was already, at baseline,
in the rare minority who moves toward danger rather than away from it. This is character material that
predates and is independent of his eventual defection, useful for describing his instinctive behavior as a
companion (positioning himself between the player and danger without being asked).

**44. Grossman's three-stage sequence for what happens after a kill — HIGH VALUE, directly load-bearing.**
(1) **Exhilaration / "survivor euphoria"** — genuine relief and satisfaction at having lived; (2) an
**overwhelming rush of remorse and guilt** ("I just killed a man and I enjoyed it — what's wrong with me?");
(3) a **lifelong process of rationalization** — if this final stage fails to resolve, it becomes a path to
PTSD or chronic guilt; if it succeeds, the killing does not have to remain psychologically damaging.
*Candidate application:* This is the single cleanest available real-world model for the actual internal
mechanics of the defector's decisive violent act, whatever it turns out to be — not "he felt bad about
killing," but a specific three-beat sequence (relief he lived → self-disgust at that relief → years of
building a story he can live inside). Stage 3 — an ongoing rationalization process, not a single completed
realization — is consistent with "managed, not cured" and gives him something to still be actively doing,
a decade-plus later, rather than something long since finished.

**45. The explicit, almost provocative core thesis: killing itself is not intrinsically psychologically
damaging — the damage comes from whether the killer can rationalize it as lawful and justified,
*before* the fact.** Direct quote (Grossman, cited in-text): "If you tell yourself that killing will be an
earth-shattering, traumatic event, then it probably will be. But if you prepare yourself mentally and can
rationalize and accept that killing is lawful and justified during combat then using deadly force does not
have to be a psychologically damaging event." The chapter is emphatic that there is "nothing wrong" with a
soldier who kills without lasting damage, and equally nothing wrong with one who is deeply troubled by it —
both are normal, and pathologizing either is a mistake.
*Candidate application:* HIGH VALUE, and arguably the load-bearing reframe for the whole brief. This
flips the standard assumption that "killing = trauma" and instead makes the *moral framework going in* the
determining variable — meaning the defector's defining wartime act does not need to be "he was traumatized
by killing." A cleaner, more specific reading consistent with his README's 1w2 "principled reformer" framing:
his crisis was not that he killed and it broke him — it's that at some point he could **no longer honestly
rationalize a specific act as lawful and justified**, and *that* — the collapse of the justification, not
the act of violence itself — is what turned him. This reframes "what wartime action earned him his place in
Cancer" away from a single dramatic kill and toward a moment where the *rationalization itself failed*
(e.g., an order he was told was lawful and necessary that he could see, in the moment, was neither) —
directly compatible with Chapter 3's dehumanization-reversal mechanism (Finding 9) and Chapter 6/12's
rules-of-engagement ambiguity material (Findings 15, 33).

**46. Procedural/motor memory as the neurological basis of both combat competence and persistent
hypervigilance — mechanically distinct from ordinary declarative memory.** Combat skills are encoded via
the basal ganglia/cerebellum/motor cortex (like riding a bike), not the hippocampus (like remembering a
poem) — "virtually resistant to decay." "Flow"/"the zone" (Csikszentmihalyi) is the psychological
experience of this overlearned procedural system running.
*Candidate application:* A precise, real mechanism for a specific, small, believable companion tell:
reflexive competence returning instantly and completely under stress (weapon handling, scanning a room,
positioning) with none of the rust a decade of peace would produce in ordinary skills — because it isn't
stored the way ordinary memory is. Distinguishes "he's still dangerous" from "he's still traumatized" as
two separate, independently true facts.

**47. Real epidemiological myth-busting the developer should actively use, not just avoid contradicting:
veterans as a population group are measurably *less* violent, less incarcerated, and more successful than
matched nonveteran civilian peers, going back to WWI** — the chapter names and rebuts a specific,
real media narrative ("the crazed vet") using Burkett & Whitley's research, and explicitly states training
in "restraint and discipline" is what soldiers actually acquire, not "impulsivity and carelessness." A
cited example: adjusted-for-population murder rates in several major U.S. cities exceed the OIF/OEF
veteran murder rate that made national headlines.
*Candidate application:* Directly protects this project's own standing "Fallout precedence" and
"no cheap tragedy" instincts — the defector should not default to being written as a barely-contained
danger. His baseline should read as *more* disciplined and controlled than an equivalent civilian, not
less, with any violence he does show being specific, triggered, and comorbid with an identifiable stressor
(per Finding 48/HOT below), never a generic ambient menace.

**48. HOT — a compact real risk-assessment framework: History, Opportunity, Triggers.** Real violence
risk in veterans is assessed via a person's history of past dangerous conduct in similar circumstances,
the practical opportunity for it to recur, and identifiable triggering conditions — not by diagnosis alone.
*Candidate application:* A clean three-part design checklist if the developer ever wants to write a
specific in-fiction scene where the defector's temper or danger becomes an active plot concern — it should
be built from a real precedent (has he done something like this before, is he in a position to do it again,
what specifically set him off), not from unmotivated volatility.

**49. BATTLEMIND — a real, named military program that explicitly reframes combat-honed traits as
assets to be *redirected*, not pathology to be erased.** Developed at Walter Reed specifically to help
returning soldiers "acquire a new habit" without discarding the old one — hypervigilance, discipline, and
in-the-zone focus are named outright as strengths that need a new target, not defects.
*Candidate application:* A strong, real, citable philosophical stance the defector himself might
articulate almost verbatim if asked how he's "gotten better" — not "I got rid of what the war made me,"
but "I found new places to point it." Consistent with and reinforcing Finding 3 in Part I (Grossman's own
"emotional distance" observation) and the broader "managed, not cured" throughline across this folder.

### Chapter 17: Myths and Realities of Pharmacotherapy in the Military (Morgillo Freeman, Lundt, Swanton,
### & Moore) — compressed, this is the book's genuinely "highly clinical, no narrative use" chapter

Drug-class-by-drug-class prescribing guidance (SSRIs, mood stabilizers, sleep aids, security-clearance

allowance rather than mined at full density.

**50. The chapter's one reusable institutional principle: "it is not the mental health visit nor treatment
that will limit one's ability to hold certain positions, but the behaviors associated with untreated
disorders."** A service member successfully treated for depression with therapy and medication is
explicitly framed as more employable/deployable than one whose *untreated* symptoms cause visible
disruption (missed formation, etc.) — the stigma is real and acknowledged, but the actual institutional
policy rewards disclosure and treatment over silent struggle.
*Candidate application:* A clean, citable in-world policy stance for how Cancer or Upper Earth's own
military bureaucracy might formally treat psychological treatment history — worth having as the *stated*
institutional position even while the informal social stigma (which the chapter also documents at length)
remains real and is what actually drives the defector's guardedness, not fear of an official penalty.
Distinguishes "the system says this is fine" from "the culture doesn't actually believe that," which is a
more textured and realistic bureaucratic-vs-cultural gap than either alone.

## PART IV — The Service Member's Family and Community: Intervention (Chapters 18–23)

### Chapter 18: War and Children Coping With Parental Deployment (Mabe) — compressed

This chapter addresses children of *currently* deploying parents (stay-behind spouse/child dynamics during
an active deployment), which has no direct bearing on the defector himself (no evidence he has children,
and the war is over) but is flagged for cross-series reuse per the developer's explicit "keep anything that
even possibly can be applied" instruction.

**51. Deployment is reframed clinically as an ambiguous loss with three named stress dimensions — loss,
uncertainty, and uncontrollability — each with matched therapeutic responses drawn from an unexpected
parallel literature: pediatric cancer.** "Ambiguous loss" = a parent alive but not present, and present but
facing possible death. The chapter borrows almost its entire uncertainty-coping framework from childhood
cancer research (three distinct components of uncertainty: probability, timing, and lack of a reference
frame from past experience) because the emotional shape is structurally identical.
*Candidate application:* Broadly reusable for any Tepenian household living through an ongoing
deployment/absence (a soldier, an off-world researcher, anyone away in dangerous circumstances) — not
specific to war. The "focus on present certainties" technique (a concrete, controllable near-term plan
substituting for an uncontrollable long-term unknown) is a strong, portable coping line for any character.

**52. A hard, real, myth-correcting number: even in an active, ongoing modern war, the deployed-parent
casualty rate was under 1%** — far below what children and families typically fear, and explicit
psychoeducational correction of this gap between fear and actual risk is named as a real, effective
therapeutic intervention.
*Candidate application:* Useful ballast against any Tepenian narrative reflexively assuming a wartime
household's dominant emotional reality was terror of death — the *far* more common lived reality, per this
literature, is loneliness, role disruption, and ambiguity, not acute fear. Worth keeping in mind for any
homefront-during-the-Long-Night-War vignette.

**53. Military family strengths, named explicitly against the media cliché of the damaged military
household** — strong community bonds, built-in mutual-hardship solidarity, family-friendly institutional
structure (healthcare, childcare, job security), and reported *increases* in self-reliance and new
competencies as a genuine outcome of deployment stress, not just its cost.
*Candidate application:* A citable counterweight to any temptation to write Upper Earth or Tepenian
military family life as uniformly grim — real research finds real resilience and real institutional
support alongside the real hardship, which is more textured and less melodramatic than either extreme.

### Chapter 19: Intimate Relationships and the Military (Lyons) — read closely, high priority for the
### romance arc

**54. "Previous trauma appears to act as a 'phantom' in the couple relationship, always present but not
always seen or understood by either partner."** Direct quote from a qualitative study of couples in
therapy (Goff et al., 2006), describing how a partner's trauma history shapes the relationship's daily
texture — arguments, withdrawal patterns, what feels safe — without either person consciously naming it as
the source.
*Candidate application:* A precise, quotable framing for the romance arc's central design problem, more
useful as a phrase than any clinical vocabulary: the war is present in the relationship from day one, felt
before it's ever discussed — matching this project's "Mystery = Soft-Detail Delivery" convention (full
resolution stays private; the player gets ambient hints long before the explicit reveal).

**55. Emotional numbing, independently confirmed across multiple separate studies (not anger) as the
single symptom most consistently predictive of relationship dissatisfaction — from BOTH partners'
perspectives simultaneously, which anger is not.** Riggs et al. found the *numbing* subset specifically
(constricted affect, detachment, loss of interest in pleasurable activities) — not general avoidance,
not anger — predicted both the veteran's own and the partner's ratings of relationship quality. A
separate Australian study (measuring different variables) found anger predicted both ratings too, but the
authors note the two studies are not actually contradictory once compared closely — numbing and anger are
both real, independently confirmed threats to intimacy, just measured differently across studies.
*Candidate application:* Reinforces and sharpens Finding 69 in the sibling `07_...Clinical_Handbook.md`
extraction (numbing as the quiet, slow-burn relational threat) with a second, independent confirmation —
strong enough now to treat as a load-bearing design decision, not just a candidate: if the defector's
central romance obstacle is written as one specific symptom, it should be emotional numbing/withdrawal,
not temper. His danger to a partner is disappearing inside himself, not exploding.

**56. A genuinely striking, counterintuitive finding on attachment style as a moderator of a
partner's own posttraumatic growth — HIGH VALUE, underused anywhere else in this folder.** Studying
wives of former POWs: for wives with a **secure attachment style**, *greater* PTSD severity in the husband
predicted *more* personal growth in the wife. For wives with an **insecure/avoidant attachment style**,
the relationship *reversed* — more PTSD in the husband predicted *less* growth in the wife. The veteran's
symptom severity alone did not determine the partner's outcome; the partner's own attachment style
determined which direction the correlation ran.
*Candidate application:* A sophisticated, non-obvious mechanism for player-agency design in a romance
arc: the *player character's* own established relational pattern (secure vs. anxious/avoidant, if this
project ever models player attachment style, or simply the player's chosen dialogue pattern of steady
engagement vs. protective withdrawal) could determine whether the defector's harder moments become a site
of shared growth or of mutual retreat — making the romance's outcome genuinely dependent on the *player's*
consistent behavior pattern across many scenes, not a single dialogue check. This is a stronger, more
game-native mechanic than a simple "raise trust meter."

**57. Partner burden is severe, real, and specifically under-addressed by existing support models —
"more than the treatment team."** In the author's own survey of 89 partners of veterans with PTSD, roughly
half reported feeling "on the verge of a nervous breakdown"; partners described managing symptoms,
structuring the household around avoiding triggers, and absorbing roles the veteran can't fill as a
larger burden than professional treatment itself. Critically, when asked what support *they* actually
wanted, the most common request was NOT more information about PTSD (most already knew plenty) — it was
**individual support for themselves** and **relief from isolation**, followed by a partners-only peer
group.
*Candidate application:* Directly actionable for the romance questline's structure: the player-as-partner
character should not be given an "education about his condition" arc (a common, slightly condescending
narrative default) — the story should instead give the player their *own* outlet, their own confidants, and
their own visible cost for the relationship, mirroring what real partners say they actually want. This also
cross-validates Finding 68 in the sibling Ch.07 extraction ("partner accommodation" as a trap) — the two
sources independently converge on the same design lesson: caretaking too hard is the wrong instinct, for
both the veteran and the caretaking partner's own wellbeing.

**58. MHAT-IV's own conclusion: unit leadership quality is "a panacea, a silver bullet" for combat
troops' mental health — bigger than combat-exposure level itself.** Troops with heavy combat exposure but
a *good* NCO had rates of mental health problems barely higher than *low*-exposure troops with a *poor*
NCO — roughly half the rate of high-exposure troops stuck with poor leadership.
*Candidate application:* A precise, citable mechanism for writing the specific officer/NCO the defector
served under as a load-bearing character in his backstory, not incidental — whether that person was a
good or bad leader is arguably a bigger single variable in his outcome than the combat itself, and gives a
natural second character (a commanding officer, alive or dead, on Upper Earth's side or lost in the war) to
anchor his account of "what happened" around.

**59. Numbers as ballast, not exaggeration: 24% of wives in one study named their husband's deployment
the single "most traumatic" event of their own lives** — a reminder that the stay-behind partner's
experience is not automatically secondary or lesser than the deployed partner's, even though the literal
danger sat with the soldier.
*Candidate application:* Grounds writing the player-character (if romancing the defector, or any future
character's spouse/partner during Long Night War flashbacks) as having a full, independently real trauma
of their own from the waiting and not-knowing — not merely a supporting role to his story.

### Chapter 20: Military Children — The Sometimes Orphans of War (Cohen, Goodman, Campbell, Carroll,

### Chapter 22: Issues of Grief, Loss, Honor, and Remembrance — Spirituality and Work With Military
### Personnel and Their Families (Drescher, Burgoyne, Casas, Lovato, Curran, Pivar, & Foy) — READ IN
### FULL, HIGH PRIORITY

**64. The formal working definition of moral injury — HIGH VALUE, the single most precise and directly
usable definition of the concept anywhere in this folder.** Developed by Litz, Drescher, Rosen, and Foy
(2007, cited verbatim): "disruption in an individual's confidence and expectations about their own or
others' motivation or capacity to behave in a just and ethical manner brought about by bearing witness to
perceived immoral acts, failure to stop such actions, or perpetration of immoral acts, in particular
actions that are inhumane, cruel, depraved, or violent, bringing about pain and suffering of others or
their death." Critically, the definition names **three distinct pathways** to moral injury, not one:
(1) *perpetrating* an immoral act oneself, (2) *witnessing* one and failing to prevent it, and (3) simply
*bearing witness* to it. The chapter explicitly separates moral injury from PTSD: its criteria are absent
from the DSM's trauma definition entirely, because combat killing performed "in accord with rules of
engagement" is institutionally sanctioned, not intrinsically traumatic in the PTSD-diagnostic sense — moral
injury is specifically about violated *ethical* expectations, not violated *safety* ones.
*Candidate application:* This is the load-bearing clinical vocabulary for the entire brief. Given Finding 45
in Chapter 16 (killing itself isn't intrinsically damaging; failed rationalization is), moral injury via
pathway (2) — **witnessing an immoral act and failing to stop it** — is the single strongest, most
textured candidate for "what wartime action earned him his place in Cancer," because it converts the
central incident from "something he did" into "something he finally refused to let happen," which is
directly compatible with his README's 1w2 "principled reformer" identity far better than a straightforward
kill would be. His defection becomes the *correction* of a moral injury already in progress — the moment he
stopped merely bearing witness and intervened — rather than the injury's origin point.

**65. Symptom domains specific to moral injury, distinct from and additive to PTSD's diagnostic criteria**
— named directly: negative changes in ethical attitudes/behavior, loss or change of spirituality (including
negative attributions about a higher power), guilt/shame/alienation, anhedonia and dysphoria, distrust in
social and cultural contracts, aggressive behaviors, and poor self-care/self-harm.
*Candidate application:* A clean symptom checklist distinct from the PTSD vocabulary already exhausted in
this folder's other extractions — "distrust in social/cultural contracts" specifically maps onto why the
defector might be guarded not just around Upper Earth topics but around *any* institution or chain of
command, including Cancer's own leadership, regardless of how well he's actually treated there.

**66. A real, cited survey finding directly relevant to the "provable wartime action" standard — HIGH
VALUE.** A Mental Health Advisory Team survey of deployed soldiers and Marines found approximately **10%**
admitted to "mistreating noncombatants" (damaging property or striking a noncombatant unnecessarily); rate
of admission **doubled** among personnel with high anger, high combat exposure, or an existing mental
health problem, and rose further with more/longer deployments. This is presented as real, base-rate evidence
that a meaningful minority of ordinary units include this specific failure mode — not an outlier crime, a
measured, non-rare occurrence.
*Candidate application:* Concrete, real-world-grounded texture for the unit environment the defector broke
from: mistreatment of noncombatants wasn't necessarily a single monstrous outlier act by one bad actor in
his unit — it's a real, measured, non-negligible baseline behavior under sustained combat stress, meaning
his intervention could plausibly have been against an ordinary, tired, angry fellow soldier rather than a
cartoonish villain, which is a harder and more morally interesting confrontation to have written.

**67. Moral injury's structural trap: silence and isolation actively entrench it, because the survivor
never gets external feedback correcting a distorted self-appraisal.** Unlike ordinary trauma, moral injury
specifically drives concealment (shame, fear of judgment) — meaning the people most in need of
corrective outside perspective are the ones least likely to seek it, creating a self-sustaining loop.
*Candidate application:* Directly explains, mechanistically, why a full decade-plus of managed-but-private
guilt is realistic rather than implausible — and gives a precise reason his eventual opening-up (to the
player, in a companion arc) has to come from someone he trusts enough to risk being morally *judged* by,
not just someone he likes.

**68. The chapter's own closing frame: the central tension for a returning warrior is between isolation
and connection**, and spirituality is explicitly defined (independent of any specific religion) as
"connection — connection to that which transcends oneself." Five APA-identified resilience factors are
named, with strong social support listed first and explicitly described as amplifying the other four.
*Candidate application:* A clean thematic frame for the character overall — his arc's throughline isn't
"heal from trauma," it's "move from isolation to connection," which reframes his romance and friendship
questlines as the actual mechanism of his ongoing recovery, not a reward external to it.

**69. Psychological First Aid's five core principles — safety, arousal reduction, social connectedness,
self-efficacy, and hope** — a real, field-tested framework (originally for disaster relief, later adopted
by Navy/Marine chaplains) for what actually helps in the near-term, distinct from formal clinical treatment.
*Candidate application:* A clean, non-clinical five-beat structure usable for any single scene where another
character (the player, a Cancer ally) is trying to help the defector through an acute bad moment — establish
physical safety first, help him physically calm down, connect him to another person, remind him of a
competence he still has, and leave him with something to hope for — in that order, matching real
crisis-response practice rather than an improvised comfort scene.

**70. An 18-question homecoming-assessment checklist for families**, useful less as clinical content and
more as a structural template: it asks about resumed routines, missed family milestones, feeling like "a bit
of an outsider" in one's own family, whether the returning member has been able to talk about the war with
a partner vs. extended family vs. children (three separate, distinct disclosure targets), and — the final,
almost unanswerable question — "How will you all know that your returning family member is really home?"
*Candidate application:* That final question is an unusually strong, quotable line for a homecoming/
reunion-themed scene anywhere in this project's other veteran or exile characters' arcs — it names the real,
unresolvable uncertainty at the heart of any reintegration story better than any clinical term could.

### Chapter 23: Future Directions — Trauma, Resilience, and Recovery Research (Peterson, Cigrang, & Isler)
### — compressed

This closing chapter is a research-agenda literature review, heavily epidemiological (PCL/PDHA/PDHRA
survey data, risk-factor meta-analyses, treatment-format comparisons) that substantially re-covers ground
already extracted from earlier chapters in this book and from `07_Treating_PTSD_Clinical_Handbook.md`.
Two genuinely new data points:

**71. A precise, additive dose-response demonstration: of crime victims who were raped, physically
injured, AND feared for their lives during the assault, 79% developed PTSD** — each additional trauma
dimension (threat to life, physical injury, the assault type itself) compounds the risk roughly
independently, the cleanest quantified illustration of the "dose-response" principle referenced more
vaguely elsewhere in this folder.
*Candidate application:* Reinforces (with Finding 12 in Part I) that the defector's defining incident is
more textured if it stacks multiple trauma dimensions rather than being a single clean threat-to-life
moment — physical injury (matching his README's chronic-injury detail) compounding with witnessed/
perpetrated harm compounding with life-threat, each layer independently plausible and mutually
reinforcing rather than redundant.

**72. A real, striking historical statistic on battlefield medicine: combat-wounded survival in Iraq
reached ~90%, versus 74–75% in Korea, Vietnam, and the Gulf War** — the highest survival rate for combat
wounds in recorded history at the time of writing, attributed to advances in personal protective
equipment and forward trauma care, not merely improved evacuation speed.
*Candidate application:* Directly useful, real-world-grounded worldbuilding texture for the Long Night
War's medical timeline — a plausible in-fiction parallel where Upper Earth or Concordian battlefield
medicine crossed a similar threshold, meaning more severely wounded survivors (with more severe chronic
injuries, consistent with the defector's own "one or two chronic wartime injuries" detail) than any
earlier conflict in Tepenian history would have produced — casting his physical wounds as a mark of a
specific medical-historical moment, not a generic "old war wound."
