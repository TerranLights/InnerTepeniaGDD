# Neuropsychology of PTSD — Real Clinical/Biological Source Material

Source: *Neuropsychology of PTSD: Biological, Cognitive, and Clinical Perspectives*, eds. Jennifer J.
Vasterling & Chris R. Brewin (Guilford Press, 2005) — an edited academic/clinical volume, 13 chapters, 352
pages. **Read in full, sequentially, cover to cover** (front matter through Chapter 13's reference list) —
no chapter skipped. This is the most clinically/scientifically dense book in the PTSD collection: it is the
source for *mechanism* (what part of memory/attention/executive function/neuroendocrine regulation is
altered, by what pathway, and what that looks like from outside as behavior), not for narrative or lived
experience — those are better sourced from the other books in this folder.

**Depth note per chapter, stated explicitly per this project's honesty rule:** Chapters 1-6, 8, 10, 12 were
read at full narrative depth and are represented below at that depth. Chapter 7 (early trauma/children) and
Chapter 9 (aging trauma survivors/Holocaust and elderly-POW cohorts) were read in full but are **compressed
here** — both are real, rigorous developmental-psychology chapters, but their subject populations (maltreated
children under 12, survivors in their 70s-90s) are the two population brackets furthest from a mid-30s/early-40s
ex-military companion, so only the load-bearing structural findings are extracted, not the full population
detail. Chapter 11 (clinical assessment) and Chapter 13 (pharmacology) are likewise compressed — 11 into a
short practical-diagnostic-domain note, 13 into a summary of the mechanistic threads that already appear
elsewhere in this file (its own content is a 20-page medication-by-medication reference table with little
behaviorally-observable content beyond what Chapter 2's neurobiology already establishes).

**Real-world-fact discipline, per this project's standing rule:** every finding below is a documented
finding *about humans* in a real 2005 clinical text. Nothing here is a specification the Cancer defector, any
robot, or any Tepenian institution is obligated to match. Divergence is expected and often better — the
value of this material is as a *mechanism menu*, not a checklist.

## PART I — Core mechanisms for the Cancer defector's stat profile and chronic injury

This is the single most useful part of the book for turning Nerve 10 / Investigation 7 / "one or two chronic
injuries" from placeholders into something mechanistically real. Read this part first.

### 1. The norepinephrine "chemical switch" — why hypervigilance and executive collapse are the same mechanism, pointed two directions

Ch. 2 (Southwick, Rasmusson, Barron, & Arnsten). The locus coeruleus (LC) — the brainstem nucleus holding
most of the brain's noradrenergic cell bodies — governs alertness, orienting to novel stimuli, and selective
attention. Its output, norepinephrine (NE), binds two different classes of receptor in the prefrontal cortex
(PFC) with different affinities: **alpha2A receptors** (higher affinity, engaged at ordinary/moderate NE
levels) and **alpha1 and beta receptors** (lower affinity, engaged only when NE floods the system under acute
stress). Alpha2A engagement *strengthens* PFC function — it improves working memory, sharpens delay-related
neuronal firing, and helps suppress irrelevant/distracting input so the organism can concentrate. Alpha1/beta
engagement under high stress does the opposite: it takes the PFC "off-line," collapsing executive control,
while simultaneously *unleashing* the amygdala (the PFC ordinarily inhibits amygdala activity; when the PFC
goes offline, that inhibition lifts). The literal phrase from the text: NE "can act as a chemical switch,
determining which brain structures have control over our behavior." The pharmacological validation of this
model is that alpha2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine) *improve* PFC cognitive function and reduce PTSD
arousal symptoms in humans, while alpha1 antagonists (prazosin) do the same by blocking the collapse pathway.

**Candidate application:** This is a real, textually precise mechanism for describing a character who is
simultaneously exceptional under threat (Nerve 10) and has documented chronic costs. The switch model means
the *same* neurochemical system that gives him his battlefield composure — moderate-to-elevated tonic NE
engaging alpha2A receptors, sharpening focus and orienting — is also the system that, pushed past a threshold
(the specific stressor is individual and could be canonized later: prolonged high-stakes negotiation, being
crowded/cornered, certain sounds/smells), can flip him into the alpha1/beta regime: executive collapse,
amygdala dominance, and behavior he does not fully control in the moment. This gives a mechanically honest
answer to "what does his Nerve 10 cost him" — it is not free; it is a system that runs close to a real
edge, and the same wiring that makes him exceptional is the wiring that fails hardest when it fails.

### 2. DHEA/cortisol ratio and NPY — the single most direct real-world analogue for "Nerve 10" as a documented resilience trait, not an arbitrary number

Ch. 2 and Ch. 13 (cross-referenced). **Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)** is an adrenal steroid co-released with
cortisol; it has *antiglucocorticoid* effects (it opposes cortisol's PFC-impairing action) and positively
modulates NMDA receptor function. Two real findings anchor this as a documented resilience marker, not
speculation: (1) Rasmusson et al. (2004) found the magnitude of DHEA's release under maximal adrenal
stimulation was **inversely** related to PTSD symptom severity in premenopausal women — higher DHEA
reactivity meant fewer avoidance/hyperarousal symptoms. (2) Morgan et al. (2000, cited again in Ch. 8 and Ch.
13) studied **U.S. military personnel undergoing actual survival training** (a real elite stress-inoculation
program) and found a **positive relationship between the DHEAS/cortisol ratio and behavioral performance
during severe acute stress**, and a companion study found an **inverse relationship between neuropeptide Y
(NPY) release and stress-induced performance decrements due to dissociation** in the same population — i.e.,
soldiers whose NPY response was more robust dissociated less and performed better under the worst stress the
training could produce. NPY is co-released with NE from noradrenergic neurons and inhibits both NE and CRF
(corticotropin-releasing factor) release, functioning as an endogenous brake on the same escalation loop
described in Finding 1.

**Candidate application:** This is the strongest available real-world grounding for Nerve 10 specifically.
It gives a legitimate, non-arbitrary reading: the character's exceptional Nerve is not "he doesn't feel fear"
but a documented real neuroendocrine profile — elevated DHEA/cortisol ratio and/or robust NPY reactivity —
that in actual studied populations (special-operations-style survival training) predicts measurably better
performance and less dissociation under maximal acute stress, exactly the profile that would explain why
Cancer's defector framework singled him out as "proven wartime loyalty through action" specifically — his
stat ceiling is the kind of thing that shows up as *performance under fire*, which is externally verifiable
in a way "he says he's brave" is not. It is real, falls short of turning him into a comic-book character
(the same studies show this trait has limits — DHEA/NPY reserves are finite and glucocorticoid systems
still take a cumulative toll even in high responders), and gives a natural hook for a Trait/perk: something
like "his composure is a real physiological reserve, not infinite willpower — it can be measurably depleted
by cumulative stress within a session, and restored by rest/downtime," which is a mechanically honest way to
use Nerve 10 as a resource rather than a flat stat.

### 3. The Mirsky four-part attention model — the cleanest available split between what improves and what degrades under PTSD

Ch. 8 (Vasterling & Brailey), citing Mirsky, Anthony, Duncan, Ahearn, & Kellam (1991). Attention is not one
thing; the Mirsky model splits it into four dissociable components, each with a different brain-behavior
relationship: **Focus-Execute** (the ability to focus on specific environmental cues from an array and
respond appropriately — e.g., letter cancellation, the standard non-emotional Stroop); **Sustain** (the
capacity to maintain vigilance over time — continuous performance tasks); **Shift** (the ability to change
attentional focus adaptively — card-sorting tasks); and **Encode** (the capacity to register, recall, and
mentally manipulate information — digit span, mental arithmetic, working memory). Across two independent
combat-veteran cohorts (1991 Gulf War and Vietnam), Vasterling and colleagues found PTSD-diagnosed veterans
performed **worse** than trauma-exposed veterans without PTSD on Sustain and Encode tasks, but showed **no
deficit** — sometimes slightly better performance — on Focus-Execute and Shift tasks. Separately, the
information-processing literature (Ch. 5) shows PTSD is associated with *enhanced*, involuntary attentional
capture by threat-relevant material specifically (the emotional Stroop effect — see Finding 8) — an effect
so automatic it survived a direct financial incentive to override it in one study (Constans et al., 2004:
combat veterans offered cash for fast/accurate performance still could not suppress the slowdown on
threat words).

**Candidate application:** This is the cleanest, most citable real mechanism for grounding Investigation 7
specifically, and for explaining how Nerve 10 and Investigation 7 can coexist with real cognitive cost
elsewhere without contradiction. The pattern is not "PTSD makes you worse at everything" — it is a specific,
real dissociation: **threat-relevant scanning, pattern-detection, and moment-to-moment focus (Focus-Execute)
are intact or enhanced; sustained attention to boring/neutral material and working-memory/encoding are
degraded.** This maps almost exactly onto a companion who is extraordinary at reading a room, catching a
lie, noticing the wrong detail in a crowd (Investigation 7) — because that is real-time Focus-Execute
threat-scanning, the domain PTSD research shows is *not* impaired and may be sharpened — while plausibly
being bad at paperwork, bored administrative tasks, or holding a long list of instructions in mind (Sustain/
Encode, the domains that are impaired). It also explains the automaticity point precisely: his vigilance is
not a chosen skill he can dial down — the Constans finding shows this kind of threat-attention resists
volitional suppression even under direct incentive not to. That is a good in-fiction explanation for why he
reads as "can't fully relax," including in Cancer, where he is safe.

### 4. Electrophysiology — the same automatic/voluntary split shows up at the level of raw brain signal, with a specific named deficit for "can't filter out irrelevant stimuli"

Ch. 4 (Metzger, Gilbertson, & Orr). Several ERP (event-related brain potential) findings converge on the same
story as Findings 1 and 3, at a finer grain:
- **P50 sensory gating deficit:** normally, the brain's response to a second identical click (in a paired-click
  procedure) is suppressed relative to the first — a basic filtering/habituation reflex. PTSD is repeatedly
  associated with **failure of this suppression** (increased P50 ratio) — the brain keeps treating a
  repeated, non-informative stimulus as though it might be meaningful. Arciniegas et al. (2000) link this
  directly to hypervigilance and attentional impairment: an inability to filter irrelevant stimuli.
- **P2 "reducing" pattern:** normal listeners show larger brain response as a tone gets louder
  ("augmenting"). At a certain intensity, a *protective* inhibitory mechanism kicks in and brain response
  starts *shrinking* instead ("reducing") — the cortex protecting itself from overstimulation. Combat
  veterans with PTSD showed this protective reducing pattern **at markedly lower sound intensities** than
  non-PTSD veterans (75% showed reducing vs. 83% of non-PTSD veterans showing the normal augmenting pattern)
  — i.e., their nervous system hits its own protective circuit-breaker sooner.
- **Mismatch negativity (MMN) and P3a ("novelty P3"):** these index automatic, preconscious detection of a
  changed/deviant/novel stimulus. PTSD is associated with **larger** MMN and P3a responses — objectively
  measurable heightened, automatic novelty/change detection, explicitly linked by the researchers to
  hypervigilance and being "on alert."
- **P3b (voluntary, sustained-attention index):** in contrast, PTSD is the most replicated finding of
  **diminished** P3b amplitude to task-relevant targets in an oddball/vigilance task — an index of *reduced*
  capacity-limited attentional resources devoted to voluntary, sustained stimulus evaluation.
- **Threat-specific P3 augmentation:** when the "distractor" stimuli in the same paradigm are combat pictures
  or words rather than neutral stimuli, PTSD participants show **larger, not smaller**, P3 responses to
  them — even though instructed to ignore them — and this pattern was accurate enough to correctly classify
  90% of Israeli combat veterans with PTSD vs. 85% of those without, using only the brain-signal response to
  trauma-relevant images.

**Candidate application:** This is the physiological floor under Finding 3 — automatic/preconscious/novelty-
and-threat detection is measurably enhanced at the level of raw brain electrical response, while
voluntary/sustained attention to task-relevant-but-neutral material is measurably reduced, in the *same*
population, on the *same* testing day. Combined with the P50 gating-deficit language ("inability to filter
out irrelevant environmental stimuli, interpreting repetitive stimuli as though they might maintain
significance") this gives a literal, named clinical concept — sensory gating — for "why can't he tune
anything out," which is exactly the kind of thing that would show up as an observable behavior: he notices
things nobody asked him to notice, he startles at things that turned out to be nothing, and he cannot always
tell in the moment which of those two facts is true until afterward.

### 5. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) and PTSD coexist, and dual-representation theory extends cleanly to explain why — the strongest real mechanism for a "chronic injury" grounded in memory gaps rather than a vague wound

Ch. 10 (Harvey, Kopelman, & Brewin) is the single best chapter in the book for the "one or two chronic
wartime/training injuries" line in the companion's profile, because it resolves a real clinical paradox that
maps directly onto a Boone-style character (Boone himself, in Fallout: New Vegas, carries a canon
head-injury-adjacent trauma). Key terms and findings, defined precisely:
- **Posttraumatic amnesia (PTA):** the period after a head injury during which the patient cannot store new
  memories — clinically defined as lasting from injury onset until continuous personal memory resumes.
  0-60 minutes = mild TBI; 1-24 hours = moderate; more than 24 hours = severe, by the Russell & Smith (1961)
  classification still cited as standard.
- **Retrograde amnesia (RA):** the separate, usually briefer, period *before* the injury that is not
  remembered, which recovers with "islands of memory" — brief fragments of intact encoding — gradually
  converging, most distant memories returning first.
- **"Islands of memory":** documented in roughly one-third of mild/moderate TBI cases — brief windows of
  apparently normal encoding *inside* an otherwise amnesic period. These are explicitly proposed by multiple
  researchers (King 1997, McMillan 1996) as the mechanism by which PTSD can develop even from an event a
  person is largely amnesic for: a few seconds of real, intensely fear-laden encoding is enough to seed
  flashbacks and hyperarousal, even though the surrounding minutes or hours are a genuine blank.
- **Coexistence rate:** despite decades of argument that amnesia for the trauma should preclude a PTSD
  diagnosis (no memory = no reexperiencing = no diagnosis, the reasoning goes), the majority of well-designed
  studies found PTSD rates of 20-40% in TBI populations — comparable to non-TBI trauma populations — including
  27% in *moderate and severe* TBI in one study (Bryant, Marosszeky, Crooks, & Gurka, 2000), directly
  contradicting the older assumption that PTSD is more likely with milder, not severer, TBI.
- **Why they coexist — dual-representation theory extended:** Brewin's VAM/SAM model (see Finding 11) is
  explicitly extended here: a period of impaired consciousness (PTA, or even loss of consciousness) can block
  formation of the *verbally accessible* memory (VAM) — the narratable, contextualized, "I remember what
  happened" record — while still permitting fragments to be laid down in the *situationally accessible*
  memory (SAM) system, the lower-level, image/sensation-based system that cannot be deliberately recalled but
  can be triggered automatically by matching cues. This means a person can have **a real, total, honest gap
  in his conscious narrative of an event and still flinch, still flash back to fragments, still carry
  measurable arousal symptoms tied to that event** — because the SAM-level fragments were encoded even though
  the VAM-level story never was.

**Candidate application:** This is the strongest single mechanism available for writing his chronic injury as
something more specific than "he has old wounds." It supports a very concrete, playable shape: **a defining
combat/training incident — quite possibly the very one that earned him his place in Cancer — that he cannot
fully narrate.** Not full amnesia (that would remove the dramatic weight); a real, clinically precise
partial gap: minutes he genuinely cannot account for, bracketed by intact memory before and after, with
occasional "islands" — a smell, a color, a specific sound — that surface as involuntary, disproportionate
reactions with no attached story he can consciously supply. This gives a Boone-style tension a genuinely
new axis beyond "public judgment vs. private redemption": the player, and possibly the character himself,
may never get the missing minutes of the story that supposedly proved his loyalty — he knows what he did in
outline, is certain of it, but the sensory truth of it is locked in a memory system he cannot deliberately
open. That is dramatically load-bearing (it can never be "solved," only lived with — consistent with this
project's "no clean resolutions" discipline) and it is not invented; it is the real, named clinical picture
for exactly this kind of gap.

### 6. Twin studies — some of this may be pre-existing, not acquired, which changes what "chronic injury" means

Ch. 8, citing Gilbertson et al. (unpublished twin data reported in this chapter) and the parallel hippocampal-
volume twin study (Gilbertson et al., 2002, cited across Ch. 3, Ch. 8, Ch. 13). Using monozygotic twin pairs
discordant for combat exposure (one twin deployed to Vietnam, the other not), researchers found: (1) smaller
hippocampal volume in the *unexposed* co-twins of veterans with PTSD, compared to unexposed co-twins of
veterans who deployed but never developed PTSD — meaning smaller hippocampal volume looks like a
**pre-existing vulnerability factor**, not purely a consequence of trauma; and (2) the combat-*unexposed*
identical brothers of PTSD veterans performed nearly as poorly on IQ, verbal memory, and executive-function
tasks as their PTSD-diagnosed twin — despite never having been to war themselves — while performing worse
than the unexposed twins of veterans who deployed and did *not* develop PTSD. The chapter's own conclusion:
this "represents a familial predisposition factor," not solely damage.

**Candidate application:** This complicates and enriches the "chronic injury" framing usefully. It means his
profile does not have to be read as pure damage from a single event — some of what produces his Nerve/
Investigation profile, and some of whatever cognitive cost accompanies it, could plausibly have been latent
in him (and perhaps in family he has, if any get designed later) before the war ever happened, with the war
as the event that *revealed* rather than *created* it. That is a genuinely different, less deterministic
shape for a backstory than "the war broke him" — it opens "the war found something in him that was already
there" as an equally well-grounded alternative, worth holding open rather than foreclosing.

## PART II — Broader findings, for voice, behavior, and narrative texture

### 7. Attentional bias to threat is automatic and resists deliberate override — already summarized in Finding 3, restated here for its narrative implication

Ch. 5 (Constans). The emotional Stroop effect (slower to name the ink color of trauma-relevant words than
neutral ones) is PTSD's single most robustly replicated cognitive-bias finding, and multiple studies
established it is **not under conscious strategic control**: Constans et al. (2004) found combat veterans
offered a cash bonus for fast, accurate performance still could not suppress the slowdown specifically on
combat words. A separate study using trained actors coached to fake PTSD found the actors' self-report and
interview responses were indistinguishable from genuine PTSD patients, but **only genuine patients showed the
attentional-bias slowdown** — meaning this specific effect cannot currently be faked and cannot currently be
willed away. Interestingly, in the same researcher's follow-up study, *anticipating* an additional stressor
(being told a combat video would follow) **suppressed** the attentional bias rather than worsening it —
suggested explanation: attention narrows under acute additional threat to only the immediately relevant
channel, at the cost of everything else.

**Candidate application:** Good grounding for a specific, playable behavioral tell: certain words/images/
subjects visibly cost him a half-beat before he can respond to something else, in a way he cannot talk himself
out of and cannot always hide, regardless of how much he wants to project control in front of people who
already doubt him. And the "anticipatory threat narrows and paradoxically calms" finding is a genuinely
counterintuitive, real, and usable detail — he may read as *more* focused, not less, in the seconds right
before something he knows is about to be actively dangerous, and *less* composed in the boring, safe
in-between moments where his attention has nowhere useful to go.

### 8. Judgment bias — trauma survivors overestimate risk and cost of unrelated future negative events, and interpret ambiguity as threat, but only after conscious elaboration (not instantly)

Ch. 5. Two real findings: individuals with acute stress disorder or high PTSD symptoms significantly
overestimated both the probability and cost of unrelated future negative events (e.g., getting a flat tire),
compared to similarly trauma-exposed people with fewer symptoms — a general elevation of perceived risk, not
limited to trauma-relevant content. Separately, interpretive-bias studies found PTSD participants resolved
ambiguous sentences toward the threatening meaning, but — importantly — only in a delayed-processing
condition (850ms), not an immediate one (100ms), suggesting **this bias is not reflexive/preattentive but
emerges during slower, more elaborative, more consciously accessible reasoning** — the opposite temporal
profile from the attentional bias in Finding 7, which resists conscious control precisely because it's fast.

**Candidate application:** Two distinct voice registers, both real: a fast, pre-conscious threat-scanning
layer (Finding 3/4/7) he cannot suppress, and a slower, more deliberate pessimism-about-the-future layer that
shows up more in what he says and predicts than in his reflexes — worth keeping distinct in dialogue writing
rather than collapsing into one generic "guarded" trait.

### 9. Explicit memory bias toward trauma-relevant material, absence of implicit/perceptual bias, and overgeneral autobiographical memory

Ch. 5. Three real, distinct findings, worth keeping separate: (1) PTSD is associated with better-than-control
recall of trauma-relevant word material in free-recall tasks (an *explicit* memory bias) — and Holocaust
survivors with PTSD specifically showed *enhanced* paired-associate learning for Holocaust-relevant word
pairs compared to neutral pairs, suggesting PTSD facilitates associative learning of trauma-adjacent
information specifically. (2) By contrast, most well-designed studies found **no** implicit/perceptual memory
bias — PTSD does not make people faster at unconsciously recognizing trauma-related word fragments they
weren't told to remember, which argues the enhancement in (1) is a *conceptual/elaborative* effect, not a raw
perceptual one. (3) **Overgeneral autobiographical memory:** when cued with a word ("candle") and asked for a
specific personal memory, PTSD (and depressed) participants tend to answer with a category ("birthday
parties") rather than one dated episode ("my ninth birthday party at the lake house") — and this
overgeneralization effect was, across several studies, actually stronger for *positive* cue words than
negative ones.

**Candidate application:** (1) and (3) together suggest a real, specific speech pattern: sharper, more
specific, more intrusively detailed recall when a conversation edges toward anything trauma-adjacent, paired
with a genuine, documented difficulty producing *specific* happy memories on request — he may struggle more
to answer "tell me about a good day" with one dated real day than he struggles to (unwillingly) recall a
specific bad one. That is a counterintuitive, textured, real detail for a companion built around contained
composure — his memory is not uniformly blunted, it is warped in a specific, asymmetric direction.

### 10. Dual-representation theory (VAM/SAM) — the mechanism behind flashbacks, and a real vocabulary for describing them precisely

Ch. 6 (Brewin), extended in Ch. 10 (Finding 5) and Ch. 12. Two memory systems, laid out with real technical
names, useful verbatim: **verbally accessible memory (VAM)** — narrative, contextualized (past/present/future
located), deliberately retrievable, communicable in words, but *limited* to what received full conscious
attention during the event, because acute stress narrows attention to the immediate source of threat.
**Situationally accessible memory (SAM)** — image/sensation-based, records lower-level perceptual detail that
was present but not consciously attended (peripheral sounds, smells, sensations), cannot be deliberately
retrieved, but is triggered automatically by matching cues, has no sense of "this happened in the past" (it
is reexperienced as present), and is unaffected by "islands of memory" — it can encode detail *inside* a PTA
gap where VAM cannot. Flashbacks = involuntary SAM activation. The theory's clinical implication, directly
stated: exposure-based trauma therapy works by building a *new*, highly detailed, distinctive VAM memory
that competes with and gains retrieval priority over the old SAM-based trace — the original fear memory is
never edited or erased, only out-competed, which is why relapse under sufficiently strong reminders remains
possible even after successful treatment. "Hotspots" (Grey, Holmes, & Brewin, 2001) — the specific, often
very brief moments of most intense emotion inside a longer traumatic memory — are proposed to be exactly the
moments of maximal VAM/SAM divergence, and are disproportionately represented in flashback content relative
to their share of the original event's duration.

**Candidate application:** Real, precise, usable vocabulary for both the character's inner mechanics and for
designing his personal questline's structure. His "one wartime action that earned his place in Cancer" could
have a small number of real hotspots — seconds-long fragments — that recur disproportionately, distinct from
the fuller VAM narrative he can and does tell people (which is necessarily the trimmed, attended, sayable
version). This also gives a mechanically real reason a "Good" ending cannot fully resolve his condition
(consistent with this project's no-costless-good-endings law): even full narrative closure — finally getting
the whole VAM story out, even to someone who believes him — does not erase the SAM-level trace; it only
builds a competing, hopefully stronger, memory. Relapse under a bad-enough trigger stays permanently possible
by the theory's own honest terms, which is dramatically useful, not a design failure.

### 11. Verbal/nonverbal processing separation, and "fast vs. slow" memory systems — grounding for a laconic, physically-oriented voice

Ch. 12 (Brewin). Two converging strands. First: several studies found PTSD patients show relative deficits on
*verbal* memory/fluency tasks specifically, while visuospatial performance is largely spared or even relatively
advantaged, alongside modest evidence of reduced left-hemisphere / relative right-hemisphere activation. In
normal (non-PTSD) participants, inducing an aversive/withdrawal state via an unpleasant film *improves*
spatial working-memory performance while *impairing* verbal working-memory performance on a matched task
(Gray, 2001) — suggesting this split is a general feature of aversive/defensive states, not unique to
diagnosed PTSD. Wild et al. found **verbal memory impairment specifically — independent of overall
intelligence — was the single strongest predictor of poorer treatment outcome** in a PTSD treatment sample.
Second: McClelland, McNaughton, & O'Reilly's (1995) "complementary learning systems" model, applied to trauma
by Brewin — the hippocampus is a fast learning system that can register new, contradictory information
immediately (at risk of "catastrophic interference" with existing beliefs if not integrated carefully); the
neocortex is a slow system that gradually, safely extracts stable rules across many repetitions ("interleaved
learning"). Trauma is by nature the kind of hippocampal "penguin" (a bird that violates "birds fly") that
either gets denied ("overassimilation" — pretending it didn't happen/doesn't matter) or overgeneralized
("overaccommodation" — concluding nothing is safe anymore) if the slow, repeated, careful comparison against
prior belief never gets to happen.

**Candidate application:** Real support for writing him as someone whose competence and composure read
primarily through *action and spatial/physical judgment* rather than verbal fluency — a plausible, grounded
reason for a laconic voice that isn't just a genre convention: verbal working memory is a documented softer
spot than visuospatial/action competence in this literature, and it is specifically verbal memory, not
general intelligence, that predicts whether someone in his position is likely to keep struggling versus
settle. The overassimilation/overaccommodation pair is also a clean, real vocabulary for describing two
possible *failure modes* of his coping, useful for branching dialogue or companion-approval writing: does he
minimize what Cancer actually cost him and what he actually did ("it wasn't that big a deal"), or does he
over-conclude from it (deciding no one anywhere can ever really trust him, full stop)? Real clinical language
exists for exactly this fork.

### 12. Exposure therapy vs. cognitive therapy — two real, distinct treatment logics, useful for how any in-world attempt to help him would actually have to work

Ch. 12. Two validated but mechanistically different treatments exist for real PTSD, and the chapter is
explicit that they target different things: **prolonged exposure** (repeatedly, vividly relive the trauma
narrative aloud, plus real-world exposure to trauma-linked places/objects) targets the fear-conditioned,
largely automatic/associative response — extinguishing conditioned fear the way animal-learning extinction
works, by building new competing associations, not by erasing old ones. **Cognitive therapy** (identify and
examine the explicit and implicit *beliefs and interpretations* formed about the event — guilt, shame, "it
was my fault," "I can never be trusted again") targets the slower, rule-based, higher-order appraisal system,
and requires, in Aaron Beck's own words (quoted in the text), "a collaborative enterprise designed to uncover
mental patterns, assumptions, and secrets, not a tool for browbeating patients." In practice the two are
usually blended.

**Candidate application:** If a future questline or companion-relationship arc involves anyone (a Cancer
counselor, a fellow defector, the player) trying to actually help him process what happened, this is a real
model for what that would credibly look like in scenes — not one big cathartic confession scene that fixes
everything, but two distinct, separately-earned kinds of progress: getting him to actually *tell* the
narrative in detail (exposure-shaped), and separately, getting him to examine and maybe revise a belief he's
been carrying about what it means about him (cognitive-shaped). They are not the same conversation and real
clinical practice treats them as complementary, not interchangeable.

## PART III — Compressed chapters (read in full, condensed here by design)

### 13. Chapter 7 (early trauma exposure and the brain) — condensed

Covers maltreated children/adolescents, not adults, so it is lower-priority for this character, but three
findings are worth preserving because they establish structural principles reused elsewhere in the book:
(a) catecholamine and HPA dysregulation from chronic early stress is associated with reduced total brain,
corpus callosum, and prefrontal volumes, with effects worse the earlier the onset and the longer the
duration — dose-and-timing-dependent, not a flat effect; (b) **gender-dependent severity**: in every study
that checked, maltreated boys showed more pronounced structural differences (smaller corpus callosum,
larger ventricular volume) than maltreated girls with matched PTSD; (c) enlarged superior temporal gyrus
(STG) grey matter — the brain region tied to social-cue/"theory of mind" processing — was found in maltreated
children with PTSD, proposed as either a trauma-driven compensatory increase in sensitivity to social/vocal
threat cues, or (in a separate finding on non-traumatized anxious children) a pre-existing anatomical
vulnerability that predates any trauma. **Cross-series flag:** the dose/timing-dependent severity principle
and the possibility of a purely developmental (non-traumatic) origin for some of these markers could both be
useful if Outer Tepenia or Cryptograph Helix material ever needs a scientifically-grounded account of how
chronic stress differently marks child vs. adult exile populations, or how a trait could plausibly be
inherited/developmental rather than acquired.

### 14. Chapter 9 (learning and memory in aging trauma survivors) — condensed

Compares Holocaust survivors (now elderly) and aging combat veterans on the California Verbal Learning Test.
Key structural finding, reused nowhere else in this file: **the *pattern* of memory impairment in PTSD
appears to shift with age** — younger combat veterans (40s) showed deficits mainly in delayed recall
specifically, while elderly trauma survivors showed a broader deficit in initial learning/acquisition rate
without accelerated forgetting once material was learned — different memory subsystems affected at different
life stages, not a uniform "PTSD worsens with age" story. Also notable: elderly trauma survivors with PTSD
showed a *flattened circadian cortisol rhythm* and *lower* absolute cortisol than younger PTSD veterans —
opposite direction from normal healthy aging, which trends toward elevated cortisol — meaning trauma's
neuroendocrine signature does not simply track or amplify ordinary aging; it runs its own distinct course.
**Not directly relevant to the mid-30s/40s companion**, but worth flagging for any future elder-defector or
long-lived-exile character work: real precedent exists for "his condition will present differently, not just
'worse,' twenty and forty years from now."

### 15. Chapter 11 (clinical neuropsychological evaluation) — condensed

Practical/diagnostic, not mechanistic — mainly useful as a checklist of what real clinicians look for. Two
points worth keeping: real PTSD-associated cognitive deficits, when present, are consistently described as
**mild** — mean scores differ from controls but usually stay within a normal range — so "impaired" should
not be written as dramatically disabling; and self-reported cognitive complaints and objective test
performance **diverge** in PTSD patients (people commonly over- or under-estimate their own impairment
relative to what testing shows) — a small, real, usable detail: he might describe himself as sharper or
duller than he actually tests, and either direction is documented as normal for the condition, not a
tell of dishonesty.

### 16. Chapter 13 (pharmacological approaches) — condensed

A medication-by-medication reference table (adrenergic agents, HPA-targeting agents, glutamate/GABA
anticonvulsants, SSRIs, atypical antipsychotics) whose mechanistic content — NE/alpha-receptor dynamics,
HPA/cortisol dysregulation, amygdala/PFC imbalance — is already covered at narrative depth in Findings 1-2
and Ch. 2/3/4 material above. Two points not duplicated elsewhere: (1) SSRIs (specifically paroxetine) were
shown in one controlled study to produce a measurable **4.6% increase in hippocampal volume** after 9-12
months of treatment, alongside real improvement in verbal declarative memory — one of the only documented
cases in this literature of a *reversal*, not just a management, of a structural PTSD marker; (2) the
serotonergic agonist mCPP and the alpha2 antagonist yohimbine, in separate pharmacological-challenge studies,
were each able to **provoke actual dissociative flashbacks** in PTSD patients (not just anxiety) but not in
healthy controls — direct pharmacological evidence that the flashback mechanism is a real, triggerable
physiological state, not purely psychological. **No in-world application intended** (Tepenia is not expected
to have or need real-world psychiatric medication names) — flagged only because the hippocampal-volume
reversibility finding is a genuinely hopeful data point worth remembering if this project ever wants a
scientifically honest account of partial healing that stops short of "cured."

## PART IV — Cross-series flag: speculative robot-cognition analogy (explicitly separate from all human-clinical material above)

**Everything in this section is speculative and clearly marked as such — it is not proposed as literal robot
biology, only as an interesting structural analogy a robot-consciousness storyline could choose to use, per
the developer's standing instruction to flag anything that "even possibly can be applied in any form."**

The book's central architecture — a fast, automatic, associative subsystem (amygdala/SAM memory/attentional
capture) that cannot be consciously overridden even under direct incentive, running alongside a slower,
effortful, rule-based subsystem (PFC/VAM memory/cognitive appraisal) that *can* be deliberately directed but
is the first thing to degrade under load — is a genuinely interesting shape for **how a damaged or traumatized
synthetic mind might malfunction**, independent of whether any of the specific neurochemistry applies. A
robot built around Inner Tepenia's own consciousness questions could plausibly have an analogous two-tier
architecture: a hard-wired, fast threat/anomaly-detection layer that persists and even sharpens under damage
(mirroring Findings 1, 3, 4, 7 — automatic threat-scanning that *cannot* be turned off, even when the unit
"wants" to, even when a supervisor orders it to prioritize something else) alongside a slower, effortful,
deliberate reasoning/narrative-construction layer that is the first capability to visibly degrade under
sustained stress or damage (mirroring Findings 1, 3, 6, 11 — executive/working-memory collapse, verbal/
narrative memory as the most fragile capability, PFC "going offline" under overload). The dual-representation
memory model (Finding 10) is an especially clean fit for a damaged AI: a robot could plausibly have a
"verbally accessible" log — the story it can tell, coherent, retrievable, edited by what it was attending to
— sitting alongside a "situationally accessible" raw-sensor trace that it cannot deliberately query but that
can be involuntarily triggered by a matching input pattern, producing something that reads, to an observer,
exactly like a flashback: the machine reliving a past event as though it were happening now, complete with
behavior driven by a stimulus nobody else in the room can see. The DHEA/NPY "documented, finite, physiological
resilience reserve that can be measurably depleted and needs real rest to recover" framing (Finding 2) is
similarly a clean, non-mystical model for how a robot's composure/resilience stat could be written as a real,
finite resource rather than an infinite trait — useful if Ice-Cold Buddhism, robot elemental design, or any
future robot-trauma companion wants a grounded answer to "can a synthetic mind actually run out of nerve, and
what does that look like from outside." None of this is proposed as canon; it is flagged as available if
wanted.
