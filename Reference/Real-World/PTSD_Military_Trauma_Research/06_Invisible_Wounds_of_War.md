# Invisible Wounds of War — Real Policy/Consequences Source Material

Source: *Invisible Wounds of War: Psychological and Cognitive Injuries, Their Consequences, and Services to
Assist Recovery* (RAND Corporation, 2008; ed. Terri Tanielian and Lisa H. Jaycox) — the landmark RAND
monograph on PTSD, major depression, and traumatic brain injury (TBI) among U.S. OEF/OIF veterans. 499 pages;
plaintext at `/tmp/claude-1000/.../PTSD_fulltext/Invisible_Wounds_of_War_...txt` (21,475 lines). This book's
distinguishing strength among the PTSD-research set is **systemic/social consequences**, not clinical
diagnosis — what combat trauma does to a veteran's career, marriage, children, housing, and public standing
once he's home, and the documented, real-world **gap between what care systems claim to offer and what
veterans actually experience.**

**Read in full:** Ch.1 Introduction (pp.3-15, "Signature Wounds" through Scope); Ch.5 "Predicting the
Immediate and Long-Term Consequences..." (pp.119-153, the entire consequences chapter — co-morbidity,
suicide, physical health, substance abuse, employment, homelessness, marriage, parenting/children); Ch.7
"Systems of Care" (pp.245-283, access-to-care and the full stigma/military-culture subsection, plus the TBI
cultural-barriers subsection and the raw focus-group transcript excerpts); Ch.8 "Conclusions and
Recommendations" (pp.431-453, the entire chapter).

**Explicitly compressed, per this project's standing allowance for low-yield US-specific chapters:** Ch.2 (The
Wars in Afghanistan and Iraq — force composition, OEF/OIF operational history: real-world-specific troop
numbers and 2000s deployment-rotation policy with no in-world equivalent); Ch.3 and Ch.4 (epidemiological
methodology and RAND's own telephone-survey design/statistics — screening-instrument comparisons and
sampling methodology, not narrative material); Ch.6 (the cost microsimulation model — U.S. federal healthcare
financing, TRICARE/VA budget mechanics, and dollar-figure modeling assumptions). Where these chapters
contained a genuinely portable fact or figure, it's noted in Finding 16 below rather than silently dropped.
Ch.7's Appendix 7.C (evidence-based clinical treatment protocols — CBT/EMDR/SSRI comparison tables) and
Appendices 7.D-7.E (U.S. state-by-state resource directories) were skimmed only; they are pure clinical/
administrative reference material with no narrative use.

## 1. "Signature wounds" / "invisible wounds" — the book's own core framing, directly reusable as terminology

The book's title concept is precise and quotable: PTSD, depression, and TBI are called **"invisible
wounds"** specifically because, unlike physical wounds that "maim or disfigure," they "remain invisible to
other servicemembers, family members, the military, and the broader society" — this invisibility is treated
as the central mechanism by which they go unrecognized, unacknowledged, and untreated. The related term
**"signature wounds"** describes conditions that are disproportionately characteristic of a specific
conflict's specific methods of injury (here: IEDs and blast exposure making TBI the signature wound of
OEF/OIF, the way "shell shock" was shorthand for WWI and "battle fatigue" for WWII/Korea — the book notes each
war has produced its own era-specific vocabulary for the same underlying psychiatric-casualty phenomenon
across all of recorded warfare).

**Candidate application:** "Invisible wounds" is a ready-made in-world phrase for how the Cancer defector's
chronic wartime injuries could be discussed by other characters — visible physical injury earns sympathy or
at least acknowledgment; whatever he actually carries from the Long Night War may be the kind that isn't
visible on him, making it easier for the rest of Concordia to see only "what he used to be" and nothing of
what the war actually did *to* him. Also useful as the specific naming convention for whatever Concordia's
own conflict-specific term for the Long Night War's characteristic psychiatric/cognitive toll might be — every
war in the book's own account gets its own period vocabulary for this.

## 2. The consequences cascade model — a structural framework, not just a finding

Chapter 5 builds an explicit integrated model (their Figure 5.1) combining two real psychological frameworks:

- **Stress-diathesis model** (Zubin and Spring, 1977): a condition (PTSD/depression/TBI) functions as a
  *diathesis* — a vulnerability, not a determinant. Negative outcomes only manifest when that vulnerability
  meets subsequent stress; a servicemember with the same condition and strong resources/support may show few
  visible consequences, while one with compounding vulnerabilities (poverty, isolation, prior instability)
  cascades hard. The model's own load-bearing implication: **"sufficient resources can act as a buffer... signs
  of resilience are not evidence the underlying condition isn't real."**
- **Life-span developmental perspective**, with two named mechanisms for how a condition metastasizes into
  a whole ruined life rather than staying contained:
  - **Interactional continuity** — enduring qualities of the afflicted person shape how others respond to
    them, and others "generally respond in kind": an aggressive person provokes aggressive responses, a
    withdrawn person gets increasingly isolated, in a self-reinforcing loop that has nothing to do with
    what actually happened to cause the condition.
  - **Cumulative continuity** — choices and behaviors at each life stage constrain the *options* available at
    the next stage; a bad decision made while symptomatic doesn't just cost the moment, it forecloses future
    paths (lost employment forecloses future employability; a period of erratic behavior forecloses future
    trust).

The combined model is explicitly described with the book's own metaphor: **"The effects of a post-combat
mental health condition can be compared to ripples spreading outward on a pond. But whereas ripples diminish
over time, the consequences of mental health and cognitive conditions may grow more severe, especially if left
untreated."**

**Candidate application:** This is a ready-made shape for the Cancer defector's *entire* backstory arc, not
just a flavor detail — "interactional continuity" is a precise mechanical description of exactly the dynamic
his README already gestures at (public judgment responding to what he "used to be" rather than what he did,
regardless of the facts, because the *reputation* itself now generates the very hostility that confirms it).
"Cumulative continuity" gives a concrete reason his path in Cancer capped where it did: whatever choices he
made in the years immediately after defecting — while symptomatic, unsupported, distrusted by everyone —
may have foreclosed doors that a "clean" post-war record would have kept open, which is a harder, more
specific answer to "why isn't he more esteemed even in Cancer" than simply "the ceiling is low for defectors."

## 3. Co-morbidity — conditions rarely arrive alone, and worsen each other

Real epidemiological finding, general population: **88 percent of men and 79 percent of women with PTSD
experience at least one other disorder in their lifetime; about half have three or more co-morbid
diagnoses.** Individuals with PTSD average 2.7 other diagnoses, and the co-morbidity count rises with PTSD
severity. TBI and depression frequently co-occur (15-61% prevalence range depending on study) and are hard to
disentangle diagnostically because their symptoms overlap substantially. TBI and PTSD co-occurrence is
real but contested in the literature precisely because TBI can cause amnesia of the traumatic event while
PTSD requires re-experiencing it — the book notes both can still occur together via "subconscious/implicit"
processing or social reconstruction of the memory from others' accounts.

**Candidate application:** Directly supports the README's note that the Cancer defector "likely carries one
or two chronic injuries from warfare and military training" — real-world data says these rarely arrive
in isolation, and having more than one compounds rather than simply adds. If his chronic injuries are
partly physical/neurological (a TBI-equivalent) and partly psychological, the overlap and mutual worsening is
itself textually accurate rather than a contrivance.

## 4. Suicide risk — the strongest single predictor found is combat-related *guilt*, not any PTSD symptom cluster

Male veterans face roughly **twice** the suicide risk of civilian counterparts; Vietnam-era veterans showed
the sharpest elevation in the first five years post-discharge. Across PTSD/depression/TBI, all three
independently elevate suicide risk, and risk compounds with substance-use co-morbidity (9-14x higher
standardized mortality ratio). The single most pointed finding: in a dedicated study of Vietnam veterans with
PTSD, **it was not any particular PTSD symptom or symptom cluster that best predicted suicidal behavior — it
was combat-related guilt specifically** (Hendin and Haas, 1991).

**Candidate application:** This is a precise, real-world-grounded answer to what could sit underneath the
Cancer defector's psychology if his defining wound is moral rather than purely traumatic — consistent with
his confirmed 1w2 Social Enneagram (a principled reformer whose defection is a moral rebellion against an
unjust war). Guilt as the load-bearing mechanism (rather than fear, re-experiencing, or hyperarousal) fits a
character whose core conflict is explicitly about what he did and how it's perceived, not simply about having
witnessed violence.

## 5. Physical health and mortality — psychological wounds become physical ones

PTSD and depression carry **elevated cardiovascular mortality** (nearly 2x risk of fatal/nonfatal coronary
heart disease for depression; increasing PTSD symptom severity independently predicts heart attack and
fatal coronary disease). PTSD sufferers report significantly more somatic complaints — stomach pain, back
pain, headaches, chest pain, dizziness, racing heart, GI symptoms, even pain during intercourse — that persist
even after controlling for actual combat injury. **Health-compromising behavior chains** are well documented:
PTSD increases smoking frequency and makes quitting harder; depression and PTSD both increase sexual-risk-
taking; both correlate with obesity. TBI mortality is dose-dependent on severity: mild TBI carried an
11.6x higher likelihood of "death" as discharge reason (versus non-TBI discharges); severe TBI carried
roughly **150x** higher likelihood.

**Candidate application:** Useful texture for the defector's physicality — the README flags "a body that
carries one or two old chronic injuries" as still TBD; this gives a real basis for making that manifest as
ongoing physical health strain (a bad heart, chronic pain, self-medicating habits) rather than only a single
localized old wound, if the developer wants the injury to read as pervasive rather than a single scar.

## 6. Substance abuse — the "self-medication hypothesis," and its specific mechanism

Up to **75 percent** of Vietnam combat veterans with lifetime PTSD met criteria for substance abuse or
dependence. The literature strongly supports the **self-medication hypothesis**: PTSD drives substance use
(not the reverse) because individuals use substances to relieve distressing symptoms — Vietnam veterans
specifically reported that alcohol, heroin, benzodiazepines, and marijuana "helped" their PTSD symptoms,
while cocaine specifically *worsened* hyperarousal. (Depression's relationship to substance use runs the
opposite direction in the literature — substance use is more often a *cause* of depressive symptoms than a
response to them, a genuine asymmetry between the two conditions worth preserving if both appear in one
character.) Nicotine specifically: heavy smoking correlates with hyperarousal/avoidance symptom severity, and
PTSD sufferers have documented harder time quitting.

**Candidate application:** Project canon already has Glitch-Coolant as the robot alcohol-equivalent
(`project_glitch_coolant`); this is real-world grounding for a *human* self-medication behavior pattern that
could season the Cancer defector's coping mechanisms without inventing anything — the self-medication framing
(using a substance specifically because it blunts a specific symptom, not just "he drinks") is more
characterful and more textually accurate than generic "troubled veteran drinks" shorthand.

## 7. Labor-market outcomes — the concrete, provable financial cost of the condition

Veterans with probable PTSD were **over three times** more likely to report not currently working, even
controlling for demographics and co-morbidities. PTSD correlated with **16 percent lower hourly wages**;
depression correlated with **45 percent lower hourly wages** (worse still with co-morbid substance use).
Beyond simple unemployment, the book distinguishes **absenteeism** (missed workdays — PTSD sufferers were
significantly more likely to miss 2+ workdays/month) from **presenteeism** (reduced productivity while
still nominally at work — depressed workers lost an average 4.6 hours/week of functional productivity versus
an expected 1.5). Educational attainment is also directly damaged: those with a mental disorder before
finishing schooling were consistently more likely to terminate education early, with cascading effects on
lifetime earning potential.

**Candidate application:** Gives Cancer's own economy something concrete to withhold from or grant to the
defector — real-world data supports a character whose economic standing in Cancer plausibly plateaus below
what his combat/survival skill would predict purely on merit, for reasons rooted in the condition itself, not
only in social prejudice. Useful nuance: this is a *second*, independent mechanism (beyond stigma/prejudice)
by which his standing stays capped — the two reinforce each other rather than either one alone fully
explaining it.

## 8. Homelessness — psychiatric symptoms and substance use outweigh combat exposure itself as predictors

Real finding, repeatedly confirmed across studies: **psychiatric symptoms and substance use were stronger
predictors of veteran homelessness than combat exposure or any other military factor** — meaning it is
specifically the *untreated aftermath*, not the war itself, that produces homelessness risk. About 75 percent
of homeless individuals with PTSD in one study had developed the diagnosis *before* becoming homeless,
supporting a causal (not merely correlated) direction. Structural/personal-limitation factors interact:
low-cost housing scarcity and reduced social-safety resources compound individual vulnerability rather than
homelessness being purely a "personal failing."

**Candidate application:** Directly relevant if the developer ever wants to texture *other*, less-esteemed
Upper Earth defectors elsewhere in Concordia (the exemplar NPC framework flagged as still-undesigned in the
defector README) — this gives a real mechanism for why some defectors end up destitute/homeless in the city
regardless of what they did during the war, distinct from and compounding the social contempt problem. Worth
flagging for the still-open "actual named exemplar defector NPCs" TODO item.

## 9. Marriage and intimate relationships — the "secondary traumatization" concept, and the earthquake metaphor

**Secondary traumatization**: the documented phenomenon in which intimate partners of a traumatized person
develop *their own* trauma symptoms (nightmares, intrusive thoughts, anxiety, dysphoria) purely from the
burden of proximity and caregiving — first named studying wives of Vietnam veterans with PTSD, later
generalized to any distress experienced by those closest to a traumatized individual. Real, specific finding:
in a study of Dutch peacekeepers, PTSD symptom severity predicted psychological symptoms in *wives* but had
**no measurable relationship to symptoms in parents** — the burden concentrates specifically on the most
intimate relationship, not family generally. **Intimate-partner violence** correlates strongly with PTSD
independent of other factors — a Vietnam-veteran study found roughly half of couples seeking treatment
together reported physical aggression in the household; depression severity showed a similarly strong,
almost linear relationship (each 20% increase in depressive symptoms associated with a 74% increase in
likelihood of physical aggression toward a partner). Two real **moderators** blunt or worsen these effects:
pre-existing vulnerability (less education, weaker extended family, prior adjustment problems predict worse
outcomes) and — the single most quotable structural finding in this section — **marriage quality before the
trauma**, described in the book's own words as analogous to **how buildings respond to earthquakes: the
stronger the structure initially, the greater its ability to weather a shock.**

**Candidate application:** The "earthquake" framing is a strong, precise metaphor worth preserving nearly
verbatim if the defector's personal questline ever explores a past relationship damaged (or barely surviving)
his defection and its aftermath — it gives a non-cliché way to write why *some* relationships in his past
might have survived contact with what he became and others didn't, tied to the relationship's own
pre-existing strength rather than to how "bad" his condition was. Also directly usable for the "Relationships
& Hooks" section flagged TBD in his README.

## 10. Parenting and child outcomes — emotional numbing specifically, not hyperarousal, damages fatherhood

Veterans with PTSD reported significantly more problems and less satisfaction with parenting. The mechanism
is specific and worth preserving precisely: a controlled study isolated *which* PTSD symptom cluster actually
predicts poor parent-child relationships, and found it was **emotional numbing and avoidance specifically —
not re-experiencing, not hyperarousal** — that damaged fatherhood, even after controlling for substance abuse
and combat-exposure severity. The book's own quoted explanation: *"the disinterest, detachment, and emotional
unavailability that characterize emotional numbing may diminish a father's ability and willingness to seek
out, engage in, and enjoy interactions with his children."* Children of PTSD-affected veterans showed
significantly higher rates of academic problems and psychiatric treatment than children of non-affected
veterans in a controlled comparison. The effects are explicitly cross-generational — the book's closing line
on this section: **"It may take decades to count the costs of these afflictions, and decades more to heal
from them."**

**Candidate application:** If the developer ever wants a child, former ward, or younger relative connected to
the Cancer defector's pre-defection or immediate post-defection life (not currently in his README, but a
plausible "Relationships & Hooks" candidate), this gives a textually precise, non-generic mechanism for how
that relationship could have specifically been damaged — emotional withdrawal rather than outward hostility
or violence, consistent with a controlled, 1w2 Type One character whose surface stays "sternly-expressed"
rather than volatile.

## 11. Stigma — the three-part real taxonomy (societal / individual / institutional)

The book supplies a precise, citable academic definition worth preserving exactly: stigma is **"a negative
and erroneous attitude about a person, a prejudice, or negative stereotype."** It then splits into three
distinct, independently-operating types, all of which need separate remedies:

1. **Societal/public stigma** — the general public's misperceptions and reactions (e.g., the belief that
   people with mental health conditions are more likely to be violent — Concordia's own version of this could
   be the belief that any ex-military Upper-Earther, regardless of individual record, is inherently dangerous
   or untrustworthy). Real, quotable finding: people report being less willing to socialize with someone
   labeled "depressed" than someone described merely as "troubled" — **the label itself, not the underlying
   condition, drives the social reaction.**
2. **Individual/internalized stigma** — occurs when a person absorbs the public's negative view of *people
   like them* into their own self-image, directly reducing self-worth, confidence in future prospects, and
   (critically) willingness to seek help or even acknowledge the problem to themselves.
3. **Institutional/structural stigma** — policies that restrict opportunity for a stigmatized group, whether
   deliberately (formal exclusion) or as an unintended side effect of otherwise-neutral rules (e.g.,
   less-generous benefits, under-resourced support relative to other groups).

**Candidate application:** This is the single most directly usable piece of material in the whole book for the
Cancer defector. It gives precise vocabulary to separate what's currently one undifferentiated idea in his
README ("contempt... regardless of what he actually did") into three distinct, independently tunable social
mechanics: (a) the *societal* layer — ordinary Concordia citizens reacting to the category "Upper Earth
ex-military" without knowing his specifics; (b) an *individual* layer — whether he has internalized any of
that verdict himself, which is a real, separate design question about his self-image versus his outward
composure (a 1w2 Type One would very plausibly show this as private self-judgment beneath a controlled
surface); and (c) an *institutional* layer — whatever formal or structural limits Cancer itself places on
defectors regardless of individual merit (echoed directly in his README's note that he's "reached as high as
anyone in his position realistically could within that ceiling" — that ceiling is institutional stigma by this
book's own definition, and could be made concrete: specific roles, ranks, or trusts he is formally barred
from regardless of proven loyalty).

## 12. Military culture as an active barrier to care — toughness, unit cohesion, and career fear

Three specific, named mechanisms the book identifies as culturally embedded barriers, independent of stigma
generally:

- **Toughness culture**: servicemembers are actively trained to prize independence, self-reliance, and
  "shaking off" injury; admitting a mental health problem reads internally as a violation of core identity,
  not just a social risk. Quoted directly: *"Every war fighter has a culture of toughness, independence, not
  needing help, not being weak, and expecting to be able to master any and every stress without problems.
  There is a huge barrier to acknowledge even to themselves that there is a problem."*
- **Unit cohesion as double-edged**: the same bonds that protect psychological resilience during combat (most
  researchers consider unit cohesion *the* single strongest protective factor against psychiatric breakdown)
  actively discourage seeking care afterward, because doing so means being seen as less reliable by the exact
  people whose trust matters most, and because treatment logistically pulls a person away from the unit's
  routine and training tempo.
- **Malingering suspicion**: one senior-NCO focus group participant estimated as many as **75 percent** of
  soldiers claiming PTSD were faking it — a real, documented example of exactly the "regarded with contempt...
  regardless of what he actually did" dynamic the Cancer defector's README already describes, but here shown
  operating *within* the military itself, among people who should know better, not just from outside civilian
  judgment.
- **Confidentiality failure as a structural, not just perceived, problem**: real quoted testimony —
  *"Anything to do with mental health in the military, the chain of command is going to know"*; *"It's
  supposed to be confidential, but that never works. It goes up the rank."* Career fear is concrete and
  specific, not vague: fear of security-clearance revocation, blocked promotion, and being reassigned away
  from valued duties.

**Candidate application:** This maps almost exactly onto how a defector's *own former* military culture would
have shaped him before he ever reached Cancer — useful for writing his interiority (why he might resist
showing weakness even to people who'd extend him grace) and for a possible dialogue beat where he describes
his own service using language recognizably drawn from this exact toughness-culture framing, distinct from and
prior to whatever contempt he now faces from Concordia. Also gives a concrete, textured reason he might never
have sought help even during the war — not because Upper Earth lacked any care system, but because seeking
it was itself professionally dangerous, which is a sharper and more specific answer than "his side didn't
care about mental health."

## 13. Real first-person quotes — raw voice material

Verbatim quotes from RAND's own military focus groups, preserved here as usable *tone* reference (not to be
copied into dialogue verbatim, but as a register/cadence model for how a hardened, guarded ex-military
character might actually talk about this, in contrast to a clinical or melodramatic register):

- *"You don't want people to think you're weird, so you bury it."* (Marine, on coping)
- *"I lied on my post-deployment forms. Whatever got me back to my family quicker..."* (on minimizing symptoms
  to avoid delay/scrutiny)
- *"In my battalion, if you go to see mental health, you're the weak guy, the weak gal. I took leave to see a
  counselor based on everything I went through over there. My unit doesn't have knowledge, but my commander
  said I have PTSD... there's a stigma. A brand new guy goes over at 18, comes back feeling 40. He worries
  about promotion. They still view it negatively, going to see a mental health care provider."* (junior
  enlisted soldier — note the specific "18, comes back feeling 40" phrase: real soldier's own language for
  premature aging via trauma)
- *"[Stigma] would vary by chain of command. It's totally different people now so wouldn't feel comfortable."*
  (on why blogging anonymously online felt safer than talking to anyone in his current chain of command —
  the specific *people* around him, not an abstract policy, determined whether disclosure felt survivable)
- *"You could talk to mom or wife or force someone else to talk to me but they haven't shared the same
  experiences that his buddies have."* (on why only people who were *there* feel like valid confidants — a
  real, well-documented reason combat veterans often find civilians, even loved ones, unable to fully reach
  them)
- *"4th of July will never be the same again."* (marine, on hyperarousal to loud noises — plain, understated,
  more affecting than a clinical description of the symptom)

**Candidate application:** Register model for the Cancer defector's voice specifically — his README already
flags "sternly-expressed" as his emotional register (1w2, controlled surface). These quotes suggest a
believable pattern: understatement rather than description, wry deflection rather than open distress,
specificity about who counts as a valid listener (fellow ex-military, or specifically people who were *there*
— which for him, uniquely, might mean almost no one left in Cancer, since he's likely the only person of his
exact background and history in the district).

## 14. DoD's own attempted remedies — useful as a *contrast* case for how Cancer might or might not do better

The book documents the actual (2007-08) DoD approach to reducing stigma: public-education campaigns
reframing PTSD as "a result of exposure to extremely stressful experiences rather than weakness of
character"; embedding mental health providers directly within units to build trust through familiarity
(program name: OSCAR); placing mental health care inside primary-care settings to reduce the visibility of
seeking it; and proposals for "safe," off-the-record confidential counseling explicitly designed to break the
link between seeking care and career consequences. Notably, the book records genuine internal disagreement
about whether these approaches actually help or whether they **"evade stigma rather than address it
directly"** — one interviewed stakeholder argued that non-stigmatizing "alternative avenues to treatment" that
avoid the official military mental-health system actually reinforce the underlying stigma by conceding that
the official system itself is something to be avoided.

**Candidate application:** Cancer's own internal handling of defectors (whatever formal or informal systems
exist for a proven-loyalty defector's ongoing reintegration) could plausibly mirror this same real tension —
a well-intentioned accommodation that quietly concedes the stigma is real and unaddressed rather than
challenging it. Worth considering whether Cancer's tolerance of the defector reads, on inspection, more like
this kind of stigma-evasion than genuine acceptance — which would sharpen rather than soften the "grudgingly
respected, never truly esteemed" dynamic already established in his README.

## 15. TBI-specific findings — chronic, invisible injury as a documented real category

Mild TBI is explicitly the hardest case in the whole book: symptoms overlap heavily with PTSD, resolve for
most people within three months, but persist and disable a meaningful minority — and there is **no
reclassification once diagnosed**, meaning a servicemember officially labeled "mild" who never recovers is
stuck fighting an official record that doesn't match his lived reality (a documented real bureaucratic
mismatch, not merely inconvenience). Because symptoms present as irritability, memory deficits, outbursts,
and concentration problems, undiagnosed TBI has a specific, real, and serious downstream consequence:
**disciplinary action and even dishonorable discharge for behavior that was actually a medical symptom**,
which then retroactively strips the servicemember of eligibility for the very veterans' benefits that would
have addressed the underlying injury. Family caregiving burden is severe and normalized: family members
provide an estimated **80 percent** of all long-term TBI care/support, frequently at direct cost to their own
employment and financial stability.

**Candidate application:** Strong, specific candidate mechanism for whichever of the Cancer defector's "one or
two chronic wartime/training injuries" (still TBD per his README) ends up being the cognitive/behavioral one
rather than purely physical — this gives a concrete, textually real backstory hook: something that reads to
outside observers as a character flaw or a disciplinary problem (temper, memory lapses, irritability) could
in fact be the lingering, undiagnosed-at-the-time symptom of a training or combat head injury, which would
make the gap between "what he actually did" and "how he's perceived" operate on *two* levels simultaneously —
the political/moral one already established, and a second, more private one where even his own occasional bad
moments get read uncharitably rather than medically.

## 16. Cost/economic chapter — compressed, but two portable framing devices worth keeping

Per the compression note above, Chapter 6's actual dollar figures (2005-2007 U.S. federal cost estimates:
$4.0-6.2 billion two-year societal cost for PTSD/depression across 1.6 million deployed personnel; mild TBI
averaging ~$27,000-33,000/case, moderate-severe TBI averaging ~$269,000-409,000/case, with TBI-related death
itself the dominant cost driver at 70-80% of total cost in severe cases) have no in-world monetary equivalent
and are not reproduced further here. Two structural framings, however, are genuinely portable and
worth preserving:

- **The "societal perspective" principle**: the book deliberately costs everything from society's total
  vantage point rather than any single payer's, specifically because "this is the only approach that never
  counts a cost to one member of society as a beneficiary or savings to another" — i.e., if a family, a
  district, or a charity absorbs a cost that a formal system declines to pay, that is *still a real cost*, just
  shifted onto whoever has the least power to refuse it. This is a clean in-world principle for scoping just
  how much of the Cancer defector's (or any veteran/defector character's) burden has been privately absorbed
  by people around him rather than genuinely resolved.
- **Lost productivity, not direct treatment, is the largest real cost component** across nearly every
  category in the book's own model — the visible, direct cost of care is consistently smaller than the
  invisible, diffuse cost of a permanently underperforming or foreclosed life. Reinforces Finding 7 above.

## 17. Chapter 8's own key-findings summary — the clearest single-paragraph statement of the book's thesis

Worth preserving nearly verbatim as the book's own best self-summary, since it's the passage this whole
extraction exists to support: **"PTSD, major depression, and TBI can have long-term, cascading
consequences... An individual with any one of these conditions is more likely to have other psychiatric
problems and to attempt suicide. Those afflicted are also more likely to have higher rates of unhealthy
behaviors, higher rates of physical health problems and mortality, a tendency to miss more days of work and
report being less productive while at work, and a greater likelihood of being unemployed. Suffering from
these conditions can also impair personal relationships, disrupt marriages, aggravate difficulties with
parenting, and cause problems in children that extend the costs of combat experiences across generations."**
The book's own closing normative statement is also worth preserving as thematic material: **"These veterans
need our attention now, to ensure a successful adjustment post-deployment and a full recovery"** — a formal
institutional voice acknowledging an obligation that, per this same book's own evidence, is not actually being
met. That gap between the stated obligation and the lived reality is the single most reusable structural
idea in the entire source.

## 18. Recommendations chapter — the "confidential/off-the-record care" concept as a systemic design idea

The book's second formal recommendation is to create **parallel, genuinely confidential channels for care**
that operate *outside* the normal chain-of-command visibility, explicitly because the existing system's
formal confidentiality promises are not trusted (correctly — see Finding 12's quotes) and because, without
this, servicemembers who most need care have the strongest rational incentive to conceal need rather than
disclose it. The recommendation is explicit that confidentiality should not be absolute (a genuine danger to
self or others still triggers disclosure) but that the *default* should protect the servicemember, not the
institution's visibility into them.

**Candidate application:** If Cancer (or any Concordia district, or an Upper Earth institution in flashback)
has any formal system for handling defectors' or veterans' psychological aftermath, this gives a concrete,
real design for what a *genuinely* trust-preserving version would look like versus a performative one (see
Finding 14) — useful contrast material for worldbuilding Cancer's actual institutions around the defector
rather than leaving them implicit.

Per standing instruction, the following is captured because it *could* season an entirely different
character, faction, or setting, even though it has no direct tie to the Cancer defector specifically:

- **The three-part stigma taxonomy (Finding 11)** is a general-purpose social-mechanics tool, not
  defector-specific — usable for *any* character or population in the Outer Tepenia trilogy or Cryptograph
  Helix carrying a socially marked condition, identity, or history (robot-specific trauma, a Cryptograph
  Helix character marked by genetic/bioengineering stigma, a war-orbital-conflict veteran in the Jovian or
  Saturnian settings). The societal/individual/institutional split is genuinely general.
- **The consequences-cascade model (Finding 2)**, especially "interactional continuity" and "cumulative
  continuity," is a general narrative-design tool for writing *any* character whose reputation or life
  trajectory is shaped more by how others responded to an initial wound than by the wound itself — broadly
  applicable to disgraced, exiled, or reformed characters across any series in this repo, not only ex-military
  ones.
- **Secondary traumatization (Finding 9)** — the finding that trauma's psychological burden concentrates
  specifically on the closest relationship rather than diffusing evenly across a family — is a reusable
  mechanic for writing *any* character's spouse, partner, or closest companion in this universe, including
  robot-human relationships where the "trauma" in question might be something entirely different in kind
  (memory loss, forced reprogramming, an AI's own version of a traumatic origin) but structurally identical in
  how it concentrates on whoever is closest.
- **The "earthquake" metaphor for relationship resilience (Finding 9)** and the "ripples on a pond" metaphor
  for untreated consequences (Finding 2/17) are both strong, source-independent, reusable images for any
  writing about how past harm does or doesn't get absorbed by a relationship or a life — worth keeping as
  general prose tools regardless of genre or series.
- **The real-world pattern of undiagnosed injury being misread as character flaw / discipline problem
  (Finding 15)** is a broadly reusable plot mechanism — applicable to any character in any series whose
  actual medical or psychological reality is being systematically misjudged by the people around them as
  moral failure, laziness, or instability, which is a common and powerful dramatic irony available well beyond
  one ex-military human companion.
- **Malingering suspicion running *inside* an in-group, not just from outsiders (Finding 12)** — the real
  finding that fellow soldiers, not just civilians, doubted genuine PTSD sufferers — is useful anywhere this
  repo wants to write a character whose own community, not just the wider world, doubts them; sharper and more
  painful than only-outsider disbelief, and reusable for factional or intra-community distrust plots in any
  series.
