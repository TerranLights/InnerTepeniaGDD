### A. Soldier-produced media, WWI trench papers

**1. The Wipers Times, 1930 omnibus facsimile (all issues, including The B.E.F. Times and The Better Times)**
- Where: https://archive.org/details/wiperstimes (metadata: https://archive.org/metadata/wiperstimes). Full-text djvu file: https://archive.org/stream/wiperstimes/WipersTimes_djvu.txt
- Type: trench newspaper, full run, primary. 373 pages, not access-restricted, searchable OCR text.
- Gives a writer: the house style of British trench gallows humor, almost entirely deadpan. Mock hotel and real-estate ads for the shattered Ypres Salient, golf notes in which hazards are gas and corpse smell, a mock insurance scheme with absurdly small payouts for death, an "Agony Column" set on the Menin Road, parodies of war correspondents. The device is bureaucratic or civilian-institutional language applied to horror. A fetch of the text returned (re-check before quoting): "Hotel Des Ramparts ... No expense has been spared" and "the second hole ... was noticeable only for extraordinary pungent odour".
- Access: free online. VERIFIED-OPEN.

**21. Marc Levy and Susan Erony, "War Jokes Wanted: No Laughing Matter" (VVAW, 2008) and Levy's 1998 interview with Bao Ninh**

- Gives a writer: the strongest single find for joke mechanics. ## 2. TOP 5 TO READ FIRST

1. **The Wipers Times (lead 1), plus The Anzac Book (5) alongside.** Both are free, complete, and made by soldiers for soldiers while the war was on. Skim the ads, "Answers to Correspondents", mock news and verse for the mechanics: institutional language laid over horror, deadpan, no explanation of the joke.
2. **"War Jokes Wanted: No Laughing Matter" and the Bao Ninh interview (21).** It is short, free, contains actual jokes, and states the rule the character needs: the joke works inside the unit and repels civilians. Also gives a cross-army check (NVA jokes about food and sex).
3. **Lydia Fish's Vietnam folklore bibliography and song project (19), with The Vietnam Funny Book (20).** These point at the full range of soldier-made artifacts: songs, graffiti, copier sheets, short-timer calendars, unofficial insignia. They show that the humor was a crafted, shared, circulated form and not just spoken quips.
4. **Colby Buzzell's CBFTW blog (24) with Gallagher's Q&A (25).** This is the modern grunt voice with no editor. Gallagher's account of deliberately saving real quips because soldiers detect fake dialogue is the most direct statement of the writing problem the project has.
5. **Spike Milligan's war memoirs (16) plus Sassoon's Counter-Attack poems (13).** These show two opposite techniques at full length: absurdist comedy that sits beside real loss, and the compressed epigram that turns on contempt for command. Together with Mauldin (17) they cover the three main target types: the enemy-of-the-day, the brass and the weather.

(If the writer wants a non-English voice, add the French trench papers (8 and 9) and Alexievich (29).)
