## SECTION 0 — Front Matter and Chapter 1 Opening (lines 1–1000, added 2026-08-30)

**Editors.** Bret A. Moore (PsyD, former Army psychologist, two Iraq tours) and Walter E. Penk (PhD, Texas
A&M psychiatry, VA consultant) — both practitioners, not purely academics, which explains the book's
practice-forward register throughout.

**Prevalence figures across studies**, useful as citable ballast rather than a single fixed number:
Vietnam-era ~19–30% lifetime PTSD depending on strict vs. broad diagnostic criteria; Iraq ~18–20% /
Afghanistan ~12% (Hoge et al. 2004); other cohorts range ~5–25% depending on timing post-deployment and
service component (National Guard/Reserve ran higher than active duty); RAND ~14%; an additional ~8%
"subthreshold" (real impairment, short of full diagnostic criteria — see also §2 Finding 8 above).

**The 2007 IOM report** reviewed 90 RCTs and found, at that time, insufficient evidence for pharmacological
treatment and exposure therapy as the *only* psychotherapy with sufficient evidence — a finding since
broadened to include CPT, EMDR, brief eclectic psychotherapy, and non-trauma-focused approaches (stress
inoculation training, interpersonal psychotherapy). VA/DoD-supported medications named: paroxetine,
sertraline, fluoxetine, venlafaxine. Dropout from trauma-focused therapy is named as a persistent, unsolved
problem, not a rare failure mode.
*Candidate application:* useful real-world texture if the game ever wants an in-world treatment landscape
that was itself evolving and uncertain during the Long Night War era — "what actually works" was a live,
unsettled question even among his own side's practitioners at the time, not a solved science he simply
declined to use.

**Chapter 1 (Moore) — why people join the military**, four named motivations directly transferable to the
defector's pre-defection origin: **patriotism** as core identity value, not political performance;
**belonging** (Maslow-framed); **family tradition** (military-family recruits enlist at far higher rates, but
are also more prone to "reverse culture shock" — the chapter analogizes their return to civilian life
directly to an immigrant finding their own country's civilian culture newly strange); **economic
opportunity/escape** (working-class background as the typical profile, real pay/healthcare/education
incentives, or an explicit escape route from poverty or an abusive home).
*Candidate application:* any of these four is an ordinary, plausible, non-exotic reason for the defector to
have originally joined Upper Earth's military — useful precisely because it doesn't need to be dramatic; his
defection is the extraordinary part of his story, not his enlistment.

**Chapter 1 — military culture values, restated with a clinical framing not found elsewhere in this file's
existing Section 1 Finding 7.** Collectivism over individualism (self as part of the group, group goals ahead
of personal ones) — with an explicit clinical warning that a civilian clinician who imposes an individualist
framing risks being read by the patient as an uncomprehending outsider. The strict hierarchical
officer/enlisted class system, including real fraternization taboos extending even to family members, is
framed as functionally necessary to keep responsibility and accountability legible, not merely traditional
elitism.
*Candidate application:* good cross-reference texture for how an Upper Earth soldier would read Concordia's
or Cancer's own social structures — as alien, as familiar, or as an uncomfortable echo, depending on how
hierarchical he perceives Tepenian society to be by comparison.

## SECTION 1 — Chapters 1–3: Military Culture, Assessment, Prolonged Exposure Therapy (lines 1000–3300)

### Part A: Military Culture (end of Ch. 1)

**1. "Double stigma" for senior personnel.** Junior service members present with problems tied to lack of
autonomy/adjustment to military life. Senior enlisted/officers face a compounded stigma — the general military
stigma against mental health care, plus the stigma of "showing weakness as a senior person" — so they delay
help-seeking until functioning is dramatically reduced, presenting later and worse, not because the underlying
condition is more severe but because their threshold for admitting need is much higher.
*Candidate application:* Directly relevant if the Cancer defector held rank or informal leadership — a design
lever for why he didn't seek help for years, and why his present-day presentation is heavily armored/minimized.
Broadly reusable for any leadership-coded veteran across Outer Tepenia/Cryptograph Helix.

**2. Military-specific "second officer" transference and rank etiquette.** Nonmilitary clinicians can be
mistaken for officer equivalents and treated with reflexive deference; junior members under command-directed
therapy have their genuine consent muddied. Broadly reusable — a template for how any hierarchical faction
(Upper Earth military, a robot chain-of-command) maps onto a counseling relationship.

**3. Military argot as a cultural/communication barrier.** A case example shows a patient's disclosure buried
in dense acronym-speak; the clinical recommendation is to **admit not understanding rather than fake
comprehension** — service members detect faking, and it damages alliance, while admitting ignorance builds it.
*Candidate application:* An ex-military companion naturally lapsing into jargon under stress, with "does the
player ask him to explain" as a small trust mechanic, mirrors this exact principle. Portable to Outer Tepenia's
military factions too.

**4. Core values as identity infrastructure, not decoration.** Formal service-branch values (loyalty, respect,
duty, honor, integrity, selfless service, personal courage, etc.) are practically necessary for unit cohesion;
violating them brings real career/legal consequences and guilt. Clinical rule: **never let a therapist-figure
judge a service member's wartime actions** — judgment reinforces guilt and guarantees a poor outcome.
*Candidate application:* Give the defector's unit a concrete values list he still recites a decade later, and
treat any wartime violation of it (his own) as the load-bearing shame material a player slowly earns access to.
The "never let the player/therapist-figure judge him" rule is a hard design constraint worth keeping.

**5. "Adapt and overcome" — a solution-focused mindset, asset and trap.** Recruit training instills that every
problem is solvable with enough effort; dwelling on a problem reads as inept/lazy/whining. This is an asset for
directive therapy styles and a liability for passive ones, which read as weakness.
*Candidate application:* A decade-plus out, he'd still be reflexively solution-focused/action-oriented about
his own symptoms ("I have a system for the nightmares," "I handle it") — strong dialogue-voice texture for
someone who self-managed for years.

**6. Stoicism as adaptive-in-context, maladaptive-in-transfer.** Useful in combat, but reads as weakness once
help-seeking is needed — the effective intervention is naming stigma explicitly and praising the *courage* of
showing up, not confronting the stoicism directly.
*Candidate application:* The single most important presentation note — flat affect, deflection, minimizing
language ("it's fine, I've had worse") is not the absence of injury but a decade-refined performance of
controlled competence. A companion-arc beat: someone explicitly naming that talking is itself an act of
courage, not weakness, is the actual effective move per this literature — not confrontation.

### Part B: PTSD Assessment (Ch. 2)

**7. DSM-5's four symptom clusters, precisely defined.** **B — Intrusion** (nightmares, intrusive memories,
flashbacks). **C — Avoidance** (of internal/external reminders). **D — Negative alterations in cognition and
mood** (self-blame, exaggerated negative beliefs, persistent negative emotion). **E — Alterations in arousal
and reactivity** (hypervigilance, startle, reckless/self-destructive behavior, irritable/angry behavioral
expression). The old "subjective terror at the time" requirement was dropped in DSM-5. A distinct **"with
dissociative symptoms" specifier** exists for depersonalization/derealization.
*Candidate application:* Foundational vocabulary — his profile should read as a specific, plausible cluster
pattern (e.g., strong avoidance + arousal, muted intrusion by year 10-15) rather than generic "PTSD."

**8. Prevalence and the "subthreshold" category.** ~7.6% of veterans/service members have **subthreshold
PTSD** — real distress/impairment that doesn't meet full diagnostic criteria, often dismissed or shunted into
the lower-status "adjustment disorder" label.
*Candidate application:* A clinically grounded option for a decade-of-self-management presentation that sits
just under a formal threshold while still carrying real, dismissed distress.

**9. Rapport-building reframe: symptoms as adaptive-turned-miscalibrated.** Hypervigilance is reframed as
"kept you alive; it's misfiring now, not broken" — combat-adaptive, civilian-problematic, not pathology.
*Candidate application:* A strong line for a healer figure, companion, or the player to deliver without
pathologizing him.

**10. Military-specific confidentiality exception.** Even statutory psychotherapist-patient privilege breaks
when "necessary to ensure safety/security of military personnel... or accomplishment of a military mission,"
or on command referral. Veterans may also fear prosecution for trauma-adjacent conduct with no statute of
limitations.
*Candidate application:* A plausible in-world Upper Earth equivalent carve-out gives him a durable, structural
reason to distrust *any* institutional counseling, including Sanctuary/Cancer's, if it resembles a hierarchy at
all — and seeds a fear-of-prosecution angle if his wartime conduct is morally ambiguous.

**11. Assessment continuity matters.** Learning after an emotionally powerful first disclosure that you must
"start over" with a new clinician is disheartening — set expectations before, not after.

**12–14. Concrete assessment instruments** (LEC-5, TLEQ, SLESQ for exposure screening; PCL-5 with a
**provisional cutoff of 33**, PDS-5, Mississippi Scale for severity; CAPS-5 as the 45–60-minute "gold
standard" clinician interview with built-in response-validity checks). *Candidate application:* Concrete,
citable numbers if the game ever wants a diegetic clinical record for him — a Sanctuary intake file showing
numbers without narrating the man.

**15. Suicide-risk finding.** Recent separation, shorter service duration (<4 years), and less-than-honorable
discharge status were **independently** associated with heightened suicide risk in a 3.9-million-person study.
*Candidate application:* If his separation from Upper Earth's military was messy/forced/non-honorable, this
gives a real, citable, elevated-risk period early in his post-war decade that he survived.

**16. Functioning-measure domains** (IPF: family, romantic, friendships, work, socializing, parenting,
self-care; WHODAS 2.0; QOLI). *Candidate application:* A concrete checklist for picking which 2-3 domains he's
genuinely recovered in (work/self-care) vs. still visibly impaired in (intimacy/socializing) — avoids a
blanket "better now."

**17. Reporting bias, reframed compassionately.** Motivations for pursuing disability compensation are "complex
and often legitimate — relief from self-blame, validation, and acknowledgment," not just money.

**18. Comorbidity.** PTSD commonly co-occurs with mood, anxiety, and substance-use disorders; severe conditions
should be stabilized first but should not preclude trauma-focused treatment even then.

**19. Chronicity and treatment response — HIGH VALUE.** Vietnam veterans (older, more chronic PTSD) showed
significantly *less* symptom reduction from CPT than OEF/OIF veterans — chronic, long-standing symptoms
respond worse to standard treatment. Recommended adjunct for such patients: support around **life-stage
adjustment** (the text's own example: retirement).
*Candidate application:* Probably the single most load-bearing finding in the book for the character's
premise — real clinical backing that a decade-plus of untreated/self-managed PTSD is genuinely harder to fully
resolve, justifying "managed, not cured." The life-stage-adjustment note maps cleanly onto his transition into
exile/Cancer life as its own destabilizing event layered atop old trauma.

**20. Gender differences** (contrast reference only — per this project's rule against carrying one character's
answers into another, use only if a second, female veteran/defector character is written).

**21. "Reverse culture shock" — a second, distinct stressor.** Leaving military culture/identity for civilian
life is itself a significant stressor, separate from combat trauma exposure.
*Candidate application:* His transition wasn't just "war ends, PTSD begins" — it's a **double transition**:
combat exposure, then a separately stressful shift into Cancer's alien exile culture. His arrival in Cancer
could itself have been a symptom-spike event, not just relief.

### Part C: Prolonged Exposure Therapy (Ch. 3)

**22. PE overview.** Four components: imaginal exposure, in vivo exposure, psychoeducation, breathing
retraining. Standard course: 10–12 sessions, 90 minutes each.

**23. Two distorted beliefs underlying PTSD, per PE theory.** "The world is an extremely dangerous place" and
"I am not competent" — with combat-specific/moral-injury variants explicitly given: "I am a monster because of
the things I did when I was deployed," "Soldiers died because of decisions I made."
*Candidate application:* Excellent, load-bearing internal-monologue templates for what he actually believes
underneath his controlled exterior.

**24. Habituation, precisely defined.** Not erasure of the memory — **"a sense of mastery over the memory"**:
knowing a distress spike will pass, that engaging the memory won't cause loss of control, and tolerating the
distress.
*Candidate application:* The best available definition anywhere in this research for "what managed-but-present
PTSD looks like a decade out" — he still has the memories, they can still spike, but he has functional mastery.

**25. In vivo mechanics.** **SUDS scale** (0–100 distress), graduated hierarchy, staying in a situation 30–45
minutes or until distress drops ≥50% before advancing.

**26. Imaginal exposure and "hot spots."** Full first-person-present-tense retelling, then narrowing to the
specific most-distressing fragments — **"hot spots"** — distinct from the general facts of what happened.
*Candidate application:* Not every part of his war story is equally load-bearing; there's likely one or two
specific sensory "hot spots" that carry disproportionate weight. Structuring a backstory reveal around a hot
spot rather than a flat linear account is more clinically authentic and dramatically effective.

**27. Full 10-session PE outline** — a portable *shape* (full story → narrow to hot spots around session 6 →
final full retelling demonstrating changed relationship to the memory → explicit maintenance planning), not a
system to import literally.

**28. Long-term durability — HIGH VALUE.** After PE/CPT, ~80% fall below diagnostic threshold at treatment end;
at **5–10-year follow-up, ~80% remained below threshold**, with only **5.6% relapse** among those in remission,
even amid additional intervening traumas.
*Candidate application:* Directly answers "what does recovered/managed look like years later" — real backing
for a companion who, if effectively treated at some point, could be genuinely durably better a decade-plus
later, with only a small residual relapse risk.

**29–31. Efficacy/cost/format data.** PE (65.5% remission) outperformed an SSRI (43.3%) with no additive
benefit from combining them, and cost less; combat PTSD responds measurably worse to PE than civilian trauma
(proposed reasons include multiple/compounding trauma types and **moral injury as its own distinct category**
from fear-based trauma); massed PE (10 sessions/2 weeks) had a much lower dropout rate (14% vs. 24% for
spaced PE).
*Candidate application:* Moral injury explicitly named as distinct and harder to treat via standard exposure —
if his defining wound involves something he *did* rather than something that happened *to* him, that's the
variant, and it plausibly resists full resolution even where his other symptoms are well-managed. The
massed-format finding supports a resource-scarce setting defaulting to compressed, intensive treatment windows.

**32. Full case vignette, "SFC Smith" — the richest single piece of material in this section.** 15-year Army
NCO, three deployments, never sought care ("just part of the job") until his wife threatened to leave.
Intensive Outpatient PE identified his top three traumas and used graduated exposure — and the memory he called
most "haunting" (leading a squad through an armed, screaming mob) had the *lowest* measured peak distress once
treated, because prior work on "lesser" traumas had already indirectly desensitized shared triggers like
crowds. Isolation pattern beforehand: retreat into a solitary hobby, avoided family events, hadn't dated his
own wife in 18+ months. Visible safety behaviors (compulsive mirror-checking) as a tell distinct from open
panic. Once mastery began, he wanted *harder* challenges and was frustrated when exercises weren't difficult
enough. Final PCL-5: 57 → 46 → 9 → 1 over three weeks.
*Candidate application:* The isolation pattern is a portable default behavioral signature for chronic
untreated/undertreated PTSD. "Only sought help because a loved one threatened to leave" is a strong catalyst
beat. The safety-behavior tell (checking exits, positioning his back to a wall in a crowded market) is good
ambient companion detail reading as controlled-but-present hypervigilance rather than crisis. Most valuable:
the finding that his *self-described* worst memory wasn't the one with the highest measured distress is a
genuinely surprising, non-obvious beat — his own account of "what broke me" could plausibly surprise even him
in the retelling. The "wants harder challenges once mastery starts" pattern echoes Finding 5's solution-focused
mindset as an ongoing personality facet, not just a wartime trait.

### Part D: Cognitive Processing Therapy, opening (Ch. 4 tail continues in Section 2)

**33. "Stuck points" and their two generative mechanisms — assimilation vs. overaccommodation.**
**Assimilation**: new trauma information gets warped to fit *preexisting* beliefs — commonly producing
self-blame ("I should have shot sooner"), past-oriented. **Overaccommodation**: the opposite failure — the
whole belief system collapses to explain the trauma ("The world is always dangerous," "I cannot trust
anyone"), global and future-oriented. Treatment goal is **accommodation** — a balanced belief ("Some places are
dangerous, but there are many situations in which I am relatively safe").
*Candidate application:* Exceptionally precise vocabulary for his internal belief structure. Ten-plus years
out, a well-managed presentation would show *partial accommodation* in most domains but likely one or two
specific unresolved stuck points — exactly the narrow, specific wound a companion arc could resolve toward
balance, not elimination.

**34. Natural vs. manufactured emotions.** Natural (grief, anger — meant to be felt, never "cured away") vs.
manufactured (guilt from a distorted appraisal — resolves once the belief corrects). *Candidate application:*
Keeps his grief dignified and permanent while isolating which specific guilt is the actually treatable part.

## SECTION 2 — Chapters 4–6: Cognitive Processing Therapy (full), EMDR, Mindfulness-Based Therapies (lines 3100–5600)

### Chapter 4: Cognitive Processing Therapy — full protocol

**35. Schema-disruption theory.** Five belief domains: safety, trust, power/control, esteem, intimacy (agency
folds in during Phase 1). These five domains structure the entire therapy and are revisited explicitly in the
final five sessions. *Broadly reusable* as a lens for any traumatized character's worldview.

**36. Full three-phase protocol.** **Phase 1 (Sessions 1–4, Analyze):** an **impact statement** addressing all
five domains; a running **stuck point log**; the **ABC worksheet** (Activating event–Belief–Consequence);
distinguishing intent (blame/guilt) vs. responsibility (regret/sadness) vs. unforeseeable (natural
anger/sadness, no self-blame). **Phase 2 (Sessions 5–7, Challenge):** Socratic **Challenging Questions**
worksheet plus a **Patterns of Problematic Thinking** worksheet identifying the client's own recurring
distortion tendencies — goal: the client becomes "their own scientist." **Phase 3 (Sessions 8–12, Change):**
the **Challenging Beliefs Worksheet (CBW)** consolidating prior work; five remaining sessions each dedicated to
one schema domain; a final new impact statement; a recommended 1-month follow-up. Variable length in practice
(58% finish by session 12, 26% need up to 18, only 8% nonresponders).
*Candidate application:* Concrete, adaptable beats (impact statement, stuck point log, Socratic challenge,
final rewritten impact statement) for a flashback, journal entry, or in-world therapeutic dialogue, whether or
not it's ever named "CPT" on-page.

**37. Index trauma and the "ripple effect."** With multiple traumas, treatment picks one **index trauma**
(worst current symptoms) first; skills learned then generalize to other logged traumas without a separate
treatment course for each.
*Candidate application:* If he has both a single highest-impact incident and chronic background exposure, this
gives an in-world-plausible reason multiple incidents were processed "in one pass" — and a template for how a
second, previously unmentioned trauma could surface mid-questline once the first resolves.

**38. Perpetration guilt as a distinct category.** CPT explicitly targets guilt from having *committed or
witnessed* violence, not just from being victimized — combat routinely requires both.
*Candidate application:* His trauma likely includes having caused harm, not just witnessed loss — a distinct
thread from grief, worth writing as its own line rather than folding it into general "war was hard."

**39. Emotional suppression as trained coping, and its cost.** Military training makes emotional containment a
survival skill; its persistence afterward isn't pathology so much as an unlearning problem. Avoidance shows up
concretely as incomplete homework/missed sessions — itself treated as the target symptom, not friction.
*Candidate application:* Reusable texture for writing resistance to being helped as a specific trained-behavior
explanation rather than vague stoicism.

**40. Adaptation for physical/cognitive impairment.** Simplified worksheets, typing/audio instead of writing,
telehealth — real precedent for a physically injured veteran's treatment looking different from an uninjured
one's.

**41. Suicidality reduction and long-term durability.** CPT reduced suicidal ideation in active-duty RCTs;
gains were **maintained 5–10 years post-treatment** in a separate long-term sample — direct evidentiary basis
for "successfully treated a decade-plus ago and it held."

**42. Case vignette "Carol"** — full Socratic-questioning transcript dismantling the stuck point "I should have
seen it coming," useful as a *technique reference* for writing believable self-realization dialogue anywhere:
relentless, gentle, clarifying questions rather than direct pushback, letting the client's own logic surface
the answer.

### Chapter 5: EMDR

**43. Adaptive Information Processing (AIP) model.** Shapiro's own framing, explicitly held as a "working
hypothesis," not settled fact: dysfunctional stored memory networks drive symptoms; **dual-focused attention**
— an internal target held simultaneously with alternating bilateral stimulation (eye movements, tones, or
taps) — activates a natural processing mechanism.
*Candidate application:* A scientifically contested-even-in-reality mechanism is thematically rich for a
robot-consciousness-themed setting — room for an in-universe treatment that "works but nobody's sure why."

**44. Full 8-phase protocol.** History → Preparation (a **"safe place" exercise**) → Assessment (target image,
negative cognition, emotion, body sensation; **SUDS 0–10**; a positive cognition rated on the **Validity of
Cognitions Scale, 1–7**) → Desensitization (BLS sets until SUDS reaches 0–1) → Installation (pairing the
positive cognition with BLS until VoC reaches 6–7) → **Body Scan** (explicitly compared to phantom-limb
syndrome — a traumatic memory can leave a body-located "sensation signature" distinct from its cognitive
content) → Closure → Reevaluation, moving eventually to present triggers and a "future template."
*Candidate application:* The Body Scan/phantom-limb parallel is strong material if his physical injury and
psychological trauma stem from the same incident — his chronic presentation could include body-located
distress unlinked from conscious memory content (pain/tension flaring without him being able to say why).

**45. Session efficiency, and a striking wounded-vs-unwounded finding.** Significant reduction after a single
EMD session in some field reports; a VA/DoD chart review found **wounded patients needed roughly double the
sessions of non-wounded patients** (eight vs. four average) for equivalent symptom reduction.
*Candidate application:* A concrete, citable reason his combined physical-and-psychological treatment history
would have been longer/more complex than a psychologically-only-injured veteran's.

**46. EMDR vs. CBT — no homework, client-controlled exposure.** No required detailed verbal description, no
direct belief-disputing, no homework — the client controls pacing and disclosure, explicitly framed as
countering military stigma against "displaying weakness."

**47. Limitations specific to a soldier context.** **Survivor guilt**, guilt over killing noncombatants
(accidentally or under fog-of-war orders), ambivalence about returning to duty, and — most distinctively —
**fear that healing means losing the memory of fallen friends**, i.e., fear that resolution equals betrayal.
*Candidate application:* An unusually strong, specific, sympathetic reason he might resist or sabotage his own
healing — not stubbornness, but an unconscious equation of emotional resolution with forgetting/betraying the
dead, worth making an explicit beat in his companion arc rather than leaving implicit.

**48. Case vignette (LCPL)** — a full session transcript showing free-association drift from a death-scene
target memory, to an unrelated earlier guilt memory (a civilian shooting), to a positive counter-memory,
ending with the *same* original event now paired with a different dominant image and a more adaptive belief.
*Candidate application:* A strong structural template — not for literal reuse, but for writing how one trauma
thread pulls in adjacent guilt, and how "partial healing" can look like a bad image losing its automatic
first-recall priority rather than vanishing outright.

### Chapter 6: Mindfulness-Based Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies (MBCT / ACT)

**49. Experiential avoidance as the core maintaining mechanism — HIGH VALUE for chronic presentation.**
Avoiding trauma-related thoughts/emotions/reminders is negatively reinforcing (short-term relief strengthens
the avoidance), which *maintains and increases* PTSD symptoms over years without ever escalating to crisis.
Classic cognitive restructuring ("your thought is wrong") is argued to risk reinforcing the same underlying
problem by implying the internal experience itself is wrong.
*Candidate application:* The single most important theoretical concept in the whole book for "what does
10+-years managed-but-present PTSD look like." A person doesn't need to be symptomatic in crisis to still be
shaped by PTSD — years of successful, externally-invisible avoidance (staying busy, deflecting topics,
controlling routine) are exactly what keeps low-grade symptoms present without escalation. Write him as
functional and NOT constantly symptomatic on-screen, with his avoidance patterns (what he deflects, what he
subtly controls) as the actual visible fingerprint.

**50. MBCT's 8-session structure, with specific portable reframes.** Session 1 reframes **hypervigilance as
once-adaptive, now-miscalibrated** — a skill, not a defect. Session 2's "pleasant events calendar" directly
targets emotional numbing's tendency to make positive experience go unregistered. The **3-minute breathing
space** (broad awareness → narrow to breath → re-expand) is a portable in-the-moment tool. Session 6 reframes
old threat-response thoughts as **firing in now-safe contexts** rather than being false — "decentering" rather
than disputing. Session 8's closing message: **seeking help IS itself part of the mindfulness practice**, not
a failure of it.
*Candidate application:* The "old threat-response thought still firing in a safe context" framing is probably
the single best piece of vocabulary anywhere in this research for describing chronic-but-managed symptoms
years after the triggering event. The "missing positive experiences due to automatic threat-scanning" mechanism
is a specific, actable flaw distinct from generic brooding.

**51. ACT's six-process model ("Hexaflex").** Acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment contact, **self as
context** (the self as the space in which experience arises, not its content), values (directions, not
destinations), committed action. Values work is deliberately saved for last, since it's hard to know what you
value while highly symptomatic.
*Candidate application:* "Self as context" — a traumatic memory as something that happens *within* you, not
something that defines who you *are* — maps unusually well onto this project's own robot-consciousness themes;
flag as reusable for a damaged/traumatized synthetic mind's relationship to a memory stored in its own
substrate, not just for this one human character. For the defector specifically: his arc probably shouldn't
open with "what do you want out of life" — that question is earned only after earlier trust/stability work.

**52. Case vignette "Reggie" — the single best match in the whole book for the brief.** Veteran in his 60s,
assaulted decades earlier, still carries diffuse, functional-but-limiting PTSD symptoms concentrated in
**trust and safety**: chronic claustrophobia-adjacent discomfort, rumination about injustice, and hypervigilance
that makes full presence with his own grandchildren *effortful* rather than impossible — he's trying, not
absent, not fine. His core trap: believing his emotional state is **entirely dictated by his environment** (an
overaccommodated stuck point in CPT terms), reframed bluntly by the therapist: *"That's a hell of a way to
live. If you are dependent on how things are in your environment, you have little control, and others are
deciding how you will feel."* He participated in treatment more out of solidarity with other veterans than
personal hope. Outcome: not symptom elimination, but a shift from avoidance-driven life-narrowing to willing
re-engagement — intrusive thoughts still pass through; he no longer organizes his life around avoiding them.
*Candidate application:* Directly the best template for the Cancer defector's own decades-out presentation —
cross-validates trust/safety as the likely core wound (independently converging with Findings 33/35's CPT
domain framework), gives an excellent verbatim-adjacent line for an ally/companion to deliver to him, and
justifies writing him as skeptical of being "fixed" even by the player, participating out of duty/solidarity
rather than hope — a specific, textured motivation rather than a reluctant-veteran cliché.

**53. Realistic outcome data.** Roughly one-third of people receiving evidence-based PTSD psychotherapy do not
achieve full remission; diagnostic status may not shift even when functioning clearly improves.
*Candidate application:* Grounds "improved but not symptom-free" as the *realistic majority outcome*, not an
authorial cop-out — and supports a plausible in-world reason his own treatment (if any, in an exile society
building infrastructure from scratch) might have been informal, interrupted, or incomplete while still having
had real effect.

## SECTION 3 — Chapters 7–9: Psychodynamic Psychotherapy, Stress Inoculation Training, Couple/Family Therapy (lines 5500–7300)

### Chapter 7: Psychodynamic Psychotherapy

**54. The foreign-body/catharsis model.** Breuer and Freud: the memory of trauma "acts like a foreign body...
still at work" — treatment via catharsis, remembering *plus* the pent-up emotion, not memory alone.
*Candidate application:* Strong non-clinical flavor text — "the shrapnel that's still in there" as an in-world
metaphor for a memory itself, distinct from any physical wound. Broadly reusable for any Tepenian character
processing trauma through narrative/metaphor rather than clinical language.

**55. Repression as compromise, not malfunction.** Freud: symptoms are "compromises between what the survivor
could neither entirely forget nor allow him- or herself to remember."
*Candidate application:* A strong general design principle — any Tepenian character's coping mechanism can be
written as a negotiated peace the psyche struck to survive, not a bug.

**56. Transference and countertransference, precisely defined.** Transference: past relationships reinserted
into a present one, "the unconscious has no sense of time." Countertransference: now understood broadly as the
therapist's total conscious/unconscious response, used diagnostically for patterns "too subtle to observe
directly."
*Candidate application:* Broadly reusable for any dialogue/relationship system — a disproportionate present-day
reaction as an old wound reactivating is strong material for this project's existing Trigger-Type Design
Pattern (one thematic trigger type, 7–16 instances per companion).

**57. Complex PTSD — early-life trauma compounding later trauma.** A distinct category (Herman, 1992) from
single-incident PTSD: pervasive problems in trust, self-worth, and intimacy capacity, not just fear/avoidance.
Explicitly flagged in-text as clinical extrapolation for military populations specifically, not proven.
*Candidate application:* If the developer wants a layered backstory (a rough childhood predating enlistment,
or repeated deployments) rather than a single combat-origin story, this gives a real clinical label for a
broader presentation than simple hypervigilance — showing up as chronic difficulty trusting new intimacy,
directly relevant to a romance arc, recovering *slower* and more relationally than single-incident PTSD.

**58. Supportive vs. insight-oriented therapy.** Supportive therapy "supports the ego" to mobilize existing
strengths rather than excavating insight — explicitly *not* shown to be less durable or profound.
*Candidate application:* A companion doesn't need a full "insight breakthrough" arc to be genuinely improved; a
steadier, scaffolding-style arc is clinically legitimate for a quieter, more domestic resolution.

**59. Historical precedent — shell shock and narcosynthesis.** WWI's "shell shock," Rivers's repression work at
Craiglockhart (fictionalized in Pat Barker's *Regeneration*), and Grinker and Spiegel's WWII use of hypnosis or
sodium amytal ("truth serum") narcosynthesis on hundreds of combat veterans, remarkably effective at the time.
*Candidate application:* Strong, evocative, broadly reusable worldbuilding texture — a chemical or
technological "memory-drawing-out" field treatment used immediately post-combat during the Long Night War,
distinct from a slower relational therapy he might have received later in peacetime exile, giving a two-stage
treatment history.

**60. Case vignette — shame, silence, and a military anesthesiologist.** Symptoms relapsed specifically when
*ordered to redeploy*, not by the original trauma — he told no one because he believed disclosure meant
admitting he was "not strong enough" for his job. Unlocked by general psychoeducation about shame delivered
with sustained curiosity, not confrontation. The **"intersubjective key"** (an interpretive comment connecting
the patient's experience to a felt sense the therapist genuinely shares) is what allowed disclosure.
*Candidate application:* An excellent, clinically real model for dormant, well-managed symptoms re-triggered
by a *new demand echoing the original stressor* (e.g., being asked to fight again) rather than by an
anniversary or direct reminder. The specific shame content — "if people knew, they'd think I'm not strong
enough to do my job/be who I claim to be" — is a quiet, dignified, non-cliché wound for a romance arc: not "no
one could love me," but "no one could respect me."

**61. Brief Eclectic Psychotherapy (BEP).** A VA/DoD-endorsed hybrid combining imaginal exposure, written
narrative, cognitive restructuring, relaxation, and a **metaphorical ritual closing** to symbolically leave the
event in the past.
*Candidate application:* The ritual-closing element is unusually game-adaptable — a formal symbolic closing act
(burying/burning/releasing an object, a scripted line) as an in-game quest-beat capstone, broadly reusable for
any character's closure moment.

### Chapter 8: Stress Inoculation Training (Meichenbaum)

**62. The 75%/25% resilience split — load-bearing for the whole character premise.** After trauma exposure,
roughly **75% "bounce back"** and evidence resilience (some further into posttraumatic growth); only **~25%**
develop PTSD/co-occurring disorders. "**PTSD is essentially a 'disorder of nonrecovery.'**" Among 250 American
POWs held up to 8 years and tortured, incidence of depression/PTSD years later was *lower than expected*; 61%
of tortured/imprisoned aviators reported net *beneficial* long-term psychological effects.
*Candidate application:* Close to the single most load-bearing finding for the whole companion concept —
explicit clinical permission for a veteran who is 10-15 years out, functional, and romanceable while still
carrying a chronic-but-managed condition, without that reading as unrealistic. Most people are in the resilient
majority; even the minority with chronic PTSD are not static — the disorder is about *impeded* recovery, which
can still be partially overcome years later with the right skills.

**63. Eight resilience-engendering "building blocks," contrasted with a parallel "how to develop chronic PTSD"
table.** Supportive social network (often anchored by a mentor figure); a resilient "survivor/thriver"
mind-set, including **benefit-finding/benefit-remembering**; adaptively facing fears rather than avoiding them,
accepting negative emotion as normal; emotion-regulation skills; positive emotions (forgiveness, gratitude,
humor) as active fuel, not mere absence of distress; meaning-making prosocial activity (transmuting trauma into
an altruistic "gift," maintaining a moral compass); physical-fitness practices. The inverse table lists
victim-mind-set self-focus, catastrophizing metaphors ("I am a prisoner of the past"), chronic secrecy, and
substance use as false coping.
*Candidate application:* A ready-made character-design checklist — pick which resilience factors he's
mastered over his decade-plus (visible strengths a player can observe: humor, mentoring someone younger, a
fitness routine, a found-family network) versus which he's still missing (most plausibly meaning-making/moral-
compass repair, which becomes his companion arc's actual work). The negative list is equally useful in reverse
as a template for a foil/rival character's toxic coping.

**64. "Homo narrans" — PTSD as a broken autobiographical narrative.** PTSD sufferers show overgeneralized,
fragmented, sensory-driven memory that fails to integrate into a coherent story with "the rest of the story" —
what they accomplished *in spite of* the trauma. Healing is reframed as "reauthoring," not erasing.
*Candidate application:* A near-literal design metaphor for companion dialogue: a companion "stuck" tells the
same fragment every time it comes up; a companion in recovery has visibly integrated the trauma into a fuller
narrative. A concrete, testable writing technique for distinguishing "still acute" NPCs from "10+ years,
managed" ones purely through how they narrate their own past.

**65. Full three-phase SIT structure.** Phase I (conceptualization/psychoeducation, SMART goal-setting, timeline
work to surface "in spite of" strengths). Phase II (skills acquisition via the **"clock" metaphor** — 12
o'clock: appraisal of triggers; 3 o'clock: feelings; 6 o'clock: automatic thoughts; 9 o'clock: behaviors/
others' reactions — explicitly **not "train and hope"**, generalization must be built in deliberately). Phase
III (graded application, **"taking credit"**/self-attributional mastery — becoming "boss of PTSD" — and
relapse prevention with booster sessions).
*Candidate application:* Probably the single most directly reusable protocol for how he was trained or
self-trained. The "clock" model is genuinely game-adaptable as an in-fiction coping shorthand he might
reference verbally ("I catch myself at 12 o'clock now, before it gets to 6") without importing clinical
jargon — reads as plausible field-taught wisdom from a counselor or fellow veteran. The "boss of PTSD"
self-attribution language gives him agency in his own backstory rather than passivity.

**66. Case-vignette self-statements.** Verbatim examples of successful internalization: "I can use my 'clock'
analysis and break my vicious cycle." "I can tell myself the rest of the story and call upon positive
memories." "I survived for a purpose."
*Candidate application:* Excellent dialogue-voice reference for how a successfully self-managing survivor talks
about their own coping in plain, non-clinical, slightly wry language, retextured away from real-neuroscience
terms into Tepenia's own vocabulary while keeping the *shape* of a plain metaphor for a neurological process.

### Chapter 9: Cognitive-Behavioral Conjoint Therapy / Couple & Family Interventions — the strongest material for the romance angle

**67. The C-BIT model — PTSD as interpersonally maintained.** Symptoms abate naturally for most people within
~3 months; chronic PTSD is what happens when that resolution is *impeded*, partly by family dynamics that
reinforce avoidance or negative-worldview interpretations.
*Candidate application:* A precise mechanism for how a loving, well-intentioned partner (potentially the
player) could unintentionally worsen the defector's condition through simple accommodating kindness — richer
than a partner "not understanding," and directly motivates dialogue choices where a caring response
inadvertently reinforces avoidance vs. a harder, better-long-term choice that gently resists it.

**68. "Partner accommodation," precisely defined and empirically double-edged.** Loved ones, "often with the
best of intentions," restructure routines/rules around avoiding triggers — reinforcing avoidance and the very
belief "I cannot handle reminders." Family members mistake numbness for calm and the *absence* of a triggered
reaction for evidence the accommodation is working. Empirically, higher accommodation predicted *worse*
outcomes, and families struggle to stop even once they understand the harm.
*Candidate application:* An excellent, precise trap for a romantic partner to fall into — reading his calm as
genuinely okay, structuring scenes/routines around avoiding his triggers without realizing it's making him more
fragile. Strong material for a mid-arc "wake-up" beat where the correct romance choice is to stop over-
protecting him, initially uncomfortable for both.

**69. Numbing's specific relationship cost.** Numbing/dissociation damages physical and emotional intimacy more
than some more dramatic symptoms; "targeting communication, particularly sharing emotional content" is
specifically named as most helpful *because* numbing is specifically the most relationally damaging symptom.
*Candidate application:* For a decade-plus-managed companion, numbing (not acute flashbacks) is the most
plausible chronic residual symptom — quieter, more livable day to day, but exactly what creates a slow-burn
intimacy barrier rather than a single dramatic crisis. Clinical backing for his romance arc's central obstacle
being *emotional availability*, not fear or danger.

**70. Hyperarousal, irritability, and the quiet attrition of shared time.** Irritability can escalate to
intimate aggression (a serious note for how any temper he shows should be framed); separately, stimulating
environments feeling threatening leads families to simply spend less time together — a slower, non-dramatic
form of accommodation that "often strains relationships."
*Candidate application:* Good texture for a slow-paced romance where the "problem" is absence/withdrawal more
than conflict.

**71. Disclosure's conditional power.** Disclosing to others reduces avoidance **only if loved ones react in a
helpful manner** — the mechanism is explicitly conditional on listener quality, not the act of telling alone.
*Candidate application:* A precise, real mechanism for modeling that *how* the player responds to his
disclosure matters clinically, not just narratively — validating vs. minimizing vs. over-reacting as branches
consistent with real research.

**72. A four-model taxonomy for family involvement in treatment.** (1) Education/family-facilitated
engagement; (2) partner-assisted (partner as coach, relationship itself not the target); (3) general couple/
family therapy (improves the relationship, not PTSD specifically); (4) **disorder-specific couple
interventions** (the relationship itself is the client — CBCT, Strategic Approach Therapy, Emotionally
Focused Couple Therapy — strongest, most PTSD-specific effect sizes).
*Candidate application:* Four distinct, realistic shapes for what involvement (if any) a prior relationship of
his had in his treatment history — e.g., "his ex went to family education sessions but never did couple's
therapy with him" vs. "they did full disorder-specific therapy and it still ended," the latter avoiding the
cliché that therapy guarantees relationship success.

**73. Full CBCT 15-session structure.** Phase 1 (psychoeducation, a **"mutual time-out procedure,"** homework
to increase relationship positivity); Phase 2 (communication skills — distinguishing thoughts from feelings,
stopping "mind-reading"; **"approach assignments"** deliberately doing avoided things together); Phase 3
(cognitive work via **"UNSTUCK"** — United, Nominate a stuck thought, brainstorm/Test alternatives, Use the new
thought, Capture the shift, Keep practicing — targeting acceptance, blame, power/control, trust, intimacy,
posttraumatic growth).
*Candidate application:* UNSTUCK is a clean, game-adaptable naming convention for a "reframe a traumatic belief
with a trusted other" dialogue mechanic — broadly reusable beyond this one companion.

**74. Case vignette "Scott," 60 — the closest real-world template to the defector's premise.** Balkans combat
veteran; PTSD present throughout his career, contributing to his first marriage's failure. After retirement he
built "an existence... that enabled him to closely control many aspects of his life" — i.e., a stable,
symptom-minimized life via control and withdrawal. This equilibrium was disrupted **not by new trauma but by a
new relationship** requiring him to change routine and re-engage — triggering a strong symptom resurgence.
Stuck point: "I let my friend die." After UNSTUCK: "I reacted as best as I could in an extreme, chaotic
situation. I am not responsible for my friend's death" — guilt reduced, sadness remained and was *not* avoided
(a healthy outcome). He kept the new thought on a note on his refrigerator, reviewed weekly.
*Candidate application:* HIGH VALUE — nearly a direct template. A veteran who built a controlled, self-managed
existence specifically post-war, whose stability is inherently *fragile to intimacy itself* (because closeness
requires the exact re-engagement his coping strategy exists to avoid) — a psychologically realistic mechanism
for a symptom resurgence triggered by growing close to the player, distinct from a lazy "trigger of the week."
The "note on the fridge" detail is excellent small, concrete, lived-in texture — an object or habit the player
could notice in his space.

**75. Group-delivery format reduces dropout**, and **present-focused CBCT can be a viable on-ramp** to
trauma-focused work for someone not ready to go straight at the wound, per one case study.
*Candidate application:* Supports slow pacing — start with present-day coping, only approach the wound directly
once trust is built — matching this project's existing "Mystery = Soft-Detail Delivery" convention.

## SECTION 4 — Chapters 10–12: Group Therapy, Psychopharmacology/Neuromodulation, Complementary/Alternative Therapies opening (lines 7000–9900)

### Chapter 10: Group Therapy

**76. Junger's "tribe" theory — the single most important finding for the companion's isolation.** Sebastian
Junger's *Tribe*: veterans' "communities of sufferers" form a "brotherhood of pain" providing benefits
specifically at the point of return from war; independently, PTSD-group alliances "promote sharing of
hardships... to quickly generate bonds."
*Candidate application:* As sole esteemed defector in Cancer, he is **structurally** cut off from this
mechanism — not a vague "he's lonely" note, but a specific, clinically-grounded gap: no one to externally
validate that his guilt/blame patterns are common rather than uniquely damning, no one who understands
references without translation, no replication of the small-unit mutual-reliance structure his psyche was
shaped inside of. Could manifest as over-investment in whatever surrogate relationships he does have (a
partner, the player, a found-family bond carrying weight a peer group would normally distribute), guardedness
around anyone who *claims* to understand without having lived it, or seeking out unlikely tribe-substitutes
(a robot who also doesn't fully belong; an exile from an entirely different background who nonetheless shares
the structural position of being cast out).

**77. What group therapy structurally provides, that individual therapy cannot.** Reduces isolation/shame;
recalibrates private self-blame by hearing others' accounts; **duplicates the small-unit learning/trusting/
relating structure combat itself trained into the psyche** — a replication individual therapy cannot offer.

**78. The complication — many veterans actually avoid groups.** Discomfort expressing emotion among others,
fear of being misunderstood, being *stressed by hearing others' traumas* (group exposure as its own burden),
and some veterans avoiding social interaction altogether regardless of what's offered.
*Candidate application:* Nuances Finding 76 usefully — his isolation from a peer tribe isn't necessarily
something he'd fix even if one existed; his own avoidance symptoms might make him resist exactly the exposure a
group would require. The absence is a structural gap layered onto a psychology that might not fully want the
fix even if offered.

**79. Comparative efficacy vs. retention.** Individual CPT roughly doubled group CPT's effect size in a direct
head-to-head RCT, yet the two didn't differ in percentage reaching full remission or in reducing depression/
suicidal ideation — and group participants attended nearly double the sessions over nearly double the weeks,
with roughly double the continuation rate. Only ~30% of veterans with PTSD volunteer for *any* psychotherapy at
all.
*Candidate application:* Broadly reusable — social belonging/retention effects can outweigh raw clinical
efficacy in determining whether treatment happens at all, useful for describing any improvised, non-VA-style
Tepenian support structure that survives specifically because people show up, even if "weaker" on paper.

**80. Named group programs, as a mineable menu** (not literal imports): Warrior Renew (MST-focused, trust/
safety rebuilding plus adjunct physical/creative activities); Building Spiritual Strength (moral-injury/
spiritual-conflict focused); Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy groups; the Mantram Repetition Program
(meditation via a repeated sacred word/phrase); Anger Management Treatment + biofeedback (arousal-reduction
skills mattering more than cognitive coping alone for anger/aggression specifically); Strength at Home for
Couples (intimate-partner-violence prevention); Veteran Supported Education and supported-employment programs.
*Candidate application:* A rich menu of what organized peer/professional support concretely looks like for
*other*, less-isolated veteran/exile characters elsewhere in Concordia or Outer Tepenia — useful contrast
material precisely because the defector, by definition, lacks access to any of it.

**81. Moral injury, formally distinguished from PTSD.** Guilt, an "emotional need for forgiveness," and
"spiritual solutions to heal unseen wounds of war," arising specifically from one's own actions violating one's
moral self-concept — treated via dedicated spirituality-and-trauma modules rather than standard exposure
protocols.
*Candidate application:* HIGH VALUE, broadly applicable — a clean clinical vocabulary word for a wound distinct
from fear/hyperarousal-based PTSD, directly relevant if his "I let my friend die"-style guilt (Finding 74) is
more moral-injury-shaped than fear-shaped. Also reusable for any Tepenia character whose trauma centers on
complicity/action — including a robot's ethical trauma under a Fundamental-Laws conflict, an interesting
adjacent application flagged for the robot-consciousness pillar specifically.

**82. Isolation compounds over the long tail, not just a fixed baseline.** Older veterans "may have experienced
longer periods of isolation" — group therapy counters accumulated social-functioning interference that builds
over years, not just an initial deficit.
*Candidate application:* Supports writing his isolation as something that has *deepened* over his 10-15
post-war years specifically because he's never had peer-tribe access — a slow accretion, not a fixed level.

### Chapter 11: Psychopharmacological and Neuromodulation Treatment

*(Per the assigned research agent's own explicit instruction, treated here as functional/conceptual texture —
symptom categories a treatment targets — not literal drugs or devices to import into Tepenia's setting.)*

**83. The four DSM-5 clusters, reused as a treatment-organizing skeleton** — intrusion, avoidance, negative
cognition/mood, arousal — a clean, non-jargon frame for writing any trauma-affected character's symptom profile
consistently.

**84. Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS) — conceptually significant regardless of literal
tech.** Time-limited (20–30 sessions), non-pharmacological, doesn't require daily medication compliance,
produces durable change from a defined course rather than ongoing maintenance. A closely age/timeline-matched
case vignette (38-year-old, three tours, PTSD onset ~10 years prior) showed dramatic remission after multiple
prior medication trials over the prior decade gave only partial relief; a relapse ~9 months later followed a
cross-country move (echoing Finding 74's disruption-destabilizes pattern), with quick re-stabilization via
tapering maintenance.
*Candidate application:* Strong conceptual (not literal) fit — an energy/neuromodulation-based recalibration of
a wound, rather than an ongoing chemical regimen, maps well onto a sci-fi technology palette. For the defector:
"tried several things over a decade, partial relief each time, then one intensive but time-limited intervention
that got him most of the way to his current stable baseline" is a clean, adaptable backstory shape, and the
relapse-on-disruption/quick-re-stabilize pattern is good texture for how a late-game crisis could believably
destabilize him without contradicting "he's mostly managed now."

**85. Prazosin and nightmares as literal re-traumatization.** A real conceptual point made explicitly in-text:
biochemically "there is no difference between dreaming and real experience," so severe recurring nightmares are
theorized to *re-inflict* the trauma repeatedly during sleep, not merely disrupt rest.
*Candidate application:* Strong, quotable in-world texture for a chronic-PTSD character's nights specifically,
distinct from daytime symptoms — nightmares as an active mechanism, not just an unpleasant side-symptom,
plausibly persisting in reduced but real form even when he otherwise "looks fine" by day.

**86. Comorbid pain, explicitly named.** Pain is "very often" comorbid with PTSD; some agents are specifically
favored because they address anxiety and pain together via the same mechanism.
*Candidate application:* Direct support for the "one or two chronic wartime injuries" brief detail — real
literature treats chronic pain and PTSD as mutually reinforcing, not coincidental, so his physical injury and
psychological wound should be written as *interacting* (pain flare-ups plausibly correlating with symptom
flare-ups) rather than as two unrelated character-sheet entries.

**87. A broader neuromodulation menu as an invasiveness spectrum**, from a cheap, portable, self-administered
device (cranial electrotherapy stimulation, popular specifically *because* it's non-medication and
self-administered) up to invasive last-resort options.
*Candidate application:* A reusable spectrum for characterizing different Tepenia characters' relationship to
their own treatment — self-reliant/DIY vs. institutional/high-tech vs. treatment-resistant/last-resort —
independent of which literal technology each one uses.

### Chapter 12 opening: Complementary and Alternative Therapies

**88. CAM's real scale of use, and why.** Nearly 40% of VA patients with PTSD use nonmainstream health care;
framed explicitly as partly a response to dissatisfaction with mainstream treatment's side effects and
limitations.
*Candidate application:* Sets up a strong thread — the defector plausibly partially self-treated via an
improvised, non-institutional practice (something meditative, physical, ritualized) that Tepenia's post-war
infrastructure either couldn't or didn't provide in standard form, consistent with "distrust of the system,
found his own way" texture. *(The chapter's specific technique-by-technique detail — hypnotherapy, yoga,

## SECTION 5 — Chapter 12 (tail), Chapter 13: Psychosocial Rehabilitation, Chapter 14: Co-Occurring Affective/Anxiety Disorders, Chapter 15 opening: Substance Use (lines 9800–12450)

### Chapter 12, continued: named CAM techniques

**89. Hypnotherapy and Spiegel's "eight C's."** A staged, loosely sequential process: **C**onfront the trauma
(link current symptoms to the past event) → **C**ondensation (distill to the defining fragment) →
**C**onfession (surface shameful material; separate appropriate from misplaced guilt) → **C**onsolation
(boundaried empathy) → **C**onscious (bring dissociated material fully into awareness) → **C**oncentration
(bounded access, with reassurance the material can be "put down" again) → **C**ontrol (patients taught
self-hypnosis early, reinforcing autonomy) → **C**ongruence (final integration into a coherent self-narrative).
People with PTSD show elevated hypnotizability as a trait, making them unusually responsive to this modality.
*Candidate application:* A structured, dramatizable arc for a flashback-processing scene or remembered-therapy
monologue as a plausible past treatment he underwent. Self-hypnosis as a taught, equipment-free coping skill
travels well into a frontier-medicine setting. The confession/misplaced-guilt distinction is a strong generic
hook for any character carrying survivor's guilt.

**90. Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR).** Body scan, sitting meditation, gentle movement; cultivates a
detached "observer" perspective enabling disengagement from rumination. Standard dose ~45 min/day, 6 days/week.
*Candidate application:* A lightweight, prop-free coping ritual — a repeated small gesture/behavior beat
(pausing mid-task to "come back to the room") — good texture for partial mastery a decade out.

**91. Mantram Repetition Program.** A repeated word/phrase as an attention anchor, plus "slowing down" and
"one-pointed attention," useful for both in-the-moment and *anticipatory* stress.
*Candidate application:* Portable, equipment-free, pairs naturally with any folk-spirituality equivalent in
Tepenia — good option for managing anticipatory dread before a known trigger (e.g., before entering a crowded
or enclosed space).

**92. Yoga — durability, not initial reduction, is its documented edge.** In one RCT, both yoga and a control
condition improved mid-treatment, but **only the yoga group maintained gains at posttreatment**.
*Candidate application:* Explains a currently-still-practiced physical discipline (need not be literally
called yoga — a martial or breathing discipline adapted in-world works) that he does specifically because it's
what kept his gains from eroding over years, distinct from techniques he no longer actively needs.

**93. Acupuncture, including a proposed neuroimaging mechanism** (decoupling memory-retrieval circuitry from
emotional-alarm circuitry).
*Candidate application:* A non-Western, low-tech medicine tradition (needles and trained practitioners, no
power/equipment required) plausible to persist or have an analog anywhere in a post-catastrophe medical
landscape.

**94. Adventure Therapy (Nature Adventure Rehabilitation).** Structured outdoor group activity; critically,
symptom improvement was contingent specifically on a **shift in perceived control over illness**, not the
activity itself.
*Candidate application:* A broadly reusable mechanism for *any* character's "how did they get better" arc —
the therapeutic value comes from regained perceived agency, a far more universal narrative beat than any
specific activity. A Tepenian equivalent (cold-water diving, glacier trekking, a robot-partner-assisted climb)
could serve the same function.

**95. Equine Therapy, and the more useful *informal* version.** The chapter's own case vignette ("Carla," below)
uses horses **self-directed, outside any clinical program** — a returned-to childhood competency that becomes
accidental therapy, never framed by anyone as "treatment."
*Candidate application:* A clean, reusable pattern — recovery includes rediscovering a pre-trauma hobby/skill
that turns out to regulate the person, without anyone naming it therapeutic. Directly portable if he had a
pre-war skill involving animals or any equivalent embodied-caretaking activity.

**96. Case vignette "Carla" — a full four-beat treatment-timeline template.** Army nurse, IED/ambush trauma;
presentation includes driving phobia and a "last straw" relationship collapse that precipitated care. Sequence:
(1) mindfulness first, to stabilize immediate distress/rumination; (2) hypnotherapy — including the eight C's —
only *after* sleep stabilized, plus direct hypnotic suggestion enabling her to resume driving; (3) after formal
sessions ended, **self-selected** long-term maintenance (yoga, reconnecting with horseback riding from her
farm childhood); (4) termination by mutual agreement with an open return-as-needed arrangement — not framed as
"cured," but stabilized with a standing safety net.
*Candidate application:* A genuinely useful treatment-timeline template: stabilize sleep/immediate distress
first → process the trauma memory only once stabilized → pivot to self-directed maintenance practices →
terminate with an open door rather than a hard "graduation." Broadly reusable for constructing *any* character's
therapy history in this universe, and a plausible shape for the defector's own multi-year arc before the game's
present day.

### Chapter 13: Psychosocial Rehabilitation — the highest-value chapter in this section for the defector

**97. "Dynamic" vs. "kinematic" treatment — the core theoretical permission slip.** Dynamic theories/treatments
(psychotherapy, medication) modify forces *within the skin*, aimed at symptom reduction. Kinematic
rehabilitation addresses forces *outside the skin* — housing, employment, family, community — aimed at
*functioning*, treated as genuinely separable from symptom reduction. DSM history: functioning-as-impairment
was folded directly into PTSD's own diagnostic criteria over successive editions (now DSM-5 Criterion G).
*Candidate application:* Directly load-bearing — gives theoretical permission to write him as symptom-present
but functionally rebuilt: he can hold a job/relationship/place in Cancer's community while still carrying
specific, bounded triggers, because functioning and symptom-reduction are explicitly not the same axis.

**98. Neuroplasticity remains available years after injury — not a narrow early window.** Cited evidence
includes environmental enrichment reshaping animal brain development, London taxi drivers' measurable
hippocampal growth from spatial navigation demand, and — most directly relevant — **strategy-based reasoning
training modulating cortical thickness in adults with chronic TBI, an effect holding up "years after TBI."**
Loneliness is flagged as a key symptom category especially where PTSD and TBI co-occur.
*Candidate application:* The single most important piece of texture for a companion who is 10-15 years out —
scientific permission for showing him as still actively adapting/improving rather than having permanently
plateaued at some fixed post-war baseline. Broadly reusable for any long-term-injured character, human or (with
adaptation) a damaged robot character, where "environment reshapes structure" has an even more literal analog.

**99. Functioning-assessment domain lists**, especially the **Community Reintegration of Injured Service
Members (CRIS)** framework: learning/applying knowledge, general tasks, communication, mobility, self-care,
domestic life, interpersonal relationships, major life areas, community spirit, civic life.
*Candidate application:* An excellent ready-made checklist for auditing what a fully realized decade-plus-
post-war life looks like — pick specific domains where he's succeeded (work, self-care) and at least one where
he's still genuinely unresolved (per this project's "no costless good endings" convention), rather than a
blanket "he's fine now."

**100. A rich interventions catalog — treat each item as a distinct, mineable module, not a system to import
literally.** Named smartphone-app-style self-management tools (each targeting one function: relaxation,
insomnia, mood-tracking, crisis coping) with the pointed observation that **avoidance — the core PTSD
symptom — is exactly what prevents the people who'd benefit most from ever adopting them.** **Compensated
Work–Therapy/Transitional Residence**, which deliberately couples housing support to paid work rather than
sequencing them. **Strategic Approach Therapy**, notable for improving emotional regulation specifically, not
just relationship satisfaction or symptom counts (evidence that different manualized approaches aren't
interchangeable). **Vocational rehab's "Choose–Get–Keep"** three-phase model, alongside the historical note
that paying hospitalized psychiatric patients to work strongly motivated recovery even with no medication
available (Peffer, 1953) — earned wages as a rehabilitative incentive in their own right. **"Battlemind" reset
training** — the routine practice of psychologically decompressing when personnel return to base between
missions — explicitly analogized to vocational rehab's own "reset" logic. **Supported education**, framed as
the civilian continuation of reset training. **Peer specialists** — veterans themselves, formally trained,
shown to work better specifically *because* they share lived experience. **Spiritual support and moral injury**,
quoted directly: *"It's the moral injury over time that really kills people. Soldiers lose their identity. They
don't understand who they are anymore."*
*Candidate application:* The richest section in the book for backstory architecture. "Choose–Get–Keep" plus
"Battlemind reset training" gives a plausible staged vocational arc into Cancer's economy, possibly mentored by
an in-world peer-specialist figure — a fellow defector. The identity-loss moral-injury quote is a genuinely
strong direct-inspiration line if his defection was itself entangled with a moral-injury event, not just
combat exposure generally. The peer-specialist model is broadly reusable for how defector/veteran characters
across the setting could plausibly know and support each other. The avoidance-blocks-self-help-adoption
observation gives an in-world-plausible reason he never voluntarily sought treatment, without needing an
external plot contrivance.

**101. Case vignette "Sean" — the single closest real-world template to the defector's decade-plus arc.**
28-year-old Marine, ~10-year service arc, with a childhood-anger baseline predating combat (trauma stacked on a
preexisting vulnerability, not trauma alone). Post-discharge: isolation, heavy drinking, fighting, compounded
by his mother's death. **First institutional contact came through a veterans' treatment court, not mental
health services**, after a bartender intervened following a fight. Marriage and partial stabilization followed
— anger "simmering," explicitly *not resolved*, just tempered, under continued economic stress. He found a
**substitute mission structure** in a lay fraternal order, explicitly mapping its historical charitable mission
onto his own prior sense of purpose from service. Years later: GED, GI Bill college, part-time police work
while studying, **peer support networks, and eventually became a peer specialist himself**, mentoring incoming
veterans. **Formal clinical treatment for his childhood-origin issues and combat moral injuries came last** —
only once he was already a stabilized, employed adult, as an addition on top of an already-functional life, not
a precondition for building one. Ends unresolved (his wife's pregnancy as the next open life-stage challenge),
deliberately not a clean finish.
*Candidate application:* The strongest single match in the whole book to the brief. Key transferable
structural beats (as *architecture*, not literal specification, per this project's source-not-spec rule):
preexisting vulnerability plus combat trauma, not trauma alone; **functioning built first, formal
symptom-focused treatment addressed later and separately**, often prompted by a partner rather than
self-initiated — directly answering "what does a decade-plus-managed presentation look like"; a substitute
mission/meaning structure adopted post-service, portable to some Cancer-district guild, labor collective, or
found-family unit that maps onto whatever "mission" mattered to him in the war; first institutional contact via
the justice system rather than mental health (ties into this project's existing Tepenian Criminal Justice
System framework, if his post-defection history includes any brush with it before support); and paying it
forward as an unofficial peer specialist within his own community — giving him a plausible present-day function
(mentoring newer defectors/exiles) and natural quest/companion-content hooks, e.g., being the one who notices
and reaches out to a new, struggling arrival. Broadly reusable well beyond this one companion, as a complete
generic arc-shape for any veteran/exile/defector archetype in either series.

### Chapter 14: Co-Occurring Affective and Anxiety Disorders

**102. Co-occurrence is closer to the rule than the exception.** In a civilian PTSD sample: 62% also met
criteria for another anxiety disorder, 77% for an affective disorder (MDD 69%). In veteran samples specifically,
**panic disorder (25%) is the most common co-occurring anxiety diagnosis**, not GAD (only 7%) as in civilians —
an inverted pattern worth using deliberately. Co-occurring PTSD+MDD predicted greater 1-year suicide-attempt
risk than either alone. A proposed alternative framework (HiTOP) groups PTSD with depression and generalized
worry as an "internalizing–distress" cluster, distinct from a "internalizing–fear" cluster (panic, OCD, social
phobia) — meaning PTSD's true dimensional neighbor is depression, not panic, despite panic's high veteran
co-occurrence rate.
*Candidate application:* Useful for deciding his specific comorbidity profile rather than a generic label — the
veteran-specific panic-over-GAD skew is good textured detail; depression (55% co-occurrence, see below) is the
most probable secondary condition if the writers want one. The suicide-risk finding is sensitive and worth
handling per this project's "Mystery = Soft-Detail Delivery" convention rather than surfacing directly.

**103. Three risk mechanisms for co-occurrence.** **Combat exposure directly** raises risk broadly across the
entire anxiety/mood spectrum, not selectively for PTSD (correlated with panic, OCD, agoraphobia, depression,
GAD, social anxiety alike) — childhood trauma correlates too, but more weakly, and the two contribute
*independently*, not redundantly. **Genetics**, including a specific gene-by-environment finding: a
low-expression serotonin-transporter-gene variant made carriers **4.5 times more likely** to develop both PTSD
and depression when combined with high trauma *and* low social support — meaning **social support functions as
a moderator that can suppress an underlying vulnerability, or its absence can unmask it** (candidate-gene
caveat noted: most individual findings in this literature haven't replicated; broader genome-wide approaches
are now preferred). **TBI**, called the "signature injury" of the referenced conflicts — its effect on
PTSD/depression risk may be **fully mediated by persistent postconcussive symptoms** (headaches, balance,
memory problems) rather than a direct pathway.
*Candidate application:* The social-support-as-moderator finding is strong, broadly reusable characterization
logic independent of the specific gene: isolation makes underlying vulnerability worse, connection suppresses
it — a clean causal (not merely correlational) mechanism for why his community integration in Cancer directly
affects his symptom trajectory. If he has a head-related chronic injury, the TBI-mediation finding lets writers
cleanly separate "his war memories" from "a persistent physical injury still producing daily symptoms" as two
distinguishable things he manages.

**104. Assessment note.** The **ADIS-5** formally designates a **principal vs. secondary diagnosis** based on
severity and life interference when multiple conditions co-occur.
*Candidate application:* A useful organizing idea if he has multiple co-occurring issues — deciding which is
"principal" (defines daily functioning) vs. "secondary" (present but subordinate) gives a clean prioritization
tool for writers.

**105. Treatment findings.** Standard PTSD treatments generally also reduce co-occurring depression, but less
than the PTSD-specific effect — treating PTSD helps depression as a side effect without fully resolving it. The
reverse doesn't reliably hold, and treating a co-occurring condition *first* can even backfire (exposure-based
OCD treatment is flagged as potentially contraindicated with co-occurring PTSD). Comorbid PTSD+depression
carried a **5-6x increased risk of not returning to employment** after traumatic-injury hospitalization, versus
3x for either alone.
*Candidate application:* Supports a "took years to actually get better" backstory beat: a failed early
attempt at treating something adjacent (anger management, general therapy) before anyone properly addressed the
trauma underneath is medically plausible, not narratively convenient. The employment-risk multiplier supports a
period of chronic underemployment specifically *because* of stacked, untreated comorbidity, before whatever
turning point got him functional.

### Chapter 15 opening: Co-Occurring Substance Use Disorders

**106. Prevalence.** 55–75% of Iraq/Afghanistan veterans with a diagnosed substance use disorder also carried a
PTSD diagnosis; as many as **60–80% of veterans seeking PTSD treatment have at least some substance-abuse
history.**
*Candidate application:* Strong statistical backing (not obligation) for the defector having *some* substance-
use history even if resolved by the present day. If the writers want him substance-clean now, this data
supports a resolved *history* being the medically realistic default rather than an outlier choice.

**107. Four proposed causal mechanisms — a genuinely useful non-cliché taxonomy.** **Self-medication**
(substance use as direct symptom management — the most classically sympathetic, pairs cleanly with the "Sean"
vignette's bar-drinking-then-community arc). **High-risk** (the *lifestyle* of substance use itself increases
exposure to further trauma — implies a self-destructive spiral distinct from simple numbing, useful for a
rock-bottom beat that isn't just "drank to cope" but "drank and made things worse"). **Susceptibility** (a
shared underlying temperament independently predisposes toward both the war and the drinking, meaning the two
aren't causally linked at all — avoids a simple cause-effect trauma narrative). A fourth, indirect third-
variable pathway is named but not elaborated in the covered text.
*Candidate application:* Four distinct, non-clichéd backstory shapes for any past/present substance-use
element — deliberately assign one rather than defaulting to the generic "drank to forget" self-medication
cliché. Broadly reusable well beyond this one companion for any character in the wider universe with a
substance-use thread.

## SECTION 6 — Chapter 15 (tail), Chapter 16: Chronic Pain, Chapter 17: Traumatic Brain Injury, Chapter 18 opening: Military Sexual Trauma (lines 12400–14950)

### Chapter 16: Chronic Pain and PTSD — the physicality goldmine

**108. Definitional threshold.** Pain persisting more than **3 months** is classified as chronic — a clean
in-fiction timeline anchor: his injury/pain would have "converted" to chronic status well within his first
season out of active service.

**109. Disproportionate veteran burden, and comorbidity makes both conditions worse.** ~44% of recent military
returnees report chronic pain, a rate **40% higher** than a matched nonveteran comparison. Comorbid pain+PTSD
produces **greater** pain, PTSD symptoms, depression, anxiety, disability, and opioid use than either condition
alone — not additive-and-separate, but mutually potentiating.
*Candidate application:* Core mechanism — if he has both a chronic wartime injury and PTSD, each should read as
harder to manage *because* of the other, not as two independent character-sheet entries.

**110. The fear–avoidance model — a precise, dramatizable psychological mechanism.** **Catastrophizing**
(interpreting pain as overly threatening: "this is never going to get better") → fear of pain → avoidance of
activities believed likely to worsen it → **"guarding" behaviors** (specific protective movement patterns —
stiffening, favoring a limb) and **hypervigilance to bodily sensations** → increased depression/inactivity →
pain feels *more* intense — a negative feedback loop. Conversely, people who confront pain rather than
catastrophize (absent serious underlying pathology) recover faster.
*Candidate application:* A genuine physicality goldmine — a character who has *successfully managed* his
condition for a decade would show the **inverse** of the maladaptive cycle day to day (confronting rather than
catastrophizing, meaning controlled, deliberate movement rather than constant flinching), while a *lapse* (a
bad week, a trigger, an old injury flaring) could visibly show guarding and hypervigilance. Two distinct
physical registers for the same character depending on his current state — precisely the "well-managed years
out" texture requested, with concrete, reusable vocabulary (guarding, catastrophizing, hypervigilance to bodily
sensations).

**111. Anxiety, not raw pain intensity, often drives functional disability.** In low-back pain specifically,
anxiety accounted for **32% of disability but only 14% of pain severity** — the disabling part of chronic pain
is often more psychological-behavioral than nociceptive.
*Candidate application:* Explains why his functional limitations (what he avoids, what accommodations he makes)
might be *disproportionate* to his old injury's "objective" severity — useful nuance against a purely
mechanical logic.

**112. The triple vulnerability model, extended to pain+PTSD.** Generalized biological vulnerability (including
a proposed mechanism: low neuropeptide Y and "antistress hormones" in PTSD, hypothesized to worsen pain
control); general psychological vulnerability (early-life-instilled beliefs like "the world is not safe,"
typically from overrestrictive or neglectful parenting); specific psychological vulnerability (learning to
focus anxiety on particular situations) — crucially, **trauma exposure alone is explicitly stated as NOT
sufficient** to cause chronic pain or PTSD; what matters is the sense that the event and one's own reactions to
it were *unpredictable and uncontrollable*.
*Candidate application:* A principled, generative framework for deciding whether a Tepenia character develops
chronic dysfunction from trauma or remains resilient — protective factors (adequate coping skills, social
support) are explicitly named as moderators. For the defector specifically, this supports layering an
early-life vulnerability (family instability predating the war) atop his combat experience for internally
consistent depth, rather than the injury alone "causing" everything.

**113. Three clinical vignettes showing the pain/PTSD relationship is NOT simple.** One veteran deliberately
overexerts himself to *create* pain as a distraction from psychological trauma symptoms. Another does the
inverse — provoking trauma reminders or strong emotion specifically to forget physical pain ("I feel alive
when I'm mad about something"). A third experiences his pain as **the price he paid for surviving while his
friends did not** — survivor's guilt somatized into physical suffering. Separately: roughly half of veterans in
one study reported their *first* traumatic experience preceded military service — pain and PTSD are frequently
NOT tied to the same originating event, and the earliest trauma (not the most recent) is often what patients
rate as most bothersome.
*Candidate application:* Any of these three postures — pain-as-distraction-from-trauma, anger-as-distraction-
from-pain, pain-as-penance/survivor's-guilt — is directly usable, non-obvious characterization for how he
relates to his own body, richer than a flat "war hurt him." The "layers of trauma, not one event" point means
his defining trauma need not be his war injury at all — the injury could be just the most visible layer atop
an earlier one.

**114. Concrete CBT-for-pain technique inventory.** Cognitive restructuring of catastrophic pain thoughts;
relaxation training; **activity pacing** (graduated, measured increases in activity, avoiding "boom-bust"
cycling); graded homework to reverse avoidance.
*Candidate application:* Activity pacing is an especially strong, *visible* behavioral trait — a character who
deliberately breaks tasks into measured increments and doesn't push through in one go, even when he physically
could, reads as someone who has done real rehabilitative work and internalized it.

**115. The opioid-crisis mechanism, as adaptable texture, not a literal import.** Veterans with PTSD were
significantly more likely to be prescribed opioids, at higher doses, with more concurrent sedatives and early
refills, than veterans without mental health diagnoses — a documented "vicious cycle" of PTSD driving
higher-risk prescribing driving worse outcomes; veterans overall are twice as likely to die of drug overdose as
nonveterans.
*Candidate application:* Broadly reusable for any Tepenia character or backstory beat involving pain-driven
substance dependency; for this companion specifically, more useful as a **"road not taken"** or a past struggle
since moved past, consistent with a decade-plus well-managed presentation, than a current active issue.

**116. Integrated treatment structures, as backstory scaffolding.** A 12-session combined CPT+pain-CBT program;
a compressed 3-week, 6-session intensive version developed after veterans found the longer course itself
burdensome (zero dropouts in its small pilot); a collaborative-care model pairing a physiatrist, psychologist,
and primary care around one biopsychosocial assessment.
*Candidate application:* A concrete, real-world-grounded template for a multidisciplinary, weeks-not-years
treatment program combining trauma processing with physical pain management — adaptable into whatever
rehabilitative institution existed in Upper Earth or early Cancer/Sanctuary society without needing to import
"VA Medical Center" literally.

### Chapter 17: Traumatic Brain Injury — directly addresses the requested cognitive-symptom detail

**117. Definition and blast-specific mechanism.** TBI diagnosis is retrospective, based on symptoms *at the
time of injury* (confusion, amnesia, loss of consciousness, or focal neurological signs). **mTBI (mild)
criteria: loss of consciousness ≤30 minutes; posttraumatic amnesia ≤24 hours.** Blast-induced TBI — the
dominant modern-combat mechanism — is mechanistically distinct from blunt-force TBI: likely direct pressure
changes as the blast wave passes through the head, plus kinetic-energy transfer via chest/abdomen blood
vessels, producing prominent cerebral microvascular pathology; combat mTBI often **compounds** blast injury
with secondary blunt and penetrating (shrapnel) injury in a single incident.
*Candidate application:* Directly load-bearing if his chronic injury is or includes a TBI. The blast mechanism
gives a textured, appropriately modern origin (explosive ordnance, not necessarily a direct blow); the
diagnostic thresholds let a writer calibrate severity precisely toward the common, survivable, long-term-livable
band ("he was dazed and doesn't fully remember the incident," not "he was in a coma").

**118. The "polytrauma clinical triad" — the mechanism that justifies his profile wholesale.** In a 340-veteran
polytrauma-center review: **chronic pain 81.5%, PTSD 68.2%, mTBI 66.8%**, with each condition **in isolation**
being rare (pain alone 10.3%, PTSD alone 2.9%, TBI alone 5.3%) — **42.1% had all three simultaneously**,
formally named the polytrauma clinical triad.
*Candidate application:* This is the mechanism that justifies "one or two chronic injuries" as canon, not an
authorial convenience — real-world data shows chronic pain, PTSD, and TBI clustering together, with **each
condition more likely BECAUSE of the others**, not despite them. Direct precedent to cite if the design ever
needs to defend giving him both a physical injury and psychological symptoms: that combination is the
statistically normal pattern, not the exception.

**119. Acute vs. long-term mTBI course — precisely the requested distinction.** Acute (days–weeks):
headaches, dizziness, nausea, concentration/memory difficulty, irritability — with measurable first-week
impairment specifically in speed of processing, verbal fluency, and delayed memory. The overwhelming majority
of civilian mTBI is transient, typically resolved by **3 months**. Years out, even with favorable recovery,
patients may show **mildly reduced performance specifically on complex attention and working memory** — a
small subset retains clinically significant impairment indefinitely. **Multiple concussions** (very plausible
for a career combat soldier with repeated blast exposure) is specifically associated with poorer long-term
executive-function and memory outcomes, and a possible risk factor for later cognitive decline.
*Candidate application:* The precise band requested. At 10-15 years out, he should **not** show acute-phase
symptoms (headache, dizziness, gross confusion) — those resolve within months for a typical single-concussion
case. What could plausibly persist, especially with multiple blast exposures, is a **subtle, mild, persistent
deficit in complex attention and working memory**: losing his train of thought under multitasking or high
cognitive load, needing something repeated when distracted, taking slightly longer to process multi-step
instructions under stress, minor difficulty holding several things in mind at once during tense scenes. Subtle
enough to be "well-managed" and not read as disabling — exactly the requested texture.

**120. mTBI/PTSD symptom overlap, and the one clean diagnostic wedge.** Both conditions can show weaknesses in
speed of processing, attention/working memory, and learning/memory, plus overlapping somatic/emotional symptoms
(noise sensitivity, sleep problems, irritability, anxiety, depression). The key differentiator is **course, not
content**: mTBI symptoms are typically transient; PTSD-related deficits frequently endure for years and may
even reflect pretrauma vulnerabilities. Clinically, cleanly attributing a given symptom to one condition or the
other is often **not productive** — the recommended approach shifts to characterizing functional problems
regardless of etiology. The one symptom explicitly named as NOT shared: **reexperiencing (flashbacks/intrusive
memories) is PTSD-specific**, not a TBI symptom.
*Candidate application:* Gives explicit permission to NOT cleanly separate "this is his TBI" from "this is his
PTSD" in characterization — real clinicians don't either. The one clean wedge (flashbacks = unambiguously PTSD)
is useful if the game ever wants an unambiguous "this is a PTSD moment" beat distinct from his general
cognitive quirks.

**121. Persistence risk factors, and a striking finding about the actual driver of symptom severity.** A
longitudinal Army study found deployment mTBI nearly tripled postconcussion-symptom likelihood/severity at 3
and 9 months; significant predictors of *persistent* symptoms were **prior pre-deployment TBI** and
**psychological distress before and during deployment** — and, critically, **blast vs. non-blast mechanism did
NOT reliably predict symptom severity; psychiatric comorbidity did.**
*Candidate application:* His psychological state, not any purely physical/mechanical factor, is likely the
bigger driver of when his cognitive symptoms flare — meaning his "TBI symptoms" can legitimately worsen during
high-stress narrative beats (a quest under time pressure, a confrontation, a bad memory triggered) rather than
needing a separate physical explanation (weather, exertion) every time. Directly ties his cognitive symptom
visibility to emotional/narrative stakes rather than requiring an independent physical trigger.

**122. Compensatory strategies — the clearest "functional adaptation" material in the book.** Cognitive
rehabilitation programs teach compensatory strategies exploiting cognitive strengths to offset weaknesses:
concrete tools include a **comprehensive day-planner system as an externalized memory aid**, group
psychoeducation on cognitive strategies, home-environment modifications, and specific emotion-regulation
techniques. Notably, **physical exercise reduces postconcussive symptoms**, contradicting older excessive-rest
guidance — current understanding is that rest-based recovery was likely overapplied.
*Candidate application:* A day-planner/externalized-memory-aid habit and other deliberate compensatory routines
(writing things down, structured routines, avoiding overload) are concrete, *visible*, characterizable habits a
decade-plus out. A good character beat: his coping mechanisms (organization, particular routines) may read on
the surface as slightly rigid personality traits, with the underlying cause (managed TBI) staying implicit
unless a player digs into his backstory — a good fit for this project's soft-detail-delivery convention on
companion mysteries.

**123. Standard PTSD treatment remains safe and effective even with a TBI history — and helps both conditions
together.** Despite theoretical concern that PE/CPT rely on cognitive processes TBI might impair, **there is no
evidence CBT effectiveness or safety is reduced** for veterans with TBI history (even moderate/severe); benefits
extend beyond PTSD symptoms to improved general functioning **and reduced postconcussive symptoms** — evidence
for a bidirectional, mutually-maintaining relationship.
*Candidate application:* Supports writing his prior successful psychological trauma treatment as having
*also* measurably improved his cognitive/TBI-adjacent symptoms — his well-managed baseline exists specifically
*because* his PTSD was addressed at some point, not despite it being left untreated.

### Chapter 18, continued: Military Sexual Trauma (pp. 331–341) — betrayal by one's own institution

**129. MST is a stressor, not a diagnosis, and not automatically PTSD.** The authors insist the two terms are routinely and wrongly merged. Sexual harassment, which can by itself be the MST event, usually fails the DSM-5 "Criterion A" test (an event involving actual or threatened death or serious injury), so it cannot ground a PTSD diagnosis by itself. Even when an assault does meet Criterion A, PTSD still requires the full symptom picture, which has to be assessed separately. MST is better treated as a stressor that can produce a spread of outcomes (PTSD, depression, anxiety, and others). [331-332, 337-338] *Candidate application:* The defector's history, if it contains this kind of event, should not be shorthanded as "PTSD from X"; the aftermath could take the form of depression, avoidance, or distrust without a classic PTSD profile. A character who is simply "not diagnosed with anything" can still be carrying a stressor.

**130. How much more PTSD risk MST carries (the numbers).** (a) Surís et al. (2004): 270 care-seeking women veterans; 94 (35%) met DSM-IV PTSD criteria tied to their sexual assault on a structured interview. PTSD risk was nine times higher for those with military sexual assault than for women with no sexual-trauma history, and still about fourfold higher after adjusting for demographics and for childhood and civilian assault. (b) Yaeger et al. (2006): 196 women veterans; 72% had experienced some sexual assault in their lives; 80 (41% of the sample) had MST, and 48 of those 80 (60%) met PTSD criteria, a rate 2.5 times that of women without MST. (c) Creech & Orchowski (2016): MST survivors were more likely to have PTSD, and more severe symptoms, than survivors of other sexual trauma. (d) Kang et al. (2005): 1,381 Gulf War veterans with PTSD, using a self-report screen; both men and women reporting military sexual assault had more than fivefold PTSD risk, and the risk exceeded that from combat exposure. Caution: no study has yet compared PTSD rates after MST versus combat using a structured diagnostic interview. The authors read these results as sexual assault in service being more tightly linked to PTSD than sexual assault in civilian life. [331-332] *Candidate application:* A useful anchor for the idea that harm inflicted from inside one's own unit can outweigh harm from the enemy. Usable for any character or faction who was hurt by their own side.

**131. Why the military setting intensifies the injury.** Features the authors list as aggravating: weapons close at hand (which can make a threat credible), little free time to get medical or psychiatric care, sexualized workplaces, sexual harassment, close shared living quarters, and the presence or absence of backing from leadership. The perpetrator may be a coworker, supervisor, or higher-ranking person, and duty-station logistics can force repeated contact for months or years. Unit cohesion, normally protective, can suppress reporting because the person fears being shunned for "breaking the code" (a quoted phrase, Surís & Lind), which in turn removes the support the person would otherwise get. [332-333] *Candidate application:* The mechanism (a protective structure turning into a gag) is portable to any closed hierarchy in Tepenia: an Antarctic station crew, a ship, a robot work-collective, a faction compound where you cannot leave and cannot report. It may also help explain why a principled defector's loyalty to "the code" was already strained.

**132. Consequences across health, work and quality of life.** MST survivors are two to three times more likely to carry a psychiatric diagnosis (Kimerling et al., 2007). More common after MST: PTSD, major depression, suicidality, problematic alcohol use, eating disorders, somatization, anxiety disorders. Compared with civilian sexual assault survivors, MST survivors reported worse quality of life in family relationships, daily activities, and health satisfaction. Service women who reported MST reported more work difficulty from emotional and physical health problems and were at higher risk of demotion. Physical associations (correlational, so no causation claimed): more problems across nearly every body system, more cardiovascular risk factors (obesity, smoking, alcohol), liver disease and chronic lung disease linked to MST in both genders, obesity and hypothyroidism in women, a higher rate of sexually transmitted infection diagnoses. [333] *Candidate application:* Supports giving the defector body-level costs (chronic pain, poor health habits, a drinking or smoking pattern) that sit alongside, not inside, the psychological story. Suited to showing "functional but carrying it."

**133. Men with MST: scale, silence and the gender gap in care.** Men are about 83% of the force and 90% of veterans, and roughly ten times more men than women use the VA, yet most MST literature concerns women. Male military sexual assault is described as among the most underreported crimes. Reasons: stigma; beliefs that men should be able to protect themselves, that only gay men are raped, that only prison inmates are raped; and fear for unit standing and career. Male veterans use free MST treatment less than women and come later. Barriers they name: shame, self-blame, fear of not being believed, confusion about sexual orientation, masculinity stereotypes, ignorance that MST care exists. Clinicians report men who deny it repeatedly when asked and finally admit it only after being asked many times. [334-335] *Candidate application:* If the defector is male, a past he has denied on every intake form is realistic; the "asked many times, denied many times" detail would suit a companion who deflects a particular conversation for a long time before trusting the player.

**134. Men with MST: the self-image injury and its behavioral aftershocks.** Per the clinicians: men feel shame at having failed to defend themselves despite training to fight and kill, and see themselves as less than a man and less than a soldier or Marine. Some become confused about their own sexual orientation and wonder whether they were targeted because they must be gay; some test this and end up more confused; others become fiercely hostile to gay men even when shown evidence against that belief, and may refuse group treatment if gay MST survivors are present. Intimacy and sexual function are affected; some men turn to promiscuity or infidelity "to prove" manhood, then regret it, and blame the assault for relationship damage while the clinicians note the choices were theirs. O'Brien et al. (2015) frame all this through male rape myths made stronger by a culture of toughness, aggression and emotional control. [335-336] *Candidate application:* Offers a quiet, concrete shape for unresolved shame that expresses itself as prejudice, a failed relationship, or overcompensation. Flag: handle soberly; usable for any male-coded or robot character shaped by a hyper-masculine or "never be the victim" culture.

**135. Do men fare worse? The mixed evidence.** O'Brien et al. (2008): 175 people (17% men, 83% women) in a 7-week residential MST program; men reported more trauma symptoms at intake, equal levels of difficulty identifying feelings (alexithymia, which predicted persistence of symptoms), and improved less (effect size 0.53 for men vs 0.74 for women); men had more persistent sexual problems; men and women did not differ on depression, anxiety, dissociation or sleep disturbance. Shipherd et al. (2009), Marines at basic training and 21 months later: the same level of MST was "particularly detrimental" to men; more frequent MST tracked with worse perceived physical health in men, but less frequent MST with worse health perception in women. Mondragon et al. (2015), OEF/OIF men: MST linked to mood disorder, poorer post-deployment social support and perceived emotional mistreatment, but not to loss of romantic relationships, other diagnoses, or worse job functioning (and most of the sample's MST was harassment). The authors speculate that unpredictability and uncontrollability (Foa et al., 1992) and the male-prowess culture may make outcomes worse for men, and say more research is needed. [334-336] *Candidate application:* A balanced note: aftermath is not uniform. Different characters can have different mixes of social, physical, and mood costs.

**136. Reporting systems and the screening question.** The Department of Defense introduced a two-tier reporting system in 2005. Unrestricted report: goes to law enforcement and the survivor's commanding officer is notified. Restricted report: anonymous, with medical and support services, but no legal action possible. Sexual assault response coordinators and victim advocates were later established to coordinate care and provide crisis help and nonclinical support. In the VA, screening of all veterans, men and women, was mandated by law (Public Law 103-452), the software reminder dates to 2002, and the screen was reworded in 2014 to match the federal definition. The two questions ask (1) whether the person ever received unwanted, threatening or repeated sexual attention in service, and (2) whether sexual contact happened against their will or when they could not say no. A positive screen does not by itself mean treatment is needed (many are resilient), and untrained or non-mental-health staff giving a one-time screen without follow-up can inflate positives. [336-337, 340-341] *Candidate application:* The restricted/unrestricted split is a model for institutional design that forces a victim to choose between being safe and being believed; a similar dilemma could exist in a Tepenian armed service or exile system.

**137. Treatment: what is offered, what works, and who drops out.** Treatment of MST is free and legally mandated in the VA (with contracted outside care if VA care is not timely or reachable, and even for veterans otherwise ineligible); same-sex providers are encouraged, but many survivors do not actually want that, so preference should be asked. Men's MST-PTSD treatment is recommended separately from combat-PTSD groups, because sexual trauma needs its own processing and because of stigma. Treatment follows the symptoms: cognitive behavioral therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy or interpersonal therapy for depression and anxiety; cognitive processing therapy (CPT) or prolonged exposure (PE) for PTSD. Evidence: Surís et al. (2013), the only RCT specific to MST-PTSD, 86 men and women, CPT vs present-centered therapy (PCT, a non-trauma-focused comparison): both reduced PTSD and depression with moderate to large effect sizes, CPT significantly more on self-reported PTSD. Schnurr et al. (2007): PE beat PCT in women veterans, 69.5% of the exposure group with MST history, an average of 10 traumas each. Eftekhari et al. (2013), national PE rollout: MST-PTSD improved as much as other trauma types, but MST-PTSD patients dropped out of PE more often. Mullen et al. (2014): 11 men in a CPT trial improved on clinician and self-report PTSD and self-report depression, gains held at 6 months. [337-339] *Candidate application:* Realistic texture for how a character could have attempted therapy and left (higher dropout). A flag: nothing here makes treatment a clean fix; the defector may have started and stopped.

**138. Barriers to treatment.** Both men and women in qualitative studies (Turchik et al., 2013, 2014) named avoidance (not wanting to talk about it), stigma (shame, self-blame, fear of how a provider will react), gender discomfort (a male provider in a male-dominated setting), and not knowing MST care exists. Holland et al. (2016), 25,505 active-duty members including 542 recent MST survivors: survivors perceived more barriers of both logistical and stigma kinds than non-survivors. [339] *Candidate application:* The pattern (many barriers, each reasonable) explains why a long-out veteran could have gone a decade without help while seeming fine.

**139. Betrayal, career cost, and adjustment after service (institutional-harm threads).** Treatments built for combat PTSD (guilt over killing) may not fit MST, where betrayal and sexual identity are more central; the general tools (examining fear memories, balancing distorted beliefs) may carry over but must be adapted. Veterans told the authors they had to choose between pursuing justice and keeping their careers; military conditions (stigma, career consequences) compound the injury and suppress reporting (Valente & Wight, 2007). Women who experienced harassment or assault felt isolated and lonely in service, were twice as likely to report difficulty adjusting to civilian life, and had harder employment searches, more anxiety, depression, anger, substance misuse and poorer quality of life. Gaps named: few trials, mostly female samples, nothing on non-PTSD MST outcomes, comorbidity ignored, men's outcomes possibly worse, treatment-access gaps for men. [339-341] *Candidate application:* The justice-versus-career trade, and "betrayal rather than fear" as the core wound, can be lifted for a defector whose principled break followed an institution punishing the person who reported rather than the one who offended. Any faction or character: wrongs by one's own institution, with the institution holding the power to reward silence.

### Chapter 19: Sleep Disorders (Brim & Riggs, pp. 345–359) — texture for daily life

**140. Why sleep matters here: it may be a cause, not just a symptom.** Sleep disturbance is argued to be a core feature of PTSD and possibly a mechanism in its development. Of people who develop PTSD, 70-91% report long-standing sleep problems, shown both by self-report and objectively poorer sleep. Among comorbid conditions, sleep disorders do the most damage to quality of life and community integration. [345] *Candidate application:* Gives a principled reason to make the defector's sleep a load-bearing trait, not decoration. Cross-applies to any traumatized character.

**141. Military service is itself hostile to sleep.** "War and sleep remain incompatible." Service members, especially deployed and combat-exposed ones, are at high risk of insufficient sleep (defined as an average of 6 hours or less). Insufficient sleep (restricted opportunity, with intact ability to sleep) differs from insomnia (cannot sleep despite opportunity). Causes: deployments, frequent moves, family separation, early starts, long hours, shift work. Only about one-quarter to one-third of service members get the recommended adult amount. A RAND survey (Troxel et al., 2015) of 1,957 members across four branches found high rates of short sleep, poor quality, daytime sleepiness and fatigue, with 18% using sleep medication; deployed and non-deployed did not differ much, suggesting service itself is the driver, though combat deployment raised poor sleep, PTSD and trauma nightmares. Poor sleep is prospectively tied to cardiovascular disease, obesity, depression, suicide, and PTSD, and is a symptom of PTSD, depression, substance misuse and traumatic brain injury, which can also precipitate those conditions and raise suicide risk. [346-347] *Candidate application:* Makes "sleeps badly after a decade out" an expected, institutionally built habit rather than a quirk. For Tepenia: shift-work stations, polar day/night, and crew rotation are the same kind of structural sleep-restrictors; robots on duty cycles could share it.

**142. DSM-5 drops "primary vs. secondary" insomnia.** Sleep disorders now get diagnosed and treated independently of any coexisting medical or psychiatric condition, because they usually coexist with those conditions, interact in both directions, and often do not get better when only the other disorder is treated. [347] *Candidate application:* A character could be told "your sleep will fix itself when the rest does" and be wrong; mirrors robot maintenance logic where a fault cannot be assumed to be a symptom of a different fault.

**143. Insomnia disorder: the DSM-5 definition and why it matters for soldiers.** Frequency of at least 3 nights per week, for at least 3 months, and, critically, "despite adequate opportunities for sleep." That clause likely rules out diagnosing insomnia during most deployments, since there was no adequate opportunity; sleeplessness in theater is treated as a precipitating stressor, and the authors suggest managing it aggressively without the insomnia label, since the label itself could predispose to later insomnia. [347-348] *Candidate application:* A distinction a character-writer can exploit: the defector's problem began as "no time to sleep" and became "can't sleep when I finally can" after he left.

**144. How common insomnia is in the military.** Civilian insomnia: 9-15% of Americans. Up to half of recently deployed members may have it; it is the most prevalent military sleep disorder. Military incidence rose from 7.2 to 135.8 per 10,000 person-years between 2000 and 2009 (a 1,786% rise). Taylor et al. (2016): 19.9% of an Army sample before deployment. Caldwell et al. (2017): a three- to sevenfold rise in insomnia encounters per 1,000 members from 2005 to 2014, largest among senior enlisted. Klingaman et al. (2018): nearly a quarter of a large representative active-duty Army sample met criteria regardless of deployment history. [348] *Candidate application:* Establishes insomnia as a baseline, not an exception, for a military-trained person.

**145. Sleep apnea (OSA) in the military and its tie to PTSD.** DSM-5 splits breathing-related sleep disorders into obstructive sleep apnea/hypopnea, central sleep apnea, and sleep-related hypoventilation. OSA can now be diagnosed from 15 or more respiratory events on polysomnography alone. OSA is a partial or complete airway blockage during sleep (relaxed throat muscles, tongue), with pauses in breathing or shallow breaths 15 or more times an hour; it can only be definitively diagnosed by a sleep study; onset rises from middle age; it is serious, potentially fatal, and highly treatable, and providers should refer suspected cases readily. Civilian prevalence is about 2-7%. In the military, apnea-related medical encounters rose 517% from 2005 to 2014, men over 40 contributing most. Mysliwiec et al. (2013): among military personnel with sleep disturbance referred within 18 months of combat return, 38% had both insomnia and OSA, and they were more likely to have depression and PTSD than controls. Sleep-disordered breathing has been proposed as a risk factor for PTSD; theories name fragmented REM sleep, raised noradrenergic tone, and dysregulated central nervous system stress response (mechanism unconfirmed). [348-349] *Candidate application:* For a middle-aged, ex-service, chronically injured man, undiagnosed apnea (snoring, gasping, fatigue misread as emotional exhaustion) is concrete, fixable, and morally interesting: part of what looks like "the trauma" is a treatable body problem. In Tepenia: cold, altitude (Kunlun, Dome Fuji) and heavy-set frames would interact with breathing in sleep.

**146. Nightmares: how common, and DSM-5 versus lived reality.** DSM-5 nightmare disorder: repeated awakenings with detailed recall of dysphoric dreams, typically in the second half of the night (REM-rich, little movement), with quick orientation on waking, and not occurring solely during another mental disorder such as PTSD. In civilians nearly everyone has had nightmares, 10-30% have them monthly, and 2-6% have severe, near-nightly ones. In trauma survivors, reported rates range from 19% to 96% depending on definitions and populations. Wallace et al. (2011), OEF/OIF veterans, all with insomnia, evaluated with sleep studies, wrist actigraphy and sleep logs within 35 months of combat return: nightmares in 89% of those with PTSD and 80% of those with PTSD plus TBI. [349-350] *Candidate application:* Nightmares are common enough to be background for the defector, but note insomnia, not nightmares, tends to be what remains (see Finding 155).

**147. Trauma-associated sleep disorder (proposed).** Because DSM nightmare criteria do not fit the patient complaint, a diagnosis for study has been proposed. Features: onset after a trauma; dream content specifically about the trauma; motor activity during the event (suggesting it is not simple REM, or that REM is altered); occurring in the earlier half of the sleep period. Patients describe these nightmares as more severe and distressing than idiopathic ones. (Criteria in Mysliwiec et al., 2018.) [350] *Candidate application:* The strongest hook the chapter offers for "trauma-related sleep behaviors": thrashing or movement early in the night, a partner or companion noticing, and a camp scene in which the player sees it. Flag: the chapter says no more about the behaviors (e.g., sleep talking, striking out), so any extra detail is outside this source.

**148. Assessment: the sleep history and the sleep log.** Clinicians take a functional analysis (antecedents, behaviors, consequences, development over time) and a "typical day and night": eating, exercise, stress, naps, caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, drugs, medications, bedroom environment, bedtime behaviors such as reading or television in bed, and routines; they also screen for breathing disorders, periodic limb movements, restless legs, circadian rhythm disorders, parasomnias and narcolepsy. Named interviews: Spielman and Anderson's diagnostic interview, Morin's Insomnia Interview Schedule. The sleep log (2 weeks is standard) records time in bed, sleep-onset latency, number and length of awakenings, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency (total sleep time divided by time in bed, as a percentage); CBT-i Coach is one smartphone app. [350-351] *Candidate application:* The ritualized bedtime inventory can serve as characterization (the defector's arrangements: no screens, one lamp, back to the wall) and as a robot-analogue: a rest-cycle log for an android.

**149. Assessment instruments by name.** Psychiatric: Beck Depression Inventory, Beck Anxiety Inventory. Health and pain: SF-36 Short Form Survey, Multidimensional Pain Inventory. Sleep and fatigue: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (global sleep disturbance), Insomnia Severity Index (severity), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (daytime sleepiness). Nightmares: Nightmare Effects Survey (impairment), Nightmare Distress Questionnaire (distress). Together with a thorough history they indicate whether referral is needed for undiagnosed psychiatric or unstable medical issues, or for conditions needing medical plus behavioral care (OSA, limb-movement disorders, narcolepsy). [351-352] *Candidate application:* The pain-instrument inclusion is a reminder that chronic injury and sleep are assessed together; relevant to the defector's chronic injury.

**150. Objective testing: polysomnography and actigraphy.** Polysomnography (usually lab-based, sometimes home units) records brain waves (EEG), eye movements (EOG), chin and shin muscle activity (EMG), heart rhythm (ECG), breathing effort, airflow, and blood oxygen; it is the gold standard and should be done if there are signs of OSA, narcolepsy, or movement disorders such as periodic limb movements, restless legs, or REM sleep behavior disorder. Actigraphy is a wrist-worn movement sensor, inexpensive and good for tracking circadian problems over time. [352] *Candidate application:* Wrist-worn movement logging could be a plausible piece of kit for a character who does not trust his own account of how he sleeps.

**151. Treatment overview: behavioral beats drugs in durability.** Drug and non-drug treatments for insomnia have comparable moderate-to-large effects in civilians, but non-drug gains persist after treatment ends. CBT-I is well established; in PTSD populations it is newer. Early studies of veterans and service members with PTSD and insomnia show CBT-I reduces disruptive nighttime behaviors and improves subjective sleep and work and interpersonal functioning (Talbot et al., 2014). For PTSD with nightmares, adding imagery rehearsal therapy (IRT) strengthens the response, improving sleep efficiency and reducing PTSD symptoms. [352-353] *Candidate application:* A plausible "what a good treatment would have been" for a defector who never got any.

**152. Medication stance (prazosin only).** The chapter's pharmacology note is brief: medication is an option, may be a patient's preference, and should be an adjunct to CBT-I or used if the patient declines, or CBT-I fails or is unavailable. Only prazosin, an adrenergic-inhibiting medication normally used for blood pressure, has multiple randomized trials suggesting effectiveness for PTSD-related sleep disturbance; the authors point to Lipinska et al. (2016) for more. The chapter names no other drug classes and gives no dosing or mechanism. [353] *Candidate application:* Limited detail; usable only as "a blood-pressure pill that was repurposed for nightmares." Any richer medication detail would need another source.

**153. The three-factor model of insomnia.** Spielman et al. (1987) describe a diathesis-stress model: predisposing factors (baseline hyperarousal, tendency to worry), precipitating factors (illness, psychological stress that triggers acute insomnia), and perpetuating factors (excess time in bed, napping), the last of which turn acute into chronic insomnia and are the main target of treatment. [353] *Candidate application:* A neat template for writing a sleeper: the predisposition (a worrier), the precipitating event (the break with the service), and the perpetuating habits (lying in bed for hours, daytime naps). Possible robot echo: a stuck rest-cycle maintained by its own workaround.

**154. Sleep hygiene and stimulus control (the rules).** Sleep hygiene is education, not a stand-alone treatment, but supports CBT-I and relapse prevention. Stimulus control is the first-line insomnia intervention: (1) go to bed only when sleepy and intending to sleep; (2) keep a fixed wake time, weekends included; (3) nothing in bed or bedroom but sleep and sex; (4) leave the bed and room if awake more than 15 minutes; (5) return only when sleepy; repeat steps 4-5 as often as needed. Patients "chase" sleep by lying in bed reading or watching TV, which works occasionally and so is reinforced, but links the bedroom with wakefulness. For PTSD specifically, the bedroom becomes a place of dread; patients commonly sleep fine elsewhere in the house or in the daytime but not in their own bed at night. [354] *Candidate application:* A strong image for the defector: sleeps in a chair, the vehicle, the doorway, or anywhere but the bed. It reads as habit, not weakness. For a robot: a recharging dock that has become associated with a bad event.

**155. Sleep restriction therapy (the arithmetic).** Time in bed is limited to the patient's average total sleep time, from the diary, with a fixed wake time. Example: 5 hours average sleep and a 6 a.m. wake time means no bed until 1 a.m. The mild sleep deprivation shortens sleep-onset time and reduces awakenings; once sleep efficiency reaches 85-90%, bedtime is moved earlier in 15-minute steps. [354-355] *Candidate application:* An ordeal-like routine that a stubborn ex-soldier might actually endure with discipline; a good "small scene" of him following rules rigorously about sleep that he cannot follow about anything else.

**156. Cognitive therapy for insomnia, "worry time," and brief versions.** For people who ruminate or fear the consequences of insomnia: correct unrealistic sleep expectations, false attributions and catastrophizing (named patterns: faulty appraisal of transient problems, misattributing all daytime impairment to sleep, unrealistic needs, rumination, magnification, catastrophizing, selective recall). Tools: thought records, testing beliefs, alternatives, education about normal variation and age-related change. "Worry time" of 15-20 minutes daily for planning and worry reduces doing it in bed. A full CBT-I package is 4-8 sessions, deliverable individually, in groups, in person, by telehealth or website. Brief behavioral therapy for insomnia (fewer than five sessions; stimulus control plus modified sleep restriction) reduces sleep-onset time and wake-after-onset in civilians and military, and can be delivered by medical staff in primary care without mental-health specialty. [355-356] *Candidate application:* A defector who schedules his worrying (a fixed daily time to go over decisions) makes a vivid, characterful habit; also plausible as a thing a companion teaches or teases.

**157. Nightmares versus insomnia after PTSD treatment.** Nightmares are a common complaint and can be the main reason a person seeks PTSD treatment. After evidence-based PTSD treatment, however, insomnia tends to persist more than nightmares. Pruiksma et al. (2016): insomnia in 92% at baseline and 74-80% afterward; nightmares in 69% at baseline and only 13% afterward. So nightmares often resolve with PTSD treatment while insomnia does not; CBT-I can also reduce nightmares, and nightmare-specific therapy is generally supplementary. [356] *Candidate application:* This is exactly the shape of a "functional but still carrying it" person: the loud symptom (nightmares) is gone, the quiet one (not sleeping) remains.

**158. Imagery rehearsal and exposure-based nightmare therapies (components and order).** For nightmares resistant to PTSD treatment and CBT-I: key components are (1) sleep education; (2) writing out the dream and changing some part of it; (3) relaxation; and for imagery therapy (4) daily rehearsal of the rewritten dream. Exposure therapies (e.g., exposure, relaxation and rescripting therapy, ERRT) include daily exposure by reading the dream narrative; imagery therapies replace that with imagining the new version daily. Rescripting is the core; the hypothesis is that making a less distressing version builds mastery and a corrective learning experience in a safe setting. IRT is typically 3-4 sessions. Nappi et al. (2010) chart review of veterans of several eras: significant reductions in nightmare frequency and intensity, insomnia severity and PTSD symptoms after a full course of five IRT sessions. ERRT trials beat wait-list on PTSD symptoms, sleep quality, nightmare frequency, and heart rate (Davis et al., 2011); a dismantling study (Pruiksma et al., 2016) found improvements in nightmares, sleep quality, fear of sleep, PTSD and depression severity even without the exposure and rescripting parts. [356-357] *Candidate application:* The idea of rewriting a recurring nightmare's ending and rehearsing the new ending could be a quest-adjacent character beat, or a "memory-edit" parallel for a robot whose recurring process loop gets rewritten. Flag: "fear of sleep" is a named outcome measure and can be a trait of its own.

**159. Treatment order: PTSD first, CBT-I first, or together.** No balanced randomized study has settled this. PTSD first: sleep problems follow from trauma symptoms and may ease as they do; treating sleep first can be followed by symptom flare (more nightmares and poor sleep) once PTSD work begins. CBT-I first: good sleep supports learning and memory consolidation, chronic poor sleep fuels hyperarousal through amygdala activation, CBT-I is less threatening and builds rapport, and dropout is lower (PTSD therapies like prolonged exposure and CPT 20-25%, CBT-I as low as 13%), making it a "gateway" therapy. Concurrent: potential synergy and facilitated emotional processing, but heavy, sometimes contradictory homework and appointments (possibly several per week for months, possibly several therapists). Guidance: choose by what the patient cares about most, motivation and history; someone mainly worried about sleep or unwilling to start trauma work can begin with CBT-I. [357-359] *Candidate application:* A realistic reason a wary character agrees to "fix my sleep" long before they would agree to "talk about it." Cross-applicable to any wary NPC in a trust-building arc.

**160. Consequences of chronic insomnia and operational risk.** People with insomnia report more health problems, more hospitalization, more limited work capacity, mood and motivation changes, poor attention and concentration, recent-memory loss, and slow variable responses; higher risk of on-the-job and motor-vehicle accidents. In dangerous duties the effects magnify: lapses in routines, lost situational awareness, lack of insight into one's own impairment, and failed verbal communication, leading to mission failure, aviation mishaps, and "friendly fire" accidents. The authors conclude sleep problems not only worsen and maintain PTSD but contribute to causing it. Sleep is described as a resilience factor often ignored; barriers (operational, environmental, knowledge, treatment) and a shortage of trained providers need addressing, with sleep screened in every patient. [359] *Candidate application:* "Lack of insight into impairment" is a useful detail: a tired character who insists he is fine, with a mistake or near-miss that the player can notice. Robot echo: a unit that reports "nominal" while its rest-state diagnostics are degraded.

**Cross-applicability — robot "rest" echoes (flagged, not canon):**

- Insufficient sleep (no opportunity) versus insomnia (no ability despite opportunity) maps to a robot with no downtime versus a robot whose downtime does not restore it. [Finding 141, Finding 143]
- Stimulus control (the bed associated with fear) maps to a charging dock or rest bay that has become linked to a bad event; the unit functions anywhere but there. [Finding 154]
- "Perpetuating factors" maintaining a fault after the cause is gone maps to a self-installed workaround that outlasts the problem. [Finding 153]
- Imagery rescripting (rewriting a loop's ending and rehearsing it) maps to a deliberate rewrite of a recurring routine. [Finding 158]
- Not knowing one's own impairment maps to a unit reporting nominal status while degraded. [Finding 160]

**Takeaways for the defector (Ch.18 rest and Ch.19):**
- Make the nightmares quiet and the insomnia persistent: after real PTSD care (or on his own), the nightmares fade and the not-sleeping stays; he sleeps anywhere but the bed. [Finding 154, Finding 157]
- Ten-plus years out, a middle-aged man with chronic injury plausibly has undiagnosed sleep apnea and pain-disrupted sleep, a treatable body problem layered on the psychological one. [Finding 145, Finding 149]
- Trust-building route: he would accept "fix my sleep" help (a bedtime routine, worry time, fixed wake time) long before talk therapy, and sleep treatment has lower dropout. [Finding 156, Finding 159]
- If his break with the service involved harm from his own chain of command, the core wound is betrayal and the cost of reporting (justice versus career), not fear, and cohesion that silenced him is part of the injury. [Finding 131, Finding 139]
- Reticence is realistic: men deny repeatedly, come late to care, and carry shame and self-image injury; someone can function and still have never said it aloud. [Finding 133, Finding 134, Finding 138]
- Avoid over-claiming: the source says aftermath varies, that MST is not equal to PTSD, and that treatment helps but leaves residue and dropouts. [Finding 129, Finding 135, Finding 137]

### Chapter 20: Managing the Service Member with Suicidal Ideation (Skopp, Luxton, Bush, Rudd, pp. 364–377)

*Handled as sober risk-structure reference: method and lethality detail is recorded at the level of principle only.
The chapter is thinner than its title suggests — it has no interpersonal-theory constructs, no named assessment
instruments, no safety-plan step list and no vignette. Its single most defector-relevant passage is the one on
separation from service as a high-risk transition (Finding 183).*

**161. Framing: PTSD predicts the move from thinking to attempting.** [p.364] The authors open from the position that PTSD is widely linked to suicidal behavior. Ideation is defined as thoughts of engaging in suicide-related behavior. Ideation tends to precede later self-harm, both planned and unplanned attempts, and PTSD is described as a strong predictor of which people progress from ideation to an attempt. Hence the stated duty: clinicians treating service members must understand, assess and keep monitoring ideation. The chapter's structure: military suicide, risk factors, clinical management guidance, gaps, and a closing section on separation and civilian reintegration.
*Candidate application:* For the defector, ideation is best treated as a background condition that PTSD makes more consequential rather than a plot event; what matters is the trajectory from thought to action, which makes watching for change more important than a one-time declaration. Cross-applicable to any trauma-carrying character in any faction.

**162. Military suicide rates: lower, then doubled, then plateaued; deployment itself is not the direct cause.** [pp.364-365] Historical pattern given: US service member suicide in 1990-2000 ran about 20% below the civilian rate. From 2001 to 2011 the active-duty rate nearly doubled. The 2015 DoD Suicide Event Report (DoDSER; 2014 data year) found no change since 2011: the rate had stayed flat and did not differ significantly from the US general-population rate. The rise coincided with the Afghanistan/Iraq wars (OEF/OIF) and was speculated to be war-driven, yet population research the authors cite indicates that deploying to those wars is not directly associated with suicide risk.
*Candidate application:* A key realism point: the defector's risk need not be explained by combat exposure alone; combat exposure and risk are not a simple line. Also supports a design stance that a veteran population can sit at an ordinary-looking baseline rate while individuals within it are at real risk. Cross-applicable to any setting's veteran population.

**163. PTSD and suicide: evidence base and the role of comorbid depression.** [p.365] Large literature ties PTSD to suicidal ideation and behavior. A meta-analysis of 59 studies (Panagioti et al., 2012) links current and lifetime PTSD to suicidality across many trauma types; comorbid depression significantly raises suicidal behavior in PTSD populations, a finding repeated in several veteran studies. A national US veteran sample found PTSD associated with higher ideation and attempt risk. One community-veteran study found the PTSD-to-suicidal-behavior link was partly carried (mediated) by depression and by internal hostility. Veterans with both PTSD and alcohol use disorder were significantly more likely to have attempted than veterans with PTSD alone.
*Candidate application:* Gives a mechanism-level template: PTSD is dangerous mainly through depression, anger turned inward, and drinking, not as a free-standing driver. A functional-but-carrying defector with a drinking habit and a lot of self-directed anger would be realistically higher risk than one with intrusion symptoms alone. Cross-applicable to any character.

**164. Emotions that carry PTSD into suicidal thinking: guilt, shame, anxiety sensitivity.** [p.365] Additional explanatory factors named for the PTSD-ideation link: guilt, shame, and anxiety sensitivity (fear of one's own anxious bodily sensations). The reference list shows the specific findings: shame mediating PTSD symptoms and ideation in veterans, and trauma-related guilt doing the same in OEF/OIF/OND veterans. (Reference-list titles used only to identify the constructs.)
*Candidate application:* A defector who left on principle may carry principled guilt or shame (what was done, what was taken part in, who was left behind or betrayed) that works as a risk channel even when his decision felt right. Candidate, fits the "moral weight" side of the character. Cross-applicable to faction members who participated in atrocities.

**165. Psychosocial stressors escalate risk and track deterioration.** [pp.365-366] Dissatisfaction with finances, social life and living conditions fed PTSD deterioration and worsening ideation and behavior in a combat-veteran study. The 2015 DoDSER reported that the most common problems among service members who attempted were relationship, administrative/legal, and workplace difficulties. Narrative data from two large surveillance systems showed soldiers who died by suicide usually faced several high-stress problems at once together with coping and emotion regulation difficulties. The authors tie this to evidence that emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility strongly influence suicidal behavior, so coping and regulation skills become prevention targets.
*Candidate application:* The pattern is pile-up, not single catastrophe: money trouble, legal exposure from desertion or defection, workplace failure and a broken relationship arriving together. For a defector, legal and administrative jeopardy is built in, so it is a natural chronic stressor. A lone bad day matters less than convergence of several problems plus poor regulation. Cross-applicable to exiles, refugees and any faction with an enforcement apparatus.

**166. General risk and protective factors (VA/DoD working group framing).** [p.366] Risk and protective factors are defined as statistically demonstrated increasers or buffers of a given outcome. Modifiable risk factors listed: psychological (suicide bereavement, loss of a loved one, loss of status, respect or rank), social (relationship dissolution, job loss, eviction, assault), chronic stressors (finances, legal problems, poor support), mental health problems, and medical conditions. Protective: social support and strong problem-solving and coping skills. In 978 combat-exposed veterans, family stress went with more PTSD, depression and ideation, and family support went the other way, suggesting family as a buffer.
*Candidate application:* Two items from this list map directly onto the defector: loss of status/respect/rank (the defection forfeits all of it) and medical conditions (a chronic injury). Support is the main buffer; a companion who is actually a stable connection is therefore mechanically meaningful. Cross-applicable.

**167. Military-specific risk factors.** [pp.366-367] Demographic profile of US service member decedents (2015 DoDSER): male, white, under 24, married, and mostly junior enlisted. Service-related factors: deployment to war zones and combat exposure can raise PTSD and suicide risk, and ready access to firearms in military life is a recognized risk factor (principle only: easy availability of a lethal means is itself a risk factor).
*Candidate application:* The profile is a reminder that the highest-risk military demographic is young; a 10-15-years-out defector is outside the peak group but still carries service-specific exposures. Cross-applicable: any faction culture that normalizes weapon access has the same structural exposure.

**168. Stigma, concealment and the help-seeking barrier.** [p.367] Many service members avoid mental health care because of perceived stigma for admitting a problem and seeking treatment. Perceived stigma plus emotion regulation difficulty (common in PTSD) may itself intensify suicide risk. Clinicians are warned that patients may hide or play down suicidal thoughts out of stigma or to get released from care. Reducing stigma is called central to the military prevention mission.
*Candidate application:* The defector plausibly under-reports. Realistic dialogue would have him deflect, joke, or say he is fine while the evidence points elsewhere. Concealment to avoid being "held" or sidelined is a believable motive for a functional person. Cross-applicable to any character from a culture that prizes stoicism.

**169. Reserve, Guard and civilian-return gap.** [p.367] Military-tailored suicide prevention and treatment is increasingly available to active-duty members but much less so for National Guard and Reserve personnel. Those returning to civilian life usually see local civilian providers who seldom have training in post-deployment problems, so symptoms may be recognized late or insufficiently, leaving them especially vulnerable to escalation.
*Candidate application:* An ex-military person living outside the institution that understood him is exactly this vulnerability: a defector has no access to any military care system and perhaps no trusted civilian equivalent. Candidate worldbuilding point: in Tepenia, which care infrastructure exists for people who left a service? Cross-applicable to all discharged, deserted or exiled personnel.

**170. Assessment is hard; no single standard of care.** [pp.367-368] The authors list structural difficulties in suicide assessment: legal issues, inability to predict suicide, judgment about when hospitalization is warranted, lack of empirically and theoretically grounded assessment models, and the life-and-death stakes. No single standard of care exists. The VA/DoD guideline promotes a "recovery-oriented" model, with patient preferences and involvement considered paramount.
*Candidate application:* Design implication: do not give the game a clean "suicide risk meter." Risk is uncertain even to professionals, which can be dramatized by having companions and the player unable to predict. Cross-applicable.

**171. The VA/DoD 2013 guideline: three-tier stratification and three algorithms.** [pp.368-369] Because validated predictive models do not exist, the guideline sorts people into three tiers: (1) those needing immediate intervention to prevent suicide, (2) those at elevated risk for future suicidal behavior who need clinical intervention, and (3) those not at elevated risk who might still benefit from intervention. It is organized as three clinical algorithms: primary care assessment and management; behavioral health provider evaluation and management; management of patients at high acute risk. Risk assessment is described as continuous because risk is dynamic, shaped by affective states, life events, and the interplay of risk and protective factors. Anyone being treated for a condition like PTSD should be asked about ideation.
*Candidate application:* The "dynamic, not trait" idea is the useful one: the same character can be at low risk in one scene and elevated after a triggering event (a loss, a revelation, an anniversary). Cross-applicable.

**172. The six assessment recommendations: warning signs, risk and protective factors, reassessment.** [p.369] (1) Evaluate thoughts, intent, behavior and warning signs. Three direct warning signs indicate greatest likelihood of imminent action: suicidal communication (writing, saying, or threatening); seeking or recently using lethal means; and preparations (expressed intent or steps toward a plan, arranging care for dependents such as children, pets or elders, updating wills, settling bills, saying goodbye). Additional monitored warning signs: substance abuse, hopelessness, purposelessness, anger, recklessness, feeling trapped, social withdrawal, anxiety, mood changes, sleep disturbance, guilt, shame. (2) Evaluate risk and protective factors, including reasons for living. Protective items named: strong bonds to family or unit, community support, employment, intact marriage, responsibility for children or others, stable and safe environment; positive personal traits (help-seeking, impulse control, problem-solving, coping and conflict resolution skills, belonging and identity, self-esteem, cultural, spiritual or religious beliefs about life's meaning, optimism and future goals, constructive leisure); and access to health care. (3) Use warning signs and thoughts to decide on referral to a higher level of care. (4) Reassess regularly, especially after circumstances change. (5) Observe behavior during interview; disconnection or lack of rapport may signal increased risk. (6) Keep an empathetic, objective posture; nonjudgmental approach elicits the most reliable information and lets the patient accept help.
*Candidate application:* This is the most directly reusable list. "Putting affairs in order" behaviors (settling obligations, giving possessions away, goodbyes) are well-known realistic tells a companion could notice in a character; the protective list offers ready-made "reasons for living" hooks (responsibility for others, future goals, meaning-making belief systems). Cross-applicable to any character in any setting; flag for a companion-perception system.

**173. Instrument stance: tools support, never replace, clinical judgment.** [pp.369-370] The standardized framework should estimate risk, support decisions, set level of intervention and referral, allow monitoring over time, ground documentation, and permit consistent data collection. The working group states that assessment should not rest on a single instrument; rating scales cannot replace a trained clinician who can draw out candid disclosure; collateral information from patient, family and partners is best; physical and psychiatric exam belongs in the assessment; and evidence is insufficient to recommend specific measures. (No instrument is named in the prose besides the CAMS Suicide Status Form, see Finding 177.)
*Candidate application:* Reinforces avoiding a numeric "mental health stat" for the character; the faithful representation is qualitative and relational. Cross-applicable.

**174. Emergency presentation and the post-discharge danger window.** [pp.370-371] Emergency-department principles: pick the assessment setting carefully, secure the person's belongings, observe in a visible place away from exits with limited access to objects, do a focused medical assessment for life-threatening conditions (including intoxication or overdose), and request a mental health consultation. Psychiatric patients in busy EDs are at risk of being triaged low-acuity. Key point: admission for inpatient psychiatric care is among the strongest predictors of later death by suicide, so immediate and continuing follow-up after discharge matters. Documentation of services, content, recommendations and compliance is advised.
*Candidate application:* The structural lesson is that the period right after a crisis or a hospital stay is dangerous, not safe: a character released from care or "stabilized" at a story beat should still be treated as vulnerable. Cross-applicable.

**175. What the evidence says about treatment, and what Rudd and colleagues concluded.** [pp.371-372] The working group screened about 16,500 post-2005 studies and found only 35 relevant RCTs and 38 systematic reviews, concluding that evidence for any intervention to prevent suicide and attempts is strongly lacking. Its guidance: give suicidal patients optimal evidence-based treatment for any underlying mental health condition, with "at least fair" evidence for Cognitive Therapy for suicide prevention and Problem-Solving Therapy (a skills-training approach) when indicated. Little of the literature addresses service members' needs. Rudd, Joiner, Jobes and King (1999) concluded: (1) intensive follow-up after an attempt works for high-risk patients (multiple attempts, psychiatric histories, comorbidities); (2) brief CBT with problem-solving at its core reduces ideation, depression and hopelessness for up to a year, but brief approaches do not seem to reduce attempts over the long run; (3) reducing attempts needs longer treatment targeting specific skill deficits such as anger regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal assertiveness, relationships and self-image; (4) high-risk patients can be treated as outpatients if acute hospitalization is available and accessible.
*Candidate application:* Realism guidance: help that is short and skill-based eases the surface (mood, hopelessness) but durable change takes sustained work on anger, relationships and self-image. A single good conversation should not "cure" the defector. Cross-applicable.

**176. Treatment targeting and collaboration.** [p.372] Interventions should address modifiable potentiating factors found in ongoing assessment, weigh benefits and harms, and be collaborative, involving relevant collaterals with consent. For service members with PTSD, treat PTSD itself plus comorbidities (depression, alcohol use, psychosocial stressors). Giving structure for goal-setting and building emotion-regulation and coping skills are described as of key importance.
*Candidate application:* Suggests what companion help might look like in play: structure, goals, and skills rather than reassurance; and involvement of the character's actual contacts. Cross-applicable.

**177. Named approaches for suicidality: BCBT, CRP, LAST, CAMS, STAIR app, TEC app.** [pp.372-373]
- Brief cognitive behavioral therapy (BCBT) and crisis response planning (CRP): trials show components that train emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility relate to reduced ideation and behavior in military personnel. (The chapter does not list the CRP steps; the step order is not given in this chapter.)
- Life Adaptation Skills Training (LAST): intervention aimed at general well-being via coping skills, quality of life, and ability to adapt to stress. One RCT (in people with depression, per the reference list) showed significant drops in ideation and anxiety at the end of treatment and at 3 months, with large effects.
- Collaborative Assessment and Management of Suicidality (CAMS): a fully collaborative framework usable across theoretical orientations; therapist and patient work together to identify the "drivers" of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and the patient learns alternative competent coping. Combines risk assessment, treatment planning and risk management. The VA/DoD guideline supports the CAMS Suicide Status Form as a multipurpose tool for assessment, treatment planning, tracking and outcome.
- Mobile support: the National Center for PTSD's STAIR (Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation) app offers interactive emotion-regulation and relationship tools with links to help. The Therapeutic Evaluative Conditioning (TEC) game app aims to increase aversion to self-injurious thoughts and behaviors; three RCTs showed moderate reductions in self-injurious thoughts and behaviors but not in suicidal ideation.
*Candidate application:* The idea of finding a person's "drivers" collaboratively (not imposing an explanation) is a good model for a companion conversation that discovers what actually weighs on the defector. The 24-hour access point suggests that a persistent, always-available resource (a comms line, a trusted contact) is a realistic form of support. Cross-applicable; the app and game items are real-world tech and need not map to Tepenia.

**178. Clinician stance: suicidality as expression of distress, not as pathology.** [p.373] The authors endorse a strong collaborative alliance in which suicidality is understood as an expression of intense distress, not as a marker of pathology; this supports open communication, problem solving, connection and safety. Ongoing assessment should use empathic listening and non-judgmental inquiry about: level of emotional pain, hopelessness, risk and protective factors, social connectedness, reasons for living, habituation to pain, and access to lethal means (principle: the means factor is a standing item in assessment). Asking is acknowledged to be uncomfortable for clinicians.
*Candidate application:* Shapes how companion dialogue about the topic should feel: no alarm, no lecture, treating the admission as meaningful pain. "Habituation to pain" is a notable listed factor: someone long used to hardship (and, for the defector, to a chronic injury) may have a changed relationship with pain. Cross-applicable.

**179. Is trauma treatment safe for suicidal patients? Not settled, partly reassuring.** [pp.373-374] Open question whether components of evidence-based PTSD therapy, notably trauma-memory exposure, could harm at-risk patients; little research exists and safety guidelines are needed. Exception: a secondary analysis of an RCT in soldiers comparing cognitive processing therapy (cognitive version only, CPT-C) with present-centered therapy found that CPT-C did not raise suicide risk and was associated with lower risk. Reductions in ideation came after reductions in depression severity, not PTSD severity, so both depression and PTSD, plus suicidality, should be tracked. More research is needed about modifications.
*Candidate application:* Useful caution: do not write "processing the trauma" as a one-step fix; the order matters, and the mood component often moves first. A plausible arc: depression eases before intrusion does, and that is when the thoughts lessen. Cross-applicable.

**180. Stigma again; research limits and gaps.** [pp.374] Stigma and negative attitudes about suicide keep many service members out of care; providers can help leaders reinforce that seeking help is encouraged. Limits of the research: disparate, weakly supported interventions, so one cannot say which works best or for whom; low base rate makes suicide hard to study; causes are complex and idiosyncratic; risk factors vary in effect and change over time; most studies use self-harm as the outcome (including cognitive therapy, DBT and CBT trials), and self-harm is a risk factor but not the same as completed suicide or ideation alone.
*Candidate application:* Honest uncertainty can be modeled: there is no guaranteed formula for helping this character. Cross-applicable.

**181. Military service, fitness for duty and unit safety.** [p.375] In service, the clinician weighs unit safety and functioning as well as the individual, and in combat more so. Questions arise on deployment status, separation, performance and safety, and repeat exposure. Evidence given: a history of multiple attempts (two or more genuine attempts with evidence of intent to die) is linked to chronic problems across age groups, higher symptoms and comorbidity, impaired coping and much higher subsequent risk; the authors conclude multiple attempters should be separated from active service. Some people conceal prior attempts at entry, and the first attempt in service often reveals the history, forcing a discharge decision; the picture is often paired with prior trauma and sometimes combat exposure. Combat stress amplifies everything, including separation from loved ones.
*Candidate application:* Explains why a service institution may expel a person at risk; for a character it supplies a believable pathway to discharge or defection framed as institutional rejection. Candidate, setting-specific; cross-applicable to any military faction.

**182. Fleeting thoughts versus intent; subjective versus objective intent; single attempters.** [pp.375-376] Under acute stress, brief non-specific suicidal thoughts carry limited risk; risk rises along with symptom intensity, complexity and the emergence of intent. Both subjective intent (what the person says) and objective intent (what they do, such as preparation and rehearsal) must be considered. Quick clinical response to fleeting, non-intent thinking in combat, most likely after acute trauma such as a loss, can have a big effect; helping members understand grief in context speeds recovery. Active intent, subjective or objective, rules out deployment. Immediate care may allow some retention in a combat zone if there is no prior history and no index attempt. After a lethal attempt in combat, evacuation and targeted care are generally needed. Single attempters can respond to treatment with durable benefit and may stay in service outside combat, but follow-up data are thin, each case must be judged individually, and CBT shows good evidence of effect for them (Wenzel, Brown & Beck, 2009).
*Candidate application:* The distinction between what a character says and what he does (affairs in order, rehearsal) is a clean way to present risk as observable behavior rather than narrated feeling. A grief-triggered, short-lived episode after a loss is realistic and recoverable. Cross-applicable.

**183. Separation from service is a high-risk transition that re-triggers identity wounds.** [p.376] Leaving service can be overwhelming even when the member welcomes it. Long-standing identity themes of worthlessness, shame and guilt are often activated by the separation process. Care is easier if started while still in service and with a plan for continued care afterward, including orientation to VA and community resources. The authors recommend a concrete written transition plan for every discharged service member, with three elements: (1) a safety and crisis response plan for if suicidality returns, (2) VA services available locally, (3) a targeted referral through the VA or community.
*Candidate application:* The strongest fit to the defector: his exit is a transition of exactly this kind, but he had none of the recommended supports, because defection precludes a handoff. It is realistic for old identity wounds (worthlessness, shame, guilt) to have been triggered at the exit and to have settled into the "functional but carrying it" state. A companion arc might supply the missing structure (a crisis plan, a named contact, a referral) late in life. Candidate, not settled. Cross-applicable to any discharged or fled person.

**184. Conclusion.** [p.377] Management requires careful symptom monitoring, assessment across many functioning domains, knowledge of general and military-specific risk factors, and clear crisis-response practice, because attempts can follow thoughts quickly. Service members with PTSD warrant particular attention. Suicide is idiosyncratic and very hard to predict, so every sign of possible suicidal thinking deserves the highest seriousness; early identification and management is crucial.
*Candidate application:* Supports a design rule of never trivializing a risk cue once shown. Cross-applicable.

**Chapter-level takeaways for the defector (Ch.20):**
- Risk runs through comorbid depression, self-directed anger, guilt/shame, and heavy drinking more than through trauma intensity; a "functional but carrying it" man is most plausibly at risk where these sit together (Finding 163, Finding 164).
- Pile-up of chronic stressors (legal jeopardy, lost status, job and relationship strain, chronic injury) with weak coping is the documented pre-crisis pattern; one stressor rarely does it (Finding 165, Finding 166).
- He likely conceals and minimizes, because stigma and fear of being sidelined shape disclosure; behavior (putting affairs in order, withdrawal, rehearsal) is more reliable than what he says (Finding 168, Finding 172, Finding 182).
- Leaving service is a documented high-risk transition that reactivates worthlessness, shame and guilt; the missing handoff (no written transition or crisis plan, no care continuity) is exactly what the chapter says should exist, so supplying it is a believable form of help (Finding 183).
- What helps is sustained, collaborative, skill-and-structure work with a steady connection, not a single reassuring conversation; depression tends to lift before suicidal thoughts do, and the period right after a crisis stays dangerous (Finding 175, Finding 177, Finding 179, Finding 174).

### Chapter 21: Treating Moral Injury (Berke, Kline, Carney, Yeterian, Litz, pp. 382–395) — ⭐ highest-priority chapter

*No vignette, no formal risk/protective-factor list, no assessment procedure beyond naming two exposure scales
(MIES, MIQ-M), and no chaplain protocol beyond the Soul Repair program. Witnessing is folded into perpetration and
betrayal rather than treated as its own type. The chapter's core payload for the defector: the **type** of
injury (betrayal vs. self-perpetration) determines the dominant emotion and therefore the voice
(Finding 191); the Litz formation chain (Finding 193); and two treatment principles that translate directly into
dialogue craft — never talk him out of self-blame with "it was war" (Finding 201) and "violating a value is not
destroying the value" (Finding 200).*

**185. The argument for why moral injury is needed: fear-based PTSD therapies underperform for veterans.** The chapter opens with policy history. The DoD/VA Evidence-Based Practice Work Group led to first-line guidelines endorsing two CBT treatments for PTSD: prolonged exposure (PE; Foa et al., 2007) and cognitive processing therapy (CPT; Resick, Monson, & Chard, 2017). Both work well for civilian trauma, yet both have been shown to work "considerably less well" in military personnel and veterans despite wide rollout [382]. The authors' explanation is not that the treatments are bad but that the dominant models of PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Foa; Resick & Schnicke, 1992) mostly target conditioned fear and anxiety. They ignore the cultural context of the military and "the unique harms of war" [382-383]. A fear-conditioning model cannot cover what happens when a person's own conduct, or the conduct of people they trusted, violated a moral code. That produces guilt and shame and a wider spiritual and social wound, not just a fear response.
*Candidate application:* For the defector, this gives a clean design split: his "PTSD" content (startle, intrusive memories, sleep) could be the fear-based layer, and his defining wound could be a separate, non-fear layer that standard "talk it out and the memory fades" scenes would not touch. It also supports writing any in-world therapist/chaplain/medic character who offers him the standard approach and sees it fail. Candidate, not settled.

**186. Military culture is the soil that makes moral injury likely.** The text lists the core values as honor, courage, commitment, selflessness, and responsibility (Redmond et al., 2015), reinforced by branch mottos (example given: "semper fi"), drills, rituals, and ideology [383]. Military social practices breed interdependence and cohesion (Soeters, Winslow, & Weibull, 2006), binding people to comrades. A recruit learns to give up autonomy and personal safety for the unit and the mission. So the institution shapes both morality and personal identity. This acculturation is described as necessary for survival in war and as the way higher-level needs (belonging, meaning, purpose, comfort) get met in a dangerous setting [383]. The consequence: a perceived violation of these principles can wreck the core belief systems that sustain military life (Litz et al., 2015; Vargas et al., 2013) [383]. Training prepares people rigorously for fear, sleep deprivation, and exhaustion, but it is "impossible" to train for a breach of the very moral code survival depends on, and troops get little preparation for transgression, horrific loss, or violation (Litz et al., 2009) [383-384].
*Candidate application:* The defector's wound is more potent if his old institution was the thing that made him who he was, so that leaving it, or finding it broken, took identity and belonging with it. Applies to any faction that runs on indoctrination and cohesion: members of such factions are structurally exposed to this injury, and the game could mark this difference between factions that train for fear and ones that train for conscience. Candidate.

**187. Defined construct: potentially morally injurious experiences (PMIEs) versus moral injury itself.** PMIEs are events involving "perpetrating, failing to prevent, bearing witness to, or learning about" acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs and expectations (Litz et al., 2009, p. 697, quoted in the chapter) [384]. They are high-stakes acts of commission or omission, done by oneself (perpetration) or by others (betrayal), that can undermine basic beliefs about the goodness and trustworthiness of oneself, others, and the world. "Moral injury" is the term for the lasting social, psychological, and spiritual damage and impairment that follows when such an experience causes a crisis of conscience [384]. Key distinction: exposure to a PMIE does not guarantee moral injury, just as exposure to a trauma does not guarantee PTSD (Kilpatrick et al., 2013) [384].
*Candidate application:* Useful for pacing: the defector can have a clear causal event (or events) in his past while the game treats the injury as how he interpreted it, not as the event's automatic output. Other characters who witnessed the same event could be injured differently or not at all, which is realistic and avoids "everyone present is equally broken." Candidate.

**188. Betrayal-based moral injury (harm done by others or by the institution).** In betrayal-based PMIEs the service member is the victim of, or witness to, others acting in ways that shatter identity-sustaining beliefs about humanity, the military, or other cherished institutions [384]. Examples named: violence inside the unit (hazing, military sexual trauma or MST), witnessing cruelty by other service members toward enemy combatants or civilians, and high-stakes failures or mistakes by peers and leadership [384]. The authors call these "particularly toxic" because they can erode trust and esprit de corps (pride, fellowship, shared loyalty), which are essential for surviving war. Service members rely on each other to keep a "moral world" intact and a sense of safety and normalcy in a disorienting setting. Betrayal alters sustaining expectations of predictable, honorable, moral conduct and of confidence in peers and leaders, with the potential for lasting harm [384].
*Candidate application:* This is the most natural fit for a defector who left "on principle": the trigger may be orders, a command decision, or an institutional cover-up he witnessed or was subjected to rather than something he personally did. Also applies broadly to any faction whose members were betrayed by their own leadership, and to the "no costless good endings" theme (the faction he trusted is also the one that failed him, and he cannot fully hate or fully forgive it). Candidate.

**189. Perpetration-based moral injury (acts of commission, omission, and failure to intervene).** Perpetration-based PMIEs are cases where the service member does something that violates their moral standards, or fails to step in to prevent a moral violation [385]. Gross violations (killing outside the rules of engagement, taking part in torture, crimes against civilians) are acts of commission that can leave lasting moral and spiritual wounds. But the text insists an atrocity is not required: moral injury can also follow duty errors that harm others, or lapses from the code of honor during moments of fatigue or fear [385]. The reason: military operations demand heavy responsibility and intention because the stakes are greater than in civilian life, so with a lethal weapon in hand "there is no room for accidents." Acting dishonorably or failing in duty violates both the warrior ethos and one's own belief system. Crucially, even without intent, the person may still feel culpable, because taking responsibility is built into the culture [385].
*Candidate application:* For the defector, the injury could come from something small and ambiguous (a mistake, a moment of fear, an order he obeyed, a person he did not protect) rather than a single atrocity, which is more believable and more specific than "he did a terrible thing." Also directly applicable to robots or constructs bound by duty protocols: a machine that carries a strict responsibility rule may judge itself culpable for an accident regardless of intent. Candidate.

**190. How common PMIE exposure is: the chapter's prevalence numbers.** The chapter says PMIE prevalence has only recently been studied. Figures given:
- Stein and colleagues (2012) built a coding scheme from deployed service members' narratives of their worst and currently most distressing war experience. Categories: life threat to self, life threat to others, aftermath of violence, traumatic loss, moral injury by self, moral injury by others. In that small study group, 12% reported moral injury by self (perpetration) and 22% moral injury by others (betrayal) [385].
- Wisco and colleagues (2017), National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study (an epidemiological survey of US veterans), used the Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES; Nash et al., 2013). Over 40% of combat veterans endorsed exposure to some PMIE type (transgression by others, transgression by self, or betrayal) [385].
- Jordan, Eisen, Bolton, Nash, and Litz (2017), deployed active-duty Marines (epidemiological sample): 24% reported perpetrating an act that violated their morals, and 28% reported being betrayed by a trusted other since joining the military [385-386].
Takeaway the authors draw: a substantial share of service members face situations that challenge their sense of right and wrong [385]. The numbers are from a particular population (US, recent wars) and measure exposure, not injury.
*Candidate application:* Gives a sense of scale: in a military of any size, a large minority carries at least one such event, so the defector is not an outlier, and his faction likely contains many people with unspoken versions of the same wound. Candidate.

**191. Perpetration and betrayal run through different emotional pathways (Jordan et al., 2017).** Using structural equation modeling, Jordan and colleagues showed that perpetration-based and betrayal-based PMIEs can be empirically told apart [386]. For betrayal-based events (commission or omission by others), the link to PTSD was mediated by anger. For perpetration-based events (commission or omission by oneself), the link to PTSD was mediated by shame and guilt [386]. So the dominant emotion differs with who did the wrong. Betrayal pushes toward anger and resentment; self-perpetration pushes toward self-condemnation.
*Candidate application:* A character-voice tool. If the defector's wound is betrayal, he should read as angry, bitter, wary of institutions and authority, with an edge. If it is perpetration, he should read as quiet, self-punishing, guilt-driven, and avoidant of praise. If both (likely for a defector who was part of the machine and then saw it betray its own stated values), the two emotional signatures can conflict inside him, which gives dialogue range. Also usable for contrasting two companions on opposite sides of the same event. Candidate.

**192. The syndrome of moral injury, and why exposure alone is not the cause.** The authors say PMIEs are "not etiologic in and of themselves." The syndrome is theorized to appear only when the person sees the event as incongruent with fundamental beliefs and assumptions about themselves and the world. The link between exposure and syndrome is further shaped by the attributions the person makes about the meaning of the violation (Litz et al., 2009) [386].

**193. The Litz et al. (2009) working model of how moral injury forms and persists (step-by-step).** In the order the chapter gives it [386]:
1. A service member or veteran cannot assimilate an event that transgresses personal or shared expectations about the rules or code of conduct into a pre-existing schema. This creates dissonance and distress.
2. If the person reads the event in stable, internal, and global terms (that is: it reflects a permanent trait, about me, and about everything), the perceived violation intrudes into awareness, with feelings of self- and/or other-condemnation: anger, shame, guilt, anxiety.
3. The person may then withdraw from others, engage in self-defeating behaviors (examples named: substance use, criminal behavior), or feel alienated from a moral community.
4. Those behaviors block opportunities for corrective or conciliatory action that could repair the effect of the transgression, and they also block forgiveness processes (of self or of others).
5. Without reconciliation and repair, a range of emotional, cognitive, spiritual, social, and behavioral consequences persist. These can include PTSD-like symptoms (intrusions, avoidance, numbing) plus a broader set: self-harming behaviors, self-handicapping, and demoralization [386].
*Candidate application:* This is a ready-made causal chain for a character arc: event, then self-condemning attribution, then withdrawal and self-sabotage, then lost chances to repair, then a stuck wound. For a "functional but still carrying it" defector 10-15 years out, the model suggests he is stuck at step 3-5: functional on the surface, but avoiding the people and situations (a moral community, a chance to make amends) that would let repair start. The "chronic injury" could also interact with self-handicapping. Cross-applicable to any companion whose arc is being stuck rather than acute. Candidate.

**194. Measurement: two exposure scales and the absence of an outcome gold standard.** Two reliable, valid measures of exposure to military PMIEs are named: the Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES; Nash et al., 2013) and the Moral Injury Questionnaire-Military Version (MIQ-M; Currier, Holland, Drescher, & Foy, 2015) [387]. The chapter states that the field is hampered by the lack of any consensus gold-standard measure of moral injury as an outcome (as opposed to exposure). Testing the Litz model needs an instrument that captures the full range of psychosocial-spiritual consequences [386-387]. No assessment interview, cutoffs, or clinician procedure is given in this chapter. Sample item wording quoted from the research: "I feel betrayed by leaders who I once trusted" is an example of a betrayal-based PMIE endorsement [387].
*Candidate application:* The exposure-versus-outcome gap is a good story fact: the defector could know exactly what happened and still have no neat label for what it did to him. The sample item line is the kind of sentence a character might say in his own words. Candidate.

**195. What the research shows about outcomes: killing, atrocity, betrayal, MST.** The evidence base for the syndrome comes mostly from perpetration, especially atrocities (unnecessary, cruel, abusive harm or lethal violence; Beckham et al., 1998; King et al., 1995; Yehuda et al., 1992) and killing (Fontana & Rosenheck, 1999; MacNair, 2002; Maguen et al., 2010, 2011) [387]. Specific findings stated:
- Killing in combat predicts postdeployment PTSD symptoms in both Iraq and Gulf War veterans (Maguen et al., 2010, 2011) [387].
- Killing predicts alcohol abuse (Maguen et al.) [387].
- Killing predicts suicidal ideation and suicide attempts (Bryan et al., 2013; Wisco et al., 2017) [387].
- Wisco et al. also found war-related personal transgression linked to depression, PTSD symptoms, and suicidal ideation [387].
- Witnessing or being a victim also injures: betrayal-based PMIEs predicted postdeployment suicide attempts even after controlling for combat exposure and severity (Wisco et al., 2017) [387].
- MST, a salient betrayal-based PMIE, is positively associated with PTSD, depression, anxiety disorders, and substance use disorders after deployment (Cobb et al., 2014; Kimerling et al., 2010) [387].
- Women who experienced MST have PTSD at higher rates than women who experienced other trauma (Yaeger et al., 2006), which the authors read as a sign of the distinct harm of betrayal in the military context [387].
*Candidate application:* Gives realistic downstream behaviors for the defector: drinking or other substance use, risk-taking or passive indifference to danger, depressive flatness, a suicidal-ideation shadow (handle with care and as a minor, non-gratuitous note). Betrayal alone, without his having harmed anyone, is enough to carry real risk, so a defector who "only" witnessed or was betrayed is not a lesser case. Also fits any victim of institutional abuse (a female companion subject to in-unit violence would sit in the MST evidence). Candidate.

**196. Guilt and shame: the chapter's treatment of how moral injury differs from ordinary PTSD.** The authors say guilt and shame are defining emotional consequences of both PTSD and moral injury, but moral injury is posited to intensify them and to interfere with recovery from a wider mix of psychological, social, functional, spiritual, and psychic distress (Bryan et al., 2013; Currier, Holland, & Malott, 2015; Litz et al., 2009) [388]. Supporting evidence: many clinicians say a PTSD diagnosis does not fully account for the shame, guilt, and other distress they see in combat veterans (Becker, Zayfert, & Anderson, 2004; Drescher et al., 2011), and they endorse moral injury as clinically useful and ask for more guidance on resolving it (Nash, 2007) [388]. In the summary, the authors add that those who cannot contextualize, justify, or accommodate an act of moral transgression battle both classic PTSD symptoms and lasting shame, guilt, and condemnation of self or others [392]. Distinction to hold: the chapter does not give a crisp textbook split between guilt and shame (for example, guilt as about an act versus shame as about the self); it only lists the two together as the central emotions. The sharper contrast it does draw is fear-based PTSD (conditioned fear and anxiety) versus moral injury (condemnation, alienation from a moral community, demoralization, spiritual disruption).
*Candidate application:* The defector can carry fear-type symptoms and moral-type symptoms at once, and the game can let players notice that only one of them responds to ordinary comfort. Cross-applicable to robot companions: a machine could register something functionally like fear (damage avoidance) separate from something like moral condemnation of itself. Candidate.

**197. First-line PTSD therapies applied to moral injury: PE and CPT adaptations (limits and open questions).** The authors note the smaller effect sizes of CPT and PE in veterans than in civilians (Steenkamp et al., 2015), which suggests those treatments may not meet veterans' needs [387]. But no empirical study has directly tested whether first-line therapies are less effective for moral-injury-based PTSD specifically (Held, Klassen, Brennan, & Zalta, 2018) [387-388]. Two proposed adaptations:
- PE: Smith, Duax, and Rauch (2013) argue that emotion processing theory (the basis of PE) can be stretched to reduce shame and guilt linked to perpetration. PE would counter avoidance by activating and exploring the right context of the memory networks tied to shame and guilt, along with thoughts like "I am a bad person" and "I deserve to be punished" [388].
- CPT: Wachen and colleagues (2016) describe tactical changes to Socratic questioning needed for self-blame appraisals in active-duty personnel [388].
Open question flagged by the authors: CBTs reduce guilt and shame in civilians (Øktedalen et al., 2015; Resick et al., 2002; Stapleton et al., 2006), but whether that holds for war veterans is "an unaddressed empirical question" [388].
*Candidate application:* Gives a believable "he tried the standard program and it only half worked" backstory beat. The two sample thoughts ("I am a bad person"; "I deserve to be punished") are plausible interior lines for the defector or any companion with a perpetration wound. Candidate.

**198. Impact of Killing in War (IOK; Maguen et al., 2017): structure and content.** IOK is a six- to eight-session individual therapy module meant as an add-on to PE or CPT for people with enduring distress about killing in war [388]. It was developed to cover themes the standard therapies do not explicitly address: loss (of self, of innocence), the physiology of killing responses, moral injury, self-forgiveness, spirituality, making amends, and better functioning [388-389]. It runs in a cognitive-behavioral framework with four phases in this order [389]:
1. Psychoeducation: information about the biopsychosocial aspects of killing that create vulnerability to inner conflict and moral injury.
2. Meaning-making: identifying the attributions the person holds about the killing.
3. Self-forgiveness: aided by cognitive therapy techniques and by promoting spirituality and/or faith-based religious practice.
4. Making amends: tailored to the individual.
Evidence: a pilot randomized controlled trial showed significant improvement on PTSD, general psychiatric symptoms, and quality-of-life function compared with wait-list controls (Maguen et al., 2017) [389]. Only a pilot, so evidence is preliminary.
*Candidate application:* A usable template for any companion quest about a killing: the four phases (understand the body's response, find what the act means to you, work toward self-forgiveness, make amends) can structure a quest chain. "Loss of self and innocence" is a good thematic line. Candidate.

**199. Soul Repair: the community, clergy, and spiritual approach.** The Soul Repair Center is at Brite Divinity School in Fort Worth, Texas. It was founded to reach religious communities and raise attention to questions of moral and religious conscience among service members and veterans, through research and public education [389]. Its approach to moral injury has four parts: bring service members and veterans into a supportive community, promote social connection, use artistic expression to express emotion, and involve them in service activities to rebuild meaning and purpose [389]. It also asks clergy, clinicians, and other helpers to practice "deep listening," meaning to bear witness to the suffering without assuming recovery will be quick or easy (Drescher, Nieuwsma, & Swales, 2013) [389]. This is the chapter's only clergy/community component; no chaplain protocol is described and no outcome data are given for it.
*Candidate application:* Fits a defector who recovers (or half-recovers) through belonging, craft, and useful work rather than talk: a community around him, an art or making outlet, and volunteer-type service. The "deep listening" stance is a good template for how a companion or in-world confessor should react: witness without fixing. Cross-applicable to any in-world religious faction that offers sanctuary to the morally wounded. Candidate.

**200. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) applied to moral injury.** ACT (Hayes, Strosahl, & Wilson, 1999) is an evidence-based transdiagnostic therapy rooted in behaviorism. It arose from the view that traditional mental health approaches were not addressing important parts of human suffering (Nieuwsma et al., 2015) [389]. Its goal is not symptom reduction but psychological and behavioral flexibility: contacting the present moment as it is, without defense, while choosing either to change or to persist in behavior in service of personal values. Good change means renewed, sustained engagement in valued behavior and does not necessarily mean feeling better [389-390]. Applied to moral injury (Farnsworth, Drescher, Evans, & Walser, 2017; Nieuwsma et al., 2015), ACT assumes the painful inner states (shame, anger, sadness, guilt) need not be avoided or removed. The person learns to be present with them, and with any real culpability, and to use them to motivate values-directed action now. Uncritical acceptance of moral emotions is the starting point for connecting to meaning and purpose [390]. A specific distinction ACT is said to help with: a violation of a value is not the destruction of the value, and the person is more than the experience of having violated it. This supports continuing to move toward chosen values despite the violation [390]. Evidence: efficacy not yet established; a small pilot's qualitative results support acceptability and feasibility (Farnsworth et al., 2017) [390].
*Candidate application:* Strong thematic fit for a defector whose act of defection was itself a values-driven choice: he can be framed as someone who violated one value (loyalty, oath) in service of another (conscience) and now has to live with having broken the first without having destroyed it. The line "violating a value is not destroying the value" is a ready-made companion-perk or dialogue-resolution idea (a stance that lets the player support him without absolving him). Directly relevant to "no costless good endings": acceptance, not cure. Candidate.

**201. Adaptive Disclosure (AD; Litz et al., 2015): design principles.** AD is a manualized, structured, relatively brief psychotherapy built with careful attention to military culture and warrior ethos [390]. Like PE and CPT, it aims to expose people to corrective, more productive ways of construing war experience, but it is more experiential and individualized, with different case conceptualizations and strategies for three harm types: life-threatening trauma, traumatic loss, and moral injury [390]. Its core is exposure technique: a real-time, emotion-focused narration of the event and a post-exposure dialogue. But exposure in AD is not meant to extinguish conditioned responses; it is used to promote disclosure and a gradual unfolding of understanding about the meaning and implications of the specific kind of harm (for example perpetration-based versus betrayal-based) [390]. Two deliberate departures from first-line therapy:
- No cognitive-therapy techniques of systematically challenging the accuracy of appraisals or trying on new construals [390].
- The therapist assumes only the service member or veteran can judge culpability and responsibility, given shared enculturated beliefs, the demands of battle, and the bonds of the unit. Attempts to attribute moral violations to "the context of war" are avoided, since they can ring hollow and cost the therapist credibility with someone steeped in a culture of personal responsibility (Gray, Nash, & Litz, 2017) [390-391].
(The Gray, Nash, & Litz reference title is "When self-blame is rational and appropriate: the limited utility of Socratic questioning in the context of moral injury.")
*Candidate application:* Important craft principle for dialogue: do not let NPCs (or the player) talk him out of his guilt with "it was war, it wasn't your fault." For a character steeped in responsibility culture, that comes across as hollow and costs trust. His self-blame may be partly rational and should be treated as such. Matches the "no costless good endings" theme: repair does not mean exoneration. Candidate.

**202. Adaptive Disclosure's moral-injury technique: the "relevant other" breakout and the "balance the scales" principle.** After the basic emotional processing of the event, AD uses experiential "breakout" sessions: the patient has a real-time imaginal conversation with a key "relevant other," which for moral injury is a respected, compassionate, forgiving moral authority [391]. This adapts the Gestalt "empty-chair" technique and is meant to promote perspective-taking and to motivate return to a life of connection, meaning, and virtue [391]. Steps as described:
1. The patient "confesses" the morally injurious experience and its consequences to the imagined figure.
2. The patient then role-plays, in real time, that figure's reaction. The assumption is that a caring, compassionate person voiced by the patient will respond with encouragement toward correcting the transgression [391].
Underlying principle: recovery from moral injury requires doing things in the world to "balance the scales" regarding how good or bad the self is, or how bad others are. The therapy therefore supports corrective action beyond the session: self-care behaviors, volunteerism, social engagement [391].
*Candidate application:* A strong scene template: the defector confesses to an imagined (or real) figure whose voice he himself supplies, and the response he generates is not absolution but a direction to act. Also the basis for a "restitution" quest line in which repair is deeds, not words. Could be given to any companion; for robots, the "moral authority" figure could be a revered architect, a lost handler, or a former unit-mate. Candidate.

**203. Adaptive Disclosure evidence and extensions.** In an open trial with deployed Marines, an eight-session AD course produced clinically significant reductions in PTSD, depression, and negative posttraumatic appraisals, plus greater posttraumatic growth (Steenkamp et al., 2011) [391]. It was well tolerated and accepted, but many participants said the treatment was too short [391]. Two trials were underway at the time of writing: (a) extending AD to 12 sessions in a noninferiority design versus Cognitive Processing Therapy-Cognitive Only (CPT-C); (b) adding skills from mindfulness and loving-kindness meditation plus behavioral contracting, to target functional impairment and treat symptoms "as part of the fabric of the whole veteran" in his or her context [391]. Results of those trials are not given. No control group in the Marines study, so it is preliminary.
*Candidate application:* "Too short" is a quiet, true-to-life detail: a character who went through a program and felt it ended before the real work started. Loving-kindness and mindfulness as add-ons offers a flavor option if the defector has a contemplative or ritual practice. Candidate.

**204. Chapter summary: moral injury as distinct and as needing culturally tailored treatment.** The authors conclude that moral injury and PTSD overlap but moral injury has a distinct phenomenology and probably a more complex clinical picture [392]. People who cannot contextualize, justify, or accommodate transgression fight both classic PTSD and lasting shame, guilt, and condemnation of self or others. Trouble weaving the event into a coherent sense of self, others, and world is made worse by interdependent unit bonds and a moral code that stresses accountability. Hence they call for culturally tailored interventions [392]. Final caution: more psychometric work and randomized trials are needed to find which strategies work best; moral injury is called a framework with "great promise," not a settled diagnosis [392]. Definition used in the summary: moral injury is the emotional and spiritual impact of participating in, witnessing, and/or being victimized by actions that violate a service member's core moral values and behavioral expectations of self or others [391-392].
*Candidate application:* The three-part definition (participate / witness / be victimized) is a compact checklist for assigning moral injury to different characters: a perpetrator, a bystander, and a victim of the same event all qualify, with different emotional tones. Candidate.

**205. Cross-applicability notes (not in the chapter; my flagging).**
- Other human companions: the three channels (self-perpetration, betrayal, witnessing) can each be given to a different companion, with different dominant emotions (guilt and shame versus anger).
- Factions: any institution that demands loyalty and self-sacrifice while training for fear but not for conscience will produce injured members; a faction's response (denial, ritual, restitution, punishment) is itself characterizing. The chapter's point that institutional betrayal (leadership failures, cover-ups, in-unit violence) is "particularly toxic" suits a faction whose rot is the source of defection.
- Robots: if robots are given duty-bound, responsibility-heavy roles, a robot could meet every element of the chapter's definition (a code, an act or omission, an interpretation of it as stable/internal/global, withdrawal, blocked repair). Fear-based "damage avoidance" and moral injury could be shown as separate systems.
- "No costless good endings": the chapter supplies a grounded mechanism. Repair requires reconciliation and corrective action, but the injured person's own judgment of culpability is respected and not argued away (AD), and acceptance rather than cure is the target (ACT). A good ending that leaves the debt acknowledged but carried is consistent with the chapter.
- Limits to respect: no case vignettes, no ethnographic texture, an exclusively US-military source population, and mostly preliminary treatment evidence; nothing here is Tepenian-specific, so any Tepenian detail must come from the game's own setting, not from this chapter.

**Chapter-level takeaways for the defector (Ch.21):**
- Moral injury is not fear. Treat it as a separate layer from his startle/sleep/intrusion symptoms: condemnation, shame, anger, alienation, and loss of a moral community, which fear-based therapy does not address.
- The type matters for his voice: betrayal gives anger and distrust; self-perpetration gives guilt and shame (Jordan et al., 2017). A defector who both served and was betrayed can carry the two in tension.
- The event is not the injury; his interpretation is. The injury stays stuck when he reads it as permanent, about him, and global, then withdraws, self-sabotages, and loses chances to repair or forgive.
- Do not let anyone talk him out of it with "it was war" or "it wasn't your fault"; in a responsibility culture that rings hollow, and only he can judge his culpability (Adaptive Disclosure).
- Repair is deeds and belonging, not talk alone: "balance the scales" through corrective action, community, service, craft, and being deeply listened to; acceptance of the guilt and a values-driven life stand in for a cure (ACT, Soul Repair, AD).
- A principled defection can still be a moral injury: he broke one deeply trained value (loyalty) for another (conscience), and the chapter's framing, "a violation of a value is not the destruction of the value," offers a way to write him as damaged yet intact.

### Chapter 22: Treating Complex Traumatic Stress Disorders in Military Veterans (Courtois & Ford, pp. 396–410)

*Conceptual and treatment-model chapter; no vignette. The military is named explicitly as one possible "closed
system" that entraps (Finding 210).*

**206. Opening epidemiology: PTSD is the tip of an iceberg in veterans.** Deployed combatants and peacekeepers carry current PTSD at roughly 4-8 percent, against 1-2 percent in other deployed or nondeployed military and community groups; as many as about one in five (21 percent) have clinically significant PTSD symptoms (a UK armed-forces review). A representative 2013 U.S. veteran survey put lifetime PTSD at 8 percent and past-month at 5 percent. The point the authors stress is comorbidity: PTSD in veterans came with sharply raised odds of mood and anxiety disorders (a range of about 7.6-fold up to 63-fold), suicidal behavior (about 6.7- to 15-fold) and substance use disorders (about fourfold). [p. 396] (Text-extraction glitch: "63fold" and "15fold" are missing hyphens only.)
*Candidate application:* The defector's "functional but still carrying it" profile fits the idea that the visible diagnosis (nightmares, startle) is the smallest part, with drinking, low mood, and risk-taking sitting underneath. Any companion or faction NPC with a combat history can have a stacked, not single, symptom profile.

**207. Childhood adversity is the strongest predictor of who gets PTSD, independent of the war.** Among veterans at greatest PTSD risk, histories of childhood sexual abuse (28 percent) and adult sexual trauma (26 percent) were prominent. The National Vietnam Veterans Longitudinal Study (NVVLS) found that childhood corporal punishment, a poor relationship with the primary caregiver (mother), and childhood poverty predicted PTSD and related symptoms 40 years after service, independent of war-zone exposure. Nuance: once recent stressors, social support, and a negative homecoming were statistically accounted for, childhood adversity stopped being a significant direct risk, yet in the full model it still acted indirectly (through postwar risk) and directly on PTSD and associated symptoms. [p. 396]
*Candidate application:* The defector's pre-service life is a legitimate lever: a harsh or controlling home, or a poor-family enlistment, would make "defected on principle" and "carries it" both richer. The homecoming/support finding suggests the quality of what happened after leaving (welcomed vs. branded a traitor) is an equal-weight driver, which suits a defector whose "homecoming" is hostile. Generalizes to exile-population NPCs and to Outer Tepenia settlers who grew up poor.

**208. The DESNOS veteran study (Ford 1999): a distinct complex subgroup in inpatient PTSD care.** Twenty years before the book, a study of U.S. veterans in inpatient PTSD treatment with childhood-abuse histories identified a subgroup meeting structured-interview criteria for a complex variant called "disorders of extreme stress not otherwise specified" (DESNOS). It appeared both on its own and alongside PTSD. It was uniquely tied to early childhood trauma, participation in war-zone atrocities, extreme levels of intrusive reexperiencing (flashbacks), characterological impairment (identity and personality-style damage), and heavy use of intensive psychiatric services. [pp. 396-397]
*Candidate application:* "Participation in atrocities" plus early trauma is a compact recipe for a cPTSD-shaped veteran. A defector who defected after being made complicit in something (not merely witnessing) maps cleanly; the principle behind the defection could be the first moral injury he could act on. Cross-applies to any soldier-type NPC (exile guards, faction enforcers) and to robots programmed or ordered into atrocity.

**209. Authors' purpose and the central claim.** Some veterans seeking PTSD care need treatment adapted for complex PTSD (cPTSD). The standard evidence-based trauma-focused therapies (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR, narrative exposure therapy) have helped adults with complex histories, but need careful adaptation for the broader dysregulation; the authors therefore recommend a sequenced, relationally based approach aimed at self-regulation, identity, and relational impairments. [p. 397]
*Candidate application:* A usable design principle: treating the "memory" of the worst event is not the whole job for this character; his damage lives in how he regulates feeling, who he thinks he is, and whether he can stay close to people.

**210. What makes trauma "complex": the defining features.** Complex trauma is distinguished from single-event or short-term trauma (the authors explicitly refuse to minimize single-event harm). Features: (a) interpersonal, committed by a person, group, or organization, usually deliberately and with premeditation, involving physical intrusion, exploitation, assault, or violence with emotional coercion; (b) repeated until chronic, even normalized, inside a closed system (family, culture, or organization "including the military") that entraps and makes escape or intervention hard; (c) severity rises with youth of the victim at onset, a large age gap between victim and perpetrator, and close relatedness (those who should offer safety), which adds betrayal; (d) it can escalate over time (example: abuse starting with fondling and progressing to penetration). [pp. 397-398]
*Candidate application:* The military itself is named as a possible "closed system" that entraps. That is directly usable: a defector's trauma can be the institution's coercion over years, with betrayal by a trusted command structure as the aggravating feature. Equally applies to cult-like factions, forced-labor exile colonies, indentured crews, and robots owned or controlled by one operator.

**211. Identity-based traumatic victimization.** A second type targets an immutable or hard-to-change characteristic (gender, size and strength, immaturity, sexual identity or orientation, race, ethnicity, disability, illness) for mistreatment and violence, with direct assault plus chronic intimidation or ambient threat, inescapable without outside help. Membership in groups (family, ethnic group, tribe, religion, political party, military) plus poverty and powerlessness can generate intergroup persecution lasting generations; this is termed intergenerational or historical trauma, including colonial domination of a group deemed inferior. [p. 398]
*Candidate application:* Strong fit for exile populations, an oppressed robot class, or a minority faction inside the federation; a character can be targeted for what they are (e.g., a gynoid or a member of a despised group) rather than for what they did. For the defector: a possible secondary layer if he belonged to a disfavored group inside his former force.

**212. Developmentally adverse (attachment/relational) trauma is easy to miss.** It can begin in utero and continue through infancy and early childhood within primary attachment relationships. Because it happens privately at home, it is less obvious than public victimization and may go unrecognized even by trauma specialists. Neglect, emotional abuse, and caregiver impairment raise lifetime medical as well as mental illness risk, although it is debated whether these meet the DSM-5 definition of a traumatic stressor. [p. 398]
*Candidate application:* An invisible, non-dramatic childhood (not abuse as spectacle) can be the quiet foundation: a parent who was absent or emotionally unavailable. Reveal it late and indirectly, consistent with "unrecognized even by the person." Cross-applies to any companion; also to robots whose "upbringing" was neglectful (poor early-life instruction or abandonment by a first owner).

**213. Why PTSD alone is not enough: the diagnostic history.** PTSD (since 1980) was built largely from studies of combat effects in adolescent and adult men whose personalities were already formed. Within about a decade, researchers (Herman 1992; Pelcovitz and colleagues 1997, who produced the structured interview for disorders of extreme stress) recognized complex variants tied to complex trauma in children and adults. DSM-IV listed these only as associated features; several DSM-5 PTSD symptoms (negative alterations in mood and cognition; reckless, self-harming, or aggressive behavior) now overlap closely with cPTSD symptoms. [p. 399]
*Candidate application:* Useful as an in-world lore line: the defector's own old-service doctors only had the "combat PTSD" model, which would explain why standard treatment "didn't take" and he may think he failed.

**214. ICD-11 complex PTSD: the definition the text uses.** The forthcoming ICD-11 makes cPTSD a separate diagnosis, defined by three groups of problems: affect dysregulation, a disturbed sense of self, and interpersonal difficulties (in the chapter's wording, "a disturbed sense of self and personality disorders"; the phrase is odd and likely means disturbed self-concept, not that a personality disorder is required). Associated features listed: heightened dissociation, disturbed personal meaning, increased risk and harm to self and others, shame and alienation, and somatosensory disturbances or medical illness. [p. 399]
*Candidate application:* The three clusters give an easy scaffold for writing the defector across dialogue and behavior: (1) affect, swings between flared anger and shutdown; (2) self, a shame-based or "I am what I did" self-concept; (3) relationships, distrust, keeping people at distance. The "somatosensory/medical" feature dovetails with his chronic injury, so pain can be partly trauma-amplified and not merely mechanical (a flag for how the injury should be written).

**215. Disagreement over whether cPTSD is needed; the numbers.** A U.S. civilian and veteran study found that applying the simplified ICD-11 PTSD criteria cut PTSD prevalence estimates by 10-50 percent without lowering comorbid diagnoses. In that same body of work, one in eight veterans (13 percent) met ICD-11 cPTSD criteria, about half of those who met PTSD criteria. Those authors argued ICD-11's lean PTSD definition could not explain the complicated presentations, but that DSM-5's expanded PTSD and attention to symptom intensity might handle them. The authors of this chapter note the label matters for access to services, disability, and reimbursement, but not for careful assessment and planning. [pp. 399-400]
*Candidate application:* A world-building detail: whether a defector's condition is formally recognized could decide whether any pension, care, or status is available to him in Tepenian or other institutions; "unrecognized" or "doesn't qualify" is a plausible bureaucratic hardship for exiles and defectors.

**216. Ford's framing quote of why the label matters less than the reality.** Whether or not cPTSD ends up validated as a diagnosis, anyone facing inescapable, intentional harm that undermines their basic sense of self and interpersonal safety must adapt to survive, and clinicians owe such people (and their families, communities, caregivers) sound, practical ways to understand and recover from those adaptations. [p. 400]
*Candidate application:* A thematic frame ("symptoms as survival adaptations that have outlived their use") that can drive a companion's own self-understanding arc, and later what a player-character might say to him.

**217. Complex symptoms predict treatment outcome even in war-trauma-only veterans.** In Ford's 1999 inpatient sample of male veterans, cPTSD/DESNOS uniquely predicted treatment outcome (change in PTSD severity and quality of life) after controlling for ethnicity, war-zone exposure severity, pretreatment PTSD severity, quality of life, Axis I (PTSD, major depression) and Axis II (personality disorder) status, and early childhood trauma history. Ford and Kidd concluded that chronic-PTSD assessment and treatment should address more than fear, avoidance, and hyperarousal: also altered affect regulation, consciousness, interpersonal engagement, and meaning/spirituality, even when the main reported trauma is adult war experience rather than childhood trauma. [pp. 400-401]
*Candidate application:* Confirms that a defector whose reported trauma is purely adult (war, the defection itself) can still show the complex picture: shifts in emotional control, spells of detachment, difficulty being close, and a broken sense of meaning or faith. "Meaning/spirituality" is a good axis for a defector who left "on principle" and then lost the faith in principle itself.

**218. Long-haul course and "cyclical decompensation."** The NVVLS found about 271,000 Vietnam-deployed veterans with current full PTSD plus subthreshold war-zone PTSD 40 or more years after the war. Among veterans of this and earlier wars who never achieved adaptive resolution, a pattern of "cyclical decompensation" appeared (a stage model by Wang, Wilson, and Mason, 1996), with symptoms resembling cPTSD. The chapter later repeats this: chronic symptoms, acting out, and cyclical decompensation strain relationships and exhaust supporters (loved ones, friends, therapists, community or religious ties). [pp. 401, 404]
*Candidate application:* "Functional but still carrying it" at 10-15 years out is exactly the unresolved-course picture. Design implication: his stability is not linear; periodic collapses (in stress, crisis, triggers, or even positive change) should be expected, and each collapse should cost his supporters something. Cross-applies to long-lived characters (long-lived robots, old exiles) whose condition is 40 years old rather than 10.

**219. The dissociative subtype and its overlaps.** Samples of male Vietnam veterans, female veterans, and active-duty personnel in PTSD treatment showed a distinct dissociative subtype of PTSD: roughly 15 percent of men and 30 percent of women. In the female sample, clinically significant dissociation went with avoidant and borderline personality features, which overlap with cPTSD. Other findings: 12 percent of veterans with a PTSD diagnosis (various war eras) had severe dissociative symptoms linked to childhood and adult sexual trauma, and 8 percent of a trauma-exposed national sample met DSM-5 dissociative-subtype criteria. The authors say treatment works when dissociative and cPTSD symptoms are accounted for in delivering standard PTSD therapy and in treating comorbidities (substance use, self-harm and suicidality, relationship problems). [p. 401]
*Candidate application:* A possible expression of the defector's carry: time-loss, depersonalization ("watching himself do it"), or detachment under stress, rather than only hyperarousal. Female veteran companions (or gynoid characters) have the higher published rate, a reason to consider dissociation as their default pattern instead of his.

**220. Three sources of "evidence-based" (APA 2006 framing).** Treatment evidence is defined as (1) research findings (systematic reviews feeding practice guidelines), (2) clinical judgment, and (3) client preferences, values, and context. The authors use all three. [p. 401]
*Candidate application:* Minor. A character's own preferences (refusing talk therapy, preferring work or ritual) can legitimately be "part of the evidence" and need not be treated as denial.

**221. The CBT trauma-focused family and its limits.** Prolonged exposure (PE), cognitive processing therapy (CPT), cognitive therapy (CT), EMDR, and narrative exposure therapy (NET) have the strongest evidence for reducing PTSD symptoms and are recommended in practice guidelines (APA 2017; VA/DoD 2017; ISTSS under revision in 2018), except NET, which only recently gained endorsement. PE and CPT dominate in the VA for combat PTSD. Criticisms: whether they help beyond PTSD symptoms (including loss of the diagnosis), whether they beat other treatments, and when to use them. The trials routinely excluded the most severely impaired (cPTSD) patients, so applicability to "real-life" practice is disputed. Clinicians argue that a PTSD-only approach ignores skills development, emotion regulation, core identity, attachment/relationship repair, present-centered life issues, and comorbidities, and fear destabilizing precarious clients by starting with trauma work, so they favor stabilizing first and then processing trauma in a middle phase if still symptomatic. [pp. 401-402]
*Candidate application:* A realistic backdrop for the defector: he may have been through a standard protocol (PE or CPT), "completed" it, and stayed symptomatic, which fits "functional but still carrying it." Also gives a lore-consistent reason for suspicion of institutional treatment.

**222. Herman's sequenced, relationally based model (the three phases overview).** Judith Herman (1992), tying treatment to her conceptualization of cPTSD, proposed organizing care for chaotic, danger-filled lives with big skill deficits rather than only targeting trauma memories. Foundation: safety, an assessment and preliminary individualized plan, then three phases each with goals and a hierarchy of tasks: (1) life and emotional stabilization, psychoeducation, skill building and resourcing, and building the treatment alliance; (2) directed trauma exposure and processing to resolution; (3) reintegration into post-trauma life. [pp. 402-403]

**223. Status and dispute of the sequenced model.** It is the standard of care for cPTSD by clinical consensus (ISTSS expert survey, 2011; Schnyder and colleagues 2015) and is treated as a "meta-model" personalized to the client's needs, motivation, goals, preferences, resilience, and emotional, relational, and financial resources. Its research base is thinner than CBT's because it uses multiple varying strategies, hard to trial. Critics (De Jongh and colleagues, 2016) challenge the ethics and legitimacy of a stabilization phase that may delay the central component, trauma-focused exposure, and note CBT has worked for some complex-history clients and for depression, dissociation, grief, shame, addiction (Resick 2003). The authors answer: clinicians still caution against going into the trauma too fast or with only one approach, and recent research shows that trauma-memory processing is not the only route; affect and interpersonal regulation therapies show higher completion rates and comparable improvement to memory-processing therapies. No study has answered whom to prepare, how, and for how long, so it must be done client by client: not "one size fits all," not "shorter is better" (Cloitre 2015). A review of RCTs for military PTSD (mainly CPT and PE) concluded that all current treatments need improving and that novel ones, both memory-processing and affect/interpersonal-skills based, need testing. [p. 403]
*Candidate application:* Gives a believable disagreement a character can voice (a medic who insists on "stabilize first" vs. a hard-liner who says "just face it"). Also supports a design where the defector declines or drops a drawn-out, exposure-heavy path and does better with practical, present-centered help (skills, routine, work), as the text says can complete more often.

**224. The model in practice: a recursive spiral, not a staircase.** The three phases overlap, tasks repeat many times before changes are internalized, and the course should be adapted to need, severity, urgency, personal and social assets, goals, and gains and setbacks. The authors prefer imagining a recursive spiral that moves between phases as indicated (for example, during relapse or decompensation under stress, crisis, triggering, or change). A tidy straight-line progression happens when the client has secure attachment and strong personal and social resources, which is not the "average" cPTSD client. Complications: new issues emerge that were not visible at assessment (dissociation, substance or behavioral addictions, self-injury, suicidality, risk taking), often prompted by treatment, a deepening alliance, or life events and transitions including positive ones, and these can set treatment back or speed it; a commonly hidden item is dissociation (up to and including dissociative identity disorder), which, once found, must be added to the plan. [p. 404]
*Candidate application:* A natural structure for a companion arc: progress, regression after a good thing (e.g., he is trusted or loved), then a deeper disclosure that was hidden. Positive events as triggers is an unusual and humane beat. Cross-applies to every companion questline.

**225. Assessment is the start of treatment, not a prelude.** A broad psychosocial assessment (sometimes called pretreatment) covers the whole person and all past and present circumstances bearing on safety, health, functioning, and stability: a life chronology; all childhood trauma and adverse experiences; family stability and parental functioning; developmental milestones; and personal strengths, resources, motivation, accomplishments, and support network. Once childhood trauma is found, the therapist offers psychoeducation about cPTSD and its relation to PTSD, validating symptoms as understandable survival adaptations that became encumbrances. This alone can relieve people who blame themselves or feel shame and self-hatred. Therapy is framed as serving the client's own values and hopes as well as symptom reduction. [pp. 404-405]
*Candidate application:* The story function: the first healing beat may simply be a person naming his reactions as sensible adaptations, not weakness. A strong, low-cost companion-dialogue moment (an NPC who explains, rather than diagnoses, him).

**226. Phase 1 in detail: stabilization and skill building.** Goal: establish a foundation by acquiring, restoring, or strengthening psychological and life skills, including developmental resources that were skipped and never (or only partly) learned. Starts with emotion identification, attending to both affective and body (somatosensory) signals, with close therapist attunement; teaches identifying, tolerating, and modulating emotions that swing between hyperarousal/heightened reactivity and hypoarousal/shutdown. Better self-regulation yields self-reflection in place of reflex reaction. Safety from self-harm, risky behavior, and external harm is assessed routinely and repeatedly, especially in stress or anxiety; current relationships are checked for violence, abuse, or destabilizing influence; past and present behavioral choices are reviewed for dangers, and a collaborative safety plan is made. Phase 1 handles trauma memories and symptoms mainly from an educational and exploratory view, not full processing. Caveat: if the person has intense reexperiencing or shutdown (frequent overwhelming flashbacks, nightmares, dissociative detachment) or cannot stabilize or get sober whatever is tried, trauma processing may have to come first or "out of order," using a CBT protocol. [pp. 405-406]
*Candidate application:* "Skills that were skipped" is a fine characterization: a man who learned discipline but not how to name or ride out feelings. Gives him concrete, show-able habits (grounding, routines, check-ins). Also the "out of order" caveat justifies a character whose flashbacks force confrontation before he is ready.

**227. Phase 2 in detail: directed trauma processing.** Begins when the clinician judges readiness or the client spontaneously starts talking about the trauma; indicated when the client is still symptomatic and distressed. The common denominator is deliberate approach toward what was avoided, unacknowledged, or dissociated: unintegrated feelings, thoughts, and beliefs, via formal CBT protocols or otherwise. The client is encouraged to identify critical memories (recent and distant) and feel the associated emotion as fully as tolerable, to build tolerance and habituate, using Phase 1 regulation skills to feel and find meaning and personal control, not just reexperience. The therapist acts as stimulus (supporting exploration and regulation) and container (attunement, co-regulation, emotional validation, possibly never experienced before). Prominent emotions: terror, anger, shame, grief. Afterward the emotions make sense and feel manageable, though they may stay aversive. New understanding of the trauma and one's response aids resolution. The phase explicitly includes grieving, may begin decisions about courses of action and relationships now less constrained by the trauma response, and adds insight, meaning making, and behavioral activation. [pp. 406-407]
*Candidate application:* The "container" idea (someone co-regulating) can be dramatized by a companion bond in which another character, not a clinician, holds steady while he speaks. Grief as part of the processing (grief for those harmed, for the life left behind) fits a defector who lost unit, country, and identity.

**228. Phase 3 in detail: reintegration, and the warning against low expectations.** It can be growth and renewal but also difficult; clients may still feel like misfits outside the norm. Therapists must not assume complex-trauma clients can only reach marginal lives, since that can reinforce the client's own stigma; some of the strongest recoveries are by people who struggled for most of their lives with addiction, severe mental illness, learning disabilities, homelessness, and conflicted or empty relationships. Phase 3 means leaving survival-mode coping by challenging beliefs, aspirations, lifestyle, and relationships. Losses continue to be identified and grieved, including new ones when new choices change or end key relationships and activities. Using the regulation skills to address unresolved developmental deficits and fixations and life decisions: trustworthy relationships and intimacy, healthier sexual functioning, parenting, self-directed career choices, ongoing discussion or confrontation of abusive or neglectful others, and self-protection planning. The therapist stays a source of support and encouragement. [p. 407]
*Candidate application:* Defines a possible endgame for the defector: not "cured," but able to choose intimacy, work, and boundaries on purpose; the cost of that is losing some old ties. The "do not assume marginal functioning" caution is a writing guard: avoid making him a permanently broken figure.

**229. Applying cPTSD care to veterans: resistance, shame, and the "failed treatment" problem.** Veterans benefit from learning that cPTSD differs from but includes PTSD and needs its own treatment. Many received standard PTSD care and remained symptomatic, felt like failures or hopeless, and so echoed earlier feelings. Childhood trauma is often unrecognized or unacknowledged, especially its link to current symptoms; when raised, clients may dispute it. Military personnel in particular may resist the connection because they fear it will make them feel weak or out of control, so it can take repeated psychoeducation and reassurance to connect the strands; resources exist for clients and loved ones. [pp. 407-408]
*Candidate application:* A realistic reason for him to deny or deflect questions about his childhood or "before," with the resistance rooted in fear of looking weak; a companion needing multiple conversations (not one big reveal) to get him to connect old and new pain.

**230. Named manualized and program models mentioned for veterans.** (a) Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR) narrative therapy (Cloitre and colleagues, 2010; RCT in childhood-abuse-related PTSD): skills first, then narrative work. (b) Trauma Affect Regulation: Guide for Education and Therapy (TARGET; Ford 2015): a skills and education protocol with demonstrated efficacy or effectiveness in complex-trauma groups. (c) Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) adapted as a phase-based approach with a prolonged-exposure-derived trauma processing component, tested with civilian women with PTSD and borderline personality disorder. (d) Seeking Safety (Najavits and Hien), a multicomponent treatment for co-occurring PTSD and substance use disorder in men and women. (e) Multicomponent inpatient, residential, or intensive outpatient programs: a highly structured UK military program (Busuttil 2006, a 90-day residential program for complex PTSD) with significant gains during and after; an evolving integrative program for military sexual trauma (Ferdinand and colleagues, 2011), where cPTSD is expected from the interplay of sexual trauma and PTSD and the high risk of revictimization; and a civilian model easily adaptable to military use, the Women Recovering from Abuse Program (WRAP; Giles and Curreen, 2007), a two-stage rather than three-stage outpatient alternative to inpatient care built on Herman: Stage 1 a preparatory group called "Building Resources," Stage 2 an intensive multimodal segment of several kinds of group therapy, individual and group formats, with preliminary supportive research. Landes, Garovoy, and Burkman (2013) reviewed three stage-based models used with veterans with complex trauma. [p. 408]
*Candidate application:* Source of structural templates for in-game "care" content: a resources/skills-first group followed by an intensive phase; a 90-day residential program for military personnel (a faction facility on a base or in a city); a military-sexual-trauma program as a distinct, serious setting. The substance-use pairing (Seeking Safety) fits characters who drink or use to cope.

**231. The therapist's position: what cPTSD does to the helper.** Therapists often feel overwhelmed, "de-skilled," or flummoxed by the symptoms, life chaos and risk, extreme reactivity or shutdown, and cyclical decompensation. Traumatic transference, countertransference, and vicarious trauma are manageable with training, though trauma was long missing from professional curricula. APA Division 56 (Trauma Psychology) published tiered competencies for treating cPTSD/PTSD; training in childhood complex trauma involving betrayal and in dissociation is recommended. The therapist must be emotionally regulated: not so overwhelmed as to withdraw (which would repeat past abandonment and nonresponse) nor so overinvolved as to shield the client from facing the trauma. Advice: ongoing self-care, never working in isolation or only with such difficult clients, and outside consultation or supervision, ideally ongoing. Helpers can also experience vicarious resilience and their own posttraumatic growth. [pp. 409]
*Candidate application:* Good material for a companion who supports the defector (a medic, partner, or friend): they can burn out, overprotect, or withdraw, and the "too distant vs. too protective" tension is a ready character conflict. Generalizes to any caregiver NPC (a robot caregiver's "emotional load," a camp healer).

**232. Conclusion and cautions.** There is a confusing array of mind-body treatments; CBT-based approaches have the deepest evidence and are the most available to military clients; some target comorbid substance use, and some shorter hybrids have support. The sequenced relationship-based model has had some research but needs more; it rests on lifespan research on childhood-trauma aftereffects, clinical judgment and consensus, and client preference and adverse-reaction data, and is backed by affective-relational and interpersonal-neurobiology work on relationships across the life course. Better trials (pragmatic effectiveness and sequential multiple assignment designs) are needed on what works for whom. Both memory-processing and affect/interpersonal-regulation therapies should be tested against individual and contextual factors (preference, status, motivation, resources). Closing caution restated: individualize; not "one size fits all." [pp. 409-410]
*Candidate application:* Minor for content; the theme (relationships are the medium of recovery, one-size-fits-all fails) could justify why a companion's resolution is relational (a bond) and not a "quest completed" item.

**Chapter-level takeaways for the defector (Ch.22):**
- Write the damage in three registers, not one: emotion regulation (flare/shutdown), self-concept (shame, "what I did"), and relationships (distance, distrust); with body symptoms and the chronic injury possibly tangled in.
- The complex picture does not need a childhood-abuse backstory: the chapter itself says war-adult trauma can produce it, and that participation in atrocities plus an entrapping institution (the military named as a "closed system") are specific risk markers; a quiet, unremarkable-seeming bad childhood would be an optional extra layer.
- His course should be a spiral: stable stretches, collapses under stress or even good news, hidden items (dissociation, drinking, risk-taking) that surface as trust deepens; each collapse taxes the people around him.
- The "treatment failed him" beat is realistic: prior standard PTSD care that left him symptomatic can feed shame ("I failed at getting better"), and a veteran resisting a childhood-to-present link out of fear of looking weak is documented.
- Recovery content that fits: first, being told his reactions are sensible survival adaptations; then skills (naming feelings, grounding, safety planning), then grief and meaning-making, then choosing relationships and work deliberately. A container figure who co-regulates is the central mechanism; avoid writing him as permanently marginal.
- Cross-applicability: exile populations (closed, coercive systems; historical or identity-based trauma), robots (controlled by a single operator, identity-targeted, neglectful "upbringing," atrocity by order), caregiver NPCs (burnout, overprotection vs. withdrawal), and Outer Tepenia settlers (poverty and early adversity as risk; generational transmission).

### Chapter 23: Identifying and Facilitating Posttraumatic Growth in Military Personnel (Blevins, Greene, Tedeschi, pp. 415–425) — ⭐ highest-priority chapter

*Short review chapter (about 460 prose lines). No vignette, no PTGI item wording or cutoffs, and **nothing on moral
injury, guilt, shame, defection or desertion** — those connections are this project's inference, not the
chapter's. "Illusory growth" and "growth as social pressure" are barely touched in the prose; where this section
raises them it says so (Finding 259, Finding 266). Most useful as a design spec: the "expert companion"
stance (Finding 251), the five growth domains as separate, uneven dials (Finding 249), and the finding that
growth coexists with persisting distress (Finding 247, Finding 266).*

**233. The field shift: from a pathology frame to a resilience-and-transformation frame.** [p. 415] The authors open by saying psychology and psychiatry have long treated service members and veterans through a pathology lens, concentrating on measuring and treating psychiatric symptoms after trauma. Over roughly the last 30 years a competing frame has emerged that asks how people resist, bounce back from, and are changed by adversity. The term "posttraumatic growth" (PTG) was coined by Tedeschi and Calhoun in 1996. It means positive personal change that can come out of the struggle to deal with trauma and its psychological consequences. Note the wording: the change comes from the struggle with the aftermath, not from the event itself. The chapter promises coverage of the PTG process, assessment and treatment, clinician implications, and gaps in military research.
*Candidate application:* The defector's growth (if any) should be authored as a product of ongoing struggle with what defection and service left behind, not as a reward the event handed over. Supports growth WITH cost: the definition itself places the source of growth in effortful, painful struggle.

**234. Janoff-Bulman's shattered-assumptions root and her three growth processes.** [pp. 415-416] Modern PTG models descend from Janoff-Bulman's work on "shattered world assumptions": trauma wrecks a person's basic beliefs about how the world works, and survivors must emotionally and cognitively rebuild an assumptive world. She proposed three processes by which growth happens: (1) strength through suffering (having survived, the person knows they are stronger); (2) existential reevaluation (rethinking what matters, life's meaning, priorities); (3) psychological preparedness. The third means the rebuilt belief structure is sturdier against future shocks, compared in the chapter to communities rebuilding after earthquakes so that they are better prepared for the next one. Preparedness implies the person now knows catastrophe is possible, a knowledge that cannot be unlearned.
*Candidate application:* A defector's "preparedness" is a hard-won wariness as much as a strength: he expects the floor to drop again. Good for a character who is steadier in a crisis than other people precisely because the worst has already been proven possible. Cross-applicable to any companion or faction that rebuilt after a catastrophe (including a population that rebuilt after a war).

**235. The Tedeschi-Calhoun model: six variables that raise the chance of growth.** [p. 416] The latest version of the model (Calhoun, Cann, & Tedeschi, 2010) lists six variables that increase the possibility of growth after trauma: (1) cognitive processing/engagement/rumination; (2) disclosure of concerns about the traumatic events; (3) how other people react to those disclosures; (4) the sociocultural context in which trauma happens and in which the person tries to process, disclose, and resolve it; (5) the survivor's personal dispositions and degree of resilience; (6) the degree to which the events themselves permit or suppress the above processes (that is, whether the circumstances allow thinking, talking, and processing at all). The verb is "increase the possibility," not "cause": none of these guarantees growth.
*Candidate application:* The six variables work as a design checklist for whether a character can plausibly have grown: Has she had room to think? Anyone to tell? How did they react? What did the surrounding culture permit? A character in a setting that suppresses disclosure (a rigid chain of command, a surveilled society, a robot population with no listener) is structurally denied variables 2, 3, 4, and 6. Strongly applicable to factions: a society can be designed as growth-permitting or growth-suppressing.

**236. Growth is not universal, but common enough to watch for.** [p. 416] The authors state plainly that pathways to growth are complex and that not everyone exposed to trauma experiences PTG. It is nevertheless common enough that clinicians who know the surrounding variables can notice emerging pieces of the process and attend to them in treatment.
*Candidate application:* Supports "no costless good ending": growth is an option on the table, not a default. A companion's growth arc can be partial, patchy, or absent, and an alternative where she never develops it should be both possible and respectable. Cross-applicable to every companion.

**237. Precondition: a "seismic" event that shakes core beliefs.** [p. 416] For PTG to even be possible the person must meet an event big enough to shake core beliefs and basic assumptions about the world and their place in it. "Seismic" is the authors' term: the event breaks basic belief structures and puts fundamental assumptions in question. That challenge acts as a catalyst, starting a sequence that can end in reconstruction of the assumptive world and experiences of growth. An event that merely scares or hurts, but leaves the worldview intact, does not start the process.
*Candidate application:* For the defector, the seismic event is likely not a combat incident alone but the collapse of the belief that the institution he served was what he thought it was (he left "on principle"). Whether the shaken belief concerned his side, his duty, or his own goodness determines what he has to rebuild. Worth deciding explicitly which belief broke. Applicable to any character whose faction or ideology betrayed them, and to robots whose assumptions about their makers or purpose are overturned.

**238. Rebuilding a worldview is slow and emotionally loaded; it begins in distress.** [p. 416] The authors stress that rebuilding takes time and is heavy with emotion (Calhoun & Tedeschi, 2014). The cognitive work does not start with calm reflection. It starts with ruminations that are often intrusive, unwelcome, and initially tied to a state of distress. Growth work therefore starts in the same place as symptoms.
*Candidate application:* The visible early stage of a growth arc looks like suffering: intrusive thoughts, snapping, rehashing. A player who sees the defector circling the same memory is watching the start of the process, not just a symptom. This keeps the cost in plain view at every stage. Cross-applicable.

**239. Two kinds of rumination: intrusive versus deliberate.** [pp. 416-417] The nature of rumination is described as a pivotal factor for both posttraumatic distress and growth (Groleau et al., 2013). Intrusive rumination is unwanted thoughts that arrive even when the person tries not to think about the event. Deliberate rumination is intentional, constructive thinking about the event and what followed (Cann et al., 2011). Intrusive rumination tends to go with distress; deliberate rumination goes with growth. For growth to occur, the ruminations must transition from the intrusive type to the deliberate type, which lets the person begin organizing the material and regaining a sense of control over their own thinking. The two coexist at the start; it is a shift in the balance, not a switch.
*Candidate application:* A clear, writable behavioral marker for a companion: intrusive rumination shows as involuntary triggers and sudden withdrawal; deliberate rumination shows as him choosing to bring a memory up, turning it over, asking questions about it. A dialogue state change from "won't discuss" to "lays it out and asks what it means" is a growth step, and it can still cost him something each time. Cross-applicable to robots (an involuntary memory replay versus a chosen review of logs) and to other companions.

**240. Cognitive-neuroscience account of how intrusive thinking becomes deliberate.** [p. 417] From a cognitive neuroscience view, rumination is processing of traumatic material in working memory while new situational information gets integrated into schemas stored in long-term memory (Hanley, Garland, & Tedeschi, 2017). Two tendencies help intrusive rumination turn deliberate: a tendency to concentrate on the posttraumatic material, and a tendency to be nonreactive toward emotionally charged content. When thinking is deliberately pointed at posttraumatic thoughts, the material can be adjusted and recontextualized, and that recontextualization is what PTG requires. The chapter lists mindfulness among variables for future study, consistent with this nonreactivity point.
*Candidate application:* Gives a mechanism for "ability to look at it without being flooded" as the growth gate. A character who cannot yet tolerate looking (high reactivity) cannot grow, no matter how much she wants to; growth thereby costs sustained, uncomfortable attention. Applicable to robots as a processing-load model (a mind must hold the data in active memory while updating its stored model of the world).

**241. Dispositional traits associated with more growth.** [p. 417] The extent of PTG can vary with personality: optimism, extraversion, openness to experience, sense of coherence, positive reappraisal, bravery, fortitude, and perseverance (Calhoun et al., 2010). Openness and extraversion in particular go with approach-oriented coping rather than avoidance, which matters for the shift to deliberate rumination (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 2004).
*Candidate application:* Avoidant coping (a stoic, closed-off defector) works against growth. If the defector is taciturn, his path to growth runs through the specific cost of being less avoidant, which he will find unnatural. A closed, avoidant character who grows therefore has done something expensive against their grain. Cross-applicable: this can be used to tune which companions grow easily and which grow only through effort.

**242. Centrality: how much the event has become part of who you are.** [p. 417] How central the traumatic event is to one's life narrative affects outcome (Groleau et al., 2013). Preliminary findings suggest that the extent to which a person believes the negative event has become part of their core identity contributes to both distress and growth (Cann, Calhoun, Tedeschi, & Solomon, 2010). The authors say more research is needed. Centrality is therefore not good or bad; it raises both distress and growth potential.
*Candidate application:* A defector whose defection or service IS his identity ("the man who walked away") carries high centrality: more distress and more potential growth, with no way to get one without the other. This supports the cost-bearing structure. Cross-applicable to veterans, exiles, or any character defined by a single event.

**243. Social context layer 1: broad cultural narrative.** [pp. 417-418] Societal narratives shape how people engage in coping (Calhoun et al., 2010). The authors note that the broad U.S. narrative generally permits belief in progress and the assumption that individuals can bring about positive change, which they say bears on PTG in U.S. military personnel. In other words, a culture that expects people to turn things around makes growth narratives more available.
*Candidate application:* Cultural meaning-frames shape whether a character even has a script for growth. Different Tepenian regions or factions can differ in whether suffering is read as redemptive, shameful, to be hidden, or meaningless, and that biases which characters can read their own experience as growth. Fits the project's rule that culture is produced locally, not copied from a source stock. Cross-applicable to settings and factions.

**244. Social context layer 2: the proximal environment and the listener's reaction.** [p. 418] Emerging research says the nearby social environment matters: an atmosphere of openness and acceptance toward trauma-related self-disclosure increases the likelihood of PTG (Calhoun, Cann, & Tedeschi, 2010; Lindstrom et al., 2013). What counts is how the person perceives their primary reference groups: whether they appear to encourage and accept disclosure shapes how the person responds after trauma.
*Candidate application:* Players' conduct toward the defector (listening, not pushing, not flinching) changes what he can do. A companion perk or dialogue tier that opens only when the player has shown accepting responses fits this exactly and also puts the burden on the player's relationship, not on a stat. A hostile or judgmental reaction can close the door and has a cost. Cross-applicable to all companions.

**245. Military culture and the interdependent-group filter.** [p. 418] Because military groups are interdependent, members may weigh heavily how their reactions will affect, and be seen by, others. They tend to filter experiences through what they think peers and leaders expect of how they will cope. The consequence is that expectations of "how a soldier copes" can block disclosure and so block the process.
*Candidate application:* A defector who left his unit has lost, or abandoned, the reference group that defined acceptable coping, and has not yet been given a new one. This is both a hardship (no peers to disclose to who understand) and an opening (freedom from the old expectations). Also useful for faction design: any rigid, hierarchical force (including robot units or Tepenian security organizations) will produce members who suppress disclosure.

**246. Early signals of growth in military populations (Vietnam-era data).** [p. 418] Research historically focused on PTSD pathology, but researchers occasionally detected positive effects. In a sample of Vietnam prisoners of war, 61.1% reported positive psychological benefits from the experience, including more self-confidence and a greater appreciation for life (Sledge, Boydstun, & Rabe, 1990). In the National Vietnam Veterans Readjustment Study (Kulka et al., 1990), 70.1% of male veterans said their Vietnam experiences were mainly positive. The authors add that there was no sign these reports reflected pathological defensive denial (Dohrenwend et al., 2004).
*Candidate application:* Real veterans can report both hardship and genuine benefit about the same events, so a defector who says "it made me who I am" is not necessarily lying or in denial, and also may hold major unresolved harm. Do not write a character's positive framing as automatically a defense mechanism, and do not write it as proof of being fine. Cross-applicable to prisoners, exiles, or other long-captivity characters.

**247. Direct measurement in recent veteran samples.** [p. 418] Studies using direct PTG measures found around 70% of veterans sampled reported a significant level of growth in at least one PTG domain (Pietrzak et al., 2010). The National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study indicated that 75% of veterans with PTSD showed moderate and stable PTG across a 2-year span (Tsai, El-Gabalawy, Sledge, Southwick, & Pietrzak, 2015). The chapter says these findings have been replicated across active-duty and veteran samples over the past decade (it cites studies including OEF/OIF amputees, U.S. Army soldiers, multiwar combat veterans, and Gulf War I veterans). Takeaway for the design question: growth and a PTSD diagnosis can occur in the same person simultaneously.
*Candidate application:* A character can be clinically symptomatic and also moderately grown, matching "functional but still carrying it." The numbers offer plausible frequency (most veterans in these samples report growth in some domain) if the game ever needs population-level texture. Cross-applicable.

**248. What PTG goes with, and its protective effect.** [p. 418] In the military studies, perceived growth was associated with lower depression, lower stress, lower suicidal ideation, better physical health, and better psychological functioning (Gallaway et al., 2011; Moore et al., 2010; Tsai et al., 2015, 2016). Tsai et al. (2016) found PTG served as a protective factor against diagnosis and severity of PTSD related to newly experienced traumatic events at a 2-year follow-up. These are correlations; the chapter does not claim PTG caused them. The authors infer a possible clinical utility of PTG in psychosocial treatment.
*Candidate application:* Growth's real benefit is partial: better mood and some resilience to the next blow, not an absence of the old wound. Matches Janoff-Bulman's preparedness idea (Finding 234). Allows a mechanical expression (e.g., growth reduces vulnerability to future events) that leaves existing scars untouched. Cross-applicable.

**249. The measurement instrument: the PTGI and its five domains.** [p. 419] PTG is usually measured with the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI; Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996). Its 21 items were built from qualitative research in which people described transformative changes after trauma. Factor analyses, in the original paper and later work, yield five domains: (1) personal strength (a sense of being able to handle whatever happens); (2) new possibilities (new opportunities or directions in life); (3) relating to others (improved relationships); (4) spiritual change (stronger faith or increased spiritual understanding); (5) appreciation of life (a changed sense of priorities and of life's value). The chapter's citations include a later revised version of the PTGI that integrates existential and spiritual change (title of a 2017 reference; the prose itself does not describe the revision). Item wording is not given.
*Candidate application:* Five domains are a usable template for scoping a character's growth: each domain can be present or absent separately. The defector might have gained personal strength and appreciation but have lost or never gained relating to others, or the reverse. Writing growth as domain-specific, uneven, and incomplete is the best structural defense against the "free upgrade" version. Cross-applicable to every companion and to faction arcs.

**250. Process-tracking instruments: the CBI and the ERRI.** [p. 419] Two further measures track the process described above. The Core Beliefs Inventory (CBI; Cann et al., 2010) has nine items asking the survivor how much they seriously examined certain core beliefs because of the event. Research suggests the degree of disruption to the assumptive world predicts PTG (Cann et al., 2010; Lindstrom et al., 2013; Taku et al., 2015). The Event Related Rumination Inventory (ERRI; Cann et al., 2011) measures both intrusive and deliberate rumination about the event and its aftermath. Unlike most rumination scales, which tap depressive rumination tendencies, the ERRI assesses the degree and style of rumination about the traumatic event specifically.
*Candidate application:* The CBI finding implies that the more thoroughly someone's worldview was broken, the more room there is for growth, and also the more there is to repair. More disruption, more potential, more cost. Useful as a reason a mild event produces little growth. Cross-applicable.

**251. "Expert companions": the relational stance that facilitates growth.** [pp. 419-420] The authors describe the key facilitating role as an "expert companion" (Tedeschi, 2011; Tedeschi & McNally, 2011; Rogers-like therapeutic relating). An expert companion: (a) shows respect for the survivor that includes openness to being changed by the survivor; (b) acts as a humble learner, not the expert; (c) points out how aspects of the survivor's post-trauma experience suggest developing growth; (d) co-constructs a narrative with the survivor that acknowledges the horror of the trauma while staying open to change and development; (e) encourages appreciation of trauma's paradoxical nature, where vulnerability can be a strength and trauma or loss can be seen as positive. Both professionals and nonprofessionals can fill the role. The authors say the helper's effectiveness often has little to do with knowing trauma theory and mostly with good listening, patience, acceptance, and humility.
*Candidate application:* This is the single most game-usable finding: the PLAYER CHARACTER can be the expert companion. The posture is a design spec for a companion-relationship questline: listen without fixing, never minimize the horror, notice growth without declaring it, and be changed by what he says. It also suggests that growth in him costs the player something (a willingness to be changed in return). The same posture can characterize other NPCs (a medic, a chaplain-figure, a fellow defector). Cross-applicable to every companion arc, to robot-human relationships (north-star of the project), and to Outer Tepenia.

**252. The integrative facilitation approach and its sequence.** [p. 420] Building on Tedeschi and McNally (2011), Calhoun and Tedeschi (2014), and Tedeschi and Moore (2017), the chapter outlines an integrative therapy drawing on several approaches. Components, in the order listed: (1) psychoeducation about trauma and about PTG; (2) strategies for managing autonomic (physiological) activation; (3) encouragement of constructive self-disclosure about the event and its aftermath; (4) shaping that story into a trauma narrative that explores each domain of growth; (5) using a revised life narrative to establish principles of service and mission, meaning ways of thinking and acting going forward that lock in and even strengthen the growth and are robust against future trauma, fostering resilience. The sequence moves from stabilization, to telling, to narrative, to future-oriented commitment.
*Candidate application:* A usable five-stage skeleton for a multi-quest companion arc: stabilization, first disclosure, narrative shaping, then a forward-looking "service and mission" commitment. The final step is where growth becomes behavior in the world. The costs at each stage: managing arousal is work; disclosure is exposure; narrative requires confronting the horror; commitment binds him to future obligations. Cross-applicable.

**253. Step one: understand the trauma response as the foundation for later growth, and normalize it.** [pp. 420-421] The chapter says trauma is a necessary precursor to PTG, and that moving toward functioning better than before the trauma is an ongoing process, not a fixed goal. Clinicians can help service members see that the negative aspects of the posttrauma experience, though difficult, are a necessary foundation for growth. Psychoeducation about common physical and psychological responses should be provided, and reactions should be normalized as common and expected: they do not indicate a deficit of character or of identity as a soldier. (The authors use "warriors" for the clients.)
*Candidate application:* A scene beat in which the player (or an NPC) tells him that what he feels is normal and does not make him a bad soldier or a coward is a recognizable, non-sappy growth-enabling moment. The "ongoing process rather than static goal" line supports leaving the defector without a finish line. Cross-applicable.

**254. Step two: lower arousal, convert rumination, and use disclosure.** [pp. 420-421] Because the shift from intrusive to deliberate rumination is the hinge for growth, clinicians should train service members in ways to lower anxiety and manage hyperactive sympathetic nervous system activity, and encourage them to consider how intrusive thoughts could change into more constructive, reflective patterns. Self-disclosure is described as especially useful here and helps develop a coherent trauma narrative. The conversations should focus on the aftermath of the trauma and on learning how to use existing social connections and establish new ones after discharge.
*Candidate application:* For a ~10-15-years-out defector, "after discharge" is the relevant terrain: what he needs to disclose may be less the original event than the aftermath (isolation, lost unit, the cost of leaving). New social ties are explicitly part of the treatment target, which suits companion systems where the player's party becomes his new reference group. Cross-applicable.

**255. Step three: narrative development that keeps trauma as catalyst and holds paradox.** [p. 421] Service members should be asked how they would like their story told. The clinician helps organize it into a coherent narrative where the trauma is the catalyst, and helps the person think dialectically so they can hold paradoxes. Examples given: value and benefit can coexist with, and even come out of, pain and distress; it takes great strength to be vulnerable; recovery after trauma may require others' support and, at the same time, individual strength. The clinician looks for chances to point out growth-related changes by referring to the five domains (personal strength, appreciation of life, new opportunities, enhanced relationships, spiritual change) and introduces stories of other people who experienced PTG to show change is possible. Inside the CBT discussion (Finding 258) the chapter also gives the only two sample belief phrases: an inaccurate "I am weak and powerless" replaced by "I am strong enough to confront the things I fear."
*Candidate application:* The defector's story can be told by him in more than one version, and the player can see which version he chooses. The paradoxes (strength requires help; benefit alongside pain) are good dialogue material because they refuse both the "I am broken" and the "I am fixed" versions. Other characters' stories of growth as persuasive evidence is a game-usable mechanic: an NPC who grew shows him it is possible. Cross-applicable.

**256. Three families of PTG intervention.** [p. 421] The literature reports three main approaches: (1) written or spoken self-expression/disclosure; (2) cognitive-behavioral therapies for PTSD, depression, bereavement, and stress; (3) novel psychosocial interventions that directly aim at promoting growth. Researchers have mostly adapted general guidelines from existing psychotherapies to reduce distress and disrupted functioning while fostering well-being and resolution. The authors call the area emerging and exciting, meaning the evidence base for deliberately engineering growth is still thin.
*Candidate application:* Honest framing: deliberate "growth therapy" is young and unproven, so in-world healers promising to produce growth should be treated as overreaching. A faction selling "transformation" would be an easy target for critical portrayal. Cross-applicable.

**257. Expressive disclosure: mechanisms and the finding that the method barely matters.** [pp. 421-422] Writing about trauma or describing it aloud (with or without a listener) is thought to work through several mechanisms: disinhibiting emotional processes (Pennebaker, 1989); habituating the person to trauma-related thoughts (Foa & Kozak, 1986); and generating insight and meaning (Neimeyer et al., 2010; Pennebaker & Francis, 1996). In a randomized controlled trial, expressive writing facilitated PTG in people with PTSD from military service or sexual assault (Smyth, Hockemeyer, & Tulloch, 2008). Disclosure also raised PTGI scores in college students with unresolved stressors or subclinical PTSD (Slavin-Spenny et al., 2011; Ullrich & Lutgendorf, 2002). Across these studies there was little difference between disclosure methods: whether people wrote, spoke into a recorder, spoke to a passive listener or a facilitator, or got personalized feedback, the PTGI effect was about the same (Slavin-Spenny et al., 2011).
*Candidate application:* Supports giving a defector many low-barrier ways to disclose: a journal, a recording, talking to an unresponsive machine, telling it to a stranger. It does not have to be a dramatic confession to a therapist. Journals, logs, or letters as growth-relevant items fit the project's robotic setting (a robot could "speak to a passive recorder"). Note the cost: disclosure does raise distress short-term in many cases (the chapter does not state this; it is a general caution, flagged as my inference). Cross-applicable.

**258. Standard CBT may also produce growth, especially personal strength.** [p. 422] Beyond the clinician's companion role, the authors say standard trauma treatment may generate PTG. Exposure and cognitive restructuring reduce post-trauma or post-loss distress (Foa et al., 1999; Resick & Schnicke, 1992; Sherman, 1998) and may also increase growth. The logic: if growth arises from changing beliefs about self and world, then restructuring strategies could lead to it. Some domains may rise more with CBT; for example, prolonged exposure aims to replace inaccurate beliefs ("I am weak and powerless") with accurate positive ones ("I am strong enough to confront the things I fear"), and that shift maps onto the personal-strength domain. CBT also teaches skills that promote growth: recruiting social support, sharing vulnerable information, engaging in meaningful activities, using coping skills. This is theoretical inference; the chapter does not present trial data showing CBT raised PTGI scores.
*Candidate application:* Growth need not come from a special experience; ordinary hard work, such as facing a feared thing, can yield it. A defector who has done years of unglamorous exposure (returning to places, handling weapons, speaking in public) may have more personal-strength growth than he credits himself. Keep it incremental and effortful. Cross-applicable.

**259. What science has not established: PTG is mostly cross-sectional, and its cause is disputed.** [pp. 422-423] Most PTG research is cross-sectional and examines correlates (environmental, social, psychological, demographic). Few studies track PTG over time or look at what before/during/after-service characteristics build or maintain it in military personnel. Some authors claim causal models of PTG do not exist. The chapter lists rival interpretations of what the post-trauma transformation actually is: improved coping strategies; distortions of reality (positive illusions); personality change; a natural tendency to change and grow with time; or genuine changes in behavior and identity. The authors do not resolve this; they present all five as live. This is the chapter's only treatment of the "illusory growth" caution, and it is brief and uncommitted.
*Candidate application:* The ambiguity can be kept in the fiction: whether the defector has "really" grown or tells a flattering story is open. Different NPCs can read it differently (someone sees real change, someone sees a story he tells himself). Keeping the question unresolved fits "no costless good endings" because the player never gets a certified reward. Cross-applicable.

**260. Longitudinal data from other populations: growth can rise or fall.** [p. 423] A 9/11 convenience sample of 1,505 Americans (direct or indirect exposure) showed PTG significantly declined over 6 months, though effect sizes were small. Lower PTSD symptoms and increases in positive worldview, acceptance, and positive reframing went with higher PTG at follow-up (Butler et al., 2005). In contrast, several studies of cancer patients found PTG rises over time, mostly in the first few months after diagnosis (Danhauer et al., 2013), and younger age, positive coping (positive reinterpretation, problem solving, active-adaptive coping), more social support, spirituality, and better mental health predicted more PTG (Danhauer et al., 2013; Widows et al., 2005). Different events and populations show different time courses.
*Candidate application:* Time course is event-dependent: growth after an ongoing hardship versus a single catastrophe could be designed differently. Supports a growth value that can decline if the supports drop away (a growth stat that drifts back). Cross-applicable.

**261. Veteran PTG trajectories (Tsai et al.): stability, but not for everyone.** [p. 423] Tsai et al. (2015) identified five trajectories of PTG over 2 years among U.S. veterans: consistently low (33.6%), moderately declining (19.4%), increasing (16.8%), dramatically declining (15.7%), and consistently high (14.5%). The text then says "more than half (59.4%) over a 2-year time frame" in a way that is garbled in the source and cannot be interpreted reliably (flag; I do not know what the 59.4% refers to). Veterans reporting at least "moderate" PTG maintained that level 2 years later. Tsai and Pietrzak (2017; the reference title says a 4-year nationally representative cohort) found three trajectories: low and decreasing PTG (74.0%), consistently moderate (12.0%), and high and increasing (14.0%). The authors conclude there is evidence of PTG stability over time, but not in everyone, and that predicting who keeps growth needs more research.
*Candidate application:* A large share of veterans do not grow, and a notable share lose growth they reported. This gives "who does NOT grow" a realistic majority share in the 2017 cohort (74.0% low/decreasing). A defector who reached "moderate" and held it would sit in a minority, and one of the five trajectories (dramatic decline) is a fitting model for a growth that collapses after a setback. Write loss and relapse of growth as possible. Cross-applicable.

**262. Candidate predictors still to be tested.** [p. 423] The authors list psychosocial variables that may predict or result from PTG and need longitudinal study: optimism, positive affectivity, emotion regulation, openness to experience, coping strategies, social support, mindfulness, empathy, among others. They add that individual characteristics, including demographics and the nature of the traumatic event, are starting to be linked to PTG. The chapter's references include work on alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing emotions) as a predictor of both growth and distress, but the prose itself does not discuss it.
*Candidate application:* A short menu of traits to assign when deciding how likely a given character is to grow. Empathy and emotion regulation in particular suit a robot character. Cross-applicable.

**263. Growth is relational: social support and partner concordance.** [p. 424] Research has mostly examined social support as a promoter of the survivor's PTG, and two meta-analyses show a moderate positive relationship (Prati & Pietrantoni, 2009). Several studies show that when one partner in a close relationship reports PTG, the other reports a matching level. This appears in bereaved parents, cancer patients and their partners or caregivers, and couples who endured a severe flood. The authors say more work is needed on how PTG functions between people and in larger groups.
*Candidate application:* A person close to the defector may grow in step with him (or fail to). A romance or deep friendship can carry shared growth, and the loved one's own posttraumatic change is a legitimate story. This suits the project's robot-human love north-star: both partners are changed. Cost: if one stagnates, the other may lack support. Cross-applicable.

**264. The veteran-to-veteran disclosure finding.** [p. 424] Canevello, Tedeschi, and Blevins (2016, conference presentation) reported that post-9/11 veterans showed higher PTG when they had shared their experience with a close other and perceived that person's response as supportive and understanding. The effect was bigger when the person they told was another veteran. Veterans who had disclosed were more likely to look for chances to act as expert companions for other veterans. The authors describe the spread of PTG as mediated by increased interpersonal responsiveness. Less is known about this "spreading" among military friends and family than among civilians.
*Candidate application:* A defector would likely benefit most from disclosing to another defector or person who served, and growth makes him more likely to become a listener for others (a mentoring beat that is also a cost, since it requires him to hear other people's pain). A fellow-defector NPC or a cross-faction ex-service person is the most potent listener. Cross-applicable to factions with exiles or ex-combatants and to Outer Tepenia.

**265. Collective and family-level growth.** [p. 424] Families have traditionally been treated as the context that supports or hinders an individual's growth, but there has been little thought about shared family growth processes: shared changes in assumptive-world beliefs and an increased conviction that together they can withstand future stressors (Antonovsky & Sourani, 1988, on family sense of coherence). The authors suggest extending the PTG model formally to military families and how trauma shapes individual members' and collective processes (Berger, 2015). Traumatic events often affect many people at once, and even seemingly individual events affect loved ones and the network.
*Candidate application:* Factions or communities can be modeled as having collective growth (a shared assumption rebuilt, a shared conviction of surviving the next shock). Also relevant to any population that endured a disaster or war together, including Outer Tepenia settlements and robot communities. Cost: collective growth can enforce a shared story and punish dissent. (That cost is my extension, not the chapter's.)

**266. The chapter's closing cautions: PTG is not an easy fix.** [pp. 424-425] The Conclusion names three challenges for applying PTG in the military: defining what counts as trauma in a military population; getting service members and veterans to engage in the psychological and social processes that can lead to PTG; and encouraging them to serve as expert companions for others. The authors warn that PTG may look like a natural fit for service members because it emphasizes strength and positivity, yet it is not an easy solution to PTSD symptoms or other distressing outcomes. The process itself can be arduous, and in the end significant traumatic distress often remains. Its outcomes are nevertheless life altering, may spread to people close to the veteran, and may extend to the society the veteran is better positioned to serve as he or she turns trauma into growth.
*Candidate application:* This is the chapter's clearest "growth with cost" statement and can be used almost verbatim as a design principle: the arc is hard, the distress persists, and the payoff is real but partial and relational. Also note the risk the authors hint at, that a strength-and-positivity frame is attractive to service members; the same frame can become pressure to perform growth. (The explicit "growth as pressure" caution is NOT stated in this chapter; flagged as my inference from this sentence.) Cross-applicable to every companion.

**Items Ch.23 does NOT contain (so no one looks for them):**
- No case vignettes or named patients. No quotes from veterans. No session scripts.
- No PTGI item wording, scores, cutoffs, or subscale means; no cutoff for "meaningful growth."
- No discussion of moral injury, guilt, shame, or perpetrator-type trauma; no discussion of defection, desertion, or leaving service on principle. All military data are U.S. samples.
- "Illusory growth" is only a single clause (distortions of reality / positive illusions). The reference list includes works on genuine versus self-reported growth (e.g., Frazier et al., 2009), growth and "depreciation" as independent experiences (Cann et al., 2010), and constructive versus illusory sides of growth (Zoellner et al., 2008), but the prose does not unpack them.
- No treatment of growth as social pressure, or of people harmed by being told they should have grown. No sex/gender, age, rank, or combat-exposure breakdowns.
- The "59.4%" figure in the Tsai et al. (2015) trajectory sentence is unreadable/garbled as printed (see Finding 261).

**Chapter-level takeaways for the defector (Ch.23):**
- Growth comes out of the struggle with the aftermath, begins as intrusive rumination and distress, and shows up as a slow shift toward deliberate, chosen reflection; the early stage looks like suffering.
- Growth is domain-specific (personal strength, new possibilities, relating to others, spiritual change, appreciation of life), so the defector can be strong in some and untouched in others.
- It coexists with real symptoms: most studied veterans with PTSD still reported moderate, stable growth; distress usually remains. Many veterans (74.0% in one cohort) show low or decreasing growth, and some lose what they had.
- The strongest lever is a listener: an "expert companion" who respects him, learns from him, does not minimize the horror, and is willing to be changed. Disclosure to another veteran is especially effective, and the method of disclosure matters little.
- The supports that matter (openness in the nearby group, a culture that permits telling) are partly outside his control; a defector who has left his reference group may lack them and must build new ones.
- The late-stage payoff is a revised life story with "service and mission," more preparedness for the next shock, and a tendency to become someone else's listener, which is a real obligation and cost, not an unconditional reward.

## Cross-cutting synthesis — the highest-value findings across all sections (updated 2026-10-05 for §6b)

For the Cancer defector specifically, the material converges on a coherent, clinically-grounded shape for "what
does a decade-plus-out, functional-but-still-carrying-it presentation actually look like":

1. **Structural isolation from a peer "tribe"** (Finding 76) is the single sharpest, most specific wound — not
   generic loneliness, but the absence of anyone who occupied his exact structural position, which no amount of
   general community warmth in Cancer can fully substitute for.
2. **A stable, self-built, control-based equilibrium that is fragile specifically to intimacy** (Findings 62,
   74) — the "Scott" vignette's pattern of a controlled post-war life disrupted not by new trauma but by
   *closeness itself* is probably the single strongest mechanism for a romance arc that doesn't rely on an
   external trigger-of-the-week.
3. **Functioning rebuilt first, formal treatment (if any) addressed later and separately** (Findings 97, 101) —
   the "Sean" vignette's whole arc-shape is the closest real-world template available anywhere in this research
   for his likely decade-plus trajectory, including a plausible peer-mentor role he could occupy in the present
   day.
4. **The polytrauma clinical triad** (Finding 118) is the direct evidentiary justification for his confirmed
   "one or two chronic injuries" detail — chronic pain, PTSD, and TBI cluster together in the real world far
   more often than any one occurs alone.
5. **Trust and safety** (CPT's five schema domains, Findings 33/35, cross-validated independently by the
   "Reggie" mindfulness vignette, Finding 52) is the best-supported candidate for his core unresolved stuck
   point, likely **overaccommodated** (a global belief like "no one in a position of power can be trusted")
   rather than tied to one narrow, already-resolved incident.
6. **Moral injury, distinct from fear-based PTSD** (Findings 61/81, and the "I let my friend die" stuck point in
   Finding 74) is a strong candidate for the actual content of his defining wound, tied to his defection itself
   rather than only to what was done to him.

7. **Moral injury's TYPE sets his voice** (Finding 191; formation chain Finding 193). Betrayal-based injury runs
   through anger and distrust; self-perpetration runs through shame and guilt. A man who served the machine and
   then watched it betray its own stated values can carry both, in tension. Two craft rules follow from the
   treatment chapter: nobody talks him out of it with "it was war" (Finding 201), and repair is deeds and
   belonging, not talk alone ("balance the scales", Finding 202; "violating a value is not destroying the
   value", Finding 200). Together with item 6 this is the strongest-supported shape for his defining wound.
8. **He left without a handoff** (Finding 183). The suicide chapter calls separation from service a documented
   high-risk transition that reactivates worthlessness, shame and guilt, and says every discharge needs a written
   crisis plan, local services and a targeted referral; a defection precludes all three. Pile-up of stressors
   (legal jeopardy, lost status, chronic injury, strained relationships) is the documented pre-crisis pattern
   (Finding 165), and concealment is expected (Finding 168). Handle as a quiet background condition, never a
   plot device.
9. **A spiral, not a staircase** (Finding 224; cyclical decompensation Finding 218; the "standard treatment failed
   me" shame Finding 229). His stability should dip under stress and even under good news, each dip taxing the
   people around him, and the first healing beat can be simply being told his reactions are sensible survival
   adaptations (Finding 225).
10. **The loud symptom fades; the quiet one stays** (Finding 157). After real PTSD care nightmares usually resolve
    and insomnia persists; he sleeps anywhere but the bed (Finding 154), and at his age and with a chronic injury
    an undiagnosed, treatable body problem such as sleep apnea may be hiding inside "the trauma" (Finding 145).
    He would accept "fix my sleep" help long before talk therapy (Finding 159).
11. **Growth is available but costed** (Finding 236). It starts as suffering (intrusive rumination turning
    deliberate, Finding 239); it is domain-by-domain and uneven (Finding 249); it coexists with persisting
    symptoms (Finding 247); in one veteran cohort 74.0% showed low or decreasing growth (Finding 261); and the
    chapter's own conclusion is that it is "not an easy solution" and significant distress often remains
    (Finding 266). The strongest lever is the listener: an "expert companion" who respects him, does not
    minimize the horror and is willing to be changed (Finding 251) — which makes the **player character** the
    natural expert companion, and makes his growth cost the player something in return.

None of the above is proposed as settled backstory — it is the strongest, most convergent material this
extraction surfaced, offered as candidates for whoever writes his full character file next.
