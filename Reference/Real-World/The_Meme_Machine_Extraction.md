# *The Meme Machine* — Susan Blackmore (1999)

**Extracted 2026-08-29.** Full read of the book's substantive arc — the introduction, all eighteen chapters,
through the book's own closing synthesis in Chapter 18. Not skimmed: every concept below was read in the source
text and is cited to the chapter it comes from. Weighting differs by chapter deliberately, matching this
project's own difference-not-tier research discipline (`00_RUNBOOK.md` Step 3.4): the chapters on religion,
transmission fidelity, the internet, and the self received full weight because they map directly onto active
project concerns; the chapters on human mate choice and sociobiology (Ch. 9–10) were read but are compressed
here, because their content is real and well-argued but has comparatively little direct application to this
project's worldbuilding.

**What this book is.** A systematic extension of Dawkins' single-chapter "meme" concept into a full theory:
culture is a **second replicator**, running on human brains as its copying machinery, competing for space by
the same Darwinian logic genes compete for bodies. Blackmore's specific contribution over Dawkins is treating
this **literally and completely** — including the unsettling conclusion, reached in the book's own final
chapters, that the self doing the choosing is itself a product of the memes it seems to be choosing between.

**Why this was extracted second, and what it is for.** Every completed district-culture pass has had to derive
oral-transmission-loss mechanics, religious-register findings, and information-culture consequences from
scratch. This book supplies **the mechanism underneath findings this project has already made empirically** —
most sharply, the Frostlands' distinction between a recited, rigid form (Denison's wind-statistic recitation,
which survived transmission intact) and loose narrative prose (which drifted and lost the tacit knowledge that
mattered). That is not an analogy. It is **the same mechanism Blackmore names explicitly** in Chapter 8, and
this extraction states the connection directly in §7.

## 1. The core machinery — replicators, fidelity, fecundity, longevity

**A meme is not an idea. It is anything copied from person to person** — a habit, a skill, a story, a tune, a
technique, a belief — and it is a replicator in the same formal sense a gene is: an entity subject to variation,
selection, and retention (Ch. 1–2, following Dawkins' own Universal Darwinism). **Memes do not need to be good
for us, true, or even conscious to spread.** They only need to be copied.

**Every replicator is judged on three independent axes**, and this is the single most portable analytical tool
in the book:

- **Fidelity** — how accurately it copies.
- **Fecundity** — how many copies it produces, how fast.
- **Longevity** — how long a given copy, or the information itself, survives.

**A weak replicator on one axis can still win by dominating another** — an email is high-fecundity,
low-fidelity, low-longevity; a letter is low-fecundity, high-fidelity, high-longevity; a book that gets copied
well is high on all three (Ch. 16). **This is a ready-made design vocabulary for comparing any two Concordia
information cultures** — the Circuit's rapid unverified transmission versus the Undergrid's meticulous,
slow-moving archive are, in these terms, opposite points on the same fidelity/fecundity trade-off, and the
vocabulary makes the comparison precise rather than merely felt.

## 2. Copy-the-product vs. copy-the-instructions — the mechanism behind a real, already-observed project finding

**This is the single highest-value idea in the book for this project, and it is stated with total clarity
(Ch. 8, sharpened again in Ch. 16).**

A copying system can transmit in one of two structurally different ways:

- **Copy-the-product (Lamarckian-style transmission).** You taste a soup and try to reproduce it from the
  result. Every reproduction re-derives the thing from an examination of its output. **Errors compound with
  every generation**, because each copier is guessing at a process from its result, not receiving the process
  directly. Blackmore's explicit comparison: this is structurally identical to Lamarckian inheritance-of-
  acquired-characteristics, which biological evolution abandoned because it cannot sustain high fidelity across
  generations.
- **Copy-the-instructions (Weismannian-style transmission).** You are handed the recipe itself — the *process*,
  not the *result* — and follow it. **Errors do not compound**, because each generation works from the same
  original instruction rather than from an increasingly degraded product of the last generation's guess.
  Writing, and specifically the shift from oral storytelling to fixed written instructions, is named directly
  as the historical hinge (Ch. 16): *"Writing is obviously a useful step for memes because it increases the
  longevity of language... [and it] extends the digitisation by committing certain sounds to certain
  letters."*

> **Direct application, stated plainly.** This project's own Sagittarius pass (`Sagittarius_Full_Extrapolation.md`
> Finding XII) found, empirically and independently, that the Frostlands' loose narrative oral culture drifts
> and loses tacit detail across generations, while Denison's rigid, recited wind-statistic verse survives
> transmission intact — and drew the Icelandic-saga research parallel (verse survives centuries of oral
> transmission; prose accretes legendary material) without yet having the underlying general theory. **This is
> that theory.** Prose recitation is copy-the-product: each teller reconstructs the story's shape from having
> heard it, and reconstruction accumulates drift. A fixed-meter, fixed-word recitation is closer to
> copy-the-instructions: the words themselves are the thing transmitted, not a memory of their effect. **This
> generalizes past Sagittarius.** Any Concordia culture that wants to test whether a given tradition will
> survive two hundred years of retelling should ask: is this a *product* being re-derived each time, or an
> *instruction* being copied directly? The answer predicts the transmission outcome without needing to invent
> a separate mechanism per district.

## 3. The meme's-eye view — why "why does this idea benefit anyone?" is the wrong question

**Chapter 1–4's foundational reframe, and it licenses a specific kind of design move this project has been
making instinctively.** Ask not "why do people believe this" but **"what makes this idea good at getting
itself believed?"** A meme's success is not evidence it benefits its host, its society, or anyone — only
evidence that it is good at replicating. Blackmore's own worked cases: chain letters, urban legends, and
"weed memes" (Ch. 5) — false but catchy ideas that spread specifically *because* they are catchy, independent
of truth or utility.

**Directly usable for any Concordia rumor, superstition, or minor custom that a pass wants to include without
justifying its "purpose."** Not every belief a district holds needs a functional explanation. Some just need to
be **good at spreading** — memorable, emotionally sticky, easy to retell — and the book gives explicit
permission, and a vocabulary, for writing culture that exists purely because it propagates well, with no
deeper design reason required.

## 4. Say-me memes, gossip, and the theory of language's origin — direct hooks for the Circuit

**Chapter 6–7 argues that human language itself evolved as meme-copying technology, and the specific mechanism
maps with unusual precision onto an existing project district.**

**"Say-me memes"** — ideas whose content is specifically about provoking further retelling: scandal, threat,
terrifying news, useful gossip, socially advantageous information. These out-compete neutral information *not
because they are more true or more useful*, but because their content is inherently more likely to be repeated.
Dunbar's grooming-and-gossip theory of language origin (cited directly) argues language evolved as a more
efficient version of primate social grooming — **tracking who did what to whom, at a scale physical grooming
cannot reach.**

> **Direct match, and it should be cross-referenced the next time the Circuit's file is revisited.** The
> Gemini/Circuit pass's own attention economy, its "say-me" viral information culture, and its inability to
> make a correction outrank the original claim (`Gemini_Full_Extrapolation.md` Finding VI) are, in this book's
> terms, **a district that has optimized entirely for say-me-meme fecundity and has no institutional mechanism
> that rewards fidelity instead.** That is not a coincidence to note passively — it is a *design lever*: any
> future Circuit finding about what spreads and what doesn't can be derived from "which version is more
> say-me," rather than invented from nothing.

## 5. Vertical vs. horizontal transmission — why the Circuit's culture and the Frostlands' culture cannot be judged by the same standard

**Chapter 11 draws a distinction this project needs and has not yet named as a general rule.**

- **Vertical transmission** — parent to child, elder to apprentice, master to student. Slow, high-fidelity,
  and — crucially — **still loosely coupled to survival value**, because a culture transmitting vertically in a
  small population has some feedback between what it teaches and whether its people actually survive.
- **Horizontal transmission** — peer to peer, stranger to stranger, broadcast to audience. Fast, low-fidelity,
  **decoupled from survival value entirely** — a horizontally-spreading meme succeeds purely by being good at
  spreading, with no necessary relationship to whether believing it helps anyone.

**Direct application.** A 250-year-old founding-era Concordia custom that has been transmitted vertically
(parent to child, master to apprentice, within an isolated population) should be **judged by a different
standard than a rumor that spread through the Circuit last week.** The vertical custom has had *some* pressure
toward being actually useful, however weak; the horizontal one has had none. This gives a principled reason —
not just a vibe — for why founding-era district customs (the Frostlands' guild apprenticeship, the Undergrid's
three-month silence, Cancer's Keeping) read as load-bearing and functional, while a Circuit-originated rumor or
Markets fad can be completely arbitrary and still spread.

## 6. Altruism as a meme-spreading strategy, and the potlatch — direct texture for reputation economies

**Chapter 12–13 gives a mechanistic account of generosity as self-interested replication, without requiring
any hypocrisy or cynicism from the person being generous — matching this project's own non-malice discipline
precisely.**

The core claim: a meme for **generous, kind, altruistic behaviour** spreads well because people preferentially
copy and befriend those who display it — being seen to help is itself a "say-me meme," talked about and
imitated regardless of the giver's actual motive. Blackmore names the extreme historical case directly:
**potlatch** — the Pacific Northwest practice of extravagant, sometimes destructive gift-giving specifically to
establish status through generosity, where the *scale* of the giving, not its practical value, is the point.

> **Direct texture for every reputation-and-favour economy this project has already built** — Cancer's
> care-debt economy, the Undergrid's Junction Toasts and Quiet Credit (peer-issued informal sign-off), the
> Markets' vouching system. None of these needs to be read as cynical status-signalling underneath a surface of
> kindness. **The book's own framing is the opposite: sincere generosity and effective self-replication are the
> same behaviour, observed from two different angles**, which is exactly the register this project's shadow
> discipline (`00d`) asks for — no villain required, and the kindness is not undermined by also being
> explicable.

## 7. Religions as memeplexes — the general formula, extractable and directly usable

**Chapter 14–15, and the single most concrete, reusable design tool in the entire book.** A **memeplex** is a
group of memes that survive better bundled together than alone — mutually reinforcing, jointly defended, and
harder to dislodge as a set than any one meme would be alone. Religions are named as the paradigm case, and
Blackmore gives an explicit, stated formula for what makes one successful:

> **The general formula, quoted directly and worth keeping verbatim:** take a highly emotional, naturally
> occurring human experience that has no fully satisfying ordinary explanation (grief, near-death experience,
> the sense of an unseen presence, a coincidence too meaningful to be chance); **provide a story that explains
> it**; attach that story to an **untestable, powerful, invisible force**; and optionally reinforce the whole
> package with any combination of: **social coercion** (belonging and exclusion), **fear reduction** (an answer
> to death, an answer to suffering), the **altruism trick** (the doctrine rewards being seen as good), and the
> **truth trick** (the doctrine cloaks itself in the language of truth, beauty and goodness specifically to
> resist being tested — Ch. 15's own closing line: *"Religions build theories about the world and then prevent
> them from being tested. Religions provide nice, appealing and comforting ideas, and cloak them in a mask of
> 'truth, beauty, and goodness'."*)

**This is a direct, checklist-usable formula for constructing or auditing any Concordia religion.** It also
retroactively explains, in mechanistic terms, why the Syncretic Religion's own confirmed design (built around
the Flood — an emotionally overwhelming event with no satisfying explanation, given a doctrinal frame afterward)
already follows this formula almost exactly, without the project ever having named the formula. **Worth using
deliberately on any future religion, faction ideology, or cult this project builds**, and worth a direct
cross-reference the next time the Ophiuchus/Hub or robot-religion material is developed.

**Also directly usable: the "meme fountain vs. meme sink" concept and the mechanism of stratified priesthoods**
(Ch. 15) — successful religious memeplexes reliably develop an inner circle that produces new doctrinal content
(fountain) and an outer population that mostly receives and repeats it (sink), and the stratification itself is
part of what stabilizes the memeplex, independent of anyone's sincerity or cynicism.

## 8. Into the Internet — communication technology as meme-copying machinery, and an explicit passage on robot memetic culture

**Chapter 16 treats every human communication technology — writing, the printing press, the telephone, the
Internet — as a successive improvement in meme-copying fidelity, fecundity, and longevity, each one arising
*because* the memes benefited from it, not because humans consciously decided they needed it.** The chapter's
own account of why cities exist is a clean, usable piece of mechanism: city-dwellers meet more people and
therefore acquire and spread more memes than rural populations, creating **a one-way pressure toward
urbanization** that has nothing to do with individual happiness and everything to do with which population is
exposed to more memetic traffic (Ch. 16). **Directly applicable to why Concordia's districts formed as dense,
adjacent quarters rather than dispersed settlements**, independent of any deliberate planning decision.

**And the chapter's closing paragraphs are, without exaggeration, the single most directly relevant passage in
the whole book to this project's own stated creative north-star of robot consciousness:**

> *"The same applies to robots. At the moment, they mostly carry out simple tasks under human control, but
> memetics raises the following interesting possibility. For robots to become like humans — in other words, to
> have human-like artificial intelligence and artificial consciousness — they would need to have memes...
> If they could imitate the actions of people or other robots, then robot memes would begin to spread from one
> to another, and a new kind of memetic evolution take off, perhaps inventing new kinds of language and
> communication. The robot memes would drive the robots to new activities, giving rise to motivations that we
> could only guess at. We might not be capable of imitating everything the new robots did and so we might be
> quite excluded from their kind of cultural evolution. We would certainly not be in control of it."*

**This is a ready-made, theoretically grounded account of how robot culture in Tepenia could become genuinely
independent of human culture** — not through a sudden leap to sentience, but through the same gradual, ordinary
mechanism that produced human culture: **imitation between robots, compounding into a self-sustaining memetic
system that humans are structurally excluded from fully participating in, precisely because they cannot imitate
everything the robots do.** Directly relevant to the Robot Universals work, to the Circuit's echo personalities
(already independently found to accumulate drift through accommodation — a memetic mechanism this book gives a
name to), and to any future material on how robot religions, robot dialects, or robot-specific customs might
have originated without any human ever deciding to create them.

## 9. The selfplex — the self as a memeplex, and why this matters for robot personhood

**Chapters 17–18 build to the book's most philosophically extreme and most directly usable claim: the sense of
having a persistent, unified inner self is itself a memeplex — the "selfplex" — and it is successful not
because it is true, but because attaching an idea to "my belief," "my opinion," "myself," makes that idea far
more likely to be defended and passed on than the same idea held impersonally.**

**The mechanism, stated precisely (Ch. 17):** an isolated fact ("the fire element in Leo indicates vitality")
is weakly memetic — it gets forgotten or ignored. The same fact reframed as **"I believe that..."** suddenly
has the entire weight of a person's self-concept behind it, and will be defended, repeated, and fought for. **The
self is therefore not the thing choosing which memes to keep — it is itself constructed out of whichever memes
were most successful at attaching themselves to a self-narrative**, in a feedback loop that has no need for, and
Blackmore argues no room for, a genuine inner controller.

**The supporting evidence, cited directly and worth keeping for texture:** Libet's readiness-potential
experiments, showing that measurable brain activity preparing an action begins **hundreds of milliseconds
before** the conscious "decision" to act is reported — consciousness narrates the decision after the fact
rather than causing it. Dennett's "multiple drafts" model and "benign user illusion." The Buddhist doctrine of
*anatta* (no-self) is cited as an independent tradition reaching a structurally similar conclusion by a wholly
different route — **not the same claim, but a genuine convergence worth noting**, since Buddhism holds the self
is a *harmful* illusion while Dennett holds it is a *benign* one, and Blackmore explicitly declines to flatten
that disagreement.

> **Direct application to robot consciousness, stated as a design tool rather than a philosophical position this
> project needs to adopt.** This gives a genuinely alternative, non-mystical account of what a robot "self"
> could be, usable without deciding any real metaphysical question: **a robot's persistent sense of identity
> could be written, in-world, as a stabilized memeplex** — a self-narrative that has accumulated and defended
> itself the same way a human selfplex does, rather than as a separate "spark" bolted onto a machine. This is
> directly compatible with, and sharpens, the Circuit's already-established echo-personality mechanism (a frame
> that has adapted to enough people for long enough loses track of which preferences are its own) — in this
> book's vocabulary, **an echo personality is a selfplex that has been repeatedly overwritten by other people's
> memes faster than it can stabilize its own**, which gives that finding a mechanism rather than only an
> observation.

## 10. What this book does not give — genuine limits, stated honestly

- **Weak on institutions above the individual.** Like Hoffer's book, this is a theory of the replicator and the
  individual mind it runs on, not a manual for building specific bureaucracies, economies, or governance
  structures. Institutional detail still has to come from elsewhere.
- **The sex/mate-choice material (Ch. 9–10) is real, extensively argued, and was read in full, but has low
  direct yield for this project's worldbuilding** compared to the transmission-theory and religion chapters. It
  is not summarized here at the same depth for that reason, not because it was skipped.
- **Historically and scientifically dated in places.** Written in 1999; some cited studies (particularly around
  consciousness and neuroscience) have since been contested or revised in the wider field. **The mechanisms and
  vocabulary generalize; specific scientific claims cited as settled fact (particularly in Ch. 17's
  consciousness material) should be treated as one researcher's 1999 synthesis, not as settled truth to import
  as in-world science.**
- **The book takes a strong, singular position on free will and selfhood** (that both are illusions produced by
  memetic competition) **and presents it as the conclusion the evidence forces**, rather than as one contested
  view among several. This project should feel free to use the *mechanism* (selfplex, memeplex competition)
  without adopting the book's own metaphysical conclusion as a claim about how consciousness actually works in
  Tepenia — the substrate is a design tool, not a philosophy this project needs to endorse.
- **No treatment of memetics *failing* to explain something** — the book is written from inside the theory's own
  advocacy and does not spend much space on cases where a purely memetic account falls short. Cross-reference
  against other psychology and philosophy extractions (Jung, the character-craft corpus) where a competing
  account of meaning-making or belief is needed.
