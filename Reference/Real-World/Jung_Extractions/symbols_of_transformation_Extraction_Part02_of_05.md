## Overview of this range

The range opens mid-way through Part II, chapter II ("The Concept of Libido"), where Jung criticizes Freud's treatment of paranoia and schizophrenia and sets out his own **energic concept of libido**: libido is psychic energy, "intentionality in general," not a specific (sexual) instinct. He argues that rhythmic activity is a property of emotion generally, develops a **"pre-sexual stage"** of infancy, and uses a patient's boring gesture to propose that **regression of libido** (caused by an obstacle or checked instinct) can be **canalized** into new activities and symbols; the Indian fire-boring ritual, Agni, soma, Prometheus, and the link of mouth, speech and fire are used as the main amplification, together with the Tejas concept. Chapter III closes with the claims that ritual is a paradigm for handling blocked libido and that fire-making was a Promethean "theft" with guilt.

Chapter IV ("The Origin of the Hero") takes up Miss Miller's third fantasy, "Chiwantopel." Jung treats introversion as a flowing back of libido to an inner object because of an internal conflict ("ambitendency"), the unconscious as universal rather than individual, and the figures that appear: the **Sphinx** (the Terrible Mother, with the Oedipus and Echidna material), the **Aztec** (animus, shadow, father), the name "Chiwantopel" (name-magic and a long amplification of infantile "anal" birth theory: "I make him out of myself"), then **Ahasuerus, Khidr, Elias, Gilgamesh**, Mithras and Helios, and the pairs of mortal and immortal brothers, concluding that solar mythology is "psychology projected into the heavens" and that the hero is the unconscious's longing for the light of consciousness.

Chapter V ("Symbols of the Mother and of Rebirth") reads the dream-city, the city-as-mother, Frobenius's **night sea journey** and whale-dragon myth, water, tree and wood symbols, the Apocalypse's two cities, and works out the central thesis against Freud: the goal of the so-called incest wish is **rebirth**, not cohabitation; the incest prohibition makes the creative fantasy inventive and spiritualizes libido; **a symbol is not a sign or allegory** but the best expression of something unknown. A lengthy "digression" treats symbolic truth, religion, faith and the psychotherapist's task. The chapter then amplifies the tree via Osiris, Isis, Hera of Samos, entwining and devouring, nightmare and the Terrible Mother, the Babylonian Marduk-Tiamat epic and Hebrew dragon-combat, and the hero as personification of libido; the range ends (before the plates) at the mistletoe and Baldur, and resumes at p.448 with the puer aeternus.

## PART II, Chapter II (continued): The Concept of Libido [pp.211-221]

*[note: chapter continues from the previous part. Paragraph numbers in square brackets within the text, e.g. [191], are the Collected Works paragraph numbers; page cites below are PDF pages.]*

### Freud's account of paranoia and the question of "reality loss" [pp.211-213]
- Jung reports that Freud concludes the "paranoidal alteration" is adequately explained by the withdrawal of sexual libido (the paranoic's changed relation to the world is due to loss of libidinal interest) [p.211, para 191].
- Jung's objection: this raises the question whether the loss of reality in paranoia and schizophrenia (which he had described in *The Psychology of Dementia Praecox*) can be due solely to withdrawal of "libidinal condition." The normal *fonction du réel* (Janet's term) cannot plausibly be sustained only by inflow of erotic interest [p.211, para 192].
- In many cases reality disappears entirely with no trace of adaptation; in such states reality is "buried under the contents of the unconscious." Not only erotic interest but all interest has vanished [pp.211-212].
- Arguments against the purely sexual reading: (a) eunuchs have had libidinal interest cut off, yet do not necessarily become schizophrenic; (b) "afflux of libido" is a questionable notion; (c) many apparently sexual contents are metaphors and analogies (fire for passion, heat for anger, marriage for a bond; plumbers and electricians with "male" and "female" fittings are not thereby blessed with potent libido) [p.212].
- Jung had therefore used "psychic energy" in the Dementia Praecox book. Explaining the schizophrenic dissociation purely by recession of eroticism inflates "sexuality" until the word means nothing; he calls "psychosexuality" a symptom of that conceptual inflation. Reality is not "nothing but a function of sex." If it were, introversion of libido in neurosis would produce schizophrenic reality loss, but even Freud says introversion/regression of sexual libido leads at worst to neurosis [pp.212-213].

### Jung's definition of libido as psychic energy [pp.213-215]
- His reserve toward the sexual theory in the Dementia Praecox preface came from the state of the libido theory then; an energic interpretation seemed to fit better than the *Three Essays*. This allowed him to identify "psychic energy" with "libido" [p.213, para 194].
- Libido: "a desire or impulse which is unchecked by any kind of authority, moral or otherwise." "Libido is appetite in its natural state." Genetically it is bodily needs (hunger, thirst, sleep, sex) and emotional states or affects [p.213].
- Developmental argument: the highest differentiations developed from simpler forms; many complex functions now devoid of sexuality were originally derived from the reproductive instinct. Ascending the animal kingdom, the huge numbers of gametes needed for chance fertilization were reduced in favor of assured fertilization and protection of young; the energy freed sought new outlets, e.g., first stirrings of the artistic impulse in animals, at first tied to the breeding season, then gaining functional independence [pp.213-214].
- Music probably belonged originally to the reproductive sphere, but to class music with sex would be like treating Cologne Cathedral in a mineralogy textbook because it consists of stones [p.214].
- To define libido as urge to propagation keeps the artificial distinction between self-preservation and species preservation. In nature there is only a continuous life-urge, a will to live; so far the concept coincides with Schopenhauer's Will. Perceiving movement from outside, we grasp it as the manifestation of an inner will: this projection of psychological perception into material reality is called "introjection" in philosophy; the physical concept of force arises the same way (Galileo: from subjective perception of muscular power). Libido as appetite is likewise an interpretation of psychic energy, which we experience as appetite; we know as little of what underlies it as of the psyche per se [pp.214-215, para 195].
- If libido once used for ova and sperm is now organized in nest-building and cannot be used otherwise, every striving and desire (hunger included) is equally an energy phenomenon [p.215, para 196].
- **Result:** libido expands into "intentionality in general"; it should be understood as "an energy-value which is able to communicate itself to any field of activity whatsoever, be it power, hunger, hatred, sexuality, or religion, without ever being itself a specific instinct" [p.215, para 197]. He quotes Schopenhauer that the Will as thing-in-itself is distinct from its phenomenal manifestations.

### Mythological formulations of the creative force [pp.216-217]
- Examples of attempts to picture the creative force known from subjective experience: Eros as cosmogonic in Hesiod; the Orphic Phanes (the Shining One, First-Created, "Father of Eros"; bisexual, equated with Priapus and Theban Dionysus Lysius); the Indian Kama; Plotinus [p.216, para 198].
- Plotinus: the world-soul is the energy of the intellect; the One compared to light, the intellect to the sun, the world-soul to the moon; also One = Father, intellect = Son. One (Uranos, transcendent), Son (Kronos, rules the visible world), world-soul (Zeus). Three hypostases of one being, which Drews notes is also the formula of the Christian Trinity (Nicaea, Constantinople). Some early Christian sects gave the Holy Ghost (world-soul, moon) a maternal significance. The world-soul has a tendency toward separation and divisibility, the condition of change, creation and reproduction; it is an "unending All of life," wholly energy, which gives birth in reality to what the intellect conceives [pp.216-217].
- The soul is fructified by the intellect (nectar and soma as drinks of fertility and immortality); as "over-soul" it is the heavenly Aphrodite, as "undersoul" the earthly Aphrodite; Aphrodite's dove is the symbol of the Holy Ghost [p.217].

### Energic standpoint versus a sexual theory of neurosis [pp.217-219]
- The energic view frees psychic energy from too narrow a definition. Instinctual processes of any kind are intensified by an afflux of energy from any source (hunger and thirst too); one instinct can be temporarily depotentiated in favor of another. To assume only sexuality is subject to this is "a sort of psychic equivalent of the phlogiston theory" [p.217, para 199].
- Instinct is mysterious, partly psychic and partly physiological, among the most conservative psychic functions, hard to change. Neuroses are therefore better explained by the patient's *attitude* to instinct than by a change in instinct; the attitude is built from congenital characteristics and environment (e.g., neurotic perversion of instinct in a young person is tied to a similar disposition in the parents; the sexual disturbance is secondary). "Hence there can be no sexual theory of neurosis, though there may very well be a psychological one" [pp.217-218].
- **Hypothesis:** it is not sexual instinct but "a kind of neutral energy" that forms symbols such as light, fire, sun. In schizophrenia, loss of the reality function does not heighten sexuality; it produces "a world of fantasy with marked archaic features." Violent sexual disturbances can occur at the onset but equally in panic, rage, religious mania [p.218, para 200].
- Archaic fantasy replacing reality demonstrates only the biological rule that when a more recent system deteriorates a more primitive one replaces it (Freud's simile: bows and arrows instead of guns). A loss of the latest acquisitions of adaptation is replaced by an earlier mode; in neurosis, by regressive reactivation of the parental imagos. In neurosis the substitute is an individual fantasy with hardly any archaic features and there is "never an actual loss of reality, only a falsification of it"; in schizophrenia reality has all but disappeared [pp.218-219].
- Cases: Honegger's paranoid patient who knew the earth was a sphere yet devised a system with a flat disc and traveling sun; Spielrein's patients whose archaic definitions superimpose on word meanings (one said the mythological analogue of alcohol is "emission of seed," i.e., soma; her cooking symbolism parallels the alchemical vision of Zosimos, in which people are transformed in boiling water in the altar bowl; the patient substituted earth and water for "mother") [p.219, para 200]. Spielrein remarked patients seem victims of a deep-rooted folk superstition [p.219, para 201].

### Symbols and concepts as handles; analogy-making [pp.220-221]
- Old superstitions were *symbols* seeking adequate expression for the unknown in world and psyche. German wordplay: *Auffassung* (conception) gives a *Griff* (handle) by which to *fassen*, *begreifen*; the resulting *Begriff* (concept) lets one take possession. The concept functionally corresponds to the magically powerful name; it renders the object harmless, incorporates it into the psychic system, and increases the mind's meaning and power (cf. name-giving in the *Alvissmal*) [p.220, para 201].
- Spielrein: a symbol owes its origin to the striving of a complex for dissolution in the common totality of thought; the complex is robbed of its personal quality; this tendency to dissolution or transformation of every individual complex is the mainspring of poetry, painting and all art. Jung: substituting "energy value" (total affectivity of the complex) for "complex," this agrees with his own view [p.220, paras 201-202].
- Analogy-making gradually enlarged the common stock of ideas and names and widened man's world-picture. Intense "feeling-toned" complexes were reflected in countless analogies, drawing their objects into the "magic circle of the psyche," producing Lévy-Bruhl's *participation mystique*. Citing Steinthal: overwhelming importance attaches to the little word "like" in the history of thought. Canalization of libido into analogy-making probably produced some of the most important discoveries of primitive man [pp.220-221, para 203].

## PART II, Chapter III: The Transformation of Libido [pp.222-]

### The boring woman patient: infantile rhythm and the "pre-sexual stage" [pp.222-225]
- **Case.** A woman with catatonic depressions and hysterical symptoms; at the start of treatment, while telling a painful experience, she fell into a hysterical dream-state with signs of sexual excitement ending in masturbation, accompanied by a violent rotary movement of the left forefinger against the left temple as if boring a hole; complete amnesia afterward. The gesture resembled thumb-sucking or nose- or ear-picking displaced to the temple [pp.222, para 204].
- Her mother later reported: at age two she sat for hours banging a cupboard door shut rhythmically with her head; then bored holes in wall plaster with her finger for hours; masturbation from about her fourth year. So boring is an earlier stage preceding masturbation and distinct from it [pp.222-224, paras 204-205].
- Jung's reading: schizophrenia's peculiarity is the sudden emergence of archaic psychology (hence countless contacts with mythology); apparently individual products are comparable with those of antiquity. The boring symptom, dating from very early, was revived when, after years of marriage, she fell back into early masturbatory habits after the death of the child with whom she had over-lovingly identified [pp.223-224, para 205].
- **Developmental model (para 206).** In infants libido first manifests in the nutritional zone: sucking, a rhythmic intake, with pleasurable rhythmic movements of arms and legs. As organs develop, libido creates new avenues; the primary model of rhythmic movement is transferred to other functions, with sexuality as ultimate goal. A considerable part of nutritional energy must convert into sexual libido and other activities, gradually over childhood and not suddenly at puberty. Two phases in the transition: **sucking** (still nutritive, but outgrowing it into rhythmic activity without intake; the hand becomes an auxiliary organ) and **rhythmic activity in general** (leaving the oral zone for other body openings, then the skin, then rhythmic movements of all kinds: rubbing, boring, picking). This activity, on reaching the sexual zone, may occasion the first masturbation. Libido carries traces of the nutritional phase into new fields, explaining ties between nutrition and sex. If an obstacle forces regression it goes to an earlier stage [pp.224-225].
- Jung proposes calling the period from birth to the first clear sexual manifestations (roughly ages 1 to 4, comparable to the chrysalis stage in butterflies) the **"pre-sexual stage,"** a varying mixture of nutritional and sexual elements. Regressions in schizophrenia and epilepsy seem generally to go back to this stage. Examples: a catatonic young woman during her engagement kissed him and said "Papa, give me something to eat!"; a servant-girl complaining of being pursued with electricity felt a queer feeling in her genitals "as if it ate and drank down there" [p.225].
- Earlier phases of libido can be regressively reactivated; this road is easily traveled; perhaps in earlier human development such transformation was a frequent and normal occurrence, so historical traces are sought [p.226, para 207].

### Fire-boring, Prometheus, and sexual symbolism of fire-making [pp.226-231]
- Abraham drew attention to the ethnological link between boring and fire-making; Kuhn elaborated it. Prometheus may be kin to Indian *pramantha* (the masculine fire-stick); the Indian fire-bringer Matarisvan; the Sanskrit verb *manthāmi* "to shake, rub, bring forth by rubbing"; Kuhn relates it to Greek *manthanō* "to learn," the tertium comparationis perhaps rhythm, the to-and-fro movement in the mind [p.226, para 208].
- Kuhn's chain: *Prometheus* possibly a cognomen of Ithas ("Flaming One"), and the "Flaming One" is the "Forethinker." Prometheus belongs to the Phlegians, related to the Indian priestly family Bhrigu (fire-bringers, "arose in the flame"), the "Shining One"; *Phlegyas* a burnished yellow eagle species. Jung's conclusion: the line from pramantha to Prometheus goes not via the word but via the idea or image, so it would be "an archetypal parallel and not a case of linguistic transmission" [pp.226-227].
- Etymological dispute (Forethinker vs. fire-drill) is unresolved; what is certain is that thinking, precaution or foresight is somehow connected with fire-boring without demonstrable word links; etymology must consider both migration of roots and "autochthonous revival of certain primordial images" [pp.227-228, para 209].
- Indian *manthana* (fire-sacrifice): the fire-stick is the phallus or man, the bored wood below the vulva or woman, the fire the child, the divine son Agni; the two woods are ritually Pururavas and Urvasi, personified as man and woman. Weber's account of the ceremony (blades of grass as "testicles," butter as "semen," three rubbings with the three meters) [pp.228-229, para 210]. A Rig-Veda hymn gives the same imagery; the pramantha is also Agni, the son: phallus is son [p.229, paras 211-212].
- Echoes: German slang Bengel, Stift, Bolzen for a lout; *Stabwurz* / "boy's-love." European survival: the *Nodfyr* (need-fire) against cattle epidemics (Germany 1828); Lanercost Chronicle 1268: false monastery folk taught rustics to make fire by friction, set up an image of Priapus, and a lay brother sprinkled cattle with holy water in which a dog's testicles were dipped [p.230, para 212].
- Jung concludes there is a widespread tendency to equate fire-making with sexuality, and the human mind clings to old forms [pp.230-231, para 213].
- **Wachandi (Australia) spring fertility rite:** dig a hole shaped like female genitals, dance round it all night with spears held like erect penises, thrusting spears into it and shouting an obscene formula. This is a sacramental mating (*hieros gamos*) with the earth; fire-making from two pieces of wood may represent the same idea with two simulacra [p.231, paras 213-215].

### Why libido transforms: obstacle, fear, regression [pp.232-238]
- Freud explains conversion of libido by the incest-taboo and fear derived from the primal horde (tyrant patriarch). Jung's critique: for an instinct to be checked, there must be higher energy on the opposing side. Fear has to be explained; he would have to add an awe-inspiring matron for daughters; he finds this derivation doubtful [pp.232-233, para 216].
- Jung's alternative: tension within a primitive group never exceeds the struggle for existence of the group as a whole. What threatens it is the endogamous tendency, checked by customs such as **cross-cousin marriage**, which balances endogamy and exogamy, giving inner stability and expansion. The source of fear lies in the real risks of the struggle for existence (fear of enemies and hunger predominates even over sexuality, which is easy for primitives: it is simpler to get a woman than food) [pp.233-234, para 217].
- **Regression by obstacle:** libido forced back by an obstacle reverts to possibilities dormant in the individual. Analogies: a dog scratches at a shut door; a man unable to solve a problem rubs his nose, pulls his lip, scratches his ear, drums his fingers, eventually produces masturbation-like gestures. Koch-Grünberg: South American Indians scratch lines on rocks while waiting for canoes (doodling); Maeterlinck's *Blue Bird*: a boy picking his nose who will one day discover a new fire when the earth has grown cold; Spielrein's patient associated boring with fire and procreation ("with a hot iron you can bore through the mountain") [pp.234-235].
- Libido forced back by an obstacle regresses not to earlier *sexual* modes but to the rhythmic activities of infancy which model both nutrition and sex. Jung allows that fire-making might have been discovered through regressive reawakening of rhythm (not the only possible route; flints could do it); his concern is the psychological process [p.235, para 218].
- Rhythmic rites are performed with seriousness and great energy, in contrast to the notorious laziness of primitives; rhythm is a classic means of impressing ideas or activities on the mind; what is impressed and organized is the **canalization of libido into a new form of activity**. Rhythmic activity transfers to sexuality proper, to "decoy mechanisms" (music, dance), and to work. The tie of work to music, singing, drumming is the bridge to sexuality, giving the primitive a chance to sidetrack the task [pp.235-236, para 219].
- **Jung corrects his own earlier view:** he once held that care for the young and cultural activities came from splitting of an originally sexual libido (partly through Freud's influence, partly because of the rhythm element). He later realized rhythm does not derive from the nutritional phase but is "a peculiarity of emotional processes in general": any excitement shows rhythmic expression, perseveration, repetition (e.g., repetition, assonance, alliteration of complex-toned reaction words in the association experiment). Rhythm gives no ground to assume a function originated in sexuality [pp.236-237].
- **Deviation into sex as escape (para 220):** the importance of sexuality makes a deviation into sex easy in regression, so it seems all troubles come from an unjustly denied sexual wish: "typical of the neurotic." Primitives seem to know the danger (Wachandi may not look at a woman during the ceremony; an American Indian warrior test of circling a naked girl, an erection disqualifying). The deviation shifts the problem onto a less dangerous plane but at the cost of adaptation and yields neurosis [p.237].
- **Inner reality as source of fear (paras 221-223):** primitive man fears also dreams, ancestral spirits, demons, gods, magicians; this psychic reality is "just as pitiless and just as inexorable as the outer world, and just as useful and helpful" if one can circumvent its dangers and find its treasures. "Magic is the science of the jungle." Civilized scorn of superstition is as sensible as scorn of medieval pikes and cathedrals; religious and political ideologies are comparable to primitive magic; where collective representations are lacking, private manias, phobias, daemonisms and mass psychic epidemics step in. Psychic reality grows more dangerous the more it is denied; instincts then meet internal as well as external resistance [pp.237-239].
- The will to suppress or overcome instinct's predominance (*superbia*) and lack of co-ordination (*concupiscentia*), unless from external necessity, derives from a spiritual source: "the numinous primordial images," whose operation draws on the individual's specific energy. Even the father's authority subdues sons only when it expresses an image that is numinous or backed by consensus; suggestion by environment is a consequence of the numinosity of the image. Against the view that inner images are merely suggested by the environment, he answers that the suggested content must have originated somehow; mythological utterances were once original numinous experiences observable even today [p.239, para 223].
- **Solar phallus case:** a small business employee with secondary-school education in Zurich (observed 1906) had a vision of a solar phallus, moving to and fro as origin of the wind; Jung found the parallel only in Dieterich's 1910 book; no direct transmission possible [p.240].
- **Archetype defined here:** "an inborn disposition to produce parallel thought-formations, or rather ... identical psychic structures common to all men, which I later called the archetypes of the collective unconscious"; they correspond to the biological "pattern of behavior" [p.240, para 224]. The archetype has "a characteristically numinous effect," gripping the subject like an instinct, and can restrain or overcome instinct [p.240, para 225].

### Mechanism of transformation (para 226) [pp.241-242]
- Whenever an instinct is checked it blocks and regresses; the energy flowing back from the inhibited sphere activates a function in another sphere, thus changing form. Wachandi example: the hole in the earth is probably an analogy of the mother's genitals, since a man forbidden to look at a woman has his Eros revert to the mother; incest being forbidden, the hole serves as mother-substitute; the incestuous energy is "desexualized," led back to an infantile level where it can take another form (another function). It succeeds only with difficulty because the primary instinct has an endogamous ("incestuous") and an exogamous tendency and must be split in two, a splitting tied to consciousness and becoming conscious. Energy changing form carries something of its previous character into the new one; the result may have the character of a sexual act without being one. Fire-boring is only an analogy of the sexual act, just as the latter often serves as linguistic analogy for other activities [p.241].
- The presexual infantile stage restores the libido to its original undifferentiated "polyvalency," confronting it with many possible applications; bound to its object (sexuality), it carries part of this function into the new form, and an analogous object is "invested" and takes the place of the one pushed back [pp.241-242].

### Chapter III continued: the earth-mother, fire from the mouth, fire as libido symbol [pp.242-258]

- **Earth-mother as ideal substitute object.** The "ideal example" of the analogous object invested after regression to the presexual stage is the nurturing earth-mother. Presexual psychology explains her nourishing character; sexuality explains her typical worship, the *hieros gamos*; from this come the age-old symbols of agriculture. In tilling and sowing "hunger and incest intermingle"; the action's aim is the fruits of the field and is "magical rather than sexual." Regression here reactivates the mother as goal of desire "as a symbol not of sex but of the giver of nourishment" [pp.241-242, para 226].
- **Hypothesis on the discovery of fire:** libido forced into regression by checked instinct reactivates infantile boring and supplies "material" to work on (*materia*, *mater*: the object at this stage is the mother). Boring requires only an adult's strength and perseverance plus suitable material; fire-making may have originated as the objective expression of quasi-masturbatory activity. Jung says no proof can be advanced [pp.242-243, para 227].
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad text** (creation by Atman, who is as big as a man and woman joined, splits into husband and wife, she hides in animal forms and he follows, creating all creatures down to ants; then he knows "I am this creation"; he rubs his hands before his mouth and brings forth fire from the mouth, the fire-hole (*yoni*) and the hands). Jung reads this as a description of conversion of libido into fire-making [p.243].
- **Infant gesture:** a one-year-old held one hand in front of its mouth and rubbed it with the other, a habit lost after some months, supporting an infantile-gesture basis for such a mythologem [p.244, para 228].
- **The mouth:** at this age purely nutritive; pleasure in feeding is genuine, not sexual ("nature has here put a premium on pleasure"). The mouth soon also becomes the organ of speech, doubling its importance; rhythmic activity there expresses concentration of libido. The mouth (and less so the anus) is thus the "prime place of origin." The Upanishad makes fire, mankind's great discovery, come from the mouth. Aitareya Upanishad: a mouth split open like an egg; from the mouth came speech, from speech fire [p.244, para 229].
- **Fire and speech.** Fire becomes speech later in the same text. In the Brihadaranyaka dialogue between Yajnavalkya and his questioner, the light of man is the sun, then the moon, then fire, then speech, then the Self (when all else is gone) [pp.245-246, para 230].
- **Mouth, fire, and speech in everyday and biblical language:** "fired" by words, "burning words," "fiery speech"; 2 Samuel 22:9, Isaiah 30:27, Psalm 29:7, Jeremiah 23:29, Revelation 11:5; fire "devouring," "consuming" (Ezekiel 15:4, Deuteronomy 4:24); Acts 2:3-4 (tongues of fire and glossolalia: the *glossa* of fire); James 3:6; Proverbs 16:27; Apocalypse dragons and Leviathan breathing fire [pp.246-247, paras 231-232].
- **Etymologies of fusion** [pp.247-248, paras 233-235]: Indo-European root *bhā* means both "bright, shining" and "speak" (Skr. bhan, Gr. phemi, Lat. fari, fatum, G. Bann); root *la* "to sound, bark" and "to shine" (Skr. las); Egyptian benben/belbel "burst forth, swell, well out" with obelisk sign, also source of light; root *wel* "wave about like fire" and "sound"; root *swen* "sound" and "sun." Music of the spheres (Pythagoras; Goethe's *Faust* "Prologue in Heaven" and Part II dawn scene; Hölderlin) [pp.248-249]. Jung ties these to Apollo with his lyre and to *color-hearing*, suggesting a "preconscious identity" between sound, speech, light and fire [p.250, para 237].
- **Key claim:** speech and fire-use, the two discoveries that distinguish man from all other beings, "have a common psychic background," both being products of psychic energy, "libido or mana" [p.250].
- **Tejas (Sanskrit)** covers: sharpness/cutting edge; fire, brightness, light, ardor, heat; healthy appearance, beauty; the fiery and color-producing faculty of the organism (located in the bile); strength, energy, vital force; passion; spiritual and magical power, influence, dignity; semen [pp.250-251]. It "describes the psychological situation covered by the word 'libido'": it denotes **subjective intensity**; anything highly charged with energy has a wide range of symbolic meanings [p.251, para 238].

### Symbolism of Agni, soma, and fire [pp.251-255]
- Agni, the god of fire, is a divine mediator with affinities to Christian ideas; the Iranian name for fire is *Nairyosagha* "masculine word." Max Müller: the Brahmans looked on fire as both subject and object of the sacrifice, a priest and a mediator between gods and men and also a god; Agni sacrifices himself and offers himself [p.251, paras 239-240]. Bhagavad Gita: the sacrifice, ghee, fire, flesh are all Brahm [p.252, para 241].
- Diotima in Plato's *Symposium*: Eros is a great daemon, intermediary between mortals and immortals, interpreting and conveying messages both ways, "bridging the gap," a cunning huntsman, philosopher, magician, alchemist, sophist; neither mortal nor immortal, living and dying and reviving in one day [pp.252-253, para 242].
- In Avesta and Vedas fire is the messenger of the gods. Daniel 3:24 (the fourth man in the furnace "like the Son of God"); the *Biblia pauperum* (1471) reads the three as the Trinity of person and the fourth as unity of being. Jung: the legend is a magical procedure in which a "fourth" is produced; the fiery furnace is a mother-symbol like the fiery tripod in *Faust* (from it come Paris and Helen, royal pair of alchemy; children baked in the oven in folk tradition); the athanor = body, alembic/cucurbita = uterus [pp.253-254, paras 243-245]. Jehovah is a fire (Isaiah 10:17); Ephraem the Syrian "Thou who art all fire"; apocryphal saying "He who is near unto me is near unto the fire" [p.254].
- Agni = sacrificial flame, sacrificer and sacrificed; as Christ left redeeming blood in wine (*pharmakon athanasias*), Agni is soma, the drink of inspiration and immortality. Soma and fire are identical in Vedic literature; the Hindus saw fire both as symbol of Agni and as emanation of the inner "libido-fire"; soma is defined as "seminal fluid"; the "somatic" significance of Agni parallels the Eucharistic Blood as body of Christ. Soma is also the nourishing drink; Amrita was churned like fire [pp.254-255, paras 246-247].

### Fire-making as robbery, ritual, and the danger of regression [pp.255-258]
- *Manthāmi* also means "to tear off, snatch, rob" (Kuhn). Legend conceives fire's discovery as robbery, akin to the "treasure hard to attain" motif; fire-making is often forbidden, achieved by cunning. Ancient Hindu law punished incorrect fire-making; the Catholic Church lights new fire at Easter [p.255, para 248].
- **Doctrine:** speech and fire-making "represent primitive man's victory over his brutish unconsciousness" and became magical devices against the "daemonic" forces in the unconscious. Both applications of libido demand attention, concentration, inner discipline, furthering consciousness. Incorrect performance causes **regression** of libido, threatening return to the earlier instinctual unconscious state: "perils of the soul" (splitting of the personality / "loss of a soul," reduction of consciousness), which increase the unconscious's power; in civilized man too, this can yield psychic disturbances, possession and psychic epidemics [pp.255-256].
- Blocked libido accumulates instinctuality, producing excesses and aberrations. **Incendiarism** is "really a regressive act of fire-making," sometimes combined with masturbation (Schmid's imbecile peasant youth masturbating while watching fires he set) [p.256, para 249].
- **Ritual as paradigm (para 250):** ceremonial fire-making reminds us of the original numinosity of fire-making; the anamnesis of fire-making is on a level with recollection of ancestors among primitives and of gods at a more civilized stage. Psychologically it is "a meaningful institution," a defined procedure for canalizing libido, "the functional value of a paradigm": showing how to act when libido is blocked. For the primitive "blocking of libido" is concrete: life ceases to flow, glamour fades, plants, animals and people no longer prosper (the I Ching has images for this); modern man feels "I am stuck," loss of zest, depression, and often must be told what is happening because introspection is poor [p.257].
- Easter fire commemorates the redemptive significance of the first fire-boring. Fire-making is the **Promethean theft**: man "made himself guilty of an unlawful intervention, incorporating a fragment of the age-old unconscious into the darkness of his mind," appropriating something precious and offending the gods; the primitive's fear of innovation explains uneasy conscience. Echoed in robbery motifs (sun-cattle of Geryon, apples of the Hesperides, herb of immortality) and in the cult of Diana at Aricia, where only one who plucked the golden bough from the sacred grove could become her priest [pp.257-258].

## PART II, Chapter IV: The Origin of the Hero [pp.259-]

### Hero as finest symbol of libido; Miss Miller's third creation, "Chiwantopel" [pp.259-261]
- "The finest of all symbols of the libido is the human figure, conceived as a demon or hero": symbolism leaves astral and meteorological images and takes human form, passing from joy to sorrow and back, standing at the zenith then plunged into night to rise again, like the sun. Man, like the sun, follows immutable laws, sinks into darkness and rises again in his children [p.259, para 251].
- Miss Miller's third creation: "Chiwantopel, A hypnagogic drama," written after an evening of trouble and anxiety, lying in the dark, receptive, relaxed, passive; lines, sparks and spirals of fire; kaleidoscopic review of recent trivial events [pp.259-260]. Jung regrets there is no information on the cause of her worry or the four intervening years since the first poem (1898; the fantasy is 1902); the lack has an advantage: interest is not clouded by sympathy for her personal fate and attention can go to general validity, "to those wider relationships where every neurotic conflict is seen to be part of human fate as a whole" [p.260, para 252].

### Introversion, resistance, ambitendency [pp.261-264]
- The state resembles that preceding intentional somnambulism and described by mediums; one must be willing to listen to faint inner voices. It indicates an inward-flowing current of libido toward an invisible goal, as if libido had found a powerfully attractive object in the unconscious. Because life is normally directed outward, an exceptional condition (e.g., lack of external objects) is presumed [p.261, para 253].
- **Statement:** the world is not too poor to offer objects of love; "it is rather the inability to love which robs a person of these opportunities." "The world is empty only to him who does not know how to direct his libido towards things and people, and to render them alive and beautiful." What compels us to create a substitute from within is not external lack but our own inability to include anything outside ourselves in our love. Real difficulties alone never drive libido back to the point of neurosis because the **conflict** that is the precondition of every neurosis is lacking; only a resistance, an obstinate "won't" opposing "will," produces regression [pp.261-262].
- Libido is like a steady stream into the world of reality; resistance, dynamically, is not a rock around which the stream flows but "a flowing back towards the source." One part of the psyche wants the external object while another strives back to the subjective world "where the airy and lightly built palaces of fantasy beckon." Bleuler's term **ambitendency** for this dichotomy of will; the most primitive motor impulses are antithetical (flexor muscles innervated even in stretching). Normally ambitendency is necessary for coordination; resistance arises from an abnormal plus or minus on one side, an unknown "third factor" that loosens normally bound opposites into a genuine "won't" and "will" [p.262].
- Freud places the root complex in the incest problem (regression to the parents yields symbols, symptoms and incestuous situations, the source of the incest relations in mythology). Jung adds the **specific inertia of the libido**, which relinquishes no object of the past; stripped of its incestuous covering, Nietzsche's "sacrilegious backward grasp" is a metaphor for reversion to the passive state in which libido is arrested in childhood objects. La Rochefoucauld quoted: idleness is the most concealed and violent passion, a Remora that stops the largest ships [pp.262-263, para 253].
- This dangerous passion "lies hidden beneath the hazardous mask of incest." It appears as the **Terrible Mother**, "the mother of innumerable evils, not the least of which are neurotic disturbances"; stagnant libido breeds baneful phantasmagorias which veil reality [pp.263-264, para 254].
- **Diagnostic argument:** if the obstacle were external, libido would flood the mind with plans to overcome it, hard thinking, not a hypnagogic poem. The passive state "scorns real solutions and prefers a fantastic substitute," so the conflict is internal: "the external object simply cannot be loved, because an overwhelming proportion of the libido prefers an internal object that rises up from the unconscious as a substitute for the missing reality" [p.264].
- The visionary phenomena of this first introversion stage are hypnagogic vision, the basis of actual visions or "self-perceptions" of the libido in symbols [pp.264-265, para 255].

### The prayer "Speak, Lord," and the uniformity of the unconscious [pp.265-268]
- Miss Miller: an impression something was about to be communicated; the words "Speak, Lord, for thy servant heareth—Open thou mine ears" repeat in her. Jung: *communiqué* is a mediumistic expression; the words are a prayer, concentration of libido on the God-image (1 Samuel 3, Samuel and Eli), used here in reverse to direct libido into the depths of the unconscious [p.265, paras 256-257].
- **Uniformity of the unconscious** (para 258): individuals differ in conscious content but grow alike seen from the unconscious; differences arise only through individuation, which justifies an essential portion of the philosophies of Schopenhauer, Carus and von Hartmann. The unconscious includes remnants of the undifferentiated archaic psyche including animal stages; animal reactions show uniformity and constancy. Our sense of human individuality may be a delusion because adaptation makes us see differences in what interests us (a European cannot at first tell Chinese faces apart). Individuality is overrated for its practical importance; "The individual content of consciousness is therefore the most unfavorable object imaginable for psychology, precisely because it has differentiated the universal to the point of unrecognizability." Consciousness works by adaptation in particulars; "The unconscious, on the other hand, is universal": it binds individuals into nation or race and to the men of the past; "by reason of its supra-individual universality the unconscious is the prime object of any real psychology that claims to be more than psychophysics" [pp.265-267].
- **The hero and religious figure (para 259):** biologically the individual is a particle in the mass; the cultural view gave man meaning apart from the mass, developing personality and the cult of the hero. Rationalistic theology's personal Jesus, the Catholic vicar of God, and personalistic Protestantism's historical Jesus meet the need for a visible hero. The religious figure cannot be a mere man; it represents "the totality of all those primordial images which express the 'extraordinarily potent'." What we seek in human form is "not man, but the superman, the hero or god," symbolizing ideas, forms and forces that grip and mold the soul: "the archetypal contents of the (collective) unconscious, the archaic heritage of humanity." In loving this inheritance people turn back to "the mother of humanity, to the psyche, which was before consciousness existed," regaining the power that comes from feeling part of the whole: the problem of **Antaeus**, strong only through contact with mother earth. Temporary withdrawal within limits favors psychic well-being. **Extraversion and introversion**: normal and appropriate reactions to complexes, extraversion as escape from the complex into reality, introversion as detachment from external reality through the complex [pp.267-268].
- **Autonomy of archetypal contents (para 260):** the invocation expresses introversion and the expectation that God will speak empties the conscious mind of activity, transferring it to the divine being constellated by the invocation, "from the empirical point of view ... a primordial image." All archetypal contents have a certain autonomy; there is nothing absurd in expecting "God" to take over the activity and spontaneity of consciousness, since primordial images can do exactly this [pp.268-269].

### The Sphinx, theriomorphic libido, and the Terrible Mother [pp.269-274]
- After the prayer: "the head of a sphinx in an Egyptian setting" appears and vanishes; she wakes briefly. Jung links it to the earlier Egyptian statue fantasy (rigid gesture as functional phenomenon of light hypnosis, *engourdissement*). The Sphinx suggests enigma and stands at the threshold of fate; she is a semi-theriomorphic representation of the mother-imago, "or rather of the Terrible Mother." With no personal context, an individual interpretation is impossible, so he turns to ethnological material on the assumption that the unconscious coins its symbols as it did in the past [pp.269-270, para 261].
- **Theriomorphic representation of libido:** instinct often appears as bull, horse, dog; hybrids and monsters not infrequent (Bertschinger's illustrations). Example: a patient with questionable relations with women dreamt Jung had speared a strange animal, half pig and half crocodile, to the wall. Jung: libido so represented is the repressed "animal" instinct; instinct is not sex alone; the man may damage instinct by lack of sexual repression ("modern" ideas about sex); the dream projects the killing of the animal onto the doctor; the pointed instrument is "the needle of the intellect." "Thus the theriomorphic symbols always refer to unconscious manifestations of libido" [pp.270-271].
- **Two reasons instinctual impulses are unconscious:** general unconsciousness shared by all; secondary unconsciousness through repression of incompatible contents, a symptom of a neurotic attitude that overlooks unpleasant facts for small present advantage [p.271, para 262].
- Repression is directed against instincts in general ("the laws governing all life"), and the regression it causes leads to childhood where parents appear decisive; but the child's inborn instincts also act (children react differently to the same parents); to the child's empty consciousness all determinants seem external and instincts cannot be told from parental will; hence animals representing instincts can also appear as attributes of parents (father as bull, mother as cow) [pp.271-272, para 263].
- Regression further, beyond childhood to the preconscious prenatal phase, produces **archetypal images** "no longer connected with the individual's memories, but belonging to the stock of inherited possibilities of representation that are born anew in every individual." Images of "divine" beings part animal and part human arise here; their guise depends on the conscious attitude: negative toward the unconscious, frightening animals; positive, the "helpful animals" of fairytale. Over-affectionate dependency on parents is compensated in dreams by fear-animals. The Sphinx is such a fear-animal with clear mother traces [p.272, para 264].
- **Oedipus reading:** sent by Hera, who hated Thebes for Bacchus's birth; Oedipus thinks he has beaten the mother-goddess's creature by solving her "childishly simple riddle" and falls into matriarchal incest (marrying Jocasta). Had he been intimidated by the "terrible" or "devouring" Mother he would have been spared; contrasted with Faust's wonder at "The Mothers." The riddle can never be solved merely by the wit of man [pp.272-273].
- **Genealogy (para 265):** Sphinx, daughter of Echidna (beautiful maiden above, hideous serpent below), born of Gaia and Tartarus; Echidna is mother of the Chimera, Scylla, the Gorgon, Cerberus, the Nemean lion, the eagle of Prometheus, dragons, Orthrus (Geryon's dog); with Orthrus, her own son, she begat the Sphinx. The double being corresponds to the mother-imago: lovely human half above, horrible animal half below, "changed into a fear-animal by the incest prohibition." "The riddle of the Sphinx was herself—the terrible mother-imago, which Oedipus would not take as a warning" [pp.273-274].
- For Miss Miller the sphinx's meaning is perhaps near that for Oedipus; there are masculine as well as feminine sphinxes in Egypt; for a woman, danger might come from the father rather than the mother, left undecided [p.274, para 266].

### The Aztec, shadow, and animus [pp.274-]
- Next vision: "the apparition of an Aztec, complete in every detail," hand open with large fingers, head in profile, armored, with a plumed head-dress like American Indians' [p.274]. Jung: a masculine figure hidden in the Sphinx is confirmed. On the personal level the Aztec represents the primitive side of the father, since she is American; in American analyses the inferior side of the personality, the **shadow**, is represented by a Negro or an Indian, whereas a European's dream would show a somewhat shady individual of his own kind. Since Miss Miller is a woman, her shadow ought to be feminine; this masculine figure, given its role in the fantasies, personifies the masculine component of the woman's personality, later called the **animus** [pp.274-275, para 267].
- **Eagle-feather head-dress (para 268):** magical significance; the Indian assimilates the sun-like nature of the bird as he assimilates courage by eating an enemy's heart or taking his scalp; the feather crest is a crown equivalent to the sun's rays (sun identification, cf. Part I); parallels from Wisdom of Solomon 5:16 and Allendorf's hymn of the soul crowned as bride and queen [p.275].

### Chapter IV continued: the hand, the Aztec, the name, anal birth theory [pp.276-287]

- **Crown hymns (paras 268-270):** Allendorf, Laurentii and Sacer hymns offering the soul/bride a crown of joy or victory and "light in his light," used as parallels for the solar crown [pp.275-278].
- **The open hand with large fingers (para 271):** the stress on the hand is odd; a parallel hypnagogic fantasy of a patient: his mother painted on a wall like a Byzantine mural, one hand raised with splayed, swollen, haloed fingers (associations: frog's sucker-fingers, then phallus), and a skyrocket from her hand that proved to be a golden-winged bird (golden pheasant). The hand has a phallic and creative significance; fire is bored with the hand, so fire comes from the hand; Agni was worshiped as a golden-winged bird [p.278-279].
- **Father, mother, anima/animus roles (para 272):** Miss Miller's childhood interest in Aztec remains and the Incas. Jung: as the Sphinx is an aspect of the mother, the Aztec is an aspect of the father. "The mother's influence is mainly on the Eros of her son"; the father influences the "mind or spirit of his daughter—on her 'Logos,'" raising her intellectuality often to a pathological degree, "animus possession" (which, per the Foreword to the second edition, finally led her to insanity). Jung observes that mother complexes are very common in America (strong maternal home influence, women's social position; "more than half the capital in America is in women's hands"); many American women develop their masculine side, compensated in the unconscious by "an exquisitely feminine instinct, aptly symbolized by a Sphinx" [pp.279-280].
- **The "demon lover" (para 273):** the Aztec is the masculine ideal for the primitive, female side of the author; the Italian naval officer she met could not compete with the "demon lover" or the angel who takes an interest in the daughters of men (hence women cover their hair in church where angels hover). Her spirituality personified as the Aztec was too exalted for her to find a lover among mortal men; the conscious attitude has no effect on unconscious expectations, which will assert themselves later as a change of life style, a neurosis or a psychosis [p.280].
- **Naming (para 274):** a name forms in her "bit by bit": "Chi-wan-to-pel," son of an Inca of Peru. Naming is like baptism, creating personality; to know a secret name is to have power over a person (Rumpelstiltskin; Isis compelling Ra to tell his real name); "to give a name means to give power, to invest with a definite personality or soul" [p.281].
- **Popocatepetl and anal birth theory (paras 274-279):** she says the name reminds her of "Popocatepetl." Jung asks why always Popocatepetl and not Ixtaccihuatl or Orizaba: because of its onomatopoeia, with a long string of pop/pouf/poop/poupée/puppis/pupus word-families (doll, child, posterior, ship's stern, blowing/kissing noises) [pp.281-282]. Patient's boyhood fantasy of volcano-like eruption; names for natural phenomena are often drawn from the nearest bodily source (e.g., "Sternschnuppe," South American "piss of the stars," the Pissevache waterfall renamed Voile de la Vierge) [p.282].
  - **Why a revered figure's name is tied to the anal region:** when something is produced from the unconscious, infantile material surfaces first; one must adopt the child's viewpoint, where that region is invested with respect and attention (Oriental tale that Crusaders anointed themselves with the Pope's excrement; a patient's fantasy of her revered father on a commode; excrement-gold link; alchemists seeking the *prima materia* in excrement, "in stercore invenitur"; a religious girl's dream of a crucifix of excrement). Children's valuations differ from ours: the young child connects defecation with a theory of propagation ("that is how things are produced"). Examples: the child in "Psychic Conflicts in a Child" who, sitting long on the toilet, was "doing a little cart and two ponies"; a patient's fairy in a toilet-wall crack giving wishes; the toilet as a place of dreams; Lombroso's two insane artists who believed they created the world from the rectum [pp.282-285].
  - **Case (para 278):** an educated woman in an asylum, apathetic and slovenly ("affective deterioration"), whom Jung doubted was deteriorated; after more than three hours' work he hit on a train of thought producing a violent outburst of affect and complete rapport; that evening she greeted him smeared with excrement, crying "How do you like me now?" Jung's later reading: an attempt to ward off the transference when acting as an adult, but an outburst of positive feeling on the level of regressive infantilism, hence the equivocal question [p.285].
  - **Conclusion (para 279):** "The birth of Chiwantopel from Popocatepetl therefore means: 'I make, produce, invent him out of myself.' It is the creation or birth of man by the infantile route." First men were made of earth or clay (Latin *lutum* mud/filth); the "throwing behind one" motif (Deucalion and Pyrrha throwing the Great Mother's bones, i.e., stones; the Dactyls from dust thrown by Anchiale); excrement as marker or souvenir in popular humor [p.286].
- **A-ha-ma-ra-ma (para 280):** a second spontaneous name; she thought "Asurabama (who made cuneiform bricks)." Jung connects with Assurbanipal, with "Aholibamah" (Byron's *Heaven and Earth*; daughters of Cain who love sons of God) and Ezekiel 23 (Aholah and Aholibah), Esau's wife, "dukes of Edom," Canaanite hill sanctuaries (*bamoth*, *ramah*); whether the neologism truly connects is "open to question." Pointing to Chiwantopel as the longed-for son of God [pp.286-287].

### Ahasuerus, Khidr, and the pair of immortals [pp.287-298]
- She also thought of "Ahasuerus" (the Wandering Jew): this gives "a glimpse into the dynamics of the unconscious creation of personality." The legend is Occidental (13th century, heavy literary elaboration); the figure would exist under another name, e.g., the Comte de Saint-Germain; Oriental parallel **Khidr** (El-Khadr), "eternally youthful Chidher" (Rückert), in Islam a saint and in Sufic circles even a deity, first traced in Koran commentaries (al-Bukhari, al-Tabari) on Sura 18, "The Cave" [pp.287-288, paras 281-282].
- **Sura 18 story:** Moses and his servant (Joshua son of Nun) seek the place where two seas meet; they forget their fish, which makes its way to the sea; they return and find a servant of God endowed with grace and wisdom (Khidr) who warns Moses he cannot bear things he cannot comprehend; Moses accompanies him through incomprehensible deeds; then follows the tale of Dhulqarnein (Alexander) traveling to where the sun sets and rises [pp.289-290, paras 282-285].
- Commentators identify the servant as Khidr, "the Verdant One, the tireless wanderer, the teacher and counsellor of pious men, wise in divine knowledge, the immortal." Per al-Tabari, Khidr and Alexander's army reached the stream of life and unwittingly drank, becoming immortal; Khidr is identified with Elias (Elijah), who ascended in a fiery chariot like Helios [p.290].
- **Matthew 16:28 and Transfiguration:** the saying "some standing here shall not taste of death" and the Transfiguration (Moses and Elias with Christ) suggest immortals besides Christ until the Second Coming; John (John 21) was believed to be one, sleeping in the earth, his breath stirring the dust above the grave [pp.290-291, paras 285-287].
- **The pair and the hierarchy (para 288):** two resembling but distinct figures: Dhulqarnein and Khidr, Khidr and Moses, Khidr and Elias; in Christianity, John leads Christ to the source of life at the Jordan. Vollers compares Khidr and Elias with Gilgamesh and Enkidu and with the Dioscuri (one mortal, one immortal) [p.292].
- **Mithras and Helios:** Klagenfurt relief (Mithras crowns Helios with a crown of rays, shakes his hand, mounts his chariot) and Osterburken monument (Mithras holds the bull's shoulder above bowed Helios, with a crown on the ground; Cumont: the divine prototype of initiation into the degree of *Miles*, sword and crown). Parallel: Heracles threatening Helios and being lent the sun-ship to reach Geryon's sun-cattle [pp.292-293, paras 288-289].
- **Christ and Peter:** Cumont's reading of Mithras's investiture corresponds to Christ and Peter. Peter's cock gives him solar character; he becomes the visible representative of God, suffers crucifixion, replaces the *Sol invictus*, is the *Miles* of Christ in the Malchus scene; the Pope wears the triple crown (a solar attribute), "solis invicti comes." "The setting sun appoints a successor whom he invests with his solar power" (Dhulqarnein gives Khidr eternal life; Khidr gives Moses wisdom; Joshua who unwittingly drank from the fountain was cast out to sea in a boat: the sea-journey motif) [p.293-294].
- **Capricorn and fish (para 290):** Capricorn, the "Goat-Fish," marks where the sun re-enters the cycle at the winter solstice: it climbs like a goat, plunges like a fish. In dreams the fish may signify the unborn child; the sinking sun is child and fish at once; "The fish is therefore a symbol of renewal and rebirth" [p.294].
- **Reading of the Moses story (para 291):** the eighty-year journey is a life-journey; Moses and Joshua grow old and lose the life-force (the fish, which swims to the sea at sunset); at that spot they find Khidr wrapped in his mantle (in another version on an island "in the wettest place on earth," i.e., just born from the maternal depths), a "son of the watery deep" proclaiming divine wisdom, like Babylonian Oannes-Ea (fish-form, rising daily to teach) [pp.294-295].
- Oannes linked with John; with the reborn sun the fish dwelling in darkness becomes the shining day-star: John the Baptist's "he shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost, and with fire" gains special significance [p.295, para 292].
- **Gilgamesh (para 293):** driven by fear and longing, crosses the sea to Utnapishtim, dives for the magic herb, is accompanied home by a banished immortal mariner; a serpent steals the herb (the fish slips back into the sea). Jensen: the banished immortal may be the prototype of Ahasuerus [p.296].
- **Dioscuri motif / dadophors (paras 294-296):** Mithraic bull-sacrifice flanked by Cautes and Cautopates (one torch raised, one lowered; life and death; like sepulchral Erotes). Parallels: the Crucified flanked by two thieves; the Baal of Edessa with Aziz and Monimos (Mars and Mercury); Babylonian triads; Mithras's secret triadic character ("threefold Mithras," Dionysius the Areopagite). Cautes and Cautopates carry a bull's head and a scorpion: Taurus and Scorpio are equinoctial signs, so the sacrifice concerns the sun's cycle [pp.296-297].
- **Interpretive statement (para 296):** "this whole solar mythology is psychology projected into the heavens"; the underlying idea: as man is composed of a mortal and an immortal part, the sun is a pair of brothers, one mortal, one immortal; "Thus the gods, or figures like Khidr and the Comte de Saint-Germain, are our immortal part which continues intangibly to exist. The sun comparison tells us over and over again that the dynamic of the gods is psychic energy. This is our immortality, the link through which man feels inextinguishably one with the continuity of all life." "The life of the psyche is the life of mankind"; the individual is "only a twig broken off from the mother and transplanted" [pp.297-298].

### Libido symbolized by sun and phallus; Attis and the incest motif; the wandering hero [pp.298-305]
- Libido symbolizes itself in the sun or in hero figures with solar attributes, and also in phallic symbols. Late Babylonian gem (Lajard): androgynous deity with a sun-haloed snake on the masculine side and a moon-crowned snake on the feminine side, a lozenge (female symbol) and a phallic wheel [p.298, para 297]. Verona inscription: sun = phallus, moon = vessel (uterus); Perga coin and Attic relief of Men flanked by figures; "sexuality as well as the sun can be used to symbolize the libido" [p.299, para 298].
- **Cautopates, Men, Attis (para 299):** Cautopates carries a cock and pine-cones, attributes of Phrygian Men, who wears the pileus and is boyish like the dadophors, Cabiri and Dactyls; Men merged with Attis, son-lover of Cybele. **Incest reasoning:** when regressing libido is introverted it always reactivates the parental imagos and apparently reestablishes the infantile relationship, but because it is adult libido already bound to sexuality it "inevitably imports an incompatible, incestuous character"; since incest must be avoided, the outcome is death of the son-lover, self-castration, or sacrifice of instinctuality, especially sexuality (abstinence) [p.300].
- **Wandering:** heroes are usually wanderers; "wandering is a symbol of longing, of the restless urge which never finds its object, of nostalgia for the lost mother." Jung's reversal of the solar-myth theory: the hero is "first and foremost a self-representation of the longing of the unconscious, of its unquenched and unquenchable desire for the light of consciousness." Consciousness, in danger of becoming a rootless will-o'-the-wisp, longs for "the healing power of nature, for the deep wells of being and for unconscious communion with life" [pp.300-301]. He quotes the *Faust* "Mothers" scene (Mephistopheles to Faust: goddesses unknown, to be reached by descending into the void with a key, a fiery tripod marking the nethermost place) [pp.302-305].

## PART II, Chapter V: Symbols of the Mother and of Rebirth [pp.306-]

### The swarm of people, horses, and the dream-city [pp.306-309]
- Miss Miller's vision after the hero's birth: a "swarm of people." Jung: this symbolizes a secret, or rather the unconscious; a secret cuts a person off from fellows, disturbing the economy of the libido (hence the benefit of disburdening secrets in treatment); a crowd, especially a streaming mass in motion, expresses violent motions of the unconscious, "an activation of the unconscious and an incipient dissociation between it and the ego" [p.306, para 300]. Horses appear, a battle is fought; Jung provisionally follows Silberer's "functional" category (rush of thoughts; horses as movement or energy), deferring the horses' deeper meaning to the mother-symbols [pp.306-307, paras 301-302].
- **The dream-city** (like a magazine cover she had seen) is perhaps a heavenly Jerusalem [p.307].
- **The city as maternal symbol (paras 303-306):** the city harbors inhabitants like children; Rhea, Cybele and Diana wear the mural crown; Old Testament treats Jerusalem, Babylon, Tyre as women (Isaiah 47, Jeremiah 50:12); strong unconquered cities are virgins, colonies are sons and daughters, cities can be harlots (Isaiah 23:16, 1:21). Myth of Ogyges and Thebe (the city and the wife share a name; the Flood called "Ogygian," to be taken up later). Seizing a land as marriage with it: Indra and Urvara ("fertile land"), the Swedish king Domaldi killed for crop failure (Ynglinga Saga), Rama and Sita ("furrow"), the Chinese emperor ploughing a furrow on accession; Shiva and Parvati (half his body given to her), the lingam in its base; chest or casket as womb, floating like the sun-chest on the waters [pp.307-309].

### Frobenius and the night sea journey [pp.309-312]
- Frobenius: the red sunrise suggests a birth of the young sun and a question of paternity; the primitive answer: the sea (woman, like the fish) previously swallowed the old sun and so became pregnant. All sea-going gods are solar figures, enclosed in a chest or ark for the **"night sea journey"** (Frobenius), often with a woman, shut up in the mother's womb and threatened by dangers [pp.309-310, paras 307-308].
- Frobenius's schema (diagram in source, not reproduced in the text): devouring in the West, sea journey to the East, fire-lighting in the monster's belly, cutting off a piece of the heart, landing, opening, slipping out, heat and loss of hair. His illustrative legend: a hero devoured by a water-monster lights a fire in its belly, cuts a piece of its heart for food, the fish lands, he cuts his way out; his hair has fallen out from the heat; he may free others also devoured [pp.310-311, paras 309-310].
- Parallels: Noah's journey; a Polynesian myth of the hero in the belly of the King Fish Kombili who cuts his way out with an obsidian knife and sees the sun rise; Hanuman in the *Ramayana* who, seized by a sea-monster, grows enormous then shrinks to thumb size, slips inside and out the other side [p.311, para 311]. *[note: the Hanuman episode continues into the next page.]*

### Chapter V continued: the swelling hero, Ogyges, the Higher City [pp.312-319]

- **Hanuman concluded:** after his first escape Hanuman meets another sea-monster, mother of Rahu the sun-devouring demon, who also seizes him by his shadow; he shrinks, slips inside her, swells to gigantic size, bursts and kills her, and escapes. This explains the Indian fire-bringer Matarisvan, "he who swells in the mother." The ark, chest, casket, barrel, ship are analogies of the womb, like the sea into which the sun sinks for rebirth. "That which swells in the mother can also signify her conquest and death. Fire-making is a pre-eminently conscious act and therefore 'kills' the dark state of union with the mother" [pp.311-312, para 311].
- **Ogyges (para 312):** he possesses the mother, the city, so under him the great flood came; typical of sun myths: the hero united with the woman "hard to attain" is exposed in a cask and thrown out to sea, lands on a distant shore and begins new life (the night sea journey is missing in the Ogyges tradition). Rule: typical parts of a myth can be combined in every variation, which makes it extraordinarily hard to interpret one myth without knowing the others. "The meaning of this cycle of myths is clear enough: it is the longing to attain rebirth through a return to the womb, and to become immortal like the sun" [pp.312-313].
- **Galatians 4:26ff.** (Jerusalem above is the mother of us all; children of promise vs. children of the bondwoman): Christians are children of the Higher City, born "not from the fleshly mother but from a symbol of the mother" (cf. American Indians: first man born from a sword-hilt and a shuttle). "The symbol-creating process substitutes for the mother the city, the well, the cave, the Church, etc." [pp.313-314, para 313].
- **Function of the substitution:** regression of libido reactivates childhood ways, above all the relation to the mother; "what was natural and useful to the child is a psychic danger for the adult," expressed by the symbol of incest. Because the incest taboo blocks the path to regression, libido can be canalized into the mother analogies thrown up by the unconscious; it becomes progressive again and attains a higher level of consciousness. With the city in place of the mother, infantile attachment (a crippling limitation) becomes attachment to the city, fostering civic virtues and a useful life; in primitives the tribe takes the place of the city [pp.314-315].
- **Apocalypse's two cities (paras 313-318):** Babylon the Great, the whore on the scarlet beast with seven heads (seven mountains: Rome; waters: peoples; kings commit fornication with her; birds as souls of the damned): "Babylon is the symbol of the Terrible Mother," the Echidna on the dragon, receptacle of the wicked, the underworld; intoxicating drink is a libido symbol (soma-fire-sun) [pp.315-317]. The hymn of Rev. 19 passes "from the lower half of the mother to the upper half, where everything that incest would have made impossible now becomes possible": marriage of the Lamb, with the bride the holy Jerusalem descending from heaven (Rev. 21), i.e., "the mother or mother-imago." In Babylon the impure maid is cast out so that the mother-bride is more surely attained in the heavenly Jerusalem. Jung praises the Church Fathers for keeping the Apocalypse as "a rich mine of primitive Christian symbols" [pp.318-319].

### Water, tree of life, phallic wood [pp.319-324]
- **Water (paras 318-320):** river of the water of life and tree of life (Rev. 22) link to the Ogyges water motif; "The maternal significance of water is one of the clearest interpretations of symbols in the whole field of mythology" (Greeks: the sea is the symbol of generation). Mithras born beside a river; Christ reborn in the Jordan and born of the *Pege* (fons amoris, Mother of God turned by pagan-Christian legend into a spring nymph); inscriptions "Fonti perenni," "Fons aeternus"; Ardvisura-Anahita; Vedic waters "most maternal." Man born of waters comes at death to the Styx and the "night sea journey"; "Those black waters of death are the water of life, for death with its cold embrace is the maternal womb" (Faust's Spirit: birth and the grave, a boundless ocean) [pp.319-321].
- **Mother-imago projected on water:** endows it with numinous qualities (baptismal water). "In dreams and fantasies the sea or a large expanse of water signifies the unconscious," which (particularly in men) is the mother or matrix of consciousness; interpreted on the subjective level it has the same maternal meaning as water [p.321, para 320].
- **Tree of life (para 321):** perhaps first a fruit-bearing genealogical tree, a tribal mother; humans from trees; heroes enclosed in the maternal trunk (Osiris in the cedar, Adonis in the myrtle); goddesses worshiped in tree form (Juno of Thespiae a bough, of Samos a plank, of Argos a pillar; Carian Diana an unhewn block; Athene of Lindus a column; Tertullian's "rough and shapeless wooden stake," Latona at Delos "an amorphous bit of wood"); Attis castrates himself under a pine because the tree is maternal. The naked wooden pole (*palus*, *Pfahl*) is phallic; *phallos* is a pole, a lingam carved of figwood (Priapus statues); *phalanx*; *phaleros* "bright, shining" from root *bhale* "bulge, swell"; Faust's "It glows, it shines, increases in my hand" [pp.321-322].
- **Rudra hymns (paras 322-323):** Rig-Veda invocations to Rudra (bountiful water i.e., urine, fiery god, ruddy bull, gorgeous helm, white-shining sun): "the various aspects of the psychic life-force, of 'the extraordinarily potent,' the personified mana-concept, come together in the figure of Rudra" [pp.322-323].
- **Goddesses as libido symbols (para 324):** the libido expresses itself in sun, light, fire, sex, fertility, growth; goddesses acquire phallic symbols because "just as the female lies hidden in the male, so the male lies hidden in the female." The tree is bisexual (Adam's body as genealogical tree; Florence manuscript picture of Adam's member as a tree in *Psychology and Alchemy*; Latin tree names masculine in ending, feminine in gender) [pp.323-324].
- **Dream (paras 325-326):** a young woman, troubled by sexual difficulties in marriage and attracted to a young man, dreams of an exotic tree with red fleshy fruits that she eats and feels poisoned. Jung: the Paradise tree: "the tree of libido, which here represents the feminine as well as the masculine side, because it simply expresses the relationship of the two to one another" [p.324].
- **Norwegian riddle and sun-child** (golden tree on the Billinsberg; sun's daughter collecting golden branches; weeping sun-child and the fallen golden apple) [pp.324-325, paras 327-328].

### Key doctrine: symbol versus sign or allegory [pp.325-326]
- "The various meanings of the tree—sun, tree of Paradise, mother, phallus—are explained by the fact that it is a libido-symbol and not an allegory of this or that concrete object. Thus a phallic symbol does not denote the sexual organ, but the libido." "Symbols are not signs or allegories for something known; they seek rather to express something that is little known or completely unknown." The *tertium comparationis* of all is libido; "the sole reality is the libido, whose nature we can only experience through its effect on us." "It is not the real mother who is symbolized, but the libido of the son, whose object was once the mother." Myth taken too concretely yields contradictions; we forget "it is the unconscious creative force which wraps itself in images." Translation example: "His mother was a wicked witch" means the son cannot detach libido from the mother-imago and suffers resistances [pp.325-326, para 329].
- Water and tree attributes of the city symbol likewise refer to libido unconsciously attached to the mother-imago. The Apocalypse's end ("there shall be no more curse") means no more sin, repression, disharmony with oneself, guilt, fear of death or pain of separation, because through the marriage of the Lamb the son unites with the mother-bride; the symbol recurs in the alchemical *nuptiae chymicae* / *coniunctio*; the Apocalypse's note is re-echoed in Doctor Marianus's last prayer in *Faust* [p.326, paras 330-331].

### Jung's critique of the Freudian causal view of symbol-formation; rebirth [pp.327-330]
- **Teleological argument (para 332):** Freud's thesis is that symbol-formation is explained solely by prevention of the primary incest tendency, a mere substitute. Jung replies: the "incest prohibition" is not itself primary; it goes back to the primitive system of marriage classes, a vital necessity in tribal organization. So the phenomena require a teleological, not merely a causal, explanation. [p.327]
- **The basis of the "incestuous" desire is not cohabitation** but "the strange idea of becoming a child again, of returning to the parental shelter, and of entering into the mother in order to be reborn through her." The simplest route, to impregnate the mother and beget oneself anew, is stopped by the incest prohibition; so sun and rebirth myths invent every mother-analogy to canalize libido into new forms and prevent actual incest (mother changed into an animal or made young again, disappearing after birth). "It is not incestuous cohabitation that is desired, but rebirth." The prohibition is an obstacle that "makes the creative fantasy inventive"; libido "becomes imperceptibly spiritualized"; "The power which 'always desires evil' thus creates spiritual life." Religions exalt this procedure into a system [pp.327-328].
- **Nicodemus (John 3:4ff.):** Nicodemus takes rebirth realistically ("enter the second time into his mother's womb?"); Jesus translates into "born of water and of the Spirit," water = mother's womb, spirit = fructifying breath of wind (Greek *pneuma* means both). Same need as the Egyptian vulture legend (vultures female only, fertilized by the wind) [pp.328-329, paras 332-335].
- **Ethical demand behind the mythologem (para 335):** "you should not say that your mother is impregnated by a man in the ordinary way, but is impregnated in some extraordinary way by a spiritual being." A son may believe a father begot him but not that he can impregnate his mother and be born young again, which regression danger prohibits; so the problem is expressed symbolically. Jesus's challenge: do not think carnally or you will be flesh, think symbolically and you will be spirit. "This compulsion towards the symbolical is a great educative force"; Nicodemus would stay stuck in banalities if he did not rise above concretism [pp.329-330].

### Chapter V continued: symbolic truth, religion, and faith (the "digression") [pp.330-339]

- **Symbolic versus empirical truth (para 335 end):** "The empirical truth never frees a man from his bondage to the senses; it only shows him that he was always so and cannot be otherwise. The symbolical truth, on the other hand, which puts water in place of the mother and spirit or fire in place of the father, frees the libido from the channel of the incest tendency, offers it a new gradient, and canalizes it into a spiritual form." Man as spiritual being "becomes a child again and is born into a circle of brothers and sisters": the mother becomes the "communion of saints," the Church, and brothers and sisters are humanity. The process was especially necessary when Christianity arose because the age had lost the sense of the unity of mankind through the contrast of slavery and citizen freedom [p.330].
- **Modern need (para 336):** it is as necessary now as ever to lead libido away from the cult of rationalism and realism, not because those have the upper hand but because the guardians of symbolic truth, the religions, "have been robbed of their efficacy by science." Insisting on bare dogma, "ethics for ethics' sake," or humanizing Christ with biographies is unimpressive; symbolic truth is undefended against scientific thought. Exclusive appeals to faith are "a hopeless petitio principii, for it is the manifest improbability of symbolical truth that prevents people from believing in it." Theologians should "see what can be done to make this faith possible," placing symbolic truth on a foundation that appeals to reason as well as sentiment, by reflecting on why humanity needed "the improbability of religious statements" [pp.330-331].
- **Libido and natural gradient (para 337):** instincts work smoothly when there is no consciousness to conflict with them; even in primitives, psychic systems oppose pure instinctuality. In any culture creative fantasy produces analogies to instinctual processes to free libido from sheer instinctuality by guiding it to analogical ideas. Libido "does not incline to anything": like water it needs a gradient. The analogies must be ideas which attract libido; their special character is that they are **archetypes**, "universal and inherited patterns which, taken together, constitute the structure of the unconscious." Christ's spirit and water to Nicodemus are typical ideas: "the archetypes are the forms or river-beds along which the current of psychic life has always flowed" [p.332].
- **Method of the book (para 338):** symbol-formation cannot be discussed without instinctual processes since the symbol derives its motive power from them and has no meaning unless it strives against instinct's resistance; undisciplined instincts would ruin man if the symbol did not give them form. Hence sexuality, whose analogies fill most symbols. Jung says an alternative explanation from the spiritual side would require the hypothesis that "spirit" is an autonomous reality with a specific energy to bend instincts; he prefers, empirically, to explain symbol-formation as a natural process while conscious of its one-sidedness [pp.332-333].
- **Sex and religious symbols (paras 339-341):** less than two thousand years ago the cult of sex was in full bloom and union with God was understood as more or less concrete coitus; the symbol-creating forces do not change. Primitive Christianity's turn from nature and sex through asceticism shows the source of its motive forces; it could transform libido because its archetypal analogies were in tune with the instinctual forces. Jung says he seeks to understand religious ideas whose value he appreciates, not dispose of them rationalistically; "blind faith" educates to thoughtlessness; he claims the really dangerous people are the "rationalizing intellectuals" who suddenly discover how irrational dogmas are, and that discarding Christianity without a new form of adaptation lets the old brutality return (individuals who drop one adaptation without another regress along the old path). He points to contemporary events (the German collapse of moral restraint) as illustration. The psychotherapist must make the foundations of religious experience clear to educated patients and enable them to "think symbolically once more"; dogmatic thinking makes thought antiquated [pp.333-336].
- **Reductive analysis for neurotics and the normal discontented (para 342):** many are neurotic because they do not know why they cannot be happy in their own way; many normal people feel restricted because they have no symbol to act as outlet for libido. They need reductive analysis down to primal facts to know their primitive personality and take account of it. The transformation by Christianity was mostly unconscious in later centuries; Jung adds a correction to his earlier remark (para 106) that unconscious transformation of libido is ethically worthless: "mere faith" cannot count as an ethical ideal either because it too is unconscious transformation; faith is a charisma but no way for those who must understand before believing; symbols can be understood, "the only viable way for those who have not been granted the charisma of faith" [pp.336-337].
- **The religious myth (para 343):** "one of man's greatest and most significant achievements, giving him the security and inner strength not to be crushed by the monstrousness of the universe"; not an external truth but "psychologically true," "the bridge to all that is best in humanity" [p.337].
- **Psychological versus metaphysical truth (para 344):** psychology as a science must hold aloof from metaphysical assertions; its subject, the psyche and its contents, are realities "because they work." All knowledge of the psyche is itself psychic; the soul is "the only experient of life and existence" and the sine qua non of the subjective reality of the world. Symbols "are always grounded in the unconscious archetype, but their manifest forms are moulded by the ideas acquired by the conscious mind." **Archetypes** are "the numinous, structural elements of the psyche," with autonomy and specific energy enabling them to attract suitable contents from consciousness. **Symbols act as transformers:** "their function being to convert libido from a 'lower' into a 'higher' form"; they work by suggestion, carrying conviction and expressing its content, thanks to the numen stored in the archetype. Experience of the archetype "seizes and possesses the whole personality, and is naturally productive of faith" [pp.337-338].
- **Two kinds of faith (para 345):** "legitimate" faith rests on experience; a second kind rests exclusively on tradition's authority, also legitimate as embodying experience important to cultural continuity, but liable to degenerate into habit, spiritual inertia, thoughtless compliance, psychic regression to infantilism, stagnation and cultural regression; traditional contents become formalities without influence on conduct. The "child-likeness" of faith makes sense only when the feeling behind the experience is alive; otherwise faith is habitual infantile dependence that prevents the struggle for deeper understanding: "This seems to be the position we have reached today" [pp.338-339].
- **Task of the psychotherapist (para 346):** since faith revolves around the "dominant ideas" that give life meaning, the prime task is to "understand the symbols anew, and thus to understand the unconscious, compensatory striving of his patient for an attitude that reflects the totality of the psyche" [p.339].

### Return to Miss Miller: the conifer and tree symbolism; the Osiris myth [pp.339-364]
- The city vision is followed by a "strange conifer with knotty branches"; "strange" suggests numinosity as in dreams; she gives no personal material, so Jung reviews the history of tree symbolism [p.339, paras 347-348].
- **Trees and hanged gods (para 349):** tree of paradise/life, Attis's pine, trees of Mithras, Yggdrasill; the hanging of Attis in effigy, Marsyas, Odin, the Germanic hanging sacrifices: "the hanging of Christ on the Cross is nothing unique in religious mythology." The Cross is Tree of Life and a Tree of Death, a coffin (German *Totenbaum*); burial in hollow trunks returns the dead to the mother for rebirth [pp.339-340].
- **Osiris myth after Plutarch (paras 349-361):** Rhea pregnant with Osiris and twin Isis, who mate in the womb ("night sea journey with incest"); Isis "born in the All-Wetness"; Pamyles of Thebes, drawing water, hears a voice proclaiming Osiris's birth. Jung's reading: Pamyles, a phallic daimon, represents the creative power which "draws" things from the unconscious (the water) and begets the god as a conscious content, an experience also of the archetype: "a drawing up from the depths"; the numinous unconscious content would remain dark unless interpreted by a voice from above as the birth of a god (cf. Matthew 3:17) [pp.340-341].
- Set (Typhon) lures Osiris into a chest thrown in the Nile and out to sea; in the underworld Osiris mates with second sister Nephthys: incest in both intra-uterine existences (brother-sister marriage was an aristocratic mark in antiquity; Zarathustra recommended consanguineous marriage). **Teleological reading:** the "ruse" is the incestuous longing to get back into the mother; "the motif of containment signifies the latent state that precedes regeneration"; evil strives to be perfected through rebirth ("Part of that power which would / Ever work evil, but engenders good"); the "rational" mind sees a subterfuge to become a child again [p.341, para 351].
- **Isis and Ra (hymn):** Isis creates a poisonous snake that wounds Ra in the foot; he retires on the back of the heavenly cow (the mother-goddess), going to her for a cure for a wound she inflicted. Jung: the real cause of the wound is **the incest taboo**, which cuts one off from the security of childhood and instinctive existence, evoking memories of the animal age with no "thou shalt"; deep resentment against the law that separated man from instinctive surrender. Until the child leaves the state of unconscious identity with the mother, he remains one with the animal psyche; development of consciousness inevitably separates one from the mother, the parents, family, relatively from the unconscious and instinct. The longing for the lost world tempts one to retreat and regress, which throws up incestuous symbolism [pp.341-343].
- **Change and archetype (para 351 end):** a new adaptation "can only be achieved in accordance with the instincts"; otherwise only a "convulsively willed, artificial product ... incapable of life." "No man can change himself into anything from sheer reason; he can only change into what he potentially is." When a mode of adaptation decays it is unconsciously compensated by the archetype of another mode; if consciousness interprets the constellated archetype meaningfully and appropriately, a viable transformation occurs; the childhood mother relation is compensated by the mother archetype when detachment is indicated (e.g., Mother Church; when that form ages, a new interpretation becomes inevitable) [p.343].
- **Longing and the Terrible Mother (para 352):** the old form keeps its attraction; "whoever sunders himself from the mother longs to get back to the mother"; this can become a consuming passion threatening all won. The mother appears as supreme goal and as most frightful danger: the Terrible Mother [p.343].
- **Chest at Byblos (para 353):** the coffer comes to rest in branches of a cedar that grows around it; the king has it cut into a roof pillar; the absence of Osiris (winter solstice) coincides with lament, his *heuresis* (finding) a feast of joy [p.344].
- **Dismemberment and dogs (para 354):** Set dismembers the body; Isis with jackal-headed Anubis reassembles it, dismemberment contrasting with the putting together of the child in the womb. The necrophagous function gives the Egyptian vulture its mother-significance (Persians exposed corpses to dogs; Tibetan sky-burial; Parsi towers of silence; the Persian custom of leading a dog to a dying man's bed to be given a morsel; Cerberus pacified with honey-cakes). Creuzer's reading: the dog ceremony is connected with the dog-star at the solstice, making death equal to the sun at its highest point; death is commonly regarded as entry into the mother's womb for rebirth. Mithraic dog leaping on the sacrificed bull: the sacrifice as the moment of supreme fruitfulness (Heddernheim relief: fruits from his horns, wine from blood, corn from tail, cattle from semen, garlic from nostrils) [pp.344-345].
- **Dogs, Hecate and Asklepios (para 355):** Hecate dog-headed like Anubis ("Canicula"), related to the moon-goddess, promoter of growth, a goddess of marriage and birth, sacrifices to Eileithyia; the dog is the companion of Aesculapius; Petronius's request for a small dog painted at the statue's foot "so that I may attain life after death" [pp.346-347].
- **Phallus, Harpocrates, Horus (paras 356-357):** the phallus eaten by fishes, Osiris's resuscitation only partial; Harpocrates born weak "in the lower limbs." The foot as organ nearest the earth represents in dreams relation to earthly reality and often a phallic or generative meaning; Oedipus, "Swell-foot." Osiris flanked by comely Horus and misshapen Harpocrates, possibly connected with the primitive notion that the placenta is the newborn's twin brother [p.347]. Osiris is often confused with Horus: Horpi-chrud, the "up-and-coming child," rising sun, versus Osiris, the sun in the Western Land: "both husband and son of the same mother." Khnum-Ra (ram) and Hatmehit; Hibis hymn [pp.347-348].
- **Nun, Nit/Neith, the cow (paras 358-361):** the cow as mother-symbol in Hathor-Isis; Nun, primary moisture, male and female ("father of fathers, mother of mothers"); Nit "the Ancient, mother of God ... the Scarab and the Vulture"; *nun* = young, fresh, also new Nile flood, the chaotic primeval waters personified as Naunet, from whom springs Nut, the sky-goddess (starry body or star-dotted cow). Ra retiring on the heavenly cow means returning to the mother to rise as Horus; "In the morning the goddess is the mother, at noon she is the sister-wife, and at evening once more the mother who takes back the dead into her womb." "Thus the fate of Osiris is explained: he enters into the mother's womb, into the coffer, the sea, the tree, the Astarte column; is dismembered, put together again, and reappears in his son Horpi-chrud" [pp.348-351].

### Embracing, entwining, devouring [pp.351-358]
- **Entwining motif (para 362):** the tree growing around Osiris; Sleeping Beauty; the girl imprisoned between bark and wood; a sun-hero freed from a creeping plant; a girl's dream of rescuing a lover from seaweed; an African hero disentangled from seaweed; a Polynesian canoe in the tentacles of a giant polyp; Ra's barge entwined by the nocturnal serpent; Queen Maya giving birth under a bending tree (Arnold's poem on Buddha's birth) [pp.351-352].
- **Hera of Samos (para 363):** each year her image "disappeared" from the temple, attached itself to a lygos-tree on the seashore, entwined, was "found" and regaled with wedding-cakes: a *hieros gamos* (Plataea and Argos wedding processions; month of Gamelion) with a further train of thought, **rejuvenation magic**; Argive Hera becomes a virgin again by dipping yearly in the fountain of Kanathos; Zeus's couch on Mount Ida (Iliad) as an echo of the western garden of the gods; Heracles and Gilgamesh hasten west, where sun and maternal sea unite in an eternally rejuvenating embrace; Artemis Orthia "Lygodesma" (willow-captive) [pp.352-353, paras 363-364].
- **Devouring (paras 365-366):** closely tied to entwining; devouring may be partial: a six-year-old girl who hated school dreamt a large red worm encircled her leg and she was tenderly interested; an adult patient with a strong mother-transference to an older woman friend dreamt a crab seized her foot as she crossed a stream. Etymology: Indo-European root *wel(u)-* "encircling, enveloping, winding" (valli creeping plant, boa-constrictor, wickeln, wave; volva, vulva; fruitful field, husk) and also "boil, undulate," "warmth," "sound," "to will, wish." In the state of "involution" the hero's hair falls out with the heat [pp.354-355].
- **Entwining trees are birth-giving mothers (para 367):** Greek ash-tree mothers of the Bronze Age men; Bundahish's Mashya and Mashyoi as the tree Rivas; Nordic creation of man from *tre*; Yggdrasill sheltering a human pair at the world's end ("the tree pregnant with death and life"); Egyptian Book of the Dead ("I know the tree of emerald green from whose midst Ra rises") [p.355].
- **Born from tree, rock, water (para 368):** Mithras shown rising half from a tree (Heddernheim) or from rock, often near a stream; Aschanes, first Saxon king, from Harz rocks beside a fountain; all mother symbols united (earth, wood, water); medieval honorific "Lady" for the tree; the Cross transformed from tree of death into tree of life (Zöckler: derivation of the Cross from the Tree of Life probable; swastika and cross as sun-wheel); legend of the Cross growing from Adam's grave via Seth's twig. Adam's guilt: eating of the Paradise tree; Jewish legend: after the Fall, the tree withered with a babe in its branches ("the 'mother' had become pregnant") [pp.355-357].
- **Lilith and the lamias (para 369):** Adam's demon-wife Lilith, who contended for supremacy, fled into the sea, was forced back by three angels and became a nightmare/lamia who haunts pregnant women and kidnaps newborns; Lamia seduced Zeus and Hera caused her children to be stillborn so she persecutes children; fairytale mothers as murderers or flesh-eaters (Hansel and Gretel); Lamia also a voracious fish (cf. Frobenius's whale-dragon): the Terrible Mother as personification of death, devouring men, animals, plants, entire countries that the hero delivers to glorious rebirth [pp.357-358].
- **Nightmare, mare, horse, mother (paras 370-373):** lamias are typical nightmares who ride their victims; spectral horses carry riders off at mad gallop (anxiety dream; Laistner on fairytales). Freud's and Jung's child-psychology findings: fear-significance of horses and sexual meaning of riding fantasies; the essential feature is rhythm, sexual only secondarily; Yggdrasill called *Schreckross* (terrible horse); peasants hang horse-skull bones on houses against nightmare females. Etymologies: Mara ("Mara trad hann": King Vanlandi trodden to death in sleep), French *cauchemar* from *calcare* "to tread"; root *mer/mor* "to die" (mors, Moira; Norns, Clotho/Lachesis/Atropos; Celtic *matres et matronae*, Caesar on matrons declaring the lots); Slavonic mara "witch," Czech mura as nightmare and hawk-moth (butterfly as symbol of the psyche; sphingids come in darkness, like nightmares); olive of Athene *moria* from *moros*, "fate"; Mar/mère/mare/Meer, "etymologically accidental" yet perhaps pointing to the primordial mother-image once our only world; "Ave maris stella"; infantile *ma-ma* in all languages; mothers Mary and Maya; child carried on mother's back as her "horse"; Odin hung on the "terrible horse" Yggdrasill [pp.358-360].
- **Isis's treachery and Horus's attack (para 374):** Isis played an evil trick on Ra and, per Plutarch, treacherously on Horus; after Horus conquered Set, Isis freed Set; Horus lifted his hand against his mother and snatched her royal diadem, which Thoth replaced with a cow's head; Horus defeated Set again [pp.360-361].

### The fight with the "whale dragon" and the Babylonian creation epic [pp.361-365]
- **Doctrine (para 374):** Horus's fight is the sun-hero's fight with the "whale dragon," a symbol of the Terrible Mother, "the voracious maw, the jaws of death in which men are crunched and ground to pieces." "Whoever conquers this monster wins to eternal youth," but must descend into the monster's belly ("journey to hell") and stay there ("night sea imprisonment": Frobenius) [p.361].
- **The fight with the nocturnal serpent "signifies conquest of the mother, who is suspected of an infamous crime, namely the betrayal of her son"** [p.361, para 375].
- **Enuma Elish** (Smith's fragments from the library of Assurbanipal, about the time of Hammurabi, 2000 B.C.): Ea, son of the watery deep and god of wisdom, overthrows Apsu (father of the great gods: the father conquered); Tiamat (mother Hubur), the gods' mother, avenges, creating giant venomous snakes, dragons, sphinxes, hurricanes, mad dogs, scorpion-men, lion-demons, fish-men, centaurs [pp.361-363]. The gods put up **Marduk**, god of spring and victorious sun, who forges weapons (the evil wind Imhullu, hurricane, cyclone, net); in combat the wind enters Tiamat's open mouth, he spears her, cuts her, tramples her carcass; he splits her "like a flat fish" in two parts, one half covering the sky [pp.363-365, paras 376-378].
- **Interpretation (para 379):** "Marduk created the world from the mother"; the mother-dragon's killing is a negative wind-fertilization; the world is created "with the libido that is withdrawn from her through the sacrifice, and through prevention of the regression that threatened to overcome the hero" (the formula to be examined in the final chapter, "The Sacrifice") [p.365].

### Hebrew parallels, the personification of libido, and divine incest [pp.365-372]

- **Old Testament dragon-combat parallels (paras 379-387):** Gunkel's parallels with Marduk and Tiamat: Isaiah 51:9f. (the arm of the Lord cutting Rahab in pieces, piercing the dragon, drying up the sea so the redeemed may pass), Psalm 89:10, Job 26:12f., Psalm 74:13ff., Isaiah 27:1 (Leviathan). Rahab, a name for Egypt and for the dragon, is the old dragon Tiamat, chaos; "the redeemed" are the Israelites and also mythologically those freed from the whale-dragon (Frobenius). Job 41:1f. (fishing Leviathan with a hook) parallels primitive myths of fishing the sea-monster [pp.365-367].
- **The hero personifies libido (para 388):** the incest prohibition prevents the son from reproducing himself through the mother; so it is not man who is regenerated as a renewed whole but the hero or god who rejuvenates himself. These figures, characterized by libido-symbols (light, fire, sun), "are, in fact, personifications of the libido" [p.367].
- **Autonomous complexes as personalities:** every split-off portion of libido, every complex, "has or is a (fragmentary) personality" (as in hysteria, schizophrenia, mediumistic spirits, dream figures). Deeper, they are archetypal formations; there are no conclusive arguments against the hypothesis that archetypal figures are endowed with personality from the outset. Insofar as archetypes are not merely functional relationships, "they manifest themselves as *daimones*, as personal agencies," felt as actual experiences, not "figments of the imagination." "Man derives his human personality only secondarily from what the myths call his descent from the gods and heroes; or, to put it in psychological terms, his consciousness of himself as a personality derives primarily from the influence of quasi-personal archetypes" [pp.367-368].
- **The god transforms first (para 389):** "It is, in the first place the god who transforms himself, and only through him does man take part in the transformation." Examples: Khnum the potter shaping his egg on the wheel ("his own generation and his own self-birth"); Book of the Dead lines (the hawk from his egg; "My nest is not seen and my egg is not broken"; the god in his egg who created himself); the "Great Cackler" Nagaga-uer [pp.368-369].
- **Why divine incest is told of gods (para 390):** canalizing regressive libido into the god "justifies the mythological statement that it is the god or the hero who commits incest." On the primitive level no further symbolization is required; it becomes necessary only when the myth discredits the god, at a higher level of morality. Herodotus's account of the Papremis festival (priests dragging the god's image in a cart, a club-fight at the temple gate with a thousand armed votaries; origin: Ares forcing his way in to see his mother) and a Pyramid Text (the king "possesses his mothers") [pp.369-371].
- **Mistletoe and Baldur (para 392):** votaries fight and kill each other for their share in "the mystery of divine incest" and thus participate in the god's act. Baldur's death by mistletoe, the one thing forgotten because "too young," parallels Osiris. The mistletoe is a parasite; the Indian female fire-stick came from a parasitic or creeping plant; the Mara rests on "märentakken" (perhaps mistletoe); mistletoe was a remedy for barrenness; the Gaulish Druid climbs the sacred oak after sacrifice to cut it. Reading: what grows on the tree is the child, or oneself rejuvenated, "precisely what one cannot have, because the incest prohibition forbids it." Baldur is the *puer aeternus*, granted only a fleeting existence as "an anticipation of something desired and hoped for"; a certain type of "mother's son" shows all the traits of the flower-like youthful god and dies early because "he only lives on and through the mother and can strike no roots in the world," in a state of permanent incest, "only a dream of the mother" (like the Near Eastern son-gods Tammuz, Attis, Adonis, Christ). Cut off from its host the mistletoe dies, so the Druid's cutting repeats symbolically Attis's self-castration and Adonis's wounding: "the dream of the mother in matriarchal times, when there was as yet no father to stand by the side of the son" [pp.371-372].
