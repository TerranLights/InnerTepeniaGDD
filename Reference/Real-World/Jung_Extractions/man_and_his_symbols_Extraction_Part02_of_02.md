## Overview of this range

**Jaffe** argues that art, like religion, is where humankind has recorded its meaningful symbols from prehistory onward. She first traces three recurring motifs through sacred and artistic history (the stone, the animal, the circle/mandala), showing how each is an unconscious projection of an archetypal content and how changes in the circle and cross track changes in the spirit of an age. She then treats twentieth-century "imaginative" painting not as a user of symbols but as itself a symbol: an expression of the psychic condition of modern humanity (loss of the Christian God-image, the split between matter and spirit, the retreat of consciousness before the unconscious, the shadow of the "chthonic spirit," and, in the 1950s, signs of a reunion of opposites). **Jacobi** presents the case of "Henry," a 25-year-old Swiss engineer, mother-bound, introverted and afraid of committing to marriage, analyzed in 35 sessions over nine months with 50 dreams. She follows the dream series (the first dream as prognosis; the fear of the unconscious and of the feminine; the "saint and prostitute" dream; the humpbacked girl as anima; the I Ching "oracle dream" with its synchronistic confirmation; the beetles; the dying old man and "unlived life"; the final "great dream" of the four friends and the naked Black man) and interprets each with Jung's categories (persona, shadow, anima, Self, the four, the sacrificium intellectus) while stressing technique: the patient's own associations, amplification, the analyst's restraint, and the self-regulating action of the psyche. The case illustrates the first half of individuation (strengthening the ego and masculinity, initiation into outer life). The range closes with **von Franz's "Conclusion: Science and the Unconscious"** (relating archetypes to biology, microphysics, mathematics and synchronicity; the unus mundus), then notes, index and illustration credits.

# PART A — Aniela Jaffe: "Symbolism in the Visual Arts" (PDF pp.229–268; printed 232–271)

## 1. Introduction: everything can become a symbol [p.229]

- Jaffe's starting claim: "the history of symbolism shows that everything can assume symbolic significance": natural objects (stones, plants, animals, humans, mountains, sun and moon, wind, water, fire), man-made things (houses, boats, cars), abstract forms (numbers, the triangle, square, circle). The whole cosmos is a potential symbol.
- Humans, with their "symbol-making propensity," unconsciously transform objects or forms into symbols, endowing them with great psychological importance, and express them in religion and visual art. The intertwined history of religion and art, back to prehistory, is the record of the symbols that were meaningful and moving to ancestors. The interplay is still alive in modern painting and sculpture.
- **Plan of the chapter:** (1) three motifs universally sacred or mysterious (**stone, animal, circle**), illustrating symbolism in art of many periods; (2) twentieth-century art treated "not in terms of its use of symbols, but in terms of its significance as a symbol itself," a symbolic expression of the psychological condition of the modern world.

## 2. Sacred symbols: the stone [pp.229–231]

- Unhewn stones had highly symbolic meaning for ancient and primitive societies: believed dwelling places of spirits or gods; used as tombstones, boundary stones, objects of veneration. Using a stone this way is "a primeval form of sculpture," a first attempt to give the stone more expressive power than chance and nature gave it.
- **Example: Jacob's dream** (Genesis, quoted at length): Jacob sleeps with stones for a pillow, dreams the ladder with angels, wakes, says "this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven," sets the stone up as a pillar, pours oil on it and names the place Beth-el. For Jacob "the stone was an integral part of the revelation. It was the mediator between himself and God." [p.230]
- Many primitive stone sanctuaries represent the deity not by one stone but by many unhewn stones in distinct patterns: the geometrical stone alignments of Brittany (Carnac, c. 2000 B.C.) and the circle at Stonehenge. The Zen rock gardens (Ryoanji, Japan) arrange rough natural stones in a pattern that seems chance but expresses "a most refined spirituality."
- Early on, people tried to express a rock's soul or spirit by working it into a recognizable form, often a rough approximation of the human figure: ancient menhirs with crude faces, Greek herms that developed from boundary stones, primitive stone idols. **Jaffe's interpretation:** "The animation of the stone must be explained as the projection of a more or less distinct content of the unconscious into the stone." [p.231]
- **Modern parallel:** the primitive tendency to hint at a figure and keep the stone's natural form recurs in modern sculpture (Hans Aeschbacher, James Rosati, Max Ernst), where the stone is allowed to "speak for itself." Quoted letter of Max Ernst (Maloja, 1935): he and Giacometti "work on granite boulders" from the Forno glacier moraine, polished by time and weather; "why not leave the spadework to the elements, and confine ourselves to scratching on them the runes of our own mystery?" Jaffe says she will show that modern artists' "mysteries" are not very different from those of old masters who knew "the spirit of the stone." The emphasis on this "spirit" shows the shifting borderline between religion and art.
- *Plates:* Carnac alignments; Ryoanji rock garden; a prehistoric menhir slightly carved into a female form (probably a mother goddess); a Max Ernst sculpture that barely alters the natural stone.

## 3. Sacred symbols: the animal [pp.231–236]

- **Cave art:** animal pictures date from the last Ice Age (between 60,000 and 10,000 B.C.), found on cave walls in France and Spain at the end of the nineteenth century; their importance recognized only early in the twentieth. They revealed a remote prehistoric culture never suspected.
- **Numinous survival:** (citing the art historian Herbert Kuhn) inhabitants of areas in Africa, Spain, France and Scandinavia where paintings exist could not be induced to approach the caves; passing nomads still leave votive offerings before North African rock paintings; in the fifteenth century Pope Calixtus II prohibited religious ceremonies in the "cave with the horse-pictures." Jaffe's conclusion: the caves were always instinctively felt to be religious places; "the numen of the place has outlived the centuries." [p.232]
- The approach to painted chambers is often through low, dark, damp passages, perhaps to safeguard the mystery; the sudden sight of the chambers after an arduous approach must have overwhelmed prehistoric visitors.
- The paintings are almost entirely animals, observed with great skill, but with details showing they were meant to be more than naturalism: many were used as targets (Montespan: an engraved horse driven into a trap is pitted with missile marks; a clay bear has 42 holes). Interpretation: **hunting magic** (still practiced by African hunting tribes). The painted animal is a "double"; its symbolic slaughter anticipates and ensures the real animal's death. This is **sympathetic magic**, based on "the 'reality' of a double represented in a picture: What happens to the picture will happen to the original." Underlying fact: strong identification between a living being and its image, taken to be its soul (why many primitive peoples shrink from being photographed). *Plate:* a bison covered with spear marks; modern parallel: destruction of effigies as symbolic killing (statue of Stalin destroyed by Hungarian rebels in 1956).
- **Fertility rites:** other pictures show animals mating (male and female bison, Tuc d'Audoubert), so the realistic image acquired magical overtones and symbolic significance: "the image of the living essence of the animal."
- **Semi-human figures in animal disguise** are the most interesting: Trois Freres cave (a man in hide playing a flute as if spelling the animals; a dancing figure with antlers, horse's head and bear's paws dominating hundreds of animals, "unquestionably the 'Lord of the Animals'"). [p.232] African parallels: in initiations, secret societies and even kingship, animals and animal disguises play a role (kings and chiefs as lions or leopards; vestiges in the titles "Lion of Judah" for Haile Selassie, "Lion of Malawi" for Hastings Banda). The more primitive the society, the more literal the title: the chief at initiation in full animal disguise "is the animal," an animal spirit or terrifying demon performing circumcision, representing the ancestor of the clan and so the primal god, the totem. Jaffe suggests the Trois Freres dancer is likely a chief transformed into an animal demon. [p.233]
- **Masks:** full disguise was in many places superseded by animal and demon masks (often unsurpassed in expressive power, and venerated like the deity); animal masks persist in Swiss folk art and the Japanese No drama. "The symbolic function of the mask is the same as that of the original animal disguise. Individual human expression is submerged," and the wearer assumes the dignity, beauty and horror of an animal demon; psychologically "the mask transforms its wearer into an archetypal image." **Dance** was originally the completion of the animal disguise by movement, performed "by demons in honor of a demon." Kuhn found heel prints around animal figures in clay at Tuc d'Audoubert, showing Ice Age dancers moved like bison (a bison dance for fertility, increase and slaughter). *Plates:* Trois Freres shaman figure; Burmese buffalo dance, Bolivian devil dance, German folk dance of "wild men"; No masks; Kabuki.
- **Link to Jung's introductory chapter:** identification of natives with the totem animal or "bush-soul." In boys' initiation the boy takes possession of his "animal soul" and sacrifices his own "animal being" by circumcision; this dual process admits him to the totem clan and makes him a man and, in a wider sense, a human being. East African peoples called the uncircumcised "animals": neither the human nor animal aspect of their souls had become conscious, so the animal aspect dominated. [p.234]
- **Psychological reading:** "The animal motif is usually symbolic of man's primitive and instinctual nature." Civilized people too must recognize the violence of instinctual drives and powerlessness against autonomous emotions from the unconscious; primitives even more so. Jaffe recalls Jung's example of the African who in a rage killed his beloved little son (and then was overwhelmed by grief): the animal demon is a vivid symbol for such an impulse, letting humans establish a relationship with the overwhelming power in themselves, to fear it and propitiate it by sacrifice and ritual.
- **Myth and religion:** many myths involve a primal animal that must be sacrificed for fertility or creation (Mithras' sacrifice of the bull, from which earth and all fruitfulness sprang; St. George and the dragon). Animal attributes are ascribed to supreme gods or gods are represented as animals in nearly every religion: Babylonian zodiac (Ram, Bull, Crab, Lion, Scorpion, Fish); Egyptian Hathor (cow-headed), Amon (ram-headed), Thoth (ibis-headed or baboon); Hindu Ganesha (elephant head), Vishnu (boar), Hanuman (ape-god) (the Hindus do not put man first in the hierarchy of being: elephant and lion stand higher); Greek Zeus approaching girls as a swan, bull or eagle; Germanic cat sacred to Freya, boar, raven and horse to Wotan. [pp.234–235]
- **Christianity:** three evangelists have animal emblems (Luke the ox, Mark the lion, John the eagle; only Matthew is a man or angel); Christ appears as the Lamb of God, the Fish, the serpent exalted on the cross, the lion, rarely the unicorn. Jaffe's reading: even the Son of God "can no more dispense with his animal nature than with his higher, spiritual nature"; the subhuman as well as the superhuman is felt to belong to the divine, symbolized by the Nativity among animals in a stable. *Plates:* Ganesha, Zeus and Leda as swan, medieval coin with Christ as man on one side and as serpent on the other. [p.235]
- **Core teaching on the animal symbol** [p.236]: the profusion of animal symbolism "shows how vital it is for men to integrate into their lives the symbol's psychic content: instinct." An animal is "neither good nor evil; it is a piece of nature." Human nature's foundation is instinct. Humans alone can control instinct by will but also suppress, distort and wound it, and "an animal, so to speak metaphorically, is never so wild and dangerous as when it is wounded." Suppressed instincts can gain control of a man and destroy him.
  - **Dream rule:** the familiar dream of being pursued by an animal "nearly always indicates that an instinct has been split off from consciousness and ought to be (or is trying to be) readmitted and integrated into life." The more dangerous the dream animal's behavior, the more unconscious the dreamer's instinctual soul and the more imperative its integration "if some irreparable evil is to be forestalled."
  - **Two dangers:** suppressed and wounded instincts threaten civilized people; uninhibited drives threaten primitive people. In both the "animal" is alienated from its true nature; acceptance of the animal soul is the condition for wholeness. Formula: "Primitive man must tame the animal in himself and make it his helpful companion; civilized man must heal the animal in himself and make it his friend."

## 4. The symbol of the circle [pp.237–246]

- Von Franz's earlier explanation recalled: the circle (or sphere) is a symbol of the **Self**, the totality of the psyche in all aspects, including the relation between man and nature; it appears in sun worship, modern religion, myths, dreams, Tibetan mandalas, city ground plans, early astronomers' spheres, and "always points to the single most vital aspect of life, its ultimate wholeness." [p.237]
- **Brahma on the lotus:** the thousand-petaled lotus; Brahma looks to the four points of the compass before creating, "a preliminary orientation, an indispensable taking of bearings." **Buddha's birth:** a lotus (eight-rayed) rises; he steps into it and gazes in the 10 directions (including up and down): a sign that he is a unique personality predestined for illumination, "given the imprint of wholeness."
- **Reading:** the survey symbolizes the human need for psychic orientation. The four functions of consciousness (thinking, feeling, intuition, sensation; Jung's chapter, p.61 of the book) are how a person receives and responds to inner and outer impressions; Brahma's four-fold survey symbolizes the integration of the four functions that one must achieve. In art the circle is often **eight-rayed**, expressing reciprocal overlap of the four functions so that four intermediate functions arise (e.g., thought colored by feeling or intuition, feeling tending toward sensation). [pp.237–238]
- **Mandalas and yantras:** in Indian and Far Eastern art the four- or eight-rayed circle is the usual pattern of religious images used as meditation instruments (Tibetan Lamaism: richly figured mandalas, usually representing the cosmos in relation to divine powers). Many Eastern figures are purely geometrical, called **yantras**. A common yantra motif: **two interpenetrating triangles**, one pointing up and one down, the union of Shiva and Shakti (male and female divinities). Psychologically: "the union of opposites—the union of the personal, temporal world of the ego with the non-personal, timeless world of the non-ego"; ultimately the union of soul with God. Like the circular mandala they represent the wholeness of the psyche or Self, of which consciousness is as much a part as the unconscious. **Distinction:** yantras and Shiva-Shakti sculptures emphasize tension between opposites (erotic, emotional), implying a *process*, the coming into being of wholeness; the four- or eight-rayed circle represents wholeness *as such*, an existing entity. [pp.237–238]
- **Zen:** the abstract circle in Zen painting (a picture "The Circle" by the Zen priest Sangai): "In the Zen sect, the circle represents enlightenment. It symbolizes human perfection."
- **Christian art:** cathedral rose windows are "representations of the Self of man transposed onto the cosmic plane" (Dante's vision of a white rose); halos of Christ and saints are mandalas, Christ's often quartered (suffering and death on the Cross and "differentiated wholeness"); circular figures on early Romanesque church walls may descend from pagan originals. Non-Christian "sun wheels" appear in Neolithic rock engravings before the wheel was invented; as Jung pointed out, "sun wheel" names only the outer aspect: what mattered was the experience of an archetypal inner image, rendered as faithfully as bulls and wild horses. Christian pictorial mandalas include the Virgin in the center of a circular tree (the burning bush as God-symbol) and, commonest, Christ surrounded by the four Evangelists (going back to the Egyptian god Horus and his four sons). [pp.238–239]
- **Architecture:** the mandala is the ground plan of sacred and secular buildings in nearly all civilizations, passing unnoticed. **The founding of Rome (Plutarch):** Romulus summoned Etruscan builders who instructed him "as in the mysteries"; they dug a round pit (where the Comitium stands), threw in offerings of the fruits of the earth, then each man added a piece of earth from his homeland; the pit was called the **mundus** (also "cosmos"). Romulus drew the city boundary as a circle with a plow drawn by a bull and a cow, lifting the plow over where gates were to be. Yet Rome is famous as **urbs quadrata** (the square city). Two theories: *quadrata* = "quadripartite" (circular city divided in four by two main arteries meeting at the mundus); or the contradiction is a symbol of the insoluble **squaring of the circle** (Greek problem; central in alchemy). In either case a true mandala, the founding a secret rite; the city has a center (mundus, covered by the "soul stone," removed on certain days when the dead's spirits rose) linking it to the other realm. [p.239]
- Medieval cities modeled on mandalas had approximately circular walls, two main arteries dividing the city into quarters leading to four gates, the church at the intersection; inspiration was the Heavenly Jerusalem (Revelation: square plan, three times four gates, but no temple at the center because God's presence is the center). The mandala plan is not outmoded (example: Washington, D.C.). **Never dictated by aesthetics or economics:** "a transformation of the city into an ordered cosmos, a sacred place bound by its center to the other world," answering the vital needs of religious man. [p.240]
- Every mandala-plan building is "the projection of an archetypal image from within the human unconscious onto the outer world"; city, fortress and temple become symbols of psychic wholeness and influence whoever enters or lives there. The projection is unconscious: Jung quoted ("Such things cannot be thought up, but must grow again from the forgotten depths..."). *Plates:* Angkor Wat; Danish fortified camp (c. A.D. 1000); Palmanova (1593) star-shaped fortress town; L'Etoile, Paris; Ethiopian rock-cut church; Francesco di Giorgio's body-proportion plan for a circular church.
- **Cross vs. mandala in Christian history** [pp.240–242]: the central Christian symbol is the cross, not the mandala. Up to Carolingian times the equilateral Greek cross (mandala indirectly implied); later the center rose until the cross took the Latin form. This corresponds to the inward development of Christianity to the high Middle Ages: removing the center of man and faith from earth and "elevating" it to the spiritual sphere ("My kingdom is not of this world"); earthly life, world and body were forces to be overcome; hopes directed to the beyond. Climax: medieval mysticism and Gothic cathedrals' increasing height defying gravity (elongated Latin cross plans; baptisteries with the font at center keep a true mandala plan).
- **Renaissance reversal:** man turned back to earth, rediscovered nature and the body, circumnavigated the globe, proved it a sphere; mechanics and causality became the basis of science; the irrational and mysticism were submerged by logical thought. Art became realistic and sensuous, "a true symbol of the spirit of the age." Church plans became more circular, but Jaffe attributes this to *aesthetic*, not religious, causes: the center of these round churches (the truly "holy" place) is empty and the altar stands in a wall recess, so they are not true mandalas. Exception: St. Peter's in Rome (Bramante, Michelangelo), where the altar stands at center ("great genius is always both of and beyond its time"). [p.242]
- Christ stayed on the Latin cross, so religious man's center stayed on a higher spiritual plane than earthly man who had turned to nature: "a rift arose between man's traditional Christianity and his rational or intellectual mind," never since healed, still splitting "the psyche of the western Christian in the 20th century." Jaffe notes the summary is oversimplified and omits secret religious movements that took account of evil and the chthonic (earthly) spirit, a minority "contrapuntal accompaniment to Christian spirituality." *Plates:* Copernican universe; Leonardo's heart study; Tintoretto; Renoir.

## 5. Alchemy, imaginative vs. sensory art [pp.243–244]

- Among sects around A.D. 1000 the **alchemists** exalted the mysteries of matter alongside the "heavenly" spirit of Christianity, seeking a wholeness of man including mind and body, with a thousand names and symbols. One central symbol: the **quadratura circuli**, "no more than the true mandala." They produced symbolic pictures of dreams and visions "as profound as they are baffling," inspired by the dark side of nature (evil, dream, spirit of earth), fabulous and dreamlike. Hieronymus Bosch is the most important representative of this imaginative art.
- Meanwhile typical Renaissance painters produced "sensuous art"; fascination with earth and nature determined visual art for five centuries, the last great representatives being the nineteenth-century impressionists.
- **Two modes (Kuhn's terms, adopted by Jaffe):** the **"sensory"** style directly reproduces nature or the subject; the **"imaginative"** style presents a fantasy or experience of the artist in an "unrealistic," dreamlike, sometimes "abstract" manner. Imaginative art flowered in the Mediterranean basin from the third millennium B.C.; its early works are not incompetence but expressions of definite religious and spiritual emotion. In the last half century art has passed through an "imaginative" phase again. *Plates:* alchemical squared circle with male and female figures; Ben Nicholson's modern squared circle ("strictly geometrical, empty form... without symbolic meaning"); Sofu Teshigahara's asymmetrical "sun wheel."

## 6. The circle and the square in modern painting [pp.243–246]

- Today the circle appears again in painting, but "the traditional mode of representation has undergone a characteristic transformation that corresponds to the dilemma of modern man's existence": the circle is no longer a single meaningful figure embracing a world and dominating the picture. It is displaced by loose groups of circles, or made asymmetrical. [pp.243–244]
- **Examples as Jaffe describes them:** Robert Delaunay's asymmetrical sun disks; Ceri Richards' painting in Jung's collection (an asymmetrical circular plane with a much smaller empty circle far to the left); Matisse's *Still Life with Vase of Nasturtiums* (a green sphere on a slanting black beam overlapping a rectangle with a folded corner: the circle and square, once united, are "torn apart or incoherently related," yet touching); Kandinsky (colored circles drifting like soap bubbles, loosely tied to a large rectangle; in *A Few Circles* a dark cloud or swooping bird bears loosely arranged bright balls); Paul Nash (in *Event on the Downs* a tennis-ball-like ball bears the Tai-gi-tu, opening a new dimension in the solitude; in *Landscape from a Dream* balls roll in a vast mirrored landscape under a huge sun); Paul Klee's *Limits of Understanding* (a simple circle above a complex structure of ladders and lines, in the spirit of Jung's remark that a true symbol appears only when there is a need to express what thought cannot think or what is only divined or felt) (here the circle keeps a dominant position). [pp.244–246]
- **Squares and rectangles** appear as often as circles (Piet Mondrian: harmonious, "musical," almost ascetic compositions with no actual center yet an ordered whole). **Key symbolic equation:** "The circle is a symbol of the psyche (even Plato described the psyche as a sphere). The square (and often the rectangle) is a symbol of earthbound matter, of the body and reality." In most modern art the connection between the two is nonexistent or loose and casual. Their separation is "another symbolic expression of the psychic state of 20th-century man: His soul has lost its roots and he is threatened by dissociation" (parallel: the Iron Curtain splitting west and east). [p.246]
- But the frequency of both forms shows "an uninterrupted psychic urge to bring into consciousness the basic factors of life that they symbolize"; in some abstract pictures they appear "as if they were germs of new growth."
- **UFOs:** the circle's "curious part" in a different phenomenon: the "visionary rumor" of round flying bodies ("flying saucers") in the last years of the Second World War. Jung explained UFOs as a projection of a psychic content (wholeness) always symbolized by the circle; this rumor, as in many dreams, is "an attempt by the unconscious collective psyche to heal the split in our apocalyptic age by means of the symbol of the circle." *Plate:* a sixteenth-century German broadsheet of circular sky objects. [p.246]

## 7. Modern painting as a symbol [pp.247–250]

- **Scope and stance:** "modern art" is used "as the layman uses" it; she means "modern imaginative painting" (abstract or non-figurative, but not always). She makes no distinction among fauvism, cubism, expressionism, futurism, suprematism, constructivism, orphism, etc.; offers no aesthetic differentiation or evaluation; takes modern painting "simply as a phenomenon of our time," the only way its symbolic content can be asked about.
- **Starting point:** "the artist has at all times been the instrument and spokesman of the spirit of his age"; his work is only partly understood by personal psychology; consciously or unconsciously he gives form to the nature and values of his time, which in turn form him. Artists agree: Jean Bazaine ("Nobody paints as he likes. All a painter can do is to will with all his might the painting his age is capable of"); Franz Marc (great artists "take the deepest soundings they can of the genuine, profoundest center of gravity of their age"); Kandinsky (1911: "Every epoch is given its own measure of artistic freedom"). [p.247]
- **The public's passion:** the fifty-year dispute over modern art; repeated prophecies that it is finished never came true; if threatened it is by degeneration into mannerism and modishness (in the USSR, where non-figurative art was officially discouraged, figurative art faces the same degeneration). Even hostile viewers are moved: "Fascination arises when the unconscious has been moved." The visitor stream and the fantastic prices show more than curiosity.
- **The human bond without a welcome** [p.248]: non-figurative art reminds the viewer of nothing in his own world; "There is no welcome, no visible accord in the cosmos created by the artist. And yet... there is a human bond. It may be even more intense than in works of sensory art." The modern artist aims at his "inner vision of man, the spiritual background of life and the world"; the work has abandoned both the concrete sensuous world and the individual; it has become "highly collective" and so touches the many, even in the "abbreviation of the pictorial hieroglyph." What remains individual is style and quality. The layman must learn new lines and colors "as he would learn a foreign language." Artists published unprecedented numbers of manifestoes: partly to justify themselves to themselves, poetic, often confused or self-contradictory "artistic confessions of faith." Jaffe lets the artists speak for themselves. What matters is always the direct encounter with the work.
- **Kandinsky's two poles:** from "Concerning Form": the art of today lies between "(1) great abstraction; (2) great realism," two paths leading to one goal; formerly the abstract was completed by the concrete and vice versa; now they seem about to separate. [pp.248–249]
- **The two gestures** [p.250]: 1913, **Malevich's black square on white** (perhaps the first purely abstract picture): "I took refuge in the form of the square" to "liberate art from the ballast of the world of objects." 1914, **Duchamp's bottle rack** on a pedestal (Bazaine: "torn from its utilitarian context and washed up on the beach, has been invested with the lonely dignity of the derelict... it is alive"). Jaffe: the object became an idol and an object of mockery; its intrinsic reality annihilated; both gestures "had nothing to do with art in the strict sense" yet mark the two extremes (great abstraction, great realism) between which later imaginative art can be understood. **Psychological reading:** the naked object (matter) and the naked non-object (spirit) point to "a collective psychic rift" that appeared symbolically before the First World War; first visible at the Renaissance as the conflict between knowledge and faith; civilization removed man from his instinctual foundation, opening a gulf between nature and mind, unconscious and consciousness. *Plates:* Frith's gambler's-downfall sequence (representational art declined into mannerism and sentiment); Malevich's *White on White*.

## 8. "The secret soul of things" [pp.250–253]

- Duchamp's bottle rack introduced the **objet trouve ("ready-made")**. Joan Miro collects things washed up on the beach each dawn, "waiting for someone to discover their personality," and assembles them; Picasso and Braque made "collages" of scraps from 1912; Max Ernst cut clippings from illustrated papers of the age of big business and assembled them, turning "the stuffy solidity of the bourgeois age into a demonic, dreamlike unreality"; Kurt Schwitters worked with the contents of his ash can (nails, brown paper, tickets, cloth); his "cathedral built for things" took 10 years and required demolishing three stories of his own house. [p.250]
- **Jaffe's reading:** this places modern art in an unconsciously perpetuated tradition: the hermetic Christian brotherhoods of the Middle Ages and the alchemists, who gave even matter the dignity of religious contemplation. Schwitters' exaltation of rubbish to a "cathedral" (with no room for a human being) follows the old alchemical tenet that the sought-for precious object is found in filth. Kandinsky: "Everything that is dead quivers... even a white trouser button glittering out of a puddle in the street... Everything has a secret soul, which is silent more often than it speaks." [p.251]
  - What the artists, like the alchemists, did not realize: they were **projecting part of their psyche into matter**, hence the "mysterious animation" and value attached to rubbish. They projected "their own darkness, their earthly shadow, a psychic content that they and their time had lost and abandoned."
  - Unlike the alchemists, Schwitters was not held by the Christian order; his monomania binds him to matter (opposed to Christianity's overcoming of matter) yet robs matter of its concrete significance by turning it into abstract composition, so it "begins to discard its substantiality, and to dissolve": a symbol of an era whose notion of the "absolute" concreteness of matter has been undermined by atomic physics.
- **Other voices:** Carlo Carra ("common things reveal those forms of simplicity..."), Klee ("The object expands beyond the bounds of its appearance"), Bazaine ("An object awakens our love just because it seems to be the bearer of powers that are greater than itself"). Jaffe: this recalls the alchemical "spirit in matter," which psychologically is **the unconscious**: "It always manifests itself when conscious or rational knowledge has reached its limits and mystery sets in, for man tends to fill the inexplicable and mysterious with the contents of his unconscious. He projects them, as it were, into a dark, empty vessel." [p.251]

### Giorgio de Chirico [pp.251–254]
- A mystic by temperament, "a tragic seeker who never found what he sought"; self-portrait inscription (1908) *Et quid amabo nisi quod aenigma est* ("And what am I to love if not the enigma?"). Founder of *pittura metafisica*. His view: every object has a common aspect and "the ghostly and metaphysical aspect, which only rare individuals see at moments of clairvoyance"; a work of art must relate "something that does not appear in its visible form."
- His works are dreamlike transpositions arising from the unconscious: Italian city squares and towers in over-acute perspective, as if in a vacuum, lit by a cold light from an unseen source; antique heads and statues; panic-stricken rigidity, nightmare, fathomless melancholy. In *Song of Love* a marble goddess head sits beside a red rubber glove (a "magic object" in the modern sense); a **green ball** acts as the uniting symbol of the "crass opposites" (without it, a hint of psychic disintegration). Jaffe takes it as a dream picture rather than a product of deliberation.
- Influence of Schopenhauer and Nietzsche; Chirico: they "were the first to teach the deep significance of the senselessness of life... The dreadful void they discovered is the very soulless and untroubled beauty of matter." Jaffe doubts he transposed the void into untroubled beauty (his pictures are often terrifying), but says he "penetrated to the core of the existential dilemma of contemporary man." [p.252]
- **"God is dead":** Nietzsche's phrase names the "dreadful void"; Kandinsky, without citing Nietzsche, wrote "Heaven is empty. God is dead." The idea troubled nineteenth-century poets (France, Germany); by the twentieth it was discussed openly and the **cleavage between modern art and Christianity** was completed. Jung also recognized the death of God as "a psychic fact of our time" (1937 quotation: "the present time is the time of God's disappearance and death"); for years he had seen the Christian God-image fading in his patients' dreams, "the loss of the supreme factor that gives life a meaning." **Caution:** neither Nietzsche's assertion, Chirico's void nor Jung's deductions says anything final about God's existence; they are human assertions based on unconscious contents that entered consciousness as images, dreams, ideas or intuitions; the origin of such contents and the cause of the transformation from a living to a dead God "must remain unknown, on the frontier of mystery." [p.252]
- **Chirico's failure** shows in his human figure: given the religious situation man should have "a new, if impersonal, dignity and responsibility" (Jung: a responsibility to consciousness), but Chirico's man is a *manichino*, a faceless puppet without soul or consciousness. In the *Great Metaphysician* versions a faceless figure sits on a pedestal of rubbish: ironic image of the seeker of metaphysical "truth," symbol of ultimate loneliness and senselessness, or a premonition of "the faceless mass man." At 40 he abandoned metaphysical painting for traditional modes and his work lost depth: "there is no 'back to where you came from' for the creative mind whose unconscious has been involved in the fundamental dilemma of modern existence." [pp.253–254]
- **Counterpart: Marc Chagall** also seeks "mysterious and lonely poetry" and "the ghostly aspect of things," but his symbolism is rooted in Eastern Jewish Hassidic piety and a warm feeling for life; he faces neither the void nor the death of God ("Everything may change in our demoralized world except the heart, man's love, and his striving to know the divine."). Herbert Read: Chagall never quite crossed the threshold into the unconscious but "has always kept one foot on the earth that had nourished him." Jaffe: "This is exactly the 'right' relation to the unconscious." *Plates:* Chirico's *Philosopher and Poet* and *Song of Love*; Carra's *Metaphysical Muse* (faceless manikin); Chagall's 1962 synagogue window. [p.254]

### Surrealism and the role of consciousness [pp.254–257]
- The question raised: how does the relation between consciousness and the unconscious take shape in modern artists' work? One answer: **surrealism**, founded by the poet **Andre Breton**, who as a medical student met Freud's work. Breton: "Can dreams not be used to solve the fundamental problems of life?... the apparent antagonism between dream and reality will be resolved in a kind of absolute reality, in surreality."
- Jaffe: Breton grasped the point (reconciling consciousness and unconscious) but his way (Freud's free association and **automatic writing**, "thought's dictation, independent of any aesthetic or moral preoccupation") led him astray. It opens the stream of unconscious images and ignores "the important or even decisive part to be played by consciousness." As Jung shows, **consciousness holds the key to the values of the unconscious**: it alone can determine the meaning of images and their significance "here and now, in the concrete reality of the present." Only in interplay can the unconscious prove its value and "perhaps even show a way to overcome the melancholy of the void." Left to itself, the unconscious's contents "will become overpowering or will manifest their negative, destructive side."
- Surrealist pictures (Dali's *The Burning Giraffe*) show wealth of fantasy and overwhelming unconscious imagery but also "the horror and the symbolism of the end of all things." **Formulation:** "The unconscious is pure nature, and, like nature, pours out its gifts in profusion. But left to itself and without the human response from consciousness, it can (again like nature) destroy its own gifts and sooner or later sweep them into annihilation." [p.255]

### Chance, frottage, and Romantic "handwriting of nature" [pp.255–257]
- Chance as method: Max Ernst quoting Lautreamont's "sewing machine and an umbrella on a surgical table" (juxtaposition of alien elements as "the most potent ignition of poetry"); Breton: "The man who cannot visualize a horse galloping on a tomato is an idiot." Many such associations were jokes, but most modern artists were after something radically different.
- **Jean (Hans) Arp:** woodcuts of leaves and squares "arranged according to the laws of chance" express the quest for "a secret, primal meaning slumbering beneath the world of appearances"; chance gives depth and points to an unknown but active principle of order and meaning, the things' "secret soul." Klee: "make chance essential." Surrealists took wood grain and cloud formations as starting points; Ernst recalled Leonardo's essay on Botticelli's remark that a paint-soaked sponge thrown at a wall shows heads, animals, landscapes. **Ernst's frottage (1925):** staring at a tiled floor marked by scratches, he made graphite rubbings, was "astounded by a suddenly sharpened sense of a hallucinatory series of contrasting and superposed pictures," collected as *Histoire Naturelle*. Jaffe notes Ernst placed a **ring or circle** over or behind some frottages, which gives atmosphere and depth: "the unconscious drive to oppose the chaotic hazards of the image's natural language by the symbol of a self-contained psychic whole, thus establishing equilibrium." [p.256] Plates include an eighteenth-century Dutch museum engraving of stones, coral and skeletons, Roman coins whose faces disintegrate with distance from Rome, and drawings by an artist under LSD-25 (1951, Germany) growing more abstract as conscious control gave way.
- Affinity with nineteenth-century **Romantics**, who spoke of nature's "handwriting" seen on wings, eggshells, clouds, snow, ice crystals "just as much as in dreams or visions": a single "pictorial language of nature." Ernst's "natural history" is thus a genuinely romantic gesture; "the unconscious... is nature." [p.257]
- **The psychologist's question:** what meaning can a chance arrangement have for the person who happens on it? "With this question, man and consciousness come into the matter, and with them the possibility of meaning." Artistic value (beautiful or ugly, harmonious or discordant) does not satisfy the psychologist, who seeks the "secret code" of chance arrangement "in so far as man can decipher it at all." The number and form of Arp's randomly thrown objects are "symbols; and therefore they can not only be felt but (up to a certain point) can also be interpreted."
- **"Pathological art":** critics, noting the retreat of man and reflection and the predominance of the unconscious, compare modern art to the art of the insane, since psychosis involves the submergence of consciousness and ego by flooding unconscious contents. Jung's 1932 Picasso essay once provoked indignation; now catalogues speak of "almost schizophrenic obsession" and Kassner said of Trakl "There was something schizophrenic about him... Yes, Trakl is a great poet." Jaffe: "a state of schizophrenia and the artistic vision are not mutually exclusive"; mescaline experiments helped this change; "more than one artist of today has sought inspiration in such a drug." [p.257]

## 9. The retreat from reality: physics and the "one world" [pp.258–262]

- Marc: "The art that is coming will give formal expression to our scientific conviction." Besides Freud and the rediscovery of the unconscious, a second influence on modern art is **nuclear physics**, which "robbed the basic units of matter of their absolute concreteness," made matter mysterious (mass and energy, wave and particle interchangeable; causality valid only up to a point). The relativities and paradoxes concern only the infinitely small and infinitely great, yet changed the concept of reality: "a new, totally different, and irrational reality has dawned behind the reality of our 'natural' world." Corresponding paradoxes appeared in the psyche, "another world dawned on the margin of the world of consciousness, governed by new and hitherto unknown laws... strangely akin to the laws of nuclear physics."
- Jung and **Wolfgang Pauli** (Nobel physicist) discussed the parallel; "the space-time continuum of physics and the collective unconscious can be seen, so to speak, as the outer and inner aspects of one and the same reality behind appearances" (von Franz's concluding essay will treat this). The laws, processes and contents of this "one world behind the worlds of physics and the psyche" are **unimaginable**, which is why much modern art became abstract: the great artists sought to give visible form to "the life behind things" — a symbolic expression of a world behind consciousness (or even behind dreams, which are rarely non-figurative), pointing to the "one" reality, "one" life, common background of physical and psychic appearances. [pp.258–259]
- **Artists' statements** [p.259]: Kandinsky: "In my mind, the collapse of the atom was the collapse of the whole world: Suddenly the stoutest walls fell... Science seemed to have been annihilated." Result: withdrawal from "the realm of nature," "the populous foreground of things": art "steadily disengaging itself from nature." Marc: "things cease to speak the more we hold up to them the visual mirror of their appearance. Appearance is eternally flat..."; art should "reveal unearthly life dwelling behind everything, to break the mirror of life so that we may look being in the face." Klee: the artist is "more concerned with formative powers than with formal products." Mondrian: cubism did not pursue abstraction to its end, "the expression of pure reality," attainable only by the "creation of pure form"; "Behind changing natural forms there lies changeless pure reality."
- **Two routes:** many sought the "spirit in matter" by transmuting things (fantasy, surrealism, dream pictures, chance); "abstract" artists turned their backs on things (Mondrian's "pure form"). Their goal was "the center of life and things, their changeless background, and an inward certitude. Art had become mysticism."
- **The Mercurial spirit:** the spirit these artists divined was an earthly spirit, which the alchemists called **Mercurius**, alien to the "heavenly" spirit of Christianity: "it was Christianity's dark adversary that was forging its way in art." Modern art, like the medieval hermetic movements, "must be understood as a mysticism of the spirit of earth, and therefore as an expression of our time compensatory to Christianity." [pp.259–260]
- **Kandinsky on inner necessity:** the importance of great art lies "in the root of all roots—in the mystical content of art"; "The artist's eye should always be turned in upon his inner life, and his ear should be always alert for the voice of inward necessity." His pictures are a "music of the spheres, a harmony of colors and forms"; "Form, even if it is quite abstract and geometrical, has an inward clang; it is a spiritual being"; the impact of an acute triangle on a circle equals "the finger of God touching the finger of Adam in Michelangelo." Marc (1914, *Aphorisms*): "The contemplation of the world has become the penetration of the world. There is no mystic who... ever attained the perfect abstraction of modern thought." [p.260]
- **Paul Klee**, "the poet among modern painters": the artist's mission is to penetrate to "that secret ground where primal law feeds growth... in the womb of nature, in the primal ground of creation, where the secret key to all things lies hidden"; "the secretly perceived is made visible"; "My hand is entirely the instrument of a more distant sphere. Nor is it my head that functions in my work." In his work the spirit of nature and of the unconscious are inseparable; it is "the most complex expression—now poetic, now demonic—of the chthonic spirit"; humor and bizarre ideas bridge underworld and human world; his tie to earth is careful observation of nature's laws and love for all creatures ("the dialogue with nature is the conditio sine qua non of his work"). *Plates:* Klee's *Death and Fire* (demonic) and *Sinbad the Sailor* (poetic fantasy). [pp.260–261]
- **Jackson Pollock:** painted in a trance ("When I am in my painting I am not aware of what I am doing... the painting has a life of its own. I try to let it come through. It is only when I lose contact with the painting that the result is a mess"). The pictures are "charged with boundless emotional vehemence," almost chaotic "glowing lava stream," a parallel to the alchemical **massa confusa / prima materia / chaos**: "the nothing that is everything—that is, the unconscious itself," seeming to live before the emergence of consciousness and being or after its extinction. [p.261]
- **Abstraction and the loss of the symbol:** mid-century purely abstract pictures without regular order are the commonest expression, but "The deeper the dissolution of 'reality,' the more the picture loses its symbolic content." The symbol is "an object of the known world hinting at something unknown; it is the known expressing the life and sense of the inexpressible"; in merely abstract painting the known world vanishes and nothing remains to bridge to the unknown. Yet such paintings often resemble **microphotographs** of the molecular structure of matter (plate: a vibration pattern from sound waves in glycerine; Georges Mathieu). Jung's key (quoted): the deeper psychic layers "lose their individual uniqueness as they retreat farther and farther into darkness... they become increasingly collective until they are universalized and extinguished in the body's materiality... The body's carbon is simply carbon. Hence 'at bottom' the psyche is simply 'world.'" [pp.261–262] So "great abstraction" and "great realism" meet again, unconsciously, artists' intention playing no part. Corollary: the artist "is, as it were, not so free in his creative work as he may think"; if unconscious, his work is controlled by laws of nature that correspond to the laws of the psyche. [p.262]
- **The danger:** the early masters (Kandinsky, Klee) never grasped "the grave psychological danger" of the "mystical submersion in the chthonic spirit and the primal ground of nature." Wilhelm Worringer read abstract art as expression of metaphysical unease more pronounced among northern peoples (they "suffer from reality," lack southern naturalness, long for a super-real, super-sensual world); Herbert Read: metaphysical anxiety now characterizes the whole modern world. Klee (Diary, 1915): "The more horrifying this world becomes... the more art becomes abstract; while a world at peace produces realistic art." Marc: "Very early in life I felt that man was ugly. The animals seemed to be more lovely and pure, yet even among them I discovered so much that was revolting and hideous that my painting became more and more schematic and abstract."
- **Marino Marini (1958 conversation with Edouard Roditi)** [pp.262–264]: his recurring subject is a nude youth on a horse. Early versions (after WWII) were "symbols of hope and gratitude" (rider with outstretched arms); over years the form dissolved into abstraction. Marini: the animal's panic increases, "frozen with terror," "paralyzed rather than rearing or taking flight... because I believe that we are approaching the end of the world... I am attempting to symbolize the last stage of a dying myth, the myth of the individual, victorious hero, of the humanist's man of virtue." Jaffe: in fairy tale and myth the victorious hero symbolizes consciousness; his defeat is the death of the individual (submergence in the mass; decline of the human element in art). Marini: "As soon as art has to express fear, it must of itself depart from the classical ideal"; found subjects in bodies excavated at Pompeii; Roditi named it a "Hiroshima style"; "for the last 15 years, sculpture prefers forms in disintegration." Jaffe: one can scarcely walk through an exhibition without sensing "fear, despair, aggression, and mockery that sounds like a cry"; the "metaphysical anxiety" may arise from the despair of a doomed world (Marini) or from the religious factor (God is dead); the two are closely connected.
- **Root of the distress:** "the defeat (or rather the retreat) of consciousness." In the mystical upsurge, everything that bound man to human world, earth, time, space, matter and natural living has been dissolved; unless the unconscious is balanced by the experience of consciousness, "it will implacably reveal its contrary or negative aspect." Harmony of the spheres yields to destruction and despair; "in more than one case the artist has become the passive victim of the unconscious." Physics parallels: discovery of the atom's structure became the basis of unprecedented weapons; "Ultimate knowledge and the destruction of the world are the two aspects of the discovery of the primal ground of nature." [p.264]
- **Jung's one weapon:** "the call for individual consciousness, which seems so simple and yet is so arduous." Consciousness is the counterpoise to the unconscious, gives the possibility of meaning, and has a practical function: the evil seen in neighbors or neighboring peoples can be recognized as evil contents of one's own psyche, "the first step to a radical change in our attitude to our neighbors." (Shadow projection.) Envy, lust, sensuality, lies and all known vices are the negative, "dark" aspect of the unconscious, which shows two faces: positive as the "spirit of nature" creatively animating man, things and the world (the chthonic spirit), negative as a spirit of evil and drive to destroy. Alchemists called it **Mercurius duplex**; Christianity calls it the devil, who also has a positive aspect as **Lucifer**, light-bringer. [p.264]
- **Conclusion on duality:** modern art as symbol of the chthonic spirit also has a dual aspect: positively a "mysteriously profound nature-mysticism"; negatively an expression of an evil or destructive spirit. "The two sides belong together, for the paradox is one of the basic qualities of the unconscious and its contents." Repeated caution: this has nothing to do with artistic and aesthetic values, only with interpreting modern art as a symbol of the time. [p.264] *Plates:* Marini's two horse-and-rider sculptures (1945 tranquil, 1951 tortured) and Pompeii body casts.

## 10. Union of opposites: signs of change [pp.265–268]

- **Spirit of the age:** "like a river that flows on, invisibly but surely"; ten years is long. Around mid-century a quieter change came within abstract painting. Photography (Cartier-Bresson, Werner Bischof) took over concrete sensuous representation, so painters pursued inwardness; but many young artists found established abstract art "offered no adventure," and sought the new "in what lay nearest, yet had been lost—in nature and man"; not reproducing nature but expressing their emotional experience of it. [p.265]
- **Alfred Manessier:** "What we have to reconquer is the weight of lost reality. We must make for ourselves a new heart, a new spirit, a new soul, in the measure of man. The painter's true reality lies neither in abstraction nor in realism, but in the reconquest of his weight as a human being." Non-figurative art for him is the one way to approach inward reality and grasp "his essential self." **Jean Bazaine:** the temptation to paint "the pure rhythm of his feeling... instead of embodying it in a concrete form" leads to "desiccated mathematics" or abstract expressionism, ending in "monotony and a progressive impoverishment of form"; a form that reconciles man with his world is "an 'art of communion'" by which he can recognize "his own unformed countenance in the world." [pp.265–266]
- **Jaffe's reading:** artists now aim at "a conscious reunion of their own inward reality with the reality of the world or of nature; or, in the last resort, a new union of body and soul, matter and spirit." Only now is the great rift (great abstraction vs. great realism) "being made conscious and on the way to being healed." Visible in the changed atmosphere: Manessier, Gustave Singier (belief in the world, serenity of forms and colors despite abstraction), Jean Lurcat's 1950s tapestries (nature's exuberance; sensuous and imaginative). Klee also attained serene harmony, having realized "the necessity of not denying evil": "Even evil must not be a triumphant or degrading enemy, but a power collaborating in the whole"; but Klee lived "near the dead and the unborn" at almost cosmic distance, while the younger generation is more firmly rooted in earth. [pp.267–268]
- **Church patronage:** just when modern painting discerned the union of opposites, it took up religious themes; "the 'metaphysical void' seems to have been overcome" and the Church became a patron (All Saints, Basle, windows by Manessier; Assy church; Matisse chapel at Vence; Audincourt, with Bazaine and Fernand Leger). Jaffe: more than broadmindedness, it signals that the compensatory function of the old hermetic movements "has made way for the possibility of collaboration"; light and chthonic spirits belonged together (cf. the animal symbols of Christ); "a new stage in the solution of this millennial problem might be reached." [p.267]
- **Closing caution and hope:** the future is unknown (bridging of opposites may yield positive results or lead through "yet more unimaginable catastrophes"); anxiety and dread still predominate; there is "still too much unwillingness on the part of the individual to apply to himself and his life the conclusions that can be drawn from art, although he might be ready to accept them in art." The artist can express things unconsciously "without awakening hostility" that are resented from a psychologist; art usually "remains in an impersonal sphere." Yet "a more whole, and therefore more human, form of expression" has become visible; for her (writing 1961) a symbol of hope is **Pierre Soulages**: behind a cataract of huge black rafters glimmers a clear blue or radiant yellow: "Light is dawning behind darkness." [pp.267–268] *Plates:* Lurcat exhibiting tapestries in a field; Manessier's *Dedicace a Sainte Marie Madeleine*; Tremois; a Soulages.

# PART B — Jolande Jacobi: "Symbols in an Individual Analysis" (the case of "Henry") (PDF pp.269–300; printed 272–303)

*Frontispiece [p.269]:* a seventeenth-century engraving, "The Palace of Dreams" (per the notes [p.312]: an illustration to Odyssey XIX; the goddess of sleep holds poppies; on her left the Gate of Horn, from which true dreams come; on her right the Gate of Ivory, from which false dreams come; Diana above left; Night with the infants Sleep and Death above right).

## 1. The beginning of the analysis [pp.271–273]

- **Purpose and framing:** a widespread belief says Jungian methods suit only middle-aged people. Jacobi grants that many reach middle age without psychological maturity (they have not completed the first part of individuation described by von Franz), but a young person can have serious problems too: someone afraid of life, hard to adjust to reality, who prefers fantasy or remaining a child. In such a young person, "especially if he is introverted," one can sometimes find "unexpected treasures in the unconscious," and bringing them into consciousness strengthens the ego and supplies the psychic energy needed to mature. "That is the function of the powerful symbolism of our dreams." [p.271] She will show how analysis aids individuation using the example of a 25-year-old engineer she calls **Henry**.
- **Henry's background:** from a rural district of eastern Switzerland. Father: Protestant peasant stock, a general practitioner, high moral standards but withdrawn, more of a father to his patients than to his children. Mother: the dominant personality ("We were raised by the strong hand of our mother"), from an academic family with wide artistic interests, impulsive and romantic, a great love of Italy, Catholic by birth but the children were raised Protestant. An older sister with whom he had a good relationship.
- **Henry himself:** introverted, shy, finely drawn, very tall, light hair, high pale forehead, blue eyes with dark shadows. He did not think neurosis brought him but "an inner urge to work on his psyche"; hidden behind it were a strong mother-tie and fear of committing to life, discovered in analysis. He had just finished his studies and taken a position in a large factory. His letter requesting an interview: he must decide "either to remain unconscious in a well-protected security, or else to venture on a yet unknown way of which I have great hopes." Jacobi frames the choice: remain a lonely, vacillating, unrealistic youth or become a self-sufficient, responsible adult.
- **Temperament and attitudes:** preferred books to society; inhibited with people; tormented by doubts and self-criticism; well read, with a leaning toward "aesthetic intellectualism"; earlier atheistic, then rigorously Protestant, finally religiously neutral; chose a technical education for talent in mathematics and geometry; logical, scientifically trained mind "but he also had a propensity toward the irrational and mystical that he did not want to admit even to himself." Engaged for two years to a Catholic girl from French Switzerland (charming, efficient, full of initiative) but uncertain about marriage; thought he might remain a bachelor scholar. [pp.271–272]
- **Jacobi's diagnosis:** markedly mother-bound. Consciously identified with his real ("light") mother, representing high ideals and intellectual ambition; unconsciously "deeply in the power of the dark aspects of his mother-bound condition," his ego held "in a strangle-hold." His clear-cut thinking remained "an intellectual exercise." The need to escape the "mother-prison" showed as hostility to his real mother and rejection of the "inner mother" as a symbol of the feminine side of the unconscious; an inner power held him in childhood. He did not realize that his urge to grow included detaching from his mother. [p.272]
- **Format and technique:** 35 sessions, 50 dreams, nine months ("so short an analysis is rare"; possible only when energy-laden dreams speed development; Jungian view: no rule for length, it depends on readiness to realize inner facts and on the unconscious's material). He led a monotonous outer life; sessions quickly turned from daily events and childhood to dreams. His dreams strongly emphasized his "call" to spiritual development.
- **Analyst's restraint (key technique note):** not everything described here was told to Henry. "The analyst can hardly be too careful and reserved. If too bright a light is thrown on the dream-language of symbols, the dreamer can be driven into anxiety, and thus led into rationalization as a defense mechanism," or can no longer assimilate them and fall into a severe psychic crisis. Only an important few of his dreams are discussed. [pp.272–273] *Plates:* the Escorial (fortress monastery; "the introvert's withdrawal from the world") and Henry's childhood drawing of the barn with battlements.
- **Childhood memories with symbolic meaning** [p.273]: (1) age 4: allowed to go with his mother to the baker's; the baker's wife gave him a crescent roll he carried proudly without eating: "Only my mother and the baker's wife were present, so I was the only man." Crescents are popularly "moon-teeth"; the moon allusion underlines the dominating feminine power he felt exposed to and, as "only man," was proud to face. (Plate: the crescent shape linked to the moon and the feminine, e.g., the crown of Ishtar.) (2) age 5: constructing a toy barn from wooden blocks in a square with a hedge like castle battlements; his sister, home from school exams, was teased by him ("You have started school but you're already on holiday") and replied he was on holiday all year; he was deeply hurt that his achievement was not taken seriously. Jacobi sees already visible his later problems with asserting masculinity and the conflict between rational and fantasy values.

## 2. The initial dream [pp.274–278]

**Dream (the day after the first visit):** on an excursion with strangers to the Zinalrothorn, starting from Samaden; they walk an hour then camp for "theatricals" and he has no active part, remembering a young woman in a pathetic role in a long flowing robe. At midday he wants to go on to the pass; all others prefer to stay; he goes up alone leaving equipment behind but finds himself back in the valley, completely disoriented, hesitant to ask; an old woman shows him the way. He ascends from a different starting point, along a cog-wheel mountain railway on the right side; on his left little cars constantly pass, each containing one hidden, bloated little man in a blue suit ("It is said they are dead"); he turns to watch for cars from behind, but his anxiety is needless. At the turn-off, people awaiting him take him to an inn; a cloudburst; he regrets that his rucksack and motorbike are not there but is told to wait till next morning; he accepts.

- **Principle (Jung):** the first dream of an analysis often has anticipatory value; the decision to go into analysis is usually accompanied by an emotional upheaval disturbing deep levels from which archetypal symbols arise, so first dreams often present "collective images" giving a perspective on the analysis as a whole and insight into conflicts. [p.274]
- **Henry's associations:** Samaden was the home of Jurg Jenatsch, a 17th-century Swiss freedom-fighter; "theatricals" recall Goethe's *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship*, which he loved; the woman resembles the figure in Bocklin's *Island of the Dead*; the "wise old woman" links to his analyst and to the charwoman in Priestley's play *They Came to a City*; the cog-wheel railway recalls the battlemented barn.
- **Interpretation, element by element:**
  - *Excursion:* parallels the decision to analyze; individuation is often symbolized by a **voyage of discovery** (Pilgrim's Progress; Dante: a traveler meets a mountain, three strange animals force him down to the valley and hell, then purgatory and paradise). Hence a period of disorientation and lonely seeking is likely; the first part of the life journey (climbing) is ascent from the unconscious to an elevated ego viewpoint, increased consciousness. Plate: the first woodcut of *The Dream of Poliphilo* (a dreamer entering a dark wood).
  - *Samaden/Jenatsch:* embodies the "freedom-seeking" within Henry (a Protestant who fell in love with a Catholic girl, who fought for liberation, as Henry's analysis was to free him from mother-ties and fear of life): a favorable augury. *Zinalrothorn:* "rot" (red) = feeling or passion, pointing to his insufficiently developed feeling function; "horn" echoes the crescent roll. [p.275]
  - *Halt and theatricals:* passivity belongs to his nature; theatre is a way of evading an active part in life's drama (spectator identifies yet pampers fantasies), though identification gave Greeks catharsis and is used in Moreno's psychodrama; the *Wilhelm Meister* association shows an inner development under way. The robed woman resembles his mother and personifies his own unconscious feminine side; via Bocklin's painting (a white-robed priest-like figure steering a coffin boat to an island) she signals his depressive mood, with a "double paradox": the keel suggests a contrary course, and the "priest" is of uncertain sex, hermaphroditic: his opposites are still undifferentiated. [pp.275–276]
  - *Mountain pass:* symbol of a "situation of transition" from an old attitude to a new; he must go alone (the ego must pass the test unaided); leaving the kit means his mental equipment has become a burden. He fails to reach the pass: his ego decides on activity while his other psychic entities (the other party members) remain passive. **Rule:** "When the dreamer himself appears in a dream, he usually represents only his conscious ego; the other figures stand for his more or less unknown, unconscious qualities." [p.276]
  - *The old woman:* the helpful old woman is "a well-known symbol in myths and fairy tales for the wisdom of the eternal female nature." Henry hesitates because accepting her help demands a **sacrificium intellectus** (sacrifice of the rational way of thought), a demand to recur. She links to Priestley's charwoman, who says "they have promised me a room of my own" (self-reliance, as Henry seeks). The advice to start from a different point means a reversal of old attitudes. [p.276]
  - *Cog-wheel railway, right side:* reflects his technical education; **right = realm of consciousness, left = unconscious** (in the history of symbolism). The little men in cars from the left: his fear of what lies behind his ego. Blue often denotes **thinking**; bloated blue men = sterile intellectual thoughts brought down mechanically, ideas that "died on the intellectual heights where the air is too thin," or lifeless inner parts. **The voice** ("It is said they are dead"): in dreams a voice is highly meaningful; Jung identified it with an intervention of the **Self**, knowledge rooted in the collective fundaments of the psyche; "What the voice says cannot be disputed." [p.277]
  - *Turn to the right; inn; rain:* the insight about "dead" formulas is the turning point; he turns toward the conscious and outer world and finds the group waiting; having surmounted dangers alone he can rejoin the "collective" for shelter and food. The cloudburst relaxes tension and makes the earth fertile; in mythology rain is a "love-union" of heaven and earth (Eleusinian mysteries: "Let it rain!" up, "Be fruitful!" down; Danae and the golden shower plate): rain is a "solution" in the literal sense. [pp.277–278]
  - *Rucksack and motorbike:* collective values; he submits twice to advice, first of an archetypal figure (the old woman), then of a collective pattern: "a milestone on the road to maturity." [p.278]
- **Overall:** the dream anticipates his development: conscious urge to ascend vs. tendency to passive contemplation; the pathetic young woman in white (his sensitive romantic feelings) vs. the bloated corpses in blue suits (his sterile intellectual world); overcoming these obstacles would require severe trials. [p.278]

## 3. Fear of the unconscious [pp.278–284]

- **Recurrent problems:** vacillation between masculine activity and feminine passivity; hiding behind intellectual asceticism; fear of the world yet attraction to it; fear of the obligations of marriage (an ambivalence not unusual on the threshold of manhood; here his inner maturity did not match his years, "often met in the introvert, with his fear of reality and outer life"). [p.278]
- **The fourth dream (the endless race):** military service, a long-distance race, alone, never reaching the goal ("Will I be the last?"); the course is all *deja vu*: start in a little wood with dry leaves, an idyllic brook, a dusty road toward Hombrechtikon near Zurich, a willow-bordered brook like a Bocklin painting with a dreamy female figure, night falls, a village where he asks the way: seven hours over a pass. **New ending this time:** after the brook he enters a wood and discovers a doe that runs away (he is proud of observing it); it appears on his left, he turns right and sees three strange creatures, half pig, half dog, with kangaroo legs, faces undifferentiated, large drooping dog ears ("Maybe they are costumed people"; as a boy he once masqueraded in a circus donkey costume). [p.278]
  - *Interpretation:* the opening resembles the initial dream (a dreamlike female figure; Bocklin's *Autumn Thoughts*; romantic, melancholic inner landscape very familiar to him). The long-distance race is an average man's fate ("It's a symbol of life," said Henry), but he goes alone and thinks "I never reach the goal" (inferiority). "Hombrechtikon" evokes his secret plans to break away from home (Hom = home, brechen = to break); because the break does not occur he again loses orientation and must ask directions. [p.279]
  - **Compensation principle:** "Dreams compensate more or less explicitly for the dreamer's conscious attitude of mind." The romantic maidenly figure of his conscious ideal is balanced by strange female-like animals: his instinctual world is symbolized by something feminine. The wood is an unconscious area, a dark place where animals live. The doe is shy, fugitive, innocent womanliness, appearing only for a moment (and on the left, the unconscious side); the three mixed-up animals are "undifferentiated instinctuality," a confused mass of instincts containing raw material for later development, "virtually faceless, and thus without the slightest glimmerings of consciousness." The pig = dirty sexuality (Circe turned her suitors into swine); the dog = loyalty but also promiscuity; the kangaroo = motherliness and tender carrying capacity. Their rudimentary traits are "senselessly contaminated"; the alchemical *prima materia* was often depicted as such monstrous mixed animals; psychologically they symbolize original total unconsciousness from which the ego can rise. [p.279] *Plates:* Henry's drawing (mute and blind creatures; the one on the ground colored green, hinting at growth); a Greek vase with a pig-man, Odysseus and Circe; a George Grosz cartoon with a man given a pig's head.
  - Henry tries to make the monsters harmless (dressed-up people): natural anxiety on finding "inhuman monsters in his inner self." [p.280]
- **The cabin boy dream:** he is a cabin boy on a sailing boat; sails spread though there is dead calm; he holds a rope fastening a mast; the railing is a wall of stone slabs on the border between water and boat; he holds the rope (not the mast) and is forbidden to look into the water. Interpretation: a borderline situation; the railing protects but obstructs the view; forbidden to look into the water (where he might discover unknown powers): doubt and fear. **General point:** a man who fears his inner depths fears the feminine in himself as much as real women: fascinated and terrified, he flees so as not to become her "prey." [p.280] (Plate: Henry's drawing of the boat with a stone wall for a railing.)
- **Mother-tie and the split between feeling and sensuality:** he found it hard to give both feeling and sensuality to the same woman. Dreams of a "monk on a secret mission" and the **brothel dream**: with a military comrade of many erotic adventures, waiting before a house on a dark street in an unknown city; entrance only for women, so the friend dons a little carnival mask of a woman's face and goes up; Henry may have done the same. Jacobi: satisfying curiosity "only at the price of a fraud"; if he divests himself of masculinity he might see the forbidden world; Henry had not overcome his inhibitions. Jacobi read a **homoerotic strain** (a feminine "mask" to be attractive to men), supported by the **playmate dream** (age five or six: a playmate tells of an obscene act with a factory director, an intimate friend of Henry's father, venerated for broad interests and laughed at as an "eternal youth": the boy warmed his hand and the man's penis). Jacobi: homoerotic play is not unusual in children; its recurrence in a dream suggests guilt, strong repression and links to his deep fear of a lasting tie with a woman. [pp.280–281]
- **The wedding dream:** at the wedding of an unknown couple, the party returns at 1 a.m. to a large courtyard; newlyweds and the other couple have quarreled; the solution is that the two men and two women retire separately. Henry: "the war of the sexes as Giraudoux describes it"; the palace in Bavaria where he saw the dream courtyard was disfigured by emergency housing for poor people (he wondered whether eking out a poor existence in ruins of classic beauty beats an active life in an ugly big city); at a comrade's wedding he doubted the marriage would last. Jacobi: the longing for passivity and introversion, fear of unsuccessful marriage, and the separation of the sexes are "unmistakable symptoms of the secret doubts." [p.281]

## 4. The saint and the prostitute [pp.281–284]

**Dream:** on a narrow mountain road, a deep abyss on the left (going down), a rock wall on the right; along the road caves cut into the rock as shelters for lonely wanderers. In one, half hidden, a prostitute has taken refuge, seen from behind from the rock side: a formless, spongy body; he looks with curiosity and touches her buttocks; suddenly perhaps she is a male prostitute. The same creature then appears as a **saint** with a short crimson coat thrown around his shoulders, strides down the road into another, much larger cave with rough-hewn chairs and benches and, with a haughty look, drives out all present, including Henry; he and his followers move in.

- **Henry's associations:** the "Venus of Willendorf" (a paleolithic fleshy fertility figure); touching the buttocks as a fertility rite he learned of in Wallis (French Switzerland) on a tour of Celtic graves: infertile women slid down a smooth tiled slope smeared with substances on bare buttocks; the coat: his fiancée has a white jacket of similar shape; the evening before, at a dance, her friend wore a crimson jacket he liked better. [pp.281–282]
- **Method note:** if dreams are not wish-fulfillments (Freud) but, as Jung assumed, "self-representations of the unconscious," Henry's state could hardly be better represented. [p.282]
- **Interpretation:**
  - *Setting:* Henry as "lonely wanderer," already descending from inhospitable heights (perhaps thanks to analysis); abyss on the unconscious left; rigid rock wall of conscious views on the right; **caves** are refuges from "bad weather" (tension); they are human work (cut into rock), like gaps in consciousness when concentration breaks and fantasy penetrates, allowing deep insight into the psychic background; also symbols of the **womb of Mother Earth**, caverns of transformation and rebirth. The dream shows his introverted withdrawal into a "cave" within consciousness when the world is too hard. [p.282]
  - *The prostitute:* a replica of inner feminine traits; the repressed image of a woman he would never approach consciously; strictly taboo yet secretly fascinating "as the opposite of a too-much-venerated mother... for every son with a mother-complex." Such a man may confine a relationship to purely animal sensuality with feelings split off, thereby staying "true" to his mother: the mother's taboo on every other woman stays effective. He sees her only "from behind," not daring to see her face. By touching her buttocks he unconsciously performs a **fertility rite** (laying on of hands often goes with healing; touch can be a defense or a curse; plate: Valentine Greatrakes). Her turning into a male prostitute: hermaphroditic figure like many mythological figures and like the "priest" of the first dream; sexual uncertainty common in puberty. [pp.282–283]
  - *Transformation into the saint:* prostitute to man to saint eliminates everything sexual; implies escape from sex only in an ascetic holy life denying the flesh. "Such dramatic reversals are common in dreams: Something turns into its opposite... as if to demonstrate that by transmutation even extreme opposites can change into each other." [p.284]
  - *The coat:* a coat symbolizes the **persona** (protective cover or mask, which "has two purposes: first, to make a specific impression on other people; second, to conceal the individual's inner self"). The saint's coat has the color of the friend's jacket (admired) and the shape of the fiancée's: his unconscious would confer saintliness on both women to protect himself from their attractiveness. Red = feeling and passion, giving "a kind of eroticized spirituality... frequently found in men who repress their own sexuality and try to rely solely on their 'spirit' or reason." (Plate: Elijah's mantle left to Elisha as the power and role passing.)
  - *Meaning for age:* escape from the flesh is unnatural in a young person: "In the first half of life, we should learn to accept our sexuality: It is essential to the preservation and continuation of our species." [p.284]
  - *Second cave:* reminiscent of early Christians' refuge; a healing holy place of meditation and transformation from carnal to spiritual. Henry is expelled with the rest of his unconscious entities: he must first succeed in outer life before immersing in a spiritual sphere. The saint also symbolizes, "in a relatively undifferentiated, anticipatory fashion," the **Self**, but Henry is not yet mature enough to remain near it. [p.284]

## 5. How the analysis developed [pp.285–286]

- After initial skepticism and resistance Henry grew interested; dreams compensated for his unconscious life and gave insights into his ambivalence, vacillation and passivity. More positive dreams followed.
- **The sunken locomotives dream (two months in):** in a harbor on a nearby lake, locomotives and freight cars sunk in the last war are raised from the lake bottom (a boiler-like cylinder, an enormous rusty freight car), a "horrible yet romantic sight," to be transported away under the rails and cables of a station; then the lake bottom turns into a green meadow. Interpretation: locomotives = energy and dynamism "sunk," i.e., repressed into the unconscious, now brought up; freight cars = carriers of valuable psychic cargo; the transformation of the lake bottom into meadow = potential for positive action. [p.285] (Plates: Turner's *Rain, Steam, and Speed*; Henry's drawing.)
- **The humpbacked girl (24th dream):** with a small dainty unknown young lady disfigured by a hump he goes to a school; others disperse for singing lessons; she gives him a private singing lesson at a little square table; moved by pity he kisses her on the mouth, knowing it is unfaithful to his fiancée though perhaps excusable. Interpretation: singing = immediate expression of feeling, which Henry fears and knows only in an idealized adolescent form; the **square table** = "fourfoldness," completeness: he must integrate his feeling side to attain wholeness; the kiss "espouses" the inner woman: he has learned to relate to "the woman within." [pp.285–286]
- **Second humpbacked-girl dream:** in an unknown boys' school he secretly forces his way in and hides behind a little square closet; an adult passes without seeing him but the little humpbacked girl enters, sees him and pulls him out. Interpretation: the same girl, both times in a school (he must learn something to aid development; he wants to learn while unnoticed and passive). A deformed little girl in fairy tales hides great beauty released by the "right man," often by a kiss: she symbolizes Henry's **soul** needing release from a spell; she is a helpful guide (awakening feelings by song; pulling him from hiding to the light of day). He must belong simultaneously to his fiancée (the real outer woman) and to the humpbacked girl (**the inner psychic anima**). [p.286]

## 6. The oracle dream [pp.287–292]

- **Introduction:** people relying wholly on rational thinking who dismiss or repress psychic life often have an "almost inexplicable inclination to superstition" (oracles, prophecies, magicians and conjurers); because dreams compensate, their emphasis on intellect is offset by dreams in which they meet the irrational and cannot escape it. Four extraordinary dreams on irrational themes were decisive milestones; the first, 10 weeks in. [p.287]
- **Dream:** alone on an adventurous journey through South America, he wants to return home; in a foreign city on a mountain he tries to reach the railway station, suspected to be at the highest point in the center; fears he is too late. A vaulted passage breaks through a row of medieval-style houses on his right (an impenetrable wall; the station probably behind); sunny painted facades; in the dark archway four ragged figures lie on the pavement. A stranger, a trapper type, appears ahead, likewise wanting the train. The four gatekeepers, who turn out to be Chinese, jump up to block them; in the fight Henry's **left leg is injured by the long nails on the left foot of one of the Chinese**. An oracle must decide whether the way opens or their lives are forfeit: the companion is bound and led inside while the Chinese consult the oracle with little ivory sticks; judgment goes against Henry but he is given another chance; he is fettered and led aside, the companion takes his place, and in the companion's presence the oracle decides Henry's fate a second time, this time in his favor: he is saved. [pp.287–288]
- **Technique in the case:** Henry's conscious mind seemed to want to ignore the dream. Because of his skepticism, Jacobi refrained at first from interpreting it ("not to expose the dream to the danger of rationalization, but rather to let it act on him without interference") and offered only one suggestion: read, then consult as the Chinese did, the *I Ching*. [p.288]
- **The *I Ching* (Jacobi's account):** the "Book of Changes," very ancient wisdom, roots in mythical times, present form from 3000 B.C. (as stated); per Richard Wilhelm both Taoism and Confucianism have their common origin in it; based on the oneness of man and cosmos and the complementary opposites Yang and Yin (male and female principles); 64 "signs" (hexagrams) of six lines each, containing all combinations of Yang (straight lines, male) and Yin (broken lines, female); each sign describes changes in the human or cosmic situation and prescribes in pictorial language the action to follow. Classical consultation used 50 small sticks; the common modern method uses three coins thrown six times (heads = male line counted as 3; tails = broken female line counted as 2). [pp.288–289] (Henry had read, probably in Jung's commentary on *The Secret of the Golden Flower*, of a strange Chinese game for foretelling the future.)
- **Synchronicity:** divining techniques are not "archaic nonsense" but rest on what Jung calls the **principle of synchronicity** ("meaningful coincidence"; essay "Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle"): an inner unconscious knowledge links a physical event with a psychic condition so that an apparently accidental event can be psychically meaningful, often symbolically indicated through dreams that coincide with it. [p.288]
- **What happened:** several weeks later, very skeptical, Henry threw the coins; the sign was **Meng, "Youthful Folly."** The upper three lines symbolize a mountain ("keeping still") and also a gate; the lower three water, the abyss and the moon: all symbols that occurred in his previous dreams. Among its statements: "For youthful folly, it is the most hopeless thing to entangle itself in empty imaginings. The more obstinately it clings to such unreal fantasies the more certainly will humiliation overtake it." He tried to suppress the effect by will but could not; left the session early, canceled the next by phone (influenza) and vanished for a month; Jacobi waited ("keeping still"). [pp.288–289] (Plate: the hexagram pages and a sword-and-helmet drawing.)
- **The sequel:** when he returned, excited and disconcerted: his intellect had suffered a great shock; he had intended to consult the book a second time (as in the dream) but the text of "Youthful Folly" forbids a second question. After two sleepless nights, on the third a luminous image appeared before his eyes: **a helmet with a sword floating in empty space.** He opened the *I Ching* at random to a commentary on chapter 30 and read: "The clinging is fire, it means coats of mail, helmets, it means lances and weapons." He understood the prohibition: in the dream the ego was excluded from the second question (the trapper asked it); likewise his semi-unconscious action asked the second question by opening the book at random onto a symbol coinciding with the nocturnal vision. [pp.289–292] (Note from the notes section: Jung writes that a repetition of the experiment is impossible because the original situation cannot be reconstructed [p.312].)
- **Dream of transformation:** Jacobi interprets all dream elements as contents of Henry's inner personality and the six figures as personifications of his psychic qualities; such dreams are relatively rare, with powerful after-effects: "dreams of transformation." The dreamer seldom has more than a few associations: Henry had recently applied for a job in Chile and been refused because they would not employ unmarried men; some Chinese let the nails of the left hand grow as a sign that, instead of working, they have given themselves to meditation. [p.289] (Plate: guardian sculptures at China's Mai-chi-san caves.)
- **Interpretation:**
  - *South America:* a hot "primitive, uninhibited, sensual" world, an excellent symbol of the unconscious, the opposite of cultivated intellect and Swiss puritanism: his natural "shadow land," for which he longed but did not feel comfortable; he is drawn back from "the chthonic, dark, maternal powers" to the light personal mother and his fiancée, suddenly alone in a "foreign city." The city on a mountain = a higher level of consciousness; mountain = place of revelation and transformation; **the "city on the mountain" is an archetypal symbol; the city whose ground plan is a mandala represents the "region of the soul" at whose middle the Self has its abode.** [pp.289–290]
  - *The railway station as seat of the Self:* surprising; in a young dreamer with relatively low spiritual development the Self is usually symbolized by a banal object from personal experience, compensating high aspirations; "Only in the mature person acquainted with the images of his soul is the Self realized in a symbol that corresponds to its unique value." As an engineer Henry would like his inner world to relate to civilization's rational products; the dream rejects this and points to a different way: "under" and through a dark arch (threshold, danger, separates and unites). [p.290]
  - *The four ragged Chinese:* four undifferentiated aspects of a male totality (four = wholeness; Jung's archetype of the four); unconscious male psychic parts barring the "way to the Self." [pp.290–291]
  - *The trapper:* his **shadow**, "his unlived, primitive side," earthy and rough; the introverted ego joined by its extraverted compensatory side (repressed emotional and irrational traits); it pushes past the ego and, personifying the activity and autonomy of unconscious qualities, "becomes the proper carrier of fate, through whom everything happens."
  - *The fight and injury:* Henry's European conscious ego collides with the ancient wisdom of the East, the extreme opposite, from "an entirely different psychic continent." The Chinese = "yellow earth," a people related to the earth as few are: the chthonic, material quality his intellect lacked. That he recognized them as Chinese shows increased awareness of his adversaries. The long nails are on the left *foot* (claws): a point of view so different it injures him; the **left leg** = the standpoint of his feminine unconscious side toward the chthonic and material, which was ambivalent and is "harmed." [p.291]
  - *Transformation requires an ending:* "Every transformation demands as its precondition 'the ending of a world'—the collapse of an old philosophy of life" (as in Henderson's initiation symbolism: the youth must suffer a symbolic death before rebirth). The engineer's scientific attitude must collapse; the repressed irrational appears as a foreign oracle with fearful, inexplicable power to decide human destinies; the rational ego must surrender unconditionally in a real *sacrificium intellectus*. Immature Henry is not prepared: he loses the first throw; he is caught, unable to go on or to return home (escape adult responsibilities). The ego is bound and set aside while the primitive trapper consults the oracle in its place: "when the ego is imprisoned in isolation, those contents of the unconscious that are personified in the shadow-figure may bring help and solution," once one recognizes them and has experienced their power; then they can be "consciously accepted constant companions." Because the shadow wins the game, Henry is saved. [p.292]

## 7. Facing the irrational [pp.292–297]

- After this, Henry listened eagerly to the unconscious; analysis became more agitated; tension that threatened the depths surfaced; he held on to growing hope. [p.292]
- **The beetles dream (two weeks after the oracle dream):** alone in his room, disgusting black beetles crawl out of a hole and spread over his drawing table; he drives them back with "some sort of magic" except four or five that spread over the room; he gives up following them (no longer disgusting) and sets fire to the hiding place; a tall column of flame rises; he fears the room will catch fire; unfounded. Henry's own interpretation (he had become relatively skillful): beetles = dark qualities awakened by analysis, danger of overflowing professional work (drawing table); black scarabs; magic because he dared not crush them by hand; setting fire = calling for "something divine" (fire like the Ark of the Covenant). [pp.292–293]
  - *Jacobi:* black = darkness, depression, death; "alone" invites introversion and gloom; in Egypt golden scarabs symbolized the sun, black ones "the opposite side of the sun—something devilish," so fighting them with magic is instinctively right; a reduced number frees him from fear and disgust; destroying their breeding ground by fire is a positive act (fire = transformation and rebirth, as the phoenix). In waking life Henry was now full of enterprising spirit but had not yet learned to use it rightly. [p.293] (Plates: Egyptian relief of scarab and Amon in the sun; Ensor engraving of insect-bodied humans; phoenix from an Arabic manuscript.)
- **The dying old man dream ("unlived life"):** an old man is dying surrounded by relatives and Henry; more people gather, each characterizing himself by precise statements, a good 40; the old man groans and mutters about "unlived life"; his daughter asks in what sense (cultural or moral?); he will not answer; she sends Henry to a small adjoining room to find the answer by telling a fortune with cards: the color of the "nine" he turns up will answer. He turns up kings and queens, then only scraps of paper and envelopes; with his sister he searches; under a textbook or notebook he finds a **nine of spades**, which can only mean that moral chains prevented the old man from living his life. [pp.293–294]
  - *Interpretation:* the message warns Henry what awaits if he fails to "live his life." The old man = the dying "ruling principle" of his consciousness whose nature is unknown to him; the 40 persons = totality of psychic traits (40 an "elevated form" of four); the old man dying = part of Henry's male personality on the verge of final transformation. The old man's "morality" has stopped him from living natural feelings and drives; the daughter (the mediating feminine principle, the **anima**) must act because the old man is silent. The fortune-telling takes place in an unused remote room (far from conscious attitude). Kings and queens = collective images of youthful veneration of power and wealth; running out of picture-cards = the inner images exhausted ("the source of pictures dries up"); he needs his feminine side (his sister) to find the last card, hidden under a textbook (arid intellectual formulas). The number **nine** = for centuries a magic number, the perfected Trinity in threefold elevation, with endless other associations; the **spade**: black = death and lifelessness, a leaf shape now black rather than green and vital; "spade" from Italian *spada* (sword), the penetrating cutting intellect. [pp.294–297]
  - **Conclusion:** the "moral bonds" (not "cultural") stop him: probably his fear of surrendering to life, accepting responsibility to a woman and becoming "unfaithful" to his mother. "The dream has declared that the 'unlived life' is an illness of which one can die." Henry realized "one needs something more than reason as a helpful compass... it is necessary to seek the guidance of the unconscious powers that emerge as symbols"; he was "expelled from the paradise of an uncommitted life" and could never return. [p.297] (Plate: Grandville woodcut on playing-card symbolism; spades linked with the penetrating intellect and death.)

## 8. The final dream (the 50th, a "great dream") [pp.295–298]

**Dream (four parts):** *Evening:* four friends sit at a long raw-lumber table drinking from three different vessels: a liqueur glass of clear yellow sweet liqueur, a wine glass of dark red Campari, a large classically shaped vessel of tea; also a reserved, delicate girl who pours her liqueur into the tea. *Night:* back from a drinking bout; one of them is the **Président de la République Française**; in his palace they watch from a balcony as he, drunk in the snowy street, urinates against a snow mound, his bladder apparently inexhaustible; he runs after an old spinster carrying a child in a brown blanket and sprays the child; the spinster ascribes the moisture to the child and hurries off. *Morning:* along the street glistening in winter sun walks a gorgeous completely naked Black man (the book's term: "Negro") toward the east, toward Berne; they are in French Switzerland; they decide to visit him. *Noon:* after a long car trip through a lonely snowy region they reach a city and a dark house where he is said to have put up; they fear he might freeze; his equally dark servant receives them; both are mute; from their rucksacks each must choose a gift, an object characteristic of civilization; Henry first picks a package of matches from the floor and offers it with deference; after all give gifts they join the man in a joyous feast. [pp.295–296]

- **Structure** [p.296]: encompasses a whole day moving to the "right" (growing consciousness): evening, night, noon (zenith); the **cycle of the day as a totality pattern**. The four friends = unfolding masculinity of Henry's psyche; their progress through four "acts" has the geometry of a **mandala**: they come first from east, then west, then toward the "capital," the center, uniting opposites at a center; descent into night of the unconscious following the sun's circuit, then ascent to the bright zenith of consciousness.
- *Evening:* the threshold of consciousness is lowered; the feminine side most easily evoked; a female figure joins (the **anima** of all four, "reserved and delicate," like his sister). The three concave vessels accentuate receptiveness (feminine); opposites (sweet and bitter, red and yellow, intoxicating and sobering) mix as all five drink in unconscious communion. The girl is the secret agent, catalyst: "it is the role of the anima to lead a man into his unconscious, and thus to force him to deeper recollection and increased consciousness"; mixing liqueur and tea approaches a climax. (Plate: Peruvian drinking vessel in woman form.) [p.296]
- *Night (Paris, for Swiss the town of sensuality and uninhibited joy and love):* differentiation: the dream ego (largely identified with the leading thinking function) vs. the **President**, the undeveloped unconscious **feeling function**. The ego and two friends (his semi-conscious functions) look down from a balcony at the President: unstable, abandoned to instincts, urinating drunk in the street, "following only his natural animal urges," a great contrast to a good middle-class Swiss scientist; this side reveals itself only in the darkest night of the unconscious. **Positive aspect:** inexhaustible urine = a stream of psychic libido, abundance, creative and vital strength (primitives regard everything from the body, hair, excrement, urine, saliva, as creative and magical); "power and plenty that often adheres to the shadow side of the ego." The **old spinster**: complement of the shy anima; still a virgin though old and seemingly a mother; Henry associated her with Mary and the child Jesus, but the earth-colored brown blanket makes the child "the chthonic, earthbound counter-image of the Savior"; the President's spraying is a travesty of baptism; if the child is an infantile potentiality in Henry, it could gain strength; the dream says no more. [pp.296–297]
- *Morning (the turning point):* "Everything that was dark, black, primitive, and powerful in the last episode has been gathered together and symbolized by a magnificent Negro, who appears naked—i.e. real and true." Opposites: darkness/bright morning, hot urine/cold snow, black man/white landscape. The friends find themselves in French Switzerland (his fiancée's home): a transformation occurred in the earlier phase; he now begins from his fiancée's place, accepting her psychological background. He had gone from east to west (into darkness, unconsciousness) and now turns 180 degrees toward the rising sun and increasing clarity, toward Berne, the middle of Switzerland: striving toward a center uniting his opposites. [p.297]
- *The Black man (Jacobi's reading, in the period's terms)* [p.297]: for some people "the archetypal image of 'the dark primal creature,'" a personification of unconscious contents; perhaps why he is so often rejected and feared by white people: the white man sees in him "his living counterpart, his hidden, dark side brought before his eyes"; white men project onto him the primitive drives, archaic powers and uncontrolled instincts they will not admit in themselves. For a young man of Henry's age he can be the sum of all dark traits repressed into the unconscious, and also "the sum of his primitive, masculine strength and potentialities, his emotional and physical power." That Henry and friends intend to confront him consciously is a decisive step toward manhood. [note: this passage reproduces the book's own mid-century racial framing, used as a projection-screen symbol; it is recorded here as the book states it, not endorsed.]
- *Noon:* greatest clarity; Henry's ego "more and more compact," able to decide consciously; still winter = lack of feeling and warmth, a psychic landscape "intellectually very cold"; the friends fear the man will freeze; the long drive through desolate country symbolizes the long wearisome search for self-development. The Black man and servant are **mute**: words (intellectual means) cannot work; a **feeling gesture** is needed: a gift "as one gives an offering to the gods," an object of civilization, the values of the intellectual white man: "Again a *sacrificium intellectus* is demanded to win the favor of the Negro, who represents nature and instinct." [p.298]
- *The matches:* Henry decides first, being the bearer of the ego whose proud consciousness (*hybris*) has to be humbled. A box of matches picked from the floor seems absurd but is right: "Matches are stored and controlled fire, a means by which a flame can be lit and put out at any time. Fire and flame symbolize warmth and love, feeling and passion; they are qualities of the heart." Gift = Henry joins a developed civilized product of his ego to the center of his own primitivity and male strength, thus can come into full possession of his male sides, with which the ego must stay in constant touch. Result: the six male persons (four friends, the man, his servant) are together at a communal meal: Henry's masculine totality is rounded out; the ego has the security to submit freely to "the greater archetypal personality within himself, which foreshadows the emergence of the Self." [p.298]
- **Outcome in waking life** [p.298]: Henry became sure of himself, took his engagement seriously, and exactly nine months after analysis began married in a small church in western Switzerland; the next day left with his wife for Canada for an appointment received during the decisive weeks of the last dreams; since then an active creative life as head of a family, in an executive position in a great industry.
- **Jacobi's summary and cautions** [p.298]: Henry's case is "an accelerated maturation to an independent and responsible manliness," an **initiation into the reality of outer life**, a strengthening of ego and masculinity, and completion of the **first half of the individuation process**; the **second half**, "the establishment of a right relationship between the ego and the Self," lies ahead in the second half of life. Not every case runs so successfully nor can be handled alike: "every case is different" (young and old, man and woman, each individual; even the same symbols require different interpretation in each case). She chose this case as an example of "the autonomy of the unconscious processes" and of the "untiring symbol-creating power of the psychic background": "the self-regulating action of the psyche (when not disturbed by too much rational explanation or dissection) can support the development process of the soul."

## 9. Closing picture pages: mandala motifs from over 1,000 dreams of one man [pp.299–300]

- *Psychology and Alchemy* (Jung) discusses a sequence of over 1,000 dreams by one man showing a striking number and variety of **mandala** representations (the mandala is often linked with realization of the Self; see pp.213 ff. of the book). The captioned pictures give a few examples. **Caveat in the caption:** the short interpretations "may... seem to be arbitrary assertions"; in practice no Jungian interprets a dream without knowing the dreamer and studying associations; "These interpretative statements must be taken as hints toward possible meanings—nothing more."
- **Examples described [pp.299–300]:** (1) the anima accuses the man of inattention; a clock says five minutes to the hour (he is "pestered" by the unconscious; tension heightened by waiting five minutes); (2) a skull he tries to kick away becomes a red ball, then a woman's head (rejecting the unconscious but it asserts itself via the ball, perhaps the sun, and the anima figure); (3) a prince places a diamond ring on the fourth finger of the dreamer's left hand (like a wedding ring: a "vow" to the Self); (4) a veiled woman uncovers a face shining like the sun (illumination of the unconscious involving the anima, unlike conscious elucidation); (5) from a transparent sphere containing small spheres a green plant grows (sphere = unity, plant = life and growth); (6) troops, no longer preparing for war, form an eight-rayed star and rotate to the left (an inner conflict giving way to harmony). [visually checked p.299: photographic montages: a woman in a red gown with a pocket-watch face; skulls turning into orange balls and a face.]

# PART C — M.-L. von Franz: "Conclusion: Science and the Unconscious" (PDF pp.301–307; printed 304–310)

(Note: Jaffe, at [p.258], refers to this as von Franz's "concluding essay.")

## 1. What has been shown and Jung's open system [pp.301–302]

- The preceding chapters tried to clarify the symbol-creating function of the unconscious psyche. Von Franz: we are still far from understanding the unconscious and the archetypes ("those dynamic nuclei of the psyche") in all their implications. What can be seen: archetypes enormously impact the individual, shaping emotions, ethical and mental outlook, relationships and whole destiny; the arrangement of archetypal symbols follows a **pattern of wholeness** in the individual and appropriate understanding of symbols can have a **healing effect**; archetypes act as **creative or destructive forces**, "creative when they inspire new ideas, destructive when these same ideas stiffen into conscious prejudices that inhibit further discoveries." [p.301]
- Interpretation must be subtle and differentiated so as not to weaken specific individual and cultural values of archetypal ideas "by leveling them out—i.e. by giving them a stereotyped, intellectually formulated meaning." Jung's concepts are "mere tools or heuristic hypotheses," forming an "open system"; his discovery of the unconscious has not merely widened but "doubled" our view of the world: we must now ask whether a mental phenomenon is conscious or unconscious and whether a "real" outer phenomenon is perceived by conscious or unconscious means. [pp.301–302]

## 2. Archetypes across the cultural fields [pp.301–303]

- If all humans have common inherited patterns of emotional and mental behavior (archetypes), their products appear in nearly every field of activity. Jungian influence cited: literature (Priestley, *Literature and Western Man*; Gottfried Diener, *Fausts Weg zu Helena*; James Kirsch, *Shakespeare's Hamlet*); art (Herbert Read; Aniela Jaffe; Erich Neumann on Henry Moore); music (Michael Tippett); history (Arnold Toynbee); anthropology (Paul Radin); sinology (Richard Wilhelm, Erwin Rousselle, Manfred Porkert). [p.301]
- **Caution:** this does not mean art and literature can be understood *only* from their archetypal foundation; they have their own laws and, like all really creative achievements, "cannot ultimately be rationally explained." But the archetypal patterns are recognizable as a "dynamic background activity" and one can often decipher in them, as in dreams, the message of a seemingly purposive, evolutionary tendency of the unconscious. [p.303] (Plate: sound waves from a vibrating steel disk forming a mandala-like pattern.)

## 3. Biology, synchronicity, creative discovery [pp.303–304]

- Jung's concepts also open new views in the natural sciences. **Pauli** suggested that our idea of the evolution of life needs revision to allow an area of interrelation between the unconscious psyche and biological processes: the assumption that mutations are random and selection keeps the "meaningful," well-adapted varieties has been challenged by evolutionists, who note that selecting by pure chance would take longer than the known age of the planet.
- **Synchronicity** may help explain rare "border-phenomena," how "meaningful" adaptations and mutations could happen in less time. Meaningful "chance" events occur when an archetype is activated; example: **simultaneous discovery.** Darwin was expanding his theory in 1844 (as the text gives it) when he received a manuscript from the unknown young A. R. Wallace in the Moluccas, a shorter but parallel exposition; each independently reached the hypothesis in an intuitive "flash" later backed by documentary evidence. The archetypes thus appear as "agents of a creatio continua"; synchronistic events are "something like 'acts of creation in time.'" [pp.303–304]
- Similar meaningful coincidences occur when there is vital need to know of a relative's death or a lost possession (often revealed by extrasensory perception); this might suggest why animal species under great pressure could produce meaningful but acausal changes in outer structure. [p.304]

## 4. Physics and psychology: archetypal roots of scientific concepts [pp.304–305]

- The most promising field (Jung's view) is microphysics. Most of physics' basic concepts (space, time, matter, energy, continuum or field, particle) were originally intuitive, semi-mythological, **archetypal ideas of the old Greek philosophers**, later made precise and now expressed mainly in mathematics. *Particle:* formulated by Leucippus and Democritus as the "atom" (indivisible unit); matter is still conceived as waves and particles (quanta). *Energy:* early Greek thinkers and the Stoics' life-giving "tension" (*tonos*) that supports and moves all things, "a semi-mythological germ of our modern concept of energy." [pp.304–305]
- Even modern thinkers drew on half-mythological images: Descartes' "proof" of the absolute validity of causality "by the fact that God is immutable in His decisions and actions"; Kepler's assertion that there are exactly three dimensions of space because of the Trinity. These concepts "do not necessarily express 'objective' facts... but spring from innate mental tendencies in man" that induce him to find satisfactory rational connections; Heisenberg: in examining nature, "man encounters himself." Pauli therefore urged parallel psychological investigation of the inner origin of scientific concepts. [p.305]
- **Complementarity (Niels Bohr):** light can be described only by two logically opposed but complementary concepts, particle and wave; position or velocity of a subatomic particle can be accurately observed but not both at once; the observer must choose an experimental setup and thereby excludes (must "sacrifice") another; the measuring apparatus must be included in the description. Pauli (quoted): microphysics cannot eliminate the observer's effects and "has therefore to abandon in principle any objective understanding of physical phenomena"; classical "determined causal laws" give way to "statistical laws" with "primary possibilities." So no law says "such-and-such will happen in every case," only what is statistically likely; scientists can no longer describe outer objects in a completely "objective" manner. Most physicists accept the role of the observer's conscious ideas but have not considered that the observer's total psychological condition (conscious and unconscious) might also play a role; Pauli: no a priori reason to reject that, but it remains unexplored. [pp.305–306] (Plate caption: Maria Mayer, 1963 Nobel prize, nuclear shell model: concentric shells containing 2, then 8, ... with "magic numbers" 20, 28, 50, 82, 126, a discovery made in an intuitive flash; the caption links it to the archetypes of sphere and number [p.304].)
- **Parallel in psychology:** Jung saw that consciousness and the unconscious also form a complementary pair of opposites. Each unconscious content is altered by being partly integrated into the observer's consciousness; dream contents, if noticed, are semi-conscious; each enlargement of consciousness from dream interpretation has an immeasurable repercussion on the unconscious. So the unconscious can be only approximately described, like micro-particles, by paradoxical concepts; "What it really is 'in itself' we shall never know, just as we shall never know this about matter." **Archetypes** could be called, with Pauli, "primary possibilities" of psychic reactions: there are no laws governing the specific form an archetype takes, only "tendencies" (see book p.67). William James compared the unconscious to the "field" concept: as in a magnetic field particles appear in a certain order, psychological contents appear in an ordered way in the unconscious. [p.306]
- **Pre-conscious order:** what we find "rational" or "meaningful" probably harmonizes with some preconscious constellation in the unconscious; our representations are sometimes ordered before they become conscious. **Gauss:** found a rule in number theory "not by painstaking research, but by the Grace of God, so to speak. The riddle solved itself as lightning strikes." **Poincare:** during a sleepless night he watched his mathematical representations colliding until some "found a more stable connection": "One feels as if one could watch one's own unconscious at work... one has an intuition of the difference between the mechanisms of the two egos." [pp.306–307]
- **Meaning vs. cause:** Jung looked for meaning (purpose): not "why did it happen (what caused it)" but "What did it happen for?"; many modern physicists likewise seek "connections" more than causal laws (determinism). Pauli expected the idea of the unconscious to spread beyond therapy to all natural sciences dealing with general life phenomena, echoed by physicists in **cybernetics** (comparing control systems of brain and nervous system with computers); Costa de Beauregard: science and psychology should "enter into an active dialogue." [p.307]

## 5. The unus mundus and numbers as the bridge [pp.307–308]

- The parallels suggest a possible ultimate **one-ness** of the realities physics and psychology study: a **psychophysical one-ness of all life phenomena**. Jung was convinced the unconscious somehow links with the structure of inorganic matter (cf. psychosomatic illness). This unitarian idea (Pauli, Erich Neumann) Jung called the ***unus mundus*** ("the one world, within which matter and psyche are not yet discriminated or separately actualized"; the notes explain it follows scholastic ideas such as Duns Scotus: the total archetypal concept of the world in God's mind before actualization [p.312]). He prepared the way by pointing out that an archetype shows a **"psychoid"** (not purely psychic but almost material) aspect when it appears within a synchronistic event, "a meaningful arrangement of inner psychic and outer facts." Archetypes fit outer situations (as animal behavior patterns fit surrounding nature) and at bottom tend to appear in a synchronistic "arrangement" including both matter and psyche. These are only hints; Jung wanted to learn much more about the interrelation before abstract speculation. [p.307]
- **Most fruitful field (Jung's view): the basic mathematical axioms,** which Pauli calls "primary mathematical intuitions" (infinite series of numbers in arithmetic, continuum in geometry). Hannah Arendt (quoted): with modernity mathematics become "the science of the structure of the human mind" (a Jungian asks: which mind, conscious or unconscious?). B. L. van der Waerden's conclusion: "the unconscious is not only able to associate and combine, but even to judge. The judgment of the unconscious is an intuitive one, but it is under favorable circumstances completely sure." [pp.307–308]
- **Natural numbers:** psychologically the most interesting a priori ideas. They serve everyday counting and for centuries have been the only means of "reading" the meaning of divination systems (astrology, numerology, geomancy; all arithmetical, all studied by Jung through synchronicity). They must be **archetypal representations**: we must think of them in definite ways (nobody can deny 2 is the only even prime, even if never thought of before). Numbers "are not concepts consciously invented by men for purposes of calculation: They are spontaneous and autonomous products of the unconscious—as are other archetypal symbols." They are also qualities of outer objects (even stripped of color, temperature, size, there remains their "many-ness") and abstract concepts of our own mental set-up: "a tangible connection between the spheres of matter and psyche," hinted by Jung as the most fruitful field. [p.308]

## 6. Von Franz's closing remarks on Jung's attitude [pp.307–308]

- These ideas are not a "doctrine" but "the beginning of a new outlook that will continue to evolve." Jung searched "with unusual freedom from conventional prejudices, and at the same time with great modesty and accuracy"; he waited years before publishing and raised every doubt himself. The apparent vagueness of his ideas comes from "intellectual modesty," an attitude that does not exclude new discoveries by "rash, superficial pseudo-explanations and oversimplifications." Life remained "an exciting mystery" to Jung, never an "explained" reality as for closed minds. Creative ideas, like keys, "unlock" hitherto unintelligible connections. Her hope: that Jung's ideas lead the individual "to a more balanced, more ethical, and wider conscious outlook," and, if the reader is stimulated to work on the investigation and assimilation of the unconscious, "which always begins by working on oneself," the purpose of this introductory book will be fulfilled. [p.307]
