## Overview of this part

Jung opens *Aion* with a compact summation of the structure of the psyche: the **ego** (the center of the field of consciousness, resting on somatic and psychic bases and subordinate to the total personality), the **shadow** (the personal dark side, a moral problem, and the easiest archetype to realize), the **syzygy** of **anima** and **animus** (contrasexual, autonomous, projection-making figures; their negative "animosity" and their positive role as psychopomps and mediators), and the **self** (the totality of conscious and unconscious, standing at the top of a scale of affective value, expressed in quaternity and mandala symbols and indistinguishable empirically from a God-image). He warns throughout of **inflation** (the ego assimilated by the unconscious, or the unconscious "psychologized" away) and insists that psychological insight needs feeling-value and lived experience, not just concepts.

From chapter V he turns to the historical figure that, in Western culture, has carried the self: **Christ**. He argues Christ is "the still living myth of our culture" and an exemplary symbol of the self, but one whose shadow was cut off as **Antichrist** by the doctrine of *privatio boni* (evil as mere lack of good). He criticizes that doctrine at length (Basil, Dionysius, Augustine, Aquinas), sets against it Gnostic, Jewish-Christian and rabbinic/cabalistic images of good and evil as the two hands of God, and examines Basilides' three "sonships." Chapters VI to IX then take up the **fish symbol** and the **astrological age of Pisces** as the synchronistic frame of two thousand years of Christianity: the Fishes as a double sign (Christ and Antichrist as hostile brothers), the 7 B.C. conjunction, Talmudic and medieval calculations, Joachim of Flora's "age of the Holy Ghost," Nostradamus's prediction of an antichristian reversal, and the ambivalence of fish, Leviathan and Behemoth in Jewish and pagan traditions.

### Foreword (Jung, May 1950) [pp.15-17]
- **Theme:** the idea of the Aeon (Greek *aion*). Using Christian, Gnostic and alchemical symbols of the self, Jung examines the change of psychic situation within the "Christian aeon." [p.15]
- Christian tradition from the start carries Persian and Jewish ideas about the beginning and end of time and an intimation of an "enantiodromian reversal of dominants": the dilemma of **Christ and Antichrist**. Speculations about time and its division were largely shaped by astrological ideas (the Apocalypse shows this), so his reflections gravitate to the symbol of the **Fishes**: the Pisces aeon is "the synchronistic concomitant" of two thousand years of Christian development. In that period the figure of the **Anthropos** ("Son of Man") was progressively amplified symbolically and assimilated psychologically, and brought changes of attitude already anticipated by expectation of the Antichrist. Since the texts put the Antichrist at the end of time, one can speak of a "Christian aeon" that ends with the Second Coming; this seems to coincide with the astrological "Platonic month" of the Fishes. [p.15]
- **Occasion:** the archetypal image of wholeness, which appears often in the products of the unconscious, has forerunners in history, identified very early with Christ (see *Psychology and Alchemy*, ch. 5, "The Lapis-Christ Parallel"). Readers kept asking him to relate the traditional Christ-figure to the natural symbols of wholeness, the self. [p.16]
- **Method and stance:** he is "moderately certain" of his observations on empirical material but aware of the risk of drawing history into his reflections. He adds one more amplification (the psychological one) to the many that have accumulated around the Christ-figure, "as though continuing the historical process of assimilation." He is not making a confession of faith or writing a tendentious tract, but considering how certain things can be understood from the standpoint of modern consciousness, things in danger of being lost to incomprehension, whose understanding "would do much to remedy our philosophic disorientation." The book grew over many years from conversations with people of all ages who, in the uprootedness of modern society, were liable to lose contact with the meaning of European culture and fall into the suggestibility that feeds the Utopian mass-psychoses of the time. He writes "as a physician, with a physician's sense of responsibility, and not as a proselyte," and not as a scholar; he cites sources so conclusions can be checked. [pp.16-17]

## Chapter I. The Ego [pp.21-25; pars. 1-12 (numbers taken from the EPUB copy; not visible in the PDF text)]

*Overview:* defines ego, field of consciousness, the unconscious, and introduces the **self** as the supraordinate concept that includes the ego.

- **Ego defined (pars. 1-2).** The self "is not meant to take the place" of the ego but "includes it in a supraordinate concept." The ego is the complex factor to which all conscious contents are related; it forms "the center of the field of consciousness" and, insofar as that comprises the empirical personality, is the subject of all personal acts of consciousness. The relation of a content to the ego is the criterion of its being conscious: no content is conscious unless represented to a subject. [p.21]
- **Limits of consciousness (par. 2).** Theoretically unlimited, empirically consciousness ends where it meets the **unknown**. The unknown has two groups: the outer unknown (outside, experienced through senses) and the inner unknown, "the unconscious." [p.21]
- **Bases of the ego (pars. 3-5).** The ego is not simple but a complex that cannot be described exhaustively; it rests on two bases, somatic and psychic. [p.21]
  - *Somatic basis:* inferred from the totality of endosomatic perceptions (themselves already psychic and conscious). Many endosomatic stimuli are subliminal; some can cross the threshold, many are too elementary to count as psychic. Jung rejects the view that all life-processes are psychic: that enlarges the concept of the psyche beyond usefulness. He proposes using "psychic" only where there is evidence of a will capable of modifying reflex or instinctual processes (refers to "On the Nature of the Psyche"). [pp.21-22]
  - *Psychic basis:* the total field of consciousness plus the sum of unconscious contents. The unconscious contents fall into three groups: (1) temporarily subliminal contents reproducible at will (memory); (2) unconscious contents not voluntarily reproducible (inferred from spontaneous irruptions into consciousness); (3) contents incapable of becoming conscious at all (hypothetical, a logical inference from group 2). [p.22]
  - The ego "rests" on the field of consciousness without being identical with it; it is only its point of reference, grounded in and limited by the somatic factor. [p.22]
- **Acquired ego (par. 6).** The ego is a conscious factor "par excellence," acquired empirically during individual life. It seems to arise from the collision of the somatic factor with the environment and develops by further collisions with the outer and inner world. [p.23]
- **Personality is more than the ego (pars. 7-8).** A complete description of the ego would still give only the conscious personality. A total description of personality is impossible even in theory because the unconscious part cannot be grasped cognitively, yet the most decisive qualities of a person are often unconscious, perceived only by others or discovered with outside help. The distinction matters even for jurisprudence (responsibility). [p.23]
- **The self (par. 9).** "I have suggested calling the total personality which, though present, cannot be fully known, the self." The ego is subordinate to it as part to whole. Within consciousness the ego has "free will," meant only as the psychological fact of the subjective feeling of freedom; this freedom is limited, just as it clashes with necessity outside, by the facts of the self, which acts on the ego "like an objective occurrence which free will can do very little to alter." The ego can even be assimilated by developing unconscious components and greatly altered. [pp.23-24]
- **Individuality of the ego (par. 10).** No general description except a formal one is possible. The elements are everywhere the same but vary infinitely in clarity, emotional coloring and scope, so the ego is individual and unique, relatively stable, and capable of far-reaching changes that may be developmental and normal rather than pathological. [p.24]
- **Relativizing the ego (par. 11).** The ego is the subject of all adaptation achieved by will, hence the prejudice that it is the center of the personality and consciousness is the psyche itself. Only since the late 19th century (with precursors Leibniz, Kant, Schelling, Schopenhauer, Carus, von Hartmann) has psychology shown empirically a psyche outside consciousness; the ego remains center of the field of consciousness but is "part of the personality but not the whole of it." One should not underestimate its dependence on the unconscious; the right measure is shown by the psychic consequences of a wrong estimate. [pp.24-25]
- **Two kinds of unconscious (personality psychology) (par. 12).** Besides the three-group division, a twofold one: an "extra-conscious" psyche with **personal** contents (integral components of the individual personality that could as well be conscious) and one with **impersonal, collective** contents, "an omnipresent, unchanging, and everywhere identical quality or substrate of the psyche per se." This is a hypothesis forced by the empirical material and the general similarity of psychic processes in all people; compared to instinct in the individual being the partial manifestation of a substrate common to all. [p.25]

## Chapter II. The Shadow [pp.26-28; pars. 13-19]

*Overview:* the first and most accessible archetype of the collective unconscious; its moral nature; projection and its isolating effect; the point where the shadow gives way to the contrasexual figures.

- **Personal vs. collective unconscious.** Contents of the personal unconscious are acquired in life; contents of the collective unconscious are archetypes "present from the beginning." Those most clearly characterized empirically are the ones with the most frequent and disturbing influence on the ego: **shadow, anima, animus.** The shadow is the most accessible, since its nature can largely be inferred from the personal unconscious (exception: rare cases where the positive qualities are repressed and the ego plays a negative role). [p.26, par. 13]
- **A moral problem.** "The shadow is a moral problem that challenges the whole ego-personality"; becoming conscious of it requires recognizing the dark aspects of the personality as present and real. This is the essential condition of self-knowledge and meets considerable resistance; it often means long, painstaking work. [p.26, par. 14]
- **Emotional character.** The inferiorities making up the shadow are emotional, autonomous, "obsessive or, better, possessive." Emotion is not an activity of the individual but something that happens to him; affects occur where adaptation is weakest and reveal a lower level of personality, where one behaves like a "primitive," passive victim of affects and incapable of moral judgment. [pp.26-27, par. 15]
- **Resistance and projection.** With insight and good will the shadow can partly be assimilated, but some features resist moral control; these are bound up with unrecognized **projections**. Recognizing projection is "a moral achievement beyond the ordinary." In these cases insight fails because the cause of the emotion seems to lie beyond doubt in the other person. The subject must be convinced "that he throws a very long shadow" before withdrawing projections. [p.27, par. 16]
- **Projection is done by the unconscious.** "One meets with projections, one does not make them." Projection isolates the subject from environment, replacing real relation with an illusory one; "Projections change the world into the replica of one's own unknown face," ending in an autoerotic or autistic condition, a dreamt world whose reality remains unattainable. The resulting *sentiment d'incompletude* and feeling of sterility are explained, by further projection, as the malevolence of the environment, a vicious circle that intensifies isolation. Example: a 45-year-old patient with compulsion neurosis since twenty, cut off from the world: "I can never admit to myself that I've wasted the best twenty-five years of my life!" [pp.27-28, par. 17]
- **Tragedy of self-made fate.** A man bungles his life and others' yet cannot see the origin in himself; "an unconscious factor ... spins the illusions that veil his world ... a cocoon, which in the end will completely envelop him." [p.28, par. 18]
- **Shadow vs. contrasexual figure.** Projections that cannot be dissolved seem to belong to the shadow, but this fails after a point because the symbols then refer to the *opposite* sex. The source is no longer the shadow ("always of the same sex as the subject") but a contrasexual figure: the **animus** of a woman, the **anima** of a man, "two corresponding archetypes whose autonomy and unconsciousness explain the stubbornness of their projections." The shadow, being the personal unconscious, is relatively easy to see through; anima/animus are much further from consciousness. When the shadow appears **as an archetype** (absolute evil) the difficulty equals that of anima/animus: "it is quite within the bounds of possibility for a man to recognize the relative evil of his nature, but it is a rare and shattering experience for him to gaze into the face of absolute evil." [p.28, par. 19]

## Chapter III. The Syzygy: Anima and Animus [pp.29-40; pars. 20-42]

*Overview:* the contrasexual projection-making factor in men and women, its mother/father roots, its negative ("animosity") and positive (psychopomp) aspects, difficulty of recognizing it, and its place in the structure of the psyche (marriage quaternio).

### The anima in the man [pp.29-32, pars. 20-26]
- **The projection-making factor.** The East calls it the "Spinning Woman," **Maya**, who creates illusion by dancing (Rousselle on Taoism: the "animal soul"). Dream symbolism shows the same: the "enveloping, embracing, and devouring element points unmistakably to the mother" (note: "mother" means everything that functions as a mother). The son's Eros is passive like a child's; he hopes to be caught, sucked in, enveloped and devoured, seeking the protecting circle of infancy "released from every care." [p.29, par. 20]
- **Dramatization:** a man living regressively, fleeing a cold, cruel world; beside him a mother tirelessly hindering him from growing up and marrying: "the secret conspiracy between mother and son, and how each helps the other to betray life." Guilt probably lies with both; the son's unsatisfied longing for life should be taken seriously. [p.29, pars. 21-22]
- **Why initiative fails.** He wants to touch reality, "to embrace the earth and fructify the field of the world," but the secret memory that happiness comes as a gift from the mother cripples initiative and staying power. The world must be conquered; it demands masculinity, ardor, courage, and "a faithless Eros" able to forget the mother and bear the pain of relinquishing first love. The mother has inculcated faithfulness, devotion, loyalty to protect him from the "moral disruption" of life's adventure; he learns too well. Consummated in this is "the immemorial and most sacred archetype of the marriage of mother and son" (*hieros gamos*), which commonplace reality (registry offices, pay envelopes, monthly rent) cannot outweigh; Jung mentions homosexuality as one possible outcome (to the mother's deepest anxiety and unconscious mythological satisfaction). [p.30, par. 22]
- **Myth of the collective unconscious.** At this level the mother is both old and young (Demeter and Persephone) and the son is spouse and sleeping suckling in one. [p.30, par. 23]
- **Dissolving the projection:** the projection-making factor is identical with the mother-imago and so is taken for the real mother. It dissolves only when the son sees that his psyche holds an imago not only of mother but of daughter, sister, beloved, "the heavenly goddess, and the chthonic Baubo." Every mother and beloved must carry this "omnipresent and ageless image," the perilous image of Woman: loyalty he must sometimes forgo; compensation for life's disappointments; solace; and also "the great illusionist, the seductress, who draws him into life with her Maya," including life's "frightful paradoxes and ambivalences." "Because she is his greatest danger she demands from a man his greatest." [pp.30-31, par. 24]
- **Term "anima."** The image is "My Lady Soul" (Spitteler); Jung prefers "anima" as more specific than "soul." He deliberately describes the living processes of the psyche in a dramatic, mythological manner rather than abstract scientific terms, which he finds more exact. [p.31, par. 25]
- **Nature of the anima.** She is the unconscious as represented by a personified feminine being in dreams, visions and fantasies; "not an invention of the conscious, but a spontaneous product of the unconscious." Not a substitute for the mother: the numinosity of the mother-imago derives from the collective archetype of the anima, "incarnated anew in every male child." (Notes: first appears as an idea in 16th-century humanist Richardus Vitus; see Fierz-David on the *Dream of Poliphilo*.) [pp.31-32, par. 26]

### The animus in the woman [pp.32-34, pars. 27-34]
- Since man is compensated by a feminine element, woman is compensated by a masculine one; not deduced but grasped empirically over long experience; Jung calls the work provisional pioneer work. As the mother is the first carrier for the son, the father is for the daughter. [p.32, pars. 27-28]
- **Animus** ("mind or spirit"). The animus corresponds to the paternal **Logos** as the anima corresponds to the maternal **Eros.** Jung uses them as conceptual aids only: woman's consciousness is characterized more by the connective quality of Eros than by discrimination and cognition (Logos); in men Eros, "the function of relationship," is usually less developed than Logos; in women Logos is "often only a regrettable accident." [p.32, par. 29] [note: these are Jung's own generalizations as stated.]
- **Opinions vs. reflections.** The animus produces "opinions instead of reflections," a priori assumptions claiming absolute truth. He is partial to argument; the "Father" (the sum of conventional opinions) plays a great role. A man possessed by the anima becomes "the animus of his own anima"; with him the issue becomes vanity and touchiness, with a woman power (truth, justice, some "ism"). Typical dialogue between animus and anima: commonplaces, clichés from newspapers and novels, "brain-splitting lack of logic," repeated millions of times in every language. [pp.32-33, par. 29]
- **When they meet:** the animus draws his sword of power, the anima ejects "her poison of illusion and seduction"; or they fall in love, with the language of love equally banal, though partners live in the illusion of a unique relation. [p.33, par. 30]
- **"Animosity."** The anima/animus relationship, positive or negative, is full of "animosity," emotional and hence collective, lowering relationship to the common instinctual basis. In the man the cloud of animosity is mostly sentimentality and resentment; in the woman, opinionated views, interpretations, insinuations and misconstructions aimed at severing relationships between people. She is wrapped in illusions by her "demon-familiar," translated "to the land of sheep" where the animus is the shepherd of her soul. [pp.33-34, pars. 31-32]
- **Positive aspect of the animus.** Through the figure of the father he expresses not only conventional opinion but "spirit," philosophical or religious ideas, or the attitude resulting from them: a **psychopomp**, a mediator between conscious and unconscious and a personification of the latter. As the anima becomes by integration the Eros of consciousness (giving relationship and relatedness), the animus becomes a Logos, giving woman's consciousness reflection, deliberation and self-knowledge. [p.34, par. 33]
- **Effect on the ego (both).** The effect is hard to eliminate: (1) it fills the ego with "an unshakable feeling of rightness and righteousness," (2) its cause is projected and seems to lie in objects. Both traits trace to the a priori archetype; consciousness is "fascinated by it, held captive, as if hypnotized." The ego feels a vague moral defeat and becomes defensive and self-righteous, a vicious circle; a feeling of inferiority, like megalomania, makes mutual recognition impossible, "and without this there is no relationship." [pp.34-35, par. 34]

### Why insight is harder than with the shadow [pp.35-37, pars. 35-39]
- Shadow insight is helped by education (everyone knows "inferior personality"; "a Sunday sermon, his wife, or the tax collector" can refresh memory). For anima/animus there is no moral education, and people prefer self-righteousness and mutual vilification to recognizing projections; irrational moods in men and irrational opinions in women seem natural, probably instinct-grounded, "to ensure that the Empedoclean game of the hate and love of the elements shall continue for all eternity." Added difficulties are intellectual, plus doubt whether one is meddling with nature by prodding into consciousness what was better left asleep. [pp.35-36, par. 35]
- The concepts are unfamiliar, therefore unpopular, mobilizing prejudice and becoming taboo. [p.36, par. 36]
- **The "veiled one" fallacy (enkekalymmenos).** Requiring projections to be dissolved is new ground. Everyone assumes "my father" is a faithful reflection of the real father. But a person's idea of someone consists of a possibly incomplete picture of the real person plus subjective modifications; for X's image of his father "the real father is only in part responsible, an indefinitely larger share falling to the son." Every criticism or praise of the father is unconsciously directed at oneself. (Footnote: the Eubulides paradox: "Can you recognize your father? Yes. This veiled one? No. This veiled one is your father.") With poorly developed Eros X feels hurt, misunderstood, betrayed. [pp.36-37, par. 37]
- Telling people is not enough; it is "like expecting the average respectable citizen to recognize himself as a criminal." [p.37, par. 38]
- Not all contents of anima/animus are projected: many appear spontaneously in dreams and many can be made conscious through **active imagination,** revealing "thoughts, feelings, and affects ... alive in us which we would never have believed possible." The "normal person" who "knows what he thinks" holds a childish attitude. The increase in self-knowledge "is still very rare nowadays and is usually paid for in advance with a neurosis, if not with something worse." [pp.37-38, par. 39]

### Autonomy, function, and divine pair [pp.38-40, pars. 40-41]
- **Anima/animus as functions.** They are the autonomy of the collective unconscious in figures; they personify contents that, withdrawn from projection, can be integrated. They are **functions filtering contents of the collective unconscious through to consciousness**, behaving so only while conscious and unconscious tendencies do not diverge much. Under tension they confront consciousness personified, like split-off systems or part-souls (but nothing has split off from the ego; they are "a disturbing accretion"). Their contents can be integrated but they themselves cannot, being archetypes: "foundation stones of the psychic structure," beyond direct cognition. Hence they remain autonomous and must be borne constantly in mind; "constant observation pays the unconscious a tribute that more or less guarantees its co-operation." Paying continual attention to the symptomatology of unconscious contents is a task of "psychic hygiene," because consciousness tends toward one-sidedness. [p.38, par. 40]
- **Compensation** works fully only where life is simple and unconscious enough to follow "the serpentine path of instinct." The more civilized and complicated one is, the more opinions, beliefs, theories and collective tendencies drown "the quiet voice of nature." One must therefore picture archetypes not as "a rushing phantasmagoria of fugitive images but as constant, autonomous factors." [pp.38-39, par. 40]
- **Fatality and the divine pair.** Both archetypes possess fatality, "quite literally the father and mother of all the disastrous entanglements of fate." Together they form a divine pair (syzygy): the male marked by *pneuma* and *nous* (like Hermes with his shifting hues); the female wearing the features of Aphrodite, Helen (Selene), Persephone, Hecate. They are "gods" in the ancient sense; their power grows in proportion as they remain unconscious: "Those who do not see them are in their hands, just as a typhus epidemic flourishes best when its source is undiscovered." In Christianity the divine syzygy survives as Christ and his bride the Church (2 Clement quoted; Mary often replaces the Church). [pp.39, par. 41]
  - Footnote: the **syzygy has three elements**: femininity pertaining to the man and masculinity pertaining to the woman; the experience of man with woman and vice versa; and the masculine and feminine archetypal image. The first can be integrated by realization; the last cannot. [p.39 n.5]

### Recapitulation: stages and the marriage quaternio [p.40, par. 42]
- Integrating the shadow (realizing the personal unconscious) is the **first stage** of the analytic process, without which anima/animus cannot be recognized. The shadow is realized through a relation to a partner, anima/animus through a partner of the opposite sex, since only there do projections operate.
- Recognition of the anima yields a **triad**, one third transcendent: masculine subject, opposing feminine subject, transcendent anima. For a woman the reverse. The missing fourth to make a quaternity is, in a man, the **Wise Old Man** (not discussed here), in a woman the **Chthonic Mother.** These four make a half-immanent, half-transcendent quaternity, the **marriage quaternio**, a schema "not only for the self but also for the structure of primitive society with its cross-cousin marriage, marriage classes, and division of settlements into quarters." The self "is a God-image, or at least cannot be distinguished from one"; hence Clement of Alexandria's saying that he who knows himself knows God. (Cross-refs: *Psychology of the Transference* pars. 425ff.; Aion later, the Naassene quaternio, pars. 358ff., and par. 347.) [p.40]

## Chapter IV. The Self [pp.41-53; pars. 43-67]

*Overview:* effects on the ego of integrating the unconscious; inflation; the ego's dual danger (assimilation by the unconscious or by the ego of the unconscious); moral decisions and "the will of God"; feeling-value and the hierarchy of psychic factors; mandala as symbol of wholeness; intellect versus experience; parallels to metaphysical ideas; the post-Christian situation. (Material drawn from the 1948 Eranos paper "Über das Selbst.")

### Inflation and the two dangers [pp.41-43, pars. 43-47]
- Integrating contents of the collective unconscious ("withdrawal of impersonal projections") enlarges the field of consciousness and the importance of the ego, especially when the ego lacks a critical approach to the unconscious; it then becomes identical with assimilated contents (e.g., a masculine consciousness possessed by the anima). [pp.41, par. 43]
- The more significant unconscious contents are assimilated, the closer the ego approximates the self (never ending), which "inevitably produces an **inflation** of the ego" unless a critical line of demarcation is drawn. That works only if the ego gets reasonable boundaries and the unconscious figures (self, anima, animus, shadow) are granted relative autonomy and reality. "To psychologize this reality out of existence" is ineffectual or increases inflation; "One cannot dispose of facts by declaring them unreal." Anyone who denies the projection-making factor becomes identical with it. Inflation is usually not conscious self-aggrandizement; it is inferred from symptoms, including environmental reactions and the "growing disinclination to take note of the reactions of the environment." (Footnote: Paul's "puffed up," 1 Cor. 5:2.) [pp.41-42, par. 44]
- **Psychic catastrophe #1:** the ego assimilated by the self. The image of wholeness then remains unconscious, sharing the archaic nature and the relative space-time of the unconscious; both numinous, with unlimited determining effect on a differentiated ego that lives in absolute space and time. Remedy: anchor the ego in consciousness, with "attention, conscientiousness, patience," accurate observation of the unconscious's symptomatology, and objective self-criticism. [pp.42-43, pars. 45-46]
- **Inflation #2 (the opposite route):** over-accentuating the ego and consciousness so that the unconscious figures are psychologized and the self is assimilated to the ego. Same result, inflation, but the remedy is reversed: room must be made for the unconscious "at the expense of the world of consciousness"; the ego's presumption must be "damped down by moral defeat." This is "not a question ... of relaxing morality itself but of making a moral effort in a different direction": the conscientious person must achieve the moral feat of loosening worldly ties (example: Brother Klaus, canonized, who left wife and children for his soul's salvation). [p.43, par. 47]

### Moral decision, "Act of God," will of God [pp.43-46, pars. 48-51]
- Real moral problems begin "where the penal code leaves off" and spring from **conflicts of duty**; solutions seldom rest on precedent or precepts. The humble or easygoing rely on outside authority; one who trusts neither others nor himself decides only through an "Act of God" (Common Law: "action of uncontrollable natural forces"), i.e., an unconscious authority creating a *fait accompli*. [pp.43-44, par. 48]
- Two interpretations: "natural forces/instincts" (satisfies the intellect but offends moral self-esteem; civilized man fears the "crimen laesae maiestatis humanae" and retroactively colors facts to claim willpower) or "will of God" (benefits self-esteem; can be accused of cloaking laxity, but usually instinctive tendencies assert themselves regardless). The inner authority is present from the start "in the intensity of the tendencies struggling for decision"; the individual takes part "more or less 'voluntarily.'" The roots of the feeling of moral freedom are unknown but exist as surely as instincts. [p.44, par. 49]
- It is psychologically more "correct" and beneficial to call instinct-forces the "will of God": one then lives in harmony with the ancestral psychic *habitus*, whose existence proves its viability. "Psychological truths are not metaphysical insights; they are habitual modes of thinking, feeling, and behaving which experience has proved appropriate and useful." [p.45, par. 50]
- Impulses are not arbitrary wishes but "absolutes which one must learn how to handle correctly." The will may suppress but cannot alter them; what is suppressed returns "loaded with a resentment that makes the otherwise harmless natural impulse our enemy." "God" here means more like Diotima's "Eros ... is a mighty daemon": the Greek *daimon* is a determining power from outside like fate, yet "the ethical decision is left to man," who must know what he is deciding. [p.45, par. 51]

### Feeling-value and the hierarchy of psychic contents [pp.45-48, pars. 52-58]
- The purely biological/intellectual standpoint is insufficient in psychology: the psychic phenomenon consists of **meaning** and **value**, the latter determined by the intensity of the accompanying feeling-tones; hence both "rational" functions (thinking and feeling) are needed. [pp.45-46, par. 52] Feeling-value is a necessary criterion: affective value measures the intensity of an idea, which expresses its "energic tension, its effective potential." [p.46, par. 53]
- **The ascending scale:** the shadow has a decidedly negative feeling-value with describable tones; anima and animus a more positive one, harder to define, felt as fascinating or numinous, with an atmosphere of "sensitivity, touchy reserve, secretiveness, painful intimacy, and even absoluteness." In affective rank anima/animus stand to the shadow as the shadow stands to ego-consciousness. When the unconscious gains the upper hand the scale reverses; affective value increases up the scale "**ego-consciousness, shadow, anima, self.**" This reversal occurs regularly in the transition to sleep, and every *abaissement du niveau mental* causes a relative reversal of values. [p.46, par. 53]
- **Objective values** (consensus omnium: moral, aesthetic, religious ideals; Lévy-Bruhl's "représentations collectives") versus subjective feeling-tones ("value quanta," recognized by the constellations or disturbances they cause). Collective ideas may lack subjective tone but keep their value, evident by attributes and symbolism. [p.47, par. 54]
- **Dream representation:** a collective idea may appear in a dream only by a subsidiary attribute (a god by his theriomorphic attribute; "the 'goddess' appears as a black cat, and the Deity as the lapis exilis (stone of no worth)"). Conversely an idea may appear in consciousness stripped of its affective weight and must be restored to its archetypal context, a task "usually discharged by poets and prophets" (Hölderlin's "Hymn to Liberty" quoted: liberty returns as a goddess/anima figure, a psychopomp to the Elysian fields). [pp.47-48, pars. 55-57]
- Symbols range from highest to lowest: the shadow belongs to "bodiless phantoms"; anima/animus appear only as projections; the self "is completely outside the personal sphere, and appears, if at all, only as a religious mythologem." One who identifies with daylight consciousness will call dreams null, though night is as long as day and consciousness is "founded on unconsciousness"; "what is small by day is big at night." [p.48, par. 57]
- **Integration requires feeling.** A content can be integrated only when its double aspect is conscious and grasped "not merely intellectually but ... according to its feeling-value." Intellect and feeling conflict by definition; one who identifies with intellect meets feeling as an enemy in the guise of the anima; an intellectual animus attacks the feeling standpoint. To realize something both ways one must come to grips with the anima/animus problem "to open the way for a higher union, a **coniunctio oppositorum**." [pp.48-49, par. 58]

### Wholeness, the mandala, and the imago Dei [pp.49-51, pars. 59-62]
- "Wholeness" looks like an abstract idea but is empirical because anticipated by the psyche in spontaneous symbols: **quaternity or mandala symbols**, found in dreams of people who never heard of them and widely in history. Wholeness is "an objective factor that confronts the subject independently of him," higher in rank than the syzygy, which represents a substantial part of it (the royal brother-sister pair, whose tension of opposites gives birth to the "divine child" as symbol of unity). [p.49, par. 59]
- Unity and totality stand highest on the scale of objective values because their symbols "can no longer be distinguished from the imago Dei"; statements about the God-image apply to empirical symbols of totality. **Mandalas** are symbols of order, occurring in patients during times of disorientation or re-orientation; as magic circles they bind lawless powers and transform chaos into cosmos. The mandala first appears as "an unimpressive point or dot"; its full range takes hard work and many projections withdrawn. [pp.49-50, par. 60]
- **Warning about intellect.** The intellect can master formulations (God within us, Christ and the corpus mysticum, the atman) and so create the illusion of possessing the thing; "one has acquired nothing more than its name." A concept is "no more than a name, a *flatus vocis* ... have no weight or substance. They sound full but are hollow." The intellect is "a great cheat and illusionist" when it manipulates values. [p.50, par. 60]
- **Psychology requires feeling.** One can pursue any science with the intellect alone "except psychology"; every psychic process has a feeling-tone, the degree to which the subject is affected. "It is through the 'affect' that the subject becomes involved and so comes to feel the whole weight of reality," like the difference between reading of an illness in a textbook and having it. "In psychology one possesses nothing unless one has experienced it in reality." [pp.50-51, par. 61]
- **Fear of the unconscious.** Many fear even their shadow; fear turns to panic with anima/animus, because the syzygy represents contents that irrupt in psychosis (most clearly paranoid schizophrenia; Nelken's case and Schreber's *Memoirs* cited). Overcoming this fear is a moral achievement but not the only condition for a real experience of the self. [p.51, par. 62]

### Concepts as abbreviated experience; mythological parallels [pp.51-53, pars. 63-67]
- Shadow, syzygy and self are factors that can be pictured only through fairly thorough experience; the "concept" is "simply an abbreviated description or definition of these facts," like criticism of a platypus by zoology; Jung cannot pass on the experience but has described the method; "One could see the moons of Jupiter even in Galileo's day if one took the trouble to use his telescope." [pp.51-52, par. 63]
- Comparative mythology readers recognize the figures: the shadow as "the adverse representative of the dark chthonic world," the syzygy as "psychic prototype of all divine couples," the self as "the eidos behind the supreme ideas of unity and totality inherent in all monotheistic and monistic systems." [p.52, par. 64]
- **Value of parallels:** they relate "so-called metaphysical concepts, which have lost their root connection with natural experience, to living, universal psychic processes." The existence of a belief does not prove its object, though coincidence of idea and reality in a "state of grace" is not impossible. Once metaphysical ideas lose power to evoke the original experience they become impediments; "One clings to possessions that have once meant wealth; and the more ineffective ... the more obstinately people cling to them. (Naturally it is only sterile ideas that they cling to; living ideas have content and riches enough ...)." [p.52, par. 65]
- The modern world wants an explanation, not a "message": how does Christ's death redeem when no one feels redeemed; what is the God-man, Trinity, parthenogenesis, eating body and drinking blood? We live sixteen of twenty-four hours in the everyday world and spend the rest unconscious; so it was a discovery that dreams sometimes contain scenes resembling the motifs of myth. [pp.52-53, par. 66]
- **Post-Christian spirit.** Until 1933 only lunatics would have been found with living fragments of mythology; then "the world of heroes and monsters spread like a devastating fire over whole nations," proving myth lost no vitality in the centuries of reason. Metaphysical ideas lose fascination because the old symbols no longer express "what is now welling up from the unconscious as the end-result of the development of Christian consciousness": an *antimimon pneuma*, "a false spirit of arrogance, hysteria, woolly-mindedness, criminal amorality, and doctrinaire fanaticism, a purveyor of shoddy spiritual goods, spurious art, philosophical stutterings, and Utopian humbug, fit only to be fed wholesale to the mass man of today." [p.53, par. 67]

*Cross-references in the book:* Chapter IV's mandala/self claims reappear in ch. XIV (Structure and Dynamics of the Self); the Christ/Antichrist pairing continues in ch. V; footnotes point forward to pars. 340, 347, 358ff.

## Chapter V. Christ, a Symbol of the Self [pp.54-89; pars. 68-126]

*Overview:* Christ as the Western culture's living embodiment of the archetype of the self; the Antichrist as its shadow; a long critique of the doctrine of *privatio boni* (evil as mere privation of good) from Origen to Aquinas; counter-models from the Clementine Homilies, the Ascension of Isaiah and rabbinic and cabalistic sources, where good and evil are right and left hands of God; Basilides' three "sonships"; closing reflections on perfection versus completeness, individuation and "world situation."

### Why Christ and Antichrist [pp.54-55, pars. 68-70]
- Jung links the "dechristianization" of the world, the "Luciferian development of science and technology" and the destruction of the second World War to the New Testament eschatology of the Antichrist (1 John 2:22; 4:3; 2 Thess. 2:3ff, the "man of lawlessness"). The "anima Christiana" knows both of the adversary and of his future usurpation of power. [p.54, par. 68]
- Christ is "the still living myth of our culture," "our culture hero," who "regardless of his historical existence, embodies the myth of the divine Primordial Man, the mystic Adam," center of the Christian mandala, Lord of the Tetramorph (the four evangelist symbols as columns of his throne). His kingdom is the pearl of great price, the treasure in the field, the mustard seed, the heavenly city; "As Christ is in us, so also is his heavenly kingdom" (Luke 17:20f; Oxyrhynchus saying "whosoever knoweth himself shall find it"). [pp.54-55, par. 69]
- **Thesis:** "Christ exemplifies the archetype of the self." He represents a totality of a divine kind, a glorified man *sine macula peccati,* "Adam secundus," corresponding to the first Adam before the Fall. [p.55, par. 70]

### Patristic image-of-God material [pp.55-58, pars. 70-73]
- **Tertullian:** the image of God in man = the human spirit having the same motions and senses as God, though not in the same way. **Origen:** the imago Dei is imprinted on the soul, not the body, and is "an image of an image"; Christ is the true image of God, after whose likeness the inner man (invisible, incorporeal, incorrupt, immortal) is made; it reveals itself in prudence, justice, moderation, virtue, wisdom and discipline. [pp.55-56, par. 70]
- **Augustine:** distinguishes the God-image which is Christ from the image implanted in man as a possibility of becoming like God; it lies in the *anima rationalis*, not the body ("where the understanding is, where the mind is ... there God has his image"); it equals the *homo coelestis* of Paul and the "inner man." Christ took on "a complete human soul," embodying totality. [pp.56-57, par. 71]
- **Psychological equivalents:** the God-image damaged but not destroyed by the Fall, restored by grace. Christ's *descensus ad inferos* is read as an equivalent of integrating the collective unconscious in the **individuation** process. The soul as Christ's bride and the Church's *hieros gamos* develop into the alchemical *coniunctio* ("chymical wedding"), giving the *lapis philosophorum* (totality) and the concept of chemical combination. [pp.57-58, par. 72]
- **Romans 12:2 / "reformation":** the totality images the unconscious produces in individuation are "reformations" of an a priori archetype (the mandala). The "renewal" is not an actual alteration but restoration of an original condition, an **apocatastasis**; a consciousness illuminated by conversion recognizes the ever-present archetype in the figure of Christ ("anamnesis"), bridging the split caused by instincts striving in opposite directions. The split is absent only when one is legitimately unconscious of instinct like an animal; a repression (artificial unconsciousness) is "harmful and impossible to endure." [pp.58-59, par. 73]

### The missing shadow; Antichrist as the shadow of the self [pp.59-63, pars. 74-79]
- The original Christian imago Dei in Christ was all-embracing (even the animal side), but the Christ-symbol "lacks wholeness in the modern psychological sense" because it excludes the dark side as a Luciferian opponent. By the doctrine of *privatio boni* (Origen onward), evil is "the accidental lack of perfection," hence "omne bonum a Deo, omne malum ab homine." [p.59, par. 74] Wholeness appeared guaranteed; but empirically evil is the opposite of good, not a diminution. [p.59, par. 75]
- **Gnostic counterpoint:** Christ "cast off his shadow from himself" (Valentinian: Christ, masculine, shed the shadow of his mother Sophia outside the Pleroma; the shadow over the world is the *princeps huius mundi*, the devil). [p.59 n.23; pp.59-60, par. 75]
- **Antichrist** in legend is a perverse imitator of Christ's life, *antimimon pneuma*, following Christ "like a shadow following the body." Jung: if Christ parallels the self, "the Antichrist would correspond to the shadow of the self, namely the dark half of the human totality." The empirical self "depicting itself spontaneously ... as an archetypal quaternity bound together by inner antinomies cannot omit the shadow that belongs to the light figure, for without it this figure lacks body and humanity. In the empirical self, light and shadow form a paradoxical unity"; in Christian doctrine the archetype is "split into two irreconcilable halves, leading ultimately to a metaphysical dualism." [p.60, pars. 75-76]
- **The Devil's rise:** the devil attains stature as Christ's adversary only after the rise of Christianity; in Job he is still one of God's sons. Christ's dogmatic figure is so spotless that "everything else turns dark beside it" and demands a psychic complement; early on the doctrine of two sons of God (the elder Satanael). The coming of the Antichrist is "an inexorable psychological law" (enantiodromia): "every intensified differentiation of the Christ-image brings about a corresponding accentuation of its unconscious complement, thereby increasing the tension between above and below." [pp.60-61, par. 77]
- **Pendulum.** The Christian disposition's inner fatality reverses its spirit: the ideal of spirituality striving for the heights collided with the earth-bound passion to conquer matter; this was visible in the Renaissance ("rebirth" which was really medieval Christianity undergoing pagan transformations, trading the heavenly goal for an earthly one, the Gothic vertical for horizontal exploration), then the Enlightenment and French Revolution, producing a world situation called "antichristian" in a sense confirming early "end of time" anticipations. "No tree ... can grow to heaven unless its roots reach down to hell." The meeting of Christ with Satan at the start of his career shows the "mysterium iniquitatis" accompanies the "sol iustitiae" as shadow belongs to light, like two royal brothers (Ebionites, Euchites) partitioning world and epoch (the thousand-year reign, the coming of the Antichrist). [p.61-62, par. 78]
- **Crucifixion of the ego.** Christ is Jung's nearest analogy to the self, as gods of antiquity are to anima/animus; the attributes of Christ (consubstantial with the Father, coeternal, filiation, parthenogenesis, crucifixion, Lamb sacrificed between opposites, One divided into Many) mark an embodiment of the self but only **one half** of the archetype; the other half is Antichrist. The Saviour crucified between two thieves means that the progressive differentiation of consciousness yields a more menacing awareness of conflict, "nothing less than a **crucifixion of the ego,** its agonizing suspension between irreconcilable opposites." This is not total extinction of the ego (which would be unconsciousness), but a relative abolition for "supreme and ultimate decisions" in insoluble conflicts of duty, where the ego is "a suffering bystander who decides nothing but must submit." The "genius" of man, the higher and more spacious part whose extent no one knows, has the final word. [pp.62-63, par. 79] (Footnote: Origen's *Contra Celsum* on the Son of God and the opposite extreme son of Satan; opposites condition one another, "an increase in the one means a diminution of the other.") [pp.62-63 nn.]

### Critique of the privatio boni [pp.63-72, pars. 80-98]
- **The Summum Bonum root.** The reason the Christian self-image lacks its shadow is the doctrine of the Summum Bonum. Irenaeus objected to the Gnostic view that the "light of the Father" could not illuminate the shadow and void within the pleroma. "For the Christian neither God nor Christ could be a paradox; they had to have a single meaning." Jung reproves the "hybris of the speculative intellect" that obliged God to be the Summum Bonum, noting a Protestant theologian's claim that "God can only be good" and that Yahweh "could have taught him a thing or two." [p.64, par. 80]
- **Sources reviewed in order** (each with quotations in the book): Tatian ("Nothing evil was created by God; we ourselves have produced all wickedness") and Theophilus of Antioch [par. 81]; **Basil the Great** (evil is *steresis*, privation, a condition of the soul arising from "light-mindedness" (*rhathymia*), neither uncreated nor created) [pars. 82-83, pp.64-65]; Titus of Bostra; John Chrysostom (*ekklisis*, turning away) [par. 86]; **Dionysius the Areopagite** (*De divinis nominibus* ch. 4: evil "does not exist at all" because everything that is derives from good) [pars. 87-88, p.67]; **Augustine** (evil as the privation of good; "the steel is not evil but the man who uses it for a criminal purpose"; "evil ... is merely a diminution of good"; desire or act of a misdirected will) [pars. 89-90, pp.67-69]; **Thomas Aquinas** (good is the appetible; "evil is not a being"; "every agent works for a good") [pars. 91-92, p.69]. Jung notes the anti-Manichaean motive behind these denials [par. 85, 89] and the 4th Lateran Council statement that devils were created good but became evil of their own motion (n.46).
- **Jung's objections:**
  1. **High/low is not causation.** That "high" implies "low" is "twisted into a causal relationship"; good and evil are "a logically equivalent pair of opposites ... the sine qua non of all acts of cognition," coexistent halves of a moral judgment, not derived one from the other. If man is the author of evil he is also the author of good; but man is "first and foremost the author merely of judgments," and his responsibility for the facts judged is hard to define (requires defining free will). [pp.65-66, par. 84]
  2. **Tracing to a psychic cause does not nullify it.** If evil arises from the "mutilation of the soul," that mutilation has an equally real cause; "When something ... is traced back to a psychic condition or fact, it is very definitely not reduced to nothing ... but is shifted on to the plane of psychic reality." If the devil fell by his own will, evil preceded man; "petitio principii" in Basil. [p.66, par. 85]
  3. **Aquinas's logic:** Aquinas cites Aristotle that a thing is whiter the less it is mixed with black without noting the reverse is logically equivalent; "good" in his argument means "convenient, suitable"; so "omne agens agit propter bonum" means "Every agent works for the sake of what suits it. That's what the devil does too." [pp.69-70, par. 93]
  4. **The cold analogy.** One could equally say Arctic winter is "only a little below" equatorial heat (230 degrees above absolute zero is the real figure he gives), so all things are "warm"; "just as cold is nothing but a diminution of warmth, so evil is nothing but a diminution of good." Premise "Deus = Summum Bonum" makes a false conclusion necessary: the perfect good could not create evil, so it created "the good and the less good (which last is simply called 'worse' by laymen)." "He who created the heat is also responsible for the cold." [p.70, pars. 94-95]
  5. **Lived reality.** "One could hardly call the things that have happened ... in the concentration camps of the dictator states an 'accidental lack of perfection'—it would sound like mockery." [p.71, par. 96]
- **Psychology's own position on good/evil [pp.71-72, pars. 97-98]:** psychology does not know good and evil in themselves, "only ... as judgments about relationships." "Good" is what seems suitable, acceptable, valuable from a standpoint; evil is its opposite; some things are extremely evil, i.e., dangerous, and "It is pointless to gloss over these evil things, because that only lulls one into a sense of false security." "Only unconsciousness makes no difference between good and evil." "Those who deem themselves beyond good and evil are usually the worst tormentors of mankind, because they are twisted with the pain and fear of their own sickness." Today it is important not to overlook "the danger of the evil lurking within"; psychology must insist on the reality of evil. His criticism holds only as far as psychological experience goes; the *privatio boni* "may therefore be a metaphysical truth" he makes no judgment about, but empirically "white and black, light and dark, good and bad, are equivalent opposites which always predicate one another." The tendency to give priority to "good" is itself a psychological fact. [pp.71-72, par. 98]

### The Clementine Homilies and Jewish sources: God's two hands [pp.72-79, pars. 99-112]
- **Clementine Homilies** (Gnostic-Christian, 2nd-4th c.): good and evil as the **right and left hands of God**; creation in syzygies. Marinus the Bardesanite: good as light and right; evil dark and left; the left also feminine (Sophia Prounikos = "Sinistra"). The view shows "the reality of evil does not necessarily lead to Manichaean dualism" and guarantees the unity of the God-image "on a plane beyond the crucial difference between the Yahwistic and the Christian points of view": Yahweh "is notoriously unjust, and injustice is not good"; the God of Christianity "is only good." [pp.72-73, par. 99]
- God "appointed two kingdoms and two ages" (present world to evil, the future world to good); man is a compound of two mixtures, female (body, emotional) and male (spirit, rational); two triads: **somatic** (desire, anger, grief) and **spirit** (reflection, knowledge, fear). The four elements (hot, cold, moist, dry) come from God; evil arises from a combination "from without" generating "choice (*prohairesis*) ... which rejoices in evils." Jung concludes that the author is unsure how evil arose; evil is not created by God "nor was it projected out of him, nor did it arise of itself." [pp.73-74, pars. 100-102] [note: Jung comments that the author's Petrine, ritualistic Christianity ties him to the Ebionite view that God had two sons, the elder Satan, the younger Christ; Michaias remarks that if good and evil are begotten alike they must be brothers.] [pp.74-75, par. 103]
- **Ascension of Isaiah:** seven heavens; Sammael and his hosts battle in the firmament; in the first heaven angels on the right of the throne are more beautiful than those on the left; in higher heavens no difference. The devil dwells in the firmament like the Gnostic archons; his sphere interpenetrates the divine sphere; "a picture of complementary opposites balancing one another like right and left hands," perhaps "a genuine yang-yin relationship" which "does not damage monotheism" and is "closer to the actual truth than the privatio boni." Jung speculates that Manichaean dualism made the Fathers realize they believed firmly in the substantiality of evil, leading to "the dangerously anthropomorphic assumption that what man cannot unite, God cannot unite either." [pp.75-76, par. 104]
- **Jewish-Christian origin of the "two sons" doctrine** passes to Bogomils and Cathars; in Judaism into the cabalistic Tree of the Sephiroth with *hesed* (love) and *din* (justice). [p.76, par. 105] Rabbinic passages collected by Zwi Werblowsky: the destroyer does not distinguish the righteous from the wicked (R. Joseph); Yahweh's momentary wrath (Exodus 33:5; Balaam knew the moment); love and mercy as God's right hand, justice as his left (midrash on 1 Kings 22:19: intercessors right, accusers left); "When the children of Israel perform God's will, they make the left hand his right hand"; God created the world on mercy and justice together ("If I create the world on the basis of mercy alone, its sins will be great; but on the basis of justice alone the world cannot exist"); Abraham's plea: "Thou wouldst hold the cord by both ends"; Yahweh prefers repentant sinners; God prays to himself ("May it be My will that My mercy may suppress My anger") and R. Ishmael's vision of Akathriel Jah Jahweh Zebaoth on the throne asking to be blessed. [pp.76-78, pars. 106-110]
- **Effect of the Job God-image:** Job's contradictory image became a subject of religious speculation in Judaism, through the Cabala influenced Jakob Böhme (love and "wrath-fire" of God, in which Lucifer burns). [pp.78-79, par. 111]
- **Psychological caution:** "Since psychology is not metaphysics, no metaphysical dualism can be derived from, or imputed to, its statements concerning the equivalence of opposites." Equivalent opposites are conditions of cognition, probably a property of consciousness rather than of the object ("perhaps even creates distinctions where no differences are discernible"). Footnote answers Victor White's charge of Manichaean tendency: what of hell and the devil if they are eternal and "consist of nothing"? Jung stresses "the unity of the self, this central archetype which is a **complexio oppositorum** par excellence." [p.79, par. 112 and n.74]
- **Consequences of the doctrine:** Jung dwells on *privatio boni* because it yields "a too optimistic conception of the evil in human nature and ... a too pessimistic view of the human soul." Early Christianity, "with unerring logic," balanced Christ against an Antichrist; only with Christ did a devil enter the world as "the real counterpart of God." Further: Basil's attribution of evil to "frivolity" leaves it a quantity negligible, "but psychological causation is something so elusive ... that everything which is reduced to it takes on the character of futility"; yet imputing the world's colossal evil to the psyche produces "a negative inflation ... a daemonic claim to power on the part of the unconscious," anticipated in the Antichrist and reflected in contemporary events of the "Christian aeon of the Fishes, now running to its end." [pp.79-80, pars. 113-114]

### The antinomial quaternion; Basilides' three sonships [pp.80-85, pars. 115-120]
- **Self as antinomy.** Christ in the Christian world represents the self, "the apotheosis of individuality" (unique, once in time); but the psychological self is transcendent and expressible only in antinomies, as light is expressed through waves and particles. Diagram 1: **UNITEMPORAL / UNIQUE / UNIVERSAL / ETERNAL.** Christ as historical personage is unitemporal and unique; as God universal and eternal; the self likewise. [pp.80-81, par. 115] (Footnote 75: Christ "did not merely symbolize wholeness, but, as a psychic phenomenon, he was wholeness" in a pre-psychological age; "The idea of totality is, at any given time, as total as one is oneself.")
- Theology calling Christ "good" and "spiritual" generates "evil" and "material or chthonic" as the Antichrist. Diagram 2 (psychological self): **GOOD** on top, **SPIRITUAL** left, **MATERIAL OR CHTHONIC** right (opposites no longer separated from the whole; the shadow of the self "turns out to be much less black"). [p.81, par. 116]
- This quaternio characterizes the self as including light and dark, masculine and feminine; the marriage quaternio, known to the Naassenes per Hippolytus; individuation is a "mysterium coniunctionis," experienced as nuptial union of opposite halves and drawn spontaneously as mandalas. [p.82, par. 117]
- **Jesus as principium individuationis:** Hippolytus reports Basilides: Jesus was "the first sacrifice in the discrimination of the natures"; the Passion occurred "for no other reason than the discrimination of composite things." Basilides' "non-existent" God begot a **threefold sonship** (*huiotes*): finest remains above with the Father; second, grosser, rises on wings like Plato's soul in the *Phaedrus*; third needs purification and falls into **formlessness** (*amorphia*). This corresponds to the trichotomy spirit (*pneumatikon*), soul (*psychikon*, ligamentum between spirit and body, with "wings of an eagle") and body (*sarkikon*). The body, heavy and dark, nonetheless holds the divine seed of the third sonship, "still unconscious and formless," awakened and made capable of ascension (*anadrome*) by Jesus because the opposites were separated in him through the Passion (his division into four; footnote on the Valentinian *Horos*/Cross as boundary-fixer, emancipator, redeemer, who formed Sophia). [pp.82-83, par. 118]
- **Three trichotomies** (Jung's table, three columns I, II, III; read row by row): [p.84, par. 118]

| Sonship (col. I) | Christ-figure (col. II) | Human part (col. III) |
|---|---|---|
| First sonship | Christ of the Ogdoad (son of the highest archon of the ogdoad, the demiurge Yahweh) | Spirit |
| Second sonship | Christ of the Hebdomad (son of the highest archon of the hebdomad) | Soul |
| Third sonship | Jesus the Son of Mary | Body |

  Jesus is "the spiritual inner man," a complete trichotomy in himself, his predecessors being the second Christ and, as first prefiguration, the Christ of the ogdoad.
- **Formlessness = unconsciousness.** The *amorphia* where the third sonship lies hidden is practically "unconsciousness"; Quispel connects *agnosia* (Epiphanius) and *anoeton* (Hippolytus), best translated "unconscious": the initial potentiality of unconscious contents, Basilides' "non-existent, many-formed, and all-empowering seed of the world." (Irenaeus: "outward and inward" refer "not to place, but to what is known and what is not known.") Jung objects to Quispel's "projection" reading: "projection does not do away with the reality of a psychic content ... **Psyche is reality par excellence.**" [pp.84-85, par. 119 and n.92]
- **Matter's numinosity.** The third sonship parallels the medieval *filius philosophorum* and *filius macrocosmi* (world-soul slumbering in matter). In Basilides the body "acquires a special and unexpected significance," a third of the Godhead lodged in materiality: an anticipation of the "mystic" significance matter later takes in alchemy and natural science. Jesus corresponds to the third sonship and is the prototype of the "awakener": the opposites were separated in him and became conscious, while in the latent seed they remain unconscious. The seed in unconscious humanity is awakened by light emanating from Jesus to a similar discrimination of opposites; this matches the fact that the archetypal image of the self appears in dreams of people with no such conscious concepts. [pp.84-85, par. 120]

### Closing reflections of the chapter [pp.85-89, pars. 121-126]
- **Standpoint (par. 121-122):** the parallel between Christ and the self is "psychological" only, "just as the parallel with the fish is mythological"; no intrusion into metaphysics or faith. God- and Christ-images are anthropomorphic and so open to psychological elucidation. The fish symbol and the lapis philosophorum each served as a parallel illuminating Christ; both disappeared; statements about the stone show such importance "that one begins to wonder whether, in the end, it was Christ who was taken as a symbol of the stone rather than the other way round." This links to John and Paul in including Christ in "immediate inner experience" as total man. **The question:** "Is the self a symbol of Christ, or is Christ a symbol of the self?" [pp.85-86, pars. 121-122]
- **Jung's answer (par. 123):** "I have affirmed the latter." He compares his method to an art historian tracing influences on a Christ-image; the archetype differs only in referring "to a living and ubiquitous psychic fact," tempting one to value the living archetype above the historical Christ (as some alchemists did the lapis). He denies "missionary intentions" and says he deals with "proven scientific facts," not confessions. **Perfection vs. completeness:** the Christ-image is as good as perfect; the archetype denotes completeness but is far from perfect; it is a paradox, "something indescribable and transcendental." Realizing the self leads to "a real suspension between opposites (reminiscent of the crucified Christ hanging between two thieves)," an approximate wholeness lacking perfection. [pp.86-87, par. 123]
- **Teleiosis:** striving for perfection (*teleiosis*) is legitimate and inborn, "one of [civilization's] strongest roots," able to become a passion; but where the archetype predominates "completeness is forced upon us against all our conscious strivings"; one may strive for perfection (Matt. 5:48) yet "must suffer from the opposite of his intentions for the sake of his completeness" (Rom. 7:21). [p.87, par. 123]
- **Christ as perfect man who is crucified (par. 124):** truest picture of the goal of ethical endeavor; Jung's "transcendental idea of the self" as a working hypothesis cannot match it because it "lacks the character of a revelatory historical event." Like atman and tao in the East, the self is "at least in part a product of cognition, grounded neither on faith nor on metaphysical speculation but on the experience that under certain conditions the unconscious spontaneously brings forth an archetypal symbol of wholeness," which implies universality and numinosity (evidence: history and modern case material, last two papers of Part I). Such images are central because the symbol stands for the **conjunction of opposites**, understandable only as paradox "since a union of opposites can be thought of only as their annihilation." [pp.87-88, par. 124]
- **Individuation as undertaken burden (par. 125):** predominance of the self archetype produces conflict, "that acute state of unredeemedness which comes to an end only with the words 'consummatum est.'" Recognition of the archetype "does not in any way circumvent the Christian mystery; rather, it forcibly creates the psychological preconditions without which 'redemption' would appear meaningless." "Redemption" does not remove a burden never meant to be borne; "Only the 'complete' person knows how unbearable man is to himself." Taking up individuation consciously avoids "repressed individuation" happening negatively: "anyone who is destined to descend into a deep pit had better set about it with all the necessary precautions rather than risk falling into the hole backwards." [p.88, par. 125]
- **World situation (par. 126):** the irreconcilability of opposites in Christian psychology comes from their *moral accentuation*, a legacy of the Old Testament's emphasis on righteousness before the law, lacking in the philosophical religions of India and China. Jung hopes the present divided world be seen via the psychological rule: "when an inner situation is not made conscious, it happens outside, as fate." If the individual remains undivided and unaware of his inner opposite, "the world must perforce act out the conflict and be torn into opposing halves." [pp.88-89, par. 126]

## Chapter VI. The Sign of the Fishes [pp.90-112; pars. 127-149]

*Overview:* Christ shares symbols with the devil; the fish symbol's pre-Christian background and its link to astrology (conjunctions of Saturn and Jupiter in Pisces, the horoscope of Christ's birth); a "double aspect" of the Saviour in Gnostic and apocalyptic texts; the Talmudic prophecy and the medieval chiliastic calculations; Joachim of Flora and the Holy Ghost movement as a "new status" and as a compensation of the Christ/Antichrist gulf; the precession of the equinoxes and the dating of the transition between the two fishes.

### Shared symbols; the fish [pp.90-91, pars. 127-128]
- Christ shares a number of symbols or "allegories" with the devil in patristic exegesis: **lion, snake (coluber), bird (devil = night bird; Christ = nycticorax, night-heron), raven, eagle, fish**; Lucifer ("Morning Star") means Christ as well as the devil. Apart from the snake, the fish is among the oldest. [p.90, par. 127]
- Jung's view of the symbol: not an allegory or a sign but "the best possible way of describing and formulating an object that is not completely knowable" (the creed is a "symbolum"). *Ichthys* as an acrostic ("Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour") is probably a later explanation of an already widespread symbol; the fish has "a long and colorful prehistory": Babylonian fish-god Oannes and fish-skin-clad priests; fish meals in the cult of Derceto-Atargatis; the Abercius inscription; the redeemer-fish of Manu; the Eucharistic fish-feast of the "Thracian riders." In Christian ideas it arises naturally from regeneration in the font where the baptized swim like fishes (Doelger). Augustine's account of the Erythraean Sibyl acrostic is given in a long footnote. [pp.90-91]
- **Astrological source suspected.** Its sudden activation and identification with Christ suggests a second source: astrology (first noted by Friedrich Muenter; also Jeremias). [pp.91-92, par. 128]

### Saturn, Jupiter, Pisces and the horoscope of Christ [pp.92-96, pars. 128-131]
- **Jewish expectation:** a 14th-century Jewish commentary on Daniel (Abarbanel, 1437-1508) expected the Messiah when Jupiter and Saturn conjoin in Pisces ("the house of justice"); Abraham ben Hiyya (d. c. 1136) and Solomon ibn Gabirol reportedly dated the Messiah to 1464. Saturn is "the star of Israel" (Amos 5:26; Sabbath = Saturn's day; the Sabaeans' "god of the Jews"); Jupiter means the king of justice. Pisces is Jupiter's house; territories include Palestine. [pp.92-93, par. 128] (Jung's footnote corrects: the actual conjunction was in Sagittarius.)
- **Saturn as the Jewish god and as the devil's seat:** medieval astrology made Saturn the abode of the devil; Saturn and the Gnostic demiurge Ialdabaoth have lion faces; the archon Michael with lion shape stands for Ialdabaoth = Saturn (Origen on Celsus); the Naassene demiurge is a "fiery god, the fourth by number"; Apelles: a "third god who spoke to Moses, a fiery one" and a fourth, author of evil. Hence the link of these with Yahweh the demiurge. [p.93, par. 128]
- **Saturn "maleficus":** dragons, serpents, scorpions, vipers, foxes, cats and mice, night birds and other "sly breeds" are Saturn's lot (Bouche-Leclercq); so does the **ass**, which makes the ass a theriomorphic form of the Jewish god (rumors that Jews/Christians worship an ass's head: Plutarch, Josephus, Tacitus, Tertullian; the Palatine "mock crucifixion"; Sabaoth the seventh archon has an ass form; the ass sacred to Egyptian Set, earlier an attribute of the sun-god and only later of Apep/evil). [pp.93-94, par. 129]
- **Medieval conjunctions by religion (Albumasar):** Judaism from Jupiter-Saturn, Islam and the Antichrist from other Jupiter conjunctions [garbled in source: the planet glyphs for Islam and Antichrist are lost; the Antichrist glyph looks like Mars; Christianity from Jupiter-Mercury is confirmed in ch. VII n.2]. Jupiter is beneficent (Iranian view: life) and Saturn death; their conjunction therefore "signifies the union of extreme opposites." In **7 B.C.** the conjunction occurred three times in Pisces, the closest on May 29 (planets 0.21 degrees apart); seen heliocentrically it fell near the equinoctial point then between Aries and Pisces (fire and water), with **Mars in opposition**, i.e., "the planet correlated with the instincts" stood hostile, "peculiarly characteristic of Christianity." If the date is right, the sun at Christ's birth would be in Gemini, "a double sign," recalling hostile brothers (Horus and Set; the Egyptian Set is "sacrificed on the slave's post"; Set and Heru-ur sometimes with one body and two heads; Set-Mercury; Rameses II's capital with a district for Sutech where the Jews were said to labor). (Notes list medieval ideal horoscopes of Christ: Virgo (Albumasar, Albertus Magnus), Libra (d'Ailly, Cardan); Kepler on God marking great conjunctions.) [pp.94-96, par. 130]
- **Pistis Sophia double Christ:** Mary tells Jesus of a "spirit" resembling him that came to her while he was a child; she bound it to the bed; when she found Jesus in the vineyard he embraced and kissed it and "you became one." Jung: Jesus is "the truth sprouting from the earth," the spirit "justice looking down from heaven": "a double personality, part of which rises up from the chaos or hyle, while the other part descends as pneuma from heaven." The Gnostic "discrimination of the natures" is vividly exemplified by the astrological determination of time, whose statements all point to the "prominent double aspect" of the birth at that moment. [pp.96-97, pars. 131-133] (Note: "Saviour of the twins" in Pistis Sophia.) [p.97 n.44]

### The Talmudic prophecy and the precession [pp.97-100, pars. 133-137]
- **Talmud (Sanhedrin 97b):** "four thousand two hundred and ninety-one years after the Creation [A.D. 530], the world will be orphaned. There will follow the war of the tanninim [sea-monsters], the war of Gog and Magog, and then the Messianic era," the world renewed after 7,000 (or 5,000) years. Rashi explains the *tanninim* as **fishes**. Jung stresses (1) it takes the battle of the fishes as an eschatological event, like Behemoth and Leviathan, and (2) it is probably the oldest testimony to the **antithetical nature of the Fishes.** An 11th-century apocryphal "Johannine Genesis" mentions two fishes in clear astrological form; both documents fall into "the critical epoch that opened with the second millennium" (to be discussed). Footnote on Gog/Magog as antithetical pair (Augustine: *tectum* "roof/house" and *de tecto* "he who comes out of the house"; occultatio/detectio). [pp.97-98, par. 133]
- **Gemini 531:** a conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in Gemini in 531 is another pair of brothers (Dioscuri; Apollo/Heracles or Apollo/Dionysus), quartile to the 7 B.C. conjunction; the motif of the brothers occurs early with Jewish Christians and Ebionites. Jung conjectures the Talmudic prophecy rests on astrological premises. [p.99, pars. 134-135]
- **Precession of the equinoxes:** known to ancient astrologers (Origen uses Hipparchus's discovery against astrology based on actual constellations (*morphomata*) versus the fictive signs (*zodia noeta*)). If 7,000 years is anno mundi 7000 the year is A.D. 3239, when the spring-point will have moved 18 degrees into Aquarius; Pierre d'Ailly and Cardan reckoned first and tenth great conjunctions (5027 B.C.; A.D. 3613), with the correct interval about 795 years. (Notes: precession constant 50.3708 seconds a year; total cycle 25,725.6 years; Platonic year earlier 36,000 years.) [pp.99-100, par. 136]

### Joachim of Flora and the Holy Ghost movement [pp.100-105, pars. 137-144]
- **5,000 years gives A.D. 1239**, an epoch of spiritual instability, revolutionary heresies, chiliasm, and the founding of the mendicant orders. **Joachim of Flora** (d. 1202; condemned in 1215): the opening of the seventh seal, the "everlasting gospel," the reign of the "intellectus spiritualis," the age of the Holy Ghost, a third aeon begun with St. Benedict; his follower Gerard of Borgo San Donnino proclaimed that in 1260 Joachim's treatises would replace the gospel of Christ. (Note: great Jupiter-Saturn conjunction in Libra, 1246; Libra a double air sign.) [pp.100-101, par. 137]
- **Pierre d'Ailly's view:** about 1189 the Saturn revolutions were completed; he laments the condemnation of Joachim and of Amalric of Bene and sees the mendicant orders as great and wonderful. Monte Cassino (founded near 529-530) is close to the Talmud's critical year 530. Joachim saw a new "status" beginning secretly as a latent preliminary stage before flower and fruit; "Everyone felt the rushing wind of the pneuma"; new ideas blazed abroad by Cathars, Patarenes, Waldenses, Beghards, Brethren of the Free Spirit and others (visible beginnings in the early years of the eleventh century, per the text). [pp.101-102, pars. 138-139]
- **The extremist creed (Hahn, quoted):** "Item, they believe themselves to be God by nature ... eternal ... have no need of God or the Godhead ... constitute the kingdom of heaven ... are immutable in the new rock ... that a man is bound to follow his inner instinct rather than the truth of the Gospel ... the Gospel contains poetical matters which are not true." [p.102, par. 139]
- **Jung's reading (par. 140):** people who "identified themselves (or were identified) with God, who deemed themselves supermen ... followed the promptings of the inner man, and understood the kingdom of heaven to be within." In a sense "modern," but with "a religious inflation instead of the rationalistic and political psychosis that is the affliction of our day." Joachim should not be charged with such ideas. The idea of supplanting the second Person with the third is so heretical that he must have felt "supported and swept along by the revolutionary currents." The numinosity was heightened by the **synchronicity** of the epoch with the beginning of the sphere of the "antichristian" fish in Pisces; the Holy Ghost movement could be viewed as an expression of the antichristian psychology then dawning. [pp.102-103, par. 140]
- **Joachim seized by an archetype (par. 141):** Just as Joachim thought the status of the Holy Ghost began secretly with Benedict, "a new status was secretly anticipated in Joachim himself"; Joachim "could have been seized by the archetype of the spirit," understood by him only dogmatically as the third Person, not as the empirical archetype, which "was originally an ambivalent dualistic figure that broke through again in the alchemical concept of spirit after engendering the most contradictory manifestations within the Holy Ghost movement." Since the age coincided with the beginning of the second Fish, it was "forced into ambiguity." Jung supposes Joachim "unwittingly ushered in a new 'status,' a religious attitude that was destined to bridge and compensate the frightful gulf that had opened out between Christ and Antichrist in the eleventh century." [pp.103-104, par. 141]
- **Spirit degenerating; the hidden devil:** "The antichristian era is to blame that the spirit became non-spiritual and that the vitalizing archetype gradually degenerated into rationalism, intellectualism, and doctrinairism." The old Trinity formula lacks the devil, who lives in the fringes of theology as the *mysterium iniquitatis*; "the less he is recognized the more dangerous he is. Who would suspect him under those high-sounding names of his, such as public welfare, lifelong security, peace among the nations, etc.? He hides under idealisms, under -isms in general, and of these the most pernicious is doctrinairism." One must return to the "original, living spirit which, because of its ambivalence, is also a mediator and uniter of opposites." [p.104, par. 141]
- **Aquarius and the union of opposites (par. 142):** if the aeon of the fishes is ruled by the motif of **hostile brothers**, the next Platonic month, Aquarius, will constellate the problem of the union of opposites. "It will then no longer be possible to write off evil as the mere privation of good; its real existence will have to be recognized." The problem can be solved "neither by philosophy, nor by economics, nor by politics, but only by the individual human being, via his experience of the living spirit." The declaration of the Assumptio Mariae (1950) is an example of symbol development: the impulse came not from the ecclesiastical authorities but from the Catholic masses, the "urge of the archetype to realize itself." [p.105, par. 142]
- **Repercussions (par. 143):** Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas (an adept in alchemy), Roger Bacon, Meister Eckhart; the Holy Ghost movement as forerunner of the Reformation; and the beginnings of Latin alchemy in the 12th to 13th centuries. The "immutability in the new rock" resembles the central idea of philosophical alchemy, the **lapis philosophorum**, a parallel to Christ the rock/stone/cornerstone (Priscillian; the water from the rock = the solvent aqua permanens; Naassenes call Adam the "rock" and "cornerstone"; sacred rock with four parts; Khunrath's "Petroleum sapientum"; 1 Cor. 10:4 and 1 Pet. 2:4). [pp.105-106, par. 143]
- **The new rock (par. 144):** the new rock takes the place of Christ as the everlasting gospel was to replace his message: through the indwelling Holy Ghost "sonship" is infused into every individual, who becomes "a new rock," "living stones" (1 Pet. 2:5), a logical development of the Paraclete and filiation teaching (Luke 6:35; John 10:34 "ye are gods"); the Naassenes anticipated "a whole tract of historical development," via monasticism to the Holy Ghost movement, via the *Theologia Germanica* to Luther, and via alchemy to modern science. [pp.106-107, par. 144]

### The fish in the Gospels and the astrological parallel [pp.107-112, pars. 145-149]
- **Christ as fish:** fish symbol first appears in Alexandria around A.D. 200 (Doelger); baptismal bath as *piscina*; "fishers of men" (Matt. 4:19), the miraculous draught (Luke 5:10); notes on Abercius (Attis, "holy shepherd") and the Pectorios inscription. [p.107, par. 145]
- **Astrology behind the Gospel stories:** Matthew 2:1ff. (the Magi) proves that Christ was seen astrologically from early on; Boll on glimpses of astrological mythology behind Christ's life and the Apocalypse (Drews's treatment is called "monomaniacal"). [pp.107-108, par. 146]
- **The aeon-pair of Fish and Ram (par. 147):** the fish symbolism shows Christ and believers as fishes, fish as the Agape food, baptism as fish-pond. For those acquainted with astrology, since Christ was regarded as the new aeon "he was born as the first fish of the Pisces era, and was doomed to die as the last ram (*arnion*, lamb) of the declining Aries era." Matthew 27:15ff preserves the old sacrifice of the seasonal god, with a partner named Barabbas ("son of the father"). [p.108]
- **The polarity of the two fishes (par. 147):** the zodiacal sign often shows two fishes moving in opposite directions, but Jung finds no pre-Christian or contemporary picture to prove this: the Farnese Atlas (north fish vertical, south fish horizontal, naturalistic); the Denderah zodiac (both same direction); Timochares (one fish); imperial coins, gems, and Mithraic monuments show both. The later polarity may stem from the real constellation in which the two fish are almost at right angles and form a cross; "This counter-movement ... was much emphasized in Christian times, and this leads one to suspect a certain tendentiousness." (Footnote: Lambspringk's two fishes as opposites to be united.) [pp.109-110, par. 147]
- **Synchronicity statement (par. 148):** no connection can be proved between Christ and the inception of the astrological age, but "the simultaneity of the fish symbolism of the Redeemer with the astrological symbol of the new aeon" is worth emphasis. It shows an archetype manifesting "on the one hand in a personality, and on the other hand synchronistically, in a moment of time determined in advance"; "long before that, the archetype had been written in the heavens by projection, so as then, 'when the time was fulfilled,' to coincide with the symbols produced by the new era." The fish belongs to the winter rainy season (with Aquarius and Capricorn); striking only because the spring-point moved into it and inaugurated an age in which "the 'fish' was used as a name for the God who became a man, who was born as a fish and was sacrificed as a ram, who had fishermen for disciples ... who fed the multitude with miraculously multiplying fishes, who was himself eaten as a fish, the 'holier food,' and whose followers are little fishes, the 'pisciculi.'" [p.110, par. 148]
- **Caution (pars. 148-149):** the synoptic eyewitness stories give no evidence of disguised astrological myths; they seem "'Just So' stories"; the symbolism might have arisen fortuitously, and the age of the fishes left little trace in Eastern cultures (Jung admits ignorance of Indian and Chinese astrology). Still, "the fact that the traditional fish symbolism makes possible a verifiable prediction that had already been made in the New Testament is a somewhat uncomfortable proposition to swallow." [p.111]
- **The two fishes and the dating of the enantiodromia (par. 149):** the northerly (easterly) fish was entered by the spring-point about the beginning of our era; the two are joined by the **commissure.** The point where the ecliptic meets the meridian at the tail of the second fish coincides roughly with the 16th century, "the time of the Reformation." The spring-point will enter Aquarius in the course of the third millennium (footnote: depending on the starting star, between A.D. 1997 and 2154; Platonic month of 2,143 years). Christ is identified with the first (vertical) fish, "followed by the Antichrist, at the end of time"; the enantiodromia begins "logically ... midway between the two fishes"; the Renaissance begins near the second fish "and with it comes that spirit which culminates in the modern age." [pp.111-112, par. 149]

## Chapter VII. The Prophecies of Nostradamus [pp.113-120; pars. 150-160]

*Overview:* a short chapter on how Nostradamus's astrological prophecies of an antichristian reversal (1792) rest on medieval conjunction theory; the North as the region of evil; the Antichrist as a figure of folklore and of Reformation polemic.

- **Thesis:** the course of religious history and an essential part of psychic development "could have been predicted more or less accurately, both as regards time and content, from the precession of the equinoxes through the constellation of Pisces," and was; it coincides with the 16th-century schism. After that "an enantiodromian process set in" as a "horizontal movement outwards, namely the voyages of discovery and the conquest of Nature"; the vertical "Gothic" striving was cut across by the horizontal, and development "grew more and more obviously antichristian," leading to the crisis of Western civilization. [p.113, par. 150]
- **Nostradamus's letter to Henry II (June 27, 1558):** a year marked by Jupiter-Mars conjunction with Mercury-quartile, then "a greater persecution against the Christian Church than ever was in Africa," in 1792, "everyone will think it a renovation of the age"; "the infernal power shall rise against the Church of Jesus Christ. This shall be the **second Antichrist**" working through temporal kings seduced by tongues "more sharp than any sword in the hands of a madman"; persecution begins with the Northern kings joined by Eastern ones, lasts eleven years, the chief Northern king to fail; a "united Southern king" outlasts him by three years; paganism returns, the Bible burned, a bloodbath for Latin countries. (Jung's note: the old tradition makes Jupiter-Mercury characteristic of Christianity, and the Mars-Mercury quartile an attack by Islam; "one never reflects that the opponent of Christianity dwells in the European unconscious.") [pp.113-114, pars. 151-152]
- **Historical determinants (par. 153-154):** Pierre d'Ailly (after Albumasar), on the eighth *coniunctio maxima* calculated for 1693 and ten revolutions of Saturn fulfilled in 1789 (97 years later): many marvelous changes in laws and "sects," so probably the Antichrist comes "with his law and his damnable sects," after the sect of Mahomet "none other will come save the law of the Antichrist." Pierre d'Ailly later criticized the calculations as "false deduction" and held that only superstitions and heresies, and especially the Antichrist, are astrologically influenced, not Christianity (against Roger Bacon's Mercury theory). [pp.114-116, pars. 153-154]
- **French Revolution (pars. 155-156):** Nostradamus's 1792 is an "improvement on 1789"; the enthronement of the "Déesse Raison" as anticipation of the antichristian trend; "renovation of the age" coincides with the revolutionary calendar starting September 22, 1792; "in the French Revolution men witnessed the enantiodromia that had set in with the Renaissance and ran parallel with the astrological fish symbol." Saturn periods of 300 years (ten revolutions) as read by d'Ailly: 11 B.C. (Christ), A.D. 289 (Manichaeism), 589 (Islam), 1189 (Innocent III), 1489 (a Church schism), 1789 (Antichrist, by inference): "Fantasy could do the rest, for the archetype had long been ready and was only waiting for the time to be fulfilled." [pp.116-117, par. 156]
- **The usurper from the North (pars. 156-158):** the Antichrist as infernal power is Typhon/Set, "who has his fiery abode in the North"; his power is triadic with a confederate in the East and one in the South, matching the "lower triad." Patristic and medieval tradition makes the north the region of the devil and evil: Jeremiah 1:14 ("From the north shall an evil wind break forth"), Isaiah 14:12ff (Lucifer on the mount in the far north), Eucherius, Rhabanus Maurus ("north wind is the harshness of persecution"), Augustine, Garnerius (the malign spirit Aquilo), Adam Scotus (a dragon's head in the north emitting triple smoke: threefold ignorance of good and evil, true and false, fitting and unfitting), Gregory the Great ("ventus urens"; Ezekiel's fiery cloud from the north). Jung observes the oddity that the prophet's vision of God arrives "wrapped in this devilish smoke"; "Where there is smoke, there is fire." [pp.117-119, pars. 156-158]
- **Antichrist in folklore (pars. 159-160):** before the Reformation the Antichrist was a popular folklore figure (the "Entkrist" editions of the 15th century); Luther was promptly called the Antichrist; Nostradamus's "second Antichrist" may imply the first was Luther, Nero or Mohammed (Nanni). Jung adds that the Nazis claimed Hitler was completing Luther's reformation. By making this prediction Nostradamus "placed himself firmly in the antichristian phase and served as its mouthpiece." [pp.119-120, pars. 159-160]

## Chapter VIII. The Historical Significance of the Fish [pp.121-135; pars. 161-180]

*Overview:* shepherd/ram/lamb imagery from the expiring Aries aeon; the star-crowned woman and the threatened child; the lamb of the Apocalypse as a shadow figure; Jewish Messiah traditions (two Messiahs; Armilus); the problem of the God-image and the Job question; dualistic aspects of the Christ-figure (Monophysite dogma as ego/self); the two Fishes as mother and son; positive and negative meanings of fish symbolism; non-Christian fish traditions (Manu, Jewish eucharistic Leviathan, Hera's fountain, the Abercius inscription); Pisces as twelfth sign, end and beginning.

### Shepherd, ram, lamb; the mother-goddess; the astrological child [pp.121-123, pars. 161-166]
- **Aeon overlap:** besides "pisciculi Christianorum," the shepherd and the lamb are a major Christian allegory: Hermes Kriophoros ("ram-bearer") and Orpheus as prototypes of the Good Shepherd; Hermas's "Shepherd" perhaps derived from the pagan Poimandres (Reitzenstein); like the Abercius fish, probably linked with **Attis.** Shepherd, ram and lamb belong to the expiring Aries aeon; in the first century the two aeons overlap and the two chief mystery gods, Attis and Christ, are both shepherds, rams and fishes. [p.121, pars. 161-162]
- **Heroic threatened childhood:** the massacre of the innocents and flight into Egypt correspond to Revelation 12:1 (woman clothed with the sun, crown of twelve stars, pursued by a dragon): kindred motifs Leto and Python; Aphrodite and her son changed to fishes in the Euphrates; Isis and Horus; the Syrian Greeks identified Derceto-Atargatis and her son Ichthys with the constellation of the Fishes. [pp.121-122, par. 163]
- **The virgin and the Aeon:** the mother-goddess is usually a virgin; the pagan Christmas cry "the virgin has brought forth, the light increases." Epiphanius's account of the Alexandrian Korion festival on the night of January 5/6 (a golden-marked wooden image carried seven times round the temple): "Today, at this hour, the Kore, that is to say the virgin, has given birth to the **Aeon**." Epiphanius cites it as pagans bearing involuntary witness to Christianity. [p.122, pars. 164-165]
- **Virgo** carries a wheat-sheaf or a child and is linked by some to the Apocalypse's "woman." [p.123, par. 166]

### The lamb of the Apocalypse as shadow figure [pp.123-126, pars. 167-168]
- The child who will "pasture the pagans with an iron staff" implies rebirth of the Messiah, "quite impossible in the Christian sphere." The "bizarre" seven-horned, seven-eyed Lamb "cannot possibly be explained in Christian terms" (Boll): a *bellicose* lamb, a conqueror, the "lion of Judah" before whom kings hide; Jung finds "a daemonic ram." He asks whether the author was influenced by "a psychological shadow-figure, an 'umbra Jesu'" united with the triumphant Christ at the end of time through rebirth, which would explain the repeated birth myth and the scant mention of Antichrist in the Apocalypse. The ram is "just about everything that Jesus appears not to be" but is not Antichrist (a creature of Satan); the duplication is instead due to "anti-Roman resentment felt by the Jewish Christians, who fell back on their god of vengeance and his warlike Messiah." [pp.123-124, par. 167]
- **Midrash of the hidden Messiah** (Elias in Bethlehem): a child born when the temple was destroyed lies "like a stone" and is blown into the sea; a bath kol says he will stay in the sea 400 years, 80 in the smoke of Korah's children, 80 under the gates of Rome, then wander great cities until the end. Jung reads: a Messiah wafted by divine intervention into the Beyond (sea = unconscious), threatened from the start, symptomatic of "an extraordinary weakness of the Messianic element in Judaism"; 560 years of latency near the Talmud's 530. [pp.124-125, par. 168]

### Two Messiahs; the God-image and Job [pp.125-129, pars. 168-171]
- **Two Messiahs** (Cabalistic): Messiah ben Joseph (firstling of his bullock; suffering, must die fighting Gog and Magog, killed by **Armilus**, the Anti-Messiah whom Satan begot on a block of marble) and Messiah ben David (rides an ass; slays Armilus, raises ben Joseph). They are compared to Moses and Aaron and to two roes (Song 4:5). Tabari: the Antichrist will be a king of the Jews; in Abarbanel's *Mashmi'a Yeshu'ah* ben Joseph "actually is the Antichrist," i.e., the suffering Messiah ultimately thought of as the antagonist of the victorious one. [pp.125-126, par. 168]
- **Split as polarity:** the Messianic weakness "consists in a split which in the end becomes a complete polarity," prefigured in Persian literature (the *Bahman Yast*'s deteriorating ages; Iron Age as "evil sovereignty of the demons with dishevelled hair"). Jung reads the doubling as an "inner disquiet" about Yahweh's "injustice and unreliability" since Job. Job states the problem plainly and Christianity answered it plainly; Jewish mysticism "went its own way" over depths Christian thinkers covered up. Example: Ibn Ezra's Spanish sage who could not read Psalm 89 (vv. 33ff on God's covenant and "thou hast cast off and rejected") because it saddened him. [p.126, par. 169; pp.126-127]
- **Jung's statement on the God-image (par. 170):** "As the highest value and supreme dominant in the psychic hierarchy, the God-image is immediately related to, or identical with, the self, and everything that happens to the God-image has an effect on the latter. Any uncertainty about the God-image causes a profound uneasiness in the self." The question is ignored for its painfulness but remains asked in the unconscious and is answered by "views and beliefs like materialism, atheism, and similar substitutes, which spread like epidemics." The ersatz "represses the real question into the unconscious and destroys the continuity of historical tradition." Christianity's insistence on God's goodness omits the corollary: "the destruction of the God-image is followed by the annulment of the human personality." Materialistic atheism "forms the religion of all those rationalistic movements which delegate the freedom of personality to the masses and thereby extinguish it." Advocates of Christianity "squander their energies in the mere preservation of what has come down to them, with no thought of building on to their house and making it roomier." [pp.127]
- **Gnostic evidence (par. 171):** Bousset: the duality of the apocalyptic Christ comes from Jewish-Gnostic speculation; Gnostic preoccupation with evil contrasts with the Fathers' "peremptory nullification of it." Valentinus's Christ born "not without a kind of shadow" which he "cast off"; Jung suggests the shadow might be "the Yahwistic law under which Christ was born." Origen's doubt about the devil's final fate shows reunion with God was discussed early; "the theory of the privatio boni does not dispose of the eternity of hell and damnation." [pp.127-128, par. 170]
- **Monophysite dogma psychologically:** since Christ as a man corresponds to the ego and as God to the self, "he is at once both ego and self, part and whole. Empirically speaking, consciousness can never comprehend the whole, but it is probable that the whole is unconsciously present in the ego. This would be equivalent to the highest possible state of *teleiotes* (completeness or perfection)." [pp.128-129, par. 171]

### The two Fishes as mother and son; ambivalence of the fish [pp.129-130, pars. 172-174]
- Christ was "assimilated into a world of ideas that seems far removed from the gospels," pagan, astrologically saturated; educated Christians may have known the 7 B.C. conjunction in Pisces; "The Fishes, however, are a double sign." [p.129, par. 172]
- **Christmas midnight:** with the sun entering Capricorn (old reckoning) Virgo stands on the eastern horizon, followed by the Serpent held by Ophiuchus. The two fishes were also seen as **mother and son**, implying they were once one (Babylonian and Indian astrology know one fish: Piscis Austrinus, Fomalhaut); the Phoenician Derceto-Atargatis, half fish, had a son Ichthys. "The sign of the prophet Jonah" may echo an older hero's night-sea journey, swallowed by a "whale-dragon" and reborn. The name Joshua (Yeshua, Iesous) is linked with the fish: Joshua son of Nun (Nun = "fish"); in the Khidr legend Joshua ben Nun's fish is revived by a drop of the water of life. [p.129, par. 173]
- **Devouring mothers:** "The mythological Great Mothers are usually a danger to their sons" (a lamp showing one fish devouring another; Fomalhaut "the fish's mouth"). Fish stand for every devouring *concupiscentia* ("ambitious, libidinous, voracious, avaricious, lascivious"; Picinellus), through relation to Ishtar/Astarte/Atargatis/Aphrodite, whose planet Venus is exalted in Pisces; yet also claim higher significance (those born under Pisces become fishermen or sailors: "an echo of the primitive totemistic identity between the hunter and his prey"; Oannes the fish culture hero; Christ the "fisher of men par excellence"). Christ is also the hook or bait on God's fishing-rod catching **Leviathan**, death or the devil (the Cyprian/Stephen of Canterbury fish-hook motif: the devil displays the bait of pleasure, the sting of sin lies hid). In Jewish tradition Leviathan is a eucharistic food for the faithful in Paradise, who wear fish-robes. [pp.130-131, par. 174]
- **Positive fish:** Christ is both the fisher and the fish eaten "eucharistically" (Augustine, *Confessions*, "the fish drawn from the deep"; Emmaus meal); the healing fish in **Tobit** (Raphael; the heart and liver for smoke, the gall for blindness). [p.131]

### Fish traditions outside Christianity [pp.131-135, pars. 175-180]
- **Monks as fishes** (Peter Damian; "cloister is a fishpond of souls"); the Pectorios inscription: believers as "divine descendants of the heavenly fish"; Clement's Alexandrian hymn "Fisher of men"; Tertullian, "we little fishes ... are born in water." [p.131, par. 175]
- **Manu's fish (par. 176):** Vishnu as a goldfish saves Manu from the flood; ritual of the golden fish on the twelfth day of the first Indian month. Indian origin of the Christian fish is not impossible but "Western fish symbolism is so rich and at the same time so archaic that we may safely regard it as autochthonous." [pp.131-132, par. 176]
- **Pisces as mother-and-son and end/beginning (par. 177):** mother and son imply "the mythological tragedy of the son's early death and resurrection." As twelfth sign it denotes the end of the astrological year and a new beginning, coinciding with Christianity's claim to be beginning and end of all things (Origen on ages). Six components of the Christian myth are already in the astrological fish: **(1) the cross, (2) the moral conflict split into Christ and Antichrist, (3) the son of a virgin, (4) the classical mother-son tragedy, (5) danger at birth, (6) the saviour and bringer of healing.** Jung adds that no old text derives the Christian fish from the zodiac, astrological evidence to the 2nd century does not stress polarity, and Doelger rightly stresses the Ichthys was always *one* fish; "There are, in short, no grounds whatever for supposing that the zodion of the Fishes could have served as the Ichthys prototype." [pp.132-133, par. 177]
- **Jewish material (Scheftelowitz) (par. 178):** pagan and Jewish fish symbolism plays a far greater role: the "chalice of benediction" decorated with fishes, fish as the food of the blessed in Paradise and a sepulchral symbol; devout Israelites who live "in the water of the doctrine" likened to fishes (c. A.D. 100); the fish is also Messianic (Apocalypse of Baruch: Leviathan rises with the Messiah; Behemoth also eucharistic food, "assiduously overlooked"; II Esdras's "man coming up from the midst of the sea"). Footnote: dove-and-fish cult in Syria; Dagon from *dag*, fish. [pp.133-134, par. 178]
- **Abercius's "very great fish from the fountain":** corresponds to the one fish of Hera's fountain in a Sassanid debate (5th c.), caught by the "hook of divinity" feeding the world; the fountain = Mary (*pege*) in Christian/Gnostic usage (Synesius hymn "Fountain of fountains, source of sources ... monad of monads"); a Boeotian vase shows the "lady of the beasts" with a fish between her legs, presumably her son. But in Christian symbology the crucifix is the hook for Leviathan, who is "death or the devil," not the Messiah. In Jewish tradition Leviathan's flesh is the *pharmakon athanasias* ("Messianic fish"); the Talmud: the Messiah comes "when a fish is sought for an invalid and cannot be procured." Yahweh's two monsters Behemoth and Leviathan "seem to represent a pair of opposites," land and water; Maimonides (via Kirchmaier): Leviathan possesses "a combination of bodily peculiarities found separate in different animals" (Jung: an archetype "complexum generalem" of "the spirit of gravity"). [p.134]
- **Star and Messiah (pars. 179-180):** the birth of an outstanding human has long been identified with a star rising (Num. 24:17); Zohar: the fish that swallowed Jonah died, revived after three days and spewed him out, "Through the fish we shall find a medicament for the whole world." Scheftelowitz holds the Abercius fish is Leviathan, the largest and "pure" fish; also "the one and only fish" in "Happenings in Persia." [p.135, pars. 179-180]

## Chapter IX. The Ambivalence of the Fish Symbol [chapter begins at p.136, pars. 181-184 fall in this part; the chapter continues in Part 2]

- **Baruch's twelfth part (par. 181):** per the Syrian Apocalypse of Baruch (29:1ff) the time before the Messiah falls into twelve parts and he appears in the twelfth; twelve points to the zodia, the twelfth being Pisces; then Leviathan rises, and "the two great sea monsters" (Behemoth is not aquatic, so they are a duplication of Leviathan: male and female; the female already killed and salted by Yahweh for the end, the male castrated) become food for the survivors. Isaiah 27:1 likewise doubles Leviathan ("the piercing serpent ... the crooked serpent ... the dragon in the sea"), which gave rise in medieval alchemy to two serpents fighting each other, one winged, one wingless, "a typical pair of opposites" (cf. I Ching hexagram 2, line 6). In Job only the singular Leviathan appears, with the polarity coming out in Behemoth. Meir ben Isaac's poem: the two monsters wound each other to death at the end; Yahweh cuts them up as food for the devout, probably connected with the Passover in the month of Adar, "the fish." [pp.136-137, par. 181]
- **Leviathan at Ugarit (par. 182):** originally one Leviathan, attested in Ras Shamra texts (c. 2000 B.C.): "When thou smitest Ltn, the serpent brh, thou wilt finish the serpent 'qltn, the mighty one with seven heads"; adjectives match those of Isaiah 27:1; pictures show a fight between **Baal and the serpent Ltn**, a conflict of god and monster, not of two monsters as later. [p.137, par. 182]
- **The splitting of the great "fish" (pars. 183-184):** the monster "gradually split into its opposite, after having itself been the opposite of the highest God and hence his shadow, the embodiment of his evil side." After the split, its opposition to God recedes; it fights itself or an equivalent monster (Leviathan vs. Behemoth). "This relieves God of his own inner conflict, which now appears outside him in the form of a hostile pair of brother monsters." In later Jewish tradition Leviathan tends to become "pure" and be eaten as eucharistic food, so that if one derives the Ichthys from this source Christ-as-fish would stand in the place of Leviathan, "the monstrous animals of tradition having meanwhile faded into mere attributes of death and the devil." (Footnote: Maimonides on Leviathan as super-animal, "just as Yahweh is a kind of superman.") [pp.137-138, pars. 183-184] The paragraph that begins "This split corresponds to the doubling of the shadow often met with in dreams..." (par. 185) starts at the foot of the range and continues in Part 2.

*Cross-references in this part:* the doubling and the shadow in dreams (par. 185) continue in chapter IX in the next part; Joachim/Holy Ghost material anticipates chapter XIV; the Gnostic material of this part (Basilides, Valentinus, Naassenes) is developed in ch. XIII; "pars. 225ff." refers forward to the Johannine Genesis; "pars. 298ff." to the *anoeton* concept and "pars. 358ff." to the Naassene quaternio.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Not extracted in detail:** the large apparatus of footnotes (Latin and Greek originals of patristic and rabbinic quotations, Migne volume and column references, Talmud and midrash citations, bibliography pointers) is compressed to what bears on the argument; where a footnote adds a distinct claim (for example the three elements of the syzygy, Origen on opposites, Jung's reply to Victor White, the Eubulides fallacy, the precession figures) it is included. Front matter (title pages, colophon, editors' notes, list of plates) is summarized only.
- **Paragraph numbers:** the PDF text prints paragraph numbers in the margin with frequent OCR damage (for example "»3" for 13, "2g" for 28); all paragraph numbers given were cross-checked against the EPUB copy and are reliable. Where a paragraph spans two pages both pages are cited.
- **Garbled passages:** planet and zodiac glyphs are lost in both the PDF text and the EPUB (see the Jupiter conjunction list in par. 130: the glyphs for Islam and the Antichrist are not legible; Christianity from Jupiter-Mercury is confirmed in ch. VII n. 2); some Greek words are OCR-damaged in the PDF text and were given in transliteration from context. No passage of argument was unreadable.
- **Basilides table (p.84):** the three-column layout is reconstructed from a damaged printing; the content (sonships, Christs of ogdoad and hebdomad, Jesus; spirit, soul, body) is consistent with the surrounding text.
- **Chapter IX is cut mid-chapter:** the range ends at the foot of PDF p.137 (par. 184 ends on p.138 with its last two lines); par. 185 (doubling of the shadow in dreams) and the rest of chapter IX are in Part 2.
- **Statements on gender and culture** (anima/animus, "primitive," Eros/Logos, remarks on Islam, Judaism and the East) are recorded as Jung states them, in his terms, without evaluation; they are period-bound formulations by the author and should be read as such when reused.
- **Jung's astronomy and history:** numerical claims (conjunction dates, the precession cycle, the "230 degrees above absolute zero" temperature, medieval dating) are reported as printed; Jung himself flags the arbitrary delimitation of the constellations and notes that Abarbanel's date for the conjunction is legendary.
