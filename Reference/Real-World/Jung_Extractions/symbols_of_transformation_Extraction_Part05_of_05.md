## Overview of this range

Part 5 is the reference matter of a very large book, but the notes are not mere citations. Jung's 1952 revision left the 1912 text largely intact and used the notes (his own, plus bracketed editors' notes) to say where his later thinking diverges: *imago* becomes **archetype**; "libido" is defended as a general psychic energy and not simply sexual; "incest" is redefined as the urge to return to childhood; the "unconscious" becomes the **collective** unconscious; the **shadow**, **anima/animus**, **self** and **mandala** are named as what the early text was circling; the God-image is declared a psychic fact that neither affirms nor denies a metaphysical God. The notes also store most of the **comparative material** (myths, rites, etymologies, poems, case fragments) behind the book's method of **amplification**: placing one fantasy beside parallel images from many cultures until its typical (archetypal) meaning shows. The index, read as a whole, shows the book's center of gravity: *libido, regression, rebirth, the mother (Terrible Mother, mother-imago), the hero, the snake, the sun, the tree, sacrifice, the self, symbol-formation, the unconscious (collective, personal)*.

### Block B: "Two Kinds of Thinking" [pp.1122-1131, notes 1-46]

Jung's notes set out the psychology of **directed** (logical, adaptive, verbal) versus **non-directed** (fantasy, dreamlike) thinking, with supporting authorities.

- **Definition of directed thinking.** Subordination to a ruling idea; Külpe's "anticipatory apperception" which "checks or suppresses" every idea outside its circle. James: the technical mark of reasoning is the ability to deal with *novel* data (adaptation). Wolff: thinking is an act of the soul by which it becomes conscious of itself and of things outside it. [p.1122]
- **Language and reason.** Nietzsche: thoughts are "shadows of our feelings, always darker, emptier, and simpler." Hamann equates reason with language; Mauthner holds there is no thought without speech and denounces "word fetishism" in science. [p.1122-1123]
- **Subjectivity of symbols.** Jung's small son called everything he wanted to take or eat "stô lôl" ("leave it!"), showing how a sound-symbol arises wholly from the subject. [p.1123]
- **Old word-meanings persist.** Hermann Paul: "Everything that has ever been in consciousness remains as an active element in the unconscious"; old meanings keep acting from "that dark chamber of the unconscious." Jung says the seductive influence of primitive word-meanings on thought is hard to overestimate. Eberschweiler's experiments (done at Jung's request) showed that during association tests even the inner association is influenced by phonetic features. [pp.1123-1124]
- **Why antiquity lacked technology** (note 15): no external compulsion, because cheap slaves removed the labor question; and the ancients' interest was turned toward reverence for the divine cosmos, a quality "entirely lacking" in a technological age. [p.1124]
- **No purposeless thought.** Quoting Freud: what we abandon when we let involuntary ideas arise is only *known* purposive ideas; unknown ("unconscious") purposive ideas then take charge. [p.1124-1125]
- **Fantasy-thinking, introversion** (note 19, a long note). Indefinite thinking is far from reflection. In experiments, cultivated subjects let to reverie showed measurable affects yet could hardly or not at all express the underlying thought. Pathological cases are more instructive, above all the **introversion neuroses and psychoses** (Bleuler's schizophrenic group): "introversion," which Jung says he "cursorily introduced in 1910" in "Psychic Conflicts in a Child," leads to an isolated inner life. There one meets "supralinguistic" fantasy-thinking that moves in "inexpressible" images and feelings; a highly intelligent patient said she knew what it was all about but could not find words. [pp.1125-1126]
- **Fantasy not simply unproductive** (note 20, a correction of James). Fantasy-thinking is unadapted and practically useless, but "in the long run the play of fantasy uncovers creative forces and contents, just as dreams do," which as a rule can be realized only through passive, associative and fantasy-thinking. [p.1126]
- **Petrarch on Mt. Ventoux** (via Burckhardt): at the summit he opened Augustine's *Confessions* at the passage on men who admire mountains and seas and "turn away from themselves," then closed the book in silence. [pp.1126-1127]
- **Scholasticism** (Wundt): value on a fixed conceptual scheme and on verbal symbols, so that analysis of word meaning replaces investigation of facts. [p.1127]
- **Dream-thinking as regression** (notes 23-26). Freud on regressive dream methods; Jung adds that psychoses confirm this and quotes Pierre Janet's independent view: a function has a firmly organized "inferior" part and a "superior" part under continuous adaptive transformation; neuroses are disturbances of the *superior* part, replaced by exaggerated "inferior" operations (emotionality, visceral disturbance). Claparède: hysteria as a "tendency to reversion," an atavistic reaction. [pp.1127-1128]
- **Child's archaic thinking** (Abraham's story): a girl of three and a half, newly with a baby brother, said to her mother, "You are two Mamas. You are my Mama, and your breast is little brother's Mama." Jung notes Latin *mamma* = breast. [p.1128]
- Later notes on autoeroticism, the hero myth (Rank, Abraham, Kerényi's "Primordial Child"), the **preconscious** as creative source (Schelling, Fichte), complexes and automatisms (Flournoy's *From India to the Planet Mars*; Schreber's *Memoirs*), and the Judas-figure as the one who sacrifices the Lamb of God and in doing so sacrifices himself (suicide), to be developed in Part II. [pp.1129-1130]
- **Christ as myth** (note 44): Drews and the theologian Kalthoff argue that the Gospel documents do not permit a biography of an historical Jesus and that the "God-man" has superhuman features at every point; see also Schweitzer. [p.1130-1131]
- **Burckhardt on *Faust*** (note 45, letter of 1855): *Faust* is "a genuine myth, i.e., a great primordial image, in which every man has to discover his own being and destiny in his own way"; the Greeks had an "Oedipus chord" waiting to be directly touched; a commentator between Greeks and Oedipus would have been absurd. [p.1131]
- Note 46 points the reader to the Appendix for the full Miller account in English. [p.1131]

### Block C: Early "Miller Fantasies" chapter [pp.1132-1133, notes 1-3]

- **Nietzsche's toad dream** (as told by Bernoulli): at a dinner Nietzsche told his neighbor that his hand on the table turned to glass and a fat toad sat on it, and he felt an irresistible compulsion to swallow it, which he did, overcoming loathing; her laughter hurt him. Bernoulli also notes Nietzsche's meticulous dress as a "fear of defilement." Jung's reading: Nietzsche, at the age when others think of marriage, was telling a young woman of a disgusting thing he must take wholly into his body; "we know what disease caused the premature ending of Nietzsche's life." [p.1132]
- Two citations from Rostand's *Cyrano de Bergerac*. [p.1133]

### Block D: "The Hymn of Creation" and the God-image [pp.1134-1148, notes 1-73]

This block holds some of the clearest statements of Jung's later positions.

- **Choice of words is always significant** (note 1; editors note the English wording may differ from Flournoy's French). [p.1134]
- **Dementia praecox / schizophrenia and the psychiatrist's bias** (note 2). Kraepelin's name "dementia praecox," Bleuler's "schizophrenia": the illness had the misfortune to be discovered by psychiatrists, so its prognosis looks hopeless because they see only the worst institutionalized cases and are therapeutically helpless. Analogy: tuberculosis described only from a Home for Incurables; chronic hysteria that degenerates in asylums does not characterize real hysteria. "Latent psychosis" is an idea the psychotherapist "knows and fears." Note 3 adds that the lines predate the First World War and "much has changed." [p.1134]
- **Imago versus complex, then archetype** (note 4). Jung "purposely" prefers *imago* to *complex* to stress that the factor has "a living independence in the psychic hierarchy" (autonomy, the essential feature of feeling-toned complexes). Critics called this a return to medieval psychology; he says it was deliberate, since the psychology of ancient and modern superstition supports it, and cites Schreber and Spitteler's *Imago* and the Roman *imagines et lares*. "In my later writings, I use the term 'archetype' instead, in order to bring out the fact that we are dealing with impersonal, collective forces." [pp.1134-1135]
- **The father-imago** (note 5): the idea that the masculine deity derives from the father-imago "need be taken literally only within the limits of a personalistic psychology"; closer study shows collective components in the father-imago from the start that cannot be reduced to personal experience. [p.1135]
- Voice and name as sun and moon (Hippolytus); Om (Max Müller); the *Hermetica* (Lib. I, 4); the laughter of God making seven gods (magical papyrus); Anaxagoras's *nous* as moving power; a patient's incest fantasy of her father blowing into her mouth (inspiration). [pp.1135-1136]
- **Analysis works backward; the unconscious also anticipates** (note 18, introduced with an expected "charge of mysticism"). The unconscious contains combinations below the threshold; analysis dissolves them into historical determinants, working backward like history. But "tomorrow is already contained in today," so a deeper knowledge of the present could allow "a moderately far-sighted prognosis." Forward-pointing subliminal combinations are the object not of analysis but of a "refined psychological syntheticism" that would follow "the natural currents of libido." This is rarely achievable consciously but "happens easily enough in the unconscious," and accounts for the prophetic significance of dreams claimed by superstition: "Dreams are very often anticipations of future alterations of consciousness." Note 19: dreams stay in memory just as long as they correctly sum up the individual's psychological situation. [pp.1137-1138]
- **A collective love-song** (note 20, Epirus): a kiss at night is told by the star, the sea, the rudder, the sailor, the neighbors, the priest, the mother and the father, to show how collective the elements of an erotic experience are. [pp.1138-1139]
- **Nerval's *Aurélia* and anima projection** (notes 21-22): the poem's atmosphere anticipates the fate of Miss Miller, "spiritual benightedness"; the raven is a synonym for the *nigredo* in alchemy. Nerval refused to believe an "ordinary woman of this world" could have the glamour his unconscious gave her; "today we know that a powerful impression of this kind is due to the projection of an archetype, i.e., that of the anima or animus." [p.1139]
- **Job** (notes 24-27): the Satan wager, Behemoth and Leviathan (Schärf on the figure of Satan); the long quotation of Job 41:19-29 on Leviathan (fire from the mouth, heart hard as stone). [pp.1139-1140]
- **God as a psychic fact** (note 29, important). "This proposition has caused much offence, because people have failed to see that it is a psychological view and not a metaphysical statement. The psychic fact 'God' is a typical autonomism, a collective archetype... It is therefore characteristic not only of all higher forms of religion, but appears spontaneously in the dreams of individuals." The archetype "is, as such, an unconscious psychic image, but it has a reality independent of the attitude of the conscious mind"; "it should not in itself be confused with the idea of a metaphysical God. The existence of the archetype neither postulates a God, nor does it deny that he exists." [p.1141]
- **Theriomorphic elements** (note 30): lacking in Christianity apart from remnants (dove, fish, lamb, the evangelists' beasts); the raven and lion marked grades of initiation in Mithraism; Dionysus as a bull, so his female worshippers wore horns; female worshippers of Artemis were called "bears." [p.1141]
- **Sin, burden-bearing, love** (notes 32-46): a string of New Testament texts on mutual confession, bearing one another's burdens and God as love, set against Jung's remark (note 38) that projected figures are "not always an illusion, for the subject himself can be the main source of these figures, as is particularly the case in neuroses and psychoses." Note 45 quotes a medieval hymn to *caritas* that drew God from heaven, wounded and bound by love. Note 13 (later block) explains that *caritas*/agape is "an unmistakably psychic function." [pp.1141-1143]
- **A spiritual goal cannot be chosen if unknown** (note 47): "One cannot of one's own free will choose and desire something that one does not know. Hence a spiritual goal cannot consciously be striven for if it does not yet exist." [p.1143]
- **Union with a god through sexual symbolism** (note 50, Reitzenstein): primitive forms of the sacrament of union with God include sexual union, taking in the god's semen, which becomes a sacred act through a human deputy or the phallus. [p.1143]
- **Fate and the mysteries** (notes 51-52). The Mithraic liturgy's "bitter necessity," the high priest of Isis addressing Lucius (changed into an ass, "that ever-rutting animal hateful to Isis," then initiated), and Lucius's prayer to Isis who "unweavest even the inextricably tangled web of fate": the purpose of the mysteries was "to break the 'compulsion of the stars' by magic power." Jung's psychology of fate: "The power of fate makes itself felt unpleasantly only when everything goes against our will, that is to say, when we are no longer in harmony with ourselves." The ancients related *heimarmene* to the "primal fire" (Stoic: all-pervading warmth, the ultimate cause), "a libido-image." Schiller: "The stars of thine own fate lie in thy breast"; Emerson: "A man's fortunes are the fruits of his character." [pp.1143-1145]
- **Augustine and Seneca on happiness and the inward turn** (notes 53-57, 62, 64-65, 73): long quotations. Seneca (letter 41): "God is near you, he is with you, he is within you." - **Mithraism and Christianity compared** (notes 56, 58): both teach ascetic morality and a morality of action; Cumont: Mithraism succeeded through a morality favoring action; its members formed a "sacred army" against evil and included *virgines* and *continentes*. [p.1146]
- **A note retained from the 1912 text** (note 59): the passages on social security are left in as typifying "the false fin de siècle sense of security," since "we have experienced abominations of desolation of which Rome never dreamed." On Rome: the vast proportion of people in "the black misery of slavery" is a main cause of the "singular melancholy" under the Caesars; those who wallowed in pleasure could not fail to be infected, through "the mysterious working of the unconscious," by their brothers' sadness, driving them to orgiastic frenzy while the better of the others fell into *Weltschmerz*. [p.1146-1147]
- **Becoming, not remaining, a child** (note 61): a theologian accused Jung of being anti-Christian but overlooked that Christ said "Unless ye *become* as little children," not "remain": "One cannot, after all, ignore the whole drama of rebirth *in novam infantiam*." Note 60: Freud has made the same error (of treating the regressive wish as the goal). [p.1147]

### Block E: "The Song of the Moth" (the sun, light, the heavenly journey) [pp.1149-1165, notes 1-94]

- **Complexes are stable** under shifting surface forms (note 1; word-association experiments). [p.1149]
- **Asceticism and the Desert Fathers** (notes 4-5): Julian the Apostate made the last unsuccessful attempt to conquer Christianity with a nature religion. The flight from the world in the first centuries was an escape from the brutality of decadent Roman civilization; "Asceticism occurs whenever the animal instincts are so strong that they need to be violently exterminated." Chamberlain called it biological suicide from racial interbreeding; Jung disagrees ("miscegenation... makes rather for a coarsened joie de vivre") and reads ascetics as ethical people disgusted with an age whose melancholy expressed the disruption of the individual, who mortified an obsolete attitude. Virgil's Fourth Eclogue made him a quasi-Christian poet and psychopomp in Dante (note 7). [pp.1149-1150]
- *Faust* passages (Part I: the longing for tireless wings, the sunset, "streams of quenchless light"). Note 11 sends the reader to the editors' appendix on the play *The Moth and the Flame*. [pp.1150-1151]
- **Agape** (note 13): *caritas* in the Vulgate renders *agape*, from *agapan*, "to love, esteem, praise"; "an unmistakably psychic function." [p.1151]
- **Identification with the sun** (notes 14-20): Lucius in Apuleius is crowned with palm leaves like rays and "adorned like the sun and set up as an image"; the Mithraic celebrant says "I am a Star wandering with you and shining up from the depths"; Sassanid kings styled themselves "brothers of the sun and moon"; every Pharaoh's soul was a split-off of the Horus-sun; the tonsure, halo, feather crown and spiked Roman imperial crown are sun images (*Solis invicti comes*); the priests of Isis shaved their heads to shine like stars. Through his prayer the celebrant "has the divine power to make the sun come out." [pp.1151-1152]
- **John's "I and the Father are one"** and related sayings are set beside the Mithraic identification. [p.1152]
- **Light and sun mysticism** (notes 23-32): Mechthild of Magdeburg; Renan ("one cult alone was reasonable and scientific, the cult of the sun"); Buber's ecstatic confessions; Carlyle's "Flame-image." Note 28: the "heavenly wanderings of the soul" comes from the image of the wandering sun that travels the world from rising to setting; Jung quotes two poems (Mathilde von Wesendonck, "Grief," and Ricarda Huch) in which sunset and rising are death and awakening, and says the heavenly journey is a special case of the hero's journeys, continued as the *peregrinatio* in alchemy and found in a Harranite Plato treatise. [pp.1153-1156]
- Nietzsche (*Ecce Homo*) and Schreber's *Memoirs* on sun-visions; Egyptian sun-god identifications (Sobk with Ra); Akhenaten's reformation "initiated with fanaticism but soon collapsed" (note 43). [p.1156-1157]
- **Syncretism** (note 44): Apuleius's "Queen of Heaven" as Ceres, Venus, Diana or Proserpina; the Humanist Mutianus Rufus likewise held there were really only two gods in antiquity, a masculine and a feminine. [p.1157]
- **Light-substance as soul and spirit** (note 45): ascribed to divinity and to the soul (Mani; the Greeks' fiery breath); the Holy Ghost appeared to the apostles as flames because *pneuma* was believed fiery; the Iranian *Hvareno* is the "Grace of Heaven" as a substantial glory; also Kerner's *Seeress of Prevorst*. [p.1157]
- **Wind as fertilizer** (notes 46-48): the Mithraic "tube" hanging from the solar disc as origin of the wind; mares of Lusitania and Egyptian vultures impregnated by the wind; Jerome says Mithras was born from a rock "solo aestu libidinis," by the sole heat of libido. [pp.1157-1158]
- **Clinical case** (note 51): a colleague's paranoid woman patient reached megalomania by suddenly seeing a strong light, feeling a wind blow on her and her "heart turned over": from then she knew God had visited and was in her. Note 52: Dr. William Alanson White, superintendent of St. Elizabeth's Hospital, Washington, D.C., gave permission to use a case from that hospital (the main text, in an earlier part, gives the case). [p.1158]
- The Mithraic vision of the young fire-haired god and of the huge god holding the golden bull's shoulder (the Great Bear, seven stars, note 57); Mithras with short sword and torch; worshippers in bloody pelts at the Lupercalia, Dionysia and Saturnalia, the last surviving in Carnival and the priapic Punchinello (note 60). [pp.1158-1159]
- **Faust's chariot** (note 62): the fiery chariot that receives the dying or departing hero (Elijah, Mithras, St. Francis); Faust flies over the sea as Mithras does. [pp.1159-1160]
- **Christ and the sun** (notes 66-72, 77-78): the Christian date of the nativity placed on the day the sun was made or on the *Natalis Invicti*; Malachi's "Sun of righteousness... with healing in his wings" and the Egyptian winged disc; a Christian rebuke of those who pray to the rising sun; Augustine: "Christ was not made the sun, but the one through whom the sun was made"; catacomb sun-symbolism (swastika, ram and bull, peacock/phoenix); Clement's twelve apostles as the twelve months of the sun; the sun's course as a snake bearing the zodiac, Manichaean attribution of the Paradise snake to Christ (John 3:14). Attis later assimilated to Mithras; the Bethlehem birth-cave was originally an Attis-Adonis sanctuary (Jerome). Cumont is cited on how the two cults, adversaries, "discovered with amazement... the similarities which united them." [pp.1159-1163]
- **Projection onto nature** (note 84, important). "Nature, the object par excellence, reflects all those contents of the unconscious which as such are not conscious to us." Many nuances of sense-pleasure and pain are attributed to the object without examining its responsibility; modern Greek and Edda folk-songs show direct projection (the sea shore "grew bright" when the maiden's skirt lifted). "Projection also accounts for all the miraculous reports of 'cosmic' events at the birth and death of heroes." [pp.1163-1164]
- Notes on Byron's *Manfred* figures (Anah, the seraph, the "light substance of her own psyche"; "libido images"), on Franz von Stuck's paintings *Sin*, *Vice*, *Sensuality* (a woman's naked body encircled by a huge snake; "at bottom it portrays the fear of death"), and the heroes' spiritual confusion after their greatest deeds (note 85). [pp.1164-1165]
- **Objective and subjective level** (note 94): interpretation of unconscious products, for instance of a person in a dream, has a double aspect: what that person means in himself ("objective level") and what he means as a projection ("subjective level"). [p.1165]

### Block F: Part II, chapter on the concept of libido: sun-god, creator, Cabiri, libido's etymology [pp.1166-1170, notes 1-32] (likely "The Concept of Libido", with matter from the Part II opening)

- Samson as sun-god; killing the lion, like the Mithraic bull-sacrifice, anticipates the god's self-sacrifice. Rudra appears in the *Shvetashvatara Upanishad* as the sole creator-god (a wind- or storm-god, so naturally creator and fertilizer). Mithras has many eyes; Loyola's vision of the multi-eyed snake may be a variant. "Whoever has God, the sun, in himself is immortal like the sun." Purusha is thumb-sized, "of a clear luminosity like a smokeless fire." [p.1166]
- Goethe's *Faust* and the *coniunctio*; the "miracle" of gold-making (note 11); the legend that serpents cleaned a man's ears and made him clairaudient. [p.1167]
- **The Cabiri and dactyls ("Thumblings")** (notes 13-22): Pliny on Idaean dactyls shaped like a thumb; the dactylic meter; Cabiri as caricatured dwarfs on a Theban vase; Varro identified the great gods with penates; Kerényi on pre-Grecian Mediterranean origin; "Phales" as companion of Bacchus. [pp.1167-1168]
- **Freud confirms the libido concept** (note 23): Freud's "Notes on a Case of Paranoia" (published simultaneously with Part I of this book in 1912) says that Schreber's "rays of God," a condensation of sunbeams, nerve fibers and spermatozoa, "are in reality nothing else than a concrete representation and projection outwards of libidinal cathexes"; his belief that the world must end because his ego attracted all the rays "sound almost like endopsychic perceptions." Jung cites this as parallel to his own libido theory. [p.1168]
- **Etymology of *libido*** (notes 24-31). Cicero: *libido* is desire for a good not yet had (opposed to *laetitia*, joy), and when reason-guided desire is *voluntas* (Stoic *boulesis*) but when it "is incited more vehemently, turned away from reason" it is *libido* or unbridled cupidity, "found in all fools." Sallust and Augustine (*City of God* XIV, xv: *libido ulciscendi* = anger, *libido pecuniam* = avarice, *libido gloriandi* = vainglory, *libido dominandi* = the lust for rule that civil wars attest; and appetite that precedes pleasure, such as hunger and thirst). Still used in Tuscany for appetite in general. Related to Latin *libet* ("it pleases") and perhaps *liberi*, *Liber* and *Lubentia*. Note 32: "On Psychic Energy," par. 37, for the energic concept. [pp.1169-1170]

### Block H: Fire-making, the fire-stick, ploughing, and the Vedic *atman* [pp.1174-1182, notes 1-55]

This block stores Jung's comparative philology for the libido-symbol of fire made by rubbing.

- **Fire-drilling and Prometheus.** Kuhn's *Mythologische Studien* on the Sanskrit root *manth/math* ("to rub, to churn"), *pramantha* (fire-stick), Greek Prometheus (editors: now read "the one who thinks ahead"), the churning of the ocean for *amrita*, and the Balinese fire-god who "has his seat in man's brain" and dances on a fiery wheel (sun-symbol). The Latin *mentula* is associated with this root; mint (*menta*) as aphrodisiac and contraceptive in antiquity; the root *ment* in *mens* "completes the parallel to *pramantha*"; protruding body parts masculine, concavities feminine in symbolism. [pp.1174-1175]
- **The female fire-stick** and the birth of Agni (Rig-Veda III, 29: the fire "son of Ila"); the *Niedfyr* (need-fire) that Charlemagne's capitulary banned as sacrilegious; a medieval Scottish chronicle of lay-brothers lighting fire by friction and setting up a Priapus image to cure a cattle plague. [pp.1176-1177]
- **Ploughing as libido-symbol** (notes 19-22). Greek *aroun* "to plough" also means "to impregnate"; Latin *arare*; a Florentine vase showing six ithyphallic men carrying a phallic plough; the medieval *carrus navalis* (Carnival). Meringer's etymologies from Indo-European **uen*: wood, ploughing/hoeing, love (Venus), pasture, ecstasy (*Wonne*), dwelling (*wohnen*), habit, toil, suffering, "to win," wound, strife. Jung reads this as a "far-reaching fusion of libido-symbols with external materials and activities." Note 22: the old custom of the "bridal bed" in the field, "as I make this woman fruitful, so I make the earth fruitful": "The symbol canalizes the libido into cultivating and fructifying the earth." [pp.1177-1179]
- **Rhythm and work** (note 27, Bücher), and **Eberschweiler** again. Note 32: the "factor of extensity" of older physics. [p.1179]
- **Upanishadic creation** (notes 33-38). Brihadaranyaka 1.4: in the beginning the Self was alone, in the form of a Person (*purusha*), was afraid and had no joy, "desired a second," and divided in two. Jung: the *atman* so conceived is bisexual; "the primordial universal being, a concept which in psychological terms coincides with that of the libido." Plato's world-soul in *Timaeus* 33 is closest. [pp.1179-1180]
- Swan, eagle and phoenix as related alchemical sun-symbols; Heine's swan song. Plato's *Symposium* on Eros ; alchemists regarded the "fourth" as *filius philosophorum* (note 50); Agni's Dionysian side; *soma* from seminal fluid; Frobenius on sun-gods. [pp.1180-1182]

## 6. Method of amplification, as visible in this range

The notes show the procedure Jung applies to the Miller fantasies, and is the clearest "how-to" visible in Part 5:

1. **Take an image** from the fantasy (a hero, a horse, a snake, a tree, a sun-hymn).
2. **Set beside it parallels** from myth, ritual, religion, folk song, poetry and psychosis (for example the Rata whale-voyage, Mithras's bull, Heracles and Hera, Siegfried's dragon, Schreber's rays, Spielrein's patient).
3. **Use etymology** as a second layer of parallel (the Sanskrit *manth*, Latin *libido*, *religio*, *mentula/menta*, German *Scheide*) to show that old word-meanings persist as the "dark chamber" feeding present thought.
4. **Read the cluster, not the single case:** what recurs is the typical figure ("primordial image", later "archetype").
5. **Translate back into psychic process:** mother = unconscious, nature, regressive longing; fire or sun = libido, psychic energy; the hero's fight with monster = overcoming the mother-imago; sacrifice = giving up the infantile attachment; the self = the whole that both ego and unconscious belong to.
6. **Guard against two errors:** reducing everything to the personal (the personalistic view, note 5 in block D) and taking mythic equations as metaphysics (note 29).

I summarized by theme, so not every index entry is listed. Names of ancient authors, deities and places (hundreds) are omitted unless they carry a concept.
- **Garbled or missing text.** Greek passages in the notes are heavily damaged by OCR/pdftotext (notes 51, 55, 56, 65, 68, 74 and others, pp.1143-1161, 1233-1236); I did not reproduce them and paraphrased only where an English gloss in the source accompanies the Greek. Notes 9 and 16 (p.1136) have missing Greek words in the source (`[garbled in source]`), and note 87 (p.1202) is an empty entry. The Wesendonck and Huch poems in block E are given in the source in the Hinkle (1916) translation.
- **Not extracted in detail:** pure bibliographic notes (hundreds); long quotations of Goethe, Hölderlin, Wagner and Rig-Veda passages (summarized as references); the Collected Works volume contents other than volume 5; the publisher's date list.
- **Clinical notes.** Where Jung uses a patient's words (Spielrein's patient; the unnamed laborer; the girl on innocence), I paraphrased; the cases are described in other volumes (the notes cite "Psychology of Dementia Praecox," "The Content of the Psychoses").
- **Editors' interpolations** (bracketed in the source) are distinguished from Jung's own notes where I could tell; the note on "Po-po" (p.1185-1186) is an editors' remark about American dialect and is not argument.
