## IMPORTANT: this is NOT Jung's own text

**The file name and the assignment describe this as a book by C. G. Jung. It is not.** The author of every chapter is the Jungian analyst and trained scientist Joseph Cambray [p.6, 7]. The book is a *secondary study of* Jung's synchronicity concept. **Jung's own monograph ("Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle") is not reproduced here.** What appears of Jung is (a) Cambray's paraphrases of the monograph, (b) short quotations from it, from Jung's letters (Pauli, Neumann, Kirsch, Seelig, White), seminars and other works, and (c) Cambray's commentary and extensions. Everything below labeled "Jung says" is Jung **as quoted or summarized by Cambray**. Do not treat this as a full statement of Jung's monograph. Items the assignment asked for:

- **Jung's definitions of synchronicity:** present only as quotations and summaries (see ch. 1 below).
- **The astrological experiment:** mentioned in about one paragraph only; the design and results are **not** reported in this book (see ch. 1, "Forerunners"). Cambray says only that it was a statistical study of marriage pairs (a type of synastry) and that Pauli was discouraging.
- **Acausal connection and the archetype/psychoid:** developed through Cambray's reading of Jung's monograph and the Pauli/Jung letters.

Labels used below: **[Jung, quoted]** = words of Jung inside Cambray's text; **[Pauli, quoted]** = Pauli in the Pauli/Jung letters as quoted; **[Cambray]** = Cambray's own claims; **[editor/commentary]** = the foreword by David Rosen.

## Overview

Cambray, writing from clinical work with trauma survivors and from a background in chemistry and physics, re-examines Jung's concept of synchronicity ("a meaningful coincidence" and "an acausal connecting principle") [p.20]. Chapter 1 traces the history of the idea in Jung's seminars (1928 onward), his collaboration with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli, the content of the 1952 monograph, Jung's stated forerunners (Leibniz, the I Ching, correspondentia, Schopenhauer), and what the monograph does for archetype theory. Chapter 2 sets Jung in the history of scientific holism and field theory (Faraday, Maxwell, Einstein), and offers Indra's net as an archetypal image of the field. Chapter 3 introduces complexity theory, emergence, scale-free networks and especially symmetry and symmetry breaking, and argues that synchronicity is a symmetry-breaking, emergent phenomenon. Chapter 4 applies this to empathy, mirror neurons and the analytic field, with a clinical case. Chapter 5 proposes "cultural synchronicities": Athenian democracy, the conquest of Mexico, the discovery of phosphorus, and serendipity (penicillin, mirror neurons). The afterword gathers the claim that the psychoid is the organizing propensity that arose at the origin of the universe, and that the mirror is the book's leitmotif.

## Series Editor's Foreword (David H. Rosen) [editor/commentary] [p.12-15]

Brief summary only. Rosen opens with a personal anecdote (a snake swallowing a koi in his garden) and a haiku, and compares them with Jung's find of a snake choked on a fish at Bollingen. He summarizes the book's chapters: chapter 1 links the concept to an early conversation between Jung and Einstein (relativity of time and space) and to Pauli, who helped Jung formulate the "psychoid archetype" that "grounds the psyche in biology (and nature)"; chapter 2 on holism, Leibniz's monads and Indra's net; chapter 3 on complexity and the "mirror complex"; chapter 4 on mirroring, resonance and "objective sympathy" (a phrase credited to John Beebe); chapter 5 on democracy, Cortés, phosphorus and penicillin. ## Introduction [Cambray] [p.20-24]

- The book grew out of clinical work: analysis can "activate, energize, and focus unconscious processes," producing experiences perceived as extraordinary from the standpoint of ego consciousness. Cambray saw clusterings of such activations around themes, which resembled what Jung described, and wanted to reexamine the notion "in light of new models of the mind and changes in scientific understanding" [p.20].
- Jung's formulation: synchronicity as "a meaningful coincidence" and "an acausal connecting principle" [p.20]. Cambray's reading: Jung wished to expand the West's core conceptions of nature and psyche, to make room for unique individual experiences "beyond the restrictions of a cause-effect reductive description of the world," and to see the psyche "as embedded into the substance of the world" [p.20].
- Jung's genius is described as seeing depth in the odd and seemingly erroneous (his early work on mediums and on the associations of psychotic patients). He sought "archetypes" as the deepest patterns in mind, culture and nature, and "discovered a science of the sacred" [p.21].
- Jung placed his major piece on synchronicity in the 1952 book *The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche*, coauthored with Pauli (Nobel laureate, founder of quantum theory) [p.21].
- **Plan of the book:** ch. 1 Jung/Pauli dialogue and scientific background; ch. 2 field theories and their archetypal background; ch. 3 complexity theory (complex adaptive systems, self-organization, emergence) and symmetry; ch. 4 empathy (archetypal and synchronistic dimensions of a Jungian field model); ch. 5 "cultural synchronicities," defined as meaningful coincidences that affect large numbers of people within a culture or at the meeting of cultures [p.21-22].
- **Personal example [Cambray]:** while formulating these ideas, on a nearly empty flight after 9/11, the author was seated next to a stranger who turned out to have a personal link to a statistician whose work questioning synchronicity he was evaluating; the encounter itself became an instance of the subject under study. He says no comparable conversation has happened since [p.23-24].
- Stated modesty: the chapters are "not meant to be the final or even the definitive word"; analytic ideas must be reexamined each generation to remain "living ideas" [p.24].

## Chapter 1. Synchronicity: The History of a Radical Idea [Cambray on Jung] [p.25-50]

### Opening: clinical acceptance and the scarab case [p.25-26]
- Synchronicity as commonly understood: "coincidence(s) without causation" yet meaningful to those involved. Psychoanalyst Elizabeth Lloyd Mayer and Carol Gilligan ran oversubscribed discussion groups at American Psychoanalytic Association meetings where clinicians told stories of anomalous experience they felt unsafe telling elsewhere. Cambray says Mayer's omission of synchronicity there reflects politics, not ignorance [p.25].
- **Jung's paradigmatic case (the golden scarab):** a young woman whose analysis was stalled by her resistance to unconscious process dreamed of a golden scarab (jewelry). As she described it, Jung heard a tapping at his window, opened it, caught a rose chafer (a scarabaeid beetle) and handed it to her, apparently breaking through her resistance [p.26].

### Chronology of the term in Jung's work [p.26-28]
1. **28 November 1928, seminar on dream analysis** (*Dream Analysis*): first reference. Coincidences clustered around bull and bullfight imagery: a seminar participant's dream (Dr. Shaw), a patient's drawings of a bull's head with a solar disc between the horns, and a letter from a friend in Mexico who had just seen a bullfight. Jung calls the dream a "living thing" but says it would be "a mistake to consider them as causal; events don't come about because of dreams," they happen with "a sort of irrational regularity" [Jung, quoted, p.27].
2. Same seminar, East vs. West **[Jung, quoted, p.27]:** "Synchronism is the prejudice of the East; causality is the modern prejudice of the West"; the East bases much of its science on coincidences. This follows Jung receiving *The Secret of the Golden Flower* from Richard Wilhelm in 1928.
3. **December 1929 seminar:** Jung explicitly uses the term, said to have been "invented" for "things happening at the same moment as an expression of the same time content," after seeing cases where people not concerned were affected [Jung, quoted, p.27].
4. **1930, memorial address for Wilhelm:** the I Ching's science rests "not on the causality principle but on one which ... I have tentatively called the synchronistic principle" [Jung, quoted, p.27].
5. **1951 Eranos lecture "On Synchronicity"**, then the full essay "Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle" (German 1952; English 1955 in *The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche*; monograph 1960; Collected Works 8). The book's second half is Pauli's essay on archetypal ideas in Kepler's theories [p.28].

### Pauli and Jung [p.28-29]
- Pauli (professor at ETH Zurich) was referred to Jung as a patient by his father after his mother's suicide and a brief first marriage, with alcohol problems. Jung referred him to a junior colleague (Dr. Erna Rosenbaum), supervising at one remove; analysis lasted eight months, with ten months of dreams, which became Part II of *Psychology and Alchemy*. The two became friends and working partners (correspondence 1932 to 1958; translated as *Atom and Archetype*; historical study by Suzanne Gieser, *The Innermost Kernel*) [p.28].
- Pauli persuaded Jung to write the monograph, offered to copublish, read and critiqued the draft, and "significantly altered Jung's views on a number of topics, including archetypes" [p.29].

### The monograph: foreword and "The Exposition" [p.29-33]
- **Foreword:** Jung admits difficulties (feelings of inadequacy about scientific training; his background was biological, never math and physics). Three reasons for proceeding: (1) growing experience of the phenomena; (2) research on fish symbolism (*Aion*) which included a set of synchronicities, among them the **1933 find at the Bollingen shore of a snake choked while swallowing a fish, both dead.** A family member reports Jung read it against his work on Christianity (Fish) and Alchemy (Snake): their inability to integrate was fatal to each. He carved the image on a stone at the base of the Bollingen loggia (fig. 1) [p.29-30]; (3) he realized he had been "alluding to the existence of this phenomenon on and off in my writing for twenty years" [Jung, quoted, p.29].
- **Chapter "The Exposition":** begins with modern physics. "Natural laws are statistical truths" [Jung, quoted, p.29]: true at macroscopic scale, not for individual events at very small scale or high velocity. Causality therefore is "only statistically valid and only relatively true" [Jung, quoted, p.30]. Footnote [p.133]: physicist Max Born's three assumptions of classical causality (causality, antecedence, contiguity) and his conclusion that "chance has become the primary notion."
- Jung's concern is rare or unique human-scale events, nonreproducible and so outside the science of his day. He steps away from biology as the home of such events [p.30-31].
- **Chance and Kammerer:** Jung acknowledges Paul Kammerer's "law of series" but finds it uninteresting as "nothing but runs of chance whose only 'law' is probability" [Jung, quoted, p.31]. He then presents a cluster of six events around the figure of the fish, noting the meaningful coincidence with an acausal connection and his own numinous experience.
- **Three key elements of synchronicity** as Cambray reads this passage: **meaningful coincidence, acausal connection, and numinosity** [p.31].
- **Schopenhauer** ("On the Apparent Design in the Fate of the Individual") is called by Jung "standing godfather to the views I am now developing" [Jung, quoted, p.31]; Pauli brought it to his attention. Pauli's gloss [Pauli, quoted]: Schopenhauer compares causal chains to meridians and simultaneousness to parallel circles, "corresponding exactly to your 'equivalent cross-connections.'" Schopenhauer borrowed from Leibniz's "pre-established harmony." [Cambray] This model is a rigid, regular global network; scale-free networks (ch. 3) fit Jung's vision better [p.31].

### Rhine and the archetypal basis [p.31-33]
- Jung turns to J. B. Rhine's parapsychology at Duke (Rhine was a correspondent), noting that affect affects results, and boredom lowers them; Jung imports this into the hypothesis [p.31-32].
- **Pauli's objection [Pauli, quoted, p.32]:** Rhine's experiments seem "a totally different type of phenomenon" from the others listed as synchronistic; he sees no archetypal basis, which "is crucial to an understanding of the phenomena," and he regrets that the archetypal aspect (their appearance as "complementary to the archetypal contents becoming conscious") is not mentioned in the latest draft.
- [Cambray] The archetypal hypothesis is reinserted into the text "as the formal factor in organizing unconscious processes and providing the affective charge" that appears as numinosity. Jung did not include Pauli's criticism of Rhine.
- **Distinction:** parapsychological events presume a causal explanation (unknown forces, "paranormal causality"), while synchronicity "would remain strictly acausal" [Cambray, p.32].
- Jung's use of Rhine supports dropping causality because such phenomena cannot be understood energetically but as "a falling together in time, a kind of simultaneity" [Jung, quoted, p.33], which gives the term *synchronicity*. [Cambray] Jung's constraint came from nineteenth-century thermodynamics of systems at equilibrium; open systems far from equilibrium (life) dissipate energy and create local order, which leads to self-organization (ch. 3) [p.33].

### Simultaneity under strain; Pauli and "meaning-time" [p.33-35]
- Several authors note that Jung's own examples violate simultaneity (precognitive dreams). Jung's attempted fix: "synchronistic events rest on the simultaneous occurrence of two different psychic states" [Jung, quoted, p.33]. Pauli [Pauli, quoted]: "synchron" "seems to me somewhat illogical, unless you wish to relate it to a chronos that is essentially different from normal time."
- **Pauli ties time to the psychoid [Pauli, quoted, p.33-34]:** insofar as synchronistic events form what Jung called a "psychoid" initial stage of consciousness, they often share simultaneity; "the meaning-connection, as primary agent, produces time as the secondary one."
- [Cambray] This reframing leaves a psychologically relevant "meaning-time," "a moment of complexity," time as an aspect of the moment, as it is a component of relativistic space-time [p.34].

### The term "psychoid" [p.34]
- Coined c. 1907 to 1908 by biologist and neovitalist **Hans Driesch** ("the basis of instinctive phenomena," a nonphysical potential with intensive, qualitative properties but no extension). Jung used it differently (and differently from Bleuler's "cortical soul") and not until 1946. Jung's usage: "quasi-psychic," at the interface where psychological and material are undifferentiated and cannot reach consciousness as such; it operates before any Cartesian split of mind and body, "rather like an aspect of the unus mundus of alchemy" [Cambray]. Cambray notes premodern (alchemical) cosmologies parallel subatomic physics: an original undifferentiated state, a world of relations rather than objects.

### Einstein, space, time and affect [p.35]
- Jung [quoted]: if space and time are only apparently properties of bodies in motion and "created by the intellectual needs of the observer," their "relativization by psychic conditions is no longer a matter for astonishment." And: an observer's emotional state can alter space and time "by 'contraction'."
- Jung to Carl Seelig (25 Feb 1953) [quoted]: Einstein, a houseguest on several occasions (one in early January 1911, between special and general relativity) "first started me off thinking about a possible relativity of time as well as space, and their psychic conditionality," and thirty years later this led to his relation with Pauli and the "thesis of psychic synchronicity."
- Archetypal basis of affect [Jung, quoted]: "Meaningful coincidences ... seem to rest upon an archetypal foundation. ... Affectivity, however, rests to a large extent on the instincts, whose formal aspect is the archetype." [Cambray] Jung is building a theory "based on the psychoid archetype as an originary point from which the subjective and objective realms emanate."

### Synchronicity and cosmogony [p.36-40]
- Another of Jung's aphoristic definitions: synchronicity as **"an act of creation in time"** [Cambray citing Jung, p.36].
- **Cosmology background given by Cambray:** Einstein's cosmological constant ("fudge factor," later his "biggest blunder"); Friedmann (1922) dynamic expanding model from a single point; Lemaitre (a Belgian physicist-priest) "primeval atom," presented 1927 at Solvay (Einstein: "Your calculations are correct, but your physics is abominable"); Hubble's red-shift; Einstein's 1933 Pasadena endorsement of Lemaitre ("the most beautiful and satisfactory explanation of creation"); Gamow 1948 "ylem" (primordial substance, Cambray notes resemblance to the alchemical prima materia); Fred Hoyle coining "Big Bang" in 1950 BBC lectures to deride it while defending the Steady State model [p.36-38].
- Jung read Hoyle (*The Nature of the Universe*; the novel *The Black Cloud*, which he saw as "how the collective unconscious is coming to an astronomer" [Jung letter, Jan 1958, quoted p.38]). Pauli warned that steady-state cosmogony "is not physics but a projection of the unconscious" [Pauli, quoted, p.38].
- Jung to James Kirsch (1953) [quoted, p.38]: if God's consciousness is clearer than man's, Creation has no meaning; "God does not in fact play dice, as Einstein says, but has invented a machine, which is far worse. Actually the story of the Creation is more like an experiment with dice than anything purposive." Cambray reads this as showing the synchronicity essay is tied to cosmogony, not only to acts of creation in time [p.39].
- **Jung on space, time and causality [Jung, quoted, p.39]:** under certain conditions "space and time can be reduced to almost zero," and then "causality disappears along with them because causality is bound up with the existence of space and time and physical changes." Therefore "synchronistic phenomena cannot in principle be associated with any conceptions of causality."
- [Cambray] This resembles physics' picture of the earliest universe; "psyche" is proposed as another potential in the primordial singularity (not an intelligent design argument, but psyche as pervading the universe as space, time and matter do) and synchronicities as traces of an original undifferentiated state [p.39-40].
- **Jung to Erich Neumann, March 1959 [quoted, p.40]:** "In this chaos of chance, synchronistic phenomena were probably at work, operating both with and against the known laws of nature to produce, in archetypal moments, syntheses which appear to us miraculous. ... This presupposes not only an all-pervading, latent meaning which can be recognized by consciousness, but during that preconscious time, a psychoid process with which a physical event meaningfully coincides. Here the meaning cannot be recognized because there is as yet no consciousness."
- [Cambray] Close to David Bohm's implicate order (though Bohm retrieves causality as hidden); psyche as "an ordering, organizing principle." By the end of the monograph Jung re-envisions simultaneity theologically: "what happens successively in time is simultaneous in the mind of God" [Jung, quoted, p.40]. Roderick Main's work on the spiritual significance of synchronicity is cited.

### Jung's identified forerunners [p.40-46]
Cambray notes Roderick Main identified eight areas of influence; Cambray selects differently.
- **Albertus Magnus / Avicenna:** emotion as the "cause" of magical events; **Goethe:** "We all have certain electric and magnetic powers within us" (quoted p.41). [Cambray] Goethe precedes Faraday's field theory (1845), a forerunner Jung does not name [p.41].
- **Society for Psychical Research** (passing mention) and the **I Ching:** Jung (who wrote the foreword to Wilhelm's translation) was struck by the oracle's capacity for intuitive grasp of the whole situation. The sages "sought to explain the simultaneous occurrence of a psychic state with a physical process as an equivalence of meaning" [Jung, quoted, p.42].
- **The astrological experiment (all that the book says) [Cambray, p.42]:** Jung wished to run an experiment using an "intuitive-mantic technique" and chose astrology, "but is not wholly comfortable with this." The experiment "relies on a statistical study of the marriage connection between individuals (a type of synastry)." Pauli "was politely discouraging about this entire project." Cambray adds that the results "hold some interest in terms of the way the affective involvement of the researchers was implicated," and that readers have often muddled or misunderstood them "who would make Jung into a New Age guru." **No design, data or numbers are given; the monograph's own account is not in this book.**
- **Taoism:** Wilhelm translated the Tao as "meaning" (key for Jung). Lao Tzu's "no-thing" (the usefulness of a clay vessel depends on its empty space). Jung: it "is only called Nothing because it does not appear in the world of the senses, but is only its organizer" [Jung, quoted, p.42]. Cambray stresses self-organization as the source of synchronistic meaning.
- **Correspondentia** (medieval/Renaissance; "As above, so below"): per Cirlot, cosmic phenomena form series on separate planes whose components are linked "in their essence and in their ultimate significance" [quoted, p.42]. Cambray: like Kammerer's series but with significant linkage between series.
- **Bohr confusion [Cambray, p.43]:** a footnote of Jung's says Pauli told him Bohr used "correspondence" as a mediating term between particle (discontinuum) and wave (continuum). Cambray says Jung garbled Bohr's *complementarity* (wave-particle duality) with Bohr's *correspondence principle* (quantum behavior reproduces classical physics at large numbers). Cambray adds that Jung's dream theory uses "compensation," which he distinguished from complementation: compensation "means balancing and comparing different data or points of view so as to produce an adjustment or a rectification" [Jung, quoted, p.43]. Cambray: correspondence, complementarity and compensation all operate in Jung's wrestling; Jung wishes synchronicity to compensate causality.
- **Hippocrates [quoted, p.44]:** "There is one common flow, one common breathing, all things are in sympathy." Cambray: a radically interconnected universe.
- **Leibniz (1646-1716)** is the most useful forerunner. His **pre-established harmony**: monads are coordinated by God without interaction; the two synchronized clocks simile (perhaps from Geulincx, which Pauli pointed out to Jung). Each monad is an "active indivisible mirror" and "a perpetual living mirror of the universe" [quoted in Jung, p.45]. Pauli connected this to his own vision of a "world clock" (two circles, common center, three pulses; Jung discusses the image in CW 12 and CW 11) [p.44-45]. Leibniz on body and soul: "a resolution in the soul is accompanied by an appropriate movement in the body" [quoted, p.45]. Jung's response: "the possibility that the relation between body and soul may yet be understood as a synchronistic one. Should this conjecture ever be proved, my present view that synchronicity is a relatively rare phenomenon would have to be corrected" [Jung, quoted, p.45; cites C. A. Meier]. Cambray: Leibniz first used "supervene" for mind-body, close to the emergentist view [p.45].
- **Leibniz and the I Ching:** Jesuit Joachim Bouvet sent Leibniz the hexagrams; the "natural order" matches binary numbers 0 to 63 (broken line = 0, unbroken = 1). Helmut Wilhelm published the story in 1943 and presented it at the 1951 Eranos conference, which Jung heard [p.45-46].

### Jung's conclusion in the monograph [p.46-48]
- **"Absolute knowledge"** is "characteristic of synchronistic phenomena, a knowledge not mediated by sense organs," which "supports the hypothesis of a self-subsistent meaning" [Jung, quoted, p.46]. Examples: medical anecdotes of apparently comatose patients later describing events accurately; Jung says "where sense perceptions are impossible from the start, it can hardly be a question of anything but synchronicity" [quoted, p.46]. [Cambray] Out-of-body experiences have now been induced in a London laboratory (Ehrsson).
- **Karl von Frisch's *The Dancing Bees*:** noncerebral intelligence; Cambray links it to modern "swarm logic" (bottom-up organization, emergence) [p.46-47].
- **Frequency and general acausal orderedness:** Jung includes properties of numbers (primes), radioactive decay, mind-body relations. Jung and Pauli drafted quaternary diagrams: Space/Time (vertical) against Causality/Synchronicity (horizontal); Indestructible Energy and Space-Time Continuum (vertical) against "Constant Connection through Effect (Causality)" and "Inconstant Connection through Contingence, Equivalence, or 'Meaning' (Synchronicity)" (horizontal) (letters 45P and 46J) [p.47].
- **The archetype's role, as quoted [Jung, p.47]:** "Archetypal equivalences [outer physical and inner psychic processes] are contingent to causal determination, that is to say there exist between them and the causal processes no relations that conform to law. ... It is an initial state which is 'not [...] the specific aspect of a modality that has the functional significance of a world-constituting factor. The archetype represents psychic probability."
- [Cambray] Synchronicity becomes a special subset of **general acausal orderedness**, and Jung's archetypal theory expands. The **archetype-as-such** (Kantian noumenon, without form or content, "only the potential to express," like the axes of a crystal lattice) is distinguished from archetypal imagery. Per Gieser, Pauli pushed the archetype away from "inborn structure" lying "latent" toward something that "constellates, or emerges at certain stages and situations in life" [Pauli, quoted, p.48]: constellating rather than causing events; thus an emergentist view. Psychology becomes "the guardian of the arts and sciences" [p.48].

### Value and relevance of synchronicity [Cambray] [p.48-50]
- Jung wished psychology to stand beside physics: he "borrowed and transmuted the language of physics" to enlarge psychology and extend physics; the exchange changed Jung's psychology, as in his view of therapy: "For two personalities to meet is like mixing two different chemical substances: if there is any combination at all, both are transformed. ... You can exert no influence if you are not susceptible to influence" [Jung, quoted, p.49].
- Synchronicity as a **universal principle** and **compensatory notion to causality**, part of a holistic science (compare nineteenth-century Naturphilosophie) [p.49].
- Jung's method: valuing slips, errors and psychotic speech (the word association test) made him look for meaning in what is discarded; by studying unique events he was "one of the first scientific psychologists to adopt ... a qualitative phenomenological approach" [p.50].
- Synchronicity as an "objective" metaphor-forming tendency of the world: disparate elements juxtaposed so as to shock and open the mind [p.50].

## Chapter 2. Interconnectedness: Visions and Science of Field Theory [Cambray] [p.51-63]

### Jung and holism [p.51-55]
- *Holism* from Greek *holos*; Aristotle (*Metaphysics*): where the whole "is something beside the parts, there is a cause" (shorthand: "the whole is greater than the sum of the parts") [p.51]. Reductionism explains complex things through their parts; the West's analytic paradigm favors it [p.51-52].
- Jung does not use "holism" but writes of **wholeness**. **Individuation** is a sustained dialectic between the conscious I and unconscious dimensions; shadow elements (menacing figures in dreams) must be faced and taken as psychologically real. It is "not a form of perfectionism but ... about completeness," including negative aspects, hence differs from morality systems seeking only the good [p.52].
- **The Self**: "center and circumference of the entire personality"; the ego is only the center of consciousness; the Self is the archetypal potential from which the ego complex emerges; its imagery coincides with the god image or its negative (daimonic); imagery ranges from the alchemical lapis and crystals through animal and human to divine, with numinous feeling tone; psychopathology relates to ruptures in the ego-Self axis [p.53]. It is a paradoxical whole "not definable as a completely logistically consistent term" [p.53].
- **Archetypes** are "virtual, empty forms," constellated by matching internal and environmental cues, psychosomatic, forming "a highly interconnected polycentric network." Jung: "It is a well-nigh hopeless undertaking to tear a single archetype out of the living tissue of the psyche; but despite their interwovenness they do form units of meaning that can be apprehended intuitively" [quoted, p.53]. Polytheism expresses archetypal diversity; the Self links to monotheism. Samuels and colleagues: "Gods are metaphors of archetypal behaviors and myths are archetypal enactments" [quoted, p.54].
- **Opposites and the quaternity:** tension of opposites resolves in a third (cf. Hegel) but wholeness requires a **quaternity**; hence Jung welcomed the 1950 Catholic dogma of the Assumption as adding the feminine fourth to the Trinity (a psychological, not theological, point) [p.54].
- **Method:** amplification (1914): bringing historical and cultural associations to unconscious material; "not reductive but constructive." Jung [quoted, p.54]: the psyche's "other side is in the process of becoming, and can only be grasped synthetically or constructively. The causal standpoint merely inquires how this psyche has become what it is ... The constructive standpoint asks how, out of this present psyche, a bridge can be built into its own future."

### Holism in science and field theory [p.55-63]
- Val Dusek (*The Holistic Inspirations of Physics*, 1999): classical field theory has holistic roots in **traditional Chinese thought, Renaissance hermetic/occult theory, and German Romantic philosophy**, the same sources Jung used [p.55]. Betty Jo Dobbs on Newton's alchemy [p.55].
- **Seventeenth century:** Copernicus, Brahe, Galileo, Kepler (Pauli's essay subject); Descartes' mind-matter dualism; **Newton**'s laws and gravitation, troubled by "action at a distance" and by absolute space and time [p.56-57]. Newton wrote more on alchemy than on physics; Leibniz (hermetic tradition per Frances Yates) opposed atomism, held time and space as relational (hence Einstein called himself a "Leibnizian"); matter as intensifications of force in a continuum; monads as mirrors [p.57-58]. Spinoza's dual-aspect monism now revived among neuroscientists [p.58].
- **Faraday:** after Oersted's 1820 discovery of electromagnetism, Faraday (devout Sandemanian, no mathematical education) conceived **lines of force** and the **field** (first presented June 1845), rejecting empty absolute space and action at a distance; suggested that such lines carry light vibrations and could account for gravitation [p.58-59]. [Cambray] "From a Jungian perspective we would identify this as the reemergence of an archetypal idea leading to a vision of a wholly interconnected universe." Faraday's religiously motivated belief in conservation helped him find electromagnetic induction (his "force" conservation was a scientific error, since energy is conserved) [p.59].
- **Maxwell** (letter 1857): "your lines of force can 'weave a web across the sky' and lead the stars in their courses" [quoted, p.59]; completed the electromagnetic equations (1862-65), showing light as electromagnetic radiation beyond the visible; this gave Jung his metaphor for archetypes having spiritual and instinctual poles [p.60].
- Parallel with nineteenth-century "magnetism" (hypnosis, mediums such as Leonora Piper; Jung's dissertation on his cousin) and a suggested comparison of "psychoid" with transcranial magnetic stimulation [p.60-61].
- **Einstein:** special relativity (1905), general relativity (1915-16), gravity as curvature of space-time. Field theories were the Zeitgeist; William James's "field of consciousness"; Jung hosted Einstein [p.61].
- **Pauli's critique of Jung's field thinking:** per Gieser, Pauli found Jung's idea of autonomous, regular unconscious processes "epistemologically unacceptable"; it recalled "the classical idea of the objective reality of the cosmos," classical field concepts and Maxwell's equations, and ignored the new epistemological situation of quantum physics: Jung "still had a tendency to treat the unconscious as a field that may be observed without considering the influence of the observation" [Gieser quoting/summarizing Pauli, p.62]. Cambray says the emergentist reading attempts to address this, with uncertain success.
- **Field reformulation of archetypes [Cambray, p.62]:** each archetype is a node in a polycentric whole, the whole a network with scale-free properties; Jung's *Psychology of the Transference* (1946) presents an interactive field model on an archetypal background; Bohm and Peat's "implicate order."
- **Archetypal field images by amplification [p.63]:** the alchemical **unus mundus**, and **Indra's net** (Hua-yen / Flower Garland school): a vast net with a reflecting jewel at every intersection, each jewel reflecting all others, "reflections of reflections continue without end." Leibniz's mirror thesis is the same pattern. [Cambray] Synchronicity is a potent manifestation of the field "with the resonant reflections of internal and external events."

## Chapter 3. Complexity, Emergence, and Symmetry [Cambray] [p.64-86]

### Complexity and emergence [p.64-71]
- **Complex adaptive systems (CAS):** nonlinear, far from equilibrium, self-organizing; "emergent properties" are behaviors greater than the sum of the interactions among parts, with new higher levels of order; sensitivity to initial conditions (butterfly effect); new order costs energy dissipation [p.64-65].
- **Re-opening Jung's energy argument [Cambray, p.65]:** Jung saw meaningful coincidence as inexplicable and acausal because it lay outside energetic phenomena; the energetics of open systems far from equilibrium was in infancy then. Emergence appears at "the edge of order and chaos," which suits tracking Jungian analytic process.
- **Insect examples:** blister beetle larvae clump to mimic a female bee (emergent "bee-ing" with no internal image possible); termite colonies (Marais, *The Soul of the White Ant*); Malaysian fireflies flashing in synchrony without a leader [p.65-66]. Freud on telepathy and insect communities [quoted, p.66-67]: "possibly it is done by means of a direct psychical transference" as an "original, archaic method" of communication. Jung's own insect examples: the scarab; the leaf-cutting ant and yucca moth (from Conway Lloyd Morgan, *Habit and Instinct*, for archetype theory) [p.67]. Cambray: two evolutionary approaches to purpose: collective self-organizing behavior vs. internalization in a single individual (the brain's roughly hundred billion neurons).
- Jung's Self "can be read as an emergent property of the psyche"; analytical psychologists applying complexity theory are listed (Hogenson, Tresan, Saunders and Skar, Knox, Wilkinson, McDowell, Martin-Vallas, Solomon) [p.67-68]. Emergence links science and the spiritual (collective intelligence, Surowiecki) [p.68].
- **Networks:** "hubs" richly linked, "nodes" with fewer links; **scale-free** (fractal-like) networks have self-organizing properties (examples: fern fronds, river systems, nerves, airline maps) [p.68]. [Cambray] The collective unconscious as the sum of all archetypes would have scale-free structure; amplification maps it. Jacobi's grid (fig. 3) is too regular; Edinger's *Anatomy of the Psyche* maps (fig. 4) relax it and imply variable link strengths; weak links stabilize complex systems [p.69-70].
- **Network reading of development [Cambray, p.70-71]:** personal complexes organize around major archetypes (hubs); object-relations and transference/countertransference patterns link hubs; later individuation moves toward marginal nodes (the "shadow" region). **Clinical dream example:** a man in his mid-thirties dreams of arriving for a session and seeing at twilight "a constellation that I've never seen before. It is new and it is nearly overhead," read as a new pattern of psychological being [p.71].

### Symmetry [p.71-76]
- Principle: "the formation of, or increase in, complexity is characterized by a breaking of the symmetry of the precursor state" [p.71].
- Etymology: Greek *symmetria*, "due proportion"; a seventeenth-century sense: equality between opposed parts (left and right) that are interchangeable with respect to the whole [quoted from Stanford Encyclopedia, p.72]. Used in the arts (Bach; Goethe: "architecture is frozen music") before twentieth-century science [p.72].
- Types: bilateral/mirror; radial/rotational (some mandalas); translational (railroad tracks; Escher's glide reflections). Invariance or equivalence is the defining feature [p.72-73]. Only 17 plane symmetry patterns exist; the Alhambra shows all; Isfahan's quasi-crystalline tiling [p.75-76].
- Mirror therapy for phantom limb (Ramachandran) [p.73]; Thornhill and Gangestad on symmetry and sexual attraction (scent, physiology) [p.74].
- Highest symmetry arises from two opposite directions: perfectly ordered structures (crystal lattices) and complete randomization (chaos, like an inert gas) [p.75].

### Symmetry and emergence; Pauli's mirror complex [p.76-79]
- **1956 Lee and Yang:** parity (reflection symmetry) not conserved in weak-force beta decay; front-page news (*New York Times*, 16 Jan 1957). Pauli's first reaction [Pauli, quoted, p.77]: "I do not believe however that the Lord God is a WEAK left-hander." Markus Fierz told Pauli he had a "mirror complex" (in dreams and in his physics). For Pauli the relation of physics and psychology "is that of a mirror image" [quoted]. He sought restored symmetry by including more variables (CPT theorem).
- **Pauli to Jung [Pauli, quoted, p.77]:** if parapsychological phenomena go deeper, "the psyche has to be taken into consideration so as to be able to see the full symmetry of the phenomenon. ... The question of 'how deep or how broad does one have to go to achieve full symmetry' ultimately seems to lead back to the problem ... of the separation of the self from the ego."
- **Jung's last personal letter to Pauli [Jung, quoted, p.77-78]:** "a constellated, i.e., activated, archetype may not be the cause but is certainly a condition of synchronistic phenomena"; occurrences "might be expected that correspond to the archetype as a sort of mirror image"; the unconscious's statements (UFO legends, dreams) point to "a 'slight left-handedness in God,'" a prevalence of the unconscious. Individuation's goal of wholeness "should mean that the mirror-image effect, which dazzles us, would be removed ... by an 'asymmetrical' Third, which prefers one direction," toward greater differentiation of consciousness; "it is precisely the infinitesimal, psychological factors, overlooked by all, that shake the foundations of our world."
- **The psychoid archetype and asymmetry [Jung, quoted, p.78]:** "the psychoid archetype, where 'psychic' and 'material' are no longer viable as attributes or where the category of opposites becomes obsolete and every occurrence can only be asymmetrical; the reason for this is that an occurrence can only be the one or the other when it proceeds from an indistinguishable One."
- [Cambray] Here Jung places the deepest psychological development and synchronicity in the realm of the asymmetric, through small breaks in symmetry.

### Jung's diagrams of the Self [p.79-81]
- In *Aion*, four octahedrons (Lapis Quaternio through Anthropos Quaternio) show highly symmetric networks (fig. 6). The unpublished sketch sent to Victor White (21 May 1948; fig. 7; printed 2007 via Ann Lammers) has the top octahedron rotated 90 degrees and diagonal lines omitted from the published version, reducing symmetry to a single mirror plane [p.80]. [Cambray] This suggests an impulse to break out of excessive ordering and remain open to the numinous; Jung may have over-symmetrized, like Einstein clinging to a static cosmos [p.81].

### Symmetry breaking and synchronicity [p.81-86]
- Linear systems: whole equals sum of parts, with redundancy; symmetry gives beauty and calm; imitative learning builds symmetry (root of empathy). In complex systems "no single aspect has adequate information to represent the whole"; symmetry breaks in **phase transitions**, rapid reorganizations, psychologically stressful but transformative [p.81-83].
- **Cosmic symmetry breaks:** (1) matter slightly exceeding antimatter between about 10^-35 and 10^-11 seconds after the Big Bang; (2) **homochirality** of life: Pasteur's 1848 separation of mirror-image tartrate crystals (fig. 8) showed molecular handedness; life uses left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars; the origin is unknown [p.83-84]. [Cambray] The original selection of chiral molecules is a candidate for a synchronistic moment of the kind Jung's 1959 letter to Neumann described [p.84].
- **Development:** early mother-infant symmetric wholeness; Winnicott (via Ogden) on insulating the baby from coincidences, "chance simultaneities of events that take place in the infant's internal and external realities at a time when the two are only beginning to be differentiated" [Ogden, quoted p.85]; developmental milestones as phase transitions (Spitz); the father classically as "asymmetric third" [p.85-86].
- **Symmetry and objectivity:** objective = invariant across observers; unique, unrepeatable, idiosyncratic events are subjective and asymmetric. Synchronicity is "the study of such events where the meaningful experience of the person ... can be understood by others ... but the unique quality of the experience cannot be wholly communicated." Grappling with it requires "a breaking of the symmetry between inner and outer aspects of the event"; the initial felt symmetry draws attention, but development "requires that we suffer awareness of the asymmetry" [Cambray, p.86].

## Chapter 4. Empathy and the Analytic Field [Cambray] [p.87-106]

### Resonance and the empty mind [p.87-88]
- Cambray earlier proposed classifying synchronistic events in therapy by intensity vs. frequency (power-law test, self-organizing criticality) [p.87].
- **Resonance** (tuning fork, glass shattering) as attunement among agents in a field [p.87-88]. Needham's anecdote: a Taoist monk answers that the I Ching's fundamental idea "can be expressed in one single word, Resonance (kan)" [quoted, p.88]. The "empty" mind is not blank but nonattached, receptive to archetypal possibilities; Chuang Tzu: "When the perfect man employs his mind, it is a mirror. It conducts nothing and anticipates nothing" [quoted, p.88]. Compare Jung's attitude toward dreams and Bion on entering the analytic field.

### Empathy history [p.89-90]
- *Einfuehlung* ("feeling-into"), coined 1873 by Robert Vischer (art historian); translated "empathy" by Titchener in 1909; Theodor Lipps: inner imitation of others' gestures, expression and tone, "a unique form of knowledge," not inference from analogy. Both Freud and Jung derived their views from Lipps. Jung: "the projection transfers unconscious contents into the object, for which reason empathy is also termed 'transference' in analytical psychology" [Jung, quoted, p.89-90]. Kohut's "vicarious introspection" is said to downplay immersive affective components [p.90].

### Neuroscience [p.90-99]
- Mind as emergent from body/brain ("embodied cognition"). Cambray recalls Jung's remark that the body-soul relation may be synchronistic, and Jung's line that "Outside the realm of psychophysical parallelism ... synchronicity is not a phenomenon whose regularity is at all easy to demonstrate" [Jung, quoted, p.91].
- **Emotional contagion** (the Orson Welles broadcast; crowds); emotions transmit in milliseconds; mimicry increases prosocial behavior; Self as inherently social [p.91-92]. fMRI similarities between feeling and observing an emotion; universal in mammals; disorders (autism, sociopathy) [p.92].
- Decety's model: shared neural representations, self-awareness, mental flexibility, emotion regulation [p.93]. Development: infant imitation; pretense at 18 to 24 months; **theory of mind** and false belief at about 4 to 6 (Wimmer and Perner); scrub jays; positive attachment helps empathy, abuse and neglect inhibit it [p.93-95].
- **Mirror neurons** (Rizzolatti, Parma; macaques): fire when observing or performing an action, tied to goal and anticipation; human homologues in Broca's area, premotor cortex; extend to touch, sound, pain (affective not sensory component of pain) [p.95]. Iacoboni's hierarchy: strictly congruent, broadly congruent, logically related, and "super" mirror neurons (possibly "a wonderfully simple neural distinction between self and other") [p.96]; shaped by social experience from the mother-infant dyad on. Culture and biology meet here, "a means of exploring the psychoid realm" [p.96].
- **Simulation theory** (Goldman): mind-reading by attenuated mimicry; joint and role play improve mind-reading, solitary pretense does not; applied to supervision and to active imagination (trying on personifications) [p.96-98]. Mirror-neuron deficits proposed in autism; attachment-injured patients scanning for microshifts [p.98].
- **Ethics:** Prinz: "a bad imitator is likely to form unstable attachments"; psychopaths imitate behavior but not emotional states; media violence and addiction cues. **Empathy is not sympathy**: a torturer can use it; "The capacity for empathic attunement is thus morally neutral" [p.98-99].
- **Cambray's thesis [p.99]:** empathy is a temporary **symmetrizing** linking Self and Other in a unifying field; reflection requires eventually **breaking** that symmetry. "Empathy then is a connecting principle." Because its causes are often unconscious and psychoid, "there can be a synchronistic field dimension to our empathic experiences."

### The analytic field [p.99-104]
- The collective unconscious as the network of all archetypes, "formal patterns, without content," realized as affect-laden, often numinous images; scale-free, so a single archetype contains its own reduced-scale network (amplification). Jacobi/Edinger models relax rigidity across generations [p.99-100].
- **Indra's net again** (Hua-yen; Fa Tsang; *Indra's Pearls* cover image "The Glowing Limit": a lacework emerging from five touching circles): interdependence, a radical field model [p.100-101]. "Constellated" archetypal nodes show the psyche as open to a field of interactions among individuals [p.101].
- **Jung's field model of the transference** (*Psychology of the Transference*) as adapted by Mario Jacoby (fig. 9): patient (P), analyst (A) and the unconscious of each, linked by paths a to f. Path "b" is unconscious affective resonance, which Jung variously calls participation mystique, "psychic" or "unconscious infection," "mutual unconsciousness" (cf. Kleinian projective identification) [p.101-102].
- **Clinical case [Cambray, p.102-104]:** a man in his early thirties with obsessional difficulties, last session of a long day; Cambray felt trapped and exhausted, nearly flu-ill but fine next day. At the session's end the patient reported a dream of a figure in a closet. Recognizing his own affect and somatic state as an ingested "projection," Cambray practiced cognitive empathy toward his own state (path "c") and then raised the dream the next week. The patient identified the figure as a child, then disclosed a previously unmentioned childhood illness whose symptoms resembled Cambray's after-session state (Cambray had had a serious childhood illness at roughly that age). The "mercurial child" in the closet became understood as lost spontaneity. Cambray calls the transitory but meaningful illness "the synchronistic aspect in the psychosomatic field," an "objective intuition" useful only once conscious [p.104].
- **Conclusion [p.104-106]:** mirror neurons may operate as "field resonators"; the analytic "third" emerges from the combined pathways and is "a property of the field not simply in/of the analyst," supporting the "wounded-healer" pattern. Mirror neurons help instantiate the transcendent function as "an embodied, psychosomatic reality." Ramachandran calls mirror neurons "the single most important 'unreported' story of the decade"; possible link to Dawkins' memes. Cambray: "an underlying synchronistic core to empathic experience that can be described in terms of a resonant field" [p.106].

## Chapter 5. Cultural Synchronicities [Cambray] [p.107-126]

Definition: "cultural synchronicities" are intensely emergent, synchronistic events that involve collective phenomena, a cultural Zeitgeist, or the meeting of cultures [p.107].

### The emergence of democracy [p.107-111]
- Fifth-century BCE Athens: a new "political time." **Kairos** shifts from "critical place or appropriate and just measure" to "the act of decision in the intensity of the briefest possible moment" [Csapo and Miller, quoted, p.108]; metaphors of hitting the mark in archery and passing the shuttle through the warp (Onians) [p.108].
- Jung uses kairos dynamically: "We are living in what the Greeks called the kairos—the right moment—for a 'metamorphosis of the gods'" [Jung, quoted, p.108]. Von Franz: the association of kairos with goddesses weaving time suggests "a 'field' in which 'meaningful connections' are interwoven like threads of a fabric" [quoted, p.109].
- **Consensus as synchronistic moment:** the demos's consensus is "an act of creation of the whole that has a synchronistic falling together in time at its core," often marked by the numinous or a shared "third"; without reflection, "the demos can fall apart into a mob" [Cambray, p.109]. *Demos* from "fat of the land" (Onians): place first, then community.
- **Cleisthenes (508 BCE):** after the Spartan invasion he redistributed the population by lottery into ten new "tribes" mixing farmers, seafarers and merchants; a council of five hundred, short terms, wide participation. Cambray reads this as an intuition of **scale-free networks of networks**: robust, adaptive, but vulnerable at hubs; emergent systems are morally ambiguous and require staying near the edge of order and chaos [p.110-111].
- Extension to organizations: conflict resolution and mediation; the emergent, asymmetric "third" is "heralded by a synchronistic event" [p.111].

### The "Conquest" of Mexico [p.112-117]
- Brief history: Cortes sails 18 Feb 1519, lands Good Friday 22 April 1519, allies with Aztec rivals, enters Tenochtitlan, is driven out July 1520, and returns to take the city on 13 August 1521 [p.112].
- **Quetzalcoatl myth (Carrasco, Nicholson):** a Toltec priest-king, deceived by a sorcerer, fled east; expected return; the year **1 Reed (1519)** matched his prophesied return to strike down kings; depictions as bearded and light-skinned; ten omens; Moctezuma's dread ("as if it was washed in chili water"), which Carrasco calls Otto's "creature feeling" of numinous dread [p.113]. Aztec myth held four previous eras ended in disaster [p.114].
- Pizarro and the Inca ruler Atahuallpa, who is said to have believed Viracocha had returned at the appointed time [p.114].
- **Caution [Cambray, p.114]:** the coincidences were used "in the service of greed and domination"; "The catastrophic results ... should serve as a clear warning to any general idealization of synchronicities as evidence of utopian spirituality." Robert Aziz identifies pathological reactions when self and experience are not differentiated, including misusing coincidence for power or grandiosity. Jung's colonial attitudes are noted; synchronicity's erosion of subject/object boundaries "moves us toward postcolonial discourse" [p.115].
- **Mirrors:** Moctezuma's omen of a bird with a mirror in its crown; Tezcatlipoca ("smoky mirror") showed Quetzalcoatl his human face and he fled; Cortes's armor as a mirror; Bergland on Toltec mirrors and an Aztec poem about the artist who "maintains dialogue with his heart" [p.115-116]. Cambray: dialogue was foreclosed; "Recognizing the mutuality, however asymmetric, of a moment of synchronicity is the first step in seeing the field one is in. When this is refused then the (archetypal) enactment is passed on; one cannot become identified with a god without great risk of sharing the fate of that god" [p.116-117]. Synchronicities "alert us to these gaps, to challenge us to see the emergent rather than magical wishes or fears" [p.117].
- **Captain Cook and Lono (Hawaii, 1779):** arrived during the ritual return of Lono (peace), returned a month later when the season had shifted to Ku (war), and was killed; debate noted (Obeyesekere, Sahlins) [p.117].

### The discovery of phosphorus [p.118-121]
- **Hennig Brandt**, Hamburg alchemist, c. 1669: seeking the philosopher's stone, he isolated a self-glowing substance from boiled urine; named from Greek "light bringing" (Latin Lucifer, the morning star, linked by Cambray to the Quetzalcoatl image) [p.118].
- Chain of chance meetings: Daniel Kraft's demonstrations; Leibniz present at Hanover, then meeting Brandt in Hamburg and finding him work; Kraft's 1677 demonstration to Robert Boyle, whose experiments on phosphorus are called the first in chemistry ("aerial nocticula"). Cambray calls phosphorus "the pivotal element in the changing approach to the world" [p.118-119].
- Projections: miraculous healing, aphrodisiac "flammula vitae" (injurious ointments); the Enlightenment's "light" and its Luciferian shadow; *lumen naturae* (Jung, "On the Nature of the Psyche") [p.119]. Dark history: "lucifer" matches, child match-factory labor (legislation 1864; Andersen's "The Little Match Girl," 1845), phosphorus bombs, **Operation Gomorrah on Hamburg, July 1943** (25 square kilometers destroyed; 800,000 homeless; Emsley's figures); lilacs and chestnuts bloomed in autumn 1943 [p.119-120].
- [Cambray] Leibniz's involvement, the timing and the unfolding use "act as a 'mirror' reflecting a dark genius." **Extended definition:** the story can be read as one emergent process across centuries; this "broadens the notion of synchronicity beyond the moment of initial occurrence of a meaningful coincidence to include extensions of emergent events throughout a protracted period of time, not based on or reducible to simple cause and effect," treated as a field phenomenon, with the caveat that cut-off points are arbitrary [p.120-121].

### Serendipity [p.121-126]
- Coined 1754 by Horace Walpole from "The Three Princes of Serendip" (Peregrinaggio, 1557): "making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things which they were not in quest of" [Walpole, quoted, p.121]. Not every lucky accident is a synchronicity; it can take research to tell [p.122].
- **Penicillin:** Fleming's 1921 lysozyme discovery; 1928 petri dish with a rare mold from a floor below (asthma work), spores landing at the exact moment, a heat wave breaking and returning; Fleming's cultivated curiosity for the unexpected. Then Florey, Chain, Heatley at Oxford (not seeking a drug), the 1941 move to Peoria, Illinois (corn steep liquor), Mary Hunt's cantaloupe mold (3,000 times more penicillin), production sufficient for D-Day, 1945 Nobel Prize [p.122-124]. Urban legend of Fleming and Churchill noted as apocryphal (n. 38).
- Ernest Jones on Freud's *Interpretation of Dreams* as "perfect example of serendipity" [quoted, p.125]. **Mirror neurons' discovery** (a monkey observed someone eating in the lab) recalled with a mythic, forgotten-details quality: "brain mythology with neuroscientific verification of the archetypal truths" [p.125].
- **Attribution of meaning:** whether serendipities are truly synchronistic can be debated; modeling in the complexity paradigm lets one detect self-organizing features. "Synchronicity then is broadened to that aspect of our narrative truth that is not based on cause and effect rationality but reveals itself through the emergence of self-organizing features that evoke a feeling of surprise, from curiously mild serendipities to stunning coincidences of great significance" [Cambray, p.126].

## Afterword [Cambray] [p.127-130]

- The work changed the author's views of science "at least equally" as it applied science to psychology (Jacob wrestling the angel) [p.127].
- **Central claim:** Jung's intuition of "principles of psychic ordering and organizing involved in acts of creation in time, to be placed on an equivalent footing with space, time, and causality" identifies "the organizing principle that is at the origins of the appearance of space, time, light, and matter." Cambray believes this is what Jung meant by **psychoid**: "the capacity or propensity for organization that emerged out of the hypothesized singularity," so that self-organization is linked to synchronicity and the psychoid "would hold the principle that has allowed the emergence of everything, including the mind and soul" [p.127-128].
- Jung's roots: Naturphilosophie and holism; his field theory leaned classical, though Pauli and Einstein helped him approach relativistic ideas [p.128].
- **Clinical suggestions:** symmetry breaking, "moments of complexity," fears and defenses against emergence; a spectrum of synchronistic intensities with empathy, resonance, enactments, projective identification and unconscious communication all having "synchronistic cores" [p.128-129].
- Network theory of the collective unconscious; cultural complexes; synchronicity as postcolonial concept (not claiming Jung personally completed that transformation); leadership that "ignores the kairos of events can quickly get out of sync with the people it is meant to represent" [p.129-130].
- **Closing leitmotif:** the mirror (Pauli's mirror complex, Jung's symmetry-breaking diagram, parity violation, mirror neurons, cultural mirrors); synchronicity, with its notion of equivalence of physical and psychological, "offers a new dimension to what mirroring is and how we experience it" [p.130].
