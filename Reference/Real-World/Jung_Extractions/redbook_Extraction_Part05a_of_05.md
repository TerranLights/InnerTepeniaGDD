## Overview

This range is the first half of "Scrutinies," the last section of *Liber Novus*. The range starts in the middle of the section: a long, harsh address by the narrator to his own "I" (ego), followed by dialogues in 1914 between the narrator, his "I," and his "soul," who has "followed the God" upward and speaks from a height. The soul predicts solitude and sacrifice; an old nameless man tells the narrator to leave science; the narrator is told that belief alone is no longer adequate and that knowledge is needed. The First World War breaks out, and the voices go silent for a year. They return in autumn 1915 with an old man named in the text as Philemon (printed in Greek capitals in the book, checked visually on PDF p.368; the text file renders it as garbled "cIlIAHMQN"), who speaks about gold, about teaching and then disavowing, and about the limits of self-sacrifice. The narrator then works through redemption as work done on oneself, the experience of God as sickness, the self and God, and the refusal of "selfless" love. In December 1915 to January 1916 he meets the shades of the dead (a dead woman who gave him Egyptian mysteries, who asks him for "blood"), a symbol called HAP, the dead one's insistence on solitude, God's will and "the church," and a long dispute with the soul, who is made to accept confinement and who hides a jewel that proves to be love. A vision of fire and the figure of Abraxas as "ruler of this world" lead to the climax of this range: the dead return from Jerusalem, asking for his "light," and Philemon delivers to them the first four sermons (and the start of the fifth) of the "Seven Sermons to the Dead," on the Pleroma, differentiation, God and the devil, Abraxas, the multiplicity of the Gods, and spirituality and sexuality. After each sermon, in this version, a short dialogue between the narrator and Philemon follows.

The soul "she," the "I," the dead, the Gods and daimons are all figures within the narrator's inner visions; editorial footnotes mark which passages are not in the original Black Books.

The editorial footnotes date each passage against the Black Books (the original diaries) and give Jung's later comments.

## 1. The address to the "I" (continuation of a section that began on an earlier page) [p.365]

*The section begins in mid-chapter in this range; the heading and opening are in the previous part.* Dated by footnote April 21, 1914. [note: footnote 8 (editor) says Jung later described this self-criticism as the confrontation with the shadow, and quotes his 1934 essay: whoever looks into the mirror of the water sees first his own image; the mirror shows the face "we never show to the world because we cover it with the persona"; seeing and bearing one's own shadow brings up "the personal unconscious" (CW 9, I, paras. 43-44).]

The narrator speaks in the second person to his own "I" ("brother I," "beast"), listing its faults:
- Boundless ambition and vanity: it acts not for the matter but for its own advantage and general recognition; it works not for humanity but for self-interest. The narrator promises it a "prickly crown of iron."
- Calculated cleverness: it speaks skillfully, adjusts light and shade, proclaims its own honorableness and good faith, exploits others' good faith, plays at modesty hoping others will mention its merit, and is hurt when they do not.
- Hypocritical composure: it preaches calm but in a real test is consumed by rage and dreams of revenge.
- Gloating, resentment, envy of other people's well-being, which it denies.
- Private thinking that suits only itself, with the pretense that it is above humanity and not responsible. The narrator insists the "I" is responsible to humanity for everything it thinks, feels and does, and that there is no real difference between thinking and doing.
- Shamelessness where no one sees; it would be mortally offended if another said these things, despite knowing they are true. It reproaches others for failings without having bettered itself; its grounds are "webs of lies covering a dirty corner." It judges others because it has no order within itself.
- It "thinks by means of" other people, using them as figures on its inner stage, which is a shameful act of power equal to the one it condemns in others who claim to love their fellows but exploit them.
- Its "love" is declared to be self-interest and desire; the greater its words about love, the more pathetic. The narrator tells it to be silent about love and to speak instead of its shame.

The narrator declares what he will do: drag out what is concealed, crush its superiority, burn out the contents it was proud of so that it becomes "empty like a poured-out vessel," proud only of its emptiness. [p.365] "You should be a vessel of life, so kill your idols." Freedom does not belong to the "I," but form; not power, but suffering and conceiving. Its self-contempt should be made a virtue, laid out like a carpet for others to walk on.

The theme of taming: "If I tame you, beast, I give others the opportunity to tame their beasts. The taming begins with you, my I, nowhere else." The narrator will whip the I until it endures the wildness of others; if wronged it must forgive in its heavy heart and not by lip service. Its sensitivity is "your particular form of violence." It should be able to bear being called dirty when pure, and having its greed praised as a virtue. "You are not your soul"; the soul is with the fiery God who "flamed up to the roof of the heavens." The I's plans for revenge are called childish, "you cannot take revenge on fate" (it might as well lash the sea); better to build bridges. The wish to be understood is rejected: "Understand yourself and you will be sufficiently understood." [p.365]

Closing paragraph [numbered {2} in the book]: after saying these and other angry words, the narrator begins to bear being alone with himself; touchiness still stirs and he lashes himself again, until even the pleasure in self-torment fades. [p.365] [note: footnote 9 says this paragraph is not in Black Book 5 and that on April 30, 1914 Jung resigned as lecturer in the medical faculty of the University of Zurich.]

## 2. The soul above, the burdened I, and the prediction of solitude (May to June 1914) [pp.366-367]

**Voice of the soul (footnote date May 8, 1914).** One night the narrator hears the soul's voice from afar: "How distant you are!" She has "become sunlike" and received "the seeds of fire." He is "down on the murky earth, in the dark smoke that the fire left us." She feels the heaviness of the earth and old pain reaching her; he asks her not to descend, because he wants what he works on to stay sunlike, and says he never wants to see her in the flesh again. He asks her to tell him something from the depths; she cannot, "since your creative source flows from there." On his uncertainty: "The uncertain way is the good way: Upon it lie possibilities. Be unwavering and create." The narrator hears wings: the bird rises above the clouds into the brilliance of the Godhead. [p.366]

**The I burdened with the dead (May 21, 1914).** The narrator turns to his "brother, the I," who stands sad and sighing; a voice speaks from the narrator: "It is hard-the sacrificed fall left and right-and you will be crucified for the sake of life." The I says it is bitter and that it suffers; the narrator says he knows but "it is not to be altered," though he does not know what it means (the passage is dated two months before the outbreak of war). Looking up, he asks the soul and hears her happy: "Much happiness has happened to me. I rise higher, my wings grow." He accuses her of living "from the blood of the human heart"; she answers that no drink is dearer. He says he knows divinity is not humanity, that "the divine consumes the human," and that he is enslaved to her. She says the victims fall by necessity, not his cruelty: "The way of life is sown with fallen ones." The narrator sees "a battlefield." [p.366]

The narrator asks the I why it groans; it says it loads itself with the dead and cannot haul their number. The narrator (not understanding his I) calls it a pagan and cites "let the dead bury their dead" [note: footnote 12 gives Matthew 8:21-22]; the I pities the fallen who cannot reach the light. The narrator says their souls did what they could and met fate: "Your compassion is sick." The soul calls from afar: "Leave him compassion, compassion binds life and death." The narrator is stung that she speaks of compassion having risen "following the God without compassion" and asks her why she did it (footnote date May 23, 1914). She says it is not meant for her to be in his world and that she would soil herself there; it was "inner necessity. I belong to the Above." He asks whether anyone suffered an irreplaceable loss through her leaving; she says on the contrary he enjoyed "utmost benefit." When he says human feeling gives doubt, she asks why what he sees should always be untrue, and says he cannot stop making a fool of himself. He says he doubts out of love for men; she says no, out of his own weakness, doubt and disbelief: "There is a divine and a human intention. They cross each other in stupid and godforsaken people, to whom you also belong from time to time." [p.366]

**Retrospective reflection on belief and knowledge.** The narrator says that since this happened two months before the war and nothing visible corresponded to it, he tried to read it as merely personal and so could neither understand nor believe it. He then argues: belief is weak in our time and it is better so; we have outgrown the childhood in which mere belief best brought people to the good; we need knowledge more than belief, and strong belief would hinder attaining knowledge. Belief is strong but empty, and too little of the whole person is involved if the life with God rests on belief alone. Men with understanding should "wrestle for knowledge to the best of their ability." Belief and knowledge both fall short; they must strike a balance. Belief gives no security and wealth of knowing; desire for knowledge sometimes takes away too much belief. [pp.366-367]

It is also dangerous to believe too much: everyone must find his own way and meets in himself "a beyond full of strange and mighty things," and taking everything literally with too much belief he "would be nothing but a lunatic." "We need differentiating knowledge to clear up the confusion which the discovery of the soul has brought in." It is better to await better knowledge before accepting things too believingly. [p.367] [note: footnote 14 says these last two paragraphs are not in Black Book 5, quotes Jung's 1912 view that belief should be replaced by understanding, and his 1945 letter to Victor White that he began his career "repudiating everything that smelt of belief."]

**Acceptance and solitude (May 24, 1914).** The narrator asks the soul whether all must be accepted; she says it is to be accepted. He is terrified by the solitude and the madness that befalls the solitary; she says she long predicted solitude and he need not fear madness. His "misunderstood fear" is his disbelief and doubt; he does not want to believe in the size of the sacrifice required. "Greatness requires greatness. You still want to be too cheap." He says he fears committing injustice against men by going his own way; she says there is no avoidance: "You must go your way, unconcerned about others, no matter whether they are good or bad. You have laid your hand on the divine, which those have not." He fears deception and prefers to speak with men, yet feels driven toward solitude and fears the solitude of his thinking that departs from accustomed paths. To his plea that resisting solitude is hopeless she says: "It is utterly hopeless. You should be forced into your work." [p.367]

**The nameless old man.** An old man with a white beard and haggard face approaches [note: footnote 16 says Black Book 4 adds that he is like one of the old saints, one of the first Christians who lived in the desert]. He says: "I am a nameless one, one of the many who lived and died in solitude. The spirit of the times and the acknowledged truth required this from us." He tells the narrator he must learn this: "Things have been too good for you." The narrator asks whether this necessity holds in a different time; the old man says it is as true today as yesterday: "you must bleed for the goal of humanity," practice solitude without grumbling so that everything becomes ready, become serious, and take leave of science, which has "too much childishness in it." "Your way goes toward the depths. Science is too superficial, mere language, mere tools. But you must set to work." The narrator does not know what work is his; endless sadness seizes him for many days. [p.367] [note: footnote 18 gives the Black Book 5 continuation, where the soul says science is "a new version of scholasticism" that must be surmounted and should be treated as "merely your language," not abandoned; the soul says "Forever above yourself and the present" and "You must put in extra work."]

**The old man's sayings (May 25, 1914).** One night an old man's voice, slow and heavy, utters disconnected sentences that terrify the narrator with fear of madness: "It is not yet the evening of days. The worst comes last. The hand that strikes first, strikes best. Nonsense streams from the deepest wells, amply like the Nile. Morning is more beautiful than night. Flowers smell until they fade. Ripeness comes as late as possible in spring, or else it misses its purpose." The narrator finds them "dreadfully meaningless"; his I squirms in pain and wails about the burden of dead it seems to carry, "a thousand dead." [p.367]

**End of the first period (June 24, 1914).** The sadness lasts until June 24, 1914. That night the soul says: "The greatest comes to the smallest." Nothing more is said, and then the war breaks out. The war opens the narrator's eyes about what he had experienced and gives him the courage to say what he has written in the earlier part of the book. [p.367] [note: footnote 21 quotes Black Book 5 here, an exclamation at taking up "this book" again, "my written unconscious," and says this is the one reference to "the unconscious" in Black Books 2 to 7.]

## 3. The osprey and the return of Philemon (1915) [p.368]

**{3} The osprey (June 3, 1915).** The voices of the depths stay silent for a whole year. In summer, alone on the water, the narrator sees an osprey plunge, seize a large fish and rise into the sky with it. His soul says: "That is a sign that what is below is borne upward." [note: footnote 22 says that in the interim Jung wrote the draft of the preceding books of *Liber Novus*, gave a talk in Aberdeen on July 28, 1914 on the importance of the unconscious in psychopathology, and did military service in August 1914, January to March 1915 (64 days) and March 1915.]

**Philemon on gold and being used (September 14 and 15, 1915).** On an autumn night an old man's voice speaks; the narrator knows it is Philemon (ΦΙΛΗΜΩΝ in Greek capitals; see overview). He says he wants to turn the narrator around, master him, "emboss you like a coin," do business with him: "You should pass from hand to hand. Self-willing is not for you. You are the will of the whole." [note: footnote 24 quotes Jung's November 1915 letter to Hans Schmid: understanding is "a terribly binding power," possibly "soul murder" when it levels out vital differences; symbols keep their secrets, and once understanding is general the symbol is ripe for destruction; it also tells a dream of a spring that had to be led back into the earth. Footnote 25 says Black Book 5 continues "Hermes is your daimon."]

Philemon then speaks at length about gold: it is no master of its own will yet rules the whole; despised and greedily desired; it lies silent with a bright face, "a king that needs no proof of its power." Everyone seeks it, few find it, even the smallest piece is esteemed; everyone denies depending on it yet secretly stretches a hand to it. Its necessity is proven by men's longing. It "ruins those to be ruined and helps those on the rise to ascend." It is a blazing hoard waiting for the taker; the greater the tribulations, the more it is esteemed; it grows from underground in the molten lava, hidden in veins and rocks, and men exert all cunning to dig it out. [note: footnote 26 refers to Jung's later discussion of the alchemical symbolism of gold in *Mysterium Coniunctionis*.]

**On teaching and disavowal (September 15, 1915).** The narrator exclaims at the ambiguous speech. Philemon says: "Not only to teach, but also to disavow, or why then did I teach?" If he does not teach he need not disavow; having taught he must disavow, because teaching means giving others what they should have acquired for themselves: "What he acquires is good, but the gift that was not acquired is bad." To "waste oneself" means to want to suppress many; deceit surrounds the giver and he must revoke his gift and deny his virtue. The burden of silence is no greater than the burden of the self that he wants to load on others; so he speaks and teaches, and the listener may defend himself against the ruse by which he is burdened. "The best truth is also such a skillful deception" that he entangles himself in it until he realizes the worth of a successful ruse. The narrator says men have deceived themselves about Philemon so he deceives them, but "he who fathoms you, fathoms himself." [p.368]

## 4. Reflections on mutual burdens, redemption, and the self [pp.368-370]

*Narrator's reflections after Philemon retires into "the shimmering cloud of uncertainty" (September 17, 1915).*

**Virtue as mutual enchantment [p.368].** The narrator thinks high barriers are needed between people, less against mutual burdens than against "mutual virtues"; so-called Christian morality of the time makes for "mutual enchantment." One should at least bear one's own burden. If I accept self-forgetting virtue I become the other's tyrant and must then make another my master; this underpins society but damages the individual, who learns to live from others instead of from himself. If everyone surrendered themselves that would be folly. Living with oneself is not beautiful or pleasant but "serves the redemption of the self." Giving oneself up makes one one's own slave, "the opposite of accepting oneself"; then "one is lived by the self": "One does not live one's self; it lives itself." Self-forgetting virtue is "an unnatural alienation from one's own essence" and it is a sin to alienate the other from his self by one's own virtuousness, e.g., by taking over his burden; the sin rebounds. [p.368] [note: footnotes 29 and 30 quote Nietzsche's *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* ("Of the despisers of the body"; "Of love of one's neighbour") as underlined in Jung's copy; footnote 29 gives Jung's 1935 seminar remark that he saw in Nietzsche's "Self" an Eastern concept, "an Atman idea."]

**Redemption (September 18, 1915) [p.369].** It is submission enough to subjugate ourselves to our self. "The work of redemption is always first to be done on ourselves." This cannot be done without love of ourselves, and need not be done at all if one can bear the given condition; the tiresome feeling of needing redemption can finally become too much, and then one enters the work. The narrator argues that every sense of beauty should be removed from the idea of redemption: the Romans did not find "the hanged Jew" tasteful, and the catacomb enthusiasm had no pleasant shimmer for them. One "blunders into" the work unintentionally; one should count oneself among the sick, not among the overhealthy who want to impart abundance to others.

Hence the other is not to be used for one's own supposed redemption: "The other is no stepping stone for our feet." The need for redemption often shows as increased need for love with which we think to make the other happy; yet we love others for our own change of condition. We do also need others; but love for them is not and should not be selfless: that would be a lie. "In terms of the self selfless love is a veritable sin." We must often go to ourselves to re-establish connection with the self, which is torn apart "not only by our vices but also by our virtues," since both want to live outside. What we neglect in ourselves blends secretly into our actions toward others. "Through uniting with the self we reach the God." [p.369] [note: footnote 32 links this to Jung's 1941 remark that integration of the self begins from the conscious side by giving an account of one's egotistical aims; footnote 33 quotes Black Book 5 ("which unites Heaven and Hell in itself") and Jung's 1941 phrase *unio oppositorum*.]

**Experience of God as sickness [p.369].** The narrator says he states this not from ancient authorities but because it happened in him, unexpectedly and unwanted. He cannot deny that the experience seized him beyond measure and continues working in him: "if it is a deception, then deception is my God." No insight or objection overcomes the experience; he could furnish a theory explaining it, but the theory would not remove the knowledge of having experienced God. He does not "believe" it; it is improbable and impossible for understanding; he is like persons overcome by delusion. "But I must say that the God makes us sick." A living God afflicts reason like a sickness, fills the soul with intoxication and reeling chaos: "How many will the God break?"

**The self and God [pp.369-370].** God appears to us in a certain state of the soul; "we reach the God through the self." "Not the self is God," though God is "behind the self, above the self, the self itself, when he appears." He appears as a sickness from which we must heal ourselves, "since he is also our heaviest wound." The God's power first lies in the self, because the self is entirely in God; "we must wrestle with the God for the self," since God is an unfathomable movement that sweeps the self into the boundless, into dissolution. When God appears we are "powerless, captivated, divided, sick, poisoned with the strongest poison, but drunk with the highest health." We cannot stay in this state, because the body's powers are consumed "like fat in the flames," so we must strive to free the self from God in order to live. [p.370] [note: footnote 34 gives Jung's later definitions: the I is the center of the field of consciousness, the self the subject of the total psyche (1921); the self as archetype of order, indistinguishable from God-images (*Aion*); the term chosen as definite enough for human wholeness and indefinite enough to express its indescribable nature (1944). Footnote 36 quotes Jung (1929): the gods "have become diseases."]

Reason can easily deny God and speak only of sickness, then heal the sick part, "but it will be a healing with loss"; we go on living "lamed by the God. Where the fire blazed dead ashes lie." The narrator prefers the living wonders of the God: "The ashes are suicide to me." "My life wants itself whole." He must therefore serve his self: "The service of the self is therefore divine service and the service of mankind. If I carry myself I relieve mankind of myself and heal my self from the God." The God he experienced "is more than love; he is also hate," more than beauty, also the abomination; more than wisdom, also meaninglessness; more than power, also powerlessness; "more than omnipresence, he is also my creature." [p.370] [note: footnote 37 gives Black Book 5's continuation: each should carry his own load; "To give all one's goods to the poor means to educate them to become idle"; pity should be "a strict educator"; "Solitude with ourselves has no end. It has only just begun." Footnote 39 notes Jung's underlining in Meister Eckhart: the soul would have to lose God.]

## 5. Philemon: "Enter the grave of the God"; the three shades; HAP [pp.370-371]

**Philemon's instruction [p.370].** The following night the voice of Philemon says: "Draw nearer, enter into the grave of the God. The place of your work should be in the vault. The God should not live in you, but you should live in the God." The narrator, who had intended to free himself from God, is disturbed that Philemon advises him to go deeper into God. He reflects that since the God ascended to the upper realms, Philemon has become different: first a magician in a distant land, then near, then the giver of an intoxicating foreign language; most of what the narrator wrote in the earlier part of the book was given to him by Philemon; now Philemon "assumed a form distinct from me" and goes other ways. [note: footnote 41 says two paragraphs here are not in Black Book 5; footnote 40 says the voice is not identified as Philemon's in Black Book 5.]

**{4} The three shades (December 2, 1915).** Several weeks later three shades approach; their chilly breath shows they are dead. The first is a woman, whose whirring recalls a sun beetle; the narrator recognizes her as a woman who, when alive, had revealed to him the Egyptian mysteries, the red sun disk and the song of the golden wings. She speaks brokenly: "It was night when I died-you still live in the day." She asks for "the word," then "The symbol, the mediator, we need the symbol." The narrator does not know it; she says he can reach for it, and a sign is placed in his hand. She cries out joyfully that it is HAP, "terribly simple, initially stupid, naturally godlike, the God's other pole." "He is in the light, the other God is in the night." The narrator fears "the son of the frogs" is the God of his day. She says HAP is the flesh spirit, blood spirit, the extract of bodily substances and organs (the passage lists many body parts and fluids). The narrator asks if she is of the devil and where his "flashing godly light" remains; she answers his body remains and "the enlightening thought comes from the body," a thought that "crawls around like a worm, like a serpent" and "a blind newt of Hell." He fears being buried alive; she tells him to "suck it up" from the carcass: "You should not understand, but suck!" He refuses three times; she says the dead need "the life juices of men" in order to share in his life and give him tidings. He calls this absurd. [p.370] [note: footnote 44 says Black Book 5 has "a phallus?" in this place and no mention of HAP; it quotes Budge's *Egyptian Heaven and Hell* that Hap is a son of Horus and that "the Phallus of his Pepi is Hap," which Jung marked in his copy. Footnote 43 gives the date December 2, 1915.]

**Hell as the way to immortality [p.371].** She looks at him as on the last day he saw her alive, when she had shown him something of the Egyptian mystery, and asks: "Do it for me, for us. Do you recall my legacy, the red sun disk, the golden wings and the wreath of life and duration? Immortality; of this there are things to know." "The way that leads to this knowledge is Hell." The narrator sinks into brooding, suspects the solitude of this way, and after a struggle with weakness and cowardice decides to take on "this solitude of the holy error and the eternally valid truth." (December 5, 1915.)

**Third night: the dead beloved and "blood."** He calls to her: "Teach me the knowledge of the worms and the crawling creatures, open to me the darkness of the spirits!" She whispers that she needs blood so she can speak, and asks whether he lied when he said he would leave power to the son. He says he said something he did not understand. She says: "HAP is not the foundation but the summit of the church that still lies sunken. We need this church since we can live in it with you and take part in your life. You have excluded us to your own detriment." She asks for blood; he says "So take blood from my heart." She says the shadow world's air is thin; the dead "hover on the ocean of the air like birds above the sea," some drifting into outer space; the near, incomplete ones want to return to earth and to the living, and she asks whether he has an animal form she can enter; she would even like to be his dog. He tells her to drink "so that what should be will be." [p.371]

**Brimo and HAP.** She whispers "Brimo" and says it is how it begins: Brimo, the old one, bore the son, "the powerful HAP, who grew out of her shame," who strove after the wife of Heaven, who arches over the earth; the mother is his wife and the wife his mother. The narrator outraged calls it an accursed teaching. She continues: "If Heaven becomes pregnant and can no longer hold its fruit, it gives birth to a man who carries the burden of sin-that is the tree of life and of unending duration." When Brimo the heavenly was pregnant she bore the dragon, first the afterbirth and then the son HAP "and the one who carried HAP. HAP is the rebellion of the Below, but the bird comes from the Above and places itself on the head of HAP. That is peace." "You are a vessel... Empty shells do not spill, they catch." She says another evening is approaching: the light of day illumines the shadow, which is itself a shadow of the sun; life becomes shadow; "Do you think that your shadow is your son? He is small at midday, and fills the sky at midnight." [pp.371] [note: footnote 51 links Brimo to Hecate, goddess of magic and the underworld, citing Jung's 1912 *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido*; footnote 52 links the arching wife of Heaven to the Egyptian sky goddess Nut (same source). Footnote 49 says Black Book 5 has "The Phallus" in place of "HAP" and cites Jung's childhood dream of the ritual phallus, p. 4 of the book. Footnote 53: this paragraph is reworked from Black Book 5.]

**The narrator's protest (December 7 and 9, 1915).** Exhausted, he asks whether she introduces "the terrible son who lived beneath me, under the trees on the water" and whether humanity should "completely go to waste for divinity." He asks whether he must live with shadows instead of the living, and protests that a living person should not give his life for dead who did not live "the eternal." The shadow replies that the living fritter away his life, that "with me you live yourself since I belong to you," that he belongs to "your invisible following and community," and that the living "see only your shadow, not you-you servant, you bearer, you vessel." He answers that the dead are formless and breathe the coldness of the grave, that he does not want to be buried alive, and asks what they offer in return: "I regard your mysteries as tricks if the living cannot live from them." She interrupts: "We are shadows; become a shadow and you will grasp what we give." [p.371]

## 6. The dead one's address: against loving men, for solitude, and for "the church" [pp.372-373]

*Dialogue continues with the shadow of the dead beloved, now named in the narrator's account "the dead one."*

**"You need not die; you must only let yourself be buried."** The narrator will not descend into the dead's darkness; she says he need only let himself be buried; he asks if in hope of resurrection; she says he suspects what will happen: triple walls and invisibility. She says at least he does not love the dead, so they will cost less than the men "who roll in your love and patience and have you make a fool of yourself." [p.372]

**Against his love for people.** She says his love of men is flight from himself: he tempts them into megalomania. His heart belongs to the dead. "Self-defense against men-so that you walk on your own two feet, not on human crutches." Men "are always wanting love to be able to run away from themselves"; "Why do fools go out and preach the gospel to the negroes, and then ridicule it in their own country?" [note: this wording is the translator's rendering of an early-20th-century text; footnote 56 says Jung was critical of Christian missionaries (1931).] She says missionaries use the gospel of love to justify war and murderous injustice, and have not cleaned their own house or driven out their own devil; love given to one's own self "burns like fire," so they prefer to love others; "It is false hypocrisy." Love is "to bear and endure oneself. It begins with this." To live is more than love; war is not love; human love is "a means like other means." Hence "above all, solitude, until every softness toward yourself has been burnt out of you. You should learn to freeze." [p.372] [note: footnote 57 gives the Black Book 5 continuation: "Now is not the time for love, but for deeds"; the deed is "your work"; "Science is what you do... There is no way back, only forward."]

**Will of the God; highest freedom.** The narrator sees only graves and asks what "cursed will" is above him. She says it is the will of the God, stronger than the narrator, "you slave, you vessel"; the Christian shrouds have fallen; the God has become strong again; "The yoke of men is lighter than the yoke of the God." "He who does not fall into the hands of men falls into those of the God." This is "the highest freedom. Only the God above you, through yourself." The God "knows only power and creation. He commands and you act." "There is only one road, the military road of the Godhead." The narrator obeys, saying he has to accept these words, and that he greets "a new light, a blood-red sun" in painful wonder, forced by a foreign will in him. [p.372] [note: footnote 58 says the handwritten manuscript changes the dialogue partner from the soul to the dead one.]

**Joy and service.** He asks if it is the sacrifice of joy. She says: "The sacrifice of all joy, provided that you do it yourself. Joy should neither be made nor sought; it should come, if it must come." He should not serve his "personal devil." "True joy is simple: it comes and exists from itself." He must devote himself to her and seek no joy: "Joy comes from fulfillment, but not from longing." "I have the power. I command, you obey." He fears destruction; she says she is life "that destroys only the unfit," tells him to build his bridge "stone upon stone" and not to take the helm: "There is no salvation without me." [p.372]

**The church.** He says he is blind and does not know where to begin; she says "It always begins with the neighbor. Where is the church? Where is the community?" He protests that he is not a prophet and not entitled to know better than others. She replies: "I want the church, it is necessary for you and for others." Otherwise what will he do with those he forces to his feet; "the beautiful and natural will nestle into the terrible and dark and will show the way." The church is "something natural." "The holy ceremony must be dissolved and become spirit. The bridge should lead out beyond humanity; inviolable, far, of the air. There is a community of spirits founded on outer signs with a solid meaning." He says it is incomprehensible. She says: "Community with the dead is what both you and the dead need. Do not commingle with any of the dead, but stand apart from them and give to each his due. The dead demand your expiatory prayers." [pp.372-373]

She raises her voice and, in the narrator's name, calls the dead: "You shades of the departed, who have cast off the torment of living, come here! My blood, the juice of my life, will be your meal and your drink. Sustain yourself from me, so that life and speech will..." [p.373, continues]

**{5} After the dead one disappears [p.373].** The dead one disappears. The narrator sinks into gloom; looking up he sees his soul above, "irradiated by the distant brilliance that streamed from the Godhead." He calls out that what happened surpasses a man's power and understanding but he accepts it for her sake and his: "To be crucified on the tree of life, Oh bitterness! Oh painful silence!" He says he "casts himself before human animals," that his virtues and best ability must be torn apart because they are thorns in the side of the human animal; "Not death for the sake of the best, but befouling and rending of the most beautiful for the sake of life." He asks whether there is nowhere a salutary deception to protect him from the Last Supper with his own carcass; "The dead want to live..." [p.373, continues]

## 7. The prayer to the dead and Philemon's speech on the soul [pp.373-374]

**Prayer to the dead (December 20, 1915; text of the dead one's evocation continues from p.373).** The dead one's invocation asks the dead to come and take the narrator's blood "so that life and speech will be yours"; says "The God forces me to address this prayer to you"; proposes to "build the bond of community so that the living and the dead image will become one and the past will live on in the present"; asks that they drink from his blood "the desire that begets evil" (quarrel, discord, ugliness, violent deed, famishment); uses the words of the Eucharist ("Take, eat, this is my body"); says he needs community with the dead so as to "fall prey neither to the community of the living nor to my desire and yours"; and ends with a request that their "closed spirit" be opened to "the redeeming light." [p.373] [note: the narrator, who has in effect been made to speak for the dead one, performs a form of Last Supper with the dead in this section.]

**The dead one's explanation.** After the prayer she says the dead need prayer, but "the God needs no sacrificial prayer. He has neither goodwill nor ill will"; the dead, still of human nature, hear prayers. "The history of humanity is older and wiser than you. Was there a time when there were no dead? Vain deception! Only recently have men begun to forget the dead and to think that they have now begun the real life, sending them into a frenzy." [p.373]

**The narrator's complaint to the soul (January 8, 1916).** He asks his soul why she saw him as the one to "drink the cess of humanity that poured out of Christendom," whether she still wants to "fly entire into the glaring white light of the Godhead," and asks that "the beaker of disgusting filth pass from me" [note: footnote 62 gives Matthew 26:39, Gethsemane]. If it is not her will, he says, she should climb past Heaven and "topple the throne of God" and proclaim the right of men, since "only Gods are able to spur on the human worm to acts of colossal atrocity" [note: footnote 63 gives Job 25:6]. He appeals to humanity to thrust off "the terrible worm of God, the strangler of men." As he speaks he realizes Philemon stands behind him and has given him the words; he feels "the presence of the good and the beautiful." [p.373]

**Philemon on the soul and the daimons (January 10, 1916) [pp.373-374].** Philemon tells him to remove "the divine, too, from your soul, as far as you can manage": as long as she arrogates divine power she is "an unruly child and a bloodthirsty daimon at the same time, a tormentor of humans without equal, precisely because she has divinity... Because you venerate her." The dead also want sacrifice so that they can live and rule; they died "still lusting for power." A child, an old man, an evil woman, a dead spirit and a devil all "need to be humored." He says: "Fear the soul, despise her, love her, just like the Gods... But above all never lose them!" A man who loses them becomes an animal and then a devil. "Cling to the soul with love, fear, contempt, and hate, and don't let her out of your sight. She is a hellish-divine treasure to be kept behind walls of iron and in the deepest vault." He warns that she is disloyal and cunning yet beautiful, with "the primordial cleverness of serpents," that she "belongs to the daimonic race of the Tom Thumbs and giants," and is only distantly related to humankind; "If you seek to grasp her in human terms you will be beside yourself." Only the excess of one's rage, doubt and love belong to her; if one does not see one's soul one sees her in fellow men, which drives one mad. [pp.373-374] [note: footnote 64 gives the date January 10, 1916.]

He then urges: tear the "bloody veil" the lost soul has woven round man, take hold of "the divine whore" and lock her up; "Let her for once taste your torment so that she will get to feel man and his hammer, which he has wrested from the Gods." [note: footnote 65 refers to the giant Thrym stealing Thor's hammer in the Poetic Edda.] [p.374] "May man rule in the human world. May his laws be valid. But treat the souls, daimons, and Gods in their way; offering what is demanded." He says: burden no man, demand and expect nothing of another from what your devil-souls and God-souls suggest; endure, be silent, and "act not on the other but on yourself, unless the other asks for your help or opinion." The narrator's own garden is full of weeds, so he should not teach his neighbor order. Acting on or thinking about others uninvited shows that one cannot distinguish oneself from one's soul and helps her "into whoring." Helping others uninvited is "Christian presumptuousness": "The Gods don't need your help, you laughable idolater." One should give opinions and advice to oneself; if one acts the daimon, one "becomes a daimon to others" and goes into frenzy. If one leaves others to their daimons and does the "cursed work of redemption on yourself," the daimons must torment themselves; open others' eyes only if they solicit it. "Draw the coat of patience and silence over your head, sit down, and leave the daimon to accomplish his work... Thus will you sit under fruit-bearing trees." The daimons would like to inflame him to take over their work; he should be "the guard before the prison of your soul," "your soul's eunuch," protecting her from Gods and men or Gods and men from her; power is given to the weak man "like a poison sting bestowed upon the little bee"; "put the soul under wraps, distinguish yourself from her, since not only your fellow men but also the Gods must live." [p.374]

## 8. The soul in prison; the gold she holds back; the dead dispute [pp.374-376]

**The soul agrees to prison (January 11, 1916).** The narrator asks the soul whether Philemon's advice is good. She says: "Do not mock, or else you strike yourself. Do not forget to love me." He finds it hard to unite hate and love; she says hate and love mean the same to her, "like all women of my kind," and she wants everything to belong to her or to no one, including the hate he gives others, because she needs everything for "the great journey that I intend to begin after your disappearance." She agrees to be imprisoned: "there I have peace and can collect myself." The human world intoxicates her; "Doors of iron, walls of stone, cold darkness and the rations of penance-that is the bliss of redemption." Being removed from conceiving matter she can think about "the journey, the rising sun the dead one spoke of and the buzzing, melodious golden wings." She says he deserves her thanks. [pp.374-375]

**The narrator's fury and tenderness.** He cries "How divinely beautiful you are!" and in the same moment rages: she has dragged him through Hell and he longs for her thanks. He calls her "you concubine of Heaven, you divine monster," tells her to sing "without blood," and says he has "gone to your school" and learned how a soul behaves, "perfectly ambiguous, mysteriously untruthful and hypocritical." She writhes and cries "Pity, have compassion"; he calls her "soulless soul-being." [p.375]

**Philemon blesses the soul (January 11, 1916 continues).** Philemon steps forward, lays a hand on his shoulder, and speaks in the narrator's name as a hymn to the soul: "You are the God-bearer"; "You live in the golden temple"; the peoples come and praise her; offerings are made (red wine, a black chicken, wreaths of ivy and roses) "in remembrance of the farewell you took"; "the day upon which you... commence the return journey from the land of men where you have learned how to be a soul." The soul follows "the son who ascended and passed over"; she carries them up as her soul and is placed "before the son of God, maintaining your immortal right as an ensouled being." "We are in the land of men and we are alive." [p.375]

**The hidden jewel (January 13, 1916).** The soul looks saddened and pleased and hurries to ascend, but the narrator suspects a secret and stops her. He accuses her of hiding a golden vessel or gem stolen from men when she drank their blood. She denies it, wording her denial gently; he insists he saw "the sparkling light of the jewel." She finally weeps and says she cannot part with it: "It is love, warm human love, blood, warm red blood, the holy source of life, the unification of everything separated and longed for." [p.375]

**The narrator claims love.** He answers: "it is love that you claim as a natural right and property, although you still ought to beg for it." He says he wants to love himself, "not you through me": the soul and Gods will "crawl and beg" for it and gather round the source, bringing gifts; he will be "a more just administrator than you godless Gods"; Gods know "no measure and no mercy," waste the goods of the poor, and drink "ambrosia and nectar" which are "the flesh and blood of men." "Learn humility and pride from men for the sake of love." [pp.375-376] He tells the Gods that their firstborn son is man, "a terribly beautiful-ugly son of God who is renewal to you all," and that the Gods bore a son of men who is his renewal, "no less splendid-terrible, and his rule also will serve you." [p.376] [note: footnote 68 identifies ambrosia and nectar as the Gods' food and drink in Greek mythology; footnote 69 notes that the next sentence is not in Black Book 5.]

**Philemon's sentence (January 14, 1916).** Philemon raises his hand: "Both God and man are disappointed victims of deception, blessedly blessed, powerlessly powerful. The eternally rich universe unfolds again in the earthly Heaven and the Heaven of the Gods... Separation once more comes to the agonizingly united and yoked. Endless multiplicity takes the place of what has been forced together, since only diversity is wealth, blood, and harvest." [p.376]

**Her land of promise.** After a night and a day the narrator finds his soul waiting and asks why she is still there. She says she saw the land promised to man, "where milk and honey flows" [note: footnote 71: Exodus 3], and recites a seven-line vision of that land: sun on the land, green forests, golden vineyards, villages of man, towering mountains with hanging fields of eternal snow, fruitfulness and fortune; "None but I saw the fortune of man." The narrator tells her she forces mortals to labor and suffer for her salvation; he demands that she do it for the earthly fortune of humankind, and then stands waiting. [p.376]

## 9. The furnace, the fire vision, and Abraxas (January 1916) [pp.376-377]

**The furnace.** The soul says: "Now then, I want to set to work. But you must build the furnace. Throw the old, the broken, the worn out, the unused, and the ruined into the melting pot, so that it will be renewed for fresh use." She calls this "the custom of the ancients... practice and incubation in a smelter, a taking-back into the interior, into the hot accumulation where rust and brokenness are taken away through the heat of the fire. It is a holy ceremony." She tells him: "Touch the earth, press your hand into matter, shape it with care. The power of matter is great. Did HAP not come from matter? Matter is the filling of emptiness. By forming matter, I shape your salvation. ...Matter is stronger than HAP, since HAP is the son of the earth. The hardest matter is the best; you should form the most durable matter. This strengthens thought." [p.376]

**{6} The soul's wisdom; the vision of fire (January 16 and 18, 1916).** The narrator forms in matter the thoughts she gives him; she often speaks about "the wisdom that lies behind us." [note: footnote 72 says Appendix C, January 16, 1916 contains a preliminary sketch of the cosmology of the *Septem Sermones*, and that "forming the soul's thoughts in matter" seems to refer to the composition of the painting *Systema Munditotius* (see Appendix A), citing scholarship by Barry Jeromson.] One night she comes with unease: she sees blazing fire hovering in the air, many flames, "the mercy of the eternal fire," "the breath of fire descends on you!" He is horrified and asks if everything must be broken, burned and destroyed. She says "Patience," and sees "an immeasurable sea of embers." She says "I also want your fear" in order "to bring it before the ruler of this world" [note: footnote 75 says Black Book 5 here has the name "Abraxas"; footnote 74 says the painting *Systema Munditotius* carries the legend "Abraxas dominus mundi."]. He says he wants to hide from the ruler of this world because he is branded and has beheld the forbidden. She says: "You have been summoned to serve him." He protests he is only a "minor" stammerer; she insists: "You possess the word that should not be allowed to remain concealed." [pp.376-377]

She sees a vision: "the surface of the earth and smoke sweeps over it-a sea of fire rolls close in from the north, it is setting the towns and villages on fire, plunging over the mountains, breaking through the valleys, burning the forests-people are going mad." The narrator, in the vision, goes before the fire with a burning robe, singed hair, a crazy look, a hoarse voice, announcing what approaches; people do not see the fire or believe him, but see his mark and sense he is the messenger; when asked "what fire?" he stammers. "Look up, see the flames that blaze over your head-look up, the skies redden." [p.377] She disappears with these words. [note: this passage is a vision narrated by the soul, not a record of external events.]

**The dark crowd at the door (January 29 and 30, 1916).** The narrator remains anxious and confused for many days; the soul is silent and unseen. One night a dark crowd knocks at the door; the soul urges him to let them speak. He rails at her for not protecting him, calling it "the devil's confusion"; she says: "Be quiet, or else you'll disturb the work." Philemon appears in the white robe of a priest and lays a hand on his shoulder; the narrator says "So speak, you dead." They cry in many voices: "We have come back from Jerusalem, where we did not find what we sought. We implore you to let us in. You have what we desire. Not your blood, but your light." [p.377] [note: footnote 79 cross-refers to p. 294 of the book, where the dead Anabaptists led by Ezechiel were heading to Jerusalem; footnote 77 says the preceding sentence is not in Black Book 5.]

[note: footnote 78 reports Jung's later statements to Aniela Jaffe that the discussions with the dead were the prelude to what he subsequently communicated to the world; "From that time on, the dead have become ever more distinct for me as the voices of the unanswered, unresolved and unredeemed"; the questions did not come from the world around him but from the dead; the dead appeared to know no more than when they died, which explained their tendency to encroach upon life and why in China important family events are reported to ancestors. Footnote 80 gives Jung telling Jaffe that Philemon lost his autonomy in the *Sermones*. Footnote 81 gives the long editorial note on the Sermones' subheading ("The seven instructions of the dead. Written by Basilides in Alexandria, where the East touches the West"), on Basilides as an Alexandrian second-century Gnostic, a Gnostic myth's structure in four acts, Jung's later remarks in *Aion* and *Memories*, and a bibliography of studies on the Sermones; summarized here, not reproduced.]

## 10. The first Sermon to the Dead (the "Septem Sermones ad Mortuos" begin) [pp.377-378]

Philemon "lifted his voice and taught them" what the text calls "the first sermon to the dead."

**Sermon I (continues on later pages; first part here).**
- "I begin with nothingness. Nothingness is the same as the fullness. In infinity full is as good as empty." Nothingness is empty and full; anything may be said of it (white, black, existing, not existing). "That which is endless and eternal has no qualities, since it has all qualities." [p.377]
- **The Pleroma** (editor's note: a Gnostic term meaning "fullness"; Jonas defines it as the fully explicated manifold of divine characteristics; Jung in 1929 said it is a state where pairs of opposites are together, nonexistent until they "become"). Nothingness or fullness is called the Pleroma; thinking and being cease in it; no one is in it, for he would be distinct from it. "It is fruitless to think about the Pleroma, for this would mean self-dissolution." [p.378]
- Creation is not in the Pleroma but in itself; the Pleroma is the beginning and end of creation and pervades it as sunlight pervades air, yet creation has no share in it, as a transparent body becomes neither light nor dark. "We are, however, the Pleroma itself," part of the eternal and endless, but with no share in it, being removed from it "not spatially or temporally, but essentially," as creation confined within time and space. Because we are parts, the Pleroma is also in us; "Even in the smallest point the Pleroma is endless, eternal, and whole." Speaking of creation as "part" of the Pleroma is only figurative. [p.378]
- Why speak of the Pleroma at all? "In order to begin somewhere," and to free hearers from the delusion that "somewhere without or within there is something fixed or in some way established from the outset." "Every so-called fixed and certain thing is only relative. That alone is fixed and certain that is subject to change." Creation is subject to change and is quality itself. [p.378]
- Creatures came into being, but not creation: creation is a quality of the Pleroma as much as non-creation (eternal death). "Creation is ever-present, and so is death." The Pleroma has everything, differentiation and non-differentiation. [p.378]
- **Differentiation is creation.** Man differentiates because his essence is differentiation; hence he must speak of qualities of the Pleroma that do not exist. Speaking of the Pleroma's qualities is really speaking from one's own differentiated state; one needs this "so that we may sufficiently differentiate ourselves." If we do not differentiate, "we move beyond our essence, beyond creation, and we fall into nondifferentiation, which is the other quality of the Pleroma... This is the death of the creature." "Therefore we die to the same extent that we do not differentiate." The creature's essence "strives toward differentiation and struggles against primeval, perilous sameness. This is called the principium individuationis." Non-differentiation is therefore a great danger. [p.378]
- Qualities of the Pleroma are **pairs of opposites**, listed: the effective and the ineffective; fullness and emptiness; the living and the dead; the different and the same; light and darkness; hot and cold; force and matter; time and space; good and evil; the beautiful and the ugly; the one and the many; and so on. "The pairs of opposites are the qualities of the Pleroma that do not exist, because they cancel themselves out." [p.378]

[note: footnotes 82 to 85 (editor) on this page: the Pleroma and Jung's later uses of it (pre-existence, potentiality, the Tibetan Bardo, the alchemist Dorn's *unus mundus*); a comparison with Meister Eckhart's distinction between Godhead and God, and with the Chinese Tao; *Unterschiedenheit* (differentiation) as the German term; *principium individuationis* as a Schopenhauer/Hartmann notion, with Jung's later definition of individuation as a process of differentiation whose goal is the development of the individual personality (1921). Summarized only.]

### Sermon I, continued [pp.379-380 begin]
- Because we are the Pleroma we have all the pairs of opposites in us; since our nature is differentiation, we have them "in the name and under the sign of differentiation." Two consequences. First, in us the qualities are differentiated and separate, so they do not cancel each other out but are effective: "we are the victims of the pairs of opposites. The Pleroma is rent within us." Second, we must possess and live them only under the sign of differentiation and distinguish ourselves from them: "Distinction from them saves us." [p.379]
- Striving for the good or the beautiful makes us forget our essence and fall under "the spell" of the Pleroma's qualities, since the evil and the ugly are one with the good and beautiful there; true to our essence, we distinguish ourselves from good and beautiful and thus from evil and ugly, and do not fall into "nothingness and dissolution." [p.379] [note: footnote 86 links the idea of polarity to Schelling's *Naturphilosophie* and the later centrality of opposites in Jung's work.]
- Objection: difference and sameness are also qualities, so is striving for distinctiveness not true to our nature? Answer: the Pleroma has no qualities; "We create these through thinking." Striving for distinctiveness or sameness pursues thoughts that flow from the Pleroma and returns one to it. "Not your thinking, but your essence, is differentiation." There is "only one striving, namely the striving for one's own essence"; if one had that, no knowledge of the Pleroma would be needed; but since thought alienates us from our essence, the sermon gives knowledge "with which you can bridle your thoughts." [p.379]
- The dead fade away grumbling and moaning. [p.379]

### Dialogue after Sermon I: Philemon's account of teaching and knowing [p.379]
*(Footnote 88 says: in the published version of the Sermones, the commentaries that follow each sermon do not appear, nor does Philemon; the preacher is assumed to be Basilides; these commentaries were added in Scrutinies. Footnote 87 says these paragraphs are not in Black Book 6.)*
- The narrator asks whether the ancients did not teach similar things and whether it was "reprehensible heresy," and why he lays this out for "this horde" blown by the night wind "from the dark bloodfields of the West." Philemon says the dead ended their lives too early; they were seekers; "their lives were incomplete, since they knew no way beyond the one to which belief had abandoned them." Since no one teaches them, he must; their Christian faith once "discarded and persecuted precisely this teaching," yet they repudiated Christian belief and were rejected by that faith; so he teaches "so that their life may be fulfilled and they can enter into death."
- "Do you, wise Philemon, believe what you teach?" "How could I teach what I believe? Who would give me the right to such belief? It is what I know how to say; not because I believe it, but because I know it. If I knew better, I would teach better." He asks whether it is good to believe something more "if one does not know better." [note: footnote 89 quotes Jung's 1959 BBC reply "I don't need to believe. I know," and says Philemon's statement seems to be its background.]
- Asked if he is certain, Philemon says: "I do not know whether it is the best that one can know. But I know nothing better." "There are no mistakes in these things, there are only different levels of knowledge... Only in your world are things always other than you know them, and therefore there are only mistakes in your world." He bends down, touches the earth with his hands, and disappears. [p.379]

## 11. The Second Sermon to the Dead: God and the devil [pp.379-380]

**{7} Setting (January 31, 1916).** That night Philemon stands beside the narrator; the dead line the walls and cry: "We want to know about God. Where is God? Is God dead?" [note: footnote 91 refers to Nietzsche on the death of God and Jung's 1938 comment that it would be more correct to say "He has discarded our image, and where will we find him again?"]

**Sermon II [pp.379-380].**
- "God is not dead. He is as alive as ever. God is creation, for he is something definite, and therefore differentiated from the Pleroma. God is a quality of the Pleroma, and everything I have said about creation also applies to him." He differs from creation by being "much more indefinite and indeterminable," less differentiated, since "the ground of his essence is effective fullness." Only insofar as he is definite is he creation, "the manifestation of the effective fullness of the Pleroma." What we do not differentiate falls into the Pleroma and is canceled; if we do not differentiate God, "effective fullness is canceled out for us." God is also the Pleroma itself, as every smallest point is. [p.379]
- "Effective emptiness is the essence of the devil. God and devil are the first manifestations of nothingness, which we call the Pleroma." Whether the Pleroma exists makes no difference since it cancels itself out; created beings, God and the devil, do not cancel each other out but stand as effective opposites. "Everything that differentiation takes out of the Pleroma is a pair of opposites, therefore the devil always belongs to God." Their inseparability is intimate and indissoluble. [p.380]
- What distinguishes God and devil: fullness and emptiness, generation and destruction. What they share is effectiveness, which "stands above both, and is a God above God, since it unites fullness and emptiness through its effectuality." [p.380]
- **Abraxas.** "This is a God you knew nothing about, because mankind forgot him. We call him by his name ABRAXAS. He is even more indefinite than God and the devil." To distinguish him from God, God is called HELIOS or sun. "Abraxas is effect. Nothing stands opposed to him but the ineffective." He stands above sun and devil: "improbable probability, that which takes unreal effect." If the Pleroma had an essence, Abraxas would be its manifestation. He is "the effectual itself, not any particular effect, but effect in general"; he is also creation, since distinct from the Pleroma; the sun and devil have definite effects and so appear more effective; "He is force, duration, change." [p.380] [note: footnote 93 (editor): Jung in 1932 said Abraxas is a Gnostic name meaning 365 (the Gnostics' supreme deity, a time god), identical with the Demiurge, usually depicted with a fowl's head, man's body, serpent's tail; Irenaeus on Basilides; Jung's early-1913 study of Dieterich's *Abraxas*. Footnote 92 refers to Jung's 1940 essay on the Trinity; footnote 94 explains Helios as the Greek sun god.]
- The dead raise a great tumult "for they were Christians." [p.380] (footnote 95: the paragraphs to the end of the section are not in Black Book 6.)

**Dialogue after Sermon II [p.380].** The narrator tells Philemon he takes from men the Gods they could pray to ("alms from the beggar, bread from the hungry; fire from the freezing"). Philemon: the dead had to reject Christian belief and therefore can pray to no God; should he teach a God they can believe in and pray to, which they have rejected? They rejected it because "the world, without these men knowing it, entered into that month of the great year where one should believe only what one knows" [note: footnote 96 refers to the "Platonic months" in an earlier note of the book]. That is "a remedy for the long sickness that arose from the fact that one believed what one did not know." He teaches "a God in whom one does not believe and to whom one does not pray; but of whom one knows." He teaches this God to the dead because they desired it, not to living men who did not ask. The dead "need salvation, since they are a great waiting flock hovering over their graves." Whoever is dying "wants knowledge, and he sacrifices pardon."

The narrator says this seems a terrible God to whom good and evil and human joy are nothing. Philemon: these dead had a God of love and rejected him; they had to know a God "to whom everything created is nothing, because he himself is the creator and everything created and the destruction of everything created," a God "to whom nothing can be attributed, who has all qualities and therefore none." Does this not destroy human bonds? He answers that the dead rejected the God of love and "thus they killed one another and dissolved the community of men"; so he teaches "the God who dissolves unity; who blasts everything human, who powerfully creates and mightily destroys. Those whom love does not unite, fear compels." He bends to the ground, touches it and disappears. [p.380]

## 12. The Third Sermon to the Dead: Abraxas [pp.380-381]

**{8} Setting (February 1, 1916).** The dead approach "like fog from a swamp" and say: "Tell us more about the highest God." Philemon steps forward (the third sermon). [pp.380-381]

**Sermon III [p.381].**
- "Abraxas is the God who is difficult to grasp. His power is greatest, because man does not see it." From the sun he draws the summum bonum, from the devil the infimum malum, "but from Abraxas LIFE, altogether indefinite, the mother of good and evil." Life seems smaller than the summum bonum, so it is hard to conceive that Abraxas's power transcends even the sun's. [note: footnote 99 on Aristotle/Aquinas and Jung's critique of *privatio boni* in *Aion*; footnote 100 gives Black Book 6 on Abraxas as God of frogs or toads, "the union of the Christian God with Satan," and Jung's later argument that the Christian God image was one-sided.]
- "Abraxas is the sun, and at the same time the eternally sucking gorge of emptiness." His power is twofold; the opposites cancel in human eyes. "What the Sun God speaks is life, what the devil speaks is death. But Abraxas speaks that hallowed and accursed word that is at once life and death." He produces truth and lying, good and evil, light and darkness in the same word and act: "Therefore Abraxas is terrible."
- Images given in a litany: splendid as a lion striking down its victim, beautiful as a spring day; "the great and the small Pan alike"; Priapos; the underworld monster, "a thousand-armed polyp, a coiled knot of winged serpents, frenzy"; the earliest hermaphrodite; "the lord of toads and frogs"; the fullness seeking union with emptiness; "holy begetting," "love and its murder," "the saint and his betrayer," the brightest light of day and the darkest night of madness. "To look upon him, is blindness. To recognize him is sickness. To worship him is death. To fear him is wisdom. Not to resist him is redemption." [p.381]
- "God dwells behind the sun, the devil behind the night. What God brings forth out of the light, the devil sucks into the night. But Abraxas is the world, its becoming and its passing. Upon every gift that comes from the sun god the devil lays his curse. Everything that you request from the Sun God produces a deed from the devil. Everything that you create with the Sun God gives effective power to the devil." [p.381]
- A closing litany: "That is terrible Abraxas. He is the mightiest created being and in him creation is afraid of itself. He is the manifest opposition of creation to the Pleroma and its nothingness. He is the son's horror of the mother. He [...] creation... the love of man... the speech of man... the appearance and the shadow of man. He is deceptive reality." [p.381] [note: footnote 101 quotes Jung's 1942 statement that an all-encompassing God must include his opposite.]

**Dialogue after Sermon III [pp.381-382].** The dead howl and rage "for they were incomplete." The narrator asks how to understand this God. Philemon: "This God is to be known but not understood. If you understand him, then you can say that he is this or that and this and not that. Thus you hold him in the hollow of your hand and therefore your hand must throw him away." The narrator asks if this does not bring despairing confusion. Philemon: the dead rejected the order of unity and community because they rejected belief in the just father in Heaven; so he teaches "the chaos that is without measure and utterly boundless, to which justice and injustice, leniency and severity, patience and anger, love and hate, are nothing." Why call "the eternally incomprehensible, the cruel contradictoriness of nature, God?" "How should I name it otherwise? If the overpowering essence of events... were law, I would call it law. Yet it is also no law, but chance, irregularity; sin, error, stupidity; carelessness, folly; illegality." A slanting wind blowing a speck of dust into the works can be "a superior strength, harder than a mountain of iron"; "the eternal law is also no law." "Human language has forever named the maternal womb of the incomprehensible God. Truly, this God is and is not, since from being and nonbeing everything emerged that was, is, and will be." He touches the earth and dissolves. [pp.381-382]

## 13. The Fourth Sermon to the Dead: Gods and devils [p.382]

**{9} Setting (February 3, 1916).** The following night the dead come sooner, muttering: "Speak to us about Gods and devils, accursed one." Philemon begins (fourth sermon). [p.382] (Footnotes 103, 102: this sentence and the paragraphs to the section's end are not in Black Book 6.)

**Sermon IV [p.382].**
- "The Sun God is the highest good, the devil the opposite. Thus you have two Gods. But there are many high and good things and many great evils. Among these are two devil Gods; one is the Burning One, the other the Growing One."
- **The Burning One is EROS**, in the form of a flame; "it shines by consuming." **The Growing One is the TREE OF LIFE**; "it greens by heaping up growing living matter." Eros flames up and dies; the tree grows slowly and constantly over measureless time. Good and evil unite in the flame and in the tree's growth; "In their divinity life and love stand opposed." [note: footnote 104 gives Jung's later remarks on Eros (1917/1928) and Diotima's account of Eros as an intermediary spirit in Plato's *Symposium*; footnote 105 refers to Jung's 1954 study of the philosophical tree.]
- "The number of Gods and devils is as innumerable as the host of stars. Each star is a God, and each space that a star fills is a devil. But the empty fullness of the whole is the Pleroma. Abraxas is the effect of the whole, and only the ineffective opposes him."
- **The four principal Gods** ("four is the number of the world's measurements"): **One** is the beginning, the Sun God; **Two** is Eros, for he binds two and spreads in brightness; **Three** is the Tree of Life, filling space with bodies; **Four** is the devil, who "opens all that is closed," dissolves everything formed and physical: "the destroyer in whom everything becomes nothing."
- "Happy am I who can recognize the multiplicity and diversity of the Gods. But woe unto you, who replace this incompatible multiplicity with a single God. In so doing you produce the torment of incomprehension, and mutilate the creation whose nature and aim is differentiation." The Gods' multiplicity matches human multiplicity: "Numberless Gods await the human state. Numberless Gods have been men. Man shares in the nature of the Gods. He comes from the Gods and goes unto the God." Equality prevails "only for the sake of man. For the Gods are many, while men are few"; the Gods, like stars, "abide in solitude, separated by vast distances," and need communion to bear their separateness [note: footnote 107 date February 5, 1916; footnote 106 notes Black Book 6 has the dead calling him "a pagan, a polytheist."].
- It is of no use to reflect on the Pleroma, nor to worship the multiplicity of Gods, "least of all... the first God, the effective fullness, and the summum bonum. By our prayer we can add nothing to it, and take nothing from it; because effective emptiness gulps down everything." "The bright Gods form the heavenly world. It is manifold and extends and increases infinitely. The Sun God is the supreme lord of the world. The dark Gods form the earthly world. It is simple and diminishes and declines infinitely. The devil is its nethermost lord, the moon spirit, satellite of the earth, smaller, colder, and more dead than the earth." "There is no difference between the might of the heavenly and earthly Gods. The heavenly Gods magnify; the earthly Gods diminish. Both directions are immeasurable." [p.382] [note: footnote 108 says in Black Book 6 "the dark guest" enters here.]

**Dialogue after Sermon IV [pp.382-383].** The dead interrupt with angry laughter and mockery. The narrator says Philemon teaches a "raw superstition" and polytheism "which the Fathers had successfully and gloriously overcome." Philemon: the dead rejected the single highest God, so how could he teach them the one God; they must believe him, but they have rejected belief; "So I teach them the God that I know, the multifarious and extended, who is both the thing and its appearance, and they also know him even if they are not conscious of him." [pp.382-383] He says the dead gave names to all beings in air, earth and water, weighed, counted and apportioned things (horses, cows, sheep, trees, land, springs), and asks what became of the "admirable tree," the "sacred frog" with a "golden eye," the 7,777 cattle whose blood and flesh they used, the "sacred ore" dug from the earth; "No, they named, weighed, numbered, and apportioned all things. They did whatever pleased them." "Yet the time has come when things speak. The piece of flesh says: how many men? The piece of ore says, how many men? The ship... The coal... The house says: how many men? And things rise and number and weigh and apportion and devour millions of men." [p.383] He says they "pulled down" the "one and only, simpleminded God" onto a heap of separate things; "this God taught you to weigh and number bones. But the month of this God is drawing to a close. A new month stands at the door. Therefore everything had to be as it is, and hence everything must become different." [p.383] [note: footnote 110 suggests the sacred trees may refer to the cutting down of sacred trees at the advent of Christianity in Germany in the eighth century; footnote 111: one sentence is not in Black Book 6. The text speaks here only in images such as "devour millions of men."]

"This is no polytheism that I have made up! But many Gods who powerfully raise their voices and tear humanity to bloody pieces." He speaks of many Gods "for the sake of their superiority"; "Now is the time when you can learn." Had the dead "atoned for the ox with the velvet eyes," done penance for the "shiny ore," worshiped the holy trees, made peace with "the golden-eyed frog," would they have raised a murderous hand against their brothers? "Who is greater, man or the Gods? Truly, this sun has become a moon and no new sun has arisen from the contractions of the last hour of the night." Philemon bends, kisses the earth, says "Mother, may your son be strong," looks up and says "How dark is your place of the new light," and disappears. [p.383]

## 14. The Fifth Sermon to the Dead: spirituality, sexuality and community (begins; the sermon continues in the next part) [p.383]

**{10} Setting.** The next night the dead approach noisily, scoffing: "Teach us, fool, about the church and holy communion." Philemon begins the fifth sermon. [p.383]

**Sermon V (first part, to the end of this range) [p.383].**
- "The world of the Gods is made manifest in spirituality and in sexuality. The celestial ones appear in spirituality, the earthly in sexuality." [note: footnote 112 quotes Jung's 1925 seminar: "Sexuality and spirituality are pairs of opposites that need each other."]
- "Spirituality conceives and embraces. It is womanlike and therefore we call it MATER COELESTIS, the celestial mother. Sexuality engenders and creates. It is manlike, and therefore we call it PHALLOS, the earthly father." [note: footnote 113 reports Jung's remark that the Mater Coelestis is not Mary or the Church but "Aphrodite Urania"; footnote 114: Black Book 6 and the calligraphic Septem Sermones have "Phallus"; footnote 115 quotes Jung's 1912 passage on the phallus as a symbol of ubiquitous creative power; footnote 116 gives Black Book 6's continuation "The mother is the grail. The phallus is the spear."]
- "The sexuality of man is more earthly, that of woman is more spiritual. The spirituality of man is more heavenly, it moves toward the greater. The spirituality of woman is more earthly, it moves toward the smaller. Mendacious and devilish is the spirituality of man, and it moves toward the smaller. Mendacious and devilish is the spirituality of woman, and it moves toward the greater. Each shall go to its own place." [note: set out here as the text states it, without interpretation; the passage is compressed, so check it on the page before reuse.]
- "Man and woman become devils to each other if they do not separate their spiritual ways, for the essence of creation is differentiation." Likewise if they do not distinguish their sexuality. "Man shall know the smaller, woman the greater." "Man shall differentiate himself both from spirituality and sexuality. He shall call spirituality mother, and set her between Heaven and earth. He shall call sexuality Phallos, and set him between himself and earth. For the mother and the Phallos are superhuman daimons that reveal the world of the Gods. They affect us more than the Gods since they are closely akin to our essence."
- If one does not differentiate oneself from sexuality and spirituality and does not regard them as "an essence both above and beyond you," one is "delivered over to them as qualities." "Spirituality and sexuality are not your qualities, not things you possess and encompass. Rather, they possess and encompass you, since they are powerful daimons, manifestations of the Gods." "No man has a spirituality unto himself or a sexuality unto himself. Instead, he stands under the law of spirituality and of sexuality." "No one escapes these daimons. You shall look upon them as daimons, and as a common task and danger, a common burden that life has laid upon you. Thus life, too, is for you a common task and danger, as are the Gods, and first and foremost terrible Abraxas."
- **Community:** "Man is weak, and community is therefore indispensable. If your community is not under the sign of the mother, it is under the sign of the Phallos. Absence of community is suffering and sickness. Community in everything is dismemberment and dissolution." "Differentiation leads to singleness. Singleness is opposed to community. But because of man's weakness with regard to the Gods and daimons and their invincible law, community is necessary, not for man's sake, but because of the Gods. The Gods drive you to community. Insofar as the Gods impose community upon you, it is necessary; more is bad." "In the community every man shall submit to others, so that the community be maintained, for you need it. In singleness every man shall place himself above the other, so that every man may come to himself and avoid slavery." [p.383]
