## Overview

The book pairs two late essays by Jung that make one case from two directions.

**Part I, *The Undiscovered Self*,** is a diagnosis of the mid-twentieth-century situation (the Iron Curtain, the hydrogen bomb, mass States). Its argument runs as follows. Modern society, East and West, drifts toward mass-mindedness: the individual is reduced to a statistical unit, responsibility is handed to the State or to organizations, and the State takes over the place religion once held. Science and rationalism, which supply the statistical picture, have stripped the individual of dignity; the Churches, tied to creeds, public institutions and literal readings of myth, cannot restore it. The only real counterweight to the mass is an individual with an inner, experienced relation to something outside the "world" (religion in the sense of a personal relation to a transcendent authority, not a creed). That relation can only be reached by self-knowledge, which means knowledge of the unconscious, including one's own shadow and capacity for evil, and which Jung insists is not the same as knowing one's conscious ego. The remedy is therefore small and slow: change in individuals, one at a time, who withdraw their projections, face their own darkness, and thereby exert an unforced influence on those around them.

**Part II, *Symbols and the Interpretation of Dreams*,** is the practical and theoretical companion, written for a general reader. It defines symbol versus sign, argues that dreams are the commonest and most accessible source of natural symbols, explains the unconscious (forgotten, subliminal and not-yet-conscious contents), sets out Jung's method of interpretation (stay with the dream's own images, "circumambulate" them, do not follow free association away from the dream), the compensatory function of dreams, the four psychological functions and the two attitude types as they bear on interpretation, archetypes as inherited tendencies to form images (illustrated with a child's series of twelve dreams), the function of religious symbols, and the loss of numinosity in the modern world. It ends by naming the single individual as the place where any change must start, and by claiming that the unconscious is a natural phenomenon, neutral like nature itself, that produces meaningful symbols.

The two parts share themes: the unconscious as a second authority besides the ego; the shadow; the dissociation of modern man, mirrored in the political split of the world; the loss of numinous symbols; the dignity and uniqueness of the individual.

## Foreword to the 2010 edition: "Reading Jung after *The Red Book*" (Sonu Shamdasani) [s.6]
*(Editor's essay, not Jung's text.)*
- *The Red Book* (*Liber Novus*) opens a new way of reading the Collected Works: it lets readers trace how, from 1914 on, Jung's self-experimentation ("active imagination," begun in the winter of 1913 and recorded in the Black Books, which are records of self-experiment rather than diaries) was turned into a psychology for a medical and scientific public.
- Jung took some of his fantasies as precognitions of the First World War; he compiled the main fantasies with interpretive commentary into *Liber Novus*, whose overall theme the foreword gives as regaining one's soul and overcoming spiritual alienation, through a new image of God in the soul. In 1928, struck by parallels between *The Secret of the Golden Flower* and his own mandalas, he set the book aside and turned to the cross-cultural study of individuation, especially through medieval alchemy.
- *The Undiscovered Self*: written after World War II amid the Cold War, the Berlin Wall and the hydrogen bomb, which put Jung once more in "an apocalyptic age." The foreword quotes his 1917 statement that "the psychology of the individual corresponds to the psychology of the nation," so that only a change of attitude in the individual begins a change in the nation. By the 1950s Jung argued that only self-knowledge and religious experience could resist totalitarian mass society; the individual had been failed by science on one side and organized religion on the other. A psychology that reconnects people with dreams and spontaneous symbols was needed, which is the theme of "Symbols and the Interpretation of Dreams," described as Jung's last written work, designed to convey his ideas to the general public.

# PART I. THE UNDISCOVERED SELF (Present and Future)

## 1. The Plight of the Individual in Modern Society [s.8, §488-504]

**Opening: an age of apocalyptic images.** Anxious futures flourish in times of distress (chiliastic expectations at the start of the Christian era, spiritual change at the first millennium). Now, as the second millennium ends, the world is again filled with images of universal destruction; the "Iron Curtain" divides humanity in two; the dangers are hydrogen bombs and "State absolutism" [§488].

**The thin stratum of reason [§489].** Subversive minorities shelter under Western humanitarianism and are checked only by the critical reason of "a single, fairly intelligent, mentally stable stratum" of the population, whose size varies by nation, education, and economic and political stress. Judged by plebiscites, perhaps forty per cent of the electorate at best. Reason and critical reflection are not man's outstanding gifts and wobble more in bigger groups. "The mass crushes out the insight and reflection that are still possible with the individual," which leads to doctrinaire and authoritarian tyranny if the constitutional State ever weakens.

**Psychic epidemics [§490].** Reasoning works only below a critical level of emotion; above it, slogans and wish-fantasies take over: "a sort of collective possession" that becomes a psychic epidemic. Then those who are merely tolerated as asocial under reason rise to the top. Jung estimates at least ten latent cases for every manifest case of insanity (there are no statistics, he notes). These people are the "adapted ones" in a collectively excited milieu, know its language, and are dangerous as sources of infection precisely because the so-called normal person has only limited self-knowledge.

**What self-knowledge is not [§491-492].** Most people confuse "self-knowledge" with knowledge of the conscious ego-personality. The ego knows only its own contents, not the unconscious. People measure self-knowledge by what the average person in their social setting knows of himself; the psyche is like the body, which the layman also barely knows. So commonly held self-knowledge is limited and socially dependent, producing prejudices ("that does not happen with us or in our family") and illusory claims of qualities that merely cover the facts.

**Why theory cannot give self-knowledge [§493-495].** In the "broad belt of unconsciousness" we are defenseless against psychic infection, and can guard against it only by knowing what attacks, how and when. Self-knowledge concerns individual facts, so theories help little: any theory based on experience is statistical, an ideal average that erases the exceptions at both ends. Illustration: the average weight of pebbles in a bed tells you little; you might never find a pebble of exactly the average weight. "Real facts" are marked by individuality; "the real picture consists of nothing but exceptions to the rule," so absolute reality has the character of irregularity. The individual is "a relative exception and an irregular phenomenon"; he is characterized by the unique, and in the last analysis can be neither known nor compared. Man as a species member must be treated as a statistical, comparative unit (this gives a universal anthropology or psychology), but understanding an individual requires laying aside all scientific knowledge of the average man and all theories, and approaching "with a free and open mind."

**Knowledge versus understanding [§496-497].** Understanding another person or oneself means turning a blind eye to scientific knowledge, a sacrifice since science enjoys universal authority. The doctor who wants both to classify and to understand a patient meets a "conflict of duties between two diametrically opposed and mutually exclusive attitudes," which can be solved only by "two-way thinking": doing one thing while not losing sight of the other. Scientifically the individual is a repeated unit, designated by a letter; for understanding he is the sole real object. The more schematic the treatment, the more the patient resists, rightly. Medicine broadly now agrees that the doctor treats the sick person, not an abstract illness.

**Education and the statistical world-picture [§498-499].** Scientific education gives an unrealistic rational picture in which the individual is a marginal phenomenon, though the individual is "the true and authentic carrier of reality." Natural science tends to present results as if they came about without human involvement, hiding the psyche's participation (modern physics excepted, which admits that the observed is not independent of the observer). The statistical picture thrusts the individual aside "in favor of anonymous units that pile up into mass formations." Names of organizations replace concrete persons; at the top stands the abstract State as the principle of political reality. Moral responsibility is replaced by *raison d'état*; moral and mental differentiation is replaced by public welfare and raised living standards; the meaning of life is placed in State policy imposed from outside. The individual is "ruled, fed, clothed, and educated as a social unit," housed and amused to mass standards. Rulers are social units too, specialized mouthpieces of State doctrine, "thoroughgoing specialists who are unusable outside their line of business." State policy decides what is taught.

**The ruler and the Leader [§500].** The "omnipotent" State doctrine is itself manipulated by those at the summit, who are the State policy ("L'état c'est moi"). They alone could use their individuality, but are more likely slaves of their own fictions. One-sidedness is always compensated by unconscious subversion: "Slavery and rebellion are inseparable correlates," so rivalry and distrust pervade the whole organism. A formless mass always produces a "Leader," who inevitably becomes victim of an inflated ego-consciousness.

**Scientific rationalism as a cause of mass-mindedness [§501-503].** Once the individual joins the mass he renders himself obsolete. One chief cause of psychological mass-mindedness is scientific rationalism, which "robs the individual of his foundations and his dignity": he becomes an abstract number in the bureau of statistics, an interchangeable unit. Seen rationally and from outside, that is exactly what he is, and one can hardly say why anyone ever endowed individual life with dignity. A friend's joke in a huge crowd: "Here you have the most convincing reason for not believing in immortality: all that lot wants to be immortal!" The bigger the crowd, the more negligible the individual. If the individual, overwhelmed by his puniness, feels his life has lost meaning (which is not identical with public welfare and higher living standards), he is already "on the road to State slavery" and has become its proselyte. We are fascinated by statistical truths and large numbers and told daily of the nullity of the individual who is not personified by a mass organization. Public figures seem carried on mass movements; it remains moot whether their message is their own or a megaphone for collective opinion.

**Collectivized responsibility [§504].** Individual judgment grows uncertain and responsibility is delegated to a corporate body; the individual becomes a function of society, which usurps the role of the real life-carrier though it is only an abstract idea like the State. Both are "hypostatized," become autonomous; the State is made a quasi-animate personality from which everything is expected, whereas it is really "a camouflage for those individuals who know how to manipulate it." Thus the constitutional State drifts into "the communism of a primitive tribe," subject to a chief or oligarchy.

## 2. Religion as the Counterbalance to Mass-Mindedness [s.9, §505-516]

**Why mass movements attack religion [§505-506].** To turn the individual into a function of the State, every socio-political movement of this kind cuts the ground from under religion, because it must remove the individual's dependence on anything else. Jung defines religion here as "dependence on and submission to the irrational facts of experience," concerning the individual's psychic attitude rather than social or physical conditions. To have an attitude toward the outer conditions of life one needs a point of reference outside them; religion gives, or claims to give, that standpoint, "a reserve" against the force of circumstances for someone who has "no other ground under his feet except the pavement." If statistical reality is the only one, it is the sole authority, judgment and decision become impossible, and the individual is a function of statistics and of the State.

**Religion versus creed [§507-509].** Religion claims the individual by another authority, which can estrange him from the world as the mass estranges him from himself; he can forfeit judgment in the former case just as in the latter. When religion compromises with the State Jung calls it a **creed**, not a religion: "a creed gives expression to a definite collective belief, whereas the word religion expresses a subjective relationship to certain metaphysical, extramundane factors." A creed is a confession for the world at large (an intramundane affair); religion lies in the individual's relationship to God (Christianity, Judaism, Islam) or to the path of salvation and liberation (Buddhism). From this "all ethics is derived," which without the individual's responsibility before God is only conventional morality. Creeds have codified and externalized themselves; the living relationship to the extramundane reference has been pushed into the background; where a church is an established institution it includes many who are "indifferent" and belong by habit (Protestantism's mention of "pietism" or "eccentricity" for anyone who claims guidance by God's will is an example). Belonging to a creed is often social, giving the individual no foundation. What founds freedom and autonomy is "the empirical awareness, the incontrovertible experience of an intensely personal, reciprocal relationship between man and an extramundane authority," acting as counterpoise to the "world" and its "reason" [§509].

**Both camps will dislike this [§510].** For the mass man State policy is the supreme principle, and the individual exists only as a function of the State; the collective believer holds that the State too is subject to God. Jung declares he makes no metaphysical judgments and leaves open whether the phenomenal world is the "opposite" of God; he notes only that the psychological opposition exists (New Testament; dictator States' hostility to religion and the Church's to atheism and materialism).

**Dictator States as religions [§511].** The individual not anchored in God cannot resist the world's physical and moral blandishments; he needs "inner, transcendent experience." Merely intellectual or moral insight into the mass man's stultification is only negative recognition. The dictator State's advantage over bourgeois reason: "along with the individual it swallows up his religious forces." The State takes the place of God; socialist dictatorships are therefore religions and "State slavery is a form of worship." The religious function falsified this way produces secret doubts, repressed, then overcompensated by fanaticism used as a weapon against opposition: free opinion stifled, "the end justifies the means," policy exalted to a creed, the party boss a demigod, supporters honored as heroes, martyrs, apostles, missionaries, one truth, heretics persecuted, and only the boss may interpret doctrine.

**The religious function as an instinct [§512-514].** When the individual becomes "social unit No. so-and-so," the religious function is sucked into the maelstrom too. Religion, as "careful observation and taking account of certain invisible and uncontrollable factors," is an instinctive human attitude with the evident purpose of maintaining psychic balance: natural man "knows" that conscious functions can be thwarted at any time by uncontrollable happenings inside or outside. So difficult decisions have always been made safe by religious measures: offerings, blessings, solemn rites, *rites d'entrée et de sortie*. Rationalists dismiss these as magic; but magic has a psychological effect, giving a feeling of security necessary to carry out a decision, which is always one-sided and a risk. The dictator also stages State acts with solemnity: brass bands, flags, parades and monster demonstrations are "no different in principle" from processions, cannonades and fireworks "to scare off demons." The difference: State pageantry gives collective security but no protection against inner "demonism," so the individual clings to the mass all the more and completes his "social depotentiation." The State, like the Church, demands enthusiasm, self-sacrifice and love; if religion presupposes "fear of God," the dictator State supplies terror. The rationalist attacking the rite's miraculous effect misses its psychological effect, which both sides exploit for opposite purposes. Likewise religion's goals (deliverance from evil, reconciliation with God, reward hereafter) become worldly promises (freedom from care, just distribution, prosperity in the future, shorter working hours), as far off as Paradise. A natural function present from the beginning cannot be disposed of by enlightened criticism: *Naturam expellas furca tamen usque recurret* ("You can throw out Nature with a pitchfork, but she'll always turn up again"). Religion reappears "evilly distorted" in the deification of the State and the dictator. Footnote (added later): a noticeable reaction in the U.S.S.R. since spring 1956.

**The lie and the "community" ideal [§515-516].** The dictator State robs the individual of rights and also of metaphysical foundations; ethical decisions no longer count, only "the blind movement of the masses," and "the lie" becomes the operative principle of political action. Both the dictator State and denominational religion stress **community**. For communism it is the basic ideal; it is thrust down people's throats and produces divisive mistrust. In the Church, and in weak-Church Protestantism as a hoped-for "communal experience," community is a two-edged tool of mass organization. "Just as the addition of however many zeros will never make a unit," the value of a community depends on the stature of its individuals. A community cannot produce a real change in individuals; that comes only from personal encounter, not from "communistic or Christian baptisms en masse." Recent events in Eastern Europe (footnote added January 1957) show how superficial communal propaganda is: "The communal ideal reckons without its host."

## 3. The Position of the West on the Question of Religion [s.10, §517-524]

**The Western dilemma and the East's strength [§517-518].** The West stands with Roman law, Judaeo-Christian ethics grounded in metaphysics, and the ideal of inalienable rights, and asks how to halt or reverse the dictator development. Criticizing the socialist State as utopian or its economics as unreasonable is useless: the West only talks to itself, and any economic principle can be carried out if one accepts the sacrifices (Jung cites Stalin letting three million peasants starve and having millions of unpaid laborers). Such a State has no crises to fear while it has a disciplined, well-fed police army; it can multiply unpaid workers; the only real danger is external military attack, which grows less likely each year. The only possibility seems a break-down of power from within, which must follow its own development (outside support has little effect; the absolute State has "an army of fanatical missionaries" and a "fifth column" protected by Western laws, and communes of believers weaken Western decision-making). Footnote (1956): events in Poland and Hungary showed more opposition than expected.

**Why rational and moral appeals fail [§519-520].** Industrial and military power with a high living standard cannot check "psychic infection spread by religious fanaticism." Appeals to idealism and reason are "bombination in the void," a puff swept away in a storm of faith, however twisted. What is faced is an unleashing of emotional forces engendered by the spirit of the times, little moved by rational reflection or moral exhortation. Many see that the antidote should be an equally potent non-materialistic faith, but the little word "should" shows its absence: the West has no uniform faith, and as "father of Marxist philosophy" shares the same intellectual assumptions, arguments and aims. A creed as a public institution "serves two masters" (relation to God; duty to the State: "Render unto Caesar...").

**Belief versus inner experience; understanding myth symbolically [§521].** Churches stand for collective convictions that many adherents hold by unreflecting belief, not inner experience; belief "is notoriously apt to disappear as soon as one begins thinking about it," and collides with knowledge. Faith is not the primary religious experience but a secondary phenomenon arising from something that happened to a person and produced trust and loyalty (the Latin is lost in the source text, `[garbled in source]` at "instilled into us"). The creeds' standpoint is archaic and mythological; taken literally it clashes with knowledge, but understood symbolically (for example, the resurrection) it can be interpreted without conflict and without losing meaning. Mankind believed in life after death long before Christianity, so symbolic reading does not remove the hope of immortality. "Is it not time that the Christian mythology, instead of being wiped out, was understood symbolically for once?"

**The parallel between State religion and Church [§522-523].** The absolutist claim of a *Civitas Dei* represented by man resembles the "divinity" of the State; Loyola's "the end sanctifies the means" anticipates the political lie. Both demand unqualified submission and curtail freedom (before God or before the State), "digging the grave for the individual"; the West shares the "scientific" rationalistic *Weltanschauung* with its statistical leveling. The West offers modern man "a variety of paths all leading to one goal which is practically indistinguishable from the Marxist ideal." Anyone who has learned to submit absolutely to a collective belief can march in the reverse direction with equal credulity if a "better" belief is offered; Jung points to what happened to "a civilized European nation," and doubts other nations are immune. He judges America perhaps even more vulnerable than Europe because its educational system is most influenced by the scientific worldview and its mixed population lacks roots in a soil "practically without history"; historical and humanistic education "leads a Cinderella existence." Europe has humanistic education but uses it for nationalistic egoisms and paralyzing skepticism. Both lack "an idea which puts the individual human being in the center as the measure of all things."

**The valuelessness of the individual: the one universal belief [§524].** "The valuelessness of the individual in comparison with large numbers is the one belief that meets with universal and unanimous assent." The "century of the common man" is an illusion: man is slave of machines, endangered by the military technology meant to protect him, his freedom guaranteed within limits in one half of the world and abolished in the other, and he holds notions that stamp his dignity as worthless. His achievements diminish him (the factory worker "pays for his share of the factory with the loss of personal property," is tied to his workplace, ground by piece-work, and fed political precepts), though a roof and daily feed are not to be despised when necessities can be cut off any day.

## 4. The Individual's Understanding of Himself [s.11, §525-548]

**Man is an enigma to himself [§525-526].** Man, "the instigator, inventor and vehicle" of all this, makes himself a *quantité négligeable*. The explanation is uncertainty of judgment: man is an enigma to himself because he lacks means of comparison. He can distinguish himself anatomically and physiologically from animals, but as a conscious, speaking being has no criterion for self-judgment; he is a unique phenomenon. Comparison, hence self-knowledge, would become possible only through contact with quasi-human mammals from other stars. Until then he is like a hermit who knows his anatomical affinity to the anthropoids but is extraordinarily different in psyche; the differing degrees of self-knowledge within the species matter little beside such an encounter. Psychology is the youngest empirical science and has difficulty getting near its object.

**Freeing psychology from prejudice [§527-528].** As Copernicus freed the world picture from geocentricity, psychology had to be freed first from mythological ideas, then from the prejudice that the psyche is a mere epiphenomenon of brain biochemistry and a purely personal matter. Brain dependence of psychic function does not itself prove epiphenomenon; the phenomena of parapsychology point to a relativization of space and time through psychic factors and cast doubt on psychophysical parallelism (denying them is "intellectual laziness" or philosophical bias). No psychology can now ignore the unconscious or parapsychology. The psyche "has a peculiar nature which cannot be reduced to anything else"; it includes the phenomenon of consciousness, "one of the two indispensable conditions for existence as such." "Without consciousness there would, practically speaking, be no world." "Consciousness is a precondition of being"; the psyche thus has "the dignity of a cosmic principle," co-equal with physical being. Its carrier is the individual, who does not produce the psyche but is preformed by it. If the psyche is of overriding importance, so is the individual, "the only immediate manifestation of the psyche."

**Two forces that devalue the individual psyche [§529].** (1) Statistics level it, robbing it of individuality. (2) The Churches grant it validity only when it acknowledges their dogmas. Science calls the will to individuality subjectivism; the Churches call it heresy and spiritual pride. Jung points out that unlike other religions Christianity presents a symbol whose content is the individual life of a man (the Son of Man) and regards this individuation process as the incarnation and revelation of God; so "the development of man into a self" has a significance not yet appreciated. The secret longing for autonomy is why it survives collective suppression.

**Fear of the unconscious [§530].** The gravest obstacle to self-knowledge is "panic fear of the discoveries that might be made in the realm of the unconscious." Even Freud told Jung it was necessary to make a dogma of his sexual theory as the sole bulwark of reason against "an eruption of the black flood of occultism." Archetypal forms, "archaic vestiges" grounded in and expressing the instincts, have a numinous quality that sometimes arouses fear; they are ineradicable, are the ultimate foundations of the psyche, cannot be grasped intellectually, and reappear in altered form when one manifestation is destroyed. Religious persons should consider this question seriously.

**Academic versus medical psychology [§531-532].** Scientific psychology must be abstract (stay at a distance from its object), which is why laboratory findings are often unenlightening; it avoids the risk of complexity by asking ever simpler questions. In medical psychology the object puts the question: "Nature experiments with the doctor." General principles lose meaning as understanding deepens; the situation becomes subjectivized (transference and countertransference), producing isolation from the environment; perfect understanding would need virtual identification of two individuals, which is impossible and breaks the understanding, since understanding presupposes preserving both individualities. So understanding should go only to the point where it is balanced by knowledge.

**The analyst's restraint [§533-535].** The same problem faces the "director of conscience," but his office obliges him to apply denominational bias at the critical moment, cutting short the individual's right to exist as such, except when the dogmatic symbol (the model life of Christ) is understood concretely and felt adequate. The analyst treats patients to whom denominations mean little and must have as few preconceptions as possible; he respects metaphysical convictions without crediting them with universal validity; the patient's individual traits should not be bent by arbitrary outside intervention. The analyst must refrain from adding to the collective factors the patient already suffers (advertising, propaganda, advice). "The nauseating 'you should,' 'you must'" is a confession of impotence; for once the patient might be offered a relationship without it. The analyst plays "counsel for the defense" against the onslaught from outside and its effects inside. Fear of letting loose anarchic instincts is exaggerated (cowardice, morality, good taste and the penal code are safeguards); the effort is rather in helping first stirrings of individuality reach consciousness. Later, evaluation of impulses is needed; the patient must act "on his own insight and decision and not from the mere wish to copy convention." Objective values then merely substitute for character. Society, composed of de-individualized people, is at the mercy of ruthless individualists, and "banding together" and the extinction of personality make it succumb to a dictator: "A million zeros joined together do not, unfortunately, add up to one." The age thinks in large numbers and mass organizations, forgetting "what a well-disciplined mob can do in the hands of a single madman"; the greatest organizations are held by ruthless leaders and cheap slogans.

**The Churches and mass action [§536-537].** The Churches also use mass action "to cast out the devil with Beelzebub," forgetting the axiom that the individual becomes morally and spiritually inferior in the mass, and neglecting their real task of *metanoia*, rebirth of the spirit. If the individual is not regenerated, society cannot be: "society is the sum total of individuals in need of redemption." Rather than making the individual the one important factor, the Churches rope him into an organization of diminished responsibility. Mass meetings produce intoxication, then the mass man succumbs to a louder slogan. Rhetorical questions: Did Christ call his disciples at a mass meeting? Did the five thousand not later cry "Crucify him!"? Are Jesus and Paul not prototypes of people who trusted inner experience and went their own way? Jung grants what the Church does: it gives amorphous mass shape, a social service, and gives individuals a meaningful form of life, but these gifts confirm tendencies and do not change the inner man, who cannot be given by environment what he must win by effort and suffering; a favorable environment even strengthens the tendency to expect everything from outside. A far-reaching inner change is urgent given mass phenomena and overpopulation; it requires "a new process of self-reflection" asking what a real, not statistical, man is.

**The infantile paradise of the mass [§538-539].** Mass movements slide down "an inclined plane made up of large numbers": where the many are there is security; what the many believe must be true; the clamor of the many promises wish-fulfillment by force, and the sweetest is "that gentle and painless slipping back into the kingdom of childhood, into the paradise of parental care," where all thinking is done from above. The mass man never asks who pays for this paradise; the authority gains power, and the individual becomes weaker. This opens the road to tyranny, which is immoral and ruthless and so freer in methods than institutions that respect the individual; opposing institutions are pushed to use the same methods and the evil spreads. Newspapers daily display the "suffocating power of the masses"; liberty, equality, fraternity help no one, since the appeal can only be addressed to "his executioners."

**Resistance and the microcosm [§540-541].** "Resistance to the organized mass can be effected only by the man who is as well organized in his individuality as the mass itself." The medieval view of man as microcosm has dropped away; man bears the cosmic "correspondence" within him through reflecting consciousness and the hereditary, archetypal nature of his instincts, which bind him to the environment but also tear him apart (desires pull different ways); he is in continual conflict and rarely has an undivided goal, and when he does it costs him repression. The natural state of the psyche is a "jostling together of its components" and some dissociation (the Buddhist "ten thousand things"), crying for order and synthesis. As a dictatorial will gives direction to the crowd, the dissociated individual needs "a directing and ordering principle." The ego would like to play this part, but unconscious factors thwart it; to reach synthesis it must get to know them, experience them, or possess a numinous symbol that expresses them. The existing Christian symbol has not done this: the "world split" runs through the Christian white man and Christian outlook failed to prevent the return of an archaic social order like Communism.

**Christianity not finished, only its interpretation [§542-544].** "It is not Christianity, but our conception and interpretation of it, that has become antiquated." The Christian symbol is living and can develop if people meditate on its premises again; this needs a different attitude to the individual, the microcosm of the self. Nobody now knows what inner experiences are possible or what psychic facts underlie religious myth; universal darkness hangs over it. The opponents hold "practically all the trump cards": big battalions, politics, science, technology; science seems to offer the highest intellectual certainty; faith facts are treated in the same context as science facts. Questioned, the Church tells the man that belonging to a church is *de rigueur*, that the faith facts were concrete historical events, that rites produce miracles, and that Christ's suffering vicariously saved him. He then either believes implicitly or rejects it as incomprehensible. State "truths" are easy to understand; religion lacks explanations (Jung cites Acts 8:30, "How can I, unless someone guides me?"). Yet the religious impulse rests on an instinctive base, so "You can take away a man's gods, but only to give him others in return": leaders of mass States are deified, and where this has not been forced, obsessive factors charged with demonic energy arise: money, work, political influence. A natural function denied conscious expression causes general disturbance: the "triumph of the Goddess of Reason" brings a general neuroticizing of modern man, a dissociation analogous to the Iron Curtain, which runs "through the psyche of modern man, no matter on which side he lives." As the neurotic is unconscious of his shadow, so the normal person sees his shadow in his neighbor or the man across the divide; it has become a political duty to call capitalism or communism the devil "so as to fascinate the outward eye and prevent it from looking within." Western man has developed an instinctive interest in his psyche and in "psychology."

**Infantile fantasy and the onset of neurosis [§545-546].** The psychiatrist is summoned onto the world stage with questions that concern the individual's most hidden life but are direct effects of the *Zeitgeist*. The material, being infantile fantasies, is called "neurotic," usually rightly, but such fantasies were mostly never conscious nor consciously repressed; they have been present unconsciously from the start. Activation occurs when consciousness is in distress; they are latent in everybody and cause disturbance only when strengthened by abnormal conditions (for example, poisonous parental influences). In adults the neurosis does not simply follow from the fantasies; it develops when the person meets a situation he cannot overcome by conscious means, a standstill in development that "opens a sluice" for the fantasies; energy flowing off from unused consciousness reinforces the unconscious's negative and infantile traits. When fantasies break into consciousness they split the person into two personalities of different character.

**Instincts and archetypes [§547-548].** Child fantasies are the imagination of the instincts, preliminary exercises in future conscious activities; the neurotic's fantasies, though distorted, hold a core of normal instinct. Instincts are highly conservative and ancient and, represented to the mind, appear as an **image** expressing the impulse visually and concretely. Jung's illustration: the yucca moth, whose psyche would contain a numinous pattern of ideas that compels its fertilizing work and helps it "recognize" the total situation. Instinct "is anything but a blind and indefinite impulse"; it is attuned to an external situation and so has "its specific and irreducible form," as old as the instinct, thus **archetypal**, "even older and more conservative than the body's form." The same applies to *Homo sapiens*: conscious activity is rooted in instinct; human knowledge consists essentially in "the constant adaptation of the primordial patterns of ideas that were given us a priori," which need modification because in their original form they suit an archaic way of life, not a differentiated environment. To keep instinctive dynamism flowing into life, "we should remould these archetypal forms into ideas which are adequate to the challenge of the present."

## 5. The Philosophical and the Psychological Approach to Life [s.12, §549-564]

**Ideas lag behind the situation; Marxist remaking [§549-550].** Ideas lag behind changes; only when the rift between outer situation and antiquated ideas is unendurable does the problem of *Weltanschauung* arise, and with it the question of how to readapt the primordial images that maintain the flow of instinctive energy. They cannot simply be replaced by a new rational configuration, which would be shaped by the outward situation, not man's biological needs, and would block the way to the original man; this fits Marxist education, which "seeks, like God himself, to remake man, but in the image of the State." Philosophy is no longer a way of life as in antiquity but an academic exercise; denominational religion's archaic rites suited the Middle Ages but are strange to modern man, yet an instinct bids him cling to them to avoid "the abyss of nihilistic despair." Even a rationalist critic should remember that religious symbols "possess a life of their own by virtue of their archetypal character"; intellectual understanding is called for only when feeling and intuition do not suffice, in people for whom intellect carries the prime power of conviction.

**Faith and knowledge split [§551-553].** The gulf between faith and knowledge is so large that the two are incommensurable, though both concern the same empirical world (theologians say faith is supported by historical facts: Christ's birth, miracles, death under Pontius Pilate, resurrection in the flesh). Theology rejects reading its records as myth but has recently tried to "demythologize" while drawing the line arbitrarily; "myth is an integral component of all religions." The rupture is "a symptom of the split consciousness" characteristic of the mental disorder of the day: modern society suffers a mental dissociation, a neurotic disturbance, like a neurotic psyche whose distress brings the patient to the analyst. The analyst must relate to both halves of the patient to make a whole, not one half by suppressing the other. The individual is "a social microcosm," reflecting society's qualities, or conversely the smallest unit cumulatively producing collective dissociation (Jung thinks the latter more likely, since the individual is the only concrete carrier of life).

**The word as god [§554-555].** The Christian epoch's highest achievement, the supremacy of the *Logos*, has become the "congenital vice of our age": the word has literally become our god. "Society" and "State" are almost personified; the State is "the inexhaustible giver of all good"; Society is credited with creative capacities. Worship of the word has a "perilous shadow side": once the word attains universal validity through centuries of education, it severs its original connection with the divine Person; there is a personified Church, a personified State; belief becomes credulity, and the word "an infernal slogan capable of any deception." With credulity come propaganda, advertising and a lie of unprecedented proportions. The word that originally announced unity of all men in the one great Man now causes mutual suspicion. Credulity is the neurotic's makeshift for quelling the doubter within. People think "telling" someone he "ought" to do something puts him right; the psychologist knows nothing is achieved by telling, persuading, admonishing or advising; he must learn the "psychic inventory" of the patient and feel his way into all corners of his mind beyond what a teacher or *directeur de conscience* can. His scientific objectivity sees the patient also as an anthropoid, bound to his body; his training looks to the unconscious instinct dominated by sexuality and the power drive (self-assertion), which correspond to Augustine's *concupiscentia* and *superbia*; their clash (preservation of species versus self) is the source of many conflicts and the chief object of moral judgment.

**Instinct, learning and uprootedness [§556-557].** Instinct has two aspects: dynamism and compulsion, and specific meaning and intention. Likely all psychic functions rest on instinct (in animals instinct is the *spiritus rector* of behavior); in civilized man instincts are so split that only a few basic ones are recognizable: sexuality and the power drive and their derivatives, so far the exclusive concern of medical psychology. Following the ramifications, investigators found configurations assignable to neither (Adler asked whether sexual expression might be a "power arrangement"; Freud admitted "ego instincts"). Hence neurotic symptoms can usually be explained in either theory; Jung concludes both are relatively valid and allow other instincts. Learning capacity, nearly exclusive to man, is probably based on the **instinct for imitation** (bird song as example) which disturbs and modifies other instincts. Learning "estranges man more from the ground-plan of his instincts" than anything, drives progressive transformation of behavior, and is the source of psychic disturbance through "his progressive alienation from his instinctual foundation": uprootedness and identification with conscious knowledge of himself. Modern man knows himself only as far as he can become conscious of himself; consciousness orients itself by observing the world, and he forgets himself and "slips imperceptibly into a purely conceptual world where the products of his conscious activity progressively take the place of reality."

**Split and the reversal that changes nothing [§558-559].** Separation from instinct produces conflicts: conscious/unconscious, spirit/nature, knowledge/faith, pathological once consciousness can no longer suppress the instinctual side. The accumulation of such people starts a mass movement claiming to champion the suppressed; since consciousness seeks the source of ills outside, political and social change is demanded as automatic cure for split personality, and when granted the same ills return in altered form: "a simple reversal: the underside comes to the top and the shadow takes the place of the light." "The devil is cast out with Beelzebub." The root is untouched. The Communist revolution debases man lower than democratic collective psychology because it robs him of freedom morally and spiritually too. The West's disadvantage (already seen with German Nazism): it can point a finger at the shadow, which is on the other side of the border, while "we are on the side of good." A statesman confessed he had "no imagination for evil"; Western man is in danger of "losing his shadow altogether," identifying with a fictive personality and the abstract picture of scientific rationalism. The political frontier thus now splits the conscious from the unconscious man; where religious orientation fails "not even a god can check the sovereign sway of unleashed psychic functions." (Footnote of 1956: the shadow followed up this overbright picture "with the Charge of the Light Brigade to Suez.")

**The shadow and duplex man [§560-561].** Rational philosophy does not ask whether the "other person in us," the shadow, agrees with our conscious plans; it does not know we carry a real shadow grounded in instinctual nature. Violating or neglecting instinct has physiological and psychological consequences. For over fifty years it could be known that there is an unconscious counterbalance to consciousness (medical psychology has the evidence), yet "we still go on thinking and acting as before, as if we were simplex and not duplex." We imagine ourselves innocuous and humane; it is "psychically unhygienic" to overlook the unconscious's reaction: neglecting stomach or heart has consequences for the whole man. Without psyche there would be no world. The future will be decided "neither by the threat of wild animals, nor by natural catastrophes, nor by the danger of world-wide epidemics, but simply and solely by the psychic changes in man": an almost imperceptible disturbance of equilibrium in a few rulers' heads could plunge the world into "blood, fire, and radioactivity," as the case of one "Leader" showed. Modern consciousness clings to external objects, making them solely responsible.

**Loss of instinct and the monarchy of the ego [§562-564].** The forlorn state of consciousness is due primarily to **loss of instinct**: more power over nature raised contempt for the merely natural and accidental, including the **objective psyche**, "everything that consciousness is not." The unconscious is objective, manifesting in contrary feelings, fantasies, emotions, impulses and dreams, "none of which one makes oneself but which come upon one objectively." Psychology has mostly been the science of conscious contents, measured by collective standards; the individual psyche is a marginal accident and the unconscious ignored out of "resistance to the mere possibility that there could be a second psychic authority besides the ego" (the ego's monarchy being threatened). The religious person is used to not being sole master in his own house: God decides in the end; yet few would dare let the will of God decide, or say how far a decision came from him. The religious person is directly influenced by the unconscious's reaction, which he calls conscience; but since the same background produces non-moral reactions too, he measures his conscience against a traditional (collective) ethic, supported by his Church. This suffices while tradition holds. When worldly-minded, religion-less people appear *en masse*, the believer is forced onto the defensive and loses the "consensus omnium"; the Church recommends more faith as if it were a matter of good will, but "the seat of faith is not consciousness but spontaneous religious experience." The chapter ends on the question every reader must face: "Have I any religious experience and immediate relation to God, and hence that certainty which will keep me, as an individual, from dissolving in the crowd?" [§564]

## 6. Self-Knowledge [s.13, §565-581]

**The route and the source of religious experience [§565-566].** The answer is positive only if the individual accepts rigorous self-examination; he gains truths and a psychological advantage: he deems himself worthy of serious attention, takes "the first step towards the foundations of his consciousness: that is, towards the unconscious, the only available source of religious experience." Jung is explicit: this does not mean that the unconscious is identical with God or set in his place; it is "the medium from which religious experience seems to flow"; the further cause lies beyond human knowledge ("Knowledge of God is a transcendental problem"). The religious person knows how his subjective existence is grounded in his relation to "God" (in quotation marks: an anthropomorphic idea whose dynamism and symbolism are filtered through the unconscious psyche). Anyone can approach the source of such experiences whether or not he believes in God. That religious experiences exist "no longer needs proof"; whether metaphysics' God is their real ground stays doubtful, but the question is "idle": whoever has had the experience is seized by it, and absolute certainty brings its own evidence.

**The prejudice against the unconscious [§567-568].** It is a misfortune that the one experience that makes sense of individual existence seems to originate in a medium that attracts prejudice ("What good can come out of Nazareth?"): the unconscious is regarded as refuse bin or "merely animal nature," although by definition it is of uncertain extent and constitution, so over- or undervaluation is mere prejudice, and sounds odd from Christians whose Lord was born among domestic animals. The mass man seeks numinous experience in the mass meeting. Psychology's insistence on the unconscious in religious experience is unpopular with Right and Left alike (for the Right revelation came from outside; for the Left man has no religious function except faith in party doctrine). Creeds contradict each other while the world becomes unitary; "exotic races have ceased to be peepshows in ethnological museums"; ideological spheres begin to interpenetrate and mutual understanding will become acute: to make oneself understood one must comprehend the other's standpoint, and the insight needed has repercussions on both sides. "The unity of mankind will assert itself irresistibly"; Marxist doctrine has staked its life on that card and the West hopes to win by technology and aid; "the colored races share our ideological weakness." [note: that sentence reflects 1950s vocabulary; quoted idea only, kept as the source states it.]

**Neglect of the psychological factor; the intelligence/heart discrepancy [§569-570].** Underestimating the psychological factor will take "a bitter revenge." Self-knowledge is unpopular, "an unpleasantly idealistic goal," and preoccupied with the shadow, which is normally denied. The task falls on leading and influential persons (to avoid another *trahison des clercs*); but it needs moral conclusions as well as intelligence, and nature rarely joins high intelligence with gifts of the heart: "the discrepancy between intellect and feeling" is a painful chapter in the history of the psyche. The task cannot be put as a moral demand; one can only make the psychological world situation clear and keep reiterating needed insights: "even the truth can spread and not only the popular lie."

**The common human shadow and the capacity for evil [§571-572].** The horror of the dictator States is "the culmination of all those atrocities of which our ancestors made themselves guilty": blood baths between Christian nations, and crimes of colonization against the "colored races" (a very heavy burden on the white man). Evil lodged in man is of "gigantic proportions"; "original sin" traced to Adam's slip with Eve is almost a euphemism. Because man is held to be only what his consciousness knows, he thinks himself harmless and "adds stupidity to iniquity": terrible things are always done by "the others," the past sinks into forgetfulness, and "normality" is "chronic woolly-mindedness." But nothing has finally disappeared; "I am a man, who has his share of human nature; therefore I am guilty with the rest and bear unaltered and indelibly within me the capacity and the inclination to do them again at any time." "None of us stands outside humanity's black collective shadow"; we are potential criminals who lacked an opportunity. Hence one should "possess some 'imagination for evil.'" Naïveté is as unhelpful as a cholera patient's ignoring contagion; it leads to **projection** of unrecognized evil onto the other, which strengthens the opponent's position because the projection carries over our own secret fear of our own evil and enlarges his threat; and it deprives us of the capacity to deal with evil. A prejudice of the Christian tradition: we are told to eschew evil, neither touch nor mention it (an apotropaic attitude, like the scapegoat driven into the wilderness).

**Dualism, the devil, the H-bomb [§573-575].** Once evil is seen as lodged in human nature unchosen, it "bestrides the psychological stage as the equal and opposite partner of good": a psychological dualism already prefigured in the political schism and in modern man's dissociation (the dualism does not come from the realization; we are split to begin with). To avoid taking personal responsibility we localize evil in criminals; the Christian view of a metaphysical principle of evil "exonerates man's conscience" by putting the burden on the devil, with correct psychological appreciation that man is more victim than inventor of his psychic constitution. Atomic physicists are not "a pack of criminals"; their work was done with dedication and could as easily have been beneficial. But the unconscious also collaborates (the hunch, intuition) and "somewhere or other the unconscious... has its finger in the pie. If it puts a weapon in your hand, it is aiming at some kind of violence." Modern man is not capable of greater evil than the man of antiquity; he has "incomparably more effective means." "As his consciousness has broadened and differentiated, so his moral nature has lagged behind... Reason alone no longer suffices." Reason could desist from experiments of such scope, but fear of the evil seen always in someone else checks reason each time. Unless a bridge is found across the psychic and political split, universal destruction hangs overhead. If a world-wide consciousness arose that all division and fission come from the splitting of opposites in the psyche, "we should know where to begin"; otherwise unconscious individual stirrings accumulate into uncontrollable mass groupings, and direct efforts to manage them are "shadow boxing."

**Dualism as the crux; withdrawing projections [§576-577].** "The crux of the matter is man's own dualism, to which he knows no answer." After centuries of belief in a unitary God who made man a little unity, the abyss opened. Every individual is a cell in international organisms and causally implicated in their conflicts, though feeling meaningless and victim of uncontrollable forces; he also harbors a dangerous shadow that is an invisible helper in "the dark machinations of the political monster." Political bodies always see evil in the opposite group, as the individual foists off what he does not want to know about himself. "Nothing has a more divisive and alienating effect upon society than this moral complacency and lack of responsibility, and nothing promotes understanding and rapprochement more than the mutual withdrawal of projections." This demands self-criticism (one cannot tell the other to withdraw projections; he does not recognize them any more than we do). We recognize prejudices only when, from a broader psychological knowledge of ourselves and others, we doubt the absolute rightness of our assumptions and compare them with the objective facts. "Self-criticism" in Marxist countries serves the State, not truth. The mass State aims at "atomization," psychic isolation: "The more unrelated individuals are, the more consolidated the State becomes, and vice versa."

**Idealism, imperfection, relationship [§578-580].** Democracies too have too much distance between man and man; leveling by appeals to idealism forgets self-criticism and the question "Who is making the idealistic demand?" (perhaps someone jumping over his shadow into an idealistic program that gives him an alibi). One wants the idealist to be ideal; "to be ideal is impossible"; idealisms sound hollow and become acceptable only when their opposite is openly admitted; without that counterweight the ideal becomes humorless and degenerates into bluff, "an illegitimate way of overpowering and suppressing others." Recognition of the shadow leads to modesty and acknowledging imperfection, which any human relationship needs: it is based "on imperfection, on what is weak, helpless and in need of support," not on differentiation and perfection; "the perfect have no need of others," and high idealism can humiliate. Where justice is uncertain and police spying and terror work, people fall into isolation, which is the dictator State's aim. The free society needs "a bond of an affective nature, a principle of a kind like *caritas*, the Christian love of your neighbor," which suffers most from the lack of understanding caused by projection; psychological thought about human relationship would serve its cohesion and strength. "Where love stops, power begins, and violence, and terror."

**Insight over idealism [§581].** These reflections are meant to promote consciousness of the psychological situation, not as appeal to idealism. Psychic change takes time; "insight that dawns slowly seems to me to have more lasting effects than a fitful idealism."

## 7. The Meaning of Self-Knowledge [s.14, §582-588]

**The shadow contains more than the negative [§582].** Exploring the soul meets the instincts and their images, powers of the greatest dynamism; whether their irruption tends to construction or catastrophe "depends entirely on the preparedness and attitude of the conscious mind." The psychologist alone knows how precarious modern man's psychic preparedness is, since he must seek out in man's own nature the helpful powers and ideas that have repeatedly led people through darkness. He may not rely on traditional "oughts and musts," or play the easy role of adviser; the work concerns one individual at a time: "nothing has happened at all unless the individual changes."

**How change spreads: mana [§583].** The spiritual transformation of mankind "follows the slow tread of the centuries" and cannot be hurried or finished in one generation. What lies within reach is change in individuals who have or create an opportunity to influence others of like mind, not by persuading or preaching but by the well-known fact that a person with insight into his own actions and access to the unconscious "involuntarily exercises an influence on his environment": deepening and broadening consciousness produces what primitives call **mana**, "an unintentional influence on the unconscious of others, a sort of unconscious prestige," lasting only while not disturbed by conscious intention.

**The unconscious Zeitgeist and modern art [§584-585].** The striving for self-knowledge has prospects because the **unconscious Zeitgeist** compensates the conscious attitude and anticipates changes. Example: modern art, apparently about aesthetic questions, is performing "psychological education" by destroying previous aesthetic views (replacing pleasing products with chill, subjective abstractions that slam the door on naïve sensual delight and the obligatory love of the object): the prophetic spirit of art turns from the old object-relationship toward the "dark chaos of subjectivisms." Art has not yet found what could hold all men together and express psychic wholeness. Great art has always drawn fruitfulness from myth, the unconscious process of symbolization, and will in future; modern art's seeming nihilism is a symptom and symbol of "universal destruction and renewal." We are living in the Greek *kairos* (right moment) for "a metamorphosis of the gods," of fundamental principles and symbols; the expression of "the unconscious man within us who is changing." Coming generations must take account or humanity may destroy itself through its own technology and science. [note: the Greek word is missing in the source text at this point; the sense "the right moment" is given by Jung.]

**Moral backwardness and the individual as makeweight [§586-587].** As at the start of the Christian era, moral backwardness has failed to keep pace with scientific, technical and social progress. Jung asks whether modern man can resist using power for a world conflagration; whether he knows he risks losing "the life-preserving myth of the inner man which Christianity has treasured up for him"; and "does the individual know that he is the makeweight that tips the scales?" Happiness, contentment, equability of mind and meaningfulness of life can be experienced only by the individual, not by a State, "a convention agreed to by independent individuals" that threatens to paralyze them. Social and political circumstances have been "boundlessly overestimated" as the sole deciding factors; social goals overlook the psychology of the person for whom they are meant and often promote only illusions.

**Closing statement [§588].** Jung offers his view as a psychiatrist, "neither spurred on by excessive optimism nor in love with high ideals, but merely concerned with the fate of the individual human being, that infinitesimal unit on whom a world depends, and in whom, if we read the meaning of the Christian message aright, even God seeks his goal."

# PART II. SYMBOLS AND THE INTERPRETATION OF DREAMS

*(Part divider at [s.15]. The Part II paragraph numbers 416 to 607 follow the Collected Works vol. 18.)*

## 1. The Significance of Dreams [s.16, §416-443]

**Sign versus symbol [§416-417].** Abbreviations (UN, UNESCO, NATO), trademarks and patent-medicine names "are not symbols, they are signs." A **symbol** is "a term, a name, or an image which in itself may be familiar to us, but its connotations, use, and application are specific or peculiar and hint at a hidden, vague, or unknown meaning." Examples: the double adze on Cretan monuments (known object, unknown meaning); a Hindu visitor thinking the English worship animals because he saw eagles, lions and oxen in churches (symbols of the evangelists, from Ezekiel's vision, which parallels the Egyptian Horus and his four sons); the wheel and the cross. "A term or image is symbolic when it means more than it denotes or expresses": it has a wider "unconscious" aspect that can never be precisely defined; exploring it leads the mind to ideas of a transcendent nature "where our reason must capitulate" (the wheel leading to a "divine" sun, though we cannot establish a "divine" being; "divine" is a *façon de parler*).

**Conscious and unconscious symbol use [§418-420].** Because countless things lie beyond human understanding we use symbolic expressions consciously (ecclesiastical language is full of them), but we also produce symbols unconsciously and spontaneously in dreams. Every act of cognition is partial: sense perception is limited by the number and quality of senses; apperception translates the observed fact into a psychic event whose nature is unknowable ("the psyche cannot know its own psychic substance"); the object itself is unknown in its ultimate nature. So every conscious event has an unconscious aspect, as perception has subliminal aspects (sound or light below or above the audible or visible). The unconscious part reaches consciousness only indirectly, shows itself through emotionality or unrealized vital importance, may become conscious later through intuition or reflection, but usually appears in a dream, "in the form of a symbolic image and not as a rational thought." Understanding dreams first made it possible to investigate the unconscious aspect of conscious events.

**Freud's method and the body as symbol-maker [§421-423].** Freud first elucidated the unconscious background empirically, assuming dream contents relate to conscious representations by association, i.e., causal dependence (based on the neurologists' and Janet's observation that neurotic symptoms connect with conscious experience). Breuer and Freud showed that neurotic symptoms are meaningful and "symbolize" just as dreams do. Examples of symptoms speaking as idioms: a spasm when swallowing ("He can't swallow it"), asthma ("can't breathe the atmosphere at home"), leg paralysis ("can't go on any more"), vomiting ("can't stomach it"). Freud's "free association" means letting the patient talk on about his dream images; a rushed doctor omits this, yet anyone talking long enough betrays himself, circling the area he is trying to hide, using symbolism that hides and points at once. A doctor sees so much of the "seamy side" that he is seldom far from truth interpreting hints as signs of an uneasy conscience; so far Freud's repression and wish-fulfillment theory is not contradicted.

**Jung's objection: the Cyrillic experience [§424-426].** A friend on a train through Russia tried to decipher Cyrillic railway notices; free-associating to the letters, he soon met his "complexes." "From any point of the compass you can reach the center directly": free association reaches the critical secret thoughts from symptoms, dreams, fantasies, Cyrillic letters or modern art, so it proves nothing about the real meaning of dreams, which "very often... have a very definite, as if purposeful, structure." So Jung paid more attention to the dream itself, its actual form and statement. Case: a patient dreamed of a drunken, disheveled, vulgar woman called his "wife" (whom in reality his wife was not). Free association would take him to his staple complexes and teach nothing about this dream; the question is what the unconscious conveys by an obviously untrue statement.

**Interpretation of the case; the anima [§427-429].** Dreams are either causal or purposive or both; they "make sense." The dream expresses the idea of a degenerate female closely connected with the dreamer, projected on his wife where it is untrue. Medieval writers said every man "carries Eve, his wife, hidden in his body": this feminine element in every man (based on the minority of female genes in his biological make-up) is what Jung calls the **anima**: essentially "a certain inferior kind of relatedness to the surroundings and particularly to women," kept concealed from others and oneself; the visible personality can be normal while the anima side is deplorable. The dream balanced the lopsidedness of a consciousness that believed itself "a perfect gentleman"; it is not evidence of the moral nature of the unconscious.

**Circumambulation [§430-434].** Jung learned to mistrust free association and to concentrate on the dream-text: "I began to circumambulate the dream itself, never letting it out of my sight, or as one turns an unknown object round and round in one's hands to absorb every detail of it." Why bother with dreams, "those flimsy, elusive, unreliable, vague, and uncertain phantasms"? They are the commonest and universally accessible source for studying man's symbolizing faculty (more complex are psychoses, neuroses, myths and art, which cannot be interpreted without the originator): "Dreams are indeed the chief source of all our knowledge about symbolism." Symbols cannot be invented; if so they would be signs or abbreviations. They occur spontaneously, need careful analysis by association, but not "free association," which leads back to complexes (an association test or just letting people talk reveals those; no dream is needed). Dreams often arise from emotional disturbance involving habitual complexes, "the tender spots of the psyche," but that is not their specific merit. Rule: "The dream is its own limitation. It is itself the criterion of what belongs to it and of what leads away from it." Only material clearly indicated by the dream images should be used; free association is a zigzag away from the theme, the new method a circumambulation around the dream-image. (Example: many images could "symbolize," really allegorize, the sexual act; the task is to see why the dream chose its own expression.) The dreamer's constant attempt to break away is a neurotic dissociative tendency rooted in conscious resistance to the unconscious and unknown, i.e., **misoneism**, fear of the new, typical of primitive societies and active in civilized ones (pioneers suffer from it; psychology as a young science meets it; "Now let's get back to your dream. What does the dream say?").

**Taking the dream seriously; East African and Church views [§435-437].** A dream must be taken seriously and assumed to mean what it manifestly says; the apparent futility tempts to a "nothing but" explanation. In East Africa a tribe denied having dreams, then explained: "Dreams of ordinary men mean nothing"; only the chief's and medicine-man's dreams (about tribal welfare) mattered, but they ceased to dream "since the British were in the country": the District Commissioner had taken over the function of the "big dream." Views on dreams are ambivalent everywhere: the Church knows *somnia a Deo missa* (dreams sent by God); science explores unconscious processes; the average man thinks little of dreams.

**The unconscious denied; consciousness is recent [§438-440].** Many scientists deny an unconscious psyche on the naïve argument that there would be two subjects in one individual, but that is exactly the case: "the great trouble of our time that so many people exist whose right hand does not know what their left is doing," a symptom of a general unconsciousness that is mankind's heritage. Consciousness took untold ages to develop (civilization dated arbitrarily from writing, about 4000 B.C.) and remains incomplete. Those who deny the unconscious assume knowledge of the psyche is complete, as if present knowledge of nature were the summit; our psyche is part of nature and its enigma limitless; the resistance is not scientific honesty but misoneism. Consciousness is "still in an 'experimental state,'" frail and easily injured. Among primitives a common derangement is "loss of a soul," a dissociation of consciousness; the psyche is not a unit; many hold a man has a "bush-soul" in an animal or tree with which he shares a psychic identity (Lévy-Bruhl's *participation mystique*; examples: a man whose brother is a crocodile is safe swimming; a tree has authority like a parent; others think a man has several souls). [note: Jung's footnote says Lévy-Bruhl later retracted the term under criticism, but that unconscious identity is a well-known psychological fact.]

**Our own dissociability [§441-443].** The same facts persist in civilized people: the right hand not knowing the left, "What the devil has got into you?", moods that possess us, vanishing facts; "self-control is a rare and remarkable virtue"; friends can tell us things about ourselves. Consciousness has not reached reasonable continuity; its dissociability is also useful, enabling concentration by dismissing everything else. The difference: consciousness purposely splitting off and suppressing a part temporarily is "a civilized achievement"; spontaneous splitting without consent is "a primitive and archaic condition or a pathological event and the cause of a neurosis." Perfect control of emotion would deprive social intercourse of "variety, color, warmth, and charm."

## 2. The Functions of the Unconscious [s.17, §444-460]

**A modest hypothesis [§444-445].** The method treats the dream as a spontaneous product of the psyche assumed only to make sense (as every science assumes its object is worth investigating); the unconscious is "at least on a level with the louse," which interests the entomologist. The hypothesis of an unconscious psyche is "so simple that it amounts to a tautology": a content of consciousness disappears and cannot be reproduced; or we have a hunch ("something is in the air"). A vanished content does not cease to exist, as a car round a corner does not. Demonstration: a continuous tone at the edge of audibility alternates audible and inaudible through periodic changes of attention; it never ceases.

**Temporarily eclipsed contents [§446-448].** The unconscious consists first of temporarily eclipsed contents that keep influencing conscious processes: the distracted man who forgets why he went to a place in his room but is guided by the lost purpose; the neurotic who "hears and does not hear, sees yet is blind, knows and does not know at the same time." Unconscious contents behave as if conscious; you can never be sure whether thought, speech or action is conscious. The "medical prejudice" that hysterics are confirmed liars ignores dissociability: anesthetic areas appear and disappear with attention; under hypnosis they can recall all that happened in the anesthetized area. Case: a woman admitted to a clinic in stupor did not know where she was or the date; under hypnosis she narrated verifiably how she fell ill, who received her, and the time of admission from a not very conspicuous clock in the entrance hall. Clinical evidence does not confine the unconscious to pathology: neurotic phenomena are normal occurrences pathologically exaggerated; everyday life is "a mine of evidential material."

**New contents arising; forgetting [§449-452].** Contents can also arise from the unconscious: really new thoughts and creative ideas "grow up from the dark depths like a lotus"; dream material may be new, not merely memory. Forgetting is normal: attention deflected like a searchlight moving; forgotten contents persist subliminally and can return after years or by hypnosis. Repressed contents are disagreeable ones lost (Nietzsche: when pride is insistent enough, memory gives way). **Subliminal perception:** the professor walking in the country with a pupil is interrupted by childhood memories; he goes back to the farm they passed and recognizes the smell of geese from his youth, perceived without noticing; this shows the energy of subliminal perception can interrupt conversation.

**Cryptomnesia [§454-457].** "Concealed recollection": a word, sentence, image or story appears in creative writing without the author noticing it as peculiar. Jung's example: Nietzsche's *Zarathustra* ("Great Events") on the descent to hell of Zarathustra, noticed by a ship's crew shooting rabbits on an isle, closely parallels a 1686 ship's-log story in Justinus Kerner's *Blätter aus Prevorst* (1831-39), about men flying into the crater of Stromboli, whom the crew recognized. Jung had read Kerner four years earlier (in 1898) and recognized the story on reading Nietzsche with *déjà vu*; in 1902 he wrote to Nietzsche's sister, who recalled that she and her brother had read Kerner when Nietzsche was eleven. The Benoît/Rider Haggard case (*L'Atlantide* and *She*) might also be cryptomnesia, except that it is an elaboration of a *représentation collective* (Lévy-Bruhl's term for general ideas characteristic of primitive societies), to be treated later.

**Consciousness as projector; the other subject [§458-460].** Subliminal material owes its unconsciousness to conscious contents losing energy (attention or emotional tone) to make room for new ones; consciousness is like a projector casting light on new perceptions and on traces of old ones, sometimes forced by a strong internal or external stimulus. Many overestimate will-power and think nothing can happen in their minds they do not intend. One should discriminate intentional contents (from the ego-personality) from unintentional ones, arising from "a subliminal part of the ego, from its 'other side,' which is in a way another subject": a normal fact. Example: a colleague, teetotaler and believer, about a friend's "idiotic" act replied "Of course he is an ass," corrected himself to "a highly intelligent man," then angrily denied having said it. His right hand did not know what his left was doing; "memory prefers to give way."

## 3. The Language of Dreams [s.18, §461-494]

**Concepts carry subliminal charge [§461-464].** Every content of consciousness has subliminal equivalents; even a carefully defined philosophical or mathematical concept is more than we assume, "at the least a psychic event, the nature of which is actually unknowable"; numbers are mythological entities (divine for the Pythagoreans). General terms such as "state," "money," "health," "society" vary between persons, in emotional tone and application, unnoticed. A dream of Jung's: a Mr. X trying to get behind him and jump on his back, which turned out to be the colloquialism "Du kannst mir auf den Buckel steigen" ("you can climb on my back") meaning "I don't give a damn what you say," bringing up an incident (his words twisted into a travesty) he had not realized made him angry. The image is symbolic since not stated directly. Jung does not believe the unconscious intends to conceal; pictorial, picturesque language is characteristic of dreams and shows our inability to understand the emotionally charged picture-language, not intentional concealment.

**The primitive mind and modern fear of the irrational [§465-467].** Civilized people discarded the "trimming of fantasy" for accurate statements, losing what primitives keep: objects surrounded by a "fringe of associations" (William James's "fringe of consciousness"): a nocturnal animal seen by day is a medicine-man in changed shape, a doctor-animal, ancestor or bush-soul; a tree has a soul and voice; certain South American Indians say they are red araras. "What we call psychic identity or participation mystique has been stripped off our world of things"; we are baffled when it reappears and conclude something is wrong. Educated people consult Jung terrified by dreams, fantasies or visions, assuming that only the pathological have them: a theologian thought Ezekiel's visions morbid and panicked when something similar happened to him. Primitive man would think of spirits or gods and never doubt his sanity; modern man resembles an old psychotic doctor who disinfected the heavens with mercury chloride and found no trace of God: "What we find instead of God is a neurosis or something worse, and the fear of God has changed into a phobia." Case: a philosophy and psychology professor with cancer phobia ("I know there is nothing, but there still might be something"), more humiliating than a primitive's belief in ghosts. "The primitive phenomenon of obsession has not vanished... It is only interpreted in a different and more obnoxious way."

**Archaic remnants reconsidered [§468-470].** Freud called archaic dream elements "archaic remnants," implying the unconscious is a dustbin of the discarded. Jung says later investigation showed such images belong to the regular structure of the unconscious in everybody; they "continue to function" and are "of vital value just because of their 'historical' nature": "a sort of language that acts as a bridge" between conscious expression and a more primitive pictorial expression that appeals to feeling and emotion, translating truths from their ineffectual "cultural" form. Example: a lady with stupid prejudices and stubborn arguments who ignores the doctor's polite urging; she dreams of a social affair where the hostess, with "all your friends are already here and are expecting you," opens a door and she steps into a cowshed. The concrete, drastic language "went home" though she would not admit it.

**Compensation: warning dreams [§471-473].** The general function of dreams is "to balance such disturbances in the mental equilibrium by producing contents of a complementary or compensatory kind." Dreams of heights, balloons, flying and falling accompany inflated self-estimation and grandiose plans; if the warning is ignored, real accidents replace it. Case: a man entangled in shady affairs who developed a passion for dangerous mountaineering ("get above himself"), who dreamed of stepping off a summit into the air; Jung warned him of death in a mountain accident, in vain; six months later he let go of the rope "as if jumping into the air" and fell with his friend. Case: a lady living in a fantasy of distinction who had shocking dreams, then menacing dreams about long lonely forest walks; a week after Jung's warnings she was murderously attacked by a sexual pervert and rescued in time (two broken ribs and a fractured laryngeal cartilage). Dreams may announce situations long before, but this is not necessarily miracle or precognition: "most crises or dangerous situations have a long incubation, only the conscious mind is not aware of it." Dreams do not always warn: "The mysterious finger may even point the way to perdition. One cannot afford to be naïve in dealing with dreams." They originate in "a spirit that is not quite human, but is rather the breath of nature, of the beautiful and generous as well as the cruel goddess"; "Civilization is a most expensive process."

**The fringe of consciousness; function of symbols [§474-476].** Dream study shows the "fringe of consciousness": seemingly redundant material are "the almost invisible roots of conscious contents," the bridge between unconscious and conscious, between the rational world and the world of instinct. The more consciousness is shaped by prejudices, fantasies, infantile wishes and the lure of external objects, the more the gap widens to neurotic dissociation and "an artificial life far removed from healthy instincts, nature, and truth." Rational talk is "far too flat and colorless"; dreams give images that reach deeper strata: "the interpretation of dreams enriches consciousness to such an extent that it relearns the forgotten language of the instincts." Instincts are perceived sensually as physiological urges and appear as fantasies, but mostly show themselves in images, many symbolic. It is better for conscious and unconscious to run in parallel than be dissociated, so symbol production is valuable. Even uninterpreted dreams can shift mood; most work subliminally; interpretation becomes desirable when the dream is very impressive or recurs, and is imperative in pathological cases unless there are counterindications such as a latent psychosis; unintelligent application of dream analysis is not advisable, especially with a one-sided consciousness and a correspondingly "crazy" unconscious.

**Typical motifs and recurrent dreams [§477-479].** Because conscious contents vary, compensation varies; most dreams are individual and atypical, but there are typical motifs: falling, flying, being chased by dangerous animals or men, being insufficiently or absurdly clothed in public, being in a hurry or lost in a milling crowd, fighting with useless weapons or defenseless, running and getting nowhere; a typical infantile motif: growing infinitely small or big. **Recurrent dreams** (some from childhood to old age) compensate a conscious defect, or date from a trauma that left a prejudice, or anticipate a future event. Jung's own recurring dream: discovering an unknown wing of his house (parents' place, with his father studying comparative anatomy of fishes in a laboratory and mother running a hostelry for ghostly visitors; a historical guest-house several hundred years old; finally a library with unknown books, in one of which he found marvelous symbolic pictures, waking with pounding heart). Weeks later a parcel arrived: a sixteenth-century parchment alchemical volume with symbolic pictures he had ordered; the dream anticipated his new field (alchemy) and the dream ended thirty years ago.

**Symbols beyond dreams; collective symbols [§480-482].** Symbols occur in thoughts, feelings, acts and situations, and it sometimes looks as if inanimate objects concur (Frederick the Great's clock stopping at his death; a mirror breaking or coffee-pot exploding at a crisis); whether or not factual, the stories' persistence shows their psychological importance. The most important symbols are collective, found mainly in religions: believers say revealed, skeptics say invented; both wrong: they are *représentations collectives* from remote ages, "revelations" only in the sense of images from dreams and creative fantasies, involuntary and spontaneous. "There was never a genius who sat down with his pen or brush and said: 'Now I am going to invent a symbol.'" **"A sign is always less than the thing it points to, and a symbol is always more than we can understand at first sight."** We pass through a sign to its goal but stay with a symbol because it promises more than it reveals.

**Symbols reveal in time; the rule for the analyst [§483].** "A symbol does not disguise, it reveals in time." If dream contents agree with a sex theory we know their essence already; if symbolic we know we do not yet understand. Interpretation differs if one treats the dream as symbolic or as disguising an already known thought; in the latter case "you find only what you know already." Advice to pupils: "Learn as much as you can about symbolism and forget it all when you are analyzing a dream." Jung makes it a rule to admit that he never understands a dream well enough to interpret it correctly, to check his own associations; explore the context of dream images thoroughly.

**Jung's house dream (with Freud) [§484-490].** *The dream:* "my house," first floor: a cozy eighteenth-century drawing-room never seen before; ground floor: dark, panelled walls, sixteenth-century or older furniture; a door and stone steps down to a vaulted cellar with stone slabs, old walls with brick-splinter mortar (Roman); an iron ring in a slab, which he lifted to find narrow steps down to a cave, a prehistoric tomb with two skulls, bones and broken pottery. *Interpretation (Jung's own):* a summary of his mental life: grew up in a house two hundred years old, furniture a hundred years old; greatest mental adventure Kant and Schopenhauer; the news of the day was Darwin; the medieval world of his parents (divine providence) had become obsolete; his Christian faith relativized by Eastern religion and Greek philosophy: so the ground floor was dark and uninhabited. His real associations (fossil man, Neanderthal, Dubois's Pithecanthropus, from comparative anatomy and paleontology as assistant at the Anatomical Institute) he did not tell Freud, who thought Jung anticipated his early death (from Jung's interest in the mummified corpses in the Bleikeller in Bremen, 1909, on their trip to America). Jung, fearing to lose Freud's friendship and sensing "the almost unbridgeable gap" between their outlooks, "told him a lie" about his associations; he suggested the skulls might be family members whose death he might desire, which Freud accepted, but Jung felt it phony. Jung had a sudden intuition "about the role which the subjective factor plays in psychological understanding": "It was not Freud's dream, it was mine; and suddenly I understood in a flash what my dream meant."

**Consequences for method [§491-494].** The episode shows difficulties of real dream analysis: so much depends on personal differences between analyst and analysand. "Dream analysis on this level is less a technique than a dialectical process between two personalities"; treated as a technique it reduces to who will dominate whom, which is why Jung gave up hypnosis: to protect the patient's dignity and freedom, letting healing grow from the patient's own personality rather than suggestion. He could not share Freud's almost exclusive interest in sex, which is often secondary to hunger, power drive, ambition, fanaticism, envy, revenge, or "the devouring passion of the creative impulse and the religious spirit." He realized that "before we construct general theories about man and his psyche we should learn a great deal more about the real human being, rather than an abstract idea of *Homo sapiens*."

## 4. The Problem of Types in Dream Interpretation [s.19, §495-520]

**Psychology involves two subjects [§495-497].** In other sciences a hypothesis may be applied to an impersonal object; in psychology two individuals confront each other, neither depersonalized, so "the application of a one-way rule is excluded" and progress requires mutual agreement. The objectivity of a result is checked against standards of the social milieu and the participants' sanity, but this does not mean complete collectivization: "a sane and normal society is one in which people habitually disagree." Disagreement is a vehicle of mental life, not a goal; agreement is equally important. Psychology depends on balanced opposites, so no judgment is final unless reversible; there is no standpoint outside psychology. Yet some generalities are needed to classify, since theory cannot be built by describing individuals alone; any likeness will do as a principle of classification. Jung chooses the easily observed fact that people are **extraverted** or **introverted**. The analyst has individual peculiarities that act as prejudices; he is no superman because he has a theory and technique, which are mere hypotheses; he should confront the analysand with "his own living wholeness"; "He will be tested just as much as the analysand."

**Types clash in analysis [§498-499].** If analyst and analysand share a type they may sail along; if one is extravert and the other introvert they may clash, especially if unaware of their type or sure it is the only right one: the value of one is the non-value of the other (one follows the majority, the other rejects it because it is everybody's taste). Freud interpreted introverts as morbidly self-engrossed, while introspection and self-knowledge can be of great value. The extravert emphasizes externals; the introvert the way he takes a situation. These two are obvious but too general; Jung looked for further basic peculiarities.

**The four functions [§500-504].** Jung observed people who never use their minds yet are not stupid, others who use minds stupidly, intelligent people who seem not to use their senses (not seeing, hearing, noticing their bodies), and others living as if the present state were final, "devoid of all imagination," with no real "tomorrow." He concluded that those who use their minds think, adapting intellectually; the equally intelligent who do not think find their way by **feeling**. Feeling needs definition: not sentiment, not opinion ("The President feels..."), not intuition ("I had a feeling"), not sensation. Feeling is "a judgment of value: agreeable or disagreeable, good or bad," not an emotion or affect (an involuntary manifestation with bodily reactions). Thinking and feeling are **rational** functions; **sensation** and **intuition** are **irrational** (intuition as hunch is involuntary, depending on external or internal circumstances, like sense perception). The four functions as means of orientation: **sensation tells you that something exists; thinking tells you what it is; feeling tells you whether it is agreeable or not; intuition tells you where it comes from and where it is going.**

| Function | Rational / irrational | Tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Sensation | irrational | that something exists |
| Thinking | rational | what it is |
| Feeling | rational | whether it is agreeable or not |
| Intuition | irrational | where it comes from and where it is going |

These four are "just so many viewpoints among others, such as will-power, temperament, imagination, memory, morality, religiousness, etc.," nothing dogmatic, and are valuable as a practical terminology: Jung finds classification useful for explaining parents to children, husbands to wives, and for one's own prejudices [§504].

**Sacrificing one's predilections [§505-506].** To understand another's dream one must sacrifice one's own predilections and suppress prejudices temporarily, "a moral effort that is not everyone's cup of tea." The patient has the same right to be listened to. The patient's resistance is not necessarily wrong; it signals that something does not "click" (not yet ready or the interpretation does not fit). In interpretation the main source of error is **projection**, filling gaps by assuming what I think is also my partner's thought; avoid it by establishing the context of dream images and excluding theoretical assumptions "except for the heuristic hypothesis that dreams somehow make sense."

**Compensation as working hypothesis; three examples [§507-509].** "There is no rule, let alone a law, of dream interpretation," but compensation is "the most promising and most fertile hypothesis." (1) A man with a high idea of his moral superiority dreamed of a drunken tramp in a ditch and said "It's awful to see how low a man can fall!"; it deflated him, and revealed a degenerate alcoholic younger brother, "and of the brother who was also himself." (2) A lady proud of her psychological understanding kept dreaming of a woman she disliked as vain and intriguing, who in the dream was friendly like a sister: she was "shadowed" by an unconscious character like that woman, unaware of her power-complex and shady motives. (3) Positive qualities can be repressed too: a modest, self-effacing man, whose judgment hinted at a higher level, dreamed of Napoleon and Alexander the Great: his inferiority complex was compensated, and the dream asks "what sort of man must I be to have such illustrious callers?"; he nursed "a secret megalomania as an antidote to his inferiority complex," keeping aloof from obligations; "no monument witnesses to his deeds"; *Ducunt volentem fata, nolentem trahunt* ("the fates lead the willing, but drag the unwilling"). Such dreams are true wish-fulfillment: they anticipate an achievement without the merit.

**Why dreams are not plain: against the "censor" [§510-512].** Freud's "censor" was supposed to twist dream images to protect sleep; but dreams just as often disturb sleep. Instead, consciousness itself has a blotting-out effect on subliminal contents; Janet's *abaissement du niveau mental*, a lowering of energic tension, makes contents vague, their relations "vaguely analogous instead of rational." The dream, "being essentially a subliminal process, cannot produce a definite thought, unless it should cease to be a dream"; it skips just what is important to the conscious mind and shows the "fringe of consciousness, like the faint glimmer of the stars during a total eclipse of the sun." Dream symbols are mostly manifestations of a psyche beyond conscious control; "As a plant produces its flower, so the psyche creates its symbols." If the unconscious contains too many things that normally ought to be conscious, its function is twisted, since repressed contents "overlay... the normal unconscious psyche and distort its natural symbol-producing function."

**Therapeutic sequence and tact [§513-518].** Psychotherapy usually begins by eliciting confession of what the patient dislikes, is ashamed of or fears (as in the older confession in the Church); but with overpowering inferiority feelings it is often more profitable first to give a positive outlook, a foundation, before painful insights. Example: dream of tea with the Queen of England or intimacy with the Pope: if the dreamer is convinced of his greatness, a damper (reductive treatment) is indicated, showing infantile wishes to equal parents; for "a worm already crushed by the weight of his inferiority" a further lowering is cruelty. "There is no therapeutic technique or doctrine that is generally applicable, since every case that comes for treatment is an individual in a specific condition." Case: a patient seen a few weeks a year for nine years; Jung knew his trouble but every approach met violent defense; he followed the patient's inclination and dreams, led away from the central problem; the patient improved, and in the tenth year declared himself cured and thanked him for "unfailing tact and patience in helping me to circumvent the painful cause of my neurosis": telling at the start would have destroyed the rapport and left him "morally bankrupt"; trust grew with his improvement. His confession followed: the original shock was such that he could not face it alone; "It needed the two of us, and that was the therapeutic task, not the fulfillment of theoretical presuppositions." Jung's conclusion from sixty years: treat every case "as a new experience," seeking the individual approach, learning the patient's language and following "the gropings of his unconscious towards the light."

**Same dream, different meaning; symbols with opposite meanings [§519-520].** Two people can have almost the same dream and need different interpretations. Example: a group of young men riding across a field, the dreamer in the lead clearing a water ditch just, the others falling in. The young dreamer was cautious, introverted, afraid of adventure: the dream says what he ought to do; the old dreamer, bold and active in life but now an invalid who would not settle, harmed himself by restlessness: the dream says what he still does; his remaining spirit of adventure was his greatest trouble. "Symbols have not one meaning only but several, and often they even characterize a pair of opposites," as the morning star (*stella matutina*) is a symbol of Christ and of the devil (Lucifer), and the lion likewise. Correct interpretation depends on context (associations) and the actual condition of the dreamer's mind.

## 5. The Archetype in Dream Symbolism [s.20, §521-559]

**When personal associations do not suffice [§521-522].** Compensation is broad, but only a minority of dreams are manifestly compensatory; for symbolic dreams the dreamer provides the associative context and one circumambulates the image, which suffices for ordinary dreams. But for outstanding, obsessive, recurrent or highly emotional dreams personal associations are insufficient; elements occur that are not individual and cannot be derived from personal experience: Freud's "archaic remnants," "aboriginal, innate, and inherited patterns of the human mind." As the body is "a whole museum of organs" with an evolutionary history, so the mind has a biological, prehistoric, unconscious history beginning with archaic man, "whose psyche was still similar to that of an animal." The psychologist needs a "comparative anatomy of the psyche": experience of dreams and unconscious products on one side, mythology in its widest sense on the other; without both one cannot see the analogy between compulsion neurosis, schizophrenia or hysteria and classical demonic possession.

**Definition of the archetype [§523-524].** Jung calls the "archaic remnants" **archetypes** or **primordial images** (Greek *archē*, origin, and *tupos*, blow or imprint). The term is misunderstood as a definite mythological image or motif; that would be a conscious representation and absurd to inherit. "The archetype is, on the contrary, an inherited tendency of the human mind to form representations of mythological motifs: representations that vary a great deal without losing their basic pattern" (example: hostile brothers). The tendency is instinctive "like the specific impulse of nest-building, migration, etc. in birds." These *représentations collectives* occur everywhere, not assignable to time, region or race, and reproduce themselves where transmission by migration is ruled out. Critics wrongly assume "inherited ideas." If they were conscious and acquired we would understand them and not be bewildered when they appear: Jung recalls highly educated people, even psychiatrists, baffled by their own or their children's dreams; a professor with a sudden vision who thought he was crazy and came in panic: Jung took a four-hundred-year-old volume from the shelf and showed the woodcut of his vision ("They knew all about your vision four hundred years ago"); he sat down "entirely deflated but once more normal."

**The child's twelve dreams [§525-530].** A psychiatrist brought Jung a handwritten booklet of dreams his ten-year-old daughter had at age eight, given as a Christmas present (each begins in the German original "Once upon a time..."). She died of an infectious disease about a year later; Jung knew her but had no chance to question her. The dreams:

| # | Motif |
|---|---|
| 1 | The "bad animal": a snakelike monster with many horns that kills and devours all other animals; God comes from the four corners, really four gods, and gives rebirth to all the animals |
| 2 | Ascent into heaven where pagan dances are celebrated; descent to hell where angels do good deeds |
| 3 | A horde of small animals frightens her; they grow enormous and one devours her |
| 4 | A small mouse is penetrated by worms, snakes, fishes and human beings; the mouse becomes human: origin of mankind in four stages |
| 5 | A drop of water seen through a microscope is full of branches: origin of the world |
| 6 | A bad boy throws bits of earth at passers-by, who become bad too |
| 7 | A drunken woman falls into water and comes out sober and renewed |
| 8 | In America many people roll in an ant heap, attacked by ants; the dreamer falls panicking into a river |
| 9 | In a desert on the moon she sinks so deep she reaches hell |
| 10 | She touches a luminous ball; vapors come out; a man comes and kills her |
| 11 | Dangerously ill, birds come out of her skin and cover her completely |
| 12 | Swarms of gnats hide sun, moon and stars except one star, which falls on the dreamer |

Jung's reading: the leading thoughts are "in a way like philosophical problems"; no personal associations; the father could not elucidate them by context. Dream 1: restitution of all things, *apocatastasis* (Acts 3:21; Origen and the early Greek Fathers; Matthew 17:11; 1 Corinthians 15:22), yet her Protestant parents "know the Bible only from hearsay" and the idea was never explained to her. Nine of the twelve concern destruction and restoration; dreams 4 and 5 give a cosmogonic myth; none shows more than superficial traces of Christian influence and they resemble primitive tales. The redeemer is the world-wide pre-Christian hero-rescuer motif, devoured by a monster and reappearing; the hero is "a typical image, an archetype, which has existed since time immemorial." The best examples of spontaneous archetypal images come from individuals, especially children, in milieus where direct knowledge of the tradition is out of the question.

**Details of the dreams [§532-534].** The "four gods from the four corners" (a corner of what? no room is mentioned): the **quaternity** is strange to a middle-class milieu; important in Eastern religions; superseded in Christianity by the Trinity; once current in medieval Hermetic circles, obsolete for two hundred years. The horned serpent appears in Latin alchemy as *quadricornutus serpens* (four-horned serpent), a symbol of Mercurius and antagonist of the Trinity, found in one author (Gerard Dorn, 17th-century Frankfurt physician and alchemist, per the translator's footnote). Dream 2 reverses values (pagan dances in heaven, good deeds in hell), a relativization of moral values "worthy of Nietzsche's genius," akin to the East.

**The compensatory meaning: preparation for death [§535-539].** What compensatory meaning did the dreams have for her? Had the dreamer been a primitive medicine-man they would be variations on philosophical themes: death, resurrection, restitution, origin of the world, creation of man, relativity of values (Laotze: "high stands on low"). They are analogous to the doctrines taught to young people in tribal initiation (what the gods or founding animals did, how the world and man were created, the end of the world, the meaning of death); in Christian civilization such instruction is given at the start of adolescence, and many think of it again in old age. The child was at both points: approaching puberty and the end of her life. There is little about the beginning of adult life and many allusions to destruction and restoration, "the opposite of what one would expect" at her age; Jung felt they foreboded disaster: the atmosphere of *vita somnium breve*, life as *ver sacrum vovendum* (a vow of a vernal sacrifice). Experience shows the unknown approach of death casts an *adumbratio*, an anticipatory shadow, on life and dreams (the Christian altar is both tomb and resurrection place). The dreams were a preparation for death, short stories like primitive initiation instruction or Zen koans; the instruction is not orthodox Christian but "like primitive thought," from the matrix that since prehistoric times has fed speculation on life and death. Thought-forms, normally dormant, are aroused to accompany a fatal issue: "Like the instincts, the collective thought-patterns of the human mind are innate and inherited."

**Instinct, psyche beyond consciousness, unconscious collaboration [§540-542].** Emotional manifestations are recognizably the same everywhere and understood among animals. Insects with complex symbioses often never know their parents. Why should man alone lack specific instincts? The psyche is not a *tabula rasa*: "the psyche is more than consciousness." Civilized people cannot say why we have a Christmas tree or colored Easter eggs; "things were generally done first and... only a long time afterwards somebody asked a question about them." Consciousness depends on collaboration of the unconscious: the next sentence of a speech, a name you cannot recall; "you depend on the goodwill of your unconscious." Seemingly personal reactions rest on "a preformed and ever-ready instinctive system with its own characteristic and universally understandable thought-forms, reflexes, attitudes, and gestures," laid down before reflective consciousness. Jung speculates that reflective consciousness may have originated in violent emotional clashes: the savage who strangles his beloved only son in anger at catching no fish, and is seized with regret; Ramón Lull, who after a long chase of his lady met her at a secret rendezvous and was shown her cancer-eaten bosom, and "became a holy man."

**Archetypes as dynamic factors; prognostic dreams [§543-546].** Often in sudden transformations an archetype has been at work for a long time "skillfully arranging circumstances" toward a crisis; archetypal forms "are not just static patterns, but dynamic factors that manifest themselves in spontaneous impulses, just as instincts do." Dreams as prognostication are an ancient belief (Artemidoros of Daldis, 2nd century: a man dreamed of his father dying in a burning house and himself later died of phlegmone, i.e., fire/high fever). Modern parallel: a patient with no knowledge of the nature of his doctor's illness dreamed three weeks before the doctor's death from a gangrenous fever (phlegmone) that he was perishing in a great fire. The unconscious perceives subliminally and "submits [them] to a sort of examination that anticipates their ultimate result," but proceeds "in an instinctive way rather than along rational lines," guided by archetypes: "a poet" rather than a doctor who would say infection and toxins; the dream calls the body a man's earthly house and the fever a conflagration. The archetypal mind "has autonomously taken over the task of prognostication" and functions like complexes with their own initiative and energy.

**Numinosity; archetypes and myth [§547-548].** One perceives the specific energy of archetypes as **numinosity**: "the fascination or spell that emanates from them." Personal complexes produce only personal bias; archetypes create "myths, religions, and philosophical ideas that influence and set their stamp on whole nations and epochs." As complex products compensate one-sided attitudes, religious myths can be read as "a sort of mental therapy for the sufferings of mankind, such as hunger, war, disease, old age, and death." The universal hero myth shows a powerful man or god-man vanquishing dragons, serpents, monsters and demons, liberating his people; narration, ritual repetition, dances, music, hymns, prayers and sacrifices grip the audience with numinous emotions and exalt participants to identification with the hero, giving "a certain form and style to the life of a society." Examples: the Eleusinian mysteries (suppressed at the beginning of the seventh century) together with the Delphic oracle formed "the essence and spirit of ancient Greece"; Christianity owes its name to another antique mystery of the god-man, rooted in the Osiris-Horus myth.

**"Invent" versus "find"; living symbols before reflecting on them [§549-553].** The modern prejudice that basic mythological ideas were "invented" by a clever prehistoric philosopher and believed by the credulous: "invent" is from *invenire*, to "come upon" or "find," and then to find by seeking, with a foreknowledge. The child did not seek her ideas and was surprised at finding them; she offered them as a Christmas gift, tying them to the Christian mystery of birth blended with the evergreen tree carrying the newborn Light; her parents could only say "Oh, it's just a Christmas custom!", whereas a serious answer would need the symbolism of the dying god in antiquity and the Great Mother and her tree. "Paradoxically enough, we know more about mythological symbolism than did any age before our own," because formerly men "lived their symbols rather than reflected upon them." Mount Elgon (East Africa): the people at dawn spit or breathe into their hands and hold them to the first rays, to *mungu* (cognate with *mana* or *mulungu*, an all-pervading "power" that we call divine); asked why, they could only say "We have always done it"; they laughed at the idea that the sun is *mungu*: *mungu* is the actual moment of sunrise. Jung: they offered their souls to *mungu*, breath and spittle meaning "soul substance" (compare Christ healing the blind with spittle, a son inhaling his dying father's last breath); "they live it while we reflect on it." Goethe's Faust: "*Im Anfang war die Tat*" (in the beginning was the deed): deeds were not invented but done; thoughts are a late discovery; man was first moved by unconscious factors and only later reached "the preposterous idea that he must have moved himself." The mind has grown to consciousness as an acorn grows into an oak, "and thus we are moved by forces from within as well as from without."

**Modern possession by unnamed powers [§554-559].** In a mythological age these forces were called mana, spirits, demons, gods, still active: hunches we flatter ourselves for, "bad luck" or pathology when they go against us; "The one thing we refuse to admit is that we are dependent on 'powers' beyond our control." Will-power is real, but "Where there's a will there's a way" is "the superstition of modern man in general"; he cultivates "a remarkable lack of introspection," and "The gods and demons have not disappeared at all, they have merely got new names": restlessness, vague apprehensions, psychological complications, "an invincible need for pills, alcohol, tobacco, dietary and other hygienic systems," and neuroses. The philosophy professor with cancer phobia again (his philosophical psychology had no unconscious; the border between philosophy and medicine faculties blocked his thinking). "Compartment psychology": an alcoholic cured by a religious movement was held up as proof of divine grace until he relapsed, whereupon the organization declared the case "pathological" and sent him to a clinic. Conclusion: "We believe exclusively in consciousness and free will, and are no longer aware of the powers that control us to an indefinite degree"; to see things rightly we must understand man's past as well as his present, hence "a correct understanding of myths and symbols is of essential importance."

## 6. The Function of Religious Symbols [s.21, §560-577]

**Autonomy of the archetypes; the Iron Curtain as shadow [§560-562].** Civilized consciousness is separated from instincts, which assert themselves indirectly through Janet's **automatisms**: neurotic symptoms, or in normal people unaccountable moods, forgetfulness, slips of the tongue; this shows the autonomy of archetypes and that we are not masters in our own house. Looking at mankind as one individual, it is "like a man carried away by unconscious powers," dissociated like a neurotic, with the Iron Curtain as the dividing line. The West's vices (the diplomatic lie, the double-cross, veiled threats), tolerated shamefacedly and covered by good manners, are thrown back in its face by the East's open and methodical application: "It is the face of our own shadow that glowers at us across the Iron Curtain." The conflict is moral and mental; the nuclear deterrent is "a desperate and undesirable answer, as it cuts both ways." Moral and mental remedies would give "psychic immunity," but fail while we insist only the opponents are wrong; "If we could only see our shadow, we should be immune to any moral and mental infection."

**The Eastern myth and our own [§563-564].** "The East has one big myth," which the West calls an illusion: "the time-hallowed archetypal dream of a Golden Age or a paradise on earth, where everything is provided for everybody, and one great, just, and wise Chief rules over a human kindergarten." The West is in the grip of the same mythology (Welfare State, universal peace, equality, eternal human rights, justice and truth, and "not too loud" the Kingdom of God on earth). But real life consists of "inexorable opposites," a battleground; Christianity expected an early end to the world and Buddhism ends it by turning away from desire; these would be suicidal if not bound up with moral ideas and practices.

**Why people need meaning; the psychiatrist and theological questions [§565-567].** Many have lost faith in a world religion and do not understand it; loss goes unnoticed until suffering comes; then they ask about the meaning of life. Statistics show psychiatrists are consulted more by Protestants and Jews than Catholics, since the Catholic Church still feels responsible for the *cura animarum*. People feel it would make a great difference to have a positive belief in a meaningful way of life, or in God and immortality. Modern man discards such ideas because he cannot find God's throne with a telescope or radar; Jung: they are not "true" *enough*, accompanying human life since prehistory, ready to break through at slight provocation. The argument from salt: even if we did not understand the need for salt, we would profit by it; so with views that help in crises. Denial of such ideas is itself a "belief," as unprovable as religious assertions. There is "a strong empirical reason" to hold beliefs that cannot be proved: they are useful. "Man positively needs general ideas and convictions that will give a meaning to his life." He can bear hardships when convinced they make sense; he is crushed to find himself in "a tale told by an idiot." Religious symbols aim to give life meaning: Pueblo Indians as sons of Father Sun have a perspective beyond individual existence, "infinitely more satisfactory than the certainty that one is and will remain the underdog in a department store"; St. Paul as the messenger of the Lord rather than a weaver of carpets.

**Myths happen; the dream is its own interpretation [§568-570].** "Myths... consist of symbols that were not invented but happened." Jesus did not create the myth of the God-man, which existed many centuries before; he was seized by it, lifted out of the carpenter's shop (St. Mark). Primitive story-tellers never asked where their fantasies came from; in ancient Greece people surmised that stories of the gods were exaggerated traditions about ancient kings and that myth did not mean what it said: "exactly what our time has tried to do with dream symbolism." The Talmud says "The dream is its own interpretation." "Is there anything in nature that is other than what it is?" (the duck-billed platypus, "that original monster which no zoologist would ever have invented"). We call its contents symbolic because they point in different directions and therefore mean something unconscious or not wholly conscious. Symbols irritate the scientific mind because they cannot be formulated satisfactorily; so does affect or emotion: both involve the intervention of the unconscious; "In many cases emotion and symbol are actually one and the same thing."

**Experience cannot be taught by words [§571-572].** The academic psychologist may ignore emotion or the unconscious; the medical psychologist cannot. Target practice is not a battlefield; "No textbook can teach psychology; one learns only by actual experience... symbols are the living facts of life." The same sign differs by use: a cross before a name means the person is dead, but the cross is a rich Christian symbol; the lingam as an all-embracing Hindu symbol versus a street urchin's drawing; mason's "monks and nuns" tiles and locksmith's "male and female keys" are not adolescent fantasies; an educated Hindu's lingam includes meanings no Westerner would connect with the penis. Much depends on the maturity of the dreamer.

**Interpretation is not mechanical [§573-577].** Interpretation "cannot be mechanized and crammed into stupid and unimaginative brains"; it demands ever-increasing knowledge of the dreamer and ever-increasing self-awareness in the interpreter; rules of thumb exist but need prudence; one can follow the right rules and still produce nonsense by overlooking a small detail; a highly intellectual person may lack developed intuition or feeling. Understanding symbols means meeting "the wholeness of the symbol-producing individual," usually requiring study of the person's cultural background; Jung treats every case as new, "a new proposition about which I do not even know the ABC"; routine is useful on the surface but "life itself takes over" at vital problems. Teaching method is hard: the pupil needs specialized knowledge (a "mental tool-shop") but the handling of tools is learned only by undergoing an analysis that acquaints him with his own conflict. Some "normal but unimaginative" people cannot realize that psychic events happen to us spontaneously, assuming what occurs is either done by them or pathological, and succumb most easily to psychic epidemics. In science, imagination and intuition matter over intellect even in physics. Intuition is almost indispensable in interpreting symbols and can win the dreamer's immediate acceptance, but is dangerous: it gives "a false sense of security" and may end in "a sort of mutual dream"; intuition must be brought down to the safe basis of knowledge of facts and logical connections. An honest investigator admits where this is impossible but does not dismiss the phenomena; "Nothing is more vulnerable and ephemeral than scientific theories, which are mere tools and not everlasting truths."

## 7. Healing the Split [s.22, §578-607]

**Natural versus cultural symbols [§578-580].** The medical psychologist is concerned with **natural symbols**, derived from unconscious contents and variations on archetypal motifs, traceable to archaic roots (Jung recommends Mircea Eliade's study of shamanism). **Cultural symbols** are those that have expressed "eternal truths" or are still used in religions; they have been transformed and consciously elaborated into the *représentations collectives* of civilized societies but keep much original numinosity and act as positive or negative "prejudices." Numinous factors "cannot be eradicated without serious loss"; when repressed or neglected, their energy "disappears into the unconscious with unpredictable consequences," reviving whatever is uppermost there and forming "an ever-present destructive 'shadow.'" Even beneficial tendencies "turn into veritable demons when they are repressed," so well-meaning people fear the unconscious and psychology.

**Loss of numinosity [§581-583].** Our times show what it means when "the gates of the psychic underworld are thrown open": the first decades' "idyllic innocence" ended; Germany "disgorged its primitivity," Russia is ruled by it, Africa "set on fire"; the world remains "in a state of schizophrenia"; the Western moral and spiritual tradition has collapsed. Primitive societies show that loss of numinosity means loss of *raison d'être*, social order, then dissolution: "We are now in the same condition." Spiritual leaders are blamed for protecting institutions rather than understanding the mystery of symbols; many believers fear science and psychology and ignore "the numinous psychic powers that forever control man's fate." "We have stripped all things of their mystery and numinosity; nothing is holy any longer." The masses and leaders do not see that it makes no substantial difference whether the world principle is called male and father (spirit) or female and mother (matter); both were numinous symbols whose importance lay in numinosity. Energy never vanishes; emotional energy of numinous phenomena reappears in unconscious manifestations compensating the disturbance of the conscious psyche. Consciousness "has deprived itself of the organs by which the auxiliary contributions of the instincts and the unconscious could be assimilated. These organs were the numinous symbols, held holy by common consent."

**Dehumanized world [§584-587].** "Physical matter" stripped of "Great Mother" is "dry as dust and entirely inhuman"; "spirit" as intellect ceases to be "Father of All." "Through scientific understanding, our world has become dehumanized": thunder is no longer the voice of a god; "No river contains a spirit, no tree means a man's life, no snake is the embodiment of wisdom, and no mountain still harbours a great demon." Man no longer has a bush-soul; the emotional energy of communication with nature "has sunk into the unconscious." This loss "is compensated by the symbols in our dreams," which speak "the language of nature," strange to us, which we must translate into rational concepts of modern speech (freed from mystical participation with things). The surface of our world seems purified of superstition, but is the inner world? Thirteen is still taboo for many; the modern person is "a curious mixture of characteristics acquired over the long ages of his mental development," with skepticism side by side with old prejudices and blind ignorance.

**Numinosity decides interpretation; the archetype as image plus emotion [§588-590].** To interpret one needs to know whether a dream image is still living for the dreamer or merely a piece of conscious knowledge: the number 13 in a dream may mean the dreamer is still under the spell of the unlucky 13 (uncomfortable in room 13 or at a table of thirteen) or merely allude to people who are; "In one case it is a still numinous representation; in the other it is stripped of its original emotionality." So archetypes appear in practice "in their original form—they are images and at the same time emotions. One can speak of an archetype only when these two aspects coincide." An image alone is "a corpuscle with no electric charge"; charged with numinosity, "that is, with psychic energy, then it becomes dynamic and will produce consequences." Treating an archetype as a name or concept is a "great mistake": it is "a piece of life, an image connected with the living individual by the bridge of emotion." The word is "an exchangeable coin in intellectual commerce," but the archetype is "living matter," belongs to the economy of a living individual, and cannot be arbitrarily detached. The cross for a good Christian must be interpreted in the Christian way unless the dream gives strong reasons to the contrary. Words such as anima and animus, the wise old man, the great mother are empty unless you have experienced their numinosity: "the names mean very little, but... the way they are related to you is all-important."

**Recovering the original mind; infantile amnesia [§591-594].** "The symbol-producing function of our dreams is an attempt to bring our original mind back to consciousness, where it has never been before, and where it has never undergone critical self-reflection": "We have been that mind, but we have never known it. We got rid of it before understanding it." The unconscious seems the deposit of shed remnants (illusions, childish fantasies, archaic thought-forms, primitive instincts), which explains resistance and fear: we are shocked less by the primitivity than by the emotionality of the contents, charged with affect and capable of causing panic; repressed, they spread through the personality as neurosis. Those who see a gap in memory try to retrieve infantile memories, but "the gaps in his childhood memories are merely the symptoms of a much greater loss, the loss of the primitive psyche... that lived and functioned before it was reflected by consciousness." As the embryo repeats its prehistory, the mind "grows up through the series of its prehistoric stages"; dreams try to bring back a recollection of the prehistoric as well as infantile world, and these memories can have a healing effect (Freud saw this). The infantile mind has far-reaching complexity from "original identity with the prehistoric psyche" (see the child dreamer above). In infantile amnesia one finds mythological fragments that also appear in later psychoses; if they reappear in adult life they may cause profound disturbance or, in others, "astonishing cures or religious conversions."

**Individuation and two ways of interpreting [§595-596].** Recollecting infantile memories and archetypal modes of functioning widen consciousness provided the regained contents are assimilated and integrated; since they are not neutral, assimilation modifies the personality. In this part of the **individuation process** symbol interpretation has a practical role: symbols are "natural attempts to reconcile and reunite often widely separated opposites." The interpreter errs if he treats only conscious memories as "true" and archetypal contents as fantasy. Dreams and ambiguous symbols owe their forms to repressed contents and to archetypes, so they allow two interpretations: **personal** (morbid influence of repression and infantile wishes) or **archetypal** (the sound instinctive basis). Archetypal contents, however fantastic, are emotional powers; if brushed aside they get repressed and recreate the neurosis; denial annihilates the regained contents and any synthesis. Often the archetype's existence is denied, or it is treated as mere images; once the interpreter strips them of numinosity "they lose their life and become mere words," then "limitless substitution begins; one glides from archetype to archetype, everything means everything": "All the corpses in the world are chemically identical, but living individuals are not." The forms of archetypes are largely interchangeable; their numinosity is a fact and "represents the value of an archetypal event," to be kept in mind throughout the intellectual process. Thinking and feeling are diametrically opposed (thinking abolishes feeling-values and vice versa); "Psychology is the only science that has to take the factor of value (feeling) into account."

**The modern predicament and religions [§597-598].** Our intellect created a world of "monstrous machines" we cannot do without; man's genius invents things that become "better and better means for wholesale suicide." Facing the "avalanche of world population," nature may anticipate attempts by turning man's creative mind against him, "releasing the H-bomb or some equally catastrophic device." "There are no longer any gods whom we can invoke to help us": the great religions suffer "increasing anaemia" because "the helpful numina have fled from the woods, rivers, mountains, and animals, and the God-men have disappeared underground into the unconscious"; we are dominated by *Déesse Raison*, "our overwhelming illusion." With her aid we rid the world of malaria and spread hygiene, and underdeveloped populations grow so fast that food becomes a problem: "We have conquered nature" is "a mere slogan." Where indeed have we conquered nature?

**Change begins with the single individual; the Rabbi's answer [§599-601].** "As any change must begin somewhere, it is the single individual who will undergo it and carry it through... it might be any one of us. Nobody can afford to look around and to wait for somebody else." One might ask whether the unconscious knows something helpful when no conscious answer is in sight. Man today finds neither religions nor philosophies provide the powerful ideas he needs. Each camp has its "if only": Buddhists (follow the noble eightfold path and have insight into the Self), Christians (right faith), rationalists (be intelligent), none of whom manages to solve the problems himself. Christians ask why God does not speak as formerly; Jung tells of a Rabbi who answered: "Nor is there anyone nowadays who could stoop so low." We have forgotten "that God speaks chiefly through dreams and visions": the Buddhist discards unconscious fantasies as distractions; the Christian puts Church and Bible between himself and his unconscious; the rationalist does not know consciousness is not his total psyche, though the unconscious has been a basic scientific concept for over seventy years.

**The unconscious is a natural phenomenon [§602-607].** We cannot afford to be "so God-almighty as to set ourselves up as judges of the merits or demerits of natural phenomena": botany is not divided into useful and useless plants, nor zoology into harmless and dangerous animals, yet we assume "consciousness is sense and the unconscious is nonsense" (do microbes make sense?). The unconscious is "a natural phenomenon that produces symbols, and these symbols prove to be meaningful"; one who has never looked through a microscope is no authority on microbes, and one who has not studied natural symbols seriously cannot judge. Neither religions nor philosophies nor rationalism have been willing to look at the psyche twice; the Catholic Church admits dreams sent by God, but most thinkers make no attempt to understand them; Protestant dogmatics hardly "stoop so low" as to consider the *vox Dei* in a dream. Jung (after "more than half a century investigating natural symbols") concludes "dreams and their symbols are not stupid and meaningless"; their information has little to do with buying and selling, but "the meaning of life is not exhaustively explained by your business activities, nor is the deep desire of the human heart answered by your bank account." While energy is spent investigating nature, "very little attention is paid to the essence of man, which is his psyche"; we receive signals from the symbol-producing part "every night"; "'It's only psychological' too often means: It is nothing." The prejudice arises because we are busy with what we think and forget what the unconscious thinks about us; Freud's teachings "inadvertently increased and confirmed the existing contempt for the psyche": formerly overlooked, now "a dump for moral refuse and a source of fear." Jung's last word: the standpoint is "onesided and unjust" and does not fit the facts; the unconscious "like nature herself, is at least neutral. It contains all aspects of human nature—light and dark, beautiful and ugly, good and evil, profound and silly." The study of individual and collective symbolism "has not yet been mastered. But at last a beginning has been made," and results seem to indicate an answer to many questions perplexing present-day mankind.
