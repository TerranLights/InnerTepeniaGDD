## Overview of this range

The range opens in the middle of the book's final long chapter (its heading and the first part fall in the previous extraction part; the Epilogue that follows is numbered IX, so this chapter is VIII). Jung finishes reading the Hölderlin hymn (the index names it "Patmos") as a descent into the world of primordial images that ends without resurrection, and uses it to state the book's central conclusion: that the regressive longing for the mother (childhood bliss, the womb, the unconscious) must be sacrificed, and that this sacrifice is what creates the world, consciousness and the "new state" of the human being. He demonstrates the claim by an amplification of sacrifice across traditions: Marduk and Tiamat, the Vedic Purusha hymn, the Upanishadic horse sacrifice, Attis and Cybele, Dionysus and Pentheus, the Mithraic bull-slaying, and the Christian crucifixion (read through Augustine's marriage-bed language), and he argues that the sacrifice of the "natural man" in Christianity is a deeper, costlier version of Mithraic sacrifice of animal instinct. He then returns to the Miller fantasy: the hero Chiwantopel's death by green snake and earthquake, the horse and snake as brother and sister, the snake as anima and regenerative or fatal instrument, and the libido's inner conflict between forward and backward striving. He also criticizes Freud's incest theory in detail. The Epilogue (chapter IX) states the therapeutic and methodological lessons of the Miller case: dissociation, the need for symbolic understanding, the limits of purely personalistic psychology, and the aim of integrating the unconscious. The rest of the range is the **Appendix** (Miss Miller's own account, in translation: autosuggestion examples, a dream poem, a hypnagogic poem, and the "Chiwantopel" hypnagogic drama with her own source notes), the **Bibliography**, a table of linguistic abbreviations, and the first part of the **Index** (A through the start of M).

## Chapter VIII (inferred; the chapter before the Epilogue): the Hölderlin hymn and the sacrifice of the mother-longing [pp.654-697] (range opens mid-chapter; chapter continues from the previous part)

**Regression and the collective unconscious [p.654].** The range opens mid-sentence: the "potential" psyche is called the collective unconscious. If the regressive libido activates this layer, life may be renewed or destroyed. Regression carried to its logical end links back to the world of natural instincts, which in its formal or ideal aspect is a kind of prima materia. If consciousness can assimilate this prima materia, the result is reactivation and reorganization of conscious contents. If consciousness cannot assimilate the contents pouring in, they keep their original chaotic, archaic form and disrupt the unity of consciousness. Jung says this mental disturbance is therefore "advisedly called schizophrenia," a madness due to splitting of the mind. [p.654]

**Hölderlin's poem read as a descent to the primordial images [pp.654-662].** Jung reads the poem, which he treats as the poet's own journey into "that wonderland of primordial images," in the same terms as the Miller material.
- Eagles dwelling in darkness, sons of the Alps crossing the abyss on light bridges: the eagle, sun-bird, dwells in darkness (the libido hidden); high above pass the mountain-dwellers (probably gods), symbols of the sun crossing the sky like an eagle over the depths. [p.654]
- "Summits of time" and lovers who must languish on far mountains: Jung reads simultaneous nearness and separation as life in the underworld, where one is united with all that was dear yet cannot enjoy it because all is shadowy and lifeless. The descending soul drinks "innocent water," the drink of rejuvenation, and asks for wings to soar up again, like the winged sun-disc rising swan-like from the water (plates VII, IXa). [p.655]
- "A Genius bore me away": the dark prelude is a premonition of what is to come; the poet begins the journey east, toward sunrise and the mystery of eternity and rebirth. Jung sets beside it Nietzsche's longing for the "nuptial ring of rings, the ring of return." [p.656]
- Asia blooming, gold-flecked Pactolus, ivy, cedars and laurels, divinely built palaces: Jung calls the vision apocalyptic, the mother-city in the land of eternal youth with imperishable spring (pl. XXIIa). The poet identifies himself with John on Patmos. [pp.657-658]
- The Last Supper, the Lord speaking death and last love, the sending of the Spirit, the breaking of the "straight-rayed sceptre": the underlying images are the sacrificial death and resurrection of Christ conceived as the self-sacrifice of the sun, which voluntarily breaks its rayed sceptre in sure hope of resurrection. [pp.658-659]
- Excursus on the solid sunbeam and sceptre: Spielrein's patient said "God pierces the earth with his ray" and that Jesus showed his love by tapping at the window with a sunbeam; Jung has met the idea of the solid substance of the sunbeam in other insane patients. He compares Thor's hammer (splits the earth, penetrates it, comes up again like a treasure in the fullness of time), Kaineus' foot, Samson's jawbone from which a fountain gushed, springs from hoof-marks. Magic wands and sceptres belong to this family of meanings; he cites the etymology of Greek skeptron (staff; skeptos, storm-wind), Latin scapus, Old High German scaft (spear). All are libido-symbols already met. Breaking the sceptre means sacrifice of power previously exercised, i.e., of libido organized in one direction. [pp.659-660]
- Jung defends the poem's passage from Asia to Patmos to the Christian mystery as a significant train of thought, not a loose association: entry into death and the land beyond, seen as the hero's self-sacrifice for immortality. When the sun has set man awaits in secret the renewal of life ("To dwell in loving night ... abysses of wisdom"). Wisdom dwells in the depths, the wisdom of the mother; being one with her grants vision of the primordial images and primitive forces that are the creative matrix of life. [pp.660-661]
- Contrast with Faust: Hölderlin, in "pathological ecstasy," senses the grandeur of what he sees but unlike Faust does not wish to bring to daylight all he has found ("The Highest would not have all at one time"). What the poet sees in his Vulcan's pit is "the Spirit as ever it was," the totality of primary forms from which archetypal images come. In the collective unconscious spirit appears as an archetype of supreme significance, expressed through the divine hero, whose Western counterpart is Christ. [pp.661-662]
- The poem dies away in lament: as Gilgamesh, bringing back the magic herb from the Western Land (pl. XIX), was robbed by the demon-serpent, Hölderlin's descent to the shadows is followed by no resurrection in this world ("the heavenly each demand sacrifice"). [pp.662-663]

**Jung's verdict: the sacrifice of the retrospective longing [pp.663-664].**
- The insight that one must give up the backward longing that wants to resuscitate the torpid bliss and effortlessness of childhood, before "the heavenly ones" wrench the sacrifice from us (and with it the entire man), came too late to the poet. [p.663]
- Jung takes it as wise counsel from the unconscious that the Miller author lets her hero die: he was little more than the personification of a regressive, infantile reverie, with neither will nor power to make good his aversion from this world by fishing up another from the primeval ocean of the unconscious (which would have been truly heroic). [p.663]
- The sacrifice is accomplished only by whole-hearted dedication to life. Libido tied up in family bonds must be withdrawn from the narrower circle into the larger, because the adult, a mere particle revolving in a rotary system as a child, must become the center of a new system. This includes at least consideration of the sexual problem; otherwise unemployed libido stays fixed in the unconscious endogamous relationship to the parents. [p.663]
- Christ's teaching ruthlessly separates a man from his family, and in the Nicodemus dialogue he gave regression a symbolic meaning; both serve freedom from family fixations and infantile feelings. [pp.663-664]
- If libido stays stuck in a childish milieu, the unconscious recreates it everywhere by projecting complexes, reproducing dependence and unfreedom; destiny falls from the stars (Tychai kai Moirai). The Stoics called this Heimarmene, compulsion by the stars, to which every "unredeemed" soul is subject. Libido fixed at its most primitive level leaves people at the mercy of affects; this was the psychological situation of late antiquity, and the saviour and physician of that age sought to free humanity from Heimarmene. [p.664]

**From individual to cosmic sacrifice [pp.664-665].** Miss Miller's vision seems to treat sacrifice as an individual problem, but its symbols (the snake that kills the horse; the hero who sacrifices himself freely) are mythological figures born of the unconscious, so it is a problem for humanity. Since the world is a product of thought, sacrificing the backward-striving libido necessarily results in creation of the world: for him who looks backward, even the starry sky is the mother bending over and enfolding him; renouncing that image and its longing yields the world as we know it. This is the meaning of cosmic sacrifice: the slaying of Tiamat by Marduk (fig. 41), from whose body heaven and earth were made; and, fullest, the Vedic hymns.

**Rig-Veda: Vishvakarman and Purusha [pp.665-668].**
- Rig-Veda asks what wood or tree heaven and earth were hewn from; Vishvakarman, the All-Creator, made the world as a "wise sacrificer," entering all beings and hiding his origin. Purusha (Man, Anthropos), the primal being, encompassed the earth on all sides. [pp.665-666]
- Jung: Purusha is a sort of Platonic world-soul surrounding the earth from outside, with a maternal character: he is the original "dawn state" of the psyche, encompasser and encompassed, mother and unborn child, an undifferentiated unconscious primal being. This state must be sacrificed (and is an object of regressive longing) so that discriminated entities, conscious contents, may arise. The gods, saints and sages sacrifice Purusha on the straw. [pp.666-667]
- Jung notes that logic does violence to the passage (how can sages sacrifice the primal being beside the gods when nothing existed but the primal being?), but if the primal being is the mystery of the original psychic state everything is clear. From the sacrifice come the butter, animals, hymns, chants, metres; the moon from his mind, sun from his eye, Indra and Agni from his mouth, Vayu from his breath, atmosphere from navel, sky from head, earth from feet. [p.667]
- Jung: this is a psychological, not physical, cosmogony: "The world comes into being when man discovers it," and he discovers it only by sacrificing his containment in the primal mother, the original unconsciousness. [p.668]

**Critique of Freud's incest barrier [pp.668-671].**
- Freud conceives what drives man toward this discovery as the "incest barrier," which blocks infantile longing for the mother and forces libido along life's biological path. Jung says the terms (incest prohibition, mother) must be understood metaphorically. Freud's dictum "To begin with we knew only sexual objects" is a sexual allegory reading adult partial truths into infancy; it would be truer to say that earlier we knew nothing but nourishing breasts. Pleasure in sucking is not proof of sexual pleasure (the caterpillar's pleasure in eating; kissing derives more from nutrition than sexuality). [p.668]
- The "incest barrier" is a doubtful hypothesis (admirable for describing some neurotic conditions): a cultural product that nobody invented, grown on the basis of "marriage classes," whose main purpose is not to prevent incest but to meet the social danger of endogamy by instituting cross-cousin marriage. The libido that marries the maternal uncle's daughter could equally possess the mother or sister; primitives are prone to promiscuity; the point is the social need to spread the family organization through the whole tribe. [pp.668-669]
- So it was not the incest taboo that forced humanity out of non-differentiation; rather the evolutionary instinct peculiar to man forced upon him countless taboos, including incest. Against this "other urge" the animal in us fights with instinctive conservatism and misoneism (hatred of novelty); "our mania for progress" is the morbid compensation. [p.669]
- Freud's incest theory describes fantasies accompanying libido regression, characteristic of the personal unconscious in hysterics; they reveal the shadow, and their language is dramatic and exaggerated. The theory matches the hysterical attitude; one should not take it as seriously as Freud did. The last act of the drama is a return to the mother's body, usually through the mouth (being devoured; pl. LXII), giving a still more infantile theory elaborated by Otto Rank. These are makeshifts: regression goes back to the nutritive layer, anterior to sexuality, and sexual language turns into metaphors from nutrition and digestion. The Oedipus complex at this level becomes a "Jonah-and-the-Whale" complex (witch who eats children, wolf, ogre, dragon): fear of incest becomes fear of being devoured by the mother. [pp.669-670]
- The regressing libido apparently desexualizes itself, going back through earliest infancy to the intra-uterine condition and, leaving personal psychology, irrupts into the collective psyche, where Jonah saw the "mysteries" in the whale's belly. There it may stick fast (Theseus and Peirithous in the underworld) or tear loose and return to the surface with new possibilities of life. [pp.670-671]
- In incest and womb fantasies libido immerses itself in the unconscious, provoking infantile reactions from the personal sphere while activating collective images (archetypes) with a compensatory, curative meaning, as myth always had. Freud makes his theory too dependent on the neurotic ideas the patients suffer from, flattering the neurotic's pretense that the causa efficiens lies in the remote past; in reality "the neurosis is manufactured anew every day" by a false attitude. [p.671]

**Rig-Veda X,90's last verse and the Brihadaranyaka horse sacrifice [pp.671-675].**
- "With the sacrifice the gods sacrificed to the sacrifice": sacrifice brings power equal to the gods. As the world is created by renouncing the personal tie to childhood, so the Upanishads teach that the new, immortal state of man is created by a further sacrifice, the horse-sacrifice with cosmic significance (text quoted: dawn is the horse's head, sun his eye, wind his breath, the year his body, mountains his liver and lungs, etc.). [pp.671-673]
- Deussen: the horse-sacrifice signifies renunciation of the world (Schopenhauer had the same train of thought). The horse stands between two sacrificial vessels (day-vessel in the east ocean, night-vessel in the west) like the sun between morning and evening (cf. fig. 3). Jung: the horse is man's steed, energy is measured in horsepower, so the horse is a quantum of energy at man's disposal, i.e., libido that has passed into the world. The "mother-libido" must be sacrificed to create the world; here the world is destroyed by renewed sacrifice of the same libido. The sacrifice can only produce another introversion like that before creation; the horse between the two vessels (birth-giving and devouring mother) suggests life enclosed in the ovum. [p.673]
- Brihadaranyaka 3,3: the Parikshitas and offerers of the horse sacrifice go to the razor-edge gap between the shells of the world-egg; Indra as a falcon delivers them to the wind. Jung: they go to the narrowest gap where they are united and divided; Indra, who in falcon form stole the soma (treasure hard to attain), is psychopomp, delivering souls to the wind, the generating pneuma, individual and universal prana, to save them from "repeated death." This summarizes innumerable myths and shows Indian philosophy as in a sense refined and sublimated mythology. [pp.673-675]

**Animal sacrifice, Attis, Dionysus, Mithras [pp.675-686].**
- In the Miller drama the first to die is the horse, the animal brother of the hero (cf. Enkidu, friend and brother of Gilgamesh). Where animal sacrifice has lost its meaning as a gift and gained higher religious significance, the animal represents the god (bull = Dionysus Zagreus, Mithras; lamb = Christ). Sacrificing the animal means sacrificing animal nature, the instinctual libido. [p.675]
- Attis: son-lover of Agdistis-Cybele; driven mad by his mother's love he castrated himself under a pine; yearly a pine was garlanded, an effigy of Attis hung on it, and it was felled; Cybele took the pine into her cave and lamented. Jung's reduction to the common denominator, libido: the son personifies longing for the mother; the mother personifies (incestuous) love for the son; the tree personifies the mother and the son's phallus; the phallus stands for the son's libido; felling = castration = sacrifice of this libido, which seeks something as incongruous as impossible. The figures are "envisagings" of libido currents, which is why attributes pass from one figure to another. [pp.675-676, fig. 42]
- Impulse to sacrifice comes from the "mater saeva cupidinum" and, as primal being, the mother represents the unconscious; regression is inimical to life and disrupts instinctual foundations, followed by a compensatory reaction of violent suppression of the incompatible tendency. It is a natural unconscious process, experienced by the ego mostly passively. [pp.677-678]
- Further tree-symbolism: Ovid's pine pleasing to the mother of the gods; Osiris overgrown by the cedar (fig. 23); Attis growing from a tree on the Coblenz relief (Mannhardt: vegetation numen; Jung: probably tree-birth as with Mithras, pl. XL); Dionysus Dendrites; Pentheus in the pine, felled and torn apart by Maenads, his mother first. The Pentheus legend contains the tree's phallic meaning, maternal nature and identity with the son, plus the Terrible Mother as counterpart of the Pietà. Attis feast: lamentation then spring joy (Good Friday and Easter); priests (Galloi, archigallos called Atys) castrated, later only scratched their arms. [pp.678-679]
- Mithraic parallel: catching and subduing the bull. The Original Man Gayomart created with his ox, living in bliss six thousand years until the aeon of Libra (house of Venus) let evil loose; they died thirty years later (cf. Zarathustra's thirty years of trials). From the ox came 55 grains and 12 healing plants; its seed went to the moon for purification, Gayomart's to the sun. The bull's soul Gosh/Drvashpa was worshipped as a female divinity, rides in a chariot like Ardvisura; so the "bull-anima" is feminine; Taurus too is a house of Venus. The myth repeats the primitive "closed circle" of a self-reproducing masculine and feminine divinity. Chinese hearth spirit Chi (a lovely maiden in red; "old women" cooks' souls) as another feminine fire; the kitchen god became male family ruler and mediator, a sort of Logos. [pp.679-680]
- Mithras sacrifices only his animal attribute, his instinctuality; he slays "willingly and unwillingly at once"; the anguished, ecstatic face (Benndorf, Cumont quoted; the Ostia head, frontispiece) is one of "sentimental resignation" familiar from patients. Jung notes the spiritual transformation of the first Christian centuries released extraordinary feeling, both charity and sentimentality and infantilism (lamb allegories); "sentimentality is sister to brutality." The morbid expression shows the split of the sacrificer, who is both sacrificer and sacrificed. [pp.681-682]
- Religio and religare: the libido building religious structures regresses ultimately to the mother, the real bond to our origins; so the Fathers' derivation of religio from "linking back" has psychological support. Sexes of the symbols are secondary. The sacrificial drama is an unconscious transformation of energy, which the ego perceives like sailors sensing a submarine volcanic upheaval. [pp.682-683]
- On psychological abstraction: it sobers and flattens the concrete drama, but gives a key to the processes behind the scenes; the mythical figures have interchangeable qualities because they lack the "existential" meaning of physical figures and enact tragedy against introspective consciousness. What matters is not which animal or god but that a sacrifice takes place, a process of transformation in the unconscious whose dynamism, contents and subject are unknown and become visible only by clothing themselves in imaginative material (like dancers in animal skins). Abstraction shows unconscious processes as "autonomous quantities," not epiphenomena; deriving the unconscious from consciousness is "empty talk"; "subconscious" presumes that the unconscious is lower rather than higher. [pp.683-684]
- Forms of sacrifice: annual maiden to the dragon (to mollify the Terrible Mother; symbol of concupiscence); milder: first-born, domestic animals; alternative ideal: self-castration, milder: circumcision (symbolical act replacing sacrifice). Through sacrifice man ransoms himself from fear of death and reconciles with Hades. In late cults the hero becomes divine protagonist, priestly self-sacrificer, renewer of life: deepening of the symbolism reverts to human sacrifice for a stronger expression of self-sacrifice. In Christianity the hero dies of his own free will. [pp.684-685]
- Mithraic krater with coiled snake and lion opposing it: krater = maternal vessel of rebirth, snake = fear and resistance, lion = raging desire. The snake glides toward the bull's blood: an offering to the underworld powers (cf. Odysseus' nekyia). Bull = living hero, snake = dead, buried chthonic hero, but also the devouring mother; bull's blood plus snake = union of opposites, source of the miraculous fertility of the sacrifice. Australian parallel: life-force wears out and must be renewed at intervals by rites. [pp.685-686]

**(Continuing the sacrificial-drama argument.) The Mithraic killing as sacrifice to the unconscious; the Christian sacrifice [pp.686-690].**
- Even on a high level the renewal rites keep their original meaning: the Mithraic bull-killing is a sacrifice to the Terrible Mother, to the unconscious, which attracts energy from a consciousness that has strayed too far from its roots and forgotten "the power of the gods, without whom all life withers or ends catastrophically in a welter of perversity." In sacrifice consciousness gives up its power and possessions in the interests of the unconscious, enabling a union of opposites and a release of energy. The act is also a fertilization of the mother: the chthonic serpent-demon drinks the hero's blood (soul); life becomes immortal because like the sun the hero regenerates himself by self-sacrifice and re-entry into the mother. [p.686]
- The Christian mystery is the same pattern: Attis unmans himself for his mother and his effigy hangs on the pine; Christ hangs on the tree of life, the wood of martyrdom (Hekate and mother; pl. XXXVI), and ransoms creation from death. By re-entering the mother's womb he pays in death for the sin of the Protanthropos Adam and regenerates spiritually the life corrupted by original sin. [pp.686-687]
- Augustine (quoted at length) treats Christ's death as a hieros gamos: like a bridegroom coming from his chamber, Christ mounts the marriage-bed of the cross and consummates his marriage, giving himself to torment in place of his bride, the "matrona" (the Church). Jung: the feeling-tone of the classical hieros gamos has turned into its opposite (torment for lust, martyr's stake for mother and mistress); what was pleasurable, the union of masculine consciousness with the feminine unconscious, is now painful and projected onto a higher psychic level as the union of God with his congregation (corpus mysticum). In modern language, this projection signifies the conjunction of conscious and unconscious, "the transcendent function characteristic of the individuation process"; integrating the unconscious always heals. [pp.687-688]
- Comparison of Mithraic and Christian sacrifice: the Christian symbol's superiority is its frank admission that not only animal instinctuality (the bull) but the entire natural man must be sacrificed. The natural man is more than the theriomorphic symbol: besides submission to the law of the species he has the specifically human ability to deviate from the law (theologically, the capacity for "sin"), which kept other paths open and made spiritual development of Homo sapiens possible. The cost: instinctive guidance is displaced by an abnormal learning capacity (as in anthropoid apes), so uncertainty requires a discerning, evaluating, selecting consciousness. If this compensates instinctual certainty it substitutes rules for instinctive action and intuition, with the danger that consciousness separates from its instinctual foundations and puts conscious will in the place of natural impulse. [p.688]
- Through sacrificing the natural man the dominating ideal of consciousness can fully assert itself and mold human nature; the ideal's loftiness is incontestable, yet on those heights doubt arises whether human nature can be molded this way without damage; only experience will show, and the attempt had to be made to learn whether this self-transformation is possible and whether the self-sacrifice of the natural man is a final solution. Mithraic sacrifice (archaic animal slaughter) aimed to domesticate and discipline instinctual man; the Christian sacrifice (death of a human being) demands surrender of the whole man: total renunciation of instincts and disciplining of specifically human, spiritual functions for a goal beyond this world. This hard schooling alienates man from his own nature and from nature generally; history shows it was possible and produced, within a few centuries, a development of consciousness otherwise impossible. [p.689]
- Such developments have their own inner logic. Materialist criticism of dogma's physical impossibility is beside the point: dogma must be a physical impossibility since it says nothing about the physical world but symbolizes "transcendental" or unconscious processes bound up with the unavoidable development of consciousness. Belief in dogma is an unavoidable stop-gap, to be replaced sooner or later by adequate understanding and knowledge if civilization is to continue. [pp.689-690]

**Miss Miller's fantasy: from horse-sacrifice to hero-sacrifice [pp.690-695].**
- Inner necessity drives the fantasy from horse-sacrifice (renunciation of biological drives) to sacrifice of the hero (human self-sacrifice, renunciation of egohood, ethically deeper). In her case this is only metaphorical: the hero Chiwantopel offers himself, and she only watches admiringly. The moral act is delegated to the hero (her "animus-figure") while she fails to do it herself. The advance from animal to human sacrifice therefore remains only an idea; her participation as pious spectator has no ethical significance. When the hero, vehicle of the vital magical action, perishes, the projection falls away and the sacrificial act recoils upon the subject, the personal ego of the dreamer. Jung says he did not foresee, owing to lack of material, that it would be a psychosis; it was a katoche, total surrender not to life's positive possibilities but to the nocturnal world of the unconscious, a debacle like her hero's. [pp.690-691]
- The snake that kills Chiwantopel: instrument of sacrifice (cf. St. Sylvester legend, virginity test, wounding of Ra and Philoctetes, lance and arrow symbolism); the knife that kills but also the phallus as symbol of the regenerative power of the grain, buried like a corpse yet inseminating the earth (pl. LXIIIa). The snake symbolizes the numen of the transformative act and the transformative substance itself, clear in alchemy. As chthonic cave-dweller she lives in the womb of mother earth, like the Kundalini serpent coiled at the base of the spine. Alchemical legend of Gabricus and Beya: during the hieros gamos Gabricus enters his sister's body completely, is buried in her womb, dissolves into atoms and becomes the soul-snake, serpens mercurialis (fig. 6). A patient fantasized being a snake winding round her mother and crawling into her. [pp.691-692]
- Green snakes in other patients: "a little green snake came up to my mouth ... as if it wanted to kiss me"; Spielrein's patient: "It is God's animal ... green, blue, and white ... can have a human mind ... divine judgment; it is a friend of children." The snake is unmistakably an instrument of regeneration (fig. 37). [p.692]
- Horse = brother, snake = sister of Chiwantopel ("my little sister"). Rider and horse form a centaur-like unit like man and his shadow (higher and lower man; Gilgamesh and Enkidu). The feminine belongs to man as his unconscious femininity, the anima, often appearing in patients as a snake. Green is the life-color and the color of the Creator Spiritus. Jung: "I have defined the anima as the archetype of life itself"; with the snake symbolism she also bears the attribute "spirit," because the anima personifies the total unconscious until she is differentiated from the other archetypes. With further differentiation the (wise) old man detaches from the anima as the archetype of "spirit," standing to her as spiritual father (Wotan to Brunhilde, Bythos to Sophia; Rider Haggard's novels). [pp.692-693]
- For Miss Miller the hero is her "brother-beloved," her "ghostly lover," the animus; she herself is his life-snake which brings him death. After hero and horse die the green snake remains: the author's own unconscious psyche, which will overpower her as it did him. [p.693]
- Conflict of horse and snake, or bull and snake, represents a conflict within the libido: striving forward and backward at once. Libido is not only ceaseless forward movement (Schopenhauer's cosmic Will, death an external mishap): like the sun it also wills its own descent, its involution. First half of life: growth; second half: softly at first, then ever more perceptibly, an altered goal. As youth's urge to expansion may hide under resistance to life, the "other urge" may hide behind obstinate purposeless clinging to life's old form. Illustration: Priapus statue at Verona pointing, with a sidelong smile, to a snake biting his phallus (pl. LXIb). [pp.693-694]
- Rubens' Last Judgment (pl. LXIV): a man castrated by a serpent, illustrating the end of the world. The fantasy of world conflagration is the projected primordial image of the great transformation, the enantiodromia of life into death. The image of consuming change originates in the unconscious and appears in dreams and premonitions; the more the conscious mind refuses the intimation, the more frightening the symbols. The snake is an important fear-symbol in dreams; because of poison it may be an early symptom of physical disease, but usually expresses an abnormally active ("constellated") unconscious and associated, mainly abdominal, physiological symptoms. Interpretation depends on individual circumstances: in youth it denotes fear of life, in age fear of death. [p.694]
- Why the unconscious overpowered Miss Miller: biographical material lacking. Jung often notes in such cases a singularly narrow consciousness, apprehensive stiffness, a horizon bounded by childish naivete or pedantic prejudice; she seems to have had emotional naivete, underestimating her possibilities and leaping into deep water where "some knowledge of the shadow would have been in place." Such people should be given as much psychological knowledge as possible: it may not prevent psychosis but improves prognosis; in border-line cases understanding can be "a matter of life and death." [pp.694-695]
- Chiwantopel's end: the volcano that gave him birth and name swallows him by earthquake. His last words name the longed-for beloved Ja-ni-wa-ma: the lisping "wawa, wama, mama" of Hiawatha's babyhood. "The only one who really understands us is the mother": German verstehen (OHG firstan) from prefix fri- = Greek peri, "round about" (antfriston, to interpret); so understanding = to "stand round about something"; comprehendere, katasyllambanein and erfassen likewise express surrounding and embracing. "When the neurotic complains that the world does not understand him, he is telling us in a word that he wants his mother." Verlaine's "Mon Reve familier" is quoted in full (a woman unknown, loved, who understands). [pp.695-697]

## Chapter IX: EPILOGUE [pp.698-702]
- The Miller fantasies end at the critical moment, when invasion by the unconscious is apparent. Miss Miller, without a clue to her visions' meaning (Flournoy, despite his feeling for values, could not explain them), could not meet the next phase: assimilation of the hero to her conscious personality, which would have required recognizing what fate demanded. Dissociation was already present: the unconscious kept producing images she had not consciously made and felt as strange and portentous; they were products of psychic energy beyond conscious control. The instinctual impulse trying to rouse the dreamer from "the sleep of childhood" was opposed by out-of-place personal pride and a narrow moral horizon. [p.698]
- Our civilization has forgotten how to think symbolically; even theologians have no use for the Church Fathers' hermeneutics; Protestant cure of souls is worse. For such patients it is "a positive life-saver" when the doctor takes the products seriously and gives access to their meanings, letting the patient assimilate part of the unconscious, repairing dissociation by that amount, and guarding against the isolation anyone feels before an incomprehensible, irrational aspect of his personality. Isolation leads to panic, often the start of psychosis. The wider the gap between conscious and unconscious, the nearer the "fatal splitting of the personality": neurosis in neurotic dispositions, schizophrenia and fragmentation in psychotic ones. Aim of psychotherapy: to narrow and abolish the dissociation by integrating the unconscious tendencies. [p.699]
- Normally the promptings are realized "instinctively" and their spiritual content insinuates itself in disguise; this passes smoothly if consciousness holds ideas of a symbolic nature ("for those who have the symbol the passage is easy," say the alchemists). Where a tendency to dissociation exists, each advance of the unconscious widens the gap; outside help is usually needed. [pp.699-700]
- Had he treated Miss Miller, Jung says he would have told her the things in this book to build her conscious mind up to understanding the collective unconscious. Without these representations collectives (psychotherapeutic even for primitives) one cannot understand archetypal associations in unconscious products; a purely personalistic psychology is not enough; whoever treats serious dissociations must know "the anatomy and evolutionary history of the mind," as a physician knows anatomy, physiology, embryology and comparative evolution. Personalistic psychology can remedy neurotic dissociation up to a point, but not transference, which appears in most cases and always hides collective contents. [p.700]
- The Miller case is a classic of the manifestations preceding serious disorder; their presence does not prove disorder must follow; it depends partly on whether the conscious attitude is positive or negative. It suited the book because Jung had no personal part in it and so could refute the charge that he "influenced" the patient. Had the case come at the first sign of spontaneous fantasy, the Chiwantopel episode might have turned out otherwise. [pp.700-701]
- Closing statement: the task was to examine an individual fantasy system in relation to its sources; the problems met are vast and the result only a superficial survey. Jung does not accept withholding working hypotheses because they may be wrong. Doctors cannot choose their territory; the strongest incentive to research has been practice and the question "How can you treat something that you do not understand?" Dreams, visions, fantasies, delusions "are expressive of a situation": not understanding the dreams means not understanding the patient's situation. He never meant to justify his theories by patients but to understand their situation, including the compensatory activity of the unconscious; the physician needs "a science of the psyche, not a theory about it." Science is not bickering about who is right but augmenting and deepening human knowledge. (Fig. 43, antique cameo.) [pp.701-702]

## APPENDIX: THE MILLER FANTASIES (begins p.703)
Editors' note [pp.703-704]: translated from Flournoy's "Quelques Faits d'imagination creatrice subconsciente," Archives de psychologie (Geneva) V (1906) 36-51; Flournoy's 5-page introduction is not translated. Miss Miller wrote in English; the memoir was translated into French (by Flournoy?); the original never came to light, so the text here is a double translation (also in the 1916 Hinkle translation, which did not give the full text). Jung worked from the French; words he considered of special point are given in French in brackets, with indication of passages Flournoy left in English. An English original appeared as "Some Instances of Subconscious Creative Imagination" in J. Am. Soc. Psychical Research 1:6 (June 1907); no evidence Jung knew it. Flournoy describes her as a young American who studied a semester at Geneva and became a journalist and lecturer.

**"Some Instances of Subconscious Creative Imagination," by Miss Frank Miller of New York [p.704 onward].** (This is the primary case text, the source of the fantasies that Jung analyzes in the main book. Extracted below as case material, in compressed form.)
I. Phenomena of transitory suggestion or instantaneous autosuggestion [pp.704-706...]. Miller's own term for a phenomenon in herself: for a few seconds the impressions or feelings of another person are so strongly suggested to her that they seem hers, though she is sure afterward they were not.
1. Caviar: she loves it; if a family member expresses disgust just as she is about to eat it, the disgust becomes hers for a few moments; a minute's effort dispels it. [p.704]
2. Eau-de-Cologne: strong perfumes nauseate her, yet a lady's praise of hers makes the pleasure hers for three to five seconds; she finds it easier to dismiss the agreeable suggestion than the disagreeable. [p.705]
3. Absorbed in a story (reading, listening, theater: Bernhardt, Duse, Irving) she has the illusion of taking part in the action, lasting up to a minute; at Cyrano, when Christian is killed, she felt a real piercing pain in her own breast where he was struck. [p.705]
4. Vivid imagination: a photograph of a steamship at sea instantly produced the throb of the engines, heave of waves, roll of the ship. [pp.705-706]
5. The Egyptian statue: while winding a towel (conical, like an Egyptian pointed head-dress) round her head before a shower, she had for a moment, with "almost breath-taking clarity," the sense of standing on a pedestal as an Egyptian statue (stiff limbs, one foot forward, insignia in hand); it faded "like a rainbow" and returned faintly. She calls this an example of creative fantasy. [p.706]
6. An artist who wanted to illustrate her publications could be made, by her, to draw Lake Geneva landscapes he had never seen; he said she used him as he used his pencil, simply as an instrument. [p.706]
Her reflections [p.707]: she attaches little importance to these fugitive phenomena; she thinks all people of nervous temperament and imagination who respond with lively sympathy to external impressions have analogous ones, and that this "sympathetic" temperament in healthy people plays a large part in producing "suggested" images. May not, under favorable conditions, something quite new come over the mental horizon, as natural in its origin as a rainbow differs from blue sky? The observations are an introduction to two or three more important ones that may shed light on more complex phenomena of persons "carried away" by them because unable or unwilling to analyze the "abnormal, subliminal, or subconscious" working of their minds.

**II. "Glory to God": a dream poem [pp.708-715].**
- Context: winter voyage Odessa to Genoa (Constantinople, Smyrna, Athens, Sicily, Italy) at age twenty with her family in 1898, after the long trip New York-Stockholm-St. Petersburg-Odessa. She spent hours in a deck chair dreaming; histories, legends and myths of the lands seen "dissolved in a kind of luminous mist" in which real things lost their being and dreams and ideas seemed the only true reality; she avoided company; wrote poems; at Catania wrote a sea-chanty adapted from a song about sea, wine and love ("Brine, wine and damsels fine") after hearing an officer sing on night watch. At Naples she was ill. [pp.708-709]
- The dream: between Naples and Leghorn, immediately before her mother woke her. First the words "when the morning stars sang together" (Job), as prelude to an involved idea of creation and mighty chorales reverberating through the universe, mixed with oratorio choruses from a New York musical society and vague memories of Milton's Paradise Lost; then words arranged in three stanzas in her own handwriting on blue-lined paper in her poetry album. She jumped from her bunk crying it was the most beautiful dream of her life; during the minutes of searching for notebook and pencil the memory faded; the first verse came easily, the second harder, the third took great effort. Some months later at Lausanne she produced a second, "more exact" version, closer to the dream. [pp.709-711]
- The poem (both versions given; the second quoted in her terms): When the Eternal first made Sound, a myriad ears sprang out to hear, and an echo rolled through the Universe: "All glory to the God of Sound!"; when the Eternal first made Light, a myriad eyes sprang out to look; and hearing ears and seeing eyes once more took up a mighty choral: "All glory to the God of Light!" (first version: "God of Beauty (Light)"); when the Eternal first gave Love, a myriad hearts sprang into life; ears filled with music, eyes with light, pealed forth with hearts full of love: "All glory to the God of Love!" [pp.711-712]
- Her source analysis: never an adept in spiritualism or the "contranatural" (distinguished from the supernatural), she looked for probable causes. What struck her: contrary to the Mosaic account in which she believed, the poem puts creation of light second, after sound. She notes that Anaxagoras too makes the cosmos arise from chaos by a whirlwind (which makes noise) and that she unconsciously followed his doctrine of nous (she knew nothing of him then) and of Leibniz's "dum Deus calculat fit mundus." Sources found: Milton's Paradise Lost (Dore-illustrated edition at home; her first line has the metre of "Of man's first disobedience"), the Book of Job (read aloud since childhood), Handel's oratorio The Creation (which appeared in the dream's confusion). At fifteen she was excited by an article about "the Idea spontaneously creating its own object"; she attended a Presbyterian church from nine to sixteen where the pastor spoke of "Chaos," "Cosmos," and "the Gift of Love." Other dream experiences: at fifteen a geometry formula solved in a dream; a forgotten Latin word; dreams of letters from friends before their arrival (explained as her sleeping calculation, confirmed by dreams of letters that never came). [pp.712-714]
- Conclusion: the dream is not so extraordinary: a kaleidoscope mixture of Paradise Lost, Job, The Creation and notions like the Idea creating its object, Gift of Love, Chaos and Cosmos, under stimulation of the voyage, fleeting countries and the sea's silence. "There was only this and nothing more." [p.715]

**III. "The Moth and the Sun": a hypnagogic poem [pp.715-718].**
- Circumstance: an exhausting day (excursion up the Saleve, telegram forcing her to leave Geneva for Paris within two hours), a hot train compartment, about an hour's sleep; near four o'clock she saw a tiny moth fluttering toward light through a curtained glass panel, lay down, nearly asleep but still self-conscious, when a poem came and could not be driven away; she wrote it down at once. [pp.715-716]
- "The Moth to the Sun": the moth longed for the sun from the first crawl to consciousness, dreamed of it in the chrysalis; myriads of its kind beat out their lives against some feeble spark caught from the sun; one hour more and its life is gone, yet its last effort, like its first desire, will be to approach the glory and, with one raptured glance, die content, having once beheld the source of beauty, warmth and life "in his perfect splendor." [p.716]
- Sources: a philosophical article read in Berlin the previous winter, which on re-reading aloud contained "The same passionate longing of the moth for the star, of man for God"; a play "The Moth and the Flame"; Byron's poems (rhythm of her last two lines like "Now let me die as I have lived in faith / Nor tremble tho' the Universe should quake!"). She sees three categories (wide-awake poems, the half-waking poem, the sleep poem) as a natural series, the intermediate state bridging them, which dispels any suspicion of an "occult" intervention in the poem produced asleep. [pp.717-718]

**IV. "Chiwantopel": a hypnagogic drama [pp.718-724].** (The source text of the fantasy Jung analyzes.)
- Miller's framing: borderland (half-dreaming) productions interest her; a careful examination would help dispel the mystery and superstition of "spirits"; she says in the hands of someone less careful about exact truth, or fond of embroidery, this could become a fantastic romance outdoing mediums' fictions. She transcribes from notes made immediately after, with only bracketed remarks and note letters. Observation of 17 March 1902, half an hour after midnight. [p.718]
- 1st Phase: after an evening of trouble and anxiety, in bed at 11:30, restless, very tired, receptive; dark room, eyes closed, feeling of waiting; great relaxation; lines, sparks and spirals of fire (nervous and ocular fatigue); a kaleidoscopic review of recent trivial events; an impression something was to be communicated; the words "Speak, Lord, for thy servant heareth--Open thou mine ears"; the head of a sphinx in an Egyptian setting; her parents called and she answered coherently, proof she was not asleep. [pp.718-719]
- 2nd Phase: an Aztec complete in detail (open hand, large fingers, profile, armored, plumed head-dress like Mexican carvings, note A); the name "Chi-wan-to-pel" forms bit by bit and seems to belong to this figure, son of an Inca of Peru (note B); then a swarm of people, horses, battle, a dream-city (C); a strange knotted conifer, lateen sails in a bay of purple water, a perpendicular cliff; sounds like "Wa-ma, Wa-ma." After a lacuna: a wood; Chiwantopel comes up from the south on horseback in a blanket of red, blue and white; an Indian in beaded buckskin and feathers (D) creeps to shoot him with an arrow; Chiwantopel bares his breast in defiance (E) and the fascinated Indian slinks away. Chiwantopel sits on a mound, lets his horse graze, and speaks in English. [p.719]
- Soliloquy [pp.720-721]: from the tip of the backbone of these continents, for a hundred moons since leaving his father's palace (F), he has wandered pursued by mad desire to find "her who will understand." He tempted women with jewels, kisses, daring deeds; Chi-ta the princess (vain peacock), Ta-nan the peasant girl ("a perfect sow"), Ki-ma the priestess (a parrot repeating priestly phrases): none understands him or has a soul sister to his (G); none could seek the shining summits with him or spell the superhuman word Love. He cries that no one in all the world will know his soul; then foretells: ten thousand moons later her pure soul will be born; her parents will come from another world; she will be fair of skin and hair; she will know sorrow before her mother bears her, will seek and find no one who understands her; suitors will not understand; she will resist temptation; "In her dreams I shall come to her, and she will understand" (H). "I have kept my body inviolate" (I). He came ten thousand moons before her time; she will come ten thousand moons too late, but she will understand.
- Death: after another lacuna a green viper darts from the bushes, stings his arm, then kills his horse first. He says: "Farewell, faithful brother! ... I shall rejoin you soon!" and to the serpent, "Thanks, little sister, you have put an end to my wanderings!" He prays: "Almighty God, take me soon!... suffer not my body to fall into corruption and decay, and become carrion for the eagles!" A smoking volcano appears (K); an earthquake and landslide; as the earth closes over his body: "Ah, she will understand! Ja-ni-wa-ma, Ja-ni-wa-ma, thou that understandest me!" [p.721]
- Her remarks and notes [pp.722-724]: she finds it an original combination, possibly a one-act melodrama; she could treat Chiwantopel as her "control" or spirit-guide as mediums do but does not. Sources: the name resembles Po-po-cat-a-pel (a Central American volcano, as pronounced to her); she once saw "A-ha-ma-ra-ma" in an Assyrian decoration, comparable to Ahasuerus, Asurabama; the previous day she had received a letter from Naples whose envelope showed smoking Vesuvius (K); childhood interest in Aztec remains and Inca history (A, B); a recent exhibition of Indians in costume (D); Cassius baring his breast to Brutus in Shakespeare (E); the departure of Buddha from his father's house and Johnson's Rasselas (F); the rhythm of Longfellow's Song of Hiawatha, followed unconsciously in the soliloquy; Siegfried's longing for Brunhild in Wagner (G, H); a lecture by Felix Adler on the Inviolable Personality (I); the dream-city matching the cover of a review she had been reading (C). She suggests it may be only a mosaic of A-K elements in the whirl of New York life (concerts, lectures, reviews) plus days spent seeking "an original idea," and fatigue and anguish of mind at midnight. P.S.: her concern for exactitude gave the account too personal a turn; she hopes it may help others free their minds of worries of the same kind and clarify mediums' more complex phenomena. [pp.722-724]
