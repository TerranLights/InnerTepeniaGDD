## 1. Overview of this range

This part contains the second half of **Liber Primus** (the book's first part, "The Way of What Is to Come") and the opening of **Liber Secundus** ("The Images of the Erring"). Liber Primus continues a long sequence of night-by-night visions of the narrator's descent into the "depths." Its recurring structure is a vision (often a dream-like scene with a named figure, a landscape, a drama) followed by sermon-like reflections to the reader ("my friends") that interpret it. Central claims:

- The narrator is the servant of a divine child; his God is a child, not an adult authority; the God "develops" and is born from the union of opposites.
- Two powers rule the narrator's time: "the spirit of this time" (cleverness, intention, usefulness, haste, the visible surface) and "the spirit of the depths" (the soul, the unknown, what resists intention).
- The way to the soul runs through a desert, through madness ("divine madness"), through a murder of the hero (Siegfried), and through a "Mysterium" with Elijah, Salome and a black serpent, ending in an initiation in which the narrator is identified with Christ and the God.
- The great war of 1914 is read as the outer image of an inner event, a collective version of the same descent and self-sacrifice.

**Liber Secundus** begins in a different register: a series of "adventures" with figures of the wider world (the Red One, an old scholar and his locked-up daughter, a one-eyed tramp), told in a lighter, more novelistic, dialogue-heavy style, interrupted by reflection on joy, the devil, gender, the banal, depth and heights.

The editor's footnotes in this range supply dates (Nov 15 to Dec 29, 1913), cross-references to the Black Book notebooks, to an earlier draft of the text, and to Jung's later lectures and publications (the 1925 Analytical Psychology seminar, *Psychological Types*, *Aion*, *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*), plus the sources behind his motifs (Nietzsche, Wagner, Dante, Augustine, apocryphal gospels, Dieterich, Frazer).

## 2. LIBER PRIMUS — Continuation of the chapter begun in the previous part  [p.265]

*[The chapter's number and title are not in this range. The text opens mid-sermon on the "heart" and the "child God".]*

### 2.1 The heart, good and evil [p.265]
- The knowledge of your heart is how your heart is: "From a cunning heart you will know cunning. From a good heart you will know goodness."
- To make understanding perfect: the heart is both good and evil. Objection: "Should I also live evil?" The answer attributed to the spirit of the depths: live what you could still live; "Well-being decides, not your well-being, not the well-being of the others, but only well-being."
- The narrator says he himself lived "into the depths" and the depths began to speak; the depths united sense and nonsense in him.

### 2.2 The servant of a child [p.265]
- The narrator recognizes that he is "only the expression and symbol of the soul," her servant, "completely subjugated, utterly obedient." The depths taught him to say "I am the servant of a child"; this brings "the most extreme humility."
- The "spirit of this time" had let him believe in his reason and see himself as a leader with ripe thoughts. The dictum was repugnant, but he had to accept: **his soul is a child and his God in the soul is a child.** [note: footnote 57 ties this to Jung's 1925 report of his reflections on the anima as the "deity".]

### 2.3 Gender and God (verse sequence) [p.265]
| If you are... | your God is... |
|---|---|
| boys | a woman |
| women | a boy |
| men | a maiden |
- "The God is where you are not." Having a God serves perfection.
- Definitions: a maiden is the pregnant future; a boy is the engendering future; a woman is "having given birth"; a man is "having engendered."
- Childlike beings: their God descends from the height of ripeness to age and death. Developed beings (having engendered or given birth, in body or soul): their God rises from the "radiant cradle" to the height of the future, maturity and fullness.
- He who still has life before him is a child; he who lives life in the present is developed. Closing pair of lines: the one who is a child in this time, his God [garbled in source: reads "Goldies"; probably "dies"]; the one who is developed in this time, "his God continues to live."
- Prosperous and woeful are those whose God is developed; prosperous and woeful those whose God is a child. Which is better: that man has life ahead, or that God does? The text: "I know no answer. Live; the unavoidable decides."

### 2.4 The divine child; transience [p.265]
- The depths taught that his life is "encompassed by the divine child"; everything unexpected and living came from the child's hand. This child is felt as "an eternally springing youth" in him.
- In childish men one feels hopeless transience; for the child, all that passes is still to come. But transience of the things approaching has never yet had a human meaning. "Your continuing to live is a living onward": you engender and give birth to what is to come. The childish is unfruitful and does not live onward.
- The narrator's God is a child, so the spirit of this time in him mocks; he says nobody will laugh at him as he laughed at himself.

### 2.5 Christ, mockery, the way [p.265]
- "Your God should not be a man of mockery, rather you yourself will be the man of mockery." One should mock oneself and rise above it.
- If not learned from the old holy books: go and "drink the blood and eat the flesh of him who was mocked and tormented," become his nature, deny his being apart from you: "not Christians but Christ."
- No one can be spared "the way of Christ"; whoever tries to swindle past the pain sleeps "on thorns and fire." "You should all become Christs."
- One does not overcome the old teaching by doing less but by doing more. Every step toward his soul is met by scornful laughter of his "devils," those "cowardly ear-whisperers and poison-mixers."

## 3. LIBER PRIMUS — "On the Service of the Soul" (Cap. iii)  [pp.265–266]

*Folio ii(v). The editor dates the night as Nov 15, 1913 (fn. 63).*

- **Event:** the following night the narrator must write down **all the dreams he can recollect, true to their wording**. He does not understand why. [EDITOR fn. 64: in Black Book 2 Jung wrote here the two pivotal dreams he had at age nineteen that led him to natural science.]
- **Address to the soul (long prayer-like monologue):** The narrator voices fear and doubt: "Into what mist and darkness does your path lead? Must I also learn to do without meaning?" He sees the things of his soul as small; the soul demands he make them large. He asks: should thought, too, no longer be? Should he give himself completely into her hands, "but who are you? I do not trust you." He recognizes injustice in his distrust; he must learn to love his soul and trust her as he trusts valiant men; he calls the scornful laughter in him "right." [note: this is dramatized dialogue between narrator and soul; the soul speaks once: "This fear testifies against me!"; the narrator replies "It is true, it testifies against you."] [EDITOR fn. 65: Black Book 2 records a whispering voice accusing him of writing to be printed, imitating Nietzsche and Augustine; Augustine's *Confessions* is the editor's model for a work addressed to God recounting wandering and return.]
- **Teaching: meaning and chaos [p.266]:**
  - Taking a step toward the soul, you first lose meaning and fall into "meaninglessness, into eternal disorder"; this is "the other half of the world."
  - "Disorder and meaninglessness are the mother of order and meaning." Order and meaning are what has become, no longer becoming.
  - You open the gates of the soul to let the dark flood of chaos into your order. "If you marry the ordered to the chaos you produce the divine child, the supreme meaning beyond meaning and meaninglessness."
  - Fear of opening the door: "we had forgotten that God is terrible." Christ taught God is love; "love is also terrible." The narrator recalls speaking to a loving soul, being seized with horror and building a wall of doubt to protect himself from his fearful soul.
- **Temptation:** you must endure the temptation of fear and doubt and acknowledge to the bone that both are justified; otherwise there is no true overcoming. Christ overcomes the temptation of the devil but not "the temptation of God to good and reason," so "Christ thus succumbs to cursing." [EDITOR fn. 68 cites the cursed fig tree, Matt. 21.] The learner must "succumb to no temptation, but do everything of your own will; then you will be free and beyond Christianity."
- **Learning to love what horrifies:** the narrator must submit to what he fears, even love what horrifies him. Example: a saint disgusted by plague infections drank the pus of plague boils, found it smelled like roses; her acts "were not in vain." If your virtues hinder your salvation, discard them; the slave to virtue finds the way as little as the slave to vices.
- **Master/servant reversal:** "If you believe that you are the master of your soul, then become her servant. If you were her servant, make yourself her master, since she needs to be ruled. These should be your first steps."
- **Six silent nights:** during six further nights the spirit of the depths is silent; the narrator oscillates between fear, defiance and nausea, prey to his passion. On the seventh night the spirit says: **"Look into your depths, pray to your depths, waken the dead."** [EDITOR fn. 71: dated Nov 22, 1913; the Black Book has "says a voice."] The narrator stands helpless, finds only the memory of earlier dreams he wrote down; he wants to throw everything away but the spirit forces him back into himself.

## 4. LIBER PRIMUS — "The Desert" (Cap. iv)  [pp.266–268]

*Folio iii(r). Date: Nov 28, 1913 (fn. 72) for the opening; the sub-section "Experiences in the Desert" is dated Dec 11, 1913 (fn. 77).*

### 4.1 Into the desert of the self [pp.266–267]
- "Sixth night. My soul leads me into the desert, into the desert of my own self." He had not thought his soul a barren, hot desert, dusty and without drink. The journey leads away from mankind, slowly wading through hot sand without a visible goal.
- He asks why his self is a desert: because he lived outside himself in men and events, and avoided the place of his soul. When he was no longer events and men, he was **his thoughts**, not his self, confronted with his thoughts. The way leads from men and events into solitude: "Solitude is true only when the self is a desert."
- Is the desert a deception that he can no longer trust his thoughts? "Only life is true"; his thinking would like to return to thoughts, men and events, since it feels uncanny. The soul says: **"Wait."** Torment belongs to the desert. [EDITOR fn. 73–75: Black Book 2 links this to anchorites in the Syrian desert; the Desert Fathers (St. Anthony, c. 285; Pachomius) as the origin of Christian monasticism.]
- Having given his soul all he could, he found it a desert; "no culture of the mind is enough to make a garden out of your soul." He had cultivated the spirit of this time but not the spirit of the depths, which turns to the things of the soul. The soul has her own peculiar world; only the self enters it, "he who has completely become his self... neither in events, nor in men, nor in his thoughts."
- Turning his desire from things and men made him "the secure prey of his thoughts"; when he withdrew desire from thoughts, the self became a desert where only "the sun of unquiet desire" burned. Creative power of desire is absent; where it is, the soil's own seed springs. Wait. If creative force turns to the soul, "your soul becomes green."
- Most cannot bear the waiting and throw themselves back at men, things, thoughts, becoming slaves and fools, until their soul becomes a fruitful field. Even one whose soul is a garden needs things, men, thoughts, but as their friend, not slave.
- **Teaching on the ancients:** everything to come was already in images; "to find their soul, the ancients went into the desert." They lived their symbols because the world had not yet become real to them. The place of the soul is a lonely desert; there they found "the abundance of visions, the fruits of the desert, the wondrous flowers of the soul." Study the images of the ancients ("collapse of empires, of growth and death, of the desert and monasteries"): they show the way of what is to come. "Everything has been foretold. But who knows how to interpret it?"
- **The word as creative act:** "When you say that the place of the soul is not, then it is not. But if you say that it is, then it is." The ancients said "in the beginning was the Word"; "The words that oscillate between nonsense and supreme meaning are the oldest and truest."

### 4.2 "Experiences in the Desert" [p.267–268]
- After a hard struggle the narrator has come nearer to his soul; he is alone with her, "with empty hands." Dialogue (soul replies quoted):
  - "If you come to a friend, do you come to take?" He asks to sit near her and feel her breath. Soul: "You speak to me as if you were a child complaining to its mother. I am not your mother."
  - He calls her a shady tree in the wilderness; soul: "You are pleasure-seeking. Where is your patience? Your time has not yet run its course. Have you forgotten why you went into the desert?"
  - His faith is weak; heat like lead; thirst; no goal visible. Soul: "Can you not wait? Should everything fall into your lap ripe and finished? You are full... you teem with intentions and desirousness! Do you still not know that the way to truth stands open only to those without intentions?"
  - He admits the soul's words are his thoughts but he hardly lives by them; he appeals to being "just a human being, weak." Soul: "Is this what you think it means to be human?" He agrees she is hard and right: "We should grow like a tree that likewise does not know its law. We tie ourselves up with intentions, not mindful of the fact that intention is the limitation, yes, the exclusion of life." We believe we can illuminate the darkness with an intention and so aim past the light; "How can we presume to want to know in advance, from where the light will come to us?" [EDITOR fn. 78: Jung's 1929 commentary on *The Secret of the Golden Flower* and Meister Eckhart's "letting go."]
- **Scorn:** his one complaint is his own scorn. Soul: "Do you think little of yourself?... do you think little of me? ... you are not writing a book to feed your vanity, but you are speaking with me. How can you suffer from scorn if you address me with those words that I give you? Do you know, then, who I am? Have you grasped me, defined me, and made me into a dead formula?" He lays his vanity down; he is her vessel, "empty without you but brimming over with you."
- **Duration:** "This was my twenty-fifth night in the desert." It took that long for the soul to awaken from a shadowy being to her own life, a free-standing being.
- **Wisdom as simplemindedness:** the spirit of this time considers itself extremely clever; "wisdom is simpleminded, not just simple." The clever person mocks wisdom, since mockery is his weapon; the mockery falls on the mocker "in the desert where no one hears and answers; he suffocates from his own scorn." One is not saved by greater cleverness but by accepting what cleverness hates most, simplemindedness; yet not as artificial fools but "clever fools." "Cleverness conquers the world, but simplemindedness, the soul." "So take on the vow of poverty of spirit in order to partake of the soul." [EDITOR fn. 79: Matt. 5:3; Jung 1934 on "spiritual poverty."]
- He overcame scorn, and "I was soon to see the desert becoming green."

## 5. LIBER PRIMUS — "Descent into Hell in the Future" (Cap. v)  [pp.268–270]

*Folio iii(v). Date Dec 12, 1913 (fn. 81).*

### 5.1 The vision (with illustration "Image iii(v)")  [p.268]
- Many voices fill the air at night; a loud voice cries "I am falling"; others: "Where to? What do you want?" The narrator shudders: should he entrust himself to this confusion, "the madness of my own darkness"?
- The spirit of the depths opens his eyes to "the world of my soul, the many-formed and changing." The vision, in sequence:
  1. A gray rock face along which he sinks into great depths.
  2. He stands in black dirt up to his ankles in a dark cave; shadows sweep over him; fear; he knows he must go in.
  3. He crawls through a narrow crack into an inner cave whose floor is black water; beyond it he sees **a luminous red stone** he must reach. He wades through muddy water; the cave is full of the shrieking of voices.
  4. He takes the stone; it covers a dark opening in the rock. He does not want to listen to the voices that keep him away, but he wants to know; he puts his ear to the opening and hears the flow of underground waters.
  5. He sees **the bloody head of a man** on the dark stream: someone wounded, someone slain, floating. He gazes at it a long time, shuddering.
  6. **A large black scarab** floats by on the stream.
  7. In the deepest reach of the stream a **red sun** shines through the dark water. Small **serpents** on the dark rock walls strive toward the depths where the sun shines; a thousand serpents crowd around, veiling the sun.
  8. Deep night falls. A **red stream of blood**, thick and red, springs up, surges for a long time, then ebbs. He is seized with fear: "What did I see?"
- [EDITOR fn. 82–85: earlier drafts add a leather-clad dwarf guarding the entrance and a six-sided crystal giving cold reddish light; Black Book 2 asks whether the dark hole is the oracle of the Pythia. Jung also related this fantasy in the 1925 seminar (the red crystal like a stone of wisdom; the scarab an ancient sun symbol; the murdered hero not understood until a dream in which he killed Siegfried, linked to sacrificing the "superior function"). The editor cites Dieterich's *Nekyia* for underworld motifs (lake, mud, serpents), which Jung marked in his copy.]

### 5.2 Prayer after the vision [p.269]
- He asks his soul to heal the wounds doubt inflicts. His spirit is "a spirit of torment," tearing his contemplation apart; he asks when he can order his thinking to be quiet "so that my thoughts, those unruly hounds, will crawl to my feet." He has sworn to trust his soul "even if you lead me through madness."
- He asks protection from science ("that clever knower, that bad prison master") and from "the serpent of judgment, which only appears to be a healing serpent." He wants to go down "cleansed... in white garments and not rush in like some thief." He asks to persist in "divine astonishment," to lay his head on a stone before her door.

### 5.3 Divine madness [p.269]
- When the desert begins to bloom, it brings forth strange plants. "You will consider yourself mad, and in a certain sense you will in fact be mad." To the extent that Christianity lacks madness, it lacks divine life. "Madness is divine," the ancients taught in images, but because they lived the image concretely in events it became a deception for us, who became "masters of the reality of the world."
- If you enter the world of the soul you are like a madman, and a doctor would call you sick. "No one can see it as sickness more than I do." The rule: "suspend judgment and wait for the fruits." [EDITOR fn. 89–90: Plato's *Phaedrus* on four types of divine madness; Erasmus's *Praise of Folly*; Schelling; William James's pragmatic rule.]
- **A distinction:** divine madness = "the overpowering of the spirit of this time through the spirit of the depths." **Sick delusion** is when (a) the depths can no longer stay down and force a man to speak in tongues and believe himself the spirit of the depths, or (b) the spirit of this time will not leave and forces him to see only the surface and take himself for the spirit of the time. "The spirit of this time is ungodly; the spirit of the depths is ungodly; balance is godly."
- The spirit of the depths erupted because the narrator spoke to his soul for 25 nights in the desert, giving all love and submission; **by day, during those 25 days, he gave his love to things, men and the thoughts of this time; he went into the desert "only at night."** He instructs: whoever does the one and does without the other is out of balance, sick.
- Fear and divine intoxication: "Love, soul, and God are beautiful and terrible." The ancients brought some of the beauty of God into this world, which looked like fulfillment; frightfulness and cruelty lay hidden in the depths of hearts. "The spirit of the depths is pregnant with ice, fire, and death." "You see in these days what the spirit of the depths bore. You did not believe it, but you would have known it if you had taken counsel with your fear." [EDITOR fn. 91: draft says it took 25 nights to comprehend the stranger.]

### 5.4 Interpretation of the vision: the sacrifice of the hero [pp.270]
- The red crystal's blood: "in the depths of what is to come lay murder. **The blond hero lay slain.**" The black beetle is the death necessary for renewal; a new sun, "the sun of the depths, full of riddles, a sun of the night," quickened the dead as spring quickens the earth; there began the terrible struggle between light and darkness and the unvanquished source of blood. "This was what was to come, which you now experience in your life." (Stated as vision of the night of 12 December 1913.)
- **Outer events as images:** "Depths and surface should mix so that new life can develop. Yet the new life does not develop outside of us, but within us." What happens outside is the image peoples live in events, as ancients did. "Life does not come from events, but from us." Whoever looks from outside sees it already was; whoever looks from inside knows everything is new. "Events signify nothing, they signify only in us. We create the meaning of events... The meaning is and always was artificial. We make it."
- The meaning of events is "the way of salvation that you create" — "the mastery of this world and the assertion of your soul in this world." This supreme meaning is not in events nor in the soul but "the God standing between events and the soul, the mediator of life, the way, the bridge and the going across." [EDITOR fn. 92: draft says the soul is the image of God, not God.]
- "I would not have been able to see what was to come if I could not have seen it in myself." The narrator takes part in the murder: "I myself am a murderer and murdered, sacrificer and sacrificed." The upwelling blood streams out of him. [EDITOR fn. 93: Jung on identity of sacrificer and sacrificed, Zosimus, Katha Upanishad.]
- **To the readers:** "You all have a share in the murder." In you the reborn one will come to be, the sun of the depths will rise, and a thousand serpents will develop from your dead matter and fall on the sun to choke it; your blood will stream. The peoples demonstrate this now. [EDITOR fn. 95: WWI, autumn 1914 (Marne, Ypres), when Jung wrote this "layer."]
- Why do men fall on brothers? Because they do not know their brother is themselves; they should sacrifice the hero in themselves, and instead kill the courageous brother. "So long as it is possible to murder the brother instead of oneself, the time is not ripe." Frightful things must happen until men grow ripe; the blood that follows the shrouding of the sun is "also the source of the new life."
- As the fate of peoples shows in events, so it happens in the heart: if the hero in you is slain, the sun of the depths rises, everything seemingly dead comes to life and turns to poisonous serpents covering the sun; you fall into night and confusion; "Birth is blood and torment." Your darkness will come to life and you will feel "the crush of total evil." The serpents are "dreadful evil thoughts and feelings." Advice: think of all the devilish things men inflict on brothers; "that should happen to you in your heart. Suffer it yourself through your own hand... not your brother, who wrestles with his own devils."
- **Princes and assassins:** the nameless men who in this age murdered a prince are "blind prophets who demonstrate in events what then is valid only for the soul." Through murder of princes we learn that "the prince in us, the hero, is threatened." [EDITOR fn. 99: the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand; fn. 98: Jung's later remark on regicide by prophetic individuals.] (Text continues on the next page, section 6.)

## 6. LIBER PRIMUS — "Splitting of the Spirit" (Cap. vi)  [pp.271–272]

*Folio iv(r). The sermon on the hero and the spirit of the time begins at the top of p.271 as continuation of ch. v; the chapter proper starts with "But on the fourth night I cried."*

### 6.1 The ruler and the hero (end of ch. v sermon) [p.271]
- Our ruler is **the spirit of this time**, "which rules and leads in us all." He has brought "immeasurable good" and fascinated men; "bejeweled with the most beautiful heroic virtue," he drives men to ever higher solar heights, "in everlasting ascent."
- The hero wants to open up everything he can; the nameless spirit of the depths evokes everything that man cannot. **Incapacity prevents further ascent.** "We must first create it by learning to live with our incapacity. We must give it life." One who learns to live with incapacity learns the valuation of the smallest things and "wise limitation."
- If heroism is erased we fall back into the misery of humanity; our foundations get stirred up by the highest tension and "we will fall into the cesspool of our underworld, among the rubble of all the centuries in us." [EDITOR fn. 101: draft says everything forgotten will be revived, "the black serpents and the reddish sun of the depths."]
- "The heroic in you is the fact that you are ruled by the thought that this or that is good... this or that pleasure should be ruthlessly repressed at all costs. Consequently you sin against incapacity. But incapacity exists." [EDITOR fn. 102: Jung's 1917 discussion of WWI on the subjective level: the authoritarian principle versus the emotional principle; "The hero... should fall."]

### 6.2 "Splitting of the Spirit" (Cap. vi) — the dialogue with the spirit of the depths [pp.271–272]
- *Fourth night:* the narrator cries "To journey to Hell means to become Hell oneself." [EDITOR fn. 103: Nietzsche, "monsters" and the abyss.] On the desert path there are, besides glowing sand, "tangled invisible beings" who "murderously attach themselves to me and daimonically change my form." He fears he has taken on a monstrous animal form "for which I have exchanged my humanity"; invisible nooses ensnare him.
- The spirit of the depths: "Climb down into your depths, sink!" Narrator: "How can I sink? I am unable to do this myself." Spirit: **"Sit yourself down, be calm."** The narrator is indignant ("it sounds like nonsense"); he accuses the spirit of overthrowing "the mighty Gods who mean the most to us"; "I would strangle you if I could seize you... But I yearn, teach me."
- Soul: **"My path is light."** Narrator: "Do you call light what we men call the worst darkness?" Soul: "My light is not of this world." Narrator: "I know of no other world." Soul: "Should it not exist because you know nothing of it?" Narrator invokes knowledge, security, solid ground; "perhaps it will do without speech and words too?" Soul: "No words."
- Narrator apologizes: perhaps he is hard of hearing, "a fool in my own madhouse," "a rascal grinning at myself in a mirror." Soul: "You delude yourself, you do not deceive me. Your words are lies to you, not me." "Who gives you thoughts and words? Do you make them? Are you not my serf, a recipient who lies at my door and picks up my alms?... Don't you know yet that it comes from me and belongs to me?" Narrator: "then my indignation must also come from you, and in me you are indignant against yourself." Soul: **"That is civil war."** [EDITOR fn. 104: Black Book 2 adds "Are you neurotic? Are we neurotic?"]
- Narrator, in pain and rage: "I can also crawl through mud and the most despised banality. I can also eat dust; that is part of Hell. I do not yield, I am defiant." He addresses the soul as the one who "donned the mask of a God, and I worshiped you. Now you wear the mask of a devil, a frightful one, the mask of the banal, of eternal mediocrity!" He asks for a moment to consider but cannot leave the battlefield; he wants to wrestle, "monkey, buffoon"; his hands grab at air, his soul's blows are air too.
- **Desert vision [p.272]:** back on the desert path, "invisible robbers and assassins and shooters of poison darts." He feels transformed into a rapacious beast; his heart glowers in rage against his prince and hero, as the nameless one of the people lunged at his prince. "Because I carried the murder in me, I foresaw it." Also: "Because I carried the war in me, I foresaw it." He felt betrayed by his king, who was not as he wished him to be, "He should be what I called ideal." His soul appeared hollow, but "in reality what I thought of her was valid for my ideal." The vision was of "civil war in me": he struggled with mirror images of himself, was murderer and murdered, the deadly arrow stuck in his heart.
- **Moral of the vision:** the murder of one man was the poisonous arrow flying into the hearts of men and kindling the fiercest war; the murder is "the indignation of incapacity against will, a Judas betrayal that one would like someone else to have committed. We are still seeking the goat that should bear our sin." [EDITOR fn. 107–108: Judas as necessary link in redemption (draft; Oegger/Anatole France); Leviticus scapegoat.]
- Aphorisms: "Everything that becomes too old becomes evil, the same is true of your highest." "Learn from the suffering of the crucified God that one can also betray and crucify a God, namely the God of the old year. If a God ceases being the way of life, he must fall secretly." "The God becomes sick if he oversteps the height of the zenith. That is why the spirit of the depths took me when the spirit of this time had led me to the summit."

## 7. LIBER PRIMUS — "Murder of the Hero" (Cap. vii)  [pp.272–273]

*Folio iv(v). Date Dec 18, 1913 (fn. 112). Editor note 111: this refers to mourning for the death of the hero.*

### 7.1 The dream-vision [pp.272–273]
- The narrator is in high mountains **with a youth** before daybreak; the eastern sky is already light. **Siegfried's horn** resounds over the mountains. They know their **mortal enemy** is coming; they are armed and lurk beside a narrow rocky path to murder him.
- Siegfried comes high across the mountains on **a chariot made of the bones of the dead**, driving boldly and magnificently over steep rocks. As he comes around the turn ahead, they **fire at the same time and he falls slain.** The narrator turns to flee; **a terrible rain** sweeps down.
- After this he goes through "a torment unto death" and feels certain he must kill himself if he cannot solve the riddle of the murder of the hero. [EDITOR fn. 115: Black Book 2 adds a dream of climbing a steep path and helping his wife follow; Jung's 1925 report: he felt enormous pity for Siegfried, "I had killed my intellect... helped on to the deed by... the little brown man with me"; the rain as release of tension; in *Memories* he said he would have to kill himself if he could not solve it. fn. 123: to Aniela Jaffe he linked the exaggeration of the will to the Germans' Siegfried Line.]
- **Resolution:** the spirit of the depths says: **"The highest truth is one and the same with the absurd."** This statement "saved me, and like rain after a long hot spell, it swept away everything in me which was too highly tensed."
- **Second vision:** a merry garden in which forms in white silk walk, covered in colored light (reddish, blueish, greenish). [Image iv(v)] [EDITOR fn. 117: Jung told Jaffe the colored sphere around figures expressed that he was one thing and something else; a vision of the beyond. fn. 118: draft says the in-between world is "a world of the simplest things... no Heaven above... no Hell beneath"; Jung's 1916 remark that "the first step in individuation is tragic guilt."]
- "I know, I have stridden across the depths. Through guilt I have become a newborn."

### 7.2 Reflections [p.273]
- "We also live in our dreams, we do not live only by day. Sometimes we accomplish our greatest deeds in dreams."
- His life was threatened since he had to kill his lord and God: "You can reach your God only as an assassin, if you want to overcome him." "Our Gods want to be overcome, since they require renewal." If men kill their princes it is because "they cannot kill their Gods, and because they do not know that they should kill their Gods in themselves."
- "If the God grows old, he becomes shadow, nonsense, and he goes down. The greatest truth becomes the greatest lie, the brightest day becomes darkest night."
- **Verse on meaning and absurdity:** as day requires night, "meaning requires absurdity and absurdity requires meaning." Day does not exist through itself, nor night; "the reality that exists through itself is day and night. So the reality is meaning and absurdity." Noon is a moment; "meaning is a moment and a transition from absurdity to absurdity, and absurdity only a moment and a transition from meaning to meaning."
- **Siegfried and the Germans:** "Oh that Siegfried, blond and blue-eyed, the German hero, had to fall by my hand, the most loyal and courageous! He had everything in himself that I treasured as the greater and more beautiful; he was my power, my boldness, my pride." Only assassination (cunning, trickery) was left if the narrator wished to go on living. "Judge not!" The text says the blond savage of the German forests had to betray "the hammer-brandishing thunder" (Thor) to a pale Near-Eastern God "nailed to the wood"; the courageous felt contempt for themselves but their life force bade them to live, and they betrayed their wild Gods, holy trees and awe of the forests. "What does Siegfried mean for the Germans! What does it tell us that the Germans suffer Siegfried's death!" He almost preferred to kill himself to spare him, "but I wanted to go on living with a new God." [EDITOR fn. 113: Siegfried in the *Nibelungenlied*, Wagner's *Ring*, and Jung's 1912 interpretation as libido symbol. fn. 122: Jung 1915 and "Wotan" (1936) on Christianity splitting the Germanic "barbarian."]
- **Christ in Hell:** after the cross Christ went to the underworld and became Hell, taking the form of the Antichrist, the dragon. "The image of the Antichrist... announces the new God." "Gods are unavoidable. The more you flee from the God, the more surely you fall into his hand."
- **The rain:** "the great stream of tears that will come over the peoples," the release of tension after death's constriction; "the mourning of the dead in me, which precedes burial and rebirth." Rain is the fructifying of the earth: "it begets the new wheat, the young, germinating God." [EDITOR fn. 124: Frazer's dying and resurrecting god.]

## 8. LIBER PRIMUS — "The Conception of the God" (Cap. viii)  [pp.273–276]

*Folio iv(v) 2. "On the second night thereafter."*

### 8.1 The speech of the spirit of the depths (verse) [p.274]
- The narrator says the "new world" seems weak and artificial; the mustard seed that grew into a tree, the word conceived in a virgin's womb, "became a God to whom the earth was subject." The spirit of the depths then erupts, filling him with intoxication and mist, and speaks a poetic prophecy addressed to "you who are to come." Key lines and images (paraphrased):
  - "I have received your sprout... in deepest need and lowliness. I covered it in shabby patchwork and bedded down on poor words. And mockery worshiped it, your child... the child of one who is to come, who should announce the father, a fruit that is older than the tree on which it grew."
  - "In pain will you conceive and joyful is your birth. Fear is your herald, doubt stands to your right, disappointment to your left."
  - The coming God will wring "truthful prayers from your believers" in tongues "atrocious to them"; he will become known "in what they hate, fear, and abhor"; his voice will be heard "amid the stammerings of wretches, rejects, and those condemned as worthless"; his realm touched by those who worshiped before the most profound lowliness; his gifts go to those who pray in terror and doubt, his light on those who kneel unwillingly with resentment.
  - "Your life is with he who has overcome himself and who has disowned his self-overcoming." [EDITOR fn. 127: Nietzsche's self-overcoming.]
  - Salvation of mercy is given "only to those who believe in the highest and faithlessly betray themselves for thirty pieces of silver"; those who "dirty their pure hands and cheat on their best knowledge against error and take their virtues from a murderer's grave" are invited to his banquet. "The constellation of your birth is an ill and changing star."

### 8.2 The ambiguity of the God [pp.274–275]
- When the prince fell, the spirit of the depths opened his vision to **the birth of the new God**. "The divine child approached me out of the terrible ambiguity, the hateful-beautiful, the evil-good, the laughable-serious, the sick-healthy, the inhuman-human and the ungodly-godly."
- The God sought in "absolute beauty, goodness... or even in godliness" is not found there; "the new God would be in the relative." If God is absolute beauty and goodness, how can he encompass the fullness of life? How can man live in the womb of the God if the Godhead attends only to half of him? Climbing to the heights of good and evil, our badness lies in torment; the good and beautiful "freeze to the ice of the absolute idea" and the bad become mud puddles "full of crazy life." [EDITOR fn. 132: Hegel's absolute idea. fn. 130–131: the encompassing God is developed in Scrutinies, Sermon 2; evil integrated into the Godhead in *Aion*, *Answer to Job*.]
- **Christ's descent:** Christ had to journey to Hell, becoming "his Antichrist, his underworldly brother," otherwise the ascent would have been impossible. "No one knows what happened during the three days Christ was in Hell. I have experienced it." The men of yore said he preached to the deceased; the narrator says it was "folly and monkey business, an atrocious Hell's masquerade of the holiest mysteries. How else could Christ have saved his Antichrist?" Christ did not remain in Hell but rose to the heights beyond. [EDITOR fn. 134–136: I Peter 4:6; apocryphal gospels; the "unknown books of the ancients"; Dieterich's *Nekyia*; Jung's later alchemical/psychological readings. The draft links the serpent with paradise's end and with Christ.]
- Our conviction of the value of the good and beautiful has become unshakable, so life can extend beyond and "fulfill everything that lay bound and yearning," which is "also the hateful and bad." "Do you think that it is dead in you? But this dead can also change into serpents. These serpents will extinguish the prince of your days."

### 8.3 Instructions for the descent [p.275]
- "Do you see what beauty and joy came over men when the depths unleashed this greatest war? And yet it was a frightful beginning." [EDITOR fn. 137: Jung's 1933 account of crossing Germany at the outbreak of war: a "feast of love," free food.] "If we do not have the depths, how do we have the heights?" Say aloud that you are afraid of yourselves: "It is wisdom to fear oneself. Only the heroes say that they are fearless."
- Practical counsel for going into the depths: with fear and trembling, mistrustful; **not alone** ("two or more is greater security since the depths are full of murder"); secure a way of retreat; go cautiously "as if you were cowards, so that you pre-empt the soul murderers." "He who journeys to Hell also becomes Hell; therefore do not forget from whence you come." "Do not be heroes, be clever and drop the heroics." The depths want to keep you. [EDITOR fn. 138: "soul murderer" in Schreber's *Memoirs of My Nervous Illness*.]
- The depths changed into death by being assaulted; "we cannot slay death, as we have already taken all life from it. If we still want to overcome death, then we must **enliven** it." Instructions: take golden cups of sweet red wine and give it to dead matter so it can win life back; the dead matter changes into **black serpents** which will put out the sun of your days and bring a night "with wonderful will-o'-the-wisps." "Take pains to waken the dead. Dig deep mines and throw in sacrificial gifts, so that they reach the dead. Reflect in good heart upon evil, this is the way to the ascent. But before the ascent, everything is night and Hell." [EDITOR fn. 140: refers back to the ch. v vision; Jung 1940 on the danger of the new consciousness being swallowed by the instinctive soul.]
- **Definition of Hell:** when the depths come to you with all that you no longer are or are not yet capable of; when you cannot attain what you could; when you must think, feel and do everything you know you do not want; when you know your having-to is also a wanting-to and you are responsible; when you know everything serious you planned is also laughable, everything fine is brutal, everything good bad, everything high low, everything pleasant shameful. "But the deepest Hell is when you realize that Hell is also no Hell, but a cheerful Heaven, not a Heaven in itself, but in this respect a Heaven, and in that respect a Hell."
- **Ambiguity:** "That is the ambiguity of the God: he is born from a dark ambiguity and rises to a bright ambiguity. Unequivocalness is simplicity and leads to death. But ambiguity is the way of life." [EDITOR fn. 142: Jung's 1952 letter: he deliberately seeks ambiguous expression.] "If the left foot does not move, then the right one does, and you move. The God wills this." The Christian God is unequivocal, he is love; "but what is more ambiguous than love?" "Nothing is easier than to play at ambiguity and nothing is more difficult than living ambiguity. He who plays is a child; his God is old and dies. He who lives is awakened; his God is young and goes on." "There is a true love that does not concern itself with neighbors."

### 8.4 The birth of the God in the narrator [pp.275–276]
- "When the hero was slain and the meaning recognized in the absurdity... I became aware of the birth of the God." The God sank into his heart "when I was confused by mockery and worship, by grief and laughter, by yes and no." The God arose from the melting together of the two; "born as a child from my own human soul, which had conceived him with resistance like a virgin" (image of the ancients). [EDITOR fn. 147: the Virgin Mary.] He did not know his soul was pregnant; the soul seemed to be the God himself.
- The ancient image is fulfilled: "I pursued my soul to kill the child in it. For I am also the worst enemy of my God." [EDITOR fn. 149: perhaps the wounding of Izdubar in Liber Secundus ch. 8.] The God could not come into being before the hero was slain: the hero as we understand him has become an enemy of the God since he is perfection; "The Gods envy the perfection of man, because perfection has no need of the Gods. But since no one is perfect, we need the Gods. The Gods love perfection because it is the total way of life. But the Gods are not with him who wishes to be perfect, because he is an imitation of perfection." [EDITOR fn. 150: wholeness above perfection in Jung's later work.]
- **Imitation:** imitation was a way of life when men needed the heroic prototype; "the monkey's manner is a way of life for monkeys, and for man as long as he is like a monkey." A time will come when a piece of that apishness falls away: "a time of salvation and the dove, and the eternal fire." Then no hero and none to imitate; "all imitation is cursed." "The new God laughs at imitation and discipleship. He needs no imitators and no pupils. He forces men through himself. The God is his own follower in man." [EDITOR fn. 151: Jung 1916 on imitation as the most pernicious thing for individuation.]
- **Singleness vs. communality** [p.276]: we think singleness is within us and communality outside; if we are in ourselves we are communal in relation to the outside, single in relation to ourselves; if outside ourselves, we are single and selfish in the communal. "If we are in ourselves, we fulfill the need of the self, we prosper, and through this we become aware of the needs of the communal." A God set outside tears us from the self; one moved into the self "snatches us from what is outside us." "No one has my God, but my God has everyone, including myself." "So it is always only the one God despite his multiplicity." One arrives at him "through your self seizing you."
- "The hero must fall for the sake of our redemption, since he is the model and demands imitation. But the measure of imitation is fulfilled." Reconcile to solitude in yourself and to the God outside; "if we are in ourselves, then the space around us is free, but filled by the God." Relations to men go through this empty space and through the God; earlier through selfishness. The spirit foretold that **"the cold of outer space will spread across the earth"**: the God will step between men and drive every individual "with the whip of icy cold to the warmth of his own monastic hearth," because people were beside themselves like madmen. [EDITOR fn. 155: refers to the dream in the Prologue.]
- **Desire:** "Selfish desire ultimately desires itself. You find yourself in your desire, so do not say that desire is vain." "Your desire is the father of the God, your self is the mother of the God, but the son is the new God, your master." Embracing the self makes the world seem "cold and empty"; the coming God moves into the emptiness. In solitude you have moved far from men and are nearer to them than ever: your God leads you "to the God of others, and through that to the true neighbor, to the neighbor of the self in others." In yourself you see your incapacity; you will no longer force others to be heroes; "Incapacity, too, wants to live, but it will overthrow your Gods."

## 9. LIBER PRIMUS — "Mysterium. Encounter" (Cap. ix)  [pp.276–279]

*Folio v(v). Chapter heading reads "Mysterium. Encounter."*

### 9.1 The vision of Elijah and Salome [pp.276–277]
Setting and sequence, as narrated:
1. The night after he considered the essence of God, he "lay in a dark depth." **An old man**, like an old prophet (editor: gray beard, Oriental robe in Black Book 2), stands before him, **a black serpent at his feet.**
2. Some distance away, **a house with columns.** A beautiful maiden steps out; she walks uncertainly: **she is blind.**
3. The old man waves; the narrator follows him to the house at the foot of a sheer rock wall; the serpent creeps behind. Inside, darkness reigns: a high hall with glittering walls; at the back **a bright stone the color of water.** In its reflection he sees **Eve, the tree and the serpent**, then **Odysseus** and his journey on the high seas.
4. A door opens on the right onto a **garden full of bright sunshine.** They step out.
5. **Dialogue.** Old man: "Do you know where you are?" Narrator: "I am a stranger here... Who are you?" "I am **Elijah** and this is my daughter **Salome**." Narrator: "The daughter of Herod, the bloodthirsty woman?" Elijah: "Why do you judge so? You see that she is blind. She is my daughter, the daughter of the prophet."
   - Narrator: "What miracle has united you?" Elijah: "It is no miracle, it was so from the beginning. My wisdom and my daughter are one." ... "her blindness and my sight have made us companions through eternity."
   - Narrator asks if he is truly in the underworld. Salome: "Do you love me?" Narrator: "I see only one thing, you are Salome, a tiger, your hands are stained with the blood of the holy one." Salome: "You will love me." "I love you." Narrator: "Leave me be, I dread you, you beast." Salome: "You do me wrong. Elijah is my father, and he knows the deepest mysteries. The walls of his house are made of precious stones... his eyes see the things of the future... Are these not worth a sin for you?" Narrator: "Your temptation is devilish. I long to be back in the upper world." Elijah: "The choice is yours."
   - Narrator: "I do not belong to the dead. I live in the light of day." Elijah recalls Salome's words. Narrator doubts a prophet can accept her as daughter and companion, "engendered from heinous seed... vain greed and criminal lust." Elijah: "But she loved a holy man... the prophet who announced the new God to the world... she is my daughter." Narrator: "How did she love him? Do you call that love?" Elijah: "What else was it?" "By her love shall you know her." "I and my daughter have been one since eternity."
   - Narrator: "You are the symbol of the most extreme contradiction." Elijah: **"We are real and not symbols."**
6. The black serpent writhes up the tree and hides in the branches. Gloom; Elijah rises; they silently go back through the hall. Doubt: "Will I come again? Salome loves me, do I love her?" He hears wild music, a tambourine, a sultry moonlit night, **the bloody-staring head of the holy one**; fear; he rushes out into pitch dark. He asks: "Who murdered the hero? Is this why Salome loves me?... was she the hand of the God?" The spirit of the depths: "Therein you acknowledge her divine power." "Must I love Salome?" [EDITOR fn. 161: Jung's 1925 account (a cosmic depth; Elijah and Salome; the old man with a young girl as a recurrent pattern; Logos and Eros) and the *Memories* remark that Salome is an anima figure, "blind because she does not see the meaning of things," Elijah the wise old prophet. fn. 157–158: Elijah as "living archetype"; Salome as fin-de-siècle fascination.]

### 9.2 Reflection: "This play... is my play" [p.277]
- The mystery play witnessed is "my play, not your play. It is my secret, not yours. You cannot imitate me." Whoever enters his own must grope "from stone to stone," embracing worthless and worthy with the same love; judgment, taste and pride must fall. "Let your hope... lead the way and serve you as a guide in the world of darkness, since it is of like substance with the forms of that world." [EDITOR fn. 162–164: earlier drafts frame this as mystery play and initiation into redemption mysteries; "You may follow me, not on my way, but on yours."]

### 9.3 Interpretation: forethinking and pleasure [pp.278–279]
- **Scene = a volcano's crater.** "My deep interior is a volcano that pushes out the fiery-molten mass of the unformed." He who enters the crater becomes chaotic matter, melts, and binds anew with "the children of chaos, the powers of darkness, the ruling and the seducing... the divine and the devilish." These powers stretch beyond his limits and connect him with all forms and beings.
- **The two powers of the depths:** *predetermination / forethinking* and *pleasure.* [EDITOR fn. 166–167: subjective interpretation of Elijah and Salome; the Corrected Draft substitutes "the Idea" for forethinking.]

| | **Forethinking (Prometheus; Elijah)** | **Pleasure (Salome)** |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Brings the chaotic to form and definition, digs channels, holds the object before pleasure; comes before thought | Force that desires and destroys forms; loves form in itself yet destroys forms it does not take |
| Nature | A seer; not powerful in itself; does not move | Blind; does not foresee but desires what it touches; is power and therefore moves |
| Need | Needs pleasure to come to form | Needs forethinking to come to form; unformed, it dissolves and flows to the deathly stillness of dispersal |
| Space | The dark space (the hall), limited; leads into depths of past and future; crystal = formed thought reflecting what is to come in what has gone | The bright garden; he who lives there needs no vision; feels the unending |
- Pleasure is not older than forethinking nor vice versa; "both are equally old and in nature intimately one. Only in man does the separate existence of both principles become apparent." [EDITOR fn. 168: Prometheus; Spitteler.]
- **The serpent as third principle:** a stranger to both though associated with both; it taught "the unconditional difference in essence between the two principles." From forethinking toward pleasure one first sees the deterrent serpent; the same in reverse. The serpent is "the earthly essence of man of which he is not conscious," its character changes by peoples and lands, a mystery flowing "from the nourishing earth-mother." The earthly (numen loci) separates the two in man but not in itself; the serpent causes man to be enslaved to one, then the other, so that it becomes error. One cannot live by forethinking alone or pleasure alone; one must take turns "obeying the prevailing law, unfaithful to the other." Thinkers practice thinking and lose pleasure; "therefore they are old and have a sharp face." Pleasure-lovers practice feeling and forget thinking; "therefore they are young and blind." Truth and error are in both. "The way of life writhes like the serpent from right to left... Thus the serpent is an adversary and a symbol of enmity, but also a wise bridge that connects right and left through longing." [EDITOR fn. 173: Jung's 1925 seminar diagram of functions (Thinking/Elijah top, Feeling/Salome bottom, Intuition left, Sensation/Serpent right); the serpent as introverting libido, anima function, wisdom.]
- **Scene reading:** the dark space = forethinking; the bright garden = pleasure. Eve and the serpent show the next step leads to pleasure and "from there again on lengthy wanderings like Odysseus," who went astray when he played his trick at Troy. A thinker who descends into forethinking finds his next step leading into Salome's garden; so the thinker fears his forethought. "A thinker should fear Salome, since she wants his head, especially if he is a holy man." The thinker "cannot be a holy person, otherwise he loses his head"; hiding in thought leads to petrification; one must turn back to "motherly forethought" for renewal. 
- Because the narrator was a thinker, the opposing principle appeared as Salome; if he had been one who feels, it would have appeared as "a serpent-encoiled daimon," slippery, dead, dangerous. "The thinker's passions are bad, therefore he has no pleasure. The thoughts of one who feels are bad, therefore he has no thoughts." The one neglected rots in darkness (mold, desolate webs). "So the serpent lies between the thinker and the one who feels. They are each other's poison and healing." He began to love Salome in the garden, a recognition that struck him: "What a thinker does not think he believes does not exist." "You begin to have a presentiment of the whole when you embrace your opposite principle, since the whole belongs to both principles, which grow from one root."
- **Elijah's saying (love sanctifies):** "You should recognize her through her love!" The object also sanctifies: Salome loved the prophet, which sanctified her; the prophet loved God, which sanctified him; Salome did not love God (profaned her); the prophet did not love Salome (profaned him); so they are each other's poison and death. "May the thinking person accept his pleasure, and the feeling person accept his own thought." [EDITOR fn. 184: Jung's 1913–17 development of introversion/thinking and extraversion/feeling, the letters to Hans Schmid, and later *Psychological Types*.]

## 10. LIBER PRIMUS — "Instruction" (Cap. x)  [pp.279–281]

*Folio vi(r). Date Dec 22, 1913 (fn. 185).*

### 10.1 Second image: the house in the crater [pp.279–280]
- The narrator stands in the rocky depth like a crater, before the house with columns. Salome walks along the wall toward the left, touching it like a blind person; the serpent follows. Elijah at the door waves; he calls Salome back. She looks like someone suffering; her hands are white and her face gentle: "I cannot detect any sacrilege in her nature." The narrator feels like a stupid boy.
- Elijah asks what he wants. Narrator: not obtrusiveness; a longing left in the house yesterday; he is tired, "head heavy as lead," lost in ignorance, has "played hypocritical games with myself"; "It seems to me as if I were more real here. And yet I do not like being here."
- Inside, before the crystal, he sees "in splendor **the mother of God with the child**. **Peter** stands in front of her in admiration—then Peter alone with the key—**the Pope with a triple crown—a Buddha** sitting rigidly in a circle of fire—a **many-armed bloody Goddess**—it is **Salome** desperately wringing her hands—she is my own soul, and now I see Elijah in the image of the stone." [EDITOR fn. 186–187: draft says the goddess is Kali; Black Book 2 compares the white man-shape to Michelangelo's Moses.] Elijah and Salome stand smiling.
- Elijah leads the narrator to a darker room: a burning red lamp from the ceiling, a marble lion in the middle; the narrator sits exhausted.

### 10.2 The dialogue with Elijah [pp.279–280]
- Elijah: "Not-knowing is guilt, but you believe that it is the urge toward forbidden knowledge that causes your feeling of guilt." "If it were not your law to be here, how would you be here?" Narrator: "I'm afflicted by fatal weakness." Elijah: "You are evasive. You cannot extricate yourself from your law... You yourself recognized what it means if Salome loves you."
- The thought: that Salome loves him. He had shunned it as far-fetched; men "confuse them with themselves." Elijah: "Will you therefore confuse yourself with a tree or animal, because you look at them and... exist with them in one and the same world? Must you be your thoughts, because you are in the world of your thoughts? But your thoughts are just as much outside your self as trees and animals are outside your body." Narrator: "My thought world was for me more word than world." He realizes he can consider his thoughts as outside himself. [EDITOR fn. 188: Jung's 1925 comment: "Only then I learned psychological objectivity... We must see our thoughts as events, as phenomena."]
- The terrible conclusion: he had thought Salome loved him because he resembles John or Elijah; he rejected this and thought she loves her badness in his badness: "This thought was devastating."
- Salome comes in, lays her arm around his shoulder, taking him for her father in whose chair he sits: **"I know that you are not my father. You are his son, and I am your sister."** Narrator: "Was this the terrible attraction... that unnamable horror of you?" "Who was our mother?" Salome: **"Mary."** Narrator: "Is it a hellish dream?... Are you out of your senses?... You are symbols and Mary is a symbol." Elijah: "You may call us symbols for the same reason that you can also call your fellow men symbols... You invalidate nothing and solve nothing by calling us symbols." "We are certainly what you call real. Here we are, and you have to accept us. The choice is yours."
- Salome withdraws. Behind the narrator, on a round altar, a high golden-red flame; the serpent has encircled the flame, eyes glittering golden. As he steps out he sees **a powerful lion going before him**; outside, a wide, cold, starry night.

### 10.3 Reflection: acknowledging yearning [pp.280–281]
- "It is no small matter to acknowledge one's yearning." Many do not want to know where their yearning is. "Yearning is the way of life. If you do not acknowledge your yearning, then you do not follow yourself but go on foreign ways that others have indicated to you. So you do not live your life but an alien one." You can never live others' lives, only pretend. If you give up your self you live it in others and become selfish toward them; this is "apish imitation"; reciprocal imitation produces the average expectation; "The image of the hero was set up for all in every age through the appetite for imitation. Therefore the hero was murdered, since we have all been aping him." The cause of apishness: "fear of loneliness and defeat."
- **"To live oneself means: to be one's own task."** It is not pleasure but "a long suffering, since you must become your own creator." You begin "with the worst and the deepest" not the best and highest. "The flowing together of the stream of life is not joy but pain, since it is power against power, guilt, and shatters the sanctified."
- **The Mother of God with the child** indicates the mystery of transformation: if forethinking and pleasure unite, a third arises, "the divine son, who is the supreme meaning, the symbol, the passing over into a new creation." The narrator is not himself the supreme meaning, "but the symbol becomes in me such that it has its substance, and I mine." He stands "like Peter in worship before the miracle of the transformation." He represents the God "as one who was a mother to the God," given "the freedom of the binding and loosing" (Matt. 18:18; editor fn. 193), which occurs in him and, via his being part of the world, through him in the world, "in the way of unavoidable effect," not his will. "I lock the past with one key; with the other I open the future." "I am its servant, just as the Pope is." [EDITOR fn. 194: draft says the Pope is an image of how God becomes human and visible lord; the supreme meaning becomes lord, "though not only in me."]
- "The symbol becomes my lord and unfailing commander... a starry and riddling image, whose meaning turns completely inward, and whose pleasure radiates outward like blazing fire, a Buddha in the flames." The symbol changes him from his one into his other: the "cruel Goddess of my interior, my womanly pleasure, my own other, the tormented tormentor." [EDITOR fn. 196–197: draft equates Kali = Salome = soul; passage on how the thinker's rejected feeling became "sensuality" and then Salome.]
- **Practical advice (para. 197):** "In the moment of your bewilderment, follow your forethinking and not your blind desire, since forethinking leads you to the difficulties that should always come first." If you look for a light you fall first into deeper darkness where "a weak reddish flame" gives enough brightness "to see your neighbor." "My forethinking rests on the lion, my power." [EDITOR fn. 198: lion of stone: pleasure dead and turned to stone.]
- **On thoughts as natural events** [pp.281–282]: He held to the sanctified form, hated the disorganized; his own law brought him here. He took the developing God for part of his self and his thought; "my thoughts are not my self... but exactly like the things of the world, alive and dead." You do not possess thoughts; they are "natural events" whose meaning you recognize only imperfectly. "Thoughts grow in me like a forest, populated by many different animals. But man is domineering in his thinking, and therefore he kills the pleasure of the forest and that of the wild animals." "Freedom is conditional." One changes things of the world that endanger welfare, after looking at their nature; likewise with thoughts. "The way of life is transformation, not exclusion. Well-being is a better judge than the law." [EDITOR fn. 199–200: Jung 1921 on the reality of unconscious contents.]
- "As I became aware of the freedom in my thought world, Salome embraced me and I thus became a prophet." The danger: "I am in danger of believing that I myself am significant since I see the significant. This will always drive us crazy."
- **Mary, Christ and Salome:** "Just as my thinking is the son of forethinking, so is my pleasure the daughter of love, of the innocent and conceiving mother of God. Aside from Christ Mary gave birth to Salome." The text cites the "gospel of the Egyptians" dialogue between Christ and Salome: "Eat every herb, but do not eat the bitter"; "If you crush the covering of shame, and when the two become one, and the male with the female, neither male nor female." [EDITOR fn. 202: the apocryphal Gospel of the Egyptians; Jung cites this logion as an example of the union of opposites.] "Forethinking is the procreative, love is the receptive. Both are beyond this world." "I won the power back again from the depths, and it went before me like a lion."

## 11. LIBER PRIMUS — "Resolution" (Cap. xi)  [pp.282–285]

*Folio vi(v). Date Dec 25, 1913 (fn. 206: "the third night"). Editor fn. 207: Jung's 1925 report that at this time he felt a conflict about going down and saw two dark principles fighting, two serpents.*

### 11.1 Vision: the serpents, the temple of the sun, Mime [pp.282–283]
- Deep longing to continue the mysteries; the struggle between doubt and desire. He stands before **a steep ridge in a wasteland** on a dazzling bright day and sees **the prophet high above him.** Elijah's hand makes an averting movement; he abandons his decision to climb and waits below.
- **Left bright, right dark:** to the right is dark night, to the left bright day, the rock separating them. On the dark side lies **a big black serpent**, on the bright side **a white serpent**; heads thrust toward each other, they fight; the black seems stronger, the white withdraws, billows of dust. Then the black withdraws: **the front part of its body has become white.** Both curl, one in light, the other in darkness. Elijah stands on the heights above. Elijah: "What did you see?" The narrator recounts; guesses the light's power will be so great that even the resisting darkness is illumined. [EDITOR fn. 210: Jung 1925: white = movement into the day, black into darkness; conflict of going down vs. up; Dante's reversed cones; Gnostic reversed cones.]
- Elijah climbs to a very high summit; the narrator follows. On the peak: masonry of huge blocks, a round embankment enclosing a courtyard with a mighty boulder like an altar. Elijah stands on the stone: **"This is the temple of the sun. This place is a vessel that collects the light of the sun."** [EDITOR fn. 208: Jung thought "a Druidic sacred place."]
- Elijah climbs down, shrinks, becomes dwarflike: **"I am Mime,** and I will show you the wellsprings. The collected light becomes water and flows in many springs from the summit into the valleys." He dives into a crevice; the narrator follows into a dark cave; hears a spring; the dwarf: "Here are my wells, whoever drinks from them becomes wise." The narrator cannot reach down, loses courage, leaves. [EDITOR fn. 209: Mime in Wagner's *Ring*.]
- The courtyard: strange, solitary, deathly silent, air clear and cool as on the remotest heights. **A serpent** (the prophet's) crawls over the stone and into the wall. He follows: a **minuscule little house with a portico** snuggling against the rock; the serpents become infinitely small; he feels himself shrinking; the walls enlarge into a huge mountain; he is below on the foundation of the crater, before the house of the prophet, who steps out.
- Dialogue: narrator: Elijah has shown him strange things; "Your world appears to me today in a new light. Just now it was as if I were separated by a starry distance from your place... But behold: it seems to be one and the same place." Elijah: "You wanted to come here far too much. I did not deceive you, you deceived yourself. He sees badly who wants to see; you have overreached yourself." Narrator admits Salome's words seemed monstrous madness. Elijah: "Step over to the crystal and prepare yourself in its light."

### 11.2 The crystal and the crucifixion initiation [p.283]
- A wreath of fire shines around the stone. He sees, in rapid succession and with rising dread: the coarse **peasant's boot**; the **foot of a giant that crushes an entire city**; the cross, the removal of the cross, the mourning ("How agonizing this sight is! No longer do I yearn—"); the **divine child with the white serpent in his right hand and the black serpent in his left**; **a green mountain with the cross of Christ on it and a stream of blood flowing from the summit**; Christ on the cross in his last hour; at the foot the **black serpent** coils and winds around his feet—he is held fast, arms outspread. Salome approaches. The serpent winds around his whole body; **his countenance is that of a lion.**
- Salome: "Mary was the mother of Christ, do you understand?" Narrator: "a terrible and incomprehensible power forces me to imitate the Lord in his final torment. But how can I presume to call Mary my mother?" Salome: **"You are Christ."**
- He stands with outstretched arms, crucified, body taut and entwined; "as if I stood alone on a high mountain." The serpent squeezes; **blood streams** down the mountainside. Salome bends to his feet and wraps her black hair round them; after a long time cries **"I see light!"** — her eyes open. The serpent falls limply. He strides over it and kneels at the feet of the prophet, whose form shines like a flame.
- Elijah: "Your work is fulfilled here. Other things will come. Seek untiringly, and above all **write exactly what you see.**" Elijah transforms into **a huge flame of white light**; the serpent wraps around Salome's foot, paralyzed; Salome kneels in wonderstruck devotion, tears falling. The narrator hurries out into the night, "like one who has no part in the glory of the mystery. My feet do not touch the ground of this earth, and it is as if I were melting into air." [EDITOR fn. 211: Jung's 1925 reading: Salome's declaration; fear that this is madness; compared with the Isis initiation in Apuleius; the lion-headed figure with a coiled snake is the Mithraic Leontocephalus; "In this deification mystery you make yourself into the vessel... in which the opposites reconcile." fn. 212: Jung's 1951 retelling; the pairing of a hierophant with a "sister/daughter" (Simon Magus and Helen, Zosimus and Theosebia).]

### 11.3 Interpretation: love and forethinking [pp.283–285]
- **Heavenly love vs. dark forethinking:** his longing led him up to "the overbright day," whose light is the opposite to the dark space of forethinking. The opposite principle is "heavenly love, the mother." The darkness around forethinking comes from its being invisible and taking place in the depths; the brightness of love from love being "visible life and action." His pleasure was with forethinking in its dark-surrounded garden; "I climbed down to my pleasure, but ascended to my love." Elijah is higher: forethinking is nearer to love than he is.
- **The serpent fight reread:** left is day, right is night. The principles are strictly separated and even hostile, taking serpent form, indicating their daimonic nature. The struggle repeats the earlier vision of the sun and black serpent: "At that time, the loving light was annihilated, and blood began to pour out. This was the great war." The spirit of the depths wants the struggle understood "as a conflict in every man's own nature."
- **The war as inner conflict:** after the hero's death our urge to live could no longer imitate; it went into the depths of every man and excited the terrible conflict between the powers of the depths. "Forethinking is singleness, love is togetherness. Both need one another, and yet they kill one another." Men, not knowing the conflict is in themselves, go mad and blame each other. "If one-half of mankind is at fault, then every man is half at fault." "If you are aggravated against your brother, think that you are aggravated against the brother in you." If you kill your contrary fellow man, "you also kill that person in yourself and have murdered a part of your life. The spirit of this dead man follows you." "You need your wholeness to live onward." [EDITOR fn. 220–221: draft naming Germans, English and Russians; Jung's 1916/1918 prefaces to *Psychology of the Unconscious Processes*: change in the individual is the beginning of change in the nation.]
- **Pure principles breed their opposite:** if he endorses one pure principle he steps to one side; the principle's forethinking becomes "an ugly dwarf who lives in a dark cave like an unborn in the womb" (Mime), "dwarfish cleverness, false and of the night," just as the heavenly mother appears below as Salome. "That which is lacking in the pure principle appears as the serpent. The hero strives after the utmost in the pure principle, and therefore he finally falls for the serpent." "If you go to thinking, take your heart with you. If you go to love, take your head with you. Love is empty without thinking, thinking hollow without love." In climbing down, "I became smaller."
- "Great is he who is in love, since love is the present act of the great creator." "But whoever distances himself from love, feels himself powerful." The thinker recognizes his nullity "as a smallest point between the infinity of what has passed and of what is to come"; feels great if he distances himself from thinking. To one in love, form is trifling but his field of vision ends with the form given; to one in thinking, form is unsurpassable, but at night he sees "the diversity of the innumerable worlds." Whoever is in love is "a full and overflowing vessel and awaits the giving"; whoever is in forethinking is "deep and hollow and awaits fulfillment." Man staggers "between too much and too little"; animals and plants have enough. "Man doesn't only grow from within himself, for he is also creative from within himself. The God becomes revealed in him." [EDITOR fn. 226–227: drafts explain why the idea appears as a Jewish prophet and pleasure as heathen Salome.]
- **Haste:** "The spirit of this time has condemned us to haste. You have no more futurity and no more past if you serve the spirit of this time. We need the life of eternity. We bear the future and the past in the depths. The future is old and the past is young." The depths "will force you into the mysteries of Christ": "man is not redeemed through the hero, but becomes a Christ himself. The antecedent example of the saints symbolically teaches us this." [EDITOR fn. 228.]
- **Will:** "Whoever wants to see will see badly. It was my will that blindness deceived me. It was my will that provoked the huge uproar among the daimons." Should he therefore not want anything? "In the end I found that I wanted myself in everything, but without looking for myself. Therefore I no longer wanted to seek myself outside of myself but within." He wants to grasp himself, go on without knowing what he wants: "and thus I fell into the mystery."
- **Willing the war** [p.285]: "You wanted this war. That is good. If you had not, then the evil of this war would be small. But with your wanting you make the evil great. If you do not succeed in producing the greatest evil out of this war, you will never learn the violent deed and learn to overcome fighting what lies outside you." Christians run after heroes and redeemers who should take the agony on themselves; "with that you pile up a mountain of Calvary over all Europe." If the war produces terrible evil and countless victims, "this is good, since it makes each of you ready to sacrifice himself." "Drink your fill of the bloody atrocities of the war... then your eyes will open, you will see that you yourselves are the bearers of such fruit." "Willing creates blindness, and blindness leads to the way." One should "will that error which you take for the best truth." [EDITOR fn. 229–233: Nietzsche on redeeming the past; Jung's 1916 remark that the will is of no use; drafts: "To be Christ oneself is the true following of Christ."]
- **The crystal:** "signifies the unalterable law of events that comes of itself." The peasant's boot (seen "on the night of Christmas day of the year 1913") is read as the sign of the horrors of the peasant war, murdering incendiaries and bloody cruelty; the giant's foot crushing a city; "I saw that the way to self-sacrifice began here." They will all be enraptured by these experiences "and will want to understand them as outer events. It is an inner happening; that is the way to the perfection of the mystery of Christ, so that the peoples learn self-sacrifice." "May the frightfulness become so great that it can turn men's eyes inward, so that their will no longer seeks the self in others but in themselves." [EDITOR fn. 234–235: German peasants' rebellion of 1525; Jung's 1918 preface on outward vs. inward seeking.]
- **The God holds the principles:** "I saw a new God, a child, who subdued daimons in his hand. The God holds the separate principles in his power, he unites them. The God develops through the union of the principles in me. He is their union." If you will one principle you are far from the other; if you will both you excite conflict, "since you cannot want both at the same time. From this arises the need, the God appears in it, he takes your conflicting will in his hand, in the hand of a child whose will is simple and beyond conflict. You cannot learn this, it can only develop in you." "Will yourself that leads to the way."
- **Fear of self and the mountain of sacrifice:** "But fundamentally you are terrified of yourself and therefore you prefer to run to all others rather than to yourself." He saw the mountain of sacrifice with blood streaming from its sides; when pride and power satisfied men and beauty beamed from the eyes of women at the outbreak of war, "I knew that mankind was on the way to self-sacrifice." The spirit of the depths has seized mankind "and forces self-sacrifice upon it. Do not seek the guilt here or there. The spirit of the depths clutched the fate of man unto itself as it clutched mine. He leads mankind through the river of blood to the mystery: In the mystery man himself becomes the two principles, the lion and the serpent."
- **Final statement:** "Because I also want my being other, I must become a Christ. I am made into Christ, I must suffer it. Thus the redeeming blood flows. Through the self-sacrifice my pleasure is changed and goes over into its higher principle. Love is sighted, but pleasure is blind. Both principles are one in the symbol of the flame. The principles strip themselves of human form."
- "The mystery showed me in images what I should afterward live. I did not possess any of those boons that the mystery showed me, for I still had to earn all of them." — **finis. part. prim. (End of part one)** [p.285]
- [EDITOR fn. 238–241: long drafted passages on the God holding love and forethinking, willing "both," Kali/Salome; fn. 241: Jung reportedly told a Dutch poet he had written *Psychological Types* on the basis of thirty pages of the Red Book, likely these three chapters of the Mysterium, and in 1925 said he fused outer (patients) and inner (unconscious) experience into the book of Types and called the fusion the "transcendent function."]

## 13. LIBER SECUNDUS — "The Red One" (Cap. i)  [pp.290–292]

*Folio 2. Date Dec 26, 1913 (fn. 7). Handwritten draft chapter title: "The Great Wandering I. The Red One" (Corrected Draft; fn. 4).*

### 13.1 Opening state and vision [p.290]
- "The door of the Mysterium has closed behind me." His will is paralyzed; the spirit of the depths possesses him; he waits without knowing for what. In the following night he reaches "a solid point." (This paragraph was added in the Draft; fn. 6.)
- **Vision:** he stands on **the highest tower of a castle**, far back in time. He sees a solitary countryside of fields and forests. He wears a **green garment** and a **horn** hangs from his shoulder: he is the **tower guard**. He sees a **red point** approach on a winding road, disappearing in forests, reappearing: **a horseman in a red coat**. The horseman rides through the gate, steps creak on the stairway, he knocks. A strange fear: **the Red One**, "his long shape wholly shrouded in red, even his hair is red." The narrator thinks: "in the end he will turn out to be the devil."

### 13.2 The dialogue (T. R. = The Red One; I = narrator) [pp.290–291]
- The Red One greets the man on the tower: "Your waiting has called me." Narrator: "Who are you?" The Red One: "You think I am the devil. Do not pass judgment. Perhaps you can also talk to me without knowing who I am. What sort of a superstitious fellow are you, that immediately you think of the devil?"
- Narrator: "My life in the castle is poor... no one climbs up to me." He waits for "some of the world's wealth, which we don't see here." Red One: "I have wandered a long time through the world, seeking those like you who sit upon a high tower on the lookout for things unseen."
- Narrator senses "something worldly, something impudent, or exuberant... something pagan." Red One: "I'm no old pagan as you seem to think." Narrator: "You're no real pagan, but the kind of pagan who runs alongside our Christian religion." Red One: "You're truly a good diviner of riddles."
- Red One mocks the narrator's solemnity ("an unbelievably ponderous and serious person"). Narrator says it is difficult to be serious "in your presence. You bring a certain gallows air with you... you're bound to be from the black school of Salerno, where pernicious arts are taught by pagans." [EDITOR fn. 8: Salerno; possibly the Academia Segreta.] Red One: "You're superstitious and too German."
- The Red One "seems to get redder, his garments shine like glowing iron." He says he hides nothing; "I simply amuse myself with your weighty seriousness and your comic veracity." Narrator: "I neither really belong to this time nor to this place. A spell has banished me to this place and time for years. I am really not what you see before you."
- Red One: "Are you something of a saint? Hardly a philosopher, since you have no aptitude for scholarly language. But a saint? ... Your solemnity smells of fanaticism... a simplicity that smacks of stale bread and water." Narrator: "you speak as one trapped in the spirit of this time... you lack the terms of comparison." Red One: "Perhaps you attended the school of the pagans? You answer like a sophist." [EDITOR fn. 9: Sophists.]
- **On Christianity:** narrator: "no one is allowed to avoid the mysteries of the Christian religion unpunished... he whose heart has not been broken over the Lord Jesus Christ drags a pagan around in himself who holds him back from the best." Red One: "Again this old tune? What for, if you are not a Christian saint? Are you a doctor of theology...?" Narrator: it is "hardly a coincidence that the whole world has become Christian... the task of Western man to carry Christ in his heart and to grow with his suffering, death, and resurrection."
- **Exchange about Jews** [p.291]: the Red One notes there are good Jews who had no need of "your solemn gospels." The narrator replies that the Jew himself lacks something ("one in his head, another in his heart") and feels it; the Red One says he's "no Jew" but defends the Jew and says the narrator seems a Jew hater; the narrator answers that such accusations are reflexes of those who feel the lack and do not want to admit it; asks whether Christianity left no mark on souls. [note: this exchange is voiced by the characters in the dialogue; I report it neutrally as content of the text, not endorsing it. The editor's fn. 10 notes the draft line "No one can flout the spiritual development of many centuries and reap what they have not sowed."]
- **Seriousness vs. dancing:** Red One: "What the devil is troubling you? Only Christianity with its mournful escape from the world can make people so ponderous and sullen." Narrator: other things bespeak seriousness. Red One: "Oh, I know; you mean life... Life doesn't require any seriousness. On the contrary, it's better to dance through life." [EDITOR fn. 11: Nietzsche's Zarathustra on learning to dance.] Narrator: "I know how to dance... Dancing goes with the mating season. I know that there are those who are always in heat, and those who also want to dance for their Gods. Some are ridiculous and others enact Antiquity, instead of honestly admitting their utter incapacity for such expression."
- Red One (dropping his mask, more serious since this is his province): "It's conceivable that there is some third thing for which dancing would be the symbol." **Transformation:** the red of the rider turns into a tender reddish flesh color; the narrator's green garments **burst into leaf.** Narrator: "Perhaps too there is a joy before God that one can call dancing. But I haven't yet found this joy... Things came, but joy was not among them." Red One: **"Don't you recognize me, brother, I am joy!"** The narrator asks to take his hand; the image fades; "Joy? Was he joy?"

### 13.3 Reflection on joy and the devil [pp.291–292]
- **"Surely this red one was the devil, but my devil. That is, he was my joy, the joy of the serious person, who keeps watch alone on the high tower"** — "his red-colored, red-scented, warm bright red joy." Not the secret joy in thoughts and looking, but "that strange joy of the world that comes unsuspected like a warm southerly wind with swelling fragrant blossoms and the ease of living." Poets know this seriousness; the devil seeks them for their "springlike joy." Whoever tastes it "forgets himself"; "nothing is sweeter than forgetting oneself." Some are petrified and too heavy, others too light.
- Method: he confronted the devil earnestly "as with a real person. This I learned in the Mysterium: to take seriously every unknown wanderer who personally inhabits the inner world, since they are real because they are effectual." "It does not help that we say in the spirit of this time: there is no devil." [EDITOR fn. 15: Jung 1928 on active imagination: "the real is what works. The fantasies of the unconscious work."]
- "A religious conversation is inevitable with the devil, since he demands it, if one does not want to surrender to him unconditionally. Because religion is precisely what the devil and I cannot agree about." "Taking the devil seriously does not mean going over to his side, or else one becomes the devil. Rather it means coming to an understanding. Thereby you accept your other standpoint. With that the devil fundamentally loses ground, and so do you." The devil abhors religion's solemnity and candor, yet religion is how he can be brought to an understanding; what struck him was dancing, his own domain; "He fails to take seriously only what concerns others because that is the peculiarity of all devils." Common ground: dancing is neither lust nor madness but an expression of joy. "I turn green like a tree in spring."
- That joy is the devil, or the devil joy, "has got to worry you." "There is always something devilish about joy"; joy tears a neighbor from his course; "life is like a great fire that torches everything in its vicinity. But fire is the element of the devil." One cannot make a pact with joy since it disappears; "you cannot capture the devil either... He is stupid if he lets himself be caught." "The devil always seeks to saw off the branch on which you sit. That is useful and protects you from falling asleep." If you run after joy you arrive at pleasure and from there "go straight to Hell, your own particular Hell." [EDITOR fn. 16–17: drafts say every attentive person knows their Hell but not their devil; seriousness suits the devil poorly.]
- Result: "The devil accepted some of my seriousness, and I accepted some of his joy. This gave me courage. But if the devil has gotten more earnest, one must brace oneself. It is always a risky thing to accept joy, but it leads us to life and its disappointment, from which the wholeness of our life becomes." [EDITOR fn. 18: draft: the devil tempts first through women; "ready to be caught in a fox trap."]

## 14. LIBER SECUNDUS — "The Castle in the Forest" (Cap. ii)  [pp.292–295]

*Folio 5. Date Dec 28, 1913 (fn. 20). Handwritten draft title: "Second Adventure" (fn. 19).*

### 14.1 The adventure [pp.292–294]
- "In the second night thereafter" he walks alone in a dark forest, lost; dark cart track; he comes to quiet dark swamp water with **a small old castle in its center**. He knocks; it rains; an **old-fashioned servant** admits him to a dark vestibule and up a worn stairway to a white-walled hall lined with **black chests and wardrobes**. [EDITOR fn. 21: Dante's *Inferno* begins with the poet lost in a dark wood.]
- A reception room with old upholstered furniture and a dim lamp. The servant opens a side door: **a scholar's study** with bookshelves on all four walls and a large writing desk at which sits **an old man in a long black robe**, careworn, dignified, with the modest-fearful look of scholars "squashed to nothing by the abundance of knowledge." He is absent and defensive. The narrator asks for lodging; "So, you want to sleep, then please yourself." The old man sinks back into his book, then remembers: "Oh—forgive me—I totally forgot that you are waiting here." The servant leads him to a small white chamber with a large bed.
- In bed he thinks of the old man's "ideal though solitary existence," then cannot help imagining **the old man has hidden a beautiful daughter** there: "a vulgar idea for a novel—an insipid, worn-out theme." He lists the clichés (castle in a forest, old man petrified in books protecting a costly treasure). He cannot sleep, wonders if the bed is bewitched, hears steps; the door opens: **a slim girl, pale as death**, comes closer: "Have you come at last?" he is horrified by "the novel wants to become real" and "a lending library" Hell. She says: "Oh, so you too think me common?"
- **Dialogue:** narrator: "Forgive me, but are you real?" She: "You wretch, how can you doubt that I am real?" She falls to her knees sobbing. He feels pity, asks in earnest whether to assume she is real. She: "I am the old man's daughter. He holds me here in captivity; not out of envy or hate, but out of love, since I am his only child and the image of my mother who died young." The narrator thinks it is "pulp fiction from the lending library." She keeps crying; "what if she were real?" He says he believes her despite everything. She: "Finally, finally a word from a human mouth!" She rises beaming and beautiful, with "a deep purity" in her look, "a beautiful and unworldly soul... to come into the life of reality; to all reality worthy of pity; to the bath of filth and the well of health." 
- She: "You spoke the redeeming word when you no longer placed the banal between you and me. Know then: **I was bewitched by the banal.**" Narrator: "you now become very fairy-tale-like." She: "Be reasonable, dear friend, and do not stumble now over the fabulous, since the fairy tale is the great mother of the novel, and has even more universal validity than the most avidly read novel of your time. And you know that what has been on everyone's lips for millennia, though repeated endlessly, still comes nearest the ultimate human truth." [EDITOR fn. 22: Riklin on fairy tales; Jung on archetypes; Marie-Louise von Franz.]
- Narrator asks her view of "ultimate truths" — he found it strange to seek them in banality; she: "The more uncommon these highest truths are, the more inhuman must they be and the less they speak to you as something valuable or meaningful concerning human essence and being. Only what is human and what you call banal and hackneyed contains the wisdom that you seek." She asserts she lives in reality as well or better than many of her sex.
- He says her father did not invite him to scholarly talk and he was aggrieved; with her it is better: "You are uncommon." She: "You are mistaken, I am very common." He: "Happy and enviable is the man who will free you." She: "Do you love me?" He: "By God, I love you—but—unfortunately I am already married." She: **"So—you see: even banal reality is a redeemer. I thank you, dear friend, and I bring you greetings from Salome."** Her shape dissolves into darkness; in the moonlight where she stood lies **a profusion of red roses.** [EDITOR fn. 23: Jung's 1951 summary: the anima as ghostly being; fairy-tale plane.]

### 14.2 Second-layer reflection: adventure, limits, gender [pp.294–295]
- **Adventure and limits:** "If no outer adventure happens to you, then no inner adventure happens to you either. The part that you take over from the devil—joy, that is—leads you into adventure. In this way you will find your lower as well as your upper limits." Not knowing limits, one runs into "artificial barriers of your imagination and the expectations of your fellow men." Life wants to jump over arbitrary barriers. One knows limits only on reaching them, which requires balance; balance requires nurturing one's opposite, "hateful to you in your innermost core, because it is not heroic." [EDITOR fn. 24: draft: inner life does not become richer at the expense of the outer; "Balance finds the way."]
- **The romantic as a step backward:** his spirit reflected on the rare and uncommon; everything ordinary in him suffered harm unnoticed and hankered after life, "hence this adventure. I was smitten by the romantic. The romantic is a step backward. To reach the way, one must sometimes also take a few steps backward." What he saw in the Mysterium as Salome and Elijah became in life the old scholar and the pale locked-up daughter: "a distorted likeness of the Mysterium." He reached "the awkwardness and ordinariness of life, where I run out of thoughts." What he formerly loved he now experiences as feeble; what he derided, as towering and craved. On accepting the absurdity, the maiden transformed into an autonomous meaning. "One inquires into the desire of the ridiculous, and that is enough for it to change."
- **Masculinity and femininity:** "Do you know how much femininity man lacks for completeness? Do you know how much masculinity woman lacks for completeness? You seek the feminine in women and the masculine in men. And thus there are always only men and women. But where are people?" The man should "seek and recognize [the feminine] in yourself as you possess it from the beginning"; the woman likewise assume the masculine in herself. "Humankind is masculine and feminine, not just man or woman." "The most masculine man has a feminine soul, and the most feminine woman has a masculine soul. The more manly you are, the more remote from you is what woman really is, since the feminine in yourself is alien and contemptuous." [EDITOR fn. 26–27: *Psychological Types* on anima/animus; later integration and withdrawal of projections.]
- If you take joy from the devil and set off on adventures, you accept your pleasure and must decide "whether your pleasure spoils or enhances you." If you remain with yourself as a man "who is himself and not of the devil, then you will remember your humanity. You will not behave toward women per se as a man, but as a human being, that is to say, as if you were of the same sex as her. You will recall your femininity." It may seem unmanly, stupid, feminine; "you must accept the ridiculous, otherwise you will suffer distress, and there will come a time... when it will suddenly round on you and make you ridiculous." For the most masculine man it is bitter to accept femininity as it seems "powerless and tawdry." Likewise for the woman.
- **Slavery to woman:** "You are a slave of what you need in your soul. The most masculine man needs women, and he is consequently their slave. Become a woman yourself, and you will be saved from slavery to woman." "It is good for you once to put on women's clothes: people will laugh at you, but through becoming a woman you attain freedom from women and their tyranny. The acceptance of femininity leads to completion. The same is valid for the woman who accepts her masculinity." [EDITOR fn. 28: Corrected Draft: "if he accepts the feminine in himself he frees himself from slavery to woman."]
- "The feminine in men is bound up with evil... The masculine in the woman is bound up with evil. Therefore people hate to accept their own other. But if you accept it... when you become the one who is mocked, **the white bird of the soul comes flying.** It was far away; but your humiliation attracted it." The mystery draws near; things happen like miracles; "A gold luster shines, since the sun has risen from its grave." "As a man you have no soul, since it is in the woman; as a woman you have no soul, since it is in the man. But if you become a human being, then your soul comes to you." [EDITOR fn. 29: Dieterich: the soul as bird in popular belief.]
- **Two worlds:** if you remain within arbitrary boundaries "you will walk as between two high walls." If you break them and the immensity inspires fear, "the ancient sleeper awakens in you, whose messenger is the white bird. Then you need the message of the old tamer of chaos. There in the whirl of chaos dwells eternal wonder." "Man belongs not only to an ordered world, he also belongs in the wonder-world of his soul." Therefore "you must make your ordered world horrible, so that you are put off by being too much outside yourself." The inner world is infinite, no poorer than the outer; "Man lives in two worlds. A fool lives here or there, but never here and there."
- **On the scholar [pp.295]:** "Perhaps you think that a man who consecrates his life to research leads a spiritual life... But such a life is also external... such a scholar does not live for outer things but for outer thoughts—not for himself but for his object." He has "thrown himself away in all the books and thoughts of others"; his soul is in great need, humiliating itself and begging recognition. Old scholars run after recognition undignified: offended if not mentioned, cast down if another says the same thing better, irreconcilable if someone alters their views; "starved souls... thirst which can never be slaked. The soul demands your folly, not your wisdom." [EDITOR fn. 30: drafts say the narrator himself was this old man, "wresting grains of sand from the infinite desert."]
- **Beyond gender, remaining human:** "Therefore, because I rise above gendered masculinity and yet do not exceed the human, the feminine that is contemptible to me transforms itself into a meaningful being. This is the most difficult thing—to be beyond the gendered and yet remain within the human." Rising above the gendered by a general rule makes you "dry, hard, and inhuman." "You may go past the gendered for human reasons, and never for the sake of a general rule that remains the same in the most diverse situations... If you act from your humanity, you act from that particular situation without general principle." Such action "does justice to the situation, perhaps at the expense of a general rule. That should not be too painful... because you are not the rule." "There is something else that is human, something all too human, and whoever has ended up there will do well to remember the blessing of the general rule. For the general rule also has meaning and has not been set up for fun. It comprises much venerable work of the human spirit." Those who cannot hold a general principle above the gendered "have become their own imagination and arbitrariness"; they "need to remember the gendered, so that they wake from their dreams to reality." [EDITOR fn. 31: Nietzsche's *Human, All Too Human*.]
- **Your own Hell:** "It is as agonizing as a sleepless night to fulfill the beyond from the here and now, namely the other and the opposing in myself. It sneaks up like a fever, like a poisonous fog." The daimonic arrives as something insipid and worn out that makes you sick; you long to return to your visible world's "supernal beauties" and curse the beyond as "the disgust, scum, refuse of the human animal." But "do not place your disgust between your here-and-now and your beyond. The way to your beyond leads through Hell and in fact through your own wholly particular Hell, whose bottom consists of knee-deep rubble, whose air is the spent breath of millions, whose fires are dwarflike passions, and whose devils are chimerical sign-boards." "Everything odious and disgusting is your own particular Hell... made up of all the things that you always ejected from your sanctuary with a curse and a kick of the foot." You enter "like a stupid and curious fool and gaze in wonder at the scraps that have fallen from your table." [EDITOR fn. 32: Jung's 1916 "Individuation and Collectivity": individuation means isolation, "inwardly into hell."]
- **Practical:** "You really want to rage, but you see at the same time how well rage suits you... Good for you if you can swear! You will find that profanity is lifesaving." In Hell, "quietly look into everything that excites your contempt or rage; thereby you accomplish the miracle that I experienced with the pale maiden. You give soul to the soulless, and thereby it can come to something out of horrible nothingness. Thus you will redeem your other into life." Your values want to draw you ahead of yourself; your being pulls you to the bottom like lead; you cannot live both at once, but "on the way you can live both. Therefore the way redeems you." "You cannot at the same time be on the mountain and in the valley, but your way leads you from mountain to valley and from valley to mountain. Much begins amusingly and leads into the dark. Hell has levels." [EDITOR fn. 33: Dante's nine levels.]

## 15. LIBER SECUNDUS — "One of the Lowly" (Cap. iii), first part  [pp.296–297]

*Chapter begins on PDF 296 and continues in the next part. Folio 11. Date Dec 29, 1913 (fn. 35). Handwritten draft title: "Third Adventure"; the Corrected Draft had "The Rogue," covered over with paper (fn. 34).*

### 15.1 The tramp [pp.296–297]
- "In the following night" he wanders in "a homely, snow-covered country." Gray evening sky; moist, frosty air. A man "who does not look trustworthy" joins him: he has **only one eye**, a few scars on his face, is poor, dirty, a tramp with black stubble. The narrator carries a good walking stick.
- **Dialogue:** the tramp: "It's damned cold." He asks where the narrator is going: to the next village for the night. The tramp: "I'd like to do that too, but will hardly manage to get a bed." He worked for a locksmith until a few days ago; now looks for work. Narrator suggests farm work; tramp refuses ("up early... work is hard and wages are low"); the country is boring; "there is no mental stimulation, the farmers are clods." The narrator is astonished: "Better that he honestly earn his keep, and when he has done that for once he can think of stimulation."
- **Cinema:** the tramp's mental stimulation in the city is **the cinema**: "You get to see everything that happens in the world." He recounts feats: "a man who ran up houses. Another carried his head under his arm. Another even stood in the middle of a fire and wasn't burnt." The narrator reflects that Hell has cinemas for those who despised them on earth, but also that saints carried their heads under their arms (Zurich's patron martyrs; fn. 36), levitated (Francis, Ignatius), the three men in the fiery furnace (fn. 37), and the *Acta Sanctorum* as "historical cinema" (fn. 38): "today's miracles are simply somewhat less mythical than technical." He regards the tramp with feeling: "he lives the history of the world—and I?"
- The tramp also saw "how the King of Spain was murdered." Narrator: "But he wasn't murdered at all." Tramp: "Well, that doesn't matter; in that case it was one of those damned capitalist kings. At least they got one of them. If all of them were taken out, the people would be free." The narrator reflects: *Wilhelm Tell* by Schiller—"the man is standing right in the thick of it, in the stream of heroic story. One who announces the murder of the tyrant to a sleeping people." [EDITOR fn. 39: Schiller's *Wilhelm Tell*, act 4 scene 3.]
- **The inn:** a country tavern, clean parlor, men with beer in a corner. The narrator is recognized as a "gentleman" and led to the better corner; the tramp sits at the far end; the narrator has him served a proper evening meal. The tramp looks "full of expectation and hunger—with his one eye."
- **The tramp's account:** lost the eye in a brawl, but "I also got my knife into the other fellow pretty nicely. After that he got three months. They gave me six. But it was beautiful in prison. At the time the building was completely new. I worked in the locksmith's... Prison really isn't all that bad." The narrator checks no one is listening; wonders whether Hell has prisons for those who never saw one; "mustn't it be a peculiarly beautiful feeling to hit bottom in reality at least once, where there is no going down any further, but only upward beckons at best?" After release he was banished, went to France: "It was lovely there." "What demands beauty makes! Something can be learned from this man."
- **The brawl:** over a woman who was carrying the other man's bastard, "but I wanted to marry her. She was already due. After that she didn't want to anymore. I haven't heard from her." He will be thirty-five in spring; "Once I find a proper job we can get married right off. I'll find myself one, I will." Something is wrong with his lungs "but that'll soon get better again." He coughs; the narrator "silently admire[s] the poor devil's unswerving optimism."

### 15.2 Death in the night [p.297]
- After dinner he goes to bed in a humble room; hears the other settle in next door, cough, fall still. Suddenly he awakens to **an uncanny moan, gurgle and half-stifled cough**, jumps up, opens the door: moonlight floods the room; the man lies dressed on a sack of straw; **a dark stream of blood** flows from his mouth into a puddle. He tries to support him; "the hand of death lies on him." He is sullied with blood "twice over"; his own hands covered. A rattling sigh; then deathly stillness.
- Narrator: "Where am I? Are there also cases of death in Hell for those who have never thought about death?" He looks at his bloodied hands "as if I were a murderer... Is it not the blood of my brother that sticks to my hands?" The moon paints his shadow black on the white walls. "What am I doing here? Why this horrible drama?" He asks the moon as witness; "it is probably all the same to the moon." "What did this one do? He worked, lazed about, laughed, drank, ate, slept, gave his eye for the woman, and for her sake forfeited his good name; furthermore, he lived the human myth after a fashion, he admired the wonder-workers, praised the death of the tyrant, and vaguely dreamed of the freedom of the people. And then—then he miserably died—like everyone else." He sits on the floor: "All lights gutter out in final despondency and loneliness. Death has entered—and there is no one left to grieve. This is a final truth and no riddle. What delusion could make us believe in riddles?"

### 15.3 Second-layer reflection: the destitute and the depths [pp.297]
- "We stand on the spiky stones of misery and death." A destitute joins me and wants admittance into my soul; "I am thus not destitute enough. Where was my destitution when I did not live it? I was a player at life." Winter went on, the destitute stood in the snow and froze. "I join myself with him, since I need him. He makes living light and easy. He leads to the depths, to the ground where I can see the heights. **Without the depths, I do not have the heights.**" If always on heights, "I wear them out and the best becomes atrocious to me. But because I do not want to have it, my best becomes a horror to me. Because of that I myself become a horror, a horror to myself and to others, and a bad spirit of torment."
- "A man who can no longer climb down from his heights is sick, and he brings himself and others to torment." If you have reached your depths, you see your height "light up brightly over you, worthy of desire and far-off... since secretly you would prefer not to reach it since it seems unattainable." We praise heights when low and claim we would have left them only with pain.
- "At your low point you are no longer distinct from your fellow beings... you also climb into the holy stream of common life, where you are no longer an individual on a high mountain, but **a fish among fish, a frog among frogs.**" Your heights are your own mountain: "There you are individual and live your very own life." If you live your own life, you do not live the common life, "the life of history and the inalienable and ever-present burdens and products of the human race." On the low point "you live the endlessness of being, but not the becoming. Becoming belongs to the heights and is full of torment. How can you become if you never are? Therefore you need your bottommost, since there you are. But therefore you also need your heights, since there you become."
- "If you live the common life at your lowest reaches, then you become aware of your self. If you are on your heights, then you are your best, and you become aware only of your best, but not that which you are in the general life as a being." "Everything is riddlesome to one who is becoming, but not to one who is. He who suffers from riddles should take thought of his lowest condition; we solve those riddles from which we suffer, but not those which please us."
- "To be that which you are is the bath of rebirth. In the depths, being is not an unconditional persistence but an endlessly slow growth." You think you stand still like swamp water but slowly flow into the sea that covers the earth's greatest deeps; firm land seems "an island imbedded in the womb of the immeasurable sea." "As a drop in the ocean you take part in the current, ebb and flow... wander vast distances in blurred currents and wash up on strange shores, not knowing how you got there. You mount the billows of huge storms and are swept back into the depths. And you do not know how this happens to you. You had thought that your movement came from you and that it needed your decisions and efforts..." (**text breaks off at the end of PDF 297, mid-sentence; chapter continues in the next part**).

## 19. Gaps and cautions
- **Range boundaries.** Starts mid-chapter (the preceding Liber Primus chapter's title and opening are in Part 1); ends mid-sentence in Liber Secundus ch. iii ("One of the Lowly"), continuing in Part 3. The text was not supplemented from memory.
- **No Introduction in this range.** All editor material here is footnotes and headings; no scholarly essay on the history or composition of the work falls within PDF 265 to 297 (other parts hold that).
- **OCR noise.** The two-column layout of the translation (a fold-over of the manuscript's two columns, with folio markers like "[HI iii(v) 2]" and bracketed image tags like "[Image iii(v) r]") causes line interleaving in the text file; some passages in Cap. v and Cap. xi had ligature damage ("sel£" for "self," "ft" for "ff", "l<" for "k"). I read through them and reconstructed obvious readings; I marked nothing as garbled in my summary because none was unreadable, but several footnotes contain struck-through/crossed-out draft text rendered as hyphenated fragments (fn. 197, 238, 240); their gist is summarized, not quoted exactly.
- **Images.** The range's illustrations (e.g., "Image iii(v) r", "Image iii(v) 2", "Image iv(v)", "Image v(v)") appear only as bracketed tags in the text; their visual content (and any calligraphy) is not described here. The calligraphic facsimile pages are outside this part.
- **Footnotes** are summarized by kind and key point, not exhaustively; bibliographic details and publisher data are omitted. A couple of footnote cross-reference page numbers appear garbled in the source ("p. 000" in fn. 187).
- **Sensitive content.** The text includes a dialogue in which characters make stereotyping remarks about Jews (Liber Secundus ch. i, p.291), a violent sequence (murder of Siegfried; assassination imagery; a death by hemorrhage), and sexual symbolism around Salome. I have reported these neutrally as events of the text.
- **Ambiguities.** The text often alternates between concrete vision and abstract sermon without marking the change; where I attribute a statement to "the spirit of the depths" or "the soul" I follow the surrounding dialogue tags. The "[2]" markers in the source mark the second-layer commentary; I treat the text following as reflective commentary unless a scene begins.
- **Not covered:** the facsimile pages (PDF 14 to 223, other units), PDF pages 224 to 264 (previous part) and 298 to 402 (later parts).
