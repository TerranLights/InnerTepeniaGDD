## Overview of this range

Chapter XIV is the book's culminating synthesis. Jung gathers the Gnostic and alchemical material of the earlier chapters into a set of four "quaternios" (fourfold diagrams, each a double pyramid), arranges them as stages of one continuous process running from the Original Man (Anthropos) down into matter and back up, and reads that process as a symbol of the self: not a static form but a dynamic cycle in which an unconscious totality becomes conscious. Along the way he summarizes the empirical symbols of the self (circle, quaternity, stone, city, vessel, human figure, animals), argues that quaternity is an organizing schema "par excellence," ties the fourth term of every quaternity to a space-time quaternio and to a "psychological a priori," offers a pseudo-mathematical formula for the whole process, and ventures a comparison with nuclear physics and the principle he calls synchronicity. Chapter XV, "Conclusion," recapitulates the whole book: shadow, then anima/animus, then the self as a complexio oppositorum; the relativity of good and evil; anima/animus as the root of masculine and feminine divinities; and the Gnostics (with the alchemists) as the best source of natural symbols of the self.

[note: The chapter begins at the start of this range, so there is no carry-over from the previous part. Chapter XIII ("Gnostic Symbols of the Self," in Part 2) is the source of the Naassene/Moses and Paradise material that this chapter summarizes; Jung says the previous chapter's examples suffice to show the "progressive assimilation and amplification of the archetype that underlies ego-consciousness" [p.240, par.347].]

## CHAPTER XIV. THE STRUCTURE AND DYNAMICS OF THE SELF (pars. 347-421) [pp.240-287]

### 1. The Gnostics as psychologists; Indian parallels (pars. 347-350) [pp.240-241]

- Jung says hints in Hippolytus show "beyond a doubt" that many Gnostics were "nothing other than psychologists." [p.240]
- Hippolytus reports them as saying the soul is very hard to find and comprehend, and that knowledge of the whole man is as difficult. Quoted Gnostic formula: knowledge of man is the beginning of wholeness (*teleiosis*); knowledge of God is perfect wholeness. [p.240, par.347]
- Clement of Alexandria (*Paedagogus* III, 1): the greatest discipline is to know oneself; whoever knows himself knows God. [p.240]
- **Monoimos** ("the Arab"), letter to Theophrastus: seek him from out of yourself and learn who it is that takes possession of everything in you, saying "my god, my spirit, my understanding, my soul, my body"; learn whence come sorrow, joy, love and hate, waking, sleeping, anger and falling in love "though one would not." Investigate closely and "thou wilt find Him in thyself, the One and the Many, like to that little point." [p.240, par.347]
- Jung compares this to the Indian Self (brahman and atman): the Kena Upanishad passage (what directs the mind, breath, speech, eye and ear; "that which speech cannot express, by which speech is expressed ... know that as Brahman") and Yajnyavalkya's indirect definition in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (the inner controller who dwells in all beings, yet is apart from them; "your Self, the inner controller, the immortal"). [pp.240-241, pars.348-349]
- Claim: even in the second century the ego was understood as the exponent of an all-embracing totality, the self, an idea "by no means all psychologists are familiar with even today." Indian influence on Monoimos is "not impossible." [p.241, par.350]
- Claim: these insights, in the Near East as in India, come from intense introspective observation that "can only be psychological." Gnosis is "undoubtedly a psychological knowledge whose contents derive from the unconscious," reached by concentrating on the "subjective factor" (the demonstrable influence of the collective unconscious on the conscious mind). This would explain the "astonishing parallelism" between Gnostic symbolism and the findings of the psychology of the unconscious. [p.241, par.350]

### 2. Empirical symbols of wholeness: the summary list (pars. 351-357) [pp.241-244]

Jung says he will illustrate the parallelism by summarizing the symbols already discussed. First, the empirical facts that led psychologists to conjecture an archetype of wholeness, the self: dreams and visions, and products of active imagination in which wholeness symbols appear. [p.241, par.351]

**The geometric/number family** [p.242, par.351]

| Symbol family | Form and notes |
|---|---|
| Circle and sphere | Circular and spherical forms, either purely geometric or as objects |
| Quaternity | Quadratic figures divided into four or cross-shaped; also four objects or persons related in meaning or arrangement |
| Eight | A multiple of four; same significance |
| The 3 + 1 dilemma | A special variant of the quaternity motif (three functions plus one different fourth) |
| Twelve (3 x 4) | Seems a solution of the 3 + 1 dilemma and a symbol of wholeness (zodiac, year) |
| Three | A "relative totality": either a spiritual totality of thought (like the Trinity) or an instinctual, chthonic one (the "lower triad" of underworld gods). When context points to the self, three should be understood as a "defective quaternity" or stepping-stone toward it. Empirically, a triad has a trinity opposed to it as complement; the complement of the quaternity is unity. |

Footnote 7: the circle has the character of wholeness because of its "perfect" form; the quaternity because four is the minimum number of parts into which a circle can naturally be divided. Footnote 10: five corresponds to the indistinguishability of quaternity and unity. [p.242]

**Derived images** [pp.242-243, pars.352-353]
- From circle-and-quaternity come the geometrically formed crystal and the wonder-working stone, and by analogy the city, castle, church, house and vessel. Another variant is the wheel (*rota*).
- The container motif emphasizes the ego's containment in the greater dimension of the self; the wheel emphasizes rotation, which also appears as ritual circumambulation. Psychologically it means concentration on and preoccupation with a center, conceived as the center of a circle (formulated as a point). This leads easily to a relation to the heavenly Pole and the rotating star-bowl; a parallel is the horoscope as "wheel of birth." [p.242, par.352]
- The container leads to its content: the inhabitant of the city or house (related to the quaternity and to the "fifth" as unity of the four) and the water in the vessel. In modern dreams water appears as a blue expanse reflecting the sky, a lake, four rivers (Jung's examples: Switzerland as the heart of Europe with Rhine, Ticino, Rhone, Inn; the four rivers of Eden), healing or consecrated water; sometimes combined with fire as fire-water (wine, alcohol). [p.243, par.353]

**The human figure** [p.243, pars.354-355]
- The inhabitant of the quadratic space leads to the human figure, "apart from the geometrical and arithmetical symbols, the commonest symbol of the self": a god or godlike human, prince, priest, great man, historical personality, a dearly loved father, an admired example, the successful elder brother, a figure that transcends the dreamer's ego personality. There are corresponding feminine figures in women's psychology.
- As the circle is contrasted with the square, the quaternity is contrasted with the 3 + 1 motif, and the positive, lovable human figure with a negative, ugly, feared "daemonic, misbegotten creature."
- **The self is a "complexio oppositorum"** (Nicholas of Cusa's definition of God, per footnote 15): male and female, old man and child, powerful and helpless, large and small, "though this does not mean that it is anything like as contradictory in itself." The apparent paradox may only reflect the enantiodromian changes of the conscious attitude, which can have a favorable or unfavorable effect on the whole. The same holds for the unconscious generally: its frightening figures may be called forth by conscious fear of it.
- Caution in both directions: do not underrate consciousness (relate unconscious contradictions causally to the conscious attitude "at least in some degree"), and do not overrate it (there are too many "incontrovertible proofs of the autonomy of unconscious compensatory processes"). [p.243-244, par.355]
- **"Uncertainty relationship":** between conscious and unconscious there is a kind of uncertainty relationship, because the observer is inseparable from the observed and disturbs it by observing; exact observation of the unconscious prejudices observation of the conscious and vice versa. [p.244, par.355]

**Animals, plants, inorganic forms** [p.244, par.356]
- The self can appear in all shapes "from the highest to the lowest," insofar as they transcend the ego personality "in the manner of a daimonion."
- Most common animal images in modern dreams, in Jung's experience: elephant, horse, bull, bear, white and black birds, fishes, snakes; occasionally tortoises, snails, spiders, beetles. Plant symbols: flower and tree. Inorganic: mountain and lake.

**The phallus** [p.244, par.357]
- Where sexuality is undervalued (by ordinary repression, overt devaluation, or a purely biological interpretation in differentiated persons), the self is symbolized as a phallus, because such conceptions overlook the spiritual and "mystical" implications of the sexual instinct. One can expect an unconscious phallicism by way of compensation; Jung offers the "mainly sexualistic approach to the psyche" in Freud as a good example. (Footnote 16 cites Hurwitz on Hasidic mysticism.)

### 3. The Gnostic quaternios (pars. 358-370) [pp.244-251]

**Framing** [pp.244-245, par.358]
The Naassenes of Hippolytus emphasize human images; of the geometric/arithmetic symbols the most important are the quaternity, the ogdoad, the trinity and unity. Jung will treat two quaternios: the **Moses Quaternio** (from section 6 of the previous chapter) and the **Paradise Quaternio** (the four rivers of Paradise). They express roughly the same idea; he will relate them psychologically and to later alchemical quaternary structures, and ask how far they are characteristic of the Gnostic age and how far they correlate with "the archetypal history of the mind in the Christian aeon."

**The Moses Quaternio (par.359-361)** [pp.245-247]

Schema (diagram as Jung draws it; upper and lower poles are the Higher Adam and the Lower Adam):

| Pole | Figure |
|---|---|
| Top | The Higher Adam |
| Right, upper | Miriam: Mother-Sister-Anima |
| Left, upper | Jethro: physical and spiritual father |
| Right, lower | Zipporah: wife of Moses and daughter of Jethro |
| Left, lower | Moses |
| Bottom | The Lower Adam |

- Reading: the "lower Adam" is the ordinary mortal man; Moses is the culture-hero and lawgiver (on a personal level, the "father"); Zipporah, as daughter of a king and priest, the "higher mother." For the ordinary man these two are the "royal pair," which for Moses corresponds to his "higher man" (the spiritual, inner man, represented by Jethro) and his anima, Miriam. [p.246, par.360]
- Because Moses stands to Jethro as the lower Adam to Moses, the quaternio must be read also from the lower Adam's standpoint: Moses (culture-hero) / Zipporah (higher mother) over the Lower Adam / Eve (ordinary woman). [p.246]
- The Naassene structure is "unsymmetrical": it becomes a **senarius (hexad)** with an exclusively upward tendency, Jethro and Miriam added as "a kind of third storey," so there is a series of steps from lower to higher Adam. Jung says this psychology underlies the long lists of Valentinian syzygies. The lower Adam, or somatic man, is the lowest stage "from which there can be only an ascent." [p.246, par.361]
- The four persons were "chosen so skillfully" that they leave room for the incest motif (Jethro-Miriam), "never lacking in the marriage quaternio," and for extending the ordinary man's structure downward toward the sub-human, dark side, the shadow: Moses marries the "Ethiopian woman," and Miriam the prophetess and mother-sister becomes "leprous" after she "spoke against" Moses and stirred Aaron against him, which Jung takes as proof that her relation to Moses has turned negative. [p.246, par.361]

Resulting lower senarius [p.247]:

| | |
|---|---|
| The Lower Adam | Eve |
| Moses | Ethiopian woman |
| Jethro, the heathen priest | Miriam, the "white" leper |

- Jethro (a Midianite priest, who departs to his own country; also named Reguel, "friend of God"; helped Moses with superior wisdom) is a numinous personality, the "wise old man" who personifies spirit in myth and folklore. Spirit has a dichotomous nature, so Jethro must be included in the quaternio also as a "lower" aspect with a magical and nefarious significance, though "this is not vouched for in the text" (footnote 21). [p.247, par.362]
- The Moses Quaternio is an individual variant of the folklore **marriage quaternio**, based on the cross-cousin marriage schema: husband / cousin-as-wife, with husband's sister and wife's brother; the sister can be replaced by the mother and the brother by a father-like figure, but the incest motif remains characteristic. It is a primary schema for love relationships and for the transference, so, like all characterological schemata, it manifests in a "favorable" and "unfavorable" form, for the relationships have the same ambivalence. [p.247, par.363]
- The Gnostic names are accidental; the schema is "universally valid." The "Shadow Quaternio" keeps the same names only because Moses' biography offers features suited to illustrate the shadow. [p.248, par.364]

**The Anthropos Quaternio and the Shadow Quaternio (pars. 365-370)** [pp.248-251]

The lower senarius reaches its nadir not in the Lower Adam but in his dark, theriomorphic prefiguration: the serpent created before man, the Gnostic *Naas*. The two double pyramids [pp.248-249, diagrams]:

| A. The Anthropos Quaternio (above) | B. The Shadow Quaternio (below) |
|---|---|
| Anthropos (the higher Adam) at the apex | Man (the lower Adam) at the apex |
| The higher Jethro; the positive Miriam | The lower Jethro; the negative Miriam |
| The higher Moses; the wise Zipporah | The Ethiopian |
| Man (the lower Adam) at the base | Serpent at the base |

- Jung says this "is no idle parlour game": the texts show the Gnostics knew the dark side of their metaphysical figures well, so much so that they caused offense (examples: identifying the good God with Priapus per Hippolytus on Justin's gnosis; the Anthropos with the ithyphallic Hermes). Basilides discussed the problem of evil ("whence comes evil?"). The serpentine form of the Nous and the Agathodaimon does not mean the serpent has only a good aspect: compare the Apophis serpent as enemy of the Egyptian sun-god, and the devil, "that ancient serpent," as enemy of Christ, the "novus Sol." The good, spiritual God was opposed by an imperfect, vain demiurge; archontic powers gave mankind a corrupt "chirographum" (handwriting), from which Christ had to redeem them (Colossians 2:14; Priscillian per Orosius, footnote 25: the handwriting is imprinted on the body). [p.248, par.366]
- **Historical drift (par.367):** with the dawn of the second millennium the accent shifted more and more to the dark side. The demiurge became the devil who created the world; alchemy began to develop Mercurius, the partly material, partly immaterial spirit that penetrates and sustains all things. As a snake he dwells inside the earth, has body, soul and spirit, was believed to have human shape as homunculus or *homo altus*, and was regarded as the chthonic God. So the serpent is either a forerunner of man or a distant copy of the Anthropos, justifying the equation **Naas = Nous = Logos = Christ = Higher Adam**. The medieval extension toward the dark side was prepared by Gnostic phallicism (Codex Ashburnham 1166, 15th century; Mercurius identified with Hermes Kyllenios in the 16th). [p.250]
- **Alchemy and modern science (par.368):** Gnostic philosophy continued in alchemy ("Mater Alchimia" is one of the mothers of modern science). Science has given unparalleled knowledge of the "dark" side of matter, penetrated physiology and evolution, and made the roots of life an object of investigation; the human mind has sunk into the sublunary world, repeating the Gnostic myth of the Nous who, seeing his reflection in the depths, plunged and was swallowed in Physis's embrace. Jung's historical sketch of the "climax": the French Revolution in the eighteenth century, scientific materialism in the nineteenth, and in the twentieth political and social "realism," which "has turned the wheel of history back a full two thousand years," with despotism, lack of individual rights, cruelty and slavery of the pre-Christian world (solved its "labor problem" by the *ergastulum*, the convict camp). "The 'transvaluation of all values' is being enacted before our eyes." [pp.250-251]
- **The two pyramids as worlds (par.369):** for the medieval and late-antique mind the Anthropos Quaternio represents the world of spirit or metaphysics; the Shadow Quaternio represents sublunary nature and man's instinctual disposition, the "flesh," rooted in the animal kingdom, specifically warm-blooded animals. The nadir is the cold-blooded vertebrate, the snake (synonymous with the dragon), because with the snake the "psychic rapport" possible with warm-blooded animals ends. That the snake is a counterpart of the Anthropos is confirmed by its being both a well-known allegory of Christ and "the most spiritual animal" (Greek, as Jung quotes it). Hippolytus says the Gnostics identified the serpent with the spinal cord and medulla, which are synonymous with reflex functions. [p.251]
- **The shadow defined; the snake as unconscious (par.370):** "By 'shadow' I mean the inferior personality, the lowest levels of which are indistinguishable from the instinctuality of an animal." Early forerunners: the "excrescent soul" (*prosartema*/appendage) of Isidorus, the son of Basilides, and Valentinus's spirits indwelling in man (animal souls of wolves, monkeys, lions); Origen on the animals contained in man (footnotes 32-34). Because the shadow is itself unconscious for most people, the snake corresponds to what is totally unconscious and incapable of becoming conscious, but which, as collective unconscious and instinct, seems to possess a peculiar wisdom and a knowledge often felt as supernatural. This is the treasure the snake or dragon guards, and why the snake signifies both evil/darkness and wisdom. Its "unrelatedness, coldness, and dangerousness" express an instinctuality that rides roughshod over moral and human considerations, and is therefore both terrifying and fascinating. [pp.251-252]

### 4. Mercurius, the serpent and the Paradise Quaternio (pars. 371-380) [pp.252-260]

**The serpent of Mercurius (par.371)** [p.252]
- In alchemy the snake is the symbol of Mercurius non vulgi, bracketed with the revelation god Hermes; both have a pneumatic nature. The *serpens Mercurii* is a chthonic spirit dwelling in matter, specifically in the bit of original chaos hidden in creation, the *massa confusa* or *globosa*.
- Since the *opus* was understood as a recapitulation or imitation of the creation of the world, the Mercurial serpent reminded alchemists of the Eden serpent and of the devil, who by their own admission "played all sorts of tricks" on them. Goethe's Mephistopheles ("whose aunt is the snake") is a version of the alchemical familiar Mercurius. Like the dragon, Mercurius is slippery, evasive, poisonous, dangerous, the forerunner of the hermaphrodite, "and for that reason he has to be overcome."

**Paradise (pars. 372-374)** [pp.252-254]
- For the Naassenes Paradise was a quaternity parallel with the Moses Quaternio and of similar meaning: its four rivers, Pison, Gihon, Hiddekel, Phrat (Euphrates). The Genesis serpent is the personified tree-numen (traditionally coiled round the tree; Luther's version of its persuasion is quoted). In the fairy tale "The Spirit in the Bottle," Mercurius can likewise be read as a tree-numen; in Ripley's "Scrowle" Mercurius appears as a Melusina-like snake descending from the Philosophical Tree. The tree stands for the phases of the transformation process; its fruit or flowers for the consummation. In the fairy tale Mercurius is hidden in the roots of an oak, i.e., in the earth. [p.253, par.372]
- For alchemists Paradise was a favorite symbol of the *albedo*, the regained state of innocence; the source of its rivers symbolizes the *aqua permanens*. For the Church Fathers Christ is this source, and Paradise means the ground of the soul from which the fourfold river of the Logos wells up. The same symbol is found in the mystic-alchemist John Pordage (divine Wisdom as the "New Earth, the heavenly Land" from which grew the Trees of Life). [p.253, par.373]
- The snake symbol leads to images of Paradise, tree and earth: "an evolutionary regression from the animal kingdom back to plants and inorganic nature," epitomized in alchemy by the secret of matter, the lapis, here understood not as the end product but as the **initial material** (the arcane substance also called lapis). [p.254, par.374]

**C. The Paradise Quaternio** [p.254, diagram]

| Position | Content |
|---|---|
| Top | Serpent |
| Four sides | Pison, Gihon, Hiddekel, Euphrates |
| Bottom | Lapis |

**The lapis as unity made of four (pars. 375-377)** [pp.254-256]
- The lapis is thought of as a unity and often stands for the *prima materia* in general. But the prima materia is a bit of the original chaos hidden in metals, particularly mercury, and is not simple ("massa confusa"); so too the lapis consists of the four elements, or must be put together from them. In chaos the elements are merely coexistent, even hostile, in "an original state of conflict and mutual repulsion," illustrating the unfolding of original unity into the multiplicity of the visible world. Out of the split quaternity the opus assembles the unity of the lapis in the inorganic realm. [pp.254-255, par.375]
- As *filius macrocosmi* and a living being, the lapis is not just an allegory but "a direct parallel of Christ and the higher Adam, of the heavenly Original Man, of the second Adam (Christ), and of the serpent." The nadir of this third quaternio is thus a further counterpart of the Anthropos. [p.255, par.375]
- The lapis rests on the union of four elements, which unfold the unknowable inchoate state, chaos: the prima materia, arcanum, "increatum" in Paracelsus and followers, regarded as coeternal with God (Jung reads it as a correct interpretation of the Tehom in Genesis 1:2). This primary substance is round (*massa globosa*, *rotundum*), like the world and world-soul; indeed "the world-soul and the world-substance in one." It is the "stone that has a spirit" (Ostanes in Zosimos); "in modern parlance the most elementary building-stone in the architecture of matter, the atom, which is an intellectual model." Alchemists call this "round element" now primal water, now primal fire, pneuma, primal earth, or *corpusculum nostrae sapientiae*. As water or fire it is the universal solvent; as stone and metal it must be dissolved and changed into air (pneuma, spirit). [pp.255-256, par.376]
- Footnote 47: Mylius equates the elements of the lapis with corpus, spiritus and anima (corpus = matter/earth; spiritus = the bond of soul and body = fire; water and air, which would belong to anima, are also "spirit"). Three elements are "moving," one (earth) "unmoving."

**D. The Lapis Quaternio** [p.256, par.377, diagram]
Lapis at top; Earth at one side; Rotundum at the bottom (the diagram's other points are not legible in the text). Zosimos calls the rotundum the omega element, which probably signifies the head; the skull is mentioned as the vessel of transformation in the "Platonis liber quartorum," and the "Philosophers" styled themselves "children of the golden head" (probably synonymous with *filii sapientiae*). The vessel is often synonymous with the lapis: the same arcanum. [pp.256-257]

**The round vessel (pars. 377-380)** [pp.256-260]
- By the old view the soul is round, so the vessel must be round like the heavens; the Original Man's form is round. Dorn: the vessel should be made "from a kind of squaring of the circle" so that spirit and soul of the material, separated from the body, raise the body to their heaven. The anonymous scholiast on the "Tractatus aureus" depicts a square whose corners are the four elements with a small circle at the center: "Reduce your stone to the four elements, rectify and combine them into one"; the One is the little circle, the mediator making peace between the enemy elements. [p.257, par.377]
- Footnote 58: the Pelican (a distilling vessel whose distillate runs back into the retort) could illustrate the process of conscious realization and the reapplication of conscious insights to the unconscious. [p.257]
- The author's lettered vessel diagram is read by Jung as "nothing else but a mandala, symbolizing the self or the higher Adam with his four emanations (like Horus with his four sons)"; the author calls it "Septenarius magicus occultus" (counting the letters F and G, Above and Below). Maria the Prophetess: the Hermetic vessel is divine, hidden from the Gentiles; "it is no nigromantic vessel, but is the measure of thy fire." Hoghelande, citing Senior: the vision of it is more to be sought than the Scriptures. [p.258, par.378]
- Philalethes sums up the synonyms of Mercurius: key to the art, the "two-edged sword" in the cherub's hand, and "our true, hidden vessel, the Philosophic garden, wherein our Sun rises and sets." Rupescissa's advice to make a vessel "after the manner of a cherub," with six wings and a round head, filled with burning water, is read by Jung as an allusion to Ezekiel's vision, whose horizontal section is a four-part mandala, equivalent to the squaring of the circle. [p.259, par.379]
- Jung's psychological translation of the recipe: "make the Hermetic vessel out of your psychic wholeness and pour into it the aqua permanens, or aqua doctrinae" (synonym: *vinum ardens*, Rupescissa's "burning water"), a hint that the adept should "inwardly digest" and be transformed by the alchemical doctrine. [p.259]
- The *Aurora consurgens* (Part II): the *vas naturale* as matrix, "the One in which there are three things, water, air and fire," three glass alembics in which the son of the Philosophers is begotten; Jung reads three alembics as allusion to the Trinity (supported by a 1588 Pandora illustration with Christ bleeding from the lance wound beside three alembics). [pp.259-260, par.380]
- Conclusion on the vessel: the round Hermetic vessel is "God himself, the (Platonic) world-soul and man's own wholeness"; another counterpart of the Anthropos and "the universe in its smallest and most material form." Jung adds that early atom models took the planetary system as prototype. [p.260]

### 5. Quaternity as an organizing schema; the marriage quaternio (pars. 381-383) [pp.260-262]

- **The quaternity is "an organizing schema par excellence, something like the crossed threads in a telescope"**: a system of coordinates used almost instinctively to divide a chaotic multiplicity (the earth's surface, the year, groups of individuals, moon phases, temperaments, elements, alchemical colors). A quaternio among the Gnostics is therefore a more or less conscious attempt to organize the "chaotic medley of numinous images" pouring in on them. [p.260, par.381]
- The form it took derives from the primitive cross-cousin marriage, i.e., the **marriage quaternio**. It differs from the primitive form in that the sister-exchange marriage has shed its biological character: the sister's husband is no longer the wife's brother but another close relative (the wife's father in the Moses Quaternio) or even a stranger. The lost cousin/brother attribute is compensated by magical qualities (higher rank, magical powers); that is, an **anima-animus projection** takes place. Jung calls this "a great cultural advance," because projection means the unconscious is constellated in the husband-wife relationship; marriage is "beginning to turn into a conscious relationship" rather than mere biological and social coexistence. [pp.260-261, par.381]
- Mechanism claimed: this happens when cross-cousin marriage becomes obsolete through further differentiation of marriage classes into six-, eight- or twelve-class systems; the activation of the unconscious comes from the regression of the endogamous tendency, the "kinship libido," which can no longer find adequate satisfaction because the marriage partner grows increasingly strange. [p.261, par.381]
- The Gnostics used two compensatory quaternities (marriage quaternio and Paradise's rivers) to organize their symbols. This matches experience in active imagination and chaotic psychic states, where quaternity symbols appear and "signify stabilization through order as opposed to the instability caused by chaos" and "have a compensatory meaning." (Triadic symbols also occur, but are rarer; footnote 70.) [p.261, par.382]
- Use of the four quaternios: first an attempt to arrange the limitless symbols of Gnosticism and its continuation, alchemy; but also useful for understanding individual symbolism in modern dreams, where images are "even more varied" and an organizing schema is "absolutely essential." Jung starts historically from the Moses Quaternio because it derives directly from the cross-cousin schema; it is only "paradigmatic." Any other marriage quaternio would do, but not any other quaternity, such as Horus and his four sons, which "is not aboriginal enough, for it misses out the antagonistic, feminine element." Footnote 71: the Gnostic quaternio is later in time than the Horus quaternity but psychologically older, because in it "the feminine element reassumes its rightful place." [p.261, par.383]
- It is "most important that just the extreme opposites, masculine-feminine and so on, should appear linked together"; hence the alchemical pairs of opposites are linked in quaternities (warm-cold, dry-moist). Applied to the Moses Quaternio: Jethro (father) and Zipporah (daughter) as one axis; Moses (brother) and Miriam (sister) as the other. [p.262, par.383]

### 6. Why a second pyramid; symmetry of the series (pars. 384-389) [pp.262-264]

- The Anthropos Quaternio corresponds to the Gnostic model; the second (Shadow) is a psychological construction derived from it, from data in the Biblical text the Gnostics used. The second must be the "shadow" of the first because the lower Adam, the mortal man, has a chthonic psyche and is not adequately expressed by a quaternity supraordinate to him; he would be an unsymmetrical figure, just as the higher Adam is unsymmetrical and has to be complemented by a subordinate quaternity. [p.262, par.384]
- The symmetrical complement of the lower Adam is the serpent: (a) by the well-known association of Adam with the snake, "his chthonic daemon, his familiar spirit"; (b) because the snake is the commonest symbol for the dark, chthonic world of instinct, and may be replaced by an equivalent cold-blooded animal (dragon, crocodile, fish). [p.262, par.385]
- The snake is not merely nefarious: it is also a symbol of wisdom and hence of light, goodness and healing (Aesculapian and Agathodaimon serpent). Even in the New Testament it is at once an allegory of Christ and of the devil, "just as we have seen that the fish was." The dragon, negative for the West, is positive in China and sometimes in Western alchemy. The inner polarity of the snake-symbol "far exceeds that of man. It is overt, whereas man's is partly latent." The serpent surpasses Adam in cleverness and knowledge, is older than he, and is "equipped by God with a superhuman intelligence." [p.263, par.385]
- Man culminates above in a "light" and good God and rests below on a dark and evil principle, traditionally the devil or the serpent personifying Adam's disobedience. Just as man was symmetrized by the serpent, the serpent has its complement in the Paradise Quaternio. Paradise is the world of plants and animals, "a plantation or garden enlivened by animals." As *serpens mercurialis* the snake is a vegetation numen, calling forth "blessed greenness" (quote from the *Rosarium*, footnote 74, on greenness as the generation of the thing), and dwells in the interior of the earth as the pneuma hidden in the stone. [p.263, par.386]
- The symmetrical complement of the serpent is the stone as representative of the earth: a later, alchemical stage whose central idea is the lapis. The lapis is the lower opposite of the serpent, and yet corresponds to man, because it is represented in human form, has "body, soul, and spirit," is a homunculus and, as the texts show, a symbol of the self. It is "not a human ego but a collective entity, a collective soul, like the Indian *hiranyagarbha*, 'golden seed.'" It is the "father-mother" of the metals, an hermaphrodite: "an ultimate unity," not an elementary but a composite unity that has evolved. [pp.263-264, par.387]
- The choice of symbol is documented in alchemical literature "from the first to the eighteenth century." The lapis is produced from the splitting and recombination of the four elements, from the rotundum; the rotundum is "a highly abstract, transcendent idea" that by roundness and wholeness refers to the Original Man, the Anthropos. Footnote 76: roundness is "a hint that rotation may be a principle of matter." [p.264, par.388]
- **The uroboros (par.389):** the four double pyramids arrange themselves in a circle forming the uroboros; as fifth stage the rotundum is identical with the first: the heavy darkness of earth and metal has a secret relationship to the Anthropos. Parallels: in religious history metals grow from Gayomart's blood (and Yima's body; Gayomart's body consisted of metals, per footnotes 77). This is explained by the identity of the lowest, most material with the highest and most spiritual, as with the serpent as chthonic and "most spiritual" animal. In Plato the rotundum is the world-soul and a "blessed God." [p.264]

### 7. The vertical schema and the double nature of the serpent (pars. 390-391) [pp.265-267]

Condensed vertical schema: **Anthropos** at top; **Christus** (left) and **Diabolus** (right) on either side at the middle; **Rotundum** at the bottom [p.265, par.390; the middle of the diagram is partly garbled in the source: "Christus ... Diabolus" is legible].
- Jung emphasizes the point of greatest tension between opposites: the double significance of the **serpent**, which occupies the center of the system. As allegory of both Christ and devil it contains and symbolizes "the strongest polarity into which the Anthropos falls when he descends into Physis." The ordinary man has not reached this tension consciously; he has it only in the unconscious, i.e., in the serpent. [p.265]
- In the **lapis**, the counterpart of man, the opposites are united "with a visible seam or suture as in the symbol of the hermaphrodite," a flaw as the all-too-human element mars *Homo sapiens*. In the higher Adam and the rotundum the opposition is invisible; presumably the one stands in absolute opposition to the other, and if both are identical as indistinguishable transcendental entities, "this is one of those paradoxes that are the rule: a statement about something metaphysical can only be antinomial." [pp.265-266]
- **Footnote 79 (mass psychology, a notable aside):** most people lack the range of consciousness to become aware of the opposites in human nature; the tensions remain unconscious but can appear in dreams. The snake traditionally stands for man's vulnerable spot, personifying his shadow, his weakness and unconsciousness. "The greatest danger about unconsciousness is proneness to suggestion"; the effect of suggestion is due to release of an unconscious dynamic, the more effective the more unconscious. Hence the widening split between conscious and unconscious increases the danger of psychic infection and mass psychosis. With loss of symbolic ideas "the bridge to the unconscious has broken down. Instinct no longer affords protection against unsound ideas and empty slogans. Rationality without tradition and without a basis in instinct is proof against no absurdity." [pp.265-266]
- **The uroboros arrangement (par.391)** [p.266]: Anthropos-Rotundum at the top; Lapis (left) and Homo (right) at the sides; Serpens at the bottom. This shows the stronger tension between anthropos-rotundum and serpens, and the lesser tension between homo and lapis, expressed by the distance of the points; arrows indicate the descent into Physis and the ascent toward the spiritual; the lowest point is the serpent. The lapis, though decidedly material, is also a spiritual symbol, while the rotundum is a transcendent entity symbolized by the secret of matter and comparable to the concept of the atom. This antinomial development of concepts matches the "paradoxical nature of alchemy." [p.267]

### 8. Maier's four fires; alchemy and the idea of energy (pars. 392-395) [pp.267-271]

- The lapis quaternity brings in the physical speculations of alchemy. In Michael Maier's *Scrutinium chymicum* (1687) a picture shows the four elements as four different stages of fire (Plate I, an image page). Maier's verses: four spheres filled with fire; the lowest corresponds to Vulcan, the second to Mercurius, the third to the moon (Luna), the fourth to Apollo (the sun). [p.267, par.392-393]
- Jung's reading: lowest sphere = Vulcan, earthly fire; second = Mercurius, the vegetative life-spirit; third = moon, the female, psychic principle; fourth = sun, the male, spiritual principle. Maier is concerned with the four elements and with four kinds of fire responsible for different states of aggregation. [p.267, par.393]

Series table as Jung gives it [p.268]:

| Maier series | Corresponds to | Planetary/divine | Element | State of aggregation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ignis terreus (earthly fire) | Sulfura et Mercurii | Sun (Apollo) | earth | solid |
| ignis aqueus | aquae | Moon (Luna) | water | liquid |
| ignis aerius | dracones | Mercurius | air | gas |
| ignis elementalis | ignis elementalis re et nomine (ordinary fire) | Vulcan | fire | flame |

Vigenere and Ripley series (as printed): *ignis mundi intelligibilis* = *ignis naturalis* ("is present in everything"); *ignis caelestis* = *ignis innaturalis* ("the heat of ashes and baths"); *ignis elementaris* = *ignis contra naturam* ("tortures bodies, is the dragon"); *ignis infernalis* = *ignis elementalis*. Vigenere (footnote 81): the intelligible fire is all light; the heavenly fire has heat and light; the elemental less light, heat and glow; the infernal fire "of heat and burning without any light." [p.268; the alignment of the two columns is garbled in the source, so the pairing above follows the printed order only.]

- Jung says paralleling states of aggregation with kinds of fire amounts to "a kind of phlogiston theory": fire is common to all states of aggregation and responsible for their constitution. The idea is old (oldest source Heraclitus; Turba, Dardaris: "The sulphurs are four souls hidden in the four elements"), expressing that elements or states can be reduced to a common denominator. Today, Jung says, that common factor is molecular movement, corresponding to a quantum of energy: so "the common denominator of the elements is energy." Stahl's phlogiston theory (a stepping-stone to the modern concept of energy) rests on the alchemical premises; "we can see in them, therefore, the earliest beginnings of a theory of energy." (Footnote 88: the primitive idea of mana is much older; the sulphur = anima equation still holds a trace of the original mana theory; mana was misunderstood as animism.) [pp.268-271, par.394-395]
- All mathematical and physical elements from which a theory of energy could be built were known in the seventeenth century. Energy is an abstract concept indispensable for exact description of moving bodies; likewise bodies in motion can only be apprehended through the **space-time coordinate system**. [p.271, par.395]

### 9. The fourth as the "Other"; the space-time quaternio (pars. 396-399) [pp.271-273]

- Movement is always established by the **space-time quaternio**, expressible by the axiom of Maria, **3 + 1**, or the sesquitertian proportion 3 : 4. It could replace the quaternio of four elements, where the unit corresponding to the time coordinate, or "the fourth in the alchemical series of elements," is characterized by one element having an exceptional position, like fire or earth (footnote 89: fire spiritual, others material; earth unmoving, others moving). [p.271, par.395-396]
- The exceptional position of one factor can also appear as its **duplex nature**: the fourth river of Paradise, the Euphrates, means both the mouth through which food enters and prayers leave and the Logos; in the Moses Quaternio the wife plays the double role of Zipporah and the Ethiopian woman. A quaternity built from the divine equivalents of Maier's four elements (Apollo, Luna, Mercurius, Vulcan) gives a marriage quaternio with a brother-sister relationship: Apollo and Luna as the pair, Vulcan and **Mercurius duplex** as the other. Mercurius in alchemy is male-female and often a virgin too. [pp.271-272, par.396]
- Space-time quaternio diagram: Height, Depth, Time (and a fourth point). [p.272] Reading both ways: if space is three-dimensional, time is the fourth dimension; if time is viewed by its three qualities (past, present, future), static space must be added as the fourth term. "In both cases, the fourth represents an incommensurable Other that is needed for their mutual determination. Thus we measure space by time and time by space." [p.272, par.397]
- **Table of the "fourth/Other" across the book's quaternities** [pp.272-273, par.397]:

| Context | The incommensurable fourth |
|---|---|
| Gnostic quaternities | The fiery god, "the fourth by number" |
| Moses Quaternio | The dual wife (Zipporah and the Ethiopian woman) |
| Paradise rivers | The dual Euphrates (river and Logos) |
| Alchemical elements | Fire (Boehme calls the "fire of Nature" the "fourth form," footnote 90) |
| Maier's gods | Mercurius duplex |
| "Christian Quaternity" (Jung's phrase: "if such an expression be permitted") | Mary, or the devil; the two incompatible figures are united in Mercurius duplex |

- Footnote 91: Sabellius's doctrine (early 2nd century) of a preworldly Monad and its three prosopa "bequeathed to posterity the first beginnings of a quaternary view of the Deity"; Joachim of Flora's charge against Peter Lombard of asserting "not Trinity, but Quaternity," three persons plus the common essence as a fourth (Fourth Lateran Council, 1215). [p.273]
- **The space-time quaternio is "the archetypal sine qua non for any apprehension of the physical world,"** the organizing schema par excellence among psychic quaternities, and corresponds in structure to the psychological schema of the functions: three relatively differentiated functions plus one undifferentiated "inferior" function (footnote 93). The 3 : 1 proportion often occurs in dreams and spontaneous mandala drawings. [p.273, par.398]
- A modern parallel to the stacked quaternities with ascent and descent appears in Jung's "mandala pictures" paper (vibrations forming "nodes," each an outstanding personality) and possibly in the Trinity illustration from Joachim of Flora (Plate II, an image page of manuscript script, unreadable in the text). [p.273, par.399]

### 10. Pseudo-Clement's creation theory (pars. 400-401) [p.274]

- The Clementine Homilies: in God, pneuma and soma are one; separated, pneuma appears as the Son and "archon of the future Aeon," while soma divides into four, corresponding to the four elements (always invoked at initiations). From the mixing of the four parts arose the devil, "archon of this Aeon," and the psyche of this world; soma had become psychized. "God rules this world as much through the devil as through the Son, for both are in his hands." God unfolds in syzygies (heaven/earth, day/night, male/female ...), the last being Adam/Eve; at the end of the fragmentation there follows a return to the beginning, the consummation of the universe through purification and annihilation. [p.274, par.400]
- Jung finds it strikingly like the basic conceptions of the alchemists, "if we disregard its moral aspects": hostile brothers Christ and the devil; the tetrameria; paired opposites and ultimate unity; lapis and Mercurius as parallels of Christ and (through snake/dragon symbolism) the devil; Mercurius duplex and the lapis uniting opposites indivisibly. [p.274, par.401]

### 11. Historical reading of the four quaternios (pars. 402-406) [pp.274-279]

- Recap: two Gnostic quaternities, one supraordinate and one subordinate to man (the "Positive Moses" or Anthropos Quaternio, and the Paradise Quaternio). "Paradise" here means the earthly Garden of Eden, not "future heaven" or the Abode of the Blessed (footnote 99). It is probably no accident, Jung says, that Hippolytus mentions precisely these two; the Naassenes knew only these. [pp.274, 277, par.402]
- In that system man is closely connected with the higher Adam but separated from the chthonic world of plants and animals; he relates to the serpent only through his shadow. This is "altogether characteristic of the age of Gnosticism and early Christianity": man was close to the "kingless race," the upper quaternity, the kingdom of heaven, and "looked upward." But "what begins above does not rise higher, but ends below." [p.277, par.402]
- Why Jung inserts the Shadow Quaternio unmentioned in the texts: "we can no longer conceive of a psyche that is oriented exclusively upwards and that is not balanced by an equally strong consciousness of the lower man." This is "a specifically modern state of affairs" and in Gnostic terms "an obnoxious anachronism" that puts man in the center of the field of consciousness where he had never stood consciously. Only through Christ could he see consciousness mediating between God and the world; by devotion to Christ's person he gradually acquired Christ's position as mediator. Through "the Christ crucified between the two thieves" man gradually attained knowledge of his shadow and its duality, already anticipated in the serpent's double meaning: one thief destined upward, one downward; the shadow is on one side "regrettable and reprehensible weakness," on the other "healthy instinctivity and the prerequisite for higher consciousness." [p.277, par.402]
- **Psychohistorical account (par.403)** [pp.277-278]: the Shadow Quaternio "only falls into place" when man's position as mediator has become real enough that he feels his consciousness of himself more strongly than his dependence on and governance by God. Man's original dependence on a pneumatic sphere (clung to "like a child to its mother") was threatened by Satan's kingdom; the Redeemer broke the gates of hell and deceived the archons; but man was thus bound to the kingdom of heaven to the same degree and separated from evil by an abyss, powerfully reinforced by the expectation of the Second Coming. **When Christ did not reappear,** a regression was to be expected: when great hope is dashed "the libido perforce flows back into man and heightens his consciousness of himself by accentuating his personal psychic processes"; he moves into the center of his field of consciousness, separates from the pneumatic sphere, approaches the shadow. Moral consciousness sharpens, the feeling of redemption is relativized, the Church exalts the power of her ritual to limit "the inrush of reality" and thereby becomes "a kingdom of this world." The transition from Anthropos to Shadow Quaternio illustrates a development leading, in the eleventh century, to wide recognition of the evil principle as world creator.
- **The serpent as turning point (par.404):** the Paradise Quaternio with the lapis brings us to the beginnings of natural science (Roger Bacon, Albertus Magnus, the alchemists), whose trend differs from the pneumatic "not by 180 but only by 90 degrees": it cuts across the Church's spiritual attitude, "more an embarrassment for faith than a contradiction of it." [p.278]
- **From lapis to physics (pars. 405-406)** [pp.278-279]: from the lapis the line leads directly to the alchemical quaternio of states of aggregation, which rests on the space-time quaternio, itself an archetypal quaternity and "a psychological a priori," an aspect of the archetypal quaternity indispensable for acquiring knowledge of physical processes. The development from Shadow to Lapis Quaternio illustrates the change of man's world picture during the second millennium; the series ends with the rotundum, or rotation, contrasted with the static quality of the quaternity. The rise of scientific materialism appears as both a logical consequence and a "deification of matter," psychologically because the rotundum coincides with the Anthropos archetype. "With this insight the ring of the uroboros closes." [p.279]

### 12. The formula of the quaternio series (pars. 407-411) [pp.279-282]

- Jung expresses the series as an equation: A = initial state (the Anthropos), D = end state (or the last state), B and C intermediates; the formations splitting off from each are a, b, c, d (small letters). Assumptions (legitimate "in a psychological formula"): one and the same substance continually transforms; each state brings forth its like (A produces a, B produces b; b produces B and c produces C); a is followed by b; the formula runs left to right. [p.279, par.407]
- It cannot be arranged linearly, only in a circle moving **to the right** (with the sun): a process of becoming conscious, "as is already indicated by the splitting (discrimination) of A B C D each time into four qualitatively discrete units" (footnote 100: corresponding to the *phylokrinesis*). The right is "ruled by conscious reason" (upright, downright, forthright); the left is the side of the heart and emotions, "where one is affected by the unconscious" (footnote 104). [pp.279-280, par.408]
- Modern science rests on a trinity of principles (space, time, causality), not a quaternity (not counting the space-time continuum of physics, footnote 101). The classical and medieval view, up to Leibniz, recognized the **principle of correspondence**, applied "naively and unreflectingly." To give a judgment on the whole (A to D) the character of wholeness, our time-conditioned thinking must be supplemented by the principle of correspondence "or, as I have called it, **synchronicity**" because our description of nature is incomplete in some respects and either excludes observable facts or formulates them negatively, as in the paradox of "an effect without a cause." The Gnostic quaternity is a naive product of the unconscious and represents a psychic fact that can be related to the **four orienting functions of consciousness**. [p.280, par.409]
- **Closing of the cycle (par.410):** the cycle returns to its beginning when D, the state furthest from A, changes into a3 by a kind of **enantiodromia**. The diagram (partly garbled in the source) arranges A=a with d, c, b around it, then D, then B, then C=c, and so on [p.281]. Jung's claims about what the formula captures:
  - the rotation of the mandala;
  - the antithetical play of complementary or compensatory processes;
  - the **apocatastasis**, the restoration of an original state of wholeness (the alchemists' uroboros);
  - the ancient alchemical *tetrameria*, implicit in the fourfold structure of unity.
- What the formula can only hint at: the higher plane reached through transformation and integration. "Sublimation" consists in an unfolding of totality into four parts four times, "which means nothing less than its becoming conscious." Contents split into four aspects have been subjected to discrimination by the four orienting functions; only the production of four aspects makes a total description possible. The Anthropos A descends from above through his Shadow B into Physis C (= serpent) and, through a kind of crystallization process D (= lapis) reducing chaos to order, rises again to the original state, now transformed from unconscious into conscious. Consciousness arises by discrimination: analysis (dissolution) followed by synthesis: *solve et coagula*. [pp.281-282, par.410]
- The correspondence is shown by identity of the letters (a, a1, a2, a3): the same factor throughout, changing place while its name and quality change. The change of place is always an **enantiodromian change of situation**, corresponding to compensatory changes in the psyche as a whole, "in this way the changing of the hexagrams in the I Ching was understood by the classical Chinese commentators." Each archetypal arrangement has its own numinosity, evident in the names Jung gives it: a to d = "kingless race"; a1 to d1 = Shadow Quaternio ("annoying," the all-too-human human being, Nietzsche's "Ugliest Man"); a2 to d2 = "Paradise" ("which speaks for itself"); a3 to d3 = the world of matter, whose numinosity in the shape of materialism "threatens to suffocate our world." [p.282]
- **The formula as symbol of the self (par.411):** the self is "not just a static quantity or constant form, but also a dynamic process"; likewise the ancients saw the *imago Dei* in man not as a lifeless imprint but as an active force. The four transformations represent a process of restoration or rejuvenation inside the self, compared to the **carbon-nitrogen cycle in the sun** (a carbon nucleus captures four protons, two becoming neutrons, and releases them as an alpha particle; the carbon nucleus emerges unchanged "like the Phoenix from the ashes," quoting Gamow). The secret of existence (of atom and components) may consist in a continually repeated process of rejuvenation, and one comes to similar conclusions in trying to account for the numinosity of the archetypes. [pp.282-283]

### 13. Nuclear physics, psyche and matter (pars. 412-414) [p.283]

- Jung admits the "extremely hypothetical nature of this comparison" but holds it appropriate "even at the risk of being deceived by appearances": nuclear physics and the psychology of the unconscious "will draw closer together as both of them, independently of one another and from opposite directions, push forward into transcendental territory, the one with the concept of the atom, the other with that of the archetype." [p.283, par.412]
- The analogy is not a digression because the symbolic schema itself "represents the descent into matter and requires the identity of the outside with the inside." Key passage: "Psyche cannot be totally different from matter, for how otherwise could it move matter? And matter cannot be alien to psyche, for how else could matter produce psyche?" Psyche and matter exist in one and the same world, each partaking of the other; research should reach an ultimate agreement between physical and psychological concepts. Evidence: mathematics' purely logical constructions have more than once coincided with the behavior of things, which, "like the events I call synchronistic, points to a profound harmony between all forms of existence." [p.283, par.413]
- **Analogy formation** is a law largely governing psychic life; thus the speculative construction is probably prefigured on earlier levels of thought: in the multifarious stages of the mystic transformation process, the degrees of initiation into the mysteries, and the classical/Christian trichotomy of pneumatic, psychic and hylic. [p.283, par.414]

### 14. Prefigurations: the Liber quartorum, geomancy, Kircher (pars. 414-418) [pp.283-286]

**The sixteenfold schema of the "Platonis liber quartorum"** (anonymous Harranite treatise, supposedly translated from Arabic in the 12th century; Theatr. chem. V). Four first principles each with three stages (together sixteen parts), and each stage has a correspondence in the vertical series: [pp.283-284, par.414]

| | I | II | III | IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opus naturalium (work of nature) | Aqua (water) | Naturae compositae (composite natures) | Sensus (the senses) |
| 2 | Divisio naturae | Terra (earth) | Naturae discretae | Discretio intellectualis |
| 3 | Anima | Aer (air) | Simplicia | Ratio |
| 4 | Intellectus | Ignis (fire) | Aetheris simplicioris | Arcanum |

- The table shows aspects of the *opus alchemicum*, which was also bound up with astrology and the "necromantic arts," evident in the use of significant numbers and invoking the familiar spirit. [p.284, par.415]
- **Geomancy:** the age-old art rests on a sixteen-part schema: four central figures (Sub- or Superiudex, Iudex, two Testes), four *nepotes* (grandsons), four sons, four mothers (written right to left), arranged in a schema of astrological houses, with the horoscope's empty center replaced by a square holding the four central figures. [p.284, par.415]
- **Athanasius Kircher's quaternity system** (*Arithmologia*, 1665) [pp.284-285, par.416]:

| Monad | Number | Names |
|---|---|---|
| I (Unum) | 1 | Monas monadike = God = Root of all things = simplest understanding = Divine Essence = Divine Exemplar |
| II | 10 (1+2+3+4) | Second Monad = dekadike = duality = intellectual (spiritual) world = intelligence of the angels = composition of the One and the Other, i.e., from opposites |
| III | 10 squared = 100 | Third Monad = hekatontadike = soul = intelligence |
| IV | 10 cubed = 1000 | Fourth Monad = chiliadike = all concrete things = body = final and concrete unfolding of unities |

  Kircher: senses affect only the body; the first three unities are objects of understanding; everything perceived by the senses must be raised to reason, intelligence, and absolute unity, where "in a perfect cycle is found the beginning and the consummation." Using the Stone as his example of concrete things, he says the Stone becomes "not so much a Stone as no Stone, but everything is the simplest unity." Jung notes the lapis is a natural example because it is the arcanum containing God or the part of God hidden in matter. [p.285, par.416]
- **Affinities with Jung's quaternio series (par.417)** [pp.285-286]: the Second Monad is a duality of opposites matching the angelic world split by Lucifer's fall; and Kircher conceives his schema as a cycle set in motion by God as prime cause, unfolding, then brought back to God by human understanding, "the end returns once more to the beginning," analogous to Jung's formula.
- **The circulatory opus (pars. 417-421)** [pp.286-287]: alchemists pictured the opus as circulatory (circular distillation, uroboros). "Just as the central idea of the lapis Philosophorum plainly signifies the self, so the opus with its countless symbols illustrates the **process of individuation**, the step-by-step development of the self from an unconscious state to a conscious one." That is why the lapis, as prima materia, stands at the beginning as well as the end (footnote 113). Michael Maier: gold, another synonym of the self, comes from the sun's *opus circulatorium*; this circle is the line that "runs back upon itself (like the serpent that with its head bites its own tail)." In the circle Nature "has related the four qualities to one another and drawn, as it were, an equilateral square, since contraries are bound together by contraries, and enemies by enemies"; Maier compares this squaring of the circle to the *homo quadratus*, the four-square man who "remains himself" come weal come woe, and names it the "golden house, the twice-bisected circle, the four-cornered phalanx, the rampart, the city wall, the four-sided line of battle," a magic circle of united opposites "immune to all injury." [p.286, par.418]
- Chinese parallel (*Secret of the Golden Flower*): "When the light is made to move in a circle, all the energies of heaven and earth, of the light and the dark, are crystallized." [p.286, par.419]
- The *organon kyklikon* (circular apparatus) is mentioned as early as Olympiodorus. Dorn: the "circular movement of the Physiochemists" comes from the earth, the lowest element; fire originates in earth and transforms finer minerals and water into air, which rises, condenses and falls again; in ascent the volatilized elements take "male seeds" from the higher stars and bring them down into the four matrices (elements) to fertilize them spagyrically: the "circular distillation" which Rupescissa says must be repeated a thousand times. [pp.286-287, par.420]
- Last paragraph of the chapter: the basic idea of ascent and descent appears in the *Tabula smaragdina*; the transformation stages have been depicted repeatedly, above all in the Ripley "Scrowle" and variants, to be understood as "indirect attempts to apprehend the unconscious processes of individuation in the form of pictures." [p.287, par.421]

## CHAPTER XV. CONCLUSION (pars. 422-429) [pp.288-291]

### 1. Recapitulation: shadow, anima/animus, evil (pars. 422-424) [pp.288-290]

- Aim of the book: to elucidate "various aspects of the archetype which it is most important for modern man to understand," the archetype of the self, introduced via concepts that appear "in the course of any psychological treatment that penetrates at all deeply." [p.288, par.422]
- **Shadow:** "that hidden, repressed, for the most part inferior and guilt-laden personality whose ultimate ramifications reach back into the realm of our animal ancestors and so comprise the whole historical aspect of the unconscious." Analysis of the shadow uncovers the **anima/animus syzygy**. Superficially the shadow is a privation of light cast by the conscious mind (and so the moral inferiority a "privation of good"), but closer inspection shows a darkness that hides influential autonomous factors, anima and animus. Their full operation is described: in a woman, "the devastating, blindly obstinate demon of opinionatedness"; in a man, "the glamorous, possessive, moody, and sentimental seductress." This makes one doubt that the unconscious is "merely the insubstantial comet's tail of consciousness." [p.288, par.422]
- If the shadow was believed the source of all evil, closer investigation shows the unconscious man also has good qualities: normal instincts, appropriate reactions, realistic insights, creative impulses. On this level evil appears as "a distortion, a deformation, a misinterpretation and misapplication of facts that in themselves are natural"; the falsifications and caricatures now appear as specific effects of anima and animus, "the real authors of evil." [pp.288-289, par.423]
- But we cannot stop there: **all archetypes spontaneously develop favorable and unfavorable, light and dark, good and bad effects.** "In the end we have to acknowledge that the self is a complexio oppositorum precisely because there can be no reality without polarity." [p.289, par.423]
- **On good and evil:** opposites acquire moral accentuation only in the sphere of human endeavor and action; we cannot define good and evil universally; "we do not know what good and evil are in themselves." They probably spring from a need of human consciousness and lose validity outside the human sphere. A hypostasis of good and evil as metaphysical entities is "inadmissible because it would deprive these terms of meaning": if everything God does or allows is "good," evil is good too and "good" becomes meaningless. But suffering (Christ's passion or the world's) "remains the same as before"; stupidity, sin, sickness, old age and death "continue to form the dark foil that sets off the joyful splendor of life." [p.289, par.423]
- **Recognizing anima and animus (par.424):** a specific experience reserved mostly for psychotherapists, though anyone acquainted with belles-lettres can picture the anima (a favorite of novelists, "particularly west of the Rhine"; Spitteler's *Imago*, and perhaps Haggard's *She* per the editors, footnote 1). The woman's animus is harder to recognize because "his name is legion." Anyone who can bear others' animosity without being infected, and can examine it critically, will discover that they are possessed. More advantageous: subject to rigorous scrutiny one's own moods and their changing influence. "To know where the other person makes a mistake is of little value. It only becomes interesting when you know where you make the mistake, for then you can do something about it." What we can improve in others is of doubtful utility. [pp.289-290]

### 2. Anima/animus as numina; the self as union of opposites (pars. 425-427) [pp.290-291]

- Although they first appear in negative form, anima and animus have an equally **positive aspect**. Because of their numinous suggestive power they have been since olden times the archetypal basis of all masculine and feminine divinities. As numina they work now for good, now for evil. Their opposition is that of the sexes: a "supreme pair of opposites, not hopelessly divided by logical contradiction but, because of the mutual attraction between them, giving promise of union and actually making it possible." The *coniunctio oppositorum* occupied the alchemists in the "Chymical Wedding," the cabalists in Tifereth and Malchuth (God and the Shekhinah), and Christian tradition in the marriage of the Lamb. [p.290, par.425]
- The dual being born of the alchemical union (Rebis, Lapis Philosophorum) is a recognizable symbol of the self. **Psychologically the self is a union of conscious (masculine) and unconscious (feminine); it stands for the psychic totality** (a psychological concept). Empirically it appears spontaneously as specific symbols, its totality discernible above all in the mandala and its variants; historically, these symbols are authenticated as God-images. [p.290, par.426]
- **Stages and religion:** "The anima/animus stage is correlated with polytheism, the self with monotheism" (footnote 3 cites Allenby's unpublished Oxford dissertation on the origins of monotheism). Natural archetypal symbolism, describing a totality including light and dark, "contradicts in some sort the Christian but not the Jewish or Yahwistic viewpoint, or only to a relative degree," the latter seeming closer to nature and a better reflection of immediate experience. Christian heresiarchs tried to steer round the rocks of Manichaean dualism in a way that took account of the natural symbol; among symbols for Christ there are important ones shared with the devil, "though this had no influence on dogma." [pp.290-291, par.427]

### 3. The Gnostics and the work's limits (pars. 428-429) [p.291]

- "By far the most fruitful attempts ... to find suitable symbolic expressions for the self were made by the Gnostics." Valentinus and Basilides were in reality theologians who, unlike the more orthodox, let themselves be influenced greatly by natural inner experience. They are, like the alchemists, "a veritable mine of information" on natural symbols arising from the repercussions of the Christian message; and their ideas compensate the **asymmetry of God postulated by the doctrine of the privatio boni**, exactly like modern tendencies of the unconscious to produce symbols of totality bridging the gap between conscious and unconscious, which "has widened dangerously to the point of universal disorientation." [p.291, par.428]
- **Method and modesty (par.429):** Jung calls the work "far from being complete, a mere sketch" showing how certain Christian ideas look from the standpoint of psychological experience. His concern was the parallelism or difference between empirical findings and traditional views, so disparities of time and language (particularly in the fish symbol) were unavoidable; one moves on uncertain ground, sometimes by speculative hypothesis or tentatively reconstructing a context. An investigator should document fully but "venture an occasional hypothesis even at the risk of making a mistake. Mistakes are, after all, the foundations of truth, and if a man does not know what a thing is, it is at least an increase in knowledge if he knows what it is not." [p.291]
