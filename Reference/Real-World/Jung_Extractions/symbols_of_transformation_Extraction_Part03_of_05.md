## Overview of this range

The range carries the book's second half to its crisis. Jung continues to read the case material (the fantasies of "Miss Miller," published by Flournoy) as a "libido myth": the hero's life is a series of deliverances from the mother, in which the mother (really the mother-imago, which stands for the whole unconscious) is both the source of life and the devouring power, and every obstacle in the hero's path wears her features. Part of the range finishes the cross/tree symbolism (cross as tree of life and mother; sacrifice as canalization, not regression, of libido). Then, in the chapters on "The Battle for Deliverance from the Mother," "The Dual Mother," and "The Sacrifice," Jung (1) interprets Chiwantopel, Miss Miller's invented hero, as an animus-figure who acts out what she fails to do; (2) uses Longfellow's *Song of Hiawatha* (which Miss Miller named as a source) as a large, systematic amplification of the same pattern: wonder-birth, two mothers, father-fight in the West, a fasting introversion, the god who is eaten (Mondamin), the whale/night-sea journey, the serpent-guarded treasure, the final sunset voyage; (3) in a long meditation on Hiawatha's end, sets out the psychology of the two halves of life and of voluntary versus forced sacrifice; (4) runs a second long amplification through Wagner's Siegfried and Brünnhilde (anima as split-off of the father, mother-imago as helper and danger); (5) extensively amplifies the serpent/dragon, the treasure (*Hort*), caves, chasms and Hecate; (6) states a method of myth-interpretation by dream-psychology (compensation, dominants/archetypes); (7) returns to the Miller drama's last act (Chiwantopel's death by viper, horse and earthquake) and reads it as the unconscious attempting to compensate an infantile, stuck conscious mind; and (8) begins a reading of Hölderlin's poems as a case of regression into the maternal depths.

## V (continued from the previous part): the tree, the cross, and the dragon [pp.449-467]

*Chapter V continues from the previous part; its opening is not in this range.*

### The hero, the shadow, and the dragon as incest-resistance [pp.449-451]
- The mistletoe is a shadow-equivalent of Baldur; the shadow turns fatal when the hero has too little vitality or consciousness to complete his task [p.449, par. 120 and following].
- The "son of the mother" as a mere mortal dies young; as a god he does the forbidden (magical incest) and gains immortality. In myth the hero does not die; he overcomes the dragon of death [par. 394].
- **The dragon represents the negative mother-imago and expresses resistance to incest, or the fear of it.** Dragon and snake symbolize fear of the consequences of breaking the taboo and regressing. Hence the recurrent tree-and-snake motif; snakes and dragons guard the treasure (example: the black horse Apaosha blocking the rain-lake, defeated by the white horse Tishtriya at the third attempt with Ahura-Mazda's help) [p.449, par. 395].
- Libido fights libido, instinct against instinct: the unconscious is in conflict with itself, and mythological man perceived this in the contrarieties of external nature without suspecting it was the paradoxical background of his own consciousness [p.450].
- Tree entwined by snake = mother protected against incest by fear (frequent on Mithraic monuments; Mithras and Men born from a rock). Snakes threatening newborns (Mithras, Apollo, Heracles; Lilith and Lamia) point to the **father as the cause of the fear**, which led Freud to his myth of the primal horde; the model is the jealous Yahweh guarding his wife Israel [p.450, par. 396].
- **The father represents the world of moral commandments and prohibitions; he is the representative of spirit, whose function is to oppose pure instinctuality.** That is his archetypal role regardless of his personal qualities, so he is often the object of the son's neurotic fears and appears as the guardian giant (Humbaba in Gilgamesh, who guards Ishtar's garden; Gilgamesh conquers him and Ishtar at once propositions him) [pp.450-451].

### The paradox of the father and the bull; Mithras, Christ, the cross [pp.451-453]
- By overpowering the mother the hero equals the sun and renews himself. The Heddernheim Mithras relief: birth from the tree, carrying the conquered bull, reaching for Sol's nimbus, Sol kneeling to Mithras. The bull = the monster = the father who, paradoxically, enforces the incest prohibition as a giant dangerous animal. Like the mother (life-giver and "terrible" devourer), the father seems to live unbridled instinct yet embodies the law that thwarts instinct. Subtle distinction: the father commits no incest; the son tends toward it [p.451, par. 124].
- Jung's criticism of Freud: Freud overlooks that **spirit too is dynamic**, as it must be if the psyche is to keep its self-regulating equilibrium. The "father" is also a subjective psychic factor in the son, so killing the bull is both overcoming animal instinct and a secret, criminal usurpation of the power of the law. "The better is always the enemy of the good": every drastic innovation infringes what is traditionally right (Christ as law-breaker; "Adam Secundus" breaking through to a relation with a fundamentally different God) [pp.451-452].
- Animals represent instinct and the prohibition of instinct; man becomes human by conquering his animal instinctuality. In the Mithraic sacrifice the conquest no longer takes the archaic form of overpowering the mother but of **renouncing one's own instinctive desires**. The change finds full expression in the crucified God hung on the tree of life: a successful **canalization of libido into the symbolic equivalent of the mother, a spiritualization**, "the very reverse of regression" [pp.452-453]. In death he unites with the mother (the tree) and at the same time negates the union.

### Cross symbolism (comparative catalog) [pp.453-461]
- Hanging victims on trees and spears: Odin ("to Odin, myself to myself," Hávamál) [pp.453-454]. Middle America: the Fejérváry codex cross with a gory god; Palenque cross; Aztec spring sacrifice of a youth or maiden to Cinteotl on a cross, shot with arrows. Osiris as crucified god mourned by Isis and Nephthys [p.454].
- The cross is not only tree of life; also rain/fertility emblem and apotropaic charm. Early Christian art shows Christ standing before the cross with open arms, not nailed [pp.454-455]. Anthropomorphic trees/crosses: Druid trees cut into cross shape, Jain "tree of knowledge" with human form, Assyrian god as a cross, archaic Greek idols (Aegina) [pp.455-456]; Agrippa's "human cross" (fig. 26).
- **Union:** In Plato's *Timaeus* the demiurge splits the world-soul lengthwise and crosses the halves in an X (chi). The world-soul contains the world like a body, which "cannot fail to remind us of the mother." Self-containment = desirelessness = divine bliss (the god in the lotus, Diogenes in his tub) [pp.456-458]. Jung says the image of "soul" somehow coincides with the mother-imago [p.458].
- Muyscas (Peru): crossed ropes over water mark the sacrifice spot; water has maternal "womb" meaning; "crossing" has a double sense (fertility magic aims at increase) [pp.458-459].
- **Crux ansata / Tum (Atum):** life, "the Living One" (ankh); Tum is "father of his mother," his attendant goddess is mother, daughter and wife; autumn-equinox feast where the sacred eye is filled; "eye" = female genitals (Indra's yonis turned into eyes), the pupil (pupilla) = a child: the great god becomes a child and enters the mother for self-renewal. Hymn: "Thy mother, the sky, stretches forth her arms to thee." Agathodaimon serpent, scarab, Khnum as potter making his own egg [pp.459-461].
- **Summary claim:** the cross is many-faceted; chief meaning is tree of life and mother; *crux ansata* forms mean life, fruitfulness and union, interpretable as the hieros gamos of the god with his mother to conquer death and renew life. This passed into Christianity: Augustine's Christ as bridegroom consummating his marriage on "the marriage-bed of the cross" [p.461, par. 411].
- The Old English "Lament of the Virgin": Mary accuses the cross of being a false tree, "step-mother" of her son; the Cross answers; the "Mother of Death joins the Mother of Life" in lamenting, Mary kisses the cross and is reconciled. Egypt preserved the union of opposite tendencies naively in the Isis mother-imago [pp.462-463, par. 415].
- **Consciousness and death:** separation from the mother is leave-taking from animal unconsciousness. The incest prohibition created the self-conscious individual who had been "mindlessly one with the tribe," and only then did the idea of final death arise (Adam's sin = becoming conscious; death came in with it). The neurotic who cannot leave his mother is held ultimately by fear of death [p.464]. Jung's reframing: the law expressed in the incest prohibition should not be conceived as narrow "incest" but as the **impulse to domestication**; religious systems are institutions that take up man's animal instinctual forces, organize them, and make them available for higher cultural purposes [p.464].

### Miller fantasy: the bay, the cliff, "wa-ma" [pp.464-467]
- Miss Miller's "bay of purple water": etymology of *bay, gulf, gouffre, kolpos* (bosom, lap, womb, fold, pocket, hollow between waves/mountains) shows the primitive underlying idea; Faust's longing to follow the sun out over the "tranquil bays" and his resolve to enter the "narrow passage" burning with hell's flames is the hero's yearning for rebirth, which leads out to sea and down into the maw of death [pp.464-466]. The next vision, a perpendicular cliff, echoes *gouffre* [p.466]. The series ends with a confusion of sounds like "wa-ma, wa-ma," conjectured to be a distortion of the primitive "Ma-ma" [pp.466-467].

## VI. The Battle for Deliverance from the Mother [pp.468-509]

### The wood, the horse, and the hero on horseback [pp.468-477]
- After a pause in the visions, a wood appears. **The forest has a maternal significance like the tabooed tree**; the forbidden grove replaces the tabooed tree. In it Chiwantopel (Miss Miller's invented Indian hero) appears on horseback from the south, wrapped in a colored blanket; an Indian archer creeps up, but Chiwantopel bares his breast and the archer, fascinated, slinks away [pp.468-469].
- **Hero and horse = man and the subordinate sphere of animal instinct.** The horse suffers the same death as the hero (he calls it "faithful brother"). The mother-imago and the horse are both libido-symbols and overlap in meaning. Parallels: Agni on the ram, Wotan on Sleipnir (fig. 28), Ahura-Mazda on Angramainyu, Christ on the ass, Mithras on the bull, Men on the human-footed horse, Frey on the boar [p.469].
- **Horse lore (amplification):** legend gives horses properties that psychologically belong to the unconscious: clairvoyant, path-finding, prophesying horses (Iliad; Caesar's human-footed horse; an ass prophesying Augustus's victory); horses see ghosts; the devil has a horse's hoof; the devil rides the sleeper (nightmare); in Persian lore the devil is God's steed; sexual instinct (goat/horse at the Witches' Sabbath; Loki as horse). Lightning as horse (a hysterical patient's childhood vision of a rearing black horse after lightning; Yama's black thunder-horse); hoof and lightning as phallic/fertilizing; Pegasus' and Baldur's horse's hoof-struck springs; Mother Holle on horseback; horseshoe as luck charm and apotropaic [pp.470-473]. Two clinical fragments: a woman forced by her husband into coitus dreamed of a wild horse kicking her abdomen [p.472].
- Horses also signify **wind** (the wild huntsman; Wotan chasing Frigg; centaurs as wind gods), **fire and light** (Helios' horses, Hector's horses, Grani leaping Siegfried over the fire) [pp.473-474]. Dio Chrysostom's mystic quadriga (four horses = four elements; the outer fiery horse sets the next alight, the world conflagrates, and the divine One is restored) is an astronomical symbol of **Time**, the same libido symbolism as the Stoic fire-fate [p.474].
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad:** the sacrificial horse is the whole world and time (dawn his head, sun his eye, year his body, "the ocean is his kinsman, the sea his cradle") [p.475, par. 424]. **Aion/Chronos** of the Mithraic mysteries (lion-headed, serpent-coiled, with keys, thunderbolt, four wings, zodiac, cock and tools) is "composed entirely of libido-images": lion = *concupiscentia effrenata*; to be entwined = to be devoured = to enter the mother's womb; **time is defined by the death and renewal of libido, dawning and extinction of consciousness**; the empty formal concept "time" is expressed in myth through transformations of libido, just as in physics time is the flow of energic process (Macrobius; Philo says only evil men make Time a god; Zrwan akarana as origin of Ormazd and Ahriman) [pp.475-476].
- Horse as tree of death and psychopomp: bier = "St. Michael's Horse," Persian "wooden horse" = coffin; Valkyries and Charon as horse-riders [p.477]. Three-legged horse of Hel and the three-legged ass of Vouru-Kasha: the libido is both fructifying and destructive [p.477, par. 428].

### Cassius and Brutus: infantility and the theatrical gesture [pp.477-483]
- Miss Miller compared Chiwantopel's bared breast to Cassius offering his dagger to Brutus. Jung reads Cassius as **identified with his mother** and behaving like a woman (Brutus: "you are yoked with a lamb"; "He'll think your mother chides"), which shows an infantile disposition [pp.478-481, pars. 429-431].
- **Definition of infantility (Jung's usage):** an individual is infantile because he has freed himself insufficiently from his childish environment and his adaptation to his parents, so that he reacts as a child toward them (demanding love and emotional reward) while also being so identified with them that he behaves like his father or mother; he cannot live his own life or find his own character. Such people may be masterful outwardly yet infantile "in regard to the demands of feeling" [pp.480-481].
- **Chiwantopel as the author's ideal projected into a man.** Miss Miller can still see her ideal in a man because it commits her to nothing; an ideal of her own sex might show her she does not measure up. The wish for a grand death anticipates the end of the illusion that the other person is the ideal; the ideal "changes its psychic localization," and when an ideal of this importance changes, "it is as though that figure had to die," producing presentiments of death and romantic world-weariness (the "Song of the Moth"). Young girls' wish to die is often indirect expression of the infantile world's wish to end and be replaced by the adult phase; "any growing beyond oneself means death." Love "is a decision in favour of life, at whose end death stands. Love and death have not a little to do with one another" [pp.481-482].
- A gesture that is too theatrical suggests a contrary will is at work [p.482, par. 433].

### The arrow: death by arrow-shot; the self-wounding [pp.483-493]
- In the drama the libido turns menacing and murder is threatened. Heroes often are archers or die by arrows, so Jung asks what death by an arrow means [p.483].
- **Anna Catherina Emmerich** (stigmatic nun): heart-pains as if pierced by arrows, which she identified with her sisters' gossip. Jung: the saint's difficulty is constant temptation; hysterics substitute a physical pain for a repressed psychic pain ("Herz" rhymes with "Schmerz"); her interpretation is a projection. The real source is renunciation of life's joys and the repressed desires' attempts to break through the barrier of repression, and **gossip "takes over the role of the unconscious" and aims at the chinks in our armor of which we know nothing** [pp.483-484]. Buddha's discourse: sensual desire that fails pierces the person with the arrow of pain. "The wounding and painful shafts do not come from outside... but from the ambush of our own unconscious" [pp.484-485, par. 438].
- Her stigmatization vision (rays ending in an arrow) is read as strongly tinged with erotic libido; stigmatization = incubation with the Saviour, a modification of the *unio mystica* as cohabitation with the god [pp.485-486].
- **The arrow = a masculine libido-symbol** (Cupid's and the sun's arrows; "arrows in the hand of a mighty man" for sons, Psalm 127; Arabic "to make sharp arrows" = beget valiant sons; Chinese bow and arrow hung at a son's birth; Scythian king's arrowhead census; the lance and Kaineus: originally a maiden, made invulnerable man, buried under trees, left as a golden-winged bird = plover, rain-bird). Kaineus shows the typical elements of a libido myth: **original bisexuality, immortality through entry into the mother, resurrection as soul-bird, production of fertility** [pp.486-487, par. 441].
- Job 16 and Nietzsche's "Unknown God" ("Pierce, rend my heart!... Why wilt thou not kill, only torture?") and "Zarathustra, most cruel Nimrod... self-hunted, thine own quarry, thyself pierced through... self-hangman" are read as the martyred and sacrificed god; Sebastian images show the artist's projection of renunciation; the Crucified pierced by the lance is "a true symbol of the man of the Christian era, tormented by his desires and crucified in Christ" [pp.487-490].
- **Interpretation:** man is his own huntsman, sacrificer and sacrificial knife. Being wounded by one's own arrow = union with oneself, self-fertilization and self-murder (Odin sacrificed to Odin). Jung cautions against too voluntaristic a reading: "nobody deliberately inflicts such tortures on himself, they just happen to him." If the symbolism is archetypal and collective, "he is no longer suffering from himself, but rather from the spirit of the age," from his collective unconscious [pp.491-492, par. 447].

### Introversion and regression as the dangerous moment [pp.492-496]
- **Being wounded by one's own arrow = a state of introversion:** libido sinks "into its own depths" and finds a substitute for the upper world: the world of memories, strongest the earliest; the child's paradisal state; in this "subterranean kingdom slumber sweet feelings of home and the hopes of all that is to be" (Hauptmann's *Sunken Bell*) [p.492].
- **The danger is great.** When libido leaves the bright upper world, whether from choice, inertia or fate, it returns to the point of cleavage, the navel, "called the mother, because from her the current of life reached us." Before a great work a man recoils and the libido streams back to the fountainhead; "the issue hangs between annihilation and new life." If the libido gets stuck in the inner wonderland, the man for the upper world is a shadow, moribund or ill; if it tears free and rises, "something like a miracle happens": the descent was a plunge into the fountain of youth [p.493, par. 449].
- Indian myth in support: Vishnu in trance brings forth Brahma on a lotus from his navel with the Vedas (birth of creative thought from introversion); his ecstatic absence brings a flood (world devoured through introversion); a demon steals the Vedas; Vishnu as fish recovers them [pp.493-494].
- **Archetype constellated by need (par. 450):** through introversion and regression, latent contents are constellated: the primordial images (archetypes) "so enriched with individual memories... as to become perceptible to the conscious mind," like crystals taking visible shape from a saturated solution. Since these introversions happen only when a new orientation and adaptation are needed, **the constellated archetype is the primordial image of the need of the moment**. Life's situations fall into a limited set of typical patterns; the archetypal structure corresponds to the average run of events. Because the archetype is numinous (it has specific energy), it attracts contents of consciousness and its entry is felt as illumination, revelation, or "saving idea" [pp.494-495].
- Hence ritual: in critical situations the mechanism of introversion is made to function artificially through magical ceremonies, sacrifices, invocations, prayers, to direct libido to the unconscious. Connecting with the unconscious is like connecting with the mother and raises the incest taboo; but "the unconscious is infinitely greater than the mother and is only symbolized by her," so the fear must be conquered to obtain the "saving" contents (the treasure hard to attain). The incest tendency, especially as the mother's or anima's amorous desire (Ishtar and Gilgamesh; Chryse and Philoctetes), "is really only the desire of the unconscious to be taken notice of." Rejecting it has bad results: Chryse changes into a venomous serpent; **the more negative the conscious attitude, the more dangerous the unconscious** [p.495, par. 450].
- Philoctetes bitten in the foot by a snake [p.496].

### Ra, Isis, the serpent and the secret name; vicious circle of fear [pp.496-504]
- Egyptian hymn: aging Ra drops spittle; Isis kneads it with earth into a worm; Ra steps on it; poison spreads "as the Nile invades his territory"; Isis demands his true name ("he whose name is spoken shall live"). Ra, only partly healed, finally retires on the heavenly cow [pp.496-499, pars. 451-454].
- Jung's reading: the aging of the autumn sun as human senility traced to serpent poisoning; the mother is blamed. **The serpent is the mysterious numen of the mother who kills but is also man's only security against death, as she is the source of life.** The worm is a deadly instead of animating form of libido; the true name is Ra's soul and magic power (his libido); what Isis demands is transference of libido to the mother, fulfilled when the god goes back to the cow [pp.497-499].
- **The vicious circle (par. 456):** forward-striving libido demands separation from the mother; childish longing resists through neurotic fears, a general fear of life; the more one shrinks from adapting, the more fear besets every step: fear of life leads to shrinking back, infantilism, and finally "into the mother." Reasons are projected: external circumstances or parents are blamed, though "he would do better to refrain from all such futile attempts to excuse his own ineptitude by laying the blame on his parents" [pp.499-500].
- **Fear of life is real panic whose source is unconscious and projected:** the young, growing part of the personality, if prevented from living, "generates fear and changes into fear." The mother-imago turns into a lamia; the real mother too can harm by morbid tenderness prolonging infancy [p.500, par. 457].
- Nothing endangers the connection with the unconscious more than a successful life, which makes a man forget his dependence on it. Gilgamesh was so successful that the gods (representatives of the unconscious) deliberated on his downfall; a serpent stole the herb of immortality while he slept [pp.500-501].
- **The demands of the unconscious act at first like a paralyzing poison** (compared to a snake bite); the cause is often obscure and mixed with external factors (work difficulties, disappointments, age, family problems). Myths say it is the woman who secretly enslaves a man (Isis from the god's spittle; Delilah cutting Samson's hair, the sun's rays) [p.501, par. 458].
- **Second half of life:** this demon-woman is "the sister-wife-mother, the woman in the man, who unexpectedly turns up during the second half of life and tries to effect a forcible change of personality," a partial feminization of the man and masculinization of the woman (cf. Jung's essay "The Stages of Life"). The man's strongest quality, his Logos principle, turns against him; the woman's Eros likewise. The man grows rigid, hiding in moods, irritability, distrust and resentment; the woman stays in emotional ties and fails to develop reason, replaced by obstinate "animus" opinions. Schreber's *Memoirs of My Nervous Illness* is cited as perfect example [pp.501-502].
- **Task of the second half of life (par. 459):** the paralysis of progressive energy seems a catastrophe, and the conscious personality resists, since the demands attack both weak points and the chief virtue (the differentiated function and the ideal). But the assault can become a source of energy for heroic conflict (Heracles, Gilgamesh): perhaps the maternal archetype's apparent enmity is a ruse of Mater Natura spurring her favored child, "the stern 'Mistress Soul,'" to become "what he always potentially was." **The hero's victory over the mother is never more than temporary.** What is regression in a young person (feminization of the man, masculinization of the woman) acquires a different meaning in the second half: assimilating contrasexual tendencies "becomes a task that must be fulfilled in order to keep the libido in a state of progression." The task is integrating the unconscious, bringing together conscious and unconscious: **individuation** (Jung refers to his later works). At this stage the mother-symbol no longer points back to beginnings but to the unconscious as "the creative matrix of the future," and "entry into the mother" means "establishing a relationship between the ego and the unconscious" [pp.504-505].
- Nietzsche's "Zarathustra self-knower... piled with a hundred burdens" is read as a man who has crawled back into the mother, like Kaineus buried under trees. **The cross, or any heavy burden the hero carries (Mithras' bull on his back in the *transitus*, Christ's cross), is "himself, or rather the self, his wholeness, which is both God and animal,"** rooted in animal nature and reaching toward the divine: "a tremendous tension of opposites paradoxically at one with themselves, as in the cross, their most perfect symbol" [pp.505-507, par. 460]. Poetry: "the art of letting the primordial word resound through the common word" (Hauptmann) [p.507].

### What the sacrifice means in the Miller case [pp.507-509]
- The sacrifice is **giving up the connection with the mother, relinquishing all childhood ties that the psyche carried into adult life**. At the time Miss Miller was living in the family circle at an age where independence was urgent; the fantasies coincided with a journey abroad. "It is not possible to live too long amid infantile surroundings, or in the bosom of the family, without endangering one's psychic health"; a person who ignores life's call to independence out of childish laziness or timidity is threatened with neurosis, which then becomes a reason to run from life [pp.507-508].
- **Chiwantopel as a typical animus-figure** (personification of the masculine side of a woman's psyche): bold adventurer who does what Miss Miller shrinks from. The animus becomes active when the conscious mind refuses to follow feelings and instincts: "instead of love and surrender there is mannishness, argumentativeness, obstinate self-assertion, and the demon of opinion in every possible shape and form (power instead of love)." He is "not a real man at all; he is a slightly hysterical, infantile hero whose longing to be loved shows through the gaps in his armour." Her critical life decisions are dressed in this garb, not yet recognized as her own [p.508, par. 462].
- That the assassin is scared off means the conscious mind postpones, burying its head in unconsciousness. **Chiwantopel must fall** because the power of decision locked in the unconscious, which keeps the hero erect, is needed by the conscious mind to wrench free of the infantile past; "the whole of the libido is needed for the battle of life" [p.509, par. 463].

## VII. The Dual Mother [pp.510-629]

### Chiwantopel's monologue; the seeker and "she who understands" [pp.510-513]
- Monologue: wandering "a hundred moons" since leaving his father's palace in search of "her who will understand"; he reviews and dismisses Chi-ta the princess (vain), Ta-nan the peasant girl (a "perfect sow"), Ki-ma the priestess (a parrot of priestly phrases) [p.510].
- **The search for the other is the search for the mother:** "She who understands" is, in infantile speech, the mother. The original concrete sense of *comprehend, begreifen, erfassen* is to seize and hold in the arms, which is what the mother does and what binds child to mother; with age this "comprehension" hinders development. Libido regresses to the sheltering ease of the mother's arms (a Hermetic text is quoted: "chained to the arms and breast of my mother") [p.511].
- **When a person stays bound to the mother, the life she should have lived runs away in conscious and unconscious fantasies**, in a woman typically attributed to a hero-figure who acts them out. He is the seeker who survives the adventures the conscious personality avoids and displays the courage the conscious mind lacks. Anyone who meets such an infantile woman is made identical with her animus-hero and set up as the ideal, "threatened with the direst punishments" for any departure [pp.511-512].
- The woman believes the hero seeks her; but "the animus, a typical 'son'-hero, is not after her at all; true to his ancient prototype, he is seeking the mother." **The youthful hero is always the son-lover of the mother-goddess, doomed to early death** [p.512, par. 466].
- **Function of myths (par. 466):** libido that will not flow into life regresses to the world of archetypes, activating images that have expressed the non-human life of the gods; in a young person his individual life is supplanted by the divine archetypal drama, and with no means of recognizing it he cannot free himself. "Herein lay the vital importance of myths: they explained to the bewildered human being what was going on in his unconscious... The myths told him: 'This is not you, but the gods. You will never reach them, so turn back to your human avocations, holding the gods in fear and respect.'" The "shining heights" and "superhuman word Love" betray the divine nature of the dramatis personae; the "pathos rings hollow and the heroics seem hysterical" [pp.512-513].

### The archetype's own life; the animus as vicarious actor [pp.513-516]
- Alternative standpoint: not Miss Miller's personal situation but the archetype's own life. Archetypes are spontaneous autonomous manifestations; their evidence is numinosity, fascination, opposition to consciousness, and long-run molding of destinies. "The primordial image is itself a 'pattern of behaviour' which will assert itself with or without the co-operation of the conscious personality." Jung refers to the dream series in *Psychology and Alchemy* for a full illustration [pp.513-514, par. 467].
- **Chiwantopel as a "fragmentary personality equipped with a relative degree of consciousness and a will to match"** (the autonomy and purposiveness of the complex). He wins the dreamer's soul "not in order to lead her back to normal life, but to her spiritual destiny": a bridegroom of death, one of the son-lovers who die young, "fast-fading flowers on the maternal tree." In a woman the mother-imago "does not, like the anima, stand for the chaotic life of the unconscious in all its aspects, but for the peculiarly fascinating background of the psyche, the world of primordial images." Danger: growing fast to the rock like Theseus and Peirithous; no return from "the realm of the Mothers." The same danger could be salvation if consciousness understood the contents; the associations contain clues; "our culture, however, has neither eyes nor heart for these things" [pp.514-515, par. 468].
- **Compensation in fantasy:** the hero as animus-figure "acts vicariously for the conscious individual," doing what the subject ought but does not; "all the things that could happen in conscious life, but do not happen, are acted out in the unconscious and consequently appear in projection." Fantasy is compensatory to the conscious attitude, as in dreams [pp.515-516, par. 469].
- Parallels for leaving the father's house: the young Buddha; Christ's "not peace but a sword" (Matt. 10:34f.); Horus snatching the head-dress from his mother. **Nietzsche's passage on the "great emancipation"** (the free spirit's decisive crisis: bound by reverence and duty, then a sudden horror of what it loved, a longing for estrangement, "glaciation," an inner thrill of victory; "like a disease that can easily destroy the man") is quoted [pp.516-517]. The danger Nietzsche sees is isolation, solitude as "that terrible goddess and Mater saeva cupidinum" [p.518]. Jung: **the libido withdrawn unwillingly from the mother turns into a threatening serpent, symbolizing the fear of death, since the relation must die; the violence of the separation is proportionate to the strength of the bond**, and the mother approaches in the guise of the unconscious [p.518, par. 473].

### On method: why compare with Hiawatha [pp.518-520]
- Miss Miller says Longfellow's *Song of Hiawatha* influenced her fantasies. Jung acknowledges the reader's doubt about "remote material" and defends the comparative method: comparing is on certain ground where the patient names sources; information is seldom complete; cryptomnesia exists but not all ideas whose origin is unknown are cryptomnesias. The way ideas are formed and arranged can be acquired, "but that need not always be the case, because the human mind possesses general and typical modes of functioning which correspond to the biological 'pattern of behaviour.'" **These preexistent innate patterns, the archetypes, can produce practically identical ideas in widely different individuals** with no individual experience to account for them. Psychotic material is strange to the patient but familiar to the expert through mythologems. "Because the basic structure of the psyche is everywhere more or less the same, it is possible to compare what look like individual dream-motifs with mythologems of whatever origin." He therefore compares American Indian myth with the modern American psyche [p.519, par. 474].
- Jung had not read *Hiawatha* until this point and found it "unusually rich in mythological motifs," which explains the wealth of associations in the Miller fantasies [p.520, par. 475].

### Hiawatha: prologue, Gitche Manito, Mudjekeewis [pp.520-525]
- Nawadaha sings Hiawatha as friend of man. The hero is **a symbolic figure who attracts libido to himself as wonder and adoration "to lead it over the symbolic bridge of myth to higher uses"** (teleological significance) and is a saviour with miraculous birth, deeds in youth, sacrifice. Canto 1 opens with an "Evangelium" (a prophet-deliverer to be sent) [pp.520-521].
- Gitche Manito stands on the Red Pipestone quarry; a river flows from his footprints, shining like the comet Ishkoodah. Parallel in Coptic "Mysteries of Saint John": water under the Father's feet, the Nile. **Feet and treading carry fertility meaning**: the Pueblo dance-step pounding the earth, Kaineus splitting the earth with a straight foot, Faust reaching the Mothers by stamping, heroes kicking the monster's gullet (Thor). Treading in dance resembles the infantile kicking, associated with the mother and recapitulating movement in the womb; foot = phallic or re-entry into the womb; the dance transports the dancer into an unconscious state (dervishes). The flowing water from the footprints is a libido-symbol for fertilizing moisture; Gitche Manito also makes fire (a forest rubbing into flame), producing water and fire [pp.521-523].
- **Mudjekeewis** (the hero's father) overcomes the great bear, steals the magic belt of wampum (the **treasure hard to attain**), and mocks the bear for whimpering "like a woman." Jung: he slays his feminine component, the anima-image whose first carrier is the mother; the deed = journey to hell = night sea journey = conquest of the monster from within = entry into the mother's womb = rebirth. As in the Zosimos vision, the entrant becomes **pneuma**: Mudjekeewis becomes the West Wind, father of the winds. Wabun's courtship of the maiden with blue-lake eyes is mentioned; "from wind and water man shall be born anew" [pp.523-525, pars. 482-485].

### Nokomis, Wenonah, and the wonder-birth [pp.526-534]
- Nokomis (grandmother) lived in the moon, was cut down from a grape-vine by a jealous lover and fell as a shooting-star; she gave birth to Wenonah, who becomes Hiawatha's mother, impregnated by the West Wind's breath; the moon = abode of souls, guardian of the seed, feminine source of life. The throwing up, fall, and birth-pangs of the mother are "typical" (a seventeenth-century story of a woman tossed by a bull; Gnostic archons letting their young fall; Hottentot sun as bacon-fat). "Plucking of the branch" hints at infringement of the incest taboo; Heine's falling star [pp.526-528].
- Wind-conception parallels: Mörike's maid who "would rather be the Wind's own bride"; Buddha's conception by a six-tusked white elephant and star with a wind blowing; four genies as palanquin bearers (cf. Magi); unicorn and dove as spermatic Word/Spirit in Christian imagery [pp.529-531].
- **Why is the hero's birth always extraordinary? (par. 493-494):** "The hero is not born like an ordinary mortal because his birth is a rebirth from the mother-wife. That is why the hero so often has two mothers." Rank: exposure and foster-parents (Heracles and Hera; Wenonah and Nokomis; Buddha's foster-mother; she-wolf of Romulus and Remus). The dual mother can be replaced by dual birth: **baptism as rebirth through the second mother**; Christ calls his death a baptism (Luke 12:50) and the pangs of rebirth [pp.531-532].
- **The dual mother:** one mother real and human, the other symbolic (divine, supernatural, or animal); when projected onto persons (step-mother, mother-in-law) complications arise [pp.532-533, par. 495].
- **"He who stems from two mothers is the hero: the first birth makes him a mortal man, the second an immortal half-god."** Hiawatha's father conquers the mother in the bear symbol, becomes a god, then begets the hero. Egyptian counterpart: the rejuvenated mother is given as daughter-wife to the sun-god "father of his mother" for self-reproduction. John's Gospel (pre-existence; the Logos; Pisa baptistery relief with "INTROITUS SOLIS") [p.533, par. 496].
- **Virgin conception, psychologically (par. 497):** a content of the unconscious ("child") has come into existence without the natural help of a human father (i.e., consciousness); the son is identical with the father, i.e., a central archetype, the God-image, has renewed itself ("been reborn") and become "incarnate" perceptibly to consciousness. The "mother" corresponds to the "virgin anima," turned not to the outer world but to the "inner sun," the archetype of transcendent wholeness, the self [p.534].

### Childhood, nature and the mother; Joël's "Primal Experience" [pp.534-539]
- Hiawatha is reared by Nokomis between forest and the Big-Sea-Water and learns "the language of nature" ("Minne-wawa!" the pines, "Mudway-aushka!" the water; the wind and goose say "wawa"). Jung: **external nature is drawn into the subject's world; the secondary object (nature) imperceptibly usurps the primary object (mother) and takes over sounds and feelings first heard from her.** Later pantheistic or aesthetic blending with nature is "a reblending with the mother," our first object and first experience of outside and inside; from the interior emerged an older, more imperishable image, a mother who changed back into a Kore, an eternally youthful figure: **the anima, "the personification of the collective unconscious"** [pp.535-536, par. 500].
- Karl Joël's "Primal Experience" (lying on the seashore, subject and object merging, outside and inside one, consciousness born at the moment of estrangement when experience is split into subject and object) is read as unmistakable mother-child reunion symbolism with the devouring sea and sun-motif; Jung's patient said "It makes me squirm, as if I'd touched a jelly-fish" on hearing of the maternal meaning of water; an Indian chieftain's "the happiness of thinking nothing and doing nothing... So we were before birth, and so we shall be after death" [pp.537-538, par. 501].

### The roebuck, the ford, and the skin [pp.539-541]
- Hiawatha's first deed: killing a roebuck at the ford; whatever he kills lies half in water, half on land. The roebuck is a magic "doctor animal," a symbol of the unconscious; the ford is the border between conscious and unconscious. Animals belong to the Great Mother; killing a wild animal transgresses against her; whoever kills the "magic" animal acquires her gigantic strength and clothes himself in its skin (Aztec flaying sacrifice: priests wear the pelts of slaughtered god-impersonators for resurrection). Hiawatha kills "his own participation mystique with animal nature" and gains giant strength; he then fights his father to avenge his mother. **The animals, the father-giant, the tyrant can be interpreted as the "Terrible Mother"** (Jung adds a case: a mother who tied her children to her fell into involutional depressive psychosis and enacted a wolf or pig, "had herself become the symbol of the all-devouring mother") [pp.539-540].
- **Imagos, not parents (par. 505):** "Interpretation in terms of the parents is... simply a façon de parler. In reality the whole drama takes place in the individual's own psyche, where the 'parents' are not the parents at all but only their imagos," arising from parental peculiarities and the child's own disposition. They are activated by instinct-derived energy, which dreams represent theriomorphically: "All the lions, bulls, dogs, and snakes that populate our dreams represent an undifferentiated and as yet untamed libido... the anthropoid psyche." Libido, like energy, is never manifest as such but only in definite forms/states [p.541].

### The anthropoid psyche, regression, therapy [pp.541-544]
- **The anthropoid psyche** does not fit the rational pattern of culture and strives back to the original unconscious state; regression runs back to childhood and finally into the mother's body. Its retrospective longing is "brilliantly depicted in the figure of Enkidu" in Gilgamesh. When demands for adaptation heighten, whether external or from within (an "enhanced subjective demand... the ever-stronger emergence of the inner, and hitherto hidden, 'real' personality"), the anthropoid psyche is "the aim and end of every regression," which sets in at the least hesitation to adapt [pp.541-542, par. 506].
- **Critique of morality and Freudian theory:** religious and conventional morality joins Freudian theory in devaluing regression as "infantile sexuality," "incest," "uterine fantasy." But what exceeds personal consciousness remains unconscious and appears in projection: the semi-animal psyche is attributed to the mother and defense against it to the father. "Projection, however, is never a cure; it prevents the conflict only on the surface, while deeper down it creates a neurosis" ("the devil is cast out by Beelzebub") [pp.542-543, par. 507].
- **Therapy (par. 508):** "therapy must support the regression, and continue to do so until the 'prenatal' stage is reached." The "mother" is an imago that holds many unconscious contents; as the first incarnation of the anima archetype she "personifies in fact the whole unconscious"; regression leads only apparently to the mother, who is "the gateway into the unconscious, into the 'realm of the Mothers.'" Regression left undisturbed goes beyond her to the prenatal realm of the "Eternal Feminine," the world of archetypal possibilities where "slumbers the 'divine child,' patiently awaiting his conscious realization." **This son is the germ of wholeness, characterized by specific symbols** [p.543].
- **Jonah in the whale:** Paracelsus says he saw "mighty mysteries"; Pirkê de Rabbi Elieser describes a pearl lighting the fish's belly. **The treasure in the darkness (the pearl, the mystery) is the ultimate unconscious goal of regression**; symbols as bridges and pointers keep libido from getting stuck in the mother's material corporeality; the Nicodemus dialogue states the dilemma (cannot re-enter the womb; must be reborn of water and spirit). "The hero is a hero just because he sees resistance to the forbidden goal in all life's difficulties and yet fights that resistance with the whole-hearted yearning... that paralyses and kills the ordinary man" [pp.543-544, pars. 509-510].

### The fight in the West, the father, the arrowsmith, the anima [pp.544-549]
- Mudjekeewis is the West Wind; the hero's battle for rebirth is in the West. The danger comes from both parents: the father makes regression impossible, the mother absorbs regressing libido so that "he who sought rebirth finds only death." Hiawatha pursues his father three days to the portals of the Sunset; "three days" = night-sea imprisonment (Dec 21 to 24), also Christ in the underworld. The father yields his wind-nature and shares the realm of the Northwest/home wind (parallel: Gilgamesh and Utnapishtim's herb, stolen by a serpent). Hiawatha receives a "pneumatic" breath-body [pp.544-546].
- On return he visits an arrowsmith with the daughter Minnehaha ("Laughing Water"). **For the hero, "even more than the rest of mankind," the mother is found in the woman he loves: "The archetype of the Feminine, the anima, first appears in the mother and then transfers itself to the beloved"** [p.547, par. 514].
- The arrowsmith father-in-law is the wise old man, "a personification of meaning and spirit in its procreative sense." The hero's father is often a master craftsman (Terah, Tvashtri, Joseph, Cinyras, Hephaestus; the woodcutter in fairytales; Rig-Veda's cosmic architect). The anima stands to the wise old man as a daughter, so the father-in-law can replace the father. Father-attributes can fall to the son himself: **the hero symbolizes a man's unconscious self, "the sum total of all archetypes," including father and wise old man, so "the hero is his own father and his own begetter"** (the legend of Mani; Wieland the Smith) [pp.547-548, pars. 515-516].

### The fast, regression/introversion, and Mondamin [pp.548-558]
- Hiawatha keeps silent, introverts, and builds a hut to fast. His question "Must our lives depend on these things?" implies a sudden transfer or withdrawal of unconscious libido from nature. His retreat revives the earliest nature-impressions, second only to the mother's, so he is "reawakening the relationship to the mother, and to something older than the mother," and will emerge reborn [pp.548-550].
- **Two aims of regression:** infantile regression "aims—without of course admitting it—at incest and nourishment" (the alma mater: warmth, love, food); a purposive introversion avoids endogamy and replaces the demand for food with intentional fasting. "Solitude and fasting have from time immemorial been the best-known means of strengthening any meditation whose purpose is to open the door to the unconscious" [pp.550-551, par. 519].
- On the fourth day he sees Mondamin, a youth in green and yellow ("friend of man," from the Master of Life), who challenges him to wrestle. **Mondamin is maize: "Hiawatha's introversion gives birth to a god who is eaten."** The Christian parallel is obvious, though no Christian influence need be assumed (Sahagún's Aztec Huitzilopochtli eucharist; Cherokee corn as "the old woman"). The god arises from the unconscious "like a transformed reflection of Hiawatha's introverted consciousness"; the deepest tie is to the mother; through her symbolic equivalent he can be born again; **the mystery of the "mother" is divine creative power**. The wrestling gives new strength: "the fight against the paralysing grip of the unconscious calls forth man's creative powers. That is the source of all creativity, but it needs heroic courage." "Hiawatha wrestles with himself in order to create himself." After three days he conquers Mondamin on the fourth, buries him in the earth, and corn sprouts. Had he failed, Mondamin would have "killed" him and he would be "possessed" by a demon [pp.551-554, pars. 522-523].
- **Remarkable point (par. 524):** it is not the man who is transformed into a god but "the god who undergoes transformation in and through man"; he sleeps in the "mother" (Hiawatha's unconscious), is roused and fought so that he does not overpower his host, himself experiences death and rebirth and reappears in the corn beneficially. He appears first as an assailant "in keeping with the violence of all unconscious dynamism." Parallel: Jacob and the angel at Jabbok. "The onslaught of instinct then becomes an experience of divinity, provided that man does not succumb to it and follow it blindly, but defends his humanity against the animal nature of the divine power." Biblical supports: "a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God," the lion of Judah, the devil as roaring lion [pp.554-555].
- Mithras and the bull carried into the cave; Christ's Gethsemane and the carrying of the cross ("takes on his shoulders the symbol of the deadly mother and... carries himself to the grave"): "Christ is a divinity who is eaten in the Lord's Supper." Also Agni/soma, Dionysus/wine, Samson's lion and the bees ("Out of the eater came forth meat") [pp.555-556].
- **Eleusinian amplification** [pp.556-561]: Iacchus as *puer aeternus*, "the fresh-ploughed boy" (cf. Etruscan Tages), plough = phallic, furrow = woman; Iacchus as Demeter's daimon, identified with Dionysus-Zagreus (torn by the Titans, heart swallowed by Zeus, reborn as Iacchus). The winnowing-basket (*liknon*) as cradle; Hiawatha's fast resembles Demeter's wandering without food; Hecate/moon gives news of the lost daughter as Hiawatha finds Mondamin by descent "to the Mothers." Bishop Asterius' testimony of a darkened hieros gamos; caves and cave-worship (Ahriman's sunless place; Mithrobarzanes; Armenian "Sister Fire/Brother Spring"; Attis; the Bethlehem cave as Attis *spelaeum*); the baskets and the neophyte's synthema ("I have fasted... taken from the cista... laid it back in the basket"); phallus in the basket; the serpent in the mystic basket and "the god through the lap" (Sabazius; snake drawn through initiates' laps; Bacchus *hypokolpios*); hierophant's cry "The great goddess has borne a divine boy" and the **mown ear of corn** as the epoptic mystery. Jung reads the snake's kissing as **"ceremonial conquest of incest"** and as the danger of regressive libido [pp.558-560].
- **Lost-and-found motif:** dying and rising paired with being lost and found (images hidden and found at spring festivals; Moses at twelve; Christ in the temple; Khidr found in place of the fish). The Eleusinian epitaph: "Death comes not as a curse, but as a blessing" and the Homeric Hymn's blessing of the initiate; Preiswerk's hymn on wheat dying in the earth; Firmicus on Attis ("the god is saved, and you too shall have salvation out of sorrow") [pp.560-562].
- **Impersonality of the Christ-image (par. 536):** "These parallels show how little there is of the human and personal in the Christ-image, and how strong is the universal and mythological element. The hero is an extraordinary being who is inhabited by a daemon." The saviour-hero "appears as a fruit of the entry of libido into the maternal depths of the unconscious" [pp.562-563].
- **Initiation and the Church as womb/grave:** head covering = invisibility = death (Farnese relief; Nandi circumcised in cone hats; nuns' veil); Proclus' neophytes buried to the neck; **the Church as the hero's grave and, underneath, the mother's womb** (Tantric *garbha griha*; Holy Sepulchre at San Stefano, Bologna, built from an Isis temple; Hal Saflieni, Malta; Etruscan Matuta ossuary-statue) [pp.563-564].

### The whale, the sea-monster fight; Megissogwon [pp.564-575]
- **Mishe-Nahma, the fish-king:** swallows Hiawatha and his canoe; he strikes the heart; drags the canoe crosswise so he is not hurled out. **The typical "almost worldwide" hero myth:** sea voyage, swallowed by a sea-monster, struggle against being bitten/crushed (kicking motif), finds and destroys the vital organ, often lights a fire inside; the fish dies and drifts ashore, a bird helps the hero see daylight (bird = sun's renewed ascent, phoenix, helpful animals/spirit; swan etymologically linked with *sun* and *sound*). African myth of death entering by the old woman who put on her old skin [pp.564-566, par. 538].
- **Interpretation (par. 539):** "the attempt to free the ego-consciousness from the deadly grip of the unconscious"; the fire in the belly is apotropaic magic dispelling unconsciousness; the rescue is a sunrise, the triumph of consciousness [p.566].
- **But the deed is not lasting (par. 540):** the hero must renew the struggle; Nokomis, like Hera to Heracles, gives him no rest. "Man with his consciousness is always a long way behind the goals of the unconscious; unless his libido calls him forth to new dangers he sinks into slothful inactivity, or in the prime of life he is overcome with longing for the past and is paralysed... The mother is thus the daemon who challenges the hero to his deeds and lays in his path the poisonous serpent that will strike him" [pp.566-567].
- **Megissogwon (Pearl-Feather)**, in the West, guarded by fiery serpents and black pitch-water, killed Nokomis' father. Snake and water are mother attributes; the black Stygian water is where the sun sinks for rebirth. Hiawatha crosses at night in the "night sea journey" using the magic oil of Mishe-Nahma (an immortality philtre) over waters whose rotting plants point to entwining and devouring (Jagaddeva's dream-book: wrapped in creepers/cords = death) [pp.569-570, pars. 541-542].
- **Megissogwon = the realm of the Terrible Mother represented by a negative father-figure or a masculine principle in the mother herself**, while Nokomis, the spiritus rector sending Hiawatha, is a feminine principle in the hero's breast: his anima; the magician corresponds to the animus of the Terrible Mother [pp.570-571, par. 543]. Hiawatha rests beneath a pine coated with fungus: tree-anthropomorphism (the Indian village peepul, anointed and pierced like a person; triad gods within), "clearly characterized as a mother-symbol." The woodpecker "Mama" advises him to shoot the topknot (a "head-birth" vulnerable point); he shoots three arrows; the woodpecker as nurse of Romulus and Remus, Picus, Picumnus/Pilumnus (*infantium dii*). He steals the invisibility-giving wampum; the corpse lies half in land and half in water, like the fish-king [pp.571-573].
- **Two tasks, then marriage (par. 548):** after conquering the Terrible Mother in the negative father's guise he can turn to his human side. The tasks: "firstly the transformation of the daemon from an uncontrolled force of nature into a power that is his to command; secondly the final deliverance of ego-consciousness from the deadly threat of the unconscious in the form of the negative parents. The first task signifies the creation of will-power, the second the free use of it" [p.574].
- **Hieroglyphs (cantos 12, 14):** the old man rejuvenated by crawling through a hollow oak; Gitche Manito painted as an egg with points to the four winds: the world-egg/cosmic birth-giver as the omnipresent mother, *anima mundi*, a **quaternity-symbol that points to the self**, the outermost circumference and innermost center, like the atman "no bigger than a thumb" [pp.574-575, par. 550]. The Spirit of Evil as the serpent Kenabeek: "fear, negation, the adversary who opposes life in its struggle for eternal duration... the spirit of regression." **"For the hero, fear is a challenge and a task, because only boldness can deliver from fear. And if the risk is not taken, the meaning of life is somehow violated, and the whole future is condemned to hopeless staleness"** [p.575, par. 551].
- Last cantos: Chibiabos (joy) drowned and called back only as spirit; Kwasind (strength) lost; famine and Minnehaha's death foretold; Hiawatha sails west into the sunset toward the Northwest Wind and the Islands of the Blessed [pp.576-577, par. 552].

### The sunset, the two halves of life, and voluntary sacrifice [pp.577-580]
This is one of the book's central, self-contained statements (par. 553).
- **The sun and human fate:** the sun rises from the maternal sea, passes the zenith, sinks back into the all-regenerating night. "In the morning of life the son tears himself loose from the mother, from the domestic hearth, to rise through battle to his destined heights. Always he imagines his worst enemy in front of him, yet he carries the enemy within himself—a deadly longing for the abyss, a longing to drown in his own source." This death is "no external enemy" but his own inner longing for "all-knowing non-existence," even at the height of philosophy or art ("he seeks death, immobility, satiety, rest"). If he tarries (Peirithous), apathy and serpent-poison paralyze him; "if he is to live, he must fight and sacrifice his longing for the past in order to rise to his own heights." Having reached noon, he must "sacrifice his love for his own achievement, for he may not loiter" [pp.577-578].
- **Religions as psychotherapeutic systems:** the young person must sacrifice childhood and dependence on the physical parents "lest he remain caught body and soul in the bonds of unconscious incest"; the regressive tendency "has been consistently opposed from the most primitive times by the great psychotherapeutic systems which we know as the religions," which "seek to create an autonomous consciousness by weaning mankind away from the sleep of childhood" [p.578].
- **The afternoon:** the process allegorized as the water of life seeping away, one must bend ever deeper to reach the source. Resistance: "we begin to combat this tendency and erect barriers against the dark, rising flood of the unconscious and its enticements to regression, which all too easily takes on the deceptive guise of sacrosanct ideals, principles, beliefs." Holding the heights leads to "stagnation and desiccation of soul. Our convictions become platitudes ground out on a barrel-organ, our ideals become starchy habits, enthusiasm stiffens into automatic gestures. The source of the water of life seeps away." Introspection reveals accumulated wants, longings, fears: "a repulsive and sinister sight" [pp.578-579].
- **Forced versus voluntary sacrifice:** "the daemon throws us down, makes us traitors to our ideals... That is an unmitigated catastrophe, because it is an unwilling sacrifice. Things go very differently when the sacrifice is a voluntary one. Then it is no longer an overthrow, a 'transvaluation of values,' the destruction of all that we held sacred, but transformation and conservation." Forms age and crumble "unless they change. But change they can, for the invisible spark that generated them is potent enough for infinite generation." "No one should deny the danger of the descent, but it can be risked. No one need risk it, but it is certain that some one will. And let those who go down the sunset way do so with open eyes." Every descent is followed by an ascent; "a truth is valid in the end only if it suffers change and bears new witness in new images, in new tongues, like a new wine that is put into new bottles" [pp.579-580].
- *Hiawatha* is "well suited to bring into play the vast potentialities for archetypal symbolization," and its products always contain "the same old human problems" [p.580, par. 554].

### Wagner's Siegfried and Brünnhilde (second long amplification) [pp.580-593]
*(Jung spells it "Brünhilde" in the source; kept as in the text.)*
- Chiwantopel's cry reminds Miss Miller of Siegfried's feelings for Brünhilde. **Brünhilde acts the part of the symbolic "spirit-mother" (mother-imago), a helper, not a persecutor like Hera**; she favors the Sieglinde-Siegmund brother-sister incest and is banished by Wotan. Siegfried's birth from the sister-wife characterizes him as Horus, the reborn sun; the human parents are "vehicles for cosmic symbols." She sends Sieglinde east on the night sea journey [pp.580-581, pars. 555].
- **Dismemberment motif:** Siegmund's broken sword; life put together from broken pieces (Medea; Plato's pegs; Brahmanaspati as blacksmith). **The sword = solar power and spermatic Word** (sword from Christ's mouth in the Apocalypse). In the Rig-Veda prayer-word becomes a cow carrying the gods in her womb: "the Logos becomes a cow, i.e., a mother who bears the gods in her womb" (the Holy Ghost addressed as mother in the Acts of Thomas) [pp.581-582, pars. 556-558]. It is "always the mother-imago which proves to be the hero's greatest danger but is for that very reason the prime source of his deeds and of his ascent. His ascent signifies a renewal of the light and hence a rebirth of consciousness from the darkness" [p.582].
- **Brünhilde as a "split-off" from Wotan** (like Athena from Zeus; angel of Yahweh, Vohu Manah, Nabu, Hermes/Logos, Gibil, the Kore in the Acts of Thomas "Maiden, daughter of the light"). The Holy Ghost worshipped in feminine form ("Come, secret mother") in the Thomas Eucharist, whose context (liberating a woman from an "unchaste demon") shows the therapeutic meaning: transformation of a sexual obsession into recognition of the positive qualities of the feminine spirit. The Ophites' "Mother of All Living," Valentinian "Word of the Mother from Above" [pp.583-585, pars. 559-563].
- **Definition (par. 563):** **anima-figures attributed to masculine deities "without exception represent a dissociation in the masculine psyche—a 'split-off' with a tendency to lead an obsessive existence of its own."** This autonomy makes the anima anticipate the masculine consciousness's thoughts and decisions, so the man is repeatedly confronted with situations he has "apparently done nothing to provoke," "the situation of Wotan, and indeed of every hero who is unconscious of his own intriguing femininity" [pp.585-586]. Wotan's lament ("She herself was the creating womb of my wish"); Wotan has "entered into his own daughter in order to rejuvenate himself" (veiled; open in the "Entkrist" legend); Brünhilde takes the role of Isis and deprives the old man of power; "the instrument of fate is always the woman, who knows and reveals his secret thoughts" [pp.586-587].
- **Mime** (crippled chthonic dwarf foster-parent, like Osiris' crippled shadow rearing Horus) is a **masculine representative of the Terrible Mother** laying the poisonous worm; Siegfried longs for the "other mother" ("If only I knew what my mother was like"), finds a hint of her in the forest bird, and his longing for the mother-imago unwittingly exposes him to looking back, so that "the human mother... immediately changes into the death-dealing dragon" Fafner, guardian of the hoard [pp.587-589].
- **The treasure (par. 569):** "The mother apparently possesses the libido of the son (the treasure she guards so jealously), and this is in fact true so long as the son remains unconscious of himself." The treasure hard to attain lies in the mother-imago, i.e., the unconscious, and "points to something like a center of the total personality, of the psychic totality which consists of both conscious and unconscious"; Jung refers to his later works on the self. Rewards: eating Fafner's heart, the magic cap, understanding bird language ("a peculiar relationship to nature, which he now dominates by knowledge"), the hoard [p.589].
- **Etymology of *Hort* (hoard) (pars. 570, 579):** from a root meaning "hidden"; Kluge links it to Greek *keutho* ("hide") and perhaps *kysthos* (cavity, female genitals); Fick links it with words for belly/abdomen/viscera; Prellwitz and the Indo-European root *kevo* "to swell, be strong" relate *cavus*, *cavea*, *enceinte*, pregnancy. Jung's point: **the crevice in the earth, interpreted at the primitive level as the "mother," came to signify the place of the treasure, which corresponds to the etymology of hoard** [pp.589-590, 600-601].
- **Chasms and offerings (pars. 570-574):** Gaia's fissure at Athens with honey-cakes after Deucalion's flood; the *lacus Curtius*; Oropos well offerings; entrances to Hades at Eleusis, Hermione, the Areopagus; the Greek virginity test by a serpent in a cave; St. Sylvester and the Tarpeian dragon with 365 steps (and the "De promissionibus" story of a mechanical dragon with a sword) [pp.590-593]. Jung's interpretation: the flood flowing off into the fissure "back into the mother"; honey-cakes offered "that she may spare one from death" [p.590].
- **The hero shares the dragon's qualities (par. 575):** invulnerability, snake's eyes; Christ identified with the serpent lifted on the cross (John 3:14); "as a man with merely human thoughts and desires, who is ever striving back to childhood and the mother, he must die on the mother-tree." Jung states categorically he is not concerned with the theological point of view, and that the comparative procedure may offend newcomers, but "the unconscious 'mind' dismisses our logical concepts and moral values" with "hair-raising irrationalism," otherwise it could not fulfill its compensatory function [pp.593-594].
- **Christ and the self (par. 576):** "Christ, as a hero and god-man, signifies psychologically the self; that is, he represents the projection of this most important and most central of archetypes." The self "as a symbol of wholeness, is a *coincidentia oppositorum*, and therefore contains light and darkness simultaneously." In the Christ-figure the opposites are polarized into the "light" son of God and the devil; Christ and Anti-Christ lie very close together (Merezhkovsky's *Leonardo da Vinci*). The dragon legend "is an essential part of the hero's life and is therefore immortal." The rationalistic claim that the dragon is "artificial" is an apotropaic mechanism of schizophrenic patients (a dream of sun and moon made of oiled paper) [pp.594-595].
- **Hecate (pars. 577):** 365 steps = course of the sun and the cavern of death and rebirth; Diocletian's crypt to Hecate at Antioch. Hecate: spook-goddess of night, horse-riding, patron of riders; sends the Empusa; key, whip, dagger, torch; dogs; identified with Cerberus ("Heracles was really bringing the vanquished mother of death to the upper world"); "As the 'spirit-mother' she sends madness, the moonsickness. This idea is perfectly sensible, because most forms of lunacy consist of affections which amount to an invasion by the unconscious and an inundation of the conscious mind." The *leukophyllos* wand: the sacred tree that may not be touched. Mother of witchcraft (Medea), syncretized with Artemis, Brimo, Persephone, Rhea, Eileithyia; birth-goddess and marriage-goddess; **crossroads sacrifices: "Where the roads cross... the union of opposites, there is the 'mother'... Where the roads divide... the cleft—the symbol of the mother and at the same time the essence of what the mother means for us, namely cleavage and farewell"** [pp.595-598].
- Python and Leto's night-sea flight to Delos; Delphi's crevice; Jerusalem foundation-stone over the abyss; churches over caves and wells; catacombs "owe their importance not to legendary persecutions but to the cult of the dead"; burial in consecrated ground "a rendering back to the mother with the hope of resurrection"; honey-cake for Cerberus, obolus for Charon (second Cerberus; Anubis); dog = underworld serpent; Erinyes, Typhon and Echidna's offspring; **the snake-pit became a treasure slot** (Cos temple; coiled granite snake with a coin slot at Ptolemaïs): "Fear of the deadly maternal womb has become the guardian of the treasure of life" [pp.598-600, par. 578].
- **The treasure is life (par. 580):** "The treasure which the hero fetches from the dark cavern is life: it is himself, new-born from the dark maternal cave of the unconscious where he was stranded by the introversion or regression of libido" (Hindu fire-bringer Matarisvan, "he who swells in the mother"). **"The hero who clings to the mother is the dragon, and when he is reborn from the mother he becomes the conqueror of the dragon."** The snake: Philo's most spiritual creature, fiery, sloughing old age; in fact cold-blooded, unconscious, unrelated; both toxic and prophylactic, Agathodaimon (Christ and devil); in Gnosticism the brain-stem and spinal cord, "an excellent symbol for the unconscious" in its sudden, painful, frightening interventions. **"Taken purely as a psychologem the hero represents the positive, favourable action of the unconscious, while the dragon is its negative and unfavourable action—not birth, but a devouring; not a beneficial and constructive deed, but greedy retention and destruction"** [pp.600-602].

### Enantiodromia and the snake as degenerate and as regenerating [pp.602-616]
- **Enantiodromia (par. 581):** "Every psychological extreme secretly contains its own opposite or stands in some sort of intimate and essential relation to it. Indeed, it is from this tension that it derives its peculiar dynamism." The best "is the most threatened with some devilish perversion just because it has done the most to suppress evil." Linguistic evidence (*better* from *bass* "good"; related to *bad*); mythology (God in one fairytale version, devil in another); mysteries degenerating into debauches [p.602].
- **Anton Unternährer** (a nineteenth-century "blasphemer" and self-styled priapic divinity): his sayings on the "devil's communion" in brothels and on the sacred "members" are quoted. Jung: "History has numerous examples of how easily the mystery can turn into a sexual orgy just because it grew out of the opposite of the orgy"; the snake that in the mysteries entered the faithful, fecundating and spiritualizing them, retains its phallic significance (Ophite snake-kissing; certain modern sects) [pp.603-605, par. 584].
- **Clinical snake dreams (par. 585):** a patient dreamt a snake shot out of a cave and bit him in the genitals just when he was freeing himself from his mother-complex ("the moment he felt the impulse to go forward he also felt the pull of the bond to the mother"; cf. Attis); a woman filled with an enormous snake after a relapse; a woman with a snake stuck in her throat [pp.605-606].
- **Nietzsche's vision of the shepherd and the snake** (Zarathustra): a young shepherd with a black serpent hanging from his mouth bites its head off and is transfigured and laughing. Jung's reading (par. 586): the snake is the unconscious psyche entering the celebrant's mouth (Sabazios mysteries), firstly to stop him from talking too much and secondly to fill him with God (*entheos*). "The snake had already bitten fast, but fear was swifter and more violent: it bit off the snake's head and spat it out." The shepherd's wild hysterical laughter was because "he had dished the compensation from the unconscious," after which he "could now reckon without his host" with consequences Jung sees in *Zarathustra* and, he adds, the German nation [pp.606-608].
- **When the compensation is feared vs. accepted (par. 587):** "The unconscious insinuates itself in the form of a snake if the conscious mind is afraid of the compensating tendency of the unconscious, as is generally the case in regression. But if the compensation is accepted in principle, there is no regression, and the unconscious can be met half-way through introversion." Jung adds that Nietzsche's problem was insoluble: the compensation "appears in a form that cannot be accepted and could only be overcome by something that is equally impossible," as when "the unconscious has been resisted for too long on principle, and a wedge violently driven between instinct and the conscious mind" [p.608].
- **Introversion as creative (pars. 588-590):** "Through introversion... one is fertilized, inspired, regenerated, and reborn." **Prajapati's *tapas*** (Rig-Veda X, 121; Brahmanas): "he heated himself with his own heat" (Deussen), brooder and brooded one; as Hiranyagarbha he is the self-begotten cosmic egg, becomes his own womb. Self-incubation, self-castigation and introversion are closely related; immersion in oneself is "a penetration into the unconscious and at the same time asceticism," yielding for the Brahmanas the creation of the world and for the mystic the regeneration of the individual. Rig-Veda X, 129 ("the One... born through the force of fiery torment... love... the germ and the seed of knowledge") [pp.609-612].
- **"This philosophical view conceives the world as an emanation of libido"** (par. 591): Schreber's end of the world through introversion = withdrawing libido from the world, making it unreal; Schopenhauer's negation of the Primal Will; Goethe: "Is not the core of nature in the heart of man?" [p.612].
- **The hero as the snake (pars. 592-594):** the hero who renews the world and conquers death "personifies the world-creating power which, brooding on itself in introversion, coiled round its own egg like a snake, threatens life with its poisonous bite, so that the living may die and be born again from the darkness" (Nietzsche's basilisk egg poem). "The hero is himself the snake, himself the sacrificer and the sacrificed" (Christ and the Moses-serpent; the Ophite serpent saviour; Agathodaimon/Cacodaimon; heroes with snake's eyes). Cecrops half snake, half man; flaying ceremonies; Mani flayed, stuffed and hung; hanging = "symbol of unfulfilled longing or tense expectation ('suspense')" (Christ, Odin, Attis; Jesus ben Pandira and Jesus ben Stada; the Uganda ritual so the king may live forever: a Mamba clansman is beaten to death and flayed for a whip, after which the king may never see his mother again); Marsyas; Scythian horse/slave stuffing; Athenian stuffed ox at the plough [pp.612-615].
- **The zodiacal hero (par. 596):** the spring god-hero descends to winter's lowest point, overcomes it, passes the summer solstice and is overcome "as if by an unconscious longing for death." His end seems like the evil designs of the sinister mother laying the poisonous snake, but the mysteries say there is no contradiction: **"The bull is the father of the dragon and the dragon is the father of the bull"** [p.615]. Nietzsche's oasis-belly/whale poem ("Hail to his belly") is cited as the same truth [pp.616-617].
- **Siegfried meets Wotan; Erda (pars. 598-600):** Wotan, plagued by care laid by Erda (earth-mother, "the old serpent in his path"), is told "Thy name is not as thou sayest." Jung: Erda, like Isis demanding Ra's name, has robbed her son of the joy of life by "poisoned sting." The way out is Wotan's willing of the gods' end: "it is not the mother who lays the poisonous worm in our path, but life itself, which wills itself to complete the sun's course, to mount from morn to noon, and then, crossing the meridian, to hasten towards evening, no more at odds with itself, but desiring the descent and the end." Zarathustra: "He who has a goal and an heir desires death at the proper time." Jung finds Nietzsche's *amor fati* "somewhat overdone," like "an ailing Superman" always one jump ahead of fate; Siegfried is "more cautious" [pp.617-619, pars. 598-602].

### Siegfried and Brünnhilde: the anima and mother-imago, the hero's fate [pp.619-626]
- At the sleeping Brünnhilde, Siegfried's terror: "Mother! Mother! Remember me... Can this be fear?" Brünnhilde (daughter-anima of Wotan) is revealed as Siegfried's symbolical or spiritual mother, "thus confirming the psychological rule that the first carrier of the anima-image is the mother." **"The mother-imago, at first identical with the anima, represents the feminine aspect of the hero himself"** ("Thine own self am I"); as anima she is mother-sister-wife and as preexistent archetype has always loved him [pp.619-622, pars. 602-607].
- Water and submersion belong to the anima/mother-imago: "The water represents the maternal depths and the place of rebirth; in short, the unconscious in its positive and negative aspects. But the mystery of regeneration is of an awe-inspiring nature: it is a deadly embrace." The "wild raging woman," horse-woman conducting the dead; the orgiastic "Laughing let us be lost, laughing go down to death" echo Apuleius' *Occide moriturus*; "Light-giving love, laughing death" [pp.622-624, pars. 608-611].
- **The hero's fate:** Hagen the one-eyed Dark One's spear strikes the vulnerable spot; Wotan slays his son. The hero is "the ideal masculine type: leaving the mother, the source of life, behind him, he is driven by an unconscious desire to find her again." Every obstacle "wears the shadowy features of the Terrible Mother, who saps his strength with the poison of secret doubt and retrospective longing; and in every conquest he wins back again the smiling, loving and life-giving mother." A "contrapuntal modulation of feeling" [pp.624-625, par. 611].

### Method of myth-interpretation by dream-psychology [pp.625-628]
- "No part of the hero-myth is single in meaning, and at a pinch all the figures are interchangeable"; the only certain thing is that the myth exists and shows analogies with other myths. Myth-interpretation is "a tricky business"; earlier interpreters had only doubtful points of orientation (astronomical and meteorological data) [p.625].
- **Modern psychology's advantage:** it has opened a field (dreams, visions, fantasies, delusional ideas) that is "the matrix of all mythology," where the psychologist finds correspondences and can observe how such contents arise and function in a living organism. The same multiplicity of meaning and interchangeability appears in dreams; yet rules help: **"dreams generally compensate the conscious situation, or supply what is lacking to it. This very important principle of dream-interpretation also applies to myths."** Investigation of unconscious products reveals recognizable archetypal structures that coincide with myth-motifs, some of which "deserve the name of **dominants**": anima, animus, wise old man, witch, shadow, earth-mother, and the "organizing dominants," the self, the circle and the quaternity (the four functions or aspects of the self or of consciousness). Knowledge of these types makes myth interpretation easier and "puts it where it belongs, that is, on a psychic basis" [pp.625-626].
- **The hero myth as an unconscious drama seen in projection** (like Plato's cave). The hero, an archetype of the self, is godlike from the start; the self is "numinous, a sort of god." Jung discusses homoousia (same nature) versus homoiousia (similar nature): for psychology the self "is an imago Dei and cannot be distinguished from it empirically." The hero is "the protagonist of God's transformation in man; he corresponds to what I call the 'mana personality.'" Danger: the ego identifies with the hero, **psychic inflation**; hence ecclesiastical repugnance for the "inner Christ" is understandable as a preventive measure. The Indian has an equally homoousian idea of God (Brahman) and so is less endangered; the Christian "has far too little introspection to realize what modifications... the homoousia of the self (Atman) would involve." Jung adds these reflections only to put the numinosity of the hero archetype in perspective [pp.627-628, par. 612].

## VIII. The Sacrifice [pp.630-653, chapter continues in the next part]

### Chiwantopel's last speech and death [pp.630-631]
- "In all the world there is not a single one!" He prophesies that ten thousand moons later a pure-souled girl, fair of skin and hair, from another world, will understand; she will suffer, resist temptation; "in her dreams I shall come to her." "I have kept my body inviolate." After a lacuna in the source, a green viper stings him and then his horse, which succumbs first. He bids the horse "Farewell, faithful brother!" and thanks the serpent as "little sister, you have put an end to my wanderings!"; he prays that his body not become carrion; a volcano and earthquake bury him; his last cry is for "Ja-ni-wa-ma, thou that understandest me" [pp.630-631].

### Jung's reading of the final scene [pp.631-635]
- The prophecy echoes Hiawatha's end, where the poet adds the white man's Saviour (Jung adds a rueful aside about the Spaniards in Mexico and Peru and Indian wars) [p.631]. **Time-gap = psychic gap:** "ten thousand moons too late" means Miss Miller's ego is separated by a gulf from Chiwantopel, who is "wholly on the other side"; no union of conscious and unconscious (the "one thing needful... to create wholeness") is possible except in dreams; "every real love-relationship consists ultimately in the girl finding her hero, and the hero his soul, not in dreams, but in palpable reality" [pp.631-632, par. 614].
- "I have kept my body inviolate," "she will not yield," is a "touch me not" attitude dictated by her "ghostly lover," confirming all enterprises have remained dreams. **Awakening the animus** typically leads to a new instinct: "the image of earthly love pales before that of the heavenly." Love of the "heavenly bridegroom" or Sophia "is in fact that 'other,' equally natural instinct to cleave to the realities of the soul"; "the world-spurning passion of the 'spirit' is just as natural as the marriage-flight of insects" [p.632, par. 615].
- **One-sidedness changes only its sign:** "Whoever loves the earth and its glory, and forgets the 'dark realm,' or confuses the two (which is mostly what happens), has spirit for his enemy; and whoever flees from the earth and falls into the 'eternal arms' has life for an enemy." Chiwantopel, who personifies her otherworldliness, "falls foul of the green snake" (green = vegetation numen; snake = world of instinct, the vital processes least accessible; **snake dreams indicate a discrepancy between the conscious attitude and instinct**: "Look out! Danger ahead!") [p.633].
- **Chiwantopel is eliminated thoroughly** (snake bite, horse killed = animal vitality, volcanic burial): an attempt of the unconscious "to compensate and help the dangerous situation of the conscious mind." So drastic an annihilation contrary to the hero's usual mythological role suggests the author's personality is "threatened in the highest degree by an invasion from the unconscious (euphemistically conceived as a 'creative fantasy')," and she has not the slightest idea what is happening; Jung notes this indeed occurred a little later "with fatal results" [pp.633-634, par. 616].
- **Invasion and takeover (par. 617):** when the conscious mind gets stuck, "the unconscious takes over the forward-striving function, the process of transformation in time, and breaks the deadlock. The contents then pouring into consciousness are archetypal representations of what the conscious mind should have experienced if deadlock was to be avoided." Her emphasis on bodily inviolateness and avoiding grave-decay shows the wish to "stop the turning wheel." "Flight from life does not exempt us from the law of age and death." The neurotic evading life "wins nothing and only burdens himself with a constant foretaste of aging and dying." If libido cannot strive forward, "it strikes back along the other road and sinks into its own depths, working down to the old intimation of the immortality of all that lives, to the old longing for rebirth" [pp.634-635].

### Hölderlin: a case of regression through poetry [pp.635-653]
- Jung says Hölderlin follows this path "in his poetry and in his life" and lets the poems speak.
- **"To a Rose"** (mother-womb of nature eternal) and **"Hyperion's Song of Fate"** (the fateless gods breathing like sleeping infants, "chastely guarded in modest bud"): the rose = the beloved; being with her in the womb of nature = still in the mother, finding "eternal germination and renewal, a potential life... without his having to submit to the labor of giving them shape." Parallels: Plutarch's Osiris and Isis mating in the mother's womb; twin-in-womb motif in Frobenius' African Big Snake legend (twins become dragon-killers); Obatala and Odudua in a calabash; the sun born from a flower bud (Plutarch), Brahma from a lotus bud, Assam bud giving birth to the first human pair [pp.635-637, pars. 618-620]. "Hölderlin was never able to forget this first and greatest happiness whose haunting presence estranged him from real life" [p.637].
- **"Man" (poem):** man's "presumption" drives him from the mother's breast: he wants to resemble "Eternal nature, mother of gods, the terrible"; the child chooses the sacred vine for his nurse (Dionysian); he later digs caves and grubs in pits, his arrogance turning to fear [pp.637-640]. Jung notes the first hint of discord between poet and nature; the vine as nurse recalls Judah in Jacob's blessing; a Gnostic gem (she-ass suckling her foal, sign of Cancer) and the Palatine mock-crucifixion (ass's head) tie Dionysian and Christian legend (Justin Martyr's indignation); ass = Saturn = "second sun," Israel's star. "Hölderlin is mainly concerned with the Dionysian nature of man"; "terrible Mother" = *mater saeva cupidinum* = unbridled Nature = Dionysus, "also Nietzsche's god, although actually Nietzsche's original experience suggests rather the sinister huntsman, Wotan" [pp.640-641, pars. 621-623]. As a child of nature he falls into discord with her "precisely because he tries to resemble the 'mother of gods'... only the Dionysian *libido effrenata*" [p.641, par. 624].
- **"To Nature," "Palinode," "Empedocles," "In Memoriam," "Achilles":** the poems record childhood's golden unity with nature, the death of "the youthful world which was my shield," nostalgia, the leap of Empedocles into Etna ("I would follow the hero into the depths did not love hold me"), the renunciation in "In Memoriam," and the cry to the sea-mother Thetis in "Achilles" [pp.641-652].
- **Jung's comments:**
  - The loss of youth "has even taken away the golden glamour of Nature... But what robs Nature of its glamour, and life of its joy, is the habit of looking back for something that used to be outside, instead of looking inside, into the depths of the depressive state." **Regression is also an involuntary introversion**, since the past is a memory and so a psychic content. "Depression should therefore be regarded as an unconscious compensation whose content must be made conscious if it is to be fully effective. This can only be done by consciously regressing along with the depressive tendency and integrating the memories so activated into the conscious mind—which was what the depression was aiming at in the first place" [p.646, par. 625].
  - "Empedocles" reveals the poet's longing for the maternal depths and the "krater" of rebirth (pearls dissolved in wine; Horace on Empedocles leaping into Etna); the libido is still held by an object ("love still holds him back") [pp.646-648, pars. 626-627]. The last stanza of "In Memoriam" "portends disaster: a gazing towards the other land"; love no longer holds, bonds broken, "loudly he calls for help to the mother" [p.650, par. 628].
  - The poems show "the poet's steady withdrawal and increasing estrangement from life, his gradual submersion in the abyss of memory," after which "the apocalyptic vision of Patmos bursts upon us... swirled round by mists from the abyss, by the gathering clouds of insanity bred by the mother" [p.652, par. 629].
  - **"Patmos":** "Near is God / And hard to apprehend. / But where danger is, there / Arises salvation also." Jung: the libido has sunk to a depth where "the danger is great" (Faust, "The Mothers"); there God is near, "the maternal vessel of rebirth, the seeding-place where he could renew his life." Life can be lived intensely despite lost youth "if looking back to what is already moribund does not hamper your step." **Looking back is fine if it does not stop at externals but asks "where the fascination of the past really springs from"**: the golden haze of childhood memories "arises not so much from the objective facts as from the admixture of magical images which are more intuited than actually conscious." The Jonah parable reproduces this: a person sinks into childhood memories, seems in deepest darkness, then has "unexpected visions of a world beyond"; the "mystery" is "the stock of primordial images which everybody brings with him as his human birthright, the sum total of inborn forms peculiar to the instincts," which Jung has called "this 'potential' psyche the **collective unconscious**." Activated by regressive libido, "there is a possibility of life being renewed, and also of its being destroyed" [pp.652-654, pars. 630-631]. *(The text of the range breaks off here, at the beginning of p.654; the sentence "Regression carried to its logical conclusion means a linking..." continues in the next part.)*

## Gaps and cautions

- **Footnote apparatus** is not extracted: the text file has footnote numbers inline but not the notes' text (several citations to sources are thus unverified, and Jung's precise sources for many mythological claims cannot be checked from this part).
- **Captions and plates:** figure captions appear in the text (e.g., fig. 26-40) but plates are referred to only as "pl." numbers; the images themselves are not described. I did not visually check any page.
- **Minor garbling in the source:** par. 449 on p.493 reads "alrof the unconscious to be eady moribund" (words from an adjacent line displaced; sense: "already moribund"); par. 462 p.481 shows "touchthey could get at him her too closely" (displaced words; sense: she fears the ideal might "touch her too closely"); p.517 shows a stray "Fig. 31. The m" in the Nietzsche quotation; p.583 "emisslonging for the mother-imagoary or agent" (displaced words; sense: "emissary or agent"). I resolved these only where the surrounding sense was unambiguous and flagged them here. `[garbled in source]` is applicable to these four spots.
- **Spelling:** the text has "Brünhilde" (kept); Jung's British spellings in quotations have been converted to American in this file, so verify wording against the original before reuse.
- **Case material:** the Miller fantasy is presented only as quoted in the range; Jung says (p.634) that a fatal outcome followed "a little later," but gives no details in this range.
- **Interpretive notes marked by me:** none added beyond clarifications in brackets and the footnote note on page numbers.
- The Hölderlin poems are summarized, not reproduced; Jung's commentary on each is partly inferential (he draws it from the poems themselves, not from biography) and the range ends before the next part's remaining discussion.
