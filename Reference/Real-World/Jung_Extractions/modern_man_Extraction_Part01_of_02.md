## Overview of this range

The range holds the translator's preface and the first five essays of an eleven-essay collection (essays 1 to 4 and about the first third of 5; the rest of 5 and essays 6 to 11 are in Part 2). Essays 1 to 3 concern the practice of psychotherapy: how to read dreams (as compensations for the conscious attitude, interpreted through the dreamer's own context and not through a fixed doctrine); the four stages of treatment (confession, explanation, education, transformation), culminating in the demand that the therapist himself be changed; and Jung's own method for people stuck in the second half of life (dreams, mythological amplification, painting active fantasies, and the shift of the personality's center from ego to self). Essay 4 offers his typology of two attitudes (introversion, extraversion) and four functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition). Essay 5 begins his account of the stages of life: consciousness as the source of problems, the development of the ego, the problems of youth, and the reversal at mid-life, pictured as the sun's noon. Recurring ideas: the unconscious as a neutral, self-regulating, compensating part of the psyche; the relativity of every school's truth (Freud, Adler, Jung); the primacy of the individual's own life-form; psychic actuality ("everything that acts is actual"); and the need for a psychology (and a culture) that turns inward.

**Key terms used in this range (Jung's own):** unconscious; compensation; assimilation; context (of a dream image); symbol versus sign; relatively fixed symbols; archetype; mana; complex (autonomous); shadow; transference; fixation; catharsis; individuation; collective unconscious; self versus ego; active fantasy; indicium; psychology of the morning and afternoon of life; extraversion/introversion; thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition; rational versus non-rational functions; inferior function; "also-I"; psychic actuality.

## Front matter: Contents list and Translator's Preface (Cary F. Baynes, Zürich, March 1933)

**Contents of the whole book** (11 essays): 1 Dream-Analysis in its Practical Application; 2 Problems of Modern Psychotherapy; 3 The Aims of Psychotherapy; 4 A Psychological Theory of Types; 5 The Stages of Life; 6 Freud and Jung - Contrasts; 7 Archaic Man; 8 Psychology and Literature; 9 The Basic Postulates of Analytical Psychology; 10 The Spiritual Problem of Modern Man; 11 Psychotherapists or the Clergy. Translators: W. S. Dell and Cary F. Baynes. [s.2]

**Translator's Preface** [s.2]. The preface says the Western world is thought to stand on the verge of a "spiritual rebirth," a basic change of attitude toward life's values, after a long period of outward expansion and a turn from facts as such to their meaning and value for individuals. Expectations of that change split into camps:
- Upholders of revealed religion expect a renaissance of Catholicism or Protestantism, with people returning to the Church to be comforted after the post-war disillusionment.
- A second group expects the total destruction of religion as a relic of superstitious barbarism, replaced by "enlightenment" through knowledge of economics and technology, with the intellect as arbiter of fate.
- Between these extremes is a middle group: people who have outgrown the Church as exemplified in Christianity but do not deny that a religious attitude to life is as essential to them as belief in science. They have experienced the soul "as vividly as the body," in ways neither traditional theology nor materialism explains. They hope that knowledge of the laws of the psyche will give a new attitude without regression to veiled medieval theology or to nineteenth-century ideology.
- Jung is said to address this last group, synthesizing his psychiatric and analytic experience into information usable by everyone. All essays but one began as lectures; four had separate German publications. Thanks are given to Violet de Laszlo (for help on "Psychotherapists or the Clergy") and to Dr. and Mrs. Jung for reading the translations.

## Essay 1. "Dream-Analysis in Its Practical Application" (written 1931) [s.2]

**Purpose and premise.** The essay deals only with practical treatment. Dream-analysis stands or falls with the hypothesis of the unconscious; without it a dream is a "freak of nature," a heap of day-residue memory fragments. Jung laments that in 1931, after Carus, Kant ("immeasurable field of obscure ideas"), Leibniz, Janet, Flournoy and Freud, the unconscious is still disputed. Dream-analysis is a method for uncovering unconscious contents causally related to a neurosis. [s.2]

### A. What dreams can and cannot show

- **Initial dreams** (the dreams reported at the very start of treatment) often bring unconscious causal factors to light clearly. [s.2]
- **Case of the self-made man with "mountain-sickness"** (dizziness, nausea, heavy head, breathlessness; a peasant's son who rose to a high post and stalled at the threshold of further advancement). Two dreams: (1) he returns to his native village and walks past former schoolmates pretending not to know them; one says "He doesn't often come back to our village" (meaning: you forget how far down you began). (2) He rushes to catch a train, loses baggage and a brief-case, arrives as the long S-curved train leaves; he sees the driver open the throttle on the curve so the rear coaches derail; he wakes in terror. Reading: frantic haste to rise further; the driver pushes ahead heedlessly and the rear coaches (the neurosis) overturn. He should have rested on his achievement. Circumstances prevented treatment; he ignored the warning and met a real professional wreck as the dream foretold. [s.2]
- **Principle:** "The dream gives a true picture of the subjective state, while the conscious mind denies that this state exists, or recognizes it only grudgingly." The dream is an involuntary psychic process not controlled by the conscious outlook. Jung treats dreams "on a plane with physiological fact" (sugar in urine means sugar, not something he expected): dreams are facts, valuable for diagnosis. They also give prognosis and show where treatment should begin ("the patient must be prevented from going full steam ahead"). [s.2]
- **Against purely causal (Freudian) interpretation.** Jung accepts that some neuroses are traumatic in origin but denies all are, or that all arise from a crucial childhood event. The causalistic approach forces the doctor to ask only "Why?" and to neglect "What for?"; the patient may spend years searching memory for a hypothetical childhood event while present matters are neglected. Dreams also offer prognosis, suggest treatment, and often concern the patient's attitude to the doctor. [s.2]
- **Three frontier dreams (one patient, three analysts).** (1) "I must cross the frontier... no one can tell me where the boundary lies": treatment failed and was soon broken off. (2) Black night, cannot find the custom house, small light far away, valley and dark wood, someone clings to her "like a madman": she left after weeks, disoriented by the analyst's unconscious identification with her. (3) With Jung: she has already crossed into a Swiss custom-house with a handbag and "nothing to declare"; the official pulls out two full-sized mattresses. She married during treatment amid violent resistance; the cause of that resistance appears nowhere in the dreams. The dreams anticipate the analytical situation, which a purely causal reading would miss. The first doctor understood and sent her on; the second dream made her leave; the third reported the frontier already crossed, which encouraged her. [s.2]

### B. Obscurity, understanding, and suggestion

- Initial dreams are often transparent; later dreams grow blurred. A dream that stays clear means an important part of the personality is untouched. The "unintelligible" dream reflects the doctor's lack of understanding: "In themselves, dreams are clear"; the doctor should not hastily blame the patient's resistance. The psychiatrist who calls a patient "confused" often projects his own confusion. It is therapeutically important for the analyst to admit not understanding sometimes, since "nothing is more unbearable for the patient than to be always understood"; otherwise the patient clings to the doctor's supposed insight, loses sense of reality, falls into stubborn transference. [s.2]
- **Understanding is subjective and must be mutual.** If the analyst understands and the patient does not, Jung stresses his own lack of understanding. What is needed is agreement as "the fruit of joint reflection." Pronouncing a theoretically sound but unaccepted interpretation is practically wrong and may cripple the patient's development. Imposing a truth appeals only to the brain; helping the patient grow up to it reaches the heart. [s.2]
- **Suggestion** is a "kind of magic that works in the dark and makes no ethical demands upon the personality," with serious limits and a bad effect on independence of character. The analytic approach (widening consciousness: bringing unconscious parts of personality to conscious discrimination and criticism) is of a higher order because it "is a challenge to the ethical sense, a call to arms that must be answered by the whole personality." Unconscious suggestion cannot be fully excluded. [s.2]
- **Rule:** an interpretation that does not win the patient's assent is invalid; search until a formulation does. Treat every dream as a new departure with no preconceived theory, ready "to construct a totally new theory of dreams" each time. [s.2]

### C. Against the wish-fulfillment view; theory and its limits

- The view that dreams are fulfillments of suppressed wishes is "superseded." Dreams can express "ineluctable truths, philosophical pronouncements, illusions, wild fantasies, memories, plans, anticipations, irrational experiences, even telepathic visions." Almost half of life is spent in a more or less unconscious state; the dream is "specifically the utterance of the unconscious." Consciousness is the "daylight realm" characterized by "concentration, limitation and exclusion"; the unconscious is a "nocturnal realm" probably as rich or richer. [s.2]
- Do not pare a dream down to a doctrine; patients may parrot the analyst's jargon even in dreams; the unconscious can seem to "strangle the physician in the coils of his own theory." Theory is a rule of thumb; Jung assumes dreams are hypothetically meaningful in order to find courage to work on them. Warning to the interpreter (paradoxical): "Do anything you like, only don't try to understand!" [s.2]

### D. Method: context, not free association

- First task with an obscure dream: establish the **context** with minute care, i.e., "a careful and conscious illumination of those chains of association that are directly connected with particular images," not "a boundless sweep of free associations." Free association leads to one's complexes from any starting point (a newspaper sentence or "Keep out" sign), not to the dream's meaning. Keep close to the dream images. [s.2]
- Technique: for a "deal table" image, ask: "Suppose I had no idea what the words 'deal table' mean. Describe this object and give me its history..." If the dreamer cannot associate anything, that darkness is itself meaningful. Return to the image again and again. [s.2]
- **Facade critique:** the "manifest" dream-picture is the dream itself and contains the "latent" meaning; a house front betrays the plan of the building. A dream is like an unintelligible text we cannot yet read, not a facade hiding something. "Dream-facade" really names the dreamer's or doctor's own non-understanding. [s.2]
- Every interpretation is hypothetical. A single obscure dream can rarely be interpreted with certainty; **a dream series** corrects earlier mistakes and reveals basic themes. Patients keep a written record of dreams plus context material; later they work out interpretations themselves, learning "to consult the unconscious without the doctor's help." [s.2]
- **Dreams as matters of life and death:** Jung's older Zürich colleague (who teased him about dreams) dreamed of climbing a snowy mountain in marvelous weather, ecstatic, and then climbing on into empty air. Jung begged him not to climb alone and to take two guides on his word of honor. Two months later an avalanche buried him (rescued by a military patrol); three months after that he stepped off a rock wall and fell on his friend; both died. "That was ecstasis in the full meaning of the word." [s.2]

### E. Assimilation, the unconscious not a monster, compensation

- Dreams reveal hidden factors of personality which disturb waking life as symptoms; treatment must work through the unconscious. The only known way: "a thorough-going, conscious assimilation of unconscious contents," meaning "a mutual interpenetration of conscious and unconscious contents," not a one-sided valuation or deformation of the unconscious by consciousness. [s.2]
- Jung criticizes the Freudian image of the "infantile-perverse-criminal" unconscious and of primitive man as a wild beast, and asks whether the World War has not shown consciousness to be "even more devilish and perverse than the unconscious." A critic charged that assimilation would undermine culture and exalt primitivity; Jung answers that this rests on the false belief that the unconscious is a monster, born of fear of nature and life. Freud's "sublimation" was invented to save us from imaginary claws; what actually exists "cannot be alchemistically sublimated." [s.2]
- **The unconscious is "a thing of nature that is perfectly neutral as far as moral sense, aesthetic taste and intellectual judgement go."** Danger arises from a hopelessly false conscious attitude and from repression; assimilation ends dissociation of personality and anxiety. Overwhelming of consciousness is likeliest when the unconscious is excluded by repression or depreciated. [s.2]
- **Compensation:** "The psyche is a self-regulating system that maintains itself in equilibrium as the body does." Excess on one side calls forth compensatory activity ("Too little on one side results in too much on the other"). Hence the interpretive rule: **"What conscious attitude does it compensate?"** The dream cannot be read with certainty without knowing the conscious situation (which tells whether a content has a plus or minus sign). Repressing a compensation (like repressing thirst) fails; if someone replaced the conscious outlook with the dictates of the unconscious, the former would be repressed and reappear as an unconscious compensation, with the unconscious reversing its face and becoming "timidly reasonable." The unconscious is not explosive in itself; it becomes so through the repressions of a self-sufficient or cowardly conscious outlook. [s.2]
- **Case: the father drunk at the wheel.** A young man dreams his careful, admired father drives a new car clumsily, wrecks it and laughs, dead drunk. Not an expression of hidden hatred (no neurotic evidence), but, asking "What for?", a compensation: the relation to the father is too good (a fils à papa, still living a "provisional life"); the unconscious lowers the father and raises the son so he can contrast himself with the father and become aware of himself. The interpretation won his spontaneous assent and did no violence to his feelings. Lesson: do no violence to the conscious personality's real values; assimilation is "never a question of 'this or that,' but of 'this and that'"; do not embark on a "Bolshevist experiment which puts the lowest on top." [s.2]

### F. Symbols versus signs; relatively fixed symbols; archetypes

- Dream symbols should be taken as true symbols, "expressions of something not yet consciously recognized or conceptually formulated," considered against the dreamer's philosophical, religious and moral convictions and immediate state of consciousness, rather than as fixed signs or symptoms. Yet there exist **relatively fixed symbols** whose meaning cannot be reduced to anything known; without them the structure of the unconscious could not be described. The indefinite content marks the symbol against the mere sign. [s.2]
- Critique of Freud: the sexual "symbols" are really "signs" for a supposedly definitive "sexuality," a term so elastic it covers glandular activity at one end and the highest reaches of the spirit at the other, "an indeterminable or variable x." Jung prefers to regard a symbol as "the announcement of something unknown, hard to recognize and not to be fully determined." Phallic symbols: the phallus is itself a symbolic image (cited: Kranefeldt); primitives use it as a ritual symbol of the creative **mana**, power of healing and fertility, "that which is unusually potent" (cited: Lehmann), and do not confuse it with the penis. Equivalents in myth and dreams listed: bull, ass, pomegranate, yoni, he-goat, lightning, horse's hoof, dance, magical cohabitation in the furrow, menstrual fluid. Underlying all is an archetypal content best expressed by the mana symbol. [s.2]
- Scientifically each such symbol would be referred to an archetype, but in practice that may be a grave blunder; the therapist should look for meaning in relation to the conscious situation and treat symbols "as if they were not fixed," avoiding routine and dogmatism. [s.2]
- **Case: the seventeen-year-old girl** (one specialist suspected progressive muscular atrophy, another hysteria). Dreams: she comes home at night and sees her mother hanging from the chandelier, swinging in a cold wind; a frightened horse tears through the house and jumps from a fourth-floor hall window. Interpretation of the equivalent figures: **mother** = archetype of origin, nature, passive creator, matter, the lower body and vegetative functions, the unconscious and instinctive life, the vessel or container (uterus), the foundation of consciousness; darkness and anxiety; includes part of the Chinese **yin**. This is "a bequest from the past," kept alive by language and inherited in the psyche's structure, not acquired by the individual. **Horse** = the non-human, sub-human psyche, the unconscious; seers and speakers in folklore; a beast of burden tied to the mother archetype (Valkyries, Trojan horse); dynamic power, locomotion, instinct, panic; sorcery; the black night horse heralds death. Meaning: "the unconscious life destroys itself" / "the animal life destroys itself." The dream does not say the individual dies (dreaming of one's own death is "no serious matter"); when it is truly about death "the dream speaks another language." The prognosis of fatal organic disease was borne out. [s.2]
- Relatively fixed symbols are determined scientifically only through comparative study of mythology, folklore, religion and language. The evolutionary stages of the psyche are "more clearly discernible in the dream than in consciousness"; dream images and instincts come from the most primitive levels of nature. Assimilation brings consciousness back into harmony with natural law and leads the patient "to the rediscovery of the law of his own being." [s.2]

### G. Closing

Analysis builds, from the unconscious's materials, an edifice completed in restoring the total personality. Successive assimilation points beyond cure to **individuation**, "the bringing into reality of the whole human being," perhaps the first urge to life. Physicians usually see only the pathological phase and lose the patient at cure; one would need to study the normal process of change over years. Analysis breaks into a purposeful continuous development and singles out phases that may seem to follow opposing courses, so casuistic comparisons between analyses yield confusion. Jung stays with rudiments and practical considerations. [s.2]

## Essay 2. "Problems of Modern Psychotherapy" [s.2]

**Summary.** Jung surveys the field of "analytical psychology" (his umbrella term) through four stages of treatment: **confession, explanation, education, transformation**. Each stage answers the limits of the one before; none replaces the others; the fourth demands that the doctor himself be transformed, which turns a medical method into a method of self-education for healthy people too. [s.2]

### A. Terminology and the plurality of psychologies
- In popular thought "psychotherapy" equals "psychoanalysis," but strictly that word belongs to Freud's method of explaining symptoms through repressed impulses, with his theory of sexuality. Adler calls his approach "individual psychology"; Jung calls his "analytical psychology," meant as the general conception embracing psychoanalysis, individual psychology and other efforts. [s.2]
- The many rival "psychologies" are mutually incomprehensible; when many remedies are prescribed for one disease, none works with certainty; the multiplicity "amounts to a confession of perplexity." Nietzsche's phrase: the mind is a "horned" problem. Jung raises, without answering, why interest in the psyche as something to be experienced has suddenly arisen (it underlies theosophy, occultism, astrology). Modern psychology bears the stamp of the consulting room, borrowing biology's postulates, which fosters hostility with philosophy, history and classical learning; the medical and biological nomenclature widens the gap between nature and mind. The four-stage scheme is offered as provisional, "as arbitrary as a surveyor's triangulation." [s.2]

### B. Stage 1: Confession (catharsis)
- The prototype is the religious confessional (a common psychic root, not a causal descent). When man could conceive sin, he concealed it; repression arose. **Secrets** act like a "psychic poison" that alienates the keeper from the community. In small doses secrets are a remedy and a precondition of individual differentiation (mystery cults, early Christian sacraments such as baptism were treated as mysteries). A secret shared with several is beneficial; **a merely private secret** is destructive, like a burden of guilt. A secret one is conscious of harms less than one concealed even from oneself, which splits off as an autonomous **complex** with its own fantasy-life. "Fantasy" is spontaneous psychic activity that wells up when conscious repression relaxes, as in sleep (dreams). Unconscious contents are not only repressed former conscious contents: the unconscious has contents of its own growing upward. [s.2]
- Effects of near-threshold contents: slips of tongue and pen, memory lapses, clumsiness, misunderstandings, "hallucinations of memory," and all neurotic symptoms (exception: shell-shock). Example: patients with an unadmitted suicidal urge who fainted before a car, swallowed corrosive sublimate thinking it cough medicine, took to dangerous acrobatics; when made conscious, common sense could intervene. [s.2]
- **Withholding emotion** is another concealment. Self-restraint practiced within a secret society or initiation (ascetic continence, stoic endurance) is healthy; as a purely private matter it is harmful (ugly moods, irritability of the over-virtuous). Men excel at hiding emotions from themselves; women with few exceptions are averse to doing this violence to themselves. Possible neurosis types: hysteric (free with emotion, keeper of a secret) versus hardened psychasthenic (cannot digest emotions). "Nature decidedly abhors a vacuum": tepid harmony from withheld emotion is unbearable. Secrets and restraint practiced in communion with others satisfy nature; only the private kind sickens. [s.2]
- Hiding one's insufficiency is a "sin in the eyes of nature"; as is living entirely on one's inferior side. True, unstereotyped confession breaks the wall that shuts a person out of feeling "a man among men." Greek mysteries saying: **"Give up what thou hast, and then thou wilt receive."** The term **catharsis** comes from Greek initiation rites; the early method put the patient (with or without hypnosis) in touch with the "hinterland of his mind," a state like what Eastern yoga calls meditation or contemplation, except that the analytic aim is to observe the spontaneously arising shadowy images and feelings. [s.2]
- **Shadow:** what is inferior "belongs to me as my shadow and gives me substance and mass. How can I be substantial if I fail to cast a shadow?" Becoming conscious of the shadow reminds one one is human like any other. Keeping it private only yields partial cure; the goal is full confession: heart confirmation and release of suppressed emotion, "no merely intellectual acknowledgement." [s.2]
- Jung does not make confession a general maxim (a common mutual confession of sins is bad taste); the point is the systematic emphasis on its significance. Limits of catharsis: (1) complicated, highly conscious patients firmly anchored in consciousness resist being pushed from it; (2) after cure the patient cannot leave the physician (binding), and a forced break causes relapse; others become fascinated by their own inner hinterland and continue catharsis alone at the cost of adaptation, like **Theseus and Pirithous**, who sat to rest in Hades and grew to the rock. [s.2]

### C. Stage 2: Explanation (Freud's method)
- Both problem groups meet at **fixation**. The patient binds to the physician as to a father: **transference** (Freud's term), a childish dependence that conscious insight cannot correct; abnormal is its obstinacy. Cause: unconscious fantasies of "incestuous" character. Jung notes that they are often never conscious (not simply "repressed"), and that dragging them out is almost like a surgical operation, unavoidable because analysis itself induces an abnormal transference. Catharsis restores contents accessible to consciousness; explanation clears up the transference and exposes contents nearly inaccessible to it. Another group of patients attaches not to the doctor but to their own unconscious, where the parental image is seen as a fantasy yet exerts the same attraction. Highly cultivated patients resist catharsis because they were already identified with their parents and draw from this identification their authority and critical power. Transference must be interpreted: dreams supply the material; Freud, in studying wishes incompatible with consciousness, found incest contents and "all the filth of which human nature is capable." [s.2]
- **Assessment of Freud.** The end-product is a detailed elaboration of man's **shadow-side**, an antidote to idealistic illusions; opponents who hold illusions on principle object, but some opponents object only to a portrait from the shadow side alone ("the essential thing is not the shadow, but the body which casts it"). Freud's "reductive" explanations lead "backward and downward" and destroy if exaggerated; yet psychology learned human works, institutions and convictions also have a black side. Examples: Salomon Reinach's explanation of the Last Supper through primitive totemism; the incest theme in Greek myths. The horror felt arises from a naive belief that "there can be heights without corresponding depths"; "when carried to extremes, opposites meet" (the enantiodromia theme, though the word is not used here). The error (Freud's own) is to think the radiant ceases to exist because it was explained from the shadow; "the shadow belongs to the light as the evil belongs to the good, and vice versa." Jung welcomes the exposure as a pendulum-swing forcing a philosophical relativism like Einstein's in physics, "fundamentally a truth of the far East." [s.2]
- **Ideas as forces:** "Nothing influences our conduct less than do intellectual ideas." But ideas that express psychic experience arising in regions as far apart as East and West are forces beyond logical justification and moral sanction, "always stronger than man"; man believes he moulds them but they mould him and make him their unwitting mouthpiece. [s.2]
- **Effect of explanation:** the patient sees the childishness of his claims on the doctor; an inflated patient accepts a more modest, wholesome insecurity; claims on others become self-responsibility; he turns to adaptation and progressive work, and turns away from the unconscious as a source of weakness. [s.2]

### D. Stage 3: Education (Adler's method)
- Insight suffices only for morally sensitive persons with driving force; the most thorough explanation can leave "an intelligent but still incapable child." Freud's pleasure principle is one-sided, especially in later development; it fits the easily adapted better than the unadapted who crave power and importance (example: the older brother who follows his father versus the overshadowed younger brother goaded by ambition). **Adler** explains many neuroses by the **urge to power**: patients "arrange" symptoms and exploit neurosis for fictitious importance, with transference and fixation serving the will to power (the "masculine protest" against fancied subjection); neurotics tilt at windmills. Adler is an **educator** (Freud is an investigator and interpreter): he takes the extra step of social education and holds adaptation and normalization indispensable; hence his neglect (at times denial) of the unconscious, a pendulum reaction to Freud. As early Buddhism recognized no gods to free itself from its inheritance of nearly two million, psychology must renounce Freud's essentially negative approach to the unconscious. Understanding causes does little to cure; neurotic habits yield only to new habits through exercise and an "educating will." Adler appeals to clergymen and teachers; Freud to physicians and intellectuals, "bad nurses and educators." The ill-formed tree "must be trained with the gardener's art upon the trellis." [s.2]
- **The sense of finality.** Each stage feels final (all confessed; all explained; all trained), which explains people using catharsis who never heard of dream interpretation, Freudians who do not understand Adler, Adlerians who reject the unconscious. Each stage rests on a basic truth, and since the world is rich in delusions a truth is priceless and defended with fanaticism. "Each of us can carry the torch of knowledge but a part of the way": we are exponents of psychic needs of our day, not creators of our truths; hence a "profound and superpersonal continuity of the human mind." The doctor is also a man and unwittingly does explanation and education while practicing catharsis. The three stages co-exist, "no more invalidate each other than do confession and absolution." [s.2]

### E. Stage 4: Transformation
- Fills the remaining deficit: the need that goes beyond being a "normal adapted social being." "Normal" is a restriction to the average, a splendid ideal for the unsuccessful but "the bed of Procrustes" for those of above-average ability, causing "unbearable boredom, infernal sterility and hopelessness"; hence "there are many people who become neurotic because they are only normal, as there are people who are neurotic because they cannot become normal." Their deepest need is to lead "abnormal" lives. A person finds fulfillment only in what he lacks. "What sets one free is for another a prison." Practical psychology offers no generally valid recipes; the physician should hold assumptions as hypothetical. [s.2]
- **Mutual influence.** The relation between doctor and patient is personal within the frame of professional treatment: two persons, neither a fixed magnitude, each with a conscious field and an indefinite unconscious sphere. Their meeting is "like the contact of two chemical substances: if there is any reaction, both are transformed." "You can exert no influence if you are not susceptible to influence." Hiding behind professional authority forfeits an important organ of information. Counter-transference is the best known of the doctor's unconscious changes; the old image of the **demon of sickness** (the healer subdues the demon passed to him, at some cost to himself). The stronger and more stable personality decides the issue, but Jung has seen patients prove stronger, usually to the doctor's disadvantage. After a quarter-century of practice he insists (and Freud seconded) **the analyst must himself be analyzed**: the doctor "is just as much in analysis" as the patient; if inaccessible to influence he loses influence; if influenced only unconsciously he sees the patient wrongly. [s.2]
- **Rule: "be the man through whom you wish to influence others."** The doctor must meet his own therapeutic demands (overcome infantilism if he believes in that, abreact if he believes in abreaction, reach consciousness if he believes in it). "The fact of being convinced, and not the subject-matter of conviction," carries weight. The doctor must work on himself with the same relentlessness, consistency and perseverance. Resistances: self-work seems unpractical, self-preoccupation seems morbid, and it is painful (he will find things opposed to normalization or that persist despite explanation, perhaps an inferior side that blights his authority); he will also find that ultimate questions cannot be solved by any treatment, and that expecting solutions from others is remaining childish. Image: a man with a running abscess is not fit to perform an operation. [s.2]
- **Consequences.** The doctor's ethical attitude becomes unavoidable; the psyche is "not only the object but also the subject; not only a function of the brain, but the sine qua non of consciousness itself." The method becomes self-education: "The medical diploma is no longer the crucial thing, but human quality instead." Analytical psychology is freed from the consulting room and may fill the void that marks the Western psyche relative to the East: Occidentals learned to tame and subject the psyche but not its methodical development; at a higher cultural level one must forgo compulsion for self-development, and analytical psychology can provide a foundation. It serves the healthy as well. Jung ends by saying that between this hope and the present is an "abyss over which no bridge is to be found. We have yet to build it stone by stone." [s.2]

## Essay 3. "The Aims of Psychotherapy" [s.2]

**Summary.** Jung gives the limits of the Freudian and Adlerian standpoints, describes his own practice with people stuck in the second half of life, and sets out a method: work with initial dreams, amplify with mythological parallels, then have the patient paint or draw active fantasies, which shifts the center of the personality from ego to self. [s.2]

### A. Opening: agreement and disagreement
- Neuroses are "functional psychic disturbances" to be cured by psychic means, but nothing agreed beyond that. Divergent opinions arise because they correspond to a real disposition or experience; to exclude them as wrong would do violence to empirical material. Freud's success reflects a current of the **collective psyche** (cited: Havelock Ellis, Auguste Forel, Anthropophyteia, post-Victorian Anglo-Saxon attitudes, French realists); Adler's likewise (an urge to power from inferiority). Both truths correspond to psychic realities; neither is the sole truth; Jung applies both as far as their relative validity goes, and holds his own views equally relative, being the exponent of a certain predisposition. [s.2]
- **Failures teach, successes confirm mistakes.** After almost thirty years, Jung's failures impress him more than successes: "Almost anybody, from the primitive medicine-man and the prayer-healer up, can gain successes in psychotherapy." One can live with an inadequate theory, not with inadequate methods. [s.2]

### B. Morning and afternoon of life
- Difficulty arose chiefly with patients **over forty**. "We may distinguish between a psychology of the morning of life and a psychology of its afternoon." Young life is unfolding and striving toward concrete ends; the youthful neurosis stems from hesitation or shrinking back. The older life is marked by contraction of forces, affirmation of what has been achieved, and curtailment of growth; the older neurosis stems from clinging to an out-of-season youthful attitude. "Just as the youthful neurotic is afraid of life, so the older one shrinks back from death." Resistance, repression, transference and "guiding fictions" mean one thing in the young and another in the old; the age of the patient is "a most important indicium." [s.2]

### C. Indicia (signposts, not prescriptions)
Jung lists indicia: age; whether Adlerian (unsuccessful, infantile self-assertion) or Freudian (successful, pleasure principle) viewpoint fits; deep-seated **resistances** (taken seriously, since the patient's own psychic constitution may know what is wanted better than the doctor); the **extraverted or introverted attitude**; the predominating psychic function; and **spiritual versus materialistic** attitude (ingrained passions; some outspoken materialism arises from denial of a religious disposition). The indicia are "not much more than mere warnings against one-sidedness." [s.2]
- The psyche is "highly equivocal": an attitude may stand in its own right or be a compensation for its opposite. Jung avoids theoretical presuppositions and lets "pure experience decide the therapeutic aims"; the physician should not have too fixed a goal; "the great decisions of human life have as a rule far more to do with the instincts and other mysterious unconscious factors than with conscious will." "The shoe that fits one person pinches another; there is no recipe for living that suits all cases. Each of us carries his own life-form—an indeterminable form which cannot be superseded by any other." Normalizing treatment is to be done first; if insufficient, the therapist follows the data of the patient's unconscious, "developing the creative possibilities that lie in the patient himself." [s.2]

### D. Jung's clinical material and stuckness
- His work begins "where treatment ceases and development sets in." Most patients have had earlier treatment; about a third have no clinical neurosis but suffer "the senselessness and emptiness of their lives," which he calls "the general neurosis of our time"; about two-thirds are past middle age. They are socially well-adapted, able people to whom normalization means nothing. Typical statement: "I am stuck." When asked "What shall I do?" Jung says he does not know; he knows only that when conscious outlook has no way forward, the unconscious reacts to the standstill. Being stuck is a typical event, the theme of fairy tales and myths (Open Sesame; the helpful animal that finds the hidden way). [s.2]

### E. The use of dreams
- Jung turns to dreams "from perplexity," not from a dream theory ("I do not know how dreams arise"). Meditating on a dream long enough, carrying it about, "something almost always comes of it": a practical hint. Sole criterion of validity: **"it works"** (the stimulating effect; understanding why is his "spare time" hobby; insisting on scientific results first is "auto-erotic"). [s.2]
- Initial dreams vary: some point to the past (forgotten, lost possibilities; the one-sidedness behind loss of **libido**, when previous goals lose value); some to present facts (marriage, social position) never accepted as conflict sources. Hardest are dreams that "reconnoitre" the future with mythological images, "unconscious metaphysics," germs of conscious thought; Jung may say "I don't believe it, but follow up the clue." [s.2]
- **Example: the sick niece.** A "normal" patient's long initial dream featured his sister's two-year-old daughter being ill (no actual illness). He then recalled that two years before he took up occultism, which led him to psychology: the child was his interest in the psyche, and the thought that occult study had something sickly about it struck home and changed his attitude. "Does a thing or a fact ever mean anything in and of itself?" It is always the human being who gives meaning; the interpretation works whatever one says about why. [s.2]
- Jung also offers his own guesses ("suggestion" does no harm: we are susceptible only to suggestions with which we are already secretly in accord; the psyche rejects error like a foreign body). He values knowledge of primitive psychology, mythology, archaeology and comparative religion as **analogies** enriching associations, so that "the least of things with a meaning is worth more in life than the greatest of things without it." [s.2]

### F. Fantasy and the supra-personal
- The method is "as if one began to build a bridge out into space," and he seconds the patient's fantasies. Fantasy is "the maternally creative side of the masculine spirit"; all works of man originate in creative fantasy; it is bound to "the tap-root of human and animal instinct" and rights itself; it frees man from "the nothing but" and liberates the spirit of play (Schiller: man is completely human only when playing). The goal: a state of "fluidity, change and growth" in which nothing is "eternally fixed and hopelessly petrified." [s.2]
- Rule: never go beyond the meaning that has an effect on the patient; make it conscious including its **supra-personal connections** (if a universal experience is taken for peculiar to oneself the attitude is too personal and isolates). A "supra-personal consciousness" open to historical continuity is needed; many neuroses come from blinding oneself to one's religious promptings through "a childish passion for rational enlightenment." Religion is "an element in psychic life whose importance can hardly be overrated," and no longer a matter of dogma and creed. Jung says his technique derives from Freud's free association, developed further. [s.2]

### G. Painting and drawing active fantasies
- While the patient merely receives dream-insight he remains in a "childhood" state of passive dependence (on dreams and the doctor), "groping about in Egyptian darkness"; what is woven by day the night unravels. Dreams that refer to photographs, paintings or films prompt Jung to urge patients to **paint what they have seen in dream or fantasy**, whether or not they are painters ("neither are modern painters"; it is "not a question of the beautiful, but merely of the trouble one takes"). A talented portraitist had to start with childish efforts: painting what is seen within differs from painting what is before the eyes. [s.2]
- The audience is people who can no longer find significance in social usefulness and have reached the question of the meaning of individual life; being "a particle in a mass" has charm only for those who have not yet advanced beyond it. Artistically fine products are treated as worthless by serious art standards, so patients do not imagine themselves artists; what matters is "the living effect upon the patient himself." [s.2]
- Why it works: it turns passive reception into active deed ("he is actually doing something about it"); the effort to give visible form forces study of the image in all its parts; drawing gives fantasy reality, weight and driving power; a patient freed once from a wretched mood by a symbolic picture will use it again: **growth of independence**, a step to maturity; no longer dependent on dreams or doctor's knowledge. The painted "active fantasies" activate him, and "that which is active within is himself, but not... his personal ego" mistaken for the **self**; the ego appears as an object actuated by life-forces. [s.2]
- **Image of the shift:** "as though the ego were the earth, and it suddenly discovered that the sun (or the self) was the center of the planetary orbits and of the earth's orbit as well." We have always "known" this with the head; we do not live it because of the bias of putting the ego at the center, from "over-valuation of consciousness." [s.2]
- **Age distinction:** the young unadapted person needs to educate the conscious will and may safely believe only in will. The patient in the second half of life must learn to experience his inner being; social usefulness is no longer an aim; creative work benefits himself, frees him from morbid dependence, and gives inner firmness; "an inwardly sound and self-confident person will be more adequate to his social tasks than one who is not on good terms with his unconscious." [s.2]

### H. The pictures: collective unconscious, centering, actuality
- Common features: primitive symbolism, "barbaric" intensity of color, archaic quality, with parallels in archaeology and comparative religion. They come chiefly from the **collective unconscious**, defined here as "an unconscious psychic activity present in all human beings which not only gives rise to symbolical pictures today, but was the source of all similar products of the past." The pictures satisfy a natural need, reconciling the part of the psyche reaching into the primitive past with present consciousness. [s.2]
- Execution is not enough: pictures must be intellectually and emotionally understood, "consciously integrated, made intelligible, and morally assimilated" (interpretation). Jung says he has not been able to work this process into publishable form, only fragmentarily. Many decisive pictures point toward a **centering process**, "a new center of equilibrium" around which the ego orbits, with a "peculiar purposefulness" that heightens the feeling for life. [s.2]
- **On illusion:** "What we are pleased to call such may be for the psyche a most important factor of life—something as indispensable as oxygen for the organism." "Everything that acts is actual." Do not confuse psyche with consciousness; the missionary calling the heathen's gods illusions is "the most stupid" standpoint for a psychologist; "To the psyche the spirit is no less the spirit even though it be called sexuality." Technical terms never touch the essence of the process, which "cannot be compassed by the rational concepts of consciousness any more than life itself"; hence patients turn to symbolic expression. [s.2]

## Essay 4. "A Psychological Theory of Types" [s.2]

**Summary.** Jung explains why a typology of the psyche is hard (the psyche is closest to us and least known), describes how complexes lead to the question of individual reaction, and presents his typology: two attitudes (introversion, extraversion) cross-cut by four functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition), giving eight variants, with the less-developed function constituting each type's inferiority. [s.2]

### A. Body, mind, and why psychology came late
- **Character** is "the fixed individual form of a human being," of body and of mind; the mind-body split is "an artificial dichotomy" born of the habits of intellectual understanding. Inferences run both ways, but it is easier to start from the visible body (known) toward the psyche (unknown, "almost unexplored country," known only through conscious functions open to deception). Past characterology started from outside: astrology, palmistry, Gall's phrenology, Lavater's physiognomy, graphology, Kretschmer's physiological types, Rorschach's "klexographic" method. Jung says research must follow this direction until some elementary psychic facts are established; only then can the reverse (bodily correlates of psychic states) be asked, which is not yet possible. [s.2]
- **Psychology is the youngest science** and suffers from preconceived opinions: it took until recently to separate ourselves from the contents of our minds. The psyche is immediate ("we yawn over" its commonplaces), so each person is convinced he is his own best authority on it. Anecdote: a psychiatrist said "There are only two normal people in this city—Professor B. is the other." Heuristic principle: others probably understand us better than we do ourselves. We lack concepts, not facts; "we are surrounded—almost buried—by these facts"; being psyches ourselves, we are almost dissolved in them and lose the power to distinguish and compare; generalizations from the stream of experience are "usually nothing more than a symptom." [s.2]
- Psychic facts are as objective as historical events even though vague subjectively ("I merely thought it"): the admission "I did actually think thus" can cost a "great moral effort." [s.2]

### B. Complexes
- Jung's field is the study and classification of psychic data inferred from outward signs; descriptive psychology of the "hinterland of the mind" compares to cellular pathology versus symptomatic pathology. **Complexes** are "emotionally toned contents having a certain amount of autonomy." "Autonomous": they resist conscious intentions, come and go as they please, are split off and lead a separate existence in the unconscious, ready to hinder or reinforce conscious intentions. [s.2]
- Complexes contain a conflict (cause or effect): shock, upheaval, mental agony, inner strife. Colloquial names: French *bêtes noires*, "skeletons in the cupboard," "vulnerable points." They hold memories, wishes, fears, duties, needs or views never come to terms with. Having complexes does not necessarily mean inferiority; they are "focal or nodal points of psychic life which we would not wish to do without"; without them psychic activity would reach a fatal standstill; they mark unresolved problems and weak spots, but also stimulus to greater effort. A complex arises from the clash between a requirement of adaptation and the individual's constitutional inability to meet it. [s.2]
- A small number of typical primary patterns, all rooted in childhood; the first is the **parental complex**, because parents are the first reality the child collides with. Its mere presence tells little: the crux is how it works out in the individual (siblings under the same influence react differently). This is the "**choice of the neurosis**" problem (Freud also faced it): in a neurotic family one child reacts with hysteria, another with compulsion neurosis, a third with psychosis, a fourth not at all. Jung says Freud's solution does not satisfy him, and he cannot answer it; the question is premature. [s.2]

### C. The brook example and the search for criteria
- How does one react to an obstacle? At a brook with no bridge, the psychomotor system is ready, but first a purely psychic decision occurs, by habit and unconsciously. Five reasons: jump for fun; no alternative; every obstacle challenges me; hates useless effort; saw no urgent need. These incentives look futile but reveal individual systems of psychic adaptation (the pleasure-seeker explains his life by pleasure; the one who sees no alternative goes through life carefully but unwillingly). Variations are numberless "as the variations of crystals" yet show basic uniformities that group them. [s.2]
- **History of typologies.** Oldest: Oriental astrologers' **trigons of the four elements**: air (Aquarius, Gemini, Libra), fire (Aries, Leo, Sagittarius), and so on (water and earth are named but their signs not listed in this passage). This is parent of the Greek physiological type-theory of the four humors: **phlegmatic, sanguine, choleric, melancholic**, which lasted nearly seventeen centuries; astrological typology "remains intact today" and is in new vogue. The chief difficulty is **criteria**. Jung's modern answer must satisfy science; he admits his solution is "the outcome of my individual prejudice," comparing himself to Columbus who found America with a false hypothesis and "a route abandoned by modern navigation"; "a science is never made by one man, but by many." [s.2]

### D. How Jung arrived at the two attitudes
- From treating many married couples he built an average truth: "active" versus "passive" natures. He refined to "reflective" versus "unreflective" (apparently passive people deliberate first; unreflective ones leap into swamps), then found hesitation often comes from habitual timidity or shrinking, and quick action from self-confidence toward the object. Final formulation: one class, at the moment of reaction, "draw back a little as if with an unvoiced 'No'" and then react; another class reacts at once, confident their behavior is right. The former has a **negative relation to the object** (**introverted**), the latter a **positive** one (**extraverted**). Merely labeling is as empty as Molière's bourgeois gentilhomme discovering he spoke prose; the types gain meaning from all the characteristics that go with them. [s.2]
- "One cannot be introverted or extraverted without being so in every respect." Introversion or extraversion is "an essential bias which conditions the whole psychic process, establishes the habitual reactions," determines behavior, subjective experience, and also **the kind of compensatory activity of the unconscious** to expect. Habitual reactions shape behavior, which brings results, which shape experience, which shapes behavior, "closing the circle of an individual's destiny." [s.2]
- **Self-correction:** Soon after publishing the two-type formulation Jung realized he had "explained too much in too simple a way." Great differences remained within each class; it took "nearly ten years" of observation to resolve. Complaint about academic psychology: "a chaos of arbitrary opinions" produced in studies by "Jovian brains"; psychology must embrace "women, ... the Chinese, and ... Australian Negroes" [note: the source's own phrase; kept as written] "otherwise we simply remain enclosed in the Middle Ages." Criteria had to be made "not out of whole cloth" on earlier workers' preparatory work. [s.2]

### E. The four functions
- **Superior function:** like the lion striking with the forepaw and not the tail like the crocodile, people react habitually with their most trustworthy and efficient function; it governs what situations they seek or avoid and so their experience. A function of habitual reaction becomes the criterion. Social types (peasant, worker, scholar, warrior) are not psychological types ("intellectual porters"). "Intelligence, like stupidity, is not a function but a modality"; likewise moral and aesthetic criteria. Jung draws functions from ordinary speech ("thinking," "memory," "feeling"), which stores images founded on experience, resembling the old faculty psychology only superficially. [s.2]

| Function | Jung's definition and characterization in this essay |
|---|---|
| **Thinking** | Rational; used "to judge or to reach a conclusion"; its adherents weigh thought heavily in decisions, in understanding and adapting to the world. |
| **Feeling** | A function in itself, to be distinguished from sensation and from "feeling" in the sense of hunch; used "to attach a proper value to something"; rational, "as discriminating, logical and consistent as thinking." (Example distinction: a feeling of regret versus a "feeling" that the weather will change or that aluminium shares will rise.) Feeling types follow a "policy" dictated by feeling and rarely reflect. |
| **Sensation** | "Perception through conscious sensory processes." Perceptive, non-rational: aware of what happens without interpreting or evaluating it. |
| **Intuition** | "Perception by way of unconscious contents and connections." Perceptive, non-rational. |

- **Rational functions**: thinking and feeling (judging: they select by principle). **Non-rational (perceptive) functions**: sensation and intuition (receptive of "what happens," which is nature, and non-rational: there is no inference proving there must be so many planets or species). "Lack of rationality is a vice where thinking and feeling are called for—rationality is a vice where sensation and intuition should be trusted." Opposed pairs: thinking versus feeling, sensation versus intuition (one cannot trust the eyes and ears and also give way to dreams and fantasies about what is round the corner). Jung stresses that defining terms is a matter of convention, like Réaumur, Celsius or Fahrenheit, or "puma or mountain-lion." [s.2]
- **Inferior function.** Developing one function leaves the others "in a more or less primitive and infantile state, often only half-conscious, or even quite unconscious"; this "specific inferiority" is characteristic of each type and part of total character ("a man can never be everything at once, never complete"). One-sided thinking brings inferior feeling; differentiated sensation and intuition injure each other. A differentiated function shows strength, stability, constancy, trustworthiness and service in adaptedness; the **inferior function** shows lack of self-sufficiency (dependence on people and circumstances), moods, undue sensitivity, untrustworthiness, vagueness, suggestibility; "We are always at a disadvantage in using the inferior function because we cannot direct it, being in fact even its victims." In a thinking type feeling is "contaminated" with sensation and intuition. [s.2]
- **Result:** two attitudes (extraversion, introversion) times four functions equals **eight variants**. Why four? "A matter of empirical fact," but with a completeness: "Sensation establishes what is actually given, thinking enables us to recognize its meaning, feeling tells us its value, and finally intuition points to the possibilities of the whence and whither." The four are like points of a compass, "just as arbitrary and just as indispensable" (latitude and longitude analogy). Jung values the theory as "a system of comparison and orientation" making possible "a critical psychology." Cross-reference: his book on types (cited in the text) gathers support; this essay does not describe individual type-variants. [s.2]

## Essay 5. "The Stages of Life" (range covers the first part; essay continues in Part 2) [s.2]

**Summary of covered portion.** Problems arise with consciousness; consciousness arises from separation from instinct. The essay traces the development from childhood through youth (the "problems" of the first half of life) to the turn at middle life, where the earlier ideals and achievements must be reversed. [s.2]

### A. Problems, consciousness, and the fall
- The essay discusses only "problems," that is, difficult, ambiguous questions with more than one doubtful answer, and admits it will rely on some faith and speculation. For civilized man, psychic life is "full of problems"; reflection, doubt and experiment are foreign to the instinctive primitive mind. "It is the growth of consciousness which we must thank for the existence of problems; they are the dubious gift of civilization." Consciousness arises as man opposes himself to instinct; "Instinct is nature and seeks to perpetuate nature; while consciousness can only seek culture or its denial." Even a Rousseauesque return to nature "cultivates" nature. Where doubt reigns and several ways seem possible, the individual leaves the guidance of instinct and falls prey to fear; consciousness, "our Promethean conquest," must do what nature did, with the fear that it cannot. [s.2]
- Problems bring an "orphaned and isolated state" and the necessity of "saying good-bye to childlike unconsciousness and trust in nature"; this is an essential symbolic teaching of Christianity: the sacrifice of the merely natural man; the biblical fall presents the dawn of consciousness as a curse (the tree of knowledge). People make problems taboo and want certainties without doubt: "certainties can arise only through doubt, and results through experiment." The path leads through darkness, so even speculation is needed. The psychic problems touch the domains of theologian, philosopher, physician, educator, biologist, historian because sciences are "symptoms of his psyche." [s.2]

### B. Origin of consciousness and the ego
- No one can say how consciousness arises, but small children can be observed: consciousness is present when the child "knows" a person or thing, i.e., links a new perception to an established context and holds both in consciousness. "Knowing" rests on a conscious connection between psychic contents. First stage: sporadic connections; early childhood has "islands of consciousness" like lamps in darkness, without continuous memory. Then the series of related contents called the **ego** forms, an object in consciousness (the child speaks of itself in the third person). When ego-contents have their own energy, "I-ness" arises and the child says "I"; memory continuity begins as continuity of ego-memories. [s.2]
- The child is not yet fully psychically born, enclosed in the psychic atmosphere of its parents; psychic birth and the conscious distinction of ego from parents occur at **puberty**, with the eruption of sexual life: "the unbearable age." Before that life is governed by impulse; external limits do not split the individual against himself. A **problem** (inner tension, being at variance with oneself) arises when an external limit becomes an inner obstacle, i.e., "side by side with the series of ego-contents, a second series of equal intensity comes into being," a second ego able to wrest leadership from the first. [s.2]

| Stage of consciousness | Character |
|---|---|
| First (recognizing or "knowing") | anarchic or chaotic |
| Second (developed ego-complex) | monarchic or monistic |
| Third (awareness of one's divided state) | dualistic |

### C. The period of youth (puberty to about 35 to 40)
- The child, when normal, has no real problems; doubts arise after growing up. Sources in youth: harsh demands of life ending the dream of childhood; false presuppositions (exaggerated expectations, underestimated difficulties, unjustified optimism, negative attitude); inner disturbances such as the sexual impulse disturbing equilibrium and the feeling of inferiority from sensitivity. Those who had to struggle hard may be spared inner problems; those for whom adaptation was easy run into sex and inferiority conflicts. Problems are **not** neurosis: "the neurotic is ill because he is unconscious of his problems; while the man with a difficult temperament suffers from his conscious problems without being ill." [s.2]
- **Common factor:** clinging to the childhood level of consciousness, a rebellion against forces drawing us into the world; "Something in us wishes to remain a child," unconscious or conscious only of ego, rejecting the foreign or subjecting it to will, indulging pleasure or power, with something like "the inertia of matter." The dualistic stage requires recognizing the strange as an "**also-I**" and an extension of horizon, a **diastole** (Goethe's term) that began at birth. [s.2]
- **Regression versus identification with the new:** religious education (putting off the old Adam; primitive rebirth rituals) tries to turn people into a new future man. But "there is nothing in the psyche that is old; nothing that can really, definitively die away" (Paul had a thorn in the flesh). Retreating into the past and fleeing the past into the new produce the same neurotic condition (one estranged from the past, the other from the future); both salvage a narrow consciousness. The alternative is to shatter it with the tension of opposites and build wider, higher consciousness. [s.2]
- **Why this seldom happens in the first half of life:** nature does not care about higher consciousness; society rewards achievement, not personality (rewarded mostly posthumously). So the youth limits himself to the attainable and differentiates particular aptitudes ("the capable individual discovers his social being"). Achievement and usefulness are lodestars for rooting oneself but cannot lead to the wider consciousness called **culture**. The price: "One man loses a valuable piece of his past, another a valuable piece of his future," as with promising youngsters met years later "dry and cramped in a narrow mould." Serious problems are never fully solved; "the meaning and design of a problem seem not to lie in its solution, but in our working at it incessantly," which preserves from "stultification and petrifaction." The youthful solution is only temporarily valid. Social achievement is nonetheless a real struggle like the child's defense of its ego, and ideals and convictions grow into one's being. [s.2]

### D. Approaching middle life
- Near mid-life, entrenched in position and standpoint, we suppose our principles eternal and cling to them as virtue, overlooking that society's rewards come "at the cost of a diminution of personality"; neglected aspects lie "in the lumber-room among dusty memories," sometimes "glowing coals under grey ashes." [s.2]
- **Signs of the change between 35 and 40:** statistically more mental depression in men about forty, neurotic difficulties in women often earlier; indirect signs from the unconscious: slow character change, re-emergence of childhood traits, weakening and shifting of interests; and hardening of convictions and moral principles, culminating near fifty in "intolerance and fanaticism," as if their existence were endangered. "The wine of youth does not always clear with advancing years; oftentimes it grows turbid." The change is delayed when the parents are alive (the period of youth unduly prolonged); the father's death can cause "an overhurried—an almost catastrophic—ripening." [s.2]
- **Example: the churchwarden.** A pious churchwarden grew increasingly intolerant and sour from forty; at fifty-five he sat up one night and told his wife, "Now at last I've got it! As a matter of fact I'm just a plain rascal," and spent his declining years in riotous living, wasting much of his fortune; "Obviously quite a likeable person, capable of both extremes!" [s.2]
- Neuroses of adult years betray the attempt to carry youth's dispositions past the "years of discretion." Old gentlemen reheating student days are merely boring and stereotyped, not neurotic; "the neurotic is rather a person who can never have things as he would like them in the present, and who can therefore never enjoy the past." As the neurotic once could not leave childhood, he now cannot part with youth; shrinking back from the second half of life. Jung doubts it is fear of death (death is distant, abstract); the basis is "a deep-seated and peculiar change within the psyche." [s.2]
- **The sun simile:** a sun with human feeling and limited consciousness rises from the "nocturnal sea of unconsciousness," finds significance in widening reach, climbs to its unforeseen zenith, and at noon begins to descend, "the reversal of all the ideals and values that were cherished in the morning"; it draws in its rays; light and warmth decline. French aphorism: *Si jeunesse savait, si vieillesse pouvait.* Humans are not suns, but "something sunlike within us" makes "morning and spring... evening and autumn of life" a psychological and physiological truth: the noon reversal changes bodily traits (stated claim: older women, especially among "southern races," develop deep voices, incipient moustaches and hard expressions; older men develop adiposity and softer faces). [s.2]
- **Range ends here:** the section break to EPUB section 3 follows (the essay continues in Part 2). [s.2]
