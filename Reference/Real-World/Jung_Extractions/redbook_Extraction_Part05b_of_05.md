## Overview of this range

This range covers the closing portion of the visionary text of *Liber Novus* (the text called "Scrutinies" in the reader's edition, whose running head reads SCRUTINIES) and then the editorial apparatus at the end of the book. In the visionary text, the figure Philemon (written with a Greek-letter spelling in the source text, here rendered Philemon; the pdftotext garbles it as "<I>IAHMQN") continues to speak to the dead (the sixth and seventh sermons), then guides the narrator ("I") through a series of encounters: a dark visitor who brings abstinence, a turbaned doctor who offers healing through women, a vision of a star-mantled mother, an appearance of a figure the narrator identifies as Christ, and a speech of Philemon about killing and the God's serpent. The narrator also speaks with Elijah and Salome. The footnotes by the editor compare these passages with the earlier manuscript, Black Book 6, and cite letters and notebook entries.

Material in this part is therefore of two kinds, labeled separately below: **A. Visionary / narrative text** and **B. Editor's material** (footnotes, appendices, bibliography, index).

## A. VISIONARY TEXT

### A1. End of the fifth sermon, and the sixth sermon to the dead (continued from the previous part) [p.384]

- The chapter continues from the previous part. Philemon's closing lines on the fifth sermon give a short set of sayings on community and singleness: "Community is depth, singleness is height." Right measure in community purifies and preserves; right measure in singleness purifies and increases; community gives warmth, singleness gives light. The summary line is a pair of paired measures: abstention belongs to community, extravagance to singleness [p.384].
- The dead stay silent and wait. Philemon then gives the **sixth sermon to the dead**, on two daimons [p.384]:
  - The **daimon of sexuality** approaches the soul as a **serpent**. She is half human soul and is called **thought-desire**. She is an earthly soul, half daimonic, akin to the spirits of the dead; she swarms in the things of earth and makes people fear them or crave them. She has a female nature, seeks the dead who are spellbound by earth and did not find the way to singleness; she is called a whore who courts devils and evil spirits, a tormentor. She cunningly lames the phallic daimon or goads him on, and she flees our grasp, thus showing the way "which our human wits could not find."
  - The **daimon of spirituality** descends as the **white bird**. He is half human soul, called **desire-thought**. He abides with the mother, descends from time to time, is manlike, chaste and solitary, a messenger of the mother who flies high above the earth. He commands singleness, brings knowledge from the distant ones who departed before and attained perfection, bears our word up to the mother. The mother intercedes and warns but is powerless against the Gods; she is called a vessel of the sun.
- The dead respond with contempt: they say they have known this for a long time and tell him to cease the talk of Gods, daimons and souls [p.384].
- Philemon smiles and calls them poor in flesh and rich in spirit. He says their mockery is the other side of their belief: they rejected faith and kept mockery, and so are bound to mockery and hence again to faith ("black less than white?"). He tells them to add his folly to their cleverness, unreason to reason, and they will find themselves; being dead, this knowledge frees them from life, strips them of their greed for men, frees the self from the shrouds of light and shadow, and compassion with men will overcome them. They will pass from the stream onto solid ground, "the unmoving stone of rest," where flame dies down [p.384].
- He lists what he has done in his teaching: fanned a glowing fire, given the murderer a knife, torn open healed wounds, made cold colder, heat hotter, falseness falser, goodness better, weakness weaker. "This knowledge is the axe of the sacrificer" [p.384].
- The dead cry that his wisdom is foolishness and a curse and ask if he wants to turn the wheel back. Philemon answers with a description of renewal after sacrifice: the earth green and fruitful again from the blood of the sacrifice, flowers sprouting, a bird of the soul coming to men, stones speaking, "Man found himself and the Gods wander through Heaven"; the fullness gives birth to "the golden drop, the golden seed, plumed and hovering" [p.384].
- The dead fall silent and slowly creep away. Philemon bends to the ground and says: "It is accomplished, but not fulfilled." He asks the fruit of the earth to sprout and Heaven to pour out the water of life, then disappears [p.384].
- The narrator addresses Philemon the following night, asking what fires he has kindled and whether the wheel of creations stands still. Philemon answers that everything runs its usual course and that nothing has happened, and yet "a sweet and indescribable mystery has taken place": he stepped out of the whirling circle. The narrator is confused (his lips move with Philemon's words; he asks whether "you" are "I"), feels bound to the wheel yet also feels it stand still. Philemon says he stepped onto what is solid, took it with him, and saved it from the wave surge, the cycle of births and the revolving wheel; the dead received the folly of the teaching, were "blinded by truth and see by mistake," will regret and come again humbly, since what they rejected will be most valuable to them [p.384].
- The narrator wants to ask more but Philemon has touched the earth and vanished; the night is silent; the narrator's soul shakes her head [p.385].

Footnotes 118 to 120 state that several of the passages above are not in Black Book 6.]

### A2. The seventh sermon to the dead [p.385]

- A day passes; on the seventh night the dead return with "pitiful gestures" and ask to be taught about men. This is the **seventh sermon**. Philemon says [p.385]:
  - **Man is a gateway** through which one passes from the outer world of Gods, daimons and souls into the inner world, from the greater to the smaller world. Man is small and inane, already behind you; you again find yourselves in endless space, in the smaller or inner infinity.
  - At immeasurable distance a lonely star stands in the zenith. It is the **one God of this one man**, his world, his Pleroma, his divinity. In this world man is **Abraxas**, creator and destroyer of his own world. The star is the God and goal of man, "his lone guiding God"; in him man goes to rest, toward him goes the long journey of the soul after death, and in him shines everything man withdraws from the greater world.
  - To this one God man shall pray. Prayer increases the light of the star, throws a bridge across death, prepares life for the smaller world, and assuages the hopeless desires of the greater. When the greater world turns cold, the star shines. Nothing stands between man and his God as long as he can turn his eyes from the "flaming spectacle of Abraxas." "Man here, God there": weakness and nothingness here, eternally creative power there. Here "nothing but darkness and clammy cold," there "total sun."
- The dead remain silent; heaviness falls from them and they rise "like smoke above the shepherd's fire" [p.385].
- The narrator asks if man is a gateway for the procession of the Gods and the stream of life. Philemon answers: the dead believed in man's transformation and development and his nothingness; they knew man creates his Gods and that therefore the Gods were of no use. They had to learn that man is the gateway through which crowds the train of the Gods and the passing of all times. Man is "the being, the sole being," "the moment of the world, the eternal moment." Whoever recognizes this stops being flame and becomes smoke and ashes; he lasts and his transitoriness is over. Philemon says life is duration, the flame dies away; he "saved it from the fire" and calls it "the son of the fire flower"; he says he saved being from time, "redeeming it from the fires of time and the darkness of time, from Gods and devils." Time is "the fire that flares up, consumes, and dies down" [p.385].
- The narrator asks when Philemon will give him "the dark and golden treasure and its blue starlight." Philemon: when he has surrendered everything that wants to burn to the holy flame [p.385].

That footnote runs onto p.386 and is summarized only.]

### A3. The dark visitor and the star-mother vision [pp.385-386]

- As Philemon finishes, a dark form with golden eyes approaches from the shadows. The narrator is startled and asks if he is an enemy. The figure says he comes from the east, following the shining fire that precedes Philemon; he says: "I am not your enemy, I am a stranger to you. My skin is dark and my eyes shine golden." He brings **abstinence from human joy and suffering**: "Compassion leads to alienation. Pity, but no compassion." He gives short lines such as "Far from longing, know no fear. Far from love, love the whole" [pp.385-386].
- The narrator asks why he is as dark as earth and iron. The visitor says he may be called "death—death that rose with the sun," who comes with quiet pain and long peace and lays a "cover of protection" so that the narrator's warmth never ceases. He says the narrator will go to men "as one veiled": his solar nature departs and his stellar nature begins. When the narrator calls him cruel, he replies "The simple is cruel, it does not unite with the manifold," and vanishes [p.386].
- Philemon asks if the narrator looked properly, says he will hear from the dark one, touches the narrator's eyes and shows him a vision: a night sky above dark earth, the sky in the form of a **woman** with a sevenfold mantle of stars covering her entirely [p.386].
- Philemon addresses her as the nameless mother "who stand in the higher circle" and who shrouds him and the narrator and protects them from the Gods: the narrator wishes to become her child; he asks her to accept the birth and renew him; he separates himself from him. He says the cold is growing and "its star blazes brighter" and that the narrator needs "the bond of childhood." He asks her, who gave birth to the godly serpent, to take this man to the abode of the sun [p.386].
- A voice from afar, like a falling star, says: "I cannot take him as a child. He must cleanse himself first." The impurity is "the commingling": he contains human suffering and joy. He shall remain secluded until abstinence is complete and he is freed from the commingling with men; then he will be taken as a child [p.386].

[note: Footnotes on p.386: the dark visitor enters in Black Book 6 on February 5 (fn. 126); several speeches differ in attribution from Black Book 6 (fns. 127 to 130); the mother is the speaker of the voice in Black Book 6 (fn. 129). Fn. 131 (dated February 21, 1916) says that Black Book 6 has a visitor who speaks of polygamy, houris and paradise, and fn. 132 gives a passage from it ("Each woman is a book, each book a woman") and a reference to the Koran 56:12-39 and the Valkyries.]

### A4. The turbaned doctor and the dismembering [p.386]

- The vision ends and Philemon departs. On the fourth night of seclusion the narrator sees a man in a long coat and turban, whose eyes "shone cleverly and kindly like a wise doctor's." He says he speaks of joy and of healing: "Women taught me this art." He offers "the bliss of paradise, the healing fire, the love of women." The narrator asks if he means "the descent into the frog swamp," that is, dissolution in the many, scattering, dismembering. The old man turns into Philemon, and the narrator understands that Philemon was the magician tempting him [p.386].
- Philemon says the narrator has not yet experienced dismemberment: he should be "blown apart and shredded and scattered to the winds"; men are preparing the "Last Supper" with him. What will remain is "your shadow"; he will be a river that pours over the lands. Asked where his uniqueness will remain, Philemon says "You will steal it from yourself." He then describes a tree: the narrator will hold the invisible realm in trembling hands; it roots in gray darknesses and sends up branches into golden air; animals live in its branches, men camp in its shade, their murmuring arises from below; "A thousand-mile-long disappointment is the juice of the tree"; silence abides in its treetop and in its deep roots [p.386].

### A5. Narrator's reflection on love and commingling [pp.386-387]

- The narrator reports what he gathered from Philemon: he must remain true to love in order to cancel out "the commingling" that arises through unlived love. Commingling is a bondage that takes the place of voluntary devotion; dismembering arises from voluntary devotion; it cancels the commingling. By remaining true to love and voluntarily suffering the dismembering, he reaches bonding with the great mother, i.e., his **stellar nature**, and liberation from bondage to men and things [pp.386-387].
- He argues that, bound to men and things, he can neither go on with his life nor reach his own deepest nature, and death cannot begin in him as new life, since he can only fear it. Only fidelity to love and voluntary devotion dissolve the binding and bring back the part of the self secretly lying with men and things. Only thus does "the light of the star" grow and does he arrive at "my truest and innermost self that simply and singly is" [p.387].
- He adds that it is difficult to remain true to love because love stands above all sins and one must overcome sin; it is easy not to recognize one is sinning; so difficult that "my feet hesitated to advance" [p.387].

### A6. Philemon and the shade (Christ) [p.387]

- At night Philemon arrives in an earth-colored robe holding a silver fish, saying he was fishing and caught it so the narrator may be comforted. A **shade** stands in darkness at the door, bearing a robe of grandeur; his face is pale and blood has flowed into the furrows of his brow [p.387].

[note: Footnote 137 on p.387 states this figure is Christ; fn. 138 dates the speech to April 12, 1916 and says that in Black Book 6 it is not attributed to Philemon.]

- Philemon kneels, touches the earth and addresses the shade: "My master and my brother, praised be your name." He praises him: out of animals he made men; he gave his life for men's healing; men still ask him for pity and forgiveness of sins. Philemon reports that men are ungrateful beggars and do not love him, only crave the neighbor's possessions; whoever gives attracts desire. He asks whether men should not learn love, fidelity to love and freely willed devotion. He mentions that the shade forgave the adulteress, sat with whores and tax-collectors, broke the Sabbath command, and so lived his own life [p.387] (cf. John 8:1-11; Matthew 21:31-32; John 9:13 ff., fns. 139 to 141).
- Philemon says men imitate rather than live their own lives; that they pray and make demands and remind him that his work is incomplete; that it would be completed if men could live their own lives without imitation. He gives a speech men *should* address to him: thanks for the salvation, that they have taken it into themselves and learned to continue the work of redemption in themselves; they will "make the most of your talent" and not bury it in the earth; they will take his troubles upon themselves "so that you may lay your weary tired hands in your lap, like the worker after a long day's hard burden." He closes: "Blessed is the dead one, who rests from the completion of his work." [p.387]
- He says people do not love the shade and begrudge him "the price of peace." He nevertheless believes the work is completed, since one who has given life, truth, love and soul has completed it: "The time has come when each must do his own work of redemption. Mankind has grown older and a new month has begun." [p.387] (fn. 142: reference to the Platonic months; see the editor's note 273 on p.315, not in this range.)
- The shade leaves (the place is empty). The narrator tells Philemon "I am a man. Forgive me!" Philemon dissolves into darkness. The narrator decides to do what is required: he accepts all joy and torment of his nature, remains true to his love, and stands alone and afraid [p.387].

### A7. Philemon's speech about the serpent of the God [pp.387-388]

- On a silent night the narrator hears a murmur of many voices and Philemon seemingly giving a speech (fn. 144 says the next two passages also occur in "Dreams," in entries for the middle of July 1917, introduced as "Fragments of the next book") [p.387].
- Philemon's speech (as the narrator hears it): after he had impregnated the dead body of the underworld and it gave birth to the serpent of the God, he went to men and saw their affliction and madness. They slew each other and sought grounds for their actions; because they were accustomed to doing only what they could account for, they devised reasons to go on killing. The sage and the canny one said "Stop"; the fool laughed because honors had been conferred upon him overnight. Stupidity, the speech says, "is a daughter of the God," so men cannot stop murdering, since thus they serve the serpent of the God without knowing it. It is better to live "despite the God." The serpent of the God wants human blood; this feeds it and makes it shine. Whoever lives has deceived the God; the greater the number who stole their lives from the Gods, the greater the harvest feeding the serpent. The God grows strong through human murder; the flame becomes the light of men, "the first ray of a renewed sun, He, the first appearing light." [pp.387-388]
- The narrator could not grasp the rest, brooded long, and was horrified "by the atrocities that attend the rebirth of a God" [p.388].

### A8. Elijah and Salome (begins; continues in the next section) [p.388]

- Soon after, the narrator sees Elijah and Salome in a dream (fn. 145 marker). Elijah is concerned. The next night the narrator calls them. Elijah says he has become weak and poor: "an excess of my power has gone to you, my son. You took too much from me." He heard strange things and his depths were disturbed. He reports a confused, alarmed voice that spoke of a knife cutting, harvest, grapes in the winepress, "the one wearing the red robe" treading the winepress from which blood flows; of gold lying below that kills whoever touches it; of a terrible fire; and a malicious message. The text on p.388 continues with Salome's reply.

### A8 (continued). Elijah and Salome, on the many Gods [pp.388-389]

- Elijah says the malicious word was about "the death of God. There is only one God and God cannot die." The narrator expresses astonishment that Elijah, who knows the future, does not know what has happened: the world has "put on a new garb," the one God has gone away, and many Gods and many daimons have come to man [p.388] (fns. 146 and 147 refer back to pp. 300 and 348 for the red-robed winepress image and the death of God).
- Salome interrupts: "What is, gives no pleasure. Pleasure comes only from the new." She says the narrator's soul would like a new husband and loves change; "We love only what is coming, not what is." Elijah says he heard a crimson, gold-colored image and a voice like distant thunder, "a thunderous pagan roar," prehistoric, "full of horror yet harmonic," and not the voice of his God [p.388].
- The narrator replies that the world has a new face: "The one God is dead—yes, truly; he died. He disintegrated into the many; and thus the world became rich overnight." He says the one God became two: **a multiple one**, whose body consists of many Gods, and **a single one**, whose body is a man and who is "brighter and stronger than the sun." The soul has also become multiple: first a serpent and a bird, then father and mother, then Elijah and Salome. He says that he must now separate Elijah and Salome from his soul and place them "among the daimons," since they know only past and future, not the being of men [p.388]. He invites Elijah to "take part in that which is." Elijah says he dislikes multiplicity ("It is not easy to think it"); Salome says "The simple alone is pleasurable." The narrator says multiplicity "is not to be thought; it is to be viewed. It is a painting," and tells Salome that over time the simple becomes boring [p.388].
- Salome then says to Elijah that "men have outstripped us" and "the many is more pleasurable. The one is too simple and always the same." Elijah asks whether the one still exists next to the many. The narrator replies that this is an "old and ingrained mistake, that the one excludes the many": the multiplicity of individual things is the one multiple God from whose body many Gods arise; the uniqueness of the one thing is the other God, "whose body is a man but whose spirit is as large as the world" [p.389].
- Elijah answers that this is new, and asks whether the new is good: what was is good; there is nothing new and there can be nothing new; everything is recurrence. He says the narrator's being is "an appearance, a darting light" and leaves with Salome. Salome looks back and whispers that being and multiplicity appeal to her, "even if it is not new and not eternally true" [p.389].

[note: Footnote 148 on p.388 quotes Jung's *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* (pp. 338-39) on Salome and Elijah: after two years they reappeared unchanged, "as if nothing had happened," because the figures of the unconscious had sunk back into timelessness and were out of contact with the "I." The editor says this appears to refer to the conversation above. Fn. 149 states the rest of the dialogue is not in Black Book 6; fn. 150 refers to the editor's note 261, p. 311.]

### A9. The narrator, his soul and the torment of the Gods [p.389]

- Afterward the narrator tries to do each task correctly but his dreams become difficult and anxious. One night (fn. 151: May 31, 1916) his soul comes worried and says she is in great torment: "the son of the dark womb besieges me," and the narrator feels the torment of the depths and the suffering of the Gods. The narrator asks if it is presumption for a man to become a mediator of the Gods, or a redeemer of the Gods after men were saved through the divine mediator. The soul says the Gods need a human mediator and rescuer; through this man "paves the way to crossing over and to divinity." She says he does too much for men, who are the masters of his world; he can help men only through the Gods, not directly. She tells him to alleviate the Gods' burning torment [p.389].
- The narrator asks where to begin; the soul says that is where separation should occur, and that "only patient comparison provides a solution" and "It requires work." She says the Gods suffer because he left them with torment [p.389].
- The narrator says men too should "get a taste" of it. The soul asks what he gains if the torment reaches him, and says the Gods want obedience. He replies that he will do what he can, but will not take back the torment he left to the Gods; "Not even Christ took torment away from his followers, but rather he heaped it on." He says: "There is no longer any unconditional obedience, since man has stopped being a slave to the Gods. He has dignity before the Gods. He is a limb that even the Gods cannot do without." [p.389]
- The soul says the Gods want him to do for their sake what he knows he does not want to do. The narrator asks what the Gods do for him: he wants "the fruits of my labor." He complains that the Gods allowed man to be so blinded that he believed there was only one God, a loving father, so that today "someone who struggles with the Gods is even thought to be crazy." He says the altars of blinded humanity "are streaming with blood," and "dearth makes contentment, not abundance. May they learn dearth from men." [p.389]
- The soul divides herself: as a bird she goes up to the higher Gods, as a serpent down to the lower Gods; she returns troubled saying the Gods are outraged at his refusal of obedience. He answers that this bothers him little, that the Gods may devise a service in return, and he will wait; "I will let no one tell me what to do" [p.389].

### A10. The devil, and the cleansing from commingling [p.390]

- The soul departs shocked and worried: she belongs to the race of Gods and daimons and seeks to convert him to their kind, as his humanity would convince him he belongs to its clan. In a dream she paints him as a **horned devil** to frighten him. The next night he calls her and says the trick is recognized and does not frighten him. She answers: "The Gods give in. You have broken the compulsion of the law." She says she painted him as a devil because he is the only one among the Gods who bows to no compulsion, the rebel against the eternal law, to which "there are also exceptions." This detour must be made by seeking counsel from the Gods, or one falls prey to their law despite the devil [p.390].
- She whispers that the Gods "are even happy to turn a blind eye from time to time," since it would be bad for life if there were no exception to eternal law. She cries that the Gods have mercy and accepted his sacrifice. The narrator says: "And so the devil helped me to cleanse myself from commingling in bondage," and the pain of one-sidedness pierced his heart "and the wound of being torn apart scorched me." [p.390]

### A11. Philemon and the blue shade in the garden [p.390]

- At noon on a hot summer's day (fn. 152: June 1, 1916), the narrator strolls in his garden and meets Philemon in the grass. A **blue shade** (fn. 153: identified as Christ in Black Book 6, p. 85) approaches from the other side. Philemon greets him with a short song of praise: the sins of the world have conferred beauty on his face; the suffering of the world has straightened his shape; "You are truly a king"; crimson is blood, ermine is snow from the coldness of the poles, the crown is the heavenly body of the sun. He says "Welcome to the garden, my master, my beloved, my brother!" [p.390]
- The shade replies, "Oh Simon Magus or whatever your name may be, are you in my garden or am I in yours?" Philemon says the shade is in his garden, in the world of men; he and Helena (his companion, called Baucis) are servants and hosts: "Simon and Helena have become Philemon and Baucis and so we are the hosts of the Gods." They gave hospitality to the shade's "terrible worm" (fn. 156: Satan), whom the shade recognized as brother insofar as he is of divine nature and father insofar as of human nature. The shade had dismissed him when he gave clever counsel in the desert; the worm finds a place in the garden, "but where he is, you will be also." [p.390]
- The shade asks whether Philemon has caught him by a trick and says that deception and lies were always Philemon's manner. Philemon replies that the shade's nature also belongs to the serpent: raised on the tree, laying aside the body like a skin, practicing healing arts, going to Hell before ascent and seeing his brother shut in the abyss (fns. 159, 160). The shade says "You speak the truth" and says he brings "the beauty of suffering. That is what is needed by whoever hosts the worm." [p.390]

### A12. Epilogue, dated 1959 [p.391]

- A page of Jung's own later handwriting, headed 1959, forms the **Epilogue**. Jung writes that he worked on the book for 16 years. His acquaintance with alchemy in 1930 took him away from it; "the beginning of the end" came in 1928, when Wilhelm sent him the text of the "Golden Flower," an alchemical treatise, in which "the contents of this book found their way into actuality" and he could no longer continue working on it [p.391].
- He says to a superficial observer it would look like madness and "it would also have developed into one, had I not been able to absorb the overpowering force of the original experiences." With the help of alchemy he could finally arrange them into a whole. He always knew the experiences contained "something precious," and so he wrote them down in a costly book and painted the images "as well as I could." He knew how "frightfully inadequate" this undertaking was but remained true to it despite much work and many distractions "even if another / possibility never ..." — the text breaks off mid-sentence at the page break marked 190/191 [p.391].
- The editor's note on p.391 says this appears on p. 190 of the calligraphic volume of *Liber Novus*; the transcription of the visionary text was abruptly left off in the middle of a sentence on p. 189; the epilogue appears on the next page in Jung's normal handwriting and also breaks off in mid-sentence [p.391].

**The finished Systema Munditotius (30 x 34 cm) [p.395].** The caption says it was published anonymously in 1955 in a special issue of *Du* dedicated to the Eranos conferences; in a letter of February 11, 1955 to Walter Corti, Jung stated he did not want his name on it. His comments, quoted in the caption, describe it as portraying "the antinomies of the microcosm within the macrocosmic world and its antinomies":
- At the very top is a young boy in a winged egg, called Erikapaios or Phanes, "reminiscent as a spiritual figure of the Orphic Gods." His dark antithesis in the depths is Abraxas, "the dominus mundi, the lord of the physical world," a world-creator of ambivalent nature.
- From Abraxas springs the tree of life (labeled *vita*), and its upper counterpart is a light-tree in the form of a seven-branched candelabra labeled *ignis* (fire) and Eros (love), pointing to the spiritual world of the divine child. Art and science belong to this spiritual realm, art as a winged serpent and science as a winged mouse ("as hole-digging activity").
- The candelabra rests on the spiritual number three (twice-three flames and one large flame in the middle); Abraxas's lower world is characterized by five, the number of natural man (the "twice-five rays of his star"). Accompanying animals of the natural world are a devilish monster and a larva, signifying death and rebirth.
- A horizontal division: on the left a circle indicating body or blood, from which a serpent rears and winds around the phallus as generative principle; the serpent is dark and light, signifying the dark realm of earth, moon and the void (therefore called Satanas). On the right is the light realm of rich fullness; from a bright circle labeled *frigus sive amor dei* (cold, or the love of God) the dove of the Holy Spirit takes wing, and wisdom (Sophia) pours from a double beaker. This feminine sphere is heaven.
- The large sphere with zigzag rays represents an inner sun; within it the macrocosm is repeated with upper and lower regions reversed as in a mirror; the repetitions are endless and diminish until the innermost core, "the actual microcosm," is reached.

### B2. Appendix B — Commentaries (Jung's own commentary on the "Mystery play" images) [pp.396-400]

Appendix B reproduces Jung's own commentary text from the manuscript stage (the *Corrected Draft*), keyed to page numbers of that draft. Footnote 1 on p.396 says the page numbers refer to the Corrected Draft and that this corresponds to pp. 245-248 of the present edition; fn. 3 (p.397) repeats this for the second set of draft pages (103-119); fn. 5 (p.400) refers to pp. 251-254. The text is Jung's reflective commentary, written in the first person about "the I." It is described below in the order of its sections. The images are the scenes of the Elijah-and-Salome encounter in the "Mystery play" chapter of the earlier part of the book (not in this range).

**Commentary on draft pp. 86-89 (the first image) [pp.396-397]**
- Headed in the margin "Enantiodromia of the life-type" with the words "Age / Male" and the commentary opens by saying it is difficult to force this image to make a statement, yet it is so allegorical that it "ought to speak." The editor's text says the image differs from earlier experiences in being "more witnessed than experienced." The images placed under "Mystery play" are more allegorical than actual experiences; not intended allegories, they appeared as visions, and Jung says that only later did he realize they could not be compared with experiences in other chapters. They seem to be "portrayals of personified unconscious thoughts," calling for more reflection and interpretation [p.396].
- The two main figures: the old sage (prophet) and the young maiden. Jung names the principles **Logos** (a spiritual principle, which "contains this commingling" of feeling, presentiment and sensation, but masters it, and means understanding, insight, foresight, legislation and wisdom) and **Eros** (an unspiritual principle of feeling; "form-fulfilling," "desire, longing, force, exuberance, pleasure, suffering"; where Logos is ordering and insistence, Eros is dissolution and movement). They are "two fundamental psychic powers that form a pair of opposites, each one requiring the other." Nous, the intellect, is called a descendant of Logos that has done away with the commingling [p.396].
- The figures are impersonal, belonging to general human history; they are "primordial images" with "a secret power" that works on human reason and soul alike, "like a spell, like magic," causing "elevation just as much as seduction." Jung (p.396 fn. 2) notes the metaphor of primordial images comes from Jacob Burckhardt and was cited in *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido* [p.396].
- The scene (the "dark earthly depth") is read as an allegory of the inner depths beneath the bright field of consciousness; sinking into it corresponds to averting the gaze from outer things and focusing on the inner dark. Gazing "without conscious expectation" lets the psychic background show its contents [p.396].
- The serpent signifies "everything dangerous and everything bad, everything nocturnal and uncanny," adhering to Logos as well as Eros as long as they work as dark, unrecognized principles of the unconscious spirit. The house stands for a fixed abode: Logos and Eros live permanently in us [p.396].
- Salome is shown as the daughter of Elijah, which Jung reads as the order of succession and as a subordination of Eros to Logos, "a special case that possesses no general validity." She is blind; Eros is not blind, so she is "no (complete) correct embodiment of Eros, but a variety of the same"; her blindness shows incompleteness and dependence on her father [p.396].
- The glittering hall walls and the crystal are read as expressions of concentration of creative attention, "used to produce such visions since time immemorial" [pp.396-397].

**Commentary on draft pp. 103-119 (continued; the later images) [p.397]**
- A figure like the prophet arouses less curiosity than the unexpected form of blind Salome, so the formative process first addresses the problem of Eros. First comes an image of **Eve**, with tree and serpent, which Jung reads as temptation and an adventurous possibility (the wandering of Odysseus). The vision then opens onto a sunny garden with red-blooming trees (a development of erotic feeling) and wells (a steady source); the cool, non-inebriating water of the wells indicates the Logos [p.397].
- Elijah says that he and his daughter have always been one, yet Logos and Eros are two. Jung writes that in his case Logos has blinded and subjugated Eros and that the necessity arises to free Eros from the clutch of Logos so that she may regain vision; hence Salome turns to the I. The soul of the man is "more inclined to Logos than to Eros, which is more characteristic of the essence of the woman." Salome denotes "bad qualities": the murder of the holy one and the incestuous pleasure of the father [p.397].
- On repression: a principle deprived of its dignity is debased; activities deprived of development degenerate into bad habits, vice, disunity of the personality and "moral suffering or a real sickness." The only way out is to "accept the repressed part of his soul, he must love his inferiority, even his vices, so that what is degenerate can resume development." Where Logos rules there is order but too much persistence; paradise, with no struggle and no development, is the fitting allegory. The murder of the holy one is read as a result of Logos being unable to protect the holy one (like Herod). Only disobedience leads out; "The story of paradise repeats itself" [p.397].
- Every development leads through the undeveloped but developable; accepting the undeveloped appears like a sin but is in fact "a greater deed than remaining in an ordered condition" at the expense of the other side of our being [p.397].
- The scene in the second image is the same place (a crater-like cavity reaching into the earth). Salome gropes blindly toward the left, which is "the side of the inauspicious" and of the heart rather than of conscious will; the serpent moves the same way and represents magical power, which "also appears where animal drives are aroused imperceptibly in us." Elijah calls Salome back; her blindness is an affliction demanding healing; the serpent also obeys Logos. A consequence is the felt smallness of the I, expressed as boyishness [p.397].
- From the viewpoint of Logos, blind movement is a sin because it is one-sided and violates the law that man must strive for the highest consciousness. Jung quotes an apocryphal saying of Jesus (Codex Bezae insertion to Luke 6:4, cited in fn. 4): "If you know what you are doing, you are blessed; if you do not know what you are doing, you are damned." The movement to the left would be permitted only if a conscious notion of it existed [p.397].

**Continuation of commentary [p.398]**
- The first step toward such a notion is to become conscious of the goal; hence Elijah asks about the intention of the I; the I must admit its blindness. Salome feels a vague happiness; healing of Eros is begun. The I at first remains inferior since it cannot survey the development of its problem, never having looked into its psychic depths; going down into the depths feels like guilt, a betrayal of conscious ideals. Since its desire to unravel the embroilment exceeds its aversion, the I entrusts itself to Logos, and deeper images arise with the help of the crystal. The first crystal image is the **mother of God with child** [p.398].
- Jung sets Eve (carnal temptation and carnal motherhood) against the mother of God (carnal virginity and spiritual motherhood): one a movement of Eros toward the flesh, the other toward the spirit. The first makes the I an Odysseus returning to Penelope; the second makes the I Peter, the rock of the Church, with the key and the pope's threefold crown [p.398].
- Jung writes that the vision of Eve leads astray toward Circe and Calypso, and the mother of God turns desire away from the flesh to humble veneration of the spirit; in the latter, Eros "almost imperceptibly becomes the spirit, the power over the flesh in the guise of love," so that spiritual power becomes "a secular power-drive in spiritual guise." He compares Christ (overcame the world by burdening himself with its suffering) and Buddha (overcame pleasure and suffering by disposing of both, entering nonbeing); he says the living I approaching Buddha's condition risks its passion regrouping behind it and becoming a "devastating, bloodthirsty Kali," and quotes a mantra of Kali's sacrificial ceremony ("Hail to you, O Kali, triple-eyed Goddess of dreadful aspect...") [p.398].
- Salome must despair of an end which would turn Eros into spirit, since Eros "cannot exist without the flesh." Resisting the flesh is resisting the I's female soul; thus this path also ends in opposition, and the I returns from beholding the figures embodying its conflict [p.398].
- Logos and Eros are reunited and appear to know the solution; the leftward movement now starts from Logos, leading into greater darkness lit by reddish light (red points to Eros). Elijah leans against the **marble lion**, the lion signifying power and the stone the solidity of Logos. The I experiences its smallness; Elijah assumes control of developing awareness [p.398].
- The crystal visions show an idea of spiritual power: the I was tempted to arrogate prophethood, but met resistance and the idea stayed "behind the curtain." The I seeks a substitute for the lost Eros in the sphere of power, usually without noticing the ruse; as a rule the I "does not possess power, but is possessed by the power-devil." Awareness has forestalled this deception [p.398].

**Continuation of commentary [pp.399-400]**
- Appearances of living figures "should not be taken personally"; such figures belong to our personality "just as much or little as our hands and feet." It is characteristic that the old man and young maiden are called Elijah and Salome (they might as well be Simon Magus and Helena), and that they are biblical figures, "one of the peculiarities of the psychic entanglement belonging to this moment." [p.399]
- With the idea of spiritual power in view, Eve and Mary as possibilities are ruled out; the third possibility is the **filial relationship**: Elijah as father, Salome as sister, the I as son and brother, corresponding to the Christian notion of childhood in God. Jung describes this as cathartic and attractive ("There is a child in each of us"), but with a drawback: "every child wishes to grow." Fleeing into childhood from Eros leads to wanting spiritual power; fleeing from the spirit leads to arrogating the power of Eros [p.399].
- Salome's remark that Mary is their mother means that the I is Christ; this prevents the childlike solution. Jung says this does not mean the I presumes excessive importance; "on the contrary, it takes a decidedly inferior position": it has exchanged being part of the crowd behind a powerful figure for solitude and forlornness, "as alien and lonely in its world as Jesus was in his, without possessing that great man's outstanding attributes." [p.399]
- A passage on culture: renouncing the Christian outlook erodes present-day morals; a discarded worldview needs replacing; people who merely reject the dogma stay intuitively attached to old views; "later generations will become increasingly aware of this"; those who notice "enter uncharted territory, which has neither paths nor boundaries." Most make do with half measures. "Destruction is easy, but rebuilding is difficult." [p.399]
- The image ends in gloom, set against the tall, quietly burning flame encircled by the serpent, read as "devotion coupled with the magical compulsion expressed by the serpent." [p.399]
- **Third image onward [p.400].** The second image's premonitions plunged the I into doubt; the desire for clarity is expressed in the image of the **beetling mountain ridge**, with Logos leading. Then come two opposites expressed by two **serpents** and the separation of day and night: daylight = good, darkness = evil, both as compelling forces in serpent form. Jung's point: "whoever encountered a black serpent would have been no less surprised at encountering a white one. Color does not dispel fear"; good is "an inherently no-less-dangerous principle than evil." The I approaches neither serpent, remains "halfway, transfixed," and watches the struggle of the two principles within itself [p.400].
- Remaining in the middle position implies the advance of evil, since anything but unconditional surrender to the good impairs it (the black serpent attacks), but the I's not partaking of evil is a victory for the good (the black serpent grows a white head). The disappearance of the serpent means the opposition of good and evil has become ineffective, a release from the unconditional power of the hitherto abiding moral point of view toward "a middle position freed from the pair of opposites." Clarity is not gained, so the ascent continues to the final point of elevation [p.400]. The commentary text in this range ends here.

[note: The commentary names Logos and Eros as principles and uses them as the book's analytic vocabulary for the Elijah and Salome figures; the commentary is retrospective writing by Jung on the visionary chapters, not part of the vision. The Appendix B text refers to images and scenes from the earlier "Mystery play" chapter that are outside this range.]

### B3. Appendix C — Black Book 5 entry of 16 January 1916 (preliminary cosmology of the Septem Sermones) [pp.401-402]

The editor introduces it: "The following is an entry from Black Book 5, which gives a preliminary sketch of cosmology of the Septem Sermones." The entry is dated 16.1.16 (the date as printed in the source reads "r6. I. r6."; this is a garbled rendering of 16 January 1916) [p.401]. The text is spoken partly in a voice that addresses the narrator, and partly by the narrator to his soul. The text breaks off at the end of PDF p.402 in the middle of a sentence; the PDF has no further text pages (see Gaps) [p.402].

Content, in order:
- "The force of the God is frightful." The voice says the narrator is in the **second age**; the first is overcome, the age of rulership of the son whom he calls the **Frog God**; a **third age** will follow, "the age of apportionment and harmonious power." [p.401]
- The narrator speaks of binding Above with Below, God and animal; something in him is part animal, part God, and a third part human. Hierarchy listed: below you serpent, within you man, above you God; beyond the serpent comes the phallus, then the earth, the moon, and finally "the coldness and emptiness of outer space." Above: the dove or celestial soul, in which love and foresight are united as poison and shrewdness are united in the serpent. Shrewdness is "the devil's understanding" [p.401].
- If he is not joined by the uniting of Below and Above, he breaks into three parts: the **serpent** (in animal form he roams, "living nature daimonically, arousing fear and longing"); the **human soul**, living forever within; the **celestial soul**, dwelling with the Gods in the form of a bird. Each is independent [p.401].
- A short chain of correspondences: beyond me stands the celestial mother; its counterpart is the phallus; its mother is the earth, its goal the heavenly mother. The celestial mother is the daughter of the celestial world, with the earth as counterpart; she is illuminated by the spiritual sun, whose counterpart is the moon. The moon is the crossing to the dead of space and the spiritual sun the crossing to the Pleroma. The moon is the God's eye of emptiness; the sun the God's eye of fullness. Sun and moon (as symbols) are Gods; the planets are symbols of other Gods. The celestial mother is a daimon among the order of the Gods [p.401].
- The Gods are described as favorable and unfavorable, impersonal, souls of stars, influences and forces, "grandfathers of souls," rulers in the heavenly world, "neither dangerous nor kind, strong, yet humble." Their number is immeasurably great and leads to "the one supreme fundamental, which contains all qualities in itself and itself has none, a nothing and everything" [p.401].
- **Principium individuationis.** Man becomes through it; he strives for absolute individuality and so concentrates the absolute dissolution of the Pleroma. The Pleroma becomes the point of greatest tension, and the individual's star is "immeasurably small, just as the Pleroma is immeasurably great." The more concentrated the Pleroma, the stronger the individual's star. The star is surrounded by shining clouds, comparable to a small sun, blue-lit, cold and solitary, "beyond death." It is called by a Greek formula given in fn. 1 as "I am a star, wandering about with you" (cited from the Mithras Liturgy in Dieterich's 1903 edition; the editor says Jung carved the continuation on his stone at Bollingen) [p.401]. "To attain individuality, we need a large share of death." The second Greek-letter phrase, "You are Gods," is cited in fn. 2 as John 10:34 [p.401].
- This God survives the death of men; to one for whom solitude is heaven he goes to heaven, to one for whom it is hell, hell. Whoever does not follow the principium individuationis to its end becomes no God, since he cannot bear individuality. The dead who besiege the living are souls that did not fulfill it, or they would have become distant stars; insofar as the living do not fulfill it, the dead have a claim on them. A sketch of the Systema Munditotius is referenced here (fn. 3; see Appendix A, p.394-395) [p.401].
- The **God of the frogs or toads**, "the brainless," is "the uniting of the Christian God with Satan." His nature is like the flame, like Eros but a God, while Eros is only a daimon [p.401].
- **Instructions on Abraxas and the one God.** "You should worship only one God. The other Gods are unimportant." Abraxas is "to be feared"; it was "a deliverance when he separated himself from me." He is "the God of the cosmos, extremely powerful and fearful," the creative drive, form and formation as much as matter and force, above all the light and dark Gods; he tears souls away and casts them into procreation; he renews himself in days, months, years, human life, ages, peoples, heavenly bodies. If you worship him, you increase his power over you; the more you free yourself from him the more you approach death, since he is the life of the universe and also universal death. The advice: remember him, do not worship him, do not imagine you can flee him. "You must be in the middle of life, surrounded by death on all sides. Stretched out, like one crucified, you hang in him." [p.401]
- The **one God in you**: "wonderfully beautiful and kind, the solitary, starlike, unmoving, he who is older and wiser than the father," who leads among the darknesses of Abraxas, gives joy and peace, is beyond death, and is "no servant and no friend of Abraxas." He is himself an Abraxas "but not unto you, but in himself and his distant world." The narrator says: "You yourself are a creator of worlds and a created being." You have the one God and you become your one God in the innumerable Gods [pp.401-402].
- "As a God, you are the great Abraxas in your world. But as a man you are the heart of the one God who appears to his world as the great Abraxas." Abraxas is described as feared, powerful, donor of madness, he who dispenses the water of life, spirit of the tree of life, daimon of the blood, death bringer. "You are the suffering heart of your one star God." Therefore aspire toward him, love him, live for him; fear Abraxas who rules over the human world and accept what he forces on you; if you do not accept, he will torment you to death and the heart of your God will suffer, "just as the one God of Christ suffered the heaviest in his death." [p.402]
- On Christ: the death of Christ took no suffering away from the world, but his life taught that it pleases the one God if the individual lives his own life against the power of Abraxas; the one God sought the earth's procreation, forgot he was already Abraxas in his own world, became human, and in turn pulls man up to complete the one. Freeing man from Abraxas follows not from withdrawing (no one can) but "through subjugating himself to it. Even Christ had to subjugate himself to the power of Abraxas, and Abraxas killed him in a gruesome manner." [p.402]
- "Only by living life can you free yourself from it." To the degree one lives, one falls victim to Abraxas's deceptions, but the star God gains in longing and power because the fruit of deception and disappointment falls to him; pain and disappointment fill the world of Abraxas with coldness and the longing sinks to the midpoint where the star's blue light arises. If one flees Abraxas from fear, one escapes pain but one's God "cannot catch fire." "So do not flee from Abraxas, do not seek him." The works of Abraxas are to be fulfilled: in one's own world one is Abraxas and forces one's creature to fulfill one's work; as the creature of Abraxas one learns to fulfill the work of life [p.402].
- On the question why: "The world is questionable. It is the unending infinite folly of the Gods, which you know is unendingly wise. Surely it is also a crime, an unforgivable sin, and therefore also the highest love and virtue." Advice: live life, do not flee Abraxas if he compels you and you can recognize his necessity; "in one sense... do not fear him, do not love him. In another... fear him, love him." The text says "You need to recognize the multiplicity of the Gods. You cannot unite all into one being." The one God is "the kind, the loving, the leading, the healing"; to him love and worship is due and he is "nearer than your soul." [p.402]
- The soul speaks last (in the range): "I, your soul, am your mother, who tenderly and frightfully surrounds you, your nourisher and corrupter." She prepares good things and poison; she is the narrator's intercessor with Abraxas, teaches protective arts, is his body, shadow, effectiveness in this world, manifestation in the world of the Gods, "your effulgence, your breath, your odor, your magical force." One should call her to live with men, but the one God "if you want to rise above the human world to the divine and eternal solitude of the star." [p.402] The text ends here in the PDF.
