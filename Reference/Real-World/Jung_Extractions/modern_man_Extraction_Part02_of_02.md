## Overview of this range

The range holds the second half of the essay on the stages of life (continuing from Part 1) and then five complete essays: Freud and Jung—Contrasts; Archaic Man; Psychology and Literature; The Basic Postulates of Analytical Psychology; The Spiritual Problem of Modern Man; and Psychotherapists or the Clergy (which closes the book). The through-line is Jung's claim that the psyche is a reality in its own right and not a by-product of matter or of consciousness. From that follow: the second half of life needs a meaning of its own (culture, inwardness, and a goal beyond death); psychological theories are "subjective confessions" and Freud's is one-sided; "primitive" mentality differs from modern only in its assumptions and survives in every civilized person through projection; great visionary art draws on the collective unconscious and compensates the one-sidedness of an age; the modern age has lost its metaphysical certainties and turned toward psychic life (Gnostic-style movements, psychology, the body); and the physician must therefore take up religious and moral questions, accepting the patient and himself as they are, so that the unconscious can produce its own healing images (archetypes).

## Section A. "The Stages of Life" (Chapter 5 of the in-text numbering; continues from Part 1) [s.3]

*This essay begins before this range. The range opens in the middle of it (the first paragraph below is the opening of the range, not of the essay). Chapter continues from the previous part; it ends before the heading "Chapter 6".*

**1. Reversal at life's noon [s.3].**
- Jung cites an ethnological report of an Indian warrior-chief who in middle age is told in a dream by the Great Spirit to live among women and children, dress and eat as they do, and he obeys without loss of prestige. Jung reads this as a true expression of the "psychic revolution of life's noon": at the start of life's decline, values and even the body tend to turn into their opposites.
- Image of a **store of substances**: in the first half of life a man uses up his masculine supply and is left with his smaller feminine remainder, which he must now put to use; a woman, conversely, lets her unused masculinity become active.
- The change weighs more heavily in the psychic realm than in the body. Examples: a man of forty or fifty winds up his business and his wife puts on the trousers and runs a small shop while he does odd jobs; many women only awaken to social responsibility after forty; nervous breakdown in the forties is common in modern business (especially the United States), and what breaks down is the masculine style of life, leaving an "effeminate" man; women in the same circles may develop uncommon masculinity and incisiveness that pushes feeling aside. Marriages often suffer catastrophe when the husband discovers his tender feelings and the wife her sharpness of mind.

**2. No preparation for the afternoon of life [s.3].**
- Cultivated people enter the second half unprepared, with no "college for forty-year-olds," carrying the false assumption that the truths and ideals of the morning will still serve. Jung's formulation: we cannot live the afternoon by the program of the morning; what was great in the morning becomes little at evening, and what was true becomes a lie.
- Aging people should know that life is not mounting and unfolding; an inner process forces contraction. For the young, too much self-occupation is almost a sin and a danger; for the aging, giving serious attention to oneself is a **duty and a necessity**. Image: the sun, having lavished light on the world, withdraws its rays to illumine itself.
- Failures to do this: hypochondria, niggardliness, doctrinaire rigidity, praising the past, remaining an eternal adolescent. These are "lamentable substitutes for the illumination of the self."
- Religions were the schools for the second half of life in the past; few now regard them so.

**3. The meaning of the afternoon: culture [s.3].**
- Longevity must have a meaning for the species. The morning's meaning is individual development, entrenchment in the outer world, propagation and child-rearing (the "obvious purpose of nature"). Continuing money-making, conquest and expansion past all reason into the afternoon damages the soul, just as a youth who clings to childish egoism pays in social failure.
- Distinction: money-making, social existence, family and posterity are "plain nature, not culture." **Culture lies beyond the purpose of nature**, and Jung proposes (as a question) that culture may be the meaning of life's second half.
- Primitive tribes: the old are guardians of the mysteries and laws, the tribe's cultural heritage. In the modern West the old compete with the young (father as brother of his sons, mother as younger sister of her daughter).
- Why the old turn backward: only a few people are "artists in life" who drain the whole cup with grace; most reach old age with a great deal of unlived life and unsatisfied claims, which turn their glance back. For such people a goal in the future is indispensable.

**4. Death as a goal; psychic hygiene [s.3].**
- All great religions promise life beyond death, which lets mortals live the second half with as much aim as the first. Modern educated people find belief difficult and demand "scientific" proof, which cannot exist either way. Jung's position: we know nothing about whether anything happens after death (comparison with whether Mars is inhabited).
- The physician's argument: a directed life is richer and healthier than an aimless one, and it is better to go forward with the stream of time. An old man who cannot bid farewell to life is as sick as a young man who cannot embrace it; both show childish covetousness, fear, obstinacy. It is "hygienic" to see in death a goal; shrinking from it is unhealthy and robs the second half of its purpose. Image: living in a house one knows will collapse within two weeks impairs all vital functions. Hence the religious teaching of a life hereafter is consonant with **psychic hygiene**; thinking of death as a transition within a life-process of unknown extent is desirable from the standpoint of psychotherapy.
- Analogy: most people do not know why the body needs salt, yet all demand it by instinct; likewise the need to believe in continued life is instinctive and universal. Therapy therefore walks "down the middle of the roadway trodden by humankind."

**5. Primordial images as psychic organs [s.3].**
- Intellect "understands" only the thinking that is a mere equation (nothing comes out but what was put in). Beyond it is **thinking in primordial images**: symbols older than historical man, "ingrained from earliest times," forming the groundwork of the psyche. Wisdom is a return to them; it is neither belief nor knowledge but agreement of our thinking with the primordial images of the unconscious, which are the source of conscious thought. The idea of life after death is one of them.
- Science and these symbols are incommensurables. Science can treat them only as given facts, as it did the thyroid gland, once thought a meaningless organ. Jung regards the images as "something like psychic organs" to be treated with great care; he may tell an older patient that his picture of God or idea of immortality is atrophied and his "psychic metabolism" out of gear. He cites the old term *athanasias pharmakon*, the medicament of immortality.

**6. The arc of life in four quarters [s.3].**
- The 180 degrees of the sun's arc divide into four parts: (1) childhood, east, where we are a problem to others but have no conscious problems of our own; (2) and (3) the middle quarters, filled with conscious problems; (4) extreme old age, where we again become a problem for others, unworried by our state of consciousness. Childhood and extreme old age share submersion in the unconscious. Both are excluded from consideration because they lack conscious problems.

**Cross-reference:** the sun/arc image and the "primordial images" are developed further in Section D and Section E below; the primitive's relation to the soul is in Section C.

## Section B. "Freud and Jung—Contrasts" (Chapter 6) [s.3]

*Complete essay in this range.*

**1. Impartiality and the ownership of ideas [s.3].**
- Jung doubts anyone can rise above their own ideas to compare Freud's views with his. Widely accepted ideas are never the personal property of their author; he is their "bond-servant." Great ideas arise at a definite time but are timeless, from the creative psychic life out of which the individual mind grows like a plant. Dealing in ideas is always a **confession**, bringing out one's best and one's worst insufficiencies, especially in psychology. [note: one sentence in this paragraph is garbled in the source: "Ideas spring from a source that is not contained create us" — `[garbled in source]`.]
- Truth: for psychology Jung proposes abandoning the claim to say what is "true" or "correct" about the psyche and aiming at **true expression**: an open avowal and detailed presentation of everything subjectively noted. One person stresses the forms into which the material is worked and thinks he created what he finds; another stresses his role as observer and says the material presents itself. "The truth lies between the two. True expression consists in giving form to what is observed."

**2. Psychologies are testimonies of types [s.3].**
- Present psychology is the testimony of individuals about what they found in themselves. Since each individual conforms more or less to a type, the testimony describes many people and, less fully, others of the human species.
- Freud's sexuality, infantile pleasure, conflict with the "principle of reality," incest: these are the truest expression of Freud's own psychic make-up and give form to what he noted in himself. Many cases answer to his descriptions. Jung says he is no opponent of Freud, only presented as one by Freud's and his pupils' short-sightedness. Freud "assisted at the birth of a great truth about man."
- **Adler** worked with the same material from a totally different standpoint and is as convincing, because he too represents a well-known type.
- Shared fault of both schools: over-emphasis of the pathological and reading man by his defects. Example in Freud: inability to understand religious experience (*The Future of an Illusion*). Jung prefers to see man in what is healthy and to free the sick person from that viewpoint. Freud's teaching generalizes from neurotic states and is valid within those limits ("not a psychology of the healthy mind"), and even its errors carry the truth of an honest avowal.

**3. Uncriticized assumptions [s.3].**
- Jung's "morbid symptom" in Freud: a worldview that is uncriticized or unconscious, narrowing experience. Freud turned his back on philosophy and never examined his premises; had he done so he would not have put his disposition so naïvely on view in *The Interpretation of Dreams*.
- Jung has taken philosophical criticism "a little at a time," because too much self-criticism can poison naïveté, a gift no creative person can do without. Criticism taught him that every psychology, his own included, is a **subjective confession**. "Knowledge rests not upon truth alone, but upon error also." This acceptance is where the line between Freud and Jung is "most sharply drawn."

**4. Difference two: spirit and instinct [s.3].**
- Jung tries to free himself from unconscious assumptions about the world and is inclined to recognize all manner of gods provided they are active in the human psyche. Natural instincts (sexuality or will to power) are propulsive forces, but they collide with something, which may be called **spirit**. Jung knows neither what spirit nor instinct is in itself; he will not explain one by the other (that would treat it as a mere misunderstanding). "There are no misunderstandings in nature" (illustrated by the earth's single moon).
- He attributes **positive value to all religions**: their symbolism matches figures in patients' dreams and fantasies; their moral teachings match patients' efforts at right dealing with inner forces; ceremonial, ritual, initiation and ascetic practice are techniques for establishing a proper relation to these forces. Biology and natural science are a "Herculean attempt" to understand the psyche from the outer world; Gnostic religions are an equally prodigious attempt to draw knowledge of the cosmos from within.
- **Picture of the world:** a vast outer realm and an equally vast inner realm, with man between them facing now one, now the other, and according to mood taking one for the absolute truth by denying the other. Jung holds this hypothesis heuristically and empirically verified and supported by the *consensus gentium*; out of it come his theory of types and his reconciliation with views like Freud's.

**5. Difference three: energy, not drives; sexuality in proportion [s.3].**
- "I see in all happening the play of opposites," from which he derives **psychic energy**, analogous to physical energy as a difference of potential (warm/cold, high/low). He subsumes the various drives under the concept of energy to avoid the arbitrariness of a psychology of drives alone, speaking of "value intensities." Freud began by taking sexuality as the only psychic driving power and granted equal status to other activities only after the break.
- Jung does not deny sexuality's importance; he wants to set bounds to the "rampant terminology of sex" and put it in its place as one life-instinct among others.
- Freudian sexuality is the **sexual obsession** that appears when a patient must be forced or tempted out of a wrong attitude: over-emphasized sexuality dammed up. The damming comes from being caught in old resentments against parents and in family emotional tangles; what shows as "infantile sexuality" is really an unnatural discharge of tensions belonging to another province of life. Rather than paddling in the flood, "open up drainage canals": find in a change of attitude or new ways of life the difference of potential the pent-up energy needs. Without this a vicious circle results, which Jung says is the menace of Freudian psychology (no way beyond the biological cycle; "incest with the past and incest with the future").
- The only release from the bond of the flesh is the opposite urge of life, the **spirit**: the "children of God," not of the flesh, know freedom. Jung quotes Barlach's *Der Tote Tag*: "man will not learn that God is his father." Faith cannot be made; it is a gift of grace; moderns must rediscover the life of the spirit by experiencing it anew.

**6. The charge of mysticism; father-complex; super-ego [s.3].**
- Jung is accused of mysticism, but man has everywhere and always spontaneously produced religious forms; to explain this away has "no sense of reality." The father-complex defended so fanatically in the Freudian school is "religiosity misunderstood," mysticism in biological and family terms; the **super-ego** is a furtive smuggling in of the image of Jehovah in psychological dress. Better to name things by their old names.
- The psyche may not be parceled out; it is a whole that embraces consciousness and is its mother. Scientific thought is only one function of it. The therapist must remember the ailing mind is a human mind sharing the whole psychic life of man. The ego is ill because it is cut off from the whole and has lost its connection with mankind and spirit. Freud "shipwrecks on the question of Nicodemus" (can a man enter his mother's womb a second time and be born again?).
- Initiation rites have taught spiritual rebirth for millennia; forgetting it brings "neurotic decay, embitterment, atrophy and sterility"; driving out the spirit makes the "salt of life" go flat. The ancient teaching of the initiations is handed down; the balance of flesh and spirit is not lost.
- Close: the contrast with Freud goes back to differing basic assumptions; assumptions are unavoidable and it is wrong to pretend to have none, hence Jung's attention to fundamentals.

**Cross-references:** the same critique of Freud and Adler as "psychology without the psyche" returns in Section E and Section G. Psychic energy and the play of opposites underlie the "enantiodromia" of Section F.

## Section C. "Archaic Man" (Chapter 7) [s.3]

*Complete essay in this range.*

**1. Scope [s.3].**
- "Archaic" means primal. Jung limits himself to the psychic life of archaic man (his state of consciousness and mode of life, not skull shape or skin color) and sets aside the findings of anthropology. This widens the theme: every civilized person is "still an archaic man at the deeper levels of his psyche," as the body carries relics of earlier evolutionary stages back to the reptilian age.

**2. "Pre-logical" thought and "collective representations" [s.3].**
- Lévy-Brühl (cited as an authority) insists on the strong difference between the "prelogical" state of mind and our outlook; "collective representations" are widely current self-evident ideas (spirits, witchcraft, medicines). Primitive man does not accept death from old age or disease as natural; magic (a spirit or sorcery) is always the real explanation. Some tribes see only death in battle as natural.
- **Crocodile anklets case:** two anklets found in the stomach of a shot crocodile were recognized as those of two women eaten earlier; the natives charged witchcraft, saying an unknown sorcerer had ordered the crocodile to bring him the women and had rewarded it with the anklets.
- Jung's point: this is only "illogical" because we start from different assumptions. Primitive man is no more logical or illogical than we are; his inferences are consistent from his presuppositions. Lightning burned the house (our natural sequence) versus a sorcerer used lightning to burn the house (his natural sequence). Like us, he does not examine his assumptions. His mental activity does not fundamentally differ; only the assumptions differ.

**3. Morals, senses, concentration: no fundamental difference [s.3].**
- Morals: codes differ (examples of taboos such as treading on a shadow, or scraping sealskin with an iron knife, matched by our own etiquette taboos such as keeping a hat on indoors), but "the process of ethical judgement is the same"; there are loyal head-hunters and sincere persons practising cruel rites.
- Senses: his keen sight, direction and hearing are a product of occupation. Jung showed native hunters "as keen-sighted as hawks" magazine photographs, and they turned them round and round until one traced the outlines and announced "These are white men." A European in Africa soon gains a sense of locality out of fear of getting lost.
- Concentration: natives tired of a palaver after two hours of simple questions yet showed astonishing endurance hunting or traveling (a letter carrier running seventy-five miles; a pregnant woman dancing nearly all night at 95 degrees). People concentrate on what interests them, and so do we.
- The sense of strangeness comes mainly from different primary assumptions: "he lives in a different world." "We might equally well say that primitive man ceases to be a riddle when we have come to know our own presuppositions."

**4. Our assumption of causality versus chance [s.3].**
- Our assumption: everything has a natural, perceptible cause, "one of our most sacred dogmas." We resent invisible, arbitrary forces, and also **chance**, which disrupts predictable order; yet we live exposed to accidents (the Arab writes "Insha-allah" on every letter). Causal law may hold about half the time, with the "demon of chance" the rest. What irritates us is not that causes are unknown but that accidents strike here and now, seemingly arbitrarily. Increasing regulation of existence reduces the need to guard against chance.
- Primitive man assumes everything is brought about by invisible, arbitrary powers, "in other words that everything is chance. Only he does not call it chance, but intention." Example: three women draw water and a crocodile seizes the one in the middle. We say chance; he sees a calculated intention, because crocodiles are timid and rarely kill people, so the unnatural event calls for an explanation: by whom was the crocodile commanded?
- He bases verdicts on facts; he has adapted to nature's regularities and fears what breaks them. **Anteater example:** a shy nocturnal animal seen by day (Mount Elgon region) is a portent comparable to a comet, as astonishing as a brook flowing uphill. The village is aroused, the animal dug up and killed, the oldest maternal uncle sacrifices a bull, and the finder descends into the sacrificial pit and receives the first piece of flesh. Compare how we would feel if water suddenly overcame gravity; we are not alarmed by an anteater, albino birth or eclipse because we know their meaning.
- Ordinary events form a coherent whole that embraces him and all creatures; hence he is conservative and does what has always been done. **Flagstaff example:** a missionary raised a flagstaff for the Union Jack on Sundays; a storm followed, and the flagstaff was blamed, starting a general uprising against him.

**5. Omens, series of chance events and "magic is the science of the jungle" [s.3].**
- Our own ancestors read the world this way (two-headed calf, a cock laying an egg, a comet, a fire, then war), which is how history was written to the eighteenth century. For primitive man the calf was an anticipation of the war.
- Basis: he noticed that chance events arrange themselves in **groups or series**; Jung cites the clinicians' "law of the duplication of cases" (a Würzburg professor's "tomorrow we shall have another just like it"; Jung's own two rare twilight-state patients in two days). "Magic is the science of the jungle" (a recent investigator's phrase); astrology and divination are the science of antiquity.
- The expert on irregular events is a clever tribal member, scholar and keeper of tradition, respected and feared, but the tribe secretly believes a neighbor's sorcerer is stronger; serious cases call a foreign M'ganga (sorcerer) from Uganda at high price, "just as with us."
- Proverbs ("Misfortunes never come singly," "It never rains but it pours") are primitive science. **Tumbler case:** a woman woke to find the rim of her tumbler snap off in a ring three times in twenty minutes, then abandoned natural causation and adopted the idea of an arbitrary power: "the primitive mind is not yet dead."
- His belief arises from experience, not out of thin air. Events do coincide in time and place (sequence example: a bird flies into the room, an accident in the street, a relative's death, the cook drops the soup tureen, key lost). Primitive man would count each item; in his world such caution is "highly appropriate shrewdness," since he is far more exposed to accident than we.

**6. Inattention and omen in the wilderness [s.3].**
- A Pueblo Indian who feels out of mood stays away from the men's council; a Roman who stumbled on the threshold gave up his plans. Under primitive conditions, lack of self-control (clumsiness, distraction, forgetting) can be mortal (crocodile-river log bridge, puff-adder, forgetting mosquito boots, forgetting to shut one's mouth when bathing). For us a distracted state of mind is the natural cause; for him, objectively conditioned omens.
- **Kabras forest expedition (Mount Elgon):** Jung nearly stepped on a puff-adder; his companion narrowly escaped a mamba; hyenas attacked the camp; earlier the car had fallen into a stream, Jung got fever. The porters saw the fulfillment of an omen from the first day. Jung recalled a nightmare his companion had told him in Zürich before departure (attacked by a mamba) and the friend later fell gravely ill with malaria. [note: Jung gives the setting: "This isn't man's country, it's God's country."] In such an atmosphere, warnings ("It is not a good place"; "The day is unfavorable") are expedient.
- A portent leads to modification of action, abandonment of an undertaking, or a change in psychic attitude. These reactions are expedient because chance events do fall in sequences and because he is unaware of **psychic causality**.

**7. Projection, mana, and participation mystique [s.3].**
- We separate subjective/psychic from objective/"natural." For primitive man the psychic and objective **coalesce in the external world**. In the face of something extraordinary the thing itself is astonishing; it is **mana**. Imagination and suggestion appear as invisible forces acting from outside. His "country" is the territory that contains his mythology and religion; places are "not good" because of the fear localized there (haunted wood, cave devils, mountain serpent, springs, fords, a voice in a tree). "Primitive man is unpsychological."
- **Dreams:** the Elgonyi porters said they never dream, only the sorcerer does; the sorcerer said he stopped having "big" dreams when the British came, since his father's dreams had told where herds strayed and when war would come; now the District Commissioner knows everything and God speaks in dreams to the British because they hold power ("dream activity had emigrated"). Similar: Papuans who think crocodiles have partly gone over to the British government (a "police crocodile" mangled an escaped convict).
- **Bush-soul:** a white man shoots a crocodile and villagers demand compensation for an old woman who died at that moment; a shot leopard and a woman's death in a neighboring village. Lévy-Brühl's term **participation mystique** for these identities; Jung finds "mystical" ill-chosen, since for primitive man they are natural; we do the same under other names.
- **Modern projection:** we assume other people's psychic processes are like ours; we attribute our own unrecognized inferior qualities to "the other fellow"; "an inferior 'soul' emigrates from one person to another"; bêtes noires and scapegoats replace witches and werewolves. "We no longer subject him to the test of drinking poison... but we injure him by means of moral verdicts."
- Primitive man is somewhat more given to projection because his mind is undifferentiated and he cannot criticize himself; but attributing a bush-soul lacks "the poison of the moral verdict." Pueblo example: he was declared to belong to the Bear Totem because he descended a ladder backward using his hands. The bush-soul is a concretized metaphor (we call someone an ass); "handling a patient" (medical phrase) is literally what the medicine-man does.
- Archaic man's world: man is contained physically and psychically, not master of it; his zoological ranking culminates in the elephant, then lion, python or crocodile, then man. Civilized man wants to dominate nature and therefore resents arbitrary powers.

**8. Summary of the archaic outlook and the hypothesis of mana [s.3].**
- Summary: the outstanding trait of archaic man is his attitude toward the capriciousness of chance, which he treats as more important than natural causes. Chance events (a) occur in series and (b) are endowed with apparent purposefulness through projection of unconscious contents. He does not distinguish the two.
- Civilized man strips nature of psychic attributes in order to dominate it; the sun-and-eye question: is the sun what lights the world, or the eye in relation to the sun?
- In the primitive world everything has psychic qualities; there is "as yet no individual psychic life," only the collective unconscious. **Baptism** (the *idea* of it, not the rite as magic) lifts man out of archaic identification with the world and makes him a being standing above it; its acceptance by mankind marks the birth of spiritual man.
- **Axiom:** every relatively independent psychic content is **personified** whenever opportunity arises (hallucinations of the insane, mediumistic communications, ghosts, séance spirits). A projected important content makes a human being mana (sorcerer, witch, werewolf). The insane person is at the mercy of voices because what he projects is his own psychic activity.
- Jung's friend Mountain Lake (a Pueblo chief) protested Jung quoting Augustine ("this sun is not our Lord, but he who made it"): "He who goes there is our father... Even a man in the mountains who goes alone cannot make his fire without him." Jung: the archaic standpoint cannot be better expressed; religious thought still keeps this state of mind alive.

**9. Is the mana idea justified? Reversing projection [s.3].**
- Jung ventures the hypothesis that the primitive belief is justified by the facts, without proving witchcraft. If we follow the primitive idea: beauty moves us and we do not create it; some people are impressive in their own right (mana personalities). Mana is "a widely distributed force in the external world" producing the extraordinary; "Being is a field of force": a crude theory of energy.
- Implications reverse projection: the sorcerer is a sorcerer and projects his power onto me; ghosts appear of their own volition. This raises the question: does the psyche arise in us, or in early stages of consciousness is it **outside** us as arbitrary powers with intentions that gradually come to take their place within the individual? Were dissociated contents ever parts of individual psyches, or primordial psychic entities (ghosts, ancestral spirits) that man gradually embodied until they constituted what we call the psyche? Evidence of a built-up psyche: possession, incarnation of ancestral spirits, immigration of souls; "God bless you" at a sneeze; the achievement of a unified personality as a "growing-together"; analogy to Mendelian units in bodily inheritance.
- **Materialism and archaic thought share a tendency:** both make the individual a mere resultant (of natural causes or of chance), interchangeable and dispensable. Primitive man is inconsistent in making an exception of the mana personality, exalted into heroes and kings who share divine immortality. Contradictions are normal in primitive thought (porters said by day that the dead are simply dead and eaten by hyenas, by night that spirits of the dead strangle travelers), and in us (universities that scorn divine intervention yet teach theology; the scientist who keeps "another drawer" for Sunday Christianity).
- Such antinomies are "the inexhaustible source of all mental effort."

**10. The Elgonyi ritual of the rising sun [s.3].**
- After weeks of finding no religious ideas, an old man said that every morning they spit in their hands and hold them up to the sun (also to the new moon). Asked why: "That is how it has always been done." They did not know the meaning.
- **Zürich analogy:** a stranger asking Messrs. Müller and Meyer about religious customs finds them denying any, then finds Müller hiding colored eggs and setting up rabbit idols at Easter, with no idea of the meaning (nor of the Christmas tree). "Archaic man does what he does—and only civilized man knows what he does."
- Jung's interpretation: an offering to the sun (*mungu*, mana, divine only at sunrise); spittle holds personal mana; breath is spirit (Swahili *roho*, Arabic *ruch*, Hebrew *ruach*, Greek *pneuma*). The gesture means "I offer my living spirit to God," a wordless acted prayer ("Lord, into thy hands I commend my spirit"). Whether this meaning was already incubated before man existed, he leaves open.

## Section D. "Psychology and Literature" (Chapter 8) [s.3]

*Complete essay in this range. Two parts: I "The Work of Art," II "The Poet," after a short introduction.*

**Introduction: two separate tasks [s.3].**
- The psyche is "the womb of all the sciences and arts." The psychologist faces two distinct tasks: explaining the formation of a **work of art** (a seemingly intentional, consciously shaped product), and revealing what makes a person **artistically creative** (the psychic apparatus). Each cannot yield the other's explanation; inferences from work to artist and back are only probable surmises (Goethe and "the mothers" in *Faust*; Wagner's occasional love of womanish clothes and the heroic *Nibelungen*).
- Causality holds confidently only in psychophysiological instincts and reflexes; beyond that the psychologist must content himself with description. "The creative act, which is the absolute antithesis of mere reaction, will for ever elude the human understanding"; it can be described in its manifestations but never wholly grasped. Two principles both valid in their relativity: psychic events are derivable (psychology); a psychic product is something in and for itself (study of art).

**I. The Work of Art [s.3].**
- Psychologist versus critic: works of dubious literary merit may be of greatest interest to the psychologist. The "psychological novel" explains itself and leaves him little. Most fruitful: works where the author has not interpreted his characters (Benoît, Rider Haggard, Conan Doyle's detective story, Melville's *Moby Dick* "the greatest American novel"). Such tales rest on implicit psychological assumptions revealed in the measure the author is unconscious of them.
- **Faust** as extreme illustration: Part I (Gretchen tragedy) explains itself; Part II calls for interpretation because the richness of material overtaxed the poet's formative powers.

**Two modes of artistic creation: psychological and visionary [s.3].**

| | **Psychological mode** | **Visionary mode** |
|---|---|---|
| Material | From conscious human experience: life's lessons, emotional shocks, passion, crises of destiny, the "vivid foreground of life" | A strange something from the "hinterland of man's mind": suggests the abyss of time before man, or a super-human world of light and darkness; a **primordial experience** exceeding human understanding |
| Relation to understanding | Never transcends psychological intelligibility; "no obscurity" | Foreign, cold, many-sided, demonic, grotesque; bursts human standards of value and aesthetic form |
| What it does | Raises ordinary content to poetic experience, forcing the reader to greater clarity | "Rends from top to bottom the curtain" of an ordered world; a glimpse into what has not yet become |
| Examples | Novels of love, environment, family, crime, society; didactic poetry; most lyrics; tragedy and comedy; *Faust* Part I | *The Shepherd of Hermas*, Dante, *Faust* Part II, Nietzsche's Dionysian exuberance, Wagner's *Nibelungenring*, Spitteler's *Olympischer Frühling*, Blake, Colonna's *Ipnerotomachia*, Boehme; and Haggard (*She*), Benoît (*L'Atlantide*), Kubin (*Die Andere Seite*), Meyrink (*Das Grüne Gesicht*), Goetz (*Das Reich ohne Raum*), Barlach (*Der Tote Tag*) |
| Reaction it provokes | Needs no commentary | Astonishment, confusion, disgust; demands explanation; the public mostly rejects it unless sensational; critics are embarrassed |

- Dante and Wagner smoothed the approach by clothing the vision in history and mythology; the moving force lies in the vision, not the history or myth. Haggard's story is a vehicle for significant material.

**The Freudian account of the visionary work, and Jung's reply [s.3].**
- Freudian view: some highly personal experience (e.g., a love experience incompatible with the conscious personality) is repressed, and the grotesque images are cover-figures and substitutes, so the art is pathological/neurotic. The material does show traits of insane fantasy, and psychotic persons sometimes produce meaning one expects of genius.
- Jung's objections: deriving the vision from personal experience makes it merely secondary, a symptom, strips its primordial quality and dismisses the revelation of abysses as illusion. This leads away from the work to the poet's psychic disposition. The poet's own opinion of his work is not the most illuminating word (Spitteler's claim that singing of an Olympian Spring and "May is here!" are the same). "We must defend the importance of the visionary experience against the poet himself."
- Evidence from the works: Hermas, Dante and Goethe, three steps across nearly two thousand years, each place the personal love-episode as connected with and **subordinated to** the weightier vision; no reason to suppose Goethe normal at Part I and neurotic at Part II. The vision is "not a symptom of something else. It is true symbolic expression—that is, the expression of something existent in its own right, but imperfectly known." It has **psychic reality**, as real as physical reality.
- Passion belongs to conscious feeling (the known); intuitions point to the unknown and hidden. Modern man shields himself with science and reason ("his enlightenment is born of fear"). Jung's questions: is there a living force beyond our daily world; is the "psyche" not a question mark confined in the skull but a door opening from another world, removing man to a "more than personal vocation"?
- Not only artists but **seers, prophets, leaders and enlighteners** touch the "night-side of life." Primitive man treats it as certain. Ancient evidence: Rhodesian cliff drawings with realistic animals and an abstract double cross in a circle (the **sun-wheel**), found across cultures including Christian churches and Tibetan monasteries; Jung says it dates from before the mechanical wheel and so is a symbol of an inner-world experience. Every primitive culture has secret teaching preserved by men's councils and totem clans and handed on in initiation; Greco-Roman mysteries and mythology are relics.
- Hence the poet turns to mythology: the primordial experience offers no words or images of its own ("as in a glass, darkly"), is like a whirlwind raising material aloft to assume shape. A particular expression never exhausts it, so the poet needs vast stores (Dante: Heaven and Hell; Goethe: Blocksberg and Greek underworld; Wagner: Nordic myth; Nietzsche: the hieratic style; Blake: invented figures; Spitteler: old names for new creatures).

**Collective unconscious, compensation, and the age [s.3].**
- Psychology can only collect comparative material and offer a terminology: what appears in the vision is the **collective unconscious**, "a certain psychic disposition shaped by the forces of heredity; from it consciousness has developed." As the body has traces of earlier evolutionary stages, so the psyche conforms to phylogeny; in dreams, narcotic states and insanity, products with primitive traits surface; mythological themes appear in modern dress.
- Key function for literature: these manifestations are **compensatory** to the conscious attitude; they can bring a one-sided or dangerous consciousness into equilibrium purposively. (Positive in dreams; negative in insanity, e.g., a person who shuts himself off then finds his secrets known to all.)
- Great poetry draws strength from the life of mankind. When the collective unconscious becomes living experience and is brought to bear on the conscious outlook of an age, it is a **creative act** of importance to everyone then alive and carries a message to generations. Examples: *Faust* touches something in every German; Dante's fame is immortal; *Hermas* just missed the canon. "An epoch is like an individual; it has its own limitations of conscious outlook, and therefore requires a compensatory adjustment," effected when a poet, seer or leader follows the unexpressed desire of the age (to the healing or destruction of an epoch).
- Brief, cautious examples of his own times: Colonna (1453) glorifies natural love apart from Christian marriage; Haggard (Victorian) deals with it with moral tension; Goethe's thread of Gretchen-Helen-Mater Gloriosa; Nietzsche's death of God; Spitteler's gods as a myth of seasons. Each speaks "with the voice of thousands."

**II. The Poet [s.3].**
- Creativeness, like freedom of the will, "contains a secret." Freud derived the work from the artist's personal experience; Rank and Stekel followed related lines; personal factors do shape the poet's choice and use of material (credit to Freudians for showing how far). But Freud treats neurosis as a mistake and shortcoming, and thereby assimilates art (like religion and philosophy) to neurosis.
- Jung: personal analysis may elucidate the personal determinants of a work, but not the **work itself**. "The personal aspect is a limitation—and even a sin—in the realm of art"; what is essential is that the work rises above personal life and speaks from the poet's heart to the heart of mankind. A "primarily personal" art deserves to be treated as neurosis. The Freudian claim that artists are narcissistic is valid for the artist as a person only: "as an artist he is his work, and not a human being." He is "objective and impersonal—even inhuman."
- **The artist as duality:** a human being with personal life, and an impersonal creative process. Analogy: an English gentleman, Prussian officer or cardinal functions in an impersonal role. Art is "a kind of innate drive that seizes a human being and makes him its instrument"; the artist is **"collective man"** who carries and shapes the unconscious psychic life of mankind, sometimes sacrificing happiness.
- **Cost:** two forces war in him (the human longing for happiness and security versus the ruthless passion to create). Artists' lives are unsatisfactory because of their inferiority on the personal side, not a sinister dispensation. Image of a fixed capital of energy at birth: the strongest force monopolizes it; the creative force can drain human impulses so that the ego develops ruthlessness, selfishness, vanity ("auto-erotism"), even vice, to keep a spark of life, like neglected children who protect themselves from those with no love to give. "His art explains the artist."
- The creative process has a **feminine quality**; the work arises from the "realm of the mothers" and outgrows its maker like a child its mother. The conscious ego is carried on a subterranean current, "a helpless observer." "It is not Goethe who creates Faust, but Faust which creates Goethe." Faust is a **symbol**: an expression of something not clearly known yet profoundly alive (not an allegory). Jung says *Faust* and *Zarathustra* could hardly have been written by non-Germans; both play on a "primordial image" (Burckhardt's term), the physician or teacher of mankind.
- **Archetype activation law:** the archetypal image of the wise man, saviour or redeemer lies dormant since the dawn of culture and is awakened whenever the times are out of joint and society is committed to a serious error. Primordial images appear in individuals' dreams and artists' visions only when called into being by the waywardness of the general outlook, restoring the psychic equilibrium of the epoch.
- The poet is the instrument of his work; he need not and cannot interpret it. A great work of art is like a dream: it never says "You ought" or "This is the truth"; it presents an image as nature lets a plant grow. Nightmare example: it may mean too much fear or too little. To understand it, let it shape us as it shaped him; then we see he drew on the healing and redeeming forces of the collective psyche, the matrix of life that gives all human existence a common rhythm.
- **Conclusion:** the secret of artistic creation and effectiveness is a return to **participation mystique**, "that level of experience at which it is man who lives, and not the individual." Hence great art is objective, impersonal, yet moves all; the poet's personal career (philistine, good citizen, neurotic, fool or criminal) at most helps or hinders but does not explain him.

## Section E. "The Basic Postulates of Analytical Psychology" (Chapter 9) [s.3 and s.4]

*Complete essay in this range (begins in s.3, ends in s.4).*

**1. The loss of the soul and the spirit of the age [s.3].**
- Until the second half of the nineteenth century mankind held the soul to be a substance; then arose a "psychology without the soul." Preparation: the Reformation ended the Gothic age's vertical yearning, and consciousness grew horizontally (voyages, empirical discoveries); belief in the substantiality of spirit yielded to belief that only matter has substance.
- Not a philosophical result: no reasoning can prove or disprove mind or matter, both being symbols for something unknown. Replacing a metaphysics of mind with a metaphysics of matter is "a mere trick" for the intellect but "an unexampled revolution" psychologically. Saying that mental phenomena arise from glands wins respect, while saying the atom's break-up is an emanation of the creative Weltgeist makes one a freak, yet both are equally metaphysical and arbitrary (Professor Daqué's academic fate is cited).
- **Spirit of the age:** "an inclination, an emotional tendency that works upon weaker minds, through the unconscious, with an overwhelming force of suggestion." Thinking otherwise is "indecent, morbid or blasphemous." Mind as epiphenomenon of matter is the decent, scientific, normal view; granting the soul's substantiality would be heresy. It is just as presumptuous to assume matter produces spirit, apes human beings, or that hunger, love and power drives produced Kant's *Critique*.
- "What or who is this all-powerful matter? It is once more man's picture of a creative god," stripped of anthropomorphism. Consciousness has grown in spatial breadth but not in temporal reach (little sense of history); we use history as a "mere arsenal of convenient arguments."
- The horizontal view reacts to the Gothic vertical view and is a manifestation of the crowd-mind. If we knew the spirit of the age we would see we explain everything physically because earlier ages explained too much by spirit, and would suspect we now err on the other side. Matter is as inscrutable as mind. Jung admits the close tie of psyche to brain, glands, body, heredity and instinct; the psyche could be an epiphenomenon in the light of "reason" and practical commitments; only doubts about the omnipotence of matter lead us to examine this verdict.

**2. Psychologies and philosophy [s.3].**
- "Psychologies without the psyche" are studies of consciousness that ignore the unconscious. There are many psychologies (an American university volume was titled *Psychologies of 1930*), as there are many philosophies. Psychology takes the psyche for its subject, philosophy the world; psychology (Nietzsche foresaw) is rising and threatens to swallow philosophy. Both are systems of opinion about subject-matter that cannot be wholly experienced; each furnishes the other's unconscious primary assumptions.
- No modern scientific psychology starts from mind as such; yet Jung attended in 1914 at Bedford College, London, a joint session of the Aristotelian Society, Mind Association and British Psychological Society on "Are individual minds contained in God or are they not?" which sounded to him like the thirteenth century; he suggests the idea of an autonomous spirit is not dead.

**3. A "psychology with the psyche": the old view of the soul [s.3, s.4].**
- To postulate mind is no more fantastic than to postulate matter. We turn to the forefathers' teaching: spirit as the life of the body, life-breath or life-force, spatial at birth, leaving at the last breath; spiritless extension, existing before and after, hence timeless and immortal. Jung proposes to test its empirical justification.
- **Etymology of "soul" [s.3]:** German *Seele*/English soul from Gothic *saiwala* and Old German *saiwalô*, linked to Greek *aiolos* (mobile, iridescent); the Greek *psyche* also means butterfly; *saiwalô* relates to Old Slavonic *sila* (strength): the soul is moving, life-force. Latin *animus/anima* equal Greek *anemos* (wind); *pneuma* also means spirit; Gothic *us-anan* (breathe out), Latin *anhelare* (pant), Old High German *atun* (breath, rendering *spiritus sanctus*); Arabic *ruh*/*ruuh*; Greek *psycho* (breathe), *psychos* (cool), *psychros* (cold), *phusa* (bellows). The soul is the "cold breath of the spirit" and the primitive endows it with an invisible breath-body.
- **Other primitive identifications [s.3]:** soul as fire or flame (warmth as sign of life); soul identified with the **name** (ancestral names reincarnating the soul in a newborn); soul identified with the **shadow** (treading on a shadow is a deadly insult; noon, the ghost-hour of southern latitudes, is threatening because the shadow shrinks); Greek *synopados*, "he who follows behind," the felt intangible presence; the dead as shadows.
- To primitive man the psyche is the **source of life, a ghost-like presence with objective reality** that can be conversed with, not something subjective and under the will [s.4].

**4. Empirical justification for the objective psyche [s.4].**
- Psychic happenings have an objective side for civilized man too: we cannot suppress many emotions, change a bad mood to a good one, command dreams, drive off obsessive thoughts; memory plays tricks; we are masters in our own house only by flattery. To equate psyche with consciousness is "perfectly ludicrous" when studying neurotics (and who today is sure he is not neurotic?).
- Hence the old view of the soul as independent, capricious and dangerous has justification; it is also source of life because ego-consciousness grows out of unconscious life (small children have psychic life without demonstrable ego-consciousness; early years leave hardly any memory). Good flashes of intelligence, enthusiasm, inspiration come from it.
- Localization: not in space except in sensation; thoughts in the head, feelings in the heart, sensation spread through the body. The Pueblo Indians told Jung Americans were mad for thinking in the head and that one thinks with the heart; certain African tribes locate it in the belly. Thoughts have no bulk; a non-spatial thought is a model of a "fourth-dimensional being." The psyche can be both "a mathematical point and a universe of fixed stars." Life and psyche existed before "I" and continue when "I" disappears in sleep. Jung sees "as little nonsense in this so-called superstition as in the findings of research regarding heredity or the basic instincts."

**5. The unconscious as source of knowledge; the collective unconscious [s.4].**
- Ancient and primitive societies used dreams and visions as information sources; Hindu and Chinese civilizations built disciplines of self-knowledge on that basis. The unconscious contains subliminal perceptions of astounding scope and contents that would hugely increase knowledge if made conscious. Insect instinct analogy: if man acted as certain insects do, he would have higher intelligence.
- Man's unconscious contains all patterns of life and behavior inherited from ancestors; every child has, before consciousness, a potential system of adapted psychic functioning. The unconscious "perceives, has purposes and intuitions, feels and thinks as does the conscious mind." **Difference:** consciousness is intensive, concentrated, transient, directed on the present, limited to one person's few decades; the unconscious shades off, is highly extensive, juxtaposes heterogeneous elements, and holds an immense fund of accumulated inheritance-factors. If personified, it would be a "collective human being" combining both sexes, transcending youth and age, birth and death, with one or two million years of experience, "almost immortal," an incomparable prognosticator, a dreamer of age-old dreams and with a living sense of growth, flowering and decay. "Unfortunately—or rather let us say, fortunately—this being dreams": the collective unconscious appears as an unceasing stream or ocean of images, not a person.
- To call it illusion is as grotesque as calling comparative anatomy nonsense. Freud's explanation from the side of consciousness would succeed only if the unconscious arose with the individual; but **"consciousness is a late-born descendant of the unconscious psyche"**, and it is wrong to explain the unconscious as derived from consciousness. Past ages held the individual soul dependent on a world-system of spirit which they named **God**, the quintessence of reality; Jung finds psychological justification in calling divine an almost immortal being with near-eternal experience.

**6. Practical psychology, matter versus spirit, and psychic reality [s.4].**
- Jung rejects blending spirit and nature by calling energy or *élan vital* "God" in practical psychotherapy, where explanations must bear fruit for the patient. The question of basing explanations on matter or spirit is "often attended by mortal danger": a naturalistic-only view can depreciate or destroy the patient's spiritual development; an exclusively spiritual view does violence to the natural man; suicides in therapy are traced to such mistakes. "Whether energy is God, or God is energy, concerns me very little."
- The modern psychologist stands between, with a risk of shallow opportunism ("coincidentia oppositorum"); an unequivocal explanatory principle has advantages. Jung's solution: the conflict of nature and mind reflects the paradox of the psychic being; where we cannot grasp something we must be willing to contradict ourselves and split it into antithetical parts.
- **Psychic reality:** all immediate experience is psychic, even physical pain. Sense impressions are psychic images; the psyche transforms reality (tone as air vibration, color as wavelength). "We are in all truth so enclosed by psychic images that we cannot penetrate to the essence of things external." Psychic images come either from a physical environment or from a mental source; both are equally real (the car I wish to buy versus the soul of my dead father). If all psychic happenings are real, matter and mind become mere designations of the source of psychic contents. Fire as psychic image of an unknown physical process; fear of a ghost as psychic image from a mental source, equally real, and equally unknown in ultimate nature.
- This explains why primitive man treats ghosts and magic on a par with physical events; his world has not yet been torn into antithetical parts; "his gods still wander through forest and field." After the primitive world divided into spirit and nature, the West took nature, the East took mind and explained matter away as illusion (maya); but there is one earth and mankind, and **psychic reality** awaits a level of consciousness that sees both as parts of one psyche. Jung names psychic reality "the most important achievement of modern psychology."
- Truth by the senses may satisfy reason but not feeling; feeling is decisive in good and evil (reason and good intentions did not prevent the World War; spiritual revolutions did not spring from reasoning).

**7. The physician's case and the spiritual determinants of illness [s.4].**
- Medicine should treat the sick person rather than the illness; psychic suffering is "the symptom of a wrong attitude assumed by the total personality," curable only by treating the whole.
- **Case of the young man with his monograph:** a highly intelligent young man produced a precise self-analysis of his neurosis and asked why he was not cured. Jung noticed he spent winters at St. Moritz or Nice and asked who paid; a poor schoolteacher who loved him had deprived herself to fund him. His lack of conscience caused the neurosis; he rejected this as "unscientific" since morals have nothing to do with science. The moral attitude is a real factor.
- Patients' common plaint: "If only I knew that my life had some meaning and purpose, then there would be no silly story about my nerves!" Outer circumstances do not give life meaning. The need for a spiritual life cannot be met by universities, libraries or churches when they touch only the head; patients' unconscious produces undeniably religious dream contents; failing to recognize their spiritual source means faulty treatment.
- General conceptions of a spiritual nature are indispensable constituents of psychic life; "their relative absence or their denial by a civilized people is therefore to be regarded as a sign of degeneration." Psychology has so far treated psychic processes through physical causation; its future task is investigating their **spiritual determinants**. Modern psychology has lifted only the veil of the biological aspect (sixteenth-century anatomy without physiology, in his comparison). Spiritually conditioned transformation processes underlie initiation rites and yoga states; their laws are unknown; much neurosis arises from disturbance of these processes. Natural history of the mind is "no further advanced than natural science in the thirteenth century."

**Cross-references:** the psychic-reality argument is applied clinically in Section G; the "spirit of the age" and compensation recur in Section F.

## Section F. "The Spiritual Problem of Modern Man" (Chapter 10) [s.4]

*Complete essay in this range.*

**1. Who is "modern" [s.4].**
- The question lies in the present and cannot be fully judged; Jung offers it with caution. "Modern man" is not the average man but one who stands "at the very edge of the world, the abyss of the future before him." Being fully conscious of the present means "the most intensive and extensive consciousness, with a minimum of unconsciousness." Merely living now does not make one modern.
- The truly modern man is **solitary**, torn loose step by step from original participation mystique with the mass. Strata: the lowest stratum lives almost as unconsciously as primitives; the next at the level of early culture; the highest, in step with the last few centuries. The modern man is "unhistorical in the deepest sense," estranged from the mass and its tradition, standing before "a void out of which all things may grow."
- **Pseudo-moderns:** a horde that overleaps developmental stages and tasks; "uprooted human beings, bloodsucking ghosts" whose emptiness is mistaken for the modern's loneliness and casts discredit on him. Honest modernity is "voluntarily declaring bankruptcy," a new vow of poverty and chastity, and renouncing history's halo; to be "unhistorical" is the "Promethean sin," a "burden of guilt." It requires **proficiency**: the modern man must be sound and have achieved as much as others and more; unless he can atone by creative ability for breaking with tradition he is merely disloyal. "Today" is a link between yesterday and tomorrow, a process of transition. The really modern man is often found among those calling themselves old-fashioned (to balance guilt and avoid pseudo-moderns).
- **Danger of elation:** the illusion that we are the culmination of history; in truth we are also "the disappointment of the hopes and expectations of the ages" (two thousand years of Christian ideals followed by the World War, barbed wire and poison gas). Modern man has seen how beneficent and how catastrophic science, technology and organization can be; peace-preparations ("in time of peace prepare for war"); Christian church, brotherhood of man, international social democracy and economic solidarity failed the "baptism of fire." Result: an almost fatal psychological shock and profound uncertainty.
- Jung's caveat: his doctor's eye "always spies out diseases," but a physician must not discover disease where none exists; he declines to say the West is diseased or collapsing. He draws on experience with hundreds of educated persons, sick and healthy, in the white civilized world; his picture is one-sided, from the inner side.

**2. When the psyche is "outside": why psychology appears now [s.4].**
- Where an external form (ritual or spiritual) adequately expresses the soul's longings (a living religion), "the psyche is outside, and no spiritual problem, strictly speaking, exists." Ancient Egypt (Pyramids, Apis tombs, Bach's music) show impersonal objectivity. Analogy: the Romans had the principles for a steam-engine but only produced Hero's toy; division of labor and specialization in the nineteenth century created the need. So a spiritual need has produced our "discovery" of psychology.
- Doctors noticed first; priests aim at undisturbed psychic functioning inside a belief system. While man lives as a herd-being he has no "things of the spirit" of his own; once he outgrows his local religion the psyche becomes "something in its own right which cannot be dealt with by the measures of the Church alone." Psychology founded on experience is a symptom of profound convulsion of spiritual life.
- **Damming image:** when channels of psychic activity are blocked the current flows back to its source; the inner man wants what the visible man does not; only in this distress do we discover the psyche as something that thwarts our will. Freud's first discovery was sexually perverse and criminal fantasies incompatible with the conscious civilized outlook. Previous cultures each produced their destructive opposite but never had to take psychic undercurrents "in deadly earnest"; for us they are "a shirt of Nessus."
- World War effect: shattering of faith in ourselves; "I now admit that I need calling to order myself"; loss of faith in rational world-organization chills enthusiasm for politics and world-reform; energies flow inward. Contrast: the medieval world (earth fixed at the center, men children of God, knowing how to act). That world is lost, like childhood faith in one's father.
- Modern man replaced metaphysical certainties with material security, general welfare, humaneness; even material security fails (progress adds force to catastrophe; cities rehearse defense against poison gas; firearms "go off of themselves"). Heraclitus's **enantiodromia** (conversion into the opposite) steals upon him. Turning inward he finds chaos and darkness: "Science has destroyed even the refuge of the inner life." Yet finding evil within is a relief: we feel we hold it in hand (grotesque example: newspapers telling a statesman to get analyzed for a repressed father-complex), an illusion that because something is psychic it is under control. Still, much evil comes from unconsciousness, and insight combats it at its source.

**3. The turn to psychic life: Gnostic revival and the East [s.4].**
- The widespread "psychological" interest of the last two decades shows modern man expects from psychic life what outer life and religions no longer give. Religions seem to him outer things; he "tries on a number of religions and convictions as if they were Sunday attire." Expressionist art anticipated this subjective turn.
- Fascination with spiritualism, astrology, theosophy, Anthroposophy: "The world has seen nothing like it since the end of the seventeenth century," comparable to Gnosticism of the first two centuries (a Gnostic church in France, two Gnostic schools in Germany; Theosophy and Anthroposophy as "pure Gnosticism in a Hindu dress"; Kundalini-Yoga). Gnostic systems are based exclusively on manifestations of the unconscious and do not shrink from the shadow-side. These movements are truly religious though they claim science (Steiner's "spiritual science," Mrs. Eddy's "Christian Science"). Modern man wants **knowledge** and personal **experience**, not faith or dogma ("He wants to know"). Dean Inge noted a similar movement in the Anglican Church.
- The questions of the age: the spiritualist asks what happens when the medium loses consciousness; the Theosophist what he will experience at higher levels; the astrologer which determinants of fate lie beyond conscious intention; the psychoanalyst what unconscious drives lie behind the neurosis.
- Freud's presentation of the psychic hinterland as "refuse and slag" backfired by awakening fascination, which Jung reads as the "fascination of the psyche," not love of dirt. Freud's motto from the *Interpretation of Dreams* (*Flectere si nequeo superos Acheronta movebo*): Jung asks "to what purpose?" and answers that the gods to be dethroned are the idolized values of our conscious world; exposing their dubious foundations repeats history (the love-scandals discredited the ancient deities).

**4. Compensation in history; the East within [s.4].**
- Since the French Revolution (enthronement of Reason in Notre Dame, like the felling of Wotan's oak) man has turned toward the psyche. Coincidence: Anquetil du Perron brought a translation of the *Oupnek'hat* (fifty Upanishads) to Europe in the early 1800s. Jung's **psychological law:** "for every piece of conscious life that loses its importance and value there arises a compensation in the unconscious"; no psychic value disappears without replacement by one of equivalent intensity, analogous to conservation of energy. A people is a more complex version of the individual; the psyche in one aspect derives from nation, collectivity or humanity (Swedenborg's "greatest man").
- Eastern thought and astrology take root in lower social levels and grow upward (Dornach temple funds; millions of Theosophists and Spiritualists). "Great innovations never come from above; they come invariably from below," like trees growing upward from earth. The silent folk follow the unconscious forces; the "refuse of man's psyche" collected in compendia (*Anthropophyteia*, Ellis, Freud) is valuable "because they serve his purpose." The result: the world takes on an uglier face until nothing outward draws us away from inner reality; Theosophy (karma, reincarnation) likewise relativizes this world. Even physics (relativity, atomic structure) "volatilizes our material world." Hence the turn to psychic life for the certainty the world denies.
- **Western self-criticism [s.4]:** "The Occidental burns incense to himself." The Pueblo governor-friend's remark that whites are "always wanting something, always restless... They think they are all crazy." Jung criticizes Western missions, polygamy suppression leading to prostitution and disease (Uganda figure), and the opium trade. [note: Jung's phrase "the Aryan bird of prey" appears here; it is the period's term, quoted only as his own rhetoric.] Freud's "unclean work" had to begin the psychology of the West because acquaintance with psychic reality starts with what repels us.
- Superstition and perversity are "transitional or embryonic stages from which new and riper forms will emerge." Western monumental outer world is imposing because we spent all that is imposing in us on it; the inner world is "shabby and insufficient."
- Resistance is healthy: relativism taken as a final principle is destructive. "The psychic depths are nature, and nature is creative life"; "Light is always born of darkness." Whatever values relativism destroys, the psyche will produce their equivalents (the Anquetil example shows psychic life surviving its eclipse).
- **The East's influence:** the West overpowers the Orient from without; the East's psychic proficiency may throw the Western spiritual world into confusion from within; the Roman Empire became Asiatic (Mithraism from Cilicia, Christianity). Western Theosophy is an amateurish imitation; astrology is Oriental daily bread; Indian texts anticipate sexual studies; Chinese science rests on indetermination; Richard Wilhelm showed him processes found by analytical psychology described in ancient Chinese texts; Oskar Schmitz traced parallels between psychoanalysis and yoga. The Theosophists' Mahatmas in the Himalayas are a myth that is "an important psychological truth": the East "lies within us." New spiritual forms will rise from our own depths; we may learn the stability that comes when claims of spirit are as imperative as social necessities, though far from it "in this age of Americanization." New forms arise "from need and distress," not wishes.

**5. The crux, the body, and the close [s.4].**
- Crux of the problem: the fascination psychic life exerts on modern man; pessimists call it decadence, optimists a promise. When the attraction is so strong that man is not repelled by what he finds, it is not sickly. Along the "great highroads" all seems outworn; modern man explores byways, like the Greco-Roman who turned to Asian mysteries; the same relentlessness as Buddha sweeping aside his two million gods.
- Possible optical illusion: millions regard these things as irrelevant; the biblical God is still living; the clever European thinks religion fit for masses and women. Jung concedes he may be refuted "like a man who predicts a thunderstorm when there is not a cloud in the sky"; but what matters in psychic life is below the horizon of consciousness ("flowers that open only in the night"; the day's life is a bad dream for many).
- **Counter-trend (practical commitment):** League of Nations/internationalism, sport, the cinema and jazz. Sport and modern dancing express a new valuation of the body; cinema and detective story allow danger-free experience of repressed excitement. The body's "revenge upon the spirit"; Keyserling's chauffeur as culture-hero. Jung's resolution: "spirit is the living body seen from within, and the body the outer manifestation of the living spirit—the two being really one"; going beyond the present level of consciousness must give the body its due. Quotation from Hölderlin: "Danger itself fosters the rescuing power."
- The West accelerates (American tempo), opposite of quietism; tension between outer and inner. Final modesty: his voice is "but one voice," his ideas "a subjective confession."

**Cross-references:** modern attitude toward religion and the pull of Gnostic experience is the background for Section G.

## Section G. "Psychotherapists or the Clergy" (Chapter 11) [s.4]

*Complete essay in this range; it is the last essay and ends the book.*

**1. Medicine discovers the psyche [s.4].**
- Urgent patient problems, more than research questions, drove medical psychology. Medicine borrowed from experimental psychology, psychopathology, psychiatry, neurology; a gulf opened between neurologist and psychotherapist (rooted in hypnotism research). The psychogenic neuroses are neither organic nerve disease nor psychosis; they have causes and cures that are psychic. The discovery that the psyche is an etiological factor in disease was unwelcome to nineteenth-century medicine, which adopted material causation.
- Attempts to reduce the psychic factor to drives: Freud (sexual drive) and Adler (power); Adler's is further from physiology. The instinct concept is vague. Test: no glandular extract cures neurosis, while psychic methods do. The doctor's words are "only vibrations in the air" but effective because of **meaning**; fictions and opinions are intangible, yet "nothing is more effective in the psychic and even the psychophysical realm." Drives and psychic processes condition each other.
- Freudian and Adlerian theories are not wrong but **one-sided**: they leave out the psyche, suit those who think they have no spiritual needs, and "do not give meaning enough to life. And it is only the meaningful that sets us free."

**2. Neurosis as suffering without meaning; the four gifts [s.4].**
- Common sense and science do not answer spiritual suffering. A psychoneurosis is "the suffering of a human being who has not discovered what life means for him." All creativeness in spirit and every psychic advance arises from mental suffering caused by spiritual stagnation and sterility.
- The doctor must convey "the healing fiction, the meaning that quickens." Doctor's dilemma: he sees that the patient has no love (only sexuality), no faith (afraid to grope), no hope (disillusioned), no understanding (failed to read his existence). Educated patients refuse the clergyman; philosophy leaves them cold. What they need is **faith, hope, love and insight**, which are "gifts of grace," not taught or learned, "since they come through experience." "Experiences cannot be made. They happen." We can draw closer, but not through "methods." The way to experience is "a venture which requires us to commit ourselves with our whole being."
- **Saul's conversion:** it came not from true love or true faith but from his hatred of Christians, following with conviction the course in which he was most mistaken; this brings the therapist "shoulder to shoulder with the clergyman: the question of good and evil."

**3. Doctor versus clergyman [s.4].**
- Sufferers consult the doctor first (they suppose physical illness). The clergyman lacks psychological knowledge and authority. Catholic priest has Church authority, economic security, even Jesuit psychological teaching (Jung's own writings studied in Rome before any Protestant pastor looked at them); the Protestant minister, family-burdened and without a monastic fallback, has a harder task. "Nowadays people go to the psychotherapist rather than to the clergyman" (a Protestant minister). Many clergy borrow from Freud and Adler, which are "psychology without the psyche" and hinder meaningful experience; most psychotherapists are Freudians or Adlerians, so most patients are alienated from a spiritual standpoint.
- Educated opinion leads mass opinion by about twenty years (Büchner's *Force and Matter* in German public libraries; Spain's sudden transformation).
- **Jung's clinical statement:** over thirty years he treated many hundreds, mostly Protestants, some Jews, and "not more than five or six believing Catholics." Among all patients over thirty-five, none had a problem that was not in the last resort **finding a religious outlook on life**; none healed who did not regain it; this has nothing to do with creed or church membership. He urges clergy and psychotherapists to join forces.
- **The questionnaire (Aarau students' conference):** answered by Swiss, German and French Protestants and a few Catholics: chose the doctor, 57 percent of Protestants and 25 percent of Catholics; chose the clergyman, 8 percent of Protestants and 58 percent of Catholics; undecided, about 35 percent of Protestants and 17 percent of Catholics. Reasons against the clergy: lack of psychological knowledge (52 percent), dogmatic/traditional bias (28 percent). Relatives of clergy all voted against the clergy. Jung calls it "a straw in the wind," valid only for educated people. Neuroses seem more frequent with the decline of religious life, though no statistics exist; many patients come not because of neurosis but because they find no meaning in life.

**4. The modern attitude and the doctor's honest confession [s.4].**
- To "What is the meaning of my life?" the patient expects nothing from clergy, philosophers or physicians but perhaps from the analyst; confessing that the therapist has no answer often begins the patient's confidence.
- Modern man's "ineradicable aversion for traditional opinions and inherited truths": he is "a Bolshevist" in the world of spirit and wants to experiment. Jung sets aside the pathological pseudo-moderns and speaks of able, courageous, upright people who have repudiated tradition for decent reasons: they cannot reconcile science and religion; do not feel redeemed by Christ's death; cannot compel belief; find sin relative; "why should not Buddha be in the right, also?"
- **Critique of Freud's causal approach:** the sexual disturbances are real but secondary; the ship-and-leak analogy (crew studying the chemistry of the water). When life loses meaning, "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" breaks loose and the drives erupt. Only a still-existing cause keeps a neurosis active (tuberculosis analogy: current foci, not past infection). Jung therefore takes religious problems as relevant and possible causes, and can admit the patient's doubts honestly ("Buddha may be right as well as Jesus"), which the pastor finds hard. The "silent Cal" Coolidge anecdote: the minister "was against" sin.

**5. Unprejudiced objectivity and self-acceptance [s.4].**
- The doctor, too, has moral scruples; the patient feels accepted only if the very worst in him is accepted; this cannot be done by words but only through the doctor's sincerity and his attitude toward his own evil side. Passing judgment, spoken or silent, breaks contact; agreeing offhand also estranges. The needed quality is **"unprejudiced objectivity"**: "a human quality—a kind of deep respect for facts and events and for the person who suffers from them—a respect for the secret of such a human life." The truly religious person has it. "We cannot change anything unless we accept it. Condemnation does not liberate, it oppresses."
- **The least of the brethren within:** feeding the hungry and loving one's enemy are great virtues; but if the poorest beggar, the enemy, is *within me* and I am the one who needs my own kindness, the Christian attitude reverses ("Raca") and we condemn and hide it. Accepting oneself in all wretchedness is the hardest task; people busy themselves with others' sins to flee themselves; those who cannot do this with impunity "break down on the road to Damascus and succumb to a neurosis." Only he who has fully accepted himself has unprejudiced objectivity, and no one can boast of it. Christ offered his traditional bias as sacrifice to the god in himself and lived without regard for the Pharisees' standards.
- **Imitation of Christ:** copying his life (aping stigmata) versus living one's *own* life as truly as he lived his, which is much harder and would be "misjudged, derided, tortured and crucified." Jung would not disturb a monk. A physician must show patients how to live without becoming neurotic.
- **Neurosis defined:** "an inner cleavage—the state of being at war with oneself"; a "dissociation of personality" between sensual and spiritual man or between ego and **shadow** (Faust's "two souls"). Healing is a religious problem: as Christian forgiveness cures civil war outwardly, the same must be applied inwardly: learn "how he is to love the enemy in his own heart and call the wolf his brother." Modern man wants to live his own individual life, not imitate Christ.
- **The sacred egoism:** the neurotic's egoism is a true will of God; the doctor must help the patient prevail in it; it may drive him into isolation, where he learns the value of his fellows' love and discovers the helpful powers of his own nature. The archdemon of egoism leads to the "ingathering which religious experience demands"; this is **enantiodromia** (reversal into the opposite) allowing reunion of the warring halves and ending the civil war.

**6. The risk of experiment; the opening of the unconscious [s.4].**
- Acceptance of the shadow "verges on the impossible": granting the right of existence to the unreasonable, senseless and evil. Modern man insists on living every side and breaks with tradition to see things' value in themselves (a German society asked whether incest is to be reprobated and for facts against it). Old arguments have lost force after nineteenth-century education (blind faith from churches, rationalism from universities). Jung still sympathizes with the daring enterprise to bring meaning back "on the basis of fresh and unprejudiced experience" (what if Paul had been talked out of Damascus?).
- The therapist must decide case by case whether to stand by a patient in a daring misadventure, with no fixed ideas of right; "only that which acts, is actual"; if an error proves more effective than a truth, follow the error first. "Light has need of darkness—otherwise how could it appear as light?"
- **Limit of Freudian analysis:** it makes the shadow conscious and "brings into action the civil war that was latent, and lets it go at that." Man has never held his own single-handed against the powers of the unconscious; he needed religion's help. Opening the unconscious means intense spiritual suffering, like a flourishing civilization overrun by barbarians or a dam burst. The World War showed how thin the walls are. Hence religious and magical practice; the medicine-man is also priest; "religions are systems of healing for psychic illness" (especially Christianity and Buddhism). "Man is never helped in his suffering by what he thinks for himself, but only by revelations of a wisdom greater than his own."
- Patients thrust the therapist into the role of priest; the therapist must tread with the patient the path of his illness, hoping the rescuing forces will rise from the same depths.

**7. Archetypes and the awakening of the psyche [s.4].**
- Jung did not know where this would lead until he found the "collective unconscious" whose contents he calls **archetypes**. Eruptions of the unconscious recur; consciousness is weak in infancy and in early history and must be built up anew in each child. Instinctive defense-mechanisms developed, appearing in fantasy as **helpful images** ineradicably fixed in the psyche. Science can establish these factors and offer a hypothesis about their source, but "this only thrusts the problem a stage back": whence consciousness, what is the psyche? "At this point all science ends."
- At the culmination of the illness destructive powers convert into healing forces: **the archetypes come to independent life and serve as spiritual guides**, replacing the inadequate ego. A religious person says guidance comes from God; with most patients Jung avoids that formulation and says "the psyche has awakened to spontaneous life." The transformation occurs when dreams or fantasies carry themes whose source in consciousness cannot be shown; to the patient it is a revelation to meet something strange that is not the "I" and is beyond personal caprice: "the beginning of the cure."
- Illustration is difficult because the process is subtle; effects often come from the deep impression made by dreams that treat his difficulties independently, from fantasy that points to something the conscious mind was unprepared for, or from connected archetypal contents; sometimes visions are seen or inner voices heard, "manifestations of the spirit directly experienced today as they have been from time immemorial." The sufferer is rewarded and can reconcile the warfare and bridge the split "upon a higher level."

**8. Final statement and close of the book [s.4].**
- Details of method are omitted; Jung's purpose was to set forth the **attitude** of the psychotherapist, which "is infinitely more important than the theories and methods." He claims his account of the modern spiritual outlook and of cure is "the unvarnished truth" and welcomes the sympathy of clergy, while recognizing the difficulties of cooperation.
- Self-description: "on the extreme left wing of the congress of Protestant opinion"; as a Swiss an "inveterate democrat," yet "nature is aristocratic and, what is even more, esoteric" (*Quod licet Jovi, non licet bovi*). Those forgiven much have loved much. A vast number of people belong in the Catholic Church because they are suitably housed there; a primitive religion suits primitive people better than Christianity, which they can only ape; there must be protestants against the Catholic Church and against Protestantism, since manifestations of the spirit are as varied as Creation.
- Last lines: the **living spirit** grows and outgrows its earlier expressions, freely chooses the men in whom it lives, and pursues its goal in many ways through history; the names and forms men give it are "only the changing leaves and blossoms on the stem of the eternal tree."
