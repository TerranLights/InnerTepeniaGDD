## Overview of this range

The book is, in Jung's own phrase, "an extended commentary on a practical analysis of the prodromal stages of schizophrenia" [p.39, Foreword, 4th ed.]. The raw case material is a set of fantasies (poems, visions, dream images) published in 1906 by Théodore Flournoy under the pseudonym "Frank Miller." Jung uses each fantasy as a thread ("the Ariadne thread") to lead through "the labyrinth of symbolistic parallels," that is, **amplification**: laying beside each image the mythological, religious, literary and historical material that shows its archetypal context.

In this part Jung (1) states why he wrote the book and why it was revised (the forewords); (2) argues that there are **two kinds of thinking**, directed (verbal, adapted, tiring) and fantasy-thinking (image-based, associative, archaic, effortless), and that the second is the source of dream, myth and symbol, not a degraded or infantile form of the first; (3) begins the Miller material: the opening "anamnesis" (suggestibility, identification, introversion), the **Hymn of Creation** (a dream poem that disguises, and also transforms, an erotic impression into a religious image of the Creator-God), and the **Song of the Moth** (a longing for the sun that opens onto sun-hero, dying-god and light/fire symbolism); (4) on the way develops his first account of **libido** as psychic energy that creates the **God-image** by way of **archetypal** patterns, of the compensating and purposive character of the unconscious, of symbol versus sign, and of the Christian and Mithraic mastering of instinct; (5) opens Part Two with a retrospective and the start of a full discussion of the **concept of libido**, contrasting Freud's sexual definition with Jung's energic one.

## Foreword to the Fourth (Swiss) Edition (September 1950) [pp.36–40]

- **Why the book needed revision.** Written 37 years earlier at top speed amid medical practice; "the sins of my youth." It felt like "a landslide": the explosion of psychic contents that "could find no room" in the "constricting atmosphere of Freudian psychology" [p.36]. [§ foreword]
- **The objection to Freud.** Not his theory of neurosis or dreams, but the "reductive causalism" of his whole outlook and "almost complete disregard of the teleological directedness which is so characteristic of everything psychic" [pp.36–37]. Freud's *The Future of an Illusion* represents the "outmoded rationalism and scientific materialism" of the late nineteenth century.
- **The book's aim.** To free medical psychology from "the subjective and personalistic bias" of Freud and Adler and make it possible to understand the unconscious "as an objective and collective psyche." Personalism "left no room for objective, impersonal facts" except instinctive dynamisms [p.37].
- **"Living with a myth."** Right after the manuscript was done Jung realized "what it means to live with a myth, and what it means to live without one" [p.37]. He cites a Church Father's definition of myth: "what is believed always, everywhere, by everybody." A person who lives outside myth is "like one uprooted," without true link to past, ancestral life, or contemporary society; reason's "plaything" never "grips his vitals." **Image:** individual consciousness is the "flower and the fruit of a season" sprung from "the perennial rhizome beneath the earth"; the psyche's ancestry "goes back many millions of years" [p.38].
- **The personal question.** "What is the myth you are living?" He found no answer, so he made knowing "his" myth "the task of tasks," because an analyst who does not know his own "personal equation" cannot make due allowance for it in treating others. This led to years of investigating subjective products of the unconscious and of devising methods to explore them [pp.38–39].
- **On method and the structure of the book.** The case symptoms are "the Ariadne thread" through the "labyrinth of symbolistic parallels … the amplifications which are absolutely essential if we wish to establish the meaning of the archetypal context." Such parallels "take up an incredible amount of space." **Principle:** "the deeper you go, the broader the base becomes"; it never narrows to a single point such as "a psychic trauma" [p.39].
- **Comparative material is indispensable** for psychology just as for comparative anatomy; knowledge of the subjective contents of consciousness "tells us next to nothing about the real, subterranean life of the psyche." Medical knowledge of neurosis is "information about an illness, but not knowledge of the soul that is ill" [pp.39–40].
- **Date and mood.** Written in 1911, in Jung's thirty-sixth year, the start of the second half of life when a *metanoia* (mental transformation) often occurs; also the time of the loss of the friendship with Freud [p.40].

## Foreword to the Third (German) Edition (November 1937) [pp.41–42]

- Essentially unaltered. The book's "thankless task": to show that problems of the human psyche cannot be tackled with "the meagre equipment of the doctor's consulting-room" nor with the layman's "understanding of the world and human nature." Psychology needs the humane sciences and the **history of the human mind**, which alone orders the empirical material and reveals "the functional significance of the collective contents of the unconscious."
- "The psyche is not something unalterably given, but a product of its own continuous development." Neurotic conflicts can be caused by "historically conditioned attitudes and mental factors," not only by glands or personal relationships.
- Rationalization alone is inadequate; "irrational factors play the largest, indeed the decisive, role in all processes of psychic transformation."

## Foreword to the Second (German) Edition (November 1924) [pp.43–45]

- Text unaltered; Jung still maintains "in substance and in principle" the views expressed.
- **Misunderstandings to correct.** (a) The book is not Jung's method of treatment; it works out the fantasy material of an unknown young American woman, pseudonym Frank Miller, first published by Flournoy. Flournoy told Jung he had "hit off the young woman's mentality very well"; in 1918 an American colleague treating her for the later schizophrenic outbreak wrote that the exposition was so exhaustive that personal acquaintance taught him not "one iota more" [pp.43–44]. (b) The heavy mythological and etymological material is **not** meant to propound mythological hypotheses or a general theory of myth; it is used "because it belongs, directly or indirectly, to the basic assumptions of the Miller fantasies." Any light thrown on mythologems is "a welcome by-product" [p.44].
- **The real purpose:** to work out "the implications of all those historical and spiritual factors which come together in the involuntary products of individual fantasy." Besides personal sources, creative fantasy draws on "the forgotten and long buried primitive mind with its host of images … in the mythologies of all ages and all peoples. The sum of these images constitutes the **collective unconscious**, a heritage which is potentially present in every individual," "the psychic correlate of the differentiation of the human brain." That is why mythological images "arise spontaneously over and over again" and agree everywhere; the substratum is "this same human psyche and this same human brain" [pp.44–45].

## Author's Note to the First American/English Edition ([1916?]) [pp.46–47]

- The task was to investigate an individual fantasy system; the problems uncovered are so large that the work is only "a superficial orientation toward those paths" that future investigators may explore. Jung is not in sympathy with suppressing working hypotheses because they may be erroneous; scientific work is "not a dogmatic contest, but … work done for the increase and deepening of knowledge." Thanks to his wife and friends.

## Epigraph (Ferrero) [p.48]

- A theory, even if partly false, is useful because it "throws light on phenomena which no one has observed" and forces examination of unstudied facts; the scientist has "a moral duty" to risk error and accept criticism so science progresses (Ferrero, *Les Lois psychologiques du symbolisme*).

# PART ONE

## I. Introduction [pp.49–53] [§1–3]

- **Starting point: Freud on Oedipus.** Reading Freud's *Interpretation of Dreams* without moral indignation, one is struck by the remark that an individual conflict, the "incest fantasy," lies at the root of the Oedipus legend. Jung compares the impression to finding an ancient relic (a Corinthian capital, a fragment of inscription) in a modern street: it opens "the higher continuity of history" and shows that people in antiquity were "just as convinced as we are of the uniqueness of their lives" [pp.49–50, §1].
- **The moral.** A man can have an unconscious, all-consuming passion for his mother which complicates his whole life, so the fate of Oedipus "seems not one whit overdrawn." Following Freud we learn that these possibilities, "too weak to compel actual incest," still cause considerable psychic disturbance [p.50]. Admitting them arouses moral revulsion and resistance that "blind the intellect and render self-knowledge impossible." If we separate "objective knowledge" from "emotional value-judgments," the gulf between our age and antiquity is bridged [pp.50–51].
- **Key claim:** "an identity of fundamental human conflicts which is independent of time and place." What horrified the Greeks is still true for us, "if we give up the vain illusion that we are different, i.e., morally better, than the ancients." Penetrating "the blocked subterranean passages of our own psyches" gives both inner sympathy and intellectual comprehension of classical civilization, and "a firm foothold outside our own culture" from which to understand its foundations [p.51, §1].
- **Prior work in this line (§2).** Riklin, Abraham, Rank, Maeder, Jones, Silberer ("Phantasie und Mythos"), Pfister. Leitmotiv: using insight from the unconscious of modern man to find clues to historical problems. The analogy between historical products and the modern individual applies to symbolism and to "the individual mechanisms governing the unconscious elaboration of motifs" [pp.51–52].
- **Jung's own program (§3).** Investigators have mostly analyzed individual problems; they must now "broaden the basis of this analysis by a comparative study of the historical material" (as Freud did with Leonardo). Psychology illuminates history and, conversely, **history illuminates individual psychology**. Jung's later writings focus on historical and ethnological parallels; he credits Erich Neumann (*The Origins and History of Consciousness*) with massively advancing this and carrying forward the ideas that originally impelled the book [pp.52–53].

## II. Two Kinds of Thinking [pp.54–85] [§4–46]

### A. Dream symbolism and the question "how are dreams symbolical at all?" [§4–11]

- **Basic principle** of analytical psychology: dream-images are to be understood symbolically, as hiding a meaning, not taken literally. The idea is ancient (Egyptian and Chaldaean interpreters, Joseph, Daniel, Artemidorus); an idea so old and widely held "must be true in some way … psychologically true" [p.54, §4].
- **Translation into psychology** [§5–6]: the dream arises "in an unknown part of the psyche and prepares the dreamer for the events of the following day." The old belief that a god or demon spoke in symbolic language becomes: the dream is a series of apparently contradictory, meaningless images that yield a clear meaning when properly translated.
- **Sexual symbolism, not sexual origin** [§7–8]: sexual metaphors fill common speech but are applied to non-sexual matters; sexual symbolism does not mean the interest using it is erotic. Affects can come from conflict situations such as self-preservation. Many dream-images do have an erotic aspect, esp. the **motif of assault** in women's dreams (burglars, thieves, murderers; instruments such as lance, sword, revolver; wild animals and snakes). Illustrations: Franz Stuck's snake pictures (Vice, Sin, Lust; pl. X) and Mörike's "Girl's First Love Song" (a net, an eel or a snake) [pp.55–58].
- **Case: the Arch of Constantine dream** [p.58, §9]. A young woman dreams a cannon before the triumphal Arch of Constantine, a bird to the right, a man to the left; a cannonball enters her purse and she holds it as something precious; then only the stock of the cannon remains with the motto "In hoc signo vinces." The sexual symbolism is obvious. **Jung's point:** if the realization is new to the dreamer, the dream has been interpreted; if she has always known it, then the interpretation is mere repetition. Endless sexual interpretation leads to the "monotony" Freud himself complained of; the sexual symbolism may be just "as good a façon de parler as any other," a dream-language that "degenerates into jargon" ("Canis panem somniat, piscator pisces," the dog dreams of bread, the fisherman of fish). Exception: when a motif repeats because it was never understood and the conscious mind needs to reorient itself by recognizing the **compensation** it expresses. The closing motto points to a deeper meaning reachable only if the dreamer becomes conscious enough to admit an erotic conflict [pp.58–59].
- **The astonishing fact** [§10]: conscious activity is influenced by products which "seem to obey quite other laws."

### B. Directed thinking [pp.59–67, §11–17]

- **Description.** Ordinary conscious thinking: from an "initial" or "leading" idea we pass, guided by a sense of direction, to a series of connected ideas. Nothing symbolical in this [p.59].
- **Directed or logical thinking = reality-thinking** ("thinking with directed attention"): thinking in words, directed outwards, a thinking adapted to reality in which inner images follow one another in the same causal sequence as outer events. It is **fatiguing**, so used for short periods; biologically it is "psychic assimilation" leaving exhaustion like any vital achievement [p.60, §11].
- **Its material is language and verbal concepts**, "directed outwards and used as a bridge," with a single purpose, communication: "so long as we think directedly, we think for others and speak to others" [pp.60–61, §12]. Language originated as emotive and imitative sounds (terror, anger, love; imitation of water, thunder, wind, animals); onomatopoeic vestiges remain (Jung's examples: *rauschen, rieseln, rinnen, river, ruscello, Rhein*; *Wasser, wissen, pissen, piscis, Fisch*).
- **Language is a system of signs or symbols** denoting real occurrences or "their echo in the human soul" [§13]. Quote from Anatole France: we think with words, which is "sensual and brings us back to nature"; a metaphysician has only "the perfected cries of monkeys and dogs" to build a world system [pp.61–62]. **Jung's gloss** [§14]: even the loneliest thinker's directed thinking is "the first stirrings of a cry to our companions that water has been found, or the bear been killed, or that a storm is approaching." Abelard: "Speech is generated by the intellect and in turn generates intellect." Hence the craving of Schopenhauer or Nietzsche for recognition: the "herd instinct" is stronger than contempt for the rabble [p.62].
- **Limits of the claim.** Language is wider than speech (the deaf-mute has his "language"); directed thinking derives historically from primitive words (Wundt: words lose their concrete significance and become signs for general ideas, so abstract thought arises from changes of meaning) [pp.62–63, §14].
- **Debate** [§15–16]: Jodl infers a "supra-linguistic" (Erdmann: "hypological") thinking, which Jung grants exists but says "is not logical thinking." Jung sides with **Baldwin** (long quotation): judgment is already "socialized"; thought and language advance together by trial and error ("meanings as worth more than they are as yet recognized"); language keeps a "synnomic" dual reference (personal and social meaning); it is "the register of tradition, the record of racial conquest, the deposit of all the gains made by the genius of individuals." Jung notes Mauthner's skeptical view that thinking is speech and nothing more, and that Baldwin leans toward the primacy of speech [pp.63–66].
- **Historical claim** [§17]: directed thinking is "an instrument of culture"; centuries of educational training forced it from the subjective, individual sphere to the objective, social sphere, which produced modern empiricism and technics. This explains why the ancients, with excellent mathematics, mechanics and craftsmanship, never developed technology: they lacked "training in directed thinking," the capacity to concentrate interest on the transformations of inanimate matter and reproduce natural processes artificially. **"The secret of cultural development is the mobility and disposability of psychic energy."** Directed thinking as we know it "is a more or less modern acquisition" [pp.66–67].

### C. Fantasy-thinking (non-directed thinking) [pp.67–72, §18–25]

- **Definition** [§18–20]. When we do not think directedly, thoughts lack leading ideas and "float, sink or rise according to their specific gravity" (Kuelpe: absence of the "inner act of the will" produces an "automatic play of ideas"; William James: "merely associative" thinking, trains of images suggested by one another, of "empirical concretes, not abstractions," even higher animals capable of it). It does not tire us; it leads away from reality into fantasies of the past or future; verbal form ceases and "image piles on image, feeling on feeling"; things are arranged "as one would like them to be." Common speech calls it "dreaming." Falling asleep shows daydreaming shades into night-dreaming [pp.67–68].
- **The two kinds of thinking compared (Jung's summary, §20):**

| | Directed thinking | Dreaming or fantasy-thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Material | speech elements, for communication | contents ready to hand |
| Effort | difficult, exhausting | effortless, spontaneous |
| Guidance | conscious aim | unconscious motives |
| Relation to reality | copies reality, tries to act on it; produces innovation and adaptation | turns away from reality, sets free subjective tendencies; unproductive for adaptation |

- **Historical development** [§21–24]. Modern science and technology owe their existence "simply and solely" to training in directed thinking. Scholasticism supplied a "dialectical gymnastics": its subjects came from fantasies of the past (how many angels on a needle point; could Christ have redeemed as a pea) and its goal was rhetorical victory in disputation, not transformation of reality. Yet the scholastic spirit (Aquinas, Duns Scotus, Abelard, Ockham) is "the mother of our modern scientific method," giving the word an "absolute meaning" and laying the foundations of "a solidly built intellectual function, the *sine qua non* of modern science and technology" [pp.68–69].
- **The ancients** [§23–24]. Further back, "science" dissolves in mist. "Our material knowledge has increased, but not our intelligence"; "We have become rich in knowledge, but poor in wisdom." The classical mind was "saturated with mythology." "All the creative power that modern man pours into science and technics the man of antiquity devoted to his myths," hence the kaleidoscopic syncretism and constant rejuvenation of Greek myths. Their activity was aesthetic: not to understand the real world objectively but "to adapt it aesthetically to subjective fantasies and expectations" (sun as great Father, moon as fruitful Mother; everything animated, anthropomorphic or theriomorphic; the sun's disc given wings or feet, pl. Ib). Children think in much the same way (animated dolls and toys) [pp.70–71].
- **Dreams and regression (Freud)** [§25]. Waking thought shows "progression" (stimulus advances from perception through association to motor end); dreaming shows "regression" (from the pre-conscious or unconscious back to the perceptual system), giving "sensuous clarity … almost hallucinatory vividness." "In regression the fabric of the dream-thoughts is resolved into its raw material." A second regression is to infantile memories ("historical" regression): the dream is a modified memory projected into the present; dreams preserve the psychical apparatus's primary method of working "abandoned as being inefficient," as the bow and arrow turns up in the nursery [pp.71–72].

### D. Ontogeny and phylogeny; myth as dream [pp.72–76, §26–30]

- **Parallel** [§26]. Mythological thinking of ancient man resembles the thinking of children, primitives and dreams; as comparative anatomy shows embryonic stages repeating racial history, so there is a probable correspondence between **ontogenesis and phylogenesis** in psychology: infantile thinking and dream-thinking recapitulate earlier evolutionary stages [pp.72–73].
- **Nietzsche** (long quotation, §27): in sleep "we pass through the whole thought of earlier humanity"; as man reasons in dreams, humanity reasoned awake for thousands of years (the first cause that occurred sufficed as explanation); dreaming is "a recreation for the brain" and shows how lately strict discrimination of cause and effect developed; "we pass about half our lives in this condition" [pp.73–74].
- **Myth as dream** [§28–29]. Freud: myths are "distorted vestiges of the wishful phantasies of whole nations, the age-long dreams of youthful humanity." Rank: myth as collective dream of a people. Riklin on fairytales; Abraham: "the myth is a fragment of the superseded infantile psychic life of the race … dreams are the myths of the individual." [pp.74]
- **Jung's correction** [§29]: put "a large question-mark" after calling myth infantile. Myths "are on the contrary the most mature product of that young humanity"; as man's fishy ancestors with gill-slits were fully developed creatures, not embryos, so the myth-making man "was a grown reality and not a four-year-old child." "Myth is certainly not an infantile phantasm, but one of the most important requisites of primitive life" [p.75].
- **Mythology is not merely taught** [§30]. If all world traditions were cut off, "the whole of mythology and the whole history of religion would start all over again with the next generation." One can withhold the material from a child but not "the need for mythology" or the ability to make it. Only a few individuals throw off mythology in epochs of exceptional intellectual exuberance, "the masses never." "Enlightenment avails nothing, it merely destroys a transitory manifestation, but not the creative impulse." Man needed "the strongest inner compulsion … the irrational force of instinct" to invent religious beliefs whose absurdity Tertullian had pointed out [p.75–76].

### E. How fantasies are made [pp.76–79, §31–36]

- Any lessening of interest or slight fatigue ends directed adaptation and fantasy takes over. From poets we learn much about fantasy, from scientists little; psychotherapists first showed fantasies "go in typical cycles" and are **compensatory** (the stammerer imagines himself an orator, the poor man a millionaire, the oppressed wage victorious war; Demosthenes made it true) [§32–33].
- **The "family romance" example** [§34]: the adolescent fancies he is the child of a rich count raised by foster-parents. For a normal child it is a passing idea; in the ancient world it was "a legitimate truth": heroes such as Romulus and Remus (pl. II), Moses, Semiramis were foundlings; others descended from gods; noble houses traced descent to heroes. **The adolescent's fantasy is "a re-echo of an ancient folk-belief."** The erotic assault dream likewise echoes mythology (rape of Persephone, Deianira, Europa, the Sabine women; marriage by capture) [pp.76–77].
- **General law** [§35]: "what, with us, is a subterranean fantasy was once open to the light of day … a conscious custom or a general belief." Only eighty generations separate us from Greek culture's Golden Age, an "almost imperceptible span" against the stretch to Neanderthal man. Ferrero (quoted): man's psychology at bottom does not change; "the fundamental laws of the mind remain the same"; strange institutions can be explained by common laws of mind we recognize in ourselves [pp.77–79].
- **Vestiges** [§36]: Dionysian phallagogies and theriomorphic gods have dwindled to the dove, lamb and cock on church towers, but in childhood "archaic thinking and feeling once more rise up in us," and we carry fantasy-thinking "which corresponds to the antique state of mind" alongside adapted thinking, just as bodies retain vestiges of obsolete organs [p.79].

### F. Fantasy-thinking is not pathological; instinct and the archaic [pp.79–82, §37–40]

- **Source of the mind's aptitude for symbolic expression** [§37]: the distinction between directed, adapted thinking and subjective thinking driven by inner motives. Uncorrected, the latter produces "an overwhelmingly subjective and distorted picture of the world," called infantile, autoerotic, or (Bleuler) "autistic" (ideal case: schizophrenia; autoeroticism is more characteristic of neurosis). Jung objects that this brings a normal process "dangerously close to the pathological," attributing it to the fact that doctors first evaluated it [pp.79–80].
- **Is the inner motive an objective fact?** Jung asks whether the mainly unconscious inner motive guiding fantasy is not itself an objective fact, rather than a mere distortion of the objective world-picture; Freud himself said unconscious motives are grounded on instinct and half admitted their archaic nature [p.80].
- **Key claim** [§38]: the unconscious bases of dreams and fantasies "are only apparently infantile reminiscences. In reality we are concerned with **primitive or archaic thought-forms, based on instinct**, which naturally emerge more clearly in childhood." They are neither infantile nor pathological, and pathological vocabulary should not be used for them; myth is far from autoerotic or autistic. "The instinctive, archaic basis of the mind is a matter of plain objective fact," no more dependent on individual choice than "the inherited structure and functioning of the brain." "Just as the body has its evolutionary history … so too does the psyche" [pp.80–81].
- **Levels of fantasy-thinking** [§39]: directed thinking is entirely conscious; fantasy-thinking is partly conscious, partly in half-shadow, partly wholly unconscious (inferred only indirectly). "Through fantasy-thinking, directed thinking is brought into contact with the oldest layers of the human mind." Products, in ascending distance from consciousness: waking dreams or daydreams (Freud, Flournoy, Pick), ordinary dreams (baffling, understood only via inferred unconscious contents), and, in split-off complexes, wholly unconscious fantasy-systems with "a marked tendency to constitute themselves as separate personalities" [p.81].
- **Introversion and regression** [§40]: introversion in later life regresses to infantile reminiscences with a slight archaic tinge; "with stronger introversion and regression the archaic features become more pronounced" [pp.81–82].

### G. Case: the Abbé Oegger (from Anatole France) [pp.82–85, §41–46]

- **Story** [§41]: a dreamy priest, preoccupied with whether Judas was damned, reasons that God chose Judas as an instrument of redemption and so could not damn him; he prays for a sign, feels a heavenly touch on his shoulder, and resolves to preach God's unending mercy. Soon after he leaves the Catholic Church and becomes a Swedenborgian [p.82].
- **Interpretation** [§42–44]: the Judas legend is a typical motif, the betrayal of a hero by a close associate (Siegfried/Hagen, Baldur/Loki, Caesar/Brutus), repeated because "envy does not let mankind sleep in peace." **Rule about myth:** tradition perpetuates not ordinary events but those "which express the universal and ever-renewed thoughts of mankind"; culture-heroes and founders of religion are "the purest condensations of typical mythological motifs, behind which the individual figures entirely disappear" [pp.82–83]. **Meaning:** Oegger was the Judas who betrayed his Lord; he first assured himself of God's mercy "in order to play the role of Judas undisturbed" [p.83].
- **General mechanism** [§44–45]: conscious fantasy may be woven of mythological or other material; it "should not be taken literally, but must be interpreted according to its meaning." Doubts apparently about the historical Judas "in reality revolve round his own personality, which was seeking a way to freedom." **Conscious fantasies "illustrate, through the use of mythological material, certain tendencies in the personality which are either not yet recognized or are recognized no longer"**; these are mostly things regarded as immoral or impossible. Two sequences run in parallel: conscious causal (his doubt about Judas leads him to thinking) and unconscious (because he wanted or had to be Judas, he first made sure of God's goodness). Judas was "the symbol of his own unconscious tendency," used because direct realization would be too painful. Hence "there must be typical myths which serve to work out our racial and national complexes" (Burckhardt: every classical Greek carries "a little bit of Oedipus," every German "a little bit of Faust") [pp.83–84].
- **Transition** [§46]: the next fantasies "owe their existence … to the exclusive activity of the unconscious": those of Miss Frank Miller (Flournoy, 1906, "Quelques faits d'imagination créatrice subconsciente") [pp.84–85].

## III. The Miller Fantasies: Anamnesis [pp.86–92] [§47–55]

- **Premise** [§47]: whenever someone relates fantasies or dreams, he is dealing with "the one that is most painful for him at the moment." The first section of Miller's text, "Phenomena of Transitory Suggestion or of Instantaneous Autosuggestion," shows extraordinary suggestibility and capacity for identification and empathy.
- **Example 1: identification with Christian de Neuvillette** (Rostand's *Cyrano de Bergerac*) [§47–50, pp.86–90]. She feels a piercing pain in her own breast, where Christian is wounded, at the moment Sarah Bernhardt as Roxane throws herself on him. Jung's reading: the theater is "an institution for working out private complexes in public"; comedy's pleasure comes from identifying one's complexes with the actors, tragedy's from the thrill that "what is happening to somebody else may very well happen to you." Her pain signals "a complex in her awaiting a similar solution" ("today to you, tomorrow to me"), the sudden end of a love. The tragic intermezzo of Christian is played against the wider background of Cyrano's unrequited love and misunderstood noble soul; he dies fighting "Falsehood … Compromise … Prejudice, Treachery … Folly," keeping only his panache. **Jung's conjecture:** the identification with Christian "is probably only a cover" for identification with Cyrano.
- **Example 2: memory of the sea** [§51]: a photograph of a steamer evokes a vivid memory ("the throb of the engines, the heave of the waves"), a screen memory suggesting impressive sea-voyage experiences connected with the problem to come.
- **Example 3: the Egyptian statue** [§52]: with a towel round her hair she feels, with "breath-taking clarity," that she is a pedestaled Egyptian statue, stiff-limbed, holding insignia. Jung: she means "I am like an Egyptian statue, just as stiff, wooden, sublime, and impassible."
- **Example 4: magical influence on an artist** [§53–54]: she made him draw landscapes he had never seen; she used him "as he himself used his pencil." Jung: the contrast with the statue betrays an "unspoken need to emphasize her almost magical influence over another person," typical of someone who often fails to establish a real emotional relationship; she consoles herself with imagined suggestive power.
- **Summary** [§55]: the examples are not striking but reveal personal problems; the libido "gained control of certain impressions and intensified them," made possible by "the free-floating energy placed at her disposal by her lack of relation to reality" [pp.91–92].

## IV. The Hymn of Creation [pp.93–141] [§56–114]

### A. The material [§56–61, pp.93–98]

- **The setting.** In 1898, at twenty, Miss Miller traveled by sea (New York, Stockholm, St. Petersburg, Odessa; later Naples). On deck she was absorbed in reveries in which legends and myths of the countries seen "came back to me confusedly, dissolved in a kind of luminous mist in which real things seemed to lose their being, while dreams and ideas took on the aspect of the only true reality" [p.93]. Near the end the ship's officers were kind; in Catania she adapted a sea-chanty after hearing an Italian officer sing on night watch; at Naples she fell briefly ill; she thought of "nothing more serious than the good looks of the officers" [p.95].
- **The dream** (Naples to Leghorn, ending as her mother's voice woke her): the words "when the morning stars sang together" began an idea of creation and "mighty chorales"; confusion with New York oratorio choruses and Milton's *Paradise Lost*; then three stanzas appeared in her handwriting on blue-lined paper [p.96].
- **The poem (summary).** Three stanzas, each: when the Eternal first made Sound / Light / Love, "a myriad ears / eyes / hearts sprang" and ears full of music, eyes full of light and hearts full of love sang "All glory to the God of Sound / Light / Love!" (She revised the first version, "God … Beauty (Light)," months later to be "more exact.") [pp.97–98]

### B. Jung's analysis [§58–94]

- **State of introversion** [§58]: after the city, Miss Miller "breathed freely again on the sea," cut herself off, and reality lost its hold. Jung links this to the prodrome of a mental disturbance in which the patient shuts out reality and sinks into fantasy until he suddenly becomes conscious of the dissociation and in "a sort of panic" makes pathological efforts to get back; this "compensating desire for re-association" is "the psychological rule," valid also for normal people (to a lesser degree) [p.94]. Hence one expects a strong new external impression to follow the introversion.
- **The overlooked impression** [§59–62]: the singing officer (a mere "flirtation" by her account). Jung argues that "relatively weak erotic impressions are often underestimated," esp. where an erotic relationship is socially or morally unthinkable (parent-child, siblings); examples of strange blindness (a mother amused by her small son's erection; a twenty-year-old daughter still on her father's lap). "A certain kind of education … tacitly aims at knowing as little as possible about these unmentionable facts" [p.99]. Initial dreams in analysis often contain erotic comments that counterbalance the patient's underestimation of the doctor [pp.99–100].
- **Regression to the father-imago** [§62–64]: when an impression is denied conscious recognition it "reverts to an earlier form of relationship" (e.g., young girls at first love, with "regressive reactivation of the father-imago"). The masculine Creator-God "is apparently derived from the father-imago" and also serves to replace the infantile relation to the father so the individual can leave the family for society, "naturally … far from exhausting the meaning" [p.100]. The poem is "the religiously and poetically formulated product of an introversion that has regressed back to the father-imago"; the operative impression was the night-watching singer, "when the morning stars sang together" [pp.100–101].
- **Chain of association** [§65]: the singer, the singing morning star, the God of Sound, the Creator, the God of Light, of the sun, of fire, and of Love. That **Sound** comes first has parallels in the creative word (Genesis), in Simon Magus (voice corresponds to the sun), in lamentation cries in the *Poimandres*, and in God's laughter at the creation in a Leiden Papyrus [p.101].
- **Miss Miller's own explanations** [§66–73]: she notes that Anaxagoras's whirlwind makes cosmos from chaos (a whirlwind makes noise) and Leibniz's "dum Deus calculat fit mundus," both creation through thought, though she says she knew neither at the time [p.102, §67]. Associations: Milton's *Paradise Lost* (illustrated by Doré, familiar since childhood); the Book of Job (read aloud to her); the metre of "Of man's first disobedience" matching her first line; Handel's *Creation*. She adds memories from ages nine to sixteen of a Presbyterian pastor speaking of "Chaos," "Cosmos," "the Gift of Love," and of an article on "the Idea spontaneously creating its own object" which kept her awake all night at fifteen [pp.102–106].
- **No accidents** [§69]: against the usual objection that she could have picked any line, Jung says "the law of psychic causality is never taken seriously enough: there are no accidents, no 'just as wells.'" That the poem is associated with the Fall focuses attention on the problem already surmised. **Paradise Lost and Job:** both are about loss caused by temptation; Job is innocent yet misunderstood by friends, doubly suffering as Cyrano does. Jung notes that neurotics often continually defend their innocence, a self-deceiving maneuver deriving energy "from those very impulses whose unpleasant character is plainly revealed" by the accusations [pp.103–104]. The emotional analogy is "having to suffer a hopeless struggle against overwhelming odds … accompanied from afar by the clangour of 'creation'" [p.105, §72].
- **Fifteen-year-old and the adolescent question** [§74–78]: the connection of cosmos from chaos with the "Gift of Love" dates to puberty. Jung recalls a fifteen-year-old girl he studied whose split-off fantasies were of "a positively mythical nature": she saw herself as "the racial mother of uncounted generations." "The unconscious is infinitely more common to all men than are the contents of their individual consciousnesses. **The unconscious is, in fact, the condensation of the average run of historical experience**" [pp.106–107, §75].
  - **Miller's problem "was the common human problem: How am I to be creative? Nature knows only one answer to that: Through a child."** But the question "how does one get a child?" is tied to the father and hits the incest-barrier; love of the father turns toward "higher forms of the father," authority, Church Fathers, the father-god. Mythology offers consolation: the Word became flesh, the divine pneuma entered the Virgin (pl. III); the whirlwind of Anaxagoras is the divine nous. The Immaculate Mother comforts by saying "I too have become a mother" through the "Idea spontaneously creating its own object" [p.107, §76].
  - **Lower and higher meaning** [§77–78]: "Everything psychic has a lower and a higher meaning" (quoting the late-classical saying "Heaven above, Heaven below … all that is above also is below"). The excitement was not just the sexual problem narrowly; "the other half is ideal creation as a substitute for real creation." For intellectual people spiritual fruitfulness is a vital necessity. It may also be a presentiment of the future from the "inconscient supérieur" (Maeterlinck), "prospective potency of a subliminal synthesis"; Jung has seen long-standing neuroses preceded by a dream of visionary clarity that "anticipates the subsequent events of his life," and he treats the sleepless night as foreshadowing "a future life-aim" [pp.108–109].
- **"Only this, and nothing more!"** [§79–83, pp.109–111]: Miller's dismissal ("There was only this and nothing more") is an unacknowledged quotation from Poe's "The Raven," a poem of despair over a lost love. "Their quotation gives the show away completely": she underestimated the singer's impression, and the unconscious kept working, producing "symbolical fantasies": first the morning stars, then *Paradise Lost*, then ecclesiastical garb, "World Creation," and finally a religious hymn that "at last finds its way to freedom." Through the father-imago the singer "becomes the Creator, the God of Sound, of Light and of Love." **Qualification:** this "is not to say that the idea of God derives from the loss of a lover"; what happens is "the displacement of libido on to a symbolical object," itself "a perfectly genuine experience" though open to improper use [pp.111–112].
- **Via dolorosa and the two powers** [§84–89, pp.112–116]: the libido's path is a "via dolorosa" (Paradise Lost, Job, Faust's wager with God via Mephistopheles). In Job the two great forces are simply good and evil; in Faust the issue is explicitly erotic, with the devil as tempter. Faust is more conscious because he admits his conflicts; Miss Miller, like Job, "admits nothing, and pretends that good and evil come from outside." A key analogy: the procreative urge remains the essential attribute of the God she praises as Creator; in Job, Satan destroys fruitfulness but God is "the All-Fruitful," and speaks with "a paean" of his creative power through **Behemoth and Leviathan** (long quotation from Job 40–41, Authorized Version).
  - **Jung's reading of Job's God** [§89]: God is as Behemoth and Leviathan: "the fruitfulness and abundance of Nature, the ungovernable wildness and licentiousness of Nature, the overwhelming danger of unchained power." God "has simply shown his other side for once, the side we call the Devil." "This God dwells in the heart, in the unconscious"; that is "the source of our fear of the unspeakably terrible, and of the strength to withstand the terror." The conscious ego is "a mere bagatelle, a feather whirled hither and thither," sometimes sacrificed, sometimes sacrificer.
  - **The God-image defined psychologically** [§89]: "since, psychologically speaking, the God-image is a complex of ideas of an archetypal nature, it must necessarily be regarded as representing a certain sum of energy (libido) which appears in projection." In most existing religions the formative factor is the father-imago, in older religions the mother-imago (omnipotent stern paternalism in the Old Testament, loving paternalism in the New; pagan maternal and theriomorphic elements; pl. IVa). The God-concept is "not only an image, but an elemental force": instinct and fate which "leads us into life." **Working in harmony with the libido** does not mean drifting with it: psychic forces have no uniform direction, often oppose each other, and "a mere letting go of oneself leads in the shortest space of time to the most hopeless confusion" [p.116].
- **Repression versus natural transformation** [§90–94, pp.116–120]. The hymn replaces the erotic problem. Without it, she would have yielded to the impression or replaced lost happiness with regret. Jung declines to decide whether it is better to let erotic tension resolve unnoticed into sublime religious poetry, and instead asks for the meaning and purpose of the "devious path of the libido." **Principle:** "There are no 'purposeless' psychic processes … it is a hypothesis of the greatest heuristic value that the psyche is essentially purposive and directed" [p.117, §90]. Explaining the poem by its root-cause (the love episode) "does not amount to very much"; "only the discovery of the purpose can provide a satisfactory answer to psychological questions" [§91]. This transformation of libido moves in the same direction as cultural modification of natural drives. But the suspicion remains that the singer was "assiduously overlooked": a degree of **repression**.
  - **Repression** [§92–93]: "an illegitimate way of evading the conflict, for it means pretending to oneself that it does not exist." The repressed conflict persists; repression leads to regressive reactivation of an earlier relationship (here the father-imago). "Constellated (i.e., activated) unconscious contents are, so far as we know, always projected," discovered in external objects. The "advantage" of projection is apparently shedding the conflict ("somebody else … now [has] the responsibility"). Here the deity replaces the human singer; earthly love is replaced by heavenly. If repression, the father-god is "a self-deceiving manoeuvre … for the illegitimate purpose of making a real difficulty unreal."
  - **The other possibility** [§94]: if the hymn came into being "unconsciously and spontaneously" without repression, then it is "an entirely natural and automatic process of transformation," and the creator-god emerging from the father-imago "is no longer a product of repression or a substitute, but a natural and inevitable phenomenon." This is found in "all genuine acts of creation, artistic or otherwise." Where causally tied to repression, products are "colored by complexes which neurotically distort them and stamp them as ersatz products." "Just as in natural birth no repression is needed … so artistic and spiritual creation is a natural process even when the figure projected is divine." This is "a universal phenomenon which forms the basis of all our ideas of God," so old that "one cannot tell whether they are derived from a father-imago, or vice versa" (the same for the mother-imago) [pp.119–120].

### C. The God-image, confession, community and love [§95–104, pp.120–133]

- **The autonomous God-image** [§95]: a God-image from a spontaneous act of creation "is a living figure, a being that exists in its own right and therefore confronts its ostensible creator autonomously"; the relation is dialectical. The naive mind concludes the figure fashioned itself in him, a possibility "no amount of criticism can disprove," because genesis is "a natural process with a teleological orientation in which the cause anticipates the goal." Using the dialectic in practice, the naive person calls upon the divine presence to unload burdens: psychologically, complexes are **consciously transferred to the God-image**, "the direct opposite of an act of repression, where the complexes are handed over to an unconscious authority" [pp.120–121].
- **Religious discipline as keeping conflict conscious** [§95]: mutual confession of sin (James 5:16) "effectively prevents one from becoming unconscious"; "that is also a sine qua non of the psychotherapeutic procedure." As the doctor takes over patients' conflicts, Christian practice appoints the Saviour (innocent, a self-sacrificer, bearing sins on the tree). Double psychic benefit: one stays conscious of the conflict ("sin"), preventing "a known suffering from turning into an unknown one, which is far more tormenting," and one lightens the burden by surrendering it to God [p.121].
- **Scientific standpoint** [§95]: the divine figure is first a psychic image, "a complex of archetypal ideas which faith equates with a metaphysical entity." Science has no competence to judge that equation; it can only establish a psychic existent with "a certain functional autonomy." The scientific approach thus puts "in place of the certainty of faith, the uncertainty of human knowledge," and isolates the conscious mind among psychic factors; only caution prevents "assimilating them and … identifying them with himself" (inflation, in later terms). Religious figures "clothe themselves so convincingly in the stuff of the individual psyche" that it seems the subject produced them: "an illusion of the conscious mind." "**In reality all inner experience springs from the unconscious, over which we have no control. But the unconscious is nature, which never deceives: only we deceive ourselves**" [pp.122–123]. The Christian community (confess to one another; Pauline and Johannine commands of mutual love, 1 John 4) answers the danger to the solidarity of faith [pp.123–124, §95–96].
- **Love and God** [§97–98, pp.124–126]: Hugh of St. Victor on love's power to bind God. If God abides in us only when we love our brother, love seems more important than God, even identical to him. Psychologically, "love" is "a function of relationship on the one hand and a feeling-toned psychic condition on the other" which "practically coincides with the God-image"; with hunger it is "the immemorial psychic driving-force of humanity." Danger of confusing human love with God's work. **"Anything psychically powerful is invariably called 'God.'"** "Love is common to both": it belongs to man "in so far as he is its master, and to the daemon if ever he becomes its object or its victim." Psychologically: libido, "the force of desire and aspiration, as psychic energy in the widest sense," is partly at the ego's disposal and partly confronts the ego autonomously. The relation of unconscious to conscious "is not merely mechanical or complementary, but rather **compensatory**," which explains why the God-image is often regarded as a personal being [§98].
- **"God is spirit"** [§99–101]: because the unconscious increasingly thrusts a spiritual vocation on man, the God-image was seen as a spirit requiring man's spirit. The Logos attenuates it further into the abstract "Christian love." Psychologically the energy of an archetype is not at the conscious mind's disposal; it communicates itself to the ego only when the ego is "gripped by an autonomous action of the archetype," so "spiritual love" implies a *donum gratiae*. "A man can then take on archetypal dimensions and exercise corresponding effects; he can appear in the place of God," as institutionalized in the Catholic Church. The community this creates is "of an archetypal order": its aim is "a transcendental symbol," not utilitarian [pp.126–128].
- **Dangers of intimacy** [§102–104, pp.128–133]: closeness constellates the **power and sex instincts**. Ancient religious experience was often bodily union with the deity; some cults were saturated with sexuality. The moral degeneracy of the early centuries produced a reaction that expressed itself in two antagonistic religions, **Christianity and Mithraism**, both striving for a higher social intercourse symbolized by a projected ("incarnate") idea, the Logos, making available for society the impulses that formerly flung man from passion to passion (*Heimarmene*, the compulsion of evil stars; "what we psychologists would call the compulsion of libido").
  - **Example: Alypius** (Augustine, *Confessions*, long quotation): dragged to the amphitheater, he vowed to keep his eyes closed, opened them at a roar, "drank deep of the savagery," and became "one of the crowd."
  - **Seneca, Letter 41** (long quotation on the sage whom a divine power seems to have descended on). "Man's domestication cost him the heaviest sacrifices"; the Stoic ideal shows what it was set up against. The age was ripe for "the Word made flesh," a mediator (*mesites*) in whose name men "could love one another and call each other brothers"; this arose from "an elementary need in the great masses of humanity" not speculative philosophy, since "humanity does not thrive in a state of licentiousness." **Meaning of Christianity and Mithraism: "moral subjugation of the animal instincts"** [p.132, §104]. Modern man, who has merely "become neurotic," no longer understands what the Christian community was meant to protect against: "For enlightened people, the need for religion is next door to neurosis."
  - **Later remarks (revision)** [pp.132–133]: Christian emphasis on spirit "inevitably leads to an unbearable depreciation of man's physical side … an optimistic caricature of human nature." After two world wars "the abyss has opened out again." "Mass psychology is egoism raised to an inconceivable power, for its goal is immanent and not transcendent."

### D. Value of the poem; Christianity's training; the modern split [§105–114, pp.133–141]

- **Verdict on the poem** [§105]: in early Christianity conscious sin was daily visible; Miss Miller not only underestimates her "sins" but misses the link between "bitter inexorable necessity" and her religious product, so the poem "loses the living value of a religious work of art," being "a sentimental rehash of an erotic experience," of "about the same ethical value as a dream, which is also none of our doing" [p.133].
- **Ethical principle** [§106]: "The unconscious conversion of instinctual impulses into religious activity is ethically worthless, and often no more than an hysterical outburst, even though its products may be aesthetically valuable. Ethical decision is possible only when one is conscious of the conflict in all its aspects." The religious attitude likewise "must be fully conscious of itself and of its foundations if it is to signify anything more than unconscious imitation." Modern disbelief in "the devilishness of human nature" goes with incomprehension of religion; original sin "vanished into the unconscious" [pp.133–134].
- **Christian training and its aftermath** [§107–113, pp.134–140]. Christianity, by centuries of education, subdued the animal instincts so a large amount of instinctual energy could be freed for building civilization; its first effect was alienation from reality and otherworldliness (Augustine on men who "admire lofty mountains and broad seas … and turn away from themselves"). The classical feeling for nature is shown by **Cumont** on Mithraism (every element divine; the initiate's walk to the grotto) and by **Seneca** on groves, caves, springs. The contrast: Augustine's "What do I love when I love my God?" (an inner light, melody, fragrance, embrace). World and beauty had to be shunned because love of created nature "soon makes man its slave" (Augustine: "subjects cannot judge"); Lucretius extols "alma Venus" (fig. 1); "paganism and nature-worship" was the threat.
  - **Result:** the flight from the world allowed man to build "an inner, spiritual world," "a type of thinking independent of external factors," "the sovereignty of the idea," which later rose to reflection and observation and enabled a new relationship with nature (Renaissance, science) that "did not collapse before the magic of external objects." Scientific honesty carries something "of the old religious ethic." But the victor became "the prisoner of the world it had conquered" [p.139–140].
  - **Modern loss** [§113]: Kalthoff saw the modern spirit as a new incarnation of the Logos; Jung says rather it was "the descent of the Anthropos or Nous into the dark embrace of Physis": "The world had not only been deprived of its gods, but had lost its soul." Knowledge of nature increased a thousandfold while "knowledge and experience of the inner world were correspondingly reduced"; dogmas dwindled to incomprehensible vestiges. Modern psychology struggles to show the soul "is not something attached to an outside, but has an autonomous inside, too," "an existent which in all essentials can only be inferred indirectly." Rationalism is "sham enlightenment" that sees dogma as a flat impossibility rather than "the symbolic expression of a definite idea with a definite content"; "what 'I' do not know simply does not exist. Therefore, for this enlightened stupidity, there is no non-conscious psyche" [pp.140–141].
- **Definition of symbol (preliminary)** [§114]: "**Symbols are not allegories and not signs: they are images of contents which for the most part transcend consciousness.**" These contents are real, "agents with which it is not only possible but absolutely necessary for us to come to terms"; in making this discovery we understand what dogma formulates and why it exists [p.141].

## V. The Song of the Moth [pp.142–193] [§115–175]

### A. The material [§115–116, pp.142–143]

- After the Hymn, Miss Miller traveled from Geneva to Paris; exhausted, in a hot ladies' compartment at 4 a.m. she saw a moth fluttering round the light; a poem sprang into her mind (hypnagogic): **"The Moth to the Sun."** Summary: the moth longed for the sun from its first crawl to consciousness, dreamed of it in the chrysalis; myriads of its kind beat out their lives against a feeble spark caught from the sun; one hour left, its last effort is to approach the sun's glory, and "having gained one raptured glance, I'll die content" for having beheld "the source of beauty, warmth, and life" in "perfect splendor."

### B. The situation and the first reading [§117–122, pp.143–148]

- **Situation** [§117]: weeks or months of silence between the poems; the unconscious has been working on the same complex ("another verbalized fragment"). The poem is hopeless and melancholy: moth and sun "never meet." Jung finds a **condensation** of two images: the moth that flies into the flame and burns its wings, and the tiny ephemeral May-fly longing for the imperishable light (cf. Faust's longing to follow the setting sun on tireless wings, and "the pinions by the spirit won / Bring us no flight that mortal clay can know").
- **Faust parallel** [§118–121]: after that scene Faust meets the black poodle, the devil, in whose fires he will singe his wings; "turned away from himself," he falls. The worship of Nature's beauty leads the medieval Christian to pagan thoughts antagonistic to his religion, as Mithraism was Christianity's rival. **"Faust's mistake was that he made the worst of both worlds by blindly following the urge of his libido."** His conflict reflects the collective conflict at the beginning of the Christian era but "takes the opposite course." Virgil's Fourth Eclogue (the returning Virgin, the new generation, the child who ends the iron age) reflects the eschatological hopes of that time; asceticism was a new adventure for many, whereas for Faust "the ascetic ideal is sheer death"; he wins life by binding himself to evil, brings about Gretchen's death, then sacrifices his life to unceasing work and saves many (his "double mission as saviour and destroyer," hinted at in the scene with Wagner). **What makes Faust significant:** it formulates a centuries-old problem as Oedipus did for the Greeks: "how to extricate ourselves from between the Scylla of world-renunciation and the Charybdis of its acceptance" [p.147, §121].
- **Failure of the hymn's solution** [§122]: the hopeful hymn "is a pose that promises, but does not fulfill." **A complex left to work itself out in the unconscious "lose[s] nothing of [its] original affectivity, though [its] outward manifestations can change almost endlessly."** The first poem was an unconscious attempt to solve the conflict by a religious attitude, as people in earlier centuries decided conscious conflicts by religion; it failed. The second attempt is "decidedly more worldly in tone": "one raptured glance," then die; the gaze turns, as in Faust, to "this terrestrial sun," with the moth-and-flame meaning mingled in [pp.147–148].

### C. Miller's associations and the libido argument [§123–134, pp.148–155]

- **Associations** [§123]: the poem made a profound impression; days later, rereading a philosophical article from Berlin, she found "The same passionate longing of the moth for the star, of man for God"; also a play, *The Moth and the Flame*; "You see how often the word moth has been impressed upon me!"
- **Reading** [§124]: the moth is Miss Miller herself; longing for God equals longing for the singing "morning star"; "star" recurs from the first material.
- **Quality of desire** [§125–127]: it is "shameful and degrading" to some that our highest longings connect with "an all-too-human passion" (a bronzed helmsman with black mustachios and the loftiest ideas of religion?). **Jung's argument:** both are objects of passionate desire; whether the nature of the object alters the quality of the libido is open. "As regards its psychological evaluation the *how* is more important than the *what*—*si duo faciunt idem, non est idem*." The quality of the desire "endows its object with the moral and aesthetic qualities of goodness and beauty": "Nature is beautiful because I love it"; "values are chiefly created by the quality of one's subjective reactions" (objective values exist but their validity depends on consensus). In the erotic sphere "it is abundantly evident how little the object counts." So when Miss Miller praises God or the sun, "she really means her love, the instinct most deeply rooted in human nature."
- **Libido, God and symbols of energy** [§128–129, pp.150–152]. As the erotic impression turns from positive to negative, **light symbols** predominate for the object; the libido having turned away from the concrete object, its object is psychic, God. "**God is the name for a complex of ideas grouped round a powerful feeling; the feeling-tone is what really gives the complex its characteristic efficacy**," an emotional tension formulable in terms of energy. Light and fire attributes "depict the intensity of the feeling-tone." "If one worships God, sun, or fire (cf. fig. 4), one is worshipping intensity and power, in other words … psychic energy as such, the libido."
  - **Form** [§128]: "Form is both an image and a mode of manifestation. It expresses two things: the energy which takes shape in it, and the medium in which that energy appears." Energy creates its own image; the medium forces it into a definite form. One man derives the idea of God from the sun; another says the numinous feelings give the sun its godlike significance (a "chicken or egg" question). Jung inclines to the view that "the psycho-energic phenomenon not only takes precedence, but explains far more than the hypothesis of the causal primacy of the environment."
  - **Thesis** [§129]: "**psychic energy or libido creates the God-image by making use of archetypal patterns, and … man in consequence worships the psychic force active within him as something divine**." The God-image is thus "a real but subjective phenomenon" (Seneca: "God is near you … within you"; 1 John 4).
- **"A game with oneself"?** [§130]: to someone who understands libido only as energy under conscious control, the religious relationship is a ridiculous game of hide-and-seek. But this is energy belonging to the archetype and unconscious. "To carry a god around in yourself means a great deal; it is a guarantee of happiness, of power, and even of omnipotence." Christianity retains traces; pagan mysteries make it explicit: at the end of consecration in the Isis mysteries (Apuleius, pl. VI) the neophyte is crowned with palm leaves, set on a pedestal and worshipped as Helios; in the Dieterich "Mithraic liturgy" the neophyte says "I am a star wandering together with you and shining up from the depths" [pp.152–153].
- **Becoming a god** [§131–134, pp.153–155]: the saint with stigmata is equated with Christ; Francis speaks of "brother sun" and "sister moon"; Hippolytus: "You have become God … That is the 'Know thyself'"; John 10:34 (Psalm 82:6, "Ye are gods"). The old belief puts deification after death; mystery cults make it happen in this world; Jung quotes an Egyptian triumphal song of the ascending soul ("I am the god Atum … I am daily together with my father Atum"). Becoming God "enormously increase[s]" importance and power and strengthens the individual against "all-too-human weakness and insecurity," but "far more important are the deeper lying processes in the realm of feeling."
- **Introversion and parental imagos** [§134–135, p.155]: whoever introverts libido (withdraws it from the external object) finds it reverting "to the individual past and digs[ging] up from the treasure-house of memory those images glimpsed long ago, which bring back the time when the world was a full and rounded whole," first of all the **imagos of father and mother**, "unique and imperishable." "The regressive reactivation of the father- and mother-imagos plays an important role in religion"; its benefits equal "the parental care lavished upon the child." "The visible father of the world is the sun, the heavenly fire, for which reason **father, God, sun, and fire are mythologically synonymous**" (honoring the sun's strength is honoring "the energy of the archetype").

### D. Light and fire as libido symbols; mystic visions [§135–146, pp.155–165]

- **Light liturgy** [§135–138]: Dieterich's papyrus: stars descend from the sun's disc ("five-pointed"), and the neophyte prays a long catalogue of light and fire attributes ("fire-ruler, creator of light …"), compared with Christian mysticism's "endless vociferations about 'love'" (Mechthild of Magdeburg and God's reply: "I Myself am Love"). **Religious regression uses the parental imago "only as a symbol"**: it clothes the archetype in parents' image and expresses its energy through fire, light, heat, fecundity. In mysticism the vision of the Divine is often "nothing but sun or light, and is rarely, if ever, personified" (fig. 2; Mithraic liturgy: the path of the visible gods through the sun's disc, "God my father") [pp.156–157].
- **Mystic testimonies** [§139–141]: Hildegarde of Bingen (the "living light," lifts all weariness: "I feel like a simple girl and not like an old woman"); **Symeon the "New Theologian"** (the Invisible, "emptiness of all forms," fire kindled from fire undiminished; searching everywhere for the uncreated splendor in the heavens and abysses, he finds it "within me … in the midst of my heart … like the light of a spherical sun"). Jung: the thing perceived as inner light, "the sun of the other world," is "an emotional component of the psyche" [pp.157–160].
- **Nietzsche** [§142–145, pp.160–163]: "Glory and Eternity" (a constellation floating toward him) and "The Beacon" (Zarathustra's flame, "a snake upreared in impatience," "This flame is my own soul"). The Mithraic liturgy parallels: stars come down after prayers and silence, and whistling and clicking the tongue are "archaic devices for attracting the theriomorphic deity"; roaring (Mechthild's "My soul roars with the voice of a hungry lion"; Psalm 42). Schizophrenia revives the old usage (Schreber's "bellowing miracle"). The "five-fingered stars" are anthropomorphic; one expects a living being (man or animal) to form from the fiery image: "libido-symbols do not stop at sun, light, and fire."
- **Fourfold scheme of libido symbolization** [§146–147, p.165]. The libido turns into fire, flame, and snake; the Egyptian living sun-disc with two Uraeus serpents combines these; the snake in Nietzsche is "unmistakably phallic"; the phallus is "the source of life and libido, the creator and worker of miracles," worshipped everywhere.

| Kind | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Comparison by analogy | libido compared to something that resembles it | sun, fire (fig. 4) |
| 2a. Causative comparison with objects | libido characterized by its object | the health-giving sun (Mithras "well-beloved") |
| 2b. Causative comparison with the subject | libido characterized by its instrument or analogue | phallus, snake |
| 3. Functional comparison | "tertium comparationis" is activity | fertile like the bull; dangerous like lion or boar (fury of passion); lustful like the ever-rutting ass |

  All these symbols, so far as they are libido-images, "can be reduced to a common denominator—the libido and its properties," in accord with historical attempts to unify the many gods [pp.165–166].
- **Unification of gods** [§147–149, pp.166–168]: Egyptian local gods (Amon, Horus, Khnum, Atum) identified with Ra; "Amon-Ra-Harmachis-Atum" invoked as "the only god, in truth, the living one"; **Amenophis IV (Akhenaten)** replaced all gods by the "great living disc of the sun" (Erman: not a sun-god but "the material sun itself" bestowing eternal life), uniting the bull, ram, crocodile and pile-dwelling gods in the disc, "a psychologically valuable work of interpretation." Hellenistic and Roman polytheism converged similarly (the prayer of Lucius to the Queen of Heaven, Apuleius). **Opposing tendencies:** striving for unity versus an equally strong "tendency to create multiplicity" (even Trinity and heavenly hierarchies); constant warfare of the two.
- **The sun's tube and wind** [§149–154, pp.168–172]: the Mithraic vision of "something that looks like a tube" hanging from the sun's disc, "the origin of the ministering wind." Jung reads the tube as phallic: wind, like sun, is a fructifier (the early German painting of the Annunciation with a tube carrying the Holy Ghost dove under Mary's robe, pl. VIII, III). **Case:** a schizophrenic patient saw an erect phallus on the sun that moved with his head and was "where the wind came from"; this became intelligible to Jung only after reading the Mithraic liturgy, whose obscure passage about the vision veering east or west matches the patient's experience. Jung also found the Ixion-on-the-wheel motif in the dream of an uneducated Black man (and a number of such cases); **conclusion** [§154, p.172]: "it is not a question of a specifically racial heredity, but of a universally human characteristic. Nor is it a question of inherited ideas, but of a functional disposition to produce the same, or very similar, ideas. **This disposition I later called the archetype.**"

### E. Mithraic liturgy, the Apocalypse, Christ as sun [§155–163, pp.172–179]

- **Visions in the liturgy** [§155–156]: after Helios, seven maidens with snake faces and seven gods with black bull faces (serpent as feminine seductive principle, later symbol of the earth; bull as fertility-symbol and "guardians of the world's axis"); Helios "young, comely, with glowing locks, in a white tunic and a scarlet cloak, with a fiery crown"; Mithras with golden hair, holding the bull's shoulder (the Bear constellation) with "lightnings … from his eyes." Equating gold and fire shows large agreement between the gods. The Apocalypse's Son of Man among seven golden candlesticks (Rev. 1, 14, 19) is parallel.
- **No direct borrowing** [§157]: the visionary images "are drawn from a source not limited to any one place, but found in the souls of many people. The symbols it produces are far too typical to belong to any one individual."
- **Light-symbolism develops, as intensity of vision grows, into the figure of the sun-hero, the "well-beloved"**, the psychological root of the sun-coronations in the mysteries [§158, pp.176–177].
- **Christ as Sol novus** [§158–163, pp.177–179]: the early Church had difficulty shaking off the pagan sun symbol: Philo (sun as image of the Logos), Ambrose ("O sol salutis"), Melito ("The sun of the East"), Pseudo-Cyprian and Chrysostom on Christ's birthday (25 December, the day of the Unconquerable Sun), "sun of righteousness" (Malachi); Christians still prostrate to the rising sun in the fifth century (Eusebius of Alexandria); Augustine insists "Christ the Lord has not been made [like unto] the sun, but is he through whom the sun is made." Traces survive in the nimbus, halos, the apostles as zodiac signs with stars; Tertullian reports pagans took the sun for the Christians' god; Manichees worshipped the sun; the magical dedication "Zeus Helios great god king Jesus"; in Armenia Christians still pray to the rising sun.

### F. The sun-hero, the dying god, ambivalence of passion [§164–175, pp.180–193]

- **What the excavation reveals** [§164]: "under the symbol of 'moth and sun' we have dug deep down into the historical layers of the psyche" and uncovered "a buried idol, the sun-hero," forever unattainable, who "rises again in rejuvenated splendour to give light to new generations." For him "the soul-moth burns her wings."
- **Dying and resurgent god** [§165]: Osiris, Tammuz, Attis-Adonis, Christ, Mithras, the phoenix. "The forces of nature are always two-faced," as with the God of Job. The moth-sun image condenses sun and moth-and-flame (the "hackneyed meaning of flying round the flame of passion until one's wings are burned"): **passionate longing "is the power which beautifies everything, but … is quite as likely to destroy everything."** "All passion is a challenge to fate, and what it does cannot be undone."
- **Fear of life and partial suicide** [§165]: the neurotic's perpetual hesitation to "launch out into life" is a wish to stand aside; "anyone who refuses to experience life must stifle his desire to live—in other words, he must commit partial suicide," hence **death-fantasies accompany renunciation of desire**.
- **Byron's "Heaven and Earth"** [§165–175, pp.181–193]. Miller's last association (a rhythm like Byron's "let me die as I have lived in faith / Nor tremble tho' the Universe should quake") comes from an unfinished "mystery" by Byron, spoken by a mortal fleeing the Deluge; she thus shows hopeless despair and doom like Cyrano's last fight [pp.181–183, §166–167]. **Jung's reading** [§168–171]: her human expectations are futile because her longing is directed to the Divine, "the well-beloved," "a divine hero has stepped into the shoes of the handsome officer." Byron's poem (motto from Coleridge: "woman wailing for her demon-lover"; Genesis 6, sons of God and daughters of men): angels Samiasa and Azaziel love Cain's daughters Anah and Aholibamah and break the barrier between mortal and immortal; the Flood follows. Translated to the psychological sphere: the rational power ruling with wise laws is menaced by "the chaotic, primitive force of passion," which must be exterminated (the Flood as the sea breaking its dykes; "motherly" waters in Indian myth). **"As a power which transcends consciousness the libido is by nature daemonic: it is both God and devil."** If evil were destroyed utterly, "everything daemonic, including God himself, would suffer a grievous loss; it would be like performing an amputation on the body of the Deity." Passion "raises a man not only above himself, but also above the bounds of his mortality … and by the very act of raising him, it destroys him" (Babel; Lucifer). "The longing of the moth is not made pure by reaching for the stars, nor does it cease to be a moth." Through excess of longing man can "draw the gods down into the murk of his passion," and in raising himself to the Divine "he abandons his humanity" [pp.183–186]. Anah's and Aholibamah's invocations of the seraphs are "an exact parallel to Miss Miller's poem" (long quotations) [pp.186–190, §171].
- **The apparition** [§172–174, pp.190–192]: the angels appear as a vision of light, and Anah's simile reveals "the theriomorphic nature of the mild god of light": the vanishing rainbow is like foam "which the leviathan hath lash'd." Leviathan "dwells where the deep fountains of the ocean are; from there the all-destroying flood ascends, the tidal wave of animal passion. The choking, heart-constricting surge of instinct is projected outwards as a mounting flood to destroy everything that exists, so that a new and better world may arise from the ruins of the old." Japhet's prophecy of a redeemer and the Spirits' reply that the same "old tears, old crimes, and oldest ill" will remain.
- **Closing of Part One** [§175, pp.192–193]: Japhet's prognostication is read on the "subjective level." With the moth's death in the light "the danger has been removed for the time being, though the problem is still far from solved." The conflict begins again, but "this time there is a promise in the air, a premonition of the redeemer, the 'well-beloved,' who mounts to the zenith with the sun and then sinks again into night … the young dying god, who has ever been our hope of renewal and of the world to come."

# PART TWO

## I. Introduction [pp.195–208] [§176–189]

- **Retrospective** [§176]. Although "The Moth to the Sun" differs from the Hymn, the longing for the sun has led into related mythological ideas: the Creator-God, whose dual nature appeared in Job, now has "an astromythological, or rather an astrological, character." He is the sun, which "transcends his moral division into a Heavenly Father and his counterpart the devil." Renan: the sun is "the only truly 'rational' image of God," for savage and for modern science: the father-god from whom living things draw life, "the fructifier and creator, the source of energy." The discord in the soul "can be harmoniously resolved through the sun as a natural object which knows no inner conflict." The sun is also destructive (the zodiacal lion of August heat that Samson slew); it "shines equally on the just and the unjust." So it suits the visible God, "the creative power of our own soul, which we call libido, and whose nature it is to bring forth the useful and the harmful, the good and the bad." Mystics who descend into themselves find "in their heart" the sun image, their own life-force; "our physiological life, regarded as an energy process, is entirely solar" [pp.195–196].
- **The Upanishadic Self** [§176–179, pp.196–199]. The Shvetashvatara Upanishad on Rudra (one God, eyes and faces on all sides, who "forg[es] all things together with his hands and wings"; source of the gods, who "of old gave birth to the Golden Seed"), Brahma hidden in the bodies of all, **purusha** "like to the sun"; the Person "no bigger than a thumb," the inner Self in the heart (also Katha Upanishad: "burning like flame without smoke, maker of past and future"). Behind these attributes Jung discerns the All-Creator and behind him the sun; God "is contained in the individual creature" and "whoever knows him is immortal."
- **Symbol versus sign** [§180, pp.199–200]: Tom Thumbs, dactyls, Cabiri have a phallic aspect because they personify creative forces, of which the phallus is also a symbol: the libido "in its creative aspect." Sexual images in dreams and speech "should not be taken literally … not … understood semiotically, as signs for definite things, but as symbols." **Definition:** "A symbol is an indefinite expression with many meanings, pointing to something not easily defined and therefore not fully known. But the sign always has a fixed meaning, because it is a conventional abbreviation for, or a commonly accepted indication of, something known." A symbol "has a large number of analogous variants, and the more of these variants it has … the more complete and clear-cut will be the image it projects of its object." Thus the creative dwarfs toiling in secret, the phallus begetting in darkness, and the key opening a forbidden door all delineate "further aspects of the process underlying them all."
- **The key and the Mothers (Faust)** [§180–182, pp.200–202]: Mephistopheles gives Faust the key that "will smell the right place" and leads him "to the Mothers." Jung: this is the libido, "not only creative and procreative, but possess[ing] an intuitive faculty, a strange power to 'smell the right place,'" almost "a live creature with an independent life of its own (which is why it is so easily personified)." The realm of the Mothers connects with the womb or matrix, "which frequently symbolizes the creative aspect of the unconscious" (fig. 12). The libido is "a force of nature, good and bad at once, or morally neutral." By uniting with it Faust accomplishes his life's work, first with evil results, then for the benefit of mankind; in the realm of the Mothers he finds the tripod, "the Hermetic vessel in which the 'royal marriage' is consummated"; he needs the phallic wand to create Paris and Helen. The insignificant-looking tool is "the dark creative power of the unconscious, which reveals itself to those who follow its dictates and is indeed capable of working miracles." The Shvetashvatara paradox of the cosmic purusha: without feet or hands he moves and grasps, eyeless he sees; "smaller than small, greater than great."
- **Dwarfs, smiths, cripples, Cabiri, dactyls** [§183–184, pp.202–204]: the phallus often stands for the creative divinity (Hermes) and is sometimes an independent being (also in children's and modern artists' drawings). Seers, artists and wonder-workers show phallic traits: crippled feet in Hephaestus, Wieland the Smith, Mani; Melampus ("Blackfoot," who introduced the phallus cult); blindness characteristic of seers; ugliness and deformity of the chthonic **Cabiri** (sons of Hephaestus; "great gods," Samothracian cult tied to ithyphallic Hermes). Their relatives, the **Idaean dactyls** (fingers or Tom Thumbs; taught the blacksmith's art by the mother of the gods; the first Wise Men, teachers of Orpheus, inventors of Ephesian magic formulae and musical rhythms), show the same disparity as the Upanishads and Faust: Hercules, a giant, is said to be a dactyl; the Dioscuri, related to the Cabiri, wear the pointed *pileus* (also Attis and Mithras), now the pixies' and goblins' cap (figs. 9, 20).
- **The divine boy** [§184, pp.203–204]: the dwarf motif leads to the *puer aeternus*, *pais* (young Dionysus, Jupiter Anxurus, Tages). The Theban Cabiri vase (fig. 14) shows a bearded Dionysus labeled "Kabiros," a boy labeled "Pais," and caricature figures labeled [garbled in source: the first name, probably Protolaos] and "Mitos" (thread; in Orphic speech, semen). The cult was supposedly a Phoenician father-and-son cult (old and young Cabir); "the cult of the big and little man." Dionysus's phallic aspects (Argive bull; Phales as Priapus). The Upanishadic great/small paradox appears here as man and boy, father and son; deformity separates the pairs.
- **Libido: why the sexual definition is rejected** [§185–189, pp.205–208]. "An exclusively sexual definition of this concept is one-sided and must therefore be rejected. Appetite and compulsion are the specific features of all impulses and automatisms." The sexual metaphors of speech and the analogies in instinctual processes, symptoms and dreams "cannot … be taken literally"; "the sexual theory of psychic automatisms is an untenable prejudice"; "the very fact that it is impossible to derive the whole mass of psychic phenomena from a single instinct forbids a one-sided definition of 'libido.'" Jung uses the term "in the general sense in which it was understood by the classical authors."
  - **Classical usage** (quotations summarized): Cicero (from the expectation of good arise desire and delight; the Stoics call reasonable striving *boulesis*, will; when divorced from reason and too violent it is *libido*, "unbridled desire," found in all fools); *libidine, non ratione*; Sallust ("rage is a part of desire"; delight in fine arms and war-horses rather than in whores and banquets; "proper concern for your country"); "libido est scire" = "I like"; *libido urinae* = urge; Augustine: libido is "a general term for all desire" (lusts of revenge/rage, money/avarice, victory/stubbornness, self-glorification/boastfulness, domination), "an appetite like hunger and thirst" even for sexuality [pp.205–207].
  - **Etymology** [§188]: *libido/lubido* from *libet/lubet* ("it pleases"), *libens*; Sanskrit *lubhyati* ("violent longing"), *lobha*; Gothic *liufs*, OHG *liob* ("love"), Gothic *lubains* ("hope"), OHG *lob* ("praise"), OBulg. *ljubiti*, Lith. *liáupsinti* [p.208].
  - **Formal statement** [§189]: "the concept of libido in psychology has functionally the same significance as the concept of energy in physics since the time of Robert Mayer" [p.208].

## II. The Concept of Libido [pp.209–210+] [§190–, chapter continues in the next part]

- **Freud's concept** [§190]: Freud introduced libido in *Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality* and defined it sexually, but libido is subject to **displacement**; "libidinal affluxes" can pass to other functions and body regions with nothing to do with sex. Freud compared libido to a stream that can be divided, dammed up, and overflow into collaterals. So, "despite his definition of libido as sexuality, Freud does not explain 'everything' in terms of sex, as is commonly supposed," but recognizes special instinctual forces able to receive these affluxes: behind this lies a hypothetical "bundle of instincts" with the sexual instinct as a partial instinct that can encroach on other instincts. The resulting theory (instinctual forces of a neurotic system correspond to libidinal affluxes taken up by non-sexual functions) is "the keystone of the psychoanalytical theory of neurosis and the dogma of the Viennese school" [p.209].
- **Freud's later doubt** [pp.209–210]: Freud, confronted with a case of paranoid schizophrenia (the "end of the world" experience, Schreber), asked whether libido might "coincide with interest in general" (i.e., whether withdrawal of libido could by itself explain loss of rapport with the world, or the remaining ego-cathexes would suffice). Jung quotes the passage word for word: Freud admits that without a "well-grounded theory of instincts" these problems cannot be solved; he retains the distinction between ego-instincts and the sexual instinct, as hypotheses "we … are quite ready to drop again." The quotation breaks off at the end of the range, mid-sentence ("in the chaos of the obscurer processes // of the mind"), and **continues in Part 2 of this extraction**.

## Method notes (how the case is handled)

1. **Take the patient's own associations first** (Miss Miller's sources), then press them to their logical conclusion where she stops ("laymen and beginners" get stuck at associations that reach the complex only indirectly) [§66].
2. **There are no accidents:** every cited line, title, or quoted phrase is selected for a reason [§69]. Unattributed quotations ("Only this, and nothing more") are traced to their source and the source's content is read as a comment on the dreamer's state [§81–83].
3. **Interpret on the subjective level** (figures and episodes as aspects of the dreamer's own psyche) [§175].
4. **Look for purpose, not only cause:** a root-cause explanation "does not amount to very much" until the purpose of a symptom or fantasy is found [§91].
5. **Compare with historical and mythological material** only where it belongs, directly or indirectly, to the case's own presuppositions [Foreword 2nd ed.]; the aim is understanding the fantasy, not a theory of myth.
6. **Weigh the ethical dimension:** an unconscious transformation of conflict is aesthetically valuable perhaps, ethically worthless; decision needs consciousness of the whole conflict [§106].
