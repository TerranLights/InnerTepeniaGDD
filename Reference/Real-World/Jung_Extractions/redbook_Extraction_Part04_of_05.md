## Overview of this range

This part covers the second half of *Liber Secundus* (the book's central visionary narrative) and the first page of *Scrutinies*. It holds the end of the third night ("Nox tertia"), the fourth night ("Nox quarta"), and the chapters **The Three Prophecies, The Gift of Magic, The Way of the Cross and The Magician** (chapters xviii to xxi), the last of which is the longest and ends the narrative proper. The recurring teaching is that the "lowest" in oneself, the rejected, ridiculous, base part, must be accepted and carried; that the opposites (above and below, good and evil, one and the other) are not resolved by choosing but are united in "growth," the one real distinction being direction (below to above); and that this union produces a standstill, then new symbols and a "magical" way of life that cannot be taught. The narrator receives a black serpent-rod (magic), is hanged on the tree for three days and nights, receives a crown reading "Love never ends," loses love-as-possession (Salome), is told a fairy tale about a king and a son made from otter lard, and finally submits to the "son" (the new God-child/Phanes-like figure) who then ascends and leaves him alone with himself. The Scrutinies open on 19 April 1914 with the narrator confronting his own "I" in a harsh, self-flagellating monologue. Editor's notes supply dates from the *Black Books*, the Draft, Jung's later writings, and sources (Goethe's *Faust*, Nietzsche, Wagner, Ovid, Voltaire, Eckhart, Swedenborg, alchemy).

# LIBER SECUNDUS (continued)

## Nox tertia (Third Night), continued from the previous part [pp.331-332]

The chapter's closing teachings, delivered in the voice of the narrator (who has descended to the "depths"):

**Accepting the lowest in oneself** [p.331]
- Whoever fails through pride, selfishness and greed is damned, yet the damnation is not cast out either. Accepting the lowest in oneself means unavoidable suffering: one does the "base thing" and rebuilds what lay in ruin. There are "many graves and corpses in us."
- Parallel with Christ: as Christ subjugated the flesh through the torment of sanctification, "the God of this time" will subjugate the spirit through the same torment; as Christ tormented the flesh through the spirit, the new God will torment the spirit through the flesh. The spirit has become "an impertinent whore," a slave to man-made words, no longer the divine word itself.
- "The lowest in you is the source of mercy": taking the sickness, baseness and contemptibility upon oneself so the God can be healed and ascend purified. The lowest is also "the eye of the evil" that stares coldly and sucks the light down. Christ imposed bloody sacrifice; the renewed God will not spare bloodshed.
- The speaker takes on Isaiah 63's winepress image (red apparel, treading alone, trampling himself in anger, spilling his blood on the earth) as self-sacrifice done "so that the God would be healed." [EDITOR: the lines are Isaiah 63:2-6 [p.331], n.206.]
- Christ said he brought "not peace but a sword"; whoever completes Christ in himself will rebel against himself, hate what he loves in himself, be mocked and "crucified," with no one to aid him.
- **The two thieves:** our lowest lies on either side of our way. One half goes to Hell, the other rises to Heaven. The two halves of the lowest are at present unseparated and asleep, so it takes long to see which is destined for death and which for life.
- **Seed and tree image:** accepting the lowest lowers a seed into the ground of Hell; the tree of one's life grows from it and joins Below and Above. At both ends there is fire; life grows between "unbearable fires," hanging "stretched" between two poles, welters up and down. Fearing the lowest means remaining joined with chaos. "He who strives for the highest finds the deepest" [paraphrase of p.331].
- Christ is said to have delivered people from this "stretched hanging" by teaching "Be crafty like serpents and guileless like doves": craftiness guards against chaos, guilelessness veils its terrible aspect, so people could take a safe middle path.

**[EDITOR] notes on p.331 (summarized):** the Draft continues each passage with extra lines. Draft additions include the "stone the builders rejected" (Psalm 118) as the lowest in you; a passage echoing Isaiah 53 (the despised one who carries our sickness); a prophecy of coming horrors ("valleys and mountains that resound with the noise of battles"), a warning not to believe the narrator, and the line "where God is nearest, the danger is greatest" [pp.331, n.205], which the editor identifies as an implicit citation of Hölderlin's "Patmos" ("where danger is, salvation also grows"). Jung's *Answer to Job* is cited for the two thieves; Dieterich's *Nekyia* for Plato's *Gorgias* motif of transgressors "hanging" in Hades.

**The mediator's law and the dead** [p.332]
- The dead of the Above and of the Below mounted and their demands grew louder. Both the noble and the wicked broke "the law of the mediator," flinging doors open above and below, dragging many after them to "higher and lower madness," and so preparing what is to come.
- Whoever goes into the one without also accepting what comes toward him becomes its victim. Seeing "the other" as an enemy outside, one fights it in others and is blinded; whoever accepts that the other is also in him "quarrels and wrangles no more, but looks into himself and keeps silent."
- **Image 113** [EDITOR, n.211]: the divine child, completed April 1919; the legend says it means "the completion of a long path"; the figure is called **Phanes** (the "newly appearing God"); a symbol Jung attributed to the sun. Editor's extended note: Orphic theogony (Chronos, Aither, Chaos, the egg, Phanes the first-born, shining, golden-winged, androgynous; from Guthrie), Jung's earlier mention in *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido*, and Phanes' appearances in the Black Books (1916 as a golden bird; 20 Feb 1917 as messenger of Abraxas; Philemon says he will become Phanes). The editor quotes at length two long Black Book 7 litanies: one of Phanes as the day, the dawn, and everything beautiful and well-measured ("He is the end of fear," "the friend of man," etc.), and a 31 July 1918 speech in which Phanes gives "the joy of suffering and suffering of joy" and a jug of stone filled with water, wine, milk and blood guarded by the Gods of four winds; and a Black Book 7 passage in which perfection is defined as poverty, sacrifice, community and solitude held together ("He who is perfect knows light and dark, but I am the light beyond day and darkness"). The editor concludes: "Phanes is Jung's God" [p.332, n.211].
- **The tree of life**: the man who accepts what approaches him "sees the tree of life, whose roots reach into Hell and whose top touches Heaven." He no longer asks who is right or what is holy; he knows **one difference only: below and above** (the tree grows from below to above, with a crown clearly differentiated from the roots). This is "unquestionable," and he knows the way to salvation.
- **Freed from the old curse:** To "unlearn all distinctions save that concerning direction" frees one from the curse of the knowledge of good and evil. If you separate good from evil by your best appraisal and strive only for the good while denying the evil you nevertheless do, your roots no longer draw "the dark nourishment of the depths" and your tree sickens. Hence the ancients said that after Adam ate the apple the tree of paradise withered; "your life needs the dark." [EDITOR n.213: legend of the withered tree in *Transformations and Symbols*.]
- Once you know a thing is evil you can no longer accept it, you cannot accept it as evil, you cannot deny it: the knowledge of good and evil is an "insurmountable curse." Returning to primal chaos, hanging between the two poles of fire, you cannot separate good from evil; you discern only direction of growth. When growth stops, they fall apart again; they are united only in growth. You grow by standing still in the greatest doubt: "steadfastness in great doubt is a veritable flower of life."
- **Doubt:** he who cannot bear doubt does not bear himself, does not grow, does not live. "The strong have doubt, but doubt has the weak"; if the weakest can say to his doubt "I have you," he is the strongest. No one can say yes to doubt without enduring wide-open chaos. Many can talk about anything; "pay heed to what they live"; examine a person's life, not his speech. "My speech is neither light nor dark, since it is the speech of someone who is growing." [EDITOR: Jung's marginal note in the calligraphic volume dates this transcription 14.IX.1922; the Draft adds a line citing "Blessed be ye poor" (Luke 6:20).]

## Nox quarta (Fourth Night), Cap. xvii [pp.333-335]
Date per editor: **19 January 1914** [p.333, n.216].

**The kitchen and the librarian's cook** [p.333]
- The narrator hears the morning wind over the mountains; the night in which life hung "between the poles of fire" is overcome. His soul speaks: the door should be lifted off its hinges to give a free passage between here and there, yes and no, above and below; airy passages should be built between all opposites, scales with a gently swaying pointer, a flame the wind cannot blow out, a stream flowing to its goal, wild herds on old game paths, life proceeding "unbroken like the path of the sun."
- The narrator "toys casually and terribly" with himself; blind darkness besieges him; a "gray worm of twilight" with a round laughing face crawls along a wall. He opens his eyes: a **fat cook** stands before him, saying he has slept more than an hour.
- **Setting:** it is the librarian's kitchen next to the library. The narrator asks, "Is this really the realm of mothers?" [EDITOR n.217: Faust's descent to the Mothers in Goethe; Jung later identified the Mothers with the collective unconscious.] He had been reading Thomas à Kempis, chapter 21 ("My soul... you must find your rest in the Lord"), and had a dream. He asks the cook whose cook she is: the librarian's, who loves good cooking ("a gourmet"). [Image 115 legend, EDITOR n.219: "This is the golden fabric in which the shadow of God lives."]
- **Dialogue with the librarian ("L"):** the narrator returns the Thomas à Kempis book and says the cook found it edifying. He asks if the librarian ever practiced temple/incubation sleep in his kitchen (an ancient Greek dream-incubation reference, n.220); the librarian never had so strange an idea; the narrator says he would learn much about his kitchen.

**The Parsifal play** [p.333-334]
- Leaving, he pushes aside green curtains in the anteroom and sees a theater: **Klingsor's magic garden**; the librarian and his cook are playing **Amfortas and Kundry** (he ailing and pale, she disappointed and furious). Klingsor, who looks like the narrator, holds the feather the librarian used to tuck behind his ear; **Parsifal** enters and also looks like him. Klingsor throws the feather venomously; Parsifal catches it calmly.
- The scene shifts; the audience (the narrator) joins in the last act. Kneeling at the Good Friday service, Parsifal enters in a black helmet, Hercules' lion skin on his shoulders, club in hand, and "modern black trousers in honor of the church holiday." The narrator bristles. Parsifal removes his helmet; there is no Gurnemanz to atone for or consecrate him; Kundry stands at a distance, head covered, laughing. The audience recognizes itself in Parsifal: "He is I."
- The narrator takes off his "armor layered with history" and "chimerical decoration," washes hands and feet at the spring in a white penitent's shirt, then dons civilian clothes, walks out of the scene and approaches himself, still kneeling in prayer as the audience. "I rise and become one with myself." [EDITOR n.221: a summary of Wagner's *Parsifal* plot and Jung's 1913 and 1921 comments on it.]

**Teaching on the other and on gifts** [p.334]
- "What would mockery be, if it were not true mockery?" He who wants to accept himself must really accept his other. Yes and no are both true and untrue; only our concepts of truth and error can yield. Certainty in one is protection against the other, but the one cannot suffice since the other is in us; if content with one, the other starves and "afflicts us with its hunger," which we misread as hunger for the one. The satiated grows lazy, the hungry grows weak: "we suffocate in fat, consumed by lack." This is a sickness "you see a lot of," not necessary: man is free to overcome the cause; once you accept the other despite your highest belief in the one, "your growth begins."
- **Self-mockery:** others mock; one can blame them and forget to mock oneself; "he who cannot mock himself will be mocked by others." Accept self-mockery so that everything divine and heroic falls from you and you become completely human.
- **On gifts and talents:** one with a special talent believes he is the gift and becomes its fool; the gift is outside of him, lives at the expense of the bearer's character, and he is always beneath it. By accepting his other he can carry his gift without disadvantage; otherwise he oversteps and is mocked. The mockery is really only the disregard of his other.
- "When the God enters my life, I return to my poverty for the sake of the God." He accepts poverty, ugliness, ridiculousness and everything reprehensible so as to relieve the God of confusion and absurdity. [Image 117, EDITOR n.222: text in image "Atmavictu," "iuvenis adiutor" (youthful supporter), "Telesphorus," "spiritus malus in hominibus quibusdam"; legend: "The dragon wants to eat the sun and the youth beseeches him not to. But he eats it nevertheless." Editor's long note on **Atmaviktu**: first appears in Black Book 6 (25 April 1917), a figure who was an old man, then died in turn to become bear, otter, newt and serpent; Jung's soul calls him a kobold and serpent conjuror; later he becomes Philemon; Jung carved a figure in 1920 that the unconscious named Atmavictu, "breath of life," connected to a childhood manikin; **Telesphorus** is one of the Cabiri, a healing daimon associated with Asclepius.]

**The dead demand sacrifice; the nameless one** [p.335]
- What stands and waits at the bottom, "impending and red-hot"? Which fire has not been put out? Innumerable victims were sacrificed to the dark depths, which still demand more. "Come here and drink blood, so that you can speak" [EDITOR n.223: the Odyssey Book 11 libation to the dead, and Jung's 1912 use of the motif]. The depths reject blood, milk and wine and seem to demand "acceptance," that is, to be seized, embraced, copulated with, a desecration of the dead.
- **The prophet and the child:** the narrator recalls a prophet who lay on a dead child, mouth to mouth, eyes to eyes, hands to hands, until the body grew warm, rose, went about the house, and lay down again; the boy snorted seven times and opened his eyes [EDITOR: 2 Kings 4:32-36, Elisha and the Shunammite's son]. So acceptance should be: "not cool, not superior, not thought out... not as a self-chastisement, but with pleasure," with an **ambiguous impure pleasure** ("lusty repulsiveness, lecherous fear, sexual immaturity") that wakens the dead.
- The lowest sleeps like death and needs the warmth of life, which contains good and evil inseparably. This is not the goal but "the way and the crossing," "sickness and the beginning of recovery," "the mother of all abominable deeds and all salutary symbols," the most primordial form of creation, like water seeping through soil. It is the first secret teacher of nature, a great sage with superhuman knowledge, "serpentlike, perishable and beneficial, dreadfully and ridiculously daimonic," "the arrow that always hits the weakest spot." You can call it neither clever nor stupid, good nor evil; it is inhuman, **the son of the earth, the dark one whom you should awaken**, man and woman at once, "immature sex."
- The narrator hears the "voice of the son of the earth" only after his own spirit lay dismembered on the altar; he was the great suffering one who needed salvation, "the chosen one since he was the most rejected." The depths fall silent; he has risen into the light of the sun and is among the living; restlessness, discord, doubt and the fullness of life rose with him.
- **Good Friday:** "Amen, it is finished. What was unreal is real, what was real is unreal." The Good Friday on which Christ died and descended into Hell is when we complete Christ in us and descend to Hell ourselves; Christ's realm covered all the world and only Hell lay outside it. Who crossed the borders of this realm with good conscience and obeying the law of love? "Who among the living is Christ and journeys to Hell in living flesh?" [EDITOR n.225 refers back to note 135 on the descent to Hell.]
- **"I am he, the nameless one."** He does not know himself, has no name, has "only just become"; "In that I abased myself, I elevated myself as another. In that I accepted myself, I divided myself into two, and in that I united myself with myself, I became the smaller part of myself." He is in his ordinary consciousness yet distinct from "my second and greater state"; his smallness lets him be conscious of the nearness of the great. [Image 119, EDITOR n.226: the accursed dragon has eaten the sun, belly cut open, must not hand over the gold of the sun; this is the turning back of Atmavictu; the youth who destroyed the green covering "helped me to kill Siegfried" (Liber Primus chapter "Murder of the Hero").]
- He has been "baptized with impure water for rebirth," a flame from Hell's fire awaited him above the baptismal basin; he received "the divine brother, the son of the earth, the two-sexed and impure," who overnight became a man with broken incisors and down on his chin; "his black horse has parted from him"; the earth belongs to him. [p.335-336]

**The dark friend** [p.336]
- "I have shot down a proud enemy; I have forced a greater and stronger one to be my friend." The dark one follows like a shadow, turns into fear if denied, must be commemorated; the narrator sets a plate for him at his table and consecrates many days to him, so others call him selfish. Unrest has moved in ("a quiet underground earthquake, a distant great roaring"); ways are open to the primordial and the future. [EDITOR n.227: the Draft adds that he put aside people, books and thoughts to do the plain and simple.]
- **Images 121 and 122** [EDITOR notes 228-232, summarized]: the four rivers of paradise and a stone with four qualities ("breadth, height, depth, and time"), identified in an inscription dated 11/1919 as the **Lapis Philosophorum**, harder than diamond, an invisible "monad which countervails the Pleroma" with four streams of Aquarius flowing from it; a "back side of the gem" (4 Dec 1919) showing Atmavictu, "the old one," as stony residue, "in the form of Izdubar" having delivered Philemon and **Ka**; Philemon gave the stone and Ka the sun sign. The editor quotes Jung's *Memories* on **Ka**, the earth-bound, bronze-and-stone herm-figure who "buries the Gods in gold and gems," contrasted with winged, lame-footed Philemon, the "meaning"; Ka as the Egyptian "king's Ka" and as an Anthroparion (a goblin-helper in Greek alchemy). A 1920 patient diary (Constance Long) records Jung's gloss: Ka = Dionysus/sensation/substance, Philemon = Apollo/intuition/form, both "supra-human."
- Image 123 inscription (4 Jan 1920): "the caster of holy water. The Cabiri grow out of the flowers which spring from the body of the dragon." [n.233]

## The Three Prophecies, Cap. xviii [pp.336-337]
Date: **22 January 1914**, "as recorded in my black book" [p.336]. Editor: Black Book 4 (n.234). Sections [1] and [2].

**[1] The soul brings the flood's contents** [p.336-337]
- The narrator asks his soul to dive into the floods whose roaring he hears; she plunges like a shot and calls up, "Will you accept what I bring?" He answers he will accept what she gives, having no right to judge or reject.
- She lists, and he accepts each ("I accept it all, how should I dismiss anything?"): (a) the old armor and rusty gear of the fathers, weapons, bones of man and horse, relics of past battles; (b) painted stones, carved bones with magic signs, talismans, pouches of teeth, hair and fingernails, all the superstitions of dark prehistory; (c) "worse": fratricide, cowardly murder, torture, child sacrifice, annihilation of whole peoples, arson, betrayal, war, rebellion; (d) epidemics, natural catastrophes, sunken ships, razed cities, famines, human meanness, "whole mountains of fear"; (e) the treasures of all past cultures: images of Gods, temples, paintings, papyrus rolls, parchment in lost languages, "books full of lost wisdom," hymns of ancient priests, stories passed through thousands of generations.
- At the last list the narrator says it is an entire world whose extent he cannot grasp. The soul: "But you wanted to accept everything? You do not know your limits. Can you not limit yourself?" He: "I must limit myself. Who could ever grasp such wealth?" **S:** "Be content and cultivate your garden with modesty" [EDITOR n.235: end of Voltaire's *Candide*; Jung kept a bust of Voltaire in his study]. He sees a small well-tended garden is better than a large ill-tended one; both are equally small before the immeasurable, but unequally cared for. "Take shears and prune your trees."

**[2] The three things; the small garden** [p.337]
- From the flooding darkness the son of the earth had brought, the soul gave "ancient things that pointed to the future": **the misery of war, the darkness of magic, and the gift of religion.** These belong together: they mean both the unleashing and the binding of chaos. War is obvious; magic is dark and no one sees it; religion is still to come but will become evident. He had sat up nights looking ahead and shuddered; his spirit could not grasp the monstrous; he let it sink again into the depths. [EDITOR n.236: the Draft adds "eight hundred years, up to the time when the One begins his rule."]
- He cannot give it to others; "little good will come to you from outside. What will come to you lies within yourself." He returns "to the small and the real, for this is the great way," to his small, measurable garden. [Image 125, EDITOR n.237: the landscape resembles one of Jung's childhood waking fantasies of Alsace flooded, with Basel as a port.]
- **Taking the knife:** he judges everything that grew "without measure and goal"; forests, winding plants, endless proliferation cover him. The depths are inexhaustible; "everything is as good as nothing. Keep a little and you have something." Ambition and the craving to master and interpret what lies beyond oneself is "lunacy, like everything that transcends its boundaries." "A knower may know himself. That is his limit"; presumed knowledge of what lies beyond suffocates "the life of those who know themselves."
- He cuts away his own pretended knowledge, the interpretive loops, the meanings he conferred on himself, "down to the marrow," until he knows only "that I am without knowing what I am." He wants to be poor and bare, "my body and its poverty," "my human animal," recalling the early human alone on sunlit earth, terrified by ghosts and dreaming of distant Gods, who struck fire from stones and lived by what lay at hand and received by grace what lay far off. [p.337-338]
- **Free gods and devils:** "A free man knows only free Gods and devils that are self-contained and take effect on account of their own force." He will not feed, name and protect gods with belief against doubt; if they work, they need neither his protection nor belief; "You should be able to cast everything from you, otherwise you are a slave, even if you are the slave of a God." Life is limited enough, "so do not pile up more limitation."
- A horror follows: weakness, poverty and unpreparedness in the face of the riddlesome multiplicity of the world. The soul then whispers the next chapter. [p.338]

## The Gift of Magic, Cap. xix [pp.338-339]
Date: **23 January 1914** (n.238).

**The black rod** [p.338]
- The soul asks, "Do you not hear something?" "Ringing," in the left ear; she says it means "Misfortune." The narrator accepts fortune and misfortune and raises his hands: he receives **a black rod shaped like a serpent, with two pearls as eyes and a gold bangle around its neck**, a magical rod. It is a misfortune "for those who possess it," yet "Magic will do a lot for you."
- The narrator fears desire and misunderstanding ("man never stops craving the black art and things that cost no effort"). **S:** "Magic is not easy, and it demands sacrifice." Not love or humanity, but **solace**: "Solace is to be sacrificed," both the solace he gives and the solace he receives. This means giving up a piece of humanity, replaced by what is called severity toward oneself and others [EDITOR n.239: Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*: every step forward in knowledge is "severity toward oneself"]. The rod demands it; the source of the rod is "the darkness that lies before you," the next thing that comes. S: "Nature: does nature offer solace? Does it accept solace?" This is "your misfortune, and the power of the black rod."
- The narrator fears being sheathed in icy severity and a "bronze carapace" for magic; he says he does not believe in magic. S: don't act so "enlightened, as if deep down you did not believe in magic"; cast aside blind judgment and critical gesture; "high time that you overcame" science.
- [Image 127, EDITOR n.240: inscription "Amor triumphat"; a long inscription finished 9 January 1921: "a fourfold sacrifice... the inexorable wheel of the four functions" (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition, per *Psychological Types*). The editor adds a Black Book 7 passage (23 Feb 1920) on the lover and the beloved, and notes Jung's North Africa trip, 4 March to 17 April 1920.]

**Taking the rod** [p.339]
- Is science essential to life? There are people who live without it, yet to overcome science for magic is "uncanny." S: "Do you want the rod or don't you?" He submits: "I want the black rod because it is the first thing the darkness grants me. I don't know what this rod means, nor what it gives, I only feel what it takes." The rod is cold and heavy like iron; "time and fate? The essence of nature, hard and eternally inconsolable, yet the sum of all mysterious creative force?" He asks where he can shelter it: "In my heart?" The soul: "The most powerful magic lives in it." He shudders: "Sometimes one must shudder before the greatest." Black iron in the heart gives secret power, "like defiance and like contempt for men." [Editor n.241: Black Book 4 has the soul telling him to be patient; Hercules also found waiting troublesome, carrying the world while Atlas fetched the apples.]
- **A prayer/invocation** (set as an unnumbered passage): "Oh dark act, violation, murder! Abyss, give birth to the unredeemed. Who is our redeemer?" Raise your hand to the darkness above, pray, despair, kneel, "but do not name Him, do not look at Him. Leave Him without name and form. What should form the formless?" The gifts of darkness are riddles; "follow the riddles," endure the terrible; protect riddles and "be pregnant with them. Thus you carry the future." [EDITOR n.242: the Moirae and the Norns spin the threads of fate.]
- **One's own way:** the tension of the future is unbearable and wants to break through narrow cracks. "There is only one way and that is your way; there is only one salvation and that is your salvation." Do not look around for help, do not compare, do not measure. "No other way is like yours." "Oh, that all men and all their ways become strange to you! Thus might you find them again within yourself." People want a foot on others' paths to avoid solitude; but "you do not come to an end with your life, and the dead will besiege you terribly to live your unlived life. Everything must be fulfilled." Why pile up the lived and let the unlived rot?
- **Power of the way:** "In it Heaven and Hell grow together." Magic is "the working of men on men," but your magic first affects you, and only if you withstand it does an invisible effect pass to your neighbor. [Editor n.243-244: Draft adds that the way's power carries others and ignites them, and that invocation is represented by a serpent. n.245: the circle where ritual acts are performed. n.246: Matthew 24:40/Gethsemane "keep watch for an hour."]
- **The cosmic verse** [pp.339-340] (laid out in columns): "The Above is powerful, / The Below is powerful, / Twofold power is in the One." North, West, East, South are summoned in turn; "The winds in-between bind the cross"; steps lead from above to below; boiling water in cauldrons; red-hot ash on the "round floor." A second verse reverses it: "The form of the One becomes double"; the far poles are separated by poles in between; "the levels are broad ways, patient streets"; the pot grows cold; day approaches "and above the clouds a distant sun." [Image 131 and 135-136, EDITOR n.248: image 135 inscription dated 25 Nov 1922: "The fire comes out of Muspilli and grasps the tree of life. A cycle is completed, but it is the cycle within the world egg. A strange God, the unnameable God of the solitary, is incubating it. New creatures form from the smoke and ashes." Muspilli/Muspelheim: abode of the fire Gods.]

**The solitary and the crowd** [pp.340]
- A narrative poem: **a solitary cooks healing potions**, makes offerings to the four winds, greets the stars, touches the earth, holds something luminous; flowers sprout, birds fly around him; "He is far from men and yet the threads of their fate pass through his hands." He has chosen "a poor scribe, someone hard of hearing, who also stutters when he writes." He says he suffers fear and distress for man's sake; words never reach men and have become shadows, so he dug up old runes and magical sayings, prepared hot potions, stewed "the roots of all human thoughts and deeds," keeping the brew fermenting through starry nights; he needs the crowd's intercession, kneeling, desperation and patience.
- **The crowd's replies** (italic dialogue): they cry that they are utterly incapable and need help more than he does; ask him to come and help; question the use of potions; he asks whether they can keep watch an hour; they answer that the work of salvation is endless ("Are you a God, that a thousand nights are as one night to you?"), that they are "not alive; cold and numbness have seized us," and that if his salvation came to its end they would need saving from it again.
- **The solitary abandons the work:** he calls them a "human swarm, you foolish bastard of God and cattle"; he says one man already let himself be nailed to the cross for them, "he blocks my way. Therefore neither will I walk on his ways, nor make for you any healing brew or immortal blood potion"; he refuses to be a savior ("for the sake of men, I abstain from being a savior"), saying they should prove their worth "by each living for himself." His potion has fermented without a piece of himself, only a piece of humanity sliced in, and "it clarified the murky foaming potion."
- **The inexplicable occurs:** a final short stanza: "You would very much like to forsake yourself and defect to each and every manifold possibility... But the road is without end." [p.340]

## The Way of the Cross, Cap. XX [pp.340-342]
Date: **27 January 1914** (n.250). [EDITOR n.249: Jung's marginal note: "25 February 1923. The transformation of black into white magic."]

**Vision of the cross** [p.340-341]
- "I saw the black serpent as it wound itself upward around the wood of the cross. It crept into the body of the crucified and emerged again transformed from his mouth. It had become **white**. It wound itself around the head of the dead one like a diadem, and a light gleamed above his head, and the sun rose shining in the east." The narrator is confused and burdened. The **white bird** on his shoulder speaks: "Let it rain, let the wind blow, let the waters flow and the fire burn. Let each thing have its development, let becoming have its day." [EDITOR n.252: in Black Book 4 this was spoken by the soul; the shift in speakers between soul, serpent and bird marks Jung's differentiation of the three-fold nature of the soul. A Black Book 6 passage (1916) says the soul, if not conjoined through uniting Below and Above, breaks into three: the serpent (earthly, daimonic), the human soul within you, and the celestial soul in bird form. The editor compares Eckhart's Sermons 52 and 85 on the soul's higher and lower powers.]

**Numbered theses [2] through 8 on the way** (the narrator's reflections, p.341):
- **2.** The way leads through the crucified: through him "to whom it was no small thing to live his own life" and who was therefore raised to magnificence. He lived what was knowable instead of just teaching it. The disgust of one who wants to enter his own life is hardly measurable: "He makes himself vomit. His bowels pain him and his brain sinks into lassitude." Many choose to love people from fear of themselves; some commit a crime "to pick a quarrel with themselves." Therefore the narrator "cling[s] to everything that obstructs my way to myself."
- **3.** "He who goes to himself climbs down." Pathetic and ridiculous forms appeared to "the greatest prophet who came before this time" (Nietzsche's Zarathustra, per editor); he exorcized them before others but was ultimately forced to celebrate a Last Supper with his own poverty, accepting these forms out of compassion (acceptance of the lowest in us). This enraged "the mighty lion," which chased down the lost and restored it to the darkness; the one with the great name wanted to erupt like the sun; his way led before the crucified and he raged against "the man of mockery and pain." "No one speaks louder of his power and greatness than he from whom the earth disappears under his feet." Ultimately his lowest crucified his spirit "so that, as he himself had predicted, his soul died before his body." [EDITOR n.253-257: all references are to Nietzsche's *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (the Last Supper chapter; the lion in "The Sign"; the ending line; the tightrope walker in the prologue); Jung's 1926 comments on it as prophetic of Nietzsche's fate. The Draft states the book's subject is "how and why man does not want to reach himself."]
- **4.** "No one rises above himself who has not turned his most dangerous weapon against himself." What suffering must humanity undergo until it gives up the longing for power over fellow men and wanting others to be the same, until it sees "himself as the enemy" and its real success. "You ought to be able to live with yourself, but not at your neighbor's expense." The herd animal "is not his brother's parasite and pest." "Someone must begin to stop being childish."
- **5.** "Your craving satisfies itself in you." You can offer no more precious sacrificial meal to your God than yourself. If you devour others your greed stays dissatisfied, for "it craves more, the most costly: it craves you." "You may ask others provided that you need help and advice. But you should make demands on no one." You are afraid of burning in your own fire; "may nothing prevent you from doing so, neither anyone else's sympathy nor your more dangerous sympathy with yourself."
- **6.** When the flame consumes you and nothing remains but ash, the flame in which you consumed yourself "has illuminated many." If you flee from your fire in fear, you scorch your fellow men; the burning torment of your greed cannot die out "so long as you do not desire yourself."

**Theses 7 and 8 on the symbol** [pp.341-342]
- **7.** The mouth utters word, sign and symbol. "If the word is a sign, it means nothing. But if the word is a symbol, it means everything." When the way enters death and rot, the way "rises in the darkness and leaves the mouth as the saving symbol, the word," leading the sun on high, for in the symbol there is "the release of the bound human force struggling with darkness." "Our freedom does not lie outside us, but within us"; one can be bound outwardly yet free; "inner freedom [is created] only through the symbol." [EDITOR n.258: Jung's distinction between signs and symbols, *Psychological Types*.]
- **8.** The symbol "is the word that goes out of the mouth, that one does not simply speak, but that rises out of the depths of the self as a word of power and great need and places itself unexpectedly on the tongue"; astonishing, perhaps irrational, "alien to the conscious mind." If accepted, it opens a door into a new room; if not, one passes the only door to the inner chambers and is exposed outside again. "Salvation is a long road that leads through many gates. These gates are symbols." Each gate seems at first to need creating, "for it exists only if one has dug up the spring's root, the symbol."
- **Mandrake and black dog:** "To find the mandrake, one needs the black dog, since good and bad must always be united first if the symbol is to be created." The symbol "can be neither thought up nor found: it becomes," like human life in the womb; conception comes through voluntary copulation and "willing attention"; but a mother may want to throw herself on the child like a monster and devour it. [EDITOR n.259: mandrake shrieks when pulled from the ground; Jung's 1945 note on tying it to a black dog's tail.]
- **The word as guide:** in the morning the word steps out of the mouth but is "murdered lovelessly" because one did not know it was the savior; if accepted the newborn grows quickly and at once becomes the **charioteer**. "The word is the God that rises out of the waters each morning and proclaims the guiding law." Outer laws are insufficient; there is only "my daily law, my daily wisdom. The God renews himself each night." The God appears twofold and ambiguous (marked by the night waters), calling right and left but wanting **the middle way**, which is the beginning of the long road. Man sees only one side and "never that which the one as well as the other encloses in itself."
- **Standstill and the wheel** [p.342]: "The point of origin is where the mind and the will stand still": a standstill that provokes outrage, defiance and fear; yet "the resolution is born from precisely this tension." The resolution is always something ancient made new: "To give birth to the ancient in a new time is creation." "The task is to give birth to the old in a new time." The soul of humanity is like "the great wheel of the zodiac that rolls along the way"; every part comes around again. "The meaning does not lie in the eternal recurrence of the same, but in the manner of its recurring creation at any given time." The question: how to create one's charioteer? Will and intention are part of oneself, insufficient to express wholeness; they take goals from the present, so they replace the future with a constant present and treat anything breaking in as disturbance, closing off life's progress. "A wise man does not want to be a charioteer." Futurity "grows out of me; I do not create it, and yet I do, ... against will and intention." If I want to create the future I work against it; if I do not, "everything happens then according to unavoidable laws to which I fall victim." The ancients devised magic to compel outer fate; "we need it to determine inner fate and to find the way that we are unable to conceive." [EDITOR n.260-261: Draft's passage on Ixion bound to the wheel versus sitting beside the charioteer; eternal recurrence in Stoicism, Pythagoreanism and Nietzsche; Löwith's study.]
- The chapter ends with the narrator, unable to find a magic within himself, deciding to become an apprentice and travel to a far country where a great magician lives. [p.342-343]

## The Magician, Cap. xxi [pp.343-361]
Dates (editor): begins **27 January 1914** (n.263); later passages through spring 1914. The Draft numbering divides the chapter into sections {1} to {8}; the calligraphic volume stops at the end of {6} at [p.356]. Editor n.262: the Handwritten Draft calls this "Tenth Adventure."

### {1} Philemon and Baucis; the lesson of magic [pp.343-346]

**The scene** [p.343]
- After long search the narrator finds a small country house fronted by a large bed of tulips. **Philemon the magician** lives there with his wife **Baucis**: elderly, narrow-interested, watering tulips and telling each other about new flowers; their days "fade into a pale wavering chiaroscuro, lit up by the past, only slightly frightened of the darkness of what is to come." The narrator wonders why Philemon is a magician; "he now appears to be a pensioned magician who has retired from service"; his magic rod lies in a cupboard with the sixth and seventh books of Moses and the wisdom of Hermes Trismegistus; he still murmurs spells for bewitched cattle for petty cash, "but it is uncertain if these spells are still correct." Baucis watches calmly from the kitchen window, her sight failing.
- [EDITOR n.264-268 summarized: Ovid's *Metamorphoses* tale of Philemon and Baucis (an old couple who alone host disguised Zeus and Hermes, are spared the flood, see their hut become a temple, and become trees side by side); Goethe's *Faust* Part 2, where Faust has Philemon and Baucis burned in their cottage, and Goethe's remark to Eckermann that the names were chosen only to elevate the characters; Jung's 1955 letter: Philemon = the loving one, "the complete opposite to the Superman Faust," and his Bollingen inscription "Philemon sacrum Fausti poenitentia" (Philemon's Sanctuary, Faust's Repentance); in alchemy the pair stand for artifex and soror mystica; and Jung's 1921 remark that the magician preserves "a trace of primordial paganism," unaffected by Christian splitting, hence "a destroyer as well as savior." The sixth and seventh books of Moses were published 1849 by Schiebel; Hermes Trismegistus = Hermes plus Thoth.]

**Dialogue: the lesson of magic** [pp.343-345]
- The narrator greets Philemon at the garden gate; the old man is deaf-seeming, white-bearded, with gray old eyes in which "something... one would like to say alive." The narrator says he wants to learn the black art, which professors no longer teach. Philemon: "There is nothing to tell"; his past magic was "sympathy" (which the narrator notes can mean compassion or sympathetic magic: "surely it would have been both"). Magic would be lost with him but "magic is reborn with each and every one of us"; it is inborn in man. The narrator agrees: all peoples in all times have the same magical customs.
- **Teachings in dialogue form:** (1) The narrator says he "leaves his so-called reason at home" when he wants to understand something, giving the object "the benefit of the doubt" (Philemon: "your reason does not deter you"). (2) When the narrator waits expectantly, Philemon says: "And what if I say nothing?" The narrator says he would think him "a shrewd fox"; Philemon: "With this, my boy, you have learned something about magic." (3) Philemon gives numbered points: first, magic does not follow ordinary understanding; second, "magic is the negative of what one can know"; third, "there is nothing for you to understand" because "magic happens to be precisely everything that eludes comprehension." (4) Magic "is neither to be taught nor learned"; to learn it one must "completely unlearn" reason, which declines with old age, being the counterpart of the drives, which are stronger in youth ("Have you ever seen young magicians?"). A magician is "proverbially old." (5) "Nor can one simply become a magician." Stupidity may be progress toward magic.
- **What does it do for him?** "I am alive, as you see." Other old men are as well: "Yes, but have you seen how?" His advantage "doesn't exactly meet the eye. I call that magic." The narrator calls this a vicious circle and says he feels dizzy. Philemon: not even the devil gets the better of him; "You're beginning to understand magic."
- **The aftermath** [p.345]: outside, people whisper "old Philemon's student... He knows the mysteries." The narrator cannot tell them they are fools because he does not know whether he has learned anything; his silence makes them more convinced he has received the black art.

**Reflections on magic** (section [2], [p.345]) [p.345]
- "It is an error to believe that there are magical practices that one can learn." One can understand only what accords with reason; magic accords with unreason; the world accords with both. A lack of understanding also "accords with unreason." "This meeting is magical and eludes comprehension." The magical "always surrounds me, always involves me. It opens spaces that have no doors and leads out into the open where there is no exit. The magical is good and evil and neither." Magic is dangerous: unreason "confuses, allures and provokes; and I am always its first victim."
- Where reason abides one needs no magic; "our time no longer needs magic"; it is unreasonable to combine reason and magic; a rational man will never use magic [EDITOR n.270: *Psychological Types* on the need of the opposite of one's condition]. But **whoever has opened the chaos in himself** needs magic "to be able to receive or invoke the messenger and the communication of the incomprehensible." The world comprises reason and unreason, and so must our way. The distinction between comprehensible and incomprehensible is arbitrary and depends on level of comprehension: part of the incomprehensible is only presently incomprehensible and may accord with reason tomorrow; the unreasonable part needs magical practice.
- "The practice of magic consists in making what is not understood understandable in an incomprehensible manner." The magical way "arises by itself. If one opens up chaos, magic also arises." One can teach the way that leads to chaos, but not magic; silence is the best apprenticeship. One needs reason for the translation of the not-understood into the understandable, though "No one can say how to use reason." [EDITOR n.271-272: the Draft summarizes magical practice in two parts: understanding chaos, and translating the essence into what can be understood.]
- **Magic as a way of living:** "If one has done one's best to steer the chariot, and one then notices that a greater other is actually steering it, then magical operation takes place." The effect cannot be predicted; "the magical is the lawless, which occurs without rules." The condition is total acceptance. Stupidity and tastelessness are part of it. A certain solitude and slowness "like a standstill" are inescapable; the uncertainty will be the greatest burden. He must hold the two conflicting powers of his soul in "a true marriage until the end of my life, since the magician is called Philemon and his wife Baucis." "I hold together what Christ has kept apart in himself."
- **The Aeon passage:** "When the month of the Twins had ended, the men said to their shadows: 'You are I'... Thus the two became one, and through this collision... that spring of consciousness that one calls culture and which lasted until the time of Christ. But the fish indicated the moment when what was united split, according to the eternal law of contrasts, into an underworld and upperworld." Christ sent what is beneath to Hell, since it strives toward the good. "But the separated cannot remain separated forever... the month of the fish will soon be over." We keep good and evil together, because too far into good equals too far into evil. But then direction is lost: things grow "from the valley to the mountain," the stream becomes a lake and an ocean without outlet unless it rises as steam and falls as rain. "Such is Philemon, who tends his garden... He rises invisibly and falls as rain on distant lands." Only pregnant women can give birth. [EDITOR n.273-277: the Platonic months (about 2,300 years); Pisces to Aquarius; Jung's *Aion* on the "hostile brothers" and the union of opposites; the Draft adds that "hibernal rains began with Christ... We teach the way to earth. Hence nothing has been removed from the Gospel, but only added to it," and a long passage on stupidity as a "hobbyhorse" that separates the mixed seeds of life. Jung's marginal note dates this transcription to Jan 1924, when Cary Baynes began her transcription.]

### {1 continued} Praise and puzzlement concerning Philemon [pp.346-348]

The narrator, after leaving, addresses Philemon in a series of reflective paragraphs, headed by [HI 146] and later numbers. Main points:
- Philemon is **the lover who survives**: he took in the Gods when everyone else refused them, served in the sanctuary when peoples cried in vain to the Gods; the goose Baucis wanted to serve fled to the Gods who revealed themselves. The hut is a temple; Philemon and Baucis serve in the sanctuary. His "final mystery": he is a lover who has united the sundered, "binding together the Above and Below." This power is neither taught nor learned.
- Philemon is "a lover of your soul, who anxiously and jealously guards its treasure." There are those who love men, those who love the souls of men, and those who love their own soul. He lies in the sun "like a serpent that coils around itself"; "your wisdom is the wisdom of serpents, cold, with a grain of poison, yet healing in small doses"; his magic paralyzes and "makes strong people who tear themselves away from themselves." He is "no Christian and no pagan, but a hospitable inhospitable one, a host of the Gods, a survivor, an eternal one."
- The narrator tried to seize him ("Christians have learned to devour their God") and did not manage; he ate himself instead: "your words left me to myself and my doubt. And so I ate myself." Philemon "nourishes yourself from yourself and force[s] men to do the same," and nothing disgusts the human animal more than itself, so men prefer to eat any creature before "they nibble at themselves." But this nourishment works and one rises satiated.
- Philemon does not give and does not act as savior or shepherd: "The virtue of giving is the sky-blue mantle of the tyrant"; "Giving is as childish as power." He leaves room for others' stupidity and jokes, believes in the dignity of man ("who is not necessarily a sheep"). "Christ spoiled men, since he taught them that they can be saved only by one," so they now demand greater things from others. Philemon is old, legendary, and returning periodically; his wisdom "entirely untrue in any given age, and yet true in all eternity"; he pours "starry water, a dew of the night."
- **Solitude of the magician:** he lives with flowers, trees and birds, not men. The people spin rumors and fairy tales about him but would laugh if he said he was a mortal who wanted to love them: they want a king who lives from himself and owes no one gratitude. "Philemon is not a man" to them. A crease in his face shows he was once young and wanted to be a man among men, but "the Christian animals" locked up the branded, free one "in a golden cage" and took "the force of his masculinity," then praised and made fables about him ("veneration"); "if they do not find the true one, they at least have a Pope, whose occupation it is to represent the divine comedy." The true one "always disowns himself, since he knows nothing higher than to be a man." Philemon voluntarily locked away his humanity so as to be for men what they wanted. For the flowers, trees and birds he is a man. [p.347]
- **The blue shades:** "humanity has completely faded for you, but its shadow has arisen"; "How much greater and happier the shadow of humanity is than it is itself!" Philemon is "a teacher and friend of the dead," who stand sighing in his shade, drink the dew of his tears, and hunger for his words. The narrator once saw him at noon speaking with a blue shade with blood on its forehead and solemn torment: "I can guess... who your midday guest was." [EDITOR n.280: in a 1 June 1916 fantasy the guest was Christ.] The narrator goes on his way "shaking my head," silent, "a wandering beggar." [pp.347]
- **Closing statement of the section** [p.347-348]: "Killing off would-be prophets is a gain for the people. If they want murder, then may they kill their false prophets. If the mouth of the Gods remains silent, then each can listen to his own speech. He who loves the people remains silent." Only after the darkest night will it be day; "cover the lights and remain silent so that the night will become dark and noiseless." "Only he who knows the darkest error knows what light is." The narrator venerates Philemon's "deceptive mantle, you father of all will-o'-the-wisps." [EDITOR n.281-282: Jung's marginal note quotes Bhagavad Gita 4.7-8; Image 154 shows "Father of the Prophet, beloved Philemon" and Jung later painted a mural of it in his Bollingen tower with a Rosarium philosophorum inscription on the stone speaking "defend me and I will defend thee... my nature is darkness."]

### {2} The serpent, the steel and the Last Supper [pp.348-349]

- The narrator continues on his way "accompanied by a finely polished piece of steel, hardened in ten fires," wearing chain mail under his coat; overnight he "became fond of serpents, and solved their riddle." Having learned magic from Philemon, he plays a flute to a great iridescent serpent on a red rock, making her believe she is his soul. [Image 155, EDITOR n.283: the anima in a church in place of the altar, with a Latin inscription from 1 Corinthians 2 and Revelation 22:17, "Ave virgo virginum," and an Arabic word for "daughters."]
- He addresses her as "my sister, my soul"; she flatteringly says "I let grass grow over everything that you do." [Date 29 January 1914 per n.284; 31 January for the next dialogue, n.287.] Dialogue: the serpent says "Banality is my element"; "The more uncommon you are, the more common I can be. A true respite." The narrator: "I notice that you like being banal." S: "Have you noticed that the becoming of the soul follows a serpentine path? ... everything spasmodic is merely destructive."
- The narrator lies on a warm stone; the serpent winds round his feet ("a line not in Black Book 4": the voice is not there identified as the serpent). At evening: "All pots are on the boil." S: "A meal is being prepared." I: "A Last Supper, I suppose?" S: "A union with all humanity." I: "A horrifying, sweet thought: to be both guest and dish at this meal." S: "That was also Christ's highest pleasure." [EDITOR n.288: Jung's *Mysterium Coniunctionis* on celebrating a Last Supper with oneself.]
- The narrator experiences opposites embracing: "Madness and reason want to be married, the lamb and the wolf graze peacefully side by side"; waves of dark and bright rivers crash together; "tears and laughter are one"; "Loving reaches up to Heaven and resisting reaches just as high." The serpent says one could say more simply that he has fallen in love "all the way from the worm up to Tristan and Isolde." She asks if religion still torments him ("How many shields do you still need?"), if morality and immorality have become one; he says the two that rose to Heaven entwined are both good and evil. S: "You deny everything that you believed... even Faust." I: "My spirit, too, is a specter."
- **Armor and fang:** the serpent becomes angry and tries to bite his heart; his secret armor breaks her poisonous fang (not in the Black Book). She hides the broken fang with her tail. She asks how he has become unfathomable; he says he learned "the art of stepping from the left foot onto the right and vice versa."

### {3} The union of the opposites and the standstill [pp.349-351]
Date: **1 February 1914** (n.297).
- **Teaching on the serpent (p.349):** "Where is truth and faith? ... where there is love, the serpentlike abides also." Christ compared himself to a serpent; the Antichrist is the old dragon. "What is beyond the human that appears in love has the nature of the serpent and the bird"; "What seems like a bird to you is a serpent to the other." "He who is becoming belongs in the desert or in a prison, for he is beyond the human"; "No one saves us from the evil of becoming, unless we choose to go through Hell." He decides to "enchant" his soul himself and have her give her wisdom.
- **Mandala (Image 159), EDITOR n.296:** a flower in the center with stars and walls with eight gates, painted after the death of Jung's friend Hermann Sigg, 9 Jan 1927; based on a 2 Jan 1927 dream; reproduced anonymously in 1930 and 1952. The editor quotes Jung's later account (the flower as ruby, wheel or wall with gates; "a window opening on to eternity") and his remark that his "Western prejudice that I was the center of the mandala was corrected."
- **The question to the serpent:** "How will it be, now that God and the devil have become one? ... Does he who recognizes and lives the unity of opposites stand still? ... Through taking this burden from life, has he also taken the force from it?" The serpent, ill-humored: "Opposites were certainly an element of life for me. Your innovations deprive me of this source of power. I can neither lure you with pathos nor annoy you with banality. I am somewhat baffled."
- **Fetching Satan** [pp.350-351]: The narrator says they have "cut off an important organ from life": the opposites united, nothing further happened; "life turned into a complete standstill." He asks the serpent to "fetch the fire." The serpent: "You might have rejected the apple." (Black Book 4: "playing Adam and Eve with me.") Marginal note: "Visio." [Vision:] "Slowly, the throne of the God ascends into empty space, followed by the holy trinity; all of Heaven, and finally **Satan** himself. He resists and clings to his beyond. The upperworld is too chilly for him." The narrator pulls Satan up by the hands; Satan protests the violent extraction ("I'm freezing"). The narrator says they have united the opposites, "among other things we have bonded you with God"; Satan: "This smells of monism... Special chambers have been heated for them." [EDITOR n.305: Haeckel's Monism, which Jung criticized.]
- **Satan's counsel:** his reaction is not personal: "I am utterly restless, quickly hurrying life. I am never contented... I pull everything down and hastily rebuild. I am ambition, greed for fame, lust for action; I am the fizz of new thoughts and action. The absolute is boring and vegetative." Advice: "revoke your completely harmful innovation as soon as possible." The narrator: "What one has grasped once, one cannot intentionally not know again." Satan: you could not exist without divisiveness; you need a party and opposites. Narrator: "We also see each other in the opposite. We have grown tired of this game." S: "And so with life." I: "It depends on what you call life. Your notion... climbing up and tearing down, assertion and doubt... You lack the absolute and its forbearing patience." Satan agrees his life "bubbles and foams"; the absolute "lives interminably slowly and wastes thousands of years." Narrator: "You are personal life, but the apparent standstill is the forbearing life of eternity, the life of divinity!" He lets Satan go; Satan crawls "like a mole" back into his hole while the trinity rises "in peace and equanimity." "We can live again, a long life. We can waste thousands of years." [p.351]

### {3 continued} Beginning again; the Cabiri [pp.351-353]
- **Beginning:** "Where to begin, oh Gods? ... The beginning is always the smallest, it begins in nothing." "The little drop of 'something' that falls into the sea of nothingness." "Nothing has happened yet, the world has yet to begin..." He recalls the fathers resting "in the womb of our bloodthirsty Europe." They stand "wed to the serpent" and look for a **foundation stone**, "a symbol" to be graspable. The devil must create it: the "paltry partisan" emerged from the manure where the Gods secured their eggs. [Image 163, EDITOR n.307: "1928. When I painted this image, which showed the golden well-fortified castle, Richard Wilhelm sent me from Frankfurt the Chinese, thousand-year-old text of the golden castle, the embryo of the immortal body." A mandala as a fortified city with walls, moats and a golden-roofed central castle, reproduced in 1930 and 1952; the editor notes the *Golden Flower* parallel.]
- **Forging the devil:** "Arise then, son of darkness and stench! ... Today you shall be forged with heavy hammers so that the gold of the Gods will spray out of your body... Steel is harder than ice." From his body come "elemental spirits, dressed in wrinkled garb, **Cabiri**, with delightful misshapen forms, young and yet old, dwarfish, shriveled, ... bearers of secret arts... first formations of the unformed gold." [EDITOR n.310: the Cabiri of Samothrace; Creuzer, Schelling; Jung's 1940 gloss on them as creative gnomes below the threshold of consciousness who also play tricks, "a treasure-house of hidden wisdom"; Goethe's *Faust* Part 2. This dialogue is not in Black Book 4 but in the Handwritten Draft.]
- **The Cabiri address him:** "We come to greet you as the master of the lower nature." They carry what slumbers in the earthly from below to above; "We are the juices that rise secretly"; they know living matter's unknown ways; they "complete what is impossible for you." They tell him not to try to pull up by force what can only rise slowly. [p.351-352]

**The sword and the brain-knot** [pp.352-353]
- Marginal note: "Thereupon I laid this matter aside for three weeks." [n.311] The narrator says he lived in daylight meanwhile. The Cabiri: "We forged a flashing sword for you, with which you can cut the knot that entangles you." They place before him the knot: **a human brain**, "a wily natural tangle of roots grown through one another... a great snarled ball and a thousand small knots." "Did you give me a sword so that its flashing sharpness slices through my brain?" Cabiri: "The womb of nature wove the brain, the womb of the earth gave the iron. So the Mother gave you both: entanglement and severing." "The entanglement is your madness, the sword is the overcoming of madness." [EDITOR n.312-313: the alchemical sword as separatio and solutio; Schelling's overcoming of madness.]
- The narrator resists ("You offsprings of the devil..."); they say they themselves are the entanglement and want their own destruction: "As sons of the devil we want destruction, but as your creatures we want our own destruction. We want to rise up in you through death. We are roots that suck up from all sides." "He who finds the way rises up over his brain. You are a Tom Thumb in the brain, beyond the brain you gain the form of a giant." "The master serves himself." He strikes: "Woe, woe! What we feared, what we desired, has come to pass."
- **The tower** [p.352-353]: "I set foot on new land. Nothing should flow back. ... My tower is of iron and has no seams. The devil is forged into the foundations. The Cabiri built it and the master builders were sacrificed with the sword on the battlements." He "stands above my brain, from which I grew," hard, a master of his own self, serving himself. The tower "grew for several thousand years, imperishable... Only he who finds the entrance hidden in the mountain and rises up through the labyrinths of the innards can reach the tower." It is "new and strange" and "forged from the glowing heat of the innards," built "out of the lower and upper beyond and not from the surface of the world." "This is the solid and the beginning." [EDITOR n.314-315: Jung's marginal note "accipe quod tecum est" referring to Manget's alchemical collection; his Bollingen tower begun 1920; the Tower in *The Shepherd of Hermas* (*Psychological Types*).]

### {4} The hanged poisoner and the beyond [pp.353-354]
Date: **2 February 1914** (n.316).
- Having united with the serpent of the beyond and accepted everything beyond into himself, the narrator asks his serpent to bring news of the beyond; she hesitates, descends. Her voice: "I believe that I have reached Hell. There is a **hanged man** here." A plain ugly man with contorted face, protruding ears and a hunchback: "I am a poisoner who was condemned to the rope." He poisoned his parents and wife "to honor God": he loved them and wanted to transport them "more quickly from a wretched life into eternal blessedness," gave them "too strong a nightcap." He admits he wanted to live for the sake of his two children and was healthier than his wife. Now in "Hell" he sometimes thinks his wife is there; they speak only of trivial things from daily life, "completely impersonal." "There is no time with us, so there is none to spend. Nothing at all happens." His father said something about a lost tobacco pipe. "Boring? ... it's pretty much all the same." He has not met the devil; asked about the dead he says the prospect "means nothing much to me." He vanishes. [Black Book 4 has "soul" where the text says serpent, n.317.]
- **Serpent's commentary:** she chose him "as the next best"; the beyond is "nothing but motion... shadowy... nothing personal whatsoever." Satan is "the quintessence of the personal... the eternal adversary," because personal life and absolute life cannot be reconciled; they are not opposites "but simply differences, just as little as you make the day the opposite of the year or the bushel the opposite of the cubit." "Particularly since we have balanced the opposites and married. I believe the dead will soon become extinct."

### The devil and the dead: the narrator's ruling teaching [p.353-354]
- "The devil is the sum of the darkness of human nature. He who lives in the light strives toward being the image of God; he who lives in the dark strives toward being the image of the devil." When he touched the depths, the sun went out. He united himself with the serpent, "just as a man unites with a woman," so the devil could not gain a grip through his serpenthood (a pact like Faust's, n.318). Mephistopheles is Satan "taken with my serpenthood"; Satan himself is "pure negation without convincing force." He fettered Satan and sacrificed "his descendants" with the sword.
- Result: a firm structure and duration; "the immortal in me is saved." By drawing the darkness over into the day he "emptied my beyond," so the demands of the dead disappeared. "Death gives me durability and solidity." Having satisfied the demands of the dead he gave up personal striving; the world took him for dead; "a great cold comes over whoever in the excess of his personal striving has recognized the demands of the dead." The voices of the dead fall silent and "his life is beautiful and rich, since he is himself."
- Those who want only the fortune of others are ugly and cripple themselves; "A murderer is one who wants to force others to blessedness, since he kills his own growth. A fool is one who exterminates his love for the sake of love." Such a person is cursed to force himself upon himself in cold nothingness. He who has recognized the dead's demands "lives alone in beauty and speaks with the dead." But the day comes when the dead's demands are satisfied; if one still persists in solitude, "beauty fades into the beyond and the wasteland comes over onto this side. A black stage comes after the white, and Heaven and Hell are forever there." [Marginal note n.320: "I still did not realize that I myself was this murderer."]

### {5} Elijah and Salome; the payment [pp.354-356]
Date: **9 February 1914** (n.321).
- Having found beauty in himself, the narrator says to the serpent that the work is accomplished; she says "Nothing is accomplished yet... This is only the beginning." The dead have almost become extinct; "Life has yet to begin. Didn't you feel empty today? Do you call that life?" She says he is entitled to a reward: "I give you payment in images."
- **Elijah and Salome:** "The cycle is completed and the gates of the mysteries have opened again. Elijah leads Salome, the seeing one, by the hand," she blushes. Elijah offers her: "May she be yours." The narrator: "I am already married and we are not among the Turks" [n.322: polygamy in Turkey banned 1926]. Elijah says her healing is the narrator's doing; the narrator says the care "was, literally, pressed out of me." Salome (to the narrator): "I want to be your maid and serve you... I will carry all your thoughts in my heart. I will kiss the words that you speak to me." The narrator weeps and says he could kneel and kiss her hands because "One can never hear enough of love being spoken."
- **The refusal of possession:** Sal: "Why only speak? I want to be yours, utterly and completely yours." I: "You are like the serpent that coiled around me and pressed out my blood... I stand like someone crucified." He says it is "impossibility that lames me," and he doubts it is her destiny to belong to him, mentions a dream of lying on needles with a bronze wheel crushing his breast. Sal offers to lay herself aside when his time has come. They exchange: "Can I not be joy to you?" "The attempt is the same as the act. Such attempts are costly." Salome: "So you really demand that I be and not be at the same time." I: "I lack the strength to hoist another fate onto my shoulders."
- **Elijah and the principle of burdens:** E: "May each one carry his load. He who wants to burden others with his baggage is their slave" [n.325: Nietzsche, master and slave morality, *Genealogy of Morals*]. Sal: "Or my master and ruler." I: "I can bear neither slaves nor masters. I long for men." "Be your own master and your own slave, do not belong to me but to yourself." He asks Salome to give from fullness, not longing: "I can satisfy myself only at the table of the satisfied, not at the empty bowls of those who yearn... Insofar as you give, you demand." He tells Elijah not to give away his daughter but to "set her on her own feet" and asks her to dance and sing before the crowd for coins, throwing him a rose now and then: "I long for the joy of men, for their fullness and freedom and not their neediness."
- **The serpent returned:** E says his serpent has been "stolen" and things have been gloomy; the narrator says he has her: "We fetched her from the underworld. She gave me hardness, wisdom, and magical power. We need her in the upperworld, since otherwise the underworld would have had the advantage." E curses him; he replies: "Whoever possesses the serpent cannot be touched by curses... Whoever possesses wisdom is not greedy for power. Only the man who has power declines to use it." "Fortune is only what you yourself create and not what comes to you." He tells them to go: "Elijah, expunge the false gleam of power from your wisdom, and you, Salome, for the sake of our love, do not forget to dance." [pp.355]
- **Reflection** [p.355-356]: Having achieved pleasure and power over himself, Salome lost pleasure in herself but learned love for another, and Elijah lost the power of his wisdom but learned to recognize the spirit of the other; Salome "lost the power of temptation and has become love." The narrator accepts Salome as pleasure but rejects her as love, since love for himself "would bind me like an iron ring that would stifle me." He wants his thinking to push love "into the world, into things, into men. For something should join men together, something should be a bridge. It is the most difficult temptation, if even my love wants me!"

### {6} Hanging; the crown "Love never ends" [pp.356-357]
Date: **11 February 1914** (n.327). **The calligraphic volume ends here:** after the bird speaks of the crown, the "transcription in the calligraphic volume of Liber Novus ends at this point"; what follows is from the Draft pp.533-56 (n.330).
- Salome still cries. The serpent (the soul in Black Book 4) says life must cost something; the narrator thinks he has paid by rejecting Salome; she says he is allowed to make demands of himself; advises thinking, then says: "I am being pulled upward." The serpent turns into a **small white bird** that soars into the clouds. The **bird** returns with "a discarded crown... on a street in the immeasurable space of Heaven, a golden crown." Inside the crown the inscription: **"Love never ends"** [EDITOR n.331: 1 Corinthians 13:8, cited again at the end of Jung's *Memories*; first in Greek letters in Black Book 4].
- The bird says "I certainly believe... that just as little happens in Heaven as in Hell." Asked what it makes of the crown: "Nothing. It truly speaks for itself." Then it becomes the serpent again ("This sentence is added in the Draft"). S: "Only for him who isn't in agreement with me" is unnerving. "To hang in the air in such a way is gruesome." "You must also be able to hang if you want to solve problems. Look at Salome!"
- **The narrator hangs:** "I hover and curse my hovering. I am hanging for your sake and for mine. First I was crucified, now I'm simply hanging, which is less noble, but no less agonizing" [EDITOR n.334: *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido* on hanging]. He expects to be decapitated "a third time" like John. Salome: "How can I? You are hanging too high. High on the summit of the tree of life where I cannot reach." He must hang "until you have devised help for yourself." Salome becomes ecstatic seeing the crown: "The crown! You have the crown!... you are to be crowned! What blessedness for me and you!" He asks what the crown means; she says cruelly: "Hang until you understand."
- **Three days and nights** [p.357]: He hangs on "the swaying branch of the divine tree, for whose sake the original ancestors could not avoid sin," hands bound. The bird, wearing "her white feather dress," flies up to fetch help from the clouds, vanishing into gray. The crown lies far below, winking gold. "Love never ends: does that mean eternal hanging?" A raven on a nearby branch: "That depends entirely on the notion... your notion of love and the other." The narrator says "heavenly and earthly love" [EDITOR n.336: Swedenborg's heavenly love], and that he wants to be a full-fledged man; the raven: "You're an ideologue."
- A black serpent coils around a branch: "I am only my own half; I'm not one, but two... magic is useless here. ... You can use me in life, but not in hanging. In the worst case, I'm ready to lead you to Hades." **Satan** condenses out of the air: "See what comes from the reconciliation of the opposites! Recant, and in a flash you'll be down on the greening earth." The narrator refuses ("let it be the end"). Satan: "Whoever gets on in a sovereign way with the opposites knows left from right." S: "Weakness too is a way; magic makes good the error." Satan: "Have you not yet once had the courage of weakness?" Salome returns next morning: "I haven't cried anymore, for good fortune and misfortune are balanced in me." Satan: "Reconciliation of the opposites! Equal rights for all! Follies!"
- **The bird's riddle:** "If you love the earth, you are hanged; if you love the sky, you hover." "Everything under you is the earth, everything above you is the sky. You fly if you strive for what is above you; you are hanged if you strive for what is below you." "Above you is what is before and over you; beneath you is what comes back under you." "The crown and serpent are opposites, and are one. Did you not see the serpent that crowned the head of the crucified?... 'Love never ends': that is the mystery of the crown and the serpent." Salome? "Salome is what you are. Fly, and she will grow wings." The clouds part, a crimson sunset ends the third day [n.337: the third day of Genesis separates sea and land]; the narrator glides down from the tree "softly and peacefully," night falls.

### {6 continued} The prison-tower; life above love [pp.357-358]
- Fear befalls him: "Whom did you carry to the mountain, you Cabiri? And whom have I sacrificed in you?" They have made him into "a tower on inaccessible crags, my church, my monastery, my place of execution, my prison." "I am my own priest and congregation, judge and judged, God and human sacrifice." A cruel law has come from chaos. [p.357-358]
- "Words are poor, and beauty does not attend them. But is truth beautiful and beauty true?" [n.338: Keats's *Ode on a Grecian Urn*]. "One can speak in beautiful words about love, but about life? And life stands above love. But love is the inescapable mother of life. Life should never be forced into love, but love into life." As long as love goes pregnant with life it should be respected; "if it has given birth to life from itself it has turned into an empty sheath and expires into transience." "I separate myself from the bearing womb. I speak no more for the sake of love, but for the sake of life." [n.339: the heroic battle for deliverance from the mother in *Transformations and Symbols*.]
- "A man needs his mother until his life has developed. Then he separates from her. And so life needs love until it has developed, then it will cut loose from it. ... Love seeks to have and to hold, but life wants more." "The beginning of all things is love, but the being of things is life." [n.340: Eros in Hesiod, Phanes in Orphism, Kama.] "Why do you force me to say that whoever loves does not live and whoever lives does not love? I always get it backward! Should everything be turned into its opposite?" [n.341: *enantiodromia*, attributed to Heraclitus.] He wonders if the sea will be where Philemon's temple stands and the sea floor where Ararat arises [n.342].

### {6 continued} The narrator's rage at the "son"; the monster [p.358]
- He addresses the mute "son of the earth" who thrusts between his soul and him: "You monster, a son, ha! a frightful miscreant, a stammerer, a newt's brain, a primordial lizard... lazy bug-eyed frog that wears pond weed on his skull's pate!" He declares it no son of his but "the spawn of the devil. The father of the devil entered into the womb of my soul and in you has become flesh."
- He accuses Philemon: "You most cunning of all fraudsters! You have deceived me. You impregnated my maidenly soul with the terrible worm... you laid the mantle of the stars on me, you played a Christ-fool's comedy with me, you hanged me, carefully and ludicrously, in the tree just like Odin [n.343: Odin hung on Yggdrasill nine nights for the runes], you let me devise runes to enchant Salome, and meanwhile you procreated my soul with the worm." He is "laughable father of the Antichrist," a would-be "Joseph father," with "a puny basilisk in the nest of the dove." The soul is called "adulterous whore." "You broke love and life in twain. From this ghastly sundering, the frog and the son of the frog come forth... They will sit on the banks of the sweet water and listen to the nocturnal song of the frogs, since their God has been born as a son of frogs."
- "Where is Salome? Where is the unresolvable question of love? No more questions, my gaze turned to the coming things, and Salome is where I am. The woman follows your strongest, not you."

### {7} The fairy tale of the king and the son [pp.358-360]
Date: **23 February 1914** (n.344). In Black Book 4 the dialogue is with the soul; Jung was told his ambition was stopping him from returning to his work; she tells the tale. [EDITOR: Jung gave a talk "On dream symbolism" 13 Feb 1914 in Zurich; vacation in Italy 30 March to 13 April.]
- Standing alone on the earth under rain clouds and falling night, his serpent tells him a story. **The tale:** A childless king wants a son. A wise woman who lives as a witch in the forest hears his sins like a priest and says: "Take a pound of otter lard, bury it in the earth, and let nine months pass." He does so and finds a sleeping infant in the pot (the lard has vanished). The queen nurses the child; he grows great and strong. At twenty the son tells the father he knows he was made by sorcery from the king's repentance and, "born from no woman," is clever; he demands the crown. The king, startled and fearing, secretly resolves to have him killed, then returns to the sorceress who says: "You confess no sin to me this time, because you want to commit a sin." She advises burying another pot of otter lard; the son grows weaker and dies, and the king digs the grave beside the empty pot. Saddened, the king returns again; the sorceress says: "You wanted a son, but the son wanted to be king himself... Then you wanted your son no more. Because of this you lost your son. Why are you complaining? You have everything that you wanted." He asks for a remedy against remorse: fill the pot again at the son's grave. After nine months the pot holds a sleeping infant who is the dead son; he grows as much in a week as other infants in a year. At twenty weeks the son claims the realm, but the father, having learned, rises, embraces him with tears of joy and crowns him. The son, now king, is grateful and honors his father as long as he lives.
- **The serpent's interpretation:** "Imagine that you are the old king and have a son." The son is the one the narrator "just spoke of": he should be crowned. The sorceress is "a motherly woman whose son you should be, since you are a child renewing himself in you." "Sufficient manhood, and beyond that fullness of childhood. Which is why you need the mother." "A creator needs the mother, since you are not a woman." "To create means: mother and child." The narrator resists being a child ("humiliating and shattering"); the serpent: "A salutary antidote against power! Don't resist being a child, otherwise you resist your son." [EDITOR n.345-346: Black Book 4 has "ambition" and "work" in place of "power" and "son."] "The son stands higher. You are smaller and weaker than the son." "Do not ask after the morrow, sufficient unto you is the day... the son grows out of himself."
- **The narrator's acceptance:** "The myth commences, the one that need only be lived, not sung, the one that sings itself. I subject myself to the son, the one engendered by sorcery, the unnaturally born, the son of the frogs." "No man has produced him, and no woman has given birth to him." The absurd entered the age-old mother; the son "sprang up and was put to death. He rose again, was produced anew in the way of sorcery, and grew more swiftly than before. I gave him the crown that unites the separated." "No man binds Above and Below together. But he who did not grow like a man, and yet has the form of a man, is capable of binding them." "My power is paralyzed, but I survive in my son. I am solitary; the people rejoice at him."
- **Inversion of values** [p.360]: "I loved the beauty of the beautiful... But now I must love the beauty of the ugly, the spirit of the foolish, and the strength of the weak... Where does that leave mockery, contempt, and hatred?" The son's mockery is bloody and his hatred "a singing fire"; "He broke me in two, he cut me up. He yokes the separated... My love remained with me."
- **The son's demand:** the narrator withdraws to his gardens and rocks by the water with his serpent; the son emerges from the water "great and powerful, the crown on his head, with a swirling lion's mane, shimmering serpent skin covering his body" [n.347: 19 April 1914].

### {8} The ascent of the God; the narrator left alone [pp.360-361]
Date: **19 April 1914** (n.347); the dialogue is with the soul in Black Book 5 (n.348).
- The son: "I come to you and demand your life... I rise again, I had become flesh, now I return to eternal glitter and shimmer, to the eternal embers of the sun, and leave you your earthliness. You will remain with men. ... Your work belongs to the earth." Asked where his country is: "In the light, in the egg, in the sun, in what is innermost and compressed, in the eternal longing embers. So rises the sun in your heart and streams out into the cold world." "You ought to live in darkest solitude, men, not Gods, should illumine your darkness." The narrator kneels in tears; the son: "Also a woman, also a mother, pregnant. Giving birth awaits you... You have the child." [pp.360] "I feel the torment and the fear and the desolation of pregnant woman."
- **He leaves** [pp.360]: "The pregnant woman belongs to fate. Release me, I rise into the eternal realm." "I will be present and not present. You will hear and not hear me. I will be and not be." "No one besides you has your God. He is always with you, yet you see him in others, and thus he is never with you... you alone have him. Thus you are alone among men, in the crowd and yet alone. Solitude in multitude, ponder this." "Why are you mourning the sun of today?" Night is mother of the day. He ascends "like the bird that rises up into the skies of morning... the last cord tears away, my wings bear me up. I dive up into the sea of light." The narrator: "I am lamed. Has the God not left my sight?"
- **After the ascent** [pp.360-361]: "Where is the God? What has happened? How empty, how utterly empty! Should I proclaim to men how you vanished? Should I preach the gospel of godforsaken solitude? Should we all go into the desert and strew ashes on our heads, since the God has left us?" "I believe and accept that the God is something different from me." The God swings high with jubilant joy; he remains "in the night of pain. No longer with the God, but alone with myself." He tells the bronze doors "I opened to the flood of devastation and murder brooding over the peoples, opened so as to midwife the God" to shut, and mountains and seas to bury them (n.351: this paragraph added in the Draft).
- **Last passage of the Draft text** [p.361]: "I came to myself, a giddy and pitiful figure. My I! I didn't want this fellow as my companion... I'd prefer a bad woman or a wayward hound, but one's own I, this horrifies me." [n.353: the remainder added in the Draft.] "An opus is needed, that one can squander decades on... I must catch up with a piece of the Middle Ages, within myself. We have only finished the Middle Ages of others. I must begin early, in that period when [...] and womb of life, therefore I shall purify you. The touchstone is being alone with oneself. This is the way." [EDITOR n.354-355: Jung's 1930 statement on regression into the Middle Ages as a historical regression into the collective unconscious's past; the Handwritten Draft ends at this point with "Finis" in a box (p.1205).]

# SCRUTINIES (opening) [pp.362-364]

- **[p.362]** Part-title page: "Scrutinies" (viewed; text-free apart from the title). **[p.363]** blank.
- **[p.364]** Section **{1}** begins; date **19 April 1914** (n.1, and Draft editor note). Footnotes: n.2, "All beginnings are difficult" is a Talmudic proverb; n.3 "To the greater glory of God," the Jesuit motto; n.4, back-reference to note 91 (p.348 in the printed edition; the death of the old God); n.5, "References to this God in the following pages are not in Black Book 5"; n.6, 20 April 1914, the day Jung resigned as president of the International Psychoanalytical Association (the editor cites the Freud/Jung letters).

**Content of the opening section (summary)**
- The narrator resists accepting "this hollow nothing that I am": "What is my I? I always presuppose my I. Now it stands before me, I before my I." They are alone together and "our being together threatens to become unbearably boring": he will "educate" the I, starting with its main flaw, "no correct self-esteem."
- **A harsh self-flagellation:** he threatens and then applies a whip ("Take that, and that. What does it taste of? Of blood, presumably? Of the Middle Ages in majorem Dei gloriam?"). Or perhaps love: "Press you tenderly to myself?" He sees the I yawn. He will not let it speak, "otherwise... you will claim that you are my soul." His **soul is "with the fire worm, with the son of the frog who has flown to the heavens above"**; the I is his "bare, empty nothing."
- He lists the I's flaws: laughably sensitive, self-righteous, unruly, mistrustful, pessimistic, cowardly, dishonest with itself, venomous, vengeful, childishly proud, craving power and esteem, ambitious, thirsty for fame; playacting and pomposity. He says it is a horror to live with this I; he promises to "tighten the vise," "slowly pull off your skin," "stitch a cloth of new skin onto you," "pull out your tongue... pin all your unjust and depraved words one by one to your body with needles so that you can feel how evil words stab." If it takes pleasure in the torment he will "increase this pleasure until you vomit with joy." "I want to get along with you. I must, damn you, you are my I, which I must carry around with me to the grave." "I am damned to haul you through a purgatory so that you too will become somewhat acceptable."
- **The God question:** "You call on God for help? The dear old God has died, and it is good that way; otherwise he would have had pity on your repentant sinfulness and spared me the execution by granting mercy." Neither "a God of love nor a loving God has yet arisen, but instead a worm of fire crawled up, a magnificent frightful entity that lets fire rain on the earth, producing lamentations." "Your God has become a fiery worm with a flat skull who crawls red-hot on the earth." He tells the I that it is "inferior... scum that disgusts me," places it in a corner. "Your progress since the early Middle Ages appears to be minuscule."
- **Section {6}** [p.364, date 20 April 1914 per n.6] begins: "Did you feel dejected today; inferior, debased? Shall I tell you why?" The page ends here; the section continues in the next part.
