## Overview of this range

This part opens a book that Jung kept private for most of his life. The first two-thirds of the range is the editor's Introduction, a scholarly account (by Sonu Shamdasani) of how Jung came to write *Liber Novus* ("New Book," popularly "The Red Book"). It places the work in the early twentieth-century moment when psychology, literature and art overlapped, narrates Jung's life from childhood to 1913, and then follows the experiment that began in 1913: Jung's visions of a flood of blood and of catastrophic cold, his decision to treat his fantasies as an experiment (what he later called "active imagination"), his recording of them in the "Black Books," the composition of the Draft and the calligraphic red-leather volume, the paintings, the "Seven Sermons to the Dead," the mandalas, the figures of Philemon and the soul (anima), his reflections in contemporary essays (the transcendent function, individuation, persona, anima, self), the debates over publishing, the later turn to alchemy and the comparative study of individuation, and the book's eventual release in 2000. Two short prefaces follow (the translators' on language and the editor's on manuscripts and layout). The range then enters the text proper: Liber Primus begins with scriptural epigraphs, a prophetic prologue contrasting "the spirit of this time" with "the spirit of the depths," the account of the October 1913 flood vision, and the first two chapters in which Jung calls to his soul and begins to listen to her.

# PART A. EDITOR'S INTRODUCTION (Sonu Shamdasani) [pp.224-252]

*Everything in Part A is the editor's account and argument, not Jung's own text, except where Jung is quoted (marked as such). The editor says he draws at times directly on his earlier book on the formation of Jung's psychology; he uses "Liber Novus" as the title throughout because there are indications that is its real title, though Jung also called it "The Red Book." [p.224 and note 1]*

## Opening [p.224]

- Jung is described as a central figure in modern Western thought, with a large international profession working under his name, widely disseminated in arts, humanities, film and popular culture, and widely regarded as one of the instigators of the New Age movement.
- It is "startling" that the book at the center of his work, on which he labored for over sixteen years, is only now being published.
- Jung nominated the book to contain the nucleus of his later works; it is recognized as the key to their genesis, yet apart from "a few tantalizing glimpses" it was unavailable for study.

## The Cultural Moment [p.225]

- The first decades of the twentieth century were a time of experiment in literature, psychology and the visual arts. Writers tried to throw off representational conventions to depict the whole range of inner experience (dreams, visions, fantasies); examples named: surrealist automatic writing, Gustav Meyrink's gothic fantasies.
- Psychologists were exploring the same terrain. Alfred Binet and Charles Richet wrote dramatic and fictional works, often under assumed names; Gustav Fechner (psychophysics) wrote on the soul life of plants and of the earth as a blue angel; André Breton and Philippe Soupault read psychical researchers (Frederic Myers, Théodore Flournoy, Pierre Janet); W. B. Yeats used spiritualistic automatic writing to compose *A Vision* (Jung owned a copy). Artists and writers collaborated on new text-and-image forms. Boundaries between literature, art and psychology "had not yet been set."
- Hugo Ball (Berlin, 1913), quoted: life is shackled; economic fatalism; church a "redemption factory"; the burning question is whether any force can end this state of affairs. [p.225]
- Against this background Jung undertook "an extended process of self-experimentation," producing "a work of psychology in a literary form." The editor says we now stand on the other side of a divide between psychology and literature, and the work could only have emerged before that divide was firm.

## Who was C. G. Jung? (childhood to student years) [pp.225-226]

- Born 1875 in Kesswil on Lake Constance; family moved to Laufen by the Rhine Falls; eldest child, one sister; father a pastor in the Swiss Reformed Church. His late memoir "From the Earliest Experiences of My Life" (heavily edited into *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*) focuses on childhood dreams and visions and "can be viewed as an introduction to Liber Novus."
- **First dream (underground):** In a meadow he finds a stone-lined hole and stairs; below, a chamber with a golden throne bearing what looks like a tree trunk of skin and flesh with an eye on top. His mother's voice says it is "the man-eater." He is unsure whether this means it devours children or is Christ. Later he understood the figure as a phallus, then a ritual phallus in an underground temple, and came to see the dream as an initiation "in the secrets of the earth." [p.225]
- Childhood hallucinations; as a boy he could evoke images voluntarily (in a 1935 seminar he recalled gazing at his grandmother's portrait until he "saw" his grandfather descend the stairs). [p.225]
- **Basel cathedral vision (age twelve):** crossing the Münsterplatz, admiring the cathedral's new roof tiles in sunshine, he felt a "terrible, sinful thought" approach and suffered days of anguish; he decided God wanted him to think it, and saw God on his throne unleash an enormous turd on the cathedral, shattering the roof. He felt bliss and relief, and an experience of "the direct living God, who stands omnipotent and free above the Bible and Church." He felt alone before God with responsibility, and realized his father lacked such direct experience. First Communion then brought nothing: "an absence of God and no religion." [p.225]
- He read voraciously, struck by Goethe's *Faust* (Mephistopheles taken seriously) and Schopenhauer (acknowledging evil and suffering). [p.226]
- **Two personalities, "No. 1" and "No. 2":** Jung felt he lived in two centuries and nostalgic for the eighteenth. No. 1 was the Basel schoolboy who read novels; No. 2 pursued religious reflection in solitude, in communion with nature and cosmos, inhabiting "God's world," connected to history, especially the Middle Ages. When No. 2 entered it felt as if a long-dead yet ever-present spirit entered the room. This interplay ran through his life: part of us lives in the present and part is connected to the centuries. The conflict intensified at career time (No. 1 wanted science; No. 2 humanities). [p.226]
- **Career dreams:** (1) digging a burial mound by the Rhine and finding prehistoric animal bones, which awakened desire to learn about nature; (2) a wood with watercourses, a circular pool, and a large radiolarian in it. He settled for science (medicine). (3) A fog dream: protecting a small light against the wind, a huge black figure threateningly close; he woke realizing it was his own shadow cast by the light. He took the dream as meaning to go forward with No. 1 and not look back at No. 2. [p.226]
- University years: wide extracurricular reading (Nietzsche, Schopenhauer, Swedenborg, spiritualism); *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* impressed him, and he feared his No. 2 was as morbid as Zarathustra; Zofingia debating society lectures. Footnote summarizes Swedenborg (1688-1772: Swedish scientist and mystic; religious crisis 1743, vision of Christ 1745; two levels of meaning in the Bible linked by "correspondences"; a "new church"; spiritual regeneration). [p.226 and note 11]

## Spiritualism, Flournoy and Jung's early career [pp.226-227]

- Modern spiritualism spread across Europe and America in the later nineteenth century, normalizing trance, trance speech, glossolalia, automatic writing and crystal vision; it drew in scientists (Crookes, Zöllner, Wallace) and psychologists (Freud, Ferenczi, Bleuler, James, Myers, Janet, Bergson, Stanley Hall, Schrenck-Notzing, Moll, Dessoir, Richet, Flournoy). [p.226]
- In 1896 Jung and fellow students held a long series of seances with his cousin Helene Preiswerk, who in trance became different personalities, including dead relatives, told stories of past incarnations and expounded a mystical cosmology represented in a mandala; the sittings ended when she was caught faking apparitions. [p.226]
- In 1899, reading Krafft-Ebing's psychiatry textbook, Jung saw his vocation in psychiatry, fusing his two personalities; he took a post at the Burghölzli (Zurich university clinic under Eugen Bleuler) at the end of 1900. [p.226]
- His dissertation analyzed the psychogenesis of spiritualist phenomena through his seances with Helene, closely modeled on Flournoy's *From India to the Planet Mars* (a medium called "Helene Smith"). The "critical shift" (Flournoy, Myers, James): whatever the truth of spiritualist claims, such experiences open insight into the subliminal, so mediums became subjects of the new psychology, and their methods (automatic writing, trance speech, crystal vision) became research tools. Janet and Morton Prince used automatic writing and crystal gazing to reveal hidden memories and subpersonalities and to hold dialogues with them, aiming to reintegrate personality. [pp.226-227]
- Marriage to Emma Rauschenbach (engaged 1902; five children). His last diary entry (May 1902) mourns the loss of solitude. [p.227]
- Art: as a youth he loved Holbein, Böcklin and Dutch painters; for about a year near the end of his studies he painted, in a representational landscape style with fine technical skill; in Paris 1902-03 (studying with Janet) and London (January 1903) he visited museums (Louvre; Egyptian, Aztec and Inca collections at the British Museum). [p.227]
- Back at the Burghölzli he did word-association experiments with Franz Riklin (methods of Galton, Wundt, Kraepelin, Aschaffenburg); the experiments failed as a diagnostic tool but revealed disturbed reactions caused by emotionally charged "complexes," yielding a general psychology of complexes. He then applied complexes to dementia praecox (schizophrenia), arguing delusions are intelligible and that insanity lies at the end of a spectrum with sanity ("If we feel our way into the human secrets of the sick person, the madness also reveals its system" [p.227]).
- He grew disenchanted with experimental and statistical methods and moved to the clinical encounter as research method. Bleuler introduced psychoanalysis about 1904; Jung began corresponding with Freud in 1906. The editor insists Jung's roots were Bleuler, Janet and Flournoy, quoting Jung: "I in no way exclusively stem from Freud." The "Freudocentric legend" has "completely mislocated" Jung's work. [p.227]
- Jung became a principal architect of the psychoanalytic movement (yearbook editor 1908; honorary degree at Clark University 1909; first president of the international association 1910): a period of intense institutional and political activity, "riven by dissent." [pp.227-228]

## The Intoxication of Mythology [p.228]

- 1908: land at Küsnacht on Lake Zurich, house built, where he lived for life; 1909: resigned from the Burghölzli to devote himself to practice and research; shift to mythology, folklore, religion; a vast private library. This culminated in *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido* (1911 and 1912).
- He found the mythological work "exciting and intoxicating." A 1925 recollection: he felt he was "living in an insane asylum of my own making," analyzing centaurs, nymphs, satyrs and gods as if they were patients, reading a Greek or a "Negro" myth as if a lunatic were telling him his anamnesis. [p.228] *[note: the quoted wording of this 1925 recollection is Jung's as reported; the term used is retained as quoted.]*
- **Method and claims of that book:** two kinds of thinking (following William James), "directed thinking" (verbal, logical, science) versus "fantasy thinking" (passive, associative, imagistic, mythology); the ancients lacked directed thinking; fantasy thinking operates when directed thinking stops. He equated the prehistoric, the "primitive" and the child. He proposed a phylogenetic layer of the unconscious, present in everyone, made of mythological images; myths as symbols of the libido depicting its typical movements; the "comparative method" later called "amplification"; "typical myths" or "primordial images" (*Urbilder*, after Burckhardt); the hero myth as the life of the individual freeing himself from the mother; incest motif as an attempt to return to the mother to be reborn. Later he heralded this as the discovery of the collective unconscious (the term came later). [p.228]
- Alphonse Maeder's 1912 articles: dreams have a balancing or compensatory function and prepare the future, not only wish fulfillment. Jung adopted this. [p.228]
- **Mythlessness:** In a 1952 preface Jung wrote that he was thirty-six, at the beginning of the second half of life when a *metanoia* often occurs; he suffered the loss of Freud and relied on his wife's support. Having finished the book he realized the meaning of living without a myth, which leaves one "uprooted." He asked himself "what is the myth you are living?", found no answer, and took on getting to know "my" myth as "the task of tasks," since one cannot treat patients without knowing one's own "personal equation." So the self-experiment was partly a direct response to a theoretical question. [p.228]

## "My Most Difficult Experiment" [pp.229-233]

### The dreams of 1912-13 [p.229]
- **Dream of the knight (1912):** a southern town on a rising street, noon, bright sun. An old Austrian customs guard passes lost in thought; someone says he is "one who cannot die," dead thirty to forty years but not yet decomposed. A knight in yellowish armor with a red Maltese cross on his back appears; he has existed since the twelfth century and walks the same route daily between twelve and one. Nobody marvels but Jung. Jung thought of Freud (the old Austrian) and himself (the knight). A voice inside says "It is all empty and disgusting." He found it oppressive; Freud could not interpret it. In 1925 Jung said the knight/Crusader is an archetypal Christian figure living from the twelfth century, "not wholly dead." [p.229 and note 35]
- **Dream of the white bird (shortly after Christmas 1912):** Jung sits with his children at a round table of dark green stone in a castle apartment with an open columned hall. A white gull or dove lands on the table; it turns into a small blond girl of eight who plays with the children, then turns back into the bird, saying: "Only in the first hour of the night can I become human, while the male dove is busy with the twelve dead." The bird flies away. [p.229]
- In Black Book 2 Jung noted this dream made him decide to start a relationship with a woman he had met three years earlier (Toni Wolff). In 1925 he said it began a conviction that the unconscious "did not consist of inert material only" but was "something living down there." He thought of the Emerald Tablet, the twelve apostles, the zodiac, but "could make nothing out of the dream except that there was a tremendous animation of the unconscious"; he knew no technique to get at it and could only wait and watch. Footnote: much later he read the bird as the anima, "elflike, i.e., only partially human" (1951). [p.229 and note 39]
- Analyzing childhood memories solved nothing; he realized he had to recover childhood's emotional tone and resumed building houses and structures as he had liked as a child. [p.229]

### Typology and the vision of the flood [p.229-230]
- At the Munich congress (September 1913) he spoke on psychological types: two basic libido movements, extraversion and introversion, and a type for each; Freud's and Adler's psychologies each took their own type as universal. [p.229]
- **October 1913 train vision:** on a train to Schaffhausen he had a waking vision of Europe devastated by a catastrophic flood; it repeated two weeks later on the same journey. In 1925 he said: "I could be taken as Switzerland fenced in by mountains and the submergence of the world could be the debris of my former relationships"; and "If this means anything, it means that I am hopelessly off." He feared madness; he thought it indicated a revolution but could not imagine one, so concluded he was "menaced with a psychosis." [p.229]
- **Winter vision:** standing at a window at night looking north he saw a blood-red glow stretching east to west across the northern horizon; asked what he thought of world events, he said he saw "blood, rivers of blood." [pp.229-230]
- The editor sets this in a period of widespread apocalyptic imagery (Kandinsky 1912; Meidner's 1912-14 "apocalyptic landscapes"; the medium Leonora Piper's 1899 prediction of a terrible war; Conan Doyle in 1918 saw it as prophetic). "Prophecy was in the air." [p.230]
- In *Liber Novus* the inner voice said what the fantasy depicted would become completely real. Initially Jung read it subjectively and prospectively (his own world's destruction), and his reaction was "to undertake a psychological investigation of himself." Self-experimentation and introspection were established tools in medicine and psychology of the time. [p.230]

### Beginning the experiment [pp.230-232]
- Jung realized *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido* "could be taken as myself" and that analyzing it leads into analysis of his own unconscious processes: he had projected his material onto a stranger (Miss Frank Miller). Previously he had been an "active thinker," averse to fantasy ("a sort of incestuous intercourse"); now allowing fantasy felt "as if a man came into his workshop and found all the tools flying about doing things independently of his will." He turned to analyzing and carefully noting his fantasies, and realized he was studying the myth-creating function of the mind. [p.230]
- He picked up the brown notebook set aside in 1902 and began writing; his inner states appear as metaphors (a desert with an unbearably hot sun = consciousness). He realized he could write reflections in sequence: "writing autobiographical material, but not as an autobiography." Later notebooks were black: the **Black Books**. [p.230 and note 52]
- **Dialogue tradition:** the editor notes the dialogical genre from Plato on, and quotes the opening of Augustine's *Soliloquies* (387 CE; dialogue with "Reason"; "who spoke? myself or someone else, inside or outside me?"). [p.230]
- **The voice that said "That is art" (1925 account):** While writing, Jung asked himself what he was doing ("certainly not science"); a voice said "That is art." It startled him; he reasoned his unconscious might be forming a personality that insisted on expression; he knew with certainty the voice was a woman's. He argued back that it was not art and felt strong resistance. He named this voice "the soul in the primitive sense," the **anima** (Latin for soul), "part of myself with a different viewpoint." "I was in analysis with a ghost and a woman." Retrospectively he identified it with the voice of a Dutch patient (known 1912-18) who held that the unconscious is art, whereas Jung held it was nature. The editor argues she was Maria Moltzer and the psychiatrist she had persuaded that he was a misunderstood artist was Franz Riklin, who turned from analysis to painting (a student of Augusto Giacometti from 1913). [p.230]
- The first November 1913 entries show Jung's "return to his soul"; he recounted the dreams that chose his career and the recent ones that led him back. As he recalled in 1925, the first writing period ended in November; he thought more introspection was needed and devised "such a boring method by fantasizing that I was digging a hole" and accepting the fantasy as real. First such experiment: **December 12, 1913** (the vision of the descent in Liber Primus, ch. 5). [pp.230-231]
- **Precedents for the method (the editor's list):** Jung's own experience with mediums in trance; St. Ignatius of Loyola's spiritual exercises (fifth exercise: "see with the eyes of the imagination the length, breadth and depth of hell"); Swedenborg's "spirit writing" (spirits guiding his hand when he wrote; an entry of January 26, 1748, quoted); Herbert Silberer's 1909 hypnagogic self-experiments (symbolic images depicting prior thought); Ludwig Staudenmaier's *Magic as an Experimental Science* (1912; self-experiment since 1901 beginning with automatic writing; characters appeared, then dialogues without writing; acoustic and visual hallucinations; argued magic is explained by hallucination and the "underconsciousness" with personifications central). Jung marked Staudenmaier's book. "Jung's procedure closely resembled a number of historical and contemporary practices with which he was familiar." [p.231]
- **Procedure from December 1913:** deliberately evoking a fantasy in a waking state, then entering it as into a drama, "dramatized thinking in pictorial form." The mythological reading (epic, myth) shows in form and style. The Black Books record fantasies in dated entries with reflections on his state of mind; they are not diaries of events, and few dreams appear; they are "the records of an experiment." In December 1913 he called the first one "the book of my most difficult experiment." [p.231]
- His own account of the scientific question: to see what happens when consciousness is switched off, giving the background activity room to emerge, like taking mescaline. A dream-book entry of April 17, 1917 notes "frequent exercises in the emptying of consciousness." Beneath the threshold everything was animated; at times he seemed to hear something, or realized he was whispering to himself. [p.231]
- **The Philemon dream (undated, between November 1913 and July 1914):** a blue sky like a sea covered with flat brown clods of earth, breaking apart to reveal blue water that was the sky. From the right sails a winged old man with a bull's horns, holding four keys, one clutched as if to open a lock, with kingfisher wings. Jung painted it to fix it in memory; while painting he found a dead kingfisher (very rare near Zurich) in his garden by the lake. Philemon first appears in the Black Books January 27, 1914 (without kingfisher wings). To Jung he "represented superior insight," like a guru; they conversed in the garden; Philemon evolved out of the earlier figure of Elijah; he was a pagan with "an Egypto-Hellenic atmosphere with a Gnostic coloration" who taught "psychic objectivity, the reality of the psyche" and the distinction between himself and the object of his thought. [pp.231-232]

### 1914: resignations and the outbreak of war [p.232]
- April 20, 1914 Jung resigned as president of the International Psychoanalytical Association; April 30 he resigned as lecturer at the University of Zurich (feeling exposed and that teaching students was unfair). June-July 1914: the thrice-repeated dream of a foreign land, a fast ship home, and the descent of an icy cold. July 10: the Zurich Psychoanalytical Society voted 15 to 1 to leave the international association (reason given: Freud's orthodoxy impeded free research); renamed the Association for Analytical Psychology.
- He maintained a heavy practice (one to nine consultations a day; average five to seven), military service each year (16 days 1913; 14 days 1914; 67 days 1915; 34 days 1916; 117 days 1917), and family duties by day, with his self-explorations in the evening. Family and profession "always remained a joyful reality and a guarantee that I was normal and really existed." The association's minutes and his letters show no sign of the fantasies. [p.232]
- July 24, 1914 (London) talk "On Psychological Understanding": contrasts Freud's analytic-reductive, causal method with the "constructive method" of the Zurich school: reductive explanation deals with half the picture (like understanding Goethe's *Faust* or a Gothic cathedral by its mineralogy); living meaning "only lives when we experience it in and through ourselves"; the constructive standpoint asks how a bridge can be built from this present psyche into its own future. The editor reads this as Jung's rationale for not analyzing his own fantasies causally, and links it to Swedenborg's symbolic hermeneutics. [p.232]
- July 28, 1914 (Aberdeen) talk on the unconscious in psychopathology: in neurosis and psychosis the unconscious compensates a one-sided conscious attitude; if the unbalanced person resists, the opposites polarize; the corrective impulses should begin healing but break through in unacceptable form. [p.232]
- June 28 assassination of Franz Ferdinand; August 1 war. In 1925 Jung said he felt "an over-compensated psychosis" until August 1, 1914. To Mircea Eliade he later recounted preparing the Aberdeen lecture on schizophrenia, thinking "I'll be speaking of myself! Very likely I'll go mad after reading out this paper," then learning of the war on the day after the lecture: "Finally I understood. And when I disembarked in Holland on the next day, nobody was happier than I." He concluded his dreams and visions "came to me from the subsoil of the collective unconscious," and that his task was "to deepen and validate this discovery." [pp.232-233]
- Jung now took the fantasy to depict not what would happen to him but to Europe: a precognition of a collective event, later called a "big" dream. He then tested whether other fantasies had such correspondence, the main subject matter of *Liber Novus*. In Scrutinies he wrote that the war enabled him to understand what he had experienced and gave him the courage to write the earlier parts. The editor: had war not been declared, *Liber Novus* "would in all likelihood not have been compiled." In 1955/56 Jung said the involvement looks like psychosis because the patient is integrating the fantasy-material to which the insane person falls victim because he cannot integrate it. [p.233]
- **List of about twelve fantasies Jung may have regarded as precognitive** (editor's list, with dates): October 1913 repeated flood vision with voice saying it will become real (1-2); autumn 1913 sea of blood over northern lands (3); December 12 and 15, 1913 image of a dead hero and the slaying of Siegfried in a dream (4-5); December 25, 1913 giant's foot stepping on a city, images of murder and cruelty (6); January 2, 1914 sea of blood and procession of dead multitudes (7); January 22, 1914 his soul asks whether he will accept war and destruction, shows images of weapons, human remains, sunken ships, destroyed states (8); May 21, 1914 a voice says the sacrificed fall left and right (9); June-July 1914 the thrice-repeated cold dream (10-12). [p.233]

## Liber Novus: composition, structure, models [pp.233-234]

- **The Draft (from summer 1914):** Jung faithfully transcribed most fantasies from the Black Books, adding to each a section explaining its significance with "lyrical elaboration." The fantasies were reproduced with only minor editing and division into chapters; the sequence in *Liber Novus* nearly always exactly matches the Black Books; "on the next night" is always accurate. Jung kept "fidelity to the event"; this was not fiction. The draft begins with address to "My friends." Difference from the Black Books: the latter were for personal use (records of an experiment); *Liber Novus* is addressed to a public. [p.233]
- November 1914: he closely studied *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* ("then suddenly the spirit seized me and carried me to a desert country in which I read Zarathustra"). It shaped the structure and style: a series of books of short chapters. But Zarathustra proclaims the death of God; *Liber Novus* "depicts the rebirth of God in the soul." Indications that he also read Dante's *Commedia*; *Liber Novus* depicts a descent into Hell, but without an established cosmology, it is an attempt to shape an individual cosmology. Philemon's role has analogies to Zarathustra in Nietzsche and Virgil in Dante. [p.233]
- In the Draft about 50 percent of the material comes directly from the Black Books; there are about thirty-five new sections of commentary, deriving general psychological principles and testing whether events in the fantasies were symbolic developments to occur in the world. In 1913 Jung had distinguished interpretation on the *objective* level (dream objects as real objects) from interpretation on the *subjective* level (every element concerns the dreamer); here he adds, in effect, a "collective" level. The second layer is "itself a hermeneutic experiment" and "Liber Novus does not require supplemental interpretation, for it contains its own interpretation." [pp.233-234]
- Jung added no scholarly references though unreferenced citations and allusions abound; he deliberately left scholarship aside, yet the work is a scholar's, composed in his library. [p.234]
- **Production:** Draft typed; one typescript hand-corrected (the Corrected Draft; an unknown reader annotated, e.g., "Awesome! Why cut?" and Jung kept the passage). The first section (untitled, effectively Liber Primus) was composed on parchment; Jung commissioned a folio volume of over 600 pages bound in red leather by Emil Stierli; the spine reads *Liber Novus*; he inserted the parchment pages, continuing with Liber Secundus. Organized like a medieval illuminated manuscript: calligraphic writing, a table of abbreviations, first book titled "The Way of What Is to Come" with citations from Isaiah and John, "presented as a prophetic work." He changed some chapter titles, added others, edited again; cuts mostly shortened the second layer. In this edition the second layer is marked so chronology is visible; reading the fantasies straight through, then the second layer, is suggested because the commentary sometimes refers forward. [p.234]
- **Paintings:** initially illustrating the text directly, later more symbolic; they are active imaginations in their own right; the combination of text and image recalls William Blake. A surviving pencil sketch shows careful composition. Jung's surviving paintings leap from representational landscapes (1902-03) to abstract and semifigurative work from 1915. [p.234]

## Art and the Zurich School [pp.234-235]

- Jung's library held few books on modern art; he owned an Odilon Redon catalogue and a study of Redon; echoes of Symbolism appear in the paintings. In October 1910 a bicycle tour of northern Italy with Hans Schmid (Ravenna frescoes and mosaics deeply impressed him; he returned in April 1914): strong colors, mosaic-like forms, two-dimensional figures without perspective. In 1913 in New York he probably saw the Armory Show; in 1925 he mentioned Duchamp's *Nude Descending a Staircase* and having studied Picasso's course. [p.234]
- Contacts between Jung's circle and Zurich Dada: Erika Schlegel (sister of Sophie Taeuber, librarian of the Psychological Club); Club members attended Dada events (Gallery Dada opening, March 29, 1917); Sophie Taeuber ran a dance class for Club members and designed costumes for a masked ball; her 1918 marionette play *King Deer* includes a parody of Freud and Jung's *Transformations*. By May 1917 relations had cooled; in 1918 Jung criticized Dada in a Swiss review. What separated Jung's pictorial work from Dada was "his overriding emphasis on meaning and signification." [pp.234-235]
- Art and painting were widely taken up in his circle: Maeder (monograph on Hodler; visions around 1916 published pseudonymously; Jung's reply: "What, you too?"); Hans Schmid wrote and painted fantasies "in something akin to *Liber Novus*"; Moltzer wanted more artists in the circle; J. B. Lang (analyzed by Riklin) began symbolic paintings; Moltzer had a book of pictures with writing she called her "Bible" and recommended Fanny Bowditch Katz do the same. Riklin exhibited in 1919 as a Swiss Expressionist in the "New Life" show with Arp, Taeuber, Picabia and Augusto Giacometti. [p.235]
- Erika Schlegel's 1921 note of Jung on Riklin: his smaller works had aesthetic value, his larger "simply dissolved"; he "vanished wholly in his art"; to analyze one must be "pointed and sharp-edged, like a knife"; "art and science were no more than the servants of the creative spirit, which is what must be served." About his own work: it was "a matter of making out whether it was really art. Fairy tales and pictures had a religious meaning at bottom." Riklin appears to have been a doppelgänger whose fate Jung wished to avoid. [p.235]
- Conclusion: the making of *Liber Novus* "was by no means a peculiar and idiosyncratic activity, nor the product of a psychosis," but reflects close intersections of psychological and artistic experiment of the time. [p.235]

## The Collective Experiment [pp.235-236]

- 1915 correspondence with Hans Schmid on types shows his theories were partly conventional theorizing, not solely from active imaginations. March 1915 to Smith Ely Jelliffe: working "about the two types of psychology and about the synthesis of unconscious tendencies." [p.235]
- During self-exploration he suffered great fear, at times holding the table to keep together; he used yoga practices to eliminate the emotions until calm enough to resume work with the unconscious. Toni Wolff was drawn into a similar stream of images but was disoriented; his wife could not help; that he endured "was a case of brute force." [pp.235-236]
- **The Psychological Club** was founded early 1916 through a gift of 360,000 Swiss francs from Edith Rockefeller McCormick (in analysis with Jung from 1913); about sixty members; purpose: study the individual in relation to the group, a naturalistic setting beyond one-to-one analysis, and a place to learn social adaptation. A professional body of analysts still met as the Association for Analytical Psychology. [p.236]
- Jung encouraged patients to do active imagination, hold inner dialogues and paint their fantasies, taking his own experience as paradigmatic ("I drew all my empirical material from my patients, but the solution of the problem I drew from the inside"). Tina Keller recalled the "red book" open on an easel during analytic hours, Jung painting or having just finished a picture; the careful work showed that "psychic development is worth time and effort." Keller did active imaginations and painted. The editor: Jung's confrontation was "a collective one, in which he took his patients along with him"; his circle was "an avant-garde group engaged in a social experiment." [p.236]

## The Return of the Dead [pp.236-238]

- The theme of the returning dead was widespread in the war (Gance's film *J'accuse*) and spiritualism revived. Jung resumed the Black Books in 1915, after nearly a year's gap, having finished the Draft of Liber Primus and Secundus. [p.236]
- **Haunting in the house (1916),** as told to Cary de Angulo (later Baynes) in 1923: a son, restless in the night, drew a picture next day of a man angling with the Devil at left saying he came for the fisherman because he catches fish, and an angel at right saying "No you can't take this man, he is taking only bad fishes and none of the good ones." The same night two daughters thought they saw spooks. In *Memories*: around five on a Sunday afternoon the front doorbell rang frantically with no one there; the house was "crammed full of spirits"; they cried in chorus "We have come back from Jerusalem where we found not what we sought." "That is the beginning of the Septem Sermones." Over three evenings the text flowed out; "as soon as I took up the pen, the whole ghastly assemblage evaporated." [p.236]
- The dead had appeared in a fantasy of January 17, 1914 saying they were going to Jerusalem to pray at the holiest graves. The **Septem Sermones ad Mortuos** ("Seven Sermons to the Dead") culminate the fantasies of this period: "a psychological cosmology cast in the form of a gnostic creation myth"; a new God, son of the frogs, Abraxas, represents (Jung understood symbolically) the uniting of the Christian God with Satan, a transformation of the Western God-image; not elaborated publicly until *Answer to Job* (1952). [pp.236-237]
- Jung studied Gnosticism for *Transformations* and again in January and October 1915 on military service. He recopied the Sermones calligraphically with the inscription "The seven instructions of the dead. Written by Basilides in Alexandria, the city where the East touches the West," privately printed "translated from the Greek original into German." He regarded it as a gift for Edith Rockefeller McCormick on the founding of the Club, gave copies to friends, and wrote to Maeder that it fell "like a ripe fruit" at a time of stress and "kindled a light of hope and comfort." [p.237]
- **Mandalas:** January 16, 1916, he drew a mandala in the Black Books (first sketch of the "**Systema Munditotius**") and painted it; on its back he wrote in English: "This is the first mandala I constructed in the year 1916, wholly unconscious of what it meant." The Systema Munditotius is "a pictorial cosmology of the Sermones." Between June 11 and October 2, 1917 he was at Château d'Oex commanding British prisoners of war; August-September he drew twenty-seven mandalas in pencil in an army notebook, from August 20 nearly daily, as "a photograph of each day." A letter from Moltzer arguing the fantasies had artistic worth troubled him; the next day he drew a mandala from which a piece was broken off, leaving the symmetry. He then understood the mandala: "Formation, transformation, the eternal mind's eternal recreation" (Goethe, *Faust* II, Mephistopheles to Faust on the Mothers); "that is the self, the wholeness of the personality, ... which can bear no self deception." The mandalas were "cryptograms on the state of my self." The self "was like the monad which I am": the mandala is its image; all paths led to the center. [p.237]

## Scrutinies and the structure of *Liber Novus* [p.238]

- The Draft had fantasies from October 1913 to February 1914. In winter 1917 Jung wrote a new manuscript, **Scrutinies**, beginning where he had left off, transcribing fantasies from April 1913 [*sic* in text; later in the Editorial Note the Black Books run from April 1914] to June 1916 with commentary; he included the Sermones and now added **Philemon's commentaries** on each sermon, stressing the compensatory character of his teaching (he deliberately stressed conceptions the dead lacked). Scrutinies effectively forms Liber Tertius. Full sequence: *Liber Primus: The Way of What Is to Come; Liber Secundus: The Images of the Erring; Liber Tertius: Scrutinies.* [p.238]
- Jung kept transcribing the Draft into the calligraphic volume and adding paintings; Black Book entries grew intermittent. Scrutinies portrays his realization of the significance of the **self** in autumn 1917 and the vision of the reborn God culminating in Abraxas. He realized much of what was given earlier was actually given by Philemon: a prophetic wise old man in him to whom he was not identical, "a critical disidentification." [p.238]
- Jung to J. B. Lang (January 17, 1918): the work on the unconscious happens first for ourselves, patients profit indirectly; the danger is "the prophet's delusion," "the devil who says: Disdain all reason and science." To Lang (March 1918): experience the contents of the unconscious before forming opinions; "we have to grapple with the knowledge content of gnosis and neo-Platonism" as the materials for a theory of the unconscious spirit; he lacks "comparative modern material"; Zarathustra is "too strongly consciously formed," Meyrink "retouches aesthetically" and lacks "religious sincerity." [p.238]
- **Differentiating voices:** in the Black Books Jung's "I" speaks the Sermones; in Scrutinies Philemon does. The main dialogue partner in the Black Books is his soul; in some sections of *Liber Novus* this becomes the serpent and the bird. In a January 1916 conversation the soul says that when the Above and Below are not united she falls into three parts: a serpent, the human soul, and the bird or heavenly soul which visits the Gods. [p.238]

## The Content [pp.238-239]

- *Liber Novus* presents a series of active imaginations with Jung's attempt to understand them, with interlinked threads: understanding himself and integrating components of his personality; the structure of human personality in general; the individual's relation to present-day society and to the community of the dead; the psychological and historical effects of Christianity; the future religious development of the West. Other themes: self-knowledge; the nature of the soul; thinking and feeling and the psychological types; inner and outer masculinity and femininity; the uniting of opposites; solitude; the value of scholarship; the status of science; symbols; the meaning of the war; madness, divine madness and psychiatry; the Imitation of Christ today; the death of God; Nietzsche's historical significance; the relation of magic and reason. [p.238]
- **Overall theme:** how Jung regains his soul and overcomes spiritual alienation, ultimately by enabling the rebirth of a new image of God in his soul and developing a new worldview in the form of a psychological and theological cosmology; it presents the prototype of the **individuation process**. Narrative: at the beginning Jung refinds his soul, then a series of fantasy adventures forming a consecutive narrative. He realized he had served the **spirit of the time** (use and value) while another **spirit of the depths** led to the things of the soul; in his later memoir terms these correspond to personalities No. 1 and No. 2, so the period is a return to the values of No. 2. [pp.238-239]
- **Chapter format:** exposition of dramatic visual fantasies in which Jung meets figures in various settings, converses, faces unexpected happenings and shocking statements, then formulates their significance as general psychological conceptions and maxims. Their value lies in stemming from the **mythopoeic imagination** missing in the rational age. The task of individuation is to establish a dialogue with fantasy figures (contents of the collective unconscious), integrate them, and thereby reconcile the spirit of the time with the spirit of the depth; this was to be the leitmotif of his later scholarship. [p.239]

## "A New Spring of Life": the 1916-18 essays [pp.239-241]

- In 1916 Jung wrote essays and a short book translating themes of *Liber Novus* into contemporary psychological language, outlining the main components of his mature psychology. Prior (1911-14) he had formulated complexes, a phylogenetically acquired unconscious with mythic images, a nonsexual psychic energy, introversion and extraversion, compensatory and prospective dream function, and the synthetic and constructive approach; the new project was a *temporal* account of higher development, the **individuation process**. [p.239]
- **"The Structure of the Unconscious" (1916):** two layers: the *personal unconscious* (acquired in a lifetime, equally able to be conscious) and the *impersonal unconscious or collective psyche* (inherited). Annexing contents of the collective psyche and taking them as personal attributes produces extreme superiority and inferiority, called **godlikeness** (borrowed from Goethe and Adler). One must differentiate personal from collective psyche; a factor is the **persona** ("mask" or "role"), the segment of collective psyche mistaken for individual. Analyzing the persona releases a stream of fantasies ("all the treasures of mythological thinking and feeling are unlocked"); the difference from insanity is that this is intentional. Three options: regress and restore the persona (impossible); accept godlikeness; or a third way, hermeneutic treatment of creative fantasies, giving a synthesis of the individual with the collective psyche: individuation. A later undated revision introduced the **anima** as counterpart of persona ("how the subject is seen by the collective unconscious"). The editor links these to the opening of *Liber Novus* (Jung separates himself from role and achievements and reconnects with his soul) and to his own mythological fantasies as stemming from collective inheritance rather than personal idiosyncrasy. [p.239]
- **October 1916 talks to the Psychological Club:** "Adaptation" (to outer and inner conditions; adaptation to the inner leads to individuation, in conflict with conformity; this brings "tragic guilt" requiring expiation and a new "collective function": the individual must produce values that can substitute for his absence from society, and socially recognizable ones; individuation is for the few; the rest should reestablish collective conformity). "Individuation and collectivity" (a pair of opposites related by guilt; society demands imitation, which in analysis can reactivate one's own values, a reply to the charge of suggestion). The editor reads this as bearing on Jung's dilemma over whether the form of *Liber Novus* would be socially recognizable, and as separating him from Dada anarchism. [pp.239-240]
- **"The Transcendent Function" (written November 1916, published 1957):** the method he later called **active imagination** and its rationale; "an interim progress report" and a possible preface to *Liber Novus*. The new attitude gained from analysis becomes obsolete; unconscious material must supplement and correct; dreams are inferior expressions because energy tension is low in sleep, so one turns to spontaneous fantasies. Technique (quoted): "The training consists first of all in systematic exercises for eliminating critical attention, thus producing a vacuum in consciousness." Concentrate on a mood, become conscious of all fantasies and associations, give fantasy free play without leaving the initial affect; express concretely (draw, paint, sculpt); visual types expect an image; audio-verbal types hear inner words or another voice; rarer is automatic writing. Then two approaches, creative formulation and understanding, both needed to produce the **transcendent function** (union of conscious and unconscious contents). Some can write the "other" voice and answer from the standpoint of the I as in a dialogue between two people. A recently recovered dream book (dreams 1917-1925) shows that his active imaginations did not derive directly from dreams and the two streams were generally independent. [p.240]
- **The Psychology of the Unconscious Processes (1917; preface December 1916):** the war brought the chaotic unconscious to the fore; the psychology of the individual corresponds to that of the nation and cultural renewal comes only through the individual's change, which is "the intimate interconnection between individual and collective events that was at the center of *Liber Novus*." After the personal unconscious is integrated one meets mythological fantasies from the phylogenetic layer, "the collective, suprapersonal, absolute unconscious"; one must separate the I from the non-I, with "firm feet in his I-function" (fulfilling duty to life, a vitally living member of society). The contents are "dominants": "the ruling powers, the Gods, that is, images of dominating laws and principles." Footnote (1943 revision): the personal unconscious corresponds to the **shadow**. Consolidating these as psychological realities outside the individual psyche releases energy; this is what Jung had done in differentiating characters, the lesson of Elijah: psychic objectivity. [pp.240-241]
- The same text: the French Revolution's era of reason and skepticism repressed religion and irrationalism, leading to the war; so acknowledging the irrational as a psychological factor is a historical necessity, a central theme of *Liber Novus*. **Types:** extremes of a type lead through **enantiodromia** (reversal into the opposite) to the other function entering; the secondary functions lie in the unconscious; developing the contrary function leads to individuation and requires the transcendent function; the unconscious holds "the wisdom and experience of untold ages." This appears in the "Mysterium" section of *Liber Novus*. Concluding line (first edition only): "Our age is seeking a new spring of life. I found one and drank of it and the water tasted good." [pp.240-241]
- **1917 talk to the Society for Psychical Research on belief in spirits:** the collective unconscious becomes active in two situations: individual crisis (collapse of hopes) or great social, political, religious upheaval, when suppressed factors accumulate and intuitive people translate them into communicable ideas, which is redemptive; in the first case the unconscious may replace reality (pathological), in the second the person is disoriented but not pathological. The editor reads Jung's own case as the second kind; his 1913 fear lay in not realizing the distinction. [p.241]

## The Way to the Self [pp.241-243]

- 1918 "On the Unconscious": we stand between two worlds, the world of external perception and that of the unconscious. Against Schiller's view that art approximates the two, Jung says union of rational and irrational truth is found in the **symbol** ("it is the essence of the symbol to contain both"); the symbol-creating function is the most important function of the unconscious, present only when we are willing to recognize it. Jung thus continues to refuse to view his productions as art; the task is to translate his conceptions into a language compatible with the contemporary outlook. [p.241]
- 1918 seminars on typology to the Club; *Psychological Types* (1921); chapter 5 "The Type Problem in Poetry": resolving opposites by a uniting symbol, with analyses of Hinduism, Taoism, Meister Eckhart and Carl Spitteler. Method: rather than discuss the issue directly, he seeks historical analogies and comments on them. [pp.241-242]
- **The self (1921):** the I is only the center of the field of consciousness, "a complex among other complexes"; the self is the subject of totality, including the unconscious psyche, "an (ideal) greatness which embraces and includes the I"; in fantasy it appears as a superordinate or ideal personality (Faust to Goethe, Zarathustra to Nietzsche); he equates it with Brahman/Atman. The **soul** has qualities complementary to the persona; a man has a feminine soul (anima), a woman a masculine soul (animus). Four uses of the soul's images: artistic, philosophical speculation, quasi-religious (heresy, sects), or squandering in licentiousness; the "fifth way" would be psychological, so psychology must distinguish itself from art, philosophy and religion. [p.242]
- **Conversation with his soul, January 5, 1922 (Black Book 7), quoted at length:** Soul wakes him ("Now is no time to sleep ... The great work begins"). "To no longer be a Christian is easy. But what next? ... You remain silent and have nothing to say. But you should speak." Jung: "you are not thinking that I should publish what I have written? That would be a misfortune." Soul: "You should not break up a marriage, namely the marriage with me... I want to rule alone... because I serve you and your calling." Calling: "The new religion and its proclamation." Three days later the soul says the new religion "expresses itself only in the transformation of human relations" and "a religion does not consist only in knowledge, but at its visible level in a new ordering of human affairs"; Jung asks how knowledge becomes life; the soul: "It is not as rational as you are inclined to think. The way is symbolic." (Footnote: in 1923 Jung said individual relations form "the invisible Church.") [pp.242-243]

## Publication Deliberations [pp.243-246]

- From 1922 Jung discussed what to do with the book with Emma Jung, Toni Wolff, **Cary Baynes** (born Cary Fink, 1883; Vassar; married Jaime de Angulo 1910; left him 1921; in analysis with Jung; married Peter Baynes 1927, divorced 1931) and Wolfgang Stockmayer. Jung asked her to make a fresh transcription (1924-25, while he was in Africa) because he had added much. Her notes, written as unsent letters to Jung, are quoted: [p.243]
  - October 2, 1922: Jung said Meyrink's *White Dominican* used the same symbolism as his first vision and speaks of a "Red Book"; he was in doubt about form: Meyrink could use a novel, he could only command the scientific and philosophical method; Zarathustra form: he was "sick of that"; autobiography; dread of publishing "like selling your house." Cary Baynes: the book and he "stood for a constellation of the Universe," taking it as purely personal was to identify with it; Goethe was caught in the same difficulty in *Faust* II; a *Dichtung und Wahrheit* ("Poetry and Truth") approach would let people choose. Jung noted much would "be counted as sheer lunacy"; material came "as runes"; he brings along "the whole business, matrix process and product," more difficult to handle. [pp.243-244]
  - January 1923: Jung asked her to copy it; "you could not find the appropriate form—as they were now ... they might come out of a madhouse"; he had worked on the principle of "voluntariness" (no corrections); some pictures were "absolutely infantile, but were intended so"; the various speaking figures (Elias, Father Philemon) are phases of "the master," the same who inspired Buddha, Mani, Christ and Mohammed; others identified with him, Jung refused, remaining the psychologist who understands the process. Her suggestion: the master must be seen as "a pillar of fire perpetually moving on and forever out of human grasp." [p.244]
  - January 1923 (another note): she suggested incorporating in the book an exposition of the forces that would destroy it ("take the wind out of their sails"). January 30: Jung said the Red Book "told of the battle between the world of reality and the world of the spirit"; he nearly was torn apart but kept his feet on the earth; the test of an idea is whether it makes an impression on reality. [pp.244]
  - Undated letter fragment by Cary Baynes: she is "thunderstruck" at the Red Book's value; it "re-envisage[s] and clarif[ies] the things that are today"; Jung's published books "are doctored up for the world... this [is] written out of his heart." May 29, 1924: Peter Baynes thought only someone who knew Jung could understand it; she thought it "the record of the passage of the universe through the soul of a man," like listening to the sea. [pp.244-245]
- Stockmayer (met 1907; first German interested in Jung's work; traveled with Jung; shared interest in Chinese philosophy, mysticism of India and Tantric yoga) wrote December 22, 1924 and April 30, 1925 of longing for the Red Book, having gone through "Scrutinies" ("the same impression as with the great wandering"), and imagining "facsimiles" and a journal of "Documents" from "the forge of the unconscious." Jung's manuscript "Commentaries" (Appendix B) may be connected. [p.245]
- Cary Baynes got through the first twenty-seven pages of Scrutinies only; then translation duties took her time. In the mid-1920s Jung re-edited the Draft on about 250 pages, modernizing language; "It is hard to see why he undertook this unless he was seriously considering publishing it." [pp.245-246]
- 1925 seminar on analytical psychology discussed important fantasies and their relation to *Psychological Types*; Peter Baynes's English *Septem Sermones* (private, 1925; Jung gave copies to some English-speaking students; to Henry Murray he wrote the ideas "are really quite wonderful things"; Barbara Hannah says he regretted publishing it and thought it should have been written only in the Red Book). [p.246]
- Manuscript "Commentaries" (mid-1920s) comments on Liber Primus chapters 9, 10, 11. Copies of the Draft were given to Cary Baynes, Peter Baynes, Aniela Jaffé, Wolfgang Stockmayer, Toni Wolff; others allowed to see it include Richard Hull, Tina Keller, James Kirsch, Ximena Roelli de Angulo (as a child), Kurt Wolff; probably also Medtner, Riklin Sr., Erika Schlegel, Hans Trüb, Marie-Louise von Franz. Peter Baynes's copy was damaged in a 1937 fire; Jung wrote in 1942 that he supposed 2 or 3 translations already existed. [p.246]

## The Transformation of Psychotherapy [pp.246-247]

- In 1912 Jung had regarded mythological fantasies as a sign of schizophrenia; his self-experiment revised this: what matters is not the content but the individual's attitude to it and whether the worldview can accommodate it. In the afterword he says the work would seem like madness to a superficial observer and could have become so. In Liber Secundus ch. 15 he critiques psychiatry's incapacity to distinguish religious experience or "divine madness" from psychopathology. [p.246]
- He reformulated psychotherapy: no longer only treating neurosis but fostering higher development through individuation. To test generality he collected patients' paintings (asking for copies so patients stay with originals). [p.246]
- **Christiana Morgan (1926), notes of Jung's advice:** use the retina first to objectify, then "just want to look in," hold the images and see how they change, "get into the picture yourself—become one of the actors"; "When I first began to do this I saw landscapes. Then I learned how to put myself into the landscape, and the figures would talk to me and I would answer them"; "I have written 1000 pages of material from the unconscious (Told the vision of a giant who turned into an egg)." Phantasies "rather thin... There isn't enough fire and heat in them... You must be your own conscious critical self in them." Jung's example: writing in his book he saw a man at his shoulder; a gold dot from his book flew into the man's eye; he asked if Jung would remove it; Jung refused until he said who he was; at last he promised to give the meaning of some hieroglyphs; Jung removed the dot and he vanished. He advised patients to put it all down "as beautifully as you can—in some beautifully bound book... Then... it will be your church—your cathedral—the silent places of your spirit"; "in that book is your soul." [pp.246-247]
- Letter to J. A. Gilbert (1929): he encourages patients to express their contents in writing, drawing or painting and "let my patients find their own symbolic expressions, their 'mythology.'" [p.247]

## Philemon's Sanctuary [pp.247-248]

- In the 1920s his interest shifted from transcribing to building his **tower at Bollingen** (land bought 1920): "Words and paper, however, did not seem real enough to me; something more was needed" — a confession in stone; "representation of individuation"; murals (including a portrait of Philemon) and carvings; a three-dimensional continuation, a "Liber Quartus," deliberately medieval with no modern amenities. Inscription: "Philemonis sacrum—Fausti poenitentia." Quote from the end of Liber Secundus: "I must catch up with a piece of the Middle Ages—within myself." 1929 letter to Richard Wilhelm: "Why are there no worldly cloisters for men, who should live outside the times!" [p.247]
- **Dream, December 23/24, 1923 (after his mother's death, January 9, 1923):** military march; excavations at a crossroads in a wood near Ossingen; a stone toad figure with a boy with a toad's head; a bust of a man with an anchor hammered into the region of his heart; a bust from about 1640; mummified corpses; a seventeenth-century barouche in which sits someone dead yet alive, who turns her head when addressed as "Miss" (a title of nobility). Jung's 1926 reading: "the death of the anima ('She does not know that she is dead')"; "Since the death of my mother, the A. has fallen silent." His confrontation with the anima had effectively reached closure. [pp.247-248]
- **Liverpool dream, January 2, 1927:** young Swiss and Jung at the docks of Liverpool on a dark rainy night; they climb to a plateau; a small circular lake in a central garden with an island; the men wonder how a Swiss can live in so sooty a city, but Jung sees on the island a magnolia tree with red flowers illuminated by an eternal sun and understands why. He painted a mandala based on the city map. Later commentary: it represented his situation; "I had had a vision of unearthly beauty, and that was why I was able to live at all"; "the center is the goal"; the self "is the principle and archetype of orientation and meaning." Jung added that he was the one Swiss; the "I" is not the self, but from there one can see the divine miracle. He stopped painting mandalas. Individuation is not linear but a **circumambulation of the self**. [p.248]
- 1926 revision of *The Psychology of the Unconscious Processes*: first half of life = natural phase (establish oneself, income, family); second half = cultural phase (conserve previous values while recognizing opposites, develop neglected aspects of personality). Individuation as a general pattern of human development; Jung's crisis the template for the midlife transition (an influence on adult developmental psychology). [p.248]
- **The Relations between the I and the Unconscious (1928):** after the personal sphere comes the impersonal sphere; its fantasies converge on a goal and resemble initiation processes; active participation required ("the next image always starts off on the higher level that has been won"). Stages: assimilation of the personal unconscious, differentiation of the persona, overcoming godlikeness, then integration of the **anima** (for men; **animus** for women). The anima is projected when unconscious (first the mother, then women who arouse feeling); one objectifies her by inner dialogue (active imagination = a type of dramatized thinking), disidentifying from the thoughts and the assumption one produced them. Most essential: experiencing, not interpreting; treat fantasies literally while engaged in them, symbolically when interpreting (the editor: they should be read symbolically, and citing statements literally out of context would be "a serious misunderstanding"). Three effects: enlarged consciousness, diminished dominance of the unconscious, an altered personality. [pp.248-249]
- After the anima one meets the **mana personality** (hero, chief, magician, medicine man, saint, "friend of the Gods"; for women the Great Mother): identification with the **magician** is inevitable and must be given up; the mana belongs to the self. The editor: this corresponds to Jung's encounter with Philemon. The self "might as well be called 'God in us'"; goal reached when the I revolves around the self "very much as the earth revolves about the sun." [p.249]

## The Confrontation with the World [pp.249-250]

- Why Jung stopped (afterword, 1959): his 1930 acquaintance with alchemy; the beginning of the end came in 1928 when Richard Wilhelm sent the text of "The Secret of the Golden Flower," in which the book's contents "found their way into actuality." [p.249]
- 1928: he painted a golden-castle mandala (page 163 of the book), which struck him as somewhat Chinese; soon after, Wilhelm's text arrived: "an undreamed-of confirmation of my ideas about the mandala and the circumambulation of the center... the first event which broke through my isolation." May 1929 to Wilhelm: they are "two bridge pillars which carry the bridge between East and West." His commentary on the *Golden Flower* (1929) was a turning point: the first public discussion of the mandala; for the first time he anonymously showed three of his own *Liber Novus* paintings as European mandalas. The "European unconscious spirit" and Eastern eschatology became a theme of the 1930s (collaborations with Hauer, Zimmer). [p.249]
- The form of the work was crucial: rather than reveal his own experiment, Jung spoke indirectly via parallels (as begun in *Psychological Types* ch. 5): the "allegorical method," commenting on analogous developments in esoteric practices and above all medieval alchemy. [p.250]
- Soon after, Jung abruptly left off: the last full-page image was left unfinished and he stopped transcribing. When he reached this central point (Tao) his confrontation with the world began and he started to give many lectures; he saw these as compensation for the years of inner preoccupation. Footnote: a 1923 conversation noted that no matter how gifted, one has not fulfilled psychological duties without functioning in collectivity ("mixing" socially). [p.250 and note 236]

## The Comparative Study of the Individuation Process [pp.250-252]

- Jung had known alchemical texts from about 1910; Flournoy (1912 lectures) and Silberer (1914 book) gave psychological interpretations. Jung's two theses: (1) in meditating on texts and materials in their laboratories, alchemists were practicing a form of active imagination; (2) alchemical symbolism corresponds to the individuation process. In the 1930s he moved from the Black Books to his alchemy copy books, an encyclopedic indexed collection of excerpts that underlay his writings on alchemy. [p.250]
- The question he pursued: is something akin to individuation found in all cultures, and what are the common and differential elements? The editor: his later work can be considered "an extended amplification of the contents of *Liber Novus*," translated into a form acceptable to the contemporary outlook; some statements in the book are first formulations of later positions; much is absent from the *Collected Works* or appears only schematically or allegorically. "Each mutually explicates the other." [p.250]
- Jung's own remarks: all his work came from these imaginings; "gigantic blocks of stone were tumbling down upon [him]. One thunderstorm followed another"; amazed it hadn't broken him as it did others, such as Schreber. To Kurt Wolff (1957): "That was the primal stuff that compelled me to work on it... The first imaginings and dreams were like fiery, molten basalt, from which the stone crystallized, upon which I could work." It cost him "45 years" to bring it into the "vessel" of scientific work. Later works present "a skeletal outline." In retrospect, the Red Book was "an attempt to formulate things in terms of revelation"; he hoped it would free him but it did not; he had to return "to the human side and to science" and understand the ethical obligations; "he had paid with his life and his science." [pp.250-251]
- 1930 seminars on Christiana Morgan's visions (an indirect commentary on *Liber Novus*); 1932 Kundalini Yoga seminars begin a comparative study of esoteric practices (Ignatius's spiritual exercises, Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, Buddhist meditations, alchemy; ETH lectures); the key insight: they are all forms of active imagination aiming at personality transformation (individuation); "Jung's ETH lectures provide a comparative history of active imagination." 1934: case of Kristine Mann (mandalas); Jung: "I have naturally used this method on myself too"; he kept quiet for at least thirteen years about the method's results to avoid suggestion; mandalas appear in all times and places and in patients of non-students. [p.251]
- The editor suggests one reason Jung withheld *Liber Novus* was to convince himself and critics that patients' developments and mandalas were not due to suggestion. Jung noted in 1936 that he had verified symbols years later in texts unknown to him, though given his learning his own material would not have been a convincing example of images emerging without prior acquaintance. [p.251]
- *Liber Novus* also holds Jung's understanding of the historical transformations of Christianity and the historicity of symbols (taken up in the alchemy writings, the psychology of Christian dogma and *Answer to Job*). His prewar visions being prophetic fed his 1952 collaboration with physicist Wolfgang Pauli on **synchronicity**, an "acausal orderedness" behind "meaningful coincidences," with archetypal constellation relativizing time and space. [p.251]
- The relation of the book to the Collected Works is not point-by-point translation: *Liber Novus* and the Black Books are "a private opus that ran parallel to ... his public scholarly opus." After 1930 he elaborated the private opus in the tower, stone carvings and paintings; in therapy he enabled patients to recover meaning through self-experimentation and symbol creation. [p.251]

## The Publication of Liber Novus [pp.251-252]

- April 10, 1942 to Mary Mellon: wait on printing the Sermones, he had in mind to add material (probably Philemon's commentaries). 1944 heart attack. Lucy Heyer's 1952 biography project; Cary Baynes considered a biography based on *Liber Novus*; Jung terminated Heyer's project 1955; 1956 Kurt Wolff's project became *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*; Jung authorized Aniela Jaffé to cite *Liber Novus* and the Black Books but she omitted most. In 1957 Jung considered donating *Liber Novus* and Black Books to the University of Basel library with a restriction of 30, 50 or 80 years because "he hates the idea that anybody should read this material without knowing the relations to his life." [pp.251-252]
- October 12, 1957 Jung told Jaffé he never finished the Red Book. In spring 1959, after illness, he took it up again to finish the last unfinished image and resume transcription: "However, he still could not or would not complete it. He told me that it had to do with death." The calligraphic transcription breaks off mid-sentence; the afterword also breaks off mid-sentence. [p.252]
- Later events: Jaffé's 1971 Eranos lecture cited two passages; reproductions shown in a 1972 BBC documentary and a 1975 Zurich exhibition; nine paintings published in *C. G. Jung: Word and Image* (1977); 1975 William McGuire's request for a facsimile edition denied; 1984 five facsimile editions made for the five families of descent; 1992 the family began examining Jung's unpublished materials; the editor found a transcription and a partial transcription and gave them to the heirs in 1997 (another from Marie-Louise von Franz); the heirs decided in May 2000 to release the work. Footnote: Richard Hull's 1961 impression ("a walking asylum... only difference... his astounding capacity to stand off from the terrifying reality of his visions").
- The editor's close: the book is "the central book in his oeuvre"; the publication "marks a caesura" and gives "a unique window into how he recovered his soul and, in so doing, constituted a psychology"; the Introduction "does not end with a conclusion, but with the promise of a new beginning." [p.252]

**Cross-references within the book:** Jung's own account of the flood vision is in Liber Primus (below, Part B); the December 12, 1913 descent is Liber Primus ch. 5; Philemon's commentaries and "I" disidentification are in Scrutinies (later parts); the editor's Appendix A (sketches and mandalas) and Appendix B ("Commentaries") are in later parts; the afterword (1959) is near the end.

# PART B. TRANSLATORS' NOTE (Kyburz, Peck, Shamdasani) [pp.253-255]

*This is the translators' own statement of method; it is editor's apparatus, not Jung's text.*

- At the outset Jung experiences a **crisis of language**: the spirit of the depths challenges his use of language and of the spirit of the time, telling him his achieved language will no longer serve on the terrain of the soul; the language he will find "would compose a vast Commedia." Quoted: "Do you believe, man of this time, that laughter is lower than worship?" [p.253]
- Policy: let Jung remain "a man who was pulled loose from his moorings" but also caught in literary modernism; neither further modernize nor archaize. [p.253]
- **Three stylistic registers**, each with its own difficulties: (1) *descriptive*: faithfully reports the fantasies and inner dialogues; (2) *conceptual*; (3) *mantic and prophetic* (Romantic, dithyrambic). They rub against each other in each chapter without any one being affected by its partners; in Scrutinies (1917) the polyphony matures with voices commingling. The conceptual and mantic registers can be considered translations of the descriptive one, moving from literal to symbolic levels, "in a modern analogue to Dante's 'modi diversi.'" [p.253]
- The design grew from the experiment: Jung sets down an initial protocol layer of narrative encounter (usually with dialogue; avoids elevated tone) and then a "second layer" of lyrical elaboration and commentary (welcomes elevation; sermonic, mantic-prophetic). "There is no other text that was subjected to such careful and continual linguistic revision." [p.253]
- The work was a hybrid of text and painting in a medieval scribal format; the translators avoided three virtual models: Peter Baynes's Victorian-idiom *Septem Sermones* (1925), an R. F. C. Hull-style rationalized version, an elegant literary rendering (R. J. Hollingdale). They echo Luther's German (and Nietzsche's parody of it), so rely on the King James Bible for comparable tonality. They cite Rosenzweig/Buber on Luther's Bible as "the great space-maker" and Luther's maxim "The mud will cling to the wheel"; they do not smooth out Jung's modes or regularize punctuation. The era's "dialogical prose imagination" (Bakhtin); David Jones's phrase "The Break" for the First World War. [pp.253-254]
- By 1921 *Psychological Types* gave Jung his main themes in a scholarly idiom; pronoun shifts show shifting imagined readers (inner circle of friends to wider public). Paintings and fantasies entered his later work anonymously as "crypted intertexts"; e.g., "Case Z" in "The Psychological Aspects of the Kore" (1941; the translators' date) summarizes twelve episodes of encounters with his own soul as "a dream-series." [p.254]
- **Seven instances of language's destabilizing power in the book (translators' list, with quoted fragments):** (1) the soul in a whisper: "Words, words, do not make too many words... have you recognized your madness...?" (2) "There are hellish webs of words... Word, the paltriest and the mightiest... the word is an image of the God." (3) "if the word is a symbol, it means everything... the saving symbol, the word." (4) the dead woman: "give me the word"; it materializes in Jung's hand as HAP, the phallus. (5) "You possess the word that should not be allowed to remain concealed." (6) "What is my word? It is the stammering of a minor..." (7) "Philemon had intoxicated me and given me a language that was foreign to me... All of this faded when the God arose and only Philemon kept that language." [p.254]
- Jung later attributed the mantic, dithyrambic second layer before Scrutinies to Philemon; the translators read it as a dramatized version of Platonic divine madness; they caution against equating Logos and Eros with the conceptual and lyrical-mantic registers. The brief 1959 epilogue breaks off mid-sentence; Jung left the book to "stand on its own." The translators say a full discussion of their choices "would fill a volume as ample as this one." [pp.254-255]

# PART C. EDITORIAL NOTE (Sonu Shamdasani) [pp.256-257]

- *Liber Novus* is "an unfinished manuscript corpus"; no single version can be called final; the edition's rationale: [p.256]
- **Manuscript sequence for Liber Primus and Secundus:** Black Books 2-5 (November 1913 to April 1914); handwritten Draft (summer 1914-1915); typed Draft (c. 1915); Corrected Draft (one layer c. 1915, one layer mid-1920s); calligraphic volume (1915-1930, resumed 1959, left incomplete); Cary Baynes's transcription (1924-25); Yale manuscript (Liber Primus without the prologue); Copy-Edited Draft (c. late 1950s). **For Scrutinies:** Black Books 5-6 (April 1914-June 1916); calligraphic Septem Sermones (1916); printed Septem Sermones (1916); handwritten Draft (c. 1917); typed Draft (c. 1918); Cary Baynes's transcription (1925, 27 pages). [p.256]
- The edition uses a revised Baynes transcription plus a fresh transcription of the rest of the calligraphic volume with the typed Draft of Scrutinies; the last thirty pages come from the Draft. Variants in the "second layer" represent Jung's "attempt at an elaboration in terms of the revelation," so notes report significant changes; criterion for inclusion: "does this inclusion help the reader comprehend what is taking place?" [p.256]
- Subdivisions added in Liber Secundus ch. 21 ("The Magician") and Scrutinies are in scrolled brackets { }; fantasy dates come from the Black Books; the second layer is marked [2], reversion to the Black Book sequence [1]. Paragraphing follows Baynes's transcription (often cued by colored initials); initials in red or blue and enlarged fonts are rendered (red initials, italics). [p.256]
- **Why Scrutinies is placed as part of *Liber Novus*:** The Black Books begin November 1913; Liber Secundus closes with material from April 19, 1914 and Scrutinies commences with material from the same day; the Black Books run consecutively until July 21, 1914 and resume June 3, 1915 (hiatus: Jung wrote the Draft). At the end of Liber Secundus Jung's soul has ascended to Heaven following the reborn God; Jung thinks Philemon is a charlatan and turns to his "I"; Scrutinies continues directly with a confrontation with his "I." The ascent of the reborn God is referred to; his soul returns and explains her disappearance; Philemon reappears and instructs him on the right relation to soul, dead, Gods and daimons. Only in Scrutinies do some episodes of Liber Primus and Secundus become clear (for example, the Anabaptist Ezekiel and companions in Liber Secundus ch. 15 who say they go to Jerusalem's holy places because not at peace; in Scrutinies the dead return saying they found nothing there and Philemon appears and the Sermones begin). Scrutinies calls the earlier sections "the earlier part of this book" in two places; Jung's words quoted from there: "And then the War broke out. This opened my eyes about what I had experienced before..." and "Probably the greater part of what I have written in the earlier part of this book was given to me by Philemon." Cary Baynes (January 8, 1958) also treated Scrutinies as part of the Red Book; Jung (January 24) did not object to lending her notes to Kurt Wolff. [pp.256-257]
- Citation convention: ellipses = three periods; no emphases added. [p.257]

# PART D. TEXT: LIBER PRIMUS, "THE WAY OF WHAT IS TO COME" [pp.260-264]

*From here on, the text is Jung's translated work (narrative and symbolic content). Page references are PDF pages; the manuscript's own folio numbers (fol. i(r), etc.) appear in the margins. Footnotes are the editor's. Latin and German originals are on the facsimile pages and the Latin epigraphs are rendered in English in brackets in the edition.*

## Title page, epigraphs and colophon [p.260]

- Jung titled the first book **"The Way of What Is to Come"** (Latin: *Liber Primus*). Beneath it is a block of Latin epigraphs from the Vulgate, each introduced "Isaias dixit" or "Ioannes dixit" ("Isaiah said," "John said"), with English renderings: Isaiah 53:1-4 (the suffering servant: "he hath no form nor comeliness... a man of sorrows... Surely he hath borne our griefs"); Isaiah 9:6 ("For unto us a child is born... Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace"); John 1:14 ("the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us"); Isaiah 35:1-8 (the desert shall rejoice and blossom; the eyes of the blind opened; waters break out in the wilderness; "an highway shall be there... the way of holiness"). [p.260]
- Colophon in Latin: "written by C. G. Jung with his own hand in his house in Küsnacht/Zurich in the year 1915." [p.260]
- *Editor's footnotes:* folios are numbered rather than pages (recto/verso); in 1921 Jung cited Isaiah 53 and 9:6 as describing the birth of the redeeming symbol "where one does not expect it," and the redeeming symbol as "that of a child," childlikeness bringing another guiding principle in place of self-will; Mysterium Coniunctionis later parallels Isaiah 35 with the union of destructive and constructive powers of the unconscious. [p.260 notes 2 to 4]

## Prologue (second layer): the spirit of this time and the spirit of the depths [pp.260-261]

*(Marked [2] in the edition: Jung's later elaboration. Addressed by an "I" speaking in prophetic cadence.)*

- Jung says that in the spirit of this time he must say no one and nothing can justify what he must proclaim; justification is superfluous since he has no choice. He has learned there is another spirit at work "which rules the depths of everything contemporary." The **spirit of this time** wants to hear of "use and value." [p.260]
- Long ago "filled with human pride and blinded by the presumptuous spirit of the times," he sought to hold the other spirit away. The **spirit of the depths** "from time immemorial and for all the future possesses a greater power than the spirit of this time, who changes with the generations." The spirit of the depths subjugated all pride and arrogance "to the power of judgment"; took away his belief in science; robbed him of "the joy of explaining and ordering things"; let devotion to the ideals of the time die out; forced him "down to the last and simplest things"; took his understanding and knowledge and put them at the service of the inexplicable and paradoxical; robbed him of speech and writing for everything not in his service, "namely the melting together of sense and nonsense, which produces the supreme meaning." [p.260]
- **The supreme meaning** (*höchster Sinn*): "the path, the way and the bridge to what is to come. That is the God yet to come." It is "not the coming God himself but his image which appears in the supreme meaning. God is an image, and those who worship him must worship him in the images of the supreme meaning." [p.260]
- Chant on the supreme meaning (set in a block of short sayings) [p.261]: It is not a meaning and not an absurdity, "image and force in one, magnificence and force together"; "the beginning and the end"; "the bridge of going across and fulfillment" (note: Nietzsche's *Zarathustra* uses "going across," "down-going," "bridge"); the other Gods died of their temporality, but the supreme meaning never dies: "it turns into meaning and then into absurdity, and out of the fire and blood of their collision the supreme meaning rises up rejuvenated anew." The image of God has a shadow; the supreme meaning is real and casts a shadow ("what can be actual and corporeal and have no shadow?"). The shadow is nonsense, lacks force and continued existence, but "nonsense is the inseparable and undying brother of the supreme meaning." Some men grow in light, others in shadows ("There are many who need the shadows and not the light"); the image of God throws a shadow as great as itself. The supreme meaning is "as wide as the space of the starry Heaven and as narrow as the cell of the living body."
- Jung's struggle: the spirit of this time wanted to recognize the greatness of the supreme meaning but not its **littleness**; the spirit of the depths conquered this arrogance and he "had to swallow the small as a means of healing the immortal in me." This burnt up his innards because it was "inglorious and unheroic... ridiculous and revolting"; the pliers of the spirit of the depths held him and he drank "the bitterest of all draughts." The spirit of this time tempted him to treat this as shadowiness of the God-image (a "pernicious deception" because the small, narrow and banal is not nonsense but "one of both of the essences of the Godhead"). He resisted admitting the everyday belongs to the image of the Godhead, hiding "behind the highest and coldest stars," but the spirit of the depths forced the bitter drink between his lips. [p.261 and note 9: refers to later episodes: healing of Izdubar, the solitary's bitter drink]
- The spirit of this time whispered "this melting together of the hot stone and the cold, that is you and only you." The spirit of the depths: "You are an image of the unending world, all the last mysteries of becoming and passing away live in you. If you did not possess all this, how could you know?" He speaks because he must: "I am the serf who brings it and does not know what he carries in his hand. It would burn his hands if he did not place it where his master orders him to lay it." [p.261]
- **Dialogue of the temptation:** the spirit of the time asks what dire urgency forces him to speak; Jung tries to reason a cause and nearly makes one up; the spirit of the depths: "To understand a thing is a bridge and possibility of returning to the path. But to explain a matter is arbitrary and sometimes even murder. Have you counted the murderers among the scholars?" The spirit of this time lays before him huge volumes of ore pages with steel-stylus words containing all his knowledge, pointing: "What you speak, that is madness." Jung: "It is true... what I speak is the greatness and intoxication and ugliness of madness." The spirit of the depths: "What you speak is. The greatness is, the intoxication is, the undignified, sick, paltry dailiness is. It runs in all the streets, lives in all the houses, and rules the day of all humanity. Even the eternal stars are commonplace. [...] worship? Where is your measure, false measurer? The sum of life decides in laughter and in worship, not your judgment." Jung adds: the supreme meaning is "laughter and worship, a bloody laughter and a bloody worship. A sacrificial blood binds the poles." [pp.261]
- His **humanity** protests: "What solitude, what coldness of desolation you lay upon me when you speak such! Reflect on the destruction of being and the streams of blood from the terrible sacrifice that the depths demand." (Note: refers to the vision that follows.) The spirit of the depths answers: "No one can or should halt this sacrifice. Sacrifice is not destruction, sacrifice is the foundation of what is to come. Have you not had monasteries? Have not countless thousands gone into the desert? You should carry the monastery in [...] of this time with iron, the call of the desert would break all chains. Truly, I prepare you for solitude." His humanity falls silent; something happens to his spirit he calls **mercy**: "My speech is imperfect... out of the impossibility of finding those words, I speak in images. With nothing else can I express the words from the depths." The mercy gives belief, hope and daring to utter the word of the depths; before he can, he needs a visible sign that the spirit of the depths in him is also ruler of the depths of world affairs. [pp.261-262]

## The vision and dream of 1913-14 (the first-layer account, with the second-layer exhortation) [p.262]

*(Marked in the edition with footnote 15; this is the original fantasy material, narrated retrospectively.)*

- **October 1913:** leaving alone on a journey, "suddenly overcome in broad daylight by a vision": a terrible **flood** covering all northern and low-lying lands between the North Sea and the Alps, from England to Russia and from the North Sea coast to the Alps; yellow waves, swimming rubble, the death of countless thousands. It lasted two hours, left him ill and unable to interpret it. Two weeks later it returned more violently with an inner voice: "look at it, it is completely real, and it will come to pass. You cannot doubt this." He wrestled two hours; felt his mind had gone crazy. Anxiety about the coming terrible event kept returning; once he also saw a sea of blood over the northern lands. [p.262]
- **Three dreams in June-July 1914** (beginning and end of June, beginning of July): each time he is in a foreign land and, overnight in the middle of summer, a terrible cold descends from space; all seas and rivers are locked in ice, every green living thing frozen. The third dream: in a remote English land he must return home by the fastest ship; in his homeland, in midsummer a terrible cold had fallen; a leaf-bearing but fruitless tree stands whose leaves, through the working of the frost, had turned into **sweet grapes full of healing juice**; he picked some and gave them to a great waiting throng. [p.262; note 20: ice wine]
- **Fulfillment:** when war broke out Jung was in Scotland and had to choose the fastest ship home; he met "the colossal cold that froze everything," the flood, the sea of blood, and found "my barren tree whose leaves the frost had transformed into a remedy." He plucked the ripe fruit and gave it, not knowing "what bitter-sweet intoxicating drink, which left on your tongues an aftertaste of blood." [p.262 and note 22]
- **Exhortation to the reader (second layer):** "Believe me: It is no teaching and no instruction that I give you." "My path is not your path, therefore I cannot teach you. The way is within us, but not in Gods, nor in teachings, nor in laws. Within us is the way, the truth, and the life." (Note: contrast with John 14:6.) "Woe betide those who live by way of examples!" "The signposts have fallen, unblazed trails lie before us." Do not gobble the fruits of foreign fields: "you yourselves are the fertile acre which bears everything that avails you." "There is only one way and that is your way." "I will be no savior, no lawgiver, no master teacher unto you. You are no longer little children." "Giving laws, wanting improvements, making things easier, has all become wrong and evil." The way leads "to mutual love in community"; laws and teachings in common drive people to solitude, which makes them hostile and venomous; "give people dignity and let each of them stand apart, so that each may find his own fellowship and love it." "Power stands against power, contempt against contempt, love against love." "The one eye of the Godhead is blind, the one ear of the Godhead is deaf, the order of its being is crossed by chaos. So be patient with the crippledness of the world and do not overvalue its consummate beauty." [p.262]
- *Draft variants (footnotes):* a friend is with Jung in the dream and wants a slower ship; the draft's variant says "One should not turn people into sheep, but sheep into people," and that whoever wants to be a doctor of the soul or the soul's shepherd offends human dignity. [p.262 notes 17-19, 28]

## Chapter 1: Re-finding the Soul [pp.262-263]

*Heading: "Re-finding the Soul" (Cap. i; the Corrected Draft title is "First Nights"). The chapter begins in the second layer, then reverts to the first layer (the Black Book entry of November 12, 1913).*

- **[2] Commentary:** At the time of the October 1913 vision, in the fortieth year of his life, Jung had achieved everything he had wished for: "honor, power, wealth, knowledge, and every human happiness." Then his desire for further increase ceased, "the desire ebbed from me and horror came over me." The vision seized him and he felt the spirit of the depths but did not understand him; yet "he drove me on with unbearable inner longing." (Note: Jung wrote to Freud breaking off relations on October 27, 1913; in his 1935 ETH lecture he said that around the thirty-fifth year "things begin to change... the going down to death.") [pp.262-263]
- **[1] The call to the soul (Nov 12, 1913):** "My soul, where are you? Do you hear me? I speak, I call you—are you there? I have returned, I am here again. I have shaken the dust of all the lands from my feet, and I have come to you, I am with you. After long years of long wandering, I have come to you again." He says he has learned "one must live this life. This life is the way, the long sought-after way to the unfathomable, which we call divine. There is no other way, all other ways are false paths. I found the right way, it led me to you, to my soul. I return, tempered and purified." He asks the soul to give him her hand, thanks life "for all the happy and all the sad hours," and says "My soul, my journey should continue with you. I will wander with you and ascend to my solitude." [p.263]
- **[2] Commentary:** The spirit of the depths forced him to say this "against myself," since he had still been under the spirit of this time and thought and spoke much of the soul ("I knew many learned words for her, I had judged her and turned her into a scientific object"). He did not consider that his soul cannot be the object of his judgment; his judgment and knowledge are the objects of his soul. Hence he had to speak to his soul "as a living and self-existing being" and "had to become aware that I had lost my soul." The spirit of the depths considers the soul "a living and self-existing being," contradicting the spirit of this time for which she is "a thing dependent on man." What he had called his soul "was not at all my soul, but a dead system." He must address his soul "as to something far off and unknown, which did not exist through me, but through whom I existed." [p.263; note 38: reflexivity of concepts in psychology; note 39: Draft: "a dead system that I had contrived"]
- **Teaching on desire and soul:** "He whose desire turns away from outer things, reaches the place of the soul." If he does not find the soul, "the horror of emptiness will overcome him, and fear will drive him with a whip lashing time and again in a desperate endeavor and a blind desire for the hollow things of the world." He runs after things and grasps them but does not find his soul, since "he would find her only in himself." "He could find his soul in desire itself, but not in the objects of desire. If he possessed his desire, and his desire did not possess him, he would lay a hand on his soul, since his desire is the image and expression of his soul." [p.263]
- **On images:** "If we possess the image of a thing, we possess half the thing. The image of the world is half the world. He who possesses the world but not its image possesses only half the world, since his soul is [...] it. My friends, it is wise to nourish the soul, otherwise you will breed dragons and devils in your heart." [p.263; note 42 links to the later concept *esse in anima*, with Cary Baynes's diary comment of February 8, 1924 on the "Bild" being half the world; note 44 Draft: "you have only seen me as half a man, since my soul had lost me"]

## Chapter 2: Soul and God (begins) [p.264]

*Heading: "Soul and God" (Cap. ii). First layer: the second night, November 14, 1913. The range ends partway through the chapter's second-layer commentary; the chapter continues on PDF p. 265 (next part).*

- **Second night: Jung calls to his soul:** "I am weary, my soul, my wandering has lasted too long, my search for myself outside of myself." He has gone through events "and find you behind all of them." Through his erring "through events, humanity, and the world," he found men, and found his soul again "first in images within men and then you yourself... where I least expected you. You climbed out of a dark shaft. You announced yourself to me in advance in dreams." The dreams "burned in my heart and drove me to all the boldest acts of daring"; she let him see truths and undertake journeys whose endless length would have frightened him "if the knowledge of them had not been secure in you." [p.264]
- He wandered so long that he "forgot that I possessed a soul"; asks where she was ("Which Beyond sheltered you and gave you sanctuary?"). "Who are you, child? My dreams have represented you as a child and as a maiden. I am ignorant of your mystery. Forgive me if I speak as in a dream, like a drunkard—are you God? Is God a child, a maiden?" He fears he babbles; speaks quietly and says he is neither drunkard nor deranged and that his heart twists in pain from a wound "whose darkness delivers speeches full of mockery": "You are lying to yourself! You spoke so as to deceive others and make them believe in you. You want to be a prophet and chase after your ambition." [p.264 and notes 49-51]
- *Black Book 2 variants (footnotes):* he found her again "only through the soul of the woman"; "I bear a wound that is as yet not healed: my ambition to make an impression"; a note on the divine child (Horus, Tages, Christ, Dionysus, Heracles; "Should the daughter of man be God's name?"). [p.264 notes 49-51]
- He finds it strange to call her a child, "you who still hold the all-without-end in your hand." On the way of the day she went with him invisibly "putting the pieces together meaningfully, and letting me see the whole in each part." She took away where he took hold and gave where he expected nothing; fate came "from new and unexpected quarters": "Where I sowed, you robbed me of the harvest, and where I did not sow, you give me fruit a hundredfold." Time and again he lost the path and found it again where he never would have foreseen it; "You upheld my belief when I was alone and near despair. At every decisive moment you let me believe in myself." [p.264]
- **[2] Commentary begins:** "Like a tired wanderer who had sought nothing in the world apart from her, shall I come closer to my soul. I shall learn that my soul finally lies behind everything, and if I cross the world, I am ultimately doing this to find my soul. Even the dearest are themselves not the goal and end of the love that goes on seeking, they are symbols of their own souls." "My friends, do you guess to what solitude we ascend?" [p.264]
- **On dreams:** he must learn that "the dregs of my thought, my dreams, are the speech of my soul"; he must carry them in his heart and go back and forth over them "like the words of the person dearest to me." "Dreams are the guiding words of the soul." The spirit of this time is the measure of "beautiful, ungainly, clever, foolish," but "the spirit of the depths surpasses it at both ends. Only the spirit of this time knows the difference between large and small. But this difference is invalid, like the spirit which recognizes it." The spirit of the depths taught him to consider his actions and decisions as dependent on dreams: "Dreams pave the way for life, and they determine you without you understanding their language." One would like to learn this language, "but who can teach and learn it? Scholarliness alone is not enough; there is a knowledge of the heart that gives deeper insight." The knowledge of the heart "is in no book and is not to be found in the mouth of any teacher, but grows out of you like the green seed from the dark earth." Scholarliness belongs to the spirit of this time, "but this spirit in no way grasps the dream, since the soul is everywhere that scholarly knowledge is not." Objection: one cannot live or think everything others do; reply: "The life that I could still live, I should live, and the thoughts that I could still think, I should think."
