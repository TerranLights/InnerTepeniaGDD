## Overview of this range

This part is the heart of Liber Secundus (the "second book" of Liber Novus), which follows the narrator's visionary journeys in the first weeks of 1914. It runs from the end of a descent into the sea of the collective, through a long sequence of adventures with a series of figures:

1. **Death and the inner life** (the moon as preserver of souls; the knowledge of collective death; the decision to die outside and live within).
2. **The Anchorite Ammonius** in the Libyan desert: a former Alexandrian rhetorician who has spent years unlearning the worship of words, who reads the gospels for their "meaning yet to come," and who has a break with the narrator when the narrator suggests he needs men. The reflective passages teach about the solitary, the desert, word-magic as protection against the boundless, and the need to accept one's darkness.
3. **Death and rebirth** (a vision of the dead flowing into the sea; a world-war vision; the teaching that life and death must balance; the "second birth").
4. **The Remains of Earlier Temples**: the narrator meets the Red One and Ammonius, both ruined by what they rejected, and recognizes that fallen ideals end as "monkey business"; he becomes a green forest daimon and turns east.
5. **The Izdubar episode** (First Day through Opening of the Egg, Caps. viii to xi): the narrator meets a trembling bull-horned hero from the East, lames him with scientific knowledge ("poison"), cannot find help, declares the giant a *fantasy*, carries him weightless, shrinks him to an egg, incubates him with incantations, and opens the egg to find him healed as the sun. Reflective passages teach about the exchange of force between a person and the God they create, about evil, emptiness and fullness, and about why the good need evil.
6. **Hell and the Sacrificial Murder** (Caps. xii, xiii): a vision of a maiden piercing a devil's eye; then a child's corpse, the demand to remove and eat part of the liver, and the revelation that the veiled woman is the narrator's own soul.
7. **Divine Folly and the Nox Secunda / Nox Tertia** (Caps. xiv to xvi): a library scene on the Imitation of Christ, an arrest and committal to an asylum, the teaching on the dead, the animal, the imitation of Christ, and finally chaos and madness accepted; the range ends mid-way through a night in the asylum where the narrator is told "there is no salvation here" and the sea/chaos/word teaching begins.

The editor's notes throughout supply historical, biblical, mythological and Jungian cross-references (Philo, John's Logos, Gnostics, Nietzsche, Christ and the Imitation tradition, Egyptian solar myths, Vedic hymns, Gilgamesh, Marduk and Tiamat) and quote Jung's later published writings and the Black Books that parallel the visions.

## 1. End of the preceding chapter: the sea, the moon, and the knowledge of death [p.298]

*(Chapter continues from the previous part; its heading is not in this range.)*

**Visionary content.**
- A passage in the second person ("you") describes a descent into the sea: from endless blue plains into black depths, through columns of dark-leaved plants, fish drawing the dreamer, white sandy coasts, slimy polyps, green water, breaking surf. The sea carries you without your "adhering" to anything.
- Far above, a golden light like the moon emerging from the tide; longing awakens and "the will for your own movement." The dreamer wants to "cross over from being to becoming" [p.298].
- The dreamer sees that the sea is "the life of the whole and the death of each individual," and feels entwined in a collective death. Despair and mortal fear. Everything (light and dark, warm and cold water, plant-animals) becomes horror; the dreamer longs for sun, dry air, firm stones, a fixed place, straight lines, rules, purpose, "singleness and your own intent."
- **The knowledge of death** came "that night, from the dying that engulfs the world." Golden wheat sinks under the reaper's scythe like a smooth wave on a beach. One who stays in common life fears death; this fear drives him toward **singleness**. In singleness he is "one who becomes" and has overcome death by overcoming common life. One who merely exists never becomes aware of life; he needs heights and singleness for that. It is good to become aware of collective death, since then you know why your singleness and heights are good.
- **The moon image:** your heights are like the moon that wanders alone and clear through the night. Sometimes it covers itself, then fills out with light again. "The death of the earth is foreign to it." It sees earth's life from afar; it is the solitary clear light of the night, "the individual being, and the near fragment of eternity." You look out cold, motionless and radiating; your hands are red with living blood (the life blood of your brother, "your own blood") but your gaze is moonlike and does not hear "the groaning and howling of the human animal."
- **The turn:** "The moon is dead. Your soul went to the moon, to the preserver of souls." The soul moved toward death. The narrator went into inner death, saw that **outer dying is better than inner death**, and decided "to die outside and to live within," so he turned away to seek "the place of the inner life."

**Editor's apparatus.**
- n.40: Jung (1912, *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido*) cited cultures that held the moon to be the gathering place of departed souls; he returns to the motif in *Mysterium Coniunctionis*.
- n.41: the Draft here adds a line about accepting "the rogue" and living and dying with him, "since we kill what we live" [p.298].

## 2. The Anchorite (Cap. iv, "Dies 1" / First Day) [pp.298 to 301]

*(Handwritten Draft title: "Fourth Adventure: First Day"; Corrected Draft: "Dies I. Evening." Dated Dec 30, 1913.)*

### Scene: the desert and the footprints [p.298]
- The narrator finds himself in a desert at night "on new paths": yellow sand in waves, a "terrible irascible sun," steel-blue sky, a deep valley with a dry river bed. He sees bare footprints leading from the rocky valley up to the plateau and follows them; he realizes the track loops into the same set he already followed (fresh and half worn-away prints run together). He follows them down, climbing red wind-corroded rock that burns his soles.
- At the valley bottom he finds a small reed-covered mud-brick hut, its plank door marked with a red cross. Inside sits a haggard man in white linen on a mat with a yellow parchment Greek gospel across his knees: **an anchorite of the Libyan desert.**
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.44 quotes Black Book 3 (Dec 30, 1913): Jung writes that things are leading him away from his scientific work; he reflects that he must do this "just for myself without hope, for the sake of God," and compares himself to the first-century Christian anchorites who left wife, wealth, glory and science for the desert. n.45: the anchorite is named Ammonius in the next chapter; Jung noted in a letter (Dec 31, 1913) that the anchorite came from the third century. The editor lists three historical Ammonii of Alexandria (a third-century Christian philosopher; Ammonius Cetus, a Platonist-to-Neoplatonist transition figure; a fifth-century Neoplatonist who tried to reconcile Aristotle and the Bible) and notes the mixing of Neoplatonism and Christianity there [p.298].

### Dialogue with Ammonius, part 1: time, reading, multiple meanings [pp.299 to 300]
- Ammonius refuses to be called "father"; the footprints are his twice-daily walks at daybreak and sunset. Other anchorites live down-valley in huts or ancient rock graves; he lives uppermost for greater solitude. He has lived there about ten years but time "passes quickly." Only idlers are bored; he is "grieving" and so occupied.
- He reads the gospels again and again. A book does not remain the same: words work "productively in your spirit," and after a week away and changes of spirit, one finds new things.
- **Central teaching: "a succession of words does not have only one meaning."** Men strive to give a sequence of words one meaning in order to have an unambiguous language; this is "worldly and constricted." On higher levels of insight the sequence has more than one valid meaning; only the all-knowing knows all meanings [p.299]. He rejects the narrator's suggestion of an exoteric/esoteric doubleness as a "bad superstition."
- **His biography:** before Christianity he was a rhetorician and philosopher in Alexandria with many students (Romans, barbarians, Gauls, Britons). He taught Greek philosophy and new systems including that of Philo the Jew, "clever but fantastically abstract." He added his own words and "wove an atrocious web of words" that ensnared listeners and himself; they gave words divine potency and thought they had committed the divine to words. "Words should not become Gods" [p.299].
- **John's prologue discussed:** when the narrator cites "God was the Word," Ammonius tells him to guard against being a slave to words and has him read from "In him was the life" onward. His reading: was the Logos a concept or a word? No: "a light, indeed a man," who lived among men. **Philo lent John the word so that John could describe the son of man; but John gave to living men the meaning of the Logos, whereas Philo gave the Logos as a dead concept that "usurped life, even the divine life."** The dead does not gain life; the living is killed; "this was also my atrocious error" [p.300].
- The narrator had read it the other way (the son of man elevates the lower to the spirit of the Logos); Ammonius answers that John has also brought the meaning of the Logos "up to man." Asked if the human stands higher than the Logos, he gives a limited answer: if the human God had not become important above everything, he would have appeared "in the Logos" and not "as the son in the flesh."
- He says he has "spent many years alone with the process of unlearning" (hard for successful teachers). At dusk he sends the narrator to sleep in a cut rock grave with a reed bed, a pitcher of water, dried dates and black bread, saying "do not forget your morning prayer, when the sun rises" [p.300].
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.46: Philo of Alexandria (20 BCE to 50 CE), Hellenistic Jewish philosopher fusing Greek philosophy and Judaism; God as the transcendent "To On"; the knowable facet is the divine Logos; debate on his relation to John; Jung in 1954 to James Kirsch called John's gnosis "definitely Jewish, but its essence is Hellenistic." n.47: Jung (1957) wrote that our age is charged with the Christian epoch's "supremacy of the word." n.48 and n.49: John 1:1-10 and John 1:14 quoted. n.50: the Draft says the grave was "Egyptian"; in Egyptian context water, dates and bread would be offerings to the dead [pp.299 to 300].

### Reflective passages [pp.300 to 301]
**On the solitary and the desert [p.300].** (Note: written in third person about "the solitary.")
- The solitary lives in an endless, beautiful desert; he looks at the whole and at inner meaning; he loathes manifold diversity when near; seen from afar, "silvery splendor and joy and beauty cloak diversity." Near him everything must be simple and innocent. No haze or cloud may stand between him and the faraway, so he loves the desert above all.
- His life would be cold without the sun, which "replace[s] his own life warmth." His heart longs for the sun and wanders to its lands. The desert is a mother who gives food and warmth at regular hours and relieves him of care, so he can turn his whole life to "the sprouting garden of his soul," which flourishes only under a hot sun.
- **The garden image:** you think the solitary is poor, but he walks under laden fruit trees, grain a hundredfold, swelling blossoms, resin and seed. When the sun sinks "like an exhausted bird" onto the sea he wraps himself up and holds his breath until the miracle of light rises in the East; "brimful delicious expectation is in the solitary." He stammers about it and seems poor in spirit. He hands you a small fruit that fell at his feet; it seems worthless but tastes like an unimagined sun; you long for the tree, the garden and the sun. You lie down "in ancient graves, whose walls resound with many voices and many colors of a thousand solar years," and sleep down through the thousand solar years.
- **Editor's note n.51:** the Draft continues with a person walking in a circle returning to the solitary one hidden in the depths, held by the warm bosom of the rock.

**On the Summa and words [p.301].**
- You sit leaning on the wall before "the Summa," the whole, "like a book" that you are greedy to devour. You cannot grasp it; here and there a light flickers, a fruit falls, your foot strikes gold; but something opaque or "smooth and impenetrable like polished steel" comes between you and it. After "all the glowing hot crucibles of the Hell of doubt" you sit again, content, years passing like a breath of air.
- **Words as protection.** The writing "always says the same, if you believe in words." But if you believe in things in whose place only words stand, you never reach the end. Life flows not only on a finite but an infinite path; the unbounded makes you anxious and your humanity rebels, so you seek limits. You cry out for the word that has one meaning and no other. "The word becomes your God, since it protects you from the countless possibilities of interpretation. The word is protective magic against the daimons of the unending." You speak the magic word ("that is that and only that") and the limitless is banished. Therefore men seek and make words.
- **The solitary breaks the wall of words and is a murderer.** He who breaks the wall overthrows Gods and defiles temples; he calls up the daimons of the boundless; he does not hear the groans of mankind. Yet you cannot find new words without shattering the old; nobody should shatter old words unless he finds a new word that is "a firm rampart against the limitless" and holds more life than the old. "A new word is a new God for old men." Man remains the same, an imitator.
- **Resolution.** "What was word, shall become man." The word lit up in the darkness and the darkness did not comprehend it (John 1); the word should become what the darkness can comprehend. **The God of words is cold and dead and shines from afar like the moon; let the word return to its creator, man, "and thus the word will be heightened in man."** Man should be "light, limits, measure." The darkness does not comprehend the word but comprehends man, "since he himself is a piece of the darkness." **"Not from the word down to man, but from the word up to man."** The darkness is your mother, who deserves reverence because she is dangerous: "Honor the darkness as the light, and you will illumine your darkness."
- **Comprehending the darkness.** If you comprehend it, it seizes you like a night with black shadows and countless shimmering stars; silence and peace come. Only he who does not comprehend darkness fears the night. Through comprehending "the dark, the nocturnal, the abyssal in you," you become utterly simple and sleep through the millennia "since the simple is what always was."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.52 ("Summa" = Latin "whole"); n.53 (Draft says "to you", Corrected Draft "to me"; the Corrected Draft switches "you" to "I" throughout this section); n.54 (Jung on protective word magic, 1940, "Transformation symbolism in the mass").

## 3. Dies II (Cap. v, "Second Day, Morning") [pp.301 to 304]

*(Dated Jan 1, 1914. The chapter heading is given only as "Cap. v."; Corrected Draft: "(The Anchorite). Second Day. Morning.")*

### The dream and the morning [pp.301 to 302]
- The narrator wakes at dawn after "a wonderful night in the distant depths of time." He recalls a white horse seen against the rising sun, which said: "Hail him who is in darkness since the day is over him." There were four white horses with golden wings pulling the sun's carriage, on which stood Helios with flaring mane. The narrator stood in a gorge, astonished; a thousand black serpents crawled into their holes; Helios ascended; he knelt and called, "Give us your light, you are flame-curled, entwined, crucified and revived." The cry woke him. He wonders whether Ammonius secretly worships the sun.
- Morning: yellow sand trickles in veins down the rocks; red sky; a large lizard on a stone awaiting the sun. He thinks over Ammonius's remarks about sequences of words having many meanings, and that John brought the Logos to man ("does not sound properly Christian... Is he perhaps a Gnostic?").
- He has no prayer: "we have no more prayers." He wonders at the nakedness and poverty of modern people and at the age-old dreams of mankind.
- **The scarab.** He watches a dark beetle push a ball and addresses it: "Are you still toiling away in order to live your beautiful myth?" Creatures that live their myth do not doubt. He finds himself praying to it ("Dear scarab, my father, I honor you") and to the stone mother ("Yours is my heart and all glory and power. Amen."), then recoils: "What nonsense am I talking? I'm worshiping an animal." He puts it down to the desert, which "demands prayers."
- **Living stones.** The stones seem animated, lined up "like a troop transport," arranged by size, small ones in groups preceding large ones: "Here the stones form states." He asks whether he is dreaming; the morning is gone in what felt like a moment.
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.57 (Jung, 1945, on the tree rooted below as well as above: "The goal is not the heights but the center," with the "inverted tree"); n.58 date Jan 1, 1914; n.59 Helios drives a four-horse chariot; n.60 Jung's engagement with Gnostic texts at this period; n.61 Jung (1952) on the scarab as a "classical rebirth symbol" (Egyptian Am-Tuat: the dead sun transforms into Khepri the scarab at the tenth station and mounts the barge at the twelfth) [pp.301 to 302].

### Dialogue with Ammonius, part 2: prayer, conversion, history of religions [pp.302 to 303]
- The narrator admits he found no prayer but dreamed he prayed to the rising sun and, absentmindedly, to the scarab and earth. Ammonius: "If you do find a word, your soul has nevertheless found inexpressible words to greet the break of day." The narrator: it was a heathen prayer to Helios. Ammonius: "Let that suffice for you." "Be astonished at nothing, and in no case condemn or regret it."
- **How Ammonius was freed from the "predicament of spinning words."** An old man (a man his father had freed from slavery and to whom he was attached since childhood) asked: "Are you happy and are you fully alive?" and described how Ammonius lectured: anxious about the judgment of listeners, weaving witty jokes to please, heaping learned expressions to impress, "restless and hasty... You are not in yourself." He credited the old man. Then the old man announced that God had become flesh in his son. Ammonius tried Egyptian equivalents (Osiris, Horus, Seth, virgin queen) and rejected them one by one; the old man insisted: a real man, from a virgin, hung on a cross, died and rose on the third day, called Jesus the anointed one, a man and yet the Son of God. Ammonius dismissed him ("this Jewish God, whom the poor honor at the harbor, and whose unclean mysteries they celebrate in cellars") but the phrase "a man and yet the Son of God" returned like an echo and brought him to Christianity.
- **Christianity and the Egyptian teachings.** Ammonius agrees only that the old teachings were "less adequate expressions" of Christianity. History of religions: "It's erroneous to believe that religions differ in their innermost essence... it's always one and the same religion. Every subsequent form of religion is the meaning of the antecedent." His father once bought a black slave from the region of the Nile's source, from a country that had heard of neither Osiris nor the other gods; the man's simple language said the same as the Egyptians believed. Those "uneducated Negroes" unknowingly possessed what the religions of cultured peoples developed into doctrines; those who read that language correctly could find pagan doctrines and Jesus's doctrine in it. He now reads the gospels to find "their meaning which is yet to come," a hidden meaning pointing to the future.
- **Temptation scene.** Asked if he found that future meaning, Ammonius: "No, not yet." Sometimes he thinks he needs others' stimulation but regards those as temptations of Satan. The narrator suggests he'd succeed if nearer men; "Perhaps you're right." Then he turns suspicious: "I love the desert... you can see glorious Helios, no, that is pagan, what's wrong with me? You are Satan, I recognize you, give way, adversary!" He lunges; the narrator is "far away in the twentieth century."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.62 and n.63: Osiris, Seth, Horus in Egyptian myth; Jung discussed them in *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido* (1912). n.64: the Corrected Draft adds that the narrator is "unreal to myself as in a dream"; Christian anchorites were perpetually on guard against Satan; the editor cites Athanasius's life of St. Anthony and Flaubert's *Temptation of Anthony*; Jung (1921) called the Devil "the voice of the anchorite's own unconscious that rises up against the forcible suppression of his nature" [p.303].

### Reflective passages [pp.303 to 304]
**The dream in the grave of the millennia [p.303].** He who sleeps in "the grave of the millennia" dreams a primordial dream of the rising sun; if you dream it now you know the sun will also rise now; we are still in the dark but day is near. He who comprehends the darkness in himself finds the light near; he who "climbs down into his darkness reaches the staircase of the working light, fire-maned Helios." **Helios's chariot ascends with four white horses; "his back bears no cross, and his side no wound"; he is not a man of mockery but of splendor and force.** The speaker is "drunk with fire," having climbed down through the centuries "and plunged into the sun far at the bottom"; asks for a human hand to hold him to the earth.

**Day, the shadow of the earth, and prayer [p.303].** The day of this world is about to break and he remains in the gorge ("the shadow and heaviness of the earth"). He asks why he should pray to the sun: "I drink the sun within me." But the desert in him demands prayers; he prays because he is empty and a beggar. In the day of this world he forgets he drank the sun; stepping into earth's shadows he sees himself naked and poor. "No sooner do you touch the earth than your inner life is over; it flees from you into things."

**Things live off you [pp.303 to 304].** A wondrous life arises in things; what you thought dead and inanimate betrays "a secret life and silent, inexorable intent." Even the stones speak; magical threads spin between you and things. "Things live their life, and they live off you": rivers bear your life to the valley, a leaf dances with you, "the irrational animal guesses your thought and represents you." Nothing in you is hidden from things; the dog robs you of your long-dead father and looks at you as he did; the cow intuits your mother; the stars whisper your mysteries; valleys cradle you. You stand like a stray child among the mighty and grasp the first person you meet, hoping he has the thought you lack (which "all things have sucked out of you"). You want tidings of one whom things have not lived but who lived and fulfilled himself: **the son of the sun**, who shines and does not suckle, whom no one sucked dry, who "cut through the webs of the earth," owned himself and was no one's servant.

**The solitary's failure [p.304].** The solitary fled the world, shut eyes and ears, buried himself in a cave within himself, but the desert sucked him dry, the stones spoke his thoughts, the cave echoed his feelings, so he became "desert, stone, and cave." He was desire and not splendor, "completely earth and not sun." He went to the desert as a clever saint who knew he was no different from other sons of the earth; if he had drunk of himself he would have drunk fire. "He did not want to find himself but rather the manifold meaning of holy scripture." Immense fullness and immense emptiness are the same. **Manifold meaning is found only in yourself, not in things:** it is not simultaneous but "a succession of meanings" that lie in the changing self. "If you change, the countenance of the world alters." The solitary "fathomed the thing but not himself." The devil came to him, as to every desirous solitary; "I had to appear to him as the devil, since I had accepted my darkness. I ate the earth and I drank the sun, and I became a greening tree that stands alone and grows."

**Editor's apparatus.** n.65: "irrational animal" is an inversion of Aristotle's "rational animal." n.66 cross-refers to Jung's description of the Pleroma (p. 347, in a later part). n.67: the Draft continues with a passage on solitude's beauty and the tree growing, "eating the earth with roots... and drinking the sun with branches," going on until every last sound of life died [p.304].

## 4. Death (Cap. vi) [pp.304 to 306]

*(Handwritten Draft: "Fifth Adventure: Death." Dated Jan 2, 1914.)*

### The vision of the dark one and the dead [pp.304 to 305]
- **Setting:** the narrator wanders to a northern land: gray sky, misty cool-moist air, lowlands where currents "flash in broad mirrors" and flow toward the sea; sparse trees, swamp meadows, murky water, an unending lonely horizon. He follows "my brother, the sea," anxious and expectant. Low yellow hills; a broad dead lake; at the horizon the sky and sea fuse into infinity.
- On the last dune stands a gaunt man in a black wrinkled coat, motionless, looking into the distance, with "a deeply serious look." The narrator recognizes "the dark one" ("There is only one who stands this way, so solitary and at the last corner of the world").
- **Dialogue.** The dark one says his heart has never beaten and he is cold. The narrator names him: "ice and the end... the cold silence of the stones... the highest snow on the mountains and the most extreme frost of outer space." The dark one: the living are never guests here; those from "the land of the clear day" who depart "never to return" flow past in dense crowds. This is where things go "into the undifferentiable, where none is equal or unequal, but all are one with one another."
- **The procession.** What looks like a dark wall of clouds on the tide is densely pressed multitudes: old men, women, children, horses, oxen, smaller animals, insects, a forest, "innumerable faded flowers, an utterly dead summer." They are stiff and cool, feet not moving, no sound, clasping themselves, gazing beyond, all flowing past in an enormous stream. The first rows meet the point where surf and stream collide; a wave of air whirls up the stream of the dead together with the sea, scatters them in black scraps and dissolves them in murky mist, wave after wave. "Dark one, tell me, is this the end?" "Look!" The sea breaks heavily; a reddish glow like blood; a sea of blood; blood and fire form a ball; **a new sun escapes from the bloody sea and rolls gleaming into the uttermost depths, disappearing under the narrator's feet**. He is alone; night falls. He recalls Ammonius: "Night is the time of silence."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.70 cross-refers to the vision in Liber Primus, ch. 5 "Descent into Hell in the Future" (p. 241) [p.305].

### Reflective passages: the war, evil, balance [pp.305 to 306]
- The solitude expands into the immeasurable and pierces the narrator with coldness; the sun still glows in him. He follows the stream "into the depths of what is to come." In the second night of 1914 "anxious expectation filled me. I went out to embrace the future... It was the enormous dying, a sea of blood. From it the new sun arose, awful and a reversal of that which we call day." The "terrible war arose," the darkness destroyed the light of the world, "and so we had to taste Hell." (The text places this as the narrator's vision, in the 1914 present, of what was coming.)
- **When darkness was comprehended**, a magnificent night came, the dream plunged him into the depths of the millennia, "and from it my phoenix ascended."
- **Virtues change into vices:** mildness becomes hardness, goodness brutality, love hate, understanding madness. You spoke of evil generally and smilingly but did not know "that it stands precisely right behind your virtues, that it is also your virtues themselves, as their inevitable substance." You locked Satan in the abyss for a millennium and then laughed at him as a fairy tale; when he raises his head the world winces; "your army of vices falls powerless." You do not know which devils are greater, "your vices, or your virtues. But of one thing you are certain, that virtues and vices are brothers."
- **Balance of life and death.** "We need the coldness of death to see clearly." Life wants to live and die; "life and death must strike a balance in your existence." Today's men "need a large slice of death, since too much incorrectness lives in them, and too much correctness died in them." What is in balance is correct; what disturbs balance is incorrect; once balance is attained, what preserves it is incorrect and what disturbs it is correct. "If I accept death, then my tree greens, since dying increases life." Joy at small things comes only to one who has accepted death; greedy looking for what one could yet live ruins pleasure. **"Therefore I behold death, since it teaches me how to live."**
- **The frosty vineyard.** Acceptance of death is like a frosty night in a vineyard full of sweet grapes; "Death ripens." Without death, life would be meaningless; "limitation enables you to fulfill your being."
- **Second birth.** Entering death with covered head, everything turns to ice; but in the shadow world "the red sun" rises, unexpectedly, and the world revolves "like a satanic apparition"; blood and murder have a peculiar beauty. What rises is the unacceptable and repulsive he has forever rejected; it is "opposed not according to the laws of reason, but... according to its own nature"; it stings from behind in the heel. It does not confront him like a manly enemy: "I perish on a dung heap, while peaceful chickens cackle around me... a dog passes, lifts his leg over me." He curses his birth seven times and prepares for "the hour of my second birth." The ancient saying *inter faeces et urinas nascimur*. For three nights he was "assaulted by the horrors of birth"; on the third night "junglelike laughter pealed forth, for which nothing is too simple. Then life began to stir again."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.71: Jung (1940) on evil as relative, partly avoidable, partly fate; same for virtue. n.72: the Corrected Draft replaces the Satan sentence with "Evil is one-half of the world, one of the two pans of the scale." n.73: Draft adds on freezing to death in solitude and seeing "clearly... what was to come." n.74: in *Transformations and Symbols of the Libido* (1912) Jung had argued that libido contains within itself the contrary striving toward death, against Schopenhauer's pure life-urge. n.75: the Draft adds "To live what is right and to let what is false die, that is the art of life"; Jung (1934, "Soul and death") on life's parabola reversing in midlife and "becoming and passing away is the same curve"; the editor refers to his own article on Jung's reflections on life and death (*Quadrant* 38, 2008). n.76 to n.79: see earlier note on frost and grapes; the wounded heel motif; the Augustine-attributed saying "we are born between faeces and urine" [pp.305 to 306].

## 5. The Remains of Earlier Temples (Cap. vii) [pp.306 to 308]

*(Handwritten Draft: "Sixth Adventure"; Corrected Draft: "6. Degenerate Ideals." Dated Jan 5, 1914. The editor notes the mosaic-like form of the page resembles the mosaics at Ravenna that Jung visited in 1913 and 1914; n.81.)*

### The meeting [pp.306 to 307]
- Wide flowery meadows, soft hills, fresh green wood. The narrator meets two strange journeymen: a **tall gangly thin man in discolored red clothes** (the **Red Rider/Red One**, now old, hair gray, clothes shabby) and a paunchy old monk who turns out to be **Ammonius**. Both look frightened at the narrator and make the sign of the cross; he realizes he is covered in green leaves that grow from his body.
- Ammonius cries "Apage, Satanas!"; the Red One says "Damned pagan riffraff!" The narrator identifies himself ("the Hyperborean stranger" who visited Ammonius in the desert, and "the watchman" the Red One once visited). Ammonius: "my downfall began with you"; the Red One reproaches Ammonius and Ammonius pokes him in the ribs (a repeated gag); they stand "sheepish and ridiculous, and yet pitiable."
- **Ammonius's account.** The narrator's remark that he needed the closeness of men to reach higher mysteries "stunned me like infernal poison." He summoned the brothers of the valley, claimed a messenger of God had appeared, and commanded them to form a monastery (refuting Brother Philetus's objection with "It is not good for man to be alone"). They founded it near the Nile, cultivated fat fields, forgot the scriptures, grew voluptuous. A longing to see Alexandria led to a pretext of visiting the bishop; he was intoxicated by shipboard life and the Alexandrian crowds and "became completely lost"; he sailed for Italy "in a dream," drank wine, saw that women were beautiful, "wallowed in pleasure and wholly turned into an animal." In Naples the Red One stood on the dock and he knew he had fallen into the hands of evil.
- **The Red One's account.** He says Ammonius would have become "an outright pig" had the Red One not been there; seeing him, Ammonius cursed drink and women and returned. The Red One's own story: after the earlier conversation (the "remark about dancing" that caught him "in the fox trap") he turned so serious that he entered a monastery, prayed, fasted and converted himself. Blindly he reformed the liturgy and, with the bishop's approval, **introduced dancing**. As abbot he alone had the right to dance before the altar, like David before the ark (n.86), but little by little the brothers, the congregation, and finally the whole city danced. Terrified, he fled into solitude and danced alone until he dropped but "in the morning the hellish dance began again." He wandered to Italy, felt freer in the south, and in Naples found "this ragged man of God," whose appearance gave him strength.
- **Their present state.** Each says the other is tolerable ("a toned-down type of devil" / "hardly the fanatical type") but both are hostile to the narrator, call him mocker and robber, and say they can never be friends. They travel together out of need: Ammonius: "Even the devil is necessary; since otherwise one has nothing that commands a sense of respect with people." The Red One: "I need to come to an arrangement with the clergy; or else I will lose my clientele." The narrator concludes: "the system is at fault."

### Reflective passages: second birth and the green daimon [pp.307 to 308]
- After seeing death and becoming "ice and night," an angry life and impulse rose; the thirst for the "rushing water of the deepest knowledge" "began to clink with wine glasses." From afar: drunken laughter, dance music, street noise, "the reek of the human animal," whores, kitchen steam. "I was born into life from below, and I grew up as heroes do, in hours rather than years." After growing up he found himself in the middle land, in spring.
- **The green daimon.** "A strange being grew through me": "a laughing being of the forest, a leaf green daimon, a forest goblin and prankster," greening and wilting with trees, "neither good nor bad, merely living, primordially old and yet completely young, naked and yet naturally clothed, not man but nature," childish, weak, deceiving and deceived, inconstant and superficial and yet reaching "to the kernel of the world."
- **Ruins of ideals.** The narrator "had absorbed the life of both of my friends"; a green tree grew from the ruins of the temple. They "had not withstood life" and "called the living a devil and traitor." Those who believed in themselves and their goodness "ultimately became mired in the natural and conclusive burial ground of all outlived ideals." After cursing comes laughter, so that the soul is saved from the dead.
- **On ideals.** Ideals are desired and pondered and exist only to that extent, but their effective being cannot be denied. One who thinks he lives his ideals "suffers from delusions of grandeur"; "the hero has fallen." Ideals are mortal; prepare for their end. "It was you who gave meaning, value and effective force to your ideal." If you become a sacrifice to the ideal, "the ideal cracks open, plays carnival with you, and goes to Hell on Ash Wednesday." The ideal is a tool, "a torch on dark paths. But whoever runs around with a torch by day is a fool." **"How much my ideals have come down, and how freshly my tree greens!"**
- **The alliance of the ruins.** When he turned green, he recognized the inner affinity of the two ruins and saw they had long had "an alliance of mutual silence." The solitary silence of the temple lured him far from men to supernatural mysteries "to the point of surfeit," while the devil prepared for his reception; he found no boundaries "other than surfeit and disgust." "I did not live, but was driven; I was a slave to my ideals."
- **Living as a tree; the two bridges [p.308].** Within himself he had become one as a natural being, but was "a hobgoblin who frightened the solitary wanderer" and avoided men; he lived neither longing (for world or for spirit) but himself, a "merrily greening tree." He learned to live without world and spirit. But men: there are two "deserted bridges" to mankind, one from above to below on which men glide down, one from below to above on which they groan upward. If he only climbs, he gives others undeserved pleasure; if he only enjoys himself, he causes others undeserved trouble; if he merely lives he is far from men. "It seems to be nearly impossible to live as a man." As long as you are not conscious of your self you can live; once conscious, "you fall from one grave into another." All rebirths could make you sick; the Buddha finally gave up on rebirth.
- **The chameleon and the lion.** After all rebirths you are still "the lion crawling on the earth, the chameleon" (Greek *chamai leon*, "lion on the ground"), a caricature changing colors, "precisely not a lion, whose nature is related to the sun, who draws his power from within himself." He recognizes the chameleon and no longer wants to crawl and change colors; he wants "to exist from my own force, like the sun which gives light and does not suck light."
- **Leaving the ruins.** Ruins stand in his way saying "with regard to men you should be this or that"; his "chameleonesque skin shudders." He pushes aside "the laughable survivors" and goes east. **"Henceforth I am completely alone."** No more "you should"; "I talk only with myself." He journeys with the west wind, talks with trees and forest wildlife, takes his bread where he finds it, provides no help and needs none. "I am man enough, I am noise, conversation, comfort, and help enough unto myself." He wanders to the far East; blue horizons suffice as a goal: "I will my rising." The image (Image 36) follows.
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.83 "Apage, Satanas" as a medieval expression; n.84 Hyperboreans (Greek legend, a land of sun beyond the north wind, worshipers of Apollo), and Nietzsche's use of "Hyperboreans" for free spirits (*The Antichrist* §1); n.85 Genesis 2:18 and the only biblical Philetus (2 Timothy 2:16-18, a false teacher); n.87 Corrected Draft "the wisdom"; n.88 Draft and Corrected Draft: "I had become a victim of my sanctuaries and beauties, and so I died miserable and depressed"; n.89 rose petals steam-distilled in Persia for perfume; n.90 Jung (1926) on the transition from morning to afternoon of life as a "transvaluation of earlier values" (the opposite of former ideals); n.94 the Draft adds that chameleons need an annual "bath of rebirth" and refers to a belief in 777 reincarnations (cites Ernest Woods's *The New Theosophy*); n.96 (Image 36 legend: "printed on Christmas 1915"; the depiction of Izdubar resembles an illustration in Roscher's *Lexikon*; "Izdubar" was an early mistranscription of what is now read as Gilgamesh, corrected by Jensen in 1906; Jung used the corrected form in 1912 and cited Jensen) [pp.307 to 308].

## 6. First Day (Cap. viii): Izdubar [pp.308 to 311]

*(Handwritten Draft: "Seventh Adventure. First Day"; Corrected Draft: "7. The Great Encounter. First Day. The Hero from the East." Dated Jan 8, 1914.)*

### The pass and the encounter [pp.308 to 309]
- On the third night a desolate range blocks the way; a narrow gorge leads in. The path is bare-footed pain: **one half of the path is white, the other black. Black is hot iron, white is ice.** He darts across, the valley widens into a rocky basin, a narrow path climbs to a ridge.
- At the pass a mighty booming like pounded ore. An **enormous man** approaches: bull horns on a great head, rattling armor, black beard decked with gems, a sparkling double axe. Face "faint and pale and deeply wrinkled," almond eyes. **Izdubar, the bull-man, trembling with consuming inner fear.** The narrator throws himself down ("like a worm in your path").
- **Dialogue about the West.** Izdubar asks if the narrator comes from the West and whether the sun sinks into the sea or touches the land, and whether there is an immortal land where the sun is reborn. The narrator says there is empty space beyond the sea; **the earth is round and turns around the sun; the sun is an unspeakably distant body in unending space.** Izdubar goes pale with "suffocating fear": "I am mortal, and I shall never reach the sun, and never reach immortality?" He smashes his axe on the rock and curses the sun, hurls the shard at it, collapses and sobs like a child.
- **The poison.** "Miserable worm, where did you suckle on this poison?" The narrator calls it science ("In our country we are nurtured on it from youth... we are all somewhat poisoned"). Izdubar: no being had cut him down, but the worm's poison lamed him "to the marrow... stronger than the army of Tiamat"; he lies as if paralyzed: "here lies your son, cut down by the invisible serpent's bite in his heel."
- **Two kinds of truth.** Izdubar: "Is poison truth? Or is truth poison?" Do his astrologers and priests not speak truth? The narrator: "It is certainly a truth, only it runs contrary to our truth." **Our truth comes from the knowledge of outer things; the truth of your priests comes from inner things.** Izdubar half-sits: "That was a salutary word."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.99 the Western lands in Egyptian myth are the land of the dead. n.100 Nietzsche (*The Gay Science*) on thinking arising from impulses that acted as poisons. n.101 Tiamat in Babylonian myth, mother of the gods who made war with a demon army [p.309].

### Fire, matches, flight [pp.309 to 310]
- At dusk the narrator makes a fire. Izdubar asks how he made fire "so swiftly and mysteriously"; **matches**. Also flying machines, a **timepiece**. Izdubar: "You certainly come from the blessed Western lands. Are you immortal?" "There is nothing more mortal than we are." Science has no method against death. Izdubar: "this science is the awful magic that has lamed me." The narrator: we grew accustomed to the poison; "what we've lost in terms of force, we've rediscovered many times through mastering the force of nature."
- Izdubar: "I draw my own force from the force of nature. I leave the secret force to the cowardly conjurers and womanly magicians." He admits the narrator's "magic" has touched him terribly. **We "had to swallow the poison of science"** or we would be lamed like Izdubar; "everyone, even the strongest, and even the eternal Gods, perish because of it." If life is dear, we sacrifice some life force rather than face certain death.
- Izdubar, suspicious that the country is "desolate, full of paralysis and renunciation," yearns for the East "where the pure source of our life-giving wisdom flows." Asks if the narrator's people have Gods: "No, words are all we have." Are the words powerful? "So they claim, but one notices nothing of this." "Science has taken from us the capacity of belief" [n.102: the issue of science vs. belief is central to Jung's psychology of religion; he cites "Psychology and religion" 1938].
- On how they live: "with one foot in the cold and one foot in the hot, and for the rest, come what may." Dark; unhappy; the narrator journeys East "to seek the light that we lack." Izdubar warns: "beware of such an overpowering light. You could go blind just as we all are somewhat blind." "I long for your truth." "As I long for the Western lands. I warn you." They fall asleep by the fire.

### Reflective passages: the serpent and the God; the mighty fallen [pp.310 to 312]
- **The three directions.** "I wandered toward the South and found the unbearable heat of solitude with myself; I wandered toward the North and found the cold death from which all the world dies. I withdrew to my Western land... and began to suffer from the sun's empty darkness. And I threw everything from me and wandered toward the East... I went to the East like a child. I did not ask, I simply waited."
- **The pass of heaviness and the two sides.** The path of life led "beyond the rejected opposites, united in smooth and extremely painful sides of the way": it burned and froze his soles. "I unsuspectingly absorb what I reject. What I accept enters that part of my soul which I do not know." The serpent whose head you crush poisons you through the wound in your heel. **"The outer opposition is an image of my inner opposition."** Outer oppositions are easy to overcome; they burn only the soles.
- **The meeting at the border.** At the highest point a miracle: one from the East hurried toward him, striving toward the sinking light. "I wanted light, he wanted night. I wanted to rise, he wanted to sink." A series of contrasts: dwarfish/enormous; lamed by knowledge/blinded by fullness of light; he from light, I from darkness; strong/weak; God/serpent; ancient/utterly new; unknowing/knowing; fantastic/sober; brave/cowardly. "We were both astonished to see one another on the border between morning and evening."
- **Mythic reading.** The narrator describes growing up as a green tree and mocking fallen heroes, "so I did not anticipate the Powerful, Blind, and Immortal One, who wandered longingly after the sinking sun, who wanted to cleave the ocean down to its bottom so he could descend into the source of life." That which hurries toward the rising is small; that which approaches the descent is great (the narrator comes from the depths of descent). **The worm aims at the heel of the Powerful One "and will prepare him for the descent that he needs."** "A little poison and the great one falls." The words of the one who rises "taste bitter" and are fatal to Gods.
- **Dearest friend.** "Alas, he is my dearest, most beautiful friend": how closely akin is the serpent and the God. "The word which was our deliverer has become a deadly weapon, a serpent that secretly stabs." The narrator and the Powerful One block each other's way: if he went the narrator's way he would fall into blindness, as the Powerful One fell into paralysis to escape his own. "I seem to be denied power, while he is denied rebirth." "I had felled the strongest, and the God climbs down to mortality."
- **"The Mighty One fell, he lies on the ground."** Counsel: **"Power must subside for the sake of life. The circumference of outer life should be made smaller."** More secrecy, solitary fires, caverns, dark forests, sparse settlements, quiet streams, silent nights, small ships and carriages, "secure in dwellings the rare and precious." "Hurrying becomes impossible, patience grows."
- **The solitary fire.** "The noise of the days of the world falls silent, and the warming fire blazes inside." At the fire the shades of those gone before wail and tell of the past. "Come to the solitary fire, you blind and lame ones and hear of both kinds of truth: the blind will be lamed and the lamed will be blinded." The fire "conquers suffering and shatters need." "The blind shall be lamed, so that he will not run into the abyss, and the lamed shall be blind, so that he will not look at things beyond his reach with longing and contempt." Both should again "respect the holy fire" and the words that "encircle the flames."
- **Logos as poison.** The ancients called the saving word the Logos, "an expression of divine reason"; there was so much unreason in man that he needed reason to be saved. "If one waits long enough, one sees how the Gods all change into serpents and underworld dragons in the end. This is also the fate of the Logos: in the end it poisons us all." Some have reason in thinking, others in feeling; both serve Logos and in secret worship the serpent. Self-subjugation (shackling, whipping) "crushed yourself but not overcome yourself" and helps the Powerful One's paralysis; he would like to force the Logos on you and others. "Give him a taste of Logos."
- **Hunting the God.** If you live as who you are, he will come impetuously against you. Remember your terrible weapon. You will be "cunning, terrible, and cold if you make the beautiful and much loved fall. But you should not kill him." **"Bind the holy Sebastian to a tree and slowly and rationally shoot arrow after arrow into his twitching flesh."** Each arrow that strikes him spares one of your "dwarfish and lame brothers." The frequent misunderstanding: "Men always want to destroy the beautiful and much loved outside of themselves, but never within themselves."
- **The God from the East.** He "came to me from the East, from just that place which I was seeking to reach." The narrator saw his power; recognized the blindness of his striving "which worked against my desire" and "opened his eyes and lamed his powerful limbs with a poisoned stab." "He lay crying like a child, as that which he was, a child, a primordial grown child that required human Logos." Compassion seized the narrator: "He whom I sought I now possessed. The East could give me nothing other than him, the sick and fallen one."
- **Half the way.** You need to undertake only half of the way; he undertakes the other half. If you go beyond him, blindness; if he goes beyond you, paralysis. **"Divinity and humanity should remain preserved, if man should remain before the God, and the God remain before man. The high-blazing flame is the middle way; whose luminous course runs between the human and the divine."**
- **The God is blind because his face became human.** "The human is the face of the Godhead." If the God approaches, plead for your life: "the God is loving horror." The ancients said "it is terrible to fall into the hands of the living God" (n.108: Hebrews 10:31): they spoke from nearness to the ancient forest, turned green like trees and ascended far to the East, and fell into those hands; they learned to kneel, beg, live in servile fear. But he who saw him "the terrible beautiful one with his black velvet eyes and the long eyelashes, the eyes that do not see but merely gaze lovingly and fearfully" has learned to cry and whimper "so that he can at least reach the ear of the Godhead. Only your fearful cry can stop the God. And then you see that the God also trembles... **The God is afraid of man.**"
- **Staying with the lamed God.** "If my God is lamed, I must stand by him." The brother "abided and grew in the light while I was in the darkness and fed myself with poison." The great hunter shoots at ever more distant goals until he tries to capture the sun; then your longing for light awakens and you meet "the worm of the shadows." **"His essence is blind excessive longing and tempestuous force. My essence is seeing limitation and the incapacity of cleverness. He possesses in abundance what I lack."** The narrator will not let the "Bull God, who once wounded Jacob's hip and whom I have now lamed" go; "I want to make his force my own." "We miss nothing more than divine force": we say "this should be achieved" and look around embarrassed to see whether anything happens; "Something always happens, but we do not happen, since our God is sick. We have seen him dead with the venomous gaze of the Basilisk on his face." "My life would have broken in half had I failed to heal my God. Hence I abided with him in the long cold night."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.103: the Draft adds "This is what I saw in the dream." n.104: cross-reference to Liber Secundus ch. 4 (p. 268f) on the Logos. n.105: in *Psychological Types* (1921) Jung treats thinking and feeling as the rational functions. n.106: the Draft adds a Davidic/Goliath line; Jung on the Babylonian creation myth: Marduk slays Tiamat and makes the world from her (so "the mighty huntsman" corresponds to Marduk). n.107: St. Sebastian, third-century martyr, depicted bound to a tree and shot with arrows; earliest representation in Sant'Apollinare Nuovo, Ravenna. n.108 Hebrews 10:31. n.109 Jacob wrestling the angel (Genesis 32:24-29) quoted at length. n.110 Image 44/45 legend: *Atharva-veda* 4,1,4, a charm to promote virility; the editor connects it to the healing of Izdubar, the wounded bull god (long quotation in the source, summarized here) [pp.309 to 312].

## 7. Second Day (Cap. ix) [pp.312 to 315]

*(Dated Jan 9, 1914. Draft: "I have slept little; unclear dreams upset me more than they have prompted the redeeming word.")*

### The dilemma and the fantasy [pp.312 to 313]
- No dream gave the saving word; Izdubar lay silent and stiff until daybreak. The narrator paces the ridge, looks back at his Western lands "where there is so much knowledge and so much possibility of help." No one would travel the hot-cold path; he fears to return to it; and what of the dangers in the East ("I do not want to go blind")? "Yes, if I were powerful like Izdubar. What use is science here?"
- Toward evening he asks Izdubar for help; Izdubar: "Let it be, may death come when it will." The narrator says swift wagons could help in his land; Izdubar: in my land your poisoned barb would not have reached me. On the East: "when you reach the plains... you will meet the powerful sun which will blind you"; by night "serpents and dragons crawl out of their holes." "Useless! Nothing is gained if you die."
- **The speech in the narrator's mind:** Izdubar is "hardly real in the ordinary sense, but is a fantasy." He appeals to Izdubar: "I think that you are not at all real, but only a fantasy." Izdubar is horrified: "It is murderous. Do you even mean to declare me unreal now that you have lamed me so pitifully?" The narrator softens: only "as real as a fantasy." Izdubar: "You are a tormenting devil." Narrator: "The hand of the doctor does not seek to torment even if it causes grief." **Name and essence:** "One often gives the sick new names to heal them, for with the new name, they come by a new essence. Your name is your essence." Izdubar (agreeing that his priests say this too): "If it helps, yes" he will admit he is a fantasy.
- **The inner voice:** a fantasy "cannot be simply negated and treated with resignation either. It calls for action." Since he is a fantasy he is "considerably more volatile." The narrator tells Izdubar he has become light as a feather and lifts him; Izdubar is "lighter than air" and the narrator has to struggle to keep his feet on the ground. Izdubar: "That was a masterstroke." "I am going to carry you down into the Western land. My comrades will happily accommodate such a large fantasy."
- **Descent.** He climbs down carrying Izdubar, nearly lifted by the wind. At the valley floor the hot-cold way no longer touches him: **an East wind blows him through the narrow rocks and across fields** with no contact with the painful way.
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.113: the Draft continues "thus spoke another voice in me, like an echo" [p.313].

### The relics, the town, the egg [pp.313 to 314]
- They meet Ammonius and the Red One ahead, who turn around and run off into the fields with horrified cries. Izdubar: "Who are these misshapen ones? Are these your comrades?" The narrator: "These are not men, they are so-called relics of the past which one still often encounters in the Western lands. They used to be very important. They're now used mostly as shepherds."
- The narrator avoids the town where "the enlightened live"; they are "dangerous" and "cook the strongest poisons," paralyzed, "wrapped in a brown poisonous vapor and can only move with artificial means." At night he brings Izdubar to a secluded garden and house. The door is too small for the giant; **"a fantasy takes up no space!"** He squeezes Izdubar to the size of an egg, puts him in his pocket and enters the welcoming house for Izdubar to "find healing."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.114: Jung told Aniela Jaffe that some of the fantasies were driven by fear, such as the devil chapter and the Gilgamesh-Izdubar chapter; it seemed stupid to have to find a way to help the giant, but he felt he would have failed if he had not; he "paid for the ridiculous solution through realizing that he had captured a God"; many of these fantasies were "a hellish combination of the sublime and the ridiculous" (*Memories, Dreams, Reflections*, pp. 147 to 148) [p.314].

### Reflective passages [pp.314 to 315]
- **Salvation by calling the God a fantasy.** "Thus my God found salvation. He was saved precisely by what one would actually consider fatal, namely by declaring him a figment of the imagination." Gods have often been thought dispatched that way ("a serious mistake"); the God "did not pass away, but became a living fantasy, whose workings I could feel on my own body": heaviness faded, hot and cold pain stopped, "the wind carried me lightly like a feather, while I carried the giant."
- "One used to believe that one could murder a God. But the God was saved, he forged a new axe in the fire, and plunged again into the flood of light of the East to resume his ancient cycle." (n.117: refers to the next chapter.) But "we clever men crept around lamed and poisoned, and did not even know that we lacked something."
- **St. Christopher.** The giant bore the Christ child with difficulty; the narrator, small as a child, bore a giant and was lifted. "Christ himself said, 'My yoke is sweet, and my burden is light'" (Matthew 11:30). **"We should not bear Christ as he is unbearable, but we should be Christs."** "This tangible and apparent world is one reality, but fantasy is the other reality." As long as the God is outside us, apparent and tangible, "he is unbearable and hopeless. But if we turn the God into fantasy, he is in us and is easy to bear. The God outside us increases the weight of everything heavy, while the God within us lightens everything heavy." Hence all Christophers have stooped backs and short breath.
- **Instructions to "you" about carrying the God [p.314 to 315].** Many have wanted help for their sick God and were devoured by serpents and dragons on the way to the land of the sun; they perished in the overbright day and became "dark men" blinded, who speak of the light but see little. "Take the God with you": if you do not have him with you in the West he will come at night with clanking armor and a battle axe; if you do not have him in the East you will tread on the divine worm that waits for your heel. "You gain everything from the God whom you bear, but not his weapon, since he crushed it." "He who conquers needs weapons. But what else do you want to conquer?" The earth is round, hangs like a drop in the cosmos; you will not reach the sun or conquer the sea, the polar snow, the desert; "above all you must conquer death. So do not be a fool, throw down your weapon... Armor is enough... God's armor will make you invulnerable and invisible to the worst fools."
- **Bear the God into the dark land** (where people "rub their eyes each morning and yet always see only the same thing"), not like the blinded who "try to illuminate the darkness with lanterns which it does not comprehend," but secretly, to a hospitable roof. **Do not wait until men "hack your God to pieces"; embrace him lovingly "until he has taken on the form of his first beginning."** Let no human eye see him ill and powerless. "Your fellow men are animals without knowing it": harmless creatures of "dark Mother Earth" until the God appears; then they rave and attack each other, "since one senses the approaching God in the other." Do not speak or show the God; sit in a solitary place and sing incantations: "Set the egg before you, the God in his beginning. And behold it. And incubate it with the magical warmth of your gaze."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.115 and n.116: the Draft says the God was "declared to be a fantasy; and it was thus assumed that he had been dealt with," and that men believed "there is no such thing as a fantasy; and if we declared something to be fantastic, then it would be well and truly destroyed" (Jung 1932 on contemporary disparagement of fantasy). n.118: St. Christopher ("Christ bearer") the third-century martyr who carried a child across a river; the child, heavier than anyone, revealed himself as Christ bearing the world's sins. n.119 Matthew 11:30. n.120: "as Izdubar came to Jung" [pp.314 to 315].

## 8. The Incantations (Cap. x) [pp.315 to 317]

*(The chapter title is missing in the calligraphic volume and is supplied from the Draft, n.121. Images 50 to 64 depict the regeneration of Izdubar, n.122.)*

This chapter is a ritual cycle of short verses, set around illustrations, in the voice of the narrator tending the egg. It is structured in stanzas separated by images.

- **Christmas nativity self-identification [p.315].** "Christmas has come. The God is in the egg." The narrator has prepared an expensive red rug from the land of morning. He identifies himself with every figure of the nativity: **the mother** (a simple maiden who gave birth and did not know how), **the careful father**, **the shepherd**, **the holy animal** that stood astonished, **the wise man from the East**, "and I am the egg that surrounds and nurtures the seed of the God in me." (n.123 Luke 2:8-11; n.124 Matthew 2:1-2.)
- **Birth pangs and attributes of the God [p.315].** "The solemn hours lengthen. And my humanity is wretched and suffers torment. Since I am a giver of birth." The God is: "the eternal emptiness and the eternal fullness. Nothing resembles him and he resembles everything. Eternal darkness and eternal brightness. Eternal below and eternal above. Double nature in one. Simple in the manifold. Meaning in absurdity. Freedom in bondage. Subjugated when victorious. Old in youth. Yes in no." (n.125: these attributes are elaborated as the attributes of Abraxas in the second and third sermons in Scrutinies, p. 349.)
- **Verses to the "light of the middle way, enclosed in the egg"** [p.315, Image 53]: embryonic, "full of ardor, oppressed," "as heavy as stone, hardened. Molten, transparent. Streaming bright, coiled on itself."
- **"Amen" series [p.315, Image 54]:** "Amen, you are the lord of the beginning... the star of the East... the flower that blooms over everything... the deer that breaks out of the forest... the song that sounds far over the water... the beginning and the end." Image 55: "One word that was never spoken. One light that was never lit up. An unparalleled confusion. And a road without end." Image 56: "I forgive myself these words, as you also forgive me for wanting your blazing light." (n.126: Jung's "Dreams" notebook of Jan 3, 1917 notes "Lib. nov. snake image III incent" as stimulus. n.127: legend "brahmanaspati," from the *Rig-Veda* via Eggeling in the *Sacred Books of the East*. n.128: the solar barge in Egypt, the sun god's struggle against Apophis, the Amduat; Jung on the sea-monster battle as the attempt to free ego-consciousness from the unconscious.)
- **The invitation [p.315, Image 57 to p.316, Image 58].** "Rise up, you gracious fire of old night. I kiss the threshold of your beginning. My hand prepares the rug and spreads abundant red flowers before you. Rise up my friend, you who lay sick, break through the shell." A meal, gifts, dancers, a house, servants; herds driven; a cup of red wine; fruit on golden dishes; "We knock at your prison and lay our ears against it. The hours lengthen, tarry no longer." "We are wretched without you and our song is worn out."
- **Obedience litany [p.316].** "We open every door for you. We bend our knees where you want us to. We go to all points of the compass... We carry up what is below, and we turn what is above into what is below... We [...] evolve around ourselves again and comprehend and live the understandable. We love and live the loved, true to your law."
- **"Come to us" refrain.** "Come to us, we who are willing from our own will... who understand you from our own spirit... who will warm you at our own fire... who will heal you with our own art... who will produce you out of our own body. Come, child, to father and mother."
- **"We asked" litany [Image 59, 60].** "We asked earth. We asked Heaven. We asked the sea. We asked the wind. We asked the fire. We looked for you with all the peoples... all the kings... all the wise... in our own heads and hearts. And we found you in the egg." (n.130: image legend "hiranyagarbha," the Vedic golden seed from which Brahma was born; Jung's copy of the *Sacred Books of the East* vol. 32 has only the opening hymn "To the Unknown God" cut; a marked passage in the *Maitrayana-Brahmana-Upanishad* names the Self "Hiranyagarbha.")
- **Sacrificial self-offering [pp.316 to 317, Image 61].** "I have slain a precious human sacrifice for you, a youth and old man. I have cut my skin with a knife. I have sprinkled your altar with my own blood. I have banished my father and mother so that [...] and made everything great that was small. **I exchanged my furthest goal for the nearest, and so I am ready.**"
- **"I am not ready" [pp.316 to 317, HI 62].** The narrator is not ready because he has not accepted "that which chokes my heart": the enclosing of the God in the egg. He is glad the endeavor succeeded but his fear made him forget the hazards: "I love and admire the powerful... and yet I lamed, carried, and made him smaller with ease." "These are the powers that make you afraid and conquer you; these have been your Gods and your rulers since time immemorial: yet you can put them in your pocket. What is blasphemy compared to this? ... it is not worth blaspheming against an egg that one carries in one's pocket." He hates the God's pitifulness; nothing stands firm: you touch yourself and turn to dust; you touch the God and he hides in the egg; "you force the gates of Hell: the sound of cackling masks and the music of fools approaches you. You storm Heaven: stage scenery totters and the prompter in the box falls into a swoon. You notice: you are not true, it is not true above, it is not true below... Wherever you grasp is air, air, air." **"That is the demise of the Gods: man puts them in his pocket... Nothing remains of the Gods other than an egg."** He considers destroying the last one and exterminating the race of Gods, but asks: how did this happen? He felled the Great One, mourned him, loved him because no mortal being rivals him. "Out of love I devised the trick that relieved him of heaviness... I took from him, out of love, form and corporeality. I enclosed him lovingly in the maternal egg."
- **Love for the child-God [p.317].** "Should I slay him, the defenseless one whom I loved? Should I shatter the delicate shell of his grave and expose him to the weightlessness and unboundedness of the winds of the world? ... Why do I love him? I do not want to tear the love for the Great One from my heart. I want to love my God, the defenseless and hopeless one. I want to care for him, like a child." "Are we not sons of the Gods? Why should Gods not be our children? If my father the God should die, a God child should arise from my maternal heart." **"Only he who loves the God can make him fall, and the God submits to his vanquisher and nestles in his hand and dies in the heart of him who loves him and promises him birth."** Prayer: "Grow in the egg of the East, nourish yourself from my love... We do not need your power but life. What does power avail us? We do not want to rule. We want to live, we want light and warmth." "A sunless spirit becomes the parasite of the body. But the God feeds the spirit."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.129: Jung's "Dreams" notebook for Jan 17, 1917 records an avalanche dream, a drawing with red spots on p. 58 of Liber Novus, and a reading about sunspots; a Black Book 6 paraphrase (a worm crawling to Heaven eating the seven blue heavens with a tongue of fire; Jung told to hide in stone because "the God is a terrible fire"). n.131: the monster's face resembles HI 29. n.132: "Dreams" notebook Feb 4, 1917: "Started work on the Opening of the Egg (Image)." n.133: Image legend "Satapatha-brahmana 2,2,4" (Prajapati produces Agni, offers himself to Agni and saves himself from Death; the Agnihotra is a sunrise/sunset fire ritual) [pp.315 to 317].

## 9. The Opening of the Egg (Cap. xi) [pp.317 to 319]

*(Draft: "Third Day." Dated Jan 10, 1914.)*

### Narrative [p.317]
- "On the evening of the third day, I kneel down on the rug and carefully open the egg. Something resembling smoke rises up from it and suddenly Izdubar is standing before me, **enormous, transformed, and complete.**" His limbs are whole; he seems awakened from deep sleep.
- Izdubar speaks as in a radiant dream (in an italic verse): "Where am I? How narrow it is here, how dark, how cool, am I in the grave?" He had been "outside in the universe" under an endlessly dark star-glittering sky, in passion of yearning; streams of fire broke from his body; he surged through flames; "I swam in a sea that wrapped me in living fires... I was ancient and perpetually renewing myself... hovering around myself amidst glowing clouds... as raining embers... Embracing and rejecting myself in a boundless game. **Where was I? I was completely sun.**"
- "Oh Izdubar! Divine one! You are healed!" "Healed? Was I ever sick? Who speaks of sickness? I was sun, completely sun. **I am the sun.**" An inexpressible light breaks from his body; the narrator covers his face; asks forgiveness for carrying him. All quiet and dark; the empty shell on the rug; everything around is "utterly plain and utterly real... Here reigned eternal light, immeasurable and overpowering."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.135: Black Book 3 (Jan 10): Jung reflects that "Izdubar's fate is grotesque and tragic, for that is what our most precious life is"; cites F. Th. Vischer's *Auch Einer* (also *Psychological Types* 1921 on Vischer); "What lies in the middle is the truth. It has many faces." n.136: Roscher on Izdubar as associated with the sun-god; the classic pattern of incubation of a solar myth; Frobenius's *Das Zeitalter des Sonnengottes* on the widespread motif of immaculate conception and the sun-god incubated in an egg. n.137: *Psychological Types* on the renewed God: "a renewed attitude... a recovery of life, because psychologically God always denotes the greatest value, thus the greatest sum of the libido."

### Reflective passages: the exchange of force; formation; evil [pp.317 to 319]
- **The God leaves the egg [p.317 to 318].** The God left the egg, healed, and "when I thought that I had caught the mighty one and held him in my cupped hands, he was the sun itself." The narrator recounts: he wandered East wanting to rise like the sun; it came toward him and told him he could not reach the beginning; he lamed the one who rushed down to set with the sun; but "I caught the sun without realizing it and carried it in my hand." "He who wanted to go down with the sun found me through his downgoing. **I became his nocturnal mother who incubated the egg of the beginning.** And he rose up, renewed, reborn to greater splendor."
- **The transfer of force [p.318].** "While he rises, however, I go down." When he conquered the God, the God's force streamed into him; but when the God rested in the egg, his own force went into the God. When the God rose, he "took my life with him": the narrator lay in the "frightful cool of the shadows of the earth," sinking "to the lowest darkness." "My soul swam like a fish in his sea of fire." He lay "like a child-bearer cruelly mauled and bleeding her life into the child." The God "had taken my wings from me, he had robbed me of the swelling force of my muscles, and the power of my will disappeared with him." "Cruelly and unthinkably the sunbird spread its wings and flew up into infinite space. I was left with the broken shells and the miserable casing."
- **Woe to the mother who bears a God [p.318].** "If she gives birth to a wounded and pain-stricken God, a sword will pierce her soul. But if she gives birth to an unblemished God, then Hell will open to her, from which monstrous serpents will rise convulsively." "Birth is difficult, but a thousand times more difficult is the hellish afterbirth." All "the dragons and monstrous serpents of eternal emptiness follow behind the divine son." What remains of human nature when the God has matured: "Everything incompetent, everything powerless, everything eternally vulgar... the afterbirth of the God and his hellish and dreadfully deformed brother." (n.138: in the next chapter Jung finds himself in Hell.)
- **Why the God suffers; suffering from evil [p.318].** "The God suffers when man does not accept his darkness." Men need a suffering God as long as they suffer from evil. "To suffer from evil means: you still love evil and yet love it no longer. You still hope to gain something, but you do not want to look closely for fear that you might discover that you still love evil." **"You do not suffer from evil because you recognize it, but because it affords you secret pleasure."** As long as your God suffers, you sympathize with him and with yourself, "spare your Hell and prolong his suffering." When the God draws near, "your essence starts to seethe and the black mud of the depths whirls up."
- **Emptiness and fullness; formation and evil [pp.318 to 319].** "Man stands between emptiness and fullness. If his strength combines with fullness, it becomes fully formative... If his strength combines with emptiness, it has a dissolving and destructive effect... Combined thus human force turns emptiness into evil." Through constant shaping, you lose force, since all force becomes tied to the formed shape; where you think you are rich, you are poor "and you stand amidst your forms like a beggar." The blinded man is seized by desire to give shape to things and compels others into service. **At this moment you need evil**: when strength is ending and desire sets in, withdraw it from what is formed into your emptiness to dissolve the formation and regain freedom. "You cannot dissolve good with good. You can dissolve good only with evil." "You are entirely unable to live without evil."
- **The image within.** Your shaping first produces an image inside you, then, through the image, an outer form that can exist without you and outlive you. Your strength is tied to the inner image, not directly to the outer work. Dissolving the formation with evil destroys the *image within*, not the outer shape. "Hence their formation causes many good persons to bleed to death, because they cannot attend to evil in the same measure." The good person who has lost force to formation will bind others to his work with unconscious cunning, becoming "bad in their goodness without knowing it," more selfish; they destroy their own work and make enemies of those they forced into service; there are plenty of slaves "who yearn for nothing more strongly than to be alienated from themselves under a good pretext."
- **Hatred and acceptance of evil [p.319].** Hating evil binds you to it as much as loving it. "Evil is to be accepted. What we want remains in our hands. What we do not want, and yet is stronger than us, sweeps us away." "We probably have to accept our evil without love and hate, recognizing that it exists and must have its share in life. In doing so, we can deprive it of the power it has to overwhelm us."
- **After creating a God [p.319].** When our whole force goes into the design of a God, we are filled with a desire to rise with the divine sun; but we forget we are "hollow forms," "sluggish matter" not entitled to share in divinity. Matter's misery creeps up "like a terrible suffering or an inescapable devilish persecution." We then believe the God calls us and make desperate attempts to follow him or preach to others to force them to follow, "to their and our detriment." "Who could suspect that he who has made the God is himself condemned to Hell?" The right way: **accept emptiness, destroy the image of the form within us, negate the God, and descend into the abyss and awfulness of matter.** The God as our work stands outside us and needs no help; "a created work that perishes again immediately once we turn away from it is not worth anything, even if it were a God."
- **Where the God is after creation [p.319].** The God is in the spiritual world, "no less valuable than the outer physical world." "Thus your soul is your own self in the spiritual world." The spiritual world, as abode of spirits, is also an outer world: you have thoughts that belong to you and obey only you, and thoughts and "beings of thought" that neither belong to you nor obey you. You give birth to beings of thought that separate from you and live their own lives, as children do. As we leave our children when old and give our body to the earth, "I separate myself from my God, the sun, and sink into the emptiness of matter and obliterate the image of my child in me... I henceforth animate the emptiness of matter from which the formation of evil grows."
- **Nature.** "Nature is playful and terrible. Some see the playful side and dally with it... Others see the horror and cover their heads and are more dead than alive. **The way does not lead between both, but embraces both. It is both cheerful play and cold horror.**" (Images 69 to 72 follow; n.139: Jung's "Dreams" Feb 15, 1917: "Finished copying the opening scene. The most wonderful feeling of renewal. Back to scientific work today, Types!"; n.140: blue and yellow circles similar to Image 60; n.141: possibly the picture Tina Keller recalled in which Jung said three intertwined snakes show how he, Emma Jung and Toni Wolff struggled with "this problem.") [pp.317 to 319]

## 10. Hell (Cap. xii) [pp.319 to 321]

*(Dated Jan 12, 1914: "the second night after the creation of my God.")*

### The vision [pp.319 to 320]
- A gloomy vault, floor of damp stone slabs, a central column hung with ropes and axes. At its foot "an awful serpentlike tangle of human bodies": a **young maiden with wonderful red-gold hair**, half over a man of devilish appearance (head bent back, a thin streak of blood down his forehead, the most powerful of three), and two similar daimons draped over her feet and body, faces inhuman, muscles taut, bodies sleek like snakes. **The maiden's hand holds a small silver fishing rod driven into the eye of the devil.** They tried to torture her to death; she defended herself with extreme despair and pierced the evil one's eye with the little hook; if he moves she will tear it out. A voice speaks: "The evil one cannot make a sacrifice, he cannot sacrifice his eye, victory is with the one who can sacrifice."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.143: Jung's marginal note to the calligraphic volume: "Satapatha-brahmanam 2,2,4" (the same inscription as Image 64). n.142: Jan 12, 1914 [p.319 to 320].

### Reflective passages [pp.320 to 321]
- **Soul in the power of evil.** "The vision vanished. I saw that my soul had fallen into the power of abysmal evil." Here no prayers or pious words help; no father, mother, right, wall, armor. "Because I wanted to give birth to my God, I also wanted evil. He who wants to create an eternal fullness will also create eternal emptiness." Nietzsche's "one must have chaos in one to give birth to a dancing star" (n.144, Zarathustra's prologue, underlined in Jung's copy). **"I wanted my God for the sake of grace and disgrace. Hence I also want my evil."** "If my God were not overpowering, neither would be my evil." He wants to go to Hell for the sake of the God as a mother would give her life for her child; he enters "the unequal battle."
- **The eye of the evil one.** Nothing is more valuable to the evil one than his eye, since "only through his eye can emptiness seize gleaming fullness." Emptiness craves fullness and drinks it with the eye. "The devil knows what is beautiful, and hence he is the shadow of beauty and follows it everywhere, awaiting the moment when the beautiful, writhing great with child, seeks to give life to the God." "Nothing is sacred to him except his eye." He is invulnerable, but nothing protects his eye.
- **The human shadow.** (A passage on the devilishness of human nature, addressed to "you" and a friend.) "I recognize the fearful devilishness of human nature. I cover my eyes before it. I put out my hand to fend it off if anyone wants to approach me for fear that my shadow could fall on him, or his shadow could fall on me." Your smile may betray "the frightfulness whose unsuspecting messenger you are... your bloodthirsty tiger growls softly, your poisonous serpent hisses secretly." The shadows wait for twilight to strangle you and me "with all the daimons of the night." A vision of intimacy followed by a cord around the neck, a nail through the temple, being dragged by the feet and gnawed by hounds.
- **The gulf and the bridge.** "No one should be astonished that men are so far removed from one another that they cannot understand one another... One should be much more surprised that men believe they are close... **Two things are yet to be discovered. The first is the infinite gulf that separates us from one another. The second is the bridge that could connect us.** Have you considered how much unsuspected animality human company makes possible?"
- **The fish hook.** When the soul fell to evil it was defenseless "except for the weak fishing rod which it could use, again with its power, to pull the fish from the sea of emptiness." The evil one's eye sucked in the soul's force; only the will remained, "that small fish hook." Because it wanted evil, it held the hook; "he who does not want evil will have no chance to save his soul from Hell." Such a person remains in the upper world as "a shadow of himself" while his soul languishes in the dungeons of daimons; higher circles of the inner world remain unattainable for him.
- **On not redeeming those who err.** "Nature also dungs its fields with men." "Take in the seeker, but do not go out seeking those who err. What do you know about their error? Perhaps it is sacred... Do not look back and regret nothing. You see many near you fall? You feel compassion? But you should live your life, since then at least one in a thousand will remain. You cannot halt dying." Otherwise you become a prophet and try to redeem the irredeemable.
- **Why not tear out the eye.** The evil one has many eyes; losing one is losing none; and the soul would fall completely under his spell. "You should not harm him, above all not his eye, since the most beautiful would not exist if the evil one did not see it and long for it. **The evil one is holy.**" Emptiness cannot sacrifice since it only lacks; "only fullness can sacrifice, since it has fullness." The narrator can sacrifice his will to evil because he has received fullness; "all strength flows back to me again, since the evil one has destroyed the image I had of the formation of the God. But the image of the God's formation in me was not yet destroyed. I dread this destruction... an unprecedented desecration of temples." "For I still did not know what it means to give birth to a God."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.145: Jung's marginal note "Chandogya-upanishad 1,2,1-7" (the High Chant and the demons; the long passage is summarized: the gods venerate the High Chant as breath, speech, sight, hearing, mind, and the demons "riddle" each with evil; only the breath in the mouth, OM, smashes the demons like a clod against a rock) [pp.319 to 321].

## 11. The Sacrificial Murder (Cap. xiii) [pp.321 to 323]

*(Handwritten Draft: "Eighth Adventure." No date given in the notes of this range; follows the Hell vision.)*

### Narrative [p.321]
- **"But this was the vision that I did not want to see, the hideous horror that I did not want to live."** Nausea; loathsome snakes in parched undergrowth; a dreary valley with arid stony defiles; weak sun in a gray sky; shriveled leaves. Among the stones: a marionette with a broken head, a small apron, and **the body of a small girl** covered with terrible wounds; one foot in a stocking and shoe, the other bare and crushed; the head a mash of blood, hair, bone and brain. A shrouded woman with a veiled face stands calmly beside the child.
- **The dialogue with the veiled woman ("S").** "What then do you say?" "This is beyond words." "Do you understand this?" "I refuse to understand such things. I can't speak about them without becoming enraged." S: "You might as well rage every day of your life, for these and similar things occur every day." The narrator: "Most of the time we don't see them... The horror is less real if all I have is knowledge."
- **The demand.** "Step nearer and you will see that the body of the child has been cut open; take out the liver." He refuses ("If someone witnessed this, they would think that I'm the murderer"). She: "I am the soul of this child. You must do this for my sake." He reaches into the still-warm visceral cavity, cuts the liver from the ligaments with his knife, and holds it out with bloody hands. S: "I thank you." Then: "You know what the liver means, and you ought to perform the healing act with it... Take a piece of the liver, in place of the whole, and eat it." He: "This is absolute madness. This is desecration, necrophilia. You make me a guilty party to this most hideous of all crimes." She: "You share in this guilt... You are a man, and a man has committed this deed." He: "I curse whoever did this for being a man, and I curse myself for being a man." S: "So, take part in his act, abase yourself and eat. I need atonement."
- **The act.** He kneels, cuts off a piece, eats it: "My gorge rises, tears burst from my eyes, cold sweat... a dull sweet taste of blood... I swallow with desperate efforts, it is impossible, once again and once again, I almost faint, it is done. The horror has been accomplished." S: "I thank you." **She throws back her veil: "a beautiful maiden with ginger hair" (the same figure as in the vision of Hell).** "Do you recognize me?" "How strangely familiar you are! Who are you?" "I am your soul."
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.147: Jung (in *Memories*, on the Liverpool dream) noted "According to an older view, the liver is the seat of life." n.148: Jung (1940) on ritual anthropophagy, sacrifice and self-sacrifice ("Transformation symbolism in the mass"). n.149: Black Book 3: "The curtain drops. What dreadful game has been played here? I realize: *Nil humanum a me alienum esse puto* (nothing human is alien to me)" (Terence); Jung to Herbert Read (1960): "as a medical psychologist ... nil humanum a me alienum esse is even my duty" [p.321].

### Reflective passages [pp.322 to 323]
- **What was accomplished [p.322].** "The sacrifice has been accomplished: the divine child, the image of the God's formation, is slain, and I have eaten from the sacrificial flesh." The child, the image of the God's formation, carried the narrator's human craving but also "all the primordial and elemental powers that the sons of the sun possess as an inalienable inheritance." The God needs all this for his genesis; when he has gone into unending space, "we need the gold of the sun. We must regenerate ourselves." **Creating a God is "a creative act of highest love"; the restoration of our human life is "an act of the Below," a great dark mystery.** Man cannot do it alone: evil does it for him, "but man must recognize his complicity in the act of evil. He must bear witness to this recognition by eating from the bloody sacrificial flesh." Through this he testifies that he is a man who "recognizes good as well as evil" and destroys the image of the God's formation by withdrawing his life force, dissociating himself from the God "for the salvation of the soul, which is the true mother of the divine child."
- **The primordial powers redeemed.** The soul bore the God while "of human nature throughout"; the primordial powers flowed into forming the God without help but dormant. "Through the sacrificial murder, I redeemed the primordial powers and added them to my soul... they are no longer dormant, but awake and active and irradiate my soul with their divine working. Through this it receives a divine attribute." The ancients taught drinking the blood and eating the flesh of the savior for healing the soul (n.151: the ritual of the mass). **"There are not many truths, there are only a few. Their meaning is too deep to grasp other than in symbols."** (n.152 refers to *Psychological Types*, 1921, on symbols.)
- **Warning to the lukewarm.** "A God who is no stronger than man, what is he? You still should taste holy dread. How would you be worthy of enjoying the wine and the bread if you have not touched the black bottom of human nature? Hence you are lukewarm and pale shadows, proud of your shallow coastlines and broad country roads. But the floodgates will be opened, there are inexorable things, from which only God can save you."
- **The fire of the sun [p.322].** The primordial force is the radiance of the sun, carried by the sons of the sun for aeons. If the soul dips into radiance she becomes remorseless as the God; "the life of the divine child, which you have eaten, will feel like glowing coals in you... a terrible, inextinguishable fire... From this you will understand that your God is alive and that your soul has begun wandering on remorseless paths. You feel that the fire of the sun has erupted in you. Something new has been added to you, a holy affliction." You cannot elude it, limit it, employ it or think about it; "you are its tool." "There is no escape. So it is that you come to know what a real God is... The fire burns right through you. That which guides forces you onto the way."
- **The God wants my life.** "The way is my own self, my own life founded upon myself. The God wants my life. He wants to go with me, sit at the table with me, work with me. Above all he wants to be ever-present." (n.153: Jung's 1909 house in Küsnacht bears the Delphic motto *Vocatus atque non vocatus deus aderit*, "called or not, the God will be present"; Jung to Eugene Rolfe, 1960: it reminds patients and himself of *Timor dei initium sapientiae*.)
- **Shame at the God.** "But I'm ashamed of my God. I don't want to be divine but reasonable. The divine appears to me as irrational craziness... an unbecoming sickness which has stolen into the regular course of my life. Yes, I even find the divine superfluous." This is followed by a **series of illustrations, Images 79 to 97** (a notation on Image 79 reads "21 VIII. 1917 fect. 14.x.17"; n.154). Several images contain runes. **Editor's apparatus on these images [pp.322 to 323]:** n.155 to n.157: the runes recur in Black Book 7, in a fantasy of Oct 7, 1917, where a figure named **Ha**, who says he is the father of Philemon and whom Jung's soul calls a black magician, shows runes and explains them in a long speech of two suns, two cones, a serpent's way between them, prisons, bridges and a hard seed, ending with "I am called Ha-Ha-Ha." The editor quotes Ha's explanations in full (very long; here summarized only as: the runes depict the relation of an upper and lower sun, cones, the serpent crossing between, and the effort to unite them). ## 12. Divine Folly (Cap. xiv) [pp.323 to 324]

*(Handwritten Draft: "Ninth Adventure, 1st Night." Dated Jan 14, 1914.)*

### The library scene [p.323 to 324]
- The narrator is in a high hall, a green curtain between two columns, a small deep room with a bluish window, two doors, left and right; he chooses **right** and enters the **reading room of a large library**. In the background sits the pale, thin librarian: the atmosphere is "scholarly ambitions, scholarly conceit, wounded scholarly vanity."
- He asks for **Thomas a Kempis's *Imitation of Christ*.** The librarian is astonished; the narrator is too. He explains that a passage struck him recently and that science "leaves us empty and sick" at times, when a book "written from the soul" means much; he reads Thomas for prayer rather than scholarship. The librarian thinks Christianity old-fashioned ("just a religion"). The narrator: "We haven't come to an end with Christianity by simply putting it aside." People set it aside at student age or younger, for dubious grounds ("the contents of belief clash with natural science or philosophy").
- **Nietzsche.** The librarian says Nietzsche has written "more than a veritable book of prayer" and Faust as substitutes. The narrator: Nietzsche's truth is "too agitated and provocative," good for those yet to be set free; "we also need a truth for those who are forced into a corner... a depressive truth, which makes man smaller and more inward." Nietzsche speaks to those needing more freedom, "not to those who clash strongly with life, who bleed from wounds." Asked about "inferiority": "I know men who need inferiority; not superiority," then "resignation." Nietzsche is "too oppositional"; "Like everything healthy and long-lasting, truth unfortunately adheres more to the middle way, which we unjustly abhor." The librarian is surprised at his "mediating position"; the narrator: "If I mediate, I certainly mediate in a very peculiar manner." The servant brings the book.
- **Editor's apparatus.** n.158 Draft title; n.159 date; n.160: *The Imitation of Christ* (early fifteenth century, ascribed to Thomas a Kempis, c. 1380-1471, of the Brethren of the Common Life and the *devotio moderna*; it urges inner spiritual life and conformity to Christ's pattern; the editor sketches the medieval debate on imitation, citing Giles Constable: imitation of Christ's divinity (deification) versus imitation of his humanity (including stigmatics)); n.161 refers to *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* [p.323 to 324].

### Reflective passage: imitating Christ [p.324]
- "The divine wants to live with me. My resistance is in vain." His thinking says: "Take as your model one that shows you how to live the divine." Our natural model is Christ. "We have stood under his law since antiquity; first outwardly, and then inwardly... We fought against Christ, we deposed him, and we seemed to be conquerors. But he remained in us and mastered us."
- "It is better to be thrown into visible chains than into invisible ones. **You can certainly leave Christianity but it does not leave you.** Your liberation from it is delusion. Christ is the way." The way ends on the cross; "we are crucified with him in ourselves. With him, we wait until we die for our resurrection. With Christ the living experience no resurrection, unless it occurs after death." (n.162: Thomas a Kempis book 2, ch. 12, on the cross. n.163: Draft continues about emulating Christ rather than imitating him: if I imitate Christ he is my goal, not my way.)
- **The paradox of imitation.** If I imitate Christ, he is always ahead and I can never reach the goal unless in him; imitation puts me outside my own time, split between Christ's life and my present life. "I must realize how Christ actually lived only his own life, and imitated no one. He did not emulate any model. If I thus truly imitate Christ, **I do not imitate anyone, I emulate no one, but go my own way**, and I will also no longer call myself a Christian." (n.164: the Draft says his own way led to the cross, "mine, which is the image of the sacrifice and of life.")
- **Christ and the prophets of the time.** A voice in him protested that his time had prophets who struggle against the yoke the past imposes. "The one demands bearing, the other discarding; the one commands submission, the other the will." (n.165: the references seem to be to Schopenhauer and Nietzsche.) "What I could not conjoin in my mind probably lends itself to living one after the other."
- **Return to life.** "I decided to cross over into lower and everyday life, my life, and to begin down there, where I stood. When thinking leads to the unthinkable, it is time to return to simple life. What thinking cannot solve, life solves, and what action never decides is reserved for thinking." The true way leads not upward but toward the depths, "since only my other leads me beyond myself. But acceptance of the other means a descent into the opposite, from seriousness into the laughable, from suffering into the cheerful, from the beautiful into the ugly, from the pure into the impure." (n.166: Draft: "Once you have considered it, you will understand the adventure that beset me the following night.") [p.324]

## 13. Nox Secunda (Cap. xv) [pp.324 to 329]

*(Second night. Dated Jan 17, 1914.)*

### The kitchen and the cook [pp.324 to 325]
- Leaving the library, the narrator turns to the left door: a **large kitchen** with a big chimney, two long tables, brass and copper pots; a large fat woman, the cook, in a checkered apron. He asks to sit, takes out his Thomas and reads. The cook mistakes him for a clergyman because of the little black book; her mother left her one on her deathbed, *The Imitation of Christ*; she prays with it in the evenings. He reads in ch. 19: "The righteous base their intentions more on the mercy of God..." (n.169). He thinks this is "the intuitive method" that Thomas recommends (n.170: Black Book 4 had "Henri Bergson"; Jung discussed Bergson and Adolf Keller's talk of March 20, 1914; July 24, 1914 Jung linked his "constructive method" with Bergson's "intuitive method"; he possessed the German translation of *L'évolution créatrice*). He thinks one can also follow one's own nose.

### The Anabaptists [p.325]
- Inner disquiet; an odd swishing and whirring; a roar like a horde of large birds; shadowlike human forms rush past: "Let us pray in the temple!" A bearded man with tousled hair and dark shining eyes stops: **"We are wandering to Jerusalem to pray at the most holy sepulcher."** "You cannot join us, you have a body. But we are dead." He is **Ezechiel, an Anabaptist**, with his fellow believers; they cannot stop but must make pilgrimage to all holy places; "we died in true belief" yet have no peace: "It always seems to me as if we had not come to a proper end with life... we forgot something important that should also have been lived." "Would you happen to know?" He reaches greedily and uncannily; the narrator: **"Let go, daimon, you did not live your animal."**
- (Editor n.172: in the Draft the speaker is "the Uncanny One." n.173: Ezekiel the sixth-century BCE prophet, whose visions Jung viewed as significant, incorporating a mandala with quaternities and "the humanization and differentiation of Yahweh" (*Answer to Job*); Anabaptists, sixteenth-century radical reformers originating in Zurich in the 1520s, rejecting infant baptism, stressing immediacy with God, violently suppressed; the first adult baptisms took place in Zollikon near Küsnacht, where Jung lived. n.174: Jung (1918, 1923, 1939) on Christianity suppressing the animal; "he did not live the animal side of himself" as Christ's "psychological sin.")

### Arrest and the asylum [pp.325 to 326]
- The cook grips his arm: "Help, what's wrong with you?" Strange people burst in, including the librarian, who laughs maliciously: "Quick, the police!" He is pushed through a crowd into a van, still clutching Thomas, and opens to ch. 13: "So long as we live here on earth, we cannot escape temptation..." (n.175). Wise Thomas always has the right answer; the Anabaptist might have made a peaceful end had he read in Cicero: *rerum omnium satietas vitae facit satietatem; satietas vitae tempus maturum mortis affert* (satiety of all things causes satiety of life; satiety of life brings the ripe time for death; n.176: Cicero *Cato Maior de Senectute*). Flanked by policemen, he is brought to a **madhouse**, a large gate, hall, a friendly superintendent, two doctors, one a small fat professor.
- **The professor.** "What's that book?" "Thomas a Kempis." Pr: "A form of religious madness... religious paranoia" (n.177: Black Book 4 reads "paranoid form of Dementia praecox"). "Nowadays, the imitation of Christ leads to the madhouse." Do you hear voices? "Today it was a huge throng of Anabaptists that swarmed through the kitchen." "Are the voices following you?" "I summoned them." The professor has the other doctor note that "hallucinations directly call up voices." The doctor objects that this is "absolutely not abnormal, but much rather the intuitive method." The professor: "The fellow also uses neologisms." Prognosis "pretty bad." The superintendent asks if he may keep the book; "a harmless prayer book." Clothes inventoried, bath, a ward; to his left a motionless man with a transfixed gaze, to his right one whose brain seems to be shrinking.
- **A reflection on madness.** "The problem of madness is profound. Divine madness, a higher form of the irrationality of the life streaming through us, at any rate a madness that cannot be integrated into present-day society, but how? What if the form of society were integrated into madness? At this point things grow dark, and there is no end in sight."

### Reflective passages: feeling, chaos, the dead [pp.326 to 329]
- **The plant that sprouts to the right then the left [p.326].** A plant sprouts a sapling on its right; when formed, the urge to grow flows back into the stem, pays an uncertain way through the dark and sprouts a new sapling on the left, an opposite direction, yet the plant grows regularly. "On the right is my thinking, on the left is my feeling." He enters the space of his feeling, previously unknown, and sees astonishing differences between his two rooms; laughs; winces at inner pain: the difference between hot and cold is too great. He leaves "the spirit of this world which has thought Christ through to the end" and steps into "that other funny-frightful realm in which I can find Christ again." (n.178: the Draft: since I was a thinker, feeling was lowest, oldest, least developed; "Overloading is not growth, which is what we need.")
- **The mercy of God [p.326].** The "imitation of Christ" led him to the master himself and his astonishing kingdom. In this realm "other laws are valid than the guidelines of my wisdom." **"The 'mercy of God,' which I had never relied on, for good practical reasons, is the highest law of action. The 'mercy of God' signifies a particular state of the soul in which I entrust myself to all neighbors with trembling and hesitation and with the mightiest outlay of hope that everything will work out well."** He cannot say what should be reached; he gropes through mist; "everything is thoroughly and convincingly accidental... contrary to my earlier way... all is error... nothing leads, as my hope sought to persuade me, but everything misleads."
- **The quiet place and the wall [pp.326 to 327].** To your shivering horror you fall into the boundless, the abyss, the inanity of eternal chaos. "Every man has a quiet place in his soul, where everything is self-evident and easily explainable," where he retreats from the confusing possibilities of life, the place about which he can say with conviction "You are nothing but..." That place is "a smooth surface, an everyday wall... a snugly sheltered and frequently polished crust over the mystery of chaos. If you break through this most everyday of walls, the overwhelming stream of chaos will flood in."
- **What chaos is.** "Chaos is not single, but an unending multiplicity. It is not formless, otherwise it would be single, but it is filled with figures that have a confusing and overwhelming effect due to their fullness." **These figures are the dead**: not only your dead (images of your past shapes) but "the thronging dead of human history, the ghostly procession of the past, which is an ocean compared to the drops of your own life span." Behind the mirror of your eyes they look greedily through the empty sockets, moaning and hoping to gather all loose ends of the ages through you. "Put your ear to that wall and you will hear the rustling of their procession." You lodged the simplest matters there "so that no one, least of all yourself, would unearth the mystery there." What you excluded and renounced "awaits you behind that wall." (n.179: marginal note "26.1.1919", apparently the date of transcription into the calligraphic volume.)
- **Those who sought the strange.** Men in history who "ensnared themselves," held captive in "wolves' lairs," who sought the highest and lowest and were wiped incomplete from the tablets of the living. One of them stands behind you, panting, besieging you in sleepless nights, sometimes taking hold of you in illness, crossing your intentions, making you "overbearing and greedy"; "He accompanies you as your evil spirit." Dark ones who roamed incognito beside those who ruled the day, devised cunning things and committed crimes for their God; **beside them place Christ, "who was the greatest among them. It was too little for him to break the world, so he broke himself."** The powers of this world did not reach him. But the narrator speaks of those who "fell prey to power, broken by force and not by themselves," whose hordes people the land of the soul and fill you with delusion and rebellion. "They forgot only one thing: they did not live their animal."
- **The animal [p.327].** "The animal does not rebel against its own kind. Consider animals: how just they are, how well-behaved... how loyal to the land... how they care for their young... There is not one that conceals its overabundance of prey and lets its brother starve... Not a one mistakenly imagines that it is an elephant when it is a mosquito. The animal lives fittingly and true to the life of its species." **"He who never lives his animal must treat his brother like an animal. Abase yourself and live your animal so that you will be able to treat your brother correctly. You will thus redeem all those roaming dead who strive to feed on the living. And do not turn anything you do into a law, since that is the hubris of power."** (n.180: Jung 1930 seminar: "in nature the animal is a well-behaved citizen... Only man is extravagant.")
- **Work with the dead [p.327].** When you open the door to the dead your horrors will afflict your brother; withdraw into solitude, since no one can counsel you in wrestling with the dead. "Do not cry for help if the dead surround you, otherwise the living will take flight, and they are your only bridge to the day. Live the life of the day and do not speak of mysteries, but dedicate the night to bringing about the salvation of the dead." Whoever "well-meaningly tears you away from the dead has rendered you the worst service." Romans 8:19-22 (the creation groaning, n.181) is quoted. "Every step upward will restore a step downward so that the dead will be delivered into freedom."
- **The creative and the evil [p.327].** "The creating of the new shrinks from the day since its essence is secret... Something evil is attached to the creation of the new, which you cannot proclaim loudly. The animal that looks for new hunting grounds cowers slinking and sniffing on dark paths." The suffering of the creative: "they carry something evil in them, a leprosy of the soul." They could praise it as a virtue, "but this would be doing what Christ does, and would therefore be his imitation. For only one was Christ and only one could violate the laws as he did." **"Fulfill that which comes to you. Break the Christ in yourself so that you may arrive at yourself and ultimately at your animal which is well-behaved in its herd." "May it suffice in terms of transgression that you do not imitate Christ, since thereby you take a step back from Christianity and a step beyond it. Christ brought salvation through adeptness, and ineptitude will save you."**
- **The dead of Christianity [pp.327 to 328].** "Have you counted the dead whom the master of sacrifice honored?" Isaiah 24:66 / the biblical passage "their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched" (n.182). **"Do penance, consider what fell victim to death for the sake of Christianity, lay it before you and force yourself to accept it. For the dead need salvation. The number of the unredeemed dead has become greater than the number of living Christians; therefore it is time that we accept the dead."** (n.183, n.185: the Draft adds a passage on a prophet "whose proximity to God had driven him insane," raging blindly against Christianity as "champion of the dead who had appointed him their spokesman"; the references are to Nietzsche.) Do not throw yourself against what has become; "if you are successful in destroying what has become, you will then turn the will of destruction against yourself." "Reverence is a blessing." "Turn to the dead, listen to their lament and accept them with love. Be not their blind spokesman." There are prophets who stoned themselves in the end. [Image 105: n.186: reproduced by Jung in 1930 in "Commentary on 'The Secret of the Golden Flower'" and again in 1952 in "Concerning mandala symbolism" as a mandala painted by a middle-aged man in treatment (central star, four human figures at cardinal points: old man, red-haired Loki or Hephaestus figure, light and dark women, rings of protozoa-like forms, sixteen globes derived from an eye motif); Jung wrote to Raymond Piper (1950) that it was by an educated man of about forty who produced it "as an at-first unconscious attempt to restore order" after an invasion of unconscious contents.]
- **New salvation restores the lost [p.328].** The new "will be built on the old and the meaning of what has become will become manifold. Your poverty in what has become you will thus deliver into the wealth of the future." What distances you from Christianity and its law of love are the dead who found no peace "since their uncompleted work has followed them." **"A new salvation is always a restoring of the previously lost."** "Did not Christ himself restore bloody human sacrifice, which better customs had expelled from sacred practice since days of old? Did he not himself reinstate the sacred practice of the eating of human sacrifice?" In your sacred practice "that which earlier laws condemned will once again be included." But Christ brought it back under "the highest law of love" so no brother would be harmed. If you have no reverence for what has become, "you will destroy the law of love" and be forced back to violent deeds, murder, wrongdoing, contempt of your brother; "confusion will rule." (n.187: Draft: "Not one title of Christian law is abrogated, but instead we are adding a new one: accepting the lament of the dead." n.188: Draft: temptation is the demand of the dead; if you know that, it becomes "the wellspring of your best work, indeed of the work of salvation"; Christ ascended leading up with him those who died prematurely under "the law of hardship and alienation and raw violence.")
- **Temptation as the demand of the dead.** "The spirits of those who die before their time will live, for the sake of our present incompleteness, in dark hordes in the rafters of our houses and besiege our ears with urgent laments, until we grant them redemption." **"What we call temptation is the demand of the dead who passed away prematurely and incomplete through the guilt of the good and of the law."** "No good is so complete that it could not do injustice and break what should not be broken."
- **The hidden work [p.328].** "We are a blinded race. We live only on the surface, only in the present, and think only of tomorrow... We want to work only with visible success. Above all we want to be paid." But "there is one necessary but hidden and strange work, a major work, which you must do in secret, for the sake of the dead. He who cannot attain his own visible field and vineyard is held fast by the dead, who demand the work of atonement from him." He must "search his soul and act in stillness at their behest and complete the mystery." "Do not look forward so much, but back and into yourself." Christ "ascends with few of the living, but many of the dead. His work was the salvation of the despised and lost, for whose sake he was crucified between two criminals." **"I suffer my agony between two madmen. I enter the truth if I descend. Become accustomed to being alone with the dead."**
- **The ancients and the dead [pp.328 to 329].** "What the ancients did for their dead! You seem to believe that you can absolve yourself from the care of the dead... since what is dead is past. You excuse yourself with your disbelief in the immortality of the soul. [...] away the sea in the outer world. You must finally understand your purpose in explaining away, namely to seek protection." (n.189: Draft: "You employ old word magic to protect yourself through superstition... nothing protects you against the chaos other than acceptance.") Closing line: **"I accepted the chaos, and in the following night, my soul approached me."** [Image 107.]
- **Editor's apparatus on Nox Secunda.** (Items n.169 to n.189 are covered in the entries above.)

## 14. Nox Tertia (Cap. xvi) [pp.329 to 330, continues in the next part]

*(Third night. Dated Jan 18, 1914. Chapter continues on PDF p. 331 and beyond; the portion in this range is the beginning.)*

### The soul on madness [p.329]
- The soul whispers urgently: "Words, words, do not make too many words. Be silent and listen: have you recognized your madness and do you admit it? Have you noticed that all your foundations are completely mired in madness?" **"You wanted to accept everything. So accept madness too. Let the light of your madness shine, and it will suddenly dawn on you. Madness is not to be despised and not to be feared, but instead you should give it life."** "If you want to find paths, you should also not spurn madness, since it makes up such a great part of your nature." The narrator: "I didn't know that this is so." Soul: "Be glad that you can recognize it, for you will thus avoid becoming its victim. Madness is a special form of the spirit and clings to all teachings and philosophies, but even more to daily life, since life itself is [...] is its mystery and its unknown law. What you call knowledge is an attempt to impose something comprehensible on life." "You have nothing to disagree with, you are in the madhouse." The fat little professor stands there; "Had I taken him for my soul?" The professor: "your speech is completely incoherent... sleep well."
- **Chaos approaches.** "Everything inside me is in utter disarray... Is chaos also a foundation?" The black billows; he sees "the ocean, the almighty nocturnal tide": a large steamer, a smoking parlor, beautifully dressed people who look at him astonished ("You look just like a ghost!"). "The sea is somewhat rough, have a hot toddy, you're seasick." "I am really in a madhouse." The professor sits at a green-covered table playing cards, laughing: "You've put all the ladies in quite a flurry this evening." The parlor erupts in laughter. "Just now I was your patient." The scene is a slide between ship's parlor and ward.
- **The fool who is Nietzsche and Christ.** A bearded, dark-eyed man comes up and says he has been there five years: "I am Nietzsche, only rebaptized, I am also Christ, the Savior, appointed to save the world, but they won't let me." Who won't? "The devil. We are in Hell... the director is the devil." He claims the professor, the devil, has "the mother of God" in his power; every evening at sundown he gets her with child; before sunrise she gives birth and all the devils kill the child gruesomely: "I distinctly hear his cries." The narrator: "pure mythology." The fool: "You're crazy and understand nothing of it." He lies down and sinks back into lassitude. (Image 109; n.192: Jung described a case of a patient at the Burghölzli in telephonic communication with the Mother of God in *The Relations between the I and the Unconscious*, 1928, CW 7 §229; n.193 Image legend: "This man of matter rises up too far in the world of the spirits, but there the spirit of the heart bores through him with a golden ray. He falls with joy and disintegrates. The serpent, who is evil, could not remain in the world of spirits.")

### The sea-sun-tree vision and the teaching on words [pp.329 to 330]
- The narrator grips his bed against the terrible waves and stares at the wall, painted a darker color below a horizontal line (a radiator like a railing) beyond which he sees the sea; **the line is the horizon**. The sun rises in red glory, solitary and magnificent, with a **cross from which a serpent hangs, or a bull split open as at a slaughterhouse, or an ass, or a ram with a crown of thorns, or the crucified one, himself.** "The sun of martyrdom has arisen and is pouring bloody rays over the sea." It rises higher, brighter, hotter, white on a blue sea; the swell subsides; summer dawn; the salty smell; a faint wide surf; the **world clock** strikes twelve; silence; everything rigid and deathly still. **A tree rises from the sea: its crown reaches to Heaven and its roots reach down into Hell.** He is alone, utterly weak: "Salvation," he whispers. A strange voice: **"There is no salvation here, you must remain calm, or you will disturb the others. It is night and the other people want to sleep."** It is the attendant; the room is dimly lit by a lamp.
- "I couldn't find the way." The attendant: "You don't need to find a way now." **"There are no paved ways into the future. We say that it is this way, and it is. We build roads by going on. Our life is the truth that we seek. Only my life is the truth, the truth above all. We create the truth by living it."**
- **The night the dams broke [p.330].** "This is the night in which all the dams broke, where what was previously solid moved, where the stones turned into serpents, and everything living froze." Is this a web of words? If so, "it is a hellish web for those caught in it."
- **Teaching on words.** "There are hellish webs of words, only words, but what are words? Be tentative with words, value them well, take safe words, words without catches, do not spin them with one another so that no webs arise, for you are [...] together. Hence the word is an image of God. The word is the greatest and the smallest that man created.**" If I fall prey to the web of words I fall prey to the greatest and the smallest; "I am at the mercy of the sea, of the inchoate waves that are forever changing place. Their essence is movement and movement is their order. He who strives against waves is exposed to the arbitrary. The work of men is steady but it swims upon chaos. The striving of men seems like lunacy to him who comes from the sea. But men consider him mad."
- **The one who has seen chaos.** "He who comes from the sea is sick. He can hardly bear the gaze of men. For to him they all seem to be drunk and foolish from sleep-inducing poisons." They want to rescue him; he would rather not join them "as one who has never seen the chaos but only talks about it." **"For him who has seen the chaos, there is no more hiding, because he knows that the bottom sways and knows what this swaying means. He has seen the order and the disorder of the endless, he knows the unlawful laws. He knows the sea and can never forget it. The chaos is terrible: days full of lead, nights full of horror."** (n.198: the Draft: "Once you have seen the chaos, look at your face... Many cross over, but they do not see the chaos; however the chaos sees them, stares at them, and imprints its features on them. And they are marked forever. Call such a one mad... he has become a wave and has lost his human side, his constancy.")
- **Christ and the chaos.** "Just as Christ knew that he was the way, the truth, and the life, in that the new torment and the renewed salvation came into the world through him, I know that chaos must come over men, and that the hands of those who unknowingly and unsuspectingly break through the thin walls that separate us from the sea are busy. For this is our way, our truth, and our life." (n.199: the preceding sentence is crossed out in the Corrected Draft; Jung's marginal note reads "φιλήμων identification" or similar, "[garbled in source]": the text gives "cpIAHMQN identification," read here as "Philemon identification," uncertain.)
- **The anointed of this time.** "Just as the disciples of Christ recognized that God had become flesh and lived among them as a man, we now recognize that **the anointed of this time is a God who does not appear in the flesh; he is no man and yet is a son of man, but in spirit and not in flesh; hence he can be born only through the spirit of men as the conceiving womb of the God.**" "What is done to this God you do to the lowest in yourself, under the law of love according to which nothing is cast out. For how else should your lowest be saved from depravity?" [Image 111 follows.] "Who should accept the lowest in you, if you do not? But he who does it not from love but from pride, selfishness, and greed, is damned." *(The text then continues on PDF p. 331 with "None of the ... therefore my own arm must save me, and my fury upheld me." ## Gaps and cautions
1. **Range boundaries.** The first page (p.298) opens in the middle of a chapter whose title and number are not in this range; the last page (p.330) ends in the middle of Nox Tertia (Cap. xvi). I did not extend either edge.
2. **Facsimile images** are not in the text; only the editor's notes about them are. I recorded image numbers and legends where given. The sequences of "[Image n]" markers inside the Incantations and after "Divine Folly" (Images 79 to 97) are only noted.
3. **Long quotations in the notes** (Bible passages, Sacred Books of the East, Black Book 7's "Ha" rune speeches in notes 155 to 157, the Chandogya Upanishad passage, Jung's long mandala description) were summarized in a line or two, not reproduced.
4. **Two-column interleaving.** The text extraction interleaves columns on most pages. I reconstructed the reading order from sense and from the folio markers; passages are in the book's order, but small slips in column attribution on pp.308, 311 and 312 are possible. Where the English text stays intelligible I did not mark it as garbled.
5. **Garbled or uncertain readings.** Several OCR slips ("Traniformations", "Drqft") were silently standardized. Note 199's marginal Greek note appears in the source as "cpIAHMQN identification"; I read it as probably "Philemon identification," but this is **[garbled in source]** and uncertain. The citation "Isaiah 24:66" in note 182 appears as given and may be a typesetting error. Printed-page references to later parts of the book (pp. 241, 268f, 347, 349) are as given in the notes.
6. **Not extracted:** nothing in the range was skipped, but all footnotes were compressed. The reflective passages are paraphrased at length but not word for word; the narrator's italicized dream-verse (Izdubar's speech after the egg opens) and the Incantations are summarized in sequence rather than quoted in full.
7. **Interpretive notes.** I gave no interpretation of my own. The `[note: ...]` items are limited to clarifications of structure and of who is speaking. Where the text calls figures "God," "Powerful One," "Bull God," etc., I used the author's terms.
