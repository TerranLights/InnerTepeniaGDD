## Overview

Von Franz presents the "process of individuation," Jung's name for the slow, mostly involuntary growth of the psyche toward a fuller, more mature personality, which becomes visible only when one follows a very long series of dreams. She builds the account as a sequence of encounters with unconscious figures, in an order she calls typical but not fixed: the first "call" (a wounding or an emptiness) [p.163-164]; the **shadow** [p.165-173]; the **anima** in a man [p.174-185] and the **animus** in a woman [p.186-192]; then the **Self**, shown through the wise old man or wise old woman, the youthful figure, the Cosmic Man, the royal couple, animals and especially stones [p.193-208]; then the **mandala** and how a person lives in relation to the Self [p.209-214]; then the **social aspect of the Self** and what the process means for groups, politics and religion [p.215-226]. Each figure is said to have a light and a dark side [p.213]. The method is empirical and illustrative: she insists that psychic material cannot be systematized except superficially and can only be described "from as many angles as possible" [p.161]. Her central practical claim is that attending to the unconscious, honestly and without a program, is the one remaining real adventure for modern people and also produces the best possible social adaptation [p.209, 220].

## 1. The pattern of psychic growth [p.157-161]

### What the chapter takes over from Jung's earlier chapter
- Jung (the book's opening chapter) introduced the personal and collective unconscious and its symbolic language. Once symbols are seen to matter, the problem becomes interpretation, and Jung showed that an interpretation is right only if it "clicks" and is meaningful to the person [p.157].
- This raised a larger question: what is the purpose of the **total dream life**, not just single dreams? Jung (who by von Franz's account interpreted at least 80,000 dreams) found that all dreams are relevant to the dreamer's life and are parts of "one great web of psychological factors" that follows a pattern. He called that pattern **the process of individuation** [p.157].
- A person who watches dreams over years sees certain figures, landscapes and situations emerge, vanish and return, changing slowly. Appropriate interpretation that influences the dreamer's conscious attitude can accelerate the change [p.157].

### The meander [p.157-158]
- Dream life makes a "meandering pattern" in which strands appear, vanish and return, with a hidden regulating or directing tendency behind it. Image (caption): a seventh-century manuscript meander; single dreams look fragmentary like the detail, but the lifetime pattern reveals psychic growth.
- A wider, more mature personality emerges and becomes visible to others. Everyday talk of "arrested development" shows we assume this growth is possible for everyone [p.158].
- It cannot be brought about by willpower; it happens involuntarily. Dreams therefore often symbolize it by the **tree**, whose slow, powerful, involuntary growth follows a pattern [p.158].

### Self and ego [p.158]
- The organizing center of the regulatory effect is a kind of "nuclear atom" in the psychic system, also the inventor, organizer and source of dream images. Jung named it the **Self** and defined it as the totality of the whole psyche, to distinguish it from the **ego**, only a small part of the whole.
- Historical intuitions of such a center: the Greek inner *daimon*, the Egyptian *Ba*-soul, the Roman *genius* of each individual; in simpler societies a protective spirit in an animal or fetish [p.158].
- **Diagram (p.158):** the psyche as a sphere with a bright field (A) on its surface representing consciousness; the ego is the field's center ("only if I know a thing is it conscious"); the Self is at once the nucleus and the whole sphere (B), and its internal regulating processes produce dreams.

### The Naskapi [p.158-159]
- Naskapi Indians of the Labrador peninsula: hunters living in isolated family groups, too scattered to develop tribal customs, collective religion or ceremonies. They rely on their own inner voices.
- Their view of the soul: an "inner companion," called "my friend" or **Mista'peo ("Great Man")**, dwelling in the heart, immortal, leaving the person at or just before death and reincarnating in another being [p.158].
- Those who attend to dreams, try to interpret them and test their truth deepen their relation to the Great Man, who sends them more and better dreams. The major obligation is to follow the dream instructions and give their contents permanent form in art. Lies and dishonesty drive him away; generosity and love of neighbors and animals attract him [p.158-159].
- Dreams guide the hunter in the outer world too (weather, hunting). Von Franz presents them as uncontaminated by civilized ideas, with natural insight into what Jung calls the Self [p.159].

### Definition and development of the Self [p.159]
- The Self: "an inner guiding factor that is different from the conscious personality and that can be grasped only through the investigation of one's own dreams." It is the regulating center that brings a constant extension and maturing of the personality, but at first only an inborn possibility. How far it develops depends on whether the ego is willing to listen. A receptive person has a Great Man that becomes "more real" and is a more complete human being.
- The ego is not made by nature to follow its arbitrary impulses without limit, but to help realize the totality: it "lights up" the system so that it becomes conscious. Example: an artistic talent the ego never notices might as well not exist. The inborn but hidden totality is not the same as a wholeness realized and lived.
- **The mountain pine:** the seed holds the whole tree latently, but each seed falls on particular soil, stones, slope, sun and wind, and the tree bends away from stones and toward light; the unique tree is the fulfillment of its totality. Realizing this uniqueness in the individual is the goal of individuation [p.159].
- Individuation goes on in all living beings by itself and unconsciously, but is "real" in the strict sense only if the individual is aware and connects with it. The conscious co-operation (free decisions) is individuation in the narrower sense [p.159].
- Unlike the pine metaphor, the human experience includes a feeling that a supra-personal force is interfering creatively, as if something looks at me that I do not see, "perhaps that Great Man in the heart." This creative aspect operates only when the ego drops all purposive and wishful aims and listens "without any further design or purpose" [p.159-160].
- Von Franz's remark on others: existentialist philosophers go right up to the door of the unconscious (stripping illusions of consciousness) "and then fail to open it" [p.160].

### Chuang-Tzu's story of the useless oak [p.160]
- Told by von Franz to an elderly woman who felt she had "done" nothing in her life (though she had made a good marriage with a difficult husband and matured as a person), who was immediately relieved.
- Tale: a carpenter named Stone calls a gigantic old oak beside an earth-altar useless (its wood would make rotting ships and breaking tools), and that is why it grew old. That night the oak appears in his dream: cultivated fruit trees are attacked and mutilated before their fruit ripens, so it has long tried to be useless; "you useless mortal man, what do you know about useless trees?" The carpenter tells his apprentice to keep quiet: the tree grew there on purpose, since anywhere else it would have been ill-treated. [Caption: earth altar under a tree in a 19th-century Chinese painting; round or square structures like this usually symbolize the Self, to which the ego must submit.]
- Interpretation: simply to fulfill one's destiny is the greatest human achievement and utilitarian notions must yield to the unconscious; the tree symbolizes individuation. The **earth-altar** (a crude unwrought stone where sacrifices are made to the local god who "owns" the land) means one must consciously surrender to the unconscious instead of thinking what one should do, what is thought right, or what usually happens; one must listen to learn what the Self wants "here and now in a particular situation" [p.160-161].

### The attitude required [p.161]
- Like the pine, we should neither be annoyed by obstructing stones nor plan to overcome them, but feel whether to grow left or right, toward or away from the slope: obeying "an almost imperceptible, yet powerfully dominating, impulse" toward unique creative self-realization, in which one repeatedly finds something not yet known to anyone. The hints come from the Self, not the ego.
- Looking furtively at how others develop is useless: problems are similar but never identical; "all pine trees are very much alike... yet none is exactly the same as another." Hence individuation cannot be summarized systematically, and each person does something uniquely their own.
- **On criticism that Jungian psychology is unsystematic:** the material is living, emotion-charged, irrational and changing; systematic description works for statistical averages but not for a single psychic event, where one can only give an honest picture from many angles. Analogy: physicists cannot say what light is, only that in some conditions it appears to be particles and in others waves. Depth psychology has reached "the same limits that confront microphysics" [p.161].

## 2. The first approach of the unconscious [p.162-164]

### Childhood [p.162-163]
- Youth is a gradual awakening; childhood has great emotional intensity, and a child's earliest dreams often show in symbolic form the basic structure of the psyche and how it will shape the person's destiny.
- **Case, "Jack Frost" dream (told by Jung to students):** a young woman haunted by anxiety who killed herself at 26 had dreamed as a small child that Jack Frost entered her room and pinched her stomach; she woke and found she had pinched herself. She was not frightened, only remembered the dream; the lack of emotional reaction to the "demon of the cold of congealed life" was abnormal and foretold the cold, unfeeling hand with which she later ended her life [p.162].
- Impressive real events can likewise anticipate the future symbolically; children forget what impresses adults and keep vivid some incident no one else noticed, which, read as a symbol, shows a basic problem of the child's psychic makeup [p.162].
- School age begins the building of the ego and adaptation to the outer world, usually with painful shocks; some children feel different and a sad loneliness comes with that sense of uniqueness. The imperfection of the world and the evil within oneself become conscious problems [p.162-163].
- When development of consciousness is disturbed, children retreat to an inner "fortress," and their dreams and drawings often show a **circular, quadrangular, "nuclear" motif** (the center of the psyche, from which the structural development of consciousness stems; the ego appears to be a duplicate or counterpart of the original center). It appears strikingly when psychic life is threatened [p.163]. Captions show a child's shocks (first school day, an attack by another child, first experience of death) and a protective circular motif drawn or dreamed [p.162].
- Two types of young people: those who earnestly seek meaning amid inner and outer chaos, and those still carried unconsciously by inherited instinctive archetypal patterns, for whom love, nature, sport and work have immediate meaning. The latter are not more superficial, only carried by the stream of life with less friction (analogy: riding a train without looking out, you notice movement only at stops and turns) [p.163].

### The call [p.163-164]
- Individuation proper (conscious coming to terms with the Self) generally begins with a **wounding of the personality** and the suffering with it: a sort of "call," seldom recognized. The ego feels hampered and projects the obstruction onto God, the economy, the boss or the spouse [p.163].
- Or all seems fine outwardly but a deadly boredom makes everything seem meaningless [p.163-164].
- Myths and fairy tales picture the stage with a king who is ill or old, a barren royal couple, a monster that steals the kingdom's women, children, horses and wealth, a demon that halts the army or ship, darkness over the land, dry wells, flood, drought and frost. The first encounter with the Self "casts a dark shadow ahead," as if the inner friend came first as a trapper [p.164].
- The cure in such tales is always something special and hard to find: a white blackbird, a fish with a golden ring in its gills, the water of life, three golden hairs of the devil, a woman's golden plait (and the woman who owns it) [p.164]. Captions: a 17th-century alchemical woodcut of an ill king; a scene from the film *La Dolce Vita* (decayed aristocratic castle, same state of emptiness); Paul Klee's *Fairy Tale* (tale of the bluebird of happiness).
- In the personal crisis one seeks something impossible to find or unknown. **All well-meant advice is useless** (be responsible, take a holiday, work less or more, take up a hobby). The one thing that works: turn directly toward the approaching darkness without prejudice and totally naively and ask what it secretly aims at and wants. The hidden purpose is usually unusual and found only through dreams and fantasies that well up from the unconscious. Sometimes the unconscious first gives helpful symbolic images; sometimes it first offers painful realizations of what is wrong with oneself, "bitter truths" to swallow [p.164].

## 3. The realization of the shadow [p.165-173]

### Definition [p.165]
- After a time one needs to readapt the conscious attitude to the unconscious and accept its seeming "criticism." Through dreams one meets aspects of one's own personality one has preferred not to look at. Jung called this **the realization of the shadow** (named because it often appears in dreams personified).
- The shadow is not the whole unconscious personality. It is the unknown or little-known attributes and qualities of the ego, mostly belonging to the personal sphere and capable of being conscious. It can also hold collective factors from outside the individual's personal life.
- Seeing it, one becomes aware of (and often ashamed of) qualities one denies in oneself but sees plainly in others: egotism, mental laziness, sloppiness, unreal fantasies and plots, carelessness, cowardice, inordinate love of money and possessions: "all the little sins" one told oneself did not matter because others do it too.
- **Test:** overwhelming rage when a friend reproaches you about a fault suggests you have found part of your shadow. The ego is really caught when one's own dreams reproach you [p.165].
- Self-education afterward is "the psychological equivalent of the labors of Hercules," beginning with the **Augean Stables** [p.165-166].

### How it shows itself [p.166]
- Not only in omissions: also in the impulsive or inadvertent act (the evil remark, the plot, the wrong decision) before one has time to think.
- The shadow is more exposed than the conscious personality to **collective infection**: a man alone feels all right, but when "the others" do dark primitive things he fears being thought a fool if he does not join and gives way to impulses that are not his. [Captions: a 1961 Polish film about possessed 17th-century French nuns; Brueghel's drawing of St. Vitus' Dance; the fiery cross of the Ku Klux Klan, as three examples of collective infection that can weld people into an irrational mob.]
- One stumbles over one's own and others' shadows especially with people of the **same sex**; the shadow of the opposite sex is seen but forgiven more easily. In dreams and myths the shadow therefore appears as a person of the dreamer's own sex [p.166].

### Dream case 1: the big house and the old school friend (man of 48) [p.166-168]
- Dreamer: a man of 48 who lived for and by himself, working hard and disciplining himself, repressing pleasure and spontaneity far beyond his nature.
- Dream: he owns a very big house in town and finds unknown rooms, mostly in the cellar, with unlocked exits (some with no locks) into other cellars and subterranean streets; laborers nearby could sneak in. Up on the ground floor, a back yard has more exits. A man laughing loudly calls him an old pal from elementary school; they stroll through streets, through an enormous circular street with a strange chiaroscuro, to a green lawn where **three riderless galloping horses**, beautiful, wild but well groomed, pass them. (He wonders if they ran away from military service.) [p.166-167]
- Reading: the maze of cellar passages is like the old Egyptian underworld, a symbol of the unconscious and of being "open" to alien influences on the shadow side; the house is the still unperceived scope of his personality. The old friend personifies a lost childhood quality (gaiety, irascibility, trust, here "his neglected capacity for enjoying life and his extraverted shadow side"). The horses suggest instinctive drives escaped from the "military service" of his conscious discipline, and "all the positive force... that was lacking before" returns [p.167].
- General point: **the shadow usually contains values needed by consciousness, but in a form hard to integrate** [p.167-168]. [Captions: de Chirico's *Anxious Journey*; papyrus of the seven doors of the Egyptian underworld (c. 1400 B.C.); mazes: Finnish stone maze, British turf maze, the tile maze on Chartres cathedral's floor, walked as a symbolic pilgrimage; underworld/maze as symbols of the first contact with the unconscious.]
- This shadow is typical of an **introvert**; an extravert's shadow looks quite different [p.168].

### Dream case 2: the Frenchman and the official (lively young extravert) [p.168-169]
- Dreamer: a young man of lively temperament who repeatedly launched successful enterprises while dreams insisted he finish a private creative work.
- Dream: a Frenchman, a desperado who takes any criminal job, lies on a couch with a cover over his face; an official accompanies the dreamer downstairs and a plot exists to have the Frenchman kill him "as if by chance"; a tall portly rich and influential man leans ill against a wall; the dreamer stabs the official in the heart ("one only notices a bit of moisture"), and is safe because the Frenchman's orderer is dead (official and portly man seem the same person) [p.168].
- Reading: the desperado is the dreamer's introversion, destitute and passive; the official and portly man are his successful outer activities, which have "no blood in his veins," only moisture: bloodless mechanisms, no loss if killed (the dreamer had in fact fallen ill when he let his dynamic energy explode too forcibly outward). The Frenchman is satisfied at the end, a **positive shadow figure that turned negative and dangerous only because the conscious attitude did not agree with him** [p.168-169].
- The dreamer associated the French with skill in love affairs: so the two figures also represent **power (official = collective adaptation; portly man = ambition) and sex**. When the dreamer stops the power drive, the sex drive stops being hostile [p.169].

### Projection and politics [p.169-170]
- The shadow problem plays a big role in political conflicts. Without insight the dreamer could have identified the Frenchman with "dangerous Communists" and the official plus the prosperous man with "grasping capitalists," and avoided seeing the warring elements within him. Seeing one's own unconscious tendencies in other people is **projection**; political agitation and back-yard gossip are full of it. Projections spoil objectivity and any genuine human relationship [p.169].
- A further disadvantage: if one projects the shadow onto the enemy, part of one's personality remains on the other side, and one keeps unwittingly doing things behind one's own back that help the enemy. If the projection is realized and one can discuss without fear or hostility, there is a chance of understanding or at least a truce [p.170]. [Captions: a Chinese Communist parade poster of America as a Nazi-marked serpent; Hitler's description of Churchill; malicious gossip in a British TV series.]

### Friend or enemy [p.170-173]
- Whether the shadow becomes friend or enemy depends largely on us. He is like any human being one has to get along with: sometimes by giving in, sometimes by resisting, sometimes by love. The shadow becomes hostile only when ignored or misunderstood [p.170].
- If a person lives out the worse side and represses the better, the shadow appears as a positive figure; for one who lives out natural emotions, it may appear as a cold negative intellectual. Whatever its form, **its function is to represent the opposite side of the ego and embody just those qualities one dislikes most in others** [p.170].
- Honest insight alone does not always integrate it: the passion in the shadow may overpower reason. A bitter outer experience (a brick dropping on one's head) may help, or a heroic decision, usually possible only if the Self helps [p.170].
- But the shadow's power does not mean always repress it: sometimes it is strong because the **Self's urge points the same way**, and one cannot tell whether Self or shadow stands behind a pressure. In the unconscious, as in a moonlit landscape, contents blur together: **contamination** of unconscious contents [p.170].
- Sometimes everything unknown to the ego is mixed with the shadow, even the highest forces. Could the Frenchman be a useless tramp or a valuable introvert? Should the horses run free? If the dream does not say, the conscious personality must decide [p.170-172].
- If the shadow holds valuable vital forces, they should be assimilated into experience, not repressed; the ego must give up pride and priggishness. That sacrifice can be as heroic as conquering passion, in the opposite direction [p.172].
- [Captions p.171: the film *Crin Blanc* (white stallion and boy plunge into the sea to avoid capture: escape into the unconscious to avoid reality); p.172: Vishnu in a demonic aspect, a Buddha with arms holding symbols of good and evil, and Luther in the 1961 play, never sure whether his break from the Church came from God or from his own pride (the "evil" side of his shadow); the shadow has a dangerous and a valuable aspect.]

### Khidr and Moses (Koran, 18th book) [p.172]
- Tale used to show the ethical difficulties: Moses meets Khidr ("the Green One," "first angel of God") in the desert. Khidr warns Moses will not bear his deeds. He scuttles a poor villagers' boat, kills a handsome young man, and restores a wall in a city of unbelievers. Moses, indignant, is left; Khidr then explains: the boat was saved from pirates, the youth was about to commit a crime that would have shamed his pious parents, the wall hid a treasure belonging to two pious youths. Moses' judgment was too hasty.
- Khidr is not simply Moses' lawless shadow: he is more the personification of **secret creative actions of the Godhead** (parallel cited: the Indian story "The King and the Corpse," interpreted by Henry Zimmer). Von Franz chose a story and not a dream because it sums up a lifetime's experience with a clarity a single dream would rarely give [p.172].

### Uncertainty and the ethics of the shadow [p.172-173]
- When dark figures appear and seem to want something, one cannot be sure whether they are a shadowy part of oneself, the Self, or both. Whether the dark partner shows a shortcoming to overcome or a meaningful bit of life to accept is "one of the most difficult problems" of individuation, and the dream symbols are often too subtle to be sure [p.172-173].
- What to do: accept the discomfort of ethical doubt, make no final decisions, keep watching the dreams. **Cinderella** sorting good and bad peas: hopeless, yet doves (or ants) come to help: helpful deeply unconscious impulses felt in the body that point a way out [p.173].
- "Somewhere, right at the bottom of one's own being, one generally does know where one should go and what one should do," but the clown called "I" can distract so that the inner voice is not heard [p.173].
- If all attempts fail: have the courage to do what seems right, ready to change course if the unconscious points elsewhere. Occasionally one should resist the unconscious urge, even at the price of feeling warped, rather than depart too far from being human (the case of people who would have to live out a criminal disposition) [p.173].
- The strength and clarity for the decision "stem secretly from the Great Man," who apparently does not want to reveal himself too clearly: maybe the Self wants the ego to choose freely, or depends on human consciousness to become manifest. No one can judge the deeds of others; as an old Zen master said, follow the cowherd who watches his ox "with a stick so that it will not graze on other people's meadows" [p.173].
- Depth psychology will change collective ethical views by forcing more individual and subtle judgment. Many people behave as if nothing had happened because honest self-examination and reorganizing one's life take courage; most are too indolent even about the conscious moral side of their conduct [p.173].

## 4. The anima: the woman within [p.174-185]

### Definition [p.174]
- Often, behind the shadow, another "inner figure" emerges: in a man a female personification of his unconscious, in a woman a male one. Jung named them **anima** and **animus**.
- The **anima**: personification of all feminine psychological tendencies in a man's psyche: vague feelings and moods, prophetic hunches, receptiveness to the irrational, capacity for personal love, feeling for nature, and "last but not least" his relation to the unconscious. Hence priestesses (the Greek Sibyl) were used to fathom the divine will.
- **Arctic shamans** as example of the anima as inner figure: some wear women's clothes or have breasts depicted on their garments, to manifest the inner feminine side that connects them with the "ghost land" (the unconscious). **Case:** a young man being initiated was buried by an older shaman in a snow hole; in a coma he saw a luminous woman who taught him all he needed and later served as protective spirit, relating him to powers beyond [p.174]. [Captions: a 17th-century sorceress with imps; a Siberian shaman, a man dressed as a woman; a woman medium in the 1951 film *The Medium*: belief persists that women are more receptive to the irrational.]

### The negative anima [p.175-177]
- The anima's character is as a rule shaped by the **mother**. A negative maternal influence produces anima moods: irritability, depression, uncertainty, insecurity, touchiness; the inner theme "I am nothing. Nothing makes any sense. With others it's different, but for me... I enjoy nothing." These moods cause dullness, fear of disease, impotence or accidents, a sad oppressive life, and can lure toward suicide, when the anima is a **death demon** (Cocteau's *Orphee*). Overcoming such assaults can even reinforce a man's masculinity [p.175].
- Names for the dangerous figure: the *femme fatale*; milder, the Queen of the Night in Mozart's *Magic Flute*; the Greek Sirens, the German Lorelei, the Slavonic Rusalka (spirits of drowned girls): **destructive illusion** [p.175].
- **Siberian tale:** a lonely hunter sees a beautiful woman on the far bank of a river waving and singing "Oh, come, lonely hunter in the stillness of dusk... My nest is near." He throws off his clothes and swims across; she flies away as an owl, laughing mockingly, and he drowns in the cold river when he tries to swim back. Meaning: the anima as an unreal dream of love, happiness and maternal warmth (her nest) that lures men from reality; he drowned running after a wishful fantasy that could not be fulfilled [p.175-176].
- Other forms: **waspish, poisonous, effeminate remarks** by which a man devalues everything (cheap twisting of the truth, subtly destructive); the Oriental "poison damsel" with hidden weapons or poison, who kills lovers on the first night: the anima as cold and reckless as uncanny aspects of nature, still expressed in belief in witches [p.176].
- If a man's experience of his mother was **positive**, the anima can make him effeminate or preyed on by women, unable to cope with hardship; a sentimentalist, touchy "as old maids," "sensitive as the fairy-tale princess who could feel a pea under 30 mattresses" [p.176].
- A subtler form: the princess in fairy tales who sets suitors riddles (or has them hide under her nose) and has them killed when they fail; she always wins. The anima involves a man in a **destructive intellectual game**: neurotic pseudo-intellectual dialogues that keep him from direct touch with life; he reflects so much that he cannot live, losing spontaneity and outgoing feeling [p.176]. [Captions: *The Blue Angel* (professor degraded by a cabaret singer), Salome with the head of John the Baptist (p.176); Oedipus and the Sphinx, a 16th-century woodcut "The Bewitched Groom" (negative anima as ugly witch) (p.178); St. Anthony tempted by a girl with bat wings (p.177).]
- The most frequent manifestation: **erotic fantasy** (films, strip-tease shows, pornographic daydreaming), a crude primitive aspect that becomes compulsive only when the man's **feeling attitude toward life has remained infantile** and he has not sufficiently cultivated feeling relationships [p.176-177]. [Caption p.178: pornography as a crude childish form.]

### Projection of the anima [p.177-179]
- Like the shadow, the anima is **projected** so that she appears to be a particular woman's qualities. It causes sudden love at first sight where the man feels he has known the woman intimately forever, which looks like madness to outsiders. "Fairy-like" women attract such projections because men can attribute almost anything to a fascinatingly vague creature and weave fantasies [p.177].
- Projection in a sudden passion can disturb a marriage and lead to the "human triangle." A bearable solution is possible only if the anima is recognized as an inner power. **The secret aim of the unconscious in the entanglement is to force the man to develop and mature by integrating more of his unconscious personality into real life** [p.177-178]. [Captions: *Eve* with Jeanne Moreau (femme fatale; poster text "Mysterious - tantalizing - alluring - wanton - but deep within her burning the violent fires that destroy a man"); *Ugetsu Monogatari* (a man under the spell of a ghost princess, an anima projection producing a destructive fantasy relationship); Flaubert's *Madame Bovary* with the quoted passage describing the lover's projection ("the beloved one of all novels, the heroine of all plays, the 'she' of all poems"): "love madness" (p.179).]
- Men also project the anima onto things: ships are "she" (the figurehead on the *Cutty Sark*; the captain symbolically her husband and so goes down with her), and cars (called "she," caressed and pampered like favorite mistresses) [p.180 caption].

### The positive anima [p.178-183]
- Positive functions: she helps a man find the **right marriage partner**; she helps dig out facts hidden in the unconscious that his logical mind cannot discern; **most vital**, she tunes his mind to the right inner values and opens the way to deeper inner depths, like an inner "radio" tuned to a wavelength that excludes irrelevancies and lets the voice of the Great Man be heard. She then becomes **guide or mediator to the world within and to the Self** [p.178-180].
- Examples: the shaman's initiation (above); Beatrice in Dante's *Paradiso*; the goddess Isis appearing in a dream to Apuleius (*The Golden Ass*) to initiate him into a higher spiritual life [p.180].

### Dream case 3: the psychotherapist and the inner Mass (man of 45) [p.180-182]
- Context: he had gone to bed thinking it hard to stand alone without the support of a church (born Protestant, no longer affiliated), envying those protected by the "maternal embrace" of an organization.
- Dream: in the aisle of an old church full of people, he sits with his mother and wife in extra seats. He must celebrate the Mass as priest with an unfamiliar big Mass book (more like a prayer book or poetry anthology) and cannot find the text; mother and wife chatter. The organ stops; he gets up resolutely and asks a kneeling **nun** behind him for her Mass book and the place. She obliges and then, like a sexton, precedes him to the altar (behind him to the left, via a side aisle). The "book" is a board three feet by one foot with ancient pictures in columns. She must read a part of the liturgy first, says it is Number 15; he cannot read the numbers, but turning toward the congregation he finds Number 15 (next to last on the board), unsure he can decipher it, and decides to try. He wakes [p.180].
- Reading: the dream answers his thought: "You yourself must become a priest in your own inner church, the church of your soul." The people are his own psychic qualities, who want him to officiate. The Mass is a symbolic act in which the Divinity is present for communication; the solution is not general but typical for a Protestant (a man who is through real faith still contained in the Catholic Church usually experiences his anima in the image of the Church herself; her sacred images are his symbols of the unconscious) [p.181-182].
- Mother-boundness and extraversion (the extraverted wife) distract him with meaningless talk; if he follows the nun (the **introverted, religious anima**, servant and priest) she leads him. Her Mass book has **16 (four times four) ancient pictures**; his Mass is contemplation of the psychic images the religious anima reveals. If he overcomes the insecurity caused by his mother complex, his life task will have the nature of a religious service [p.182].
- The anima here is in her proper role as **mediator between ego and Self**. The four-times-four configuration points to totality: the nucleus of the psyche "normally expresses itself in some kind of fourfold structure" [p.182].

### The four stages of the anima [p.182-183]
| Stage | Figure | Level |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Eve** | purely instinctual and biological relations |
| 2 | **Faust's Helen** | romantic and aesthetic level, still with sexual elements |
| 3 | **the Virgin Mary** | raises love (eros) to spiritual devotion |
| 4 | **Sapientia** (wisdom) | wisdom transcending even the holiest and purest; another symbol: the Shulamite in the Song of Solomon |

- In modern people the fourth stage is rarely reached; the Mona Lisa comes nearest to a wisdom anima [p.182-183]. [Captions: Gauguin's primitive woman and a Renaissance girl as Cleopatra; Helen and Paris; van Eyck's Virgin (red robe = feeling or eros spiritualized); Athena and the Mona Lisa as fourth-stage examples (p.181-182).] The motif of fourfoldness recurs and "will be discussed later" [p.183].

### Practice: the anima as guide in practical terms [p.183]
- A man takes seriously the **feelings, moods, expectations and fantasies** sent by the anima and fixes them in some form: writing, painting, sculpture, musical composition, dancing. Worked patiently and slowly, more deeply unconscious material wells up and connects with the earlier material.
- After a fantasy is fixed it must be examined **intellectually and ethically with an evaluating feeling reaction**, and regarded as **absolutely real**, with no "lurking doubt that this is 'only a fantasy.'" Practiced with devotion over a long time, the individuation process "gradually becomes the single reality" and can unfold in its true form [p.183].
- Literary examples of the anima as guide: Francesco Colonna's *Hypnerotomachia*, Rider Haggard's *She*, the "eternal feminine" in Goethe's *Faust*. A quoted medieval mystical text where an anima figure speaks ("I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys... mediator of the elements... the law in the priest and the word in the prophet... I will kill and I will make to live and there is none that can deliver out of my hand") [p.183]. [Captions: a 17th-century engraving of the anima as mediator between this world (a monkey = man's instinctual nature) and the next (hand of God), parallel to the woman of the Apocalypse with 12 stars, moon goddesses, Sapientia and Isis; Blake's drawing of Beatrice leading Dante along a tortuous path; a film scene from *She*.]

### History: medieval cult of the lady [p.184-185]
- In the Middle Ages the fantasy world of the unconscious was recognized more clearly. The **knightly cult of the lady** attempted to differentiate the feminine side of man's nature toward the outer woman and the inner world; the lady is a personification of the anima. In Wolfram von Eschenbach's Grail legend the Grail bearer is named **Conduir-amour** ("guide in love matters"), who taught the hero to differentiate feelings and behavior toward women [p.184].
- Later this individual effort was abandoned when her sublime aspect fused with the **Virgin**, object of boundless devotion; and **when the anima is conceived as all positive, her negative aspects find expression in the belief in witches** [p.184].
- Cultural parallels: China's Kwan-Yin and the popular "Lady of the Moon" (who gives poetry, music, even immortality); India's Shakti, Parvati, Rati; Islam's Fatima [p.184-185]. [Captions: courtly love as influenced by the cult of Mary; a knight kneeling to his lady with death behind him; a witches' sabbath painting; Church and Synagogue personified as Mary and the sinful Eve. "When the anima is projected onto an official personification she tends to fall apart into a double aspect, such as Mary and witch" (p.185).]
- Two dangers: **worship as an officially recognized religious figure** costs her individual aspects; **regarding her as only personal** risks her being found only in the outer world, making the man a victim of erotic fantasies or compulsively dependent on one actual woman [p.185].
- The remedy: "the painful (but essentially simple) decision to take one's fantasies and feelings seriously" prevents stagnation; the anima then becomes what she originally was, "the woman within," conveying the vital messages of the Self [p.185].
- [Captions p.184: Peter Birkhauser's painting of a four-eyed anima (the four eyes allude, like the 16 pictures, to the possibility of wholeness); a "soul landscape" painting by Slavko: a naked dark-skinned woman (anima), a bear (animal soul), a double tree (individuation, inner opposites uniting), and a glacier that is also a four-eyed, animal-like face from which the life-stream flows: the Self. It "provides a good example of the way an unconscious symbol can inadvertently find its way into a fantasy landscape."]

## 5. The animus: the man within [p.186-192]

### Character [p.186]
- The animus has good and bad aspects like the anima, but appears less often as erotic fantasy or mood and more often as a **hidden "sacred" conviction**. When preached with a loud, insistent masculine voice or imposed by brutal emotional scenes, the underlying masculinity of the woman is easily seen; in an outwardly very feminine woman it can be an equally hard, inexorable power: "something... obstinate, cold, and completely inaccessible."
- Typical animus themes in a woman's ruminations: "The only thing in the world that I want is love and he doesn't love me"; "In this situation there are only two possibilities and both are equally bad." **"The animus never believes in exceptions."** His opinions are usually right in a general way yet seldom fit the individual situation: reasonable but beside the point [p.186].
- As the anima is shaped by the mother, the animus is shaped mainly by the **father**, who gives the daughter's animus "unarguable, incontestably 'true'" convictions that never include the personal reality of the woman as she actually is [p.186].

### The death demon and the destructive animus [p.186-188]
- **Gypsy tale:** a lonely woman receives a handsome stranger despite a dream warning that he is the king of the dead. She presses him to reveal who he is; he first refuses (she will die) and then admits he is death himself, and she dies of fright. Mythologically he is a pagan father or god image, king of the dead (as Hades abducting Persephone); psychologically he is a form of the animus that **lures women from all human relationships and especially from real men**: a cocoon of dreamy thoughts, desires and judgments about how things "ought to be" that cut her off from life [p.186-188].
- As robber and murderer: **Bluebeard**. The animus personifies the semiconscious, cold, destructive reflections that invade a woman in the small hours, especially when she has failed to realize some obligation of feeling: calculating, malicious, intriguing thoughts about inheritance, even wishing others' death ("When one of us dies, I'll move to the Riviera," said a woman to her husband) [p.188].
- By nursing secret destructive attitudes a wife can drive her husband, and a mother her children, into illness, accident or death, or keep children from marrying (an old woman showing a picture of her son, drowned at 27: "I prefer it this way; it's better than giving him away to another woman") [p.188].
- **Paralysis and nullity:** an unconscious animus opinion can produce strange passivity, paralysis of feeling, deep insecurity: "You are hopeless. What's the use of trying?" [p.188]
- [Captions: Joan of Arc (animus as "sacred conviction"); a 16th-century dance with death; Hades and Persephone (p.186); Heathcliff in *Wuthering Heights* as a negative demonic animus figure, probably Emily Bronte's own; Doré's Bluebeard; the highwayman Claude Duval (p.187); wreckers luring ships to rocks, as a negative group animus (p.188); a band of bandits in the film *The Bandit*; Fuseli's illustration of *A Midsummer Night's Dream* (a comic twist on tales where a girl's love releases a man from a spell) (p.189).]

### Possession [p.190]
- When any such personification takes hold, it feels as if we ourselves are thinking and feeling it: the ego identifies with it, cannot detach it. One is "possessed"; only afterward does one see with horror having said and done things opposite to one's real thoughts and feelings [p.190].

### Dream case 4: the veiled burglars (woman of 45) [p.190-191]
- Dream: two veiled figures in black hooded coats climb through the balcony and torment the dreamer and her sister. The sister hides under the bed, is pulled out with a broom and tortured. The leader pushes the dreamer against the wall making magical gestures; the helper sketches on the wall; she says, to seem friendly, "Oh! But this is well drawn!" and suddenly the tormentor has the **noble head of an artist**, says proudly "Yes, indeed," and cleans his spectacles [p.190].
- Reading: in life the dreamer suffered attacks of anxiety, haunted by the thought that loved ones were in danger or dead (the sadistic aspect). The double figure suggests a psychic factor that is dual in effect. The sister, who ran away and was tortured, had in life died young, artistically gifted but little using her talent. The burglars are really **disguised artists** who give up their evil intentions if the dreamer recognizes their gifts, which are her own. Behind anxiety lies genuine danger, but also a **creative possibility**: she had some painting talent and doubted it was meaningful; the dream tells her to live it out, whereupon the tormenting animus becomes creative and meaningful activity [p.190-191].
- The animus often appears as a **group of men**, symbolizing that he represents a collective rather than a personal element. A woman speaking from her animus says "one," "they," "everybody," and uses "always," "should" and "ought" [p.191].

### Beauty and the Beast, and the forbidden husband [p.191-192]
- Many myths and fairy tales tell of a prince turned by witchcraft into an animal or monster and redeemed by a girl's love: the animus becoming conscious (Henderson discussed "Beauty and the Beast" in the preceding chapter). The heroine often may not ask about her lover or meet him only in the dark and may not look at him; blind trust is meant to redeem him, but never succeeds: she breaks the promise and finds him again only after a long quest and much suffering [p.191-192]. [Captions: Wagner's *Flying Dutchman*; Psyche forbidden to look at Eros (p.190).]
- The parallel in life: the conscious attention a woman must give her animus problem takes much time and suffering. But if she realizes who and what he is and what he does to her, instead of being possessed, the animus can become "an invaluable inner companion" endowing her with **initiative, courage, objectivity and spiritual wisdom** [p.192].

### The four stages of the animus [p.192]
| Stage | Form |
|---|---|
| 1 | mere **physical power**: athletic champion, "muscle man" (caption example: Tarzan) |
| 2 | **initiative and capacity for planned action** (captions: Shelley as "romantic," Hemingway as "man of action") |
| 3 | the **"word"**: professor or clergyman (caption: Lloyd George, political orator) |
| 4 | **incarnation of meaning**: mediator of religious experience that gives life new meaning (caption: often projected onto Gandhi) |

- In his highest form he gives a woman "spiritual firmness, an invisible inner support that compensates for her outer softness," sometimes connects her mind with the spiritual evolution of her age, and can make her **more receptive than a man to new creative ideas**, which is why women were used as diviners and seers; her positive animus's creative boldness stimulates men to new enterprises [p.192-193]. [Caption: a woman who falls for a picture or film star is projecting her animus: Rudolph Valentino drew the projection of thousands of women, even after death (1926 funeral).]
- **Marital complication:** one partner's possession by the animus (or anima) often irritates the other so that he or she is possessed too; animus and anima drag conversation to a low level and create an irascible emotional atmosphere [p.193].
- Positive animus: enterprising spirit, courage, truthfulness, and at the highest level spiritual profundity, through which a woman perceives the underlying processes of her cultural and personal situation. This presupposes that her animus stops representing opinions above criticism: she must find courage and inner broadmindedness to **question the sacredness of her own convictions**, and only then will she take in the unconscious's suggestions, especially those contradicting animus opinions, and the manifestations of the Self get through [p.193].

## 6. The Self: symbols of totality [p.193-208]

### Personifications [p.193-196]
- After long enough wrestling with anima or animus so one is no longer partly identified with it, the unconscious changes its dominant character and appears in a new symbolic form: the **Self**, the innermost nucleus. In a woman's dreams it is usually a superior female figure (priestess, sorceress, earth mother, goddess of nature or love); in a man's, a masculine initiator and guardian (an Indian guru), a **wise old man**, a spirit of nature [p.193]. [Captions: Demeter with Triptolemus and Kore; Cinderella's fairy godmother; a helpful old woman in Andersen (female Self) (p.194); Merlin, a guru, a winged old man with keys from one of Jung's own dreams ("superior insight") (p.195).]

**Folk tale 1 (Austrian):** a king orders soldiers to watch the corpse of a bewitched black princess who rises each midnight and kills the guard. One soldier despairs and flees to the woods and meets an "old guitarist who is our Lord Himself," who tells him where to hide in the church and how to behave; with his help he redeems and marries the princess. Reading: the guitarist is a personification of the Self; with his help the ego avoids destruction and redeems a dangerous aspect of the anima [p.193].

**Folk tale 2 (Eskimo):** a lonely, disappointed girl meets a wizard in a copper boat, the "Spirit of the Moon" (giver of animals, bestower of hunting luck), who carries her to the heavens. In his absence she visits a little house where a **tiny woman clothed in bearded-seal intestinal membrane** warns him of plans to kill her (he is a Bluebeard-like killer of women). The tiny woman makes a rope to descend at the new moon, when she can weaken him; the girl must keep her eyes shut on the way down but does not open them as quickly as told (as stated in the text), and is turned into a spider, never human again [p.193-194].
- Reading: the divine musician is the wise old man (like Merlin or Hermes); the little woman is the parallel figure in the feminine psyche; the musician saves the hero from the destructive anima and the little woman protects the girl against her animus (the Moon Spirit). But things go wrong in the second, a point von Franz says she takes up later (the Self's ambivalence, section 8 below) [p.194, 213].

### Young and old: the youth on the horse (middle-aged man) [p.194-196]
- The Self is not always old; these paradoxical figures try to express something not contained in time, simultaneously young and old [p.194].
- Dream: a youth rides into the dreamer's garden (open, with no fence); the dreamer does not know if he came on purpose or if the horse carried him against his will. The horse is small, wild, powerful, boar-like, with thick bristly silvery-gray coat (a symbol of energy). The boy jumps off and leads it carefully so it will not trample the new bed of red and orange tulips planted by the dreamer's wife [p.194-196].
- Reading: the youth is the Self and with it **renewal of life, a creative elan vital and a new spiritual orientation**. Devotion to the unconscious's instructions can turn stale life into a rich, unending inner adventure full of creative possibilities [p.196]. [Caption p.196: Peter Birkhauser painting of a black four-armed youth (nocturnal origin) with a sun-like round object behind and a flower hovering before his hands; the four arms recall fourfold wholeness symbols.]

### Dream case 5: the river and the small powerful girl (woman in late forties) [p.196]
- Dream: she is washing the pavement in front of a church with water, then runs down a street as high school students are let out. She comes to a **stagnant river** crossed by a board or tree trunk; a mischievous student bounces on the board so it cracks and she nearly falls ("Idiot!"). On the other bank three little girls play; one extends a hand she thinks too small to help, but pulls her effortlessly up the bank.
- Reading: she is religious but cannot remain in her (Protestant) church and has lost access though she keeps the entry clean. The stagnant river is the slowed flow of life from an unresolved religious problem; crossing a river is a frequent image of a fundamental change of attitude. The student is her own earlier thought that she might satisfy her spiritual yearning by attending high school, which the dream does not respect. The small, supernaturally powerful girl is a personification of the Self [p.196]. [Captions: river crossings accompanying turning points: Washington crossing the Delaware, the D-day landing; the Self often appears at crucial times in the dreamer's life (p.195).]

### The Cosmic Man [p.196-199]
- The ages the Self assumes show it is with us throughout life and exists beyond the consciously realized flow of life, which creates our experience of time. It is also omnipresent, often appearing as a **gigantic symbolic human being who embraces and contains the whole cosmos**. When this image appears in a person's dreams "we may hope for a creative solution to his conflict," since the vital psychic center is activated [p.196-197].
- Myths: Adam; the Persian **Gayomart**; the Hindu **Purusha**; the Chinese **P'an Ku**, who gave heaven and earth form: his tears made the Yellow and Yangtze rivers, his breath the wind, speech thunder, glance lightning, mood the weather; dying, his body became the five holy mountains (head = T'ai in the East, trunk = Sung in the center, right arm = Heng in the North, left arm = Heng in the South, feet = Hua in the West), his eyes the sun and moon [p.196-197].
- **Four, eight, sixteen:** symbolic structures that refer to individuation tend to rest on the motif of **four** (four functions, four stages of anima and animus, the shape of P'an Ku). Natural unhampered manifestations of the center are fourfold or derive from the series 4, 8, 16; **16, four fours, plays a particularly important role**. Only under specific circumstances do other combinations of numbers appear [p.197].
- **Adam** in Jewish legend: God gathered red, black, white and yellow dust from the four corners of the world, and Adam "reached from one end of the world to the other"; when he bent his head was in the East, feet in the West. All mankind was contained in Adam and his soul was "like the wick of a lamp composed of innumerable strands": total oneness of all human existence beyond individual units [p.197].
- **Gayomart**: a huge figure emitting light; at death metals sprang from his body and gold from his soul; his semen fell on earth and became the first human couple as two rhubarb shrubs. P'an Ku shown covered with leaves like a plant, perhaps because the First Man was a self-grown living unit with no animal impulse or self-will; a people on the Tigris still worship Adam as the hidden "super-soul" or protective spirit of humanity, said to have come from a date palm (plant motif again) [p.197, 199].
- [Captions p.198: the title page of Hobbes's *Leviathan* (gigantic figure made up of all the commonwealth's people); P'an Ku covered in leaves; a Cosmic Lion Goddess holding the sun in an 18th-century Indian manuscript. Caption p.197: modern figures who personify the Self in dreams: Schweitzer and Churchill in men's dreams; Eleanor Roosevelt and Elizabeth II in women's.]
- **Inner image, not outer fact:** in the East and in some Gnostic circles the Cosmic Man was understood as an inner psychic image. In Hindu tradition he is within each person, the only immortal part, redeeming the individual by leading him out of creation and its sufferings back to the eternal sphere, but only if man recognizes him and wakes from sleep to be led. The **Purusha** ("man" or "person") lives within each heart and fills the cosmos [p.199].
- He is also the **goal** of creation: Meister Eckhart, "All cereal nature means wheat, all treasure nature means gold, all generation means man." So the whole inner psychic reality of each individual is ultimately oriented toward the archetypal Self. **Human existence will never be satisfactorily explained by isolated instincts or purposive mechanisms (hunger, power, sex, survival, species perpetuation): "man's main purpose is not to eat, drink, etc., but to be human"**: the inner reality manifests a living mystery that only a symbol can express [p.199].
- Names: in the West largely identified with Christ, in the East with Krishna or Buddha; in the Old Testament the "Son of Man," later Jewish mysticism **Adam Kadmon**, late-antique movements **Anthropos**. Like all symbols, it points to an unknowable secret: the ultimate unknown meaning of human existence [p.199-200]. [Captions: a Rhodesian rock painting of a creation myth (First Man = the moon, mating with morning and evening stars); Grunewald's Christ with Cosmic Man's majesty (p.199).]
- Achievement of the goal is not an external event: for the Hindu it is not the world dissolving into the Great Man but **the ego's extraverted orientation toward the external world disappearing** to make way for him; the ego's discursive flow of representations and desires calms when the Great Man within is met. Outer reality exists for us only in so far as we perceive it consciously, and we cannot prove it exists "in and by itself" [p.200].

### The royal couple and the bisexual symbol [p.200-203]
- The universality of the Great Man symbol: it represents what is whole and complete, so it is often conceived as **bisexual**, reconciling male and female. This union also appears in dreams as a divine, royal or distinguished couple [p.200-201]. [Captions: Siva and Parvati joined hermaphroditically; Krishna and Radha; a Greek head shown by Jung to be subtly two-sided; Jung's note that the head "has, like his analogs Adonis, Tammuz, and... Baldur, all the grace and charm of either sex" (p.200).]

**Dream case 6: she-bear, lioness and royal couple (man of 47)** [p.201-203]
- Dream: on a platform he sees below him a huge, black, beautiful she-bear with a rough but well-groomed coat, standing on hind legs polishing a flat oval black stone on a stone slab so it shines. A lioness and her cub nearby polish bigger round stones. The she-bear turns into a fat naked woman with black hair and dark fiery eyes; he behaves erotically provocatively, she moves to catch him, he flees back up the scaffolding. Later he is among many women, half primitive with rich black hair (as if transformed from animals), half "our women" with blonde or brown hair. The primitives sing a very sentimental song in melancholy high voices. A young man in a golden crown set with rubies arrives in a high elegant carriage with a blonde young woman, probably his wife, uncrowned; the lioness and cub seem to have become this couple, belonging to the primitives. All the women intone a solemn song and the carriage slowly travels toward the horizon [p.201-202].
- Reading: the nucleus of his psyche first shows in a vision of a royal couple from the animal and primitive layer. The she-bear is a kind of mother goddess (Artemis was worshiped in Greece as a she-bear). The dark oval stone is his innermost being, true personality. Rubbing and polishing stones is very ancient (hidden "holy" stones wrapped in bark in European caves; Australian aborigines believe dead ancestors live in stones and rubbing increases their power, "like charging them with electricity") [p.202].
- He had refused a marital bond with a woman; his fear of being caught made him flee to the "spectator's platform" and passive watching. The stone being rubbed shows his inner being is shaped and polished by "the frictions of married life." Once polished it shines like a mirror in which the bear sees herself: **only by accepting earthly contact and suffering can the human soul become a mirror in which the divine powers perceive themselves**. The dreamer escapes into reflections ("higher place"), leaving his anima undifferentiated: the nondescript women split into a primitive and a civilized half [p.202]. [Caption: a mirror in dreams as the unconscious's power to mirror the individual objectively, a shocking view; Medusa could only be viewed in a mirror (Caravaggio). Other captions: the Celtic bear-goddess Artio found at Berne (= "bear"); Australian aborigines with sacred stones; alchemical royal pair of lions (p.201).]
- The lioness and son are the mysterious urge toward individuation (round stones are symbols of the Self). Lions as royal couple symbolize totality; in medieval symbolism the **philosopher's stone** is a pair of lions or a human couple riding lions. So the individuation urge often **appears veiled, hidden in the overwhelming passion one feels for another person**: passion beyond the natural measure of love "ultimately aims at the mystery of becoming whole," which is why one feels that becoming one with the other is the only worthwhile goal [p.202-203].
- While the totality is a pair of lions it is still contained in passion; when they become king and queen the urge has reached **conscious realization** and the ego can see it as the real goal of life. Before the transformation only the primitive women sang sentimentally (his feeling on a primitive level); in honor of the humanized lions the primitive and civilized women chant one hymn: **inner split has become inner harmony** [p.203].

### Active imagination and the deer [p.203-204]
- **Active imagination** (von Franz's definition): a way of meditating imaginatively by which one deliberately enters into contact with the unconscious and makes a conscious connection with psychic phenomena; "among the most important of Jung's discoveries." Comparable to Zen, Tantric Yoga, or the Jesuit *Exercitia*, but fundamentally different because the meditator stays "completely devoid of any conscious goal or program": a free individual's solitary experiment, the reverse of a guided attempt to master the unconscious. For Jung's description, see his paper "The Transcendent Function" [p.203-204].
- A woman's meditation: the Self appears as a **deer** that says: "I am your child and your mother. They call me the 'connecting animal' because I connect people, animals, and even stones with one another if I enter them. I am your fate or the 'objective I.' When I appear, I redeem you from the meaningless hazards of life. The fire burning inside me burns in the whole of nature. If a man loses it, he becomes egocentric, lonely, disoriented, and weak" [p.204].
- The Self as **animal** represents our instinctive nature and its connectedness with surroundings (why myths and fairy tales are full of helpful animals) [p.204]. [Captions p.203: the magic fox of Grimm's "The Golden Bird," Hanuman carrying two gods in his heart, Rin Tin Tin.]

### The Self woven into the world [p.204-205]
- The "nuclear atom" of the psyche is somehow woven into the whole world, inner and outer. Higher life forms are tuned to the space-time continuum: animals have particular foods, building materials, territories, and time rhythms (grass-eaters bear young when the grass is richest). A zoologist's phrase: the "inwardness" of each animal reaches far into the world and **"psychifies" time and space** [p.204-205].
- Likewise, in ways beyond our comprehension, our unconscious is attuned to our group, society, and beyond that to the space-time continuum and nature. The Naskapi Great Man gives hints on where and when to hunt, and the hunter evolves from dreams the words and melodies of magical songs that attract animals [p.205].
- This specific help is not for primitive man alone: dreams guide civilized people in inner and outer life, and many dreams concern outer details (the tree by the window, one's bicycle or car, a stone picked up on a walk), which can be raised to symbolism and become meaningful, so one may emerge "from a cold, impersonal world of meaningless chance" into "a world of our own, full of important and secretly ordered events" [p.205].
- But in civilized life most dreams concern the ego's development of the **"right" inner attitude toward the Self**, because modern thinking and behavior disturb the relation far more than in primitives, who "live directly from the inner center." Our uprooted consciousness is entangled in foreign external matters, and conscious mind creates an illusion of a clearly shaped "real" outer world that blocks other perceptions, though we are unconsciously connected to our psychic and physical environment [p.205].

### The stone [p.205-207]
- The Self is symbolized with special frequency as a **stone**, precious or otherwise (as in dream 6); also as a **crystal**, whose mathematically precise structure evokes the intuition of a spiritual ordering principle in "dead" matter: the crystal stands for **the union of extreme opposites, matter and spirit** [p.205-206].
- Why stones? The "just-so-ness" of their nature. People pick up unusual stones without knowing why, as if holding a living mystery; stones have been collected since the beginning of time as containers of the life-force. Ancient Germans believed dead spirits lived in tombstones; stones on graves may express that something eternal of the dead remains [p.206].
- Man's innermost center is akin to a stone perhaps because the stone is mere existence at the farthest remove from the emotions, feelings, fantasies and discursive thinking of ego-consciousness: the simplest and deepest experience, of something eternal, when one feels immortal and unalterable [p.206].
- Stone monuments to famous men or events: Jacob's stone where he had his dream; stones left on tombs of local saints; many cults use a stone for God or a place of worship; the **Ka'aba, the black stone of Mecca** [p.206-207]. [Captions p.205: rocks beneath Mt. Williamson; Mt. Rushmore; the sacred stone in the Temple of Jerusalem at the center of the city and, on a medieval map, of the world. Captions p.207: the Black Stone of Mecca carried by four tribal chieftains at the four corners of a carpet; the Stone of Scone, stolen from Westminster Abbey by Scottish Nationalists in 1950 and returned in 1951; the Blarney Stone.]
- Christian symbolism: Christ is "the stone which the builders rejected" that became "the head of the corner" (Luke) and the "spiritual rock" giving the water of life (1 Corinthians) [p.207].
- **Alchemy:** the **philosopher's stone** (lapis) sought as the secret of matter or the divine working in it; some alchemists dimly saw it as a symbol of something found only within the human psyche. Morienus (an old Arabian alchemist): "This thing is extracted from you: you are its mineral, and one can find it in you... If you recognize this, the love and approbation of the stone will grow within you" [p.207].
- The lapis symbolizes something that can never be lost or dissolved, something eternal, compared by some alchemists to the mystical experience of God in one's soul. It usually takes prolonged suffering to burn away the superfluous psychic elements concealing the stone. Most people have some profound experience of the Self at least once; **a genuinely religious attitude consists of an effort to discover that experience and keep in tune with it**, so the Self becomes an inner partner to whom one's attention is continually turned [p.207].

### Synchronicity [p.207-208]
- That the highest and most frequent symbol of the Self is lifeless matter points to the still unknown relationship between **psyche and matter** (psychosomatic medicine; it may prove that "psyche" and "matter" are the same phenomenon seen from within and from without). Jung's concept: **synchronicity**, a "meaningful coincidence" of outer and inner events not causally connected; the emphasis is on "meaningful" [p.207-208].
- Examples (von Franz's): an aircraft crashing before one's eyes as one blows one's nose is a meaningless coincidence. But if one buys a blue frock and the shop mistakenly delivers a black one on the day a near relative dies, the coincidence is meaningful through the symbolic meaning our society gives black. Where Jung observed such coincidences, the individual's dreams showed an **archetype activated in the unconscious** (the recipient of the black frock might also have dreamed of death): the archetype manifests simultaneously in inner and external events, and the common denominator is a symbolically expressed message [p.208].
- The Chinese theories of medicine, philosophy and building rest on a "science" of meaningful coincidences: the classical texts asked not what causes what but what "likes" to occur with what. Astrology, oracles and omens are similar attempts at a non-causal explanation of coincidence [p.208]. [Caption: a painting by Hans Haffenrichter resembling a crystal pattern.]
- It is "a completely open and insufficiently explored matter" for future psychologists and physicists. **Synchronistic events almost invariably accompany the crucial phases of individuation**, but pass unnoticed because the individual has not learned to watch for them and relate them to dream symbolism [p.208].

## 7. The relation to the Self [p.209-214]

### The modern emptiness and the one real adventure [p.209]
- More and more people, especially in large cities, suffer terrible emptiness and boredom as if waiting for something that never arrives; movies, television, spectator sports and political excitement divert them briefly before they return exhausted to "the wasteland of their own lives."
- "The only adventure that is still worthwhile for modern man lies in the inner realm of the unconscious psyche." Many turn to Yoga and Eastern practices, but these offer no new adventure: one takes over what is known to the Hindus or Chinese without meeting one's own inner life center. Eastern methods concentrate the mind inward (similar to introversion in analytical treatment), but Jung evolved a way of reaching the inner center and the "living mystery of the unconscious, alone and unaided," utterly different from a well-worn path [p.209].
- Daily attention to the Self is like living on two levels or in two worlds: one gives one's mind to outer duties as before while alert for hints and signs in dreams and external events by which the Self symbolizes its intentions, "the direction in which the life-stream is moving" [p.209].
- **Chinese simile of the cat watching the mousehole:** allow no other thoughts, attention neither too sharp nor too dull. The quoted text: training done thus will be effective in time, "like a ripe melon that automatically falls," and whatever it touches will cause the supreme awakening, "like one who drinks water and alone knows whether it is cold or warm... a great happiness similar to that one feels in meeting one's own father at the crossroads." The adventure is unique to each person and "cannot be copied or stolen" [p.209].

### Two ways of losing contact with the center [p.209-210]
1. **One-sidedness from a single instinctive drive or emotional image** (a sexually excited stag forgets hunger and security; primitives call it "loss of soul"), and **excessive daydreaming**, which circles around complexes and threatens the concentration and continuity of consciousness [p.209-210].
2. **Over-consolidation of ego-consciousness**: a disciplined consciousness is needed for civilized tasks (a railway signalman cannot daydream), but it tends to block impulses and messages from the center. Hence many civilized people's dreams try to correct consciousness's attitude toward the Self [p.210].

### The mandala [p.210-212]
- Mythological images of the Self stress the four corners of the world; the Great Man is often at the center of a circle divided into four. Jung used the Hindu word **mandala** ("magic circle") for such a structure, a symbolic representation of the psyche's "nuclear atom," whose essence we do not know. A Naskapi hunter pictured his Great Man not as a human but as a mandala [p.210].
- Other cultures use mandalas to **restore lost inner balance**: **Navaho sand paintings** to bring a sick person into harmony with himself and the cosmos and restore health (captions: a healer makes a sand painting with the patient seated in it; a plan of one that the patient circles before entering) [p.210-211]. In Eastern civilizations similar pictures consolidate the inner being or enable deep meditation; contemplation of a mandala is meant to bring inner peace and the feeling that life has found meaning and order. It does so too when it appears spontaneously in the dreams of modern people with no tradition: perhaps with greater effect, since knowledge and tradition sometimes blur or block the spontaneous experience [p.210]. [Caption p.210: the modern Brasilia cathedral's circular structure images perfect balance; city boredom offset by artificial excitement.]

**Dream case 7: the golden quadrangular disk (woman of 62)** [p.212; checked against the PDF page]
- Dream, a prelude to a new creative phase: a landscape in dim light; a hill crest in the background, with a **quadrangular disk shining like gold** moving along the rising line; dark plowed earth beginning to sprout in the foreground. She sees a round table with a gray stone slab top; at that instant the disk stands on the table (having left the hill, she does not know how or why) [p.212].
- Reading: landscapes in dreams and art often symbolize an inexpressible mood. Dim light = the clarity of daytime consciousness dimmed, so "inner nature" can show in its own light. The disk, until then an intuitive idea at the edge of her mental horizon, now becomes the center of her soul's landscape; the sprouting seed is the long attention paid to her dreams bearing fruit (reminding of the Great Man and plant life). The disk moves to the **"right" side, the side of consciousness and adaptation** (left = unadapted, unconscious reactions, sometimes "sinister"). It comes to rest on a **round stone table** and has found a permanent base [p.212].
- **Round = natural wholeness; quadrangular = its realization in consciousness** (von Franz cites Aniela Jaffe's chapter later in the book). When square disk and round table meet, conscious realization of the center is at hand. The round table as symbol of wholeness: King Arthur's Round Table, derived from the table of the Last Supper [p.212].
- Image check (PDF p.212): two small paintings by the dreamer show a flat gold-orange diamond or square on a green hill crest over brown earth, and the same square resting on a small round white table below. A 15th-century manuscript picture of the Round Table (the Grail vision) is reproduced beside it [p.212].
- Principle: whenever a person turns to the inner world and tries to know himself "not by ruminating about his subjective thoughts and feelings, but by following the expressions of his own objective nature such as dreams and genuine fantasies," the Self emerges sooner or later, and the ego finds an inner power with all the possibilities of renewal [p.212].

### The dark side of the Self [p.213-214]
- **Every personification of the unconscious (shadow, anima, animus, Self) has a light and a dark aspect.** The shadow may be base or an impulse toward growth; anima and animus can bring life-giving development and creativity or **petrification and physical death**; even the Self is ambivalent, as the Eskimo tale's little woman who offered rescue but turned the girl into a spider (printed p.196 reference) [p.213].
- **The dark side of the Self is the most dangerous because the Self is the greatest power in the psyche.** It can make people "spin" megalomaniac or other delusory fantasies that possess them; they feel with mounting excitement that they have solved the cosmic riddles and lose touch with human reality. **A reliable symptom: loss of the sense of humor and of human contacts** [p.213].

**Iranian tale: "The Secret of the Bath Badgerd"** [p.213-214]
- Prince Hatim Tai is ordered by his king to investigate the mysterious Bath Badgerd ("castle of nonexistence"), from which no one returns. At a round building a barber with a mirror leads him into the bath; as he enters the water, thunder, darkness, the barber vanishes and the water rises until Hatim, swimming desperately, reaches the top of the round cupola; he prays and grabs the **centerstone** of the cupola; thunder again, and he stands alone in a desert. After long wandering he comes to a beautiful garden with a circle of stone statues and in the center a **parrot in a cage**. A voice says he will likely not escape: Gayomart found an enormous diamond brighter than sun and moon and hid it by building this magical bath; the parrot is part of the magic; at its feet lie a golden bow and arrow on a golden chain; Hatim may shoot at the parrot three times; if he hits, the curse lifts; if not, he is petrified like the others. He misses once (legs turn to stone), twice (stone to the chest), and the third time shuts his eyes, says "God is great," shoots blindly and hits. Thunder and dust: the parrot becomes an enormous beautiful diamond and the statues revive and thank him [p.213-214].
- Reading: Self symbols are the First Man Gayomart, the round mandala-shaped building, the centerstone and the diamond. The **demonic parrot is the evil spirit of imitation** that makes one miss the target and petrify psychologically. **Individuation excludes parrot-like imitation of others:** people in every country have copied in outer or ritual behavior the original religious experience of Christ, Buddha or another master and become "petrified." To follow a great spiritual leader does not mean copying the pattern of his individuation but living one's own life with equal sincerity and devotion [p.214].
- The vanishing barber with the mirror is the **gift of reflection** Hatim loses when he most needs it. The rising waters are the risk of **drowning in the unconscious and getting lost in one's emotions**. To understand the unconscious one must not get "beside oneself" but stay emotionally within oneself; the ego must keep functioning normally. "Only if I remain an ordinary human being, conscious of my incompleteness, can I become receptive to the significant contents and processes of the unconscious" [p.214].
- The unresolved tension: feeling one with the whole universe while being a miserable earthly creature. If I despise myself as a statistical cipher, life has no meaning; if I feel part of something much greater, how do I keep my feet on the ground? It is very hard to keep these inner opposites united "without toppling over into one or the other extreme" [p.214]. [Captions: Masson's painting of the river Heraclitos overwhelming a Greek temple (overemphasis on reason leading to an eruption of instinct); a 15th-century illustration from *Roman de la Rose* of Logic confused by Nature; Mary Magdalen gazing into a mirror (de la Tour), the mirror as true inward-looking reflection.]

## 8. The social aspect of the Self [p.215-226]

### Individual worth against mass numbers [p.215-216]
- Population growth, especially in cities, depresses us: "I am only so-and-so living at such-and-such an address, like thousands of other people." Reports of unknown deaths increase the feeling that our lives count for nothing. This is when attention to the unconscious helps most: dreams show how each detail of one's life is interwoven with the most significant realities, making palpably true "that everything depends on the individual." One may feel the Great Man wants something and has set special tasks, and that gives strength "to swim against the stream of collective prejudice" [p.215].
- The task is not always agreeable: a dream may forbid next Sunday's trip with friends and demand creative work; obeying the unconscious means constant interference with conscious plans, so the obligation of individuation is often felt as a **burden** rather than a blessing [p.215-216].
- **St. Christopher** as the fitting symbol: proud of his strength, he served only the strongest: a king, then the devil when he saw the king feared him, then Christ when he saw the devil feared the crucifix. Told by a priest to wait for Christ at a ford, he carried people across for years; once, on a dark stormy night, a small child asked to be carried; he lifted him easily, but with each step the burden grew heavier until, midstream, "as if he carried the whole universe"; he realized he carried Christ, who gave him remission of his sins and eternal life [p.215-216].
- The miraculous child is a symbol of the Self that literally "depresses" the ordinary human being while being the only thing that can redeem him. Christ child with a world sphere in art: child and sphere are universal symbols of totality [p.216]. [Captions: British housing development and a Swiss athletics display as images of conformity; Blake's *Songs of Innocence and Experience* and the "divine child"; a 16th-century St. Christopher; a St. Christopher key medallion.]

### Isolation or a different kind of bond [p.216-218]
- Obeying the unconscious one often cannot do as one pleases or as others wish; it may require separation from family, partner or other ties. So people say attending to the unconscious makes one antisocial and egocentric; as a rule not true, because of the **collective (social) aspect of the Self** [p.216].
- Practically: a person following his dreams for a long time finds they concern relationships with other people (a warning against trusting someone; an agreeable meeting with someone never noticed). Two interpretations: (1) the figure is a **projection**, a symbol for an inner aspect of the dreamer (a dishonest neighbor in the dream pictures one's own dishonesty: "dream interpretation on the subjective level"); (2) the dream sometimes genuinely tells us about others, since man is in tune with surrounding beings and perceives their sufferings, problems and values instinctively, independent of conscious thought. Dreams let us look at these subliminal perceptions [p.217].
- To decide which, requires honest, attentive thought, but ultimately **the Self orders and regulates human relationships so long as the ego detects delusive projections and deals with them inside himself instead of outside**. In this way spiritually attuned, similarly oriented people find one another and create a **group that cuts across the usual social and organizational affiliations**, not in conflict with others but different and independent. Familiar bonds (kinship, common interests) are replaced by "a bond through the Self" [p.217-218]. [Captions p.217: kinship of consciousness as the nucleus of cultural development: the French Encyclopedists, the Dadaists (Max Ernst painting), research physicists at Britain's Wills Laboratory.]
- **Outer activities and obligations belong exclusively to the outer world and harm the secret activities of the unconscious**; through unconscious ties those who belong together come together. This is one reason why attempts to influence people by advertisements and political propaganda are destructive, even when inspired by idealistic motives [p.218].

### Can the unconscious be influenced? [p.218]
- One cannot influence one's own dreams; those who claim to are like von Franz ordering her disobedient dog to do what she notices it wants anyway, preserving the illusion of authority. Only a long process of interpreting dreams and confronting what they say gradually transforms the unconscious, and conscious attitudes must change too [p.218].
- **Misuse of symbols for public opinion:** true symbols impress the masses, but whether the mass unconscious is gripped cannot be calculated: no music publisher can predict a hit. No deliberate attempt to influence the unconscious has yet produced significant results; the mass unconscious preserves its autonomy like the individual one [p.218].
- Where manipulators add commercial pressure or violence they achieve temporary success, but this only represses genuine unconscious reactions; **mass repression leads to the same result as individual repression: neurotic dissociation and psychological illness.** Such repressions fail in the long run because they oppose our instincts [p.219].
- The unconscious may use a motif from the external world: many modern dreams involve **Berlin** as a symbol of the psychic weak spot, the place of danger and where the Self is apt to appear, where the dreamer is torn by conflict and might unite inner opposites. An extraordinary number of dream reactions to the film *Hiroshima Mon Amour* expressed that either the two lovers (French girl and Japanese man) must unite (union of inner opposites) or there would be an atomic explosion (complete dissociation, equivalent to madness) [p.219; also captions p.218].
- [Captions p.219: positive anima figures as helpers (David inspired by the muse, a goddess saving a sailor, Lady Luck on a Monte Carlo postcard); Delacroix's Liberty leading the revolutionaries and the film *Metropolis* (a woman urging robot-like workers toward spiritual liberation) as the anima's liberating function.]

### Small groups [p.219-220]
- From the study of social behavior in higher animals, small groups of **about 10 to 50 individuals** give the best living conditions for the animal and group; man seems no exception: physical well-being, psychic health and cultural efficiency seem to flourish best in such groups. The Self apparently tends to produce such small groups, creating at once **sharply defined ties of feeling between certain individuals and a feeling of relatedness to all people**. Only connections created by the Self give assurance that envy, jealousy, fighting and negative projections will not break up the group. Thus "an unconditional devotion to one's own process of individuation also brings about the best possible adaptation" [p.220].
- This does not remove collisions of opinion and conflicting obligations; one must constantly withdraw and listen to find the individual standpoint the Self intends [p.220].

### Dream case 8: the museum attic and Miss X (a man devoted to freeing his country) [p.220-222]
- Fanatical political activity (not essential duties) seems incompatible with individuation. Dreamer: a man devoted entirely to freeing his country from foreign occupation [p.220].
- Dream: with compatriots he climbs to the attic of a museum, a hall painted black like a ship's cabin. A distinguished middle-aged lady, "Miss X, daughter of X" (X a national hero who centuries ago tried to free the country, comparable to Joan of Arc or William Tell; in reality he had no children), opens the door. Two aristocratic ladies' portraits in flowery brocades come to life: first the eyes, then the chest seems to breathe. People go to a lecture room where Miss X says that through her intuition and feeling the portraits came alive; some indignantly call her mad and leave [p.220].
- Reading: Miss X is an anima figure purely created by the dream (like "Wilhelmina Tell"). The name implies **today the dreamer should not try, as X did, to free his country in an outer way: liberation is accomplished by the anima, the dreamer's soul, who brings the images of the unconscious to life.** The black color: darkness, night, a turning inward; the museum is also a ship: when the mainland of collective consciousness is flooded by unconsciousness and barbarism, this museum-ship of living images may become a saving **ark** to another spiritual shore. Portraits in museums are dead remains of the past; images of the unconscious are often regarded so until found alive [p.220-221].
- The indignant people are the dreamer's side that follows collective opinion and rejects bringing psychic images to life ("what if they begin dropping atom bombs on us? Psychological insight won't be much help"): statistical thinking and extraverted rational prejudice. But genuine liberation now starts with a **psychological transformation**: "To what end does one liberate one's country if afterward there is no meaningful goal of life?" If man finds no meaning, it makes no difference if he wastes away under a Communist or capitalist regime; freedom matters only if used to create something meaningful. So finding inner meaning must have priority [p.221].
- **Criticism of mass influence:** newspapers, radio, television and advertising rest on sampling techniques that reveal collective attitudes and on the prejudices, projections and complexes (mainly the power complex) of the manipulators. Statistics do no justice to the individual (the average stone in a heap may be five centimeters, but few stones are exactly that size). The second factor can create nothing positive. But **a single individual devoted to individuation often has a positive contagious effect, "as if a spark leaps from one to another,"** usually without intending to influence and often without words [p.221].

## 9. Individuation and religion [p.221-226]

### Religious symbols and the unconscious [p.221-222]
- Nearly all religious systems contain images that symbolize individuation or some stages. In Christian countries the Self is projected onto the second Adam, Christ; in the East onto Krishna and Buddha. For people really contained in a religion, religious symbols psychologically regulate their lives and dreams revolve around them [p.221].
- **Catholic women's dreams:** when Pius XII declared the **Assumption of Mary**, a Catholic woman dreamed she was a Catholic priestess (her unconscious extending the dogma: "If Mary is now almost a goddess, she should have priestesses"). Another, resistant to minor outer aspects of her creed, dreamed her home church was pulled down and rebuilt but the tabernacle with the host and the statue of the Virgin were to be moved to the new church: man-made aspects need renewal; the basic symbols (God become Man; the Great Mother, the Virgin Mary) survive [p.221].
- These show the unconscious's living interest in conscious religious representations [p.221-222]. [Captions: a 15th-century Mary statue containing God and Christ, an expression of the Great Mother archetype (p.222); a Book of Hours miniature of Mary with the Trinity: the Assumption of the Virgin as domina rerum made the Trinity fourfold, corresponding with the basic archetype of completeness (p.223).]

### The fourth element [p.222]
- General trend Jung often observed in modern Christian (Protestant or Catholic) unconscious material: a tendency to round off the trinitarian Godhead with a **fourth element, tending to be feminine, dark and even evil**. It always existed in religious representation but was split from God as his counterpart: matter itself or the lord of matter (the devil). The unconscious now seems to want to reunite these extremes: "the light having become too bright and the darkness too somber." The central symbol of religion is most exposed to unconscious tendencies to transform [p.222].

### Tibetan abbot on mandalas; two aspects [p.222]
- A Tibetan abbot told Jung that the most impressive mandalas are built by imagination or directed fantasy when the psychological balance of the group is disturbed or when a thought cannot be rendered because it is not contained in the sacred doctrine and must be searched for. Hence **two aspects**: the mandala serves a **conservative purpose** (restoring a previously existing order) and a **creative purpose** (giving form to something new and unique; perhaps even more important and not contradictory, since what restores the old order usually involves some new creation). The older pattern returns "on a higher level": **the ascending spiral, which grows upward while returning again and again to the same point** [p.222].

### Dream case 9: the spiral and the wing of Satan (a simple Protestant woman) [p.222-224]
- In a dream she is ordered to paint the Godhead; later (also in a dream) she sees it in a book. Of God she sees only his wafting **cloak**, with light and shadow in beautiful drapery, contrasting with the stability of a **spiral in the deep blue sky**; she does not look closely at the other figure on the rocks; waking, she realizes it was "God himself" and gets a lasting frightful shock [p.222].
- The Holy Ghost is usually a fiery wheel or dove; here a **spiral** appears: a new thought "not yet contained in the doctrine," arising spontaneously. That the Holy Ghost works for further development of religious understanding is not new; the spiral form is [p.222-223].
- Second painting (also from a dream): the dreamer with her positive animus stands above Jerusalem as the **wing of Satan** descends to darken the city. The wing reminded her of God's cloak in the first picture; in the first the spectator is high in heaven and sees a terrific split between the rocks, and the cloak's movement is an attempt to reach Christ (the figure on the right) that does not quite succeed. In the second, the same thing is seen from below, from a human angle: what, seen from above, is a part of God moving and spreading with the spiral above it, is, from our human reality, **the dark uncanny wing of the devil** [p.223].
- Collective meaning beyond the personal: they may foretell **the descent of a divine darkness upon the Christian hemisphere** that points toward further evolution. Since the spiral's axis moves not upward but into the background of the picture, further evolution leads neither to greater spiritual height nor down into matter but "to another dimension, probably into the background of these divine figures. And that means into the unconscious" [p.223].
- Image check (PDF p.224): the left painting shows concentric blue-and-white spiral rings in a deep blue sky above a large pale rounded form, with two robed, hooded figures on rocky ground to either side; the right painting shows a large dark tattered wing or cloak descending in smoke-like lines over a city of low flat buildings, with two small human figures below [p.224].
- When religious symbols differing from those known emerge from an individual's unconscious, it is feared that they will wrongfully alter or diminish officially recognized symbols; this fear makes many reject analytical psychology and the unconscious [p.223].

### Three religious types [p.224]
1. People who **genuinely believe** their religious doctrines: symbols "click" with what they feel inside; consciousness and the unconscious background are in relative harmony; they can look at new psychological findings without prejudice, and unorthodox dream details are integrated into their view.
2. People who have **completely lost their faith** and replaced it with conscious rational opinions: depth psychology is for them an introduction to newly discovered areas, causing no trouble in testing dreams.
3. People who in one part (probably the head) no longer believe while another part still does. Example: **Voltaire**, who attacked the Church with rational argument (*ecrasez l'infame*) but by some reports begged for extreme unction on his deathbed: head unreligious, feelings and emotions still orthodox. Like a person stuck in the automatic doors of a bus, neither out in free space nor back inside. Their dreams could help but they find it hard to turn to the unconscious because they do not know what they think and want. "To take the unconscious seriously is ultimately a matter of personal courage and integrity" [p.224].
- Why the no-man's-land: all official religious doctrines belong to **collective consciousness (what Freud called the super-ego)**, but once, long ago, they sprang from the unconscious [p.224]. Theologians and historians of religion often hold that there was once a "revelation"; von Franz has searched for years for concrete evidence of the Jungian hypothesis and notes most rituals are too old to trace [p.224-225].

### Origin of rituals from individual unconscious revelation [p.225]
**Black Elk (Oglala Sioux, from *Black Elk Speaks*):** at nine, seriously ill, in a coma he had a tremendous vision of four groups of beautiful horses from the four corners of the world and, seated in a cloud, the **Six Grandfathers**, ancestral spirits, who gave him six healing symbols and new ways of life. At 16 he developed a terrible phobia of approaching thunderstorms: "thunder beings" called him "to make haste," like the horses' thunder in his vision. An old medicine man said his fear came from keeping the vision to himself; he must tell it to his tribe. He did, and later he and the tribe acted out the vision in a ritual with real horses; many felt better and some were cured of diseases. "Even the horses seemed to be healthier and happier after the dance." The ritual was not repeated because the tribe was destroyed soon afterward [p.225].

**Eskimo eagle festival (Colville River, Alaska):** a young hunter shot an unusual eagle, was struck by its beauty, stuffed it and made it a fetish with sacrifices. Hunting far inland, two animal-men messengers led him to the land of the eagles; a dark drumming was the heartbeat of the dead eagle's mother; the eagle spirit appeared as a woman in black and asked him to start an eagle festival to honor her dead son. After being shown how, he found himself exhausted back at the meeting place, returned home and taught his people the great eagle festival, which they have performed faithfully since [p.225].

- Lesson: **a ritual or religious custom can spring directly from an unconscious revelation experienced by a single individual**; groups then develop religious activities with enormous influence on society, shaped and reshaped by words and actions, beautified, increasingly definite. The **drawback of this crystallizing**: more and more people have no personal knowledge of the original experience and can only believe what elders and teachers say; they no longer know such happenings are real or what the experience feels like [p.225].
- Old forms resist further creative alterations from the unconscious; theologians sometimes defend "true" symbols against the discovery of a religious function in the psyche, forgetting that their values owe existence to that function. "Without a human psyche to receive divine inspirations and utter them in words or shape them in art, no religious symbol has ever come into the reality of our human life" (prophets and evangelists). If someone objects there is a religious reality independent of the psyche, von Franz asks: "Who says this, if not a human psyche?" [p.225-226].

### Closing: what the discovery of the unconscious shuts and opens [p.226]
- The discovery of the unconscious **shuts one door forever**: the illusion that one can know spiritual reality "in itself" (as Heisenberg's principle of indeterminacy shut out the delusion that we can comprehend an absolute physical reality). It compensates by opening "an immense and unexplored new field of realizations, within which objective scientific investigation combines in a strange new way with personal ethical adventure" [p.226].
- A second door: it is practically impossible to impart the whole reality of one's experience in the new field; much is unique and only partially communicable. Hence also closed is the illusion that one can completely understand another and tell him what is right for him. Compensation: the **social function of the Self**, working hidden to unite separate individuals who belong together; "intellectual chit-chat is thus replaced by meaningful events that occur in the reality of the psyche" [p.226]. [Caption: Erhard Jacoby's painting of a man, woman and child looking at the same scene and seeing different details, against a gray background of the unknown: each psyche perceives the world slightly differently; the outer world exists only through conscious perception.]
- Final claim: entering individuation seriously means a completely new orientation toward life and, for scientists, toward outer facts; how it will work out cannot be predicted, but "Jung's discovery of the process of individuation is a fact that future generations will have to take into account if they want to avoid drifting into a stagnant or even regressive outlook" [p.226].

### Sets of four
- Four stages of the anima: Eve, Helen, Mary, Sapientia [p.182]. Four stages of the animus: physical power, initiative, the word, meaning [p.192]. Fourfold structures of the Self: 4, 8, 16 [p.197]; four corners of the world, circle divided into four [p.210]; the 16 pictures in the Mass book [p.182]; the Trinity completed by a fourth, dark/feminine element [p.222]; the four functions of consciousness named in passing [p.197].
