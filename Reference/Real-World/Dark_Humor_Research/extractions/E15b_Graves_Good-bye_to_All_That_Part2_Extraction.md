## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-05 is.** Graves's best-known prose book, an autobiography written "at the age of thirty-three" (1929) by a Special Reserve officer-poet of the Royal Welch Fusiliers; see E15a for the framing. In my range it is the war narrative proper: Graves's attachment to the Welsh Regiment's Second Battalion ends (L4429-4439), he joins the regular Second Battalion Royal Welch Fusiliers (Laventie, Cuinchy, Loos), then the First Battalion (Festubert, Montagne le Fayel, the Somme), with two stretches at the Havre base and one at the Third Battalion's home camp. Chapters XIV-XX are the memoir's centre of gravity: Loos (L5042-5827), the Fricourt trenches (L6690-6918), the Somme and his wounding at High Wood (L7143-7698) and the false report of his death (L7705-7768). Documents printed inside the narrative: two letters from the colonel (a formal death notice and a relieved personal letter, L7514-7534, L7623-7645), a *Times* advertisement-department letter and cutting, a midnight battalion order, the Loos orders as copied (L5142-5240), and a full pamphlet, "A Mother's Answer to 'A Common Soldier,'" with press testimonials (L7788-7927).

**Evidence base.** One narrator, one class, one regiment, one army, 1915-January 1917. Chapter XIII is presented as contemporary letters and diary extracts (June 1915); the rest is retrospective narrative (1929) built partly on a rough draft written during the war (the text says at L7971-7972 that the "earlier war chapters" are based on a novel he began in 1916 and left unfinished). Many anecdotes are second-hand: the Middlesex survivors' reception (told by Hill), the High Wood account (the brigade-major and O. M. Roberts), the Havre instructors' mess, Sassoon's feats at Mametz Wood, the Adelphi major's story. The narrator marks some as hearsay ("it was said," "I was told") and gives one pseudonym ("Phillips, that was not his name," L6523-6524). The NCOs and men speak in brief quoted bursts; the officer-narrator reports what the men said and how he classified it.

**What it does NOT contain.** No theory of humor; the explicit statements about what humor or joking does are occasional and on particular occasions (E15b-3, 26, 52, 65, 73, 80, 120, 125). No extended other-ranks narrator (Sergeants Townsend and Smith, Baxter, Tottie Fahy and 33 Williams appear only as quoted speakers or reported actors). No account of the 1 July 1916 attack (Graves was at Litherland). No detailed executions; the shooting of two alleged spies (E15b-7) and of one man by an officer in each of two attacks (E15b-78) are reported plainly or at second hand. No women's voices except billet landladies (E15b-58, 84) and the hostess in Kent (E15b-124). The enemy speaks only in a grenade message (E15b-29), a megaphone exchange (E15b-32), a machine-gun call (E15b-62), a prisoner's remark and a weeping wounded officer (E15b-114). Nothing here of the post-war chapters, which belong to E15c.

**Format and damage.** The Gutenberg text is proofread, has no page markers (I cite chapter numeral plus line number "L"), carries transcriber marks (a diamond for corrected misprints, which I drop), illustration captions inline, and eight end-of-book footnotes (L12313-12342). Footnotes [3]-[8] annotate passages in my range (E15b-9, 20, 35, 47, 57, 114); [1] and [2] belong to E15a. The battalion order at L7396-7411 is printed in spaced columns that read across (the "aaa" is a telegraph stop). Text-file typos such as "casualities" (L4173) and "Henrv" (L5655) are left alone or avoided by paraphrase; one obvious misprint is bracketed in a quotation (E15b-99). L4130-4152 overlap E15a's range (a stiff arm in a trench is also in E15a-53) and L8181-8190 overlap E15c's start (Cléry, E15b-131); I read about ten lines past L8190 for context only.

**Extraction scope and length.** The brief asked for every humor-bearing passage, and this stretch is anecdote-dense, so the findings run well above the rules file's 10-18% guide (about 27,000 words against roughly 45,000 words of source in the range). Findings flagged "(restrained handling)" are the ones whose subject (atrocity, executions, sexual material, mutilation, decomposition) is summarized only.

**Quotation conventions.** Quotations are exact except for (a) American spelling (the project rule, in quotations too: "honor," "color," "armor," "neighbor," "encyclopedia," "checks," "colored"); (b) square brackets for my insertions, ellipses for my cuts, and the text's own dashes standing in for oaths are kept; (c) italics dropped. "My gloss" marks any meaning the text does not give. **Period caricature is documented, not endorsed**: Scots ("Jocks"), Irish, Welsh dialect, Canadians and Australians, French civilians, colonial and Indian troops, Belgians, Portuguese, Algerians, the Public Schools men, and class and regional rankings of reliability.

**Retrospective vs. period text.** Chapter XIII's letters are the closest to the time; the Loos orders, the colonel's letters, the battalion order and the press items are documents kept by the narrator; everything else is recollection from about 1929, with the habit of flash-forward footnotes ("Jenkins was killed not long after," fn. [3]) and of closing an anecdote with the fates of the people in it.

### A. Cambrin-Cuinchy letters, billets, and the Welsh regiment's last weeks (L4140-4440)

**[E15b-1]. Boundary item: the miners' platoon and a comrade's stiffened arm (a passage that straddles the start of this unit's range).** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4130-4147)
A dead sanitary-man lies on the fire-step awaiting burial, one arm stretched across the trench. Passing comrades push it aside with a gibe addressed to him, or "shake hands" with it, one using the greeting "Put it there, Billy Boy." Graves's explanation follows at once: "Of course, they're miners and accustomed to death. They have a very limited morality, but they keep to it." The code he then lists: they will not rob anyone "in their own platoon," treat every stranger as an enemy until proved a friend, and then "there is nothing they won't do for him." A lance-corporal's letter home (read in censoring) tells his wife that the French girls are pleasant, so she need not worry, but that he far prefers her. (The first ten lines of this passage also fall in E15a's range, E15a-53.) The humor here is in-group handling of a comrade's body, and the narrator's gloss gives it two causes: occupational familiarity with death (miners) and a tight, bounded loyalty.
*Candidate application:* A unit drawn from a dangerous trade may treat the dead with a familiar roughness that outsiders would read as callous; the author's framing is that this coexists with a strict inner code of loyalty.

**[E15b-2]. The platoon sweepstake before an attack: survivors divide the pool.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4197-4203)
Graves reports that before "a show" the platoon pools its cash and the survivors split it afterward; his man Beaumont had won about five pounds after the Rue du Bois action. Graves calls it "a sweepstake of the sort that leaves no bitterness behind it," and gives the reasoning in three clauses: "Those who are killed can't complain," the wounded "would have given far more than that to escape as they have," and "the unwounded regard the money as a consolation prize for still being here." Mechanism: a gambling custom turned into a rough insurance and a tacit acknowledgement of who survives; the narrator explains its social function himself. The same diary entry (L4185-4188) records that Beaumont, "the last unwounded survivor of the original battalion, except for the transport men," had just been killed (the manner of death is not reproduced here).
*Candidate application:* A unit custom that prices survival openly, and has an agreed rule for why no one resents it, is a documented form of fatalistic bookkeeping; the writer places the dead man's winnings and death in the same entry without comment.

**[E15b-3]. The stated limit: jokes work on the wounded and the dead, but not on a slow dying.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4170-4185)
After a heavy day (seventeen casualties from bombs and grenades in one company) Graves describes a man with a head wound lying in the trench for hours, and says he had never seen human brains before and "had somehow regarded them as a poetical figment" (the graphic details are summarized, not reproduced). Then comes the narrator's explicit rule of license: "One can joke with a badly-wounded man and congratulate him on being out of it. One can disregard a dead man." But "even a miner" cannot make a joke "that sounds like a joke" over a man who takes three hours to die of such a wound. Context: he had arrived, on his own account, from a "gentle introduction" to the front into a nasty salient where "casualities are always heavy" (spelled as printed in the text), and had been walking the trench whistling *The Farmer's Boy* "to keep up my spirits" when he came on the scene.
*Candidate application:* A rare direct statement of where the joking register fails: not at death, and not at the lightly or seriously wounded, but at prolonged helpless dying. A character may know this boundary from experience and keep silent in front of it.

**[E15b-4]. A Béthune notice and a schoolgirl's shorthand: the plain and the dry side by side.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4149-4168)
Billeted in Béthune, Graves lists the amenities (a swimming bath, shops, a cake-shop, a hotel with dinner, a theater for brigade "gaffs") and then copies a notice he saw by the canal: "Troops are forbidden to bomb fish. By order of the Town Major." (Mechanism: administrative prohibition naming a thing soldiers evidently did.) In the same entry, the French family's elder daughter explains why her school notes are in abbreviations: the teacher spoke fast because part of the school was a troop billet, the Germans were shelling it, and each return from the cellar left less time. Graves reports this without comment and without a joke.
*Candidate application:* Civilian life in a billet town is reported without irony; the humor comes from the army's own notices and from the narrator's list-making.

**[E15b-5]. The company sergeant-major and the officer in the currant bushes.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4214-4224)
In Vermelles's overgrown gardens Graves and the company sergeant-major (a regular) began eating currants from opposite ends of a row, met, and both "remembered our dignity." They exchanged salutes and walked off, came back "hoping the coast was clear," saluted again, and finally "had to leave the currants and pretend that we were merely admiring the flowers." Graves says he does not quite know why he felt that way, supposing it "natural" in the regular and that "it was courtesy to his scruples that made me stop." The last sentence of the paragraph is the reveal: "along came a couple of privates and stripped the bushes clean." Mechanism: a comedy of rank etiquette (a two-man mock-ceremony of salutes) with the lower ranks as the ones who actually get the fruit.
*Candidate application:* The frontier of rank courtesy as a source of harmless, shared embarrassment between an officer and a regular senior NCO; the joke works because neither will break first, and the privates do not have the same scruple.

**[E15b-6]. Cricket in a ruined town: a parrot cage for a wicket, and Skelton on the dead bird.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4226-4246)
An officers-versus-sergeants cricket match is played in an enclosure out of the enemy's view: Graves scores twenty-four, the bat is a rafter, the ball a rag tied with string, "the wicket was a parrot cage with the grisly remains of a parrot inside," a bird that had starved when the French evacuated. Graves recalls a verse by the sixteenth-century poet Skelton that says a dead parrot "doth not putrify" and that "all things mortal shall turn unto nought" except the soul. The match is "broken up suddenly" by German machine-gun fire at a British aeroplane; the bullets, falling from height, had "a penetrative power greater than an ordinary spent bullet," and "It was not aimed at us." Mechanism: the improvised game, the literary allusion laid over a neglected corpse, and then the abrupt practical interruption, given with the same measured tone.
*Candidate application:* A well-read officer who reaches for a poem at the sight of a dead pet is a documented type of cultured gallows register; the sergeants-versus-officers match is a rank-leveling ritual with a rule (they play, stripped of status, at the front).

**[E15b-7]. Spies shot: a plain sentence in a paragraph about idleness.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4248-4254)
The diary entry says the days are idle and that no drill or fortification work is possible so near the Germans, and in the same paragraph states "To-day two spies were shot," a civilian who had hung on in a cellar and apparently signaled news and a German soldier disguised as a Royal Engineers corporal found tampering with telephone wires. No joke and no comment follow; the next sentence turns to the officers' revolver practice.
*Candidate application:* An execution reported as one clause between a remark on boredom and a pastime is a documented way the narrator keeps these events at the register of local news.

**[E15b-8]. A glass case of artificial fruit as a target: mock mercy-killing.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4254-4267)
Officers practicing with revolvers fetch the only undestroyed object in the billet area, a glass case of artificial fruit and flowers, and set it on a post at fifty yards. Jenkins says he has always wanted to smash one ("My aunt had one. It's the sort of thing that would survive an intense bombardment."). After many misses they move to twenty yards and a volley knocks the post over without breaking the case; Jenkins says it "must be bewitched." Walker then says "No, it's in pain; we must put it out of its suffering," and finishes it at close range. Graves's own aside: "For a moment I felt a tender impulse to rescue it. But I smothered it." Mechanism: the vocabulary of putting down a wounded animal applied to a domestic ornament; the "tender impulse" shows the narrator's moment of misplaced feeling. Jenkins is dead "not long after" (fn. [3]).
*Candidate application:* Subalterns under no pressure may displace violent habit and mercy-killing language onto an object; the footnote's flat note that Jenkins died soon after is typical of the memoir's placement of loss.

**[E15b-9]. Church glass and the Irish soldiers' warning.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4269-4283; fn. [3])
In a ruined church with eight unexploded shells sticking in the tower, Graves breaks out a plate-sized piece of stained glass and gives it to Jenkins as a "Souvenir"; it proves to be a medieval image of St. Peter's hand with the keys. Two Munster men (Irish Catholics) tell Jenkins "it will bring you no luck." Footnote [3]: "Jenkins was killed not long after." Mechanism: no joke, but the juxtaposition of souvenir-taking, a superstition voiced by other ranks, and the later footnote makes an implicit causal suggestion that the text does not state.
*Candidate application:* Soldier superstition is voiced by ordinary men, the officer ignores it, and the author's afterword leaves the correlation unspoken.

**[E15b-10]. Banter about the post-war: the sergeant-major, the barrack square and the civilian clothes.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4285-4292)
Walker ragged Dunn, a regular who had come straight from Sandhurst: he will be sorry when the war ends, his "occupation will be gone," he will have to go back on the barrack square to learn to form fours "regimentally," and Walker will pay the sergeant-major "half a crown to make you really sweat" while standing "in civvies at the barrack-gate laughing at you." Mechanism: mock-prophecy of peace as the real danger to the regular's career; the target is the professional's identity and peacetime drill.
*Candidate application:* Wartime volunteers joking that the regular's skill is worthless in peace, and the regular being the butt, is a documented pattern of banter between the two types of officer.

**[E15b-11]. Furber: a "great joke" on a man with shattered nerves, and the pessimist everyone likes.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4294-4302)
A company commander named Furber has "his nerves ... in pieces," and someone played a "dirty joke" on him by rolling an undetonated bomb down his cellar steps "to frighten him." "This was thought a great joke." Furber is also "the greatest pessimist out here": he has bet the adjutant that the trench lines will be no more than a mile from where they are two years hence. "Every one laughs at Furber, but they like him because he sings sentimental cockney songs at the brigade gaffs." Mechanism: a prank aimed at the visibly fragile; a pessimist's prediction as a wager; affection earned by singing.
*Candidate application:* A documented case in which the unit treats a man with broken nerves both as a butt and as a favorite; the memoir records the prank without the narrator's disapproval and without any rebuke from authority.

### B. Chapter XIV: superstition, bad news, billet sex-talk and the road to the Royal Welch (L4307-4470)

**[E15b-12]. "Pessimism made everyone superstitious": a premonition, a shell, and a ghost.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4310-4355)
Graves says the division's defensive spirit made him pessimistic and then superstitious: "Pessimism made everyone superstitious." Three small stories follow. (1) Sergeant Smith recounts that his predecessor platoon officer foretold his own death on the eve of an attack, instructing the sergeant to send his kit home, divide five hundred francs among the survivors (keeping a hundred himself), and to burn his diary, which the parents must not see; the sergeant ends "And that's how it was" and says he did exactly as asked. (2) Graves, walking a trench, throws himself flat for no reason he can name and a whizz-bang lands where he had been; the sergeant rushes back and asks "Are you killed, sir?" (3) He sees "a ghost," Private Challoner, killed at Festubert in May, who had promised "I'll meet you again in France, sir," pass the window of a company billet where the officers were eating a celebration dinner (fish, new potatoes, green peas, asparagus, mutton chops, strawberries and cream, and three bottles of Pommard), salute and walk on, leaving only "a fag-end smoking on the pavement." The dry last line: "Ghosts were numerous in France at the time."
*Candidate application:* The premonition narrated by an NCO with no humor, and the one-line closer to the ghost story, show two registers in one chapter: the sergeant's plain loyalty to a dead officer's instructions and the narrator's own deadpan shrug.

**[E15b-13]. The Welsh miner on listening duty: "it's when they stop."** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4357-4377)
Under constant mining and counter-mining, Graves enters an upper boring about twenty feet under the German lines and finds a Welsh miner (one of the battalion's own men now in the Royal Engineers) on listening duty. The man whispers: "So long as they work, I don't mind; it's when they stop." He does two-hour spells by candlelight, reading a paper-backed novelette called *From Mill Girl to Duchess* (allowed because it does not interfere with listening). Graves adds that tunnelers "were notorious thieves," who "would snatch things up from the trench and scurry off with them into their borings; just like mice." (Earlier in the same paragraph he describes an R.E. officer who won the Victoria Cross for racing to re-tamp a failed charge in a duel of mines.)
*Candidate application:* The listener's remark converts the enemy's noise into reassurance and reveals the dread of silence in one sentence; the cheap romance novel beside it is the sort of absurd detail that the narrator notices without commenting.

**[E15b-14]. Bad news from home: two ways to react, and an officer who went out on a raid.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4379-4393)
Graves states: "Bad news in the trenches might affect a man in either of two ways. It might drive him to suicide (or recklessness amounting to suicide), or it might seem trivial in comparison with present expediences and be disregarded." Unless his leave was due he was "helpless." An illustration: a year later an officer of the North Staffordshire Regiment learned from home that his wife was living with another man, went on a raid the same night and was reported killed or captured (the men with him said there had been a fight and they came back without him), and was arrested two days later at Béthune trying to board a leave train with the intention of shooting the wife and her lover (the text uses a harsher phrase), and was cashiered rather than shot for desertion in the face of the enemy; he went to another regiment as a private. "I do not know what happened afterwards." No joke or comment.
*Candidate application:* The plain register for private catastrophe that the unit cannot touch; the military justice outcome is reported without humor.

**[E15b-15]. Billet talk, teasing and the army brothel (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4395-4427)
The bad news in the letter concerns Graves's friend Dick, from a cousin who still attends Charterhouse (Graves tells us he judged it "a very cruel act of spite," and a later letter from Dick partly reassures him; the specific allegation is not reproduced here). Graves describes the "uncleanness of sex-life in billets": two officers describe sleeping with a mother and daughter in the same billet and tossing a coin for the mother; the army's licensed brothel ("the Red Lamp") had a queue of about 150 men, each woman served "nearly a battalion" a week, and the assistant provost-marshal said three weeks was the usual limit, after which the woman "retires on her earnings" with a mock-genteel flourish (wording not reproduced). Graves was "always being teased" because he would not go with even the "nicer girls," and he excused himself "in the only way they could understand": by saying he did not want to catch venereal disease. Billet talk included boasts about "the peculiar bed-manners of the French women," told in mock-French. "I was glad when we were back in trenches."
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* the army's own officials joke in euphemism about the women; the narrator's refusal is excused in the group's own terms, not moral ones, to avoid breaking solidarity.
*Candidate application:* A character who declines the group's sexual custom must supply an excuse the group's code accepts; the chapter records the pressure and the dodge, not a moral lecture.

**[E15b-16]. Meeting the Prince of Wales in the Béthune public bath: capping a boast.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4445-4459)
Graves is alone with the Prince (a lieutenant in a siege battery) at the public bath one morning. "He was graciously pleased to remark how emphatically cold the water was and I loyally assented that he was emphatically right." Afterward, Graves joked to his friend Frank, "I have just met our future King in a bath." Frank topped it by saying two days before he had had "a friendly talk with him in the A.S.C. latrines" (the location is only a comparative indignity; no bodily function is described). Graves also overhears the Prince complaining indignantly that General French refused to let him go up into the line, and describes the Prince's favorite café, the *Globe*, as "reserved for British officers and French civilians; principally spies by the look of them."
*Mechanism:* mock-courtly report of a banal exchange, then the officers' game of trumping the other's anecdote with a less dignified setting; the narrator also gives the heir's own grievance as a one-line aside.
*Candidate application:* Brushes with royalty are rendered in mess-style one-upmanship, and the heir's wish for the line is stated without comment.

**[E15b-17]. Fifty-two hours to go seventeen miles: the deliberate leisurely transfer.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4429-4467)
Graves and Robertson (two Third Battalion officers posted to the Second Battalion) are told a train will leave in a few minutes; they "decided to miss it" and stay at a hotel overnight, spend a day walking in fields at a junction, and reach battalion headquarters at Laventie after "fifty-two hours to come seventeen miles." The text says they "agreed to take our journey as leisurely as possible." There is no complaint from the authorities recorded. Before leaving the Welsh they note that the men "crowded round to shake hands and wish us luck."
*Candidate application:* An understated, passive resistance to a posting (stretching a journey by scheduling) is treated here as a pleasure to be taken, not a grievance.

### C. The Royal Welch Fusiliers, Laventie: regimental custom as ritual contempt (L4468-4930)

**[E15b-18]. The adjutant's non-welcome, and the Surrey-man's briefing on regular-battalion custom.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4466-4559)
Arriving at battalion headquarters, the two new officers get no handshake, drink or welcome; the adjutant says "I see. Well, which of you is senior? Oh, never mind." The East Surrey subaltern known as "the Surrey-man" explains: this is a regular battalion, one of only four still "more or less its old self," and the "peace-time custom of taking no notice of newly-joined officers" lasts about six months. Rules he lists: junior officers keep silent in the battalion mess ("We've got to jolly well keep still and look like furniture"); "Only officers of the rank of captain are allowed to drink whisky or turn on the gramophone"; mess bills are high from a prewar debt; subalterns who cannot ride attend riding school every afternoon, with pack-saddles instead of riding saddles and crossed stirrups; all wear shorts by regimental order; "The battalion thinks it's still in India" and the officers treat French civilians in the way they treated colonial subjects (the slur Graves records is not reproduced here). The Surrey-man's summary of their attitude: "The battalion doesn't recognize it socially," although in the trenches he would rather be with them than any other battalion, because "the senior officers do know their job" and the NCOs "are absolutely trustworthy." On the riding school's abuse, he says: "The funny thing is that they don't realize that they are treating us badly—it's such an honor to be serving with the regiment. So the best thing is to pretend you don't care what they do or say."
*Candidate application:* A regimental culture that preserves peacetime hazing and silence as a form of belonging, with the competence of its NCOs and field officers a reason subordinates endure it. The advice to feign indifference is a rule of license for the junior.

**[E15b-19]. The gramophone: a record as a weapon, and the colonel's reversal.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4561-4601)
At his first lunch in the battalion mess (fifteen officers in chairs reading illustrated papers) Graves's greeting "Good morning, gentlemen" gets no answer. A gramophone plays a comic song about a married couple's oil stove; a red-faced senior officer ("Buzz Off") storms in and demands to know which "bloody wart" put it on ("in the Royal Welch a subaltern had to answer to the name of 'wart'"), and orders "the *Angelus*." Two subalterns obey. When the colonel enters, he complains: "Who on earth puts this wretched *Angelus* on every time I come into the mess? For heaven's sake play something cheery for a change," and with his own hands replaces it with the comic song before the lunch gong sounds. Mechanism: authority's contradictory demands, with the junior blamed for both; the narrator reports the farce as an initiation scene.
*Candidate application:* A scene in which seniors' caprice is the whole joke, and no junior may laugh; the narrator, a newcomer, records it with the clarity of one who is not yet a participant.

**[E15b-20]. The colonel's questions at lunch, "Which is which?", and the vow "I'll survive you all."** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4606-4650; fn. [4])
At the foot of the table the colonel asks the adjutant who "those two funny ones" are; when Robertson answers "I'm Robertson, sir," he says "I wasn't asking you." He then singles out Graves ("T'other wart's wearing a wind-up tunic") for wearing stars on the shoulder rather than the sleeve; Graves, mouth full, "swallowed the lump of meat whole" and answers that it was a Welsh Regiment order; the colonel sends him to the master-tailor. Graves says there was "a severe struggle in me between resentment and regimental loyalty," and murmurs "You damned snobs. I'll survive you all. There'll come a time when there won't be one of you left serving in the battalion to remember battalion mess at Laventie." "This time came, exactly a year later." Footnote [4]: "The quartermaster excepted." The mess waiter had already told the 42-year-old solicitor Robertson that whisky is against orders for young officers.
*Candidate application:* The private vow of the humiliated, with the author's retrospective confirmation (almost everyone in that mess was gone within a year) and the footnote's dry exception, is a model of how humor and the casualty rate mix in the memoir.

**[E15b-21]. The patrol as admission test, and the prize that was probably wine and rainwater.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4666-4724)
On his first night in the line, Graves's company commander sends him out on patrol, "the regimental custom to test new officers in this way." Both Royal Welch battalions held it "a point of honor to be masters of No Man's Land from dusk to dawn," and a message passes down the line nightly: "Pass the word; officer's patrol going out." The patrol is to learn whether a German sap-head is occupied; Graves and Sergeant Townsend crawl through the wire with socks cut at the toes pulled over bare knees, advance ten yards at a time, wait, and find it empty. The only thing in it is a wicker basket holding "something large and smooth and round, twice as large as a football." Graves carries it back; it is sent from battalion headquarters to divisional intelligence, where "everybody was very interested in it" and the theory is that it contains a chemical for re-dampening gas masks. "I now believe it was the dregs of country wine mixed with rainwater. I never heard the official report." The colonel says to the company commander within the Surrey-man's hearing: "Your new wart seems to have more guts than the others." Graves concludes "the only thing that the regiment respected in young officers was personal courage." He adds a Second Battalion officer's remark, after revisiting the Laventie trenches once the war was over, on how small No Man's Land proved to be: "It was like the real size of the hollow in a tooth compared with the size it feels to the tongue."
*Candidate application:* The institution's elaborate treatment of a trivial prize (an official theory, a report that never comes back) is a documented comic pattern of intelligence work; the colonel's praise is the only reward the patrol earns.

**[E15b-22]. The wound-calculus: the best way to last the war is to be wounded.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4726-4744)
Graves lays out his reasoning with an actuary's calm: "The best way of lasting the war out was to get wounded." The best time was at night in the open, when unaimed fire made a vital wound less likely, when dressing stations were not busy and back areas not being heavily shelled; therefore "It was most convenient to be wounded ... on a night patrol in a quiet sector," where one could crawl into a shell-hole and wait for rescue. He then adds a grim caveat about German patrols, and a field practice for patrols that find a wounded enemy (the details, which involve killing a wounded man to take insignia, are summarized and not reproduced here).
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* a deadpan cost-benefit appraisal of one's own injury, offered as a reason for volunteering for patrols, which also explains his willingness to go out often. The humor is only in the register, not in a joke.
*Candidate application:* The wish for a "cushy" wound (the Welsh other ranks' term, E15a) appears here in an officer's version, as optimization; compare other-ranks versions in Richards (see the roll-up).

**[E15b-23]. "A carefully worked out formula for taking risks": odds as moral vocabulary.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4761-4797)
Graves says every man had such a formula. Everyone would take "any risk, even the certainty of death, to save life or to maintain an important position," about a one-in-five risk to take life when a wider object (a known sniper, fire superiority) was served, perhaps a one-in-twenty risk to bring in a wounded German ("gentlemanly" Royal Welch standard), "a one-in-two-hundred risk" to hurry and "when dead tired a one-in-fifty risk." Other divisions were less restrained toward enemy wounded (the narrator's claims are summarized, not reproduced). He notes that some battalions with low morale took one-in-fifty risks "in mere laziness or despair," and that the Royal Welch men disapproved of the Munsters for being said to "waste men wicked" by not keeping under cover in reserve lines. He adds: "At no time in the war did any of us allow ourselves to believe that hostilities could possibly continue more than nine months or a year more, so it seemed almost worth while taking care."
*Candidate application:* Fractional risk-talk as a kind of in-group etiquette and a measure of a battalion's morale; the flat statement of the shared self-deception about the war's length is a claim about coping.

**[E15b-24]. A shot not taken, and a rifle handed over.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4767-4775)
Graves says he "only once refrained from shooting a German I saw," at Cuinchy: through a telescopic sight he saw a man bathing in a third-line trench seven hundred yards away. "I somehow did not like the idea of shooting a naked man," so he handed the rifle to the sergeant: "Here, take this. You're a better shot than me." The sergeant "got him, he said; but I had not stayed to watch."
*Candidate application:* The narrator's squeamishness is covered by a flippant excuse to a subordinate, who does the killing; neither joke nor guilt is stated, and the narrator walks away.

**[E15b-25]. A sergeant-major's saying on German officers.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4749-4752)
Describing patrol composition, Graves notes German patrols were six or seven men under an NCO, and that German officers left as much as they decently could to their NCOs: they did not, "as one of our sergeant-majors put it, believe in 'keeping a dog and barking themselves.'"
*Candidate application:* A regimental sergeant-major's proverb about leadership, used here as a mild gibe at the enemy's officers and implicit flattery of his own.

**[E15b-26]. Captain Thomas's elaborate salutes: "a private joke at the expense of the war."** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4875-4890)
Company commander Thomas, a regular of seventeen years' service, polo player and "extremely silent" ("it was not sullenness but shyness"), on leave in London walked about the West End "astonished at the amateur militariness," and "to be more in keeping with it he gave elaborate awkward salutes to newly joined second-lieutenants and raised his cap to dug-out colonels and generals." Graves says: "It was a private joke at the expense of the war." He heard of it only by accident. Thomas also receives food hampers from Fortnum and Mason that the officers share, and Buzz Off "had a good nose for a hamper."
*Candidate application:* A professional's privately enacted parody of the civilian-wartime officer class, told by a junior who learned of it second-hand; the narrator names it in words as a joke against the war.

**[E15b-27]. "Chasing the warts": the salute drill and the stated purpose.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4892-4917)
Billets mean battalion mess, riding school and peacetime parades. When Graves, as orderly officer, dismissed a guard after the senior officer crossed the street, Buzz Off had been waiting in a doorway; he came out in a great show of anger, ordered "As you were, stand fast," asked where Graves had learned to salute ("At the depot, sir"), and sentenced him to an hour of saluting drill every morning before breakfast for a month under a staff-sergeant. Graves's judgment: "This was not a particular act of spite against me but the general game of 'chasing the warts,' at which all the senior officers played. It was honestly intended to make us better soldiers."
*Candidate application:* A senior's staged ambush of the junior is reported with the narrator's own fair-mindedness, as an institution's way of teaching, not a personal grudge.

**[E15b-28]. Kitchener's remark on the passing battalion.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4919-4928)
After about three weeks with the Royal Welch, the brigade marched past Lord Kitchener; he is reported to have "commented to the brigadier on the soldier-like appearance of the leading battalion" but said cynically, "Wait until they've been a week or two in the trenches; they will lose some of that high polish." Graves: "He apparently mistook us for one of the new-army battalions" (the Royal Welch were regulars).
*Candidate application:* An old general's dry prediction that polish does not last, reported in hearsay form ("we were told"), is an authority's own gallows remark that the unit tells itself with satisfaction at the misidentification.

### D. Cuinchy again: messages from the Germans, rats, and patrols (L4930-5040)

**[E15b-29]. The German message in a rifle grenade, and "an exchange of courtesies between officers of opposite armies."** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4933-4959)
The Germans opposite at the Cuinchy canal bank "wished to be sociable" and sent messages over in undetonated rifle-grenades. One, evidently addressed to the Irish battalion the Royal Welch relieved, read in the text as printed:
> "We all German korporals wish you English korporals a good day and invite you to a good German dinner to-night with beer (ale) and cakes. You little dog ran over to us and we keep it safe; it became no food with you so it run to us. Answer in the same way, if you please."
Another was a German army newspaper printed in Lille with Russian defeats and, in another column, an account of a German submarine's destruction by British armed trawlers that British papers had not been allowed to print. Graves's gloss on the battalion: it "never allowed itself to have any political feelings about the Germans"; a professional soldier fights whom the King orders; the Christmas 1914 fraternization, in which the battalion was among the first to take part, "was not an emotional hiatus but a commonplace of military tradition—an exchange of courtesies between officers of opposite armies."
*Mechanism:* the enemy's mock-formal invitation (with a joke about the dog, who "became no food" with the Irish), transmitted through a grenade; Graves treats the exchange as professional etiquette, not softness.
*Candidate application:* A regular unit's non-ideological view of the enemy as a counterparty in a profession; humor travels across the line in a form that pretends the war is an invitation.

**[E15b-30]. Rats and the "welcome" prank (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4961-4967)
Cuinchy was "one of the worst places for rats" because they came up from the canal and fed on the many corpses. When a new officer came to the company, he was given a dug-out with a spring bed "as a token of his welcome"; that night he saw two rats on his blankets fighting over a human body part (the exact part is not reproduced here). "This was thought a great joke."
*Candidate application:* The welcome of a newcomer by a ghastly arranged scene is recorded as a joke at the greenhorn's expense with no authorial dissent.

**[E15b-31]. A moonlit patrol, a humming German, and the accusation of "cold feet."** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L4969-5018)
The colonel asks for a patrol along the tow-path; the moon is so bright "it dazzled the eyes." The company commander, Childe-Freeman, offers to telephone battalion that it is too bright. Graves has knocked over a cup and a plate at supper from nerves, yet goes: "But I knew Buzz Off would accuse me of cold feet." In the open they plan rushes from shell-hole to shell-hole timed to gunfire; craters hold corpses of wounded men who crept in and died, "Some of them were skeletons, picked clean by the rats" (restrained). Thirty yards from a German working-party and its covering party, they see a German lying on his back twelve yards away "humming a tune. It was the 'Merry Widow' waltz." The sergeant shows his revolver and "raised his eyebrows inquiringly"; Graves signals "no." On the way back machine-gun fire forces them to stand and walk irregularly. At the trench Graves telephones the artillery to put shrapnel on the German front trench at the tow-path; "We heard the clash of downed tools and distant shouts and cries; we reckoned the probable casualties." At next morning's stand-to Buzz Off asks for particulars, then "cursed me for 'not scuppering them with that revolver of yours. Cold feet,' he snorted as he turned away."
*Candidate application:* The senior's accusation of cowardice is the force that sends the narrator out; the same man spares a humming German and then calls down artillery on his comrades, recording only the "probable casualties." The tune humming is a plain domestic detail with no remark.

**[E15b-32]. The megaphone conversation: courtesy, bawdy interruption and a very well held trench.** (PW-05, ch. XIV, L5020-5037)
The Royal Welch were ordered to shout across to the Germans to find out how strongly the front trenches were manned at night. A German-speaking officer with a megaphone asked "Wie gehts ihnen, kamaraden?"; a delighted voice shouted back in German-English "Tommee, hast du den deutsch gelernt?" Firing stopped and a conversation across fifty yards began. The Germans would not name their regiment or "talk any military shop." One shouted a bawdy remark about the girls of La Bassée and Béthune (not reproduced); the British speaker "refused to discuss this" and asked how the Kaiser was, to which the Germans "replied respectfully that he was in excellent health, thank you." Asked "And how is the Crown Prince?", someone shouted an obscene insult in English "and was immediately suppressed by his comrades." Then "a confusion of angry voices and laughter," and the Germans sang the "Wacht am Rhein." Conclusion: "The trench was evidently very well held indeed."
*Mechanism:* formal enquiry after the sovereign as comic ceremony; an intelligence ruse defeated by discipline (nothing said that could be used), while the laughter and the insult show what the exchange was for the men.
*Candidate application:* A cross-line exchange in which each side keeps military discipline and enjoys the manners.

### E. Chapter XV: leave, the Loos orders, the messing and the song, the gas (L5042-5440)

**[E15b-33]. Cross refuses leave: "the regimental tradition."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5063-5075)
Graves says leave came round about every six to eight months, heavy casualties shortened it and offensives suspended it. "Only one officer in France" was said to have refused: Cross of the Fifty-second Light Infantry, alleged to have said his father fought with the regiment in South Africa and had no leave, his grandfather in the Crimea likewise, "I do not regard it in the regimental tradition to take home-leave when on active service." Graves adds: "Cross was a professional survivor and was commanding the battalion in 1917 when I last heard of him."
*Candidate application:* A regimental ancestry gag that becomes a tradition of refusal, with the narrator's wry label ("professional survivor") as the punch.

**[E15b-34]. London on leave: the bombing story and "Oh, but that was in France."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5077-5107)
At home in September 1915 Graves finds "the general indifference to and ignorance about the war was remarkable," the catchword being "Business as usual," and his family's house (his uncle was the German consul) under police watch. When friends spoke only of Zeppelin raids three streets away, he told them of an early-morning aeroplane bomb at Beuvry that killed three soldiers billeted next door and a woman and child; they asked what he did, and he said "I went to sleep again; I was tired out." They replied "Oh, but that was in France," and the look of interest faded "as though I had taken them in with a stupid catch." He spent the rest of leave walking in the Welsh hills in an old shirt and shorts. Back at the battalion, "The Actor" asked in sequence whether he had gone to dances, shows, a hunt, or slept with nice girls ("No, I didn't. Sorry to disappoint you."), and, learning he had only walked, said "Good God," and then "chaps like you don't deserve to go on leave."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's deadpan answer exposes the civilian sense that only home-front air raids count; a mess comedy shows the other officers' expectation of what leave is for.
*Candidate application:* A civilian-facing exchange in which the soldier's true story is rejected as out-of-place by its location, and a mess exchange in which his rejection of dissipation is the joke.

**[E15b-35]. The Loos orders dictated by a captain with a laugh, a pessimism and a euphemism.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5109-5245; fn. [5])
On 23 September 1915 company commander Thomas returns from battalion headquarters with a notebook and gives each officer a map and orders to copy for the attack set for Saturday 25 September. As he reads the three objectives (a fortified farm behind three enemy trench lines and two railways, the town of Auchy, the village of Haisnes), The Actor's "shoulders ... were shaking with laughter" and he asks who is responsible. Thomas names a divisional staff captain by a coarse nickname (not reproduced) and quotes a couplet about him: he "wore red tabs upon his chest, / And even on his undervest." Thomas then speaks plainly: it is "what they call a subsidiary attack," with no supports, to "keep the enemy busy while the folk on our right do the real work"; "Personally, I don't give a damn either way. We'll get killed anyhow." The officers laugh; Thomas: "All right, laugh now, but by God on Saturday we've got to carry out this funny scheme." He calls the gas "damnable" ("It's not soldiering to use stuff like that even though the Germans did start it"), corrects himself on the euphemism ("sorry, excuse me this once, I mean accessory-companies"), disparages the new gas companies as "chemistry-dons from London University" and schoolboys trained three weeks, and says "Of course they'll bungle it. ... But let's be merry." Fn. [5] explains that a special order imposed "severe penalties on anyone who used any word but 'accessory' in speaking of the gas" to keep it secret, "but the French civilians knew all about it long before this." He ends: "If we get through the German wire I'll be satisfied. Our guns don't seem to be cutting it."
*Mechanism:* laughter at the staff plan, euphemism by order, and the commander's open prediction of losses coexist; the leader jokes and keeps dictating.
*Candidate application:* An officer who treats the order's absurdity as a shared joke while carrying it out, with no concealment of his doubts from subordinate officers but a request that "the men" not be told.

**[E15b-36]. Sergeant Townsend and "Mons Angels."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5247-5254)
Graves repeats to the platoon that three fresh divisions and the Cavalry Corps are behind them and "the inevitable success attending our assault." Sergeant Townsend asks if that is so, and says "excuse me, sir, I'm thinking it's only those chaps on the right that'll get reinforcements. If we get half a platoon of Mons Angels, that's about all we will get." (The Angels of Mons: my gloss, a widely circulated 1914 legend of angelic help in the retreat.) Graves answers, addressing him by name: "you are a well-known pessimist. This is going to be a good show."
*Candidate application:* An NCO's sardonic recasting of the miracle story as the only reinforcement to expect, and the officer's deflecting label ("well-known pessimist"), which the reader knows will be justified.

**[E15b-37]. The Last Supper messing.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5256-5272)
On the eve of the attack the battalion mess joins the staff of a new-army division in a large house in Béthune; "It was, someone pointed out, like a caricature of The Last Supper in duplicate. In the middle of the long table sat the two pseudo-Christs, the battalion colonel and the divisional general." Everybody was drinking a lot; subalterns allowed whisky "for a treat" raised glasses with "Cheero, we will be messing together to-morrow night in La Bassée." Only the company commanders looked worried; Captain Samson was "biting his thumb." Childe-Freeman remarked: "The last time the regiment was in these parts it was under decent generalship. Old Marlborough knew better than to attack the La Bassée lines; he masked them and went round."
*Mechanism:* sacred parody with commanders cast as Christ, and a historical gibe at the present generalship.
*Candidate application:* The eve-of-battle joke and the contrasting silence of the company commanders (who know the plan) are paired in the text.

**[E15b-38]. A drunken staff colonel's forecast.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5274-5286)
The G.S.O. 1 of the new-army division, next to Graves and drunk, tells the adjutant (an old polo friend) that the general commanding "doesn't know where he is; doesn't know where his division is; can't read a map properly," has marched the men off their feet and left supplies behind so that they have eaten iron rations, and that "to-morrow's going to be a glorious balls-up, and the day after to-morrow he'll be sent home." Then "quite seriously": "Really, Charley, it's just like that, you mark my words."
*Candidate application:* A senior staff officer's gallows judgment of his own commander, voiced drunk in front of a junior, and then said again straight. The narrator comments nothing.

**[E15b-39]. Marching songs: comic songs against the Welsh battalion's defeatist ones.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5288-5320)
Returning to Cambrin that night, the men (mostly Midlanders) sing comic songs (*Slippery Sam*, *When we've Wound up the Watch on the Rhine*, *I do like a S'nice S'mince Pie*) to concertina. The Second Welsh, says Graves, would never have sung the "Watch on the Rhine" song; their only songs about the war were defeatist, and he gives the opening of one:
> I want to go home, / I want to go home, / The coal-box and shrapnel they whistle and roar, / I don't want to go to the trenches no more,
(There were "several more verses in the same strain.") Hewitt, the Welsh machine-gun officer, had written a more offensive-spirited version about a German attack at Givenchy that failed ("They never got home"), "But the men would not sing it, though they all admired Hewitt." On reaching Cambrin that night, Graves adds, the junior officers "complained loudly" at not being given the honor of leading the attack: "We were the senior regiment, they protested, and entitled to the 'Right of the Line.'"
*Mechanism:* a song's defeatist content is accepted by the group; a more patriotic rewrite by a respected officer is rejected, though he is admired.
*Candidate application:* A documented case in which soldiers would sing a plaintive "I want to go home" but not a bloodthirsty version, and the unit's ethos is read off its song repertoire.

**[E15b-40]. The rum storeman: a ritual double tot lost in the mud.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5373-5392)
In the siding on 25 September, A Company waits for the rum, since "the tradition of every attack was a double tot of rum beforehand." When Thomas's orderly brings the order to move up, the storeman arrives with the rum jar, red-faced and retching, drunk, and falls on his face in a sump-pit; the stopper flies out and "what was left of the three gallons bubbled on the ground." The Actor says nothing: "It was a crime deserving the death-penalty." He puts a foot on the man's neck and another on his back, treads him into the mud, orders "Company forward," and the company goes forward over the body; "that was the last heard of the storeman" (the violence is described only in this restrained summary).
*Mechanism:* mock-judicial register ("a crime deserving the death-penalty") for the loss of the ritual rum; the punishment is performed on the spot in front of the company.
*Candidate application:* The tradition of pre-attack rum has such weight that its loss is a capital-level offense in the narrator's idiom; the scene is rendered with no authorial reproach.

**[E15b-41]. The gas fiasco in the narrator's dry bureaucratic voice.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5394-5411)
Graves reconstructs what happened in the front line from later accounts: at half-past four the gas company commander phoned divisional headquarters, "Dead calm. Impossible discharge accessory." The answer: "Accessory to be discharged at all costs." The spanners for unscrewing the cylinder cocks were, "with two or three exceptions," misfits, and "the gas-men rushed about shouting and asking each other for the loan of an adjustable spanner." The few cylinders opened sent the gas "whistling out," forming a cloud a few yards away that spread back into the trenches; shelling then broke more cylinders; "The gas-company dispersed." Earlier (L5355-5361) the narrator, at the siding, had asked the lightly wounded Middlesex men what had happened; "Bloody balls-up" "was the most detailed answer I could get," and some of the wounded were "yellow-faced and choking, with their buttons tarnished green." The narrator's paragraph prefaces itself with "What happened in the next few minutes is difficult for me now to sort out."
*Mechanism and register:* the comedy of misfitting spanners and the exchange of orders is told in a dry, sardonic register and sits next to casualties; the narrator's admission of difficulty marks where the account stops being managed.
*Candidate application:* The soldier's own one-phrase verdict ("bloody balls-up") and the staff's order "at all costs" are two clean examples of the incompatibility of communication up and down the chain.

### F. The Loos fighting, 25 September-3 October 1915: dry irony beside heavy loss (L5436-5827)

**[E15b-42]. "A story in itself": how one German machine-gun stayed in action, told as a chain of postings.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5443-5487)
Graves breaks off the battle narrative to explain why a single German machine-gun in a small salient (the "Pope's Nose") was still firing. The chain: a colonial governor nominates the eighteen-year-old son of a rich planter to serve as a full lieutenant; he is sent to the First Middlesex, "good-hearted enough but of little use in the trenches," and is called "Young Jamaica." His company commander, nicknamed "The Boy" (twenty years' service and "the unusual boast of having held every rank from 'boy' to captain in the same company"), tries to teach him. Because Jamaica outranks the experienced second-lieutenants, the colonel moves him to the brigade trench-mortar company in May or June: "Trench-mortars at that time were dangerous and ineffective; so the appointment seemed suitable." The Royal Welch sent a rival, Tiley, an ex-planter "attached from another regiment" who "had showed his resentment at the manner of his welcome somewhat too plainly." By September mortars were an important arm and Jamaica, being senior, commanded the company. When The Boy was mortally wounded climbing the parapet and crawled past Jamaica's emplacement, Jamaica left his guns to fetch stretcher-bearers; Tiley knocked out machine-guns until his own gun burst; the Pope's Nose gun, opposite Jamaica, kept firing.
*Mechanism:* causal irony: seniority rules and the hazing of a newcomer, applied in peacetime fashion, become a battlefield cost; the narrator delivers it as an anecdote of staffing, not a complaint.
*Candidate application:* A character can tell a loss as a chain of administrative decisions in a level voice; the nicknames ("The Boy," "Young Jamaica") are the unit's own compression of a man's history.

**[E15b-43]. Maison Rouge Alley: the alternating shouts, an officer who "died, but of heart-failure," and a rivalry that bonds two regiments.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5489-5519)
Reserves moving up through a communication trench under shelling with lachrymatory shells produce a "continual scramble backwards and forwards" and a litany of cries that Graves lists without attribution: "'Whose orders?' 'What's happening?' 'Gas!' 'Back!' 'Come on!' 'Gas!' 'Back!'" Childe-Freeman, who had reached the front line with only fifty of his men and "felt this keenly as a disgrace," blew his whistle; they were stopped by machine-gun fire before passing the British wire. The narrator adds: "Freeman himself died, but of heart-failure, as he stood on the parapet." Samson then led C Company and the rest of B across, because "he would not have it said that the Royal Welch had let down the Middlesex." Graves notes that the Royal Welch and Middlesex "were drawn together in dislike of the Scots" (the brigade's other three battalions and its brigadier), and quotes the adjutant voicing "the general opinion," a disparaging generalization about the Scottish battalions' cleanliness, boasting and manner of charging (national caricature, paraphrased not reproduced).
*Candidate application:* Inter-unit contempt (here aimed at the allies on the same flank) acts as glue between two units that then die together; the narrator's "but of heart-failure" is an example of the understated clause that carries a death and a verdict.

**[E15b-44]. The shout across the shell-holes, and the sergeant's reply: "Not cowards, sir."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5522-5533)
An officer later told Graves how he had run his platoon forward by rushes with covering fire; when it did not rise to his whistle he jumped up and shouted "You bloody cowards, are you leaving me to go alone?" His platoon sergeant, "groaning with a broken shoulder," answered "Not cowards, sir. Willing enough," adding in an obscene intensifier (omitted here) that they were all dead; a machine-gun traversing as they rose had caught them. No comment follows.
*Candidate application:* The plainest example in this part of the register changing at once: an officer's abuse is answered with a three-word correction from an NCO who is himself wounded.

**[E15b-45]. The Actor, the dying Boy, and "the most heartless beast I've ever met."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5537-5564)
Graves finds The Actor in a narrow trench that stretchers cannot pass. The Boy lies badly wounded on a stretcher, and Jamaica stands over him in tears: "Poor old Boy ... He's the only one who was decent to me." The Actor (who, a minute earlier, had called the gassed Surrey-man a shirker, a charge Graves says "was unfair") tells Jamaica to move the man to a dug-out, "He can't live now and we're late getting up," then shakes him, orders him back to his mortars with a pointed question about why he is not firing at the machine-gun, kicks him down the trench, and orders the stretcher lifted across the top of the trench so that the company can pass. Jamaica, leaning against a traverse, says weakly "I do think you're the most heartless beast I've ever met." The narrator gives no verdict.
*Candidate application:* A scene in which the officer who is a mess-room joker is the one who prioritizes the company over a dying man, and the grieving junior is cast as the witness; the text leaves both responses standing.

**[E15b-46]. Fear, rum, Samson's apology, and Sergeant Townsend's "feeble, bitter jokes."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5573-5599)
Waiting in the front line with the gas still thick and the bombardment falling short onto them, Graves says "My mouth was dry, my eyes out of focus, and my legs quaking under me," finds a water-bottle of rum and drinks about half a pint, which "quieted me and my head remained clear." Captain Samson lies wounded about twenty yards out; several attempts to bring him in cost three men killed and four wounded; the narrator reports that Samson, badly hit and groaning, ordered his orderly back, saying he was "riddled and not worth rescuing," and "sent his apologies to the company for making such a noise." During the two-hour wait "The men were silent and depressed. Sergeant Townsend was making feeble, bitter jokes about the good old British army muddling through and how he thanked God we still had a navy. I shared the rest of the rum with him and he cheered up a little." Meanwhile the adjutant, now in command and telling Thomas "he didn't care a damn about orders; he was going to cut his losses," refuses to send A and D companies over without new orders from brigade.
*Candidate application:* Humor offered by an NCO at a bad moment is rated by the narrator as weak and bitter and not rejected; its function is mood, and the rum does the rest. A dying man's apology for noise is the strongest case in the part of courtesy as a last act.

**[E15b-47]. The night after: Samson found, Swainson's "I'm about done for," and the footnote that reverses both outcomes.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5621-5643; fn. [6])
At dusk the survivors go out to bring in the wounded. "The first dead body I came upon was Samson's"; Graves found that Samson "had forced his knuckles into his mouth to stop himself crying out and attracting any more men to their death" (the number and nature of his wounds are not reproduced). Major Swainson of the Middlesex, wounded in the lungs, stomach and a leg, begs Graves to loosen his belt; Graves cuts it with a bowie-knife he had bought for fighting; Swainson says "I'm about done for." Footnote [6] reverses the expectation: "Major Swainson recovered quickly and was back at the Middlesex Depot after a few weeks," whereas Lawrie, a Royal Welch company quartermaster-sergeant back at Cambrin, "was hit in the neck that day by a spent machine-gun bullet which just pierced the skin, and died of shock a few hours later." Of the night's work Graves says "The Germans behaved generously"; he does not remember a shot until near dawn, when a few warning shots ended the work. He was "surprised at some of the attitudes in which the dead had stiffened—in the act of bandaging friends' wounds, crawling, cutting wire." He then gives casualty figures for the Argyll and Sutherland (700, fourteen of sixteen officers killed) and the Middlesex (550, eleven officers killed).
*Candidate application:* The footnote is the memoir's device for turning a "dying" man's remark into an irony and a slight wound into a death; the narrator never labels either as comic.

**[E15b-48]. The popular book's "flat caps on the left."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5601-5619)
While rumors of disaster and of success on the right circulated, Graves watched the reserves advance toward Loos and Hill 70 under heavy shelling ("they were troops of the new-army division whose staff we had messed with the night before"). He notes that the Highland Division on his right was celebrated by Ian Hay in *The First Hundred Thousand*: "I suppose that we were 'the flat caps on the left' who 'let down' his comrades-in-arms." The same trenches "stank with a gas-blood-lyddite-latrine smell."
*Candidate application:* A veteran's wry note on how the published, home-front version of the same day assigns blame to his own unit; the target is the printed war.

**[E15b-49]. The Middlesex colonel's meat pie: a cold reception for survivors and a subordinate's quiet answer.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5645-5683)
Two Middlesex second-lieutenants (Henry and Hill, newly commissioned and, Graves says, welcomed in the Middlesex "something like mine in the Royal Welch") spent the day in shell-holes in the rain; Henry, according to Hill, dragged five wounded men into his shell-hole and built a parapet from his hands and a bowie-knife; Hill's platoon sergeant lay screaming for hours with a stomach wound begging for morphia, and "he was dying, so Hill gave him five pellets" ("We always carried morphia with us for emergencies like this"; wounds and the implied overdose summarized, not detailed). Reporting back with about ninety men from all companies, they found the colonel and adjutant sitting down to a meat pie. The colonel said "So you've come back, have you? Well, all the rest are dead," assigned the survivors to companies, and dismissed them with no offer of food or whisky; the adjutant called them back only to tell them to correct men whose shoulder-straps were unbuttoned. They overheard the colonel complaining of having only two blankets. When Choate (also unwounded) reported, "he leaned over the table, cut a large piece of meat pie and began eating it. This caused such surprise that nothing further was said." He then drank a glass of whisky, saluted, and left.
*Mechanism:* the survivors' small, silent act of reclaiming hospitality, made possible because the superiors are too startled to enforce etiquette.
*Candidate application:* The regimental colonel's chilly protocol after a massacre and a junior's one-act rebuke of it; told second-hand by Hill, with Graves approving by reporting the whole.

**[E15b-50]. Thomas's death, and the new-army troops "like a crowd coming back from a cup final."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5685-5697)
Only six Royal Welch company officers remained the day after; "Next morning there were only five. Thomas was killed by a sniper. He was despondently watching the return of the new-army troops on the right." The new-army troops, pushed "blindly into the gap" without orders or rations, "flocked back, not in a panic, but stupidly, like a crowd coming back from a cup final," with their officers "in groups of their own," which the veterans "could scarcely believe" they saw. Graves's judgment of Thomas: "Thomas need not have been killed; but he was in the sort of mood in which he seemed not to care one way or the other." Compare Thomas's earlier "I don't give a damn either way" (E15b-35).
*Candidate application:* The man who was the dry joker of the orders session dies in a single sentence; the narrator links the death to his earlier tone without calling it a joke that came true.

**[E15b-51]. The Actor's impossible sleep: farce in the middle of the aftermath.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5697-5708)
After the companies were merged for night watches, Graves took first watch and could not wake The Actor at midnight: "I shook him, shouted in his ear, poured water over him, banged his head against the side of the bed. Finally I threw him on the floor," and then heaved him back, "in a depth of sleep from which nothing could shake him"; even "Stand-to!" did not rouse him. At nine in the morning The Actor "was furious with me for not having waked him at midnight."
*Mechanism:* exhaustion told as slapstick, with the sleep deprivation named only indirectly (Graves says elsewhere he got eight hours' sleep in ten days).
*Candidate application:* A pairing of two officers at the end of their strength, where the comic exchange is a reproach for not doing the impossible.

**[E15b-52]. After the attack: carrying the cylinders, "everyone was drunk together," and loosened rank.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5710-5733)
The day after, the Royal Welch carry the dead down and then carry the unbroken gas-cylinders to another position, which was "worse than carrying the dead; the cylinders were cast-iron and very heavy and we hated them. The men cursed and sulked, but got the carrying done." Told that they will attack again on the 27th, Graves says it was "difficult for me to keep up appearances with the men; I felt like screaming," and he told them only about three in the afternoon; "They took it very well." His observation on rank: "The relations of officers and men, and of senior and junior officers, had been very different in the excitement of the attack. There had been no insubordination, but a greater freedom, as if everyone was drunk together. I found myself calling the adjutant Charley on one occasion; he appeared not to mind it in the least." For about ten days his relations with his men resembled those in the Welsh Regiment; "later discipline reasserted itself and it was only occasionally that I found them intimate."
*Candidate application:* Documented loosening of rank formalities under extreme shared danger, followed by a re-hardening: a "greater freedom" temporary and tied to the event.

**[E15b-53]. Waiting to attack again: "It's murder, sir," the S'nice S'mince Pie, and a callback to the spanners.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5735-5753)
At four o'clock on the 27th, the second gas attack goes off properly: "There was a strong wind and it went over well; the gas-men had brought enough spanners this time." The Germans were silent; the brigadier sent a feeling-patrol of twenty-five Cameronians and an officer, and only two wounded men returned. Graves's company waits on the fire-step with fixed bayonets for five hours. "My mind was a blank except for the recurrence of 'S'nice smince spie, s'nice smince spie.... I don't like ham, lamb or jam and I don't like roley-poley....'" (the comic song heard on the march, E15b-39), and "The men laughed at my singing." The acting company sergeant-major said "It's murder, sir," and Graves agreed, "Of course it's murder, you bloody fool," and added "But there's nothing else for it, is there?" At nine the attack was put off, to be ready at dawn; "No order came at dawn."
*Candidate application:* The officer's involuntary comic-song loop, which the men laugh at, and the single frank exchange between an acting sergeant-major and an officer sit together without a joke covering the second.

**[E15b-54]. Nine days of sleeplessness, whisky, and the dead who swell (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5755-5770)
"From the morning of September 24th to the night of October 3rd I had in all eight hours of sleep. I kept myself awake and alive by drinking about a bottle of whisky a day." He had never drunk it before and "seldom drank it since; it certainly was good then." They had no blankets, greatcoats or shelters and the rain continued; each night they went out to bring in the dead of other battalions, and the Germans "continued to be indulgent." Graves then describes in detail the decomposition of the unrecovered dead and says he "vomited more than once" while superintending the carrying; those details are not reproduced here.
*Candidate application:* A dry understatement ("it certainly was good then") beside a plain account of physical collapse; the narrator's own body is not joked about in this passage.

**[E15b-55]. Lance-Corporal Baxter: the tender-hearted man who walked out with a handkerchief.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5771-5792)
On the morning of the 27th a wounded Middlesex man, conscious after two days, cried out near the German wire. Baxter ("tender-hearted," "the man to boil up a special dixie of tea for the sentries of his section when they came off duty") ran along the trench asking for a volunteer: "Of course no one would go; it was death to put one's head over the trench." He asked Graves, who "excused myself as the only officer in the company" and offered to go at dusk. Baxter went himself: "He jumped quickly over the parapet, then strolled across waving a handkerchief"; the Germans fired to frighten him, then let him come up, and watched as he dressed the man's wounds, gave him rum and biscuit, and promised to come back; he returned in the evening with a stretcher party and the man recovered. Graves recommended him for the Victoria Cross, "being the only officer who had seen the thing done; but he only got a Distinguished Conduct Medal."
*Candidate application:* An act of private kindness narrated with no joke and no flourish; the narrator's own refusal is stated frankly, and the award gap is stated flatly.

**[E15b-56]. The Highland neighbors: a staff engineer's remark and a "Cease fire" misheard as "Retire."** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5794-5815)
Graves walks nearly a quarter-mile down the Tenth Highland Light Infantry trench before seeing a sentry or an officer: "There were dead men, sleeping men, wounded men, gassed men, all lying anyhow." A Royal Engineers officer says "If the Boche knew what an easy job it was, he'd just walk over and take this trench." Graves reports to The Actor that their flank may be "in the air," and they turn the boundary communication trench into a fire-trench facing right. On the night of the 27th the Highlanders mistook Royal Welch men out getting in the dead for the enemy and fired wildly; the order "Cease fire" passed along the trench "until it came to the H.L.I., who misheard it as 'Retire.'" They rushed back down the trench and were stopped by a sergeant of the Fifth Scottish Rifles "at the point of the bayonet."
*Candidate application:* A comic error of transmission (a rumor-chain failure) recounted at the neighbors' expense with the same neutral voice as the casualty reports.

**[E15b-57]. Relief, a composite battalion, and a Victoria Cross not awarded for want of an officer witness.** (PW-05, ch. XV, L5817-5827; fn. [7])
On 3 October the Royal Welch were relieved by a composite battalion of about 100 Royal Warwickshire men and about 70 Royal Welch, "all that was left of our own First Battalion." Frank Jones-Bateman had his thigh broken "while stripping the equipment off a wounded man in No Man's Land; the cartridges in the man's pouches had been set on fire by a shot and were exploding." Footnote [7]: "He was recommended for a Victoria Cross but got nothing because no officer evidence, which is a condition of award, was available." (Compare Baxter's DCM in E15b-55, where the narrator himself was the sole officer witness.)
*Candidate application:* A decoration economy governed by evidence rules, noted by the narrator as a fact that is also an irony.

### G. Annezin, the First Battalion, and the winter at Montagne (L5832-6330)

**[E15b-58]. Old Adelphine Heu and "the clause about the money": civilians' own humor, and Graves's blanket judgment of the French.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L5835-5903)
Billeted at Annezin, Graves is woken by his landlady's gossip: a stingy daughter-in-law, a rogue of a mayor, the whole village's shamelessness. She tells him (in a story involving a young soldier at Les Briques Farm in 1870, the same farm named in his objectives, and a child who died; sexual content summarized, not reproduced) of her youth, then "She told me that all the girls in Annezin prayed every night for the war to end and for the English to go away as soon as their money was spent. She said that the clause about the money was always repeated in case God should miss it." Graves then states that troops in the Pas de Calais "loathed the French," quotes his own letter ("I find it very difficult to love the French here") on overcharging and watered beer (broad national generalizations, documented here not endorsed), and then adds a counter-view in his own retrospective voice: "it was all the same to the peasants whether they were on the German or the British side of the line—it was a foreign military occupation anyhow."
*Mechanism:* the civilian's prayer joke targets the soldiers' money and the pious pretext for it; Graves records it without remark while also recording the soldiers' mutual contempt.
*Candidate application:* The civilians among whom soldiers are billeted have a humor of their own at the soldiers' expense; soldiers' contempt for them and the later retraction by the narrator are both documented here.

**[E15b-59]. 13 October: a false alarm, accordion and penny whistle, and one sententious remark.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L5905-5935)
When the guns flare up again around Loos, Graves is at the Globe in Béthune drinking champagne cocktails with friends when the assistant provost-marshal calls for officers of three brigades; Robertson says "Oh God, ... that means another show," and fears "that'll be the end of us." Back at Annezin, with the battalion out in fighting order, "The men were in high spirits, even the survivors of the show. They were singing songs to the accompaniment of an accordion and a penny whistle." At the height of the "mad-minute" of artillery noise they stopped and looked at each other, and Sergeant Townsend said "sententiously: 'That's the charge. Many good fellows going west at this moment; maybe chums of ours.'" The call-out was cancelled; Graves adds that "It had been another dud show, chiefly to be remembered for the death of Charles Sorley," one of three important poets killed in the war.
*Candidate application:* Singing stops exactly when the noise is a real signal for dying friends; the sergeant's formal phrase is the only attempt to name it.

**[E15b-60]. Back to routine, and the Special Reserve captain who offered to hide his rank.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L5937-5963)
"It was as though there had been no battle except that the senior officers were fewer and the Special Reserve element larger." Graves is gazetted captain in the Third Battalion, which has trebled in size; promoted "over the heads of officers who had longer trench service," he offers the adjutant not to wear his badges of rank; the adjutant says "not unkindly: 'No, put your stars up. It can't be helped.'" Graves knows the colonel and adjutant "had no use for Special Reserve captains unless they were outstandingly good," and that a Special Reserve major and captain had been sent home from the First Battalion with a confidential report of inefficiency, with two more to follow, "likely to be of more service in the training of troops at home." He also admits: "I was in such a depressed state of nerves now that if I had gone into the trenches as a company officer I should probably have modified my formula for taking risks," and was "fortunately" attached to the brigade sappers.
*Candidate application:* A man whose promotion is the result of mortality statistics, and who knows the regiment's view of him, tries to nullify his own rank; the adjutant's reply is the only humane note in the passage.

**[E15b-61]. The Middlesex colonel's speech and the servant's whisper: "Not on purpose I don't, sir!"** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L5968-5976)
Hill of the Middlesex told Graves that the colonel had addressed the survivors in billets about avenging the dead in a new attack on La Bassée: "I know you Diehards! You will go like lions over the top." Hill's servant "had whispered confidentially to Hill: 'Not on purpose I don't, sir!'" Mechanism: an other-ranks aside, one sentence, that turns the commander's rhetoric into a joke on the first clause.
*Candidate application:* The servant's whisper to an officer, not to the colonel, is the classic "joke at the speech" in one line; its safety lies in the confidence and in the target of the pun (the colonel's "lions").

**[E15b-62]. The machine-guns' daily call and response at stand-to, and "no horror in the continual experience of death."** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L5982-6003)
Graves says his remaining autumn service "was uneventful. There was no excitement left in patrolling, no horror in the continual experience of death." The only incident he records is a technical method he and an officer named Owen devised for dealing with night machine-guns, using strings with cartridges to fix a line from many sentries. He adds: "There was a daily exchange of courtesies between our machine-guns and the Germans' at stand-to; by removing cartridges from the ammunition belt it was possible to rap out the rhythm of the familiar call: 'Me—et me do—wn in Pi—cca-di—ll—y,' to which the Germans would reply, though in slower tempo, because our guns were faster than theirs: 'Se—e you da—mned to He—ll first.'" (The dashes in the original mark the drawn-out notes.)
*Mechanism:* a music-hall song tapped out with firearms and answered in the same rhythm with a rhyme-change insult; the narrator adds the technical reason for the slower answer.
*Candidate application:* A documented example of call-and-response banter across the line through the weapons themselves; the same paragraph states that habituation has removed horror.

**[E15b-63]. A press cutting about Dick: the end of a private idol (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6005-6020)
A cutting from *John Bull* (editor Horatio Bottomley) protests unequal treatment of aristocratic offenders, using a police-court case involving a sixteen-year-old schoolboy, named in the text as Dick, the friend whose weekly letters about poetry had been Graves's "greatest stand-by"; the article describes an improper approach to a Canadian corporal near the school, who reported it (wording not reproduced). Graves writes "This news was nearly the end of me," concludes that "Dick had been driven out of his mind by the war," and adds "It would be easy to think of him as dead." There is no humor in the passage.
*Candidate application:* The one grief in this range that has no joke at all is not a death; the narrator's response is to recast the friend's behavior as war-madness and to imagine him dead.

**[E15b-64]. The decline curve: how long an officer lasts, neurasthenia, and the two-bottle company commander.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6022-6055)
Graves lays out, as a table in prose, how an officer's usefulness varies with time: poor in the first three weeks, best between three weeks and four months "unless he happened to have any particular bad shock," declining as "neurasthenia developed," still "more or less all right" at six months, "a drag on the other members of the company" at nine or ten months without a rest, and after a year or fifteen months "often worse than useless." "Officers had a less laborious but a more nervous time than the men. There were proportionately twice as many neurasthenic cases among officers as among men," although a man's average life before being killed or wounded was twice an officer's. Those between twenty-three and thirty-three lasted longest; "I was too young." He reports Dr. W. H. R. Rivers's explanation of long-term decline as an endocrine process ("I think the thyroid") that "cheated" a man "into further endurance," and says "It has taken some ten years for my blood to run at all clean." Of the heavy drinkers: "I knew three or four officers who had worked up to the point of two bottles of whisky a day before they were lucky enough to get wounded or sent home in some other way," and a "two-bottle company commander" who "in three shows running, got his company needlessly destroyed because he was no longer capable of taking clear decisions."
*Candidate application:* The most direct statement in this part of what the later years cost, delivered in the same measured voice as the jokes; useful as evidence for what humor sits beside.

**[E15b-65]. Trench feet as morale: the trench phrase "the cow calved," and the rule against talking of wounds.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6057-6089)
In a good battalion, Graves says, the men were "bent on going home either with an honorable wound or not at all"; in a bad battalion "they did not care 'whether,' in the trench phrase, 'the cow calved or the bull broke its bloody neck.'" "In a really good battalion, as the Second Battalion was when I went to it first, the question of getting wounded and going home was not permitted to be raised." The 1914-15 winter had only four or five trench-foot casualties in the Second Battalion and eight or nine the next; "the don't-care battalions lost very heavily indeed." The official lecture formula blamed tight boots and puttees; Graves says "Trench feet was caused rather by going to sleep with wet boots, cold feet, and depression," and "only came if he did not mind getting trench feet or anything else, because his battalion had lost the power of sticking things out." A battalion of dismounted cavalry on the Somme lost half its strength in two days from it while the Second Battalion had no cases in the same trenches.
*Candidate application:* The unit's norm that wound-wishing is unspeakable (contrast the "cushy one" talk of the Welsh and of Graves's own private calculation, E15b-22) is stated as the mark of a good unit, with a morale theory of illness.

**[E15b-66]. First versus Second Battalion: "more human," and a senior officer kicking a private for a slack salute.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6098-6113)
Graves, posted to the First Battalion in November, says the two differed because the Second had just finished eighteen years overseas and the First had not been out of England since the South African War: the First was "less old-fashioned in its militarism and more human" (a clause on whom the men had consorted with abroad is not reproduced). "It would have been impossible in the First Battalion to see what I once saw in the Second—a senior officer pursuing a private soldier down the street, kicking his bottom because he had been slack in saluting." The First was "as efficient and as regimental" and "a much easier battalion to live in."
*Candidate application:* A regiment's two battalions can carry two cultures of discipline; the narrator's comparison turns on whether the senior officers physically punish a salute lapse.

**[E15b-67]. Meeting Sassoon: a book of essays, cream buns, and "war should not be written about in a realistic way."** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6120-6145)
In a friend's company Graves sees on the table a copy of the *Essays of Lionel Johnson*, the first book he had seen in France "that was not either a military text-book or a rubbish novel," and finds on the fly-leaf the name Siegfried Sassoon. They walked to Béthune talking about poetry and ate cream buns. Graves showed Sassoon drafts from his first book of poems; Sassoon "told me that they were too realistic and that war should not be written about in a realistic way," and showed him a poem beginning "Return to greet me, colors that were my joy, / Not in the woeful crimson of men slain...." Graves: "This was before Siegfried had been in the trenches. I told him, in my old-soldier manner, that he would soon change his style."
*Candidate application:* An exchange on whether war may be rendered plainly, at the moment of the narrator's own turn to plain rendering, with the narrator's mocking "old-soldier manner" as his self-description.

**[E15b-68]. Festubert: the lunatic asylum, the map's caption, and a trench that sank into the marsh.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6147-6163)
Festubert "was nightmare, and had been so since the first fighting there in 1914, when the inmates of its lunatic asylum had been caught between two fires and broken out and run all over the countryside." The British line crossed ground "marked on the map as 'Marsh, sometimes dry in the summer,'" a chain of high-command island trenches connected only at night, "a murderous place for patrols." The battalion had been "nearly wiped out here six months previously." Ordered to build a reserve line, they could make no progress on frozen ground; they raised a couple of hundred yards two or three feet high "at the cost of several men wounded"; others built it up later and "We were told later that it gradually sank down into the marsh, and in the end was completely engulfed."
*Mechanism:* the map caption's deadpan optimism and the sinking trench give the passage a futility joke that the narrator does not mark as one.
*Candidate application:* Place-lore that treats a lethal landscape through its official description.

**[E15b-69]. Tottie Fahy, Field Punishment No. 1, and Sergeant Dickens's stripes.** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6165-6215)
Graves's servant Private Fahy ("known as Tottie Fay, after the actress"), a Birmingham silversmith and reservist, met an old India boozing chum, Sergeant Dickens, at the First Battalion and celebrated; next morning Graves's buttons were unpolished and there was no shaving water. Graves then saw Tottie awarded "twenty-eight days for 'drunkenness in the field'," tied by the ankles and wrists in the form of an X to a limber wheel for several hours a day ("'Crucifixion,' they called it"). "I shall never forget the look that Tottie gave me": he tried to salute and could not, "his eyes filled with tears." Graves told him he was sorry to see him in trouble and adds "That drink, as it proved, did him good in the end": Old Joe the quartermaster took him as the only trained officer's servant in the battalion and got a few days remitted ("Tottie was safer in billets with Old Joe than in trenches with me"), and in summer 1916 he went home on his reservist leave and became a "starred" munitions worker; "Good luck to him and to Birmingham." Dickens ("one of the best N.C.O.'s in either battalion") held the DCM and Bar, the Military Medal and the French Médaille Militaire and "two or three times" had been promoted to sergeant and "each time been reduced for drunkenness," escaping field punishment because losing his stripes was considered enough; "whenever there was a battle Dickens would distinguish himself so conspicuously as a leader that he would be given his stripes back again."
*Mechanism:* an informal regimental cycle: drink, demotion, battle, reinstatement; a sentimental servant's disgrace told with warmth.
*Candidate application:* A regimental tolerance for the drunk hero, and a field punishment named with the sacred word by the men.

**[E15b-70]. Montagne le Fayel: the schoolmaster's pamphlet on living to a hundred, and a colonel nicknamed "Scatter."** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6217-6271)
For divisional training the First Battalion moved by train (the men in trucks marked "Hommes 40, chevaux 8") and a night march to Montagne le Fayel, whose "Picard peasants" Graves found "much more likeable than the Pas de Calais people." Billeted with a retired schoolmaster who lived on vegetables, he is given a vegetarian pamphlet titled *Comment Vivre Cent Ans*: "We already knew of the coming Somme offensive, so this was a good joke." The colonel, who appears in Sassoon's book as Colonel Winchell, "was known in the regiment as Scatter, short for Scatter-cash, because when he first joined the regiment he had been so lavish with his allowance." He "put the battalion through its paces with peace-time severity," training for open warfare after a Somme breakthrough; "Even those of us who did not believe in the break-through thoroughly enjoyed these field-days," though "This training seemed entirely unrelated to war as we had experienced it." They also played rugby; Graves names David Thomas and Sassoon as "the closest friends I made while in France."
*Candidate application:* Nickname from a vanished extravagance; an ironic gift given the coming slaughter; and training that all know is irrelevant is still enjoyed.

**[E15b-71]. Scatter's schoolroom address: the offense was calling a lance-corporal "Jack."** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6273-6314)
Officers and warrant officers are summoned to the village school, squeezing into school desk-benches ("David and I hurt ourselves standing up, bench and all"), where the colonel lectures from the teacher's desk. After general accusations (pocket-flaps undone, hands in trouser pockets, sentries strolling on beats, rowdiness in the estaminets, slack saluting) and a threat to stop leave, he reaches the case: "I have here principally to tell you of a very disagreeable occurrence." A private had addressed a lance-corporal by his first name: "he called him Jack. The corporal made no protest. To think that the First Battalion has sunk to a level where it is possible for such familiarity to exist between N.C.O.'s and the men under their command!" The corporal was reduced to the ranks and the private given field punishment. Graves: "I tried to catch Siegfried's eye, but he was busy avoiding it, so I caught David's instead. This is one of those caricature scenes that now seem to sum up the various stages of my life." Around the schoolroom walls ran "a fresco ... illustrating the evils of alcoholism," from a boy offered a drink by his mate to wife-beating, murder and delirium tremens.
*Mechanism:* the officers' suppressed laughter, with eye contact as the only channel, and a backdrop whose moral mural parodies the colonel's own moralizing.
*Candidate application:* A well-documented scene of institutional absurdity witnessed by three friends who cannot laugh; the narrator names it a caricature even as he records it.

**[E15b-72]. Blue Lamp and Red Lamp, and "a gift of two months longer life" (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XVI, L6316-6328)
The only complaint about Montagne, Graves says, was that women were harder to find than around Béthune; "I remained puritanical." He notes that there was an officers' establishment, the "Blue Lamp," as well as the men's "Red Lamp," in each big town behind the lines, and ends with an ironic sentence that this separation was important for discipline (sexual detail not reproduced beyond this summary). In January he and a captain of the Queen's, the two longest out in the Seventh Division's brigades, are chosen to instruct at the base: "it was a gift of two months longer life to us."
*Candidate application:* The class separation of brothels is treated by the narrator as a dry joke about discipline; the base posting is priced in life-months.

### H. The Havre "Bull Ring": the instructors' mess talks about morale, atrocity, patriotism and religion (L6336-6682)

*Scope note: this long stretch is largely conversation in the instructors' mess, reported by the narrator as the pooled judgment of "we." Several parts deal with atrocity against prisoners and civilians and with national and religious stereotyping; those are summarized in restrained terms and logged in the register at the end. Period stereotypes are documented here, not endorsed.*

**[E15b-73]. The Bull Ring lecture: the semi-facetious talk replaced by the truth about Loos.** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6336-6353)
Graves is one of about thirty instructors at the Havre base depot, assigned to trench relief, trench discipline and arms-drill. When rain forced a lecture in the concert hall before three thousand Canadians, "instead of giving my usual semi-facetious lecture on 'How to be happy though in the trenches,' I paid them the compliment of telling them the story of Loos, and what a balls-up it was, and why it was a balls-up." It was, he says, "the only audience that I ever held for an hour with real attention." He expected the commandant, whose object was to "inculcate the offensive spirit," to be furious; "he took it well," and more lectures followed.
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* a comic-instructional routine exists as the default for drafts, and a plain account of a failed battle is offered to the Canadians as respect; the frank version holds attention better.
*Candidate application:* An ex-officer may have a facetious "How to be happy though in the trenches" register for those who must be reassured and a plainer one for those he respects; the narrator frames the plain one as a compliment.

**[E15b-74]. The mess's rankings of divisions and of nations: pride, cost, and a game played "for spite."** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6355-6392)
The instructors "talked more freely there than would have been possible either in England or in the trenches" on morale, reliability and war-morality. They agreed that about a third of the British Expeditionary Force was dependable on all occasions, a third variable, and the rest unreliable, being kept in positions of comparative safety with about a quarter of the casualties of the best divisions. "It was a matter of pride to belong to one of the recognized best divisions," although "promotion, leave, and the chance of a wound came quicker in them." A list followed of which regions, religions and overseas contingents were the most dependable, and a parlor-game ranking of "the cleanest troops in trenches, taken in nationalities" (both lists are period stereotypes; the contents are not reproduced here). The narrator reports that two of the nations were placed at the bottom of the cleanliness list "for spite; they were not really dirtier than" two others.
*Candidate application:* Mess talk that ranks units and peoples, and openly admits that part of the ranking is spite, is a documented form of in-group sport; the good divisions are proud and used up faster.

**[E15b-75]. Atrocity talk in the mess (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6394-6446)
The mess agreed that propaganda atrocity reports were "ridiculous"; that civilians sometimes displayed children's injuries to win sympathy and presents, representing as enemy cruelty what shell-fire (British or French as likely as German) had done; that sexual violence was unnecessary given a supply of willing women in occupied areas; and that the real difficulty was drawing a line about violence against soldiers, as when the British came to adopt the knives they first thought atrocious. The one clear opportunity for "true atrocities" was the stretch between a prisoner's surrender and arrival at headquarters; nearly every instructor knew of specific cases of prisoners being killed on the way back, for reasons the narrator lists (revenge, envy of a comfortable prison camp, fear, trouble of escorting), and the escorts' stock explanation at headquarters invited no questions. The mess believed the same occurred on the German side.
*Candidate application:* A group that discusses its own side's worst conduct in analytical, euphemistic terms ("an opportunity of which advantage was only too often taken") with no jokes in the analysis; the humor in the next finding comes from the tellers' register, not the analysts'.

**[E15b-76]. Telling it as a boast: the two first-hand accounts and Graves's gloss (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6448-6481)
The troops with the worst reputation for violence against prisoners were the Canadians and later the Australians; the Canadian motive was said to be revenge for an unsubstantiated story of a crucified comrade, and the narrator adds a regional jibe about Australians' convict ancestry (paraphrased). He says the mess could not decide how much of the reputation was deserved and how much came from "the overseas habit of bragging and leg-pulling," and records the key observation: "to have committed atrocities against prisoners was, among the overseas men, and even among some British troops, a boast, not a confession." He then gives two first-hand accounts "later in the war," one from a Canadian-Scot and one from an Australian, each told in a swaggering, jovial register (the Australian calls it "the biggest lark I had"), each describing the killing of prisoners (in one case after taking their valuables); the accounts are not reproduced.
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* violence recounted as a joke by its perpetrators, in the vernacular, as a bid for status; the narrator names bragging and tall-telling as part of the culture of the telling.
*Candidate application:* Documented, for two overseas contingents in one observer's account: atrocity told as a boast. A character's relation to such stories (teller, doubter, listener who is sickened) is a place where "humor" shades into something else.

**[E15b-77]. The colonial soldier, the jam tin and the head (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6483-6510)
An old woman at Cardonette, billeted with Graves in July 1916, mimed for him, in pantomime, the story of an alleged mutilation of German soldiers by French colonial infantry (not reproduced). Graves adds that the presence of "colored troops" in Europe was, in the German view, "one of the chief Allied atrocities," and "We sympathized" (the period racial attitude is documented, not endorsed). One instructor then told a mess-table story of a French colonial orderly at a corps headquarters who traded for tins of jam with a cook, was told in jest to bring back an enemy trophy as the price of tomorrow's tin, and did so (the story is told in a pidgin imitation, and the gruesome object is not described here). The teller "did not need to end the story, but swore it was true, because he had seen" the object.
*Mechanism:* a cook's throwaway demand is taken literally; the punchline is a physical object; the teller's insistence on its truth is the guarantee.
*Candidate application:* A story-type in which a joking request is met with horrifying literalism; it also shows the mess's racial caricature of colonial troops.

**[E15b-78]. "Phillips—that was not his name": an officer's confession and the narrator's sorrow (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6512-6525)
A captain from a Surrey regiment blamed his unit's decline on a poor reserve depot: "It all depends on the reserve battalion at home." One night in the sleeping hut he told Graves that in each of his last two attacks he had been driven to kill one of his own men to get the rest out of the trench, and that this was why he had applied for base duty (wording not reproduced). Graves: "This was not the usual loose talk that one heard at the base. He was a good fellow and he was speaking the truth. I was sorrier for Phillips—that was not his name—than for any other man I met in France. He deserved a better regiment. There was never any trouble with the Royal Welch like that."
*Candidate application:* The same mess that jokes and rank-orders produces a private confession that the narrator treats as true and not as talk; the pseudonym is the narrator's protection.

**[E15b-79]. "Never lost a trench": a regimental boast with its own rules of interpretation.** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6526-6536)
"The boast of every good battalion in France was that it had never lost a trench; both our battalions made it." Graves explains that "This boast had to be understood broadly; it meant never having been forced out of a trench by an enemy attack without recapturing it before the action ended." Not counting: a captured trench lost for lack of reinforcements, a retirement by order, or a flank left open by a neighbor's break. "And in the final stages of the war trenches could be honorably abandoned as being entirely obliterated by bombardment, or because not really trenches at all, but a line of selected shell-craters."
*Mechanism:* a legalistic series of exclusions that preserves a regimental claim against reality; the narrator's last clause is a joke about the exclusions.
*Candidate application:* Unit pride maintained by fine print; the narrator knows it and says it.

**[E15b-80]. Drill and regimental pride: a Canadian jibe, "I didn't know why and I didn't care," and "one large creature."** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6538-6584)
The mess agreed on the value of arms-drill for morale; one speaker called good drill "a single movement of one large creature." When Graves drilled Canadians in groups of four or five hundred, their spokesmen "asked what sense there was in sloping and ordering arms and fixing and unfixing bayonets. They said they had come to France to fight and not to guard Buckingham Palace." Graves replied that troops came in three kinds (guts without drill, drill without guts, both), that the last were "much the best men in a show," and "I didn't know why and I didn't care," and that "when they were better at fighting than the Guards' Division they could perhaps afford to neglect their arms-drill." Graves's test of good drill: whether a company, given an incorrect word of command, can carry through the intended order or stand still without disorder. The mess concluded "regimental pride was the greatest moral force that kept a battalion going as an effective fighting unit, contrasting it particularly with patriotism and religion."
*Candidate application:* A stated hierarchy of cohesion: regiment first, nation and God far behind; the Canadians' Palace joke and the officer's flat answer are a small rank-negotiation exchange.

**[E15b-81]. "There was no patriotism in the trenches": the caste system of honor and the despised grades.** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6586-6605)
"There was no patriotism in the trenches. It was too remote a sentiment, and rejected as fit only for civilians. A new arrival who talked patriotism would soon be told to cut it out." Britain was "Blighty," a quiet place to return to, "but as a nation it was nothing." The "nation" for the trench-soldier included staff, supply and base troops and then civilians "down to the detested grades of journalists, profiteers, 'starred' men exempted from enlistment, conscientious objectors, members of the Government," a "carefully graded caste-system of honor." The trench-soldier assumed the German had the same system but believed most newspaper reports about Germany, believed "little or nothing" of those about England, and "never underrated the German as a soldier"; "Newspaper libels on Fritz's courage and efficiency were resented by all trench-soldiers of experience."
*Candidate application:* The targets of trench contempt, listed in graded order; the rule that patriotic talk is silenced is itself a form of license enforcement.

**[E15b-82]. Religion, the sacrilegious fire-order, and the chaplains.** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6607-6668)
Graves says "not one soldier in a hundred was inspired by religious feeling of even the crudest kind," and that in the instructors' mess "we spoke freely of God and Gott as opposed tribal deities." A regular sergeant from the Second Battalion, who "did not hold with religion in time of war," objected to press stories that the figure on a wayside crucifix was always miraculously unhurt ("it fairly makes me sick, sir") and, to illustrate, had shouted during a practice fire-order from a hilltop, "Seven hundred, half left, bloke on cross, five rounds consecrate, fire!"; his platoon, "even the two men whose letters home always had the same formal beginning: 'Dear Sister in Christ,' or 'Dear Brother in Christ,' blazed away." (The sergeant's remark about Indian troops uses a slur and is not reproduced.) The troops were "ready to believe in the Kaiser as a comic personal devil." On chaplains: the mess had no respect for them as a body, since they were under orders to stay behind with the transport; Roman Catholic priests, enjoined to be where the dying were, were respected; one colonel "got rid of four new chaplains in as many months" and then applied for a Catholic chaplain "alleging a change of faith in the men." An old soldier behind Graves at an Anglican sermon before Loos on the battle against sin muttered "Christ, as if one bloody push wasn't enough to worry about at a time." Someone told of a Mesopotamia chaplain who preached before a battle on the commutation of tithes: "This was much more sensible than the battle against sin, he said; it was quite up in the air, and took the men's minds off the fighting."
*Mechanism:* a mock-ritual fire-order that turns a sacred object into a range marker, and the preference for the irrelevant sermon over the relevant one.
*Candidate application:* Irreverence aimed at the chaplain's role and the church's miracle stories, with a sharp exception for priests who share the danger; a documented sorting by conduct, not creed.

**[E15b-83]. The demonstration grenade: the instructor's caution that killed him.** (PW-05, ch. XVII, L6670-6682)
Graves says he felt "a bit better after a few weeks at the base, though the knowledge that this was only temporary relief was with me all the time." Passing the bomb-instruction table one afternoon he heard a crash: a Royal Irish Rifles instructor, giving unofficial instruction before the proper instructor arrived, had said to the men "Now, lads, you've got to be careful with this chap," and "To illustrate the point he rapped it against the edge of the table. It killed him and another man and wounded twelve others more or less severely."
*Candidate application:* An irony of safety instruction told in two sentences without comment, at a safe base, within the same chapter as the mess's confident theorizing.

### I. Chapter XVIII: the Fricourt trenches, the Welsh boy and the night of the superstition (L6690-6918)

**[E15b-84]. "Triste la Guerre," rats at mess, and the choir of A Company.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6696-6727)
Billeted in a farmhouse kitchen near Fricourt, A Company shares it with an old woman who is "senile and paralysed; about all she could do was to shake her head and say: 'Triste, la guerre.' We called her 'Triste la Guerre.' Her daughter used to carry her about in her arms." The trenches were rat-ridden: "We used to eat with revolvers beside our plates and punctuate our conversation with sudden volleys at a rat rummaging at somebody's valise or crawling along the timber support of the roof above our heads." And: "A Company officers were gay. We had all been in our school choirs except Edmund Dadd, who sang like a crow, and we used to chant church anthems and bits of cantatas whenever things were going well. Edmund insisted on joining in."
*Mechanism:* a nickname made from a person's single refrain; a mess in which gunfire at rats is part of dinner; collective singing as the sign that things are going well.
*Candidate application:* The nickname turns a dependent civilian into a mascot (a documented cost of living in someone's house); the choir is an officers' cohesion ritual keyed to the day's mood.

**[E15b-85]. "Sirr, sirr, there is a trenss-mortar in my dug-out": laughter at the dialect, then the count.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6729-6739)
At dinner a Welsh private ("33 Williams," identified by his regimental number) rushes in "hysterical with terror" and shouts to Richardson "Sirr, sirr, there is a trenss-mortar in my dug-out." Graves: "This in sing-song Welsh made us all shout with laughter." Richardson said "Cheer up, 33 Williams, how did a big thing like a trench-mortar happen to be in your dug-out?" but Williams "went on again and again" with the same sentence. Edmund Dadd went out and "found that a trench-mortar shell had fallen into the trench, bounced down the dug-out steps, exploded and killed five men." Williams "had been lying asleep and had been protected by the body of another man; he was the only one unhit."
*Mechanism and limit:* the officers' laughter is at the accent and the incongruous sentence, before they know what has happened; the narrator records both the laugh and what it was a laugh at (regional dialect mockery documented, not endorsed).
*Candidate application:* An example of laughter that precedes information; the narrator does not comment on the officers' mistake, only sequences it.

**[E15b-86]. The canister: simile, contents, and a gun crew that was not allowed to surrender.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6747-6765)
The "greatest trial" was a German trench canister: a drum of explosive that "looked like salmon paste, smelt like marzipan, and when it went off sounded like the day of judgment," with a hollow of scrap metal that included, in one dissected dud, "the cog-wheels of a clock and half a set of false teeth." The men could not agree how it was fired until 1 July, when the battalion attacked and found "a wooden cannon buried in the earth and fired with a time-fuse." The crew offered to surrender "but our men refused; they had sworn for months to get the crew of that gun" (the fate of the crew is implied, not stated).
*Candidate application:* The comparison of an explosive to food and to the Last Judgment in one clause is the narrator's own gallows simile; the unit's vow of revenge on a particular weapon's crew is a documented motive for refusing surrender.

**[E15b-87]. "Touch wood": the adjutant's slip and the night that followed.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6767-6868)
At "Trafalgar Square" (a trench junction) the adjutant says, welcoming the new Stokes mortars, that in a month the battalion has had about three hundred casualties, "five hundred casualties in the ranks since Loos, and not a single officer." "Then he suddenly realized that he had said something unlucky. David said: 'Touch wood.' Everybody sprang to touch wood, but it was a French trench and unriveted. I pulled a pencil out of my pocket; that was wood enough for me. Richardson said: 'I'm not superstitious, anyway.'" Next night David Thomas, uncharacteristically gloomy ("Oh, I'm fed up and I've got a cold"), is shot through the neck and walks down to the dressing-station; Graves "was pleased at this news" because he might be "out of it long enough ... to escape the coming offensive." Graves leads the company back for rum and tea, hears a cry for stretcher-bearers and a man says "Captain Graves is hit": "There was a general laugh and we went on," but it was Richardson, caught by shells in the wire with a corporal, who died at the dressing-station although the doctor had found no wound in any vital spot (the narrator gives the cause as a heart weakened by rowing, with the shock and the cold water). Word then came that David had died of choking after the doctor told him "You'll be all right, only don't raise your head for a bit"; he had given an orderly a letter for a girl in Glamorgan with "Post that." The adjutant came in "ghastly": "I feel responsible in a way for this; what I said yesterday at 'Trafalgar Square.' Of course, really, I don't believe in superstition, but...." A cry of "Stretcher-bearers!" then announced that Pritchard, coming off duty after silencing the enemy mortars, had been killed by a whizz-bang; "The casualties of that night were three officers and one corporal." (On the evening before, while A Company piled sandbags on the parapet in bright moonlight forty or fifty yards from a German sap, the German sentries ignored them; Graves says "It often happened" that when both sides were busy on defenses "they turned a blind eye to each other's work," that rival wiring-parties "as good as borrowed each other's mallets," and that "The Germans were much more ready to live and let live than we were." He knows of only one other daylight moment of mutual non-firing besides Christmas 1914, at Ypres, when flooded trenches forced both sides out on top.)
*Mechanism:* a ritual (touching wood) performed in a trench with no wood, a jokey assertion of disbelief, and a succession of deaths that the adjutant ties to his slip; the single laugh at a false report that Graves was hit.
*Candidate application:* Superstition and its disavowal ("I don't believe in superstition, but...") as the narrator's record of how a unit makes sense of a run of deaths; the laugh at "Captain Graves is hit" shows the group's habit of laughing at an alarm that turns out wrong, until it is not.

**[E15b-88]. The old woman's refrain at the news, and the narrator's plainest statement of loss.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6870-6878)
"It seemed ridiculous when we returned without Richardson to A Company billets to find 'Triste La Guerre' still alive and to hear her once more quaver out 'Triste, la guerre' when her daughter explained that the jeune capitaine had been killed. The old woman had taken a fancy to the jeune capitaine; we used to chaff him about it." Graves: "I felt David's death worse than any other death since I had been in France. It did not anger me as it did Siegfried. ... It just made me feel empty and lost." (Sassoon, acting transport officer, went out looking for Germans each night.)
*Candidate application:* The comic nickname and the old woman's fondness return as an accidental choral comment on a death; the narrator marks the difference between his own response (emptiness) and a friend's (anger and risk-taking).

**[E15b-89]. "For 'trump' we always used to sing 'crump'": the anthem pun and the survivors of the choir.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6880-6895; L6913-6918)
The anthem the officers sang, "He that shall endure to the end, shall be saved," repeated itself in Graves's head "like a charm"; and the line about "the last trump": "For 'trump' we always used to sing 'crump.' 'The last crump' was the end of the war and would we ever hear it burst safely behind us?" (a "crump" being a heavy shell burst). He then states plainly: "I knew that my breaking point was near now, unless something happened to stave it off.... It was not that I was frightened. Certainly I feared death; but I had never yet lost my head through fright, and I knew that I never would. Nor would the breakdown come as insanity; I did not have it in me. It would be a general nervous collapse, with tears and twitchings and dirtied trousers. I had seen cases like that." (The last phrase includes a bodily detail summarized here.) The tally: "three out of five of the officers of the First Battalion at that time were killed in the Somme fighting," "Scatter's dream of open warfare was not realized," and "Of A Company choir there is one survivor besides myself."
*Mechanism:* a one-letter pun that turns the Last Judgment into the end of the shelling; the narrator's forecast of his own collapse in the same register.
*Candidate application:* A choral pun as coping device, a self-prediction of breakdown without pity, and the last paragraph's count of the choir: the humor and the loss are given in the same chapter without comment.

**[E15b-90]. The gas-helmet nickname and a broken nose.** (PW-05, ch. XVIII, L6897-6905)
The battalion is issued a new gas-helmet with a mouth valve, "popularly known as" a coarse nickname built on its goggles and valve (the source dashes out one word; not reproduced). Graves finds he cannot breathe through the mouthpiece because "boxing with an already broken nose had recently displaced the septum," so he could not use the only type "claimed to be proof against the new German gas"; the doctor advised an operation.
*Candidate application:* Soldier nicknames for new equipment arrive with the equipment; the narrator's own body defeats it, and he is sent for an operation (E15b-91).

### J. Chapter XIX: convalescence, the Third Battalion mess, and the Givenchy raid (L6926-7135)

**[E15b-91]. A cottage "in defiance of the war," and a speech that was "commonplace, idle and false."** (PW-05, ch. XIX, L6926-6957)
In April 1916 Graves has his nose operated on in a London military hospital at no cost, then goes to Harlech and buys a cottage from his mother "in defiance of the war, as something to look forward to when the guns stopped ('when the guns stop' was how we always thought of the end of the war)," planning bread-and-butter meals and poetry; the psalm line "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills" was "another charm against trouble." At a Welsh literary dinner his father takes him to, the Australian Prime Minister was "perky, dry and to the point" while Lloyd George delivered a "glory of the Welsh hills" speech: "I knew that the substance of what he was saying was commonplace, idle and false, but I had to fight hard against abandoning myself with the rest of the audience." Close up, the Prime Minister's eyes "were like those of a sleep-walker."
*Candidate application:* The home-front speaker is the rhetoric the trench culture most distrusts; the narrator admits its pull. The cottage is a private future-fantasy of the kind that the soldier cannot joke about.

**[E15b-92]. "Brains," the Third Battalion mess, and the sportsman's confession game.** (PW-05, ch. XIX, L6959-7015)
At Litherland, the mess of the militia majors (country gentlemen) uses Aubrey Attwater, a wounded Cambridge man, as a house encyclopedia: "'Now, Brains! Tell us about Shakespeare. Is it true that Bacon wrote him?'" and "'Well, Brains! What do you think about this chap Hilaire Belloc? Does he really know when the war's going to end?'" Attwater "would humorously accept his position as combined encyclopedia and almanac." On guest nights a senior major puts each neighbor on his honor to confess a sin against sportsmanship: shooting grouse two days before the season, killing a sitting pheasant with a stone, poisoning a trout-stream, shooting foxes to protect hens. The medical officer says he once kept ten shillings a friend had asked him to put on a horse; a Scottish Borderers guest leaps up: "And was not the name of the horse Strathspey? And will you not pay me my ten shillings now immediately?"
*Mechanism:* a parlor game of trivial sins, with the coincidence as punchline; the nickname "Brains" as a role.
*Candidate application:* Home-service mess humor, far from the line, is genteel and anecdotal; the contrast is part of what the narrator is registering.

**[E15b-93]. Arguing about an explosion that never came.** (PW-05, ch. XIX, L7017-7030)
Next to the camp stood a factory making a sensitive explosive; the munition workers had "permanently yellow faces and hands." Attwater argued in mess that if it blew up the closeness of the camp would save it, since the shock would pass over and set off another camp a mile away, while most of the officers held it would kill the camp's three thousand men and part of Bootle. One Sunday smoke rose from the factory; the fire-brigade put it out. "So the argument was never decided."
*Candidate application:* A mock-scientific debate about one's own possible destruction, left unsettled by a fire-brigade; typical of the mess's habit of reasoning coolly about disaster.

**[E15b-94]. The Givenchy mine, the revenge raid, and a regimental record that reverted to the pike.** (PW-05, ch. XIX, L7035-7077)
Arriving at the Second Battalion on 5 July, Graves finds it in the middle of a retaliatory raid: scared Saxon prisoners with new uniforms and packs "full of good stuff to loot"; one gets "a good talking-to" from the Birmingham company sergeant-major, who "was shocked at a packet of indecent photographs found in the man's haversack." The raid answered the biggest mine yet blown on the Western Front, which caught B Company ("the B's were proverbially unlucky"); the crater was afterwards called Red Dragon Crater after the regimental badge; Blair, B Company commander, was buried to the neck and survived. Stanway, "an acting-major" and former company sergeant-major, organized the raid ("Stanway's revenge") with the colonel; Graves's part was to write a record of it "not ... for divisional headquarters but a page of regimental history," in which he "noted that for the first time for two hundred years the regiment had reverted to the pike," some raiders having used butchers' knives secured with medical plaster to broomsticks.
*Mechanism:* the raid is recorded in the manner of regimental history, with an antiquarian flourish about improvised weapons; the sergeant-major's moral shock at the prisoner's possession is a quiet comic note.
*Candidate application:* The unit keeps two records of the same event, the official one for headquarters and the "page of regimental history," in which wit is permitted.

**[E15b-95]. The official journalist's war-cries and the battalion's reaction.** (PW-05, ch. XIX, L7079-7085)
An official journalist at headquarters wrote an account of the raid; "The battalion enjoyed the bit about how they had gone over shouting 'Remember Kitchener!' and 'Avenge the Lusitania!'" One man said: "What a damn silly thing to shout," and went on: "Old Kitchener was all right, but nobody wants him back at the War Office, that I've heard. And as for the Lusitania, the Germans gave her full warning, and if it brings the States into the war, it's all to the good."
*Mechanism:* the men's pleasure in a press version of their words is the pleasure of knowing it false, followed by one soldier's deflating gloss.
*Candidate application:* The official press voice is a documented target; the soldier's reply is a calm, political-economic dismissal, not an insult.

**[E15b-96]. A "chilly reception," a spy rumor, and the consolation of an approaching battle.** (PW-05, ch. XIX, L7087-7121)
Back in the Second Battalion, a diary entry by another officer records "Graves had a chilly reception, which surprised me." Graves explains: a former Third Battalion comrade, now only a second-lieutenant in the Second Battalion while Graves is a captain, felt jealous, and "it amused him to revive the suspicion raised at Wrexham by my German name that I was a German spy." The coincidence that a notorious spy caught in England was named Karl Graves led to a rumor that he was Graves's brother. Graves: "My consolation was that there was obviously a battle due and that would be the end either of me or of the suspicion. I thought to myself: 'So long as there isn't an N.C.O. told off to watch me and shoot me on the slightest appearance of treachery.' Such things had been known." He notes his mother's correspondence with her German sisters (a register of deaths and mutual lectures on the war's justice), and describes Doctor Dunn, who in autumn 1917 doffed his red-cross armlets and took command when a shell knocked out the headquarters staff.
*Mechanism:* the narrator's conversion of a suspicion into a grim bet on the next battle, which will either kill him or end the rumor.
*Candidate application:* An outsider-by-name's dry fatalism; suspicion as a social weapon among officers; the doctor shown as the respected, feared pillar of the unit.

### K. Chapter XX: the Somme, July 1916 (L7143-)

**[E15b-97]. Marching to the Somme: the dead animals, and cigarettes thrown away in lachrymatory mist.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7143-7168)
The Second Battalion marches up in mid-July toward the new battle area: "What struck me most was the number of dead horses and mules lying about; human corpses I was accustomed to, but it seemed wrong for animals to be dragged into the war like this." At the fringe of Mametz Wood, among the dead of the new-army battalions, in a thick mist holding lachrymatory fumes, "we coughed and swore. We tried to smoke, but the gas had got into the cigarettes, so we threw them away. Later we wished we had not, because it was not the cigarettes that had been affected so much as our own throats." They see a captured German gun with "First Battalion Royal Welch Fusiliers" chalked on it. Graves also passes on hearsay that the German regiment the First Battalion overran at Fricourt had been on "a special disciplinary spell" because an inspecting staff officer found all its officers in a deep dug-out in Mametz village instead of with their men ("It was said that throughout that bad time in March ... there had been no officer of higher rank than corporal").
*Candidate application:* The narrator, by his own account used to human corpses, is still bothered by animal dead; the cigarette bathos is a deadpan correction of a small mistake.

**[E15b-98]. "Mad Jack": Sassoon, the book of poems, and "British patrols."** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7169-7204)
Graves learns that Sassoon "distinguished himself" at the Quadrangle by taking single-handed a position that the Royal Irish Regiment had failed to take the day before; he went over in daylight with bombs under covering fire and scared the occupants out, then "sat down in the German trench and began dozing over a book of poems which he had brought with him" without reporting. The attack on Mametz Wood was delayed two hours because British patrols were reported still out: "'British patrols' were Siegfried and his book of poems." The colonel stormed, "It would have got you a D.S.O. if you'd only had more sense." Sassoon's nickname in the Seventh Division was "Mad Jack"; he had also won a Military Cross for bringing in a wounded lance-corporal. Graves sent him a rhymed letter via a transport man about the times "after the war" (a visit to "the Caucasus and Persia and China") and "what good poetry we would write."
*Mechanism:* the official category "British patrols" absorbing a man reading poems in an enemy trench; the colonel's rebuke as a statement of how rewards are earned; the rhymed letter as a defiance of the war in verse.
*Candidate application:* A decorated eccentric known by nickname ("Mad Jack") and the institution's reply to a feat that was "pointless" and unreported; the letter is a private dream-world exchanged by post.

**[E15b-99]. Dadd's complaint: success as a punishment.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7212-7222)
Edmund Dadd, commanding A Company, says "It's not fair, Robert": A Company's reputation as best company has made the colonel use it as the leading company of every show, "we get our objectives and hold them, and so we've got to do the same again the next time"; he is "indispensable," so he goes over every time; "I've had five shows in just over a fortnight and I can't go on being lucky every time." "The colonel's about due for his c.b. Apparently A Company is making sure of [it] for him." (the printed text reads "of if")
*Mechanism:* a mock-grievance in the idiom of fairness and promotion, which is also a plain statement of statistical dread.
*Candidate application:* The best unit is spent fastest (compare E15b-74); the joke on the colonel's decoration is a rank-directed gibe that cannot be reported.

**[E15b-100]. Mametz Wood: a drill-book bayonet cry (restrained handling) and a superstition about souvenirs.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7225-7252)
Going into the shattered wood for German greatcoats to use as blankets, Graves passes dead of the Prussian Guards Reserve ("big men") and of the new-army Welsh battalions ("little men"), including a decaying German corpse propped on a tree and two men who bayoneted each other simultaneously (details not reproduced). A survivor later told him he had seen a young soldier of the Fourteenth Royal Welch killing a German "in parade-ground style, automatically exclaiming as he had been taught: 'In, out, on guard,'" which the teller found "the oddest thing he had heard in France." Graves adds that he "found myself still superstitious about looting or collecting souvenirs. The greatcoats were only a loan, I told myself."
*Candidate application:* The drill-ground command voiced in the act of killing is a documented grotesque that a witness classes as "odd," not as horrific; the narrator's own taboo on souvenirs is a private superstition.

**[E15b-101]. The periscope with a bullet-hole, and the mother who had it exchanged.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7245-7252)
His one allowed souvenir was a little metal trench periscope sent from home, which, held above the parapet, "offered only an inch-square target to the German snipers" until, at Cuinchy in May, a sniper "drilled it through, exactly central, at four hundred yards range." Graves sent it home with no note, "My mother, misunderstanding, and practical as usual, took it back to the makers and made them change it for a new one."
*Mechanism:* the front's evidence of a near miss is read by civilian common sense as a faulty product.
*Candidate application:* A documented example of the civilian-facing gap (the object from the line cannot explain itself) told as a family joke.

**[E15b-102]. A hysterical guide under arrest, a company that left without formalities, and a standing order on gas.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7254-7278)
Moving up on 18 July to relieve the Tyneside Irish near Bazentin le Petit, the guide "was hysterical and had forgotten the way; we put him under arrest and found it ourselves." The first real (non-lachrymatory) gas shells hit in the village; the standing order was "not to put on one's respirator but hurry on," and "we lost about half a dozen men." The relieved company "hurried out without any of the usual formalities; they had been badly shaken"; their officer could not say where the Germans or the neighbors were, and Graves "cursed him and he went off."
*Candidate application:* Breakdown reported as an administrative event (arrest, curse, no formalities), with no comment on the guide's state.

**[E15b-103]. Spilt tea, a kipper saying, "one for sorrow," sleep under shelling, and the mouse.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7280-7308)
Under heavy shelling at noon, the concussion spilt Graves's tea and filled it with dirt three times running; "I was in a cheerful mood and only laughed," since a parcel of kippers from home, "they were far more important than the bombardment," as he put it. He recalled his mother's saying: "Children, remember this when you eat your kippers; kippers cost little, yet if they cost a hundred guineas a pair they would still find buyers among the millionaires." A draggled tame magpie (belonging to the Germans just driven out) came into the trench: "'That's one for sorrow,' I said. The men swore that it spoke something in German as it came in, but I did not hear it." He slept through the bombardment: "I decided that I would just as soon be killed asleep as awake," having "learned to go to sleep sitting down, standing up, marching, lying on a stone floor," yet alert instantly for stand-to. A nightmare of someone choosing the place to knife him ended when "I woke up with a start, shouting, and punched the small of my back where the hand was. I found that I had killed a mouse that had been frightened by the bombardment and run down my neck."
*Mechanism:* a cheerful domestic frame (kippers, magpie, mother's saying) held against shelling; a nightmare resolved into a mouse.
*Candidate application:* The ability to sleep through bombardment while waking to a single command is stated as acquired skill; the mouse is the anticlimax that the narrator sets down without further comment.

**[E15b-104]. The cruciform strong-points: staff orders that Graves and his captain "looked at our map and laughed."** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7310-7324)
Brigade orders D Company to build two cruciform strong-points at map references that turn out to be half-way to Martinpuich and "occupied in force by the enemy." Graves: "Moodie, the company commander, and I looked at our map and laughed." Moodie replied that he "would be glad to do so, but would require an artillery bombardment and strong reinforcements"; the colonel verified and moved the sites about three hundred yards forward. A strong-point is two crossing thirty-yard trenches wired all round, which "looked, in diagram, like a hot-cross bun."
*Candidate application:* The same pattern as the Loos orders (E15b-35): staff paper that can be laughed at by the company officers and answered with a polite, fully serious reply that makes the point.

**[E15b-105]. A bearded corpse on the moonlit road, "I needed a charm," and the Gordon Highlanders' hollows.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7326-7340)
Along the Bazentin-High Wood road lay a German sergeant-major "on his back in the middle of the road, his arms stretched out wide," short, powerful and black-bearded: "He looked sinister in the moonlight; I needed a charm to get myself past him. The simplest way, I found, was to cross myself." On the sunken road's bank, defenders had scraped small hollows and wounded men had crawled in: "put their heads and shoulders inside and died there. They looked as if they had tried to hide from the black beard. They were Gordon Highlanders."
*Candidate application:* The narrator's cross-the-self charm and the quiet image of the dead in hollows show a plain register for the dead of one's own side; no joke.

**[E15b-106]. The Public Schools Battalion patrol: a night comedy of mistaken identity.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7342-7374)
At a strong-point, the whisper goes round "Get your rifles ready. Here comes Fritz"; Graves, lying flat, sees massed figures seventy yards off, restrains the men ("They may even surrender") and sends for a Lewis gun and a flare pistol. When Moodie fires over their heads, "a tall officer came running towards us with his hands up in surrender. He was surprised to find that we were not Germans." He belongs to the Public Schools Battalion of their own brigade; the "patrol" is "half a company of men wandering about aimlessly between the lines, their rifles slung over their shoulders, and, it seemed, without the faintest idea where they were or what information they were supposed to bring back." Graves's contemptuous class account: the battalion's best men were taken as officers for other regiments, leaving those "unfitted to hold commissions; yet unfitted by their education to make good soldiers in the ranks"; it "was a constant embarrassment to the brigade."
*Mechanism:* a mutual-surrender farce in which both sides believed the other the enemy; the narrator's explanation is a class judgment.
*Candidate application:* A unit's contempt for an unreliable neighbor produces its own jokes (see the colonel's remark below); the comic episode and the contempt reinforce each other.

**[E15b-107]. The gunner's carved chalk and "both he and it survived the war."** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7376-7392)
Picking over a shelled German limber (the driver and horses were dead) Graves takes "a large lump of chalk wrapped up in a piece of cloth; it had been carved and decorated in colors with military mottos, the flags of the Central Powers, and the names of the various battles in which the gunner had served," and sends it to Doctor Dunn: "I am glad to say that both he and it survived the war; he is in practice at Glasgow, and the lump of chalk is under a glass case in his consulting room." Battalion strength was now "only about four hundred."
*Candidate application:* The deadpan pairing of a man and an object ("both he and it survived") for a souvenir whose maker is unknown and probably dead.

**[E15b-108]. The reserve briefing: the colonel's joke about the Public Schools Battalion, and a battalion order that names a churchyard.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7394-7430)
Graves has kept a midnight battalion order of 20 July (printed in spaced-out telegraph columns, with "aaa" as the stop; in the text file it reads across the columns). He adds: "S14b 99 was a map reference for Bazentin churchyard." At the officers' conference, over the noise of a battery of French 75s, the colonel explains that they are in reserve behind the Cameronians and the Fifth Scottish Rifles with the Public Schools Battalion in support, and "if we are, it will mean that the Jocks have legged it. As usual" (Graves labels this "an appeal to prejudice") and, of the Public Schools men, "so if we are called for, that means it will be the end of us." "He said this with a laugh and we all laughed." Orders: "shake out in artillery formation" and "hang on like death" in the wood; then "good-bye and good luck."
*Mechanism:* the commander's laughing statement of low confidence in neighboring units, endorsed by the whole conference.
*Candidate application:* A commander who shares a joke about the odds before the attack and then gives sober orders; the narrator marks one line as prejudice and records the shared laughter.

**[E15b-109]. Division's "usual inappropriate message," and a private's court-martial that he and two NCOs escaped the battle for.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7432-7450)
"Division could always be trusted to send through a warning about verdigris on vermorel-sprayers, or the keeping of pets in trenches, or being polite to our allies, or some other triviality, when an attack was in progress." This time the order sent a C Company private, with a lance-corporal escort and a sergeant witness, back to the assistant provost-marshal for a court-martial on a charge of killing a French civilian in a Béthune estaminet a month earlier (the civilian, drunk, had taunted him; the manner of the killing is not reproduced). "At the court-martial the private was exculpated; the French civil representative commended him for having 'energetically repressed local defeatism.' So he and the two N.C.O.'s missed the battle."
*Mechanism:* a sardonic list of staff trivialities; and an ironic outcome in which a killing is praised and the accused is thereby saved from the attack.
*Candidate application:* The staff's habit of irrelevant warnings is a stable target; the murder trial that doubles as a reprieve is told as an administrative curiosity.

**[E15b-110]. Wounded at High Wood: dry reckoning of the line of the wounds, the marble chip, and the Nietzsche charm (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7456-7491)
The battalion lost about a third of its strength to a barrage before its attack began; Graves was among them when an eight-inch shell burst about three paces behind him: "I was able to work that out afterwards by the line of my wounds." He felt "punched rather hard between the shoulder-blades, but had no sensation of pain," then blood in his eye; he called to Moodie "I've been hit." He had read as omens two small wounds on his left hand matching those on his right at Loos, and repeated to himself, as a charm, a line of Nietzsche in French ("you cannot kill me," from a poem about a man on the scaffold with the red-bearded executioner standing over him); he adds that the Nietzsche volume had contributed to the suspicion that he was a spy. His wounds: a fragment through the thigh high near the groin (he reckons from his stride that a worse injury was just missed; wording not reproduced), a finger wound, and a main wound through the chest from below the right shoulder. "The wound over the eye was nothing; it was a little chip of marble, possibly from one of the Bazentin cemetery headstones."
*Mechanism:* the narrator treats his own wounds as a geometry problem and ends with a dark irony about the chip's likely origin.
*Candidate application:* An officer may face his own wounding as an inference exercise and as a test of an earlier omen; one irony (the headstone chip) is allowed to stand.

**[E15b-111]. "Old Gravy's got it, all right": the wink, the corpse-clearing, and the formal letter of condolence.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7493-7538)
Doctor Dunn dressed the wound under the barrage; Graves remembers only "winking at the stretcher-bearer sergeant who was looking at me and saying: 'Old Gravy's got it, all right.'" At the overworked dressing-station he lay unconscious in a corner more than twenty-four hours; the colonel, told he was done for, left; "The next morning, the 21st, when they were clearing away the dead, I was found to be still breathing." On the ambulance, "I remember screaming." That morning the colonel wrote the "usual formal letters of condolence" to the next-of-kin of six or seven officers. Graves prints the one to his mother: "I very much regret to have to write and tell you your son has died of wounds. He was very gallant, and was doing so well and is a great loss. He was hit by a shell and very badly wounded, and died on the way down to the base I believe. He was not in bad pain, and our doctor managed to get across and attend him at once." and notes the colonel "made out the official casualty list and reported me died of wounds. It was a long casualty list, because only eighty men were left in the battalion."
*Mechanism:* the soldiers' nickname form ("Old Gravy"), the wink as a signal to the stretcher-bearers, and the formulaic letter whose details (no bad pain, doctor at once) are the standard comfort.
*Candidate application:* The wink at the stretcher-bearer is the wounded man's own signal that he is staying in the register; the formal letter shows the form's comforts.

**[E15b-112]. Heilly: amused by the bubbles, and a dialogue of refusals with an exhausted doctor.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7540-7569)
In a hot marquee hospital, semi-conscious, Graves "realized my lung-wound by the shortness of breath. I was amused to watch the little bubbles of blood, like red soap-bubbles, that my breath made when it escaped through the hole of the wound." He asks the exhausted doctor for a drink. "Would you like some tea?" "Not with condensed milk in it." "I'm afraid there's no fresh milk." (Tears came to his eyes: "I expected better of a hospital behind the lines.") "Will you have some water?" "Not if it's boiled." "It is boiled. And I'm afraid I can't give you anything with alcohol in it in your present condition." "Give me some fruit then." "I have seen no fruit for days." A few minutes later the doctor returned with "two rather unripe greengages," and Graves "promised him a whole orchard when I recovered." Told to stay put for his best chance, he insists on moving; the doctor later says all cases are being evacuated because Guards casualties will arrive; "I had no fears now about dying. I was content to be wounded and on the way home."
*Mechanism:* the patient's absurd list of refusals as a comic duet with a patient doctor; amusement at one's own wound.
*Candidate application:* A badly wounded man's fussy exactness is both comedy and a sign that he is surviving; the doctor's tenderness (the greengages) is unspoken.

**[E15b-113]. The brigade-major's "most beautiful bit of parade-ground drill," and the priest who brought the Scotsmen back.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7571-7591)
A wounded brigade-major in the next bed tells Graves he watched the battalion's High Wood advance through field-glasses: "The way your battalion shook out into artillery formation ... was the most beautiful bit of parade-ground drill I've ever seen. Your company officers must have been superb." Graves: "I happened to know that one company at least had started without a single officer." The major continues: the Public Schools Battalion "came away as soon as it got dark; and so did the Scotsmen. Your chaps were left there alone for some time. They steadied themselves by singing. Later, the chaplain—R.C. of course—Father McCabe, brought the Scotsmen back. They were Glasgow Catholics and would follow a priest where they wouldn't follow an officer."
*Candidate application:* The compliment to "officers" for a drill that ran on its own is the earlier "all-one-body" drill theory (E15b-80) borne out, an inference of mine; the singing and the priest are two documented props of cohesion.

**[E15b-114]. The hospital train: the German officer's weeping, and "died of wounds" as the more circumstantial report.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7593-7621; fn. [8])
Because lifting would risk hemorrhage, Graves rides the train on his stretcher laid across a bunk. "A German officer on the other side of the carriage groaned and wept unceasingly. He had been in an aeroplane crash and had a compound fracture of the leg. The other wounded men were cursing him and telling him to stow it and be a man, but he went on, keeping every one awake. He was not delirious, only frightened and in great pain." On 24 July, his twenty-first birthday, "my death officially occurred." His own letter, dated the 23rd (he had lost count), reached his parents two days after the colonel's of the 22nd, with fn. [8] saying "I cannot explain the discrepancy between his dating of my death and that of the published casualty list"; his parents could not decide whether he died just after writing: "'Died of wounds' was, however, so much more circumstantial than 'killed' that they gave me up." An aunt saw his name on a ward list at Rouen and wrote to reassure his mother.
*Mechanism:* the wounded policing a weeping enemy's unmanliness; and a dry reading of the official phrase as more convincing than the plain one.
*Candidate application:* A contrast between the group's demand that a wounded man keep quiet and the narrator's sympathetic gloss; the archive's tone about his own recorded death is that of a clerk.

**[E15b-115]. The colonel's second letter: "put you in a dug-out in Heaven."** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7621-7647)
On 30 July, the colonel writes "Dear von Runicke": "I cannot tell you how pleased I am you are alive. I was told your number was up for certain." He reports "a rotten time," the battalion "succeeding in doing practically the impossible" then collecting "that rotten crowd" (presumably the Public Schools Battalion and the Scottish units; the letter does not name them) "and put[ting] them in their places, but directly dark came they legged it. It was too sad," and "It is not fair putting brave men like ours alongside that crowd." He writes of the men's "magnificent and wonderful disregard for death as I saw that day. It was almost uncanny—it was so great," and of an old officer's saying that "the men would follow you to Hell; but these chaps would bring you back and put you in a dug-out in Heaven." He ends "Good luck and a quick recovery. I shall drink your health to-night."
*Candidate application:* The colonel's praise is itself in the unit's idiom of understated hyperbole; the same man wrote a formal death letter eight days earlier.

**[E15b-116]. Convalescent petulance: the septic finger, the sympathy for war wounds only, and "Rob All My Comrades."** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7649-7664)
Graves says he had "little pain ... but much discomfort," mostly from a septic finger no one had dressed and sticky plaster on his thigh. He compares it with his nose operation two months earlier, for which he "had won no sympathy at all from anyone, because it was not an injury contracted in war." Weak and "petulant and muddled," he is outraged by the R.A.M.C. bugling: "The 'Rob All My Comrades,' I complained, had taken everything I had except a few papers in my tunic-pocket and a ring which was too tight on my finger to be pulled off; and now they mis-blew the Last Post flat and windily, and with the pauses in the wrong places, just to annoy me." He told an orderly "to put the bugler under arrest and jump to it or I'd report him to the senior medical officer."
*Mechanism:* the backronym "Rob All My Comrades" for R.A.M.C. (a soldiers' joke here attributed to Graves's complaint) and the invalid's mock-command to an orderly.
*Candidate application:* A traditional insult for the medical corps is the narrator's vehicle for self-mockery of his own state; the passage also states plainly that only war injuries earn sympathy.

**[E15b-117]. O. M. Roberts's High Wood account (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7666-7682)
A Welsh boy in the next bed, wounded in the groin before reaching the wood's edge, recalled lying in a shell-hole and seeing a German officer working round the wood killing wounded men with a pistol, and "some of our lightly-wounded were, apparently, not behaving as wounded men should; they were sniping." The German shot him in the arm and fired again; Roberts, weak, got his revolver out and, with both hands, killed him at five yards; he then fainted. (Graphic details of the killing are not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* The one-line "not behaving as wounded men should" shows an unspoken etiquette of the wounded as non-combatants; the story is told plainly.

**[E15b-118]. Aspirating the lung, and recognizing the First Battalion by its singing.** (PW-05, ch. XX, L7684-7698)
Doctors mark the displacement of his heart on his skin with an indelible pencil; the aspiration "sounded a serious operation" but "hurt no more than a vaccination," and he read the *Gazette de Rouen* during it (the details of the drawing-off are not reproduced). That evening he heard "a sudden burst of lovely singing in the courtyard where the ambulances pulled up. I recognized the quality of the voices. I said to Roberts: 'The First Battalion have been in it again,' and asked a nurse to verify it; I was right."
*Candidate application:* A unit identifiable by its voices arriving as wounded; the narrator's newspaper-reading during a medical procedure is the same aloofness as the earlier bubbles.

### L. Home, the "death," and the war-madness at home (L7705-8075)

**[E15b-119]. Waterloo: the roar of the crowd, a father "hopping about on one leg," and the one thing disliked in the army.** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7708-7723)
At Waterloo a "huge hysterical crowd" roars at each stretcher; "It seemed that the Somme battle was regarded at home as the beginning of the end of the war." One figure detaches: "my father, hopping about on one leg, waving an umbrella and cheering with the best of them. I was embarrassed, but was soon in the ambulance." Highgate gave him a private room: "What I most disliked in the army was never being alone, forced to live and sleep with men whose company in many cases I would have run miles to avoid."
*Candidate application:* The narrator's most direct negative statement about unit life (no solitude) sits beside the home-front crowd's cheering; comradeship is, in his account, partly imposed proximity.

**[E15b-120]. Being "supposed to be dead": "the joke contributed greatly to my recovery."** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7725-7768)
At Highgate he hears "for the first time that I was supposed to be dead; the joke contributed greatly to my recovery." "The people with whom I had been on the worst terms during my life wrote the most enthusiastic condolences to my parents: my housemaster, for instance." He keeps a letter of 5 August 1916 from *The Times* advertisement department, a model of formal courtesy, and the cutting itself: "Captain Robert Graves, Royal Welch Fusiliers, officially reported died of wounds, wishes to inform his friends that he is recovering from his wounds at Queen Alexandra's Hospital, Highgate, N.," printed in the same column as the item "Mrs. Lloyd George has left London for Criccieth." Letters sent to him in France came back marked "Died of wounds—present location uncertain.—P. Down, post-corporal." "The only inconvenience that my death caused me was that Cox's Bank stopped my pay and I had difficulty in persuading it to honor my checks." Sassoon wrote that he "was overjoyed to hear I was alive again. (I wondered whether he had been avenging me.)," adding he "was nine parts dead from the horror of the Somme fighting."
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* the joke is the gap between a man's own state and the records of him, and Graves says the joke aided his recovery; the bureaucratic forms (newspaper, post-corporal, bank) are the humor vehicle, and the "avenging" aside is a joke about a friend's recklessness.
*Candidate application:* A direct statement of humor's function (convalescence) tied to a specific comic situation; the post-corporal's annotation is a good example of institutional deadpan applied to a man's own death.

**[E15b-121]. The Paddington casualty list and Siegfried's bitter line about the colonel's C.B.** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7770-7782)
In September at Paddington, Sassoon buys *The Times* and they find "the names of practically every officer in the First Battalion, either killed or wounded. Edmund Dadd was killed." His brother Julian, in Sassoon's company, was wounded through the throat and "only able to talk in a whisper" (details of the wound minimized). Graves: "News like this in England was far more upsetting than when one was in France. I was still very weak and could not help crying all the way up to Wales." Sassoon "said bitterly: 'Well, I expect the colonel got his c.b. at any rate.'"
*Mechanism:* the same gibe that Dadd made before his death (E15b-99) is repeated by a friend as an elegy.
*Candidate application:* The bitterness attaches to the commander's decoration; the narrator's weeping, which he states without apology, is the "plain" half of the same day.

**[E15b-122]. "Newspaper language," and the "Little Mother's" letter reproduced without comment.** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7782-7929)
Graves says of England: "He could not understand the war-madness that ran about everywhere looking for a pseudo-military outlet. Every one talked a foreign language; it was newspaper language. I found serious conversation with my parents all but impossible." As "a single typical memorial of this time" he prints in full a pamphlet, "A Mother's Answer to 'A Common Soldier,' by A Little Mother" (a letter that, per the cover, sold seventy-five thousand copies in under a week and was praised by the Queen), followed by its press testimonials, without a word of commentary. Samples (public domain, example is the point): "There is only one temperature for the women of the British race, and that is white heat." "We women pass on the human ammunition of 'only sons' to fill up the gaps, so that when the 'common soldier' looks back before going 'over the top' he may see the women of the British race on his heels, reliable, dependent, uncomplaining." Testimonials include "I defy any man to feel weak-hearted after reading it.... My God! she makes us die happy.'—One who has Fought and Bled" and a "Bereaved Mother" who "would now gladly give my sons twice over."
*Mechanism:* satire by exhibit: the text is allowed to condemn itself through its own phrases, and the narrator's frame names it "newspaper language." (That Graves intends it as satire is inferred from the frame, not stated.)
*Candidate application:* The home-front voice as the soldier's alien tongue; a veteran-character may hold the same view without needing to comment.

**[E15b-123]. Goliath and David: the marginal note that reverses the biblical legend.** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7931-7969)
At Harlech with Sassoon, the two decide "its continuance seemed merely a sacrifice of the idealistic younger generation to the stupidity and self-protective alarm of the elder." Graves gives the "facetious marginal note" he wrote on his poem *Goliath and David*: "War should be a sport for men above forty-five only, the Jesse's, not the David's. 'Well, dear father, how proud I am of you serving your country as a very gallant gentleman prepared to make even the supreme sacrifice. I only wish I were your age: how willingly would I buckle on my armor and fight those unspeakable Philistines! As it is, of course, I can't be spared; I have to stay behind at the War Office and administrate for you lucky old men.' 'What sacrifices I have made,' David would sigh when the old boys had gone off with a draft to the front singing *Tipperary*. 'There's father and my Uncle Salmon and both my grandfathers, all on active service. I must put a card in the window about it.'" He adds that they defined the war "by making contrasted definitions of peace": for Sassoon hunting, nature and music; for Graves "chiefly children," whom he played with in the French villages.
*Mechanism:* inversion: the staff-officer son thanks the old soldiers for their sacrifice, and the home-front card in the window is parodied; the target is the older generation and the safe jobs.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's satire of "sacrifice" talk from non-combatants, aimed at the age structure of who serves; a concrete example of the intergenerational resentment the mess shared.

**[E15b-124]. The Kent house: a dead son's room, and "It's worse than France."** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7974-7992)
Visiting a wounded First Battalion friend in Kent, whose elder brother died at the Dardanelles and whose mother "kept his bedroom exactly as he had left it, with the sheets aired, his linen always freshly laundered, and flowers and cigarettes by his bedside" and "went about with a vague bright look on her face," Graves is kept awake by rapping noises and then at three a "diabolic yell and a succession of laughing, sobbing shrieks." He meets the mother in the passage, "to my surprise, ... fully dressed": "It's nothing. One of the maids has hysterics. I am so sorry you have been disturbed." In the morning: "I'm leaving this place. It's worse than France."
*Mechanism:* the hostess's flat reassurance over a night of terror; Graves's verdict compares the house to the front.
*Candidate application:* Home-front bereavement as horror in its own right, told with a one-line exit; the narrator's "worse than France" is a compliment to neither.

**[E15b-125]. Litherland with Sassoon: "Every one was mad," the poets' reputation, and the officer who read the *London Mail* to his platoon.** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L7996-8017)
Back at Litherland in November, Graves and Sassoon "decided that it was no use making a protest against the war. Every one was mad; we were hardly sane ourselves." Sassoon: they must "keep up the good reputation of the poets" (as men of courage). The best place was France, away from "the more shameful madness of home service," where "Our function there was not to kill Germans, though that might happen, but to make things easier for the men under our command." For the men the difference was between an officer "whom they could count as a friend, someone who protected them as much as he could from the grosser indignities of the military system" and "any thoughtless, petty tyrant in an officer's tunic." As an example of the first kind, Sassoon, told to rehearse once more the Fricourt attack that had already been rehearsed for a week until it was "almost stale," instead "led his platoon into a wood and read to them—nothing military or literary, just the *London Mail*. Though the *London Mail* was not in his line, Siegfried thought that the men would enjoy the 'Things We Want to Know' column."
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* a stated theory of the junior officer's role (protect the men from petty tyranny), exemplified by a comic subversion that spares the platoon.
*Candidate application:* An officer's humane humor is a refusal to enforce pointless drill, framed as a joke on himself ("not in his line").

**[E15b-126]. The golf club: playing the fool, and the waiter's "more satisfactory consignment of meat on Boxing Day."** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L8019-8039)
Officers of the Royal Welch were honorary members of a nearby golf club; Sassoon played seriously and Graves "limited myself to a single club ... I played the fool and purposely put Siegfried off his game." In a time of strict rationing, the club's Liverpool businessmen ate well. On the day before Christmas, at the cold buffet of hams, beef, tongue, turkey and chicken, Sassoon "satirically asked" the waiter: "Is this all? There doesn't seem to be quite such a good spread as in previous years." The waiter apologized: "No, sir, this isn't quite up to the usual mark, sir, but we are expecting a more satisfactory consignment of meat on Boxing Day." Graves ends: "The dining-room at the club-house was always full, the links were practically deserted."
*Mechanism:* sarcasm at a profiteering club answered in earnest by the staff; the waiter's apology is the joke.
*Candidate application:* The home-front rich as target of irony between two junior officers; the failure of the satire to register is part of its point.

**[E15b-127]. The Adelphi major and the sergeant-major's stomach: a tall tale of spare parts.** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L8041-8075)
At the Adelphi Hotel (a favorite of the Mersey garrison officers, with a cocktail bar "generally crowded with Russian naval officers, always very drunk"), a drunk major of the King's Own Scottish Borderers takes Graves aside after a salute: "It's nice of you to salute me, my boy, but I must confess that I am not what I seem. I wear a crown on my sleeve and so does a company sergeant-major; but then he's not entitled to wear these three cuff bands and the wavy border.... I'm a sham. I've got a sergeant-major's stomach." The story: shot in the stomach at Beaumont-Hamel, "busy dying" in a field-hospital where a company sergeant-major with a head wound was also "busy dying, too, and he did die," the surgeons took out the sergeant-major's long gut and grafted it into him: "Wonderful chaps these medicos. They can put in spare parts as if one was a motor-car. Well, this sergeant-major seems to have been an abstemious man; the lining of the new gut is much better than my old one; so I'm celebrating it. I only wish I had his kidneys too." Graves "was quite accustomed to drunken senior officers, so I answered respectfully: 'Really, major, and how did you come to get that?'" An R.A.M.C. captain adds that stomach wounds are the worst, that he survived one by lying still and keeping stretcher-bearers off with a revolver for forty-eight hours, "I couldn't count on a spare gut waiting for me at the dressing-station."
*Mechanism:* a gallows tall tale, apparently improbable as medicine, in which the dead man's body parts become the living man's improvement; the second speaker caps it in the same idiom.
*Candidate application:* A drunk's tale of survival told as a comic transplant, then matched by a medical man's practical version; both speakers use "spare parts" as a joke form. (Whether the graft is true is not asserted; the narrator does not check it.)

### M. Return to France, winter 1916-17 (L8077-8190)

**[E15b-128]. "Fit for service overseas," and the old soldier's kit: wire-cutters "strong enough to cut German wire."** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L8077-8103)
At the medical board in December, asked whether he wanted a few more months of home service, Graves said: "No, sir, I should be much obliged if you would pass me fit for service overseas." In January he went out "as an old soldier; my kit and baggage proved it": a pocket-torch with a fourteen-day battery, "a pair of insulated wire-cutters strong enough to cut German wire (the ordinary British army issue would only cut British wire)," a pack like the men's, an eiderdown sleeping-bag in oiled silk, Shakespeare and a Bible printed on India paper, Catullus and Lucretius in Latin, and two light folding canvas arm-chairs, one for the quartermaster. Officers now carried rifles and bayonets and rolled trousers over puttees "because the Germans had been taught to recognize them by their thin knees." His whipcord tunic bore "a neat patch above the second button and another between the shoulders," "my only salvage from the last time out."
*Mechanism:* the kit list as a joke about what experience teaches, ending with the tunic's two patches (the entry and exit holes, implied but not stated).
*Candidate application:* An old soldier is marked by a small set of practical alterations; the wire-cutters joke is dry institutional criticism.

**[E15b-129]. A draft of ten young officers and a brothel (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L8105-8125)
Citing Charles Edmonds's book for the expectation that young officers be "roistering blades with wine and women," Graves says the ten who came out with him "did their best": three contracted venereal disease at Rouen, each for the first time with a woman; these strictly raised Welsh professional-class boys "knew nothing about prophylactics." One, very drunk, woke Graves at night to say what a wonderful thing sex was; Graves answered in disgust, wishing aloud that he had washed; the boy, "very Welsh and on his dignity," replied in an exaggerated accent that he had washed his face and hands (the exchange is paraphrased). "There were no restraints in France as in England; these boys had money to spend and knew that they had a good chance of being killed within a few weeks anyhow. They did not want to die virginal." Base venereal hospitals "were always crowded"; "The troops took a lewd delight in exaggerating the proportion of army chaplains to combatant officers treated there" (paraphrased); the brothel "saved the life of scores of them by incapacitating them for future trench service."
*Candidate application:* The narrator's stated reasoning for the young officers' behavior (nothing to lose) and the dry final clause (the brothel as lifesaver) show an actuarial humor; the literal-minded answer is an ethnic-dialect gag (documented, not endorsed).

**[E15b-130]. Bayonet-training: the revised principle and the screaming instructors (restrained handling).** (PW-05, ch. XXI, L8127-8147)
The Bull Ring's instructors "were full of bullet-and-bayonet enthusiasm"; the drafts were now "forcibly enlisted men or wounded men returning," and at that dead season it was hard to feel enthusiastic. The doctrine had changed: the 1914 manual "had laid it down politely that the soldier's ultimate aim was to put out of action or render ineffective the armed forces of the enemy," now "not considered direct enough for a war of attrition"; troops were taught "that their duty was to hate the Germans and kill as many of them as possible." Bayonet practice demanded that the men "make horrible grimaces and utter blood-curdling yells," and the bayonet-fighting instructors' "faces were permanently set in a ghastly grin," screaming graphic incitements at the dummies (not reproduced) and mocking those whose charge looked gentle: "Anyone would think that you loved the bloody swine, patting and stroking 'em like that." Graves: "Once more I was glad to be sent up to the trenches."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's contrast of the two doctrinal phrasings (euphemism versus command to hate) as a joke on the training establishment; the instructors' mock-berating of gentleness.
*Candidate application:* The base's manufactured hatred is a target the front shares; the narrator treats it as a form of theater.

**[E15b-131]. The Second Battalion transformed, the colonel shot by his "rotten crowd," the rum ban, and the village named on a steam-roller.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8155-8190; the last nine lines overlap E15c's start)
At Bouchavesnes "It was a very different Second Battalion. No riding-school, no battalion-mess, no Quetta manners. I was more warmly welcomed this time; my suspected spying activities were forgotten." The colonel had been shot in the thigh the day before "by one of the 'rotten crowd' of his letter, who mistook him for a German and fired without challenging." Doctor Dunn asked "with kindly disapproval what I meant by coming back so soon"; Graves said "I could not stand England any longer," and Dunn had him declared unfit for trench service and put in command of the headquarter company (clerks, cooks, tailors and so on) on the frozen Somme. The divisional general's "teetotal convictions on behalf of his men" stopped the rum issue except for emergencies and the sick list rose: "The men had always looked forward to their tot of rum at the dawn stand-to as the one bright moment of the twenty-four hours." Of the ruin of Cléry (the highest standing part "a short course of brick wall about three feet high"): "A broken-down steam-roller by the roadside had the name of the village chalked on it as a guide to travelers. We often lost a horse or two at Cléry, which the Germans continued to shell from habit."
*Mechanism:* three ironies in sequence: the pre-war regimental culture dissolved by losses, the colonel's insult returned as an accident of fire, and a road sign made of chalk on a steam-roller.
*Candidate application:* The unit's earlier snobbery gone with those who practiced it (cf. E15b-20), the one-bright-moment rum, and the deadpan "shell from habit" are the closing notes of this stage of the memoir.

## Pattern roll-up

*(What this unit establishes, from one officer-memoir by one narrator; "Graves" below means the narrator as he presents himself, 1915-17 events written up in 1916-29. Finding numbers are in brackets. The memoir offers no theory of humor; its statements about what joking does are occasional, on particular occasions.)*

**Functions.**
- *Cohesion and identity:* regimental custom is the main device and has a harsh side: silence toward new officers, the title "wart," battalion mess, riding school, the patrol as admission test, "chasing the warts" (E15b-18, 19, 20, 21, 27); regimental pride named "the greatest moral force" ahead of patriotism and religion (E15b-80); a boast with fine print ("never lost a trench," E15b-79); leave refused "in the regimental tradition" (E15b-33); the second-battalion culture dissolved by losses (E15b-131) against a "more human" first battalion (E15b-66). Nicknames of roles and refrains: Buzz Off, wart, The Actor, The Boy, Young Jamaica, Scatter, Brains, Tottie Fay, Old Gravy, Mad Jack, von Ranke ("Von Runicke"), Triste la Guerre, 33 Williams (E15b-18, 19, 42, 69, 70, 84, 85, 92, 98, 111). Group song: comic songs against the Welsh battalion's defeatist ones (E15b-39), the officers' choir (E15b-84, 89), men singing until the noise becomes a real signal (E15b-59), a unit identified by its singing (E15b-113, 118), the machine-gun call-and-response (E15b-62). Pooled risk: the platoon sweepstake (E15b-2) and the miners' code (E15b-1).
- *Boundary:* against the Scots, "Jocks" (E15b-43, 108), the Public Schools Battalion and the new-army troops (E15b-50, 106, 108, 115), Special Reserve officers (E15b-60, 96), staff and their orders (E15b-35, 38, 104, 109), the home-front caste (journalists, profiteers, "starred" men, conscientious objectors, ministers, E15b-81; golf-club rich, E15b-126; the "Little Mother" and the old, E15b-122, 123), chaplains (E15b-82), peoples and regions ranked for reliability and cleanliness (E15b-74), French civilians (E15b-58).
- *Coping:* superstition and its disavowal (E15b-12, 87, 89, 100, 105, 110); the "crump" pun (E15b-89); the kipper parcel under shelling (E15b-103); a comic-song loop under fear (E15b-53); the worked-out wound and risk formulas (E15b-22, 23) and the shared belief that the war could not last more than a year (E15b-23); rum and whisky (E15b-46, 54, 64, 131); the cottage and the "contrasted definitions of peace" (E15b-91, 123); amusement at his own lung wound (E15b-112); sleeping through bombardments (E15b-103).
- *Resistance and rank negotiation:* Captain Thomas's mock salutes in London (E15b-26); advice to "look like furniture" and "pretend you don't care" (E15b-18); the deliberately slow journey (E15b-17); "I'll survive you all" (E15b-20); the servant's whisper (E15b-61); Sassoon reading the *London Mail* to a platoon instead of rehearsing (E15b-125); the Canadians' Buckingham Palace jibe (E15b-80); a soldier's flat answer to the press war-cries (E15b-95); Sergeant Townsend's "Mons Angels" and the acting sergeant-major's "It's murder, sir" (E15b-36, 53); Dadd's complaint that success is punished (E15b-99); Choate's meat pie (E15b-49); the laughing conference (E15b-108); the loosened rank of the attack (E15b-52).
- *Discipline (the institution's side):* the staged ambush of a junior explained by the narrator as "honestly intended" training (E15b-27); field punishment named "Crucifixion" by the men (E15b-69); a corporal reduced for being called "Jack" (E15b-71); the drunk storeman and the rum (E15b-40); an accusation of "cold feet" as the force that sends the narrator out (E15b-31); a prank on a man with broken nerves (E15b-11); a recognized drunk hero repeatedly demoted and reinstated in battle (E15b-69).
- *Narrator's own claims about humor and morale:* jokes work on the badly wounded and the dead, not on a slow dying (E15b-3); a "private joke at the expense of the war" (E15b-26); a good battalion does not permit talk of getting wounded (E15b-65); the facetious lecture replaced by candor as "the compliment" he paid the Canadians (E15b-73); "the joke contributed greatly to my recovery" (E15b-120); an officer's job is "to make things easier for the men" (E15b-125); "no patriotism in the trenches" and its talkers "told to cut it out" (E15b-81); in the attack "a greater freedom, as if everyone was drunk together" (E15b-52).

**Targets.** Staff paper, orders and the "usual inappropriate message" (E15b-35, 104, 109); the gas companies and their spanners (E15b-41); generals and colonels in their absence (E15b-28, 38, 99, 108, 121); decorations and the evidence rules for them (E15b-55, 57, 99, 121); the press, propaganda and the "newspaper language" of home (E15b-48, 95, 122); chaplains and miracle stories (E15b-82); home-front rhetoric and the old who send the young (E15b-91, 122, 123, 126); the training establishment and its hatred-drill (E15b-73, 130); regimental snobbery and peacetime survivals (E15b-10, 18, 19, 20, 71); neighboring units (E15b-43, 56, 106); the self (E15b-5, 21, 103, 112, 116, 128); French civilians (E15b-58); the enemy, mostly through courtesy and parody (E15b-29, 32, 62), with the Kaiser a "comic personal devil" (E15b-82) and German officers by hearsay (E15b-25, 97). The dead are never the target in the narrator's own voice; objects stand in for them (E15b-6, 8) and the comrades' gibe at a dead man's arm is reported with an explanation (E15b-1).

**Forms and mechanisms.** The flat closing clause after an anecdote or a death ("Ghosts were numerous in France at the time," "died, but of heart-failure," E15b-12, 43, 47, 50, 107, 131); the end-of-book footnote that reverses an outcome (E15b-9, 20, 47, 57, 114); official language over horror ("accessory," "died of wounds," "British patrols," "energetically repressed local defeatism," and a shell blamed for dead prisoners, E15b-35, 114, 98, 109, 75); mock-ceremony (E15b-5, 8, 26, 37); mock-legal register ("a crime deserving the death-penalty," E15b-40); parody by substitution ("crump" for "trump," a fire-order using a crucifix as a range marker, E15b-89, 82); call-and-response through weapons and across the lines (E15b-32, 62), enemy courtesy notes (E15b-29); callbacks inside the memoir (spanners E15b-41/53; Thomas's "we'll get killed anyhow" E15b-35/50; the colonel's C.B. E15b-99/121; the "rotten crowd" E15b-115/131; the snobs' mess E15b-20/131; touching wood E15b-87); superstition-then-disavowal ("I don't believe in superstition, but...", E15b-87); tall tales told with a swearing guarantee (E15b-77, 127); dialect-based laughter that precedes the facts (E15b-85, documented not endorsed); documents exhibited with no comment (the "Little Mother" pamphlet, the *Times* cutting, the two colonel's letters, E15b-122, 120, 111, 115); a map caption and a kit list as deadpan (E15b-68, 128); a nickname made from a person's single refrain (E15b-84, 88); quoted regimental sayings (E15b-25, 65, 115).

**Rules of license.**
- Junior officers keep silent in the senior mess, and only captains may drink whisky or play the gramophone (E15b-18, 19); the junior's license is a private mutter ("You damned snobs," E15b-20) or a private joke told later.
- The company mess may laugh at staff orders; the men must not be told (E15b-35); a colonel may laugh with his company commanders at the odds (E15b-108); a drunk staff colonel may say the same truth drunk and then "quite seriously" (E15b-38).
- An other-ranks aside at a speech is made to an officer in confidence, not to the commander (E15b-61); an NCO's "feeble, bitter jokes" are tolerated and answered with rum (E15b-46); patriotic talk is silenced by the men (E15b-81); in a good battalion wounds and going home "not permitted to be raised" (E15b-65).
- The wounded may be joked with and the dead disregarded; a slow dying may not (E15b-3).
- Familiarity between ranks is licensed in the extremity of an attack and re-hardens afterward (E15b-52), and is a punishable offense where the colonel is punctilious (E15b-71).
- Atrocity told as a boast is accepted as a boast in the overseas troops (E15b-76); the officers analyzing it in mess do not joke (E15b-75).
- A mocking laugh at a man's accent is licensed among officers even when the man is terrified (E15b-85).

**Limits and failure modes (where the register turns plain).**
- *A slow, helpless death* is the one thing the narrator says cannot be joked over (E15b-3).
- *Named dead* are stated flat and without joke: Beaumont (E15b-2), Samson (E15b-46, 47), Thomas (E15b-50), Richardson, David Thomas and Pritchard (E15b-87, 88), Dadd (E15b-121), Jenkins (fn. [3], E15b-8, 9); the one grief the narrator names as worse than all others in France is David's (E15b-88); weeping is recorded without apology (E15b-121).
- *Fear and breakdown:* Furber's ruined nerves as a butt (E15b-11), the hysterical guide under arrest (E15b-102), 33 Williams laughed at before the count (E15b-85), Phillips's confession (E15b-78), the narrator's plain statement of fear (E15b-46) and his forecast of "a general nervous collapse" (E15b-89), the decline curve (E15b-64), drink as the way out for officers (E15b-54, 64).
- *Killing outside combat:* two spies shot (E15b-7), one man killed by his own officer in each of two attacks (E15b-78), prisoners killed on the way back and told as boasts (E15b-75, 76, 77), a canister crew refused surrender (E15b-86), a civilian's killer acquitted and thanked (E15b-109).
- *Humor that misfires or hurts:* the rat prank (E15b-30), the bomb down Furber's cellar steps (E15b-11), the laugh at "Captain Graves is hit" (E15b-87), the colonel's laughed-at joke about the Public Schools Battalion and the events that follow (E15b-108, 115), the officer-hazers' "warts" game (E15b-18, 27), the storeman (E15b-40).
- *Where translation to civilians fails:* the Beuvry story (E15b-34), the periscope (E15b-101), the father's cheering (E15b-119), Kent (E15b-124), the "foreign language" of newspaper (E15b-122), the lecture to the drafts (E15b-73).

**Time course.**
- *June-September 1915:* the Welsh battalion's nervous chaff and a platoon's gallows rituals (E15b-1 to 11), then the Royal Welch's hazing culture and its courtesy to the enemy (E15b-18 to 32).
- *Loos, 24 September-3 October 1915:* the orders laughed at, then the failure told in dry detail, a man's apology for noise, the loosened rank, the comic-song loop and "It's murder, sir" (E15b-35 to 57).
- *Winter 1915-16 and spring 1916:* habituation ("no horror in the continual experience of death," E15b-62), the decline curve (E15b-64), the choir and the "crump" pun (E15b-89), the touch-wood night (E15b-87, 88) and the narrator's forecast of his own breaking point (E15b-89).
- *July 1916-January 1917:* his own wounding told as a geometry problem and a hospital duet (E15b-110, 112), the false report of his death (E15b-120), the strange home (E15b-119 to 127), the old soldier's kit and a battalion without its old customs (E15b-128, 131).
- *Retrospective devices:* the flash-forward clause and the end-of-book footnotes (E15b-9, 20, 47, 57, 114) mean the fate of most people named is told within a line of their appearance.

**Civilian-facing register.** The Beuvry bomb story answered with "Oh, but that was in France" (E15b-34); the bullet-holed periscope exchanged by his mother (E15b-101); the Waterloo crowd and the cheering father (E15b-119); newspaper language and the "Little Mother" (E15b-122); the Kent mother's "One of the maids has hysterics" (E15b-124); the golf-club waiter (E15b-126); the *Times* notice and the bank (E15b-120); the press war-cries (E15b-95) and the popular book's "flat caps on the left" (E15b-48). The pattern in this unit is that the soldier's account is either rejected as out-of-place, misread as a defect, or answered in the civilian's own idiom.

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** Nothing here is desertion. The relevant material is the soldier-poets' decision that "it was no use making a protest against the war," that the best place for them was back in France protecting the men from "any thoughtless, petty tyrant in an officer's tunic" (E15b-125), and their growing doubt that the war should continue (E15b-123); Sassoon's silent insubordinations (E15b-98, 125); the narrator's own suspected-spy status owing to his German name and family (E15b-96, 110); an officer who tried to go home to kill his wife's lover and was cashiered to a private (E15b-14); the Special Reserve officer as an outsider (E15b-60).

**Cross-cultural / regimental differences.** One British regular regiment, 1915-17; observations on Canadians (E15b-73, 80), Australians (E15b-76), Scots (E15b-43, 108), the Irish (E15b-9, 74), Welsh dialect (E15b-85, 129), colonial troops (E15b-77), French civilians (E15b-58) and Germans: the enemy's notes and megaphone manners (E15b-29, 32), his machine-gun reply (E15b-62), a German officer weeping on a hospital train (E15b-114) and the narrator's remark that Germans were "more ready to live and let live" (E15b-87). All of it is the narrator's and his mess's generalization, not data.

**Craft observations (what the memoir's own method shows).** The narrator places the joke before the loss and the loss in a closing clause; prints documents in full and lets them condemn themselves (E15b-122, 111, 115); marks an anecdote as hearsay or pseudonymous when it is (E15b-78); uses a footnote to overturn his own earlier line (E15b-47); and contrasts two registers inside one chapter (the "crump" pun and the plain collapse forecast, E15b-89; the whistling and the dying man, E15b-3).

**Contrast with the other-ranks voice (Frank Richards, E13a/E13b), where the text here supports it.** *Shared:* the staff, orders and decorations as targets (E13a-14, 22, 12, 77; E13b-1, 11, 10); chaplains with an honored exception (E13a-43, 44; E13b-134; compare E15b-82); contempt for the Public Schools battalion (E13b-2, 50; compare E15b-106, 108, 115); the correct pessimistic forecast of Loos told as truth-telling (E13a-62, 63, 67; compare E15b-35, 36, 50); the wound as a prize (E13a-24, 29; E13b-73; compare E15b-22); nicknames (E13a-17; compare E15b-84 and the list above); courtesy and respect toward the enemy soldier (E13a-9, 31, 54; compare E15b-29, 32); no joke at a named comrade's death and the saddest news in the plainest sentence (E13b-24, 132; compare E15b-47, 50, 88). *Different:* Graves's wit runs through literary and institutional forms (a verse about a dead parrot, a poem's marginal note, a regimental-history page, a Nietzsche line used as a charm, E15b-6, 94, 110, 123), whereas the Richards roll-ups list yarns, curses and sayings; the memoir shows a class system in the same regiment (silence, riding school, "wart") that the other-ranks roll-ups mention only as officers' conduct; and Graves's one explicit rule against raising the question of getting wounded in a good battalion (E15b-65) sits against Richards's other-ranks admiration for the self-wounded (E13b-73), which may reflect rank, battalion or period rather than a contradiction (see the gaps). The enlisted voice in Graves's pages is quoted briefly and mediated.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Range boundaries.** My range begins mid-letter at L4140; L4130-4152 are also in E15a's range (a stiff arm in a trench is E15a-53 there and E15b-1 here). It ends at L8190; L8181-8190 are also E15c's opening (Cléry, E15b-131). I read about ten lines past L8190 for context and used nothing from them.
- **Retrospective, shaped voice, partly second-hand.** The memoir is a 1929 reconstruction; the text says its earlier war chapters rest on a rough 1916 novel draft (L7971-7972). Dialogue is reconstructed; many events are told by others (Hill, Roberts, the brigade-major, the instructors, Sassoon's hearsay feats); the narrator gives a pseudonym once (E15b-78). The Adelphi major's graft (E15b-127), the Turco story (E15b-77) and the Cardonette pantomime are tall tales or hearsay and the text does not verify them. Do not treat any quoted line as a verbatim trench remark.
- **One narrator, one class, one regiment, one army, one phase.** Special Reserve officer, later captain, of the Royal Welch Fusiliers, 1915-January 1917, mostly in the two regular battalions and at base depots. It cannot be generalized to other armies, other ranks, or later years.
- **Humor mostly on the officers' side of the line.** The ranks' wit appears in brief quoted bursts (Townsend, the servant, Baxter's lack of any, Williams, the old soldier at the sermon) and is classified by the narrator; the long set-pieces of wit are officers' (Thomas, The Actor, Moodie, Dadd, Sassoon, the staff colonel, the brigade-major).
- **Tension with Richards, unresolved.** Graves says that in a "really good battalion, as the Second Battalion was when I went to it first," wounds and going home were "not permitted to be raised" (E15b-65), and he privately works out the best way to get wounded (E15b-22); Richards, in the same regiment, reports the old hands admiring self-wounders (E13b-73) and treating the wound as a prize (E13a-24, E13a-29, per the roll-ups). Both may be true of different ranks or dates; the texts here do not reconcile them.
- **Possible overreach in my own inferences.** I flag two: the tie between the brigade-major's praise of "the most beautiful bit of parade-ground drill" and the earlier drill-theory (E15b-113) is mine; and the suggestion that the "Little Mother" exhibit is satire rests on the frame, not on a statement (E15b-122).
- **Missed or minor material.** I skipped technical passages (sniping ascendancy, wiring rules, the trench-mortar and canister mechanics beyond their jokes, the string-and-cartridge method, the battle orders' map details), the Loos battle tactics beyond what bears on humor, and the narrator's descriptions of landscape; I summarized rather than quoted much of the Loos aftermath and the Havre mess atrocity talk.
- **Format and OCR.** The battalion order (L7396-7411) is columnar; text-file typos ("casualities," "Henrv," "Nietsche," "peus," "of if") were not corrected in the text file; I bracketed one in a quotation (E15b-99) and avoided the others. Diamond transcriber marks were dropped.
- **Ethnic, national and religious generalizations** (Scots, Irish, Welsh dialect, Canadians, Australians, colonial troops, French, Belgians, Portuguese, Algerians, Germans, Catholics, the Public Schools men) are in the narrator's and the mess's mouths; documented, not endorsed. Items I summarized rather than quoted are in the register below.
- **Quotations** were checked against the text file only, by an automated comparison of every double-quoted span (British spellings converted to American per the project rule); no printed edition was consulted.
