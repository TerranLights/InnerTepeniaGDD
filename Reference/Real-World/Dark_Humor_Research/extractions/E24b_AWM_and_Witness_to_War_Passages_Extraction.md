## Source-shape notes (read first)

**How the passages were chosen (this limits everything below).** Per the harvest log, the AWM transcripts were found through an outside search engine (about 48 transcript PDFs reached out of thousands AWM holds, because AWM's own collection search is closed to tools), plus the Jane Ross Vietnam series fetched whole (31 items), plus 66 Amplify transcripts. Passages were then cut mechanically around humor words (humor, joke, laugh, funny, prank, nickname, stir, light relief, amusement and similar), with up to three sentences of context each side (at most 350 words). About half of the first "funny" hits were screened out because the word meant "strange" or was a bare chuckle. **So the 55 passages are a keyword-selected set, not a sample of what these veterans said, and nothing here supports a claim about how often humor comes up, or what it never touches.** The harvester also tagged each passage (gallows-humor, humor-as-coping-explained and so on); those tags are the harvester's reading and I do not rely on them, though I note where my own reading differs.

**AWM passages: who is in them.** 55 passages from **39 narrator-sources** (38 individuals and one unnamed group, the Murray Border Veterans Association). By war: **Vietnam 28 passages / 19 narrators**; **Second World War 15 / 11**; **Korea 4 / 3**; **Malaya (Emergency) 3 / 3**; **First World War 2 / 1**; **UN Cambodia 1992 3 / 2**. ## Findings

### A. Forms, topics and targets (mostly AWM)

**[E24b-1]. The comic mishap under danger: the ordinary intrudes on the worst moment.** (AWM-S01927 p.31-32, Spinkston, Vietnam infantry platoon level; AWM-S00499 p.17, Weekes, 1RAR platoon commander; AWM-S02120 p.11-12, Earle, armored captain; AWM-S00820 p.13, Harris, 6RAR private; AWM-S00808 p.8, Butcher, 9RAR private; AWM-S02868 p.15, Col. A.T. Hall, Malaya transport platoon commander)
Six narrators tell the same kind of story, and it is the commonest form of "funny" in the Vietnam and Malaya material. Weekes recalls his batman, in the thick of a night action, loudly demanding a replacement for a personal shelter sheet that had been shot full of holes; Earle's party on a beach under fire watches a patrol boat's passengers all run to one side whenever it turns, and a sergeant announces that bullets are bouncing off his gun shield; Harris, who says "I treated Vietnam as a big joke up until that occasion" (an occasion the passage links to carrying two dead to the landing zone), recalls going to ground under fire while carefully covering a mug of tea with a hand against the falling leaves; Butcher relays, second-hand ("they reckon"), a patrol-night story in which men fled leaving their weapons, a cobra settled over the machine gun, and for two hours the men threw stones at it, silent, because noise would give away their position; Spinkston's party where natural-gas balloons were lit and forty people dived under tables, the narrator first, with a pair of conspicuous trousers remembered ever after; Hall's Malaya convoy ambush, in which an elite SAS soldier's belt was cut by a bullet as he jumped down, his trousers fell, and his unit "rolled around laughing" while Hall's drivers (whom Hall calls better disciplined) went on to attack the ambushers. In every case the comedy is bathos: a mundane grievance, a mundane object, or an embarrassing body event set against fire. The target is the self or one's own side. The enemy is absent as a target, though a lethal threat is present. Two of the six (Spinkston, Hall) have the narrator's own side laughing at someone's loss of composure. Harris's tea is the one story whose own passage places it beside deaths, and Harris marks that occasion as the end of treating Vietnam as a joke (E24b-25); the other five tellings include no death.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's comedy repertoire can run almost entirely on anecdotes of this shape: precise, named, self-implicating, with no enemy and no victim. It is the safest register to show a civilian, which may explain why it surfaces when asked for "funny stories".

**[E24b-2]. Not at the time, but afterwards: the retrospective frame is stated outright.** (AWM-S01927 p.31-32, Spinkston; AWM-S01403 p.10, Jelley, WWII signaller, POW on the Burma-Thailand Railway; AWM-S02868 p.15-16 transcript context, Col. A.T. Hall)
Spinkston says of the gas-balloon night: "That wasn't funny at the time, but afterwards it was," and generalizes that Australian soldiers "can always drag out something that was funny" about even serious situations, "not at the time" but "when they sit down and talk about it afterwards". Jelley, describing a month of enforced exercise to music while emaciated, says "looking back is rather humorous" and notes how absurd it "must have looked". Col. Hall (transcript context, same interview, page 16) says that "later on" his platoon laughed about a soldier who tackled an enemy fighter, after which both sat up, looked at each other and ran in opposite directions; the narrator adds that the soldier was "just as frightened as the young CT". Three different wars and settings give the same time structure: the incident is fear in the moment, a story after.
*Candidate application:* The funny version of a bad moment is built later, by retelling among people who shared it. A character may be able to say exactly when a story became funny.

**[E24b-3]. The Dear John noticeboard: a deliberate, group-run, stated-purpose humor ritual.** (AWM-S00818 p.20, Sayce, 4RAR infantry officer; AWM-S00825 p.28, W. Thompson, sergeant major AATTV/1RAR/4RAR)
Two narrators describe pinning a soldier's breakup letter ("Dear John") on a noticeboard. Sayce, who says rifle companies "very often" adopted this approach for men with problems, describes the aim: instead of letting the man "mull over it" and "become all morbid and sad", he was "encouraged" to post the letter so everyone could read it and "have a good laugh", and the group would tell him how lucky he was to be free of someone who "clearly didn't appreciate him". Sayce's own rationale: it may have saved men from "bottling it up", and showed they were not alone in this. W. Thompson says a lot of such letters would be stuck up in the soldiers' canteen, "the way of saying 'well that's it'", and says that this letter is when "the blokes broke", whereas not getting mail at all was a topic for mere teasing ("you didn't get any mail Harry"). The interviewer's own word is "kill or cure". This is the one custom in the file that is described as a method, by an officer, with a theory of why it works, and the same custom appears in a second narrator's account as ordinary soldiers' practice.
*Candidate application:* A documented unit practice that turns a private hurt into a public laugh on purpose, with an explicit mental-health logic. It could be done to a character, by a character, or be the thing he later recognizes as help.

**[E24b-4]. Mock-danger pranks that borrow the real equipment.** (AWM-S00811 p.5, J. Thompson, sapper, 1 Field Squadron) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
A sapper recalls corps training pranks: harmless switches and detonators, pinched from the training kit, were rigged to doors, beds and lavatories so that they would "just go bang" when used. The narrator calls it "innocent fun", names it a "weird sense of humor", compares it to a civilian-life prank at a veterans' club, and says that even now they would like one "especially on April Fools' Day". The narrator's own gloss on the difference from the real thing is that there was never a real bomb on the end, "as long as your heart can take it". What this establishes: a trade skill (demolitions) is the source of the joke form; the prank simulates the exact danger the trade works with; and the prankster's memory is fond and unbroken into civilian life. The passage's material is a prank built around toilets, which the project's content boundary excludes from design use; it is kept as research only.
*Candidate application:* The people who handle the dangerous thing may make it their favorite joke. A character with a hazardous trade could treat a harmless imitation of it as the funniest thing there is.

**[E24b-5]. Captives' tricks on captors, and the absurdity of the captors' own orders: six of nine prisoner-of-war passages.** (AWM-S00763 p.39 and p.52, Heckendorf, WWII sergeant, POW; AWM-amplify-ReginaldBain_Part1 [1:19:23-1:21:20], Bain, RAAF, POW in Germany; AWM-S03025 p.53-54, Braithwaite, WWII POW; AWM-S01403 p.3 and p.10, Jelley)
There are nine passages from five WWII prisoner narrators (Jelley 3, Braithwaite 2, Heckendorf 2, Bain 1, McPherson 1). In **six** the humor targets the captors or their apparatus: (a) Heckendorf: drivers in a convoy took a fuel tank off a middle truck, sold it and put an empty one back, with a guard riding only in the lead and rear trucks, "for a long time"; and when the Japanese officer searching the camp for a hidden wireless came out, they had "milked" the officer's own car, drawing a mild curse at all Australians while the narrator and interviewer laughed; (b) Heckendorf: after prisoners were issued sticks and ordered to stop escapes, one struck a Japanese inspector at night, the narrator calls it "quite funny", and he escaped punishment by saying he was doing his duty; (c) Bain: prisoners confused roll-call counted in fives by ducking down and running to the other end to be counted twice, "always mucking their parade up", and the Germans "used to take it in fair part"; (d) Braithwaite: ordered to carry unexploded bombs on poles, prisoners staggered toward the guards so the guards' heads "bob down", "a little bit of a joke", while nerves gave way to fatalism; (e) Jelley: an officer prayed that a Japanese plane landing would crash, and it did; (f) Jelley: prisoners made to do dance-type exercise to a Japanese string orchestra "must have looked absolutely ridiculous". The three others are Jelley's statement of humor as survival (E24b-14), Braithwaite's jokes-and-songs in a ship's hold (E24b-12), and McPherson's dog story (E24b-31). Heckendorf says the Japanese "had no idea it was going on" (the fuel trade), and Bain says the Germans tolerated the roll-call games, so the passages show the humor working either as concealed sabotage or as a nuisance the captors chose to absorb.
*Candidate application:* For a character from an environment of surveillance, humor can be a covert practice with a cost if noticed. The jokes that matter are those the supervisors cannot see.
*Cross-use:* Exile and supervised-labor communities.

**[E24b-6]. A nickname made from losses, and the group stopping when it hurt.** (AWM-S01285 p.19-20, Bill Cook, RAAF 460 Squadron ground staff)
Cook says that as aircraft "E Edward" was lost again and again, the man "in charge" of it was nicknamed "Killer Howard" (the passage does not say whether that was a pilot or a ground-crew chief), "till in the end we had to stop it because he started getting down". At a later reunion, when Cook greeted the man by the old name, the reply was that Cook still hadn't "got off that name". The harvester tagged the passage both as nickname and as limits, and it is both: a gallows nickname attached to the person positioned as the source of the losses, noticed as wounding, dropped, and still carried decades later. Cook's own state is relevant too: Cook says "the whole thing got you down in the end", and the interviewer remarks that Cook sounds like someone who could "let things get on your nerves".
*Candidate application:* A group may jointly notice that a nickname has become cruel and drop it, while the name outlives the stopping in the person's mind and the other's habit.

**[E24b-7]. Standing catchphrase and call-and-response, enjoyed without a reason the narrator can give.** (AWM-S01305 p.19, Percy Bird, WWI AIF)
In billets at night someone would shout a stock phrase ("The old squire's been foully murdered") and the others would answer "What? Again?"; another cry was that a named establishment (Young and Jackson's) "has been burnt". Bird, speaking in 1983, calls it "Poor joke I thought it was" and cannot say why it was done. What this establishes is a form: a repeated formula whose point is repetition and a unison reply, not content, from a First World War Australian. The harvester's gallows tag is a reading; the passage gives no sign of what it was a response to.
*Candidate application:* Unit catchphrases may mean nothing outside the repetition, and a long-out veteran may remember the form without remembering the need.

**[E24b-8]. The unit "character," and the sanctioned entertainer.** (AWM-S01927 p.11, Spinkston; AWM-S00825 p.27, W. Thompson; AWM-S00828 p.5-6 plus transcript context, C. Wright, 6RAR/2RAR national-service private)
Spinkston, asked about national servicemen in the platoon, recalls a section commander who "had an incredible sense of humor" and "kept the platoon entertained", and felt "pity" when the man's tour ended because "we couldn't get him to stay". W. Thompson gives the fullest theory in the file: soldiers without "that bit of a larrikin" are "lacking something"; when conditions are hard you need that person "to come out and just sort of get, crack everyone up"; the example is a soldier who stripped and swam in a flooded red-clay pit; "that's the sort of thing that keeps you going", and without it "we'd all be depressed". Thompson adds a diagnostic: you can tell a man has problems because "they get solemn" and stop communicating properly, and then "they'll fail you". C. Wright, a national serviceman, tells jokes and sings bawdy songs; in the transcript context (after the passage) a battalion RSM who watched one performance posted them to the support company with an order to entertain the troops whenever possible, excusing them from guard duty. The humor role is therefore named, recognized, and in this one case formally allotted by a senior NCO.
*Candidate application:* Units have a recognized comic role and notice when its holder goes quiet. A character who was once that person may feel the loss of the role more than anyone around him notices.

**[E24b-9]. Quips at authority: the price depends on place and listener.** (AWM-S02691 p.16-17, Robertson, Korea sniper 3RAR/1RAR; AWM-S00805 p.13, Tarr, 7RAR private; AWM-S00812 p.26, Walker, 8RAR major)
Three narrators show three outcomes. In Korea a colonel told a newly arrived group they were a "filthy rotten lot"; one of "the bright sparks" asked whether that meant they could go home now, and the RSM marched them out, with drill so hard that "it never stopped from there on" (Robertson, who also says the colonel had a nickname the men used). In Vietnam, Tarr says that in the bases personal discipline "was treated as a bit of a joke", with "Yes Sir" and a giggle, while people permanently at Nui Dat "used to get a bit intense about it", and that out on patrol "everyone knew the rules". Walker, an officer, tells of a soldier in the canteen who "was going to try it on a bit" with the line that the only two good lots of soldiers in Vietnam were the Australians and the enemy, who should team up against everyone else; Walker found it "very funny", probably agreed, and says it changed no one's effectiveness: "free of politics". The same genre, a joke at the expense of orders or the war, produced a punishment when aimed at a colonel's face, a base-bound giggle when aimed at routine, and shared laughter in a canteen.
*Candidate application:* License is a function of where, in front of whom, and whether the person answered to rank at that moment. A character can know all three variables without being able to say them.

**[E24b-10]. A superior's folly told with fondness.** (AWM-S01140 p.25, Schuman, SAS then infantry company 2IC, Vietnam; AWM-S01305 p.29-30, Bird)
Schuman tells, laughing three times, of a transport captain at Vung Tau who, in an era when relationships with local women were forbidden, after "a gut full of grog" tried to swim to Australia holding a rifle and a woman; Military Police put the officer in irons, and the officer was sent home. The verdict: "a good officer of transport until he fell in love". Bird tells of a sergeant, at a function with speeches and with "a few in" already, calling out the captain's nickname ("Daphne") in public and then grinning at Bird. The two passages share a tone: a superior mocked without hostility, at a moment licensed by drink, a ceremony or distance.
*Candidate application:* Mockery of brass is not always resistance; here it is closer to affectionate knowledge of a person's failings, which a character who left the service on principle may mix with real resentment.

**[E24b-11]. Banter in the air, and the leash on it.** (AWM-S00546 p.47, Doubleday, RAAF/RAF Bomber Command pilot; AWM-S01297 p.13-14, Firkins, RAAF 460 Squadron; AWM-S00582 p.17, H. Wright, Pathfinder Force; AWM-amplify-AlexanderRobinson_Part2 [29:14-30:37], A. Robinson, 101 Squadron) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
Asked directly whether there was "light hearted banter", Doubleday says yes, giving an example over a heavily defended target: a crewman voiced a fear of what captors would do to them because of their identity, and another told the crewman briskly to sit down because they hadn't "come 500 miles" to be shot down. Doubleday adds that it "was necessary at times to say, cut the chatter, keep your eyes in the sky". Firkins describes pre-flight "smoking and joking and laughing" while there was always "a hell of a knot in the stomach" and the crew were "trying to be terribly brave", the ground crew cheerful and giving thumbs up. H. Wright tells of a crewman who was airsick on 45 of 48 operations and who "used to make a joke of it": a tin tied with a pink ribbon, carried out to the aircraft, used and cleaned each time; the interviewer names this a symbolic thing, and Wright agrees. Robinson says the crew talked about other things but never about how they felt, and "the exception was if it was a funny story".
*Candidate application:* In a crew the humor is real but is bounded by the job; the line is drawn by the one responsible for the others. A self-directed ritual joke can turn a disqualifying weakness into something the person can carry.

**[E24b-12]. Songs, jokes and stories in a crowded ship's hold, and the supply running out.** (AWM-S03025 p.3-4, Braithwaite, WWII POW)
In the hold of a prisoner transport ship, too crowded for everyone to lie down, the narrator says "you'd try to keep a few people occupied by telling jokes and singing songs", which led to people recounting civilian pasts ("country boys were particularly interesting"), and after about ten days "you soon ran out of material". It documents the finite supply: humor and story as a rationed resource under confinement, not an inexhaustible coping tool.
*Candidate application:* Long confinement exhausts the stock of jokes; what is left is repetition or silence.
*Cross-use:* Exile ships, sealed habitats, long waits.

**[E24b-13]. Cross-service, cross-sex "stirring," and the problem of reading intent.** (AWM-amplify-MeganHall [28:26-29:16 and 32:20-33:08], Megan Hall, UN Cambodia 1992, Army signalwoman; AWM-amplify-MichelleStevens [22:46-24:21], Michelle Stevens, UN Cambodia 1992, RAAF aircraftwoman)
On a UN mission, both narrators describe teasing as the working medium between groups. Megan Hall "used to stir up the boys" when they talked to bar hostesses, and was called "mother"; Hall describes colleagues asking the women for help with equipment, "we used to give them a hard time", the men gave the women a hard time if they couldn't lug a generator, and it was "friendly stirring", with "no real digs at anybody". Stevens describes Army-versus-Air-Force jokes ("what would you know, you're in the RAAF"), says "we give as much as we take", and says that with some officers "you can't tell if they're joking and just having a dig", while with their own boss they can tell it is "only in jest". This is not combat humor; it is a peacekeeping posting in 1992 with no danger talk in these passages. It establishes that whether a remark is a joke or an insult depends on a relationship the listener has to be able to read.
*Candidate application:* Banter can be the main way a person who differs from the group is accepted, and the same words from a person one does not know can be indistinguishable from a put-down.

### B. What the veterans say humor did, in their words

**[E24b-14]. Humor as a survival trait and as a leader's skill in captivity.** (AWM-S01403 p.16, Jelley, WWII signaller, POW on the Burma-Thailand Railway; AWM-S02289 p.15, Greville, Korea, 1RAR captain, POW)
Jelley lists what carried them through as a prisoner: small body size (less food needed), long hours (familiar from civilian work), "a sense of humor" inherited from a parent, and a positive outlook; "without having a sense of humor it would have been even more difficult to see those days through". Greville, asked how prisoners bonded and kept morale, credits a senior officer, a Canadian artillery captain who took over when more senior men were removed: "a very quick-witted man with a great sense of humor" who "kept people nicely balanced and together". Two prisoners from two wars place humor on a list of survival qualities, one as inherited temperament, the other as the leader's most useful trait. Both answers respond to broad questions about surviving or morale, not to a humor prompt.
*Candidate application:* In a prisoner or exile group the person who can make others laugh may be the one who holds the group together, and the quality may be spoken of as temperament rather than as a technique.

**[E24b-15]. The stock explanation: pressure and balance.** (AWM-S01927 p.32, Spinkston; AWM-S01143 p.16, Gilbert, Vietnam infantry; AWM-S00808 p.8, Butcher; AWM-S00499 p.17, Weekes)
Asked whether a sense of humor is important, Spinkston says "Absolutely": Australian soldiers "can see something funny in practically everything", and humor "relieves the pressure on what they are doing and allows them to see the funny side of things, and almost enjoy some of the things". Gilbert: "War is a serious business but you can't take it too seriously all the time"; there must be room for humor "when the pressure is not fully on and not fully off". Butcher, after the cobra story: "you always laugh at something, humor always comes out", and, asked if the good parts are remembered better, "You tend to." Weekes lists "the soldiers' good humor" next to an even mix of national servicemen and regulars, no drug incidents and a "well-oiled working machine". Two of the four (Spinkston, Gilbert) are answers to humor prompts and two arise unprompted, so the explanation is stock enough to appear either way; it is also the explanation veterans give when asked, not necessarily the whole of what they know. Gilbert's formula places humor in a middle band of tension.
*Candidate application:* The "relieves the pressure" account is what a veteran would say to an interviewer or a civilian. A character may give that answer fluently and have a more specific, less presentable one.

**[E24b-16]. "So we wouldn't go mad": joking about what one would not normally joke about.** (AWM-S00802 p.5 and p.9, Bourke, 1RAR scout/corporal) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
Bourke says nerves were "probably awful, we played it down", and, asked whether that was so they would not frighten themselves: "So we wouldn't go mad I guess, we joked about things that you wouldn't normally joke about." Asked later how they dealt with what they saw (Bourke's reply to the question is a counter-question, "You tell me how you deal with it?"), Bourke describes mock-greeting jokes between comrades about where the wallet was hidden "'cos if you don't make it back I'm going to snaffle your money", and, to those staying at base, wishing them a hit in the stomach, "joking". Then: "We got very sadistic, I found that we all did, including me", with an example of cruelty to animals that Bourke does not expect the interviewer to find sensible ("It wouldn't make sense to anybody"). In the transcript context Bourke also says they tried to write the experience as a book, a sister read it and "broke down", and Bourke tore it up. The passage does not separate what Bourke calls joking from what Bourke calls sadism, and neither does the interviewer. The humor described is about one's own possible death, a comrade's wounding, or money, not about the enemy.
*Candidate application:* The comrade-to-comrade joke about one's own death has a practical pretext and an intimacy, and a veteran may later be unable to tell it apart from cruelty he was also part of.

**[E24b-17]. Where the release happened: leave towns, bars, and the rear.** (AWM-S00812 p.39-40, Walker, 8RAR major; AWM-S00825 p.28, W. Thompson)
Walker, asked whether rest and convalescence at Vung Tau helped, says that was what it was "all about": letting people "blow out" and "let all that stress somehow go". Walker, then adjutant and working shifts in the command post, describes a day of drinking rum at an American officers' club, being politely asked to stop playing drums on a hotel stage, and conducting about twenty bar hostesses in a sing-along because the narrator "wasn't going to buy them a bloody drink"; they "thought it was a great joke". Walker calls it "pretty harmless", says "I think I'm typical", and names "that need to blow out ... to release the tension somehow". W. Thompson distinguishes where stress sat: field soldiers "got on and did what they were doing" with "really wasn't much time to think", whereas the people in Vung Tau "had more time to sit around and think about things". So the rear is both the place for licensed release and, in Thompson's account, where unprocessed stress accumulates.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may separate *field humor* (task-bound, short) from *leave humor* (drink, performance, excess), and may place the real damage in the second.

**[E24b-18]. Morale seen from above, and the outsider's price of admission.** (AWM-S02860 p.17-18, Morrow, Malaya, 1RAR commanding officer; AWM-S00981 p.14, Osmar White, WWII war correspondent)
Morrow, asked what was a cause for amusement or light relief, answers "I can't bring to mind any odd one", and describes instead what the battalion did for soldiers coming out of ten days in the jungle: hot water for showers, good rations, "like a Christmas dinner", the band turned out. "Little incidents did happen, I can't recall them now, but they would soon be happy"; the interviewer offers "Practical jokes or things like that." (the passage ends). White, asked whether the troops saw correspondents as outsiders, says it was "very often a personality matter": "stuffy" British correspondents with swagger sticks were disliked, while White was accepted "provided you really were prepared to be one of the boys" (with Australian and American troops alike), and the troops had a standing joke that White's arrival meant "bad news" and departure "worse news". The two passages give a commander's view of morale as material comfort plus ceremony, and an outsider's account that a joke aimed at the outsider is the sign of acceptance.
*Candidate application:* An officer may sincerely believe morale was made by showers and a band while the humor was elsewhere, among the soldiers. An outsider (journalist, medic, adviser, exile) is tested by being joked at, and the right response is to take it.

**[E24b-19]. Banter between categories, and the compliment that was not a joke.** (AWM-S00826 p.6, Haines, 6RAR/2RAR corporal, later Provost; AWM-S02873 p.32-33, Mealing, 3RAR mortar NCO)
Haines, speaking in a veterans-association context and about stress, says "there's always the joke between the grunt and the others, there's always the joke between nasho and reg" (national serviceman and regular); "it was a bit of fun"; being told the only reason they were in the army was being "too dumb to get another job" they took as "the banter that goes on between soldiers", not nasty; and Australian servicemen are "renowned for having a really strange sense of humor", such that "you'd have to think that there was something wrong with them". Mealing: at an Anzac Day gathering a former soldier said "Whenever you took a patrol out and asked for volunteers, I always volunteered because I knew I'd always get back"; Mealing could not tell whether that was a compliment ("maybe I didn't go far enough"); the interviewer: "It's pretty amusing when they say what they mean instead of pulling your leg"; "he was full of beer which made it better." In both, leg-pulling is the default register between insiders, a barbed or sincere remark is read twice, and drink licenses the sincere version.
*Candidate application:* Sincerity can be the surprising event in a culture of banter, and a veteran may not know how to answer it.

### C. The interviewer-prompted passages in the AWM Vietnam series, and what they avoid

**[E24b-20]. The map: who was asked about humor, and by what wording.** (AWM-S01927, S01143, S02873, S02860, S02299, S00546; transcript context for S01927, S01143, S02299)
**Six of the 55 passages follow a direct question about humor**, from six narrators. Gary McKay's interviews: Spinkston was asked "Do you think a sense of humor is important?" (and, earlier in the transcript, whether being in hospital was "the funniest event that you can remember"); Gilbert was asked "Were there any funny times that you can remember?", then "Like firing birthday salutes?", then "A sense of humor on operations - where do you put that in the list of priorities?", and says "I saw that on the list of conversation topics", so at least this narrator had the topic list in advance. Colonel David Chinn's interviews (scripted numbered topics): Mealing (Topic 21, "events that were cause for amusement or light relief ... anything particularly humorous?"), Morrow (Topic 20, the same wording, then "Practical jokes or things like that."), Church (Topic 22). In the Morrow, Mealing and Church transcripts the humor topic sits directly before a topic on events and administrative actions affecting morale, so the question is framed as part of morale. Ed Stokes asked Doubleday whether there was "light hearted banter". Of the six, only Spinkston and Gilbert are Vietnam; I treat all six as the prompted set. One further passage (McPherson, AWM-amplify) is a request to tell a named anecdote and is discussed in E24b-31. Weekes's tape, made as comments on a draft battalion history, mentions "the funny incidents" as something recorded "particularly for the book of Coral".
*Candidate application:* Everything veterans say about "humor" in this archive is partly an answer to a scripted prompt such as "amusement or light relief", a phrase that points toward relief anecdotes.

**[E24b-21]. The Jane Ross series: no interviewer asked about humor.** (13 Ross transcripts in the folder: S00802, S00805, S00808, S00810, S00811, S00812, S00813, S00818, S00820, S00823, S00825, S00826, S00828; transcript context)
The harvest log describes the Ross series as one where veterans "are asked open questions and several answer on humor directly". I scanned every interviewer turn (the lines marked J.R) in the 13 transcripts for humor, funny, laugh, joke, amus, light relief and morale. **No interviewer turn asks a narrator about humor.** The turns with those words are laughter markers, one anecdote Ross volunteered about a ration-pack label, one morale question (how a platoon commander handled a man with a morale problem) and one question about whether the Vung Tau relaxation was beneficial. (Limits: a pattern scan plus my own reading; some turns carry spaced-letter extraction artifacts that a word search would miss.) So the Ross passages are all unprompted in the sense that matters: humor arises as an answer to questions about something else. Sayce, asked about morale problems, describes the Dear John noticeboard (E24b-3); Walker, asked about relaxation, the need to "blow out" (E24b-17); Bourke, asked whether they played it down so as not to frighten themselves, says they joked (E24b-16); Burstall, asked what the soldiers thought at the time of a killing, gives the joke (E24b-26); Harris, asked whether the memory made them jumpy, reports having treated Vietnam as a big joke (E24b-25); Butcher, asked what happened on patrols, tells the cobra story (E24b-1); W. Thompson, asked whether men were sent home from the company, gives the larrikin theory (E24b-8).
*Candidate application:* Humor comes up in these interviews as the answer to a question about the hard thing, which is itself information: in these veterans' minds it belongs next to stress and coping.

**[E24b-22]. What the humor-prompted answers contain, and what they avoid.** (AWM-S01927 p.31-32, S01143 p.15-16, S02873 p.32-33, S02860 p.17-18, S02299 p.25-26, S00546 p.47)
Spinkston gives by far the longest answer: a hospital ward of wounded men that was "an incredible funny time" (transcript context), troops in the middle of an assault stripping off for red ants (transcript context), a machine gunner who talked in their sleep and startled a gun crew (transcript context), the gas-balloon night, then "There were a lot of funny things and a lot of serious things". Mealing offers a tortoise carried ten days and then eaten by the Iban, at which "Everybody laughed", and a corporal who won the whole company's money at two-up and was guarded by the mortar platoon from borrowers. Church offers a Christmas Eve officers' party, strong hot buttered rum and no memory of returning to the platoon position, then: "nothing else was really funny, there was not much to laugh at." Morrow remembers none and redirects to showers and the band (E24b-18). Gilbert says "I can't really recall anything of really being funny", rejects the interviewer's "birthday salutes" ("I don't recall it as being funny"), then affirms humor's importance in the abstract (E24b-15). Doubleday's is the one prompted answer with gallows content (E24b-11). **What the answers avoid:** none concerns enemy dead, wounded comrades or civilians as a target; two (Mealing, Church) are about an animal, money or alcohol; two (Gilbert, Church) say outright that there was little or nothing funny; Gilbert's sits right after the narrator's account of a comrade's death.
*Candidate application:* Asked for "a funny story", a veteran may offer a safe one (party, animal, money) and stop; the dark material is available but arrives by another route.

**[E24b-23]. The harder humor arrives in unprompted answers.** (AWM-S00802 p.5, 9; S00810 p.8; S00813 p.22, 27 versus the prompted set; also S01140 p.33-34, Schuman) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
In the sample, passages in which killed people are the object of a joke occur only in unprompted Ross answers: Burstall (a joke made after a civilian woman was shot in a cleared village, E24b-26) and Bell (an exchange of jokes about killing Vietnamese children, E24b-26), both other ranks (a lance corporal and a private); Bourke's comrade jokes and "sadistic" behavior (E24b-16) come from a corporal. None of the six prompted answers, and none of the other eight Vietnam passages (seven McKay, one Weekes), contains such a joke. The one dark item in the McKay group is Schuman's narration of a first kill, told with laughter, ending "welcome to the war" and "First contact", and it is a rueful anecdote, not a joke at the victim's expense. The pattern is interviewer-linked and possibly rank-linked (the Ross narrators behind these items are other ranks, while most of the prompted narrators are officers), but the series differ in question design, the interviewers differ, the counts are tiny (2 of 20 Ross passages), and the selection was by keyword. I report it as an observation, not a finding about rank.
*Candidate application:* What a veteran will say depends on who is asking and what they ask; a character's darkest humor may be reserved for contexts in which the question is about the experience, not about humor.

**[E24b-24]. The audience check: narrators decide in the moment whether the recording can hear it.** (AWM-S00810 p.8, Burstall; AWM-S00802 p.9, Bourke; transcribed laughter across several passages)
Burstall, mid-account of a village clearance, laughs and asks "Is this going to be censored?"; the interviewer offers to "turn it off"; the narrator goes on, frames the remark that follows as an attempt to "convey the attitude that most of us had at that time", and begins "I mean that's a terrible ..." before continuing. Bourke asks "Does that make sense to you?" and then "It wouldn't make sense to anybody, but that's what happened." Narrators' transcribed laughter also marks grim or awkward content: Schuman after the first-kill story ("First contact. (laughs)"), Harris after the tea-mug story, Heckendorf after the petrol and inspector stories, Butcher after the cobra. This is laughter as a delivery tone, not a signal that the content was funny, and it documents the narrators checking and pre-framing their audience before saying a callous thing.
*Candidate application:* A character may check whether the room is safe, and distance himself from the line as he says it ("that's a terrible ..."), and still say it.

### D. Limits: what stopped the humor, what could not be joked about

**[E24b-25]. The "joke" era ends when the real thing arrives.** (AWM-S00820 p.13, Harris, 6RAR private; AWM-S00802 p.2, Bourke; AWM-S02299 p.26, Church, Korea platoon commander; AWM-S01143 p.15, Gilbert)
Harris: "I treated Vietnam as a big joke up until that occasion", an occasion the passage ties to carrying two dead to the landing zone while other soldiers were "howling their eyes out"; on first hearing fire Harris thought "some silly buggers are firing on us, this will all stop in a minute". Bourke volunteered thinking it "a bit of a joke" and "a big joke", flew into a first operation among US troops firing at nothing, stepped on a dead enemy soldier on the trail, and "all of a sudden it just dawned on me this wasn't a game". Here "joke" means unseriousness more than wit; the passages mark the point at which an unserious frame is replaced. In Korea Church's one funny episode is a rear-area party, and after it "nothing else was really funny". Gilbert's refusal to remember funny episodes comes straight after the account of a man killed after sharing plans for after the war. Harris and Bourke each mark the first dead as the end of a lighter register, and neither describes the earlier joking as having been a resource that held.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's early joking posture can be remembered as naivety, and the moment it ended is a fixed point in his story.

**[E24b-26]. Callous humor, and the narrators' own lines about it.** (AWM-S00810 p.8, Burstall; AWM-S00813 p.22 and p.27, Bell, 3 Sqn SAS private; AWM-S00802 p.9, Bourke) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
Burstall, asked what the soldiers thought when a civilian woman was killed in an evacuated and destroyed village, reports that a soldier shouted a sexual quip at the shooter, and says "I mean that's a terrible ... That's the attitude." Ross asks whether it was just verbal; Burstall: "Oh, I think it went right down... we didn't care", then that it "was just a joke", while "the attitude became fairly callous, and as the tour went on, the attitude hardened". Bell (p.27) mentions an exchange of jokes about killing Vietnamese children (not reproduced) and adds "Used to joke about things like that", in a passage whose point is that soldiers who joined civil-aid projects came back changed for the better. Bell (p.22) draws the clearest line: a man in the unit "actually enjoyed killing", and "it wasn't the sort of put on fun thing that some ... the grim humor that some people have and that is a defense mechanism I think". So three narrators place the callous joke on a scale: Bell calls grim humor a defense and sets enjoyment apart; Burstall calls the same register an attitude that hardened; Bourke does not draw a line (E24b-16). None says the callous humor helped.
*Candidate application:* A character can recognize two things as different from the inside: grim humor as armor, and the person for whom it was no longer armor. The line is the narrator's own theory, not a documented fact.

**[E24b-27]. Humor as the permitted channel for what cannot be said straight.** (AWM-S02124 p.49, O'Reilly, 3 Cav APC sergeant; AWM-amplify-AlexanderRobinson_Part2 [29:14-30:37], Robinson; AWM-S00802 p.9, Bourke)
Before the Long Tan fight, O'Reilly and another soldier each dreamed the other was wounded; they told each other, in front of others, in a bantering form ("I dreamt that you got wounded", each said to the other), and the narrator says "we were joking about it, but it was dead serious". The dream came true at Long Tan, and the passage goes on to the dream partner's death. Earlier in the same passage another soldier had died after a letter ended an engagement; "The doctors admit they should have been able to save him." Robinson, a bomber crewman, says the crew never talked about how they felt, and "the exception was if it was a funny story". With Bourke's wallet jokes (E24b-16), these three show a comic or bantering form used to say something about death or fear in a group where saying it straight was not available, with both channels (the joke and the dead seriousness) acknowledged by the narrator.
*Candidate application:* A character may tell the truth only in joke form, and the people closest to him may know to hear the second layer.

**[E24b-28]. "Don't joke about it": a setting that shuts the joke down.** (AWM-S02691 p.86, Robertson, Korea sniper) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
Robertson, with a swollen leg, tells the unit medical orderly "Not one of the dreaded social complaints, I hope", and is told "Don't joke about it" and "we've got to check this out"; Robertson then argues the improbability, and is referred on; the narrator later tells the whole exchange as a comic story. In a medical frame where the thing might be real, the orderly refuses the joke's license, and the narrator, retelling it afterward, regains it.
*Candidate application:* Gallows license lapses where the joke might be diagnostic, and a veteran who keeps joking may be the one who learns that in a clinic.

### E. After service: homecoming and the funny version

**[E24b-29]. Vietnam homecoming: a quip that fails, and veterans' own jokes at each other's expense.** (AWM-S00820 p.28-29, Harris; AWM-S00825 p.29, W. Thompson; AWM-S00826 p.6-7 plus transcript context, Haines; AWM-S00823 p.13, Murray Border Veterans Association)
Harris wore uniform and ribbons into town once; an old man asked if they had been to "Veetnam", Harris replied "No, but I had been to Vietnam" (the narrator laughs in retelling), and the man shouted "murderer" down the street: "So I never wore my uniform again." W. Thompson says Vietnam men met jokes from older veterans ("you blokes were reading magazines, we were filling them in our show"; "Vietnam bun fight"; "There was only one war and that was the big war"), that many "never joined the R.S.L", and, laughing, how Vietnam men spot the old-soldier bluffer who keeps saying "the 9th" without naming a unit. Haines calls the return home for many "the most tragic" part, with "comments made by people, which were pretty nasty at times" (transcript context). The Murray group say it was a "very touchy subject" before and one can say "you served in Vietnam" now without holding one's breath, and tell two jokes about home-front absurdity: call-up papers for national service mailed to a regular already in Vietnam (answered with a request for airfare back), and a letter from the anti-conscription group Save Our Sons claiming the narrator's wife was unfaithful ("I wasn't even married"), with the narrator more struck that the group had worked out the unit and rank from the postal code. In these accounts humor appears in four slots: the failed reflex quip, other veterans' derision, the bluffer-spotting counter-joke, and stories about the absurdity of the home front.
*Candidate application:* A deadpan reflex that works among soldiers can fail instantly on a civilian who has already decided what the speaker is, and the veteran may stop presenting as one.

**[E24b-30]. The funny version in company, the sad version alone.** (AWM-S00823 p.13 transcript context, Murray Border group; AWM-S00808 p.8, Butcher; AWM-S00802 p.5, Bourke)
In the transcript just before the Murray group's two jokes a speaker says "Most guys just think about the funny times"; in a crowd the bad times are not remembered, and they come back when a man is at home, alone, at night, or drinking after a partner has gone to bed upset, and "People can't understand how we felt". Butcher, asked whether the good parts are remembered better than the bad: "You tend to"; after patrols the men had "a few beers" and talked about home more than about what had happened. Bourke: "It's very hard for me to sit here and tell you what happened". These Ross-series narrators describe one split: the shared register is funny stories and home talk, and what is not said in company comes at night. The group's own account is of humor as what the company's memory keeps, not of humor as the problem. (The group's speakers are not individually identified, and the line sits outside the harvested passage.)
*Candidate application:* Public memory is the funny version and private memory is the other, and a veteran's reunion mood and his night-time mood can be the same man.

**[E24b-31]. A captivity story told as comedy, and what stayed after.** (AWM-amplify-DaisyMcPherson_Part1 [29:55-31:18], McPherson, WWII Army nurse, prisoner of the Japanese)
Asked to tell "the story about the dog and the puppies" ("Forgotten about that", the narrator says, laughing), McPherson describes a stray dog arriving in Yokohama and a fellow prisoner saying "now we're going to have some meat"; they "stole everything we possibly could" to fatten it; the dog "got fatter and fatter and we were thrilled"; on the morning it was to be killed it had puppies, "And we give it all this food for the darn puppies!" The comedy is a plan collapsing into reversal, told at the prisoners' own expense. Mid-story the narrator breaks off to say: "when I came home, this is quite true, I was not game to go in a shop on my own because I knew I would steal." A habit learned in captivity is stated inside the laughing story and is not removed by the telling. (The harvester's tag puts the passage under after-service; the afterlife is the narrator's own sentence.)
*Candidate application:* A character may tell survival stories as comedy, and the adaptation the comedy describes may still be live in his behavior.

### F. Differences by war and unit (counts, with their limits)

**[E24b-32]. What each cluster is about, and why the counts are weak evidence.** (AWM-wide; my own classification of the 55 passages)
*Vietnam (28 passages, 19 narrators).* Nine passages contain an explicit statement of what humor does (Spinkston p.31-32, Gilbert, Butcher, W. Thompson p.27, Sayce, Bourke p.5, Walker p.39-40, Haines, Bell p.22), against two in the 27 non-Vietnam passages (Jelley p.16, Greville). That difference tracks interview design (Ross asked about stress, coping and readjustment; most non-Vietnam interviews are life histories) and era (the Vietnam interviews were recorded 1987-99, during public attention to veterans' readjustment); I do not read it as a difference in the wars. The only two passages where killed people are the object of a joke (E24b-23) are Vietnam. Four passages concern homecoming (E24b-29). No Vietnam passage in the file has the enemy as the target of a joke; where the enemy appears it is as source of danger.
*WWII (15 passages, 11 narrators).* Nine passages from five prisoners (Jelley 3, Braithwaite 2, Heckendorf 2, Bain 1, McPherson 1): six aim at captors or their orders (E24b-5). Five passages from five air force narrators (Cook, Doubleday, H. Wright, Firkins, Robinson): all five concern the working life of a bomber squadron (banter and its leash, a ritual object, pre-flight joking, a nickname made from aircraft losses, silence about feelings with funny stories as the exception). One passage is a war correspondent (White).
*Korea (4 passages, 3 narrators).* One about rank (Robertson), one medical (Robertson), one prisoner-leader (Greville), one "not much to laugh at" (Church).
*Malaya (3 passages, 3 narrators).* Two answers to Chinn's amusement topic (Mealing: anecdotes; Morrow: none), and Col. A.T. Hall's ambush story, with a unit-comparison joke inside it (the SAS laughing; the transport platoon "better disciplined").
*WWI (2 passages, 1 narrator).* Both are ritual and nickname forms (E24b-7, E24b-10).
*UN Cambodia 1992 (3 passages, 2 narrators).* Teasing as the working medium between services and sexes (E24b-13); no danger.
Because the selection is keyword-driven and the interviews differ in design, none of these counts can support a claim that one war or branch jokes more, or differently, from another.
*Candidate application:* Use the clusters as a menu of documented forms (prisoner tricks, crew banter, Vietnam stress-and-coping explanations, peacekeeping teasing), not as national or era traits.

### G. Witness to War, Part A: written veteran accounts (U.S., mostly WWII; 21 passages, 19 distinct stretches)

*These are memoirs written years or decades later by U.S. veterans (three Marines by their own text, a B-17 pilot, an Army battalion adviser and others), so they differ from the AWM interviews in genre as well as nationality. Eight of the 14 documents are uncorrected OCR; I quote only phrases that read cleanly.*

**[E24b-33]. Marbaugh (Marines, WWII and Korea; three documents): the unit clown, the recruits' giggles, and banter on a hillside under fire.** (WTW-doc124 p.38-39 and p.14-15; WTW-doc120 p.4-5; WTW-doc129 p.45-46; all OCR) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
At a night-vision school on Samoa, Marbaugh writes that "we had our clown to break the tension and make life more bearable": a lieutenant, tall and gangly with "a perpetual mischievous grin", who could imitate anyone and whose favorite routine was a parody of the President's wartime speeches, which Marbaugh says could still be heard in memory. (The passage then turns to a painful injury to this officer, not reproduced.) On a field problem with exhausted men, an officer screamed "Charge!" and a clown beside Marbaugh answered wearily and sarcastically; Marbaugh "got so weak from laughing I couldn't have charged if I'd tried". At recruit training, tired and hungry recruits murmured a line from the issued leaflet about the first thing they would receive being a good meal; "we all got the giggles", and "That only further infuriated the D.I.'s" (the drill instructors' abusive language in this passage is not reproduced). In Korea, as Marines "marched up firing", "the men joked among themselves", one chanted a rhyme, and the leader, laughing, said "Knock off the skylarking!"; Chinese troops shouted taunts in English and were answered: "I'm coming, just give me time!"; the leader kept talking to the men as bugles sounded. Across three documents the pattern is the same: a named clown role, humor as exhausted resistance to an officer's order, and banter as a reply to the enemy's own shouted jokes.
*Candidate application:* The clown is a position a unit fills, often held by an officer, and comic undercutting of an order is a sign of fatigue, not insubordination. Enemy-shouted taunts answered in kind are a documented exchange form (Marines/Chinese, Korea).

**[E24b-34]. Five authors' own one-line explanations of what joking did.** (WTW-doc11 p.5, Fox; WTW-doc151 p.51, Bramlett, B-17 pilot; WTW-doc88 p.17, Goldsmith; WTW-doc141 p.11, Block; WTW-doc97 p.4-5, Mueller) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
Fox, on an amtrack landing, describes one man rushing out shouting a mock enemy salute and slogan (not reproduced) and comments: "Only an 18 year old Marine in the attack could mix humor with death." Bramlett's B-17 crew, diverted by weather, ate canned rations in eight inches of snow at an unfamiliar field: "It would be hard for anyone to imagine that we sat there laughing and joking and kidding each other, but we did. This was just our way of relieving some of the tension we all felt after a mission." Goldsmith, wet through for days in foxholes: "made weak jokes about it. It was good to be alive even if we were wet and cold," then notes that front-line troops "caught even more hell". Block credits a sergeant's quips on the field telephone, answered "Engine room---Tugboat Annie speaking!", and describes the first shell as a "banty rooster": "his sense of humor was welcomed and often disarmed us of the fright", though "he never knew it". Mueller, on a troopship before it was torpedoed: the men "laughed it off" as superstition, "laughed that off as purely rumor" when submarines were mentioned, and after the explosion stood "talking and joking, trying to keep our wits about us". These five stand next to the AWM statements (E24b-15): "relieving some of the tension", "break the tension", "disarmed us of the fright", from different branches and nationalities, the same tension-release vocabulary. Two details are new: Bramlett knows an outsider will find it hard to imagine, and Block's humorist had no idea of the effect.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may be aware that the joking that steadied a crew sounds strange from outside, and the person who steadied the room may never learn he did.

**[E24b-35]. Manhoff (Marine scout, WWII): jokes about "hardships" until a death, nicknames that meant nothing, an officer's limited link, and VJ-day bad jokes.** (WTW-doc82 p.137, p.108-109, p.108, p.184)
On Okinawa, which at first "had seemed like a training exercise" compared with the last landing, men made jokes about the "hardships" of war; then a well-liked member of the section was killed in broad daylight, which "brought reality back": "It was a black day." Of newly assigned scouts with names like "Killer" Kane (from a comic strip), Manhoff says none of the nicknames "connoted anything" and were drawn from cartoon characters of the time; two or three draftees "took some ragging for being 'involuntary volunteers'", which Manhoff judges "wasn't really fair" since all had volunteered for the Corps after being drafted. On officers: none "should try to be 'one of the boys'" because the military "is not a democracy"; but some "have the knack of establishing a limited personal link with their troops through a joke, a question about home or a girl friend", which strengthens loyalty, without losing "the necessary separateness of command"; competence and looking after subordinates are what count. At the war's end, with no parades available, the Marine officer-candidate group "mostly made bad jokes about 'some guys have all the luck' while celebrating quietly inside our heads" and docked at home with "No bands, no flag-waving".
*Candidate application:* A military memoirist can distinguish a nickname that carries meaning from one that does not, and can say that an officer's best use of humor is a small, bounded personal link, not friendship. Bad jokes can cover relief.

**[E24b-36]. Jamison (U.S. Army battalion adviser to a Vietnamese unit): jokes across a language boundary, and ranks turned into nicknames.** (WTW-doc13 p.10 and p.15-16; WTW-doc15 p.10) *[restrained-handling item: see register]*
Jamison, one of four Americans advising a 450-man Vietnamese battalion, made a poster in mock Vietnamese that, read aloud, sounded like an English question about a body part; passing Vietnamese read it out and then shrugged in bewilderment at "the American Captain rolling around on his bunk giggling like an idiot". Jamison says this humor "embarrassed" a fellow adviser, who "never got used to it". Another sign made by the Americans while they were shelled: "The humor was lost on the Vietnamese." Since saluting and first names were unthinkable for these Regulars who were "excruciatingly aware" of representing the Army, the four used their Vietnamese ranks, which "became, in daily usage, our affectionate nicknames for one another". Separately, Jamison cites an Army "standing joke", "There's always that two percent who never get the word", and applies it to the one enemy fighter who failed to withdraw and was captured (the treatment of the prisoner is not reproduced).
*Candidate application:* A small group embedded in another people's unit builds jokes in the host's language that the hosts cannot parse, and shifts titles into pet names to hold formality and warmth together. The humor marks the group's boundary from its hosts.
*Cross-use:* Advisers, liaison teams, exiles living inside a host community.

**[E24b-37]. Robishaw and Goldner: rank-adjacent pranks, and a fear made public.** (WTW-doc148 p.21, Robishaw, ground crew; WTW-doc30 p.5, Goldner, OCR)
Robishaw tells of a man in another group who, afraid of being sent overseas, told his commanding officer he was not properly trained. The officer proceeded "to correct his training deficiency" by having the man in full field equipment dig a foxhole outside squadron headquarters and dive into it whenever anyone on the office staff blew a whistle; "Other forms of 'training' were invented"; word spread across the base and vehicles were routed past for "a peek"; Robishaw and two companions drove by slowly at lunch and saw a sergeant directing the man crawling toward a coal shed. Robishaw's parenthesis, "(I think we all were, really)", admits everyone shared the fear. Goldner, in a dice game raided by the first sergeant, was named the cheat by the real cheat, who then swapped loaded dice for honest ones by sleight of hand and told the sergeant "These aren't loaded dice"; Goldner, who had seen the switch, was told to carry on. So in one story an officer converts a man's open fear into a base-wide spectacle that lets the audience laugh at what they feel; in the other a trickster fools a sergeant and the narrator is both victim and beneficiary.
*Candidate application:* Public mockery of one man's fear can work as the unit's shared release of its own, with command's collusion. A cheat who saves you after setting you up is an intimate, rank-evading pairing.

**[E24b-38]. Likanchuk (Kilroy compiled story, WWII): a running joke makes a nickname, set beside a casualty.** (WTW-doc45 p.3-4)
The passage reports that a friend was killed on a day the narrator was at the aid station, names the unit's first casualty, then says "All the guys had an ongoing joke, which was, if you are looking for Peter, just look up", after which "they called me ape", because the narrator hung the repair lines in the trees to keep tanks from running over them. The narrator also played a mouth harp, and the guys' "biggest request" was "Knock IT OFF". The humor sits in the same paragraph as the deaths with no comment. The nickname comes from a working habit, not a flaw, and is affectionate teasing; the music is tolerated with a command to stop.
*Candidate application:* A nickname from a work habit is a mark of belonging. The same page can hold a death and a running gag without the writer treating this as odd.

### H. Witness to War, Part B: what the archive's catalogers call humorous (descriptions only, not veterans' words)

**[E24b-39]. What Part B is, and what it can and cannot show.** (WTW-video699 through WTW-video13103; 50 descriptions)
Each entry is the site's own blurb for a video clip, one to four sentences, with the clip's duration (from :48 to 11:01). The narrator never speaks in the text. **Counts:** 26 carry the "Humorous Events" tag (25 also contain a humor word in the description), 24 do not and entered the set because a humor-related word appears; by war, Vietnam 21, WWII 21, Korea 5, Operation Iraqi Freedom 2, Operation Desert Storm 1 (these proportions reflect the harvester's queries and caps, not the archive's composition). The descriptions almost never give the content of a joke. The only quoted "texts" are nicknames and labels: "Wamba", "The Walking Dead", "Cherry Boy" and a crude second label for new co-pilots, "Pops", "Big Stoop", "Slick Arm Chief", "The Bloodhounds", "Chick", "Crash", plus the Australian soldiers' nicknames in 4969, which are not given. Everything else is "funny incident", "prank", "laughs" or a one-clause topic.
*Candidate application:* Part B is a map of where an archive's staff see humor, and a pointer to clips to watch, not evidence of what was said.

**[E24b-40]. The kinds of events the catalogers describe as humorous (my counts; categories overlap).**
- **Pranks and tricks, 15** (699, 1156, 1210, 1278, 1480, 1528, 1929, 5827, 6562, 6979, 6993, 7213, 9767, 11150, 11443). By target, as the one-sentence descriptions allow: comrades or the narrator as victim 4 (699, 1480, 6993, 7213), officers or seniors 3 (1156 the famed Marine "Chesty" Puller, 1528 a new lieutenant, 6979 a platoon leader), newcomers 1 (1210, an "amusing but dangerous trick" by veteran hospital corpsmen on new nurses), enemy or captors 4 (1278 encouraging allies to fire repeated rounds at a lone enemy soldier, "a joke they played while facing serious danger"; 1929 and 9767 prisoners' tricks on guards; 11443 a ruse on enemy fighters), unit mischief 3 (5827, 6562, 11150). **Three of the 15 end badly for someone** (1480, an accidental stabbing; 7213, a hangover; 6993, the prank "got" the narrator).
- **Nicknames, 11** (4969, 5569, 5654, 6198, 7187, 7488, 10284, 11985, 11999, 12837, 12961): from appearance or age ("Chick", "Pops"), from a role ("Crash", the division joker), from a unit's casualty rate ("The Walking Dead"), from how much fire a new co-pilot's aircraft drew, from an initiation question (a "juicer or a head": alcohol or drugs), from a mascot dog made an honorary captain, a guard, a loved Marine.
- **Incidents with a superior or authority, 6** (1036, 1324, 1695 "a funny incident" with a commander or captain, plus 5827 painted helicopters, 7488 a senior chief's rescue of a young chief from becoming "the butt of jokes" by coining "a colorful new term", 11680 an absurd stop-fire order).
- **Danger, the enemy or the dead present at the moment of laughter or irony, 12** (1278, 2308, 4277, 5188, 5978, 6039, 6422, 10119, 10133, 11085, 11421, 11680).
- **Captivity, 6** (1490, 1929, 5569, 9179, 9767, 10119).
- **Generic statements about keeping spirits up, or comic-relief figures, 6** (1074, 4277, 4465, 6562, 7670, 11443).
- **Humor tied to someone later lost, 4** (8832, 9342, 12961, 13103).
- **Homecoming or after-war, 4** (5827, 6039, 7088, 11788).
*Candidate application:* The cataloger's menu is: prank, nickname, a funny moment with a superior, laughter under danger, and humor remembered because the person is gone.

**[E24b-41]. What the descriptions show about the catalogers' view.** (as listed)
(a) *The archive does not separate the comic from the gallows.* The catalogers write "an amazing juxtaposition of humor and horror" (10133: two men laughing while collecting enemy dead, flagged graphic), "a moment of both humor and horror" (5188), a "macabre dance" among camp survivors (10119), a "humorous clip" about betting on soldiers who stole from the dead (2308), "a bit of irony after the war" (6039, killing of prisoners), and a "morbid observation" about berthing at the bottom of a ship (6422). (b) *Humor is attached to people who were lost.* Four entries pair a man's humor with his death or a funny story with a poignant one (8832, 9342, 12961 "a Marine that everybody in the outfit loved for his good humor", 13103 "a good sense of humor" before a fatal escort mission); the pairing echoes Manhoff (E24b-35) and the AWM tributes to humorous comrades (E24b-8). (c) *Limits and costs appear in the descriptions.* Ellis (9179), a prisoner in North Vietnam, is said to have had to wait "three whole months in captivity before they felt comfortable enough to laugh with each other", because "everyone was on edge all the time"; Kiney (1490) recalls German prisoners of war getting annoyed at how lighthearted some American troops acted, so lightness read as insult from the captured side. (d) *Homecoming entries read as ironic.* Rohde (11788), greeted by protesters who "spit on and mocked" the veteran and refused a beer, closes with "Welcome home, Vietnam veteran"; Van Riper (7088) calls the "baby killer" abuse "ironic" and credits remaining on active duty, where Vietnam veterans "talked it out regularly", for the lack of PTSD; Marotte (5827) says "A sense of humor could get you in a little trouble" and that, after avoiding abuse at home, "After thirty years, the men from his unit began to find each other". (e) *A cross-reference to the AWM set.* In 4969 an American describes the Australian soldiers as each having a nickname and being "strange in his own way", an outsider's version of Haines's "really strange sense of humor" (E24b-19); the description says that something happened when an American sat with them that "left Dash and the other Aussies laughing" but gives no content.
*Candidate application:* The recurring pairing of humor with loss and with homecoming reads as the archive's own sense of where humor matters: it is remembered about the people who are gone.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions (in the veterans' own words).**
- Pressure release and balance: the stock explanation from Australians (Spinkston, Gilbert, Butcher, Bourke "so we wouldn't go mad") and, in nearly the same words, from U.S. memoirists (Bramlett "relieving some of the tension", Marbaugh "to break the tension", Block "disarmed us of the fright"). [E24b-14 to -17, -34]
- Cohesion through named roles: the unit character or entertainer (Duggan, the larrikin, the signaller who was posted to entertain, "Fearless Freddie"), and the quick-witted leader who "kept people nicely balanced and together". A man who goes solemn is a warning sign. [E24b-8, -14, -33]
- Deliberate, theory-backed group care: the Dear John noticeboard, described as a method with a stated rationale (prevent bottling up, show he is not alone). [E24b-3]
- A licensed channel for what cannot be said straight: premonition, fear, one's own death. [E24b-16, -27]
- Acceptance and boundary: being joked at is how an outsider is admitted (correspondent, UN women, advisers), and in-group jokes in a language the hosts cannot parse mark the boundary. [E24b-13, -18, -36]
- Covert resistance by the powerless: prisoners' tricks, recruits' giggles, a soldier's quip about the war. [E24b-5, -9, -33]

**Targets.** One's own side and oneself (comic mishap, the single largest form); brass and rank (nicknames, folly stories, parody of the President); captors; peers and newcomers (pranks); other categories of soldier (nasho/reg, Army/RAAF, grunt/rear); danger itself (bathos under fire, banter over a target); the person at the center of losses (a gallows nickname); in two Vietnam passages, killed civilians (restrained). The enemy as a joke target is rare outside the prisoner passages and the Korean shouting exchange. [E24b-1, -5, -6, -9, -10, -26, -33]

**Forms and mechanics.** Bathos anecdote; nickname (from a work habit, a loss, a role, an age, a casualty rate); stock call-and-response; prank built from the real hazardous equipment; mock-formal language ("Yes Sir" and a giggle; ranks as pet names); repartee across the lines; sound-alike puns in a host language; songs and jokes as a finite supply; a personal ritual object (the airsick crewman's ribboned tin); a public ritual (noticeboard). [E24b-1, -4, -6, -7, -12, -33, -36]

**Rules of license.** Place and listener set the price of a quip at authority (colonel versus canteen versus base). Narrators check the audience before saying a callous thing and distance themselves as they say it. A clinic ends the joke ("Don't joke about it"). Alcohol licenses both the sincere remark and the mockery of a superior. An officer's a "limited personal link with their troops through a joke" is good practice and "one of the boys" is not. Operational discipline ends banter ("cut the chatter"). [E24b-9, -10, -18, -19, -24, -28, -35]

**Limits and failure modes.** The "joke" register of an unseasoned soldier ends at the first dead or the first body; a group may drop a nickname that wounds, and the name outlives the dropping; narrators draw their own lines between grim humor as defense and enjoyment, and between a joke and a hardening attitude; stock of jokes runs out under confinement; some narrators cannot recall funny episodes at all or say there was not much to laugh at; the rear and leave towns are where the unprocessed stress sat, in one narrator's account; Part B's catalogers add a prisoner who needed three months before the first shared laugh. [E24b-6, -12, -17, -22, -25, -26]

**Time course.** Funny afterwards and not at the time; the funny version is what the company's memory keeps, and the bad times come alone, at night; tellings years later are comic reconstructions (the dog story, the tea mug); one narrator in 1983 no longer knows why a catchphrase was funny. [E24b-2, -7, -30, -31]

**Civilian-facing register.** A deadpan reflex that works among soldiers fails on a hostile civilian; a U.S. veteran is aware that an outsider may find it hard to imagine; the stock explanation given to interviewers is the presentable one; the Vietnam homecoming produced derision from older veterans as well as strangers; the home-front absurdity (call-up papers to a man already there, a letter from a protest group) became funny stories. [E24b-15, -29, -34]

**Defector, exile, dissent.** No defector material. The nearest analogues: prisoners' covert tricks and sabotage-by-joke; the embedded adviser team with its own jokes unreadable to the hosts; a soldier's quip that the only good soldiers were "us" and the enemy, "free of politics" in the officer's account; veterans' own humor about the anti-war movement and home-front politics. [E24b-5, -9, -29, -36]

**Cross-cultural.** Australian vocabulary and forms are specific (larrikin, stirring, nasho/reg, "take it in fair part", Anzac Day gatherings); the U.S. memoirs share the same tension-release vocabulary, and one U.S. clip description calls the Australians' sense of humor "strange", as an Australian narrator does of himself and his peers. Differences by war are not supported by these counts. [E24b-19, -32, -41]

**Craft observations.** Not present as such. The one methodological caution the material gives a writer: what veterans say about humor is shaped by the question (scripted "amusement or light relief" topics produced tame anecdotes; questions about stress and coping produced the dark material). [E24b-20 to -23]

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Selection bias.** All 76 verbatim passages are keyword cuts; the AWM set reaches about 48 outside-search PDFs, 13 of the 31 Ross transcripts (the other 18 are not in the folder), and 6 Amplify passages. Counts here are counts of passages in the file, not rates among veterans; no claim about frequency or absence follows.
2. **Interviewer effects.** The one set of direct prompts is small (6 passages) and uses scripted wording ("amusement or light relief"; "A sense of humor on operations - where do you put that in the list of priorities?"). Gilbert says the topic list was seen beforehand. Ross's questions were about coping, stress and readjustment, not humor; the interviewers also differ (a historian, Colonel Chinn, McKay).
3. **A correction to the harvest log.** The log says in the Ross series "several answer on humor directly"; my scan of the 13 transcripts' interviewer turns found no interviewer question about humor. The narrators raise it themselves when asked about stress, morale and attitudes. I did not check the 18 Ross transcripts that are not in the folder.
4. **A correction to the brief.** Only 26 of the 50 Part B clips carry the "Humorous Events" tag (catalog flags); 24 are in the set on a humor-word match.
5. **Transcript damage.** Several Ross passages carry spaced-letter extraction artifacts (for example in the Walker, Burstall, W. Thompson and Murray passages); where a quote was affected I paraphrased. Passages begin and end mid-answer. Amplify transcripts are machine-made and volunteer-edited. Eight Witness to War documents are uncorrected OCR (docs 11, 30, 88, 120, 124, 129, 141, 151); stray characters appear in the OCR quotations I used.
6. **Overlaps and double counts.** Two pairs of Part A passages overlap (docs 13 and 82), so the 21 are 19 distinct stretches; my Part B categories overlap by design.
7. **Harvester tags are not independent evidence.** Several tags do not match my reading (for example the WWI call-and-response tagged gallows with no gallows content in the passage; Schuman's first-kill narration tagged humor-about-dead although it is a rueful anecdote).
8. **Retrospective and curated.** Nearly everything is told long after the events (from the UN Cambodia items, which speak in the present tense and were probably recorded during or just after the posting, to more than sixty years for the First World War narrator), often in a veterans' event, book-project or welfare context, and the funny version is what a group's memory preserves (E24b-30). The Murray group are unnamed speakers and the key line sits outside the cut.
9. **No video content.** Part B is descriptions only; whether the clips are comic, gallows or neither cannot be told from the text. The catalogers' phrases ("humor and horror", "ironic") are theirs.
10. **Tensions between sources.** Spinkston says Australian soldiers can find something funny in practically everything; Gilbert, Morrow and Church each say they cannot recall any funny episode. Haines calls the barbs "banter"; Burstall, Bourke and Bell describe the same period's jokes as callous or sadistic. The material does not reconcile these; they may reflect rank, unit, interview setting or temperament.
11. **What could not be answered.** Whether humor changes with years out of service (no narrator speaks as a modern ex-service person); the defector or exile case; the Australian reception of Vietnam veterans beyond five narrators' accounts; any East Timor, Iraq or Afghanistan material (audio only, or the UNSW film archive).
12. **Terms.** AWM items are CC BY-NC with "personal, non-commercial, research and study use"; Witness to War states "All Rights Reserved". This file uses own-words distillation and short quotations only; every quotation should be re-read at its source before it enters a design document.
