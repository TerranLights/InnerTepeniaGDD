## Findings

**[E09b3-1]. A pastoral verse for the transport driver, set on a road that ends at a named danger point.** (PW-01 L22923-22968; Vol. 2 No. 1, August 1917, back half of the issue. L22900-22922 are OCR debris of a page header and ornament; nothing there is readable.) Five stanzas describe a mule-driver with a "gay Hallo," a jaunty cap, the "care of an old-time beau," a hummed tune and "dulcet tones and low." He works through "sylvan glades where the pavé runs," "smooth hard roads," "With never a jolt and never an oath," and goes home to a "rose-decked villa" with angels keeping a vigil over his sleep. The one hard fact set into the idyll is a place name: "Till at Hell Fire Corner he drops his loads." Quotation: "And nothing can ruffle him night or day / As he brings up timber, or food for the guns." Mechanism: the idyll is false by the reader's knowledge (a mule driver who never swears, a villa where a dugout is), and the place name carries the real information without comment. The last line is ambiguous in itself (a sleeping man watched over, or a euphemism for a man who is not going to wake); the text gives no marker, so I do not read it as a death joke. Target: the romanticized picture of the supply driver; the road work the paper's own plain poem "Roads" (E09b2-23) treated seriously. The verse is the transport counterpart of the evening verse that folds air raids into the weather (E09b2-35).
*Candidate application:* a character who tells the supply-line story as a postcard, with the one true noun dropped into it, is a recognizable voice that conveys danger by under-statement of the place and over-statement of the comfort.

**[E09b3-2]. "Regent Street Tunnel": a stage sketch in which two commanding officers hand over a place no one can stand.** (PW-01 L22979-23100.) A scene in a tunnel dugout among a C.O., an Adjutant, a Bombing Officer, an Intelligence Officer and later the incoming C.O. The sequence is: a complaint about the smell and the water rising toward the boots; the tunnel is blown in and floods; the Bombing Officer brings "the nooze" that "the Germans have no shells" and a burst of shelling is heard on the roof; a gas alarm is raised and proves false after five minutes of fumbling respirators ("I can't stand these d----d gas-hats," "My nose is quite sore where the d----d thing pinches"); the relief is arranged "all the guides know their way" and takes fourteen hours (the guides lose A Company and it comes back to try again by daylight); the C.O. asks for port, then whiskey, then learns "only well water" is left and "Collapses." The incoming C.O.'s questions form a litany: "Is it always here?" "Always." "And the water?" "And the water." "Where does one sleep?" "One doesn't sleep." The handover ends in mutual apologies ("I'm really awfully sorry about those rotten guides." "Oh! that's quite all right.") with a "Chorus of 'Cheerioh's!'" and a last aside: "I hope I never see this damned place again." Mechanisms: (a) the false rumor announced and then contradicted by the next sound; (b) the false alarm whose only cost is a sore nose, which is the thing complained of; (c) courtesy as the register of the relief, so that the failure of the process (a whole night lost, a company wandering) is apologized for like a spilled drink; (d) a single punch-line word of collapse for the drink supply. Targets: the guides, the relief system, the tunnel, the gas helmet, the rumor, and the habit of polite English apology. Signs of license: oaths are dashed ("B- ----y," "d----d"); "damned" is printed whole in the closing aside. No casualty occurs on the page, although a tunnel is "blown in" and a company is missing for hours.
*Candidate application:* the handover scene is a good model for how two people from the same institution talk about the unbearable: with logistics and apologies, never with the word for fear. Handing a post to a successor is itself a ritual that permits complaint.

**[E09b3-3]. New serial: a melodrama of rear-area officers, in which the tragedy is a son's joining the Railway Transport Officers.** (PW-01 L23103-23215; "OUR SPLENDID NEW SERIAL. A TALE OF THE GREAT WAR," first installment, "To be Continued.") The cast list is a set of titles and puns: an A.P.M. (the Provost Marshal's assistant) named "Vere de Brett"; a Military Landing Officer "Custance Fitzgerald" and his son "Arthur"; a W.A.A.C. named "Vera Marjorumley" (read from OCR); her maid ("Sheila Popping," OCR "Sueita Poppina"); and a sergeant "Leo Swinger — at the Base" (a pun on "lead-swinger," a shirker, my reading). The installment: the Colonel in "the abominable dugout whence he conducted his arduous duties" refuses his son more money for "riotous living at Wipers," pleading "surely you realize we are at war, and with my life in daily or hourly peril." The son replies "I'm going to join the R.T.O.'s!" and the father's "head fell on his arms, and sobs shook his form." The A.P.M. asks "can nothing be done to stop him?" and the answer is "he has gone for ever." Next day Arthur has "the blue band adorning his hat, and the admiration of an excited populace." The A.P.M., "amidst the shot and shell," pursues his duties in an "evil-smelling, dank hole" and loves a girl who loves the son. Mechanism: serial melodrama in which the stakes of a front-line tragedy (a son lost to the war, a father's sobs) are transferred onto rear-area staff, each of whom claims danger and sacrifice; "has gone for ever" and "the mad course" belong to a boy who joined a staff corps. Targets: the rear-area officers' claim of being in peril; the R.T.O. corps and its blue band; the base. It continues the rum-serial's replacement by a pastiche serial (E09b2 and E09b1-9) and takes up in plot form the staff-versus-line boundary.
*Candidate application:* inverting who is the "hero going to the front" (here the hero goes to a staff corps) is a way a soldier culture jokes about its own prestige hierarchy.

**[E09b3-4]. A leave-return verse fragment.** (PW-01 L23217-23234; the verse's opening is not on the page, and the heading is lost.) Quotation: "Two swift hours in a Pullman car / Aboard and afloat, and you're back again / In the land of the crump and the shooting star." The journey back is "Fifteen hours—rather more than less— / Of discomfort and boredom," and the stanza closes with the soldier guessing "That some of the bloom is off the peach / Of ten days leave." Mechanism: a plain-speaking comic verse whose only figure is the "bloom off the peach" understatement for the end of leave, against a fast sea crossing and a slow, uncomfortable railway. No oath, no target but the railway and the crossing. It places the cost of leave in the return, not in the going (compare the leave-train in E09b2-29 and the "Poile's coupons" gag in E09b2-10). Fragment status means a heading or earlier stanzas could change the reading.

**[E09b3-5]. Two letters to the Editor: the base man who wants a badge for never having been up the line, and the "ratepayers" who protest the night noises.** (PW-01 L23240-23299.) (a) A signed pun-name, "E. Ternal Ledswinger" (read "eternal lead-swinger"), writes in the voice of a man who enlisted at the outbreak of war and "never yet been to the front line"; he calls himself and his like "martyrs" who have endured "soft jobs in England or at the base" with "yearning hearts while others had all the fun of the front-line," says "only our deep sense of duty" has kept them from "breaking our bonds and going up the line," and asks for "some permanent and distinguishing badge." Mechanism: mock-grievance by a shirker who asks a distinction for the absence of exposure; the badge is the mirror image of the wound stripe or the service chevron; the vocabulary is the vocabulary of the sufferer, applied to the safe. Target: base-wallahs and the appetite for decorations. (b) "Indignans," writing for "ratepayers," asks the "city-fathers" to act on the "increasing noisiness of a set of hooligans who operate chiefly during the night hours" and "rely chiefly on fireworks," in "an otherwise peaceful village," and adds the district's "motor buses ... in one unending stream day and night." Mechanism: a parish-council letter in which the night's barrage is a nuisance of local youths and the supply traffic is a nuisance of traffic; the "ratepayer" persona is the one E09a-52 used for the soup letter, and "city-fathers" recurs from E08-19. Target: the civilian genre itself, the shells as hooliganism.
*Candidate application:* civilian-nuisance vocabulary applied to ordnance is a good register for a unit that has lived with noise long enough to complain about it as it would about neighbors.

**[E09b3-6]. "Late News from the Ration Dump": a rumor column whose items contradict each other and the issue.** (PW-01 L23302-23340.) Items, in order: the Germans are short of shells; "The Pope is raising an army to come and stop the war"; "We have the supremacy of the air— ESPECIALLY AT NIGHT"; "The Germans have no guns"; a plan to dig in and wait for China (a national reference I summarize rather than quote); the Kaiser has been arrested by Hindenburg and shot as a spy; "The Kiel Canal is closed to the public, as wheels are being put on the German Fleet to enable them to deal with the tanks," followed by a bracketed editor's rebuttal, "[This must be a canard, as no practical people would consider wheels necessary in Flanders.]"; and a closing "The Germans have no bombs." Mechanisms: (a) the refrain "The Germans have no (shells/guns/bombs)," each printed within an issue where the sketch of E09b3-2 and the night-noise letter of E09b3-5 show otherwise; (b) the peace wish spoken as rumor (the Pope's army); (c) a capitalized caveat that turns an air claim into its opposite; (d) the editor's rebuttal that is itself a joke about mud ("wheels ... in Flanders"). Source of the rumor is the ration dump, a rear point where the supply men meet the line. The column continues E09b1-18 (the rumor column of wishes with a disclaimer) and E09b2-20.
*Candidate application:* a gossip tradition in which a place (the ration dump) is the known source of the wildest unverified good news is a believable mechanism for any group that has to wait.

**[E09b3-7]. "Answers to Correspondents" held over for lack of space, and a correspondence-art-school advertisement with a front-line testimonial.** (PW-01 L23342-23409.) The usual column is replaced by "Owing to lack of space, answers to our numberless correspondents are unavoidably held over." (The column that usually carries the paper's jokes is itself reduced to a copy-shortage gag; the text gives no further explanation.) The ad beneath it, "CAN YOU SKETCH?", runs on puns: "Some of you may be able to draw corks. Very few of you can draw any more money. Probably some of you can draw sketches." The "pupil" testimonial: "The other day by mischance I was left out in No Man's Land. I rapidly drew a picture with a piece of chalk of a tank going into action, and while the Huns were firing at this I succeeded in returning to the trenches unobserved." Then "COULD YOU HAVE DONE THIS?" and an instruction to send "Francs 500" and a "copy of the following on a check," with "my fourteen prospectuses," and "PLEASE SIGN YOUR NAME IN THE BOTTOM RIGHT-HAND CORNER TO PREVENT MISTAKES"; the advertiser is the "Corps Christmas Card Company." Mechanisms: the correspondence-course ad with its inflated testimonial; a decoy (a tank drawn in chalk) that the enemy shoots at while the artist walks home. Target: the mail-order advertising culture; the tank as a novelty thing the enemy will fire at.
*Candidate application:* decoys as miracle-product testimonials are a form in which the speaker's survival story is told as someone else's marketing.

**[E09b3-8]. A trade advertisement for a tunnel and dugout "vacuum cleaning company."** (PW-01 L23412-23441.) Headline: "WHY SUFFER FROM TRENCH FEET? WHY RUN THE RISK OF PILES, DIPHTHERIA & PERI-HEPATITIS. We Can Help You!!!" It guarantees "a well-drained, aired and ventilated Tunnel or Dug-out at reasonable cost," says "WE are an entirely new firm and no tunnelers or R.E. are employed," offers an "Illustrated booklet" for "six stamps" (cheap, though "free" in the lead), "WE SPARE NO EXPENSE," ending in a couplet, "For a tunnel cool and sweet, / Write to us we're hard to beat," with telegraphic address "HEDGE ST." Mechanism: the service-firm ad; the selling point is the absence of the engineers who built the tunnels; the list of ailments is the list of what the reader has. Target: tunnelers and Royal Engineers (the tunnels' builders and drainers), the drainage failure; the company works for the same wet place as the sketch of E09b3-2. The same device as the property and services ads for the Salient in E08-5 and E08-35.

**[E09b3-9]. An economy poster for water, aimed at the Staff's cars and the soldier's thirst.** (PW-01 L23452-23490.) "HAS IT EVER STRUCK YOU ??? That water is a necessity, not only for the radiators of the Staff's cars, but for lots of other things. Therefore ECONOMIZE in it. There is no need for you to drink it." The ad then points to "Mr. George Barrie," who "will not PROVIDE YOU with a BOOKLET (or anything else) FREE. BUT—He CAN provide you with the correct and PATRIOTIC drinks," with a couplet "Don't do anything that's risky, / Mix your water with some whiskey," and addresses "Alcoholic" and "No. XXX Hop." (The telephone code "Hop" is unresolved.) Mechanism: parody of a wartime economy poster, with a priority list (the Staff's car radiators before the soldier's drink) and a patriotic turn that makes alcohol the correct substitute. Target: Staff cars; water shortage (set in the same issue as the tunnel's "only well water left"); a purveyor of drink. Continues the whiskey/water running joke of E09a-20, E09a-38 and E09b2-27 ("whiskey in the water bottle").

**[E09b3-10]. New masthead, Vol. 2 No. 2 (Saturday, September 8, 1917), and the Cloth Hall revue bill that puns on a retreat of an ally.** (PW-01 L23495-23533.) The masthead repeats the roll of absorbed titles (the Wipers Times, the "New Church" Times, the Kemmel Times and the Somme-Times) and the price of one franc (E09b2-13). The bill: "CLOTH HALL, WIPERS. (Lifting Roof and Perfect Ventilation.)" for a "Grand New Revue Entitled: 'GOOD-BYE-EE-E, WE MOS-COW,'" "INTRODUCING SELECTIONS FROM 'RIGA Le T GO.' INCLUDING THE ENTIRE 'BALLY RUSSE,'" closing "We hope to be able to announce a Return Visit of this Celebrated Company very shortly." Mechanisms: (a) the ruined hall's missing roof recast as a feature ("Lifting Roof and Perfect Ventilation"), a continuation of E08-5; (b) puns on the Russian ally's collapse (the German capture of Riga in early September 1917 is my gloss; the text does not explain) that make the retreat a farewell performance, with the hope of a "Return Visit"; (c) "Prices as usual, book early" as the stable ending of the paper's bills. Target: the Russian ally's retreat; the ruins; no soldier or officer is named.
*Candidate application:* an allied collapse treated in the soldiers' paper as a theatrical exit with a hoped-for return shows how gallows humor can mourn an ally without saying so.

**[E09b3-11]. "Second Autumn Polygon Race Meeting": the offensive as a race card with the enemy as prize.** (PW-01 L23536-23565.) Events "ZERO STAKES," "THE VON ARNIM PLATE," a "HINDENBERG POINT-TO-[POINT]" (the end of the word is OCR damage), the "GHELUVELT GOBLET," and the "CROWN PRINCE'S PLATE." "STARTERS AND CLERKS OF THE COURSE: Messrs. PLUMER AND GOUGH." Also (the first word of the first line is damaged in the OCR and "entrance" is my reading): "[ENTRANCE] TO BARBED WIRE ENCLOSURE FREE TO ALL TEUTONS," and "EXCELLENT TRAIN SERVICE. TRAINS NOW RUN RIGHT UP TO THE COURSE," with the secretaries "KEW & CO." Mechanisms: the sporting register for battle (E09b1-29, E09b2-25 and E08-15); the barbed-wire enclosure as the prisoners' cage with a free entry; two real army commanders named, without barb, as the officials who start the race; the railway's reach to the course is the one practical fact. Targets: the offensive; the enemy commanders as race names; the prisoner's cage. A pairing with the revue bill above: allied retreat on one side of the page, a boast of advance on the other.
*Candidate application:* naming the commanders as starters, not as butts, shows what a unit can allow itself with a general it trusts.

**[E09b3-12]. A traveling cinema's bill: the paper's running characters and a joke on its own censor.** (PW-01 L23568-23593.) "TODD'S TRAVELING Kinematograph, Ltd," with "PERFORMANCES NIGHTLY (If the engine will go.)"; this week "REGGIE VISITS PARIS," "A SCREAMING FARCE FEATURING THIS Serio-Comic IN A New ROLE (PARTLY CENSORED.)"; also "Porky Plays Poker. A TRAGEDY." and "MANY OTHER THRILLING FILMS." Mechanism: a cinema bill; the in-joke characters (Reggie from E09b2; "Porky" is new to me in this series, I cannot place him) appear as film titles; "PARTLY CENSORED" is a joke about the censor, one of the paper's few direct ones in this range, and its object is the content of a man's leave. "A TRAGEDY" labels a card game. Target: the censor; the entertainers; the engine of a field cinema.

**[E09b3-13]. "Western Advance Co., Hooge": a moneylender's advertisement in which the Commander-in-Chief is "General Manager."** (PW-01 L23605-23631.) "[D. HAIG, GENERAL MANAGER.] Makes ADVANCES at SHORT NOTICE UNDER PRIVATE ARRANGEMENT. NO SECURITY. SECRECY GUARANTEED. PRINCIPAL REMAINING TILL END OF WAR. AGENTS IN ALL CIVILIZED COUNTRIES. Wires—CUT. Phone—1918. Code—Bab." Mechanism: the double sense of "advance" (a loan; a territorial advance); "no security" and "secrecy guaranteed" are both the lender's promise and the army's practice; "Principal remaining till end of war" is the bleakest line (the capital stays in place); "Wires—CUT" is the barbed-wire task; "Phone—1918" names the year the writers expect the war to be still on. Target: the Commander-in-Chief, named in a plain bracket; the offensive's returns. Compare E09b1-15 (the same C-in-C teased through a pun on his name, "Dean Haig") and E08's rule of teasing seniors by type: here the name is printed plainly in a role bracket and the barb is on the offensive, not the man. (A search of the text, done only to check this point, finds "Haig's Company" in the August theater bill at L21707, so naming him is not new in this range.)
*Candidate application:* a paper that names the supreme commander but aims the joke at the arithmetic of the offensive shows a distinction soldiers draw between the man and the plan.

**[E09b3-14]. The September 8 editorial: winter dread in the form of a Browning pastiche, a jab at the home press, a welcome for the women's corps, and a plea for copy.** (PW-01 L23641-23709; Vol. 2 No. 2, 8 September 1917, signed "THE EDITOR.") The motto is "Oh, to be in Belgium, / Now that Winter's here!" (a turn on a well-known English spring poem, my gloss). The body: "Before this appears in print it's more than likely that the weather will have gone round to May, but certainly at present it seems that Summer is a thing of the past," followed by a dread of winter before "we've had time to dig up our thick underclothing from the spare kit dump," then "Brisk exhilarating walks over the snow-clad plains of Belgium. Oh, my sainted aunt!" The editors turn on the home papers: "Most of our English contemporaries seem to fill their columns with reports of the doings of some people called Ramsay Macdonald, Grayson and others" (two peace-leaning politicians, my gloss), and say "to chronicle the doings of a few nonentities seems to argue a lack of knowledge of what the people want to read about. However these penny dailies are generally very slow in feeling the public pulse." Then: "The whole B.E.F. will welcome with open arms the latest recruits to its ranks in the shape of the W.A.A.C. ... the morale of the troops has gone up 100 per cent. (so has the candidature for a spell at the base.)" and "We must again remind everyone that copy is necessary." Mechanisms: the print-lag joke (the weather will have changed by publication); the pun on the lack of "the people" the home press mistakes; the arithmetic of morale (a statistic that is a joke about desire for a safe posting); "morale" appears as the editors' own word, in a joke, not in a program. Target: the home press and its politicians; the weather; the women's corps (as an object of the troops' attention, handled lightly, see E09b3-25).
*Candidate application:* a unit's own newspaper is where "the people want to read about" gets contested against the home press; the editorial voice rejects the civilian's picture of what is news.

**[E09b3-15]. A ballad of the ration boat on the flooded ground.** (PW-01 L23715-23806; the heading is damaged in the OCR and reads like "If the rain and the war ... last"; unsigned.) A narrative ballad in rhymed couplets: "The Summer had been long and cold, / And Intha Pink was growing old." A sodden man ("Atkins of the P.B.I.") brings "the news that Hooge was dry" and a steering correction, without which "They'd get no rations up that night." The vessel "Aunt Sally" arrives, the rations are "stowed inside," they "steamed along at full six knots," then "that sickening scrape" strands them: "Her bottom's out!" a tin of biscuits is used to stem the leak, they row away on biscuit tins with "pick-axe helves." The reason is in the closing couplet: "The waters, tired of rising higher, / Uncovered the Cathedral spire! / Uncharted, it had caused their plight, / No rations reached the line that night." Mechanism: the flooded battlefield rendered as sea, with a nautical register (knots, charts, "hove in sight"); a recurring last line ("They'd get no rations up that night") that reverses at the end ("No rations reached"); the Cathedral spire (the paper's old running object, E08-10) recast as an uncharted rock. Target: mud and water and the supply chain. No casualty occurs (the sailors "dodged the shells, ignored the shots").
*Candidate application:* when terrain turns hostile, a unit's humor may remap it as another environment (here sea); that remap lets the supply failure be told as an adventure with a happy-sad last line.

**[E09b3-16]. "Jim": an affectionate verse for a trench terrier (plain register).** (PW-01 L23809-23834; signed R.M.O.) Three stanzas: "A hard little, scarred little terrier, / With a touch of the sheep-dog thrown in— / A mongrel—no matter, / There's no better ratter / In trenches or billet, than Jim." Second stanza: "A tough little, rough little beggar; / And merry, the eyes of him. / But no Tartar or Turk / Can do dirtier work / With an enemy rat, than Jim." Third: at nightfall the dog nuzzles "in my coat" and growls "in your dreams." Mechanism: a sincere pet poem with one martial joke (the "enemy rat," the "Tartar or Turk" comparison, a period national figure of fierceness). No irony; the only mark of the surroundings is "trenches or billet" and the nightfall. It is a plain-register item placed in the comic pages (cf. E09a-14, E09b2-23). The same initials sign "Seen from an Aid-Post" (E09b3-29); I do not identify the author.
*Candidate application:* the animal as a warm presence in an unwarm place is a way a character's tenderness can be shown without any speech about feeling.

**[E09b3-17]. Teech Bomas on "How the Men of Blankshire Baffled the Berliner."** (PW-01 L23840-23894.) The special correspondent's piece on a trench raid or small attack, in heavy alliteration (Blankshire, Berliner, baffled, bilked, bayonets, Berliners battled bravely) with the standard secrecy and permission tics: "I am allowed to chronicle the epic deeds of the men from Blankshire," "The scene of this historic encounter was— well I mustn't tell you that," "as described in my last." The men "came kicking footballs" (a reference to the football-kicking attack story from the Somme, my gloss) and "completely puzzled the Potsdammers"; a count of "600 burly Berliners" fills the cages; the enemy "kamerading" (the shout of surrender). Then a pastoral closing in which field mice resume their lives, larks sing and flowers bloom. The summary sentence is the one that matters: "the baffled Berliners battling the Blankshires is but typical of the events which, strung together, make the big offensive." Mechanisms: alliteration as noise that hides content; the permitted-secrecy tic; the heroic-scale count; the pastoral aftermath. **One paragraph, which sets the enemy's dead in the pastoral scene and implies that men slow to surrender were killed, is not reproduced (restrained handling; see the register).** Target: the war correspondent; the habit of turning a small raid into the whole offensive; the football legend. Continues E09b1-20, E09b2-22 and E08-78.

**[E09b3-18]. "What a Hope!": a leave application in verse with a transparent excuse.** (PW-01 L23903-23937.) A Captain, M.C., writes to a Colonel in verse to ask the G.O.C. for leave: "For four long months I've not seen Blighty." The excuse is a "wealthy aunt" "Who's lying on a bed of pain; / And never will get up again, / At least the doctors say that's so, / And so I think I ought to go." He adds the bank ("The same applies to Messrs. Cox") and a promise that when leave is "hoped-for" it will be had, his "work will please the C.R.E., / As well as charm the G.O.C.," and signs "Your humble servant, O.C. 'D.'" The title is a British phrase meaning "no chance." Mechanism: the official letter form with a transparently motivated excuse, the aunt's expected death welcomed as a legacy, the honorific close; "hoped-for" in quotation marks signals the writer's own disbelief. Target: the leave system's reliance on pretexts; the bank (Cox & Co., E08-76). Compare the depot verse where the lie is a dead wife and the man is "crimed" (E09b3-28).

**[E09b3-19]. A short verse about an observation balloon and a shot airman.** (PW-01 L23940-23950; unsigned.) Eight lines: the "sausage" balloon is "a high one," the enemy shoots at it, airmen "swooped down on that Hun," and the last couplet is a euphemism for his death. Mechanism: nursery-meter doggerel with a stock German exclamation and a euphemism for the enemy airman's fate ("where all Fritzes go"); I quote only the opening line, "The sausage was a high one," and describe the rest. Target: the enemy's soldier. This is the plain enemy-as-target type (E09b2-25) and carries no regret.

**[E09b3-20]. "Excelsior": a rum-jar parody of the Longfellow poem, where the sergeant is the casualty.** (PW-01 L23956-24060.) Stanzas end "Excelsior!" at first, then with refrains of "So leave it 'ere," "So go no more," "'E'll 'it the jar." A youth carries "a mighty gallon jar of rum" up a "muddy C.T." in a barrage; a sergeant, then a corporal, then a private urge him to stop. The corporal's warning is the sharpest: "Another who the Hun defied / He got a bullet through the 'tum,' / And broke his blooming jar of rum, / So go no more." The youth replies "I must get there whate'er betide, / Though Hell may storm both near and far / I'll get there with this needed jar," reaches the company H.Q. "And to the precious jar he's true— / He still it bore." Then the sergeant "raging stormed" and "He told that youth who proudly bore / The jar through all! He told him more / And more and more," until "He paused for breath, he gasped and died, / And dying fell he down beside / An empty jar." Mechanisms: (a) a famous poem's structure (the warning figures, the youth's refusal, the death at the end) used for a rum run; (b) the earlier fatality is reported in one clause in the middle of the rhyme, with the broken jar as the loss that counts; (c) the reveal is that the jar is empty and that the one who dies is the man who berates; the text does not say how the jar came to be empty. Target: the rum ration and the heroics around it; the sergeant's tirade. Compare E09b2-4 (rum-jar song), E08-20, E08-55 and E09a-40; here the jar, not the youth, is the thing risked.
*Candidate application:* the whole economy of risk in a unit can be told through a ration; the story's sting is that the one who dies is the loudest person in the room.

**[E09b3-21]. A farewell to one of "the few remaining Original members of the Division."** (PW-01 L24063-24081; the heading is damaged in the OCR ("6 VET") and the person's name is lost.) A plain paragraph: "It is with the greatest regret that we have to say 'Good-bye' to one of the few remaining Original members of the Division. This loss will be deeply felt by those who have served with him for so long a time, and who have known that his never-failing kindness and support were available at any moment. We wish him 'Good Luck' wherever he may go, and ask him to accept our gratitude for the help and encouragement he has given us in many difficult moments." No device; the single fact worth marking is "few remaining Original members" (the Division's wastage stated as a count of who is left). Continues E09b2-7, E09a-18, E09a-27 and E09b1-11.

**[E09b3-22]. "Our Diary, by Lieut. Samuel Pepys": a seventeenth-century diarist's voice for the 1917 line, with fear as "the megrims" and grief as the price of sardines.** (PW-01 L24087-24135.) The entries run in one day: arrival at new billets "feeling very fatigued and with a touch of the megrims"; "One tells us that the Huns have a practice of shooting in the surroundings, so that we are all of a windiness"; "Orders have come ... that we shall depart from here very soon and go down to the activities. We have all got the megrims"; "Woodbines are up one penny on the packet, which does perturb us"; a rumor "that the R.T.O. of Bath has not got a decoration in the new list. This is not understandable, and savours of a canard"; a party must "reconnoitre the field of battle. This is a most unlikeable business, and I have the megrims worse, so that the [leech] has given me a potion" (OCR reads "teech"); "Sardines are up two pennies the tin, which is a scandal, it occurs to me that there may be much profits accruing to some persons." The letter from "Anne" reports an air raid on London: "the enemy has appeared in some balloon or ship which flies, and has thrown exploding missiles at London. This has disturbed me much, as the taxes will not allow of the provision of new clothes for Anne if she should spoil her present ones." And: "There is a story that no rum issue will be made this night. We all have the megrims, and whiskey is up 2 francs on the bottle. This is a terrible war." The last entries: a new "gas-bag" "of a poor pattern" and envy of Captain Spanker's better one, then "bath parade," "to bed at 9 of the clock, tired and with the megrims." Mechanisms: the archaic vocabulary (megrims, windiness, "writ") carries the fear and the dread of going up; the price rises and the missed decoration are felt in the same register as the order to go to the line; the air raid at home is converted to a clothing-cost worry; "This is a terrible war" is attached to the price of whiskey; the gas helmet is a status object ("envious of that of Captain Spanker"). Target: profiteers ("much profits accruing to some persons"), the decoration lists, the rum issue, and the diarist himself. A strong example of fear told without a fear word.
*Candidate application:* an archaic or formal voice is a vehicle for admitting fear in comic disguise ("a touch of the megrims" as the unit's word for dread); for a character it supplies a euphemism system.

**[E09b3-23]. "A few more Military Terms Defined" (continuing the mock dictionary of E09b2-31).** (PW-01 L24138-24167.) Three entries. "R.T.O.—Rude to officers. A pleasing brusqueness marks their manner when dealing with officers who have the impertinence to come away from the line for a spot of leave. This is affected to emphasize the strain of their position." "E.F.C.—Every franc counts. Derived from the thrifty methods of those A men employed therein, who evidently think that miss not, want not—after the war." (The "A men" are read, tentatively, as fit men kept in canteen work; the OCR line is damaged.) "DUDS.—These are of two kinds. A shell on impact failing to explode is called a dud. They are unhappily not as plentiful as the other kind, which often draws a big salary and explodes for no reason. These are plentiful away from the fighting area." Mechanisms: acronym expansion as insult (the same initials as the earlier serial's tragedy, E09b3-3); "unhappily" for the scarcity of dud shells; an equivocation on "dud" (a failed shell; a failed person) that sets the rear-area high-salary officer as the more dangerous kind. Target: R.T.O.s, the canteen organization, and the paid rear-area officer.

**[E09b3-24]. "With apologies to Rudyard Kipling": three drinking-and-leave maxims in the ranks' voice, with the explicit rule against thinking.** (PW-01 L24170-24200; the heading is OCR-damaged but the name Kipling and "apologies" are legible.) The rhymed instructions (each ending on a line about "a soldier"): "When you're waiting for zero, to go o'er the top, / And yer mind gets a-wondering what you will stop, / Just go to yer bottle, and neck a wee drop, / Cos thinkin' ain't good for a soldier." Then: under a barrage "Don't wander round looking for dugouts and so, / Just flop where you happen to be, don't you know / Any 'ole's good enough for a soldier"; and "When yer click for a leave, and yer warrant's come through, / Don't waste any time thinking what you will do, / Just grab up yer pack, leg it quick fer Berloo, / Any leave's good enough for a soldier." (OCR gives "goof" for "good" in the second; repaired.) Mechanism: a Kipling soldier-poem structure (my gloss: "The Young British Soldier," a list of "When ... don't ..." instructions to a recruit, the text itself does not say which poem) filled with cheerful advice that is, in fact, a statement of the practice: drink at zero hour, lie flat anywhere in a barrage, take any leave. The one phrase that names the function is "what you will stop," the euphemism for being hit, and "thinkin' ain't good for a soldier" is the plain rule that humor, drink and doing enforce together. This is the second explicit statement in this source that drink replaces thought in the face of danger (E09a-48, "An Appeal").
*Candidate application:* a veteran character who gives instruction in the form of rules for the new man ("don't think, take what you get") has a documented ancestor in this form.

**[E09b3-25]. "Anne, the Chemise Corporal": an officer's imagining of what the women's army will bring to the line (restrained handling).** (PW-01 L24213-24357; signed "N. L."; subtitle: "An anticipation of what may be when the woman's army is in full swing.") A comic story in the officers' register about a Women's Auxiliary Army Corps corporal, a man driver of a supply wagon bringing the laundry, and a stores tent, kept by an "Undies Sergeant," whose stock of beauty goods and clothing is listed as ready "to hand over as Trench Stores." The driver, who is "quite the little gentleman" and once worked for a London department store, is taken to tea and shown the stock. A passage about a relieving division from Central Africa and its quartermaster treats that division in a caricatured way (not reproduced; see the register). The narrator pretends to be outraged that "no army order" forbids a woman of the corps to speak to a man, and promises to go to G.H.Q. "to rake up the name of the cold hearted monster responsible for this order." The couple's courtship uses leave warrants (one in a color said to be for the man and one in another for the woman), a quick marriage with a joke about separation allowances, and a decorating joke about "the complete equality of the sexes." The narrator's last twist: a baby is the corps' contribution, found on a food advertisement ("So Anne did something in the Great War, didn't she? What?") and the closer "BLESS HER!" Mechanisms: the fashion-page register applied to supply and stores; a story that uses the bureaucratic forms (orders, warrants, allowances) as the plot; the end revealed through an advertisement. Target: the women's corps as an object of the troops' interest, the army's regulations, the stores system, and colonial troops (caricature). The piece is mild in content and the humor depends on gendered and national stereotypes of its time; I treat it as a document of that, not as a model. Compare the editorial welcome of the W.A.A.C. (E09b3-14), the serial's W.A.A.C. (E09b3-3) and the fashion columnist's earlier appearances (E09a-59).

**[E09b3-26]. "To a Marraine": a sonnet to a wartime correspondent-godmother, with an ambiguous last line.** (PW-01 L24358-24380; signed C.L.F.) The sonnet's conceit: "We are two bubbles in a glass of wine, / Smiling to one another, I and you," hoping "we should meet, some day, perhaps to dine." The sestet turns to fragility: "We are two bubbles, floating in Life's glass, / Transparent, joyous, fragile, delicate," and "to-morrow" a "cruel lip of Fate" may "burst this fairy bubble of my dream," with "unshaven" before "cruel lip" as the single odd modifier. The marraine custom (a French woman who corresponds with a soldier; the term is French for "godmother") is the unexplained context. I cannot tell whether the "unshaven" is a deliberate bathos or simply a sincere image; the poem has no marker of mockery. A plain-register entry among the comic ones, expressing contingency (tomorrow may end it) through a glass-of-wine figure, in the register of a young officer's romantic verse.

**[E09b3-27]. "As Of Old": the David and Uriah story retold in scriptural pastiche as a captain's scheme against a lieutenant (restrained handling).** (PW-01 L24389-24450.) The pastiche begins "It came to pass in the year one thousand nine hundred and seventeen" and gives the cast as "Davin, a captain of the Hittiteshire Regiment" and "Lieut. Urian" (the regiment's name is the pun); leave is "a yellow parchment," Blighty "the land of Berlighty," the senior command is "certain elders" who "became perturbed," the War Office appears in the captain's threat of "judgment," a trench raid is "a citadel in the land of Noman" and the rations are "a mess of Makonokie and Rhum." The captain on leave covets the wife of a subordinate he has seen bathing; refused, he arranges to have the lieutenant posted to his command and orders him to "take the forefront of the battle"; "Urian did fill himself with Rhum, and likewise his platoon, and did sally forth." In the barrage the captain, at his dugout door, is killed by a shell; the lieutenant returns having taken the objective, "did find his captain dead. And he did become captain in his stead." The moral line is "A sub. in a dug-out's worth two on the wire." Mechanisms: biblical register for army procedure (E09b1-26); a parody of the original story's villain with an ending that punishes him by chance, not by God; the dug-out proverb (a twist on "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush") that does not say whether the dugout is safe. I paraphrase the sexual and violent elements and quote only the moral and one line about rum. Targets: officers who exploit rank over a junior's wife; the rum before an attack; the stray shell as the plot's judge.
*Candidate application:* the story is evidence of an officer-class belief that abuse of rank is punished by the war's randomness and that the junior's rum is his courage.

**[E09b3-28]. The depot verse of the unfit long-service soldier (dialect, signed H. H. W.).** (PW-01 L24463-24575; the heading is lost.) A twenty-one-year soldier "hawkin' rags and bones at Wigan" before the war tells in couplets how he "joined to fight the Germans, and I crossed the angry sea. / Now I pick up bits of paper in a Depot, marked 'P.B.'," that his low medical grading keeps him there ("D.A.H., and bad bronchitis, and I'm groggy at the knees"), that "the soldiers think it funny ... For they chuck down bits of paper just to see me pick 'em up," and that on windy days they "form a ring, and make a horse of me, / And back their bits of paper, ten to one against P.B." He attempted leave on a lie: "I tried to get some leave I did, I said my wife was dead, / They found I'd never had a wife, and I got crimed instead." He "joined a working party going on up to the line," hid in a drain-pipe from shelling, and was told "Get out you fool, you're in a twelve-inch gun!" Of the experience: "The things that happened in the line have fairly turned me daft." The Battalion medical officer "passed me back again P.B.," and the closing resolution is "So here I am and here I'll stop; I'm bound to see it through, / I'll pick up bits of paper if my Country wants me to." Mechanisms: the downgraded soldier's grievance told as a comic dialect lament; absurd work (paper-picking) turned into a spectator sport by the troops (a sign of a ranks' cruelty that the verse records without comment); a lying leave application whose pretext (a dead wife) is exposed; the slapstick of hiding in the barrel of a gun; a trace of a nervous effect ("turned me daft") given as one line and then passed over; a closing line of dutiful humility. Target: the base, the medical categories, and the speaker's own bad luck. A rank-and-file voice written (like the other dialect verses, E08-28) by a hand I cannot place; the cruelty of comrades toward the unfit is the mark of the boundary between fit and unfit that the paper sets in many places.
*Candidate application:* the chronically downgraded old soldier who keeps going is a stable comic-tragic type; the verse also shows the in-group's habit of teasing the man who cannot fight.

**[E09b3-29]. "Seen from an Aid-Post": a road ballad whose refrain turns from "great" to "bloody."** (PW-01 L24585-24658; signed R.M.O.) The speaker opens in a catalog: "There are many roads in Flanders, where the horses slide and fall," roads "that finish at our trench," one is "a great road, a straight road, a road that runs between / Two rows of broken poplars, that were young and strong and green" (the poem names the road "that leads to Menin," from "old Poperinghe, through Vlamertinghe and Wipers" past "the solid Ramparts"; calling it the Menin Road is my gloss). A parenthetical in the second stanza is the one flat joke: "(It's a focus for Hun whiz-bangs and a paradise for snipers)." The third stanza: the road is "cursed by smokers; for you dare not show a light," "shunned by daytime" and "mainly used by night," with "limbers" bringing "ammunition to the guns, that guard the Salient's roads." The last stanza breaks into the first person: "And for hours and days together, I have listened to the sound / Of German shrapnel overhead, while I was underground / In a damp and cheerless cellar, continually trying / To dress the wounded warriors, while comforting the dying," and the refrain closes "On that muddy road, that bloody road, that road that runs between / Two rows of broken poplars, that were young and strong and green." Mechanism: the refrain with a shifted last stanza (as in E09a-27), where "great" becomes "muddy ... bloody" (the word is both the oath and the literal; the text does not mark which); the "poplars that were young and strong and green" as a plain image of what the war did. The only comic gesture is the parenthesis. This is the aid-post's counterpart to the plain poem "Roads" (E09b2-23). The initials match the "Jim" verse signature (E09b3-16); that the speaker is a Regimental Medical Officer is my inference from the subject, not a statement in the text.
*Candidate application:* a medical person's vantage (underground, listening, dressing the wounded) as the source of the group's plainest poem; the refrain trick as a way to change the meaning of a pleasant phrase.

**[E09b3-30]. "A Poet's Dedication": a love sonnet whose beloved is the rum ration.** (PW-01 L24661-24705; signed C.L.P.) A fourteen-line sonnet in elevated style that addresses an unnamed "fount of Inspiration" and "Creator of my verses, sonnets, songs," offering "adoration" at its "Shrine"; the longing is that of "one wet and war-worn warrior" who forgets "all daily cares and wrongs, / In the delight of nightly expectation." The apostrophe: "O Motive Force, that makes a soldier move / Great mountains of oppression from his soul; / Let others sing about the varied goal / Of Great Ambition, Women, War and Love, / Such plaudits always leave me cold and dumb, / Only your charms I praise, O Toto Rum." (The OCR reads "Toto Rum"; I take it as the rum ration, tentatively "tot o' rum," and flag the reading.) Mechanism: the sublime register for a trivial object; the last-word reveal; the ranking of the rum above "Women, War and Love." The functional claim, in joke form, is that the ration lifts "oppression" from the soul and is anticipated "nightly." Compare E09a-40, E09a-48 and E09b2-4; this is the most open statement of the ration as emotional relief in the range.

**[E09b3-31]. "Somewhere in Wipers" by Cockles Tumley: the visiting correspondent's tour, with the soldier's opinion withheld.** (PW-01 L24711-24777; byline "COCKLES TUMLEY," introduced as "Our representative ... has just paid a visit to the Front, and here describes his experiences in his inimitable manner.") A list-structured parody: "You can't imagine what I've seen. Neither can I! Stay, I will tell you. I've worn a tin hat! I've eaten a tin of bully beef! I've talked with a general! I won't tell you what he said, but you can take it from me THE WAR IS OVER." Then: "I've been in the support line, which is much more dangerous than the first. I've been in the reserve line, which is much more dangerous than the support. I have been in Div. H.Q., which is more dangerous still. And I have even been back to G.H.Q." "I have discussed the situation with the soldiers themselves. I can't tell you what they thought of it." The "lessons": "I have learnt that there's a lot of meat in a tin of bully. I have learnt that an army biscuit is a hard nut to crack," "that a tipping duckboard needs no push," "that Belgian beer wants a good deal of bush." Later: "Every German prisoner I spoke to said the same thing. I can't tell you what it was, but THE WAR IS WON." A slogan ("Watch the Q.M."); an anonymous Tommy "who had answered the call of King and Country": "I can't tell you his answer, but it impressed me wonderfully." The close: "Well, I will write more next week when my head is clearer. I must go now and have my photo taken in a gas-bag and tin hat." Mechanisms: (a) danger rising the farther one is from the enemy (a reversal of the line's own rule); (b) the "I can't tell you" device that withholds precisely what a soldier said (so that the soldier's real view is the silence the correspondent keeps, a joke on the censor and the correspondent's careful omission); (c) capitalized assertions of victory without content; (d) the correspondent's "learned" discoveries, each a pun or ration joke; (e) "my head is clearer," which I read as a hint of drink or a hangover (the text does not say); (f) the photograph in the gas-helmet. Target: the war correspondent and visiting civilians, rear echelons' claim to danger; the recognition that what a soldier said is the thing the press never prints. Continues E09a-26, E09b1-20, E09b2-22 and E08-70.
*Candidate application:* "I can't tell you what the soldiers thought" is a compact way for a veteran character to mock a civilian's account, by repeating what they omit.

**[E09b3-32]. "Aunt Annie's Corner: Tender Talks to Tiny Tots," as the unit's move out of the line.** (PW-01 L24783-24832.) The same syllabified baby-talk as E09a-32 and E09a-49, with the movement of units dressed as nursery news: "Aunt Annie is very cross with you as you have not written to her late-ly. This is very un-kind of you, as she brought you away from that nas-ty mud-dy place to the nice country." Items: "Little Billy Buggan has got a nice new house and a great big piece of land to play on, so he will be able to have great fun with his little friend Reggie"; "Mommy Flint went to a nice dog show ... says that there are no naughty boys over the way to throw stones at him. He has got a little Scotch friend staying with him"; "Porky and Jerry have been having a lot of games they tell me"; "We have got a lot of new Totties who have joined our little circle lately. They are all nice boys. I am sure we shall all be great friends, and play soldiers nicely together. Now that Xmas is coming we shall have a lot of parties." Signed "Your Ever Loving AUNT ANNIE." Mechanisms: infantilizing address as a way of describing a move to a rest area and the arrival of replacements ("new Totties") as a kindergarten intake; the "naughty boys ... to throw stones" as shelling from the enemy side; a speech act that tells soldiers the Army's care for them is a nursery's. The names are the paper's running characters (Billy Buggan and Reggie, whom E09b2 could not place; here they are "little friends"), plus "Porky" again (see E09b3-12). The hope for a Christmas free of the line is spoken in September. The Chapter 2 heading of the serial begins at L24836, outside my range, and I looked at it only to confirm where my range ends.
