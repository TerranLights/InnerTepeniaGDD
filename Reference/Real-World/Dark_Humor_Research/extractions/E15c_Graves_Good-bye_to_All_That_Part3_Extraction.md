## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-05 is.** The Project Gutenberg text (eBook #76911) of Robert Graves's 1929 autobiography. The author says he began writing it on 23 May 1929 and finished the main text on his thirty-fourth birthday, 24 July 1929, drawing "on contemporary records for most of the facts" and on memory for the rest (L12157-12168, E15c-89). My range, L8170 to the end of the book's own text at L12357 (followed by the Gutenberg transcriber's notes, L12362-12392, and the *** END *** line at L12396), holds: the last pages of chapter XXII (winter 1916-17 at Frises and Cléry, Graves's last front-line weeks); XXIII (Somerville hospital, Oxford cadet battalion, Osborne House convalescence); XXIV (Sassoon's July 1917 protest, the medical board, Craiglockhart); XXV (Litherland, garrison battalions, the January 1918 wedding, Rhyl, Sassoon's later letters, the Armistice); XXVI (Limerick, demobilization, influenza, Harlech 1919); XXVII (Oxford and Boar's Hill, 1919-20); XXVIII (Lawrence, Hardy, the shop, the move to Islip); XXIX (Islip, 1921-25); XXX (the Oxford B.Litt. thesis, the Egyptian appointment); XXXI-XXXII (Egypt, 1926); an unnumbered coda, "The story trails off here" (1926-29, ending "The End"); the "Dedicatory Epilogue to Laura Riding"; eight end-of-book footnotes; an author's erratum. The first twenty lines of my range (L8170-8190) overlap the last lines of E15b's range.

**What this part is about, and what it is not.** Graves's last front-line weeks are the winter of 1916-17 (the last events before the bronchitis that sends him to hospital are placed "towards the end of February"); he never returned to France. So "the war's last period" here is **a home-front war**: hospitals, an Oxford cadet battalion, garrison battalions, a camp near Liverpool, Ireland, and, for the front, only Sassoon's letters (E15c-20, -22, -35, -36, -37) and drafts leaving for France (E15c-30). The book contains **no front-line humor from 1917-18 narrated at first hand**, and no account of the Armistice beyond one solitary walk (E15c-40). Roughly two thirds of my range (chapters XXVI-XXXII and the coda, L9479-12357) is **post-service life**, 1919-1929: the nearest thing in the whole collection to a long, first-person, dated record of an ex-officer's civilian years, written by the man himself within ten years of leaving.

**Evidence base.** One narrator: an Anglo-Irish-German public-school officer of the Royal Welch Fusiliers, a wounded officer (a captain by the end), neurasthenic, poet, married with children, often short of money, who becomes a university student, a shopkeeper, a village councilor, a professor in Cairo and a writer. The post-war years are narrated at a distance of days to ten years, which is much shorter than the war chapters. The book is a literary autobiography with a stated authorial comic stance (his own recurring label, "caricature scene": E15c-16, -33, -69, -76, -85). Dialogue is reconstructed. Letters of Sassoon, Lawrence and others are given either in full (Sassoon's declaration, Lawrence's Egypt letter, Hardy's talk as a "record of the conversation") or, more often, in Graves's indirect summary; the summaries are not verbatim.

**What it does NOT contain.** No other-ranks narrator after Graves leaves the battalion (the other-ranks voice survives only as quoted lines: the Litherland draft, the Islip beggar-girl, Corporal Summers, Dym Bacon). No humor texts of the *Wipers Times* kind; no songs after the Armistice except nursery verses and one regimental march. No civilian audience told war jokes by the narrator: the book records instead the narrator declining to speak of the war (E15c-74), giving a civilian-facing speech he has "forgotten" (E15c-49), and, once, giving a flat, anti-glory reading (E15c-77). No account of the defector's problem as such (leaving on principle): Graves's dissent is political agreement with Sassoon, a choice to keep serving, and an exit by demobilization, not by protest. No treatment record: Graves chooses self-cure (E15c-70), and the only clinicians are Rivers (at second hand), Head (anecdotes) and the board doctors. No appendix or later-edition preface: the text file is the 1929 text with the author's own erratum (E15c-93).

### A. The winter of 1916-17 at Frises and Cléry, and the end of Graves's trench service (L8170-8370)

*The range opens in the last pages of chapter XXII. Graves, wounded in the Somme and sent home, had returned to the Second Battalion, was judged "unfit for trench service" by the battalion doctor and put in charge of the headquarters company (clerks, cooks, tailors, shoemakers, pioneers, transport men) living in dug-outs by the frozen Somme.*

**[E15c-1]. The winter of 1916-17: misery reported flatly, with a few dry details and almost no jokes.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8170-8208)
Graves says he had "never been so cold in my life" and that it "made me shudder to think what the trenches must be like." He walks the rations twelve miles up and back each night. In reserve billets at Suzanne, "tinned tomato" stew on metal plates "came in hot from the kitchen next door" and still had ice on the plate edge before the meal was finished; the river is frozen two feet thick and men play inter-company football on it; there are no civilians, no unshelled houses, only a few moorhens and ducks; fodder is short, so the horses are sick. The village of Cléry is down to "a short course of brick wall about three feet high"; a wrecked steam-roller "had the name of the village chalked on it as a guide to travelers," and the Germans "continued to shell" the village "from habit." The paragraph ends with the narrator's own statement of method: "I have kept few records of this time, but the memory of its misery survives." Mechanism: the humor is only in two details (a signpost chalked on a wreck, shelling kept up by habit) and the chapter's general tone is plain endurance.
*Candidate application:* The coldest, worst phase is rendered with almost no jokes and with the narrator saying outright that memory outlasts records. A character's worst period may be the one he can narrate least comically.

**[E15c-2]. The teetotal general who stopped the rum, and the sick-list that followed.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8175-8181)
The general commanding the division "had teetotal convictions on behalf of his men" and stopped their rum issue except in emergencies. Graves says the "immediate result was a much heavier sick-list than the battalion had ever had," and explains why: the tot at dawn stand-to was "the one bright moment of the twenty-four hours"; "When this was denied them, their resistance weakened." Mechanism: no joke; an officer's causal claim that a ritual comfort held men up and that a moral order from above broke it. (Compare E15b-40 and E15b-35 on the pre-attack rum; here the rum is withdrawn, and in the next finding it is promised as an inducement and fails.)
*Candidate application:* A small daily ritual, removed by a commander's principle, can be credited by the men's own officer with real health consequences; the grievance is "convictions on behalf of his men," that is, morality exercised on others' behalf.

**[E15c-3]. The dentist who grumbled and pulled the tooth in sections.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8209-8225)
After a twenty-mile ride to the army dental station for an abscess, Graves meets a dentist "under the weather like everyone else," who rants about the folly of having volunteered "at such a low salary," the destruction of the nation's teeth by unqualified men at home, the fees they charge, and the Medical Corps' refusal to promote dentists beyond lieutenant. He then breaks the tooth off twice, blames the forceps the Government supplied, and finally gets it out "in sections" over about half an hour; Graves "rode home with lacerated gums." The narrator comments only that the man was out of sorts. Mechanism: a professional's self-pitying complaint running in parallel with an inept procedure; the humor is observational and told straight.
*Candidate application:* The grumbling non-combatant professional (a classic army type) is rendered with no authorial commentary; the comic result is the gap between his sense of grievance and his work.

**[E15c-4]. A capital court-martial the narrator evades by finding a willing substitute.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8227-8245)
Graves is appointed to a field general court-martial on an Irish sergeant charged with "shamefully casting away his arms in the presence of the enemy"; Graves has heard unofficially that the man had been "maddened by an intense bombardment," thrown his rifle down and run with the rest of his platoon. A secret army order had just stated that "cowardice was only punishable with death and no medical excuses could be accepted." He sees only two options, sentencing the man to death or refusing to sit (in which case he would be court-martialed and "a reconstituted court would bring in the death verdict anyhow"), and he will not do the first "for an offense which I might have committed myself in the same circumstances." He resolves it by finding the one other eligible officer, a captain who was "hard-boiled and glad of a trip to Amiens," to sit in his place: "I met the situation by evading it." The register is flat throughout, with one dry phrase ("hard-boiled and glad of a trip to Amiens") that describes the substitute's motives.
*Candidate application:* A moral objection to the institution's penalty can be solved quietly by delegation, not protest; the narrator does not claim it as courage. (Restrained handling: a capital charge for what the narrator calls shell-induced flight; no execution is described.)

**[E15c-5]. "Official lying": twenty executions on file against a minister's denial.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8247-8254)
Graves says he first met "official lying" at the Havre base in May 1915, reading back-files of army orders in the officers' mess at the rest camp: about "twenty reports of men shot for cowardice or desertion," yet within a week the responsible minister in the House of Commons, answering a pacifist member, "had denied that sentence of death for a military offense had been carried out in France on any member of His Majesty's forces." Mechanism: no joke; a plain statement of the gap between the army's internal record and the civilian-facing parliamentary answer. (E15a-50 records a second-hand execution; this entry supplies the base-level frame.)
*Candidate application:* Evidence for a documented civilian-facing register that is flatly false, and for a soldier-narrator learning the gap from paperwork; a defector's distrust of "official" accounts can rest on seeing both versions.

**[E15c-6]. A Bible text read aloud to the acting C.O.: the boundary of license is blasphemy, not the other commandments.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8256-8282)
The acting commanding officer, sick, irritable and drinking "a lot of whisky," shares a dug-out with Graves and the quartermaster. Graves, reading the Bible, finds the verse "The bed is too narrow to lie down therein and the coverlet too small to wrap myself therewith" and remarks to the acting C.O. (James, the acting C.O., who answers by calling Graves "von Runicke," a nickname on the narrator's German family name) that it is "pretty appropriate for this place." James, "genuinely furious," replies: "Look here, von Runicke," he says, "I am not a religious man. I've cracked a good many of the commandments since I've been in France; but while I am in command here I refuse to hear you or anyone bloody else blaspheme Holy Writ." Graves adds that he liked James a lot: they had met on his first day with the regiment when James, home from Canada, was "hilariously throwing chairs about" and "reciting Kipling to the prairie-dogs"; he "cared for nobody, was most courageous, inclined to be sentimental." Mechanism: a joke misfires against a hidden limit (the sacred text), and the offended officer's self-description ("I've cracked a good many of the commandments") is itself the comic sentence.
*Candidate application:* A hard-drinking, profane, sentimental officer may hold one narrow line sacred, so the line between acceptable and unacceptable jokes is individual and not predictable from his behavior.

**[E15c-7]. Brigade wants to "bite off" a salient: the trench soldiers' reading and the rum-allowance form.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8283-8322)
Graves, as senior officer present, attends the C.O.s' conference where the brigadier wants to take a German salient as "proof of the offensive spirit of his command." The narrator states the soldiers' view: "Trench soldiers could never understand the Staff's desire to bite off an enemy salient," since a salient exposes attackers to fire from both flanks and "our obvious duty was to keep them there as long as they could be persuaded to stay." The explanation the battalion settled on: "it was the passion for straight lines for which headquarters were well known, and that it had no strategic or tactical significance." The attack had twice been proposed and twice cancelled for the weather. Graves reproduces a field message that sets out, in gridded phrases, the rum allotment for the forthcoming operations (7½ gallons). His comment: "Even this promise of special rum could not encourage the battalion." The company commanders tell him that crossing three hundred yards of knee-deep thaw mud would take four or five minutes under fire. The general asks "in a fatherly way" how old he is and whether he is not proud to attend the conference at twenty-one; Graves answers that he "had not examined my feelings, but that I was an old enough soldier to realize the impossibility of the attack." The colonel of the Cameronians agrees and "the attack was finally called off"; the battalion "was much relieved." Mechanism: a rank-and-file derision of staff aesthetics ("straight lines"); a deadpan evasive answer to a patronizing question; the rum form as comic document. 
*Candidate application:* A twenty-one-year-old acting battalion commander whose deflection ("I had not examined my feelings") both declines the flattery and states the professional judgment; the story shows an officer's honest objection succeeding where the men's grumbling could not.

**[E15c-8]. "The last dead man I was to see in France, and like the first, a suicide."** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8326-8334)
Going up with the rations through shelling, Graves passes the chaplain, who is "gabbling the burial service" over a man covered with a waterproof sheet; "It was a suicide case. The misery of the weather and the knowledge of the impending attack had been too much for him." Then the memoir's sentence: "This was the last dead man I was to see in France, and like the first, a suicide." (The first is E15a-46.) The paragraph moves directly on to the destroyed water limber. No humor and no comment; the narrator draws the only symmetry in the passage himself. (Restrained handling: a soldier's suicide, summarized; no method is stated in the source for this one.)
*Candidate application:* The bracketing of a soldier-narrator's front-line experience by two suicides, told without comment, is a plain-register marker; the chaplain's haste is conveyed in one word ("gabbling").

**[E15c-9]. The transport men's horse-stealing pride, and the lost team found under stain.** (PW-05, ch. XXII, L8336-8366)
A shell hits the company limber (water tins); the two horses, prize-winners for the best-matched pair of the division, vanish. Graves and Sergeant Meredith search for them in the dark through miles of morass until after midnight, because the battalion's pride rests on its animals: "Our transport men were famous horse-thieves, and no less than eighteen of our stable had been stolen from other units at one time or another, for their good looks." Two were "borrowed" from the Scots Grays; the horse Graves rode to the dentist came from the French police and "had a tendency to pull to the wrong side of the road," being the left-hand horse of a squadron. The inversion arrives with the quartermaster's letter to Graves in hospital: the horses had been found "unhurt except for grazes on their bellies" in the possession of "the machine-gun company of the Fourth Division; the machine-gunners were found disguising them with stain and trying to remove the regimental marks." Mechanism: boast about institutional theft (they "had never lost a horse to any other battalion") reversed by the narrator's own search; the punishment of the thieves by their own method. Also in the same passage (L8356-8360) the R.A.M.C. major at Rouen threatens "I'll have you court-martialed" if he finds Graves in his hospital again with "those lungs," a mock-threat that expresses concern; Graves, asked where in England he would like to go, answers "at random, 'Oxford.'"
*Candidate application:* A unit may be proud of its theft from other units and be robbed by the same code; the comedy comes from the self-proclaimed honor of thieves.

### B. Oxford, the cadet battalion, and the writers (L8371-8552)

**[E15c-10]. Hospital at Somerville: the narrator "both liked and disliked" the thought of being through with the war.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8377-8386)
Sent to a college converted to a hospital, Graves reflects that "it could not last long now," that he "disliked being away from the regiment in France" and liked "to think that I would probably be alive when the war ended." Sassoon had tried to follow him to the Second Battalion and was "unspeakably relieved" to know Graves was back at Oxford, though Graves "felt I had somehow let him down." Mechanism: no joke; the ambivalent pairing of survival-wish and loyalty guilt, stated in one sentence.
*Candidate application:* The first sign of a war's possible end produces divided feeling, and the friend who stayed or went on is the one the narrator measures the divide against.

**[E15c-11]. The cadet battalion: "few of the new officers were now gentlemen," selected by watching them play.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8388-8432)
As an instructor in an Officer-Cadet Battalion at Wadham, Graves reports that "Though the quality of the officers had deteriorated from the regimental point of view (in brief, few of the new officers were now gentlemen), their deficiency in manners was amply compensated for by their greater efficiency in action." About a sixth of candidates were failed: public-school boys "without the necessary toughness," or "rather stupid platoon sergeants and machine-gun corporals who had been out too long and were thought to need a rest" (recommended from France "on compassionate grounds"). The test was games: "The ones who played rough but not dirty and had quick reactions were the men we wanted." His own platoon: New Zealanders, Canadians, South Africans, two Fiji men, an English farm laborer, a Welsh miner and two or three public-school boys, "a good lot and most of them were killed later on in the war." (The sentence on their deaths sits last in the paragraph, as elsewhere.) He keeps going two months "on a strychnine tonic" and then faints and falls downstairs. Mechanism: the narrator's class vocabulary ("gentlemen," "manners") against his own admiration for the new officers' competence; the dead placed in a subordinate clause.
*Candidate application:* The sergeant "out too long" recommended for a rest as an officer is a documented euphemism for combat fatigue handled by promotion to a training job; the author applies the army's own sporting test of character.

**[E15c-12]. Oxford inverted: the don who saluted his pupil, and the president of seventeen societies.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8434-8453)
Wartime Oxford is "dislocated": the St. John's don "destined to be my moral tutor" is a corporal who drills in the parks and "saluted me whenever we met"; a college scout holds a commission and instructs in the other cadet battalion; there are about 150 undergraduates (Rhodes scholars, Indians and the unfit). Thomas Earp, to keep the Oxford tradition alive, "was president and sole member, he said, of some seventeen undergraduate social and literary societies" and handed the minute-books back to the returning university in 1919. Graves contributes to a small literary paper, *The Palatine Review*, and sees Aldous Huxley. Mechanism: the war reversing the university's hierarchy, delivered as flat list; the one-man societies a figure of institutional continuity maintained as a joke.
*Candidate application:* Role reversal (don saluting pupil) is the war's most frequent comic effect on home-front institutions; the one-man society is a deliberate preservation of form by means of absurdity.

**[E15c-13]. Bertrand Russell's strike-breaking question and the soldiers' view of the war and of munition workers.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8471-8493)
At Garsington, among pacifists and conscientious objectors (Clive Bell farming, Huxley, Strachey, Russell), Graves notes that the young writers in the army "none of whom now believed in the war," and records Russell's challenge: if a company of your regiment were brought to break a strike of munition makers, "would you order the men to fire?" Graves: "Yes, if everything else failed. It would be no worse than shooting Germans, really." Russell is surprised and asks whether the men would obey. Graves: "Of course they would," I said; "they loathe munition makers and would be only too glad of a chance to shoot a few. They think that they're all skrim-shankers." Russell: "But they realize that the war's all wicked nonsense?" Graves: "Yes, as well as I do." The paragraph ends: "He could not understand my attitude." Mechanism: a deadpan answer that treats the pacifist's hypothetical as a plain tactical question; the narrator's statement that his men see the war as nonsense and would still obey. ("Skrim-shanker" is the army slang for a shirker, here applied to civilian war-workers.)
*Candidate application:* A strong documented case of a combat soldier's contempt for the civilian war-economy (munition makers) held side by side with agreement that the war is nonsense and a willingness to obey; the combination baffles the civilian pacifist, which is the narrator's point.

**[E15c-14]. Strachey before the tribunal: the air-cushion and "only to this war."** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8495-8507)
Lytton Strachey chose to face a military tribunal as a conscientious objector, and the story Graves repeats is that he inflated an air-cushion during the hearing "as a protest against the hardness of the benches." Asked "I understand, Mr. Strachey, that you have a conscientious objection to war?" he replied in his falsetto, "Oh no, not at all, only to this war." Graves rates as "Better than this" his reply to the chairman's stock question, a hypothetical about a German soldier assaulting his sister (the question and the one-line answer, which turns on a pun, are summarized here and not reproduced; the answer deflects with mock gallantry). Mechanism: a civilian's repartee against the tribunal's scripted questions; Graves collects the exchange as an anecdote told to the company at Garsington, which he rates by its effect on a questioner "which had previously never failed to embarrass the claimant."
*Candidate application:* The wit of a civilian objector is admired by the soldier-narrator as a performance, which is a documented overlap between the dissident and the front-line register (a scripted question punctured by a precise answer). (Restrained handling: a sexual-assault hypothetical and its pun answer.)

**[E15c-15]. The famous writers and the front: Wells talking without listening, Galsworthy asking for slang, Novello's silk dressing-gown.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8509-8552)
Graves says that in 1916 he met more well-known writers than ever before or since, with "two unsuccessful meetings." George Moore, irritated by Graves's "neurasthenic twitchings," told him not to fidget; Graves "taunted him" with putting cactus in the Holy Land. H. G. Wells, "full of military optimism," "talked without listening" and described his organized staff-conducted "Cook's Tour" of the front "and seemed unaware that I and the friend who was with me had also seen the sights." Birrell, corrected twice on biblical points, quoted Carlyle: "Young man, you are heading straight for the pit of Hell!" Galsworthy, editing a charity magazine, "asked me technical questions about soldier-slang—he was writing a war-play and wanted it accurate," and "except for these questions listened without talking" (Graves rates him therefore a better writer, if a less forceful propagandist, than Wells). Ivor Novello, "world famous as the author and composer of the patriotic song" about keeping the home fires burning, receives him in "a silk dressing-gown, in a setting of incense, cocktails, many cushions"; "I felt uncomfortably military," and he took off his spurs (he was a temporary field-officer at the time) "out of courtesy" to the furnishings, in a closing phrase that puns on a word for a cushion that also doubles as a slur on the host (the wording is not reproduced). Novello, in the Naval Air Service, "was able to keep the home fires burning until the boys came home." Mechanism: Graves's mockery is built on the civilian celebrity's distance from the front (Wells's tour, Novello's songs); the Galsworthy item shows civilians borrowing soldier slang for authenticity; the spurs line is a pun with a sexual-orientation innuendo aimed at the composer (summarized; see register). The footing of the humor is an officer's contempt for the patriotic home front.
*Candidate application:* A record of the front-line soldier as unwilling source and mocking audience for civilian writers; the "technical questions about soldier-slang" is a rare note on how soldier language reached the civilian stage in the war's last years.

### C. Convalescence at Osborne House: caricature scenes, a mock society, a hoax (L8554-8671)

**[E15c-16]. The royal palace as convalescent home: "another of the caricature scenes of my life."** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8554-8571)
War Office rules ended convalescence at one's own home because officers "kept late nights, drank, and overtaxed their strength," so Graves is sent to a home for officers at Osborne House on the Isle of Wight; his bedroom "had once been the royal night-nursery of King Edward VII and his brothers and sisters." Patients walk Queen Victoria's favorite walks, "play billiards in the royal billiard-room, sing bawdy songs in the royal music-room, drink the Prince Consort's favorite Rhine wines among his Winterhalters," and are made honorary members of the Royal Yacht Squadron, where Graves sits "in a leather chair in the smoking-room of what had been and is now again the most exclusive club in the world, drinking gin and ginger, and sweeping the Solent with a powerful telescope." The narrator's label: "This is another of the caricature scenes of my life." Mechanism: incongruity between the social register of the palace and the status of the patients; Graves names his own technique of labeling scenes as caricature.
*Candidate application:* The recurring "caricature scene" (field-boots at a Piccadilly wedding, spurs in a drawing room, sword in a palace) is the narrator's way of marking events whose form is absurd against their content; a character may use such a stock label for his own post-war life.

**[E15c-17]. The Benedictine abbey: "poetry was not regarded as improving," and a joke with himself in French.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8573-8613)
At the French Benedictine abbey at Quarr near Osborne, the monks' plain-song "made us for the moment forget the war completely." Many of the monks, Graves is told, were ex-army officers who had, "I was told," "turned to religion after the ardors of their campaigns or after disappointments in love," and who saw the war as a dispensation to restore France to Catholicism. Asked by the guest-master whether there is a book he would like, Graves asks if there is a poetry section; the monk "smiled kindly and said, no, poetry was not regarded as improving." Asked by the Father Superior why he does not become a Catholic, Graves puts him off with "we have a proverb in England never to swap horses while crossing a stream. I am still in the war, you know," then offers "Peut-être après la guerre," and tells us: "This was a joke with myself": it was the stock answer that girls of the Pas de Calais were told by priests to give soldiers who solicited them (summarized; the soldiers' request is not reproduced). He half-envies the monks, "finished with wars and love affairs." Mechanism: self-directed in-joke built on borrowed idiom; gentle deflection of a sincere question by a flippant proverb.
*Candidate application:* A character may deflect sincere invitation (to faith, to change) by a quip that he labels, to himself, as a private joke; the attraction of the withdrawn life is also stated plainly.

**[E15c-18]. The Royal Albert Society: a mock-society as the counter to a gloomy, neurasthenic hospital.** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8615-8655)
"Osborne was gloomy. Many of the patients there were neurasthenic and should have been in a special neurasthenic hospital. A. A. Milne was there ... and in the least humorous vein." Graves and Vernon Bartlett, deciding "something must be started," found the "Royal Albert Society" to revive interest in the Prince Consort; Graves is president, with "regalia" of a Scottish dirk, Hessian boots and side-whiskers, and "Official business was not allowed to proceed until the announcement had been duly made that the whiskers were on the table." Membership rules (students of the Prince's life, natives of Alberta, residents by the Albert Nyanza, holders of the Albert Medal, and so on) are as elaborate as a real society's; members report reminiscences collected from palace servants; there are about fifteen members and "ate strawberries." When about a dozen officers, "most of them drunk," come in claiming qualifications (one's grandfather built the Albert Memorial; one worked at the Albert Docks; one had the Albert Medal), they begin "giving indecent accounts of the private life of the Prince Consort." Bartlett and Graves "got worried; it was not that sort of society." As president, Graves rises and tells "in an improved version the story which had won the 1914 All-England Interregimental Competition at Aldershot for the worst story of the year," linking it to the Prince Consort by claiming he had been told it by John Brown and "it had prevented him from sleeping for three days and nights, and was a contributory cause of his premature death." "The story had the intended effect; the interrupters threw up their hands in surrender and walked out." Graves then reflects "how far I had come since my first years at Charterhouse seven years back, and what a pity it was that I had not used the same technique there." (The worst-story's content is not given; see register.) Mechanism: parody of a learned society's ritual; the mock-ritual's rules (the whiskers) invented by neurasthenic patients as deliberate morale work; a bawdy-story weapon used to repel bawdy intruders by outdoing them under a mock-historical pretext.
*Narrator's claim:* The society is begun because the hospital is gloomy; the humor is made, on purpose, as a remedy, and Graves explicitly tracks his own growth from a school victim of bawdy humor (E15a-8) to a master of it.
*Candidate application:* An invented society with absurd rules is a documented way for convalescent officers to create their own order; it is policed by the founders against rowdier humor of the same sort.

**[E15c-19]. The beach-corpse hoax, and "Anything to make people laugh. But it was hard work."** (PW-05, ch. XXIII, L8657-8671)
On the beach, Graves and Bartlett find an old ship's fender whose frayed rope looks like hair. Bartlett says: "Poor fellow, I knew him well. He was in my platoon in the Hampshire Regiment and jumped overboard from the hospital ship." Further along they find an old pair of trousers, a coat, socks and a boot; they dress the fender as a body, drape seaweed over it "where necessary," and walk on. They then fetch a coastguard, who stops a few yards off and says, holding his nose, "Pooh, don't he 'alf stink!" They leave him with the "dead," and next day read in the Isle of Wight paper "of a hoax that 'certain convalescent officers at Osborne' had played on the coroner." Graves adds that he and Bartlett "were nonsensical, and changed the labels of all the pictures in the galleries. Anything to make people laugh. But it was hard work." Mechanism: a prank on civilian officials (coastguard, coroner) using a faked suicide-by-drowning corpse, with the invented dead comrade; a second prank (swapping picture labels) in the same register. The narrator's closing sentences name the motive (making people laugh) and the cost (effort).
*Candidate application:* A direct statement that the invented humor of neurasthenic convalescents was work they felt compelled to do, aimed at civilians, using the language of the front (the dead comrade, the platoon). (Restrained handling: a faked drowned corpse and an invented suicide; no decay is described beyond the coastguard's reaction.)

### D. Siegfried Sassoon, the 1917 protest, and the neurasthenic hospital (L8676-9020)

**[E15c-20]. Sassoon's letters: the "bloody wart" insult, the "military duet" joke, and Gosse's "libel on the British House of Lords."** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8679-8725)
Graves summarizes Sassoon's March 1917 letter from the Second Battalion: asked to "pull myself together and send him a letter" because he was "horribly low in spirits"; he "had not been made at all welcome," and a Special Reserve acting captain called him "a bloody wart" and referred to "the bloody First Battalion" (the custom of E15b-18/19 persisting a year later, now between officers of the same regiment); Sassoon "had swallowed the insult." He writes that he has "a half-witted platoon awaiting his orders to do or die" and a sore arm from an inoculation after the doctor's remark about his toughness. Sassoon jokes that he and Graves appear in the same issue of the *Nation* as "a military duet singing to a pacifist organ," "the poets who mean to work together some day and scandalize the jolly old Gosse's and Strachey's." In a parenthesis, Graves says that the "final end" of their correspondence "ten years later" came from his own failure "to observe the proper literary punctilios towards the late Sir Edmund Gosse," who had criticized an allegorical poem of Sassoon's in *The Old Huntsman* as a possible "libel on the British House of Lords" (the lines depict "slender sickly lord[s]" going home "because he could not stand the din"), since "The peerage ... had proved itself splendidly heroic in the war."
*Mechanism:* the "wart" insult is passed down from the old regulars to the Special Reserve (a bullied group re-bullying); the pun on organ/duet; the establishment critic's solemn reading of a satirical verse.
*Candidate application:* Two poet-officers who joke in letters about their own position (a "military duet" in a "pacifist organ"), and the friendship that ended a decade after the war over a literary punctilio: a documented case in which a wartime comradeship lapses over a small civilian issue. 

**[E15c-21]. The V.C. refused because the operation failed, and "some German Siegfried."** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8727-8736)
Graves tells how Sassoon, in the Hindenburg Line, led a bombing party of six to regain trenches the Cameronians had lost, was shot through the throat and continued bombing until he collapsed; his name was sent in for the Victoria Cross, "refused, however, on the ground that the operations had not been successful; for the Cameronians were later driven out again by a bombing party under some German Siegfried." (The wound is summarized as stated.) Mechanism: the narrator's closing pun on the poet's name compresses a bureaucratic rule (the award depends on the operation's outcome) into one ironic sentence.
*Candidate application:* The award system's outcome-contingency is mocked in a single closing clause; the heroic act is told in the same flat register as the refusal.

**[E15c-22]. Sassoon back in England: corpses on the pavement, rage at "bloody politicians," and a book sent "for a joke" to Haig.** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8738-8800)
Graves reports that Sassoon told him that "often when he went out he saw corpses lying about on the pavement." Sassoon's April 1917 letter from hospital reports from Yates that four officers were killed and seven wounded at Fontaine-les-Croiselles (a "perfectly bloody battle"), with an advance of half a mile as "some consolation"; "in the very next sentence" he raves at "all the good men being slaughtered that summer, and all for nothing," blaming "bloody politicians and ditto generals." He wishes he could protest but even assassinating the Premier or Haig would only have him shut up "in a madhouse like Richard Dadd" (Graves glosses Dadd, who drowned his own father), and a refusal to go back out "they would only accuse him of being afraid of shells." The official communiqués "were lying unblushingly about the casualties," even for Vimy. He knows "it was only a beautiful dream" to take a safe home job; he is "beastly weak and in a rotten state of nerves"; "The gramophone in the ward plagued him beyond endurance." And then the line that is a joke: *The Old Huntsman* "had come out that spring after all, and, for a joke, he had sent a copy to Sir Douglas Haig. He couldn't be stopped doing that anyhow." In June, Sassoon writes that "perhaps it was as well that we didn't meet, neither of us being at our best; at least one of us should be in a normal frame of mind when we were together." The poems he is writing are, he says, "digs at the complacent and perfectly —— people who thought the war ought to go on indefinitely until every one was killed except themselves"; the pacifists want "something red hot in the style of Barbusse" but he can't. He hears the guns "thudding all the time across the Channel" in Kent and doesn't know "whether he wanted to rush back and die with the First Battalion or stay in England and do what he could to prevent the war going on." (The text prints "——" for an omitted word, as in the source.)
*Mechanism:* the single act of sending his anti-war book to the Commander-in-Chief "for a joke" is the one humorous act in the dissent; the dead on the pavement (hallucination) and the avoided meeting are plain.
*Candidate application:* Dissent expressed as a private joke aimed at the top commander, the friend's wish not to meet because one of two must be "normal," and the double bind (go back and die, stay and fail) are all documented as how a decorated officer in a nerve collapse experienced the war's politics.

**[E15c-23]. "Finished with the War": Sassoon's declaration, which Graves reproduces entire, and the plain register of political protest.** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8803-8880)
Sassoon's July 1917 letter encloses a clipping. On one side an article ("The C.O.'s must be Set Free") calls conscientious objectors brave men, the press "lying, corrupt, and dastardly," etc. (Graves quotes it as printed.) On the other side, "A Soldier's Declaration," made to the commanding officer by Second-Lieutenant S. L. Sassoon, Military Cross, "as an act of willful defiance of military authority, because I believe that the war is being deliberately prolonged by those who have the power to end it." Graves reproduces the text entire: the war "upon which I entered as a war of defense and liberation, has now become a war of aggression and conquest"; "I am not protesting against the conduct of the war, but against the political errors and insincerities for which the fighting men are being sacrificed"; and his hope "to destroy the callous complacence with which the majority of those at home regard the continuance of agonies which they do not share, and which they have not sufficient imagination to realize." Graves's reaction is "anxiety and unhappiness": "I entirely agreed with Siegfried about the 'political errors and insincerities'; I thought his action magnificently courageous," but Sassoon "was not in a proper physical condition to suffer the penalty," Graves is "most bitter with the pacifists who had encouraged him to make this gesture" because "not being soldiers, they could not understand what it would cost Siegfried emotionally," and "as a gesture it was inadequate. Nobody would follow his example either in England or in Germany. The war would obviously go on, and go on until one side or the other cracked." Register: no humor anywhere in the declaration or in Graves's reading of it.
*Candidate application:* The key civilian-facing statement of the book, a soldier speaking in plain, non-humorous prose to those who "do not share" the agony; the scene shows how humor is dropped when a soldier decides to address civilians as a political act.

**[E15c-24]. Rigging the medical board: "The irony of having to argue to these mad old men that Siegfried was not sane."** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8898-8990)
Graves intervenes: he gets himself passed fit for home service, writes to a minister's secretary to keep the letter out of the papers, writes the senior major of the Third Battalion, and "rig[s]" the medical board by appearing as a witness. He realizes the colonel of the board "was patriotic and unsympathetic," the major "reasonable but ignorant," and the captain "a nerve-specialist, right-minded, and my only hope"; he is "most deferential to the colonel and major, but used the captain as an ally." He plays "the rôle of a patriot distressed by the mental collapse of a brother-in-arms, a collapse directly due to his magnificent exploits in the trenches," cites Sassoon's "hallucinations" of corpses in Piccadilly, and states: "The irony of having to argue to these mad old men that Siegfried was not sane! It was a betrayal of truth, but I was jesuitical." Graves was "in nearly as bad a state of nerves as Siegfried myself and burst into tears three times in the course of my statement." The colonel says as he leaves: "Young man, you ought to be before this board yourself." Graves worries Sassoon will "undo my work by appearing too sane." Mechanism: institutional irony in which a madness diagnosis is the escape route, performed by a man who is himself unwell; the board's own unintended remark confirming it.
*Candidate application:* A documented example of using shell-shock as an administrative tool for a friend's protection, narrated with the narrator's own admitted lie and tears; the board colonel's remark shows the institution half seeing it.

**[E15c-25]. "Dottyville": the great joke of escorting a friend to the neurasthenic hospital.** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8947-8995)
Before the board, Sassoon tells Graves "he had just been down to the Formby links and thrown his Military Cross into the sea." After it, Sassoon is sent to Craiglockhart "a convalescent home for neurasthenics" near Edinburgh with Graves detailed as escort. "Siegfried and I both thought this a great joke, especially when I missed the train and he reported to 'Dottyville,' as he called it, without me." Mechanism: the nickname for the neurasthenic hospital ("Dottyville") and the reversal of escort and patient (the escort misses the train); the pair treat the diagnosis as comedy though both are patients in fact.
*Candidate application:* A mutually self-aware joke about the mental-hospital nickname between two officers who both know they are unwell; the humor is used jointly to cover a protection they arranged by exaggerating illness.

**[E15c-26]. How the regiment would read the protest: "ratting," "cold feet," "not acting like a gentleman," and "every one was mad except ourselves."** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8950-8967)
At Liverpool, Graves argues with Sassoon: "that he was right enough in theory; but that every one was mad except ourselves and one or two others, and that it was hopeless to offer rightness of theory to the insane. I said that the only possible course for us to take was to keep on going out to France till we got killed." He asks what the First and Second Battalions would think: "They would say that he was ratting, that he had cold feet, and was letting the regiment down by not acting like a gentleman." Even Old Joe, "the most understanding man in the regiment," could not understand. "The army could, I repeated, only understand it as cowardice, or at the best as a lapse from good form. The civilians were more mad and hopeless than the army." Sassoon "would not accept this view," but because he was ill and knew it, agreed to the board. Register: plain argument; Graves admits he is arguing against his own agreement with the protest's content.
*Candidate application:* A direct statement of the categories (cowardice, a lapse of form) by which an officer's community would classify a principled exit, voiced by a man who agrees with the principle: useful for a defector who left on principle and knows how comrades read it.

**[E15c-27]. Craiglockhart and Rivers; Owen "accused of cowardice by his commanding officer."** (PW-05, ch. XXIV, L8996-9020)
At Craiglockhart, Sassoon is in the care of W. H. R. Rivers, who "had over a hundred neurasthenic cases in his care and diagnosed their condition largely through a study of their dream-life." Graves notes Rivers had earlier treated their friend Dick and judged him fit to enlist again. "Before I returned from Edinburgh I felt happier"; Sassoon begins the poems that became *Counter-Attack*. Another patient, Wilfred Owen, "had had a bad time with the Manchester Regiment in France; and, further, it had preyed on his mind that he had been accused of cowardice by his commanding officer. He was in a very shaky condition. It was meeting Siegfried here that set him writing his war-poems. He was a quiet, round-faced little man." Register: plain; no humor.
*Candidate application:* The convalescent hospital as a place where poets worked: Graves's report that a cowardice accusation "preyed on" a man's mind is stated without any joke.

### E. Chapter XXV: Litherland, the garrison battalions, the wedding (L9021-9319)

*Graves, back on duty after the Craiglockhart business, is at the Third Battalion's camp at Litherland near Liverpool, then at Oswestry and Rhyl with the garrison battalion.*

**[E15c-28]. Litherland: a man in a gas-mask beside a manhole, and "the band-music saved me."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9028-9040)
Graves says the medical board had been right that he should not have been back on duty. "I realized how bad my nerves were when one day, marching through the streets of Litherland ... I saw three men wearing gas-masks standing by an open manhole." They were bringing up a man gassed by waste chemicals from a munitions factory that had entered the sewer (the man's yellowed condition is summarized, not described). "The company did not pause in its march so I had only a glimpse of the group; but it was so like France that I all but fainted. The band-music saved me." Mechanism: no joke; the narrator's own self-diagnosis, and the last line, which credits the regimental band with holding him upright in front of his men. (Restrained handling: a dead civilian in a sewer, summarized only.)
*Candidate application:* A civilian accident that resembles the front triggers a near-collapse, and ceremony (marching music) is what keeps the public face intact; the narrator stays in formation.

**[E15c-29]. The court-martial of "the rabbit": a pun-laden report, and the narrator's turn to self-reproach.** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9042-9064)
Graves sits on a camp court-martial of a civilian who was alleged to have enlisted under the Derby Scheme and not to have presented himself. The man's solicitor produces a letter from a corporal in France, who admits that he enlisted in the "rabbit's" name on leave "because he had heard that the rabbit had been rabbiting with his wife." The enlistment form's recorded eye color (blue) did not match the man's brown eyes, so the story was "true so far"; but the man's munitions-work protection certificates were proved forged, so he was "deemed to have enlisted" and a deserter, and the prescribed sentence was two years. The narrator's telling plays throughout on "rabbit" (a nasty-looking little man; "squealed rabbit-fashion") and then turns: "It made me feel contemptible, as part of the story." Mechanism: slang-pun comic narration of a farce that ends in a real sentence, with the narrator's last line breaking the comic frame against himself.
*Candidate application:* A documented example of the officer-narrator's comic report on a man he is helping to convict, and the point at which he stops enjoying his own telling.

**[E15c-30]. The drafts in handcuffs, and the old soldier's "Easy come, easy go, sir."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9066-9111)
Graves observes that men warned for drafts "were always more cheerful about going in the spring and summer when there was heavy fighting on than in the winter months when things were quiet," and in a parenthesis quotes Attwater, that large drafts sent off in the critical weeks of spring 1918, when the Germans broke through the Fifth Army, "went down to the station singing and cheering enthusiastically." On the station at Litherland he hears the colonel's standard speech to a draft ("going cheerfully like British soldiers to fight the common foe ... some of you perhaps may fall"); "The draft cheered vigorously; rather too vigorously, I told myself." Among them are old First Battalion men, including 79 Davies, 33 Williams, and "Dym Bacon" Davies, whose nickname (Welsh for "there isn't any bacon") came from his first-morning breakfast complaint. A man with a D.C.M., Médaille Militaire and Military Medal but "no stripe" answers Graves's "Lost them again, sergeant?" with a grin: "Easy come, easy go, sir." Then "the train came in and I put out my hand with 'Good luck!' 'You'll excuse us, sir,' he said. The draft shouted with laughter and I saw why my hand had not been wrung, and also why the cheers had been so ironically vigorous. They were all in handcuffs." They had overstayed leave on purpose to be sent back to the First Battalion rather than to Mesopotamia; the colonel, "not understanding," had guardroomed them for the next draft, so they were going back to the battalion of their choice under military-police escort. The narrator adds that the men "bore him no ill-will" (the colonel had no active service but was "good-hearted," built a cinema hut, was mild in orderly room).
*Mechanism:* the joke is on the officer (who offers a handshake to a draft in irons); the men's irony turns the colonel's farewell speech into a performance; their cheering is the weapon. A decorated, demoted man's one-line epigram on lost honors.
*Candidate application:* Several documented types in one scene: men choosing a unit and a danger by absenting themselves; irony as the cover for what is plain disobedience; a good-hearted officer who does not understand and is not resented; and "easy come, easy go" as a veteran's description of decorations and stripes.

**[E15c-31]. Gas and noise phobias; the medical-board ladder; two ironic quotations exchanged in letters.** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9113-9137)
Graves decides to leave Litherland, whose winter mist mixed with T.N.T. fumes made him "cough and cough until I was sick." Back to France was "absurd as yet": "Since 1916 the fear of gas had been an obsession; in any unusual smell that I met I smelt gas—even a sudden strong scent of flowers in a garden was enough to set me trembling. And I knew that the noise of heavy shelling would be too much for me now. The noise of a motor-tire exploding behind me would send me flat on my face or running for cover." So he schemes to reach Palestine, "where gas was not known and shell-fire was said to be inconsiderable," by climbing the medical-category ladder: B2 (garrison service at home), then B1 (garrison service abroad), then A1. In the same passage, he reports Sassoon's letter from Craiglockhart: "What do you think of the latest push? How splendid this attrition is! As Lord Crewe says: 'We are not the least depressed.'" and says "I matched this with a remark of Lord Carson's: 'The necessary supply of heroes must be maintained at all costs.'" Mechanism: two shell-shocked officers trade politicians' official sayings as ironic ammunition; the narrator's own phobias reported flatly, then used as the reason to choose a different front.
*Candidate application:* The exchange of quoted officialese between two friends in hospital is a documented epistolary form of gallows humor; the fear symptoms (smell of flowers read as gas, a tire's burst read as shell) are named exactly and without comedy.

**[E15c-32]. Oswestry, Rhyl and Cork: a Morse error that sent a battalion to the wrong city, and a quartermaster outwitted.** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9139-9250)
At the garrison battalion, the colonel is "good" but "the material at his disposal was discouraging": mostly compulsory enlistments, and officers "with few exceptions, useless." Graves's loading of battalion stores with fifty chosen men "in two hours less than the scheduled time" wins the colonel's praise. When an invasion scare during a German fleet sortie sends the fit men to York, "There was a slight error, however, in the Morse message from War Office to Western Command. Instead of dash-dot-dash-dash they sent dash-dot-dash-dot, so the battalion was sent to Cork instead. Yet it was not recalled, being needed as much in Cork as in York" (Irish unrest, and Irish troops "giving away their rifles to Sinn Feiners"). Left in command of the remainder, Graves gets 3,300 Irish leave-men from France "thrown under my command" for four days ("rowdy and insubordinate"); 500 of 6,600 blankets are missing, "had probably been sold in Rhyl to pay for cigarettes and beer." He wins the Court of Inquiry by insisting the camp quartermaster leave his own office while evidence is taken ("His ignorance of my line of defense saved the case"), and uses the battalion mess-president's acceptance of presents from wholesale caterers to retaliate for being asked to pay a mess bill twice. The colonel recommends him for the Russian Order of St. Anne with Crossed Swords: "So, after all, I would not have left the army undecorated but for the October revolution, which cancelled the award-list." The same chapter (L9152-9156) names Howell Davies, who "had had a bullet through his head and was in as nervous a condition as myself"; they argue late at night "shouting each other down" about the war and poetry.
*Mechanism:* bureaucratic farce told deadpan (the single dot of a Morse symbol decides a city); the narrator's scoring of points against an official by procedure; the decoration jokingly lost to a revolution.
*Candidate application:* A documented case of an exhausted, nerve-damaged officer winning small administrative fights with procedure and quiet blackmail in a reserve unit, and finishing the account with a joke on the award that never came.

**[E15c-33]. The wedding as "caricature scene": field boots, plaster icing, and a bride who took a bottle.** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9158-9291)
Graves recalls his courtship of Nancy Nicholson (who was sixteen when they first met and eighteen at the wedding, to his twenty-two), the warning that she is a feminist, and the London lung specialist's verdict that "my general nervous condition made it folly for me to think of active service in any theater of war." At the January 1918 wedding at St. James's, Piccadilly, George Mallory is best man; Nancy, who "had read the marriage-service for the first time that morning and had been horrified by it," nearly refuses. "Another caricature scene to look back on: myself striding up the red carpet wearing field-boots, spurs, and sword; Nancy meeting me in a blue-check silk wedding dress, utterly furious; packed benches on either side of the church, full of relatives; aunts using handkerchiefs; the choir boys out of tune; Nancy savagely muttering the responses, myself shouting them out in a parade-ground voice." At the reception "the wedding cake was in three tiers, but all the sugar icing was plaster" (sugar being nearly unobtainable); there is a sigh of disappointment when the case is lifted off; when a dozen of champagne is opened and there is a rush, Nancy says "Well, I'm going to get something out of this wedding, at any rate," grabs a bottle, and after three or four glasses changes into her land-girl's breeches and smock; his mother, who had been "thoroughly enjoying the proceedings," says "Oh dear, I wish she had not done that." "The embarrassments of our wedding night were somewhat eased by an air-raid." Mechanism: the narrator repeats his own formula ("caricature scene") for a life event, and the army's habits (parade-ground voice, sword) are the comic intrusion into the civilian rite. (Restrained handling: one oblique reference to the wedding night, not elaborated.)
*Candidate application:* The soldier-as-groom in full kit at his own civilian ceremony is a documented example of war's habits spoiling a civilian ritual, played for comedy by the narrator and by the bride's own refusal to behave.

**[E15c-34]. The Archbishop's postcard and other dry civilian items in the idle months.** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9293-9319)
In the idle garrison life (orderly room "about ten minutes a day," three chargers to ride) Graves visits the Archbishop of Wales, whose son had been killed in the First Battalion; they share "a taste for the curious," and he keeps a postcard in the Archbishop's hand headed with a dinner of horse-meat eaters from 1868 (the postcard has only the heading and the signature). Graves adds a parenthesis about bishops he has met, including the Bishop of Liverpool at Harlech in 1923, who "came out from the sea in great pain, having been stung in the thigh by a jellyfish," and was "tut-tutting miserably to himself that he had been under the impression that jellyfish only stung in foreign parts." The son's death is stated once, in a subordinate clause, in a paragraph of curiosities.
*Candidate application:* The consistent placing of a dead son in a clause between a postcard and a jellyfish is the memoir's habit made visible once more (see roll-up).

### F. Sassoon's later letters, the last months of the war and the Armistice (L9321-9475)

**[E15c-35]. Old Joe's letter from Polygon Wood: "the battalion got its rations."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9338-9353)
Sassoon, after some months at Craiglockhart, reports (Graves is repeating a letter written the previous October) a long letter from Old Joe: the First Battalion had just come back to rest from Polygon Wood; conditions were "more appalling than anything he had yet seen," three miles of morass, shell-holes and dead men and horses to cross with rations. Joe had been "lying out all night in shell-holes and being shelled"; "several of the ration party were killed, but at least, according to Joe, 'the battalion got its rations'." Sassoon writes: "If only the people who wrote leading articles for the Morning Post about victory could read Joe's letter!" Mechanism: a sergeant-style dry rider on a casualty report (the task completed); a soldier's wish that the newspaper's civilian authors might read a plain account.
*Candidate application:* A field officer's understated report that "the battalion got its rations" after men were killed is the memoir's best example of an institutional duty serving as the whole moral of an ordeal; the Morning Post line is the civilian-facing complaint again.

**[E15c-36]. Sassoon returns to the war and Graves: "good form" is "suicidal stupidity," and a poem "that comes off whichever way you read it."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9355-9389)
Sassoon, after months in hospital, "decided that the only possible thing to do was, after all, to go back to France." He writes the poem about ghosts who reproach him in dreams for not being in the line and demands in writing that Rivers have him sent back to the front at once. He sends Graves a letter "reprehending me for the attitude I had taken in July": "It was suicidal stupidity and credulity, he said, to identify oneself in any way with good form or gentlemanliness, and if I had real courage I wouldn't be acquiescing as I was." Graves reports he "forget[s] how I answered" and might have said "when I was in France I was never such a fire-eater as he was. The amount of Germans that I had killed or caused to be killed was negligible compared with his wholesale slaughter." His assessment of Sassoon: "the direction of Siegfried's unconquerable idealism changed with his environment; he varied between happy warrior and bitter pacifist." He then discusses the Sassoon poem beginning "To these I turn, in these I trust, / Brother Lead and Sister Steel," which "was originally written seriously, inspired by Colonel Campbell, v.c.'s bloodthirsty 'Spirit of the Bayonet' address at an army school. Later he offered it as a satire; and it is a poem that comes off whichever way you read it. I was both more consistent and less heroic than Siegfried." Mechanism: the same text read as sincere or as ironic depending on the reader's state; Graves's closing self-assessment is dry rather than humble.
*Candidate application:* A strong documented case in which the same verse switches register by context (sincere to satire), an argument that humor and earnestness about weapons can be one text; and the friend's reversal ("good form" as stupidity) after months of hospital reflection.

**[E15c-37]. Palestine and France again: "for the nth time," "damn everything except staying with his company," and the solicitor's polite letter.** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9391-9418)
Sassoon is sent to Palestine and then to France; he writes in April that he "expected that the First and Second Battalions had about ceased to exist by now for the nth time" and in late May, quoting Duhamel, writes in his "happy-warrior vein": his men are the best he has served with, he is training them "bloody well," "his flamelike ardor" has come from nowhere, and his military efficiency "was derived from the admirable pamphlets that were now issued, so different from the stuff we used to get two years before." He says that on reading Graves's letter he thought "damn Robert, damn every one except his company, which was the smartest turn-out ever seen, and damn Wales and damn leave and damn being wounded and damn everything except staying with his company until they were all melted away." (A parenthesis here describes how the dead lie on the ground; the passage is summarized and its words are not reproduced; see register.) Graves must remember "this mood" when he sees Sassoon "(if I saw him) worn out and smashed up again, querulous and nerve-ridden. Or when I read something in the casualty list and got a polite letter from Mr. Lousada, his solicitor." "There never was such a battalion, he said, since 1916, but in six months it would have ceased to exist." Mechanism: a catalog of damnation in one breath that ends in loyalty to a company; the reference to a solicitor's polite letter treats a future death notification as a form of correspondence.
*Candidate application:* The war-weary poet's happy-warrior mood and the narrator's cool bracketing of it by "the casualty list" and "a polite letter" is a documented way of holding both attitudes at once.

**[E15c-38]. A haunted house at Harlech, a mother's death, and a brother killed "two months later."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9420-9447)
Graves, on leave from a cadet course, stays in a Tudor house near Harlech, "the most haunted house that I have ever been in, though the ghosts were invisible except in the mirrors": they "would open and shut doors, rap on the oak panels, knock the shades off lamps, and drink the wine from the glasses at our elbows when we were not looking." The owner was a Second Battalion officer "whose ancestors had most of them died of drink." The only visible ghost was "a little yellow dog that appeared on the lawn in the early morning to announce deaths. Nancy saw it one day." Nancy's mother, ill with Spanish influenza, "reduced her temperature with aspirin and pretended that she was all right" to see her son Tony on leave. "But she knew that the ghosts in the mirrors knew." She died on 13 July; "While she was dying her chief feeling was one of pleasure that Tony had got his leave prolonged on her account. (Tony was killed two months later.)" A week later Graves hears that on the day of her death Sassoon "had been shot through the head while making a daylight patrol through long grass in No Man's Land," and that Sassoon wrote "a verse letter which I cannot quote, though I should like to do so. It is the most terrible of his war-poems." Mechanism: the ghost comedy (ghosts drinking wine) in the same paragraph as a family death; Tony's death in a parenthesis; the final refusal to quote the verse letter is the most explicit marking of a limit in this part of the book.
*Candidate application:* Compare E15b-12's ghost chapter closer; here the comedy of the haunted house is a prelude to a death and a withheld text. The refusal to quote is a stated limit: some war writing the narrator will not carry into his own book.

**[E15c-39]. "I went on mechanically," the cadet candidates, and the poems of the "falsities for public delectation."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9449-9465)
The cadets now are "mostly Manchester cotton clerks and Liverpool shipping clerks"; "they were all experienced men from France and were quiet and well behaved. We failed about one in three. And the war went on and on." Graves is writing *Country Sentiment*, "romantic poems and ballads" with a group of pacifist war-poems at the end, dedicated to Nancy: "Instead of children as a way of forgetting the war, I used Nancy." One poem about French civilians contains "so many lies": he said that old Adelphine Heu of Annezin gave him a painted china plate and that her pride was hurt when he offered to pay; "The truth is that I bought the plate from her for about fifteen shillings and that I never got it from her," because the daughter-in-law claimed the plate as her own, and "I never got my money back from Adelphine. This is only one of many of my early poems that contain falsities for public delectation." Mechanism: the narrator's own correction of the sentimentalized, public version of a wartime incident, with the unflattering, transactional truth and a wry tone about being cheated.
*Candidate application:* A direct statement from a veteran-author that his published verse of the war years contained "falsities for public delectation": a documented case of the civilian-facing, sweetened account being corrected years later by the same teller, and being done with money as the humbling fact.

**[E15c-40]. The Armistice: "cursing and sobbing and thinking of the dead."** (PW-05, ch. XXV, L9467-9474)
"In November came the Armistice. I heard at the same time news of the death of Frank Jones-Bateman, who had gone back again just before the end, and of Wilfred Owen, who often used to write to me from France, sending me his poems. Armistice-night hysteria did not touch the camp much, though some of the Canadians stationed there went down to Rhyl to celebrate in true overseas style. The news sent me out walking alone along the dyke above the marshes of Rhuddlan (an ancient battle-field, the Flodden of Wales) cursing and sobbing and thinking of the dead." Register: there is no joke. The two deaths (named, with no dry tag) are the news that arrives with the peace, and the narrator withdraws from the celebration instead of joining it. The single parenthetical ("the Flodden of Wales") is a historical gloss, not a comedy.
*Candidate application:* The book's exact record of the end of the war is a solitary walk, with grief and anger at the same time, away from both the camp's reserve and the Canadians' celebration; this is the sharpest evidence in the book that the end of the war does not release the joke register but removes it.

### G. After the Armistice: Limerick, demobilization and the first civilian year (chapter XXVI; L9479-9875)

*From here to the end of the book the narrator is out of the fighting and then out of the army. This is the stretch of the memoir that most directly bears on humor and memory after service.*

**[E15c-41]. Limerick, 1919: "they made a joke of politics," the stock Irish picture, and a substitute for a search party.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9482-9516)
Graves overstays his leave for the birth of his daughter Jenny ("neither coal-black nor affected by the shocks of the previous months," a wry reference to a friend's earlier insinuation about the family, which is not reproduced; see register) and then "lied my way out of the over-staying of leave." He joins the Third Battalion at Limerick, "a Sinn Fein stronghold" where "there were constant clashes between the troops and the young men of the town, yet little ill-feeling"; "Welsh and Irish got on well together, as inevitably as Welsh and Scottish disagreed. The Royal Welch had the situation well in hand; they made a joke of politics and used their entrenching-tool handles as shillelaghs." The town "looked like a town that had been through the war," with street holes "like shell-craters." An old man at an antique shop tells him no new houses are built, since when one falls "the survivors moved into another," and that "everyone died of drink in Limerick except the Plymouth Brethren, who died of religious melancholia." A stereotyped slum scene on O'Connell Street follows (summarized, not reproduced); Graves concludes "Ireland was exactly as I had pictured it. I felt its charm as dangerous." When detailed to lead a search party for hidden rifles, he asks the adjutant "to find a substitute; I said that I was an Irishman and did not wish to be mixed up in Irish politics." Mechanism: peacetime garrison duty described in the vocabulary of the war (shell-craters, shillelaghs made of entrenching-tool handles); the narrator uses his family nationality as a polite excuse, which repeats his earlier tactic of evading by finding a substitute (E15c-4). (Restrained handling: stereotyped depiction of Irish squalor and a mildly racialized joke; not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* Garrison duty in a hostile town, in the post-war interval, is carried by the troops with banter and improvised weapons; the officer's own unease is dealt with by a convenient identity claim.

**[E15c-42]. A nursery verse and "The British Grenadiers": "I had ceased to be a British grenadier."** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9518-9549)
Graves records "a new loyalty, to Nancy and the baby, tending to overshadow regimental loyalty now that the war was over." While he is writing a rhymed nonsense letter to Nancy and Jenny (a verse that plays on "Simple Simon to the Pieman" and "Babylon" and "Banbury Cross" and asks if there is "any song sweet enough / For Nancy or for Jenny?"), the battalion returns from a route-march and the drums and fifes strike up *The British Grenadiers* under his window. "The British Grenadiers succeeded for a moment in forcing their way into the poem" (he writes the march's lines "Some speak of Alexander, / And some of Hercules,") "but were driven out" by his own: "But where are there any like Nancy and Jenny, / Where are there any like these?" He concludes: "I had ceased to be a British grenadier." Mechanism: the regimental march physically invades a lullaby-verse and is expelled within the verse itself; the closing sentence is both wry and a plain statement of change.
*Candidate application:* A documented moment at which an officer registers that the army's music no longer claims him; the comic form (a verse fighting a march) makes a serious statement about identity safe to say.

**[E15c-43]. Resigning: "our scars and our commanding-officers' testimonials would get us whatever we wanted."** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9551-9572)
Graves decides to resign "at once," consults the demobilization priority list (agricultural workers and students first) and chooses a government-grant course at Oxford, "a convenient place to mark time until I felt more like working for my living." He describes the illusion of the demobilized: "We were all so accustomed to the war-time view, that the only possible qualification for peace-time employment would be a good record of service in the field, that we took it for granted that our scars and our commanding-officers' testimonials would get us whatever we wanted." And: "A few of my fellow-officers did manage, as a matter of fact, to take advantage of the patriotic spirit of employers before it cooled again, sliding into jobs for which they were not properly qualified." He notes it "seemed absurd at the time to suppose that university degrees would count for anything in a regenerated post-war England." Mechanism: retrospective irony about the collective belief that service would be a currency; "before it cooled again" is the dry timekeeping.
*Candidate application:* A documented expectation among demobilized officers that service itself is a qualification, retrospectively mocked by the narrator, with a note that the employer-goodwill window closed quickly.

**[E15c-44]. The new-army officers' manners, two mess ante-rooms, and the captives set free.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9578-9590)
The adjutant is short of officers who can be trusted with company accounts: "The latest arrivals from the new-army battalions were a constant shame to the senior officers. Paternity-orders, stumer checks, and drunk on parade were frequent. Not to mention table-manners, at which Sergeant Malley would stand aghast." The mess has "two mess ante-rooms, the junior and the senior," and a junior officer who was "regimentally a gentleman" (North Wales landed gentry or Sandhurst) is invited into the senior one. "All this must have seemed very strange to the three line-battalion second-lieutenants captured in 1914, now promoted captain by the death of most of their contemporaries and set free by the terms of the Armistice." Mechanism: regimental snobbery restated at the moment it is about to disappear; the dry last sentence about promotion by casualty.
*Candidate application:* The peacetime regiment returning to its old caste lines, while the men who survived to be exchanged are promoted by others' deaths, is stated as a joke on both sides.

**[E15c-45]. "The Handcuff King," the promised theatricals, and an uncle's cattle.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9592-9615)
The adjutant cancels Graves's appointment to a company only when he promises to help with the battalion theatricals for St. David's Day (he will play Cinna in *Julius Caesar*): "His change of mind saved me over two hundred pounds. Next day the senior lieutenant in the company that I was to have taken over went off with the company cash-box, and I would have been legally responsible. Before the war he used to give displays at Blackpool Pier as The Handcuff King; he got away safely to America." Graves also visits his uncle, a retired naval officer farming near Limerick who has had "his ricks burnt and cattle driven," and who says: "They have been out there all winter," he said, "and I haven't had the heart to go out and look at them these three months." Mechanism: a coincidence that rescues the narrator, ending on a pun on escapology (the man who escapes from handcuffs escapes with the cash-box); the uncle's plain remark about cattle is the book's plainest note of local despair.
*Candidate application:* The comic escapologist as thief (a pun that echoes the draft in handcuffs of E15c-30) shows the memoir joking with its own motifs; the uncle's line marks a limit where the register turns plain.

**[E15c-46]. The run for the last train: a squad commandeered, and the demobilization officer in the taxi.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9607-9680)
Informed that demobilization in Ireland will be stopped the next day, and held by the adjutant ("We're not going to let you go. You promised to help us with those theatricals"), Graves, with incipient influenza, "decided to make a run for it." He waits until the adjutant leaves the orderly room at 6:05, gets the colonel's signature on the statement that he had handled no company moneys ("I hurried in, saluted, got the colonel's signature, saluted, hurried out"), and, lacking transport, shouts to a corporal from the First Battalion: "Corporal Summers, quick! Get a squad of men. I've got my ticket and I want to catch the last train back." Summers "promptly called four men; they picked up my stuff and doubled off with it, left, right, left, to the station." Graves tumbles into the moving train and throws a pound note: "Good-bye, corporal, drink my health." He still lacks the code-marks that only the battalion demobilization officer (an ally of the adjutant) can supply; in a fever, "my mind was working clearly as it always does in fever," he reads in the *South Wales Echo* of a London railway strike and runs from the train at Paddington to take the only taxi. He offers to share it with a stranded officer and his wife, and when the officer asks how he can repay him, Graves says the only thing he wants is "the proper code-marks to complete my demobilization papers"; the stranger raps on the glass and says "Well, I happen to be the Cork District Demobilization Officer and I've got the whole bag of tricks here." "So he filled my papers in." At Wimbledon he is released at once because Ireland is officially a "theater of war." Mechanism: a farce of procedure built on the army's forms, the commandeering of a squad as a personal servant, and a coincidence told without comment.
*Candidate application:* The exit from the army is a comic chase against the army's own paperwork; the commandeered squad doubling the baggage is an affectionate parody, and the officer's own nerve-damaged readiness (fever clarity) is the engine.

**[E15c-47]. A false terror at supper, two ex-nurses, and "not drowned, merely unfaithful."** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9680-9712)
Arriving at Hove, Graves has "a sudden terror that made me unable to speak. I seemed to see Nancy's mother," who had died; she was "looking rather plump and staid and dressed unlike herself, and did not appear to recognize me." "It was like a bad dream... I knew I was ill, but this was worse than illness." Then Nicholson introduces the woman: "This is Nancy's Aunt Dora, just over from Canada." Within days everyone but Nicholson, the baby and one servant (who "kept it off by a gypsy's charm," he thinks a lizard's leg in a bag) has influenza. The two ex-nurses found are one "competent, but frequently drunk" who ransacks wardrobes, and one "sober but incompetent" who stands at the open window and cries "in a stage-voice: 'Sea, sea, give my husband back to me.' The husband, by the way, was not drowned, merely unfaithful." A doctor says he has no chance (septic pneumonia, both lungs), "This, now, was the third time in my life that I had been given up as dying," but "having come through the war, I would not allow myself to succumb to Spanish influenza." Mechanism: the hallucination is plain (and deflated by the host's matter-of-fact introduction); the nurse's dramatic line is turned by the narrator's dry aside; the narrator's resolution in terms of the war.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's post-war hallucination (a recognized dead person at table) is reported with no humor, and is followed at once by a comic deflation from a civilian who knows nothing of it; the two registers adjoin.

**[E15c-48]. The mutiny of the Guards, the hope of an ex-service rising, and the satisfied men.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9709-9734)
Graves is up in time to see "the mutiny of the Guards," about a thousand men of all regiments marching out of Shoreham Camp in protest at discipline. "The reaction against military discipline between the Armistice and the signing of peace delighted Siegfried and myself." Sassoon had campaigned in the post-armistice General Election for the pacifist Philip Snowden against "a threatening civilian crowd, trusting that his three wound stripes and the mauve and white ribbon of his military cross would give him a privileged hearing." Graves says: "We now half hoped that there would be a general rising of ex-service men against the Coalition Government, but it was not to be." Instead "the men were content to have a roof over their heads, civilian food, beer that was at least better than French beer, and enough blankets at night. They might find overcrowding in their homes, but this could be nothing to what they had been accustomed to; in France a derelict four-roomed cottage would provide billets for sixty men. They had won the war and they were satisfied. They left the rest to Lloyd George." The "only serious outbreak" was at Rhyl, "a two days' mutiny of Canadians with much destruction and several deaths. The signal for the outbreak was the cry: 'Come on, the Bolsheviks.'" Mechanism: dry comparison of living standards (a four-room cottage billeting sixty), stated as the reason men did not rise.
*Candidate application:* A documented observation by two decorated officer-dissidents that the men's tolerance for peacetime hardship was shaped by what they had accepted in the line; their hoped-for rising did not occur.

**[E15c-49]. Harlech, 1919: the uniform off, and the speech about "the glorious dead."** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9736-9748)
Graves "discarded my uniform, having worn nothing else for four and a half years," finds only a school suit that he has outgrown, and "found it difficult to believe that the war was over." He has "no experience of independent civilian life" since he left school for the army. "The Harlech villagers treated me with the greatest respect. At the Peace Day celebrations in the castle I was asked, as the senior of the officers present who had served overseas, to make a speech about the glorious dead. I have forgotten what I said, but it was in commendation of the Welshman as a fighting man and was loudly cheered." Register: no irony and no humor; the speech he delivered is the one the occasion wanted, and he says he has forgotten it. (Contrast with the later village memorial service in E15c-77, where he reads a different text.)
*Candidate application:* The civilian-facing register is complied with, as an act the narrator does not remember, in 1919; the later chapter shows him declining it.

**[E15c-50]. War in the body and the habits: shells on the bed, dead friends' faces, the battlefield view, commandeering, lying, the billet checklist, foul language.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9749-9773)
"I was still mentally and nervously organized for war; shells used to come bursting on my bed at midnight even when Nancy was sharing it with me; strangers in day-time would assume the faces of friends who had been killed." On the hill behind Harlech "I found that I could only see it as a prospective battlefield," working out how to hold the Artro valley "against an attack from the sea," where to put a Lewis gun and the rifle-grenade section. "I still had the army habit of commandeering anything of uncertain ownership that I found lying about; also a difficulty in telling the truth—it was always easier for me now when overtaken in any fault to lie my way out." He applies the trench takeover routine to his civilian situation: "Food, water supply, possible dangers, communication, sanitation, protection against the weather, fuel and lighting—each item was ticked off as satisfactory." Other "loose habits of war-time survived": hitching rides from passing motors, talking to fellow-travelers "without embarrassment," and relieving himself at the roadside "without shame, whoever was about" (summarized). He kept "the technique of endurance, a brutal persistence in seeing things through, somehow, anyhow, without finesse, satisfied with the main points of any situation." And: "But I modified my language, which had suddenly become foul on the day of Loos and had been foul ever since." Mechanism: a plain, itemized list in military format (the checklist) applied to a bedroom and a hill; the narrator's own dry, matter-of-fact tone for symptoms; "ticked off as satisfactory" is the closest to a joke.
*Candidate application:* A single paragraph with the best direct evidence in the book for what an ex-officer carries after demobilization: nocturnal hallucination, tactical reading of landscape, lying as a reflex, institutional checklists, a changed vocabulary that he has to retrain.

**[E15c-51]. Money, sleep and a list of disabilities: "I was ashamed of myself as a drag on Nancy."** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9773-9796)
"The chief difference between war and peace was money." Graves had "never had to worry about that since my first days at Wrexham," had saved about £150 in War Bonds, drew a £60 disability pension, and he and Nancy "lived as though we had an income of about a thousand a year" with a nurse and servant. "I was very thin, very nervous, and had about four years' loss of sleep to make up." (He also had an intestinal parasite from bad water in France, summarized.) "My disabilities were many; I could not use a telephone, I was sick every time I traveled in a train, and if I saw more than two new people in a single day it prevented me from sleeping. I was ashamed of myself as a drag on Nancy." The recent pneumonia "had set me back to my condition of 1917." Mechanism: a dry itemization of physical and nervous incapacities with the narrator's own judgment in one sentence.
*Candidate application:* The list is a documented inventory of an officer's civilian inability (telephone, train, new faces) with the shame stated; the "money" line recasts war as a place with no cost.

**[E15c-52]. The Daily Herald, Versailles, and the three home-news items.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9798-9819)
Sassoon, literary editor of the new *Daily Herald*, gives Graves books to review; the paper is "anti-militarist," "the only daily paper that protested against the Versailles Treaty and the blockade of Russia." Graves says the treaty "shocked me; it seemed to lead certainly to another war and yet nobody cared": at the critical Paris decisions, "public interest was concentrated entirely on three home-news items: Hawker's Atlantic flight and rescue, the marriage of Lady Diana Manners, and a marvelous horse called The Panther, which was the Derby favorite and came in nowhere." The *Herald* "spoiled our breakfast for us every morning" with reports of unemployment, "ex-service men refused re-instatement in the jobs that they had left," lockouts and strikes; "Nancy and I took all this to heart; we now called ourselves socialists." Mechanism: a deadpan list of three trivial items set against the treaty, with the horse's failure as the closing beat.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's account of the home front's attention shows a veteran's measure of civilian indifference; the horse "came in nowhere" is the dry sting.

**[E15c-53]. The family's allowance for his "shell-shocked condition," withdrawn for socialism.** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9821-9833)
"I had fought gallantly for my country—indeed I was the only one of my father's five sons of military age who had seen active service—and was entitled to every consideration because of my shell-shocked condition; but my socialism and sympathy for the Bolsheviks outraged them." His father reminds him that his brother Philip had once been a pro-Boer and a Fenian "but had recovered from his youthful revolutionary idealism," and hopes he will go to Egypt in the consular service "and there get over my revolutionary idealism." Mechanism: the narrator uses the family's own phrases ("every consideration," "shell-shocked condition") as a qualified credit that politics can cancel; the humor is in the "but."
*Candidate application:* Documented: a veteran's disorder is acknowledged by his family as an allowance, and politics can withdraw it; "shell-shocked" is at once a medical and a social excuse.

**[E15c-54]. Nancy refuses to treat his war as comparable, and "putting the war into the background."** (PW-05, ch. XXVI, L9834-9850)
Nancy's overriding concern is "judicial equality of the sexes"; "She refused to see my experiences in the war as in any way comparable with the sufferings that millions of married women of the working-class went through. This at least had the effect of putting the war into the background for me; I was devoted to Nancy and respected her views in so far as they were impersonal." Her anger at "male stupidity and callousness became an obsession," to the point she "could not bear a newspaper in the house" and they join the Constructive Birth Control Society "to the scandal of my family." Mechanism: a civilian partner's denial of the war's exceptional status has a therapeutic side effect; Graves reports it without protest.
*Candidate application:* An ex-officer's wartime suffering is put on a scale of other people's suffering by someone close, and the effect is relief, not resentment: a documented alternative to the "no one understands" pattern.

### H. Oxford 1919-1920, Boar's Hill, and war-shock among the poets (chapter XXVII; L9878-10200)

**[E15c-55]. Oxford with the soldiers back: "Yes, sir" undergraduates, a "Soviet" over the catering, and the tutor who no longer saluted.** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L9892-9925)
"The University was very quiet. The returned soldiers had little temptation to rag about, break windows, get drunk, or have tussles with the police and races with the bulldogs as before the war." The boys direct from school are quiet too, "they had had the war preached at them continually for four years." The history don G. N. Clarke (ex-infantryman and prisoner) tells Graves: "I can't make out my pupils at all. They are all 'Yes, sir' and 'No, sir.' They seem positively to thirst for knowledge and they scribble away in their notebooks like lunatics. I can't remember a single instance of such stern endeavor in pre-war days." The ex-service men, "scores of captains, majors, colonels, and even a one-armed twenty-five-year-old brigadier," "though not rowdy, were insistent on their rights": at St. John's "they formed what they called a Soviet, demanding an entire revision of the college catering system; they won their point and an undergraduate representative was chosen to sit on the kitchen-committee." The elder dons "who had regarded all soldiers, myself included, as their noble saviors, now recovered their pre-war self-possession. I was amused at the difference in manner to me that some of them showed. My moral tutor, however, though he no longer saluted me when we met, remained a friend." (A phrase describing the dons' wartime fear of an invasion of Oxford is summarized, not reproduced.) Mechanism: the role-reversals of E15c-12 are reversed back; the "Soviet" is a mock-political name for an organized mess grievance.
*Candidate application:* Veterans return not as rowdies but as insistent organizers (their old tool is the collective complaint), and the civilians who once deferred recover their manner; the narrator is amused.

**[E15c-56]. Blunden: "we would talk each other into an almost hysterical state about the trenches," and the agreement to write it down.** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L9927-9937)
Graves bicycles daily down from Boar's Hill with Edmund Blunden, who had permission to live there "on account of gassed lungs." "Edmund had war-shock as badly as myself, and we would talk each other into an almost hysterical state about the trenches. We agreed that we would not be right until we got all that talk on to paper. He was first with Undertones of War, published in 1928." (Graves's own book, the one being read, appeared in 1929.) Register: no joke. The stated mechanism of the shared talk is escalation (each feeding the other), and the agreed remedy is written, public, narrative.
*Candidate application:* The most precise statement in the book of what happens when two veterans talk about the war: not relief but heightening, and a joint decision that the cure is to put it on paper; the book in the reader's hand is the result.

**[E15c-57]. The "Jubilee Murderess" landlady: gallows comedy about a civilian crime (restrained).** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L9939-9964)
The Blundens rent rooms in a semi-detached cottage belonging to Mrs. Delilah Becker, "locally known as the Jubilee Murderess," who reads prayers aloud at the open window, prays for the long-dead Prince Consort and Adelaide, and was "said to have murdered her husband" in Jubilee year after he had misbehaved with a servant (the allegation is summarized). The neighbors "swore that she had buried him in the garden"; Mary Blunden wryly suggests, in a remark about an odd smell, that he is more probably buried in their half of the cottage; when she goes out the landlady lays a carving-knife on the table where they will see it as they pass; and she says of the husband's old suit that she wonders "what has happened" to it, correcting herself midword from "when he was" to "when he went away." Graves "liked Delilah very much and sent her a pound of tea every year until her death"; her stock sayings are "Fair play's good sport, and we're all mortal worms" and "While there's wind and water a man's all right." Mechanism: a local legend of an unproven killing kept alive as a shared joke by lodgers, who are themselves war-shocked; the landlady's own self-interrupting remark.
*Candidate application:* A civilian instance of the same device (euphemism that fails midword, a threat-by-placement, a sanctioned rumor) in the household of two veterans: the war-damaged lodgers appear at ease with it. (Restrained handling: a suspected domestic killing and an allusion to a decomposition smell, not reproduced.)

**[E15c-58]. Anglo-Saxon poems as trench scenes, and the involuntary scenes that "persisted like an alternate life."** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L9966-10003)
Graves finds the English Literature course tedious, and the Anglo-Saxon lecturer "candid about his subject," saying it was "of purely linguistic interest." Graves disagrees: "*Beowulf* and *Judith* seemed good poems to me. Beowulf lying wrapped in a blanket among his platoon of drunken thanes in the Gothland billet; Judith going for a promenade to Holofernes's staff-tent; and *Brunanburgh* with its bayonet-and-cosh fighting—all this was closer to most of us at the time than the drawing-room and deer-park atmosphere of the eighteenth century. Edmund and I found ourselves translating everything into trench-warfare terms. The war was not yet over for us." Then the involuntary scenes: "In the middle of a lecture I would have a sudden very clear experience of men on the march up the Béthune-La Bassée road," with French children calling for bully beef (in children's pidgin, not reproduced), "the stench of the knacker's yard," or Laventie High Street with an N.C.O.'s roar of "Party, 'shun!" and the Second Battalion men in shorts springing up, or a barn with a Welsh Regiment platoon at cards by candle stubs, or a Cambrin dug-out with a signaller, "muddy legs coming down the steps," a crash and "the tobacco-smoke in the dug-out would shake with the concussion and twist about in patterns like the marbling on books." "These day-dreams persisted like an alternate life. Indeed they did not leave me until well on in 1928. I noticed that the scenes were nearly always recollections of my first four months in France; it seemed as though the emotion-recording apparatus had failed after Loos." Mechanism: the translation of an old poem into army billet language is a joke with a purpose (it explains why it speaks to them); the flashback list is plain and visual, with the "marbling on books" simile as the one aesthetic touch.
*Candidate application:* The best compact description of how war-language filters a veteran's reading ("platoon," "billet," "staff-tent") and of the intrusive replay of early scenes (not the worst ones) for roughly ten years.

**[E15c-59]. Ex-soldiers' anti-French feeling and Blunden's "Except against the French."** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L10005-10016)
"The eighteenth century was unpopular because it was French. Anti-French feeling among most ex-soldiers amounted almost to an obsession. Edmund, shaking with nerves, used to say at this time: 'No more wars for me at any price. Except against the French. If there's ever a war with them I'll go like a shot.'" Graves adds that pro-German feeling grew once the war was over: "it was possible to give the German soldier credit for being the best fighting-man in Europe," that the blockade rather than the army beat them, "that in Haig's last push they never really broke," and "even that we had been fighting on the wrong side; our natural enemies were the French." Mechanism: a pacifist vow with a deadpan exception delivered by a shaking man; the reallocation of enemy status among veterans.
*Candidate application:* Documented: veterans after a war can re-draw the enemy line (the old ally becomes the "natural" enemy, the old enemy a respected soldier); a character can voice a vow of no more war with a comic carve-out.

**[E15c-60]. The college board's dry formulas and the tutor beaten for a Shelley.** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L9966-9973; L10018-10022)
Percy Simpson, Graves's tutor, "had been beaten by his schoolmaster for having a Shelley in his possession" and "protested between the blows: 'Shelley is beautiful! Shelley is beautiful!'" but warned Graves never to disparage the eighteenth century in the examination. At the end of Graves's first term, the college board's spokesman "coughed and said a little stiffly: 'I understand, Mr. Graves, that the essays that you write for your English tutor are, shall I say, a trifle temperamental. It appears, indeed, that you prefer some authors to others.'" Mechanism: an institution's euphemistic idiom ("a trifle temperamental") for a plain preference, in the same register as the army's. 
*Candidate application:* The civilian institution has its own euphemistic officialese; the veteran in it hears the same register in a new setting.

**[E15c-61]. Gilbert Murray's walk in sevens: "come to me again."** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L10031-10044)
Visiting Gilbert Murray, Graves asks: "Exactly what is the principle of that walk of yours? Are you trying to avoid the flowers on the rug or are you trying to keep to the squares?" and notes "I had compulsion-neuroses of this sort myself so it was easy to notice one in him." Murray, wheeling round, says "You're the first person who has caught me out"; it is a habit "of doing things in sevens," and the professor of psychology he asked "assured me it wasn't a dangerous habit. He said: 'When you find it getting into multiples of seven, come to me again.'" Mechanism: a clinician's dry limit on a symptom; the narrator's identification of a symptom in another by his own.
*Candidate application:* An ex-soldier recognizes a compulsion in a civilian because he has one, and the doctor's reply makes a joke of the threshold; recognition among the afflicted is a bond.

**[E15c-62]. Robert Nichols and the legend of the "Three Musketeers."** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L10077-10089)
Graves calls Nichols "still another neurasthenic ex-soldier": "Nichols served only three weeks in France, in the gunners, and was in no show; but he was highly strung and the three weeks affected him more than twelve months affected some people." Nichols lectured in America on British war-poets and "A legend was started of Siegfried, Robert and myself as the new Three Musketeers. Not only was Robert not in the Royal Welch Fusiliers with Siegfried and myself, but the three of us have never been together in the same room in our lives." Mechanism: the narrator's deadpan correction of a public myth about the soldier-poets.
*Candidate application:* Documented: neurasthenia is not proportional to service length, and a public legend of comradeship can be false in its basic facts; the correcting voice is mild and comic.

**[E15c-63]. The Canadian colonel's night: bridge, bathing, a castle turret and a concert party.** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L10093-10180)
At Harlech, Graves, taking his brother's place at bridge with a young ex-airman and a Canadian colonel ("about forty-five—tough, nervous, whisky-drinking, a killer"), watches the colonel declare "this is the rottenest bridge I've ever sat down to. Let's go bathing!" They bathe at 10:30 on a January night and dry themselves on pocket handkerchiefs; when the colonel proposes to "raid the concert" and "pull some of the girls out," and calls Graves "you bloody poet," Graves says: "Not yet. You've had your treat, colonel. I went bathing with you and it was cold. Now it's my treat. I'm going to take you climbing and make you warm. I want to see whether you Canucks are all B.S. or not." That diverted him. He leads the colonel up the castle rock and a ruined turret in the dark by matchlight ("The colonel swore and sweated, but followed"), by which time the colonel is "most affable—almost affectionate." The colonel then visits the concert artists, is courteous to the women but takes against the Welsh tenor, "was delighted to find that he had been for some reason or other exempted," calls him "a louse and a bloody skrim-shanker and a lot more" (and an obscene insult, not reproduced), until the tenor's wife, the soprano, threatens to throw him out. The tenor "turned courageous and shouted out something about reading the Scriptures and how morally unjustifiable it was to fight. We all laughed till we wheezed." The colonel then tries to raid a bar, is refused, "merely told the barman what he thought of the Welsh, broke a glass or two and walked out," and, walking arm-in-arm with Graves, "confided as one family man to another that his nerves were all in pieces because his missus was in the family way. That was why he had broken up the bridge-party. He had wanted her to have a good sleep. 'If we go back quietly now, she'll not wake up; we can bid in whispers.'" They return, play bridge in silence until 3:30, and the colonel falls asleep on the sofa. Graves ends: "I was sick and shaking for weeks after this." His younger brother, still at Charterhouse, "was rather ashamed of me now as a non-golfer, a socialist, and not quite a gentleman," and Graves had "assured him that I would try not to wound his feelings." Mechanism: managing an unstable, drinking ex-officer by redirecting his aggression into a physical challenge, with the narrator's own war-skills (leading, climbing in the dark) deployed in a civilian setting; the contempt for the exempted tenor is the army's old "skrim-shanker" contempt, still active; the colonel's tenderness for his wife is the reveal.
*Candidate application:* A documented portrait of a post-war ex-officer with unmanaged nerves and violence, handled by an ex-comrade by challenge and companionship; the contempt for those who did not serve persists as insult, and the comedy (the tenor's pacifist reply, "We all laughed till we wheezed") is shared by the whole party, including the target. The delayed cost to the narrator ("sick and shaking for weeks") is stated.

**[E15c-64]. The marriage failing, and Dick's "caricature likeness."** (PW-05, ch. XXVII, L10182-10199)
The second child, David, is born in March (1920); "She began to regret our marriage, as I also did. We wanted somehow to be dis-married—not by divorce, which was as bad as marriage—and able to live together without any legal or religious obligation to live together." Nancy had decided on four children in the order girl, boy, girl, boy: "She had the children in the order she intended and they were all like her drawings." Graves meets his former school friend Dick "for the last time; I found him merely pleasant and disagreeable... it seemed absurd to have ever suffered on his account. Yet the caricature likeness remained." Mechanism: dry notes of loss: a joke on planning and on a love that has gone down to a "caricature likeness."
*Candidate application:* The coolness with which the narrator describes the end of a passion (the "caricature" label returns) is the post-war counterpart of the war-time deadpan.

### I. Boar's Hill, the shop, Islip and the ex-service men (chapters XXVIII-XXIX; L10204-11055)

**[E15c-65]. T. E. Lawrence: "a fellow-soldier who had come back to Oxford for a rest," his pranks, and the toy soldier.** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10207-10289)
Meeting Lawrence at All Souls, Graves says "He was merely, to me, a fellow-soldier who had come back to Oxford for a rest after the war. But I felt a sudden extraordinary sympathy with him." Lawrence gives him audit ale, comments on his poems, which "reflected my haunted condition; the Country Sentiment mood was breaking down," and makes "a number of suggestions" which Graves adopts. On the mantel is "a four-thousand-year-old toy, a clay soldier on horseback from a child's grave at Carchemish." Lawrence's college schemes are told in military language: to plant mushrooms in a decaying quadrangle lawn "so that they would have to re-turf it altogether" (dropped when he was called away to the Middle-Eastern settlement); and "to steal the Magdalen College deer" by claiming that they were All Souls' by time immemorial: "Great things were expected of this raid. It fell through for the same reason as the other." A servants' strike "was said to have been engineered by Lawrence." Mechanism: veterans' peacetime mischief as planned operations aimed at institutions; "sympathy" between ex-combatants that Graves cannot account for and says "remained."
*Candidate application:* Documented: ex-soldiers' peacetime pranks organized as raids and ruses against an institution; two veterans who find immediate sympathy without a shared unit.

**[E15c-66]. The deserted army camps of Salisbury Plain: "a million men."** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10303-10308)
Bicycling to Devon, Graves and Nancy go "over Salisbury Plain past several deserted army camps; they had a ghostly look. There was accommodation in these camps for a million men, the number of men killed in the Imperial Forces during the war." Register: plain, no joke; the equivalence of beds and dead is given as one sentence in a travel paragraph.
*Candidate application:* The abandoned camp as an image of the dead is stated, not elaborated; the narrator's typical way of placing a large number in a subordinate sentence.

**[E15c-67]. Rank after the war: "Captain Hardy," "The Captain," and "Captain Grouse."** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10325-10328; ch. XXIX, L10832-10833, L10938-10946)
At Dorchester, Thomas Hardy asks Graves "why I no longer used my army rank." "I said it was because I was no longer soldiering. 'But you have a right to it; I would certainly keep my rank if I had one. I should be very proud to be called Captain Hardy.'" At Islip, "The villagers called me 'The Captain,' otherwise I had few reminders except my yearly visit to the standing medical board." Osbert Sitwell's gift of a brace of grouse arrives in a bag labeled "With Captain Sitwell's compliments to Captain Graves"; Graves cannot face plucking and gutting and gives them to a neighbor, and answers: "Captain Graves acknowledges with thanks Captain Sitwell's gift of Captain Grouse." Mechanism: the retired rank as a courtesy title that the old novelist envies and the ex-officers turn into a pun, the bird elevated by rank.
*Candidate application:* Documented: after service the rank survives as a joke among ex-officers (a title bestowed even on a grouse), as a villager's nickname, and as a thing others think the veteran has a right to; the narrator's own reason for dropping it is that he is "no longer soldiering."

**[E15c-68]. Hardy on critics who miss the joke, "S, N, and C," and sending young men to war.** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10408-10449)
Hardy complains that the critics accuse him of pessimism, that one cited as an example of gloom "a poem he had written about a woman whose house was burned down on her wedding-night," and says: "Of course it is a humorous piece," and the man "must have been thick-witted not to see that." He had gone through his last book marking poems S, N, and C "according as they were sad, neutral or cheerful. I found them as nearly as possible in equal proportions; which nobody could call pessimism." When Lawrence disparaged the *Iliad*, Hardy answered "Oh, but I admire the *Iliad* greatly. Why, it's in the *Marmion* class!" and "Lawrence could not at first believe that Hardy was not making a little joke." (Earlier at tea, "Hardy... began making jokes about bishops," adding that "he considered bishops were fair game.") On the war: "He told us of his work during the war, and said that he was glad to have been chairman of the Anti-Profiteering Committee... 'It made me unpopular, of course... but it was a hundred times better than sitting on a military tribunal and sending young men to the war who did not want to go.'" Mechanism: an old writer's insistence that a dark comic poem is a joke; ambiguity of intent (Lawrence cannot tell); the anti-tribunal remark ties the civilian side to the officer-narrator's war.
*Candidate application:* A documented complaint about dark humor being read as gloom, and an authorial method (a three-letter tally) for judging tone; the old man's remark casts the tribunal members as the ones who sent the young to war.

**[E15c-69]. The Boar's Hill shop: "Shop-Keeping on Parnassus," "Playing at Robin Hood," and the tricks of trade.** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10454-10620)
Nancy proposes a village shop in a built timber hut (army huts being unobtainable "at any reasonable price"); the *Daily Mirror* advertises it with the heading "Shop-Keeping on Parnassus." Graves labels the scene: "Another caricature scene: myself, wearing a green-baize apron this time, with flushed face and disordered hair, selling a packet of Bird's Eye tobacco to the Poet Laureate with one hand and with the other weighing out half a pound of brown sugar for Sir Arthur Evans' gardener's wife." Takings reach £60 a week. "The moral problems of trade interested me. Nancy and I both found that it was very difficult at this time of fluctuating prices to be really honest; we could not resist the temptation of undercharging the poor villagers of Wootton... and recovering our money from the richer residents. Playing at Robin Hood came easily to me. Nobody ever caught us out; it was as easy as shelling peas, the shop-boy said." They also pass off a cheaper tea as the dearer when stock runs out. Commercial travelers tell them tricks: "never to cut cheese or bacon exactly to weight, but to make it an ounce or two more and overcharge." Mrs. Masefield buys a tin of Vim and a packet of Lux weekly, paying cash from a cash-box she carries, "to show that there was no ill-feeling." The enterprise is closed after six months with a loss (about £500 in debt reduced to about £300); Nancy's father sends a £100 note in a match-box and Lawrence supplies the rest by giving Graves four chapters of *Seven Pillars* to sell in the United States. Mechanism: the narrator treats the venture as another "caricature scene" and treats the dishonesty as civic charity ("Robin Hood"); the shop-boy's idiom matches the army-era idiom for ease.
*Candidate application:* The ex-officer's wartime habits (improvisation, lying "easy") applied to petty trade, narrated with amused self-indulgence; "caricature scene" is a reusable self-label.

**[E15c-70]. "War horror overcame me again": the choice not to be cured.** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10551-10570)
"War horror overcame me again. The political situation in Europe seemed to be going from bad to worse. There was already trouble in Ireland, Russia and the Near East. The papers promised new and deadlier poison gases for the next war. There was a rumor that Lord Berkeley's house on Boar's Hill was to become an experimental laboratory for making them. I had bad nights. I thought that perhaps I owed it to Nancy to go to a psychiatrist to be cured; yet I was not sure. Somehow I thought that the power of writing poetry, which was more important to me than anything else I did, would disappear if I allowed myself to get cured; my *Pier-Glass* haunting would end and I would become merely a dull easy writer. It seemed to me less important to be well than to be a good poet. I also had a strong repugnance against allowing anyone to have the power over me that psychiatrists always seemed to win over their patients. I had always refused to allow myself to be hypnotized by anyone in any way." He decides "to see as few people as possible, stop all outside work, and cure myself," reading psychology and applying it, having learned "the rudiments of morbid psychology from talks with Rivers, and from his colleague, Dr. Henry Head." Register: no humor; the paragraph's trigger is a newspaper item about gases, and the reasoning is about art and control, not symptoms.
*Candidate application:* A direct statement of a veteran's refusal of formal treatment because he equates the symptom with the source of his creative work and fears surrendering control to the clinician: a documented non-treatment pattern with a stated rationale.

**[E15c-71]. Dr. Head: "Typical, typical! Capital, capital!" and the explanation of ghosts.** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10571-10586)
Graves admires Head's "scientific integrity": testing a man he suspected of homicidal mania, Head "made some suggestion to him which came within the danger-area of his insanity; the man picked up a heavy knife from his consulting table and rushed threateningly at Head. Head, not at all quick on his legs and dodging round the table, exclaimed: 'Typical, typical! Capital, capital!' and, only as an afterthought: 'Help! Help!'" Head also explained why some people see ghosts by the bed: "They always come to the same side of the bed, and if you turn the bed round you turn the ghost round with it. They come to the contrary side of the lesion." Mechanism: the clinician's absorption in his case overriding his self-preservation, delivered as an anecdote with an afterthought; the ghost explanation reduces a haunting to neurology. (Restrained handling: a knife attack on a doctor, summarized.)
*Candidate application:* A documented clinician's gallows humor (professional delight in a symptom while being attacked), and a medical deflation of the ghost motif that the memoir has used since E15b-12 ("Ghosts were numerous in France at the time").

**[E15c-72]. The "no-cottage": staff-work for a house, and irony at the agent's desk.** (PW-05, ch. XXVIII, L10627-10660)
Nancy asks Graves to find a cottage in three weeks; he says "ironically: 'Describe it in detail; since there are no cottages we might as well have a no-cottage that we really like.'" She gives an exact specification (six rooms, water, beamed attic, walled garden, river, tower not spire, ten shillings a week). He lays "a ruler across the Oxford ordnance-map," finds villages of the right bearing and distance on the river, learns which have shops and which a towered church, then asks a house agent for exactly that cottage at Islip: "The house-agent laughed politely." The agent then says the description matches the "World's End Cottage," for sale not rent; Nancy sees it and says "Yes, this is the cottage all right," and, told they lack the money, "If we could find the exact house, surely to goodness we can find a mere lump sum of money." His mother buys it and rents it to them at the same ten shillings. Mechanism: the irony of "no-cottage" turned into a staff exercise (map, ruler, reconnaissance) that succeeds; the agent's "laughed politely" is the institutional response.
*Candidate application:* The ex-officer's reconnaissance technique (ruler on the map) applied to housing, undertaken with irony and rewarded by absurd luck; the comic register here is warm.

**[E15c-73]. Islip football, reintroduced by "the ex-soldiers."** (PW-05, ch. XXIX, L10691-10711)
"There had been no football in Islip for some eighty years; the ex-soldiers had reintroduced it." A ninety-year-old villager complains it is "unmanly" compared with the village game of his boyhood (he claims three deaths in one match; the telling is summarized, and he concludes "Her was a grand game"). Graves, center-forward, "was often booed now for charging the goalkeeper while he was fumbling with the shot he had saved," because the Charterhouse game he knew was rougher. The football club "was democratic. The cricket club was not." Mechanism: the veterans' sport as a village institution revived by the war's survivors; the narrator's rough style is a leftover of the school and the army; the old man's tall tale is comic.
*Candidate application:* Documented: ex-service men reintroducing a team sport in a village as a civic act and a leveling one (the club "democratic"), and the former officer's physical roughness being booed.

**[E15c-74]. "Reminiscential neurasthenia": the yearly medical board and the railway warrant.** (PW-05, ch. XXIX, L10830-10838)
"Nancy begged me not to talk about the war in her hearing, and I was ready to forget about it. The villagers called me 'The Captain,' otherwise I had few reminders except my yearly visit to the standing medical board. The board continued for some years to recommend me for a disability pension. The particular disability was neurasthenia; the train journey and the army railway-warrant filled out with my rank and regiment usually produced reminiscential neurasthenia by the time I reached the board. The award was at last made permanent at forty-two pounds a year." Mechanism: the narrator's coinage ("reminiscential neurasthenia") names, with deadpan precision, a symptom brought on by the forms and travel of the claim itself.
*Candidate application:* Documented: the bureaucracy of a pension reproduces the symptom for which the pension is paid, and the veteran's account of this is dry; the household's rule (no war talk in Nancy's hearing) is stated alongside it.

**[E15c-75]. Ex-service men at the door, and Daisy's account of the begging trade.** (PW-05, ch. XXIX, L10839-10893)
"Ex-service men were continually coming to the door selling boot-laces and asking for cast-off shirts and socks. We always gave them a cup of tea and money. Islip was a convenient halt between the Chipping Norton and the Oxford workhouses." One, an out-of-work steam-roller driver whose wife had died in childbirth, brings three children, and Nancy takes in his thirteen-year-old daughter Daisy to train for service; Daisy hates school, is teased and fights, and describes the begging trade in her own idiom: "Good beggars always ast for something particlar, something they seen lying about. It's no good asking for food or money." The child she carried was an asset: she used to say to door-slammers "This is my little orphan brother." Daisy was "artful," says her father sings with her *On the Road to Anywhere*, and describes the workhouse "Spike" as "our winter home." One day a tramp comes to the door and Daisy, to the Graveses' shock, turns him away: "I know you, Nosey Williams," she said, "you and your ex-service papers what you pinched from a bloke down in Salisbury, and the bigamy charge against you a-waiting down at Plymouth. Hop it now, quick, or I'll run for the cop." She tells "the true histories of many of the beggars," saying "There's not one decent man in ten among them bums" and that most are on the road because "the cops have something against them, so they has to keep moving." In spring her father calls for her; she later writes to ask for money. Mechanism: an adolescent's insider debunking of a begging trade that trades on veteran status, with forged service papers a known device; the Graveses' charity is amused and shocked at the same time.
*Candidate application:* Documented: after the war, the ex-serviceman is a recognized figure at the door (a "halt" on the workhouse circuit), and forged "ex-service papers" are an item in the beggars' trade; the child's cool exposure of this is the comic center.

**[E15c-76]. The parish council: cottages for ex-soldiers, the cenotaph versus the recreation ground, and "farmers who had stayed at home."** (PW-05, ch. XXIX, L10979-11008)
Graves sat on the parish council for a year ("I wish I had taken records of the smothered antagonism of the council meetings"). Disputes: an application for new cottages ("Many returned ex-soldiers who wished to marry had nowhere to live with their wives"), opposed by the Conservative members "because it would mean a penny on the rates"; and a recreation ground, opposed on the same grounds, with the Conservatives noting the village after the Armistice had "turned down a recreation ground scheme, preferring to spend the memorial subscription money on a cenotaph." "The Labor members pointed out that the vote was taken, as at the 1918 General Election, before the soldiers had returned to express their view. Nasty innuendoes were made about farmers who had stayed at home and made their pile, while their laborers fought and bled. The chairman calmed the antagonists." He closes with the "caricature scene": "myself in corduroys and a rough frieze coat, sitting in the village schoolroom... debating as an Oxfordshire village elder whether or not Farmer So-and-so was justified in using a footpath across the allotments as a bridle-path, disregarding the decayed stile which, I urged, disproved his right." Mechanism: a quarrel between a memorial and a living amenity, with the veterans' absence at the vote as the grievance; the narrator's own parody of himself as a village elder.
*Candidate application:* Documented civic form of the grievance between those who served and those who stayed: a monument voted in before the soldiers returned.

**[E15c-77]. The war-memorial service: a different reading, and medals kept "buttoned up inside their coats."** (PW-05, ch. XXIX, L11009-11027)
The rector asked Graves "to speak from the chancel steps of the village church at a war memorial service" and "suggested that I should read poems about the war." "Instead of Rupert Brooke on the glorious dead, I read some of the more painful poems of Sassoon and Wilfred Owen" about men dying of gas poisoning and the bodies of the dead in the mud (summarized; the phrase in the text is graphic and is not reproduced). "And I suggested that the men who had fallen, destroyed as it were by the fall of the Tower of Siloam, had not been particularly virtuous or particularly wicked, but just average soldiers, and that the survivors should thank God that they were alive and do their best to avoid wars in the future. The church-party, with the exception of the rector, an intelligent man, was scandalized. But the ex-service men had not been too well treated on their return and it pleased them to be reminded that they were on equal terms with the glorious dead. They were modest men: I noticed that though obeying the King's desire to wear their campaigning medals, they kept them buttoned up inside their coats." Register: no humor; the episode is the plain-spoken counterpart to the Peace Day speech of E15c-49, and the men's medals are the plain sign of how they carry decoration. 
*Candidate application:* Documented: the same veteran who gave the "glorious dead" speech in 1919 gives a flat, anti-glory reading in the early 1920s, to the scandal of the respectable and the quiet approval of the other veterans, who wear their medals out of sight.

### J. The end of the Islip years, the novel he could not finish, and the Egyptian interlude (chapters XXX-XXXII; L11099-12083)

**[E15c-78]. "The death of my friends was following me in peace-time as relentlessly as in war"; the army friends fall away.** (PW-05, ch. XXX, L11183-11228)
Graves notes that after 1926 "I saw no more of any of my army friends, with the exception of Siegfried, and meetings with him were now only about once a year." Blunden had gone to a chair in Tokyo, Lawrence was in the Royal Tank Corps (having enlisted in the Air Force and been made to leave when a question about his assumed name was threatened in the House), and "When Sir Walter Raleigh died I felt my connection with Oxford University was broken, and when Rivers died, and George Mallory on Everest, it seemed as though the death of my friends was following me in peace-time as relentlessly as in war." A friend, Sam Harries, "died of cerebral malaria" in India a week or two after arrival; a young woman friend died in childbirth after a midwife failed to sterilize her hands (Graves had assisted at the birth of his own fourth child, Sam, and says he "could not have believed that a natural process like birth could be so abominable in its pain and extravagant messiness"). "Many deaths and a feeling of bad luck clouded these years. Islip was no longer a country refuge. I found myself resorting to my war-time technique of getting through things somehow, anyhow, in the hope that they would mend." Register: plain; no joke; the deaths are told in a run, with the narrator's one comparative sentence about war and peace.
*Candidate application:* The post-war friend-network thinning by ordinary deaths, with the same sense of pursuit as in the line, and the survival technique brought back for domestic troubles, is a documented late-time-course pattern: the army friendships do not persist as social ties, and the exception (Sassoon, once a year) is a single fixed point.

**[E15c-79]. The novel that brought back neurasthenia: "the poison of war-memories."** (PW-05, ch. XXX, L11291-11300)
"I also made several attempts during these years to rid myself of the poison of war-memories by finishing my novel, but I had to abandon them. It was not only that they brought back neurasthenia, but that I was ashamed at having distorted my material with a plot, and yet not sure enough of myself to retranslate it into undisguised history. If my scruples had been literary and not moral I could easily have compromised, as many writers have since done, with a pretended diary stylistically disguising characters, times, and dates." He says he had the same difficulty with a school novel in his last year at Charterhouse. Mechanism: no joke; a plain account of writing as a hazard (returns the symptom) and a moral objection to the fictional device. (Compare ch. XII, L3369, where he says he "wrote it as a novel" and has "now to retranslate it into history.")
*Candidate application:* Documented: attempts at therapeutic writing may bring the illness back, and an ex-soldier-writer may reject fictionalizing his war on moral, not literary, grounds; this is the stated reason the finished book is "undisguised history."

**[E15c-80]. Lawrence's letter on the Egyptian job, and Sassoon's "The Image" at the quay.** (PW-05, ch. XXXI, L11308-11367)
Lawrence writes of the post: "I think the coin spins evenly" (it may be interesting or terrible); it will not "drive you into frantic excesses of rage"; "Execute it decently, as long as you draw the pay, and enjoy your free hours (plentiful in Egypt) more freely"; "I recommend the iced coffee at Groppi's. And so, my blessing." (Lawrence's descriptions of Egyptians and of the expatriate society are condescending and are not reproduced; see register.) At the departure, Sassoon, seeing Graves off, tells him: "Do you know who's on board? 'The Image!' He's still in the regiment, going to join the battalion in India. Last time I saw him he was sitting in the bottom of a dug-out gnawing a chunk of bully-beef like a rat." Graves says this man (a school and regiment acquaintance, wounded at High Wood at the same time) is "a man with whom I had nothing in common; there was no natural reason why we should have been thrown so often in each other's company." At Gibraltar "I remembered the cancelled War Office telegram and thought what a fool I had been to prefer Rhyl." Mechanism: a friend's one-line caricature of a comrade by a wartime image (the rat in the dug-out); a nickname ("The Image") kept after the war; the narrator's own memory of a wartime plan he had abandoned.
*Candidate application:* Documented: ex-comrades pass on wartime pictures of a third man as shorthand and nickname, and the comradeship is circumstantial ("no natural reason") but persistent; a calm, ironic advisory letter from one veteran to another on a civilian job ("execute it decently").

**[E15c-81]. Veteran status as a social key in Cairo: the pension-list canteen, "bled in the cause of civilization and France," and the barrack-square voice.** (PW-05, ch. XXXI, L11373-11433)
To cut grocery bills Graves shops at the British army canteen: "I presented myself as an officer on the pension list." The Belgian dean of the faculty, "tough, witty," had "acquired a certain slyness and adaptability during the war when, as a civilian in Belgium under German occupation, he had edited an underground anti-German publication." The one-legged professor of French literature "greeted me at first in a patronizing way: I was his young friend rather than his dear colleague. But as soon as he learned that I also had bled in the cause of civilization and France I became his most esteemed chum." British officials instruct Graves to "keep the British flag flying" in the faculty; "This embarrassed me." In the one weekly lecture he "often had to shout at the top of my loudest barrack-square voice to restore order." He observes that university correspondence is in classical Arabic and that "The 'very learned Sheikh' Graves had to take his letters to the post office for interpretation," and that his lectures "in the end became a dictation of lecture-notes for lectures that could not be given." Mechanism: the pension and the wound as passes to canteens and to collegial warmth; the parade-ground voice persisting in the lecture hall.
*Candidate application:* Documented: veteran status is traded as a social currency among ex-combatants of different nations (a wound for France), and as a bureaucratic one (the pension list at the canteen); the habits of command (the loud voice) re-emerge when a civilian classroom becomes disorderly.

**[E15c-82]. The satirical document: an examination letter, "contemporary comments pinned to the letter," and the claim that sarcasm is respected (restrained handling of ethnic condescension).** (PW-05, ch. XXXII, L11610-11878)
Graves reproduces a letter listing the English works read by an Egyptian teachers' diploma class (an odd mix of Dryden, Pope, Goldsmith, *Macbeth*, Conan Doyle and a popular-science book) and "contemporary comments, pinned to the letter," including three student essays (on evolution, on Lady Macbeth, on the best use of leisure) printed with the students' errors. Among the comments is a claim about management: "The most satisfactory way to treat him is with a good-humored sarcasm, which he respects; but if he once gets politically excited nothing is any use but an affected violent loss of temper." The commentary ends by quoting phrases from the student's essay back as a prediction about his own "professional successors": "fatigable and fond of leisure." Mechanism: satire by quotation of official documents and students' own phrases, in a long-established British colonial-comic genre; the narrator's theory of handling classes (sarcasm, performed anger) echoes the staged ambush and staged anger of the Royal Welch mess (E15b-27). (Restrained handling: the passage is built on ethnic and national generalizations about Egyptians and mockery of non-native English errors; documented here as a form, not reproduced or endorsed.)
*Candidate application:* The device of a juxtaposed official document and a cutting private gloss, and the "affected" (performed) loss of temper as a management tool, are recorded as the author's habits of mockery after the army; nothing here is about the war.

**[E15c-83]. Lord Lloyd at bridge: "All right."** (PW-05, ch. XXXII, L11914-11923)
After calling on the High Commissioner at the end of the academic year and being invited to dine, Graves wins twenty piastres from him at bridge. "He asked me how I found Egypt and I said: 'All right,' with an intonation that made him catch me up quickly. 'Only all right?' That was all that passed between us. He believed in his job more than I did in mine." Mechanism: a two-word deadpan, with a loaded intonation, delivered to a superior who then asks the one question; the narrator's closing sentence puts the difference in belief.
*Candidate application:* The old rank-negotiation device (a minimal, loaded reply to a senior's question) survives into a civilian appointment as the narrator's way of declining enthusiasm without insubordination.

**[E15c-84]. The levee and the soiree: the "soldier's eye," the bathos of meeting the King, three campaigning medals and the missing St. Anne.** (PW-05, ch. XXXII, L11932-11985)
At King Fuad's levee, Graves, expecting a throne room and "Eastern magnificence," meets a quiet Turkish-looking gentleman in Court-dress and then is shown to the cloak-room: "I had already met King Fuad. And no Eastern magnificence or wheezing cry." On the staircase stand enormous black soldiers with lances: "My soldier's eye commented on their somewhat listless attitudes; but, no doubt, they pulled themselves smartly to attention when the Egyptian army general staff went past." The French professors "arrived in full evening dress with swallowtail coats and white waistcoats," with decorations round their necks: "They looked like stragglers from an all-night fancy-dress ball." At the royal soiree he claps the best comedian and is told it is a command performance. "I was wearing Court-dress again but, not to be outdone by the Frenchmen, I had put on my three campaigning medals—and wished that I had that St. Anne of the Third Class with the Crossed Swords" (the Russian award cancelled by the revolution, E15c-32). He pockets confections for his children. Mechanism: a veteran's inspection eye applied to ceremonial guards, with a polite caveat; decorations as one-upmanship at a civilian court; a callback joke about an award that never came.
*Candidate application:* Documented: after service, military habits of inspection and a sense of decorations as status competition survive; campaign medals are worn only when the civilian company is showing off its own.

**[E15c-85]. "There are plenty of caricature scenes to look back on in Egypt": the faculty row, "The Limit," and the closing flourish.** (PW-05, ch. XXXII, L12009-12076)
Graves again uses his label. In the Faculty Conference Room, "blustering" at his colleagues in English, he declares "so far as I am concerned all dead Frenchmen can go bury themselves at their own expense" in support of the professor of Latin, who refuses a forced contribution to a funeral wreath, while the colleagues mutter "Inoui." A list of anecdotes follows: a clerk run over by the Minister of Justice's son; a rich girl complaining of poor value at fifteen guineas a week; an English cotton manufacturer who "defended the conditions in his factory on the ground that the population of Egypt since the British came had been increasing far too rapidly, and that pulmonary consumption was one of the few checks on it"; and a fortune-teller, "'The Limit,' so named by Australians during the war, who told my three years' fortune by moonlight under the long shadow of the pyramid of Cheops—told it truly and phrased it falsely." The list ends with a stage bow: "(Empty cigar-box—no applause—and I thank you.)" (Some items of the list are ethnic or sexual caricature and are not extracted.) Mechanism: the list as showman's patter; a war nickname ("The Limit") surviving into a foreign city; and the manufacturer's dark-comic callousness reported without comment.
*Candidate application:* The same label ("caricature scenes") as in the wedding, the shop and the parish council recurs here; the war-era nickname "The Limit" (given by Australians) is an example of nickname-as-biography, carried to a civilian setting.

### K. "The story trails off here": the ending, the disavowals and the epilogue (L12085-12357)

**[E15c-86]. Why the book ends where it does: "gradual disintegration."** (PW-05, "The story trails off here" section, L12085-12092)
After the return from Egypt, the narrator writes: "The story trails off here. But to end it with the return from Egypt would be to round it off too bookishly, to finish on a note of comfortable suspense, an anticipation of the endless human sequel. I am taking care to rob you of this. It is not that sort of story. From a historical point of view it must be read, rather, as one of gradual disintegration. By the summer of 1926 the disintegration was already well-advanced. Incidents of autobiographical pertinence became fewer and fewer." Register: plain; he refuses a comic or consoling ending.
*Candidate application:* The author tells the reader the story of the post-war years is not a recovery narrative, and says so in the book's own terms.

**[E15c-87]. Lawrence's "Please sell when read," Shaw's postcard, and "all my academic manners" lost.** (PW-05, "Trails off" section, L12094-12133)
Back in England and out of money, Graves sells books, and "Lawrence came to the rescue with a copy of his *Seven Pillars* marked, 'Please sell when read,' which fetched over three hundred pounds." When Graves, at a publisher's suggestion, begins a book about Lawrence, George Bernard Shaw sends a postcard: "A great mistake. You might as well try to write a funny book about Mark Twain. T. E. has got all out of himself that is to be got.... Write a book (if you must) about the dullest person you know; clerical if possible. Give yourself a chance." The book sells at ten thousand copies a week, and Graves hears that "Shaw had mistaken me for my Daily Mail brother." Meanwhile, rejected for an adult-education lectureship after a nine-month delay, "I had lost all my academic manners and wrote wishing the entire committee in Baluchistan, to be tickled to death by wild butterflies." Mechanism: a comrade's deadpan inscription as a gift; an elder writer's wrong guess; a tame-sounding but extravagant curse of a committee delivered after the manners have gone.
*Candidate application:* Documented: the post-war veteran's loss of civilian restraint comes out as a precisely-worded, absurd curse of an institutional board; and a comrade's help is given as a joke inscription.

**[E15c-88]. The 1927 collected poems as disavowal, and a poem on the terror of death.** (PW-05, "Trails off" section, L12135-12155)
In 1927 Graves publishes a selective collected volume, "intended as a disavowal of over half the poetry that I had so far printed." He quotes the opening of "one of the later ones":
> This, I admit, Death is terrible to me,
> To no man more so, naturally.
> And I have disenthralled my natural terror
> Of every comfortable philosopher
> Or tall dark doctor of divinity.
> Death stands at last in his true rank and order.
He likens his action to Skelton's wish to "scrape out the scrolls" and "erase it for ever out of her ragman's rolls," but allows anthologists to reprint the rejected pieces "if they paid highly enough" ("that was their business and I was glad of the money") and stops publishing new poems in periodicals. Mechanism: the opening admits terror in plain declarative form ("naturally"), dismisses consoling authorities ("comfortable philosopher," "doctor of divinity"), and ends on a phrase with a military ring (Death in his "true rank and order"); the paragraph's disavowal of earlier work matches E15c-39 on "falsities for public delectation."
*Candidate application:* A veteran-poet's late statement that death is terrible and that he has outgrown the comforts offered; "rank and order" has a military ring applied to death. Quoted at length because the example is the point.

**[E15c-89]. The author's own note on method: "No incidents ... are invented or embellished," "what I was, not what I am," and "that was literature."** (PW-05, "Trails off" section, L12157-12181)
"On May 6th, 1929 Nancy and I suddenly parted company." He began the autobiography on 23 May and writes these words on 24 July, his thirty-fourth birthday; "another month of final review and I shall have parted with myself for good." On sources: "I have been able to draw on contemporary records for most of the facts, but in many passages memory has been the only source. My memory is good but not perfect." He remembers songs after two hearings "but there are always odd discrepancies between my version and the original." "No incidents, however, are invented or embellished. Some are, no doubt, in their wrong order; I am uncertain, for example, of the exact date and place of the megaphoned trench-conversation between the Royal Welch and the Germans, though I have a contemporary record of it. I am not sure of some of the less-important names (even of Lance-corporal Baxter's, but it was at least a name like Baxter). To avoid the suggestion of libel I have disguised two names. Also, I make a general disclaimer of such opinions as I have recorded myself as holding from chapter to chapter ... This is a story of what I was, not what I am. Wherever I have used autobiographical material in previous books and it does not tally with what I have written here, this is the story and that was literature." Mechanism: the narrator's statement on reliability, with a joke on a forgotten name ("at least a name like Baxter"); the claim that earlier, published versions were "literature" while this one is "the story."
*Candidate application:* An important source-reliability note for every finding in E15a-E15c: the author asserts no invention but admits error in order and names, disguises two names, and distinguishes this book from his earlier war writing as "story" versus "literature." (See the Gaps section for the tensions.)

**[E15c-90]. The closing catalog: "taken life ... and been killed," "seen a corpse that has died a natural death," and "learned to tell the truth—nearly."** (PW-05, "The End" paragraph, L12183-12204)
The narrator wonders "whether it is justified as a story" and lists what he has done "by the age of twenty-three": "been born, initiated into a formal religion, traveled, learned to lie, loved unhappily, been married, gone to the war, taken life, procreated my kind, rejected formal religion, won fame, and been killed." (The last item evidently refers to the false report of his death after the July 1916 wounding, told earlier in the book at L7609-7615 and annotated by fn. [8]; the closing paragraph does not explain it.) At thirty-four, "many things still remain undone," and the second list runs through things never done: never "been on a journey of exploration, or in a submarine, aeroplane or civil court of law (except a magistrate's court on the charge of 'riding a vehicle, to wit a bicycle, without proper illumination, to wit a rear lamp')," never "mastered any musical instrument, starved, committed civil murder, found buried treasure," and (two sexual items, summarized) and "or seen a corpse that has died a natural death." Things done: "ridden on a locomotive, won a prize at the Olympic Games ... been examined by the police on suspicion of attempted murder, passed at dusk in a hail-storm within half a mile of Stromboli when it was in eruption, had a statue of myself erected in my lifetime in a London park, and learned to tell the truth—nearly." The book ends: "The End." Mechanism: a comic checklist in the form of a storybook résumé in which "taken life" and "been killed" stand beside "procreated my kind" and "rejected formal religion" with equal weight, and "civil murder" and "a corpse that has died a natural death" are the things not experienced: the war's deaths are treated as the ordinary ones. (Restrained handling: two sexual items in the list are summarized.)
*Candidate application:* A compact, documented example of gallows humor as closing flourish: a life summarized as a checklist where the killings and his own reported death sit in an ordinary row, and where the lack of "a natural death" is the joke; "nearly" marks the one place the register admits a limit.

**[E15c-91]. The Dedicatory Epilogue: "soldier's oaths," a joke after a near-fatal event, and a surgeon's remark (restrained handling).** (PW-05, "Dedicatory Epilogue to Laura Riding," L12209-12300)
The epilogue, addressed to the poet Laura Riding, explains that Riding is left out of the body of the book so the last chapters "have a ghostly look," and that the silence is false "if it makes the book seem to have been written forward from where I was instead of backward from where you are." Graves recounts a sudden April 1929 journey to Ireland, on the same Fishguard boat that had been his hospital boat twelve years before, then back via Rouen "to recollect the hill-top where you seemed to die as the one on which I had seemed to die thirteen years before," and his sudden arrival at a farm at Hilton to burst in upon David Garnett, "scandalizing him with my soldier's oaths as I denied him a speaking part in your parable." Of 27 April 1929 the text says only "a fourth-story window and a stone area and you were dying"; it then says that "it was a joke between Harold the stretcher-bearer and myself" that she survived (the content of the joke is not reproduced), and recalls that on 16 May a surgeon, Mr. Lake, is reported to have made a dry professional remark in the operating theater about the unusual sight of the exposed spinal cord (the wording and the anatomical detail are not reproduced). Each item is followed by "After which." Mechanism: the narrator's soldier's oaths and a stretcher-bearer's joke return in a 1929 crisis, with the surgeon's professional appreciation as the clinical counterpart; the repeated "After which" is a ratchet toward the paragraph where anecdotes stop (next finding).
*Candidate application:* Documented: in a crisis years after the war, the same registers recur (soldier's oaths, a stretcher-bearer's joke, a surgeon's dry professional comment) and are described as "anecdotes ... so precious to me as vulgar glosses" on the crisis; the narrator needs them. (Restrained handling: a near-fatal event at a window, not explained in the text, a joke about it, and a surgical remark about an exposed spine.)

**[E15c-92]. "After which, even anecdotes fail": "It is enough now to say that I have endured."** (PW-05, "Dedicatory Epilogue," L12297-12310)
The narrator mentions his story *The Shout*, which "has left you out entirely. And, because you are left out, it is an anecdote of mine." Then: "After which, even anecdotes fail. No more anecdotes. And, of course, no more politics, religion, conversations, literature, arguments, dances, drunks, time, crowds, games, fun, unhappiness. I no longer repeat to myself: 'He who shall endure to the end, shall be saved.' It is enough now to say that I have endured. My lung, still barometric of foul weather, speaks of endurance, as your spine, barometric of fair weather, speaks of salvation." Register: the book's last words are plain and elegiac; the "anecdotes" (his word for the comic, narrative units of the whole memoir) are said to fail, and the list of what ends includes "fun" and "unhappiness" together.
*Candidate application:* The author's explicit terminal statement that anecdote, which has carried the whole memoir, stops working when the crisis passes a threshold; humor and unhappiness end together; endurance is stated plainly as the achievement. This is the clearest final-position evidence in the collection for the limit of the anecdotal-humor mode.

**[E15c-93]. End matter: the footnotes, the author's erratum, and the Skelton cobbler.** (PW-05, footnotes and erratum, L12313-12357)
Footnote [1], a jest from *Merry Tales of Skelton*, early sixteenth century, attached to a passage in the range of E15a: a cobbler asked to go "in the King's wars" and "to be killed for my labor" is called a coward by Skelton and answers "No," said the cobbler, "I am not afeared: it is good to sleep in a whole skin." Footnotes [2]-[5] are as in E15a/E15b ([2] "I do not know what happened to Miller. This was written in the summer of 1916."; [3] "Jenkins was killed not long after."; [4] "The quartermaster excepted."; [5] the order forbidding any word but "accessory" for the gas). Footnote [6]: a major recovered quickly "and was back at the Middlesex Depot after a few weeks," whereas a Royal Welch company quartermaster-sergeant "was hit in the neck that day by a spent machine-gun bullet which just pierced the skin, and died of shock a few hours later." Footnote [7]: a man "was recommended for a Victoria Cross but got nothing because no officer evidence, which is a condition of award, was available." Footnote [8]: "I cannot explain the discrepancy between his dating of my death and that of the published casualty list." The erratum corrects a statement about "the Rupert Brooke Fund" (the Islip money paragraph, ch. XXIX, L10958-10963, which is not otherwise extracted here): "there is not really any 'Rupert Brooke Fund' administered by Mr. Marsh. I much regret this error which arose from an imperfect recollection. R. G." Mechanism: footnotes record the flat afterthought, the dry exception and the unfair rule (VC denied for lack of officer witnesses); the erratum is the author's own example of a false statement in the text.
*Candidate application:* The footnotes follow the memoir's habit of recording a death or an award in a short, plain, late-placed clause; the erratum is evidence that a confident memoir sentence ("administered by Mr. Marsh") can be wrong from recollection, a caution for every "exact" quotation of events in the book.

## Pattern roll-up

*(What this unit establishes, from one officer-memoir by one narrator; "Graves" below means the narrator as he presents himself, with events of 1917-26 written up in 1929. Finding numbers are in brackets. This part of the memoir is mostly about life after the fighting; the lens headings below are therefore weighted toward time course, civilian-facing register, and humor without a unit.)*

**Time course (the main yield of this unit).**
- *Last weeks at the front, winter 1916-17:* the worst phase is narrated with almost no jokes (E15c-1), a rum ration removed by a teetotal general is credited with raising the sick list (E15c-2), and the narrator's last dead man in France is a suicide, "like the first" (E15c-8). The staff's offensive aims are read by the trench soldiers as aesthetics ("the passion for straight lines," E15c-7); local pride is expressed as horse-theft (E15c-9).
- *Hospital and convalescence, 1917:* humor is made on purpose and described as work. Officers at a neurasthenic-heavy convalescent home found a mock society with an invented ritual (the whiskers on the table, E15c-18), stage a corpse hoax on civilian officials (E15c-19) and label the setting a "caricature scene" (E15c-16); the narrator says "Anything to make people laugh. But it was hard work" (E15c-19). The hospital nickname "Dottyville" is shared by two patients as "a great joke" (E15c-25).
- *Garrison and cadet duty, 1917-18:* the narrator's nerves are worse than he admits (E15c-28), his phobias are named exactly (gas smelled in flowers, a tire's burst, E15c-31), and he schemes through medical categories to reach a front where gas is unknown. Drafts going to France are cheerful in the spring battles and irony is their weapon (E15c-30).
- *The Armistice:* the one passage where there is no joke at all; Graves walks alone, "cursing and sobbing and thinking of the dead," as two named friends' deaths arrive with the news (E15c-40).
- *First civilian year, 1919:* the body and habits still belong to the war: shells bursting on the bed, dead friends' faces on strangers, hills seen as battlefields, commandeering, lying, a billet checklist applied to a room, language "foul ... ever since" Loos (E15c-50). The first public duty is a "glorious dead" speech that he has since forgotten (E15c-49). The wedding, the discharge farce and the nonsense verse invaded by a regimental march are told as comedy (E15c-33, -42, -46).
- *Oxford and Boar's Hill, 1919-21:* with Blunden he talks "into an almost hysterical state" and they agree to write it down (E15c-56); scenes from the first four months in France replay unbidden in lectures "like an alternate life," until "well on in 1928" (E15c-58); he translates old poems into trench terms (E15c-58); ex-soldiers' opinion re-draws the enemy (E15c-59); the narrator takes a flat "war horror" decision against psychiatric cure for the sake of his poetry (E15c-70).
- *Islip, 1921-25:* the war has become a yearly medical board (the train journey and warrant "produced reminiscential neurasthenia," E15c-74), a village nickname ("The Captain," E15c-67), ex-service beggars at the door (E15c-75), a cenotaph voted before the soldiers returned (E15c-76), and one memorial service at which he reads the painful poems instead of Brooke (E15c-77). The wife asks him not to talk about the war (E15c-74).
- *1926-29:* "gradual disintegration" (E15c-86); army friends gone but one, and "the death of my friends was following me in peace-time as relentlessly as in war" (E15c-78); attempts to write the war novel bring neurasthenia back (E15c-79); the book ends with a comic checklist that includes "taken life" and "been killed" and "seen a corpse that has died a natural death" (E15c-90), then an epilogue where "even anecdotes fail" (E15c-92).

**Civilian-facing register.**
- The narrator's own early verse "contain[s] falsities for public delectation": a sentimental poem about a French woman and a china plate is contradicted by the money-and-ownership truth (E15c-39); the book itself distinguishes "the story" from "literature" (E15c-89), and corrects a confident sentence in an erratum (E15c-93).
- What civilians receive: a speech about "the glorious dead" at the 1919 Peace Day (E15c-49); polite, then rejected, answers to questions like Russell's (E15c-13); the Wells "Cook's Tour" view (E15c-15); Galsworthy's request for soldier slang for authenticity (E15c-15); a minister's parliamentary denial of executions against twenty reports on file (E15c-5); "Business as usual" and "Oh, but that was in France" (E15b-34).
- The decision to withhold: "I have forgotten what I said" (E15c-49); "a verse letter which I cannot quote" (E15c-38); the war novel abandoned (E15c-79); "Nancy begged me not to talk about the war" (E15c-74).
- The one occasion of plain speech to civilians is political and flat: Sassoon's declaration (E15c-23) and Graves's own memorial reading (E15c-77); neither contains a joke.

**Functions.**
- *Coping:* the mock-society and the corpse hoax (E15c-18, -19); the two-poets' trade in politicians' sayings ("How splendid this attrition is!"; "The necessary supply of heroes must be maintained at all costs," E15c-31); "Dottyville" (E15c-25); the comic checklist of the book's end (E15c-90); the narrator's coined phrase for his symptom (E15c-74).
- *Cohesion among veterans:* Blunden's talk and quip (E15c-56, -59); Lawrence's immediate "sympathy" and pranks (E15c-65); the colonel at Harlech and the tenor's pacifist reply that "we all laughed till we wheezed" (E15c-63); Sassoon's "The Image" nickname (E15c-80); Sitwell's "Captain Grouse" (E15c-67); the French professor who becomes "his most esteemed chum" once he learns Graves "bled in the cause of civilization and France" (E15c-81).

**Rules of license.**

**Limits and failure modes (where the register turns plain).** Suicide (E15c-8); executions and capital charges (E15c-4, -5); the gassed civilian and the near-faint (E15c-28); the Armistice (E15c-40); the friends' deaths (E15c-38, -78); the list of symptoms (E15c-50, -51); the refusal to be cured (E15c-70); the uncle's cattle (E15c-45); the epilogue's "even anecdotes fail" (E15c-92). **Defector, dissent and exit.**
- *The act itself:* Sassoon's plain declaration, written to commanding officers, with no humor (E15c-23); the "rigged" board and the nickname "Dottyville" turn the same event into a farce (E15c-24, -25); the one jest at the center of the dissent is sending his book "for a joke" to Haig (E15c-22).

- *How an ordinary exit is made:* a farce of paperwork and a hurried train (E15c-46); a uniform put away in a school trunk (E15c-49); a resignation "at once" once the narrator's "new loyalty" overtakes the regimental one (E15c-42); and the lingering identity of rank-as-title (E15c-67).
- *Humor without a unit:* the narrator's civilian humor is built on solitary forms (the caricature label, pun, the closing checklist) and on a few veteran friendships, not on a group; the friendships thin to one annual meeting by 1926 (E15c-78).

**Civilian-facing and veteran-to-civilian translation (summary).** The memoir records five translations: (1) the narrator gives civilians what they want and later forgets it (E15c-49); (2) gives the plain version when it is a political act (E15c-23, -77); (3) gives the funny version about himself and not about the war (E15c-33, -69, -85); (4) tells the civilian world's own incongruities in the army's vocabulary (E15c-41, -50, -58, -72); (5) says nothing to the wife about the war by agreement (E15c-74) and says it all to another veteran (E15c-56).

**Cross-cultural / regimental differences.** One British officer; but also: the Welsh-Irish banter of the Limerick garrison (E15c-41); the Canadian colonel and Canadian troops at Rhyl (E15c-63, -48); an Australian-given nickname in Cairo (E15c-85); French and Belgian colleagues whose war service functions as a bond (E15c-81); Egyptian students and officials treated in the colonial register of the period (E15c-82, -84, -85), documented here only as a form.

**Craft observations (what the memoir's own method shows).**
- The dead go in the last clause (E15c-11, -34, -38); so do the awards (E15c-21, -93).
- The narrator marks what he will not give: "I have forgotten what I said" (E15c-49); "I forget how I answered" (E15c-36); "which I cannot quote" (E15c-38); "I cannot explain the discrepancy" (E15c-93).
- A recurring, self-assigned label ("caricature scene") turns a life event into a scene whose incongruity the reader is invited to see, and each use sits at a moment of social strain (a wedding, a shop, a parish meeting, a faculty row).
- Humor is placed against its limit within a few lines: the mock society beside a gloomy neurasthenic ward (E15c-18); the "great joke" of Dottyville beside the board rigging (E15c-24, -25); a gas-mask scene and a joke about a rabbit (E15c-28, -29); the haunted-house comedy and the dying mother (E15c-38); Delilah and the Blundens (E15c-56, -57).
- The closing sequence (E15c-86 to -92) takes the memoir from "It is not that sort of story," to a comic checklist, to "After which, even anecdotes fail," to "I have endured": the reader is shown the end of the humor as the end of the book.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Range boundaries and overlap.** My range starts mid-chapter XXII at L8170; E15b's range ends at L8190, so the first twenty lines overlap and nothing in them is extracted twice (the winter-1916-17 items begin at L8170 in my E15c-1). E15a's file was in a draft state (source-shape placeholders) and E15b's file ended at E15b-41 (L5440) when I read them, so cross-references to their finding numbers may need re-checking after those passes are finished.
- **This is not the front.** From L8340 Graves is out of France; no first-hand trench humor of 1917-18 is available here; the war's last year appears through letters and drafts. "Late war" in the brief is, in this source, a home-front and hospital war.
- **One narrator, one class, one regiment, one decade.** Anglo-Irish-German, public-school, officer, poet; patterns cannot be generalized to other armies, ranks or later wars. Post-war humor is documented for **one man's** civilian life, which became a literary life; the humor in it is partly literary (puns, allusions, the "caricature scene" device), not necessarily what a non-writer veteran would use. Almost none of it is about the war itself: the war is the **unspoken** subject (E15c-74) or the **recurrent intrusion** (E15c-50, -58).
- **Reliability.** The author says "No incidents ... are invented or embellished" (E15c-89), but also says his earlier published war writing was "literature" and this book "the story," concedes errors in order and names (including a name he can place only as "a name like Baxter"), admits he disguised two names, corrected a false statement in an erratum (E15c-93), says that after the war "it was always easier for me now ... to lie my way out" (E15c-50), and says his poem about the French woman and the plate was false (E15c-39). Earlier in the book (ch. XII, L3369, in E15a's range) he says of his 1916 war account that he "wrote it as a novel" and has "now to retranslate it into history." Reports by others that the book exaggerates or misremembers are outside this file and are **not verified here**. Treat all dialogue as reconstruction, not as verbatim speech.
- **Sassoon's letters are mediated.** Everything attributed to Sassoon's letters in my range (E15c-20, -22, -35, -36, -37) is Graves's summary, sometimes in indirect speech. Sassoon's declaration (E15c-23) is given in full by Graves from a newspaper cutting; I did not compare it with other printings.
- **The causes of some events are not given.** The text says only that Sassoon "had been shot through the head" on a daylight patrol (E15c-38); the epilogue's near-fatal event at a window (E15c-91) is not explained in the text; I did not add outside knowledge.
- **Civilian comic anecdotes not extracted (not humor about the war).** The bishop and jellyfish (partly in E15c-34), the audit-ale prince and the Lindsay verse at All Souls, the oil-financier's chairs, Mrs. Masefield and the rabbit, the Anglo-Saxon lecturer's candor, the Islip nonagenarian (only in part), the autograph hunter, Mr. Hicks the showman, Sir Lionel Philips and Kimberley, Mr. Wise and "squitch," the Egyptian student's marriage and the divorce-and-inheritance joke, the white silk socks, the millionaire on the balcony, the Egyptian examination essays (only as a form), the camels and the royal soiree's refreshments. Some are comic and some are cultural or sexual material that I left out for that reason (see the register).
- **Colonial, ethnic and religious material.** The Egyptian chapters (L11308-12076) and some Irish, Welsh, Indian and Jewish remarks (L9487-9512, L11126-11145, L10815-10816) rest on the period's generalizations. I extracted only items that bear on veteran identity, rank, medals or the narrator's registers, and summarized the rest. The narrator's own statement of "a strong prejudice, amounting to contempt, against anyone of non-European race" (L11126-11129) is **not extracted**.
- **Line numbers.** Citations are to the local text file as numbered by the Read tool; ranges are generally exact to within a few lines. Headings of some sections are my own labels (the coda and the epilogue have no numerals).
- **Length.** This extraction runs well above the 10-18% guideline because the post-war chapters were flagged as the collection's best direct evidence on humor after service and the brief required recording them exactly. A reader wanting a short version should start at E15c-40 and read to E15c-92.
