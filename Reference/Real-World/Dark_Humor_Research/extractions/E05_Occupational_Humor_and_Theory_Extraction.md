## Findings

### From AC-07 (Kuiper 2012, humor and resiliency; theory/review, not military)

**[E05-1]. Gallows humor in emergency work: what the case reports say it does.** (AC-07, [p.3])
Kuiper begins the evidence with anecdotal and case-report studies of people who meet extreme events at work: police, firefighters and medical staff, with examples from emergency rooms and AIDS-service organizations. Such workers often use cynical or "gallows humor" to release tension, vent feelings and cope. The proposed mechanisms are (a) detaching or distancing the self from the event and (b) fostering group cohesion and social support, both in order that personnel can still act effectively in the traumatic setting. Kuiper's own methodological verdict is that these reports are rich in descriptive detail and very true to real conditions, but offer almost no experimental control, so no firm conclusions follow.
*Candidate application:* For a soldier-type character, the documented job of gallows humor is operational as well as emotional: it keeps the person able to work. The ex-soldier's humor can be written as a skill whose original purpose (function under load) outlasted the load.

**[E05-2]. Failure modes already visible in the same case reports: masking and alienating.** (AC-07, [p.3], [p.9-10])
The same case reports describe uses of humor that are bad for the user: "inappropriately masking emotions or pain, or alienating others by using cynical humor" (p.3, citing Kosenko and Rintamaki 2010 on AIDS-service workers). Kuiper adds that in that source the humor used to mask emotion or pain left working relationships less effective (p.9-10). Kuiper's criticism is that these reports mostly gave the negative effects little thought, because they had no theoretical model that could hold both the helpful and harmful findings together; the proposed answer is the humor styles model (E05-9).
*Candidate application:* The masking failure mode is a way to show a character who is "fine" and funny while the humor is doing concealment work; the alienation failure mode is a way to show the same cynicism costing standing with people who are not in the club.

**[E05-3]. The physical-health cost: more humor, worse cardiovascular and lifestyle risk in a police cohort.** (AC-07, [p.3], [p.13])
Kuiper cites a longitudinal study of police officers (Kerkkänen, Kuiper and Martin 2004; the reference title says "three-year longitudinal study of Finnish police officers") in which officers who showed greater humor were at increased risk of cardiovascular disease, greater body mass and more smoking. Kuiper calls this a serious challenge to a simple humor-equals-resilience link. The closing section generalizes: humor's benefits show up much more for psychological well-being than for physical health, which is called a particular challenge to resiliency models that still needs explaining. (The text copy gives no sample size or effect sizes for the police study.)
*Candidate application:* A character can be mentally upright and funny while the body keeps a separate ledger of the cost; the chronic-injury and lifestyle-wear details of an ex-soldier need not be "explained" by humor but can run alongside it.

**[E05-4]. Naturalistic evidence under bombing and bereavement.** (AC-07, [p.3-4])
(a) Civilians who lived through bombing and air raids (Gavrilovic and colleagues 2003) used humor among their coping strategies, and its use was linked with fewer post-traumatic "intrusion" symptoms a year later; Kuiper's caveat is that the strategies were analyzed together and measured retrospectively, so the humor effect cannot be isolated. (b) A stronger prospective design (Ong, Bergeman and Bisconti 2004) followed 34 older widows after a spouse's death, measuring humor coping, perceived stress, positive affect and depression separately: more humor in the period after the death was linked to greater emotional resilience, specifically a better ability to keep positive emotions distinct from negative ones, and fewer depressive symptoms. (c) Elderly Japanese women talking with friends about illness or a spouse's death converted painful self-disclosures into humorous comments that could then be shared appropriately (Matsumoto 2009).
*Candidate application:* The "positive and negative emotions kept separate" finding gives a gentle, non-pathologizing picture of what grief-humor does: it lets a person stay in a sad situation with a second emotion running beside it.

**[E05-5]. Humor and survival (epidemiology), with an age limit.** (AC-07, [p.4])
In a Norwegian two-year prospective study of patients with end-stage kidney disease, those with a greater sense of humor had survival rates averaging 31 percent higher. In a seven-year prospective study of more than 50,000 residents of one Norwegian county, higher sense of humor was linked with significantly reduced hazard ratios, regardless of gender and of current health (poor or good), but the benefit became much less pronounced above age 65.
*Candidate application:* Only useful as evidence that "humor protects" is a real research claim with an age boundary; do not treat it as a rule for any character.

**[E05-6]. Stress buffering: high coping-humor people do not sag as stressors pile up.** (AC-07, [p.4-5])
The moderator finding that the firefighter study (AC-08) builds on: Martin and Lefcourt (1983) predicted, and found, that people high in coping humor showed little or no rise in negative mood as the number of negative life events went from low to high, whereas low coping-humor people showed a marked rise. The proposed route is distancing from the stressors. Kuiper notes the buffering effect has been replicated in other studies. A related finding (Kuiper, Martin and Dance 1992) is that high-humor people also kept their positive affect up as negative events increased (low-humor people dropped significantly), and only high-humor people gained positive affect as positive events increased.
*Candidate application:* The buffer concept is the quantitative form of "the funny one holds up better under accumulated load." A character whose humor has gone silent (see failure modes) can be shown as someone who has lost the buffer, and the load starts to show.

**[E05-7]. Appraisal studies: humor changes how the event is read, not only how it feels.** (AC-07, [p.4-5], [p.10-11])
Several laboratory and survey studies tie high coping humor to more positive "challenge" appraisals (and lower "threat" appraisals) of upcoming stressors: an exam (Kuiper and colleagues 1993); a drawing task, where higher coping humor also went with more positive affect, motivation and effort (Kuiper, McKenzie and Belanger 1995); and everyday problems, where a high-humor group reported less stress and anxiety than a low-humor group despite equal numbers and types of problems, attributed to more positive reappraisal (Abel 2002). Geisler and Weber (2010), using an unsolvable bogus intelligence test, found humor use raised external attributions for failure (blaming circumstances) and positive affect right after the test. **But the same study found the humor users spent less time and did worse on a subsequent test**, the reverse of the bounce-back that resilience predicts (p.10). Kuiper's point is that the humor-appraisal-affect-behavior chain can behave very differently even over short periods.
*Candidate application:* Humor can feel like recovery while the next performance suffers: a good model for a character who jokes their way out of a failure and then does not do the work it should have prompted.

**[E05-8]. Mechanism: humor is distancing and reappraisal that does not deny the event; it can both lower the bad feeling and add a good one.** (AC-07, [p.10-12])
Kuiper locates the most common explanation in distancing: a cognitive shift that offers a less threatening perspective on the situation. Following Geisler and Weber, Kuiper treats humor as an "antecedent-focused cognitive-change" strategy (it changes how the situation is interpreted before the emotion fully forms) and says it differs from other cognitive strategies in that it "does not deny the negative experience" but construes it as less threatening (p.11). Two effects are distinguished: down-regulating (reducing the expression and consequences of negative emotion) and, possibly, up-regulating (the mirth itself introduces positive emotion, which lessens the gravity of the situation and widens attention to positive features of the surroundings; this is linked to the broaden-and-build theory). Kuiper presents the proposed causal sequence (humor leads to mirth, mirth to flexibility and a wider range of experience, and this to resilience) as hypothesis to be tested, not as established.
*Candidate application:* This is the cleanest statement of why deadpan horror-humor can coexist with full awareness: the speaker is not denying the event; the distance is the tool. A character who loses the distance (humor stops working) is losing the tool, not the feeling.

**[E05-9]. The humor styles model: four styles, two helpful and two harmful.** (AC-07, [p.7-8])
Kuiper adopts Martin and colleagues' (2003) model of sense of humor as four individual-difference styles. *Affiliative:* warm, non-hostile jokes and spontaneous banter that amuse others respectfully; adaptive; strengthens relationships, reduces conflict, raises group morale. *Self-enhancing:* maintaining a humorous perspective on life to handle personal stress; adaptive; protects the self without costing self or others. *Aggressive:* teasing, sarcasm, ridicule and disparagement with no regard for the effect on others; maladaptive; used to put others down, and consistent use can alienate people and impair relationships. *Self-defeating:* excessive self-ridicule, being the butt of jokes and laughing along when put down, in hope of approval; maladaptive; a futile bid for relationships at the expense of self-regard, and it can hide negative feelings about the self and avoid dealing with problems like low self-esteem and emotional neediness. The standard measure is the 32-item Humor Styles Questionnaire (8 items per style). Reported correlates: affiliative and self-enhancing humor with lower depression and anxiety, higher positive affect and self-esteem; self-defeating humor with the opposite; aggressive humor with hostility and sarcasm, and with the tendency to criticize and manipulate. The four-style structure has been found across European, North American, Middle Eastern and Eastern samples. (Kuiper stresses the styles are individual differences: some people typically use the two adaptive styles, others the two maladaptive ones.)
*Candidate application:* A usable vocabulary for differentiating soldier-type characters by humor *style* rather than by how dark the jokes are: two people can both joke about death, one affiliatively and one aggressively. Note that the model does not directly classify "dark" content; it classifies *what the humor is doing to self and others*.

**[E05-10]. How listeners respond to each style, and a subtle harm.** (AC-07, [p.8-9], [p.10])
Studies cited by Kuiper find people are less likely to want future interaction with someone showing higher self-defeating or aggressive humor than with someone showing more self-enhancing or affiliative humor, and that the adaptive humor comments left the recipient feeling more positive and less negative about themselves. In dating couples, partners' greater affiliative and lesser aggressive humor went with more relationship satisfaction, closeness and better conflict resolution (Campbell, Martin and Ward 2008). Some, not all, of these interpersonal effects held across cultures. Kuiper also flags a subtler harm: people who reported high use of both affiliative and aggressive humor scored higher on racial prejudice and social dominance, and showed a nonchalant attitude to humor that downplayed harm and helped mask bias (Hodson and colleagues 2010). (Described only in general terms here; the source gives no joke text.)
*Candidate application:* "Warm" banter and "cutting" banter can sit in one person; the nonchalant "it's only a joke" posture is itself the documented disguise for bias, which suggests a moderator for how a character's easy humor reads to outsiders.

**[E05-11]. Humor skills can be trained (civilian evidence), with open questions.** (AC-07, [p.12])
Kuiper notes most writing on training humor use is anecdotal and speculative. One more rigorous study (Crawford and Caltabiano 2011) randomly assigned community volunteers to a humor-training group, a social control group or a no-intervention group; over eight sessions, the humor group alone gained in positive affect, self-efficacy, optimism and sense of control (maintained at a three-month follow-up), with reduced perceived stress, depression and anxiety. A pilot with clinically depressed adults pointed the same way (Falkenberg and colleagues 2011). Open: whether people with aggressive or self-defeating styles can be taught to swap to the adaptive styles, and whether people low on all four styles are good candidates.
*Candidate application:* Humor as a trainable resource is a documented idea; it fits a culture or faction that deliberately cultivates it (and one that treats it as trade skill).

**[E05-12]. Kuiper's conclusions and caveats.** (AC-07, [p.6], [p.9], [p.12-13])
Humor has an important but bounded role in resilience: case studies, moderator research and appraisal work converge that more humor can help people cope with traumatic situations, and humor increases positive experience and positive affect. But there is "very limited empirical support" for the stronger claim that humor leads to personal growth or bounce-back (p.13). Past positive-psychology failures to find humor effects (a null finding in a 149-adolescent longitudinal sample, Gillham and colleagues 2011) may be because the humor measure counted only the positive side. Much existing work is cross-sectional; Kuiper asks for longitudinal, diary-based designs that record daily affect and daily use of each style.
*Candidate application:* The honest scientific position is "humor often helps with distress, inconsistently helps with recovery, and has costs": a sound footing for writing a character whose humor is neither a cure nor a symptom.


### From AC-08 (Sliter, Kale & Yuan 2014, coping humor in firefighters; civilian emergency responders, not military)

**[E05-13]. The sample and design.** (AC-08, [p.1], [p.6])
A two-wave survey of firefighters in one large Midwestern U.S. city, three months between waves, with the outcome measures collected at the second wave and absenteeism taken from department records. 685 survey packets went to every firefighter working 24-hour suppression shifts; 208 took part at Time 1 and 179 returned usable Time 2 surveys (the analysis N). The authors state a 29 percent response rate (but see Gaps: the figures given work out nearer 26 to 30 percent depending on which wave is counted). Final sample: all male, average age 48.0 (SD 6.66), 79 percent white, average 20.9 years as a firefighter (SD 6.91), 60 percent rank-and-file, 27 percent lieutenants, the rest higher rank. The department had barely hired for about ten years, so the workforce was older and more experienced. Three job types took part: pumper (fire suppression), hook-and-ladder (victim rescue) and rescue squad (first responders to fires, medical emergencies and car accidents). Wave 1 was mid-January, in snow country, when weather-related calls (crashes, falls on ice, heating fires) push exposure to trauma up. The Fire Chief issued a formal order encouraging participation and promising confidentiality; participants received small payments ($15 for the first wave, $25 for the second).
*Candidate application:* The profile (older, long-serving men in a hierarchical emergency service whose chief ordered participation) is the closest civilian analogue in this unit to a career-service population; but the study is a survey of traits and symptoms, not a record of what anyone actually says or jokes.

**[E05-14]. What was measured as "coping humor," and what was NOT.** (AC-08, [p.3], [p.6-7])
Coping humor is defined as the trait tendency to use humor to deal with stressful or demanding situations; it is distinct from optimism and from positive mood because it names a behavioral strategy rather than a mood tendency. It was measured with the 7-item Coping Humor Scale (Martin and Lefcourt 1983; reliability .71; 4-point agreement scale), whose items ask about looking for something comical to say in tense situations and finding something to joke about in trying ones. Trauma exposure was a 17-item firefighter-specific checklist (developed with firefighter focus groups) asking how often, over the last month, events such as fatal car accidents, fires causing death or injury, or being in danger of death or serious injury had occurred (1 never to 5 extremely often). **Gallows humor was not measured at all**, nor was humor shared in a group; this is a self-rated personal trait.
*Candidate application:* Whatever this study shows about "coping humor," it does not show that group-level dark joking is protective; do not use it as evidence about a unit's culture.

**[E05-15]. Exposure to traumatic events predicted all three outcomes.** (AC-08, [p.8], [p.10])
Controlling for experience and negative affectivity (the tendency to feel bad in general), more traumatic-event exposure at Time 1 predicted more PTSD symptoms (standardized b = .20, p < .01), more burnout (.17, p < .05) and more days absent (.20, p < .01) at Time 2. In the Impact of Event Scale, with cutoffs the authors say are validated to indicate likelihood of a diagnosis (and explicitly not a diagnosis itself): 30 percent of the firefighters were at no risk, 43 percent had a possibility (likely partial symptoms), 21 percent had about a 75 percent chance and 5 percent a near-100 percent chance of meeting PTSD criteria. Average days absent over the three months was 1.23 (SD 1.31), median 1, most common value 0. The authors note this firefighter PTSD link is less consistent in earlier studies (some found none) and that the absenteeism link had not been supported previously.
*Candidate application:* In a career emergency-service population, roughly a quarter showed a high likelihood of PTSD on this screening instrument while all were still working active suppression shifts; this is a useful reality check against a "functional but carrying it" character profile.

**[E05-16]. The buffer finding: humor weakened the exposure-to-symptoms link, for PTSD and burnout.** (AC-08, [p.8-10])
For both PTSD and burnout the exposure-by-coping-humor interaction was significant (standardized b of magnitude .25, p < .01 in each; the minus sign is lost in the text copy, but the authors' simple-slope results below show the direction; the interaction adds 5.7 percent of the variance in PTSD and 5.9 percent in burnout beyond controls, exposure and humor). Simple-slope tests: for PTSD, exposure predicted symptoms strongly among low coping-humor firefighters (b = .40, p < .001) but not significantly among high coping-humor ones (b = .12, not significant); for burnout, low coping humor b = .37 (p < .01) versus high b = .03 (not significant). In plain terms: among firefighters who rated themselves high in coping humor, heavier trauma exposure was not reliably linked to worse symptoms three months later; among low-humor firefighters it was.
*Candidate application:* The moderator logic is "humor's job is to break the dose-response line between what you see and what it does to you." It supports writing characters with the same exposure whose outcomes differ by habit of mind, without making the habit a virtue.

**[E05-17]. Humor did not buffer absenteeism, and the authors give three possible reasons.** (AC-08, [p.9], [p.11])
No moderating effect on absence. The authors offer: (1) humor may protect the affective and cognitive consequences (burnout, PTSD) and not the behavioral one; (2) the absence measure is noisy (absences can be voluntary or involuntary, and on-the-job-injury days were excluded), so power may be low; (3) humor may simply not reduce absenteeism, a real null. They did not measure why people were absent; they note theory that absence works as avoidance and distancing from the stressor.
*Candidate application:* A character can be a warm, funny presence and still be the one who stays away; humor does not obviously keep someone at the job.

**[E05-18]. Why humor might buffer: three proposed routes, none tested here.** (AC-08, [p.4-5], [p.12])
(a) *Reappraisal:* under the transactional theory of coping (Folkman and Lazarus), high coping-humor people appraise stressors in a more positive light, ruminate and re-experience less, and may avoid cognitively disengaging from the job; humor is characterized as an emotion-based strategy that yields fewer negative and more positive emotions. (b) *Social bonding:* high-humor people are reported as less lonely and more likeable, which builds belonging and social support; the authors cite nurse-patient rapport and, for absence, the finding that camaraderie and a relaxed workplace go with lower absenteeism. (c) *Physiology:* laughter's relaxation effect, likened to exercise, and endorphin release; the authors themselves note these may not apply directly to an emergency context. They concede (p.12) that none of the three mechanisms was explicitly tested, so how humor buffers remains "up to interpretation"; their claim is only that the basic buffering effect is established as a first step.
*Candidate application:* The three routes give three different failure modes to write: reappraisal fails if the event is too large to reframe; bonding fails when the person leaves the group (an ex-soldier loses route b entirely); physiology is the weakest of the three for emergency settings.

**[E05-19]. Military-adjacent evidence cited, secondhand.** (AC-08, [p.3])
In passing the authors cite an Israeli Defense Forces study (Bizi, Keinan and Beit-Hallahmi 1988) finding that trait humor, as rated by peers, was positively related to performance under stress; also that military studies show combat exposure harms well-being (Day and Livingstone 2001; Vinokur and colleagues 2011). These are mentioned in a sentence each; this source does not describe their methods or sizes.
*Candidate application:* A pointer to chase if peer-rated humor under stress ever needs a military-specific basis; peer rating (not self-rating) is the interesting feature, since a soldier's humor reputation is made by others.

**[E05-20]. What the authors say about gallows humor (not measured, flagged for future work).** (AC-08, [p.11], [p.13])
They define "black" or "gallows" humor as humor used in the face of, or in response to, a grim or hopeless situation, most common among people who face death and dying regularly (police, firefighters, medical staff). Drawing on others, they relay that it is often described as "the only way to get by" (Garrick 2006), "a method for maintaining sanity in an 'insane' situation" (Kuhlman 1988) and as giving people a sense of control over the uncontrollable (Thorson 1985). They say anecdotal evidence shows firefighters often use it and that it may be effective but "should be empirically examined." In future directions they suggest that the *verbalized* form of gallows humor may be a group-level construct that differs from work group to work group and occupation to occupation, and may have different moderating effects than general coping humor.
*Candidate application:* The group-level, workgroup-specific nature of gallows humor is an authorial hypothesis, not a finding; it fits a design where every unit has its own humor culture rather than one "military humor."

**[E05-21]. Practical implications and the weakness of debriefing.** (AC-08, [p.11-12])
The authors observe that employers often provide debriefing or counseling on request, but that typical post-incident debriefing has been shown to be of little effectiveness (citing Harris, Baloglu and Stacks 2002), and propose that coping training with an emphasis on humor (restructuring the situation, reappraisal) may be a place to start. They note training programs have mostly been tried with clinical groups (a pilot with people with depression), with promising generalization beyond the training context.
*Candidate application:* The idea of formal "debrief culture" being weaker than informal peer practice is worth testing as a world feature, but only as a cited position here, not as established.

**[E05-22]. The authors' own limits.** (AC-08, [p.12-13])
Self-report for all but absenteeism, so common-method bias possible (they randomized survey order, controlled negative affectivity, and separated predictor and outcome waves); no control for lifetime prior exposure (firehouses differ in call volume); confidential but not anonymous, so self-selection out of the study possible; the three mechanisms untested; coping humor measured as a stable trait (the authors note it can be trained); all-male, one-city, older sample.
*Candidate application:* None; recorded so these results are not stretched.


### From AC-12 (Bitterly, Brooks & Schweitzer 2017, humor and status; civilian workplace and laboratory experiments, NOT military)

*Reading note: the text copy of this article is badly damaged. The first five pages interleave two columns with a diagonal publisher watermark, and all minus signs, "less than" and "greater than" symbols and Greek letters were stripped. I rely on the clean tables (Tables 2-4, Appendix A) for numbers and on the prose for direction. Findings below say so where it matters.*

**[E05-23]. The core claim: a joke attempt is a risky, costly signal.** (AC-12, [p.1-3])
Across eight experiments (plus two pilots) the authors argue that an attempt at humor changes how others rate the teller's status, in both new and existing relationships, and that the change can go either way. The mechanism has two channels. (1) Merely *attempting* a joke signals **confidence**, because the teller risks falling flat or giving offense and cannot know in advance what an audience will accept (worst with an unfamiliar audience). (2) Only a *successful and appropriate* joke signals **competence**, because it shows the teller read the room, saw the opening and delivered. Status rises when both signals are positive. The authors define a humor attempt as one person's effort to amuse; they treat a joke as involving three roles (the expresser, the target and the audience, which can overlap, as in teasing or self-deprecation), and they use the "benign violation" account of what makes something funny: it must breach some norm (physical, linguistic, social or moral) and also feel safe or harmless enough not to offend. They say plainly that earlier research looked almost only at successful humor and that failed attempts, which are common, had been neglected.
*Candidate application:* For a soldier-type character, this is a clean model of why a risky joke is also a bid for standing: willingness to say the dangerous thing shows nerve, and only reading the room correctly shows judgment. A character who has been out of the unit for years has lost the room-reading input while keeping the nerve.

**[E05-24]. Joking at work is close to universal (Pilot 1).** (AC-12, [p.4])
200 employed adults recruited online (118 male, 82 female; average age 29.4) were asked to recall a joke a coworker had told. Only one participant (0.5 percent) could not recall one; 74 percent of the recalled jokes had been heard within the past month; participants agreed (about 5.4 on a 7-point scale) that their coworkers frequently make jokes and (about 5.0) that other coworkers make similar jokes. Rated appropriateness averaged 4.94 of 7 (SD 1.90), which spreads from clearly appropriate to clearly not (the percentages given for the two sides of the midpoint, 27 and 70 percent, lost their inequality symbols in the copy; I read them as below and above the midpoint). So both appropriate and inappropriate jokes are common in ordinary workplaces.
*Candidate application:* Background normality: even in civilian offices, the distribution of jokes straddles the appropriateness line; a unit whose jokes cross it more often is a difference of degree, not of kind.

**[E05-25]. Successful, appropriate humor raises status in a brand-new group, and shifts a behavioral measure too (Studies 1a, 1b).** (AC-12, [p.5-8])
*Study 1a:* 160 adults in a lab (66 male, 94 female; average age 24.9), in 16 sessions of 6 to 15 people, each wrote a customer testimonial; two confederates (actors) read testimonials aloud, the first always serious and the second either serious or humorous (a pet-waste-service testimonial with a small joke). Participants rated the humorous presenter higher on status (about 5.0 vs 4.4 on a 7-point index), competence (5.3 vs 4.9) and confidence (5.6 vs 4.7); the humorous version was rated slightly less appropriate than the serious one (5.2 vs 5.7) but well above the midpoint, not inappropriate. *Study 1b:* 190 participants, another testimonial and joke (a small joke about a national flag), plus a behavioral measure: participants could allocate 25 points to presenters (or keep them) to say who should lead a later group task. The humorous presenter received more leadership points (about 6.7 vs 4.9; the point means have large spreads, SD about 6.3 and 4.8) and higher attitudinal status (4.46 vs 4.23). Competence mediated both status measures; confidence mediated the attitudinal measure but not the leadership points. Gender of participants showed small inconsistent effects (men rated higher in 1a, lower in 1b on attitudinal status).
*Candidate application:* A small, funny, harmless remark in front of strangers changes who they would follow. In a fresh unit the joker is in the running for informal leadership from day one.

**[E05-26]. In an existing relationship, recalling a successful joke raises a coworker's perceived status; a failed one does not lower it (Study 2a).** (AC-12, [p.9-10])
120 online participants (70 percent male; average age 31.5) listed five coworkers they had known less than a year, then, for the third, recalled either the last greeting (serious), the last appropriate joke they found funny (successful), or the last appropriate joke they found *not* funny (failed). Status ratings: successful joke 4.94, failed joke 3.94, serious comment 4.18 (successful higher than both; failed vs serious not significantly different). The coworker's rank relative to the participant (senior, equal, subordinate) did not change the pattern. Affect (positive or negative mood measured by a standard 20-item scale) did not mediate it. The authors flag limits: recall may have shifted traits salient in memory, and participants might have misattributed a joke to the wrong coworker.
*Candidate application:* Reputation for being funny is partly built from remembered successes; "the one who's funny" is a status asset that outlasts any single joke, and a mild joke that dies was not penalized in this design (but see Study 4b, E05-29, where a failed appropriate joke did lose status).

**[E05-27]. Confidence from trying, competence only from succeeding (Study 2b).** (AC-12, [p.10-11])
274 online participants imagined a job interview where the manager asks "where do you see yourself in five years" and the candidate gives a serious answer, or a mild joke that the manager laughs at, or the same joke met with silence. Status: laugh 4.95, silence 4.22, serious 4.15. Competence: 5.21, 4.63, 4.73 (only the laugh condition higher). Confidence: 6.10, 5.52, 4.73 (all three differ: trying the joke raised confidence even when it fell flat). Both confidence and competence mediated the status effect in the laugh condition; in the silence condition the confidence effect was positive and the competence effect was not. The authors note that this "failed" joke was not a large failure (it was funny and appropriate), which may be why it did not lower status relative to the serious answer.
*Candidate application:* In three of the four designs that tested it (2a, 2b, 4a), an in-bounds joke that fell flat cost nothing measurable relative to not joking, which suggests a safety margin for gentle humor; Study 4b is the exception (E05-29), and E05-28 shows where the real danger zone starts.

**[E05-28]. When the joke is inappropriate, the competence penalty outweighs the confidence gain (Studies 3a, 3b).** (AC-12, [p.11-13])
The authors repeated the interview design with jokes pilot-tested as inappropriate for the setting: a stock sexual-innuendo catchphrase in Study 3a (274 online participants; 57 percent male) and a sexually objectifying quip about a receptionist in Study 3b (228 lab participants; 42 percent male). In both, the candidate who joked was seen as *more confident* but *less competent*. Study 3a status: serious 4.58, joke met with laughter 3.86, joke met with silence 3.30 (all three differ); competence 5.08, 3.92, 3.27; confidence 5.25, 5.70, 5.46 (laughter condition above serious; silence condition in between, not different from either). Study 3b status: serious 4.20, laughed-at 3.97 (not significantly different from serious), silence 3.27 (lower than both); competence 4.65, 3.69, 3.06; confidence 4.64, 5.52, 5.37. Competence mediated the status loss; the audience's laughter softened but did not erase the damage in 3a, and in 3b laughter brought status back to roughly the level of not joking. The authors' reading: an inappropriate joke tells the audience the teller does not understand or follow social boundaries.
*Candidate application:* Confidence-with-poor-judgment is a recognizable social type; in a character, an inability to feel where the line is can produce exactly this signature (bold, funny, and quietly discounted).

**[E05-29]. Funniness and appropriateness are separate dials (Studies 4a, 4b).** (AC-12, [p.13-16], Tables 4-5)
*Study 4a:* 186 lab adults (34 percent male; average age 20.1) in five conditions: serious; appropriate joke that lands; appropriate joke that fails; inappropriate joke that lands; inappropriate joke that fails. The appropriate joke was a last-words quip about hidden money; the inappropriate one was a sexual boast; the question was what they would do if they won the lottery. Status: serious 4.07; appropriate-landed 4.62 (highest); appropriate-failed 3.68; inappropriate-landed 3.73; inappropriate-failed 2.97 (lowest; the only condition significantly below the serious baseline). Competence: 3.95, 4.45, 3.74, 3.00, 2.34. Confidence: 4.29, 5.78, 5.03, 6.00, 5.30 (all four joke conditions above serious). *Study 4b:* 509 online adults, five conditions, tire-reuse question, with jokes rated about equally funny but very different in appropriateness (a gentle jab at a fitness subculture vs a sexual pun on a tire brand name). Status: serious 3.94; appropriate-landed 4.84; appropriate-failed 3.27 (here significantly below serious, unlike the failed appropriate jokes in 2a, 2b and 4a); inappropriate-landed 3.61 (not significantly below serious); inappropriate-failed 2.57 (lowest). Mediation pattern across studies: the indirect path through confidence was positive for every joke condition; the path through competence was positive for appropriate-and-landed, near zero and negative for appropriate-failed (not significant), and significantly negative for inappropriate jokes whether or not they landed.
*Candidate application:* Because funniness and appropriateness are independent, a character can be reliably very funny and reliably costly to be around; the authors' own data say that costs fall hardest when the audience is silent.

**[E05-30]. Audience laughter works as social proof and even rewrites how the joke is judged.** (AC-12, [p.3], [p.17])
The authors argue that laughter is a public demonstration of approval, that people laugh more when others laugh, and that people attend more to people others approve of. Their evidence: merely reading that a manager "laughed" or "sat in silence" changed participants' ratings of whether the joke was funny and appropriate, and of the teller. They call perceptions of humor "malleable" (p.17) and say that someone skilled at eliciting laughter may face far fewer consequences for inappropriate jokes. They also note that the earlier literature found humor related to group performance only in cases where a joke was followed by laughter or by another joke (Lehmann-Willenbrock and Allen 2014, cited background).
*Candidate application:* One person laughing first can make a risky joke safe: a documented mechanism for the unit's laugh-leaders, and for why a silent room is read as a verdict.

**[E05-31]. Mood is not the mechanism.** (AC-12, [p.9-10], [p.15-16], Appendix F)
Positive and negative affect, measured with a standard 20-item mood scale, did not mediate the humor-status link in Study 2a (measured after recalling the joke) or in Study 4b (measured immediately after the joke in the scenario): positive mood did not lift status after a successful joke, and negative mood did not lower it after a failed inappropriate one. The authors conclude it is the inference about the teller (confident, competent or not) and not the audience's feelings that carries the effect.
*Candidate application:* "They liked the teller because they were in a good mood" is the wrong explanation; the better one is that the joke told them something about the teller's nerve and judgment.

**[E05-32]. What the authors say about context and norms (the closest the paper comes to "rules of license").** (AC-12, [p.2-3], [p.18])
Humor norms "vary across contexts and individuals" (p.2), forecasting appropriateness is difficult, and appropriateness depends on both the type of joke (self-deprecation, puns, insults, sexual innuendo are the examples they list) and its fit to context. The same sexual content that is inappropriate at a professional interview "might be viewed as acceptable in a more casual setting" (p.4). The authors predict that risk is higher with unfamiliar audiences, where the teller cannot know what will be received, and say the situations where humor may help most (new settings, first impressions) are also the ones where it is most dangerous. In their future-directions list they name factors that likely shift how risky a joke is: delivery (cues, timing, frequency), the teller's age, gender and status, audience size and diversity, whether the target is present or absent and known or a stranger, the setting, and the relationships (hierarchy, closeness, liking). **They did not test any of these**; the studies compared appropriate and inappropriate content in two professional frames (a testimonial in a lab group; a job interview).
*Candidate application:* This is a plain-language checklist for the "who may joke, with whom, when" lens; soldier cultures sit at the far end of the same dimensions (high closeness, shared stakes, clear hierarchy), but the paper itself gives no evidence about them.

**[E05-33]. Recovering from a failed joke: speculation only.** (AC-12, [p.18])
The authors say important work remains on recovery after an inappropriate failure. They suggest, without testing, that an apology may be the most effective way to regain status, that a self-deprecating joke or a shift of focus might also work, and that if a joke is extremely inappropriate (their example is a widely condemned public tweet joking about a serious disease and race, which cost its author the job) the damage may be unrepairable.
*Candidate application:* Repair moves (apology, turning the joke on oneself, changing the subject) are described here as conjecture; their real-world forms in military settings would need a different source.

**[E05-34]. The paper's practical advice and its own caveats.** (AC-12, [p.17-18])
Humor is framed as a social and managerial skill that organizations might select, train and promote for; but the prescriptive advice is to "use humor with caution" (p.18), because the contexts where it helps most (initial impressions, novel situations) are those where norms are least known. Stated limits: only short spoken jokes were studied (not physical humor or storytelling); very small joke sets (nine different jokes across the studies); mostly online vignette ratings or short lab sessions with strangers, not intact groups with histories; gender of teller, target and audience left for future work (the authors flag that gender-related jokes may behave differently); cultural norms and other forms of humor expression (physical humor, storytelling) untested; only individual-level outcomes for the joke teller were measured, not effects on dyads, groups or organizations (the authors guess that organizations encouraging humor may recruit and retain better, untested); in Study 2a mood was measured at recall rather than when the joke was told (Study 4b measured it immediately). The authors also suggest, without testing, that funny people may be better liked and gain influence over time.
*Candidate application:* Treat this as an experimental analogue of the "audience decides" principle, not as data on how a unit's insiders treat a joke they have heard a hundred times.


### From AC-18 (Lancaster & Phillips, UK ambulance-service humor and well-being literature review; civilian paramedics, NOT military)

*Reading note: the text copy is an unproofed manuscript with word-processor tracked-change debris ("wellbeingwell-being", "eight8"), editor comments, and a data-extraction table (Appendix 1, [p.20-23]) whose columns were interleaved by the conversion. Where I use the table I say so. Page markers `[p.N]` equal the manuscript's own page numbers.*

**[E05-35]. What this review is, and how thin its evidence base is.** (AC-18, [p.1-2], [p.4-8])
A small, systematic-style literature review that asks how humor in the UK ambulance service affects clinicians' well-being. Nine databases were searched (final search 14 January 2021); the search found 37 records, 26 after duplicates, and **only four studies met every criterion**: peer-reviewed original primary research, UK-based, English language, published 2005 or later (the cutoff is the 2005 report that remade the service), and about paramedics or ambulance staff and humor in relation to well-being. Two authors screened independently with no disagreement. The four were appraised with a standard qualitative checklist (scores 6 to 9; three rated "high," one "medium") and analyzed thematically into four themes: **types of humor, the source and boundaries of humor, offloading, and camaraderie.** Two of the four studied humor directly; in the other two humor surfaced as a by-product of studying resilience. The authors' stated limits: limited database access and no gray literature. **My caution (not the authors'):** one exclusion criterion drops any study that did not find humor to be an affecting factor on well-being, which structurally favors positive findings, and the closing claim that humor has "a positive impact on well-being and a cathartic effect" rests on four small qualitative studies, none designed to look for harms.
*Candidate application:* Use as a map of what is known about UK paramedic humor (almost nothing, qualitatively), not as proof that dark humor is healthy.

**[E05-36]. The four underlying studies (what they are).** (AC-18, [p.8-9], [p.20-23] Appendix 1)
(1) **Scott 2007**, a focus-group study of how paramedics, emergency-department nurses and police traffic officers express humor in "sudden death work," in three emergency departments and three ambulance stations in northern England; the review's text says only three paramedics took part (the table reads as nine focus groups with three each of nurses, paramedics and traffic officers; the two descriptions are not reconciled in the text copy). The oldest of the four; local; no ethical approval stated; rated high. (2) **Charman 2013**, a study of humor in relations between police officers and ambulance staff: 45 semi-structured interviews, 22 of them with ambulance staff (paramedics, technicians, emergency care assistants), carried out in crew rooms; the largest sample of the four; the review flags possible confidentiality problems (crew-room setting), thinly described methods and no stated ethical approval; rated medium. (3) **Williams 2012**, interviews with eight second-year undergraduate paramedic students on how they handle emotion work on placement; humor appears alongside "talking it through" as one of two main coping themes; small, not diverse (the table's gender line is internally inconsistent for a sample of eight, so I do not report a split); rated high. (4) **Clompus and Albarran 2016**, a psycho-social study of resilience: seven participants (five paramedics) in a regional urban-and-rural ambulance service (the table's ethics line points to south-west England), with a first phase of 60- to 90-minute biographical narrative interviews and a second phase of 45- to 60-minute semi-structured interviews, using a free-association narrative method; they had aimed for ten participants; the study center was in organizational unrest during data collection; rated high. The review's critique: all four small, localized, and short on ethnic and gender diversity.
*Candidate application:* The underlying studies are the primary leads if UK emergency-service humor is ever needed; none concerns military personnel.

**[E05-37]. The well-being background the review cites (secondhand figures).** (AC-18, [p.3])
The introduction reports, from other sources, that UK ambulance staff had the highest sickness absence due to stress in the national health service (2014 figures); that a mental-health charity survey found 91 percent of ambulance staff had experienced stress and poor mental health (self-reported, which the authors say may overstate it); that in a 2004 study 10 percent of ambulance workers showed signs of clinical depression and PTSD prevalence was 22 percent; and that one author claims paramedics have the highest PTSD prevalence of any emergency service. The review also relays that humor is a controversial topic in healthcare because it is easily misread and needs the right circumstances, yet "humanizes" experience in stressful settings (citing earlier clinical ethnographies), and that earlier work found cynical humor lets emergency workers express socially unacceptable impulses with a cathartic effect.
*Candidate application:* Numbers to compare against when calibrating "how many emergency-service veterans are carrying trauma" (they are not military figures).

**[E05-38]. Types of humor: quick-witted quips dominate among ambulance staff.** (AC-18, [p.9-10], [p.22])
Scott's study identified seven categories of humor. The review's text names only the one most often used by ambulance staff, *quick-witted quips* (the other categories were more common among the nurses and traffic officers). The Appendix 1 cell, read through the interleaved layout, appears to list the seven as: twist in the tale; quick-witted quips; moral to the story; ironic expressions; "vulture mentality"; "cadaver rhetoric"; and censoring of humor expression. **I cannot confirm that list without the original study; it is flagged as a lead, not a finding.** The review's explanation for the dominance of quips is that fast, thoughtless wit becomes second nature, grows out of wanting to see the lighter side of a stressful situation, and issues in "foolish jesting" with crewmates, which in turn builds camaraderie. It adds that tragic events appear to push emergency staff toward darker humor (citing Scott).
*Candidate application:* The mix suggests a distribution: for the crew the commonest register is the rapid quip, with the heavier registers (if the garbled table's labels are right) present but less habitual.

**[E05-39]. Unwritten boundaries, reported in all four studies.** (AC-18, [p.12])
The unwritten rules staff set for themselves, per the review: no jokes about children or terminally ill patients; keep jokes among colleagues you know well; do not retell them to friends and family outside the service. All four studies mentioned boundaries, and participants treated them as important. The review reads their purpose as respect for patients and their families (respect being a core stated value of the health service) and notes that humor at the public's expense may cross the line, and that whether the boundaries are enough is "subjective to each person." It adds, in the conclusion, that classifying what counts as appropriate humor is very subjective.
*Candidate application:* This is a concrete, sourced set of license rules (protected targets; relationship floor; no export to outsiders); the third rule (not retold outside the service) is the civilian-facing wall in miniature.

**[E05-40]. Targets: each other first, the public second, other services third.** (AC-18, [p.11-12])
From Charman 2013 (the review's main source on this): the most common source of humor was other members of the ambulance staff, most often on breaks, to relax; the second was the public, with patients often the subject; the third was other emergency services, most often the fire service, where the humor was always described as friendly. Interviewees attributed the fire-service banter to the fire crews' being able to sleep on night shifts, which ambulance crews rarely do; the review suggests professional jealousy. The review also notes (Charman, and Scott) that the *exposure itself* is often the root of the humor.
*Candidate application:* A nice detail for rivalry between related services: cross-service teasing is friendly, anchored in a concrete working-conditions grievance (sleep), not in contempt.

**[E05-41]. Offloading and camaraderie: the benefits the four studies report.** (AC-18, [p.10-13], [p.20-23])
*Offloading* appears in all four: participants saw humor as a way to release worries and stress and to see the lighter side. Williams's student paramedics unanimously identified humor as a coping strategy that let them express emotion in hard situations and "move on." Clompus and Albarran (table) report that humor diffuses tense work moments and distracts staff from harrowing scenes. Across Charman, Clompus and Albarran, and Scott, participants saw humor as informal peer support and as an informal debrief after jobs. *Camaraderie* appears in three: Scott found it a consequence of joking and laughing together; Clompus and Albarran found it came from the release itself (emotional release through humor earned social support from crewmates); Charman linked humor to teamwork and relationship. The review's chain: boundaries and sources produce the quick-quip register, which builds camaraderie, which lets staff trust crewmates enough to offload worries, which is protective. It also mentions (outside the four studies) that a firefighter study, our AC-08, suggested coping training should focus on humor.
*Candidate application:* The "informal debrief" idea is worth testing as a mechanism in a unit: a joke-ritual after a hard call does the job a formal after-action process does not.

**[E05-42]. The review's conclusions, gaps and cautions.** (AC-18, [p.13-14])
Gaps named: very little UK research on the direct effect of humor on ambulance staff well-being, less still on paramedics alone; larger and more diverse samples needed; and "a complete lack of research" on any *negative* effects of humor on UK paramedics (the abstract lists this as a need for further research). The authors suggest appropriate humor should be encouraged between staff for stress relief and camaraderie, but that "appropriate" is subjective, and that any encouragement should rest on the unwritten boundaries the staff themselves described (they also cite a nursing review advocating an "evidence-based style" of humor use).
*Candidate application:* The missing-negative-effects gap is the exact place where the Kuiper and Sliter sources (above) supply the failure modes this review cannot.

**[E05-43]. Studies the review excluded, listed by title only (leads, not read).** (AC-18, [p.7-8], Figure 5)
Excluded titles (with the review's reasons): Bochantin (2017), "Ambulance thieves, clowns and naked grandfathers" (did not specify paramedics or ambulance staff); Scott (2013) on sudden death, dehumanization and disengagement (no humor-well-being link); Rowe and Regehr (2010) on cynical humor among emergency-service professionals (not UK; secondary); Williams (2013) on emotion work in student paramedics (no humor link); Mildenhall (2012) literature review on informal paramedic coping; Roscoe (2017) "Sometimes laughter is the best medicine" (not UK); Kuiper (2012), our AC-07 (not UK, not paramedics); and Christopher (2015) "An introduction to black humor as a coping mechanism for student paramedics" (secondary). **I have read none of these; titles only.**
*Candidate application:* Two leads worth fetching for dark-humor specifics: Rowe and Regehr 2010 and Christopher 2015.


### From AC-31 (Freud 1928, "Humor"; theory, not about soldiers)

*Spelling note: Freud's English translation uses the British spelling of this word; per the project rule, quotations below are Americanized ("humor") and nothing else is altered. Citations are to the journal pages (S. 1 to S. 6) shown in the text copy. See Gaps on the copyright notice at the end of that file.*

**[E05-44]. Starting point: humor as a saving of feeling, in two arrangements.** (AC-31, S.1-2)
Freud begins by recalling that the earlier book on wit (1905) treated humor only "from the economic point of view": the pleasure comes from a saving in the expenditure of affect (emotion that would otherwise have been spent). He then distinguishes two ways the process can occur: (1) one person adopts a humorous attitude and a second person, a spectator, enjoys it from a distance; (2) one person regards another (a real or imagined figure, for example a character drawn by a writer or comedian) in a humorous light, with no humor required of the person described, and the audience shares the humorist's enjoyment. In both, the humorous attitude can refer to the subject's own self or to other people, gives pleasure to the one who adopts it, and gives a similar pleasure to onlookers.
*Candidate application:* The two arrangements cover both kinds of soldier humor worth distinguishing in writing: the person joking about their own plight (arrangement 1) and the one who turns someone else's misfortune into a joke for a third party (arrangement 2).

**[E05-45]. The gallows example and the mechanism of the hearer's pleasure.** (AC-31, S.1-2)
Freud's "very crude example" of humor made about oneself is the condemned man: "when the criminal who is being led to the gallows on a Monday observes, 'Well, this is a good beginning to the week'" (S.1). The man makes the humor himself, gains a satisfaction from it, and the listener, who did nothing, feels a similar satisfaction "from a distance." The mechanism: the listener sees someone in a situation that leads the listener to expect an emotional display (Freud lists anger, complaint, pain, fear, horror, possibly despair) and prepares to follow suit; the expectation is "deceived" because the person makes a joke instead, and the hearer's humorous satisfaction comes from the feeling the hearer did not have to spend. For the humorist, the essence is sparing oneself the affects the situation would naturally produce and overriding "with a jest the possibility of such an emotional display." Freud adds that the hearer must have copied the process in the humorist's mind, so that the real puzzle is what happens in the humorist.
*Candidate application:* The joke's work is not in the words but in the deceived expectation of emotion; the audience's reward is relief. This helps explain why a flat, unmoved delivery is part of the effect: the listener's relief depends on the teller not displaying the expected emotion.

**[E05-46]. What humor is not: stoic acceptance is wise but not humor.** (AC-31, S.2-3)
Freud tests the definition against a variant of the same example: the condemned man says instead, "It doesn't worry me. What does it matter, after all, if a fellow like me is hanged? The world won't come to an end." Freud grants this displays "the same magnificent rising superior to the real situation," is "wise and true," but "does not betray a trace of humor," because it rests on an appraisal of reality that runs counter to humor's. Freud's summary: "Humor is not resigned; it is rebellious." Humor, in his view, signifies the triumph not only of the ego but of the pleasure principle, strong enough to assert itself against adverse circumstances.
*Candidate application:* A usable test for writing: calm, philosophical fatalism ("what's one more death") and gallows humor are different things in this account. The first accepts the facts; the second refuses them. A character can be fatalistic without being funny, and funny without being resigned.

**[E05-47]. The "fine and elevating" quality, and the family of defenses humor belongs to.** (AC-31, S.2-3)
Beyond the liberation that wit and the comic also give, humor has "something fine and elevating": "the triumph of narcissism, the ego's victorious assertion of its own invulnerability." It "refuses to be hurt by the arrows of reality," and treats blows from the outside world as merely occasions for pleasure. Freud places humor in "the great series of methods devised by the mind of man for evading the compulsion to suffer," a series that begins with neurosis and delusions and includes intoxication and states of ecstasy, because humor, like them, denies reality's claim. What distinguishes it is that it does so "without quitting the ground of mental sanity" and so has a dignity that wit lacks (wit aims at gratification or an outlet for aggression). Freud notes the apparent puzzle that these two achievements (denying suffering, staying sane) seem hard to reconcile.
*Candidate application:* An explicit theoretical statement that gallows humor is a refusal to suffer on reality's terms, an old and risky defense in the same family as numbing; useful for a character whose humor and whose drinking (or other numbing) serve the same end.

**[E05-48]. Where the humorous attitude comes from: the adult toward the child, and the super-ego's kindness.** (AC-31, S.3-5)
Toward others, Freud (recalling the earlier book's suggestion) says the humorist takes the attitude of an adult toward a child, smiling at the triviality of interests and sufferings that seem big to the child, and thus acquires superiority. But Freud calls the more original situation humor toward oneself, to ward off suffering. The answer uses Freud's model of the mind: the "super-ego" (the internalized parental authority that usually holds the ego in strict subordination) can shift the psychological emphasis ("cathexis") so that the super-ego is inflated and the ego appears tiny and its interests trivial, which makes it easy to suppress the ego's potential reactions. The humorist's meaning is then, in Freud's words: "Look here! This is all that this seemingly dangerous world amounts to. Child's play–the very thing to jest about!" (S.5). Freud notes it is surprising that the usually stern super-ego should speak "such kindly words of comfort to the intimidated ego," and says this shows how much remains to be learned about it. Freud adds that the jest itself is not the essential thing; it only has "the value of a proof" of the attitude.
*Candidate application:* For a character with a harsh inner voice, humor may be the one moment that voice turns gentle; the gallows joke then reads as self-comfort given in the voice of an indulgent parent. Candidate only; it is a psychoanalytic account.

**[E05-49]. Freud's own caveats.** (AC-31, S.5-6)
(1) The pleasure of humor is "never so intense" as that from the comic or wit and "never finds a vent in hearty laughter," yet we assign it high value as liberating and elevating. (2) The super-ego is "really repudiating reality and serving an illusion." (3) The hypothesis of large shifts of psychic energy between agencies "looks like a new hypothesis, conceived ad hoc," which Freud defends by analogy with the earlier explanations of falling in love, paranoia and the alternation of depression and mania, and Freud notes a customary caution about commenting on normal mental life from pathology. (4) Not everyone can do it: the humorous attitude "is a rare and precious gift," and many people cannot even enjoy humor when others present it.
*Candidate application:* Two usable character points: humor of this kind is quiet more often than loud (a smile, not a roar), and some people in any unit simply do not have the capacity or the taste for it, which is a source of social friction apart from any morality.


### From AC-30 (Sumner, essay on Kurt Vonnegut; literary-biographical, about a WWII soldier-author; no humor theory)

*Reading note: the essay (about 4,450 words, printed pages 46-57 of the journal, so printed page = N + 45) is about the writer and his Second World War novel about the Dresden firebombing. It is not a study of humor, never uses the terms "gallows humor" or "black humor," and describes the horror only in summary; I extract only what bears on humor, detachment and deflection after atrocity. All readings of Vonnegut's psychology are Sumner's interpretation, not clinical evidence. I summarize rather than reproduce the passages on the dead and on the prisoners' transport.*

**[E05-50]. A book that mixes mordant humor with grief, and is "the least funny book."** (AC-30, [p.1-2])
Sumner describes the novel as oscillating between realism and science fiction, "mordant humor" and grief, relieved by moments of lyrical imagery. It is "suffused with irony" and rich with "interludes of comic relief," yet Sumner calls it the author's "least funny book," a spare, haunted American counterpart of the German "rubble literature" of the late 1940s (the author's friend, the former infantryman and writer Heinrich Böll). The author cautions in the book's opening pages, as Sumner quotes it: "There is nothing intelligent to say about a massacre." The tone, Sumner concludes, is more sad than angry.
*Candidate application:* Craft point: the humor that works after atrocity is sparing, sits in interludes, and is surrounded by a prose that does not joke. A soldier whose speech is mostly plain and only occasionally dark can read as more credible than one who jokes constantly.

**[E05-51]. Deflection and quarantine after atrocity.** (AC-30, [p.1-2])
Sumner describes the author as having downplayed the importance of the Dresden experience for years, saying it affected his character far less than family deaths, a marriage ending and similar later events (a statement Sumner quotes from one of the author's own later books). Sumner reads this as "a defense, an attempt to quarantine the episode," and notes that the author later declared Dresden off-limits in interviews "only to bring it up himself, again and again, like a compulsion." Sumner ties the struggle to write it to a survivor's need to bear witness. In the author's own comparison of a friend's stories of soldiers who go home "with the war part missing," the author says that is like his own memory of Dresden: "Actually there's nothing there." Sumner also notes that the author came home to find almost nothing about the raid in the newspapers, and concluded that his government, helped by the press, could lie.
*Candidate application:* A usable pair of behaviors for the ex-soldier: minimizing the event while returning to it uninvited, and a memory that is a blank where the event should be. The civilian silence (nobody back home knew or asked) is a documented cause of the isolation.

**[E05-52]. Humor as the price of an ambiguous loyalty.** (AC-30, [p.2])
The author, who was of German-American descent, worried that his reflections on the destruction of a German city would be taken as special pleading. He compared himself to a writer he admired who had briefly sided with the losing side in his own country's war: both were associated with the enemy, "And so you have to be funnier than most people," he said, being careful in describing the privations of the other side (as Sumner quotes it). In the essay this is a passing remark about authorial position, not a theory.
*Candidate application:* A defector or exile may find that humor is the way an outsider with doubtful allegiances earns a hearing: the joke lowers the guard of an audience that might otherwise hear sympathy for the other side as betrayal.

**[E05-53]. The detached register: scientific distance, flat understatement, and the refrain.** (AC-30, [p.2-3], [p.7], [p.9], [p.11])
(a) Sumner compares the author's reduction of people and places to constituent elements with a chemist-survivor of the Holocaust: each adopts the scientific distance of their training "as both a means of survival and, later, a mode of communication," recording extreme circumstances as though watching an experiment. (b) In a letter to his family from a Red Cross station in late May 1945, published after the author's death, the author reports having told the guards what he planned to do to them when the Russians arrived, and then reports the result in two short sentences: "They beat me up a little. I was fired as group leader." Sumner uses the letter to show the author was more like the novel's steadfast older prisoner than its passive hero. (c) The author called his book "a nonjudgmental expression of astonishment." Speaking in 1977, he gave one reason for the bombing as a sardonic account of bureaucratic momentum ("they'd already burned down everything else"), framed as a bored question about what to do that night. (d) The refrain "so it goes" follows reports of death in the novel and, Sumner says, conveys "bone-deep sense of irony and resignation in the face of defeat" and, nonetheless, "a will" to carry on.
*Candidate application:* Four separable devices to attribute to a veteran voice: professional or technical distance as a speaking style; understatement for violence done to oneself; sardonic bureaucratic explanations of horrors; and a ritual closing phrase that marks a death and moves on.
*Cross-use:* The scientific-distance register would suit a robot or technician character describing atrocities in the vocabulary of their own discipline.

**[E05-54]. De-glorification and the absurd death.** (AC-30, [p.3-4], [p.5], [p.9])
Sumner places the novel with Catch-22 as bookending the 1960s with an absurdist de-glorification of the "Good War" narrative. The author deliberately showed soldiers as "the babes in arms they really were," not film swashbucklers: the book's subtitle is "The Children's Crusade," its hero is a passive clown, and the author recalled as a young scout in the Battle of the Bulge that he "imitated various war movies I'd seen." The older prisoner, whose single moment of speechmaking is interrupted by an air-raid siren, is later shot for keeping hold of a teapot in the ruins; Sumner cites this as the absurd, meaningless death the novel treats with the same flat refrain. The book was condemned and in a few cases burned by people claiming offense at its occasional "rough soldier's language" (Sumner suspects a pretext for a political objection), and a school board in Missouri ruled it inappropriate in 2011.
*Candidate application:* Documents a cultural pattern: humor that deflates war glory is read as anti-patriotic by some audiences and as the only honest register by others.

**[E05-55]. The connected critic: proud, troubled, still a patriot.** (AC-30, [p.8-9], [p.11])
Sumner argues the author was a man of the left, a pacifist by inclination, an early public critic of the Vietnam War, who nevertheless remained a patriot of an old-fashioned civic kind, called WWII a "good war" after all despite the winners' crimes, and said he would have enlisted for Vietnam out of a sense of duty even though he thought it wrongheaded. Sumner uses the philosopher Michael Walzer's phrase "connected critic" for a lover of country who anguishes over its failures, and calls the author "a brokenhearted American dreamer." Sumner also reads veteran characters in the author's other books (a veteran in a sanitarium with flat affect and guilt, "all the signs of what we would now call PTSD"; a decorated Vietnam-era veteran who speaks honestly about "the culture of lying" he served) as recurring figures, and reads the hero's time travel as an escape from the demands of domestic life after return.
*Candidate application:* A model for a defector who left on principle yet kept some loyalty: criticism that comes from attachment, not from contempt. The PTSD labeling is Sumner's modern gloss on fiction, not a diagnosis.

## Pattern roll-up

*None of these patterns is documented for soldiers by these sources. They are documented for civilian emergency workers, general populations, laboratory and workplace samples, one 1928 theory essay and one veteran-author's writing. Carry any of them to a soldier-type character only as a candidate.*

**Functions and mechanisms (what humor does psychologically)**
- *Distancing by reappraisal:* humor changes how the event is read without denying it, lowering threat and the emotion that would follow (AC-07 E05-8; AC-08 E05-18). It may also add a positive emotion alongside the negative one (AC-07).
- *Saved feeling:* the hearer expects an emotional display and gets a joke; both teller and hearer are spared the feeling (AC-31 E05-45).
- *Rebellion, not resignation:* calm fatalism is not humor; humor is the ego refusing to be hurt, a member of the family of defenses against suffering, though one that keeps the person sane (AC-31 E05-46/47).
- *Social route:* bonding, informal peer support and an informal "debrief" after calls (AC-18 E05-41; proposed in AC-08).
- *Stress buffering:* in 179 firefighters, high self-rated coping humor went with a much weaker link between trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms and burnout three months later (AC-08 E05-16); the same buffer pattern in earlier student studies (AC-07 E05-6).
- *Status signaling:* trying a joke signals confidence; only a successful, appropriate joke signals competence; together they raise status (AC-12 E05-23 to E05-27).

**Targets**
- Colleagues are the commonest target (especially on breaks), then the public and patients, then other emergency services (friendly teasing, anchored in a concrete grievance such as the fire crews' ability to sleep on night shifts) (AC-18 E05-40).
- The self as target is the original case in Freud's account (AC-31 E05-48), and, in the humor-styles model, becomes a failure mode when it is excessive self-ridicule used to buy approval (AC-07 E05-9).

**Forms and mechanics**
- Deadpan about one's own fate in the face of doom (AC-31 E05-45); understatement about violence done to oneself (AC-30 E05-53); technical or scientific distance as a speaking style (AC-30); a ritual closing refrain after a death (AC-30).
- Quick-witted quips were the most common type among ambulance staff in one small, old study (AC-18 E05-38); the other category names are leads only.

**Rules of license**
- Unwritten boundaries reported by UK ambulance staff: not about children or terminally ill patients; between colleagues who know each other well; never retold to people outside the service (AC-18 E05-39).
- Appropriateness depends on both the type of joke and its fit to context; norms vary; risk is highest with unfamiliar audiences (AC-12 E05-32).
- The audience's laugh works as real-time license and social proof; silence after an inappropriate joke is the worst outcome for status (AC-12 E05-28, E05-30).
- Aggressive and self-defeating styles make others less willing to interact again (AC-07 E05-10).

**Limits and failure modes**
- *Masking and alienating:* humor used to hide emotion or pain, or cynical humor that alienates others, left working relationships less effective (AC-07 E05-2).
- *Body cost:* more humor went with higher cardiovascular, weight and smoking risk in a police cohort (AC-07 E05-3); benefits are mainly psychological, not physical.
- *Relief then worse performance:* after a failure, humor users felt better and then did worse on the next test (AC-07 E05-7).
- *Not a universal buffer:* no buffering of absenteeism in firefighters (AC-08 E05-17).
- *Inappropriate jokes:* signal low competence, outweigh the confidence signal, and lower status most when nobody laughs; an extreme case may be unrepairable (AC-12 E05-28, E05-33).
- *Nonchalance as disguise:* a cavalier "it's only a joke" posture was linked to hidden prejudice (AC-07 E05-10).
- *Deflection that becomes compulsion:* minimizing a war experience while returning to it uninvited; memory with "nothing there" (AC-30 E05-51).

**Time course**
- Effects differ over even short periods (AC-07 E05-7); bereavement humor linked to resilience over time (AC-07 E05-4); the survival benefit shrinks above age 65 (AC-07 E05-5); the veteran-author's quarantine of his war lasted decades before he wrote it (AC-30).

**Civilian-facing register**
- Not retelling jokes outside the service (AC-18 E05-39); civilians' ignorance and silence about the event as a source of the veteran-author's isolation (AC-30 E05-51).

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics**
- Association with "the enemy" or the other side raises the bar: "you have to be funnier" (AC-30 E05-52); criticism that comes from attachment, not contempt, in a person who stayed proud of service (AC-30 E05-55).

**Cross-cultural**
- The four humor styles appeared in European, North American, Middle Eastern and Eastern samples; some but not all interpersonal effects held cross-culturally (AC-07 E05-9, E05-10); UK humor varies by region (AC-18 E05-38).

**Craft observations**
- Sparing humor inside plain prose; "least funny book"; humor in interludes (AC-30 E05-50). De-glorification of "the Good War" through flat, absurd treatment of death (AC-30 E05-54).

## Gaps and cautions

1. **No source here is military.** The firefighter, police, paramedic and general-population evidence says nothing about how soldiers actually joke; the status experiments used online or laboratory strangers in professional frames; the Vonnegut essay is criticism. Treat everything as analogy.
2. **AC-08, minus signs lost.** The text copy dropped minus signs in tables and in some statistics, so the direction of humor's own correlations with burnout and PTSD cannot be read from the numbers; the buffer direction is secure from the authors' simple-slope prose (low-humor slopes strong, high-humor slopes not significant). **AC-08, response rate arithmetic:** 685 packets sent, 208 returned at Time 1, 179 usable at Time 2: the authors state a 29 percent response rate, but 179/685 is about 26 percent and 208/685 about 30 percent. **AC-08 used only self-rated trait humor, and only men;** humor and trauma exposure were both measured at Time 1, and the authors say the proposed mechanisms were not tested.
3. **AC-12, text damage.** Pages 1-5 are interleaved and watermarked; inequality symbols and minus signs are missing in most statistics. I used Tables 2-4 and Appendix A for numbers. The Pilot 1 appropriateness percentages (27 and 70) have lost their inequality symbols; I read them as below and above the scale midpoint. The mediation table (Table 5) has stripped signs; I give only the prose direction. **Tension inside AC-12:** a failed but appropriate joke did not lower status in Studies 2a, 2b and 4a but did in Study 4b; the authors' general statement that such failure did not harm status should be read with that exception.
4. **AC-18 is weaker than its conclusion.** Four small qualitative UK studies (three paramedics in one; eight students in another; seven participants in a third), with a built-in selection toward positive findings, and a conclusion that humor has "a positive impact on well-being." The review also says in passing that firefighters' humor use (AC-08) functioned to "offload" stress, which is looser than what AC-08 measured (a buffering of PTSD and burnout, with offloading and social bonding only proposed). **Internal conflicts in the review:** Scott's paramedic count (three paramedics in the text; nine focus groups of three in the table); Williams's sample of eight with a gender line that reads eight males and two females. **The review cites Kuiper (AC-07)** for a "strong link ... between humor and an increase of in resilience" (spelling Americanized); Kuiper's own conclusion is that support for humor leading to growth or bounce-back is "very limited," with the benefits mainly in coping and positive affect. Read the review's line as a looser paraphrase than the source.
5. **AC-07 vs AC-08 vs AC-31, differing pictures of "humor as best coping."** AC-08 opens by crediting Freud (a 1960 translation of the 1905 book on jokes, not the 1928 essay read here) with calling humor perhaps the most effective of all coping mechanisms; the 1928 essay instead places humor in a series of defenses that includes neurosis and delusion and says its pleasure is the weaker kind. Kuiper stresses humor's benefits are bounded and style-dependent.
6. **AC-31 copyright status.** The manifest records this item as public domain; the text copy ends with a German notice that the translation is protected until 2032 and published with the journal's permission. I treated that as a conflict and quoted only the gallows example and a handful of short sentences (all Americanized in spelling per the project rule). The developer should settle the status before any longer quotation is made.
7. **AC-31 and AC-30 are not research.** Freud's account is an untested psychoanalytic model (the author calls part of it ad hoc); Sumner's readings of Vonnegut's psychology are interpretation. Neither supplies a sample, a rate or a moderator.
8. **Leads to chase (titles only, not read):** Rowe and Regehr 2010 (cynical humor among emergency service professionals); Kosenko and Rintamaki 2010 (humor in AIDS-service organizations: the forms, functions and foibles); Kerkkänen, Kuiper and Martin 2004 (Finnish police humor and health, the body-cost study); Christopher 2015 (black humor for student paramedics); Scott 2007 and Charman 2013 (the two UK studies with humor as the focus); Geisler and Weber 2010 (humor after failure, the short-term performance cost); Bizi, Keinan and Beit-Hallahmi 1988 (peer-rated humor and performance under stress in the Israeli army, cited in AC-08).
9. **Not available in the text copies:** all figures (AC-08 plots, AC-12 bar charts), the underlying studies of AC-18, and the German original of AC-31.
