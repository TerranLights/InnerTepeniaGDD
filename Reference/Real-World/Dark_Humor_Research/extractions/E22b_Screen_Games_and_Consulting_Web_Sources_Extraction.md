## Source-shape notes (read first)

**Citation convention.** These are web-page text copies with no page numbers. I cite the section heading where the page has one, plus **L = line number in the local .txt**. Every file starts with SOURCE / TITLE / FETCHED lines (fetched 2026-10-05). Spelling is American throughout, including inside quotations (where a source uses a British spelling I either Americanize it or paraphrase instead of quoting). Where a page is a **digest of someone else's article** (WA-068, WA-073), the chain is two steps long and I say so.

**How to weigh the pages.** Four kinds of material are mixed: (1) **documented statements by people who made or advised on the work** (a cast member and a director on *Blackadder*, a memoirist on *Jarhead*, a quest writer, a lead writer, an executive, advisors); (2) **outlet or fan interpretation** (the *Generation Kill* listicle, the Boone blog, the *Disco Elysium* essay, the *Helldivers 2* opinion piece); (3) **trade and advice pages** on veteran consulting; (4) **bare navigation or abstract text** (the GDC Vault page). **Almost none of it is about how real soldiers actually joke.** It is about how work *depicting* that joking was made, received or advised.

## Findings

### From WA-064 (Blackadder Goes Forth, how the ending was built)

**[E22b-1]. The stated license: aim at "the madness," not the dead, and pay for it with accuracy.** (WA-064, "Going to War", L25)
Tony Robinson recalls the cast's "nervousness" about joking about suffering while people marked by the First World War were still alive. Their answer, as he tells it: get costumes, props and historical moments "as right as we possibly could," and make the ending say clearly, in his words, "We're not taking the piss out of those who died, but the madness that led to those people dying." The source does not say whether anything of the kind was spoken on screen; it is recalled intent. The mechanism is a **two-part license**: exactness of detail earns the right to joke, and naming the target (the institution's absurdity) separates the joke from contempt for casualties.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's bitterest jokes can hit the machine that sent people out while the work stays exact about the life; the dead are the one category the humor visibly steps around.

**[E22b-2]. What the finale's jokes do (scripted by comedy writers, not soldiers).** (WA-064, "The Final Episode", L43-55)
Four moments: an officer admits the crisis only by inflating it into an absurdly elaborate building (institutional, real-estate language over a fear he cannot say plainly); he feigns madness to escape the attack, and the joke concedes the plan cannot work where "another madman" would go unnoticed (the target is the surrounding madness); a private recites a "war poem" that is only the sound of guns; the same private gives a garbled, literal account of how the war began and is corrected. All are fiction. The article offers no evidence on how closely they resemble real trench humor.
*Candidate application:* A depiction can divide a squad's comedy by role: the sardonic officer who inflates, the simple private who is literal, and the situation's madness as the main target. A shape for comic roles, not proof of real speech.

**[E22b-3]. The ending withdraws the humor; the change of register carries the meaning.** (WA-064, "The Ending" and "Two Points of Order", L57-73)
The plan was to end, as earlier series did, with the characters' deaths. The filmed charge was unusable (unfinished scenery, no rehearsal; the cast "fell on the floor like plucked carrots," says Robinson), so director Boden and the editor slowed the advance, faded to a plain piano version of the theme and cut to poppy fields found late at night in the BBC news library. Boden refused the usual brisk closing music because it would "pull the rug from under" a somber moment, and had to appeal to the head of comedy and the controller of BBC One because that meant no credits. The scripted version, which was shot, had the central officer only feigning death and slipping away (kept on the remastered DVD set); an old-man epilogue was never filmed. Deaths are implied, not shown. The mechanism: **the comedy runs the whole length and the ending refuses to release it**: no laugh, no cue back to the comic frame. The accident became the design.
*Candidate application:* Gallows humor need not be explained or resolved; the weight can land in one beat where the joking stops and nobody retrieves it. It works once.

**[E22b-4]. Comic wording is made line by line, and writers and performers may disagree.** (WA-064, "Everybody Argued", L29-39)
A cast containing several writers kept rewriting, spending about two hours on whether one animal name was funnier than another ("We dissected every line"). The two scriptwriters felt the changes were for the worse; the director thought they added value; Robinson feared the scrutiny would kill the show. The article does not rule. Participants' memories only.
*Candidate application:* Knowing military life and timing a joke are separate skills; neither a veteran advisor nor a research file supplies the second.

### From WA-065 (Generation Kill, banter authenticity)

**[E22b-5]. Authenticity of bearing and kit was built by embedding the real Marines.** (WA-065, L19-41)
Three former 1st Recon Marines worked on the show (one played himself; one acted and advised; one advised). Two led a six-day boot camp on weapons, small-unit tactics, vehicle driving and maintenance, and early PT. Each actor's loadout copied what the real Marine wore; actors cleaned their own weapons and stayed in uniform through five and a half months; the one concession was the heavy ceramic armor plates, left out for the African heat. The article does **not** say the advisors worked on dialogue or humor.
*Candidate application:* The bodily and procedural routine (handling gear, sitting, walking) is the base layer under which spoken banter reads as real.

**[E22b-6]. "Spot on" dialogue: the claim, how thin the evidence is, and how outsiders are addressed.** (WA-065, L5-15, L43-59)
The outlet says the series set "a new standard" and presented Marines "as the complex individuals they were in real life and not as caricatures," with the "underappreciated value of gallows humor in combat" part of the story. Its evidence for authentic dialogue is one event: after release, the book's author (who lived with the unit about two months) and the Marines sat for an HBO interview in which they "joke and banter" and make "some of the same quips" as the show; the outlet concludes that "a mix of cringe-worthy jokes and dark humor" captures the particular brand of humor in tight units. **Not established:** any named veteran's verdict, any joke example, or what was invented. Other details: the outlet's character summaries (a stoic leader, an "ever sarcastic yet affable" corporal, a lance corporal "simultaneously endearing and menacing"); the embedded reporter is never named but called by function and affiliation ("reporter," "scribe," the magazine's name) and sometimes by insults paired with a political label (the page does not say the real unit did this); and an off-screen line in episode four is an homage to a line in *Platoon* (summarized; see register), so soldier characters borrow from earlier war films.
*Candidate application:* The best documented authenticity route is a first-hand embed's notes plus the real subjects afterward, not an advisor's sign-off. An outsider who is tolerated but not trusted can be renamed by function; a unit raised on war films may use their lines as jokes.

### From WA-066 (Swofford on Jarhead)

**[E22b-7]. A veteran author's rules: do not flatter the self, do not hand soldiers a slogan; and what the interview does not say about humor.** (WA-066, L156-171, L189-227)
Swofford says he had to resist leaving out "not attractive" things about himself, getting "ego out of the way," because a flattering portrait of himself as a young Marine "would have been a much lesser book"; he cites a writer's line that the memoirist must sell out himself or herself. On politics, he says that most of the people actually fighting, "they're not pro-war," that "They want to kill people, but they don't want to kill people" in the sense of not wanting to see suffering, and that "pro-war" and "anti-war" are home-front tags that matter little "to the guys on the ground." He says the book is "passed around from Marine to Marine" and predicts military viewers will treat the film as the defining film of that war: **his own prediction, in a promotional interview, not a measured reaction.** The critic's phrases (not Swofford's): the film is "funny and frightening all at once"; war films like those the book's Marines watch "capture the perverse thrill of war." **Nothing in the interview concerns how the Marines joke.** A passage on sexual language is summarized in the register.
*Candidate application:* A soldier character can be ambivalent rather than ideological, and his own unattractive impulses are part of the realism. Watching war films together as leisure is a documented detail.

### From WA-067 (Avellone's GDC report)

**[E22b-8]. Process facts for the New Vegas DLC: a line budget, a theme per pack, research, and table reads.** (WA-067, L9-15)
Each of the four DLC had to be a "very self-contained short experience" because no player could be assumed to own all four. All four together were limited to about 10,000 lines, a hard maximum; the team padded with "trickery" such as mute characters who spoke in gestures, which "we were only able to get away with ... for so long," said Avellone. Each DLC had named themes behind everyone's actions (two quoted for *Dead Money*: "Rigging the Game" and "Greed as a Savage Force"), and writers researched books, films and games with similar style. Dialogue was tested by table reads in which staff read characters they had not written, to catch stumbles and mispronunciations before costly studio time. **Nothing on Boone, companions, humor or voice.**
*Candidate application:* Having someone other than the writer read lines aloud is a cheap test of whether a speech pattern is speakable; a line budget forces a character to be sketched in few strokes.

### From WA-068 (Fenstermaker on "One For My Baby")

**[E22b-9]. Developer statements on how Boone's quest was built.** (WA-068, L9)
Documented developer statements (writer Eric Fenstermaker, as quoted by PC Gamer and relayed by GameBanshee): the quest was part of the original proof-of-concept shown to the publisher, with a ghoul quest, to demonstrate the game's intent; because every major character in the town could be killed, a player could get any of them shot by the companion "for any reason you could concoct in your own mind"; a findable bill of sale uses language modeled on real pre-Civil War slavery contracts and discloses the one fact found nowhere else, that the companion's wife was pregnant when sold (summarized; see register). Fenstermaker concedes this is melodramatic but argues that in a "totally wild west environment" it is plausible and shows "extremes of inhumanity and desperation are commonplace here," which serves the stakes and the player's investment. **The editorial's own words (not the writer's):** taking the easy way out is "an extremely conscious act of callousness"; Boone is a former sniper "isolated both literally and figuratively," keeping nightly vigil in the mouth of a giant dinosaur statue ("somewhat goofy") and giving the player his treasured 1st Recon beret as a signal. **Name discrepancy:** this page writes the wife's name "Clara"; WA-069/070 write "Carla." These files cannot settle it; check the game text.
*Candidate application:* The documented design logic is to make the companion's grief the pivot of a quest in which the player's choices carry irreversible consequences. **Nothing here is about Boone's humor;** the only comic element is the silly statue, which makes a contrast.

### From WA-069 and WA-070 (fan blog on Boone, Parts I and II)

**[E22b-10]. A fan reconstruction of the backstory, and reticence as the character's engine.** (WA-069, L29, L67-93, L105; WA-070, L7-37, L63-71)
Fan analysis, openly speculative about the developers' process. The backstory as the blogger lists it, unsourced: a top shooter assigned to an elite sharpshooter squad; a participant in an incident in which his own army killed unarmed civilians; resigns from guilt; marries; wife and unborn child taken by slavers; he tracks them down, concludes he cannot save her and ends her life as a mercy; asks whether he could have done more; starts the game suicidal (summarized; see register). Craft advice: the writer should know far more than the player is told ("it is relevant to know Boone fought in the NCR army, is a great sniper, and hates the Legion"), and a short bio "will shape the way he speaks and the decisions he makes." On character, the thesis is "an ex-soldier looking for redemption" who "won't open up about his feelings"; his look (tight shirt, shades, beret, rifle) is read as "all restraint and almost no venting, except through violence. A ticking bomb waiting to go off." The one in-game line quoted in full is his refusal to explain: "I know, all right? And that's all you need to know," which the blogger says "creates mystery and tension." The blogger's claim that "most people, let alone ex-military, will never spill their guts easily" is an unsupported generalization.
*Candidate application:* For a Boone-style character, the fan reading to note is that what is withheld is the characterization; but this source treats reticence as his whole voice and never mentions a lighter side, so it neither supports nor denies one.

**[E22b-11]. The trauma-as-engine pattern in miniature.** (WA-069, L47-53; WA-070, L73-95)
In brainstorming, the blogger reasons: "Tour was up? Not conflictive enough," then "Saw something bad. No, did something bad. Witnessing is passive, doing is active. More fodder for conflict." Wrongdoing is picked because it generates drama; this is the blogger's imagined design logic, not Obsidian's. The blogger then describes endgame branches, unverified: argue he should get over it because people die in war, and he ends as a cold hired killer; take the "happier" branch, and he converts regret into protecting others, instead of a vendetta the blogger calls "an elaborate way of committing suicide" (see register).
*Candidate application:* A worked example of the pattern warned against in E22b-21: guilt chosen for narrative utility. The remedy visible in the blog's "happier" branch is a way back that the character earns.

**[E22b-12]. Verification: none of the Boone-related pages discusses Boone's humor.** (WA-067, WA-068, WA-069, WA-070, WA-071)
I read every line of all five and then searched each for humor words (humor, joke, funny, banter, sarcasm, quip, laugh, wit, deflect). **Zero hits about Boone** in WA-067, WA-069 and WA-070; WA-068's only tonal word is "goofy," about the statue; WA-071 is an abstract naming no character. The humor in WA-069/070 is the blogger's own self-deprecating asides, not Boone's. The earlier finding is confirmed: these sources document his quest, backstory, reticence and design logic, and nothing about whether or how he is funny. Any claim about Boone's humor must come from the game's own text or other sources.

### From WA-071 (GDC Vault, Sawyer)

### From WA-073 (Kim Kitsuragi)

**[E22b-14]. Reveal a partner's history through situations, not a dialogue tree.** (WA-073, L7-9)
Lead writer Robert Kurvitz says the team was surprised people like Kim so much, felt he was "a real person and our friend," and wanted him "to open up through the scenes and situations you get into," not "by clicking on him and cleaning out his tree." The digest adds that the player is unlikely to learn everything on one playthrough and that skill combinations unlock interactions other builds cannot. Two-step relay of a PC Gamer piece; nothing on Kim's humor or past.
*Candidate application:* For a Boone-style companion, the documented alternative to the confession scene is letting history leak through events and the player's own build, rewarding replay.

### From WA-074 (Disco Elysium essay)

**[E22b-15]. A joke-shaped line read as despair, and the masking choice.** (WA-074, L31-45)
At a moment when the partner offers rare encouragement, the player can answer with thanks, a pragmatic "no ex-alcoholics" remark, or the bleak "I don't want to get better – I want to get worse." The essayist notes the last "might sound like a strange thing to say, even kind of a joke, a comical expression of recalcitrance from an unrepentant drunk," but reads it as "the simplest distillation of the worst thing depression does." Every option is a thought the protagonist already had; the player chooses which to voice. The essayist hesitated over the bleakest "because I knew Kim wouldn't want to hear that," and chose the gentle one, as they report doing in life: the contrast between "the thought that bubbles to the surface" and "what I know they want to hear." This is **the essayist's interpretation, from a reader writing about their own depression**, not a developer statement and not about soldiers; it concerns concealing a bleak thought to spare a friend, not joking. The essay adds that dialogue variety is "usually" there "for humor or for the purposes of allowing roleplaying" and that the game "is extremely funny" (its only direct statement about the game's comedy).
*Candidate application:* A veteran character can deliver a quip-shaped line that a close listener hears as a plain statement; and a companion who has the grim thought and picks the acceptable line for a partner's sake shows concealment without a joke.

**[E22b-16]. Escalating "jokes" as a warning signal that exhausts its audience (restrained).** (WA-074, L11, L51)
The essay says other characters recall that the protagonist made increasingly graphic "jokes" about wanting to die, and that the few people left "can only barely tolerate him," their concern "twisted into anger and resentment." Together this describes **a failure mode of dark humor in a fictional character: repeated, escalating jokes that function as warnings eventually wear out those who would answer them.** The essay does not analyze it as humor; I am connecting two of its statements.
*Candidate application:* If a character's gallows humor has historically doubled as a distress signal, his audience may have learned to stop hearing it, and their weariness belongs to the characterization.

**[E22b-17]. Design device: personality traits as intrusive voices.** (WA-074, L19-27)
Each skill is an aspect of the protagonist's personality that interjects with its own view and sometimes squabbles; heavily invested, one demands indulgence, another is hypersensitive to disrespect, another preens. The essayist argues this simulates intrusive thought better than one inner monologue. A passage on a self-directed cruel option and a mechanic tied to suicidal thought is summarized in the register. Not about soldiers.

### From WA-075 (Spec Ops: The Line GDC talk summary)

**[E22b-18]. Tie the character's change to the violence.** (WA-075, L11-15)
A news item on a free GDC 2013 video by Walt Williams ("We Are Not Heroes: Contextualizing Violence Through Narrative"). Williams outlines a four-step process "dubbed 'the illusion of casuality'" (the page's spelling) to make moment-to-moment violence meaningful. Only two points are named: **"taking the player out of the equation"** when embracing the gap between what players can and cannot control, and **evolving a character through narrative traits**, here from "professional to unhinged," tied to the main combat. The overview credits the game with a narrative "that spoke directly to the act of playing it." Nothing on humor or veteran input.

### From WA-076 (EA on Army of Two's failed humor)

**[E22b-19]. The failure case: the quip after the kill.** (WA-076, L5-11)
The outlet reports EA admitting humor in the first *Army of Two* "went down like a lead balloon," made worse by protagonists' "tasteless attitude toward their prodigious amount of killing" for profit in settings of real conflict. EA Montreal's boss Alain Tascan gives the diagnosis: "The mistake we made was we thought that what is going to make a certain type of person laugh, is going to make everybody laugh. The reality is, you can laugh off everything but not with everybody." The failing combination: "killing terrorists for money and then fist pumping and saying something funny afterwards, in a place where real life our troops are dying." And **the target was unreadable**: "People from the left thought we were on the right; people from the right thought we were on the left." Four failures in one statement: niche humor delivered to a mass audience; a joke right after a killing with celebration; killing for pay; a real setting with real casualties. This is company self-criticism, with no complaint, sales or review data.
*Candidate application:* The source's test is not whether a character may joke after violence but whether the work shows the joke from inside a group that has earned it, so it does not read as the game's own attitude. A quip tacked onto a kill, with no cost shown and no audience in the fiction, is the documented failure.

**[E22b-20]. The realism-versus-filter claim.** (WA-076, L15-19)
Tascan says the studio has "real recordings from mercenaries in the field," that the first game "took ten percent" of what was said in war, and that "it's not like in the movies, it's not appropriate": as a mass-market medium, "we have to filter." The sequel, he says, is "more Bruce Willis funny than Steven Seagal funny": two action-film comic registers the page does not explain (I read it only as a move to a wry, less swaggering tone). **Established:** an executive's claim that real field talk was rawer than anything used and that fidelity was not the goal. **Not:** the recordings are undescribed, from contract fighters rather than enlisted soldiers, and uncheckable. The outlet's remark that "we can only imagine what the real guys actually get up to" is speculation.
*Candidate application:* Any depiction of gallows humor is a filtered one; the real choice is which filter, and it should follow the work's relationship to its audience.

### From WA-077 (games' lazy depiction of PTSD)

**[E22b-21]. The trauma-as-backstory warning.** (WA-077, L9-11)
Engadget's note on a Eurogamer video ("Low Batteries") examining *Metal Gear Solid V*, *Spec Ops: The Line*, *Deus Ex: Human Revolution* "and a few others" reports: games use PTSD "as short-hand to fill in character backstory or motivations" and are sometimes lazy about it. The writer likens this to the kick-the-dog trope and to film and TV using sexual violence as motive shorthand at the victim's expense (summarized; see register), and hopes games will improve. The opening one-in-three statistic is unsourced; I do not rely on it. Not stated: the video's reasoning, good-practice examples, any veteran voice.
*Candidate application:* If a defector's or soldier's service trauma is his only characterization, it is shorthand (compare E22b-11). A candidate remedy: give him content that is not his injury (skills, tastes, opinions, a sense of humor), so the trauma is one thing about him.

### From WA-078 (why veterans play Helldivers 2)

**[E22b-22]. How veterans reacted to a satire of the military machine.** (WA-078, L25-47, L57-71, L80-82)
Author Clay Beyersdorfer (Army veteran, stand-up comedian) argues that *Helldivers 2* (2024 co-op shooter) is satire that "doesn't glamorize war. It pokes fun at it," making the player "a cog in a machine that only half works and is pointed in the wrong direction." The reaction described: veterans watching "with a mix of laughter and gallows humor" and sending group-chat screenshots that begin by saying the game feels exactly like some deployment. The recognized material is **institutional failure**: an incomplete operation order, a "busted radio," a vague promise of support, "enthusiastic but ineffective leadership," missions "written by someone who got halfway through the planning process and then went on leave," leadership defaulting to "whoever's the loudest — or the least wrong." Two named veterans: a Navy veteran calls it "basically 'Starship Troopers' online" that "doesn't take itself seriously," and says its over-patriotism enhances the humor "when we knowingly commit tongue-in-cheek war crimes against robots and bugs in the name of democracy" (a joke inside a fictional bug-and-robot war). Idioms the author says veterans use when a mission goes sideways from poor comms or missing gear: "big Army energy" and "green suit solutions." The author's thesis: the game gets right "the in-between. The waiting. The screwups. The dark humor." **Limits:** one opinion essay, two interviewees chosen unexplainedly, no survey, no dissenter, U.S. veterans of the Global War on Terrorism era only.
*Candidate application:* Veterans' dark humor is documented here as aimed chiefly at **the system that sends them out under-equipped and under-informed**, and a depiction earns recognition by getting that target right and not making the soldier a hero. Jokes about "the plan" and its authors are more characteristic than jokes about the enemy.

**[E22b-23]. Humor and chaos as a substitute unit, reunion across distance.** (WA-078, L51-53, L73, L80-82)
The author says the game's loose format lets veterans scattered "across time zones, duty stations, or retirement destinations" play together, so "the humor and chaos become a bonding tool — and a nostalgic one." The Army veteran quoted: the best part is time with friends he has not seen in years: "The last time I had this much fun with these guys, we were in the barracks." The game is called a "digital deployment with old friends, complete with inside jokes, minor disasters, and just enough structure to keep it from collapsing," and the author says it taps the "unit cohesion" veterans remember as "the glue." The nostalgia is selective: it keeps the shared chaos and drops the authority ("without any paperwork, formations, or command climate surveys"). Silent about veterans without such friends or who left on bad terms.
*Candidate application:* Joking with former comrades can re-form the unit at a distance; a defector who left without those friends has lost the audience that makes the joking work and may seek a substitute (a crew, a game, a bar) that supplies the chaos without the command.
*Cross-use:* Exile or defector communities could form unit-like joking circles around shared absurd situations, without a chain of command.

### From WA-043 (MOAA on realism in military games)

**[E22b-24]. What advisors say they fix.** (WA-043, L5-19)
Retired Army Lt. Col. Hank Keirsey (advisor on the *Call of Duty* series since its first game, 2003) says developers are "wickedly smart" but "have not been on the ground in a combat engagement," so "sometimes their dialogue is off, the equipment is wrong, the tactics wrong," and former service members "can help to steer the game a bit closer to the mark." Fun is "foremost," but, he says, many players know when a game strays too far into fantasy and want accuracy, down to the radio procedure when a sergeant calls for artillery. Marine veteran Sgt. Maj. James Dever describes teaching stunt performers tactics and weapons handling, correct uniforms, and how weapons fire. So the documented remit: **equipment, tactics, procedure, uniforms, weapons handling and (per Keirsey) dialogue in the sense of what sounds right.** **Nothing about humor, banter or tone.** The sources are the advisors themselves, who sell the service; the article describes advisors finding "their niche" in a "lucrative" industry.
*Candidate application:* Ask an advisor whether procedure, vocabulary and equipment sound right; the sources do not show advisors shaping what characters find funny, so that needs a different reader (a veteran of the relevant kind reading for tone).

**[E22b-25]. Reception evidence is the advisors' own, and their experience has an era limit.** (WA-043, L21-35)
Retired Marine Capt. Dale Dye (founder of an actor-training company) says his mission is to "teach civilians what it's like to be a man or woman in the military profession," that he and veteran colleagues bring experience "to instill the game experience with emotional truth," yet admits *Medal of Honor* was mostly World War II, which "has differences from my wartime experiences." He says the studio received "excellent responses" from veterans and tells one story: active-duty Marines on Iwo Jima, escorting a team and veterans of those battles, brought older *Medal of Honor* games they said they played constantly aboard ship to be signed. **This is the advisors' own report, not data.**
*Candidate application:* For a defector of one particular period, an advisor from a different era or branch is a partial reader at best; ask which part of his experience matches.

### From WA-044 (breaking into military advising in Hollywood)

**[E22b-26]. What a screen advisor does and how scarce the work is.** (WA-044, L7-19, L35-51)
Written for veterans, from a podcast with two consultants and a Q&A with veteran actor Trevor Scott (the supply side, not the hiring side). Documented: advising is "sporadic and highly competitive"; big productions use "the handful of well-known military advisers who have been in the industry for a long time"; beginners start on student and short films, often unpaid; it is "a business of connections and networking," through veteran film groups or a GI-Bill-funded film school; living in Hollywood helps but is not required (remote advising on films shot abroad). Scott: "Sometimes you're teaching actors tactical stuff; sometimes you're just there to spot-check uniforms or listen to dialogue and see if it rings true." Qualities sought: tactical proficiency (or other skill) and temperament: willing to learn while teaching, pleasant to be around, following protocol; a veteran with "a chip on their shoulder" is warned against.
*Candidate application:* A realistic advisor contributes detail-checking and "does it ring true" listening, with limited power; ask for that, not authorship of the character.

**[E22b-27]. The limits of the remit: accuracy yields to budget, schedule and, in one stated case, the joke.** (WA-044, L55-67)
Scott says an advisor learns to "pick and choose your battles," since no production is fully accurate, "just like no cop show or medical drama." Reasons given: budget (no up-armored turrets, optics or night-vision goggles); an actor who cannot get a military haircut because of another job days later; and **comedy**: "Maybe it's a comedy and the joke gets killed if the actor playing the PFC is at rigid parade rest the entire scene." Interrupting a star's monologue over a wrinkled collar could end the job. His rule: "make sure that all of the big things are right and that the military is being represented in a fair way," then as many small details as possible. His example of a slip: a qualification tab on the wrong side of the uniform top given to the actor playing a sergeant major, missed because he was with other actors ("Of all the millions of little things I fixed on that show"), which his friends still tease him about. It documents the audience: **veteran friends notice small errors and "never let you live down" them.** One interviewee's account of one TV comedy; nothing on tone or the content of jokes.
*Candidate application:* Comic effect can outrank literal accuracy in what reaches the screen, and veteran scrutiny falls on details more than tone; a depiction can get the big thing right (fair representation) and still draw teasing over small ones.

**[E22b-28]. The vetting lesson, supply side: claimed credentials are common and are checked.** (WA-044, L23-27)
Under "Watch Out for Stolen Valor": many applicants "claim to be something they are not," some claiming to be veterans, others veterans claiming elite units they were not in; the consultants running the company "know the difference." The advice comes from a career page with an interest in encouraging applicants, and gives no number or incident.
*Candidate application:* Credential claims are a known problem: ask for specifics a real service member can give (unit, dates, job) rather than relying on a title.

### From WA-045 (interview with a Ghost Recon Breakpoint writer and advisor)

**[E22b-29]. One person as writer and advisor, and the "authenticity versus fantasy" balance.** (WA-045, L9, L21)

### From WA-049 (Elliott Kay, writing military fiction)

**[E22b-36]. A veteran author's rule: talk to people who have been there, and expect veterans to split on whether you got it right.** (WA-049, L5-17)
Kay (four years in uniform; military science-fiction writer) writes after a convention panel on which every panelist was a veteran. Some combat veterans hold that nobody can understand combat without being there; Kay agrees there is truth in it but says it "shouldn't hold you back" and that "A good writer should make every reasonable effort to speak with people who've been in (or at least close to) the situations they want to write about." **A documented split in veteran reaction to their own books:** some veterans, including combat veterans, wrote that Kay "nailed combat"; others said the protagonists would not last two minutes in real combat. **[E22b-37]. Source materials, and the "half the experience" point.** (WA-049, L19-51)

## Pattern roll-up

**Rules of license**
- Earned by exactness of detail, and by naming the target: the institution's madness, not the dead (E22b-1).
- Audience-fit is the failure line: "you can laugh off everything but not with everybody" (E22b-19); humor right after a killing, with celebration, for pay, in a real ongoing war, with an unreadable target (E22b-19).
- Veterans themselves license a satire that aims at the machine and does not glamorize war (E22b-22).

**Targets**
- The plan, the op order, leadership and equipment: the system that sends people out under-equipped (E22b-22); the war's incoherent causes (E22b-2).
- Outsiders addressed by function or label (E22b-6).

**Forms and mechanics**
- Inflation and understatement; feigned madness; naive literalism (E22b-2); insider idioms such as "big Army energy" and "green suit solutions" (E22b-22); nicknames and borrowed film lines (E22b-6); a bleak line shaped like a quip (E22b-15).

**Functions**
- Bonding and re-forming a unit at a distance (E22b-23); concealment of a bleak thought to spare a friend (E22b-15).

**Limits and failure modes**
- The quip after the kill (E22b-19); humor withdrawn at the end so the weight lands (E22b-3); escalating jokes as a distress signal that wears out the audience (E22b-16); humor and disclosure sharing one forum (E22b-34).

**Civilian-facing register**
- Mass-market filtering of real talk (E22b-20); civilians want the sad half of the experience, not the pride or the fun (E22b-37).

**Defector, exile and unit-less specifics**
- Substitute units around shared chaos (E22b-23); the ex-comrade antagonist needs a motive the player understands (E22b-30); trauma as shorthand (E22b-11, E22b-21).

**Craft observations**
- Backstory known to the writer, revealed through events, governs speech (E22b-10, E22b-14); reticence as characterization (E22b-10); line-by-line comic iteration (E22b-4); table reads by non-authors (E22b-8); embed notes and subjects present (E22b-5, E22b-6); no flattering self-portrait, no slogan (E22b-7); change tied to the violence (E22b-18).

**Boone specifically**
- Documented developer statements: the quest's design and the melodramatic bill-of-sale detail (E22b-9); process of the DLC (E22b-8). Fan analysis: backstory, reticence, trauma engine (E22b-10, E22b-11). **Nothing in the set concerns Boone's humor (E22b-12).**

**Getting veteran input**
- Advisors fix equipment, procedure, uniforms, tactics and "what sounds right"; nothing shows them shaping humor (E22b-24, E22b-26); accuracy yields to budget and, once, to a joke (E22b-27); credentials are claimed falsely and are checked (E22b-28); a consultant's separate legal status can become the project's problem (E22b-31, E22b-32); one person as writer and advisor is a route (E22b-29); veterans split on the same work (E22b-36); forums are a skewed window (E22b-35); organized veteran-writer programs exist (E22b-33).
