## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-03 is.** The Project Gutenberg proofread text (eBook #70441, produced from Internet Archive/Canadian Libraries page images) of the 1916 Cassell volume *The Anzac Book*, "Written and Illustrated in Gallipoli by The Men of Anzac," for the benefit of the Corps' patriotic funds. The title page and Editor's Note say it was collected between 15 November and 8 December 1915 in the trenches and dug-outs at Anzac, finished at Imbros and aboard ship after the evacuation was announced, and signed off 29 December 1915. My range holds: Birdwood's introduction (dated 19 December 1915), the Editor's Note, then the literary items from "The Landing" (printed p. 1) through the beginning of "The Book of Jobs" (printed p. 136), including Sir Ian Hamilton's long dispatch extract (pp. 71-94). Contents are verse, sketches, anecdotes, parodies, essays, a rumor allegory, an alphabet, and many cartoon captions.

**Evidence base.** A volunteer, editor-selected anthology. The call went to "the whole population of Anzac"; the Editor says "the greater number of the contributors were private soldiers," that response was "enormous," and that long pieces were cut to "a half or a quarter." Bylines are mostly initials, pen names ("Soldieroo," "Ben Telbow," "Tentmate," "Q. E. D.," "Genesis Gallipoli," "Crosscut") or a rank plus unit; many contributors are not Australian or New Zealand (Royal Garrison Artillery, Suffolk Yeomanry, Northamptonshire and London regiments, R.A.M.C., Ordnance), so "Anzac" in this book means everyone on the beachhead. Because the editors selected and trimmed, and the book was made for a home public and for Birdwood's and Bean's own staff, it is a curated sample, not a census of what men said. Almost all of it is from the late, static phase (November-December 1915); the landing and the August battles appear only in retrospect or in Hamilton's dispatch.

**What it does NOT contain.** No jokes about named dead Australians or New Zealanders (the comic sketches of dead comrades are pseudonymous, E11a-24); no mention of executions, desertion, mutiny or shell shock by name; no unit-level grumbling about named officers; no sexual material beyond a Cairo bar-room anecdote (E11a-24) and a poem's reference to "absent, loving wives" (E11a-54); no atrocity by Anzac troops; no pictures (cartoons survive only as captions, and several illustrations are listed with no caption at all). The only "outside" item is Edgar Wallace's poem (E11a-42).

**Format and damage.** The Gutenberg text is clean, with italics shown by underscores (which I drop in quotations) and footnotes gathered after each item. There are no page markers, so I cite the **printed page from the Contents list plus line numbers ("L") of the text file**; for items that straddle pages the printed page is the starting page. Illustration captions are sometimes interleaved into the text where the original drawings stood. The Contents list also names later sections (Special A. & N. Z. A. C. Orders, evacuation items, telegrams, "Correspondence," "Anzac Fashions," "Answers to Correspondents," "Advertisements") that fall outside my range and are not described here. I skipped the Project Gutenberg license header at L1-40.

**Quotation conventions.** Quotations are exact except that (a) British spellings are converted to American per the project rule, in quotations and titles as well as in my text (e.g., "honor," "gray," "civilization," "pajamas," "airplane," "cozy"), (b) square brackets mark my insertions, (c) a few archaic or place spellings (Servia, Roumania, Mahomed) are left as printed, and (d) **graphic passages are summarized, not quoted**, as instructed (the landing wounds, the shell casualties in "Beachy," the wounds in "Icy," the burial in "Killed in Action," the atrocity passage in "The Flood"). Glosses of slang or of real people that the text does not itself explain are mine and are marked "my gloss." **Period national and racial caricature is documented, not endorsed**: Turks ("Abdul"), Indian soldiers ("Johnnie"), Maltese, Egyptians, a Maori medical student, Sikhs, Cairo entertainers, and a German-dialect line all appear in period terms.

**Retrospective vs. period text.** Everything in the literary items is written in November-December 1915. The Editor's Note, Birdwood's introduction and the editors' footnotes are slightly later (after the evacuation order) and know the camp is being abandoned; the verse and sketches do not.

## Findings

### A. Front matter: the commander's frame and the editor's account

**[E11a-1]. Birdwood's introduction: the corps commander's own frame is "cheerfulness," comradeship and a tribute to the Navy, not humor.** (PW-03, Introduction by Lieut.-Gen. Sir W. R. Birdwood, printed pp. ix-xii; dated "Anzac, December 19, 1915"; L322-424)
Birdwood thanks the "inhabitants of our little township" for giving "the free use of their brains and hands," and asks critics to "remember the circumstances under which the contributions have been prepared, in small dug-outs, with shells and bullets frequently whistling overhead." He explains the word "Anzac" as a telegraphic code address he picked for the Army Corps in Egypt, later applied at General Headquarters' request to the beach, "Anzac Cove." He writes that eight months there stamped on every man "days of trial and anxiety, hopes, and perhaps occasional fears, rejoicings at success, and sorrow—very deep and sincere—for many a good comrade whom we can never see again," and that having "to look death straight in the face so often" has "made better men of every one of us." He closes with "the never-failing courage, determination and cheerfulness" of those who "fought and given their lives." One paragraph is a thank-you to the Royal Navy, ship by ship, ending "Boys! Hats off to the British Navy." The humor of the book is never discussed; the word he uses for the men's temper is "cheerfulness," and he quotes the Harrow school song about growing old ("What will it help you that once you were strong!") as the way the memory may feel in later years.
*Candidate application:* The commander's description of soldier morale ("cheerful," "courage") and the soldiers' own comic output are two different accounts of the same weeks; a commander-character may sincerely believe the first and never read the second.

**[E11a-2]. The Editor's Note: a "mere pastime" for the winter that became a relic when the evacuation was announced.** (PW-03, Editor's Note by the Anzac Book Staff, printed p. xiii; dated "Aegean Sea, December 29, 1915"; L439-551)
The editor states the book was produced "in the lines at Anzac" in the last weeks of 1915, "practically every word" written and "every line drawn" under a waterproof sheet or sandbag roof "within the range of the oldest Turkish rifle, and under daily visitations from the smallest Turkish field-piece," with "the crack of the Mauser bullets overhead" never ceasing; "at least one good soldier ... who was preparing a contribution for these pages, met his death while the work was still unfinished." The purpose was "a New Year Magazine to help this little British Australasian fraternity in Turkey to while away the long winter in the trenches"; the idea came from Major S. S. Butler of the Corps Staff; a notice of 14 November called for contributions from "the whole population of Anzac"; the closing date was 8 December; profits were to go to patriotic funds. The news of the evacuation arrived "from the sky" while editing was under way, so "this production, which was to have been a mere pastime, had now become a hundred times more precious as a souvenir." Materials were "iodine brushes, red and blue pencils, and such approach to white paper as could be produced from each battalion's stationery." The response was "enormous"; "the greater number of the contributors were private soldiers." Prize categories show the editors' own taxonomy: Cover; Drawing; Drawing (Comic); Prose Sketch; Prose (Humorous); Verses; Verses (Humorous). The only "outside" contribution is Edgar Wallace's poem. The editor stresses that the book was composed "in the full prospect of Christmas at Anzac," and that two storms (17 November, a thunderstorm, and 27-28 November, a blizzard with snow that Australians had never seen) fell in the same three weeks.
*Candidate application:* A morale publication that begins as filler and becomes a relic once the posting ends is a plausible origin for how a unit's humor later gets treated as precious; the humor/serious split was institutionalized in prize categories from the start.

### B. The landing and the first section of the anthology

**[E11a-3]. "The Landing": the unjoking baseline. A private's plain account of 25 April, with the only humor being a remark on the men's mood before.** (PW-03, "The Landing," by A. R. Perry, 10th Batt. A.I.F., printed p. 1; L572-715)
A plain first-person account: the platoon sergeant tells the men to "have a good, hot supper—there's business doing"; they eat bully beef stew aboard the battleship; "They laugh and joke as though picnicking"; then silence, barges, the landing under fire, men hit (described in the text with graphic detail that I summarize rather than quote), the first shrapnel ("baptized"), and a lieutenant wounded whom the narrator tries to help before being hit himself. The writer ends aboard a troopship, wounded, with the line "But I would not have missed it for all the money in the world." The one rumor is rendered in a parenthesis: after the order "Fix bayonets!" made the blades flash in the sunlight, the writer asks whether an enemy officer shouted it ("for they say such things were done"). No irony, no joke inside the combat narrative; the joking is placed before it, as a mood that stops when the barge fills.
*Candidate application:* A combat narration with no humor in it, placed first in a comic-heavy anthology, sets the register the humor is measured against. The soldier's closing line is of the type "I would not have missed it," a pride that is not a joke.

**[E11a-4]. "The Reminiscence of a Wreck": a Lewis Carroll parody about scavenging government timber from a storm wreck.** (PW-03, "The Reminiscence of a Wreck," Lieut. A. L. Pemberton, R.G.A., "Taylor's Hollow, 8.12.15," printed p. 7; L735-805)
The editors' preface explains that wood for dug-out roofs was "priceless," a storm left wreckage that was "strictly the property of H.M. Government as represented by the officer commanding the Royal Engineers," and "'A gift from Heaven,' one Australian was heard to remark." The verse follows the meter and plot of "The Walrus and the Carpenter": a Sergeant and a Junior Sub "wept like anything to see such quantities of wood" and "smiled a furtive smile which boded little good," then wait behind a bank "for the C.R.E. to go." The Carroll line is turned: "The time has come, the Sergeant said, to talk of many things— / Of bully beef and dug-outs, of Kaisers and of Kings, / And why the rain comes through the roof, and whether shrapnel stings." The last stanza: "Next morning came the C.R.E. to see what could be done; / But when he came to count the planks he found that there was none— / And this was hardly odd, because they'd collared every one." A sergeant and a junior officer collude against a senior engineer; the C.R.E.'s authority over timber is shown as a bureaucratic fiction ("If fifty kings and fifty queens and fifty C.-in-C.'s / Presented fifty indents and bowed low upon their knees, / I hardly think that they would get more than a few of these"). Mechanism: famous nursery-adjacent verse used as a wrapper for theft and for scarcity; the crocodile tears ("With sobs and tears they sorted out those of the largest size") belong to Carroll but fit the scheming. Note the author is a British Royal Garrison Artillery lieutenant, not an Australian or New Zealander.
*Candidate application:* Collusive scrounging across rank (sergeant plus junior officer) against a rear-echelon supply authority is a documented comic type; its legitimacy is that the item is scarce and the authority is far away.

**[E11a-5]. "An Australian Home in 1930": the future as a permanent dug-out.** (PW-03, "An Australian Home in 1930," by "Soldieroo," 2nd Field Co., Aust. Engrs., printed p. 9; L814-908)
A visitor in 1930 reaches Freddy Prince's "war selection" (a farm taken up on a soldier's allotment): a gate marked "Prince's Jolly," a "wide and gradually deepening sap" instead of a drive, a front entrance that is a dug-out, an old shell-case that serves as a doorbell, children who "charge you at the double," and the father whose wife issues him the news because he "didn't want to strain the one eye that was left to him." Homely detail is trench vocabulary carried into peace: "the milking sap hadn't been cleaned out the previous day because Georgie had forgotten he was pioneer"; "Jerry O'Dwyer had shot two crows from the new sniper's pozzy"; children use a periscope; two "biscuit photo-frames" are smashed ("it's time they began to break up after fifteen years"). The wife's news is that "the latest cable says it's still occupied by the German submarines" (Lake Achi Baba), and a child asks, "Achi Baba used to be a hill once, wasn't it, daddy?" — the stalemate at Helles, where Achi Baba was never taken, becomes a joke about a hill flattened by howitzers and replaced by a lake. The father looks into pipe smoke and says, "Yes, it was a hill once." The last line: the family live "as happily as if there were no war." The disabilities (the one eye) and the unnamed "many others" who adopted the underground home are given the same flat tone. Footnote defines "Pozzy or Possie" as "Australian warrior's short for 'position,' or lair."
*Candidate application:* The fantasy that the soldier's makeshift world becomes the normal domestic world (and that the war's unwon objectives become geography) is a form of coping in which the future is the present with children added. A single serious beat, the father's smoke-gaze at a hill that no longer exists, is allowed to puncture it.

**[E11a-6]. The cartoon "The 'Ric'": the new arrival and the understating officer.** (PW-03, illustration captioned "The 'Ric,'" Ted Colles from a sketch by C. McCrae, placed between "Non Nobis" and "The Aegean Wind," about printed pp. 13-14; L952-968)
Caption: *New Arrival (as something hums past the parapet):* "'Strewth! Wot's that?" *Officer:* "Only a ricochet." *N.A.:* "An' d'we use 'em, too, sir?" Mechanism: the veteran officer's understatement ("only") is read by the greenhorn as the name of a weapon; the question exposes how far the newcomer is from sharing the veteran's vocabulary. Target: the newcomer's ignorance, mildly; the joke is in-group because it requires knowing that ricochets are accidental.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's understated "only a ..." for a lethal event is a standard comic-instruction beat; the newcomer's naive follow-up is the laugh.

**[E11a-7]. "The Aegean Wind": a mock-complaint about weather as a personal welcome.** (PW-03, "The Ægean Wind," by H. B. K., printed p. 14; L980-1003)
Verse from the island staging area of Lemnos: "It blows the milk from out your tea, / The laces from your boots." The hyperbole is directed at a famous poetic subject ("Is this the soft Ægean wind / Which Byron raved about"), and the last stanza supposes the wind is "some invention / Which Providence has made / To give a breezy welcome to / The Third Brigade." Mechanism: literary-romantic setting vs. discomfort; weather given an intention toward the writer's own unit. Written about the pre-landing phase, so no Turks appear.
*Candidate application:* The first comic target of an arriving unit is often the weather and the place's glamorous reputation, not the enemy.

**[E11a-8]. "Our Fathers" and the sincere-verse register.** (PW-03, "Our Fathers," Capt. James Sprent, A.M.C. (3rd Field Amb.), printed p. 14; L1008-1025; prize noted at L521)
A sonnet on the settler ancestors: "Wandering spirits, seeking lands unknown, / Such were our fathers, stout hearts unafraid," arguing that the war is the first payment for "years of ease," "We shall defend your Empire or be slain." Sprent is the same captain the editors name as winner of the serious verse prize (Editor's Note, L521-522). The point for this extraction is register: the book's prize-winning serious verse is formal, imperial and filial, and carries no irony. Compare "Non Nobis," by the editor himself (C. E. W. B., p. 11; L924-947), a four-stanza prayer that "from good and great / Nothing save good can flow," and "Non nobis, Domine." Both mark where the editors place their sincere voice.
*Candidate application:* Parallel to the humor, a unit's formal, borrowed-register elegy (hymn, sonnet, Latin tag) is used for the dead and the cause, and these are not undercut by the comic pieces.

### C. "Glimpses of Anzac" (prose-sketch prize winner): humor and its withdrawal

**[E11a-9]. "Glimpses of Anzac": monotony as the thing the men revile, a shared "dogged grin," and the naked-bathing run under shrapnel.** (PW-03, "Glimpses of Anzac," Hector Dinning, Aust. A.S.C., 9th Co., printed p. 17; L1053-1277)
The opening premise is that "It's the monotony we revile, not—to a like degree—hard work or hard fare": the same beach, the same trench wall, "the same Turks." After a gale, the three piers are reduced to one: "A collective dogged grin overlooked the beach that morning," and the repairs "began forthwith; so did the bursting of shrapnel over the workmen. This stroke of Allah upon the unfaithful was not to go unassisted." On bathing: Birdwood's head is seen "bobbing beyond the sunken barge inshore" at six-thirty daily "and a host of nudes lined the beach"; "Beachy Bill" catches a bathing squad, "ducking and splashing shorewards, and scurrying by men clad only in the garment Nature gave them." The key question as shrapnel whips the water is "Will it ever stop?" (which means "Will the pellets ever cease to whip the water?"). A chaplain is a light-horse colonel "indistinguishable" in dug-out from the privates; he lies listening "to the forceful lingo of his neighbors, and confessedly admiring its graphic if well-garnished eloquence." The "visualizing of unstinted civilian meals is a prevalent pastime": "Some men excel in this." A colonel-chaplain "talk[ed] for two hours of nothing but grub," and the author concludes "Truly we are a people whose god is their belly." On vermin: "Of the plague of nocturnal vermin little need be said explicitly," so men "retire late and thus abridge the period of persecution."
*Candidate application:* The humor in the prose sketch is light and observational; it is coherent that a unit's central complaint is repetition (boredom) rather than danger, and that night-time vermin is something the writer declines to describe.

**[E11a-10]. The same sketch's turn to the serious: the off-duty dead, the burial party under fire, mail, and the thin church parade.** (PW-03, "Glimpses of Anzac," L1125-1274, continuing)
Right after the bathers: "The work of enemy shell behind the actual trenches is peculiarly horrible. Men are struck down suddenly and unmercifully where there is no heat of battle. A man dies more easily in the charge." The text lists deaths while "unloading a cart, drawing water for his unit, directing a mule convoy ... returning from a bathe or washing a shirt." One example is a man hit while retiring to read his "just delivered mail"; the narrator describes the wound's severity (summarized here, not quoted) and notes he "lives but a few minutes—with his pipe still steaming, clenched in his teeth." That night the party stands "about the grave in which he lies beneath his groundsheet" under a monitor's gunfire, while the "worn, big-hearted padre" intones the intercession. The writer adds that shells "sometimes burst in the midst of the burial party," the grave "hastily filled," and "fresh graves are to be dug forthwith for stricken members of the party. To die violently and be laid in this shell-swept area is to die lonely indeed." Church attendance is "scanty"; the address is "short and shorn of cant"; voices "go up in feeble unison, punctuated by the roar of artillery." Mail is "an anodyne": "Their arrival eclipses considerations of life and death—of fighting and the landing of rations." There is no joke in any of these paragraphs. The place where the humor stops is exactly where the dead are those who were off duty doing something small and domestic.
*Candidate application:* The deaths that stop the comedy are the unfair or domestic ones (off duty, reading mail), not the heroic ones; that is where a writer can mark a sincere register without breaking the comic frame.

**[E11a-11]. The parcel monologue in dialect, with dashes for oaths.** (PW-03, "Glimpses of Anzac," caption beneath the plate "At the Landing, and Here Ever Since," drawn in blue and red pencil by David Barker; text by Hector Dinning; L1283-1295)
A soldier unpacks a parcel from home in vernacular, line by line: "'Struth! a noo pipe, Bill!—an' some er the ole terbaccer. Blimey! Cigars, too!—'ave one, before the mob smells 'em...." The gifts get comic ranking: handknitted socks are "bonzer," and "Sling them issue-things inter the sea"; "'Angkerch'fs!—orl right w'en the —— blizzards come, an' a chap's snifflin' fer a —— week on end." The monologue ends "Aw, that's a dinkum —— parcel, that is!" The text prints "D——d" and "——" in place of oaths; the footnote at L1304 defines "Dinkum" as "Australian for 'true.'" Mechanism: dialect monologue as the vehicle for tenderness, unsentimentally masked with oath dashes; the hidden, shared treasure ("before the mob smells 'em"), and discarded army issue. The editors' typography (dashes for oaths) is itself an editorial layer that the Gutenberg text preserves.
*Candidate application:* In a comic piece, a soldier's affection for home can be shown only through the gifts and the oaths, and the printed dashes record that the civilian-facing version has been cleaned.

**[E11a-12]. "Parables of Anzac": two anecdotes of the veteran's deadpan and the self-mistaken enemy.** (PW-03, "Parables of Anzac," from a correspondent in the Australian Field Artillery, "Sea View," Boltons Knoll, near Shell Green, printed p. 23; L1312-1366)
(I) "From Shell Green": new reinforcements are "eagerly collecting spent fuses and shells as mementoes" during a bombardment; a falling fuse hits a man walking on the Green; a reinforcement rushes out, sees he is not hurt and asks, "Want the fuse, mate?" The other "looked at him calmly": "What do you think I stopped it for?" (II) "The Turk in the Periscope": Bill Blankson, "a real hard case, happy-go-lucky, regardless of danger," comes off a fortnight of sapping with unshaved stubble and sees "a dark, dirty face with a wild growth of black stubble glaring straight back at him" in the periscope, and "scrambled up the parapet, fully intending to finish the Turk." "He had mistaken his own reflection for a Turk's." A footnote glosses Chatham's Post, "attacked by the Turks for several days in November." Mechanisms: the veteran's retort that recasts an injury as an acquisition; the self-as-enemy mistake that converts the foe into a grubby mirror. The author calls them "parables," a mock-scriptural label.
*Candidate application:* The wording "Johnny Turk" and "hard case" mark a register in which the enemy is a familiar neighbor, and the veteran's retort prizes calm over eagerness.

**[E11a-13]. "The Yarns that Abdul Tells": the enemy's own storytelling tradition passed on to the Anzacs.** (PW-03, "The Yarns That Abdul Tells," by A. P. M., "writes an officer who knows them intimately," printed p. 24; L1378-1403)
The writer says the Turks "behind the black and white sandbags opposite" pass the time with storytellers of the Asia Minor cafés, whose hero is "a certain Nastradi Hodja (who really existed at one time, and made a reputation by his wit as well as through his stupidity)." The example: the wife hears a noise at night; he says "It was only my shirt that tumbled down the stairs"; "Does a shirt make such a noise?" "No ... but I was in it." Mechanism: an absurd explanation whose final clause reveals the speaker was the thing that fell. The anecdote presents the enemy as people with a comic tradition of their own and invites the Anzac reader to share it, yet the writer also repeats the stereotype of "stupidity" for the folk hero.
*Candidate application:* Documentation: the Anzac Book reports the opposing soldiers' humor with some respect (this is the only joke in my range that is explicitly the enemy's).
*Cross-use:* The idea that enemies tell each other the same kind of jokes as one's own side.

**[E11a-14]. The verse elegies and the flower-bird: where the book keeps its pastoral, patriotic voice.** (PW-03, "The Graves of Gallipoli," by L. L., printed p. 25; L1413-1458. "To a Lyre-Bird," by H. J. A., 8th Batt. 2nd Infantry Brigade, printed p. 26; L1465-1487. "Graves at Anzac" photographs, L1493-1502)
"The Graves of Gallipoli" is a classical-allusion elegy: the "Trojan said" that those who fell "under their father's eyes" were happier than "we who die in exile," then the stanza "And we will mourn them proudly as of right— / For meaner deaths be weeping and loud cries: / They died pro patria!"; the epitaph "Traveler, south or west, / Go, say at home we heard the trumpet call, / And answered" (my gloss: it echoes the ancient Greek "Go tell the Spartans" epitaph, and the Trojan quotation just before it points the same way). The explicit norm is that the proper reaction to these deaths is pride and not weeping. "To a Lyre-Bird" praises an Australian bird that mocks other songs, "thousand mocking notes of mirth," as a "singer ... gyv'd to the soiling earth, yet singing still divine." Neither is a joke; both are the book's home-country, nature-and-patriotism voice, and the lyre-bird (Australian) is the one native-fauna image among the front-line pieces. Photographs of cemeteries, including "The Grave of a Brigadier—Col. H. N. Maclaurin, killed April 27, 1915," show the dead with captions only.
*Candidate application:* Honor-norm: weeping and loud grief are labeled as "meaner" in the sincere verse, which sets a social rule the humorous pieces can then be measured against.

**[E11a-15]. "The Never-Ending Chase": lice as the only subject of a two-stanza anticlimax.** (PW-03, "The Never-Ending Chase," Am. Park, printed p. 30; L1520-1539)
Two quatrains: the narrator, "weary and ill at ease," sees a gunner "carefully / Scanning his sunburned knees," asks what he is looking for, and gets only "a long-drawn sigh / As he quietly killed one more." The word "chase" in the title and the delaying question set up a hunting or a lost-property story; the reveal is the louse hunt. Mechanism: bathos by withholding the object; the word "quietly" makes the killing a private, habitual act.
*Candidate application:* The vermin hunt is so universal that the poem never names the insect (the writer needs no word), a sign of an in-group shorthand.

**[E11a-16]. "Anzac Dialogues": banter across the Australian, British and Indian ranks, with the Turkish gun "Beachy," bully beef and grumbling at its center.** (PW-03, "Anzac Dialogues," N. Ash (no unit printed here; the same name carries "11th A.A.S.C." on "A Gray Day in Gallipoli," E11a-51), printed p. 31; L1544-1641)
A fatigue party among a stack of bully-beef boxes: "Tommy Kangaroo" (Australian) and "Tommy Atkins, or 'Kitch,' as he is beginning to be called" (a British soldier; my gloss: the nickname alludes to Lord Kitchener), plus an Indian muleteer who says "Beachy no good—mule good." The Australian says, via the footnote, that "Pommy" is "short for pomegranate, and used as a nickname for immigrants" (the book's gloss). The dialogue about "Pommy" has an explicit rule: "we don't call the like of 'im 'Pommies' because we dislike 'em, but just as a matter of description," although a Briton who "gets 'is back up and calls us sons of convicts in return for us chuckin' off at 'im" is "told lots of things—sometimes true and very often untrue." On the Turkish guns: "there's old 'Beachy'[footnote: 'a battery of Turkish guns, well known on Anzac Beach'] at it again ... 'Ee's a fair cow, 'e is. Made me spill two buckets er water this mornin'." On rear-echelon work: "A man's got a chance to hit back [in the trenches], but down 'ere it's up to putty. It's bad enough to be eatin' bully beef, but carryin' it as well is rotten. I couldn't look a decent bullock in the face now for what I've said about 'im when 'e's tinned." The Turkish war-cry is rendered as "yellin' to Aller to stick to 'em" and the speaker curses it, since "Keepin' a man stuck on 'ere" is the real grievance. The brand slogan is used as a pun: "Prime boys jugglin' Best Prime Bully Beef." A mate's closing line: "You're always growlin'—you'll want flowers on your grave next." Side details: the Australian says he gave his regimental badges to a young stage singer in a Cairo music hall, and Kitch remarks "You're about the fifteenth lad that I know has given his baadges to 'er"; at the end Bill promises to try to get Kitch a badge "to send to my married sister's little lass." Oaths are printed as dashes (D——d).
*Candidate application:* Documented comic types here: banter that turns national nicknames into descriptions; chronic grumbling that the group jokes about by predicting its end; the rear-echelon man's grievance that he is targeted without a chance to reply.

**[E11a-17]. "From Quinn's Post": a conventional love lyric under the name of the most dangerous post at Anzac.** (PW-03, "From Quinn's Post," Pte. V. N. Hopkins, A.M.C. att. 17th Aust. Bn., printed p. 32; L1656-1673)
A sentimental poem: "Celestial star that crossed my path ... Drift back, bright star, and comfort me / In this unending, dreary day." There is no joke, no Gallipoli detail and no irony in the text; the only trace of the place is the title. The contents list gives the title and the author's attachment ("Pte., A.M.C., att. 17th Aust. Bn.") and nothing else; the poem itself is the content. The register is the Victorian love-lyric, used straight (whether it is meant sincerely cannot be settled from the text, but nothing in it signals irony).
*Candidate application:* Documentation of a soldier's non-comic, unironic longing in a conventional register; the war is excluded from the text.

**[E11a-18]. "The Happy Warrior": a refrain that lines up sand, flies and shells as equivalent nuisances.** (PW-03, "The Happy Warrior (A Soliloquy), Somewhere in the Anzac Zone," by M. R., illustrations initialed D. B., printed p. 33; L1695-1737)
Three seven-line stanzas each open "In my ... dug-out by the sea," with "I'm as happy as happy can be," and each ends on a different nuisance: "And we always have sand in our tea" (washing with sand because water is scarce); "For you always have flies in your tea!" (the cook and colonel "agree that you must have some semblance of grace ... to prevent you from damning the eyes of the savage and pestilent flies"); and "Tho' the hens have vacated the land, / Yet we always have shells with our tea!" Mechanism: the refrain reads as a list of domestic annoyances in which shells sit alongside sand and flies; the "happy" persona in the title is a parody of Wordsworth's poem. The line about the "cook and colonel" agreeing is a rank-collapsing joke: a decorum about swearing shared by the cook and the colonel.
*Candidate application:* Equivalence-by-list: placing the lethal item as the third in a trio of nuisances is a documented technique for flattening threat.

**[E11a-19]. "How I Shall Die": a peaceful-death fantasy.** (PW-03, "How I Shall Die," Pte. Chas. Lowry, 9th Aust. Battalion, printed p. 34; L1749-1768)
Three quatrains in which a speaker waits for evening, then "My little barque I'll gently slide / Into the still lagoon," while "Here storms are fierce and nations wage / Across the seas their strife." The final stanza is "I only wait the last, long call— / Perchance a short farewell— / Then gently for the mists to fall / O'er silent hill and dell." No weapons, no wounds, no comic touch; the title names the subject, the poem avoids the manner. In the middle of an anthology of vermin and shells this is one of the book's rare first-person pieces about one's own death, and it imagines a gentle one.
*Candidate application:* Documentation: a private soldier's imagined death is a dream of calm water; the way he does not imagine it (violent) is itself a pattern.

**[E11a-20]. "Beachy": an extended disguise story where the Turkish gun is a suburban train, and the reveal is a hospital bed.** (PW-03, "Beachy," Ted Colles, 3rd L.H. Field Ambulance, printed p. 35; L1778-1990)
A narrator visits a friend's "diggings" on a cold night; every few minutes there is "a whir and rattle past the door." The host: "Oh, don't go until after the next one"; "There's plenty of time yet ... There'll be a lot more of 'em. So you might as well sit tight in, safely and comfy, and try another cup." He adds that "they're running to a peculiar sort of time-table to-night—passing about every seven minutes," and recommends the "short cut ... past the corner of the cemetery. That's where they stop. So for God's sake shake it up." The story uses suburban imagery (a "footway," "monuments," a "suburb," "a passing suburban train") until the narrator is struck down on the cemetery track and wakes in a bed, aboard a hospital ship, slowly working out that the "friend" is in a dug-out lit "by a slush lamp made from bacon fat," that the rumbling was "a hurtling Turkish shell," and that the hot-water boy "Razzy" was one of a gang of Maltese laborers brought later to unload stores. Asked whether anyone else was there, the A.M.C. man answers "Only a Maltese—at least, we think he was" (my summary of the graphic detail that follows: the laborer was killed in the same blast), and the narrator, who a paragraph earlier had thought of "Razzy" as "a foreigner of some sort ... in some way very different and in a degree inferior" (documentation of period attitude to the laborer; the death is treated in the lightest register in the story), finds on trying to move his arm that his left hand is gone, told to him matter-of-factly. His response is the story's closing line: "Well, it's a rummy world," before he "drift[s] out of it." The title refers to "Beachy Bill," named in other pieces. The illustration "Racing Beachy Bill" by H. C. Wimbush is printed beside it.
Mechanisms: (1) a long frame of domestic comfort that conceals a battlefield; (2) the shell interval turned into a train timetable and, concretely, into a hospitality rule (stay for the next, leave after); (3) the narrator's slow recovery of the real, in his hospital bed, with detached comedy; (4) a final loss told in the same register as the rest.
*Candidate application:* A comic frame can carry the whole life-threatening fact until the end, where the writer lets it all go through at once; the shell-timing practice (waiting out intervals) is a documented, domesticated survival habit.

**[E11a-21]. "The Anzac Home — and a Contrast": a British officer's mock-complaint about his dug-out, then a London daydream cut by "Your turn for the trenches."** (PW-03, "The Anzac Home—and a Contrast," E. Cadogan, 1/1 Suffolk Yeomanry, printed p. 41; L2010-2093)
The dug-out "resemble[s] those of an exceedingly small family tomb—one which might belong to a family too proud not to possess a family tomb at all, but too poor to possess one of adequate size and comfort." "Where the fourth wall strictly should be is an exit which lets in the draft. Over my head are stretched waterproof sheets which let in the water." His kit "arranged round my feet like so many pet dogs begging for biscuit." Dinner is "an exceedingly stringy rabbit, extracted from a tin of an ominous purple hue," "bread, with the crust like a cinder, to which fondly cling bits of sacking and mules' whisker; the corpse of a cheese; and ... tea made in the stew dixie, and tasting more of dixie and stew than of tea." The reverie: a London November afternoon, "my servant glides into the room," cigars, port, "our big-game shoots, of our campaigns." The philosophical joke is the mock-syllogism "my contentment is due to contrast between my content with the existing situation and my past discontent with other situations." The cut: "'Wake up, old chap—three o'clock. Your turn for the trenches. It is snowing hard and the Turks are very active.' Contrasts indeed!" The same reveal-by-waking frame appears in "Beachy" (E11a-20). Register note: the contributor is a British yeomanry officer; the diction (servant, smoking-suit, big-game shoots) is upper-class British, and the humor is mock-genteel, not Australian vernacular.
*Candidate application:* The comedy of an officer class's imaginary comforts, cut off by a duty call, is a documented structure; it differs from the vernacular, privates' humor in the same book by tone and vocabulary.

**[E11a-22]. The cartoon "The Ass / The Victim": the idiotic question and the flat reply.** (PW-03, illustration by David Barker "after B. Hartman," L2106-2114)
Caption: *The Ass:* "Are you wounded, mate?" *The Victim:* "D'yer think I'm doing this fer fun?" Same shape as E11a-12 (fuse): a person asks the obvious; the injured person's retort turns the obvious into comic absurdity. The man on the receiving end is named "The Victim," the questioner "The Ass," a labeled cast.
*Candidate application:* Documented comic type: the "stupid question" retort; the retorter is always the wounded or hit, never the questioner.

### D. Vermin and "Anzac types"

**[E11a-23]. "Flies and Fleas": the war-reporting vocabulary applied to insects, with a surrender fantasy.** (PW-03, "Flies and Fleas," A. Carruthers, 3rd Australian Field Ambulance, printed p. 44; L2119-2173)
Public-domain text; I quote at length because the example is the point. The writer says his "benevolent neutrality" toward flies and fleas has become "one of most deadly warfare. No 'Hymn of Hate' has yet been composed which would give expression to the hatred which has possessed me." The flies: "Do you but go into the trenches in the endeavor to perform your duty to your country, and the flies immediately try to dissuade you by getting into your eyes, ears, nose and mouth." "You may imitate the Kaiser and 'strafe' them for all you are worth, but it is only waste of breath; they glory in this and come back all the more." The flies stepping onto syrup: "they will not retreat—that would be against all their traditions. Instead, they will struggle their way towards the center, where they gladly give up the contest and die. They are born conquerors. I doff my hat to them in spite of my hate." Then the fleas, "their allies": "Advancing in open or close formation, according to circumstances, the enemy attacks on every flank with fixed bayonets, in the handling of which his units are experts. If driven off, they come again in still greater numbers; they appear to have unlimited reserves of reinforcements which can be mobilized on the shortest notice. ... Counter-attacks in the dark are all in the favor of the enemy, and morning finds that they have withdrawn their forces to advantageous cover in the blankets, from which it is impossible to dislodge them. Keating's Powder is of no avail against the Gallipoli fleas; it requires a still higher explosive to have any effect." The closing: "The honors have so far fallen to the enemy. Personally, I would be inclined to discuss terms of peace, but I doubt not he is too depraved to accept my advances."
Mechanisms: military language turned on vermin (formation, flank, reserves, counter-attack, withdrawal to cover); the admiration of the enemy's persistence ("born conquerors"); the mock-surrender, a private-fantasy of negotiation that is unavailable against the Turks and that this enemy refuses; the use of the contemporary propaganda terms "Hymn of Hate" and "strafe." An orderly of a field ambulance writes a text that the editors prized; the sketch has no mention of the human enemy.
*Candidate application:* Vermin are a safe stand-in enemy because they can be admired, hated, and negotiated with in a way the actual enemy cannot, and the writer's hatred and admiration are aired in the idiom of the war's own press.

**[E11a-24]. "Anzac Types, 1: Wallaby Joe": the bushman sketch that ends at a grave marker.** (PW-03, "Anzac Types: 1.—Wallaby Joe," W. R. C., 8th A.L.H., printed p. 45; L2186-2285)
The sketch portrays an Australian bushman of about 38, "tall and lean, but strong as a piece of hickory," laconic, who can "swear profusely, and in a most picturesque vein"; when "a bursting shell from a '75' on one occasion blew away a chunk of prime Berkshire which he was cooking for breakfast, his remarks were intensely original and illuminative." In a Cairo beer hall, a portly performer says "Gib it kiss" (period caricature of a foreign dancer, with a "colored orchestra"); "But Joe had fled." He is "about as sentimental as a steamroller," yet "leaving the old nag behind hurt some." In the trenches he is "worth four ordinary men" at sapping; when his mate hears a rustle, Joe whispers "A hare." One night his mate returns "pale-faced and wild-eyed" and says Joe has been shot. "We brought the body in. He had been shot through the heart—a typical affair of outposts." The final lines show the wooden cross: "No. 008 TROOPER J. REDGUM, 20th Australian Light Horse. Killed in Action." Mechanism: the ordinary comic sketch (shyness, swearing) structures the reader's fondness, and then death is delivered by a flat official phrase ("a typical affair of outposts") and a formal cross; the surname on the cross (Redgum, a tree) is evidently a pen-name, consistent with "His real name matters little." The unit on the cross (20th) differs from the author's (8th), also consistent with a pseudonymous sketch.
*Candidate application:* The comic portrait followed by an official epitaph is a pattern for how a unit may remember a loved comrade: the funny version is told, then the formal record is stamped on it.

**[E11a-25]. "Anzac Types, 2: The Dag": a vernacular yarn about a fool who survives, with an officer's lenient joke.** (PW-03, "Anzac Types: 2.—The Dag," E. A. M. W., printed p. 47; L2287-2385)
Narrated in an Australian vernacular by a mate ("'Enessy was a dag if ever there was one!"). Episodes: (1) the newcomer in camp teased with the cry "Marmalade" replies "Wot's your complaint, mate?" and then "You'd better see a doctor, and 'ave it operated on right away"; (2) he steals bread and jam from the quartermaster's store while on picket, and when the new Q.M. man catches him he says "I thought I 'ad a fair cop ... I 'eard someone moving about in 'ere"; (3) on first seeing the trenches: "Strike me pink! But where's them Turks they talk about?" ... "Why, for us blokes to shoot at, of course!"; (4) a lit cigarette in his overcoat pocket at stand-to; the officer: "Well, Henessy, your pocket's on fire!" and then "Don't do it again!"; (5) on guard at the drinking water, he shoots a stray Indian donkey ("poor little Indian donkeys"), is "chaffed pretty considerable by 'is cobbers," and the next night approaches another shape with a big stick; this time it is an enemy and he is hit; he shoots back and kills the Turk (told in a euphemistic comic idiom about the dead man "go[ing] to join 'is Prophet"). He is evacuated; "that was near three months ago. I 'ear that the blighter is back on the beach now"; "But strike me! 'E's a bloomin' dag!" The footnote "[9]" glosses "cobbers" (mates). Mechanisms: the gaffe-prone but lucky fool; cooperation among the cover story and the lenient officer; and the closure by the narrator's affectionate insult. The yarn disposes of the Turkish dead in a single comic idiom, at the same speed as the rest of the anecdote.
*Candidate application:* The "dag" is a documented type: an inadvertent clown who is protected by the group (the officer lets him off; the cobbers chaff, but he is back at the beach), and the story's cruelty is limited to the enemy's death.

### E. Anzac types, animals, rumor, the enemy as a person, and the ration (printed pp. 49-69)

**[E11a-26]. "Bobbie of the New Army": a mock-eulogy for an officer who has merely gone sick, where the real grief is for his parcels.** (PW-03, "Anzac Types: 3.—Bobbie of the New Army," by "Tentmate," 11th London Regt., printed p. 49; L2392-2444)
"Bobbie's gone sick. This probably doesn't interest you. But, oh, how we miss him." Bobbie is "the Beau Brummell of the trenches," "the spirit of cheerfulness," on whom "bullets flew off ... at a tangent." Of the officers who arrived with the regiment only three remain; the others went "some wounded, some sick, and some—well, just left," a gap that the writer fills by quoting two lines of the "Burial of Sir John Moore" verse ("Where the foe and the stranger will tread o'er their heads") rather than naming death. When Bobbie is promoted to a company his "head didn't swell"; "Nature (in collusion with the M.O.) asserted her imperious will," and in hospital his "immaculately parted hair" is "framed in snowy-white pillows" ("won't there be a weeping and a wailing when the nurses have to let you go?"). The close is the turn: "'it's an ill wind that blows nobody good.' Bobbie's chocolate sweetened the bitterness of parting; Bobbie's tinned fruit sustains us in his absence; Bobbie's cigarettes sooth our sorrow." Mechanism: the full dress of a funeral tribute applied to a sick-leave evacuation, with the survivors consoled by his food and tobacco; the one line that approaches real loss (the dead officers) is carried by quotation and a dash. The contributor is a British (London Regiment) soldier.
*Candidate application:* Evacuation-by-illness is a commonly mourned-for-comedy occasion: the mock grief is partly real (they do miss him) and partly an inventory of what his leaving yields.

**[E11a-27]. "The Indian Mule Corps": a plain tribute, with "Beachy Bill" as the one thing that stops work.** (PW-03, "The Indian Mule Corps," by B. R. (A.S.C.), printed p. 50; L2456-2483)
A short appreciation: "What should we at Anzac have done without 'Johnnie' and his sturdy little mules?" The drivers are "hard workers, intelligent and anxious to please"; carts are beached "on that wild seashore" and drawn out at night; "One thing, however, really does hang up work for a time, and that is 'Beachy Bill' in action. Even then some of the 'Johnnies,' who are less fearsome than the rest, go on with their work, and have from time to time been hit." The closing is "all praise to the Indian Mule Corps." Documentation of the register: it is sincere and condescending in the same breath ("sturdy little mules," "little beasts"), and it uses "Johnnie" for Indian soldiers, the same label that the Anzac Dialogues piece (E11a-16) turns on, where an Indian muleteer is given a pidgin line. In the same piece Beachy is a work-stopper, not a joke.
*Candidate application:* Documented: soldiers' nicknames for allies from another army, used affectionately in print and with period condescension.

**[E11a-28]. Longing lyrics: "Hill 60," "From My Trench," "A Little Sprig of Wattle" (home voices with no joke in them).** (PW-03, "Hill 60," by C. J. N., printed p. 50; L2493-2509. "From My Trench," Cpl. Comus, 2nd Batt. A.I.F., printed p. 57; L2753-2771. "A Little Sprig of Wattle," A. H. Scott, 4th Battery, A.F.A., printed p. 67; L3213-3237)
"Hill 60" is a love-lyric from a soldier who "makes earth his bed / In this gray catacomb of unnamed dead" (the only reference to the dead and the hill's reputation), and who says "memory softens present pain." "From My Trench" sets a "Turkish moon" against a farewell walk "through the tree stems—you and I," wondering "whether then I cared as much / As now I do when far beyond your touch"; the editors' footnote notes the distant "rumble of the guns" at Helles was "constantly in the air at Anzac." "A Little Sprig of Wattle" is a tender verse about a mother's letter in which a pressed sprig of wattle lay: "The old home snug amidst the pines ... Is all ablaze with wattle." No irony, no coping humor, no war detail except the background. These three show how the book stores homesickness: in plain lyric, apart from the comic pieces. Compare E11a-17 and E11a-5.
*Candidate application:* Sincere longing and romantic regret are reserved for a different register from the comic one; a character may have both and not mix them.

**[E11a-29]. "Jenny": a mock-solemn obituary for a camp-pet donkey foal, with the editors' correcting footnote.** (PW-03, "Jenny," Lance-Corp. F. C. Dunstan, B Depot, 6th A.A.S.C., printed p. 53; L2520-2572)
The tone is pompous eulogy: "Her freedom of action was the envy of every soldier along the beach. Her disregard for the enemy's bullets and shells commanded our unbounded admiration. But whether her immunity for six months was due to the kindness of the Turks or their bad shooting, or her own good judgment, who can say?" Her parents "once having crossed over the border into civilization ... emphatically refused to return whilst the objectionable Hun element obtained in their native country" (a joke that the donkeys are refugees). "Her refined voice was music compared with the common 'hee-haw' which characterizes her kind, or the peremptory foghorn of the sergeant-major." Then the register shifts: "But now she is no more. Our sorrow is immeasurable." She died of a shrapnel wound; the mother "never left the babe," the "invincible iodine" failed, and "big Australian playmates" buried her in a "neat little grave." The editors' footnote 10 states the parents' origin is "a myth" and gives the real story: "Murphy, who bore a red cross between his two long ears," who with Pte. Simpson of the 3rd Field Ambulance "is said ... to have carried 72 wounded men from the firing line through Shrapnel Gully," before his master "met his death on one of these errands of mercy"; Murphy was hit by a shell but "happily survives." The sketch is mock-heroic and genuinely grieved at once; the editors' footnote treats the true donkey-and-stretcher-bearer story in a flat, straight register.
*Candidate application:* An animal can be the one safe object of expressed unit grief; the comic frame (rank-like honors, foghorn sergeant-major) shelters the sorrow rather than denying it.

**[E11a-30]. "Marching Song": a cheerful refrain over a list of kit that ends in the burial.** (PW-03, "Marching Song," by C. J. N., printed p. 54; L2577-2616)
Each stanza names one item and its fate, with the refrain "Boots, belt, rifle, and pack." The first stanza states the whole trick: "All you'll doff when you lie down to sleep; / All they'll take off when they bury you deep." Others: "Nails that rang gay on the cobbled street / Will have pierced through the sock into somebody's feet"; "Belt—for water-bottle and sword: / One to save life; the other—oh, Lord! ... One will be dry and the other wet" (my reading: the dry water-bottle and the sword wet with use); "Rifle—the soldier's only friend— / True, if you treat her well, to the end: / Feed her with five, and the tune she'll play / Will reach the heart of a Turkish Bey"; "Pack—that holds what a man most wants ... A Bible, a photo of heart's desire; / But you'll throw it away when you charge—or retire." The last: "Keep them oiled and keep them dry, / They'll see you home safely—by and by." Mechanism: a march-time catalog where each item's use is turned toward death (burial, killing) through quick rhymes; the final "by and by" is the conditional promise. Killing is in the rifle verse ("reach the heart"), phrased as a tune the rifle plays.
*Candidate application:* The gear list as a form for stating the odds: each thing is cared for because it might "see you home," and the line about burial is allowed inside the cheerful meter.

**[E11a-31]. "Furphy": the rumor chase, with the source who is never anyone.** (PW-03, "Furphy," by Q. E. D., printed p. 56; L2628-2748)
The colonel says of an optimistic rumor, "These furphies are the very devil," and the narrator sets out to find the first teller. The editors' footnote 11 explains the word: "Furphy was the name of the contractor which was written large upon the rubbish carts that he supplied to the Melbourne camps. The name was transferred to a certain class of news item, very common since the war, which flourished greatly upon all the beaches." Each person on the beach and in the gullies passes the same pattern: a news item with a rising number of allied soldiers who will land (Greece, 100,000; Italy, 200,000 then 300,000 at Helles with Achi Baba "to be taken to-night"; Roumania, 400,000; Russia, 500,000; France, 600,000) and a source who dissolves under questioning (the first informant describes "a tall man—no, he thought he was only middle height"; another says "he didn't seem to walk at all"; "'Just a bloke ... 'E 'ad two legs, two arms, and a 'ead, two eyes——' ... 'But, dammit, did 'e?'"). At Lone Pine the rumor is that the Turks, short of food, water and ammunition, will surrender; "A 75 just then knocked a portion of parapet over me. I remarked that anyone could see the information was right about Abdul being short of ammunition." At the end the narrator sees Furphy as "a man with an indescribable gait" who "seemed to float," who "sank into the earth" with "an oily grimace," and "there were small horns behind his ears" (my reading: Furphy is the devil). Mechanism: a chase allegory in which the rumor is a personified, tireless, untraceable figure, and the shell that interrupts the Lone Pine rumor undercuts it.
*Candidate application:* Rumors in a stalled campaign inflate in a predictable ladder (each retelling adds a hundred thousand) and are all about relief or enemy collapse; the man who "just went by" is the standard source.

**[E11a-32]. "Abdul": verse signed with the editor's initials, a toast to the enemy as a gentleman.** (PW-03, "Abdul," by C. E. W. B. (the editor's initials), printed p. 58; plate "Abdul" by Ted Colles facing p. 58; L2776-2828)
The poem begins as a toast list ("We've drunk the boys who rushed the hills, / The men who stormed the beach ... But, before the bowl is cool, / There's one chap I'd like to mention, / He's a fellow called ABDUL"). Abdul is "his hat, / Bobbing down behind a loophole ... And we mostly blaze at that," a night-time patrol with "signals—hoots and chirrups— / Like an early morning lark," "his big, black shape went smashing, / Like a rhino, through the trees." The enemy's attacks are "flung in, rank on rank ... And we've had some pretty shooting, / And—he knows the way to die." The sentimental turn describes the dying Turk (summarized, not quoted: his suffering is stated and "it fairly tore our hearts out; / But it's in the beastly game"). The last stanza concedes his "name be black as ink / For murder and rapine, / Carried out in happy concert / With your Christians from the Rhine," but "We will judge you, Mr. Abdul, / By the test by which we can— / That with all your breath, in life, in death, / You've played the gentleman." Mechanisms: toasting; mock-politeness ("Mr. Abdul"); an in-group judgment ("the test by which we can") set against atrocity reports and German allies; period racial descriptors ("black shape," "dark eyes") documented as of the time. The nickname "Abdul" for the Turkish soldier appears throughout the book's sketches and rhymes (E11a-13, 25, 31, 34, 47, 69 and others; in Hamilton's dispatch the name occurs only in a place name).
*Candidate application:* Respect for the opposing front-line soldier, expressed through the idiom of sport and gentlemanly conduct, is a documented stance that sits next to unqualified hostility toward the distant political leadership of the other side (see E11a-58).

**[E11a-33]. "A Confession of Faith" and the plain-spoken verses about one's own death.** (PW-03, "A Confession of Faith," Capt. James Sprent, A.M.C., printed p. 59; L2838-2862; with "How I Shall Die" (E11a-19) and "The Price" (E11a-49))
Sprent's four stanzas ask how he would be remembered: "Remember with a pang and yet no pain"; "I would not that a single heart should ache— / That some dear heart will ache is my one grief"; the last: "Bury the body—it has served its ends; / Mark not the spot, but 'On Gallipoli,' / Let it be said, 'he died.'" It is a first-person will, no joke in it, with a request for modest grave-marking, written by the same medical captain who wrote the formal "Our Fathers" (E11a-8).
*Candidate application:* A thoughtful soldier states a plain wish for how the dead should be marked; the later comic pieces about biscuit and vermin come from the same pages.

**[E11a-34]. "Our Friend the Enemy": a sympathetic sketch of the ordinary Turk, written as a sketch by "one who knows him very well."** (PW-03, "Our Friend the Enemy," H. E. W., A. & N. Z. A. C., printed p. 60; L2867-2935)
The first section portrays Abdul at home in a coastal village: fishermen, barber on the eve of Bairam, a mayor and his new fountain, "I am a happy man," a good wife, a ram bought for tomorrow's sacrifice. Then "Abdul is struggling along the main road ... He has been told there is a war. He does not question; he knows not the cause. It is fate." In the battle "His regiment is hard put to it, and, like sheep without a master, the men are preparing to disperse. Already German machine-guns from their rear are on to them. The road home means death. Like a man he faces the rush of his opponents." The last lines show him wounded and seeing "his home, his wife, his plantation of maize so promising," then "Allah was great—it was Kismet." No humor; the sketch relies on period Orientalist conventions (fatalism, "Kismet," exotic marketplace detail), documented here as of the time. Its point is humanization: the enemy has a home and a household. It also repeats a claim that German machine-guns stand behind the Turks (unverified in this text).
*Candidate application:* Documentation: the Anzac Book carries a pro-enemy-humanity sketch by one of the Corps, in the same pages as the comic foe-names.

**[E11a-35]. "Army Biscuits": the mock-heroic essay in praise of the thing the teeth lose to.** (PW-03, "Army Biscuits," O. E. Burton, N.Z.M.C., printed p. 61; L2940-3038)
Public-domain text; I quote at length because the example is the point. It opens with a refrain exclamation ("Biscuits! Army biscuits! What a volume of blessings and cursings have been uttered") and moves through catalog and inflated diction: "Biscuits, like the poor, are always with us"; "We eat our meat, not with thankfulness, but with biscuits. We lengthen out the taste of jam—with biscuits. We pound them to powder. We boil them with bully. We stew them in stews." The hardness is dramatized through teeth: "Remember the cracking of your plate, the breaking of this tooth ... how your finest gold crown weakened, wobbled, and finally shriveled under the terrific strain of masticating Puntley and Chalmer's No. 5's" (a brand pun; I read it as on Huntley and Palmers, my gloss). The taste: "Is it the delicious succulency of ground granite or the savory toothsomeness of powdered marble? Do we perceive a delicate flavoring of ferro-concrete with just a dash of scraped iron railings?" "Army biscuits taste like nothing else on the Gallipoli Peninsula." The mock-historical turn: "Agamemnon and the Greeks camped for ten years on the windy plains of Troy without one box of army biscuits. When Xerxes threw his pontoon bridge across the Narrows ... his transport included none of Teak Green and Co.'s paving-stones for the hardening of his soldiers' hearts" (another brand pun, my gloss: Peek Frean). Note the sly "armies have marched and fought, made sieges, retired according to plan" (official-phrase pun). The close joins comedy and a serious claim: after the war the biscuit "will have great scarcity value as curios, as souvenirs which one can pass on from generation to generation, souvenirs which will endure while the Empire stands," and "we can move to other theaters, we can change our clothes, our arms, and our generals, but we must have our biscuits, army biscuits, else we are no longer an army."
Mechanisms: mock-heroic panegyric; the classical-history parody right where the writers are (the Narrows and Troy); the quick pun on "retired according to plan." The target is the ration, and through it the army's habit of calling hardship hardening. The biscuit recurs in "How I Won the V.C." (E11a-46), and the issued-gear-versus-food complaint recurs in the "Anzac Limericks" (E11a-44).
*Candidate application:* The one shared, unchanging ration gets a comic panegyric; its comic frame states what the unit will keep as a relic.

**[E11a-36]. Two cartoon captions: "Gawd help the first bloomin' Turk I see to-night" and "Do they think we're on a bloomin' pic-nic?"** (PW-03, an illustration carrying the first caption at L3043, and the drawing "A Present from Home" by David Barker, Gallipoli '15, carrying the second, L3048-3056)
I report only the captions because the images are not in the text. The first is a line spoken by a soldier (the drawing's situation is not stated; the line promises ill to the next Turk). The second sits under a drawing titled "A Present from Home" and is presumably a complaint about unsuitable gifts (the image is not in the text; the caption itself says only that the senders must think the army is "on a bloomin' pic-nic"). It resonates with E11a-53 ("What Frank Thought"), where a girl sends hair-oil and pajamas.
*Candidate application:* The home-to-front gift mismatch is a standing cartoon topic.

**[E11a-37]. "The Lost Poem": a day at a quartermaster sergeant's dug-out, where requests drown out the muse.** (PW-03, "The Lost Poem," R. A. L., 1st Aust. Stat. Hosp., printed p. 65; L3061-3202)
The narrator visits the regimental poet, "also Q.M. Sergeant," who "when he is not writing requisitions or taking 'baksheesh' out of our rations, and watering our rum, ... writes poetry," surrounded by "bully-beef tins, empty cases, and his ill-gotten shares of our daily issues." His cover letter to the editor says "We have a Quartermaster, but, of course, I do all the work." Interruptions: galvanized iron for dug-outs; rubber boots; the cook, furious about a quarter-issue of water ("How the —— can I cook seventy beef teas, forty puddings and two hundred milk diets"), who recalls cooking "for shearing sheds out on the Barcoo, where it never rained"; "three hot-water bottles, a tin of Bovril, and some brandy for a sick soldier" ("I wonder how sick you have to be before you get brandy?"); the orderly officer about tents; boots with no laces; "a consignment of Christmas billy-cans" ("Do you think I've got your bloomin' billy-cans?"); and a list of five callers in a row: one wants paper to write to his girl, one "an old blanket and some twine to make a shroud for a man who had died," one asks the war news and, told to go somewhere "warmer than Port Darwin," "asked quietly if either of us could tell him if sheep would do well around Adrianople after the war." The climax: the sergeant-major bursts in about great-coats ("Men dying from cold because they've got no coats, and you writing poems"), asks if he is writing requisitions and is answered "It's a poem," after which he "fainted away" from "a tremendous shock from some unknown cause." The poet will not write now: "it would have been a splendid thing. All about a pretty girl in the surf who met a fellow from the bush...."
Mechanisms: a catalog sketch; the requisition economy as the supreme reality; the shroud request placed between a request for paper and one for war news, with no change of voice; the dream of settling land; the sergeant-major's collapse as farce. Targets: the Q.M.'s graft (alleged jocularly), supply failures, the sergeant-major, the poet's pretensions. The sketch is set in the stores of a hospital unit (the author's is the 1st Australian Stationary Hospital; ward and sick-diet requests appear), and it repeats accusations of graft in a joking voice.
*Candidate application:* A unit's logistics man is the standard target of affectionate graft jokes, and the day's work list is a good place to put a death inside a routine without comment.

**[E11a-38]. "The True Story of Sappho's Death": a classical parody with a battle footnote and a censor joke.** (PW-03, "The True Story of Sappho's Death," M. R., printed p. 68; L3242-3277)
The headnote claims it was "Deciphered—with much labor—by a bomb-thrower of the New Zealand Infantry Brigade from a very old tablet dug up in the trenches at Quinn's Post." The verse parodies Byron's "Isles of Greece": "The Isles of Greece! The Isles of Greece! / Where burning Sappho sang, / Both day and night, without surcease— / She didn't care a hang!" Sappho sings so much "She couldn't sing at all. / Her manager he docked her pay: / She didn't fill the hall!" and then jumps into the sea "One mile from Mitylene"; "monks, who never can unbend, / Press-censored all her lays!" The moral: "You cannot burn the candle-fat / At both the candle ends!" The footnote: "[NOTE.—The epic loses much of its beauty through a hurried translation from the Ancient Greek during a Turkish attack.]" Mechanism: high-culture subject turned into music-hall management; the translator's working conditions as the excuse; a brief jab at the Press censor via monks. The same initials "M. R." sign "The Happy Warrior" (E11a-18) and "The Unburied" (E11a-40), so one contributor (or one set of initials) writes both the comic and the elegiac.
*Candidate application:* A bomb-thrower or similar frontline specialist is a plausible mock-translator; the battle footnote is a stock device that reminds the reader of the author's circumstances.

**[E11a-39]. "The Everlasting Argument": three nationalities argue the pronunciation of "Sikh" over a Sikh soldier.** (PW-03, "The Everlasting Argument," C. D. Mc., R.S.D., 11th Aust. A.S.C., printed p. 68; L3287-3300)
"Said Pat Malone to a big, brown Sick, / 'They say at fighting you're mighty slick.' / Said Jock McNab, 'Have you noticed the Seeks? / They wear their shirts outside their breeks.' / Said Cornstalk Joe, 'Say what you like, / I'll swear S-i-k-h spells Sike.' / Ses I to 'em all, 'What need to bicker? / Pronounce it so's to rhyme with shikker.'" Mechanism: stock national figures (Irish, Scottish, Australian), a spelling squabble and a pun on "shikker" (drunk); the Sikh soldier is the occasion, not a speaker. Period stereotype names and a color-descriptor ("big, brown") are documented as of the time.
*Candidate application:* Documentation: the Anzac Book jokes about allied soldiers as objects of banter by the other soldiers; the soldier who is discussed does not speak in this text.

**[E11a-40]. "The Unburied": a snow elegy for the dead between the lines.** (PW-03, "The Unburied," M. R., N.Z. Headquarters, printed p. 69; L3305-3325)
"Now snowflakes thickly falling in the winter breeze / ... Have softly covered with a glittering shroud / The unburied dead." Then "I hear a sound of music in the upper air ... the beating of the wings of migrant birds / Wafting the souls of these unburied heroes / Into the skies." The writer is the same M. R. as in E11a-18 and E11a-38. The poem names the fact that the dead lie unburied in no man's land (the dispatch's truce of 24 May dealt with part of that problem, E11a-41) and answers it with snow and birds. The first snow was a novelty for most Australians (Editor's Note), and here it is used for consolation rather than for the grumble pieces' complaints about cold.
*Candidate application:* The unburied dead are the item in the book's lyrics that is transformed by nature imagery rather than addressed; the humorous pieces do not touch them.

### F. The official summary, the outside tribute, and the burlesque verses (printed pp. 71-136)

**[E11a-41]. Pause for the official summary: "The Story of Anzac" (Sir Ian Hamilton's dispatches), and the dry phrases within it.** (PW-03, "The Story of Anzac," printed pp. 71-94, a pasted-in extract from Sir Ian Hamilton's final dispatch; L3344-4558)
The book reprints a long official summary (the landing; the counter-attacks; Quinn's Post; the May 24 truce; Lone Pine; the August battles; Hill 60). I read it in full; it is formal, sidenoted and sentimental ("my dear comrades who will return no more"). It matters for this extraction for three reasons. (1) It is the book's own baseline of official voice. (2) It states the facts that the soldier humor compresses: at Quinn's Post "our fire trenches are mere ledges on the brink of a sheer precipice falling 200 feet ... The enemy's trenches are only a few feet distant"; "As many as 1,400 shells have fallen on Anzac within the hour"; a truce on 24 May "from 7.30 A.M. to 4.30 P.M." to bury "some 3,000 Turkish dead" between the lines; the Light Horse at the Nek lost "three-fourths of that devoted band"; in the August fighting "Ten commanding officers out of thirteen had disappeared from the fighting effectives" (the dispatch's own tally for the units it is discussing). (3) Hamilton himself uses a few dry understatements that read like the soldiers' idiom: Anzac "was cast to play second fiddle to Cape Helles"; "The shelling both of the trenches and beaches has been impartial and liberal"; "the barbed-wire here being something beyond belief"; "the Turkish sniper is no match for the Kangaroo shooter, even at his own game"; "Two Turkish officers were caught in their pajamas"; and the account of a ruse in which, for nights running, H.M.S. Colne shelled Old No. 3 Post "exactly at 9 P.M." for ten minutes to teach the garrison "how they should lose" it. The dispatch contains no joke about the dead; its note on the enemy's losses is a tally ("It is a consolation to know that the Turks suffered still more seriously"), unlike the soldier poems' regard for the Turk (E11a-32, E11a-50).
*Candidate application:* The official history and the comic anthology are bound together in one book, so that a veteran may know both the numbered account and the jokes, and the dry phrase can cross from one to the other.

**[E11a-42]. "Anzacs" by Edgar Wallace: the outsider's tribute, which uses soldiers' own slang for the dead and the sick.** (PW-03, "Anzacs," Edgar Wallace, printed p. 95; L4563-4603; "the sole 'outside' contribution," Editor's Note L524)
A civilian writer (published with consent) addresses the men: "Some of you got a V.C., / Some 'the Gallipoli trot,' / Some had a grave by the sea, / And all of you got it damned hot," and pictures the wounded "limping through town / In the faded old hospital blue." "I'd sooner be 'Anzac' than 'lord.'" The phrase "Gallipoli trot" (my gloss: dysentery) is the soldier idiom for illness, used by the outsider in a tribute poem. The writer's stance is envy of the veterans' title. Documentation of civilian-facing register: it speaks the vernacular flatteringly and shows the wounded in the street, not in the trench.
*Candidate application:* How the civilian world speaks of the men in their own words is a separate register from the men's own, and the Editor specifically marked it as "outside."

**[E11a-43]. "To My Bath": a learned mock-ode that apologizes for itself.** (PW-03, "To My Bath," H. H. U., Northamptonshire Regt., printed p. 96; L4608-4653)
The speaker begs the Muse's indulgence ("This lyric may be bad, O Muse, / But do not press on me too hard; / In times of war you must excuse / Somewhat your bard"), mocks the dug-out ("A dug-out where I have to bend / My back, and even lodge my knees / Against the roof, would suit our friend / Diogenes"), and blames the verse form on the war: "Consider my attempt to write / Iambic tetrametric lines / As influenced by gelignite / And bombs—and mines." It sighs for "thy white enameled banks / Through clouds of steam," compares himself to "corybantic drakes (Or ducks, just as the case may be)," and closes with a Shakespeare pun: "But baths are not our rations in / Gallipoli. 'Tis too far south— / 'The bubble reputation's in / The cannon's mouth.'" Mechanism: an officer's classical-allusion burlesque of poetic composition, with the plain object (a bath) addressed as a beloved; the bath-hunger is the same domestic longing as E11a-9 (daily bathing) and E11a-21. The contributor is a British (Northamptonshire) officer.
*Candidate application:* An educated officer's coping voice uses allusion and self-deprecation, in contrast to the Australian privates' vernacular pieces.

**[E11a-44]. "Anzac Limericks": a named gun with a revenge threat, and the complaint that issued gear is not food.** (PW-03, "Anzac Limericks," C. D. Mc., printed p. 96; L4658-4673)
(1) "There's a certain darned nuisance called 'Beachy,' / Whose shells are exceedingly screechy; / But we're keeping the score, / And we're after your gore— / So look out, 'Beachy Bill,' when we meet ye." The gun is addressed as a person who will be met in revenge; the threat is a rhyme. (2) "They've given us all respirators, / And we've bundles of ancient Spectators; / But we'd give up the two / For a good oyster stew, / Or a dixie of chipped pertaters." Mechanism: a priority list in which the issued items (gas respirators, old magazines from home) are traded for a hot meal. "Beachy" or "Beachy Bill" (the editors' footnote: "a battery of Turkish guns, well known on Anzac Beach") is named in eight separate pieces in my range (E11a-9, 16, 20, 27, 44, 47, 52, 57, plus an illustration, "Racing Beachy Bill"); it functions as the shared, personified hazard of the beach.
*Candidate application:* A unit personifies its most persistent artillery piece (a name, a schedule, a character) and addresses it in comic threats; the gear issued from higher up is judged by whether it is edible.

**[E11a-45]. A caption about the enemy's own intelligence report on the Anzacs' clothing.** (PW-03, illustration "The New Star," Ted Colles after F. J. Leigh, and a caption block "Turkish Divisional Orders (some time in July)," L4678-4686)
The caption block reads: "The Australian and New Zealand Army Corps is approaching the limits of its resources. Most of the men have no clothing at all, except trousers, and even these are now being cut into pieces, so that one pair of trousers is sufficient for four men." The book presents it as a quotation from a captured Turkish order; I cannot verify it from this text. Mechanism: the enemy misreading the Anzacs' summer undress (shorts cut from trousers) as destitution, which the Anzacs treat as a joke on the enemy's information.
*Candidate application:* Captured enemy documents that misread one's own side are a durable comic item, whether or not authentic; it turns shortage into a source of pride.

**[E11a-46]. "How I Won the V.C.": a tall-tale braggart's monologue, subtitled as what to expect after the war.** (PW-03, "How I Won the V.C.," by "Crosscut," 16th Batt. A.I.F., printed p. 98; L4691-4872; the Editor's Note names T. H. Wilson of the 16th as winner of the humorous-verse prize (L521-523), and the verse's hero is "Wilson"; whether "Crosscut" is Wilson is likely but not stated)
Subtitle: "(The sort of thing we must expect to hear after the War is ended)." Public-domain text; quoted at length because the mechanism is the point.
False modesty: "Yes, that's the V.C. How I won it, / It isn't for me to relate. / (We heroes are always so modest, / And boasting's a thing that I hate.)" The impossible scale: "It was five thousand feet to the summit ... nine tiers of trenches ... twelve 42 centimeters." The officer's appeal: "Pass the word there for Lance-Private Wilson; / He'll find us a way out of this!" and the private's insolence: "And nodded (I never salute)"; "I don't want no red-tapey orders, / And I don't want no kudos nor pelf"; "And you'll find that the bloke called Horatius / Was only a trifle to me!" The charmed life: "I got thirty-two shots thro' my trousers / And nine shrapnel balls thro' my coat; / And a Japanese bomb burst beneath me ... But it proved the best thing that could happen, / For it pushed me half-way up the slope." The ration gags: "As for rations—well, all I took with me / Was a tin of Fray Bentos or two, / And in my breast pocket I planted / A nice Army biscuit to chew." "Then a fifteen-inch shell came straight at me ... But it struck on that hard Army biscuit / And rebounded—and blew up a Turk! ... Here's the biscuit—that dent in the middle / Is where the shell struck it, of course!" The winning stratagem: knowing "the Turks were half-famished, / And fought on one biscuit a day," he throws the salt tinned meat into their trench, and "For they'd whacked the Fray Bentos among them, / And each man had perished from thirst." (My reading: the trick works on the soldiers' own grievances, that salt tinned beef makes a man thirsty and water is scarce.) The colonel: "'Corporal Wilson, / You've dished up the beggars in style.'" The close: "I don't want no honor or glory, / But—that's how I won the V.C."
Mechanisms: (1) the verse anticipates and mocks the post-war boaster who tells civilians a medal-winning yarn, with a rank slip (Lance-Private, then Corporal) as a built-in sign of inflation; (2) the hero's weapon is the soldier's two chief grievances, the Army biscuit (indestructible) and the salt bully beef (thirst); (3) the Turks die of thirst, not of shot, the book's own shared hardship turned on the enemy; (4) the colonel's enthusiastic cry "Bonzer ... Gorstrafem ... Hooray!" shows the officer in the same comic key. The killing of the enemy is told as a trick played with food. 
*Candidate application:* The men pre-rehearse and parody the "war story" that civilians will want, which is a self-protection against heroic retelling; the joke lands because every reader knows the real version.

**[E11a-47]. "Icy": the man the squadron called a cold-foot, and the ritual apology.** (PW-03, "Icy," E. A. M. W., printed p. 102; L4877-4978)
The narrator says the squadron considered Icy "a 'cold-foot'"; the nickname came from his involuntary ducking whenever "Beachy Bill" screeched overhead, followed by a smile "as much as to say, 'I can't help it!'" Mates "take a mean advantage of his good nature" and give him the sweeping. During night digging in front of a Turkish sap, Icy is on the covering party, and a machine-gun enfilades the diggers (summarized: several men are hit and two killed). Icy is missing; cries and "fragments of language of the category known in Australia as 'bullocky'" come from the Turkish flank; later he is found badly wounded, "his sixteen bullet wounds and scratches all told" being the text's own count (I do not quote the description of the state he is found in), the enemy gun beneath him, "tripod and all." "There went down to see him a crowd in which I was amongst the foremost, which apologized to Icy very humbly. And, do you know, he only smiled back at us in that funny old way of his." Footnote 18: "Bullocky—stands both for the bullock driver and for his chief gift" (i.e., swearing). Footnote 17: the Turks used "French 75's" seized as the guns were coming from France during the blockade of Servia. Mechanism: a group that labels a man by his startle reflex is put right by an act it did not expect; the apology is made as a crowd; he does not comment. Beachy's shell is an involuntary-flinch test.
*Candidate application:* The flinch is easily mistaken for cowardice and used as an in-group label; a unit that discovers its mistake makes a ritual apology, which the quiet man receives with the same smile as before.

**[E11a-48]. "The Trojan War, 1915": the private as a classical hero.** (PW-03, "The Trojan War, 1915," J. Wareham, 1st Aust. Field Amb., printed p. 104; L4993-5021)
"We care not what old Homer tells / Of Trojan war and Helen's fame. / Upon the ancient Dardanelles / New peoples write—in blood—their name." The soldiers went for "Some Mother-Helen sad at home, / Some obscure Helen on a farm," and in the last stanza "in honor to plain Private Bill / Great Agamemnon lifts his hand!" Sincere elevating verse that uses Troy's geography; it shares the setting with the comic Agamemnon line in "Army Biscuits" (E11a-35), where the same Greek camp lacks biscuits.
*Candidate application:* The same classical backdrop can be used to honor and to joke; the "plain Private Bill" is the honor-bearer in both.

**[E11a-49]. "The Price": a nightmare of the dead, with a stated reason to bear it.** (PW-03, "The Price," Corpl. Comus, 2nd Bat., A.I.F., printed p. 104; L5026-5039)
"Dead figures writhe and beckon in my dream; / Wild eyes look into mine; / While I, bewildered, watch the bloody stream / With misty eyes ashine. / It rends my heart, and I am nothing loath / To have the murder cease. / Horror it is and carnage, yet are both / Part of the price of peace." The contributor also wrote "From My Trench" (E11a-28). This is a two-stanza record of a soldier's dream of the dead, in 1915, with an answer attached ("the price of peace") that justifies and does not cancel the horror. No humor.
*Candidate application:* A documented first-person nightmare statement from within the campaign, with a justification attached; useful for how a veteran might later explain his dreams.

**[E11a-50]. "Killed in Action": the burial party, the paperwork, and the line where the joking stops.** (PW-03, "Killed in Action," Harry McCann, Headquarters, 4th Aust. Light Horse, printed p. 105; L5044-5094)
A long, sincere poem about one day's burial. Its key lines concern how joking behaves around a death: "There's a band of quiet workers, artless lads who joked and chatted / Just this morning; now they're sullen and they keep their eyes away / From the blanket-hidden body" (I omit the line describing the body). Then the institutional line: "There are records in the office—date of death and facts pertaining, / Showing name and rank and number and disposal of the kit— / More or less a business matter, and we have no time for feigning / More than momentary pity for the men who have been hit." The poem imagines the mother and the young wife and child; it asks: "Could the Turk have seen this picture when his trigger-finger rounded, / Would his sights have blurred a little had he heard that mother's prayer?" and answers: "But he only saw his duty, and he did it, fighting fair." It ends: "Short and quick and silent suffering; would to God it ended there."
*Candidate application:* The poem states the rule outright: joking stops when the body is present, the paperwork is mechanical, pity is brief by necessity, and the enemy is not blamed.

**[E11a-51]. The "gray" pieces: "A Gray Day in Gallipoli" and "Gray Smoke," and the cluster from the 11th A.A.S.C.** (PW-03, "A Gray Day in Gallipoli," N. Ash, 11th A.A.S.C., printed p. 106; L5106-5135; "Gray Smoke," R. G. N., 11th Aust. A.S.C., printed p. 131; L6143-6169)
N. Ash's single-sentence prose poem repeats "gray" eighteen times in thirty lines of text: "the gray world's messenger whose name is Loneliness," "the gray sea's bride, the gray cloud-bearing Mother Wind," and the two roads: some look east to "the chances of the blue and gold life in Constantinople" (my reading: capture) and others to "that other gray life forever with the gray seas, gray skies and gray forgetfulness on these ghostly, forsaken gray shores of Gallipoli." "Gray Smoke" addresses an old pipe: "Gray smoke of yours, gray thoughts o' mine," and ends on "better things than gray-like smoke and grayer hopes." Both are melancholic set-pieces with no humor. Note: N. Ash also wrote the comic dialect "Anzac Dialogues" (E11a-16), so the same hand writes both registers. By the printed unit tags, N. Ash ("A Gray Day"), R. G. N. ("Gray Smoke") and C. D. Mc. ("The Everlasting Argument") are all of the 11th A.A.S.C.; the same initials "C. D. Mc." also sign "Anzac Limericks" and "Sense or ——?" (no unit printed there; I take the initials to be one person, which is an inference). On that reading one Army Service Corps unit supplies six pieces in my range across comic and sad registers (E11a-16, 39, 44, 51 (two pieces), 60).
*Candidate application:* The same service-corps writer can supply comic dialogue and low lyric, and a unit's wits and melancholics may be the same men.

**[E11a-52]. "My Anzac Home": the estate-agent invitation, with the shell as a feature of the view.** (PW-03, "My Anzac Home," Corpl. George L. Smith, 24th Sanitary Section, R.A.M.C.T., printed p. 107; L5143-5183)
Refrain: "my cozy little dug-out on the hill." "Come and see my little dug-out—way up on the hill it stands, / Where I can get a lovely view of Anzac's golden sands; / When 'Beachy Bill' is shelling, I can see just where he lands." Rooms: "It isn't quite as roomy as the mansions of the Tsar; / From sitting-room to bedroom is not so very far." Vermin: "The fleas they wander nightly, as soon as I've undressed, / And after many weary hunts I've had to give them best." The cupboard is "a home for orphan'd mice"; lighting is "some fat in syrup tins." Weather: "But if it's wet and stormy—well, I go to sleep outside / Of my cozy little dug-out on the hill," with the editors' footnote 19: "The roof of a dug-out, as usually designed, is a device for keeping the shrapnel out and letting the water in." The close anticipates nostalgia: "Well—perhaps some day I'll miss you, and will long to live once more / In the little cozy dug-out on the hill." Mechanism: estate-agent patter with a refrain; the shelling as a view feature; the final "perhaps" as the only gesture at later grief.
*Candidate application:* Describing a bad place in the language of a rental advertisement is a durable form, and the last stanza's "perhaps some day I'll miss you" is the one line of anticipated fondness.

**[E11a-53]. "What Frank Thought": the belated parcel, the civilian slacker, and the soldier who cleans his rifle with the hair-oil.** (PW-03, "What Frank Thought," A. J. Boyd, A.N.Z.A.C., printed p. 108; L5194-5299)
A private reads two letters from "Jessie," one "posted in Sydney thirteen months ago." "Am I having my leg pulled?" The letters assume Egypt is the front ("Dearie, be sure to keep your hat on at all times. Egypt, I hear, is awfully hot about Christmas-time"), report that "Frank" has not enlisted ("In his case, 'it would be super-patriotic,' he says, to [serve in the ranks]"; "if it is dangerous for the men, how much more dangerous must it be for the officers. He says he will insist upon leading his men in all charges"), mention races and yachting, and close "Please don't get shot, dear. We intend to send you lots of nice things for Christmas." The parcel is hair-oil, "twenty-five costly cigars," pajamas, and a tobacconist's booklet. The soldier, "always a philosopher," "cleaned his rifle with the hair-oil, cut up the pajamas to make pull-throughs, and to newly arrived reinforcements distributed the cigars." The editors' footnote 20 explains that tents would be "an open invitation to shrapnel" and that "'The nearest shops' were about three miles behind the Turkish lines." Mechanism: the home front's reports in its own voice make the joke, with the soldier's practical reuse of the gifts as the laugh; the title names the slacker whose thoughts only come through the letter. Targets: the home front's ignorance, men who do not enlist, the officer-class posture ("leading his men"), the mails' delay.
*Candidate application:* Documented: contempt for the able-bodied civilian slacker and for the home front's misconceptions is a stable theme, voiced through a letter read aloud and answered in action.

**[E11a-54]. "Arcadia": the reverse advertisement for Anzac.** (PW-03, "Arcadia," Bombardier H. E. Shell, 7th Battery, Aust. F.A., printed p. 110; L5313-5376)
After listing the boarding-houses and pubs of a lifetime, the speaker "struck an ideal spot" and lists its advantages, each an absence or a military reality: "The rent is free, no board to pay, / No land or income taxes"; "If I should say I need some clothes, / Someone will just 'take action'"; "And should my health appear to fail ... My doctor hands me—not a bill, / But just a Number 9" (footnote 21: a "universal remedy ... in the form of a pill"); "The atmosphere's so full of iron, / It acts as quite a tonic" (shell fragments); "politicians don't exist, / Nor e'en a by-election"; "And married men are always true / To absent, loving wives"; "the crowning gift of all is—no- / One's happiness is marred, / Finding answers to the questions / On that d——d War Census Card." The reveal: "Let me whisper—it is ANZAC! / Anzac by the Dardanelles." Mechanism: advertisement or real-estate verse in which everything praised is lack; the iron-in-the-air line hides the danger inside a health claim. Targets: taxes, bureaucracy ("take action"), the one-pill doctor, politicians, the census form.
*Candidate application:* The comic inventory of what the place does not impose is a structure for expressing what civil life costs and what the army removes.

**[E11a-55]. The "first wheelbarrow" cartoon: the staff compliment and the laborer's retort.** (PW-03, cartoon "Our First Wheelbarrow (An Historic Incident)," L5395-5404)
Caption: *Staff Officer* (meeting a "perspiring Australasian staggering under his twentieth half-ton load in the very first wheelbarrow ever improvised at Anzac—till then sacks had been used"): "Hallo, my man! That's a very good scheme. Did you invent that barrow?" *Perspiring Australasian:* "I wish I could find the beggar who did!" Mechanism: the staff officer's cheerful approval shows he does not see that a better tool means more loads; the laborer's retort converts the inventor's honor into blame. Same shape as the "stupid question" pieces (E11a-6, 12, 22).
*Candidate application:* Documented: the "my man" patronage by a staff officer and the working soldier's resentful reply.

**[E11a-56]. "The Caveman": a trench dream that says plainly that nothing has improved, ended by the N.C.O. at the shoulder.** (PW-03, "The Caveman," J. M. Collins, 9th Battalion, printed p. 113; L5420-5461)
The dreamer addresses a "dark and hairy caveman" in cheerful rhyme (the caveman's "scanty fare," the "maiden fair" clubbed "on the cranium" (period crude joke), and the claim "You were better off than we are—you'd no uniform to tear"), then reverses: "Things have changed, poor Mr. Caveman, since you went your simple way, / But we're living still in caves, sir, dug most carefully in clay. / We call them trenches, dug-outs, saps; but, call them what we may, / They are made to hide our skins in, just as in your heathen day." The sharpest stanza: "Two thousand years ago came One—taught 'Peace on earth, goodwill'; / Unceasingly we've preached it since, and that 'Thou shalt not kill.' / And all these toilsome, changeful years we've retrograded till / We are with you, Mr. Caveman, for we're simple cavemen still." The waking: "'Twas the N.C.O. on duty, saying: 'Come, my lad, your turn!'" This is the same dream-then-duty closing as "Beachy" (E11a-20) and "The Anzac Home—and a Contrast" (E11a-21). Mechanism: a comic frame that carries an anti-war argument (the war as regression) that is not in the officer-level verse; the waking line recasts the argument as sleep-talking and sends the speaker back to duty.
*Candidate application:* A critique of the war's meaning is allowed through if it is a dream and ends with the sentry roster.

**[E11a-57]. "An Anzac Alphabet": the soldiers' roster of targets in twenty-six couplets.** (PW-03, "An Anzac Alphabet," J. W. S. Henderson, R.G.A., printed p. 115; the text prints the lines as illustration captions, L5470-5558)
Public-domain text; quoted at length because the list is the point. Selected entries: "A is the Airplane buzzing above, / Sending us tokens of friendship and love" (bombs); "B's Beachy Bill, such a marvel of cunning, / A message from whom sends the best of us running"; "C is the Chilliness felt in the feet / When bullets commence to invade our retreat" (cold feet as fear, cf. E11a-47); "E is for Eye-wash, a wonderful lotion, / Employed by the man who is keen on promotion"; "F is the Fool who got caught in a trap, / By pulling the tail of a mule in a sap"; "G is the General devising a strafe, / And cursing his highly incompetent staff"; "H is the wretched unfortunate Hill, / Bombarded and mined but impregnable still"; "I's the Intelligence officer who / Is said to exist at G.H.Q."; "L is the Liar who loves to relate / Achi Baba has fallen, and gives you the date"; "M is the Major observing from latitudes / Tending to strained and discomforting attitudes"; "N is the Navy bombarding a lair, / Ignoring the fact that there's nobody there"; "O is the Optimist struck by a splinter, / Happy to think he'll be home by the winter"; "P is the spotlessly uniformed Paragon, / Living in splendor on H.M.S. 'Aragon'"; "Q is the Questions we ask with a wail, / Do skippers like whiskey, and where is our mail?"; "R's the report of the latest success, / Strictly compiled for the use of the Press"; "S is the Sniper; it's also his Sickness / On finding his cover is lacking in thickness"; "T's the Telephonist cutting off stations / In the midst of important conversations"; "V is the Victory talked of by editors, / Who wish to get rid of importunate creditors"; "X is the Xmas that some day will come / When turkey and sauce will be served with our rum." At J the writer admits "Forgive a digression and spare me the time / To think of a word that will make a good rhyme ... Remember it's J and the word may be Joking," and between U and V an editorial note says "This illustration has had to be postponed pending a final statement by Mr. Hilaire Belloc as to the date of the certain exhaustion of German resources" (a jab at armchair war prophecy; the note does not say which illustration).
Mechanisms: the child's alphabet used as a pretext for a targets list; each couplet is a one-joke diagnosis of a type; the roster covers the aircraft, the named gun, fear, sham display, staff, intelligence, the Navy, the rear-ship officer, the press, the rumor-monger, the optimist; the front-line Turk is absent (Turkey appears only as the Germans' dupe at W) and the Kaiser appears as K. The writer is a British Royal Garrison Artillery man.
*Candidate application:* The set of targets is a map of who the front resents: staff, intelligence, publicity, rear comfort, and whoever makes the optimistic claims.

**[E11a-58]. "The Kaiser to His Secretary": ventriloquizing the enemy's leader in split-word rhymes.** (PW-03, "The Kaiser to His Secretary (Dictated), Xmas, 1915," H. B. C., printed p. 119; L5574-5663)
The Kaiser dictates a Christmas letter: "'Peace upon earth and unto men goodwill!' / Such words rang true of yore. (Excuse my laughing.)" The rhyme scheme splits words across lines for effect ("anach- / ronism"; "U- / Nion Jack"; "Deutschland U- / ber Alles"). He insists "Not till all worlds shall tremble at my nod, / Shall peace on earth be countenanced by ME— / or God." He orders "Little Willie" to "select a new cathedral's spire, / Bombard it! Seize it! Never mind the losses! / Tell him its peal of bells will make more ir- / on crosses" (the line puns on "iron crosses," the German decoration; the text does not explain the exact point). Hindenburg, if he cannot advance, "must dig a / New line, 'according to our plans,' due west / of Riga" (retreat in the official phrase). He plans to act "when I'm satisfied that General Bird- / Wood and his Anzacs at the Dardanelles / Are busy studying the latest word / In Hells," then go to Egypt "by a rather safer route through A- / sia Minor," and replace the Double Eagle with "A Vulture." Mechanisms: the enemy leader as a self-exposing buffoon; the poem knows the war news (Verdun, Riga, Bulgaria, Greece); the split-rhyme trick as comic delivery. Contrast with the Turk "Abdul" of E11a-32: the Kaiser is mocked, the front-line Turk respected. Another case in the same key: "The Flood" (E11a-68).
*Candidate application:* The home leaders of the enemy are fair game for ridicule while the opposing front-line soldier is not, and a Kaiser parody is a safe topic that shows what the soldiers know.

**[E11a-59]. "The Anzac Thunderstorm": the one thing that stopped the war.** (PW-03, "The Anzac Thunderstorm—From the Trenches," I. A. Saxon, 21st Aust. Battalion, printed p. 122; L5668-5734)
A descriptive piece with a note of mock-politeness about the Navy ("the playful work of tearing pieces from the hillsides of Achi Baba ... the many small acts of friendliness to which Jack Tar is prone") followed by awe at the lightning, which "revealed" "the rude wooden crosses" in "bold relief," then "The storm has had its effect on those manning the trenches. The bubbling rattle of the machine-gun, the sharp crack of Turkish rifles ... are noticeable by their absence. The turmoil of an hour ago has turned to unbelievable quiet." The only humor is the arch description of the naval bombardment. Both armies falling quiet in the face of weather is the sketch's closing image.
*Candidate application:* Weather is the one authority that silences both sides and gives the men an uncontested awe.

**[E11a-60]. "Sense or ——?": the censor as a stock character, and an exchange of favors.** (PW-03, "Sense or ——?," C. D. Mc. (Sergt.), printed p. 123; L5744-5771)
Four verses on "a certain hard-hearted old censor." He "won't let us tell, / When we say 'I'm quite well,' / Where we've planted the water condenser"; he strikes out "the shell-smoke as dense" and also "that some of our non-coms, are denser" (a pun smuggled past in the complaint); yet "this same rigid old censor ... Will let it go through— / Tho' he knows it's not true— / If we tell her our love grows intenser!" The last stanza: "he let down the rails / For our Christmas mails, / So three jolly good cheers for the censor." Mechanism: a pun-based list in which the censor passes sentimental falsehoods and blocks military facts; the closing cheer is conditional gratitude (mails). The initials are the same as in E11a-39 and E11a-44.
*Candidate application:* The censor is a target of mild humor, not hatred; the soldiers know he lets the romantic lie through and blocks the plain fact, and they say so in verse that is itself printed under editorial control.

**[E11a-61]. "Our Sailors — The Amphibious Man": a mock press profile of a naval officer ashore.** (PW-03, "Our Sailors—The Amphibious Man," Lieut. A. L. Pemberton, H.Q. Staff, 24th Siege Bde., R.G.A., printed p. 124; L5783-5842)
Introduced as "the following invaluable article" by "our special correspondent having been permitted the exceptional privilege of obtaining some insight into the work of the Navy," the narrator tries to recognize the sailor from his army-style khaki and finally spots "the silver anchor in a golden hoop." The sailor says "'Shiver my corrugated iron!'" and answers a question about his training: "'Iron is heavier than water, and a pinnace afloat is worth six aground.'" Asked about "the landing of all these vast quantities of stores," he says, "'We do not land them ... We unship them.'" The narrator: "I nodded understandingly. So that, then, was the key to the great mystery." and earlier "Here, I realized, was a man of perception—one who was fitted to guide the destinies of a great nation." Mechanism: parody of a journalist's access profile and of naval jargon; the sailor's solemn tautologies. The tone is affectionate, in line with the Navy tributes in Birdwood's introduction (E11a-1).
*Candidate application:* The press's staged access and the service-versus-service vocabulary quarrel (landing versus unshipping) are targets for gentle mockery, not hostility.

**[E11a-62]. "Possies": the staff officer's false etymology and the commanding officer's silence.** (PW-03, "Possies," by "Ben Telbow," printed p. 125; L5858-5872)
A general asks what a "Possie" is; the C.O. explains it is "Australian for recess, either firing or sleeping. It's a contraction of 'position.'" The Chief Staff Officer, "in a tone which admitted no argument," corrects him: "'Posse!—p-o- double s-e. Posse—a small force. Your firing recess is manned by a small force—what?' And the C.O. was overcome by very great emotion." Mechanism: the senior officer corrects a native usage with a wrong derivation; the C.O. cannot answer without insubordination. A quiet rank joke: the officer who knows the word's origin must hold his tongue.
*Candidate application:* A subordinate's controlled "emotion" as the reaction to a superior's confident error is a documented comic beat.

**[E11a-63]. "Mr. Airplane": a private's dialect salute to the naval flyers.** (PW-03, "Mr. Airplane (With compliments to the R.N.A.S. in the Dardanelles)," H. G. Garland, 16th Aust. Battalion, printed p. 126; L5882-5927)
"I'm Tommy Brown, Australian, / Who's fightin' here on land; / An', strike me, Mr. Flyin' Man, / I'd like to shake your hand." The speaker says that flying beside the pilot "my nerves go all a-shake / To think you're up so high," imagines "old Jacko" (the Turk) firing at the plane "As if the job were fun, / An' Jacko was a-firin' / A nipper's toy popgun," and when the pilot signals the battleships "oh, strike me blue an' pink— / Then don't the Turkies swear!" Mechanism: admiring dialect address; another enemy nickname ("Jacko"); the pun on Turkey. The first line of E11a-57 (A) shows a different view of the airplane from the ground.
*Candidate application:* Respect for a daring branch is expressed through a plain-speaking persona ("Tommy Brown") and a pun on the enemy; nicknames for the Turk are plural (Abdul, Johnny, Jacko, John Turk).

**[E11a-64]. "Anzac in Egypt, 1: Mahomed—and Australia": the guide who made up what he did not know.** (PW-03, "Anzac in Egypt: 1. Mahomed—and Australia," signed "C.", printed p. 127; L5940-6015)
A Nile excursion anecdote. "Mahomed was Mahomed. He was also a guide. The combination meant that he knew everything, and what he didn't know he made up, and what he made up he told so often that at last he believed it." A passing aside: "theology doesn't appeal much to soldiers. Padres have reduced their services to a maximum of twenty minutes." The soldiers weary of Napoleon ("Napoleon hadn't even known Australia"), and the youngest subaltern asks about Sir George Reid (my gloss: the former Australian Prime Minister, then alive; the text does not explain who he is): the guide says "I know all about Saint George Reid. He a great man. There is his mound over there ... dead a hundred years ... If a sick man goes there, he gets cured quickly." The group "tipped Mahomed generously." Mechanism: the tourist trap turned around: the soldiers feed the fabricator a name and he fabricates; the narrator reads the guide's thoughts as hostility to "infidel dogs" (period stereotype, documented). The piece is about leave in Egypt before the landing and has no war.
*Candidate application:* The Egypt memory is a comic leave story, centered on soldiers outwitting local salesmen; the era's colonial attitude to Egyptians is recorded as such.

**[E11a-65]. "Anzac in Egypt, 2: Anzac in Alex": a nostalgic prose sketch of a wineshop and a mixed friendship group.** (PW-03, "Anzac in Alex," L. J. Ivory, 4th Howitzer Battery, N.Z.F.A., printed p. 128; L6018-6137)
A leisurely description of "old Benci's little wineshop in Alexandria," its Chianti "at a price to fit a ranker's pocket," the cosmopolitan street, a "dapper" and "sallow" crowd (period descriptors), the friends who gathered "behind the wooden grille that ... shut us off effectively from the eyes of the night patrol": the New Zealand medical corps man Tillett, a Ceylon tea-planter sergeant, and a Maori friend whom the narrator calls "well-born and well-educated" and says "only the full lips and the slightly flattened nose betrayed his origin" (period racial characterization, documented), who "like so many of the best of his race, ... was no 'sticker.'" The final section marks the loss: "those were our palmy days in Alexandria—the days before the swarm of Tommies came, and our pockets began to empty," and "Sir John Maxwell's 'Iron Law of closing time.'" Not comic; it is a leave-time memory in a warm, elegiac register. The "three bomb-throws ... if the last of the three happened to be a 'googly'" is its only front-line image.
*Candidate application:* A rear-area bar and its improvised multi-national circle are remembered as a golden time, with the authority that ended it (military police, closing time) named lightly.

**[E11a-66]. "A Wail from Ordnance": the rear-echelon's grievance and the joke about the clock that will make them run.** (PW-03, "A Wail from Ordnance," Lieut. Kininmonth, A.O.C., printed p. 132; L6179-6228)
"We're only in the Ordience, / Not troopers of the line; / We don't attack no enemy, / Nor in the papers shine." They hand out "underpants and socks, / And boots and coats galore" and "keep their clothing up to dick." After "They blew us from our depot south / A bit along the beach," they settle until "they trained on us one day / A blasted four-point-one." The daily shelling: "One eye is cocked for cover, / And one ear is for the whiz, / An', until the fuss is over, we / Postpone our daily biz." The prophecy: "Now, when the war is over, / ... A striking clock will wake us, / A blow-out make us run, / And cry again our old refrain: / 'Gott straf' that four-point-one!'" Mechanism: rank-and-file dialect speech written by an officer; self-deprecation about being rear echelon ("we don't attack no enemy"). This is the only joke in my range that projects the war's startle reflex into peacetime: the striking clock and the blow-out (a tire burst, or a similar loud bang) as triggers.
*Candidate application:* A 1915 joke already recognizes that a loud sound in peacetime will bring back the reflex; the joke is told as a refrain, with the shell designated by its caliber.

**[E11a-67]. Two captions and a pointer to the Anzac trench papers: "Haw, haw, no shave?" and the facsimile of *Dinkum Oil*.** (PW-03, cartoon L6233-6235; facsimile caption L6240-6245, "Dinkum Oil," printed p. 134)
Cartoon: *Young Officer:* "Haw, haw, no shave?" *Australian:* "He, he, no——razaw!" (the Australian mimics the officer's drawl, with a dash for an oath, in answer to a taunt about his beard). The caption beside the facsimile page says it reproduces "our quondam contemporary, *Dinkum Oil*, chiefly composed and edited by Sergt. Noonan, 6th Battn. A.I.F. (previously editor of *Sniper's Shots*). The *Dinkum Oil* (which is Melbourne for 'True News') appeared during June and July, 1915, and circulated in the 1st Australian Division." This is the book's own evidence that there were earlier Anzac trench papers (at least *Sniper's Shots* and *Dinkum Oil*) in mid-1915, produced by a sergeant, and that the title is an in-group joke on "true news" in a place of furphies (E11a-31). The facsimile page itself is not in the text file.
*Candidate application:* The mock-news title "True News" for a trench sheet is a joke that depends on how false the news in circulation is.

**[E11a-68]. "The Book of Anzac Chronicles, 1: The Flood": the Kaiser's war told as a Genesis pastiche.** (PW-03, "The Book of Anzac Chronicles: 1. The Flood," by "Genesis Gallipoli," printed p. 135; L6257-6367)
King Hun is told by "the Gott of Boasts" to "Bring together all your ships of the sea, your wealth of the land, and your mighty men of valor ... Let thou and thy son take but six days' rations in your haversacks, for on the seventh day thou shalt dine at the Palace of the Buckinghamites with the King of the Allyites as thy mess orderly." The counting: "umpteen million men of valor, two ships, seventeen anchors, fourteen shillings and fivepence in gold, umpteen billion rolls of paper money and ennygottsquantitee gas. But they left the two ships at home, fastened to the seventeen anchors." The king says "It is enough, IMSHEE!" and "So they imsheed" (Egyptian-Arabic leave-taking slang adopted by the Anzacs, my gloss). A passage of atrocity propaganda (the Huns breaking into villages and killing a household and its animals) is told in the same biblical cadence; I summarize rather than quote it. Then "the Man-from-the-west with the strong arm and the iron jaw" (my gloss: Kitchener) announces "I am K. of K., and THIS IS THE END OF THE SECTION." The Huns "stopped, yea, verily," and "the King of the Huns and Little Willie ate their iron rations instead." The flood is stopped by "Mighty Winds"; the King decides "it was a dream, and instead of 'The Day' is now nothing but 'The Night.' So he fell asleep. And great was the fall thereof." Mechanism: scriptural pastiche as a vehicle for anti-German propaganda burlesque; the soldier idioms ("umpteen," "imshee," "section") slip into the scripture; the German dialect line ("Gott strafe, but who vas dis dat do dry stob our leedle game?") is period caricature, documented. The target is the Germans and the Kaiser; the Turks do not appear.
*Candidate application:* The Germans are the book's comic villain (the Kaiser, "the Huns"); the Turks of the line are treated as a separate case (E11a-32, 34).

**[E11a-69]. "The Book of Anzac Chronicles, 2: The Book of Jobs" (begins at the end of my range): the landing as Old Testament, with the enemy defending "the land of their forefathers."** (PW-03, "The Book of Anzac Chronicles: 2. The Book of Jobs," W. R. Wishart, No. 1 Aust. Stat. Hosp., printed p. 136; L6370-6413 only; the item continues past my range)
In the part I read: the landing date is "the seventh day of the week of the fourth month of the year, being the twenty-fifth Sunday after the Melbourne Cup"; "Birdy" commands, "Take from the Turks the land of Gallipoli"; the Colonels, Majors and Captains issue the order "after much lengthy pow-wowing," and the 'Boss' is quoted ("Ye shall girdle yourselves about with ammunition"); the enemy are "the sons of Abdul," and "they proved themselves 'hard-doers'; yea, verily, they were a stubborn folk, for they had builded unto themselves dug-outs and trenches on the land of their forefathers, and were aware of the coming of the invader." Mechanism: staff orders and nicknames ("Birdy," the "Boss") in King James cadence; the Turks acknowledged as defenders of their own land. The remainder, including the chapter's later verses, is for the next extraction pass (I stopped at L6413).
*Candidate application:* The soldiers' own parody of the orders chain uses the commander's nickname and the Turks' right to defend their homeland as a matter of course.

## Register notes: the Anzac Book against the British trench-paper pass (E08), only where the Anzac Book text supports it

(The E08 side below is taken from the E08 extraction's own account of the Wipers Times, not re-read here.)

1. **Form.** The Anzac Book is a one-off, editor-curated souvenir with prize categories ("Prose (Humorous)," "Verses (Humorous)," "Drawing (Comic)") and a stated charity purpose (E11a-2). E08 describes a recurring officers' mess paper. The Anzac Book was written to be read by home audiences, and it carries editors' footnotes that translate slang and explain jokes' hidden premises (dinkum, cobber, bonzer, skite, pommy, possie, furphy, bullocky; "tents would invite shrapnel," E11a-53). E08's jokes are decoded by insiders through role names; here the code is footnoted for outsiders.
2. **Voice and rank.** The Editor says most contributors were privates, and some pieces are signed by enlisted rank (Pte. Lowry, Pte. Hopkins, Cpl. Comus, Lance-Corp. Dunstan, Bombardier "Shell"), so sustained Australian vernacular narration is present as an ordinary voice (E11a-11, 16, 25, 46, 63). Officers also write dialect verse (Kininmonth, E11a-66), so vernacular does not by itself mark rank. The mock-genteel British officer voice (Cadogan, E11a-21; "To My Bath," E11a-43; Pemberton, E11a-4, 61) appears in the same book beside it; a cartoon shows an Australian answering an officer's "Haw, haw" with a mimicking "He, he" (E11a-67).
3. **Targets.** The Anzac Book's rank-directed jokes aim at **staff, G.H.Q., intelligence, the press, the rear-ship officer, the quartermaster and the sergeant-major** (E11a-37, 55, 57, 62), while the battalion officers who appear in anecdotes are mild or sympathetic ("Don't do it again!," E11a-25; the C.O. who must hold his tongue, E11a-62). It also aims at **vermin, flies, sand, water, biscuits, and the beach itself** (E11a-15, 18, 23, 35, 52), the dust-and-heat setting, with a recurring named gun (E11a-44).
4. **The enemy.** The text splits the enemy in two. The Turk of the line is "Abdul," "Johnny Turk," "John Turk," "Jacko," "Mr. Abdul," with a toast, a humanizing sketch, a Nasreddin Hodja joke, and the acknowledgment that he defends "the land of their forefathers" (E11a-13, 32, 34, 69). The Germans, the Kaiser and "the Huns" are the comic villains (E11a-58, 68). E08, per its extraction, casts the enemy as a rival business firm and a sporting opponent, and I make no claim here beyond this: the Anzac Book's own text shows a respected front-line enemy with a ridiculed distant one.
5. **The dead.** As in E08 (no jokes about named dead), the Anzac Book keeps its dead out of the comedy: comic sketches of a dead comrade use a pseudonym and end in a formal epitaph (E11a-24), the one mock-funeral is for a donkey (E11a-29) or a sick officer (E11a-26), and the actual dead appear in elegy (E11a-14, 40, 50).
6. **Items in the Anzac Book that E08's summary does not list** (this is a comparison against that summary only, not against the Wipers Times itself): a rumor allegory (E11a-31); an anticipated post-war startle joke (E11a-66); a pre-written parody of the post-war war story (E11a-46); and the book's own pointer to earlier Anzac trench papers (*Sniper's Shots*, *Dinkum Oil*, E11a-67), which shows that such papers existed at Anzac in mid-1915.

## Pattern roll-up (what this unit establishes, with the two best examples of each device)

**Functions.** *Pastime and souvenir:* the book is made "to while away the long winter" and becomes "a hundred times more precious" (E11a-2). *Domesticating the hazard:* equating shells with sand and flies (E11a-18), the future dug-out home (E11a-5), the cozy-dug-out refrain (E11a-52). *Venting at the rear and the staff:* the Alphabet (E11a-57), the Lost Poem (E11a-37), the Wail from Ordnance (E11a-66). *Comradely rite:* the crowd's apology to Icy (E11a-47) and the mock-mourning for Bobbie (E11a-26). *Pre-emptive mockery of the post-war story:* "How I Won the V.C." (E11a-46).

**Targets.** Staff/G.H.Q./intelligence/press (E11a-57 G, I, M, R; E11a-55 wheelbarrow, E11a-62 "posse"); the quartermaster and supply (E11a-37, E11a-44 respirators and old magazines); the censor, gently (E11a-60); the Navy, affectionately (E11a-61); the home front and the able-bodied slacker (E11a-53, E11a-36); the Kaiser and the Huns (E11a-58, 68); flies, fleas, lice (E11a-23, 15); the biscuit, bully beef and water (E11a-35, 16, 37); the enemy's gun by name (E11a-44, 20); and the self (E11a-12 mirror, E11a-47 flinch).

**Forms and mechanics (device: two best examples).**
- *Military language turned on vermin:* "Flies and Fleas" (E11a-23: "Advancing in open or close formation ... the enemy attacks on every flank with fixed bayonets"; "I would be inclined to discuss terms of peace"); "The Never-Ending Chase" (E11a-15: "As he quietly killed one more").
- *Mock-heroic panegyric of the ration and mock hero tale:* "Army Biscuits" (E11a-35: "Agamemnon and the Greeks camped for ten years ... without one box of army biscuits"); "How I Won the V.C." (E11a-46: "it struck on that hard Army biscuit / And rebounded—and blew up a Turk!").
- *Genre substitution (advertisement, estate agent, press profile):* "Arcadia" (E11a-54: "The atmosphere's so full of iron, / It acts as quite a tonic"); "My Anzac Home" (E11a-52: "When 'Beachy Bill' is shelling, I can see just where he lands").

- *Rank-collapsing or rank-suspending beats:* the cook and colonel agreeing on restraint about swearing (E11a-18); the Sergeant and Junior Sub colluding (E11a-4); the officer's mild "Don't do it again!" (E11a-25).

**Rules of license.** Mockery of seniors is **by type, not name** (the Alphabet's G, I, M; "the staff"); only distant public figures are named (the Kaiser, Hindenburg and Belloc as targets; Kitchener appears as a heroic figure). The newcomer, the staff officer and the civilian are the butts of the "stupid question" and "my man" jokes; the wounded or burdened man is the retorter (E11a-6, 12, 22, 55). The Navy is mocked only with warmth (E11a-61). The censor is mocked for blocking facts and passing lies, and thanked for letting the Christmas mails through (E11a-60).

**Limits and what is not joked about.** Combat narrative of the landing (E11a-3); off-duty deaths, the mail-reader and the grave under fire (E11a-10); the burial party, the blanket and the paperwork, where "joked and chatted just this morning; now they're sullen" (E11a-50); the unburied dead (E11a-40); a man's own death and his nightmares (E11a-19, 33, 49); home longing (E11a-17, 28); the front-line Turk (E11a-32, 34). The anthology states its own rule for grief: weeping and "loud cries" are for "meaner deaths" (E11a-14), and pity is "momentary" because of the paperwork's pace (E11a-50). Failure mode recorded: the flinch mistaken for cowardice and the group's "mean advantage" taken of a quiet man, put right by an act the group did not foresee (E11a-47).

**Time course.** Anticipation of the later telling (E11a-46); of the nostalgia (E11a-52); of the lasting reflex ("A striking clock will wake us," E11a-66); of the dug-out as lifelong home (E11a-5); of the biscuit as a relic (E11a-35). In the moment, the weather is the only thing that quiets both sides (E11a-59). The Editor himself marks the change in meaning once evacuation was announced (E11a-2).

**Civilian-facing register.** The book is a translated, footnoted, oath-dashed, trimmed anthology for readers "at their firesides" (E11a-1). The editors correct myths in a straight register (the donkey Murphy, E11a-29), the outside poet uses the soldiers' slang for illness (E11a-42), and the home-front letters in "What Frank Thought" (E11a-53) show what the front thinks civilians do not understand.

**Defector/exile-adjacent.** Little in my range: the verse "we who die in exile" (E11a-14); a half-line about capture as "the chances of ... life in Constantinople" (E11a-51); the one joke shared with the enemy (E11a-13); and the book's own origin as a thing made by men who knew they were being withdrawn (E11a-2).

**Cross-cultural.** Within the Corps: Australian vs. British soldier banter, with "Pommy" explained as "a matter of description" (E11a-16); an officer's British voice vs. vernacular (E11a-21, 43, 66, 67); allies and foes as named types (E11a-27, 39, 63); Egypt and the East in period colonial terms (E11a-24, 64, 65); German ("Hun") as comic villain (E11a-58, 68).

**Craft observations (from the book itself).** The J line ("Remember it's J and the word may be Joking," E11a-57) and the note "The epic loses much of its beauty through a hurried translation ... during a Turkish attack" (E11a-38) are the two places where contributors comment on the act of joking in print; both treat it as an ordinary interruption.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Part 2 is not covered.** My range ends at L6413, in the middle of "The Book of Jobs" (E11a-69). The rest of that item, "The Perfectly True Parable of the Seven Egyptians," "The Silence," "The Growl," "My Lady Nicotine," "The Raid on London," "Sing!," the second alphabet, "To Sari Bair," "On Water Fatigue," "When It's All Over...." the Special Orders (including the evacuation and telegrams), the cover designs, "Correspondence," "Anzac Fashions," "Answers to Correspondents" and the advertisements belong to the next unit. Statements here such as "the only joke in my range" are limited to L1-6413.
- **Selection.** Editors chose and trimmed; Birdwood and Bean's own staff fostered the book. No inference to the general frequency of any kind of joke is safe.
- **Authorship.** Initials may or may not denote one person across items (M. R., C. D. Mc., E. A. M. W., N. Ash); I flagged where I treat initials as one writer. Whether "Crosscut" is T. H. Wilson is an inference (E11a-46). Pen names hide ranks.
- **Images.** Several comic effects depended on drawings that the text file does not contain. I report captions only (E11a-6, 22, 36, 45, 55, 67).
- **Claims the book makes that I have not verified:** the Turkish divisional order quoted in a caption (E11a-45); the donkey Murphy's feat (the editors' own footnote, E11a-29); the claim that German machine-guns stood behind the Turkish line (E11a-34); the statement in the Hamilton extract that "Two Turkish officers were caught in their pajamas" (E11a-41); and numerical claims in the dispatch. The original page images should be consulted in a browser for any quotation relied on in a design document.
- **Footnote wording.** The editors' footnote 11 calls Furphy's carts "rubbish carts"; I report the text as printed, and a check against the original scan would settle any transcription doubt.
- **Period caricature.** Descriptions of Turks, Indians, Maltese, Egyptians, a Maori man, Sikhs, Cairo entertainers and Germans are period and offensive in places; they are listed here as evidence of the book's attitudes only (E11a-24, 32, 34, 39, 64, 65, 68).
- **Word choices that are not spellings** ("whilst," "amongst," "towards," "shorewards") are left as printed.
