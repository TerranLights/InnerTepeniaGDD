## Findings

**[E10a-1]. The "Aunt Annie" nursery column ends by welcoming "new Tot-ties" and promising Christmas parties.** (PW-01, No. 2, 8 Sept. 1917, L24800-24832)
The tail of a recurring mock children's column (its opening is in E09b3's range) is written in broken-syllable baby-talk ("lit-tle," "Reg-gie," "Tot-ties") and treats officers' moves and meetings as children's news: one "Tottie" has "a great big piece of land to play on" and a friend named Reggie; another has moved to "the country" where "there are no naughty boys over the way to throw stones at him" (my reading: a move out of the shelled area); two others "have been having a lot of games"; and "a lot of new Tot-ties who have joined our little circle lately" are "all nice boys," who will "play soldiers nicely together." The column signs off as "Your Ever Loving Aunt Annie." Mechanism: infantilizing register as a cover for the movement of officers, new drafts, and shelling; "play soldiers" names the whole business as children's play without comment. Target: self and the officers' circle (including the reader who is one of the "Tottie" cast).
*Candidate application:* A group's way of announcing new arrivals in a nursery idiom (a standing joke that the new people are children playing at the job) could mark a unit that turns over often; the register lets the veterans say "these are replacements" without saying it. Flag: the column's real referents are inferred (the OCR text does not name the units).

**[E10a-2]. Serial "For King & Country" (Chapter 2): a cheap-romance and spy-fiction parody built on rear-area provost officers, the first women's auxiliary corps, and a chapter that ends on the alarm.** (PW-01, No. 2, L24838-24979)
Headed "Our Splendid New Serial" and billed as "a tale of the Great War," the cast list puns on rank and role: Major Brett, "an A.P.M." (assistant provost marshal, the military-police officer of an area), a colonel, the colonel's son an officer in the "R.T.O.'s" (railway transport officers), a "W.A.A.C." (a member of the women's army auxiliary corps, newly founded in 1917, my gloss) and her maid, and "Sergt. Led Swinger — at the Base" ("lead-swinger" being slang for a shirker). The chapter is a catalog of the A.P.M.'s luxury and drinking: his key "suspended from a thick gold chain," then "a thick platinum chain," a "treble whiskey and Perrier," a drink counted up to the fifth; he "hiccoughed violently and so suddenly that the brawlers ceased their struggles." His voice is "the dulcet and courtly tones for which he was famous." A sergeant brings in "a spy" found "at the corner of the communication trench where the poor old woman sits," who proves to be an officer in an absurd disguise (dress jacket, "blue celluloid collar," bowler hat; the A.P.M. then "hastily emptied the contents of the drink cabinet" at the sight). The sweetheart explains the disguise: an officer of the railway transport service "is not allowed to talk to a W.A.A.C." The A.P.M. gets "a dark and cunning look" ("Here was his chance"), and the chapter ends: "At that moment the gas alarm sounded." Mechanism: the serial form with the cliffhanger replaced by the war itself; the base-area policeman is the villain by role; the parody of purple romantic fiction is carried by lists of luxury and drink. Targets: the A.P.M. and rear-area police; the Base; the rule against fraternizing between officers and women; the spy hysteria (a "poor old woman" at the trench corner as the suspect). Compare the earlier serials ("Narpoo Rum," E09b1-9, E09b2-6): the previous serial ended on a staff accusation, this one on an A.P.M.'s plot; both put the rear-area officer in the villain's seat.
*Candidate application:* The rear-area military-police officer as the stock villain of the front-line paper is a role-based target; the interesting point is that the cliffhanger is "the gas alarm," i.e., the front's real interrupt cuts the comic plot.

**[E10a-3]. Letter from the "Society for Providing Free Gin for Generals": the charity appeal inverted.** (PW-01, No. 2, L24985-25024)
A mock correspondence letter in a charity-secretary's voice asks readers to support a society whose cause is "entirely praiseworthy": Jack (sailor) and Tommy "have their rum provided by a benevolent government, what about our generals?" A "soirée and sale of work" at an invented hotel will be held, "some of the best people are giving their services," and "a small toll will also be levied for exit" (a charge for leaving the hall). "Gin for Generals" is to be "on everyone's lips during the coming months." Signed with a pun name and "Hon. Sec." Mechanism: the charity-appeal letter reversed so that the rank-and-file's rum ration is the model of what is owed to the staff; the toll to leave is the extortionate-charity gag. Target: general officers' comforts; the home-front charity culture (soirées, sales of work) that "supports the troops."
*Candidate application:* Parody charities and societies are a stable device for aiming at the institution through home-front forms; the pity runs upward, which exposes who is actually comfortable.

**[E10a-4]. "The Soldiers' Friend (with apologies to the Daily Mail)": answers to correspondents as a catalog of how pay, pensions, medical boards and "national importance" actually treat people.** (PW-01, No. 2, L25041-25105)
The answers column opens by saying that "owing to lack of space" answers to "our numberless correspondents are still held over," then prints a short column under a newspaper's advice-column title. The items are written flat, as bureaucratic fact. One states that a soldier's pay "ceases on the day on which he is killed" (a plain administrative sentence; the joke is that the column is a "friend"). Another tells a whiskey distiller that he "should get total exemption, being engaged on work of national importance" (the home-front exemption joke). A wife is told her separation allowance is "probably correct" because her husband "probably got a court-martial, and not a Field Marshal's job." A correspondent classed "C 3" is told that a serious loss of sight "should make you unfit for general service" and urged to "appeal again" (a medical-board joke: the board's refusal of an obvious disability; described in restrained terms, see the register). A husband is told to "consult his Regimental Medical Officer," who "is sure to find him a very sympathetic person" (the M.O. as the opposite of sympathetic, my reading). The last item is a language joke about a French sign ("Sage Femme" has "two meanings," my gloss: midwife and wise woman, a mild innuendo). Mechanism: the answers-to-correspondents column as deadpan administrative reply; the harm is in what the rules permit. Targets: pay and allowance rules, medical boards and the R.M.O., exemption of civilian war-profiteers, the press that styles itself the "Soldiers' Friend."
*Candidate application:* A veteran's gallows humor about the benefit and medical systems can run entirely in the form of "official answers" that are accurate and cruel; the reader supplies the injury.

**[E10a-5]. Farewell and welcome to a G.O.C.: the plain paragraph, with the second change inside the year.** (PW-01, No. 2, L25108-25120)
"Once again it is necessary to say 'Good-bye' to our G.O.C." The departing general "was with us" only a "short" time, "yet in that time he had become popular with all ranks"; the paper wishes him "the best of luck," and welcomes the new G.O.C., who "will doubtless soon achieve the popularity enjoyed by his predecessors." No device. (E09b2-16 noted a new G.O.C. in August; here is the next change, so the Division had three commanders in under four months by the paper's own reckoning, my inference from the two notices.) Mechanism: none; the plain farewell column is the standing register for departures (E09b2-7).
*Candidate application:* Frequent command turnover as a plain notice item; the joke register is suspended for the person, and the only trace of the strain is "Once again."

**[E10a-6]. Little Willie and Big Willie: the Kaiser's family speaking, with the British generals' names as the pun.** (PW-01, No. 2, L25123-25131)
A short exchange in a column of German family chat: the Crown Prince as "Little Willie" asks "when will our heaven-protected troops thrust back the hordes that seek to enter our sacred Vaterland, Papa?" and "Big Willie" answers with a pun verse on four British generals' names: "When the Rawlies cease from Goughing, / And their Plumers Byng no more." Mechanism: the enemy's father-and-son in a catechism; the pun turns the commanders' names into verbs of attack; the premise (the enemy asking when the Allies will stop entering their land) is itself a statement of what the writers think the war's direction is in September 1917 (my reading). Target: the enemy leaders (as in E09b1-12 and E09b2-5), while the British generals appear only as honored puns.
*Candidate application:* Making the enemy's leaders the vehicle for the writers' own confidence is a reliable morale joke; the pun on one's own commanders' names is safe praise.

**[E10a-7]. The "Helpman Mind-Training System": a memory-course advertisement as a ladder of rank and overdraft.** (PW-01, No. 2, L25134-25161)
A mock testimonial advertisement for an obvious parody of the Pelman Institute's memory course ("As approved by 'John Bull'. Now used by Nineteen Field Marshals, Three Hundred Lieut.-Generals, Thousands of Officers and over Ten Million Men"). Testimonials: a subaltern whose mind wandered so that he "generally forgot what my balance at Cox's was," and now "I have been reminded of it on several occasions" (the course restores the memory of an overdraft: the course makes things worse); a deputy assistant director of inland water transport who "used to be considered a dud" and is now "down for the next medal he has to give away" (promotion and medals as the reward of a trick); a munitions worker whose "income has gone up by 100 per cent"; and "One who knows" who has "had a letter printed in the Daily Mirror." Mechanism: advertisement hyperbole plus testimonials that each undermine the product. Targets: the staff medal economy; the munition workers' wages; the letters to the press; and the officers' overdrafts at the army bank agent. See also E10a-20 (a long Grandpa piece in the same number on the same course).
*Candidate application:* The overdrawn subaltern is a recurrent figure; the "medal he has to give away" is the cheapest way to say the honors are patronage.

**[E10a-8]. "Mr. Venn's Vanities" and the gum-boot advertisement: trench foot and cold feet in one list.** (PW-01, No. 2, L25164-25230)
Two mock outfitters' ads. (a) "Chat-belts" (a belt "after the improved pattern invented by Captain Shine") at "Plain—100 francs; embroidered with your regimental badge, or a portrait of your best girl—300 francs"; and "Venn's Vests in wool, silk, or corrugated iron (for snipers)," with a couplet "If you want to look your best, / You must order a Venn's Vest." (b) A gum-boot advertisement: "If you have got trench feet, flat feet, or cold feet, we can cure you! Try our gum-boots thigh"; "Fur-lined gum boots for generals" as a "special new departure"; agents "Messrs. Q.M. Dados & Co."; and the verse "Soon will come the snow and sleet, / That's the stuff to spoil your feet! / If you do not want to die, / You must wear our gum-boots thigh." The ad's printer line follows: "Printed and Published by Sherwood, Forester & Co., Ltd." Mechanism: the ad names one real disabling condition (trench foot, which the verse treats as a path to death), one exemption-by-disability term (flat feet), and one idiom for fear (cold feet) as three ailments with one cure; the iron vest for snipers is the joke about the personal threat; fur-lined boots for generals is the caste joke. Targets: ordnance and quartermaster stores, generals' comfort, the winter's mud, sniping.
*Candidate application:* The pun that lumps a medical hazard, a dodge and a fear-word into one list is a strong small device for how an institution blurs injury and cowardice; the verse's flat "If you do not want to die" is the plain statement beneath it.

**[E10a-9]. The new masthead for Vol. 2 No. 3 and the Cloth Hall bill: the enemy's collapse as an opera season.** (PW-01, No. 3, 1 Nov. 1917, L25233-25278)
The masthead now reads "B.E.F. Times, with which are incorporated The Wipers Times, The 'New Church' Times, The Kemmel Times & The Somme-Times. No 3. Vol. 2. Thursday, November 1st, 1917. Price 1 Franc." (a continued roll of absorbed titles as in E09b2-13; the "Kemmel Times" is included). A theater bill "Cloth Hall, Wipers" announces "The Zepp. Troupe, 'Tumblers'" with "Wilhelm and Co." presenting "Grand New Operas," two punning titles on Wagner's opera of the twilight of the gods and on a retreat ("Fritz ran and I'm sold"), and an impresario with a pun name singing "Heigho! The Kiel Row." Glosses (mine; flagged uncertain): the "Zepp Troupe" as the shot-down airships; "Kiel row" as the naval unrest in German ports in the summer of 1917; the impresario's name echoing the German chancellor then in office. Mechanism: theater bill as the container for the enemy's troubles, "prices as usual, book early." Target: the enemy command and its political-naval strain.
*Candidate application:* By late 1917 the enemy's difficulties are the subject of the bill and the British line is the audience; the booking joke ("book early") is the confident register.

**[E10a-10]. "The Great Army Peace Movement": the wish for peace stated as an advertisement, with the C-in-C as president.** (PW-01, No. 3, L25284-25314)
A bold-lettered mock society advertisement: "ENROLL TO-DAY!!! WE WANT PEACE!!! The Society has been formed with the object of insisting on PEACE at the earliest possible moment. You are asked to help! Big advances in the cause have been made this year and we are holding a GREAT ANTI-WAR DEMONSTRATION at PASSCHENDAELE. All are welcome. President: D. Haig, Esq. Speakers: R. Tillery and T. Atkins." Mechanism: the plain wish is said in the form of a political society's handbill; the "advances" pun turns the offensive into the society's progress; the place named is the battlefield of the then-current offensive, and the president and speakers are the commander-in-chief, the artillery, and the private soldier (names as puns). Target: the battle itself and the peace politicians (E09b2-24) at once, while conceding the wish. The paper had mocked the peace politician in August (E09b2-17, E09b2-24); here the wish is voiced by the army through the mockery of the politicians' form.
*Candidate application:* A soldiers' own anti-war society, with the commander as president, is the way to say "we want it over" while keeping the joke on the offensive; a defector character might know the form and the way it was used as cover.

**[E10a-11]. "Wipers & District Gas Coy.": poison gas as a public-utility prospectus.** (PW-01, No. 3, L25317-25348)
A utility-company advertisement: the company "has lately opened a new dairy, conducted on up-to-date and hygienic principles"; "owing to the ever-increasing use of Gas in the neighborhood of Wipers, the Company are increasing their plant to cope with the extra demand," hoping "to supply all (and more) than the consumers want"; "branch offices at any Artillery Headquarters"; "stock will be issued at 18/"; "rapid and regular delivery guaranteed"; and a milk-seller's jingle with "purity of supply guaranteed" and "milk delivered 3 times daily." Mechanism: the gas bombardment described as retail supply, with the enemy as "consumers" and the company's capacity as the artillery's; the dairy and the milk jingle sit beside it as pastoral cover. Target: the artillery and chemical-warfare industry; the language of progress and supply.
*Candidate application:* Describing weapons in the idiom of commerce ("supply," "stock issued," "the consumers") is a stable device; note that no injury is shown or named, and the paper's other gas content is the "gas alarm" (E10a-2).

**[E10a-12]. Editorial of 1 November 1917: the paper threatens to close for want of copy and blames the Division's lack of interest.** (PW-01, No. 3, L25354-25438)
The editorial opens with a joke that the paper "doesn't seem" likely to "run many more numbers before we have the 'Special Double Potsdam Number'" (i.e., the paper will not survive to the end of the war; or the war will end by Potsdam; the sentence is ambiguous and I read it as a joke on both), then a rain joke ("We wonder how the Huns have managed to bribe Mr. Jupiter Pluvius, he is certainly the only one who has never failed them yet"). The main statement is a plain warning: "unless greater assistance is forthcoming in the shape of copy we shall be compelled to shut down." The editors say that "eighteen months ago and even up to a year ago" members of the Division "used to help us by sending along copy," often with a modest cover letter, but "nowadays it is a very different story, and we think only two members of the Division make an attempt at helping us along. It cannot be lack of ability, so that we must put it down to either laziness or lack of interest, and, if the latter, then the sooner we shut down the better." They then turn to "the sporting side of Divisional life," asking for division football contests and a cup or medal tournament with "one committee member by Brigades," on the principle that "those who play well together work well together." The appeal ends: "Write any old thing and send it along, it may not be used but it may be useful. The Editor." Mechanism: the editor's own scolding, plain and short, bracketed by two jokes. The editors' stated purposes (as in E08): the paper is for the Division, and its life depends on contribution; sport is presented as the means of cohesion. Signs of exhaustion: the contributor base has shrunk to two in a year (the editorial does not discuss casualties or turnover as a cause; that is my inference and a gap). The closing offer of "a Grand Xmas Number" sets the next issue.
*Candidate application:* A group's cultural output can fail from within, and the editors' reading ("laziness or lack of interest") is the optimistic one; the unspoken reading is that the contributors who wrote in 1916 were no longer there.

**[E10a-13]. "The Burning Question": the football argument that runs through the attack, the wounds and the hospital train.** (PW-01, No. 3, L25444-25548)
A four-part verse. Three Tommies "in a trench one day" discuss the mud, the enemy, the rum, and leave, but "the point which they argued from post back to pillar / was whether Notts County could beat Aston Villa." On the night before the attack, equipment made ready and watches consulted, one voice rings out through the sergeants' calls for steadiness: "I tell yer Notts County can beat Aston Villa!" The refrain follows the men over the top ("through mud and through wire"), to a stopped position where "through the din came a voice: 'Say, Jack Miller!'," to the consolidated objective where "the air, charged with moment, grows stiller and stiller," and to a closing stanza in which two men are wounded ("two 'Blighties'"), pass through the aid post and clearing station, and lie "packed side by side in a clean Red Cross train," "happy in hopes to see Blighty again," while a voice "through the bandages, muffled" still says "Jack Miller, I bet you Notts County can beat Aston Villa!" The losses are a clause ("in number grown less"; "no time to heed those who are falling") and nothing is graphic. Mechanism: a trivial argument as the thing that carries the speaker through every stage; the verse's last stanza lets the same line survive the wound. Function: coping and cohesion by the shared football loyalty, and a statement of what the ranks actually talk about at zero.
*Candidate application:* An absorbing, insistent trivial quarrel (sport, wages, a rival) in the mouth of men at the moment of danger is a documented pattern; the effect is to keep the register ordinary while the situation is not.

**[E10a-14]. "Haig Harries the Huns": the correspondent's piece on the Flanders mud, with a one-man-on-the-next stack.** (PW-01, No. 3, L25553-25603)
Teech Bomas (the paper's mock war correspondent, E08 onward) writes: "At break of day the hunters are about, and Haig's harriers hourly harry the Huns," with the earth "a yielding morass which clasps mortals by the thighs" and "conditions which would have driven Napoleon to a headlong flight," in which he cries for a horse then says "What's the good of one anyway." He offers two tall tales: an aeroplane that "scattered hundreds of generals advancing to the attack in motor cars, although both its wings had been blown off in the gale," and a feat of "self-sacrifice" in which one man of a county regiment stood on another's shoulders "to enable the third to climb on the shoulders of the top one and so reach the top of the mud and stop a counter attack." He ends with a flat conclusion: "But why take up one deed when everything was epic," and a note that he is "going for my usual winter's tour to warmer climates after the end of Nov. so there will be no more operations on a large scale to chronicle." Mechanism: the epic correspondent is shown to follow the season, not the war; the mud is the enemy in his tales, and the heroes' stunt is a way of staying above it; the aeroplane story puts the generals in cars. Target: the war correspondents; the staff in cars; the Passchendaele mud. The last line also implies (as the paper elsewhere) that large operations stop with the weather (my reading of the seasonal pattern).
*Candidate application:* The correspondent who leaves for the winter is a precise class joke: the people who narrate the war are on a calendar, the ones in the mud are not.

**[E10a-15]. "Our Diary, by Lieut. Samuel Pepys": the trench in a diarist's complaint about breeches, coal prices and "the Peelers."** (PW-01, No. 3, L25613-25670)
A Pepys-style diary in archaic idiom. The writer has taken up residence "in a new part of the trench, a noisome place," where the mud is "of a terrifying stickiness" and he fears for his breeches "which cost me one guinea at the Hope Brothers' establishment in Cheapside," and has spoiled a new coat on the wire. His wife "is lavish in expenditure" and writes of "coal which is now up 10/- the ton and potatoes." An old friend, "Major the Honorable Reginald," is "like to leave us," which "grieves me, as to drink a stoop of ale with him was one of my few pleasures in this abominable country." It is "raining with an intensity not to be believed" and "I think the Government should move in the matter, but one hears that they are taken up with other matters of lesser concern." The enemy has "again sent an airship to London, and has broken more windows. This is intolerable, and one must blame the Peelers" (the police). He attends a speech on discipline that "did intrigue me mildly"; sees "the Countess of Kruisstraat in the Park" (a trench crossing as a society beauty); and ends with the night's work: "I must take a party out for the sandbagging, to bed at 7 of the clock, after a poor dinner." Mechanism: the diarist's trivial grievances (clothes, prices, bad dinner) take the place of the danger, and the one real loss (a friend's departure) is told in the same pastiche voice. Targets: the home front's prices and air-raid complaints; the Government; the staff lecture on discipline; the self.
*Candidate application:* A period voice (here Pepys) is used so that the writer can complain about the war's discomfort with distance and without sincerity; the departure of a friend is allowed one sincere clause.

**[E10a-16]. "Five Minutes": a zero-hour countdown written with minimal comedy and with the writer's own self-commentary.** (PW-01, No. 3, L25676-25755; signed C.L.P.)
The piece is a series of short entries counting down from "Zero minus five" (the headings are "two hours" and then "five," "four," "three," "two," "one" in the text, my best reading of the OCR). The first entries are in a plain, excited voice with a purple image: "Our guns are busier than ever they have been before. See:—gigantic flashes of light; gigantic flashes of sound; a hundred hundred gigantic, jeweled hands rapidly running over the whole horizon, playing concerted Hell on the Harpsichord of Hate to an audience of terrified Teutons." The narrator then comments on his own sentence: "That is good. I must remember it, if I get out of this alive." The next entries: "I didn't think I could be sorry for those Huns. Not since they got my mate at Vimy"; a short round falls and the complaint "Wonder why these Artillery blokes don't scrap their worn-out eighteen-pounders"; a flash he takes for a dump, then "My mistake. It's the sun coming up," and a thought of a girl "in her little cottage at home" with "What silly ideas to come into my head at a time like this!" The last entry is the sergeant and the officer, "a cool one he is, and the best one we've ever had in Beer Coy," and the sergeant's order to "give them" the enemy "'ell," answered "You bet, WE WILL!" Then: "I dare not criticize their questions. And the foolscap query before my wondering gaze—I vainly strive to answer their Q 99065." Mechanism: a sonnet of praise to the staff's appearance that ends by quoting a file number; the awe-inducing staff is reduced to forms. Target: staff officers' appearance and their questions. The title ("To the Authors of 'Woss'") is unclear; my reading is that it addresses the writers of the staff's circulars.
*Candidate application:* The brass hat's polish against the infantry's mud as an image that does not need explaining; the form number is the reveal.

**[E10a-75]. "A Wail": a four-line complaint about losing hair.** (PW-01, No. 5, L29610-29617)
"Life has many disappointments, / Days are never free from care, / For in spite of using ointments / Steadily I'm losing hair." Mechanism: a trivial lament filling a half-column; the "life has many disappointments" cadence promises more than it delivers. Target: self.
*Candidate application:* Space-filling small lamentations about the body as the paper's baseline level of complaint.

**[E10a-76]. "The Sapper": a tribute in technical idiom to the engineer's unthanked labor.** (PW-01, No. 5, L29627-29678; signed H.J.C.P.)
A poem on the Royal Engineer: "No gain to him, his labor unrequited, / His toil the sport of wind or wet or Hun; / No glory his in dredging or revetting / The sliding mud-banks of the danger zone." He has met "the wayward mind, the flights of fancy, the change and chances of the battle plan, / And having gazed upon his work completed, / He's dug it up and done it all again." The second and third stanzas are a catalog of his materials and skills ("the berm," "the wily A frame," "the concertina," "the giant elephant" (corrugated shelter), "apron wire," "the standing slope of liquid mud," "duckboards can be used for firewood," "the mystery of the deepest sump," "expanded metal," "wire both barbed and plain or binding"), and the last two lines: "You ask for what you want—he does the rest." Mechanism: praise by an inventory of trade terms; the poem's emotional center is the line about redoing the work. Compare the inter-arm verses of E09b1-23 to E09b1-25, in which the sappers were teased; here they are honored. Target: the planners who change the plan.
*Candidate application:* Technical vocabulary as an honorific register; labor that is redone because of a change in plan is the engineer's grievance, told as a compliment.

**[E10a-77]. A prose sketch by H. Davis: the old soldier who dug holes to save the Government the cost of shells, and the medical officer's "N.Y.D.—Mental."** (PW-01, No. 5, L29682-29745; signed H. Davis; the heading is lost in the OCR)
The same American medical officer as E10a-58 (the signature matches) writes of his usual walk before dinner (the OCR of the stated purpose is damaged), in which he spots a lone man digging toward the enemy's lines, "bent" and "old," stalks him "taking my six-shooter in one hand and courage in the other," and finds "a Tommy, old, and armed only with a spade," who "had been digging holes in the ground, until that part of the field looked like a colander." The narrator reasons that the man was either seeking a sniper's bullet or was a traitor ("signals to aeroplanes or something"; the first reading is told in restrained terms, see the register). The old man explains in an economic lecture: iron mined and treated by "hundreds of men," made into shells, put into costly guns, sent on with cordite, "all this costs money, two, three or maybe five pounds a-piece"; he counts eight holes: "'You see, sir,' he said, 'I have saved my Government twenty to forty pounds ... for that is all these shells are doing, digging holes, and I can do it cheaper.'" The narrator reports him to the C.O.; "the poor old man was sent to Blighty by the Artillery M.O., who had heard the tale and diagnosed the case as 'N.Y.D.—Mental.'" The coda: "in a little cottage in Yorkshire you might see our old friend with his people all about him, the fire burning brightly. In a corner there is a spade all cleaned and polished, and as the old man sees it there is a twinkle in his eye. While out here the Artillery M.O. is slowly writing on scraps of paper: 'Mental—Query?'" Mechanism: a rational critique of the war's cost voiced as a madman's eccentricity, with the diagnosis (N.Y.D., the code of E10a-60) as the way out; the twinkle in the old man's eye makes the reader wonder if he feigned it; the final line turns the doubt back on the medical officer. Target: the cost of the war; the shell barrage as hole-digging; the diagnostic system; the officer who reports.
*Candidate application:* The strongest dissent-by-absurdity in the range: the old soldier states the argument that the war is a waste in an accountant's voice, is certified mentally unfit and goes home, and the person left suspecting his own sanity is the officer who certified him. A defector-type character could be built on exactly this structure (an argument that cannot be refuted and so is diagnosed).

**[E10a-78]. The Frankau quarrel: a contributor's furious verse against the editor for altering "Long Ago," and the editor's grovel.** (PW-01, No. 5, L29755-29890; signed Gilbert Frankau, dated 6/1/18; and "Justification" by the Editor)
Under a heading that calls it "a plain statement of a disgusting crime wrought by the Editor of the 'B.E.F. Times'" who altered the last three lines of Frankau's poem (E10a-48) "in such a way as to make him appear guilty of writing false quantities," Frankau writes a comic curse: he woke at noon "from nightmared slumber" to find "your special Christmas Double Number," read it "and wished that I were dead," swears "never again to send a single copy of my verses" to "you ... who ought to spend ten years in jail, my friend," because "those lines don't even scan." He lists lesser crimes the editor might have committed ("forged checks, committed arson, ... rifled a church, or thrashed a parson, or—drunk on Belgian beer—sandbagged a Brigadier") and ends "He dared to edit me." The editor's "Justification" in verse: "Dear Gilbert, once again we don / To show our deep contrition / Sackcloth and ashes. Hope is gone, / You hate our new rendition." He says that "the line we gently camouflaged / Presaged times alcoholic," and that "nights wild and woolly you impute / To one who seldom takes beer," and ends "Yet now to calm a poet's soul / And eke to get more 'copy' / We grovel in the ashes, coal, / And own our line was sloppy." Mechanism: the contributor and editor ridicule each other in verse; the exchange shows the editors cutting a line of a contributed poem for what it implied about alcohol (the text says only that the line "presaged times alcoholic," my reading is that the final lines referred to drinking), and publishing the poet's rage as a feature; "to get more copy" repeats the paper's need for contributions (E10a-12).
*Candidate application:* Editorial softening and its penalty (the author's public fury); the document records the paper's one editorial intervention in a contributed poem and the humor that is made of it.

**[E10a-79]. "Martialgia": a mock medical lecture that turns volunteering and conscription into three epidemics, with the staff's "benign form" and the infantry's "extremely high" mortality.** (PW-01, No. 5, L29896-30068; signed R.M.O.)
A mock lecture "on a new disease" that warns "the flippant" to go elsewhere ("Mr. Teech Bomas's next door"; "Mr. Pepys, two blocks further on"). "Nothing so common as Trench Feet, the delight of the Higher Command; nothing so limited as Shell Shock, the last endeavour of the unwilling." The disease is "widespread as the world itself," mainly among males aged 17 to 45, "extremely infectious at this period of life"; "a few women have been smitten with khaki fever"; certain ailments confer "a certain amount of immunity," though "instances have been reported at tribunals" in which blindness, and other disabilities (summarized; see the register), "have been no bar to infection." Three "epochs or epidemics": 1914 ("a profuse discharge of martial fervor; whence the name"); the second, "Derby's Disease," in which "patients were notified exactly when they were likely to become infected" and "skilled munition-makers, who had regarded themselves as unlikely to catch the complaint, were admitted to the Recruiting Hospitals"; and the third, conscription, with "the concurrent propagation of a new religion" whose feature was "hyperaesthesia of the conscience, associated with an affliction of the feet," in which the infected "were speedily sent abroad on short sea voyages," and "those who escaped the infection were isolated, and kept under observation in spacious and luxuriously appointed institutions, where baths and clean sheets were provided once a week; and hot water bottles were a nightly issue." An "Hibernian eruption" is expected. Symptoms: early "a sense of bien-être" and "prodigious tasks in the matter of laying duckboards and making roads"; later "malaise," "fed up from the onset"; hypochondriacs "of the plumbo-rotary type" who demand medical attention "in spite of frequent M and D's," for which "an occasional dose of the F.P. Mixture No. 1 has a marked effect in clearing up the introspective symptoms." Two forms: the common one divided "into sub groups ... distinguished by initials, e.g.: R.A., R.E., P.B.I.," with "the mortality ... extremely high," and the "benign form," "characterized by a roseolar eruption, which breaks out upon the forehead and clavicles" (the red and other colored tabs and hat-bands of staff officers), whose patients "are robust and well nourished," in "a state of exaltation, with a tendency to hilarity or boisterousness, which is not unnatural, as there is practically no likelihood of a fatal termination." The author "is engaged on a more detailed article ... for the B.M.J. and Lancet." Mechanism: the epidemiological paper's form (incidence, history, symptomatology, forms) carries a complete class analysis: volunteers, Derby-scheme men, skilled workers and objectors are the epidemics' stages, and the staff is the benign form. Target: conscientious objectors and their treatment (a mocking description), munition workers who sought exemption, the medical profession's phrases, and the staff's hat-band. Shell shock is dismissed in a clause ("the last endeavour of the unwilling"), the only mention in the range, quoted because it is exactly the stance (a joke register that excludes psychological injury) and is not argued.
*Candidate application:* The fullest statement in this range of how the front sorts the army into those who are exposed and those who are not; the dismissal of shell shock in one clause places the limit of the joke's sympathy (see the roll-up).

**[E10a-80]. "Profit and Loss": a ballad that sets the Kaiser's six sons against a German farmer's seven, and ends with the mother weeping for the Kaiser's.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30072-30158)
A narrative ballad in long couplets: "William Hohenzollern, the King of all the Huns, / Had quite a lot of country and he also had six sons"; against him "Karl Baumberg lived in comfort with his frau and family," seven sons and three daughters, "just enough of everything and wished for nothing more (This happy time, you understand, was just before the war)." The seven sons are "quickly clad in suits of gray and labelled 'food for guns,'" and are accounted for by place names in the Allied sectors and at Verdun (the deaths are summarized, see the register). The one left is maimed; the mother "freely curses both the Kaiser and his brood"; a sister tries to make a stew "out of water and a horse bone" when an official order comes to fly flags "for the victory of Verdun." The father realizes "that the victories are worthless if they bring no end in view," and curses the Kaiser, "But his frau is quietly sobbing for—the Kaiser has six sons." Mechanism: a ballad of the enemy's home front used for a plain comparison of loss and privilege ("Profit and Loss"), ending on a line in which the mother's grief widens to the Kaiser's sons (the line reads as pity or as fear for the next war's casualties, my reading, ambiguous). The ballad is the paper's fullest sympathetic portrait of an enemy family, and the only humor is the heading and the tone of the opening couplet. Target: the Kaiser; the official victory orders.
*Candidate application:* A late-war soldier's ability to write an enemy household as a household (rationing, flags by order, mother's tears) is documented here; the final line withholds the moral.

**[E10a-81]. "A Day in Rest": a stage dialogue of a post-exercise staff conference in an estaminet, ended by an air raid.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30164-30341)
A skit in script form. A notice: "All Officers will attend the Conference at the 'Estaminet des Voyageurs' on the termination of operations." Dramatis personae with alliterative names beginning with B (a major-general director, two lieutenant-colonels, a brigadier "G.O.C. (Blue)," an umpire-in-chief, his assistant, "P.B.I.C.O.," a barmaid named Bessie, "a bevy of subalterns"). The subalterns' chorus: "We are weary, we are languid, / We are sore distressed, / What we want is not this rot, but / Beer and rest." The barmaid brings buckets of beer; the director: "Beer?" "Who ordered these?"; the junior assistant umpire, "blushing badly," is blamed; the colonel begins to read "the scheme" ("The enemy are retiring toward the Windyberg line, our troops are in hot pursuit"); the director cuts him off; when the assistant offers that "a Staff Major from G.H.Q." thought the scheme "a good one," the director replies "Most interesting, but hardly has any bearing on to-day's operations!" and "Barris collapses." Then the barmaid shrieks that "the Hun planes are here!"; one brigadier "hastily seizes Bessie, and commences stroking her hair" (mild innuendo, summarized in the register); bombs are heard; "Bancock bolts the beer, and the conference breaks up in confusion, leaving Bone master of the situation." Mechanism: the training scheme's conference (with the "enemy retiring" scenario in which the staff always wins) is overturned by a real raid; the senior officers' fear and appetite are shown in stage directions; the P.B.I. commander drinks the beer. Target: staff exercises and their umpires, red-hats, the barmaid trope, the brass under air raid.
*Candidate application:* A rest-area conference where the real enemy ends the performance of authority, and the only functioning response is that of the man who bolts the beer.

**[E10a-82]. "K.R.": a verse in praise of King's Regulations as the soldier's Bible, ending every stanza on the initials.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30348-30392)
Five stanzas in a mock-serious style with a refrain of "K.R.": "Life in the Army, you must know / Is hedged about with Regulation"; "we have to mind each P and Q ... but always be conforming to / K.R."; the volume must be "known off pat, / With all its manifold restrictions / Of para this and para that, / Not wholly free from contradictions"; "it deals with every sort of case, a most complete encyclopedia ... Thus it will readily be seen / Why no excuses ever are / Accepted, when we contravene / K.R."; "the powers who sit enthroned on high maintaining splendid isolation, draft an occasional A.C.I. and publish it for information; yet is our Bible and our Creed, our Lodestone, and our Guiding Star ... K.R."; the last stanza apostrophizes "O shades of Authors past and gone," "O peerless Lore, and priceless Myth, careers in Arms you'll sternly mar, if they're not in accordance with K.R." Mechanism: the rule book as sacred text (the scriptural register of E10a-26, E10a-60), the refrain's exhaustive deadpan, "not wholly free from contradictions" as the barb; the claim that no excuse is ever accepted is the center. Target: the regulations; "the powers ... enthroned on high."
*Candidate application:* The rule book as an unquestioned creed whose contradictions and the absence of excuses are the defining facts; a character who knows the creed and stopped quoting it.

**[E10a-83]. "Another Wail": the officer's overdraft with a War Bonds slogan on the bank's envelope.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30396-30421; signed Bill Shakespeare)

**[E10a-84]. The "Hidden Word" competition: a verse prospectus for a coupon contest whose prizes are boxes of undergarments.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30427-30454)
A verse in the manner of a newspaper missing-word contest: "It's a simple competition and hilariously funny," entrance money, "ten and sixpence for a dozen weeks or so," twelve coupons to be arranged to spell a word, "seven in number" prizes of "dainty things," "to bachelor or benedict unbounded pleasure brings," "quite unlike those ragged things" the servant washes and hangs on the line "that stretches from your dug-out to the Sergeant Major's door," with a closing couplet: "You mustn't wear the prize yourself, / except perhaps on Sundays, / For think, they're seven boxes of Penn's / dear delightful undies." **[E10a-85]. "What Might Have Been": the war's effect on famous poets, in five parodies, including an estaminet scene in blank verse and a Browning lyric to a sergeant.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30466-30589; signed R.)
A column-filler "on the other side of the picture," on what would have happened "if the War had coincided with several well-known poets." (1) "W. Shakespeare" in a dugout "near Hell-Fire Corner," in a scene at an estaminet at Dickebusch, in soldier-French dialogue: "Tommy.—'Beer, mamselle, compree, what?' Waitress.—'Nutting doin'.' Atkins.—'Narpoo! Owell then get some old vang blang!' Waitress.—'No got it, m'sieur, you 'ave some vin rouge.' ... 'Apree le guer?'" (a scene in the soldiers' pidgin French); the author adds that "this is only a short example but space is limited." (2) A cricket poet (Craig): "The Middlesex boys then took the field ... The blighted Hun he would not yield, / So they hit him for six." (3) Browning: "Teach me dearest Sergeant, / Don't look glum. / You may eat my bully, / Drink my rum. ... Join a working party, / Put up wires. ... I am on F.P., dear, / Leagues and leagues." (4) A Scottish poet's dialect lines on "Thamas," who "draigl't a' his wee bit kilt, / Coming thro' the wire," with the note "this is easy, as you only have to alter the spelling of the word to make it fit." Mechanism: the established poets' forms filled with soldiers' items (bully, rum, F.P., working parties, wire, the estaminet, soldier French) and the author's own admission of space-filling. Target: the poets, the soldier's pidgin French, the kilted regiments; the paper's own pressure to fill the page and the Staff's waiting.
*Candidate application:* A device for the paper's last page: the list of poets is both a joke and a space-filler; the Browning lyric states the sergeant-private relation as a love lyric about the rum and the fatigue.

**[E10a-87]. Two letters, January 1918: the leave journey and the weaklings in the beer queue.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30733-30783)
(a) "Voyageur," on his "journey from London to here on returning from leave," gives "considerable thought" to "whether it would not be shorter and more comfortable to go via New York, San Francisco, Japan, Brindisi and Genoa"; asks "why only allow eight persons in a compartment, surely twelve could get in"; and approves the rule that anyone on the leave train for "five or six days" cannot get a drink as the train always arrives "after hours": "This is quite right, as it might lead to undue hilarity, and prevent the pessimism necessary to thoroughly enjoy the journey." (b) "A. Sapper," waiting in "a beer queue at the 'Foresters Arms,'" sees "many who were certainly not fit to wait perhaps hours in the cold. It is a scandal that these poor weaklings should be allowed to run the risk of trench feet just for a spot of beer." He asks the paper to act because "sometimes it is finished before I can get my elbow on the counter." Mechanism: the deadpan complaint letter, the ironic endorsement of the system (the "pessimism necessary"), and the pose of concern for others that is concern for oneself. Target: the leave train, the beer supply; trench feet as an excuse.
*Candidate application:* The leave journey of days is the cost of leave; the letter's joke is that pessimism is a duty on the leave train; the trench-feet "concern" is another use of the list that began in E10a-8.

**[E10a-88]. "A Few Things We Really Want to Know": the paper's new targets in January 1918: labor leaders, home-front food complaints and margarine.** (PW-01, No. 5, L30786-30831)

The mood is of irritation at the home front: "imaginary condition" of shortage; "sense of proportion." Target: labor leaders and their rules for the army, the home masses and their food complaints. The phrase "perhaps one exception" is unresolved in the OCR.
*Candidate application:* By January 1918 the paper's resentment of the home front is explicit and no longer disguised as a joke; the claim that the Prime Minister reads the paper is its own boast.

## Pattern roll-up

**Catalog of NEW devices in my range (devices of E08, E09a, E09b1 and E09b2 continue and are not re-listed; E09b3 was not available to me, so a few items below may have first appeared in L22,900-24,830; finding numbers in brackets).**

3. **A serial killed off by the front's real weapon,** with the editors' bracket blaming "leave" and the self-insult "rottenest serial," then a detective serial on the same villains (provost officers) [E10a-2, E10a-30, E10a-47, E10a-86].
