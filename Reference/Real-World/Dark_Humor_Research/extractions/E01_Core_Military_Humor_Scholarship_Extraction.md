**AC-01 (Johais 2025, Schlagfertigkeit).** Journal article, about 9,000 words plus references. Evidence base: in-depth interviews and group discussions with 35 current or former Bundeswehr soldiers in 2022, plus observation at veteran-association events, base visits and the 2022 Bundeswehr Day; interview quotations are translated from German by the author. Argues that the German word *Schlagfertigkeit* (quick, pointed, situation-specific repartee; literally "strike-readiness") names the soldier humor style, because it bundles four soldierly virtues: being able to fight, to take it, to be a loyal comrade, and (by the military's own gendering) to be a man. **Does NOT contain:** humor about death, wounds, enemy dead or combat trauma (the examples are barracks, training, deployment-camp and dare humor); any defector, dissenter or post-service material; full joke texts (the Catholic-priest joke and the joke-battle graffiti are described by topic only); counts of how many interviewees were women; any consequences for those who refused or failed (the paper documents successes and "legends"). Its reference list is a map of the field and I did not extract it; the names to chase are noted in Gaps.

**AC-27 (Sløk-Andersen 2019).** Journal article (about 15,000 words including notes, references and two responses). Evidence base: four months of participant observation (spring 2016) as a conscript-style participant in one platoon at one of eight Danish army camps, 38 interviews (26 with conscripts, 12 with superiors), plus two days at a second camp in 2017. Danish conscripts serve about four months, are not deployed, and in this study about 17 percent are women. Theory is Sara Ahmed's affect work (mood, attunement, the "killjoy") plus Butler-style performativity. **Does NOT contain:** combat, deployed or wartime humor; death or injury humor; any resistance humor aimed up at officers beyond one brief example; other armies (except through the two responses); any ex-soldier perspective. The article's heavy emphasis is sexual humor and the policing of sexuality; it states it will not try to draw the funny/offensive line itself. The two responses (Gilman; Plester) are printed in a two-column layout that the text conversion interleaved and garbled; I reconstructed their arguments and quote only phrases I could confirm. Four figures are photographs and are not in the text copy (captions only).

**AC-17 (Tidy 2021).** Accepted manuscript, about 8,600 words, double-spaced text. Evidence base: British Ministry of Defense official public obituaries of UK service members killed in Afghanistan (456 fatalities, 2001-2015) and Iraq (179, 2003-2009), read for references to humor, banter, wit and jokes; names omitted by the author. Feminist argument that humor (above all "banter") is a gender practice that supports military institutions and their violence rather than only resisting them. Also draws briefly on court and press accounts of British and U.S. abuse of prisoners. **Does NOT contain:** soldiers' own unfiltered speech (every example passed through the institution's obituary process, and Tidy treats that filtering as the finding); gallows humor in the narrow sense (Tidy remarks that the obituaries only vaguely hint at it); any joke text apart from short remembered quips; defector or veteran material; any positive account of humor as resistance (deliberately, the article argues the opposite case).

**AC-40 (Johais 2024, WARFUN taboo).** Journal article, about 7,600 words including footnotes (a PubMed Central full-text copy, so no page markers or section headings). Same 2022 interview base as AC-01 (35 current or former German soldiers). Argues that German anti-militarist political culture produces a taboo on associating war with fun, that this taboo shaped the official "citizen in uniform" soldier model (which leaves no room for a distinct military humor), and that soldiers internalized it and therefore hide their humor from civilians, which deepens their alienation from society. About half the text is German political history. **Does NOT contain:** joke texts beyond two short sayings and a few anecdotes; non-German soldiers; any treatment of combat humor itself (it is about concealment, not content); veterans as a subject beyond how society frames them.

**AC-25 (Johais 2023 blog, "No laughter, no war").** Short web essay (about 2,200 words). Three German soldier sayings, each unpacked through the situation in which it is used, from the same 2022 interviews (more than 30 interviewees). **Does NOT contain:** dark or gallows humor, humor about the enemy or the dead, or anything outside German soldiers in training, garrison and Afghanistan deployment. Footnote 2 states the author uses male pronouns throughout because the military is still male-dominated.

## Findings

### From AC-01 (Johais 2025, Schlagfertigkeit)

**[E01-1]. The standard list of what military humor does, and the paper's different question.** (AC-01, [p.2-3])
Johais restates the functions earlier scholars assign to military humor: (a) a "controlled form of resistance" that vents anger and frustration about hardship without destabilizing the institution or hurting performance; (b) group cohesion through "a shared universe of meaning" and an inside/outside boundary; (c) a means of formal and informal discipline and a testing ground for power between subordinates and superiors; (d) individual coping and stress relief in a total institution and in acute danger. Johais then sets these aside as functions that also exist in other workplaces and asks what is culturally specific to soldier humor: what it teaches about being a soldier. The answer developed is that the humor trains the soldier subject by imitating soldiering in play.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's humor can be written as doing double duty: relief for him or her, and unacknowledged training of the group in what a "proper" soldier is. The ex-soldier's humor, years out, may still carry those standards even when the person has rejected the institution.

**[E01-2]. A joke works only if the audience can decode it, and failure is signaled by silence; military humor is more than jokes.** (AC-01, [p.3])
Joking is interactive and referential: it succeeds only when a shared interpretive system lets the audience decode the figurative or ambiguous meaning. Groups negotiate proper behavior through reactions to humorous attempts; a joker who breaks group expectations is "reprimanded" by absence of laughter, so joking either recognizes someone as a member or marks them as an outsider ignorant of the group's humor tradition and moral limits. Johais widens "humor" past speech acts to caricatures, pranks, tests of courage and rites of passage.
*Candidate application:* Silence after a joke is a documented, low-cost sanction; it can show a character's standing or exile more economically than any argument. Soldier cultures also carry humor in objects and rites, not only talk.

**[E01-3]. Soldier humor does not fit the standard humor typology: "give as good as you get," and banter versus hazing is a matter of tone.** (AC-01, [p.4])
A workplace-humor typology sorts humor by target (self or others) and effect. Johais, citing ethnography by Basham and Tidy's obituary study, says present-day British "service humor" does not fit it, because it demands the capacity to "give as good as you get": every player may attack comrades and be attacked in turn. These teasing-and-testing practices run from affectionate to aggressive; depending on tone they are called banter (seen as, especially male, bonding) or hazing (condemned as transgressive conduct that endangers the institution's reputation). The same paragraph cites Madigan on British First World War troops, whose comic dialogues, songs and cartoons in troop-produced publications used sarcastic self-deprecation to define courage as "sticking it" (endurance despite fear) against the public's image of fearless eagerness to die.
*Candidate application:* Reciprocity is the license: a character who dishes it out but cannot take it, or the reverse, falls outside the game. WWI "sticking it" is a real precedent for a soldier culture that defines courage against the civilian hero story.

**[E01-4]. Schlagfertigkeit defined.** (AC-01, [p.4-5])
The German word combines *Schlag* (strike, punch) and *Fertigkeit* (skill); it originally meant the fighting readiness of a soldier or army and now also means verbal readiness. As a rhetorical skill it is brief, pointed, apparently spontaneous, situation-specific, gets its force from countering expectations and surprising the opponent, and requires speaking "ex tempore" according to circumstances, often by exaggerating or breaking convention. It is treated as both talent and trainable skill, and it only works in the moment if the speaker has already thought the topic through. In a military setting it requires knowing the culture well enough to reproduce or redefine it in verbal duels.
*Candidate application:* The "quick comeback" can be written as preparation disguised as spontaneity: a veteran's seeming offhand answer is the product of years of rehearsed material and shared context.

**[E01-5]. Verbal duelling is a ritual pattern mostly practiced by men and boys; women avoid it in public.** (AC-01, [p.5])
Johais, citing a gender-and-humor review, describes "verbal duelling" as ritualized humorous attack, predominantly male, openly acted out in male groups such as sports teams where it is considered a catalyst for cohesion and performance; women avoid doing it in public to escape social punishment for violating gender expectations, though they tease in similar ways among themselves. Johais notes the German forces deviate from the hegemonic warrior model (antimilitarist society, "citizen in uniform") yet still prize humor skill.
*Candidate application:* Who is allowed to duel in public is itself a rule of license; a mixed or all-robot unit would need an explicit reason why the duelling rule is or is not gendered.

**[E01-6]. Officers recognize and use humor skill; informal humor activities are tolerated or protected.** (AC-01, [p.6])
Bundeswehr superiors value humor as a "character trait and competence" and it is mentioned in official assessments. One interviewee, referring to an earlier era, said soldiers could use paid office hours to organize fun events such as informal rituals or Christmas parties. Another self-declared joker described Kosovo, 2012: his company, back from a mission only three weeks, was ordered to redeploy as NATO reinforced Northern Kosovo amid Serb-Albanian tension around Serbian elections, and morale "was at rock bottom" at the line-up. His comrades bet whether he was about to be punished or promoted when the commander called him in; in fact the commander had noticed that he was "responsible for the company's morale" because he kept them from hanging their heads. The soldier had earlier made that commander laugh by claiming the post himself (that he was in the company "because I am the funniest" and that the government had charged him with morale). Johais comments that the commander then used the soldier's skill for his own ends.
*Candidate application:* A unit's joker may hold an unofficial but recognized role the commander knowingly relies on; the ex-soldier may miss or resent having been that person.

**[E01-7]. A joke on command from the newcomer: humor as an audition under superior eyes.** (AC-01, [p.6-7])
After a night-flight debrief with about twenty soldiers and beer, a soldier who had just returned to military flight training after a period of studies was ordered by his new superior, as "the newcomer who just passed his first night flight," to tell a joke. He called the request forced. He chose a joke whose butt was the Catholic Church and priests' alleged sexual inclinations, and knew it was high-risk because the superior was a strict Catholic. After a moment of silence everyone burst into laughter, and he later joked that his next evaluation turned out very well. Johais links this to Sløk-Andersen's report of a Danish company commander ("Boss") who devoted a week to "a good mood and attitude" with daily best-joke competitions (see E01-26).
*Candidate application:* Humor can be a hazing-adjacent assessment: telling the joke is a test of judgment about the room, and the silence beforehand is the dangerous part.

**[E01-8]. Repartee as an edge in competition between equals.** (AC-01, [p.7-8])
One interviewee described a rival of the same rank, strong on professional competence but poor at handling situations and driven to prove himself better. The interviewee, who was *schlagfertig*, said the rival repeatedly "presented points of attack," and he would throw out a joke in response; he says he exploited this "every now and then." Johais reads it as humor skill giving a comparative advantage where rank and other skills are equal.
*Candidate application:* Where formal rank is flat, wit is the currency for status; a person who cannot be funny and cannot stop trying to be best is a standing target.

**[E01-9]. The courage-test "fun group": escalating paid dares for reputation.** (AC-01, [p.8-9])
Inside one platoon, a voluntary sub-group of about twenty regularly tested one another's courage. Members funded a money pot, and the pot grew with the difficulty. Early tasks (drinking two liters of beer in one go, eating half a kilo of ground pork) paid a few euros; being hit in the testicles with a shovel handle was a mid-level challenge worth about fifty euros; the largest pot ever, 2,500 euros, was offered for hoisting a German flag on a miniature Eiffel Tower at the entrance of a French unit's camp. The interviewee said some tasks were disgusting but he was not ashamed: the recognition felt like being crowned, "You became a legend." Johais' point is that the real prize was a reputation as fearless and tough, not the euros. The group members also exposed themselves in embarrassing postures, took part in officially forbidden acts, and were bound by secrecy (see E01-15).
*Candidate application:* Dares are a currency system in which pain and disgust convert to status; an ex-soldier may find that nothing since has paid out recognition so cleanly. Secrecy is part of the bond.

**[E01-10]. The joke battle with the French in a shared camp.** (AC-01, [p.9])
During a mission abroad where Germans lived in a camp under French command, German soldiers read several signals as French animosity: a single croissant at breakfast while other nations took as many as they wished, clothes washed too hot or vanishing in the laundry, and unmistakable relics such as a Hitler salute or two fingers held under the nose as a Hitler beard. The Germans struck back with humor: they invented sayings and modified common jokes against the French, translated them into French or English, and wrote them at places everyone frequented (toilets, watchtowers, the dining hall). The French answered with jokes translated into German, which spurred the Germans further: "We got really creative because we had the incentive to top what we read." Johais describes it as a "veritable joke battle" echoing old antagonisms. (A live-fetch blog by Johais, E01-65, reports how this camp conflict later escalated; the two accounts probably describe the same events.)
*Candidate application:* Humor is a way to answer a grievance that cannot be answered through the chain of command; written graffiti in shared spaces is a documented medium. The escalation loop (each side tops the other) is the mechanism worth watching.

**[E01-11]. The Afghan barber dare.** (AC-01, [p.9, 12])
While a unit he commanded crossed a market in an Afghan city (a few bullet-riddled containers used as shops), a comrade jokingly proposed that the commander get his beard cut by a barber there. The commander found being shaved by a scowling man with a razor, with comrades standing behind with weapons, "a borderline experience" that was a test of courage; "Everyone thought it was very funny – except for me. But I played along." In retrospect he gave two reasons: he is always triggered by "I bet you don't dare," and, on reflection, doing it was "a mega sign of confidence in them." Johais reads the episode as courage plus loyalty: he showed he would trust his comrades with his life.
*Candidate application:* A prank that stages danger is also a trust demonstration; refusing it could read as distrust of the group. A character may remember it as both the funniest and the most frightening hour of a deployment.

**[E01-12]. "Being able to take it": status in the military comes from compliance as much as from aggression.** (AC-01, [p.8-10])
Johais cites figures that only about a quarter of German soldiers in a high-risk ISAF phase were actively in battles and about half experienced enemy fire, whereas every soldier must submit to rank, discipline and state authority; status therefore comes from being "compliant, skilled, and pro-actively obedient" (Crane-Seeber, quoted) and recruits are turned into proud soldiers who take pleasure in surrender to the state. *Schlagfertigkeit* embodies both capacities, and a soldier summed up the reciprocity as the Latin *do ut des*: you must be able to bear yourself what you do to others.
*Candidate application:* The humor of a soldier-type character should show him or her both dishing out and absorbing. A person who left on principle may have lost the taking half of the exchange without realizing the group's cohesion rested on it.

**[E01-13]. The instructor's gift: a grotesque sculpture and the test of his reaction.** (AC-01, [p.10])
Giving instructors presents at the end of basic training is a common custom. One recruit group handcrafted a grotesque sculpture of their notoriously hard instructor, labeled with his name and rank badge, showing him in a sexualized pose of beating a soldier with a whip. The interviewee judged the instructor's reaction decisive: he "could live with being made an idiot" and had the "taker qualities to bear the payback." Johais reads the instructor as showing both the sadistic and the masochistic "pleasures" of the proper soldier. (The same sculpture appears from the commander's side in AC-40; see E01-55.)
*Candidate application:* Revenge-humor from below is conventionally allowed once, at the end of training, and the ruling person's grace under it is what earns later respect.

**[E01-14]. Nicknames: attack on a quirk, accepted as recognition.** (AC-01, [p.10-11], note 2 [p.16])
Nicknames highlight an individual characteristic (appearance, habit, performance) and nominally leave the bearer free to accept, challenge or ignore them, but soldiers rarely resist or merely tolerate; they identify with the assigned role. Documented cases: a young-looking comrade nicknamed "Schnulli" (a belittling form of the German word for soother) who then ordered and wore a patch with it; a soldier called "Mr. Disinfection" for over-using hand disinfectant on his first Afghan deployment, who performed the role on cue when the others were waiting for it; a man who first refused an abbreviation of his first name because it matched a pop singer ("Dude, I can't stand that"), yet has been called nothing else since and uses an icon of it as a signature. Johais often sensed regret from soldiers who had no nickname. The Mr. Disinfection interviewee explained that outsiders see a gray mass, but soldiers notice the smallest quirks (praying before patrol, checking a rifle fifteen times, going to the toilet twenty times) and name them: "We recognize you that well." So the giver uses intimate knowledge to attack, and the receiver shows willingness to bear a quirk put on show. Separately, tactical nicknames exist: British soldiers used them in place of ranks so the enemy could not identify superiors; Germans used them off-camp or before locals for anonymity.
*Candidate application:* A nickname is evidence of being known; a character may still answer to an unflattering one and treat its loss, after leaving, as a loss of being seen.

**[E01-15]. Comradeship through shared embarrassment, complicity and secrecy.** (AC-01, [p.12])
The fun group's challenges strengthened the "band of brothers" by mutual exposure in embarrassing postures, joint transgression of official norms, and a pledge of secrecy imposed by the illegality and shame involved. Johais ties cohesion to "fellow suffering" in formal drill plus informal ritual and personal intimacy (King 2006).
*Candidate application:* Shared shame is a bonding technology; a former soldier may be unable to describe his best friendships to a civilian without describing things that sound unforgivable out of context.

**[E01-16]. Women and repartee: the counterstrike, the reversal, and "say it loud."** (AC-01, [p.12-13])
Female soldiers across interviews felt that "it was never good enough, long enough, strong enough." Documented exchanges: a woman nicknamed "Püppi" (from "puppet," for her small size) answered a male comrade's sexual teasing (during a conversation about close combat) by immediately putting the joke into practice, and he found himself on the floor; he recounted it approvingly. A female soldier said comrades test constantly, for example asking whether she has her period, and the answer must be *schlagfertig*: she turned the question back at the man, and added that you must say it loudly so the others start hooting. She learned from her career start that repartee passes male comrades' tests. Johais reads every reputation-building retort as a "certificate," an investment in acceptance.
*Candidate application:* For a minority or "outsider" soldier, the public, loud, reversible retort is a survival and promotion skill; the audience is part of the move.

**[E01-17]. Turning an invasion of the sergeant's authority back on the provoker.** (AC-01, [p.13])
As a sergeant on a night inspection round in a basic-training company, a female soldier met a recruit sleeping naked with his bare backside showing from the blanket. She ordered everyone up, said she did not care how he slept, and, since he chose to sleep naked, he could fetch a bucket of water and clean. He did not refuse, "made himself a laughing stock," and the story spread. She judged he had done it deliberately to provoke a female sergeant, and that she signaled to the group by staying calm: no screaming, no "ugh."
*Candidate application:* Provocation can be defused by making the provoker carry the consequence in front of his peers; calm and a duty-order are the weapon.

**[E01-18]. The extreme-bet end of the continuum, and why the paper treats it as ambiguous.** (AC-01, [p.14-15])
The fun group's only woman, drunk, was offered 150 euros to drink tea made with her used tampon, and did it, becoming a "legend" and, by the male interviewee's account, laughing about it herself; it was her first and last challenge. The same group's bets for men included sexual-fluid challenges paid 100 euros each. The interviewee said what stayed with the group was not the extremeness but that she broke the expectation that primitiveness comes only from men. Johais argues the act was both emancipatory (refusing shame about the female body in a military that deems it unfit) and submissive (accepting the group's rules), cautions that "We do not know how the woman felt," and notes she spread the story herself to maximize recognition. The text also records that officially prohibited collective practices in the group included joint masturbation, treated as normal by the interviewee. (Described here only as documented evidence of how far bet-and-recognition dynamics can go.)
*Candidate application:* The research shows the limit case of recognition won through transgression, where consent, humiliation and status cannot be cleanly separated; a character could carry this ambivalence about something he or she did or watched.

**[E01-19]. Johais' conclusion: humor bridges contradictions inside the soldier model.** (AC-01, [p.15-16])
The soldier is required to be dominant (warrior) and submissive (disciplined subject), masculine and tidy. Johais invokes incongruity theory but turns it: humor here does not close the gap between "what is expected and what is happening" but bridges an in-built contradiction between soldierly virtues. Female soldiers appropriate the skill deliberately and male comrades are first surprised, then appreciate it.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's jokes may cluster at the exact points where the institution asked him or her to be two incompatible things at once.

### From AC-27 (Sløk-Andersen 2019, The butt of the joke?)

**[E01-20]. Context: a four-month conscript platoon, a self-image of informality.** (AC-27, [p.3-5, notes])
About 4,200 conscripts a year serve in a 170-year-old Danish system; only men can be drafted and women may volunteer; about 17 percent of this group were women. Most serve four months, are not deployed and not in "real" combat. Danish male soldiers interviewed distance themselves from supposedly hyper-masculine militaries (the U.S. given as the example), claiming better critical thinking, a more informal tone and more respect for women. Sløk-Andersen lived as a participant: same drills, uniform, a mixed dorm.
*Candidate application:* Caution for transfer: this is garrison humor in a short-service peacetime force, strong evidence for peer policing and weak evidence for combat humor.

**[E01-21]. The opening scenario: a joke that polices closeness.** (AC-27, [p.2])
During a halt on a two-and-a-half-hour march with 25 kg packs, three men took their trousers off to cool their legs, standing in underwear in the woods. Two other conscripts (Juul and Karlsen) lay together 15-20 meters away, one with an arm around the other, smiling. One of the men with trousers down loudly said it looked like *Brokeback Mountain*; others joined in, several sneaked over and threw themselves on top of the two in a heap while laughing. The two looked surprised and exhausted under the weight and did not resist. Sløk-Andersen's point: baring one's legs before the platoon was fine; tenderness between men was not, and the joke, not an order, enforced it.
*Candidate application:* The joke as a "pile-on" shows that policing humor can be physical and playful at once; the targets' silence is the rule that keeps it going.

**[E01-22]. Sexual idiom as the default register, from commander down.** (AC-27, [p.8-10])
The platoon commander (Lt. Petersen) repeatedly joked that this "is not a summer camp," that the men might play flute but "we don't touch each other's instruments" (field notes, week 17); after a three-day woods drill he said they were looking forward to showers, sleeping indoors and a euphemistic masturbation phrase ("beat the bishop a bit"). Orders themselves were seasoned with sex: the kneeling-position instruction ("take a knee") was corrected, when done wrongly, by an exclamation naming a sex act; being close behind the person ahead was described by a crude sexual phrase; praise used erection or ejaculation analogies; male conscripts' laughter-provoking topics included who was short enough to perform a sex act standing and which of two body types one would rather have sex with. Nobody complained to the researcher. The only objection was from a female conscript, Olsen, normally adaptable, who found it "gross" when a sergeant asked the group to step closer by using a female-anatomy phrase; she said nothing in the moment. Sløk-Andersen notes that almost every sexual reference assumed a sexually active male body, so a reference to female anatomy stood out and exposed the pattern.
*Candidate application:* Institutional humor can be a shared vocabulary that makes objection feel like a category error; the person who feels the wrongness first is often the one the idiom was never written for.

**[E01-23]. The Hald interrogation: potency enforced by joking questions.** (AC-27, [p.10-11])
On drill, break-time talk turned anything (stumps, holes, moisture) into sexual double meaning, each one drawing laughter. Rumor held that conscript Hald had hooked up with a female conscript, and several men interrogated him at length, asking among other things whether he "couldn't get a boner." He tried several times to end it diplomatically; one of the woman's best friends cheered it on. Afterward he told Sløk-Andersen that they had only kissed and slept side by side; he stayed silent out of consideration for her, and that silence kept him inside the image of an active heterosexual man. Sløk-Andersen's reading: the humor policed both unwanted closeness and the required display of heterosexual potency.
*Candidate application:* An unanswered question is an answer in a unit; a character may hold back truth to protect a third person and pay for it with mockery.

**[E01-24]. Sexual joking as a substitute display where sex is forbidden.** (AC-27, [p.11])
At camp, couples kissing and sleeping together were visible, but kissing and watching pornography were forbidden on duty. Sløk-Andersen suggests the sexualized humor allowed soldiers to show potency in settings where it could not be practiced.
*Candidate application:* Where an act is banned, a joke about it can do the job of the act; look for humor clustered around whatever the institution restricts.

**[E01-25]. Two kinds of "potency": sexual and leadership.** (AC-27, [p.11-13])
Using the two dictionary senses of "potent" (powerful and influential; sexually able), Sløk-Andersen argues good soldiers had to perform both. Karlsen (the man under the pile) was by his friend Juul's account a tough, excellent soldier who deserved Soldier of the Month but who held back and "let others take control"; the platoon commander, an admitted introvert who acts extroverted ("you have to"), said that if you are invisible "it's very easy to score a low grade," since conscript grades determine career chances. Fischer was named Soldier of the Month for active participation, proactivity and taking responsibility, and was also open about his sexual prowess. Karlsen's perceived considerateness and sensitivity were read as non-heterosexual; Sløk-Andersen met no openly gay soldiers in the fieldwork and ascribes it partly to this humorous policing.
*Candidate application:* A soldier culture can read quiet competence as failure and read tenderness as a security risk; a defector who was valued for something other than loudness may have been misread by his own unit.

**[E01-26]. Institutional license: "good mood" week and the "certain jargon."** (AC-27, [p.7, 13])
The company commander ("Boss") once declared a week focused on creating "a good mood and attitude," producing daily best-joke competitions, many about male sexuality; even sergeants who were supposed to discipline the conscripts lightened the atmosphere by joking or urging others to. A camp advisor, presenting on the working environment and sexual harassment, said a naughty joke is not prohibited but to be considerate, and that "We have a certain jargon here." Sløk-Andersen's claim: with humor so central, it became almost inappropriate to show offense at sexual jokes, because "it was just a joke."
*Candidate application:* When leadership both sponsors and polices humor, the license line is vague on purpose, and the person who objects carries the cost of knowing where it was.

**[E01-27]. Attunement and the "killjoy": the theory.** (AC-27, [p.3, 13-14, 16, 19-20])
Using Sara Ahmed, Sløk-Andersen treats a humorous mood as an attunement: it enables being with others, but "being with in a similar way," so a person who is affectively "out of tune" threatens their own recognition as a subject. A "killjoy" is someone who ruins the atmosphere by speaking up or will not laugh at the right points. Sløk-Andersen's argument is that fear of ruining the good mood kept both men and women from objecting; in her words "perhaps it was more fun for some than others." She stresses that she is deliberately not drawing a funny-versus-offensive line, because soldiers and researchers cannot reliably do so, and that previous research tends to assume stable joker, butt and audience roles whereas she treats recognition as never settled.
*Candidate application:* The cost of a refusal to laugh is social, not formal; a character who snapped, or stopped laughing, once may be remembered by the group for exactly that.

**[E01-28]. "What do you call Peter in the military? An extra!": the butt as a measure of failing.** (AC-27, [p.14])
Conscripts who could not take part in drills (illness, injury) were used as "extras" to play enemies, casualties or civilians. A sergeant's joke, "What do you call Peter in the military? An extra!", was repeated in the line-up and for weeks after. Peter, 19, who had an old injury and had been an extra several times, laughed along but privately was annoyed to miss "some of the most fun and cool things in the military." Sløk-Andersen's reading: the laughter made visible that a good soldier is able-bodied. Everyone was the butt once in a while, "the price to pay," but being it too often (as Peter was) marked you as outside; laughter "seemed to stick to some more than others."
*Candidate application:* Humor can be a mechanism of exclusion that the excluded person participates in; a wounded character's laughter at injury jokes can sit on top of real grief about being left out.

**[E01-29]. Sergeant Bolt's dance: humor as brief inversion of hierarchy, then restoration.** (AC-27, [p.14-15])
During an inspection in the woods the strict second-in-command started moving his hips and singing the chorus of Rihanna's "Work," then cursed and said something crude about wanting to have sex with her. The platoon laughed because of the break from his normal strictness. Sløk-Andersen sees humor opening room to play with expected performances, but order returned when he asserted his dominant heterosexual position.
*Candidate application:* Authority figures may use humor to be briefly human; the safe way back is to reassert the norm, which is also the cost.

**[E01-30]. The genital punch as a test of whether you can "take it."** (AC-27, [p.16])
Male conscripts were sometimes punched in the genitals; the incidents functioned as a test of whether the receiver could "take it" as the butt of a physical joke, while amusing bystanders. Karlsen, punched and curled up in pain almost unable to move, did not oppose, scream or report, though the regulations allowed reporting. Sløk-Andersen's inference is that he did not want to spoil "the good mood" and performed potency through the ability to "take it" physically and affectively.
*Candidate application:* The unreported injury, absorbed so as to stay in the group, is a candidate origin for a chronic-pain ethos: wincing is outside the game.

**[E01-31]. The harassment meeting: when the humor frame makes complaint feel like a betrayal.** (AC-27, [p.16-17])
At a second camp, five female conscripts were asked to expand on accounts told to a sergeant. They did not use the term "sexual harassment" themselves (the company commander did). They described daily comments of a sexual-availability kind; "last straw" incidents involved male soldiers in underwear, being pulled into a dorm room, and being surprised in the shower, which male peers treated as playful. Spiteful comments had already come from men after rumors of reporting. When the commander suggested the next day's farewell party might be canceled, one woman protested that she would then report nothing, because causing the cancellation would make them killjoys. Sløk-Andersen reads this through Ahmed's notion of "snap" as the point when someone tires of being the butt, and notes the women knew they had breached the humorous attunement.
*Candidate application:* The last-straw moment is a documented pattern: a long period of absorbed humor followed by a sudden break; the group's response to the break is social pressure about the group's pleasure.

**[E01-32]. The commander's wrong diagnosis, the "overly potent" offender, and the male double bind.** (AC-27, [p.17])
The commander assumed the trouble came from newly mixed dorm rooms; the women said the men they shared rooms with were the least problem and that men from other platoons caused it. A few days later the commander told Sløk-Andersen one male soldier had initiated most incidents; that soldier was a candidate for Best Soldier and expected to continue into a military career, and the commander doubted he would now be honored or made a sergeant: in Sløk-Andersen's phrase, he had been "overly potent" toward his female colleagues. The commander said he would take a complaint from a man just as seriously, but men like Karlsen found it as hard to complain, perhaps because men are assumed potent and not in need of protection (an internal harassment survey surveyed only women).
*Candidate application:* The institution may reward the same traits in public that it penalizes in private; a person who used to be that rising star may know what that cost.

**[E01-33]. The researcher inside the humor: "taking it" as an embodied choice.** (AC-27, [p.17-19])
At 6:05 a.m. breakfast a man asked Sløk-Andersen, in sexual slang, whether they had had crude sexual success that weekend, casting the researcher in a penetrative role that differed from the one assigned other women. In a maintenance room, while weapons were polished, Torsten (locking eyes) mimed a sex act with the lock of his rifle; Sløk-Andersen replied he could do that all day without affecting them; he stopped and returned to polishing. Nearby, a soldier who had finally unlocked a comrade's strap gloated with a homophobic slur at those who had failed. Sløk-Andersen did not call anyone out, choosing not to be a killjoy and to show she could "take it" while neither joining in nor objecting, and describes it as an embodied experience of pride and will followed by awareness of being a gendered body in an all-male room.
*Candidate application:* The cool, flat, unamused answer was one of the few documented ways to refuse without being a killjoy; its effect was to end the performance, not to start a fight.

**[E01-34]. Conclusion points: the carnivalesque safe zone and who humor is fun for.** (AC-27, [p.19-21])
Sløk-Andersen reports that jokes about sex between men were plentiful and did not threaten male heterosexual potency "as long as this stayed in the carnivalesque sphere of humor." The humor made service more pleasant for most, bypassed the grave backdrop of military work (echoing Carol Cohn's 1987 finding that talk about nuclear weapons is "fun"), and told conscripts and superiors who was "in tune" and so who had "what it takes" for a career. Women were rarely setters of the tune.
*Candidate application:* A group can make same-sex material safe by placing it inside a joking frame while punishing the same material outside it; frame, not content, is the rule.

**[E01-35]. The prior Scandinavian and military literature the paper maps.** (AC-27, [p.6-7])
Sløk-Andersen lists documented functions of military humor in earlier ethnography: discipline, excluding what disrupts efficiency and cohesion, protecting honor within hierarchy, building bonds, expressing critique or complaint without disrupting cohesion (Ben-Ari and Sion on Israeli combat reservists; Engman on the Swedish navy; Godfrey; Bjerke and Rones). Engman's Swedish-navy work stresses the "carnivalesque" chance to challenge the order. Bjerke and Rones (Norwegian conscripts) describe humor as a way to handle frustration and disagreement "without being someone who causes problems" and as a fine line between funny and offensive. Lyman's fraternity study shows jokes can fail.
*Candidate application:* Useful reading pointers for further extractions (see Gaps); no new evidence here.

**[E01-36]. Gilman's response (U.S. folklorist): the same joke frame, different stakes.** (AC-27, responses, [p.27-29])
Gilman argues that misogynist and homophobic humor pervade both the Danish and U.S. militaries and extend to harassment and violence; the joke frame trivializes whatever message it carries, making humor a hegemonic mechanism of socialization and gender policing. Differences Gilman names: Danish women are volunteers (so may feel greater pressure to accept sexist joking to belong) while in the U.S. everyone volunteers (so objecting to something one chose is hard); Danish service lasts four months, but U.S. terms are multi-year and most have deployed to war in recent decades, so belonging and being "warrior-worthy" cost more. Folklorist context: citing Dundes and Pagter, the aim of much sexual humor between heterosexual men is to humiliate an opponent by feminizing him, and the butt is symbolically placed in a sexual position while the power rests with the joker. Gilman describes the U.S. "gay chicken" game (drawing on Wallis and Mechling) (two men move toward a kiss; the one who does not pull away shows confidence in his heterosexuality) and says whether a physical line is crossed depends on who participates, the context and how everyone responds; a man with proven occupational and social potency usually gets only laughter, while one whose manliness is already in doubt may see joking touch feed his devaluation and harassment. Gilman adds that in the U.S. interviews (the "Don't Ask, Don't Tell" era) a good soldier who was gay could be accepted more readily than a heterosexual judged inadequate, who would also have his heterosexuality questioned. A Marine veteran interviewed in 2012 told Gilman that everyone had been through hell, that being a wreck about wife and kids at home did not stop anyone handling business overseas, and that the unit worked through such problems while they "ribbed each other over it as usual"; it was never looked down on as feminine, the veteran said, apart from the jokes. Gilman stresses that low-ranking men who joke sexually hold little real power, must be roughly masculine while living through fear, loneliness and neediness, and mostly feel comfortable sharing pain only inside their band of brothers, mitigating vulnerability through humor. (Layout is garbled in the text copy; Gilman's exact sentences are not reliable.)
*Candidate application:* Ribbing about emotional struggle is a documented way for U.S. male service members to let a peer talk about pain without naming it as pain; the joke is the permission slip. *Cross-use:* applicable to any all-male or male-skewed unit culture with a "no feelings" rule.

**[E01-37]. Plester's response (New Zealand workplace-humor scholar): the dark side.** (AC-27, responses, [p.30-31])
Plester praises the data but argues the article assumes "soldiering" is masculine, does not fully acknowledge the "dark side" of humor, and could treat the sexualized joking as sexual harassment, domination or "injurious language" (Butler), or alternatively through Freud's release theory (sex and aggression are expressed as "just a joke" or banter because they cannot be released openly). From Plester's own earlier tech-company study comes the report of women performing masculinity to avoid being seen as a spoilsport, and Plester notes that sexual jokes serve as "currency" in male bonding. Plester suggests Nick Butler's idea of laughter as a "corrective" would sharpen the paper's "policing" concept.
*Candidate application:* Two readings of the same joke (bonding currency; domination) coexist in the scholarship; a character can legitimately remember it both ways.

### From AC-17 (Tidy 2021, The part humor plays in the production of military violence)

**[E01-38]. Evidence base and the ubiquity claim.** (AC-17, [p.2-3, 6])
Tidy reviewed the official MOD obituaries: 456 deaths in Afghanistan (2001-2015), 179 in Iraq (2003-2009), most with a public obituary (the text says "all 456 obituaries were read" and elsewhere "more than 450"; see Gaps). Tidy found it difficult to find one that does not remark on and commend a dead soldier's sharp wit and strong banter, with comrades contributing funny stories or dry observations and recalling the dead person's role in unit cohesion and the mission through morale-boosting banter. Tidy anticipates that anyone who has been in the British military would not find this surprising (footnote 1) and suggests memoirs, online forums and observation could also be mined. The names are omitted to avoid distress to families; rank and cap badge are kept as indicators of class.
*Candidate application:* An institution's own public language about its dead may be saturated with the funny-person trope; a defector may have a visceral reaction to it as a genre.

**[E01-39]. Banter is the single theme that crosses rank, branch and cap badge.** (AC-17, [p.3-4])
Three exemplar extracts: (1) a Lance Corporal in 3 Royal Welsh fitted into the company seamlessly because of a keen humor and "banter which only soldiers seem to understand," with advice offered regardless of rank and "cutting sarcasm"; (2) a Sapper in 32 Engineer Regiment known for one-liners followed by a trademark laugh, always in troop banter, who is remembered "getting a lot of stick" for his volleyball skills or lack of them; (3) a female Captain in a Royal Logistics Corps explosive-ordnance-disposal squadron who "could put up with" the search team's humor and banter and enjoyed a joke with the "lads."
*Candidate application:* The recurrent descriptors (dry, cutting, one-liners, stick for a weak skill) give a vocabulary of how British service humor is named by those who like it.

**[E01-40]. Humor is a gender practice: who gets to be active, and who is the audience.** (AC-17, [p.7-10])
Using Connell's gender theory, Tidy argues humor is one of the ways gender relations are "achieved." Being active in humor (funny, joking, banter) is coded manly; women are cast as appreciative audience; funny women are treated as transgressive. Banter is described as characterized by boundary testing and teasing, sexual bravado and in-jokes among men, a verbal horseplay that tests and rehearses belonging to and positioning within masculinity and groups of men; women are often on its receiving end ("locker room banter" defenses), though women also banter as a signal that they fit "with the lads." Banter produces and strengthens boundaries between group and outsiders. Tidy asks who actually experiences the moment as fun: to enjoy banter you must be seen to be doing it right and be able to reciprocate, "to give as good as you get," and that is only possible within particular configurations of power.
*Candidate application:* The ability to be amused is itself a status marker; a defector who no longer finds the group's jokes funny has also lost a claim to membership.

**[E01-41]. Humor as the definition of a good soldier; the "honorary man."** (AC-17, [p.11-12])
In obituaries, soldiers are praised as typical or ideal: a Sapper from 28 Engineer Regiment was "the epitome of a true Sapper," who got on with any task "with great humor"; a Royal Regiment of Scotland Corporal was "the perfect man," described by his commanding officer as "robust, committed and blessed with a fine line in banter." The female Captain's ability to "put up with" and return banter marked her as fitting, one of the "lads," which Tidy links to King's "honorary man."
*Candidate application:* In a banter culture, a sense of humor is part of the job description; a character may be afraid to lose his or her dry wit because it was the proof of belonging.

**[E01-42]. The REME Craftsman: banter that tests officers, and the right to "get away with it."** (AC-17, [p.13-14])
An obituary for a Royal Electrical and Mechanical Engineers Craftsman, "a morale asset for the Squadron," records that his wit "kept his fellow soldiers going under very difficult and demanding conditions." His specific banter was boundary testing and one-upmanship toward officers plus in-jokes binding him to men of his rank. His Sergeant Major recalled that at Squadron parades he stood bolt upright in the sun "with a cheeky grin, deliberately avoiding my stare." He took particular pride in recovering the Officer Commanding after that officer rolled his vehicle in treacherous weather, then "seized the opportunity to get one-up on his 'boss'" with tales and quips that earned "great kudos" from the other blokes; the obituary says he was "one of the few who could get away with it." Tidy reads this as a class-marked, working-class form of soldierly masculinity that tests authority and builds emotional connection useful to violence.
*Candidate application:* Documented license: a person may be allowed to mock the officer who has just been embarrassed, if trusted and proven; the margin of "getting away with it" is narrow and individual.

**[E01-43]. Banter and hazing share a logic; the Private whose banter was not benign.** (AC-17, [p.14-15])
Tidy, drawing on Higate's study of private security companies, says hazing can be practiced through "humor and irony" with laughter and "willing and active participation" by all, affirming closeness; banter and initiation hazing share the logic of ritualized abuse, humiliation, boundary-making and closeness, and being "able to take" either binds a person into the group. An approving obituary of a Lance Corporal noted that when it was his turn to receive banter "he took it with his customary smile." The counter-case: an obituary of a Rifles Private noted that he was often the "center of some laughs and jokes" over his "personal admin" (care of body and uniform). It did not record that he took his own life while deployed to Iraq, leaving a note of grievances against several people including his Platoon Sergeant; at the inquest the question of bullying was raised and witnesses denied it. Tidy concludes banter and hazing are linked processes that are coded differently: banter benign, hazing a scandalous excess that makes visible the violent gendered relations running through banter.
*Candidate application:* The documented failure mode: a man chronically at the center of the laughter for a reason he cannot fix, whom the unit and the institution later describe as having been ordinary banter. The case may be handled with extreme care in any depiction.

**[E01-44]. Humor structuring violence against prisoners and civilians.** (AC-17, [p.15-17])
Tidy argues humor does not only bind a group but can shape violence itself, and lists documented cases: (1) Abu Ghraib: the smiling, jokey faces of U.S. torturers posing with the dead; Rush Limbaugh defended them as "people having a good time," like a fraternity prank, which Tidy accepts as true but only as a good time for some people at others' expense, a one-sided pleasure that binds the perpetrators and exposes victims. (2) The 2011 killing of an Afghan insurgent by the British Marine known as "Marine A," accompanied by a quip adapting Hamlet's "shuffle off this mortal coil" plus an insult. (3) A 2004 video of British soldiers beating Iraqi boys in Al Amara in which the filmer laughs and tells them, in mock-scolding words, they will "get it" as "naughty little boys"; the video was reportedly shown for entertainment at a British base in Europe before a whistle-blower leaked it. (4) At Camp Breadbasket in Basra, a prisoner described another being tied to the forks of a forklift and moved up and down while two soldiers in a watchtower laughed loudly; sentencing, the judge said the soldier had "used the prisoner to amuse himself."
*Candidate application:* The dark end of the spectrum: laughter during violence against the powerless is documented, and a soldier-type character who left the service on principle may have a specific memory of a joke that was the moment he or she saw the line. Handle with the same seriousness the source does.

**[E01-45]. The public narrative: obituaries tell a three-part story that makes the war look like a fun job.** (AC-17, [p.17-21])
Tidy argues the obituaries' humor does outward-facing work. Structure: (1) the dead person was an excellent soldier and good man, with humor and morale-boosting: a Gloucestershire-regiment Lance Corporal "so cheerful and cheeky he could be forgiven anything"; a Royal Marine Lance Corporal respected "largely because of his dry sense of humor and ability to see the lighter side of life," relied on to lift morale "regardless of the situation"; a recce soldier who "knew how to make the best of things." (2) He or she was well liked in a tight group sharing fun: a Royal Marine who "loved to play the joker," quick to prank but first to laugh at a joke on himself; a Staffordshire Regiment private who got wine on a battlefield tour in Belgium when not allowed to drink, was caught and took his punishment "with a smile on his face"; a Corporal known for notorious Ibiza clubbing outfits. The reader is "let in on the joke." (3) They died doing the job they loved. Tidy reads the effect as reassurance: the dead were "ordinary lads having a laugh," violence is pushed to the background, and humor makes soldiers relatable and "just like us," making war and its deaths palatable.
*Candidate application:* There is a genre of institutional, civilian-facing funny-soldier story that is a sanitized, reassuring product; a defector's own humor may be partly a refusal of that genre.

**[E01-46]. What the obituary jokes are about: discomfort, not trauma.** (AC-17, [p.19-20])
Tidy observes that although "raising morale" might hint at gallows humor, the specific examples concern physical discomfort: one Corporal, when everyone was wet and miserable after a desert thunderstorm, stripped and plunged into a huge muddy puddle to show being filthy was no bother and make comrades smile. The obituaries' humor examples include pranks, drinking, outfits, embarrassing stories and wet-weather antics, rarely death, injury or the enemy.
*Candidate application:* The public record of soldiers' humor leaves out its darkest register by construction; a character can contrast what was said on the base with what is said about the dead.

**[E01-47]. Tidy's caution: do not assume humor resists power; race and class qualifiers.** (AC-17, [p.2, 5, 13, 21-22])
The article's thesis is that scholars have mainly stressed humor as rupture and resistance but humor can equally sustain and enable oppressive institutions and violence. Tidy cites Basham's finding that joking in the predominantly white British military takes "particularly racial forms," and states in a note that raced and classed patterns of fit and humor in the military would be a fertile area for study. Tidy also lists the functions other scholars find (Godfrey: disciplinary technology; Brown and Penttinen: everyday resilience and resistance; Nilsson: unit cohesion).
*Candidate application:* For writing a soldier-type character, resist the default that the soldier's sarcasm is anti-authority; much of it, per Tidy, upholds the unit's and the institution's purposes.

### From AC-40 (Johais 2024, The WARFUN taboo)

**[E01-48]. The taboo and its origin story: a research permit refused with "War is no fun."** (AC-40, opening section)
The paper's project is titled WARFUN. The Bundeswehr rejected Johais' application for a research permit, the last sentence of a personal note reading "War is no fun," and the official stressed how inappropriate the wordplay was given the war in Ukraine. Johais defines a taboo as an especially forceful social norm whose regulative effect is to prevent something being done, said or even thought, with constitutive effects that include unintended consequences. The "WARFUN taboo" rules out associating war with fun; Johais argues it is particularly entrenched in Germany but "far from universal," since many states glorify war as glorious, pleasurable and adventurous.
*Candidate application:* Whether a society allows its soldiers to be seen enjoying themselves is a culture-level variable, and it shapes whether a soldier's humor is concealed or displayed.

**[E01-49]. Why the German case: anti-militarist political culture, in brief.** (AC-40, "political culture" section)
Key points: the post-1945 founding narrative negated the Nazi past through "Never again war" and "Never again Auschwitz"; the Bundeswehr (founded 1955) was embedded in NATO; after 1990 the second maxim was reinterpreted as a duty to intervene (Srebrenica 1995, Kosovo 1999); the twenty-year Afghan operation (2001-2021) changed the scale, and the "Good Friday Battle" of 2 April 2010 was the first extended combat by German soldiers since World War II; yet when the Defense Minister described a "war-like" situation, even that tentative wording drew parliamentary criticism. Chancellor Scholz's 27 February 2022 speech (budget increase, weapons to Ukraine) publicly broke a related taboo to cheering applause, though public opinion stays divided. Johais' summary: the taboo has "curtailed war semantics" without preventing the use of force.
*Candidate application:* Background only; the useful lesson is that a taboo can coexist with fighting and shapes language and self-image, not action.

**[E01-50]. The official soldier model leaves no room for a distinct humor.** (AC-40, "soldier models" section)
The "citizen in uniform," adopted as the official model in the Bundeswehr's inner-leadership philosophy in 2008, is a member of a democratic society stripped of distinctive soldierly or martial attributes, and implies soldiers do not cultivate an internal military culture, "including a special sense of humor," that runs against society's mindset. Johais also stresses that the absence of fun in soldier models is not only German or only due to the taboo: soldier models are meant to mark soldiers off from civilians as virtuous, and militaries have tactical reasons to keep images of soldiers relaxing and enjoying themselves from the public, to preserve a tough, professional image. The historical parallel: Madigan found humor "a central, almost defining component" of British WWI troop culture while civilian commentary portrayed a heroic "willingness to die"; soldiers identified instead with a tragicomic hero whose courage was a resilient willingness to take whatever the war dished out.
*Candidate application:* A civilian-facing soldier image and a soldier's own self-image can be diametrically opposed on exactly the point of humor; this is the gap a returning soldier falls into.

**[E01-51]. How the public frames soldiers, and how the Bundeswehr portrayed itself.** (AC-40, "soldier models" section)
Public frames: soldiers dismissed "as murderers" (earlier, more one-sided), then victims suffering death, injury and harsh conditions (media in Afghanistan), or "armed social workers"/development aides. The Bundeswehr's own Facebook coverage of Afghanistan avoided irritating an anti-militarist public by three absences (no enemy fighters, no soldiers' emotions, no physical or mental harm) presenting an "unemotional, rational professional." After the ISAF mission ended in 2014, attention moved to returned veterans, framed as "marginal men" with mental-health problems who fail to reintegrate, a pathologizing that lets society avoid its relation to violence.
*Candidate application:* The civilians' available story of the ex-soldier (damaged, or a murderer, or a social worker) leaves no space for a humorous, functioning, still-carrying person; that gap is where concealed humor lives.

**[E01-52]. Interviewees' reactions: irritated, then relieved, then "closed circle."** (AC-40, "soldiers' reactions" section, fn 66-69)
Some interviewees were skeptical or irritated at the topic; one said "war" and "fun" were for them two mutually exclusive terms. When asked specifically about humor, though, they were often pleasantly surprised; one thanked Johais for being the first person to raise it, saying the relation of soldiers and humor "is usually ruled out per se," and added: "Soldiers have a very specific humor which is hardly comprehensible for non-soldiers." Another said outsiders used to know soldier jokes and jargon when civil-military ties were closer, but soldier humor is now confined to a "closed humor circle," within which humor culture is vibrant.
*Candidate application:* Being asked, sincerely, about the humor can itself be a relief; a character who has never been asked may answer with more than expected.

**[E01-53]. The dilemma: "Shooting by itself is fun," but not on people.** (AC-40, "soldiers' reactions" section, fn 70-71)
One interviewee mimed a machine gun, shaking his arms, to describe a movie image of helicopter soldiers shooting at Vietnamese people in woods, and called it a "perverse form of fun": "that's how you don't want to experience yourself." Yet in the same interview he admitted enjoying shooting and called it self-evident that "Shooting by itself is fun!" Johais infers he distanced himself not from enjoying shooting but from enjoying shooting at, and possibly killing, someone. Johais frames this as cognitive dissonance created by the taboo, and distinguishes humor culture from combat thrill and the adventure of tours.
*Candidate application:* A soldier can hold the memory that something was fun and the judgment that that kind of fun is monstrous at once, and the line between them may be very specific (target, not act).

**[E01-54]. Deployment fun withheld from family: the "two blocs."** (AC-40, "soldiers' reactions" section, fn 72)
One interviewee said it would never have occurred to him to share moments of fun during deployment. He described two blocs: the family that has to cope alone and worries, and himself on the other side doing his job routinely and (whispered) "occasionally having fun," which he felt you cannot admit because home problems and stress would mix up with "he has fun."
*Candidate application:* Concealing the humor is an act of consideration as well as self-protection; the ex-soldier's silence may feel to civilians like coldness and to him like tact.

**[E01-55]. Three civil-military collisions: the memo, the pun, the sculpture.** (AC-40, "soldiers' reactions" section, fn 73-76)
The same interviewee gave three episodes. (1) A comrade sent a change-of-private-address notice phrased like a military order about moving a command post, presumably to be funny; the interviewee could not laugh, felt embarrassed when his wife saw it and judged it a "weirdo" thing, and said that in a civilian context it is inappropriate to carry that language into private life ("citizens in uniform"). (2) In basic training in the late 1980s an instructor's saying punned on *Glied* (a word meaning both a rank in a formation and, as slang, the penis) in a crude sexual image about the sky, joining a military order to fall in line with a sexual joke; he remembered thinking where he had landed, adapted quickly, but did not dare repeat at home what he heard during the week (his mother already worried that he had started drinking beer within two weeks). (3) At the end of basic training, relatives were invited to the swearing-in and shown the barracks; his commander was concerned about the obscene presents displayed in corridors, above all the whip-wielding sculpture of a notoriously hard instructor (the same object as in AC-01; see E01-13). The interviewee argued against removing it: it is coarse but "everyday life," many relatives were soldiers themselves, and "we don't want to deceive them." Johais reads these as two strategies: separate the humor in situations that risk personal relations, or show it where visitors are expected to know the culture.
*Candidate application:* Different people from one unit disagree about how much to show civilians; the ex-soldier's choices (hide, show, translate) can be written as a personal rule born from one or two specific embarrassments.

**[E01-56]. Alienation and concealment of identity.** (AC-40, "soldiers' reactions" and conclusion)
Soldiers described the strongest disconnect as the "murderer frame" ("You are a murderer and we dislike the Bundeswehr anyway"), and said that at times when you "outed yourself" as a soldier "you were lucky not to get a punch" (the public attitude reportedly changed after the war in Ukraine). Two soldiers described a garden party with "remaining hippies" where a companion whispered not to reveal anything, because "We won't get out alive!" A less dramatic reason for silence is expected lack of interest or understanding: one interviewee said soldiers do not do what this interview was doing (talking to outsiders, not even family), because they would not understand, and summed it up as "We stay among us." Johais' conclusion: the taboo's unintended side effect is that soldiers who withhold their humor become further alienated from the society they are meant to fit.
*Candidate application:* For an ex-soldier living among civilians, withholding is a default, with humor surfacing only with trusted insiders; the ex-soldier may be conspicuously funny only with someone who "was there." *Cross-use:* applicable to any faction whose members hide their culture from a hostile or uncomprehending majority.

### From AC-25 (Johais 2023 blog, "No laughter, no war")

**[E01-57]. Framing: how can a "docile" person be funny?** (AC-25, opening)
Johais sets Foucault's disciplinary power (docile bodies produced by surveillance, isolation, exercise and control of time and space) and Goffman's total institution against the observation that humor is integral to military culture, and claims soldiers find reasons for amusement "not despite but because of" military life. Humor "makes people stay."
*Candidate application:* Humor can be a retention mechanism; an institution may tolerate and even rely on it.

**[E01-58]. "Who knows nothing, knows dress code": deniable criticism of superiors.** (AC-25, first saying)
A classic German soldier comment means the superior has no idea what he is talking about. It is aimed at superiors from another branch who, unable to judge weapon handling or movement and yet obliged to give feedback, focus on dress (polished boots, closed pockets, loose straps, trouser seams, shaved faces). Soldiers accept grooming rules but sense when they are abused to cover incompetence; they do not tell the superior but use the line with peers to create "a shared universe of meaning." A 35-year-service interlocutor explained that frank words are not allowed in a hierarchy, so a person seeks channels such as irony, "dresses" statements so everyone understands but could deny having said it; his standard formula to a general is a polite remark that something can be done that way, which he calls "the highest form of disapproval" and which the general understands. The superior saves face and may take the point.
*Candidate application:* The strongest documented form of dissent in the ranks is a polite phrase whose meaning everyone decodes; a character who left on principle may have run out of deniable ways to say it.

**[E01-59]. "It's not over till it's over" as instruction turned running gag.** (AC-25, second saying)
An instructor answered recruits' moaning about a long strenuous exercise with a phrase that, unlike its English equivalent, meant not hope but that duty ends when the superior says so. He also counted push-ups unpredictably (not 1 to 50, but skipping and repeating numbers), replacing civilian common sense with military time. Johais notes some superiors take sadistic pleasure in arbitrary orders, while a good one instills trust through experience. The instructor's platoon later adopted the phrase as a running gag among themselves and toward other units, a motivation mantra for holding out in arduous deployment situations: a disciplining line became self-motivation and reflected a maturing relationship between superior and subordinates.
*Candidate application:* Phrases can migrate from a superior's weapon to the troops' shield; a quote that began as an instructor's cruelty may be the one the veteran repeats fondly.

**[E01-60]. "Shooting to the left and handing out candies to the right": ironic compression of a split mission.** (AC-25, third saying)
An interlocutor used this to describe the mix of emotions in deployment: confidence and camaraderie, then, upon detecting or suffering an improvised explosive device, a sudden slide into anxiety. The line summarizes counterinsurgency in Afghanistan: fighting insurgents ("shooting to the left") and winning hearts and minds ("handing out candies to the right"). Johais reads the ironic tone as creating a "feeling of distance" that helps soldiers cope with contrasting roles.
*Candidate application:* Mission contradictions (fighter and aid worker) are a rich source of ironic summary; the ex-soldier's own sharpest lines may compress a policy contradiction he or she had to live.

**[E01-61]. The blog's conclusion: four uses of humor.** (AC-25, conclusion)
Johais lists: a safe way to express criticism, a way to cope with ambivalent feelings, a didactic tool, and a source of self-motivation. Although it gives soldiers some agency, this stabilizes the institution by making people stay; serious-only discipline is less successful. Footnote 3 explains mission-type tactics (the commander sets the objective and subordinates decide how), the German forces' predominant style, a context for why subordinates have room to think.
*Candidate application:* Humor can be both resistance and glue at once; the research does not resolve which.

### From AC-26 (Johais 2025 blog, "International joking relationships") — LIVE-FETCH SUPPLEMENT, NOT THE LOCAL FILE; paraphrase only, verify in a browser

**[E01-62]. Joking relationships among soldiers of different nations; German reticence.** (AC-26, live fetch, sections on joking relationships and "the shameful self")
Johais uses Radcliffe-Brown's "joking relationship" (ritualized mutual teasing that expresses recognition of a bond while managing hierarchy through a mix of friendliness and antagonism) to read how German soldiers relate to other armies in multinational settings. In the telling of the fetched text, German soldiers feel constrained by World War II legacy and shame: one anecdote has a Dutch colleague asking how long it takes to get from Berlin to Amsterdam and, after the German soldier said he was not sure, answering that it takes five days by tank; the German soldier felt unable to laugh openly for fear of how third parties would read it. Soldiers explained that humor requires spontaneity that self-control prevents, and described a default of harmless or self-deprecating humor in unfamiliar international company.
*Candidate application:* A group's history can make some jokes unlaughable for its own members even when funny; the person must police their own face.

**[E01-63]. Institutional change, forbidden jokes and banned patches.** (AC-26, live fetch, sections on institutional changes and "the patch examples")
The fetched text reports that women's admission to all branches (2001) and the suspension of conscription (2011) changed what men say; one soldier said men no longer make the jokes they share among themselves when women are present; jokes about minorities and gender are officially prohibited yet, per female soldiers, persist informally. Two patch episodes show command policing humor on insignia: a patch circulating in Afghanistan with a crusader holding meat and pseudo-Arabic lettering, banned as religiously offensive; and an armored-division patch contest in which the general approved the abbreviation "Die 1" ("the first" in German), until American soldiers read it as the English "die first," whereupon command banned and destroyed it. A third item: "Jukuhu" owl patches and tours, a code and cover story for informal trips to a U.S. base's shops (PX) at Bagram.
*Candidate application:* Insignia, patches and code words are a documented medium for soldier humor and for the command's counter-moves; a coded phrase for a forbidden shopping trip is a model of in-group euphemism.

**[E01-64]. How German soldiers read Americans and the British; the mess-game initiation stories.** (AC-26, live fetch, sections on Americans and British)
Per the fetch: Americans appear as an admired, affluent, efficient big brother (a base with its own city, pizzeria and shops), also seen as drilled like robots, low in education (summed up as a keep-it-simple formula), leading by fear and punishment, nicknamed "rednecks" for sunburned necks under simple caps, and as humorless because of strict gender-relations rules. The British are the model: humor as national sport, unbeatable at black humor with intellectual depth. A German who watched British mess dinners (formal, with regimental traditions) said the dinners escalate after the port wine: games included crawling through a tunnel of armchairs and emerging with torn shirts and bloody scratches, dancing longest with burning newspaper tucked in the buttocks, and (most dangerous) stripping a drunk soldier, wiring him to a bed and dragging it across a parade ground behind a horse. The witness admitted it was extremely dangerous and asked whether one could call it humor, but cited the claim that the tougher the unit, the harder the initiation, creating elitist esprit de corps.
*Candidate application:* Documents a view that initiation hazing is the cost of an elite group's cohesion; the same story shows an outsider both admiring and uneasy.

**[E01-65]. The French rivalry's escalation, and the "true friends."** (AC-26, live fetch, section on the French rival and Austrians/Italians)
The fetched text describes the same French-camp conflict as E01-10 in more detail: a simmering conflict with the French commander, perceived discrimination (croissants, laundry, Nazi gestures), the translated-joke battle in toilets, watchtowers and dining hall, and then an escalation: after French personnel allegedly refused to help a German soldier who was attempting suicide, seen as a betrayal of comradeship, German special forces hoisted a German flag at night on a five-meter miniature Eiffel Tower at the camp entrance (this appears to be the 2,500-euro dare in AC-01). Discovery brought punishment and a physical confrontation, which ended the humor, and the interviewee described such multinational cooperation as at best commanded comradeship, not felt. By contrast, Austrians and Italians were remembered as the closest allies, with whom banter was funny because of a similar sense of humor. Johais concludes that soldiers perform geopolitics through everyday practices.
*Candidate application:* Humor stops when physical retaliation or a perceived betrayal of comradeship enters; a shared sense of humor is described as what separates real allies from imposed ones. Handle the suicide detail with care.
*Cross-use:* inter-faction or inter-city joking relationships (who may banter with whom) in Outer Tepenia-style multinational settings.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions.** Cohesion through shared interpretation and boundary-drawing (AC-01, AC-27, AC-17). Controlled resistance and deniable criticism (AC-01, AC-25). Discipline and hierarchy testing, including quiet pushing on officers by those with earned license (AC-17 REME case, AC-01). Coping and distance from contradictory roles (AC-25 candies saying; AC-01). Morale as a recognized, used skill (AC-01 Kosovo commander). Retention: "makes people stay" (AC-25). Socialization into the soldier subject through playful imitation of fighting, taking and loyalty (AC-01). Policing of sexuality and of who counts as potent (AC-27). Sustaining the institution and its violence (AC-17). Concealment as civil-military coping (AC-40).

**Targets.** Peers' quirks (nicknames, "Mr. Disinfection," AC-01). The newcomer (ordered joke, AC-01). The injured or unfit (AC-27 "extra" joke). The sexually "wrong" (AC-27 pile-on scene). Superiors from another branch or who hide incompetence (AC-25 dress-code saying). The instructor (sculpture, AC-01, AC-40). The former enemy or ally-rival (French joke battle, AC-01, AC-26 live). The mission's contradictions (AC-25). The self, as a price of belonging (AC-01, AC-17). The prisoner, in the abuse cases (AC-17).

**Forms and mechanics.** Quick, surprising repartee (Schlagfertigkeit). Banter, one-liners, "stick." Nicknames and patches. Pranks and paid dares. Graffiti joke battles in shared spaces. Running gags from a superior's phrase. Sexual idiom woven into orders. Irony compressing a mission. Euphemistic polite phrases as deniable dissent. Gifts and objects (the sculpture). Mimicking and role performance on cue (Mr. Disinfection).

**Rules of license.** Reciprocity: "give as good as you get," "do ut des." The earned right (the REME Craftsman, "one of the few who could get away with it"; the joker whom the commander knows). Superiors may sponsor humor yet the "certain jargon" is never defined. Women must be loud, quick and reversible; their acceptance is "honorary." Reaction is the license test: laughter, silence, the straight face. Humor can be refused only at the cost of being a killjoy.

**Limits and failure modes.** Laughter that "sticks" to one person (Peter). Banter that merges into hazing and, in the one documented case, accompanies a suicide that the obituary omits (Tidy). Humor during violence against prisoners and civilians (AC-17). Complaint made impossible by the joke frame (AC-27 harassment meeting). Dares that become extreme and ambiguous in consent (AC-01 bets). Jokes that cannot be laughed at because of history (AC-26 live). Escalation ending the humor in punishment or fighting (AC-26 live). Humor that is dishonest by omission when used publicly (AC-17).

**Time course.** WWI British "sticking it" humor as a precedent (AC-01, AC-40). Civil-military closeness once let civilians know soldier jokes (AC-40 interviewee). Phrases that start as instructor discipline and become the platoon's own motivation (AC-25). Institutional change (women, end of conscription) altering what is said (AC-26 live). Very little on combat-time versus post-service change in these sources.

**Civilian-facing register.** Soldiers hide humor from family and public (AC-40), sometimes out of tact, sometimes fear. The institution's public face is a sanitized "fun lads" story (AC-17) or an emotionless professional (AC-40). The two civilian images of the veteran (murderer, damaged) leave no space for a humorous functioning person (AC-40).

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** None of these sources studies defectors or ex-service. What they do document: deniable, indirect dissent inside the ranks (AC-25), the cost of not laughing (AC-27), concealment among outsiders (AC-40), and a taboo on explaining soldier humor to civilians. The "left on principle" experience is untouched.

**Cross-cultural differences.** German (AC-01, AC-25, AC-40, AC-26 live): anti-militarist culture, citizen in uniform, mission-type tactics, humor as skill and as hidden. Danish conscripts (AC-27): sexualized idiom, a four-month conscription camp. British (AC-17, and as seen by Germans in AC-26 live): banter, mess games, an "unbeatable" black humor reputation. U.S. (Gilman response; AC-26 live): multi-year, deployed force; "gay chicken"; the U.S. as foil in the Danish men's self-image. Israeli combat reservists, Swedish navy and Norwegian conscripts appear only via citations.

**Craft observations.** Tidy's lesson: institutional descriptions of a unit's humor are a genre with a predictable structure (excellent soldier, good mate, died doing what he loved) and a tendency to tell the safe jokes (discomfort, pranks). Johais: the soldier known as funny is often the one whose quirks others noticed and named. AC-27: humor in a unit is not neutral background; what is not laughed at is also a rule.

I used a live fetch of the real post (URL in the source table), summarized by a tool, so wording is unverified; E01-62 to E01-65 need a browser check and a re-collection of the actual text for the corpus. The miniature Eiffel Tower episode appears in AC-01 (interview 8 July 2022) and, apparently, in AC-26; AC-01 gives the 2,500-euro bounty and no consequence, AC-26 (live fetch) gives a night operation by special forces and punishment. Treat as one body of evidence, not several.
- **AC-17 internal count.** "All 456 obituaries were read" fits only Afghanistan (456 fatalities); Iraq adds 179 (total 635) and the intro also says "more than 450." Which set was read is unclear.
- **AC-01 versus AC-17 on banter/hazing.** AC-01 reports Tidy as distinguishing banter from hazing by affectionate versus aggressive tone; Tidy's own text (E01-43) stresses that the two share one logic and differ chiefly in how institutions code them. AC-01 also attributes the phrase "service humor" to Basham and Tidy; the phrase does not appear in Tidy's accepted manuscript (only "give as good as you get" does), so it is likely Basham's term.
- **AC-01 text damage.** The text copy has an apparent typo ("receiving a crone," almost certainly "crown," p.9) and a stray citation fragment at [p.15]. No other damage affecting content.
- **AC-27 responses are garbled** by two-column conversion; Gilman's and Plester's wording above is reconstructed, and only short confirmed phrases are quoted. Figures are not present. Check the original in a browser for exact Gilman wording before relying on E01-36 in detail.
- **AC-40 has no pages or headings**; footnote numbers were counted by position and spot-checked.
- **Evidence limits.** All five peer-reviewed sources are about garrison, training, short-service or deployment humor of peacetime-era Western forces (German, Danish, British) and none studies combat death humor, humor about the wounded, or the dead. The material most relevant to gallows humor proper (Tidy on the obituaries' omission; AC-25's candies saying; AC-01's barber dare) is indirect.
- **Not covered by this unit:** defector or ex-service humor, humor in old age, songs and memes, humor about the enemy dead. Pursue the references AC-01 and AC-27 point to, not extracted here: Godfrey 2016 (humor as disciplinary technology), Ben-Ari and Sion 2005 (Israeli reservists), Bjerke and Rones 2017 (Norwegian conscripts), Basham 2013 (British forces ethnography), Hockey 1986 (*Squaddies*), Madigan 2013 (WWI humor and courage), Brown and Penttinen 2013 (humor in wartime), Higate 2012 (hazing in private security), Üngör and Verkerke 2015 (humor during genocide), Fine and de Soucey 2005 (joking cultures), Davies 2001 ("Humor is not a strategy in war"), Tomforde's work on German combat soldiers. Their reference titles are the only evidence of their content here and I did not read them.
