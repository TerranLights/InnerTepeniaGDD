## Source-shape notes (read first)

**Page-marker convention.** Where I cite `[p.N]` for a text copy with page markers, N is the marker in the text copy (the PDF page). For AC-09, printed journal page = N plus 26 (the article runs printed 27-45).

**AC-09 (Bhowmick & Vijaya 2023, Indian Army).** Journal article, about 10,700 words; read lines 1-866 in full, then skipped the reference list (lines 867-1057). Evidence base: 12 semi-structured interviews (60-110 minutes each, 13 open-ended questions, collected 4 October to 15 November 2021, some in Malayalam and Kannada with back-translation) with retired Indian Army personnel, chosen by purposive then snowball sampling and analyzed with Braun and Clarke thematic analysis plus a thematic-network diagram. All 12 are men, aged 44-70, with 16-39 years of service. By my count of Table 1, nine are commissioned officers of Colonel rank or above (one Lieutenant General, three Brigadiers, five Colonels), and three are an NCO, a Subedar (the table labels it "platoon commander") and a Jawan (an ordinary soldier), so the picture is mostly an officer's view of humor. Frame: three themes (organizational, leader, team humor), with a functional reading (stress, cohesion, newcomer assimilation) and an alternative resistance reading. **Does NOT contain:** joke texts beyond a handful of anecdotes; any women's account (the authors say so); any Navy or Air Force material; an analysis of what is specifically Indian (the authors say cultural context was "not directly addressed"); a taxonomy of humor styles (they say they did not explore specific styles); gore or combat death humor (one gallows anecdote only); post-service humor (the participants are retired, but they speak about humor during service); the views of anyone who could not cope with ragging (the authors flag this).

**AC-06 (Mogstad 2024, Norwegian soldiers in Afghanistan).** Journal article, about 10,500 words, from the PubMed Central XML; the text copy has no page markers or section headings, so I cite by paragraph line number ("L") in the text copy. I read every line (L1-299); the endnote list (L141-295) is almost entirely citations, which I read for leads only (E02-21). Evidence base: in-depth, semi-structured, sometimes repeated interviews (1-3 hours, in Norwegian, translated by the author) with 28 current and former Norwegian soldiers (22 men, 6 women, aged 30-58), chosen for combat experience across ranks and specialties, plus media, document and archival research and visits to camps, academies, veteran centers and museums; thematic, narrative and discourse analysis. The article foregrounds three anonymized soldiers (Simon, Katrine, Peter), with details altered after consultation. Context: Norway's Afghanistan mission, 2001-2021, about 9,200 personnel, tours of three to nine months, a professional volunteer force. This is the companion to Johais's "WARFUN taboo" (E01). **Does NOT contain:** jokes, nicknames or pranks as texts (the phrase "gallows humor" appears only briefly, in the framing and in one quotation); a breakdown of who was an officer or enlisted among the 28; any defector or dissenter material; post-service humor in any depth (a few remarks on later careers and on nostalgia). It is about **fun** (an affect Mogstad distinguishes from humor via Fincham), so many findings are about fun in war and are marked as such.

## Findings

### From AC-09 (Bhowmick & Vijaya 2023, Indian Army)

**[E02-1]. The one gallows anecdote: "no tea" under fire.** (AC-09, [p.2])
A retired Colonel described his men pinned down by heavy enemy fire for a whole day: tired, hungry, some wounded, after sunset in bad weather. Evening is normally tea-time for Indian soldiers, and in the middle of this someone blurted out that they had not gotten their tea. Everyone laughed; the reply was "we could die here, and you're thinking about tea?" The authors cite it as gallows humor in the sense of intentional coping humor in situations "too serious to be funny" (they credit Thorson 1993) and explain it by incongruity theory: a remark that violates what the moment is "supposed" to contain produces more laughter, which they take to explain why gallows humor peaks in emergencies (citing Duncan 1982). They say it eased the stress and put everyone at ease. (The anecdote is cited to a named personal communication dated 9 October 2021, while the participant table uses coded participants; I cannot tell which participant it is.)
*Candidate application:* The structure is a hunger for an ordinary comfort breaking through at the worst moment, with the laugh coming from the mismatch between mortal danger and a domestic routine. A soldier-type culture's gallows humor can be built on a missing daily ritual (tea, a meal, a smoke) rather than on death itself.

**[E02-2]. Humor as an expected part of the institution, not a leak in it.** (AC-09, [p.1-2], [p.7-8], [p.14])
The authors open from the puzzle that a rigid, formal, obedience-based organization should be humor-hostile, and then report that every participant treated humor as built in. The epigraph from a participant calls the army "one big humor workshop"; another says humor has been consolidated into the organization's culture "over centuries" (a participant's belief, not tested). Participants say the army expects its officers to bring humor into everyday life, and that a leader who can laugh with the troops has established a good connection; the authors' discussion lists humor as "one of the expected leadership traits" of officers. Where humor is found, per the participants: daily routine, informal gatherings, social events, and the early training days (physical training and sports). Participants also place humor in every state of service: peace, field, or war.
*Candidate application:* An army can officially or customarily require humor from its leaders. A character from such a culture may carry a duty to be funny that continues long after the duty ends, and may not recognize it as a duty.

**[E02-3]. Mess parties ("badakhanas"): the institutional venue for humor, and the long day.** (AC-09, [p.8])
Badakhanas are mess parties and social gatherings meant to build relationships; humor in them is sometimes impromptu and sometimes planned as drama, skits, songs and dance, with music and liquor, and participants say outsiders may mock them but they are essential to army life. One participant described the day with his men beginning at 4:30 a.m. and sometimes ending near 11:30 p.m. at a mess party, with "no switch off button," and said "you start living like family."
*Candidate application:* A unit with a regular ritual evening for skits and songs gives the humor a calendar and a stage; a former soldier might miss the occasion more than the jokes. The "no off switch" detail is a plausible source of the exhaustion and the closeness at once.

**[E02-4]. Drill instructors' put-down humor, and tea after the ordeal.** (AC-09, [p.8])
A participant said humor starts in training: drill instructors (called *drill ustad*) have an institutionalized way of putting recruits down with humor, which "eventually helps you to work hard." The authors read this as ridicule that improves performance. A second, complementary ritual is described: right after a demanding event (firing, an endurance run, an obstacle course) there is a place with tea, lemonade and "fun frolic," and even someone who felt hurt realizes it is all "part of the game." Participants add that after demanding tasks "a lot of liberties" are allowed in humor.
*Candidate application:* The pairing (humiliating humor during the ordeal, communal levity right after it) shows how a culture teaches that the cruelty is a game. A defector might recall the second half more fondly than the first, or the reverse.

**[E02-5]. Ragging: seniors tease and trick newcomers, and the worst of it ripens into a bonding story.** (AC-09, [p.8-9], [p.14])
Ragging is described as intentional teasing of incoming juniors by seniors, framed as ice-breaking and socialization. Participants said it helps newcomers make connections faster, settle in, and feel part of the "family"; one said that if the person ragged realizes it is only "to break even" it becomes a joke. The authors note the episodes were "quite intense." Examples reported: staging the kidnapping of an officer along with the theft of his wife's jewelry; taking an officer's identity card and delivering him to another unit as a suspected imposter; and the *sahayak* (orderly) ruse, in which seniors dress as orderlies, carry the luggage and attend to a newly joined officer in every way until, after some days, the newcomer finds out it was a senior in disguise. One participant said "it is harassment initially," that he had been driver, orderly and room bearer in such pranks, that his wife, friends and children were party to it, and that after "the first two-three days" it becomes "lifelong memories and team bonding." The authors add that each additional year increases how funny an episode seems (psychological distance, citing McGraw and Warren 2010), and that this growing funniness itself builds memories and integration.
**Documented limit:** the authors state the views on ragging came from experienced officers who "probably coped better with ragging," and that the experience could differ, and the effect could be negative, for those who could not cope. They also describe this humor as strengthening in-group identity while excluding out-groups (citing Tajfel), and call the practice "intentional bullying" in the discussion.
*Candidate application:* Rank-inversion pranks (a senior who plays servant) and families recruited into the joke are specific, writeable details. The survivorship caveat matters for a defector: the person who left may be exactly the one the cheerful ragging story leaves out.

**[E02-6]. Leader humor: the case that it is a tool for engaging "minds," and self-deprecation as the preferred form.** (AC-09, [p.9], [p.10-11], [p.13-14])
Participants described joking or laughing with juniors as part of the job of easing their stress. One senior said he does not command 860 soldiers but "860 minds," which are only used well when "happily engaged," and that humor is how he brings that about. A leader who jokes is perceived as confident, competent, authentic and approachable; one participant said such a leader "agreed to be human, agreed to be vulnerable." The authors report that self-deprecating humor was considered the most preferred and effective form among Indian Army seniors: one participant argued that when the team sees you as a "demigod," you are a commander and not a leader, and another said people love it because a higher-ranking man is willing to show vulnerability. The authors' discussion also says too much cynical or aggressive humor from a leader damages work relationships (citing outside studies).
*Candidate application:* For an officer character, self-mockery is the credible way to be liked up and down the chain. A character who once used it to be a leader may now find self-deprecation either very easy or unbearable.

**[E02-7]. How rank distorts feedback: the failed joke that is laughed at anyway, and the one man who did not laugh.** (AC-09, [p.9-10], [p.14])
Participants said a leader must read nonverbal feedback (nods, smiles, body language) and, because that is not always possible at the top, also judge by results over time (respect, cohesion, stress eased); one said you never get a clear assessment. They also said juniors need "permissions" to use humor and that "people around you laugh more" when you hold authority. The authors conclude that leaders get more legitimate opportunities to tell jokes, including flat ones, and that laughter at a senior's joke may be genuine liking or a display of alignment with the hierarchy. A Brigadier told of a party where a fellow commander was telling weak jokes (the text calls them "PJs"; I read that as Indian-English slang for poor jokes, which the text does not define) and everyone laughed except one man. Asked why, he answered, in the participant's recollection, that he did not have to laugh because he was not in that commander's brigade and the commander would not write his Annual Confidential Report (the performance review). The authors read it as showing that someone outside the joker's reporting chain can resist hierarchy-flavored humor, and that a leader should still watch for a frown and back off that line.
*Candidate application:* Obligatory laughter tied to who writes your evaluation is a concrete, portable mechanism. A character who has left the institution has lost the audience that had to laugh, and may be unprepared for rooms that do not.

**[E02-8]. Licensed subversion: skits, songs and one deadpan reply aimed at the seniors, with a stated limit.** (AC-09, [p.11-12], [p.15])
Participants and authors say mess-party rituals let subordinates joke at their seniors' expense, so the status order is partly reversed in humor; they say it equalizes power and lets anger be released "in a funny way." Examples: skits in which a young soldier imitates the commanding officer (described as not crude, subtle but mocking); troops cracking jokes at the leader at a *badakhana*, where the leader "should have a big heart to take those jokes"; in a Gurkha battalion, a traditional *jhamre* dance performed at functions, "more like rap," with lyrics the soldiers write, in which "if the boys like you, they will make it on you" (being the target is a sign of acceptance); and an officer's own account of a meeting where a senior said the time had been set at X but it was now X+15, to which he answered that on his last visit snacks and tea had been served at X+15, so that was when he came; everyone laughed and understood that nothing meaningful was going on. The authors treat a leader's own joking as a cue that benignly violates the formal relationship and signals followers may do so too, and they suggest leaders can use such dissent humor to learn of tensions without penalizing the dissenters.
**Documented limit:** a participant said it was "regulated, not given an open hand" and "it should not go all out and insult the senior people because then the teamwork won't happen."
*Candidate application:* A culture can have a licensed outlet that doubles as an early-warning system for command. A defector might be the one who used the license for something real and learned where the regulation sits.

**[E02-9]. Team humor: noise as a sign of health, and nicknames that rotate their target.** (AC-09, [p.12-13], [p.15])
A participant said a team that is loud, cracking jokes and "creating a nuisance" is well bonded and will be effective in difficult situations, while a team that is serious and stiff is more likely to have its egos "take a beating" under stress. Another recalled a time when they had to stay hungry, he told a joke, and the team said they had forgotten their hunger listening. The authors say humor is one factor among shared adversity, strong identity and military pride, and they note it is unclear whether team humor depends on individuals with a strong sense of humor or arises collectively. Nicknames always come at somebody's expense but the target shifts from member to member, so humor works as a leveller of the team. Examples: a jawan nicknamed "use me"; an officer nicknamed "tonali" for flaring nostrils when angry; an army commander whose finger stayed bent after an injury, called "ungli" (finger); and a nickname drawn from a notorious historical dictator's name, applied to a personality. A participant said nicknames are essential to team building but "never to put the person down."
*Candidate application:* The injury-derived nickname used by subordinates and apparently accepted is a small, specific example of humor about a wound that is not an attack. A rotating target is itself a rule of license: everyone gets a turn.

**[E02-10]. What the authors say the study cannot show, and secondhand leads.** (AC-09, [p.2-4], [p.15-16])
Limits stated: Army only, no Navy or Air Force; no women (representation is small); unclear whether team humor starts collectively or with individuals; humor styles not examined; cultural context not directly addressed; ragging views from those who coped. Future-research suggestions include comparing how women officers use humor, and how failed leader jokes are perceived over time. The literature section carries several claims I did not see in the sources themselves, listed here as leads only (secondhand): the Private Snafu training films (Culbert 1976) as comedy that helped soldiers accept undesirable service; Priest 1990 (U.S. Military Academy cadets who used humor coped better with training stress and were less likely to quit); Godfrey 2016 (British military memoirs: subversive humor challenging hierarchy, gallows humor building comradeship); Davies 2001 (humor in war as solidarity and coping); Vivona 2014 (outsiders cannot understand an occupation's need for humor).
*Candidate application:* None beyond leads; the limits list is useful as a reminder of what an "Indian Army" voice here would have to supply from elsewhere (women, enlisted, combat).

### From AC-06 (Mogstad 2024, Norwegian soldiers in Afghanistan)

*(Citation convention: the text copy has no page markers or section headings, so I cite by the paragraph line number in the text copy, "L", plus a description of where it falls. The article is about **fun**, not humor as such; gallows humor appears only in short passages. Interviewees "Simon," "Katrine" and "Peter" are the author's anonymized names.)*

**[E02-11]. Two kinds of fun in war: the tolerated kind that confirms war is tragic, and the kind treated as deviant.** (AC-06, L23-37, literature framing)
Mogstad argues that war and fun are normally seen as opposites in Western moral thinking. Some fun is accepted: when soldiers are off duty or inside the base "waiting for war," or in organized recreation and holiday celebrations; and dark or gallows humor is "an expected and acceptable feature of soldiering," read as stress release or coping. Mogstad's observation is that such accepted fun is usually justified by a function (consoling soldiers, bonding, cohesion), and that reading fun as escape or coping "rests upon and reinforces" the idea that war is ultimately experienced as tragic, so these forms are tolerable because they confirm civilians' own moral picture of war. Other fun is coded as deviant and sick, associated with indiscipline or wrongdoing. Two examples are given: a convicted U.S. soldier's remark, about prisoner abuse at Abu Ghraib, that it was partly "just for fun" (commentators used it to mark the abuse as exceptional and perverse); and a character in Carsten Jensen's novel *Den Første Sten* (The First Stone), a charismatic Danish platoon leader in Helmand who treats the war as a game, switches sides opportunistically, and says he is there to see how far he can take it. Mogstad uses both to show fun in war depicted as moral nihilism, and then asks whether fun in war must always mean either stress release or misconduct.
*Candidate application:* There is a published argument that a society tolerates a soldier's gallows humor only as proof of the soldier's suffering. A civilian-facing ex-soldier may be expected to perform that suffering with his humor, and may feel the pressure to explain jokes as coping when he actually enjoyed the mission.

**[E02-12]. Soldiers know civilians will take "fun" badly, so they translate: the public word versus the private word.** (AC-06, L25-27, L29, L57, L61-63)
Mogstad reports a Norwegian public that sees its soldiers as exceptionally ethical peacemakers (building schools and wells), with media coverage of Afghanistan veterans focused on casualties, injuries and trauma. The interviewees were firmly aware that civilians may find associating war with fun problematic. Many said they would not use the word *gøy* ("fun") about Afghanistan in public or with civilian friends and family; some felt it was not accurate and preferred "thrilling," "interesting," or "enjoyable"; others used *gøy* or *moro* (the second word is closer to humor and entertainment; *gøy* has strong connotations of childhood, carefreeness and play) freely and unprompted, and several described the whole mission as *gøy*. Most interviewees, far from being shocked by the project's name WARFUN, found the idea that soldiers have fun in war trivial and commonsensical; several said they agreed to talk because the angle was not the usual one about veterans' struggles and traumas, which they said fed "negative stereotypes" or "stigmas." Mogstad writes that interviewees said the project signaled interest in their "full experience."
*Candidate application:* A veteran may have two vocabularies, one for the unit and one for civilians, and may resent being treated as only a trauma case. The translation habit (swapping "fun" for "interesting") is a small, concrete behavior for a character to show.

**[E02-13]. Fun as touring, mastery and thrill: "playing war" is partly a boredom story.** (AC-06, L67, Simon)
Simon (a member of what the text calls a "Military Observatory Team" in northern Afghanistan for seven months in 2008-09, then 23 years old) described the tour as like backpacking: traveling to meet police, village leaders, border guards and NGO staff, camping on hills, talking to locals. He was also frequently bored, wet and cold (a whole tour in an open vehicle, temperatures down to about minus twenty), and said so while still calling the experience overwhelmingly positive, with a sense of mastery (*mestring*) and "the tingling sense of excitement that anything can happen at any time." The author notes veterans often described soldiering as about one percent action and 99 percent waiting, that they were often frustrated with superiors and rules, and that despite this they described the tours in affirmative terms and many said they missed them, associating them with cheerfulness, lightness, camaraderie and adventure. Simon described Afghanistan as a significant but closed chapter, personally meaningful but not necessarily useful to wider society (he said he felt more useful in his current police job).
*Candidate application:* The longing for deployment can be a longing for lightness and aliveness, not for violence. A defector may miss exactly what he is least able to say aloud.

**[E02-14]. Fun and heavy responsibility together; humor after a violent incident; the same appetite later in emergency work.** (AC-06, L85-93, L51, Katrine)
Katrine (a combat interpreter at age 20 in Kosovo, later an area specialist embedded with combat troops, two Afghanistan tours, now a paramedic) said she loved working under pressure and making fast decisions with direct consequences ("what road is safest to use? where are we most likely to get attacked?"), and called that "very fun." Mogstad uses this to challenge the idea that fun is frivolous. About a particularly violent episode, Katrine said that afterwards "we felt the gravity of the situation. But we did not stop having fun together. We were always joking around." Mogstad notes this could be read as stress-release coping, but stresses that Katrine said she wanted to be there and to fully feel the physical and emotional intensity of the work. She compared this with paramedic work: "the crazier and more urgent, the more fun." More generally the author notes that interviewees who left the army often took jobs as police officers, firefighters or emergency health workers.
*Candidate application:* A character who left the service may have moved into work that keeps feeding the same appetite for urgency, which is a different story from one who is merely damaged. The coexistence of gravity and joking (no hierarchy between them) is the documented pattern.

**[E02-15]. Fun often arrives afterward; distance from the enemy matters; "a sense of control" is the condition.** (AC-06, L95-101, Peter and the general interviews)
Peter (artillery-hunter specialist, four Afghanistan tours, combat including bomb attacks and ambushes) said that during combat it is not so much fun but afterward comes extreme relief and the sense that "this was fun," like skydivers and climbers' kick when it goes well and it is over (the original uses strong profanity for how unpleasant it is in the thick of it). Mogstad reports that when asked if combat was fun, some said it could be if the enemy was far enough away that "it almost feels like you are shooting in a video game," while others frowned at the idea; a decorated soldier said "If you think combat is fun, you have not been shot at from a close enough distance." For soldiers with close-combat experience it became fun after the fact, back at base, decompressing with comrades. The majority said they had fun only when they felt "a sense of control," which they attributed to technological and military superiority over the Taliban, trust in their skills and comrades, and the ability to call on U.S. air support. Most described the risk as "calculated" or "just the right amount of thrill" (*akkurat passe spennende*), while Peter and a few said they thrived in chaos. Peter also described the whole mission as fun, naming "the jargon, the gallows humor, and the entire lifestyle you have down there," with a Red Bull shared in a hole in the ground.
**Documented limit:** the author states that the analogies to skydiving and backpacking work only because soldiers could return to a safe base and unwind, and that their feelings were shaped by an asymmetric, distant relation to Afghans.
*Candidate application:* The "fun" arrives as retrospective relief and as an after-action story, and is conditional on a sense of control; a unit in a symmetric, uncontrolled war would not have this register available. Distance is a switch: remote shooting can be gamelike, close combat is not.

**[E02-16]. *Guttastemning* (the boys' mood), and women's inclusion in the fun.** (AC-06, L103)
Male interviewees often used the Norwegian term *guttastemning* for the type of fun they had: a euphoric mood that boys experience when "in their right habitat" (Mogstad's gloss from a dictionary site), which the author adds usually involves jokes and pranks. Mogstad notes military emotion rules are gendered and war is a masculine space, but the few women interviewed said gender became less relevant abroad and they felt more included and belonging in Afghanistan; Katrine took part in her unit's cultural practices and jokes, so was having fun rather than being made fun of (the author's paraphrase). The author states plainly that this does not mean women were never marginalized or harassed, and that some women said they carefully policed themselves to fit masculine norms. Mogstad connects this to the claim that fun involves a disregard of power differences.
*Candidate application:* A unit's joking culture can be experienced as a place of inclusion by the very person it might exclude at home, with a cost in self-policing. Do not generalize beyond these few interviewees.

**[E02-17]. The "bubble": fun as release from domestic responsibility, retold years later.** (AC-06, L107)
Interviewees described the deployment as a welcome break from the tedium and commitments of home (driving kids to school, bills, what to cook for dinner, work), saying "nobody can reach you." The author stresses this was not an escape from a meaningless life: most spoke warmly of home and looked forward to returning. Fun was social, affirmed by others, and made lasting memories which, years later, were still cherished and retold; Mogstad treats "fun" as partly a discourse applied afterward, colored by time and nostalgia and tied to the soldier's sense of self as adventurer or explorer.
*Candidate application:* An ex-soldier may be homesick for the bubble while loving the home he left it for, a mixed feeling that is not the same as trauma.

**[E02-18]. "Playing war": a self-aware phrase that is also a self-critique, and the innocence behind it.** (AC-06, L121-125)
Interviewees commonly said they were "playing war," and Mogstad takes the phrase seriously as a description of a war of choice with limited, calculated personal risk, in which winning was not their central objective: a chapter and a story, a chance to test themselves and see the world, a temporary distraction from domestic routine. The author cautions that the frame treats Afghanistan as a playground for Western powers and erases Afghans' experience of serial war. But interviewees often used the phrase self-reflexively, to say they had not been in a "real war" with high stakes (they cited the war in Ukraine, or Afghanistan seen by its civilians), and they were often critical of Norwegian politicians and military leaders for being unwilling to risk soldiers' lives or give them sufficient resources, and for a "vague mandate to plant the Norwegian flag." Mogstad reads it as partly critique of their own limited contribution. One interviewee called the job "an ethical paradox" because it involves using violence and sometimes taking a life, against childhood values; Mogstad argues the soldiers' sense of innocence (a self-image as ethical Norwegians and apolitical soldiers who felt little responsibility for the war's causes and effects) mattered more to their fun than any sense of transgression.
*Candidate application:* The ironic label for one's own war ("playing war") can carry both a confession of triviality and a complaint against the command. For a defector the same phrase may have curdled into the reason he left.

**[E02-19]. When the fun stops: danger, responsibility, age, and lost innocence.** (AC-06, L129-139)
Mogstad documents four ways fun faded. (1) Worsening security: some stopped having fun when ambushes and bomb attacks increased; two bomb attacks in 2010 that killed five experienced soldiers were particularly unsettling for interviewees who lost close friends, and for some fear and grief became dominant at least temporarily; Katrine began to question whether the mission was worth losing her life. (2) Responsibility: several stopped having fun when they became leaders of younger soldiers or, in Peter's case, parents of young children at home (fun depends on responsibility being light, not necessarily absent). (3) Age and experience: some no longer found it fun once older ("not immortal," "been there, done that"), and one said he got "substitute fun" watching younger soldiers on their first mission. (4) Lost innocence: Simon said he would be interested in going back "yes and no," because as "a proper adult" he now knows better and does not have "the conscience" to shoot anyone there, even someone shooting at him, since that person is probably a poor farmer who needs money or was pressed into a war he did not want, and too many innocent people have died. Mogstad reads this as redirecting the innocence from the soldiers to the civilian population.
*Candidate application:* The path "fun, then sobered by loss, responsibility, aging, and conscience" gives a defector a plausible sequence in which the humor changed without a single breaking event. Note that these are accounts of **fun**, not of jokes specifically.

**[E02-20]. The functional account is acknowledged but treated as incomplete.** (AC-06, L113-119)
Mogstad does not deny functions: having fun with comrades bolstered morale, wellbeing and unity, and some interviewees said joking around helped them cope or release stress during or after difficult events such as close combat. The argument is that functional and normative readings leave out fun as ordinary, on-duty, mission-wide experience, which came from particular conditions (volunteer professional troops, calculated risk, innocence, a war of choice). The author states that the three foregrounded cases are not representative in a statistical sense and that the analysis is a scholarly interpretation, not a claim to speak for the interviewees.
*Candidate application:* A character's humor should not always be reducible to a symptom or a coping tool; it can simply be one of the things he liked.

**[E02-21]. Leads from the reference list (secondhand; titles only, not read).** (AC-06, endnote list after L141)
Titles I did not read but which the author uses for fun, boredom and gallows-adjacent work: Basham, "Waiting for War: Soldiering, Temporality and Gendered Politics of Boredom and Joy in Military Spaces"; Brown and Penttinen, "A 'Sucking Chest Wound' Is Nature's Way of Telling You to Slow Down" (*Critical Studies on Security* 1(1), 2013); a cross-check against AC-22's reference list gives its subtitle as "Humor and Laughter in War Time" and its pages as 124-126, so it is a short piece on wartime humor, and AC-22 quotes two sentences from p.124 (see E02-23), which makes it a strong lead (not read by me); Hockey, *Squaddies: Portrait of a Subculture* (1986); Welland, "Joy and War: Reading Pleasure in Wartime Experiences" (2018); Lair, *Armed with Abundance* (consumerism and soldiering in Vietnam); Wool, *After War: The Weight of Life at Walter Reed*; Mæland and Brunstad, *Enduring Military Boredom* (2009); Fincham, *The Sociology of Fun* (2016), the theoretical anchor. Johais's "WARFUN taboo" (covered in E01) is cited as the companion paper.
*Candidate application:* None; leads only.

### From AC-22 (Kantar 2018, Gallipoli / Gelibolu)

*(Citation convention: [p.N] is the page marker in the text copy; printed journal page = N plus 152. The article is in English with a Turkish abstract (Özet). Soldiers named below are the Anzac diarists and letter-writers the article uses; Kantar's reading of each passage is her interpretation, not the soldier's.)*

**[E02-22]. The case for humor in the line, from a wounded corporal who wrote it up after going home.** (AC-22, [p.2])
Corporal Paul Haworth (Australian War Memorial papers, PR0156) was wounded at Gallipoli in August 1915 and, back home, wrote a letter to his family that became "almost a book." He says censorship during the war was so strict that all one could put in a letter was that one was still alive, and he recalls asking, after witnessing the war's horrors, how men keep from losing their minds. His account of what saves them: "A hearty laugh is almost priceless in the firing line." The man valued most in the front trench, he says, is the one who can keep comrades light: who, while the enemy is shelling that stretch of trench, or just as they are leaving it for an attack, or when a man or two has been buried by a shell burst, can say something that makes the others forget the danger for a second. Kantar's gloss is that ordinary soldiers' trauma is ignored during combat and overlooked when the soldier is hailed as a hero at home, and that humor therefore became "the best weapon for survival."
*Candidate application:* There is a documented role of the man whose job (unofficially) is to make the trench laugh at the worst moments, and a documented reason the true account arrives only after return, when censorship and heroics are over.

**[E02-23]. Kantar's five functions of war humor, and the philosophical kit used.** (AC-22, [p.1], [p.3-6], [p.11-12])
Kantar's source base is the diaries and letters of Anzac soldiers (Australian and New Zealand) in the Australian War Memorial and the Wellington National Library archives, read through classical and modern theories of the comic. Kantar states five functions that the examples illustrate: humor to challenge death; to insult the opponent; to overcome unwanted feelings of pity and fear; to communicate with the enemy; and to react against the unquestionable power of military authority. The context claim is that long, close trench contact forced both sides to know the enemy, sometimes to find weak points, sometimes to share "a common bond of desperation," and that some Anzacs came to respect the Turks for "fair" fighting. The theoretical sources leaned on (all secondhand): Umberto Eco ("comedy is the quintessential human reaction to the fear of death"), Brown and Penttinen ("There is more to the human experience of war than suffering and trauma"), Üngör (humor under mass victimization), Plato's *Philebus* (mixed distress and pleasure; laughter has malice in it), Aristotle (comedy shows people as "worse"), Hobbes ("sudden glory," superiority laughter), Bergson (an "absence of feeling" needed for the comic), Frye (ironic comedy's border with cruelty, "the element of play is the barrier that separates art from savagery") and Bakhtin (humor against official fear and intimidation). Two of Kantar's own inferences are worth keeping apart from the soldiers' evidence: that a pretended absence of feeling toward the enemy gives license to kill and then make light of it afterward; and that humor can also bring soldiers closer, and make the enemy look more human, by stressing common weakness against random violence. Kantar also cites a joke that circulated among U.S. soldiers, "If the enemy is in range, so are you," as an example of humor that makes war's uncertainty "knowable" by denying responsibility and stressing that soldiers are in a war not of their making (via Brown and Penttinen).
*Candidate application:* A usable taxonomy of purposes, including one (insulting the enemy) that is a hostile, not a coping, function. The "denies responsibility" joke type is a candidate for how a conscripted or ordered soldier talks about a war he did not choose.

**[E02-24]. Superiority humor and animal imagery aimed at the enemy, cheerfully in letters home (restrained summary).** (AC-22, [p.3], [p.5-7])
Kantar reports that some Anzac letters call the war a picnic or fun and killing a sport, and says such optimistic ideas about the war change drastically over the long battles. Kantar argues that humorous portraits of the Turkish enemy almost always imply a Western superiority over a laughable Easterner, and that by depicting the formidable enemy as "worse than in actual life" the writer lifts himself above him (Kantar's Aristotle-and-Hobbes reading). The main case is Lieutenant H.R. McLarty, who in a letter to his mother in May 1915 says the new dugouts would be comfortable but for the flies and the lice, "they are far worse than the Turks." Kantar reads it as lowering the enemy below ordinary pests and as telling the mother at home that conditions are tolerable and the enemy no match. In another letter, three months later, McLarty turns the removal of enemy dead from captured trenches into an "enjoyable" spectacle with an insect joke (the graphic wording is not reproduced here); Kantar reads it as black humor that blocks any empathy for the dead as people and as an instance of "pleasurable malice." Kantar adds that Anzac writers often used animal imagery to dehumanize the enemy and justify killing.
*Candidate application:* Documented: the dehumanizing joke written to reassure the home front. A character from a unit that did this might later find that his own letters from the time are the hardest thing to reread.

**[E02-25]. Grief set beside an "amusing" anecdote, and mockery of captives (restrained summary).** (AC-22, [p.7])
In his diary of 25 April 1915 (the Gallipoli landing), Leslie Lott records the death of an old friend, "one of the bravest," shot in the head, and in the same entry relates an incident about an Indian soldier that he calls "amusing": a soldier whose mule was killed by a Turkish sniper went out and took a violent personal revenge, and came back smiling. (Violent details not reproduced.) The next day Lott records an officer telling the men they were so cold-blooded one would think they were shooting rabbits and not people. Kantar reads these entries as the soldiers admitting to enjoying killing or watching it, with the humorous story set beside the grief "as if to counterbalance" it, and the hunting analogy as war becoming a game. In his account of the Lone Pine charge, Lott says "some funny things happened," describing a group of Turkish soldiers surrendering from a tunnel in a manner he treats as comical (their pleading is described in a way I have not reproduced); he then, in the same entry, reports an unrelated shell that killed a number of them, and the loss of nearly all his unit's officers. Kantar reads this as humor sustaining "a sense of self-righteousness, and perhaps sanity," in the middle of mutual carnage, and argues that mocking helpless captives goes beyond the fair exchange in which both sides are supposed to be killing each other.
*Candidate application:* Documented pattern: the funny story placed right after a death as a counterweight, and the narrator's tone toward helpless prisoners as a limit case that his later self may judge harshly. Write this as retrospective, not as the diarist's current view.

**[E02-26]. Mockery of the enemy's religious cries, with a colonial frame (restrained summary).** (AC-22, [p.7-8])
Haworth describes Turkish soldiers shouting their religious cries as they attacked: "As soon as we heard them we began to laugh. It really was a joke." He did not believe the shouting troops were the best of the Turkish army, and says they were repulsed as easily as the night before. Kantar reads the laughter at the cries as snubbing the enemy's belief system, which Kantar links to colonial-style justification, and notes that many Australians called themselves "Colonials" in their diaries. Haworth also writes, in a passage not reproduced here, a sexualized and stereotyped remark about Turkish women to explain his lack of qualms about killing; Kantar uses it to show the outsider's contempt for the country they were fighting in.
*Candidate application:* Humor can carry the contempt that makes violence easier; that does not make it unusual, only a documented limit of "harmless" soldier humor. A defector's guilt may center exactly on what he once found funny about the other side.

**[E02-27]. Jokes across a few yards of trench: the wire note, the booby-trapped "friendly note," and the surrender joke.** (AC-22, [p.1], [p.8-9])
Kantar's abstract says soldiers on both sides used humor to communicate with the enemy when the trenches were only a few yards apart, and that the Turks also poked fun at the Anzacs' combat competence. All the evidence in the article is nonetheless relayed by Anzac writers. Examples: (1) Haworth reports that the Australians spent a long time laying barbed wire, and next morning the Turks had taken all of it and left a note asking, in effect, that the wire be cut longer next time because it was slightly too short for their use, "Thanks, all the same." Haworth writes "The Turk is a humorist regardless of what people say." (2) Straight after, Haworth tells how Turkish soldiers, several of whom spoke English, tricked the Australians into believing a friendly note had been sent, when it was a bomb that killed one man and wounded three; Kantar says Haworth means the reader to remember that friendly exchange is only a pause in the violence. (3) The New Zealander Lt. Charles Saunders, writing on 20 May 1915 about the Turks being only about 30 yards away, said of the Anzacs' irregular wire that it would not stop them and "as we will have to go ahead sooner or later, it will probably be in our own road"; Kantar reads the self-mockery as making helplessness less alarming. (4) Private H.T.C. Alcock, writing to his mother on 25 August 1915 at Lone Pine, where the Turkish trench was about fifteen yards away, asked if a friend had been killed and then, in the next paragraph, told of notes thrown across: one saying (as given, with an expletive blanked out) that the Anzacs were too weak to advance and too strong to retire and the Turks the same, so what were they going to do about it; another, "If you don't surrender in twenty-four hours we will!" Kantar reads this as acceptance of one's own weakness, "one last attempt of human communication," and as the humorous story following the sad question so as to lighten it.
**Documented limit:** the friendly-looking exchange can be a lure (Haworth's bomb note), and the writers know it.
*Candidate application:* A gallows joke shared across a gap is a way of recognizing the other side as people without ceasing to fight. The shared joke about mutual helplessness (both sides stuck) is a pattern for a long stalemate.

**[E02-28]. A joke about the Anzacs from the Turkish press, and an honest wish to run (racial content summarized).** (AC-22, [p.8-9])
Saunders also copied into his diary a newspaper cutting from a Constantinople paper that mocked the colonial (Anzac) troops with a story that they had been driven into the sea but swam back, in a racist caricature of them (wording not reproduced). Kantar reads it as the Turks overturning the usual image of "black savages" so that it lands on the colonial troops, and as the Turks picturing themselves as the superior race (Kantar's interpretation of a cutting as Saunders saw it, not a Turkish source read directly). His reply was a joke at his own expense: if he had been driven into the sea, any aquatic gift he had would have carried him to a ship or to a nearby island, "certainly not back to Anzac." Kantar argues the double nature of humor gave him license to admit he wanted to leave the battlefield, a feeling that said seriously could have marked him as a deserter or traitor, and that "the irony of humor can put a safe distance between the reality of war and the truth of human psyche striving for survival."
*Candidate application:* Humor as the only safe channel for the wish to quit is a pattern for a defector's backstory: the real position may have been said first as a joke.

**[E02-29]. Absurd humor against hopelessness: the new toothbrush, and the shell that was met with laughter.** (AC-22, [p.9-11])
Sergeant Major Henry Kitson wrote on 15 May 1915 about a heavy bombardment in which a shell landed among his group, killing or putting eight men out of action, and closed his account of it by saying he was fortunate to secure a toothbrush, "new needless to say." Kantar reads this as a man in a dirty ditch who, instead of mourning the eight comrades just lost, pretends to rejoice over a small piece of ordinary life, a "thin line" between sanity and madness; Kantar adds that Kitson writes that after losing more than half of four thousand comrades in a day, one does not turn to see where a shell has landed. Saunders (23 April 1915) tells of a heavy shell, called by the soldiers' nickname "Jack Johnson" (after the African American heavyweight boxing champion, as the text explains), that landed in a bivouac of Australian Light Horse and wrecked it; what he heard was "peal after peal" of "ribald and hilarious laughter," again after a second one, and, he says, no one was hurt. He found the colonials' laughter in the face of death admirable ("laughing in the midst of death and treating it all as a joke"). Kantar calls this laughter taming death and says absurd humor lets a soldier who feels he is a failure "stay level-headed."
*Candidate application:* Two documented micro-forms: the displaced ordinary object (the toothbrush) that carries the grief, and the shared laugh at an artillery round as a unit-wide reflex of refusing to be impressed. A character who once used one of these may have kept the habit of grabbing a small ordinary thing when something terrible happens.

**[E02-30]. Humor against the officers: Saunders's doubt about being praised, and Dodd's near-arrest.** (AC-22, [p.10-11])
Humor as dissent against authority is the last function. Saunders, writing on 31 May 1915 about training in Egypt, recalls that the officers who lectured them kept saying they had wonderful talent and were "just the sort of men wanted," and the men always wondered "Was it sarcasm or not?" He says they got tired of it, and adds a jab that even the best engineers' corps has "many fools"; Kantar reads this as him allowing himself, and ordinary soldiers, to be less than perfect, and suggests he suffered ridicule from his superiors, though the text of the diary shows only that he could not tell whether praise was sarcasm. Major Arthur William Dodd (AWM) went further: after shooting an enemy soldier who was aiming at an English officer ("I felt very sick, but such is war"), his group commander asked him in "a most sarcastic voice" what he thought he was doing "wasting all the ammunition," and in the argument Dodd refused the order and told him he "could go and jump in the Dardanelles." After a dressing down and the fear of bringing shame on his battery, Dodd came to see it as foolish; he had just turned twenty-one. Kantar calls this humor against the sarcasm of authority "the empowerment of mimicry," and says Dodd risked his career. Kantar also notes that Haworth complained of censorship and that perfect heroes are not allowed to show frailty or criticize superiors.
**Documented limit:** Kantar says we do not know whether Dodd gives the whole picture; this is his own account.
*Candidate application:* Documented: the officer who uses sarcasm as a command tool, and the junior whose flippant reply nearly ends in arrest; the line between a licensed joke and insubordination is thin and not on the junior's side.

**[E02-31]. Limits and cautions Kantar states, and apparent gaps.** (AC-22, [p.1], [p.12])
Stated: there are no personal accounts of the war by private Turkish soldiers, so all Turkish-side humor appears through Anzac writers; Anzacs were better educated and encouraged by their superiors to keep diaries (so the archive is skewed); Kantar analyzes "only five" of many Anzac accounts and calls for wider study of war diaries and letters; studies of wartime humor are "very limited." My observation: the article names seven writers (Haworth, McLarty, Lott, Saunders, Kitson, Alcock, Dodd), so the "five" appears to be loose counting or a different unit of analysis. Two small inconsistencies I noticed: Haworth is called a corporal in most places and "Colonel Haworth" once; and the footnotes date Saunders's "Jack Johnson" shell anecdote 23 April 1915, two days before the landing, which looks like a footnote error (I report the date as given). The argument rests on a handful of anecdotes read through philosophy, not on a systematic survey. The article's own project context is a Turkish national research project, "From Hostility to Lasting Friendship" (TÜBİTAK, 2013-2015); the theme of the project colors the article's attention to humor between enemies.
*Candidate application:* Treat this as an anthology of vivid cases and a framework, not as a measured sample.

### From AC-11 (Cleveland 2003, Vietnam War songs)

*(Citation convention: [p.N] is the page marker in the text copy of this web article. The article quotes song texts; I paraphrase them and quote only short non-graphic phrases. The songs were sung or collected during the 1960s-70s; Cleveland's article is from 2003.)*

**[E02-32]. The frame: songs as the unofficial window on a unit's culture, and what the article is built from.** (AC-11, [p.1], [p.5], [p.7-9])
Les Cleveland (a World War II veteran and researcher of military occupational folklore, based in Wellington, New Zealand) argues that troops in Vietnam used songs the way earlier generations did, to define their situation. Cleveland applies Edgar Schein's organizational-culture concept: a group's taught-as-correct pattern of assumptions for coping with its external problems and holding itself together, which can be studied through official customs or, informally, through its folklore. For a combat unit the key requirement is esprit de corps and a sense of group identity; Cleveland names the central paradox for infantry and aircrew: individual survival often demands collective dedication, in which a person risks life for others. Evidence base: a "selection" of songs current during the war, drawn from archives and collections (a 1976 Library of Congress collection of 160 songs by U.S. military personnel gathered 1962-1972 by General Edward Lansdale; Lansdale's 1967 recordings of thirty songs; a 1969 folklore master's thesis by Saul Broudy; a Berkeley military-lore archive; Joseph Tuso's book on Air Force songs; cadence books; and Cleveland's own field collection of soldiers' songs). **Does NOT contain:** a count of songs, any account of how the selection was made, performer interviews (apart from a few remarks on when a song was sung), or full documentation of dates and singers for most items; Cleveland notes little comparable research had been done in the U.S.
*Candidate application:* Songs are a documented medium for gallows humor because they circulate, get varied and are sung together. A faction's repertoire (rather than one person's jokes) can show its culture.

**[E02-33]. Cohesion songs: sharing, the pilot who is the machine, and war as a game.** (AC-11, [p.1-2])
A song called "Grunt" teaches a rule of the infantry: learn to improvise with what you have and share it ("Share between you what you've got"). An Air Force song of the F-105 "Thud" pilots expresses total identification with the aircraft ("It is my body, I am its brain") and then undercuts it: the pilot loves the plane and it loves him, but he gets the creeps from having only one seat and one engine. Cleveland reads it as technology and specialization shaping how closely a workforce integrates, and notes that Tuso compares the officers' mess culture to a warrior band. Another pilot song, "Wild Weasel" (to the tune of "Sweet Betsy from Pike"), presents the dangerous mission of hunting enemy missile sites as playing "the big game." Cleveland says the war appealed to some participants as a kind of game.
*Candidate application:* A pilot or operator whose song claims to be one with the machine while joking about its single point of failure is a compact image of loving what might kill you.

**[E02-34]. Resistance songs: lifers, brass, parodied heroes, rotation dates.** (AC-11, [p.2-4])
Cleveland sets popular patriotic songs (Barry Sadler's "Ballad of the Green Berets," 1966) against protest songs and against folk compositions circulating among the troops that voice "unheroic and highly resistant attitudes." Examples Cleveland cites: a unit songbook item from the 35th Tactical Fighter Wing at Phan Rang, with a profane refrain, that satirizes the leadership (a call-and-response in which the men repeat "our leaders" and then call what they are fed nonsense); a parody of "The Caissons Go Rolling Along" by draftees, aimed at career soldiers ("lifers," with "R.A." read by Cleveland as Regular Army), mocking them as drinkers who stink and "even try to think"; a parody of the "Ballad of the Green Berets" that turns the heroic paratroopers into frightened men screaming that they do not want to die; a parody of "Take This Hammer" that advocates abandoning combat altogether by handing the rifle to the surrender program (the *Chieu Hoi*); and "Short Timers' Blues" (a rotation date ended the twelve-month tour of drafted personnel), a jokey song about fear creeping up and a hurried doctor's visit before rotation. A group song "Dear 'Ole Dad," collected from a veteran who learned it in 1965 and "always sung as a big group effort, usually in bars," wishes for "a war, just like the war that mutilated dear 'ole Dad," with a closing nod that this one at least would be fought by Geneva rules, to be shouted "with gusto." "Freedom Bird" is a dreamlike yearning for the plane home. Cleveland attributes the strong going-home sentiment to the policy of individual rotation as well as to conscription and ideological objections. "We Are Winning" (to "Rock of Ages") mocks the official line, naming McNamara (the article footnotes him as "U.S. Secretary of State," which looks like an error: he was Secretary of Defense).
*Candidate application:* Parody of the heroic song is a documented form; "short-timer" humor (counting down to rotation) is documented too. A group song sung in bars is a ritual that sounds like a joke but lets a crowd say something bitter in unison.

**[E02-35]. Songs travel across wars and armies: "Saigon Warrior" and the New Zealand barracks song.** (AC-11, [p.4-5])
"Saigon Warrior" (Broudy 1969, recorded from an Australian woman, Maggie, so possibly Australian or ANZAC in origin, per Cleveland) is a variant of a World War II complaint song that could be sung of any base or headquarters; Cleveland gives a New Zealand Army version, "Waiouru's a Wonderful Place," about a disliked training camp, still in circulation in 1986, and a second Vietnam variant, "Ol' Phan Rang," that follows the old text more closely. The Vietnam version swaps in captains, majors and light colonels with their hands in their pockets and nothing to do, and satirizes "Saigon Commandos" who lunch at a fashionable Saigon club and wear a Bronze Star "for writing reports about the war." Its last stanza predicts that after the war you will meet Saigon warriors wherever you roam, and you will know them on sight, and (a pun on "airborne") they are not in your class, because they show none of the illness or wear of field service, only the effects of a desk job (the "chairborne" pun). The refrain nonsense phrase "dinky dau" is explained by Cleveland as a corruption of a Vietnamese phrase meaning crazy, which Cleveland links to an Australian ballad's "dinky die" (Cleveland's inference; the text says it "makes no sense unless" so related). Cleveland adds that other songs with WWI and WWII origins also surfaced among Americans in Vietnam (fragments of "The Quartermaster's Store"; a parody of "Mademoiselle from Armentières"), but that except for Air Force pilots, American Vietnam songs do not keep many direct links to the Allied WWII repertoire, even though the content has strong similarities.
*Candidate application:* A song can be a handed-down template that each generation re-points at its own rear-echelon targets. The "you'll meet them at home" stanza is a documented example of humor that anticipates postwar life, specifically the civilians-in-borrowed-glory problem.
*Cross-use:* Exile or Outer Tepenia cultures could inherit a base-camp complaint song from an earlier era and re-aim it, a plausible and sourced mechanism.

**[E02-36]. Managing fear and guilt: nicknaming the enemy, booby-trap jokes, and black-humor pilots' songs about civilian harm (restrained summary).** (AC-11, [p.5])
Cleveland writes that fear of the enemy, death, wounding or captivity "could be brought under social control" by expedients such as calling the enemy "Charlie" and joking about booby traps, claymore mines, mortars, running out of fuel, being hit by a missile, or being forced down and becoming "a guest at the Hanoi Hilton," the prison camp in North Vietnam. Cleveland then says: "Guilt and anxiety over having to engage civilian targets could be relieved by the black humor of pilots' songs," and names two, "Two, Four, Six, Eight, Who're You Going to Defoliate" and "Strafe the Town." Cleveland quotes one verse (the text does not say which of the two it belongs to) that turns a gesture of aid to children into an attack on them (wording not reproduced), and says this "perfectly reflects the mad, Catch-22 contradictions of the war," in which a "fatal, obsessive technology of over-kill" destroyed some of the people it was supposed to save. The article treats this as evidence about guilt management, and does not say how widely the verse was sung or how the singers felt about it.
**Documented limit:** the attribution of function ("could be relieved") is Cleveland's inference from song texts, not testimony from the singers; the article offers no singer accounts.
*Candidate application:* A strong pattern for a fictional soldier with something to answer for: the humor that was a pressure valve for guilt over harm to civilians is the humor that is hardest to defend later. The euphemistic nickname for the prison camp is also a good example of institutional-language softening of a threat.

**[E02-37]. Folk song as an attempt to reach command; and the song as comparison between armies.** (AC-11, [p.5-6])
In 1967 General Lansdale recorded songs at his Saigon home and sent the tapes to President Johnson, the Vice-President, the Defense and State Secretaries and officials in Saigon including General Westmoreland, intending to convey the political and psychological nature of the war and to change policymakers' assumptions; Cleveland says it was "to no avail" and calls it perhaps the only known case of folklore used as a vehicle for intelligence. One song quoted is an advisory team's plea to Westmoreland that they "can't take much more," are nearly out of ammunition and fuel, and will be "out of work for sure." Cleveland adds the opinion that command might have preferred small-group counterinsurgency operations to massive firepower. On the comparison between armies: Cleveland reports (Ross 1983) that Australian soldiers thought Americans undisciplined, unprofessional and trigger-happy and criticized U.S. officers for wasting lives, and that New Zealand rank-and-file veterans Cleveland interviewed considered both Australians and Americans inferior in jungle patrolling.
*Candidate application:* A song as a message to people who do not listen: an institution that has no channel for a complaint may receive it as "culture" and file it. Cross-army contempt (each army says the other is sloppy) is a documented pattern in this source, the only cross-army comparison it makes.

**[E02-38]. Defiance in dress and gesture, and the stated breakdown of discipline.** (AC-11, [p.6])
Cleveland says resistance reached demoralization in some U.S. formations, with insubordination, refusals of duty, violence against unpopular NCOs and officers (the term used is "fragging"), black-market trading and drug use; at the level of "comic self assertion," some soldiers showed personal autonomy from the organization by giving peace signs, wearing non-regulation clothing and growing idiosyncratic mustaches.
*Candidate application:* Humor of the visual, wearable kind (the mustache, the sign) is a documented non-verbal form of soft defiance; it sits on a continuum with outright refusal.

**[E02-39]. Elite units and the opposite register: hyperbole, tall tales and the dedication cadence.** (AC-11, [p.6-7])
In the conclusion Cleveland says aggressive formations such as the Marine Corps and some Airborne units have an "elaborate sub-culture of hyperbolic, aggressive, self-assertion": a Marine boot-camp cadence casts the recruit as an epic backwoods frontiersman in a tall tale (the text is profane and sexualized, and is not reproduced); Airborne cadences stress physical fitness and regimental pride, and one, a four-line cadence, has the soldier ask to be boxed up and sent home if he dies on the drop zone, with his wings pinned on his chest and his girl told he "done my best." Cleveland reads the songs of such organizations as expressing both individual socialization crises and dedication to the group, and concludes that Vietnam's songs combine lessons from earlier wars' occupational folksong, unit exploits, "good-natured fun," and criticism of those running the war, and that they hold lessons for maintaining a military organization in "peacetime indifference and even hostility." Cleveland stresses the symbols (pilots' machine identity, the paratrooper's wings) as markers of a highly dangerous task.
*Candidate application:* The same population can sing the swaggering tall tale and the bitter parody, and which one gets sung depends on audience and occasion. An elite-unit culture would have a heroic register that outsiders mistake for the whole.

**[E02-40]. Leads from the references and gaps in this article.** (AC-11, [p.7-9])
Leads (secondhand, titles only): Cleveland's own *Dark Laughter* (1994) and "Soldiers' Songs: The Folklore of the Powerless" (1985); Tuso, *Singing the Vietnam Blues* (1990); Broudy, *G.I. Folklore in Vietnam* (1969); Burke, "Marching to Vietnam," *Journal of American Folklore* (1989; cited for the discussion of a counting chant); Lansdale's Library of Congress collections; Hopkins, *Songs from the Front and Rear* (1979); Getz, *The Wild Blue Yonder* (Air Force songs). ### From the small items (abstract, landing-page and finding-aid level only)

**[E02-41]. Horn 2011 (South African prisoners of war, WWII): humor as coping in captivity and as a device in later interviews, abstract only.** (AC-19, L10-11, keywords L13-26)
The local copy is a repository record (Stellenbosch University) of about 190 words: title, the 2011 date, a one-paragraph abstract, keywords (humor, intergenerational communication, memory, prisoners of war, South Africa, Stalag, World War Two) and the citation (*South African Historical Journal* 63(4), pages 537-552). The abstract says the South African POW experience is much neglected in WWII writing and that the article analyzes it through experience and use of humor. Claims, as I paraphrase the abstract: (1) humor as a means of coping during captivity reflected the captives' "humor identity" at the time of the war (the abstract's term, spelled in American English here); (2) when the former prisoners were interviewed later, the coping mechanism was adapted to give a "dual perspective," as one of several strategies interviewees used to smooth the interview and aid recall of often painful memories; (3) a search for what was unique to South African POWs ended with the conclusion that humor was a universal coping and morale-boosting mechanism, both in the war and during the interviews. **Does NOT contain:** any example, joke, number of interviewees, interview dates, or description of the camps' humor; none of the article's body text.
*Candidate application:* The one thing this abstract supplies that the other sources here do not is a mechanism for **humor after service**: an old soldier telling painful memories may reach for humor as a way of getting through the telling, not only as something done at the time. A defector or other veteran retelling the past to a companion may do the same.

**[E02-42]. Chapman and Ellin 2014 (Dominion cartoon strips in Great War field publications): the argument in the abstract only.** (AC-21, L16)
The local copy is a repository page (Warwick) that states the full text is not available from the repository (request a copy from the authors); the content is the citation (*The Round Table* 103(2), pp. 175-192, with a DOI) and an abstract of about 200 words. The abstract says Dominion soldiers' Great War field publications are fairly well known, but that the multi-panel cartoon strips some of them carried have been neglected as a record of satirical social observation; that visual narrative humor offers a "bottom-up" view of the spirit of the army, shown as stoicism buoyed by "a culture of internal complaints"; that troop concerns in the early comic strips of Australians, Canadians, New Zealanders and British were similar; that they shared an editorial purpose of boosting morale through everyday stories elevating the "anti-heroism" of the citizen soldier, pictured as a transnational everyman in the empire's service; and that "disparagement humor" had a "regenerative value" that redefined courage as the very act of endurance on the Western Front. **Does NOT contain:** a single cartoon description, any soldier's name, publication title, or example gag; and the abstract's claims are the authors' summary, not checked here.
*Candidate application:* An army's own comic strips (a soldier-made medium) can function as a standing public form of complaint; the abstract's key idea is courage redefined as endurance, which is the same "sticking it" idea E01 reported for British troop publications, so the two sources point the same direction (the E01 claim cites a different author).
*Cross-use:* A faction of conscripted or exiled workers might have its own bulletin or strip carrying the same function.

**[E02-44]. Australian War Memorial press release, "Laughter: a powerful ally" (exhibition on humor and entertainment in the Australian military).** (AC-43, L3-21)
The local copy is a press release of about 465 words announcing an exhibition in the Reading Room of the Memorial's Research Center, on display for 12 months with curator-led talks through 2020 (the file shows "Last updated: 30 March 2021," while the manifest dates the release 2019). It is about **entertainers visiting or serving with troops**, not about soldiers' own gallows humor. Content: the exhibition examines how Australian comedians and performance troupes have boosted troop morale from the First World War to Afghanistan, with art, bill posters, costumes and photographs. Co-curator Robyn Van-Dyk says entertainment was an important part of troops' lives in WWI, with shows mixing professionals and enthusiastic service personnel who had a flair for entertaining; that in WWII dedicated Army Entertainment Units were formed; and that from then on professional shows were organized by the Department of Defense, with entertainers flown into war zones such as Timor, Afghanistan and Iraq. One anecdote: after a show, a serviceman approached Franklin and said "We're here because we have to be here – what the hell are you doing here?" Franklin replied that he was there because the troops were there, wanting them to know people at home were thinking of them. **Does NOT contain:** any joke text, any account of what soldiers laughed at, or evidence on effects; it is an institutional promotional text.
*Candidate application:* A documented stance of the one who is stuck toward the one who chose to visit ("we have to be here; you don't") shows the boundary that comedy from outside must cross. A veteran who once served and later performed for troops is also a documented path from soldier to entertainer (Franklin, per the press release).

## Pattern roll-up

*(Each pattern is limited to the army and era named; none is generalized beyond it. Finding numbers in brackets.)*

**Functions**
- *Stress relief and cohesion, stated by participants:* Indian Army veterans say humor eases stress, builds team spirit, and that a loud, joking team is a well-bonded one (India, retired personnel; E02-6, E02-9). Norwegian veterans say joking helped them cope after close combat, though the author insists fun was also ordinary and on-duty (E02-14, E02-20). An Anzac corporal wrote that the man who can make the trench laugh under shelling is the most needed man (E02-22).
- *Facing death:* the tea-under-fire laugh (India, E02-1); the shared laugh at an exploding shell and the new toothbrush after a blast (Gallipoli, E02-29); fear managed by naming the enemy and joking about booby traps and prison (Vietnam, U.S. pilots, E02-36). Authors read these as taming death (Kantar, via Eco).
- *Guilt management:* Cleveland attributes to pilots' black-humor songs the relief of guilt and anxiety over civilian targets (E02-36); Kantar reads an Anzac diarist's laughing account of captives as keeping up "self-righteousness, and perhaps sanity" (E02-25). Both are authors' inferences from texts, not singers' testimony.
- *Resistance and dissent:* licensed skits, songs and dances at the officers' expense in the Indian Army (E02-8); parody and complaint songs by draftees against lifers, brass and rear-echelon "warriors" in Vietnam (E02-34, E02-35); a diarist's doubt about being praised, and a major's near-arrest for a flippant reply (Gallipoli, E02-30); Dominion trench comic strips as "a culture of internal complaints" (abstract only, E02-42).
- *Communication with the enemy:* notes and quips thrown across a few yards of trench (E02-27), shown by an Anzac writer as both warm and a possible lure.
- *Rank and status:* leaders get laughter for flat jokes (E02-7); self-deprecation is the preferred leader style (E02-6); a soldier outside the joker's reporting line can withhold the laugh (E02-7).
- *Identity and self-story:* "fun" as a narrative applied afterward that supports a soldier's sense of self as adventurer (Norway, E02-17, E02-20).

**Targets**
- Self (leader self-mockery, E02-6; a diarist's joke about swimming to a ship, E02-28). Team members by rotating nicknames, including from an injury (E02-9). Seniors and the institution (E02-8, E02-30, E02-34). Career soldiers, politicians and rear-echelon staff (E02-34, E02-35). The enemy, by comparison to vermin and by mockery of religion (E02-24, E02-26; restrained). The mission itself ("playing war," E02-18). The newcomer (ragging, E02-5).

**Forms and mechanics**
- Songs and parodies of heroic songs, sung together, sometimes in bars (E02-34); a base-camp complaint song handed from one war and army to the next (E02-35); skits, mess-party entertainments and a soldiers' rap-like dance with lyrics about the commander (E02-8); nicknames (E02-9); notes across the trench (E02-27); letters home that fold a joke after a sad question (E02-27); displaced-object deadpan (the toothbrush, the missing tea) (E02-1, E02-29); rank-inversion ruses (senior as orderly, E02-5); unofficial comic strips (E02-42, abstract only); copier folklore and sketches (leads only, E02-43).

**Rules of license**
- In the Indian Army sample: the mess-party and its skits license subordinates to mock seniors, within regulation ("should not go all out and insult"); juniors may need permission to joke; nicknames are fine if never meant to put the person down (E02-8, E02-9).
- A target who is mocked is thereby accepted (the Gurkha dance, E02-8); the leader who cannot "take it" would fail the same ritual.
- In Norway: fun is allowed in the mission mostly with "a sense of control," and the word for it is chosen by audience (E02-12, E02-15).

**Limits and failure modes**
- Ragging is remembered fondly by those who coped; the authors say the failures are missing from their sample (E02-5).
- Obligatory laughter hides whether a leader's joke worked (E02-7).
- Humor that carries contempt for the enemy or for captives (E02-24, E02-25, E02-26) and the joke that lures (a booby-trapped "friendly" note, E02-27) are documented in Gallipoli letters.
- Fun in war faded for Norwegian veterans with ambushes and deaths of friends, with responsibility for others, with age, and with a loss of innocence (E02-19).
- Insubordination dressed as a joke can bring near-arrest (E02-30).

**Time course**
- Each added year makes a ragging episode funnier (E02-5). "Fun" is often declared after the danger, once back at base, and is colored by nostalgia (E02-15, E02-17). Fun fades with age and conscience (E02-19). A Gallipoli corporal wrote his fullest account after returning home (E02-22). South African POWs, interviewed decades later, used humor as a device for telling painful memories (abstract only, E02-41). A Vietnam parody song predicts meeting the rear-echelon "Saigon warriors" at home (E02-35).

**Civilian-facing register**
- Norwegian veterans often avoid the word "fun" with civilians and substitute "interesting" or "enjoyable," while resenting the trauma-only story of veterans (E02-12). An Anzac officer reassured his mother with an insect joke about the enemy (E02-24). Society tolerates gallows humor as proof of suffering, argues Mogstad (E02-11).

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics**
- None of these sources is about a defector. Closest: draftee songs about abandoning combat (E02-34), a diarist's joke that was the only safe way to say he wanted to flee (E02-28), and a subordinate outside the reporting chain who would not laugh (E02-7).

**Cross-cultural differences (only where sources compare or are army-specific)**
- India: badakhanas, drill ustads, ragging with an orderly ruse, Gurkha jhamre (E02-3 to E02-9). Norway: *gøy/moro*, *guttastemning*, the self-image of an innocent peacekeeping nation (E02-12, E02-16, E02-18). Gallipoli: Anzac "Colonials" and Turks in a close trench war (E02-22 to E02-31). Vietnam: U.S. draftees versus "lifers," pilots' songs, and Cleveland's remark that Australians and New Zealanders each considered the Americans, and one another, inferior at jungle work (E02-37). Mogstad also compares Norway with Germany through Johais's WARFUN taboo (E02-11).

## Gaps and cautions

- **Scope.** Each source covers one army, war or occupation; nothing here supports a claim about "soldiers" in general, and several findings rest on a handful of people (Indian Army: 12, nine of them senior officers; Norway: three foregrounded cases; Gallipoli: seven diarists; Vietnam songs: an unstated selection).
- **Retrospective evidence.** AC-09's participants are retired and tell of past humor; AC-06's are interviewed years after tours; AC-22's main letter-writer, Haworth, wrote his long account after returning home, and the others' texts are archive papers whose later editing is not discussed. Retelling shapes the funniness (as AC-06 and AC-09 both say).
- **What is missing:** women's accounts in AC-09 and AC-22 (and the authors say so for AC-09); Turkish soldiers' own words (AC-22); singers' own testimony (AC-11); full text of Horn 2011 and Chapman and Ellin 2014 (AC-19, AC-21: **need the originals read in a browser or by request**); any content of the bibliography entries in AC-32.
- **Authors' inferences are separated from evidence** where I could tell: Kantar's philosophical readings of anecdotes (E02-23 to E02-30), Cleveland's attribution of guilt relief to songs (E02-36), Mogstad's reading of "playing war" (E02-18), and the Indian authors' theory section (E02-10).
- **Apparent errors in the sources:** AC-22 says "five" accounts but names seven; calls Haworth both corporal and "Colonel"; dates the Jack Johnson shell anecdote to 23 April 1915, before the landing. AC-11 footnotes McNamara as "Secretary of State" (he was Secretary of Defense); Tuso is dated 1990 and 1999 in different places; one line carries a stray HTML tag. AC-06 writes "Military Observatory Team" (probably "Observer"). AC-43's manifest date (2019) does not match the "last updated" stamp (2021) or its 2020 talks. AC-09's Table 1 note numbers do not line up cleanly with rank labels.
- **Terms not defined in the sources:** "PJs" (AC-09; my guess is poor jokes), the kind of tea ritual, *jhamre*; Norwegian terms are glossed by Mogstad. "Hanoi Hilton" and "Charlie" are glossed in AC-11 only by context.
- **Tension between sources, not contradiction:** AC-06, following Fincham, says fun disregards rank, while AC-09 shows humor strongly shaped by rank. They describe different things (fun versus humor), which is why I kept the word "fun" for Mogstad findings.
- **Cross-reference to other units:** the Brown and Penttinen paper on wartime humor is cited in AC-06 and AC-22 and is probably the most valuable unread lead (E02-21); E01 also reported a WWI "sticking it" concept (Madigan), which Chapman and Ellin's abstract echoes (E02-42).
