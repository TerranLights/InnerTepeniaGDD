## Source-shape notes (read first)

**The set.** A Blogger feed of the blog *My War* (handle CBFTW) captured 2026-10-05: **72 posts, about 30,000 words including headers and links, in eight yearly files** (2004: 20 posts; 2005: 18; 2006: 8; 2007: 18; 2008: 4; 2009: 2; 2021: 1; 2023: 1; nothing for 2010-2020). The author is Colby Buzzell, a U.S. Army infantry specialist (11B; M240 machine gunner in a Stryker brigade, 1st Battalion, 23rd Infantry, in Mosul, Iraq, arriving November 2003 and leaving about October 2004, per clippings in the feed). The blog became the book *My War: Killing Time in Iraq* (Putnam, October 2005), which is **not** in this feed. Copyrighted; I quote only phrases and single sentences.

**The main caution: the feed is mostly not Buzzell's own writing.** By my count (a judgment; many posts mix categories): **3 posts carry connected prose in his voice** (the 2004-06-22 first post, "Three Loud Explosions," the song list); **about 27 are short posts in his hand** (a title, one quotation, or a pointer to a magazine piece or broadcast; two are empty); **about 40 are pasted third-party items** (newspaper and magazine articles, reviews, radio listings, a publicity page of blurbs, an Army creed); **2 are spam (2021, 2023)**. The long narrative posts that made the blog famous (the "Men in Black" post and others of summer 2004) are **not** in the feed: clippings in the feed say he deleted his archives in September 2004 and that his book reprints several. So what the feed holds about his humor is his few short posts plus what reporters and reviewers and he himself (in interviews) said about it. **It documents the reception, framing and cost of the humor more than its content.**

**Per file.**
- **MV-CBFTW-2004** (June 22 - Sept. 28; about 7,000 words). The founding post, a pasted creed, a song list, a short note on an attack, one-line quotation posts, a mock-vague one-sentence post, pasted press on the Aug. 4 firefight post and the censorship episode (Tacoma News Tribune, NPR, Wall Street Journal, LA Times, Defense Today, AP), a punk singer's e-mail, a veteran's praise of the lost post. **Does NOT contain:** any long narrative post; reported barracks banter beyond a few lines. Two posts are empty. One (headed 2004-08-14) contains an article dated 2009.
- **MV-CBFTW-2005** (about 6,700 words). Almost wholly pasted press, reviews and broadcast listings about the book and soldier blogging, plus three one-line posts. **Does NOT contain:** blog narrative. Buzzell's interview quotations appear secondhand.
- **MV-CBFTW-2006** (about 4,200 words). Pointers to magazine pieces and broadcasts; one long Oklahoma Gazette feature (mostly about another soldier-blogger, with Buzzell's quotations on motives, censorship, reporters and life after discharge); a New York Review of Books essay (April 2008) on why soldiers enlist. **Does NOT contain:** blog prose. The Gazette piece carries graphic combat and a slur from the other blogger; I summarize only Buzzell's parts.
- **MV-CBFTW-2007** (about 9,200 words, the largest). Documentary reviews, prize notices, profiles (San Francisco Chronicle, Daily Yomiuri, Guardian, a Nov. 2008 CNN piece under a 2007 header), a Toronto Star article on the Army's blog rules, a Washington Post review of a travel piece he wrote, a 2009 radio listing, pointers (two to a men's magazine). **Does NOT contain:** the pieces themselves. First statements of PTSD and the end of his marriage.
- **MV-CBFTW-2008** (about 160 words) and **MV-CBFTW-2009** (about 940 words). Four pointers to magazine pieces (one with a two-sentence jest about an Army "invitation"); a page of about 30 publicity blurbs and one more pointer. These are the last items plausibly posted by the author (June 2009).
- **MV-CBFTW-2021 and MV-CBFTW-2023** (one post each). **Not Buzzell's writing**: machine-style commercial copy on steel stairs (2021) and on selling a house (2023). **Neither contains any reflection on the war.** The brief expected reflective posts; there are none (see [E18-35]).

**What the feed as a whole does NOT contain:** the book; the original narrative posts; the death letter, the tombstone and religion jokes, the "janitor" joke and the photograph scene that Pinkston takes from the book (E06-24, E06-25); substantial soldier banter, unit nicknames or sayings; anything he wrote after June 2009.

## Findings

### From MV-CBFTW-2004 (June-September 2004: founding posts and the censorship episode)

**[E18-1]. The founding post: a bored infantryman starts a diary "to kill some time," in Army diction with a punk title.** (MV-CBFTW-2004, post 2004-06-22 "Blogging From A Combat Zone", L4-46)
About eight months into the deployment, Buzzell says he learned of blogging from a Time article and expects it to pass the time: diary entries, rants, links, "whatever," no formula; he compares the improvisation to the Sex Pistols simply starting to play. He gives the unit's mission in official diction (to find, capture and kill "non compliant forces") and adds, in the tone of a performance review, "So far we've done pretty damn good." The plain half: "Lots of lows, and very little highs"; every day identical (patrol, observation post, traffic control point, same chow, same faces); time very slow; he has aged. The title "My War" is from a Black Flag song of alienation, whose lyrics he posts in full without comment. Mechanisms: institutional euphemism followed by casual understatement; boredom, not heroism, as the declared motive.
*Candidate application:* A soldier's first public voice can announce itself as boredom and borrowed music, with the institution's euphemism repeated straight-faced as its own joke. The "my war" claim owns the experience while refusing the official frame.

**[E18-2]. Posted without comment: the Infantryman's Creed and a song list built on contrasts.** (MV-CBFTW-2004, posts 2004-06-22 "INFANTRYMEN'S CREED", L48-87; 2004-06-24 "Soundtrack To Violance", L89-120; compare the pasted New Yorker item in post 2004-08-14, L183-192)
(a) The Army's creed ("Queen of Battle," "I yield not to weakness," "Follow Me") is posted entire with no gloss; sincere, ironic or branch pride cannot be told from the post. (b) The song list has 30 entries (one names three songs): death-metal and hardcore titles about killing and war (one about bombs over Baghdad) beside an Armstrong ballad of a wonderful world, a Dean Martin love standard, "Give Peace A Chance," a pop hit about happy people, a Sousa march, Wagner's Valkyries ride, the Imperial March and film themes. The humor is the sequence. The pasted New Yorker item (an article dated June 2009, so the post's date is wrong) quotes Buzzell: metal helped him "get all into it," and a patrol once blasted old movie themes from a vehicle; the Good, the Bad and the Ugly theme lifted him when he did not want "to play soldier today."
*Candidate application:* Self-scoring a war with film and incongruous music is a documented way to manage the sense of playing a role; "play soldier" suggests the role can feel like a costume on bad days.

**[E18-3]. Plain note when the shooting starts, and quotation-only posts that carry the mood.** (MV-CBFTW-2004, posts 2004-06-24 "Three Loud Explosions", L122-124; 2004-07-29 "I Dont Want To Live Alone", L126-129; 2004-08-05 "Men In Black", L131-133; 2004-08-10 "I'm Soo Fucked", L147-150)
"Three Loud Explosions" is a five-sentence log entry: three explosions about five minutes apart and brief small-arms fire on the way to the internet cafe, cover in a concrete bunker, three dust clouds beyond the wire, the concussion felt, "No word yet what just happened." No joke; a flat teaser line ends it. Three other posts survive as a title plus one quotation: a title of dread of living alone over a line (attributed in the post to Orwell; see Gaps) about rough men doing violence so others sleep safe; the notorious "Men In Black" post represented only by "Quote of the day: 'I just want this day to end'"; and a panicked title over a Bukowski line about writing to keep from "total madness." The last is the nearest 2004 statement of what writing does for him.
*Candidate application:* The comic voice is not constant; a first-hand attack is logged in the plainest terms. A soldier may say "I am frightened" by quoting someone else and letting the title carry the feeling.

**[E18-4]. "Sniper Fire (?)": a post made of mock vagueness.** (MV-CBFTW-2004, post 2004-08-12 "Sniper Fire (?)", L152-154)
The whole body: "The other day, we went somewhere, and did something." The question mark in the title reads as a wink. It falls a week after the firefight post and about when command lectured him on operational security (E18-7), so it reads as a parody of the redaction asked of him: a report stripped of place, time, unit and action. The post does not say so. The Wall Street Journal (E18-7) says that a few days after the first counseling he left "a mocking message" for the intelligence officers he assumed were reading, and was confined to camp; whether this is that message cannot be shown, because the clipping does not quote it.
*Candidate application:* Mock compliance with a censor (reporting nothing, formally) is a resistance form that is obviously not a mistake and therefore gets noticed.

**[E18-5]. The near-miss bullet kept as a future "show and tell."** (MV-CBFTW-2004, post 2004-08-13 "STRYKER BRIGADE SLAMMED BY INSURGENTS", Tacoma News Tribune article, L156-181)
The article quotes his account of the biggest Mosul fight of the deployment: a platoon in the kill zone of an ambush by dozens of men in black, "Hollywood" explosions all around, and plainly, "I've never felt fear like this ... I cannot put into words how scared I was." A buddy's helmet was pierced front to back and he got a concussion. The next day the crew cleaned shell casings out of the vehicle and fixed parts, and chaplains and counselors went around. He found a flattened 7.62 mm round by his hatch, pocketed it, and joked that as an old man he could bring "the bullet that al-Qaida tried to kill me with" to a grandchild's show and tell. The joke pairs a domestic image with the attempted killing and gives himself a long life. **Secondhand through the newspaper; the original post is not in the feed.**
*Candidate application:* A souvenir of a near miss, imagined as a family artifact, turns danger into an anecdote the speaker expects to survive to tell; years on, the real object may be in a drawer and the imagined classroom has not happened.

**[E18-6]. A veteran's reading of the "Men In Black" confusion: the "green" gunner's honest muddle.** (MV-CBFTW-2004, post 2004-08-07 ""Green" Gunner", L135-145)
Buzzell posts an e-mail from Ed Fitzgerald, described as one of the original Green Berets (it reached him through the author of a Vietnam SEAL book). Fitzgerald says the "green" gunner captured the terror and confusion: in a firefight a man tracks only a fraction of events (about one-fortieth, or perhaps a tenth for the most experienced); fiction and tall tales give a hero who sees and narrates everything; Buzzell gave it "with all the warts," including uncertainty about what he was hitting and shooting too close to his own men. Praise for the absence of heroic coherence. No joke in the post; it states the blog's anti-heroic register from a credentialed outsider. The account itself is not in the feed.
*Candidate application:* Telling a story with the confusion left in sounds, to civilians, less competent than the hero, and to veterans more credible.

**[E18-7]. The command's reaction and what review did to the voice.** (MV-CBFTW-2004: posts 2004-08-19 "Stay Tuned", L198-202; 2004-08-25 "RADIO NPR", L207-216; 2004-08-27 "Army Blogger's Tales Attract Censors' Eyes", Wall Street Journal, L218-241; 2004-09-06 "LA TIMES", L244-252; 2004-09-11 "Article In Defense Today Magazine", L254-277)
All pasted journalism, so it reports Buzzell. Sequence as reported: the Aug. 4 post ("Men in Black," a long account of an hours-long firefight) was drawn on by the Tacoma paper; the Pentagon clip service sent it to commanders in Iraq; within hours his battalion commander summoned him to an office where a printout of his writings was marked up, and asked first whether he was a big Hunter S. Thompson fan. Two details were challenged (that the unit ran low on water; the steps to get more ammunition) and removed. He was banned from patrols (AP: five days of missions), put on radio guard, confined to camp after a mocking message to intelligence officers, and placed under review by his platoon sergeant and a superior officer. His verdict to the Journal: "I was never edited before. Now I am," and it was no longer fun. He deleted his archives, posted a single Johnny Rotten line about being cheated and a Guernica image captioned "OVER AND OUT"; before that, "Stay Tuned" was only the full First Amendment and "story developing...". The command side: his top commander (a brigadier general) said Buzzell was the only one of about 8,000 soldiers under him to come under scrutiny; the battalion commander said the Army had no wish to muzzle him, called him gallant and a gifted storyteller, and the Journal says the command even nudged him to continue. A spokesman told NPR the vetting was operational security; a Brookings fellow said it was politics; an Army lawyer-blogger called the Army a slow bureaucracy, not a sinister one. The Journal adds that soldiers told Buzzell they strip the curse words and send his writings home as their own.
*Candidate application:* The documented price of an unedited voice is institutional notice, and the response can be mild, mixed and admiring while still a muzzle. Review turned writing from fun into a chore; silence (a blank page, a quoted amendment) became his reply.

**[E18-8]. How reporters characterized the humor, and the grenade-and-dollar scene.** (MV-CBFTW-2004: posts 2004-08-27 L218-241; 2004-09-11 L254-277; 2004-09-28 "Blogs offer view of soldiers' lives", AP, L292-312)
Outsiders' descriptions: the Journal says "gripping" combat accounts mixed with "amusing vignettes from the dusty grind"; Defense Today says "frank, profane and often funny," long rants without standard spelling or punctuation, with a profile listing drinking, skateboarding, reading and fully automatic 7.62 weapons among his interests. Comparison points: Hunter S. Thompson (his own named inspiration), Michael Herr's *Dispatches*, a Vietnam-era enlisted pirate radio. The one anecdote in the AP piece that is Buzzell's own writing: on patrol in a poor Mosul neighborhood, boys came up with unexploded shells and demanded dollars; then a very thin boy ran up with a live grenade, the squad shouted for him to drop it, and he did, wearing "a proud smile" that said "look what I just found" and asked the squad leader for money; the grenade did not go off, and the squad leader explained a previous division had paid children for ordnance. The AP's wider survey lists the genre's stock absurdities (an office sign telling people the desert is hot and to quit whining; the chemical-light tip for a dark latrine), which belong to other bloggers.
*Candidate application:* The absurd reversal (a lethal object offered as a trade good, a child's pride, a squad's terror in one frame) is a recognizable infantry story shape; reporters read the voice as bar-room raconteur plus war correspondent.

**[E18-9]. An outside ally's e-mail, and two empty posts.** (MV-CBFTW-2004: post 2004-09-23 "MESSAGE FROM JELLO BIAFRA", L279-290; posts 2004-08-15 "Mad Mortar-men Goose Chase", L195-197, and 2004-08-22 "GGGOOOAAALLL!!!", L204-205)
Buzzell posts, without comment, an e-mail from the singer and activist Jello Biafra thanking him for alerting them to his situation, calling him courageous (with a self-flagged pun on guts and guns), framing soldiers who bypass corporate media as real patriots, and ending "Bring The Troops Home!" It shows the blog's audience included anti-war punk networks; it does not show Buzzell's own view of the message (E18-19 gives his later explanation). The two empty posts survive as titles only (a mock-heroic alliterative one and a sports-announcer shout); their content is unknown.
*Candidate application:* A soldier under scrutiny may be claimed by outside political camps; posting the support without comment accepts it and leaves his own position open.

### From MV-CBFTW-2005 (almost entirely press and reviews; his own voice survives in interview quotations)

**[E18-10]. What the blog did for him, in his interview words.** (MV-CBFTW-2005: post 2005-08-22 "The New Ernie Pyles", Washington Post, L144-155; post 2005-09-06 "'My War' -- a soldier's wild ride", San Francisco Chronicle, L157-186; post 2005-02-01 "Columbia News Service", L10-48)
To the Post he called the blogging "therapeutic": after a patrol he would sit at a computer and it was a release; he wrote for himself, not a book deal, "a way to deal with the madness" that made days pass faster. To the Chronicle: after the big firefight he told himself to "just go from the beginning," typed as fast as he could, posted, lit a cigarette and had no idea what he had done; he had never heard the word "blog," and did not tell his parents about it because of the swearing; he was overwhelmed by hundreds of e-mails ("I didn't join the Army to cause problems"). Columbia says he posted anonymously until the Tacoma paper quoted him and that he posted from the base's internet cafe the day after the battle, drained.
*Candidate application:* The stated function is release and time-passing, not testimony. Hiding the blog from his parents because of register shows the civilian-facing translation problem at its smallest scale: the family gets the sanitized soldier.

**[E18-11]. The wire squib and "Now here's what really happened": mock-correction of official language.** (MV-CBFTW-2005: posts 2005-02-04 "Sunday's Atlanta Journal Constitution", L61-79; 2005-08-03 "NEWSWEEK INTERNATIONAL", L130-142; 2005-09-06 "'My War'...", L157-186; 2005-09-06 "Reuters", L188-204)
Four clippings describe the structure of the post that made the blog famous: he pastes the short, bland wire-service account of the Mosul "clash" (three or four paragraphs; Newsweek says it predicted Mosul would soon return to normal), then opens his own account with a line announcing what really happened (about 8,000 words, per Newsweek). The book is said to set it beside a dry military statement. The Journal-Constitution reviewer says it comes with "irresistible gallows humor" and anger; the Chronicle says its moments are "too surreal to be anything but true" and gives one: when the platoon took fire at a mosque, some soldiers pulled out new digital cameras to photograph the action while a machine gunner threw the heavy-metal horns "like it was an Ozzy Osbourne concert." Buzzell's stated reaction, quoted twice: it made him wonder what else happens in Iraq that never reaches people at home. The posts themselves are not in the feed.
*Candidate application:* Setting the institution's neutral phrasing against the unit's lived confusion is the documented comic-and-critical mechanism; the humor is the gap, not a punchline.

**[E18-12]. How critics named the humor, and where they disagree about its politics.** (MV-CBFTW-2005: posts 2005-02-08 "Review" (Publishers Weekly), L81-85; 2005-02-17 "Kirkus Review", L87-96; 2005-09-18 "Library Journal Review", L206-210; 2005-09-22 "Newsweek Books", L261-269; 2005-10-06 "ESQUIRE", L271-276; 2005-02-01 "Columbia News Service", L19; MV-CBFTW-2006: post 2006-09-02 "Radio Allegro", L21-30)
All secondhand and about the book as much as the blog: "relentlessly cynical," a "sentimental misanthrope" who pours scorn on everyone from cooks to generals to the president, on the media and the antiwar movement, yet whose book, the reviewer says, portrays American forces as the good guys in a dirty war (Publishers Weekly); a skater-punk slacker who captures "the daily absurdities and occasional terrors" of the front and quotes *Full Metal Jacket* constantly, that film being, the reviewer says, the shared coin of his fellow soldiers (Kirkus); "gritty, obnoxious and often hilarious" (Newsweek); the Esquire editor places its ancestry in comic-absurdist war novels (M*A*S*H, Catch-22). Military bloggers' nickname for him: "Blogfather" (Columbia). A 2006 radio blurb calls him "blogger, soldier, and American Hero," a label at odds with his anti-heroic voice (E18-6). An anti-war singer read the blog as an ally (E18-9); Publishers Weekly reads a pro-troops portrait under the scorn.
*Candidate application:* A voice that scorns everyone from cooks to presidents resists capture by any camp, a model for a soldier-defector claimed and disowned by both sides. A shared film as the unit's quotation stock is a documented banter resource.

**[E18-13]. The "warped, sick war humor" self-diagnosis, as the press quotes it.** (MV-CBFTW-2005, post 2005-09-06 "Reuters", L188-204; compare E06-25)
Reuters quotes an August 2004 blog entry reprinted in the book: Buzzell has developed "that really disturbing, warped, sick war humor about everything," and Reuters summarizes the passage as describing how a gruesome photograph of a dead Iraqi prompted laughter. This is the sentence Pinkston uses (E06-25). **What this reading adds or does not:** the original entry is not in the feed, so I can confirm only that the sentence is Buzzell's, dates to August 2004 (the month of the firefight post and the censorship episode), is general ("about everything"), and is treated by reporters as candor. I cannot confirm Pinkston's specifics of the photograph, which come from the book. Restrained handling: the photograph is not described.
*Candidate application:* The soldier names the change in himself in the present tense and without alarm; the calm is the evidence of how normal it has become.

**[E18-14]. Self-mockery as introduction: the "guy" job list, the drug-test story, the escape from sameness.** (MV-CBFTW-2005: posts 2005-09-06 "'My War'...", L157-186; 2005-09-06 "Reuters", L188-204; 2005-02-01 "WARRIORS - Program 06-02-05-A", L50-59; 2005-09-21 "Soldiers Weigh In on Iraq", L212-259; 2005-02-17 "Kirkus Review", L87-96. MV-CBFTW-2006: post 2006-10-29 "The New York Review Of Books", L119-136)
Reporters repeat three self-portraits from the book: (a) a list of his jobs at 26, each tagged "guy" (flower delivery guy, valet guy, cash-register guy, through a record-store guy), which the Chronicle columnist uses as proof of "loose ends"; (b) the recruiter's tips on passing the military drug test, and a wait of a couple of weeks to be clean (the Journal, Reuters and a radio blurb all mention it); (c) his reason for enlisting, "sick of living my life in oblivion" with every day the same, which he compresses (NYRB, quoting the book) into a five-word chant of eating, a bodily function, working, sleeping, repeating. He adds that he wanted "the great adventure," so that if nothing else he could say he did something; the NYRB retells a Marine friend's pitch that military life was a frat party with weapons and paychecks, and draws the general point that few soldier-memoirists say they enlisted to defend the country; they enlisted to escape dead-end work, relationships, family trouble and boredom. The Journal's author box lists his "worst moment" as getting his first leave canceled. The mechanism is self-mockery by inventory, offered before anyone else can supply it.
*Candidate application:* A character can introduce himself by enumerating his failures in a flat list; the bored recruit who joined to escape routine and found more routine is a rueful irony with a long life.

**[E18-15]. The first plain statements about leaving: defenseless without the weapon, and no tolerance for petty authority.** (MV-CBFTW-2005: posts 2005-02-04 L61-79; 2005-02-17 L87-96; 2005-02-01 L10-48; 2005-09-18 L206-210)
Quoted from the book by reviewers: turning in his weapon after almost eleven months, he felt "completely defenseless and vulnerable" (the clipping brackets an expletive). He cannot imagine a boss yelling about five minutes' lateness or saying he is "not smiling enough at the customers." To Columbia in 2006: "I just want to be a civilian for awhile," and the reporter says he was "eager to distance himself" from the tour; Library Journal suggests he may have "come full circle" back to the civilian life of loss before the war.
*Candidate application:* Two documented first symptoms of discharge: bodily (the missing weapon) and social (intolerance of petty workplace authority and required friendliness). A defector's story could begin at the surrendered weapon.

**[E18-16]. Another soldier's sardonic blog and what command did with it: the Hartley case.** (MV-CBFTW-2005, post 2005-02-01 "Columbia News Service", L10-48, esp. L22, L36-41)
The article (not about Buzzell) reports National Guard sergeant Jason Hartley (author of the "Awesome!" guide in E06-20), who described his flight route, posted a prisoner photograph, and wrote sardonic remarks about civilian deaths and children that he said were deliberately over the top, aimed at the military's casual attitude toward civilian casualties. His commander was not amused: lecture, a $1,000 fine and demotion. Hartley called it a "knee-jerk reaction" to "a sardonic blog." The article adds that some soldiers say superiors use operational security to mask disagreement with a blog's politics or "sense of humor," and that blogs had become "much more self-conscious." Restrained handling: his specific wording is paraphrased.
*Candidate application:* Satirical over-statement is read literally by command and punished by rank, not content; a soldier who learned that in uniform may write differently afterward.

**[E18-17]. The plain turn: an engagement with no joke.** (MV-CBFTW-2005, post 2005-05-12 "USA Today, today.", L98-114)
USA Today quotes the Aug. 5, 2004 account at another point: he sees men creeping around a corner and hiding behind a stack of truck tires, then one with a rocket launcher; he admits he "freaked," collects himself, puts the sights on them and fires, and sees no one move afterward. Hesitant and exact; no embellishment. Restrained handling: the passage on killing is summarized, not quoted.
*Candidate application:* The comic voice stops where the first-person act of killing is narrated; the admission "I freaked" shows the narrator is not performing the hard man.

### From MV-CBFTW-2006

**[E18-18]. Why he wrote: no one out there had actually gone out the gate, plus a parody of cheerleader blogs.** (MV-CBFTW-2006, post 2006-09-21 "Oklahoma Gazette", L42-117, esp. L69-75)
He found blogs through a Time article and thought of them like the 1990s fanzines he read. He did not read soldier blogs long, he says, because some were shut down and most of the rest recited "brainwashed rhetoric": a mock chant that the Iraqis love us, that the speaker loves the Army, the job, the country and the president. If he wanted that, he could read the Army recruiting site. He could not find one blog by someone who loaded his weapon daily, went on missions and saw "up close and personal" what it was like. Mechanisms: parodic recitation of cheerleading; target: other soldiers' public voices and the recruiter's page; the stated mission is a gap in the record. He also says he pasted an officer's disclaimer onto his blog (opinions are his, not the Army's), thinking it would protect him; it did not.
*Candidate application:* Contempt for the approved soldier-patriot register, delivered as a parody of its own phrases, may be a reflex a veteran keeps for years.

**[E18-19]. The Biafra post as a test of the Army's claim, and a fellow blogger's verdict.** (MV-CBFTW-2006, post 2006-09-21 "Oklahoma Gazette", L42-117, esp. L76-87; compare E18-7, E18-9)
Buzzell says military statements claimed soldiers had free speech, he thought they did not ("That's full of crap"), and he tested it by asking a punk singer who is a free-speech advocate for a guest post; "the Army totally blew a head gasket," and he knew the "unless it jeopardized the mission" line was a lie. In 2004 the post carried no comment (E18-9), so this is a later reframing. He says he was never demoted, that officials could not control unfiltered dispatches, and that he finally pulled the plug when his chain began previewing posts. Another soldier-blogger quoted, Fred Minnick, calls Buzzell the "Rosa Parks" of military blogging, says everyone including officers thought the blog good for the Army, and that the Army stopped him out of fear it could not contain him; Minnick adds that he himself self-censored, learned he was read anyway ("Centcom kind of like goes through all the blogs"), and heard commanders had considered assigning people to blog. Minnick's claims are unchecked.
*Candidate application:* The soldier describes provocation as an experiment on the institution; the humor is the cheek of the move. A defector's later accounts of early defiance may recast it as a test he designed.

## Pattern roll-up (documented patterns this unit establishes)

*Scope: one American infantryman, Mosul, 2003-04, and press about him 2004-2009. Most evidence is what reporters and reviewers said, a few short posts and interview quotations. Nothing below measures frequency.*

**Functions**
- **Time-killing and release** (E18-1, E18-10); writing as "a way to deal with the madness."
- **Truth-telling against official language:** the wire squib followed by "what really happened" (E18-11); the humor is the gap. The counter-example is heroic coherence (E18-6).
- **Resistance to the review regime:** mock-vague reporting (E18-4), a bare First Amendment (E18-7), an outside ally's message as a test (E18-19), later mockery of the rule's scope (E18-29).
- **Identity and affiliation:** punk, skate, Bukowski, Thompson, *Full Metal Jacket* as shared quotation stock (E18-12); an obscene punk-flavored handle (E18-1, E18-20).

**Targets**
- Institutional language and procedure (mission statement, wire squib, review, recall notice), embedded reporters, cheerleader soldier-bloggers, recruiters, civilian tokens of support (E18-1, E18-11, E18-18, E18-22, E18-23, E18-32); himself (E18-14, E18-28).
- Not documented in the feed: jokes about the unit's dead; jokes about the enemy dead appear only in one secondhand summary (E18-13).

**Forms and mechanics**
- Deadpan vagueness (E18-4); understatement in institutional diction (E18-1); mock-correction (E18-11); absurd inventories and juxtaposed lists (E18-2, E18-14); mock invitation (E18-32); reductio (E18-29); pun and hedge in titles (E18-25, E18-33); parodic recitation of cheerleading (E18-18); film framing of danger ("Hollywood explosions," the horn-throwing gunner, the show-and-tell bullet; E18-5, E18-11); quotation as statement (E18-3, E18-7).
- **Scraps of soldiers' own speech in the feed** (all secondhand): war cries and a mock "Indian yell" while loading up (WSJ quoting the blog, E18-7's source); a rock-concert hand sign mid-firefight (E18-11); film quotation as shared code (E18-12); "grunt" and "trigger puller" as labels; "men in black" as the insurgents' label; the milblogger nickname "Blogfather" (E18-12); a medic-blogger's exaggeration about what counts as a security breach (E18-29). **No barracks dialogue, sayings or joke texts of substance are in this feed.**

**Rules of license**
- **Audience:** the family does not get the blog (E18-10); an outsider's joke at soldiers' expense draws a terse correction (E18-26); review and fame change the audience (E18-7).
- **Over-statement read literally:** command punished a sardonic blog (E18-16). Soldier-bloggers publicly claim limits on what soldiers "would never" laugh at (E18-29; contested).
- **Who may mock the rules:** pro-war and anti-war bloggers alike (E18-29).

**Limits and failure modes**
- **Drift named by the writer:** "warped, sick war humor about everything" (E18-13); reviewers' note of "the exhilaration" violence breeds (E18-34).
- **Where the voice goes plain:** the first-person act of shooting (E18-17); the moral jolt of firing at a mosque (E18-21); the first frightened moment (E18-5); the later cost (E18-23, E18-26, E18-31); being unable to say he served (E18-24).
- **Review changes the pleasure, not necessarily the content** (E18-7).

**Time course**
- 2004 in theater: deadpan mission statement, mock-vague post, film-scored bravado, a plain log of an attack, a self-diagnosis of warped humor; command curtails the blog within weeks.
- 2005: first plain statements about leaving (the missing weapon, no tolerance for petty authority; E18-15); fame as strain and ride (E18-10, E18-26).
- 2006-2008: resentment of "support the troops" rituals, heavy drinking, a marriage ended, PTSD, a recall dodged (E18-23, E18-26, E18-31); wary of talking about the war; fluent in print.
- 2008-2009: civilian comic essays (a deadpan travelogue; titles of minor-vice and recession pieces); the war a credential; the institution answered with mock courtesy (E18-30, E18-32, E18-33). **After June 2009 there is no authored content in the feed** (E18-35).

**Civilian-facing register**
- Soldiers stripped the curse words and sent the blog home as their own (E18-7); he hid it from his parents (E18-10); the media made him a hero or a comic-book episode (E18-12, E18-24); he stopped telling people he had been in Iraq (E18-24).

**Defector/dissent specifics (for a character who left on principle)**
- The documented relationship to the institution is neither loyalist nor defector: honorably discharged, calls the war a mistake only in 2008, claimed by both camps (E18-9, E18-12, E18-31). Relevant devices: mock compliance, a recall answered as a social invitation, a test of the institution's free-speech claim, and a chain of command that responds in mixed ways (counsel, confine, bar from missions, nudge to continue; E18-7, E18-19, E18-32).

**Cross-cultural.** U.S. Army only; one infantryman, one deployment (Mosul, 2003-04); a punk-skate subculture voice; no other army, no women's voice.

**Craft observations** (reviewers, secondhand)
- The honest muddle reads as credibility (E18-6); pairing the official squib with the lived account is the central device (E18-11); critics' ancestors for the tone: Catch-22, M*A*S*H, *Dispatches*, Hunter S. Thompson (E18-12, E18-34).
