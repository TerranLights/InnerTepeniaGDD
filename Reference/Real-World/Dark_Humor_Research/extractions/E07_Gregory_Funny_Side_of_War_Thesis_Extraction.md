## Source-shape notes (read first)

**AC-23 (Gregory 2016, *The Funny Side of War*).** A History PhD thesis (University of Kent, October 2016; two volumes joined in one text file; stated word count 99,450, the text copy runs about 121,000 words with footnotes and bibliography; double-spaced, 23,207 lines, 471 page markers). It studies British wartime *newspaper cartoons* as evidence of what humor did in 1914-18: how it related to censorship (Chapter 1), advertising and the stage (Chapter 2), civilians who "avoided obligations" (Chapter 3), the relation between soldiers' and civilians' humor (Chapter 4, the most relevant), and later memory and reuse (Chapter 5). Evidence base: thousands of press cartoons, with a qualitative, thematic selection (no counts or sample sizes) from the British Cartoon Archive at Kent and online newspaper archives, chiefly by Poy (*Evening News*, *Daily Mail*), Haselden (*Daily Mirror*), Staniforth (*Western Mail*), Bairnsfather (*Bystander*), Dyson (*Herald*) and the *Punch* artists; plus newspaper articles, official censorship records, theater reviews, trench and troop journals (*Blighty*, *Khaki*, *Mudhook*, *Lead Swinger* and others) and a handful of soldiers' private sketches and letters (Southgate, W. J. Evans, Lomax, Lamb). The army and era are British (some Australian and Canadian material), 1914-18, with an afterlife to the 1980s.
**What it does NOT contain:** (1) the images: every figure in the text copy is "Image redacted" with only a caption, so every cartoon is known only through Gregory's description (cited by caption in the thesis, not seen by me); (2) soldiers' unmediated speech: almost all "soldier" humor in it passed through a newspaper or a civilian-run magazine (even *Blighty* and *Khaki* were produced by civilians for soldiers) or a family scrapbook; (3) trench-journal texts (the *Wipers Times* is named as an example and said to carry few cartoons; no trench-journal jokes are quoted at any length); (4) soldiers' own reception data (the thesis admits readership and reaction cannot be measured); (5) defectors, deserters, conscientious objectors in their own voices (they are targets, not speakers), and any veteran later-life material other than one 1918 drawing and the 1930s afterlife of a character; (6) humor about death and the dead in any detail, which the thesis says was largely not drawn. The thesis is also internally untidy in places (figure numbers cited against the wrong drawings in Chapter 1; two publication dates for one cartoon; see Gaps).
**What I read, and how.** Read line by line with the Read tool (about 650-800 lines per call, which is roughly 250-400 lines of text because the copy is double-spaced): the front matter (contents, acknowledgments, abstract, abbreviations, figure list; lines 1-1021), the whole Introduction (1021-2964), the whole of Chapter 1 (2964-6327), Chapter 2's introduction (6327-6800), its full "Cartoons into Theater" section (8865-9650) and conclusion (9650-9863), and samples of its advertising and theater sections (lines 7060-7320, 8100-8430, 8600-8865); Chapter 3's introduction (9863-10312) and conclusion (12290-12591); the whole of Chapter 4 (12700-16599, including its three sections and conclusion); the whole of Chapter 5 (16599-19914, including its conclusion); and the thesis Conclusion (19914-20368). **Skimmed or skipped:** the Bibliography and archival lists (20368-end); the repeated Volume 2 title and contents (12591-12700); Chapter 2 lines 6800-7060, 7320-8100 and 8430-8600 (advertising and the theater-in-cartoons section: Shakespeare and music-hall references in cartoons); Chapter 3's body, lines 10312-12290 (cartoons of profiteers, the wealthy, pacifists and cowards, which are civilian-on-civilian). I judged these not to bear on soldiers' own humor, but I did not read them, so their content is not in the findings. Roughly 60,000-65,000 words of text were read. Page citations `[p.N]` are the PDF page markers; in the body of the thesis the printed page is N minus 21 in Volume 1 (through [p.252]) and N minus 23 in Volume 2 (from [p.255]).

## Findings

### From the Introduction (AC-23, [p.22]-[p.56])

**Page convention.** `[p.N]` is the marker in the text copy, which is the PDF page, and the marker precedes the page's content. In the body of the thesis, printed page = N minus 21 through [p.252] (Volume 1; the Introduction starts at [p.22] = printed 1) and N minus 23 from [p.255] on (Volume 2, after its repeated title and contents pages; Chapter 4 starts at [p.255] = printed 232). Front matter uses roman numerals ([p.1]-[p.21]).

**[E07-1]. The thesis's working model of humor: incongruity, superiority, relief, and the admission that war cartoons are "both playful and serious."** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.28]-[p.30])
Gregory defines humor loosely as "the cause of laughter" and uses it as the umbrella term over wit, mockery, farce, satire and the rest. The thesis sorts how humor is produced into the three classic accounts: incongruity (things out of place, and in a cartoon several out-of-place things in one frame at once), superiority (one group made to look or feel above another; the same device inverted when a person is shown to be less important than they should be, which adds incongruity), and relief (release of tension, said to be the account most often applied to war). Two cautions of Gregory's own: the superiority mechanism simultaneously creates an "other" and binds the group on each side, so the same joke separates and joins; and relief is classically instantaneous, whereas a still drawing may give comfort that is slower, which the thesis says is the case with Bairnsfather's images. Gregory concedes that explicit definitions are hard because humor is "both playful and serious" at the same time (quoting Driessen), and that this is what makes it useful to a historian, since it shows "both the good and the bad together."
*Candidate application:* Useful as a three-question checklist for any soldier joke the project writes: what is out of place, who is placed above whom, and what pressure does it let out. The slow comfort version of relief (a joke that is still being turned over hours later) is a distinct thing from the instant release and may suit a character who has years of distance.

**[E07-2]. Is wartime humor a "weapon"? Against the enemy, against the self, or neither.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.30]-[p.31])
Gregory surveys a debate in a 2001 special issue of the *Journal of European Studies*. One camp treats humor as a propaganda strategy aimed outward (an example named is an early French animated cartoon); another treats it as an aid to survival, a "weapon against the self" in the form of coping; a third, Christie Davies, argues that humor is not a strategy in war at all and so is not a weapon. Paul Fussell is cited for the view that the humor of war is innately ironic because war never goes as anticipated, though Gregory flags that Fussell speaks of this largely through memory, and that his military detail has been challenged.
*Candidate application:* The three positions are a map of how different characters might describe their own jokes: as a tool (aimed at someone), as medicine (aimed at self), or as nothing, just noise. Disagreement among the characters about which it is would be realistic, because scholars disagree too.

**[E07-3]. Humor as agency: the person who makes others laugh holds a small power, and soldiers used it to protect a sense of normal.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.31])
Gregory states, from secondary literature, that humor "defuses tension" in groups and also empowers the person who produces it, because making others laugh confers perceived power or agency whatever the setting, and that many soldiers used this for their own survival. Soldiers needed humor to keep up their "social reality" and sense of normalcy; it worked as motivation; things that could not be ignored had to be lived with, and humor made that easier. The thesis calls it a "benevolent weapon" that soldiers turned on themselves. When it turns to cartoons, the same benevolence is located in the viewer, civilian or soldier, who receives comfort from the image.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character may hold standing in a group for the one thing he or she can reliably do, make the others laugh, and lose a real source of agency on leaving. Writing the joker as someone whose power is the ability to relieve others fits the "agency" reading.

**[E07-4]. In British wartime newspapers there were "right" and "wrong" kinds of laughter, and a national-humor contest used as morale material.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.35]-[p.36])
Gregory reports that the British press ran a steady run of pieces on soldier humor and laughter (citing *The Times* items from January and September 1915, among others), repeatedly tied it back to Victorian and Edwardian humor, and compared the humor of the warring nations. In these pieces the right laughter was the kind that inspires and comforts others and the wrong laughter was designed to make others feel less valuable. They often set British humor against German to flatter the British troops or shift public opinion; the thesis cites 1915 speeches by the actor-manager Herbert Tree arguing that Germany, above all the Kaiser, lacked the sense of humor needed to compete in war. Newspapers therefore did not only print cartoons but talked publicly about what soldier laughter was and meant.
*Candidate application:* A civilian-facing story about soldiers' cheerfulness ("they keep smiling") existed from the first winter of the war and was itself a morale instrument; a character may resent being used as the proof of that story. The "right and wrong laughter" distinction is a ready-made folk rule about humor that comforts versus humor that diminishes.
*Cross-use:* A nation or faction that claims a superior sense of humor over its enemy as a marker of fitness is a documented propaganda move.

**[E07-5]. Trench journals: unofficial humor versus official order, and where cartoons appear in them.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.32]; [p.49]-[p.50])
Gregory notes a surge of scholarly interest in "trench journals" (soldier-made magazines) and quotes John Fuller's phrase "trench newspaper fever" for the way almost every troop and battalion produced one; one quoted self-description says a paper caters for "no tastes but [their] own." The thesis distinguishes two kinds: journals produced by the soldiers themselves, which carry unofficial strands of humor "similar to that found in the now well-known *Wipers Times*," and journals run by the military or naval authorities, which stress regularity and order and carry drill notices. Prices ran between one and six pence; the real readership cannot be counted because copies were passed along. Home-published journals carry cartoons more prominently, because printing facilities in the trenches were far more limited, though some trench titles (naming *Gasper* and *Growler*) still put an illustration on the cover, which is taken as a sign of how much audiences and editors valued the image. Soldiers generally compiled these for other soldiers, but many were not printed in the trenches at all: they were sent home for printing and returned for distribution. The thesis's own point is that studies of trench journals had not yet concentrated on the cartoons.
*Candidate application:* The unofficial/official split is a usable model for a faction with a soldiers' newsletter alongside an authorized one: same format, opposite attitude to humor. Printing at home and returning by post is a reminder that even "by soldiers for soldiers" material often passed through civilian hands.

**[E07-6]. Soldier-drawn cartoons reached the civilian press through a magazine that welcomed amateurs, and one soldier's characters became the wartime template of the ordinary fighting man.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.38], [p.46]-[p.47])
The *Bystander* was notably more open than *Punch* to anonymous and amateur contributors, including soldier artists. Captain Bruce Bairnsfather sent in a chance drawing in 1915 on the grounds that his work was about the same size and quality as other cartoons in the paper; his *Fragments from France* drawings gained popularity for their perceived realism, in his own words "first hand impressions." His grumbling regular characters Bert, Alf and especially Old Bill were figures that working-, middle- and upper-class readers at home could identify with, and they became a new benchmark for the cartooning of the soldier: where some artists still showed the impeccable hero, Bairnsfather's soldier was "everyman being a hero in their own small way." Gregory also notes that the thesis's sources include private and personal cartoon collections whose "in" jokes were meant for a specific audience.
*Candidate application:* A mid-war pivot from the flawless-hero image to the grumbling, mud-covered ordinary man is a documented shift in how soldiers were allowed to appear in public humor. A soldier character might feel an irritated recognition and an irritated ownership toward the civilian-friendly version of his or her own grumbling.

**[E07-7]. A paper may censor its own cartoonist: the Dyson case.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.43])
The Australian socialist cartoonist Will Dyson drew for the labor paper the *Herald* before the war and, after he went to the front as an official war artist with the Australian forces in mid-1915 (where he was twice wounded), supplied other papers as well. Gregory reports that as the war went on there was a growing public wish less for his "penetrating" imagery and more for realism and truth about what was happening; critics said his "capitalists were too bloated, working men too idealized"; and eventually the *Herald* itself censored his work and limited the "number of devils" in it. The source for this detail is a biographical dictionary, not the papers themselves, and the thesis treats it briefly.
*Candidate application:* A friendly outlet limiting its own artist for being too stylized shows that self-censorship by the sympathetic side, not only by state censors, shapes what a community's humor looks like in print.

**[E07-8]. The thesis's central claim for our purposes: front-line and home-front humor are "remarkably similar," with one difference in how wounds are shown.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.54], [p.56])
Gregory sets out the Chapter 4 argument. Older scholarship held that soldiers and civilians were "alienated" from each other; newer scholarship stresses continuity and communication; Gregory's contention is that despite perceived distance the two groups are "remarkably similar" in the approaches they take to making the rest of society laugh. A further layer is the wounded soldier, who is no longer a civilian and no longer an active soldier. Gregory's stated finding is that in cartoons civilians depict merely superficial wounds while soldiers are "more accepting of life changing deformities," which Gregory says could simply be acceptance versus denial and which is tested against reported public responses to the wounded. In the Introduction's closing paragraph Gregory adds that it is a commonly held assumption that military humor differs from civilian humor, and that this will turn out "not necessarily so."
*Candidate application:* Counter-pressure against a flat "soldiers have their own dark humor that civilians cannot share" model. A character might discover that humor they took to be incommunicable had civilian cousins, and that the real gap was in what the civilian version left out (the permanent wound).

**[E07-9]. Humor as the thread of normalcy, and the scope limits of the study.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.56]; [p.39]-[p.40]; [p.51])
Gregory says that throughout the war humor was used "as a standpoint of normalcy" connecting wartime life back to what had gone before, and that cartoons were an ephemeral piece of home carried into the war. Method limits Gregory states: the thesis is qualitative and thematic, because thousands of cartoons exist and no quantitative account is possible; it concentrates on single-frame newspaper cartoons, leaving out postcards and posters; much of the Poy material comes from anonymous scrapbooks of clippings without dates, so dating depends on matching to digitized newspapers; there is almost no readership or sales data for the *Bystander*; and Gregory's acknowledgments say their German is limited, so German-language material came through other people's help. A few paragraphs of publication history (*Punch* circulation rising from about 113,000 to nearer 150,000 in 1913-15 and each copy said to be shared among about nine readers; the *Daily Mail* as a "busy man's paper") are background for judging who the audience was.
*Candidate application:* Best treated as a method caution: the cartoon record is the record of what the press chose and could print, not of what soldiers said among themselves.

**[E07-10]. A periodical's own boundary of taste: *Punch* defined itself against "smutty and puerile" joking.** (AC-23, Introduction, [p.47]-[p.48])
Gregory notes that in the 1840s, when most of Victorian society found humor in "smutty and puerile joking," *Punch* deliberately set itself boundaries of respectability, and that respectability was part of the brand that carried it into the war, when it was seen as a "bastion of upper-middle-class ideology." The limit on what could be joked about was therefore also a commercial positioning decision, and it applied to the whole of the war-era *Punch*.
*Candidate application:* Respectable outlets filter the humor that reaches the public; a character who reads only the printed record may think the soldiery was more genteel than it was.

### From Chapter 1, "Policing Humor: DORA, Censorship and Taking Cartoons to Court" (AC-23, [p.57]-[p.123])

*Reading note: the chapter is mainly about home-front press control under the Defense of the Realm Act (DORA, passed 8 August 1914) and how cartoonists drew the Act and the censor. Only the parts that bear on what could and could not be joked about, on soldiers' public image, and on self-censorship are extracted.*

**[E07-11]. The censorship regime never mentioned humor; vagueness bred self-censorship.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.62], [p.64], [p.70]-[p.71], [p.73]-[p.75], [p.83])
Gregory finds no passage in the DORA press rules, in parliamentary statements or in press-committee papers that addresses humor or cartoons as such; the inference offered is that a nation at war did not regard humor as a threat, and that the government treated it as a natural faculty that needed no explicit discouragement, with Victorian ideas of "the right sort of humor" assumed to carry over. The rules instead forbade publishing matter useful to the enemy and, in vague wording, matter "likely to cause disaffection or alarm." The vagueness is read as deliberate (room for punishment), and the working system was guidance notices to editors, marked "private and confidential," replaced and destroyed, plus a Press Bureau staffed mostly by civilians. Most papers policed themselves. Gregory also notes a case of invisible self-censorship: the *Punch* editor reportedly received drawings from contributors that were never seen again, so what was thought inappropriate may simply have been binned and cannot now be assessed.
*Candidate application:* The usable pattern is control by ambiguous rule plus collective self-policing, where the thing never stated is the thing not joked about. What was not printed is unknowable, which is a caution for anyone writing the "official" humor of a faction from its published record.

**[E07-12]. Cartoons got more room than text, because a picture argues two ways at once.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.82]-[p.83], [p.121])
The thesis argues that cartoonists enjoyed more liberty than writers in the same newspapers: Hansard shows debates over newspaper articles but no equivalent about visual material; a cartoon can present more than one side of an argument, so the censor cannot easily tell which side to cut; and the brevity and simplicity that gave cartoons their appeal also helped them avoid the censor (a drawn signpost gives no usable direction). The authorities, on this account, nonetheless let the press "manipulate the censorial controls to their own advantage," while certain pictures were still refused when judged necessary.
*Candidate application:* Ambiguity is a license mechanism: a joke that can be read two ways survives where a plain statement would be cut. A soldier-type character's habitual deniable phrasing has a documented institutional reason.

**[E07-13]. An official war artist was censored for the look of his soldiers and for British dead; the censor's concern was how a British soldier ought to appear.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.76]-[p.78])
Christopher Nevinson, an official war artist (formerly a volunteer ambulance driver), had two paintings censored in 1917-18. The censor objected to one group portrait because the soldiers were "not sufficiently good-looking" and not what was expected of British soldiers; that ban was lifted after discussion on the ground that whether the human form is attractive is subjective and has no place in war art. The second painting, showing dead men caught in barbed wire, was held under a ruling that photographs of dead men were not allowed, and Nevinson argued it was not a photograph. He exhibited it with a paper slip across it reading "Censored," which the national press reproduced; he later recalled being reprimanded at the War Office, ironically because the word "censored" was itself forbidden under DORA. A scholar cited (Michael Walsh) thinks the ban answered Nevinson's earlier published recollections rather than the picture alone, and Gregory says his motive cannot be fixed. This is not a joke case but a boundary case for pictures of the war.
*Candidate application:* The censored object is the soldier's body: ugly faces, and above all the dead of one's own side. A character may remember the line between what the home audience was shown and what was seen, and may regard the official smile as its own kind of lie.

**[E07-14]. A neutral's darker cartoons were allowed, and "British humor" was officially understood as restrained and stoic.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.79]-[p.80])
The Dutch cartoonist Louis Raemaekers drew somber and outraged war cartoons that British papers and publishers ran without censorship. Contemporary justification was that, as a neutral, he could not be expected to produce "British humor," only "international images," and that his pen was driven by "outraged humanity." British humor, by contrast, was held to be restrained and stoic, resting on Victorian and Edwardian moral ideals in which mockery was meant to correct. Gregory adds two qualifications: much of his work was published within the first year of the war or after atrocity headlines, which may explain the laxer treatment; and the dead in his drawings are mostly German, while Allied troops are shown fighting on, which Gregory contrasts with the objection to Nevinson's British dead. (The images discussed concern gas, flame and death; they are not reproduced or described here beyond this.)
*Candidate application:* Permission to be dark can depend on who is speaking (a neutral) and whose bodies are shown (the enemy's). The national self-image of "restraint and stoicism" is a rule that governs which humor counts as ours.

**[E07-15]. The censor as an easy and unaccountable target, mocked as mindless but never savagely.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.96]-[p.105], [p.109])
Cartoons of the censor recur as an old man with pencil and crumpled paper, and the joke is the audience's superiority over his incompetence or mindlessness. Documented examples described: one drawing has a censor cutting "the definite article" because nothing definite may slip through; another shows several censors in succession reducing a war report to nothing; a children's-book image gives the censor a pencil for a head, with a "free press" shown hanging in the background; a cartoonist draws himself submitting work to a panel of censors who are themselves the subjects of his drawings and who cannot take the joke. Gregory reads some of these as tinged with empathy for the censors (the cartoonists and their papers also self-censored, so knew the frustration), notes that none is vicious, and sees two flurries of censor cartoons, in 1915 (setting up the Press Bureau, subjective rulings) and 1917 (new pamphlet and picture rules). Public reception cannot be measured, because readership tallies do not exist; the audience is inferred from the jokes' references (for example, a play on a famous novel's title implies educated, probably female readers). A *Punch* drawing from December 1915 shows a gagged war correspondent being told by a peer and an MP that he should have stayed quiet or joined their ranks and then he could say what he liked, which doubles the target onto the privileged who talk freely.
*Candidate application:* A faction's humor about its own censors may be affectionate contempt that stays inside the bounds the censor allows, and the real safe target is the faceless process, not a named superior. Relevant to the defector: the long habit of mocking the censor can sit alongside compliance.

**[E07-16]. The same control figure changes tone with the phase of the war: from comfort to harridan.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.84]-[p.96])
DORA became a personified figure (a stout, grandmotherly or matronly woman) only late in the war: she is rare before 1917 and common in 1918. Gregory's reading is that when home-front morale was low in March 1918, a comforting maternal figure who takes away the nation's toys and sends it to bed offered relief and acceptance, in contrast to the earlier patriotic mother who sent sons off; after the Armistice the same figure turns into an old harridan blocking progress or a drunk, and cartoons mock her with more freedom, which the thesis ties to relaxed controls (for example the 1921 loosening of alcohol laws) and a post-war backlash. In short, the humor about an institution's restrictions shifted from grudging consent during the war to open mockery afterward.
*Candidate application:* The ex-soldier's jokes about the institution they left may be sharper now than anything they would have said while serving, and for the same reason: distance removed the cost. The reverse pattern (gentle humor under pressure) is the documented in-service one.

**[E07-17]. The "Reported Missing" prosecution: the one cartoon of the soldier taken to court, and what it tells us about the limit.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.113]-[p.120])
In January 1916 the *Bystander* printed a drawing by Second Lieutenant C. E. B. Bernard titled "Reported Missing": a lone private, drunk, with a bottle of rum and a sun helmet, slumped by a tree in an unlocatable dry, rocky country, with a glum face. Gregory notes that the setting might have recalled Gallipoli and that the artist deliberately gave no rank or location. The prosecution, under DORA, called it a "gross libel" on the forces and argued that it prejudiced recruiting and discipline (the Derby scheme was failing and conscription was about to be introduced), that the Foreign Office feared it would demoralize troops and give the enemy a picture of Allied soldiers, and that the title implied absence without authority at a time when the British army was known for harsh discipline, including the death penalty for desertion. The thesis adds three readings: the figure is solitary, so he is outside the comradeship the army promoted; he looks sullen and regretful rather than resolute (the contrast case is Bairnsfather's grumbling men, who are plainly still part of the war); and he is the opposite of the clean, orderly "Tommy" the papers had advertised. The publishers, editor and artist were fined £200 in total (about £8,600 in present money by the thesis's figure), the editor personally £50; the editor had already been dismissed by the owners for views on visual material (they said unrelated to the picture), though he had also promoted a number of Bairnsfather drawings, which were praised for helping morale. The artist, an officer and outside contributor, was not steeped in the paper's internal self-censorship. The case set no legal precedent, was reported widely in national and regional papers, and public reaction to it is not documented.
*Candidate application:* A precise statement of the forbidden joke: not that soldiers drink or grumble, but a soldier shown alone, demoralized, ashamed or idle with no sign of being part of the war, in a medium the enemy and recruits can see. A character might know of such a case and have learned from it what could be said where.

**[E07-18]. Chapter 1's conclusion: cynicism within bounds, grudging consent, and visible exceptions.** (AC-23, Ch.1, [p.120]-[p.123])
Gregory concludes that the press shadowed the rules, mocked the idea of the censor while accepting the need for it, and that by 1919 the authorities agreed the press had been compliant; humor about censors and DORA stayed inside what censorship would allow, which Gregory reads as some self-mockery by the censors' own side. The fact that the humor sold in volume is taken as evidence that the public did not entirely disagree. The evidence of consent is "grudging" rather than enthusiastic, and the rare exceptions (Nevinson, "Reported Missing") show control applied "when deemed necessary."
*Candidate application:* A model for "humor as controlled resistance": a safety valve that is tolerated precisely because it does not exceed certain bounds, and that the institution sometimes polices hard at a few visible points to keep everyone else self-policing.

### From Chapter 2, "Stepping off the Page: Advertisements, Cartoons and Theatrical Performance" (AC-23, [p.124]-[p.196])

*Reading note: I read the chapter introduction ([p.124]-[p.131]), the full "Cartoons into Theater" section on Bairnsfather's "The Better 'Ole" ([p.176]-[p.192]) and the conclusion ([p.192]-[p.196]), and sampled the advertising and theater-in-cartoons sections ([p.138]-[p.143], [p.159]-[p.166], [p.170]-[p.176]). I did not read the rest of those two sections line by line, because their subject (cartoons selling soap and recycling Shakespeare and music-hall acts for the civilian public) is far from soldiers' humor.*

**[E07-19]. The joke mechanism of the most famous soldier cartoon of the war: calm grumbling inside danger.** (AC-23, Ch.2, [p.177]-[p.178])
Gregory describes Bairnsfather's "One of Our Minor Wars" (*Bystander*, 24 November 1915): two scruffy soldiers sit in a shell hole under bursting shells, a rifle on the ground, and one says to the other "Well if you knows of a better 'ole, go to it!" (quoted caption). The danger is visually plain but the faces show annoyance rather than despair. The joke is an incongruity: the suggestion that a better hole can be found when explosions are making new holes all the time and no hole is safe. The characters accept their situation as much as they grumble about it, an attitude Gregory says British soldiers were known for, "even though little could be done to improve it." Gregory links the "sardonic" tone to what is commonplace in trench journals and other military sources, and cautions that the claim that one of the two men is Old Bill is later hindsight, not stated by the artist.
*Candidate application:* A usable template of how a front-line joke works in a single beat: a request for an impossible improvement, delivered in the register of a complaint about service. The humor consists of treating mortal danger as a mere inconvenience of position.

**[E07-20]. The first Bairnsfather drawing was a real event; soldiers' humor carried "cultural authority" with the public.** (AC-23, Ch.2, [p.179]-[p.181])
Bairnsfather's first submission to the *Bystander* (January 1915) showed soldiers in a dugout asking "where did that one go to?" It was a recorded event: he and a group returned to a barn for safety and a shell took away one of its doors, and the men reportedly asked just that. Gregory argues that at home soldiers were thought to hold "cultural authority" as first-hand witnesses, that locating soldiers in a cartoon implied they were really there, and that Bairnsfather being a serving soldier amplified this; in a foreword he said only that he recorded his times out there "in the way that they appeared" to him, which the home audience took as authority. After the first drawing the paper wrote to him in France asking for more and sent a check for two pounds; "One of Our Minor Wars" was drawn quickly, thought little of by him, and ran in a Christmas issue that sold out; he later drew crowds at charity events and, after discharge, at lectures and "chalk and talk" shows. The thesis notes that nothing in his notebooks or companions' recollections shows he expected the response.
*Candidate application:* Front-line humor can start as a repeated remark from one real moment, and civilian audiences may then treat it as testimony, which changes what the originator can later say. A character may resent or distrust anyone who monetized that kind of spontaneous joke.

**[E07-21]. Documented soldier jokes in the Bairnsfather stage version: leave, the length of service, jam, and tobacco.** (AC-23, Ch.2, [p.182]-[p.188])
Gregory lists items from the 1917 play *The Better 'Ole* (by Bairnsfather and Arthur Elliot) that were lifted from his cartoons. (a) Leave: the idea that "leave" is a figment of the soldier's imagination; the cartoons include leave drawn as a short-lived joy, as a jump for joy, and as a leave train on which the soldier ages before reaching home; the thesis notes soldiers vastly desired leave. (b) Length of service: a stage exchange where one man asks another how long he is "up for"; "Seven years," says the second; "Lucky devil," says the first, "I'm duration!" (the wording is quoted from the play script); the point is that a regular's fixed term now looks enviable to a man who enlisted for the war's duration, which regulars and men on leave would grasp. (c) Jam: a cover cartoon of Old Bill asking when it is going to be strawberry, which became a song in the play (the ordinary soldier gets apple and plum while a supply corps gets strawberry jam and rum); Gregory notes there is no direct complaint against officers, just irreverent grumbling about their jam, and reads this as consistent with Alexander Watson's argument that relations between men and officers were strong. (d) Tobacco: a sergeant major puts down a box under bombardment, lights his pipe and calls "'Arf a mo' Kaiser," quoting a tobacco-fund cartoon by Bert Thomas that audiences knew; smoking is named a "sensible relief" and cigarettes a form of currency.
*Candidate application:* The whole catalog is gripes about duration, leave, food and perks, directed at the system and the rear echelons rather than at named superiors, and delivered as a running set of shared in-jokes. A faction's humor could be modeled on three or four such recurring complaints that need only be named to be funny.

**[E07-22]. Reception: how the play was received, and what is and is not documented about soldier audiences.** (AC-23, Ch.2, [p.189]-[p.192])
*The Better 'Ole* ran 817 performances at the Oxford Theater in London, earned almost three thousand pounds in its first week, and toured to the United States, Australia and Europe. *The New York Times* said Bairnsfather's humor was offered "for the comfort of the soldiers" and called the play "the real thing"; British reviews praised "humor and pathos" and the *Play Pictorial* compared Old Bill to Falstaff and Dogberry. Gregory notes that few reviews describe the audience's behavior, though some mention encores and cheering. Gregory observes that soldiers on leave sought out theaters, that there was also negative reaction to civilians attending, and that the play's pathos was "without malice or vindictiveness." The soldiers in the play interact with French café life, which Gregory links to Sassoon's "Blighty Hunger" (a hunger for something normal from home). Criticism of theater in wartime came from the Bishop of London and from Smith-Dorrien (given in the thesis as "Colonel"; 1916), who held that frivolous entertainment was unsuitable "in these crucial times" and could corrupt working men turned soldiers; against that, Lena Ashwell's concert parties went to the front and seats were reserved for recovering soldiers.
*Candidate application:* The documented soldier response is evidence of comfort and recognition, but direct evidence of soldiers' own reactions is thin; the thesis relies on reviews and impresarios. A senior officer's disapproval of laughter in a crucial time is an easy-to-model institutional stance against the morale value the troops themselves wanted.

**[E07-23]. The chapter's wider claim: wartime humor kept going because it was a necessity, and it moved across media.** (AC-23, Ch.2, [p.124], [p.125]-[p.132], [p.192]-[p.196])
Gregory states that theaters and the daily cartoon stayed open and popular despite moral objections, that war plays proper were a small share of stage output (mostly spy plays and recruiting plays), and that music hall retained a working-class communal character. The conclusion says entertainment "needed to be 'kept going'" for both civilian and military morale, particularly for the wounded, and that cartoonists reused a common "armory" of recognizable characters and story shapes (theater plots, familiar proverbs, product slogans) so that half the joke is already told when the audience recognizes the borrowed frame. A side finding: cartoonists inserted the Kaiser into existing advertisements to turn an unfunny image into a joke about a diminished enemy. Two smaller items from the sections I sampled ([p.138]-[p.142], [p.172]-[p.176]): *Punch* advertised itself with the claim that "every soldier needed a laugh" (with pictures of laughing soldiers), though no sales figures for the trenches exist; and a 1918 review of a popular stage revue noted that a character was absent and had gone the way of so many others, "pushing up daisies in Flanders," a passing euphemism that Gregory says would carry contemporary pathos for the audience (a 1916 newspaper headline, "The Wounded and the Bing Boys," pairs wounded soldiers with the same show).
*Candidate application:* A reminder that much "soldier humor" is borrowed frame plus new target; a character's jokes will often be recycled jingles and slogans bent to the situation.

### From Chapter 3, "Avoiding Obligations: Profiteers, Pacifists and the Wealthy" (AC-23, [p.197]-[p.252])

*Reading note: I read the chapter introduction ([p.197]-[p.205]) and the conclusion ([p.247]-[p.251]) only. The body sections on profiteers, the wealthy and irresponsible, and pacifists and cowards are civilian-on-civilian ridicule and were not read line by line.*

**[E07-24]. Humor of correction aimed at the people who "avoid their duty," with the soldier as the unstated standard.** (AC-23, Ch.3, [p.197]-[p.205], [p.248]-[p.251])
Gregory's chapter argues that the wartime cartoon targets at home were the profiteer (new money made from the war), the wealthy and irresponsible (old money that "missed the purpose" of sacrifice), and the pacifist and the coward, all treated as a kind of "enemy within" who failed the "duty of sacrifice." The humor is "exaggeration for correction," in the moralizing Victorian tradition: show the wrong behavior so the public learns not to do it. The soldier appears as the contrast: the pacifist is drawn as the reverse of the "staunch, pragmatic Tommy who sacrificed all for war." The same method works at different levels of savagery: the profiteer is nearly demonized (above all by the socialist cartoonist Dyson, whose profiteer pictures moralize more than they amuse), the wealthy are mocked with some respect retained, and pacifists are shown as pathetic shirkers, which the thesis says left no room for conscientious objection (and it notes recent historians have shown the good work conscientious objectors did, none visible in the cartoons). The pacifist and the coward are also run together in the humor, and the gendering of that ridicule is blunt (described only in general terms here).
*Candidate application:* The humor that defines a soldier's own role in civilian eyes is often built by ridiculing the one who did not go. A veteran character may know that a "support for the troops" culture is partly made of contempt for someone else, and may or may not share it.

**[E07-25]. A cartoon coward who gave the language a word: the rabbit and the "funk hole."** (AC-23, Ch.3, [p.204]-[p.205], [p.250]-[p.251])
Poy's character Cuthbert the rabbit (from late 1916) was a young shirker who sends his father, grandfather or children off to the war while he hides in "funk holes"; by the war's end "Cuthbert" was a byword for cowardice, yet he was also sold as a children's toy in October 1917 (the Women's Institute made them). Gregory notes the rabbit allowed the cartoonist to avoid some human morality, and that the "funk hole" also served as a jibe at politicians who found their own funk holes in Parliament. The thesis says this expresses a desire that "so many" felt to hide underground, while pointing out the poor choice.
*Candidate application:* Soldier slang (the funk hole as a place of hiding) crosses to the home press and is turned into a character; a veteran might notice that their own word for a fear everyone shared has been converted to an insult for others.

**[E07-26]. The limits stated for subversion: humor touches authority's lines but does not cross them.** (AC-23, Ch.3, [p.197]-[p.200])
The chapter opens with a general limit that Gregory carries through the thesis: most wartime cartoons give "light forms of subversion," and "do not generally cross the lines of authority." Gregory suggests the "British sense of humor" lets a cartoonist touch those lines without reprimand, and that papers worked alongside the government, not against it; the only exception given is the "Reported Missing" prosecution (see E07-17). Gregory draws on a Berlin study (Jan Rüger) for the idea that open criticism of authority was ill-advised, whereas silent impersonation was ambiguous enough to be read differently by different audiences. Gregory also notes that cartoonists' commission records mostly do not survive, so it is unknowable whether a target was the cartoonist's choice or the paper's.
*Candidate application:* The documented rule of license is "play at the edge, never over it," and ambiguity (not stating) is the working method. A faction under a central authority could be modeled on the same pattern.

### From Chapter 4, "Blurring the Lines: Humor between Frontline and Home Front" (AC-23, [p.255]-[p.339])

*Reading note: Chapter 4 runs [p.255]-[p.339] (printed 232-316). Its sections are the introduction ([p.255]-[p.262]), "Images of the Military from Home" ([p.262]-[p.297]), "Military Images from the Front" ([p.298]-[p.317]), "The Unifying Factor" ([p.317]-[p.335]) and a conclusion ([p.336]-[p.338]). I read the entire chapter line by line. Findings are grouped by section.*

#### Chapter introduction and "Images of the Military from Home"

**[E07-27]. The chapter's design and the alienation-versus-continuity question.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.255]-[p.262])
Gregory sets the question: older scholarship (from the 1970s, drawing on war memoirs and poetry of futility) described soldiers as "alienated" from civilians; newer work (letters, diaries, family archives, for example on Liverpool territorials who asked families for gifts and necessities) shows closeness. The thesis tests the two by comparing how civilian cartoonists draw the soldier with how soldiers draw themselves (trench journals, letters, diaries), using the humor itself as evidence. Three working distinctions: (a) the public "hero, victim or villain" images of the soldier, with Madigan's argument that courage was redefined from knightly ideals to "sticking it"; (b) jokes shared with family versus "internalized" jokes only comprehensible in the trenches; (c) for civilians the "them" are the enemy, but for soldiers the divide is between those who are there and those at home in the strongest sense, and, to a lesser degree, the divides of station and rank. Gregory notes the popular belief that soldier humor was darker, but that this "is not apparent in the cartoons of soldiers"; there is acceptance of the realities of war that can look darker than civilian humor, "but even here, there remains an element of continuity."
*Candidate application:* A useful two-axis model for any soldier-type character: which jokes are family-safe translations and which are only legible to people who were there, and where the character places the "them" (people who were not there, or officers).

**[E07-28]. How civilian cartoonists drew the soldier, and where their jokes came from: the knight, the amateur, the changed boy.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.262]-[p.283])
Early-war government and press images presented the soldier as uniform, fit, clean, flattering to a nation of equals ("clean-looking fellows," *The Times*, 1914) and compared him with medieval knights (a 1915 drawing casts Lloyd George as a knight leaving a castle to join "Kitchener's Army," with a maiden's favor tied to his arm). Humor in domestic cartoons mostly comes from breaking that uniformity: the new, untrained wartime volunteer is the butt (a drawing from February 1915 shows a man trussed in his own puttees, who looks wounded or captured until the caption reveals he is only a recruit; the thesis notes the layers of meaning: pity first, then relief). Diptychs of "before enlistment, after enlistment" show a small, deferential office boy or suitor becoming a large, authoritative soldier before whom the employer shrinks or the girlfriend leans in, and Gregory reads them as showing the soldier's raised public status (no longer the "scum of the earth" of a century before). A mother-sends-her-son drawing carries a caption reminding him to see that the sheets are "properly aired"; Gregory says it gains humor with hindsight, given how damp and poor trench accommodation was, and that its comfort for readers lies in the unchanged mother-son relation. Cartoons tended not to look past the war's end: few show the soldier after the war.
*Candidate application:* The home-front soldier-image has a stock template (knight, child-soldier, changed boy) against which real soldiers' humor can be written as a deliberate puncture. A character might be irritated by or play along with the airing-the-sheets kind of well-meant ignorance.

**[E07-29]. Humor sent to the troops: *Punch* ads, *Blighty* and *Khaki*, and what civilians thought soldiers needed.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.270]-[p.273], [p.302]-[p.303], [p.313]-[p.314])
Civilians were urged by *The Times* advertisements to buy *Punch* and similar cartoon journals and send them to the trenches; the thesis reads this as either real belief in their morale value or a good ad campaign, and as a way to send a "memory of home." *Blighty* (from 1916, "a budget of humor from home") was a free weekly sent to the Navy and the British Expeditionary Force, patronized by a field marshal and two admirals; it reprinted cartoons from *Punch*, *London Opinion*, the *Bystander* and others, added soldiers' own cartoons, and carried ads (tobacco, gramophone records, Quaker Oats, lemonade, toffee, Lipton's tea, and, for officers, watches, kits and hair cream), which Gregory reads as a sign that the home front's picture of what soldiers wanted was shaped by class. *Khaki* (1915) grew from a domestic philanthropy and said its aim was to cheer and be a companion to the fighting forces. Soldiers in turn traced and copied published cartoons (a soldier-cartoonist reproduced Jack Walker's "The Cure," and a naval man copied Bairnsfather), which Gregory takes as evidence of a shared appreciation and a unified sense of humor between front and home.
*Candidate application:* There is a loop: home sends humor out, soldiers recycle it back. A character might remember receiving a bundle of cheerful periodicals sent by well-wishers and the mixed gratitude and irritation that came with them.

**[E07-30]. Enemy humor: the reversed insult ("contemptible little army") and the sport frame of "playing the game."** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.283]-[p.290])
Home cartoons drew the German soldier as short, round, aging, hunched, running from a single British soldier, or absurdly rewarded and punished by the Kaiser for the wrong things (in the thesis's description of one 1915 drawing, rewards go to those who committed atrocities against civilians and punishment to those who missed their targets; the description is summarized here and the details are not reproduced). Gregory identifies several mechanisms: incongruity, the audience's sense of superiority, and inversion (the Crown Prince held up as "an example" while notorious for his behavior). A recurring joke is the reversal of the phrase about the "contemptible little army": a caption has the Kaiser's soldiers "walk over" the "contemptible little British Army," and the picture shows the opposite. Gregory notes, via Fussell, that the phrase was devised in the War Office and "fathered upon the Kaiser," that it was meant as rally material, and that the BEF took it up and called themselves the "Old Contemptibles" comically, so the soldiers themselves used the insult as a badge. Another vein is the sport frame: in a *Daily Mirror* strip the two German princes complain that the Allies "don't play the game properly," which a British reader takes in reverse (it is the Germans who are unsporting); Gregory links this to a widespread public idea of war as a game with sportsmanship, used as a recruiting tool too.
*Candidate application:* Two usable mechanisms: appropriation of an enemy (or command's) insult as a unit's name, and ridicule of the enemy as cowardly or unfit to protect the in-group from fear. The sport frame explains how an army can joke about war as a game without being callous.

**[E07-31]. What the home front would and would not joke about regarding the wounded.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.290]-[p.297])
In domestic cartoons the wounds shown are bandaged arms and legs, temporary and manageable, and they "reaffirm the soldier's heroic persona"; there is no sense of the discharge-earning "blighty wound," nor that it was wanted. Shell shock does not appear because it is a mental state not easily drawn. Gregory says severe wounds such as those from gas (citing the painter John Singer Sargent's *Gassed*) "did not naturally provoke humor" because they touched too closely on melancholy that is difficult to laugh at; hospitals also wanted the visibility of the wounded limited, and blindness had been newly reframed from a beggar's affliction to a hero's sacrifice. When the wounded appear in cartoons they tend to be foolish rather than heroic, with the degree of foolishness tied to the date. Examples described: an October 1914 drawing in which a woman accuses a man with a wooden leg of being a shirker (the joke is mainly in the caption and falls on her lack of observation; it reflects the white-feather campaigns); a March 1915 drawing in which a man in uniform, asked how he was wounded, turns out to have been in a squabble over a shirt (soldiers remain "just men" and the wounded man is laughed at "as well as with"); and a 1915-16 *Daily Mirror* series in which a well-meaning middle-class girl carries wounded soldiers in a motorcycle sidecar, to their remark that they were "safer in France." Gregory sees the soldiers' stoicism ("old contemptible" gumption) as the through-line.
*Candidate application:* The line the civilian public drew was between fixable and unfixable injury and between body and mind; the unfixable and the psychological went undrawn. A veteran with a lasting injury could feel that the culture's jokes about "wounded Tommy" are about someone they are not.

#### "Military Images from the Front"

**[E07-32]. Soldiers' own drawings of the wounded: glum faces, lasting wounds, and a joke that stays a joke.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.298]-[p.303])
Corporal George Harry Southgate (17th London Regiment) kept a scrapbook of sketches made in northern France in 1915 (the intended audience is unclear, possibly himself). In "The Visitor," a visitor tells a glum bandaged soldier they are pleased to see him "looking happy": the irony of caption against face is clear and the soldier himself shows no humor. In "What! You Don't Know Me" a man with head bandage and missing fingers holds out his hand, surprised not to be recognized, while the other man is in full uniform; Gregory notes the ambiguity (the bandage may be temporary) and that facial wounds appear in the soldiers' drawings but not in home-front ones. Gregory adds that the French coined their own phrase for badly facially wounded men, which is outside the thesis's scope. Trench-journal images of the wounded show "far more acceptance in visualizing lasting wounds" than home-front artists, yet "do not lose their humor": they "openly acknowledge the problems of war, and simultaneously reduce its impact." Two 1915 examples from *Khaki* (a philanthropic periodical for the troops) are summarized: one has an amputee ask a companion why he does not use his crutches in the manner of a gentleman driving a pair (a class joke, with the second man needing only one); the other has a man in a wheelchair, asked whether bullets or shells took his legs, answer that it was a tram, which Gregory reads as an improbable, casual acceptance. (Described in summary terms; the injury wording is not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* A soldier's joke about a lasting injury deflates the glory of its cause (the anticlimactic cause) or the cheerful-visitor delusion, and does so inside the circle of those who share the injury. The wounded soldier laughing at civilian cheerfulness is the documented stance.

**[E07-33]. What the trench journals show of soldiers' humor: coping by distancing, cheer as the belief that "pulls us through," boredom, and regimental specificity.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.303]-[p.307])
Gregory restates the received picture: soldier humor today is imagined as darker than the evidence suggests (partly through twentieth-century television and film and as a predominantly British picture), making laughter out of everything unpleasant, and "the worse it was the more they laughed" (Fussell). Gregory notes that officers of the time acknowledged the prevalence of front-line humor among British troops, and that Khaki Opinion ran pieces such as "The Laughing Army." Points Gregory draws from secondary sources and journals: humor as a means of distancing from unpleasant realities (Le Naour); Cockney soldiers' belief, recorded by Robertshaw, that it was "only us keepin' so ruddy cheerful as pulls us through"; endless monotony turning soldiers to poems and columns just to stay amused; much journal humor revolving around sport and music hall, trying to keep a sense of the "British way of life"; boredom and lack of information breeding rumor and fiction, "the human impulse to make fictions"; journals as a map of soldiers' "mental landscape"; regimental specificity (horse jokes would not suit an infantry or artillery paper but cavalry jokes would, so the shared war experience across ranks also separates regiments); the "them and us" present in journal cartoons; and the act of writing for the journal as cathartic for the writer, with circulation bolstering morale and unifying whole regiments. Journals were often hospital, convalescent or home-printed, which made pictures easier; the *Wipers Times* is said to carry only a few cartoons; French trench journals were full of cartoons.
*Candidate application:* For a cohort-specific, unit-specific character, the humor should be hyper-local (arm of service, regiment) and should start in boredom, with a shared fiction-making habit. The belief that the men's own cheerfulness is what pulls them through is a recorded in-group story, and a character may still hold it years later while knowing it was one.

**[E07-34]. Soldiers' cartoons about themselves: incongruity plus empathy, and the working man's initiative.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.307]-[p.310])
Two documented items. A 1917 *Mudhook* cartoon, "Suitably Camouflaged," shows an unclothed soldier observed by two young Dutch girls, wearing camouflage underwear; Gregory reads the viewer soldiers as invited to feel superiority over the man caught undressed, but also empathy for being abroad and out of the comforts of normality (sexual content only mild; summarized). "Pipsqueak's Page" in the *Lead Swinger* (a field ambulance journal, April 1916) draws questions as pictures (for example how a girl of fourteen becomes forty-four in the home front; how a particular harness is supposed to be worn) and several portraits of soldiers as men of initiative: using water supplies for the guns, hauling a bath's worth of water to an upturned howitzer to warm it, and one man proudly beside the makeshift hammock home he built. Gregory ties this to working-class manual ingenuity and cites Adrian Gregory's claim that the war was in a sense the "salvation" of the working classes, who were needed and given purpose and social standing.
*Candidate application:* A pattern of soldiers' pride in improvisation, jokes with a "look what we made of it" tone, as opposed to jokes about suffering. A character with an engineering or trades background could characteristically turn hardship into a made object.

**[E07-35]. The same man draws differently for different recipients: letters home, diary maps, and the child's letter.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.310]-[p.317])
Soldiers' letters were censored and many followed set formulas, but some men wrote more freely and drew. Southgate's letter drawings show home scenes (cyclists on a pavement), which Gregory reads as continuity, memory of home, and leisure drawing by a practiced amateur, though reciprocal letters are lost so intent cannot be proved. Diaries and maps use depersonalized images (crowds without faces, or labeled terrain), not humorous, and may have served as personal reminders or information for superiors. Canon Lomax's sketches show soldiers at rest, with camaraderie rather than laughter. Captain Henry Lamb (a battalion medical officer) drew illustrated letters to his young nephew in 1918, including a self-portrait of a man squatting to read his nephew's letter while bombs burst around, with a face of annoyance and acceptance, and he grumbled in the text about having no trenches and having to be as comfortable as one can behind "any old bank." Gregory notes that stronger humor is justified by the child recipient.
*Candidate application:* The strongest documented choice is that soldiers tuned humor to the recipient (a child gets comic annoyance at danger; a diary gets maps; a family gets home scenes). A character may have several registers (the one for the child, the one for the diary, the one for comrades) and may find that the last has no listener now.


#### "The Unifying Factor" and the Chapter 4 conclusion

**[E07-36]. A sergeant's cartoon in *Blighty*: mocking the officer-class slogan with the least tidy man in the picture.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.319]-[p.321])
*Blighty* ran a regular double-page "from the fleet and the trenches" and, on 18 October 1916, printed a drawing by Sergeant R. S. Reid. A sergeant berates an unshaven soldier in a smartly turned-out troop of Australian soldiers (the faces of the others look uncertain); the man says he was on duty, and the sergeant says he should "never let yer dooty hinterfere with yer personality" (spelling per thesis caption). The drawing answers a remark by an unnamed "military critic" that personality is one of the most important qualifications for an officer. Gregory explains the mechanism: the sergeant borrows an officer's standard to scold his men, while being himself the worst-fitting, least tidy figure in the group; the humor is incongruity, with the quibble over shaving set against "bigger issues of policy." Gregory also notes that it was understood by British and dominion readers alike, and uses it as evidence that divides of rank and regiment ("them and us") were present in soldiers' humor and "never overcome completely by shared trench experiences."
*Candidate application:* A model for humor aimed at upward-class cant by a middle-rank speaker who is compromised in the same respect as the men scolded, with the target being a quoted officer remark, not an officer in person. The non-commissioned layer as the humor's home.

**[E07-37]. Humor across the lines: cartoons thrown into enemy trenches under "live and let live," and the weak role of the enemy as a joke target for soldiers.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.323]-[p.325]; [p.337])
Gregory reports (from a newspaper's own account of January 1915 and from secondary scholarship) that British soldiers threw copies of Haselden's "Big and Little Willie" book into opposing trenches, sometimes to annoy but more often at the enemy's request under the live-and-let-live system; Nicholas Hiley is cited for the thought that for German soldiers this may have been the nearest they came to their remote monarchy. Soldiers also threw across cartoons of a comic spy character to provoke. Yet Gregory notes that soldiers' own cartoons show far less separation of "them" (enemy) from "us" than home-front cartoons do, and that contemporaries remarked the British laugh at the enemy "but more so they laugh at themselves." Elliot's group-consciousness theory is used to suggest a "greater esprit de corps" in the trenches that overrode oppositions and an artificial union created by location and distance from home, with the enemy a partner in that location. Gregory concedes this reading cuts both ways: it can be taken as a larger divide of understanding between home and front, which is what the alienation thesis claims.
*Candidate application:* The enemy is the least prominent target in soldiers' own humor in this evidence; the targets are self, rank, regiment, rear echelons. A character who stopped hating the enemy first and the institution second has a documented pattern behind it.

**[E07-38]. Leave without reintegration: why the humor matched at front and home, and what returned soldiers said about being misunderstood.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.317]-[p.318], [p.325], [p.329]-[p.330])
Gregory applies Van Gennep's rites of passage: soldiers went through rites of transition and separation when they left civilian life and rites of incorporation into the army, but because men could return on leave without any formal reintegration, "complete separation and formal reintegration often never happened," which Gregory offers as part of why war humor is matched at home and front. Gregory cites Fussell for soldiers' sense of transformation (rebirth or conversion) and notes that "civilian selves who went out to fight often did not come back as the same men." The counter-evidence Gregory admits is soldiers' later memoirs and wartime letters speaking of false empathy and a distinct lack of civilian understanding, "often humor was misunderstood in this manner also." For many soldiers, plays like *The Better 'Ole* were among the few ways civilians could glimpse what it was like and the humor soldiers applied to it.
*Candidate application:* A direct statement of the civilian-facing problem: the humor translated, but the experience behind it did not, and the veteran's sense of being misunderstood is documented from the war years, not only after.

**[E07-39]. Bairnsfather's soldiers: understatement, domestic calm under fire, and the artist who staged his own "atmosphere" on leave.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.326]-[p.331])
Gregory states that many of Bairnsfather's drawings were actually produced at home or in military hospitals, not at the front, though inspired by it. Gregory describes the style: the "everyman," "boy next door" soldier replaces the "elevated public school officer" and the soldier seen as emotionless and "almost robotic" in earlier propaganda; his men are shown on active service, bored and uncomfortable, longing for leave rather than enjoying it, and idle when the chance arises (idleness being a misnomer in trench warfare, where the waiting was long). Documented examples: "The Innocent Abroad," in which a novice sitting in a box in the rain says it was an "alright" night but he "had to get out and rest a bit now and again," treating a dire situation almost as a holiday; "Coiffure in the Trenches," in which a barber with a shell flying overhead tells his seated customer to keep his head still or he will "have yer blinkin' ear off," so that homely calm is set against danger; "Observation," in which soldiers watch the enemy to see what he is eating; and "Getting the Local Color," a leave-time self-parody in which the soldier-artist sits reading while staff produce wind (bellows), rain (a hose) and bombs (a drum) to recreate the "atmosphere" for a good "Fragment," the gardener and children visibly bored. Gregory says other armies particularly noted the British for "almost casually ironic humor" that served to distance them from the situation.
*Candidate application:* The recorded markers of the style are understatement ("alright night"), the intrusion of domestic routine, and a refusal to dramatize. The leave-time self-parody is also a record of a soldier-artist who already treated front-line stories as material to be staged.
*Cross-use:* The thesis's line about earlier public images of soldiers as "emotionless, almost robotic" and then restored as human is a ready human-side precedent for how a public image of soldier-machines might be softened by a joker who is one of them.

**[E07-40]. Soldiers' myths, and the named-shell superstition used for two opposite jokes.** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.332]-[p.335]; also Ch.1 [p.68])
Gregory notes that myth-making was an "easy form of trench entertainment" when truth and news were scarce, and that myths passed between soldiers and civilians through letters and the press; the best ones (the "contemptible little army" tale) merged with public perception and fed cartoons. Atrocity myths feed cartoons too, but "there is less humor" in them. The fear that a bullet or shell bears your name appears in memoirs; Bairnsfather's "My Dream for Years to Come" shows a sleeping soldier dreaming of a shell heading straight for him, which would have "resonated strongly with soldiers" and "serves to make light of such worries." Home-front cartoonists inverted the superstition: Jack Walker's "super-gun" fires shells labeled with the Kaiser's hate, malice and envy, which are harmless, and Poy labeled shells with the letters of the Kaiser's name (compare the shells "for William" in the munitions cartoon, Ch.1), so that the named-shell fear is turned into a threat from the home side.
*Candidate application:* A superstition about being singled out by a projectile can be both a frightened soldier's joke and a civilian propagandist's inversion; the same trope belongs to different speakers.

**[E07-41]. Chapter 4's conclusion: continuity with a margin, wounds as the margin, and humor as "succour and relief."** (AC-23, Ch.4, [p.336]-[p.338])
Gregory's own summary: cartoonists at home and in the trenches influenced each other "back and forth"; the domestic picture is a civilian perspective, because the home media were the main supplier of civilians' understanding of the army; the volume of trench journals shows "some subtleties of difference," chiefly about the wounded. Domestic cartoons use mostly temporary and often not war-caused wounds, whereas soldiers allow permanent handicaps such as missing limbs, accepted through humor "to alleviate the trauma," and no evidence exists of facial disfigurement being joked about, "perhaps that this form of wound was beyond a joke" (the thesis's phrasing). In soldiers' cartoons the enemy is a much smaller target than in home cartoons, and soldiers draw on the normal from home "as a form of comfort." Gregory's closing line for the chapter: humor was "succour and relief," making links to home provides continuity, and highlighting differences "to make fun of them" makes the differences easier to manage for both populations.
*Candidate application:* A usable boundary map of one army and era: limb loss joked about by the wounded among themselves, facial disfigurement not, mental breakdown not drawn. The unspoken third category (shell shock) went unjoked in the drawn record.

### From Chapter 5, "Reconfiguring Humor: Cartoon Memories Before, During and After War" (AC-23, [p.339]-[p.408])

*Reading note: I read the whole chapter (introduction, "Victorian and Edwardian Humor," "Wartime Developments," "Post-War Memories," conclusion). Much of it is about cartoonists reusing earlier art and about the author's concept of "commercial memory" (cartoons sold with the intention of being remembered); only the parts about limits, time course, soldiers' reception and the afterlife of soldier humor are extracted.*

**[E07-42]. The Victorian unwritten rules about what to joke about, and the claim that the trenches broke them.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.356]-[p.357])
Gregory says that for the Victorians there was no judgment against humor as such: making fun of serious things was expected "so long as it was not spiteful," and a lack of fun was a waste of time. Informal limits nonetheless existed: no jokes about cannibalism or other things "improper or inhuman"; regret about obscenity; reprimand of rude practical jokes; laughter directed away from important topics such as religion. The thesis then asserts that when war came in 1914, "many of the class divides that had implemented these unwritten rules of humor were broken in the trenches": the grotesque could now be laughed about, and rude practical jokes, which "kept men on their feet," became far more commonplace among troops. The evidence cited is a 1966 article on Victorian laughter, with no soldier source named for the trench claim.
*Candidate application:* A cultural rule-set of what may be joked about, enforced by class and respectability, which loosens under front-line conditions. A soldier-type character can be shown crossing a civilian taboo without noticing it, and a civilian who finds it shocking. The claim is plausible but thinly sourced in this thesis (see Gaps).

**[E07-43]. In-group clarity, out-group insult: the principle behind harsh caricature.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.349]-[p.350])
In discussing Victorian caricature, Gregory notes that sharply critical caricature of groups (the thesis cites depictions of the Irish with ape-like features and the use of the same iconography against Darwin) could be seen as humorous or not "depending on the audience's perspective": "For those in the group making the jokes then the humor was clear, whereas for those outside, there was only insult." (The ethnic caricature is described here only in general terms.)
*Candidate application:* A short rule of license: the same joke is humor from inside the joking group and insult from outside, which explains why a soldier's joke can be unrepeatable to a civilian and why the same material harms when told by the wrong person.

**[E07-44]. Time course within the war: little humor at first, more as the war settled, and humor as a way of "killing the fear."** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.367]-[p.371])
Gregory says that at the start of the war little humor was made of the war itself; recruitment and patriotic allegory dominated, and even *Punch* and Raemaekers drew somber pictures of departing soldiers. As the war went on there was "more opportunity to create humor out of situations of war," shown especially by Bairnsfather's "illustrations of survival in the worst of circumstances," and by 1917 (conscription introduced) cartoons addressed more specific worries; an example is a Bairnsfather drawing of men in a flooded trench under a submarine-scare title, which Gregory reads as a mixture of incongruity and relief (relief at being alive despite the picture; incongruity of a submarine-danger joke in a trench). On whether humor is a "weapon," Gregory remarks that beside tanks and guns it looks "artificial," but that cartoon and caricature could "symbolically kill" by exposing absurdity, and that "killing the fear" strengthened the memory positively: looking back, soldiers "were able to consider the humor of the situation ironically, rather than focusing on the dread." A related mechanism on the home front: the powerful figure is made small (the Kaiser rained on by a cartoonist's ink; the Kaiser's iron fist drawn with a nail through it; the prime minister drawn progressively smaller as his standing fell), so that "any power he might have had is negated."
*Candidate application:* A usable model for a character's arc: early-service humor is thin and patriotic, mid-service humor becomes the working tool, and later humor is retrospective irony that edits the dread out. The "make the threat small" move (shrinking by drawing) is a documented fear-reduction technique for the enemy and for one's own leaders.

**[E07-45]. The afterlife of one soldier joke: the "better 'ole" motif, reused by its author, by others, and eventually on the moon.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.388]-[p.396])
Bairnsfather himself reissued "One of Our Minor Wars" with variations: after being sent to meet American troops (whom he found "jolly good men"), he drew Old Bill with an American soldier, no rifle but a bottle in the mud, and the caption that they were "both in the same 'ole now"; in 1918 volumes "Anglo-Saxon" and "The Point of View" put men of other nations into the hole with Old Bill, sharing a cigarette or rolling their eyes while explosions continue. The pattern: always two men in the hole, explosions behind, Old Bill gruffly all-right, the newcomer fearful or arrogant, the mood resignation. Poy's 1917 adaptation shows the coward Cuthbert's family in beds labeled for government departments in an underground funk hole. Later cartoonists (Low, Strube and others) reused the "ole," placing it on the moon in 1967 (a probe as armament) and, in 1984, putting two political leaders in a bleak war setting where they look wrong for it. Gregory's point is that the humor stays "resignation" ("they are not in a good place, but there are certainly worse situations around them"), that the war context can fall away while the motif persists, and that every reuse recreates the memory for a new audience.
*Candidate application:* A joke built around resigned acceptance of an unfixable situation outlives its occasion; the motif of "two men in a hole, one calm, one not" is a usable social template (the calm veteran and the new arrival).

**[E07-46]. The commercial and civilian afterlife of the soldier-joker: souvenirs, a waxwork, a pub, and the myth that he was real.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.397]-[p.402])
In the 1920s and 1930s Bairnsfather cartoons were printed on crockery marked "A Souvenir of the Great War," and Old Bill appeared on toys, car mascots and playing cards, priced from pennies to a pound or more; Madame Tussaud's waxwork of 1932, "The Man Who Won the War," was described in its guide as a tribute to "countless obscure heroes of the trenches who 'stuck it out'" (quoted from the exhibition catalog), and in Gregory's gloss to heroes who cheerfully "joked in the face of death"; and a pub named for Old Bill stands near the Menin Gate in Ypres, where war tourists gather. Old Bill was, like a Victorian comic character, believed by many to be real. Gregory reads this as a "commercial memory" in which every viewer could place a father, son or husband in the hole.
*Candidate application:* The soldier's gallows humor gets converted by the civilian public into an affectionate commemorative figure (heroes who cheerfully "joked in the face of death"); that sanitized version is the gap between how the veteran remembers it and how it is sold.

**[E07-47]. Reception among soldiers and officers, over time.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.356], [p.367], [p.403])
The thesis says Bairnsfather's humor was recognized by French and American armies as a way to build stronger morale, and that he was called up again in the Second World War as an "officer cartoonist." On the army's attitude, Gregory reports that some loved and some despised his work, "particularly among the military," but that generally they appreciated his abilities and merely complained about his choice of content at a time when other ideas were being promoted by the army itself. His cartoons continue to be read as carrying the "truth" of trench life and of recognized war humor and resilience, despite the later (1960s) turn to a futility narrative. Gregory also says "many soldiers saw cartoons positively as a means of fighting on."
*Candidate application:* A realistic split in the troops' reception of a celebrated "voice of the trenches": admiration alongside irritation that it diverts from the official message, and later canonization against the grain of the dominant bleak narrative.

**[E07-48]. The post-war image: Old Bill in an armchair, alone.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.403]-[p.404])
Bairnsfather's "Once Upon a Time" (*Bystander*, Christmas 1918) shows Old Bill seated after the war in an armchair with a cup of tea and the memories of the trenches pictured beside him. Gregory reads the humor as maintained but with "more pathos, and almost sadness," a "distanced vulnerability" that Gregory says would be reflected in many returned soldiers; Bill, who rarely smiled during the war, is seated alone "without anyone to roll his eyes at," so the humor and the memory of the war are both altered, and the memory is seen "with resignation and resilience."
*Candidate application:* The nearest documented image to the project's ex-soldier: the loss of the audience that made the joke work. Humor survives the unit but is left without a counterpart, and acquires an edge of loneliness.

**[E07-49]. Humor as memory, and the tension between celebration and solemn commemoration.** (AC-23, Ch.5, [p.343], [p.345]-[p.346], [p.385]-[p.387])
Gregory notes that the standard literature on war memory takes a solemn approach and ignores humor, which "appears crass" beside monuments; yet wartime jokes are recalled in memoirs and documentary interviews, so humor is "a catalyst for war recollection." The thesis cites Adrian Gregory's account of tension between veterans who wished to celebrate survival and those who wished to remember the dead solemnly, and says armistice observances combine both, "similar to what might be observed through cartoons." Gregory adds cautions from memory research that memories are transient, misattributed or colored by present bias, and that jokes can be built on false recollections or on myths about the war. The answer given is that the physical cartoon preserves the original while the personal memory shifts.
*Candidate application:* The ex-soldier's jokes about service may be felt as disrespectful by those who commemorate solemnly, and may be felt as the only honest tribute by those who survived; both positions have a documented history.

**[E07-50]. Conclusion of the thesis: what Gregory says the humor shows.** (AC-23, Conclusion, [p.409]-[p.417]; also [p.413])
The summary claims relevant to this lens: humor "was, and still is an essential part of the military, and indeed war"; but "as much as we may assume that this humor is different to civilian humor, it has become clear that this is not necessarily so," because of continuity with pre-war (Victorian and Edwardian) humor and its use as "a mark of normalcy, even when that might not be directly found." Humor was used "to negate power and influence as it became appropriate to do so and reduce public worries." Gregory repeats the right and wrong laughter distinction (comforting versus belittling) and notes that correction humor "make[s] others look bad so that the audience can feel good about themselves." For trench journals Gregory repeats that studies had treated the text, and the contribution here is the visual. Gregory also admits that total viewer numbers "remain impossible to calculate."
*Candidate application:* The thesis's own bottom line argues against building a separate, wholly distinct "soldier humor" from nothing: borrow the continuity (class and region of origin, music hall, local sense of humor) and then add what is specifically front-line (resigned understatement, rank and regiment divides, permanent-wound jokes).

## Pattern roll-up

*Documented patterns this unit establishes. Every one is for British press and troop humor of 1914-18 unless stated, and most are filtered through newspapers and civilian-run magazines (see Gaps).*

**Functions**
- Relief and comfort are the thesis's repeated verdict ("succour and relief"); soldiers' humor is described as a "benevolent weapon" turned on themselves, giving the joker a small power and the group a sense of normal [E07-3, E07-41, E07-50].
- Distancing and understatement: the casually ironic tone that other armies noticed in the British, with danger treated as inconvenience and homely routine intruding on it [E07-19, E07-39, E07-33].
- Fear reduction by making the threat small, and by retrospective irony ("killing the fear") [E07-44].
- Cohesion by contrast: the in-group is defined against rank, regiment, home front and, much less, the enemy [E07-27, E07-36, E07-37].
- Humor as an official and commercial morale instrument as well as a private one (home-sent magazines, press commentary on soldier laughter, shows for the wounded) [E07-4, E07-29, E07-46].

**Targets**
- Soldiers' own cartoons aim mostly at self and the conditions of service: duration, leave, food, perks, wounds [E07-21, E07-32, E07-39].
- Rank and regiment: the sergeant quoting an officer-class slogan while being the scruffiest man in the picture; regimental in-jokes [E07-33, E07-36].
- The enemy is a much smaller target for soldiers than for home cartoonists; at home the enemy is made small, old, cowardly or foolish [E07-30, E07-37, E07-41].
- The censor (faceless, mindless, unaccountable) and the shirker, profiteer and pacifist are civilian-side targets; the soldier is the unstated standard [E07-15, E07-24].

**Forms and mechanics**
- Caption-versus-image incongruity; a reversed or borrowed phrase (the "contemptible little army" turned into a unit name); recycled slogans and frames (a tobacco jingle, a famous caption, a hole in the ground) [E07-19, E07-30, E07-45].
- Self-parody by the originator (the leave-time "atmosphere" staged for a good drawing); euphemistic stage gags about the dead ("pushing up daisies") [E07-39, E07-23].
- Humor meant for different recipients differs: child's letter, diary map, family sketch, comrade [E07-35].
- Humor is "layered" by later reuse; the same motif can carry resignation across eras [E07-45].

**Rules of license**
- Play at the edge of authority, not over it; ambiguity and deniability are the working method; the press self-policed and cartoonists enjoyed more room than writers [E07-11, E07-12, E07-26, E07-18].
- A neutral could be darker than a British voice; a speaker's nationality and whose dead are shown changed what was allowed [E07-14].
- In-group clarity, out-group insult: the same joke is humor or injury depending on who is inside [E07-43].
- Soldiers' own drawings may show a lasting limb loss with a joke; the home press did not [E07-31, E07-32, E07-41].

**Limits and failure modes**
- Forbidden in public: the lone, drunken, demoralized or absent-without-authority soldier; British dead; plain or ugly soldiers; the "not sufficiently good-looking" British soldier [E07-13, E07-17].
- Not drawn at all: shell shock; facial disfigurement ("beyond a joke"); severe gas wounds; atrocity myths (less humorous) [E07-31, E07-41, E07-40].
- Victorian and Edwardian unwritten limits (religion, obscenity, rude practical jokes, "improper or inhuman" subjects) are said to have been broken in the trenches, which the thesis asserts rather than demonstrates [E07-42].
- Misunderstanding by civilians is itself a recorded failure mode; humor does not carry the experience across [E07-38].

**Time course**
- Early war: thin humor, patriotic allegory; mid- and late war: more humor out of war conditions, more topical worry (conscription, submarines) [E07-44].
- Attitude to controls shifts from grudging consent in wartime to open mockery afterward [E07-16].
- Postwar: the joker alone at home with his memories, humor edged with pathos [E07-48]; the same figure commercialized as souvenirs and as heroes who cheerfully "joked in the face of death" [E07-46]; the motif outliving its war [E07-45]; the 1960s "futility" turn contrasted with a persistent sense that the old cartoons are "true" [E07-47].
- The tension between celebrating survival and commemorating the dead solemnly places humor on the "crass" side of formal memory [E07-49].

**Civilian-facing register**
- Civilians sent soldiers humor and wanted soldiers to appear cheerful; they drew wounds as temporary, soldiers as altered but intact, and soldiers' grumbling as endearing [E07-28, E07-29, E07-31, E07-46].
- The home-front version of soldier humor is a translation that drops the permanent wound and the psychological one.

**Defector, exile, dissent**
- Nothing direct. The nearest material is the censor-mocking tradition (a controlled valve), the artist-officer prosecuted over a cartoon, an ally paper that censored its own cartoonist, and the leave-without-reintegration argument [E07-7, E07-15, E07-17, E07-38].

**Cross-cultural**
- British "restrained and stoic" humor was explicitly contrasted with German humor and with a Dutch neutral's anger; other armies (French, American) are said to have recognized the morale value of Old Bill; French trench journals carried more cartoons; a cartoon about Australian troops was readable to British and dominion audiences alike [E07-4, E07-14, E07-33, E07-36, E07-47].

**Craft observations**
- The single most effective documented template is a calm, grumbling speaker in an unfixable situation, with the caption doing the work (the "better 'ole"); the second is the domestic detail intruding on danger; the third is the self-aware staging of "atmosphere" [E07-19, E07-39].
- Soldier characters were successful when they were "everyman" and human, not flawless, but the reversion to a flattering civilian version is a standing risk [E07-6, E07-28, E07-39].

## Gaps and cautions

1. **No images.** All figures are redacted in the text copy; every cartoon described here is Gregory's description. Gregory's readings of expression, size and composition cannot be checked without the British Cartoon Archive or the cited newspapers. Several drawings are interpreted by Gregory in ways that may be debatable (for example, reading the sizes of figures as status, or reading a facial expression as annoyance and acceptance).
2. **Civilian filter.** The thesis's "soldier" evidence is thin and heavily mediated. *Blighty* and *Khaki* were produced by civilians for soldiers; the *Bystander* was a London weekly; Bairnsfather's drawings were mostly made at home or in hospital; the soldier-drawn items are a small number of single cartoons and scrapbook pages (Southgate, Evans, Lomax, Lamb, a few journal pages). Gregory's headline claim, that front-line and home humor are "remarkably similar," rests on that small and partly civilian-produced sample and on the argument that soldiers were mostly recent civilians. Treat it as a hypothesis the thesis favors, not a finding.
3. **No reception data.** Gregory states repeatedly that readership and reaction cannot be measured. Statements that soldiers took to cartoons positively, or that some loved and some despised Bairnsfather, come from reviews, memoirs and secondary sources, not from a sample.
4. **Absence arguments.** The limit findings (no facial disfigurement, no shell shock, no British dead, no discussion of humor in the censorship rules) are arguments from what does not appear. The thesis admits what was censored or binned (for example, *Punch* submissions never seen again; destroyed press notices) cannot now be assessed.
5. **One army, one era, one medium.** British, 1914-18, mostly single-frame press cartoons (postcards and posters excluded by choice; trench-journal texts little used). Nothing here is evidence for songs, nicknames, pranks, slang or speech.
6. **Dating and numbering errors in the thesis.** In Chapter 1 the cartoon "Reported Missing" is dated 26 January 1916 in the text and 25 January in the caption; the figure numbers used for it ("2.4") and for Nevinson's "Paths of Glory" ("2.6" in one place) do not match the captions; the text says "the year before, in 1916" when moving from 1917-18 events. Smith-Dorrien is called "Colonel." The Dyson-censored-by-the-*Herald* point comes from a single biographical dictionary entry.
7. **Thinly sourced claims worth chasing.** (a) That unwritten Victorian rules of humor "were broken in the trenches" (E07-42): cited to a 1966 article on Victorian laughter, with no soldier source named. (b) The remark that the popular picture of darker soldier humor "is not apparent in the cartoons of soldiers" (E07-27, E07-33) is a claim about a small and filtered sample set against a popular and scholarly picture that says the opposite; it should not be read as showing that front-line humor was not dark. (c) The reading that front-line cartoons show "far more acceptance" of lasting wounds, supported by a few drawings including two in *Khaki*, which was a civilian philanthropy (E07-32). Names to follow (from the thesis's own references, not read): Madigan 2013 on "sticking it" and British courage; Fuller 1991 *Troop Morale and Popular Culture*; Seal 2013 *The Soldiers' Press*; Robertshaw 2001 and Le Naour 2001 in the *Journal of European Studies* special issue; Ashworth 1980 on live-and-let-live; Tim Cook 2015 on Canadian soldiers' humor; Hiley's work on cartoon books (2007) and on Haselden. The thesis's mention that popular histories hold the darker material (Korankye, *Die Laughing*; Weeks, *A Bloody Picnic*; Doyle and Walker, *Trench Talk*; Pegler, *Soldiers' Songs and Slang of the Great War*; Arthur, *Forgotten Voices*) is a lead to the gallows-humor texts the thesis itself does not quote.
8. **Language note.** American English spelling has been applied throughout this file, including in quotations and titles (the thesis uses British spellings such as "humour," "defence," "theatre"), per the project rule; wording of quotations is otherwise exact except where the thesis text carries its own typos (kept as printed, flagged "per thesis" where noticed, for example "dooty" and "hinterfere" in the sergeant's caption).
9. **Author pronouns.** The thesis does not state its author's pronouns; I used "Gregory" or "the thesis" throughout.
10. **Cross-checks needed in a browser.** Any use of the *Reported Missing* case details (fine amounts, who was fined, why the editor left) should be checked against the *Times* and *Manchester Guardian* reports of 19 February 1916 that the thesis cites; and the Bairnsfather facts (first submission, a check for two pounds, 817 performances) against Holt's biography, which the thesis cites.
