**Lens for this unit.** The Cancer defector is a former soldier living among strangers with no unit left. This unit is therefore read for what it establishes about humor as **group-making under dispossession**: functions, targets, forms, limits, rules of license, change over time, and who is excluded. It is mostly about civilians and displaced people, not professional soldiers; soldiers appear only at the edges (Ukrainian soldiers' memes in AC-28; a few incidental soldiers in AC-10).

## Findings

### From AC-10 (Üngör & Verkerke 2015, victims' humor in the Holocaust, Sarajevo and Syria)

**[E03-1]. Why genocide humor differs from war humor: the public sphere collapses and a group must re-humanize itself.** (AC-10, [p.2-3])
The authors distinguish genocide from war. In war there is suffering but no impending extinction, and the people suffering keep wide access to public culture (both world wars produced "war comedies" in Germany and France). In genocide, humor "collapses in options" because the victim group's access to the public sphere shrinks drastically, and the assault is on the group's collective identity and culture. The new relation of superiority to inferiority itself breeds humor aimed at the new social reality. Drawing on victimology's three responses to victimization (fright, flight, fight), the authors say humor can touch all three, and that because the victim group is continuously dehumanized it must keep re-humanizing itself by holding on to a modicum of normalcy, often through cultural activity including humor. They also note that all three groups studied had a prehistory of self-deprecating, sarcastic humor as long-governed minorities, which gave them a ready form to bend toward the catastrophe.
*Candidate application:* A character whose unit and public life were stripped away can be written as someone for whom joking is one of the few kinds of public behavior still available, and for whom it signals "I am still a person" more than "I am amused." An existing self-mocking habit (one that predates the loss) is the likeliest thing to survive and be repurposed.

**[E03-2]. The three-function frame: coping, cohesion, criticism, ranked by political salience.** (AC-10, [p.3-4])
The authors organize everything around three functions "in order of salience": coping, cohesion and criticism. Coping is an emotional mechanism of reducing or escaping pain; laughing at victimization is said not to trivialize it but to dispel it by redirecting emotional energy. Cohesion is bonding through humor that outsiders cannot access, because it draws boundaries; many jokes are conditional (they work only if the audience has the needed knowledge), and highly conditional in-group jokes are "hermetic." Criticism is the micro-act of telling a joke to a listener as protest culture against an oppressor. The ranking logic is stated plainly: all humor copes to some degree, some also builds solidarity out of shared experience, and critical humor is the most activist and political. The article also lines the three classical theories up against the cases: relief theory (tension release, social as well as physical), incongruity theory (the gap between the world victims expected and the one they got), and superiority theory, turned around by Ted Cohen into "a response of the weak to their suppressor."
*Candidate application:* The frame gives a usable checklist for any scene of dark humor in an exile community: what is it relieving, who is it binding, and what is it aimed at. Write the functions as stacked, not alternative.

**[E03-3]. Coping through absurd normalcy: the full-train joke.** (AC-10, [p.5])
A Holocaust survivor (Leontine Tels-de Jong, interviewed in the Shoah archive) recalls a deportation journey from Westerbork to Theresienstadt in an overcrowded carriage without enough water, food or toilet facilities. In the middle of describing it she starts laughing as she remembers that the train kept stopping for signals, and that a man beside her proposed, in the voice of a polite announcer ("Ladies and gentlemen..."), that when the train stopped again everyone should pretend it was already full; the whole carriage laughed. The authors read it as a moment where tension broke by joking about the absurdity of the situation, a joke that sets the language of normal life against abnormal conditions. They add the psychological background: ordinary assumptions that let people plan are "snatched away" in genocide, and Viktor Frankl's remark that an abnormal reaction to an abnormal situation is normal, plus Lipman's reading of such humor as a "mad reaction" to preserve sanity.
*Candidate application:* The pattern to borrow is the ordinary social phrase (a polite, everyday formula) applied to a monstrous situation; the laugh is the sound of the old rulebook failing. The same survivor laughing again mid-testimony, while retelling it in an interview, is a documented form of delayed relief.

**[E03-4]. Mocking one's own victim role as coping; adaptation, not resistance, is what survival required; humor as a sign of having adapted.** (AC-10, [p.5-6])
Janny Moffie, a Dutch Jewish survivor of Auschwitz, said she made many jokes to keep her mind off the camp; one example is telling the woman next to her, while they chopped frozen ground, that this was just the work she had trained for, a mock claim of professional fitness that makes her own forced-labor role the butt. She also talked about books and favorite recipes to stay mentally strong. The Dutch survivor and memoirist Elie Cohen (1959) argued that only prisoners who had adapted to the new circumstances could laugh; they stopped asking why it was happening and tried to function with some normalcy, joking "as if normal life still existed," which he saw as both an escape and a kind of indifference. The authors stress that long-term survivors in the concentration camps were those who made themselves indispensable to the camp economy, so for them adaptation meant survival and physical or mental resistance "would lead nowhere"; Frankl's sequence of shock, then apathy ("emotional death"), then a remaining capacity to choose an attitude supplies the frame.
*Candidate application:* Humor as a sign that someone has crossed from shock into functioning is a time-course marker: a character who jokes about the new place is further along than one who cannot. The mock-professional remark ("this is what I trained for") is a mechanism an ex-soldier might aim at civilian drudgery.

**[E03-5]. Cohesion in the camps: humor as shared currency, and a trait the group looks for in a newcomer.** (AC-10, [p.6-7])
Making someone laugh consolidates relationships (Ziv), and the authors argue that for in-groups (the examples are doctors joking about patients, paramedics' gruesome storytelling) the jokes create a common language and sense of uniqueness precisely because they would be misread or found offensive by outsiders; context is everything and a joke that works in one setting is inappropriate in another. For camp prisoners, many survivors reported that bonding with other prisoners was necessary to survive. Elie Cohen wrote that comradeship was vital and that lone wolves did not last long, and that humor was one form of sharing that supported it. Anna Pawelczynska's sociology of Auschwitz lists the traits needed to be accepted into a prisoners' group, and "a sense of humor" sits in that list next to friendliness, readiness to help, patience, tolerance, self-control and quick reflexes. Anti-Nazi humor, and cabaret in the ghettos, produced solidarity among the persecuted (citing Morreall, Rapaport); self-ridicule also bound people together.
*Candidate application:* Humor as an entry qualification for a mutual-aid group is the strongest documented link to the defector's situation: in a community of strangers, being easy to laugh with may function as a credential that substitutes for the unit's shared history. The group's trait list is useful because humor is listed among practical virtues, not as a luxury.

**[E03-6]. Critical humor, its ambiguity, and the oppressor's tolerance of it.** (AC-10, [p.7])
The authors flag the open question whether camp humor produced belligerence or fatalism and inactivity (Lipman). They also report that the Nazis allowed and even encouraged some humor in the camps, for example a camp theater at Westerbork, which Frankl said was used as distraction to keep prisoners from revolting and which may also have entertained the camp leaders. Within the prisoners' circulation there were jokes critical of Germans and of prominent Jews alike. Pawelczynska's example of the mechanism is imagining a raging SS officer humiliated, which made him less menacing. A sample joke set at the entrance to a killing facility (a prisoner asks for a last small comfort and a companion hushes him not to make trouble) is read by the authors as mostly about the Jewish group's supposed accommodating fatalism, not about the killing; by ridiculing themselves, they argue, the victims also "undermine the power of the perpetrator," a "spiritual weapon" for feeling equal or superior. They quote Pawelczynska on the survivors' stance: "Resistance was expressed in the constant effort to maintain inner freedom while outwardly adapting."
*Candidate application:* An institution can permit humor because it is safe for the institution, so permitted humor and subversive humor must be distinguished. Self-mockery that targets one's own group's passivity can double as a disarming of the oppressor, a layered target an ex-soldier might use about his own compliance in a system he left.

**[E03-7]. Sarajevo: numbness versus laughter, mental survival through jokes, and a shifted moral line.** (AC-10, [p.8])
In the siege of Sarajevo (1992 to 1996), the authors describe two ways of responding to the changed daily meanings. Some people disconnected psychologically (the term *prolupati* names emotional numbness and irrationality after an excess of pain) and a few stood defiantly in the open as if nothing was happening, a normalcy bought at the cost of losing the fear needed for physical survival; others called such people "zombies of the war." Others responded with a dark but strong sense of humor. A group of Sarajevans launched a radio show, the Surrealist Hit Parade, joking about the Serb political leaders and about daily life, and defended it with the argument that doing nothing while someone destroys your life is the worst response, and that one has to find humor to function through months of depression. Survivors distinguished physical survival (dodging shelling, cutting trees for firewood, fetching water, food and medical supplies) from mental survival, which meant telling jokes about all of that. The moral boundary of what was funny moved, and morbid humor about the new daily life became mainstream.
*Candidate application:* The contrast between the numb-and-fearless and the joking-and-afraid is a useful characterization axis: humor can be what keeps fear alive and useful. For a defector, "numb and functional" versus "funny and still scared" are two different ways of carrying the same past.

**[E03-8]. Sarajevo cohesion: acceptance conditional on laughing at yourself; the checkpoint as shared identity marker.** (AC-10, [p.8-9])
Following Ivana Maček, the authors report that acceptance into a group during the Bosnian war was conditional on the capacity to laugh at oneself; only then could solidarity form among people in the same predicament. Curfews and the checkpoint system were among the first measures imposed in a captured town, and checkpoint jokes exposed the absurd, inhuman logic of the network. The sample joke (summarized in restrained terms) has a checked traveler stopped just before curfew and killed by the guard on the claim that he would not have made it in time anyway, then queried by an officer who points out the man was within the rule; its target is the arbitrary reasoning of armed authority, and the authors say experiences of the checkpoints and the jokes about them advanced group identity so strongly that having passed through checkpoints became a defining marker of any survivor of the war.
*Candidate application:* A shared ordeal that is also a shared joke topic is a ready identity marker for a refugee community; a character who did not share the specific ordeal may be unable to enter the joke. Self-laughter as an admission test, not a nicety, is the transferable mechanism.

**[E03-9]. Siege culture: a parody guidebook, counts of events, and humor aimed inward as well as at the aggressor.** (AC-10, [p.9-10])
The authors argue that Sarajevo's artistic and humorous responses were directed at the inhabitants as well as the besiegers; art let people take action instead of waiting on the situation, and "by seeing themselves as artists, instead of victims, Sarajevans disempowered the oppressor." An independent production company (the FAMA foundation) organized political entertainment, with many performances held during the day because electricity was mostly out; Larisa Kurtović's estimate, as cited, is about 182 theater productions, 170 exhibits and 48 concerts during the siege. One project was the *Sarajevo Survival Guide* (written April 1992 to April 1993, published 1994), a parody of a travel guide that tours a visitor through surviving without transportation, hotels, taxis, telephones, food, shops, heating, water, information or electricity; funny in form and an accurate record of daily life, intended to show the city "not as a victim but as a place of experiment, where wit could still achieve victory over terror." Humor was framed as a "weapon" of the powerless to gain a sense of control in a situation fully controlled by a far stronger enemy. Maček: the self-mocking perspective achieved "a sense of control and distancing from everyday circumstances."
*Candidate application:* Parody of an institutional document form (a guide, a notice, a manual) applied to the actual conditions of dispossession is a documented humor genre and a plausible shape for exile-community folklore. It also shows the community teaching newcomers the rules by joke.

**[E03-10]. Syria before the uprising: humor that made repression tolerable, neither protest nor safety valve.** (AC-10, [p.10])
Citing Lisa Wedeen, the authors say that under four decades of Assad rule Syrians neither challenged power directly nor accepted the regime's version of reality; political humor existed but was not necessarily overtly anti-regime, was "not a form of protest" and yet "not merely a safety valve either," and transformed repression into a tolerable experience. The regime demobilized civil society and discouraged art and culture initiatives.
*Candidate application:* A third category beside resistance and venting: humor that lets people stay inside a system they cannot change. A defector who left on principle may be uneasy with how much of his old humor fit this category.

**[E03-11]. Syrian coping humor: shortage, winter, the fear of one's own death, and sniping.** (AC-10, [p.10-11])
Examples the authors give, each read as a way of facing fear, sadness and anger: a joke about a household with a live bird for dinner but neither a knife nor fuel, in which the bird itself chants the president's praise (target: the regime and the shortages together); three rebels photographed posing and laughing beside an armed snowman in an article on winter hardship (read as coping, and perhaps as a comment on the opposition's shortage of manpower, which the authors flag as their own speculation); a documentary scene in which a young man lying on a couch is photographed by a friend who says he is going to upload the picture "when you die," a joke on the social-media convention of posting solemn portraits of people killed by the regime; and, in the city of Homs under sniper fire, activists who tossed a bag of sand into the open to show how the snipers respond, drawing laughter from onlookers, and a filmed man who remarked in the middle of exposure to fire, in the manner of someone out for a morning run, that he did this every day. The authors say the absurdity works because these jokes contradict the bitter mainstream attitude toward snipers, whose random deaths terrify everyone. A prominent Syrian satirist is quoted: "humor was the only way I could deal with my anger."
*Candidate application:* Mock-bravado under danger is a recurring form, with the absurd contrast between casual speech and a lethal setting as the engine; its audience is the immediate group and the camera. Mocking the ritual of one's own commemoration is a high-license move reserved for people inside the danger.

**[E03-12]. Syria: from cohesive protest humor to bitter dark humor as violence escalated; humor that only the shared ordeal can decode.** (AC-10, [p.11-12])
The authors say that before the regime's violence escalated sharply in winter 2011-12, protesters' humor was largely cohesive and drew people across class, gender and age into a common cause; chants and slogans by young leaders gave symbolic unity. After the escalation, the humor "became bitterly dark." They use the film *Return to Homs* to show the shift: an activist-singer who led demonstrations in two working-class districts joked about siege, sniping and bombardment in a way that went beyond the older Homs tradition of self-sarcastic, regional-identity humor, and these were jokes "that only make sense to Syrians who shared those violent experiences across Syria"; in the authors' words victim humor became "a major vehicle of solidarity and group identity."
*Candidate application:* Humor that was a regional or class marker before the catastrophe gets re-keyed to a new, nation-wide or group-wide ordeal; the shared event replaces the old shared place. A unit-less ex-soldier community could develop its own in-jokes keyed to the shared experience of leaving.

**[E03-13]. Humor as political criticism in Syria: decrowning the leader, flipping the regime's own symbols, and puppets that break the fear.** (AC-10, [p.12-13])
Because direct criticism of the president had not been tolerated, a cartoonist's first drawings of Assad depicting him fleeing were a direct undermining of power and were carried at demonstrations; photographs of people holding caricatures spread, and the formerly near-sacred official portrait, in the authors' account, ended up in toilets, with the president's ears and neck turned into targets of ridicule. The critical logic is stated through a researcher's phrase: the leader has to be "exorcized; decrowned through ridicule." Counter-propaganda took the regime's own forms: a billboard campaign with a raised hand and a slogan of being "with the law" was parodied with lines such as "I lost my shoes"; a banner mocked the terrorist label by stating that the weapons were visible (they were banners); and the regime's dehumanizing label for protesters, a word for germs, was turned into a slogan of pride ("We are all germs") with the president recast as a disinfectant doctor, which the authors describe as inverting insults into collective identity. A self-funded finger-puppet series, *Top Goon* (first episode 23 November 2011, two seasons of five-minute episodes), portrayed the president as an infantile puppet with exaggerated physical features, whose enforcer sang him a lullaby threatening the population (not reproduced); other episodes dealt with moral choices, coping with loss, torture and international passivity, and the director said the aim was "to break down the wall of fear."
*Candidate application:* Inverting an institution's insulting label for the dissenter into a badge is a precise mechanism for a defector community's self-name. Shrinking a feared figure visually (infantilizing) is the weapon-of-the-weak form.

**[E03-14]. Defensive versus offensive humor, and which forms each case used.** (AC-10, [p.12])
The authors call Jewish and Bosnian humor ambiguous between defensive and offensive: it targets the Other but was "mostly directed inwards rather than directed at the German or Serb oppressor," so also defensive. Syrian humor, directed inwards and outwards, is a more unequivocal expression of criticism, protest and confrontation and is inseparable from political activism. By form: oral jokes dominated among the Jewish victims; oral jokes plus printed media for Bosnia; cartoons, slogans, satirical shows, pictures and videos plus oral jokes in Syria (which the authors tie to social media's reach).
*Candidate application:* The direction of the joke (in versus out) is a diagnostic for a community's stage; mostly inward-aimed humor suggests no power to confront, outward-aimed suggests organization. Medium shapes what can be shared among a dispersed group.

**[E03-15]. After victimization: the functions persist, therapy sees humor as a door, and survivors feel guilt and shame about having laughed.** (AC-10, [p.13-14])
The authors argue that dark humor continues after victimization because the earlier functions still matter. Jacqueline Garrick, using humor in individual and group therapy with Vietnam veterans (the one soldier-population claim in the article, secondhand), reported that exchanging humorous stories created a safe atmosphere in which painful memories could be shared more easily, and that humor released tension when it grew too great; survivors often described themselves as having a "sick" sense of humor and felt guilty about what they laughed at. Garrick recommends that therapists help patients see humor as a vital part of coping, because focusing on humorous memories encourages cohesion and validation and laughing together eases shame and loneliness. Shoah-archive interviews show the guilt directly: one child of survivors recalled that his family joked a great deal after the war and he saw it as pushing feelings away rather than coping; another witness said she and friends laughed a lot about the war in 1940-42, "real Jewish humor," but when asked for an example refused ("No, no, because in our heart we knew it was not so funny"); two others said they laughed a lot (one that it "sounds kind of crazy"; the other "It's unbelievable but we did," adding that the jokes "were so sick"). The authors read the defensive tone as guilt and note the contradiction between enjoying oneself and telling a gruesome story.
*Candidate application:* The pattern a former soldier can show: insists the humor was good, can recite it only in the right room, and cannot reproduce the example for an outsider. The refusal to repeat the joke is itself the documented behavior and a stronger characterization than reciting one.

**[E03-16]. Rules of license: who may joke, and what makes a joke "allowed" after the fact.** (AC-10, [p.14-15])
The authors say the outcome of a joke about the Holocaust depends heavily on who the joker is: it is more accepted for Jewish people to joke about their history and the perpetrators, while for perpetrators' descendants joking about their own role or about the victims is far more taboo and risks being heard as antisemitic; jokes ridiculing Hitler and the Nazis are more acceptable because they delegitimize, but must avoid being too light lest they trivialize. A Jewish German comedian (Oliver Polak) is reported to argue that mocking himself as a Jew gives his German audience an opening to laugh instead of showing guilt. Channa Kalmann is cited: laughing at the Holocaust itself is not appropriate and there is still a moral boundary; humor is allowed when the teller's intention is clear, to cope with the past or prevent oblivion rather than for a cheap laugh. The authors add that the funniest jokes "balance on these moral boundaries," that a joke with a target does not make teller or hearer aggressive toward the target, and that in the digital age audiences are unbounded: dark jokes among genocide scholars work in the room of fellow scholars and lose their sense when printed in a newspaper.
*Candidate application:* The license is earned by position (victim, survivor, insider) and signaled by intention; the same line from an outsider is read differently. Context collapse (a private joke leaking to a public audience) is a ready complication.

**[E03-17]. Bosnian humor after the war: a broken taboo, hostile ethnic jokes, self-critique of passivity, and the relief value of war jokes in stand-up.** (AC-10, [p.15-16])
Directly after the war, the authors say Bosnian humor became distinctively aggressive, with former enemies as the butts; the war "broke a taboo" on ethnic jokes with unmistakable hostility (a joke in which a Bosnian asks to be struck harder so as to hurt a tied-on enemy is the sample, not reproduced in detail). Traditional Mujo-and-Suljo jokes, built on a naive but sometimes shrewd duo, now addressed the postwar situation, one of them making light of war casualties (a lost limb as the one thing others in the city lack; summarized, not reproduced). Following Anna Sheftel, the authors say Bosnians mock their own victimhood and perceived passivity (the popular belief that they realized too late that the war was aimed at them); post-war humor critiques rather than glorifies that role and lets Bosnians "regain their dignity and intelligence," a laughter serving as both critique and release about shame and foolishness. The film *No Man's Land*, about three soldiers (one Serb, two Bosnian Muslims) trapped between lines, is the key text: in one scene the Bosnian soldiers remark on a radio story about the mess in Rwanda while in their own war (a paradoxical comment on another's catastrophe), and the director is quoted that Bosnians laughed a lot during the war and that humor "gives you a distance... It was our secret weapon." The authors also mention Balkan stand-up comedians: one Serbian comedian says war jokes relieve the audience and that he is tired of them but that those jokes are popular, and a Bosnian comedian makes a joke about a beauty contestant who could only wish for peace for her own country.
*Candidate application:* After the danger ends, humor can turn hostile toward the former enemy and critical of one's own side's conduct; both are documented, and both coexist with sorrow. A character may be fed up with his own war jokes and still keep telling the ones an audience demands.

**[E03-18]. Humor changes as people habituate: the authors' trajectory for Syria.** (AC-10, [p.16-17])
The authors hypothesize, carefully ("we cannot draw definitive conclusions"), that Syrian humor will not cease but become darker, perhaps reaching cynicism, because "the intensity of the violence is commensurate with the levels of coping that are needed." Their evidence: artists of the town of Kafranbel, famous for humorous banners, produced a notably darker banner after a chemical attack, addressing the world with sarcasm about when help would come; a video of children laughing off a bomb landing on a nearby street after taking cover, which the authors call brutalization; and an election-time social-media slogan wordplaying on the regime's deadly record. The change is described in general terms as adaptation to violence in which "moral boundaries shift and they laugh at things they would not have laughed at before." They end by calling humor a survival instinct, a source of morale and a way of keeping dignity, and say a Holocaust survivor's remark that they "could still laugh at things" could have been said by a Syrian.
*Candidate application:* A gradient: more exposure moves the line of what is funny and turns playfulness toward cynicism; a character's current "unacceptable" humor can be traced to a before and after. Use sparingly: the claim is a hypothesis, not a measured finding.

**[E03-19]. The authors' conclusion: "experiential deformation," humor that outsiders cannot decode, and the myth of the passive victim.** (AC-10, [p.17-18])
Summing up: across three very different cases the functions are strikingly similar (coping, cohesion, criticism), and "in order to cope, one needed to adapt; in order to adapt, one needed to joke." On cohesion, jokes were hard to tell outside the ghettos and camps, Sarajevo or Syria, partly because they were macabre and partly because only the victimized would understand; dark humor was an "experiential deformation" peculiar to victims, and in Maček's phrase "people who did not have the same sort of experience, who judged situations by peacetime standards, had no way to appreciate the jokes." On criticism, joking about oppressor and self lets victims oppose forced victimization by reversing roles, and the "alternative culture" in each case resisted the attempt to destroy the group's culture. Finally the authors argue that the popular picture of victims as passive is mistaken: people who joked about hardship, oppressors and themselves were aware of what was happening, commented on it and coped.
*Candidate application:* The strongest line for the defector's civilian-facing register: his jokes may be unintelligible to people who judge by peacetime standards, which is a source of isolation and of a smaller circle of people who can laugh with him.
*Cross-use:* Robot or gynoid communities in exile could carry the same mechanism: a humor that only those who passed through the same event can decode.

### From AC-28 (Bondarenko 2025, Ukrainian black-humor war memes)

**[E03-20]. What the study actually is: a cognitive-linguistics case study of memes about death, not a content analysis with counts.** (AC-28, [p.1-2], [p.4-5], [p.18-19])
Bondarenko sets out to explain why black-humor memes are so widespread in Ukrainian social networks when the usual "benign violation" account looks unconvincing, by modeling the memes as conceptual blends. The corpus is 100 memes ("BH_Memes") taken from nine Telegram channels and bots (small ones, from a few subscribers to just over 5,000), drawn from a personal collection of over 700 memes gathered from the start of the full-scale invasion on 24 February 2022 to April 2024. Selection criteria: explicit visual or verbal black-humor elements referring to macabre or taboo concepts (death, war, hell, execution, violence, mutilation, deadly sickness, calamity); messages "relatively clear to cultural insiders"; and reliance on popular Ukraine-war narratives. The author calls the sample "random" but the criteria make it purposive, and the text does not say how many of the 100 were actually analyzed (about eleven numbered figures, each with one to three memes, are discussed). Memes were annotated with a visual annotation tool. The author states that most Ukrainian war black-humor memes engage the concept of death, explicitly or implicitly. **No per-category counts, no frequencies of targets or functions, and no audience or authorship data are reported.**
*Candidate application:* Treat this source as a catalog of mechanisms and targets seen in one chosen set of memes, not as evidence of how common any of them is among soldiers or civilians.

**[E03-21]. Definition, emotions, and the study's own claim that these memes are not mainly about amusement.** (AC-28, [p.1-4])
The paper uses "black humor" for the dark, morbid form also called sick, gallows or morbid humor, and defines the memetic kind as humor that confronts death, chaos, violence, disasters and personal crises through exaggeration and transposition. It aligns with the two "negative" humor styles: aggressive (aimed at enemies) and self-defeating (self-deprecating). The introduction asserts that Ukrainians turn to humor in chats to cope and that memes alleviate stress (citing Freud and the benign-violation theory), but the author's own account later is different: these memes "tend to evoke gloating, grief, anger, or despair" and serve primarily to share and express those complex emotions, so the genre is partly an expression channel, not a mood-lifter. No data tests the coping claim. The discussion of memes' spread names three communicative drivers (humor, intertextuality, incongruity) and Shifman's three humor elements (playfulness, incongruity, superiority).
*Candidate application:* A character's dark humor can be written as emotional sharing (gloating, grief, rage together) rather than as relief; people laugh and feel worse and still need the exchange.

**[E03-22]. Insider decoding and the "paradox": negating national values reinforces them.** (AC-28, [p.4], [p.8-9], [p.18])
The memes are best understood by cultural insiders who know the relevant national concepts, narratives and news (and the paper adds that even insiders respond differently). Most national-concept memes carry a clear positive or negative value stance; black-humor memes are the exception because they make an inherently negative taboo (death) look acceptable or even positive, above all when the target is an enemy or when the joke is self-deprecating. The author's central claim is that this superficial negation of values ends up reinforcing them, and that new nation-building concepts such as DEATH TO THE ENEMY have emerged and circulate widely; an informal greeting with that sentiment is said to be listed in a 2024 English-Ukrainian military dictionary.
*Candidate application:* A shared dark joke can function as a loyalty marker: telling it affirms belonging to the group's values while sounding like their opposite. A defector's refusal to laugh at the standard enemy-death joke may therefore read as disloyalty, not squeamishness.

**[E03-23]. An in-group target: a pop-culture meme aimed at Ukrainian pessimism about the postwar future.** (AC-28, [p.9])
The one example the paper gives of a meme aimed inward uses a scene between two characters from the TV series *Game of Thrones* who are adversaries: one predicts that after the war things will be worse (the survivors damaged, destruction, internal strife, elections), and the other replies that the speaker will not live to see the victory anyway. The author reads the target as the scepticism of some Ukrainians about the country's future and the punchline as reinforcing the national concept INVINCIBILITY and the motivation to survive and rebuild. The author uses this to argue that no benign situation accompanies the threat here, so the benign-violation account does not fit.
*Candidate application:* Borrowed fiction (a streaming-series dialogue) as the container for a very dark in-group quarrel shows how an exile or besieged group uses shared popular culture as a common language; the joke polices morale by threatening the pessimist with death.

**[E03-24]. Why the author says benign violation does not fit, and the theories the author offers instead.** (AC-28, [p.9-10])
Ukrainian black humor, the paper argues, often conveys bitterness and frustration, both as a post-traumatic response and as outward rage against the enemy, so the usual theory (humor is a threat that is also safe) does not describe it. The author turns to Affective Disposition Theory (enjoyment depends on the audience's emotional stance toward victims and victors) for the enemy-directed memes, and to Relief Theory for self-deprecating memes, where the cathartic effect "arises from an audience's recognition that others share a similar experience" (the paper's example is a punchline of the type "absolutely nobody," with no setup given in the text).
*Candidate application:* Enemy-directed dark humor is not tension-relief at a safe distance; it is aggression shared among people already on one side. Self-directed dark humor is the form that works through recognition of shared suffering.

**[E03-25]. Making enemy mass death routine: celebration, profit, bags and games (described restrained).** (AC-28, [p.10-13])
The author sorts the enemy-death memes by the metaphor that routinizes death, always converting a taboo into something ordinary. (a) **Death as a holiday or party:** the daily enemy-casualty totals published by the Ukrainian military headquarters become cake decorations or a party setup; the official code word for deceased personnel is reused as an insider tag; a punchline echoing the adversary's official refrain that everything is going according to plan is turned against it; a calendar-holiday meme (International Women's Day) casts the enemy leader as gift-giver. (b) **Death as profit:** memes about the compensation promised to families of conscripts killed in action (first a budget car, later reportedly fur coats, food and firewood for widows in distant provinces), read by the author as mocking the state that values soldiers' lives so cheaply, the poverty of ordinary families, and, in the author's account, relatives who look for profit in a death, with the compensation items as recurring visual tokens. (c) **Death as bags and games:** the enemy's use of plastic bags for the dead becomes a children's sack race with the finish line tagged with the deceased-code number, and a "new uniform" joke. The author's stated mechanism: only some elements are metaphorized and the rest carries over untransformed, so the blend reads that these soldiers' lives are worthless.
*Candidate application:* Documented technique: an adversary's own bureaucratic vocabulary (casualty codes, official refrains, compensation notices) is the raw material, and the joke's insiders are those who can read the code. A soldier-type character who finds this humor vile and a different one who finds it funny are both plausible.

**[E03-26]. Answering the enemy's propaganda by performing the accusation; "meat" wordplay.** (AC-28, [p.14-16])
Two further enemy-death families are described in restrained terms. (a) Memes that adopt, as a joke genre, a claim from the adversary's propaganda that the dead are being arranged to form messages visible to drones: the dead become a "font," with captions imitating a complaining voice from the enemy's online community, and one paired with a photo of a Russian city's crowd forming a pro-war appeal; the author says the memes select a single aspect (the killing) and leave out that the dead are soldiers of an invading army, a point the author notes under the cognitive operation of salience. (b) The metaphor of enemy soldiers as meat (rooted in the commonly used term for tactics that throw soldiers at fortified positions in large numbers), including a supermarket meme that depends on a Russian wordplay (the same spelling reads as "counters" or "regiments" depending on stress) and on the names of two heavily contested battle sites, and a coined brand-name blend. These rely on insider language competence and local knowledge.
*Candidate application:* Taking the opponent's accusation and performing it with exaggeration is an old defiance move; a plausible risk (not stated by the source) is that the joke ends up confirming the enemy's description of the person telling it. Wordplay that requires the right language and knowledge sorts outsiders from insiders again.

**[E03-27]. Death as the thing being joked about through everyday frames: a discount, a harvest, a concert, a live news report (described restrained).** (AC-28, [p.16-18])
When "death" lends elements to ordinary frames, the author says the blend keeps pushing the same agenda: death is a norm and human life is valueless. Examples: a retail-sale joke around Black Friday 2023 using the military codes for wounded (300) and deceased (200) so that a dead enemy is a better "discount" than a wounded one; a narrative that enemy soldiers carry sunflower seeds so their remains will feed birds and then flowers, giving a battlefield-as-sunflower-field punchline. A third family jokes about the deaths of notorious figures to diminish the significance of their deaths: the natural death of a pro-separatist pop singer became a running euphemism ("going to his concert") for being killed because most of the separatist leaders who sang alongside him were assassinated, then combined with the unconfirmed death of a mercenary leader in an air crash; and the killing of a pro-war military blogger by a bomb disguised as a gift produced a pun on "conflict zone" (literally "hotspot" in Ukrainian), "reporting live" from it.
*Candidate application:* A euphemism that begins as a joke about one death and hardens into group slang for dying is a plausible pathway for in-world soldier vocabulary. Note also that the named targets in this family are described by the author as figures of questionable or notorious reputation, so the license rests on a judgment that the dead person was a perpetrator.

### From AC-34 (Žukienė 2014, cartoons of the Lithuanian displaced-persons press in Germany; read in Lithuanian, translations mine)

*Quotation convention for this section: English phrases in quotation marks are my own translations of the Lithuanian (the original wording is given in Lithuanian where it matters), except where tagged "English summary," which is the article's own English.*

**[E03-28]. The author's premise: displaced-persons life is usually studied as misery, but the press shows people who also joked, dreamed and celebrated.** (AC-34, [p.3], English summary [p.18])
The author says researchers of the DP period mostly write about a refugee-hostile environment, poverty, overcrowded barracks, camp isolation and national quarrels (citing Wyman), whereas the humor press, photographs, memoirs and letters make it obvious that people who suffered were not only sad, hungry and confused but also amused themselves, dreamed, celebrated and made plans for a better future. Writers, artists and theater people who gathered in the German camps brought with them a formed pre-war tradition of written and drawn humor and "quite successfully" tried to laugh even when there was real reason for sadness. The magazines also carried biting short texts, verse, "updated" folk songs and rewritten proverbs, which the author reads as the Lithuanian knack for rhyme being used to soften a rough exile reality in a cheerful form. The English summary describes the images as showing daily life "as if crying and laughing at the same time."
*Candidate application:* An exile community's humor can be written as a deliberate cultural import (a pre-loss tradition carried by specific artists and writers), not as something that springs up from nowhere. Its function is to show that the group is still more than its misery.
*Cross-use:* Tepenian Act 1 exile settlers, who would also bring trades and traditions with them.

**[E03-29]. How a camp humor press arose: typed sheets first, a printed surge in 1946, April Fools' issues, an end around 1949.** (AC-34, [p.3-5], notes 2, 12)
The first Lithuanian humor publication in exile, *Špilkos* ("Pins" by my reading), appeared in November 1945 as a few typed sheets, place of origin unmarked; a second, *Menkės balsas*, reached northern German camps on 31 December 1945 in the same unillustrated duplicated form and appeared three more times in 1946. The author notes 1946 as the year of abundance (thirteen satirical titles in camps in towns including Nürtingen, Flensburg, Hanau, Memmingen, Kempten, Celle, Hafkrug and Dillingen), and speculates that after the Allied victory over Nazi Germany the war refugees felt somewhat safer and the improved mood broke out in the effort to have humor publications. Some were one-offs issued for 1 April (a footnote lists several April Fools' titles of 1946-47); others ran two or three years until about 1949, when emigration processes suppressed all DP cultural activity in Germany. The look varied: one to three typed pages with amateurish, sentimental colored-pencil drawings, or print-shop magazines on rough yellowish paper with line and dot illustrations; the best-designed were *Dypukas* and *Dievo paukštelis*, both showing the graphic culture of pre-war Lithuanian print. Art was by Mykolas Paškevičius (an artist of Belarusian origin who had fled to Lithuania in the war and then lived in the camps with Lithuanians), Povilas Osmolskis (the most active cartoonist), Vytautas Kasiulis (for whom caricature was a sideline), Borisas Borjeras and others, many unsigned; the writer Pulgis Andriušis edited *Dievo paukštelis* and is called the guiding spirit of exile humor. A short unsigned verse in the first issue of that magazine (1 April 1946) gives the tone: "Sveiki sulaukę Pirmo balandžio, be arielkėlės ir be skilandžio" (roughly: greetings on reaching April First, without a drop of liquor and without a smoked sausage).
*Candidate application:* Humor infrastructure in a displaced community is physical and improvised (typed sheets, then small print runs), keyed to a calendar occasion, and it appears when the community feels just safe enough. The date of the safety is itself a story beat.

**[E03-30]. Shortage and food as the first and steadiest target, with consolation as the early purpose.** (AC-34, [p.5-6], notes 17-18)
The author divides the Germany-period humor into an early stage (1945-46), when artists mostly looked cheerfully at the wretched refugee household, the lack of food and clothing, "as if trying to console themselves and others," and a later stage (1946-47) dominated by emigration. Survival is named the most important question for a person who has lost home; food was the subject of many literary works and cartoons. The editor-writer's zone-by-zone riff: in the British zone Lithuanians' "political situation" is so good there are herring and eels, so nobody grows a goiter; in the French zone the same writer used deliberately garbled German of the kind spoken by people who do not know the language (the author's footnote translates it as: few drinks, many congresses, many speculators, little food); and in the American zone food is canned, so the countrymen fear turning into cans themselves, and not even a named poet's call would draw them out of the tin box. The cartoons repeat the idea (a "food map" and an unsigned cartoon titled "Museum in the Year 2546"), which the author uses to show the editor's ideas fed the artists.
*Candidate application:* The recurring topic is the stomach, and the register is gallows-adjacent comfort: jokes about where to get fed, in which zone and under whose rules. Mock pidgin (the host language as the exiles speak it) is a documented device.

**[E03-31]. The care package as a social license: a parcel changes what a person may say to officials.** (AC-34, [p.6-7])
In a well-known feuilleton collection, *Siuntinėlis iš Amerikos* ("A Little Parcel from America," 1946), the writer describes a DP named Petruoka dreaming of a parcel received; the parcel, stamped and corded like a dowry chest, sits on a table made of crates "like a sacrament on an altar" (Lithuanian: "lyg sakramentas ant altoriaus"). In the matching drawing Petruoka, almost out of his mind with delight, goes to government offices smoking an American cigarette and insulting the officials; the neighbors do not object to his insolence, saying anything is allowed him now because he "received a parcel from Cincinnati" (Lithuanian: "gavo siuntinį iš Sinsinačio"). The drawing lists the contents (sugar cubes, coffee beans, cigarette packs, chocolate; the text adds toothpaste, buttons, thread). The author adds a snapshot of a student posing with a parcel beside a hand-drawn flower as a thank-you to the unknown donor, and notes that aid parcels eased the shortage and people were grateful.
*Candidate application:* Possession of a scarce outside good (a parcel, a contact, a supply source) can raise someone's license to speak rudely, and the community's joke names the rule while obeying it. A sacred-object simile for a food parcel is the same register as soldiers' reverence for rations.

**[E03-32]. The benefactor rule: aid organizations are portrayed with respect, the aid dependency is self-mocked, and one cover is simply chilling.** (AC-34, [p.7-8])
Cans became a recurring symbol of the relief agency UNRRA and of the United States, drawn sometimes as a blessing and sometimes as an openly loathed food. The first cover of *Dievo paukštelis* shows UNRRA as a sun beaming cans, cigarettes, playing cards and even influence on matters of the heart; the back (last) cover of the same magazine, drawn by the editor, shows a DP sitting at a table loaded with tins complaining of too few calories, compensating with strong drink and dreaming of steaks, a self-mocking portrait of insatiable need; a 1947 cover shows an UNRRA man feeding tin-can food to the "God's little birds" of the magazine's title, the insatiable war refugees. The three aid organizations named, UNRRA, IRO and BALF, were the center of refugees' talk, verse and cartoons, and because the material situation depended on them they are pictured "very respectfully." A glossary of exile life printed in the magazine gave them funny definitions: UNRRA, a shelter "from the brown and red plague" (Lithuanian: "užuovėja nuo rudojo ir raudonojo maro"); BALF, "shoes, coats, patched quilted jackets and underskirts." When UNRRA wound down in 1947 one cover showed a figure kneeling at an UNRRA grave, which the author calls "not funny but chilling," a sign of how much aid mattered and how feared its loss was.
*Candidate application:* Jokes aimed at the hand that feeds are constrained: the dependent group mocks itself (its appetite, its dependence) while praising the donor. A scene in which a newcomer mocks the aid-giver would be a documented violation, and the cold cover is a reminder that the humor stops where real fear starts.

**[E03-33]. Internal self-government as a target: strict administrators, would-be revolutionaries, and rule-breakers.** (AC-34, [p.8-10])
The author says the camps' residents elected DP committees and camp administrators from among the Lithuanians themselves, and the cartoons show that harsher administrators drew criticism and mockery from their own people; in one strongly exaggerated cartoon a camp administrator is drawn as the commandant of a concentration camp and the camp as an extermination camp (summarized restrained; the author reads it as compatriots' criticism of a strict administrator). Osmolskis's cartoon "Hellish Comedy" (*Pekliškoji komedija*) mocks a brave local "activist," a camp resident ready to overthrow the authorities; the artist wrote that all this trouble would only give the devil work, since such activists merely spoil their compatriots' affairs. The author calls it unusually didactic for Lithuanian cartoons and an example of how cartoonists can shape opinion for or against. Cartoons threatened nearly every punishment of hell for those who break the common rules of camp life, and a cartoon "Sound of Hell" (my rendering of *Peklos garsas*) singles out repatriation of refugees from the French zone as its own hell-worthy punishment. A "not funny at all" 1947 cover shows a couple being driven out of the U.S.-run zone by an UNRRA representative for disobeying orders, tied by the author to the constant need for permits to leave a camp.
*Candidate application:* A self-governing group laughs at its own rule-enforcers and at its hotheads in the same breath; the loud agitator is a figure of fun in a community that depends on order. For a former soldier, the mocked "activist" and the strict administrator are two roles he could be cast in by the strangers around him.

**[E03-34]. Rules of license under supervision: censorship, forbidden topics, and the missing courtship and gender humor.** (AC-34, [p.3-5])
All publications were checked and censored by the military authorities before printing, and German printers could not print without permission. The author reasons that rightless war refugees would have found it dangerous to play with politics, so the spectrum of themes and the meaning of images must be read against close supervision. Named off-limits topics: the conduct of the victorious Allies, Soviet officers' repatriation agitation, the 1948 currency reform, black-market speculation, and barter. Few drawings address culture and morals, and the pre-war staples (human social failings, men's and women's relations, flirting) are almost entirely absent from camp cartoons, as though subtler questions of human contact had receded into the distance. Political drawings about the threat of being returned to Lithuania appear in the earliest stage but disappear from the later DP press. The author also reports that in *Dievo paukštelis* there are no drawings that ridicule specific named persons.
*Candidate application:* What a displaced group does not joke about (its protectors' conduct, its own black market, courtship) is as characterizing as what it does, and the missing categories are partly outside pressure and partly the cost of survival. "No named individuals as targets" is a possible house rule of a small dependent community.

**[E03-35]. Emigration as the dominant later theme: humiliating screening, a happy-ending story, and leaving Europe with open delight.** (AC-34, [p.5], [p.10])
In 1946-47 the dominant topics shift to emigration, "screenings," and imagined pictures of other countries (mostly America). The author says that for Lithuanians, like Latvians, Estonians, Ukrainians and Jews, the question of life prospects was the most important, so affidavits and screenings recur constantly, and Allied authorities wanted everyone out of devastated Germany quickly (citing Wyman). The cartoonists, especially the editor-writer, narrate a whole story of a DP agonizing over departure: doubts about where is best to go, joy on receiving an affidavit because departure means an end to degrading inspections, labor offices, coal mines and "the martyrdom of hunger on 500 calories." Two multi-figure sheets, "Hold On, They Are Already Taking Us" and "We're Going" (my renderings of *Laikykit, jau veža* and *Važiuojam*), pack nearly all the DP problems into one page: uncertainty and imagined dangers, humiliating health checks, document handling at American consulates, pictures of the German refugee camp and American cities; another shows a DP family ten years later on the American shore. In the cartoonists' eyes, leaving Germany is a story with a happy ending, with Europe left "with undisguised joy"; the figure is confused by forced, dignity-hurting procedures but forever dreaming and hoping.
*Candidate application:* The exile's hope has a bureaucratic shape (affidavits, screening); the jokes describe the sequence of papers and indignities. Contemplating the happy ending is the comic engine, and the humiliation is what it is set against.

**[E03-36]. Political satire when it does appear: named culprits, a shrunken self-image, and an amateur booklet.** (AC-34, [p.10-11])
The press was dominated by social cartoons; political ones were few. One early political cartoon appeared in 1946 in an amateur booklet, *Pro plyšį* ("Through the Crack"), whose artist used a biting tone, alluding to the Soviet deportations of Lithuanians to Siberia (the text dates them 1940), to convey the painful, topical subject of being returned to the Soviet Union. The author calls Lithuanian political cartoons notably open and committed: they show the refugees understood the causes of their tragedy, its likely consequences and its direct culprits, with all the satirical heat directed at the two main culprits of the Second World War, Stalin and Hitler, and a Stalin portrait that "does not lack frightening features." The Lithuanians themselves are drawn by the two main artists as small, shrunken people blinded by Stalin's sun and running from it.
*Candidate application:* The contrast between an aimed, unequivocal satire of the ones responsible and a self-portrait as diminished is a stable pairing: fury outward, shrinkage inward. The aggressor is shown as monstrous; the victim group is shown as small.

**[E03-37]. The DP male type changes over time, and the woman's image narrows.** (AC-34, [p.11-15])
The author says the DP man is a much more prominent character than the DP woman, and from the drawings reconstructs a sequence for the 1946-49 male type: impoverished but cheerful lad; confused, frightened of Stalin, running away; suspected of crimes; a peaceful good-natured fellow like an angel; and finally, in the later U.S. comic, a prosperous, rather aggressive type defending his home and rights. The pre-war Lithuanian woman, shown in the Kaunas magazine *Spaktyva* as a fashionable beauty, becomes in Osmolskis a small wronged girl pulled by the pigtails (a cartoon titled "Lithuania Between Two Liberators," whose meaning the text does not unpack), and in the editor's German-period drawings only an angry housewife; the author calls this a narrower range of images than before the war.
*Candidate application:* A displaced community's stock comic types are a diagnosis: the poor merry fellow, the frightened runner, the suspect, the saint. The suspect type (the DP drawn as someone presumed to have committed crimes) is the one a stranger-in-exile character may find himself cast in.

**[E03-38]. Collective merrymaking: aid goods turned into parties, and pre-war carnival traditions kept and updated.** (AC-34, [p.8])
The author cites an artist's 1947 interview about the Freiburg arts-and-crafts school staff, who were glad to receive woolen cloth from the American Lithuanian relief organization (each teacher got three meters, enough for suits to last a while) and recalled a barrel of American bacon and a weekly ration of white wine. Delighted that neither compatriots in America nor the French authorities had forgotten them, the artists organized parties and carnivals; in Freiburg they held noisy, merry "Cultural Fridays," mostly in one artist's apartment. The author concludes that even in hard exile the tradition of collective merrymaking did not vanish but developed; the pre-war Kaunas carnival was not forgotten, but whereas pre-war revelers dressed up as fairy-tale figures, harlequins and Orientals, a photograph of an exile carnival (about 1948) shows much more topical characters, political figures.
*Candidate application:* Gifts of goods become occasions for social gatherings, and the costumes show a community's concerns change from fantasy to current politics. A regular in-person gathering (a weekly "night") is a plausible structure for a defector community's humor.

**[E03-39]. After resettlement: a revived magazine, humor that is "different and about other things," a named target, and the old DP type vanishing fast.** (AC-34, [p.5], [p.12-15])
One issue of *Dievo paukštelis* was revived in the United States in 1955, noticeably lower in artistic quality, which the author uses to ask how and why Lithuanian humor changes. Letters from Lithuanians in the U.S. to the editor then in Australia said that U.S. Lithuanians laugh a lot but differently and at different things (citing Misiūnas); a journalist observed that in America the laughter is cruder and mostly in comic-strip form, telling a whole story rather than an event. The 1955 cover ironically shows a prosperous Lithuanian (dollars on trees, whisky on the table, a dog barring the door to fundraisers) and, novelly, targets a named person (a name plate on the door), which the author reads as a "chill" of compatriots' separation and fragmentation. A comic strip tells a European's change into an American. A puzzles page announces a new era in two rhyming riddles about the cuckoo versus the DP (the DP "bewails his woe," in my gloss) and about the old versus the new immigrant (the old one "gets more Lithuanian," the new one "gets more American"); the DP image was being erased quickly. A well-known U.S. cartoonist's comic (about 1949) takes up the problem of speaking English through a Lithuanian landlord who has adopted the American way of life meeting prospective tenants, and the author describes the resulting type as prosperous, rather aggressive and mean, speaking a mix of English and Lithuanian, strong and defending his home and rights; the author says this is no finished portrait, since the image changes with life.
*Candidate application:* Documented drift over time: humor moves from shared-ordeal topics to wealth, assimilation and named individuals as the group disperses and its members' fortunes diverge; the author notes the weary, bewildered DP image was being erased quickly from the revived magazine's pages. A resettled character may discover that the people he now jokes with laugh at different things than the ones he lost.

**[E03-40]. Humor from the homeland side: Soviet-Lithuanian satire of emigrants.** (AC-34, [p.2], [p.16])
In the 1950s, after the satirical magazine *Šluota* was revived in Soviet Lithuania, emigrants became one of its important topics, framed as the matter of "capitalists, the rotten bourgeoisie." The author says its joking was malicious, ideologically confrontational with both capitalism and each person individually, and that a 1956-57 cartoon portrayed a general the Soviets disliked together with an American in a disrespectful, insulting way. The author also calls Soviet-era cartoons thoroughly propagandistic and one-sided, of interest mainly to historians of propaganda.
*Candidate application:* The exile or defector has a double: the humor made about him by the side he left, which is hostile, named-target and ideological. It is the opposite number of the exile community's own self-mockery.

**[E03-41]. The author's conclusions: exile humor was moralizing and addressed to the whole stricken community; it drew the group weaker than the facts warranted.** (AC-34, [p.16-17], English summary [p.18])
Against a background of uncertainty, poverty, sadness, insecurity and psychological war trauma, the author says making readers smile was not easy, so the humor "was neither light nor carelessly funny" (English summary). The grotesque satirical images emphasized the sad side of DP life. Exile humor is "moralizing," awakens compatriots' conscience, tries to sharpen minds and correct some behavior, because its addressee is every compatriot hit by the shared misfortune; the aim was to ironize and mock a little but also to steer public opinion in the desired direction. Though most themes were social (daily life, aid, work, emigration), the cartoon's message almost always had a political tinge and expressed the position of a large group, the camp's residents as one nation. Caricature feeds on a small deviation from the norm and exaggerates the "defect," making the person depicted a hostage of the grotesque situation, both funny and a loser; the Lithuanian DPs appear weak (in situation, not character), a mass helpless before bosses, seen as victims of circumstance more than they were, since the facts of their social, political and cultural activity show the traits of a strong, united, determined people. The author closes that smiling at negative things and laughing in the hardest time is also a trait of strong personalities.
*Candidate application:* The reader should note the author's own interpretive move: the community's humor draws it weaker than it was, and the laughter itself is read as evidence of strength. A defector's self-mockery could be written as understatement of his own competence.
*Cross-use:* A Tepenian exile or settler magazine could be a vehicle for moral instruction as well as gags, which is a different function than coping or resistance.

## Pattern roll-up

Organized by the lens headings the sources actually speak to. Numbers in brackets are findings in this file. Scope: documented for genocide victims and besieged or persecuted civilians (AC-10), one set of Ukrainian war memes (AC-28) and one displaced-persons press (AC-34); **none of it documents professional soldiers' own humor**, and each pattern should be treated as a candidate pattern for a soldier turned exile, not a soldier finding.

**Functions**
- **Coping, cohesion and criticism, stacked, in that order of political salience** (AC-10 [E03-2, E03-19]). All humor copes; some also binds; some also attacks. A scene should say which of the three is doing the work.
- **Coping by the collision of normal-life phrasing with abnormal conditions** (full-train joke, mock-professional remark, "pretend the train is full") [E03-3, E03-4]; humor as a sign of having crossed from shock and apathy into adaptation [E03-4].
- **Cohesion: humor as an entry qualification** for the mutual-aid group in camps (listed among practical traits) and in besieged Sarajevo (acceptance conditional on laughing at oneself) [E03-5, E03-8]; shared ordeal as identity marker (having passed checkpoints) [E03-8]; hermetic jokes only the victimized can decode, "experiential deformation" [E03-2, E03-12, E03-19].
- **Criticism and the oppressor made small**: imagining the enraged guard humiliated; infantilizing the dictator; inverting the regime's insult into a badge; parodying the regime's own posters; performing the enemy's accusation back [E03-6, E03-13, E03-26].
- **Moral instruction and opinion-steering**, a function beyond coping and resistance, documented in a displaced-persons press whose addressee is the whole stricken community [E03-33, E03-41].
- **Emotional sharing, not relief**: Ukrainian memes evoke gloating, grief, anger and despair and are said to serve mainly to express them together [E03-21, E03-24]; belonging and national value reinforcement [E03-22].
- **Re-humanization**: a persistently dehumanized group keeps cultural activity, including humor, to hold normalcy [E03-1, E03-9].

**Targets**
- Own group's passivity or fatalism (self-ridicule that also undercuts the perpetrator) [E03-6, E03-17]; own hunger, dependence and appetite [E03-30, E03-32]; own pessimists [E03-23]; own rule-enforcers, hotheads and rule-breakers [E03-33]; one's own funeral photo and fear of death [E03-11].
- The oppressor's symbols, leader, propaganda and bureaucratic language [E03-13, E03-25, E03-36].
- The enemy's dead, the enemy state's treatment of its soldiers' lives, notorious enemy-side figures (in the Ukrainian sample) [E03-25 to E03-27].
- The aid-giver is, by rule, mostly spared [E03-32]. Named individuals are conspicuously absent in the German camps and appear only after dispersal [E03-34, E03-39].

**Forms and mechanics**
- Oral jokes (camps), printed parody and radio (Sarajevo: parody guidebook, hit-parade show), cartoons, slogans, puppets and video (Syria), typed sheets and cartoon magazines (Lithuanian DP camps), memes in chat channels (Ukraine) [E03-14, E03-29, E03-20].
- Recurring devices: absurd normalcy; mock professional competence; parody of an institutional document form (travel guide, glossary of aid acronyms); borrowed pop-culture dialogue; reuse of insider codes (casualty codes, brand names, a pun on word stress); mock pidgin of the host language; turning a taboo into a routine or festival frame; performing an accusation back; euphemism that hardens into slang [E03-3, E03-9, E03-23, E03-25, E03-26, E03-27, E03-30].
- Visual and cultural vehicles reused from before the catastrophe: pre-war magazine graphics, the carnival tradition with new topical costumes [E03-29, E03-38].

**Rules of license**
- **Position earns license**: victims may mock themselves and perpetrators; perpetrators' descendants may not joke about victims; mocking the perpetrator is safer but must not be too light; the joker's clear intention (to cope or prevent forgetting) is what is said to allow it [E03-16].
- **Audience matters**: a joke among insiders loses its sense in print (context collapse) [E03-16].
- **Scarce-goods license**: a recipient of an outside parcel may insult officials and the neighbors let it pass [E03-31].
- **Dependence limits the joke**: aid organizations are portrayed respectfully, with the one cover that is not funny at all [E03-32].
- **Supervision limits the joke**: pre-print censorship, named forbidden topics (victors' conduct, repatriation agitation, currency reform, black market, barter) [E03-34].
- **Taboo erodes with habituation**: the moral line moves in Sarajevo; humor turns darker or cynical as violence escalates in Syria (authors' hypothesis) [E03-7, E03-18].

**Limits and failure modes**
- Humor can be permitted because it is harmless to the oppressor (camp theater as distraction) [E03-6].
- Numbness (prolupati) as the alternative to jokes: loss of the fear needed to stay alive [E03-7].
- Guilt, shame and defensiveness about having laughed, with refusal to repeat the jokes to an outsider [E03-15].
- Pre-existing self-mockery of passivity can become a negative group image (the shrunken refugee) that understates the group's actual activity [E03-37, E03-41].
- Post-war aggressive ethnic humor breaks a prewar taboo [E03-17]; hostile humor aimed at the exiles from the homeland side [E03-40]; fragmentation and named targets after dispersal [E03-39].
- Chilling rather than funny covers where fear of losing aid cannot be joked into comfort [E03-32].

**Time course**
- During: first cohesive and playful, then darker as violence increases (Syria); early consolation about poverty, then emigration as theme (DP camps) [E03-12, E03-30, E03-35].
- Just after: functions persist; guilt; therapy sees humor as a door; hostile jokes toward the former enemy and sharp self-critique about passivity (Bosnia) [E03-15, E03-17].
- Long after: second and third generations use humor in books and stage work; the term "survivor" itself becomes a joke subject [E03-15]. In a resettled community, humor shifts to wealth, assimilation and named individuals [E03-39].

**Civilian-facing register**
- Insider jokes are hard to tell outside the ordeal, partly because macabre and partly because only the victimized can decode them; peacetime standards fail to appreciate them [E03-19]; insiders can refuse to repeat a joke to an interviewer [E03-15].

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics**
- Humor as an entry qualification for a group of strangers, with shared-ordeal in-jokes as identity markers [E03-5, E03-8, E03-12].
- Pre-loss tradition carried by specific people (writers, artists) into the exile press [E03-28, E03-29].
- The exile community's own governance, rule-enforcers and hotheads as targets [E03-33].
- A homeland-side humor aimed at the exile (spiteful, named target, ideological) as the opposite number [E03-40].
- Adaptation of a hated institution's vocabulary and propaganda as raw material [E03-25, E03-13].

**Cross-cultural differences**
- Jewish, Bosnian and Syrian cases all had a prior self-deprecating minority tradition [E03-1]; the Syrian case is the one tied unequivocally to political activism, and the media shift from oral to social media [E03-14]; the Ukrainian sample is nation-building and enemy-directed [E03-22]; the Lithuanian press is moralizing and community-addressed, with strict external controls [E03-34, E03-41].

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Brief versus source (AC-10).** The unit brief expected Armenian, Cambodian and Rwandan material; AC-10 does not treat them (Rwanda appears once, in a film joke). Its three cases are the Holocaust (camps, mostly), Sarajevo and Syria. For Armenian, Cambodian or Rwandan victim humor a different source is needed.
2. **No soldiers, no defectors in any of the three.** Soldiers touch the material only through a therapy study with Vietnam veterans cited secondhand (AC-10 [E03-15]), two Bosnian soldiers in a film, armed rebels in a photograph, and Russian soldiers as targets (AC-28). Everything here is a candidate transfer from civilian victims and displaced persons to a former soldier, not an observation about soldiers.
3. **AC-28 is not a content analysis.** The brief expected categories, targets and functions; the paper offers conceptual-metaphor families and a theoretical argument. No counts, no author or audience data, no test of its coping claim, no discussion of limits beyond saying benign-violation theory does not fit. The sample is called random but selected by three criteria. Eleven figures of memes are not in the text copy; my reading depends on the author's descriptions. The author's own stance is partisan and the descriptions of enemy-side facts (casualty totals, compensation, propaganda claims, assassinations) are not independently checked. The sample's silence on jokes about Ukrainian dead or wounded is a property of its selection (popular war narratives, insider clarity), not evidence that such jokes do not exist.
4. **AC-34 was read in Lithuanian.** My translations are mine; idiom and wordplay are the weakest (the *Dievo paukštelis* and *Dypukas* titles, rhyming riddles, a few insults I did not translate). The English summary is brief and appears to end after two paragraphs (the PDF has 18 pages and stops at printed p.62); it may continue on a page not in the file. The 26 illustrations (cartoons and photographs) were not viewed; the author's descriptions and captions are all I had. The evidence is a bound collection of magazines plus archival photographs; the voice is that of artists, writers and editors, not of ordinary camp residents.
5. **Internal tensions in AC-34.** (a) The footnote list of 1944-48 humor titles includes *Kuntaplis* and *Aitvaras*, which the main text identifies as pre-war Kaunas magazines. (b) The text says over twenty titles in 1944-48; the English summary says about twenty in 1946-48. (c) The author says political cartoons were few under censorship, yet the conclusion says the message of the cartoons "almost always had a political tinge." (d) The text dates the Soviet deportations 1940, whereas the mass deportation of Lithuanians is generally dated 1941; a check against another source is needed before relying on the date.
6. **AC-10 cautions.** Many examples rest on documentaries, news articles and blogs (Syria), a news-blog compilation (Sarajevo jokes), or a single interview line; the claim that the Nazis "allowed and even encouraged" some camp humor rests mainly on Frankl and the Westerbork theater. The authors state they cannot draw definitive conclusions on Syria's post-victimization humor, and the "humor will get darker and reach cynicism" claim is a hypothesis. "Anna" and "Anne" van Raalte appear in text and reference list respectively. Garrick (2006) is cited secondhand here and is the same lead flagged in E04.
7. **Leads from titles only (not read).** In AC-10's references: Lipman 1993, *Laughter in Hell*; Herzog 2012, *Dead Funny: Telling Jokes in Hitler's Germany*; Obrdlik 1942, "Gallows humor: a sociological phenomenon," *American Journal of Sociology* (titled exactly on the project's topic, under Nazi-occupied Czechoslovakia); Pawelczynska 1979, *Values and Violence in Auschwitz*; Maček 2009, *Sarajevo under Siege: Anthropology in Wartime*; Sheftel 2011 (dark humor as counter-memory in Bosnia); Kurtović 2012; Kalmann 2009 and 2013 (humor in Holocaust literature); Wedeen 1999; Tangherlini 2000 (paramedics' storytelling); Garrick 2006; Camps-Febrer 2012 (political humor against the Syrian regime); Ostrower 2000 (Tel Aviv thesis). In AC-28: Avdagić 2019 and Willinger et al. 2017 (black-humor processing). In AC-34: Misiūnas 2004, *Barakų kultūros knygos* (Lithuanian DP publishing, 1945-1952), the 2013 reprint of Andriušis's *Ir vis dėlto juokimės*, and Wyman 1998, *DPs: Europe's Displaced Persons*.
8. **Original needed in a browser or print:** the AC-28 meme figures (to check the author's readings) and the AC-34 illustrations.
