**AC-14 (Pinkston 2018). The priority source; read in full.** An M.A. thesis in English of about 16,500 words including notes and a Works Cited list (about 50 printed pages). The title page identifies the author as a U.S. Army captain with an M.A. in intelligence studies, so this is an officer's reading of the memoir record. Method: close reading of published accounts, with no stated selection criteria and no sample size. By my count it uses about 15 nonfiction Wars on Terror (WoT) sources (soldier and Marine memoirs, a compiled blog anthology, a Marine oral-history volume, two journalists, two spouses, Abu Ghraib court and press testimony), three fictional works (two by veterans, plus a 2008 film), about ten historical cases (British World War I songs, two Marine officers' final transmissions, a World War I hero's diary, the Indian Wars, Babi Yar), and three non-military community-joking cases (Gein, Borden, Challenger). Part I is introduction and theory, Part II is history, Part III is the WoT accounts; there is no separate conclusion heading. The theory is borrowed from philosophy, writing manuals and popular works (Bergson, Casey, Legman, Sutton, Koestler, Smith, Grossman, Hedges); **it cites none of the empirical military-humor scholarship** (nothing of the Johais/Tidy/Sløk-Andersen kind covered in E01). **The copy has no abstract, table of contents or acknowledgments.** The manifest lists only the `2_` main-text URL, and the PDF begins with the title page, so any `1_` front-matter file was never collected; I could not read it and nothing in the main text depends on it. **Does NOT contain:** any interviews or data of its own; any post-service humor (Pinkston says outright that one memoirist gives no account of return to civilian life); any defector, dissenter or exile analysis (the conscientious-objector memoirist Aidan Delgado is used only for unit humor and decline); any non-American army in the WoT; Afghan or Iraqi voices beyond two interpreters' jokes and detainee testimony; women soldiers' humor (women appear only as journalists, spouses, a detainee abuser, and targets of one soldier's joke); a catalog of forms such as nicknames or pranks (one nickname origin and one stock joke appear); and any measure of how often or how much any humor occurs. Examples are sometimes violent, sexual or slur-laden; I describe them minimally and do not reproduce slurs.

**AC-29 (Jensen). Mostly out of scope; about a tenth is relevant.** A literature/game-studies M.A. thesis of about 28,000 words including a table of contents and Works Cited (about 62 printed pages). The title block names Jens Damborg Jensen, a second name (Brian Graham; role not stated) and the date "31 May 2014"; **that date cannot be right for the text as it stands**, because the thesis discusses *Call of Duty: Black Ops III* and *Battlefield Hardline* (both 2015) as released, so the copy is a later revision (the URL says "Final Version"). Method: a comparative case study of five games (*Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare*, *Modern Warfare 2*, *Battlefield: Bad Company*, *Bad Company 2*, *Spec Ops: The Line*) through three lenses: Frye's narrative mythoi (romance versus satire), representation of enemies and soldiers, and "moral disengagement" in players. **It is not about humor.** After reading it all, a term count gave: "humor" 1 (as "humorous end"), "joke" 1, "laugh" 1, "banter" 0, "funny" 0, while "satire" and its forms occur about 48 times, meaning a literary mode, not comedy. The plot summary, narrative theory, American-exceptionalism politics, and Russian/Middle Eastern enemy-representation passages were read and skipped as irrelevant to this project except where a point is extracted below.

## Findings

### From AC-14 (Pinkston 2018), Part I: introduction and theory

**[E06-1]. The thesis's claim: combat humor is both relief and risk, and the title is a quotation about barbarism.** (AC-14, `[p.2-3]`, `[p.49-50]`)
The epigraph and opening take "death hilarious" from a passage in Cormac McCarthy's *Blood Meridian* in which a war party looks "like a company of mounted clowns, death hilarious", so the title links humor with violence from the first page. Pinkston then states the thesis: in war, humor is a catharsis that relieves emotions that would otherwise overwhelm or impede combatants, but it can also desensitize them to other human beings and impair both lawful, productive performance in combat and later reintegration. The memoirs of WoT participants are said to show this duality: humor as coping, and humor as an enabler of violence and cruelty. The closing "crux" is that combat humor "can lead soldiers to very unfunny places", and that accounts of it should work as a warning to soldiers and society, not as stigma against returnees. Pinkston disclaims throughout that most soldiers are not killers or racists.
*Candidate application:* Treat a soldier character's humor as a tool with a documented dark slope, not as pure charm: the device that carries a unit may also be what hollows a person. For the Cancer defector, the useful question is how far down the slope he went, or only watched others go.

**[E06-2]. Pinkston's working vocabulary: "constructive distraction," "remaking," "rationalization."** (AC-14, `[p.3]`, `[p.7]`, `[p.13-15]`)
Pinkston takes "constructive distraction" from a TIME essay (Jeffrey Kluger) describing humor as a way of "pushing back" against trauma, and uses it as an umbrella for any mental move that makes an event bearable. "Remaking" is Pinkston's own recurring verb for recasting a person, death, act or enemy as lighter, smaller or contemptible. "Rationalization" comes from Gershon Legman: an attempt to make a charged situation understandable or "even endurable, if only as a 'joke'". Pinkston applies all three to jokes, songs, slang names for the enemy, euphemisms for killing and a diary's wording, so some of Pinkston's "humor" is distancing language that is not funny at all.
*Candidate application:* Keep two things apart that Pinkston blurs: jokes meant to be funny, and renaming habits that create distance without laughter. A soldier's speech can carry both, and the second is detectable by what words are avoided.

**[E06-3]. Communities joke about horror within hours or days, and compulsively: Gein, Borden, Challenger.** (AC-14, `[p.3-6]`, `[p.9]`)
Pinkston opens with three non-military cases. (a) After the 1957 Ed Gein discovery, the Wisconsin town of Plainfield produced "geiners," dark riddle jokes about the killer; the psychiatrist George Arndt called their spread so common it could be called a "mass repetition compulsion". The two examples given are riddles (one punning on the killer being let out to find a New Year's Eve date by digging one up; one defining a "Gein Beer" as having body and no head). (b) The 1890s Lizzie Borden case, which Arndt cited as the only other American criminal case where a local population jokes collectively (a riddle where she goes to "axe" her sister when a salesman calls). (c) The 1986 Challenger explosion, where J. Jerome Zolten recorded jokes within hours or days (an acronym riddle; a comedian's quip about no homework for the children) and described black humor as a way "to subvert pain through joking". Pinkston adds that a psychologist (Rudolf Mathias, cited through Schechter's Gein biography) likened the Gein jokes to "the jokes exchanged among soldiers who are going into battle". Pinkston's reading is that all three are distancing from trauma with a hostile edge toward the target; Pinkston's Schadenfreude explanation for the Challenger jokes (envy of the teacher aboard) is speculation.
*Candidate application:* A catastrophe's first joke wave is a communal reflex that can be dated to the first hours. A district or unit hit by a disaster can show a joke cycle that starts almost at once and repeats.
*Cross-use:* Applies to any non-military community (an exile town, a disaster-struck district); the pattern is not specific to soldiers.

**[E06-4]. Pinkston's theory of why the jokes work: distance, incongruity plus truth, and disdain as antitoxin.** (AC-14, `[p.6-9]`)
Four strands, all borrowed. *Distance:* Henri Bergson's "disinterested spectator", illustrated by John Casey's story of a stage actor's pratfall that drew laughter when it happened far upstage, laughter except in the first two rows halfway down, and silence plus "Ow" at the lip of the stage; far away, the audience become "cartoon watchers", so closeness decides laughter or sympathy. *Incongruity and truth* (Casey): a joke needs an incongruous image and something recognizably true. *Disdain:* Dana Sutton says humor expresses disdain toward its targets and acts as "a kind of antitoxin" against their power to make us feel bad; a line of thinkers (Koestler, Cicero, Baudelaire, Freud, Brottman) stress hostility, and Richard Smith's Schadenfreude (pleasure in another's misfortune, often an envied person's). Brottman adds that what people want to take their minds off is, finally, "always and only death". Casey also says laughter is "on the side of the bad boys", relief for those under drill sergeants and the like, and Pinkston says military memoirs are full of such "bad boy" service members.
*Candidate application:* A usable question for any soldier joke: where is the spectator standing, what true thing is named, and at whom is the disdain pointed? The "Ow" reflex suggests a person who can suddenly no longer laugh has been moved closer, not become humorless.

**[E06-5]. Pinkston's taxonomy of combat humor: four kinds and a slide.** (AC-14, `[p.9]`, `[p.17]`, `[p.22]`, `[p.37]`)
Pulled together from scattered statements, Pinkston distinguishes: (1) **incidental humor**, funny with the military setting beside the point (Bourke's Apache anecdote, the curfew joke), neither therapeutic nor aggressive; (2) **therapeutic humor**, constructive distraction from fear, death, killing, grief, boredom; (3) **aggressive humor**, disdain toward a target (enemy, superior, rear-echelon soldiers, civilians), usually harmless when the target never hears it; and (4) **violence as the joke**, where harm to helpless people is itself funny, which Pinkston calls distraction "though not a constructive one". Pinkston describes combat humor as sliding from (2) processing, to (3) aggressive mocking, to (4) humor derived from violence and death, and says aggressive humor becomes "counterproductive" when soldiers seek out violence only to find it funny. The categories are Pinkston's inference, untested, and the same example is often assigned to more than one.
*Candidate application:* A single gradient lets a writer place a person, unit or whole culture: what is funny (an event, an absent target, a present victim) shows how far along the slide they are.

### Part II: historical cases

**[E06-6]. World War I British troop song: "I Have No Pain, Dear Mother, Now."** (AC-14, `[p.10-11]`)
A parody song (sung to the tune of "My Love Is Like a Red, Red Rose," from Brophy and Partridge's *The Long Trail*) in which a dying soldier tells his mother he has no pain but is very dry and asks to be connected to a brewery and left there to die. Pinkston reads it with Casey's two tests: incongruity (a badly wounded man taken to a brewery, not an infirmary) and truth (soldiers would indeed have valued a brewery visit). Treating death flippantly may shrink the fear of it, making death "benign or comical" in Kluger's phrase. No singer reports what it did; the function is Pinkston's reading.
*Candidate application:* A soldier culture's wounded-man songs can substitute the vice for the hospital; the wish is for comfort in dying, not for rescue.

**[E06-7]. Two last-transmission jokes from World War II: Devereux and Dollins.** (AC-14, `[p.11-12]`)
Marine Major James Devereux, commanding Wake Island's defense, sent a final telegraph message (cited via Grossman) asking that more of the enemy be sent, using an ethnic slur. Marine Major Ray Dollins, an aerial spotter at Iwo Jima hit by ground fire and spiraling toward the sea, was heard on the observer radio frequency singing a Rodgers and Hammerstein song with the lyrics altered to a "terrible feeling" that everything was "coming my way"; the singing stopped at the last line and the plane crashed. Pinkston: both mocked death at the moment of dying and diminished the hearers' trauma by remaking death as mockable; hearers were fellow Marines on radio or telegraph. The sources are secondary (Grossman; Newcomb) and no hearer's reaction is quoted.
*Candidate application:* The last joke over the radio is a recognized form whose audience is the unit that has to survive it; a veteran might remember one such transmission as the moment humor did the most work.

**[E06-8]. Euphemism and enemy-naming as distancing: Grossman's catalog, the interpreter's story, York's diary.** (AC-14, `[p.13-16]`)
Dave Grossman's list: soldiers rarely "kill"; the enemy is knocked over, wasted, taken out, mopped up; the enemy is given dehumanizing ethnic nicknames; weapons get benign names; a bullet becomes a "round". Anecdote: American soldiers in Vietnam asked a former Viet Cong interpreter whether it was right to call the VC derogatory names; the interpreter said it made no difference, his own jungle company called Americans an animal name ("monkeys") and they killed and ate monkeys. Pinkston reads this as distancing on both sides; Chris Hedges says a soldier who sees the enemy's humanity "makes a troubled and ineffective killer". Alvin York's World War I diary, though otherwise detailed, never writes of shooting people, using "touched off" about killing three times and saving "kill" for what he didn't want to do more than he had to; Pinkston reads this as making killing unreal at the moment of execution. He contrasts York (distance from the act) with the Vietnam terms (distance from the enemy person). This is vocabulary, not jokes.
*Candidate application:* What words a soldier character will not use for killing, and what labels they use for the other side, are characterization; the avoidance can last years after service.

**[E06-9]. Enlisted mockery of older superiors: "Old Soldiers Never Die."** (AC-14, `[p.16]`)
The World War I British parody verse keeps the familiar refrain ("old soldiers never die") but ends the second verse with young ones wishing they would. Pinkston reads the enlisted singers as mocking older officers and senior NCOs who "caused so much of their combat grief", and as expressing bitterness that older men farther from the fighting die at a lower rate than younger front-line enlisted soldiers. This is Pinkston's inference; no singer is quoted.
*Candidate application:* Parody of an institutional anthem is a documented way the bottom rank voices resentment of the top without open insubordination.

**[E06-10]. A history of laughter turning to violence: Babi Yar and "a bizarre carnival."** (AC-14, `[p.17]`)
Pinkston's bridge from coping to cruelty: a witness to the Babi Yar killings in Ukraine said German soldiers beating victims toward the ravine "laughed happily, as if they were watching a circus act." Hedges writes that war "turns human reality into a bizarre carnival" and empowers those with a taste for killing. Pinkston: most combat veterans are neither racist nor murderous, but the combat environment encourages aggression and so humor in combat grows more aggressive and violent, and the risk of remaking a person is dehumanizing them so far that violence itself becomes funny.
*Candidate application:* The warning-sign for a character or culture is when the joke stops needing a point and the violence itself is the amusement.

**[E06-11]. Incidental humor: Bourke's Apache anecdote.** (AC-14, `[p.17-18]`)
Captain John G. Bourke, a junior officer in the Indian Wars, recalled as one of the funniest incidents he knew watching a famed, bold Chiricahua man clinging to rocks (a fall would have broken limbs) to avoid meeting his mother-in-law. Pinkston: incongruity (a fierce warrior panicked) plus truth (the mother-in-law trope); the military setting is incidental; neither therapeutic nor aggressive.
*Candidate application:* Not all soldier humor is gallows humor; ordinary comic observation about family and fear of embarrassment runs alongside it and gives a character a non-dark register.

**[E06-12]. The skull jokes and the Fort Bowie throat gesture.** (AC-14, `[p.18-19]`)
Two cases from the frontier. (a) Soldiers under Custer found a skull with an apparent bullet hole, and correspondents with the column marveled at how quickly the men went from discussing its origins to joking; Pinkston reads it as remaking the situation to distract from the chance their own skulls might lie there one day (Pinkston's inference). (b) At the surrender of Apache leaders (Geronimo and Naiche), a boy named Sam Kenoi (via Angie Debo) saw enlisted soldiers approach "when the officer wasn't looking" and draw hands across their own throats; Kenoi said the Apache's discomfort greatly amused the soldiers; when the Apache were loaded into wagons at Fort Bowie the military band played "Auld Lang Syne" and the soldiers' laughter confused the Apache. Pinkston: neither target understood the joke, but it expressed aggression toward captured opponents.
*Candidate application:* Humor staged in front of a target who lacks the code (a song title as the joke) is a documented form; so is the enlisted habit of doing the dark thing behind the officer's back.

**[E06-13]. Laughing at massacre: Sand Creek, the Piegan village, and the officer who refused.** (AC-14, `[p.19-21]`)
In the 1864 Sand Creek attack, Captain Silas Soule's letter, which Pinkston quotes at length (not reproduced here), records horrifying killings of helpless people and Soule and other military witnesses reported Chivington's men "laughing as they shot helpless, cowering, unarmed and non-hostile Indians." Pinkston: the laughter shows the victims had been "remade" so far that violence became inherently funny, and Soule refused to take part because he saw "human beings." In 1870 a Piegan witness, Bear Head, described Major Eugene Baker walking through a burning friendly village laughing at charred corpses. Pinkston calls this "distraction, though not a constructive one" and says emotion at completion was good humor.
*Candidate application:* The documented counter-figure is the officer or soldier who does not laugh and says so; a defector-type character may be written as that person, with the cost that follows.

**[E06-14]. Pinkston's stated purpose for the atrocity cases.** (AC-14, `[p.21]`)
Pinkston says the purpose is not to claim soldiering is made of killers and racists, nor to criticize humor as a coping method, but to show war as a "bizarre carnival" suited to killers' skills and racists' distance, in which non-killers use humor constructively as the Gein-town joke-tellers did; the atrocity cases (Sand Creek, Baker, Babi Yar) are cautionary examples of too much distance from targets of disdain.
*Candidate application:* A character can be presented as someone who knows exactly where the line is and has seen others cross it, without being moralistic.

### Part III: Wars on Terror accounts

**[E06-15]. Incidental humor: the Afghan interpreter's curfew joke.** (AC-14, `[p.22-23]`)
Marine Matthew Bogdanos (*Thieves of Baghdad*, with William Patrick) records a joke an Afghan interpreter told American soldiers often: in a province with a ten o'clock curfew, one of two sentries sees a man in the street at nine forty-five and shoots him dead; when the other protests that it is not yet curfew, the shooter answers that he knew the man and he never would have made it home by ten. Pinkston: the joke mocks the absurd sentries, not "the Afghanis as a people group"; truth (real curfews and armed sentries) plus incongruity; military setting incidental; Pinkston calls it a "non-therapeutic, non-aggressive introductory point."
*Candidate application:* Host-nation interpreters and locals carry their own dark jokes and trade them with troops; a joke about arbitrary lethal authority can be shared across the line.
*Cross-use:* A defector or exile community may swap jokes with the people it once policed.

**[E06-16]. The warning given to an outsider, and the T-shirt: "If you die first, we split your gear."** (AC-14, `[p.23]`)
Before journalist Katherine Skiba went to Iraq with the 101st Airborne in 2003, veteran correspondent Joseph Galloway told her the grunt has "a perverse and often black sense of humor" and will pull her chain given the chance. At Fort Campbell, Skiba saw in a store outside the main gate a T-shirt aimed at deploying soldiers showing three soldiers in battle, one apparently dead, the other two going through his rucksack, captioned "If you die first, we split your gear." Skiba snickered, taking it as the soldier's "fight-to-the-death spirit". Pinkston: mockery of death, therapeutic for troops on the eve of deployment.
*Candidate application:* Gallows humor circulates commercially (merchandise sold at the base gate) as well as orally; and there is a ritual of warning an outsider about it.

**[E06-17]. The journalist's learning curve: "Gooey," and the perfect going-away party.** (AC-14, `[p.24]`)
After a suicide bombing of a U.S. convoy in Afghanistan, journalist Kim Barker asked a veteran BBC reporter what the situation was and he answered "Gooey"; Barker, though used to journalists' dark humor, wrote that it made her cringe. By the end of her tour, when a bomb went off outside her hotel on the day of her farewell party in Pakistan and gunfire followed, she "started laughing" and thought it "the perfect going-away party." Pinkston reads this as rationalization of constant chaos. Barker is a civilian journalist, not a soldier, and she dates her own change.
*Candidate application:* Acclimation can be dated and observed from outside: first the cringe at someone else's one-word joke, later the laugh at the same sort of event.

**[E06-18]. "Laughter is our only defense": the groin wound.** (AC-14, `[p.24-25]`)
In Staff Sergeant David Bellavia's memoir (*House to House*, with John R. Bruning), a fellow soldier takes a wound to the genitals; while the medic bandages it the others stand around joking. Bellavia calls such a wound "every soldier's nightmare" and states the function: dwell on it and drive ourselves crazy, or make fun of it, and "Laughter is our only defense." Pinkston: the narrator themself names the therapeutic mechanism.
*Candidate application:* The most feared wound is the one most joked about; humor goes where the fear is, even in the presence of the wounded person.

**[E06-19]. The home front's version: spouses and dark humor.** (AC-14, `[p.25]`)
Army wife Wendy Marr, quoted in Burden's anthology, waiting for a husband deployed to Afghanistan, says she learned fear, that she could not "kick your own butt out of depression," and learned to laugh at situations that would make most people shake their heads: "dark humor can help save your sanity." Lily Burana, whose husband deployed right after 9/11, wrote that she joked with him as he packed because her fear "ballooned out in front of me so big and ominous that humor was the only way I could see around it." Pinkston pairs them with Bellavia: Burana for fear, Marr for depression, Bellavia for dread and fear.
*Candidate application:* Gallows humor is not limited to those under fire; a partner left behind uses it too, and the soldier and the home-front spouse may share a joking vocabulary.

**[E06-20]. Hartley's "Awesome!" guide.** (AC-14, `[p.26]`)
Army Specialist Jason Hartley's memoir (*Just Another Soldier*) offers a mock usage guide: "Awesome!" is the reply to a chore assignment (burning waste), to news that another company shot at second platoon, and to news that insurgents killed a busload of Iraqi police graduates. Pinkston: one word applied to drudgery, near-fratricide and the deaths of allies shows the narrator remaking each to distance themselves from drudgery, horror and fury, and frustration.
*Candidate application:* A single flat enthusiastic word applied indiscriminately is a documented flattening device; it also shows the speaker's tragedy ranking has been suspended.

**[E06-21]. Laughing at near misses and incoming fire.** (AC-14, `[p.26-28]`)
Several accounts: Bellavia records a soldier chuckling about a mortar that "should've taken my head off". In Burden's anthology, Marines of Gunnery Sergeant Nicholas Popaditch at Fallujah "laugh a little" after returning fire, and Popaditch marvels that Marines find humor in nearly anything; an Army sergeant "Michael" and Sergeant "W." in Baghdad in 2003, hopelessly looking for cover under fire, share "a quick laugh," then use a lull to relieve themselves while joking; First Lieutenant Gregory Tomlin (*The Gods of Diyala*) joked in "gallows humor" about mortar impacts. Pinkston concedes this might be nervousness or relief, but the memoirists link it to genuine humor. First Lieutenant Neil Prakash, sniped at atop a tank at Fallujah, calls it "funny as hell" and says coming close to death and living is "absurdly funny"; he and the crew laughed more once safely behind the hull. Prakash states his reasoning (a hidden man trying to kill you and not yet succeeding).
*Candidate application:* Survival absurdity is a primary generator: the closer the miss, the funnier. A character can be written laughing at the exact moment others would be shaking, and later unable to explain it.

**[E06-22]. The "charmed" company: near-miss stories as performance, and pretending not to care.** (AC-14, `[p.28-29]`)
Sergeant Aidan Delgado (*The Sutras of Abu Ghraib*), a military police soldier, describes a company with an almost charmed status: after one shelling, Specialist Lyons shows a CD case in the crook of her arm punctured by a fragment that missed her arm by inches; an older cook, Pollard, tells how a mortar round exploded behind the portable toilet he was in, the shrapnel outlining him without hitting him, and mimes leaping out with his pants around his ankles while everyone laughs. Delgado says the stories remind them how random their fate is, that "with every strike, the mood gets more serious" because the luck cannot last, and that "making light of the constant bombardments" they carry on "pretending not to care." No one in the company was cut by shrapnel. Pinkston: the humor gives psychological distance but hides the truth; they do care, and the laughter cannot last, like the luck.
*Candidate application:* Near-death stories become a performance genre (reenactment with mime), and the humor is explicitly a mask over rising dread.

**[E06-23]. A joke that works, then a joke that fails: the fake IED, the chaplain, and Sucholas.** (AC-14, `[p.29-30]`)
After a lieutenant in his unit died, Bellavia wired a piece of human waste he found in a bathtub and told his captain he had found an IED; the captain laughed. Later the unit chaplain asked Bellavia if he wanted to pray before the mission; Bellavia wrote it left him "cold with fear," alone, and wondering if he were "beyond redemption." Soon after, Specialist Sucholas tried to cheer him with a running gag (Sucholas had made it "a dozen times these past days" and Bellavia "usually laughed") that Sucholas would die for a conspiracy to reelect George W. Bush and that Bellavia would go to Hell for his part. Bellavia, who writes he has done things even God cannot forgive, did not laugh, and Sucholas left puzzled that Bellavia would not even "fake amusement." Pinkston corrects Bellavia's own explanation: the joke failed not because Bellavia was distracted but because he was fixed on reality at that moment.
*Candidate application:* The same person can be the cheerer one hour and the unreachable one the next; and faking amusement is a social courtesy whose refusal tells the joker something is wrong.

**[E06-24]. The death letter: a joke that cannot be sent home.** (AC-14, `[p.30-31]`)
Specialist Colby Buzzell (*My War*) wrote a mock death letter for his parents conceding they were right and he should have gone to college. His other death jokes were comfortable: telling his wife he wanted a tombstone saying he would rather be there than in Iraq; changing his official religion to Rastafarian so incense and Bob Marley would be at a gun salute. But the letter "kinda creeped me out," he thought his parents would not see the humor, and he threw it away. Pinkston: considering his parents removed the distraction and the real possibilities refocused him.
*Candidate application:* Gallows humor breaks at the boundary of the audience that would actually grieve; the same joke is fine in the barracks and unbearable addressed to the family.

**[E06-25]. Buzzell's drift: from judging to joining.** (AC-14, `[p.32-33]`)
Buzzell first judged others: "a couple degenerates that chuckled" at Abu Ghraib images on a gym television; later he overheard a squad joking in the dining facility about the shooting of an Iraqi man, one soldier finding it funny that he turned out to be "just a janitor." Then Buzzell wrote of himself, in a self-aware passage, that he had developed "really disturbing warped, sick war humor about everything"; one week earlier, flipping through another soldier's digital camera, he saw a photo of a dead man killed in a mosque, with eyes and mouth open, and "without even thinking about it" burst out laughing because it looked comical. Pinkston: Buzzell moved from using humor as therapy for death to death, especially an enemy's, as a source of humor.
*Candidate application:* A usable time-course: the person first criticizes the drift in others, then discovers it in themself by an involuntary laugh and tells it candidly. Photographs and cameras are a typical carrier.

**[E06-26]. Aggressive humor at targets who never hear it: fobbits, Rumsfeld targets, a presidential conspiracy.** (AC-14, `[p.33-34]`)
Army First Lieutenant Matt Gallagher (*Kaboom*) writes that combat-experienced soldiers mocked "fobbits," soldiers who never left the forward operating base; one soldier made a sexual remark about overweight female soldiers in the dining facility, out of their hearing. Jonathan Powers, searching an abandoned Iraqi base range in Baghdad in 2003, found targets bearing Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld's face, and thought "the funniest thing in the world" that the Iraqis had trained on them (quoted from Trish Wood's oral history). Bellavia's Sucholas also mocked President Bush. Pinkston reads each as aggression toward a disdained target, comfortable rear-area troops or the leadership responsible for the war, and says none caused harm because the targets did not hear the joke.
*Candidate application:* Disdain is aimed in two directions: down-and-away (the rear-echelon "fobbit") and up (the political leaders), which fits a divide between the exposed and the comfortable.

**[E06-27]. Mice displayed in "torture chambers," and leaders who tolerate it.** (AC-14, `[p.34-35]`)
Marine Lieutenant Colonel Seth Folsom (*Where Youth and Laughter Go*), touring Afghan patrol bases with a Brigadier General, found what a Marine had built in the courtyard of one: "vermin torture chambers," with captured mice shown in states of torment or execution. Folsom ordered the lieutenant in charge to clean it up; his Sergeant Major said you had to admit whoever did that was "pretty talented"; the General only shook his head. Pinkston: three levels of authority registered little shock, tolerance Pinkston explains by what these Marines had seen (next finding). The mice are the one case here of staged cruelty aimed at animals rather than people.
*Candidate application:* Macabre art and cruelty toward a safe target are things leaders may quietly tolerate in units under strain; the leaders' response (cleanup order, an admiring aside, a head shake) is itself a record of the culture.

**[E06-28]. The carnage behind the humor, and jokes at a dying enemy.** (AC-14, `[p.35-37]`)
Folsom's accounts: a vehicle struck a low electrical line, a tire rim sliced the back of an Afghan police soldier's head, and Folsom and his Marines spent fifteen minutes cleaning remains from the site and the perimeter wire to honor the dead and mitigate the accident with Afghan authorities; an explosive-ordnance sergeant, D'Augustine, accidentally triggered an IED in his face and the remains arrived "disassembled" like a department-store mannequin, identified by a tattoo. In a third episode Folsom ordered an air strike on three men placing an IED near the base, leaving two dead and one wounded; as the wounded man twitched, the watching Marines joked he was stretching, tired or doing yoga ("More dark humor ensued," Folsom writes). Visiting the site later, Folsom himself remarked to no one in particular on a dead man's missing brain. Pinkston: Folsom and his Marines used humor to rationalize constant carnage; laughing at the enemy who was trying to kill them was "simply doing their job."
*Candidate application:* The commander is a joker too, not just an observer; and the stock of overwhelming sights explains why the jokes are what they are.

**[E06-29]. Dominick King: "I want to be able to laugh about it."** (AC-14, `[p.37]`)
Marine Dominick King (the passage comes from Trish Wood's oral-history volume, though Pinkston writes that King "wrote") recounts body clearance after the Battle of Fallujah: bodies two weeks old in the street, decomposing fast in the weather, dogs eating remains or carrying body parts. He says he was "pretty desensitized" and that the sight "didn't register as it should have"; "Sometimes we laughed about this stuff." His stated reason: "I want to be able to laugh about it, maybe just out of protection for my conscience." Pinkston: King credits humor with enabling him to function; a professional-role coping device.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's own wording is the best documented statement here of humor as conscience-protection; also note the double edge: numbness is named alongside the laughter.

**[E06-30]. The decline of Delgado's unit: slur, prisoner shootings, and Abu Ghraib.** (AC-14, `[p.38-40]`)
Delgado records soldiers turning "hajji" (an Arabic honorific for a Muslim who has made the pilgrimage) into a pejorative; later members of the unit shot unarmed prisoners, ostensibly for trying to throw rocks, and the young soldiers around smiled and laughed at the news. Pinkston extends this to Abu Ghraib, arguing humor may not be a sufficient explanation but "might be a necessary factor," and citing Specialist Lynndie England's explanation that posing in photos and leading a naked prisoner on a leash "was just for fun" and a way to "joke around," and the laughter of the soldiers present. A prisoner's testimony says a soldier urinated on him and Specialist Charles Graner laughed; another prisoner testified that laughing soldiers abused him in several ways. After the photographs became public, an Iraqi interpreter made his own dark joke (about Americans restoring electricity to Baghdad in a crude sexual way), which Pinkston calls the victims' side's constructive distraction.
*Candidate application:* A documented four-step chain in one unit: slur, then violence toward the helpless, then laughter at the news; and the victims have their own dark humor too.

**[E06-31]. Pinkston's warning against moral equivalence.** (AC-14, `[p.40]`)
Pinkston says that comparing Sand Creek and Abu Ghraib is not a claim of equal suffering or a judgment on the wider wars; they are used only to show that humor was in both cases an enabler (not the only one) of criminal violence against helpless people, and that the potential "reappears in war after war."
*Candidate application:* Use as a guardrail when writing a soldier's atrocity memory: humor is an enabling factor, not an explanation.

### Part III continued: fiction and film

**[E06-32]. Klay's "Recoil" joke: the stock reply to a civilian's question.** (AC-14, `[p.40-42]`)
Marine veteran Phil Klay's *Redeployment* story "After Action Report" includes a joke a Marine character calls common in the Marine Corps: a journalist (cast in the joke as liberal and after the "touchy-feely side of war") asks a Marine sniper what it is like to kill a man and what he feels when he pulls the trigger; the sniper answers with one word, "Recoil." The story: two Marines, Timhead and Ozzie Suba, are haunted by the shooting of an Iraqi boy in Fallujah; Timhead insists Suba take credit, which Suba does, receiving congratulation as a "killer" while Timhead obsesses over the child and the child's family; Timhead insists he is "over" it and Suba agrees it "doesn't matter." Pinkston: Klay includes the joke to show how little this kind of distraction accomplishes and how insufficient humor is against psychological damage; the joke exposes the lie of both men's statements.
*Candidate application:* The soldier's flat technical answer to a civilian's intimate question is a documented deflection; it works as a joke only when told by someone who is hiding the real answer.
*Cross-use:* Useful for a companion fielding a question about their past.

**[E06-33]. Scranton's "pucks": an acronym used as distance for abuse.** (AC-14, `[p.42-43]`)
Army veteran Roy Scranton's novel *War Porn* (the term, as Scranton defines it on his website, covers graphic-violence images and videos brought back from combat zones and viewed voyeuristically, often circulated without context, sometimes evidence of war crimes). A veteran character, Aaron, shows a friend photographs of U.S. soldiers mistreating Iraqi prisoners, jokes about beating them, says a lot of what they did was because they were bored, and calls the prisoners "pucks" from the acronym PUC (Person Under Control). Pinkston: the acronym creates distance, remaking prisoners as objects of mockery and abuse; later Aaron commits a rape and shows no regret, which Scranton (in an interview) frames as being infected by the evil he was part of; Pinkston reads Aaron as a man who laughs at torture because it is inherently funny, distanced "from humanity." Fiction, not evidence of frequency.
*Candidate application:* Institutional acronyms become slang for the people they classify; photograph collections are a carrier of shared humor.

**[E06-34]. *In the Valley of Elah*: "Doc," and "a way to cope."** (AC-14, `[p.43-45]`)
In the 2008 film (with veterans in soldier roles), a soldier, Mike Deerfield, is haunted by driving over an Iraqi child; when he calls his father, a Vietnam veteran who tells him it is "just nerves talking," he is left frustrated. After return, Deerfield is killed by two fellow soldiers (one later dies by suicide and the other confesses). Penning describes a video: Deerfield, pretending to be a medic, put his hand into a wounded detainee's wound asking "Does this hurt?" and the detainee screamed; it "became a theme," and was how Deerfield got the name "Doc." Penning calls it "pretty funny," "just a way to cope," and says they all did stupid things. In interrogation, Penning says that after killing and dismembering Deerfield they stopped at a fast-food place because it was late and they hadn't eaten, and cannot sustain feigned remorse. Pinkston: this scene joins both sides of combat humor, therapy language applied to the infliction of pain; Penning's only sincere emotion appears when describing the torture.
*Candidate application:* Nicknames can originate in a cruel joke and then be worn as affection; "coping" can become the vocabulary that covers aggression, a thing to listen for when a character explains an old story.

**[E06-35]. McCarthy and Owens: the reader's, and soldier's, Faustian bargain.** (AC-14, `[p.45-47]`)
Wade Hall says McCarthy uses violence and humor to say life's epitaph is "demonic laughter." Barcley Owens points to a scene in *Blood Meridian* where a member of a scalp-hunting gang is refused service by a restaurant proprietor, shoots him, and another gang member makes a dismissive remark and tells a companion to find some plates; Owens says McCarthy uses violence "for a casual, dismissive comic moment that understates the horror," so that "by laughing, we participate in the violence," a "secret commerce of laughing and queasy participation." Pinkston extends the "Faustian bargain" to soldiers, whose laughter makes them participants in violence, with intense, long-lasting mental effects.
*Candidate application:* Understatement over atrocity makes the audience complicit; a character's deadpan can carry the same complicity.

**[E06-36]. Smithers: the end of the slide in the narrator's own words.** (AC-14, `[p.47-48]`)
Marine infantryman Jason Smithers (in Wood's oral history; Pinkston calls it a memoir), recalling the Sunni Triangle in early Operation Iraqi Freedom: he and comrades hated being there and hated the people who were "killing our friends"; he says it "kind of turned me racist in a way" and they used slurs for Iraqis. A restrained detainee, he says, was laughing at them, so a friend kicked him in the face "to make him stop laughing"; Smithers: "I thought it was pretty funny" and wishes he had filmed it. He kept "set-up" photographs posed with bodies, pictures of wounded Iraqis and beaten Iraqis, and pictures of buddies "having fun," including some who later died. They filmed firefights as people film dirt-bike stunts, ranked the dead by who looked "cooler" (bloodier, bigger hole), and Smithers likens it to "pictures of flowers." Smithers: "to us this was normal"; "I think I gained something"; civilians see a human being in a dead person's eyes, "We didn't do that." Pinkston: aggressive, depersonalizing humor, the logical result of the Faustian bargain.
*Candidate application:* The documented endpoint has a distinct tell: the person reports no loss, only gain, and treats the civilian reaction as the abnormality. A defector character could embody the opposite: the person who did feel the loss and left.

**[E06-37]. Veterans' outcomes and Pinkston's closing frame.** (AC-14, `[p.48-50]`)
Pinkston stresses that Penning and Aaron are fictional and not a likely real outcome; citing Grossman, veterans of past wars and of Iraq and Afghanistan are less likely to be incarcerated than non-veterans of the same age and sex. Pinkston says veterans rarely commit crimes but kill themselves at about 22 per day and (his wording) "as many as 20" are WoT veterans; the film's Bonner (suicide) reflects the real pattern better than the murderers. Pinkston concludes soldiers must prevent being "swallowed up by the very darkness against which they hope to guard," and that when humor turns from therapy to aggression and horror "the resulting demonic laughter will mean that the joke ultimately is on them." **The suicide statistic is as Pinkston states it and should not be reused without checking (see Gaps).**
*Candidate application:* The closing frame makes humor a thing a person can lose control of, with the risk falling on the user; this is a ready note for a veteran character's private fear about his own jokes.

### From AC-29 (Jensen), selected for the squad, banter and soldier-depiction craft

**[E06-38]. The stock comic squad of *Bad Company*: nerd, gun-nut, short-timer sergeant.** (AC-29, `[p.23-24]`)
Jensen describes the *Battlefield: Bad Company* team of four soldiers, followed across both games from one fixed perspective: the player character Preston Marlowe (a new recruit to "B Company," a unit of troublemakers used as cannon fodder), narrated in cutscenes and loading screens as a laconic action hero in the Bruce Willis *Die Hard* mold; Private Sweetwater (the "nerd" archetype: glasses, runs his mouth constantly, flirts ineptly with their commanding officer); Private Haggard (the southern "gun-nut" who likes NASCAR, monster trucks and explosions and yells a political insult during combat); and Sergeant Redford (a no-nonsense African-American superior three days from retirement). Jensen calls them "clear action movie stereotypes" with almost no arc, and says the games lean toward the comedy side of romance (an "action romp"), where *Modern Warfare* leans toward tragedy and war drama. This is the baseline the thesis later says *Spec Ops* copies and breaks.
*Candidate application:* A comic soldier squad built from fixed types (mouthy, explosion-loving, short-timer) is the genre's default; knowing the default helps a writer decide whether to use it or deliberately depart from it.

**[E06-39]. Audience pressure turned the "lovable rogues" into knights (Jensen's speculation).** (AC-29, `[p.24-25]`)
Jensen argues that *Bad Company 1* presents its heroes as "lovable rogues" (the plot: B Company hunting a mercenary leader paid in gold bars so they can get rich), while *Bad Company 2* makes them noble: Haggard, selfish in the first game, is first to volunteer to rescue a captured ally; Marlowe gives a rousing speech that convinces the squad to risk everything; and the plot becomes a patriotic rescue of America from a Russian with a superweapon, almost the same as *Modern Warfare 1*. Jensen's explanation is that audiences reacted much better to *Modern Warfare 1*'s serious tone than to *Bad Company 1*'s comic tone; Jensen hedges ("could also have something to do with," "seems more likely") and offers no sales or review data.
*Candidate application:* If a comic squad is to carry real stakes, the shift from comic to serious tone is a recognized design problem; Jensen's example shows the usual commercial fix (make the jokers noble) and its cost (the rogue quality is lost).

**[E06-40]. A comic enemy leader: Serdar, a mocked dictator.** (AC-29, `[p.28]`)
*Bad Company 1* has a fictional Caucasus-country president, Zavimir Serdar, designed as a parody of a stereotypical Middle Eastern dictator: a uniform in the style of Saddam Hussein and Muammar Gaddafi, a mansion, a gold-plated helicopter. He is not menacing but "clownish," constantly spouting one-liners and telling odd stories from his time in office. Jensen reads this as fitting the game's action-comedy tone and also as a possible mocking send-up of Saddam Hussein, released two years after Hussein's trial and execution ("adding insult to hanging"), implying how ridiculous and non-threatening such rulers are "when the chips are down." Jensen adds that these games portray Middle Eastern leaders either as comic and incompetent or as evil and menacing, never as competent, which Jensen reads as an American-exceptionalist viewpoint. The reading of the mockery's intent is Jensen's, unsupported by developer statements.
*Candidate application:* Mocking a defeated or hated enemy leader is a classic target of soldier humor; a game or fiction that gives the comic version in place of any competent version is also making a political claim about the enemy.

**[E06-41]. Deadpan or absent reaction to a squadmate's death, and the brisk rebuke to wear.** (AC-29, `[p.33]`, `[p.37-38]`)
In the *Modern Warfare* series, Jensen says, a fellow soldier's death "often comes and goes without remark," and when it does draw comment the reaction is "dead-pan and lacking in emotion": after Soap's unit loses three men in a helicopter crash, the commander's only reaction is "bugger." In *Bad Company 2* the squad shows "a little more humanity": Haggard has a brief moment of grief at the death of their pilot, Flynn, but two levels later seems to have forgotten, which Jensen takes as the genre's model for how characters respond to death. When a soldier in *Modern Warfare 2* does show "actual mental wear" (called a rarity), his sergeant answers briskly that their weapons still work and so they can keep kicking ass. Jensen's argument is that the genre abandons realism on emotional consequences on purpose, to protect player enjoyment, though it advertises realism; Jensen cites a study on combat mental-health risks (Hoge et al.) as what is missing.
*Candidate application:* Documented gallows understatement (clipped, flat, or one-word) can be distinguished from this genre convention by what comes afterward: in Pinkston's accounts the mood darkens, jokes fail and costs show, while in the genre the death is simply forgotten. A good soldier-character voice keeps the understatement and also keeps the bill.
*Cross-use:* Useful where robot soldiers' deaths or losses are handled, and for whether a consciousness-bearing robot squad can forget a loss two levels later.

**[E06-42]. Squad commentary as a tutorial in how to feel about killing.** (AC-29, `[p.17-18]`, `[p.36-37]`)
Jensen's "moral disengagement" framework (from Hartmann and Vorderer and Klimmt et al.): players temporarily suspend moral standards, helped by cues such as a good reason to fight and, ideally, non-human enemies. Jensen's claim about squads is that friendly characters supply such cues. In *Modern Warfare 1* the squad treats the killing of a ship's crew as routine, and the main character never reacts, so the player infers indifference. In *Bad Company 1*'s tutorial, Haggard talks about how fun it is to blow things up while the player blasts a hole in a house, teaching that destruction is fun and, by extension, that enemies are objects. Friendly characters in both series cheer whenever an airstrike lands. In *Modern Warfare*'s "Death From Above" mission, the player acts as an AC-130 gunship gunner, with commentary from the navigator that mixes military jargon ("request to engage") with gleeful "Kaboom!" and "Good kill!" and ends with a remark that "this is gonna be one hell of a highlight reel." Jensen calls it "a very strange mix of military realism and a flippant attitude towards killing people." The scene is secondhand through Jensen; wording unverified.
*Candidate application:* A squad's banter over violence tells the audience how to feel as well as showing how the characters feel; writing it as cheering at a kill is a design choice with moral effect, not neutral color. Pinkston's accounts (laughing at a dying enemy, filming firefights, a "highlight reel" of photographs) suggest the real-world roots of this register, which I note as my own juxtaposition, not Jensen's.

**[E06-43]. Insubordinate banter toward command as a marker of American individuality.** (AC-29, `[p.34-35]`, `[p.40]`)
Jensen reads the *Modern Warfare* soldiers' habit of questioning high command, sometimes disobeying, and at times remarking that Command has "got their head up their ass," as showing individual initiative that contrasts with the "hive-like" Russian enemy and that fits the genre's American-exceptionalist heroism. Separately, Jensen reads a soldier's question whether the Russians they are to meet are "the good Russians, or the bad Russians" as a reminder of the good-versus-evil binary. (Jensen does not discuss tone; the question may read as wry, but Jensen treats it as evidence of the binary.) Caveat: the quoted lines are secondhand and the interpretation is political.
*Candidate application:* Grumbling at command is a stock marker of a likable soldier in games; in a real-world pattern (Pinkston's songs and Rumsfeld targets) it is also resistance humor. A writer can distinguish decorative complaint from the kind that costs something.

**[E06-44]. One authorial gag inside an atrocity level: the "Delayed" board.** (AC-29, `[p.39]`)
In arguing that the "No Russian" airport-massacre level is a shock-for-publicity device with little narrative or gameplay purpose, Jensen notes that the game "does not even take" it "all that serious" because in the middle of the level the player sees a large board of departing flights that all change to "Delayed." That is the thesis's only identified joke inside a game atrocity; it is a gag by the designers, not banter by a character. Jensen offers no analysis of its tone, and one gag is thin evidence for his larger claim.
*Candidate application:* Designers sometimes place a dry joke inside an atrocity scene as a tonal signal; the risk is that it undercuts the scene or reads as indifference.

**[E06-45]. *Spec Ops*: the squad starts as the Bad Company types so it can be broken.** (AC-29, `[p.44]`, `[p.49-50]`)
In *Spec Ops: The Line* the first five minutes establish the three-man squad as a "motor-mouth" (Lugo), a tough guy good with explosives (Adams) and a "stoic no-nonsense blank slate" (Walker), which Jensen calls "almost a point-for-point recreation" of *Bad Company*'s soldier-buddy cast, deliberately, so players assume a standard shooter. Players are meant to expect one of two fates: noble death defending the leader, or bonding through hardship. The game does neither and uses Adams and Lugo as foils: both question Walker's push deeper into the city from the start; Lugo, the voice of reason, objects to using white phosphorus and is the first to die, killed by a mob; Adams's resentment grows and he dies in a suicidal attack rather than follow Walker further. The relationship among the three "starts to disintegrate." Jensen says Lugo and Adams begin as caricatures "as unreal as any found in the Bad Company games" and that their reactions to horror help "humanize them." **Jensen never says that Lugo's talkativeness or any squad joking fades, and cites no joke by any of the three.**
*Candidate application:* The thesis supports a craft pattern (a comic type used as a mask and then made human by how it cracks), but the humor-fading link would be my extension and must be checked against the game. A mouthy character who goes quiet or harsh is a plausible change signal, not a documented one.

**[E06-46]. Psychological wear as strength, and the denial line.** (AC-29, `[p.50]`)
Jensen argues the game does not characterize its soldiers as weak: even as their mental state worsens, Lugo and Adams keep acting as soldiers, taking orders and trying to survive, and their ability to realize and accept that they are losing control is read as an indicator of strength, in contrast to Walker, who cannot. In a quiet moment after Lugo's death, Adams asks what happened to them; Walker answers that nothing happened, they are soldiers. Jensen reads that as the game declaring the unchangeable soldier, "the aforementioned idealized knight," "completely mad," and Walker as "a cautionary tale" of an ordinary man trying to be a demigod hero.
*Candidate application:* A defensible stance for depicting soldiers: change is shown, not as weakness, and the one who can name it is distinguished from the one who denies it. The ex-soldier defector might be written as the one who could name it.

**[E06-47]. Register of orders as a degradation signal.** (AC-29, `[p.52-53]`)
Walker's way of delivering squad commands changes as he descends: from simple military phrasing (an order to take out a hostile on the roof) to screaming that he wants a man dead, with execution animations growing steadily more brutal. Because the player cannot control this, Jensen says, it breaks the player's identification with Walker. Walker's character model also visibly degrades from "ruggedly handsome" to burned and broken, a change that Jensen says would have been "almost impossible" in the first-person view most shooters use, which Jensen offers as a reason the game is third-person (quoting Payne for the claim about identification).
*Candidate application:* Change in a soldier can be signaled through shifts of register (procedural to violent) and of body, without stated feelings; the same logic could track a squad's jokes thinning or turning bitter.

**[E06-48]. Banal small talk as the signal that enemies are people.** (AC-29, `[p.54]`)
Jensen notes that if the player leaves 33rd-battalion sentries undisturbed, the player can overhear them talking about everyday things like asking for a piece of gum or really wanting a cigarette. Jensen says this is deliberate, so the player sympathizes with the individual soldiers on the other side, "portrayed as just as human as Walker and his men." *Spec Ops* otherwise refuses an indisputable "bad guy" side.
*Candidate application:* Trivial chatter is a low-cost, documented way to mark a person as a person, and its absence (enemies who never speak) is the genre's way of removing the person.
*Cross-use:* For robots as soldiers or enemies, whether they small-talk is a direct signal of whether the work treats them as persons. Jensen's own theory notes that non-anthropomorphic enemies make killing easier to accept; Jensen's later remark that a newer game has the player "fighting robots in place of human beings" (`[p.60]`) is consistent. Inner Tepenia's robot-consciousness premise would reverse this convention.

**[E06-49]. The game turns its own jokes on the player: loading screens and "you brought this on yourself."** (AC-29, `[p.56-57]`)
Jensen says other games use fourth-wall awareness "to a humorous end" but *Spec Ops* uses it to implicate the player. Its loading screens, at first helpful tips, turn into aggressive messages directed at the player, including "Do you feel like a hero yet?" and a line saying that if the player were a better person they would not be here; others say killing for entertainment is harmless, or that the U.S. military does not condone killing unarmed combatants, followed by "But this isn't real, so why should you care?" Characters repeat "you brought this on yourself." When Lugo says there is always a choice about using phosphorus, Walker answers there really is not. Jensen reads all of this as laying blame for the horror on the player (quoting Payne that Walker's rationalizing parallels the gamer setting aside disquiet to finish the game). The loading-screen wording is quoted secondhand through Jensen.
*Candidate application:* A dark joke or sardonic tone can be turned on the audience as accusation; the register is the same, but the target moves.

**[E06-50]. Design claims about realism, enjoyment and the cost of showing it.** (AC-29, `[p.19]`, `[p.54]`, `[p.59-60]`)
Jensen's claims about depicting soldiers: (a) games that sell themselves as realistic but let the player kill thousands of human enemies "without the player-character batting an eye" are, Jensen says, "making light of the human cost of war"; (b) real soldiers "would never describe any fighting they do as enjoyable" (stated without citation; see Gaps), and *Spec Ops* is therefore built to be un-fun (repetitive on purpose); (c) a satire built from the target's own tools can be self-undermining (kill-count achievements and a multiplayer mode reward the behavior it condemns), and risks players quitting before the turn. Jensen cites Marcus Schulzke that studies of real soldiers show opponents "generally recognize their shared humanity and have great difficulty overcoming this feeling in order to kill each other."
*Candidate application:* The craft question is whether the work's rewards contradict what it says about soldiers. Jensen's Schulzke point and Pinkston's verbal-distancing catalog (E06-8) are complementary: soldiers find killing hard because of shared humanity, and the euphemisms and enemy-names are devices they use to get past it (my reconciliation, not either author's).

## Pattern roll-up (documented patterns this unit establishes)

**Functions.**
- Narrator-stated coping (named by the narrators themselves): "Laughter is our only defense" (Bellavia, groin wound); dark humor can "save your sanity" (Marr, spouse); humor as the only way to see around fear (Burana, spouse); "protection for my conscience" (King); "pretending not to care" (Delgado); "just a way to cope" (Penning, fictional). Pinkston's own reading adds distance from death, killing, drudgery, fury and loss. (E06-18, -19, -22, -29, -34)
- Humor as a cheer-up tool for a superior after a death (Bellavia's fake IED); the same function turned on the cheerer by a comrade (Sucholas). (E06-23)
- Resistance and disdain: songs wishing older superiors dead; targets bearing a Defense Secretary's face; a joke about a presidential conspiracy; mockery of rear-echelon "fobbits." (E06-9, -26)
- Community-wide reflex after horror within hours or days. (E06-3)
- Merchandise and spoken warnings as transmission: a T-shirt sold outside a base gate; a veteran's warning to a journalist. (E06-16)

**Targets.**
- Death, the wounded body's most feared wound, mortar fragments, the sniper, the dying enemy, the dead enemy's face, one's own near-miss. (E06-18, -21, -22, -25, -28)
- Superiors and leaders (songs, targets); rear-echelon troops; detainees; civilians the unit disdains; a defeated dictator in game comedy. (E06-9, -26, -30, -40)
- The self (a self-written death letter; Prakash's laughing at being sniped). (E06-21, -24)

**Forms and mechanics.**
- Parody song of an institutional or familiar tune (WWI); riddles after a crime; acronym riddle after a disaster. (E06-3, -6, -9)
- Last-transmission humor over radio or telegraph. (E06-7)
- One-word flat affirmation applied to everything ("Awesome!"). (E06-20)
- One-word technical deflection to a civilian's question ("Recoil"). (E06-32)
- Reenactment mime of a near-death event as a comic performance. (E06-22)
- Prop jokes (fake IED; a mock death letter; religion changed on paperwork). (E06-23, -24)
- Euphemism and renaming (Grossman's list; York's "touched off"; "pucks" from PUC); a nickname born of a cruel joke ("Doc"). (E06-8, -33, -34)
- Photographs and filmed firefights as humor carriers ("war porn"). (E06-25, -33, -36)
- Incongruity plus truth, and spectator distance, as the mechanism of why it is funny. (E06-4)

**Rules of license.**
- The "Ow" distance rule: closer to the victim, less laughter. (E06-4)
- Audience matters: a joke that is fine in the unit fails when addressed to the family. (E06-24)
- Courtesy to the joker: a comrade is expected to at least fake amusement. (E06-23)
- Leaders joke too, and tolerate macabre displays. (E06-27, -28)
- Enlisted jokes behind the officer's back; target who cannot decode. (E06-12)

**Limits and failure modes.**
- Humor fails when the person is fixed on guilt or mortality (Bellavia after the chaplain), or when the audience is the family (Buzzell). (E06-23, -24)
- Humor covers dread and rising seriousness and cannot outlast the luck. (E06-22)
- Slide from therapy to aggression to violence-as-joke; laughter at helpless victims at Sand Creek, a village, Babi Yar, and Abu Ghraib; the laughing detainee provoking violence. (E06-5, -10, -13, -30, -36)
- Numbness named beside the laughter (King); the report of gain, not loss (Smithers). (E06-29, -36)
- Narrator-reported absence of regret as the endpoint (Smithers; fictional Aaron and Penning). (E06-33, -34, -36)

**Time course.**
- Within a deployment: cringe, then acclimation (Barker); judging others, then involuntary laughter at a dead man's photo (Buzzell); a unit's slur, then prisoner shootings, then laughter at the news (Delgado). (E06-17, -25, -30)
- After service: nothing in this unit beyond fiction and Pinkston's statistics (see Gaps).

**Civilian-facing register.**
- A warning to the outsider journalist; the journalist's own cringe; the sniper's one-word reply to a squeamish civilian; the father's "just nerves." (E06-16, -17, -32, -34)

**Defector, exile and dissent.**
- Not documented. The one dissenter named, a conscientious objector (Delgado), is used only for unit humor; Soule at Sand Creek is the lone historical figure who refused and did not laugh. (E06-13, lead list)

**Cross-cultural.**
- Two interpreters' jokes (Afghan curfew; Iraqi after Abu Ghraib); WWI British songs; the Viet Cong interpreter's mirror-image naming; a Nazi-era example. All filtered through American or British authors. (E06-8, -15, -30)

**Craft observations (Jensen and Pinkston).**
- Stock comic squad archetypes as the genre baseline; subversion works because the baseline is familiar. (E06-38, -45)
- Deadpan and absence of reaction to death as a genre convention that reads as unreality when realism is advertised; documented gallows understatement comes with a bill. (E06-41)
- Squad cheering and commentary is a moral cue to the audience. (E06-42)
- Change shown through reaction, register of orders and body; psychological wear is not weakness; denial is the dangerous trait. (E06-46, -47)
- Banal small talk humanizes; faceless, silent enemies dehumanize. (E06-48)
- Fiction by veterans uses a single stock joke to expose what the joke covers. (E06-32)

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Pinkston's evidence is selected illustration, not a sample.** No selection criteria, no counts, and each example is read to fit the three-part scheme (therapy, aggression, violence-as-joke); the same material is sometimes assigned to more than one. Distinguish where narrators state a function themselves (Bellavia, Marr, Burana, King, Delgado, Buzzell, Prakash, Smithers, England) from where Pinkston supplies it (Folsom's Marines, the WWI songs, Devereux and Dollins, Fort Bowie, Hartley). My candidate applications mostly rely on the former.
2. **"Memoir" is sometimes loose.** Pinkston says King, Smithers and Powers "wrote" in memoirs, but Pinkston's footnotes cite Trish Wood's oral-history volume (`[p.33]`, `[p.37]`, `[p.47]`); these are interview excerpts, edited by someone else. Several "memoirs" were co-written (Bellavia with Bruning; Bogdanos with Patrick; Tomlin with Cage); Burden's book is a compilation of contributors' dispatches.
3. **Only one army, one era, one occupational slice in the WoT sources.** All are American, mostly Army and Marine ground combat in Iraq and Afghanistan (the dated accounts mostly from 2003 onward; the thesis does not date every account), plus a few officers; no air, naval, support-unit or non-American perspective; women appear only as journalists, spouses and a detainee abuser. Do not generalize to other armies.
4. **No post-service or defector material.** Pinkston acknowledges his key endpoint memoirist gives no return-to-civilian account; the sole "after" data are fiction (Penning, Aaron, Bonner) and two statistics. The conscientious-objector memoirist Delgado is the strongest defector-adjacent lead in the list and needs to be read directly.
5. **The suicide statistic is unreliable as stated.** Pinkston writes that veterans kill themselves at about 22 per day and "as many as 20" of those are WoT veterans, citing a film and a defense-news article. My understanding, not verified here, is that the often-quoted 22-per-day figure concerned veterans of all eras and has been questioned; verify before any use.
6. **The Wake Island message is an anecdote cited to one secondary source.** Pinkston takes Devereux's final transmission from Grossman; I have not checked whether its wording and attribution are established, so treat as a leads-only item.
7. **The claim that unheard aggression does no harm is Pinkston's assertion.** It overlooks the effect of such talk on bystanders within the unit (the sexual remark in the dining facility had female soldiers as its target). The E01 sources on banter and gender (Tidy; Sløk-Andersen) bear on this.
8. **Apparent errors in AC-14.** A Suba/"Suma" slip (`[p.44]`), "Fomson" for Folsom in footnote 93 (`[p.37]`), "The Long Train" versus "The Long Trail," "Einzatsgruppen"; two quotations include editorial ellipses. Check the memoirs themselves before quoting.
9. **Offensive terms.** The thesis quotes slurs and graphic passages in full; I paraphrased and did not reproduce them.
10. **AC-29 is largely not about humor.** The brief's interest in humor-as-change-signal is only partly answerable: Jensen documents register shifts in orders, deadpan death, cheering NPCs and comic archetypes, but **not** the loss of banter in *Spec Ops*. Any account of how Lugo's talkativeness or the squad's tone changes requires playing the game or reading a transcript or interview with its writers.
11. **AC-29's game lines are secondhand and unverified.** The dialogue Jensen quotes (the "bugger" line, the airstrike commentary, loading-screen texts, "Command's got their head up their ass") comes through the thesis, with at least one paraphrase presented inside quotation marks; do not quote from this extraction.
12. **AC-29's date and provenance are unreliable.** The "31 May 2014" title-block date precedes the 2015 games the text discusses; the manifest citation gives no year.
13. **Tension on real soldiers' enjoyment.** Jensen asserts (uncited) that soldiers would never call fighting enjoyable; Pinkston's Smithers reports fun, filming firefights the way people film dirt-bike stunts, and Prakash calls being sniped "funny as hell." These may not be strictly contradictory (survival absurdity versus enjoyment of killing) but Jensen's claim should not be used as evidence about real soldiers.
14. **Needs the originals.** The best next reads from AC-14's list are Bellavia, Burden, Buzzell, Delgado, Folsom, Hartley, Gallagher, Wood's oral history, and the Abu Ghraib testimony volumes; for AC-29, the Payne and Schulzke articles named in its Works Cited (Payne, "War Bytes" and "Marketing Military Realism"; Hartmann and Vorderer 2010; Schulzke 2013) are the craft-relevant leads.

Read: AC-14, AC-29; complete: yes (every line of both text copies read with the Read tool; skipped for extraction only the Works Cited of each and, in AC-29, the table of contents and the passages on narrative theory, American exceptionalism, enemy-representation politics and plot, which were read and found not to bear on humor or squad depiction beyond what is extracted above).
