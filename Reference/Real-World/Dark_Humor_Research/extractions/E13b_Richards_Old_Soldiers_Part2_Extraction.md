## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-07 is.** The 1933 memoir of a regular infantry private of the 2nd Battalion, Royal Welch Fusiliers, who in the later chapters serves as a battalion signaller (and, in attacks, runner). The text file is a proofread eBook (the transcriber notes at L7439-7452 say misspellings and printer errors were corrected; the companion pass E13a, which read the header, identifies it as the fadedpage/Distributed Proofreaders Canada eBook), not raw OCR; the project manifest's "uncertain" U.S. copyright status is respected, so quotations are short and findings are own-words. **My range is L3500 to the end (L7454; I read from L3440 for context):** the later part of Chapter XII (the Corps Commander's order, the rat, the café, the "quick-firers"), XIII (the Red Dragon mine and the July 1916 raid), XIV-XVI (the Somme, High Wood, Les Boeufs, Trones Wood), XVII (winter 1916-17 and the German retirement), the second XVII and XIX (Arras and the Hindenburg Line, Whitsun 1917), XX (the Belgian coast, leave, Polygon Wood), XXI (five weeks at the Rouen base), XXII (Passchendaele winter, Bois Grenier 1918), the next "XXII" (the March 1918 offensive), XXIV (Minimum Reserve), XXV (the final advance), XXVI (Le Cateau, early November), XXVII (the Armistice) and XXVIII ("Rewards for War Service"), which closes with the postwar account and the last pub scene. The text has **no page markers**; I cite chapter and line numbers ("L"). The printed chapter numbers repeat in the file (see Gaps).

**Evidence base.** One narrator's retrospective account, composed about 1932 (he writes "this year of 1932," L7347, and "Armistice day to-day," L7396) from memory (his own statement, L7411-7412). One battalion, one army, one rank-level (private, signaller, runner, with an offered and refused promotion), and the range holds the memoir's heavy-war years: July 1916 to the Armistice, plus the postwar pension account. The voice is a prewar regular's: it sorts people into old hands, new men, Base soldiers and civilians. Because it is a published, shaped memoir of 1933, it prints things that wartime letters could not.

**What it does NOT contain (within my range).** No theory of humor, and almost no direct statement of what jokes are for: the nearest are "It made us merry and helped us to forget" (E13b-30), "just to give the Staff officer a shock" (E13b-11) and "a kick against the motor lorry drivers of the A.S.C." (E13b-42). No songs except two hymn-tune items (the A.S.C. parody, E13b-42, and the closing title song, E13b-155); no verse; no letters beyond one telegram text (E13b-106). Shell shock is named once ("horribly shellshocked," E13b-22), plus "going queer" and "a nervous breakdown" (E13b-22, E13b-46). Executions: none carried out in the battalion in the text (a commuted death sentence, E13b-39; wishes that others be shot, E13b-45, E13b-91). No account of the narrator's own fear beyond a few admissions (E13b-93, E13b-122, E13b-123). The Old Soldier, central in Part 1, is absent after his transfer (recalled only in E13b-12, E13b-49, E13b-115). Almost no German or French voice: a German doctor and a prisoner sketcher (E13b-142), a few French civilians.

**Format and damage.** Described in the Gaps section (repeated printed chapter numbers; garbled chapter-opening letters; stray "J," "Sth," "Tm"; the unexplained slang "pound"). The first ten lines of my range overlap the companion pass.

**Quotation conventions.** Quotations are exact except for (a) American spelling in place of British (the project rule has no exception for quotations; for example "honor," "favorite," "labeled," "jeweler's"), (b) my square-bracketed insertions (including "[I]" where the text prints "J"), and (c) **graphic, sexual, slur, atrocity and bodily-function passages, which are summarized and not quoted** (see the register). Glosses of slang that the text does not give are marked "my gloss." Period national and ethnic caricature is documented, not endorsed.

**Retrospective vs. period text.** All retrospective (1932-33). Where the narrator says "I expect" or "I was told" he is reconstructing; where a comrade speaks, the words are the narrator's rendering. Several passages carry flash-forwards (what became of a man after the war), which are from the time of writing, not the time of the event.

## Findings

### A. Chapter XII (from L3500): the Corps Commander, the rat, the café, the "quick-firers"

**[E13b-1]. The Corps Commander's order against a "pessimistic feeling" and swearing: the order is answered by swearing, and the narrator sides with the pessimists.** (PW-07, Ch. XII "The Corps Commander and Quick Firers," L3533-3576)
Battalion Orders passed through the narrator, who as signaller's runner read them with a comrade before handing them to the Company Commander. The order, from the Lieutenant-General commanding the corps, complained of a "pessimistic feeling" (talk that the enemy could never be shifted, that the war had become a stalemate), told officers to eradicate it, and added that swearing, worse among officers than men, "must also cease." The narrator reports that the Company Commander's language for the rest of the day "was delightful to listen to," and that it would have done the general good to hear it. The narrator then says the general was right that the feeling prevailed (the dead of the Loos fighting still lay in front of the trenches and the old hands wanted a chance to "get a bit of our own back"), and that it was easy to be optimistic from a chateau "many many miles behind the front line." A second grievance from weeks earlier: the same general had ordered brasses polished when out of action (the battalion's standing order had been to keep them dull); the brasses then caught the sun on the march up, so that German observation balloons "must have thought that hundreds of small heliographs were moving into action." The narrator writes: "Many prayers were offered up for his soul." A man named Duffy "swore" the general was a director of a metal-polish firm, and another man said the general "would sooner lose his trenches than his button-sticks." Mechanisms: reversal (an order against swearing that is answered by the superior's own swearing); a mock-pious phrase for a curse; a conspiracy joke explaining a senseless order by the commander's supposed commercial interest; imagined enemy view of the army's folly. Target: the corps commander and the whole chain from Corps to Company, described by the narrator as a chain of orders that ends with "company commanders and platoon officers who with the men had to do the real dirty work" (L3560-3561, quoted in part). The narrator does not joke about the dead lying out in front (the text describes rats feeding on them; I do not reproduce the wording).
*Candidate application:* A soldier-character's contempt for senior command can take the form of reading an order aloud for pleasure and sharing the officer's swearing response; the target is the distance between the order-writer and the mud, not the idea of obedience itself.

**[E13b-2]. The Public Schools battalion: the regulars' verdict on volunteer civilians.** (PW-07, Ch. XII, L3500-3505)
The brigade is reshaped and the 20th Royal Fusiliers (a Public Schools battalion) joins; the narrator calls them "very decent chaps but hopeless as soldiers," whose one proficiency was swearing, and notes that their mail was "always twice as large" as the rest of the brigade's together. The narrator adds that "we old hands" were sorry the brigade had been broken up. Mechanism: affectionate belittling with a class marker (mail volume as evidence of a wider, wealthier home circle); the swearing line turns the hated drill-ground virtue into the only thing the newcomers had mastered.
*Candidate application:* A regular's treatment of a conscript or volunteer unit as "very decent chaps but hopeless as soldiers" is a boundary joke that grants humane credit while keeping the old hands' competence exclusive.

**[E13b-3]. Dann and the rat: laughter used, then regretted, then withheld; humor refused as protection.** (PW-07, Ch. XII, L3579-3607; payoff in Ch. XIV, L4143-4176, see E13b-23)
Dann and the narrator throw clods at a very large black rat that stares at Dann and does not flinch. The narrator "burst out laughing"; Dann, pale, says he knows his number is up and that the rat will be near "when I do go West" wherever the war takes them. The narrator tells him not to "talk so wet." The narrator reports that Dann "was a very brave and cheery fellow, but from that day he was a changed man," continued to work and never shirked, and lost the cheerfulness. Old soldiers asked the narrator what was wrong, "But I never told them; they might have chaffed him about it." The two never referred to the rat again, and Dann never went rat-shooting again. This is the book's plainest statement of the unwritten norm that superstition is a target for chaff, and that the narrator will shield a comrade from it. It also shows the narrator's own first reaction (laughter) and the narrator's later regret in the silence that replaces it.
*Candidate application:* A soldier who knows the group's default response to a comrade's omen is mockery, and who decides to suppress it for one man, is a model for loyalty that is expressed by withholding a joke. *Cross-use:* Robot characters with a premonition glitch could be given the same protective silence.

**[E13b-4]. The officer who shouted at the Germans: suspicion, a sergeant detailed to watch, and an ambiguous shot.** (PW-07, Ch. XII, L3610-3633)
A platoon officer, thick of speech and always looking "three sheets in the wind," goes on patrol and curses the Germans in their own language; a sergeant tells the narrator he had to put a hand over the officer's mouth. A sergeant crony tells the narrator "in confidence" that he has been ordered to shoot the officer on sight if he acts suspiciously (the old hands suspected he was passing information by shouting). On a night patrol the officer is hit in the buttocks from along the line, shouts louder, and is carried off; an old hand remarks that "such a beautiful wound as that" would have made him run all the way to the dressing-station. The paragraph ends with the narrator's deadpan alternative: "Of course it may have been the sergeant himself who fired that shot from just behind." Mechanism: the rank-etiquette inversion (an NCO as watcher over an officer), the insinuation delivered as a throwaway last sentence, and the standard envy-joke about a "beautiful" (Blighty-bound) wound. Target: an officer who is unreliable and apparently drunk. What the narrator does not do is treat the possible shooting of an officer by an NCO as a moral problem.
*Candidate application:* Another candidate type: the rumor that an unreliable officer is a spy, held in the ranks with the same weight as the senior command's own caution, ends in a dry last-line insinuation.

**[E13b-5]. Gas at breakfast: a comic list, then the cost in one line.** (PW-07, Ch. XII, L3636-3640)
After a morning gas attack the narrator, who had just cooked breakfast, reports that "buttons and badges and the bacon and tea and everything else turned as green as grass"; there was no breakfast "and some of the men were vomiting very badly." Mechanism: a cooking inventory as a measure of the gas, with the human cost (sickness) added without elaboration.
*Candidate application:* Gas as a domestic nuisance (ruined breakfast, discolored brass) is a way to talk about the weapon through its effect on routine.

**[E13b-6]. The Distinguished Conduct Medal celebration that ends tied to a wagon wheel.** (PW-07, Ch. XII, L3640-3670)
The narrator and Paddy, newly decorated, celebrate after hours in a café that was "well known" to them with wine ("ving blong," my gloss: soldiers' rendering of a French wine name), a gramophone and two young women. Two military policemen walk in at 9:30; the two soldiers ask them to overlook it, thinking of the café's owner (the young women and their mother) who would be in trouble; the policemen say they cannot, the Assistant Provost-Marshal being up the road, and take names from pay-books (the narrator notes that without pay-books the men could have given false names). A divisional general's postscript says commanding officers have been too lenient with this kind of crime. The Company Commander, Captain Higginson (an Artists' Rifles officer), reading out the charge "Found drinking after hours in a café," "tried to be serious but failed, and burst out laughing," but says he has no option. The two men then have "a lovely yarn to tell the Colonel, which any judge in the land would have accepted as the truth"; the sentence is eight days of Number One Field Punishment, "twenty days less than we expected"; and the narrator writes that no two men could have celebrated the medal better "tied to those G. S. wagon wheels." The Provost-Sergeant's discretion over whether ankles were bound is noted: "We were lucky." Mechanisms: the decoration-then-punishment irony; the narrator's claim that the officer's laughter at the charge is what sympathetic officers do; the shared understanding that the colonel's hearing is a performance to which a good "yarn" is the expected response; field punishment narrated with relish and a hint of boast.
*Candidate application:* The old-soldier ethic of the "lovely yarn" at a disciplinary hearing, and a company officer who is complicit in his private amusement but bound by procedure, is a recurring type.

**[E13b-7]. "Quick-firers": the field postcard as euphemism, and the prank that cost a comrade his home for a week.** (PW-07, Ch. XII, L3673-3758)
Letters were censored by platoon officers (no place names, casualties or conditions), and "green envelopes" were introduced for private correspondence, opened only by a Base Censor. When time was short men sent printed field-service postcards, which they called "quick-firers": the printed phrases were crossed out until "I am all right" remained. The narrator and two others, "for a lark," sent a man called Tim six cards signed with French women's names with kisses, one signed "Mdlle. Yvonne" with three times the kisses, timed to reach his home after he arrived on leave. Tim's wife, reading the cards, believed he had been unfaithful, accused him of "pretending you have been fighting with the Germans and up to your waist in blood and mud," and left with the children to her mother's; the mother-in-law abused him in the street; his leave was ruined. The narrator writes that "we all liked Tim very much" and that had they known they would not have sent the cards. The narrator's letter of explanation, signed by three men with addresses, was not believed; the wife wrote to Tim that it was written to screen him. The narrator closes the story: Tim was lightly wounded two months later and sent home, and "I have heard since the War that he and his wife are living happily together." Mechanisms: practical joke aimed at a comrade's home, a joke whose delivery depended on civilian readers; Tim himself laughed at the signature while hung over, which the wife read as guilt. What the narrator says about it: the prank was "for a lark," its cost was disproportionate, and affection for the victim is stated as the reason for regret. It is the narrator's rare account of a joke that harmed.
*Candidate application:* A prank whose reach extends to a comrade's family is a documented way a unit's humor can misfire when it crosses into the civilian world.

### B. Chapter XIII (from L3761): the Red Dragon mine and the July raid

**[E13b-8]. Leg-pulling about a dug-out, and then the mine.** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, "The Red Dragon Mine and a Raid," L3765-3838)
Before the mine, the narrator at 11 p.m. visits B Company's three signallers, who are "doing a good old soldiers' grouse" that they are the only signallers on the Western Front without a dug-out; the narrator "pulled their legs" by describing the comfort of his own and when he left they "cursed me until they were blue in the nose." The narrator adds: "Old hands were continually pulling one another's legs: our dug-out was really a poke of a hole." The night is too quiet; old hands do not like it. At 1:30 a.m. a mine blows under B Company and eight men survive from the whole company. The narrator's after-thought: if B's signallers had had a dug-out he would have stayed "swopping yarns and brewing tea" and "would have gone West with them." Mechanism: the leg-pull is itself the old hands' normal social act, and its sequel (what the leg-pull spared the narrator) is left unmarked by any comment other than the conditional sentence. Also the narrator's remark that old hands distrust quiet is a piece of folk risk-reading that runs in both directions in this chapter (a battalion elsewhere reports a "very quiet time"; the same quiet before the mine).
*Candidate application:* The exact social act that saves a man (not being with his friends) is described as banter's side effect, with no gloss on survivor feelings.

**[E13b-9]. Sergeant Bale, and where the narrator's tone stops.** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3808-3813)
The narrator comes upon the body of Sergeant Bale, one of two sergeants decorated with the D.C.M. in the same ceremony as the narrator and Paddy. The text states the manner of the death in graphic terms (a shell fragment, and the sight left behind), which I summarize and do not reproduce. The narrator follows at once with a plain tribute: Bale was "an old Expeditionary Force man and one of the best N.C.O.s in the Battalion." No joke, nickname or idiom appears in this paragraph or in the paragraph that follows on the survivors.
*Candidate application:* The death of a named, decorated senior NCO is the content around which the narrator's humor is conspicuously absent; the tribute is formal and rank-based.

**[E13b-10]. "Hammer" Lane's retort and the Military Cross "he really deserved something different."** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3815-3841)
The survivors of B are led by "Hammer" Lane, "an old soldier." When a platoon officer of C Company, seeing Germans behind the position, shouts that they will have to surrender, Lane's answer is "Surrender my bloody arse!" and an instruction to meet the enemy front and rear. A counter-attack ends the fight. Decorations follow: Captain Stanway gets the D.S.O. ("thoroughly earned"), Lane gets the D.C.M., and the officer who shouted that they would have to surrender is awarded the Military Cross, of whom the narrator writes: "He really deserved something different." Mechanism: deadpan understatement about an unjust award; the contrast between two reactions in one trench. Target: the officer, and the decoration system that rewards rank. The narrator's phrase leaves the "something different" unnamed.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-narrator's most pointed condemnation of an officer's failure can be one sentence of mock-modesty about a medal.

**[E13b-11]. The dead German officer propped for the Staff Colonel.** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3844-3862)
The next morning is peaceful, "the larks were up singing beautifully," and the narrator says it was generally like this "after a show." When a Staff Colonel visits to see the crater, a few old soldiers had arranged the body of the dead German officer on the fire-step with a lighted candle and a small Bible and, as the Colonel approached, a lighted cigarette in the mouth (restrained summary of a macabre prop arrangement). The Staff officer "didn't stay long." The narrator says this was "done out of no disrespect to the dead German officer, but just to give the Staff officer a shock," and adds that "We all hated the sight of Staff officers," whose chief skill seemed to be checking men for improper saluting out of action. A prisoner then told the men that the officer had been the bravest and most popular officer in his battalion, and "the same chaps that had propped him up" gave him "a decent burial" behind the trench. Mechanisms: a corpse as the instrument of a prank at the expense of a rank; an explicit disclaimer of disrespect toward the enemy dead, with proof (the burial); the target being the Staff, not the German. The narrator says what the humor is for: to give the Staff officer a shock, and a reminder of the front line he does not come to when shelled.
*Candidate application:* A model of licensed corpse-humor: the dead enemy is the prop, the live brass is the target, and the earned right is demonstrated by a proper burial afterward.

**[E13b-12]. Fishing by grenade, and the Old Soldier's "That's what they call civilization."** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3872-3887)
Resting in a canal village, the narrator and Paddy fish for three hours with cheap rods and catch nothing while an elderly Frenchman beside them catches half a dozen fish. They move down the canal, pull the pins on two Mills bombs they had brought "in case we met with no success," drop them in, and soon "had more fish than what we could carry back." The method was prohibited, with twenty-eight days of Number One Field Punishment as the "least punishment" for a man caught. The narrator then recalls that the Old Soldier (a prized comrade, since transferred to the sappers) always fished this way and used to say that a man who killed a dozen Germans with bombs is complimented and may be decorated, but that a man who stunned one fish with a bomb gets twenty-eight days: "That's what they call civilization." Mechanism: the army's moral accounting reversed; the sage persona attached to a named nickname ("the Old Soldier"), with a standing catch-phrase; the joke made by a pet comrade is retold at the narrator's own act of rule-breaking. Target: the military code, not the enemy.
*Candidate application:* The wise old hand whose one-liner is the company's commentary on regulations is a stock figure; the line works because it states the contradiction without raising the voice.

**[E13b-13]. Raid planning: betting against the signal system, and "amusement" at an order about identity discs.** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3890-3960)
The narrator reports that raids are "always an element of luck": an earlier raid at Cuinchy, with wire left uncut, was "a washout," with many casualties, including a Liverpool sergeant, Joe Williams, "with whom I was very pally." No joke is attached to the losses. For the July raid, signallers prepare looped and laddered spare lines; "one old signaller wanted to bet that no station would be in touch with any other fifteen minutes after the raid commenced," backing "a cert" because old hands knew a bombardment would sweep the lines away. An order that the raiders leave pay-books and badges behind, so that a dead man could not be identified by regiment, while all keep identity discs stamped with number, rank, name, battalion and regiment, "created a lot of amusement." The stretcher-bearer Duffy said that the "Big Pot" who issued it "should have been immediately rose to the rank of a Field-Marshal" (my gloss: "Big Pot" for a senior officer; the text does not define it). Mechanism: sarcastic promotion of the author of a pointless order; gambling idiom used as professional pessimism about staff arrangements. Target: senior staff and planners.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's dry wager that an elaborate plan will fail within a stated number of minutes is a piece of professional humor.

**[E13b-14]. The two sanitary men: scroungers, tall tales and a hyperbole about a grandfather.** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3934-3942, L4059-4064, L4078-4083)
"Fizzer" Green and Seth Elliott, the company's sanitary men, are described as "two old soldiers and two of the greatest scroungers on the Western Front." The narrator relays a company saying that they would have dug up their grandfather's body and cut it open if they thought he had swallowed a penny before he died (a hyperbole about greed that involves a corpse; I summarize rather than quote), and an anecdote that Elliott once found gold coin in the clothing of a dismembered German casualty (restrained: the severed limb is not described in the original's terms here). For the raid they fix knives to broomsticks with sticking plaster borrowed from the Aid Post. Afterward they "swore they had killed a dozen each," but the narrator observes that the display of watches on the fire-step suggested they had little time to kill, being so busy looting: "enough of watches to have opened a jeweler's shop." The narrator then adds: "But they were very brave men for all that." Fizzer was a steeplejack in civil life. Mechanism: mock tall-tale scoring (claimed kills against visible loot); a praise-of-the-comic-thief; folk hyperbole. What the humor does: marks the gap between boast and proof while affirming courage.
*Candidate application:* The company scrounger as a comic figure whose theft the narrator can mock but whose courage he vouches for.

**[E13b-15]. Looting as universal, and the stated purpose: "we might be dead cocks."** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L4064-4075)
The narrator states that "Looting was universal amongst us old hands," that prisoners were made to hand over valuables, that those who did not get them were beaten to it by men in the Back Areas, and that loot was sold to non-combatants (the Army Service Corps, men at Divisional Headquarters). The money financed a good time out of action, because "we knew we might be dead cocks the next time we were in the line." The casualties of the night are given in a single sentence (about sixty). No jokes about the dead are added. Mechanism: justification by mortality; an economy in which the front-line soldier sells to the rear. Target (implied): the rear-area soldier as buyer.
*Candidate application:* A character's drinking and spending as a response to "dead cock" arithmetic, narrated without apology.

**[E13b-16]. The raid night: the joke about "safer out in no-man's-land" and the prisoner who pulled the narrator from the mud.** (PW-07, Ch. XIII, L3961-4053)
During the counter-bombardment of their own trench the two signallers "decided we would be safer out in no-man's-land," and go out running wire; both are mired to the waist, their rifles' bolts clogged with mud, their arms' white identification bands unreadable under mud. At the enemy trench they meet a German who throws up his hands, jabbers, and sobs "like a child." One of the two signallers proposes killing him as the only way out of the difficulty; the narrator prevents it (restrained summary; the exchange is quoted in the original in blunt terms that I do not reproduce). They bring the prisoner back; the narrator sticks in a flooded shell hole and, when the prisoner has leapt it and stands, the narrator, holding a useless rifle, menaces him; the prisoner stays, and a signaller "made the prisoner, who was a magnificent made man, pull me out." Back in their own trench, three men of the raiding party who had stepped aside for them are killed by a shell (restrained: the text describes the bodies; "It was tough luck for them after surviving the raid"). The narrator closes the raid by saying that it had been "a great success" with over forty prisoners, "the only thing that had been a failure were the signalling arrangements," and the narrator's own work was the signaling. Mechanisms: the understated risk-calculation; the comic reversal in which the prisoner rescues the captor; the dry pivot on the narrator's own department as the failure. Where humor stops: the three dead are described in a single sentence with a plain idiom and no joke.
*Candidate application:* A character's matter-of-fact conversion of "safe" into "outside the trench," and the rescue of a captor by a captive, are small authentic touches of how risk and role are reversed.

### B2. Chapter XIV (from L4086): the Somme, July 1916

**[E13b-17]. Blighty wounds for the youngsters, "through the pound" for the old hands: the preferred death and the feared one.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, "The Somme: Capture of High Wood," L4100-4108)
At the start of the Somme chapter the narrator records that "all old hands" were being killed while youngsters with a few months' service were "receiving lovely blighty wounds" (my gloss: a wound bad enough to be sent home, "Blighty" being England). The old hands "often used to remark" that when they got hit it would be "a bullet through the pound" (the text does not define "pound"; the companion pass E13a, reading its own contexts, takes it as slang for the head, which fits an instant death; my gloss, unverified) "or stop a five-point-nine all on our own." The narrator adds that they thought the men who died instantly were lucky ("they didn't know anything about it"), and that this was "the death we all wished for if death must come our way," the alternative being blindness or disfigurement, which is stated plainly as the thing dreaded. Mechanism: a formulaic fatalist remark that the old hands exchange; its logic (the young get lucky, we get killed) shows up again at Dann's and Duffy's deaths (E13b-19, E13b-24). What the narrator does not joke about: blindness and disfigurement.
*Candidate application:* The old regular's gallows formula is a statistical complaint about luck, phrased as an envy joke at the expense of youngsters. The limits of the formula are marked: instant death is blessed, disfigurement is not funny.

**[E13b-18]. Rum, and the two sergeant-majors who did not take it.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4090-4097)
The narrator introduces the new colonel, Crawshay, as "the best we ever had," a stickler for discipline who was always in the front trenches and had "a cheery word for everyone." Captain Stanway has left to command a new service battalion, and the narrator says "He and Fox had been the only two sergeant-majors of the Company that never pinched our rum" (Stanway evidently having been a sergeant-major of the company earlier; the text does not say when). The aside is delivered inside an otherwise earnest tribute. Mechanism: backhanded praise; the rum ration as the commodity over which NCO authority is judged. Target: the sergeant-majors as a class.
*Candidate application:* The measure of an NCO or officer, in a unit's private ledger, can be what he did with the men's rum.

**[E13b-19]. Dann, the spent bullet, and the first banter of the Somme.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4122-4128)
In the first hours at Mametz Wood a young signaller, Thomas, is hit in the knee by a spent bullet, "our first casualty on the Somme." Dann says he does not suppose it will be his luck to get a spent bullet; it will be one at short range "through the pound or a twelve-inch shell all on my own." The narrator replies, "as usual," that Dann would be "damned lucky" to stop either and could not "grouse much afterwards." Dann agrees: "You're right enough about that, Dick." The exchange is the ritual form of the fatalist banter described in E13b-17; the narrator calls it "as usual," signaling that it is routine between the two.
*Candidate application:* A two-man ritual in which one man names the worst death and the other jokes that he would not be around to complain is a bonding form between old comrades.

**[E13b-20]. "Hammer" Lane and the officer's foot.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4129-4140)
The first officer casualty of the Somme for the battalion is the same lieutenant awarded the Military Cross (E13b-10). A small ricocheting fragment from "Hammer" Lane's shovel hits the officer in the foot; the officer "began to holler like mad," and the Colonel asks Lane if he is badly hit. Lane answers "No, sir" and that the officer is "making a lot of bloody row over nothing at all." The Colonel orders him to shut up and never speak of an officer in that way again. The narrator adds that Lane later said the officer could not have made more noise if his belly had been ripped open. Mechanism: the same rank-boundary joke, now addressed up the chain to the Colonel, and the Colonel's rule that this register may not be used to him. Target: the officer who is loud and the narrator's earlier point that he was decorated.
*Candidate application:* The rule, stated by an officer, that a man may not talk about officers this way, shows that the same remark is sayable in the platoon and not in the Colonel's hearing.

**[E13b-21]. Scrounging in Mametz Wood, and Dann's "quick-firers" before his death.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4143-4155)
The narrator goes "on the scrounge" into the wood for German topcoats and food (nights were cold for July), finding two tins of Maconochie, half a loaf of stale bread ("a wonder the rats hadn't got at it") and the coats, among the dead of both sides. He reports to Dann that they will have a feed; Dann replies "Righto" but says he thinks he will "write out a couple of quick-firers first" (see E13b-7 for the term). Mechanism: the routine business of letter-writing (the "I am all right" card) as the thing Dann does just before he dies. No comment is made on the coincidence.
*Candidate application:* The dying man's last act being the ritual reassurance card is a device the narrator leaves unremarked.

**[E13b-22]. Shell-shocked officers and an orderly's collapse: stated as sights, never joked about.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4179-4185 and L4238-4241)
Two young artillery officers whose guns have been blown up are led past "hysterical and horribly shellshocked," "the two worst cases I ever saw"; the narrator says that anyone "would have been doing them a kindness" to end their misery (restrained paraphrase; the original names the act). Later, under heavy shelling of the cemetery position, an old stretcher-bearer "went mad and started to undress himself," screaming; the men fought with him to get him to the Aid Post; he "had been going queer for the last month or two." No joke or nickname attaches to either. The phrase "going queer" (my gloss: in the narrator's idiom, a mental breakdown) is the only euphemism in these sentences. This is one of the book's clearest locations of silence about shell shock: the narrator describes the cases, uses the one mild euphemism, and does not joke.
*Candidate application:* Shell shock is the limit of the register: it gets observation and a mercy-language that is itself shocking, not humor.

**[E13b-23]. The rat at Mametz Wood: the payoff, told without a joke.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4158-4176)
Under shelling, Dann sits writing his cards, is hit in the neck by a spent bullet, and with a terrified look points behind the narrator, where the same huge rat sits on the trench-back looking at Dann; it vanishes into a shell hole; a shell bursts; Dann is dead, and the rat is dead beside him, thrown there by the blast. The narrator grabs it by the tail and swings it back into the shell hole, "getting the creeps," and says he had no doubt it was the same rat from Hulloch, though the two places are far apart: "It was the only weird experience I had during the whole of the War." He adds that no one near knew of Dann's death until he told them, and that without handling the rat he would have thought he had "been seeing things, like the many of us who saw things on the Retirement" (the 1914 retreat). Humor is entirely absent; the narrator's earlier laughter (E13b-3) is not mentioned. The text discloses the narrator's habit of verifying the supernatural by touch.
*Candidate application:* The one unjoked, unexplained omen of the book is carried by a physical object the narrator disposes of with his hands; the reader is not told whether the rat was an omen or the narrator's belief shaped the memory.

**[E13b-24]. The cemetery shelling and Duffy's dying remark: the last joke of a friend.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4223-4258)
Under a heavy bombardment of a shallow trench running into a cemetery, the narrator reports that shells throw corpses and coffins out of the graves (restrained; the text is blunt) and that the men "could only sit tight and grin and bear it." A man has his face wounded and two friends debate ending his life; he dies before they decide (restrained summary). Duffy is hit low down on the right side, and the narrator, knowing "his case was hopeless," asks the Company Commander to help carry him; the Company Commander replies "Certainly." On the way a splinter buries itself in the ground beneath the narrator's raised foot; Duffy, dying on the stretcher, says: "Hard lines, Dick!" and that a youngster in the narrator's place "would have had a beautiful Blighty wound through the foot," while the old ones "aren't lucky enough to stop one that way." The narrator tells him to shut up and that he has every chance; Duffy answers simply, "You know better than that, Dick." They shake hands; Duffy dies about two hours later. The narrator writes: "No man could wish for a better pal, and I never scrounged much around the Company, when in the line, after he was gone." Mechanism: the dying friend uses the old hands' own ritual envy-joke (E13b-17) as his last expression of care; the narrator tells him to be quiet; the grief is expressed as a change in behavior (no more scrounging) and not in words. The joke is not answered with a joke.
*Candidate application:* The hardest-hitting use of gallows formula in the memoir is the dying man's use of it for the other's sake; the survivor's response is silence and a changed habit.

**[E13b-25]. The signal station at the mill: the register of work under fire, and contempt for the frightened.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4259-4338)
The narrator and an old Cameronian signaller set up a heliograph and telescope at a mill 600 yards from High Wood to relay messages between the attacking force and Brigade. A barrage described as "one of the worst" the narrator was ever under falls on them all day; "The barrage of the previous day was a flea-bite to this one." Five other signallers sheltering in a shell hole were "absolutely useless and terror-stricken" and the narrator "cursed them until I was blue," without result. A Brigade runner volunteers to write down messages and, when hit by a splinter, says "Carry on with the message, Dick, I can bandage this myself." Both signallers in the wood are killed mid-message, and the message is never completed. A Manchesters captain from another division asks to send a message and is refused (orders limited the station to its own Brigade); the narrator calls him "a very brave and humane man," then says his Brigadier would have taken no notice anyway. Mechanism: the "flea-bite" belittling of the previous day; the tone of working professionals toward terror in strangers; the grim irony of a protocol that blocks a dying battalion's message. No jokes about the dead; the verbal humor is confined to the one hyperbole and the comrades' swearing. The narrator is harsher toward the five signallers' fear than toward the shellshocked officers (E13b-22), a distinction between fear in his own working group and the breakdown of others.
*Candidate application:* A character can be contemptuous of fear in men who are supposed to be doing the same job, and tender about breakdown in others; the difference is the job.

### C. End of Chapter XIV to the first part of Chapter XVII (L4338-5037): after High Wood, the winter of 1916-17

**[E13b-26]. The signaller who would not go back: resistance told without blame.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4338-4351)
Among the lightly wounded passing the mill is an old signaller of the battalion, limping with an ankle wound. He tells the narrator that if he gets through the barrage and is sent home, an oath invoking the Savior and all his angels (my paraphrase of a sacred-names oath) would never shift him back to France. The narrator says "He was true to his word." Once healed he avoided draft after draft, was sentenced to six months in prison, told the Governor after a week that he had done close to two years' active service, "done his whack," and did not mind staying in prison "until the bloody War finished"; the narrator last heard of him a month before the Armistice, still in England. The narrator calls the front line "the blood-tub" (the phrase, my gloss: the fighting line) in the line "pronounced fit for the blood-tub." Mechanism: a vow made as a joke-oath that the man then literally keeps; the narrator's neutral report of a refusal that the army punished. Target: the army's system of drafts and the prison's offer of release for volunteering. What the narrator does not do: condemn him or attach any joke to the prison term.
*Candidate application:* Of direct use for a defector or exile figure: the memoir's other-rank narrator approves, in a flat register, of a man who refused to return and accepted prison, by noting only that he kept his word. *Cross-use:* Exile characters who consider they have done their whack and decline further service.

**[E13b-27]. A thought experiment about statesmen under the barrage.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4359-4367)
After carrying a message to Brigade, the narrator returns past a company of Argyles who had been hit in a bombardment in his absence and finds only the remains (restrained: the original lists body parts; I do not reproduce them). He then writes a sentence-long wish: that if the leading statesmen and generals of the warring countries were threatened with the barrage of 20 July 1916, and told they would face a similar one in a week, they would perhaps have "signed a peace treaty before the week was up". Mechanism: a conditional satirical thought experiment aimed at leaders, delivered in the memoir's retrospective voice (the "I have often wondered since"). Target: statesmen and generals as a class, who are imagined as people who would solve the problem if exposed.
*Candidate application:* An enlisted survivor's version of the anti-war argument is not a speech but a hypothetical about leaders' safety; it keeps the tone of someone who has stopped expecting an answer.

**[E13b-28]. Parcels for the dead: a custom narrated without comment.** (PW-07, Ch. XIV, L4370-4378)
After the relief, the narrator gives the Brigade's losses (three-quarters casualties; ten of eighteen signallers lost) and notes: "It was the custom that all parcels that arrived for men who were casualties should be distributed among the survivors." The Public Schools Battalion's commander sent mailbags of parcels to the Royal Welch, and "We lived on luxuries for the next few days." Mechanism: the juxtaposition of mass casualty and luxury food is recorded flat; the custom (a distribution rule) is stated as institutional. The narrator does not remark on the irony, and does not joke about whose parcels they were.
*Candidate application:* A unit's economy of the dead (parcels, tobacco, razors) is a documented place where gallows humor can be left entirely implicit.

**[E13b-29]. Rank etiquette among the old hands: "a sergeant or nothing."** (PW-07, Ch. XV, "Trenches in High Wood," L4391-4395)
With "not fifty" of the original Expeditionary Force men left apart from the transport (L4398-4399), any old hand could have had stripes for the asking; Freezer, who had "just had a lot of Number Ones washed out as usual" (his punishments quashed), says he was offered full corporal and refused: "He would either be a sergeant or nothing." "Two days later he was made a sergeant." Mechanism: deadpan sequencing (an announcement of refusal followed by its fulfilment); the old hand's bargaining position. The line shows that rank is something the veterans decline or accept on their own terms.
*Candidate application:* Veterans treat promotion as a favor they grant, not a prize they win, once losses have made them scarce.

**[E13b-30]. Crown-and-Anchor winnings, fifty bottles of champagne, and the narrator's account of what drinking is for.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4398-4410)
The narrator reports that the village was packed with troops, with a dozen Crown-and-Anchor boards outside each café; he and Paddy won over a thousand francs (about forty pounds) and spent it on fifty bottles of champagne, keeping the rest until next day: "We were making sure of something." (Retrospective: the narrator adds that "we had not yet reached halfway in the War," and that 1917 and early 1918 were worse.) The old signallers "had a muck-in with that lot of champagne," and the narrator states the rule: "We never used to drink anything, when we could afford to drink, unless we could get properly drunk on it"; "It made us merry and helped us to forget." Younger soldiers joining from that time "soon followed our example." Mechanism: gambling windfall converted at once to shared drink; the narrator's own statement of the function of drunkenness (merriment and forgetting). He does not say what is forgotten.
*Candidate application:* Heavy drinking presented by an other-ranks narrator as a deliberate, shared, almost professional practice, and transmitted to replacements as a norm.

**[E13b-31]. The new officer's drill on the march, and Snooky's prayer.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4422-4443)
The signallers, runners and regimental police march as a platoon of their own; a newly joined officer in charge refuses them the ten-minute rest by turning each halt into arms drill ("Halt! Order arms!", then "Slope arms!") until it is time to fall in again; the Adjutant spots it, takes him aside, and there is no more drilling. "Many impromptu prayers went up for him," but an old hand, Snooky, says he will offer "a pukka one" in billets. Snooky, who had served in India with the narrator, has "a mania for praying" dating from a touch of the sun there that old soldiers called the "Deolalic Tap" (the text's gloss: sun-touched; my gloss: a recognized comic category for madness). Next day the narrator finds Snooky on his knees "praying beautifully" that on the next tour every German shell would, by heavenly guidance, land on the new officer and destroy him for "the misery he had caused the humble, God-fearing troops" (restrained paraphrase; the original asks for his body to be blown to pieces). Mechanisms: mock-liturgical prayer as curse; a nickname for sun-madness; the formula "the usual soldiers' prayers" (also E13b-1, E13b-43). Target: a newly arrived officer who drills men at rest.
*Candidate application:* Snooky is a documented recurring comic figure: the unit's ritual intercessor who gives the group's resentment a liturgical form and so makes it safe to say aloud.

**[E13b-32]. The new officer's club: the old hand's warning and the officer's refusal to hear it.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4446-4456)
The same officer is twirling a spiked club ("very handy weapons in a trench"). The narrator replies that he would be lucky ever to use one, as he had every chance of being killed on the way; the officer says he has heard that "yarn" before, that old soldiers "thought we knew everything," and that every old soldier he met "had given him the jerks" (my gloss: made him uneasy). The narrator says the officer might have "a good jerk" before he was many days older; the officer threatens to put him "on the peg" (my gloss: on a charge). The narrator adds that all but two of the young officers who joined since arriving in France "had proved themselves real good men, but this man was the limit." Mechanism: the veteran's prediction as a rank-crossing insult that the officer takes as insubordination; the narrator's statement of fairness (most young officers were good) before the verdict. What it shows is the license: the old hand may warn, the officer may punish, and the narrator makes clear the disliked officer is the exception.
*Candidate application:* The veteran's prophecy of a green officer's death is a common double act of warning and contempt; the narrator qualifies it by limiting the target to one man.

**[E13b-33]. The Leaning Virgin at Albert.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4459-4461)
The statue on the church tower hung head downward, and "the French people believed that when it fell the War would come to an end." One-sentence mention with no comment from the narrator; included as a civilian prophecy that the narrator reports without adopting.
*Candidate application:* Civilian superstitions about the end of the war reported by a soldier as local color, not as hope.

**[E13b-34]. Sol's death: the offered shack and "All the good ones seemed to be going West."** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4463-4493)
At Fricourt Wood the narrator and Salisbury ("Sol"), a signaller who had held the D.C.M. and the Russian Order of St. George, smoke and yarn; Sol offers to share his better shack; the narrator declines because they must leave for Division in the morning (a forty-eight-hour course in the Fullerphone). That night heavy shelling flattens both shacks; the narrator, whose shack had been built over a shell hole, crawls out; he finds Sol and gets the Aid Post corporal's torch; Sol, "badly hit in three places," dies in half an hour without regaining consciousness. The narrator's only gloss: "All the good ones seemed to be going West," and that if his shack had not been built over a shell hole he "would have been finished too." There is no joke in the passage; the only verbal ornament is the shared yarning that precedes it. A second small refusal: the narrator turned down the offer for trivial reasons.
*Candidate application:* The death of a named friend is narrated by an offer that was declined for a trivial reason and by a flat verdict on "the good ones," with no joke afterwards.

**[E13b-35]. The bacon ration: "I chose a dip in the fat and considered I had backed a winner."** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4496-4503)
At Divisional Headquarters the cooks give each man a choice between a bit of bacon "about half the size of a Woodbine packet" or "a dip in the fat." The narrator notes that "there was always a bit of sharking done with it, the same as with the rum" in the battalion; he chose the fat and "considered I had backed a winner"; Paddy "was lucky that he was not put in clink for airing his views on the subject." Mechanism: gambling idiom ("backed a winner") applied to a food trivia; the mock-heroic stakes of rations; the unstated threat of arrest for comment. Target: the divisional cooks and rear organization, which are, in the narrator's terms, worse than the battalion's own rations system.
*Candidate application:* Ration cheating up the chain of supply is the type of small injustice about which the old hands joke most freely, because the targets are far enough and the stakes are daily.

**[E13b-36]. The signaller who could not bear shellfire, and the narrator's whisky.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4509-4552 and L4590-4596)
In the High Wood trench the narrator is paired with a young signaller of about nine months' service ("a very decent chap") who had never recovered from an early experience: a bullet that parted his hair through his helmet, after which he ran back screaming, "more frightened than hurt"; "since then it was a pity to see him when we were under intense shellfire." During a heavy bombardment (sixty company casualties by 10 a.m.) he is "huddled up in a heap on the fire-step" and "shaking like a madman"; the narrator tells him to "buck up," but he does not move off the fire-step for thirty-six hours, taking no food, drinking from his water bottle. When rations and the narrator's parcel finally arrive (E13b-41), the narrator gives him "some stiff doses" of whisky "but which had no effect on him: it was good stuff wasted." Mechanism: the narrator's only joke is the waste of the whisky; the verdict "a pity to see him" is sympathetic and does not scorn. A contrast with E13b-25: the narrator's contempt there was for men he expected to work.
*Candidate application:* A character who offers a frightened comrade the best drink he has and then records it as wasted shows the limit of the cure and a dry joke about it at once.

**[E13b-37]. The bay built up with dead, and the "grinning" faces: macabre sight, stated plainly.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4521-4540)
The narrator notes that parts of the parapet had been built up with the bodies of men killed earlier, with limbs showing; in one bay only the heads of two men were visible, whose faces seemed to be grinning at the new arrivals (restrained: the original uses the word "horribly" and describes the bodies). Survivors of the 20 July attack explain: the ground was hard, a man killed nearby was used as head cover and covered with earth, and "No doubt in many cases this saved the lives of the men that were digging themselves in." Later the narrator says the company threw its dead onto the back of the parapet during the shelling (restrained). No joke; the explanation is practical.
*Candidate application:* A practical, unjoking explanation for a grotesque sight is the register when the narrator is not licensing humor.

**[E13b-38]. The runaways the young sergeant did not report.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4555-4567)
After a bombardment, about half a dozen newly joined men run past the narrator without rifles; a young Platoon-Sergeant behind them shouts "Don't let any of them pass you, Dick. They're running away." German bombers dodging tree to tree had crept close and thrown bombs into the trench. The sergeant drives them back "but never reported them," because they would have been court-martialled and "probably shot." The narrator adds that proper lookout would have shot the bombers before they got in range. No joke and no comment on the sergeant's choice beyond the report.
*Candidate application:* A non-commissioned officer's silent decision not to report panic is a documented form of protective withholding (compare E13b-3).

**[E13b-39]. The man blown out of the trench who was tried for desertion, and the sentence commuted.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4570-4584)
A shell kills nine of a group of ten (restrained: the original describes the bodies); the tenth, thought blown to pieces, is brought back to the battalion six weeks later under escort for court-martial for desertion. He explains he was thrown into a shell hole, woke in the dark with stars overhead, and ran in terror until he reached the back areas. He was sentenced to death, commuted to imprisonment "owing to his extreme youth," and "still stayed with the Battalion"; the narrator believes the Colonel, who knew the full facts, "was instrumental in getting the young chap away with it." In 1917 the young man was killed or taken prisoner. No joke or euphemism; the death sentence is stated and its commutation attributed to the Colonel. Register item for execution content.
*Candidate application:* The one named near-execution in the narrator's own battalion is narrated with sympathy and no humor; the Colonel's quiet intervention is the only rank-negotiation in it.

**[E13b-40]. The prayer answered: the new officer killed by the barrage, and a mock-pious epilogue.** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4587-4590)
The officer who said the old hands "gave him the jerks" (E13b-32) is "blown to pieces by this same barrage" (restrained: wording simplified). Snooky, whom the narrator meets "a little later," said "it was wonderful how his prayer had been answered so quickly," and that "little prayers sometimes did a great deal of good." The narrator does not comment further and the passage immediately turns to the ration party and the whisky parcel. Mechanism: deadpan comic fulfillment of the curse-prayer, pushing mock-piety to a statement of fact about the dead. It breaks the usual rule that the dead are not joked about; the narrator's earlier line that this officer was "the limit" (E13b-32) is the license. The narrator does not say whether he laughed.
*Candidate application:* The license to joke about a dead officer is conferred by his earlier conduct; the humor targets what he did, not the death.

**[E13b-41]. The ration party blown up and the parcel labeled "Sauce."** (PW-07, Ch. XV, L4590-4596)
The narrator reports that on the first evening at High Wood "the whole of the ration party, mail and all, were blown to bits," and in the same sentence's neighborhood that he was expecting a parcel with a pint-and-a-half tin of whisky that was to be "labeled 'Sauce'" and wondered whether it "had gone West." The next evening rations, mail and the parcel arrive safely; he leaves half the whisky with the signallers at Headquarters. Mechanism: the sender's disguise of liquor under an innocuous label (the label is the home-front's complicity); the narrator's concern for the parcel set beside the report of the dead ration party. The narrator does not remark on the juxtaposition. A forty-year-old newcomer, Sealyham, proves "like a veteran" and is introduced as "one of the best amateur elocutionists I ever heard" (a business owner from the Midlands who joined "because he was patriotic").
*Candidate application:* The soldier's worry about a parcel in the same breath as the loss of its carriers is the type of displacement that gallows humor does without a joke.

### D. Chapter XVI (L4603-4887): "Les Boeufs and Trones Wood"

**[E13b-42]. The A.S.C. song: a parody sung sadly, with the target explained.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4607-4633)
During the march from the Somme the men sing, "sadly," to the tune of "The Church's One Foundation," a song beginning "We are in Kitchener's Army / The ragtime A.S.C." and declaring "We cannot fight, we cannot shoot," ending with the Kaiser saying "Hoch, Hoch! Mein Gott" at the prospect of such a lot "to draw six bob a day." The narrator explains: "This song was sung out of no disrespect to Kitchener's Army, but was a kick against the motor lorry drivers of the A.S.C.," who received between four and six shillings a day (more than a private's pay). Mechanism: hymn-tune parody with a mock-German response; singing slow and mournful as a delivery of resentment; the narrator's own gloss on the target (pay grievance against rear-echelon drivers) and disclaimer toward Kitchener's volunteers. Target: the A.S.C. and the pay scale.
*Candidate application:* A pay-differential song aimed at an adjacent specialty is a standard boundary song; the narrator's disclaimer shows the group distinguishes banter targets by service type, not by army.

**[E13b-43]. "The Peer": an old regular officer with pre-war manners, and the prayers that "were in vain."** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4635-4661)
An old regular officer, newly arrived from West Africa and put in charge of the signallers, is called "The Peer." The narrator notes that discipline was "still good" but "not so severe as it had been," with a "better spirit of comradeship between officers and men," and "gone were the days when a man would be brought up and punished for having the lace of his rifle-sling not properly tied." The Peer had not lost his pre-war ways: on the first morning he notices mud on the narrator's boots (the cause being illness after bad wine, a bodily matter I summarize), calls him "absolutely filthy," refuses to hear his explanation ("How dare you speak to an officer before he addresses you?"), and orders the narrator and Paddy to parade in full marching order and cleaned two hours after the day's march, then orders the same for all signallers. Snooky, now the signallers' cook, is reprimanded for something trivial. The men offered "the usual soldiers' prayers," and Snooky prayed whenever he was not cooking, but "all his prayers were in vain." The text's last word on it: "The Peer survived the War." Mechanism: an aristocratic nickname for a stickler; the sarcastic ledger of prayers that did not work, in the same sentence structure as the answered prayer (E13b-40).
*Candidate application:* A nickname that makes the officer's class and manner the joke, plus the group ritual of praying against him, is a stable pattern.

**[E13b-44]. The boil with lice, and the supposed clean shirt.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4675-4682)
In the Aid Post the doctor lances a large boil on a man's back, the sergeant dresses it; the next morning the man says he had a restless night and would have ripped the dressing off if he could reach it. Under the dressing the narrator sees lice in the wound (a bodily/vermin item, summarized). "No wonder the man had spent a restless night, and yet only forty-eight hours previously the man had had a bath and a supposed clean shirt." Mechanism: bathos on a clean-up that fails; the word "supposed." Target: the army's cleanliness arrangements.
*Candidate application:* Lice are used as a measure of how little the official hygiene actually reaches the soldier.

**[E13b-45]. "It's rotten, Dick, how the good ones are popped over so quick": a grief that turns to swearing, and a wish for a shirker's execution.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4709-4739)
In the sector at Les Boeufs, the narrator finds a young signaller dead on the line he was repairing. An old Battalion Headquarters signaller says he is sorry, "a damned good kid," begins to swear, and when he cools says that "the good ones are popped over so quick and the rotten ones simply get nice blighty wounds." He relates that a "bun-punching" full corporal, newly sent out as a signalling corporal, had refused his turn on the lines as beneath a corporal; when the sergeant threatened to report him the old signaller says he hoped the man would refuse "so that I would have the pleasure of reading that he had been shot at dawn for cowardice" (restrained paraphrase; the original names execution). The corporal went; a shrapnel fragment hit his arm; he "howled like a stuck pig" and ran back; the narrator-speaker dressed the wound and said he would have had his head knocked off "if he had his due." The old signaller adds: "That's the third we have had hit to-night on the blasted line; but the other two were good kids." The narrator adds that this corporal "was a pukka bun-puncher" whom he had seen buying condensed milk "and sucking them like a kid." Mechanisms: the old hands' luck formula (E13b-17) reversed into a moral rule (the bad ones get the good wounds); a grief that goes through swearing; a nickname ("bun-puncher") for the shirker (the companion pass E13a records, from Part 1, that the text there glosses "bun-puncher" as a teetotaller, "char wallah," "wad-shifter"; here it evidently carries that sense of a soft, abstaining, unsoldierly type, and the corporal's sucking of condensed milk fits it; the usage recurs in E13b-55 and E13b-109); contempt used where in the memoir humor stops. This is the main place in my range where the old hands' contempt leaves comedy and enters a wish for execution, which I flag.
*Candidate application:* The group's real boundary: grief is expressed as rage at the one who avoids the same risk, and the thing joked about (a Blighty wound) is the thing the shirker is envied and hated for.

**[E13b-46]. The Peer under the barrage: respect earned, the nickname's sting removed.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4742-4782)
At the advanced signal station The Peer visits during a barrage and "made no effort to get into the dug-out": "He showed he had guts and went up twenty holes in my estimation." The narrator says that The Peer "did more ducking that night than ever he had during the whole of his life before," and when asked if he had been under a worse barrage, the narrator says it was "a popcorn barrage compared to some" (and The Peer "thought I was romancing"). A party of lost French troops is encountered; the narrator tells the reader that The narrator says the experience "had did him and us a lot of good": The Peer "dropped his pre-War ways and started to act like a sensible human being." Captain Radford, an officer "highly respected and very popular with the old hands," asks "What would the old hands say about this crowd?" and the narrator replies "no doubt they would curse a bit." The narrator reports without comment that his Company Commander, Captain Higginson, had been sent home three weeks earlier "with a nervous breakdown," and that the new Company Commander (name uncertain, "Greaves or Grieves") is "a tall slight chap with a stutter, and a very decent officer." Mechanisms: a hyperbolic belittling of the barrage to an outsider; the officer's reconciliation through shared danger; an officer who asks the old hands' opinion by proxy; a breakdown reported plain.
*Candidate application:* Respect for an officer can be won by one night of staying in the open with the men; the old hand's comic belittling ("popcorn") is addressed to the officer as a test.

**[E13b-47]. The tree-hung German at Trones Wood.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4785-4792)
The first sight on stepping from the hut is a dead German hanging in a branch, who "seemed to be carefully watching over the hut"; the narrator, with Hammer Lane out scrounging for wood, explains that a shell had thrown the body onto a branch, and the figure looked like "a man who had been hung up on a hook." The personification as a watcher is the only figurative turn; the description is otherwise brief (restrained: the mechanism of how the branch held the body).
*Candidate application:* Anthropomorphizing a corpse as a watcher is as close as the memoir gets to a macabre simile.

**[E13b-48]. A will given to a friend, and the batman's rations.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4795-4813)
On the night before going in for forty-eight hours the narrator instructs Paddy that if anything happens to him he may do what he likes with an expected parcel and give the tobacco to a pipe smoker (a practical will). On the way in, a shell lands on the party passing a shot-down plane; the dead include the Regimental Sergeant-Major's batman, "carrying a lovely bag of rations," which an old signaller picks up: "During the following day each one of us put on a little weight and the R.S.M. lost a little." The narrator adds that there were more rations in that bag than the whole of the signallers had together. Mechanism: the joke is displaced from the death (the batman is not named and not joked about) to the RSM's loss; the signallers' gain is the pivot. Target: the RSM and the privileged food that went with rank.
*Candidate application:* Death-adjacent comic accounting that jokes at the expense of a rank rather than the dead man.

**[E13b-49]. The Colonel's raincoat and Sealyham's razors: the Adjutant on a bad line.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4819-4837)
At Battalion Headquarters the Colonel's servant reports the Colonel's new raincoat missing; the Colonel takes it "as the fortune of war" while the servant "seemed more cut up over the loss." Sealyham reports his haversack with "the two best razors in France" stolen at the moment the Adjutant is shouting "What's that?" repeatedly into a weak telephone line; Sealyham, thinking that one of the group is answering and pulling his leg, "launched out with a flow of language which would have done credit to the Old Soldier." The Adjutant, Mr. Mann, put the phone down, "burst out laughing" and had the narrator tell Sealyham to be quiet until the call was finished. Sealyham finds the haversack nearby, empty: "Haversacks were easy to get, but razors were scarce." Mechanism: comedy of crossed lines (literal telephone), the repeated phrase "What's that?"; swearing as virtuoso performance; the rank-honoring detail that the Adjutant, an excellent officer of twelve months, laughs. Target: the thieves of the relieved troops (anonymous), and the servant's grief.
*Candidate application:* A Headquarters farce about theft of an officer's kit and a private's razors stays good-natured because the Adjutant participates in the laugh.

**[E13b-50]. The Public Schools company's false report, and the Colonel's rage.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4840-4855)
During the November attack, the Public Schools captain on the left tells the Colonel they were held up by heavy machine-gun fire; the narrator says "which was all bunkum," the Colonel tells the captain so, and tells him that he and "his rotten men" were no good as they had endangered the lives of decent soldiers; the narrator had "never seen the Colonel in such a rage." Compared with E13b-2's affectionate belittling of the same battalion's soldiering, this is where the boundary joke becomes contempt, because the left flank is exposed. No humor in the passage.
*Candidate application:* A unit may be treated as a comic outsider until its failure exposes others, at which point the tone changes entirely.

**[E13b-51]. The wounded Englishman kept by Germans, and Paddy's parcel: the rumor cascade.** (PW-07, Ch. XVI, L4857-4885)
A mopping-up party about to throw bombs into a hidden dug-out hears an English voice shout "Don't throw bombs in here"; inside are six Germans who had carried in, dressed and cared for a wounded Royal Welch man "as if he had been one of their own men." The narrator adds no comment on the Germans' conduct. A paragraph later: back at the transport, Paddy meets the narrator, "thunderstruck," and says: "I thought you was on the way to Blighty." Rumors had run through the transport that Headquarters had casualties: first that the narrator had "a lovely one in the muscle of the arm," then that he had lost an arm and a leg, then that he was seen walking back with his arm in a sling smoking his pipe; Paddy "devoured your parcel and given the tobacco away, the same as you told me." Paddy tells the number of yarns so that "I almost smelled a rat" (the narrator's phrase). The narrator closes: "I was hard up for tobacco at the time and I thought my luck was dead out." Mechanism: the rumor that escalates through the typical Blighty-wound stages, ending with the wounded man calmly smoking; the comic hardship of a will obeyed too promptly. Humor stops at the wounded Englishman in the German dug-out, where the narrator reports the enemy's humane act flat.
*Candidate application:* The rumor chain from "a lovely one in the muscle of the arm" to amputation to pipe-smoking is a good model of how battalion gossip inflates and then deflates; the comic cost is a parcel of tobacco.

### E. Chapter XVII (from L4888): the winter of 1916-1917

**[E13b-52]. The villagers who "wanted our money but not us," the stolen tubs and the cesspool.** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, "Somme: Winter 1916-1917," L4892-4906)
Back on rest twenty miles from Abbeville, the narrator says the villagers "wanted our money but not us." Wood is scarce; with Sealyham, Lane, Paddy and an old signaller, Ricco, planning a night with wine and a pint-and-a-half of rum, the narrator and Ricco steal two large tubs from the local farm woman, who sold eggs and chips and "highly watered" drink and was "a proper shark," break them up, and throw the hoops in her cesspool (a concealment). The night: "a good drink, a good fire," yarning and singing in turns; Sealyham is "the star performer" with impersonations of Dickens characters. The woman "made more row over the loss of her tubs than what she would have done if she had lost her whole kith and kin in the War"; more tubs vanished in the next few days. Mechanisms: theft justified by the victim's profiteering; the narrator's comparison of her grief for tubs to the grief she might have had for kin; the concealment in an unlooked-at place. Target: a civilian shopkeeper. The line about "kith and kin" is a comment on civilian proportion that the narrator offers without apology.
*Candidate application:* An enlisted unit's attitude toward the civilians among whom it is billeted is transactional and sometimes contemptuous; the humor is at the civilian's expense and the soldier's theft is "fair" because of her prices.

**[E13b-53]. The intelligence lecture and the departing French.** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L4909-4928)
At a signalling course at South African Corps, a Chief Intelligence Officer tells the class that during the whole Somme operation "only one important message had been picked up from the enemy," and that by the time it was decoded it was too late to use; the narrator reports this flat. A few pages later, when the battalion relieves a French battalion, the French signallers "instead of decently showing us which way our lines ran" packed up and were out "like greased lightning," and the other French soldiers likewise "as if the whole of the German Army were at their heels." Mechanism: an ally mocked for hasty relief. Target: the French; the intelligence service.
*Candidate application:* Allies' behavior at a relief is a standard comic subject for a British ranker, with the Royal Welch narrator treating it as a measure of the sector's danger.

**[E13b-54]. Wooden crosses burned: "no good to the dead but they provided warmth for the living."** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L4931-4941)
In the frost the trenches ran into the River Somme (frozen over a foot thick), the sector was "one of the most desolate places that I had been in in France," and fire-buckets and firewood were scarce; The men took wooden crosses from lonely graves for fuel, and the narrator's only comment is "They were no good to the dead but they provided warmth for the living." No joke is attached; the sentence is a moral justification, and it is the only justification the narrator offers.
*Candidate application:* An act that would be shocking to civilians is justified in one practical sentence and not joked about.

**[E13b-55]. General Pinney, the rum and "more prayers offered up for him than for any general in France."** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L4941-4949 and L4994-4996)
The Division was "the worst in the whole of France for rum issues": half a ration or none; the Divisional General, Major-General Pinney (named in the text), set up places two miles behind the line where men returning could have "a cup of hot tea and one small biscuit." The narrator imagines that in "his comfortable quarters many miles behind the Front" the general "couldn't see what benefit rum was to any man," and that "there were more prayers offered up for him than for any general in France." The men called him a "bun-punching crank," more fitted to command "a Church Mission hut at the Base than a division of troops." After the thaw the narrator says the battalion lost nearly a hundred men "through trench feet ... and from having our rum ration taken from us." Mechanisms: the prayer-formula again (E13b-1, E13b-31, E13b-43); the term "bun-punching" (the companion pass E13a notes that Part 1 glosses "bun-puncher" as a teetotaller; the reading fits a general who docked the rum and is likened to a Church Mission hut manager; compare E13b-45); the narrator's line that physical loss follows from the withdrawal of rum, the only place in the memoir where he attributes casualties to a policy of abstinence. Target: a named divisional commander.
*Candidate application:* Rum is treated as medicine and as a right; a commander who withholds it becomes the target of the same formulas the unit uses against the corps commander.

**[E13b-56]. The falling airman and the officer who kept the valuables: "a distant relation."** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L4952-4973)
A German scout plane burns and a bundle falls from it: the airman, whose body the narrator and Hammer Lane reach; every bone broken (restrained summary); a second airman is still strapped in the burning aircraft (restrained: the original describes the burning body). A new officer takes the valuables and papers "to the Colonel" but hands over the papers and keeps the valuables; the narrator's remark: "He must have been a distant relation to A Company's old sanitary men, who had now both gone home wounded." Mechanism: a lineage joke tying an officer's theft to the scroungers' tradition; the narrator's own search of the body is unremarked. What the narrator does not joke about: the two airmen; the only humor is on the officer's looting.
*Candidate application:* A looting norm that the other ranks treat as honest scrounging becomes comic when an officer takes the same loot under pretense of duty.

**[E13b-57]. Colonel Crawshay shot by his own side: "very cut up."** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L4976-4991)
During a patrol to capture a German patrol in the snow, the Colonel, Dr. Dunn and Lane, all in white smocks, are fired on by a Lewis gunner of the Public Schools Battalion on the right who had been relieved without being told of the patrol; the Colonel is "badly hit," the other two untouched. "The whole of the Battalion was very cut up over the loss." The narrator hears after the war that he recovered but is often in nursing homes. The narrator writes no joke and uses no nickname; "we knew we would be very lucky" to get another as good. No chaff at the Public Schools battalion appears here either, though it was their sentry's fire.
*Candidate application:* Friendly-fire injury of a loved commander is reported with sorrow and without an accusation or a joke; the humor boundary (E13b-50) is silent where the offender could have been mocked.

**[E13b-58]. The signallers' new education, and Paddy's two-to-one bet.** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L5001-5012)
The signaller's section now includes "a schoolmaster, two school teachers, two bank clerks and a young architect" (a list the narrator gives without commentary). When the Germans retire to the Hindenburg Line, Paddy offers to bet two to one that the German front line would still be four miles this side of St. Quentin in twelve months; the narrator adds: Paddy "would have been an easy winner," yet "one famous war correspondent had written that it was only a question of hours before our victorious troops would occupy that place." Mechanism: the wager as the old hands' reply to press optimism; the "easy winner" retrospective. Target: war correspondents and optimism.
*Candidate application:* Betting against an announced victory is the old soldier's way of reading the newspapers.

**[E13b-59]. Bob the sketcher and "The Ramrod": a prank on a pessimist, and the contrast with what he did next.** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L5015-5037)
Bob, an old soldier, a former scout turned signaller, is "a very humorous sketcher" who, seeing an early humorous sketch of troops in the trenches in *Punch*, drew an addition and sent it to the editor, who replied with an autographed letter that Bob prized above everything he owned. He is "a born pessimist" with admiration for the German General von Mackensen, whom he calls "The Ramrod," and says that no general on any side except The Ramrod had "the brains of a gander." Paddy, "winking at me," tells him that von Mackensen is coming to the Western Front; Bob springs up: "Thank God for that news!", says the war "will now soon be over," that the Ramrod will "roll up the British and French Armies like a man rolling a blanket up," and that he will surrender and "volunteer to do batman to The Ramrod," adding that it "will be a pleasure to black his boots." The next paragraph (L5034-5037) begins the sequel: the following month in the Hindenburg Tunnel Bob received a flesh wound and, in the narrator's words, "went stone-mad for a while, bayoneted two Germans and fought like a tiger until his arm gave out" (restrained summary of the fighting) before being sent to England. Mechanism: a wind-up of a pessimist; the hyperbolic surrender fantasy as a way of saying the generals are fools; the later behavior contradicts the fantasy and is told without comment. Target: generals; the Allied command.
*Candidate application:* The old hand's loud defeatist joke is a safe expression of contempt for his own side's command that does not predict his battlefield behavior.

### F. Spring and summer 1917: Arras, the Hindenburg Line, Whitsun, the coast and Polygon Wood (L5037-5736)

*(Note on numbering: the OCR prints "CHAPTER XVII" twice, at L4888 and L5104; the second is evidently XVIII, and L5288 is headed XIX, L5507 XX. I cite the printed headings as they appear in the text file.)*

**[E13b-60]. Bob's letters: the pessimist's obsession survives his wound.** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L5037-5039)
After Bob is evacuated (E13b-59), the narrator corresponds with him "for a time," and "in every letter I received there was a newspaper cutting about Mackensen on the Eastern Front." A one-sentence coda: the running gag outlasts the unit. No comment on his bayonet fighting (L5034-5037) or on the contradiction with his surrender fantasy.
*Candidate application:* A comrade's private obsession carried as a postal running joke is a way for a dispersed unit to keep a shared character alive.

**[E13b-61]. The new colonel, Mr. Sassoon introduced, and the narrator's refusal of promotion.** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L5042-5057)
The new Colonel, Garnett, is "a decent old stick but not to be compared with the colonel we had just lost." Two new officers are "of a far better stamp," and "Mr. Sassoon, who was wearing the ribbon of the Military Cross was soon very popular with the men of the Company he was posted to." The narrator describes his own arrangement: no parades, he cleans the signallers' billet each morning for the officer's inspection and is then "free to go scrounging around"; he had "refused promotion many times" because an N.C.O. would be "on parade day and night"; he prefers cleaning to "the monotonous parades"; with Lane (who also does no parades) he has "glorious days" in villages "before going back to the blood-tub where we never knew what might happen to us." Mechanism: the old-soldier persona stated as a technique: a regular who has mastered the unit's wants and keeps out of its demands; the line about "enjoyment ... before" the front is the stated function of the leisure. Target: parades and promotion as burdens.
*Candidate application:* The veteran who declines rank to keep his freedom of movement, and who justifies it by the dread of the line, is a stable character type.

**[E13b-62]. Signalling gadgets: the staff's inventions as a running dry joke, and "delightful language over the line."** (PW-07, Ch. XVII, L5060-5101)
The narrator gives losses (seven signallers killed and six wounded between July and August, six more around Les Boeufs; "losing fifteen out of twenty" runners in one show), then reviews equipment: the Lucas Lamp (used successfully only in the last months, "when the shelling was not a flea-bite to what it had been"), an aeroplane signaling sheet "all right in Back Areas where there were no ironfoundries exploding around one," a wireless that could send but not receive ("a rotten failure," after which "We never saw no more of those instruments"), and "the amplifier" that picked up enemy messages "once in a blue moon." He "found it very amusing" to listen in on the amplifier station: one front-line signaller told another to "pull the bloody frog out of his throat," and "There was some delightful language over the line after that." Mechanism: the signaller's professional deadpan on useless staff equipment, with shellfire described as a foundry; the narrator's taste for others' swearing, which recurs (E13b-1, E13b-49, E13b-75). Target: the staff's inventions.
*Candidate application:* A specialist (signaller, medic, driver) tells the story of the army through the failures of his trade's equipment.

**[E13b-63]. The corps general in the snow: Bob's decoration sarcasm and the brooms on the carts.** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII [printed "XVII"], "Arras: Hindenburg Line I," L5108-5117)
On the march to Arras, in rain and wet snow, the Corps General and staff stand under a tree to inspect the battalion. The old hands cannot understand why he ventured out, and Bob says "[I] expect the old B—— will receive a big decoration for this day's work" (the text prints "T" for "I"; the blank is as printed). The narrator is told later that the general "had played up holy hell" with the Colonel because the cooks' carts carried brooms stuck up on them. Mechanism: the decoration-for-nothing sarcasm (compare E13b-10, E13b-13) aimed at a general; a trivial inspection complaint as the evidence of misplaced zeal. Target: the Corps General.
*Candidate application:* The sarcastic prediction of a medal for a staff officer's showing-up is a ready formula.

**[E13b-64]. The cavalry at Basseux: laughing at the recruits' optimism.** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII, L5120-5131)
Seeing cavalry lined up on a road, a young soldier new to action says "I expect they are getting ready for a break through." "We old hands laughed like anything" and Paddy says: "They couldn't break through my granny's apron-strings!" and that they might as well be on rocking-horses. The narrator adds history: since the first months of the war cavalry had mostly been converted into infantry; Household Cavalry in the trenches at Clery had half down with trench feet; Bengal Lancers he saw riding forward on the Somme were "very nearly wiped out"; "One good German machine-gunner who kept his nerve could easily mow down a squadron of cavalry." Mechanism: veterans' laughter as a hazing of naive optimism; domestic-image insult ("granny's apron-strings"); the narrator's technical aside. Target: the cavalry as an arm and the recruits' hopes.
*Candidate application:* The old hands' collective laugh at a recruit's hopeful guess is the unit's way of teaching what the war is.

**[E13b-65]. What the narrator tells recruits about the dead: "a dead crow."** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII, L5147-5156 and L5159-5182)
On the march to the Hindenburg Line, passing three or four hundred British dead laid out in rows for burial (restrained: described plainly in the original), the narrator tells the newly joined, who "turned a bit queer at the sight," that "if they were alive in a month's time they would take no more notice of a dead man than they would of a dead crow." In the captured tunnel, when a newcomer begins with a candle to drag things from beneath a bed, the narrator "pulled him sharply away" because German stick-bombs lay under the beds, and gives "a couple more instructions what to do and what not to do." Mechanism: a prediction of numbness delivered as a rule of thumb and as a joke-like image; practical safety instruction in the same chapter. The remark is the narrator's own statement of the expected desensitization.
*Candidate application:* The veteran's pedagogy: tell the newcomer in advance that the shock will wear off, with an animal comparison; the humor is the bluntness.

**[E13b-66]. Praise for Sassoon in the old soldier's idiom, and "a decorated shoulder."** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII, L5185-5200)
A hundred bombers under Mr. Sassoon are sent to help the Cameronians attack the Hindenburg Trench; part of it is taken and lost again; Sassoon is shot through the top of the shoulder. That night an old soldier, one of the few survivors of old B Company, tells the narrator: "it would have done your eyes good to have seen young Sassoon in that bombing stunt" ("God strike me pink, Dick"), that he put him in mind of Mr. Fletcher, "a bloody treat to see the way he took the lead," and that "it's wicked how the good officers get killed or wounded and the rotten ones are still left crawling about"; "If he don't get the Victoria Cross for this stunt I'm a bloody Dutchman." The narrator adds that this was "the universal opinion of everyone who had taken part," "but the only decoration Mr. Sassoon received was a decorated shoulder where the bullet went through," and that he had been with the Battalion only a short time but "long enough to win the respect of every man that knew him." Mechanisms: a private's idiom of praise (oath, comparison to an earlier loved officer, wager); the narrator's dry sentence on the lack of a medal, a variant of the decoration sarcasm turned in an officer's favor; the repeated old-hand formula that the good die and the rotten survive (E13b-45). Target: the decoration system, not the officer.
*Candidate application:* The other-ranks verdict on an officer is delivered as a wager and a comparison to a prior officer; the narrator's final line pays him the compliment of wanting him decorated.

**[E13b-67]. The best shell-hole water in France, and the officers' compliments on the tea.** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII, L5202-5217)
At night the men used shell holes as latrines (a bodily matter I summarize) and also drew drinking water from shell holes. On the third night the narrator realizes the water brought by one man came from a hole two of them had used; the man proudly offers to show him "the best shell-hole water in France," which proves to be the same hole; the man, aghast ("Hell, how was I to know?"), says he had also taken a jar of water from it for the officers' cook, who gave him Woodbines and bread for it and told him "the officers had complimented him on the excellent tea." The narrator fills his jar elsewhere, "and the same thing may have happened there for all I knew." Mechanism: a situation joke with a rank-aimed punch line (officers' praise); the narrator's wry last clause. Target: the officers (inadvertently served), with the water carrier as the comic victim.
*Candidate application:* A joke that turns on officers unknowingly benefiting from the men's worst conditions has a stable shape.

**[E13b-68]. The cared-for German cemetery.** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII, L5220-5226)
After relief, Paddy and the narrator visit a German cemetery "beautifully kept" with British dead given graves "equally as well cared for as their own men's," crosses made from the men's identity discs, and a British airman's grave with two propellers as marker. No comment of any kind; the paragraph is set between the shell-hole water story and the attack.
*Candidate application:* The enemy's tidy burial of British dead is reported without a joke and without irony; a sign of the humor's boundary around the war's enemy dead.

**[E13b-69]. The attack that failed and the trench the Germans abandoned.** (PW-07, Ch. XVIII, L5239-5285)
In the second attack, the dead "were laying thick" across the ground and "not a man got further than halfway" (restrained: the original is blunt). A day without food or water, the narrator searches the dead for supplies and finds "a half-loaf of bread, some biscuits and two bottles of water, which I would not have sold for a thousand pounds." The next morning an Argyles soldier who had lain wounded in a shell hole for days limps over from the German position and reports that the Germans had retired in the night; the narrator's dry summary: "Our objective which we had failed to take we now occupied without having a shot fired at us." Mechanism: hyperbole of value; the understated irony of an objective evacuated after costly failure. No jokes at the dead.
*Candidate application:* The coda that the enemy left the position after the failed attacks is a standard irony recorded without comment.

**[E13b-70]. The French wife and the "identification parade": a civilian-facing story with sexual content, restrained.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, "Arras: Hindenburg Line II," L5292-5303)
A story about a French soldier's wife who, before her husband's expected leave arrival, was (the narrator implies) selling sexual favors to British soldiers in the village, then returned in disarray to claim she had been assaulted by soldiers; the narrator says a rumor of an identification parade circulated and that, had it taken place, "she could have identified at least forty men" (restrained summary; I do not reproduce the original's wording of the alleged assault or its price joke). The narrator's moral: "British soldiers often got blamed for things which they were entirely innocent of." Mechanism: a joke at a civilian woman's expense and at the French husband's credulity; a wrong blamed on soldiers. This is the memoir's one sexual-content anecdote in my range and it is aimed at a civilian woman, not at comrades.
*Candidate application:* Civilian accusations against soldiers are answered in the memoir by a joke that discredits the accuser; the pattern's cost is that it also trivializes an alleged assault.

**[E13b-71]. Haig's order on the rifle versus the bomb: the one time the narrator agrees with the brass.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5306-5320)
A message from the Commander-in-Chief is read out criticizing a division that, counter-attacked after a successful attack, relied on bombs rather than the rifle and lost its gains. The narrator writes "The Commander-in-Chief was right" and that "Everybody at this time seemed to be bomb mad." He explains that Mills bombs were useless for stopping attacks (a throw of twenty to thirty yards) and that young soldiers "had been taught more about the bomb than about the rifle." The passage contrasts sharply with E13b-1: the narrator mocks brass for the misplaced order but credits it where it matches his craft knowledge.
*Candidate application:* The contempt for senior command has an internal rule: it is aimed at orders that ignore conditions, not at orders that agree with the soldier's own competence.

**[E13b-72]. A shot at the schoolmaster and the rule of the unloaded rifle; accidents.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5323-5337)
The narrator, taking a captured sniping rifle from the schoolmaster and pointing it down, fires a round he had forgotten was in the breech; the bullet strikes the ground between the man's feet in the tunnel, the candle is blown out, and the narrator does not know whether he has shot him "until someone hurriedly lit it again." He then recalls that he had "witnessed so many accidents" he had taken the habit of pointing muzzles down, citing a man on the Marne who killed two men standing in line with one bullet (restrained: the original is plain). Mechanism: near-miss told in the same level voice as the accident; no joke about the fatality. 
*Candidate application:* Accidents are a category in which the narrator narrates his own error, with no comic framing, and states the habit it created.

**[E13b-73]. Self-inflicted wounds: "wise birds."** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5340-5355)
The narrator says that during the war "there were many cases of self-inflicted wounds and a few suicides in the Battalion" (restrained: no detail on the suicides), and that "Some men would deliberately wound themselves to get out of it," and of their punishment, "I never heard." He describes three men at a listening post who agreed to shoot one another through the forearm during enemy fire; two succeeded and the third, left unwounded because the two could not raise their rifles, "considered his luck was dead out"; and two men in a shell hole who shot each other above the ankle. In both cases "the men left the Battalion honorably wounded; they were wise birds and evidently didn't believe in getting punished after they were shot." Mechanism: wry admiration framed as comment on the army's punishment system (the word "honorably" is the narrator's ironic one); the third man's bad luck as the comic beat. Where the humor stops: the suicides get only a phrase, no joke, and the narrator does not condemn or approve the self-wounders.
*Candidate application:* The ranker narrator's attitude to self-wounding is neutral amusement at the ingenuity and at the army's inability to punish a wounded man, in contrast to his hatred of the shirker who kept safe without wounds (E13b-45). 

**[E13b-74]. The tug-of-war, the bottle of whisky, and Lane's mistake.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5358-5378)
At the First Battalion's visit, the narrator and Owens, the Officers' Mess-Sergeant, make up a tug-of-war team of twelve with ten officers and win. They drink a bottle of whisky by knocking the neck off and pouring it into a cap ("I expect we drunk about one half and wasted the other"), sleep in a cornfield, and are woken by what the narrator thinks is heavy rain: Lane, "half-drunken," had mistaken the two sleepers for a shell hole (a latrine joke that I summarize). The narrator then reasons that Lane "had unwittingly done us a good turn, saving us from a court-martial for desertion": "Another half hour's sleep and we should have been posted absent," as they had to march at once for the attack. Mechanism: a drunken mishap that turns out to be rescue; narrator's use of "unwittingly" for a humiliation that acts as an alarm. The tug-of-war team returns with a darker coda (E13b-78).
*Candidate application:* Drunken misadventure with enlisted men and officers mixed on one team (a sports exception to rank etiquette) is a recurring scene.

**[E13b-75]. Whitsun 1917: the philosophy under shellfire, and what the narrator fears.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5389-5416)
"Whitsun of 1917 was a fine day from the weather point of view, but a damned rotten dirty day from our point of view": half the battalion is lost in the rush across, the Cameronians held up, a barrage three hundred yards deep, lines gone, the Lucas Lamp useless in smoke and dust. The narrator says: "It was only a matter of luck whether we would make a rapid exit into the next world or not." He sorts men by their behavior under shellfire: "Some men were perfect philosophers under heavy shellfire," while others "would cower down, holding their heads in their hands, moaning and trembling." For himself, a shell that lands among them is not feared, "we would never know anything about it"; it is the "large flying pieces" that could take limbs or "rip our bellies open and still leave us living" that he dislikes (restrained paraphrase of the wounds). This restates the preference first stated in E13b-17: instant death welcomed, survivable mutilation dreaded. Mechanism: the weather/point-of-view contrast; "perfect philosophers" as the group's term for the calm.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's classification of men by their conduct under fire is the old hands' informal psychology, with "philosopher" as praise.

**[E13b-76]. The pontoon players in the shell hole: a grouse that saved three lives.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5407-5416)
During the worst of the barrage, two runners and a signaller are in a shell hole by a sandbagged shack playing pontoon, "as if they were in a barrack-room at home." The runners are called away with identical messages (so that if one is hit the other may arrive); the signaller, "doing a good old-soldier's grouse," says "as soon as his luck began to turn the bloody game was broken up." Five minutes later the shack is "blown to bits," and the men inside it; "Undoubtedly the card party would also have been killed if they had still been playing in the shell hole." Mechanism: the grouse as a ritual; the dramatic irony (the interruption he cursed saved him); the narrator states the irony and does not add a joke. The practice of sending identical messages by two runners is also noted.
*Candidate application:* Card games under fire as an assertion of normality; the grumble about luck as a mark of the old hand who grumbles at everything.

**[E13b-77]. "only you, Sergeant Owens and [I] are left": an officer's gallows remark to a signaller.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5419-5438)
By evening the 5th Scottish Rifles' Adjutant has been shot while running a message; only one officer returns from the attacking troops, Captain Radford, who meets the narrator and says: "Well, Richards, only you, Sergeant Owens and [I] are left out of that tug-of-war team of the day before yesterday" (the text prints "J" for "I"; see E13b-74). Dr. Dunn "had been wandering about no-man's-land attending to the wounded"; "How he didn't get riddled was a mystery," and some of the men "were saying that he was fed up with life and was doing his level best to get killed." The narrator closes the day: "It was a disastrous day for all concerned" (the text prints "concemed"), and the attack "had been a complete failure all along the line." Mechanism: an officer uses the sports team, a shared lighthearted event, as the count of the dead; an officer and a private speaking in an informal register after the battle; the narrator reports the rumor about the doctor's motive without endorsing it. Where humor stops: the final sentence is plain.
*Candidate application:* A senior-to-junior gallows remark that uses a recent social event as the ledger is a documented cross-rank intimacy, delivered without a laugh recorded.

**[E13b-78]. The new divinity student: swearing as acculturation, and the arithmetic of replacement.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5452-5462)
Twelve new signallers arrive, most well off; one, "a bit religious," says he had been studying for the ministry. The narrator tells him that if he is alive in four months "his language would be the same as mine"; the student does not believe it but says "every old soldier he had met at home had told him the same thing." The narrator reports: in three months, "which was a month less than I had said," he was the only one left of twelve and "nobody would have thought ... that he had ever studied for the ministry"; his bad language "won universal approval," he became "highly proficient in drinking a bottle of ving blong," and "proved himself a very brave and good soldier as well." He was killed in December on Passchendaele Ridge (the narrator's last line on him). Mechanism: a veteran's prophecy that comes true faster than predicted; swearing and drinking as the unit's rites of acceptance; the casualty ratio (eleven of twelve in three months) stated in a subordinate clause. The narrator's tone: approval.
*Candidate application:* Verbal conversion (a man's language changing) as the marker that a newcomer has been absorbed; the narrator's prophecy, and old soldiers elsewhere making the same prophecy to recruits, are a civilian-facing and front-line ritual alike.

**[E13b-79]. Commissions as an exit, nicknames, and sports.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5465-5478)
The narrator notes that after mid-1917 "it was very easy for a man to get a commission in the infantry," and that many "put in for one simply to get sent home to a Cadet School," hoping the war would end before the training did, while old regular NCOs were commissioned in the field. Lane, "former feather-weight champion of India," wins a boxing match with a Cameronian champion, Newman, "a cockney and a very decent chap"; a new officer nicknamed "the Athlete" wins the running events. Mechanisms: dry reporting of an evasion route without moralizing; a nickname based on the officer's skills; sport as a rank-spanning pastime.
*Candidate application:* Commission-seeking as a way out of the line is described without scorn.

**[E13b-80]. Mustard gas: tall stories, a venereal rumor, and the rumor that came true in part.** (PW-07, Ch. XIX, L5481-5504)
When the Germans first use mustard gas, "all manners of yarns were going around": a transport man, among men "noted for their tall stories," solemnly says the Germans killed everyone in the front line and Back Areas for five miles; the narrator "did a good grin over that one." The "worst yarn of all" was that the gas, even uninhaled, would burn the genitals and leave a man like one in the last stages of venereal disease (restrained paraphrase); "several lectures were given to reassure the men that it was all bunkum." The narrator then reverses: "Later we were to find out for ourselves that mustard-gas was the most deadly sort that was used during the course of the War," describing burning through the new box respirators, temporary blindness and blisters, and a warning that shell holes in a shelled area were not safe for latrine use (restrained summary). He had letters from gas cases who recovered "with no after-effects," but "the men who slightly inhaled and are still living must still be suffering terrible agonies." Mechanism: rumor inflated by transport troops and corrected by officers, then partly vindicated by experience; a sexual-organs rumor. In his closing sentences the narrator's present tense (1933) enters: no joke about the survivors' suffering.
*Candidate application:* A rumor that is mocked and then found to point at a real horror is a documented sequence; the narrator's later-life sentence about permanent suffering is the clearest present-tense sorrow in this part of the book.

### G. Chapter XX (from L5507): Third Ypres, the coast, leave, Polygon Wood

**[E13b-81]. Belgian civilians who left, and the French who "worried less about their lives than about their property."** (PW-07, Ch. XX, "Third Battle of Ypres: Polygon Wood," L5511-5539)
On the Belgian coast the narrator observes that civilians who had lived quietly since 1914 left once British troops arrived and the shelling began, "complaining very bitterly" at the cost; in one pretty seaside village "there hadn't been enough of shells exploded in it to have frightened a poll parrot away," yet no one remained; they were "not such good stickers as the French people who worried less about their lives than about their property." In a short exchange he records that a Canadian officer said it was good "the States came in" as the French "were ready to throw the sponge up," and that two signallers overheard a Staff colonel say the same. Mechanism: contempt for civilians' desertion of the area, contrasted with another people's attachment to property; the overheard Staff remark as rumor. Target: Belgian civilians (partly), the French Army's morale (by report).
*Candidate application:* A soldier's judgment of civilians' staying power is expressed as a comparison of nationalities, with property as the measure.

**[E13b-82]. The prison camp with two prisoners.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5542-5544)
"One of the largest concentration prison camps I ever saw" was built in the area, for "between ten and fifteen thousand prisoners," but "all I saw in it were two solitary prisoners who must have been very lonely in so large a place." Mechanism: bathos at the expense of planners' optimism; the same device as the correspondent who predicted victory (E13b-58).
*Candidate application:* An empty facility prepared for victories that do not arrive is a type of dry joke at planning staff.

**[E13b-83]. Leave in England: the gold watch, and the patriotism that "never extended beyond" smoking concerts.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5547-5560)
The narrator's first leave in eighteen months: he spends two days hiking in mountains, is "presented with a gold watch, in recognition of winning the D.C.M.," of which he says "it has been touch-and-go with it several times since the War" and that without the inscription "I should have parted with it." Every man of military age he met "wanted to shake hands with me and also ask my advice on how to evade military service," or, if forced to go, which corps would keep them from the firing line. The narrator's verdict: they were "wonderfully patriotic at smoking concerts given in honor of soldiers returning from the Front, but their patriotism never extended beyond that." Mechanism: the civilian handshake as hypocrisy; the postwar poverty of the veteran slipped into a subordinate clause (the watch nearly pawned). Target: military-age civilians.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's civilian-facing register is contempt, expressed as a report of what civilians asked him; the memoir also reveals his own postwar hardship in passing.

**[E13b-84]. The man shot through both cheeks: "a nice little dimple."** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5563-5572)
Landing at Boulogne, the narrator meets a man who had been shot through the cheeks at Bois Grenier in April 1915: "If anything, that bullet had improved his appearance," and he "now had a nice little dimple on each side of his face." The man now has a Staff job as a military policeman at the Docks and says "very seriously" that if he had the German's name he would send him "the largest parcel he could pack and a hundred-franc note as well"; he has "one of the smartest fillies in Boulogne." The narrator adds, "I could not help thinking how lucky some men were and how unlucky were others." Mechanism: a facial wound described as a cosmetic benefit, with the wounded man's own gratitude to the shooter; a cushy job as the reward for the wound. The joke is the narrator's, about a man who is cheerful; the closing sentence is a moral summary on luck.
*Candidate application:* The wound that improves a man's fortunes is the old hands' ideal of luck (Blighty wounds, E13b-17); the wounded man's thanks to the German who shot him is a notable inversion.

**[E13b-85]. The air raid and the decoy plane.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5575-5584)
In the camp outside Dunkirk, a German plane held in a searchlight is fired at by every kind of weapon including rifles; "Whilst everyone was busy firing at that one, his friends were busy dropping their bombs on Dunkirk." Dry description of the whole camp's attention being diverted. No comment on the cost.
*Candidate application:* A small joke on the army's collective firing at a decoy; the narrator's tone is mild.

**[E13b-86]. Back at the Battalion: reluctant corporals, Major Poore's "What, what!", and Casson looking for a friend.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5587-5606)
Ricco and Paddy have been made full corporals ("Paddy had taken a lot of persuading before he consented to be made an N.C.O." and was "lucky enough to miss the next show"); the Acting-Colonel is Major Poore, a big man about fifty, slightly deaf, whose "favorite expression was 'What, what!'" (a verbal tic as the nickname-equivalent). A tall young lieutenant, Casson, who spent his leave trying to find news of Mr. Sassoon, is thought by the old hands "a man and a half to spend his leave looking for a pal"; the narrator records "I wrote it down first here as Carson, but an old soldiering pal tells me that I had it wrong" and notes Casson was "said to be a first-class pianist" and "as cool" a soldier. Mechanism: affectionate characterization through a verbal tic and a loyal act; the narrator's own memory-checking via a comrade. The narrator does not mention what had happened to Sassoon (in my range so far).
*Candidate application:* The unit's valuation of an officer through a single act of loyalty to a friend, and the tic that identifies a senior officer, are typical of other-ranks character sketching.

**[E13b-87]. Smokeless fires, and a breakfast "which might be the last we would ever have."** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5609-5620)
A Very-light dump explodes "like witnessing a fireworks display at home." On the morning of 26 September 1917 the narrator and Ricco, "crack hands at making smokeless fires," cut pick-handles into thin strips and cook bacon and tea for themselves and the bank clerk and architect with "no more smoke than a man would have done smoking a cigarette." The narrator adds: "We had at least made sure of our breakfast which might be the last we would ever have." Mechanism: craftsmanship boasting (expertise at smokeless fires), with the last clause stating the gallows reason.
*Candidate application:* A soldier's pride in a small craft that serves the next meal, framed by the possibility that it is the last.

**[E13b-88]. Polygon Wood: the track, the dud shell, and "a full belly."** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5623-5691)
The battalion goes to relieve Australians at Polygon Wood; a bombardment hits them strung out on an eighteen-inch track, "like as if a Giant Hand had suddenly swept them one side" (restrained: half a dozen men fall); "If a man's best pal was wounded he could not stop to dress his wounds for him." In a captured pillbox the narrator finds eighteen dead Germans with no marks, killed by concussion. Captain Mann, the Adjutant who had laughed on the telephone (E13b-49), "fell with a bullet through his head" (restrained); Major Poore and Casson leave the pillbox for a shell hole "safer there than in the pillbox, yet in less than fifteen minutes an howitzer shell had pitched clean in it, killing the both of them." At that strong point, the narrator and Mr. Diamond (an Australian officer with a D.C.M. from Gallipoli) are sharing a tin of Maconochie when a dud shell lands in the trench, killing the man behind the narrator and burying itself beside him. The narrator's account: "Our Maconochie was spoilt but I opened another one"; "If that shell had not been a dud we should have needed no more Maconochies in this world." He had found eight tins in a sandbag, passed six along, but "no one seemed to want them with the exception of the bank clerk and architect who had got into my way of thinking that it was better to enter the next world with a full belly than an empty one." Mechanism: a catastrophe narrated through food (the ruined tin, the replacement), the death behind him left to a clause, and the stated maxim ("a full belly") explicitly acquired by two educated recruits. The passages about the Adjutant, the Major and Casson (whom the narrator had praised in E13b-49 and E13b-86) have no jokes.
*Candidate application:* The old-hand maxim transferred to the new educated soldiers is a good example of a unit's philosophy being taught by example; the mealtime frame lets the narrator describe a near-death without a joke about the dead man.

**[E13b-89]. Dr. Dunn "temporarily resigned" from the Medical Corps.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5695-5726)
After a German plane has machine-gunned the line (the Aid Post Sergeant killed), the narrator writes: "Dr. Dunn temporarily resigned from the Royal Army Medical Corps." The doctor demands a rifle, bayonet and bandolier, refuses the narrator's advice to take a revolver, rallies troops retiring from a position in front of the bank, and "unofficially took command," as only Captain Radford and four platoon officers were left. The narrator carries a verbal message at dusk to the Lewis-gun team of the 5th Scottish Rifles: he slides into a shell hole and lands on a dead German; passes a wounded German "shrieking aloud in agony" ("I could not stop for no wounded man," my paraphrase of the original's blunt phrase); is challenged "in good Glasgow English." Later, he sleeps with the bank clerk in a shell hole that held two dead Germans, "soon fast asleep." Mechanisms: the formal euphemism "temporarily resigned" as the deadpan comic summary of a doctor taking up arms; the Glasgow accent as an identity check that doubles as a joke; the sleep beside the dead as the narrator's flat evidence of numbness. Where humor stops: the wounded German is passed without comment.
*Candidate application:* An administrative phrase for an unofficial heroism ("temporarily resigned") is a model of how institutional language, used by an enlisted narrator, praises a man who breaks the rules for the right reasons.

### H. End of Polygon Wood, the Rouen base, Passchendaele winter, March 1918 (L5736-6435)

**[E13b-90]. Brigadiers who never left Brigade Headquarters; the pillbox that "must have all been having a snooze."** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5740-5758)
After the night, the Australian Brigadier is seen sorting out his men with the first line; the narrator says it was "the only time during the whole of the War that I saw a brigadier with the first line of attacking troops," and that "Some brigadiers that I knew never moved from Brigade Headquarters." He notes "an excellent spirit of comradeship between officers and men" among the Australians and that they were "very brave men." A German pillbox a hundred yards ahead had been silent; "They must have all been having a snooze, otherwise some of us would have been riddled"; when its machine-gun does open up on the narrator, the Major and the Doctor, "We did some wonderful jumping and hopping." Mechanisms: absent-brigadier jab as contrast; self-mocking description of an undignified scramble; mocking the enemy as sleepers. Target: brigadiers.
*Candidate application:* An enlisted man's compliment to the Australians is expressed in terms of officer-man comradeship and a brigadier's presence; both are unusual enough to be recorded.

**[E13b-91]. The Athlete's dug-out and the sergeant: pity for a green officer, and contempt for the sergeant who advised him.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5761-5796 and L5876-5889; Ch. XXI, L5962-6004)
A sapping platoon under The Athlete (the officer nicknamed after winning the sports, E13b-79) is found with its officer and sergeant "down the dug-out" while the men are outside; the Major gives the officer "a severe reprimand," and the narrator writes: "I felt very sorry for The Athlete," it being his first real action and he having "the most windy sergeant in the Battalion with him" (my gloss: "windy" means afraid; the sergeant had narrowly escaped a cowardice charge after an Arras attack). The sergeant sends back for instructions "as he was not quite certain what he had to do," nearly costing the Major his temper; the pillbox surrenders without the platoon's help. Hours later the pair are still in the dug-out. Weeks later, in a village far from the front, The Athlete asks the narrator (a private) if he would mind "going for a walk with him," an irregular request that "might easily have been brought up for a breach of discipline" for an officer; he says "in confidence" that the Colonel read him charges about his conduct and he does not know whether he will be court-martialled. The narrator says that if called as a witness he would do him "more harm than good," because the sergeant "was the most windy man in France and didn't care a damn who else got in the soup as long as he didn't," and The Athlete should never have taken his advice. When the narrator returns, the signallers ask where he has been, and "when I told them I had picked up with a fair young maid they called me a scrounging old hound." Two days later an old soldier on the transport asks whether The Athlete "would be stuck against the wall and shot"; he was not court-martialled and later won the Military Cross; the narrator says that if the sergeant had had his deserts he "should have been given a couple of severe reprimands and then put against the wall and shot" (restrained; execution language), and that a young officer will naturally look to an experienced sergeant for guidance. Mechanisms: the narrator's sympathy for an officer whose fault is bad counsel; confidence kept by a cover story that draws on the unit's bawdy teasing; the transport's gossip network. Where humor stops: the question of court-martial and execution is stated plainly.
*Candidate application:* The ranker who keeps an officer's secret behind a bawdy cover story and who assigns the blame to the NCO adviser is a recurring cross-rank loyalty pattern. The cover story works because the unit's teasing is predictable.

**[E13b-92]. The grieving escort who killed his prisoners: restrained.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5800-5817)
A soldier escorting six German prisoners to the casualty station tells the narrator that about an hour earlier he had lost "the best pal I ever had" and that he was not going to take the prisoners far; later he returns and says in effect that he has carried out what he said he would. He is killed by a shell splinter within twenty yards of the narrator. The narrator comments that he had often heard comrades say they had "done their prisoners in" on the way back but that this "was the only case I could vouch for," and that "no doubt the loss of his pal had upset him very much." (Restrained: I do not reproduce the man's wording or the method.) No joke or euphemism is attached by the narrator; the euphemism lies in the men's own talk about their prisoners.
*Candidate application:* Boasting about killing prisoners is described as a commonplace of talk, while the narrator separates talk from the one verified case and gives grief as its explanation; no humor.

**[E13b-93]. The runner's day: "nothing out of the ordinary," and the Blighty wound still hoped for.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5820-5863)
Carrying a message to a company, the narrator sees a German "shamming death" pop up behind the line of men who had passed him; he fires first and "made sure he was dead before I left him" (restrained summary). The narrator's comment: "This little affair was nothing out of the ordinary in a runner's work when in attacks." He delivers the message only three minutes before the advance; a third of the company are casualties in the time behind the bank. Dr. Dunn "seemed to be temporarily back in the R.A.M.C." (echoing E13b-89). A spent splinter hits the narrator's calf: "I thought at the time that someone had hit me with a coal hammer"; had it not been spent it would have "given me a beautiful blighty wound, which I don't mind admitting I was still hoping for." Mechanism: the narrator's flat judgment on killing as routine; the honest confession of a three-years-late wish to be sent home. Where humor stops: the killing and the casualty ratio.
*Candidate application:* An old soldier's admission, after three years and a medal, that he still hopes for a Blighty wound is a candid statement that the formula (E13b-17) is not just banter.

**[E13b-94]. Food from the dead ration party, and the "forefathers" remark.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5866-5873)
Ricco, returning from a run to Brigade, finds the ration party of another battalion "all been killed" and brings back "a lovely sandbag full of officers' rations": tinned goods and three loaves. The narrator observes of the bank clerk and architect, who eat a tin of cooked sausages with relish, that he wondered whether their forefathers "had not been pure-bred Germans" (a mild national-stereotype joke that I summarize). He adds: "The officers who the bag of rations were intended for could never have enjoyed them better than we did." Mechanism: the same displacement as E13b-48 (the joke is at the intended eaters' expense, not the dead carriers'); a trivial ethnic quip aimed at comrades. Target: the officers, then the educated recruits' appetite.
*Candidate application:* A reliable comic beat of the memoir: food seized from dead carriers is enjoyed more than the officers would have enjoyed it.

**[E13b-95]. "Come back, you bloody fools!": the Australians' warning, the Major's wound and "a pretty tune."** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5892-5906)
The narrator accompanies Major Kearsley toward the new positions; when they cross the Australian line the Australians shout "Come back, you bloody fools!" and warn that the Germans have everything covered by machine-gun fire; "We took no notice." A machine-gun opens and the Major is shot through the leg above the ankle; the narrator dresses it; they discuss waiting until dusk, but the Major says he must hand over command, so they hop back by a zig-zag course; the narrator says "the machine-gun had been playing a pretty tune behind us." Mechanism: a comradely insult as a safety warning that is ignored; a musical metaphor for machine-gun fire.
*Candidate application:* An ally's rude warning, ignored from duty, is a way the memoir shows the Australians as peers.

**[E13b-96]. The track to the clearing station, and the irony of the nice Blighty wounds.** (PW-07, Ch. XX, L5909-5947)
Command is handed to Captain Radford, who says he "would much prefer the Doctor taking command"; the Major says that cannot be as the Doctor is a non-combatant (the narrator's earlier "temporarily resigned" joke, E13b-89, is thus given its legal counterpart). The track to Clapham Junction casualty station is lined with stretcher-bearers and bandaged men who had been killed on it, and the narrator writes: "Many men who had received what they thought were nice blighty wounds had been killed along this track" (restrained). He also counts "over twenty" tanks out of action; Mr. Diamond, hit through the arm, says "Hello. I'm glad to see you alive." The narrator tells the Major not to "take too much notice of the Doctor," who "would have made a better general than a doctor," that he has seen worse bullet wounds heal in a fortnight, and wishes him back within two months. On returning, the narrator learns his friends "thought I had gone West and were as delighted to see me as I was them." Mechanism: the formula (the lucky wound) shown to fail; the narrator's reassurance to the Major mixed with a joke about the Doctor (a joke about someone he admires). 
*Candidate application:* The luck formula's limit: a "nice" wound on a shelled track is lethal; the narrator writes that without comment.

**[E13b-97]. The old soldier at the field hospital: "as fat as a water-buffalo," combed out and killed seven weeks later.** (PW-07, Ch. XXI, "Five Weeks at Rouen Base Camp," L6016-6031)
Sent down for an operation, the narrator meets a former battalion man who had left on the 1914 retirement and since then "been on some excellent jobs down at the Bases"; he is "well away with the doctors and nurses, who thought the world of him as an orderly"; he says that "if he could avoid it he was not going a yard further" and he had "seen enough of the poor wounded devils" from there. The narrator adds: "He was as fat as a water-buffalo," and "Seven weeks later he had been combed out, rejoined the Battalion, and killed." Mechanism: the deadpan chain "combed out, rejoined the Battalion, and killed," the same shape as Freezer's refusal and promotion (E13b-29). The narrator's attitude to this man is not hostile.
*Candidate application:* A one-sentence irony delivered as a chronology and not as comment; the base-job holdout did not escape.

**[E13b-98]. The Rouen sergeant and the narrator's cursing "up hill and down."** (PW-07, Ch. XXI, L6034-6057)
Walking to the hospital near the "Bull Ring" (the base training camp), the narrator asks a sergeant the way; "As soon as he spoke I knew he had never seen the sky over a front-line trench." The sergeant, taking the narrator (whose ribbons are hidden by the overcoat) for a young soldier, scolds him for not remembering his way and demands to be addressed by his rank. The narrator "exploded": when he arrived at Rouen "there was not enough of tent-canvas in the place to make a patch for a sore eye, and not a hut large enough to hold a trench-louse"; he refuses to say "Sergeant"; "I could have been a full sergeant when he was sucking milk at his mother's breast"; he "cursed him up hill and down, using Hindustani and French and other languages until I was blue in the nose," and the sergeant "made no response and cleared off." The narrator adds that many at the Base held good jobs "and were supposed to be unfit for the front line," and that "a few weeks in a front-line trench ... would have done them the world of good." Mechanisms: rank etiquette reversed by service seniority; polyglot cursing as accomplishment; hyperbolic insults about camp conditions; the old-soldier persona overriding rank. Target: base-camp NCOs.
*Candidate application:* The line-versus-base contempt, and the veteran's license to curse a sergeant who has not served, are among the most consistent lines in the memoir. The hidden ribbons mark that the license is invisible and must be asserted verbally.

**[E13b-99]. The doctor's board: "no good for an old soldier to be ill."** (PW-07, Ch. XXI, L6060-6094)
At Rouen a doctor who has "never been no further than Rouen" asks where the narrator won the D.C.M. and how long he has been in France without picking up his chart; on learning, he exclaims "They want men like you back in the front line" and sends him for three weeks' rest; he sends a rosy-cheeked three-month soldier with a boil to the large hospital ("Poor boy"); four old soldiers of the twenty-eight sick men are sent to rest. The narrator's conclusion: "it was no good for an old soldier to be ill: his place was the front line where he must stay until he got killed or wounded." The convalescent camp ("known by a map reference") held "between six and seven thousand men in it, and the majority were swinging the lead," and one young man "looked strong enough to carry an eighteen-pounder gun over his shoulder." Mechanism: the narrator's bitter generalization from a doctor's inattention; hyperbole about the malingerer's strength. Target: base doctors and the malingerers.
*Candidate application:* The old soldier's complaint is that the army's care system gives sympathy to the young and replaces the old with duty; the rule is stated as a law of the system.

**[E13b-100]. A Military Medal the narrator does not feel he earned, and the Doctor's missing Victoria Cross.** (PW-07, Ch. XXI, L6097-6106)
Ricco's letter reports that Kearsley has a bar to his D.S.O., Radford and Dr. Dunn the D.S.O., and "The bank clerk and I had been awarded the Military Medal." The narrator says "I had did no more than the other men," would have preferred it go to the architect or Sealyham, and "thought the Doctor would have been awarded the Victoria Cross," since in addition to taking charge, which "could not be officially rewarded," he had been "under continual shell and machine-gun fire." The narrator: "the coolest man under fire that ever stepped foot in France." Mechanism: decoration cynicism turned into advocacy; the narrator's refusal of his own credit.
*Candidate application:* When a man receives a medal he regards as arbitrary (the unit's old formula that it ranks below the D.C.M.), his reaction is to argue for others.

**[E13b-101]. Dr. Dunn on "the treatment I had received down the Base."** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, "Bois Grenier Again," L6113-6123)
Back in the huts, the Doctor asks if he is better; the narrator says he was "delighted with the treatment I had received down the Base": "a three weeks rest, a new suit of khaki, a new pair of boots and sent back up the line." The Doctor "did a good grin" and said the new clothes "would come in very handy as the Battalion was going in the line the following evening to hold the line in front of Passchendaele." Mechanism: a sarcastic report to a sympathetic officer who answers with a gallows rejoinder; a shared irony between a private and an officer.
*Candidate application:* The officer-man exchange where both see the system's logic and neither is surprised is an intimacy between the doctor and the narrator throughout.

**[E13b-102]. The Divisional General's "brainwave" and "dead men to borrow them from."** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6126-6145)
After Third Ypres, "Our Divisional General had another brainwave": the battalion was ordered into the line without puttees because it had been snowing. The narrator describes the duckboard track to Passchendaele Ridge (constantly shelled) and the dead lying everywhere (restrained: the narrator says men trampled bodies at night and "hundreds of men had received their burial in this way" in mud). Then: "Twenty-four hours after we were in the line the majority of the Battalion were wearing puttees," and those without them "found plenty of dead men to borrow them from." Mechanism: "brainwave" as sarcasm for a staff order; "borrow" as euphemism for taking from the dead; a stable bit of gallows pragmatism. Target: the Divisional General (the same type as the Corps Commander of E13b-1).
*Candidate application:* The euphemism "borrow" for taking from the dead is the unit's moral solution to a ridiculous order; the order is the target, not the dead.

**[E13b-103]. Sergeant Owens, who turned back to ask the Colonel something.** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6148-6162)
Owens (the Officers' Mess-Sergeant of the tug-of-war, E13b-74) wishes Lane and the narrator "the best of luck," walks halfway to the mess cart, returns saying he forgot to ask the Colonel something, and on the way back is killed instantly by a shell six yards from him. The narrator: "The job that he had, a man could have taken an insurance policy on his life; if he had not forgot something he might have still been knocking about this ball of clay." "He had did me many a good turn ... it was rotten luck the way he had gone West." Mechanism: a bitter irony about a safe job and a forgotten question; the idiom "ball of clay" for earth; "gone West." No joke at the dead man's expense.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's account of a "safe" man's death is built on a small contingency (a forgotten question), the shape the old hands treat as proof of fate.

**[E13b-104]. Chronic pain stated once: "from now on I suffered in silence."** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6175-6178; also Ch. XXI, L6007-6019)
The narrator's complaint (a chronic lower-body ailment that the Brigade doctor had said would trouble him especially on marches and that needed an operation; I summarize the bodily detail) returns "as bad as ever" after a few days at Passchendaele: "from now on I suffered in silence." He was fine resting, but "when in the line if it was very muddy, and on long marches, I used to go through some agony." No joke attaches. The statement of silent endurance sits next to the earlier account of the doctor who would not hear it (E13b-99).
*Candidate application:* Of use for the defector's chronic injury: an enlisted narrator announces the condition once, plainly, then states that he stopped mentioning it.

**[E13b-105]. Christmas: "the two C.O.s tossed for it, and ours won."** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6165-6172 and L6181-6188)
At Poperinghe, "Either we or the Cameronians had to go up the line as a working party on Christmas day, so the two C.O.s tossed for it, and ours won" (the word "won" being used for the unwelcome result); the narrator reports "a good Christmas dinner and also a good drink," with the cafés doing "a roaring trade" despite occasional shelling. The bank clerk is invited by a gunner who is a bank inspector in the same firm, with an officer of the same battery a bank clerk in the same firm; the narrator found it "very funny" and "wondered what the gunner's feelings would be if his officer told him to smarten himself up." Mechanism: deadpan ironic "won" for a chore; civil-rank/army-rank inversion as comic situation.
*Candidate application:* Army hierarchy that inverts civilian hierarchy is a source of quiet comedy in a citizen army.

**[E13b-106]. Sassoon's telegram, and the narrator's silence on what came before.** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6191-6203)
Working at Battalion Headquarters in the Ypres area, the narrator receives a message from Brigade "which had come from England": a telegram to Major Kearsley and the Royal Welch Fusiliers, signed Sassoon, saying "Am ordered to Egypt can you do something to get me back to France." The narrator hands it to the Major "wishing he could do something in the matter, as it was only once in a blue moon that we had an officer like Mr. Sassoon"; the Major "was unable." Sassoon was in Palestine for a time with a dismounted yeomanry regiment "made into the Twenty-fifth Battalion of the Royal Welch," then sent to France and "soon back in England, wounded in the head." The narrator gives no hint, in my range, that he knows of Sassoon's 1917 protest or anything else that distinguished his departure. (A reader of the Sassoon extraction can place the telegram against the officer-poet's own account; I make no claim about it.)
*Candidate application:* An enlisted man's appreciation of an officer is measured by whether that officer wants to return to the line and the narrator's wish that he could be returned; the other-ranks view of this officer is as a good fighting officer.

**[E13b-107]. Rumor of Kearsley's death, and the broken leg.** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6206-6211)
Major Kearsley rides through frozen Ypres, his horse slips, he is thrown and breaks his leg the day before leaving to command a service battalion; the narrator "thought his luck was dead out." Years later, the narrator heard he had been "knocked over in the last month of the War, but I am glad to say that the rumor has proved false"; "He is alive and kicking." Mechanism: accidents after combat survival as the standard "dead out" luck; a rumor of death that reverses.
*Candidate application:* The memoir uses "dead out" for ill luck of any sort, including an accident, and "alive and kicking" for an erroneously reported death.

**[E13b-108]. Unfit drafts, "old Buffalo Bill," and the doctors who "had gone seriously insane."** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6214-6248)
In January 1918 the Brigade is reorganized; the drafts from home are "a sorry looking lot of men" who "got worse" over six months: "The doctors that had passed some of them Al cannot have had tender consciences." One man had a double rupture and was sent to hospital; a C3 lorry driver from London was passed A1 by a doctor who walked along ranks of naked men "without hardly looking at one of them" and sent to France "hardly strong enough to carry his rifle." The narrator sets this beside the "fat greasy oily men" at the Base and concludes that "some of the doctors had gone seriously insane," and imagines what "old Buffalo Bill" (per the companion pass E13a's inventory, the platoon's nickname from Part 1 for a revolver-waving company commander, which explains the revolver joke) would have done commanding a company of such men: he "would have had a half dozen revolvers in his belt which he would have been pulling out, three at a time in each hand" and, if he had not shot them in a couple of days, "it would have been because he had dropped dead from exhaustion." Then Tich, watching the Bantam Battalion drill, says the battalion "was coming to something when little men like that were sent to us"; every man they watched was a good inch taller than Tich, who "imagined he was as big as a Prussian Guard." The narrator adds: "the Bantams turned out all right." Mechanisms: a comic image of a violent officer forced by weak men; self-blind joke on Tich; the narrator's repair of the joke at the end ("good soldiers in action"). Targets: the medical boards; the unfit recruits (with sympathy: "These men could not help their physical disabilities").
*Candidate application:* The ranker's contempt is directed at the doctors, not the weak recruits; the joke about Tich's size is affectionate and corrected at once by the soldiers' later performance.

**[E13b-109]. The Divisional General's farewell and "the old bun-punching wallah had docked our rum."** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6251-6257)
The Divisional General (E13b-55) inspects the battalion before it is transferred to the 38th (Welsh) Division, says that "when the history of the Division was written we would be very prominent in it," and after dismissal Ricco remarks "that the most prominent thing in that history should be about the way the old bun-punching wallah had docked our rum." The narrator: "I am told that the history did get written some years later but this important item was omitted." Mechanism: the rum grievance as the true history; the narrator's final dry comment on the omission; "wallah" (my gloss: an Anglo-Indian suffix for a person associated with a thing; the same formation as "char wallah," a teetotaller, in the companion pass E13a) with "bun-punching" (see E13b-45, E13b-55), so that the general is a teetotal crank who took away the rum. Target: the divisional general.
*Candidate application:* The unit's counter-history (what the history should have said) is a stable genre of its own; the rum grievance outlives the commander's posting.

**[E13b-110]. The "Pork and Beans" and the rumor about the Portuguese.** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6260-6273; and Ch. XXIII [printed "XXII"], L6365-6369)
In the Laventie sector the Portuguese, "whom we called the Pork and Beans," look "a ragtime lot"; a yarn goes round that when they were in the front line the Germans would come over, take their rations, and humiliate them in a bodily way, telling them they were not worth taking back as prisoners (restrained summary of a mocking rumor). After the March 1918 offensive the narrator relays that "the way the Portuguese ran, the best runners that ever lived would never have caught them." Mechanism: a nickname for allied troops (the text does not gloss it; my gloss: from rations), a rumor, and a hyperbole of flight. Note period national caricature; the narrator does not report any Portuguese soldier speaking.
*Candidate application:* The unit's contempt for an ally is expressed through an invented story about the enemy's treatment of them and then confirmed by hearsay of their flight; the joke has no first-hand evidence.

**[E13b-111]. Back to the Bois Grenier cemetery: the crosses made "a bit respectable," the vanished wine-seller.** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6276-6286)
After nearly three years, Paddy and the narrator visit Bois Grenier cemetery: "I had a job to find the graves of Stevens and others I knew," the crosses nearly hidden by grass, nettles and poppies, and "We made them a bit respectable before we left." The tall red-brick house stands, "the ladies had left," the farmer is still tilling; "The ving blong man had vanished," having "either been killed or cleared out with a fortune." No joke accompanies the graves; the dry guess about the wine-seller (a figure from the memoir's earlier chapters) is the only comic touch.
*Candidate application:* The old soldier's tenderness toward comrades' graves is carried out by tidying and stated with the plainest verbs; the wry guess about a civilian's fate is permitted nearby but not at the graves.

**[E13b-112]. "A man with ordinary luck should live for ever around here": a reassurance and its answer.** (PW-07, Ch. XXII, L6289-6314)
Marching up one night, the narrator talks to a married man just back from leave, who left home forty-eight hours earlier; the narrator says "this was a quiet part of the Front and a man with ordinary luck should live for ever around here." "Almost as I said these words" a single shell bursts at their right and a piece of it kills the man at once (restrained). A comrade writes to the wife; she replies that "some terrible mistake had been made" because he told her he would spend two or three nights at the rest camp; another letter says she has been officially notified but "still believed that a mistake must have been made." The narrator then explains why a pal's letter may arrive before the War Office's: casualty returns go in at nine every morning, so a man killed at 9:15 is reported the next day, and "sometimes it was impossible to tell whether a man was killed, wounded, or missing." Mechanism: the narrator's own reassuring hyperbole immediately answered; the wife's refusal to believe. He offers no comment on his own remark. Where humor stops: the entire episode.
*Candidate application:* The single worst case of tempting fate in the memoir is his own; the narrator records it without self-reproach and moves to the paperwork explanation.

### I. Chapter XXIII (printed "XXII"; L6317): the German March offensive and after

**[E13b-113]. The narrator's reply on whether the Germans would break through.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, "The German March Offensive," L6321-6330)
The "young signalling officer" asks whether the Germans would break through; the narrator replies that "if every man made full use of his rifle" they would find it difficult, "but that the British Army was not like it used to be and anything might happen." No joke. A statement of his belief that the army has changed.
*Candidate application:* The old regular's conviction that the army had declined in quality is stated to an officer in blunt terms and without a joke.

**[E13b-114]. Ricco's regular commission, and his teasing parting promise.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6333-6340 and L6350-6359)
Ricco, "being a regular soldier," puts in for a regular commission (more money and kit grants than a temporary one) and urges Paddy and the narrator to do the same, "but we would have nothing to do with it"; he is first told he can have only a temporary one and refuses it; later he is granted a regular commission and says he "would be thinking of us when he was laying in his nice comfortable warm bed at home whilst we were laying in a shell hole or a wet and muddy trench." The narrator calls him "an extraordinary man in many ways and as tough as steel": shot through the chest at Loos, back in under five months "as chirpy as a cricket," and wrongly given only the Military Medal ("a rotten shame he was not awarded the D.C.M."). Mechanism: the departing friend's mock-cruel promise as affectionate teasing; the narrator's pointed refusal of the same route. Target: self and comrades; the advantage of the commissioned.
*Candidate application:* A comrade who leaves for a safer commission is allowed to tease those left behind, because the teasing is understood as the other way of saying he will miss them.

**[E13b-115]. The transport's "private line" to Haig's office; the rumor that proved partly true.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6375-6379; compare Ch. XXI, L5987-5993)
Earlier the narrator had said transport men "knew everything that was happening on the Western Front" and that the Old Soldier used to say they had "a private telephone line to the Commander-in-Chief's bedroom." In April 1918 the transport's "private line to Sir Douglas Haig's office" delivers the story that the Germans had captured more than a hundred thousand troops and fifty million pounds' worth of goods in canteens; the narrator: "I found out later that there was more truth in what he had told me than what I gave him credit for." Mechanism: the transport's gossip as a comic institution, and a reversal in which the gossip is partly right (compare E13b-80).
*Candidate application:* The unit's recognized rumor channel, whose reliability is the running joke, is validated by events; the narrator concedes it with the same understatement he uses elsewhere.

**[E13b-116]. The leaning Virgin finally falls.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6380-6390)
The Germans hold Albert and the "upside-down statue on the ruined church was still hanging." Bombing planes and artillery shell the town daily, "but the statue seemed to be bearing a charmed existence." One morning, after a big shell, "the statue was missing." Newspapers said the Germans "had wantonly destroyed it"; the narrator says that was "believed by the people that read them," and adds from "good authority" that if they had they "had a good right to it," because it "was made in Germany by a German firm and it was neither old nor valuable." Mechanism: a debunking of press atrocity propaganda by a man who watched the shell; the superstition from E13b-33 (the war would end when it fell) is not mentioned and the war continued; the narrator does not point out the irony. Target: newspapers and civilian readers.
*Candidate application:* Civilian myth, wartime press and soldier's knowledge differ on the same object; the soldier's version is a correction delivered flat.

**[E13b-117]. "Merry and Bright" and a better division.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6390-6395)
In the 38th Division rum and bread are better; the Brigadier "often used to visit the troops in the front line," and "was called 'Merry and Bright'." The narrator adds that "He was a decent old stick" and that he "must have belonged to a different kind of species than the brigadiers I had come in contact with during the last three years." There is also "an excellent Divisional Concert Troupe." Mechanism: a nickname with an affectionate-ironic edge; the zoological joke ("different kind of species") on brigadiers who stay away from the line (E13b-90). Target: brigadiers in general.
*Candidate application:* A brigadier who goes to the line gets a nickname that is a compliment; the narrator's contempt for brigadiers in general stays intact.

**[E13b-118]. Prisoners as shields, and the machine-gun that waited.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6398-6408)
Escorting lightly wounded prisoners through mist, the narrator and another man carry one who collapses ("only a slip of a lad"); the mist rises and nothing happens until they are safe in a sunken road, when a machine-gun traverses the ground they had crossed: "which was as good as telling us that we had been seen and would have been riddled if we had not had prisoners with us." No joke; the narrator reports the enemy's restraint as a deduction.
*Candidate application:* The enemy's sparing of a party that carried its own wounded is recorded as a fact to reflect on, and not commented on.

**[E13b-119]. The cellar fumes: "Fate played a dirty trick."** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6411-6432)
Dr. Dunn's servant and an Aid Post orderly are found dead in a cellar after sleeping with a charcoal-and-coke fire; the Aid Post Sergeant, who slept near the steps, survives, goes to hospital, rejoins two months later, and is killed shelling the transport town "when entering a house trying to rescue some wounded men." The narrator: "He was a South African veteran and Fate played a dirty trick with him." He adds a report that French soldiers "fed up with the War" used the same means as a favorite method of suicide (restrained: I summarize without the method's wording). Mechanism: accident narrated with grim irony in the narrator's idiom for fate; the suicide remark as a mere hearsay aside. 
*Candidate application:* The narrator uses "Fate played a dirty trick" for a man who survives one death and meets another; the memoir's ironies have this shape repeatedly (E13b-97, E13b-103).

### J. Summer 1918 to the first days of November (L6435-7134): Freezer's end, leave, Minimum Reserve, the last advance

**[E13b-120]. Sergeant Freezer at the pay table, and the drill instructor who was "jumpy as a bag of fleas."** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6435-6460)
Freezer (the old hand who "would either be a sergeant or nothing," E13b-29), slightly drunk, asks the young platoon officer paying out for an advance of pay; he is "over £40 in credit"; refused and told he should be satisfied with the same pay as other sergeants, he replies that he was "more pounds in credit than the majority of the young sergeants were shillings," which the narrator says was "perfectly true." Ordered from the table, Freezer seizes a camp dixie of dirty slops, throws them over the officer, rubs the dixie down his coat and curses him "until he was pink." He is court-martialled, reduced to the ranks and sentenced to two years' imprisonment, "one of the few cases who were sent away to do their imprisonment." The narrator's verdict is not condemnation: Freezer's drill "may have not been up to the standard of a drill sergeant but he was highly skilled in the real arts of war," officers "were never too proud to accept his advice then" and "the men under him would follow him anywhere." In the next paragraph a first-class drill instructor who in action "was no wiser than a newborn babe and as jumpy as a bag of fleas" is treated by the men "with contempt"; after missing two attacks by going sick, he did not miss the third "and he was the first to pass into the next world." Mechanisms: a comic assault on an officer (farce with kitchen slops) reported as plain fact, with a long qualification in the man's favor; the drill-instructor's story deployed with a sequential deadpan. Target: the young officer (mild) and the parade-ground-only NCO (hard).
*Candidate application:* A veteran who strikes an officer is punished fully by the army and still honored by the ranks; the memoir sets "real arts of war" against drill as the unit's measure of an NCO.

**[E13b-121]. "I have still got some sense left": the water pump, and the bank clerk's laughing consolation.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6472-6523)
In the shelled village of Bouzicourt, when a man tells the narrator and the bank clerk the best water is at a farm yard opposite the church where many men gather, the narrator says they will look elsewhere; asked if he has "the wind up," he answers "Not exactly" and "But I have still got some sense left." He reasons that the church, though useless for observation, is "an excellent guide" for German observers. About half an hour later a large shell lands in the yard, "killing or wounding twenty men who were waiting their turn to use the pump." Later, on leave in Wales, he visits the bank clerk and four runners in a Cardiff hospital, gas cases from mustard gas that fell two hours after he left; "When I told him that he had met a far better fate than what he would have done if he had gone to that pump for water, he laughed and agreed with me." Mechanism: the retort to the "wind up" jibe is understated and later proves right; the joke of comparative luck is addressed to a gas casualty and he accepts it. The narrator notes of the men: "Their eyes looked very bad but they could see all right" and that the bank clerk had "become a pukka old soldier in action."
*Candidate application:* Comparative-luck consolation (you are better off than the man who stood at the pump) works as a joke between friends because it is both true and kind.

**[E13b-122]. Leave trains: "more trembling than ever they did when in the front line."** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6493-6504)
On the way to leave, the narrator is warned during the night; the railhead is shelled by long-range guns as the train waits ("about twenty yards from the train"), and at Abbeville enemy planes bomb the town. He writes: "During the course of the War I expect thousands were killed going and returning from leave," and men waiting for leave trains at the constantly shelled Poperinghe railhead "used to do more trembling than ever they did when in the front line." Mechanism: the plain assertion that fear peaks when safety is in sight. Not a joke; one of the few explicit statements of fear in the memoir that names men "trembling" without scorn.
*Candidate application:* Fear is strongest on the threshold of leave; the memoir states this as general knowledge and with no contempt for the men concerned (contrast E13b-25).

**[E13b-123]. Civilians at home: the exempted, the queue complainers, and the speech.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIII, L6526-6551)
On leave in his mining valley the narrator reports that many "publicans, business men and outdoor workers" too old for active service but young enough for home service "rushed to the mines" to be exempted by "Work of National Importance" and "were too windy even to join their own town guard." A few were exempted at "umpteen tribunals" until the tribunals were ashamed, and "only one of them, to my knowledge, was ever sent out on active service," reaching the front line and "got one of his boots wet" as the Armistice was proclaimed: "He won his medal cheap." People complaining of food queues are told that he did not know what they would have said "if they had to live on four army biscuits and a spoonful of jam a day," and "I expect they would have ordered their coffins right away." A concert is given in honor of his D.C.M. and M.M., with a "big Pow-Wow" under a councillor who was a schoolmaster he admired, and he made a small speech and "never felt so windy in the whole of my life before." Mechanisms: sarcasm at exemption seekers; a retort to complainers delivered in reported form; a self-deprecating joke that the bravest man in the room was most afraid of a speech. Targets: civilian exemption-seekers and complainers.
*Candidate application:* The veteran's civilian-facing register has three parts here: contempt for evaders, pointed comparison for the complainers, and comic confession of stage fright; the last is the only one that is against himself.

**[E13b-124]. Dr. Dunn's departure, and a toast.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, "Minimum Reserve," L6558-6565)
On rejoining, the narrator learns the Doctor has left as a gas casualty after "hung on until he could hardly see, and then had to be led away"; "everyone that knew him said that we would never see his equal again." Years later he heard Dunn was alive and practicing in Glasgow: "Here's good luck to Dr. Dunn!" No joke or nickname; a toast replaces one.
*Candidate application:* The memoir ends several of its comrade stories with a toast addressed in the present tense, which is how the narrator's present voice enters and steps away from humor.

**[E13b-125]. The stretcher that seemed "a paperweight," the young man who ducked, and the death by the fall.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6579-6608)
After a failed raid the narrator, Paddy, two young soldiers and a "young frail Wesleyan Minister" (who carries the rifles) carry a big wounded man (over fourteen stone) on a stretcher across open ground at first light. The young men insist on resting; Paddy and the narrator "began to swear"; when a rifle shot hits the dirt, "the rate we were now walking anyone would have thought we were carrying a paperweight." At fifty yards from their trench a machine-gun opens; one youngster "pulled his hand off the handle and ducked," the stretcher tilts, and the wounded man falls; the youngster says he stumbled; Paddy says "You bloody liar" and that for two pins he would shoot him (restrained: the threat's wording). At the Aid Post the youngster "disappeared very quickly," and the wounded man died: "the fall had undoubtedly been the finish of him." Mechanisms: the paperweight joke is a comic image of fear's effect on speed; the humor stops at the fall; the narrator and Paddy hold the youngster responsible without a joke. 
*Candidate application:* A moment where the group's comic light touch cannot survive a death caused by another's fear; no one jokes and the youngster vanishes.

**[E13b-126]. "Sausages": the balloon raid and the thrilled youngsters.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6618-6648)
Two miles behind the line the narrator tells young signallers to watch "our observation balloons, which we called 'sausages'" (the text's gloss: balloon nickname) because a plane may well swoop and set one afire before noon; after half an hour a man jumps with a parachute and an enemy plane strikes the balloon with tracer bullets, then repeats five more times in a little over half an hour while "never" firing on the parachutists; then a British plane chases him off, and "I think the German got away with it." The narrator: "The young signallers said it had been very thrilling," and "I don't expect they would have said it was thrilling if they had been the occupants of the sausages." He adds an earlier episode in July 1916 in which a German airman hovering over a wounded British airman held fire until the wounded man landed. Mechanism: an experienced predictor of a spectacle and a dry deflation of the young spectators' excitement by naming the victims' position; note the enemy's sparing of parachutists. Target: the young signallers' thrill.
*Candidate application:* The veteran's habit of turning a spectator's thrill into a reminder of the participants' view.

**[E13b-127]. Americans, and a band "enough to make an owl laugh."** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6651-6660)
On a night out the soldiers meet American troops in a café, "a fine lot of men" and "very sociable," and at the Royal Air Force sports an American band's ragtime playing draws attention away from the races, with "funny capers" that were "enough to make an owl laugh." The narrator enjoyed the evening and says men promised to meet again.
*Candidate application:* The Americans in this memoir are enjoyed as a source of comedy and company, and the humor is appreciative, not aimed.

**[E13b-128]. Radford's farewell, "torture" from signallers' dug-outs, and the Croix de Guerre for twopence.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6663-6680)
All officers with two and a half years' continuous front-line service could go home; few remained; Captain Moodie (an Artists' Rifles man, "respected" by D Company's old soldiers) goes to Brigade, and Captain Radford says goodbye to Paddy and the narrator, saying that what he could not forget was "the smoky dug-outs of us old signallers around the Givenchy area," which had given him "more torture than anything he had been through." The narrator adds: "There was no two and a half years' rule about N.C.O.s or men, of course." After Polygon Wood Haig decorated Radford with a French Croix de Guerre; the Captain "wanted to sell it for twopence," and "To pukka soldiers those Croix de Guerres were jokes"; the narrator adds that even gendarmes in Paris were being awarded them. Mechanisms: an officer's affectionate joke at the other ranks' dug-outs as a farewell, and the narrator's deadpan "of course" on the rank-based exit rule; a decoration treated as comic currency. Target: the foreign medal and the unequal exit rule.
*Candidate application:* The officer who leaves tells the men what he will miss in terms of what annoyed him; the narrator notes bitterly that the same rule does not apply to other ranks.

**[E13b-129]. Minimum Reserve: the shirker with three columns in the paper, and saluting drill by numbers.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6683-6699)
Sent to Minimum Reserve for the first time, the narrator describes a signaller who had been there four times and "never taken part in any attack, always going sick just beforehand"; "That man swung the lead to some purpose": when he went on leave "there were three columns in his local paper giving his war record and the wonderful escapes he had had." When Minimum Reserve was abolished, "he was killed in the first attack he had been in." At camp the signallers drill with company men; the first morning is "saluting drill by numbers," with N.C.O.s standing in for officers, and when the narrator salutes Paddy "we both nearly burst out laughing." By afternoon he was "that fed up that I was wishing myself back in the line." Mechanism: the swung-lead man's civilian fame and his death told in two sentences; the comic absurdity of saluting an NCO who is his closest friend; the line's relative preferability. Target: Minimum Reserve drill, the evader and the paper that praised him.
*Candidate application:* The evader's local newspaper fame is a miniature of the civilian-facing translation problem.

**[E13b-130]. The sick report and the American doctor: complicity, "Shake!" and "Who is the doctor? You or I?"** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6702-6759)
Fed up, the narrator tells Paddy to report him absent; Paddy tells him to stay off parade and say he was cleaning the tents, as the order said every man bar the orderly corporal and cooks must parade. The Major demands to know why he is absent; to avoid getting Paddy "crimed" for reporting him present, the narrator says he hurt his big toe, and the Major orders a special sick report. At the Field Ambulance a young American doctor tells him to pull off his boot; the narrator says nothing is wrong and explains everything, including the false report; the doctor asks about his ribbons, says "Shake!", then, when he again says nothing is wrong with the toe, replies "Who is the doctor? You or I?" and writes "attending hospital and excused all duties," adding that as long as he was seeing the sick the narrator would have "an easy time." The narrator thinks he "had received more consideration from an American doctor than from any of my own countrymen." The Major "when he read the sick report his jaw dropped a foot," having been denied the pleasure of trying him "on the serious crime of 'going sick without a cause.'" The narrator calls the Major "like a good many more that I came across in Back Areas: the only thing they believed in was saluting drill and spit-and-polish parades," who needed "a few months in the front-line trenches" to "realize that there was a war on." At the next hospital call a young English doctor says "All right, Richards, carry on!" and that he did not think the toe would get better "until you leave here." Mechanisms: an elaborate chain of covering lies protecting Paddy; the doctor's formula "Who is the doctor?" as joking authority that grants a favor; the old-soldier persona gets the doctors' collusion; the narrator's contempt for Back Area officers. Targets: Back Area Majors; saluting drill.
*Candidate application:* The old soldier whose service record opens doors with sympathetic doctors; the chain of mutual cover (corporal, Paddy, American doctor, English doctor) is a rank-spanning conspiracy in miniature.

**[E13b-131]. Sealyham's missing decoration, and Ricco's two pages of swearing.** (PW-07, Ch. XXIV, L6758-6759; Ch. XXV, "The Final Advance," L6766-6788)
Sealyham, sent to the Base with a very bad knee: "If ever a man earned a decoration he had, but he never got one." On the Somme again, a new American doctor replaces Dr. Dunn; Colonel Cockburn has been wounded "in rushing a machine-gun post with the sergeant who was in charge of the runners; that was the sort of man he was." Ricco's letter, censored by himself as an officer, has "first two pages ... full of delightful language," so many swear words on two sheets that the narrator "wouldn't have thought it possible." Ricco was granted a commission in the field without cadet school, joined the Cheshires, found Stanway his new Colonel, and then the narrator lost track: "from that day to this I have never found out whether he is alive or dead," and "I hope he is very much alive and enjoying all the good things on this ball of clay." Mechanisms: swearing as a gift between friends (the third "delightful language" in this part of the memoir: E13b-1, E13b-49, E13b-62); the officer's self-censoring; a present-tense good wish. Target: none; affection.
*Candidate application:* A letter whose first two pages are swearing, from a newly commissioned friend, is a deliberate mark that rank has not changed the voice.

**[E13b-132]. Pontoon again, and Paddy's death.** (PW-07, Ch. XXV, L6791-6837)
At Havrincourt Wood the narrator, Paddy and two young signallers play Pontoon by candle in a boarded shack while shelling begins; each time a shell blows the candle out "One of us would remark 'That's a near one' as we relit it"; gas shells arrive with a soft thud, helmets ready. When the shelling slackens Paddy says "I hope they will now give us a bit of peace." A few minutes later a shell explodes outside; the two youngsters call out they are hit; "Paddy said nothing." The narrator finds him unconscious with a wrist three parts severed and a head wound (restrained); the Aid Post Sergeant says it is a compound fracture but "he had a chance"; gas makes them put on helmets; Paddy "talking deliriously" and dies about an hour later at the Casualty Clearing Station. The narrator's text: "It was tough luck, after going through the whole War right down to September 1918 to get killed the way he did," followed by "I had lost another good pal." The next paragraph: "There was a lot of gas hanging around that night and I felt pretty rough"; Lane is wounded; "All my pals seemed to be leaving me one way or the other." There is no joke, nickname or idiom of the old hands in this passage (the "That's a near one" remark is the last piece of ritual talk). Compare E13b-76, the earlier card game under fire in which the interruption saved the players.
*Candidate application:* The death of the narrator's closest friend (his companion in the D.C.M., the café, the fishing, the raids) gets six sentences in the narrator's plainest style; the ritual remark and the last wish for "a bit of peace" are the whole of the elegy.

**[E13b-133]. Le Catelet: the American dead and "plenty of guts."** (PW-07, Ch. XXV, L6840-6874)
In October the narrator's division arrives where an American division had attacked; eleven tanks stand stationary in what proved a concealed minefield; scores of small mines "about the size of a kitchen kettle with the spout and handle off" were dug up; a hundred yards beyond, "hundreds of dead Americans had been collected and laid out in rows" (restrained). The narrator, looking at the high banks, "marvelled how any of the Yanks had got into the place" and concludes "They must have had plenty of guts." Then "Many a good man went West that night" at a failed night attack. No joke at the Americans' expense; the kettle simile is the single image.
*Candidate application:* The memoir's treatment of the Americans in the last weeks is without sarcasm and ends with a statement of respect.

**[E13b-134]. The chaplain who might have given a weakling his place: the clergy as "a funny crowd," and the exception.** (PW-07, Ch. XXV, L6891-6927)
On the sunken road a new Church of England chaplain, "powerfully-built" and "looked strong enough to carry a Lewis-gun in each hand," is described; the narrator imagines him offering a "weakling" his rifle and place in the attack, which "the One above would have considered it a very Christian act," but "he never said anything of that order at all." He generalizes: "The Clergy on both sides were a funny crowd," who prayed for victory but "did not believe in doing any of the smiting themselves"; all were non-combatants "with the exception of the Catholic priests who were forced to serve in the French Army." Then he meets the frail Wesleyan Minister, "although he had chastised me several times for my language," going over with the first wave in order to attend the wounded; the narrator says he admired "his pluck but not his sense," advises him to wait, and the minister goes over with the first wave, "dropping dead before he had run ten paces." The paragraph that follows goes straight on to the fighting. No irony touches the Wesleyan minister. Mechanisms: anticlerical sarcasm at the class of chaplains ("smitten hip and thigh" being a Biblical idiom turned back), with a named exception honored. Target: chaplains in general.
*Candidate application:* The old soldier's contempt for a class (chaplains) is lifted by a man who does what he preaches; he is mourned without a joke.

**[E13b-135]. The respect for the old Germans of 1914.** (PW-07, Ch. XXV, L6925-6927)
The narrator says that "if the German troops of 1914-1915 had been holding the positions around the village I don't believe it would have been taken in a month of Sundays," and elsewhere in this part notes that the German prisoners look "half-starved, the majority of them being mere lads" (Ch. XXVI, L6942-6943). Mechanism: the narrator's respect for the old enemy is stated as a counterfactual, a form of praise that also belittles the 1918 enemy.
*Candidate application:* The old regular's contempt for the late-war enemy is a way of honoring the early-war enemy and the narrator's own earlier cohort.

### K. Chapter XXVI (L6936): "Le Cateau Again," advance, and the Armistice month

**[E13b-136]. Darby, the only man who enjoyed the war: the poison, the Base, and the last thing he wanted.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L6954-6976)
Two runners, Pip and Darby, enter a house that was part chemist's shop, part café; Darby tastes a bottle and spits it out; his tongue is badly bleeding and the Doctor says one swallowed drop would have killed him; he is sent to the Base and then to England, "which was the last thing he wanted." The narrator: "He was the only man I ever met who enjoyed the War—of course I mean of us front-line soldiers." Darby had worked in every American state, was on a Canadian ranch at the war's outbreak, was invalided and pensioned out after a kick from a horse, "worked his passage to England, thereby forfeiting his pension," joined the British Army, and was a runner for eighteen months and wounded twice; after the second wound He "had been no further than the Base," and the narrator supposes "he had asked the Doctor not to send him home." He was "well-educated" and "always quoting Kipling." The narrator adds that in a letter from Pip a year after the war, Darby was living nearby "but the poison had affected his speech a little," and "I hope he has completely recovered by now." Pip, who was in charge of a golf course before the war, "is still in charge of one." Mechanisms: a joke about scrounging gone wrong; the narrator's exceptional comment on a man who loved the war; the habit of reporting each comrade's postwar condition. Where humor stops: the postwar speech damage.
*Candidate application:* The memoir's one volunteer-for-more is cut down by his own scrounging and sent home against his will; the narrator's present-tense hope is the only emotion given.

**[E13b-137]. "Salaamed" by French women, and a house burned for firewood.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L6979-7004)
Two women in a liberated street step off the pavement and bow deeply as the narrator and Tich pass; "For the moment I was back in India and was being salaamed" and he explains that during German rule they must have been obliged to give way to German soldiers. Street names had been changed to German royals and generals; "I expect if the United States had not entered the War there would have still been German names on them." Cognac in German water-bottles is found. In an abandoned house with beautiful furniture the men "soon knocked up for firewood," build a fire in a punched bucket in a room with a piano ("One of our chaps was a good pianist and we were soon as happy as birds"), pull the beds down to the cellars and "slept the sleep of the just." Outside, French civilians cut meat from dead horses; the narrator ate horse stew with the French in 1914. Mechanisms: an ironic proverb ("sleep of the just") after wrecking an absent owner's house; the colonial memory (India) as the nearest comparison for subservience. Target: none specifically; the narrator's gloss on occupation.
*Candidate application:* The narrator reads civilians' habits of deference as lingering effects of occupation; his own men's burning of furniture is mentioned in the same flat register.

**[E13b-138]. Brigands, strings, and the relief food.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L7007-7017)
Men billeted in a barn find tins of condensed milk hidden under straw by owners who had gone back with the Germans; neighbor women say the owners "had been brigands" who were "too friendly" with officials distributing neutral relief supplies and "could get anything they wanted," while they themselves "were lucky to get anything at all." The narrator: "It was the same as everything else in the War—there were certain ways of pulling the strings." Mechanism: cynical equation of occupied-zone corruption with army favoritism. Target: those with pull.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's cynicism about "pulling the strings" is a general theory he applies to the army, the Base and the occupied zone alike.

**[E13b-139]. The Staff Officer who sprinted: "a bally cheek."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L7020-7060)
Near Le Cateau a young signaller and the narrator scrounge idle telephone wire from abandoned dug-outs in a valley when three officers approach; the narrator "very nearly fainted when I saw that one of them was a divisional staff-officer," thinking that something was bound to happen when one of his kind was so near the front line. The Staff Officer, finding they are out of their own Divisional Area, calls it "a bally cheek" to salvage wire there and orders the narrator to drop the reel; as he starts a "severe dressing down," two shells burst: "The way he and the two others sprinted back out of it, anyone would have thought they had been in strict training for months for some important track event." The narrator shouts after them "to come back and take his wire." For over an hour the enemy bombards the valley until the dug-outs and shacks "were now blown to bits," and the two get their wire later and run a line to the isolated company, "which saved the runners some work and perhaps their lives." Mechanisms: the Staff's flight as the comic climax (the third such Staff-versus-shell joke after E13b-11 and E13b-90); a mock-sports simile; the narrator's shout as impertinence; the practical justification. Target: the divisional staff.
*Candidate application:* The Staff Officer who enforces boundaries of divisional areas at the wrong time and runs from shells is a recurring figure.

**[E13b-140]. Rabbits and bolt-holes; the last village.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L7063-7086)
After an attack, a German plane strafes the railway track and men bolt into small holes in the bank: "rabbits would not have bolted into them quicker than we did"; "We tried to bring him down but he got away with it." At Englefontaine, a straggling village with the Forest of Mormal behind, the battalion on 4 November attacks under an artillery smoke screen and takes the other half of the village in two hours; "There was some bayonet work that morning but the majority of the enemy surrendered without putting up a fight." Mechanism: animal simile applied to the speaker; neutral report of the last battle. 
*Candidate application:* The final action is narrated in the same procedural register as the earlier ones; it contains no valediction.

**[E13b-141]. "We would have innocently murdered them all": the cellar and the kiss.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L7089-7101)
In Englefontaine the narrator's rule is "When bombing dug-outs or cellars it was always wise to throw bombs into them first and have a look around them after," but civilians were in some cellars, so they shouted down; another man and he shouted twice, got no reply, and were about to pull the pins when a woman's voice cried out and a young woman came up, "gave the both of us a hearty kiss," and explained in rapid French that her family had been too frightened to answer. The narrator: "If the young lady had not cried out when she did we would have innocently murdered them all." The joke is the kiss; the phrase "innocently murdered" is the narrator's statement of the near miss, and it carries no comedy.
*Candidate application:* The nearest the memoir comes to confessing a moral near-disaster is a two-word oxymoron, "innocently murdered," that the narrator leaves without comment.

**[E13b-142]. The German doctor "the most unlucky man in France," and the prisoner sketcher.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L7104-7121)
A captured German doctor "who could speak excellent English" says he is "the most unlucky man in France": if the British had not attacked that morning he would have been leaving for Germany on leave the next evening, and "had calculated that by the time his leave had expired peace would have been proclaimed" so that he would not have had to return; he "honestly believed that their men could not hold out for another fortnight," said the front troops had been on "scanty rations," those behind worse off and "the people in his country were more or less starving." A German prisoner, "an excellent sketcher," draws lightning sketches on postcards of the narrator and another man "just as we were" (unwashed, unshaven, "in a state"); they pay him with "a tin of bully beef and a cigarette," and "He was quite satisfied"; he had lived a couple of years in Chelsea. Mechanism: the German doctor's joke is the same unlucky-leave irony the British would make (cf. E13b-24, the old hand's lament at a Blighty wound that missed); the narrator quotes it with no comment. The sketcher's portraits mirror Bob's sketches (E13b-59).
*Candidate application:* The enemy's own gallows joke and the portrait-for-bully-beef trade show a shared register at the war's end, in which the narrator treats the enemy as a recognizable type of person.

**[E13b-143]. Loot, tame rabbits and a poor lot of soldiers.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVI, L7122-7131)
Tich and the narrator find two bottles of Black and White whisky, "evidently a bit of loot from the canteens they had captured on the Somme, in their advance in March" (confirming the transport's rumor, E13b-115), and two tame rabbits; the narrator thinks the German holders "must have been a very poor lot of soldiers" because the "pukka old German soldiers would have soon had them in the stew pots." They eat from the gardens; "The whisky livened us up and when I got down to it to snatch a few hours' sleep Tich was singing a song." Mechanism: a rabbit as the measure of the enemy's quality; the rumor from E13b-115 verified by an object. 
*Candidate application:* The old regular judges the enemy's soldiering by whether they eat the rabbits, as he judged his own side's new recruits by loading drill.

### L. Chapters XXVII and XXVIII (L7133-7454): the Armistice, demobilization, and the postwar account

**[E13b-144]. Armistice morning: "an Armistice was not the same as a Peace."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII, "The Armistice," L7147-7167)
On the morning of 11 November a small enemy shell crashes through the roof of the house the narrator's party is leaving, "but nobody was hit"; a little later the party passes a battered cooker with two dead cooks of another battalion, killed by "one of the last shells that the enemy had fired on this part of the Front" (restrained; stated plainly, no remark). The architect, now a corporal on Brigade Staff, says "all manner of yarns" about peace are flying; the official news arrives; "Everyone was glad to hear the news," though "an Armistice was not the same as a Peace and there was no booze in the village to add to our cup of happiness." Mechanism: the narrator's reaction to the war's end is an understatement of gladness qualified by pedantry about the term and a missing drink; the two dead cooks are the Armistice morning's last casualties and are not given a joke.
*Candidate application:* The end of the war is received by this other-ranks narrator with gladness limited to one sentence and a joke about booze; no triumph and no collapse.

**[E13b-145]. "A twenty-thousand-to-one chance," Pontoon, and "stony broke."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII, L7170-7180)
Apart from some transport men there were "not more than two or three of us left that had seen it through since the commencement, and ours was supposed to be a lucky battalion"; "I expect we had pulled off a twenty-thousand-to-one chance." They celebrate by playing Pontoon in a cellar, with money accumulated and unspendable in recent months; after about six hours he is "stony broke": "I had got the money easily and now I lost it easily." The next day he sells German field-glasses and automatic pistols to try again "with the same result": "My luck had changed with a vengeance," but "I consoled myself with the thought that I had arrived in France broke and would leave it in the same way." Mechanism: survival odds expressed as a gambling figure; celebration by gambling; the final accounting of the war as a break-even, which is the narrator's closing joke about what it cost and gave. 
*Candidate application:* An old soldier's Armistice speech is a gambling loss and the line that he is where he started.

**[E13b-146]. The women awaiting the French mail: the plainest statement of what the war could do to a man.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII, L7183-7200)
In the Occupied Areas the women, who had received no news of their men in the French Army since August 1914, are "eagerly waiting for the French mail." The narrator: The people "had no conception of what the War had been really like" and did not consider that their menfolk, if living, "might perhaps be insane, blind, or maimed for life, which was worse than being killed." He tells of a young woman in Aulnoye with a boy over four whom his father had never seen; the mail arrives a few days later and she is told her husband "had been killed on August 12th, 1914, which was before her boy was born." No joke, no idiom; this passage restates in the narrator's present voice the fates he feared in E13b-17 and E13b-75 ("worse than being killed") and sets the war's end against civilians who had been shut out of it.
*Candidate application:* The memoir's end-of-war sorrow is delivered through a civilian woman who is told the date of her husband's death four years late; the narrator adds only the generalization.

**[E13b-147]. The Peer's lecture on peacetime discipline: "so deadly a crime."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII, L7203-7219)
A few days after the Armistice The Peer, in temporary command, lectures the N.C.O.s that discipline had become "a little lax" and must "revert back to its pre-War standard," including that any N.C.O. caught walking out with a private would be reduced to the ranks. The narrator recalls that before the war a young lance-corporal caught walking out with a private was brought before the Colonel to explain "so deadly a crime," but that "a more brotherly love had existed between N.C.O.s and men" as the war went on; "Ninety-five per cent of the N.C.O.s that attended the lecture were men that had joined up for the duration of the War," and most "did not care a damn whether they were reduced or not," being "only waiting their time to be demobilized." The architect, who gave the narrator full particulars (the narrator was not present), considered it "a lot of bally rot," glad he "had not been a soldier in pre-War days." Mechanism: the narrator's own regular's perspective reports a rule he once lived under and now sees as absurd; hyperbolic wording ("so deadly a crime") for a prewar rule. Target: the Peer and the regular army's return to prewar manners.
*Candidate application:* The end of war brings back the rank rules that the fighting had relaxed; the citizen-soldiers jeer at them.

**[E13b-148]. The burnished helmets: "praying for it to break out again."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII, L7220-7232)
The Peer "had another brainwave": every man to parade with his steel helmet burnished; the helmets had a dark green coat that took six hours to scrape off and two more to polish. "There was more swearing over this order than I had heard for some time," and the veterans "were praying for it to break out again." On parade the helmets shone so in the sun "I found it impossible to look at them." The Brigadier inspects and says the men look very smart but as no orders had been issued from Division on burnishing helmets, they must be repainted; "As soon as the parade was dismissed all helmets had to be taken to the pioneers' shop to be restored to their original state." The narrator: "The only good it had done was to improve the praying language of the men." Mechanisms: the war as preferable to the peacetime parade; the reversal of an order by a higher order that makes the entire labor pointless; "praying language" as the old formula for swearing. It echoes the Corps Commander's polished brasses (E13b-1), with the same structure at the war's end.
*Candidate application:* The unit's reaction to a pointless peacetime order is to wish for the war; the comic structure (labor, inspection, reversal) is the same as the 1915 brasses.

**[E13b-149]. Demobilization: a captain Fox, coaching a man to pass the mining test, and our "smoke barrage."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII, L7235-7266)
Miners go home first. As the narrator boards a lorry he sees "my old Platoon-Sergeant and Company-Sergeant-Major, now Captain Fox, commanding B Company," and hopes "to read that he is commanding a battalion of his old Regiment of which he was so worthy an N.C.O." At Cambrai the miners are quizzed by mining officers; one man tells the narrator he had never been on top of a coal pit; "I gave him a few particulars and he passed the mining officers all right," and "I expect many more did the same." On the train to the coast the narrator and seven others stack wood and a fire-bucket in a coach full of French civilians and keep the fire going to Boulogne, "we very nearly drove the French passengers crazy with our smoke barrage," while they did "nothing but spit, cough and curse until they had reached their destination"; "it was very hard for us to drop out of habits which we had cultivated during the War." Mechanisms: a quiet conspiracy coaching a comrade to get out early, which the narrator attributes to others as well; the "smoke barrage" metaphor for civilians' annoyance; a closing sentence in which wartime habits persist. 
*Candidate application:* Fire as "everything" persists as a reflex past the war and annoys civilians; the persistence is described as a habit rather than a trauma.

**[E13b-150]. Postwar: "enough of medicine to float the British Navy," and the man who got wet on the bridge.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVIII, "Rewards for War Service," L7273-7323)
Demobilized at Liverpool on 5 December, the narrator is asked by the doctor if he still suffers from his complaint (the piles complaint, E13b-104; I summarize), says he does, "left the Army A1," and found it worsened; he writes: "I drunk enough of medicine to float the British Navy," and the pills he swallowed, made into cannon balls, would (he says) have flattened all the concrete pillboxes on the Western Front. In August 1921 he applies for a Medical Board; he meets a man from his village who "had not served in the War" but had guarded a railway bridge, got wet one night and spent a week in hospital with rheumatism; "I sympathized with him and said it was marvellous how he looked so well after what he had been through." The board tells him it is not interested in the past, awards a disability pension "of eight shillings a week for sixty-five weeks" for the aggravated complaint and nothing for his rheumatism; the bridge-guard man, awarded twelve shillings a week for sixty-five weeks and massage, says "I thought you wouldn't: you have never been admitted to hospital with it and that's what they go by." The narrator explains the rule: boards go "by what hospital service a man had entered on his medical history sheet and not by his front-line service," so a man with four weeks' English service and a few days in hospital "would have a better chance" than "a man who had done four years in the firing line"; such a man "had no more chance than a crow of being awarded a disability pension." Mechanisms: a comic hyperbole (medicine and pills as naval and artillery stores) for a long ordeal; sarcasm of sympathy toward a dodger; a bureaucratic rule stated as a paradox. Target: medical boards and the pension system.
*Candidate application:* The medical-board paradox is the other half of E13b-99 (the Rouen doctor): the system ignores the front-line record at the hospital and again at the pension office. For a veteran-exile character, the lesson is that sarcasm is the register in which a system's logic is described.

**[E13b-151]. The operation, the nurse and "1914-1915 language," and the gratitude list.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVIII, L7326-7341)
Two months later the narrator is operated on; the Staff Nurse tells him he was "the worst case she ever saw with my complaint," and that he "had used the worst language she had ever heard"; he "apologized and said that I had probably gone back to my 1914-1915 language." The operation "was a success in one way but not in another, and I have never been the same man as what I was before"; his final allowance is seven shillings and sixpence for seventy weeks. He tells civilian friends, who are "astonished that I did not receive a pension of some sort," that he is "quite satisfied, and thankful that I was not blind, that I had my limbs, that I was not horribly disfigured and that I was not an inmate in a mental home like tens of thousands of poor men still are who served in the War." Mechanisms: the nurse exchange reverts the narrator to the first-year-of-war voice (his swearing as a measure of pain); the gratitude list names the four fates he feared throughout (blindness, loss of limbs, disfigurement, madness) as the things he did not suffer. Where humor stops: the last sentence; "tens of thousands" are still in asylums in the present tense (1932).
*Candidate application:* The author's present-day statement of what he is thankful for restates the dread list from the war years, and is given to civilians who do not understand why he is satisfied.

**[E13b-152]. Pensions for non-combatants, "Old Contemptibles," and "lead-swingers and dodgers."** (PW-07, Ch. XXVIII, L7344-7393)
In his village in 1923 "the majority of men receiving disability pensions" had "never left the British Isles or who had served at the Bases and never seen the sky over the firing line"; some are still drawing life pensions larger than the men wounded in action. He lists examples (two months on Salisbury Plain; six months elsewhere in England; a man who "actually went as far as Ireland and endured terrible hardships in a cook-house") and uses a racial slur in describing the amount of work one of them does besides (restrained: I do not reproduce the phrase). These men "are greatly admired and are often spoken of as the men who worked their knobs during the War" (my gloss: the phrase is not explained; evidently those who worked the system). His verdict: "if the whole of the British Army had been like them during the War there would have been no pensions and not much England." For regular reservists invalided out, his arithmetic of service pensions is given; an old artilleryman, "wounded and gassed" and invalided, receives "roughly five shillings a week" and "is what the papers call one of the Old Contemptibles, and indeed no man has been treated with greater contempt than what he has." The narrator concludes: "This is a funny world and I have come to the conclusion that lead-swingers and dodgers gets on best in it," tells of "long periods of unemployment" since 1921 and "thousands of old soldiers like me who are worse off." A group of ex-servicemen who had been "lead-swingers and dodgers throughout the War" claim a greater share of work for having "served their Country"; one "had contracted several dangerous complaints through shaking the Crown and Anchor dice down at the Base." Mechanisms: the pun on "Old Contemptibles" and "contempt"; the sarcastic list of "terrible hardships"; the dice-shaking line as sarcastic medical history. Target: pension boards, non-combatant claimants, and the civilian honoring of them.
*Candidate application:* The veteran's grievance is not at the enemy or even at the war but at the post-war rewards structure that honors those who avoided the front; the pun is the clearest wordplay in the memoir's end.

**[E13b-153]. Armistice Day: medals without bars, and the scalp comparison (restrained).** (PW-07, Ch. XXVIII, L7396-7408)
On "Armistice day to-day" the narrator watches ex-servicemen on parade in medals, and says those who served at the Bases "a hundred miles behind the front line are wearing their medals more proudly than the men who served in the firing line," that "There is no distinction between the War medals," and that in former wars a "pukka old soldier" would be nearly ashamed of a war medal without a bar for each engagement (such medals having a vulgar slang name in the text that I do not reproduce). He then compares the parade with the habit of Native American warriors of wearing the scalps of slain enemies as proof of soldiering ("we just wear war-medals"), says some parade as if they had been in action when their only "action" was with women in the brothel districts behind the Front and at the Bases (restrained paraphrase), and concludes that the Native warriors "were vain but they were honest" (restrained: the ethnic comparison and the brothel reference are summarized; the narrator treats the comparison as favorable to the warriors but uses a period ethnic term). Mechanisms: sarcastic comparison; sexual innuendo about rear-area service ("charming damsels"). Target: Base soldiers wearing medals.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's closing satire of medal-wearing is the culmination of his decoration cynicism (E13b-10, E13b-100, E13b-128): the front-line soldier would rather have a medal that proves action.

**[E13b-154]. The author's note: "nicknames instead of names for safety," drift from comrades, and the last rank inversion.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVIII, L7411-7418)
The narrator says he relied "on my memory for the greater part of my story," may be wrong on dates or town spellings, "there are no fictitious characters, though I have given nicknames instead of names for safety in some cases." (This bears directly on the nicknames in my range: some are the author's protective substitutions, not the soldiers' own terms; the text does not say which.) In 1920 the bank clerk, the architect and he "spent a very enjoyable evening together"; he corresponded with them and Sealyham until 1922, and "it was my fault and not theirs that we ceased corresponding." The bank clerk is now "a Second Cashier, and under him in the same branch were two clerks, one who had been a captain and the other a lieutenant during the War." Mechanisms: the author's disclaimer on accuracy; a plain admission of his own drift from friends; the final rank-inversion joke (cf. E13b-105), left without comment. Target: none.
*Candidate application:* A comrade-network that dissolves by one man's neglect is acknowledged in one line, and the final humor beat is that the bank clerk now outranks two former officers.

**[E13b-155]. The last scene: the old soldier, a pub, spittoons, and the title song.** (PW-07, Ch. XXVIII, L7421-7436)
The last paragraph is of an old soldier of the regiment, living three miles away, "who still calls me Dick," who served as a rifle-and-bayonet man for ten months of the war and then as an officer's groom, who was one of the men who "used to pop over to the Native trench at Fromelles" and the groom who gave up his billet to a parson (both episodes from earlier in the memoir, outside my range), and "he still don't care a damn for any man." When times were good they meet in a pub to talk over old times, but if the conversation turned to the colonial ("Native") troops or to parsons he would "launch out with a flow of language," twice nearly getting them turned out of pubs. By closing time, the narrator says, the man had in imagination consigned great numbers of both groups to spittoons and trampled them into the sawdust (a comic hyperbole with an ethnic target that I summarize rather than quote; "Native troops" is the period term in the text for Indian troops, to the best of my understanding, and I flag it as unverified in my range). "We generally wound up our evenings with the old song, set to the tune of a well-known hymn, 'Old soldiers never die, they simply fade away.'" The text then ends with transcriber notes (not part of the memoir). Mechanisms: the last character is a persona-in-purest-form (the man who does not care a damn), the last joke is at the expense of two classes he hates (colonial troops and parsons, a pair that links to E13b-134), the last act is the title song in a hymn tune, as with the A.S.C. song (E13b-42). There is no peroration, no moral, no direct statement of what the war meant.
*Candidate application:* The old soldier's afterlife is a pub, a flow of language, and a hymn-tune song about not dying; the narrator presents the persona as the survivor and ends with it.

**[E13b-156]. How the memoir ends: attitude to the war's end, to leaving the Army, to civilians (synthesis).** (PW-07, Ch. XXVII-XXVIII, L7147-7436)
(1) *The war's end:* gladness limited to one sentence, qualified ("an Armistice was not the same as a Peace," E13b-144); celebrated by gambling; accounting that he left France as broke as he arrived (E13b-145); then the pointless peacetime parades make men wish the war back (E13b-148); the only grief is for the dead cooks, the dead of 1914 whose widow-to-be learns late, and the maimed. (2) *Leaving the Army:* no nostalgia and no regret; "I was lucky" to leave early as a miner (E13b-149); the one unit-level affection is for specific people (Fox, the bank clerk, the architect, Sealyham, Dr. Dunn) expressed in wishes ("Here's good luck to Dr. Dunn!"; the hope to read that Fox commands a battalion). (3) *Civilians:* contempt for those who evaded (E13b-123), complaining civilians (E13b-123), military-age men at smoking concerts (E13b-83), the press (E13b-58, E13b-116), pension claimants and Base medal-wearers (E13b-152, E13b-153); gratitude list that civilians do not understand (E13b-151); the old Allied civilians are mixed (liberated French kiss, bow and brigands, E13b-137, E13b-138, E13b-141). (4) *The tone:* the narrator's humor outlasts the war as bitterness at the postwar settlement, not as a longing for the army.
*Candidate application:* For a character fifteen years out: the register is a pension grievance and a pub song, not a memorial; the old comrades survive in postcards and "good luck."

## Pattern roll-up (what this unit establishes, organized by the lens headings; finding numbers in brackets)

**Functions.**
- *Cohesion and identity:* the old-soldier persona as a style of conduct (declining rank, E13b-29, E13b-61; arrangements about parades, E13b-130); ritual banter formulas shared by two men (E13b-19, E13b-24); mutual leg-pulling (E13b-8); swearing as a shared art and acculturation marker (E13b-1, E13b-49, E13b-62, E13b-78, E13b-131); the title song as the last act (E13b-155).
- *Boundary:* against the Staff, brigadiers and base-camp NCOs (E13b-11, E13b-90, E13b-98, E13b-139); against the A.S.C. by pay (E13b-42); against volunteers and the Public Schools battalion (E13b-2, E13b-50); against civilians who evaded or complained (E13b-52, E13b-81, E13b-83, E13b-123); against pension-drawing non-combatants (E13b-152, E13b-153); against chaplains (E13b-134) with an honored exception; against allies (French, Portuguese, E13b-53, E13b-110).
- *Coping:* drinking as a deliberate function, "helped us to forget" (E13b-30); gambling as the response to risk and the war's end (E13b-13, E13b-58, E13b-145); comparative luck as consolation (E13b-121); looting and scrounging as the means to "a good time" under "dead cocks" arithmetic (E13b-15); keeping an enemy's picture and rabbits (E13b-142, E13b-143).
- *Resistance and discipline:* a vow to refuse return and accept prison (E13b-26); the sergeant who does not report panic (E13b-38); the sick-report conspiracy (E13b-130); the café punishment relished (E13b-6); self-wounding admired as wisdom (E13b-73); wishing the war back rather than do peacetime drill (E13b-129, E13b-148).
- *Rank negotiation:* officers laugh at a charge but are bound to pass it up (E13b-6); an officer's warmth is measured by presence under fire (E13b-46, E13b-66); the Doctor "temporarily resigned" (E13b-89, E13b-96); privates asked for confidence by an officer (E13b-91); the Colonel's rule against speaking of an officer so (E13b-20); the peacetime return to rank rules (E13b-147).
- *Grief:* never in jokes; grief shows as swearing (E13b-45), as a changed habit (E13b-24: no more scrounging), as a toast (E13b-124), as a good wish (E13b-131).

**Targets.** Corps and divisional commanders and their orders (E13b-1, E13b-55, E13b-102, E13b-109, E13b-148); Staff officers (E13b-11, E13b-139); brigadiers (E13b-90, E13b-117); decorations and the medal system (E13b-10, E13b-66, E13b-100, E13b-128, E13b-153); the Army's equipment and planning (E13b-13, E13b-62, E13b-82); the A.S.C. and Base (E13b-42, E13b-98, E13b-99, E13b-129); chaplains (E13b-134); doctors and medical boards (E13b-99, E13b-108, E13b-150); civilians (above); the press (E13b-58, E13b-116); individual unpopular officers (E13b-4, E13b-10, E13b-20, E13b-31, E13b-32, E13b-43); the self (E13b-6, E13b-74, E13b-123 stage fright, E13b-90 "jumping and hopping"); the enemy treated mainly with respect (E13b-11, E13b-16, E13b-68, E13b-118, E13b-126, E13b-135, E13b-142) except for the 1918 German soldier's quality (E13b-143).

**Forms and mechanisms.** Deadpan sequencing, a refusal and then its fulfillment, or an evasion and then a death (E13b-29, E13b-97, E13b-129); understatement at the point of death or danger ("tough luck," "rotten luck," "It was the only weird experience," E13b-16, E13b-23, E13b-103, E13b-132); mock-prayer as curse and its comic fulfillment (E13b-31, E13b-40, E13b-43, E13b-55); sarcasm as promotion or decoration ("Field-Marshal," "a big decoration," "something different," E13b-10, E13b-13, E13b-63); the wager as realism (E13b-13, E13b-58, E13b-145); hyperbole of scale (medicine "to float the British Navy," E13b-150); simile from sport or domestic life ("track event," "granny's apron-strings," "a paperweight," E13b-64, E13b-125, E13b-139); the practical joke with a flawed reach (E13b-7) and the staged prank on the Staff (E13b-11); euphemism for taking from the dead ("borrow," E13b-102) and for the army's work ("brainwave," E13b-102, E13b-148); a pun ("Old Contemptibles," E13b-152); rumor inflation and rumor vindication (E13b-51, E13b-80, E13b-115, E13b-143); nicknames of affection and of contempt (E13b-157); parody of a hymn tune (E13b-42, E13b-155).

**Rules of license.**
- Jokes about officers are tied to conduct: earned by cowardice, drunkenness, stupidity or looting, withheld from the brave and the present (E13b-10 vs. E13b-66, E13b-46, E13b-89); the brave are praised in the same idiom.
- The old hand may warn, taunt or curse an officer privately, but the officer may charge him (E13b-32) and the Colonel forbids the register in his hearing (E13b-20).
- The dead enemy is a prop only for a prank on a rank, with a decent burial after (E13b-11).
- A comrade's death is never the target; the target is the rank or the rule (E13b-48, E13b-94).
- The joke at an officer's death is allowed when conduct licenses it and the cause is a mock-prayer (E13b-40).
- Swearing is a skill judged by connoisseurs (E13b-62, E13b-131).

**Limits and failure modes (what he does NOT joke about).**
- Named comrades' deaths: Dann (E13b-23), Duffy (E13b-24), Sol (E13b-34), Bale (E13b-9), Owens (E13b-103), Paddy (E13b-132), the married man (E13b-112), the Wesleyan minister (E13b-134): each is narrated plainly, usually with one idiom ("went West," "tough luck," "rotten luck").
- Fear and breakdown: the shellshocked officers and the stretcher-bearer (E13b-22), the frightened signaller (E13b-36), the runaways (E13b-38), the youngster who ducked (E13b-125), Higginson's "nervous breakdown" (E13b-46); contempt appears only for fear in men who ought to be working (E13b-25).
- The feared fates (E13b-17, E13b-75, E13b-146, E13b-151): blindness, disfigurement, mutilation, madness, "worse than being killed."
- Executions: only the commuted sentence (E13b-39) and wishes against shirkers and a sergeant (E13b-45, E13b-91); no shootings at dawn.
- Wounded and dying enemies: passed without comment (E13b-89); prisoners killed by comrades: reported with grief as the cause and no joke (E13b-92).
- Gas: the joke-rumor (E13b-80) gives way to a plain account of the survivors' suffering.
- Failures of the prank: Tim (E13b-7) is the one place where a joke is admitted to have done harm.
- The humor fails where it is carried to civilians: the wife of the "quick-firers" (E13b-7), the woman with the late notification (E13b-146), friends who cannot understand his satisfaction (E13b-151).

**Time course.**
- *In the line:* the old hands' formula changes meaning as the cohort is destroyed: first envy of the young (E13b-17, E13b-24), then the narrator's own admitted hope for a Blighty wound in 1917 (E13b-93), then the failure of the formula on the casualty track (E13b-96).
- *Over the war:* humor persists into 1918 but the narrator's last friend is killed in two sentences (E13b-132); the old hands' ranks dwindle (E13b-29, E13b-145).
- *At the war's end and after:* gladness is slight (E13b-144), the peacetime parades make men wish for the war (E13b-148), the postwar humor is bitterness about pensions and medals (E13b-152, E13b-153), and the habits remain (E13b-149).
- *Old age (as written in 1932):* the narrator reports comrades' present status and offers toasts (E13b-124, E13b-131, E13b-136); the pub song is the last act (E13b-155).

**Civilian-facing register.** The narrator in 1933 prints what he would not have been allowed to write in 1916: unvarnished contempt for brass, evaders, doctors and pension boards (E13b-1, E13b-123, E13b-152), a story about a civilian woman's lie (E13b-70), and the gratitude list that civilians do not understand (E13b-151); censorship of wartime letters is described (E13b-7) and the oath dashes survive in places ("the old B——," E13b-63). Rumors and the press are corrected (E13b-116).

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.**
- The signaller who vowed not to return, took prison, and "done his whack" (E13b-26), with the narrator's approval ("true to his word").
- The soldier who told the Governor he did not mind prison "until the bloody War finished" (E13b-26).

**Cross-cultural / era.** British regular, Royal Welch, 1916-1918 and 1932: respect for the Australians' presence and warnings (E13b-90, E13b-95), the Americans as sociable and brave (E13b-127, E13b-133), the French as an object of mild ridicule at relief and as civilians (E13b-53, E13b-81, E13b-137), the Portuguese as a nickname and a flight joke (E13b-110), Indian-army vocabulary imported by regulars (E13b-31, E13b-98, E13b-109, E13b-137). ## Gaps and cautions

- **Retrospective, shaped voice.** Written about 1932 (L7347, L7396) from memory (the author says so, L7411-7412), with composite or protective nicknames possible (E13b-154). The companion pass notes that outside sources report literary help in preparing the book; the text file is silent on this and I did not verify it. Quoted sayings are the narrator's reconstruction.
- **One man, one regiment, one rank.** All patterns are of a regular private-signaller of the Royal Welch Fusiliers in 1916-1918, with a prewar regular's attitudes (contempt for conscripts, NCO-officer boundaries) that a conscript or New Army voice would not necessarily share.
- **Text-file condition.** The file is a proofread eBook, not raw OCR; the transcriber notes at the end (L7439-7452) say misspellings and printer errors were corrected and that majority spellings were used where variants occur.
