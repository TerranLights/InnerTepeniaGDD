## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What the whole unit is.** *Terminal Lance* is a webcomic by Maximilian Uriarte, an infantry Marine (assaultman and squad leader; Iraq in 2007 and 2009 per his own notes, MV-TL-08 and MV-TL-21) who began it while still on active duty and moved to a Combat Camera shop in his last months (MV-TL-01). It is written by and for the lower-enlisted Marine ("lance corporal" is the title's rank), with strips about barracks, NCOs, gear, deployments and, from late 2010, life after enlistment. It is **Marine Corps only**, **infantry viewpoint**, **male viewpoint** (women appear as the butt of jokes, as topics of debate, or as speakers inside a man's strip, never as authors in this sample), and **American**. The extraction covers the sample and the title list, not the whole body of work.

**Sample bias, stated plainly.** The 60 pages were chosen by **title keywords** (death, deployment, veteran, VA, memorial, medical, home, Veterans Day and similar). The sample is therefore heavily weighted to serious, after-service and institution-facing topics: by my count about 73% of the pages have a serious family as their main topic, against 12% of the title list (E19-58). **Do not read the sample's proportions as the strip's proportions.**

**MV-TL-00 (title list).** One file of 1,000 lines: date and slug of each post in the site's post sitemap (2010-01-05 to 2026-09-26). **Contains:** titles only (slugs, lower case, punctuation removed, some words dropped), which I counted by theme with a stated method (E19-58). **Does NOT contain:** any strip, any author's note, any measure of tone; and it is **probably incomplete** (exactly 1,000 entries; at least a dozen titles linked from the sampled pages are missing, E19-60). About 37 of its entries carry a September 2026 import date, not a writing date (E19-57).

**MV-TL-01 to MV-TL-57 (page text and strip image).** Each is a WordPress page: the strip as one image (jpg, jpeg or gif) and the author's note ("Written by Maximilian"), plus site boilerplate (menu links, "From the archives" boxes of three posts, "One response" or "Leave a Reply" lines) that I read and skipped. **Evidence base:** one author, one corps, 2010-2026, uneven (by year: 2010-2011, 10 pages; 2012-2013, 11; 2014-2015, 12; 2016-2018, 12; 2019, none; 2020-2026, 15). **Contains:** strip description (by looking), the author's account of origin and intent, a few reader comments on 2022-2026 pages, and a handful of non-strip posts (two lists of Veterans Day offers and one video-game review; the film review at MV-TL-32 carries a one-panel strip). **Does NOT contain:** readers' comment text on the 2010-2018 pages (only a count heading); any strip animation (GIFs show a first frame); any dialogue transcribed beyond a few words (copyright). **Format cautions:** the 11 GIF strips (MV-TL-06, 10, 12, 14, 15, 18, 21, 25, 29, 30, 32) are seen only as a first frame; web versions are about 700 px wide; lettering was legible except where garbled by design. The 2010-2011 pages were extracted from a later site template (the line "Infantry Marine turned Combat Artist turned animator turned bestselling author turned dad" is a present-day tagline). Some extracts duplicate paragraphs and comments (an extraction artifact). Four strips were printed first in the Marine Corps Times and reposted later (MV-TL-12, 33, 36, 38).

**MV-TL-58 to MV-TL-60 (behind-the-scenes pages, no image).** Short notes by the author on a 2023 strip and a 2026 strip, and a paywall stub (MV-TL-60) whose content is not in the saved copy. Dated 2026-09-24 by the site, which is an import date for two of them (E19-57).

**What this unit does NOT contain, overall.** Interviews (the author's interview material is in E21, WA-035 to WA-037); sales or reception data; the strips of the great majority of the 1,000 posts; women's, officers' or non-infantry voices except as the author depicts them or as commenters; any defector or exile content; any strip about suicide or post-traumatic stress in the sample.

## Findings

### From the 2010 strips (MV-TL-01 to MV-TL-06)

**[E19-1]. "Jambo" (2010-01-22, #6; MV-TL-01): a pure in-group greeting with no target, marked by a boundary line in its own subtitle.**
One wide panel: three uniformed guards with rifles shout the same greeting at passing Marines, the loudest one with his shirt thrown open in exuberance. No joke is made at anyone's expense; the point is the shout itself. The subtitle ("If you went to Iraq, you'll get it") announces that the strip is a password: only those who were at the gate know why it is funny. The author's note identifies the guards as Ugandan contract guards at the chow halls and posts of Iraq's major bases, remembers yelling the greeting from a vehicle turret at Camp Fallujah, and reports that the guards answered even more enthusiastically; he asks readers deployed elsewhere to greet them for him. The same note shows the author's own career line: his battalion commander had refused to let an assaultman squad leader transfer out, and he got to the Combat Camera shop only with "some 4 months left."
*Candidate application:* The cheapest and warmest piece of unit culture is a ritual exchange with non-combatant locals or contractors that outsiders cannot decode; a veteran can be recognized by whether the old greeting makes his face change.

**[E19-2]. "Deployments are just like Final Fantasy Games" (2010-03-30, #25; MV-TL-02): the war narrated through the interface of a console role-playing game, and the author's first public defense of the strip.**
Three panels in game grammar: the base PX drawn as a village item shop selling "potions" (energy drinks) and branded gear; a status screen with two Marines shown with rank as "level" (E3 and E4), health and magic bars and a "Level Up" flash; then leaving the base wire becomes a random encounter, with a battle menu (attack, aim, air strike, item) over a squad facing armed men in the open. Mechanism: a one-to-one mapping of the deployment's loops (resupply, promotion, contact) onto game systems, so the war is made *less* serious by a frame its audience grew up on, the reverse of the civilian film image. The opponents are drawn as faceless game enemies; the joke does not individuate or mock them. The author's note says the reference might fly over older Marines' heads, that he is "a huge nerd," that a stealth-action game was "about 50% of the reason" he joined, and that most junior enlisted are gamers. The same note records the first big press moment (the strip's story ran in the Marine Corps Times; he passed a million hits) and a defensive paragraph: complaints come mostly from people who judge by the title; the strip is "situational and observational humor based on my actual experiences"; he has "never bashed the Marine Corps"; and the alternative to being offended is simply "don't read it." The same note adds that he knows people of "exceedingly high rank" read it.
*Candidate application:* A soldier generation that grew up on role-playing games may narrate its own war in their vocabulary (levels, loot, encounters). A veteran character's gallows humor can borrow the *structure* of games (random encounters, grinding) without any sample lines.

**[E19-3]. "Calling Home" (2010-06-11, #42; MV-TL-03): the shared phone bank, where privacy is gone and every call is overheard; and the officers' free satellite phones as a rank grievance.**
Three panels at a phone center on a base in Iraq (an operational-security poster on the wall, crude graffiti on the partition). A Marine calls home and cannot get his partner's attention through the noise; the man in the next booth explodes in threats of violence at his own partner; the man on the other side makes a sexually explicit call. The Marine hangs up, explaining that he will call back because one neighbor is about to commit violence and the other is occupied in a private act. Mechanism: escalation by pile-up with the protagonist as the dry, resigned observer; three men, three failure modes of long-distance relationships (suspicion confirmed, rage, lust). *(Sexual and violent wording not reproduced; see the register.)* The author's note says the phones were "warm, greasy" and wired to plywood, that late nights at Camp Fallujah are "fond in retrospect," and that at the time they were as miserable as everything else in Iraq. Then the real grievance, offered as an aside: officers and senior NCOs never appear at call centers because the platoon sergeant and commander had their own satellite phones and called home free every night "while we paid exuberant amounts of cash."
*Candidate application:* Deployed soldiers' private lives are public (the phone line, the mail bin); the class line between enlisted and officers is drawn at who pays to talk to home. The grievance is delivered in the author's note, not in the strip, so the strip stays about the neighbors.

**[E19-4]. "Airport Soldier Envy" (2010-07-02, #48; MV-TL-04): inter-service contempt exposed, in the author's own note, as envy of civilian generosity.**
Two men in an airport line: an Army Soldier in digital camouflage and a Marine in a plain T-shirt (Marines cannot wear camouflage uniforms in public). The Marine mutters an insult; the Soldier says "Huh?"; the Marine says "Nothing"; in the last panel he mutters a second insult, still unheard. Mechanism: the muttered insult that is never delivered (cowardly pose), the target being both the Soldier and the Marine's own pettiness. The note supplies the turn: every Marine hates seeing soldiers in the airport, but "the real reason" is envy, because uniformed Soldiers get free beers and upgrades to first class and Marines, restricted to service and dress uniforms, do not. He adds that if Marines could wear camouflage uniforms to the airport, it "has nothing to do with motivation or pride in the uniform or any of that bullshit": it is about "free drinks."
*Candidate application:* Civilian gratitude shows up from the beginning as a currency the troops quietly count; the veteran who sneers at civilians' thanks and the one who pockets it are the same person on different days (see the Veterans Day strips below).

**[E19-5]. "Deployment Goggles" (2010-10-07, #71; MV-TL-05): perceptual distortion by deprivation; with the author's early post on benefits and a warning about what not to post.**
Two panels with the same caption, "Average female Marine," the first drawn plain and awkward, the second (after a card reading "5 Months into your Deployment Later") drawn as glamorous. Mechanism: a perceptual joke whose target is the male Marine's own judgment, aimed at nobody in particular; the author's note defends the strip as "not designed to be sexist," says the effect "goes both ways," and explains it with the claim that the fewer women around, the more attractive every one becomes, which he says he saw at a rear-area base. *(Sexualized depiction not reproduced; see the register. A companion strip, "Deployment Goggles II," appears in this sample as MV-TL-17.)* Two other items in the same note matter more for this research. First, he is changing his degree to a dual major in illustration and animation and says he "may as well get all I can out of the GI Bill," and he opens the site's forum to questions on the GI Bill and "other VA benefits" as "a haven" for those who have "made the journey to the other side": the site was a benefits-information exchange from its first year. Second, he warns readers not to submit photos that could get them in trouble should "some SNCO of the butthurt tribe" take offense: the lower enlisted audience is understood to be policed by senior NCOs.
*Candidate application:* Three distinct elements for a character: a deprivation joke the soldier knows is a trick of his own mind; the habit of treating veteran benefits as a practical, shared, forum-answered problem; and wariness about what leaves the unit.

**[E19-6]. "Happy Thanksgiving" (2010-11-25; MV-TL-06; GIF, first frame only): the sentimental question answered by two blunt items, plus a plain instruction in the note.**
The single visible frame is a low, close view of boots and a torn field-ration pouch on the ground; another Marine asks a Lance Corporal what he is thankful for; the Lance Corporal answers with a short list: a first item I could not decode from the image alone (it reads as gear or slang), then a crude sexual item. Mechanism: bathos (asked for sentiment, answers with the basic and the crude). *(Wording not reproduced; see the register. Any animation or later frame in the GIF is not visible to me.)* The note is the plain part and is a coping instruction, not a joke: having Thanksgiving "in the chow hall with your friends" is better than "sitting around, moping about how you're not at home."
*Candidate application:* The gallows layer and the plain layer sit one under the other; the crude answer to the sentimental question and the quiet advice to stay with the group are the same coping move.

### From the 2011 strips (MV-TL-07 to MV-TL-10)

**[E19-7]. "Married Marines" (2011-07-26, #135; MV-TL-07): the communication structure of a unit, and who falls out of it.**
One wide panel: a row of Marines seen from behind in formation, all in camouflage uniform; the one in the middle stands in gym clothes (bright yellow shorts), and the man beside him says he sent a text. The joke is the failed defense of the sender: the "word" that spreads through the barracks "like a brush fire" never reaches the married man who lives off base. The author's note calls the barracks "the dark, soulless heart of the Lance Corporal Underground," calls the married Marine's life "perceived luxury" that alienates him from it, and floats the thought that the omission could be a deliberate "treacherous" spite against the luxury. Also in the note: he is about a year out of the Corps and feels his memory "dragging," and he teases his graphic novel *The White Donkey* with a joking list of what it is not.
*Candidate application:* Cohesion is carried by an informal channel (word of mouth in the barracks); the people with an outside life pay a price in belonging, and the comic cost is a punishment in front of the whole formation.

**[E19-8]. "Proper Civilian Attire" (2011-09-06, #146; MV-TL-08): a literal-compliance reductio on rank's authority over off-duty life.**
Three panels. A sergeant major in dated short pants, knee socks and boots lectures a formation about "improper civilian attire" (backward caps, flip-flops, open shirts); he points to himself as the standard and says that anyone who does not look like him this weekend is wrong; a Lance Corporal asks the one question that exposes the standard (how a Marine dressed like that is supposed to find romantic company). Mechanism: the enforcer *embodies* a standard that is visibly absurd; the junior's question is obedient in form ("Any questions?") and insubordinate in substance. *(The question's wording is crude; not reproduced.)* Target: senior NCOs' control of off-duty life and a culture the author calls "institutionalized," whose members were dressed by parents before enlisting and, twenty years on, "no one has told them they look like an idiot." He adds that the Corps runs "10-15 years behind the times," with on-base shops still taking only cash. One line shows his own default understatement: of a quiet summer he writes "I've had worse summers, after all (Iraq 07 and 09)."
*Candidate application:* The best subordinate joke here is a sincere-sounding question; deadpan understatement about combat tours inside an ordinary complaint ("worse summers") is the register for a veteran.

**[E19-9]. "War and Peace" (2011-10-25, #157; MV-TL-09): the dread of peacetime, and war as the thing that gave the enlistment its purpose.**
Three panels. Two Marines read that the wars are ending and note that it is "strange to think" there will be no war to fight, and that one does not even know what a "peacetime Marine Corps" is like. In the third panel a senior NCO who overheard spills his coffee down his front and cries that this is the one thing he cannot face. Mechanism: reversal (the war's end, the civilians' hope, is the lifer's nightmare). The author's note is the most reflective of the 2011 posts: he "joined to go to war" in 2006; his whole enlistment was "always about the next thing" (boot camp, then infantry school, then a battalion, then Iraq); war "was always the elephant in the room," which gave "purpose" and "a common enemy." He imagines peacetime service as "a place full of bullshit and bad acting," says from hearsay that it is miserable for those who have lived through it, and is "glad I missed out on" it. (His mental picture of peacetime service is a Hollywood one, the film *Heartbreak Ridge*, which he cites; he says plainly he has never experienced it.)
*Candidate application:* A soldier whose identity is built on a war that ended; the garrison years afterward as the thing he fears more than the war.
*Cross-use:* The project's Interwar periods (the long garrison stretches between wars): a character or faction whose military purpose has shrunk to ceremony, inspections and uniform regulations can be written from this (with the caution that this is one Marine's hearsay about peacetime).

**[E19-10]. "Veteran's Day Dinner" (2011-11-11; MV-TL-10; GIF, first frame only): the veteran as comic figure of entitlement, inside a sincere frame.**
Three panels. At a chain restaurant a Marine in a Corps T-shirt asks the waitress if he gets a free dessert too and is told the promotion covers only the meal. With his mouth full (the lettering is garbled to show it) he erupts in a rant about everything he has sacrificed and the ingratitude of the whole establishment, which he labels communist. Thirty minutes later, at a second chain, he sits in the same shirt and asks the same question. Mechanism: self-satire of the veteran's entitlement, the hypocrisy shown by repetition (the rant changes nothing). The author's note supplies the guardrail: Veterans Day "isn't about free food or discounts"; it is about honoring those who served and those who died, so find a veteran of "a war long past" and shake his hand; and "don't feel bad about enjoying the free food." He also says "God bless to all veterans, old and new" and hopes the dining halls overseas have prepared something.
*Candidate application:* A veteran can mock the free-food circuit and use it, in the same hour; the joke is aimed at himself and the sincere remark about older veterans sits beside it without apology.

### From the 2012-2013 strips (MV-TL-11 to MV-TL-16)

**[E19-11]. "Back Home" (2012-01-27, #175; MV-TL-11): the unchanged civilian friend and the question that never changes.**
Three panels on one couch: a friend plays a console game while another man enters. Panel one, the man leaves for boot camp and the friend says "kill some bad guys for me." Two years later, back from Iraq in dress uniform, saying he has had "profound and life-changing" experiences, he is asked first whether he killed anyone. Two years after that, on his way to class, he says he got out of the Marines two months ago after the friend asks how the Army is treating him; he is then asked again whether he killed anyone. Mechanism: repetition inside an unchanged frame; the friend does not change, does not learn the service branch, and asks the same question each time. The note drops the joke for plain advice: friends at home will "largely be the same way they were when you left them"; "What's really changed is you … and you'll never be the same again"; home becomes "a distant memory"; the veteran should "try your best not to alienate everyone"; "if you need help with it, don't be afraid to seek it"; friends and family "will never understand, but they can try if you give them a chance."
*Candidate application:* A veteran's grief about unshared experience is delivered as advice to the next one, not as complaint; the sentence that offers help is rare in this corpus and is spoken as a peer would.

**[E19-12]. "Not Everything Leaving the Body is Weakness" (Marine Corps Times strip, posted 2012-02-14; MV-TL-12; GIF, first frame only): a physical-training slogan taken at its word.**
Three panels. A running formation is told that pain is weakness leaving the body; one runner is sick on the road; the instructor teases him about the "weakness" being out; the runner answers that it was beer and pizza. Mechanism: deflation of a motivational slogan by literal reading, with the junior's deadpan reply. The post exists only because the author was ill (an emergency-room visit) and supplied an older strip printed in the Marine Corps Times, where he had a weekly strip; he points readers to that paper.
*Candidate application:* Slogans of toughness are met with the body's actual evidence.

**[E19-13]. "Full Medical" (2012-03-02, #182; MV-TL-13): the benefit promised by the recruiter and the care denied by the barracks.**
Three panels. A recruiter (his name placard carries a crude pun) tells a prospective enlistee that the benefits are the best part, with full dental and medical care "whenever you need it." Six months later "in the fleet," a new Marine asks a Corporal if he may go to the battalion aid station because he has a fever; the Corporal says no, but he does have a drinking straw for him, and explains in crude terms that it is so he can stop complaining and endure. Mechanism: promise versus practice, with the peer enforcing the institution's real rule. The note names the mechanism: "full medical and dental benefits" are enough to get anyone to sign, but in the infantry "you avoid BAS at all costs, or are pressured into avoiding it for fear of belittlement"; "medical treatment for the infantry" is the platoon corpsman briefly looking you over and offering advice you already knew; if you do go, you are sent to "some shady overtaken barracks building" where you may not see a doctor. The sequel in the note is the key evidence: at his final physical at the headquarters battalion clinic (a good, clean one), he listed so many lingering problems the doctor had to write on the back of the form, and when she asked why none were in his record he told her "the infantry doesn't go to BAS." *(Insult terms and vulgar self-description in the note not reproduced; see the register.)* He also says two strips came out of his recent illness, and that when new acquaintances see the strip "they get an eyeful" of vulgarity.
*Candidate application:* A chronic injury with no paper trail because the unit's culture punished going sick: the cost arrives years later at the benefits office as an undocumented claim.

**[E19-14]. "Memorial Day 96" (2012-05-29; MV-TL-14; GIF, first frame only): the sanctimony check, and the author's own plain observance.**
Three panels of two Marines. One, hungover, is chided by the other: the four-day weekend ("a 96") is given to honor the fallen, not to be drunk; the hungover one, called by a barracks nickname that plays on his looks (restrained, see register), retorts that every drink was "dedicated to our Fallen brothers," and that he has had enough "guilt-tripping on Facebook" and does not need it from a fellow Marine. Mechanism: an in-group parody of the performative mourner (another veteran, not a civilian) and of the drinker's own justification, with the dead left out of the joke. The note gives three types of people: Abe (spends the day drinking as most Americans would a long weekend), Garcia (silent reflection) and a "third kind," those who post badly edited motivational pictures to make civilian friends feel guilty. He says he chose to be "more of a Garcia": with his wife he visited a veterans' memorial and left flowers. He concedes most such posts are well meant and ends: "If you had a barbecue and enjoyed yourself, more power to you."
*Candidate application:* A veteran's contempt for other veterans' public grief theater is not contempt for the dead; the humor is aimed at the performance, and the observance itself is private.

**[E19-15]. "Seek Medical Attention" (Marine Corps Birthday and Veterans Day week, 2012-11-13; MV-TL-15; GIF, first frame only): the senior NCO's over-enthusiasm as a medical condition; and the discount-day accounting.**
One strip of three panels with a doctor and a patient (a Gunnery Sergeant): a parody of the drug-advertisement warning about prolonged arousal, applied to the staff NCOs' emotional reaction to the Marine Corps birthday and Veterans Day. The doctor says many senior NCOs have come in with it ("a moto-boner," meaning over-motivation), that it is the combination of the two holidays, and that the standing treatment is transfer orders to infantry battalions: "should clear it right up." *(Sexual wording not reproduced; see the register.)* Mechanism: medicalization of a rank's zeal; target: staff NCOs' mythologizing, with the "cure" being to send them to the work the lower enlisted do. The note defends discount-day: at a pizza chain, a manager handed him and a friend a voucher; when a fan wrote that free food on Veterans Day is a shallow business ploy, he answered "This is America, dammit. I'm getting fed and people are making money, works for me."
*Candidate application:* A pragmatic veteran refuses both the guilt of taking the discount and the fan's cynicism about it; "works for me" is the register.

**[E19-16]. "Have you ever Killed Anybody?" (2013-04-26, #264; MV-TL-16): the question every returning Marine is asked, answered by a threat-joke, and what the author's note says about why it is asked.**
Three panels at a party. A man in a backward cap and chain pendant, holding a drink, says he has heard the veteran has just returned from "Iraqistan" and asks whether he killed anyone; a woman in glasses asks the same; a third man in a game-franchise T-shirt asks it a third time. The veteran answers no, then adds that he is starting to consider it (that is, killing someone), with the questioners as the implied candidates. Mechanism: the rule of three, then a deadpan answer that is neither yes nor no and that turns the questioners into the ones at risk. The note drops the joke. It cites a Marine friend (an Iraq veteran) who said the question puts the veteran in a "vulnerable position": answering gives away "a large part of your experience to someone with little to no genuine interest"; "yes" gets him written off as crazy, "no" gets him written off as unworthy; the question is "never in the interest of understanding" but of "validating their own expectations." The note ends bleakly: civilians "don't care about the war," or about counter-insurgency or the time with local people; "They don't care."
*Candidate application:* The one answer a veteran can give that costs him nothing is a dark joke; the plain statement of why the question hurts is spoken once, in the author's voice, outside the strip.

### From the 2013 to early 2014 strips (MV-TL-17 to MV-TL-22)

**[E19-17]. "Deployment Goggles II" (2013-05-14, #266; MV-TL-17): the same perceptual joke at the homecoming gate, and the author's note on how military humor pages decay.**
Two panels. A returned Marine, scarred and wide-eyed, is asked twice for his identification at a gate by a female Marine whose face we do not see; he apologizes, says he has not seen a woman in over five months and calls her the most beautiful thing he has seen; the second panel reveals her as a deliberately grotesque caricature (gap-toothed, haggard) answering coyly. Mechanism: the reveal; the target is the deprived man's judgment, but the punchline is carried by a woman's unattractiveness, so the strip leans on the same device as the first "Goggles" strip (E19-5) and the tension is plain: the author's note disclaims sexism, yet the visual joke depends on the woman's appearance. The note opens with blunt advice (in coarse language, not reproduced) to returning men: avoid anyone you would not have wanted before leaving, go home to old partners. The more useful material is a paragraph on military Facebook pages that a congresswoman had criticized: such pages "start off as funny places" where Marines and soldiers "laugh at boots and such," then "quickly degrade into endless sandwich jokes and (clearly) inappropriate and blatant misogyny"; he adds that the worn-out joke "was funny like the first (over) 9,000 times" and closes "Shit gets old, bro." He also reports he is "officially a college graduate" and says the GI Bill "worked wonders," telling post-service Marines with "no direction" to go to school because "they pay you to go." *(Sexual and coarse wording in the note not reproduced; see the register.)*
*Candidate application:* A named failure mode of group humor: a shared joke community starts as in-group wit and decays by repetition of one cheap joke into contempt for a group outside; the veteran who outgrew it says so in four words.

**[E19-18]. "Unexpected Guest II: Memorial Day" (2013-05-28; MV-TL-18; GIF, first frame shows three panels): the legend summoned to settle the argument, and the legend's own period bigotry played as a gag.**
Three panels. One Marine declines a friend's beach barbecue invitation because Memorial Day is "not National Barbecue Day": he will spend the day on social media making people feel bad; the friend calls that foolish. A ghost, Chesty Puller (a legendary Marine general whose name is a byword in the Corps, drawn glowing and descending on the scene), arrives and rules that both are right: the day is for remembering the fallen, but the fallen would have wanted the living to enjoy the freedom they paid for. In the last panel the ghost sends them off and adds a dated, racist and sexist remark about the sort of company they should find; the two Marines wince ("that was really racist") and excuse it ("a different time"). *(The ghost's remark is not reproduced; see the register.)* Mechanism: an authority from the dead hands out permission to enjoy the holiday; then the legend's own bigotry deflates the pedestal; the strip gives the myth its due and its limit in the same gesture. The note is plain on two points: Memorial Day is dedicated to remembering the fallen and "should be regarded with absolute respect," and "a moment of silence, a thought of remembrance, a deep breath in solemn respect" is enough for "any ghost of Marines past"; both camps online irritate him, civilians who deny the fallen respect and "vets and active service members alike witch-hunting and upsetting themselves over it." He closes with taking his dog to the park: "That’s my dog–he’s my best friend. I’d fight for that."
*Candidate application:* The dead are left out of the joke on principle; the joke can use the *legend* of a dead hero, including his unacceptable parts, as long as it does not mock the fallen; and the day is honored by a short silence plus ordinary living.

**[E19-19]. "Veteran Card" (2013-07-05, #275; MV-TL-19): the veteran identity used as a dodge, and the author's line between asking for gratitude and demanding it.**

**[E19-58]. Counting the 1,000 titles by theme: the serious, after-service topics the sample was chosen for are about one title in eight; most of the body of work is about other things, and over half of the titles cannot be assigned a theme from the slug at all.**
*Method statement.* The file lists 1,000 post slugs with dates (2010-01-05 to 2026-09-26). I removed the leading "terminal lance" and the strip number from each slug, then tested the remainder against twelve keyword families, **first match wins, in the order below**, using whole-word or stem matches (for example, "memorial," "rest in peace," "legends never die" for memorial; "medical," "corpsman," "BAS," "sick," "corona," "peanut butter," "body composition" for medical; "benefit," "VA," "GI bill," "tax," "retirement," "discount" for pay and benefits; "boot camp," "recruit," "MEPS," "drill instructor," "basic school" for training; "deploy," "Iraq," "combat," "war," "MRAP," "terp" for deployment; "veteran," "life after EAS," "civilian," "stolen valor" for after-service life). I then read every title assigned to the six serious families and removed false positives by hand (for example, both posts called "Civil War" are pop-culture and infantry-versus-support strips, not war strips, and a keyword for "guard post" was dropped). Titles that matched nothing are reported as "not assignable from the slug alone"; **the slug is often a bare joke title ("The Stare," "That Guy," "Literally"), so this count is a floor for every theme, not a measure.** A keyword match shows the slug's topic, not the strip's tone, and a "benefits" slug may be a pun (such as "Friends of Benefits," E19-22).

## Pattern roll-up

*Everything below is documented for one American infantry Marine's webcomic and its readers (mostly Marines and veterans of the Iraq-era, 2010-2026) and should not be generalized beyond that. Numbers are `E19-n` findings.*

**Functions**
- **Boundary and cohesion by in-group reference.** A password-strip that only those who were at the gate can decode (E19-1); "boot" and "POG" as outsider labels (E19-23, E19-27, E19-53); the barracks as a closed club whose price of admission is staying single and staying in (E19-7, E19-40); shared leisure (a squad around a laptop) as a ritual (E19-23).
- **Coping.** Bathos in the face of homesickness, followed by plain advice to stay with the group (E19-6, E19-30: "being miserable together" as the mechanism); gallows joke beside open grief in the same post (E19-25, E19-51, E19-55); "we still went" as the whole claim of dignity for a purposeless war (E19-36).
- **Rank negotiation and resistance.** The obedient-form question that exposes an absurd order (E19-8); the literal-minded junior who takes an insincere courtesy at its word (E19-45); the command's own viewpoint ("they hate you as much as you hate them," E19-33); the staff NCO's over-motivation as a medical condition (E19-15, E19-32, E19-44); privilege gaps stated in notes, not strips (officers' free phones, E19-3; the duty roster, E19-20).
- **Morale, discipline and safety.** The author defends functional standards (E19-31) and finds hidden function in petty rules after leaving (E19-49); a comic threat doubles as a heat-casualty safety brief, confirmed by a medic reader (E19-56).
- **Grief.** The dead are left out of the joke and are drawn instead as still serving, still complaining, still Marines (E19-37, E19-42, E19-50); permission is given each Memorial Day to enjoy the day, with a moment of silence as the observance (E19-14, E19-18, E19-37, E19-50); a death is mourned by quoting the dead man's own catchphrase (E19-41).
- **Identity.** The veteran taxonomized as a type, with himself included (E19-26); the veteran who feels counterfeit beside older veterans' regalia and civilians' flags (E19-21); the veteran as the one who changed while the friend on the couch did not (E19-11).

**Targets**
- *Self and own type:* the entitled veteran at the free-meal counter (E19-10, E19-19, E19-29), the vain veteran (E19-28), the veteran's outburst (E19-24), the infantryman's gatekeeping (E19-53), the lance corporals' own misbehavior (E19-33).
- *Senior NCOs and the command:* dress codes and dated tastes (E19-8, E19-49), moto zeal (E19-15, E19-32, E19-44), hollow holiday invitations (E19-45); *officers:* free phones (E19-3). *Recruiters:* the benefits promise (E19-13).
- *Institutions:* the VA's front door but not its clinicians (E19-38); the medical system's culture of not going sick (E19-13, E19-39, E19-43); boot camp (E19-27, E19-43, E19-52); the Corps' peacetime (E19-9); the wars' lack of purpose (E19-25, E19-36, E19-55).
- *Other services:* Army (E19-4), Navy corpsmen (E19-39, E19-46, E19-56), Coast Guard (E19-37).
- *Civilians:* the questioner (E19-11, E19-16), the café talker (E19-24), the preschool audience (E19-51); corporate patriotism is accepted, not attacked (E19-10, E19-15, E19-29).
- *Other veterans' mourning performance* (E19-14, E19-18, E19-42).
- *Not targeted:* the dead; the enemies are drawn as game opponents or as backdrop (E19-2, E19-25); the one dead person the author ever mocked is apologized to by name (E19-41).

**Forms and mechanics**
- Understatement ("I've had worse summers," E19-8); reductio and literal reading of rank's instructions (E19-8, E19-45); role reversal (E19-9, E19-33); the rule of three and escalation (E19-10, E19-16, E19-29); the bait-and-switch (E19-22, E19-34); a promise set against its fulfillment (E19-13, E19-38).
- Borrowed frames: console role-playing game (E19-2), film and game franchises (E19-35, E19-41, E19-55), a drug-advertisement warning (E19-15), a labeled diagram (E19-26), a horror-film shot (E19-52), a mock heroic action film (E19-47).
- Mock-bureaucracy applied to death (a contract, gear issue and a command clarification for the afterlife, E19-42, E19-50); readers extend such bits in comments (E19-50).
- Recurring named figures: the everyman Abe, a mustached senior NCO, the legendary general who returns as a ghost (E19-14, E19-18, E19-41).

**Rules of license**
- The author's own claims: humor "based on my actual experiences" (E19-2); he makes fun of "everyone equally" (E19-33); he "do[es] not hate anything other than ignorance" and shows the Corps "in the most honest light I can comprehend" (E19-21); service (infantry, Iraq twice) is the stated warrant (E19-21).
- No opinion about the recently dead (E19-28); an apology once a dead person's real work is considered (E19-41); a disclaimer plus blessing for medical workers when joking at the edge of a pandemic (E19-46); standards with a combat purpose are not mocked (E19-31).
- Mockery of civilians is bounded by the plain statement that their ignorance is the product of the service (E19-24).

**Limits and failure modes**
- Humor communities decay: pages that begin as in-group wit become repetitive contempt for outsiders and for women (E19-17).
- The veteran outburst confirms the label "messed up veteran" (E19-24).
- Entitlement parody is a self-check against the same behavior (E19-10, E19-19, E19-29).
- The author's own misses: the Ermey strip (E19-41); strips that lean on women's looks despite disclaimers (E19-5, E19-17).
- The heroic war story shrinks to trivia in memory (E19-36).
- The medical culture's concealment of injury: the infantry "doesn't go to BAS," and the cost arrives later as an empty record (E19-13, E19-38).

**Time course**
- 2010-2011: deployment and barracks (E19-1 to E19-9); 2012-2016: the unchanged friend, the killing question, the veteran type and its benefits (E19-11, E19-16, E19-19 to E19-26); 2016-2023: afterlife posts, parenthood and graduate study via the GI Bill, repeated Memorial Day and Veterans Day strips (E19-37, E19-42, E19-50, E19-52); 2023-2026: plainest posts (E19-51, E19-55).
- The grievance against a petty rule is restated twelve years later as respect for its hidden function (E19-49); the dread of peacetime (E19-9) and the later fatigue at a new war (E19-55) bracket the whole run.
- The veteran's emptiness comes back "slowly" (E19-26); the old war material overwhelms its owner fourteen years on (E19-51).

**Civilian-facing register**
- Three stances to civilians appear in successive years and do not agree: they will not understand but can try if given a chance (2012, E19-11); "they don't care" (2013, E19-16); their ignorance is the sign the job was done and the veteran should let it go (2014, E19-24). The 2018-2023 posts shift to sharing with civilians on his own terms (E19-51).
- The honest answer to "Did you kill anyone?" is the dark joke; the reason the question hurts is stated once, outside the strip (E19-16).
- Gratitude is accepted as a transaction (free meals, discounts: E19-4, E19-10, E19-15, E19-19, E19-29, E19-44), with older veterans' company kept separate from the free food (E19-10).

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics.** Nothing in the sample concerns a defector. The nearest material: the insider who criticizes the institution and claims not to hate it (E19-2, E19-21); the antiwar-leaning tone of the 2014 and 2026 war posts (E19-25, E19-55); the "us and them" veteran (E19-24); and a veterans' forum and site as a community outside the unit, set up for benefits questions as early as 2010 (E19-5, E19-48).

**Cross-cultural.** One service (Marine Corps), one country. Other armies appear only through reader comments (a conscript in a Nordic army, E19-49; an Army medic, E19-56) and the inter-service gags (E19-4, E19-37, E19-39).

**Craft observations.** A silent comic "didn't need" dialogue (E19-57); a borrowed meme template is the right tool for a repeating cycle (E19-55); drawing the ordeal as a horror film (E19-52); repeated ritual strips ("the afterlife post," "Life After EAS") build a shared vocabulary (E19-59); the author's own regret about a past strip is itself a craft note about when a character joke is unfair (E19-41).

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Sample bias.** The 60 pages were chosen by serious-topic title keywords. About 73% have a serious family as their main subject, against 12% of the title list (E19-58). Do not infer the strip's tone from the sample.
2. **The title list is probably truncated** (exactly 1,000 entries; at least a dozen titles linked from the sampled pages are missing, E19-60). All percentages are over listed titles.
3. **Keyword counts are a floor**, 56% of titles are unassignable from the slug, and a different keyword scheme would move category counts by a few points. The hand-tag of the sample (44 of 60) is my judgment; overlaps were resolved by main topic.
4. **GIF first frames only** (11 of the 57 images): MV-TL-06, 10, 12, 14, 15, 18, 21, 25, 29, 30, 32. The 2010-2014 GIF strips (holiday and commentary strips) may animate or carry later frames I could not see. Three of the 57 images are not strips (two Veterans Day title cards, MV-TL-48 and 51, and promotional game art, MV-TL-55), so 54 images are comics. MV-TL-58, 59 and 60 have no image; MV-TL-60 is a paywall stub.
5. **Image limits.** Web versions are small (about 700 px wide for the GIFs); lettering was legible but some wording is garbled by design (a Marine speaking with his mouth full, E19-10) and a few items I could not resolve: the first item in the Thanksgiving answer (E19-6), the itinerary entries in E19-29, and exactly which speaker is which in the phone-bank strip (E19-3). I described rather than guessed.
6. **Page text is a WordPress extract.** Menu links and "From the archives" boxes were skipped. For paragraphs and comments the extractor duplicated text (every comment appears twice; the 2014 "Anatomy" post has every paragraph twice). **Comment text is absent from the 2010-2018 pages** (they show only a "One response" count) and **present on 2022-2026 pages** (MV-TL-49, 50, 52, 53, 54, 56, 57). Commenters are handles with unverifiable claims; one comment is automated spam (E19-52).
7. **Dates.** Four strips were first printed in the Marine Corps Times and reposted later, so the strip's copyright year precedes the post date (MV-TL-12, 33, 36, 38). About 37 posts, including MV-TL-58 to 60, carry a September 2026 import date that is not the date of writing (E19-57). Older (2010-2011) pages were extracted from a later site template.
8. **Tensions inside the source.** The author's 2011 note says he was married in his infantry platoon; his 2017 note says he "just tied the knot" (E19-7, E19-40; unreconciled). He disclaims sexism while two strips depend on women's looks (E19-5, E19-17). His three stances toward civilians differ by year (E19-11, E19-16, E19-24). He says he "never bashed" the Corps (E19-2) yet calls the experience miserable (E19-21 and the interview in E21).
9. **Facts from outside the pages.** I glossed a few terms the pages do not explain and flagged each (the medic call "corpsman up," allergy tags, a funeral-protest figure, the likely meaning of a coarse series title). Statements about 2021 and 2026 events (the Capitol breach, a 2026 war with Iran and the Strait of Hormuz) are the author's and commenters', not verified by me, and 2026 events are beyond what I can check.
10. **What is missing.** No women authors; no officers' voices; no defector or exile content; no strip about suicide or PTSD in the sample; the only treatment of killing is the civilian's question (E19-16) and the film review (E19-32); the Iraqis appear as backdrop or as children in a photograph (E19-51). The title slugs most likely to hold the trauma-adjacent material (E19-60) were not sampled.
11. **Copyright.** Strips are copyrighted images. I paraphrased every strip; quotation is limited to a few words at a time from speech bubbles and short phrases from the author's own page text.
12. **Process note.** I ran my keyword scripts and temporary files in the agent scratchpad; four stray JSON files I created in the source folder by mistake were moved out to the scratchpad before the final listing (no source file was altered).
