**Evidence base.** The same division's officer-written paper (E08, E09a, E09b1): pen names, unsigned editors, ranks speaking only through officer-written dialect; no private soldier's own text and no images (cartoons survive only as captions, if at all). The period glosses I give (U.S. entry into the war, the Arras and Third Ypres offensives, the retaking of Bapaume) are from general knowledge and flagged as mine.

**OCR and format damage (honest report).** Column-fragmented text; headings and drop capitals lost; numerals and grid references unreliable; some names damaged. I cite **issue and line numbers ("L")** of the text file; there are no page markers. **Quotations** are exact except that single-character OCR slips are repaired silently where certain, larger repairs are in [square brackets], and British spellings are converted to American per the project rule, in quotations too.

**What it does NOT contain.** No images (the cartoons and the page ornaments are lost). The editors' own statements about the paper's purpose or about morale are few in this range (an apology, a call for copy, a book announcement, the farewell column's function); there is no passage like E08's explicit program of *esprit de corps*.

**Content handling.** No gore, atrocity or sexual violence occurs in my range. ## Findings

### A. B.E.F. Times No. 5 (10 April 1917), closing pages (L20904-21675)

**[E09b2-1]. "The Sub": a verse about the junior officer who reads the society weeklies for the advertisements.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20904-20969; signed "R.M.O.")
A rhyming portrait of the subaltern who "loves the Merry 'Tatler'" and "the Saucy 'Sketch'" (illustrated society magazines, my gloss), and who reads those papers for the advertisements of ladies' underclothing, which "fairly make him long for leave." The verse says he has acquired a whole vocabulary from "weekly papers" that he lacked before the war, and it ends with a naive resolution to bring his mother a gift from that department of fashion, with the war named as the teacher of "naughty Continental tricks." The wording of the clothing items and the final gift is paraphrased, not reproduced.
Mechanism: mock-moral portrait of a "simple youth" corrupted by the war, in which the corrupting agent is advertising and the only "vice" is shopping for leave; the last-line reveal (the gift for "mamma") turns longing into a boy's innocence. The joke gives the sexual longing of men without women a safe form: the image of a catalog. Targets: the subaltern himself (affectionately), the weeklies and their fashion columnists, and the home front as a shop window.
*Candidate application:* Desire for home, intimacy and ordinary life can be carried by a consumer object (a catalog, a brochure) so that the soldier-type's longing is stated through what he reads, not what he says.

**[E09b2-2]. Brigade Intelligence Summary No. 30: the "Note" that over-reads the innocent.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20972-21134; signed in the OCR-damaged form "Middling Old Lieut., Bde. Intelligence Officer," a pen name that makes the officer an aging junior)
A full parody of the daily summary in its standard numbered headings (Attitude of the Enemy, Enemy's Artillery, Our Artillery, Trench Mortar Activity, Sniping, Patrols, Intelligence, Reports from Other Sources). The same device runs through each: a bare fact, then a deadpan "Note" that draws a wrong conclusion. Examples: the enemy fired "12 whizbangs" at a feature known as Pope's Pimple, after which "Pope has now no pimple worthy of mention" (a landmark erased, told as a loss of vocabulary); a gas shell was answered by the sentry's "jam tin" (the gas alarm), "this proved most effective," and a Note warns the report "should be accepted with considerable reserve" because the sentry "is not remarkable for his veracity"; our own bombardment is justified by "operation order No. 3,000" and "Our casualties were slight"; a patrol sent to learn whether a sap was occupied went "ten yards" from the trench at 10 p.m., found an empty tin labeled "PLUM JAM," and was back at "10:03 p.m.," having "secured the above valuable identification," with a Note that this is "a clever ruse of the enemy to convey the impression that his rations include an issue of plum jam," and a second Note that the tin may have held jam "issued for the consumption of our own troops," though the patrol officer "has reason to believe otherwise." The observers' section records a man with a "shrapnel helmet" and a dog, and a "stout man with red face and glass eye" asleep by the road; the dog goes to the sleeper, the sleeper throws a stone at the dog, the dog disappears, and the first man is seen "wiping with a sandbag the head of the stout man," who "was now evidently thoroughly awake, and showing unmistakeable signs of anger." The Notes say "it is thought that possibly a relief was taking place" and "that possibly dogs have been trained to rouse sleeping sentries in case of alarm"; smoke at a map square is "a clever ruse of the enemy to convey the idea that a fire had been ignited." In the last item, two men wearing spectacles disappear behind a hedge, our 60-pounders fire high-explosive at the spot, the men reappear and take cover "strangely hampered in their movements," and the Note concludes the hedge "conceals a strong point of considerable importance."
Mechanism: the intelligence form's habit of finding hidden meaning in anything is turned on a series of ordinary trench sights (a tin, a sleeping man, smoke); the official observation of men under fire is carried by the single adverb "strangely"; the patrol that goes ten yards and returns in three minutes with a rubbish "identification" parodies the requirement to bring back proof. The jam is the ration joke of the whole war (E08-37). New relative to E09a-10, E09a-24, E09a-29 and E09b1-27: (a) no real text is quoted, the summary is wholly invented; (b) every item carries its own wrong inference; (c) the patrol is mocked by speed, a quiet statement about patrols that do the minimum; (d) our own artillery is described by authority number and the line "Our casualties were slight" as in the real form.
Targets: Intelligence and Operations paperwork, patrol requirements, observers, the gas sentry (a worthy fellow whose word cannot be trusted), the 60-pounders.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type who has read many real reports may mimic the "Note" voice, treating any innocent fact as evidence of a sinister plan; it is a way to say "the people upstairs find a plan in everything" without insulting any named person.

**[E09b2-3]. "How Congress Declared War": the U.S. entry as a poker game, told by a correspondent hiding behind a Chaplin picture.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21140-21197; by "Tuckis Shurtin," described as "our special correspondent")
A second correspondent parody in the line of Teech Bomas (E08-78, E09b1-10). The correspondent "managed, by the wonderful enterprise and skill always shown by him on these delicate operations," to hide behind a life-size picture of Charlie Chaplin in the White House and so "was thus present at the most momentous meeting" in American history, which he reports "in his own picturesque language." The meeting is told entirely in stage-American slang as a card game: the President "chewing the end of a five-cent che-root" says "I'm for a show-down"; the "Bull-Moose" (my gloss: the former President Theodore Roosevelt) "took the floor and bucked" and offered "half a-million boys" to "skin every Hun in Yurrup"; a politician "showed a busted straight"; another "threw in for the Bull-Moose"; the President answers that he will "hand it to Willie," and the piece ends with a college cheer, "Oh! Willie, beat it! Theo's on your track, and he's hungry."
Mechanism: war declaration as gambling and stage Western speech; the correspondent's absurd vantage point as a sample of the press's claim to know secret meetings (cf. the "Special Correspondent" at the front who is never at the front); the allies' late entry greeted with ribbing and welcome together. The national caricature (period American slang) is documented, not extended.
Targets: the press correspondent's claim of access, the late-arriving ally's brashness, the Kaiser (as "Willie"), the cinema star as a stand-in for America.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's attitude to a newly arriving ally can be teasing welcome in the idiom of the ally's own entertainment; the joke is aimed at the telling (the press's gall), not at the help.

**[E09b2-4]. "They Don't Believe": the sergeant's rum-jar song.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21205-21237)
A song parody (the refrain lines "They didn't believe me, they didn't believe me" echo a popular 1914 song of the same sort, my gloss) in the voice of a sergeant whose rum jar came up "just as full as it should be" and is now short. His claims: "Sergeants very seldom touch / Rum, at least, not very much," "Must have been the A.S.C.," "Anyway, it wasn't me!" The refrain admits that "They seem to know a sergeant's thirst, / I fear they all believe the worst," and the sergeant promises "fatigues for them where'er I be" when he finds the accusers. The last line, "The man who tapped the jar could not be me!", closes on a denial that the reader has been trained to disbelieve.
Mechanism: the rum jar (E08-20, E09a-20) seen from the NCO who serves it; the confession by denial; the NCO's own recourse, fatigues, as the only power he has; the song form lets the ranks sing it. New relative to E09a/E09b1: the first time the rum theme is voiced by the man responsible for issuing it. Targets: the sergeant (affectionately), the A.S.C., the rum ration's weak point (the jar in transit).
*Candidate application:* NCO characters are the middle layer that gets blamed from both sides; a joke about the sergeant's thirst is a joke the sergeants can make about themselves with a small threat attached.

**[E09b2-5]. Stop Press filler: the enemy's sickly Kaiser and the "food hogs."** (PW-01, No. 5, L21240-21261)
Three one-line "news" items. "Rioting is again reported in Berlin. The Kaiser has gone to bed with whooping cough and ricketts" (the enemy leader as a sickly child, in the line of E08/E09a leader-jokes); "The New German War Loan has reached the stupendous figure of 50,000 marks owing to the successful U Boat campaign" (a tiny sum named with the enemy's own boast); and "Two juvenile food hogs were arrested yesterday. On examination it was found that their pockets were full of brandy balls" (my gloss: "food hog" was the 1917 home-front term for hoarders and over-eaters, and this is a campaign aimed at the home front's own rations).
Mechanism: mock news in the shape of a column filler; the third item turns the home front's rationing panic into the arrest of children for sweets. Targets: enemy morale and finance, the U-boat claims, the home front's food policing. 
*Candidate application:* A one-line absurd arrest of children for sweets is a template for how soldiers hear about civilian shortages: as comedy about people who have never been hungry in the relevant sense.

**[E09b2-6]. "Narpoo Rom," the serial's last two chapters: the rum was never stolen.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21267-21412; the serial's name is printed "Narpoo Rom" in the OCR, "Narpoo Rum" elsewhere)
Under the banner "Our Splendid New Serial" (repeated from the No. 4 installments, E09b1-9) with the line "By the Author of 'Shot in the Culvert,'" the cast list is reprinted: Cloridy Lyme (a Sanitary Inspector), Madeline Carot (a French girl), Intha Pink (a Pioneer), General Bertram Rudolph de Rogerum (the Earl of Loose), Lord Reginald de Knellthorpe (his son), Q. Wemm (a Storekeeper), L. Plumernapple (a soldier), Herlock Shomes (the Great Detective) and Dr. Hotsam (his admirer). Most of the names are puns (chloride of lime; rogue and rum; plum-and-apple jam, my glosses, flagged as inference; the cast was introduced in E09b1-9). A chapter headed in the OCR "3" (probably 8) follows: in "the cold gray light of dawn" the sanitary inspector, working through "the stricken streets of Bapaume" (my gloss: the town retaken in the March 1917 German withdrawal) with "every appearance of profound distaste," complains in dialect: "When I joined this 'ere mob I 'ad visions of red coats and flashin' bayonets; and now I spends my time spearin' bits o' paper and orange peel on a pointed stick." A "sausage" (observation balloon) drifts by with two men hanging in the rigging; they are Shomes and Hotsam; Shomes admits "my deductions are sometimes at fault but very rarely I think," tears a scrap from the inspector's stick and has Hotsam read it; the scrap's text is lost in the OCR but appears to carry the printers' imprint of the paper itself, the same device as the torn envelope in Chapter 4 whose letters pointed at the paper's own letters column (E09b1-9). **Chapter 9, "Back at Quality Street"** is the solution. Shomes, in his bunk, says "the whole thing is so absurdly simple" and "There never was a problem, and the rum was never stolen." Hotsam asks for the reasoning; Shomes reminds him that "at the time the rum was supposed to have been stolen, it was almost impossible to buy whiskey in this country," and that Hotsam saw "the Earl and his staff many times during those trying days": "Did they strike you as men who had suddenly become total abstainers?" Hotsam answers "No, I cannot say they did." Shomes begins "Well, just think a little, my dear Hotsam, whiskey was unobtainable then, what did they—" and breaks off: "Pass the whiskey and put on the gramophone my good fellow, I think we are entitled to a tot."
Mechanism: the serial that began as a pastiche of the detective story ends on an accusation of the senior staff, spoken as a rhetorical question and not completed; the detective's last act is to drink, so the solution is both stated and dropped. The whiskey shortage (E09a-20, E09a-38 "No whiskey, no war") supplies the motive. The sanitary inspector's complaint is the retaken-town version of the infantryman's wish for glory, a small deflation that shows what recapture means in practice (tidying). Targets: the Staff (the Earl "and his staff"), the detective genre, the paper's own serial, the army's idea of glory.
*Candidate application:* The long mystery whose answer is that the brass drank the supply is a structurally clean story for a soldier-type: the unsolved "theft" is a grievance, and the solution is a joke the brass would be embarrassed to hear.

**[E09b2-7]. "Au Revoir": the farewell column becomes a standing feature, for the departing Commander Royal Engineers.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21422-21452)
The editors write: "'Tis sad but true that with nearly every issue of the paper we have to reserve this column in order to say good-bye to some distinguished member of the Division." This time it is the "late C.R.E.," Brig.-General A. Craven, congratulated on "his hard earned and well-deserved promotion." The editors add that he was "a very good friend to us all, and a staunch supporter of the paper," and that "the whole Division appreciates his ever ready assistance, and the consideration he has always shown both in work and play." A line "Good luck and God speed" stands alone at L21481 after the next item and probably belongs to this farewell.
Mechanism: plain register, no joke; the structural statement is the editors' admission that senior officers pass through the division at the rate of one per issue, so the paper's standing function includes witnessing departures (cf. E09a-18, E09a-27, E09b1-11). Targets: none. Signs of what cannot be joked about: a senior who backed the paper is farewelled in the formal register, as in each earlier farewell.
*Candidate application:* A unit culture that treats a patron's promotion with a plain public notice, set beside daily jokes, shows where its sincerity lives.

**[E09b2-8]. "Correspondence" by "Fed-Up": artillery as a neighbor's nuisance.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21455-21478)
A letter to the Editor from a "peaceful citizen, although somewhat behindhand with my rates," complaining of "the increasing rowdiness of the district": "when a quiet citizen wishes to cultivate his own small holding, it is not quite the thing to plant a 12-inch howitzer in the middle." He announces he will vote "against the present candidate at the forthcoming election."
Mechanism: the local-politics complaint letter (as in E08-12) with a heavy gun as a municipal nuisance and the war as an election; one of the paper's stock voices, "Fed Up," whose complaint takes the form of a ratepayer's. The target is the army's disregard for the "district" (the civilians' field and the soldier's own sense of living in a place that is not his), and the shelling itself.
*Candidate application:* Complaints that treat danger as bad neighborliness are the lowest-cost form of venting; they keep the speaker in the role of ratepayer, not victim.

**[E09b2-9]. The "Result of Competition": the unpayable prizes and the line that "money is not everything."** (PW-01, No. 5, L21487-21512)
The paper announces prizes of "10,000,000 Francs," "5,000,000" and "2,500,000" "won by the first three contestants in our Mammoth Competition," who "reclaimed the checks," which "owing to a slight misunderstanding with Cox & Co." were "returned R/D." The Competition Editor invites the winners to send in the old checks, "new ones will be issued in lieu," and hopes the new ones "will meet with a better fate, but, after all, MONEY is not everything."
Mechanism: the rigged-contest gag (E08-54, E09a-5) now finished by bounced checks; the army's agent bank (Cox & Co., E08-76) as the paper's creditor; the philosopher's closing line. Targets: the paper's finances and the official prize culture.
*Candidate application:* An institution that offers impossible rewards for dull tasks is a source of comedy among soldiers who know the paymaster's real limits.

**[E09b2-10]. "Business Announcement": Mr. Poile's coupons and the instruction to "hope on."** (PW-01, No. 5, L21515-21551)
"Mr. Poile (late Poile and Trotter) begs to inform his numerous clients that owing to the shortage of paper he is unable to supply his popular yellow coupons as hitherto. The few he still has in hand are booked up for the next two years." Applicants are told "to ensure smooth running after this period expires" to "hope on, and send in their names and qualifications. Something may happen and then again it may not." A couplet closes the notice: "If at first you don't succeed, / Carry on and take no heed."
Mechanism: the tradesman's notice as rationing; the text does not say what the "yellow coupons" are, and I cannot identify them. A plausible reading (my gloss, unconfirmed) is leave or another scarce, queued-for item, since the notice describes a waiting list with a two-year backlog and an invitation to apply on "qualifications"; the "Something may happen and then again it may not" is the tone in which the paper has already talked about leave (E08-52, E09b1-4). The same "Poile" firm advertises "Ten Days in Blighty" as its travel department's principal feature in the August number (E09b2-14), which supports the leave reading. The "Carry on" couplet is a catchphrase of the period used as a joke ending.
Targets: queues and quotas, the paper shortage as general excuse.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type may speak of any scarce thing in the form of an apologetic waiting-list notice; the form itself carries the knowledge that the wait will not end.

**[E09b2-11]. "Cage Hotels, Limited": the prisoner-of-war cage as a holiday resort for the enemy.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21554-21586)
A resort advertisement recommends "any of these Hotels for a SUMMER HOLIDAY to all Gentlemen (?) of German nationality who are in need of a real rest after the noise and nerve strain attendant on life in the trenches." The hotels are "pleasantly and airily situated, in pretty parts of France," with "Electric and Barbed Wires Throughout," "Good Shooting in the Vicinity" and "Attendance Free." The advertisement's last couplet is "You'll find our charges very light, / Compared with those you had last night."
Mechanism: genre substitution (the holiday brochure), with the cage's real features (wire, guards) recast as amenities and the couplet recalling the enemy's night under our bombardment. The phrase "a real rest after the noise and nerve strain" is a plain statement of what the writers themselves want, aimed at the enemy as a way of saying it; the enemy is cast as a co-sufferer, as in E08-50. Targets: the enemy's soldier as a man who needs a rest, the army's prisoner policy, the sentimentality of resort advertising.
*Candidate application:* A character may offer the enemy the gift of rest in a mock invitation, using the enemy as the vessel for what the character cannot say about his own exhaustion.

**[E09b2-12]. "Emo's Fruity Ports": the patent-medicine ad for subalterns, with the evening raid as a symptom.** (PW-01, No. 5, L21589-21622)
"C.O.s LOOK AT YOUR SUBALTERNS' TONGUES! Do they suffer from headache and feel depressed in the morning? Do they want to go over the top in the evening? What they want is: Emo's Fruity Ports. Get a bottle to-day!" The tag is "If your Subs are out of sorts, give them Emo's Fruity Ports." (The name echoes a well-known fruit-salt remedy, my gloss.)
Mechanism: the quack advertisement (E09a-6, E09b1-16) with a twist: the symptoms list shows the daily pattern of the young officer (depressed in the morning; eager to attack at night) and prescribes drink, with the commanding officer as the addressee who must look at his juniors' tongues. The wish "to go over the top in the evening" is presented as a state that a bottle produces, the same function as "Dutch courage" in E09a-48. Targets: alcohol as an officer's medicine, the junior officer as a patient, the commanding officer's care.
*Candidate application:* The senior who "doctors" his juniors with drink shows how a unit can officially treat dread as a stomach complaint.

### B. B.E.F. Times Vol. 2 No. 1 (Wednesday 15 August 1917), opening (from L21679)

**[E09b2-13]. The new masthead: the paper's family tree, and the theater bill datelined "Cloth Hall, Wipers."** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L21679-21723)
The masthead reads "THE B.E.F. TIMES. WITH WHICH ARE INCORPORATED The Wipers Times, The 'New Church' Times, The Kemmel Times & The Somme-Times. No 1. Vol 2. Wednesday, August 15th, 1917. Price 1 Franc." The front page's first item is a theater bill: "CLOTH HALL, WIPERS. Under Entirely New Management. THE VENTILATION OF THIS THEATER HAS BEEN ENTIRELY OVERHAULED DURING THE SUMMER MONTHS." The special attractions are "Haig's Company in a Stirring Drama, Entitled: PILKEM'S PROGRESS," and "William's Troupe: 'The Cockchafers'" (the scene description is lost in the OCR). The bill ends "PRICES AS USUAL. BOOK EARLY. WE HAVE A BOMB PROOF CELLAR IN THE EVENT OF AIR-RAIDS."
Mechanism: the masthead recites the paper's own history as a merger of former titles, so the paper's survival is shown by its list of dead and living names; the theater bill is the old civilian-genre substitution of E08-5 and E09a-53 (resort sheets, music-hall bills) and shows that the division is back at Ypres, the paper's original home, after the four-month gap. "Pilkem's Progress" puns on the devotional classic and names the Pilckem sector (my gloss: the opening of the Third Battle of Ypres, 31 July 1917, two weeks before the issue); the ventilation joke refers to a ruined building with no roof; the bomb-proof cellar completes the gag. "Under Entirely New Management" is the paper's way of saying the division has been through a change of command or sector without describing it. "Prices as usual" echoes E08's tourist ads. Signs of what cannot be joked about: no mention of the battle's casualties in the bill; the offensive is a "stirring drama."
*Candidate application:* The return to a destroyed place is announced by reopening the old civilian-genre joke for it; a unit that is sent back to where it started uses the same jokes, now worn.

**[E09b2-14]. "Poile and Co., Universal Providers": the travel department whose "principal feature" is leave.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L21726-21759)
An advertisement on the opening page: "THIS EMINENT FIRM HAS RECENTLY TAKEN OVER LARGER AND MORE LUXURIOUS PREMISES, AND HAS OPENED A NEW BRANCH. WE CAN NOW SUPPLY ALL YOUR WANTS," followed by "DO YOU WANT YOUR CROPS GATHERED? WE CAN HELP YOU." (the text does not say what the crops are, and I cannot resolve it). The firm's boast is "CAREFUL ATTENTION TO BUSINESS, POLITENESS AND DILIGENCE," and then: "OUR TRAVEL DEPARTMENT IS STILL OUR MOST POPULAR BRANCH, AND OUR PRINCIPAL FEATURE IS STILL AS MUCH IN DEMAND AS EVER," the feature being "TEN DAYS IN BLIGHTY," with the small print "THE ROUND TRIP DOES NOT INCLUDE HOTEL EXPENSES, DRINKS, ETC., BUT A COMFORTABLE JOURNEY IS GUARANTEED." The address is given as "KEW DOVECOTE."
Mechanism: leave sold as a package tour, a joke that began in E08-52 and continues at E09b1-4; the phrase "round trip" quietly states that leave is a loop that ends at the same place; the guarantee limited to "a comfortable journey" is a pointed understatement beside the leave-train joke later in this issue (E09b2-29). **This ad shows that the "Poile" firm of the April number (E09b2-10) sells leave among its goods in the August number, which supports reading the "yellow coupons" there as leave documents (my inference, still unconfirmed).** Targets: leave administration, the "universal provider" idiom, the paper's own shop-front.
*Candidate application:* A character can speak of the single desirable thing in the language of a mass-market travel product, which keeps the wish public and the cost (the "round trip") visible.

**[E09b2-15]. The "Exploration Co., Ltd." fisheries prospectus and the "Rabbits!" ad: food as a thing to be found.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L21762-21799; the two ads are interleaved column by column in the OCR, so I cannot assign every line)
One ad is a speculator's prospectus for "extensive fisheries in the district" and a planned fleet, saying that "The district is free from the U Boat menace" and inviting readers to "READ OUR BROCHURE: 'From Ditch to Dug-out,'" with the couplet "When in trenches you can wish / For nothing but a nice fresh fish." The other, headed "RABBITS!" three times, announces "NEW SEASON'S STOCK HAS JUST ARRIVED, AND WE ARE DISPOSING OF THESE TASTY LITTLE BEASTS AT COST PRICE," with "TRY A TENDER YOUNG BUCK RABBIT," sold by "Bunter & Co." at "Supply Villa." The address block gives a telegram name, "Pioneers," and a telephone number, "42 and 59 Hop." (both pastiche).

(My gloss: "dug-out" also meant a retired officer called back into service; the text does not say so, but the Area Commandant entries read that way.)
Mechanism: the false derivation as an inventory of a unit's contempt: Staff by their dress and perks, military police by their manners, rear-area commanders by their housing; the "trench" entry places the infantry as the speakers of "trenchant remarks," i.e. complainers in the paper's own voice. "Reason obscure" is a repeated tag for unearned perks. Targets: the G.O.C. and Staff (by uniform and honors), military police, Area Commandants, the camouflage industry.
*Candidate application:* Mock dictionaries give a ready form for a unit's private vocabulary; each entry maps the group's attitude to a rank, a role or an object in one line.

**[E09b2-32]. "Our Short Story": the one-line tale of a teetotal quartermaster.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L22670-22677)
Under the heading "Our Short Story" the whole item is "There once [lived, the verb is damaged in the OCR] a teetotal Q.M.," followed by a row of asterisks and "THE END."
Mechanism: the story is ended at the point where the improbable subject has been named; the asterisks do the work of what would happen next, and the implication (that no such man exists, or that his story would not be printable) is left to the reader. The device resembles the unsayable story at Christmas (E09a-26). It also continues the paper's theme of the rum jar and the supply chain (E09a-20, E09b2-4, E09b2-27). Targets: the quartermaster as a drinker.
*Candidate application:* A story told in one line and cut off is a way to hand the listener a joke whose punchline is shared knowledge.

**[E09b2-33]. "Our Sale or Exchange Column": small ads that state the wish to leave.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L22686-22745)
The editors introduce it: "This branch has been established for the convenience of our subscribers and all small wants are advertised at a reasonable charge." Five ads:
1. "WANTED.—To rent for the winter season, DRY WARM DUG OUT. Must be commodious and in healthy locality; untroubled by hawkers and Huns. Good price offered for suitable residence. Apply.—Reggie, c/o of this paper." (The winter is expected to be spent in the line.)
2. "FOR SALE.—TWO TANKS. Slightly soiled. Price moderate. Or would exchange for a pair of rabbits. Apply.—41, Dammstrasse." (The German-sounding address makes the seller the enemy, or a pun on "damn"; the text does not say.)
3. "FOR SALE.—PLEASANT COUNTRY ESTATE, situated in one of the nicest parts of Belgium. Heavily wooded. Has been shot over. Owner desirous of leaving. Apply.—Feddup, Glencorse Wood." (The estate agent's phrases "heavily wooded" and "shot over" are literal in a place of stumps, and "desirous of leaving" is the wish, as in E08-35.)
4. "FOR EXCHANGE.—TWO FIELD DRESSINGS, in good condition, and ONE IODINE AMPOULE unused. Would exchange for HATBAND and TABS (red or blue—the latter preferred.) Write.—Harassed, Canada Street." (The soldier offers his wound kit, the sign of those who may need it, in return for the badges of a staff officer; the text does not explain "blue.")
5. "GENTLEMAN, Young, in sixth form at Oxford and Cambridge Colleges, feels that his talents are wasted in the P.B.I. Would like a job cleaning the windows of a leave boat. Apply.—One Pip, Krab Krawl."
6. "SEVERAL YOUNG GENTLEMEN, with University degrees (Heidelburg) would like to be taken as boarders in English country districts. Would do agriculture labor, in exchange for lodging and keep. Apply.—Fritz, Lens." (German prisoners as boarders and farm hands; compare E09b2-11.)
Mechanism: the classified ad with small wants (E09a-16, E09a-33, E09a-46) used for honest requests: shelter for the winter, an estate to leave, a trade of one's first aid for a staff badge, a menial job on the leave boat that would at least put the man on the way home. The enemy appears twice in the column as another party who also wants out (the "Huns" as annoyers of a dugout seeker, and "Fritz" asking to be boarded). Targets: the winter, the Salient as an estate, Staff insignia, the infantry's "wasted talents," the enemy as a fellow petitioner. Signs of what could not be joked about: a first-aid kit for a staff badge is the item closest to the underlying fear of being wounded, and it is stated as a swap.
*Candidate application:* Small-ad format lets a character state many wishes (shelter, a staff post, a boat job) in the mode of a trader; none of them need be said in earnest.

**[E09b2-34]. "Cricket" by Mr. F. B. Pilsen: the florid sports-reporter's idiom for a regimental match.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L22748-22796)
A report of a match between "the lads from the Midlands" and "the lads from Kent," in the sports-page's synonym habit: the wickets are "the guardians of the ash grove" and "Number One's timber yard," the ball is "the elusive pillule," and "Mr. Extras" is "responsible for the majority" of 23 runs; the report calls cricket "the King of Sports," mentions "the lanky American expert," and ends with "the lads from the Strawberry County only clicked by three chips." The text does not say which units or divisions were involved.
Mechanism: parody of the sports reporter's thesaurus, and the evidence that the division played cricket in the weeks around the August offensive; neither war nor enemy appears in the piece. It continues E08/E09a's turf and cricket reports as an everyday register. Targets: the sports page. 
*Candidate application:* A veteran's attachment to sport as a normal thing is evidence of what the unit held onto; the joke is only about words.

**[E09b2-35]. A verse on night raids: the evening's "more" rain and the noise that does not pass.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L22799-22809; unsigned)
"When the golden sun sets in the West, / And the sausages all go to roost, / Tho' the rain may be o'er, / We shall soon have some more, / And the Hun comes with bombs us to boost, / When the Archies should all be in bed, / They are kicking up Hell with a zest, / And the noise of the day / Hasn't all passed away, / From my camouflaged tent in the West."
Mechanism: a pastoral evening verse (sunset, birds to roost, children to bed) with the observation balloons as birds and the anti-aircraft guns as the household that will not go to sleep; "Tho' the rain may be o'er, we shall soon have some more" folds the bombs into the weather, and the final line gives the speaker's place as a tent. The verse is about sleep: day noise does not stop at night. Targets: air raids, anti-aircraft guns, the "camouflaged tent" as a home.
*Candidate application:* The unit's complaint can be the lack of quiet at the end of the day, not the danger; a character may mention sleep before he mentions fear.

**[E09b2-36]. "The Prussian Press": mock-translated enemy newspapers that are wrong about Britain and boastful about their own side.** (PW-01, Vol. 2 No. 1, L22815-22883)
The editors preface: "We reproduce below a few extracts from the Prussian papers which have fallen into our hands lately. They all tend to show the wilful misrepresentation of facts which is rife in the German Press." Four extracts with mock titles (names of food and abuse, flagged below):
1. A Berlin daily says British "secret agents report that in many places the people are getting so thin that gratings in the streets are being carefully fenced off," and that Parliament's "Ladies' Grill" is closing because the restaurant is short of chops and steaks.
2. A second paper says that "the last reserves of man power" of the British are used up and "the English pigs are calling up children now," as one agent "yesterday saw one of eight years in the abominable khaki."
3. A third reports a speech by Tirpitz praising German sailors who "sing twice daily the Hymn of Hate," while the British fleet "stay outside and nothing can tempt them up the Kiel Canal," so the sailors "will put up with the disappointment, and sing the Hymn three times daily in future."
4. A fourth reports the Kaiser's address to "his brave Pomeranians," in mock-German word order: "once more you have a victory over the Pig-dog enemy gained. You waited till they nearly-up-to-you were, yet could they not catch you." "Your bread ration doubled-be-shall when our gallant U Boats bring us flour." The close is "Take my blessing and pull in your belts. I'm going to dine."
Mechanism: translation parody (word order, hyphen-chains, invented newspaper titles), the enemy's propaganda made ridiculous by its own claims (the victory is a retreat; the starved are told to tighten belts by the man who dines); the Kaiser appears again as the leader who eats while his men are hungry. The first two extracts show how the British home-front hunger and conscription were read in the enemy press as victories, with the child-soldier claim a bitter detail inside a comic frame. The mock newspaper names and the epithets "pig" and "pig-dog" are period abuse, noted and not extended. Targets: the German press and its propaganda, Tirpitz, the Hymn of Hate, the Kaiser's food, U-boat promises.
*Candidate application:* Mock-propaganda from the other side is a place for a unit to say what it fears the enemy believes; the form is safe because the accused is the liar.

**Forms and mechanics.** Fictional official form with deadpan notes (the intelligence summary); correspondent's purple prose against a flat last sentence; song parody; mock translation; mock etymology; classified ads; patent-medicine parody; resort and theater bills; turf report; cricket report; pastoral verse; one-line story; mock parliamentary report with audience reactions in parentheses; the last-couplet reveal (mustache); the swallowed oath (a dash, then "let's turn to brighter subjects"); the asterisked oath; the editor's flat refusal to elaborate.

**Rules of license (as visible).** (1) The paper's seniors are teased by role or type ("Any Infantry Brigadier," the G.O.C. as "gold or carrots," the Q.M.), and the new G.O.C. and the old "Professor" are each described plainly and warmly [E09b2-16, E09b2-21, E09b2-31]. (2) A departing senior who supported the paper is written plainly [E09b2-7]. (3) Loss is stated in one clause and then the subject is closed, and the named dead are placed in a block of their own [E09b2-16, E09b2-28]. (4) The war is a nuisance to the paper, not a subject to be argued ("when it interferes with the publication of a journal it's a——") [E09b2-17]. (5) The peace politician is mocked and the wish for peace is conceded by cheering (my inference) [E09b2-24]. (6) Swearing is dashed or asterisked, and "Hell" is printed [E09b2-17, E09b2-21, E09b2-29, E09b2-35]. (7) The enemy's soldier may be given the same wish to leave as the speaker, and the enemy's leaders may be given the fear [E09b2-11, E09b2-26, E09b2-33, E09b2-36]. (8) Units are not named when "it isn't done" (E09b2-22), and the paper's own jokes about the censor are absent from this range.

**Limits and failure modes.** The paper cannot keep to schedule (a four-month gap, "scratch" number) [E09b2-16, E09b2-17]; the editors' leave ends in "bare-faced robbery" and perhaps fear of raids [E09b2-18]; the senior's praise is routine and he knows it [E09b2-21]; the hopes of summer are told in the past tense [E09b2-23]; a sixth-form "talent" is wasted in the infantry [E09b2-33]; a first-aid kit is offered in exchange for a staff badge (the nearest the range comes to naming fear of being hit) [E09b2-33]; the friendly-fire "short round" is a rule not to argue [E09b2-27]; the story of the teetotal quartermaster has no continuation [E09b2-32]; no account of the Third Ypres opening appears except through jokes about telling it.

**Time course (tone across my range).**
- *April 1917, the closing pages of No. 5:* a spring issue whose humor is still the Staff, the rum jar, the home-front press and the ration (jam); the U.S. entry is a poker game, the enemy's captured soldiers are offered a holiday, the serial ends on an accusation of the senior staff, and the farewell column notes that departures come with "nearly every issue" [E09b2-2, E09b2-3, E09b2-6, E09b2-7, E09b2-11]. The leave joke is still, on one reading, "Poile's coupons" (queued, unobtainable) [E09b2-10].
- *The four-month gap:* the August editorial blames "an awkward providence and more war."
- *15 August 1917, Vol. 2 No. 1:* the paper is back at Ypres under "entirely new management" and with a new commander [E09b2-13, E09b2-16]. The humor is now more *about the telling of the offensive* (the correspondent, the dancer's letter, the race report, the Brigadier's song) and *the Staff*, and a plain register is closer to the surface than in the winter issues: the editorial's "more than a fair proportion of the old brigade," the memorial block, "Roads" and the Brigadier's "remorse" [E09b2-16, E09b2-21, E09b2-23, E09b2-28]. No serial appears in the part of Vol. 2 No. 1 that I read, and the pages are filled with short forms (classifieds, a one-line story, mock dictionary). Peace politics now appear as a target (Stockholm; the "Labor" meeting) [E09b2-17, E09b2-24]. The paper plans its own bound edition [E09b2-19].
- *Overall (E09b1 to E09b2):* from April to August the jokes on the Staff, rum and leave persist; the editors' statements shift from "au revoir" in a business-notice frame (E09b1-17) to a statement of heavy loss and a closing refusal to elaborate; the paper begins to document itself (masthead, book) and to speak of what will be remembered ("Roads").

**Civilian-facing register.** The home front is a place of food hogs, profiteers and restaurateurs, society magazines and prizes; London is expensive and possibly dangerous; the visiting American dancer is a stage version of the outsider who arrives at the front to be amused and ends up part of the circuit of the leave train and the ruins [E09b2-1, E09b2-5, E09b2-18, E09b2-29]. The press (the correspondent, the "high-brow pens") is the home front's instrument and the paper writes to correct it [E09b2-22, E09b2-29].

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** Very little in this range, and none that corresponds to leaving the army. The nearest items are: (a) the enemy soldier as someone who wants out (cage "hotels," "boarders") [E09b2-11, E09b2-33]; (b) the wish to be elsewhere stated in classifieds (an estate "desirous of leaving," a leave-boat window cleaner) [E09b2-33]; (c) the peace politicians as a figure the paper rejects while conceding the wish [E09b2-24]; (d) the infantryman who offers a first-aid kit for a staff badge, a swap that expresses wanting to leave the line by promotion (my inference) [E09b2-33]; (e) the editorial's "good luck to those who have left us," the departed as members still on the paper's list [E09b2-19].

**Cross-cultural.** One army (British, one division's officers' paper), August 1917 and April 1917, Western Front, with foreign voices only as parody (an American dancer; mock-German newspapers; a stage-American Congress). Do not generalize.

**Craft observations (editors' own and by inference).** End a purple passage on a flat sentence ("In fact we attacked at dawn"). Put the reveal in the last couplet (the mustache) or the last word (the rum). Let a senior's praise carry one parenthetical doubt. Put the dead in a block with no device and keep it separate from the jokes. State a loss in a single clause and stop. Use a mock translation to make the enemy's own claims ridiculous. Let classified ads say the wishes the editorial cannot. Keep a single-line story short enough that the missing part is the joke. Cross-reference earlier jokes (Reggie, Cox & Co., the Professor) so a returning reader finds the old world still there.
