## Source-shape notes (read first)

**PW-03, part 2 (L6,400-8,001).** The back third of the anthology that E11a described (front half: Birdwood, the Editor's Note, the literary items "The Landing" to the beginning of "The Book of Jobs"). Evidence base is the same: a volunteer, editor-selected, soldier-written souvenir collected 15 November to 8 December 1915 at Anzac, with a large majority of private contributors (Editor's Note, per E11a-2), published for a home public with footnotes that translate slang. This range holds: the end of the Genesis-style "Book of Anzac Chronicles" (items 2 and 3), eight verse and prose pieces ("The Silence" to "When It's All Over...."), six **official orders** and a block of **telegrams** (the commanders' own voice, printed straight), four unseen cover designs, then the book's **parody back matter**: "Correspondence" (two letters), "Anzac Fashions" (a captioned fashion column), "Answers to Correspondents" (a mock advice column), and "Advertisements" (public notices, personals, wanted, a variety-theater bill, and mock commercial advertisements). The back matter is the densest joke material in the whole book and is genre parody throughout. **I read every line from L6,400 to L8,100**; from L8,002 onward the file is the Project Gutenberg license text, which I confirmed and skipped as boilerplate.

**What PW-03 part 2 does NOT contain.** No pictures (many captions stand alone, and two or three jokes depend on drawings I cannot see, flagged where they occur); no joke about a named dead comrade; no mention of shell shock, desertion or executions by name; no sexual material beyond a pun-name on a variety bill and an implied bawdy limerick series that the item refuses to describe; no mention of the evacuation in the verse and sketches (the commanders' orders mention it, the comic items do not). The two pieces I treat as most reliable for *how the men's humor sounds* are those with enlisted bylines ("The Growl," "Sing!," "When It's All Over....," the Fashions captions). The Answers and Advertisements are signed in part by identified sergeants and bombardiers (Pte. T. Colles; Sergt. Noonan, editor of the earlier trench paper *Dinkum Oil*) but are otherwise unsigned.

**Format and citation.** No page markers in the text file. I cite the **printed page from the Contents list** (e.g., "printed p. 142") plus line numbers ("L") of the text file. Illustrations appear as bracketed captions only; I report captions and say when the joke must lie in the drawing.

**Quotation conventions.** Exact except: (a) British spellings converted to American per the project rule ("honored," "sympathize," "neighboring," "pajamas"), in quotations and titles too; (b) square brackets mark my insertions; (c) a few archaic or place spellings left as printed; (d) **graphic, ethnic, or bodily material is summarized, not quoted** (see the final register); (e) period dialect spellings (apostrophe-initial words, "w'en," "wot") are kept. **Period national, racial and religious caricature is documented, not endorsed.** Glosses of slang, units, or real people that the text does not explain are mine and marked "my gloss."

**PW-04, Bairnsfather (L1-1,342 of the file; license text after L1,350).** The Project Gutenberg transcription (eBook #25951, proofread by volunteers, released 2008) of the 1917 G. P. Putnam's Sons (New York and London) edition of *Fragments from France*, the collection of a captain's weekly cartoons from the British front, drawn for the illustrated magazine *The Bystander* (the Foreword says so and a caption, "Getting the Local Color," says the drawing is a "weekly 'Fragment'"). Its contents: a Foreword by *The Bystander*'s editor (L72-219) and **157 captioned cartoon blocks** (L221-1325) in the printed order, with a transcriber's note (L1328-1342) that two half-pages were rejoined and that punctuation was kept to match the drawings. Period covered by internal clues: from the Loos offensive of September 1915 to 1917 (Verdun, Fricourt in July 1916, tanks, "Romance, 1917"); the Putnam imprint is 1917, so a 1918 date in my brief cannot be met by this file. Evidence base: one officer-cartoonist, a civilian magazine's audience first and a soldier readership second (the Foreword quotes two letters of reception), the British Army's western-front trenches. **What the file does NOT contain:** the drawings (which carry the faces, the scale of peril and, in many items, the entire joke); page numbers; any prose by Bairnsfather beyond captions and the cartoonist's brief notes inside the blocks (the Foreword is an editor's); dates for individual cartoons; any named dead British soldier; sexual material. Only the captions, titles and the occasional in-drawing text (advertisement panels, a film card, a correspondence-course ad) survive, so I report them as captions and say where the joke must be in the picture. The book's "Old Bill" is named twice in this text.

**PW-06, Sassoon (L1-1,451 of the file; license text after L1,459).** The Project Gutenberg transcription (eBook #8930) of the 1918 American edition of Siegfried Sassoon's *Counter-Attack and Other Poems*, with an introduction by the soldier-poet Robert Nichols (written in New York, "Nov. 20th-23rd," year not printed), a French epigraph from Barbusse's *Le Feu*, and a dedication to Robert Ross. **39 poems** are printed in the order of the contents list. Evidence base: one officer-poet (captain, Royal Welsh Fusiliers, Military Cross, per Nichols), writing satirical and anti-war verse for a home audience in 1917-18; two poems carry a date or place ("The Rear-Guard," Hindenburg Line, April 1917; "Survivors," Craiglockhart, October 1917). **What the file does NOT contain:** any prose by Sassoon himself; any statement by him on humor; the other ranks' own words except as quoted speech inside his poems; any date for most poems. Most of the 39 poems are serious or graphic and are listed, not quoted (E11b-64). One reported soldier's reaction to an earlier volume is carried by Nichols's introduction only (E11b-45).

## Findings

### Part I. PW-03, *The Anzac Book*, lines 6,400-8,001

#### A. The end of the Genesis-style chronicle and its companion parable

**[E11b-1]. "The Book of Jobs," verses 6-19: the whole campaign as scripture, with a commander's nickname, a house-brand of vulgarity, and a pointed mention of the press.** (PW-03, "The Book of Anzac Chronicles: 2. The Book of Jobs," W. R. Wishart, No. 1 Aust. Stat. Hosp., printed p. 136; L6400-6462, continuing from E11a-69)
Verses 6-19 narrate the landing and the months after in King James cadence. The enemy "were a stubborn folk, for they had builded unto themselves dug-outs and trenches on the land of their forefathers" (v. 6; the writer concedes the defenders' standing, as E11a-69 noted). The troops "did that which was right in the sight of the 'Boss,' for they used their 'Blocks' and held the ground, which seemed impossible to those not possessed of faith in his judgment" (v. 9); whether "faith in his judgment" is sincere or faintly ironic cannot be settled from the verse, and the tone around it is affectionate. The living conditions get Biblical monstrosities: the troops "burrow holes into the earth like unto the rabbit" (v. 10); "the Valley of Shrapnel was even as Gehenna, fraught with many dangers to the unwary" (v. 11); the pioneers "prepare a track crooked, making it thereby difficult, yea, insurmountable" (v. 11); the Brigades are "human pack-horses" reinforced by "the Lost Horse Regiments" (v. 12; my gloss: dismounted Light Horse, the text does not say so); "the host of Birdwood flourished amazingly, even to the extent of rum and porridge" (v. 13); December "waxed 'plurry' cold, even unto a fall of snow, and the erstwhile Land of Jacko did breed much 'flue' and 'pneu'," and the sickness is likened to the plagues of ancient Egypt (v. 14; the verse uses a period ethnic short-form for the Egyptians, which I do not reproduce); "the Iodine Infantry were magnanimous with their potions" (v. 15; a nickname for the medical corps, tied to the Editor's iodine brushes). Verse 18 is the sharpest: "Now, the rest of the Acts of Kitchener, and all that he did and said, have they not been written in the Peninsula Press and other vaporous rags, erstwhile our 'filthy contemporaries'?" (a Kings-and-Chronicles formula applied to the press; the text does not explain "Peninsula Press"). The last verse seals the genre: "this is the Dinkum Book of Jobs, as will be written in the Book of Revelations" (v. 19). Mechanism: the chain of command's orders and the soldier's slang (Blocks, plurry, flue, pneu, dinkum, rum and porridge) sit inside a venerable register, and the sacral cadence turns every issue (rum, porridge, a snowfall, a crooked path) into a divine act. Targets: the press, the new weather, the tracks the engineers build (the "crooked" order), the supply of rum.
*Candidate application:* The unit's own scripture-parody places the commander in a role affectionate enough to take a nickname ("Birdie," "Boss") while the press is the enemy of the narrative. A defector who left an institution may still carry its register for mocking the press that reports it.

**[E11b-2]. "The Perfectly True Parable of the Seven Egyptians": scriptural cadence around a safety lesson about discarded munitions.** (PW-03, Capt. A. Alcorn, No. 1 A. S. Hospital, printed p. 138; L6465-6494)
A group of Egyptian laborers reaches Anzac and, lacking light, one goes to look for fuel "that peradventure had been washed to the beach and had escaped the claws of Apollyon, the Camp Commandant." He finds a tin can with a wick and takes it to be a lamp of his forefathers; when it will not light, they throw it on the fire. The outcome is a crash and a number of casualties, stated flatly in the final verse before the moral (I summarize the outcome and the stereotyped nickname the text uses for the men and do not quote them). The moral, in the same cadence: "Verily it is not meet at Anzac to put to 'base' uses such jam-tin bombs and other trifles as Apollyon abandoneth, even when you find them kicking about on the seashore." Mechanism: a Biblical-parable frame ("Perfectly True") carries a hard fact of beach life (improvised bombs lying about) as a warning dressed as a joke; the victims are the colonial laborers and the cautionary lesson is aimed at everyone. The "Camp Commandant" as Apollyon (Revelation's destroying angel) is a rank-and-file jab at the camp's administration, without a name. The piece shows the register's limits: the body count is a punchline, and the victims are not the writer's own. This is the book's one documented case of the Anzac comic voice using a foreign laborer's death as the joke's turn.
*Candidate application:* A soldier culture's joke about the casualties of an *out-group* worker (hired labor, locals) is a different thing from jokes about its own casualties; the book gives the out-group one a straight, almost safety-officer, delivery. Treat as evidence of boundary lines in the humor, with no apology implied.

#### B. Verse and prose of the last literary section

**[E11b-3]. "The Silence": a serious night-watch verse with Death personified; no joke.** (PW-03, Pte. R. J. Godfrey, 7th Aust. Field Amb., printed p. 141; L6524-6540)
Two quatrains: "This is indeed a false, false night; / There's not a soldier sleeps, / But like a ghost stands to his post, / While Death through the long sap creeps." The second quatrain describes "an eerie filmy spell o'er all—A murmur from the sea; / And not a sound on the hills around—/ Say, what will the silence be?" An illustration listed beside it is captioned "Death as a soldier." Register: the book has a piece with no irony, placed between an Anzac parody and the soldiers' grumbling verse; the silence itself is the threat (cf. E11a-59, the weather that quiets both sides).
*Candidate application:* The humorous anthology still reserves a few poems for stand-to dread; a unit's expressive range includes unironic dread in a plain meter.

**[E11b-4]. "The Growl": the licensed grumble, with the hierarchy of grievances laid out in the poem's own terms.** (PW-03, E. M. Smith, 27th Battalion, printed p. 142; L6545-6603)
Seven stanzas in dialect meter. The speaker says the recruiters promised "'ardships, good and plenty, / And a chance to 'do and dare,'" and that after "a scrap or three" the thing the men are "goin' crook for" is "There's only tea for tea." The second stanza states the priority: the men can take iron rations "Tho' they 'and 'em out like 'Ell," would "charge the blankey Turkeys / Thro' a cataract of shell," but what "narks us more than any" is "The sea's too rough to land our stores; / There ain't no jam to-day!" Third stanza: in the trenches under fire, "Johnny Turk's machine-guns / Does the interviewin' trick," "We give 'em all they gave us, / And a bit of interest, too, / But w'y don't someone tell 'em / We're just perishin' for stoo!" The rain and the lack of dug-outs ("our 'bivvies' isn't dug") are "naught, / If they'd give us all we wanted / Of the steak wot comes to port." The list of supply failures ends with a sharper sarcasm: "W'en it rains they give us lime juice, / W'en it's 'ot they give us rum; / The baccy don't arrive because / The mule train didn't come. / The mail is 'arf a day be'ind, / And w'en it comes to light, / We blanky well can't read it, / 'Cause it's dark as Egypt's night." Then the license clause, which is the poem's center of gravity: "But, anyway, that's roustin', / You don't want to 'eed our 'owl; / They say as 'ow a soldier / 'As a perfect right to growl. / If it's bully beef till Doomsday, / We ain't goin' to make a fuss—/ So long as we can lick the Turks, / That's good enough for us." Mechanism: the inverted hierarchy of hardship (shells are tolerable, missing jam is not); the enemy's machine guns as an interviewer; a closing disclaimer that retracts the grievance in the same breath that names the soldier's right to voice it. The word "blanky/blankey" stands in for the oath, as in the Answers (E11b-17).
*Candidate application:* The soldier's complaint pyramid in this poem puts supply and routine at the apex and danger at the bottom, and then claims a *right* to the growl; the "perfect right to growl" is a stated social norm (the group's own rule of license), followed by a loyalty reflex.

**[E11b-5]. "My Lady Nicotine": a thank-you to the cigarette donors that names what smoking does.** (PW-03, H. G. Garland, printed p. 142; L6613-6652; illustration caption "APRICOT AGAIN!" drawn by David Barker, L6628)
Five quatrains in a graceful meter: "The hills of old Gallipoli / Are barren and austere, / And fairy folk, unhappily, / Are few indeed out there." The one fairy is "My Lady Nicotine," whose "gift is small and seemingly / Of little value, yet / It teaches me so charmingly / To think and to forget." The writer insists "I do not pen unfeelingly / These random lines of thanks, / For I, in old Gallipoli, / Am fighting in the ranks." Subtitle: "(With thanks to all givers of cigarettes)." The plain statement is that tobacco affords the soldier a way "to think and to forget," which is a sincere avowal of the use made of it. The one joke here is the "fairy folk" gag in the first stanza; the poem otherwise reads as a sincere comment. The adjacent caption "APRICOT AGAIN!" survives without its drawing (my reading, unconfirmed: the jam of the day, as the Alphabet and "The Growl" make jam a recurring ration complaint; the text does not say).
*Candidate application:* A civilian-facing thank-you in the book's mild register records a soldier's own statement of what the ritual smoke does (reflection and forgetting) in the one sentence without any joke.

**[E11b-6]. "The Raid on London": a mock-historical chronicle in which a wounded Australian conquers London on crutches.** (PW-03, "Private Pat Riot," 9th Battalion, printed p. 143; L6659-6787)
A long prose item, the book's only sustained treatment of the soldier on convalescent leave in Britain. Its comic frame is a conquest narrative: England "has been conquered by Julius Cæsar, William of Normandy, nearly (but not quite) by William of Germany, and, lastly, by plain Bill of Australia," who "did not invade the city with his shield in front of him. He was carried on it; he came a conqueror on crutches." The private enlists after reading "between the lines of his newspaper," and takes "a quite insanguinary oath (for once)" (a joke on the soldier's swearing). The writer hits the war correspondent: "It is fairly certain that future historians will teach that Australia was discovered not by Captain Cook, explorer, but by Mr. Ashmead Bartlett, war correspondent." (my gloss: Ashmead-Bartlett's dispatches had publicized the landing). A legal pun summarizes how the soldier came to be wounded: he was "acting co-respondent in the case of Crescent v. Southern Cross" when a sniper's bullet "put him out of court" (I leave the wound itself out of the discussion; the pun turns it into a courtroom phrase). The London sequence is a series of colonial-innocence gags: being "bushed" in the city and asking a police constable the way each time; "taxicab drivers" taking him round in circles; astonishment that "historical and household words 12,000 miles away" were borne by "quite unpretentious streets and lanes"; the English "gasped and said he must be joking" when told he had traveled a thousand miles to enlist; the servants in great houses who tell him they have been in the house ten years while he says that in Sydney a girl in service would "think she owned the house" after that long; hotel beer glasses that are too small ("sat in the seats of the scornful"); his habit of tipping in silver and giving away pennies. The class gag: "What a class war failed to do, a race war has done" (the war brought the poor and the nobility together to feed the private). The wartime peak is a Zeppelin raid on the city seen from a theater: the audience rushes to the doors, a man shouts that it is "safer in than out," the actresses "stuck pluckily to their parts"; the soldier stays seated "for example's sake," then goes out, sees "great gaping holes in the roadway, an overturned motor-bus and some wrecked buildings" and sits on a bridge "little dreaming that he was fulfilling Macaulay's prophecy." The closing paragraph turns to patriotic sincerity: the soldier is "glad to return to the front," is "assisting in the pruning of Prussia," and will one day "toast it in a big, big toast." Mechanism: reversal of the imperial hierarchy (the colonial "poor relation" as conqueror), a gently self-deprecating bush persona, the war correspondent as discoverer, and the straight account of an air raid set inside the comedy. Targets: the correspondent, London pomp, the home front's wonder at the colonial. The civilians' hospitality is not mocked.
*Candidate application:* An enlisted man's own comic account of being feted at home is fond, class-leveling and naively proud, and the front is where he is "glad to return." The dissonance between being a "conqueror" and a convalescent is carried by the crutches line, not by complaint.

**[E11b-7]. "Sing!": a one-line retort that deflates a staff officer's praise.** (PW-03, printed p. 145; L6790-6806)
Quoted in full, because the retort is the point: "Trooper Sing, of the 2nd Light Horse Brigade, on the right, was said to have sniped his two hundredth Turk. But his name and fame had not spread all around the lines, for a Staff Officer, in visiting the snipers of Quinn's Post, came upon a Light Horseman who, very justifiably, was priding himself upon having definitely hit twelve of the enemy. 'Did you hear that fellow Sing on the right of the line——' began the Staff Officer. 'Well, sir, they don't sing in front of me,' put in the Quinn's Post man promptly. 'They're too b—— well frightened!'" (my gloss: Billy Sing, a celebrated Australian sniper; the text gives only the surname and the brigade). Mechanism: a pun on the surname ("Sing") interrupts the officer mid-sentence and turns a question about a rival's tally into a claim about the speaker's own effect on the enemy; the oath is dashed in print. The Quinn's Post man cuts a staff visitor off in public, and the officer is shown learning that the front man's 12 does not feel like 200. Target: the staff officer's question; the exaggerated tally (the text itself says Sing "was said to have" sniped 200); and the man's own count, since the speaker asserts only that the enemy is "frightened."
*Candidate application:* A veteran (or his writer) interrupting a visiting superior with a pun is a documented rank-negotiation beat: the subordinate wins the exchange without insolence by joking inside the officer's own sentence.

**[E11b-8]. "Another Attempt at an Anzac Alphabet": the second alphabet, with a confession of fear in several letters and a defense of the supply officer.** (PW-03, "Ubique," 21st Indian Mtn. Battery, printed p. 146; L6810-6896; footnote 24, "O.O." glossed as "Ordnance Officer")
A couplet per letter (compare the first alphabet, E11a-57, by a different byline: "Ubique" is the motto of the Royal Artillery, my gloss). The documented devices:
- *Newcomer's naivety and self-blame:* "A was the Anguish that spread o'er my face / When I saw the remarkable look of the place"; "I was the Idiot who stuck up my head / Before I was taught to take cover instead"; "D was the Daring I failed to display / When fragments of shrapnel came whizzing my way" (the writer admits a lack of courage in plain words); "K was the Knowledge I quickly acquired / Of hiding whenever the enemy fired."
- *Fear openly stated:* "N was the 'Night stunt,' with trembling heart, / Expecting each moment the Maxims would start"; "Q stands for 'Quick,' to the tunnel we dash / When a horrible missile explodes with a crash."
- *Military language turned on vermin:* "L was the Louse that lurked in my vest, / Reconnoitred my person, and tickled my chest."
- *The staff officer fused with the censor:* "S is the gilded Staff Officer—who / Censors my letters and tears them in two."
- *A defense of the supply officer:* "O's the O.O.; let's give him a cheer— / It isn't his fault that nothing comes here." (the book's second alphabet softens the supply grievance by crediting the officer's lack of control).
- *Fear of aviation, ironically given:* "T is the Taube that drones in the sky / (Thank goodness, I haven't been ordered to fly!)".
- *Genre substitution:* "U is the Underground sap we expand— / There's a twopenny tube to the Narrows in hand" (the mine tunnel as London's twopenny Tube, with the Narrows as the terminus).
- *Lethal item in a cute frame:* "X the 'X-periments' made with a bomb— / A neat little cross on a nice little tomb" (the diminutives "neat," "little," "nice" carry a casualty of careless handling; the item is not graphic).
- *Mutual theft:* "W the Wire we put round our works— / We generally find that it's pinched by the Turks."
- *Rumor:* "R are the Rumors we hear every day / That the Turkish moral has quite faded away."
- *The only sincere letter:* "V is for Victory. How we shall sing / Rule, O Britannia, and God Save the King!" (no irony signaled).
- *A pun on the rations:* "J was the Jam with our rations and rum— / We found it was almost invariably 'Plum.'"
- *Mule corps:* "Z is the mule corps recruited from Zion, / Bearers of water and rations of iron" (my gloss: the Zion Mule Corps; the pun on "rations of iron" is iron rations).
- Two couplets I summarize and do not quote: "G," a digestive complaint blamed on tinned goods; and "M," about the night firing of a monitor (a warship) that kept the writer awake, whose last half-line I cannot construe.
Mechanism overall: the alphabet is an inventory of what the newcomer learns and the unit endures, and among its letters the writer names fear, folly and incompetence in the first person, in the same easy rhyme as the fleas.
*Candidate application:* The newcomer's arc (naivety, folly, fear, learning) told as a list of the self's mistakes is a documented comic mode, and it admits fear plainly where the alphabet's form protects the speaker.

**[E11b-9]. "To Sari Bair": a mock-courtship of a mountain, ending in bathos.** (PW-03, "Ben Telbow," 10th Aust. Battalion, printed p. 148; L6901-6930)
Five quatrains addressed to the hill as a disdainful beloved; the second stanza makes Anzac's "fiery vow" that "Thou art for him alone." The third turns to swearing as a weapon: "To drive your Abdul from his lairs, / He comes in proud array; / And loud he swears, and when he swears / The Turkish hosts give way." The fourth shifts the curse to the Germans: "Dear goddess, wise in ancient lore, / Let Abdul curse the Hun; / The waning Crescent fades before / Australia's Rising Sun." The last stanza brings it down: "But cheer up, poor old Sari Bair, / And smile 'midst battle smoke, / For Anzac, wild of eye and hair, / Is quite a decent bloke." Mechanism: high-flown classical courtship address (the first two stanzas personify the mountain's body in terms I do not reproduce) collapses into a slang reassurance. The poem treats the Turk with the same nickname used elsewhere, makes him the one who should "curse the Hun," and gives the Anzac the last word as a "decent bloke."
*Candidate application:* A unit that treats an objective as a haughty woman it intends to win and then reassures her is using romance as a way to describe the assault; the bathos "decent bloke" is a self-portrait of the humane aggressor.

**[E11b-10]. "On Water Fatigue," and the captions around it: the duty-man's grudge against a conscientious enemy gunner, and three one-line joke captions.** (PW-03, Trooper George H. Smith, 7th Light Horse, printed p. 148; L6937-6964)
The verse, one eight-line stanza: "I'd like to get the Hun who sends / The little bits of shell / Which buzz around as wearily / I top that blooming hill. / He only does his duty, / But my only shirt I'd sell / For half a chance to give the cuss / A non-return to H——!" (the text calls the gunner "the Hun" and does not say whether it means German gunners serving with the Turks or uses the word loosely). The poem separates impersonal duty ("He only does his duty") from the personal grudge of the man carrying water. The captions include: "Sergeant (during wet weather): 'Hallo, my lad, have you got nails in your boots?' / Slowly Freezing Australasian: 'Well, I'm that —— cold I hardly know if I've got nails in my feet.'" (the sergeant's inspection question is answered by a numbness hyperbole, which turns the duty-man's weather into a bodily report). Two other captions, "On Water Fatigue: 'Me next!'" and "A Cheery Optimist," survive without their drawings, and I cannot tell what the first is joking about (a queue, or a turn at being shot at). A "NEWS ITEM: The TURKISH extremity WAS blocked on our left.—" caption is a pun whose referent I cannot decode from the text alone.
*Candidate application:* The carrier's wish for revenge on the unseen gunner, qualified by a professional's grudging respect for the gunner's job, is a documented micro-form (grievance without hatred).

**[E11b-11]. "When It's All Over....": the soldier's refusal of the hero label and a fear of the post-war pedestal that does not help one up.** (PW-03, Harry McCann, 4th A.L.H., printed p. 151; L6969-7009)
Four sestets in long dialect lines, the book's most direct statement of how the men wish to be seen at home. After a first stanza of triumph ("the Allies put the acid on the Hohenzollern crowd") the speaker says the soldiers did not "care a cussword if his soul were saved or sold" and "were bound for home and beauty." He then states the wish to be found unchanged: "we're wantin' but to find them just the same and nothin' more— / Just the same old dear old home-folks that we knew before the war. / And I'm hoping they'll be looking for the boy that used to be, / Not a hero with a halo for the crowd to come and see." The third stanza names the target: "Oh! I've snarled to read the phrases that the writers coined for us—/ 'Deathless heroes—lasting glory,' and the other foolish fuss; / For we're simple sinful soldiers, and we're often rude and rough, / And our characters ain't altered since we donned the khaki stuff." A parenthesis has a mate called "Smithy" call this "the outpourin's of an overburdened soul," answered with a threat to "stuff a blanket in that long-offendin' hole." The fourth stanza turns on a mate: "As I gaze on Bill, me cobber, sure I smile a little smile, / For his happy, careless nature doesn't fit the poet's style; / No, he don't resemble Cæsar in his looks or in his speech, / Nor Napoleon nor Cromwell." The last stanza fixes the real fear: "I've a grouch on jingo writers and the poets and them all, / Who have placed us common persons on a public pedestal; / Will they dust our coats and speak to us and help us when we fall, / Or paste a different label on us—something very small? / It's their fault I'm entertaining just a tiny little dread / That me friends may want a hero with a halo round his head." Mechanism: the mock-heroic refused; rank-and-file self-description as "simple sinful soldiers"; the exchange with a mate inside a poem; and the sudden practical doubt, about a labeling reversal, that the public will fall silent when a man falls. The poem is pre-written, in December 1915, for a homecoming that has not occurred.
*Candidate application:* A soldier's dread that the pedestal will be taken away when he stumbles ("help us when we fall") is stated here as a joke-shaped worry in 1915, long before reintegration studies. A defector may recognize both halves of this, the halo and the "something very small."

#### C. The official voice, printed straight inside the joke book

**[E11b-12]. "Special A. & N. Z. A. C. Orders" and the telegrams: the commanders' own register, with no humor, placed between the poems and the parody.** (PW-03, printed pp. 152-158; L7019-7365)
Seven documents are "published for information." (I) Birdwood's landing order (April 1915): plain and practical, admitting "we are going to have a real hard and rough time of it," telling men not to "throw away their food the first day out" or "finish their water bottles as soon as they start marching," and ending on a four-word mnemonic: "Concealment whenever possible, / Covering fire always, / Control of fire and control of your men, / Communications never to be neglected." (II) Hamilton's special order of 7 September (signed by the Chief of the General Staff) praising Lone Pine and the August night march, and naming "the Maoris, Sikhs, Gurkhas, and the new troops of the 10th and 13th Divisions from the Old Country." (III) A corps order praising the troops on the torpedoed *Southland*, who "quietly fell in prepared to meet whatever fate might be in store for them," at "a moment when the nerves of the bravest were liable to be so highly tried." (IV) Kitchener's message of 25 November relaying the King's appreciation, to which Birdwood adds: "Boys! We may all well be proud to receive such messages." (V) Birdwood's farewell of 1 December, opening "BOYS,—I cannot tell you how really sorry I am to be leaving 'Anzac'," and declining to say goodbye or thank-you ("it would only be presumption on my part"). (VI) The evacuation order of 21 December, calling the withdrawal "a withdrawal in the face of the enemy," "without a hitch," "without parallel in the annals of war," carried out "by surprise, with the opposing forces at close grips," and conceding one line of feeling: "No soldier relishes undertaking a withdrawal from before the enemy. It is hard to leave behind the graves of good comrades, and to relinquish positions so hardly won and so gallantly maintained as those we have left." It adds that the troops were "second to none as soldiers of the Empire." (VII) Telegrams from the King (congratulating the "successful evacuation of 'Suvla' and 'Anzac' without loss of troops or guns"), from the Commander-in-Chief in reply, and from the Secretary of State for War. Nothing in these documents is joking; they are the voice the comic items elsewhere in the book parody (the staff voice, E11a-37, 57). Two features are useful as contrast: the corps commander addresses the men as "Boys" in his own signed orders (as in his introduction, E11a-1), and the evacuation order has exactly one sentence of loss-language, where the rest is success language. The book's comic items never mention the evacuation, although the Editor wrote after the order was known (E11a-2).
*Candidate application:* A soldier culture's jokes about staff language are measured against actual staff language, which here is sincere, formal and self-congratulatory but not callous; a unit may mock the document's phrasing while its author is a sympathetic commander.

**[E11b-13]. The "Very New Officer" cartoon: a private answers an officer's question in the officer's own accent.** (PW-03, illustration caption, L7367-7375, between the Kitchener photographs and the cover designs)
Quoted in full, because the exchange is the point: "*Very New Officer:* 'Well, my man, what's the matter with you?' / *Australasian:* 'That —— —— over there hit me on —— shoulder —— with his —— pick!' / *V.N.O.:* 'I don't quite get you.' / *Aust.:* 'That fellah ovah theah struck me heah.'" Mechanism: the man's first answer is the vernacular, with an oath in every other slot (printed as dashes); the officer's "I don't quite get you" shows him unable to parse it; the man's second answer is a **translation into the officer's own drawl**, which retains the content and mocks the form. Targets: the new officer's unfamiliarity with the men's idiom; the man's own idiom is not repudiated, only the demand for translation. A second cartoon of this kind is in E11a-67 ("Haw, haw, no shave?" / "He, he, no——razaw!"). Both turn on the other rank repeating the officer's accent back to him; neither is insolent in content.
*Candidate application:* A soldier culture's rank negotiation may be carried out by mimicry of the superior's speech, which is both deferential in form and withering in effect.

**[E11b-14]. The other captions and plates of the section: official photographs, a drawing of seven-league boots, and four rejected cover designs.** (PW-03, L7297, L7351-7396)
The captioned illustrations around the orders carry no joke text I can decode except the boots: "SNIPING.—" (caption only), "The End of the Story" (caption only; it follows the evacuation orders and telegrams, and I cannot see what it shows), two **official photographs** "circulated on behalf of the Press Bureau and supplied by the Central News" (Kitchener speaking to the men at Anzac "General Birdwood is standing beside him. The tents in the background are a hospital"; Birdwood showing Kitchener the Turkish position from Russell's Top), "The 'Terror' of the 5th," and "Four Designs for 'The Anzac Magazine' Cover" by Maj. D. A. Lane (12th Aust. Battn.), G. W. Hutson (R.E.), F. P. Hewkley (1st Aust. Div. Sig. Coy.) and A. H. Bardin (1st Aust. Div. Engrs.), with no text. The one joke caption: "FOR CONSTANTINOPLE / THESE SEVEN-LEAGUE TRENCH BOOTS OUGHT TO DO THE TRICK" (signed C. Leyshon-White, with "15"): a giant-strides cartoon of reaching the campaign's goal, with the understated "ought to do the trick," printed in a book that closes with the evacuation. The bracketed note on the Kitchener photograph (that the tents are a hospital) appears to answer the reader's wonder at tents in view, a matter E11a-53 touched (my reading only).
*Candidate application:* The official photographs inside the soldiers' book are the one place where press imagery is accepted without parody; the seven-league boots show a hopeful objective drawn as a joke in the weeks the objective was given up.

#### D. The parody back matter: letters, fashions, advice column, advertisements

**[E11b-15]. "Correspondence," letter 1: five axioms of a Field Ambulance, in mock-Euclid, by a unit's own member.** (PW-03, printed p. 161; L7401-7438; signed "NOBBY, Actg. D.A.L.C. & P.O.," initials unexplained)
The letter claims the permission of "the great-grandchildren of the justly celebrated Mr. Euclid, late of these parts, deceased" and sets out axioms: "A Field Ambulance shall be an irregular conglomeration of humanity and other animals, which shall never under any circumstances conform to any fixed order or condition." (Ax. 1); the number of N.C.O.s "shall exceed the number of men by 50 per cent., in order that the said N.C.O.'s may have a twenty-four hours' rest when on duty, supervising fatigues" (Ax. 2, with a mock citation: "taken from the detailed account of Corps Orders made by William the Conqueror in the year 1066"); the unit "shall never under any circumstances move off on the first instance, but all necessary fatigue for the moving shall be fully indulged" (Ax. 3, citing a mock paragraph, "Vide AAZQP, Para. 14490053"); "All batmen in a Field Ambulance shall be equal to anything" (Ax. 4); and a lost unit's finder, "provided he doesn't require it for a war curio," shall "place it in the most isolated place available, and forget it, because several others are trying to do the same" (Ax. 5). Mechanisms: mathematical-axiom form applied to regulation; a fake cross-reference with a ludicrous paragraph number; anachronistic authority (1066). Target: the writer's own unit, its surplus NCOs, its immobility, and the batmen; the self-mockery is by an insider (the letter is a self-description presented as an outsider's study).
*Candidate application:* Institutional self-mockery by a unit's own staff, in the form of mock regulation, is a documented genre; it reveals what the unit privately knows about itself (inertia, rank-heavy staffing) without leaving the unit.

**[E11b-16]. "Correspondence," letter 2: a chatty letter to the enemy, mixing the Christmas parcels, a taunt, and a threat.** (PW-03, "To Abdul Mustafa Mahomet, Bird Trenches (or Neighboring Green)," signed "Australia Joe," Corpl. A. V. McCann, "C" Squadron, 4th A.L.H. Regt., dated "From Holly Spur, Suicide Valley, Anzac. Decem., 1915," printed p. 161; L7441-7496; footnote 26 explains the Christmas "billy")
The writer addresses the enemy as an old correspondent: "You've been so abnormally quiet—say, Abdul, has something gone wrong? Not a charge or a sign of a riot, not for ever and ever so long." He relays rumor, "They tell me you're sick of campaigning," and then pictures the enemy's home "in the wood, where there's peace, and a good fire burning, and the rations are plenty and good." He reports a Christmas truce of grumbling on his own side ("we've buried our growls for a while"), then makes a short religious-difference remark about Christmas that I paraphrase rather than quote (period religious othering). The weight is on the Christmas parcels: "the cards and the boys' Christmas billies are here, and no kid, they're a treat. Plum duff for the boys who've been fighting on the biscuit and beef (army store)," with a tribute to "Folks who reckon that loving is living, whose hearts are as big as their land." The close is a taunt and a tip: "pick your men when you're picking a quarrel," and a postscript: "they've been stating that you're scared to come out any more; don't forget there's a welcome here waiting, a warm one, you bet; au revoir." Mechanism: the epistle to a counterpart enemy who can never answer; rhymed rhythm hidden in prose (internal rhyme throughout); the enemy as an old friend who has stopped writing; the reversal at the end into a threat given as hospitality. The letterhead uses the grim place names plainly ("Holly Spur, Suicide Valley"). Whether this McCann is the Harry McCann of "When It's All Over....." (E11b-11, also 4th A.L.H.) is not stated.
*Candidate application:* A soldier addressing the enemy as a neighbor, with a mix of kindliness, home-boast and menace, mirrors E11a-13/32/34's treatment of the enemy as a familiar; the quiet of the other side is itself treated as news.

**[E11b-17]. "Anzac Fashions," Summer and Winter: a fashion column of captions on the kit, the weather, and officers' vanity.** (PW-03, Bom. A. H. Scott, 4th Battery F.A., printed pp. 162-163; L7501-7543)
Summer: "Australian sharpshooter disguised as a bush deceives a bird"; "First signs of summer: discarded puttees. Infantryman down from the firing line"; "Water-carrying in hot weather is hard work and requires few clothes"; "Sun-flaps on caps and shorts had quite a good run"; "Officer (incog.) armed with stick and bullet-pierced periscope. (No periscope complete without bullet holes testifying to hair-breadth escapes)"; "Gas helmets will never be as popular as home-made shorts"; "The English-made slacks (for the 'Australian giants') were much too slack except under the armpits"; "Slacks and a roll of blankets give a very Australian appearance." Winter: "An all-Australian outfit is all right for keeping out the cold (if not the wet) / But if you have a figure and want to cut a dash on the Anzac Beach get a 'British warm'"; "A mackintosh cape and gum knee-boots are grand for wet weather / But when old Zero pays you a visit put 'em all on." Mechanisms: fashion-plate genre (see "Arcadia," E11a-54); deadpan understatement of the cold ("old Zero"); the unit's improvised dress as style. Targets: officers who visit the line for show (the pre-holed periscope as a prop, "incog."), the supply of English-sized kit for taller Australian men, and the "British warm" coat as officer-class fashion (my reading of "British warm"; the text does not define it). The gas-helmet caption obliquely acknowledges the threat of gas and dismisses the issued helmet as a joke item.
*Candidate application:* The officer who comes forward to be seen at the front is a figure of mild satire; the rank-and-file are shown as inventive dressers.

**[E11b-18]. "Answers to Correspondents," part 1: fear treated as a medical complaint, generals as an unfunny turn, and a fine on swearing as a fund-raiser.** (PW-03, by "our Inquiry Office Orderly, Pte. T. Colles, 3rd L.H.F.A.," printed p. 164; L7548-7568, 7575-7581)
Three exchanges, quoted at length because the answers are the jokes:
- *Fear:* "ANXIOUS.—Your cold feet complaint must not necessarily be a chronic affliction. Many chaps have been permanently cured by a little vigorous pedestrian exercise: such as vaulting the parapet and bogging into a dinkum bayonet charge. So cheer up! It will go away of its own accord once you get 'warmed up to it.'" Mechanism: the idiom "cold feet" is answered as a literal ailment and cured by the very act it dreads; the form treats fear as ordinary and curable by action, with no shame attached to the questioner.
- *Generals:* "COMIC-CUTS.—Sorry all the generals you have so far seen do not come up to your humorous expectations. When you *do* meet the general of whom you approve, we should advise you just to drop him a line and let him know. It will warm the poor old fellow up." (my gloss: *Comic Cuts* was a British comic paper). Mechanism: sarcastic mock-sympathy; the generals are as a class a disappointment, and the remedy is to praise one if one is found.
- *Swearing:* "CHAPLAIN OF THE —TH.—Dear kind-hearted old chap! Haven't you quite enough to do here without worrying your head over the progress of war-relief funds in Australia? Anyhow, it may please you to know that it is proposed to impose a special fine for every time the word 'blanky' is used by men or officers; the proceeds to go to the various funds. So you need have no fear of the said funds not reaching the million £ mark in quite a short time now." Mechanism: universal profanity ("men or officers," a rank-collapsing universal) as a revenue stream; the softened oath "blanky" is the same word-substitution used in "The Growl" (E11b-4) and printed throughout the book.
*Candidate application:* An advice column that treats fear as curable and swearing as the one thing everyone does regardless of rank is a documented comic frame, with a clergy figure as straight man.

**[E11b-19]. "Answers to Correspondents," part 2: the knitters, the thief made batman, and a wishlist denied.** (PW-03, same column; L7597-7618)
- *Home-front parcels:* "PARCEL POST.—You say you wouldn't mind an occasional case of eat-and-drinkables in the parcel as well as the socks and shirts and box of liquorice powders. They will all be useful, but anyway, think of your poor flurried aunts and sisters at home, fighting their way with knitting-needles—wild-eyed and tousle-haired—through a deadly maze of skein-wool entanglements! It's horrible! We're better off where *we* are." Mechanism: the danger vocabulary of the front (fighting, deadly maze) is borrowed to describe women knitting for the soldiers; the questioner's mild complaint about the contents is silenced by mock sympathy, and the final sentence reverses who has the harder job. (Cf. the home-front items in E11a-53 and the parcel monologue in E11a-11.)
- *Kleptomania:* "ADJUTANT.—Yes, it's a pity that one of your men—such a seasoned veteran and a capable and obedient soldier, too—should have such kleptomaniacal tendencies. But we wouldn't advise you to have him sent back. Make him your batman, instead.... Why, the man was born for the position!" Mechanism: the batman (an officer's servant) is the job for a thief; the adjutant is the target, and the veteran's theft is tolerated as an asset.
- *Amenities:* "FUZZY.—Your suggestions will be handed on to the proper quarters. The only objection to the suggested cinema show at Reserve Gully might be that the boys in the firing trenches would make it too hot 'sneakin' off to the pitchers' every night.... Afraid you've no hope of seeing a pub built over the road, opposite William's Pier.... Yes, it's possible that our motor wagons might run penny section moonlight trips to Salt Lake and back to Anzac. But we fear that there is no hope of a *palais-de-danse* there." Mechanism: the wish for the ordinary pleasures of home (a cinema, a pub, a dance hall, a moonlight excursion) is answered with an administrative tone that refuses one item at a time, and the only argument offered against the cinema is that the men in the firing line would leave their posts to attend.
*Candidate application:* The deprivation that the humor dwells on is mundane and civilian (a pub, a picture show, a dance); the plausible comic reply is the administrator's polite "no."

**[E11b-20]. "Answers to Correspondents," part 3: the censor-chaplain, and a run of illness and wound jokes that I summarize rather than quote.** (PW-03, same column; L7553-7557, 7570-7573, 7583-7595)
- "SNIDGER.—We quite agree with you that this is a soldiers' journal rather than a Sunday School prize book. Nevertheless, the chaplain-editor feels we must decline the limerick series you submitted us. Our head printer is a married man with a growing family, and sternly refuses to handle your matter. So that settles it." The wording is quoted in full because it is non-graphic. The content of the rejected limericks is not stated (the item implies bawdy matter; my inference). Mechanism: censorship of the anthology itself, by a chaplain-editor and a printer who "sternly refuses," told as a refusal of someone's bawdy contribution; it documents a *line* between what the soldiers wrote and what the printed book would carry.
- "HUNGRY ALWAYS" (L7570-7573): a mock medical answer to a man with a serious stomach illness, advising a light diet and closing with a mock invitation to experiment with unripe fruit instead of the doctor's advice and with the closing line "Let's know how you get on." The closing sentence is a dark-humor shape (invite the patient to report the outcome of a bad idea).
- "SEEDEE BOT" (L7583-7588): a digestive complaint blamed on a combination of tinned rations, with a remedy of "whisky and Schweppes with steak and kidney pudding" (the remedy is the absent luxury). The symptom wording is not reproduced.
- "UNDIGNIFIED" (L7590-7595): a man wounded by a shrapnel pellet in an embarrassing place is told he should be "proud to display to his admiring relatives and friends those honorable scars received on active service," and then blamed for "the ostrich act" when the shell burst. I do not state the wound's place (the text implies it through the ostrich image). Mechanism: the honor-scar role in the home frame versus the recognized shame of a wound sustained while ducking.
*Candidate application:* The wound that earns no honor, and the illness that comes from the rations, are two documented comic registers; the former plays on the home audience's honor frame and the latter on the unit's own helplessness before the supply system.

**[E11b-21]. "Advertisements," part 1: public notices, lost-and-found and wanted columns.** (PW-03, printed p. 165; L7632-7747)
A bundle of one-line items, most quotable in full:
- "NOTICE.—The Turkish artillery is requested to refrain from wasting ammunition whilst our meals are being served." (mock-courteous request to the enemy)
- "PUBLIC NOTICE.—The Electric Elevator will not be working up the gullies for a while. Some cook stole the current to make a pudding." (an imaginary infrastructure; the cook as thief)
- "KABA TEPE MUSEUM.—Come and see! A piece of long extinct Australian butter now being exhibited. *Admission.*—One slice of bread." (rations as museum object; bread as currency)
- "BENEVOLENT HOME.—The Editor has established a home for lost newspapers and books. Any books or papers may be left there and no questions asked."
- "MEDICAL.—Men suffering from a feeling of fullness after eating, are requested to state where they got the extra rations from." (hunger is so general that fullness is evidence of theft)
- "WARNING.—Men are advised to keep their eyes open for an individual wearing pink pajamas, green glasses, straw hat and khaki mackintosh. It is thought that this is a spy in disguise." (spy mania burlesqued; Sergt. Noonan's later "spy outfit" notice, below, shares the theme)
- "LOST BY A POOR PERSON.—A strong pipe, last smelt in someone's pocket up Monash Gully." (the pipe known by its smell)
- "LOST.—Pair of field glasses. Finder please return same to our Champion Optimist."
- "WANTED.—The address of a good barber. One able to cut hair and shave preferred. Apply any platoon."
- "WANTED.—Section commander requires pair of good field glasses to find his men when there is shrapnel about." (the men vanish under cover)
- "Q.M.S. requires a man who can even partly satisfy mess orderlies."
- "EXCHANGE.—Corporal would exchange a wristlet watch (not going) for a spring mattress or a tin of MacConochie's Rations." (my gloss: Maconochie's, a tinned stew ration; the spelling is as printed)
- "WANTED.—Fifty thousand Turkish prisoners for wharf-lumping, road-making, and building officers' dug-outs. Plenty of permanent work for men of right stamp. Apply any beach fatigue party—Australian N. Z. Army Corps." (the enemy imagined as a labor pool; the officers' dug-outs as a grievance)
- "WANTED.—Some nice girls to stroll with on the Engineers' North Pier." (the absent female company)
- "FULL private wishes to buy guide book to London. Places safe from Zeppelin to be marked with a cross." (a rank pun, "full private," and fear of the home-front raid, cf. E11b-6)
- "TO LET.—Nice dug-out on the skyline. Owner leaving for field hospital." (an estate-agent genre; the exposed position's last tenant has been wounded; not graphic)
- "MAN with good memory would like the job of taking messages from the troops to friends in Cairo."
- "WANTED TO BUY.—The 2nd Brigade will buy large or small quantities of old beer. Fresh beer not objected to."
- "READ Prof. Fire Trench's book on the killing of insect pests with a shovel."
- "BUSINESS FOR SALE.—Mess orderly will sell goodwill of a flourishing business for a box of fags."
- "COMPLETE SPY OUTFIT FOR SALE.—Including pair of blucher boots, sombrero hat, two cutlasses and a yashmak. Owner having failed to be discovered for two days is going out of business." (signed Sergt. Noonan, 6th Battn., the *Dinkum Oil* editor of E11a-67)
Mechanisms: genre substitution (the small-ads column) turns the beach's real scarcities (bread, butter, beer, mattresses, a barber) and grievances (officers' dug-outs, theft, being shelled at meals) into trade. Almost every one-liner is a *request* or an *offer*, and the unrequited wish is the joke. The section-commander field-glasses item aims the joke at the men's habit of taking cover. Targets: the cook, the quartermaster sergeant, the mess orderly, the officers' dug-outs, the editor, the spy-hunt, the Turkish gunners.
*Candidate application:* A small-ads column is a convenient container for a unit's inventory of lacks, and "no questions asked" and the unnamed theft run through it as a shared norm.

**[E11b-22]. "Advertisements," part 2: the Personal column's accusations and absences.** (PW-03, L7670-7698)
- "LAST seen two months ago in Scotland, at the Duke of Buckington's grouse shoot. Pte. Bert Backblocks. Left Gallipoli with serious wound in fingernail early in May. Any information as to present whereabouts of above will be gratefully received by Adjutant, 101st Battn." Mechanism: a missing-person notice that is in fact an accusation against a man who got himself evacuated with a trivial wound and is feasting in good society; the "serious wound in fingernail" is the bathos.
- "CHARLIE.—Come back, dearest. A warm welcome is prepared for you. Loving arms will enfold you.—SERG.-MAJ. BAWLER." (the sergeant-major as a deceptive "dearest," promising a welcome that reads as a threat; "warm welcome" is the same phrase as in the letter to Abdul, E11b-16)
- A verse notice: "OH how we missed you, dearest Bill, / On that famed August nine, / We think about you, Billie, still / In Cairo drinking wine." Mechanism: sarcastic missing-you addressed to a man who was absent from the battle, marking who was present as a boundary of belonging.
- "MISSING.—A little tot. It's rum where it's got to." (a pun on the rum ration)
- "MISSING FRIENDS, ETC.—Will the girl who smiled at William Tomkins last Boxing Day please write to him at once?"
*Candidate application:* The trivially wounded evacuee and the man absent from the big fight are documented targets of an in-group's pointed notice; the boundary is drawn by who was there.

**[E11b-23]. "Advertisements," part 3: the "Beach Theater of Varieties" bill, where shelling is a variety act.** (PW-03, L7780-7820)
The bill promises two sopranos and contraltos billed as "Celebrated Pair of High Kickers and Whistling Wonders" (one of whose stage names is a pun on a bodily function that I do not reproduce), "the world-renowned JACK JOHNSON" (for one night only; my gloss: the period soldiers' nickname for a heavy German shell, taken from the name of the boxer; I do not elaborate), and then: "TIRED TIM, the Juvenile Acrobat, AND KABA TEPE, the Turkish Juggler, will combine in their ASTONISHING DISAPPEARING TRICK. N.B.—The Anzac Artillery guarantees that this is positively the last appearance of the above artists." Mechanism: the music-hall bill as genre; the "disappearing trick" and "last appearance" are euphemisms for destruction by gunfire, the acts named after Turkish positions and nicknames; the "guarantee" is a promise that the guns will end the performance. The text does not explain "Tired Tim" and I do not guess.
*Candidate application:* Counter-battery fire told as a variety bill's "last appearance" is a documented euphemism where the lethal outcome is not named.

**[E11b-24]. "Advertisements," part 4: Krupp's, the arms-maker's advertisement with "unasked-for testimonials" from the enemy.** (PW-03, L7829-7874)
"KRUPP'S (ESSEN, HUN-LIMITED)": "NON-EXPLOSIVE SHRAPNEL... THE BEST ALL SIZES. ENTIRELY UNASKED-FOR TESTIMONIALS FROM ENEMY SOLDIERS: 'I don't mind your shrapnel in the least.'—Pte. —— ——, ——. 'Please send me some more. They make such beautiful souvenirs.'—2nd Lieut. —— ——, ——, ——." The price list: "Try our '1892' Converted / 17 Bob the Ton. / New Fuses and Carriage extra." "Agents (for import to Turkey): Foxy Ferdy and Co." (my gloss: a period nickname for Ferdinand of Bulgaria; the text does not explain). "N.B.—The firm will undertake the packing and forwarding of all orders by the direct route, but will not guarantee their arrival at destination. When ordering, please state whether you prefer goods packed as pianos, soft goods, or chocolates. Terms: CASH IN ADVANCE." Mechanisms: the advertisement as genre; "non-explosive" (shells that do not burst) is the product; the "testimonials" are the Anzacs' own indifference to the shells, and souvenir-collecting (cf. the fuse-collecting reinforcements in E11a-12); the packing-as-pianos line is a gibe at the supposed smuggling of arms to Turkey. *Candidate application:* Shells as products whose only customer review is "They make such beautiful souvenirs" is a stable device: the dud as a gift to the target.

**[E11b-25]. "Advertisements," part 5: black-market, hat shop, and a wine merchant's slogan that ends on a death pun.** (PW-03, L7879-7982)
- *"BEACHCOMBERS (Strictly Limited): Universal Providers and Importers to the Troops AT EXORBITANT PRICES. All Best Brands of Army Rations on Sale Daily. Direct and Regular Shipments by our Special Trawlers. IDEAL MILK—a Speciality. MEDICAL COMFORTS, from 30/- upwards. Terms—Very Prompt Cash. No Questions Asked. THE OLDEST HOUSE IN GALLIPOLI Estab. April 25, 1915. Still Going Strong. SUPPORT HOME INDUSTRIES."* (the ad's lines run together here; decorative panels between them are omitted). Mechanism: an open mock-ad for the diversion of army and hospital stores to private sale ("Army rations," "Medical comforts"), with the date of the landing as the firm's founding.
- *"CAPS—All Breeds"*: four drawn caps listed as "Staff—Hun 10/-," "Very Smart 19/6½," "'Nicholas' 19/-," and "A la Mode (Special Line) 1/10-½," plus "Badges (Cap), 21/- upwards." The drawings are lost and I cannot decode which hat is which joke; "Staff—Hun" as a cap name puts the staff officer's cap beside the enemy's name. The firm's name in the ad is a surname-pun carrying a period ethnic stereotype of the profiteering merchant, which I do not reproduce.
- *"SPILLER, BOOSER & CO., Anzac and London... Agents in Gallipoli for all the Leading Brands of Wines and Spirits": "CHAMPAGNE 2290/- doz. DRINK OUR WINES YOU'LL NOT LIVE ——To Regret it.——"* The slogan breaks at the dash so that a promise of no regret becomes a warning that the drinker will not live to regret it, a death pun that costs nothing to print. "Our Genuine Old Imbros Whisky (GUARANTEED RARE) £12 per doz. botts. NONE OTHER SEEN AT ANZAC. Ask the boys yourself—see what they say—or the G.O.C." ### Part II. PW-04, Bairnsfather, *Fragments from France* (G. P. Putnam's Sons, New York and London, 1917; text lines 40-1,326 of the file; the license text from L1,350 on skipped)

*Reading note for this part.* The text copy has **no drawings**. Each cartoon survives as a bracketed `[Illustration: ...]` block that holds its title, any speech lines, and any narrator's note. Where the title is the only text ("They've evidently seen me."), the joke is in the drawing and I say so. I count 157 such blocks. In this text copy, "Old Bill" is **named only twice** (L1127, L1161) plus the cartoonist's catchphrase; the recurring pair in the captions is "Bert" and "Bill" (and "Alf"), so I describe captions by situation and do not assume which drawn figure speaks.

**[E11b-27]. The Foreword, by the editor of *The Bystander*: the "fed-up" face read as a mask over resolve, and two testimonies about how the cartoons were received at the front.** (PW-04, Foreword, L72-219)
This is a civilian editor's frame, not Bairnsfather's own account, and it is addressed to a home and (given the 1917 New York imprint) an American readership. Claims worth recording as claims: the book is "not fun poked by a mere looker-on" but "the fun felt in the war by one who has been through it"; it renders "the great war reduced to grim and gruesome absurdity"; the cartoonist, "some time a regular soldier, some time an engineer," had "little other idea than to sketch for mischief, on walls and shirt cuffs, and tablecloths," and "the war insisted"; his satire turns "good-humoredly always" (the editor's spelling, converted here) on "his fellow-warriors" and not on the enemy, unlike Gillray; the "fed-up" look on the faces is defined as "the reverse of the kind that implies 'give up'," and a mock-French soliloquy renders the soldier's stance: he is there, it bores him, and by all the gods he stays. The editor hopes the book will outlast the war as "a standing reminder of the ingloriousness of war, its preposterous absurdity, and of its futility as a means of settling the affairs of nations," and also boasts that such a cartoonist is "a colossal German defeat" (the same page carries an anti-war moral and a morale-propaganda boast). Two quoted reception reports: a letter "from somewhere in France" that praises the sketches as a "stimulus to the recollections of an old soldier" and the power of "expressing in black and white a condition of mind"; and a Scots officer's account to a newspaper: "We sit moping over a smoky charcoal fire in a dug-out. Suddenly someone, more wide-awake than others remembers the 'Fragments.' Out it comes, and we laugh uproariously over each picture. For are these not the very things we are witnessing every day, incidents full of tragic humor? The fed-up spirit you see on the faces of Bairnsfather's pictures is a sham—a mask beneath which there lies something that is essentially British." (the editor selected it; one officer's view, unverifiable). The editor's national-character theory, that Britons "must never appear to be regarding their occupation too seriously" and that their frivolity "is apparent and not real," is period propaganda and an editor's inference, not a documented trait; I record it only as how the book was sold. A photograph note says the cartoonist is shown "less than a quarter of a mile from the German trenches" beside "a hole made by a 'Jack Johnson' shell," which supports that the soldiers' term "Jack Johnson" named a heavy shell (relevant to E11b-23).
*Candidate application:* A civilian outlet can package a soldier's gallows humor either as proof of national spirit or as an anti-war document, and the same cartoon serves both; the soldier-character's own reading ("recognition," a laugh at "the very things we are witnessing") is the one the officer's letter gives, and it is recognition, not escape.

**[E11b-28]. Genre substitution: the dug-out "to let" advertisement and the "situation vacant," with the casualty as the former occupant.** (PW-04, "Where to Live—[ADVT.]," L221-236; "Situation Shortly Vacant," L279-282)
Quoted in full, because the genre is the joke: "IN ONE OF THE CHOICEST LOCALITIES OF NORTHERN FRANCE. TO BE LET (three minutes from German trenches), this attractive and WELL-BUILT DUG-OUT, containing one reception-kitchen-bedroom and UP-TO-DATE FUNK HOLE (4ft. by 6ft.), all modern inconveniences, including gas and water. This desirable Residence stands one foot above water level, commanding an excellent view of the enemy trenches. EXCELLENT SHOOTING (SNIPE AND DUCK). —Particulars of the late Tenant, Room 6, Base Hospital, Bonlog c." (the last word as printed in the text file). Mechanisms: the estate agent's puffery over squalor ("all modern inconveniences"); "gas and water" as a double meaning (flooding and the weapon); "snipe and duck" as game birds and as the sniper's and the soldier's actions (my reading); "the late Tenant" in a hospital (hurt, not stated dead) as the contact point. "Situation Shortly Vacant" runs the same trick in a job notice: "In an old-fashioned house in France an opening will shortly occur for a young man, with good prospects of getting a rise." (I read "opening" and "rise" as the double meanings; the drawing is absent and may show the exact peril.) The Anzac Book uses the same device in a single line ("TO LET.—Nice dug-out on the skyline. Owner leaving for field hospital," E11b-21); the two sources are independent here, and this text copy does not date the Bairnsfather cartoon.
*Candidate application:* The "property listing" with a wounded or vanished occupant is a documented compact form that conveys the hazard of a position and the churn of its users without a body in sight.

**[E11b-29]. Verse: "My Dug-Out" (a mock lay with a pun on being rescued from burial) and a star-shell as the romantic evening star.** (PW-04, L242-273)
"My Dug-Out: A lay of the trenches," quoted in full: "What is this slimy dismal hole / Where oft I'm lurking like a mole / And cursing Germans heart and soul? / My Dug-Out // Where is it that beneath the floor / The water's rising more and more / And where the roof's a broken door? / My Dug-Out // Where is it that I try to sleep / Betwixt alarms, when up I leap / And dash through water four feet deep? / My Dug-Out // Where is it that I'll catch a chill / And lose my only quinine pill / And probably remain until---- / I'm dug out? / My Dug-Out." Mechanism: a riddle refrain naming each misery in turn; the last stanza breaks the line before "until----" and lands on a pun, with "dug out" meaning both rescued from the burrow and exhumed or relieved. The reader hears what the dash holds back. The small cartoon "That Evening Star-shell" quotes the aria "Oh, star of eve, whose tender beam / Falls on my spirit's troubled dream" over a flare (the quotation is given as from Wolfram's aria in *Tannhaeuser*; the picture is absent).
*Candidate application:* The structure of naming the misery, naming it again, breaking the sentence before the worst word and then punning is a documented way to speak of burial and death without the word.

**[E11b-30]. The veteran's deadpan and the catchphrase "If you knows of a better 'ole, go to it."** (PW-04, L291, L317-321, L382-387, L1010-1016, L493-496; Foreword L94-96)
The best-known caption in the book appears with no scene attached in the text copy: "Well, if you knows of a better 'ole, go to it." The Foreword says the cover shows "that shell-swept 'ole," so the setting is a shell-hole under fire; the caption itself is the retort to a complaint, and it is already quoted as the cartoonist's maxim inside another caption ("Springtime in Flanders": "as Bairnsfather says, 'If you knows of a better 'ole, go to it.'"), so the line had circulated. Its mechanism is that a bad place is defended as the only one available. The same veteran deadpan runs through the newcomer pairs: "The Young and Talkative One: 'Who made that 'ole?' The Fed-up One: 'Mice.'" ("So Obvious"); "Out since Mons: 'Well, what sort of a night 'ave yer 'ad?' Novice (but persistent optimist): 'Oh, alright. 'Ad to get out and rest a bit now and again.'" ("The Innocent Abroad": the novice's night was so far from restful that his rest was to leave the trench); "UP LAST DRAFT: 'I suppose you 'as to be careful 'ow you looks over the parapet about 'ere.' OUT SINCE MONS: 'You needn't worry, me lad; the rats are going to be your only trouble.'" ("The Long and the Short of It"; whether the reply means the place is quiet or the parapet is hidden I cannot tell from the caption). The two arrival labels are the book's own: "Out since Mons" (a veteran of 1914) and "Up last draft" (a recent arrival).
*Candidate application:* Status marked by arrival date, with the old hand's flat reply to the new man's worry, is a recurring micro-scene; its core is the veteran refusing to confirm that fear is called for.

**[E11b-31]. The caption voice: two registers, soldiers' dialect and the narrator's mock-official diction, plus the mate who does not answer.** (PW-04, passim; examples at L334-337, L604-608, L747-750, L1202-1205, L960-963, L486-490)
The soldiers speak in spelled dialect ("'Ave a squint through these 'ere, Bill"), usually in pairs. The narrator, in italic notes, speaks in the voice of a civil servant or a society paragraph: "Private Jones (late 'Zogitoff,' the comedy wire artist) appreciably reduces the quantity of hate per yard of frontage" (a euphemism for some act of war against the enemy; the drawing would say which); "Having omitted to remove the elastic band prior to descent, Herr Franz von Flopp feels that the trial exhibition of his new parachute is a failure"; "causes considerable anxiety to his platoon." The recurring dialogue forms: the cheerful question that gets a pragmatic answer ("What was that, Bill?" "Trench mortar." "Ours or theirs?"); the complaint answered by a refusal to share the risk ("Pop out and get it, Bert." "Pop out yerself."); sympathy misplaced onto an object ("I wish you'd get something for that ---- cough of yours. That's the second time you've blown the blinkin' candle out!"); a telephone check that plays on survival ("Are you there?" "Only just"); directions by wreckage ("Yer knows the dead 'orse 'cross the road? Well, keep straight on till yer comes to a p'rambulator 'longside a Johnson 'ole."); and **"(No answer.)"** as a stage direction after a grumble to a mate who will not rise to it, used twice ("These 'ere staff cars do splash a lot, don't they, Bill?" and "I wish they'd 'old this war in England--don't you, Bill?"). A parallel to the Anzac "twopenny tube" (E11b-8) is the cartoon "Those Tubular Trenches": "'Is this right for 'eadquarters?' 'Yes, change at Oxford Circus.'"
*Candidate application:* The pairing of a talker and a silent mate, and the second voice that reports the scene in civil-service prose, gives a ready template for how a soldier-character's complaint is received: usually with silence.

**[E11b-32]. Oaths as shibboleth and as noise: the dashes, the "blinkin'" substitute, and the sentry's password.** (PW-04, L304-311, L358, L1037-1050, L1184-1186, L617-623, L314)
Quoted in full: "*Sentry:* ''Alt! Who goes there?' / *He of the Bundle:* 'You shut yer ---- mouth, or I'll ---- come and knock yer ---- head off!' / *Sentry:* 'Pass, friend!'" ("No Possible Doubt Whatever"). Mechanism: the stranger's flood of oaths proves he is one of the sentry's own. Elsewhere: a ration-party caption is nearly all dashes (a series of oath-filled lines; the note says Private Blobs "has always thought a machine for imitating the sound of ration parties (and thus drawing fire) an excellent idea, but simply hates his evening for working it," so the noise of the carriers' swearing is itself a hazard); "'Call me a Tank again, my lad, and I'll knock yer ---- 'ead off!'" (the tank as an insult; and "Can-Tank-erous," where a driver is asked "Where the 'ell are ye comin' with that Turkish bath o' yours?" so the new machine got a bathhouse nickname); "'Yer lucky ----, I'm duration.'"; "'Gott strafe this barbed wire.'" (a German slogan used as a grumble, as in E11a-66). The print convention is dashes for strong oaths and "blinkin'" or "bloomin'" (about nine such forms in the captions) for the mildest; whether the dashes are the cartoonist's or the publisher's is not stated.
*Candidate application:* Profanity as the proof of identity, and the loud ration party as an accidental weapon against its own side, are both documented jokes; the dashes show a civilian-facing cleaning of what the men say.

**[E11b-33]. Higher command in the captions: the paperwork message in mid-battle, the manual quoted over the scene, and the communiqué that erases an exploit.** (PW-04, L405-411, L571-576, L1026-1034, L402, L329-331, L459-461, L531-537, L1202-1205, L464-467)
Quoted in full where the sentence is the joke. (a) "*The Things that Matter.* Scene: Loos, during the September offensive. Colonel Fitz-Shrapnel receives the following message from 'G. H. Q.':—'Please let us know, as soon as possible, the number of tins of raspberry jam issued to you last Friday.'" (b) "*Immediate and Important!* Never has Private Smith's face felt so large and smooth as when he hands his Captain the following message at what he feels is an unsuitable moment: 'The G.O.C. notices with regret the tendency of all ranks to shave the upper lip. This practice must cease forthwith.'" (the private is the clean-shaven bearer; the order is about mustaches). (c) "*Supra-Normal.* Captain Mills-Bomme's temperature cracks the thermometer on seeing his recent daring exploits described as 'On our right there is nothing to report.' (He and his battalion had merely occupied three lines of German trenches, and held them through a storm of heavy Lyddite for forty-eight hours.)" (two layers of understatement: the report and the narrator's "merely"). (d) The manual-quotation trio, each a printed sentence set over a scene I cannot see: "A Maxim Maxim. 'Fire should be withheld till a favorable target presents itself.'"; "Another Maxim Maxim. 'Machine guns form a valuable support for infantry.'"; "The Conscientious Exhilarator. '*Every encouragement should be given for singing and whistling.*'—(Extract from a 'Military Manual.') That painstaking fellow, Lieut. Orpheus, does his best, but finds it uphill work at times." (e) A bare title, "'The Spirit of our Troops is Excellent.'" (the drawing carries the joke). (f) Staff cars splashing mud on men in the road (L1202-1205). (g) "Our Democratic Army": a navvy-battalion man tells a colonel, "I say, yer mate's dropped 'is cane" (rank flattened by the navvy's idiom; the drawing is absent). Officers in the fictional cases carry pun-names (Fitz-Shrapnel, Mills-Bomme, Chutney V.C., Orpheus, Somepush, Prendergasp), where the enlisted are plain or music-hall names (Smith, Jones, Blobs, Shinio).
*Candidate application:* The staff is mocked by quotation: the document's own sentence is enough, because the gap between its priorities and the scene is the joke; the individual senior officer is mocked by pun-name and the named men's grievance is the erased valor or the senseless order.

**[E11b-34]. The "sensation" template: "What it looks like—and what it feels like," with the dread carried by drawings the text copy lacks.** (PW-04, L906-916, L995-1002, L443-445, L1053-1061, L432, L1092-1097, L1138-1141, L598-601, L652-660, L520-523, L1131-1135, L696-702, L1208-1211, L393-399)
A set of titles use a fixed frame for inner states, under the title "The Offensive. What it looks like—and what it feels like.": "Whilst the preliminary bombardment is on, one gets the idea that this is what's happening to the enemy machine guns. Yet somehow or other, when one starts for that 220 yards handicap across the turnip field, it feels something like this." (the final "this" is a drawing); "That tin hat feels something like this on the way to the Offensive / And about like this when you get there / My Hat! Helmets, Shrapnel, One."; "What It Really Feels Like. To be on patrol duty at night-time."; "That 'Out Wiring' Sensation" (sound words only: "Bang Bang / Crash / Bang / Crash"); "That 16-inch Sensation"; "That Periscope Sensation"; "That Provost-Marshal Feeling... A sensation only to be had at a Base--in other words, a base sensation." (a pun against base-area military police). Time-and-stasis jokes: "In and Out (I). That last half-hour before 'going in' to the same trenches for the 200th time" and "(II). That first half-hour after 'coming out' of those same trenches"; "This interesting view for 6 months ... or This for half an hour / War!—As it is for most of us."; "The Communication Trench. PROBLEM—Whether to walk along the top and risk it, or do another mile of this."; and the irritability set: "RECIPE FOR FEELING LIKE THIS—Bully, biscuits, no coke, and leave just cancelled" (the wish to start an offensive on one's own, where the cause is cold, food and a cancelled leave and not an order), and "It's the Little Things that Worry," in which the thing that annoys Private Lovebird is the dug-out, because his leave was postponed. Before an attack: "'We shall attack at Dawn' / 'Never mind about that now, drink this.'" (three-chapter strip; the rum comes before the attack and the order is brushed aside), and "Unappetising: Moments when the Savoy, the Alhambra, and the Piccadilly Grill seem very far away (the offensive starts in half an hour)" (the wish for London's restaurants, with the attack in a parenthesis). Mechanism: the cartoonist names the felt state with a neutral noun ("sensation," "feeling") and lets the drawing exaggerate it; the text copy therefore shows the *form* and not the fear.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-character who describes dread only as "a sensation," "something like this," and by the physical size of a helmet or a held breath is following a documented idiom; the aggression that comes from lack of leave and food is a second documented register.

**[E11b-35]. Leave: the organizing wish, its transit as a joke, and the cartoonist's own use of it.** (PW-04, L470, L740-744, L1214-1224, L1265-1273, L1276-1280, L1305-1307, L478-483)
Leave appears in about fifteen caption lines. Items: "Five days leave! Taxi!" (caption only); "Con Moto Perpetuo": "'OUR BERT' (going on leave—having asked a question, and having listened to three minutes' unintelligible eloquence): 'And 'ow does the chorus go?'" (a man who has stopped listening); "On the 'Leave' Train. You will never quite realize how closely we are bound to our French Ally until you have had the good fortune to travel on one of those 'leave' trains—six a side, windows shut, fifty miles to go, and eighteen hours to do it!" (the "closely bound" pun on crowding; the heading imitates a French security poster, with the last word as printed in the file, "ENSEMBLE," which I read as a spoof; my gloss); "Leave. Dep.: Paddington 2.15. Arr. Home 4." (the home journey's speed against the French train); "Thoroughness: 'What time shall I call you in the morning, sir?' (Colonel Chutney, V.C., home on short leave, decides to keep in touch with dug-out life.)" (an officer on leave sleeps in a dug-out at home); "Getting the Local Color. In that rare and elusive period known as 'Leave' it is necessary to reconstruct the 'Atmosphere' of the front as far as possible in order to produce the weekly 'Fragment.'" (the cartoonist's own note: the cartoon is a weekly product and the front must be recalled on leave); "That Leave Train" (a second lieutenant "Enoch Arden" arrives on leave and a line "The train was a bit late darling" follows; I take the name as a reference to Tennyson's poem of the husband who returns to find his place filled, my gloss, and I cannot tell from the caption whether the drawing is about delay or infidelity, so I do not elaborate).
*Candidate application:* For a unit the war is measured in leave; the comedy is in the journey, the distraction on the way, and the officer who cannot stop living the front at home.

**[E11b-36]. The home front, as the front sees it: tribunals, the film spoof, rations at home, and the polite letter.** (PW-04, L1161-1172, L505, L1109-1111, L1114-1116, L1194-1199, L361-363, L773-830)
- *Old Bill as president of the tribunals* (quoted in full): "'Well, what's your job, me lad?' / 'Making spots for rocking-horses, sir.' / 'Three months.' / 'Exemption, sir?' / 'Nao, exemption be ----d! Three months' hard!'" The home-front exemption tribunals are mocked through a figure the front would trust to be harsh on shirkers; the title is a wish ("If Only They'd Make 'Old Bill' President of Those Tribunals").
- *The film spoof.* A two-card burlesque of a wartime melodrama film, credited to "Flanders Film Mfg Co—Milwaukee, Wisconsin. U.S.A.": a general "plotting an offensive," his nurse daughter in love with a lance-corporal in the pay department who "after extensive & painful researches, has invented a new bomb," an "unscrupulous adventurer in the pay of a powerful Government" who is seen "substituting PLUM & APPLE for the new explosive," the daughter who "steals her Father's private Howitzer and races to the Offensive," and the title "How Dick Manvers Got His Star." The caption's editor writes that "every familiar feature of the Film is happily caricatured" including "the meticulous inaccuracy characteristic of the American film in matters of detail." The target is the home audience's war entertainment; the editor's remark is the publisher's frame for an American reader (and the film's maker is an American firm), so the joke on American film accuracy sits in an American-published book.
- *Home-front rations and money:* "Romance, 1917: 'Darling, every potato that I have is yours' (engaged)."; "Cox's": "Yes, you are, one pound nineteen and elevenpence overdrawn, and that includes next month's pay," with the narrator's note "When one feels rather in favor of floating a War Loan of one's own." ("Cox's" is, in my gloss, Cox & Co., the army's agents and bankers; the caption does not say.)
- *The polite letter:* "Dear ---- / 'At present we are staying at a farm...'" (L361-363; the Foreword says the "farm" is shown on a double page, and the contrast is between the letter's wording and the drawing; the text copy has only the first line.)
- Other civilian-facing captions with the drawing missing: "What's all this about unmarried men?" (caption only) and "Modern Topography: 'Well, you see, here's the church and there's the post-office.'" (the ruined village described by its remains).
*Candidate application:* The front's view of the home is sardonic but accurate in detail: it knows the exemption tribunals, the film plots, the potato shortage and its own euphemistic letters; the comedy comes from the gap, not from contempt for the home people as people.

**[E11b-37]. Running gag and civilian past: "Shinio," the juggler, and the limit of tolerance for a comrade whose habit endangers the others.** (PW-04, L334-337, L345-349, L352-355, L1080-1084, L1125-1128, L714-718)
Four entries on one man or type: "Private Smith, the company bomber, formerly 'Shinio,' the popular juggler, frequently causes considerable anxiety to his platoon" ("Keeping His Hand In," L352-355); "Private Smith (late Shinio, the popular juggler) appreciably lowers the protective value of his section's shrapnel helmets by practicing his celebrated plate and basin spinning act" ("Still Keeping His Hand In," L1080-1084); and "Old Bill has practically decided to get Private Shinio (the ex-comedy-juggler and hand-balancer) transferred to another platoon" ("In the Support Trench," L1125-1128, the one narrator note where Old Bill acts). The paired entry "Our Adaptable Armies" gives "Zogitoff, the comedy wire artist" the same treatment (L334-337), and "Frustrated Ingenuity" gives the sniper's "The Little Plugstreet" invented by a private (L345-349; "Plug Street" is the soldiers' form of the place name, as the book also prints in the advertisement at L669). "The Saint" shows Private Scattergood, looking saintly in the moonlight, "filling 100 sandbags at 11 p.m." (L714-718). Mechanism: pre-war music-hall skills reappear as a hazard (a bomber who juggles); the collective response is not to ostracize him but to try to move him on, which is the mildest form of removal.
*Candidate application:* A unit's comic tolerance has a ceiling set by safety: a comrade whose quirk could get the others hurt is first joked about, then politely transferred.

**[E11b-38]. The future seen from the trench: archaeology, tourism, hauntings, surplus and the war's duration.** (PW-04, L633-641, L644-649, L340-342, L1238-1242, L1283-1286, L1019-1023, L617-623, L451)
The book contains five cartoons that jump ahead. (a) "Valuable Fragment from Flanders: It All Comes to This in Time" quotes a future paper: "This interesting fragment, found near Ypres (known to the ancients as Wipers), throws a light on a subject which has long puzzled science, i.e., what was the origin and meaning of those immense zigzag slots in the ground stretching from Ostend to Belfort? There is no doubt that there was some inter-tribal war on at this period.—*Extract from* 'The Bystander,' A.D. 4916." (b) "In Nineteen Something: General Sir Ian Jelloid at Home": the general "has now let his country seat, 'Shrapnel Park,' and says he finds the new abode infinitely cheaper, and not a bit draughty, if you keep the breech closed" (a siege-gun as a house, a surplus joke). (c) "A.D. Nineteen Fifty: 'I see the War Babies' Battalion is a coming out.'" (d) "The Tourists, 19..?: 'Remember this place, Bert?' 'Yes, it's where we used to chuck the fish to you, ain't it, Bill?'" (the wartime places as a zoo seen by visitors). (e) "The Ghost of Dead Pig Farm—19..?: At midnight, an indignant, husky voice is heard to say: 'B---- these blinkin' sandbags.'" Companion jokes about the war's length: "'Old Moore' at the Front: 'As far as I can make out from this 'ere prophecy-book, Bill, the seventh year is going to be the worst, and after that every fourteenth!'"; "Nobbled: ''Ow long are you up for, Bill?' 'Seven years.' 'Yer lucky ----, I'm duration.'" (regular enlistment as a sentence, with "duration" the worse). The Anzac Book carries similar projections (E11a-5, 46, 52, 66; E11b-11 here).
*Candidate application:* The trench imagines its own afterlife as misread archaeology, tourism and haunting; the haunting in the one caption that gives it is a husky voice still swearing at the sandbags, which makes the ghost an ordinary grumbler.

**[E11b-39]. The advertisement as satire of war-as-business: the "Learn to Fight" correspondence course, the battalion's trade card, and the used Government car.** (PW-04, L864-903, L663-673, L1310-1324)
(a) "LEARN TO FIGHT. Anyone with a taste for Fishing, or Moth Collecting can learn to fight. Anyone can put a hook in a worm, or a pin in a moth. WE DEVELOP THAT INSTINCT, and by our Postal Course of Instruction, will help you to earn big money by fighting. Subjects Taught:— Bayonet work, bombing, & asphyxiation. ... A lieutenant writes:— Unfortunately I had not got as far as your chapter on Upper Cuts or I feel sure I should not be where I am now ... Clearing Station GezainCourt. ... The demand for fighters exceeds the supply. Write today. The Asphyxobomb School of Instruction [Advt] Hooge." The caption adds: "Now that the war has become a world business, we must at any moment expect the appearance of this sort of thing in our papers." (I do not reproduce the sentence about "the work of a former pupil," which points to a drawing I cannot see.) Mechanism: the correspondence-course ad ("anyone can") for the skills of killing and injury; the patient is a wounded lieutenant who writes a testimonial from a clearing station. (b) A battalion's trade card: "THE 1ST BLOBSHIRE RIFLES EXPERT GAS & BOMB MANIPULATORS / TRENCHES TAKEN AT SHORTEST NOTICE / COUNTER ATTACKS QUOTED FOR / OUR SPECIALITY: HOLDING MINE CRATERS FOR 24 HOURS / TELEGRAPHIC ADDRESS 'PLUMAPPLE' PLUGSTREET," with the note that Colonel Ian Jelloid "believes in advertising as an antidote to stagnant warfare." (c) The book's last item and its warning: "ROLLS-DAIMLER, 1917.—Four-seated Coupe body (tres coupe). Hardly been used, beautifully finished (almost completely). One dickey seat (*very* dickey), detachable rims (two already detached). Only driven 10 miles (Albert to Gommecourt). Excellent shock absorber (has absorbed any amount). In exceptional condition. L650 (or good bath chair). BARGAIN.—Captain Somepush, No. 2, Red Cross, Rouen." (the pound sign appears as "L" in the text), followed by "Merely a Warning ... To those who may be contemplating picking up a Government car cheaply after the war. Insist on seeing photograph. Don't be satisfied by just reading the advertisements." The book thus opens with the "late Tenant" ad (E11b-28) and closes with a used-vehicle ad whose seller is a Red Cross officer, and in both the hospital is the punchline's address.
*Candidate application:* The commodification of war and the surplus economy of the peace are jokes available to the front in 1916-17, and an ad is a safe container for the damaged body (the lieutenant, the bath chair).

**[E11b-40]. Duds, souvenirs, the men as zoo animals, and military language turned on vermin.** (PW-04, L499-502, L560-562, L1064-1070, L1144-1147, L1103-1106, L949-952, L555-557)
- "The Dud Shell—Or the Fuse-Top Collector: 'Give it a good 'ard 'un, Bert; you can generally 'ear 'em fizzing a bit first if they are a-goin' to explode.'" (advice on striking an unexploded shell to get its fuse cap, delivered cheerfully; the souvenir-collecting of fuses is the same pastime as in E11a-12 and the Krupp advertisement of E11b-24, "They make such beautiful souvenirs.")
- "Natural History of the War: The Flanders Sea Lion (*Leo Maritimus*). 'An almost extinct amphibian, first discovered in Flanders during the Winter of 1914-15. Feeds almost exclusively on Plum and Apple Jam and Rum. Only savage when the latter is knocked off.'" (the soldier as a rare zoo species of the flooded trench; the rum ration as the one thing that changes his disposition); "Happy Memories of the Zoo: 'What Time do they Feed the Sea-Lions, Alf?'"; "At the Brewery Baths: 'You chuck another sardine at me, my lad, and you'll hear from my solicitors.'" (the bathers as sea lions, with a civilian's legal threat).
- "Those Raiders at the Seat of War: 'I wish the 'ell you'd put a cork on that blinkin' pin of yours, Bert!'" and "Coming to the Point: 'Let's 'ave this pin of yours a minute. I'll soon 'ave these winkles out of 'ere.'" (my reading, not stated in the captions: the "raiders at the seat of war" are lice or fleas and the pin picks them out like winkles, the same device as E11a-15 and E11a-23).
- "The Professional Touch: 'Chuck us out that bag o' bombs, mate; it's under your 'ead.'" (a bag of bombs as a pillow, said without comment).
*Candidate application:* The unit's own vocabulary of war (raiders, pins, souvenirs, the "sea lion") is turned on the conditions of its life; the rum is treated as a real stabilizer.

**[E11b-41]. Superstition and the rule against the badly timed gallows joke.** (PW-04, L548-552, L285-288, L1295-1297)
"Those Superstitions. Private Sandy McNab cheers the assembly by pointing out (with the aid of his pocket almanac) that it is Friday the 13th and that their number is one too many." is a gallows joke from a comrade, listed as a nuisance ("cheers" is ironic). "The Tactless Teuton. A member of the Gravediggers' Corps joking with a private in the Orphans' Battalion, prior to a frontal attack." names the badly timed gallows joke as "tactless" and assigns it to a German figure, with the mock unit names carrying the macabre premise; whether the German is the joker is as printed in the title, and the drawing is absent. "A Puzzle for Paderewski: 'It's a pity Alf ain't 'ere, Bert; 'e can play the piana wonderful.'" (the caption alone does not say whether Alf is away, on leave, or dead).
*Candidate application:* The book codifies by title that a death joke aimed at a man about to attack is tactless, while the same genre is allowed among comrades; the rule is about timing and audience, not subject.

**[E11b-42]. The enemy in this book: mostly absent as a person, and where present given comic types; items I summarize rather than quote.** (PW-04, L1245-1250, L971-973, L590-608, L526-528, L429, L676-679, L626-630, L1289-1292, L565-568)
By my rough count, only about ten of the 157 captioned items mention Germans or "the enemy," and most captions are about mud, rations, leave, the staff, newcomers and vermin; the Foreword says the satire is turned "on his fellow-warriors." Where the enemy is shown, the captions use: German comic types in pun-names tied to food, drink and the stage, shown in absurd mishaps and a dream about the size of the British Army (I describe and do not reproduce their names; period national caricature); the enemy watched through field glasses and shown to be eating a meal, with dashes in place of the noun the speaker uses for him (L565-568); a mistaken-trench joke ("'Is this 'ere the Warwicks?' 'Nao. 'Indenburg's blinkin' Light Infantry.'", L626-630, the Intelligence Department); and a music-hall catchphrase aimed at a general ("George versus Germany," L1289-1292, where the comedian Mr. Robey must remember to say "I'm surprised at you, Ludendorff!"). Four captions I summarize and do not quote: (1) "Alas! Poor Herr Von Yorick!" (L971-973; Fricourt, July 1916, a Hamlet joke about an enemy dead man); (2) "Alas! My poor Brother!" (L1245-1250; the explanatory note refers to a wartime atrocity rumor about what was done with German dead, and I do not reproduce the note's wording); (3) "Trouble With One of the Souvenirs" (L526-528; a soldier hands an object to a comrade so as to deal roughly with an enemy figure, where I cannot tell from the caption whether the figure is a prisoner, a corpse or a trophy); (4) a caption about a speaker making something out of an enemy helmet (L429; the drawing is absent). A fifth, "His Secret Sorrow" (L676-679), likens a man's face to damage from the forts at Verdun, and I cannot tell whether the target is a wound or ugliness.
*Candidate application:* In this source the enemy is a comic stock figure for the home audience and a distant hazard for the soldier; the soldier's own jokes spend their force on the army and the mud, and the comic treatment of enemy dead is one place where the register turns hard.

**[E11b-43]. Literary and classical parody: the literate tag as inner voice.** (PW-04, L938-943, L919-921, L579-587, L1258-1262, L846-854, L1119-1122, L508-510, L611-614)
"Omar the Optimist" (a *Rubáiyát* parody, quoted in full): "Here with a loaf of bread beneath the row, / A muttered curse, but ne'er a whine, and thou— / Beside me, singing in the wilderness, / The wilderness is Paradise enow." (the "bough" of the original becomes the "row," read here as the din; the ethic stated is *a muttered curse, but never a whine*); "The Imminent, Deadly Breach. 'Mind you don't fall through the seat of yer trousers, 'Arry!'" (a Shakespearean phrase, my gloss, applied to torn trousers); "Sir Plantagenet Smythe, at the battle of VIN ORDINAIRE 'On! On! ye Noble English!' / 2nd Lieut. P. Smith, at the taking of 'dead-pig' farm 'Come on you chaps! We'll show these ----s Which side their ---- bread's buttered!' / Other Times, Other Manners. The Decline of Poetry and Romance in War." (the heroic speech and the junior officer's slang compared); "Curfew": the lieutenant in the belfry for a better view is haunted by the line "The curfew shall not ring to-night"; "Veni 1914 / Vidi 1915 / Vici! 1916 / Augusts Three. To each year its type." (a Caesar parody whose drawings are absent); "There Was a Young Man of Cologne" (the cartoonist's note that he has "forgotten the rest of the poem, but it's something about 'a bomb' and 'If only he'd known'"); "The Historical Touch: 'Well, Alfred, 'ow are the cakes?'" (King Alfred; the drawing is absent); "Old Saws and New Meanings—By Bairnsfather. There is certainly a lot of truth in that Napoleonic maxim, 'An army moves on its stomach.'"
*Candidate application:* The soldier who half-remembers the famous line and applies it to the nearest absurdity is a documented type; the Omar quatrain's rule (curse, do not whine) matches the Anzac "perfect right to growl" (E11b-4).

**[E11b-44]. What this text copy does and does not give, and what is not joked about in it.** (PW-04, whole file)
The copy gives 157 captions and notes and a foreword. It does not give the drawings, which carry the expressions ("the fed-up faces"), the scale of the peril and in many cases the actual joke; so for a good share of the items I can report only that a joke exists. Visible in the captions: no named dead comrade; no jokes about the soldier's own wounded body beyond the "late Tenant" in a hospital, the lieutenant at a clearing station and the "bath chair"; no mention of cowardice, desertion, shell shock or executions by name; no sexual material. The only moment in the captions where the register is plainly harder than the rest is the comic use of an enemy's dead (E11b-42), and the one place where the Foreword turns serious is the claim that the book reminds readers of "the ingloriousness of war" (E11b-27).
*Candidate application:* The book is an illustrator's weekly product for a civilian magazine; it chooses recognition over horror and puts what it cannot say in the picture.

### Part III. PW-06, Sassoon, *Counter-Attack and Other Poems* (1918; Project Gutenberg #8930; text lines 37-1,451, the license text from L1,459 on skipped)

*Reading note for this part.* The file holds Robert Nichols's introduction (L109-357) and 39 poems in the order of the contents list (L63-103). Only two poems carry a place or date: "The Rear-Guard" ("Hindenburg Line, April 1917") and "Survivors" ("Craiglockart [sic], Oct. 1917"). I treat the poems that **work through irony, sarcasm, epigram, mock-voice or bitter wit** as findings (E11b-45 to E11b-62), then list every other poem in one finding (E11b-64) with a line saying it is not humor, and say which of those pass over graphic matter that I do not reproduce. Sassoon is an **officer-poet**; the text shows him as a captain with the Military Cross (Nichols, L149-156).

**[E11b-45]. Nichols's introduction: how a fellow poet describes the satire, and one reported soldier's reaction.** (PW-06, "Introduction," L109-357)
Nichols (a friend and a poet-soldier, writing in New York, with the year not printed in the text file) divides the earlier work into "the idyllic and the satiric" and says "War has defiled one to produce the other" (L160-162). The introduction calls Sassoon "to some extent a humorist" (L224-225; spelling converted here) and says that in the prior book the humor "is not ... all of such a wise and gentle tenor," and "breaks out into lively bitterness" (L226-229). Of this book, Nichols says the poet "breaks out, almost like a mad man, into imprecations, into vehement tirades, into sarcasms, ironies, the hellish laughters that arise from a heart that is not broken once for all but that is newly broken every day" (L281-286), and singles out "the detached irony of 'Does it Matter?'" (L307-308). Nichols's account of reception: "This book (in consequence almost wholly of these bitter poems) enjoyed a remarkable success with the soldiers fighting in France. One met it everywhere. 'Hello, you know Siegfried Sassoon then, do you? Well, tell him from me that the more he lays it on thick to those who don't realize the war the better. That's the stuff we want. We're fed up with the old men's death-or-glory stunt.'" (L268-275; one anonymous soldier's remark, as the introducer reports it; the "this book" in context is the preceding volume, *The Old Huntsman*, whose poems the sentence refers to). Nichols also insists the target is not the cause: "bitter as these imprecations are against those who mismanaged certain episodes in the war, the ultimate foe is not they but the German Junkers" (L291-294), and quotes Sassoon as saying that no word should ever be said "in any way countenancing war" (L323-329). The book's own epigraph is a passage from Barbusse's *Le Feu* in French (L49-60) that says (my paraphrase) that war is as hideous in its moral effects as in its physical ones, violates good sense, cheapens great ideas, and develops every bad instinct in the men who go through it.
*Candidate application:* The one reported soldier's view is that bitter satire aimed at the home audience is welcome at the front ("lays it on thick to those who don't realize the war"), and that the target is "the old men's death-or-glory stunt." Nichols's description of the poems as "hellish laughters" is a fellow poet's gloss, not a soldier's.

**[E11b-46]. "Conscripts" (quoted by Nichols from the earlier book): personified abstractions drilled as recruits, and a class confession reversed.** (PW-06, in the Introduction, L231-266)
The poem is an allegory of the officer-poet training his own poetic virtues as a squad. Quoted: "'Fall in, that awkward squad, and strike no more / 'Attractive attitudes! Dress by the right! / 'The luminous rich colors that you wore / 'Have changed to hueless khaki in the night.'" and "They gasped and sweated, marching up and down. / I drilled them till they cursed my raucous shout. / Love chucked his lute away and dropped his crown. / Rhyme got sore heels and wanted to fall out." The comic mechanism is the abstract nouns (Love, Rhyme, Joy, Wisdom, Fancy) given recruits' complaints. The turn is in the last two stanzas: the lovely abstractions were "killed" in France or "went home, because they couldn't stand the din," while "the kind, common ones that I despised, / (Hardly a man of them I'd count as friend), / What stubborn-hearted virtues they disguised! / They stood and played the hero to the end, / Won gold and silver medals bright with bars, / And marched resplendent home with crowns and stars." Here the officer confesses a prior contempt for the plain men, and the joke becomes a re-evaluation. The poem comes from the earlier book as quoted; it is not in the 39.
*Candidate application:* The officer-poet's own wit starts as drill-ground humor about his refined self, and the humor ends in respect for the men; the confession "hardly a man of them I'd count as friend" is stated outright.

**[E11b-47]. "Base Details": the mock-voice of the base-area major, in conditional mood, ending on an epigram.** (PW-06, L656-669)
Quoted in full, because the construction is the point: "If I were fierce, and bald, and short of breath, / I'd live with scarlet Majors at the Base, / And speed glum heroes up the line to death. / You'd see me with my puffy petulant face, / Guzzling and gulping in the best hotel, / Reading the Roll of Honor. 'Poor young chap,' / I'd say—'I used to know his father well; / Yes, we've lost heavily in this last scrap.' / And when the war is done and youth stone dead, / I'd toddle safely home and die—in bed." Mechanism: the speaker impersonates his target by saying "if I were" and then fills in the body (fierce, bald, short of breath, puffy), the habits (guzzling, the Roll of Honor read at the hotel), the stock sympathy ("Poor young chap") and the euphemism ("lost heavily ... last scrap"). The last line is a built epigram: "die—in bed," where the dash holds the reader for the contrast with "youth stone dead." The target is the staff officer at the Base. No named man.
*Candidate application:* A front-line officer's contempt for the rear-area staff is capable of being built in ten lines out of the target's own voice, belly and sympathy phrases, and the dash before the last words is a recurring tool.

**[E11b-48]. "The General": a ballad whose cheer is overturned by its last line.** (PW-06, L674-685)
Quoted in full: "'Good-morning; good-morning!' the General said / When we met him last week on our way to the line. / Now the soldiers he smiled at are most of 'em dead, / And we're cursing his staff for incompetent swine. / 'He's a cheery old card,' grunted Harry to Jack / As they slogged up to Arras with rifle and pack. / * * * * * / But he did for them both by his plan of attack." Mechanism: a sing-song meter with soldiers' idiom ("cheery old card") for the first view of the general; a row of asterisks as a scene break; the last line, a single flat statement. The soldiers' cursing is pointed at "his staff," and the general himself is merely cheerful; the real condemnation is "by his plan of attack." The weight falls on the gap between the men's amiable grunt and what the plan did.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's story that begins with a beloved but remote general's cheerful greeting and ends on what the plan cost is a documented shape, with the reveal withheld to the last line.

**[E11b-49]. "The Fathers": the club scene, with old men trading their sons' postings as if they were sport.** (PW-06, L637-651)
Quoted in full: "Snug at the club two fathers sat, / Gross, goggle-eyed, and full of chat. / One of them said: 'My eldest lad / Writes cheery letters from Bagdad. / But Arthur's getting all the fun / At Arras with his nine-inch gun.' / 'Yes,' wheezed the other, 'that's the luck! / My boy's quite broken-hearted, stuck / In England training all this year. / Still, if there's truth in what we hear, / The Huns intend to ask for more / Before they bolt across the Rhine.' / I watched them toddle through the door— / These impotent old friends of mine." Mechanism: rhyming couplets that sound like the fathers' talk; the first father says "cheery letters" and "all the fun" for a son in the line; the second father complains that his boy is "broken-hearted" at not yet being sent; the observer's last two lines turn to the physical (toddle, "impotent") and shift to a first-person "old friends of mine," so the satire has a personal cost.
*Candidate application:* The home-front men who treat the war as a competition among their sons are a target built by quoting their talk, and the poet's own relationship to them is stated in the last line.

**[E11b-50]. "Editorial Impressions": a wounded lad's deadpan agreement with the visiting author.** (PW-06, L759-777)
Quoted in full: "He seemed so certain 'all was going well,' / As he discussed the glorious time he'd had / While visiting the trenches. / 'One can tell / You've gathered big impressions!' grinned the lad / Who'd been severely wounded in the back / In some wiped-out impossible Attack. / 'Impressions? Yes, most vivid! I am writing / A little book called *Europe on the Rack*, / Based on notes made while witnessing the fighting. / I hope I've caught the feeling of 'the Line' / And the amazing spirit of the troops. / By Jove, those flying-chaps of ours are fine! / I watched one daring beggar looping loops, / Soaring and diving like some bird of prey. / And through it all I felt that splendor shine / Which makes us win.' / The soldier sipped his wine. / 'Ah, yes, but it's the Press that leads the way!'" Mechanism: the visitor speaks in stock phrases ("amazing spirit of the troops," "splendor," "bird of prey"); the soldier answers with an apparent compliment ("big impressions") that is a jab, and the last line gives the visitor's own position back to him as sarcastic assent. The wounded lad "grinned." (Compare the war correspondent in "The Effect," E11b-52, and the Anzac Book's correspondent jokes, E11b-6.)
*Candidate application:* The wounded man who agrees with the war-tourist in the tourist's own cliché, with a grin and a drink, is a documented figure of the soldier's contempt for the press and for visiting authors.

**[E11b-51]. "How to Die": a sentimental death scene set against a sarcastic account of "decent taste."** (PW-06, L558-574)
Quoted in full: "Dark clouds are smoldering into red / While down the craters morning burns. / The dying soldier shifts his head / To watch the glory that returns: / He lifts his fingers toward the skies / Where holy brightness breaks in flame; / Radiance reflected in his eyes, / And on his lips a whispered name. // You'd think, to hear some people talk, / That lads go West with sobs and curses, / And sullen faces white as chalk, / Hankering for wreaths and tombs and hearses. / But they've been taught the way to do it / Like Christian soldiers; not with haste / And shuddering groans; but passing through it / With due regard for decent taste." Mechanism: the first stanza is the sentimental pastiche (radiance, a whispered name) written straight; the second turns on "some people" and their account, which the speaker adopts in mock-instructional language ("the way to do it," "due regard for decent taste"). The target is writing and sermon about deaths, with the soldier's own death held outside both. "Go West" is a period slang for dying (per the poem's own use).
*Candidate application:* The civilian image of the soldier's death as an obedient, tasteful scene is a documented target; the poem's own sarcasm sits in the phrase "decent taste."

**[E11b-52]. "The Effect": the war correspondent's sentence turns into a refrain in a bomb-carrier's head (the poem's body is graphic and I summarize it).** (PW-06, L579-605)
The epigraph is quoted: "'The effect of our bombardment was terrific. One man told me he had never seen so many dead before.'—*War Correspondent*." The poem returns to that phrase as an italic refrain that "danced up and down" the mind of a man hauling bombs along a ruined road, with a second platitude, "How peaceful are the dead," and a bitter question: "Who put that silly gag in some one's head?" The poem then moves to a particular comrade's death, described with graphic wording that I do not reproduce, and ends in a street-vendor's cry that sells corpses by the pair (I summarize that device only: the speaker's own voice mimics a market seller as a way to refuse the correspondent's count). Mechanism: a trivial journalistic sentence is repeated until it becomes a taunt; the speaker names it a "gag," and finishes by selling the count in the voice of the market.
*Candidate application:* The war correspondent's stock sentence turned into a refrain the soldier cannot shake is a documented form; the poem's break into a vendor's cry is the register at its most bitter, and the middle of the poem is graphic.

**[E11b-53]. "Twelve Months After": a roll call of last year's platoon, a music-hall song twisted, and a last line of fact.** (PW-06, L614-628)
Quoted: "Hullo! here's my platoon, the lot I had last year. / 'The war'll be over soon.' / 'What 'opes?' / 'No bloody fear!' / Then, 'Number Seven, 'shun! All present and correct.' / They're standing in the sun, impassive and erect. ... Young Gibson with his grin; and Morgan, tired and white; / Jordan, who's out to win a D.C.M. some night; / And Hughes that's keen on wiring; and Davies ('79), / Who always must be firing at the Boche front line. // 'Old soldiers never die; they simply fide a-why!' / That's what they used to sing along the roads last spring; / That's what they used to say before the push began; / That's where they are to-day, knocked over to a man." Mechanism: the men's exchange ("What 'opes?" "No bloody fear!") is given in their idiom, with the oath printed in full (unlike the Anzac Book's dashes); each man gets a one-line trait; the soldiers' version of a known song ("fide a-why," a comic mispronunciation of "fade away") is quoted, and then repeated as a past tense. The final line is a flat casualty statement ("knocked over to a man") that cancels the marching-song cheer. The text dates nothing but the title's "twelve months." This is the one poem here in which named dead men are given their own joking speech; the poet does not joke about them, he quotes their joke and then states where they are.
*Candidate application:* The comic mispronounced song and the sergeant's roll call become the format for remembering the dead; the humor is placed in the men's own mouths and then overtaken by the last line.

**[E11b-54]. "Does It Matter?": a mock-consoling voice that lists three injuries and three comforts (the content is maiming and trauma; I quote only the refrain lines).** (PW-06, L708-728; Nichols calls it "the detached irony of 'Does it Matter?'," L307-308)
Three stanzas, each opening with a rhetorical question that names a loss (a limb; sight; the nightmares of the front) and then answers with a consolation offered by the home world: "For people will always be kind" (L713; again L720) and "And no one will worry a bit." (L728). Mechanism: the cheerful voice of the comforter is built from the civilian's own phrases ("splendid work," "people will always be kind," "you can drink and forget"); the refrain does the work, and the third stanza replaces the "kind" refrain with "no one will worry a bit," which is the most direct statement of indifference in the book. The wording of the losses is not reproduced here (maiming and mental breakdown).
*Candidate application:* The reassuring civilian voice that lists what a veteran will be offered in place of help is a documented structure; its last line gives away what the poem means.

**[E11b-55]. "Fight to a Finish": a fantasy of the returned soldiers turning on the press and Parliament.** (PW-06, L737-750)
Quoted in part: "The boys came back. Bands played and flags were flying, / And Yellow-Pressmen thronged the sunlit street / To cheer the soldiers who'd refrained from dying, / And hear the music of returning feet." Then, in brief: the fusiliers fix bayonets and charge the crowd of pressmen; the line "At last the boys had found a cushy job" gives the soldier's slang for an easy post as the description of the attack on the mob; the closing couplet, with the speaker in the first person, has him and his "trusty bombers" turn and go "To clear those Junkers out of Parliament." Mechanism: the homecoming parade is written as a comic revenge fantasy; the "cushy job" and "refrained from dying" are soldiers' phrases; the target is the "Yellow-Press" and the politicians, tied together by the "Junkers" of the parliament. The violence against the crowd is a fantasy and I summarize it.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's wish to take the war home to the press and the politicians is a documented comic mode, and its slang ("cushy job") comes from the unit.

**[E11b-56]. "Glory of Women" and "Their Frailty": satire addressed to women at home (the last six lines of the first are graphic and I do not reproduce them).** (PW-06, L808-821, L830-843)
"Glory of Women" opens with a list of what "you" (the women at home) love in a soldier: "You love us when we're heroes, home on leave, / Or wounded in a mentionable place. / You worship decorations; you believe / That chivalry redeems the war's disgrace. / You make us shells. You listen with delight, / By tales of dirt and danger fondly thrilled. / You crown our distant ardours while we fight, / And mourn our laurelled memories when we're killed." The joke is in "a mentionable place" (the wound that can be discussed), and the same distinction between a wound that earns honor and one that brings shame appears in the Anzac Answers (E11b-20, "Undignified"). The final six lines turn to a battlefield scene of rout and then address a German mother "knitting socks to send your son"; the last image is graphic and I do not reproduce it. "Their Frailty" is not funny: it says a woman forgets "the doomed and prisoned men / Who agonize and fight" while her man has a "Blighty wound," then prays for peace when he is sent back, and concludes: "Mothers and wives and sweethearts,—they don't care / So long as He's all right."
*Candidate application:* The soldier's address to the women who love him conditionally is a documented bitterness. The knitting motif appears in both sources of this unit with opposite aims: the Anzac column jokes about the knitters' danger (E11b-19) and Sassoon uses the knitting mother as a hinge.

**[E11b-57]. "Joy-Bells": a reductio on church bells and prelates.** (PW-06, L1267-1280)
Quoted in full: "Ring your sweet bells; but let them be farewells / To the green-vista'd gladness of the past / That changed us into soldiers; swing your bells / To a joyful chime; but let it be the last. // What means this metal in windy belfries hung / When guns are all our need? Dissolve these bells / Whose tones are tuned for peace: with martial tongue / Let them cry doom and storm the sun with shells. // Bells are like fierce-browed prelates who proclaim / That 'if our Lord returned He'd fight for *us*.' / So let our bells and bishops do the same, / Shoulder to shoulder with the motor bus." Mechanism: a proposal that the bells be melted for munitions, taken to its conclusion; the third stanza equates bells with bishops who claim divine support and ends on a bathetic image (bells and bishops "shoulder to shoulder with the motor bus," that is, put to war work). The target is the church as a recruiter; the humor is a dry, reductio-style one.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-character who scorns clerical war-cheer may do it by proposing that the bells and the bishops be requisitioned too.

**[E11b-58]. "To Any Dead Officer": a talk with a dead friend with jokes inside it, a critique of "wounded and missing," a mock-politician's voice, and a last understatement.** (PW-06, L988-1033)
An elegy with satire and a break into tears. Humor-bearing elements: (a) the opening conceit, a phone-call to the dead ("Well, how are things in Heaven? I wish you'd say, / Because I'd like to know that you're all right."), which turns near the end into "Are you there? ..." (the same phrase as Bairnsfather's telephone cartoon, E11b-31); (b) a description of the friend's own wit: "You joked at shells and talked the usual 'shop,' / Stuck to your dirty job and did it fine: / With 'Jesus Christ! when *will* it stop? / Three years... It's hell unless we break their line.'" (a statement that gallows joking and shop-talk were this officer's way of staying on the job, alongside a plain cry); (c) a critique of the official euphemism: "Next week the bloody Roll of Honor said / 'Wounded and missing'—(That's the thing to do" followed by a parenthesis that names what the phrase covers (the wording is graphic and I do not reproduce it); (d) a mock-politician's voice in the last stanza: "tell Him that our Politicians swear / They won't give in till Prussian Rule's been trod / Under the Heel of England... Are you there? ... / Yes ... and the War won't end for at least two years; / But we've got stacks of men... I'm blind with tears, / Staring into the dark. Cheero! / I wish they'd killed you in a decent show." Mechanism: the speaker uses the public voice (the politician's promise, the report phrase) as a mask that cracks; "Cheero!" is the cheery sign-off that cannot hold; "a decent show" is the understatement of wishing his friend a proper battle. Nichols calls this poem "magnificent" and says it was "written just before America entered the war" (L287-289).
*Candidate application:* Joking and tears sit in one speech: the officer remembers his friend's dark wit as a trait, uses the phone-to-heaven frame, and breaks it with a cheerful word that the grief overturns.

**[E11b-59]. "The Investiture" and "Dead Musicians" III: humor as a thing that needs company, and soldier slang and tunes as the way to summon the dead.** (PW-06, L874-888; L1335-1346)
"The Investiture" imagines a dead friend arriving in a heaven that is a formal reception ("God says something kind because you're dead," with a bandaged head that I do not quote). The speaker then says what he would do if he were there: "If I were there we'd snowball Death with skulls; / Or ride away to hunt in Devil's Wood / With ghosts of puppies that we walked of old. / But you're alone; and solitude annuls / Our earthly jokes; and strangely wise and good / You roam forlorn along the streets of gold." This is the one place in the book where jokes are described: the macabre play ("snowball Death with skulls") belongs to friends, and a joke does not survive a lone listener. In "Dead Musicians" part III the speaker says that when he tries to call the dead he does it "In slangy speech," charming their ghosts "With fox-trot tunes," then quotes a snatch of song in italics ("'Another little drink won't do us any harm.'" and a line about a "Blighty wound so neat") and ends: "And so the song breaks off; and I'm alone. / They're dead... For God's sake stop that gramophone." Mechanism: the men's songs and slang are the means by which the officer-poet's memory recovers them, and the music is stopped when it does too well.
*Candidate application:* Humor and song are what remains of a person among friends, and they stop working when the listener is alone; the officer-poet knows the men's idiom well enough to use it for remembering, not for joining in.

**[E11b-60]. "Song-Books of the War": the afterlife of soldiers' mirth in the mouths of later boys.** (PW-06, L1086-1110)
The poem predicts that "In fifty years" young people reading "a snatch of soldier-song, / Savage and jaunty, fierce and strong" will find in "the angry marching rhymes / Of blind regret and haggard mirth," only "the dazzling times / When sacrifice absolved our earth," and that an old man who remembers will say "War was a fiend who stopped our clocks / Although we met him grim and gay," while the boys "with grin and sidelong glance, / Will think, 'Poor grandad's day is done.' / And dream of those who fought in France / And lived in time to share the fun." Mechanism: the soldiers' songs are named "haggard mirth" by their poet-officer, and the poem's irony is that the future will read them as glamour; the last line's "fun" belongs to the boys' misreading. This is the only place in the book where the soldiers' humor is named as a genre ("soldier-song," "haggard mirth"), and it is about time course.
*Candidate application:* The poet expects the soldiers' mirth to be remembered by the next generation as proof of a golden time, which is a distortion; a veteran-character may carry that suspicion about how his own jokes will be received.

**[E11b-61]. "Dreamers": the bathos of what soldiers dream of.** (PW-06, L535-549)
Stanza one says soldiers are "citizens of death's gray land" who "think of firelit homes, clean beds, and wives." Stanza two, quoted: "I see them in foul dug-outs, gnawed by rats, / And in the ruined trenches, lashed with rain, / Dreaming of things they did with balls and bats, / And mocked by hopeless longing to regain / Bank-holidays, and picture shows, and spats, / And going to the office in the train." Mechanism: the longed-for things are small and civilian (a holiday, a film, a squabble, a commute), so the fantasy is of ordinary life, and the bathos is the point; "spats" are both gaiters and quarrels. Compare the wishlist of cinema, pub and dance hall in the Anzac "Answers" (E11b-19) and the restaurants in Bairnsfather's "Unappetising" (E11b-34).
*Candidate application:* What soldiers miss, in three independent sources here, is the dull civil routine and small pleasures, and the humor comes from stating them in a list.

**[E11b-62]. "Repression of War Experience" and "Survivors": the forced cheerful self-talk, and the official line in quotation marks.** (PW-06, L1179-1220; L1246-1255)
In "Repression," a man alone at night talks himself through a routine in a falsely jolly voice: "Now light the candles; one; two; there's a moth; / What silly beggars they are..." and "Now light your pipe; look, what a steady hand, / Draw a deep breath; stop thinking, count fifteen, / And you're as right as rain... / Why won't it rain? ..." and "Books; what a jolly company they are." The cheerful pitch (a coping script, with a pun on "right as rain") breaks within lines, and the poem ends in panic at the sound of the guns (not quoted). It is not a joke; it documents the mock-cheer voice as a technique that fails. In "Survivors" the voice is the official line: "No doubt they'll soon get well; the shock and strain / Have caused their stammering, disconnected talk. / Of course they're 'longing to go out again,'— / These boys with old, scared faces, learning to walk," and the rest names their haunted nights; the sarcasm is the scare quotes on "longing to go out again." Dated Craiglockhart, October 1917, in the text file.
*Candidate application:* A character who tries to talk himself calm in a falsely cheerful voice, and who hears the staff's phrase "longing to go out again" as a lie, is a documented pair; the first is a private failure, the second a public one.

**[E11b-63]. The officer-poet and the other ranks' humor: what the text supports.** (PW-06, passim; L267-275, L614-628, L682-685, L1383-1394, L1335-1346, L1010-1013)
What can be said from the text: (1) The poems' speaker is an officer and says so by position (he drills conscripts, shouts at them, leads a company: "Conscripts," "The Dream"). (2) The other ranks' humor appears in his poems as **overheard or remembered speech and song**, not as something he joins: "What 'opes?" "No bloody fear!" and the comic song in "Twelve Months After"; Harry's grunt "He's a cheery old card" in "The General"; the sentries' mumbled "Wirers going out" in "Wirers"; the tune and slang in "Dead Musicians." (3) He credits an officer friend with joking ("You joked at shells," E11b-58) and himself, at least in the poems, jokes by satire aimed at the home front, the staff and the press (E11b-47 to E11b-57). (4) In "The Dream" III he writes that "Old soldiers with three winters in their bones / Puff their damp Woodbines, whistle, stretch their toes: / *They* can still grin at me, for each of 'em knows / That I'm as tired as they are ... Can they guess / The secret burden that is always mine?—/ Pride in their courage; pity for their distress; / And burning bitterness / That I must take them to the accursèd Line." The grin passes across the rank line on shared fatigue; the officer holds a burden the men may not guess. (5) Nichols reports that men in France liked the earlier bitter poems and called for more, so the hostility to the "old men's death-or-glory stunt" was shared across the rank line, on one anonymous report. The text does **not** show Sassoon joining the men's own comic forms (slang nicknames, mock-orders, dialect monologue); his bitterness is in standard English with soldier idioms quoted inside it.
*Candidate application:* An officer-character who loves his men's humor, records it faithfully, uses it to remember them, and does not claim it as his own is an available and text-supported type; the grin that crosses the rank line is the one that says "I'm as tired as you."

**[E11b-64]. The other poems: not humor, and where the register turns (several pass over graphic matter I do not reproduce).** (PW-06, as listed)
- "Prelude: The Troops" (L367-394): a solemn address to "my brave brown companions"; the one nod toward the soldiers' humor is the phrase "Can grin through storms of death" (L377). Not humor.
- "Counter-Attack" (L403-444): a narrative of a failed defense, with graphic description of the dead and of one man's death; **not reproduced**. The one line of bitter jocularity inside it: "And then the rain began,—the jolly old rain!" (L415), an English-pleasantry phrase set after the description of the dead. Not humor otherwise.
- "The Rear-Guard" (L455-482): a man in a captured tunnel, a dead man mistaken for a sleeper; the encounter and the dead man's appearance are graphic; **not reproduced**. Not humor.
- "Wirers" (L491-505): a night wiring party and a man badly hit; **summarized**; the closing line is a dry irony: "But *we* can say the front-line wire's been safely mended." (L505).
- "Attack" (L514-526): the advance and a one-line prayer; not humor.
- "Lamentations" (L694-703): a man howling over a brother's death in a guard-room; the last two lines are ironic in the voice of authority ("In my belief / Such men have lost all patriotic feeling."), with the sympathy standing on the other side. The grief is summarized.
- "Suicide in the Trenches" (L786-799): a cheerful soldier, the winter conditions ("crumps and lice and lack of rum"), a suicide stated flatly, and "No one spoke of him again." **The act is summarized, not quoted.** The last four lines address the "snug-faced crowds with kindling eye / Who cheer when soldier lads march by," and tell them to "Sneak home and pray you'll never know / The hell where youth and laughter go." Not funny; it uses the form of a nursery verse.
- "The Hawthorn Tree" (L852-865): a woman's voice; sentimental, not humor.
- "Trench Duty" (L897-910), "Break of Day" (L919-979), "Sick Leave" (L1042-1054), "Banishment" (L1063-1077), "Thrushes" (L1119-1128), "Autumn" (L1137-1145), "Invocation" (L1154-1170), "The Triumph" (L1229-1237): serious lyric and narrative, no humor.

- "In Barracks" (L1412-1428) and "Together" (L1437-1451): quiet lyrics; "In Barracks" ends by calling the bugle's notes "lying" ("Another night; another day.").
*Candidate application:* The book's register is overwhelmingly serious. Its few humorous poems are satires on those who are not at the front (E11b-47 to E11b-57), and the poems that name the soldiers' own dead stay out of joke form.

## Pattern roll-up (what this unit establishes)

**Functions.**
- *Pastime and souvenir; irreverent relief:* the Anzac back matter is written for the pleasure of the writing (the mock advice column, the ads, the variety bill), and the Editor's "mere pastime" is the stated aim (E11a-2; E11b-18 to E11b-25).
- *Venting at supply, staff and home:* "The Growl" (E11b-4), the Alphabet's O. O. and Staff Officer (E11b-8), the cartoon trio on GHQ, the mustache order and the communiqué (E11b-33), Sassoon's staff and home-front satires (E11b-47 to E11b-57).
- *Self-mockery of the unit:* Nobby's Euclid axioms (E11b-15), the Blobshire trade card (E11b-39), the Shinio gag (E11b-37).
- *Rank negotiation:* the private who answers a staff officer inside the officer's sentence (E11b-7), the man who translates his oaths into the officer's accent (E11b-13), the "Haw, haw / He, he" pair (E11a-67), the navvy's remark to the colonel (E11b-33).
- *Grief and the dead:* kept mostly outside the joke; where the dead appear in joke form they are enemy dead (E11b-42), an unnamed dud-fusing casualty (E11b-2, foreign laborers), or the elegiac frame (E11b-58, 59).
- *Anticipating the afterlife of the war:* the soldier's refusal of the pedestal (E11b-11), the Flanders archaeology and the ghost at the sandbags (E11b-38), Sassoon's "haggard mirth" (E11b-60).

**Targets.** The staff (G.H.Q., G.O.C., the staff officer who censors letters, the Base major), the press and the war correspondent, the supply officer (sometimes defended), the quartermaster, the cook who steals, the clergy and the bishops, the home-front old men, women who love conditionally, the home-front tribunals and war films, civilians' knitting, the new officer, the visiting officer with the bullet-holed periscope, officers' dug-outs, the enemy gunner (as a professional with a personal grudge), the dud shell, vermin, rations (jam, bully, biscuit), weather and mud.

**Forms and mechanics.**
- *Genre substitution:* the estate agent's "to let" with a hospital occupant (E11b-28; Anzac E11b-21); the fashion column (E11b-17); the advice column (E11b-18 to E11b-20); the classified ads and the Krupp ad (E11b-21 to E11b-25); the correspondence-course ad and the used-car ad (E11b-39); the film synopsis (E11b-36); the variety bill (E11b-23).
- *Scripture and literary parody:* the Book of Jobs (E11b-1), the Parable (E11b-2), Omar's quatrain (E11b-43), the "Conscripts" allegory (E11b-46).
- *Euphemism and understatement:* "Only a ricochet" (E11a-6), "the late Tenant" (E11b-28), "Wounded and missing" and "a decent show" (E11b-58), "a mentionable place" (E11b-56), "the communiqué's 'nothing to report'" (E11b-33), "late 'Zogitoff'... reduces the quantity of hate per yard" (E11b-31).
- *Mock-voice of the target:* "Base Details" (E11b-47), the correspondent's phrase (E11b-52), the club fathers (E11b-49), the mock-politician (E11b-58), the staff's quoted sentences (E11b-33).
- *The last-line epigram:* "die—in bed" (E11b-47); "by his plan of attack" (E11b-48); "knocked over to a man" (E11b-53); "no one will worry a bit" (E11b-54).
- *Pun as a gallows vehicle:* "I'm dug out?" (E11b-29); "Sing!" (E11b-7); "YOU'LL NOT LIVE ——To Regret it" (E11b-25); "a neat little cross on a nice little tomb" (E11b-8); "Are you there?" / "Only just" (E11b-31); "the Orphans' Battalion" (E11b-41).
- *Military language turned on vermin and domestic life:* "Reconnoitred my person" (E11b-8); "Those Raiders at the Seat of War" (E11b-40, my reading); "Louse ... reconnoitred" matches E11a-23.
- *The inventory of lacks:* the want-ads and lost-and-found (E11b-21), the Alphabet, the Growl.
- *The oath on the page:* dashes in the Anzac Book and in Bairnsfather; "bloody" printed in full in Sassoon (E11b-53); the substitute "blanky" (E11b-4, E11b-18).
- *The conversational pair and the narrator's deadpan:* Bert and Bill; "(No answer.)" (E11b-31).
- *Telephone and "Are you there?":* in Bairnsfather (E11b-31) and in Sassoon (E11b-58) as a frame for a possible death.

**Rules of license.**
- *Who may joke at whom:* the other rank mocks the new officer by mimicry (E11b-13) and the staff officer by pun (E11b-7); the book names no living senior by name except where distant (Kitchener, Hamilton, Birdwood as "Birdie"); Sassoon names no one.
- *When and with whom:* a death-joke aimed at a man about to attack is labeled "tactless" in a Bairnsfather caption (E11b-41); Sassoon says jokes do not survive a lone listener (E11b-59).
- *The earned growl:* "a perfect right to growl" (E11b-4) and "a muttered curse, but ne'er a whine" (E11b-43), both followed by loyalty statements.
- *Out-group casualty:* the one item in the Anzac back matter whose punchline is casualties of people other than the writers' own is the Parable of the hired Egyptian laborers (E11b-2); in Bairnsfather the few captions that touch enemy dead sit at the harder end of the book (E11b-42). These are the two places in this unit where whose death may be joked about shows a boundary.
- *Fear is allowed to be named:* the Alphabet's "trembling heart" and "the Daring I failed to display" (E11b-8), the "cold feet" reply (E11b-18).

**Limits and what is not joked about.** Own named dead: none in the Anzac back matter or in Bairnsfather's captions; Sassoon names comrades only where the register has turned to plain statement ("Young Hughes was badly hit," the comrade "Dick," and the roll call of a platoon that is all dead, E11b-52, E11b-53, E11b-64). The grieving man in the guard-room and the suicide are not joked about (E11b-64); one's own mutilation is given only in the consoling-voice structure (E11b-54); the evacuation draws no joke anywhere in the Anzac back matter (E11b-12). Bairnsfather's captions never mention the dead among his own, and the text copy hides the drawings that might.

**Time course.** Anticipation of how the war will be remembered: the Anzac "pedestal" poem (E11b-11), "Valuable Fragment" and "The Ghost of Dead Pig Farm" (E11b-38), the used-vehicle warning (E11b-39), Sassoon's "Song-Books" (E11b-60). The Anzac Book's own evacuation orders and its closing ads show the joke surviving the decision to leave (E11b-12, 25, 26).

**Civilian-facing register.** The Anzac Book's footnotes and dashes (E11a-11; E11b-32); the "Raid on London" (E11b-6); Bairnsfather's *Bystander* foreword and the editor's national-character claims (E11b-27); the film spoof and the "Dear ----" letter (E11b-36); Sassoon's attacks on women, press, clergy and old men (E11b-47 to E11b-57).

**Cross-cultural.** British (Bairnsfather), Australian and New Zealand (Anzac), Indian and British gunners (the Alphabet's byline) in one beachhead; the enemy is "Abdul" in the Anzac Book and "the Hun" or a comic German in Bairnsfather; period national caricature appears in both (E11b-42, E11b-25).

**Craft observations.** (From the sources, not from scholars.) The Anzac Book stages its own joking: an "Answers" column that admits the book is not "a Sunday School prize book" (E11b-20); a book-ending "FINIS" after an ad (E11b-26). Bairnsfather's note on leave, "Getting the Local Color," says the cartoon must be recalled in order to be drawn (E11b-35). Nichols describes Sassoon's sarcasm as the poet's own "hellish laughters" (E11b-45).

## Gaps and cautions

- **No drawings.** PW-03's illustration captions and, far more, PW-04's 157 caption blocks survive without their images. For a large share of Bairnsfather's items the joke or the dread is in the drawing, and I can only say a joke exists. I did not infer content from the Foreword except where it states it (the cover's "'ole," the "farm" double page, the "Jack Johnson" shell).
- **"Old Bill."** The brief calls PW-04 "Old Bill" cartoons, but the text file names Old Bill only twice (L1127, L1161) and the catchphrase appears in captions; the recurring pair is Bert and Bill. The drawn "Old Bill" with his mustache cannot be recovered from the text. The Putnam imprint is 1917, so the file cannot include 1918 cartoons (the brief says 1916-18).
- **Dating inside PW-04.** The text copy does not date individual cartoons (only "Loos, during the September offensive," "Fricourt—July, 1916," "Romance, 1917," "ROLLS-DAIMLER, 1917," and the tank captions, which imply late 1916). I cannot tell whether the to-let ad preceded the Anzac Book's single-line version (E11b-28).
- **Unreadable or unresolved items (I give the caption and say I cannot decode it):** "Veni 1914 / Vidi 1915 / Vici! 1916 / Augusts Three" (E11b-43); "A Miner Success" (L1150-1153); "The Late Comer" (L377-379); "Birds of Ill Omen" (L1156-1158); "In Dixie-Land" (L966-968); "Proposal in Flanders" (L294-301); "That Sword" (L435-440); "His Initiation" (L513-517); "Caps — All Breeds" (E11b-25); "On Water Fatigue: 'Me next!'" and the "NEWS ITEM" caption (E11b-10); the "M" letter of the Anzac alphabet (E11b-8); and the exact referent of "Argon" in "The Book of Jobs" (E11b-1).
- **Glosses of mine** that are not stated in the sources and should be checked before relying on them: the identity of "Sing" (E11b-7), "Ubique" (E11b-8), the Zion Mule Corps (E11b-8), "Foxy Ferdy" (E11b-24), the "Triumph" and "A.P.M." in the brandy ad (E11b-25), the Shakespeare phrase in "The Imminent, Deadly Breach," the "Enoch Arden" reading and the vermin reading of "Raiders at the Seat of War" (E11b-35, E11b-40), and "Cox's" (E11b-36).
- **Wilder claims in the framing material.** The Bystander editor's national-character theory (E11b-27) and Nichols's account of Sassoon as an idealist, a humorist and an opponent only of the war's mismanagers and the Junkers (E11b-45) are the introducers' frames, written for a civilian audience (and, in Nichols's case, a New York audience); they are not evidence of what soldiers said. The one reported soldier's quote (E11b-45) is anecdotal and unattributed.
- **PW-06's relation to the soldiers.** The text offers Sassoon's own page-level voice only. I do not infer his rank-relationship from outside the text; the introduction says he is a captain with the Military Cross.
- **Period caricature.** The back matter of the Anzac Book and some Bairnsfather captions use national or ethnic nicknames and pun-names (Egyptian laborers, Turks, Germans, a merchant-firm name). I listed them as evidence of attitudes only and, where the wording is a slur or a stereotype, did not reproduce it.
- **Spellings.** American spellings throughout, including inside quotations; I converted the sources' British forms (for example "honorable," "sympathize," "favorable," "colors," "humor," "practicing," "Roll of Honor") and said so at the head of the file. "Bagdad," "Craiglockart" (as the poem's dateline prints it), "whilst," and the books' dialect spellings are left as printed.
