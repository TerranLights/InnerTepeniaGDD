# S2: Forms, mechanics and devices (how the humor is built)

**Lens:** S2, forms, mechanics and devices. **Date:** 2026-10-06. **Files read:** all 38 (`E01` to `E25e`; roll-ups, gaps and source-shape notes of every file, then selective finding-level confirmation). **Status:** Research synthesis, NOT canon; no voice or dialogue is written for any character. Real-world material here is mechanism, not specification.

**Evidence-kind key used in the strength column:** Sch = scholarship on soldiers; Civ = scholarship or theory on non-soldiers; Pri = period primary text (trench papers, anthology, songs and glossary, cartoons); Mem = memoir; Ora = oral-history harvest; Mod = modern veteran writing, webcomic, blog or performance; Cra = craft statements by novelists, editors, comedians, instructors or game makers.

## 2. Executive summary

The commonest build is to lay a plain official or civilian form over a hard fact and let one or two flat words carry the horror: advertisements and estate listings over a shelled town [E08-5, E08-35], returns, honors lists and intelligence summaries over a raid [E09a-51, E09b2-2], a wire-service squib set beside what happened [E18-11], a mock bureaucracy over the afterlife [E19-42] (S2-P1, P7; strength A). Understatement and bathos (P2), euphemism and renaming (P3), nicknames (P4), parody of songs and verse (P5), repartee (P8) and pranks (P9) each appear in at least three kinds of source, 1916 to the 2020s.

Most documented forms are procedural or list-shaped rather than single punchlines [E25b-52] and depend on a shared institutional vocabulary (P42, A).

The most consistent build rule is the plain turn (P17, P18; A). The comic voice runs, then the death, grief or disclosure is placed in a different register (one clause, flat, signed apart, or beside the jokes) and is not explained. It appears in the 1916-18 trench paper, the Anzac anthology, both First World War memoirs, a webcomic and modern essays. The explicit craft rules (lead up, close flat, recycle in-jokes, flag your own daring) come from editors' practice, novelists and comedy instructors, so they rate B.

Licensing is partly carried by form (P26, B): teasing by type not name, the institution as target, the dead routed to a separate register, the harshest line put in another's mouth.

Exclusions: named own-side dead are not the target of any form in the trench paper, anthology or songs, and rarely elsewhere (a few conditioned exceptions; P37, A); anonymous or enemy dead do appear as form-bound punchlines in a minority of items (P38, B). Failure forms: the quip tacked onto a kill (P40, contested), the stock comic squad (P41), the jargon-dependent joke (P42, A), wear-out and the prank built on real fear (P43), satire read literally (P44).

Cautions: about 420 findings (E08 to E10b) rest on one paper edited by two officers; oral-history sets are keyword harvests; images, audio and gesture are absent, so timing and delivery are thin.

## 4. Detailed sections

### 4.0 How to read the strengths: the independence map

Strength tags below count distinct sources, not findings. (1) E08 to E10b are one paper, a division's officers' paper over 1916-18; its roughly 420 findings are one source for any pattern, and the ranks speak in it only through officer-written dialect [E08 gaps]. The 1930 glossary's postscript cites the paper as a source and E07 names it, so E07, E12 and E08-E10b are not fully independent. (2) Richards (E13) and Graves (E15) served in the same regiment in the same war, and a "Mr. Graves" appears in Richards's pages [E13a gaps]; their agreement is regimental. (3) E24a to E24d are keyword harvests, and the interviewer's prompt shapes what is told [E24a-29, E24b-20 to E24b-23, E24c-25, E24d-43]. (4) E21 pages cluster: six on one organization, two on one film, three on one cartoonist [E21 gaps]; one comedian appears in three sources [E21-21, E25d-1, E25d-23]; one satire founder appears in three outlets [E21-25, E25d-32 to E25d-35]; Prakash and Bellavia appear in E06 (through an anthology and a memoir) and in E24c (Army interviews): the same narrators and battle, and for Prakash apparently the same tank-and-sniper scene. (5) Three E01 sources (AC-01, AC-25, AC-40) rest on the same 2022 German interviews by one author, and a fourth, a live-fetched blog by that author, yields E01-62 to E01-65, which are unverified.

### 4.1 Devices of overlay and naming (P1 to P7)

**An institutional or civilian form laid over a hard fact (P1, P7).** This is the best-documented device. The trench paper's editors say how it began: "little incidents of daily life" were turned into adverts because they had no copy [E08-2]. The front page imitates a resort sheet and the horror rides on one or two flat words ("duds," "overcome") [E08-5]; the Salient is sold as an estate with "underground residences ready for habitation" while an owner has "gone on a long journey" [E08-35]; shelling becomes "a further rise in property" and street noise [E08-10]; a mock insurance scheme prices a life at eleven shillings and sevenpence [E08-21]; a quack advertisement treats belief in the leaders as a disease [E09a-6]. The same move survives with new materials: a wire-service squib set beside the unit's account [E18-11], parody diagnoses and a meeting glossary [E25b-24, E25b-52], and a gate-of-Heaven strip that extends the enlistment contract into mock theology, spares the dead and mocks the Memorial Day posts instead [E19-42]. The Anzac anthology's back matter turns an estate agent's "to let" into a hospital tenant [E11b-28]. Oral sets do not transcribe the form but hold its raw materials: hoaxes built from a forged form, badge or notice [E24d-4] and jokes aimed at the institution's documents and signs [E24d-7]. Klay and Abrams name acronym language and scrubbed public language as target and vehicle [E22a-13, E22a-23, E22a-24]. In a besieged city the same form is a parody guidebook [E03-9]. Caveat: the evidence is written, and one paper supplies most of the early examples.

The list or catalog is the same overlay in another shape, often with a sober or lethal item buried in it: a quartermaster's day in which a request for twine to make a shroud sits between requests for paper and war news [E11a-37]; a classified column that states honest wishes (a dry dugout) [E09b2-33]; modern grievance lists with two plain lines hidden inside [E25b-33]; "things to remember" without hierarchy [E25e-4]; staff returns and intelligence summaries that find a hostile plan in a tin can [E09a-10, E09b2-2].

**Understatement, deadpan, bathos (P2).** Danger is treated as inconvenience and the small loss is set beside the large: the spilled rum jar is the "awful thought" [E08-20]; "Only a ricochet" [E11a-6]; "tough luck" at a death [E13b-16, E13b-103]; tea lost under fire [E02-1]. The oral-history version is the comic mishap under danger, with no enemy and no victim [E24b-1], the nobody-was-hurt story as the retold canon [E24d-14], and the same near-miss deadpan in four national forces [E24c-30]. Freud's 1928 essay gives the theory (humor as a saving of feeling, deadpan about one's own fate) [E05-44, E05-45]. One caution: the genre-convention version of deadpan, where a squadmate's death is forgotten, reads as unreality; documented gallows understatement "comes with a bill" [E06-41].

**Euphemism and renaming (P3, P4).** The 1930 editors state the mechanism themselves: "Some of the terror disappeared, together with the pomp, from war and military glory, when the soldier decided to call his steel-helmet a Tin-hat, his bayonet a Tooth-pick" [E12b-33]. Shells are "iron rations" [E12b-3]; the grave is a "landowner" [E12b-4]; "go west" revealed "suppressed emotion" [E12b-28]; "hanging on the old barbed wire" was the answer to a roll-call when a dead man's name was called [E12a-60]. Modern parallels are domestic names for the enemy and leisure names for a threatened place [E23-38, E23-40], and official euphemism used as an alibi [E23-4]. Nicknames attack a quirk and are accepted as recognition [E01-14], rotate their target [E02-9], and are made from surname, body, event, borrowed character, role or inversion [E24d-1]; they outlive the unit [E24d-3], can be built from a unit's losses and dropped when they hurt [E24b-6], and call signs are invented on a headset, with rudeness set by rank outside the vehicle [E25a-4].

**Parody and mock-ceremony (P5, P6).** Hymn tunes carry trivial or grim texts [E12a-14, E12a-30]; sentimental ballads are flipped to a thirst or a lost rifle [E12a-27, E12a-52]; one song "conquers" the wish for peace "with ridicule" [E12a-39]; Kipling's "If" is refilled with the function of the grin stated inside it [E10a-22]; a counting-down nursery rhyme is first run on enemy soldiers [E09b1-19], then on the infantry's own side where attrition is by detail and nobody dies [E10a-23]. Vietnam draftees parodied heroic songs and handed a base-camp complaint song down across wars and armies [E02-34, E02-35]. Mock ceremony includes the mock society [E15c-18], mock court with fines and ordeals [E25c-15], and scriptural pastiche, used for the Armistice as a billeting dream in which quartermasters have taken the best mansions [E10b-44] and for fear: a man says he is "not afraid" but has a "palsy," the doctor writes a code, the officer answers with punishment, and the ancient register lets the reader look at it [E10a-60]. Oral sets hold little of this [E24d-11, E24a-7].

### 4.2 Devices of interaction (P8 to P16)

**Repartee (P8).** Johais defines *Schlagfertigkeit* as brief, pointed, apparently spontaneous, situation-specific, getting force from countering expectation, and trainable [E01-4], under the rule of "give as good as you get" [E01-3]. A modern account scores wit by rebuttal speed [E25a-3]; Graves learned to reply in kind to bawdy insult and won a platoon with an answer about lice [E15a-8, E15a-47]; repartee also crosses lines, as notes between trenches and a megaphone exchange [E02-27, E15b-32] and as a graffiti joke battle between allied contingents [E01-10]. Women's participation is documented as counterstrike and reversal [E01-16], and the frame can make complaint impossible [E01-31].

**Pranks (P9).** Four families: initiation of newcomers, reprisals between units, pranks aimed up, and pranks from the trade's own props [E24a-4, E24d-4, E24d-5, E24d-6, E24b-4, E25c-29]. A prank run through the target institution's own channels ends in a negotiated amnesty and a face-saving fiction [E25c-2]. Corpses appear as props with a licensing move attached: a dead enemy officer propped for a staff colonel and buried decently afterward [E13b-11]; an invented drowned comrade used on civilian officials, which Graves calls "hard work" [E15c-19]. The misfires are recorded too [E13b-7, E15b-30].

**Running gags, rituals and call-and-response (P10, P11).** A four-month letters war about a cuckoo that ends in a mock libel trial [E08-59]; a webcomic's recurring figures and the claim that repeated ritual strips build a shared vocabulary [E19-14, E19-59]; jokes held as "standing" property and passed down by seniority [E24a-25]; a First World War cry with a unison reply whose point was repetition, remembered without its reason [E24b-7]; a short-timer's refrain turned into a laughter-suppression game and reused until it carries comedy, comfort and silence at once (a piece of unverified genre, possibly fiction) [E25a-21, E25a-22]; the Dear John noticeboard, run on purpose with a stated mental-health rationale [E24b-3].

**Self-deprecation, rank play, deniability, ventriloquism (P12 to P15).** Leader self-mockery is the preferred form in the Indian Army sample [E02-6], and acceptance in besieged Sarajevo was conditional on laughing at oneself [E03-8]; whether it helps is contested [E04-8, E05-10]. Rank play includes a sergeant major's literal answer to a colonel's lament [E21-3], the deniable order met by literal-minded privates [E21-21], mock-formal address as play and test [E25a-14], and brief inversion then restoration [E01-29]. Deniable phrasing is "the highest form of disapproval" in a polite phrase to a general [E01-58]; the paper put its harshest lines in the mouth of a contributor from another theater [E09a-45], gave complaints about the war to the enemy [E09a-17], and let the Kaiser dictate to a secretary [E11a-58]; a general sings a patter song with his own name as refrain [E10b-79]; politicians are quoted back [E15c-31]; a regime's own announcer is mimicked [E23-21].

**Borrowed frames (P16).** Film quotation as shared code [E18-12], a pop-culture line as countersign [E25e-63], a vehicle named for a cartoon coyote after a bomb [E25b-9]. The First World War analogue is borrowed slogans and song titles [E07-45, E08-81]. Obsolete borrowings break authenticity [E23-42].

### 4.3 The plain register and the two versions (P17 to P19)

The regularity with the widest base is that the comic voice is present in one stretch and absent where the writer puts the death. The trench paper prints a grief poem with no joke [E08-38], a memorial block with no device [E09b2-28], an editorial salute to the dead [E10a-70], and ends in an administrative note [E10b-80]; its fictional dead are disposed of in a clause or by a noise, which the extraction reads as the paper's formula for keeping its fiction away from its real losses [E10b-87]. The Anzac anthology keeps its dead in elegy [E11a-14, E11a-40, E11a-50]; Richards delivers the saddest news in the plainest sentence [E13b-24, E13b-132]; Graves places the joke before the loss and the loss in a subordinate clause [E15a-5, E15a-10, E15a-36, E15b-47]. Modern writers do it too: the dead are drawn still serving in a webcomic [E19-37], a milblog joke is followed by one unadorned fact [E25b-53], and the closing sentence of a memoir piece goes plain [E25e-21, E25e-51]. A cross-genre reading of modern pieces finds the comic voice and the plain turn "structurally paired" [E25a-9, E25a-12, E25a-48]. The sitcom case put the same move in an ending [E22b-3]. Support is A across period text, memoir and modern writing; it is not shown for speech.

**The funny and the sad version (P19).** Klay's "decoy stories" are funny stories that keep people at a distance [E22a-58]; a fictional narrator sizes up the listener before choosing a version [E22a-57]; veterans report that the funny version travels and the frightened one does not [E25c-17, E25c-37]; Australian veterans tell the funny-only version at reunions and to children [E24d-24]; the funny version is told in company and the sad one alone [E24b-30]. The wartime British home press drew wounds as temporary [E07-28], and Graves records "Oh, but that was in France" [E15b-34]. Programs claim the funny version bridges to civilians [E21-34]; a veteran essayist says the full version must also be said [E25c-17].

### 4.4 The joke-building rules the sources state or imply (P20 to P25)

**Lead up, do not quip (P20, B).** Heller: "you have to lead up to it," and three wisecracks in a row fail without a sense of narrative progress; he sensed trouble in a film adaptation because a chapter was funny only through its build-up [E22a-37]. A veteran comedy program teaches setup, punchline and tagline [E21-34], and the sitcom writers rewrote wording line by line [E22b-4]. Against this, most documented soldier forms are list-shaped or procedural, not punchline jokes [E25b-52], and a one-line story works by compression [E09b2-32].

**Close flat (P21, A).** The trench-paper extractions' craft observations, drawn from the paper's practice and partly from the editors' own remarks, are the best evidence: end a sketch on a flat statement; end a purple passage on "In fact we attacked at dawn" [E09b2-22, E08-68]; end a poem on an administrative object [E10a-24, E10a-50, E10a-74]; let the last line of an epigram carry the cut [E11b-47, E11b-48, E11b-53]. The same close appears in Graves's dark-comic final sentence [E15a-22, E15a-42] and in the song glossary's deadpan definitions [E12a-54]. This is one rule with two directions: deflation (comic) and the plain collapse of section 4.3.

**Name the target's own words, and be exact (P22).** Heller scaled a real wedding's menus and took a vice president's own phrases [E22a-38]; the trench paper heckles an official text in brackets under a "verbatim" tag [E09b1-27] and extends a real irritation into mock regulations [E10a-28]; Duffel Blog's writers say to place vulgar material in a bland setting and "put some truth in" [E21-25]. On exactness: license is earned by detail [E22b-1], and an authentic joke can hang on a witnessed detail every listener shares [E25d-1].

**Withhold and compress (P23, B).** Songs leave the key word to the audience [E12a-53, E12a-50]; the wound is a gap in a one-sentence story [E10a-41]; a one-line "story" ends in a hum [E09a-26]; a webcomic needs no dialogue [E19-57]. The refusal to explain is explicit in the editors' "we will leave the matter there" [E09b2-16] and in the sitcom's ending, which refuses to release the comic frame [E22b-3].

**Flag your own daring (P24, B).** "Good heavens, what have I said? Well there! Let it stand" [E09a-30]; an alphabet announces a limit and then says the true line [E09b1-6]; "the word may be Joking" [E11a-57]; "This is a family journal" cuts off a vice joke [E10b-83]; Graves labels strained scenes "caricature scene" [E15c-16]; a modern performer's first joke asks permission and gets "the pink elephant out of the room" [E25d-26].

**Callbacks and the stable cast** appear in the extractions' craft notes on the paper (keep a cast, recycle in-jokes, use the cast in the last number) [E09a roll-up, E10b roll-up] and recur in a webcomic [E19-59]. Inside a single text, Graves calls a motif back across chapters (the gas-company spanners, a captain's grim forecast, a colonel's decoration) [E15b-41, E15b-53, E15b-35, E15b-50, E15b-99, E15b-121]; see P10.

**Timing (P25).** At the macro scale the evidence is broad: funny "not at the time, but afterwards" [E24b-2, E24d-17], laughter held for the debrief [E25a-19], the funniest line of a firefight laughed at later [E25b-1], an injury jokeable once the outcome is secure [E22a-42]. Against it, relief laughter is recorded as simultaneous with danger [E24a-5, E24d-14]. At the micro scale only a performer's pause is documented (letting the audience look at a wound; letting a hung silence turn) [E21-10, E21-19]. The corpus contains no audio, so delivery is the weakest-supported element of S2.

### 4.5 How a joke is licensed in form (P26, P27)

Who may joke is the subject of other lenses. What S2 can add is what the form itself signals. Four form-carried devices recur: teasing by type, role or nickname rather than name ("Yet here I may not name him") [E08-74, E08-9, E12a-7]; a genre whose target is the institution's own forms (returns, honors lists, ads) [E08 roll-up targets, E09a-51]; the dead routed to a separate register (section 4.3); and the harshest line placed in another voice (P14). Explicit statements that the target is "the madness, not the dead" come from a sitcom account [E22b-1], and a corpse-prop prank whose narrator states that the target was the Staff and shows respect by a decent burial [E13b-11]. For the rest, the claim that form signals target is my inference from where targets cluster, and should be read as such.

Fear is a tension within the form rules (P27, D). The trench paper never lets the one who feels it name it; fear is "palsy," "the megrims," "windiness" [E10a-60, E09b3-22, E09b3-24, E10a-8]. The Anzac Alphabet names a "trembling heart" and a reply of "cold feet" is allowed [E11b-8, E11b-18]; Australian veterans say fear goes through a joke or silence [E24d-13]; the glossary allows "wind-up" in the past tense and casually, but not about another man now [E12b-29]. The difference may track censorship and an officers' voice, which is an inference.

### 4.7 Change over time and between armies (P35, P36)

Inside the one paper the objects of mockery move from place (the ruined Salient) to institution (Staff, returns, honors) to supply (rum, whiskey) and to the telling of the offensive, with the home front, demobilization and the paper's own end arriving last; the plain register thickens near the end and the editors say outright, only after the guns stop, that the war was "the vilest disaster" [E09a roll-up, E09b2 roll-up, E10a roll-up, E10b-69]. This is one unit's drift (C). Songs belonged to a phase and died with it [E12a-4]. Between wars the forms persist (near-miss deadpan, captor gags, pranks) [E24c-30], and the Vietnam complaint song passed from one war and army to the next [E02-35]. National-style claims exist ("the typically English irony" [E12b-33]; the Australian self-image [E24d-41]; German and British styles in a fetched blog [E01-62 to E01-65, unverified]) but the counted oral-history sets cannot support differences by war or army [E24a-27, E24b-32, E24d-39]. I treat style-by-army as contested (D), not established.

### 4.8 What the forms do not include (P37 to P39)

**Named own-side dead (P37, A).** In the trench paper no named dead man is joked about, in any of seven extractions [E09a-8, E10b-46, E08 limits]; the Anzac book's comic sketches of dead comrades use pseudonyms and end in an epitaph [E11a-24]; the one song with a dead comrade breaks its own frame [E12a-49]; Richards's named dead are narrated plainly [E13b-23, E13b-24]; Graves's too [E15b-47, E15b-50]. Oral sets say the same [E24a-9, E24c-6, E24d-10, E24d-21], as do a webcomic [E19-28, E19-41], essays [E25c-13] and fiction [E25a-48]. The one modern author who mocked a dead man apologizes to him by name [E19-41]. Exceptions are all conditioned: an unflattering "honest" eulogy that draws a laugh [E25c-25], the dead reframed as living through the funniest memory [E24d-29], an invented corpse [E15c-19], an officer's death joked where his conduct licenses it and the cause is a mock-prayer [E13b-40], and a private joke at a possibly innocent dead civilian that produced guilt [E25b-11].

**Enemy and anonymous dead (P38, B).** The primary First World War paper does not exclude them. A sniper "golf match" ends with the enemy player "finished" [E08-15]; a counting-down rhyme removes ten enemy pioneers and ends on the survivor who "couldn't see the fun" of being shot at [E09b1-19]; an enemy corpse is an ingredient in a soup gag (summarized) [E09a-52]; a correspondent's dispatch sets enemy dead in a pastoral closing (summarized) [E09b3-17]; a parody dispatch from the 1918 Russian intervention makes a killed local man its punchline (restrained summary) and the paper does not comment [E10b-72]. In every case the form is a frame (sport, nursery, inventory, travelogue) and the dead are anonymous. Oral sets record a frozen corpse used to frighten a cook, laughter at enemy dead in the water, and an observer disturbed that anyone was "sitting there laughing" [E24c-6]. A Ukrainian meme sample makes enemy mass death routine through holiday, profit, bags and discount frames [E03-25 to E03-27], and its own silence on Ukrainian dead is a property of the sample [E03 gaps]. Hatred of the enemy command, by contrast, is printed in an earnest register outside the comic tools [E09b1-28, E10b-52], and "the enemy as a person" is an edge [E24d-22].

**Other items recorded as unjoked (P39).** A slow helpless dying [E15b-3], the Armistice [E15c-40], a mass-grave guard shift [E25a-12], shell shock and facial disfigurement in press cartoons [E07-31, E07-40, E07-41]. Each is one source's statement. Three structural absences also matter: the corpus holds no defector's joke beyond about a dozen lines from two deserters [E23 gaps], no joke text actually told in a unit from the interview sources [E22a gaps], and no Boone-related humor material at all [E22b-12]. Bodily-waste humor is excluded from the oral harvests by the project's content boundary; its absence is a boundary decision, not a finding [E24d gaps; E24b-4].

### 4.9 Documented failure forms (P40 to P44)

**The quip after the kill (P40, D).** The clearest craft statement is a studio's self-criticism: the failing combination was killing for pay, then a celebratory quip, in a setting with real casualties and a target nobody could read, so the humor "went down like a lead balloon" [E22b-19]. Memoir and oral sources describe the underlying register: sporting vocabulary for killing, atrocity told as a boast, a narrator (genre unverified) who draws his own line on which killing may be funny [E13a-13, E15b-75, E15b-76, E25a-7]. They also show it occurring: an officer calls the sight of an enemy fighter struck by his own fire mission "the funniest thing I've ever seen," and in the same breath says feeling sorry for the enemy is rare but happened here and that he cannot account for the order [E24c-24]. So the failure is audience-relative, and the sources disagree about whether the soldiers themselves would call it failure.

**The stock squad (P41, B).** Jensen finds the comic squad of fixed archetypes (mouthy, explosion-loving, short-timer) is the genre baseline [E06-38], that deadpan or absent reaction to a squadmate's death reads as unreality when realism is advertised [E06-41], and that squad cheering at kills is a moral cue [E06-42]. The studio statement adds niche humor delivered to a mass audience [E22b-19]. These are depiction sources.

**Jargon and context collapse (P42, A).** A joke succeeds only if the audience can decode it, and silence is the sanction [E01-2, E05-28]. A former Marine's field story was interrupted at every line by civilians asking what the terms meant [E21-33]; a comedian's helicopter joke "kills" in theater and gets "I don't get it" from a deployed Marine [E25d-1, E25d-2]; a strip is a password for those who were at the gate [E19-1]; a poem says the jokes "do not translate" [E25a-57]; a clerk is the wrong audience [E25b-45]; printing an insider joke strips its sense [E03-16].

**Wear-out, escalation, the prank on real fear (P43, B).** Readers heckled the running gag [E08-59]; sarcasm wears off with exposure [E25c-18]; humor communities decay into repeated contempt [E19-17]; a character's escalating jokes can exhaust those who would answer them [E22b-16]; a prank designed around the target's real danger [E25e-49, E25b-6], banter and hazing sharing a logic [E01-43], and a rat prank or a bomb thrown down a cellar [E15b-30, E15b-11].

**Frame failure (P44, C).** Satire read literally (a U.S. Senate leader's office answered a fake news item) [E25d-34] and a sardonic blog read literally by command [E18-16].

## 5. Contradictions and open questions

1. **Fear named or not (P27).** The trench paper never lets the speaker name his own fear [E10a-60, E09b3-22, E10a-8]; the Anzac anthology and Australian veterans do or can [E11b-8, E11b-18, E24d-13]. Unresolved: censorship, officer authorship and army all differ at once.
2. **The quip after the kill (P40).** A studio calls it the failing combination [E22b-19]; veterans' accounts record laughter at enemy deaths and the disturbed observer [E24c-6, E24c-24], and one writer (genre unverified) draws his own line inside his unit's looser one [E25a-7]. The sources agree that audience decides; they disagree on whether the soldiers see a failure.
3. **Does self-mockery help (P12)?** A tool of leaders and an entry ticket [E02-6, E03-8] against lower life satisfaction in a veteran survey and excess self-ridicule as a failure mode [E04-8, E05-10]; a third study finds injurious styles nearly null [E04-29]. Constructs differ [E04 gaps].
4. **Written forms print the plain beside the funny; oral tellings omit it (P17, P19).** The trench paper, the memoirs and modern essays place the plain turn next to the joke [E08-38, E15b-3, E25b-53]; the oral sets describe a funny-only telling for audiences and reunions [E24d-24, E24b-30, E24a-22]. The "unjoking baseline beside the jokes" may be a written or edited form rather than a speech practice. This is the most important open question for S2.
5. **Songs and the staff.** The song editors say staff were not satirized in song [E12a-7] yet print a staff stanza [E12a-31], and say nobody sang under bombardment [E12a-10] while a song about a shell is recorded [E12a-37].
6. **Soldier cartoons and darkness.** Gregory finds soldiers' own cartoons no darker than the home press [E07-27, E07-33]; songs and the trench paper show darker material [E12a-49, E10a-60]. Gregory's sample is thin, partly civilian-produced, and images are absent [E07 gaps].
7. **Wounds as a topic.** "Not permitted to be raised" in a good battalion [E15b-65] against self-wounders admired and the wound as prize [E13b-73, E13a-24]; same regiment, different rank or phase.
8. **Prank culture.** Documented in all four oral-history sets [E24a-4, E24b-4, E24c-3, E24d-4]; one broadcast-and-essay set reports none [E25d roll-up]. Likely a selection effect.
9. **Funny version as bridge or shield (P19).** [E21-34] against [E25c-17], [E22a-58].
10. **Media or nation?** Ukrainian memes make enemy death routine [E03-25 to E03-27]; the U.S. webcomic leaves the dead out [E19-28]; the trench paper does both in small doses [E08-15, E10b-87]. The files cannot separate medium, nation, war stage and sample [E03-20, E03 gaps].

Open questions: whether form signals target (P26) holds beyond the cited cases; whether mock-ceremony is thin in oral sets because it is rare or because it is not described with the harvest's keywords [E24d-11]; how the forms behave without a unit [E25b-36, E25d-19].

## 6. What would resolve the gaps

- **Audio and video of delivery** (timing, pauses, tone): the broadcast and oral-history audio behind E25d, E24d and E21 are not in the corpus. Ask for transcripts with laughter marked, or listen and note. Tier C items for the performance side: the Uriarte podcasts and the veteran-comedy episodes [C-73].
- **Images:** Bairnsfather's drawings [E11b gaps], the British press cartoons [E07 gaps], and the Wipers Times cartoons are absent; the original images, and the Terminal Lance strips beyond the serious-topic sample [E19 gaps], would test the caption-versus-image claims.

## 7. Design-relevant candidates

Each is a constraint a writer could treat as a real-world fact about this kind of humor, with its strength tag. These are candidates, not canon. No dialogue or sample joke is offered, and the character's origin military is left open.

1. **[A]** The form is built from the institution's own paperwork: notices, returns, regulations, ads, contracts. A character's idiom about harm can be drawn from the document types of whichever service he left, and the form, not a punchline, does the work (P1, P7).
2. **[A]** His funny stories can have a seam: one sentence where the register drops, with nothing added after it (P17, P18).
3. **[A]** Understatement should be scaled to the danger and should keep its bill: documented gallows understatement is followed by cost, while the genre's version forgets the death [E06-41] (P2).
4. **[A]** A nickname is evidence of being known; losing one by leaving can be a loss of being seen [E01-14] (P4).
