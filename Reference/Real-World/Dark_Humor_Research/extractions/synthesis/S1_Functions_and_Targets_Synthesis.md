# S1 — Functions and Targets: What Soldiers' and Veterans' Dark Humor Does, and At Whom or What It Is Aimed

**Lens:** S1, functions and targets · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Files read:** all 38 (every roll-up, every "Gaps and cautions," every source-shape head; findings consulted for this lens, see the last line) · **Research synthesis, NOT canon; no voice or dialogue is written for any character.**

*Conventions.* Finding IDs are cited as `[E24d-18]`. Strength tags follow the brief: **A** = at least three kinds of source; **B** = several independent items in one kind, or two kinds; **C** = single source or anecdote; **D** = contested. Counts of oral-history items are **passages in keyword harvests, not rates** (E24a-d select for people who say "humor"). **Independence warning, repeated here once:** E08-E10b are ONE trench paper (one division, two officer-editors, 1916-18); E13a/b are one memoirist; E15a-c one memoirist; E21's six ASAP pages are one organization; E19 and three E21 pages are one cartoonist; E06 re-uses Buzzell, Bellavia and Gallagher, who also appear in E18, E24c and E22a. "Findings" counted across these files overstate independent support.

## Executive summary

Five functions are documented well: **tension release** (stated by participants in almost the same words across armies, but usually located after the danger, not under fire), **cohesion and boundary-making**, **morale** (used on purpose by units, commanders and editors), **controlled complaint upward** within lines the institution tolerates, and **distancing** through euphemism, understatement and displacement onto objects. **Grief** is documented mostly by absence: the dead are kept out of the joke and carried in an adjacent plain register. **Identity policing** (who can take it, who counts as a good soldier) is strong in scholarship and in modern writing. "Opening a conversation" is documented only thinly.

The **targets** with the strongest support are the self, newcomers, seniors/staff/rear echelon (teased by type or role rather than by name), the institution's language and forms, the home front and press, and everyday misery (weather, rations, rum, leave). Two counter-findings hold, with qualifications: **the enemy is a minor target in soldiers' own humor** (leaders, propaganda and luck rather than soldiers), though dehumanizing enemy humor is documented in letters home, songs and memes; and **named own dead are almost never the butt** (the ritual, the paperwork wording, rank or the event is), while stranger and enemy dead sit in a harder register. The wounded own the first joke.

The claims **poorly evidenced** are the popular ones: "humor protects against PTSD" rests on five self-report studies (four cross-sectional) of different constructs (one in-service soldier sample), none measuring gallows content, and they disagree about mocking styles; therapeutic claims come from one organization's testimony; guilt relief is mostly scholars' inference.

Participants converge on a short vocabulary (release, sanity, mateship), partly the presentable version given to interviewers; scholars add socialization and institutional uses participants do not state; editors and promoters add "cheerfulness" and "healing." The documented **failure mode is humor working too well**: standing in for debriefing where disclosure is penalized, and cohesion that disciplines the objector. Resistance humor frequently **serves the organization**. Officer voices and other-ranks voices differ in form and in the stage on which they joke, not in keeping the dead out of the joke.

## 1. Functions with good support (P1-P5, P6-P14)

**Release, cohesion, morale, complaint, rank (P1-P5).** Tension release is the function participants state most, in nearly the same words across unrelated sources: Australians "so we wouldn't go mad" [E24b-14..17], U.S. memoirists "to break the tension" [E24b-34], a corporal laughing "because I don't want to cry" [E25e-67], and 1930 editors on songs that helped a man "pick his uncertain way back to sanity" [E12a-5]. The qualifier is timing. The same sources place the release after danger: "no one chirruped under a bombardment" [E12a-10], fun that often arrives afterward [E02-15], and narrators who separate funny-at-the-time (relief) from funny-later (distance) [E24d-17]. One New York narrator reports no humor at the front (NYSMM-24457, E24c roll-up). Cohesion works through a code that must be decoded; silence is the sanction [E01-2], and a nickname shows the bearer is known [E01-14]. The one quantitative test of the cohesion claim is negative: AC-03 found no association between aggressive or self-defeating humor and connectedness [E04-6], while AC-37's single humor item overlaps with cohesion and morale and adds small independent effects [E04-24]. Morale is the function institutions notice: a German commander knowingly relied on a soldier "responsible for the company's morale" [E01-6], an Indian Army senior described leading "860 minds," not 860 soldiers [E02-6], and the Wipers editors ran an explicit esprit-de-corps program [E08-60]. Discipline runs both ways: an instructor's phrase became the platoon's own mantra [E01-59]; recruits' grotesque gift to a hated instructor tested the instructor's ability to take it [E01-13]; the Wipers editors disciplined speech with an order to "SMILE" and a printed fear of the blue pencil [E09b1-6,22]. Complaint travels upward in licensed or deniable forms: mess-party skits "regulated, not given an open hand" [E02-8]; a polite phrase to a general called "the highest form of disapproval" [E01-58]; nicknames as verdicts on officers that the chain of command cannot formally hear [E24d-2]. Rank sets license: the proven Craftsman was "one of the few who could get away with it" [E01-42]; laughter at a senior's weak joke may be alignment, and one man outside the reporting line did not laugh [E02-7]. *Independence:* the Wipers items are one paper; the Indian findings rest on twelve retirees, nine of them colonels or above [E02 source-shape].

**Grief, identity, distancing, the unsayable, testing, signal (P6-P14).** Grief is documented by what the jokes avoid and by what sits next to them: the dead stated in one clause [E15b-47], "gone" and "cheery faces are missing" [E09a-8], a separate plain poem beside the comic pages [E10a-29], and the Armistice, the one passage in Graves without a joke [E15c-40]. Humor as an active remembrance tool is thinner (P7): toasts and status reports in Richards [E13b-124,131], honest roast-style speech about a dead teammate that draws laughter [E25c-25], the dead "living" in the funniest memory [E24d-29]; the Anzac mock eulogies are for a sick officer and a donkey, not the dead [E11a-26,29]. Identity policing is the best-documented function in scholarship: the humor trains the soldier subject [E01-1], bridges the demand to be dominant and docile at once [E01-19], and sorts who is "in tune" [E01-27]; the conscript "extra" shows the able-bodied norm [E01-28]; women are cast as audience, butt or "honorary" [E01-40,41]. Distancing is named by the 1930 editors as the mechanism of the slang itself [E12b-33] and by Kuiper as reappraisal that "does not deny the negative experience" [E05-8]. The unsayable finds a form: fear named only through a doctor's code and a punishment [E10a-60], premonition and one's own death joked [E24b-16,27], honest wishes placed in classified ads [E09b2-33]. Testing the listener (P11) is review-level (AC-03's shibboleth) plus anecdotes: a newcomer ordered to tell a joke under a superior's eyes [E01-7], an outsider tested by their reaction and removed [E25c-21]. **Opening a conversation (P12) is not documented as a distinct function** in the corpus beyond the nearest items: Graves and Blunden talking themselves into an "almost hysterical state" and agreeing to write it down [E15c-56], one interviewee's relief at being asked about soldier humor [E01-52], and a fiction piece [E25a-43]. In-the-moment signaling (P13) rests on one head-wound account in which jokes told so that everyone would know Tran was OK preceded a delayed breakdown [E21-22], a badly wounded man who keeps up some humor [E24d-9], and the man who goes solemn as a warning [E24b-8]. A register change as clinical early warning (P14) is only a proposal by two civilian studies [E04-13,20], with the causal direction untested [E04-22].

## 2. Functions claimed but poorly evidenced (P15-P17)

**"Humor protects against PTSD" (P15, D).** The five quantitative studies measure five different things, and none measures gallows content [E04 Gaps 1; E05-14]. *Pro:* in the one in-service Army sample (20,901 soldiers analyzed), self-rated ability to "keep your sense of humor in tense situations" had a small independent negative association with PTSD symptoms alongside cohesion and morale, with indirect effects the authors call "miniscule" and a warning that humor may help only until it is used to avoid [E04-23,24,25]; in 179 male firefighters, trait coping humor weakened the link from trauma exposure to PTSD and burnout three months later (low-humor slope b = .40, high-humor not significant) but did not buffer absenteeism [E05-16,17]; in 105 Israeli couples a decade after terror attacks, benign (affiliative, self-enhancing) styles tracked fewer symptoms in the user [E04-27,28]. *Con:* in 2,291 Swiss police officers "dark comic styles were never associated with fewer pathological symptoms, only greater," with cynicism largest [E04-16]; self-defeating humor tracked lower life satisfaction in 93 veterans [E04-8]; injurious styles had almost no link either way in the terror-survivor sample (one of 24 correlations) [E04-29]; Kuiper finds "very limited" support that humor produces growth or bounce-back [E05-12], reports humor users feeling better and then doing worse on the next test [E05-7], and a police cohort with more humor and worse cardiovascular risk [E05-3]. Every study is cross-sectional or two-wave self-report, and all name direction as unresolved [E04-22,30]. **Where sources disagree is on mocking styles** (AC-03, AC-05 against AC-38), not on whether benign, relationship-building humor goes with better outcomes. Only AC-37 and AC-03 involve military samples, and AC-03 mixes active-duty and discharged people with no time-out data [E04 Gaps 4].

**Therapy and guilt (P16, P17).** Comedy-program claims come from organizers and participants: an unpublished evaluation covering all of one organization's arts programs, a "14 percent" less-stress figure relayed second-hand, and a charity's uncited list [E21-35]. The same pages disagree with themselves: "not therapy, but therapeutic" [E21-20] against "telling jokes is my therapy" [E21-22]. Advocacy pieces assert that gallows humor is "essential" [E25c-20..22] and that it keeps people going [E25d-32,41,45], while another veteran's own gallows joke after a friend's death "None of that worked" and telling the story helped [E25c-7]. Guilt relief is attributed by scholars to Gallipoli letters and Vietnam pilots' songs [E02-25,36] and stated by one narrator as "protection for my conscience" [E06-29]; another private joke at a possibly innocent dead civilian produced guilt, not relief [E25b-11].

## 3. Targets: who and what

**Self, newcomers, seniors, the institution, the home front (P18-P22).** The self is the largest single form in the Australian War Memorial set (E24b roll-up) and 10 of 140 Rutgers passages (E24a tallies); in a German unit it is the price of the reciprocity "do ut des" [E01-12], and in Terminal Lance the entitled-veteran strips are a self-check [E19-10,19,29]. The unit itself is a target in its songs and strips: the volunteer and the unit's competence [E12a-14], the lance corporals' own misbehavior [E19-33]. The caution is that the "self-defeating" style tracks lower life satisfaction [E04-8] and that being the butt too often marks a conscript as outside [E01-28]. Newcomers are tested: Indian ragging can ripen into a bonding story for those who coped, with failures missing from the sample [E02-5]; Graves's battalion used silence and a night patrol as admission [E15b-18,19,27]; Australian hoaxes borrow forged authority (a form, a badge) [E24d-4]. Seniors and staff are the dominant upward and sideways target and are teased by type, never by name, with the limit printed on the page ("Yet here I may not name him") [E08-74]. Songs aim at near NCOs and quartermasters, while staff and brass appear only in slang and silence [E12a-7]; command appears in 37 of 140 Rutgers passages (the extractor's coding) and carries the harvester's tag in 50 of 172 UNSW passages [E24a tallies; E24d-39]. The institution's language is a standing target: genre substitution in the Wipers [E08-5,35,73], pink forms and communiqués in Graves [E15a-35,48], acronyms and scrubbed public language in Klay and Abrams [E22a-13,24], the obedient-form question [E19-8], and insider satire because "you make fun of what you know" [E21-37]. The home front is aimed at for ignorance, press and support rituals [E08-70,78; E19-4,24], with a contempt register that the Wipers' own pages come near to [E10a-88].

**The enemy (P23-P24).** The documented counter-finding holds in soldier-made, unit-facing genres. Gregory finds far less separation of enemy from "us" in soldiers' own cartoons than in home-front ones, and the enemy a much smaller target [E07-37,41]. The Wipers ridicule enemy leaders and communiqués far more than enemy soldiers, who appear as co-sufferers, as in the prisoner-cage "hotel" where the writers' own wish for "a real rest" is put in the enemy's mouth [E08 roll-up; E09b2-11,33]. The Anzac Book separates a respected front-line Turk from the comic distant Kaiser [E11a-13,32,34]; Richards records admiration and neighborliness [E13a-9,31,54]; enemy-directed humor is 21 of 140 Rutgers passages [E24a], mostly spectacle, luck and propaganda in UNSW [E24d-8], and a few cartoon-framed lines in the milblogs [E25b-49]. **The counter-evidence is genre-specific and harsher:** vermin imagery, insect jokes about enemy dead and mockery of captives in Gallipoli letters written home [E02-24,25,26] (Kantar reads "pleasurable malice"); Vietnam pilots' black-humor songs [E02-36]; Ukrainian memes that treat enemy mass death as routine [E03-25,26,27], from a purposive and partisan sample whose silence on own-side loss the extraction calls a property of the sample [E03-20; E03 Gaps 3]; and Marines joking at a wounded enemy [E06-28]. UNSW's hard enemy items are the ones narrators label or flinch at, and one narrator who cleared a battlefield found family photographs and says there was no teasing of the dead [E24d-8]. Net: minor target in the unit's own talk, a harder register when the enemy is dead, dying, captive or addressed to the home audience.

**The dead, the wounded, fear, and the dead's equipment (P25-P27).** Named own dead are the most consistent absence in the corpus: no joke at a named dead man across the Wipers files [E08 limits; E09a-8; E10b-46], Richards narrates named deaths plainly, usually with one idiom [E13b-23,24,34], Graves puts the dead in the last clause [E15b-47], the Anzac comic sketch of a dead comrade is pseudonymous and ends in an epitaph [E11a-24], Terminal Lance draws the dead as still serving, and its author apologizes for the one dead person the strip mocked [E19-37,41], and the oral-history sets find the named dead comrade almost never the butt [E24c roll-up; E24d-10]. The butt is the **ritual, the wording, or the stranger**: the casualty-return phrase [E24d-10], the burial that went wrong [E24d-10], an enemy or stranger in a harder register [E24d-10]. Exceptions: honest roast-style speech about a dead teammate [E25c-25], a corpse hoax on civilians [E15c-19], and one private joke at a possibly innocent dead civilian that produced guilt [E25b-11]. The wounded own the first joke: a badly wounded man keeps up some humor [E24d-9]; limb loss is joked about among the wounded but not facial disfigurement or breakdown [E07-41]; slow helpless dying is the thing Graves says cannot be joked over [E15b-3]. A newly wounded officer's rebuke to a laughing group was answered with "What do you want us to do, cry?" [E24d-20]; outsiders' jokes about the wounded are policed [E25b-15,17,55]. Fear and mortality travel through **displaced objects and procedures**: pay that stops at death [E12a-41; E10a-4], gear to be split [E06-16], "borrow" for the dead's kit [E13b-102], a beer and a quarrel over a bed [E24d-10,11], the rum jar mourned ahead of the man [E08-20,49], a command center joking about films, not the casualties on its screens [E25c-13]; the paramedics' version is humor at "events around the periphery" [E04-10]. Fear itself is named in the past tense or in disguise ("the megrims") [E09b3-22; E12b-29].

**Everyday misery, the mission, genre and era (P28-P31).** Weather, mud, rations, rum, leave and vermin dominate soldier-made print, songs and memoir [E08-20,37,55; E11a-15,18,23,35; E12a-46], yet food is nearly absent from the Rutgers harvest (one passage) [E24a roll-up], an instrument effect, since that harvest did not search for it. The mission is targeted obliquely: as competence not purpose [E22a-22], as "playing war" [E02-18], as "We're Here Because" [E12a-20]; in one paper doubt about the outcome was ruled out while complaint about conditions stayed in [E09b1-22; E10a-43], and the editors said plainly what the humor had not only after the guns stopped [E10b-69]. The target mix depends on genre: songs (near NCOs, quartermasters) [E12a-7], papers (staff, press, home), memoir (institutions), the oral-history harvest (danger and own odds 42, command 37, enemy 21, peers and newcomers 20, wounded and dead 17, self 10) [E24a], the webcomic (self, type, institutions) [E19-58], milblogs (up, sideways, inward) [E25b-49]. Branch contrasts in the UNSW set are modest (navy: boredom humor, a notice-board of letters, the captain's nickname; air: radio-net wit and the beer-and-belongings routine; infantry: the sergeant major, company commander and rear echelon) [E24d-40]. Era and army differences are asserted by single sources only: Vietnam lifers, brass and rear echelon [E02-34,35], post-9/11 "pogue/Fobbit/POG" exposure ladder [E25d-3,29,37], 1990s peacekeeping with register-policing toward hosts [E24d-37,38]; E24c finds the same near-miss and captor-gag forms in four national forces and no supportable differences [E24c-30].

## 4. Whose function is it? (P32-P34)

**Participants.** Their own explanations converge on a short vocabulary: release, sanity, tension, mateship [E24d-12; E24b-14..17]. Two cautions come from the extractions themselves. The explanation offered to an interviewer is the presentable one, and the question shapes the answer: scripted "amusement or light relief" prompts produced tame anecdotes, stress and coping questions produced dark ones [E24b-20..23]. And veterans seldom call their own humor a mask; the cost is noted mainly by outsiders such as a niece or an interviewer (E24a roll-up). In memoirs the claims are occasional: Graves offers statements on particular occasions, not a theory [E15a roll-up], and Richards gives almost none beyond "It made us merry and helped us to forget" [E13b-30]. Pinkston's own method separates where narrators state a function (Bellavia, King, Delgado, Buzzell) from where Pinkston supplies it [E06 Gaps 1].

**Scholars** add functions participants do not state: socialization into the soldier subject [E01-1], bridging a built-in contradiction between virtues [E01-19], sustaining the institution and its violence [E01-47], Kantar's five functions including a hostile one, insulting the opponent [E02-23], guilt relief (the authors' inference [E02-25,36]), and Mogstad's argument that society tolerates gallows humor because reading it as coping "reinforces" the picture of war as tragedy [E02-11]. Pinkston's slide from therapy through aggression to violence-as-joke is a borrowed-theory scheme with no counts [E06-5; E06 Gaps 1]. Scholars also disagree: Davies's "not a strategy in war" against the weapon and survival camps [E07-2].

**Editors, institutions and promoters** frame the humor beyond the texts: the 1930 foreword's "cheerfulness" against the editors' own "more often hysterical than natural" [E08-1,3]; the 1930 song editors' "typically English irony" [E12b Gaps 1]; a magazine editor's national-character theory and an introducer's portrait of Sassoon [E11b-27,45]; the obituary genre that tells only safe jokes, about discomfort rather than trauma [E01-45,46]; reviewers' "hilarious" as marketing [E18 Gaps 5]; index lines that over-read an essay [E25c Gaps 4]; program pages that promise healing [E21-35].

## 5. Humor that works too well (P35-P37)

**It stands in for debriefing, and disclosure is penalized.** The UNSW "limits" set is the clearest instrument: of 24 passages sorted by what they show, only a few show a joke failing; more show humor working too well, with four in which humor replaces debriefing and the institution lets it [E24d-18]. A Somalia infantryman describes early-1990s debriefing as "laugh about it and sort of off with it" and says serving soldiers deny problems because "the army'll probably get rid of em" [E24d-23]. Australian veterans at reunions "talk about the funny things" and "nobody introduces the subject," detail fades with no second teller, and in one family the children met the serious material by film [E24d-24]. Paramedics say the humor "covers up processing" and "creeps into family" life [E04-11]; AC-37's authors speculate that humor helps until it is used to avoid [E04-25]; Sandbox and milblog posts, Fox's softened stories and an essayist's remark that containment "calcifies" describe a funny version posted or told and an unfunny remainder withheld [E25b-48; E25e-81; E25d-40]. Graves declined psychiatric cure to protect the poetry, and Graves's wife asked for silence about the war [E15c-70,74]. *Counter:* one veteran says the story, told, helped where the joke did not [E25c-7]; no UNSW narrator says they stopped being able to joke [E24d-18].

**Cohesion that excludes or disciplines.** In a Danish conscript company, laughter "seemed to stick to some more than others" [E01-28], taking a punch silently performed belonging [E01-30], and women describing daily sexualized comments feared being "killjoys" if a farewell party were canceled [E01-31]. A British obituary praised a private at the center of laughter over "personal admin" and omitted a death by suicide during deployment; Tidy treats banter and hazing as one logic coded differently [E01-43]. Modern essays record pressure to laugh along with contempt for a minority soldier [E25c-4,5], a webcomic community that decays into contempt for outsiders and women [E19-17], joke-victims who accept teasing [E25b-12], a prank designed around a just-returned friend's real danger [E25e-49], and a group that punished anonymous humor collectively [E25b-46]. Women appear mostly as butt, audience or signal in the UNSW set [E24d-35]; AC-06 and AC-01 report some women's inclusion [E02-16; E01-19]. **Laughter that accompanies violence against helpless people** is documented in restrained terms by Tidy and Pinkston [E01-44; E06-5,10,13,30,36] and in a Gallipoli account of mockery of captives [E02-25]; in Graves's mess, officers analyzing prisoner-killing did not joke [E15b-75].

## 6. Funny versus the institution's use of the humor (P38-P39)

The scholarship's most repeated finding is that resistance humor is **tolerated because it does not destabilize**: a "controlled form of resistance" that vents anger without destabilizing the institution or hurting performance [E01-1]; humor that "makes people stay" and so stabilizes [E01-57,61]; a Kosovo commander who used the joker's skill for the commander's own ends [E01-6]; leadership that sponsors and polices a vague "certain jargon" [E01-26]; a licensed mess-party outlet that also warns command of tensions [E02-8]; cartoons that "do not generally cross the lines of authority" [E07-26]; senior officers who knowingly supported the Wipers [E08-1,4]; satire tolerated or absorbed [E21 roll-up]; and early-1990s Australian debriefing that amounted to laughing it off [E24d-23]. Tidy's caution is that scholars have overstressed rupture [E01-47]. The institution also **uses** soldier humor outward: obituaries that make the war look like a fun job [E01-45], magazines sent to the trenches and merchandise after [E07-29,46], an open call for "lawless" war jokes that drew mostly clean ones [E21-1], public-affairs copy [E21-21], and a defense department that flies entertainers to war zones [E02-44]. It **polices** at the edges: a prosecution over one cartoon [E07-17], banned patches [E01-63], a command that punished a sardonic blog [E18-16], a verse ordering pessimists to "SMILE" [E09b1-22], and an old soldier certified "N.Y.D.-Mental" whose cost argument was a complete case against the war [E10a-77].

## 7. Officer voices and other-ranks voices (P40, only where the files support it)

*Officer voices:* Graves, Sassoon, the Wipers editors (an officers'-mess paper by inference; the ranks speak only through officer-written dialect [E08-28]). *Other-ranks voices:* Richards, the Anzac Book's private contributors (editor-curated, written for a home audience [E11a source-shape]), and most oral-history narrators (Rutgers rank is not coded [E24a]; most UNSW narrators are other ranks [E24d-40]).

**Form and stage.** Officer wit runs through written and institutional forms (charge sheets, forms, communiqués, a mock letter [E15a roll-up]; canonical parody and mock ads in the Wipers [E08 roll-up]) and through mess ritual: the silent "warts," the night patrol as admission, a mock society and a corpse hoax in a convalescent home, where Graves says "Anything to make people laugh. But it was hard work" [E15b-18,19,27; E15c-18,19]. Sassoon's satire aims at staff, press, clergy, women and home-front elders [E11b-47..57]. Other ranks joke through yarns, curses, sayings, nicknames and refusal: collective profanity as an answer to orders, sabotage with an alibi, a staged prank on a staff officer [E13a-14,38; E13b-11], medals and doctors and pension boards as targets [E13b-100,150,152].

**Rank and license.** Officers tell of being pranked and taking it well; other ranks tell of the reprisal and its cost [E24d-40]. Graves's company mess may laugh at staff orders, but the men are not to be told [E15b-35]; Richards's officers laugh at a charge and are bound to pass it up [E13b-6]. Junior officers kept silent in the senior mess and muttered privately [E15b-18,20].

**Function claims.** Graves offers occasional claims ("the joke contributed greatly to my recovery"; an officer's job "to make things easier for the men") [E15b-120,125]; Richards says little [E13b-30].

**What is shared.** Staff, orders and decorations; territorials; chaplains with an honored exception; the wound as a prize; nicknames; courtesy toward the enemy; no joke at a named comrade's death, the saddest news in the plainest sentence [E15b roll-up contrast with E13a/E13b]. **What is unreconciled:** Graves's "not permitted to be raised" talk of wounds in a good battalion against Richards's old hands admiring self-wounders [E15b-65; E13b-73]. Both are one Royal Welch regiment, so rank cannot be separated from regiment, battalion or period; and the enlisted voice in Graves is quoted briefly and classified by the narrator [E15a roll-up].

## 8. Contradictions and open questions

**Contradictions (IDs on both sides).**
1. *Does humor protect?* [E04-24; E05-16; E04-27] against [E04-16,17; E04-8; E04-29; E05-12; E05-7]. Not reconcilable: different constructs, populations and outcomes.
2. *Does humor bond?* No association between two humor styles and connectedness [E04-6] against participants saying a loud team is a well-bonded one [E02-9] and the small independent effect in AC-37 [E04-24].
3. *Is humor rank-blind or rank-shaped?* "Fun" disregards rank [E02-11,16] against humor strongly shaped by rank [E02-7]; different words (fun versus humor).
4. *Is there humor at the front?* one New York narrator reporting no humor at the front (E24c roll-up) and "no one chirruped" [E12a-10] against men making wisecracks under their first shelling [E24d-20]; Gilbert, Morrow and Church recall nothing funny where Spinkston finds something funny in practically everything (E24b Gaps 10). May reflect rank, unit, setting or temperament.
5. *Talk of wounds.* "Not permitted to be raised" in a good battalion [E15b-65] against old hands admiring self-wounders [E13b-73; E13a-24,29]; one regiment.
6. *Staff in song.* Staff do not appear in song [E12a-7] against a leap-frog parody with a staff stanza [E12a-31].
7. *The enemy as target.* Minor target [E07-37,41; E24d-8] against vermin imagery, captive mockery, dying-enemy jokes and war memes [E02-24..26; E03-25..27; E06-28].
8. *Women.* Inclusion reported [E02-16; E01-19] against exclusion and the killjoy trap [E01-31; E24d-35].
9. *Therapy.* "Not therapy, but therapeutic" [E21-20] against "telling jokes is my therapy" [E21-22]; "essential" [E25c-22] against "None of that worked" [E25c-7].
10. *Do soldiers' jokes get darker than popular pictures say?* Gregory's cartoons are less dark, from a small civilian-filtered sample the thesis itself warns against over-reading [E07-33; E07 Gaps 7b] against first-hand texts that are darker [E24d-10; E06-28].
11. *Does the humor stop after service?* No UNSW narrator says they stopped being able to joke [E24d-18] against one who lost humor in service and found it after leaving [E25d-16], an inability to laugh at an ordinary joke [E25c-37], a self-reported darkening [E25a-31], and an unverified claim that jokes "lose its healing cautery" [E21-2]. **D.**
12. *Front and home humor.* "Remarkably similar" [E07-8] against civilian incomprehension documented in the same thesis and in memoir [E07-38; E15b-34,101,119,122].

**Open questions.** Who the audience is at the moment of each joke (almost no source records it in situ). What happens 10-15 years out: the evidence is one man's decade [E15c], reunion narrators [E24d-24], one thesis memoir [E25e], and a few essays. **Defectors:** the only first-hand deserter jokes are about a dozen lines from two Russian deserters in one news feature, where the humor stops when the subject becomes the command's treatment of its own men [E23-1,2,6; E23 Gaps 1]; Vietnam-era deserter sources contain no deserter's joke [E23 Gaps 1]; Syrian defectors' report has no humor at all, which is an absence, not evidence [E23-25]. **Boone:** nothing in the screen, games and consulting set concerns Boone's humor [E22b-12]. **Bodily and excreta material** is excluded from the UNSW harvest by project boundary, so a large register of ordinary army humor is unassessed as a target [E24d Gaps]. **Shell shock and breakdown** are essentially unjoked in print (one clause [E10a-79]; undrawn [E07-31]). No source counts how often any function or target occurs; all tallies are of passages in keyword harvests.

## 10. Design-relevant candidates

*Each is a candidate drawn from documented mechanism, not a specification, not dialogue, and not tied to any particular army. Strength tags are the pattern's.*

1. **Candidate (A):** Specify a character's humor by function and by target separately; a scene can make clear which job a joke is doing (release, belonging, complaint, test, signal) without stating it. [P1-P5]
2. **Candidate (A):** The release is retrospective; under danger the register is flat, quiet or absent, and the funny version is told afterward. [E12a-10; E24d-17; E02-15]
3. **Candidate (A):** Safe, frequent targets are upward and sideways (command, staff, rear, other services) and inward (self, one's own type); seniors are teased by role, type or private nickname rather than by name. [P18-P22; E24d-2]
4. **Candidate (A):** Named own dead are not the butt; the ritual, the paperwork wording or the event may be, and a stranger or enemy dead sits in a harder register the teller may flag or flinch from. [P25; E24d-10]
5. **Candidate (A):** Grief is carried in a separate plain register: one flat clause after the funny story, often the last sentence, with no joke added after it. [P6; E25b-53; E15b-47]
6. **Candidate (B):** A character with a chronic injury may hold the first license over jokes about it and may police outsiders' jokes about the wounded. [P26; E24d-9; E25b-55]
7. **Candidate (A, with the second clause B):** The enemy is not the usual target of unit talk; where enemy-directed humor appears it belongs to home-facing, helpless-target or propaganda-adjacent registers that the character may remember with unease. [P23, P24]
8. **Candidate (A):** Humor can substitute for debriefing where disclosure costs; a fluent, funny character who cannot say a plain sentence about themself is a documented type. [P35; E24d-23]
9. **Candidate (A):** What is withheld is part of the characterization: the funny-only telling at reunions, the stories kept from family out of consideration, the unfunny remainder never posted. [E24d-24; E01-54; E25b-48]
10. **Candidate (A):** Cohesion has a price; the person who objects becomes the killjoy, and the group may remember a departed member for the one time they stopped laughing. [P36; E01-27]
11. **Candidate (C):** An observer who knows a person's usual register may notice when it changes; present this as a plausible, untested signal, not a diagnostic. [P14]
12. **Candidate (B):** In the moment, joking can tell the unit that a person is still functional after a hit; the delayed recognition may come later and elsewhere. [P13; E21-22]
13. **Candidate (A):** Humor-as-complaint is tolerated until it crosses a line the institution polices; a character who left on principle may have run out of deniable forms, which is a documented cost of the form itself. [P4, P38; E01-58]
14. **Candidate (A):** A character may keep two vocabularies, the unit's and the one for civilians, and give outsiders the presentable explanation. [E02-12; E24b roll-up]
15. **Candidate (B):** A joke can be a test: watch the face, expect silence as the sanction; being sincerely asked may itself be a relief (C). [P11, P12]
16. **Candidate (B):** Fear is named in the past tense, in disguise, or through a form; "not afraid, but" is a documented construction. [E10a-60; E12b-29]
17. **Candidate (A):** Self-mockery is both a leader's currency and the "take it" half of reciprocity; a character may have lost the taking half without noticing the group's cohesion rested on it. [E01-12; E02-6]
18. **Candidate (B):** If the character was an officer, their wit more plausibly runs through written, institutional and mess-ritual forms; if enlisted, through yarns, sayings, nicknames and deniable defiance. Both keep the dead out of the joke. [P40]
19. **Candidate (B):** Laughter that is not humor (laughing until crying, a giggle at the wrong moment) is documented as a symptom; a companion who has seen it may react to a kind of silence the character does not know they make. [E25e-18; E24d-25]
20. **Candidate (B):** Without a unit the register survives where colleagues or a therapy group share it, and ex-unit threads act as a check-in channel, but the unit's jokes stop working without the unit. [E24d-30; E25d-19; E25b-36]
21. **Candidate (D):** Do not write humor as reliably protective against trauma, or as reliably a symptom; the research supports benign, relationship-building humor going with better outcomes more consistently than mocking styles, and nothing measures gallows content. [P15]
22. **Candidate (C):** Boredom, not terror, is the most-stated engine of everyday unit humor in the essay corpus; guilt relief is plausible but rarely stated and should not be presented as established. [E25c-1,24; P17]
