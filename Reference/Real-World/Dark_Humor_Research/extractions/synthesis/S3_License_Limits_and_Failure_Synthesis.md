## 1. Executive summary

License to joke about death and injury is **relational, not topical**. The corpus documents three checks that operate together: *who* (shared risk and presence; the injured own the first joke), *before whom* (in-group, rank present or absent, civilians, even a different unit or specialty), and *reciprocity* (the joker must also be able to take it). Silence is the cheapest sanction; refusing to laugh costs social standing, and the documented costs are described mainly for women and minority soldiers [E01-27, E01-31, E25c-4].

The most consistent no-go zone is the **named dead comrade** (strength A, about 25 items across WWI text, oral history and modern writing). Close behind are slow dying and grave wounds, a bereaved family present, and, with more variation, fear in another man in the present tense, breakdown, executions, and one's own side's lethal blunders. What *is* joked about is the periphery: institutions, wording, staff, the odds, oneself. One production's stated rule is aim "at the madness" and not the dead [E22b-1].

Limits are **signaled by register, not announced**: a plain, unjoked beat, often one sentence, with no joke added after it [E25b-53, E08-38, E15c-40]; a farewell; a stated refusal by an editor; silence; an expected expletive left out [E12a-11]. A change in a person's humor is read by peers as a distress signal [E24b-8, E24d-19, E22b-16]. The Dutch-firefighter study the brief names for this is **not in the corpus** (fetch failed; Tier C item C-02).

Failure modes are well documented: misfire (the laugher's position, not the topic, makes it wrong), masking (usually named by outsiders, seldom by the veteran), contempt and numbness (the Abu Ghraib frame), hazing that shares banter's logic, and the quip after the kill. The right to laugh typically arrives **later** than the event.

Sources **conflict** on self-wounders (admired or unspeakable), on staff (exempt from satire or its main target), on suicide jokes (honor or failed shield), on whether dark humor alienates civilians, and on whether it protects or harms. **Nothing in the corpus documents a principled defector's license**; the nearest items are thin and mostly single-source.

## 3. Detailed sections

### 3.1 The earned right: who may joke about death and injury (S3-P1, P2, P10)

The corpus states the earned-right rule in the participants' own voices and in scholarship, in the same shape across wars. Veteran comics say "we've kind of earned the right" because "we're the ones that went" [E21-18]; a milblogger holds that soldiers laugh at almost anything, "There is one caveat; you must belong to that club," and extends it to allies who shared the war [E25b-15, E25b-16]. Oral histories show the injured owning the first joke and bystanders being judged: a wounded soldier complains about the rescuers' lateness, a sergeant reasons that laughing proves the wound is not fatal, and a Korea veteran recounts reserve officers laughing at a wounded pilot's "million dollar wound" and links it to the narrator's own later reduced sympathy [E24d-9, E24a-9]. Graves puts it as a rule from the officers' side: one can joke with a badly wounded man and disregard a dead one [E15b-3]. Lyons draws the line by severity: jokes about the wound a man walks away from, none about the grave ones [E25c-8].

Scholarship transfers the rule from soldiers to other groups (victims may joke about their ordeal and their persecutors; descendants of perpetrators may not) [E03-16], and Tidy's obituary study records one Craftsman "one of the few who could get away with it" [E01-42].

**Independence:** E21-18 and E21-20 are one article about one show; the E21 set as a whole is six pages from one organization plus one documentary [E21 gaps]; E25b-15 and E25b-16 are one blogger. **Counter-evidence is thin but real:** an injured comic long doubted that survivors may joke at all [E25d-36], and the cast of a trench sitcom described nervousness and answered it with exact detail and a stated target, "the madness," not the dead [E22b-1, recalled intent]. Intent matters in several accounts: to cope or remember, not for a cheap laugh [E03-16, E22a-60, E25c-25].

### 3.2 Audience, rank and the room (S3-P3, P4, P5)

A joke works only if the audience can decode it through a shared interpretive system, and the group's reprimand is the absence of laughter [E01-2]. Three audience limits recur. **Civilians:** paramedics fear being overheard and say "reading the room" is the rule [E04-9]; a veteran comic's story fails because civilians keep asking what the jargon means [E21-33]; one Australian veteran says the register is what "in civvie street you'd be in jail for" and judges for themselves who may hear it [E24d-27]. **Other units:** a joke that is said to "kill" with troops in theater died with a deployed Marine over a satellite link [E25d-1, E25d-2]; drug jokes flop with active-duty troops [E21-33]. **Rank:** the price of a quip at authority depends on place and listener, with outcomes ranging from punitive drill to a canteen laugh [E24b-9]; the Wipers Times teases seniors by type or nickname and never by name [E08-74], WWI songs target the ranks close at hand and are silent on staff [E12a-7], and WWII narrators say jokes about brass were mostly silent or retrospective [E24c-4]; an Indian Army officer outside the joker's reporting line could withhold the laugh [E02-7].

For "who, when, how often," the evidence is thinner. Lab and online experiments (not soldiers) find that an inappropriate joke is read as incompetence and that silence is the worst outcome, while one early laugh can make a risky joke safe [E05-28, E05-30]. In soldier sources, a superior's recurring joke decays into a hazard the subordinates cannot refuse [E25a-33, E25a-35]. **The Dutch-firefighter study the brief names for "who, when, how often" is not in the corpus** (manifest AC-33, fetch failed, 0 words; Tier C item C-02).

### 3.3 Reciprocity, newcomers and nicknames (S3-P6, P7, P8)

Reciprocity is the license in the German, British and Indian material: every player may attack and be attacked, and "being able to take it" is part of status [E01-3, E01-12, E02-9]. Newcomers are tested rather than tolerated: an ordered joke told under superior eyes, where the silence beforehand is the danger [E01-7]; ragging remembered fondly by those who coped (the failures are absent from the sample) [E02-5]; fake errands as the largest prank family in the Rutgers harvest [E24a-4]; an officers' mess that ignores new officers for about six months and plays "chasing the warts," which the narrator reads as "honestly intended" training [E15b-18, E15b-27]. The register is learned, and a cheerful newcomer can be shut out by a unit that does not joke [E24a-14, E24a-13].

Nickname rules are the most detailed license in the oral histories: rank removes the license, the worst thing is to rebel, and a name's life depends on the bearer's toughness [E24d-2, E24d-3]. A group may notice a nickname built on a man's losses has started to wound, drop it, and still find the man carrying it decades later [E24b-6]. An Indian Army participant says nicknames are essential but "never to put the person down" [E02-9].

**Caution:** much of this is retrospective fondness from people who coped; E02-5 says outright that those who could not cope with ragging are missing.

### 3.4 Safe targets: the periphery, the institution, the odds (S3-P9)

Where the sources specify what is joked about, they converge on the periphery of the event: circumstance, logistics, wording, institution. Paramedics aim at "events around the periphery" [E04-10]; Australian narrators joke about the event, the ritual or the institution's wording, not the dead comrade [E24d-10]; a Helmand command center joked about films and impressions while casualty reports scrolled [E25c-13]; the WWI papers aim at staff, rations, rum, leave and the home press [E08-74, E12a-7]; one trench sitcom's team stated its rule as target "the madness," not the dead [E22b-1]. The commonest Rutgers target is danger and one's own odds [E24a-1], and the Sandbox posts aim up, sideways and inward, rarely at the enemy [E25b-49].

**Scope caution:** E04-10 is paramedics; E22b-1 is a recalled intent about a fictional production; both are analogies to soldier practice, not soldier evidence.

### 3.5 No-go: the dead and the gravely wounded (S3-P11, P12, P21)

The named dead comrade is the corpus's most consistent exclusion. In the Wipers Times, no joke is made at the expense of a named dead man across the whole run (one paper); losses appear as euphemism, a count, or a signed plain poem [E08-38, E09b2-28, E10b-46]. The Anzac Book's sketches of a comrade who dies use a pseudonym and end at a grave marker [E11a-24], and its burial-party poem states where the joking stops [E11a-50]. Richards reports named deaths in one flat idiom [E13a-4]; Graves puts the dead in a closing clause [E15a-22, E15a roll-up] and writes the Armistice with the news of two named deaths and no joke [E15c-40]. Oral-history extractors find the dead "almost never the butt" [E24a-9, E24d-10]; one officer refuses even a retrospective gallows laugh at a man killed by a supply box: "a certain respect among the men" [E24d-21]. Modern writers describe a command-center room going silent at a name [E25c-13] and a corpsman hurt by joking in ambulances carrying the dead [E25d-41]. Slow dying is the one thing Graves says cannot be joked over [E15b-3].

**What the four WWI sources set in plain text.** *Wipers Times:* named dead, a friend's grief poem, farewells to patrons, the memorial block, the Armistice poem, and earnest hatred of the enemy command, all outside the joking devices [E08-38, E09a-27, E09b2-28, E10b-49, E09b1-28]; objectors meet contempt, not comedy [E08-47]. *Anzac Book:* the burial party and the paperwork [E11a-50], a norm that weeping is for "meaner deaths" [E11a-14], the commanders' orders and the evacuation without a joke [E11b-12], a grieving man and a suicide in Sassoon's verse [E11b-64]. *Richards:* named comrades in one flat idiom, shell-shocked officers, the feared fates, a commuted death sentence, prisoners killed by a grieving escort [E13a-4, E13b-22, E13b-146, E13b-39, E13b-92]. *Graves:* slow dying, suicides, executions as one clause, the Armistice, the memorial reading [E15b-3, E15a-46, E15b-7, E15c-40, E15c-77].

**Documented exceptions:** an honest, unflattering truth about a dead Ranger that draws a roar and is then followed by honor [E25c-25]; a retold last exchange with a man who died soon after [E24c-31]; a comic's suicide jokes said to honor friends [E21-20]; a squadron's casualty-return quip about an unnamed drowned soldier [E24d-10].

**Out-group dead are different and costly.** Casualties among foreign laborers are the punchline of one Anzac parable [E11b-2]; the correspondent parody shades into cruelty in a 1918 Russia dispatch [E10b-72]; and a regular's memoir records a dead enemy officer's body used as a prop to shock a staff colonel, with a disclaimer of disrespect and a proper burial after [E13b-11]; modern writers record a run-over civilian becoming a unit joke and a photograph traded for [E25e-8] and a sheep-triggered blast laughed at beside a child's injury [E25e-62]. Pinkston reads such laughter as dehumanization [E06-10, E06-13]; Tidy reads it as a one-sided pleasure that binds the perpetrators [E01-44].

### 3.6 No-go: fear, breakdown, self-wounding, desertion, executions, own blunders (S3-P13 to P17)

**Fear.** The one tolerated frame is the admission in the past tense, made casually: "wind-up" implied "no disgrace," while "windy" said of another man in the present was derogatory [E12b-29]. Close variants appear: fear named in an archaic voice so that the reader can look at it ("the megrims") [E09b3-22], a newcomer's verse confessing a failure of courage in plain words [E11b-8], and a diarist's joke at their own expense as the only safe way to say the wish to leave [E02-28]. "Cold feet" was a character fault [E12a-66]. Australian veterans say fear goes through a joke or through silence [E24d-13]. Contempt for fear is applied unevenly: Richards scorns five signallers in a shell hole yet is sympathetic to a young signaller who cannot bear shellfire [E13b-25, E13b-36]. Pranks on frightened men are reported without rebuke [E13a-52, E15b-11]; a victim of a prank that exploited real fear calls the pranksters "bastards" and finds it funny in retelling [E25b-6]; a laugh at a panicked man was cut short by a narrator who recognized their own fear in it [E25a-25].

**Breakdown.** Wipers mentions shell shock once, as a dismissive aside in a mock medical lecture ("the last endeavor of the unwilling"), the one place the topic is joked about in that paper [E10a-79]; Richards states two shell-shocked officers as sights, never joked about [E13b-22]; a captain banned wisecracks about a man who had broken down, and the man broke again two days later [E24a-16].

**Own blunders.** Friendly fire is the one lethal error of one's own side that recurs across the corpus. Richards narrates two incidents without a joke [E13a-7, E13a-23]; a Korea-era pilot read a maintenance chief's laugh over a failure traced to the chief's own crew as callous [E24a-18]; a near miss with no casualty can be told as a story [E25c-31]. In the Wipers Times the infantry jibe that no gunner admits a short round [E09b2-27], the gunners answer in rhyme that keeps one sincere line and a shared tear [E09a-60], and a later verse closes the quarrel with a statement of trust [E09b1-25].

**Executions** appear as one clause [E15b-7], a second-hand account in unadorned prose [E15a-50], or a gap between orders and a minister's denial [E15c-5]. Wipers never mentions them [E08 shape notes]. The one documented joke form is the condemned man's own understatement [E05-45, E22a-45].

### 3.7 No-go: family, women, minorities, suicide (S3-P18 to P20)

**Family.** A mock death letter was thrown away because the parents would not see the humor [E06-24]; a German soldier would never share deployment fun with a family coping alone [E01-54]; paramedics seldom tell family the distressing parts [E04-12]; Graves's wife asked Graves not to talk about the war [E15c-74]; and humor goes quiet before the bereaved: wounded patients before Gold Star mothers (a likely fictional account) [E25a-15], a veteran "caught laughing" as a dead friend's mother entered [E24d-21].

**Women and minorities.** The documented costs are consistent across kinds of source. In Danish conscript life, fear of being the "killjoy" kept women from reporting harassment, and by the author's account kept men from objecting too [E01-31, E01-27]; German women must answer tests loudly, quickly and reversibly [E01-16]; Tidy's "honorary man" is accepted by banter [E01-41]; a German soldier says men stop sharing certain jokes when women are present, while banned jokes persist informally per female soldiers (live-fetch paraphrase, unverified) [E01-63]; a modern essay records the penalty for refusing racist "jokes" as being labeled "that guy" [E25c-4, E25c-5]; a comic strip's author notes humor communities decay into contempt for outsiders and women [E19-17]; an Australian commander banned ethnic naming [E24d-22]. **Caution:** E25c-4 and E25c-5 are advocacy and no teller of the contested jokes speaks.

**Suicide** is contested (section 4): one comic says suicide jokes honor lost friends [E21-20]; another veteran's gallows line after a friend's suicide "did not work" [E25c-7]; WWI memoirs tell suicides plainly [E15a-46, E15c-8].

### 3.8 How limits are signaled (S3-P22 to P28, P39)

**A register shift to plain text** is the dominant signal. The Wipers Times prints grief as a signed or marked poem with no joke [E08-38], switches mid-item from the joke to a plain memory [E08-40], states loss in one clause and refuses to continue [E09b2-16], and prints the dead as a block with no device [E09b2-28]. The same shape appears in Graves (the Armistice passage has no joke at all [E15c-40]) and in modern writing, where "the plain beat usually has no joke added after it" [E25b-53]; Fox places the plain sentence at the end of a funny piece [E25e roll-up]. Narrators in oral history announce the change aloud [E24a-30].

**A farewell** serves similarly [E09a-27, E09b3-21, E10b-80]. **Silence** is sanction and signal [E01-2, E24d-20, E24c-7]. **Editors' stated refusals** include the "earnest periodical" that refuses smut [E08-54], a contributor's verse that announces that the speaker must "take heed what I write" before its strongest line on the war's cost and names the editor's blue pencil [E09b1-6], a verse ordering doubters to "cut the grousing" and smile [E09b1-22], and a verse appealing to hush doubters [E10a-43]. **Omission** also signals: an expected expletive dropped meant urgency [E12a-11], and the Last Post was never given words [E12b-41] (one pair of editors; C). **Command and censor** police humor: a sardonic blog read literally and punished [E18-16], guards posted at latrines over graffiti [E25b-46], a banned defection joke in a Berlin brigade [E24a-16].

**Distress signaling.** A man who "goes solemn" is a warning sign [E24b-8]; the teller's disgust is the stop signal for a radiographer [E24d-19]; a proposal to read dark-humor profiles as early warning is untested [E04-20, E04-22].

### 3.9 Failure modes (S3-P29 to P36)

**Misfire** is a matter of position, not topic: a responsible party laughing [E24a-18], a joke that dies with a different unit [E25d-2], a retort that offends a clerk [E25b-45], a satire believed literally [E25d-34], laughter before contact read by a sergeant as disbelief [E25d-4]. **Masking:** relatives and interviewers name the cover more often than veterans do [E24a-19]; paramedics themselves name detachment and creep into home life [E04-11]; one veteran says they were "covering up" for ten years until a spouse said they were not the same [E24c-22]; Vonnegut's quarantine of Dresden is read as deflection that returns uninvited [E05-51]. **Contempt and numbness:** the memoirist who judges others' laughter at abuse images, then laughs involuntarily at a photograph of a dead man [E06-25]; a nickname for a convoy-flattened civilian [E25e-8]; Tidy's cases of laughter during violence against prisoners [E01-44]; the 1918 correspondent parody that cheapens a massacre [E10b-72]. Counter-moments exist: a private joke at a possibly innocent dead civilian that produced guilt [E25b-11], and the one officer at Sand Creek who refused to take part [E06-13].

**Hazing and killjoy.** Banter and hazing "share a logic" and differ chiefly in how they are coded; in one documented case the man at the center of the laughter died while deployed; the obituary did not record it, the inquest raised bullying, and witnesses denied it [E01-43]. **The quip after the kill** is named a design failure: an outlet reports a publisher's admission that the humor "went down like a lead balloon" [E22b-19]. **Accuracy versus the joke:** an advisor says accuracy yields to budget and, once, to a joke [E22b-27]; a novelist says strict honesty is not the standard [E22a-15]; Heller says a comic war book tells the reader it is arranged [E22a-39]; Fox says the stories were softened for civilian readers and were still found "too much" [E25e-81] (craft statements, not field evidence). **Pranks on fear** cost the victim a shaking hand [E25b-6]. **Laughter as symptom:** a giggle under a bed read as a flashback, not a joke [E24d-25]; escalating "jokes" about dying that exhaust the few who remain [E22b-16].

### 3.10 Time, distance and the room without the unit (S3-P37, P38, P40, P41)

Narrators state the rule: funny later, not then, or funny only while you are not the one involved [E24a-12, E24d-17]; the authors of the Indian Army study note that each added year makes a ragging episode funnier [E02-5]. Heller says a hurt (bad reviews, not war) becomes jokeable once the outcome is secure [E22a-42]. The WWI papers show a gradient inside the war: little humor at first, more as the war settled [E07-44], and plain statements, "the vilest disaster," printed only after the guns stopped [E10b-69, E10b-46]; the Wipers editors themselves called the laughter "more often hysterical than natural" [E08-3]. **Vietnam's late comedy** is two novelists' impression, "30 years late," not a survey [E22a-30]. An injured comic took a long break from the stage and dated the return to two years before the piece [E25d-36].

**Audience loss changes the joke:** Sassoon's verse says "solitude annuls / Our earthly jokes" [E11b-59]; Gregory reads Bairnsfather's post-war figure as left "without anyone to roll his eyes at" [E07-48]; a milblogger says that once the unit is gone it "just isn't as funny anymore" [E25b-36].

**Civilian alienation** is contested (section 4): concealment deepens alienation in German interviews [E01-56], whereas the one quantitative test found no measurable alienation [E04-6].

## 4. Contradictions and open questions

**C1. Were self-wounders admired or unspeakable? (S3-P15, D).** *Admired or neutrally amused:* Richards's narrator calls two pairs who shot each other "wise birds" who left "honorably wounded," and records that youngsters were "receiving lovely blighty wounds" while old hands expected a quick death [E13b-73, E13b-17]; Welsh other ranks counted a clean leg or arm wound as luck [E13a-24]; Graves privately calculates the best way to be wounded [E15b-22]. *Unspeakable or serious:* the 1930 editors give the self-inflicted wound its own entry with no joke, "a most serious crime," camouflaged by sympathetic superiors but met with special hospitals "severe to the pitch of persecution" [E12b-20], while the code admired the skillful lead-swinger only if the swinger did not endanger others [E12b-20]; and Graves says that in a good battalion going home wounded "was not permitted to be raised" [E15b-65]. The E15b gaps note this may reflect rank, battalion or period rather than a contradiction. **Independence:** Richards and Graves are one regiment; the editors' claims are retrospective (1930).

**C2. Were staff exempt from satire? (S3-P4).** The songs editors say staff officers drew only "dark and dangerous emotions," not satire, in song [E12a-7], yet they print a leap-frog parody with a staff stanza [E12a-31; E12a gaps 2a]; the Wipers Times, an officers' paper, targets staff in nearly every issue [E08-74, E09a roll-up, E10a roll-up]; Richards and Graves mock staff and orders, in one case with a dead enemy officer used as a prop to shock a staff colonel [E13b-11, E15b-35]; Graves's company commander, Captain Thomas, laughs at a staff plan with the officers and asks that the men not be told [E15b-35, candidate-application note and roll-up]. Best reading: the exemption is medium- and rank-specific (privates' songs versus an officers' print paper versus private mess talk).

**C3. Suicide jokes: honor or failed shield? (S3-P20, D).** A veteran comic says nothing is off limits and that suicide jokes honor friends who died [E21-20]; another speaker made a jibe at the bedside of a friend who survived an attempt, licensed by friendship, a shared running joke and the spouse's request, while adding that many would call it inappropriate [E25d-20]. Against: a veteran's gallows line after a friend's suicide "none of that worked," and what helped was telling the story [E25c-7]; escalating jokes about wanting to die wear out the audience [E22b-16, fictional]; WWI memoirs tell suicides plainly [E15a-46, E15c-8, E13b-73]. **Independence:** the "for" side is one comic at one show reported by one outlet [E21-18, E21-20] and one speaker's single anecdote [E25d-20]; the jokes themselves are not in the text.

**C4. Does dark humor alienate civilians? (S3-P41, D).** *Yes or often:* concealment from family and public deepens German soldiers' alienation [E01-56, E01-54]; a veteran's habitual register "made rooms go quiet" [E04-33]; a retort that failed on a store clerk [E25b-45]; a veteran told "we know all about the war stuff" shut down [E24d-27]. *Mild or no:* a gentle correction at a civilian office, easily fixed [E04-32]; no measurable alienation of friends from two injurious humor styles [E04-6]; society tolerates gallows humor as proof of suffering [E02-11]. The constructs differ (a questionnaire style versus actual joke content), so these cannot be stacked.

**C5. Does dark humor protect or harm? (S3-P31, D).** Protective: coping-humor buffering in firefighters [E05-16], a small independent protective effect in Army soldiers [E04-24], benign humor in terror survivors [E04-27]. Harmful or neutral: self-defeating humor tracking lower life satisfaction [E04-8], dark styles tracking symptoms at equal trauma exposure [E04-18, E05-10], almost no link for injurious styles in terror survivors [E04-29], a body-cost finding in police [E05-3]. All cross-sectional; every author says direction is unknown.

**C6. Does humor stop at the front?** "No one chirruped under a bombardment" [E12a-10] and nothing funny at the front [E24c-7], "We don't joke around here" [E24a-16], versus narrators who kept joking under fire, or told accident-without-casualty stories from danger [E24a-17, E24d-14], and Graves's "always joking" [E15a-53]. The same editors print a shell-warning song [E12a-37] while saying songs did not change the shelling [E12a-5; E12a gaps 2b]. Rank, unit, interview setting or temperament may explain it [E24b gaps 10]; the sources do not reconcile it.

**C7. Is fear named?** Wipers roll-ups: fear is "never named by the word" and appears in archaic disguises [E09b3-22, E10a roll-up]. The Anzac alphabet states fear and a failed courage in plain words [E11b-8]; the songs record "wind-up" as casually admissible in the past tense [E12b-29]. Different papers, ranks and audiences.

**C8. Banter or hazing?** A tone-based line (affectionate versus aggressive) [E01-3] versus Tidy's argument that they share one logic and differ in institutional coding [E01-43; E01 gaps]; a defender argues crude jokes must be separated from harassment and hazing and that juniors should learn their own boundaries [E25c-34].

**C9. Can the dead be joked about?** "No, never" for an officer's own dead [E24d-21], and none at a known name [E25c-13], versus a squadron's casualty-return quip [E24d-10], an honest roar after a dead Ranger [E25c-25] and a comic's stated honor [E21-20]. The difference appears to be relationship (named comrade versus unnamed), frame (honor follows) and teller.

**C10. Callous humor: defense or hardened attitude?** Bell calls grim humor a defense and sets enjoyment of killing apart; Burstall calls the same register an attitude that "hardened"; Bourke draws no line; none says it helped [E24b-26].

**C11. Was humor ever lost after service?** One harvest finds no narrator who says they stopped being able to joke [E24d-18], while others report a lost capacity [E24c-22, E25d-16] and a retired officer links the loss to a staff posting [E25d-16]. Different archives and selection.

**C12. How soon may a war be joked about in public?** Two novelists' impression that Vietnam comedy came about 30 years late [E22a-30] concerns published comedy; field humor in Vietnam is abundant in oral history and songs [E24b, E24d, E02-34, E21-3]. The delay is for public, commercial comedy, not for in-group joking.

**Open questions (no evidence in the corpus).**
- *Does someone who left a military on principle keep, lose or reshape the license?* Not documented. The nearest items: two Russian deserters kept jokes about bodies and nicknames but stopped when the topic became the command's treatment of its own men [E23-1, E23-2]; a sent-home soldier treated as a deserter [E25d-9]; a banned defection joke in a Berlin brigade [E24a-16]; true statements told lightly taken as jokes [E23-3].
- *Do those who refuse a group's humor have a documented path back?* Only the "that guy" penalty is documented [E25c-4].
- *What do the jokers say in their own defense about contested humor?* No joker's voice appears in E25c-4/5 or in E01-31.
- *Which categories are excluded by the corpus itself?* Bodily-waste humor was excluded from E24d by project boundary (a decision, not a finding) [E24d gaps]; interviews where narrators answered "No, we didn't have that" were not saved [E24d source-shape]; the Wisconsin transcripts are held out.
- *Do any of these limits travel to non-Western armies?* Only E23 (Russian deserters, a North Vietnamese interview paraphrase [E21-7], a Norwegian study [E02-12]) touches them, thinly.

## 6. Design-relevant candidates

*These are constraints or facts a writer could treat as real-world features of this kind of humor. They are candidates, not canon; each carries its strength tag; none is dialogue or a voice. Real-world material is mechanism, not specification, and the character's origin military is undecided.*

1. **License is three checks at once** (who shared the risk, who is in the room above and below, whether the joker can also take it). The same person can be fluent with one group and silent with another without changing. [A]
2. **The named dead comrade is outside the joke.** The documented ways around it are narrow: an unflattering truth told first and honor after, by someone from the unit; a retold last exchange; a comic's stated honor. Treat any use as a marked exception with a cost. [A rule; C for each exception]
3. **The safe target is the periphery:** the institution's wording, staff, the odds, rations, the self. The stated aim of at least one team was the "madness," not the dead. [A]
4. **A limit shows as a plain beat:** one short sentence in which the register drops, with no joke added after it, and which other people in the scene recognize without being told. [A]
5. **Silence is the first sanction.** Refusing to laugh carries a social price, and a person who snapped or stopped laughing once can be remembered for it by the group. [A]
6. **A narrow category with no humor at all can coexist with a fluent comic voice** elsewhere (a specific site and its aftermath; a particular dead comrade). [B]
7. **Fear:** admitting past fear casually is documented as acceptable; contempt for present fear in another man is documented but uneven; pranks on the frightened exist. A character who guards against both is consistent. [B]
8. **A joke is unit- and occupation-specific.** It may die with deployed troops of another specialty, with civilians who need the jargon translated, or with a listener the teller is deliberately testing. [A]
9. **Companions can read distress from how the character jokes:** going solemn, escalating, or disgust at their own joke. The character may not name it. [B]
10. **The mask is usually named by others** (spouse, relative, interviewer); veterans seldom call their own humor a cover, though some say so in retrospect. [B]
11. **Banter and hazing share a logic.** The documented harm falls on the person who stays the butt for a reason they cannot fix; a character may carry the memory of laughter they joined (handle with care). [A]
12. **Women and minority soldiers pay documented costs** for refusing or answering back: the "killjoy" or "that guy" label, honorary acceptance, silence. A character can be the target, the one who laughed along, or the one who refused. [A; advocacy sources included]
13. **Contempt and numbness arrive by drift:** first judged in others, then noticed in oneself through an involuntary laugh or disgust. The corpus supplies self-detected signals, not a threshold. [B]
14. **The right to laugh arrives later than the event.** Early-war humor is thinner and the plain statement of what the humor covered comes after. A person whose position is not vindicated may not yet have access to a joke about it (an inference from E22a-42). [A; C for the inference]
15. **Humor thins when the unit is gone:** group forms give way to solitary ones (self-labeling, puns, lists), and shared jokes may stop being funny without the people. [B]
16. **Suicide jokes are contested.** They are documented as honoring the dead for one comic and as failing for another; do not make them a fixed trait. [D]
17. **Self-wounding and desertion** draw admiration in some accounts and silence in others; the difference may be rank, battalion or period. [D]
18. **A principled defector's license is undocumented.** The thin evidence: jokes intact until the topic became the command's treatment of its own men, a banned defection joke, a sent-home soldier treated as a deserter. [C]
19. **Dissent humor in the best-documented source aims at conditions, brass and paperwork, not at the war's aim.** A character who left on principle may have crossed a line that paper held (an inference). [B for the source pattern; C for the inference]
20. **Institutions read sardonic humor literally.** Command, censors and bureaucracies punish the genre; a character from such a setting may self-censor in writing. [B]
21. **Own-side blunders:** the responsible party's laughter reads as callous; a near miss with no casualty can be a story. [B]
22. **A tolerance norm exists:** one veteran writer's rule is never to ridicule someone for their dark sense of humor, because everyone is coping. [C]
23. **The quip after a killing is a documented design failure** for audiences and a documented drift in memoirs; if used, give it consequences. [B]
