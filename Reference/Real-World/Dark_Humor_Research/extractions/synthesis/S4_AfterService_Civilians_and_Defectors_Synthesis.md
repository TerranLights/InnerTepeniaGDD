# S4 — Time course, after-service humor, the civilian-facing register, and defector, exile and dissent evidence

**Lens:** S4 (time course inside a war; transition; after service; civilian-facing register; leaving, dissent and defection; humor without a unit; moral injury) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Files read:** all 38 (the Pattern roll-up and Gaps and cautions of every file, plus each file's source-shape notes; findings read in full for E04, E15c, E23 and E25e, and consulted by ID everywhere else) · **Status: Research synthesis, NOT canon; no voice or dialogue.**

## Executive summary

The corpus is strong on how humor changes inside one long war, moderate on its behavior after service, and nearly empty on the case this lens exists to serve: a person who left a military on principle and lives among civilians without a unit.

**Well supported.** Inside a war, the one long run available, the Wipers Times (1916-18), shows comic targets drifting from place to institution to home front to demobilization, the editors' tone sliding from program cheerfulness to fatigue, and a plain register kept apart from the jokes (S4-P1 to P4). Oral histories from several archives agree that incidents are funny afterward, not at the time (P5). Own-side named dead are almost never the butt (P3, A). After service, the funny version circulates while the heavy material goes unsaid (P14, A); humor survives but narrows to veterans, private channels, or a managed product for civilians (P15, A); reflexes and vocabulary persist for years (P16, A). The civilian-facing register is built from decoy stories, translation, sanitizing and concealment, often as tact toward family (P25-P27). Graves's 1919-29 chapters (E15c) are the longest dated first-person record: humor survives as comedy of scenes and puns, the war intrudes, and the book closes on "even anecdotes fail" (P18).

**Contested.** Whether veterans' dark humor alienates civilians (null survey against narrative misfires, P31); whether dark styles harm (P19); whether joking fades (P6); whether veteran-only company helps (P13).

**Thin.** Leaving and dissent. No collected source documents the humor of a person who left a military on principle. The only first-hand deserter humor is about a dozen lines from two Russian deserters, months after exit, translated, inside a journalist's feature (P36). The Vietnam-era exile sources contain no deserter's joke. "Defectors as the butt of jokes" could not be confirmed (P40). No source ties humor to moral injury (P44). The Wipers editors stayed loyal critics, and Fox's 2004 exit has no stated reason (P39).

**For design,** the safest transfers are structural: a time-lag, versions sorted by audience, a seam where the register drops, humor kept for those who were there.

## 1. Time course inside a war (lens a)

**The Wipers Times as a clock (P1-P4, P7).** The paper is the only source with the same editors writing for the same unit across a whole war. It ran from the Ypres Salient in February 1916, changed titles as the division moved, and was stopped by battle for about four months after the Somme (E09a-8), for four months in spring and summer 1917 (E09b2-16), and for most of 1918 after the March offensive (E10b-36). In the Salient the humor is at its most acute on ruins, shelling and the town as property, with a plain farewell that treats fear as an emotion worth having had (E08-40) and a poem forecasting that small details fixed "when the nerves are tension high" will haunt the survivors (E08-64). By December 1916 the editors write that "Many cheery faces are missing from the Division" (E09a-8), and the mockery has moved to the Staff, returns and the drink supply (E09a roll-up). At Christmas the editorial is openly cynical about "Peace on earth" and a humor column reports the editor "fed up" with the war as a subject (E09a-23/28); in March 1917 a threat to give up "the war or the editorial chair" is withdrawn as bitterness (E09a-54).

By August 1917 loss is stated in one clause and the subject closed (E09b2-16), the dead sit in a memorial block with no device (E09b2-28), and a farewell counts who is left (E09b3-21). Fear is admitted only in disguise, as "the megrims" (E09b3-22). From November 1917 the paper is strained (E10a-12), plain pieces are densest (E10a roll-up), and the wish for the end becomes explicit: a wager at "six to four on" (E10a-40), a verse hushing doubters at home (E10a-43), and a sketch in which an old soldier makes a complete cost argument and is certified (E10a-77). After the guns stop the editors say what the jokes had not: the end met with apathy, the war the line men know as "the vilest disaster" (E10b-69), the dead who "will never be forgotten" (E10b-46); the run ends in a business note citing the demobilization of the printers (E10b-80).

*Independence and scope.* This is one paper, an officers' mess paper by inference, read in seven consecutive passes by extractors who read earlier roll-ups first. The "cheerfulness" label belongs to a 1930 foreword (E08-1), and the 1930 Editor's own retrospect finds peace "awful" and friendships the lasting thing (E08-4). Nothing in the corpus lets us test whether a private soldier's humor drifted the same way. Other period sources are snapshots: the Anzac Book is the late static phase of one campaign (E11a Source-shape), yet anticipates its own afterlife (a V.C. tall tale subtitled as "what to expect after the war," E11a-46; a dug-out remembered in advance, E11a-52; a refusal of the post-war pedestal, E11b-11). Sassoon names the songs "haggard mirth" and predicts the next generation will misread them as fun (E11b-60). Songs "belong to a phase and die with it" (E12a-4).

**Time-lag (P5, A).** Four kinds of source give the same structure: "funny afterwards," not at the time (E24b-2), with narrators marking which laugh they mean, the relief-laugh or the distance-laugh (E24d-17), the rule that a thing is funny "when it is not happening to you, or when it is over" (E24a-12), "fun" as a story applied afterward and colored by nostalgia (E02-15/17), a joke funny only "after the pain and excitement had faded" (E25a-31), and processing that happens "after we return home" (E22a-25). Because every one of these sources is retrospective, the finding describes how veterans narrate, not how humor sounded in the moment.

**Fading versus continuing (P6, D).** Norwegians stopped having fun with ambushes, command responsibility, age and conscience (E02-19). A submariner reports a "sea change" after the first patrol while another says that after first artillery fire "we all joked all the time" (E24a-17). Two Australians mark the first dead as the end of a "joke" era (E24b-25), while another says Australians find something funny in almost everything (E24b-15). Richards's old-hands' banter formula changes meaning as the cohort is destroyed and fails on the casualty track (E13b-93/96); Graves describes habituation, a "decline curve," and forecasts a collapse (E15b-62/64/89). The sources do not explain why one narrator's humor stopped and another's did not, and branch and setting are confounded (E24a-17).

**Modern single-deployment arcs.** Fox's book is the best single arc: comic in training, exhilaration without irony at the 2003 line of departure (E25e-7), laughter at an anonymous dead man (E25e-8); in 2010 a unit "ready to have some fun" (E25e-70), a communal peak in the middle (E25e-35/37/49/61), fatigue and contempt (E25e-28/34/43-45), then numbing at the end of the rotation (E25e-62/73), summarized by the extractor in E25e-84 (an extractor's reading of a non-linear book). The Sandbox posts never span more than about two and a half years and drift toward policing others' humor (E25b-55).

## 2. Transition (lens b)

**Hospital humor as work (P8, C).** Graves is the clearest case in which a participant calls humor labor. Officers at a convalescent home, many neurasthenic and in "gloomy" company, found a mock society with invented ritual, policed it against ruder humor with a worse story (E15c-18), staged a corpse hoax on civilian officials (E15c-19), and called the neurasthenic hospital a "great joke" (E15c-25). The sentence "Anything to make people laugh. But it was hard work" is the evidence. Fiction by veterans renders hospital banter between wounded patients (E25a-13/16) and a ward quip between veterans (E25a-40, genre unverified), but these show craft, not practice. Laughter on a ward can also be a symptom (E24d-25, P21).

**The end and the first year (P9-P11).** Graves's Armistice passage has no joke at all (E15c-40). Richards writes that "an Armistice was not the same as a Peace" and that gladness was slight (E13b-144). The Wipers editors joke at the staff's paper offensive, not at the end (E10b-84). In the first civilian year Graves reports shells bursting on the bed, strangers wearing dead friends' faces, hills read as battlefields, commandeering, lying, a billet checklist applied to a room, and language that had become "foul" at Loos (E15c-50), plus a list of civilian disabilities and shame at being a drag on a spouse (E15c-51). Demobilization is comic farce told about paperwork (E15c-46), and the belief that scars and testimonials would buy jobs is mocked in retrospect, with a note that employer goodwill "cooled" (E15c-43).

**The transition literature is thin (P11-P12, P31).** AC-15 (20 UK veterans) is about the self, not humor. Its entire humor content is three participant accounts and two sentences of authors' gloss (E04 source-shape): a tasteless joke and misjudged banter corrected in a couple of months with "a couple of learning points" (E04-32); speech that made "rooms go quiet" that the speaker thought normal (E04-33, not labeled humor in the text); slang that makes military friends laugh and civilians frown (E04-35). The claim that military humor "does not translate" is the authors' inference (E04-34). The study has no time-since-service data and no principled exits (E04-37). The only quantitative veteran study pools active, inactive and discharged volunteers and has no time-since-separation figure (E04-5; E04 Gaps 4).

**First weeks in modern voices.** One soldier home weeks says old comrades relate their benders "in a light sort of way" and "we pretend it's funny," that it "just isn't as funny anymore" now the unit has scattered, and ends by declining to go on (E25b-36). Another, 19 days home, answers the repeated questions with a one-line shield (E25b-44). A discharge-day post answers "HONORABLE DISCHARGE" with a bitter joke and the open question of who the writer is now (E25b-40). A first homecoming ends with a clean invented battle story substituted for stories that fell flat (E25c-37). Buzzell, two years out, reports heavy drinking, says "support the troops" dies once soldiers are home, and in the same breath says not to know what people could do (E18-23). Fox, after 2010, is alone with a drink at ten in the morning (E25e-3) and laughs until crying in a single therapy hour (E25e-18).

Buzzell's post-service evidence is secondhand: the feed holds three posts of connected prose in his voice, the 2021 and 2023 posts are not his, and his later humor is known through press and titles (E18 Source-shape; E18-35).

*What is missing:* a study following the same people's humor across years since separation. AC-03, AC-05, AC-37 and AC-38 are all cross-sectional (E04 Gaps 2), and the 10-15 year window the project cares about is documented only by Graves (about a decade), Richards (1933), Fox (about 2011-17) and scattered oral histories.

## 3. After service (lens c)

**What persists (P15, P16, A).** Graves, home in 1919, reports commandeering anything of uncertain ownership, lying as a reflex, a language retrained by effort, and a village that calls them "The Captain" (E15c-50/67). Graves coins "reminiscential neurasthenia" for the symptom that the train journey and the army railway warrant produced on the way to the annual pension board (E15c-74), a deadpan coinage showing that the claim's own forms reproduce the symptom. Richards, demobilized in 1918, reverts to "1914-1915 language" under pain (E13b-151) and nearly "drove the French passengers crazy" with a smoke barrage on a troop train, noting how hard it was to drop "habits which we had cultivated during the War" (E13b-149). Gallagher calls swearing a "universal gift" of the military to its leavers (E22a-5); an Australian says the register is what "in civvie street you'd be in jail for" (E24d-27); Fox walks a safe suburb as a patrol and finds machine-gun fireworks "peaceful" (E25e-77). Nicknames and rank outlive the unit: a pun on a grouse, "Captain Grouse," among ex-officers (E15c-67), and Australian nicknames that outlive the unit (E24d-3).

**What is withheld (P14, P25, A).** The pattern is deliberate and audience-specific. Graves says of the 1919 Peace Day speech "I have forgotten what I said" (E15c-49), cannot quote a friend's verse letter (E15c-38), and notes a spouse's request not to talk about the war (E15c-74); attempts to write the war as a novel "brought back neurasthenia" and were abandoned (E15c-79). Sandbox authors state or enact omission in at least seven posts (E25b-48). An essayist tells the funny Iraq stories but not the frightened ones, and after eight years of marriage had not told a husband the details, who insisted "you have to actually say the words" (E25c-17). Australians say "Nobody introduces the subject" at reunions (E24d-24), and two old veterans insult each other daily and never discuss what they saw (E21-41). German soldiers conceal deployment fun from family as consideration (E01-54). Counts are passages, not rates: the UNSW harvest tags 28 of 172 passages as after-service, and by the extractor's reading civilians' failure to get the joke is documented in four or five of them (E24d-26); Rutgers tags 10 of 140 (E24a Tallies A).

**What curdles (P19-P21).** The quantitative literature disagrees. Self-defeating humor tracks lower life satisfaction among connected veterans (E04-8); in Swiss police, the dark comic styles were never more common among officers below the clinical cutoffs, and cynicism was strongest (E04-16); the in-service Army sample links keeping a sense of humor under stress to slightly fewer symptoms (E04-24); terror survivors show almost no link for injurious styles (E04-29). Every study is cross-sectional and measures different constructs (E04 Gaps 1-3). Narrative sources describe curdling concretely: a soldier-blogger's own "warped, sick war humor" (E18-13; E06-25), laughter "until my stomach hurt" at a death by animal-triggered bomb (E25e-62), a radiographer's "warning sign" that the humor had lost "contact with humanity" (E24d-19), a joke at a possibly innocent dead civilian that left guilt (E25b-11).

**Graves, 1919-29 (P18).** The book is the longest first-person dated record of an ex-officer's civilian years, written within about ten years of leaving. Humor persists as a self-applied label ("caricature scene") for a wedding in field boots, a shop selling tobacco to the Poet Laureate, a parish council (E15c-33/69/76), as puns and mock-official lists (E15c-29/45/82/85), and as a closing checklist in which "taken life" and "been killed" sit in an ordinary row (E15c-90). The war intrudes: alternate-life scenes in lectures that "did not leave me until well on in 1928" (E15c-58); talk with Blunden that escalates "into an almost hysterical state" until they agree to write it down (E15c-56); a deliberate refusal of psychiatric cure because the symptom feeds the poetry (E15c-70). Army friendships thin to one annual meeting by 1926 (E15c-78). The end is explicit: "gradual disintegration" (E15c-86) and "After which, even anecdotes fail" (E15c-92). *Limits:* one literary man whose humor is partly literary; the author disclaims invention but admits errors and calls earlier published versions "literature" (E15c-89, E15c Gaps). Richards's 1933 chapters are the other-ranks counterpart: bitterness about pensions and medals (E13b-152/153), gratitude for not being blind, maimed, disfigured or mad (E13b-151), drift from comrades ("my fault and not theirs," E13b-154), and an old soldier in a pub for the last scene (E13b-155).

**Old age (P17).** Reunion repertoire is a stable funny-only canon with teasing, and sometimes a rift between younger and older members (E24a-23). Old grudges can persist with jokes the speaker knows should not be made (E24a-23, Kolodziej). At reunions of fifty or sixty, "we have tears in our eyes laughing," but, in the speaker's words, about "something that happened to one of their mates," and the bad things are blocked out (E24d-24). A Vietnam veteran gives a rule that grave wounds are not joked about, and repeats "I don't dwell on the bad stuff" fifty-three years on (E25c-8); the extractor notes the refrain is also a narrative policy (E25c-8). One humorist is reported to have grown bitter and less funny with age (E22a-52, a former student's essay). A Norwegian population study finds humor's survival benefit shrinks above 65 (E05-5), but it is not military. **The WWII-veteran "search for meaning" finding (Moran 2008) is not in the 38 extraction files;** it exists only as a lead (11a B15) and cannot be cited here. The corpus has no source on a veteran's humor across decades of one life.

**The comic-turned-professional (P22-P24).** Claims are plentiful and evidence is not. Six pages concern one organization's programs and one instructor appears in three (E21 Gaps 1). The organization's own 18-month evaluation (with a control group) is unpublished in this set, covers all its arts programs, and a figure attributed to it is modest (E21-35). Participants say stand-up is "therapeutic," a performer says "telling jokes is my therapy," and organizers say "not therapy" (E21-20/22). What the pages do document: a performer who found military material unwelcome in civilian clubs and took it to troops (E21-16); a retiree who lost humor in service ("sucked it out of my soul"), then found it again after leaving (E25d-16); a wounded veteran who doubted the right to joke out of loyalty to the dead and resolved "if I survived, I'm still allowed to speak" (E25d-36); a retiree's good-faith exit told as an "amicable divorce" with a pension called alimony (E25d-21). The earned right is stated as "the ones that went" (E21-18).

**Medics and caregivers.** No collected source documents combat medics suppressing humor after discharge (the dissertation that would, Argento 2025, is Tier C C-05). The nearest evidence is non-military or in-service: paramedics who fear being overheard, who admit humor "covers up processing" and "creeps into family and personal life," and who keep distressing material from family (E04-9/11/12); a nurse who jokes with patients as a service and keeps their own emotion private (E25b-29/30); a corpsman in anguish at joking around the dead whose commander would not ban the humor (E25d-41).

## 4. The civilian-facing register (lens d)

**Translation and sanitizing (P27, A).** Jargon is the first barrier (E21-33), and a poem in a collective voice says the jokes "do not translate into any of your languages" (E25a-57). An open call for the most lawless war jokes mostly drew clean ones (E21-1). Institutions publish the sweetened version: obituaries tell a three-part story that makes war "look like a fun job" (E01-45), and a British minister denied in Parliament what a base mess's back-files recorded (E15c-5). In 1915-16 Graves meets "Business as usual" and "Oh, but that was in France" (E15b-34), "newspaper language" and a reprinted pamphlet left without comment (E15b-122), a mother who returns a bullet-holed periscope as faulty (E15b-101), and a hostess who says of a night of shrieks that "one of the maids has hysterics" (E15b-124). Graves later calls their own early verse full of "falsities for public delectation" (E15c-39). Civilians want the sad half of the experience, not the pride (E22b-37), and cycle through awe, pity and pathology (E22a-61).

**The decoy and the funny version (P26, A).** Fox is asked "was it scary" and tells a rehearsed, true, funny story that does not answer the question, and enjoys the questioner's horror (E25e-5). Klay relays the term "decoy stories," funny stories "removed from what is really important to the teller" that keep people at a distance (E22a-58), and a fictional narrator sizes up the listener, picks the funny or the sad version, and says "it's the same story" (E22a-57). A reporter-essayist finds the funny story "goes over better" than the terrified one (E25c-17); a Marine home on leave invents a clean heroic battle after true stories fall flat (E25c-37). Fox's thesis is a larger version of the same negotiation: dialogue was softened to be "palatable," and civilian readers still found it "too much" (E25e-81). A quick retort to the stock question appears in both WWII oral history (E24a-22) and a 2007 milblog (E25b-44).

**Who may be asked, who may answer (P24, P28).** A novelist relays that the offense in "did you kill anybody" is the asker's lack of moral seriousness, not the topic (E22a-60). A webcomic strip answers the repeated question with a deadpan threat and drops the joke in the note, which calls the question a "vulnerable position" for the veteran (E19-16). A laugh at an official ritual draws a Lord Mayor's anger and the answer that service earned it (E24d-28); a laugh caught at a post-war gathering before a dead man's mother is remembered as "a thousand deaths" (E24d-21). A deputy and combat veteran tells young Marines "no one will ever care" and to avoid non-Marines, and Fox calls it kindness learned the hard way (E25e-25).

**Gratitude and the thanking civilian (P29, B).** Terminal Lance treats thanks as a transaction (free meals, discounts), keeping older veterans' company separate from the free food (E19 roll-up, Civilian-facing; E19-10). A joke told to a store clerk asking for a "troop" donation fails and turns to resentment (E25b-45); a unit sorts care-package refuse into a junk pile beside expired donated goods (E25e-48). *Caveat:* the Terminal Lance sample was chosen by serious-topic keywords and is about 73 percent serious (E19 Gaps 1); the same author's stance toward civilians moves across about a decade from "they can try" (2012, E19-11) to "they don't care" (2013, E19-16) to ignorance as proof the job was done (2014, E19-24) and, by 2018-23, to sharing on the author's own terms (E19-51).

**Partner effects (P30, C).** In 105 Israeli couples one spouse injured in an attack, the injured person's symptoms were lower when the uninjured partner used affiliative humor, and the effect ran asymmetrically (E04-27/28); the sample is civilian and married (E04-26). Graves's spouse refused to treat the war as comparable to working-class wives' suffering, which had the effect of "putting the war into the background" (E15c-54), and asked that it not be discussed at home (E15c-74). A military spouse writes that deployment produces "a finely honed sense of gallows humor," that civilians may find it "off," and that much of the author's day goes to appearing normal (E25b-43). Fox's team answers a friend's private worry about what to tell a wife in the same technical joke vocabulary (E25e-35). Paramedics shield family from distressing material, which the authors link to distance (E04-12).

**The disputed claim (P31, D).** The one veteran survey with a civilian-connectedness measure found no association between aggressive or self-defeating humor styles and closeness to civilian friends (R = 0.18, p = 0.24) or family (p = 0.12), and connectedness to civilian friends predicted life satisfaction, as veteran-friend connectedness did (E04-6/7). Narratives report the opposite, rooms gone quiet (E04-33), a workmates' dinner at which a veteran was told "we know all about the war stuff" and, in the veteran's words, "I just shut up" (E24d-27), and an author's claim that hidden humor deepens alienation (E01-56). The two do not contradict each other strictly: the survey measures joking style in 93 volunteers, not content, setting or audience (E04-5); the narratives are single rooms. Neither can say whether any one veteran's jokes land.

## 5. Leaving, dissent and defection (lens e)

**How thin the evidence is.** E23 collected ten defector, deserter or exile sources. By kind they are a journalist's feature, two pieces by one book author (an interview and a publisher's blog), a critic's essay, an obituary, a wire report, a student essay on civilian Syrian humor, a serious radio report, a novelist's essay and a book review (E23 Source-shape). Only one contains a deserter's jokes: a news feature on two Russian deserters in Finland, about a dozen lines, translated, with pseudonyms, pending asylum applications, and headings and teaser lines written by editors (E23 Source-shape; E23 Gaps 1-2). Four Vietnam-era sources are histories and promotional pieces by outsiders and contain no deserter's joke (E23-8/11/12/26/27). The Dovlatov essay is a critic's reading of an ex-guard's autofiction (E23-13). The Belenko piece is an obituary built on an obituarist's recollections (E23 Source-shape). The North Korean piece is a wire report on women performers, not soldiers (E23-20). The Syrian defector report contains no joke, laugh or nickname at all (E23-25). Outside E23, defection-adjacent evidence is memoir (Graves on Sassoon; Richards on refusers; Fox), one radio broadcast (E25d-9), and press about Buzzell. No oral-history archive contains a deserter or defector (E24a-d roll-ups).

**The Russian deserters (P36, C).** The men's war jokes stay intact: a gallows reading of bodies as route markers, a ritual courtesy to the dead, nicknames by offense (E23-1). The humor ends at command extortion: the nickname story told laughing ends quietly with "they died too" (E23-2). The reasons for leaving are stated flat, with no joke about the cause or its leaders (E23-6). The escape is narrated as a light caper (E23-5). A true disclosure told lightly was taken by colleagues as a joke, which both hid and isolated the man (E23-3). Official language becomes a lever or a trophy (E23-4). The picture covers months, not years (E23-7).

**Sassoon as read by the regiment (P33-P34, C).** Graves predicts the units would call the protest "ratting," "cold feet," "not acting like a gentleman," "a lapse from good form," and says even the "most understanding man in the regiment" would not grasp it; "the civilians were more mad and hopeless than the army" (E15c-26). Owen is described as having had an accusation of cowardice by a commanding officer that "preyed on his mind" (E15c-27). The declaration itself, addressed to civilians who "do not share" the agony, contains no humor (E15c-23). The jokes are private and small: sending the book "for a joke" to the commander-in-chief (E15c-22), the hospital nickname (E15c-25), politicians' sayings traded between letters (E15c-31), and a later reversal in which the dissenter calls good form "suicidal stupidity" (E15c-36). A reported soldier reaction to the dissenter's satire, via a poet's introduction, says the more the poet "lays it on thick to those who don't realize the war the better" (E11b-45, anecdotal). Richards, of the same regiment, reports refusers without blame or jokes: a man who deserted before an attack over unpaid wages (E13a-74), a signaler who would not return and was "true to his word" (E13b-26), a sergeant honored by the ranks for assaulting an officer (E13b-120). The Wipers paper meets objectors with contempt, not comedy, in 1916 (E08-47), though by late 1917 an objector is a comic letter-writer (E10a-63); also in 1916 a cartoon of a lone drunken absent soldier was prosecuted under wartime law (E07-17).

**The man sent home (P35, C).** A National Guard sergeant, Joe Betts, evacuated with a disc injury, reports being treated "like a deserter" by part of the unit, some blaming Betts for being home, and losing the church and straining the marriage (E25d-9). Plain throughout.

**Fox's exit (P39, B).** The text says the author left active duty after a first tour in fall 2004 for a civilian job, and later returned to a Marine unit; it gives no reason, no principle and no regret about leaving (E25e-87, citing the text's "a change"). The in-service dissent is small and covert: two officers' whispered commentary in a briefing that becomes a countersign greeting (E25e-63), a hidden mercy for a coerced bomb-planter (E25e-72), a laugh line about a replacement-unit man who "conscientiously object[ed]" to a lack of sleep (E25e-50), one sentence on a for-profit war (E25e-83). The extractor concludes the narrator is "a model of an insider's loyalty-and-contempt split, not of a defector" (E25e-87).

**The Wipers editors and the Army (P39, B).** G.H.Q. "remained kind" and censored "only two or three times," and senior officers backed the paper (E08-4); the title change came "for reasons not unconnected with the censor" in the editors' 1918 preface (E08-3). Dissent runs upward at Staff, Brass and returns, never at the war's aim (E09a roll-up, Defector); doubt about the outcome is hushed (E09b1-22; E10a-43). The only exits joked about are sanctioned ones: leave, Blighty, a staff badge, a medical certificate (E10a roll-up, Defector; E09b3 roll-up, Defector).

**In-service dissent forms (P32, A).** Deniable criticism by polite formula, "the highest form of disapproval" (E01-58); licensed skits and songs at the officers' expense (E02-8); draftees' songs wishing the war away and parodying heroic tunes (E02-34); a diarist's joke as the only safe way to voice the wish to flee (E02-28), and a flippant reply that nearly ended in arrest (E02-30). Buzzell, discharged honorably and claimed by both camps, used mock compliance and a free-speech test (E18 roll-up, Defector).

**Not confirmed (P40).** The lead-pass claim that defectors are the butt of others' jokes rests on a snippet from a dead page about Syrian protest banners (11d, lines 196 and 358). E23 reports North Korean defectors "often shunned" and no jokes told about them (E23-22), and the Syrian defector report has no humor (E23-25). The same lead notes list a register "learned in the institution" that must be relearned after leaving; E23 supports only one performer's statement of being "still wedded to North Korea's stiff style" (E23-20).

## 6. Humor without a unit (lens f)

**Exile and displaced-person humor (P41, B).** Three scholarly sources, none about soldiers, describe humor as community-making under dispossession. Across Nazi camps, besieged Sarajevo and Syria, the authors find the same three functions, and "in order to cope, one needed to adapt; in order to adapt, one needed to joke" (E03-19). Humor was an entry qualification for a mutual-aid group and, in Sarajevo, acceptance was conditional on laughing at oneself (E03-5/8). Jokes were hard to tell outside the ordeal, "experiential deformation," and insiders could feel guilt about laughing and refuse to repeat a joke to an interviewer (E03-15/19). The Lithuanian displaced-persons press (cartoons by artists and editors, read in Lithuanian by the extractor) was "neither light nor carelessly funny," moralizing, and addressed to the whole stricken group, with its own rule-enforcers and hotheads as targets (E03-33/41); after resettlement the humor turned to wealth, assimilation and named individuals, and the homeland's satire turned on the exiles (E03-39/40). Ukrainian war memes are nation-building and enemy-directed, and the sample's silence on own-side loss is a property of its selection (E03 Gaps 3).

**What carries to one man alone, and what does not.** Plausibly carried: coping by collision of ordinary phrasing with abnormal conditions (E03-3/4); reuse of the old institution's own vocabulary as raw material (E03-13/25); guilt about laughing; jokes that cannot be translated for outsiders (E03-15/19). Not carried, because each needs a group: entry qualification, mutual-aid cohesion, moral steering of a community, and an audience that decodes the joke. *The corpus's first-hand evidence of a person without a unit is thin and single-source:* "it just isn't as funny anymore" and a post that ends in refusal (E25b-36); army friendships thinned to one annual meeting (E15c-78); small private jokes that "need no audience" (E25e-60, the extractor's phrase) and a night walk where the hands ache "empty" (E25e-77). The in-service Army data link humor, morale and cohesion as moving together (E04-24); the extractor infers a veteran with humor but no unit lacks two of three, which is an inference, not a finding.

**Substitute units (P42, B).** Veterans keep ties through meme threads that double as check-ins (E25d-19), a text thread of jokes about a war that continues without them (E25e-78), comedy rooms framed as a substitute battle buddy (E21-17), and a multiplayer game that keeps the shared chaos and drops the authority (E22b-23). All four are promotional or single-voice; none documents the isolated veteran who lacks such a circle. Timbuktu displaced-resident humor appears only in the lead notes (11d, line 201) and is not extracted.

## 7. Moral injury and humor (lens g)

**The gap.** The phrase "moral injury" appears in the 38 extraction files twice: as an interviewer's suggestion about a short story's last scene (E22a-63) and in an extractor's candidate-application sentence about an order to tolerate an ally's abuse (E25e-33). No finding derives from a source that ties humor to moral injury as a construct. Molendijk 2025 (humor in military "dirty work") and Molendijk 2018 (moral injury) are in the manifest but not collected; they are Tier C items C-01 and C-09. The earlier lead notes cite Molendijk 2025 as the nearest, but nothing in the extraction corpus rests on it.

**Nearest evidence (P44, C).** (1) *Humor as guilt management, authors' inference:* black-humor pilots' songs relieve guilt and anxiety over civilian targets (E02-36); a diarist's laughing account of captives keeps up "self-righteousness, and perhaps sanity" (E02-25). Neither is testimony. (2) *The teller's own line:* a radiographer reads disgust at their dark humor as a "warning sign" of being there too long (E24d-19); a ship's cartoon about a bombed school bus helps until the thought "I'm responsible too" arrives (E24d-19); a laugh caught before a dead man's mother is remembered as "a thousand deaths" (E24d-21). (3) *Guilt after a joke:* a post catches a callous joke about a possibly innocent dead civilian and asks what the joke is for (E25b-11). (4) *Orders:* an order to ignore a partner force's abuse, with the unit's "common joke" beside it (E25e-33); Graves arranges to avoid sitting on a capital court-martial and records army "official lying" in flat prose (E15c-4/5). (5) *In-group objection:* a corpsman in anguish at joking and eating around the dead (E25d-41); a failed gallows line after a friend's suicide, after which telling the story helped "sometimes" (E25c-7). (6) *A narrator's stated function:* humor as "protection for my conscience" (E06 roll-up, Functions). (7) *Fading fun and conscience:* a Norwegian soldier no longer has "the conscience" to shoot at a probable farmer (E02-19). (8) *Fiction:* shared laughter at a prank on a powerless queue that makes a scene "more tolerable," beside one silent dissent (E25a-51/52). **What the corpus cannot say:** whether humor delays, expresses or defends against moral injury; whether it differs from ordinary distress; anything over time.

## 8. Contradictions and open questions

1. **Does veterans' dark humor alienate civilians?** No association in 93 volunteers (E04-6/7) against rooms gone quiet, a workmates' dinner shut down, and concealment said to deepen alienation (E04-33; E24d-27; E01-56). Different instruments: styles versus occasions.
2. **Does joking fade?** Fade with exposure, responsibility, age (E02-19; E24a-17 Pike; E24b-25) against continue (E24a-17 other narrators; E24b-15). Branch and setting confound.
3. **Are dark styles harmful?** Self-defeating humor tracks lower life satisfaction (E04-8) and dark styles track distress (E04-16) while injurious styles show little link in terror survivors (E04-29) and in-service coping humor ability is modestly protective (E04-24). All cross-sectional; direction unknown (E04 Gaps 2-3).
4. **Is veteran company a cure or an entrenchment?** Relief of being "more understood" (E04-35) against the authors' warning that it may reinforce the military self (E04-36), and a mentor's advice to avoid non-veterans (E25e-25).
5. **Is the withheld repressed or tactful?** Consideration for family (E01-54) against humor that "covers up processing" and creeps into home life (E04-11, paramedics). The cost framing mostly comes from relatives and interviewers (E24a-19), while veterans mostly describe benefit (E24d-31).
6. **Therapy or not?** "Not therapy, but therapeutic" against "telling jokes is my therapy" (E21-20/22), with no published outcome data (E21-35).
7. **Old age:** warm reunions and a stable canon (E24a-23; E24d-24; E25c-8) against one humorist's growing bitterness (E22a-52).
8. **Period label versus period statement:** "cheerfulness" (E08-1, a 1930 foreword) against the wartime editors' "more often hysterical than natural" (E08-3).
9. **Open:** whether the Wipers drift is general or the editors' own fatigue; whether any of these patterns hold 10-15 years out for a person who left on principle.

## 10. Design-relevant candidates

*Each is a candidate constraint, not a finding about the character, with its strength tag. No dialogue is written.*

- **(A) A time-lag.** Experiences become funny in the telling, long after; the character may know which laugh was relief and which was distance. (P5)
- **(A) Own-side named dead are almost never a target.** The character may joke at institutions, the rear and the self, and state a death plainly in a clause, then move on; a joke at an anonymous or enemy death is where the register can curdle, and the character may know it. (P3, P20)
- **(A) Versions sorted by audience.** A funny, true, beside-the-point story for strangers; a plainer one for a few; the heavy one mostly unsaid; the "same story" told several ways. (P14, P26)
- **(B) A seam.** The register drops in a story's last sentence, and nothing is added. (E25b-53; E25e roll-up, "Where the writing turns plain"; E15c roll-up, Craft)
- **(A) Persistent reflexes.** Vocabulary, command voice, tactical reading of rooms, a rank-title or nickname that outlives the unit; civilians notice, the character may not. (P16, P12)
- **(B) Misfires as feedback.** The room's reaction is the only signal; most misfires are mild and fast-correcting, a few costly. (P12)
- **(A) Concealment as tact.** The character withholds humor to spare a partner or family, and it reads to them as distance. (P25)
- **(C) A partner's refusal to rank the war** can ease its pressure; a household may set a no-war-talk rule. (P30)
- **(D) Veteran-only company** is relief from translating, not a cure; the evidence points both ways. (P13)
- **(B) Old-veteran silence.** Insult trading and funny-only reunions with no talk of what was seen. (P17)
- **(B) Warning signs.** Disgust at one's own joke; laughter at the wrong moment; laughter that is a symptom; a companion who reads the register as it changes (an untested proposal, E04-20). (P20, P21)
- **(C) Humor as labor.** When unwell, the character invents rituals or hoaxes to cheer others, and calls it work. (P8)
- **(C) No recovery-arc ending.** The longest record keeps humor as comedy of scenes and refuses a consoling close. (P18)
- **(C) If the character left on principle, the best-documented contour is thin:** jokes about the enemy, the dead and the old side's eccentrics intact; humor stops where the subject is the command's treatment of its own men; the reasons for leaving stated flat; the escape, if any, told as a caper. (P36, one source)
- **(C) A true statement said lightly** is heard by civilians as a joke: camouflage and isolation at once. (P36)

### A5. Independence caveat (read before using any row)

1. **Graves and Sassoon are linked.** The novel's friend, David Cromlech, is commonly identified with Graves in outside criticism; the files flag this as not stated in the text (E26a-54, E26b-84), and the novel renames the regiment, while Graves's text names the regiment in the declaration's headnote (E15c-23) and describes the same-regiment "wart" custom in Sassoon's letter (E15c-20). So the two accounts of July 1917 are two retellings of one set of events by two men who were both in them, one a year after the other. - **(C) Disaffection can begin as a wish to believe again, not a wish to rebel.** The first stage may be a longing to be a believer, a postponed question, sarcasm at safe targets (the Church, the rear, the press) and an unattributed retort from inside a group; the first dissent need not be heard by anyone.
