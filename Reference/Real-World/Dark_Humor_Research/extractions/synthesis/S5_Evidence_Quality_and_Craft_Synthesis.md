## 1. Executive summary

The corpus is large but narrow. Seven WWI British and Anzac primary texts supply 1,202 of its 2,259 findings (53%, checklist), and one divisional officers' newspaper, the Wipers Times, supplies 423 of those [E08-3, E08-28, E10b-80]. It is strong on forms, devices, rules of license and register shifts in English-speaking armies, above all WWI British officers; weak on frequency, other armies, and enlisted, women's, minority, enemy-side and Afghanistan/Iraq voices. For the principled defector it is weakest where it matters most: humor of people ten to fifteen years out of service, and of anyone who left on principle. The only deserters' jokes are about a dozen lines from two men in one article [E23-1, E23-2].

Selection shapes every layer. The roughly 490 verbatim oral-history passages are keyword harvests (counts are passages, not rates), and interviewers often supplied the "gallows humor" label [E24a-29, E24d-43, E24b-20 to E24b-23]. Period texts were curated by editors [E11a-2, E12a-1, E08-3]; memoirs date from 1929-33 [E13b Source-shape, E15c-89]; modern writing is civilian-facing and edited [E25b-48, E25c-17]. A content filter and a bodily-waste boundary left atrocity, slur, sexual and suicide humor summarized rather than quoted (about 700 register lines; mine). The few surveys are small, cross-sectional, self-report studies of different constructs, and none measures gallows content [E04-15, E04-22, E04-24, E04-29].

The passes corrected at least fourteen earlier assumptions: the Buzzell feed holds about 3 posts of connected prose out of 72 [E18-35]; the Terminal Lance sample is 73% serious topics against 12% of titles [E19-58]; "Old Bill" is named twice in the Bairnsfather text [E11b Gaps]; no Boone page mentions humor [E22b-12]; several index lines over-read their texts [E25c-14].

On craft, six claims recur: both registers in one beat; funny and sad versions chosen by audience; the quip after the kill fails; trauma as backstory shorthand is lazy; advisors fix procedure, not humor; veterans split on depictions. Reliability is modest: mostly novelists' statements, one developer admission [E22b-19], one remembered TV ending [E22b-3], no systematic veteran-reception data.

Priorities (Section 5): post-service and defector humor (C-05, C-07, C-69), the retry of withheld items, Boone's text (C-70 to C-72).

### 3B. What kinds of claim the corpus can and cannot support (S5-P3 to P13, P18)

**Quantitative claims.** The corpus holds two kinds of number, and neither measures how often soldiers use gallows humor. The first is survey results: 93 volunteer U.S. veterans [E04-6, E04-8], 2,291 Swiss police [E04-15 to E04-19], 20,901 U.S. Army soldiers (one self-rated humor item; effects "small," [E04-24]), 210 Israeli survivors and spouses [E04-29], 179 firefighters [E05-16]. All are cross-sectional and self-reported; each author says direction cannot be established [E04-22; E04 Gaps 2]. "Dark humor" means different things in each: two Humor Styles subscales, four mockery styles, one self-rating, four styles [E04-15, E04-23]. The findings cannot be stacked, and they conflict [E04-8, E04-16, E04-29]. The second kind is harvest counts: 140 Rutgers passages, 172 UNSW, 102 in six archives, 76 AWM and Witness to War passages. The files state that these are counts of passages inside a keyword net [E24a-27, E24b-32, E24c-30, E24d-17]. A tag such as "gallows-humor 38" is the harvester's label, and at least five UNSW passages owe the tag to an interviewer's wording [E24d-43]. Terminal Lance is the one place a base rate exists, and it shows the sample overstated serious content six-fold [E19-58].

**Frequency, and specific armies.** No source supports a claim that one army, war or branch jokes more or differently. Each file says so: Korea and Cold War samples are too small to compare [E24a-27]; Vietnam-versus-other differences "track interview design and era" [E24b-32]; cross-army forms look the same in the oral-history sets but the counts are weak [E24c-30]. Which army each part can speak for: British Army, WWI (E07-E15); Anzac (E11, E24b, E24d); U.S. forces, mostly Army and Marines, Vietnam to Afghanistan (E06, E18-E19, E21-E22, E24a, E24c, E25); German and Danish peacetime and deployment (E01); Indian and Norwegian (E02); Israeli, Russian, Ukrainian, Syrian and North Korean cases only through scholarship, journalism or one memoir (E03-E04, E23). Army-specific findings rest on small bases: nine of twelve Indian Army interviewees were senior officers and Norway's picture rests on three foregrounded cases (E02 Source-shape, Gaps); the Marine Corps University set is all officers (E24c Source-shape).

**The keyword harvest and the word "humor."** A veteran who joked constantly but never said "joke," "laugh" or "humor" is invisible; one who said it once is over-represented [E24a-27]. Rutgers narrators supply 81 single-passage cases among 107 (E24a Source-shape). The harvest log warns that wordless understatement was likely missed [E25b Gaps]. Searches with "no" or "not" could not be run, so limits were reached by other routes (E24d Source-shape). Narrators who answer "no, we didn't have that" are absent [E24d Gaps]. Because interviewers often supplied "gallows" or "black humor" and some narrators refused the frame [E24d-43], a tag is not the narrator's own category; and the harder humor surfaced in unprompted answers while prompted answers were tame [E24b-22, E24b-23]. The effect is to over-represent people comfortable naming the practice and to under-represent concealment and absence, which the sources themselves say are common [E21-1, E25b-48, E24b-22].

**Retrospective bias.** Oral histories are 35 to 60 years after the events (E24d Source-shape); the WWI memoirs were written 1929-33, Richards from memory and Graves partly from a 1916 novel draft, and both reconstruct speech [E13a Source-shape, E15b Source-shape, E15c-89]. Narrators themselves say the funny version is what a group's memory keeps [E24b-30, E24d-24]. Within the WWI primary texts the split is roughly even (checklist counts, mine): about 556 findings from period texts (the Wipers issues, Anzac Book, Bairnsfather captions, Sassoon) and about 646 from 1929-33 retrospective texts (Songs and Slang, Richards, Graves); the 1930 Wipers prefaces are hindsight and are labeled as such [E08-1, E09a Gaps]. The period texts are in turn mostly officers' or editors' work [E08-28, E11a-2].

**Editor and curator bias.** The Anzac Book editor trimmed to "a half or a quarter" and footnoted for home readers [E11a-2]. The Songs and Slang editors chose "the most pointed" version and suppressed others [E12a-1]. The Wipers editors were two officers; the censor's role is known only from their jokes and a 1930 remark [E08-4]. Modern venues repeat the pattern: the Sandbox had an editor and a civilian audience [E25b-48]; War Horse edits and fact-checks [E25c Gaps]; Fox softened chapters and civilians still found them "too much" [E25e-81].

**Selection and cleaning.** What sits in the public record is already cleaned: strangers asked for war jokes sent clean ones [E21-1]; Songs and Slang dashed words "to avoid the rigors of the law" [E12a-19]. The filter then removed more (3G).

**Over-representation and missing voices.** WWI primary texts are 53% of findings (checklist); English-language sources dominate. Missing or thin: women (two Rutgers narrators [E24a-26]; 9 of 172 UNSW passages [E24d Gaps]; about 3 of 86 in E24c; no woman author at Terminal Lance [E19 Gaps]); minorities (no race coding in E24a; one advocacy essay on racial humor [E25c-4]); enemy-side voices (Turkish soldiers only through Anzac writers, E02 Source-shape; Russian jokes by a translator's selection, E23 Source-shape); Afghanistan and Iraq depth (none in UNSW or Rutgers [E24d Gaps, E24a-27]; six Fallujah passages [E24c-29]); and defectors [E23-1].

### 3D. Craft and depiction: what writers, designers and veterans say, and how far to trust it (S5-P24 to P34)

**Reliability ladder.** From strongest to weakest: (1) *participant or developer statements that name a cost*: EA's executive admitting the first *Army of Two* humor "went down like a lead balloon" [E22b-19]; the *Blackadder* director's account of the ending [E22b-3]; (2) *veteran-writers' reports of their own method*: Fox on what the formats do [E25e-79 to E25e-82], Best on the "dry" book voice against team-room talk [E21-40], Gallagher on saving real quips [E22a-2]; (3) *novelists' craft claims*: Klay, Abrams, Heller, Vonnegut as relayed by McConnell [E22a-12, E22a-37, E22a-44]; (4) *outlet and fan interpretation*: the *Generation Kill* listicle [E22b-6], the Boone blog [E22b-10], the *Helldivers 2* opinion piece [E22b-22]; (5) *academic readings of games*: Jensen [E06-38 to E06-50]. Klay and Abrams were staff officers, not combat arms [E22a-16, E22a-23].

**Both registers in one beat.** The most replicated craft claim. Klay: the best comedy is "both humorous and serious at once" [E22a-12]. Heller: a joke needs build-up, and three wisecracks in a row fail [E22a-37]. *Blackadder*'s ending withholds release: no cue back to the comic frame [E22b-3]. In veteran writing the plain beat is a short unjoked line at the end of a funny narrative [E25b-53, E25a-9, E25a-48, E25e-82]; in WWI primary texts the plain poem or one-clause loss sits beside the jokes [E08-38, E15b-89, E13b-24]. The Wipers editors put the plain statement inside the joke form once [E10a-22]. Reliability: strong as a pattern; each instance is a writer's choice.

**Funny version and sad version.** Klay's narrator sorts versions by listener and says the gory-funny one was false to the unit [E22a-19, E22a-57]; Klay credits "decoy stories" as distance devices [E22a-58]. Fox says the civilian reader tolerated less than the truth and still found the softened version "too much" [E25e-81]. Veterans in oral history say the funny version goes outward [E24a-22, E24b-30]. Strangers are easier than intimates [E22a-20].

**The quip after the kill.** The single documented failure case: killing for pay, a celebratory quip, a real setting with real casualties, and an unreadable target [E22b-19]. The diagnosis is audience-fit: "you can laugh off everything but not with everybody." Corroboration is indirect: veterans police their own lines [E21-13, E24d-22, E25b-11]; Terminal Lance's author apologizes once for a joke about a man who has died [E19-41]; Jensen reads squad cheering over kills as a moral cue to the player [E06-42]. The license model: exactness of detail and aiming at "the madness" rather than the dead [E22b-1].

**Trauma as backstory.** An outlet summarizing a video essay says games use PTSD as characterization shorthand [E22b-21]. The Boone fan blog illustrates the logic: "doing is active," so guilt is chosen for utility [E22b-11]. Remedies named: give the character content that is not the injury [E22b-21], include well-adjusted veterans [E25d-38], and let history leak through situations rather than a confession tree [E22b-14]. Reliability: craft judgment from outlets, not tested.

**Veterans' reactions to depictions.** Thin: one author's report that veterans split on whether the author's protagonists "nailed combat" [E22b-36]; Klay reports mostly positive reactions and that bureaucratic stories resonated most [E22a-17]; two interviewees on *Helldivers 2* [E22b-22]; a veteran reviewer defending footage but disciplining one joke [E21-13]; an outlet's "spot on" claim for *Generation Kill* with no named veteran verdict [E22b-6]. No survey exists.

**What advisors do and do not cover.** Documented remit: equipment, procedure, uniforms, tactics, "does it ring true" listening [E22b-24, E22b-26]. Accuracy yields to budget and, once, to a joke [E22b-27]. One advisor says input reaches "character vernacular" in a single clause [E22b-29]. The *Generation Kill* embed shaped bearing and kit, not documented dialogue [E22b-5]. Nothing shows advisors shaping what characters find funny, so a veteran of the relevant kind reading for tone is a separate role [E22b-24].

**Comic-professional craft.** Veteran comics and satirists give practical rules: disarm the room with a risky opener, then turn it; know the audience; jargon is the first barrier; what flops with a chain of command differs from what lands with troops [E21-19, E21-33, E25d-26]; the program's stated theory is a "back door" and permission to laugh [E21-34]; satire works when it is "based in reality" and when insiders write it [E21-26, E21-25, E21-37]. Reliability: practitioners' statements, often in promotional pages, with no outcome data [E21-35].

**Where the sensibility comes from.** Heller says the joking temperament predated the army and the critical outlook came after it [E22a-35, E22a-34]; McConnell calls the knack partly native and cultivable [E22a-49]; Vonnegut ties black humor to helplessness [E22a-44] and names the cost, the punch-line reflex that closes the subject [E22a-51]. Heller adds that an injury becomes jokeable once the outcome is secure, a point drawn from bad reviews rather than war [E22a-42]. Reliability: single writers' self-reports, some 50 years later, and relayed by a friend or student; useful for mechanism, not for how soldiers vary.

**Boone.** Documented: the quest was in the original proof-of-concept; the bill of sale is deliberately melodramatic; Boone is a sniper keeping vigil, and the statue is "somewhat goofy" [E22b-9]; the DLC line budget (about 10,000 lines for four DLC) and table reads [E22b-8]; a fan reading of reticence as the engine [E22b-10]. Not documented: any joke, banter or sarcasm [E22b-12]; the Sawyer GDC talk (abstract only) [E22b-13]; Fenstermaker's podcast and Boone's text (Tier C: C-70 to C-72).

**Other craft items.** Terminal Lance: a silent comic needed no dialogue [E19-57]; recurring series build a shared vocabulary [E19-59]. The plain turn inside a funny story is a "seam" a listener can feel (the extraction's phrase) [E25b-53]. Understatement over death as genre convention reads as unreality unless the bill is shown [E06-41].

### 3G. The content-filter log and what it means for completeness (S5-P35 to P37)

**What was withheld.** Part A: two outright denials, both on the Wipers Times range (CF-01, CF-02); recovered by splitting the range; the extraction agent's inference, unconfirmed, is that the trigger was a verse mock-confession listing atrocity charges (RH-06). Part B: fifteen hand-entered items (RH-01 to RH-15). Part B2: auto-rolled registers from 32 files, about 715 lines (mine). My keyword tally, approximate and overlapping: graphic wounds, death or casualties about 34%; sexual content about 23%; enemy dead, corpses, prisoners or atrocity about 23%; slur, ethnic or religious caricature about 18%; profanity about 10%; bodily function about 6%; suicide or self-harm about 6%. Separately, the project's bodily-waste boundary excluded about a dozen UNSW items across eleven interviews, one Witness to War story (search log, Tier B storycorps/witness/AWM) and a toilet prank kept but flagged [E24d Gaps, E24b-4].

**What that means.** Mechanism, target and reaction survive; wording and prevalence do not.
- *Atrocity and enemy-dead humor.* Present but summarized: Gallipoli letters and Wipers items on enemy dead [E02-24, E09a-52], Ukrainian memes on enemy casualties [E03-25 to E03-27], oral-history laughter at enemy dead and corpse props [E24c-6, E24d-8], Pinkston's endpoint [E06-13, E06-36]. The strongest documented pattern is that such jokes surfaced in unprompted answers by other ranks, 2 of 20 Ross passages [E24b-23], and that narrators police them [E24d-22, E25b-15].
- *Slur and ethnic humor.* Nicknames built on ethnicity, skin tone and enemy labels are listed but not given [E24d-1, E24d-2, E24c-6, E24c-11]; one advocacy essay describes the pressure to laugh along [E25c-4, E25c-5].
- *Sexual humor.* The largest ordinary register in several sources (Gallagher names a very large quantity of crude sexual joking as what fills the boredom [E22a-2]), yet it is the register most often paraphrased [E25a-35, E25d-23].
- *Suicide and self-harm humor.* Reported by topic only [E21-20, E18-26]; one essay's method of attempt withheld entirely [E25a-39]; a rant omitted [E25e Gaps]; Pinkston's 22-a-day statistic is unreliable [E06 Gaps 5].
- *Bodily-waste humor.* The richest register in some Australian interviews is absent by boundary [E24d Gaps].

The corpus therefore likely understates both the raw end (how cruel unit humor can get) and the crude ordinary middle. A writer should not infer from its tone that humor was milder than it was.

## 6. Design-relevant candidates

*Constraints a writer could treat as real-world facts about this kind of humor. Each is a candidate with its strength tag; none is dialogue; none fixes the defector's origin military.*

- **Two versions, sorted by audience (A).** The same story can be told funny or sad depending on who is listening, and the polished version can be a wall [E22a-57, E22a-58, E25e-81, E24b-30].
- **Plain beat, no joke after it (A).** Where the register drops, one short unjoked line carries the weight and nobody retrieves the mood [E25b-53, E22b-3, E25e-82].
- **Do not follow a kill with a quip (B).** The documented failure is a celebratory line after violence for pay in a real setting [E22b-19]; the model that works aims at the machine and not the dead [E22b-1, E22b-22].
- **Trauma is one trait among several, not the whole characterization (B).** Content beyond the injury, and the category of well-adjusted veterans, are the named remedies [E22b-21, E25d-38].
- **History leaks through events, not a confession tree (C).** A partner's past can be revealed through situations and the player's build rather than a dialogue tree [E22b-14].
- **License is earned and policed internally (B).** Veterans refuse some of their own cohort's jokes while defending the cohort against outsiders [E21-13, E25b-15, E24d-22].
- **Jokes are unit- and occupation-specific and can die in transit (C).** Anchored in a witnessed detail [E25d-1, E25d-2].
- **Reticence and a stranger audience (C).** More may be said, and said flatly, to a stranger than to people whose relationship the speaker cannot afford to alter [E22a-20].
- **The polish of a joke is not evidence of the state of the wound (C).** One comic novelist remained "just as conflicted" [E22a-29]; Vonnegut's smoking joke coexisted with emphysema [E22a-48].
- **A unit-less veteran needs a substitute audience (B).** A game, a meme thread, a hunting trip, a veteran pair [E22b-23, E25d-19, E25c-12, E25e-74].
- **Time after service has no single curve (D).** Humor can thin, persist as ritual, darken, or return after leaving; choose per person [E22a-52, E24a-23, E25a-31, E25d-16].
- **Self-mockery is not proof of ease or of distress (D).** One survey ties self-defeating humor to lower life satisfaction; another finds no link [E04-8, E04-29].
- **A change of register may be read by peers as a signal (C).** A proposal in the literature, not a validated practice [E04-20, E04-13].
- **Any depiction is filtered; choose the filter (C).** A studio executive says real field talk was rawer than anything used and that fidelity was not the goal [E22b-20].
- **Advisors check procedure and vocabulary, not what is funny (B).** Use a veteran of the right kind to read tone and expect veterans to split [E22b-24, E22b-36].
- **Defector humor is a posture, not a documented corpus (C).** Separating the people from the institution, aiming contempt at competence, and speaking from both inside and outside are what the craft sources offer [E22a-5, E22a-22, E22a-33].
- **Boone-style reticence is a fan reading, and Boone's humor is unknown (C).** Do not cite the model for or against a lighter side [E22b-10, E22b-12].
