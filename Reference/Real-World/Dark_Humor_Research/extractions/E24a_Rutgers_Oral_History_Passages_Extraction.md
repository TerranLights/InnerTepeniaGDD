## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What it is.** A keyword-and-phrase harvest, not a sample. The pull scanned 1,064 Rutgers transcript pages (21.6 million words), chose windows where the veteran *describes humor, joking, a prank, a nickname or laughing as a practice, or tells the joke or story itself*, and kept each window as the humor stretch plus about three sentences either side (cap 350 words; mean window here is about 137 words, 19,192 words of passage text in all). Windows often start and end mid-topic and are cut with `[...]`. They are not complete accounts, and a window rarely shows what the narrator said next.

**What the selection does NOT represent (stated in the harvest log, and confirmed in the data).**
- **It is biased toward people who say the words.** The retrieval ran on *humor, joke, laugh, nickname, kidding, gallows* and about 230 siblings. A veteran who joked constantly but never used those words is invisible; a veteran who said "humor" once is overrepresented. The log itself says most narrators have only one or two genuine passages and that about a dozen kept passages are mild ("a joke told in passing, a seasickness gag").
- **Narrator distribution (computed from the file):** of 107 named narrators, **81 have one passage, 22 have two, two have three (Feinberg, Freedman), one has four (Vellucci), one has five (Sees).** Four narrators supply 15 of 140 passages (10.7%). Vietnam-era passages are 29 of 140, and three narrators (Sees, Vellucci, Freedman) supply 12 of those 29, so the Vietnam picture is mostly three voices.
- **The wars are unbalanced:** by my reading of each passage's setting, **WWII 94, Korea 12, Vietnam-era 29, Cold War 4, one relayed family story (#121)**. Counts for Korea and Cold War are too small to support any comparison (see [E24a-27]).
- **Branch is unbalanced:** by passage, **Army 81 (including one Army medical narrator), Navy and Navy-affiliated 27 (including WAVES, Navy Air Corps), Army Air Forces/Air Force 15, Marine Corps 14 (including one Marine-then-Army narrator), Coast Guard 1, American Field Service 1, one non-veteran niece 1.** By interview (110): Army 58, Navy-affiliated 23, Air 14, Marine 12, other 3.
- **Voices missing or thin:** two narrators are in women's services (Godfrey, Drelich; 3 passages). The passages do not identify narrators by race, so I cannot say whether any narrator is Black or from any other group; no breakdown by race or ethnicity is possible. **Officers versus enlisted** is mixed and not coded in the archive metadata; I read rank from content only where the narrator says it.
- **Descriptor limits.** The archive's war label belongs to the narrator, not always to the passage: #7 (Arthur, labeled Cold War) tells a WWII family joke; #124 (Taylor, labeled Vietnam) describes a fence-line guard assignment at what reads as a Cuba base (Leeward Point, cut water pipeline) with no Vietnam content; #136 (Whitson, Korea) is a pre-service ROTC scene; #121 (Smith) relays a relative's stories (apparently of an older war: "the trenches"). I coded setting from content and flag these.
- **Edited transcripts.** Rutgers text carries the archive's `[laughter]` marks and editor's notes; the laughter marks are the transcriber's and say nothing about what the laughter meant.
- **Rights.** Own-words distillation, few-word quotations only, per the archive's terms. Passages are not reproduced.
- **Boilerplate skipped.** The passages file's own header (lines 1-9: reading conventions, count and the permission notice) is not analyzed; every passage after it was read.

**Two kinds of passage are present and I count them separately.** *Specific:* the narrator tells a joke, prank, nickname, quip or comic incident (112 of 140, by my coding). *Commentary only:* the narrator talks about humor as a practice without a concrete item (28 of 140: #10, #11, #16, #26, #34, #35, #36, #37, #38, #49, #59, #75, #76, #79, #84, #91, #94, #107, #109, #112, #114, #115, #118, #119, #120, #133, #134, #139).

## Findings

### 1. What they joked about; the forms; the targets

**[E24a-1]. Danger itself is the commonest target, and the commonest device is deflating the odds.** (42 of 140 passages by my coding; see #2, #8, #9, #24, #25, #30, #32, #40, #45, #50, #77, #97, #108, #117, #122, #124; ROHA-1380 p.15, 742 p.46, 741 p.27, 685 p.18, 1739 p.6, 652 p.21, 574 p.19, 614 p.37-38, 589 p.29, 442 p.60, 1664 p.24, 1727 p.26-27, 199 p.30, 165 p.23, 1755 p.8)
The danger jokes in these passages are mostly *one-line reversals of an official or hopeful frame*. Examples, described: Marines (Aiello, USMC WWII) say nobody ever lost a beachhead because the landing craft drops you and leaves, so there was nowhere to go but forward; an Army officer ordered to a training post in the South in the fall (Bach, Army, WWII and Korea) calls it a winter trip while knowing Korea is next, and the speaker labels the remark "gallows humor"; a supply sergeant's quip while issuing parachutes (a return policy if it fails, said with a laugh) was itself the joke (Clark, AAF); ground crews say they will patch flak holes with chewing gum (Ginobile, AAF); a PT-boat man says an enemy could blow the boat up with a small-caliber rifle because of its unprotected fuel tanks (Frankel, USN WWII); an artillery listener's joke has the soldier running, digging in and finishing a prayer before the shell lands, recalled as a shell struck five feet away and proved a dud (Silverman, Army WWII); Marines on a fence line issued eleven rounds each share the standing joke about shooting ten and saving the last round for oneself (Taylor, USMC; the context reads as Guantanamo-type duty); a Navy trainee reports the LST acronym joke, "large slow target," and the discovery that no instructor had ever ridden one (Schreiber, USN WWII). One narrator describes *euphemizing the voyage*: a "free trip" to Europe, and jokes about "going to war on a ferryboat" (Brown, Army WWII, #24, #25). A submarine crewman posted high tells the captain there are bees up there; the captain laughs, orders the crewman down and names them machine-gun rounds (Casper, USN WWII, #30). Another Navy man's seasickness line (first you fear you will die, then you fear you will not) is called "the standard joke" (Nisonoff, #90). A friend at battalion headquarters who checked the casualty lists for the narrator each day teased that the sector had become as quiet as a safe rear town, which the narrator calls "our little joke" during a lull in which units began holding Sunday picnics (Gurbisz, Army transportation, Vietnam, #54).
*Mechanism:* an inherited, portable formula applied to a new danger; the humor is borrowed rather than invented (the "standard joke," the "standing joke"), and several are reduced to a single word or acronym (#9: a man on the phone with a woman in another office during a buzz-bomb alert borrows a rifle-range signal for a total miss to describe the incoming bomb; both then get under their desks, wait out the blast, and pick the call back up).
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's danger humor is more credible as a worn, shared formula than as an original wisecrack; the repertoire is part of what a unit hands a new member.
*Cross-use:* Robot frames with a known failure mode could hold a similar inherited set of mock-odds formulas.

**[E24a-2]. Command, rank and the institution are the second-commonest target (37 of 140), and the safe direction depends on whether the target can hear.** (see #3, #13, #23, #27, #29, #46, #51, #55, #69, #96, #104, #105, #110, #111, #128, #129, #135, #138; ROHA-354 p.7, 725 p.50, 690 p.20, 674 p.21, 667 p.10, 588 p.35, 572 p.31, 467 p.33-34, 286 p.22, 1881 p.24, 1976 pp.29/32, 1378 pp.28/35-36, 70 p.43, 69 p.35)
The command material sorts into modes. (a) **Affectionate or admiring** stories about a commander's quirk (a ship's captain has a lobster pulled from a trap and leaves a banknote in a bottle for the lobsterman, Agron, USN WWII, #1; a general's profane pep talk to departing troops that they laughed at, Beaumont, Army WWII, #13). (b) **Covert mocking nicknames** (#23 "Dugout Doug" for MacArthur, used among officers recalled from reserve duty and "never in his face"; #3 "Shaky Six" for a hesitant battalion commander). (c) **Imitation and ridicule among peers at night** (Burger, Army WWII: the narrator and an older buddy often spent evenings mocking and imitating the sergeant and officers, which the narrator says sharpened an awareness of class, #27). (d) **Cartoon in a licensed outlet** (a unit newspaper ran a cartoon of the disliked medical officer after he angered the enlisted men; the narrator says the man had "no sense of humor," Koft, USN WWII, #69). (e) **Rank-line jokes taught in boot camp** (a Navy women's boot camp joke about whether an officer's spouse is kissed or saluted, #51). (f) **Mockery of the institution's own contradictions** ("no excursions into Laos" read in Laos, #128; a unit's running quip that men picked for a cadre were either failures or stars, #105). (g) **Pranks and jokes aimed at officers, where the target's reaction is the whole payoff:** a captain who presented himself in a general's insignia to a colonel, who afterward called it the funniest joke ever played on him (Wiggin, Army WWII Pacific, #138); an enlisted man who asks a famous general's son (his own squadron commander) for the father's well-known revolvers, because the narrator knew the man "had a sense of humor," and the commander laughed and later remarked to the troop commander on the soldier's mouth (Vellucci, Army Vietnam, #129; the remark is from context consulted, p.36); a lieutenant joking in a helmet marked with stars when a real general's car pulled up (Sees, Army Vietnam, #110; the outcome is outside the window); a wardroom of officers that staged a huddle around a sleeping "ball hog" who joined in, unaware (Gutman, USN WWII, #55); and men of a mechanized unit who joked about their colonel's famous cavalry namesake and a newspaper headline about Native soldiers serving with him (Sees, #113, restrained; context consulted pp.68-69, where the narrator also jokes with the colonel about a planned river crossing resembling a famous defeat).
*Mechanism:* the narrators treat the target's capacity to take it as *the thing being tested* (Vellucci's stated reason for the joke; Koft's contrast with a man who had none). The nickname travels; the face does not hear it.
*Candidate application:* A character who ridicules authority can be written as fluent in *reading which officers can take it*, and the roster of who can is part of what the character carries.

**[E24a-3]. Nicknames are verdicts that stick, and several are accidents of circumstance.** (10 passages by my coding; #3, #5, #23, #46, #65, #83, #98, #100, #101, #102; ROHA-354 p.7, 1523 p.26-27, 690 p.20, 588 p.35, 1741 p.30, 390 p.25-26, 46 p.41, 310 p.28, 300 p.26, 294 p.24)
Types seen: **by trait or origin** (an American Indian machine gunner "Chief," a Hawaiian platoon sergeant, men called by home state; the narrator says nicknames, not last names, are what is remembered, Arbasetti, Army 1st Cav., Vietnam, #5); **given by the narrator in passing and kept for a year** (Freedman, Army Vietnam, names a heavy, winded lieutenant "Puff"; adding that the narrator is bad at nicknames, #46); **commander nicknames** (above); **unit monikers that record fate**: a Marine infantry company that picked up the grim name "Walking Dead" because it was hit wherever it went (Johnson, USMC Vietnam, #65, told in passing while dismissing a novelist's account); a division adopted a pun on a fortress it faced for three months, and printed unofficial membership cards afterward (Prout, Army WWII, #100); a pilots' room called "The Funny Farm" (Retz, USN Vietnam, #102); **ethnic or religious labels** (a headquarters company of soldiers with Italian names who were kept away from the horses, #83; a call sign built on being the only Jewish officer in the troop, #98 — both handled in restrained terms, see register); **people named for what the group made of them** (Reamer, Army WWII Pacific, #101: a merchant seaman badly disfigured by shrapnel who did a machine-gun routine that got "all kinds of laughs," followed by the narrator's remark that the group always had nicknames for people, by role or education; the window does not say whether this was a prison-camp group, so I do not count it as captivity with certainty).
*Candidate application:* Nicknames give a one-word history of an event or a flaw; a character may have two names, the formal one and the one a unit attached to a single incident.
*Cross-use:* Exile or defector groups might carry nicknames that encode how each person arrived.

**[E24a-4]. Pranks fall into four families, and the largest is the initiation prank on a newcomer.** (23 by my coding: about 10 on newcomers or recruits (#4, #14, #20, #39, #41, #52, #56, #89, #92, #135), 4 among peers as horseplay or enforcement (#12, #55, #63, #102), 6 aimed up at officers or authority (#29, #96, #110, #125, #129, #138), 3 outward at the enemy or local people (#93, #123, #132); some fit two families, so these are my best assignments; #12, #14, #20, #29, #39, #41, #52, #55, #56, #63, #89, #92, #96, #102, #110, #123, #125, #129, #135, #138; ROHA-729 p.14-15, 1365 p.21-22, 1381 p.36, 616 p.7-8, 548 p.15, 345 p.20, 1420 p.30, 294 p.24, 511 p.63, 1664 p.11, 162 p.25-26)
A recurring *fake-errand* genre: a recruit sent for a left-handed wrench (Epstein, AAF, #39); new sailors told to change money at a bank for "Hawaiian money" (Bogart, USN, #20); a young woman trainee who was told a horn exists to warn the aircraft ahead (Haines, Army WWII Pacific, #56, recounting others' teasing); a pilot who proudly reported his plane's twenty-four "kills" and was told they were albatross strikes (Paraskevas, USN Air, #92; "pulling your leg," said the flight engineer); experienced training-base crews who slipped a solid irritant into a lamp as new crews passed through (Nisonoff, USN, #89). *Peer enforcement dressed as a prank* appears: a submarine crew scrubs a man who will not wash (Baumgarten, #12); Marine boot-camp instructors ridicule recruits whose letters smell of perfume and give them some extra task (Everhard, USMC Vietnam era, #41); a boot-camp bathroom prank involving fire (Bebbington, USMC WWII, #14; restrained, sanitation). A **dark prank** is a piece of shrapnel left in a man's foxhole by "one of the clowns," which the narrator labels "sense of humor" (Irwin, USMC WWII, #63). A **joke against local civilians in a war zone** (Sobin, AAF WWII: a crew drops empty whiskey bottles, through the chute used for navigation markers, on a town that kept its lights on at night; the lights went out and the crew "laughed and laughed," #123; the window does not say whose town it was), and **taunting counterparts with war news** (Phillips, AAF Pacific, #93; the passage does not say whether the Japanese the narrator teased were prisoners or language-work colleagues, so I do not file it under captivity).
Further examples: a company commander's tall tale to a junior officer about the reliability of the local militia the narrator was to liaise with (Alberts, #4; the window's wording about the militia is ethnic and is paraphrased); a unit sergeant who shouts that "the barber's looking for you" to a recruit evidently overdue for a haircut (Popielowski, Army Vietnam, #96, who says the call was ignored twice without penalty and calls it "the humorous side"), and an experienced airman, already through the European war and known for his sense of humor, who needled a young lieutenant instructor with feigned awe at the man's youth and the size of the aircraft, saving his sharpest question for the final lesson on losing power at takeoff (Whitlock, AAF WWII Pacific, #135; the window ends before the payoff). A soldier who had dug a hole as punishment hired local laborers to do it, then had to pay them again to fill it when the lieutenant saw an empty can in it and ordered the can buried (Taylor, USMC, #125; the narrator's wording about the laborers is a slur and is not reproduced).
*Mechanism:* the fake errand tests who is new and teaches that credulity costs; peer-enforced hygiene makes humor do the work of rules; pranks at officers' expense are mostly told as stories in which the officer's own reaction decides whether it was a joke or an offense. Officers appear as both targets and co-conspirators (#55, #135, #138).
*Candidate application:* A veteran character may recognize and reflexively test newcomers; that character's own initiation story can be told as a prank, not as a trauma.

**[E24a-5]. The funniest *retold* items are physical accidents under fire, and the laughter is simultaneous with the danger.** (23 anecdotes; #33, #60, #66, #67, #85, #87; ROHA-2148 p.29, 541 p.32, 493 p.21, 474 p.35, 385 p.11, 382 p.33-34)
A heavy soldier runs after a gun truck that will not slow while shells fall short; everyone, including the runner, laughs and he is screaming with anger at the same time (Harmon, Army WWII, #60). A rushed assault on a shed produces a flock of chickens and no kills, and the men "stood and laughed" (Mickett, Army WWII, #85; narrator: "You gotta have some fun"). Two men dive into one foxhole and cannot untangle while shells burst; the narrator says they "had to laugh," then felt weak-kneed an hour later, then retold it with the other man weeks before the interview (Mojo, Army WWII, #87). A driver is first out of his truck when a suicide plane hits a ship (Csontos, USN, #33). Germans capture a lone GI in order to surrender to the Americans (Kalugin, Army WWII, #66). A storm sprays a colonel in white pajamas with fuel oil (Kingston, USN, #67), a story the narrator qualifies as funny only for those not in it.
*Mechanism:* slapstick under threat, where the comedy is the gap between the danger and the ridiculous body; the story survives as a reunion piece.
*Candidate application:* A character's *funny war stories* can be the ones where nobody was hurt; the stories that omit a casualty may be the ones that get told most.

**[E24a-6]. Songs, cartoons and printed humor mediate the soldiers' humor, and narrators measure their own against popular depictions.** (6 form-coded; #4, #19, #31, #61, #69, #71, #78, #118, #119; ROHA-354 p.24, 712 p.36, 655 p.24-25, 531 p.12, 467 p.33-34, 465 p.13, 429 p.28, 191 pp.31-33)
Items: a ribald ballad about a shipwrecked party's long trek home, recited in the field by Marines (Alberts, USMC Korea, #4; the source of the song is not stated; the speaker's own gloss on bawdy material among many men is "what else can you laugh at"); improvised music on a board, a comb and a bottle by some men in an infantry company (Kolodziej, #71); a USO comedian's acronym joke about the port of embarkation (Ciampa, Army WWII, #31); a wartime newspaper cartoon, remembered by a former POW, of the GI who answers an offer of a Purple Heart by asking for an aspirin (Blaher, #19, with Mauldin's cartoon characters named); a Sad Sack panel about a German prisoner's sign "Ship to USA" (Logan, Army WWII Pacific, #78); and Hilberg's comparison of the narrator to the Mauldin GI with sunken eyes (#61). Sive (Army WWII, #118, #119) credits Mauldin and Ernie Pyle, and "the better novels," with documenting the infantry's "necessary humor," and uses their writing as authority. On the Vietnam and Korea side the measuring-stick is film or novel: Taylor (#125) says the unit was nothing like *Platoon*; English (Army Korea, #38) says a MASH unit resembled the television show without the joking; Johnson (USMC Vietnam, #65) dismisses a novel's account.
*Mechanism:* soldiers appear to learn what their humor is *supposed to look like* from print and screen, and use it as both template and foil.
*Candidate application:* For a character who left a military in a fictional universe, the equivalent is a stock of in-world cartoon, serial and entertainment figures against which to compare the experience.

**[E24a-7]. Mock rituals: short-timer taunting, mail-call ridicule, nightly mimicry.** (4 coded; #27, #41, #116, #131; ROHA-674 p.21, 609 p.18, 1607 p.16, 1378 p.46)
In Vietnam, a man with two days left taunts the new arrival who has 365; the narrator insists it was "busting your chops" in jest, not malice (Siani, USN Vietnam, #116). A man near rotation builds a paper ladder because he is "so short" he needs one to climb into his bunk; Vellucci (Army Vietnam, #131, context consulted pp.45-46) says that is how, in a spot like that, you have to "stretch for humor," that the narrator did not try it until the last thirty days because thinking ahead can hurt you, and that the same custom existed among troops in Germany. The ritual is a countdown made comic, and it needs a fixed tour to exist. Two other rituals in the group are of a different kind: a mail-call custom in which boot-camp instructors ridicule scented letters (#41) and the nightly mimicry of superiors by a pair of trainees (#27).
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type culture with a fixed term of service can have a countdown comedy tradition; one with indefinite service may invent a different one.

**[E24a-8]. The enemy is mostly a comic figure of incompetence, credulity or reversal, not an object of hatred; the hate-adjacent items are few and are handled in restraint.** (21 by my coding; #17, #18, #22, #28, #43, #53, #57, #58, #66, #72, #78, #82, #93, #95, #103, #106, #109, #122, #123, #132, #140; ROHA-1674 p.24, 712 p.30, 692 p.30, 671 p.36, 2013 p.79, 563 p.30, 547 p.21, 1547 p.27, 493 p.21, 465 p.21, 429 p.28, 1849 p.43, 328 p.20, 313 p.10, 256 p.59, 165 p.23, 162 p.25-26, 58 p.40)
Types, described: (a) **ridicule of the enemy's competence or nerve**: a "standard joke" in the U.S. Navy that the Italian fleet always ran the other way, with the arrangement of its gun turrets as the proof (Yoken, USN WWII, #140); a flier's running gag that enemy antiaircraft crews must also have had a tax exemption for combat, since they always got one element of the shot wrong (Snyder, USAF Korea, #122); a story of Germans who captured one American in order to surrender to him (#66); Austrian troops waving handkerchiefs whom a commander told his men to ignore, because prisoners have to be fed and guarded (Hance, Army WWII, #58, who describes the telling as giving "the funny side of the war"). (b) **mockery of the enemy's information control**: a nightly loudspeaker claim about a castle that had long since fallen, a "standing joke" among captured Americans (#18). (c) **reversal jokes exposing mutual suspicion**: Vietnamese villagers greeting passing soldiers with a stock phrase, and the soldiers joking that the same people would say the opposite when the Viet Cong came through (McClung, Army Vietnam, #82), which the narrator follows with "you didn't know who to trust." (d) **teasing a captive counterpart with news of their side's losses** (#93) and **ex-prisoners mock-announcing a takeover to German prisoners of their own** (#106, context consulted, see [E24a-20]). (e) **enemy wounded as the butt** (#132, restrained). (f) **a mirror**: a Korean prisoner asked to identify a guard who had abused prisoners says all the American guards look alike, echoing the GIs' own remark about prisoners; the interpreter laughs and the American officer, who shouted at the interpreter, then wonders whether the prisoner spoke from fear (Gordon, Army Korea, #53; the GIs' slur is not reproduced). (g) **children, a landmine and a shoeshine** (#28, restrained).
*Mechanism:* the commonest jokes make the enemy fallible or the situation absurd and leave the menace intact. Where the humor turns toward the enemy as a race or a body, the narrator usually marks it (see [E24a-18]).
*Candidate application:* A character from a long cold or hot war may carry a stock of "the other side" jokes about incompetence and propaganda; the ones that dehumanize are more likely to be the ones the character now winces at.

**[E24a-9]. The wounded and the dead: the injured own the license, third parties are suspect, and the dead are almost never the butt.** (17 by my coding; #6, #19, #37, #38, #61, #62, #64, #80, #86, #99, #101, #107, #112, #114, #115, #132, #136; ROHA-1523 p.30, 1525 p.12, 531 p.12, 417 p.41, 511 p.61, 382 p.24, 238 p.28, 2031 p.24)
*The injured joking:* young Marines in orthopedic casts "loved to joke around" and did not say "poor me," in the account of a WAVES ward staff member who read it as pleasure at being alive and going home (Drelich, #37). A man the narrator knew lost a hand to a shell in a bunker and made it a family joke: his children teething on it, a false hand held in position at formal events such as his own wedding, a pension drawn (Madden, USN WWII/Korea, #80). An exhausted, underweight infantryman sent to hospital was told his muddy coat was a "walking foxhole" and, flown to England, told a nurse the plane was flapping its wings (Hilberg, Army WWII, #61). *Jokes at the award line:* a senior officer, told that one of the narrator's men was shot returning from a night errand, notes dryly that a sniper had fired at him on the same route and it could be the same man, and proposes writing the man up for a Purple Heart (Irwin, USMC WWII, #62; the dry tone is my reading, the harvest tags it gallows humor); a wartime cartoon in which the filthy GI declines a Purple Heart and asks for an aspirin (#19). *Medical absurdity:* a doctor's advice to a man with damaged feet (keep them warm, change socks three times a day) which the narrator calls "humorous" and followed about once a month (Mojo, Army WWII, #86). *The joking of third parties:* reserve officers who were WWII veterans laughing at a wounded fighter pilot who had come to speak, saying he had got "the million dollar wound," which Whitson (Army Korea, #136) connects to the narrator's own later reduced sympathy for the wounded. *Breakdown:* a captain bans wisecracks about a soldier who "went off his head" and asks the men to greet him warmly; two days later the man broke again and was transferred away (Schmidt, Army WWII, #107). *The dead:* an annoying man who sang a jingle in people's ears until the narrator "lost the joke" (after the second time) was killed that day, and the narrator, who cannot recall the man's name, describes feeling "kind of confused" by it (Arbasetti, Army 1st Cav., Vietnam, #6); officers joked at breakfast and one was dead by dinner (Sees, #114, context consulted); a narrator argues with a colonel that the narrator and the two men left behind had "joked with" each other, so the narrator had not abandoned friends (Sees, #112, context consulted pp.56-57). Restrained: #64, #99, #101, #132 (injuries and a commander's comment on civilian life).
*Mechanism:* the license follows risk and relation: the wounded and their comrades may joke; an authority polices jokes about a breakdown; a third party's joke about a wound reads as status contempt. The *dead* appear in these passages as the person whose last joke or irritation is remembered, not as a target.
*Candidate application:* In a soldier-type character, the line can be that the character jokes about their own injury but does not let others joke about a friend's. The irritant who dies the same day is a documented pattern: the annoying joke becomes the thing that is missed.

**[E24a-10]. Smaller targets: oneself, other services and allies, medals, and women or sex; each carries its own rule.** (self 10; other services 8; awards 4; women, sex and mail about 6)
- **Self.** The self-targeted passages are mostly *bounded embarrassments* in which the narrator is the fool and the unit is the audience: a WWII Marine veteran serving in Korea with the Army eight years after the last shell of the earlier war dives for cover at the sound of a mortar and drops a water can while longer-serving men laugh, which became "a big joke" when the narrator reached the unit (Krugh, #74); a crowded cargo plane in which the narrator tells the only man with a parachute that if he jumps, the narrator will be on top of him (Leonard, Army WWII, #77); a B-17 crewman who worked the radar and told crewmates the missions were flown to get the narrator to the work (Ershow, AAF, #40); a standing joke that the narrator's Bronze Star was for being the only officer in the division to avoid venereal disease (Freedman, Army Vietnam, #48, "just a joke"); a narrator's brother, a Coast Guardsman who reached the Pacific just before the war ended, joking that the surrender came because the enemy heard he was on the way (Arthur, Army, #7, "another joke in the family"). The group's laugh at a veteran's old reflex is not hostile in these accounts; it marks the newcomer as one of them (#74).
- **Other services and allies.** Ribbing is formulaic and two-sided: Coast Guard and Navy swap lines about shallow water and wet socks (Bosenberg, #21); Marines mock the other services and add "we still do" (Velcheck, USMC Vietnam, #127); Americans needle British allies over the spelling of lieutenant, and watch Turkish soldiers with ceremonial swords (May, Army Korea, #81); men in the field mock "Saigon warriors" who never left town (Freedman, #47); a trainer's prank on incoming crews (Nisonoff, #89); a Marine's remark that the Navy drops you and leaves (#2).
- **Medals and awards.** Four passages make decorations the joke: #19 (aspirin for a Purple Heart), #48 (Bronze Star), #62 (a Purple Heart for a sniper's victim) and #70 (a ship's joke that for every fifty trips to the head a sailor may add a mock copper pot to the campaign ribbon).
- **Women, sex, home mail.** Mild and few: a bawdy ballad (#4), scented-letter ridicule (#41), the venereal-disease joke (#48), a "Dear John" letter that the men around the narrator read together and answered collectively in the narrator's name, after which the woman broke her engagement to someone else (Kolodziej, Army WWII, #71), and Marines in casts "taking advantage of females" in a ward staff member's phrase (#37). No passage in the set is primarily about sexual content; none carries it as a dark joke. (Restrained handling: see register.)
*Mechanism:* each of these is a membership ritual: the self-fool is accepted; the other-service jokes assert unit identity; the award jokes puncture institutional rewards.
*Candidate application:* A character's tasteful list of *who we mock* (other branches, rear echelons, the awards) says more about identity than the darkest joke does.

### 2. What narrators say humor did (in their own explanations)

**[E24a-11]. Twenty-seven passages carry an explicit explanation; the explanations cluster around tension, morale, staying sane, and laughing because alive.** (#4, #8, #11, #15, #19, #33, #34, #35, #42, #44, #58, #59, #67, #68, #71, #76, #84, #85, #87, #88, #115, #118, #119, #126, #130, #131, #133; ROHA-354 p.24, 735 p.7, 712 p.36, 2013 pp.42/93-94, 1547 p.27, 542 p.26, 470 p.20, 465 p.13, 445 p.34, 387 p.21-22, 385 p.11, 382 p.33-34, 374 p.22-23, 191 pp.31-33, 640 p.17, 2066 p.25, 1378 pp.37/46)
Narrators' own stated functions, in their own vocabulary:
- **Tension and spirits.** Feinberg (Army medic WWII, #44) says that when people are tense, a bit of humor to calm them, "to break the tension," is something Feinberg describes doing and valuing in others. Meyers (Army WWII, #84) says that on first hearing artillery the thought was that the jokes were over, but they joked constantly anyway, "like whistling in a cemetery," to keep spirits up, and then adds that when things get very bad the best thing is to sleep. Hansen (Army WWII Pacific, #59) sets joking and fun against a life described as grim and unhappy but necessary, and notes that men differed on whether they took military life seriously or only wanted it over. Alberts' phrase for bawdy material is "what else can you laugh at" (#4). Kolodziej (#71), asked about battlefield humor, answers that in any group someone has a talent and that "you always amuse yourself."
- **Endurance and sanity.** Sive (Army WWII, #118, #119, two passages) twice says that what the infantry had to develop was "necessary fatalism" along with "necessary humor," and that for infantry, unlike air crew who were rotated out after a set number of missions, this was the only comfort and way of "staying sane." Mauldin and Pyle are named as the record. Barry (Army WWII, #11) was told by a WWI veteran at the farewell party that a man can "die from laughing" in the Army and that it would carry a man through tough spots, and Barry says it proved true.
- **Laughing because alive.** Leader (Army WWII, #76): after a strafing run so close the pilot's face was visible, Leader and a buddy "roared with laughter," and Leader asks "Why laugh?" and answers that it was because they were alive. Mojo (#87) laughs at being hopelessly tangled with another man in a foxhole while shells burst, and says the narrator's legs felt like jelly an hour later; Mickett (#85) laughs at the chickens that flew out of a building the squad had just stormed; Csontos (#33) laughs in the retelling at the driver who left his truck first when a suicide plane hit. Sees (the narrator of #112; context consulted, outside the harvested window) describes lying on the floor of the extraction helicopter laughing that they were out, and crying on reaching the unit's night position.
- **Imagining the later laugh.** A fellow trainee who hated the Army and getting up in the morning would say to the men sitting around complaining that "twenty years from now," if they were alive, they would be laughing about it; the narrator thought it good for the group (Mortensen, Army WWII, #88).
- **Bravado as performance.** Kneller (101st Airborne, #68) says that when the Germans attacked the narrator kidded around, tossing a coin and claiming one of the attackers, and then adds "I was acting": the narrator was frightened, and once the big shells and tanks came in says so plainly.
- **Tone-setting by the narrator about the story itself.** Hance (#58): you "keep your sense of humor." Dauster (#34): in a life stripped to a "primitive" level, small things "provoke hilarity." Dougherty (Army Cold War, #35): they "joked and laughed" a lot in an officer-training environment that fed men to Vietnam, but "it was serious business." Vellucci (#131) says a man has to "stretch for humor" near the end of a tour but it depends on psyche.
- **Vocabulary.** The phrase "sense of humor" appears in 12 passages: as a person's trait (a buddy, an officer, an instructor, a reunion member, and the doctor who lacked one; #27, #69, #129, #135, #137), as something you "have to have" (#19, #58) or cannot easily be without in the face of an absurd order (#128), as the explanation the narrator gives for a prank (#63), and as a collective trait of Americans (#53, #73) or of soldiers (#130). "Gallows humor" or "black humor" appears in three (#8, #15, #139).
- **Not a function but a cost:** see [E24a-19].
*Mechanism:* no narrator offers a theory beyond "relief," "spirit," "sanity" and "because we were alive"; none says humor *made* anyone braver. The strongest claim, Sive's, ties it to the absence of an exit date.
*Candidate application:* A veteran character's own account of why the character jokes would be short and practical (it gets you through, it keeps spirits up), not psychological; longer psychological explanations do not appear in these narrators' own words.

**[E24a-12]. The proximity-and-time rule: funny when it is not happening to you, or when it is over.** (#11, #16, #35, #36, #67, #126; ROHA-735 p.7, 1674 p.11, 1594 pp.11/14, 474 p.35, 2066 p.25; context consulted: Mickett p.45)
Four narrators state the rule themselves. Barry (#11): you can see something humorous in almost everything that happens, but if it happens to you you take a different attitude. Kingston (USN WWII, #67): the colonel soaked in fuel oil was funny "as long as you weren't involved." Velcheck (USMC Vietnam, #126): after a mortar round punctured a case of rations the narrator says it can be laughed at now, but it "wasn't funny then." Dougherty (#35, #36): "We laugh about it now," while the time itself was "serious business." Berke (USAF Korea, #16) supplies the case in the negative: after an in-flight failure that nearly killed the pilot, the maintenance chief arrived in a jeep laughing, and the pilot was appalled; the failure traced to a panel left unfastened by the chief's own crew, which is why the narrator reads the laugh as callous (context consulted: pp.11-12). A related Mickett passage (context consulted p.45, outside any harvested window): a battalion officer's over-bold advice after a minefield scare was laughed at, the narrator says, "Not there, but, afterwards."
*Mechanism:* two distances do the work: being the observer rather than the victim, and time. The same event can be comedy for one man and outrage for another in the same minute (#16).
*Candidate application:* In scenes, the same joke can be told by a man who was not at risk and heard as an insult by a man who was; the *right to laugh* often arrives later.

**[E24a-13]. Humor as an offering to the group, and how the offering fails: leaders, newcomers and the rest.** (#15, #17, #30, #42, #44, #68, #75; ROHA-720 p.48, 1674 p.24, 1739 p.6, 2013 pp.42/93-94, 470 p.20, 445 p.4)
Several narrators describe someone *trying to use humor on others*. Feinberg tried a quip in a bombed cellar to ease three tense medics and it was "like talking to the wall" (context consulted p.42, the window ends before the quip); later, when a company's men were pinned down, the senior man Feinberg had been following (the platoon guide, as the context reads) walked up grinning with a profane complaint about the situation and Feinberg "could've kissed him" (#44, context consulted). Leader (#75, context consulted p.4), a replacement, made a cheerful remark on arrival at an infantry company and was told flatly that no one joked there because men were getting killed; Leader had been trying to be upbeat because everyone looked down. A briefing in Berke's Cold War Air Force squadron before bombing practice in North Africa (#17) built a dire warning about crashing near local homes, and a deadpan rule about mounted local patrols and shepherds' white flags, into one routine that "everybody laughed" at, though the narrator notes there were always shepherds and nobody ever saw a rider (restrained: the warning concerns mutilation). A submarine captain laughed at a crewman who called machine-gun fire "bees," then ordered the crewman down (#30). The Marine artilleryman Berglund (#15; context consulted pp.47-49) describes a warrant officer past retirement joking, while an enemy gun was walking rounds toward the battery, that if the next one hit him it would save the Treasury his pension; the men laughed, and the next day a new lieutenant who slept ten feet away told the captain something must be wrong with him because he had been frightened and the others had laughed. Berglund calls it "gallows humor" twice.
*Mechanism:* the joke is an offer that must be accepted; the same line can be a lifeline, a miss or a breach, depending on who is already in the register (see [E24a-14]).
*Candidate application:* A character who gives comic relief for a living can also be written as one who misjudges the room, once in the book.

**[E24a-14]. The register is learned, and a newcomer can be shut out by it or let in by it.** (#5, #15, #20, #39, #56, #74, #75, #84, #92; ROHA-1523 p.26-27, 720 p.48, 1381 p.36, 616 p.7-8, 548 p.15, 1532 p.35-36, 445 p.4, 387 p.21-22, 1420 p.30)
Entry mechanisms: *proof by presence* (Arbasetti: you feel "part of the established family" once you have stayed, not run away, and a newer man arrives; the narrator says nicknames are what is remembered of the men; #5); *proof by prank* (the fake-errand pranks, [E24a-4]); *proof by mocking reflex* (Krugh's dropped water can, #74); *exclusion by the wrong register* (Leader's cheerful remark, #75; Berglund's lieutenant who read laughter as a sign something was wrong with the veterans, #15). Meyers (#84) places the first night of artillery as the moment the "jokes are over" idea first occurs to a newcomer, followed immediately by the fact that the jokes did not stop. Berglund's own poem about a friend, read aloud by the interviewer (context consulted), says of the jungle shelling that "there was laughter even then," which is the same point: humor and fear share the same hours.
*Mechanism:* humor is the thing a unit hands to a new man and takes back from a newcomer who gets the note wrong.
*Candidate application:* A character who has been out for years may feel the loss as the loss of an audience that knew what they meant.

### 3. Limits: what could not be joked about, when humor stopped, humor refused

**[E24a-15]. Seventeen passages (my coding; the harvest tags 16) show a limit, and they fall into six types.** (#10, #16, #26, #36, #38, #42, #75, #79, #91, #94, #95, #103, #107, #109, #112, #114, #136)
(a) **An authority sets or polices the line:** #36 (Dougherty), #75 (Leader), #107 (Schmidt), #26 (Burger), #112 (Sees): 5. (b) **The register changes after real exposure, or joking and death sit side by side:** #94 (Pike), #114 (Sees): 2. (c) **Laughter that misfires, wounds, or shows contempt or numbness:** #16 (Berke), #79 (MacKenzie), #103 (Robinson, restrained), #109 (Schreiber, restrained), #136 (Whitson): 5. (d) **Humor absent, or an attempt that fails:** #10 (Bache), #38 (English), #42 (Feinberg): 3. (e) **The funny-story shield fails on contact with the place:** #91 (Pape): 1. (f) **The joker pays:** #95 (Pope): 1.
Not coded as limits but belonging to the same family: #115 (humor as cover, [E24a-19]), #131 (anticipation can hurt), #49 (refusal to trade war stories, [E24a-22]), #64 and #99 (restraint requested or laughter judged alien).
*Caution:* in this harvest a "limit" passage is one in which the narrator uses the word humor, joke or laugh *while* describing what could not be laughed at, so it is a good sample of what narrators *named* as limits, not of every unspoken one.

**[E24a-16]. Authority draws the line, and the lines it draws are about morale, credibility and loss.** (#26, #36, #75, #107, #112; ROHA-674 p.4, 1594 p.14, 445 p.4, 238 p.28, 1976 p.57; context consulted for #36, #75, #112)
- *"We don't joke around here"* (Leader's first sergeant, #75): a replacement arrives in the dark among dirty faces, offers an upbeat remark, and is told men are getting killed here; Leader says everyone looked down and the aim had been to lift them. The rule is unit-wide, delivered to a stranger.
- *The defection joke* (Dougherty, Army Cold War Berlin, #36, context consulted p.14): a soldier in a brigade selected for clean records told fellow soldiers he was going to "jump over the wall," was reported, and was out of the city in a week; Dougherty says one "couldn't even joke about" such things. Dougherty notes that now "we laugh about it" but the posting was "very serious business" (the tripwire of the Free World, in his phrase).
- *The breakdown order* (Schmidt, #107): a captain tells the men to make no wisecracks about a comrade who had broken down, to greet him warmly and leave it at that; two days later the man broke again and was transferred. The order is a limit and a script at once.
- *The training outburst* (Burger, #26): a trainee leaping about roaring to let off the demeaning pressure of training was answered by a sympathetic sergeant who told the trainee to wait until a real enemy stood in front of him (the sergeant's epithet is not reproduced); Burger links the episode to the contradiction of being trained to kill a stranger "no one will ever really understand" until they are placed there.
- *The after-action rebuke* (Sees, #112, context consulted pp.56-57): a brigade colonel at a post-battle review accuses a lieutenant of leaving two friends behind; Sees answers that the narrator had eaten, slept, "joked with," and smoked with them, and was threatened with insubordination charges. The point is that shared joking is offered as proof of grief and of the right to judge the decision.
*Mechanism:* the limit is enforced upward in three ways: protect the group's morale, protect the mission's credibility, and protect a man's dignity or grief. Sees's defense in #112 is the joking itself, offered as the measure of closeness.
*Candidate application:* A veteran character may know exactly which jokes are fatal in which posting, and the list will be specific to the character's service (the Berlin soldier could not joke about the Wall; the front-line replacement could not joke at all).

**[E24a-17]. After the first real exposure, the humor goes quiet for some, and for others it keeps going; the narrators disagree.** (#84, #94, #114; ROHA-387 p.21-22, 323 p.43, 1976 p.74)
Pike (USN submarine WWII, #94): before the first war patrol the crew was "joking, like kids do"; after the first patrol, with the boat shot at and depth-charged, "they were serious guys," and Pike describes the change as a "sea change" in the whole crew. Meyers (#84) in the same war says that after the first artillery "we all joked all the time," and Berglund's account (#15) has the battery laughing under shell fire. Sees (#114) shows both at once: the officers joked over breakfast and by dinner one was dead, a newly married man who was "short" (near the end of his tour). Narrator-level differences cannot be separated from branch (a submarine) in this sample; I flag it.
*Mechanism:* *joking stopped* and *joking continued* are both in the data; neither narrator offers an explanation for why theirs differed.
*Candidate application:* A character can be someone whose humor stopped for a period and came back, or one who never changed: both appear in the record.

**[E24a-18]. Laughter that misfires: it is the laugher's position, not the topic, that makes it wrong.** (#16, #79, #103, #109, #136, plus #64 and #99; ROHA-1674 p.11, 420 p.28, 1355 p.21, 1728 p.7, 2031 p.24, 507 p.11, 311 p.20)
- *The responsible party laughs* (Berke, #16): see [E24a-12].
- *The visitor laughs at the memorial* (MacKenzie, USN WWII, #79): years later, at the memorial over a sunken battleship, Japanese tourists were "laughing and joking" while MacKenzie thought of the men below; MacKenzie says it did not seem funny, that no visible reaction followed, and that the narrator was "not happy" about it. The narrator does not accuse the tourists of disrespect; MacKenzie reports an inner reaction. (The passage tag also lists humor after service.)
- *The reserve officers laugh at a wound* (Whitson, #136, see [E24a-9]).
- *A joke on the narrator's identity* (Schreiber, USN WWII, #109): a Jewish-American sailor who spoke German with Germans in custody (the window implies prisoners) reports that when the sailor said so, some made a joke about it and others were offended, and that their sergeant was quick to strike anyone whose remark he disliked (restrained).
- *Group laughter at killings* (Robinson, Army WWII, #103, restrained): the narrator says that at the end of the war, when some Germans were slow to leave a house, American troops killed them, "everybody laughed," and the narrator adds that the men had seen "so much rottenness" by then. No function for the laughter is offered; I read it as the one passage in the set where humor is the sound of desensitization, and it is a single account.
- *Locals laugh at an injury* (Jackson, AAF WWII, #64, restrained): a dock worker's leg was run over by a train and his colleagues laughed hard; the narrator is unsure whether it was seen or only told and finds that uncertainty strange; the narrator's wording about the workers is of its period and is not reproduced.
- *A superior jokes about a life* (Prince, American Field Service WWII, #99, restrained): after an accident, the narrator's commanding officer advised what to do next time to make sure; the narrator says "he was kidding, I'm sure," but that it showed the value of life where they were, and asks that it be left out of the transcript.
*Mechanism:* the same laugh is read as friendly when it comes from an equal in a shared risk and as contempt from a third party; a narrator's own report of the discomfort (MacKenzie) is more reliable than an accusation.
*Candidate application:* A character's anger at "tourists" who laugh at a memorial or a wound is credible and consistent with several of these men; it need not be spoken.

**[E24a-19]. Humor as cover, and the cost of the cover.** (#6, #10, #49, #87, #91, #115, #130, #131; ROHA-2143 p.5-6, 336 p.54, 741 p.51, 1378 pp.37/46)
The strongest statement of cost in the harvest comes from a **non-veteran**: a niece (Shuford, #115) says her Vietnam-veteran uncle handled grief, including the loss of friends and his childlessness, by telling jokes, which she calls a "façade" over a sadness that was "too graphic" for him to share and that he "carried to his grave." Among veterans, two sentences carry the cost: Pape (Army WWII, #91) told the kids "funny stories" about camp life after the war, but on returning to Normandy bad memories shielded for 25 years came back; Vellucci (#131) says that thinking ahead can hurt a man near the end of a tour and (#130) "I still carry it with me" while describing the soldiers' joke about boring civilians. Mojo (#87) registers the delayed physical reaction an hour after the funny foxhole (legs "like jelly"). Bache (#10, interviewer's question) says that postwar college talk among veterans was "mostly traumatic stuff," not humorous. The Rutgers interviewer in the Feinberg interview voices the idea from the other side ([E24a-29]): a man who "was telling jokes the entire way" and then cried.
*Mechanism:* veterans' own comments seldom frame their humor as cover; the cover framing comes from relatives and interviewers who watched it. This is itself a finding about whose vantage is available.
*Candidate application:* A defector-type character may be written so that the people close to the character, not the character, name the humor as a mask.

### 4. Captivity and other extreme conditions

**[E24a-20]. Captivity: humor at the captor's information, weather and fallibility; humor across the wire is possible and costs the guard; liberation reverses the targets.** (#18, #43, #73, #95, #106; ROHA-712 p.30 and p.36-37, 2013 p.79, 463 p.47-48, 313 p.10, 256 p.59; context consulted for #19 and #106; #101 uncertain)
All five clear captivity passages are about Europe or a Pacific supply drop; four of the five narrators were infantry-division soldiers captured in Europe in 1944-45 (Blaher, Pope and Salerno of the 106th Infantry Division; Feinberg of the 35th, as the archive descriptors and context indicate), and the fifth (Konrad, AAF) is an airman who dropped supplies to camps.
- *Standing jokes about the captors' news:* the nightly loudspeaker claim that a long-fallen castle was "still defended" (Blaher, #18), which prisoners dismissed as propaganda; the narrator's own summary of the captivity is "boredom." Context consulted (p.36-37): at roll call in cold weather the "standing joke" was a mock complaint about the "hot sun," and a narrator's rule that you must understand the humor in the particular situation.
- *Guards' jokes that prisoners overheard:* the guards joked that the small flying bombs overhead were "the Luftwaffe" (Feinberg, #43).
- *Cross-line humor and what it cost:* a guard who had been a stage comedian before the war told the prisoners jokes at night and asked about Bob Hope; his commanding officer saw him laughing with them and sent him next day to the Eastern Front, the posting he most feared (Pope, #95).
- *A prisoners' joke from the sky:* freed or still-held prisoners in camps in Japan and China painted a cross in rocks for the supply planes and wrote beneath it that the drop should land here; Konrad, who flew the drops, keeps the photograph and ties it to "Americans with their sense of humor" (#73).
- *After liberation, a reversal:* in an American hospital in France where German prisoners worked the grounds, the staff, on seeing the state of the arriving former prisoners, cut the Germans' mattresses and meals in retaliation, and a fellow patient wearing a German cap (the narrator guesses SS) leaned from a window and addressed the working Germans in mock-German orders announcing that the Americans had come back and taken over; Salerno calls it "the joke of the whole time" there and then says the arrival of the former prisoners changed how the American army treated German prisoners (#106, context consulted p.59).
*Mechanism:* captive humor aims at what the captors control (information, weather) and at their fallibility; it cannot aim at their power. When power flips, the humor flips and runs beside retaliation.
*Caution:* all five are from Germany or the Pacific; no captive of the Korean or Vietnam wars appears, and no Japanese-held captive speaks in their own voice.
*Candidate application:* An exile or prisoner culture may keep jokes about *the broadcast* (the other side's official line) and a ritual weather complaint; a guard-captive joking relationship exists but endangers the guard.

**[E24a-21]. Standing-off postings without combat: the humor of the tripwire, the fence and the alert.** (#17, #35, #36, #124; ROHA-1674 p.24, 1594 pp.11/14, 1755 p.8; the few Cold War voices)
Only three passages come from Cold War narrators and concern a posting (#17, #35, #36), plus one fence-line passage (#124) whose archive label is Vietnam (the fourth Cold War-labeled passage, #7, is a WWII family joke). They show a different shape from combat humor. Dougherty's Berlin posting required spotless records and constant alert; the humor was real (they "joked and laughed") but bounded by career risk (#35, #36). Berke's nuclear-delivery squadron turned its briefing into a bit (#17) and, in the same interview, a scrambled-takeoff mishap into a durable "bar story" (context consulted pp.24-25; outside the window). Taylor's Marines on a perimeter with eleven rounds each made a standing joke of the arithmetic (#124, [E24a-1]).
*Candidate application:* A character from a long standoff (a border, a deterrent force) may have humor built from procedure, alert phrases and absurd arithmetic of readiness, with a sharper line around anything that sounds like defection. The record is thin: four passages.

### 5. After service: telling it, reunions, age

**[E24a-22]. The civilian-facing register is the funny version, and several narrators say so; one refuses to trade war stories at all.** (#10, #22, #49, #91, #120, #121, #133, #134; ROHA-1594 p.14, 692 p.30, 586 p.38, 336 p.54, 181 p.46, 174 p.9-10, 74 p.38, 71 p.40; 741 p.51)
- *The filter is chosen.* White (Army WWII, #133): you remember "the nice and humorous events," and that is why White is relating the funny things, preferring to remember the war years that way (the passage ends mid-thought on the narrator's luck in two bad situations). Whitestone (Army WWII, #134) says the things that come to mind are "the funny things that happened"; the interviewer replies that a lot of people think about the funny stories; Whitestone then marks a change by announcing what follows is "not a funny story." Pape (#91) told the kids "funny stories" for years. Skinner (AAF WWII, #120): among ex-GI classmates after the war they would "con each other," and talk about the nitty-gritty only after a few drinks and when someone raised a death or wound, but most of the talk was "the humorous things" that happened in the service. A veteran's relayed memory of two uncles (Smith, #121): one uncle told humorous stories about himself and little of the military part, another, a mortar-unit sergeant, said little about the war but told funny stories, and a story of a man drinking castor oil from the bottle in a machine shop is the one that survived.
- *The reflexive retort to the child's question.* Brandow (Army WWII, #22), speaking to school classes, was asked the stock question about how many Germans were killed and replied without thinking with a quip about never having killed an American; the children laughed; Brandow says it was not meant to come out that way, and found the children "good" and interested.
- *The refusal.* Galinsky (AAF WWII, #49): among ex-service friends right after the war they did not tell war stories; it was "another chapter" and not a dominant theme; Galinsky illustrates, "almost humorously," with the mock contest that war-story swapping would have become (who flew more missions, who was shot down more times), which is why it was "not the thing to do."
- *The opposite report.* Bache (#10): asked whether postwar student veterans' talk was about humorous incidents or trauma, says it was "mostly traumatic stuff" and that no humorous ones come to mind (see [E24a-29]: the same narrator told a gallows story in the course of the narration at p.27; no interviewer turn appears in that window).
*Mechanism:* the funny version is chosen for audiences who ask (children, classmates, an archive interviewer), and the gap between the funny and unfunny versions is announced by the narrator ("not a funny story").
*Candidate application:* A character can have a polished, cheerful set of "war stories" for outsiders, a second set for other veterans, and one that stays untold; narrators sometimes announce the switch in words.

**[E24a-23]. Reunions, age, and the former enemy: the humor persists, changes target, and sometimes gets re-read from the other side.** (#19, #72, #79, #87, #100, #127, #137; ROHA-712 p.36 and p.37, 465 p.21, 420 p.28, 382 p.33-34, 310 p.28, 2068 p.7-8, 2031 p.56-57; context consulted for #19 and for the Berke item)
- *Cohort institutions:* Prout (#100): a division's pun on a fortress it faced for three months was later printed on unofficial membership cards, "a fun thing over the years." Blaher (#19; context consulted pp.36-37) went to the division's reunions for two years and then "couldn't do it anymore," but friends still get together to talk and "kid one another," and at a recent Memorial Day parade teased Blaher for riding in a convertible with Merchant Marine veterans; a friend who would not talk about his captivity now marches, still silent. Whitson (Army Korea, #137) describes the unit's reunion association, relays a joke by a member of Native descent about historic battle sites (Whitson adds that the man has "a good sense of humor"; restrained), and reports a near rift between the young and old members.
- *Old jokes about other services persist:* Velcheck (#127): Marines "always made fun of" the other services, "and we still do."
- *Retelling:* Mojo (#87) retold the tangled-foxhole story with its other participant weeks before the interview, about half a century after.
- *Toward the former enemy:* Kolodziej (Army WWII, #72) says that men of that generation still carry grudges (some will not buy a Japanese-made car), that Kolodziej admits to an occasional joke that should not be made, and that when a German is rude to Kolodziej the urge is to say the narrator might have killed the man's grandfather; the interviewer calls it "front line humor"; the explanation offered is that you cannot hate a man long enough to kill him and then simply like him afterward. Brandow's classroom reflex is the same genre ([E24a-22]). MacKenzie (#79) reports a memorial scene as a limit rather than a joke ([E24a-18]).
- *Re-reading a funny story in old age:* Berke (context consulted, pp.24-25; outside the window) tells a takeoff mishap in which the narrator came close to crashing as a "good bar story" for years, and then says that on reaching the seventies the narrator thought about what the commander, who had launched the flight, must have seen: the end of a career or a duty to tell a family. The story is the same; the vantage moves from the pilot to the commander.
*Mechanism:* retold humor stabilizes into a repertoire (reunion cards, a story used at dinner), and old age adds the other participant's view of the same event. Postwar humor about a former enemy depends on who is present and what the narrator thinks of the impulse.
*Candidate application:* The Cancer-district defector's humor, ten to fifteen years out, may be entering the stage where the old formulas are repeated with a rueful awareness of how they sound to others.

**[E24a-24]. The soldier's own joke about boring civilians.** (#130; ROHA-1378 p.37; context consulted pp.36-38)
Vellucci (Army Vietnam) tells of an Australian special-forces sergeant major who said, of his daughter's friends, that he and the American would "bore" them to death with war stories once the war was over, and who grumbled the same line in the field after any rough incident. Vellucci says soldiers have a sense of humor about this because they know people do not want to hear war stories, or not all the time, and the line is used in the interview itself, with a joke that the narrator will come back and "bore" the interviewers some more after a break, adding "I still carry it with me." The formula keeps the stories while pre-empting the listener's boredom.
*Mechanism:* a mock self-deprecation that names the audience's likely reaction and so licenses telling.
*Candidate application:* A veteran in company that does not share that past can use the same device to tell the story: announce that it is boring.

### 6. Inherited formulas, women's and relayed voices

**[E24a-25]. A large part of the repertoire is inherited and passed down by seniority.** (#9, #11, #18, #31, #70, #90, #108, #111, #117, #124, #140, #7; ROHA-741 p.27, 735 p.7, 712 p.30, 655 p.24-25, 467 p.36, 345 p.27, 1727 p.26-27, 1976 p.32, 199 p.30, 1755 p.8, 58 p.40, 366 p.8)
Eight passages label a joke as standing, standard, shared or family property ("the standing joke," #18 and #124; "the standard joke," #90 and #140; "the joke on board ship," #70; "the joke was," #111; "another joke in the family," #7; "the joke of the whole time," #106), and a ninth recalls one the narrator already knew (#117). Others hand down lore: a trainee class learns the LST acronyms from senior classes (#108); a WWI veteran gives a draftee a maxim at the farewell party (#11); a rifle-range term turns up in a phone call (#9); a USO comedian trades in an acronym joke (#31). Schreiber (#108) adds that the joke's premise, that nobody who rode an LST ever came back to say so, coincided with the real fact that the ships were treated as expendable and that trainees never met a veteran of one.
*Mechanism:* the shared repertoire is learned in training, from seniors and from family, and the narrators treat it as shared property rather than as personal wit.
*Candidate application:* A character's stock of jokes can be dated and sourced (who gave it to the character, in which posting), which also makes it a way to place the character.

**[E24a-26]. Women's and relayed voices: thin, and not to be generalized.** (#7, #37, #51, #52, #56, #115, #121; ROHA-572 pp.31/65, 1525 p.12, 548 p.15, 2143 p.5-6, 174 p.9-10)
Two narrators in women's services supply three passages: a boot-camp joke on whether to kiss or salute an officer-husband (Godfrey, #51, who adds "you believe all that stuff"); her first airplane ride, sickened by a pilot's slow rolls that she suspects was "some kind of bad joke," and the lesson that you clean your own cockpit (Godfrey, #52); and a ward observation of casted Marines joking (Drelich, #37). A third passage is about a young woman trainee teased with a false story of an aircraft horn (#56). Relayed or non-veteran voices: a niece on an uncle's joking façade (#115), a veteran on two uncles' funny stories (#121), and a brother's joke told in a family (#7). These are three passages from women out of 140, so nothing about gender can be inferred beyond the fact that hazing-by-prank and rank-jokes appear in the women's accounts too.
*Cross-use:* The relayed passages show how a family or outsider *describes* a veteran's humor; the veteran's own account is a separate document.

### 8. The interviewer's role

**[E24a-29]. The interviewer asked about humor in only a handful of places; most humor was volunteered or came out of questions about something else, and the interviewers' schemas show.** (#7, #10, #12, #14, #18, #22, #71, #72, #76, #79, #97, #98, #131, #134; ROHA-366 p.8, 741 p.51, 729 p.14-15, 1365 p.21-22, 712 p.30, 465 pp.13/21, 1664 p.24, 46 p.41, 1378 p.46, 71 p.40; context consulted: Kolodziej p.13, Feinberg pp.96-97, Mickett p.45; a script count over the 40 full transcripts)
*How often:* of the 140 windows, 40 include a turn by a second labeled speaker (counted by script; most are follow-up questions). Humor-directed prompts are visible in **five** of them: Bache (#10, asked whether postwar veterans' talk "centered around humorous incidents" or around trauma); Baumgarten (#12, asked "was there any nicknaming going on" among the submarine crew); Kolodziej (#71, the interviewer calls "battlefield humor" "another question we have" and, in the context consulted, refers to a list of questions another team member has); Kolodziej again (#72, the interviewer names "front line humor"); Pressman (#98, asked whether there was a call sign or nickname). A script search of the interviewer turns in the 40 full transcripts (about 1.15 million words) found 16 turns containing humor-related words, of which about five are genuine prompts about humor or nicknames; the rest are incidental ("you are kidding" as surprise; "funny" meaning odd). So the great majority of these passages are *not* interviewer-prompted.
*What the prompts produced:* 
- **A binary question and a stock answer.** The Bache question (#10) set humorous incidents against trauma; the answer was that no humorous ones were remembered, "mostly traumatic stuff." The question was about postwar student-veteran talk, not wartime, and the same narrator had earlier told a gallows story in the course of the narration (#9, p.27; no interviewer turn appears in that window), so the answer reflects the frame more than the narrator's lack of humor.
- **A nickname question answered with a prank.** Baumgarten (#12) was asked about nicknaming and answered with a crew scrubbing a man who would not wash and the start of a remark about what became of commanders promoted past a certain rank (the window cuts off).
- **A protocol question answered with an inventory.** Kolodziej (#71) had just told a humor story (the group-composed reply to a "Dear John" letter) before the interviewer asked about "battlefield humor"; the answer to the question itself was an inventory of amusements: improvised music, dice games, sharing mail. The interviewer's own label in #72 ("front line humor") is the one that draws the explanation about hating enough to kill.
- **A call-sign question answered with an identity story.** Pressman (#98) explained three call signs, one given because of the narrator's religion in a unit where there were few.
*Questions about something else that produced humor (five passages):* the family's reaction to a brother's service (#7), a boot camp's "worst experience" (#14: the narrator reframed it as "awful humor things"), a question citing other prisoners' memory of propaganda (#18), whether the narrator felt they would get through the tour (#97: "I kidded with the guys"), and how the narrator counted down the last days (#131). Narrators reach for humor to answer questions about fear, the worst thing, and waiting.
*What interviewers' follow-ups show about their schema:* after Leader's account of roaring with laughter after a strafing run the next question is "was anyone killed" (#76); after MacKenzie's reaction to laughing tourists the next is what the narrator thought of the Japanese (#79); after Whitestone says funny things are what comes to mind, the interviewer remarks that many people do (#134). In the Feinberg interview (context consulted, pp.96-97) the interviewer (KP) tells the narrator about another interviewee who "was telling jokes the entire way" and then cried, adding that the interviewer was struck that the man "has not processed it fully," and asks Feinberg whether that "professional" composure had always been the case; in the Mickett interview (context consulted, p.45) a second interviewer asks "You had a good laugh there?" and gets "Not there, but, afterwards." The first is the interviewer's own theory that jokes are a covering of unprocessed trauma; the second is a short prompt that elicits the time-lag rule ([E24a-12]).
*Mechanism:* interviewers treat humor as an access point and a possible mask; narrators, when prompted, tend to give categories (music, dice, a nickname) rather than jokes.
*Candidate application:* A civilian character asking a veteran whether it was funny gets a stock answer, and the better material comes from questions about something else (fear, the worst time, the last days).

**[E24a-30]. Narrators manage the recording: signposting, asking for omissions, apologizing for the funny version.** (#58, #99, #130, #134; ROHA-1547 p.27, 311 p.20, 1378 p.37, 71 p.40; context consulted: Sees p.69)
Hance (#58) says he is giving "the funny side of the war" and then, as the interviewer's next words in the window turn to a concentration camp visited, the narrator is led straight to the other side (restrained). Prince (#99) tells of a superior's grim remark about how to be sure of a death and asks that it be left out of the transcript in case the man concerned is still alive, followed by laughter. Whitestone (#134) marks the register change aloud. Vellucci (#130) pre-empts boredom with a standing line. Sees (context consulted p.69) says the aim is to relate war stories with "nothing to do with the battles." Kolodziej (#72) mentions that the narrator's wife will confirm the narrator makes jokes that should not be made.
*Mechanism:* the narrators are aware of an audience of historians and students, and some tailor the humor to it; a few ask to withhold lines.
*Candidate application:* A character recounting service to a stranger or a recorder may foreground the mild and ask that something stay off the record, and the request is itself characterizing.

## Pattern roll-up

*Everything below is what these 140 keyword-selected passages from U.S. veterans establish; none of it is a prevalence claim about veterans.*

**Functions (narrators' own terms).**
- Relief of tension and keeping spirits up; "whistling in a cemetery" is the one image offered.
- A way through when there is no exit date (the infantry versus rotated air crews).
- A reflex of being alive after a near miss (laugh, then the legs go).
- Acting calm for the others, and knowing it is acting.
- Membership: the new man's reflex mocked and accepted; the prank that tests; nicknames that mark acceptance.
- An imagined later laugh ("twenty years from now") that makes a bad present bearable.
- A countdown ritual where there is a fixed tour (short-timer taunts, the paper ladder).
- Cost: noted mainly by outsiders (a niece, an interviewer) and by two veterans in a sentence; veterans seldom call their own humor a mask.

**Targets.** Danger and one's own odds (42/140); command, rank and the institution (37); the enemy as fool, propaganda or reversal (21); peers and newcomers (20); the wounded and dead (17; the injured own the license); oneself (10); other services and allies (8); medals (4); women, sex and mail (about 6); conditions (6). Nearly absent: food; the named dead as butt.

**Forms and mechanics.** One-line reversals of official or hopeful frames; borrowed standard jokes ("the standing joke," "the standard joke"; nine passages); nicknames as verdicts; fake-errand and initiation pranks (about ten of 23); pranks aimed up at officers (six), judged by the target's reaction; slapstick under fire told as the reunion story; ribald songs, cartoons and unit papers as licensed outlets; mock rituals (countdown, mail-call, nightly mimicry); the retort to a civilian's question.

**Rules of license.** Who is injured, who shares the risk, who laughs; the target's capacity to take it (the officer who "had a sense of humor"); proximity and time (funny when it is not happening to you; "not there, but, afterwards"); the newcomer's position; authority's veto in a few postings (a front-line unit, a Cold War brigade at the Wall, a captain's order about a breakdown).

**Limits and failure modes.** Authority bans (5); the register changes after first exposure for some men and not for others; laughter that misfires from a third party, a responsible party, a visitor (5); absence or failed attempts (3); the funny-story shield cracking on a return to the place (1); the joker punished (a captor guard); an outsider's reading of humor as a façade over grief.

**Time course.** In combat: laugh-then-shake. After: "we laugh about it now." At reunions: stable repertoire, cards, teasing, a rift between younger and older members. Old age: stories re-read from the other participant's vantage (Berke); old grudges and a joke the speaker knows should not be made (Kolodziej).

**Civilian-facing register.** The funny version chosen for audiences who ask (children, classmates, an archive); the retort to the stock question; the formula of boring people with war stories that licenses telling; announcing a register change ("this is not a funny story"); asking for an omission.

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics.** None directly (no passage is about leaving or opposing a service). Analogues: captive humor aimed at the captor's information and weather; humor across the wire with a cost to the guard; the reversal after liberation; a banned joke about leaving a posting (the Wall); contempt for institutional mismatch ("no excursions" read in the place; cadre selection quip). The record has nothing on humor *without* a unit.

**Cross-cultural.** U.S. narrators only; an Australian sergeant major's formula (relayed), a German guard who had been a comedian, ally banter (British, Turkish), an enemy-navy joke and a prisoner's mirror remark are all seen through American eyes.

**Craft observations.** Narrators compare their experience to Mauldin, Pyle, the Sad Sack, M*A*S*H, *Platoon* and a novel, and use them as templates or foils; interviewers read humor as an access point or mask; a binary question (humor versus trauma) shapes the answer; the better material comes from questions about something else (fear, the worst time, the last days).

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Keyword harvest.** Every passage was selected because a humor-related word appears near military content. The sample overrepresents people who talk about humor with those words and omits wordless joking, jokes told without a framing word, and people who did not speak of it. The log's own numbers (81 of 107 narrators with one passage) show how thin the per-person record is. Proportions in my tallies describe this harvest, not veterans.
2. **The wars and branches are lopsided** (94 WWII; 12 Korea; 29 Vietnam-era, 12 of them from three men; 4 Cold War). Korea and Cold War are too few to compare; no Gulf War, Iraq or Afghanistan; no captive of Korea, Vietnam or the Japanese speaks in their own voice; no narrator identified by race or ethnicity; only two women narrators; officers and enlisted not coded.
3. **Windows are truncated.** Several passages end before a punchline or outcome (#110, #135, #139) or begin mid-sentence; the harvest tags are the harvester's, not the narrators'. My coding is a second set of judgments; I expect disagreement on about a dozen passages.
4. **Descriptor mismatches:** #7 (WWII family joke under a Cold War label), #124 (a Cuba-fence passage under a Vietnam label), #136 (pre-service scene), #121 (a relative's story, older war), #115 (non-veteran). I coded setting from content; the archive's labels would give slightly different counts.
5. **Uncertain readings I did not resolve:** whether #93's Japanese counterparts were prisoners or colleagues; whether #101's group was a prison-camp group; who exactly "he" is in #44 (the platoon guide, as the context reads); the Native soldier's two battle names in #137 (the passage does not say what they were naming); Irwin's Purple Heart exchange (#62), which I read as dry irony; whether Berke's #17 setting is a bombing range as I read it. These are flagged where used.
6. **Confounds:** time since service (median interview year 2001 for WWII, 2010 for Vietnam-era) and narrator concentration; branch and setting; the harvest's own query words ("nickname," "short timer," "prank").
7. **Context consulted** went beyond the harvested windows in a dozen places; those uses are flagged "(context consulted)" and I did not read whole transcripts. Items that the passage alone could not support are marked.
8. **Rights.** Quotations are a few words each and were checked against the text I read; the archive requires permission for longer use; no passage is reproduced.
9. **Content handling.** Several items are summarized in restrained terms (register below); details a later pass may want to recover are in the register with line numbers.
10. **Unread material:** the passages file was read in full (958 lines). Only parts of 13 full transcripts were read; the other 27 local transcripts were not consulted except through the scripted count of interviewer turns, which is not a reading. The 196 Rutgers transcripts not downloaded (log §8) and the 29 unmatched roster names are open.
