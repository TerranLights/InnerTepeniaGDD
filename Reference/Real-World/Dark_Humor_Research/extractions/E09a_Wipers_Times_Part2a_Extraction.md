## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What my range holds.** (1) The second half of **Somme-Times No. 1** (printed date 31 July 1916): the rest of the Teech Bomas dispatch, a society-column, trench dialogues, a serial chapter, contests and ads (L12481-13137). (2) **B.E.F. Times**, the successor title ("with which are incorporated The Wipers Times, The 'New Church' Times, The Kemmel Times & The Somme-Times"), **Vol. 1 No. 1 (Friday 1 December 1916)**, **No. 2 (the "Grand Xmas Double Number," Monday 25 December 1916)**, **No. 3 (Saturday 20 January 1917; the editorial is dated 1 January)** and **No. 4 (Monday 5 March 1917)**, the last of which runs on past my line limit. The weekday and date pairings of the four B.E.F. issues are internally consistent (1 Dec 1916 was a Friday; 25 Dec a Monday; 20 Jan 1917 a Saturday; 5 Mar a Monday), unlike some earlier datelines (E08 Gaps). The imprint on the ads is a pun: "Printed and Published by Sherwood, Forester & Co., Ltd., 'Sommewhere' in France." There is a **four-month gap** in the paper between 31 July and 1 December 1916 (the Somme battle); the editors account for it in E09a-8.

**Evidence base.** The same division's officer-written paper as E08 (the text names the "24th Division" and, in verse, the "Seventy-third" brigade). Contributors still sign with pen names (Teech Bomas, Cockles Tumley, Aunt Annie, Violet, Pioneer, P.B.I., One & All, Kurly Kertin, Impecunious, One Spot) or initials (C.L.P., J.H.W., T.G.). Gilbert Frankau, a named poet who had left the division, contributes from Italy (E09a-56). One long piece comes from the Indian Army in Mesopotamia (E09a-45), and the gunners answer an attack on them in verse (E09a-60). The editors say copy is scarce in almost every editorial; many items quote newspapers, which makes this stretch more reactive than E08's.

**What it does NOT contain.** No images (cartoons survive only as captions or not at all). No private soldier's own writing; the ranks' voice is officer-written dialect. **No named dead are joked about, and the Somme casualties are never named**; the paper registers them only as "many cheery faces are missing" (E09a-8). No mention of shell shock, desertion or executions. Readership evidence is only the editors' remarks. Whether the censor actually struck anything is not shown; there are three visible censor markers (E09a-21, E09a-34, E09a-45) and one stated change of title "for reasons over which we have no control" (E09a-8).

**OCR and format damage (honest report).** Column-fragmented text; scrambled ads; numerals, names and parts of titles unreliable (several poem titles, prices, grid references and the last line of some verses). I cite **issue and line numbers ("L")** of the text file; there are no page markers. **Quotations** are exact except that (a) single-character OCR slips are repaired silently where certain (e.g. "Enrope"), (b) larger repairs are in [square brackets], (c) British spellings are converted to American per project rule, in quotations too (so that humor, color, neighborhood, flavor, mustache, siphon, gray, pajamas, airplane, installment, realize, organized, favorite, "check" for the bank draft and "net" appear in American form), and (d) hyphen-syllabified baby-talk in Aunt Annie's column is kept as printed because the device is the point. **Glosses** of real persons and things the paper parodies (Northcliffe's press, Bottomley's *John Bull*, "Sabretache," "Eve," Hindenburg, Bethmann-Hollweg, Enver, General Maude, the German peace note of December 1916, "Minnie" as a trench-mortar) are mine unless the text says so.

**Content handling.** Items involving gore, enemy dead or sexual innuendo are described in restrained terms; two items (a soup-kitchen letter that treats enemy dead as an ingredient; Mesopotamian verses about plunder and local women) are summarized, not quoted.

**Retrospective vs. period.** Everything here is period text (1916-17), written by the people it describes, within weeks of the events it touches (Somme, tank debut, Verdun, the German peace note, Kut, the Hindenburg retreat). It is the opposite of E08's 1930 prefaces.

## Findings

### A. Somme-Times No. 1 (printed 31 July 1916), second half (L12481-13137)

**[E09a-1]. Teech Bomas, the war correspondent "in the middle of the battlefield."** (PW-01, Somme-Times No. 1, L12481-12562; dateline "No Man's Land," date partly legible as 20/7/16)
The dispatch begins E08-78. The correspondent writes: "There are a lot of bullets but I don't mind that. Also the air is thick with shells. That also I don't mind." The text then tells of "the gallant dash of the Umpshires" into "the pick of the Prussian Guard," the writer climbing up (the object is garbled in OCR) to join "two A.S.C.'s" (supply-corps men) who "were also watching the proceedings," the "submarine co-operating with the cavalry," and peasants "leaving their happy farms in No Man's Land" with mongrel dogs in little carts. The punch lines are pseudo-profound tautologies: "Taking a place is one thing but putting it back is another. Profound but true, and so the wood was won." Mechanism: parody of the thrilled, vague, safe-seeming correspondent of the home press (compare E08-22, E08-70), built from impossibilities and empty gravity. Target: the press, not the troops it describes; the real "gallant dash" is invoked by a county nickname, not mocked.
*Candidate application:* The one kind of "war writing" this unit mocks without reserve is the glib second-hand account; a veteran character could be warm about soldiers and savage about whoever narrates them.

**[E09a-2]. Three small turns: the clean shirt, the officer who does not know his own men, and the Kaiser's mistake.** (L12564-12583, L12725-12764)
(a) A Staff-style superior asks a man in shirtsleeves why he wears a cardigan on a hot day: "To keep my shirt clean, sir!" (b) A "Company Commander" quizzes a man in a trench about a low-flying aircraft, mistaking him for a Lewis gunner; the soldier's flat literal answers show he is the signalling corporal of the howitzer battery covering the trench, and the officer collapses ("(Collapse of K.C.C.)"); the sketch closes on E08's formula, "The war proceeds," signed P.B.I. (c) A rhyme: "The Kaiser once said at Peronne / That the Army we'd got was 'no bon,' / But between you and me / He didn't 'compris' / The size of the job he had on." Mechanism: retort that answers the stated question and exposes the questioner; the senior's ignorance of his own people; the enemy's early contempt turned back with soldiers' pidgin French.
*Candidate application:* The "literal subordinate" turn is a classic low-risk way to criticize an officer in front of the officer; the enemy joke is the confident boast that history reversed.

**[E09a-3]. The Somme-Times fashion column: a dance in the rear while "all leave" is stopped, and the question "what did you do in the Great War?" turned into a pun on "Staff."** (L12593-12721)
The column's persona ("Vi") complains that a July wardrobe "would read more like November" and describes a dance near "that well-known Camp of Little Muddy Huts." Because "All leave being stopped—of course—" and "a whole army of P.M.'s" (provost marshal's military police; my gloss) were on the alert along the road to the station, "our gallant partners had to follow the time-honored custom and depart from camp by motor in the opposite and unguarded direction"; the writer adds, in a damaged line, that men enjoy anything more if it is out of bounds. A paragraph on London revues and a wind-blown skirt gag follow. The columnist then fends off collaborators and closes by imagining herself as an old lady asked "What did you do in the Great War Granny?" who will answer "I was on the Staff, dears—the Staff of the 24th Division Times." Mechanism: a society-column persona (by December "Violet," E09a-34) that reports front-line facts, here a leave stop and an evasion of the military police, as social inconveniences; the closing pun plays on a recruiting-poster slogan (my gloss) and on "Staff" in two senses.
*Candidate application:* Joking about the machinery that blocks leave in a persona that treats it as a social obstacle is a documented, deniable way to complain; the stop on leave marks the division's move into the Somme battle (E08-82).

**[E09a-4]. "From Bugler Boy to Brigadier": chapter 8 completes the promotion fantasy.** (L12767-12872)
Continues E08-66. Willie Pritchard, "Colonel in command of one of the finest infantry battalions in the British Army," sees "that all his men had had enough to eat, and had cleaned their horses" and that "the cannons had been swabbed down" (an infantry battalion given horses and cannon). Surrounded and out of food and water for four days, he is about to surrender when the band of the "Hon Tiddley-Umpshires" arrives with the Commander-in-Chief at its head; "The Commander-in-Chief promoted him Brigadier on the spot," and he is reunited with Maggie, now a Red Cross nurse; the villain Clarence, "Muttering 'Curse you,'" strides off. Mechanism: pastiche that confuses arms of service and cuts every hard step out of the rise through the ranks; promotion arrives by the chief's whim.
*Candidate application:* The ranks' idea of how a hero becomes a general (by accident, on the spot) is itself a joke about promotion.

**[E09a-5]. The rigged contest, the "Parrotidgin," and "Things We Want to Know."** (L12876-12992)
The "Mammoth Competition" is a limerick last-line contest whose first two prizes "have again been won by the Editor and Sub. Editor respectively"; the winning lines are "I'M SUCH AN INVETERATE JOKER" and "I'VE FALLEN IN LOVE WITH A STOKER," and the notice ends "As usual no other efforts were worthy of consideration, so we were compelled to return the balance of the prize money to our bank." A letter from "Grandpa and Nickett" announces "a recent discovery of ours ... by judicious inter-breeding," the "Parrotidgin," a cross of parrot and pigeon that "can deliver its message by word of mouth," useful to the War Office. The gossip column asks about the "brunette infantry officer who succumbed to measles of the 'tum variety" and whether "most of the Division goes to the BIG Town to see the CATHEDRAL." Mechanism: rigged-contest gag (E08-54), mock science that mocks the failure of message-carrying, and gossip by role. Target: the paper itself, communications, and the rear town.
*Candidate application:* A unit can joke about its own communication failures through fictional inventions, which keeps the joke on the system.

**[E09a-6]. "Are you a victim to optimism?": the paper's sharpest line on leadership.** (L13005-13051)
A medical-quackery ad: "DO YOU SUFFER FROM CHEERFULNESS?" "DO YOU WAKE UP IN A MORNING FEELING THAT ALL IS GOING WELL FOR THE ALLIES?" "DO YOU SOMETIMES THINK THAT THE WAR WILL END WITHIN THE NEXT TWELVE MONTHS?" "DO YOU BELIEVE GOOD NEWS IN PREFERENCE TO BAD?" and the last: "DO YOU CONSIDER OUR LEADERS ARE COMPETENT TO CONDUCT THE WAR TO A SUCCESSFUL ISSUE?" A "yes" to any one means "IN THE CLUTCHES OF THAT DREAD DISEASE. WE CAN CURE YOU. TWO DAYS SPENT AT OUR ESTABLISHMENT WILL EFFECTUALLY ERADICATE ALL TRACES OF IT FROM YOUR SYSTEM." The firm is "Walthorpe, Foxley, Nelmes and Co.," telephone "GRUMBLESTONES," telegrams "GROUSE." Mechanism: inversion; optimism is the pathology and grumbling the cure, so that the questionnaire is a list of the official line, which the paper treats as symptoms. It is the most direct statement in my range about the high command's competence, placed in a medical ad as the division enters the Somme battle. Contrast the army's later label for the paper, "cheerfulness" (E08-1), and the editors' own "hysterical" (E08-3).
*Candidate application:* Grousing as an institutionalized, even prescribed behavior; a character may treat optimism from above as the suspect symptom.

**[E09a-7]. Betting-shop and tailor ads: "All in, Run or not."** (L13057-13131)
Nunthorpe, Cox & Co. (E08-76) are "opening their book on the Summer Meeting": the "BAPAUME STAKES," "All in, Run or not," "We Always Pay!!!"; in the "Midsummer Handicap," "ATKINS and ANZAC" are the heavy favorites and "THE FLYING HUN" a long shot; the rhyme reads "Do not trust financial crocks, / Put it on with Nunthorpe, Cox." A tailor's notice (Mr. Howfield, who "DISSOLVED PARTNERSHIP" with Mr. Caulett "owing to a little difference of opinion," E08 repeated ad) offers "The same financial latitude" as before and "If you're going o'er the bags, / Come and see our new glad rags." Mechanism: the objective of the offensive (Bapaume) as the prize in a horse race; "o'er the bags" is the soldier's phrase for going over the parapet.
*Candidate application:* Cast the battle as the sporting calendar and the odds as the soldiers' own confidence; "all in, run or not" is the bookmaker's phrase about the stake being lost whether or not the horse starts.

### B. B.E.F. Times Vol. 1 No. 1 (Friday 1 December 1916) (L13138-14395)

**[E09a-8]. The restart: a four-month silence, a title changed "for reasons over which we have no control," and the first note of missing faces.** (PW-01, B.E.F. Times No. 1, editorial, L13255-13383)
The editors write that at one time "it seemed that our tenth number would also be our last, as the press was marooned in the midst of a disturbance which is taking place down South" (the Somme-Times was the tenth number). The outfit is "once more safely housed," in "new premises, although drafty," in a "quieter situation where the street calls and other noises are not so persistent." Then: "Many cheery faces are missing from the Division; and it seems we must get a new lot of contributors." And: "For reasons over which we have no control we are compelled to alter the title of our journal, and so we now appear under the all-embracing name of 'The B.E.F. Times.'" They beg every reader to "send along every incident which might be adapted, humorous or otherwise." This is the **period statement** behind the 1918 preface's later gloss that the title change was "not unconnected with the censor" (E08-3); here the cause is left unnamed. The same editorial recalls "the dear dead days when we fondly imagined war to be other than wallowing in a dirty ditch," jokes that Napoleon's field-marshal's baton in every knapsack is now "a tin of bully in a sandbag" (a real baton would have to be dumped "to make room for another pair of socks or you'd be over 35 lbs"), and announces a "special correspondent, who has just spent two years in a German front line trench disguised as a Hun," who "had breakfast with the Crown Prince" (E09a-13).
*Candidate application:* The Somme's dead and wounded surface only as a single dry clause ("cheery faces are missing"), while the paper continues its bits; the same editorial register can hold a bereavement in a subordinate clause.

**[E09a-9]. The No. 1 bills and ads: the tank revue, the railway to the battlefields, and winter kit as haute couture.** (L13158-13246)
"GUILLEMONT HIPPODROME. THIS WEEK—GREAT REVUE. HULLO TANKO," "BOSCH BEAUTY CHORUS," and a curtain-raiser "IT'S A WAY THEY HAVE IN THE ARMY"; "'No Treating' Order Absolutely Ignored, and We still have a Promenade" (the wartime ban on buying rounds, my gloss). The "L.B.M.L. Railway" announces "Our new Branch Offices are now Open. We can take you over the Battlefields of Europe. TOURISTS SPECIALLY CATERED FOR," with "Books of Tickets at Greatly Reduced Prices" and "TWO EXPRESSES DAILY." The winter-kit ad: "The very latest things in Trench Coats with a flounced 'Moiré' lining and dainty motifs. / Send at once or you'll be late, / A soldier must be up-to-date." Mechanism: civilian-genre substitution (E08-5, E08-18), now with a revue named for the new weapon (the tank first fought in September 1916), the battlefield as tourist route, and kit sold in fashion language.
*Candidate application:* The new weapon becomes show business within weeks; the tourist-after-the-war joke already appears in 1916.

**[E09a-10]. The "return" verse: Staff paperwork in light-opera meter.** (L13392-13471)
"'O.C. Companies will render a return,' / 'Tis imperative that Q. should quickly learn, / If you're short a tin of bully / Please investigate it fully." Further stanzas put every item on one level: "Is your Company addicted to trench feet? / Do you save the bones from out your daily meat? / Have you men who can hew lumber / If so kindly send the number, / Is your S.A.A. affected by the heat?" and "Why was plum and apple jam / Substituted for the ham?" The last verse imagines the commander's "careworn smile" and his sleeping murmur: "Have I got a man from Burma, / Or a sergeant who can go and dam the Nile?" A companion verse, "Tommy in France," renders the soldier's pidgin ("Come along Bill to the old 'staminet,' / Though the beer may be rotten it still is a 'wet,' ... What's that old lady? Oh! damn it, 'Napoo'"). Mechanism: the Staff form-letter as a patter song; trench foot sits in a list with jam and bones, so that a medical casualty is flattened into a return.
*Candidate application:* A veteran character who jokes in the grammar of forms ("please render a return") is using documented institutional language as cover.

**[E09a-11]. "Fragments from France": the German who used to be a waiter.** (L13477-13568)
A mock-biblical narrator ("And it came to pass ... having partaken of a frugal meal of chlorinated tea and Tickler") looks over the parapet of "WHISKEY CRATER" and sees "Ginger Fritz of Doodles," once a restaurant waiter, in field gray. The German levels his musket; the narrator shouts, "'Waiter! A brandy and soda! A bun for the lady friend!'" and at the familiar voice the man's "lethal weapon clattered to the ground, and with leaps and bounds he answered the call." Led down the communication trench, the prisoner is a source of thought about "my week's leave ... Doodles of course would be one of my first calls!" until the narrator remembers an Army Order and "I can't go on leave for at least another four months." (The title echoes Bairnsfather's cartoon series, PW-04; my gloss.) Mechanism: the enemy as a person with a peacetime job and a trained reflex; the happy ending is a rule that blocks leave. Compare E08-50 ("fed up and miserable" prisoners).
*Candidate application:* The enemy soldier as a former waiter or shopkeeper is a humanizing joke; the frustrating rule at the end is the real target.

**[E09a-12]. "Virtue": the morality battalion for men on leave.** (L13569-13624)
Verse: "There is shortly going to be / A morality O.C., / Who will see that vice does not spoil your digestion," with a headquarters near Leicester Square, perhaps staffed with "a Bishop or M.P." The man on leave at Victoria will be seized by "a gent," taken to headquarters and "provided with an aged chaperone"; he will dine at Lyons or an A.B.C., be allowed "one cigarette," "Hand your cash in charge, and then / Off to bed as clock strikes ten, / Feeling that in former days you were a beast." The last stanza predicts the man will find, "when leave's expired," that his fancies "will have tired / For the glass that sparkles, and for lips of coral." Mechanism: the home-front purity campaign aimed at soldiers on leave (E08-47's disgust at objectors is replaced by mockery of the moralizer); the irony is that such treatment would send the men back to the line as "reformed."
*Candidate application:* Leave in a capital where the civilian authorities treat the soldier as a delinquent is a standing grievance; the humor is a pre-emptive parody of the chaperone.

**[E09a-13]. "My Impressions" by the man who breakfasted with the Crown Prince, and a Stop Press column.** (L13629-13760)
The correspondent, "perfectly disguised as a Bond Street Art Dealer," wins an invitation by admiring the Prince's "valuable collection of 'Objets de vertu'" (including a painting bought "from a poor Belgian woman"). The Prince, "this spoilt favorite of fortune," says "Verdun be damned," complains his speech to the troops about "places in the sun" got no "hoch," reports that "Tirpy and Falky" (Tirpitz, Falkenhayn; my gloss) have annoyed the Emperor, and on the military situation says: "'Tophole'... 'If it lasts five or six years longer I shall have one of the best collections in the world.'" The Stop Press: a "great robbery" at the "Munque Art Galleries" with suspicion on "William, jun., the Potsdam pincher"; Generals "von Sauerkraut" and a "Hauptmann von Godtzer" want "to pair for the winter"; "a traverse has been sunk ... It is feared that this is likely to be a total loss. Submarines are suspected"; and "Miss Minnie Werfer arrived at the front, but went off suddenly" (a mortar-bomb pun; E08-83). Mechanism: the enemy leader as a looting dilettante who wants the war to go on because it fills his collection; the Munque Gallery gag (E08 and Dec Things We Want to Know) is now a robbery.
*Candidate application:* The enemy's heir as a tourist-collector who profits from the war is the soldier's envy turned into mockery of the enemy's upper class; the same envy aimed at one's own staff appears in E09a-16 and E09a-42.

**[E09a-14]. "An Appreciation" by Pioneer: the plain voice turns angry at a civilian moralist.** (L13767-13846)
A verse that begins in lament ("Gone is the summer, and gone are the flies ... Winter again") and becomes a tribute: "So here's to the P.B.I., / Who live in a ditch that never is dry; / Who grin through discomfort and danger alike, / Go 'over the top' when a chance comes to strike; / Though they're living in Hell they are cheery and gay, / And draw as their stipend just one bob per day." It names the winter kit ("boots, gum, thigh"), the duckboard slide, the star-shell for light, "When your clothes are wet, and the rum jar's dry, / Then you want all your cheeriness, P.B.I.," and then addresses the newspaper letter-writer who "Thinks that our soldiers are drinking too much": "Leave the Tommy alone Mr. James So-and-Such. / There are vices much nearer home waiting your touch ... When you write to the 'Times' stick to 'Little Bo-Peep,' / As a subject she's really much more in your line / Than licentious soldiery, women, and wine." It ends: "So here's to the lads who can live and can die, / Backbone of the Empire, the old P.B.I." Mechanism: the register shifts from comic to earnest-tribute to direct angry address, without a punchline; the target is the armchair critic of the ration of rum. It is the first piece in this stretch to defend the soldier's **cheerfulness** in the soldier's own voice: the verse says the men "take what may come with a grouse just skin-deep" (the grumbling is superficial, the cheer the thing that holds), and puts the cost of that cheer in the lines about the dry rum jar and the "pulverized trench where the mud's knee-high"; it does not claim the men are happy. Set against the optimism ad (E09a-6), it shows the paper holding both positions: optimism from above is a sickness, cheerfulness from below is a duty to be defended from the civilian.
*Candidate application:* When a civilian moralizes about a soldier's drinking, the soldier's humor drops its play and speaks plainly; this is a place where a veteran character could abandon irony.

**[E09a-15]. The tanks: who invented them, and Teech Bomas rides one.** (L13849-13879, L14035-14094)
A note says "We notice much discussion going on in our English contemporaries re the inventorship of the tanks. We could settle the matter here and now, but modesty compels us to keep the matter secret. If they have been helpful in winning the war we are content." A prisoner couplet ends "'Ho yus,' said Tommy with a grin, 'and bloomin' well napooed.'" Teech Bomas, "passenger on one of them" in the "September morn," writes in an alliterative rhapsody: "Like great prehistoric monsters they leapt and skipped with joy when the signal came" and the machine "went through Bapaume pushing over the church in a playful moment" (premature; Bapaume was taken only in 1917; my gloss) and "came to rest in a crump-hole." Enemy losses are inflated to millions in the manner of the press (restrained paraphrase). Mechanism: the correspondent's style attached to a sensational new weapon, with the gentle ridicule of a nation arguing over who invented it while the troops pay the price; the soldier's phrase "napoo" ("finished") used as a verb.
*Candidate application:* New weapons arrive as press miracles, and the units who must use them are the ones who mock the miracle-language.

**[E09a-16]. The matrimonial column: brass advertising themselves as lonely.** (L14097-14168)
"Our Matrimonial Column" offers "the greatest discretion and secrecy." The ads: "CAPTAIN.—35, handsome and dashing appearance. Thoroughly domesticated and capable of looking after the home. Feels lost in his present position of Camp Commandant. Fond of dogs. ... Money no object as he has his pay.—Write Vatican, c/o this paper"; two Brigadier-Generals, one "Bashfulness has made him take this way of settling his future happiness"; a Lieutenant-Colonel "Gone gray through loneliness ... Widow preferred. Money no object, but would like one with small public house"; another "Just recovered from wound, feels lost now without feminine attentions." Mechanism: the classified-ad form applied to senior and rear-area officers, who are shown as sentimental, dependent and mercenary; a wound is "feeling lost." Running figures: the Camp Commandant (E09a-33, E09a-43, E09a-52) and the brass nicknames ("Vatican" is "a red-hat," E09a-52).
*Candidate application:* The ranks' view of rear and senior officers as emotionally dependent and idle, repeated across an ad format, is a standing device.

**[E09a-17]. "The War Lord and the Chancellor": the poet puts doubt into the enemy's mouth.** (L14175-14247)
A Lewis Carroll parody ("With apologies to the late Lewis Carrol[l]"): the Kaiser and the Chancellor walk hand in hand over "the devastated land" and say "If this belonged to us ... it really would be grand." The Chancellor doubts that "fifty Huns with fifty guns" would win in half a year. The Kaiser: "'You always were a pessimist,' ... 'Oh! Highest One it is because / I always look ahead; / Before this War is finished you / And I will both be dead.'" The Kaiser wails that he has vowed to win "by any means ... With Zeppelins and submarines, and waves of poison cloud"; the Chancellor replies, "'Oh! chuck it Bill ... You know quite well with "frightfulness" / We've tried them everywhere, / And got it back with interest.'" The Kaiser rages that the "Army so contemptible" shall be sent to eternity; the poem ends with the Chancellor speaking "of love and peace and food" at length and, on pausing, finding the War Lord had expired (OCR damaged; my reading). Mechanism: the enemy's doubts, exhaustion and mortality are the **soldier's own** thoughts voiced by the enemy; "pessimist" is the Kaiser's insult for the speaker who says out loud that both will be dead. Gas is named once in the poem ("waves of poison cloud") among the Kaiser's chosen means, and the Chancellor says "frightfulness" has been "tried ... everywhere" and come back on its users "with interest," the soldier's own view of reprisal.
*Candidate application:* A soldier's pessimism can be expressed more safely as an enemy's; the poem's "both will be dead" is a flat prophecy under a children's-verse meter.

**[E09a-18]. "Extracts from Contemporaries" and farewells.** (L14252-14293)
The device quotes a document and lets it stand: "From 'Corps Intelligence Summary': 'ALARMS.—Dogs have been HEARD BARKING in the trenches and also Geese. Confirmation is required as to whether the geese are in the trenches or wild geese flighting.'" A second extract, from "Bull" of 18 Nov 1916 (probably *John Bull*; my gloss), begins an anecdote of a home-based battalion "FIFTEEN THOUSAND STRONG" in Derbyshire and Yorkshire towns (the OCR breaks it off). Immediately after, in the plainest voice: "We have been asked by Lt.-Colonel Cunningham and Major Twiss to say 'Good bye' for them to the many friends they have in the Division ... in losing Lt.-Colonel Cunningham and Major Twiss, the whole Division feels it has lost two good pals, and that wherever they may go the wishes of the Division will be with them." (A Major Twiss may be the illustrator "J. O. Twiss" of E08-4; the text does not say.) Mechanism: deadpan quotation of an absurd bureaucratic query; juxtaposed with an unironic farewell notice.
*Candidate application:* An officer who leaves is bid goodbye as a "pal," not a rank; the paper's ironies stop for those it loves.

**[E09a-19]. Ads: the "Dopelet" and the Divisional Stores.** (L14296-14395)

Mechanism: drink as the universal cure for both emotional poles (compare E09a-6, where optimism alone was the disease); the "Jerry-built" pun (enemy as "Jerry" and shoddy building); the shell-proof dug-out where the shelling is a lullaby.
*Candidate application:* Alcohol as the remedy for mood extremes in a mock-medical ad, and the shellfire-as-lullaby couplet, are the documented comic voices of the rum-and-bunker economy.

### D. B.E.F. Times Vol. 1 No. 3 (Saturday 20 January 1917; editorial dated 1 January) (L16239-17955)

**[E09a-40]. The New Year editorial: "soldiering for pleasure," the whiskey scare, and the plea for copy.** (L16358-16484)
"Whatever it may have in store for us, we can at least be thankful we are not Huns." The editors say they would predict "1917—Disturbances in Europe," will leave the prediction to "Major Taude, B.C.," and note that they have "completed our first year of journalism" and are "talking learnedly of 'dis-ing' and chases, furniture," and grateful that they found "the old 'Jigger'" (the press, apparently) to fill the "spare time which we have on our hands during these pleasant winter months." On winter: "it should change places with Autumn and be called the 'Fall.' Personally we think there's a lot too much fall about it. We are often glad to be able to think we are only soldiering for pleasure. It would be terrible to think that we HAD to do it." On spring: "'In the Spring a young man's fancy lightly turns to thoughts of —' grenades, hand, Mills and any other lethal article he can lay his hands on." On politics: "President Wilson is rather undermining Mark Twain's reputation" (the peace notes, my gloss). Then the real alarm: "a terrible rumor has reached us that whiskey will shortly be 'na-poo' ... as the sub-editor crudely puts it 'No whiskey, no war.' Can anyone imagine calling at the 'Culvert Arms' and being told that whiskey was 'Off'?" and "many of the most popular dug-outs in the line had to shut their gas-curtains. However, all this and more we will beat for England's sake, but it is going to be a dry summer." The buried-check notice (E09a-36) follows and the closing request: "As our previous appeals have met with very little response we should like to make a fresh effort and remind all readers that copy is urgently required if we are to keep up to 12 pages, and beg one and all to send something along." Mechanism: conscription reframed as voluntary leisure ("It would be terrible to think that we HAD to do it"), which is the most compressed statement of the coercion theme in the paper; the drink shortage is treated as a war-ending threat ("No whiskey, no war"). The unit-level grumble about copy is now permanent.
*Candidate application:* The wry reframe "I am only here for pleasure" is a documented way to say "I cannot leave"; and a unit's morale is publicly tied to a supply.

**[E09a-41]. The epigram on falling, and a poem on the Hun who wakes the sleeper.** (L16489-16561)
An epigram on a popular song's opening line (my gloss): "Let me like a soldier fall, / Upon an open plain; / For if I trip and fall in a trench, / I could never get up again." A poem (title damaged, perhaps "Disturbing") begins in a cold dug-out and a daydream of "green fields, and peace, and lovely girls, or in my club I'm drinking, / When outside—bang—and of the war, I'm thinking, thinking, thinking," curses the Hun "who interrupts my fancies" ("No Wilson I," nor skilled in "temporisals," so "naught is left for me to do, save swift T.M. reprisals"), and aims at one enemy in particular: "I hate all Huns, yet most hate I, that surly livered blighter, / Who with persistence breaks my sleep, with his ten times-a-nighter." Resolve: "when we'll hold his requiem mass, / our Stokes shall do his knelling." Mechanism: the epigram re-sings a heroic death-wish as a complaint about mud (the trench is so deep that a man who stumbled in could never climb out); the poem's hatred is aimed at the particular enemy who interrupts sleep, not the abstract "Hun."
*Candidate application:* The specific, personal enemy (the night mortarman) is hated more than the nation; the loss of sleep is the grievance.

**[E09a-42]. "Rhymes Without Reason" by P.B.I.: the infantry strafes Gunners, Sappers and the Gilded Staff.** (L16689-16854)
The "Foreword" asks the Muse, "from the muddied trench," to "give utterance to malicious thought, / Shouting aloud the things we never ought / Even to dream of ... With tongue in cheek let us set out to strafe / Gunners and Sappers, and the Gilded Staff." **Gunners:** "Gunners are a race apart, / Hard of head and hard of heart, / Like the gods they sit and view / All that other people do ... Retaliation do you seek? / Ring them up, and—wait a week! ... Should a shell explode amiss, / Each will swear it was not his." **Sappers:** "Sappers are wonderfully clever by birth, / And though they're not meek, they inherit the Earth ... (And when I say 'work,' I should really explain— / That we find the Labor, while they find the Brain). / They build nice, deep dug-outs as quick as can be, / But quicker still mark them 'Reserved for R.E.' ... this speed of theirs seems to decline / As the scene of their labors draws near the Front Line." **Staff:** "Realizing Men must laugh, / Some Wise Man devised the Staff: / Dressed them up in little dabs / Of rich variegated tabs: / Taught them how to win the War / On A.F.Z. 354: / Let them lead the Simple Life / Far from all our vulgar strife: / Nightly gave them downy beds ... Often, often, gave them leave, / Decorations, too, galore ... Yet, alas, or so says Rumor, / He forgot a sense of Humor!" The "Afterword" describes the long walk back up "the interminable C.T., / Watched by the greater part of Germany" to "a Rabbit-hutch": "How goes the Battle? Have they Minnied much?" Mechanism: inter-arm rivalry in a sustained stanza form; the Staff exists "realizing Men must laugh," i.e., the army's own joke, and its flaw is humor; the sentence about retaliation is the infantryman's complaint that the artillery do not answer in time (see the gunners' reply, E09a-60, and the editor's note that "the gunners" fired back, E09a-54).
*Candidate application:* Each arm gets one stable grievance (gunners: unresponsive and short-shooting; sappers: shelter reserved for themselves; Staff: no sense of humor, many decorations). This is the paper's documented "map" of inter-service license, aimed at peers and one level up, never at the dead.

**[E09a-43]. "Things We Want to Know" in January, and a quoted wire about captured gold and brandy.** (L16860-16921)
"The name of the Camp Commandant who bought up all the whiskey in Bethune owing to early advice of the coming drought." "The name of the M.O. who is not a doctor." "If it is true that he takes an umbrella on his walks, as 'Archie' duds are so plentiful." "Whether two notorious red-hats were disappointed over a little question of 'Who's HE taking with him.'" "Ref. G.R.O. Q.M.G.'s Branch No. 2073:—(a)—What is a BONA-FIDE rum drinker. (b)—Whether whiskey cannot be supplied on the same terms to BONA FIDE whiskey drinkers." Then "EXTRACT FROM 'TIMES,' 15/1/17": "GERMAN GOLD CAPTURED NEAR RIGA ... the treasury chest of the 364th Infantry Regiment containing 335,000 marks (16,750 sovs.) in coin ... 10,000 bottles of brandy," followed by a two-word tag that reads "Some chest!" (OCR slightly damaged). Mechanism: the rum-and-whiskey regulations are read for the loophole a drinker would exploit ("bona fide"); the wire's list of trophies ends in brandy, which the editors, in this number, would not overlook. The Camp Commandant joke (E09a-16, E09a-33) now has a motive.
*Candidate application:* A scheme to corner the drink supply is the standard comic vice of the rear-area officer; "bona fide drinker" is the soldier's reading of a regulation.

**[E09a-44]. "Ode to Marraine" by the M.O.: the dangerous home-made cake.** (L16927-16972)
A sonnet-form poem to a "marraine" (the French wartime godmother who sends gifts): "I love you for your kindliness and grace, / And wonder how it happens, that you deign / To send me sweets and gifts, my dear MARRAINE ... However long this war goes on ... I'll adore you, to the end; / But all the Doctor's Magic cannot shake / From my inside, this unaccustomed pain, / I pray you, I implore you, not to send / Another sanguinary home-made cake." Signed "THE M.O. (C.L.P.)." (The same initials sign "The Leave Warrant," E09a-57.) Mechanism: bathos; the doctor is the victim of kindness; the sentimental form (praise of a distant woman) is undercut at the last line; "sanguinary" is a mild oath that hints at bloodier things.
*Candidate application:* A gift sent in good faith that harms the receiver is a gentle form of the home-front misunderstanding joke (E08-43).

**[E09a-45]. A "B.E.F. Alphabet" from Mesopotamia: another front's complaints in the same form, with the editors' censor marker.** (L16988-17219)
An introductory note: "The following has been sent us from the Indian Army by one of our old Divisional friends. He suggests that someone should have a shot at the 'B.E.F. Alphabet Up-to-date.' Will some please try and submit efforts early.—Ed." The verses, each ending "Mesopotamia," include: "E was the Energy shown by the Staff / Before the much-advertised Hanna strafe, / Yet the net result was the Turks had a laugh / At our Staff in Mesopotamia"; "I is the Indian Government, but / About this I'm told I must keep my mouth shut, / For it's all due to them that we failed to reach Kut- / El-Amara in Mesopotamia"; "J is the Jam, with the label that lies, / And states that in Paris it won the First Prize, / But out here we use it for catching the flies"; "N is the Navy that's tied to the shore ... How I wish I had joined as a sailor before / I came out to Mesopotamia"; "O are the Orders we get from the Corps, / Thank goodness by now we are perfectly sure / If issued at three they'll be canceled by four"; "P are the Postal officials who fail / To deliver each week more than half of our mail, / If they had their deserts they'd all be in jail"; a stanza on the rations, and one that hopes the men who supply them go "to a spot as hot and as dry / As this rotten old Mesopotamia." Two stanzas imagine plunder and the local women when a city falls (summarized only). The "W" stanza, "stands for Wonder and pain, / With which we regard the infirm and insane ... campaign / We're waging in Mesopotamia," is followed by the editors' footnote "(*CENSORED—ED.)" (the third line is damaged), and the last verse jokes: "I've tried very hard and at last I hit / On a verse which this damnable letter would fit, / But the Censor deleted it—every bit / Save the last word 'Mesopotamia.' ... Yet here we're going to be left till we rot / In the middle of Mesopotamia." Mechanism: the alphabet-rhyme (a children's form) as an inventory of grievances (heat, jam, mail, orders, the Staff, the Indian Government), one explicitly un-printable; the footnote is the editors' own, so the cut may be theirs (the text does not say that an outside censor acted), and the final stanza says that the contributor's own censor deleted everything of his closing verse except its last word.
*Candidate application:* The same grievances (orders canceled, mail lost, Staff vain, jam bad) appear in a different army, theater and climate, so they are the **generic** soldier's inventory; the one cut line (a verdict on the conduct of that campaign) tells us what the editors would not print.
*Cross-use:* a grievance alphabet is a ready template for an exile community's complaint list.

**[E09a-46]. Violet in January: a reader doubts she is a woman, and the column answers with the paper's own cross-references.** (L17229-17391)
The persona says "There is little enough to record in the world of dress just now" (January sales). Orders for her "famous feathered egg machines" arrive from subalterns who ignored "the Editor's clearly printed Instructions to Subs to leave my last Chronicle unread"; one writer suggests "Dead Cow Farm would be an ideal site for a poultry fancier"; a would-be buyer's check "In payment for one dozen hens in full lay" bounced and the persona asks "Shame on you, Reginald,—I am wondering if you are the 'Co.' of that noted firm, Nunthorpe, Cox and Co." A reader named Thomas "must be a Scotchman, he's such a cautious, wary fellow. He'd love to meet Violet if only he could be sure that she is really and truly of the fair sex, but he doesn't want to risk turning up ... to find himself face to face with a brother officer with a mistaken sense of humor." The column replies that no "mere man" could give such detailed lingerie descriptions, then turns on the matrimonial column ("Whoever has heard of a Gunner of all people, with 'An exaggerated sense of modesty'? Or of a 'Bashful' Brigadier-General?"), says "the pink garter brought in by the patrol of the X Regt. on the 27th ult. (vide Intelligence Summary) was not mine," and explains that she was wired to sort the marriage replies (the Editor feared "his susceptible subs. would have credited every applicant with the charms of Venus"). Mechanism: the persona's reality is a shared joke with an open wink about the hoax, and the paper's separate items (matrimonial ads, intelligence summary, bookmaker) now refer to each other (E09a-24, E09a-33).
*Candidate application:* By mid-1917 the paper's in-jokes are a closed world with cross-references, which both builds cohesion and excludes newcomers (a reader can ask "is she real?" and be teased).

**[E09a-47]. The enemy at council: American slang for the Central Powers, and a rhyme on the peace note.** (L16676-16683, L17394-17458)
A nursery-style rhyme: "There was a little Hun, and at war he tried his hand, / And while that Hun was winning war was fine you understand, / But when the others hit him back he shouted in alarm, / A little drop of peace wouldn't do me any harm" (a parody of the music-hall "Another little drink wouldn't do us any harm," which the earlier Somme-Times bill used, E08-81). "Germany's Secret Meeting" by "Kurly Kertin," "Special Correspondent with the Huns," Potsdam, 21 Jan 1917: disguised "as a statue in the Siegesallee," the correspondent overhears the Emperors of Germany and Austria, the Sultan and Ferdinand of Bulgaria in the slang of an American gangster film: "'Say, Pop, its up to you, and cut out the rough stuff.' 'Nix on me,' said the big guy, 'let Ferdy open up.' 'Well,' said Ferdy ... 'Its oats for mine ... dollars is good, but oats sound better to me now.' ... 'Dollars yer can have but nix on oats. If another slice of Yurrup helps you any its yours.'" The Sultan ("The coffee cooler") "offered to swop three full harems for a good square meal but found nothing doing. The meeting then broke up in disorder, and the various potentates hit the trail for home. This is the absolute inner history of the last meeting of the Central Powers." Mechanism: wishful intelligence: the enemy's allies are starving and quarreling; the Kaiser would pay in territory but not in food; slang from American films makes the potentates petty criminals. (The peace-note jokes, E09a-17, E09a-21, E09a-26, E09a-37 and this one, always put the request on the enemy's lips.)
*Candidate application:* The soldier's hopes of an enemy's collapse (shortage, disunity) are delivered as tabloid "inner history."

**[E09a-48]. "An Appeal" signed "One & All": whiskey as the source of courage, stated in the open.** (L17467-17523)
"There are various types of courage, there are many kinds of fear, / There are many brands of whiskey, there are many makes of beer, / There is also rum, which sometimes in our need can help us much, / But 'tis whiskey—whiskey—whiskey! hands the courage which is 'Dutch.' / In moments when the front is still—no hustling whizzbangs fly— / In all the world you could not find a braver man than I! / Yet on patrol in No-Man's-Land, when I may have to stalk a / Benighted Hun, in moments tense I have recourse to 'Walker.' / 'Tis Scotland's best which helps me rest, 'tis Mountain Dew which stays me / When Minnies rack my wearied soul, or blatant H.E. flays me, / 'Twas by its aid that I endured Trones Wood and such-like places. / In times of stress my truest friend accelerates my paces." It ends with a petition: "Take what you will save only this—my evening tot of whiskey, / It gives me warmth, and helps to make a soaking much less risky, / Oh! G.O.C.'s now hear our pleas respectfully presented, / Lend us your aid in this our plight, and we will be contented." Mechanism: a comic frame (the whiskey scare, E09a-40) around a plain statement that drink is how men do patrols, endure mortar fire, and get through a named wood on the Somme; "Dutch courage" ("hands the courage which is 'Dutch'") is the soldier's own term; the "pleas" to commanders is the form of the joke.
*Candidate application:* This is a documented self-statement of alcohol as an operational aid, from the people who used it, in a petition addressed to the generals; note that the poem's signature is collective ("One & All"), not a confession by a single man.

**[E09a-49]. "Aunt Annie's Corner" in January: the Gunner's spurs, the Sappers' trenches, and the staff officer who "went to live with the big boys."** (L17526-17600)
"Two of my Tot-ties are very sad." "Poor Lit-tle Gun-ner had a love-ly pair of breech-es given to him, but a nasty boy took them and hid them. When he told the other boys about it they all laugh-ed at him and said that Mother had tak-en them a-way from him ... the other boys laugh be-cause ... they have not got such nice breech-es and spurs. They are jeal-ous." "Lit-tle Rabs" (the sappers) "makes nice tren-ches, and the naught-y rain comes and wash-es them a-way ... if you keep on and work hard-er, some day you will get some-thing done. You must not take any not-ice of the big pi-on-eers who you say laugh at you." And "Lit-tle Mike Bell tells me he has gone to live in a new place with the big boys, and has a nice new hat, a red one, and a nice coat with a red col-lar. Isn't he a luck-y boy, Tot-ties? I hope he will re-mem-ber all the nice things we taught him." Mechanism: infantilizing replies to arms and ranks that echo the "Rhymes Without Reason" grievances (E09a-42); the red hat and red collar are a staff officer's marks (my gloss), so the "new place with the big boys" is a transfer to the Staff.
*Candidate application:* An officer's departure for the Staff is mocked as being taken into the "big boys'" club; the instruction to "remember the nice things we taught him" is a tiny pledge to loyalty.

**[E09a-50]. "A Plea from the Trenches": a reply to a newspaper's account of the "comfort" the chiefs have arranged.** (L17606-17675)
The paper quotes the *Daily Sketch* of 2 January 1917, "MAKING THE TRENCHES 'COMFY'": "The mothers, sisters and sweethearts of the men in the trenches will be delighted to hear of the latest instance of the solicitude of the military chiefs for their comfort and good health. Every yard of roofing felt that can be found in the United Kingdom is being commandeered for the lining of the trenches on the Western Front. It is sound policy as well as strong sentiment which is at the back of such wise provision." The reply, signed "ONE SPOT," begins: "Dear Mr. Gossip,—This is really splendid reading that you have given us. Now we shall be able to carry on with no fear of the future. The lack of lining in our trenches has been the one dark spot in our existence. How often we have sat on our well-aired beds and regretted the fact that we had no roofing felt to line our trenches." It asks the Government to "buy up all the carpet and send it over" for dug-out steps, and linoleum for the duckboards ("my pal Bill got corns on his feet"), because it would give such joy to the "Mothers, sisters and sweethearts." The letter ends: "door-mats with 'Welcome' on will come later. Do help us for we cannot help ourselves." Mechanism: mock gratitude that asks for more of the useless; the final line, "we cannot help ourselves," is the only sentence of plain dependence in the piece; the target is the comforting tone of the press toward the home front and the military chiefs' PR.
*Candidate application:* Over-gratitude is a standing soldier's weapon against official reassurance; a character who thanks a speaker so profusely that the speaker withers is using this exact device.

**[E09a-51]. The B.E.F. Gazette's honors list: mock orders for staff by job.** (L17678-17715)
Dated 1 January 1917: "We are pleased to be able to state that a large and assorted collection of orders from foreign potentates has been received and the Division comes in for a good share. They have been allotted thus:—To be High Sheik of the Order of the 'Numero Neuf.' The A.D.M.S.... To be Knight Commander of the Most Ancient Order of the 'Magdalenes.' The A.P.M.... Mechanism: the New Year honors list rewritten as jokes on each officer's job ("Numero Neuf" for the medical chief, apparently a purgative-pill brand; "Magdalenes" for the provost marshal; the engineers' expanded metal for the field company; the glosses are mine); the target is decorations "galore" given to staff (E09a-42).
*Candidate application:* Decorations are mocked in terms of the recipient's job, not the recipient.

**[E09a-52]. Correspondence, Answers to Correspondents, and the soup-kitchen ads.** (L17719-17946)
Letters: "PAX," "a brain worker to whom peace and quiet are a necessity," complains of a "lady" across the street "known as 'Minnie'" ("Can nothing be done to move, or at least to have her kept quiet?"; E08-83 again). "A 'Lover of Nature'": "As I was going over the top last week I distinctly heard the call of the cuckoo. *Candidate application:* Noise nuisance is the paper's way of saying "shelling" in a form that can be printed; the sandbag answer is the rare place the paper tells a soldier something true.

### E. B.E.F. Times Vol. 1 No. 4 (Monday 5 March 1917) (L17956-18887)

**[E09a-53]. The March bill and the "Mammoth International Sports Meeting": the offensive as athletics, Berlin as the finish.** (L17975-18048)
The "POSSK THEATER OF VARIETIES" offers "THE MAUDE TROUPE IN THEIR SCREAMING FARCE, WHAT'S THE BAG, DAD?" with "ENVER" in a song (damaged), and film plays: "COMEDY—WILLIE'S TURKEY. The "PRELIMINARY NOTICE": "MAMMOTH INTER-NATIONAL SPORTS MEETING. Under the patronage of most of the CROWNED HEADS OF EUROPE. THIS MEETING has been organized at fabulous expense to take the place of the OLYMPIC GAMES previously billed to take place in BERLIN. *Candidate application:* The unit's long game is the march to Berlin, and the date of the start is "to be notified"; the infantry's bitterness is carried in the single phrase "Open to P.B.I. only."

**[E09a-54]. The March editorial: web-feet, the gunners' reply, the Staff's silence, "the war," and "the old crowd."** (L18061-18195)
"Can you imagine the blithesome Hun sticking his head out of a dug-out and surveying with any pleasure the prospect of a dawning Spring? Winter's snowy mantle has gone, and in its place we have several feet of Belgium's best. The M.O. tells me that he has noticed on several occasions lately the development of web-feet in some of his clientele. The M.O. having some slight reputation for veracity (perhaps more traditional than actual) one must accept what he says, and wonder how it is all going to end." On the January verses: "One contributor in the last number seems to have drawn the gunners' fire, but the red-hats remain still distant and aloof, ignoring the gibes of less-effulgent people." On the War Loan: "We really must congratulate the B.E.F. on the success of the War Loan, but feel sure we can beat 800,000,000 next time, and still save a bit out of our pay." On leave: "Having lately been on leave we have had a good opportunity of learning how things are going on the Western Front. One cannot help feeling surprised at the obviously enormous amount of military genius allowed to run spare," which they blame on what they call an "unobservant and decadent Government." On turnover: "We must express the regret that is felt generally at the departure of so many of the old crowd for newer and larger fields of activity. Of course it is nice to feel that most of the stars of the Army come from the Division yet one misses their cheery faces." On copy: "we shall be able to keep up to twelve pages" and the serial's long installment is "attributable to the fact that we turned it over to a subaltern with ideas. Some ideas, you will agree ... taking a toss in an airplane. Truth is stranger than fiction." Then: "We have come to the conclusion that we shall either have to give up the war or the editorial chair. The sub editor has just remarked that if that's so we'd better give up the war as no one would notice it. That is only the bitterness of a disappointed man, and probably arises from a temporary severance of diplomatic relations with Messrs. Cox and Co." And, closing: "What a country! One writes nice things about the disappearance of winter's snowy mantle, and then it goes back on you and starts all over again." Mechanism: the editors report both retorts and non-retorts to their own jokes (the gunners answered, E09a-60; the Staff did not): a measure of who is willing to play. The sub-editor's aside is the bleakest sentence in my range: giving up the war would not be noticed, and the joke is taken back as personal bitterness, with the overdraft (E09a-55) as the excuse.
*Candidate application:* The Staff's non-response to being teased is itself a documented asymmetry; and a quip that says the war would not be missed is allowed through under a humor tag ("only the bitterness of a disappointed man").

**[E09a-55]. "If I were King," and a wounded subaltern's verse to the army bankers.** (L18202-18273)
A rhyme: "If I were King! Ah! Bill, if I were King, / I wouldn't touch an 'A' frame or a thing, / I'd watch the sergeant split his bloomin' thumb, / And, when he wasn't looking, drink his rum, / I'd make the corp'ral rations to me bring." Next, a verse "To the Kindly manager of Cox" (Cox & Co., the army's bankers, my gloss; compare the bookmaker pun on "Cox," E08-76) from a hospitalized subaltern signed "IMPECUNIOUS," R.E.A.: "I am sadly on the rocks, / For a time my warring ceases, / My patella is in pieces; / Though in Hospital I lie, / I am not about to die; / Therefore let me overdraw / Just a very little more, / If you stick to your red tape / I must go without my grape, / And my life must sadly fret / With a cheaper cigarette, / So pray be not hard upon / A poor dejected subaltern." The "ALLEGED ANSWER FROM COX'S" replies in kind: "Sir, the kindly heart of Cox / Cannot leave you on the rocks ... We will make but one request— / Keep this locked within your breast, / For if others know, they'll say, / 'Good old Cox is sure to pay, / Only take him the right way.'" Mechanism: a wound (a shattered kneecap) turned into an overdraft request, with "I am not about to die" as the reassurance; the fantasy of rank ("If I were King") is a list of petty acts of license over a sergeant and a corporal, not of power.
*Candidate application:* A wound is told as a financial and comfort problem, which avoids describing the body; and the ranks' fantasy of power is stealing a rum ration.

**[E09a-56]. Gilbert Frankau's verse from Italy: the former officer tells his old comrades that the hero is a fraud.** (L18285-18358)
The poem is headed by his name and the "Telegraphic address: Movie-King, Italy." It opens: "Here, in these sunny southern climes / (Its pelting in Milan this minute) / It reaches me—our 'Wipers Times' / And idly I peruse what's in it." The clang of Milan trams becomes "the crash of British damns, / The roar of British eighteen-pounders," and he lives "once more in savage North / Where life is wet, and weird, and risky," drinks "once more the Sherwoods' whiskey," and recalls "When sweet was ration rum, and sweet / The work we did for General Capper." In place of "German shells and brass-hats' strictures," he writes, "Armed to the teeth, I sternly face / Your war ... as shown in moving pictures!" The scene then shifts to Italy, where "Marchesine," "divinely tall, divinely dark," smile "on an English Capitano, / Who wears a sword, and spotless breeks" and spurs and "plays the hero stunt ... and shrieks / With laughter—he can scarce dissemble." The poem's turn is addressed to the old comrades: this "tried / To breaking-point of even your humor, / That never since de Rougemont died / Was hero such a perfect 'stumer'" (a "stumer" is a dud or fraud; de Rougemont was a famous teller of tall tales; my glosses). They know, he says, "who tramped the Menin Road, / And visited the Culvert nightly / That I, your bard who pens this ode, / Ne'er left his crump-proof dug-out lightly, / But slaked the thirst of martial ire, / To his own utter satisfaction, / With miles of buried D.5 wire / And chits he 'passed to you for action.'" He ends with "A very humble New Year's greeting" to those who "fight / In trenches where is no steam-heating." Mechanism (my reading from "moving pictures" and his telegraphic address: that he is involved with war films, and that the "Capitano" is himself): the poem is nostalgia for the line from a place that has none, and a self-demotion; the man now being treated as a hero, and perhaps acting one, announces to his former unit that the role is false and that his own war was telephone wire and paper (compare the "chits" of E08's staff jokes).
*Candidate application:* The ex-member's letter to the old unit is the closest thing in my range to **humor without a unit**: the civilian-facing hero is set against the unit's knowledge of who did what, the speaker mocks his own safety, and the greeting is to the people still in the trenches. "Tried to breaking-point of even your humor" implies that the unit's laughter has limits that the hero act pushes against.
*Cross-use:* a defector or exile whom civilians treat as a hero may deflate the title with the people who know better.

**[E09a-57]. "The Leave Warrant": a sonnet to a form.** (L18365-18401)
Signed "C.L.P." (the initials that sign the M.O.'s Marraine poem, E09a-44; inference: same contributor). "Week, after weary week, I work and wait / Patiently wondering, when 'twill be my lot / To find a carpet, wond'rously wrought / On mystic looms, in some enchanted state ... To bear me hence, to other lands ... But willingly indeed I would forego / This Magic Mat, for just a little bit / Of printed, primrose parchment—and to know, / That on its face my name three times was writ. / For 'tis a genie's golden key to fit / The Gate of Leave—'Chin chin, you chaps, cheer O!'" Mechanism: a fantasy of the magic carpet traded down to a printed form with one's name on it three times; leave as a magical object, the bureaucratic paper its key.
*Candidate application:* The leave warrant (a paper) is the object of fantasy in a form that is deliberately overheated; leave as the central hope (E08-52, E08-86) is now a sonnet.

**[E09a-58]. "Ours or Theirs": a trench-mortar shoot as a drawing-room farce.** (L18407-18587; signed J.H.W.)
Scene 1, Battalion H.Q.: "The Adjutant is discovered doing nothing in particular." The heavy and medium trench-mortar officers arrive; "H.T.M.O.: I'm the flying pig merchant"; the Adjutant: "(grimly): Oh! I know you all right!! Sit down and have a drink!" They arrange a "little shoot" for 2:30 and drink; the Adjutant offers more ("No, thanks very much!"; "(hastily): Thanks very much!"). The observation post is at Tenth Avenue: "I shouldn't have thought you could see much from there." / "Well you see, if you study the map, you'll find the contour gives it about three yards higher than—" / "Quite so!" Scene 2, the O.P., "TEMPERATURE: ZERO": the Adjutant waits in "his British Warm"; "Thirty minutes elapse"; the officers arrive "cheerily: So sorry. Afraid we're a bit late." The telephonists shout "Hallo, hallo, hallo" and a wire fails; a dud; a misfire ("Rifle mechanism blown out, sir"); a round the officer thinks is a dud lands after "I used a 19 fuse" ("(A gigantic explosion is observed)"). A runner reports the battalion's "cook 'ouse and men's latrines 'ave been blown up, sir. A toffee apple landed right between 'em, sir." ("Oh, damn. I'm awfully sorry.") The last gun is "out of action, sir. Bed jumped out." The Adjutant, "(shivering)": "Thank God. A most interesting afternoon, you fellows. Let me know what time the hymn of hate comes off tomorrow. Cheero!" No casualty is mentioned. Mechanism: a short-shoot and a breakdown of communication rendered as the manners of a social call (apologies, drinks, "bad luck, old man"); the only report of damage is to buildings; the adjutant's politeness is the last line of defense. The title, "Ours or Theirs," asks on whose side the shell fell.
*Candidate application:* Friendly-fire risk is carried by good manners, here with a drink on both sides of the shoot; the speaker is spared the report of any human harm.

**[E09a-59]. "Letters from Edie": a civilian sister's letter as parody of home-front chatter.** (L18593-18727; page header "Letters from ..." partly damaged)
A sister writes "My very dearest Clarence" (the name of the serial's villain, E09a-4) in a mock-feminine voice: she has been ill ("Two ulcers and many spots did make their abode in my throat ... 'Yes, dear boy, the little woman nearly slipped through our fingers this time, but we've managed to pull her through'"), a hair-lotion testimonial ("grandmother ... aged 96 ... For 60 years she could eat nothing but gin and bitters ... notwithstanding food regulations, she consumed an entire ox for her breakfast") with an editor's note that the photographs are unreproducible; her costume ("a mauve silk nightie, with spiders' webs embroidered on it"); and news: "Elsie ... has just taken a post as assistant cook in a hospital at Barnet! ... She calls it her 'part' of cook, and is at present very occupied in getting a nice uniform." Then: "England is, as you doubtless know, in a ferment at present because:—Our leading halfpenny journal is raising its price from a halfpenny to a penny—the 'Daily Mail' says so, so it must be so." She includes her "Futuristic" poem "Worm" ("Thou thing— / Slimy and crawling ... Death meaning nought / To thee ... Oh, Hell!") and adds, "Believe me or believe me not, but I dashed that marvelous thing off in a few moments! Wonderful." Mechanism: a home-front voice shown as trivial, ill-informed and absorbed in image; war work reduced to a uniform; modernist verse mocked; death used as a poetic ornament by someone to whom it means nothing. Compare E09a-34 and E08-58.
*Candidate application:* The home letter that mentions "Death" once as a poetic term and "Hell" as an interjection is the paper's quiet comment on the gap between civilian and trench vocabularies.

**[E09a-60]. "Apologia Pro Vita ... " by the gunners: friendly fire explained in rhyme, with fraternal tears.** (L18736-18880; signed in part "BEES EVEN" and "two tyro lispers")
A reply to the infantry's "Rhymes Without Reason" (E09a-42): "Had our good champion still been in our midst, Sir ... Thou hadst not dared to publish what thou didst, Sir, / What P.B.I. then had not dared to write. / Our Frankau, Sir, would soon have found an answer / To overwhelm, to pulverize, to squash / The prattlings of your poetasting prancer" and "while we mourn at our good GILBERT'S going, / Or rather mourned, for he's been gone some time, / We take this opportunity of showing / The ethics of ballistics in a rhyme." The reasons: bad maps ("The fault's entirely due to map distortion / Then strafe 'Field Survey,' Sir, but don't strafe me"); "Mistakes by Meteor"; and, most plainly, "When not the German gets it, but the Briton, / Don't grind your teeth, and curse me for a scamp, / The obvious, only, reason I have hit on; / My predecessor left the cordite damp. / Our tears with yours fraternally we mix, Sir, / When accidents occur. We merely state / A gun's a gun, and not a life elixir, / Especially when one can't calibrate." Frantic messages ("'RACHEL is rowdy X.6.d.3.8'") strike the writers "well-nigh" with "Saint Vitus" because the grid reference falls "Behind your own lines." If the infantry still doubts, "The fault's with the 'Ballistic Co-efficient' / (In 'Gunnery,' Vol. 9, it's all defined.)" The envoi calls the two authors "two tyro lispers" with only "a wheeler—broken kneed" against Frankau's "soaring steed," and ends: "FRANKAU WOULD NEED A KRATS IF HE WERE SHOOTING!!" (the last word's OCR is partly damaged). Mechanism: an excuse list that turns shortfalls into technical causes; the "damp cordite" laid on a predecessor; and the one sincere couplet about fraternal tears set inside the comic frame.
*Candidate application:* The gunners' reply is the rare place in the paper where friendly fire is addressed from the side that causes it: excuses, a blame-shift, and one sentence of shared grief, in the same poem.

## Pattern roll-up

**Catalog of NEW devices and recurrent ones, with finding numbers (my range only; E08 devices carry over and are cross-referenced above).**
1. **The optimism-as-disease inversion** (a quack ad that makes cheerfulness and belief in the leaders a pathology) [E09a-6], later paired with a bar that cures both optimism and pessimism [E09a-38]. *New relative to E08.*
2. **The editorial self-flag that marks a risk and leaves it standing** ("Good heavens, what have I said? Well there! Let it stand"; the sub-editor's remark taken back as "only the bitterness of a disappointed man") [E09a-30, E09a-54].
3. **The staff-return and intelligence-summary parodies** (form letters and daily summaries as patter or "facts") [E09a-10, E09a-24, E09a-29].
4. **Classified-ad format for brass** (the matrimonial column; brass as lonely, mercenary, "slightly war-worn") [E09a-16, E09a-33, E09a-46] and the in-house persona who comments on it [E09a-46].
5. **Infantilizing address** (nursery column in syllabified baby-talk; "soldiers lent to play with") aimed at arms and ranks [E09a-32, E09a-49].
6. **Rhymed inter-arm quarrel** (infantry vs. gunners vs. sappers vs. Staff, with a reply) [E09a-42, E09a-60].
7. **Over-gratitude to an official reassurance** (a "plea" thanking the press and chiefs for roofing felt and asking for carpets) [E09a-50].
8. **The mock honors list** (orders by job) [E09a-51].
9. **Wounds and gas as consumer and financial inconveniences** (overdraft for a smashed kneecap; the gas sprayer as syringe or soda siphon; "just recovered from wound, feels lost without feminine attentions") [E09a-16, E09a-20, E09a-22, E09a-55].
10. **Alcohol as supply crisis, cure and courage** (the missing rum serial, "No whiskey, no war," the Camp Commandant who bought the drink, "bona fide" regulation, "Dutch courage" in One & All's verse) [E09a-20, E09a-23, E09a-38, E09a-40, E09a-43, E09a-48, E09a-52].
11. **Enemy leaders voiced by the paper to say the soldier's own doubts** (the Chancellor's "both dead"; Potsdam council; peace-note ditties) [E09a-17, E09a-21, E09a-26, E09a-37, E09a-47].
12. **Cross-referencing in-jokes** (pink garter, Munque Gallery, Nunthorpe-Cox, Reginald, Camp Commandant, Minnie as lady/neighbor, cuckoo, Teech Bomas's absence) [E09a-13, E09a-16, E09a-24, E09a-33, E09a-46, E09a-52].
13. **Plain-register pieces** (brazier poem, "Gone," farewell notices, "An Appreciation," the childhood-war poem, Frankau's nostalgia, "Godspeed" in E08) [E09a-14, E09a-18, E09a-25, E09a-27, E09a-31, E09a-56].
14. **The story that cannot be told** ("It was Xmas morning in the trenches! M-m-m-m-m-m.") [E09a-26].
15. **Outside contributions and quoted press** (a Mesopotamian alphabet; Frankau from Italy; *Daily Sketch*, *John Bull*, *Times* extracts) [E09a-18, E09a-43, E09a-45, E09a-50, E09a-56].
16. **The treasure hunt on the battlefield and the sports meeting that finishes in Berlin** [E09a-36, E09a-53].
17. **Self-parody of the war correspondent and press baron** (Teech Bomas as tourist, Cockles Tumley's demagoguery, Major Taude) [E09a-1, E09a-15, E09a-26, E09a-30].

**Functions.** *Coping*: understatement and bathos around the broken rum jar and the wound-as-overdraft; ritual objects (brazier, leave warrant, whiskey) [E09a-23, E09a-25, E09a-55, E09a-57]. *Cohesion*: cross-referencing gags and a stable cast that newcomers must decode [E09a-46]; the farewell refrain [E09a-27]. *Boundary*: the infantry vs. gunners vs. sappers vs. Staff map [E09a-42, E09a-49, E09a-60]; "Open to P.B.I. only" [E09a-53]; the civilian moralist and the home-press as an outgroup [E09a-12, E09a-14, E09a-50, E09a-59]. *Resistance upward*: optimism ad, Taude, honors list, Staff verses, "Good heavens, what have I said?" [E09a-6, E09a-26, E09a-30, E09a-42, E09a-51]. *Morale management*: the editors' own statement that they are "soldiering for pleasure" and the plea for copy [E09a-40, E09a-54]. *Grief*: only in a separate register [E09a-14, E09a-25, E09a-27, E09a-56].

**Targets.** *Staff and rear echelons* (Camp Commandant, red-hats, A.P.M., honors, Gilded Staff, "Vatican") [E09a-16, E09a-33, E09a-42, E09a-43, E09a-51]; *arms of service* (gunners, sappers) [E09a-42, E09a-49, E09a-60]; *the press and civilian moralizers* [E09a-1, E09a-14, E09a-23, E09a-30, E09a-50, E09a-59]; *the enemy's leaders and allies* more than the enemy's soldiers (the Crown Prince as looter, the War Lord and Chancellor, the Central Powers' council) [E09a-13, E09a-17, E09a-47]; *the enemy's soldier* as a former waiter [E09a-11] and as the night-time mortarman who steals sleep [E09a-41]; *the home front* (charity, leave rules, women's war work, pantomime culture) [E09a-12, E09a-34, E09a-35, E09a-59]; *the weather, mud, rats and rations* [E09a-23, E09a-28, E09a-31, E09a-54]; *the paper itself* (contest rigging, copy shortage) [E09a-5, E09a-40]; *the war as a whole*, only in the one short sub-editor quip and the optimism ad [E09a-6, E09a-54].

**Forms and mechanics.** Genre substitution (bills, ads, bazaar, honors list, soup emporium); parody of canonical or popular verse (Carroll, the soldier's song, "Little Bo-Peep," Gray-like elegy, Futurist free verse); the delayed-reveal last line (soap ode, the cake); the refrain with a shifted last stanza ("Gone"); the official-form flatness (intelligence summary, return); the literal reply; syllabified baby-talk; epistolary forms (letters, Cox's reply in rhyme); dialect speech for the ranks; serial pastiche with a ritual cliffhanger; the one-line story that ends in a hum.

**Rules of license (as visible).** (1) Seniors by nickname or by arm; the Staff do not reply, the gunners do [E09a-54, E09a-60]. (2) A leader who is loved is farewelled plainly [E09a-27: Jelf; E09a-18: Cunningham and Twiss]. (3) The editors mark their own daring ("Let it stand") and the censor is acknowledged as a reader to be placated [E09a-30, E09a-34]. (4) The harshest lines (the Indian Government, the "infirm and insane" campaign) are put in the mouth of a contributor from another theater whom the editors introduce as having "sent" the piece [E09a-45]. (5) Complaints about the war itself are given to the enemy [E09a-17] or turned into a personal-mood joke [E09a-54]. (6) The Camp Commandant, red-hat or Staff officer is teased by role, not by name.

**Limits and failure modes.** No named casualty is joked about; "gone" and "cheery faces missing" are the terms for loss [E09a-8, E09a-27, E09a-54]. A wound is told as inconvenience with a reassurance "not about to die" [E09a-55]; a possible death is a profanity pun [E09a-27]. The one dangerous item of friendly fire is told from the gunners' side with one sincere line [E09a-60] and as a drawing-room farce with no casualty [E09a-58]. The enemy dead appear once, in a soup gag that I have only summarized [E09a-52]. The paper cannot keep its page count [E09a-8, E09a-40, E09a-54]. A contributor leaves the paper under joking suspicion [E09a-34]. The editors admit the war is a tired subject [E09a-28, E09a-54].

**Time course (evolution of tone across my range).**
- *31 July 1916, the end of the Somme-Times No. 1:* the old voice persists (correspondent, society column, serial, contest, ad) while leave is stopped and the division enters the battle [E09a-1 to E09a-7]; the sharpest item is the optimism ad [E09a-6].
- *Four-month gap; 1 December 1916 (B.E.F. Times No. 1):* the press was "marooned" in the Somme "disturbance"; "many cheery faces are missing"; the title changes "for reasons over which we have no control" [E09a-8]. The new numbers are built on Staff and rear-area targets (returns, matrimonial column, morality O.C., Crown Prince), with the infantry's plain angry verse about civilian moralists and rum [E09a-10 to E09a-14, E09a-16]. Drink is established as the central theme (the rum serial) [E09a-20].
- *25 December 1916 (Grand Xmas Double Number):* the editorial is openly cynical about "Peace on earth," the editors "fed up" with the war as a subject, and the "pessimist" appears for the first time as a character [E09a-23, E09a-28]. The warmest and bleakest items sit together (brazier; the story that ends in a hum; "Gone"; the childhood-war poem) [E09a-25, E09a-26, E09a-27, E09a-31]. Peace-note jokes begin [E09a-21, E09a-26, E09a-37].
- *20 January 1917 (No. 3):* the editors speak of soldiering as "pleasure" and of drink as the war's fuel; a rash of inter-arm and grievance pieces (Rhymes Without Reason, the Mesopotamia alphabet); the paper's world is now cross-referencing itself [E09a-40, E09a-42, E09a-45, E09a-46]; "An Appeal" states the courage-by-whiskey function openly [E09a-48].
- *5 March 1917 (No. 4):* spring and mud again; the editors' own threat to "give up the war or the editorial chair," taken back as bitterness [E09a-54]; a departed colleague writes from Italy, calls his own hero role a fraud and his war paperwork [E09a-56]; friendly fire is treated both as farce and as excuse [E09a-58, E09a-60]; the offensive is a sports meeting with the finish line in Berlin [E09a-53].
- *Overall:* the objects of mockery move from place (Salient ruins; E08) to institution (Staff, returns, honors, rear-area figures, the press) and to supply (rum, whiskey, soup); the plain register persists and is concentrated at farewells and seasonal feasts; departures of colleagues (Cunningham and Twiss, Jelf, the Transport Officer, Frankau) are recorded in verse and notice, not in narrative.

**Civilian-facing register.** The paper is read at home but replies to a home press that it considers sentimental, patriotic and ignorant (*Daily Sketch* roofing felt, *Daily Mail* "suppression," *John Bull*, *Times* wires, moralists) [E09a-14, E09a-23, E09a-50]. The civilian voice is parodied in the "Edie" letter, Aunt Annie and the fashion columnist [E09a-32, E09a-34, E09a-59]. A former member who is now treated as a hero in Italy tells the old unit he is a fraud [E09a-56].

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** The only exile-like voice is Frankau's: a former member of the unit, now elsewhere, who writes warmly of the old posting, says that the hero role he now plays is a fraud, and describes his own war as telephone wire and paperwork [E09a-56]. The Mesopotamia alphabet shows the unit's humor adopted by outsiders [E09a-45]. Dissent runs upward against Staff and the Government (the "decadent Government," "the Indian Government," "our leaders") but not against the war's aim; the Kaiser, not the war, is the villain [E09a-6, E09a-17, E09a-45, E09a-54].

**Cross-cultural.** One army, one division's paper, 1916-17, Western Front, with one contribution from Mesopotamia. The jokes are British-officer jokes (red-hats, Camp Commandant, Cox & Co.); do not generalize.

**Craft observations (editors' own and by inference).** Keep a stable cast and recycle in-jokes across issues (garter, Minnie, Cox, Camp Commandant); give each arm one stable grievance; end a piece on a flat statement ("The war proceeds," "It was Xmas morning in the trenches! M-m-m-m-m-m."); put the reveal in the last word (soap, cake); let editorial bluster break on a small material grievance (the rum jar); use real names of places for fictional ads, and have readers or contributors supply copy; and, when copy runs short, quote the press and let the quotation do the work ("geese in the trenches").

## Gaps and cautions

- **Retrospective vs. period.** All content here is period text; the "cheerful" label for the paper in E08 belongs to the 1930 foreword, not to these issues. Do not read the 1917 editors' jokes about censorship as proof of what any censor struck; the visible markers are only [E09a-21, E09a-34, E09a-45].
- **Authorship and audience.** Pen names; "Violet" is treated in the text as a staff member who is paid more than Teech Bomas, and a January letter in the text jokes about doubts that she is a woman; I do not infer the author's identity. The paper's officers'-mess character is an inference; the ranks speak in officer-written dialect. The voices of "One & All," "Impecunious," "Pioneer," "J.H.W." and "T.G." are unidentified.
- **Identifications are glosses.** "Minnie Werfer," "Hind and Berg," "Bethmaniano," "Enver," "Maude," "Wilhelm," "Tino," "Eve," "Sabretache," "Herod's," "Cox & Co." and the Mesopotamian place names are explained from general knowledge; the text does not explain them. "Numero Neuf," "L.B.M.L.," "Walthorpe, Foxley, Nelmes," "Kew Wemus" and the "Irish Troupe" are unresolved.
- **OCR damage.** Titles (the childhood poem, "Disturbing," "Apologia," the Edie letter heading), several verse last-lines (the Chancellor poem; the observer verse; the soap ode's last lines), numerals and grid references, and the "W" stanza of the alphabet are unreliable; I did not quote what I could not read cleanly and flagged each place. The 5 March editorial's "allowed to run spare" is printed as the OCR gives it and may be misread.
- **Date oddities.** No. 3's editorial is dated 1 January but the issue is dated 20 January; the *Times* extract in it is dated 15 January and the "Germany's Secret Meeting" dateline is 21 January 1917. The cuckoo letter in the January number places the first cuckoo "this spring," improbably in January, which may itself be the joke. Treat content dates as written, issue dates as printed.
- **Overlap with the next unit.** My range ends at L18,700 but I read to L18,887 to complete "Letters from Edie" and "Apologia Pro Vita"; the next unit (starting L18,650) will also cover those and the rest of No. 4 from L18,888 (a rear-area piece, only glanced at).
- **Content handling.** The "Indignans" soup letter [E09a-52] and two Mesopotamian stanzas [E09a-45] are summarized and not quoted; the Tank dispatch's enemy-casualty line [E09a-15] is paraphrased.
- **Period stereotypes.** National, ethnic and gender caricature is present (a "Scotchman" as cautious; a Sultan with "harems"; female columnists as consumers; "Hun" as a general term). This is documentation of one army's period humor and is not material for characterizing any Tepenian people (see the project's "No National Stereotypes" law).
- **Minor items not extracted separately.** A short repeated tradesman ad (Poile & Trotter), the "How doth the busy little Hun" quatrain, a few captions and some Munque Gallery asides; they repeat devices cataloged here or in E08.
- **Length.** This extraction is far above the usual 10-18 percent of source words because the source is public-domain primary text in which the quoted example is the evidence (the brief asked for exact examples of each device, and the OCR text carries much white space and column debris, so word count overstates the source's real length). Findings that repeat a device are cross-referenced rather than repeated.
- **Candidate applications** are structural suggestions only; this source documents British WWI officers' humor and is not a template for any Tepenian faction.
