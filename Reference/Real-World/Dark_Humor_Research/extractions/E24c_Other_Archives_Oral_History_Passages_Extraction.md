## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What the whole unit is.** A keyword-and-phrase harvest, not a sample: **102 passages (about 15,650 words of passage text) from about 86 narrators or interviews** in six archives, picked by the harvester from far larger corpora by searching for humor, joke, laugh, nickname, kidding and similar words (log §§1-3). The log states what the selection does NOT represent, and I confirm it in the data: it favors people who say the words; it was run once on each archive, with different search machinery in each (full local text of 606 New York transcripts; a site transcript-search on 507 Korean War Legacy interviews; a title-and-description search plus a cue-scored crawl of Canadian video clips, 1,400 of 4,179 fetched; local grep of two Army volumes; grep of 17 of 33 Marine career interviews; grep of 55 Alabama PDFs); and the **captivity cue** in the Canadian crawl drives that archive's picture (§9 and §3.1). The six archives cover different wars, so counts across them mix archive method with national or war differences, and I flag this wherever a comparison is made.

**New York State Military Museum Veterans Oral History Program (OH-OTHER-NY_MIL-066).** 45 passages (about 6,970 words) from 40 of 626 transcripts (the B-17 crew group interview is one transcript with three passages; Cosgrove supplies three, Buck two). By war, my reading: **World War II 31** (including one German Army narrator whom the archive's index lists as Vietnam War in error), **Vietnam-era 8** (4 Air Force, 2 Army, 2 Marine), **Korea 3** (all Army), **Desert Storm and Panama-era 2**, **Iraq 1** (a National Guard soldier). Mostly volunteer interviewers, standing questions in at least 26 transcripts (log §3.2). **Does NOT contain:** the interviewers' questions in most passages, rank and age for most narrators, any systematic Navy or Marine or enlisted breakdown, or reuse terms (the pages state none). Page labels are PDF pages; running page headers sometimes leak into the text (an interview-title header sits inside the Greene passage). Passages begin and end mid-account.

**Korean War Legacy Foundation (OH-OTHER-KOREAN-045).** 23 passages (about 3,280 words) from 20 of 507 transcribed interviews, found by the archive's own transcript search. By nationality and branch: **U.S. 19** (Army 12, Marine 2, Navy 3, Air Force 2), **New Zealand 3** (two narrators), **United Kingdom 1**; no passage is by a South Korean or other Asian narrator, so "Korean War veterans" here means UN-side Western veterans. One narrator's interview (Mendez, a Puerto Rican in the U.S. Army) is an archive translation interleaved with the original; the Hall passage is Hall reading a personal diary aloud and its middle is garbled in the transcript. Timestamps are the transcript's. The log's note on the search: "laugh" hits are mostly transcriber markers, and several plain-word concepts returned zero queries ("gallows humor," "sick humor," "morbid").

**Veterans Affairs Canada, "Heroes Remember" (OH-OTHER-VAC_HE-113).** 21 passages (about 3,150 words) from 18 narrators' clips (McGee supplies four). Clips are 1-5 minute excerpts of longer interviews, so a passage is often the whole answer and the interviewer is mostly unseen. By war: **First World War 2, Second World War 15, Korea 1, Afghanistan or recent Canadian Armed Forces 3**; 19 Army, 2 Air Force. **Nine of the 21 passages concern captivity** (eight Japanese, one German), which reflects both the Canadian Hong Kong experience and the harvester's cue words ("POW," "prisoner" scored high in the crawl). The 2,583 clips with none of the cue words in title or description were mostly not fetched. All narrators are men; the originals use British spellings, which I do not reproduce in quotations. The government's terms permit non-commercial reproduction with credit; I still use short quotations here.

**Army CSI *Eyewitness to War* (OH-OTHER-ARMY_C-042).** 6 passages (about 810 words) from four narrators, all U.S. Army, all interviewed in 2006 about the November 2004 assault on Fallujah: Captain Neil Prakash (tank platoon leader; three passages), Captain Chris Boggiano, Staff Sergeant David Bellavia (squad leader) and Major Lisa DeWitt (battalion surgeon). **This is the only modern-war set in the unit.** The harvester dropped a seventh passage by a CNN reporter (not a veteran) and corrected a misattributed narrator (log §9). **Does NOT contain:** any question about humor, any narrator describing what humor does, enlisted junior soldiers, or the 2-7 Cavalry and Marine units' voices. A search of the full text shows at least one more "funniest thing" passage (Captain Cobb, Vol. II) that the harvest did not select; I did not read it, so the six are a sample, not the full humor in the volumes.

**Marine Corps University career interviews (OH-OTHER-USMCU_-109).** 6 passages (about 1,090 words) from three long interviews: Generals Charles Krulak (3) and Robert Barrow (1) and Captain James Webb (2). **All officers**, so enlisted gallows humor is not here; the harvest note says these are included for command humor and nickname patterns (the log adds one gallows-flavored unit in-joke). The text is OCR with scan errors left (I do not rely on garbled words). Sixteen further large PDFs were not fetched.

**University of Alabama Vietnam War Oral History Archive (OH-OTHER-UA_VIE-107).** One passage (about 350 words) from a student-run interview with a Marine; machine-generated transcript, with errors. Treat it as a single voice.

**Cross-cutting limits.** All passages are retrospective (told years or decades after, except the Fallujah set, which is two years after). None is a recording of humor in the moment. Few passages give a joke's wording; most describe an incident told as funny. No defector or exile content. No passage uses the term PTSD. War labels come from the archives; I note two mismatches (NY Guenther is indexed as Vietnam but is a WWII German Army soldier; NY German's passage is about a friend killed in Panama, though the interview carries a Desert Storm label).

## Findings

### Part A. New York State Military Museum (OH-OTHER-NY_MIL-066)

**[E24c-1]. What narrators say humor did: a channel for what is not discussed, a stress-intensified pleasure, and the narratable remainder.** (NYSMM-17671 p.11; NYSMM-1443 p.29; NYSMM-339 p.34; NYSMM-541 p.20; NYSMM-652 p.8; NYSMM-533 p.5; NYSMM-17663 p.11; NYSMM-17591 p.13)
Fourteen of the 45 passages carry an explicit statement from the narrator about what humor does or whether it belongs in war. Three distinct explanations recur. *A substitute channel:* Bly (USAF, Vietnam-era, 509th Bomb Wing) says "We live by humor" because much else goes unspoken, and illustrates with an aircraft engine dropping off on takeoff and nearly landing on the base golf course. *An intensifier:* Rodd (USMC, 26th Marines, Vietnam) says a sense of humor is something you create, and that incidents grew funnier because of the daily stress; the example is a latrine wall inscription about someone's last day, read in Rodd's first days in country. *The narratable remainder:* Cosgrove (Army, 1st Armored Division, WWII) says the "real tough stuff" cannot be put into words, and keeps "the things that let you survive," so the funny stories are what can be told. Others give shorter versions: Fleisher (USAF, Vietnam) says war is scary yet full of humorous moments (the anecdote is Fleisher's being bumped from a flight by pallets of lettuce for generals); Greene (USAF; the passage places the capture inside North Vietnam, with Chinese captors) lists "having a sense of humor" among what got Greene through; Fendt (Army, Korea, gate guard) says "a lot to laugh about" beside a lot to cry about; Blair (Army, 8th Armored, WWII) says the war "isn't all horror stories"; the B-17 crew interview (USAAF, 457th Bomb Group; ex-POWs) says that without their sense of humor they "always had names for people" and a funny moment.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who is fluent and funny about the war may be telling the sayable subset, and say so. Humor can also be the standing explanation offered for what goes unsaid.

**[E24c-2]. Funny afterward, hysterical during, deadpan as the release.** (NYSMM-516 p.6; NYSMM-397 p.6; NYSMM-526 p.2; NYSMM-17624 p.4; NYSMM-24459 p.5)
Several passages show the mechanism in motion. Fanniff (Army, 173rd Airborne, Vietnam) says many incidents were scary when they happened and humorous afterward; the example is a comrade who dove into the bunker during a mortar attack wearing only a weapon and a helmet. Deitz (Army Air Forces, 15th Air Force, ball-turret gunner) describes a stuck live bomb and two men's embarrassing lapses on the way to clearing it, then says the crew went into "gales of laughter," that it "wasn't funny" in itself, and that it "seemed funny at the time": laughter as a pressure valve when the stakes were lethal. Favata (Army, WWII) describes a bombing in which no one spoke until a big man's wry remark about digging a cellar made everyone burst out laughing: one deadpan line breaks collective silence. Battillo (Army, HQ 4th Corps, Italy) reports a sergeant's grim comfort that you never hear the shell that hits you, answered with a dry "I feel better now"; the narrator frames this as typical ("there's always humor attached"). Buck (USMC, 1st Marines, WWII) describes an apparent ambush on a trail that resolved into a friendly laborer dancing on a log, and ends "you had to laugh."
*Candidate application:* Humor in these passages is mostly relief from a threat that did not land, or a line that names the fear so no one has to. A character can be funniest right after the thing that almost killed the character.

**[E24c-3]. Pranks: a boredom economy that escalates, plus the one the victim did not find funny.** (NYSMM-339 p.27; NYSMM-17684 p.9; NYSMM-242 p.3; NYSMM-681 p.7; NYSMM-486 p.4; NYSMM-17676 p.10)
Cosgrove (Army armored, North Africa) says rear-area monotony in Morocco turned into a competition in which everyone tried to top each other; the winning entry was sabotaging a latrine's support timbers so a row of men dropped through. The captain interrogated the whole unit the next day and never learned who did it; Cosgrove confessed years later at a reunion, when the captain laughed, though not "at the time." Brach (Army Air Forces, WWII) says pranking was constant because "you had to amuse yourself," and describes a bunkmate's habit of coming in drunk and the water prank played on that bunkmate; Brach adds, unprompted, that nobody ever stole from anybody there (the passage's explanation, about "the men in the grove," is unclear), so the pranks apparently sat inside a trust norm. Cartwright (USAF, Vietnam, radar maintenance) says people "did things all the time," including chasing colleagues with an energized antenna and, in the passage's central anecdote, a sergeant who powered the radar without its safety circuits to see what would happen. Gunn (Army, WWII, 31st Division) accepts practical jokes in general but says the hot foot "wasn't funny," the only one named as over the line. Eggleston (Army, 1st Infantry Division, WWII) was the victim of an enemy booby trap rather than a comrade's prank, and says everyone laughed at the victim while Eggleston "didn't think it was funny" (a concussion had blinded Eggleston for a time). Borden (Army, Korea) notes with amusement that Chinese radio operators called in on the hourly commo check, and the unit learned to tell its own people by voice.
*Candidate application:* The prank is a timed, ranked social game with a reveal date; the target's laughter can come years late. The line between game and injury is named by individuals, not by a rule.

**[E24c-4]. Humor from above and about brass; rank and risk limit who may joke with whom.** (NYSMM-1 p.5; NYSMM-17638 p.6; NYSMM-1203 p.8; NYSMM-541 p.20; NYSMM-17644 p.6; NYSMM-17609 p.4)
Two passages show commanders joking about the danger they control. Abaied (Navy, destroyer USS Laffey, WWII) recalls the skipper announcing more shelling of a coast and then taking it back with "just kidding"; the interviewer's reply is that the skipper was "a funny guy." Belfield (Army, 22nd Armored Division, WWII) recalls a transport captain's loudspeaker announcement that the old wooden ship had survived torpedo hits and was the safest in the convoy; the interviewer calls it humor. In both, the narrator reports the joke with approval, not resentment. Jokes about brass come from below and are mostly silent or retrospective: Mulligan (Army major, WWII) says officers "laughed silently" at a regular colonel who had never done field duty and lectured them on what officers never wear; Fleisher and Bennett (USMC aviation, Vietnam) tell war stories whose butt is institutional priority (lettuce for generals) or the triviality of a mission (flying birthday cakes to infantry, imagining the next-of-kin wording). Baranowski (USAF Reserve, Desert Storm) states the license rule outright: with officers the relationship was professional and the joking "to a minimum" because someone could be killed any day, whereas among the comrades who lived together it was looser.
*Candidate application:* The passages suggest that rank-based license tracks risk: the closer the joke is to who may die, the fewer people make it. Commanders' grim jokes are reported with approval, while jokes about commanders are silent or retrospective.

**[E24c-5]. Captivity: humor aimed at captors and at conditions, and a joke that could have gone wrong.** (NYSMM-17591 p.12, p.13, p.21; NYSMM-1274 p.16; NYSMM-ab061ad9 p.19; NYSMM-652 p.8; NYSMM-17565 p.16)
Seven passages are by prisoners of war (the B-17 group interview supplies three; five interviews in all). WWII Army Air Forces prisoners in Germany tell of a mass gesture of contempt at a halt on the rail line (a hundred fifty men making the same motion at the guards, who were unmoved), a bucket-dump prank on a guard who crept under the barracks to eavesdrop (followed by mock courtesy), and a general claim that Americans always found "a funny moment." O'Shea (Army Air Forces, 351st Bomb Group) says the camp food earned nicknames, and the prisoners told themselves the insects were protein: "It's a joke now." Browe (Army medical battalion, Philippine Division, held in Japan; context consulted, full transcript lines ~925-970) says a smart remark made to impress the camp interpreter could have turned serious, and ends that "you better know ahead of time" whether a joke is funny; the surrounding transcript says showing any Japanese invited beatings, and that Browe kept a poker face under questioning while comrades matched their stories. Greene (USAF) lists humor among what got Greene through and jokes now that the wine refused then would be accepted today; Brown (Army, Korea; context consulted, transcript lines ~545-596, a camp where men died daily, escapes, punishment in a pit, then a hospital) says the treatment survived was strange and then "the funny thing is it worked," and that Brown laughs about the scar.
*Candidate application:* Captive humor is covert, aimed at the guard's ignorance, and carries real costs when misjudged; the later retelling flips danger into the funny version.

**[E24c-6]. Humor about the dead, the wounded and the enemy: a documented minority, told matter-of-factly, with the discomfort sometimes recorded.** (NYSMM-17663 p.11; NYSMM-311 p.5; NYSMM-24459 p.6; NYSMM-533 p.5; NYSMM-1179 p.4; NYSMM-913 p.16)
Six passages joke about the dead, the wounded or the enemy (restrained summary; see register). Blair (Army, WWII) tells of infantrymen who used a frozen enemy corpse to frighten a cook in the mess line, citing it as proof that people keep their humor in war; the cook screamed. Colyer (Army, 27th Division, WWII) says that from a ship Colyer and others laughed at enemy dead in the water. Buck (USMC, WWII) tells of a quip after a gruesome incident in the unit and of comic survivals (a man hunting his dental plate under fire; another somersaulting from a blast unhurt). Fendt (Army, Korea) found a gate check that turned up a corpse in a basket funny. Miller (Army Air Forces air-evacuation squadron, WWII; the passage implies a flight nurse) tells light anecdotes about psychiatric evacuees, including one patient's delusion that lasted the whole flight home, and says a "bit of humor" can be seen in spite of the war. Kneiper (Army chemical mortar battalion) quotes the narrator's own thought when the driver, after firing at a sniper's tree, called out cheerfully for approval: the narrator was disturbed that anyone was "sitting there laughing" while someone was being killed.
*Candidate application:* The dead are used as props or butts in a few passages and rarely named as comrades; the friction appears from the one who watches someone else laugh. The target is the stranger, the enemy, or the afflicted, rarely one of their own.

**[E24c-7]. Limits: no humor at the front, laughter that is "not really a laugh," the butt who does not laugh, ethnic ribbing, and a political joke that cost a man his freedom.** (NYSMM-24457 p.3; NYSMM-338 p.3; NYSMM-1703 p.20; NYSMM-518 p.38; NYSMM-490 p.10; NYSMM-677 p.11)
Booth (Army artillery, WWII) says combat is destruction and killing, so "nothing funny": humor and fun lived in the barracks; at the front there is "a certain amount of humor but it isn't any fun," mostly boredom ranging up to terror. Corte (Navy Seabee, WWII) shot at an unseen night mover that proved to be a wild boar; men kidded Corte, and the base commander told the kidders to be thankful, since it could have been an enemy crawling with a grenade; Corte says they laughed but it was "not really a laugh." Tanner (USMC, 5th Marines) says the Marines watched enemy shells bracket nearby ships, which then backed off fast, and that "in a way we laughed," then asks "Can you imagine laughing at something like that?" Farfaglia (Army, 25th Infantry Division, WWII) was asked to tell the "fart that saved your life" story; the narrator says it is "funny" but that it was a serious night, then tells of a platoon sergeant killed by a friend who took the sergeant for the enemy, which is the hypervigilance behind the funny outcome. Elisha (Army, 27th Division, WWII) says ribbing about ethnic origin was part of service life, some of it good-natured and some hurtful, with stereotypes about a Jewish soldier's courage assumed by others. Guenther (German Army, WWII; the archive indexes the interview as Vietnam War in error) tells, smiling, of a leader-mocking political joke told by a man who was informed on and got half a year in a penal unit clearing mines, which Guenther equates with expecting to die.
*Candidate application:* The strongest limits are physical (the front is not funny; the joke after the fact is), moral (someone else's death beside the laugh), and political (the wrong audience can kill). A character may keep a hard joke for after the danger, and not for during it.

**[E24c-8]. Nicknames and group labels: ship brigade, camp food, a dead friend's name.** (NYSMM-393 p.9; NYSMM-1274 p.16; NYSMM-587 p.4)
DeCorse (U.S. Merchant Marine, WWII) says merchant sailors called their rusty, unpainted ships the "Rust Bucket Brigade," and explicitly names it "our black joke," because so many of the ships did not come back: a collective label that makes the odds into a unit name. O'Shea's food nicknames (see E24c-5) are the same device applied to hunger. German (Army, 452nd General Hospital, Desert Storm era) says "mostly everybody has nicknames," recalls that a friend later killed in the Panama operation got that nickname from the drill sergeant's failing to pronounce the name, and gives the narrator's own from a Pittsburgh Steelers receiver; the narrator's talk of nicknames sits inside the account of the friend's death, and the interviewer's next question returns to nicknames.
*Candidate application:* Nicknames are the commonest gentle form here; they can name hardship, odds, or a dead friend without a joke being told.

**[E24c-9]. The post-Vietnam passages are about waiting and anxiety, not combat, and they are three.** (NYSMM-685 p.9 [context consulted, transcript lines ~355-382]; NYSMM-17609 p.4; NYSMM-587 p.4)
Only three of the 45 passages come from after Vietnam. Haag (Army National Guard, 108th Infantry, Iraq, a tour spanning the 2004 U.S. election, in a 3rd platoon whose sister platoon took repeated roadside-bomb casualties) says "everybody always jokes around" about what will go wrong on the way home: something will happen on the way to Kuwait, the tour will be extended. The context shows the joking is a hope-guarding ritual: the unit was told to be ready to leave on Christmas Eve while at a talent show, the helicopters left without them, and the narrator's own reminder was not to get hopes up because it was still a helicopter ride. Baranowski (Desert Storm) is E24c-4; German (Just Cause friend) is E24c-8.
*Candidate application:* In the modern passages the joking is about the institution's unpredictability and one's own hope, and is performed as a pre-emptive pessimism.

**[E24c-10]. After service and the interview frame.** (NYSMM-24502 p.28; NYSMM-339 p.27; NYSMM-17591 p.12; NYSMM-17684 p.9; NYSMM-24457 p.3)
Bohen (Army, 35th Tank Battalion Recon, WWII) tells of a chaplain's speech at the Fort Dix discharge telling men who had lived "like animals" for years to say please at table; Bohen calls it "a big joke." (The speech's profane example is not reproduced.) Cosgrove's reunion confession and Greene's wine joke are other post-service retellings. One B-17 interviewee had "been waiting for years" to hear another man confirm the same funny thing, so the group interview was itself the occasion. The interview frame matters: Booth refers to a questionnaire that asked what was humorous, Brach hesitates about whether a prank "should be on television," and Gunn's answer to a direct question about humorous things is practical jokes (the harvest log records at least 26 NY transcripts in which the interviewer asked a standing question about humor or pranks).
*Candidate application:* Veterans' public humor stories are shaped by the standing question and by an audience; the funniest version is the one fit for the camera.

### Part B. Korean War Legacy Foundation (OH-OTHER-KOREAN-045)

**[E24c-11]. Veterans' own explanations: lightness to avoid mistakes, humor "in everything," looking on the bright side, and laughter that arrives later.** (KWLF-rebecca-baker t=0:15:00; KWLF-charles-l-hallgren t=0:31:30; KWLF-patrick-v-hickey t=0:26:30; KWLF-walter-kreider-jr-2 t=0:38:00; KWLF-maurice-morby t=0:38:30; KWLF-gerald-land t=00:21:00)
R. Baker (Navy) says that in the surgical teams described someone would "make light" with a joke when it got tight, that outsiders cannot understand why doctors do it, and that the alternative is that "you make mistakes": the explanation is task performance, not mood. Hallgren (Air Force, five months clearing unexploded bombs) tells how a local woman named her baby after the bombs, describes the christening with rice wine, and concludes there is humor in everything. Hickey (Army, New Zealand) narrates diving into a slit trench with another man during shelling while the others laughed at Hickey's warning; the interviewer says the story is told as funny when Hickey must have been scared, and Hickey answers that you "look on the bright side." Kreider (Army, 1954) ends a farce about a captain and a driver with the claim that the unit had "a good sense of humor." Morby (Army, United Kingdom) says shells that ricocheted off a river were not funny at the time but "very funny afterwards," and builds a pun on "incoming mail" ("second-hand mail") with mock admiration for the enemy's raft-and-wreck "hobby" (the passage's slur for the enemy is not reproduced). Land (Army; Christmas and New Year's at the front) describes homesick quiet broken by a rifle shot and a tank's answering boom in a comic rhythm that "lightened up the atmosphere."
*Candidate application:* Different explanations sit side by side: humor as an operating tool, as perspective, and as retrospective relief. The punning euphemism for incoming fire is the compact form worth noting.

**[E24c-12]. Survival-by-chance laughter, with the narrator's gloss that it was serious.** (KWLF-david-white t=0:36:30; KWLF-bob-wickman t=0:22:01; KWLF-patrick-v-hickey t=0:26:30)
White (Army, directing fire) says an enemy mortar round landed on White's position and only partly detonated; White and the sergeant picked it up, "looked at each other and laughed." Wickman (Navy, with Marines) tells of being told to stand up in the open to relieve a bodily need, then ducking a machine-gun burst; Wickman frames it as "very serious, but it was kind of funny." Hickey's trench anecdote (E24c-11) is the New Zealand version. In each, the laugh follows a near miss that was random and survived together, and the narrator adds the second frame (serious) in the same breath.
*Candidate application:* The shared laugh after a near miss is cohesion in one beat; the narrator who keeps adding that it was serious is the one who knows how close it was.

**[E24c-13]. Humor and the chain of command: bureaucratic irony, a sergeant's unwelcome laugh, a pranked sergeant.** (KWLF-baldwin-f-myers t=0:32:30; KWLF-john-j-baker t=1:02:30; KWLF-walter-kreider-jr-2 t=0:38:00; KWLF-patrick-v-hickey t=0:50:30)
Myers (Army, October 1950) saw Myers's own name on the casualty list marked with a code word ("gravy") and told the colonel that this was why no pay was arriving; the colonel explained it meant missing in action, and "we had a laugh about it." In the same passage Myers later meets the lieutenant dragged out of a paddy and jokes that, had Myers known the man was an officer, the lieutenant would have been left there; the lieutenant introduces Myers to colonels as a hero, which Myers disclaims. J. Baker (Army, Taegu command) laughed when a new lieutenant planned to drop surrender leaflets on a Chinese army the sergeant knew had just driven the Americans back hundreds of miles; the lieutenant answered that the Chinese were stupid and did not know what was going on. Kreider's captain is drawn as a Napoleonic figure in a vehicle with an incompetent driver who put them in a ditch, the unit's reaction being that kind of helpless laughter. Hickey (Army, New Zealand) tells of a gun crew who invented a lonely soldier in Korea and had a story about him printed in a weekly news the passage ties to an 11-day sea voyage; the passage implies the invented soldier was the sergeant, who was told the mail had come and had to fetch two sacks of letters from women in New Zealand in person.
*Candidate application:* The enlisted humor aimed up is documented here as deflating pretense (the leaflet plan, the casualty code) and as collective revenge on an unpopular NCO that uses the institution's own mail channel.

**[E24c-14]. Captivity: communal spectacle aimed at the guards' rules.** (KWLF-charles-ross, two passages; no timestamps)
Ross (Army, held in a Chinese-run camp) tells two stories. In one, a fellow prisoner's soap-waxed handlebar mustache made people laugh at the sight of him; the Chinese instructors ("comrades," in Ross's words) ordered it cut; the camp barber, a Filipino prisoner, then shaved the man's head except for a mustache-shaped strip of hair hidden under the issued cap, revealed by bending over; "everybody would just crack up," and Ross calls it "beating them at their own game." In the other, after Ross picked up the wrong undergarments by mistake and got called "thief" by the audience, the men staged a mock trial one idle Sunday: a judge in an overcoat for a robe, a fellow sergeant as defense counsel, a knotted cord as a prop; Ross says it was "just for fun" with nothing else to do.
*Candidate application:* Captive humor is theatrical, collective and designed to survive scrutiny: the joke is hidden under a cap or staged as a game, and the captors' prohibition becomes the premise.

**[E24c-15]. Humor about the wounded and the dead: the wounded man's own banter, the contemporaneous diary, and a recovery sign told as humor.** (KWLF-john-j-baker t=0:39:00; KWLF-keith-g-hall t=0:58:30; KWLF-clayborne-lyles; KWLF-antone-jackim t=0:39:30)
J. Baker (Army) recalls a 19-year-old private with a fatal abdominal wound who teased the sergeant about the reflexive shouting for a medic (a comrade had been killed days before) and then said they were all alone; the narrator ends angry and helpless (restrained summary; see register). The wounded man's banter is the joke; the sergeant does not laugh. Hall (Army, New Zealand; context consulted, transcript lines ~1015-1060) reads from a diary kept for three years an entry that records a sergeant killed instantly in a minefield, then a line headed "Funny things that happened," then the commanding officer's broken nose from a jeep in a ditch: the adjacency was written at the time and is read aloud without comment (the transcription of the middle of the entry is garbled). Lyles (Navy) says a downed pilot shot in the buttocks and brought aboard was "a big joke," but a serious matter. Jackim (Air Force) says that Jackim's own recovery after heavy blood loss showed in a bodily sign nurses watched for, and calls it humor (restrained; see register).
*Candidate application:* The wounded may make the joke themselves; the other party carries the weight of it afterward.

**[E24c-16]. Limits: the joke that was physically punished, the joke aimed at a dead man, the training-versus-war boundary, the scrubbed show, and the retort the enemy did not laugh at.** (KWLF-jack-allen t=0:40:00; KWLF-dennis-e-hultgren t=0:35:00; KWLF-gustavo-mendez-2 t=0:17:30; KWLF-jack-whelan t=00:43:30; KWLF-chaplain-ralph-lindon-smith-jr t=0:17:30; KWLF-duane-trowbridge t=13:30)
Allen (Marine) says that the men who "goofed around" in the cold paid in frostbite, and that frozen fingers and feet were no subject for jokes because they were real. Hultgren (Army, a unit that handled the recovered dead; context consulted, transcript lines ~594-625) was in charge when a subordinate offered a recovered arm with a joke; Hultgren threatened to ask the company commander to send the man to the front for disrespect, and the man went quiet (restrained; see register). Mendez (Army, Puerto Rican; the archive's translation; context consulted, lines ~258-281) rebuked a friend who laughed after falling during a withdrawal: this is "the war," unlike their training camp; the friend was shot repeatedly soon after, captured, and exchanged two months later. Whelan (Army, on a troop transport; context consulted, lines ~525-553) says the chaplain made Whelan cut the bawdy routine for the ship's Christmas show, so Whelan used the chaplain's joke book; the Navy and some officers' wives liked it but "the troops on Christmas Eve were having none of it." Smith (Army chaplain) tells of a Chinese lieutenant searching for the dead who called Smith an American aggressor; Smith answered "summer vacation," and the officer "didn't laugh" and walked away. Trowbridge (Marine) says Trowbridge and others dummied grenades by removing the powder and pulled the pin among comrades, who scattered; later they said it was not "very funny," though Trowbridge thought so and repeated it.
*Candidate application:* The limits are consequences (injury), reverence (the dead), setting (training versus war), and audience (an audience of troops can reject institutionally censored comedy; an enemy officer will not trade banter).

### Part C. Veterans Affairs Canada, "Heroes Remember" clips (OH-OTHER-VAC_HE-113)

**[E24c-17]. What the veterans say humor did, and the forms it took: an entertainer in a cellar, positive remarks as morale, teasing after a raid, a remark that "lightened" the shelling.** (VAC-713366 Ganong; VAC-710156 McGee; VAC-711716 Cromwell; VAC-709903 Kelly; VAC-710636 Baggs)
Ganong (First World War, 104th Battalion, infantry) recalls that on the night before going into the line at Neuville-Saint-Vaast in 1916 a ventriloquist who had survived the Somme entertained the men in a ruined cellar and "made the night much better"; Ganong adds, as an aside, that people good at amusing others were killed later and this man was killed soon after (the passage uses a period racial label for the entertainer, not reproduced). McGee (WWII, Winnipeg Grenadiers, Hong Kong prisoner) says morale was kept up by each man saying something positive to others, with humor, while the men starved; the example is a banana peel nailed to a hut wall beside a sign calling the sight stranger than fiction, the kind of thing they laughed at "to keep us busy." Cromwell (WWII, Royal Canadian Army Service Corps, transport) says that anyone claiming not to have been scared is not telling the truth, and describes men running for cover during bombing "like rats in holes," then returning to laugh at and tease each other about where they had ducked, then a cigarette and a cup of tea. Kelly (WWII, 6th Airborne Division, intelligence officer) recalls a comrade's remark in a ditch under artillery that a malfunctioning shell "had a bayonet on it," which "lightened the life a bit." Baggs (WWII, 166th Field Artillery, sergeant) says a shell fragment tore Baggs's trousers and the gunner's reply to Baggs's anxious question was that the trousers were gone, not the man.
*Candidate application:* The recurring structure is a threat survived, then a deadpan or teasing remark that converts the sound or the fear into a joke. The bayonet-shell remark is the compact example: a joke about the sound of the thing that could kill.

**[E24c-18]. Far East captivity: jokes at rank, at the captor's absurdity, and performed deference.** (VAC-710162 McGee; VAC-710155 McGee; VAC-712600 Gerrard; VAC-709264 Bérard)
Nine of the 21 passages come from men held prisoner (eight by the Japanese, one in Germany); the Hong Kong prisoners, from the Winnipeg Grenadiers and Royal Rifles of Canada, dominate. McGee tells of "spikers," men so desperate for tobacco that they followed smokers to collect butts, and of one huge spiker who pinned a naval officer's dropped cigarette under a boot and told the officer politely that the officer was standing on the man's tobacco; McGee says the men found these things funny then and is unsure about now. McGee also says the whole 1,800-man work party laughed when a Japanese officer announced they would take two mountains and dump them into the sea; eighteen months later the airfield site was flat. Gerrard (Royal Canadian Signals, a prisoner working in a blacksmith shop) describes a guard who stood with his heels beside a glowing steel bar; the older foreman and the narrator both saw it and silently looked away, and the guard soon danced about trying to get the boots off: "the greatest hot foot" Gerrard had ever seen. Bérard (Winnipeg Grenadiers, platoon leader) says guards lined the prisoners up as if to execute them for sport and took a "real big kick" from their fear; the men shook hands and said farewells and agreed to humor the guards in case it saved their lives (the guards' period slur is in the passage and is not reproduced).
*Candidate application:* The captive's humor includes silent participation (the foreman's look away), the audience's amusement at a bigger man's cheek, and an agreed performance of terror. Performed deference toward captors appears here when survival was the stake (Bérard).

**[E24c-19]. Comic misunderstandings and institutional absurdity: the unpopular acting commander, the wheels, the offer of a whiskey peace table, the "grave" that was a garbage pit.** (VAC-712111 Henley; VAC-712364 Warren; VAC-709085 Chrysler; VAC-710149 Bevan)
Henley (First World War, sergeant) tells that an unpopular officer promoted temporarily to company commander, dressed in showy kit, headed for the improvised latrine just when an enemy shell landed in it; the men could not go near the officer afterward (restrained summary; see register). Warren (WWII, 166 Squadron, wing commander) says that after flying-school crashes the first question was whether the wheels were intact, never whether the pilot was; "you could make all kinds of jokes about it." Chrysler (Korean War, Army driver) recalls that during a quiet stretch an enemy officer radioed an offer of a table and whiskey for "peace talks"; nothing came of it, and Chrysler calls it comical. Bevan (WWII, 13th Field Regiment, gunner) tells that a young captive was told to dig a pit for kitchen garbage while nearby village women lined a fence weeping, believing he was digging his grave; it had not occurred to the gun crew; the interviewer remarks that the women were concerned for the captive's life, and the narrator adds that they feared "German-style" treatment.
*Candidate application:* Joking at the brass is retrospective, at the institution's priorities, and often unintended (Bevan's comedy is misreading by others, not a joke told).

**[E24c-20]. The two modern Canadian passages: a comrade's wry remark, and a joke made the night before a fatal convoy.** (VAC-713025 Fitzgerald, Afghanistan, PPCLI master-corporal; VAC-712978 McCue, CAF, PPCLI sergeant)
Fitzgerald says that during a fire fight in a ditch of foul water, a comrade kneeling beside Fitzgerald tapped Fitzgerald's leg to say, in a stock soldier's idiom (a mild profanity, not reproduced), that they were in as bad a spot as it gets; Fitzgerald's reply was an incredulous "Are you serious?" Fitzgerald says such moments pick a person up and are the memories relied on now in down moments. McCue tells that the night before a convoy a young soldier McCue had come through ambushes and bombs with knocked on the door in the Kandahar airfield barracks to say the soldier had a bad feeling; McCue reassured the soldier, who answered with a joke about using "soldier skills," and "we both had a chuckle"; the soldier was killed nine days before the end of his tour. McCue asks whether the man knew.
*Candidate application:* In a modern war the joke is a private two-person exchange, and the survivor keeps it. The comrade's jokey acceptance of fear before the convoy is made vivid by the death.

**[E24c-21]. Limits: laughter that is not happy, nothing to laugh about, the grim "funny part," and grave jokes that the narrator now doubts.** (VAC-709179 Fawcett; VAC-709241 Babin; VAC-709358 Murphy; VAC-710162 McGee)
Fawcett (WWII, 158 Squadron rear gunner, prisoner in Germany; half the cigarettes went to guards) says hunger and the repeated question of what would happen today meant that the prisoners had "a lot of laughter, but it wasn't happy laughter." Babin (WWII, Royal Rifles ambulance driver, held in Hong Kong and then Japan) tells the ketchup-and-tomato-juice comedy of a man who thought he was hurt when an air-dropped food platform burst after the Japanese guards left the Niigata camp, immediately after noting the same drop killed a civilian woman and her child, and ends that there was not much to laugh about in such places (restrained; see register). Murphy (WWII, Royal Rifles, infantryman) uses "the funny part" to introduce a grim detail of burial parties in the camp, where bodies longer than the small coffins were forced into them (restrained; see register). McGee's clip about the tobacco confrontation ends by doubting that it is funny now.
*Candidate application:* Veterans can use "funny" as an anchor that signals that a grim fact is next; the narrator and the listener decide at that moment how to take it.

**[E24c-22]. After service and old age: a long-running bet, "covering up," and a lost capacity to find things funny.** (VAC-710161 McGee; VAC-711533 Wiseman; VAC-709147 Purse; VAC-713025 Fitzgerald)
McGee (WWII, Hong Kong prisoner; recalling around 1994-95) tells that after McGee's last surviving sibling died McGee joked with a fellow prisoner about who would outlive whom, and they bet a bottle of scotch; at the other man's funeral McGee spoke to a nephew about the bet, and some time later the nephew turned up at McGee's door with a bottle and said he was "paying off a debt." Wiseman (Canadian Armed Forces, warrant officer; war not specified) praises a leader who told soldiers to tell their stories and says that if people want to laugh and joke, let them, "perhaps they're covering up too," as Wiseman did for ten years, until, with the marriage at rock bottom, Wiseman's wife said the husband was not the same. Purse (WWII, Winnipeg Grenadiers, Hong Kong prisoner) says that in captivity emotions never showed, and that after getting home Purse had great trouble even appreciating humor and needed a long time to recover. Fitzgerald (see E24c-20) keeps a jokey memory as a resource for current bad days.
*Candidate application:* The old-age use is dark-humored friendship across a lifetime (the bet is a joke about outliving), humor as a mask that holds until a spouse names it, and a period in which humor itself is unavailable.

### Part D. Army CSI, *Eyewitness to War* (Fallujah, November 2004) (OH-OTHER-ARMY_C-042)

Context consulted (full volumes, `csi_eyewitnesstowar_volumei.txt` OH-OTHER-ARMY_C-043 and `..._volumeii.txt` OH-OTHER-ARMY_C-044): the interview headers and the questions before and after four of the six passages (Prakash pp.164-170; Bellavia p.280; Boggiano p.320; DeWitt pp.149-150). Each use is flagged.

**[E24c-23]. What the Fallujah soldiers joke about: piled-up absurdity, the allies' theater, the brass's metrics and rules, and the leader's beard.** (CSI-EW-II-168; CSI-EW-II-170; CSI-EW-I-320; CSI-EW-I-149; CSI-EW-II-280)
Captain Prakash (A Company, 2-63 Armor tank platoon leader) tells that after a mine disabled Prakash's tank (context consulted, Vol. II lines ~6030-6054), Prakash and five others were crouched by it when a sniper opened fire; with the mine plow bringing up mines beside them they all crammed into a turret built for three. Prakash calls it "ridiculous" in the form "only in Fallujah and only in Iraq," then "the funniest thing," then "throw me a bone." Prakash's second passage is about a rehearsal speech by an Iraqi battalion colonel who got more emotional and faster until the interpreter could not keep up; Captain Boggiano said not knowing what the man said but being "fired up," and Prakash adds that it was great to see leaders show passion (context consulted, Vol. II lines ~6138-6157). Captain Boggiano says (answering a question about the after-action review) that the official enemy-killed numbers were always a good laugh, because no one counted bodies and the aim was to leave the city before the Marines collected remains that had lain for days (restrained; see register). Major DeWitt (battalion surgeon) says the radio order, delivered at 2 a.m. in a mortared tent, that everyone must wear body armor outside the tent was something to laugh at (noting that mortars do not distinguish inside from out); DeWitt told the junior soldier to let the lieutenant sleep, and slept too. Staff Sergeant Bellavia (squad leader, TF 2-2) describes, on a day of hard fighting, the company commander arriving with a very long beard when the soldiers expected to be told to shave; the captain said that was the reason for the visit, "we all had a good laugh," they shared "private humor" about the soldiers' doings, and they laughed again when the captain warned Bellavia that a soup-can improvised grenade might simply contain a fuse.
*Candidate application:* The Fallujah jokes are about compounding bad luck and about the institution's words (the body armor order, the body count), with a shared leader-subordinate banter. Note that the distance to the target is small: laughter at a speech the group did not understand, and at an order the speaker chose not to pass on at once.

**[E24c-24]. The enemy-casualty passage and the body-count irony are the hardest edges, and the narrator's own comment does not resolve them.** (CSI-EW-II-164; CSI-EW-I-320; context consulted, Vol. II lines ~5880-5906)
Prakash, early in the assault, called in a repeat artillery fire mission against a building after seeing an enemy fighter, staggering out of smoke and apparently in distress, struck by the next rounds, and calls this "the funniest thing I've ever seen" and says it was unbelievable; in the same breath Prakash says feeling sorry for the enemy is rare but happened here ("they just got slaughtered") and that the reason for giving the order is unknown to Prakash. The passage and its context describe the deaths in graphic detail that is not reproduced here (restrained; see register). Boggiano's comment (above) is not a laugh at a death but a laugh at a number, with a grimmer justification attached. Both narrators speak of an enemy who is unnamed and in bulk, and neither laughs at a comrade's death in these passages.
*Candidate application:* A soldier may use "funniest" for something the speaker says cannot be accounted for, and the narrator volunteers that no account can be given, which is itself a mark of an unresolved item.

**[E24c-25]. The interview frame: historians' operational questions, humor as an unprompted aside, a two-year distance.** (CSI-EW-II-164, -168, -170, -280; CSI-EW-I-149, -320; context consulted for headers and neighboring questions)
The interviews were conducted in 2006 by historians of the Combat Studies Institute (the Operational Leadership Experiences project) for an unclassified public record; the introduction warns the interviewee to avoid classified detail, and questions are about planning, movement, rules of engagement, relations with the Marines and equipment. Of the four passages whose neighboring questions I read, none answers a question about humor (I did not check the question before Prakash's pp.164 and 168): Prakash's Iraqi-ally story answers a question about contact with Iraqi units; Boggiano's laugh answers a question about the after-action review; DeWitt's tent scene comes in an account of the night before the assault (the preceding question is about a novel); Bellavia's captain scene sits inside a chronological account that the interviewer introduces by naming the captain's death, and it is followed within the narrative by news of that death (context consulted, Vol. II lines ~10379-10450); the harvested window ends at the laugh. The narrators are Army officers except Bellavia, a staff sergeant; the language in the originals is unredacted profanity.
*Candidate application:* Modern soldiers' humor appears as a way to narrate the war in an official record: tough language, a comic aside, and the quick return to the operational point.

### Part E. Marine Corps University career interviews (OH-OTHER-USMCU_-109)

Context consulted (full transcript `usmcu_barrow_Gen_Robert_H_Barrow.txt`, OH-OTHER-USMCU_-110 series; lines ~1262-1291): only for the Barrow passage, flagged below. The OCR text has scan errors left in ("Yung Mu" for Vung Mu, "banalion," "root eel lar" for root cellar); I cite names as the passage prints them and do not lean on a garbled word.

**[E24c-26]. Command humor from three Marines: an in-joke made of a near-disaster, a solemn speech with a bathetic tail, and fatalistic radio deadpan.** (USMCU-Krulak p.27 [two passages]; USMCU-Webb p.103; USMCU-Webb p.139)
Krulak (later Commandant; here a junior officer on a Vietnam landing) tells that a very gung-ho company commander ordered a grenade thrown into what was taken for an enemy fighting hole; it was a family's root cellar, an old man emerged holding the grenade with its tape still on, followed by a woman and children, and "we almost killed these people." Krulak says it was traumatic then, but "kind of been a joke" since, and the battalion made a mock-heroic honorific of the place name, used as a jokey sign of unpreparedness. In the second passage the battalion executive officer, on the ship's intercom an hour before the landing, gave a grave speech about going into combat for the first time and ended on a profane order not to bring cameras ashore; Krulak's reaction was "Are you kidding me?" (profanity not reproduced). Webb (rifle platoon commander, Vietnam 1968-69) tells of being pinned between fire from two sides on Ho Chi Minh's birthday ("happy birthday, Uncle Ho") and of crawling to a company commander whom Webb used to joke was "the only Royal Marine" in the war, because of the fatalistic, level manner, speaking on the battalion net as if he were reporting that they were about to die; the interviewer chuckled along. In the second Webb passage, the regimental operations officer decided Webb was "essential to the successful completion" of the war (Webb's irony), keeping Webb in country while Webb's Basic School classmates phoned the office from Da Nang, laughing, to roast Webb as they left for home.
*Candidate application:* Command-level humor in these three is about unpreparedness, ceremonial bathos and understatement, and it is told by men who later held senior rank, years after, so the stories have been polished by retelling. The roast from the people going home to the person kept behind is a distinct form.

**[E24c-27]. Peer ribbing: the prank the butt carried for a year, and mock terror that infuriated the listeners.** (USMCU-Krulak p.53; USMCU-Barrow p.41 [context consulted])
Krulak, then a Marine student officer at the Army's Command and General Staff College, tells that Krulak's classmates, as a joke on Krulak's place in the running for the top academic prize, covered the issued briefcase in white lamination labeled with the prize name; Krulak kept it on the desk all year, the professors "cracked up," and Krulak finished high and earned the respect of Army officers, which Krulak attributes to a "willingness to take all the jokes." Barrow (later Commandant; a lieutenant in 1943) says that on the day before the Basic School class graduated, with orders for scattering coming, those headed for the Fleet Marine Force poked fun, and one man would throw himself on his bunk crying that he did not want to die and wanted guard duty at a navy yard; it "infuriated us." The full transcript (context consulted) suggests, though the passage is ambiguous, that the fury was tied to the orders being handed out that day: Barrow was being sent to a stateside barracks assignment, not to the fleet, so a joke about wanting a navy-yard billet struck a sore spot.
*Candidate application:* In these officers' accounts, the right response to a prank is to carry it publicly and gain standing; the mock-terror joke stings the people whose assignment is the thing being mocked.

### Part F. University of Alabama Vietnam War Oral History Archive (OH-OTHER-UA_VIE-107)

The only passage is machine-generated text with transcription errors ("dispersing" for disbursing, in the log's reading: the log identifies the narrator as a Marine disbursing clerk); I rely only on sentences that parse.

**[E24c-28]. The nickname from a cartoon, and the narrator's theory of why funny memories stick.** (UA-3229 Rocco, USMC, 3rd Marine Division, Dong Ha, p.2)
Rocco says casualties and the scenes of handling them after bunkers took direct hits can still be recalled: you did what was needed, "you didn't care" about completeness because you were still coming down from being terrorized (restrained summary; see register). ### Part G. Across the six archives: the brief's cross-cutting questions

**[E24c-29]. Fallujah 2004 beside the earlier wars: what carries over, what is absent, and why the absence may be the frame.** (CSI-EW-II-164, -168, -170, -280; CSI-EW-I-149, -320; compared with the New York, Korean War Legacy, Canadian and Marine passages cited in each item)
*Distance.* The six Fallujah passages were recorded two years after the battle; the other 96 passages are mostly told decades after the events (WWII, Korea and Vietnam narrators), and, for Haag, Fitzgerald, McCue and a few others, some years after. The Fallujah "funniest thing" is therefore nearly contemporary, and the flip from not funny to funny (E24c-32) cannot show up in it yet.
*What carries over.* (1) A laugh after absurd compounding danger: Prakash's tank-sniper-minefield scene parallels NYSMM-526 (Favata), KWLF-david-white and KWLF-bob-wickman. (2) A leader who joins the joking: Bellavia's company commander resembles the skipper in NYSMM-1, the transport captain in NYSMM-17638, the colonel who laughs at the casualty code in KWLF-baldwin-f-myers and the company commander in USMCU-Webb p.103: **five officers in four wars (WWII 2, Korea 1, Vietnam 1, Fallujah 1)**. (3) The institution's own words or numbers as target: Boggiano's body counts and DeWitt's body-armor order parallel the casualty code (KWLF-baldwin-f-myers), the lettuce pallets (NYSMM-541), the wheels-first rule (VAC-712364) and the field-duty-free colonel (NYSMM-1203). (4) A laugh at an enemy casualty: Prakash's passage parallels NYSMM-311 (Colyer, WWII). Prakash alone adds, in the same breath, pity and an inability to say why.
*What the six passages do not contain.* No prank, no nickname, no captivity, no after-service content, no narrator statement about what humor does (by contrast, 27 of the other 96 passages state a function, 28%) and no limit statement except Prakash's puzzlement. I attribute this mainly to the frame (below and E24c-25) and to n = 6, not to a change in soldiers: a New York interviewer asks directly about humor and pranks; the Army historians never do.
*Interviewer framing across the set.* Fallujah: operational questions, humor volunteered in answers. New York: at least 26 transcripts carry a standing humor or prank question (log §3.2). Korean War Legacy: interviewers sometimes press (E24c-11, Hickey). Marine Corps University: the interviewers chuckle along (USMCU-Webb p.103) and stay neutral otherwise. Canadian clips: the question is mostly unseen, but two passages answer questions about morale and the return home.
*Candidate application:* A modern soldier-type character's humor, as the Fallujah accounts record it, is in-the-moment absurdity and talk about the institution; the enemy-casualty joke is the hard edge, and the speaker's own commentary leaves it unresolved.

**[E24c-30]. Humor across armies: what the passages support, with counts.** (all six archives; counts from the Tallies)
*Counts.* U.S. forces 76 passages; Canadian 21; New Zealand 3; United Kingdom 1; German Army 1. There is no South Korean narrator. These numbers come from six archives with six selection rules, so they cannot be read as national samples.
*Where the same form appears in more than one army.* (a) **The deadpan or teasing remark, or the shared laugh, after a near miss**: 11 passages, four national forces (U.S. WWII, Korea, Vietnam and Fallujah; Canadian; New Zealand; United Kingdom): NYSMM-17624, NYSMM-526, KWLF-david-white, KWLF-bob-wickman, KWLF-patrick-v-hickey, KWLF-maurice-morby, VAC-709903, VAC-710636, VAC-711716, CSI-EW-II-168, USMCU-Webb p.103. (b) **Gags aimed at captors**: U.S. 3 (NYSMM-17591 p.12 and p.21, KWLF-charles-ross mustache), Canadian 2 (VAC-712600, VAC-710155); in both, the humor targets the captor's ignorance of what is happening and is hidden or deniable. (c) **Laughter that has an edge**: "not really a laugh" (NYSMM-338, U.S.), "wasn't happy laughter" (VAC-709179, Canadian, in German captivity), "wasn't very funny at the time" (KWLF-maurice-morby, U.K.). (d) **The explicit statement that humor is present in war or helps with it**: U.S. (NYSMM-17663, NYSMM-541, NYSMM-533, KWLF-walter-kreider-jr-2, KWLF-charles-l-hallgren), Canadian (VAC-710156) and New Zealand (KWLF-patrick-v-hickey, who calls it looking on the bright side): the same explanation in three forces.
*What the passages do not support.* Any claim that one army jokes more, darker or differently. The Canadian set is 43% captivity (9 of 21) against 12% of the U.S. passages (9 of 76), and the cause is the Canadian harvest's cue words and the Hong Kong experience, not national style. The non-U.S. rank-license cases (KWLF-patrick-v-hickey, New Zealand: a prank on an unpopular sergeant; VAC-710162, Canadian: an enlisted prisoner's polite cheek to a naval officer) resemble U.S. cases (NYSMM-339 p.27; NYSMM-1203), but one case each is not a comparison. The single German Army narrator (NYSMM-677) shows only that a political joke told to the wrong listener could cost half a year in a penal unit.

**[E24c-31]. The last joke: a retold exchange with someone who died soon after.** (KWLF-john-j-baker t=0:39:00; VAC-713366 Ganong; VAC-712978 McCue; CSI-EW-II-280 Bellavia)
Four passages in four wars and two armies pair a joke with a death that was imminent or followed. In Korea, a dying soldier teases a sergeant (U.S.; E24c-15); in the First World War, a ventriloquist who raised the men's spirits the night before the line is killed soon after, and the narrator remarks that the amusers died (Canadian; E24c-17); in Afghanistan, a soldier jokes the night before a convoy and is killed nine days before the tour ends, and the narrator wonders whether the man knew (Canadian; E24c-20); at Fallujah, the squad leader's account of a day with the company commander includes a shared laugh and, a few minutes of narrative later, news of that commander's death (U.S.; E24c-25; context consulted). In none does the narrator say the joke caused anything or that the death changed the joke; the retelling keeps the funny exchange.
*Candidate application:* The remembered comrade is preserved through a particular exchange rather than through a eulogy; a character may carry an in-joke from someone who is gone and tell it with the laugh intact.

**[E24c-32]. Time course, civilian life and old age.** (NYSMM-1274 p.16; NYSMM-516 p.6; NYSMM-339 p.27; KWLF-maurice-morby; USMCU-Krulak p.27; VAC-710162; VAC-709147; VAC-711533; VAC-710161; VAC-713025; NYSMM-24502 p.28)
*Change over time stated by narrators.* Five passages explicitly mark a flip from not funny to funny (O'Shea's camp food, Fanniff's scary incidents, Morby's ricochets, Krulak's "traumatic then" Vung Mu story, and Cosgrove's captain laughing at the reunion); two mark the reverse or a loss (McGee is unsure the tobacco story is funny now; Purse had trouble appreciating humor after coming home). Another (Deitz) says it seemed funny during.
*In civilian life.* Six passages of 102 treat humor after service: Wiseman (joking can cover up; ten years before help), Purse (lost the capacity for a while), Fitzgerald (the in-fire joke is now a resource in down moments), McGee (a lifelong bet about outliving a comrade, ending in a bottle at the door), Bohen (a chaplain's discharge-hall speech as a joke about returning to polite company) and Cosgrove (confessing a prank decades on). No passage uses the term PTSD in the text I read. Wiseman's is the only one that connects joking to concealing distress.
*Old age.* The WWII and Korea narrators were interviewed decades after the events (one gives the age as 70 [VAC-712600]), and their present-tense relation to the stories is mixed: "It's a joke now" (O'Shea), laughing at a surgical scar (Brown), a joke told with a smile that once cost a man half a year in a penal unit (Guenther). McGee's bet is the one case of humor sustained between two old men and beyond one's death.
*Candidate application:* Humor can have a time course with at least four tracks: funny-when-safe, funny-then-not, unavailable for a while, and a friendship joke that outlives people. A veteran ten to fifteen years out could plausibly sit on any of them.

**[E24c-33]. Craft observation: the funny version is the one for the audience.** (NYSMM-24457 p.3; NYSMM-17684 p.9; NYSMM-339 p.34; NYSMM-518 p.38; KWLF-patrick-v-hickey; USMCU-Webb p.103; NYSMM-17638 p.6; NYSMM-1 p.5)
Across the set, narrators shape the humorous account for the listener: they mention a questionnaire that asked what was humorous (Booth), worry a prank is not fit for television (Brach), say the tough material cannot be put in words but the humorous can (Cosgrove), and tell a story as funny only to add that it was a serious night (Farfaglia). Interviewers reward the funny version ("a funny guy," "at least he had a sense of humor"), occasionally resist it (Hickey's interviewer notes the narrator must have been scared), and sometimes laugh along (the Marine historians). Narrators sometimes concede the tone-switch mid-sentence ("very serious, but it was kind of funny").
*Candidate application:* For a character telling an old story to a stranger, the funny version is the default and the second frame is the tell. A good scene can let the listener's laugh decide how much of the other frame is allowed to appear.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions.** Substitute channel for what is unspoken (NYSMM-17671); intensified by stress (NYSMM-1443); the narratable remainder (NYSMM-339 p.34); pressure valve with a crisis (NYSMM-397); task performance in surgery (KWLF-rebecca-baker); morale among prisoners (VAC-710156); perspective (KWLF-patrick-v-hickey); a stored resource for later low moments (VAC-713025); the mind "takes care of us" (UA-3229). Cohesion is shown more than stated: shared laughs after near misses (E24c-2, -12, -30).
**Targets.** The odds and the near miss (the commonest); the institution's words, numbers and priorities (casualty code, lettuce pallets, wheels, KIA counts, body-armor order, an officer's field-duty ignorance); an unpopular sergeant or acting commander (covert); captors (deniable); peers (pranks); self (briefcase, nickname after a near miss); an enemy casualty (a minority, E24c-6, -24). Almost never a named dead comrade.
**Forms and mechanics.** Deadpan after a shell; euphemism and pun ("second-hand mail"); nickname with fate in it ("Rust Bucket Brigade," food names, a cartoon coyote); prank with a reveal years later; staged ritual among captives (mock trial, hidden mustache); an in-joke made of a command failure; mock-sympathy to a captor ("Oh, so sorry"); the roast of the one left behind by those going home; a laminated briefcase carried publicly.
**Rules of license.** Reading the room (E24c-4's rank-and-risk rule; E24c-7's political danger); the officer's joke about danger is allowed and returns in silence or afterward; the target must be able to take it (hot foot is over the line for one narrator; ethnic ribbing was partly hurtful; a political joke could kill); the one who carries a prank publicly earns standing (USMCU-Krulak p.53).
**Limits and failure modes.** No humor at the front (NYSMM-24457); laughter that is "not really a laugh"; humor about frostbite and the dead is out (KWLF-jack-allen, KWLF-dennis-e-hultgren); training versus war (KWLF-gustavo-mendez-2); an unrefined routine rejected by an audience of troops (KWLF-jack-whelan); the retort an enemy will not laugh at (KWLF-chaplain-ralph-lindon-smith-jr); joking that conceals for years (VAC-711533); a lost capacity (VAC-709147).
**Time course.** Funny afterward (five passages); funny then, doubtful now (one); unavailable for a while (one); hope-guarding pessimism in the modern waiting (NYSMM-685); the instant "funniest" in Fallujah.
**Civilian-facing register.** The funny version for a camera or stranger; the second frame in the same breath; a discharge-hall joke about manners; the narrator who says the tough material has no words.
**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** None for defectors. Captive humor as covert, deniable and staged, aimed at a hostile institution's rules (KWLF-charles-ross; NYSMM-17591 p.21; VAC-710155); the German Army narrator's lethal political joke (NYSMM-677).
**Cross-cultural.** Same forms in four national forces for near-miss deadpan and captor gags; no supportable differences (E24c-30).
**Craft observations.** Interviewer frame shapes what is told (E24c-33); the second frame is the tell; last-joke retellings are a ready device (E24c-31).

## Gaps and cautions

- **Selection bias is the dominant caution.** The passages were pulled by humor words, so they overstate humor's frequency and favor narrators who talk about it. They say nothing about how often any soldier joked or what proportion of any narrator's account is humor. The log's own list of what the selection does not represent applies in full.
- **Different archives, different machinery.** Counts across archives mix method with national and war differences. The Canadian captivity share is the clearest artifact.
- **All narrators are retrospective, and mostly old.** Only the Fallujah set is near the events. No passage records a joke in the moment.
- **Gender and rank.** About three apparent women among 86 narrators (by name and role only). Officers are over-represented in Marine, Army CSI and some NY passages; enlisted humor is thin at Fallujah.
- **No South Korean or other non-Western voice** appears in any of the six passage files, though the Korean War Legacy archive holds many such interviews. No defector or exile content.
- **Text damage.** OCR errors (USMCU), machine transcription (UA), a garbled diary read-aloud (KWLF-keith-g-hall), translated and interleaved text (KWLF-gustavo-mendez-2), leaked page headers (NY Greene).
- **Unread material.** At least one further "funniest thing" passage in Eyewitness to War Vol. II (Captain Cobb) was not selected and not read; the 16 unfetched Marine PDFs and 2,779 unfetched Canadian clips are open threads (log §10). The six Fallujah passages are a sample of the volumes' humor, not a census.
- **Apparent tensions.** NY Bly's header says Vietnam and the text mentions a "Chinese base"; I take the narrator's own words as the setting and do not resolve it. The passage tags sometimes disagree with the text (for example KWLF Brown's "captivity" tag fits only once the full transcript is read, which I did).
- **Reuse.** These archives' terms vary; every item here is own-words with few-word quotations. Nothing here is a sample line for any character.
