## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What my range holds.** PW-01, L30,901-37,163, which is the whole closing stretch of the omnibus. (1) The back page of **B.E.F. Times Vol. 2 No. 5 (22 January 1918)**, L30901-31060: five mock ads (a camouflage device, balloon parachute tours, a sketching course, a course in waiting, the printer's line). (2) **B.E.F. Times Vol. 2 No. 6 (Tuesday, 26 February 1918; price 1 franc)**, L31063-32812: a theater bill, a "Trench University" lecture-series ad, a souvenir ad, an editorial, a verse on the London food queues, a Pepys-style diary, a "Stick it" verse, a leave-nostalgia verse, an American soldier's slang letter, a verse on divisional rivalry, a Transport Officer's nursery-rhyme column, a poets-in-the-trenches piece, a telephone sketch of two rear-area officers, an Old Testament raid, a leave-junction sonnet, a mock rule book for shelling and raids, a pipe elegy, the opening chapter of the serial "Zero! The Hound of the Raskershires," a letter, a rigged competition, and six back-page ads. (3) **The Better Times No. 1 (November 1918)**, L32814-34739, a revival on a captured press after about nine months of silence: theater bill, ads (including surplus huts and an insurance parody), an editorial, a Shakespearean orderly-room scene, a Russian-language lesson, a post-war rank-reversal verse, a future-Menin-Gate story, the Armistice told as a dream about billets, an editorial postscript added after 11 November, an Armistice poem, a demobilization answers column, the first chapter of a romance serial, a Teech Bomas dispatch on the autumn breakthrough, and a run of letters on the 1914 Star. (4) **The Better Times, "Xmas, Peace and Final Number" (December 1918)**, L34742-36651: theater bill, ads, an editorial on the end of the war, a verse chronicle of five Christmases, a Russia dispatch, resurrection and plain verses, Teech Bomas at the Armistice table, a general's patter song, **the editors' "Bon Chance" last-number note (L35714-35735)**, a verse on recalled old officers, a Rhine-crossing verse, a second Russian lesson, "The Horrors of Peace," a second orderly-room scene, the last chapter of the romance serial, four letters, and the last ads. (5) L36652-37163: scan debris ("Digitized by Google" pages, a University of California library due-date slip, blank and speckled pages). **No 1930 foreword, index, appendix or note follows the final issue in this file.**

**Evidence base.** One infantry division's officer-written paper, 1918 (the editors are named only once, as F. J. Roberts, Editor, and J. H. Pearson, Sub-Editor, in the last-number note); pen names throughout; ranks speak only through officer-written voices; no images survive except as captions. The omnibus's own 1930 prefaces are at the front of the file and belong to E08. The period glosses I give (the German offensive of March 1918, the Armistice, the Rhine crossing, the December election, the North Russia landings, demobilization priority for miners) are from general knowledge and flagged as mine; the pages name only the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month and the Rhine.

**OCR and format damage (honest report).** Column-fragmented text; headings and drop capitals often lost or garbled; numerals unreliable; the Russian-language lessons are unreadable; page order of mastheads is scrambled in the Final Number. I cite **line numbers ("L")** of the text file; there are no page markers. **Quotations** are exact except that single-character OCR slips are repaired silently where certain, larger repairs are in [square brackets], and British spellings are converted to American per the project rule, in quotations too (so quotation spellings are not letter-for-letter).

**What it does NOT contain.** No private soldier's own text and no images. No mention of shell shock, desertion or executions. No named casualty is joked about. No statement of casualty totals except the verse that denies a division's claim. No 1930 retrospective note on the last issue (see above). No issue between 26 February and November 1918. The editors' statements about morale and purpose are few: the "Stick it" verse, the clown-and-tears verse, the plea for copy, and the plain paragraphs on the end of the war and the dead.

**Content handling.** The range contains one dehumanizing correspondent's dispatch on Russia (E10b-72), a stanza on corpses and mud in the Armistice poem (E10b-49), several fictional deaths in the closing serial (E10b-87), mock-German and stage-American caricature, and mild sexual innuendo in two verses. These are described in restrained terms and logged in the register at the end. No cutoff or filter failure occurred in this run, and the file was saved in several appends as the rules require.

## Findings

### A. B.E.F. Times Vol. 2 No. 5 (22 January 1918), back-page advertisements only (L30901-31060)

**[E10b-1]. "Kamooflage" patent device: the testimonial in which the enemy mistakes the trench for a Staff College.** (PW-01 L30895-30930, back page of Vol. 2 No. 5.) A mock advertisement for a trench camouflage device, "GUARANTEED TO BAFFLE THE ENEMY," with "A Grateful Customer" testimonial signed "Three Pips": the "Huns have mistaken it for a Staff College, and refrained from shooting at it. My nights are now undisturbed." Mechanism: consumer-testimonial form plus a reversal of what draws fire; the joke target is the Staff College (staff as an institution so unreal or so remote that an enemy would not bother with it), and the wish stated under the joke is an undisturbed night. The word "Kamooflage" is spelled phonetically as a new coinage, which is itself period evidence of the term's novelty (my gloss).
*Candidate application:* Wants can be voiced as a product ("my nights are now undisturbed"); the staff or the training establishment can be the safest place on earth by definition.

**[E10b-2]. "Wymeroo": parachute tours by "Sausage" (observation balloon).** (PW-01 L30933-30973.) A travel ad: write to "Sausage," France; the firm "is famous for the rapidity of its descents"; "Parachuting a speciality"; "Splendid scenery and view of war taking place in the district"; motor trips arranged; verse: "In this awful pitch and toss age, / Don't omit to try a sausage." Mechanism: the observer's emergency exit from a balloon is sold as a sightseeing thrill, and the war is "scenery." Target: balloon duty and the hazards of an exposed job, never stated; the only mention is "rapidity of its descents." (Observation balloons were called sausages; gloss mine, the ad's address and the pun on food confirm it.)
*Candidate application:* The dangerous special assignment is advertised as a tourist feature, with the danger visible only in a speed claim.

**[E10b-3]. The sketching course.** (PW-01 L30979-31012.) A correspondence-art ad: "Sketching is the latest fad of the Army, and many are coining millions out of their dud productions"; "Copy any well-known picture in the National Gallery, and send it along to me. (The copy not the picture, as you might get nabbed.)"; a 100-guinea course, payment in advance; a "Telegraphic Address: 'Perhaps.'" Mechanism: a fad (war sketching) meeting the profit motive; the parenthetical aside carries the joke. Target: soldier-artists and the trade in them; the war as a market.

**[E10b-4]. "Can You Wait?": the skill of waiting and the tribunals.** (PW-01 L31018-31053.) An ad for a five-guinea, six-lesson course after which "we guarantee you will be able to wait anywhere"; "ALSO THERE'S A CHANCE OF 'WAITING' TILL THE WAR'S OVER, THUS AVOIDING INQUISITIVE TRIBUNALS"; firm "Cuthbert & Slacker, England"; verse "If you're single and A I, / Come and learn to wait, my son." The garbled heading asks, as far as the OCR shows, what is the good of joining up if one cannot wait. Mechanism: military waiting (the dominant experience of service) is taught as a teachable skill, and the home-front shirker dodging conscription tribunals is the target through the firm's name. A rare inversion: the soldier is the one who can wait; the slacker "waits" at home.

### B. B.E.F. Times Vol. 2 No. 6 (Tuesday, 26 February 1918; price 1 franc) (L31063-32812)

**[E10b-5]. Masthead and printer.** (PW-01 L31063-31077; printer's line at L31056.) "THE B.E.F. TIMES. WITH WHICH ARE INCORPORATED The Wipers Times, The 'New Church' Times, The Kemmel Times & The Somme-Times. No 6. Vol. 2. Tuesday, February 26th, 1918. Price 1 Franc." The roll of absorbed titles continues unchanged from Vol. 2 No. 1 (see E09b2-13). The printer's line on the preceding back page reads "Printed and Published by Sherwood, [Mee] & Co., Ltd." (OCR damaged). This issue is the first in my range that has its own masthead; the preceding gap from 22 January is about five weeks.
*Candidate application:* A unit's paper that names its dead predecessors on its masthead keeps its own history on display.

**[E10b-6]. The Cloth Hall theater bill, third winter.** (PW-01 L31080-31112.) "CLOTH HALL, WIPERS. THIS WEEK. 'RONDER,' THE CELEBRATED CARD MANIPULATOR AND ILLUSIONIST. HAS PERFORMED BEFORE MOST OF THE CROWNED HEADS OF EUROPE ... All Tricks will bear the closest scrutiny." Also "THE WILLIES," "GEORGE LLOYD AND MANY OTHER ATTRACTIONS," and a show title damaged by OCR (probably a "wind up in the West" pun; uncertain); "BOOK EARLY. PRICES AS USUAL." Mechanism: the standing mock theater bill datelined at the ruined Cloth Hall (as in E09b2), now with a conjuror whose act "will bear the closest scrutiny." Target: nothing specific; this is the running device that keeps the ruins as a place of entertainment.

**[E10b-7]. The Trench University lecture series.** (PW-01 L31115-31153.) An ad from "The Anglo-American University Extension Lecture Syndicate, Active Service Branch" (Secretary "S. U. M. Nutt," telegrams "WATAHOPE, B.E.F."): "WHY LET THE HUNS WIN THE WAR AFTER THE WAR? They are preparing by means of a Trench University ... while YOU are only studying C.R.O. [Corps Routine Orders], and similar periodicals." Four lectures: March 3, "SHELLS. What they are. Their uses. How to find them. Having found them, how to use the field dressing, etc., etc." (Professor W. Z. Bangs, R.A.); March 10, "ALCOHOL AND ITS TERRIBLE RAVAGES. With particular reference to the wicked issue of rum to the troops" (Professor T. T. Bilge, H2O), with "THIS IS EXPECTED TO BE A VERY POPULAR LECTURE, AND TO AVOID DISAPPOINTMENT YOU SHOULD BOOK EARLY"; March 17, "THE GREAT WAR; and how we won it every year until the Germans had no more reserves left" (Professor Hillary Bullock); March 24, "THE GERMAN AT HOME. His culture. Kindly Nature. Chivalry. Is he one of Nature's gentlemen? Etc., etc." (a "[...] Snowden," name damaged; read as the pacifist politician). Mechanism: an adult-education drive (the army's educational schemes; gloss mine) parodied as a lecture syllabus; the shell lecture's "having found them, how to use the field dressing" puts being hit in a how-to sequence in a flat tone; the rum lecture is the temperance cause that the troops would crowd to; the third is the press's yearly victories; the fourth is the pacifist's kindly Hun. Target: the educational scheme, temperance, press optimism, pacifists.
*Candidate application:* The institution's uplift program can be answered with a syllabus in which each topic is the soldier's real lesson (what a shell is, what to do when hit).

**[E10b-8]. The souvenir manufacturing company, Cambrai.** (PW-01 L31156-31183.) An ad headed "DO NOT READ THIS!!! UNLESS YOU HAVE A GIRL AT HOME" offering priced souvenirs: German shoulder straps (a shilling each, ten shillings a dozen), the same with a surcharge for being marked, "SHELL HOLES, COMPLETE: 50/- each," duckboards (English 5/-, German 10/-), "IRON CROSSES: 6d. a gross," Bibles, and "Photographs (for fiancées)." Verse: "To please your best girl, it is clear, / You must procure a souvenir." Firm: "Souvenir Manufacturing Company, Cambrai." Mechanism: the battlefield trophy as a manufactured commodity (the "souvenir" is made, not found), with a price scale that puts German goods above English and the Iron Cross, the enemy's highest decoration, at a fraction of a penny each. The surcharge item is a reference to blood on a trophy and is paraphrased only. Target: the souvenir trade, the girl at home who wants a trophy, and the enemy's decorations.
*Candidate application:* The thing the home front wants from the war can be priced, and a joke about it carries the soldier's contempt for the people who ask.

**[E10b-9]. Editorial of 26 February 1918: spring withheld, the Hun as small boy with a half-brick, chevrons and the sub's pay.** (PW-01 L31193-31306.) The editors "almost feel inclined" to start the "yearly song of Spring," but "remembering past seasons we refrain, and bottle it all up until our next, or next but one, number"; farming experts are detailed "so that we run no risk of planting carrots upside down" and there should be a chance "that we are not the 'spudless army' in the Summer." On the enemy: "the Hun has rung his bell and blown his horn, since when he appears to have adopted the 'you hit me first' attitude ... the Hun very much reminds one of a small boy with half-a-brick in his hand, and a large window nearby. He'd love to do it if he could only be sure of getting away." On new awards: "Those who were soldiers before a certain date are getting a medal, others are getting more pay, and all are getting chevrons. How we've managed to sleep at night without chevrons all this time is one of the astounding features of the war. As a Tommy remarked when preparing to go over the top, 'If only I'd got me bloomin' chevrons I'd die 'appy.'" On the sub's raised pay: the "guileless sub will be able to adopt a more arrogant manner when talking to Mr. Cox," a parody verse ("When we were subs together; We drew but seven-and-six a day"), the memory of "the furtive approach, the pleading eye and the plaintive request for a small overdraft, 'only another tenner, I'm going over to-morrow,'" and "a lot of young Rothschilds for subs." The editors feel "left out in the cold." Copy from newcomers to the Division is welcomed "in real Oliver Twist style." Mechanism: the editorial still carries the yearly-spring gag and the Cox's bank cross-reference; the enemy's threatened offensive (rung bell, blown horn) is shrunk to a child's bluster; the chevron remark attaches a trivial wish to the moment of attack, and the overdraft before "going over to-morrow" shows how the ritual of going over was handled as a money matter. The editorial's date (26 February) is a few weeks before the German offensive of 21 March 1918 (gloss mine; the paper does not name it). Target: the army's award scheme, the sub's finances, the enemy's boasting.
*Candidate application:* An imminent big event can be minimized in the enemy's bluster and the men's own small, material wishes; the dying wish for a stripe is the deadpan form of "what do I actually want."

**[E10b-10]. "The Battle of Oxford Street" (verse by "Woolf"): mock-heroic ballad of the London food queues, addressed to the Germans.** (PW-01 L31313-31423.) Opens "Dear Germans list while I tell the tale / Of the awful battle in Oxford Street." Crowds fight with "all the food they'd bought"; "Mr. Selfridge fought with a leg of ham, / In the fearful battle of Oxford Street; / Snellgrove 'stopped' a large pot of jam" (using the front's word "stopped" for being hit); "Marshall walloped poor J. H. Evans / With a leg of mutton"; Scottish soldiers are fetched to quell it but "were not beguiled / To shoot." Ends: "Now, my dear little Huns just believe my tale ... And in future for food and peace don't wail, / Remember the horrors of Oxford Street. / And if you're hungry and tired of war, / Remember that William has food galore, / And a nice soft job, so think twice before, / You fight like they did in Oxford Street." Mechanism: front-line battle-report idiom and refrain turned on civilian food queues and department-store names; framed as a leaflet to the enemy that dissuades him from complaining about hunger by showing home is worse (and tells him the Kaiser eats well). Target: the home-front shortages and queues; the Kaiser's comfort; implicitly, the claim that the home front is its own battlefield.
*Candidate application:* Civilian hardship can be reported in the soldiers' own battle idiom, with the soldiers insisting it is serious and the civilians ridiculous at once.

**[E10b-11]. "Our Diary" by "Lieut. Samuel Pepys": the rumor of an enemy attack as unreliable gossip.** (PW-01 L31429-31483.) Pepys pastiche: "This week there has been much of rumor but, as always, rumor is not of a reliability. They do say that the Hun will come at us in numbers. I do not believe it though at times his foolishness is unbelievable." Then high play "at 'C's'" and the "house of Cox" in "a very bad way" thereby; "Anne" who "did fall in behind a lot of people for to buy sugar, and did wait five hours, then to find she was not in the right place, being indeed in some sort of singing hall"; the writer's wish that she not "adventure it at this time with many strange engines of war in the seas"; the fashion for dancing, "the G— himself did trip a measure"; and the day's close: "To-night I must take a party for the revetting so early to bed. My new man is of a poor intelligence, the dinner was bad, and the rum bottle had met with a misadventure on the way, so think I have the megrims." Mechanism: diary pastiche that sets rumor, home-front queues, submarine danger and staff dancing alongside the actual evening task (a revetting party) and the broken rum bottle (the standing bathos of E08-20). The rumor of a German attack is disbelieved in print five weeks before it happened.
*Candidate application:* The unit's reading of an expected enemy offensive can be printed as dismissive gossip, which carries both the wish that it not happen and the habit of discounting rumor.

**[E10b-12]. "Stick it": the morale verse that lists the ills.** (PW-01 L31493-31548.) Five-line stanzas, each ending "Stick it." Opening: "What matter though the wily Hun / With bomb, and gas and many a gun / In futile fury, lashes out, / Don't wonder what it's all about— 'Stick it.'" Others cover mud and cold ("When soaked in mud, half dead with cold, / You curse that you're a soldier bold"), working parties ("Don't curse the sergeant, 'taint his show, / The work's to do, just grin and [go]"), thin Belgian beer, a leaking billet, a parade that "busts all the lovely plans you've made," being shelled by day and bombed at night, a lively shirt (lice), thirst and hunger, and the close: "As someone said, there's no road yet / But had an end, your grinders set / On this one thing, that if you grin / And carry on, we're sure to win— 'Stick it.'" Mechanism: second-person exhortation by list, with the refrain as the instruction; nothing in it is turned into a joke except the Hun's "futile fury." It is the nearest thing in this range to a sincere morale text and the only verse that tells the reader what to do with the list of ills. No author is given.
*Candidate application:* A unit can carry a one-word instruction to itself (the refrain) that survives every list of hardships; an exhortation that cites specific ills (parade, billet, thirst) is more credible than a general one.

**[E10b-13]. "Sic Transit Gloria" (verse by "Minor Key"): leave as it was, and the home it now returns to.** (PW-01 L31557-31689.) The first stanzas mourn the old leave ("The magic word 'Leave' could inspire; / It was rest from our labors and change of employment"): clubs, tea shops, the Berkeley and Carlton, the Gaiety, with a line about devotion "to Bacchus or Venus; or both" (mild; paraphrased). Then "alas! These good times are now finally ended, / 'Tis Gilead without any balm— / (I trust that the Censor will not be offended / And think I am spreading alarm), / At every turn there's some warlike injunction, / And now they are using the tanks / To squat in the squares of our cities, and function / As banks"; "All food is controlled, and is eaten by order, / In rations minutely assigned"; the hoarder is "imprisoned and heavily fined"; "The food queues have tales which meander and wander / For miles where there's food to be had, / Defying all orders and driving Lord Rhondda / Quite mad"; the sugar card "One ounce and one seventh per day"; "The gold of the Indies can purchase no butter, / No tea finds its way to the cup; / When we go to the butcher's we find that the shutter / Is up." The last stanza: the thought of the departed pleasures does not make us glum, "We still roar when the question comes, 'Are we downhearted?' / 'Na Poo!'" Mechanism: nostalgia in a light verse meter, undercut stanza by stanza by rationing; the poet's censor aside; the refrain-question answered by the army's own slang for finished. Target: the home front's austerity and its rules (Lord Rhondda, the Food Controller, is gloss mine; "tanks as banks" refers to the war-loan tank tours, gloss mine). The shift from earlier leave jokes (the queue, the cancellation) is that leave itself now disappoints because home has been rationed.
*Candidate application:* The soldier's dream of home can be damaged by home itself, and the verse can stay cheerful by answering the army's cheer-leading question with its own slang.

**[E10b-14]. "Star-Spangled": an American soldier's slang letter home.** (PW-01 L31696-31804, signed "H. DAVIS," to "Mike.") A newcomer writes that he said the war must be over, but "the atmosphere is sultry with hunks of iron"; the steel helmet "coping" and the sense of needing "a good, active, paid-up, life insurance policy" without it; the helmet is described, in a blunt idiom that is paraphrased only, as not always stopping what it is meant to stop; the gas respirator ("chest-protector") is "as light as airy nothing" at the start of a march but after an hour feels as if someone slipped "a few hundred rounds of .303" into it; the gas drill ends with the face "in a bag" and sucking on "something like a nursing bottle ... only you don't get any milk"; a slang euphemism for dying of the enemy's gas after a few breaths is paraphrased only. A joke on chlorinated water: "This outfit, over here, isn't on the water-wagon like our Army is, these lads can get beer and things, they buy them you know. Well, some bright guy thought that if the water was doctored up a bit, well, maybe more beer would be bought. Do you get me?" Then the censor: "I got to cut out talking like that or the censor man will find another gob of ink to fresco my letter again." The close describes British officers: "some officers who stand very straight, and others who have the side elevation of a ? mark. Don't slip a cog on the fast guys like I did, they are not punk soldiers. These lads have been out in front and have bumped their brain-boxes often enough on the roof out here to make them curl up a bit." Mechanism: the outsider's stage-American voice (as with the dancer, E09b2-29) is used to re-describe the unit's own equipment and customs (helmet, respirator, gas drill, chlorinated water) as novelties, and to explain the stoop of the experienced officers as the effect of the trench roof. Target: the equipment; the water; the American newcomer's own error. Period caricature of American speech is documentation only.
*Candidate application:* A newcomer's naive letter can carry the veterans' own description of what the gear is for and what the experienced stoop means.

**[E10b-15]. The verse that denies the casualty claim ("You've not").** (PW-01 L31810-31870; heading and stanza order damaged by OCR.) A rhymed scolding of a unit that thinks it has done more than any other. Fragments: "Although you think you've had less rest / than any other div." ... "You've run the war for three long years / Come cheer up sonny, dry those tears— / You've not"; "Though you have battled everywhere, and had most fearful loss ... You say you've lost your thousands, but / Your figures you will find 'all phut' ... You talk of thousands you have lost— / You've not"; "Although you think you do more work than any other div, / And criticize the crowd that you relieve ... all are treated equally"; the moral "not unkindly meant": "Don't think that others get the cake / While you the roughest always take; / Remember when you so compare, / 'Tis of your team you're talking air— / So hot." Mechanism: a refrain that contradicts the claimant ("You've not"); the rebuke applies to the competition between divisions over who has suffered more, including a claim of casualty totals. Target: divisional self-pity and loss as a boast. This is the only item in the range that openly treats a casualty total as a claim to be argued (the verse says the figures are wrong), and it does so by deflating the claimant rather than the loss. Caution: the OCR scrambles line order, so which division is addressed and who is "relieved" is not established.
*Candidate application:* A unit's loss can be a currency it brags in, and the rule on the page is that comparing wounds is a hot-air contest.

**[E10b-16]. The Transport Officer's column: the editors' apology and four nursery-rhyme parodies.** (PW-01 L31876-31915.) A bracketed apology: "We must apologize for this column, and know our readers will forgive us, as it was written by our T.O. Its presence in the paper is due to the interference of war with the liberty of the press.—Ed." The rhymes: a shell-hit that was a dud ("Little Tom Buffet, / Thought he would snuff it / When hit on the chest with a shell; / The shell was a dud-un / So all of a sudden / He rose and is now doing well"); a "Baa, baa, black sheep" parody in which the rum is divided between the transport, the companies and the Quartermaster ("The rest is for the Q.M. / And all his merry boys"); "Dickory, dickory, dock" with "Fourteen days—F.P." (field punishment) as the price of "a bit of a spree"; a verse about a crocked horse and a veterinary officer; and a bayonet-fight rhyme in which a pun on a garment makes the thrust comic (paraphrased only, not quoted). Mechanism: nursery meter for grim or military content (as in E08); the reversal of a likely death by a dud shell; the Quartermaster takes the rest of the rum; the punishment as a nursery rhyme. The editors' apology turns the paper's shortage of copy into the war's interference with the press. Target: the Transport Officer, the Q.M., the field-punishment regime.
*Candidate application:* A near-death can be told as a nursery rhyme whose only turn is that the shell did not go off; the unit's own punishment (F.P.) can be sung as a rhyme.

**[E10b-17]. "The Reverse": if the poets ruled the language of war.** (PW-01 L31921-32046.) A continuation of "what might have been the result had some of the old poets lived in these days": a Tommy with a duckboard on the Menin Road speaking Hamlet's "To be or not to be?" in blank verse, interrupted by a sergeant ("Nahthen! Hop it quick or else, / You'll find some F.P. waiting you me lad"), and answering "Oh! Soft-blown zephyrs flavoured well with rum, / Waft o'er me when I see the sergeant come. (Moves On.)"; a platoon officer at Zero hour in Omar Khayyam quatrains ("Awake! For see the barrage lifts at last, / Take up your bombs. For us the die is cast / Come all together, top the parapet, / This moment holds the present, future, past"), whose second quatrain says either he fixes the Hun or the Hun fixes him; a Staff Officer "with a fat job and love of Browning" saying, as his car splashes you "from head to foot with Belgium's Best," "Take it, only take it ... You may have the car, dear, / Don't mind me. / I am but a red-hat, / Don't you see"; and a sergeant with "passion for Ella Wheeler Wilcox" who reasons that "There ain't enough for me and you / Of this 'ere rum, So 'taint no good / Of cutting it in two, / And so I'll drink the lot—narpooed; / The tin's for you." The editors' coda: "If this principle was adopted throughout the Army it would make things much easier for us all, and take the rough edges off life in the trenches." Mechanism: canonical verse imposed on a trench moment (as E08-33, E08-48), here framed as a conceit about language itself, with each form matched to the type who would use it (the sergeant, the staff officer, the subaltern at zero). The deadpan line "the die is cast" in the zero-hour verse is the only place in this item where attack and death are near. Target: sergeant's rum, staff car, the stock of the attack.
*Candidate application:* Fitting a grand form to a small act (a sergeant's rum swindle) shows how the unit hears the language of duty.

It also supplies an example of an old regular's claim to have served with Kitchener (compare E10b-42).

**[E10b-82]. "At Last": "Our Cavalry have crossed the Rhine."** (PW-01 L35857-35897.) A plain verse headed by a quoted newspaper line ("'Our Cavalry have crossed the Rhine'—Daily Paper"): "Our Cavalry have crossed the Rhine, / Ring out the bells with peal on peal, / Our Cavalry have crossed the Rhine, / Those words to all of us appeal; / No blatant music-hall refrain / Or ill-timed boast of what we'd do / To smash the Kaiser and his train, / And then to 'Wind a Watch Up' too. / Yet words which bridge four years of gloom / Which made us doubt the sun could shine, / Throughout the World—the message boom:— / Our Cavalry have crossed the Rhine." Mechanism: the repeated line as a bell-ring refrain; the reference to a pre-war music-hall song ("Watch on the Rhine" pun) as the blatant boasting the soldiers rejected; no joke. It dates the issue: the cavalry crossing of the Rhine was in the second half of December 1918 (my gloss), so the "Final Number" went to press around Christmas. Target: none; the piece is sincere and the only one in my range that quotes the press with approval.

**[E10b-83]. "Student's Corner," Lesson 2: nouns, proper and improper.** (PW-01 L35900-35995.) **[E10b-84]. "The Horrors of Peace": the armistice as a paper offensive.** (PW-01 L35997-36030.) "We have had a good look at the horrors of war, and now we are undergoing another sort of frightfulness. What a life! Can anyone tell us of a nice war where we could get work and so save our remaining hair from an early grayness? 11.00 hours on the eleventh of November was zero hour, and the redhats attacked in mass. The barrage of paper fell right on our trenches, and mixed with the H.E. was gas in enormous quantities. The supports were rushed into the orderly room in time to save the line from giving. Numbers are against us. Also the disposition of the enemy is much in his favor. His left flank rests on Education, and his right on Recreation. He has carefully selected shock-masses of 'Returns,' and with these is rapidly undermining our morale. We are taking up a defensive position in various towns, and there we are going to hold on at all costs. Meanwhile we are drawing up a list of our 'fourteen points,' and these may form the basis of a suitable armistice. As I remarked before, if anyone knows of a nice war, or if one can be arranged, we hope we shall be allowed first call." Mechanism: a battle report in which the Armistice hour is the zero hour for the staff's peacetime paperwork (the "redhats" as attacking troops, forms as shells and gas, "Returns" as shock troops, the army's education and recreation schemes as the enemy's flanks), a defensive stand "at all costs" in the towns, and a counter-list of "fourteen points" (an allusion to President Wilson's list; gloss mine). The first and last sentences ask, as a joke, for "a nice war where we could get work." Target: the army's peacetime administration and the staff. This is the single most pointed gallows reversal in my range: the war is wished back as a place where the writer had work and a role, while the peace is the enemy. The paper's tone toward the Armistice here is not relief but the transfer of the war's metaphors to the staff.
*Candidate application:* The first thing to be mocked after the end is the staff's new offensive of forms, and the wish for "a nice war" is said as a joke about work but exposes what the war's structure gave the men.

**[E10b-85]. "The Orderly Room of Other Times," No. 2: "With the Ironsides."** (PW-01 L36045-36170.) The second historical orderly-room scene (E10b-37). "Oliver Rumwell" walks up and down "with a spare mace in his hand, muttering 'Bauble! Bauble!'" while his adjutant "Praise-the-Lord-Good-and-Plenty" asks him to hear "one of your Ironsides, one Sing-Hymns-Loud-and-Lusty, who hath erred by lying long on his pallet." Target: reveille discipline, the commander's drinking, forced cheerfulness (the enforced song). The two scenes together present the orderly room as an institution whose punishments are the same in any age.
*Candidate application:* Compulsory enthusiasm can be written as a man singing his loyalty under a sergeant's sword, with the sentence taken in the same breath.

**[E10b-86]. "Have you voted yet?": the election-pamphlet ad.** (PW-01 L36173-36194.) "HAVE YOU VOTED YET? If not, send for our pamphlet, HOW TO VOTE AND WHY! WHO WON THE WAR? RAMSEY, MACDONALD & CO., Election Publicity & Advice Agency, Leicester Square, London." Mechanism: a one-line advertising joke on the December 1918 general election (my gloss; the date is not in the ad), with the firm's name echoing the pacifist politician already mocked as "MacBonald" (E09b2-24) and the question "WHO WON THE WAR?" as a tagline. Target: the politicians and the election's war claims. The paper, in its last number, shows soldiers being asked to vote; the item is the only election item in my range.

**[E10b-87]. The serial's last chapter: everyone is disposed of "in this number."** (PW-01 L36200-36379.) The feuilleton "Virtue Wins; or, The 100-1 Chance" (E10b-54) is reprinted with its cast list and finished in Chapter 2 within a single page. The gambler Ambrose, at a crown-and-anchor board in his flat, loses everything, kills the gambler Rugenoir and then himself (summarized; the chapter's wording is not reproduced); the baronet Sir Marmaduke dies of "a sudden and painful malady" with Sybil, Estelle and the butler at his bed. The next sentences are the most compressed lines of the serial: "Seeing that all was over, they gave way to uncontrolled grief and went down to tea. Handling: the several deaths in this chapter are described in summary only.
*Candidate application:* When a unit's story has to be finished, the comic way is to give every character a one-line fate and a moral; the actual dead of the war stay out of the serial.

**[E10b-92]. The last advertisement and the last words: "COME ON, RISK IT."** (PW-01 L36630-36651.) The last printed item before the printer's line is an ad for surplus goods, with a picture: "I have a large consignment of bundles as per design. As I cannot tell from either the design or the commodity whether they are bundles of cigars or bundles of asparagus, I am offering them at seven bundles for a pound. COME ON, RISK IT." Then: "Printed & Published by Sherwood, Forester & Co., Ltd. B.E.F." Mechanism: the last ad sells an unidentified thing at a fixed price on the buyer's risk; the paper's final words are the advertiser's dare, and the printer's credit follows. There is no closing editorial, no "au revoir," no list of titles and no statement of what the paper had been for; the editors' goodbye is the "Bon Chance" note in the middle of the issue (E10b-80), and the paper stops on a joke. Target: the surplus trade; the buyer's blindness.
*Candidate application:* The paper's last line is a dare with no explanation, and the goodbye proper is placed elsewhere and in business language; a unit's closing can be an ordinary piece of filler that happens to be last.

## Pattern roll-up

**What the final eight months of the paper establish (my range, 22 January to December 1918).** The humor continues its established mix (mock ads, mock bills, the correspondent, canonical verse, serials, answers to correspondents) and picks up three new subjects: the *home front's rationing and queues* (before the German offensive), the *end of the war and demobilization*, and the *paper's own end*. The Armistice itself is treated at five removes: the biblical billeting dream, the Tomar-Sat-Kins verse, the armistice-table scoop by a spaniel-disguised correspondent, the "horrors of peace" paper offensive, and the plain poem. The editors' plain statements are brief and concentrated in two places: the Better Times No. 1 postscript (E10b-46) and the Final Number editorial and "Bon Chance" note (E10b-69, E10b-70, E10b-80).

**Catalog of NEW devices in my range (earlier devices from E08, E09a and E09b2 continue and are not re-listed; finding numbers in brackets).**
1. **The testimonial in which the enemy treats the unit's trench as a Staff College** and so does not shoot [E10b-1].

5. **The manufactured battlefield souvenir with a price scale** [E10b-8].
6. **The overdraft request before "going over to-morrow"** and **the Tommy's last wish for a stripe** [E10b-9].

**Functions.** *Coping:* the sub's overdraft, the chevron wish, the Q.M. in the shell lecture and the quartermasters in Heaven [E10b-7, E10b-9, E10b-44]; the notice-and-rules fantasy that makes shelling a sport [E10b-21]; the Tommy's pipe in the captured dugout [E10b-22]; the verse in nursery meter where the shell is a dud [E10b-16]. *Cohesion:* the standing cast (Cox's bank, Teech Bomas, the Q.M.), the masthead roll of absorbed titles, the printers' regiment on every billet [E10b-5, E10b-44, E10b-64]; the farewell verse to the American doctor [E10b-56]; the editors' thanks [E10b-80]. *Boundary:* the home front's queues and rationing [E10b-10, E10b-13]; the slacker [E10b-4]; the press (Teech Bomas ×2, the Russian correspondent) [E10b-57, E10b-72, E10b-78]; the new rear-area staff and the "redhats" of peace [E10b-18, E10b-84]; the "later fighters" claiming a star [E10b-50, E10b-59]. *Resistance upward:* the Staff College testimonial, the general's patter song, the sentry's wink, the Corps Commander's ribbon flap, the Ironsides' coerced song [E10b-1, E10b-45, E10b-79, E10b-85, E10b-88]. *Grief:* only in the plain register: "reverence and love," "Jim and George," "God send that we may forget" [E10b-46, E10b-49]; the paper's own loss as obsequies [E10b-74]. *Morale:* the "Stick it" verse; the clown's job as stated in "Our Second Time on Earth"; the explicit statement that the end was met with apathy and that the line men know the war for "the vilest disaster" [E10b-12, E10b-69, E10b-74]. *Identity:* the song of the "blue-clad mobs"; "the same old grouse" [E10b-69].

**Targets.** *Staff, rear-area administration and brass:* the Staff College testimonial, Area Commandant and Town Major, the Corps Commander's patter song, the sentry ballad, the dug-outs, the redhats of peace, the poker pupil [E10b-1, E10b-18, E10b-45, E10b-63, E10b-79, E10b-81, E10b-84]. *The home front:* queues and rations (the Oxford Street verse; "Sic Transit Gloria"), the slacker and tribunals, souvenir buyers, parlor games, the election [E10b-4, E10b-8, E10b-10, E10b-13, E10b-86, E10b-88]. *The press:* Teech Bomas (breakthrough, armistice), the Russian special correspondent, the Daily Paper's cavalry line [E10b-57, E10b-72, E10b-78, E10b-82]. *The enemy:* as a bully with a half-brick, a removals firm, an "also ran," a delegation that faints, an exiled seller of surplus machinery, and the earnest hate in "Aux Repatriés" [E10b-9, E10b-52, E10b-61, E10b-63, E10b-78, E10b-89]. *Ranks and rewards:* chevrons, stars, ribbons, prize money [E10b-9, E10b-50, E10b-59, E10b-60, E10b-88, E10b-91]. *The paper itself:* the rigged competitions, the copy plea, the "touting for copy" ad, the editors' own pay [E10b-25, E10b-34, E10b-51, E10b-70]. *Allies:* the American newcomer's letter, the American doctor, the American pipe ad, the "doubtful starter" [E10b-14, E10b-56, E10b-61, E10b-68]. *The Russians and the intervention* (a dehumanizing item; see Limits) [E10b-72].

**Forms and mechanics.** Mock advertising (testimonial, rival firm, surplus sale, correspondence course, resort and lecture bills); theater bill with named generals and leaders as acts; mock correspondent's dispatch with flat last sentences; diary pastiche (Pepys); mock rule book; scriptural pastiche (twice); historical orderly-room scenes in Shakespearean and Puritan voices; comic-opera patter song; ballad with a wink for its last stanza; nursery-rhyme parody; stock-letter verse ("in the pink"); sentimental-ballad bathos ("Memories"); phrase-book parody; mock-translation not present; turf report; limerick; letters to the editor with addresses as jokes; the answers column with demobilization questions; the serial with cast list, ritual cliffhanger and then a one-page kill-everybody finish; the plain poem built from catchphrases as section heads; and the bracketed editor's aside ("This is a family journal"; "it had to be finished in this number").

**Rules of license (as visible).** (1) Seniors are teased by role or invented name (Sir Chloride H.K.L.M. Bunter; the Area Commandant; the Corps Commander's wife) and the Staff's own errors are shown at their worst by dialogue and verse [E10b-18, E10b-79, E10b-88]. (2) The editors may mark their own daring with a bracketed disclaimer and a nod to the censor, which still appears in this range ("I trust that the Censor will not be offended"; "the censor man will find another gob of ink") [E10b-13, E10b-14]. (3) Casualty claims are not to be competed: the verse that says "You've not" [E10b-15]. (4) The war's real dead are named only in plain-register lines ("reverence and love," "lie in France and Belgium") and never in the jokes [E10b-46, E10b-49]. (5) Outsiders (Americans, Russians, the wives and the enemy) may be given the voice the paper cannot use for itself, and the Americans' letters keep a stage dialect [E10b-14, E10b-68, E10b-72]. (6) Hatred of the enemy is expressed in earnest only by the padre's verse and the 1918 Christmas verse's "curse the Hun" [E10b-52, E10b-71]. (7) The editor may cut off a joke about sex or vice with a "family journal" remark [E10b-83]. (8) The paper may joke about its own staff's shortage and the demob of its printers, and may end without a ceremony [E10b-80, E10b-92].

**Limits and failure modes (what could not be joked about, and where the humor fails).**
- **The dead and the wound.** In this range no named person who died is made funny. The dead are named only in the editorial ("those who lie in France and Belgium") and, in the plain poem, as "Jim and George, your pals" and "the corpses" on the roads (paraphrased). The fictional dead of the serials are disposed of in a clause (E10b-87) or by a noise (E10b-54), which is the paper's formula for keeping its fiction away from its real losses. The wounded man's course is told in plain verse, not a joke (E10b-75).
- **The offensive's casualties and the Armistice.** The Armistice is never told as a joke on the soldier. The jokes are on the correspondent, the delegates and the staff. The poem that tells it directly says that the soldiers did not believe it, that the column plodded on and that small wants outweighed empires (E10b-49). The editors report "apathy" (E10b-69).
- **A joke that shades into cruelty.** The Russia dispatch reduces a massacre to an animal and a killed man to a punchline; the same item treats the intervention as a place to shoot (E10b-72). The paper treats this as comedy and does not comment; the item is a place where the technique of casual violence by a pretend correspondent runs into a real population it did not know. It is also the last item in the paper to use the technique at length.
- **The real "wish for a war."** E10b-84 ("Can anyone tell us of a nice war where we could get work") shows that when the war ended the writers felt its absence, and the joke is the only vehicle for saying so.
- **The paper's end.** The end of the paper is not given the paper's usual joke form: it is stated administratively (demobilization of miners among the printing staff, "the order of the bowler hat"), thanks are given, and nothing else is said (E10b-80).

**Time course (tone timeline, 22 January to December 1918).**
- *22 January 1918 (Vol. 2 No. 5, back page only):* the advertisements are old-style: camouflage, balloon tours, the sketching craze, the skill of waiting [E10b-1 to E10b-4]. The rank target is the Staff College. The war is not named as a subject.
- *26 February 1918 (Vol. 2 No. 6, the last issue before the long gap):* the paper is in its third winter. The editorial's yearly spring song is held back; the Hun is a small boy with a half-brick; pay rises and chevrons are the news; rumors of an enemy attack are disbelieved in a Pepys pastiche and passed along as "ghastly tales" in a leave-junction sonnet; the home front is a battle of queues; leave itself has lost its appeal because home is rationed; the Americans are arriving; "Stick it" is exhorted; and the last serial of the old run ends on a cliffhanger [E10b-9 to E10b-23]. The paper ends this issue with ads for beautifying and soap (lice) and a promise of nothing.
- *March to autumn 1918:* no issue appears. The editorial of November says the old press was "lost last March" and that the new press was found in an advance [E10b-36]. Nothing in the pages states what happened to the paper's staff in the interval except "many regrettable vacancies."
- *November 1918 (Better Times No. 1):* a revival on a captured press; the theater bill now carries Foch and the Kaiser in exile; ads for the army of occupation and for closing camps; the first insurance joke about a condemned man; Teech Bomas on the breakthrough; the Armistice as a billeting dream; the postscript editorial states relief ("glad the ghastly affair is well over"), the best part of the war as the last two months, and the dead [E10b-30 to E10b-52]. Plain pieces increase: "Aux Repatriés," the Armistice poem and the editors' paragraph on the dead. The new answers column asks about money, stars and the order of demobilization [E10b-50]. The C.O.'s forebodings introduce the transition out of service [E10b-48].
- *December 1918 (the "Xmas, Peace and Final Number"):* an issue about the aftermath: "The Fifth and Last" Christmas verse, the Rhine crossing, the election, the pay and rank claims, the staff's paper offensive; the plain statement of apathy and the "vilest disaster"; the editors' thanks; the serial dispatched in a single page. Humor is now more about peacetime institutions (education schemes, returns, dances, ribbons) than about the front. The paper's last word is a surplus-sale dare.
- *Overall:* from February to December the humor's objects move from home-front shortages and the Staff, to the new Allied and enemy leaders as stage turns, to demobilization, medals and peacetime bureaucracy; the plain register thickens at the end; and the editors say outright what the humor had not (the war as "the vilest disaster"; the dead "never forgotten"; the regrets of leaving the army) only after the guns stop.

**The end of the paper (summary of what the pages show).** (a) *The goodbye:* a short business note headed "Bon Chance," signed by the editor F. J. Roberts and the sub-editor J. H. Pearson, mid-issue, giving demobilization of the miners among the printers as the cause and wishing "the best wishes for Xmas and the future" [E10b-80]. (b) *The editorial of the same issue:* no goodbye; a plan for a weekly of 1,000 copies and a possible daily [E10b-70]. (c) *The cover:* "Xmas, Peace and Final Number" [E10b-64]. (d) *The serial:* closed in one page with the editor's note that it had to be finished in the number [E10b-87]. (e) *The last printed words:* an advertiser's "COME ON, RISK IT." followed by the printer's credit [E10b-92]. (f) *No 1930 afterword, index or appendix follows in this file;* the omnibus's own prefaces are at the front (E08). (g) *What the editors say about the paper's function:* "bidding smiles take the place of your tears" (pantomime clown) [E10b-74]; "shy and retiring subaltern" and "copy must roll in" [E10b-70]; no statement on morale beyond that.

**Civilian-facing register.** The home front is seen in this range through its rationing (sugar cards, queues, Lord Rhondda), its shops (Oxford Street), its department-store names, its Christmas puddings and parlor games, its election and its dances; and as the audience for souvenirs and for the "Star" ribbon in a cerise blouse [E10b-8, E10b-10, E10b-13, E10b-86, E10b-88, E10b-90]. At the end the civil-service and clerical figures (Ministry of Fancywork, Hotel Velvet) are the ones who "told our friends to enlist" and claim credit [E10b-59]. The conditions of the home are described as worse, but not more dangerous, than the front's.

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** There is no dissent against the war or the army as such. Two items bear on leaving service: the C.O.'s forebodings about subordinates "plunged from war to the world" and the guardroom as a remembered haven [E10b-48]; and the editorial's regrets about reverting to civvies, "tinged with many regrets" [E10b-69]. The verse "Memories" and the bowler-hat note put the civilian life in view as a comic space [E10b-77, E10b-80]. The inverted-rank verse imagines private soldiers instructing colonels, and then rebukes its speaker [E10b-41]. The unit's humor does not address a person who left on principle or without leave. The enemy leader in exile (the Kaiser selling a machine from his "Retreat") is the one exile in the pages [E10b-89].

**Cross-cultural.** One army, one division's paper, 1918. American voices appear three times (the soldier's letter, the Yankee doctor, the pipe ad), always as parody or affectionate outsider; French is used for small phrases; Russian and German are mock-languages. Do not generalize.

**Craft observations (editors' own and by inference).** Let the last line be a flat statement or a dare (the paper's own last words). Put the real feeling in a different register and sign it separately (the padre's verse; the Armistice poem; the editorial postscript). Use section heads made of the phrases soldiers actually heard. End a long mock-heroic piece with a plain couplet that turns on the speaker (E10b-41). When a serial must end, kill the cast in one page and say why in a bracket. Put the death of a man in a limerick only when the form's last line can carry it ("He never will do it again"). Make the departure of a colleague a rhyme about what he did for the unit's mood (the Yankee doctor). Keep a running cast (Cox, Teech Bomas, the Q.M., Reggie) and use them in the paper's last number so that the reader sees the old world still there.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Range and boundaries.** I read L30,880-37,163 in full (I began at L30,880 to see the start of the back page I was assigned from L30,901). My range begins inside the back-page ads of Vol. 2 No. 5 (22 January 1918; masthead at L29175, not mine). It contains: the last part of that issue's ads (L30901-31060); Vol. 2 No. 6 (26 February 1918; L31063-32812); The Better Times No. 1 (November 1918; L32814-34739); the Better Times "Xmas, Peace and Final Number" (December 1918; L34742-36651); and about 500 lines of scan debris and library slips after the printer's credit (L36652-37163), including "Digitized by Google" pages and a UC Berkeley library due-date slip. **There is no 1930 afterword, index, appendix, publisher's note or back matter after the last issue in this file;** the 1930 matter (Plumer's foreword; "Twelve Years After") is at the front (E08).
- **Gap between No. 6 and the Better Times.** No issue between 26 February and November 1918 is reproduced. I infer from the November editorial that the press was "lost last March" (the German offensive, my gloss) and the paper lapsed for about nine months; the omnibus does not say what happened.
- **Volume and number labels are inconsistent in the OCR.** The Better Times No. 1 reads "Vol. A" (L32825); No. 2 reads "Vol. 4" at L34756 and "Vol. 1" at L34920; the cover says "Xmas, Peace [and] Final [Number]"; the October-November dating of No. 1 is a single word, "November, 1918." I did not resolve the numbering.
- **Whether the title block of the Final Number names "The Better Times."** The OCR shows the title block twice, with the order of lines scrambled (L34745, L34909); I could not confirm that the last issue is titled "The Better Times." The cover title is damaged.
- **"Final" is on the cover and in the editors' note, but the editorial of the same issue plans a weekly.** The two statements are consistent only if the editors learned of the demobilization of the printers after writing the editorial (the note at L35719 says "this is our last number"). This is my reading; the pages do not explain the order of writing.
- **Datings are mine.** The Rhine crossing (mid-December 1918), the election (14 December), the Armistice (11 November), the German offensive (21 March 1918), the Hindenburg Line breakthrough and Valenciennes, the Canadians at Mons, the Murman landings, the opening of the "Star" award and demobilization priority for miners are all from general knowledge and flagged where used; the paper names none of them except the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month and the Rhine.
- **OCR damage.** Numerals (prices, prize sums, insurance sums, "Vol." numbers, the "11" in "11 a.m." which reads "44" at L33923); titles of several items ("Eye-Wash," the Forebodings piece, the post-war rank verse, "[Fame?]: A True Story," the "Demobilization" ad, the Alberta verse, "Tricolor" is clean); the Russian text of the lessons (illegible); the cartoon captions; the title block of the Final Number; and page furniture. Where I quoted a damaged word I bracketed it or noted the OCR reading. British spellings in quotations were converted to American per the project rule (for example "plowshares," "gray," "jail," "demobilized," "neighborhood"), so quotation spellings are not letter-for-letter.
- **Identifications are glosses.** "Teech Bomas," "Cox," "Kewem," "Artoco," "Ackpipemmus," "Korque," "Bra-Sat," "Sherwood Foresters" (the printers' regiment; the paper's printer's line uses it as a firm name "Sherwood, Forester & Co."), "Plumer and Co.," "Ronder," "Boche," "Fritz," "Alphonse," "Jonathan," "K. A. Iser," "Govie," "T.O.," "Tights Old Car Soap," "Windigul," "Cabajo" and the period slang "napoo," "compree," "narpooed," "pip-squeak," "sausage" are explained from general knowledge or inference. Several are uncertain: the reading of "Kewem" as the quartermaster; the staff abbreviations in the billeting dream; "Wotto, no tarf" (E10b-47); "Hopsogay" (a play on "hop"); and "Gallawalla."
- **Authorship.** The only names given with authority are the editors F. J. Roberts and J. H. Pearson (L35732-35735). Pen names (Woolf, Minor Key, Samuel Pepys, C.L.P., Teech Bomas, Alberta, O.F., R.W.M., Gould Garvice, The Padre, J.C., H. Davis, "Three Pips," Cuthbert Cushionbe, A. E. G. Beams, J. M. Wangleur, O. Mye-Molars, A True Contemptible, William Woolfit, B.F., Ingenuity, A Contemptible's Wife) cannot be tied to people. The ranks speak through officer-written voices; the American letter signed "H. Davis" may be written by a Briton imitating an American (my inference; the paper's habit in E09b2-29).
- **Period stereotypes.** The pages use national and gender caricature (stage-American speech in three items; mock-German and mock-French for the delegates and the Kaiser's exile; "Hun," "Boche," "Fritz"; the "Native Labor Company" as a faceless object; a Russia piece that dehumanizes its population). This is documentation of one army's period humor and is not material for characterizing any Tepenian people (see the project's "No National Stereotypes" law).
- **Unverified claims.** Every report in the paper's own voice (the press found at a captured town, the Avesnes Advertiser, the race meeting, the "1,000 copies") is the paper's assertion.
- **Minor items not extracted separately.** The "Johnny Sapps. —No." answer line (E10b-24, mentioned); some ornament and cartoon captions; the three prize-column tags; OCR-only fragments at L30880-30900, L32476-32480 (cast list), L33070-33080 (a damaged signature), L34917-34928 (title block), and the UC Berkeley library slips at L36679-36706.
- **Length.** Findings run well above 10-18 percent of the source's words because the source is public-domain primary text in which the quoted example is the evidence and the OCR text is mostly white space; findings that repeat a device are cross-referenced.
- **Candidate applications** are structural suggestions only; this source documents British WWI officers' humor and is not a template for any Tepenian faction.
