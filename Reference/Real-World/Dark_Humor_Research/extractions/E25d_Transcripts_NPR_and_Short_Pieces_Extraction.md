**MV-B-055 This American Life #280, "In Country" [BCAST].** A produced public-radio hour (about 10,100 words in the copy; aired 2005-01-07, nearly two years into the Iraq war). It has four parts: a **Prologue** (host Ira Glass and comedian Tom Irwin, back from a month entertaining troops in the Sunni Triangle, about a joke that "would kill" with troops; L11-43); **Act One, "When Weekend Warriors Work On Weekdays"** (L45-751), built from the documentary series *Off to War* by filmmakers Craig and Brent Renaud, following about 57 Arkansas National Guard soldiers from Clarksville (an engineer unit within the 39th Brigade, deployed spring 2004; reservists are about 40% of troops in Iraq per the host) from call-up and training through Kuwait and Taji, with phone interviews of soldiers Matt Hertlein (19), Sgt. David Short and Sgt. Joe Betts (42, a minister, re-enlisted involuntarily); and **Act Two, "Marine Life"** (L753-1009), a satellite-phone call between Marine 1st Lt. Rob Miller (age 30; weapons platoon, Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 8th Marines) and his uncle, contributor Jack Hitt, recorded just after the November 2004 battle of Fallujah. There is a short credit tag (L1011-1035). **Honest limits, stated first:** (1) the humor content is **thinner than the episode's own framing promises**: the Prologue announces that "we do not even understand their jokes," but after the Prologue the soldiers' joking appears only in scattered lines, and most of the hour is plain testimony about training, armor and fear; (2) **the host's and filmmakers' wit is not the soldiers' wit**: several comic descriptions of the training footage are the host's narration (labeled below); (3) much of Act One is secondhand (the filmmakers relay what friends said and did); (4) there is **no reflective account of having killed**: killing appears only as operational reporting, a retributive vow after a comrade's death, and a pre-deployment statement of willingness, all summarized under the register below; (5) nothing here shows how the soldiers' humor changed after service: the speakers are in-theater (Miller, Short, Hertlein) or just sent home (Betts); (6) the Prologue's own tease ("what insurgents play over loudspeakers... what Americans play") is delivered only through Miller's anecdotes. Speaker names are as printed; **Irwin's "Army veteran" status is the project index's description, not stated in this transcript**. Credits, funding boilerplate and an audio-sync toggle were skipped.

## Findings

### From MV-B-055 (This American Life #280, "In Country") [BCAST]

**[E25d-1]. [BCAST] The joke that "kills" in theater and what the studio hears.** (MV-B-055, Prologue, L15-43)
The host introduces the episode with one joke, relayed by comedian Tom Irwin (host's account: Irwin spent over a month entertaining troops in Fallujah, Mosul, near the Iranian border and elsewhere). The joke, in Irwin's wording as printed (L19), praises the Black Hawk helicopter and its pilots, then turns: the one group who hate the helicopters are Iraqi sheep farmers. The host says it "would kill," and Irwin confirms it is "very strong" (L27). Irwin explains why in the speaker's own terms: Black Hawks are how everyone gets around; they fly very low, so the crew can see people and watch animals scatter. The gag works through a witnessed detail common to every listener on a base, and its target is the troops' own aircraft's side effect on a civilian livelihood, not any harm to a person. Irwin stalls while describing it in the studio (L27-29): they say they are "so conditioned to have it have a different reaction," and then, "sitting in this studio," call it "the greatest bad reaction I've ever heard." The text copy cannot settle whether that last phrase means the studio's flat response or the troops' reaction recalled by contrast; either way Irwin is registering the gap between the two rooms. The host's frame follows (L33-37): listeners cannot understand soldiers' day-to-day habits, "what annoys them most," or "even understand their jokes," and Irwin adds that "you have to be in Iraq, sort of." Evidence base for the claim: one comedian's tour, one joke, and the host's own reaction; no audience data.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's best-landing jokes may depend on a shared, mundane, witnessed detail that an outsider hears as flat or mildly sinister; the comic effect lives in the audience's recognition, not the premise. The speaker's own "conditioned" reaction (his sense of what lands) can be recalibrated only by returning to civilian rooms.
*Cross-use:* Any faction defined by a shared technical environment (flight crews, orbital workers, polar crews) could carry equivalent in-jokes built on a machine's by-products.

**[E25d-2]. [BCAST] The same joke fails with a deployed Marine over the phone.** (MV-B-055, Act Two, L933-961)
Near the close of Act Two, Jack Hitt tells Marine Lt. Rob Miller the helicopter joke (setup as a riddle: "you know who hates them?"). Miller answers it back as a question, then, after repeating the punch ("Iraqi sheep farmers hate Black Hawks"), says, "I don't get it" (L945). The Prologue's implication (that deployed troops get it) is therefore **not borne out by this exchange**. The source does not say why. Three readings are possible and none is supported: a poor secondhand telling over a satellite link, a joke that depends on riding in helicopters (Miller is a ground officer in a Marine infantry company in Fallujah), or Miller's mind on other things. The comedian's tour audiences are described by Irwin, not tested here.
*Candidate application:* "Deployed" is not one audience; ground troops and aviation-riding troops can have different in-jokes, and a joke retold by a civilian may die in transit. Useful to make a character's jokes unit-specific and occupation-specific rather than generic soldier humor.

**[E25d-3]. [BCAST] Who gets the comedian, and the field unit's resentment.** (MV-B-055, Act Two, L949-961)
Told that Tom Irwin has been touring, Miller (a Marine lieutenant, speaking of his platoon) says Irwin was "out here," and "all the pogues got to watch him, because everybody else was in the field" (L953). "Pogue" is, in the usual meaning (the transcript does not define it), slang for rear-area personnel. Miller adds, of his men: "It burns up all my boys. And they always get ticked off at that kind of stuff. They're like, why are they doing a show for them? They're not getting shot at" (L961). The "That was pretty funny" in the same line is ambiguous (either the entertainment or the irony of who got to see it). So the earned-right idea shows up in a different guise: the troops resent entertainment delivered to people who have not paid the field's price. The comedian himself says nothing about this in the transcript.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character may resent morale provision aimed at rear-area audiences; whom a show is *for* can sort the unit. His humor may be reserved for those who share his exposure.

**[E25d-4]. [BCAST] Training-ground laughter read as disbelief, not coping.** (MV-B-055, Act One, L177-271, L575)
The documentary's training footage (Fort Hood, Fort Polk; six months) shows middle-aged, out-of-shape reservists, narrated by the host in a comic register (see [E25d-14]). One soldier's own joke is on camera: asked to put his belly in the shot, he says it will be gone by the time he gets home (L193). The host later notes that in-country the soldiers do lose weight (L427), so the self-mocking prediction comes true. The more telling line is Sgt. Joe Betts's own account of what he saw that made him say many soldiers were not taking training seriously: "The laughter," with people sitting around saying this is not what will happen over there (L271). Filmmaker Craig Renaud says something similar of the engineer unit he followed: "a lot of guys weren't taking it that seriously" until the first death (L575). In both accounts, laughter and banter before contact are read by a sergeant and a filmmaker as unserious disbelief, the opposite of the coping function. This is a minister-sergeant's and a filmmaker's interpretation of a reserve unit in training, not a measurement. **The source does not say whether joking changed after the first deaths**, only that "everybody's demeanor changed" (L575).
*Candidate application:* Pre-contact joking and mid-contact joking are different things; a hardened character may recall his early laughter as a naive stage. A leader character may read laughter as a warning sign.

**[E25d-5]. [BCAST] Two 19-year-olds: blame-banter, the "camping trip" line, and a hometown joke.** (MV-B-055, Act One, L99-173, L125)
In footage from the Kuwait desert, Matt Hertlein describes eating from a brown plastic sack "on probably the most expensive camping trip America has ever seen" (L101), and his friend Tommy Erp turns on the pair's reserve bargain ("What happened to one weekend a month, two weeks a year?", L105). The two then trade accusations of who talked whom into joining: Hertlein says the man he is today is thanks to Erp, who sold the Guard as drinking on weekends and partying in college; Erp answers with sarcasm about how much fun Hertlein is having in college; Hertlein's comeback is a jab at Erp's registration (L111-121). The filmmaker adds a hometown detail: at the last party before deployment, their friends joked about not being able to wait for them to leave, because there would be more jobs for those who stayed (L125). The pre-deployment exchange on the couch ("I think it'll be fun") is quoted at L133-137; asked about it nine months later, Hertlein says "it doesn't even seem like me saying it" and that he is "not even watching myself" (L173). What the exchange documents: buddy enlistment, reciprocal blame as an in-group habit, understatement about the cost of the bargain (the "camping trip"), and, via the filmmaker, a community's own dark joke about the economics of who is sent away. The hometown party joke is **secondhand** (the filmmaker is relaying).
*Candidate application:* Friends who enlisted together can run an old, affectionate blame-game that survives the war; a community can joke about the opportunity cost of its own young men leaving. The distance between "it'll be fun" and the later self is itself a documented marker of time in theater.

**[E25d-6]. [BCAST] "A scene out of The A-Team," then the plain grievance.** (MV-B-055, Act One, L435-515)
The Arkansas engineer unit had 4 vehicles armored of 42 (host, L435), and the soldiers scavenged scrap steel and old vests to bolt onto truck doors. Filmmaker Craig Renaud says his own convoy ride into Iraq was in a vehicle with a pine-box frame and sandbags that they hoped might slow bullets, not stop them (L439). A soldier on camera says they were promised up-armor kits and did not get them, so "we're going to go ahead and try to fabricate something" (L443). Asked by the host about the footage of himself welding an old vest to a door, Hertlein says it was "like a scene out of The A-Team" (a popular American television series about improvised vehicle armor and gadgets), the transcript marks laughter, and he immediately turns plain: "I think we're just on the bottom of the list. Priority goes to the regular Army, and the Marine Corps... We just get whatever's left over" (L471-475). Sgt. Betts, asked if it made everyone mad, answers flatly "Of course that make people mad" (L491), then rebuts Defense Secretary Rumsfeld's "you go to war with the army you have" line by saying the military knew months ahead and calling the lack of armor a message that "I don't care about the soldiers" (L507-515). The host's own irony ("the most powerful army in the history of man scrounging in scrap heaps," L459) is the host's, not the soldiers'. Mechanism: a popular-culture frame makes an improvised danger tellable, then yields at once to a statement about rank in the supply chain; the **anger** at the institution is voiced without any joke.
*Candidate application:* A veteran from a low-priority component can reach for a film or TV reference to make a dangerous improvisation tellable, and the same person says the real grievance in a flat voice with no joke attached. Reserve and guard status as a source of institutional resentment is documented here for one unit.

**[E25d-7]. [BCAST] Training-exercise wryness: the observers' remark.** (MV-B-055, Act One, L321-341)
During the Fort Polk exercise, Iraqi-American role-players speak only Arabic; in the convoy scenario the soldiers misread a protest over meat prices as political, and the commander is "killed" in the simulation (L327-333). The host's description of the scene: officers and trainers stroll through the smoke and the wailing role-players "saying things like, well, I guess that'll teach them not to leave their vehicles unattended" (L341). This is a trainers' dry remark about the exercise's own casualties, a gallows-style institutional understatement from the people running the simulation rather than the trainees. The transcript gives only that one example, relayed by the host.
*Candidate application:* The institution's instructors can have their own deadpan register about the failures of the people they train; a recruit hears institutional humor aimed at him before he ever sees combat.

**[E25d-8]. [BCAST] The first days of fear, and the leader who does not joke.** (MV-B-055, Act One, L539-621)
On the convoy into Baghdad, Betts's flat commentary on arrival is dry: "I already know I hate Baghdad," then, as a mortar round is fired at the base, "Welcome home, guys... 1 day down, 364 days to go" (L543-551). A soldier is killed within ten hours of arrival (rocket attack), a second two days later in an ambush; the filmmaker saw a medic come back covered in blood (L575). Sgt. Short stands before the damaged vehicle with his men and says that this is very hard for him because he loves every one of them, and that he wants them to realize it is real; he then pledges to go after the people responsible (retributive vow; restrained, see register) (L595-599). Short tells the host that soldiers came to him afraid ("I'm terrified," "I'm consumed with fear") and that one young man said he had lost his faith; his answer was to be present, "we're going to do this together," with prayer (L607-617). The host says the unit then "turn[s] it around" and complains little. In this segment, **no one jokes**; the one dry line is Betts's countdown, said before the casualties. Short's leadership response is presence, not levity. Evidence is a single reserve unit in its first week, retold by filmmaker, host and sergeant.
*Candidate application:* The first deaths in a unit can end the early banter and bring a different register: a leader whose only tool is staying with the frightened, in a faith idiom. A character's humor may have a documented "before" he is reluctant to talk about.

**[E25d-9]. [BCAST] The sent-home soldier: ostracism, no joke.** (MV-B-055, Act One, L657-747)
Joe Betts, evacuated to Germany with a disc injury, tells the host the downside of going home is hearing from people who do not understand: "if you didn't get hit with a shrapnel, you shouldn't be at home" (L677); "if your arm isn't broke, you didn't get shot, so why are you at home?" (L685). Some of his unit's own men think he faked it to escape marital trouble (Hertlein: "I don't know for sure," L693-701). Betts says it bothered him that they treated him like a deserter; he avoids public places but must attend his daughters' basketball games, where "that's all they want to talk about" (L713). Men on leave from his unit did not call; he learned that some of them blamed him for being home (L737-739). He lost his church (attendance fell to seven, then none) and his marriage was strained; his post-injury job is sitting by the phone at the armory (L745-747). The segment is plain throughout. It documents a sanction that falls on the one who leaves the unit early: suspicion, silence, and an expectation of visible wounds.
*Candidate application:* For a soldier-type character who left for reasons not visible as injury, the broadcast documents the social cost: peers' contempt and civilians' fixation on the story. Directly relevant to the defector's reception, since Betts's own account is of being treated as a deserter although the medical evidence was in the record.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions.** *Fear and tension:* the documented stance is understatement and a borrowed film or show frame while working at full competence ([E25d-10], [E25d-6]); a stoic quip that names the worst case and puts everyone in it is the Pressfield model ([E25d-46]), a claim not evidence. *Cohesion and boundary:* the shared code is meant to be unsafe for outsiders; memes between distant ex-unit members are a maintenance and check-in channel ([E25d-19]); a joke can need a listener who has been in Iraq and still fail with a deployed Marine ([E25d-1], [E25d-2]). *Coping claims:* a founder's, a commander's and a memoirist's one-sentence assertions that humor keeps people going ([E25d-32], [E25d-41], [E25d-45]); no measured outcome anywhere in this unit. *Grief and guilt:* humor around the dead hurt one corpsman ([E25d-41]); a blast survivor doubted the right to joke at all ([E25d-36]). *Rank and exposure:* pogue, Fobbit and POG name a ladder of exposure that the low-ranked turn on themselves ([E25d-3], [E25d-29], [E25d-37]).
