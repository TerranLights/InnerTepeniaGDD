## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What my range holds.** (1) The closing pages of **B.E.F. Times Vol. 1 No. 4 (Monday 5 March 1917)**, L18880-19851: the second half of a rear-area piece signed "Grandpa" (it begins before my range and its heading is garbled), a column-filler anecdote, the **"B.E.F. Alphabet"** (A-Z, every stanza ending "in the trenches"), serial chapters 4-7 of **"Narpoo Rum,"** a farewell to a Brigadier, a letter, an "Agony Column," an orphaned dialect-verse fragment (L19659-19673), a concert-party bill and three back-page advertisements. (2) **B.E.F. Times Vol. 1 No. 5 (Tuesday 10 April 1917; price 1 franc)**, L19850-20900 and onward: a theater bill ("The Fossileum"), "Publisher's Announcements," an advertisement, the editorial (an "au revoir" before the "Big Push"), a rumor column, a counting-down rhyme, a Teech Bomas dispatch, a mock-heroic sonnet, a verse against pessimists, the inter-arm verse exchange and its reconciliation, a scriptural pastiche about Returns, intelligence-summary extracts with editorial asides, a Rubaiyat parody, and a turf report (ending at L20898). No. 5 **continues past L20900** (the next piece, "The Sub," starts at L20904 and belongs to the next unit), and the editorial's promised final chapter of "Narpoo Rum" is not in my range. The imprint on the ads is the same pun as in E09a ("Printed and Published by Sherwood, Forester & Co., Ltd.," here partly damaged).

**Evidence base.** The same division's officer-written paper (E08, E09a): pen names (Grandpa, Teech Bomas, "Fed Up"), the editors writing unsigned. The paper is still an officers'-mess paper by inference. The ranks speak only through officer-written dialect. The period is **March-April 1917**: the German withdrawal toward the Hindenburg Line, the capture of Baghdad, the American entry into the war, and the opening of the Arras offensive (9 April 1917) all fall in or just before these issues; I identify them as glosses from general knowledge, flagged as mine.

**What it does NOT contain.** No images (cartoons survive only as captions or not at all). No private soldier's own writing. No named dead are joked about. No explicit mention of shell shock, desertion or executions. The only censorship evidence in the range is the paper's own acknowledgements (see below).

**OCR and format damage (honest report).** Column-fragmented text; headings, drop capitals and some stanza initials are lost; numerals ("59" for 5.9) and several proper names are unreliable. I cite **issue and line numbers ("L")** of the text file; there are no page markers. **Quotations** are exact except that (a) single-character OCR slips are repaired silently where certain, (b) larger repairs are in [square brackets], (c) British spellings are converted to American per the project rule, in quotations too ("wagon," "realized," "colored"). Glosses of real persons and events the paper parodies (Northcliffe's press, Tirpitz, Hindenburg, Wilson, Haig, Beatty, the Kaiser, "Little Willie," Abdul, Minnie, the "Big Push") are mine unless the text says so.

**Content handling.** Nothing in this stretch reached gore. Items with a death, mutilation or sexual hint are described in restrained terms and logged in the register at the end; period national and ethnic caricature is noted as such and not extended.

## Findings

### A. B.E.F. Times No. 4 (5 March 1917), closing pages (L18880-19851)

**[E09b1-1]. "Grandpa" on the rear area: the further back, the more the staff types appear like wildflowers.** (PW-01, B.E.F. Times No. 4, L18888-18973; the piece's heading is garbled in the OCR)
A signed first-person piece about leaving "the Front" behind and finding the rear echelon. Adjutants, Town Majors, A.P.M.'s, Quartermasters, instructors, lecturers, "Missionaries, Experts, Staff Officers" "become like common hedgerow flowers in Springtime," and the writer once met a man who had seen "a stray hothouse seedling from G.H.Q." The next number is promised to explain where "all the 'Paper' comes from," to be bought "for your unborn children: the horror of going there will keep them always good." The writer is posted to a rifle range, where "far more rounds per day" are fired "in anger" than in the line, as "repairs prove." The Range Officer's day is a list of petty fatigues (picking up chocolate tin-foil and paper, helping up a "refractory target," visiting sentries to explain that "it really is not part of their duty to look death in the face by walking about in rear of the stop butt"). He rests with "the meagre unexpended portion of the previous day's ration in one hand and a fat flask in the right, a true horn of plenty." The piece ends on a stumble: "to the bosom of my—(I beg your pardon, nothing of the sort: I forgot! AM STILL B.E.F.)". A scriptural pun on a priest's rod, applied to the range, carries a mild innuendo and is paraphrased only.
Mechanism: nature-writing and "happy, thank you" deflection over a posting that is safe in the line's sense but dangerous in an everyday sense (the sentries wandering behind the stop butt); the stumble at the end is the mask slipping (a man starting to say "my wife" or "my home" and remembering where he is). The rear area is described with the language used for wildlife, and the horror of Staff is not death but paper. Targets: Staff and rear-area officer types, G.H.Q. as a distant species, the training establishment, the paper's own habit of promising an exposé "in letters of blood-red."
*Candidate application:* A veteran character could be funniest and most guarded about the safe postings (range, depot, staff) while sliding past anything about home mid-sentence; the slipped word can be a tell, not a joke.

**[E09b1-2]. "To Fill a Column": the editors confess no copy, then tell a Brigadier-and-Padre anecdote and complain about paper prices.** (PW-01, No. 4, L18977-19015)
The editor writes "I can't think of any good tales to tell you" and then tells one: a Brigadier and a Padre, both down with influenza, are put in the same room; the Brigadier's stories were "spicy" and the Padre "very unresponsive"; each independently sent for his clothes and told the doctor he felt so much better that he was leaving. The piece then switches to the Editor "sitting with a towel round his head," unable to decide whether to "double the price of this journal, or reduce the size," because paper has gone up, while "The Northcliffe" press (my gloss: the press baron's newspapers, a target since E08) "by an effort of superb patriotism has doubled the price of their efforts." The item ends "Wait and see."
Mechanism: filler turned into a joke about filler (E08-2's origin story; the copy-shortage complaint of E09a); the anecdote is a two-man failure of rank-and-religion rapport (the senior bawdy, the clergyman unresponsive) resolved by both fleeing back to duty; the press-price line aims "patriotism" at profit. Targets: the Brigadier and the Padre (teased by type, neither named), the home press.
*Candidate application:* Two senior figures who cannot stand each other's company and cure themselves of illness to escape it; a soldier-type's anecdotes may be about what authority figures do to avoid one another rather than about danger.

**[E09b1-3]. "A B.E.F. Alphabet" (A to Z): the form, the refrain and the origin in a request.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19018-19286)
A twenty-six-stanza alphabet in which every stanza ends on the same idea, "in the trenches." Each stanza is a letter and a theme (Army, Blighty, Cavalry, Duckboards, Editor, Flying Corps, Gas, Hun, Infantry, Jar, Kultur, Leave, Minnie, Nominal Rolls, Observer, Pediculi, a Q stanza whose heading is lost to OCR, Returns, Sappers, Trenches, Universe, Vices, Whiskey and Whizz-bangs, the Unknown "X," Yarns, Zero). In No. 3 (E09a-45) the editors printed a "B.E.F. Alphabet" from Mesopotamia and asked "someone" to "have a shot at the 'B.E.F. Alphabet Up-to-date'"; this appears to be the reply, and unlike the Mesopotamian one it contains no "CENSORED" footnote (its censorship humor is in stanzas E and T, below). The text does not say who wrote it.
Mechanism: the children's alphabet as an inventory of grievances and objects, with a refrain that makes every item share one fate (the trench); each stanza ends by a flat return to the place, so even a rhyme about home returns the reader to the trench. The following findings treat the stanzas by function.
*Candidate application:* A shared inventory song in which every verse resolves to the same place is a ready-made unit tradition: new members are tested on whether they can add a verse.

**[E09b1-4]. Alphabet stanzas on home, leave and the cavalry: relief is expensive, rumored, or canceled in advance.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19041-19060, 19135-19143)
**B** for Blighty reports "Prices are rising, and food is so dear / That a 'sub' can't afford to even go near; / It is cheaper to stay in the trenches": home inverted into a place too costly to visit. **C** for the Cavalry, who "have not seen their gee-gees for many a day, / But soon they will mount them and gallop away, / And we'll all say good-bye to the trenches": the long-promised cavalry breakthrough as a rumor of release (a gloss of mine: the "gallop through" hoped for after a breakthrough). **L** for Leave: "Ten days in Blighty away from the mire. / Hope springs eternal, and ne'er will expire / In the breast of the men in the trenches." The leave stanza is the only fully sincere statement of desire; "hope" is stated flatly and not undercut.
Mechanism: reversal (home is unaffordable), rumor (cavalry), and plain longing placed where a joke would be expected. Targets: the home front's prices, the army's slow breakthrough, the leave system (as in E08-52, E08-86).
*Candidate application:* A character from a long war may joke that home is "too expensive" while meaning he cannot go back; the genuine wish sits in the one stanza that is not a joke.

**[E09b1-5]. Alphabet stanzas on Staff, paper and the Press: the rear as a producer of paper and the civilian as a producer of vice.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19158-19163, 19187-19204, 19240-19251, 19273-19279)
**N** for Nominal Rolls: the daily, weekly and monthly rolls sent to "Q," which "we'd do ... gladly and much worse things too, / To finish the war in the trenches" (the stated bargain is that any amount of paperwork would be accepted if it ended the war). **Q** (heading lost): "Princes of paper, the pride of our race— / Every movement and minute be sure they can trace / And send back to the man in the trenches." **R** for Returns "to be rendered by noon," listing the number of men who "have seen a blue moon, / Speak Japanese, or have been to Rangoon," in absurd categories (the form of E09a-10, E09a-24). **V** for Vices, "discovered by those who have been more or less claimants to fame through a line in the Press, / But never have shone in the trenches": the vices of soldiers are the invention of those who write about them. **Y** for Yarns: some true, others "spun by those 'back,' who have never been through, / Or stood their whack of the trenches."
Mechanism: bureaucratic forms as absurd lists; the "princes of paper" as the only enemy who can trace "every movement and minute"; the journalist's moral discovery of the soldier's vices as invention. Targets: Q and the returns, the rear's trackers of the front, civilian and press moralizers (continuing E09a-12, E09a-14, E09a-50), yarn-spinners behind the line.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type's contempt for "those who write it up" is a standing boundary marker: the veteran says what the trench was, and what the paper said it was, in two different registers.

**[E09b1-6]. Alphabet stanzas on the censor-editor and on swearing: the paper's own power as a joke.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19063-19075, 19078-19083, 19219-19225)
**D** for Duckboards: laid "end to end" they'd "girdle the Earth, and to Heaven ascend," but "they've caused a peculiar blend / Of language to thrive in the trenches" (profanity is acknowledged in one clean phrase). **E** for the Editor, "ruddy in hue": "He'd blue-pencil this if I said all I knew, / So I'll wish him good luck or—between me and you— / He'll send me exploring Hun trenches": the writer calls the editor both the censor and a source of punishment, and the threatened penalty is a trench raid, not a reprimand. **T** for the Trenches themselves: "this is where / I must take heed what I write, or I'll swear!" followed by the strongest line of the poem: "Which have blackened our souls, and have whitened our hair: / Oh! Life is a dream in the trenches."
Mechanism: three stanzas about expression itself (what can be said, what the editor will cut, what the speaker will swear), and the most direct line about the war's psychological cost placed in the one stanza the speaker says he must censor himself in. It is a worked case of the "self-censor, then say it" move: the speaker announces the limit, and the sentence that follows is the one the limit was for.
*Candidate application:* A character may say "I must watch what I say here" immediately before saying the true thing; the announced limit is the signal that the next line is the point.

**[E09b1-7]. Alphabet stanzas on weapons, rum, vermin and the enemy: the familiar objects get one stanza each.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19118-19132, 19149-19155, 19175-19184, 19207-19213, 19254-19270)
**J** for the Jar: "if its contents are rum. A welcome awaits it whene'er it may come." **K** for Kultur: the Germans "endeavor to force down our throats with big guns ... They send shells in packets, they send them in ones: / But Kultur's Nar-Poo in the trenches" ("Nar-Poo" being the army slang for finished or gone, used as the paper's standing word). **M** for Minnie, "whose shriek rends the night"; "They say that her bark is much worse than her bite ... But she isn't much loved in the trenches" (the personified mortar, E08-83, E09a). **P** for Pediculi: "horrible pests" who are "dear little fellows" and yet "unwelcome guests" to the infantry (lice as pets and pests at once). **S** for the Sappers, "who sin without shame" and "invented the five-bob 'A' frame" (continuing the inter-arm jab of E09a-42). **W** for Whiskey and Whizz-bangs: "Of the former I've almost forgotten the smell, / While the latter contribute to make it like Hell." **X**, "the unknown," is Fritz, who "is now thinking (and we all agree)" that his future "will be much hotter than e'er in the trenches." **H** for the Hun is the stock hard line: "Yet a Hun is a Hun, and as such he must pay / For making us live in the trenches."
Mechanism: personification and understatement for weapons; the affectionate diminutive for vermin; the missing drink recorded as a loss of smell; the enemy given a single moralizing stanza and a "hotter future," as in E08-82 and E09a-17.
*Candidate application:* An accepted ritual is to name the small daily irritants (lice, a particular mortar) with fond or reduced language, and to state hatred of the enemy in a single stock formula that does no other work.

**[E09b1-8]. Alphabet stanzas on the war itself: "Universe," "Infantry," "Observer," "Flying Corps," "Gas," "Zero."** (PW-01, No. 4, L19026-19038, 19086-19099, 19110-19115, 19166-19172, 19228-19237, 19282-19286)
**A** for the Army: a veteran who "fought for a year from the Somme up to Meteren." **F** for the Flying Corps is the one sincere compliment: "we express our admiration: could we do less?" with the airmen "often" helping "us out of a mess." **G** for Gas carries a double sense (my reading): "poisonous GAS that's emitted by fighters behind the line only half-witted, / But very pugnacious," so that bluster is gas and the speaker pities those who must live near it. **I** for the Infantry (prefixed "P.B."): "One bob per diem and milk in their tea; / They work day and night, after which they are free / To start on a job in the trenches" (the ranks' pay and workload as the joke). **O** for the Observer, who from his post sees "stout German generals dancing in tights" (the fantasy that observation reports contain). **U** for the Universe, "whose fate 'tis plain / Is now being settled in mud, slush and rain, / By strafing which spreads from Nieuport to Lorraine, / A line which is marked by our trenches": cosmic stakes reduced to a muddy line. **Z** for Zero, "the time we go over": "Most of us wish we were way back in Dover, / Making munitions and living in clover / And far, far away from the trenches." The last stanza states the soldiers' wish to be elsewhere, in the war-worker's well-paid safety, in rhyme.
Mechanism: bathos (universe in mud), mock-heroic (the universe's fate), irony of pay and work, the airmen as the only unironic object of praise, the "Zero" stanza as the admission of fear in an easy rhyme. Targets: the home war-worker (envy), the rear's boasters ("gas"), the paper's own army.
*Candidate application:* The one real wish ("way back in Dover, ... living in clover") expressed as envy of a safe civilian job is a recognizable coping form: a veteran who jokes about the best job he could have had if he had not enlisted.

**[E09b1-9]. Serial "Narpoo Rum," chapters 4-7: the cast list as pun, and the tear-gas jar.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19295-19551)
A new set of installments under the banner "OUR SPLENDID NEW SERIAL," "by the author of 'Shot in the Culvert.'" The **dramatis personae** is a stack of puns: "Cloridy Lyme—A Sanitary Inspector" (chloride of lime), "Intha Pink—A Pioneer" ("in the pink"), "General Bertram Rudolph de Rogeruin—The Earl of Loose," "Lord Reginald de Knellthorpe—His Son," "Herlock Shomes—The Great Detective," "Dr. Hotsar [Hotsam]—His Admirer," "L. Plumernapple—A Soldier" (the ration jam), "Madeline Carot—A French Girl," and "Q. Wemm—A Storekeeper." The chapters are numbered out of order in the OCR.
**Chapter 4**: Shomes lands his airplane "in the Square of the ancient town of Ypres," dines at the Hotel des Ramparts, then dons "tin-hat, raincoat and gum boots thigh" and walks up the Menin Road; "the weary whirr of a five-nine" announces his arrival, and the Culvert Arms "was no more." He "hastily injected a double dose from his vermoral [Vermorel] sprayer" and, in the ditch, finds a muddy envelope readable only in torn letters ("TOR / IMES / TERS ..."), pointing at the paper's own letters column. **Chapter 5** (OCR "8"): the Earl and Hotsam at an estaminet window find Lord Reggie comforting the weeping Madeline; the Earl and Hotsam burst into tears; Hotsam puts on his gas helmet, corks a jar and opens the window; Reggie explains that Madeline told him she had seen some rum jars and, thinking they might contain "the rum that I am suspected of stealing," he came to examine them: "they appear to contain tear gas." The Earl, "with a new burst of tears," joins Reggie's and Madeline's hands. **Chapter 6**, "The End of Shomes?": Hotsam receives "one of the dreaded pink forms" telegram to meet at Ypres, finds Shomes in his plane, and they take off; "Suddenly a flash! a crash! and two men and an [airplane] were falling through the night." **Chapter 7**, "At Last?": the Pioneer sits in "a large shell-hole in the grounds of [a castle]. He was not happy. He had been knocked down by a G.S. wagon, machine-gunned on the road, whizz-banged in the trench, and, finally, had taken his 'A' frame to the wrong dump." He thinks "of war ... of snow ... of rum," realizes the Brigade's supply was stolen by "some scoundrel," and resolves to find it; at Madeline's estaminet he declares himself "a bloomin' policeman now," reports that he saw a staff officer leave at 8:15, and is offered "some rum." The installment ends "WAS IT?" and "(Read next thrilling installment.)"
Mechanism: the recruiting-fiction, detective and melodrama tropes (E08-16, E08-57, E08-66) now colonized by the rum-theft plot of E09a-20; the tear-gas jar is a solved mystery that turns into sentimental reconciliation; the pioneer's list of misfortunes is a litany of hazards and mishaps in the same register ("knocked down ... whizz-banged ... wrong dump"), so a shell and a misrouted wagon sit at one level; the apparent death of the hero and sidekick is delivered as a heading's question mark. Targets: popular fiction, the rum supply, the Staff officer as suspect (the culprit's hint points upward), the sentimental-French-girl convention.
*Candidate application:* A serial told in the unit's idiom can absorb every real danger as one more plot twist without stopping the plot; a veteran who liked the serial may remember its jokes better than the events they paralleled.

**[E09b1-10]. "Special News": Teech Bomas returns from his "winter trip" and the war resumes "as per schedule."** (PW-01, No. 4, L19554-19562)
Our "special correspondent Mr. Teech Bomas, having returned from his winter trip to more sunny climates, we will now carry on with the war as per schedule," with the next item titled "Seconds out of the Ring." (E09a's roll-up lists Teech Bomas's absence as a running gag.)
Mechanism: the war as a seasonal sporting calendar whose active period begins when the correspondent returns; "Seconds out of the Ring" is boxing language for the start of a round. Target: the press correspondent, the seasonal pattern of the fighting.
*Candidate application:* A sense that the war has a "season," marked by who returns to the front, can anchor a character's talk about year-cycles.

**[E09b1-11]. Farewell to a Brigadier: the plain register at the paper's own edge.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19570-19595)
"Once again we say good-bye to a Brigadier, this time to our oldest friend in the Division, Gen. B. [Mitford, per the OCR 'Mitrorp'/'Mirrorp'; the name is not certain], C.B., D.S.O., who was with us in the beginning." The editors recall the "mob of aspiring and perspiring embryo soldiers" doing "wonderful feats under his skilful guidance on the Sussex Downs. How well we came to understand the terrors of war during those final rushes on Chanctonbury Ring!" and add that the staff "mourn the loss of one of the first supporters and helpers of our paper": the General "assisted at the inception of the 'Wipers Times,' and has ever since given the greatest help possible." The whole Division "bids him God-speed," and "our congratulations and good wishes are none the less hearty if with them are mixed the natural regrets one feels at losing a good pal."
Mechanism: a joke at the training period's expense ("terrors of war" at a rehearsal on a hill) inside an otherwise sincere notice; the explicit acknowledgement that the paper had a senior patron since its start (confirming E08-4's hint of senior support). Targets: none (the one fully affectionate item about a Brigadier; the barb is on the trainees themselves).
*Candidate application:* A senior officer who protected the unit's humor is remembered in a notice that is plain and warm; the unit's comic voice allows itself one joke at the "terrors" of peace-time soldiering.

**[E09b1-12]. Letters and the Agony Column: the cuckoo gag, the lovers' notice for mortars, the estate "traveling East," the confectioners.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19601-19653)
The cuckoo gag (E08-59; E09a) continues: a letter signed "FED UP" asks for the name and address of "a 'Lover of Nature'" and promises that he "will not love Nature any more; neither will he hear any more cuckoos. No sir! not this Spring nor next." The Agony Column: (i) "MINNIE.—Meet me at Flying Pig 8.30.—Tock Emma." (my gloss: Minnie is the enemy mortar of earlier issues; "Flying Pig" a heavy mortar shell; "Tock Emma" the signalers' spoken form of T.M., trench mortar; so a lovers' assignation between weapons); (ii) a damaged notice from "W.O." (may be the War Office) as "young, inexperienced," seeking an appointment "with fighting to gain experience" (the verb "write" follows; the middle is garbled, so the target is uncertain); (iii) "FOR SALE or EXCHANGE.—Large Country ESTATE, pleasantly situated on the banks of the Somme. Owner traveling East for the benefit of his health. No reasonable offer refused. Would exchange for a couple of white rabbits, or something edible." (my gloss: the German withdrawal from the Somme region in March 1917); (iv) "MESSRS. ABDUL & CO. regret that ... they are unable to supply any more Turkish Delights for the present, as there has been such a run on them" (my gloss: Baghdad's fall, March 1917, and "run" as a rout). A short nursery-rhyme quatrain pairs "a little Turk" and "a little Hun" who "lived in Bapaume" and "said to the other, as they shivered with alarm, / 'To find another home wouldn't do us any harm.'"
Mechanism: the classified-ad as news-reporting under cover of a property notice; the enemy's retreat as a euphemism ("traveling East for the benefit of his health"); the enemy reduced to confectioner and tenant; the rhyme gives both enemies a shared fear and a shared sensible solution. Targets: the Germans and Turks (as retreating sellers), the War Office (if the damaged line is read that way), the paper's own readers (the Lover of Nature).
*Candidate application:* Euphemisms for the enemy's setbacks in the style of property notices offer a way a soldier can discuss victory without triumph.

**[E09b1-13]. Concert-party and "Wiggins' Warm Wollies" back-page advertisements: the entertainment bill and the "undervest."** (PW-01, No. 4, L19692-19772)
"THE PROFESSOR AGAIN PRESENTS HIS World-famed Concert Party IN AN ENTIRELY NEW LINE, ... NEW SET OF STAR ARTISTS," with song titles including "I NEVER COULD STAND PIMPLES" and "ANOTHER LITTLE DRINK, Etc.," and "Tweeny and Co. in grand spectacular performance of 'Life.'" The ad adds that "A chorus of real soldiers has been obtained at fabulous cost to perform." The next ad: "ARE YOU GOING IN THE BIG PUSH? THEN DON'T FAIL TO WEAR ONE OF OUR 'UNDERVESTS!' KEEPS OUT DRAUGHTS. RAIN. WHIZZ-BANGS. AND HAVE BEEN KNOWN TO BLUNT A 5.9" [OCR: "59"], ending with a couplet: "If the big push you are in, / Wear our Wollies next your skin."
Mechanism: advertising hyperbole (an undershirt that stops a heavy shell) set beside an ad for a concert party whose chorus is "real soldiers" (an inversion: the soldier as expensive entertainer). The words "Big Push" appear in an advertisement as an event the readers are expected to be about to join; the paper treats the offensive as imminent (I did not check whether the phrase occurs earlier). Targets: the entertainment industry, comfort-parcel and underwear advertising, the offensive.
*Candidate application:* A cheerful comfort-item ad that promises to stop a shell fits a culture where the small thing carried is credited with large protection; the soldier-chorus joke is a quiet statement that the real soldiers are the show.

**[E09b1-14]. "Popular Songs" ad: song-title gags assigned to leaders and enemies.** (PW-01, No. 4, L19775-19812)
"Dances, Ray & Bunter" announce "their new book of popular songs" "which includes the following great hits," each paired with a singer. Clear pairings: "I never stopped running till I got home," "sung by ABDUL" (my gloss: the Turk, with Baghdad's fall in March 1917); "O lucky Zep," Tirpitz; "I want to go back," Hindenburg (my gloss: the withdrawal to the Hindenburg Line); "Have a banana," Devonport (my gloss: Lord Devonport, the Food Controller, who is named in a food joke at L20707, E09b1-27, which supports it). The OCR separates the other titles from their singers, so the pairing is uncertain: "I want to be a soldier," "Young men taken in and done," and "We don't want to fight" sit among the names "The Wheeldon Family" and "Senators Glee Party" (my glosses, both uncertain: a family prosecuted in 1917 over anti-conscription activity, and the U.S. Senate's reluctance over arming ships in March 1917). A convivial drinking-song title about the dead is assigned to U-boat skippers (paraphrased).
Mechanism: song-title parody, with each song's real meaning turned onto a public figure's situation (the Turk running, the Zeppelin lucky, the general withdrawing, the Food Controller offering fruit in a shortage). "Young men taken in and done" reads as a conscription-and-swindle pun. Targets: enemy leaders and allies, neutrals, home politicians and profiteers.
*Candidate application:* Giving each public figure a song title in the voice of his own predicament is a quick way a unit can sum up the news.

### B. B.E.F. Times No. 5 (Tuesday 10 April 1917), front matter and editorial (L19850-20029; continues past L20029)

**[E09b1-15]. No. 5, front matter: the "Fossileum" theater bill and "Publisher's Announcements" turned on the leaders.** (PW-01, No. 5, L19850-19910)
The masthead is "The B.E.F. Times, with which are incorporated The Wipers Times, The 'New Church' Times, The Kemmel Times & The Somme-Times"; "No 5. Vol 1. Tuesday, April 10th, 1917. Price 1 FRANC." The "Fossileum" theater bill promises "The Loma Troupe of Quick Change Artists," "Spring Cleaning" (a domestic drama), a "Great American Film Play, entitled 'Teddy Get Your Gun'" ("some film"), and Murray's "Colormatrograph" tour "Through Palestine (Series No. 3: Jerusalem)" (my gloss: the U.S. entry into the war of 6 April 1917; Roosevelt's offer of volunteers). The "Publisher's Announcements" list books by leaders: "God's Good Man—An Autobiography by William Hohenzollern (Author of 'The Innocents Abroad,' 'Misunderstood,' 'The Christian,' etc.)"; "A Thief in the Night—By Little Willie"; "The Last Hope—Professor Hindenburg (Author of 'Westward Ho')"; "It's Never Too Late to Mend—Dr. Wilson"; "Eric, or Little by Little—Dean Haig"; "The Cruise of the Catch-a-Lot—By Bill Beatty."
Mechanism: title parody attached to a person's role (the Kaiser's self-justifying autobiography, a crown prince as thief, a general whose "last hope" is a retreat, a President slow to act, the British Commander-in-Chief rendered as the headmaster of a school story whose title is "Little by Little," and the Navy's chief as a fisherman who cannot catch). The British Commander-in-Chief appears here by name, which stands out against the paper's usual practice of teasing seniors by role or nickname (E08 roll-up, "Rules of license"); I have not checked whether he is named earlier.
Targets: the Kaiser, his heir, Hindenburg, President Wilson, Haig, Beatty. Signs of limits: the Commander-in-Chief's joke is mild, a school-story title, and it is aimed at the slowness of attrition (my reading), not at his person.
*Candidate application:* Titles for the leaders' memoirs-to-be are a classic soldier genre; the "Little by Little" title shows that even a reverent unit lets the top be teased through a pun that can pass for praise.

**[E09b1-16]. "The Drink Habit Acquired in Three Days": the quack advertisement inverted again.** (PW-01, No. 5, L19913-19933)
"If you know anyone who doesn't drink alcohol regularly, or occasionally, let me send my free book, 'CONFESSIONS OF AN ALCOHOL SLAVE.' It explains ... How to quickly become an Expert 'Bona-fide Toper.'" The advertiser says: "For the first 15 years of my life I was a rabid teetotaler, since the age of 16 I have never been to bed sober." He can "cure anyone," addresses "J. SUPITUP, Havanotha Mansions," and gives his telegraphic address as "RATS."
Mechanism: the patent-medicine "cure" advertisement turned upside down (the affliction sold is abstinence); the name puns do the work ("Sup it up," "Have a nother"); the "bona fide" regulation joke recurs (E09a: licensing hours and "bona fide" travelers). Drink is again the central subject (E09a-20, E09a-23, E09a-40, E09a-48). Target: the temperance moralists and the drink regulations.
*Candidate application:* A drinking culture can present its own habit as a cure-selling business; the device keeps the topic public and comic while making it a shared joke about being "converted."

**[E09b1-17]. Editorial No. 5: spring, summer time, thrown-away kit, "several million francs" in the treasure chest, and the editors' au revoir.** (PW-01, No. 5, L19941-20016)
"Spring has at last really come! And with it an unusual amount of hurry and bustle all round; likewise forecasts, prophecies and conjectures, all frothy and furious. We have all put our watches on one hour, and are now spending our spare time throwing away our surplus kit, sharpening our pistols and swords, and having our boots soled and heeled." The editors say that the number is produced under "rather more difficult circumstances than usual" and that "whatever may be lacking is due to circumstances over which we have no control." Teech Bomas contributes again. The number "sees the finish of our serial 'Narpoo Rum,'" and they remind readers of the "Mammoth Competition" announced in the Grand Xmas Double Number, noting: "We still have several million francs left in our treasure chest which we should like to dispose of before the 'Big Push' commences, more on account of its bulk than for any other reason." The close: "It may be a long time before we can produce another number, in which case we wish to take this opportunity of bidding all our friends 'au revoir,' the best of luck, and thanking them all for the kind support we have always received since the day in 'Wipers' long ago when we found an old printing outfit looking for a job."
Mechanism: the editorial starts as a spring-and-hurry joke (summer time, preparations as cleanup and weapon-sharpening) and ends in plain thanks; the "bulk" gag deflates the prize treasure; the "circumstances over which we have no control" echoes the 1 December 1916 phrase about the title change (E09a-8) and is the paper's standard formula for "we are going into battle." The farewell recalls the origin story (E08-2: the old printing outfit at Wipers).
Targets: the paper itself, the "Big Push," the competition. Signs of limits: the editors do not say where the division goes or why the paper may stop; they say it by timing (Easter week, new watches, kit thrown away) and by the "au revoir."
*Candidate application:* Before a major operation a unit's joker may do the work of a farewell speech as a business notice, with a joke about the treasury; the thanks are made in a clause set apart from the joke.

### C. B.E.F. Times No. 5 (10 April 1917), body (L20023-20900)

**[E09b1-18]. "News from the Ration Dump": a rumor column of wishes and absurdities, with a disclaimer.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20023-20076)
A column of one-line news items under a heading that places the source of rumors at the ration dump (my gloss: where news and gossip circulate with the supplies). The items: "The Esquimaux have broken off diplomatic relations with Germany" (the string of neutral countries breaking with Germany, played absurdly); "The Huns are shortening their line in the West" with a plan to send divisions on a "punitive expedition" (the end of the line is lost to OCR); "Patrols of British and French cavalry swam the Rhine last night near Cologne, and are now meeting with sharp resistance in the suburbs of Berlin"; "A party of A.S.C. were seen working in the reserve line" (the supply corps as men who do not work); "The Czar of Russia has antiquated" (OCR; probably "abdicated," my gloss, after the March 1917 revolution); "Horatio Bottomley has accepted the Turkish throne on condition they make a separate peace" (the press politician of E09a); "Leave is about to reopen on the Western front"; "The German fleet has bombarded Wapping Old Stairs, and ruined the carpet"; and "40,000 Huns have surrendered. They were so thin that they walked down one of our C.T.'s in fours." The column closes "[The Editor takes no responsibility for the truth of the above statements.]"
Mechanism: the rumor sheet as parody of the home press's victory bulletins (the cavalry that swims the Rhine; surrender numbers); bathos (a bombardment's only casualty is a carpet); the wish item stated flat among absurd ones (leave reopening, the rest of the war won). The leave line is the one item that is a true hope; it sits in the column of fantasies, where it can be disowned by the editor's disclaimer. The thin Germans walking "in fours" down a narrow trench is a joke on enemy hunger, a theme the Teech Bomas dispatch of the same number repeats with the captured bread (E09b1-20). Targets: the home press, the supply corps, the press politician, the enemy.
*Candidate application:* A unit's rumor column may include the one real wish among the absurd ones precisely because the format lets it be denied; a veteran may recall the "ration dump" as where hope was passed around with a disclaimer.

**[E09b1-19]. "Ten German Pioneers": a counting-down nursery rhyme in which each stanza removes one enemy soldier.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20083-20152)
A ten-verse counting-down rhyme in the pattern of the ten-little-figures nursery song (I do not reproduce the best-known English ancestor's title). Each stanza dispatches one enemy pioneer, from a trivial accident (a dropped cigarette) through battlefield weapons already named in the paper (a whizz-bang, a "Flying Pig"), a neighbor's act, a transfer to Verdun, a surrender (a man who "shouted 'Kamarad'"), a man who "felled himself" while felling a tree, one who "tried to stop a tank," and one who walked into a gun; the details of the deaths are summarized and not quoted. The final stanza: "One German Pioneer couldn't see the fun / Of being shot at any more, and so the war was done."
Mechanism: nursery-rhyme meter with one-line removals and a rhyming resolution; the only survivor's reason for ending the war is that he "couldn't see the fun" of being shot at, which is the paper's own fatigue voiced by the enemy (a variant of E09a-17's device of giving the soldier's doubts to the enemy). The earlier stanzas present the enemy's soldiers as the author's co-sufferers as well as victims (they "didn't like the war," were "glad to be alive," dreamed "of Heaven"). Target: the enemy's rank and file (unusual; the paper aims more often at leaders, E08 roll-up). Limit: all dead are enemy and anonymous; nothing of this kind is done to British soldiers.
*Candidate application:* Nursery forms let a unit joke about killing without naming anyone; the last-line wish for the war to end is a standard way a soldier states exhaustion through the enemy.

**[E09b1-20]. Teech Bomas's dispatch "On the Heels of the Fleeing F[...]": the press correspondent parodies the German retreat.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20161-20242; heading cut by OCR)
Dateline "FRANCE, Sunday Afternoon." The correspondent "rode through 174 of the 187 blasted villages liberated during the past couple of days by our troops" on a bicycle, and now sits "in what remains of the bar parlor of its main Estaminet," eating a frugal meal and talking to "the oldest inhabitant." He tastes captured German ration bread, "darkish blue-black in hue," and uses a string of unpleasant adjectives for its taste. The scenes are "thrilling, awe-inspiring, blood-curdling—in short the whole outfit was epic": the liberated villagers (listed by age, sex and condition; the catalog is paraphrased) cheer him in their "gladdest clothes" while village bands play. He asks a woman what became of the German officers; she whistles and answers "Bosch officier, no ben, plenty zig-zag," and the correspondent remarks that this "in itself trivial" incident "sums up the situation." He then passes the British outposts into the enemy rearguard: "Rapidly twisting up the ends of my mustache and turning my cap inside out, I was able to escape recognition," and finds the Germans "gibbering with fright," sweat dripping "pale pink" as they "leapt from tree to tree." A "very tired German 17-inch shell" bursts under his cycle: "Luckily the only damage done was a slight puncture to my near off side wheel. This proves how the Bosch H.E. has deteriorated during the past few months." He must leave "to a later dispatch" what he will "do and see this afternoon."
Mechanism: the correspondent as comic hero of a pursuit he performs by bicycle; arithmetic hyperbole (174 of 187 villages); a disguise farce; understatement of a heavy shell as a flat tire (bathos); the enemy's cowardice drawn in cartoon terms. This is the paper's treatment of the German withdrawal of March 1917 (my gloss), and it is written against the home press's way of narrating it: the claim "liberated" and the villagers' cheering are not mocked, only the reporter's exaggeration. The civilian's remark is the only sentence given as speech.
Targets: war correspondents and press triumphalism; the enemy's soldiers (as cowards, a recurrent paper stereotype; E08-82); the claims of enemy shell "deterioration" as an official talking point. Signs of limits: the villagers are treated as sincere, with no joke at their expense apart from the list's comic cadence.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who watched a victory unfold may be most irritated by the way reporters told it; the correct joke about a real advance is aimed at the telling, not at the villagers or the dead.

**[E09b1-21]. A mock-heroic sonnet whose speaker is revealed in the last lines as a Base staff officer.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20245-20292; heading garbled in OCR, probably "Arma virumque cano")
The speaker begins with a battle-lover's pose: "No Prayers of Peace for me; no maiden's sigh. Give me the Chants of War, the Viking's Song," asks for tales of "noble men and brave" who "bleed and die," and calls himself "A warrior, and no carpet knight." The turn: "The D.S.O., the M.C. grace my breast; / My brow is bound with laurels and with lace; / I love this war." He adds that he is "different from the rest / Of you poor blighters. I live at the Base, / And use the Brain inside my red hat."
Mechanism: a bellicose poem whose reveal is the speaker's post; the heroic-literature quotation of the title, the decorations and the "red hat" (Staff) are the evidence against him. The device is the delayed-reveal last line of E09a (soap ode, cake). Target: the decorated Base officer who loves the war from a distance; the honors system as an index of position rather than danger (compare E09a-51 and E09b1-26). 
*Candidate application:* The loudest war-lovers are marked as men who "live at the Base"; a veteran's contempt for patriotic verse can be stated as a position on where the speaker lives.

**[E09b1-22]. "To All 'Doubting Thomases'": a verse that orders the pessimists to stop grousing and smile.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20300-20361)
A second-person scold in rhymed stanzas to those "of mournful mien, whose bleatings rend the air," who "spread an air of gloom where'er you go." The charges: forecasts that change daily ("One day 'tis 'this' and next day 'that,' your bogies come at will"), "You said a year ago the war was lost—we're fighting still," and the habit of finding disaster in setbacks and traps in victories ("'We'll be lured into a trap,' or 'we can ne'er hold what we've won,' / And 'we'll all be starved to death' your constant shout"). The speaker notes that "mostly you are those who ne'er have known the joy / Of living in ten feet of mud and slime," with the sarcastic "ecstasy ... sheer delight without alloy / When you're dodging crumps and Minnies all the time." The close: "So in future cut the grousing, and for God's sake wear a grin," then "Cut the everlasting wail and smile, man, SMILE!" The author is not named; the placement among the paper's verse suggests an editorial or staff contribution (my inference).
Mechanism: direct address, mock-ecstasy about the trenches, a rhetorical complaint about the habit of complaint. It is the paper's most explicit statement in this range of what the humor is for: morale, and the removal of "wail." Note the rule it states: **doubt about the outcome is the offense; complaint about conditions is not** (the same issue-run has just printed an alphabet that complains of mud, lice and absent rum). The speaker places the pessimists outside the line (they "ne'er have known" the mud), which links this verse to the "pessimist" character of E09a-23, E09a-28 and the civilian moralists of E09a-14.
Targets: pessimists (rear, home or in the ranks), home forecasters. Limit: the verse allows the grievance of conditions to the one who has stood in the mud, and takes it from the one who has not.
*Candidate application:* A culture of enforced cheer treats doubt about the war's outcome as a breach, and a veteran may still complain loudly about the conditions; the two rules are different, and a defector would be the one who breaks the first.

**[E09b1-23]. "Concerning Apologies": the infantry's verse about the gunners', sappers' and Staff's replies.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20367-20429; signed "P.B.I.")
Epigraph: "Only the Wise apologize, / Fools always must explain" (attributed to "a great modern poet"). The verses summarize the replies to the infantry's earlier rhymes (E09a-42): the Gunner "grew pensive" but "quickly developed a counter-offensive; / And though the rounds mostly were duds, or fell short, / They showed themselves able to make some retort." The Sappers, who "never shirk / From anything looking the least bit like WORK," swore "He shall rue it!" and "asked for two large Working Parties to do it." The Staff, "though surprised, did not gibber or storm, / But dealt with it all on the Authorized Form": "G" said he knew whom it referred to and passed it "for action" to "Q"; "Q" "patronizingly gave it a smile, / Remarked, 'Poor old "G" Branch!' and wrote on it 'FILE.'"
Mechanism: a running quarrel carried on in meter, each arm given its stereotype (gunners' unreliable rounds, sappers' avoidance of work, Staff's forms and filing), with the infantry as the author of the account. The "duds, or fell short" pun joins a poetic failing to a real danger (shells that fall on one's own side; see E09a-60), in a form that makes it a joke at the gunners' expense. Targets: the gunners, sappers, Staff (G and Q) branches. 
*Candidate application:* Inter-arm ribbing is a stable ritual of a combined force; the stock grievance of each arm is short enough to be stated in a line.

**[E09b1-24]. "The Sanguinary R.E.": the Sappers' reply.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20432-20535)
Addressed to the infantry: "Ah! P.B.I., too well we know your woes ... Preserving us to labor on your drains." The sappers ask to be heard ("A few poor words on our side please to hear"): "Our fingers to the bone are worn for THEE, / Yet still one hears thy working parties cry, / 'We're working for the R.E.'" (the infantry's complaint about fatigue parties lent to the Engineers). They build "a modest shelter, / Just rain and windproof" and before they turn "there enter / Platoons of infantry in quite indecent haste"; the working parties ask for help "with trepidation" and a "cinquantaine" (a group of about fifty) of "fed-up troops" appears late and "crawls off slowly," and half keep the tools they borrowed; at the end of the journey "most of these are sick and tired of war, / Digging's a pastime only fit for fools, / Let the d----d R.E. go and look for more" (the dashes are the paper's). The last stanza turns: "Dear P.B.I.! how gladly would we quit / 'A' frames, and duckboards, berms ... To carry our victorious P.B.I." (they dream of bridges and trestle pathways for the advance).
Mechanism: the engineers' complaint answers the infantry's; the reply uses the same hired-hand grievance in the opposite direction (the infantry take the sappers' shelters and the working parties dodge their work). The final stanza converts the quarrel into a dream of service. Targets: the infantry's work-party habits; the engineers' own role. The oath is dashed.
*Candidate application:* A fatigue-party grievance is told as a mutual dependency, with a closing stanza that rewards the other arm's victory; the dashed oath is the standard censor-by-typography of the paper (E08-34).

**[E09b1-25]. The reconciling verse: "Gunners and Sappers, and P.B.I."** (PW-01, No. 5, L20546-20577)
An unsigned verse that declares the exchange over: "Gunners and Sappers, and P.B.I., / Now each in turn has had his say, / And shown in poetical rivalry / That, though good at their jobs, they're as good in play." It names the reason for the barbs: "At times when the nerves are [strained] taut, / And frayed at the edges, as well may be, / All tempers are—well! just a wee bit short, / Then one must strafe a little impatiently" (the word "strained" is OCR-damaged; the sense is clear). The close: "Yet their trust in each other has stood the test ... Each at his job has proved 'the best,' / Gunners and Sappers, and P.B.I."
Mechanism: the explicit gloss on the quarrel-in-verse: the insults are what frayed nerves look like, and the quarrel is licensed because trust has "stood the test." It gives the cohesion rule that the paper's inter-arm humor otherwise leaves unstated. Targets: none (the verse sums up). It is also the clearest in-text statement in this range of the license rule: that one may "strafe" a neighbor in verse because the trust beneath it is not in doubt.
*Candidate application:* When a unit insults a neighboring unit it depends on, the insult has a stated limit (trust), and an explicit closing statement of mutual regard marks the end of the round.

**[E09b1-26]. "A Story Without a Moral": scriptural pastiche about Returns that reward the wrong men.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20583-20647)
In Old Testament cadence ("And it came to pass ...") a General Officer Commanding asks his A.A. and Q.M.G. for a Return of men who had "refused to undergo the hardships of INOCULATION," and the staff produce a list of fifty names; at Corps it is "labelled with the mystic sign 'P.A.,' which, being interpreted, means 'put aside.'" Later the General asks for a Return of men who have done deeds worthy of the Military Medal; "this Return also" is sent forward and labelled "P.A." The outcome: "those men who had refused to be INOCULATED were duly awarded with the MILITARY MEDAL." The last line is "Oh! great is the Corps."
Mechanism: Biblical register for administrative chains of command (the staff officers' titles in the "begat" cadence), a mix-up of two lists that turns the punishment list into the reward list, and a heading that denies there is a moral. Target: the Corps staff and the awards system; the joke makes decorations the product of clerical error and says nothing about the men who earned them. Comparison: E09a-51 (the mock honors list) and E09b1-21 (the decorated Base officer); this is the third item in two issues aiming at the awards.
*Candidate application:* A soldier who is cynical about decorations may not mock the dead or the brave; he blames the machine that assigns them, in the unit's idiom of lists.

**[E09b1-27]. "Verbatim Extracts From Intelligence Summaries" with the editor's asides, plus a "true extract" from a rations return.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20650-20707)
The form: a deadpan quotation from an official summary, followed by a bracketed aside in the editor's voice. Under TRENCH MORTARS: the "Flying Pig" dropped a round in "our front line" and "completely wrecked" a trench, the crater being "14 feet deep and 25 feet across"; the report adds "It is consoling to think that over 40 rounds have been fired from this gun into the enemy trenches during the last week." Aside: "(Very consoling to the P.B.I.)". Under OPERATIONS: a party of about ten Germans entered the lines and was driven out with five killed (paraphrased); aside: "(Some bombers!)". Under MOVEMENTS: thirty men "seen doing Extended Order Drill"; aside: "(We hope it wasn't painful.)". Then "a true extract from a return of reserve rations from a certain garrison": "Commodity—Bully Beef. Quantity—1 Tin. Remarks—Not Full," with the aside "(Where's Lord Devonport?)" (the Food Controller, my gloss).
Mechanism: the editors' heckling of an official text; the aside takes the side of the soldier who receives the shell and mocks the statistic that is meant to reassure; the rations item implies a garrison on one partly empty tin of bully beef, and its aside points at the civilian food authority. Compare the intelligence-summary parodies of E09a-24 and E09a-29, which wrote their own summaries; this one claims "verbatim" and "true" authorship of the original (the claim cannot be checked from the text). Targets: the intelligence summaries, the staff habit of reassurance by statistics, the home food administration. Limit: the casualty (five killed) is reported by the original document and the aside mocks the bombers' claim and the arithmetic, not the dead.
*Candidate application:* Annotating an official statement with a one-line aside is a durable soldier habit; the aside speaks for the person the report leaves out.

**[E09b1-28]. "Rubaiyat of William Hohenzollern": the Kaiser's voice used for an atrocity catalog (handled in restrained terms).** (PW-01, No. 5, L20713-20836; the first line is "Awake, old Tirpz! Bid Hindenburg arise," a plain line)
A Rubaiyat-form poem in the Kaiser's voice, addressed to Tirpitz and Hindenburg, that lists the charges of wartime frightfulness made against Germany in Allied propaganda (named place-atrocities, killing of civilians, looting by the Crown Prince, terror at sea), has the Kaiser claim a divine role and "Kultur," and ends with a plan to blame others for the fire if the venture fails and to "have 'Der Tag' of our desire" even in defeat. I do not reproduce the lines; they carry violent and sexual-violence accusations in the voice of the target.
Mechanism: the same Rubaiyat parody form as E08-48, now used as a mock-confession in which the enemy leader says aloud what the paper's earnest hate says about him. There is no deflating last line and no rival small grievance, unlike the paper's jokes about its own side; the humor register is nearly absent and the item is closer to propaganda in verse form. Target: the Kaiser and his two chiefs. 
*Candidate application:* The unit's hatred of the enemy command is expressed in a stock form that does not use the paper's ordinary comic tools; a veteran might treat that register as the one the army gave them, in contrast to the one they wrote themselves.

**[E09b1-29]. "The Bully-Barlin Stakes": an artillery or anti-aircraft shoot as a race meeting and society column.** (PW-01, No. 5, L20842-20898; the final words are lost at the page break)
A turf report: "The Bully-Barlin Stakes took place on Friday last under the most auspicious conditions, the going being good and the average time of those who completed the course distinctly fast." The "social event" notes: "Admiral Jellicue" (OCR; Jellicoe, my gloss) "tastefully dressed in that inconspicuous blend of brown relieved by a soupcon of red"; a "Comtesse de Callonne" in "a chic sandbag coat and skirt"; a band that plays "selections" with signalers' titles ("You're through to Q, sir, but nobody's awake" and a "tragedy" about burying cable). The race: "Archie and Archie II were hot favorites" (Archie, the army's name for anti-aircraft fire, my gloss), the odds were shortened by "information tic-tacked back to the firm Strafit and Hate," so that "the prices and range lengthened," and "The Archies, however, proved as disappointing as ever to their backers," finishing a good second to "Captain Tarker's 'Bommy,' owner up," which "got off the mark like two Turkish Pashas paced by a Roumanian General"; third came a Reverend on "'Ironscrappes,'" and a mob of "Also rans." It is "rumored that vast sums exchanged hands over the favorite's defeat."
Mechanism: betting and gunnery vocabularies fused ("prices and range lengthened"; "tic-tacked back"); the dangerous course described as an event ("those who completed the course"); the AA gunners' ineffectiveness as a loser's tip; the Staff's uniform as fashion. The reading of "Bommy" as a bomber with "owner up" (the pilot) is mine and uncertain; the meeting's location (Bully and Barlin are French places near Lens, my gloss) suggests a change of sector. The similes use national caricature (Pashas, a Roumanian General as the figures of flight; the paper's stock). Targets: the anti-aircraft gunners and rear-area signals, the Staff, the naval chief (as a society guest), the army's chaplains (a mounted Reverend).
*Candidate application:* A shoot or a raid can be reported in a sporting register that makes the danger a quirk of the course; a veteran's stories may use the language of odds to speak of what could kill.

## Pattern roll-up

**Catalog of NEW devices in my range (E08 and E09a devices continue and are not re-listed; finding numbers in brackets).**
1. **The refrain-alphabet:** twenty-six stanzas that all end on one place ("in the trenches"), so every object, rank and grievance is assigned to the same fate; it answers a request the editors printed in No. 3 [E09b1-3 to E09b1-8; cf. E09a-45].
2. **The announced limit followed by the true line:** "this is where / I must take heed what I write, or I'll swear!" then "blackened our souls, and have whitened our hair"; the editor named as the man with the blue pencil and a trench raid as the penalty [E09b1-6].
3. **The bracketed editorial heckle on an official text** ("Very consoling to the P.B.I."; "We hope it wasn't painful"; "Where's Lord Devonport?"), under a "verbatim" or "true extract" claim [E09b1-27].
4. **Scriptural pastiche for bureaucracy** ("And it came to pass"), with a heading that denies a moral and a clerical mix-up that gives a medal to those who refused an order [E09b1-26].
5. **The rumor column of wishes with an editor's disclaimer** (leave reopening, cavalry swimming the Rhine, thin prisoners "in fours") [E09b1-18].
6. **The counting-down nursery rhyme applied to enemy soldiers,** ending on the survivor who loses interest in being shot at [E09b1-19].
7. **The mock-heroic poem with a last-lines reveal of the Base** ("I live at the Base") [E09b1-21].
8. **The imperative-cheer verse** ordering pessimists to "SMILE," which separates doubt about the outcome (forbidden) from complaint about conditions (allowed) [E09b1-22].
9. **Inter-arm verse exchange with a closing statement of trust** (infantry, gunners, sappers, Staff; the reconciling verse that explains the quarrel as frayed nerves) [E09b1-23 to E09b1-25].
10. **The sporting register for gunnery** (turf report where "prices and range lengthened") [E09b1-29].
