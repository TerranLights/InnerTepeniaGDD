## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-07 is.** The 1933 memoir of a regular infantry private of the 2nd Battalion, Royal Welch Fusiliers, as an eBook produced by Distributed Proofreaders Canada for fadedpage.com (eBook #20200322, posted March 2020; the header says the work is in the Canadian public domain but "may be under copyright in some countries"; the project manifest marks its U.S. status as uncertain, so I quote briefly and distill in my own words). The title page reads "By Private Frank Richards D.C.M., M.M. / Late of the Second Battalion Royal Welch Fusiliers." The book runs in twenty-eight chapters (the contents list at L63-138 is garbled, with numerals detached from titles). **My range is lines 1-3,515:** the front matter, then Chapter I "The Start" (August 1914) through the first sentences of Chapter XII "The Corps Commander and Quick Firers" (the range ends at the Old Soldier's transfer to the sappers and miners, L3514-3515). Chapters covered: I (mobilization, Rouen, Amiens), II (Le Cateau and the Retirement), III (Marne and Aisne), IV (Fromelles, October-November 1914), V (Houplines, winter 1914), VI (Christmas 1914), VII (Bois Grenier, 1915), VIII (Laventie, Givenchy and Cuinchy, summer 1915), IX (Loos, September 1915), X (winter 1915-16 and the R.W.F. Crater, February 1916), XI (Bangalore torpedo and a decoration ceremony, March 1916), and the opening of XII. The text from L3516 onward (the corps commander's order against pessimism and swearing, the Somme, Arras, Ypres, 1918, the Armistice and "Rewards for War Service") is covered by a companion pass (presumably E13b) and is not extracted here. (I read about eighty lines past my boundary, to L3598, by overlap; nothing past L3515 is used.)

**Evidence base.** A single narrator's retrospective autobiography, written roughly eighteen years after the events, in a plain vernacular prose with non-standard grammar left as printed ("had took," "was woke," "had did"). It is an anecdotal memoir, not a diary: dates and some sequences are approximate, and the narrator tells the reader when he is reporting hearsay ("I was told," "I expect"). Several passages carry flash-forwards about the later fate of named men ("killed at Loos," "survived the War"), a feature of memory rather than of the 1914-16 moment. The narrator is a professional regular, so the humor documented belongs to the 1914-16 pre-War-army culture of one regiment, not to conscripts or the New Armies, whom he mentions with condescension (E13a-30, E13a-82). Several recurring characters are named by nickname or role ("the Old Soldier," "Duffy," "Buffalo Bill," "Deadwood Dick"), and I cannot tell from the text whether any is a composite. Outside this text, it is reported that the book was prepared with literary help; the text file is silent on this and I did not verify it (the young "Mr. Graves" who appears in E13a-60 is plausibly connected, but that is outside knowledge). **Treat the voice as a published, shaped one.**

**What it does NOT contain (within my range).** No theory of humor and almost no direct statement of what jokes are for (the closest are "gave full vent to our feelings," E13a-73, and the Army's habit of yarns "to buck up the spirits of the troops," E13a-69); no songs except one hymn parody (E13a-43); no letters or verse texts (an officer's Christmas poem is mentioned but not given, L1465-1468); no explicit discussion of shell shock, "nerves," or trauma beyond one colonel's "nervous breakdown" (L1201) and the sarcastic "cure" for nerves at Cuinchy (a fear idiom vocabulary and a few eccentric cases do the work; see E13a-2, E13a-37, E13a-59); no executions carried out (death sentences appear as threats or are commuted, E13a-26, E13a-74); no post-war material except flash-forward notes; no account of the narrator's own grief beyond one line each ("I felt very sore after Stevens," "I missed him very much"). The humor on the page is almost all situational anecdote plus the characters' sayings.

**Format and damage.** The text is clean, proofread, and has **no page markers**; I cite **chapter plus line numbers ("L")**. Chapter openings begin with a drop-cap, and the first words are garbled or detached in places (L145-146, L263-264, L453, L636-638, L1122, L2554, L3060). Contents lines are scrambled (L63-138). Words that read as probable OCR or dialect and that I flag where used: "tollet" (L547, L1232, L1239; probably "toilet" or West-Country "tallet," a loft; my gloss), "troast" (L518, roast), "dicksee" (L1073, L1238; context says a cooking pot, my gloss: "dixie"), "wronk" (L1980, unclear), "readjust" (L2112, evidently "road"), "pound" (L2162 and L2593, apparently slang for the head; my gloss from the two contexts), "bloody bake" (L2162, unclear), "f ts" (L2629, a vulgar word with letters dropped), "the Gs" (L1412, probably Germans), a stray "T" for "I" (L1022). Spellings in quotations have been converted to American per project rule (for example in "carcasses" and "Colors"); archaic or proper-noun forms that the project treats as names (place names, regimental names) are left as printed.

**Quotation conventions.** Quotations are exact except for the American-spelling conversion and my square-bracketed insertions. **Graphic passages and the passages involving slurs, sexual content, bodily functions or atrocity are summarized, not quoted** (see the register). Period national, racial and class caricature is documented, not endorsed. "My gloss" marks any slang meaning the text does not itself give.

**Retrospective vs. period text.** Everything in the memoir is retrospective (1933). Where the narrator says "I expect" or "I was told" he is reconstructing; where he reports the Old Soldier's or Duffy's remarks he is reconstructing speech from memory, so quoted sayings should be treated as the narrator's rendering.

## Findings

### A. The memoir's frame (findings [E13a-1] to [E13a-4] span the whole range)

**[E13a-1]. The narrator and the "old soldier" persona: a regular private who writes down the talk of regulars.** (PW-07, whole range; title page L39-51; Ch. I, L145-149, L184-186; Ch. VIII, L2414-2438; Ch. X, L3260)
The narrator is a recalled reservist: eight years with the Colors (nearly seven in India and Burma), five and a half in civil life as a coal miner's timberman's assistant, then a private in the 2nd Royal Welch Fusiliers from August 1914. From summer 1915 he is a battalion signaller and runner (E13a-58) and in February 1916 earns the D.C.M. (E13a-77). His comrades call him "Dick" (no reason is given). The persona is the pre-War regular as a type: he knows the Army's ways ("pukka"), dislikes parades and drafts, takes pride in the regiment (no good Royal Welch soldier falls out on the march; the Royal Welch oldest survivor would post his own sentries, L2946-2948), distrusts staff and press. "Old soldier" is simultaneously a genre of talk (the yarn, E13a-5, E13a-56), a role (the platoon's capitalized "Old Soldier," E13a-22, E13a-55, E13a-72, E13a-73), and a standard of judgment ("old hands," "pukka old soldier"). The humor appears in three voices: the narrator's deadpan, the Old Soldier's grumble-satire, and Duffy's pessimism (Duffy is a time-serving soldier with six years' service, L227, "a pessimist in his way but a first-class soldier and good all-round chap," L607-609).
*Candidate application:* A veteran character can carry several comic voices at once: a straight-faced narrator, a grumbling sage and a fatalist, each valued by the others; the regiment, not the nation or the cause, is what he states loyalty to.

**[E13a-2]. Attitude to the war, the Army and fear: the war is a job done well by professionals; fear is a vocabulary of idioms and rare plain lines.** (PW-07, whole range; Ch. IV L718-719; Ch. V L1328-1331; Ch. VII L1656-1658; Ch. IX L2851-2852)
Within this range the narrator never states a view on the war's aims; the war is work, marksmanship is a source of pride (E13a-13) and the Army is judged by competence, not by flags. His grievances are against specific groups: staff and generals who "didn't have to live in the trenches" (L2051-2052), contractors (E13a-20), correspondents (E13a-73), clerical and civilian pieties (E13a-55), and rules that are ceremony without sense (E13a-14). The thesis he states plainly: "we suffered more in our morale than in our health" (L1330). Fear appears chiefly through idiom: to "put the wind up" someone (L809, L2027), "windy" men (L2261, L2389), "cold feet" (L826), "dead-beat" (L289), "in a state" (L1656); in third-person measurements ("McGregor reckoned that he had lost a stone in weight" during the short time six Uhlans were in a farmhouse where he sheltered a wounded man, L691-694); in compressed first-person lines ("a couple of seconds ... a couple of years," E13a-10); and in the preference for a clean wound (E13a-24, E13a-29). He treats open fear in a comrade with pity and an experiment (E13a-52) and treats compulsive daring as a curiosity (E13a-37). On distance from danger he is specific: "I never saw a general above the rank of brigadier-general in a front-line trench" (L2048-2050).
*Candidate application:* Fear in this voice has its own register of idioms and measurements; direct statements of fear are rare, brief, and tied to a concrete action or number.

**[E13a-3]. What the humor does, as shown and as stated.** (PW-07, whole range; stated: L2912, L3029-3030)
The narrator rarely names a function. Two explicit statements: the men "gave full vent to our feelings" over tea and rum when a newspaper reported the Loos attack as a joy-ride (E13a-73), and the Army's own yarns of French or Russian breakthroughs "were dished up to buck up the spirits of the troops," which the men no longer believe (E13a-69). Functions shown across the range: **cohesion and identity** (yarning, nicknames, shared grumbling, E13a-5, E13a-17); **boundary** against Territorials, new units and public-school soldiers (E13a-30, E13a-82), against civilians and the press (E13a-56, E13a-73, E13a-76), against padres (E13a-43, E13a-44); **resistance and rank negotiation** (the feather bed, the saluting contest, the dumped rabbit wire, "you may dismiss the guard," E13a-38, E13a-14, E13a-66, E13a-81); **discipline's escape valve** (the officer's grin that tears up a charge, E13a-42, E13a-49); **coping with the dead and the odds** (the dead as the lucky ones, the ghosts who grouse, "breakfast in Hell," E13a-39, E13a-51, E13a-63); and **prediction** (the Old Soldier's blunt forecasts of the Loos failure, E13a-62, E13a-63, E13a-67).
*Candidate application:* The humor is both a group's wall and its truth-telling channel: the sage's grumbles turn out to be accurate, and the officers' humor, when it appears (a grin, a quip), marks where rank bends.

**[E13a-4]. What the narrator does NOT joke about.** (PW-07, whole range; key passages L1692, L2503-2509, L2845-2846, L2957-2969)
No named comrade's death is made the object of a joke. Stevens, shot while the section laughs at another man's fall, brings "no laughing matter" (E13a-36); Miles and Pardoe are "tough luck" (E13a-19); Green's unfinished sentence on the telephone is followed by "how nearly I had been in Green's place" (E13a-58); Captain Thomas gets plain praise ("a soldier to his finger-tips," E13a-71), as do Fletcher, Lloyd and the man of the Buffs (E13a-28). The 1,500 casualties at Loos and the wounded crying out between the lines are told as facts (L2845-2846; E13a-67). Military necessity is voiced as "orders were orders." Two further limits: the narrator does not mock a frightened man's fear itself, only stages a prank around it (E13a-52); and he does not joke about the moment of his own danger beyond the single "couple of years" line (E13a-10). Where the dead are the subject of a joke they are generic or unnamed: the lucky dead and the grousing ghosts. The humor often resumes at once after a death (the lost breakfast, E13a-58; the cheer after Loos, E13a-71) without comment.
*Candidate application:* The boundary is a person's name: a comrade who has a name and a face is mourned in plain facts; abstractions of the dead are fair game.

### B. Outbreak, the Retirement, the Marne and the Aisne (Chapters I-III, L141-630)

**[E13a-5]. The opening pub scene: reservists on the night war is declared trade tall tales, each wiping out a whole enemy people.** (PW-07, Ch. I "The Start," L152-166)
On 4 August 1914 the narrator, a reservist and coal miner, is drinking with fellow old soldiers when the news arrives. Before it does, the evening is a storytelling contest: each man's campaign yarn destroys the enemy of his war (one empties South Africa of Boers, the narrator does the same for India and Burma, a third hangs Chinese by the hundred, and an old artilleryman closes the night by dropping howitzer shells on an imaginary mining village). The tellers are described as "a little top-heavy" by then. The narrator frames it affectionately and self-includingly: he counts his own India-and-Burma tale as one of the round. The slur and the mock-massacre phrasing in the third tale are not reproduced (see register). Mechanism: one-upmanship in hyperbole as the sociable form of old-soldier talk; the audience's skepticism is built in. The same register returns when the narrator's home friends later "put it down to an old soldier's yarn" (E13a-56).
*Candidate application:* A veteran community can bond through competitive exaggeration where everyone knows the rules; the genre is the credential.

**[E13a-6]. Pre-war soldiers' arrival habits: the Kitchener pamphlet ignored, and Billy's multilingual café order.** (PW-07, Ch. I, L195-221, L253-256)
On reaching any new station "we pre-War soldiers" first asked how it was for drink and girls; the Army's warning pamphlet signed by Lord Kitchener about French wine and women was, in the narrator's words, issued to no purpose "for all the notice we took of them." The comic set piece is Billy, who boasts he can make natives understand him anywhere, ordering wine in English, Hindustani and Chinese with one French word, then cursing the landlord for not understanding; the narrator reproves him and Billy answers with a blanket contempt for foreigners (summarized, not quoted). The chapter ends on a sly line about the men's cap and collar badges, given to local girls, plus "other souvenirs" that might be "a blessing or curse to the ladies" (a veiled reference to sex; no detail). Mechanism: the narrator-as-straight-man; veteran swagger deflated by the landlord's incomprehension; innuendo kept to a clause.
*Candidate application:* The old soldier's privileges (drink, women, ignoring orders) are stated as a code and treated comically, while the open contempt for locals is voiced by a comrade and answered by the narrator.

**[E13a-7]. The Retirement: friendly fire and the man under the pram are told in deadpan.** (PW-07, Ch. II, L336-364)
At dusk the 2nd R.W.F. fire on galloping horsemen they have just been told must be enemy; they were their own cavalry (19th Hussars). The narrator reports that several horses fell and then, flatly, "I never heard whether any of them had been killed." A civilian Englishman with a French wife had been talking to the narrator moments before; when the firing starts he is found under his baby's pram. He emerges blustering that he will report the soldiers; the narrator adds in parentheses "(He didn't say a word about his mother-in-law.)", and Billy asks what he thought he could do under there. The narrator closes the scene with "I expect that family fell into the hands of the enemy during the night." Target: the civilian's bluster and the hiding. The friendly-fire error is not joked about at all; its comedy attaches only to the bystander's hiding and the narrator's aside. Mechanism: parenthetical deadpan; the story is told so the reader supplies the joke.
*Candidate application:* A soldier narrating a blunder may keep a bystander as the comic object and give the error itself in a single flat clause.

**[E13a-8]. The Retirement's exhaustion: shared hallucination, gratitude as a joke wish, and envy as admiration.** (PW-07, Ch. II, L367-374, L405-410, L440-446)
After days of marching men sleep as they walk, carry rifles "all shapes," and see things: Stevens points out a "fine castle" that is not there ("Very nearly everyone were seeing things"). Ladies in a carriage get out so footsore men can ride; the narrator thanks them with a wish that "if the ladies had all our wishes they would be riding in a Rolls-Royce for the rest of their lives." A comrade named Slavin reports seeing Billy, in hospital with a touch of fever, riding in a grand car with two French ladies and waving so that "anyone would have thought he was a bloody lord"; the narrator's tone is admiring ("Billy was lucky enough to be sent home"). Mechanism: hyperbolic wish as thanks; envy phrased as a compliment about swagger.
*Candidate application:* Gratitude among hard men can be voiced as an extravagant wish rather than a thank-you.

**[E13a-9]. Narrow squeaks discovered afterward: the bullet through the tin of bully beef, bullet holes in tunics, a rifle barrel peeled like a banana.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L465-471; Ch. IV, L879-884; Ch. V, L1363-1369)
A pattern repeated three times: the lucky man does not know how close it was until a comrade shows him. Berry, on the Marne, finds a bullet has passed through his haversack, a tin of bully and one of his socks and lodged in the other; the narrator's closing line is "No, Berry didn't know what a narrow squeak he had had until I pulled his haversack off." After the mist patrol, Miles's trousers and the narrator's sleeve have bullet holes nobody noticed; before that Duffy had said the narrator "cleared the parapet like a Grand National winner." At Houplines a German sniper's bullet goes up the muzzle of Blacktin's rifle and splits the barrel "the same way as a man would peel a banana," leaving only a bruised shoulder; the narrator calls it "the greatest shooting feat" he saw. Mechanism: object-based deadpan (the hole in the sock), sporting simile for a flight, and admiring the enemy's marksmanship.
*Candidate application:* Survival stories can run on a physical object that proves it, with the survivor the last to know.

**[E13a-10]. The door-panel crawl: old soldiers work the rank system to push the risk onto the newest man.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L485-499)
Seven men must enter a locked house in an enemy-held village; they knock a panel out of the door, a hole big enough for one man at a time, and "each one of us had thought it out" that the first man through has little chance. One old soldier is "very nearly" persuading the young lance-corporal, with twelve months' service, that it is his duty as a lance-corporal to lead; the officers then arrive and order the narrator to go first. His own account of the crawl is "It took me a couple of seconds to crawl through, but it seemed like a couple of years." Nobody is inside; a back door stands open. Target: the lance-corporal (as a joke on the rank's burden) and the situation. Mechanism: rank etiquette used cynically; the persuader's pitch is the humor; fear is voiced only in the "couple of years" line.
*Candidate application:* Experienced soldiers can run the chain of command as a way to share out danger, and the one who is overruled then reports his own fear in a single line.

**[E13a-11]. Folk code and luck: "pukka soldiers" would not poison food; cards as the old soldier's Bible; a lucky gambler told he is marked for death.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L518-527, L599-609)
Finding an abandoned meal, the men reject warnings that the Germans are poisoning food and wells because "pukka soldiers" would never poison good food and drink; they eat until "we very nearly busted," then sleep in the beds. At rest the men gamble; the narrator's saying is "A pukka old-soldier's Bible was his pack of cards." Duffy tells the narrator he is "in God's pocket" with his winnings and therefore will be killed in the next action, a folk belief that lucky gamblers "very soon had their lights put out." The narrator describes Duffy as "a pessimist in his way but a first-class soldier and good all-round chap." Mechanism: soldiers' honor code stated as a joke; superstition delivered as a friendly insult.
*Candidate application:* A character's prophecy of doom for a comrade's luck can be affectionate; the pessimist is also valued as a soldier.

**[E13a-12]. The dead and the decorated are reported flatly; the medal for a wound gets a dry note.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L530-543)
Second-Lieutenant Thompson is badly wounded and lives "about half an hour"; two men carry him out, Jackson wounded in the wrist. The narrator records the awards: Jackson the D.C.M. and the French Médaille Militaire, Edwards only the French medal "because his wrists escaped injury" (dry: the wound is what makes the larger award). The paragraph ends by reporting, without comment, that the narrator was told Jackson was killed at Festubert and that Edwards was killed at Loos. No humor attaches to the death; the dryness is confined to the decoration rule. Mechanism: bureaucratic logic noted as a fact; later fates in a clause.
*Candidate application:* The Army's reward system can be a quiet joke on the soldiers who understand it, without disrespect to the individuals.

**[E13a-13]. Killing described in the vocabulary of musketry and sport; the dead are looted.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L553-560; Ch. IV, L858-863; Ch. V, L1356-1358)
The narrator calls fire on retreating men "excellent shooting practice," and the dead Germans in the village were "soon relieved of any valuables." Later, on the mist patrol, the narrator says of men running from their trench that "we bowled them over like rabbits," which "put me in mind of firing at the 'running man' on a peace-time course of musketry." Snap-shooting at stovepipes in the enemy trench, until smoke comes from dozens of holes, is also told as a game. The memoir's early firefights are narrated as marksmanship by men proud of their trade. Mechanism: the training-range lexicon as euphemism and boast. (Graphic details are summarized, not reproduced; see register.)
*Candidate application:* A professional's cold register for killing can be a source-based mark of competence and distance, not mere callousness.

**[E13a-14]. The saluting order: regulars answer an Army Order with a contest of profanity and a verdict that the War is lost.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L577-596)
A General Army Order thanking the men for their "magnificent spirit" is followed twenty-four hours later by another saying men have stopped saluting officers on active service. Both officers and men had assumed the rule was suspended; the men "simply stood to attention and answered 'Sir.'" At the next day's saluting drill "each one of us tried to outdo the other in our flow of language." The old pre-War soldier "heartily disliked saluting parade and church parade." Duffy's verdict: "we were bound to lose the War." The narrator adds, in a rare serious voice, that the time would have been better spent on learning "something about a machine-gun." Target: staff-level pettiness. Mechanism: collective profanity as the response; fatalistic prediction as joke.
*Candidate application:* Orders about form while men are dying produce a particular comedy: loud compliance wrapped in swearing, with one dry practical lament.

**[E13a-15]. A colonel punishes returned stragglers despite an order; the men offer "beautiful prayers" for his soul.** (PW-07, Ch. III, L612-629)
Men separated on the Retirement rejoin; Sir John French's order says not to punish them; Colonel Delmé-Radcliffe takes "no more notice of that order than a crow" and gives extra route-marching. The narrator says no good soldier of the Royal Welch would fall out except from fever and that the men "thought it very unfair." The man who had taken part in a bayonet charge two days before "offered up some beautiful prayers for the Colonel's soul: they would have been a revelation to any bishop." Target: the colonel. Mechanism: mock piety (curses as prayers). The narrator also shows loyalty to the regiment's marching ethos.
*Candidate application:* Curses are often called "prayers" in this voice; the resentment is real, the pride in marching standards is also real.

**[E13a-16]. Good NCOs, bullies, and an adjutant admired for his swearing.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L646-663)
About eighty per cent of the battalion at this date are Birmingham men whom the narrator rates the best soldiers and pals he knew. The adjutant "could give us all chalks on at swearing and beat the lot of us easily"; the men admire this. The narrator names two NCOs (Company-Sergeant-Major Stanway and Sergeant Fox) as the best he served under, directing the loading of a train expertly when the new company commander, whom the men call a first-class bully, raves at them. He then says that in any battalion "there were always a number of bullies," some among officers, and "bullies as a rule are bad soldiers, but he was an exception." Mechanism: competence-based taxonomy rather than rank; swearing as a skill that earns admiration.
*Candidate application:* In this culture, swearing skill, practical competence and fairness are what rank an NCO or officer, not the badge.

### C. Fromelles and the first battle of Ypres (Chapter IV, L632-1115)

**[E13a-17]. "Buffalo Bill" and "Deadwood Dick": the platoon names its revolver-waving officers after Wild West showmen.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L744-792, L803-805; Ch. V, L1374-1404)
The new company commander passes along the trench threatening to blow men's brains out if they are slow to squeeze aside; the platoon officer imitates him "in a half ashamed manner." "The platoon nicknamed them Buffalo Bill and Deadwood Dick." Buffalo Bill hurls a clod at the narrator, who thinks "All my discipline vanished" and calls him a swine, unheard thanks to the barrage. In the same chapter he is "as cool as a cucumber" under fire, with "plenty of guts, I'll say that for him"; the narrator also reports he would have shot a man firing high. The nickname stays with him throughout. Deadwood Dick, who gets on well with the narrator, later crawls out on a night patrol; the narrator mistakes his upraised legs in the mist for a German patrol and nearly fires; the officer's answer is that the narrator suffered "an optical illusion." Mechanism: nickname as comic deflation; the grudging-courage clause ("I'll say that for him") is the unit's honest ledger.
*Candidate application:* Nicknames mock an officer's style while the same men credit his nerve; the two verdicts are held side by side and neither cancels the other.

**[E13a-18]. Contempt for the neighboring Indian infantry: fear is read as cowardice, with period slurs and a crude pun.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L808-832)
When the narrator's right-hand neighbor battalion loses its white officer, the men firing blindly into the air and wailing among their dead are bullied by a detail of twelve R.W.F. men (all ex-India men who speak the language) sent each night to stiffen them; the passage includes beating and threats. The narrator concludes the troops "suffered from cold feet" (a pun on press explanations about the cold weather). The paragraph ends with a coarse sexual joke by an "old Expeditionary Force man" using a racial slur about what the soldiers were "good at." Period colonial attitudes, documented not endorsed. The only unambiguous humor is the "cold feet" pun, which turns the newspaper's excuse into an insult; the rest is contempt. Wording and slur are not reproduced.
*Candidate application:* A unit's own fear is told as bravery's near-miss (compare E13a-8, E13a-10), while a neighboring unit's fear is turned into ethnic contempt; documentation of that double standard is the usable point.

**[E13a-19]. Two named comrades die the same day; the narrator takes boots and puttees, and refuses the money.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L887-906)
Miles had just boasted of a sniping hit when he is killed by a head shot; that evening Corporal Pardoe, who had been on the mist patrol with the narrator, is killed in the same way. The narrator's sole comment is "it was tough luck on the both of them." The dead are carried back to a burial place behind the line; if "clothes or boots were in good condition we never hesitated to take them." The narrator takes Pardoe's good puttees because his own are "in ribbons." Pardoe's money belt held about sixty sovereigns; none "went back to his next-of-kin," and the narrator says he "didn't want to touch" it. No joke attaches to the men themselves. The only soldierly dry note is how thoroughly the dead are salvaged for use.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's practical stripping of the dead for equipment is stated plainly, with a personal line drawn at cash; the humor stops entirely at named comrades.

**[E13a-20]. Food scarcity and the contractors: rotten jam and rotten Maconochie's draw wishes for executions.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L913-919; Ch. VI, L1444-1457)
Early rations (four biscuits, a tin of bully for two, jam for six, a rum ration) include jam "rotten" from the start; the narrator derides the firm for the "stuff they put in instead of fruit and sugar!" and records a man's oath to shoot the managing director and all the other heads on return to England. At Christmas the good tinned stew is credited to two named firms, while another firm's tins held "a rotten piece of meat and some boiled rice"; the narrator says its head "should have been put against the wall and shot." Target: war profiteers at home. Mechanism: hyperbolic retribution (the men demand a firing squad for fraud).
*Candidate application:* A soldier's rage at civilian profiteers is voiced as a mock-execution wish; the target is distant, the anger specific.

**[E13a-21]. Trench sanitation and the spiked helmets: souvenirs rejected for latrine use.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L924-939) *[restrained handling]*
The narrator lists, in plain terms, the improvised sanitation of the first winter (tins, a pit later dug in a dip). After a night attack the dead wore spiked helmets; the men first think of keeping them as souvenirs, then conclude "it was no good keeping souvenirs of that sort when any moment we may be dancing a two-step in another world," and use them for a toilet purpose instead. "Dancing a two-step" recurs as the narrator's phrase for being dead (compare E13a-45, where the same phrase describes an angry butcher's dance, and E13a-51). The humor is the reasoning: the prize of victory is put to base use because the owner expects not to survive to display it. (Bodily detail not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* Trophies lose value in a culture that expects to die; the substitute use is a way of insulting the enemy while avoiding sentimentality.

**[E13a-22]. The Old Soldier's grumble-satire: the single wire a giraffe could walk under, and the Government feeding men to become tigers.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L959-968, L1050-1056)
The platoon's "Old Soldier" (a role-name, not a rank; see E13a-1) remarks that the British Government must be "terribly hard up": short rations, no rifle-oil, no shells, and now Engineers bringing up one single strand of barbed wire "which a bloody giraffe could rise up and walk under," enough "to make good soldiers weep tears of blood." On a day of three biscuits and a spoonful of jam he adds that the Government is trying to make the men "as fierce as Bengal tigers" so they will devour the Germans they kill. The narrator notes this was the last time Engineers wired the Battalion's front; afterward "we always put out our own." Target: higher authority's incompetence. Mechanism: itemized grievance escalating to absurd image; hunger as a joke.
*Candidate application:* The platoon sage satirizes the supply system in one runaway sentence; the practical sequel (do it ourselves) shows the satire has an edge.

**[E13a-23]. Friendly-fire on an artillery officer and a last-stand night.** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L988-1043)
Three men arriving unannounced from behind are challenged; two drop and one mumbles; the narrator's chaps shoot him; he is the young artillery officer who had been bringing tobacco, shot through the jaw and side while laying a telephone line; the men were signallers who left it to him to answer. "We never heard any more news of him." No joke. Later that night, with every rifle jammed from mud and heat, the men are ordered out in front to meet a bayonet charge to "the last man." The narrator's pal Smith says this is "our last time around" but he hopes they take a few with them. The enemy's rocket is "a very poor one." Men doze in line; the narrator says "Sleep will beat any man and under any conditions." No charge comes. Mechanism: understatement over expected death; the narrator mocks the enemy's flare; sleepiness as the limit of fear.
*Candidate application:* In a possible last stand a comrade's farewell can be dry and procedural; and exhaustion, not heroism, is the thing they remark on.

**[E13a-24]. Officer, cow and chickens: threats that backfire, a wound counted as luck, and a punishment that is "a blessing in disguise."** (PW-07, Ch. IV, L1077-1108)
Buffalo Bill orders an officer's servant to catch the escaped cow or have his brains blown out, then yells "Come back, you ruddy fool, and never mind the cow!" as the man runs into the enemy's view. A shell kills the cow; the man gets "a nice wound in the leg" and goes to England, where "I expect when he got home he blessed Buffalo Bill, also the cow and the German who shot him." The narrator generalizes: "anyone who got a clean wound through the leg or arm was an extremely fortunate man." Elsewhere Buffalo Bill forbids the pinching of farm chickens, saying everything on the farm is his; "not many hours later there wasn't a feathered fowl left." His favorite punishment, forty-eight hours' digging in a support trench, was "a blessing in disguise," since things were easier behind. The narrator sums him up: "he treated all men in the same way—like dirt." Mechanism: bathos (the officer's panic for a cow), the Blighty wound as luck, the men's quiet answer to a threat (the chickens vanish).
*Candidate application:* The wound that sends a man home is a prize; a comic chain of events can end as the narrator wishing the unwilling hero's blessings on everyone in the chain, including the enemy.

### D. Houplines (Chapter V, L1118-1405)

**[E13a-25]. The King's inspection: "more loyal or lousier troops."** (PW-07, Ch. V, L1131-1139)
At the review of troops by King George V the narrator says "No king in the history of England ever reviewed more loyal or lousier troops than what His Majesty did that day," since the men look clean but "in our shirts, pants and trousers were whole platoons of crawlers." The narrator had seen the same man as Prince of Wales at an Agra garden party in 1905 and says he looked no older. A sergeant is decorated that day. Mechanism: a pair of adjectives with the second a deflation; lice described as military units (see also E13a-32).
*Candidate application:* Reverence and filth are held in one sentence; the loyalty is sincere, the lice are the comic correction.

**[E13a-26]. Field Punishment No. 1 prisoners and "Broncho": the hardest cases are the best soldiers, and sentences are washed out under fire.** (PW-07, Ch. V, L1142-1190)
Fifty-eight men under Field Punishment No. 1 (tied to carts for two hours a day) are tied to railings in the street, where local women parade and laugh; one prisoner says he does not mind being tied but resents being stared at (with a national slur not reproduced). The narrator says "some hard cases" among them were "the finest soldiers, in action." Broncho, "the hardest case," is warned by the Colonel for the third time that he will be shot; he replies that he does not care. Next morning he gets another twenty-eight days. A fortnight later, with communications cut by shelling, Broncho shouts "I'll take the bloody message," goes through the barrage and returns with an answer; the accumulated sentences are "washed out." The narrator notes "most of his crimes were caused by overbearing non-commissioned officers" and the old saying "in the Army they tamed lions," but "Broncho was never tamed." Mechanism: defiance as running gag; the system's own contradiction (punishing then forgiving the same man) is the dry point.
*Candidate application:* The unit's worst offender is also its best volunteer, and the institution only knows how to reward him by canceling the punishment.

**[E13a-27]. The pigeons: a civilian's pedigree birds are eaten and the narrator plays innocent.** (PW-07, Ch. V, L1203-1246)
On police duty, a five-man detail (a corporal and four men) evicts civilians and "gave her best" to the one old lady who would not leave. They live "like lords" on poultry, wine and vegetables. A man with a permit comes for two prize pigeons whose photographs hang on the wall; he weeps, saying they have "pedigrees as long as ships' cables." The men point to their bellies as "empty as drums," and tell him to take whatever pigeons he finds. The narrator adds, deadpan, "I expect we had had a very expensive feed." Mechanism: a pantomime exchange across language, with the joke on the missing evidence.
*Candidate application:* Soldiers can treat looting as a comic misunderstanding in which the owner's grief is acknowledged but the verdict is hunger.

**[E13a-28]. Plain praise has no jokes in it: the man of the Buffs, and later Fletcher and Lloyd.** (PW-07, Ch. V, L1259-1276; Ch. VII, L2194-2236)
A soldier of the Buffs who killed six Germans in a café and died of his wound is buried beside them; the old lady will not let anyone sleep in his bed, saying "a grand soldier had died in it," and the narrator agrees: "the heart of a lion." (An adjacent civilian anecdote about an assault by a German soldier, told by an old woman, is passed over here; see register.) Later, young Mr. Fletcher, who spoke German "like a native," planted a flag on the parapet and was reckoned by all the men of B Company the bravest man in France, is told he has the D.S.O. and is shot through the head minutes later while looking over the parapet; "I never saw men so cut up over an officer's death." An officer who eavesdropped in no-man's-land is killed, and Private Lloyd carries him back; the narrator says Lloyd deserved the D.C.M. None of this is ironized.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's idiom for sincere admiration (guts, heart of a lion, "cut up") has no irony; the places where the humor leaves the page are marked by respect for brave men and grief for officers who earned it.

**[E13a-29]. Hardship told as practical technique: the chest-or-head wound calculation and wound-seeking in the water-bailing party.** (PW-07, Ch. V, L1289-1331)
Sentries stand head and shoulders above the parapet at night, and the narrator explains the logic: "it was better to be hit through the chest and shoulders than through the head; although that was all according to a man's luck." A man named Davies loses a thumb while bailing; "every one of us volunteered to take his place," and a man who got the bucket first "deliberately invited a bullet through one of his hands," but the bullet hit the bucket and "he spent the remainder of the day grousing and cursing over his bad luck." "Everyone was praying for a wound through the arm or leg, and some were doing their level best to get one." The paragraph then states the narrator's thesis: "we suffered more in our morale than in our health." Mechanism: wound-wishing narrated as a luck system, with the inversion that a miss counts as bad luck. (The wound details are minimal and not graphic.)
*Candidate application:* A defining culture of the stalemate is that a clean wound is a prize and a miss is grievance; the narrator uses it to show morale, not courage.

**[E13a-30]. Regulars and Territorials: contempt, then respect, then contempt again for a windy relief.** (PW-07, Ch. V, L1334-1353; Ch. VIII, L2376-2394)
The narrator guides a Territorial platoon (the 5th Scottish Rifles) in for instruction and gets lost: it takes an hour and a half for a ten-minute route. He says the regulars "never held a very high opinion of the Territorials," but "we got to like" this battalion, the best he saw. Later, taking over from a different Territorial unit in a neglected sector, the regulars find breast-high trenches, unused wire, and men afraid to work out front at night. They start working loudly on the parapet, "cursing flashes"; when an officer asks for quiet, the Old Soldier tells him to "put a sock in it" and clear out with his men "as they only made good soldiers weep." The narrator says Buffalo Bill "would have shot the lot of them at sight" and that a German raid would have had "a beanfeast." Target: part-timers' caution. Mechanism: loud work as insult.
*Candidate application:* The regulars' pride is mostly displayed by noise and rank-pulling toward other units; a unit that proves itself gets absorbed into the "us."

### E. Christmas 1914 and its aftermath (Chapter VI, L1407-1517)

**[E13a-31]. Christmas truce: banter, beer rated weak but gratefully received, and French civilians' disgust.** (PW-07, Ch. VI, L1411-1486)
Both sides post Merry Christmas boards; two men from each side meet in the open and the whole company follows, Buffalo Bill arriving too late to prevent it. The German officers lack cameras, "neither were our officers"; a Saxon says he worked in Brighton and is "fed up to the neck" with the war. A German company commander offers two barrels of beer and sends a tray of glasses; Buffalo Bill gives a plum pudding. The officers agree the unofficial truce will end at midnight, but nobody opens fire first and the quiet in fact lasts all of Boxing Day. On Boxing Day a German shouts across asking about the beer; the men answer that it was "very weak but that we were very grateful for it." The narrator's verdict: "French beer was rotten stuff." Relieved that night, the men learn nearly every British unit mucked in, and in Armentières French women spit and shout, to which the men reply "until we were blue in the nose," with the Old Soldier excelling himself in "bad language in many tongues." Mechanism: the truce told without sentiment as banter, trade and shared complaint; the civilians' contempt is answered with the profanity contest.
*Candidate application:* The truce is a day of ordinary talk; the narrator's tone toward the enemy is neighborly and toward the home-front critics (here the French) a rival contest.

**[E13a-32]. Lice as a campaign: the bath-house reissue, ladies' bloomers, and frozen shirts.** (PW-07, Ch. VI, L1487-1505; Ch. VII, L1719-1729)
Baths are rare; shirts exchanged at the bath-house still carry eggs in the seams, so "the heat of his body would hatch them." The narrator, who needed pants, found a pair of women's bloomers in a looted house; the lice "swarmed into those bloomers by platoons" (the narrator says they had "more room to maneuver"), he became "the lousiest man in the company," and Duffy and the Old Soldier said he looked "sweet enough to be kissed." Later the men hang shirts on the wire in hard frost to kill the lice, but the survivors recover and bite "as if they had had no food for weeks"; "they always beat us." The Old Soldier compares the louse's staying power to an unnamed female trait (a misogynist aphorism, paraphrased). Mechanism: military metaphor for vermin ("platoons," "room to maneuver"), comrade's teasing as mild bawdy.
*Candidate application:* Lice are the one enemy the men cannot beat; they get the army vocabulary because the men cannot defeat them either.

**[E13a-33]. "Jimmie" and the old 'uns: a lieutenant's 1918 remark that it takes a lot to kill the old hands.** (PW-07, Ch. VI, L1508-1517)
A popular lieutenant of the Third Battalion, nicknamed Jimmie by the men, who was always quoting Kipling, survived longest of the 1914 officers except the Quartermaster. When the narrator last met him in June 1918 as a second-in-command of a service battalion, he shook hands and said "it takes a hell of a lot to kill us old 'uns!" The narrator is glad to hear from a friend that he survived the War. Mechanism: shared survivor boast between an officer and a private, a rare cross-rank intimacy bound by 1914 service.
*Candidate application:* The 1914 cohort's survivorship forms a rank-crossing bond; the boast is the form of mutual recognition.

### F. Bois Grenier, 1915 (Chapter VII, L1520-2336)

**[E13a-34]. French civilians: the furniture thief, the old man robbed, and the priest marched at rifle point.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1538-1598, L2189-2193)
On police duty in a half-empty village, a hunchbacked carter takes his own and neighbors' furniture; an old man weeping at his own empty house is shown another house to loot instead; the narrator remarks "it was only a question of time before everything would be blown to bits." The carter is later seen running when a shell kills his horse, and the narrator imagines him a prosperous furniture dealer: "He was eminently gifted that way." The narrator and his comrades suspect a priest and a young woman of spying because shelling lessens when he visits (perhaps "the German artillery always packed up about this time for their dinners"). The narrator arrests him with a rifle in his belly and marches him to Headquarters, where the Adjutant and Colonel "burst out laughing." The narrator concludes "I honestly believe to this day that he and the young lady were spies." The text also notes that local French speak "proper old-soldier's English" and "could curse better than some of us, and not know it."
*Candidate application:* The comic effect rests on a young private doing a policeman's job with absurd literalness; the narrator's self-assurance in his spy theory is itself the joke.

**[E13a-35]. Ditch water, a patrol that plays dead, and a sergeant who walked the wrong way.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1624-1678) *[restrained handling]*
Orders against drinking from a ditch in which a patrol found dead bodies are ignored: "our insides were now as tough as the outsides of our bodies." On a night patrol the narrator and two others find themselves inside a large German patrol's ring and "pretending we were dead men," the narrator lying half in water; he reports "we were in a state." Another night, a sergeant insists the way home lies toward the enemy lines, the two men with him decline to follow, a flare proves them right, and the sergeant, who had threatened to report them, is hit by fire at the German wire and the body hangs there next day. The narrator tells it flatly with no joke about the sergeant. Mechanism: bodily toughness as a boast; rank's authority failing in the dark; the privates' correct judgment narrated without gloating.
*Candidate application:* An NCO's confidence and a private's survival instincts can collide fatally in the dark, and the survivor reports it as a fact, not an anecdote about himself.

**[E13a-36]. Stevens is killed while the section is laughing at a comrade: the humor stops mid-moment.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1686-1714)
The narrator and Stevens watch Berry, boots off and trousers rolled, slip on his back in water while fixing a hand-pump; "we burst out laughing." Then Stevens "dropped down in a sitting position"; a sniper has shot him through the head. The narrator comments: "but this was no laughing matter." Stevens lived about fifteen minutes. He was a married man with children, the narrator says, "different to the majority of us," who stayed faithful in France. That night he is buried in the village cemetery. The narrator then spends many hours in a hidden sniping post and says he "amply revenged Stevens"; he adds that when a man is hit by expanding bullets some men cut the tips of their own bullets, and "one man of ours whom I knew very well never went sniping unless he had cut the tips." (Wound and ammunition detail summarized; see register.) Mechanism: juxtaposition within one paragraph; grief enacted as action.
*Candidate application:* The comrade's death is reported in plain facts and answered with revenge work, not with jokes; the joke immediately before it is allowed to stand.

**[E13a-37]. The man who fired rapid rounds over the parapet, exposed, and "didn't believe in iron plates."** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1732-1744)
A man in the company wakes from every nap, seizes his rifle and fires fifty rounds exposed on the fire-step, then cleans it and sleeps again; "How he didn't have his head drilled through we could never make out. He didn't believe in iron plates." He is ordered to stop daytime firing but blazes all night instead. The narrator treats this as a curiosity and a feat of ammunition consumption ("more rounds in a week than what the majority of men fired in a year"), with no medical or psychological explanation offered. Mechanism: deadpan cataloging of an eccentric.
*Candidate application:* The narrator frames possible combat breakdown as a character quirk with no commentary; the comedy is in the arithmetic.

**[E13a-38]. The feather bed: a collective prank on an officer, carried out under the cover of ducking from shells.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1747-1786)
Buffalo Bill, after threatening to tie men on the wire at night as punishment, has a timber shack built with a stove, then sends for the best feather bed in the village, while the men sleep on wet fire-steps. "We didn't mind his shack or stove, but the bed was the limit." Duffy and the narrator dump it in a water-filled shell hole, roll it in mud, and hand it over wet. At the village they tell the N.C.O. in charge that they "fell down on a few occasions through ducking from shells" and "perhaps the bed had got a little wet." The officer roars that the N.C.O. must find a bed "if you have to pull it from underneath the President of the French Republic," and the N.C.O., who "knew perfectly well what had happened to the other," will not let the two touch the replacement and escorts it himself. The narrator ends in mock regret: "all our good intentions for our worthy captain's comfort had come to nothing." Mechanism: sabotage with plausible deniability; the cost lands on the N.C.O. in the middle.
*Candidate application:* The prank targets visible inequality (the officer's comfort) and is covered by the shared shell-ducking alibi; the narrator names the cost to the N.C.O. in the middle.

**[E13a-39]. A searchlight, men imitating stumps, and the ghosts who will grouse.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1789-1800)
Caught in the beam of "the most powerful searchlight," carrying parties freeze with heads on chests to resemble stumps or posts. One man predicts that when the War is over "the ghosts of dead soldiers would be marching over these fields every night, cursing and grousing," so that no farmer would be able to live there. The narrator adds that the men were dry only when frozen and that "nobody ever had a cold" that winter; the company had no trench fever or frostbite. Mechanism: the dead imagined as an unending grumbling column; the prophecy makes grousing eternal.
*Candidate application:* The dead soldiers of this culture are imagined as still complaining; their afterlife is an extension of the unit's habits.

**[E13a-40]. "To oblige him": the enemy mallet-man and the British pot-shots.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1803-1818)
The narrator observes that the enemy's trenches were as bad as the British and that several Germans were "deliberately inviting a bullet through their hands or arms" (see E13a-29). One raises a trench mallet, holds his arms still, and brings it down; the British watch through a periscope and "to oblige him" take pot-shots at his hands, with a man calling out when the mallet rises, but they fail; the German finally jumps out and exposes his whole body and is hit ("he had been unlucky"). Two men of the company try the same experiment on their own side and are not hit. Mechanism: a courtesy offered ("to oblige") to someone engineering a wound, with the same logic that grants a British wound as luck. (Summarized in restrained terms; see register.)
*Candidate application:* The enemy's wound-seeking is treated as a mirror of one's own, with a sardonic helpfulness that does not cancel the violence.

**[E13a-41]. Trench "ving blong": rum-jar errands and a drink "to make a man talk about his relations."** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1826-1837)
A Frenchman from Armentières sets up a drink shop in a solitary house near the communication trench; the men carry rum jars "pretending we were going for water," fill them with beer and bring back bottles of "ving blong" (the soldiers' phonetic rendering of vin blanc) in a sandbag. A pint and a half bottle cost a franc; the narrator says it later got dear and worse, "a grand drink; a drink to make a man talk about his relations." Mechanism: euphemism of the water errand; soldier-French as a respelling (my gloss on "ving blong").
*Candidate application:* The cover story for drink is a task the officer expects; the drink is valued for making men talk about home.

**[E13a-42]. "Fizzer" Green's defense: a deadpan courtroom fantasy that nearly gets him acquitted.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1840-1866)
Green, drunk, falls into a frozen ditch on the way up the line. At the Commanding Officer's hearing he says he has been a "strict teetotaller" for six weeks (his rum ration going to Private Elliot, "who could bear him out"), and that he falls because of "sharp lightning attacks of rheumatic paralysis in the legs," a family complaint through generations, one uncle having collapsed in front of a horse-tram and been killed. He asks for an adjournment to obtain medical evidence from home. "Everybody in the Orderly Room had a difficult job to refrain from laughing," and the C.O. gives him 28 days Number One instead of a court-martial: "I think it was the horse-tram that saved him." Green afterward judges that the yarn "was good enough to get a man dismissed on a charge of murder." (My gloss: "Fizzer" is not explained in the text; in Army slang it is an old term for a charge or a defaulter's sheet.) Mechanism: elaborate straight-faced fabrication; the audience's laughter is a sentence reduction.
*Candidate application:* An officer who cannot hide his laughter can reduce a sentence; the comic defense is a performance for the room, accepted by the room.

**[E13a-43]. Church parade, the hymn parody, and the shelling that answered the prayers of the parade.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1869-1896)
"Any pukka old soldier would have much preferred a dose of opening medicine" to church parade, which ninety-five per cent of the battalion "thoroughly detested." One Sunday German shells fall so close that the parade is dismissed; "that was the only time that we offered up a genuine prayer for the welfare of German gunners," and the narrator imagines "J.C. and his angels" doing "a quiet grin." The mouth-organ band plays hymns; "we liked the tunes of some of the hymns, but not the words," so the men sing their own lyrics to "O God, Our Help in Ages Past"; the example given is a four-line piece about John Wesley's thin dog being thrown into Hell (profanity in its last line paraphrased, not quoted); there were six verses, "each one a little more racy than the first." Mechanism: parody lyrics over a sacred tune as the men's own liturgy.
*Candidate application:* The hymn tune is kept and the words replaced; the parody is sung "with great gusto" at jollifications, an in-group ritual.

**[E13a-44]. The parsons: a drinking chaplain as folk hero, and a death carried in a pun.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1899-1923)
One chaplain "could drink our Quartermaster-Sergeant blind"; on his first visit he drank each company commander's whiskey out, then walked down the trench "as sober as a judge"; on the second visit every company commander said they had run out and he never returned. The old hands "admired him very much" and said that when he died he would be "put in charge of the largest and finest drinking bar in Hell." Another chaplain takes a groom's good billet with the old lady and her daughter and warns him off "my little bit of meat"; the groom calls him a dirty old pig. The "best parson" the battalion had, a young Wesleyan minister, "if he had listened to me one morning he might have still been preaching sermons in this world instead of preaching them in the other." Mechanism: the chaplain-drinker as folk hero; the last sentence is a euphemism for a death and regret, delivered as a flat joke.
*Candidate application:* The unit's judgments of padres (the drinking one admired, the one who warned a groom away from his billet's young woman scorned) and the way a death is carried in a pun on "preaching them in the other."

**[E13a-45]. Walking-out dress and a shot-up café: the Army reacts with one sentence.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1926-1937)
Out of the line, "walking-out dress" is a rifle and a 50-round bandolier, and men leave their drinks in cafés to fire at German aircraft. Two men "with a drop on" shoot up a café's bottles; another shoots up a butcher's shop; the narrator says "Perhaps he mistook the carcasses for Germans." The butcher dances and yells for the military police, who arrive "as soon as the man had expended his bandolier." The result: "Shortly after that, walking-out dress was abolished." Mechanism: the narrator's deadpan guess, the dry institutional footnote.
*Candidate application:* A unit's rowdy conduct is recorded without moral comment; the Army's remedy is a one-line administrative change.

**[E13a-46]. The Dog-Major: an officer's transport scheme defeated by a dog, and the animal's afterlife.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L1940-2009)
Buffalo Bill buys a big dog and a harness cart to carry stores to the trench; the appointed man becomes "the Dog-Major," on the lines of the Goat-Major who keeps the regimental goat. "The idea was all right but the dog had other ideas": the dog leaves the track, tips the cart, bolts at a shell, and requires an hour to recover. The narrator proposes shooting or bayoneting it and reporting shrapnel; the Dog-Major refuses. A comrade who believes in transmigration of souls declares the dog is inhabited by old soldiers and ten Indian crows and "no human being on this Ball of Clay would ever succeed in making him work." Bayonet jabs appear in the kennel; the Dog-Major is excused trench duty and told to live in the kennel; the cart is "blown to pieces," and the dog barks with delight. The dog stays with the company, learns to flatten against walls at the whine of shells, is lost, found by two old soldiers nine months later, and killed by a shell splinter behind the lines in 1917: "He had survived four or five months in a front-line trench but was killed miles behind one." Mechanism: anthropomorphic comedy; sabotage by animal and men; the sentimental obituary in one dry sentence.
*Candidate application:* A pet of the unit is adopted as a comrade whose shirking is cheered; the narrator tells the animal's end as an irony.

**[E13a-47]. A ration party saved by a puttee, and a death from a souvenir.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2012-2065) *[restrained handling]*
Carrying bread, the narrator and Bolton stop to fix Bolton's puttees just before a bombardment catches the rest of the party on the road; they bolt into a front-line latrine pit and arrive knee-deep, to the other men's loud insults and holding of noses; the narrator says most of the party were killed and "Bolton's puttees had undoubtedly saved us." The company officer holds his nose and says they smell "like polecats"; they scrape with knives, plaster themselves with clean mud, freeze overnight, and take "a bit of chaffing" for days. Six weeks later Bolton and two friends die trying to take the fuze out of a dud shell for a "nosecap" souvenir they would sell to rear-area men; one flings it down saying "Oh, bust it!" and it explodes; a fourth man is untouched. The narrator calls it "a very peculiar way" to die, and notes only that Bolton's face was unmarked. Between these stories he drops a line on rank: "it was as hard to get a new suit of khaki as it was to get a Divisional general to come up the front line"; he never saw a general above brigadier in a front trench, and the brigadier "didn't have to live in the trenches." Mechanism: the stench as comic relief in an episode where the narrator treats the lives saved flatly; the explosive death told without pun.
*Candidate application:* A near-death can arrive as a smell joke, and the same soldier's later death gets only plain facts; the contrast between the two registers is the point.

**[E13a-48]. Buffalo Bill leaves; the transmigration man's pedigree, and "a great soldier and a great bully."** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2076-2091)
Mr. Richardson, popular, leaves for the First Battalion and is later "killed at Fricourt on the Somme" (a clause). Buffalo Bill leaves too; the narrator calls him "a great soldier and a great bully" who "got some fine decorations and a brigade in the end, and survived the War." The comrade who believes in the transmigration of souls says Buffalo Bill had two earlier lives, as a great general and as a great slave-driver, and in his next will be "a bloody roaring Bengal tiger." Mechanism: reincarnation as a comic framework for a disliked officer's qualities. The replacement, Captain G. O. Thomas, "was a pleasure to serve under."
*Candidate application:* An officer who has been loathed and respected is summed up in a fantasy past-life biography; the dryness lies in the "survived the War" afterword.

**[E13a-49]. The AWOL and the rehearsed colic: "any excuse was better than none."** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2094-2151)
Selling jam to French civilians, three men linger over mixed wine and stout while two of them settle a dispute about who won the "Shwebo Grand National," a regimental race in Burma years before; shells interrupt the fight and the men arrive late for the Company's parade to go into the line, a charge the narrator calls "punishable by death" (and "at the very least" ten years). They report to the front line themselves. Before Captain Thomas they use a story rehearsed for five days: the other two had colic and could not move. "All old soldiers believed that any excuse was better than none." "Captain Thomas did a good grin," scolds them, and tears the charge up. Duffy and the Old Soldier say they would have been "shot at dawn" had they done it. The narrator settles the race dispute by naming the Old Soldier as arbiter, "an authority on races, particularly where regimental officers were concerned." Mechanism: pre-agreed alibi; the officer's knowing grin as acquittal.
*Candidate application:* A company commander with discretion can quietly absorb a serious charge when the culprits are good men; the story's role is to give him cover.

**[E13a-50]. Smith's wound and the doctor's answer: "Have you got the wind up?" "No, but I want to know the bloody truth."** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2154-2173) *[restrained handling]*
Smith, a comrade in the next bay, falls shot in the face; the narrator reports the track of the bullet and that Smith recovers consciousness and names him. The doctor calls him "the luckiest man in France"; asked if he has a chance, the doctor says "Have you got the wind up?" and Smith replies he wants "the bloody truth," which the doctor then gives in detail. Smith recovers, is sent back out, and "was wounded again in the head" on the Somme. The line "stopped it" and an obscure phrase about "the pound" are in the text (see OCR cautions). Mechanism: the doctor's diagnosis by idiom (to have the wind up = to be afraid); the patient's refusal of euphemism.
*Candidate application:* A wounded man who asks for the plain truth is rejecting the soothing register the staff uses; his tone sets the limit on the staff's euphemism.

**[E13a-51]. "Lucky ones": the dead imagined dancing, and a definition of Paradise.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2176-2186)
Around a fire, men debate whether the spirits of those killed early can look down on them. One says they certainly can, and are "dancing a two-step" in "holy glee" at having "scrounged out of this blasted misery" while the survivors are bound to be "popped over in the course of time." "They were the lucky ones and we are the unlucky ones." The narrator adds: "Our idea of Paradise during the 1914-1915 winter was a shelter over our heads and dozens of buckets of fire around us". Mechanism: the afterlife as release from the front; Paradise reduced to heating.
*Candidate application:* A bleak but shared view that the dead are the lucky ones is voiced as a jest over a fire; the definition of Paradise is a wish list.

**[E13a-52]. Rum: a ration too small to "make a louse drunk," the tiered sips, the shark officer, and the windy man given a swig.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2239-2273)
The narrator rejects press stories that troops were doped with rum before attacks: "In my battalion we never got enough of rum to make a louse drunk." The ration passes through the C.S.M., platoon sergeants and section corporals, each taking "his little drop," so "damned little" reaches the men; one platoon officer "was a proper shark," his platoon "sent up many prayers for his soul, which were not answered, as he went away sick." Extra rum before patrols or raids "made one reckless." A platoon member who is "one of the most windy men" the narrator saw, jumping at every shot, is secretly given a few swigs from a pint traded for two nosecaps; for half an hour "he was the bravest man in France," shouting at the Germans to come over and meet him, and the men have "a job" restraining him from "attacking the German Army single-handed." The narrator first says "it was pitiable to see him." Mechanism: prank-experiment on a frightened man; the cost of the joke (near suicide-by-enemy).
*Candidate application:* The unit treats a frightened comrade with pity plus experiment, and the comic result shows that his courage is chemical; the narrator does not mock the fear itself.

**[E13a-53]. Billy Townshend and the "bullet" in the backside: a private's prank on a proud sergeant.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2276-2308) *[restrained handling]*
Sergeant Billy Townshend, the Battalion's former middle-weight champion, proud that he does not swear like others but uses substitutes that mean less, which the narrator calls "a damned sight worse than swearing properly," returns from wiring with a torn trouser seat and a bleeding scratch from a barbed-wire prong. Duffy "gave me the wink" and tells him he has been shot in the buttocks; Billy runs "like a madman" to the Aid Post, where he returns next day claiming to have refused evacuation, in a tale of bravery. For a long time the two ask solemnly if he feels "ill effects from his bullet wound"; he "simply used to grin," because "it took a lot to ruffle Billy." On the same scene a man who got a bullet through both cheeks is reported plainly. Mechanism: a straight-faced false diagnosis; the target's retrospective embroidery. The prank works because a bullet wound is a prize and a fright. (Vulgar wording paraphrased.)
*Candidate application:* Privates can tease a respected sergeant about a wound; the sergeant's tolerance ("grin") is part of his authority.

**[E13a-54]. Talking across the lines: "Hello, Second Royal Welch Fusiliers, how are you?" and the violinist's encore.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2312-2320)
Opposite the Battalion the enemy knew who was holding the line and had English speakers; one night a voice calls out, identifying itself as the same regiment "that you spent your Christmas day with in Houplines." In summer evenings a German violinist plays opera selections; the men "always gave him a clap and shouted for an encore." Mechanism: the enemy as audience and as neighbor; applause in no-man's-land.
*Candidate application:* An intelligence-aware enemy's friendly shout carries a trace of menace and neighborliness at once; the narrator reports only the courtesy.

**[E13a-55]. "That poor old Chap above": the Old Soldier on both sides' prayers.** (PW-07, Ch. VII, L2323-2330)
Reading that British bishops and German clergy are each praying for victory, the Old Soldier says "God's truth!" that "that poor old Chap above must be very nearly bald-headed through scratching His poll, trying to answer the prayers of both sides." He would put all the parsons and ministers of both countries in a waterlogged trench for a week, after which "it's a hundred to one they would be offering up the same kind of prayers that we do now." Target: both churches. Mechanism: a reductio on divine impartiality and a test of what prayers become in the mud (the men's "prayers" are curses; see E13a-15).
*Candidate application:* The old soldier's anticlerical wit is about the clergy's distance from the trench, not the divinity.

### G. Leave, Laventie, Givenchy and Cuinchy, summer 1915 (Chapter VIII, L2337-2547)

**[E13a-56]. Leave: the soldier's true story is heard as an old soldier's yarn, and a dry line about Victoria Station.** (PW-07, Ch. VIII, L2341-2373)
The narrator's five-day leave (one day to go, three at home, one to return, carrying 150 rounds) is spent drinking; friends are disappointed he brought no German helmets, and "when I told them what use I had made of a couple of them they laughed and put it down to an old soldier's yarn" (see E13a-21). At Victoria Station the open platform shows regular soldiers returning, dancing and singing with relatives, "as happy as birds"; the narrator adds "I wonder how many of them saw Victoria Station again." He notes soliciting women outside and the later consequences for young soldiers ("wished afterwards that they had been blown to pieces"), and says his own caution was a matter of age and "afraid of the consequences." Mechanism: truth is mistaken for a tall tale because it is too coarse for civilians; the retrospective aside is flat.
*Candidate application:* The civilian-facing register gives the old soldier the problem of being disbelieved when he tells the truth; the platform scene is told with the flat hindsight of a survivor.

**[E13a-57]. A buried chest of wine, and the Old Soldier's idea of the proper state to leave a front-line trench.** (PW-07, Ch. VIII, L2399-2411; the narrator places it at the Laventie trenches)
Men filling sandbags at Laventie dig up a chest of bottles, including champagne, and by stand-to most are "three sheets in the wind"; one has to be stretchered to the Aid Post. The Old Soldier remarks that every man in a front-line trench should be carried out in the same condition. The man is sentenced to five years' imprisonment and has it washed out months later. Mechanism: a gloss that treats drunkenness as the sensible response to the position; the sentence, as with Broncho and Jones, is a formality later canceled.
*Candidate application:* A drastic sentence for drunkenness on duty is in practice a paper threat, and the narrator reports it as such.

**[E13a-58]. The signaller's privileged post, and the morning Green asked to swap.** (PW-07, Ch. VIII, L2414-2438, L2475-2509)
Warned back on the signallers, the narrator sets out the post's advantages (no trench work, no patrols, exempt from stand-to, billeted apart) and its single risk: "many a good signaller went West in the act of repairing a broken wire." His verdict: "A signaller's life on the whole was far more pleasant than a rifle-and-bayonet man's." On a day when he had been posted to a company that was not his own, a signaller named Green begged to exchange ("It won't take you a minute, Dick"); the narrator refused rather than unpack the rations. Next morning the narrator, on the telephone with Green in A Company's line, hears him say that they were having "a very warm time of it" and that he wished— and the line goes dead; the four signallers of A and Captain Thomas's servant had been killed by one shell. The narrator: "I thought to myself how nearly I had been in Green's place." No joke follows; the very next paragraph returns to Duffy and the Old Soldier, who "were both cursing flashes" because a trench-mortar shell had blown their bay "to bits and their breakfast as well" (the narrator: "I had lost one or two breakfasts that way and could quite understand their feelings"), while they had spent the morning bandaging wounded. Mechanism: the narrator's own near-miss is stated plain; the breakfast grievance comes straight after, with no commentary.
*Candidate application:* The ruined breakfast can take the grief its owners will not voice; the narrator's refusal of a favor, followed by its consequence, is carried without self-reproach or explanation.

**[E13a-59]. "A better place for a cure": a brickfield sector described as medicine for nerves.** (PW-07, Ch. VIII, L2458-2472)
At Cuinchy the sector is "absolutely alive" with rats, sentries blow whistle signals to warn of trench mortars (which way to scamper), the men have "necks like giraffes" from looking up, and mines may lift the trench at any moment. The narrator concludes: "it was a very healthy part of the Front, what with one thing and another, and anyone who were suffering with their nerves could not have come to a better place for a cure." Target: nerves, fear and the sector itself. Mechanism: sarcastic medical language over a list of hazards.
*Candidate application:* Fear and nerves are mentioned as a matter of tone: the place is called a cure, which acknowledges the condition while treating it as a regimental joke.

**[E13a-60]. "Mr. Graves": three small appearances of a young officer.** (PW-07, Ch. VIII, L2441-2447; Ch. IX, L2919-2928; Ch. XII, L3506-3508)
(My gloss: the text gives only "Mr. Graves" and "Young Mr. Graves"; the brief indicates the officer-poet Robert Graves, already extracted elsewhere in this folder. I did not confirm the identity from this text alone.) A young officer who joins the company is "soon highly respected." On his first night on patrol he and a sergeant bring back a large bottle in a basket from an empty German listening-post; "all sorts of yarns" circulate about the contents (a chemical for the German respirators) but nobody learns what it was. After the first Loos attack he "worked like a Trojan" bringing in the dead and wounded; when a sentry carelessly fires a round, he calls him a damned fool and asks "why he was starting the bloody war again." He was later wounded in the chest on the Somme, again in 1917, and "only lasted a few weeks." The narrator records the officer's joke-in-fatigue and nothing of verse.
*Cross-use:* Compare how this other-ranks view of a particular young officer sits beside that officer's own account in the extraction of his memoir in this folder; the two narrators differ in rank, register and what they choose to remember.

**[E13a-61]. The medical officer who kept old hands in the battalion, and the sick call dodged for "a sloppy parade."** (PW-07, Ch. VIII, L2528-2547; Ch. XII, L3509-3513)
The narrator, ill with fever and ague, is held at the Aid Post by the doctor because an old officer had spoken for him; later "it was very nearly impossible for old hands who was sick to get sent to hospital." Dr. Dunn "would doctor him himself" because he and the colonel "knew... the moral support that the old hands gave to the young soldiers." In the first year, the narrator says, nobody reported sick in the line except with serious fever; "when out of action a man would often fall sick to dodge a sloppy parade." Dr. Dunn, the Boer War D.C.M. yeomanry trooper, was "the bravest and coolest man under fire that I ever saw." Function: the old hand is valued as a morale resource; the narrator and the officers both know it.
*Candidate application:* The veteran as a unit's morale asset is a concept the regimental establishment itself voices; sickness, in this culture, is judged by whether it happened in the line or on parade.

### H. The Battle of Loos (Chapter IX, L2550-3053)

**[E13a-62]. The enemy's shouted warning, and the old hands' reasoning that the attack will fail.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2559-2615)
On 22 September a German calls across in English that the British may "come over on the 25th" and send their gas over, and promises "we'll smarten you up." Duffy, told this, says "the Jerries always gets to know things before we do," and "we all took it for granted that this was genuine information." The three old hands (narrator, Duffy, the Old Soldier) reason the attack through "from all points of the compass" and agree "we didn't have a dog's chance"; a "Young Soldier" calls them "three bloody old pessimists." The Old Soldier calls on "J.C. and his Angels" to knock sense into the youngster, argues that shells are not hitting the enemy trenches (the enemy would sit in deep dug-outs), and tells him to look through the periscope: "My old granny in one hour with a blunt scissors could cut more wire than what our shells have cut." The Young Soldier looks and concedes. They also worry the gas crews will "get the wind up" and that cylinders will be smashed so the gas settles on their own side. The old hands also blame short-falling shells on "Territorial batteries," which the narrator labels "very unfair." Mechanism: the pessimists are correct; the granny and scissors image is the expert's demonstration to a young man.
*Candidate application:* The old-hand pessimism is vindicated by events but is never presented as wisdom; it is the grumble of men who read the ground and the wire and were not asked.

**[E13a-63]. Whizzbang quip, the prophecy, and the Captain who "breakfasts" in a village that was taken three years later.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2628-2656)
Duffy says the men's own eighteen-pounder shells (soldiers' name, "whizzbangs") would do about as much damage as a bag of wind sent over. Leaving, the Old Soldier tells the narrator: "You mark my words, Dick," the attack will be a coarse-worded muddle and "unless J.C. is very kind to us the majority of the Brigade will be skinned alive." At the briefing the company commander describes pushing on to Haisnes "where we would breakfast," in a tone as though it were "a cake walk"; one old hand says that if the Captain had said they would breakfast in Hell "he would have believed him just the same, or better." The narrator adds in parentheses: "(This village was taken three years later.)" Mechanism: coarse-word understatement by Duffy; sarcastic counter-itinerary (breakfast in Hell); dry parenthetical dating the failure.
*Candidate application:* An officer's optimistic briefing is rewritten by the men as a trip to Hell; the narrator later dates the objective's capture.

**[E13a-64]. The last evening: the Old Soldier's "beano," the Red Lamp queue, and the glossary of the "Bible-puncher."** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2659-2687) *[restrained handling]*
Paid five francs, the Old Soldier proposes a "beano," "as it's probably the last day we will spend on this Ball of Clay." The three old hands have, after one look at a brothel on an earlier visit, found their own arrangements with local women; they visit them. In town a queue of about three hundred men, "mere lads," waits outside a brothel; Duffy says they are determined to have a short time before they go West. The Old Soldier, indignant that they are showing up the Army, tells them to swim in the canal to cool off, and when the narrator remarks that the old hands are on the same errand he is called "a bloody Bible-puncher" (a term for any religious man, with a "man that's carrying the brick," and for a teetotaller, "char wallah," "bun-puncher," "wad-shifter"). Mechanism: the old hands' sense of standards (their liaisons are respectable, the lads' queue is not) is itself the joke; the pre-attack spree is the Old Soldier's dark joke on his own survival odds. (Sexual detail not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* Sexual conduct on the eve of battle is graded by the old soldiers according to etiquette and dignity, with a stock vocabulary of nicknames for the abstainers.

**[E13a-65]. The time-expired men refused a place with the transport: "both married men and both were killed."** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2690-2717)
Two B Company men due to leave the Army the day after the attack ask the Colonel to leave them with the transport; he refuses and "dismissed them." The narrator: "They were both married men and both were killed the following morning." In a parenthesis he records that weeks earlier the Colonel scolded other time-expired men for wanting to go home when the country needed men; one replied that he intended still to serve his country, "but in a safer place and with no hardships to endure," with better pay (four shillings a day for home work against eighteen pence for a first-class soldier). Mechanism: the cheeky frank answer is the humor; the two deaths receive no comment.
*Candidate application:* An enlisted man's honest self-interest is voiced before an officer as an anti-patriotic quip; the two deaths that follow are told flat and are the cost of the colonel's refusal.

**[E13a-66]. The signallers' kit: reels of wire, flags, blinds and rabbit-hutch wire, "like Father Christmas."** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2727-2745, L2787-2788)
A divisional signals officer's "brainwave" adds to each signaller's load a reel of wire, two flags, signalling blinds, and a roll of rabbit-hutching wire "which with our full fighting order made each of us look like Father Christmas." The wire scheme "had been tried out umpteen miles behind the front line where there were no shells and bullets flying about and had proved a great success." The narrator: "I had never seen it before and I never saw it again"; he and Dann dump it in a shell hole. He comments on the general pattern of signalling "stunts" that were "an absolute washout" in attack. He had also refused to carry entrenching tools for an N.C.O., "nor for J.C. either," and Captain Thomas backed him. Mechanism: image of a loaded absurd Father Christmas; the staff test-in-safety irony; quiet disobedience (the dumped wire).
*Candidate application:* New gear devised in safe conditions is a recurring comic target; the men's remedy is to dump it and carry on.

**[E13a-67]. The attack at Loos told flat: ladders that do not fit, a drunk officer, "orders were orders," and 1,500 men gone in under an hour.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2803-2867, L2771-2784)
Before the attack the narrator joins Dann in a rum swig that Dann got off a drunk company storeman ("We at least were making sure of our rum ration beforehand"), watches the gas cloud roll a short way and "melt away" as the wind fails, and sees the trench "reeking with gas." In the front line the Captain commanding B lies dead of heart failure on the fire-step; a platoon officer, an ex-Guards N.C.O. commissioned into the Fusiliers, is "as drunk as a lord," and the Major tells him to "behave like a gentleman." The scaling-ladders are too short on the trench floor and too long on the fire-step. "Not a man in either company got more than thirty yards from his own trench"; the drunk officer is shot through the thigh and "very lucky"; the Major lies out mortally wounded and three men are shot down in turn trying to reach him. The Adjutant halts the remaining companies; the men in the trench are forbidden to fire at enemy observers who stand up on the parapet, for fear of hitting wounded men lying out, because "orders were orders and had to be obeyed"; and "in less than an hour... the Brigade had lost fifteen hundred men, the majority being killed." The narrator says the stopped companies would otherwise have been "kicking their heels up in a different world from this one," and calls the outcome, in a coarse phrase, a failure from start to finish "as the Old Soldier had truly remarked." Mechanism: flat statistical reporting; irony confined to the ladder detail, the drunk officer's luck and the callback to the prophecy.
*Candidate application:* The two comic elements (the unfit ladders, the drunk officer's lucky wound) sit in a plain account of the slaughter; there is no joke on the dead.

**[E13a-68]. Morris's cough and the storeman in the mud: skill at dodging, and a sudden dark parenthesis.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2870-2893)
Private Dale is genuinely gassed, coughing and vomiting; Morris is told off to take him to the Clearing Station and, per Dale's later account, "dug up a cough from somewhere that would take some beating"; the doctor evacuates both as gas cases. On the train Morris tells Dale he is not gassed but will work it to get home; at the Base hospital his coughs are "as loud as high shrapnel exploding" and he is in England within twenty-four hours "as a bad gas case." Dale is sent back up the line and is severely wounded four months later. Separately, the company storeman arrives "as drunk as a broken cart-wheel" with the rum jar and falls on his face; Deadwood Dick "trod him into the mud" in the rush to move off; "(The storeman was forgotten lying there face downwards in the mud and that was the last we heard of him.)" Mechanism: faked symptoms narrated with admiration; the parenthesis plays the forgotten man as a joke whose punchline is that no more is known.
*Candidate application:* Malingering done well is an art with an audience; the man left in the mud is both a comic image and a small abandonment.

**[E13a-69]. "Bloody B.S.": the Old Soldier's reply to a French breakthrough and the narrator's list of rumors.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2899-2916)
When a message says sixty thousand French have broken through in Champagne, the Old Soldier's only comment is "Bloody B.S."; the company "took it with a grain of salt." One old hand says that if it were true, the Germans had let them through and "roped them all in." The narrator adds the Retirement's best yarn, that a huge Russian army surrounded Berlin and gave the German Government twenty-four hours to accept terms or be "blowed to bits," and concludes that such news was "dished up to buck up the spirits of the troops," so that "we had now got so that we didn't believe anything." Function: boundary between the front and every source of news.
*Candidate application:* A unit that has been lied to reflexively greets good news with a brief rude abbreviation; the narrator makes the habit explicit.

**[E13a-70]. A major's death and the stiffened dead: where the narrator stops joking.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2919-2935) *[restrained handling]*
After the failed attack the company brings in wounded and dead overnight. Mr. Graves tells a few men how bravely Major Samson died, in pain he suppressed so as not to draw fire on lightly wounded men nearby; the details of the wound and the body are summarized and not reproduced. The narrator notes that some of the dead had stiffened in postures, one in a "boxing attitude," another pointing; a parenthesis recalls a man at Arras in 1917 stiffened in a rifleman's posture with a mutilated face "like a man who was doing a fiendish grin as he was taking aim," which the narrator offers as an image without a joke. Function: the passage records what the narrator will describe but not joke about.
*Candidate application:* The mimicry in death (a grin, a boxing stance) is something the narrator observes with fixed attention, but the closest he comes to humor is a simile.

**[E13a-71]. After the slaughter: a stolen rum jar, "no more cheerier men in France," and a captain's last remark.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2938-2969)
Next morning the narrator walks into a neighboring service battalion's trench and finds no sentries (they had lost some six hundred men), sleepers who drop off again when shaken, dead and wounded lying about; his comment is that in the Royal Welch the oldest surviving soldier would have posted sentries himself. He then meets the Old Soldier with two others carrying a three-parts-full rum jar; they fill water-bottles with it; for forty-eight hours "there were no more cheerier men in France than some of the old hands of my platoon" and many brews were made. Then he warns Captain Thomas, standing exposed on the fire-step, to keep down; Thomas answers "Thanks very much... I am watching the show over on our right," and is shot through the neck minutes later. The narrator: "We were very cut up over his death: he was a soldier to his finger-tips and a very brave and humane man. He always treated us as men and not as machines." Mechanism: the cheer after the carnage comes from rum; the tribute to the captain has no jokes.
*Candidate application:* Grief and relief share one heading in the same chapter: the survivors' cheer is chemical and short; the officer's death is mourned in plain praise.

**[E13a-72]. The gas cylinders: a curse list and the champagne-supper joke.** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L2972-2982)
Carrying the good gas cylinders to the left flank, the narrator says that if the men's wishes had any effect, the inventor "would have been in untold agony both before and after his death." The Old Soldier swears "for some minutes, hardly using the same word twice," then says if the gas is sent over again "it won't do no good": the Germans "will simply inhale it and think they are having a champagne supper." Mechanism: the curse as a catalogue; the joke's last image reverses the weapon's intention (compare the Rolls-Royce wish, E13a-8).
*Candidate application:* A weapon that hurt its own side becomes the object of fervent wishes against its inventor, and the enemy's imagined enjoyment is the punchline.

**[E13a-73]. Duffy's smoke trap, the war correspondent, and the medal "For Distinguished Lying off the Field."** (PW-07, Ch. IX, L3007-3052)
Duffy's deep dug-out has one outlet, and wet wood on the fire makes anyone who comes down unwelcome "very nearly suffocated"; the seated men are above the smoke. Inside, the Young Soldier reads aloud a famous correspondent's account of the 25th, in which the troops had "gone over the top laughing and joking as if they had been on a joy-ride back in Old England." The men, with tea and rum, "gave full vent to our feelings." The narrator says the correspondent was "the top of every soldier's roll" for lying; in May 1917 the men mistook a civilian visitor for him and gave "three cheers for the Biggest Liar on the Western Front," which the visiting civilians took in good part. The Old Soldier threatened to petition for a medal inscribed "For Distinguished Lying off the Field." The narrator adds that war correspondents in front-line trenches "were rarer than full generals." Mechanism: the home-front version of the war as the target, answered by a satirical decoration. The correspondent is not named in the text.
*Candidate application:* The civilian-facing, sanitized "laughing and joking" story is exactly what the men despise; their response is parody honors and public mockery.

### I. Winter 1915-16, the R.W.F. Crater, and the decoration ceremony (Chapters X-XII, L3055-3515)

**[E13a-74]. Private Jones: a first-rate soldier deserts before an attack over five francs of pay, gives himself up by a trick, and is sent back as a sergeant.** (PW-07, Ch. X, L3060-3123)
Jones, a "grand front-line soldier," has his field punishment washed out for patrol work, but the young attached officer refuses him his pay, fearing he will be "getting in trouble again." Jones, who had received no pay for twelve months, says "no bloody pay, no bloody fighting," and falls out on the march to the assembly trenches. The narrator and the old hands knew "he would keep his word"; the narrator states plainly "There was only one penalty for being absent from an attack, and that was death." Jones spends three months drifting through the rear areas on lorry lifts with no one questioning him, until, helping an Assistant Provost-Marshal's men, he asks the A.P.M. to "put me in the clink" with the man he has just helped arrest. The narrator tells him he will be shot and that he had better fatten him up first; Jones "didn't care a damn about being shot, but on the other hand he didn't mind being fattened up a bit." The death sentence is commuted to ten years; after three weeks in prison, offered the front again, Jones says "I don't mind ... as long as I don't have to go back to my old battalion." He is later a full sergeant in a service battalion; "Good soldiers were not kept very long in prison after the year 1915." Mechanism: deadpan rendering of a principled walkout; the narrator's tone is comradely rather than condemnatory.
*Candidate application:* Desertion as a labor-style grievance over unfair treatment, forgiven by the Army when the soldier is a good one, is a distinct pattern from fear-based flight; the memoir tells it as a comic tale.

**[E13a-75]. Waders, Tich and the language that "improved fifty per cent."** (PW-07, Ch. X, L3126-3163)
At Christmas the signallers "bought two chickens and pinched seven." The long thigh waders sink men into mud; the man releases himself by changing to boots and, falling on his back, utters curses "loud and deep enough to blow up every wader factory in the world." A five-foot signaller, Tich, who is a poor swimmer, is fished out of the flooded trench "like a young walrus"; he wants to go over the top in daylight, "he might as well be bloody well shot as drowned," and by the time they reach Headquarters "his language had improved fifty per cent." Mechanism: the percentage joke measures profanity as a reading of emotion; Tich is a nickname for a small man (my gloss). The narrator also describes "getting a good breaking in" of the new man.
*Candidate application:* Profanity as the gauge of distress: the narrator scales it up and down as a thermometer.

**[E13a-76]. Leave, again: the drunken presentation speech in which Tiny confesses to looting the dead.** (PW-07, Ch. X, L3166-3179)
On a second leave the narrator is a guest of honor at a "smoker" at a hotel in Blaina; he receives a pipe and pouch. The drunk old soldier Tiny, given a wristwatch, thanks the Chairman but says he has "pinched dozens of better watches than this one from the dead Jerries laying out between the lines" and calls on Richards to confirm it. "The majority of the company took it in good part: they knew Tiny very well." Mechanism: a truthful confession that the civilians take as comic bravado; the narrator watches and records, without objecting. (The looting is not graphic.)
*Candidate application:* The home audience's inability to hear the literal truth of a soldier's remark (compare E13a-56) is the protection that lets the soldier say it.

**[E13a-77]. The R.W.F. Crater: a "cheery" warning, a signalers' pact, and "we were the lucky ones."** (PW-07, Ch. X, L3194-3266)
Before a silent night attack to retake a mine crater, the narrator is told by a man of the holding battalion, after learning that an earlier party of 200 failed ("two hundred didn't get back, not by a big number"), that "you'll surprise me greatly if you do capture that crater." The narrator: "He was such a cheery individual that I was delighted when 11 p.m. arrived." The narrator and Paddy agree, if one falls, the other carries on; once the crater is taken, they keep the line alive by alternately running out to repair breaks under a barrage, and the narrator says of Paddy's return, "I didn't expect to see him come back, but he did." Sixty casualties; the crater is named for the regiment. The narrator and Paddy and two sergeants receive the D.C.M.: "We had did no more than the other men; we were the lucky ones." He adds that a man recommended in a successful operation has "far more chance" of a medal than in a failed one. Mechanism: gallows irony of the "cheery individual"; the narrator's honest deflation of his own decoration.
*Candidate application:* The narrator reports his honor as luck and attributes it to the outcome of the operation, not to the individual deed.

**[E13a-78]. The Bangalore Torpedo: an invention mocked, a volunteer trio, an explosion that was a shell, and a hoax by the enemy.** (PW-07, Ch. XI, L3274-3342)
A new wire-cutting device arrives, tried successfully "in some Back Area or other." The men say that if its inventors had to fix it on the wire in no-man's-land "they'd not reach halfway across" before returning "to change their under-pants." No one answers the first call for volunteers; at the second, three old soldiers volunteer "as a matter of Regimental esprit de corps," led by "Freezer," an old India hand who is "continually" on Field Punishment and has sentences washed out. They place it; a group of officers count down the seconds, "five seconds to go... CRASH!" which is a German shell exploding near them; the torpedo fails. By day an old soldier tells the young men the torpedo "hadn't finished yet" and will go off and roam the German Front, then back areas, then Germany, with "a rage against barbed wire," until no strand "large enough to stick a louse with" remains. Brigade orders it retrieved so the enemy will not get "the Secret of it"; the same three go; Freezer says it is "a damned sight lighter"; the Germans had emptied it and hung the shell back on their wire for the British to fetch, which explains why they did not fire; "I expect they did a good grin over the Secret of that torpedo." The three got an extra drop of rum, not medals. Mechanism: mock-epic prophecy of a monster device; the enemy's practical joke on British secrecy; the volunteer ethos with a regimental name for it.
*Candidate application:* The volunteers act out of regimental pride, are rewarded with rum, and are the butt of an enemy prank that the narrator treats with admiration.

**[E13a-79]. "Nutty" the batman's supper: a shelling interrupts, and the joke is French pidgin.** (PW-07, Ch. XI, L3359-3379) *[restrained handling]*
Nutty, a time-expired batman and "proper Don Juan" of whom no fowl between Houplines and Bethune "wouldn't attempt to rise on the wing and fly away in terror," hosts the narrator with roast duck, champagne and two refugee women. When a shell lands in the yard during a private interlude, a woman exclaims in pidgin that the Germans are bombarding; Nutty answers (in the same pidgin) that Nutty will be doing so too in one minute; the narrator "and the young lady with me burst out laughing"; Nutty calls for quiet. "It hadn't cost Nutty a cent. He wasn't the Regimental-Sergeant-Major's batman for nothing." Mechanism: a sexual pun in broken French; the batman as quartermaster-level scrounger. (Sexual content summarized, wording of the pun not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* The scrounger's gallantry and the sexual joke under shellfire are both treated as the privileges of a batman; the narrator is audience and beneficiary.

**[E13a-80]. Sunday cock-fights, and the café family weeping over a stuffed rooster.** (PW-07, Ch. XI, L3381-3406)
The narrator prefers the mining villages' Sunday (church, café, cards, then cock-fights) to English Sundays; cock-fighting was banned in mid-1916 "until the War was over. It went on just the same." In one café a stuffed cockerel prompts the landlord, then his wife, then their nineteen-year-old daughter to burst into tears ("going at it as if tears were her life's profession"); he had been the district champion for three years and won thousands of francs, and was found dead in his cot. The couple says they would sooner have lost a child. The narrator adds a remark about these people's fondness for money (a period stereotype, not reproduced) and says he "quite believed" them. Mechanism: grief over a bird in a region where men are dying, observed with dry sarcasm.
*Candidate application:* Civilian mourning for an animal is a comic contrast the soldiers notice; the narrator's amusement, though dismissive, is not cruel.

**[E13a-81]. The D.C.M. ceremony: a private returns a general's salute, and tells a general the line is dirty "in more ways than one."** (PW-07, Ch. XI, L3409-3468)
Summoned from the Cuinchy trenches to be decorated, the narrator and Paddy ride in the Brigadier's car; the guard turns out for a General Salute at the flag on the car, and the narrator leans out of the window, raises a hand and says "All right, Sergeant ... you may dismiss the guard!" "We could still hear his language after the car had driven into the Barracks." The parade troops, warned three days before, are polished enough "to have mounted guard at Buckingham Palace," while the recipients, unwashed for three days, are "as black as two sweeps." General Munro asks where the narrator came from; Cuinchy; he says it must be very dirty there; "I told him it was, and in more ways than one; and a good honest grin swept over his face." After this, parades for decorations were scheduled in advance, and the men now washed and shaved on relief day in boiled shell-hole water. Mechanism: a private's impersonation of a general by gesture, and an innuendo to a general that draws a grin.
*Candidate application:* The decorated soldier's license to joke is limited but real: a moment when a general's grin signals he accepts the dig at the staff.

**[E13a-82]. The decoration inflation, the Public Schools battalion, and the Old Soldier's transfer.** (PW-07, Ch. XI, L3478-3490; Ch. XII, L3497-3515)
The narrator reports an old officer's remark that the M.C. was "simply introduced to save awarding too many D.S.O.s," and says of the Military Medal that "without a shadow of a doubt" it was introduced "to save awarding too many D.C.M.s," with no grant and "fifty Military Medals" for each D.C.M. "The old regular soldiers thought very little of the new decoration." Joining the brigade, the 20th Royal Fusiliers (a Public Schools battalion) are called "very decent chaps but hopeless as soldiers; the only thing they ever became proficient in was swearing," and their mail "twice as large as the rest of the Brigade's put together." The segment ends, at the limit of this pass, with a plain line: the Old Soldier has been transferred to the sappers and miners and "I missed him very much." Mechanism: cynical bookkeeping on honors; class-affectionate teasing of the new unit's competence; a rare unironic statement of missing a comrade.
*Candidate application:* The regular's contempt for new honors and new units is a cohesion marker; the departure of the platoon's sage is registered once, without a joke.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions.**
- Cohesion and identity: the yarn contest and old-soldier talk (E13a-5, E13a-56), nicknames (E13a-17, E13a-83), regiments' pride in marching and shooting (E13a-13, E13a-15), rank-crossing survivor bond ("us old 'uns," E13a-33).
- Boundary: against Territorials and new units (E13a-30, E13a-82), against civilians and the press (E13a-56, E13a-73, E13a-76), against padres (E13a-43, E13a-44), against neighboring Indian infantry (E13a-18, in contempt).
- Resistance and rank negotiation: collective profanity answering orders (E13a-14); sabotage with plausible deniability (E13a-38); dumping staff gadgets (E13a-66); the N.C.O. who will not let the culprits touch the replacement (E13a-38); the officer's grin that tears up a charge (E13a-42, E13a-49); a private's dismissal of a guard and joke to a general (E13a-81); open walkout (E13a-74).
- Coping: the dead as lucky ones and Paradise as a shelter and a fire (E13a-51); ghosts who still grouse (E13a-39); the wound as a prize (E13a-24, E13a-29); lice as an enemy army (E13a-32); rum after slaughter (E13a-71).
- Prediction as truth-telling: the Old Soldier's correct forecasts of Loos (E13a-62, E13a-63, E13a-67).

**Targets.** The Army's staff, orders and inventions (E13a-14, E13a-22, E13a-59, E13a-66, E13a-78); decorations (E13a-12, E13a-77, E13a-82); contractors (E13a-20); correspondents (E13a-73); clergy (E13a-43, E13a-44, E13a-55); company officers as caricatures (E13a-17, E13a-24, E13a-38); a proud sergeant (E13a-53); frightened comrades (E13a-52, gently) and civilians (E13a-7, E13a-27, E13a-34, E13a-80); the enemy treated with a combination of admiration and neighborliness (E13a-9, E13a-31, E13a-54, E13a-78). The narrator himself is a target in several stories (E13a-10, E13a-32, E13a-34).

**Forms and mechanisms.** Deadpan and parenthetical asides (E13a-7, E13a-68, E13a-9); understatement ("a very healthy part of the Front," E13a-59; "tough luck," E13a-19); euphemism for death ("went West," "a two-step," "stopped it," E13a-83, E13a-51); mock-piety (curses called prayers, E13a-15, E13a-52, E13a-55); mock-legal rhetoric (E13a-42); tall tales and prophecies (E13a-5, E13a-78); hyperbolic wishes against inventors and ladies alike (E13a-8, E13a-72); nicknames from the Wild West (E13a-17); practical jokes with alibi (E13a-38, E13a-53); hymn parody (E13a-43); the percentage joke measuring profanity (E13a-75); simile from the training range for killing (E13a-13); the abbreviation "B.S." for officially supplied news (E13a-69).

**Rules of license.** Old hands joke with and about officers more freely than new drafts; officers are judged by conduct in a trench ("guts") and a grudging-courage clause is attached even to a hated one (E13a-17). The Adjutant is admired for out-swearing the men (E13a-16). Rank mediates licence: the N.C.O. protects the culprits but is not told the truth (E13a-38); the company commander's discretion can absorb a serious charge if the culprits are good men and bring a story (E13a-49); a decorated man may joke to a general and be grinned at (E13a-81). A joke at a sergeant's expense is allowed to a private if the sergeant is "not ruffled" (E13a-53).

**Limits and failure modes.** Humor stops at named deaths (E13a-4, E13a-36); the narrator's grief is shown as revenge sniping (E13a-36); the old soldier's contempt for others' fear is applied unevenly (E13a-18 vs. E13a-10, E13a-52); the humor can turn to contempt for an ethnic outgroup (E13a-18) and to cold sporting vocabulary for killing (E13a-13); a prank on a frightened man risks his death (E13a-52); the staged "to oblige him" courtesy shades into acceptance of violence (E13a-40); eccentric behavior that may be breakdown is told as a quirk (E13a-37); the humor resumes after deaths with no transition (E13a-58, E13a-71).

**Time course.** In the range, 1914 improvisation and hunger become the 1915-16 routine of rotted jam, rats and lice, and institutional targets (decorations, corps orders); the earliest sporting killings (E13a-13) are followed by the later moral weight of Loos (E13a-67). The narrator's own flash-forwards ("killed at Loos," "survived the War," "taken three years later") add a retrospective dryness (E13a-12, E13a-63).

**Civilian-facing register.** The truthful soldier is heard as a yarn-spinner (E13a-56, E13a-76); the official and press story is a "joy-ride" the men despise (E13a-73); French civilians' hostility to the Christmas truce is answered with profanity (E13a-31); a landlord weeps over a rooster and the narrator notes the contrast (E13a-80).

**Defector/dissent specifics.** Private Jones's desertion before an attack as a principled strike over pay, and his clever surrender (E13a-74); Broncho and Freezer, repeat offenders whose sentences are canceled for bravery (E13a-26, E13a-78); the time-expired man's honest answer to a colonel (E13a-65); the men who feign illness (E13a-61, E13a-68). The narrator does not treat any of these men with contempt.

**Cross-cultural / era.** All of this is the British Army's pre-War regular infantry in 1914-16, in one regiment with its own customs (the Goat-Major, pride in marching). Slang is of that army and of its India service (wallah, pukka, "Ball of Clay").

## Gaps and cautions

- **Range boundary.** My extraction stops at L3515 (the Old Soldier's transfer). The following passage in the same chapter (the Corps Commander's order against pessimism and swearing, trench sapheads, the rat that Dann takes as an omen) lies in the other pass's range and is not used.
- **Retrospective shaping.** The memoir is written eighteen years after; quoted sayings are the narrator's reconstruction; named characters may be composites; the book may have been edited by others (not verified here). Do not treat any one-liner as a verbatim trench joke.
- **Only one rank, one regiment, one army, one phase.** The Royal Welch regular's voice of 1914-16 does not speak for conscripts, the New Armies, other regiments, or later years. Many of the strongest patterns here (the old hand as sage; wound-wishing; nicknames from the Wild West) may be specific to that voice.
- **What the sources leave open.** The text does not say what the Old Soldier and Duffy, the two main comic voices, were like outside the narrator's telling; it does not give the later fate of Duffy or the Old Soldier within my range (the Old Soldier is transferred, E13a-82; Duffy is last seen "glum," L3182-3184); it does not say whether the young "Mr. Graves" of the range is the officer-poet.
- **Handled-with-restraint material.** Racial and ethnic contempt (E13a-5, E13a-6, E13a-18), brothel and sexual material (E13a-6, E13a-56, E13a-64, E13a-79), bodily-function material (E13a-21, E13a-47, E13a-63) and graphic wounds and deaths (E13a-35, E13a-36, E13a-47, E13a-50, E13a-70) are summarized, not quoted; the original wording should be read in a browser if its register matters.
- **OCR/dialect items** listed in the Source-shape notes (for example "tollet," "pound," "wronk," "bloody bake") should be checked against a page scan before relying on any gloss.
- **Quotations** are checked against the text file only; no printed edition was consulted.
