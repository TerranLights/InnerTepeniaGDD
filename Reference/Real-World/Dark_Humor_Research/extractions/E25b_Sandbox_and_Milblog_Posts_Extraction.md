## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What the unit is.** Forty-six short posts, about 46,000 words: 35 from Garry Trudeau's Doonesbury "The Sandbox" troop-blog archive (2006-2011), read from Internet Archive copies of the monthly archive pages because the original TypePad site is gone, and 11 from five personal milblogs (Eighty Deuce on the Loose, Fobbits Need Ice Cream Too, Old Blue's blog, Teflon Don's Acute Politics, desertflier). All are first-person, nonfiction, written by the people involved during or shortly after the experience, which makes this the closest thing in the collection to real-time Iraq and Afghanistan voices of ordinary troops. Authors are mostly junior and mid-grade Army officers and NCOs, a few Air Force officers, one Navy corpsman, one chaplain, one Reserve-component spokesman, one nurse (two posts), and one military spouse. **The brief mentions "A Nurse" as a post signature; no file in this folder is signed that way. The only nurse-authored posts are the two by RN Clara Hart.**

**Evidence base and its limits.** Single posts, not a random sample. The harvest scored 865 Sandbox posts by humor keywords and read the candidates, so the selection is **biased toward posts that mention or perform humor** (the harvest log itself says posts using no keyword, such as understatement and mock-formal prose, may have been missed). The Sandbox was an edited, public, civilian-facing venue: authors submitted to an editor ("David," named in the Traversa post) and knew a general readership would see them; these are *not* private diaries. Several posts are explicitly written *for* a civilian audience (B.C., Air Force Wife, JP, the Four Amigos). Authors are self-selected writers: the posts are articulate, literary in places and aware of their own humor, and one author (Adrian B.) names the blog "The Satirist at War." **Almost nothing here is verbatim barracks talk;** it is humor as the writer chooses to render it for readers, with OPSEC redaction, a "line I won't cross" (Eddie), a platform editor, and one "[Removed for screening]" gap in a milblog post.

**Identification cautions.** Seven posts signed "Adrian B." are filed as `bonenberger_*`; the surname is the harvester's inference and does not appear in any post. I refer to the author as **Adrian B.** The milblog "Kaboom: A Soldier's War Journal" is named in one LT G post, but the posts themselves give only "LT G," so I use that. `sandbox_airforcewife_for_civilians` is by a military **spouse**, not a veteran, with the husband deployed and (per the post) casualties taken by the husband's colleagues. Doc in the Box is a Navy **corpsman**; no post here is by a physician. Old Blue's header changes between posts ("Stationed in Afghanistan" in 2007-08; "Returned to: Afghanistan" in March 2010, after he trained NATO staff in Germany).

**Format damage.** Photos are dropped (captions remain: Semper Gumby, Four Amigos); email addresses are redacted; the Sandbox's own metadata block (Name, Stationed in, Hometown, Milblog) precedes each post. Place and unit names are replaced by "[OPSEC]" or single letters in several posts. A few posts are lightly garbled in transcription (Revenge of the Plan's glossary lists "RTF" for a term used as "RTB"). Index "why it matters" lines are the harvester's gloss; I used them only as a pointer and checked each against the text. At least one index line overstates the humor content (see Gaps: Old Blue's Landstuhl post is mostly about a charity volunteer); in several others (Cleland, McGee) the humor is a few lines inside a post about something else.

**What this unit does NOT contain.** No defector, deserter or ex-military-in-exile voice; the only "after service" voices are McGee (weeks home), the Usual Suspect (days from discharge), LT G on leave, desertflier (19 days home), Joe (between deployments), @WR and Raab. No veterans ten or more years out. No women combatants' own posts (the female voices are a nurse, a spouse, and incidental). No enemy or local-population voices except as quoted by Americans. No direct recording of the humor as spoken: all of it is rendered in prose. No data on how readers responded (comments were not captured). Most authors appear to be U.S. Army (branch is often not stated, and one post refers to Marines); the Air Force, Navy and Reserve voices are few.

## Findings

Findings are in index order, **grouped by author** (milblog posts sit with the same author's Sandbox posts), with cross-cutting findings at the end.

### B.C. (Afghanistan, artillery, month eight of the deployment)

**[E25b-1]. The funniest line of a firefight was only laughed at later: laughter has to wait for safety, then leaks out at the near miss.** (MV-B-070 "Getting Shot At," 2007-02-09)
The post is an email home, forwarded to the Sandbox, describing the sounds, sights and feelings of a gunfight for readers who, the author says, will mostly never have the experience. The author's comic centerpiece is a moment when bullets were passing close without any audible shot: they called to a vehicle-mate that "I think someone's shooting at me," which sounded so absurd that the author "still want[s] to laugh about it" but did not at the time, because the shooting was real, and only became comfortable to laugh at once the engagement ended. In the feelings paragraph the author lists "stark fear, adrenaline, and excitement," notes that four-letter expressions are common, and observes that "there's a good bit of laughter and joking too," most often right after a near miss, in a pattern like "Oh shit, that was close. Hahaha." The author's own explanation is "Gotta break the tension, I suppose." Elsewhere the post's tone is deliberately jaunty about weapons (artillery as a "beautiful noise" because it is friendly; the enemy "can't shoot worth a damn"), and the humor stops cleanly for the plain material: the fear, the six hours that passed in what felt like five minutes, and the elation that follows survival, which the author states in a single line about being alive while the enemy is not. The post closes on its purpose: the author nearly omitted the topic to avoid worrying the family, then judged that telling "how truly terrible it is" serves them better, and adds that "Hawkish behavior is the realm of people who haven't done this before."
*Candidate application:* A veteran may carry a small stock of absurd near-miss stories that he can tell with a laugh, while the same event includes a plain, unjoked layer (fear, elation at survival) that he does not narrate in the funny version. His honesty with family may be a deliberate choice against protection.
*Cross-use:* Exile or warrior societies: a "tension-break" laugh after a near miss is a short, reflexive, low-craft form that precedes any worked-up joke.

### Vampire 06 (Afghanistan, field-grade officer on an embedded training team)

**[E25b-2]. "Scared to death, laughing like crazy and relieved, all in the same moment": the driver's question that is funny precisely because a bomb may be seconds away.** (MV-B-100 "A Day in the Life," 2008-11-17)
The post builds tension on purpose: the author compares the rising tension to drinking ten, then twenty, espressos before rush-hour driving, while a convoy with Afghan Army counterparts approaches a reported roadside bomb and waits for jets to arrive for a show of force. At about 150 meters out the driver asks, in effect, where the jet is ("Dude, where's the F-15?"), a line the author says made them "laugh at a totally inappropriate time" ("Because this is funny!") and rates above what a well-known comic actor could have done. The author's reply, a flat reading of radio-call language ("inbound 30 seconds out with good tally"), makes it funnier. The jet arrives at once, with a loud boom overhead that confuses everyone about whether the bomb went off, and the anticlimax is celebrated, not regretted. The author's summing-up is a single line naming the three simultaneous states: scared to death, laughing, relieved. The post contains no one hurt and no death, so the humor can be written openly; the Sandbox's other posts with a death close by (McGee) do not read like this.
*Candidate application:* Gallows humor at the apex of a threat can be a deadpan exchange of procedural words with no punchline, and the speakers know it is only funny because of the setting. A veteran may struggle to explain later why that exchange was hilarious.

**[E25b-3]. "Laugh or you will go insane": the stated function, the immature catchphrases, and the question about how to explain it at home.** (MV-B-101 "Laugh or You Will Go Insane," 2008-12-29)
The post is a reflection on coping inspired by a specialist from another unit who asked the author how he would explain the deployment when he got home. The author says: "We laugh at totally inappropriate times and things. You have to laugh or you will lose it here." Documented forms: meaningless catchphrases shouted at unanswerable trivia-show questions (none of the team can say where the joke came from, which seems to be part of why it endures), a hide-behind-a-rock quip from a captain waiting for a bomb to go off that real life lacks a soundtrack, and a mock-defiant answer to a mental-health questionnaire (asked whether they have obsessive-compulsive impulses, the author answers that those impulses keep them alive, and half-asks readers to rescue them if they are still checking for their rifle at the mall). The author labels the group's humor "immature" and adds "I don't know if it's healthy or not, but it makes us feel alright." Other coping methods listed in the same post are TV, music, workouts, reading and blogging; the author deliberately avoids one book as too close to the bone. The writer names the cost of the team's structure: embedded trainers are drawn from many units and do not return together, which breaks the bonds that normally carry humor and trust. The author's own earlier deployment (to Haiti) is the case of a person who "failed to learn the lessons about what to do when you come home," with turnaround of twelve hours between handing in ammunition and walking city streets, and they credit today's better transition counseling. They end by advising the younger soldier to talk, and with a trivia-show joke aimed at him.
*Candidate application:* A character who cracks in-group catchphrases may defend them as maintenance, not performance, and may give a younger person advice in joke form because the joke is the only register where the advice is bearable. Sources of humor lost on leaving include the team itself.

### Eddie (82nd Airborne team leader, Iraq; milblog "Eighty Deuce on the Loose" and two Sandbox posts)

**[E25b-4]. The mortar near miss told as a series of small comedies: stuff, weapons, pizza, "arrest" and then a plain count of the luck.** (MV-B-081 "A Close One," 2007-08-20)
The author, in the Green Zone for the PX and food, describes a mortar landing about 25 meters away. The humor: the author frets that someone might steal the shopping bags in the middle of the attack ("Wouldn't want someone stealing our stuff in the middle of a mortar attack, right? :)"), a soldier threatened with arrest for not running fast enough to the bunker (the author would "love to get arrested for that"), and a wounded soldier's loud complaint, while being treated for a shoulder fragment, that his pizza would go to waste (the author predicts the Purple Heart will earn him a free pizza). The targets are the excited non-combat base personnel (Air Force and FOB-dwelling security staff screaming at everyone to hurry), and the self. The post turns plain at the end: the group was unhurt only because the round burst above a solid roof and the fragments passed overhead, and the author concludes that anything that does not kill or hurt becomes a good story, then adds: "The question is how many great stories until it becomes a horrible story." Style cues: emoticons, exclamation, a hint of showing off to home readers.
*Candidate application:* Mockery of rear-area personnel who overreact is a standard release, and the humor can be partly status work ("we are calm, they are not"). The writer's last two sentences show a character who knows the funny-story genre is bought with other people's luck.

**[E25b-5]. "Laughter is an instant vacation": the explicit statement that humor at one's own and the platoon's expense was the best part of a deployment, plus the prank economy.** (MV-B-082 "Instant Vacation," 2008-01-22)
The post opens with a quotation attributed to the comedian Milton Berle and states the thesis: with men who "have become like brothers," a group can be in a horrible situation and make the best of it, and the last two days included some of the author's best times in Iraq, "not because of anything we have done mission-wise, but just having a good laugh at the expense of myself and others in our platoon." Documented forms, described: (1) a pack of cigarettes rigged to pop, given by a senior NCO's driver; the author's first thought on being startled was of a sniper, which "passed quickly," and the author then made it a personal mission to trick others, including an Iraqi Army soldier ("in good fun") and a squadmate with about 15 local children watching, who laughed and pointed too, with the author remarking "It's always great to see others suffer"; (2) a screamer-video "maze game" used with a $20 bounty to trap victims with an audience of 15; (3) the "Cornbread Challenge," a bet-and-forfeit eating contest that began as a punishment between squadmates, grew rules, a platoon-board list, side bets (the author won about $30), and a videographer; spectators gathered "in hopes of seeing someone fail"; the contest's result (failure by vomiting) is described in restrained terms in the post and is recorded in the register; (4) a siren incident at the chow hall in which a newly arrived unit hit the floor while the author's group stayed seated, and the author then walked around the prone people to fetch a to-go box ("I got a good kick out of it"), laughing most of the way back with the others. The overall structure is a "currency" system: bets, scoreboards, signup lists, video documentation, and repeat victims.
*Candidate application:* A unit's humor may run on ritualized games with rules, stakes, spectators and records, and may include peers from the host army as targets and participants. The author's pleasure in "others suffer" is ordinary, not cruel, within the unit, and the same pleasure is directed at newer arrivals' fear.

**[E25b-6]. When the prank is on you: fear first, then laughter, then "bastards."** (MV-B-059 "GERNADE!!!!," 2007-03-19)
A milblog post about the author, dozing in a truck after a night with little sleep, being woken by what the author took for a live grenade. The central portion of the account is replaced in the text by "[Removed for screening]," so the author's own wording of the scare is not available; what remains is the aftermath: the author realized "they had played the worst practical joke" ("There was no grenade"), says that from the teammates' side "it probably would of been funny as hell," told them "yall are still bastards," and notes a hand was still shaking a little on getting back to the truck. The author adds that in hindsight it is funny and gets laughed about in the retelling.
*Candidate application:* The prank that exploits the group's most real fear is accepted because the victim can see that, from outside, it is funny; the shaking hand shows the cost. Truncation is a limit here (see Gaps).

**[E25b-7]. The radio call as comedy: "one of the calmest radio transmissions," and the blooper-reel framing of ordinary mishaps.** (MV-B-061 "Slight electrical fire," 2007-03-01; MV-B-062 "Look at the worlds greatest Army NOW!," 2007-07-27)
In the first, a truck's equipment smokes, then actually burns behind a panel; the author radios the convoy leader in a flat request ("Can we stop the convoy? I have a slight electrical fire...") and later hears that everyone found it one of the calmest radio transmissions they had ever heard, particularly given that the subject was the speaker's own truck being on fire. The humor is understatement as a professional register, and its payoff is retroactive recognition by peers. In the second, a convoy takes a wrong road at night into a deep mud street, several vehicles get stuck, a vehicle door swings open and throws a mud-covered team leader onto his back, and the author titles the post "82nd Airborne Greatest Bloopers" and imagines how the neighbors at sunrise must have found "the worlds greatest Army bogged down in the mud." Injuries from the bump (a gunner's sore back, a lieutenant's elbow) are mentioned briefly and then set aside with a casual "my bad." In the same post the author plainly notes celebratory gunfire after a soccer win and an interpreter's remark that people will die from the falling bullets; the register changes from comic to flat at that point.
*Candidate application:* Self-mockery of the unit's competence, framed with a mock slogan, is safe and is a common frame; the speaker's calm voice under stress becomes a trait others tease and respect.

**[E25b-8]. Boredom as the engine: games, "would you rather," wrestling as a rite, and the blogger's own censorship line.** (MV-B-060 "E True Hollywood Stories - Infantry Guys," 2007-07-03)
During a night of security work, four soldiers in a truck invent a song-guessing game with scoring and steals (rules emerging "through heated debates"), then a "would you rather" game whose questions the author calls "crazy" enough that "I have a line that I wont cross with my blog," so the content is withheld; the questions kept the truck awake for hours and were later repeated with others in the platoon. Another night, after a stop, soldiers wrestled; the author says that in Iraq it is more often squad-on-squad pile-ons "trying to choke each other out or get the other guys to tap," described as "all in good fun," and that "its a tradition" for new arrivals to wrestle each other; the new men were pushed into it by "a few, well chosen words," the group watched their clumsy match with amusement, and a second, more skilled bout satisfied the group's appetite once blood had been drawn, which the author calls an outlet for "alpha male" aggressiveness. The author frames the whole as what infantrymen are "really like": normal people who, put together for days, "seem to be in a world of our own."
*Candidate application:* The unit's humor is largely a technology for absorbing hours of waiting; the recorded form is *invented games with house rules*, and the content that goes furthest is deliberately not written down for outsiders.

### Teflon Don (Army, Ramadi, Iraq; one Sandbox post and one milblog post)

**[E25b-9]. A shared meme culture in a war zone: Chuck Norris jokes written on bunkers, a radio exchange about him, and a near-miss told through a cartoon.** (MV-B-097 "A Surreal Day," 2007-01-05)
The author explains that Chuck Norris jokes are "a gigantic cult phenomenon" and that sayings about him are "written inside bunkers, latrines, vehicles." A Bradley crew asks over the radio whether the commander has heard that a famous actor will take one of their vehicles, and the commander asks whether this is "one of those jokes you guys tell all the time"; the author's own truck-mates riff in the same vein, asking why the actor would need a vehicle when he could simply kick the roadside bombs away. The actor then really visits the country the next day, and the author wonders "how many kids had their illusions shattered." That night a bomb built from a shell on a roller skate is pulled across the road toward the author's convoy; the author describes it as the product of enemies who "have been watching too many old cartoons" and who called Acme to order it, and the truck is nicknamed "Roadrunner" for having survived an attack by "Wile E. Coyote." The post ends: "That's about all I can handle for one day."
*Candidate application:* Pop-culture memes supply a ready-made grammar of absurdity that can be pinned to a real hazard without minimizing it and is shared across ranks; a vehicle nickname from a near miss becomes a durable unit artifact.

**[E25b-10]. Medics' pranks and what medics find funny.** (MV-B-058 "What's Up, Doc?," 2007-01-24)
A short milblog post. A medic gives the author a flu shot with a joke that the vaccine ran out so the clinic is using a local copy; the joke relies on an ethnic term for Iraqis that is not reproduced here (see register). The author reads it as medic humor aimed at the patient's expense. A day later another medic collects the author's identifying data on a pretext (the aid station "lost" the record), which turns out to be a setup for a joke shock pen. The author's reaction is a one-word complaint. The post says medics "don't find jokes about allergies funny" while giving a needle, and the author frames the two incidents as "the kinds of things they do find funny."
*Candidate application:* Medical personnel can run a tradition of pranks on the patient-colleague; the author's complaint shows that being the butt is expected and accepted, and the ethnic term shows that casual slurs are present in the in-group register of this period (not endorsed by the source).

### CAPT Beau Cleland (Iraq)

**[E25b-11]. "Compassion fatigue": the author catches a callous joke at the expense of a possibly innocent dead civilian, laughs, and then asks what the joke is for.** (MV-B-078 "Compassion Fatigue," 2008-05-12)
The post's frame is a worry that the author is "getting callous about death and suffering." Three examples: (1) a displaced elderly woman asking for help the author cannot give (the dominant emotion is tiredness); (2) a bandaged, drunk civilian weeping to the author's vehicle about police abuse, whom the author has shooed away and who is then slapped by neighbors ("Tough day for that dude"); (3) a radio-watch log kept during a day of heavy fighting in Sadr City, in military shorthand, which the author quotes in part. At the end of the log, after a report of a civilian shot by a tank crew on suspicion of carrying a bomb, the entry for the explosive-ordnance team clearing the bag ends with a tagline of the sort printed on cigarette packs. The author states plainly that they made "a joke about a (possibly) innocent civilian," laughed alone, and enjoyed the irony "even with the bitter guilt heaped on top." Content of the death is not reproduced here (see register). The reasoning that follows is the post's core: "this place has so much suffering, so many problems" that internalizing it would be mentally destructive; "Laughing is a necessary defense mechanism against the inevitable bad feelings"; the author did not kill the man but "would have" in the gunner's place and still felt uneasy; "I did it to keep myself sane." The post closes with a distinction between helping people daily and *making myself not care when I can't afford to*.
*Candidate application:* The key craft element is that the joke was told to an empty room, privately, and the guilt arrived with the laugh in the same moment. A veteran character may preserve one joke of this type that he has never told anyone, and its purpose may be to stop something worse.

### SFC Toby Nunn (Kuwait / Iraq)

**[E25b-12]. Roles in a prank culture: the ringleader, the victim who does not strike back, and the self-inflicted gag.** (MV-B-091 "Sometimes the Best Victims Are Ourselves," 2007-09-27)
The author states the unit norm in one sentence: "We enjoy a joke at another's expense more than anything." Rivalry between teams and squad leaders is encouraged by the author ("which I of course promote"). Home, the author "gave up hunting," cannot handle loud noises ("my heart goes off like a Led Zeppelin drum solo"), sleeps only drunk, spends twenty minutes pretending to play a bar video game while a memory of a decision on a patrol replays, and describes the Vet Center counselor with ironic respect ("a very smart lady") and a comic avoidance routine (promising to start running and quit smoking tomorrow, "or maybe the day after," and returning each week to say the same). Friends who served together relate benders and bar fights "in a light sort of way" and "we pretend it's funny"; one admits near-fainting in a store and recommends the counselor. The author states: "They're off living their lives somewhere else now, and it just isn't as funny anymore." The post then turns to the author's refusal to be punched back (fear of not stopping) and a recollection of a firefight and the death of a platoon commander, with a crude, loving last sentence of encouragement said to him (not reproduced), ending "I don't want to talk about it anymore," followed by a return to the opening topic (a request for deer meat). Guilt about watching the war on television is stated plainly. The jokes cluster in the middle of the post; the last third has none.
*Candidate application:* The unit's jokes require the unit; apart from it a veteran keeps the form (irony toward the helpers, a smaller comic stance toward his own symptoms) but not the function. Abrupt changes of subject are themselves a form of the deflection the humor used to do.

### J.P. Raab (Army National Guard; returning to Afghanistan)

**[E25b-37]. The platoon clown as an unlisted job: a vulgar older specialist whose stories are demanded, heckled and valued.** (MV-B-095 "McFlurry," 2011-09-14)
The author profiles Specialist McFlurry, an older, rumpled National Guard soldier who has stayed at the same rank for years, and whose stories ("a complex and ever-winding path of vulgarity and self-deprecation") bring laughter, then shouts of "Shut the f--- up, McFlurry!" or "I love you, McFlurry!" The squad leader sets a standard: "Finish strong." Soldiers shout him down and also defend him, "because, well, they're bored as hell and want to laugh with – or at – someone." The author reads McFlurry as an older man among young soldiers recapturing "some of the magic of his prime" and enjoying even negative attention. In one scene an operations officer on a "sensing session" talks to him, apparently not noticing he is shirtless and dirty; the author calls him "the face of the American fighting man." The thesis: "There's no Military Occupational Specialty designator for 'Platoon Clown', but there should be," and the unit will "need a good laugh" months on. The post is set in pre-deployment training.
*Candidate application:* A unit may keep an older, low-status entertainer on the books because he supplies relief on demand; the role is mutual, tolerated contempt and affection, and officers outside the unit do not see him.

### SGT "Roy Batty" (Baghdad)

**[E25b-38]. Playing with a bomb-disposal robot: the author mugs for its camera, and the unseen operator answers.** (MV-B-096 "Robbie," 2006-10-30)
In a bored moment the author and two others watch a small remote-controlled bomb-investigation robot roll past, apparently without an operator in sight. The author runs after it, drops to all fours and opens their mouth wide in front of its camera. It stops; circles; the camera raises on an arm, and when the author mouths "Go home!" it shakes left and right: no. The same post, in its opening, admits writer's block and praises another author as "actually funny," and calls the Baghdad streets a source of "inspiration" in the form of either a harmless "plop" or something "denser and unfortunately louder."
*Candidate application:* Soldiers quickly give a utility machine a personality, a pet name and a comic gender-coded joke, and the humor is completed by the unseen operator's participation.
*Cross-use:* **Robots.** A documented real pattern for human characters toward machines in the setting: affectionate anthropomorphizing by bored troops, with the machine (or its operator) cast as co-comedian. The post states the machine's job (investigating possible roadside bombs) in one clause but the comedy proceeds without touching on what that job means for such a machine.

### Traversa and three hut-mates (USAF captains, Kabul; a four-author post)

**[E25b-39]. A year in one hut: humor as "the glue," converging into one shared sense of humor, and the explicit nod to non-combat guilt.** (MV-B-098 "The Four Amigos," 2007-05-02)
An end-of-tour post by four Air Force captains who lived in one hut, wrote a joint post for the Sandbox and wrote also on their own blog. Each speaks: one (Templeton) says "Laughter is the best medicine," "some may have found our humor rude or even inappropriate at times, but for us it was the glue that held us together"; one (Toomer) says that "about seven or eight months into this adventure" their humor "began to become universal," that finishing each other's jokes was "a sure sign we had lived together WAY too long," that Toomer's own bad days were broken by watching the others laugh at the tirades, and that humor "was the only thing that kept me sane throughout my year here"; Traversa denies that any of their humor is "sick" ("highly sophisticated") and says it felt like "living in an episode of M*A*S*H." Toomer prefaces: "None of us are combat troops, and I do not mean to compare our plight with that of combat troops," then notes that they traveled daily outside the wire in light-skinned vehicles under "a very real threat," and that frustration with a young national logistics system and Afghan leaders' "general apathy" made it "a hard environment in which to find much humor or peace." They name themselves with mock institutions ("the Cult of the Evil Monkey," "R-5 Studios," "the West Berlin Public Affairs Office"); another captain says an inter-service joke used among Army colleagues is "Welcome back to the ARMY Air Corps." A separate observation: one author is leaving the Air Force because of force cuts.
*Candidate application:* A small group of non-infantry specialists can develop a private shared humor over months, with in-jokes institutionalized as fake offices, and they may qualify their right to the humor by admitting that others have it worse.

### @WR (returned from Iraq)

**[E25b-42]. "Find humor everywhere," with a rule against ridiculing another person's dark humor.** (MV-B-103 "Ten Life Lessons the Army Has Taught Me," 2007-04-30)
Lesson 7 says the author has been in "pretty crappy" places and situations and "forced myself to find some humor, somewhere"; "It helps you cope," taking the sting out of painful or awkward moments. ### Air Force Wife (military spouse; husband deployed)

**[E25b-43]. The spouse's version: "a finely honed sense of gallows humor," the "weird" moods, and a request to civilians.** (MV-B-069 "For Civilians," 2010-01-13)
A post addressed to the civilian neighbors who want to help. Five requests. Relevant to humor: (3) "You can't live this lifestyle and not develop a finely honed sense of gallows humor," with the example of a husband's bad reaction to local food and the later closing of a favorite stand by military police for health violations ("In Baghdad. Other points: if you ask how the husband is, "prepare to hear the answer," with "the vast majority" of the answer being "funny anecdotes"; mood swings she calls "schizophrenic and bi-polar" (the author's own loose use of clinical terms; she is not describing a diagnosis); the day the war "is neatly tucked" in the back of the mind versus days it is not; guilt when someone else's husband is a casualty ("my husband dodged a bullet that someone else caught"); and "a big part of my day is trying to appear 'normal'" while managing children's activities and waiting for calls. The post holds that humor is present but one of several contradictory states, and it ends on a plea for relationship.
*Candidate application:* The partner on the home front develops her own gallows humor, shares it with the veteran, and hides much behind "normal"; a veteran's spouse or friend may be both audience for and author of the same jokes.

### Carl (Air Force officer; desertflier)

**[E25b-44]. The one-liner that ends the conversation: "The best thing about Iraq is that I'm not there."** (MV-B-065 "The Best Thing About Iraq," 2007-10-16)
Nineteen days home, the author drives to the mountains and pranks a park ranger with a fake rock concert, then abandons the joke "lest I be accused of having fun at someone else's expense." ### Joe (National Guard infantry; milblog "Fobbits Need Ice Cream Too"; three posts, 2011-2012)

**[E25b-45]. The joke that fails on a civilian: telling a store clerk the author is a troop.** (MV-B-066 "Old Navy," 2011-07-02)
Asked at a checkout to donate to "the troops," the author laughs and says they are one; the clerk is unamused and offers smaller donation levels. The author imagines a violent response (not reproduced), is restrained by a girlfriend who says the author has "already donated 5 years of his life, with one of those in a combat zone," pays, and vows to stop shopping there. The joke here is a one-line retort whose failure turns into resentment toward commercial patriotism and toward rear-area troops.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's attempt at a dry correction of a civilian's assumption can go wrong because the other person has no shared context; the resentment afterward is aimed in two directions (civilian sentimentality and in-service rear echelon).

**[E25b-46]. Latrine-wall graffiti as the unit's anonymous humor, and a command's guard-every-latrine response.** (MV-B-067 "Shit house poetry," 2012-04-04)
The author describes marking latrine walls as an Army tradition ("Dudes are bored and/or frustrated and writing on a plastic wall with a sharpie is a way for them to vent") that is "harmless," some lines becoming worldwide stock phrases and others local. Because a command found one line it considered unacceptable, it ordered guards posted at every latrine, logging each person and inspecting after each use until someone confessed. The author laughs ("haha April Fools, right? Nope"), reads it as group punishment, and recalls an earlier deployment where a unit stood a daily 30-minute formation over a joking tag written on a care package. The author's retort: "nothing makes me want to write graffiti ... more than being forced to spend my free time guarding" against it. (Content of the graffiti: see register.)
*Candidate application:* Anonymous writing is a bottom-up humor channel that commands try to police, and the policing is itself the next joke; humor as quiet resistance escalates when punished.

**[E25b-47]. "That Guy": a satirical case file on the unit's worst soldier, ending with the author's own quiet competence.** (MV-B-068 "That Guy: Issue #1," 2012-08-22)
A long list-style profile, with "That Guy" as refrain, of a staff NCO who avoids work, games online all day, steals government property to sell at home, and mishandles soldiers' pay, promotions and morale equipment; the post names his "extremely racist jokes" and talking trash about women serving among his flaws, without quoting any. It lists the damage (soldiers missing back pay, broken laptops) and the payoff: when the author takes over the paperwork, one soldier is "so excited" over $1,500 in back pay he "would have thought he had won the lottery," and the author has made the unit "thousandaires." It ends with the offender sending live ammunition through the mail and the author's hope that something will happen to him, doubting it will. The humor is contempt organized as a numbered "issue" of an imagined series.
*Candidate application:* A unit's moral code is visible in what it lists as the dirtbag's sins; racist and sexist jokes are placed *among the offenses*, which implies they are not the unit's normal register. The case-file form is a safe way to criticize a member the chain of command will not discipline.

## Cross-cutting findings

**[E25b-48]. What the writers leave out of posts home, and say so.** (MV-B-070, 077, 060, 072, 101, 069, 090)
At least seven posts state or enact omission. B.C. nearly left the firefight out to avoid worrying family (MV-B-070). Adrian B. says writing about dangerous things "makes me think about those things," and that booze is the other aid, so "no dramatic epics" (MV-B-077). Eddie keeps a "line that I wont cross with my blog," and the milblog post about a prank has a "[Removed for screening]" gap (MV-B-060, 059). Vampire 06 keeps the conversation with the young soldier private ("that's between he and I") (MV-B-101). The Air Force wife says funny anecdotes will be "the vast majority" of any answer but are "not everything" (MV-B-069). McGee ends by declining to talk and changing the subject (MV-B-090). OPSEC is cited as a limit on detail (for example MV-B-072) and accounts for further omissions. The result is a systematic tilt: the funny version is what is posted.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's funny stories may be a curated subset he offers because they are safe, and the unfunny remainder is what he withholds, including from those who love him.

**[E25b-50]. Humor across the language line: local partners as co-authors of the joke, and the condescension in the margins.** (MV-B-073, 079, 102, 082, 086, 093, 071)
Mutual joking is shown with interpreters and local army counterparts: Doc's party gambit (MV-B-073), the translators' April Fool (MV-B-079), the interpreter's wrestling match and pantsing (MV-B-102), a prank cigarette on an Iraqi Army soldier "in good fun" (MV-B-082), an Iraqi general's deadpan trump (MV-B-086). The margin shows condescension in the same period: Afghan behavior called "capricious" and "whimsical" (MV-B-093); American notes that locals are "nothing if not polite" (MV-B-086); and in one post the power asymmetry is visible but not discussed (the interpreter pantsed by an officer, MV-B-102). Old Blue rejects mocking *allies* of equal standing (MV-B-063) but accepts mocking Afghans as a target in his own list (MV-B-094).
*Candidate application:* The warm, mutual joke and the superiority joke coexist in the same writers; a character's affection for a local friend does not prevent condescension toward the local population.

**[E25b-51]. Names, nicknames and handles as humor: vehicles, people, call signs and the writers' own handles.** (MV-B-097, 094, 091, 095, 084, 100, 096, 098)
A vehicle becomes "Roadrunner" after surviving a cartoon-style bomb (MV-B-097); a unit's prayer-giver is "The Reverend Cookie Monster," with companions "Burt Schtickem" and "Gonzo" (MV-B-094); a frequent victim is "Mr 300," a ringleader "JP" (MV-B-091); the unit clown is "McFlurry" (MV-B-095); a patient names a nurse "Doc Clara" (MV-B-084); an embedded-team officer posts as "Vampire 06," a name that reads as a call sign (MV-B-100); a sergeant posts as "Roy Batty," the name of a character in a well-known science-fiction film (my observation; the post does not comment) (MV-B-096); a hut gives itself fake institutions (MV-B-098); a worst soldier is "That Guy" (MV-B-068). Some nicknames are earned by an event, some assigned by a role, and some are borrowed from pop culture.
*Candidate application:* Naming is a low-cost, high-frequency humor form that fixes a story to a person or object; a faction's nicknames can encode its history.

**[E25b-53]. Where posts turn plain: the pattern of the joke followed by one unadorned fact.** (MV-B-070, 081, 075, 077, 100, 078, 090, 085, 089)
Across the corpus the plain line is short and unjoked, often a single sentence or clause at the end of a funny narrative: "I am still here, and he is not" after a gunfight (MV-B-070); "how many great stories until it becomes a horrible story" after a near miss (MV-B-081); the grandfather's death in a list of highlights (MV-B-077); the "crippling depression of everyday routine" in a comic mock-epic (MV-B-075); "I'd rather forget" (MV-B-077); the patient's "I get so tired of being on display" (MV-B-085); the radio-battery and PowerPoint lines in a list of gripes (MV-B-089); and McGee's refusal to continue (MV-B-090). The plain beat usually has no joke added after it.
*Candidate application:* A realistic soldier-type's funny story has a seam, one sentence where the register drops, which an attentive listener can feel and which the veteran does not elaborate.

**[E25b-54]. Humor and care: caregivers' and patients' humor, and the rule that the patient is never the butt.** (MV-B-084, 085, 092, 058, 064)
Three vantage points. A nurse (MV-B-084, 085) answers patients' jokes and uses jokes to cover awkward visits; the patient initiates, the nurse reciprocates, and the nurse's emotion stays private. An observer (MV-B-092) reports that hospital staff laugh "none of ... at the expense of the patients." A soldier-blogger (MV-B-064) turns the rule into a boundary against outsiders ("you must belong to that club"). The contrast case is the medic prank on the patient (MV-B-058), which is an in-group, low-stakes prank on a healthy patient (a flu shot), not a seriously wounded one.
*Candidate application:* A healer character can be funny at the patient's side and still hold a firm line that the patient is not the target.

**[E25b-55]. Humor over a deployment: what the repeat authors show, and what they cannot.** (Eddie, Adrian B., Old Blue, Vampire 06, Teflon Don, Joe)
*Eddie (milblog Mar 2007 to Sandbox Jan 2008):* the author is the frightened butt of a prank in March 2007, ribs "FOB-dwelling" personnel in August 2007, and in January 2008 is both startled victim and ringleader of a prank cycle while a newer unit dives for cover; the January 2008 post is the most explicit statement that shared humor is "one of the greatest things" of the deployment. The victim-then-perpetrator sequence appears inside the single January 2008 post, so it is not clearly a change over time. *Adrian B.* (see E25b-25): targets shift from self toward the institution. *Old Blue (Nov 2007 to Mar 2010):* a thanksgiving that includes joking (Nov 2007), mock doctrine (Apr 2008), organized outrage over outsiders' jokes about the wounded and about allies (Dec 2008, Mar 2009), and a post that is mostly about care (Mar 2010); the humor-related posts drift toward policing others' humor, but the sample is selected for humor and is not a continuous record. *Vampire 06 (Nov to Dec 2008):* from a comic tale of an approaching bomb to an explicit reflection on laughing and on home. *Teflon Don* (two posts about three weeks apart, Jan 2007) shows meme humor and a medic prank. *Joe (2011-2012)* mixes between-deployment and in-theater writing, refers to an earlier Iraq tour, and is the most irritable and satirical voice in the set (my judgment). No single author's posts span more than about two and a half years, and no author is observed after leaving service.
*Candidate application:* Over a deployment the humor may move among fear management, status play and rule-policing; the best-attested constant is the comic list of grievances.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions.** *Coping and tension release:* laughter at near misses and at absurd radio calls (E25b-1, 2, 4, 7); self-stated function ("Laughter is an instant vacation," "Laugh or you will go insane," "keep myself sane," "Find humor everywhere"; E25b-3, 5, 11, 42). *Cohesion and glue:* hut-mates and platoon members converge on a shared humor over months (E25b-5, 39, 13); the prank economy (E25b-5, 12). *Venting up the chain:* grievance lists, meeting glossaries, mock doctrine (E25b-20, 23, 24, 33, 14). *Morale roles:* the platoon clown, the storyteller (E25b-37). *Negotiation and rapport across cultures:* the interpreter's party gambit, the translators' prank, the general's deadpan (E25b-21, 27, 31). *Care:* the nurse and patient (E25b-29, 30). *Exit:* the discharge joke (E25b-40).

**Targets.** Up and sideways dominate: command, staff, rear echelon, allies, other services, civilians' gifts and questions, and the self (E25b-49). The enemy is rarely a target and then mostly through cartoon framing (E25b-9). Dead and wounded are not targets in the sample, with a private exception that is immediately followed by guilt (E25b-11) and explicit exclusion rules (E25b-15, 17, 54). Locals are co-joke-makers and also targets (E25b-50).

**Forms and mechanics.** Mock-bureaucratic and glossary; mock-epic; lists and rules; catchphrases and meme grammar; understatement and radio-procedure deadpan; pranks, rigged objects, bets and scoreboards; observational taxonomies and case files; nicknames and handles; parody diagnoses; wordplay (E25b-52, 51). Absurd inventories of failures (the paved road, the stuck convoy) are narrated in an epic or historian's voice (E25b-20, 7).

**Rules of license.** "You must belong to that club" (wounded, E25b-15); "if you served with them... good-natured fun" (allies, E25b-16); "never ridicule someone for their dark sense of humor" (E25b-42); no laughs at the patients' expense (E25b-17); joke-victims accept being teased and rarely strike back (E25b-12); venting belongs inside the wire (E25b-33); the writer's own lines not to be crossed in public (E25b-8, 48). Misfires: the clerk (E25b-45); the ranger prank abandoned (E25b-44); the failed bald-head joke turned (E25b-31); the mocked allies (E25b-16).

**Limits and failure modes.** A private joke at a possibly innocent dead civilian that produced guilt (E25b-11); jokes that stop working once the unit is gone (E25b-36); omission and the funny-version-only habit (E25b-48, 19); avoidance by one-liner (E25b-44); self-stated "I don't know if it's healthy" (E25b-3); group punishment of anonymous humor (E25b-46); a prank exploiting real fear (E25b-6); condescension and power asymmetry in mixed-nationality horseplay (E25b-41, 50).

**Time course.** Within a deployment: targets shift (Adrian B., Eddie, Old Blue; E25b-25, 55); humor can converge among hut-mates by month seven or eight (E25b-39). After return: the unit's jokes stop being funny without the unit (E25b-36), the questions of civilians are met with a shield joke (E25b-44), and the discharge is greeted with an exit joke (E25b-40).

**Civilian-facing register.** The posts are themselves translations: the funny anecdote as the main answer (E25b-43), the rules for civilians (E25b-32, 43), the Old Navy failure (E25b-45), the "best thing about Iraq" line (E25b-44), and the explicit "for civilians" address (E25b-1, 43).

**Caregiver and spouse vantage.** The nurse's jokes are a service, the patient's jokes are vital signs, and their own emotion is hidden (E25b-29, 30); the spouse has her own gallows humor and a parallel concealment of "weird" moods (E25b-43).

**Defector/exile specifics.** Only the Usual Suspect's exit voice (E25b-40) and McGee's civilian aftermath (E25b-36) approach it. Nothing in the set is about humor *without a unit* over years, or about leaving on principle.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Selection bias.** The Sandbox archive was keyword-scored for humor and the author-chosen posts are mostly written to be read by civilians. A humor-rich post can be an editor's or an author's favorite, not typical talk. The harvest log itself warns that humor lacking keywords may have been missed.
- **Internet Archive copies.** The Sandbox posts are read from archive copies of monthly pages (the original TypePad site is gone). Photos and videos are absent; captions are the only trace. Comments, which might show reader reaction, were not captured. Email addresses are redacted. Publication dates and "Posting date" fields differ by a day in places; I give publication dates. Two Sandbox months (2006-06 to 2006-09 and 2010-05) were not archived; 2012-01 failed.
- **Truncation and screening.** The "GERNADE!!!!" post has its central scare replaced by "[Removed for screening]" (MV-B-059). Others use OPSEC substitutions. The index "words" count (harvest) differs slightly from manifest counts; I used the manifest.
- **Author identity.** "Adrian B." surname unstated; "LT G" is not identified by name; "Roy Batty," "Vampire 06," "Teflon Don," "Old Blue," "Eddie," "B.C." are handles. I make no inferences beyond the posts' own metadata. The Hart posts give different blog names ("From Our Perspective" in MV-B-084; a different URL in MV-B-085); I did not resolve the discrepancy.
- **Old Blue's dates.** The Sandbox header says "Stationed in: Afghanistan" for the 2007-08 posts and "Returned to: Afghanistan" in March 2010; the harvest log describes the blog as covering Afghanistan 2006-09 and the index gives the author's service as Afghanistan 2007-08. I did not reconcile these, and the number of deployments is unclear.
- **Humor content is thin in several files.** The Landstuhl post (MV-B-092) is about a volunteer, with one sentence about staff humor. The Sandbox nurse posts (MV-B-084, 085) are about patient humor, not death. The "Doc in the Box" taxonomy (MV-B-080) is light. Cleland's and McGee's posts contain humor in a few lines inside posts about something else.
- **Pronouns.** The posts do not state pronouns for their authors (the nurse, the chaplain and the spouse are identified by role or name only); I used "the author," "the nurse" or "they."
- **Misattributions inside the posts.** Quotations credited to Milton Berle (MV-B-082), to an ancient strategist (invented, MV-B-093) and to Plato (MV-B-103) are the authors' own attributions and were not verified.
- **Era and army.** All material is U.S. forces in 2006-2012 (Army heavy, with Air Force officers in advisory and logistics roles). Nothing generalizes to other armies, wars or the present.
- **Loose clinical vocabulary and slurs.** Several posts use slurs, mental-illness terms or scatological words in the authors' idiom of the period; these are recorded in the register, not reproduced, and the use of a term in a post is not endorsement by the source or by this extraction.
- **Candidate applications are suggestions, not canon.** No sample lines or dialogue were written for any character.
