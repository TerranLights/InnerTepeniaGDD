## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-01 is.** A 1930 book that photographically reproduces every number of the trench newspapers produced by one British division as it moved from the Ypres Salient ("Wipers") to Neuve Eglise, Kemmel and the Somme. The title page names the Editor (Lieut.-Col. F. J. Roberts) and Sub-Editor (Major J. H. Pearson). My range holds: a foreword by Field Marshal Lord Plumer (May 1930); the Editor's retrospective preface "Twelve Years After" (1930); the wartime preface "How It Happened" (written before the war ended); and ten issues in facsimile: **Wipers Times** Nos. 1-4 (12 Feb [date partly legible], 26 Feb, 6 Mar, 20 Mar 1916); **"New Church" Times** Nos. 1-4 (17 Apr, 1 May, 22 May, 29 May 1916); **Kemmel Times** No. 1 (printed date 3 July 1916); and **Somme-Times** No. 1 (31 July 1916), which breaks off at L12,481 where a "Message from Teech Bomas" begins (the next extraction continues it). Later titles (B.E.F. Times, Better Times) and the paper's end in 1918 are outside this range and appear here only in the editors' two prefaces.

**Evidence base.** One division's paper (the text names the "24th Division"), produced by and for the division; the Editor says the first issue ran to 100 copies and (22 May) the run was raised to 250. Writers use pen names (the Padre, Cockles Tumley, Belary Helloc, Teech Bomas, Grandpa, P.B.I., Cynthia, Auntie Annie, and others); the poet Gilbert Frankau signs poems by name; the engraver "Sapper Couzens R.E." and "Peter" are named for sketches. From content (messes, subalterns, company commanders, brigade staff, "chits"), the paper reads as an **officers'-mess paper**; private soldiers speak only through officer-authored dialect verse. That is my inference, not a statement in the text.

**What it does NOT contain.** No images (cartoons survive only as captions or not at all; where a caption is the joke I report it). No readership data beyond the editors' own remarks. No private soldiers' own writing. No mention of executions, desertion, mutiny, or shell shock by name. No crosswords or riddle items in this range. Visible censor gags (E08-11, E08-46, E08-51) and statements about censorship (E08-3, E08-4, E08-6) are the only evidence on the subject; there is no instance of a censor actually striking a line. Jokes about the enemy, the home front, Staff and rear echelons are plentiful; jokes about **named** dead are essentially absent.

**OCR and format damage (honest report).** The text is column-fragmented; advertisements are often interleaved or scrambled; numerals and proper names are unreliable (prices, prize sums, the balance sheet of No. 4, some pen names); drop capitals are lost. **I skipped** the Google cover/frontispiece garbage (L1-~307 and scattered pasted fragments such as L428-447, 562-592, 718-722), blank pages, and pure OCR noise. There are no page markers; I cite **issue date and line numbers ("L")** of the text file. **Quotations** are exact except that (a) single-character OCR slips are repaired silently where the correction is certain (e.g. "Enrope" to "Europe"), (b) larger repairs are in [square brackets], and (c) British spellings are converted to American per the project rule, in quotations as well as in my own text. **Glosses** identifying real people or things that the paper parodies (a press-baron's columnist, the pin-up artist Kirchner, "Sabretache" of *The Tatler*, "Johnson" as a heavy-shell nickname, "Blighty" as a wound that sends one home) are mine unless the text says so.

**Retrospective vs. period text.** The Plumer foreword (1930) and "Twelve Years After" (1930) are hindsight. "How It Happened" was written during the war (it speaks of "the fast approaching finale"). The issues are period text.

## Findings

### A. Front matter: the army's label, and the editors' own account

**[E08-1]. The army's own label for the paper: "cheerfulness."** (PW-01, Foreword, Field Marshal Lord Plumer, May 1930, L398-422)
Plumer says the book "is not a serious narrative of the events which took place in the Ypres Salient" but that a perusal "will give the reader a vivid and correct impression of the wonderful spirit of cheerfulness which prevailed notwithstanding all the sacrifices, hardships and privations the troops were called upon to undergo," and thanks the producers, on behalf of all who held commands there, for "maintaining this spirit." It is a senior commander's retrospective gloss, filing the humor under morale; the editors' own preface (E08-3) is less comfortable with that word.
*Candidate application:* The institution's later description of soldier humor ("good spirit") may differ from what the humorists say it was; a veteran character could carry both versions and offer only the official one to civilians.

**[E08-2]. How the paper began: a wrecked press, no copy, and daily life turned into advertisements.** (PW-01, "How It Happened," L593-714)
The editors found an old printing house "just off the Square at Wipers. Some printing-house and some square!" A sergeant who had been a printer said he could make the press print. They had "paper ... ink in plenty, everything in fact except 'copy.' As none of us were writing men, we just wrote down any old thing that came into our heads. Little incidents of daily life in the Salient were turned into adverts. or small paragraphs." They lived "in rat-infested, water-logged cellars by day and at Hooge by night," and edited from a casemate in the old ramparts that held "a piano ... a gramophone, a printing-press and a lot of subalterns." When shelling got heavy they "would turn our whole outfit on together": piano, gramophone and subalterns against heavy shells on the roof, an effect that "has to be heard to be realized." At dusk they went out, "donning boots, gum, thigh," to work until dawn "in feet of liquid mud composed of—various things better left unsaid," and still the ink-covered sergeant demanded "Copy wanted, sir!" The first two numbers were set one page at a time; the sergeant and his helpers "would go to the door and look at the atmosphere" and dash to the press only "if all was moderately quiet." When a 5.9-inch shell later destroyed the works it was "the most fortunate thing that could have happened," because the editors were put on to a better hand press and more type. No. 1 had 100 copies ("Had we only known ... we'd have printed 300"); the "dud" prices (up to 200 francs) were meant to save paper, but "several of the more ardent spirits took these prices seriously and weighed in with the necessary," so the price was dropped to 50 centimes.
*Candidate application:* A unit's humor can start as nothing more than a daily demand for filler, written from whatever happened that day; and playing loud music against a bombardment is a documented comfort ritual of this unit. The enemy's shell supplying new equipment is a ready-made irony.

**[E08-3]. The editors' own statements on tone, censorship and limits (1918 preface).** (L685-708)
Three remarks matter. (a) The later issues are to be called "B. E. F. Times" "for reasons not unconnected with the censor" (L685-686; those issues lie outside this range). (b) On production: "Have you ever sat in a trench in the middle of a battle and corrected proofs? Try it. That is what happened on the Somme." (c) The editors' caveat to readers: "Remember that the hilarity was more often hysterical than natural, and that these are our first efforts at journalism, so your criticisms may be kind." There is also a Staff joke: the press is "marooned" with a town major until "we can commandeer a tank as a traveling printing works, as the red-hats are most unsympathetic at present, and awfully keen on fighting." This is the clearest insider reading of the paper's tone: laughter as stress discharge, not lightness.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's own account that the laughter "was more often hysterical than natural" is a usable truth-claim to put beside any claim of untroubled good spirits.

**[E08-4]. The Editor's 1930 retrospect: parody of the war-memoir boom, the end of the original press, and peace as anticlimax.** (L449-559)
The Editor reports being "asked a thousand times where copies of The Wipers Times could be obtained" and having to reply "Napoo." Re-telling the German offensive of 21 March 1918, the Editor writes that the shells "all fell on or near the Editorial Sanctum," that after "taking two aspirin and placing helmet, gas, in position" the Editor and Sub-Editor "drank a case of whiskey, shot the Padre for cowardice and said 'good-bye to all that.' (The influence of these modern War Books is most insidious.)" That is a jab at the memoir wave of 1929-30 (this folder holds Graves's *Good-bye to All That* as PW-05). The press was lost; after many months a new one was picked up at Avesnes for "The Better Times" and "The Final and Best Times"; illustrations came from J. O. Twiss, who "discovered that, with the aid of candle grease, a spot of sulfuric acid, and a piece of marble from a shell-strafed wash-stand, one could make blocks." On censorship: "G.H.Q. remained kind to us up to the end, and only two or three times hauled us over the coals by censoring"; the paper existed "in no small measure" through Major-General Sir John Capper and other senior officers, "who encouraged and helped us in every way in those early days." On peace: "these awful years of peace"; "Peace of the 1929 vintage is nothing to write books about"; "When you get 'fed up' with the joys of peace, grab your Wipers Times and you'll soon be longing to be on a 'working party' again." And: "Thank Heaven that friendships made in those days have held."
*Candidate application:* Twelve years out, this humorist is comic about the war-narrative market, sour about peace, and sincere only about friendships; a defector-type character's humor about "books about the war" could take this shape.
*Cross-use:* exiled ex-soldiers who mock the civilian market for their own story.

### B. Wipers Times No. 1 (Saturday, 12 February 1916)

**[E08-5]. The Salient as a resort town: hotel, railway, music-hall and cinema advertising.** (No. 1, L725-857; recurs in every issue)
The front page imitates a resort advertising sheet. "Wipers Fish-Hook & Menin Railway ... Until further notice trains will not go beyond Gordon Farm Station, line beyond that closed for alterations." "Hotel des Ramparts ... NO EXPENSE HAS BEEN SPARED BY THE NEW MANAGEMENT IN THE RE-DECORATING AND RE-FITTING OF THIS FIRST-CLASS HOTEL ... SPECIALLY RECOMMENDED TO BUSINESS MEN." A Cloth Hall bill offers "THREE DUDS WORLD'S BEST KNOCKABOUTS," "BOUNCING BERTHA THE LITTLE MARVEL" and "THE JOHNSONS: A Shout. A Scream. A Roar." The Menin Gate Cinema shows "THE ROAD to RUIN, 15,000 Feet Long, EVERY FOOT A THRILL. People have been so overcome when the final stages are reached as to necessitate help being given to enable them to reach home." Later issues extend the frame: "Winter Gardens ... built on the roof ... Hotel Prices as usual" (26 Feb); "an Exhibition of 1916. SCENIC EFFECTS BY 'FRITZ.' ... GONDOLAS ON THE MOAT NIGHTLY. POMPEII A BACK NUMBER ... ANY 'BUS, Motor Lorry, or Ambulance, will drop you there" (26 Feb); "TAKE THE 'MOAT WATER' CURE. TERMS VARY" (20 Mar); a Cook's-style tour of "all the health resorts of LOVELY BELGIUM ... NO SALIENT FEATURE OMITTED" with a tea shop of "Walloon Waitresses" (17 Apr); a char-a-banc syndicate to "Messines ... The old church of Messines is well worth a visit" (29 May). Mechanism: **genre substitution**: civilian consumer advertising laid over a shelled landscape, with the horror carried by one or two flat words ("duds," "overcome," "closed for alterations," "Ambulance will drop you there"). The editors say the adverts were "little incidents of daily life" (E08-2), so each is a coded report.
*Candidate application:* A soldier culture might describe its postings in travel-brochure and estate-agent language that an outsider can only decode by knowing the incident behind each phrase.
*Cross-use:* any besieged or exiled community that talks about its habitat in the language of leisure and property.

**[E08-6]. The first editorial: the first statement on censorship, the enemy as a rival firm, and disclaimed responsibility.** (No. 1, L867-929)
"Having managed to pick up a printing outfit (slightly soiled) at a reasonable price, we have decided to produce a paper. There is much that we would like to say in it, but the shadow of [censorship] enveloping us causes us to refer to the war, which we hear is taking place in [Europe], in a cautious manner." The delay is blamed on "many unwelcome visitors near our printing works" and "the difficulty of obtaining an overdraft at the local bank," with "pieces of metal of various sizes" having "punctured our press." The enemy is "our local rival, Messrs. Hun and Co.," and hostile attention would be "an unfriendly act." They "accept no responsibility for the statements in our advertisements" and "offer our condolences to those who have paid 20 francs for a copy." A boxed note adds: "The Editor takes no responsibility for the views expressed, or the thirst for information on the part of our subscribers." Mechanism: the commercial-editorial voice; shelling as "unwelcome visitors"; and a **joke that is itself a compliance with censorship**: the paper says it can only mention the war "cautiously" and says so with a wink.
*Candidate application:* A shared wink that "the war is something we hear about" turns a speech restriction into in-group humor, without ever naming the forbidden subject.

**[E08-7]. A plain-voiced poem in the first issue: "The Ration Carriers."** (No. 1, L937-995; signed D. H. R.)
Verse on the night road from Poperinghe to Ypres under fire: "Where the pavé's rent with Johnson / And the mud's just ankle deep / Where you darsn't light a fag up / 'Cos the Boche's eyes are skinned." The fear is stated outright: "Then your mouth goes kind of dry boys, / And your stomach's awful sick." And the casualty: "There's a crash that echoes skywards, / And a scream of mortal pain. / Then you curse the blasted Kaiser / And just march on again." The last stanza addresses civilians "back in Blighty / Who haven't got the grit / To go and take the shilling" and asks them to remember "The men who carry rations." There is no joke in it. From issue 1 the paper carries unironic work beside the comedy, and its function there is witness and pressure on the home front.
*Candidate application:* The sincere register of a unit can be about work (carrying food through fire), dialect-plain, with the stoic reaction compressed to one line ("just march on again").

**[E08-8]. "Reflections on Being Lost in Ypres at 3 a.m.": humor at the edge of elegy.** (No. 1, L998-1102; signed "The Padre")
It opens with comic self-reproach ("I wish I had been more studious as a youth ... I should not have neglected the subjects I disliked") and a comic simile (the "heavy thud of a 'crump,' like some old and portly body falling through a too frail chair with a crash to the floor," is "an unerring guide to the main square"). Then it moves to the ruined town by star-shell: "a city of ghosts, the city of the dead, for it and with it the sons of three nations have suffered and died." It closes on a hopeful omen ("a little maid of Flanders was found playing") and the prediction that Ypres will be remembered as "the home of the British soldier." One line records how the unit polices complaint: a "grouser" airs his views "but receives little sympathy, for the men are bent on their work, and do it with a will." The Padre's name is later given to the paper's charity (E08-19).
*Candidate application:* A chaplain-type voice can hold the sincere register for a unit, with humor framing the opening and then yielding; and open grumbling is socially discouraged while work is on.

**[E08-9]. "People We Take Our Hats Off To" and "Things We Want To Know": gossip by role, never by name.** (No. 1, L1120-1188; both recur in nearly every issue)
"Hats Off" is a mock honor roll; in No. 1: "The person who re-introduced the sale of whisky in Pop.," "The gallant C.O. who has just got his Brigade," and "The person who introduced the order forbidding Company Commanders to go beyond their front line trench." (Whether the last is sincere thanks or sarcasm is not marked.) "Things We Want To Know" is an anonymous question column: "The celebrated infantry officer who appears daily in the trenches disguised as a Xmas tree." "The name of the brunette infantry officer whose man got hold of the Carrier pigeons (sent to this celebrated Company Commander when his communications in the front line had broken down) and cooked them. Also who were his guests?" "Why the dug-out of a certain Big Man is so much affected by subalterns of tender years, and if this has anything to do with the decorations on his walls." Mechanism: naming by role lets only insiders decode; questions license insinuation (the "decorations" reading is supported by later Kirchner items, E08-53); the pigeon item is also a joke on failed communications. Later "Hats Off" entries become straight praise: "The French of Verdun" (6 Mar), "The French" (20 Mar), "The British Navy. The Russians. The French. The Canadians." (June).
*Candidate application:* Anonymous gossip-by-role is a low-risk way to needle seniors and bind insiders; an outsider cannot tell which entries are fond and which are barbed.

**[E08-10]. Agony Column and Occasional Notes: understatement, euphemism, reversal.** (No. 1, L1194-1265)
Personal ads in a soldier's voice: "Will any patriotic person please lend a yacht and £10,000 to a lover of peace. Size of yacht immaterial.—Lonely Soldier"; "For Sale, cheap, Desirable Residence. Climate warm, fine view. Splendid links close by, good shooting. Terms moderate. Owner going abroad.—Apply Feddup, Gordon Farm"; "Dearest, I waited two hours on the Menin Road last night, but you didn't come." Notes: "We regret to report a further rise in property to-day." "The culinary department at the Hotel des Ramparts is temporarily out of action, and the emergency kitchen is in use. This should not deter intending guests ... as ... no new cooks were necessary as a result of the accident." "Street noises are becoming a bigger nuisance daily. Several noted residents have complained that their rest is seriously interfered with." "There is a crack visible in the cathedral spire. We should like to start a Subscription list to have this repaired." Mechanism: shelling as "street noises"; rubble as "a rise in property"; the loss of cooks left unsaid; "Owner going abroad" (leaving by transfer, or by death; unspecified). The cathedral crack becomes a running gag through at least No. 4.
*Candidate application:* The municipal-notice voice (service interruption, nuisance complaint) handles destruction and loss without naming a casualty; an ex-soldier might still speak of lethal events in that register.

**[E08-11]. "The Night Hawks": London geography mapped onto the trenches, with a censor gag.** (No. 1, L1274-1337; by "A Pioneer")
"Talk not to me of vain delights, / Of Regent Street or Piccadilly. / A newer London, rarer sights / I visit nightly willy-nilly." The night working party walks duckboard tracks named for London streets "in gum boots thigh," through "crump holes deep in mud." One couplet: "Where strange concussions fill the air. / (I wish they'd keep the—CENSORED—things level)." Another carries a mock footnote: "(for authority see D.R.O.)." It ends: "So thus the London which we knew / Remembered is along the line." Mechanism: home toponyms transplanted; the dash-out is turned into the joke; mock citation of routine orders.
*Candidate application:* Naming routes after home streets is comfort and satire at once. 
*Cross-use:* settlers or exiles naming terrain after what they left.

**[E08-12]. The mock "Correspondence" column: ratepayer and nature-lover complaints that translate the war.** (No. 1, L1343-1416; later L2570-2667, 3105-3169, 4898-4942, 8343-8508, 11614-11675)
Invented letters under civic pen names ("Pater Familias," "Pro Bono Publico," "Well Wisher"). A father proposes against Zeppelins "a high barbed wire entanglement being erected round the British Isles." Another complains that "the lighting by night in Oxford and Regent Street has been terribly neglected, star-shells being sent up at very irregular intervals." Later: "a big leak broke out in the Rue de Lille; and it was only by promptly donning my helmet-gas that I was able to proceed on my way" (26 Feb); "Several noted farmers have complained of wanton injury done to their fields ... the irritating frequency with which they find neat round holes dug in their fields" (22 May); an antiquarian whose theodolite shows the church tower at Neuve Eglise has "leant 3° 4' 16" to the west for many years" and has "increased by 1°6'18"" (22 May); the dust, and the absence of water carts (July). Mechanism: the pompous rate-payer voice reclassifies gas, star-shells and shell holes as civic nuisances; running feuds in the column (E08-59) build in-group continuity.
*Candidate application:* A "complaints" feature lets a unit vent specific irritations in a voice that addresses no one in command.

**[E08-13]. "Answers to Some of Our Many Correspondents": deadpan literalism as reassurance.** (No. 1, L1419-1461; every issue thereafter)
"Jeck (Zouave Wood).—No, when on patrol work and you hear the words 'Ach Gott! ich bin ganz fed-up geworden' issue from an unknown trench, this does not necessarily signify that you have worked too far over to your left and stumbled into the French lines" (the enemy voiced in the British idiom of exhaustion). "Wind Up (Hooge).—Certainly not. A 'Whizz Bang' does not leave the gun after it hits your trench, but just before" (the anxious question is answered by pedantic literal correction). "T.T.O. ... when unknown females write to you with requests for photographs, it would be safer to send for references first." Later: "Subaltern.—No, the death penalty is not enforced in the case of murdering an adjutant, as you can always be able to prove extenuating circumstances"; "Young Officer.—It is not 'the thing' to wear turned up slacks and shoes when 'going over the top,' in fact, you run the risk of being sent back to your unit if discovered" (17 Apr); "Knowledge.—No, Ypres is not pronounced 'Wipers' because it was once the center of the handkerchief industry" (1 May). Mechanism: an unseen question implied by a literal answer; etiquette applied to attacks; murder of an administrator treated as a legal technicality.
*Candidate application:* The Q-and-A format gives a unit a fictional space to trade real trench lore in comic form.

**[E08-14]. "Borrowed Plumes": mocking another paper's war reporting, and the sentry who "had to be killed."** (No. 1, L1497-1530)
The editors quote "our esteemed contemporary 'D.C.C.'" (not identified in the text). A German band heard at 11:30 "leaves us cold, as we take it to be only another phase of frightfulness." Then an account of a raid: "They climbed into the trench and surprised the sentry, but unfortunately the revolver which was held to his head missed fire. Attempts were made to throttle him quietly, but he succeeded in raising the alarm, and had to be killed." The editors' comment: "This we consider real bad luck for the sentry after the previous heroic efforts to keep him alive." (The text does not say whose sentry; the sympathy only makes sense for a defending, presumably enemy, sentry.) Mechanism: a killing narrated as a regrettable failure of stealth; the impersonal official-report style is the real target.
*Candidate application:* The passive, neutral grammar of operational reports is itself a source of black humor.

**[E08-15]. War as sport: the sniper "golf match," the odds board and the race card.** (No. 1, L1536-1686; No. 2, L2793-2830)
"Sporting Notes": "There is some good shooting to be had in Railway Wood, but game is getting wilder"; "The fishing in the Moat and Zillebeke is falling off, as the fish is getting shy." "Golf Notes" report a match between "Tom Sniper, the Wipers professional," and "Wilhelm Boschun, that champion of Hollebeke," on a course "planned almost entirely on the pot-bunker system," with hits scored as shots: at the sixth "Tom Sniper was hit by his opponent on the elbow"; at the ninth the pro hit "clean and hard, hitting Boschun in the neck. He claimed the hole in a dignified manner which much impressed his supporters"; at the second "an extraordinary pungent odor ... affected the putting of both players"; the seventeenth, "known as the Appendix," ends when "Tom played a beauty, which caught Wilhelm full in the mouth and finished him." Then: "Tom was met with the usual request to stand a bottle for holing out in 1, and this was promptly disposed of in the Club House at Gordon Farm." The "old firm of Nunthorpe, Cox and Co." posts odds: "5 to 1 Mist. 4 to 2 East Wind or Frost. 8 to 1 Chlorine." No. 2's race card has "East Wind" winning "by a nosecap," "Chlorine" falling "at the Culvert," "Whizz-bang ... soon shot his bolt," and the Stop Press result "3.30 HOOGE. Whizz-bang 1. H.E. 2. Others fell. 8 runners." Mechanism: the sports-report genre with a sniper duel as gentleman's golf, a kill as a hole in one, weather and weapons as racehorses. In No. 3 the column simply ends: "practically all types of sport have had to be suspended."
*Candidate application:* A "game" vocabulary lets a unit speak of killing and gas in the language of leisure; the dead opponent even gets a name and a blow-by-blow.

**[E08-16]. The first mock-serial, "Herlock Shomes at it Again: Shot in the Culvert."** (No. 1, L1697-1756; No. 2, L2151-2238)
The cast list includes "Bill Banks—A Corpse," "Lizzie Jones—A Questionable Person Living at Hooge," "Intha Pink—A Pioneer (in love with himself)," "Honoria Clarenceaux—The Heroine," "Herlock Shomes" and "Dr. Hotsam, R.A.M.C." Chapter 1 gives the pioneer a soliloquy that becomes a catchphrase: "They gives me a blooming nail, they gives me a blooming 'ammer, and then they tells me to go and build a blooming dug-out." In chapter 2 Honoria sees "the lifeless form of Bill Banks, gazing placidly at the sky," shrieks three times and falls "in the dark and silent waters of the Bellewarde Bec—the waters flowed on"; Shomes uses a spray apparatus on his own forearm while tuning his violin (a Holmes-and-drug-habit pastiche, my gloss), says "You know my methods, Flotsam!" and falls in too. The refrain "The waters flowed on" and the closing note "Next week:—A fresh supply of characters" are the serial's signatures. Mechanism: detective-and-penny-dreadful pastiche run as the paper's long joke; the corpse is a stage prop; the flooded ground keeps leaking into the plot.
*Candidate application:* A running pastiche that keeps absorbing trench realities tests how much of the real the fiction can bear.

**[E08-17]. Back-page ads in No. 1: the souvenir contest, ambulance "taxis," and damaged goods.** (No. 1, L1762-1846)
"Our NEW COMPETITION. Can YOU read character? Are YOU a judge of human nature? YES!! Then YOU take the prize. Send 5s. to Fritz opposite the Ducks when a handsome nosecap will be sent post free" (the enemy as seller; a shell's nose-cap as the free gift). "TAXIS! TAXIS! TAXIS! ... highly decorated cars ... can be easily known by the Red Cross painted on each side. Whistle three times or ring up" (ambulances). "On February 30th. Sale. A Large Quantity of SECOND-HAND FURNITURE SLIGHTLY DAMAGED. HARDWARE. Tell your friends about it. 9, RUE D'MENIN. Telephone: 14, RUINS." Two interleaved ads (building land that "commands an excellent view of the historic town of Ypres," and a tonic that "cures cold feet") name "Bosch & Co. Menin" as agent. Mechanism: the ambulance offered as a cab service is the thing nobody wants to need; "slightly damaged" is trade euphemism; "February 30th" is never.
*Candidate application:* Medical evacuation joked about as customer service shows where the "forbidden" content lives: in the service most dreaded and most needed.

### C. Wipers Times No. 2 (26 February 1916)

**[E08-18]. Weapons and branches as music-hall and cinema acts.** (Nos. 2-4, 17 Apr-3 July; e.g. L1858-1960, 2932-2978, 3980-4016, 5066-5130, 6213-6262, 8810-8844, 10634-10658)
Shell types become performers and the unit's own branches become companies. "ARCHIE the new mechanical invention ... NOT A TOY ... A Child Can Use It" (anti-aircraft gun; "Sole Agents ... Messrs. Anti, Aircraft and Co."); "The Great Silent PERCY BRINGS THE HOUSE DOWN"; "THE THREE SISTERS HUN-Y in their little song scena entitled STAR SHELLS SOFTLY FALLING"; "THE BROS. WHIZZ-BANG. These merry little fellows get there every time" (26 Feb); "INFERNO ... 50,000 Artistes ... at enormous expense. Music and effects ... by the International Orchestra." (26 Feb). "Messrs. INFANTRY, ARTILLERY & Co. present their screamingly funny farce, entitled BLUFF ... QUARTERMASTER & COMPANY, The World's Famous Back Chat Comedians ... A STIRRING DRAMA, ENTITLED: MINED. A MOST UPLIFTING PERFORMANCE." (6 Mar); "SAPPER AND PARTY in their SCREAMING FARCE ... 'STUCK IN A GUM-BOOT'" and "JOCK McGREE in his famous song 'Trenches Ain't the Proper Place for Kilts'" (20 Mar). A toy ad says "GUARANTEED ABSOLUTELY HARMLESS. Thousands Have Been Sold" (the product is a picture in the original and unnamed in the OCR; the pitch implies a grenade, my inference) (20 Mar). Don't forget your P.H.G. and take it in with you." Mechanism: the persona's gallantry mocks home-front columns; the rumorist typology documents how rumor served as a trench pastime; the F.P. No. 1 punchline (field punishment, tied to a fixed object) is a rank-and-discipline reversal in which the punished soldier has the last word.
*Candidate application:* Rumor-mongering as an in-group role (optimist and pessimist types) and the punished private's literal answer are both reusable.

**[E08-76]. "Nunthorpe, Cox & Co.": the commission agents who are never where you left them.** (12 Feb, L1674-1686; 1 May, L7389-7412; 22 May, L8450-8508; 29 May, L8970-8995, 9283-9368; Kemmel, L11636-11675)
A recurring odds-offering firm: "Best odds always on offer" (12 Feb); "This Eminent Firm of Commission Agents has once more moved into better and more commodious premises. And are still doing business on their old lines. 'In one place we seldom stay. / But you'll find we always pay'" (1 May); a reader's letter about a bet on "Chlorine" in which a "tremendous bellow" from the telephone left him deaf and the firm claimed he had backed "Bosche, who was a non-starter" (22 May); the paper's "investigation" (29 May) in which a commission "met with every sort of attention and hospitality ... being experienced men of the world they did not let this influence their minds," learns the firm's moves are due to "serious drainage difficulties," and warns readers against dealing with "this rather shady firm"; the firm's own letter (July) says "uneasiness still prevails" and returns a "note, value 5 francs, found shortly after the call of your representative," to which the editors add: "[Our representative asserts that he lost 50 francs. Verb. sap.—ED.]" Mechanism: a fictional bookmaker whose real referent is never stated (the item about the Zillebeke Bund, E08-21, suggests a unit that took casualties and moved often); the editors play an investigative paper that is bribed and rebuked.
*Candidate application:* A running gag about a firm that must keep moving is a way to talk about a unit that is being shelled out of its quarters without saying so.

### I. Kemmel Times No. 1 (printed date Monday, 3 July 1916)

**[E08-77]. The Kemmel editorial: plain mourning for Kitchener, derision for the enemy's Jutland claims, a peace rumor, and another change of name.** (L10698-10840)
"Many startling things have happened since our last number. Firstly there is the death of Lord Kitchener. True he was known to most of us by name only, yet it was a name that meant a lot to us, and although nothing can make any difference to the result of the war, one would have liked him to have seen that end which he will have done so much to bring about." On the naval battle: "Had Fritz been content with saying that, having found more trouble than he had anticipated, he hit out and ran away, one might have passed it all over, and said that for a Hun he did as well as could be expected. However, the pitiful spectacle of William, Tirpitz and Co. shedding tears of blood in their anxiety to prove to a pack of poor deluded sausage eaters, that they had blown the British Navy off the map, ceases to be amusing and becomes a disgusting spectacle of monomaniacal absurdity." A casualty is named without a joke: "we should like to express the regret felt throughout the whole Division at the temporary disablement of Colonel Cunningham, and the hopes of all that he will soon be with us again." Then the old voice returns: "We have lately heard from several people 'in the know' in England that peace is to be declared this month. Should it take place before this issue is before the public, then will all subscribers please call at our offices, when they will be presented with a copy and a gold watch"; and "We have lately taken up another and more luxurious suite of offices in a very prosperous neighborhood, and are consequently compelled once again to change the name of our periodical." Contrast: on 22 May a poem used "Lord K—t—n—r" as the signing authority for a joke (E08-67); after the death he is treated gravely. Mechanism: a switch of register mid-editorial, with the joke resumed afterward.
*Candidate application:* Death and injury of known persons get the plain voice, even in a paper built on jokes; the joke about the same person before the death is not repeated after it.

**[E08-78]. War-correspondent parody: "How the Peasants Left Hooge" and Mr. Teech Bomas in No Man's Land.** (Kemmel, L10922-10977, 11013-11046; Somme-Times, L11959-12020, 12481-12519)
By "P.B.I.": "Of course, as Teech says, it was a thrilling sight to see the peasants of Hooge trekking back to Wipers in the merry month of June. There was Lizzie of the corner estaminet well to the fore ... Have you ever seen the peasants of Hooge frolicking in 'No Man's Land' and picking gooseberries in Railway Wood? No! Well, you surprise me. I really must tell Teech ... However, on the strength of Teech's observations, the Editor has cut this special reporter's salary down to a minimum for missing all these thrilling sights." (My reading: an evacuation or retreat from Hooge in June reported as a holiday outing; the text does not say. "Teech Bomas" is my gloss for a home-front war correspondent.) Kemmel's question column adds: "Whether the London papers are aware there are a few British troops on the western front," and "Whether Tina's knowledge of troop movements is more profitable than her canteen." In the Somme-Times (31 July) the editors announce they have hired Teech Bomas "at an enormous salary" because "he sees incidents overlooked by all others," and his dispatch begins: "I write from the middle of the battlefield. There are a lot of bullets but I don't mind that. Also the air is thick with shells. That also I don't mind. Let me tell you all about it while I can think clearly. Before the battle commenced I took up a favorable position in No Man's Land, the little larks were larking and the morning was fine. Then hell broke loose and as things got really hot I climbed up the rope of a [word garbled] and joined two A.S.C.'s who were also watching the proceedings." (The dispatch continues beyond this unit's range.) Mechanism: parody of a correspondent's cheerful euphemism and misplaced presence; targets: press euphemism ("trekking back," "gallant dash"), the correspondent's pretended bravery ("I don't mind that"), and, by the A.S.C. aside, the rear echelon as spectators.
*Candidate application:* The civilian reporter who finds the battlefield "thrilling" is a durable target; soldiers' contempt for how the war is reported at home is part of their civilian-facing register.

**[E08-79]. "The Old Brigade": a roll-call elegy for a dispersed staff, and the one joke at the Salient's expense.** (Kemmel, L11220-11342; signed Frankau, 19/6/16)
"In New Church yard, on a smashed tombstone, / A Gunner Adjutant sits alone ... His hair is long and his eyes are wet / With the unstaunched tears of a vain regret." The refrain: "Oh where are the Nuts of the old Brigade, / The Nuts that used to be here?" Portraits by nickname: "Johnnie the Brigadier / The joy of his glittering Staff? In the hottest hour of the bloodiest fight / He was never flurried or impolite, / When he asked for a COUNTERSTRAFE"; "Bob o' the big mustache"; "the Major-man ... With his Kirchner ladies all in a row, / And his seventeen pairs of boots"; "Caw-Caw the Captain bold, / The pride of the tailor shop ... Or writing to fairies he never showed me / When we used to billet in Pop?"; "Nick-it-and-run" who "tells you secrets the Boche has known / For the better part of a year" over the telephone; "my boy of the Signal Corps / ... Who damns his buzzing and curses his line / When the horns shriek Gas-alarm?"; and "my lost interpreters ... galloping hard on the hard high road / To draw their pay in Baloo?" Close: "Oh give me the Nuts of the old Brigade, / The Nuts of the right good cheer! / I wish I were with them, wherever they went, / Though I'd rather it wasn't The Salient ... And so would the old J. Beer." Mechanism: a mock lament (the narrator is a gunner adjutant on a gravestone) for staff friends who have been posted away (the poem does not say any died); affection is carried by nicknames and vanities; the telephone-secret line is a joke at loose lips.
*Candidate application:* Nostalgia for a staff "family" expressed as a roll call of nicknames is a different register from grief for the dead; the narrator misses them wherever they are.

**[E08-80]. "How Tirpitz Won the Battle of Jutland": the enemy's claim of victory as a ballad.** (Kemmel, L11482-11608)
"Von Tirpitz was an admiral, his beard flew bold and free ... 'The day has come, ten thousand "Hochs" and though I stay at home / My spirit will be with you.'" The captains "tried with one accord to raise a pleasant grin; / Yet each one wondered when and how the trouble would begin"; Tirpitz peers out and mutters "'I'd rather you than me!'" and "consoled himself with beer." He drives nails into "his statue" each time bad news arrives (the German custom of the nail statue, my gloss): "And then into his statue drove another ton of nails." The returning captains: "'We've won a great big vict'ry, all the foe is underneath.' / 'If that is so,' quoth Tirpitz, 'why this frantic need for haste ...?' / 'Well, to be honest 'twas because the British fleet was there.'" Ending: "If we can't win by ships and guns we can at least by tales." Mechanism: a ballad whose joke is the gap between claim and behavior; the enemy's communiqués are the target, not enemy sailors. Compare E08-14 and E08-77.
*Candidate application:* Mocking an enemy's propaganda by showing its authors' behavior is a different move from mocking the enemy's soldiers; characters could be sharper on one than on the other.

### J. Somme-Times No. 1 (Monday, 31 July 1916)

**[E08-81]. The Somme-Times bill: a touring concert party whose route is the planned advance.** (L11852-11942)
"THE CONTALMAISON OPERA HOUSE ... The Great Spectacular Drama, Entitled: 'THERE'S ONE MORE RIVER TO CROSS' INTRODUCING THE CELEBRATED MALE IMPERSONATOR, LITTLE WILLIE. THE THREE LORELEI IN THEIR SONG SCENA ... 'OH WILLIE COME HOME BEFORE YOU GET HURT.' The Original Bottle-nosed Comedian, FRITZ, IN HIS NEW SKETCH 'I'VE HAD SOMME.'" The "Albert-Pozieres-Bapaume Circuit" touring party "by special arrangement with PROFESSOR SCRAPPER" sings: "'When the midnight choo-choo leaves for Pozieres'"; "'Up I came with my little lot'"; "'Come along over the garden wall'"; "'I'm much more happy than when I was free,' Sung by Messrs. BOSCH"; "'Pray tell me gentle Hunlet are there any more at home like you'"; "'Another little drink wouldn't do us any harm.'" Mechanism: popular song titles bent to the new battlefield; the captured enemy "much more happy" (prisoners); the touring route is the intended advance (Albert, Pozières, Bapaume are Somme names; my gloss). Compare the earlier bills (E08-18).
*Candidate application:* The same vaudeville frame travels with the unit to a new front; the prisoner who is happier captured is a documented joke on surrender.

**[E08-82]. The Somme-Times editorial: the farewell, the blank pages, and "the only good Hun is a dead Hun."** (L11956-12072)
"WELL! Here we are in July with the thermometer still hanging round in the fifties. The war seems to be drawing nearer to its only conclusion, and two or three friends of ours in the Division are thinking of buying a farm at Hooge and settling down immediately they get back from Hunland, although we personally think they would have a much better chance of decent profits if they mined there instead of farmed." The 24th Division "will be very near its second birthday" (an open gymkhana is proposed). The cricket match shows "some of our cheery Brigadiers (the war forgetting, by the war forgot from 2-7 p.m.) gamboling on the village green." The judge of the competition "has retired to his dug-out with a case of whisky, 10 sacks of mail and a headache." Then: "Since the above was written certain things have happened which have decided the name of the present issue. Whether we shall have time or not to fill all the pages is another thing, and our readers will understand if the number is produced with a blank page or two. We think it better to take this course than wait till after the show to produce a full number. And now there is one thing we would wish to do, that is, to wish God-speed to all our pals in the Division. We've all had many weary and many good times together. Who that has known it can forget the joy of a spot in the Culvert dug-out, and the many other haunts known to most of us? Whatever comes we can rely on the old Division to give a good account of itself. So here's to you all, lads, the game is started, keep the ball rolling and remember that the only good Hun is a dead Hun. Good luck be with you all, and when we pull out—well—call in at the editorial sanctum for a spot and a chin." Mechanism: the editors' usual bluster collapses into a short plain farewell as the division enters a battle; the stock line about the Hun appears in earnest.
*Candidate application:* On the eve of battle the editorial voice drops the bluster and uses the plain, conventional language of departure, including a hard line about the enemy.

**[E08-83]. A limerick on the Somme and "To Minnie" (a trench mortar as a faithless lover).** (L12076-12164)
"There was a young girl of the Somme, / Who sat on a number five bomb, / She thought 'twas a dud 'un, / But it went off sudden— / Her exit she made with aplomb!" "To Minnie (Dedicated to the P.B.I.)": "In days gone by some aeons ago / That name my youthful pulses stirred, / I thrilled whene'er she whispered low ... Ah Minnie! how our feelings change, / For now I hear your voice with dread, / And hasten to get out of range / Ere you me on the landscape spread. / Your lightest whisper makes me thrill, / Your presence makes me hide my head ... You fickle jade! you traitrous minx! ... No place is sacred, I declare, / Your manners most immodest are, / You force your blatant presence where / Maidens should be particular. / You uninvited do intrude, / You force an entrance to my couch ... When next my harassed soul you vex / You'll get some back at any rate." Mechanism: the limerick kills a civilian with a joke about a dud, and the exit "with aplomb"; the poem personifies the German trench mortar (the later cricket piece mentions "dear Minnie at Spanbroekmolen") as a woman whose name once meant a sweetheart, so that fear is expressed as the language of a failed romance.
*Candidate application:* Naming a weapon with a girl's name turns fear into spurned love; the "dud" joke rehearses the paper's most dangerous truth (a thing that looks dead and is not).

**[E08-84]. The cricket report: "combining same with a little bombing practice."** (L12169-12239)
"WITH APOLOGIES TO CAPT. F. B. WILSON (Late of the Daily Mirror). X.I.B. v. LOCREHOF. It was a great game, we won of course, and Roger Rum got a blob, bless him. Anyway I won ten bob ... Was I there? Search me as they say in Horace ... the day before when the Editor was batting I removed all three pegs with the second ball ... The Professor turned up for a moment, and we assured him we weren't playing too much cricket but merely combining same with a little bombing practice ... The wicket was as beautiful as Tina, and we had a priceless day although Bobbie wasn't playing." Mechanism: breathless sports-journalist pastiche and in-jokes (Tina, Bobbie, "Professor"), the sort of piece that only the division could decode; the "bombing practice" aside lets the writer note that the match is also training.
*Candidate application:* Sport between brigades is a cohesion device recorded in the same comic voice as the rest; the officers who "play" are the same ones lampooned.

**[E08-85]. "Godspeed": a plain-voiced poem of a year's lessons.** (L12245-12315)
"For a year we've taken what came along, / We've fought or worked and we've held our line, / Till August finds us 'going strong,' / The game's afoot and the goal's the Rhine." "Through summer's heat and the winter's gloom / We've tasted the joys that the Salient holds, / A filthy dug-out our only room, / Where our only comfort a jar enfolds. / We've learned the game in a grim hard school, / Where mistakes had a price that 'twas hard to pay, / With Death sitting by and holding the rule, / And conducting our studies by night and day. / But we've also learned, and 'tis good to know, / That the pal of a dug-out's a friend worth while, / For friendship made 'neath the star-shell's glow / Means 'Help every lame dog over a stile.' / Now we have arrived in pastures new, / Where the Hun's taking lessons that once he gave. / Here's the best of good luck to all of you / In the teaching of blackguards how to behave." Mechanism: a school metaphor for the year (mistakes with a price, Death as the master); the content is stoic and affectionate and carries no comic device apart from the "game" frame; the one comfort in the dug-out is a rum jar.
*Candidate application:* A year-end summary in plain verse names friendship as the main thing learned; it is a template for what a veteran would say if asked what the unit gave him or her.

**[E08-86]. "Eve, Blanche and Phrynette": Frankau's verse against home-front columnists, and "LEAVE IS CANCELED."** (L12321-12475; dated 3/7/16)
"Three picture-papers Smith and Sons send weekly, / And three dear ladies write therein from Blighty; / Write every week to tease us in our exile, / Of Maude's latest hat and Edith's newest nighty." Each columnist speaks in her own idiom. "Eve," of *The Tatler* (the text names the weekly), cajoles "Soldier-man": "Look on my frocks 'cos they're the rage ... And I puff 'Tina' in my page—and so we're quits O Soldier-man!" ... "But you may only dream, and scan my funny face, dear Soldier-man, / And wonder if you can't or can, FOR LEAVE IS CANCELED Soldier-man!" "Blanche," of *The Bystander* (the text names it), invites sailors to tea and adds: "in the car you'll hold me rather close because I'm scared of Zepps." "Phrynette": "I tell you's stories, frilly ones ... What would you's do, poor lonely you's, without my letters to peruse, / I seem to hear you's voices shout a hundred thousand loud Na Poo's!" Mechanism: the target is the kind of patronizing flirtation and fashion chat that is sent to soldiers; the soldiers' own word "exile" names the front; the cancellation of leave arrives in the refrain, two days after the Somme opened.
*Candidate application:* Soldiers calling their posting "exile" and answering civilian chatter with a rude refrain ("Na Poo") is a documented civilian-facing position.
*Cross-use:* exiles who receive cheerful letters from home.

## Pattern roll-up

**Catalog of devices (best two examples each; finding numbers in brackets).**
1. **Civilian-genre substitution (ads, bills, notices, estate agents, tours, insurance).** "THE ROAD to RUIN ... People have been so overcome ... as to necessitate help being given to enable them to reach home" [E08-5]; "FOR SALE: THE SALIENT ESTATE ... Underground residences ready for habitation" [E08-35].
2. **Understatement and euphemism for casualties and shelling.** "We regret to report a further rise in property to-day" [E08-10]; "a celebrated firm of commission agents took the knock" [E08-21]; also "stopped one" [E08-38], "gone on a long journey" [E08-35], "hostile demonstrations" (Kemmel, L11681-11687, not given a separate finding).
3. **Bathos: the small loss beside the large one.** The broken rum jar as "awful thought" [E08-20]; "Considerable damage done to the 'Toughs' Rum Ration" [E08-49]; "Or a crump your rum jar hits / Never mind" [E08-55].
4. **Mock news, Stop Press, Parliament, sport.** "3.30 HOOGE. Whizz-bang 1. H.E. 2. Others fell. 8 runners" [E08-15]; "Mr. Pennant answered 'Tape Measures'" [E08-27].
5. **Press and correspondent parody.** "Belary Helloc" and "16 men on the Western Front" [E08-22]; "Teech Bomas" in No Man's Land [E08-78].
6. **Answers to Correspondents; Things We Want to Know; Hats Off (gossip by role).** "A 'Whizz Bang' does not leave the gun after it hits your trench, but just before" [E08-13]; "Who discovered the salient. Why." [E08-35].
7. **Canonical-poem and song parody.** Gray's Elegy [E08-33]; the Rubaiyat [E08-48]; Tennyson on leave [E08-52]; popular-song titles at the Somme [E08-81].
8. **Serials as pastiche.** The detective serial that ends with every character dead in "the liquid fire attack" [E08-57]; the recruiting-fiction hero promoted "Colonel here and now" [E08-66].
9. **Dialect dialogue verse (the ranks' voice).** "'If that is so, well then I 'ope / You lose the ruddy war'" [E08-28].
10. **Mock regulations, dictionaries and definitions.** "Infantryman ... An animal of weird habits" [E08-39]; the fly competition's artillery ban [E08-63].
11. **Staff and bureaucracy satire.** "'Indent now for helmets gas'" [E08-23]; the staff lecture [E08-73].
12. **Sketch/scene formats.** The Brigadier's inspection ("The war goes on") [E08-68]; the reserve-day diary [E08-69].
13. **Violence-upward ads.** Tip duckboards for a hated company commander; the Pyrotechnic Institute priced by rank [E08-51].
14. **Running gags (cumulative in-jokes).** The cuckoo arc ending in a libel trial [E08-59]; "Are we as OFFENSIVE as we might be" [E08-30]; Nunthorpe, Cox & Co. [E08-76]; the crack in the cathedral spire [E08-10]; Kirchner [E08-53]. 
15. **Personified weapons.** "THE BROS. WHIZZ-BANG. These merry little fellows get there every time" [E08-18]; "To Minnie" [E08-83].
16. **Meta-humor about the paper (editorial hype, circulation, prices, copy shortage, poetry flood).** "Copies printed—100. Disposed of—100. Returned—NIL" [E08-19]; "a hurricane of poetry" [E08-41].
17. **Contests and riddles.** Rigged "last line" competitions [E08-54]; numerology crank [E08-24]. (No crosswords in this range.)
18. **The plain register (not humor, but part of the same paper).** "The Ration Carriers" [E08-7]; "To My Chum" [E08-38]; "Adieu to the Salient" [E08-40]; the poem on the Line [E08-64]; "Godspeed" [E08-85].
19. **Cartoons (captions only).** The pioneer's dashed-out soliloquy [E08-34]; "Questions a Platoon Commander Should Ask Himself" [E08-30].

**Functions.** The army's retrospective label is "cheerfulness" (Plumer, E08-1); the editors' own is "more often hysterical than natural" (E08-3). Documented functions in the pages: venting grievances in a form that addresses no one (complaint column, dialect verse; E08-12, E08-28); cohesion through running in-jokes and by-role gossip (E08-9, E08-59, E08-76) and an explicit editorial program of *esprit de corps* (E08-60); coping by understatement, noise-masking and bathos (E08-2, E08-10, E08-20); a controlled channel for resistance upward (Staff, Q, intelligence, inspections, "offensive" slogan, violence-upward ads; E08-23, E08-30, E08-51, E08-68, E08-71, E08-73); grief carried in a separate plain register (E08-38, E08-40, E08-85); identity and morale management by the unit paper itself, with senior officers' knowing support (E08-1, E08-4, E08-74).

**Targets.** *Staff, Brass and rear echelons* (Staff markers E08-53; Q E08-23; A.S.C. E08-37; Intelligence E08-49, E08-71; lecturing Staff E08-73; brigade inspection E08-68; "city fathers" E08-19; adjutant E08-13, E08-39); *the hated company commander and "Brass Hat"* (E08-51); *the enemy*, handled four ways: as a rival firm ("Messrs. Hun and Co.," E08-6, E08-19), as a sporting opponent (E08-15), as co-sufferer ("fed up and miserable" prisoners, E08-50; "Old Fritz's awful fate," E08-55), and as propagandist (Tirpitz, E08-80); the enemy's leaders and communiqués are ridiculed far more than enemy soldiers; the paper also drops in earnest enemy-hatred (E08-82, E08-71 on the Red Cross); *the home front* (press baron and correspondents E08-70, E08-78; society columnists E08-58, E08-86; flag days and charity E08-62; recruiting ads E08-61; gift-givers E08-43; "slackers" at the seaside E08-27; a "gloomy" capital E08-42); *the place* (the Salient as worthless property, E08-35; ruins as a resort, E08-5); *weather, mud, rations, rum* (E08-37, E08-55, E08-20); *new officers and manuals* (E08-36).

**Forms and mechanics.** Genre substitution; understatement and euphemism; bathos (the jar over the man); mock-heroic against canonical verse; nursery meter for grim content; pastiche serials that absorb reality and then die of it; mock dictionary and mock rules; pseudo-statistics; time-stamped diary; the ellipsis that carries a death ("Picking up a maul ... they saw .... The war went on"); personification of weapons; running gags and catchphrases; frames that put the soldier in an animal or consumer category; dialect for the ranks; the editor's bluster that deflates into the plain numbers; and signed pen names that let seniors be teased by type.

**Rules of license (as visible in the pages).** (1) Seniors are teased by type or nickname, never by name, and the limit is stated on the page ("Yet here I may not name him or the Editor would glum be," E08-74; "People We Take Our Hats Off To / Things We Want To Know," E08-9). (2) Affection can accompany the barb (the "priceless" Brigadier appears in a sketch, then a verse, then a mention at cricket; E08-68, E08-74, E08-84). (3) The ranks' voice is apparently written by officers (E08-28). (4) Profanity is dashed out in the cartoon caption (E08-34); bawdy submissions are refused as unfit for an "earnest periodical" (E08-54). (5) Serious items are signed or marked as a different register and sit beside the jokes (E08-7, E08-8, E08-38). (6) The censor is acknowledged by jokes ("CENSORED," E08-11; "Censored—ED.," E08-46; "telegraphic regulations," E08-51), and the unit is not named (E08-58); the Editor later says G.H.Q. censored "only two or three times" (E08-4).

**Limits and failure modes.** No joke at the expense of a named dead man; the dead appear only as fiction props ("Bill Banks—A Corpse," E08-16), as euphemism (E08-21, E08-38) or in the plain register (E08-38, E08-40); real casualties and Kitchener are handled plainly after the event (E08-77); pacifists and objectors meet disgust, not comedy (E08-47); the editors admit the laughter is "hysterical" (E08-3); a running gag can wear out and be heckled by readers ("Damn the cuckoo," E08-59); a flood of verse can overwhelm the paper (E08-41); the paper cannot pay its writers (E08-45); the paper's leave jokes end with the news that leave is canceled (E08-52, E08-86).

**Time course.** In the ruined Salient (Feb-Mar 1916) the humor is at its most acute on ruins, shelling and the town as property; the first "Adieu" (20 Mar) and first change of title (17 Apr) mark departures with plain-voiced writing (E08-40, E08-42, E08-44). At the quieter Neuve Eglise/Kemmel sector (Apr-June) the humor becomes parish-pastoral, then increasingly Staff-, intelligence-, leave- and home-front-aimed, with more parody columns and contests (E08-45, E08-62 to E08-76); the paper grows from 100 to 250 copies (E08-2, E08-63). In the Kemmel issue (printed 3 July) the editors deal plainly with Kitchener, Jutland and a wounded colonel (E08-77) and, on the move to the Somme, "wish God-speed" with a blank-page warning and a stock hard line about the Hun (E08-82, E08-85). Looking back, the 1930 Editor is comic about war-memoirs and sour about peace (E08-4). A poem of 22 May already predicts that small things seen "when the nerves are tension high" will haunt the survivors (E08-64).

**Civilian-facing register.** Civilian genres (advertising, hotels, charity, fashion, correspondents) are the paper's vocabulary for saying what the war is like; the paper is read at home (a society weekly reviews it; E08-58); the editor in 1930 reports that younger readers "seem to delight in it" and need explanation of the advertisements (L529-536). The front's verdict on home is that it is gloomy and ill-informed (E08-42, E08-78, E08-86).

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** Little. The paper's voice is institutional-loyal; dissent runs upward against Staff and Brass (E08-51, E08-71), not against the war or the army; objectors are an outgroup (E08-47). The posting is called "our exile" (E08-86). The 1930 Editor's peace is "awful," friendships are the lasting thing (E08-4), which matches a retired soldier's humor about civilian life rather than about the army.

**Cross-cultural.** One army, one division, officers' paper, 1916 (Western Front). Do not generalize.

**Craft observations (the editors' own).** Turn "little incidents of daily life" into ads (E08-2); write what is already in people's heads (a serial that runs on the Holmes and melodrama they know; E08-16, E08-66); recycle in-jokes (cuckoo, spire, Kirchner, Tina, Bobbie); let the editorial voice brag in numbers that the page then undercuts ("printed 100 ... returned NIL"); use real place-names as street, hotel and theater names; leave a punch word for the reader to supply (limerick contests; "Why."); end sketches on a flat statement ("The war goes on"); and expect that later readers will need explanation of the advertisements (1930 preface).

## Gaps and cautions

- **Retrospective vs. period.** The Plumer foreword and "Twelve Years After" are 1930 hindsight; "How It Happened" is wartime. Do not read the 1930 preface's joking about censorship as evidence of what the censor did in 1916.
- **Authorship and audience.** Authors are mostly pen names; Frankau and a few artists are named. The paper appears to be officers'; I cannot establish how much non-officer readership or writing existed. Letters to the editor, "Answers to Correspondents" and reader contests are almost certainly written in-house.
- **Identifications are glosses.** "Belary Helloc," "Cockles Tumley," "Teech Bomas," "Sabretache," "Kirchner," "Johnson," "Minnie," "Blighty," "Na Poo," "bromo" and "indent" are explained from general knowledge; the text itself does not explain them. "D.C.C.," the "commission agents," "Pro. Coster" and the "Toughs" are unidentified.
- **OCR damage.** Numerals (prices, prize sums, the balance sheet, an allowance sum in the Northcliffe poem), a few titles (the untitled poem on the Line, "Strafe Kaptin B"), parts of ads and an occasional line (the Teech Bomas dispatch's "[word garbled]" noun) are unreliable. I did not quote anything I could not read cleanly.
- **Date oddity.** Kemmel Times No. 1 bears a printed date of 3 July 1916, but its content (Kitchener's death, Jutland, the June fighting at Hooge as I read it, a poem dated 19 June, a letter dated 8 June, "peace ... this month") looks like June. Conversely a poem in the 31 July Somme-Times is dated 3/7/16 and announces that leave is canceled. Treat dates as printed and possibly nominal; some content was written weeks before its dateline.
- **Unverified claims.** The Red Cross and crosses claim (E08-71) and every statistic or event reported in the paper's own voice are the paper's assertions.
- **Period stereotypes.** The paper uses national and gender caricature (Germans as "sausage eaters," Turks and "Turkish Delight," a "Mormon," Walloons and "bucolic Belgians," female columnists as foolish consumers). This is documentation of one army's period humor and is not material for characterizing any Tepenian people (see the project's "No National Stereotypes" law).
- **Minor items not extracted separately.** Short repeated tradesmen's ads (Poile & Trotter, Howfield & Caulett, Jerum & Co., the Munque Art Gallery, a house agency), a few filler verses ("Jack and Jill," "A Major once polite"), "What Hopey," the apology to the poet (22 May), "Auntie" and "Tina" asides, and some captions. They repeat devices already cataloged.
- **Not in this unit.** The rest of the Somme-Times and later titles (B.E.F. Times, Better Times, Final and Best Times) and the end of the paper in 1918 belong to the next extractions; the only evidence of the end here is the 1930 and 1918 prefaces (E08-3, E08-4).
- **Length.** This extraction is well above the usual 10-18 percent of source words because the source is public-domain primary text in which the quoted example is the evidence; the brief asked for exact examples of each device. Findings that repeat a device are cross-referenced rather than repeated.
- **Candidate applications** are structural suggestions only; this source documents British WWI officers' humor and is not a template for any Tepenian faction.
