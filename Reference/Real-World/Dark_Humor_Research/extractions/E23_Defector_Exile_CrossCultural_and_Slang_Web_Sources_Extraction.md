## Source-shape notes (read first)

**The lens for this unit.** The defector/exile group (WA-082 to WA-091) is the highest priority because the project's character is a soldier who defected on principle and lives among strangers. The honest headline is that **only one of the ten sources, WA-082, contains actual joking by actual deserters, and it is a handful of lines inside a terrifying feature written by journalists.** The Vietnam-era sources (WA-083, WA-084, WA-090, WA-091) are histories and promotional pieces by outsiders that report *behavior* and *fate*, not the exiles' jokes; the one place a comic voice dominates (the book interview, WA-083) is **the author's own storytelling about the deserters, not the deserters' own jokes**, and I flag that wherever it occurs. WA-085, WA-086, WA-087 and WA-088 each give one distinct angle (an ex-guard turned émigré writer, a practical-joking defector pilot, defectors performing comedy on host-country television, dissent humor under an authoritarian regime). WA-089 is a serious-register defector report with no humor in it, which I record as an absence and not as evidence.

**WA-082 (Barents Observer, in cooperation with NRK; about 4,700 words; no byline in the text copy).** A long news feature built on interviews with two Russian deserters from Sakhalin, given the pseudonyms "Dmitry" (coerced into a contract after a detention; 46 days at the front) and "Solomon" (mobilized in the autumn 2022 call-up; thirteen months at the front). Both are now in Kajaani, Finland, awaiting asylum decisions. Evidence base: their translated first-person accounts, plus a few things the journalists say they were shown (a video of a contract signing, a drone clip, a retained voice message). The outlet's own headline figures are that more than 50,000 soldiers have deserted since the full-scale invasion and that runaways face up to 15 years. **What it does NOT contain:** any statement by either man about humor in general, why soldiers joke, or with whom; any joke about the war's stated aims, the leadership or Putin; any joke told since arrival in Finland (the one aside that could be read as dry, the bicycle remark, is not marked as a joke); the original Russian; independent verification of individual claims. The headings and the teaser lines at the top are the editors', not the men's. The copy repeats the "Note to readers" paragraph (a conversion artifact). Much of the article is graphic (combat deaths, torture, coercion); I summarize those parts restrainedly.

## Findings

### From WA-082 (Barents Observer / NRK: Russian deserters Dmitry and Solomon)

**[E23-1]. Three documented forms of front-line humor, and who reports each.** (WA-082, L15, L61-77, L93, L119-133)
The account contains three small pieces of front-line joking. **(a)** Solomon says that during an assault, when his group came upon a body in a particular condition, they joked that it meant they were walking the right way: the dead were being read as route markers. It is given in one clause, the next sentence is that shooting began immediately, and the editors both put it in the teaser lines at the top and head the section "They Used Bodies as Road Signs," so it is the editors who foreground it, not Solomon. **(b)** Dmitry says people grow so used to death that they speak to the dead as if alive, offering a kind farewell; his own example is the short phrase "Come on, mate, take care." (The section heading renders it as "Kid" instead of "mate," a translation or editing variance.) He gives it as an observation about habituation, and whether anyone laughed is not stated. **(c)** Dmitry, laughing as he tells it, recalls two soldiers nicknamed for their petty offenses: one who stole sausages ("Sausage") and one who skimmed a till and lost the money in a casino ("Cash Register"). The documented forms are therefore: a joke that reads the dead as a navigational cue (restrained summary), a ritual courtesy to the dead, and nicknaming by the offense.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who deserted can keep the full front-line repertoire intact: gallows jokes at the dead's expense, ritual politeness to bodies, and offense-based nicknames. The article's editors show how an outsider will find the first of these the most quotable part.

**[E23-2]. The humor dissolves at the point where the target becomes the command's treatment of its own men.** (WA-082, L119-141)
Dmitry tells the nickname story laughing. The same anecdote then continues: the two men opened a tin of condensed milk at night and were fined 100,000 rubles each, "for a single tin"; he reflects that these were eighteen-year-old boys who would likely be dead the next day and that their own side was still shaking them down for money; then, quietly, comes the flat outcome ("In the end, they died too. Every single one of them."). The laughter is in the first lines and gone by the last, and the turn happens precisely when the subject shifts from the two soldiers' eccentricity to the command's extortion. In the same section he says what he feared most was his own commanders rather than the enemy (summarized; the wording involves self-harm). Two of the most extended passages in the article, Solomon's detailed account of losing his comrades in an assault and Dmitry's account of coerced contract-signing, contain no joking at all. This supports, for one speaker and one anecdote, the earlier-research finding that deserters' war jokes stay intact while humor dissolves around command brutality; it is a single-anecdote documentation, not a pattern across many speakers.
*Candidate application:* The fault line for a defector's humor is the subject, not the intensity: he can joke about the enemy, the dead and his own side's eccentrics, but the institution's abuse of its own men is the subject on which his voice drops. A scene can start a story in laughter and be unable to finish it in the same register.

**[E23-3]. A true statement told lightly is filed as a joke, and understatement at the moment of capture.** (WA-082, L185-191, L219-227)
After about eighteen months in hiding, Solomon told his boss he was on the federal wanted list and the boss thought he was joking. He told a colleague that if anyone came looking, she should say he did not work there, and she laughed. When he resigned, saying he was leaving for Europe because he was a deserter and could not stay in Russia, she asked, "Wait — you weren't joking?" Solomon tells it without comment. The mechanism: ordinary people assume nobody would say such a thing aloud, so the disclosure functions as camouflage; whether Solomon meant the jokes as cover, as relief, or both is not stated. Separately, Dmitry recounts the end of his own hiding: security officers surrounded the building and began sawing through the door; he called out that he would come out in ten minutes, and "smoked a cigarette and surrendered," told flat and short with no comment.
*Candidate application:* A man who lives with a fact that is dangerous to say may habitually say it in a joking tone, and may be both protected and isolated by civilians who never take him literally. The deadpan, minimal telling of his own capture is a mode distinct from the laughing anecdote form.

**[E23-4]. Institutional language turned into tools and trophies.** (WA-082, L145-147, L247, L315, L461-475)
Four items use the army's or the state's own language. **(a)** Dmitry obtained a forged death certificate for his living mother; the lawyer phoned and opened with "Congratulations, your mother has died," then emailed the document, which Dmitry used to tell his commanders his mother had died. The ironic congratulation is the lawyer's phrase, repeated by Dmitry. **(b)** In the detention barracks, reveille was shouted as "Dress Code Number Four," which meant full combat gear in heat, followed by marching and push-ups; Solomon relays it as a plain label, with no ironic marker in the text. **(c)** Solomon told an investigator his passport had "burned up during the Special Military Operation," using the official euphemism as an alibi; the escorted trip to the migration office that followed became his escape. **(d)** Dmitry has kept a voice message from a Sakhalin officer, who by Dmitry's account sounded "genuinely offended" that Dmitry had gone to Europe despite the promise of a transfer to the artillery, and asked what he was supposed to call him now; the officer's complaint, "You tricked me," treats desertion as a breach of a personal arrangement. The source does not say whether the men found any of these funny; they are documented as stories they tell.
*Candidate application:* A defector's relationship to bureaucratic euphemism can be practical: he may use the official phrase as a lever, hear a command's injured tone as the funniest thing in his archive, and treat a punishment's cheery label as a tell for how the institution thinks. The voice message as a kept trophy is a concrete prop-type detail.

**[E23-5]. Detention-and-escape stories with a comic shape, told in the men's own voices; the journalists' irony is separate.** (WA-082, L237-241, L279-297, L311-339, L349)
Three episodes are told as capers. **(a)** The detainees paid for and installed the bars of their own detention barracks (welding and painting them); one inmate used to laugh at them and ask when they would finish building their own prison. This is a wisecrack reported secondhand by Solomon. **(b)** Dmitry won day-leave from an investigator through a mutual acquaintance and small gifts (crab, whiskey, cognac, a modest sum), then remarks, "And this was supposed to be a prison." **(c)** Solomon, on an escorted errand, bought combat boots because the issued pair was poor, then held up the old boots and said he would throw them away; the escort nodded without looking up; he threw them in a bin and ran for about half an hour in his green uniform "like a madman." The investigator then texted to ask why he had run. All three have the same build: a flat set-up and a sudden break. The journalists add their own glosses, such as "surprisingly modest" for the investigator's demands and "Ironically" before the robbery by the first driver; those are the outlet's irony, not the men's. The tone in the men's own lines is light, in contrast to the flat, unadorned account of how detainees were treated.
*Candidate application:* An exit can be remembered as a comedy of small deceptions while the reasons for leaving remain unjoked. A narrator-voice (journalist or companion) can lean into irony that the defector himself does not use.

**[E23-6]. No jokes at the cause or its leaders: flat contempt for the rear, and the periphery's distance from the cause.** (WA-082, L35, L45, L65, L153-157, L491-523)
Neither man is reported joking about the war's stated aims or the leadership. Solomon says he never believed the stated justifications and went in without a goal; Dmitry says that after days without water he concluded that, apart from water, he needed nothing. Their scorn is expressed as flat statements: people who say they love war sit safely behind the lines; the further from the fighting, the greater the enthusiasm; perhaps ten or fifteen percent really want to fight; a driver who called himself "a warrior" is described as "disgusting." Solomon says most of the battalion were men from Sakhalin who kept asking what they were doing there, and that the narrative of defending the homeland "doesn't really take root" in someone from the island. Dmitry also describes, with disgust, a position where drone operators laughed and filmed while a drone pursued a soldier and he concluded they did not understand what they were doing (restrained summary): laughter that, in his telling, marks other people's numbness rather than his own side's joking. One of them (unspecified) recalled an elderly woman in Europe who complained she could not get her bicycle repaired and summed it up: "People here worry about pleasant things." The remark is not marked as a joke or as a complaint and can be read either way. The earlier-research note that these men's war jokes are intact while the talk of why they left is unjoked is borne out here: the reasons are stated plainly.
*Candidate application:* The butt of a defector's contempt may be those who are most eager and least exposed, not the cause itself. A character's distance from the host culture can be shown by a gently incredulous remark about what neighbors worry about, with no joke structure.

**[E23-7]. Aftermath, source filter and the limits of what is reported.** (WA-082, L27-35, L479-489)

a joke that a monument to George Orwell has been put up almost everywhere; and a parent's account of a son beaten and spat at since he began wearing a pro-leader T-shirt (summarized). **The refrain:** the page's subtitle and two jokes (one set in a prison truck in 2023, one about a family asked why a son is dead in a coffin) answer a question about the regime's crime with the claim of being "not into politics," an abstention that the setup makes grotesque. **Soldier-on-the-phone jokes**, which the writer introduces as "harsher" and "often very dark": a soldier says he is a prisoner of war in Ukraine and the mother asks him to exchange some dollars; a soldier says he is a prisoner and the mother says she does not know him because TV said she has a daughter (my reading: state television overrides a mother's own knowledge; the page does not decode it); a soldier says he is hiding in a hut in a "meatgrinder" and the mother answers that they need a blender, not a meatgrinder (the front's own slang misheard as shopping); and a body returned in a zinc-lined coffin met with the abstention refrain. **About leaders:** a canteen joke in which the leader's order is extended to his subordinates (my reading: the court has no choices of its own); the page does not explain). *Candidate application:* Documented device types: the civilian's misreading of military language, the abstention refrain as a defense, and the frightened ordinary person as the central figure. In these jokes the soldier is the caller and the mother the foil; the soldier himself rarely gets the punch line.
*Cross-use:* A faction with state television and conscription (an Upper Earth state or a subnet garrison) would produce the same civilian-misreading jokes.

### From WA-080 (Moscow Times op-ed: the real point of Russian and Soviet jokes)

**[E23-30]. The argument: jokes mark a standing gap between ruled and rulers, and they outlast systems.** (WA-080, L5-11, L27, L39-49)
The columnist says that in the 1980s the CIA collected Soviet jokes and probably shared them with the American president, who took them as evidence of "a pretty cynical attitude toward their system"; the list was declassified in 2013 and one joke the president told repeatedly is quoted: an American boasts that he can stand in front of the White House and shout against the American president, and the Russian answers that he can also stand in front of the Kremlin and shout against the American president. The columnist's reading: the cynicism was real but out of context, because it predated and outlasted the system. He cites Gregor Benton (1988) that a society with the vote has little "urgent need" of political jokes, and then prefers Emil Draitser (1989): folklore does not approve of state institutions, and "folklore is a private endeavor." The conclusion: the jokes are evidence not of the Soviet system's rot but of a lasting gap between rulers and the people ("Mind the gap"), and in a country with that gap the governed "step back" from the state even under a good government; he warns not to punish the people who tell the jokes. The columnist also claims that in the Stalin era people were routinely sent to camps for telling jokes (stated, not documented here).
*Candidate application:* A culture's jokes may encode a permanent distance from the institution rather than a reaction to any one regime. A defector from such a culture may keep the distance after leaving, since the distance is not about the institution he left.

**[E23-31]. Forms documented across eras: the reward that is a punishment, the unnoticed generals, the leader's words against the facts.** (WA-080, L13-37)
Examples (described): an apocryphal 1866 joke about a failed assassination attempt on the czar in which a nobleman is the shooter and the peasant who saved the czar is rewarded by being ennobled (my reading of the punch: the reward turns the savior into a member of the shooter's class; the columnist does not decode it); pre-revolutionary jokes about a runaway lion that survives by eating generals because nobody notices them missing, and a czar who meets a look-alike peasant and receives an answer implying something about the czar's parentage; a post-revolution joke in which the leader answers starving peasants by advising grass and, when they fear mooing, observes that he is not buzzing after eating honey; a Stalin-era joke in which devils are the first refugees from hell after the dictator arrives; a Yeltsin joke in which the leader announces that the nation, at the edge of a precipice, now takes a step forward; the "last Yeltsin joke" told on January 1, 2000, about his hangover after resigning; and a Putin joke where all other candidates must attend a rally for him "or else." The columnist says there are hundreds if not thousands of Putin jokes, "even if most of them aren't particularly funny." Mechanisms: the joke applies the language of reward, advice or announcement to a situation in which it is absurd, and the leader's own words are the punch line.
*Candidate application:* The leader's pronouncement as the target is the documented standard form; the unnoticed missing (the generals, the subordinates) is a form where institutional bloat is the joke.

### From WA-012 (Hadassah Magazine excerpt: Joel Chasnoff, *The 188th Crybaby Brigade*, IDF)

**[E23-32]. Comic frame: roles as formulas, the crisis count, and the catalog that hides a sober line.** (WA-012, L9-11, L13-19, L29-43, L189-191)
The narrator, a skinny American who had acted in drag and "passed out when he saw blood," builds the passage on two repeated devices. **(a) The role formula:** each person's function is stated flatly as a duty, and the duty is the source of the friction (the scribe's "job is to decide how we wear our sleeves"; for eighteen-year-old Israelis, "their job is to challenge anyone who tells them what to do"; for the commander, "it is Sergeant Eran's job to make me feel like a schmuck"). **(b) The crisis count:** the narrator must report the platoon's headcount at each formation, and men wander off, so each formation is a small emergency, with the count changing hourly. Interleaved is a refrain about the soldiers' fathers, built as lists of trades and professions; in one repetition, in a line that sounds like the others, the narrator says that most fathers are living, one is dying and one died when the son was three. The last repetition says every soldier is united by being a Jew, and the next line adds "Except for the Russians." This is a professional comic's craft, not a transcript.
*Candidate application:* Institutional absurdity and role formulas are a documented comic mode; a sober fact tucked into a list of ordinary ones is a way a funny voice can carry loss without stopping for it.

**[E23-33]. Unit prestige and the tank corps: "nobody wants tanks."** (WA-012, L89-113)
On the third morning the colonel stages a tank demonstration to excite the new men; the narrator reports that only six of the platoon had asked for armor, that most ended up there through flat feet, heart murmurs and asthma or because the preferred units were full, and that the unit has a reputation map: paratroopers are heroes, infantry from one famous brigade are tough, naval commandos are "he-men," pilots live with swimming pools, and armor soldiers are "wholesome, intelligent, nose-to-the-grindstone" and unnoticed. A buddy supplies a line contrasting whom a daughter would date and whom she would marry. The colonel's speech jabs at paratroopers praying at the Western Wall because they "have to," and the men cheer the show "like kids watching Shamu do tricks." The sequence is arranged as "Problem One" and "Problem Two" and ends with the colonel asking who wants to be a paratrooper now.
*Candidate application:* Inter-unit rivalry and the less-glamorous unit's self-deprecation are a documented cohesion device; a veteran's humor may carry a unit's old standing in the pecking order.

**[E23-34]. Barracks language: the glossary, the nightly "mistakes" ritual, and the stated reasons.** (WA-012, L147-181, L211)
The narrator says the platoon speaks a language made of Arabic proverbs, current Hebrew slang, military acronyms, English imports and invented words, which he picked up within days "because the words so perfectly describe the life of a soldier." Documented categories: (a) **types of soldier:** the "Big Head" (goes beyond duty) and its opposite the "Small Head" (does the minimum and hides in a stall), a nerd, a hapless slob, an imbecile, and a soldier who does stupid things only under pressure (distinguished from the imbecile and illustrated by a friend who complained about flies at a platoon meeting); (b) **the suffix "-nik":** a label for any soldier by his most prominent trait (a kibbutz member, a noncombat desk soldier, one with a medical excuse, an outsider from abroad); (c) **disdain:** the narrator says army life is "frustrating, miserable and downright disappointing" and most words of disdain are sexual in nature (not reproduced), plus an Arabic-sounding word whose meaning the narrator says he does not know but which the platoon uses when angry, and an English profane word that in army Hebrew means a mistake; each night the platoon holds a formal formation for mistakes at which the sergeant reads the day's list of errors and punishment follows; (d) **non-verbal:** a tongue click that means, in effect, no, and how stupid are you for asking that; single syllables that mean "incredible," "you don't say," or wow, aren't you some kind of big shot; a finger wag; and a particularly flexible word for which the narrator lists seven glosses, from a simple "so?" to an exasperated demand to know what is wrong with the listener; and (e) **address:** the soldiers call each other "my brother" and often skip given names. The narrator also records a hard rule, not to be a "friar" (a sucker who gets taken advantage of).
*Candidate application:* A unit language includes types of soldier, prefixes for traits, and sounds that carry contempt as well as meaning; a veteran might still say the sounds years later and see civilians misread them. The nightly formal reading of mistakes is a model for institutional ritual that a platoon vulgarizes.

**[E23-35]. Humor against the in-unit outsiders: who is mocked, who laughs, and the narrator's own limit.** (WA-012, L75-87, L119-123, L137, L189-191)
Within the platoon, an immigrant group (the "Russians," five men) sits alone at meals, plays checkers while the others play guitar, and is mocked constantly by one platoon-mate, Ben Gerber, who imitates their accents and the way they sprinkle military Hebrew into Russian sentences, "and we laugh"; a rumor that they are going for a religious procedure disguised as a dentist visit is repeated by Gerber, who offers to verify it (details omitted). The narrator also records the army's ethnic officer gap in two answers to the same question: one buddy offers an elaborate sociological explanation, and another buddy answers flatly that the army is racist. The narrator describes Gerber as pummeling people with one-liners and insults and adds that "too much Gerber makes you sick." The observant soldiers, who must skip meals to pray, draw resentment from the platoon (displaced from the sergeant's blame for the forgotten sandwiches), but the excerpt records no mockery of them.
*Candidate application:* Unit humor can target the nearest outsider and the group laughs along; a narrator can flag its own complicity. A comedian in the unit can be both loved and tiring.

**[E23-36]. The volunteer outsider: confession and the "lone soldier."** (WA-012, L9-11, L115-119, L145, L205-211)
The narrator is a "Lone Soldier" (a soldier without family in the country), 24, among eighteen-year-olds, who joined partly to be with a woman in Israel and against his father's wishes; his self-description in the unit's character list ends "deathly afraid of combat" and torn between being a warrior and hoping never to fire a weapon. He reports being "wedded" to his rifle, and that being parted from it feels like being stripped in front of a crowd. He also confesses that he has learned to think like a soldier in ways he is not proud of: when a volunteer was sought for a twenty-four-hour guard shift just before the Sabbath, he hid in the latrine rather than be a "friar," and reflected that a national hero would not have done so, "Obviously, I wasn't Yoni Netanyahu." The excerpt contains the narrator's own word for an outsider (a soldier from outside Israel is a "hutznik"). The confession is delivered in a self-mocking tone.
*Candidate application:* Self-mocking confession of cowardice or self-interest is a documented form; a volunteer who joined a foreign army for love may be the butt of his own funniest lines.
*Cross-use:* A human or robot who joins a different group's army or community would find the same word for "from outside."

### From WA-016 (PRX: British Army slang from Afghanistan)

**[E23-38]. Domestic, banal names for the enemy and for the war's places, and the stated reason.** (WA-016, L11-25)
The opening examples: a method of checking for improvised explosive devices (called "armorbarma" here; the source spells its first element in the British manner), the main Afghan base nicknamed after a British holiday camp ("Butlins" for Camp Bastion), members of a rival unit ("craphats"), a highly decorated Afghan truck ("jinglytruck"), and the enemy as "Terry," short for "Mr. 'Terry' Taliban." The article says these names "sound oddly light-hearted," and Hennessy, a former army captain, explains that the British Army has "a particular tradition of black humor" and gives the mechanism: "It's much easier to fight someone if they are an object of ridicule than if they are an object of fear." He adds that "Terry" is "not intended to humanize the enemy — quite often the opposite." The piece links "Terry" to "Jerry" for German forces in the world wars and to the ironic "Tommies" of the first war. Hennessy recalls a famous British comedian called Terry Thomas, "a bit of a ridiculous clown," and says he loved that the nickname "was more ridiculous than threatening." The device is giving a foreign enemy a banal suburban British name to cut the opponent down to size. The source does not say why the base was called "Butlins" beyond the general light-heartedness; the holiday-camp irony is my reading.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's slang can shrink the enemy by making the name domestic and ridiculous; the stated goal is to reduce fear, not to humanize. A defector or exile from one army might keep using such names for the people he fought, and the people around him might not hear them as contempt.

**[E23-39]. Borrowed words as heritage, worn with pride.** (WA-016, L15, L27-29)
The piece says the British Army has a centuries-long tradition of adopting foreign slang from the places it serves: "dhobi" (Hindi for laundry) and "bukshee" (free of charge, from a word that means a bribe) survive from the empire, and Afghan Army terms such as "kandak" (battalion) and "tolai" (company) have been taken in. The article says these words are "looked on with pride as a sign of military heritage and history," and Hennessy predicts that in a hundred years, sergeants at Sandhurst will still use "kandak" to show historical credibility. The pride claim is the writer's and the prediction is one speaker's.
*Candidate application:* Unit vocabulary accrues from where the unit has served, and old hands use it as a badge of lineage; a character's slang can carry the geography of his old postings.
*Cross-use:* A force that has served on several worlds or stations would plausibly accumulate such borrowed terms.

### From WA-017 (McClatchy-Tribune: "Mortaritaville" and "fobbits")

**[E23-40]. Stated reasons and documented examples of Iraq-era slang: familiarize the terrifying, bind the group, find a humorous angle.** (WA-017, L207-235)
The article says soldiers deployed in Afghanistan and Iraq, like those in Germany, Vietnam or Korea before them, created a language "filled with black humor, cultural references and even the occasional crudity." Reasons given by the three speakers: a Maryland National Guard lieutenant colonel says soldiers "try to make the best they can of their situation and give things kind of a humorous angle"; an Indiana University linguist says terms like "Mortaritaville" take the edge off a potentially frightening environment beyond the soldier's control, "making a really terrifying experience manageable by attempting to make it familiar," and calls slang "language for them made by them to consolidate their social relationships," which matters because survival depends on those relationships; a major says he has heard fellow service members called "fobbits" (the writer explains the word as a contraction of forward operating base and a Tolkien creature known for sedentary habits), meaning someone who "never leaves the base." Documented examples: "death blossom," from a 1984 science-fiction film, for fire sprayed in all directions; "Mortaritaville" and "Bombaconda" for a large base near Balad that was frequently mortared (the first a pun on a Jimmy Buffett song); "Rainbow Lake," a sign put up by an oil-filled puddle on a base in Afghanistan; "hillbilly armor" for improvised vehicle armor made of scrap metal; and "Frankenstein" for a truck with heavy add-on armor. The writer says the terms often link military life to home or a favorite movie.
*Candidate application:* The humor in this kind of slang is mostly one step away from the threat: it re-labels the place being mortared in the vocabulary of leisure (a beach song, a lake), and the insult for the stay-at-home soldier is a fantasy creature. Familiar cultural reference is the working tool.

### From WA-018 (NPR: U.S. Military Lingo glossary)

**[E23-41]. A catalog of slang devices: official euphemism, pop-culture borrowings, comfort and status terms, and a term that critiques the institution.** (WA-018, L19-137)
The introduction says soldiers in the Iraq and Afghanistan wars built a vocabulary from popular culture and "the doublespeak of the military industrial complex," and gives as an example of the latter the drawdown, which soldiers call "the retrograde," an old euphemism for retreat: the army never "retreats," it conducts a "tactical retrograde." Devices documented in the entries: **(a) euphemism and understatement:** "kinetic" for violent; "blowed up" for hit by an IED (the glossary's example treats it as something a soldier can count over a year); "operational tempo" applied equally to patrols and to making presentation slides; **(b) pop-culture borrowing:** "Groundhog Day" for a deployment where every day is identical; "Secret Squirrel" for highly classified, with the gloss that secrecy confers status; "woobie," the poncho liner, named for a baby's attachment to a blanket; **(c) sorting soldiers by exposure:** a family of terms for those who do not leave the base or lack combat experience ("fobbit," "plant eater," a term for a person other than a combat soldier, "Powerpoint Ranger"), set against "meat eater" for special forces whose focus is violence; "geardo" for a soldier who spends heavily on fancy gear with little tactical need; "oxygen thief" for a useless or talkative soldier; **(d) comfort hierarchy and the base as a small town:** a trailer with its own bathroom reserved for generals, a civilian coffee shop that is the social center ("Green Bean"), and a big airfield's weekly "Salsa Night" near a restaurant chain; **(e) institutional self-critique:** the "Self-Licking Ice Cream Cone," defined as a doctrine or process that seems to exist to justify itself and produce irrelevant measures of its own success, with the example of continually reporting captured weapons while the raids that seize them anger villagers; **(f) abbreviation as private code:** "rumint" (rumor plus intelligence), "Tango Mike" (thanks much), and an initialism using the phonetic alphabet for a profane exclamation (the source masks the profanity); **(g) field improvisation:** "Ranger Pudding," a cracker spread with a paste of creamer, sugar and water; "moon dust" for the flour-fine dust that covers southern Afghanistan.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's vocabulary will contain official euphemism that he repeats deadpan, borrowed pop-culture handles, and a few terms that quietly indict the institution; the status words sort people by exposure, not rank.

**[E23-42]. Craft observation on authenticity, and what the glossary leaves out or softens.** (WA-018, L19-25, L59-69, L97, L119-121; derogatory and injury terms summarized restrainedly)
The writer says it is painful for U.S. soldiers when films and discussions use obsolete slang, such as "chopper" for a helicopter (troops say "bird") and "GI" (replaced by "Joe"); "chopper" is, per the entry, a motorcycle. The writer states the list is not exhaustive and that a few phrases were "too salty for publishing." The glossary does include, in neutral definitional language, a derogatory term for local nationals with compounds for shops and merchandise, a rude abbreviation for interpreters, a term for a person assumed hostile who runs from an attack, a euphemistic image term for the effect of certain gunshot wounds, and a resupply drop named by reuse of a death-handling container (none of these is reproduced here).
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's authenticity can be lost by one outdated word, and the glossary shows that the vocabulary reaches into the cruelest material (the neutral definitional tone there is the compiler's, not evidence of how soldiers sound).

## Pattern roll-up

**Defector / exile / dissent specifics (the priority group).**

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Deserter humor is thin and single-sourced.** Only WA-082 reports jokes by deserters (about a dozen lines, two men, translated, pseudonymous, in a story whose headings and teaser lines are the editors'). The men's asylum applications were pending; the article names the status but does not discuss any effect on how they tell it. It is also a very short-term picture (the moment of exit). The Vietnam-era sources (WA-083, WA-084, WA-090, WA-091) are outsiders' accounts and contain no deserter's joke.
2. **Whose irony?** In WA-082 the journalists add "Ironically" and "surprisingly modest"; in WA-083 and WA-084 the comic register is the book author's and publisher's; in WA-091 the amusement is the reviewer's. I flag these in the findings; do not carry them over as the exiles' own humor.
3. **Authored material is not recorded talk.** WA-085 (Dovlatov) is autofiction as read by a critic; WA-012 is a professional comedian's reconstruction; WA-079 and WA-080 retell jokes without collection data. Treat as craft evidence and as documented devices, not as transcripts.
4. **A prank and a quip, not a pattern.** WA-086 gives one prank and one remark and asserts a "penchant" without further examples; the obituarist's political commentary is not evidence about Belenko.
5. **Dates and bylines missing** for WA-082 (no byline), WA-083 (no year; about 2018 by inference), WA-087 (no date; about 2012 by inference), WA-088 (no byline; about early 2013 by inference), WA-079 and WA-080 (no date).
6. **Tensions and variances.** WA-079 relays that after the war began there are almost no new Putin jokes; WA-080 says there are hundreds if not thousands of Putin jokes (not necessarily in conflict, since one is about new jokes and the other cumulative, but unreconciled). WA-082's section heading renders a farewell to the dead with "kid" while the quote has "mate." WA-079's writer claims arrests for jokes and WA-080's columnist claims camp sentences in Stalin's time; neither is documented in the copy.
7. **My readings flagged in the text:** the chicken joke's punch (E23-23); the "smart house" apartment joke (E23-29); the Karakozov joke's punch (E23-31); "Butlins" (E23-38); Belenko's "Beltway Bandits" quip's target (E23-18). Also, from outside the sources: the name of the deserters' aid group in WA-082 ("Idite Lesom," glossed in the article as "Get Lost") is, I believe, a Russian idiom whose literal sense is "go by way of the forest," which would pun on the border crossing that followed; the source does not say this, and it needs verifying before use.
8. **Not read, not available in the text copies.** The NRK Norwegian original (WA-082); the Arkhipova article (WA-079); the cartoons themselves (WA-003); the audio and transcript of the Australian interview (WA-026); the Sassoon text (WA-001); the verse of *Vasily Tyorkin* (WA-014); the American veterans' slang audio (WA-016); the Library of Congress and Texas Tech archives (WA-011, WA-024); the song archive (WA-004). All would need a browser or a library.
9. **Pronouns and spelling.** Where a source does not state an author's or speaker's pronoun I used names or roles. Source spelling in British English was paraphrased or Americanized (noted for the "armorbarma" term, E23-38). URLs unchanged.
10. **Do not confuse** Matthew Sweet (author of *Operation Chaos*; WA-083, WA-084) with Matthew Turner (novelist, WA-090), and note that the VICE and Pan Macmillan pieces cover the Sweden exile while WA-090 covers only the smuggling from Japan.
