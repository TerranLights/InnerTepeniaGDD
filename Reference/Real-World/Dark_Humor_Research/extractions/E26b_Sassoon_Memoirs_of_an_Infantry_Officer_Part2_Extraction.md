## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-09 is.** The Standard Ebooks plain-text edition of Siegfried Sassoon's *Memoirs of an Infantry Officer* (1930), the second volume of an autobiographical novel in which a first-person narrator, "George Sherston," recounts his service in a regiment called the Flintshire Fusiliers (a renamed regiment) and a depot he calls Clitherland, writing thirteen years or so after the events. Names of people, units and places are changed. My range, L560 to the end, holds: ch. VII §I-II (Rouen base, February 1917: the guard room, the hospital tent, the notice board, the cathedral, the Hotel de la Poste); ch. VIII (the Second Battalion at Camp 13, the march to Arras, the Hindenburg trench and tunnel, the bombing attack of 16 April and the narrator's wound, the journey home); ch. IX (hospital at Denmark Hill, a London convalescent hotel, convalescence at Nutwood Manor, the narrator's growing doubts, and letters from the front); ch. X, "Independent Action" (the editor Markington, the mentor Tyrrell, the writing of the declaration, the Cambridge pretext, the double life at home, Clitherland and the acting commander, Liverpool and Formby, David Cromlech's visit, the Medical Board, and the arrival at "Slateford War Hospital," where the book ends at L1096). L1098-1147 are the Standard Ebooks colophon and "Uncopyright" boilerplate.

**Reader's guide.** The brief's priority material, the decision to protest and the Army's handling of it, is E26b-49 through E26b-88 (hospital doubts at E26b-49 to -52; the decision at E26b-61 to -66; the split loyalties at E26b-67 to -74; the statement, the Colonel, the friend and the Board at E26b-75 to -88). E26b-1 to -48 cover Rouen, the Second Battalion, Arras and the Hindenburg line, the wound, and the first stage of convalescence.

**Evidence base.** One narrator: an infantry second-lieutenant with a Military Cross, private means and a regimental family connection, in February to July 1917. British Army (the New Army battalions and cavalry appear as types), Western Front (Arras sector, Hindenburg Line) and home front (London hospital, a country house, Liverpool). The text embeds documents given as verbatim: a communiqué (L721), an operation order (L769), the declaration (L1009), a Quartermaster's letter (L911), a servant's letter (L913), press clippings (L885), the Archbishop's message (L839) and a medical officer's published remark (L851). Dialogue is reconstructed. The narrator's comic stance is stated in the text itself (parenthetical asides and a dry voice) and the book's own announced method is "I am merely describing my own experiences" (L709).

**What it does NOT contain.** No enlisted narrator (other ranks speak in short quoted lines, the Sergeant at the base and a voice from the ranks); no trench-newspaper material; no songs beyond one popular chorus and fragments; no hospital treatment or hospital doctor after the decision, no return to the line, and no civilian life after the war, because the volume ends on arrival at the hospital; no court-martial or prison scene (the protest is stopped before either); no account of the dissenter's effect on his unit. The one beating of conscientious objectors is a Major's anecdote, not an on-page event. No defector as such: the narrator's protest is an officer's declaration, ended by a medical board, not an exit from the service.

Italics are lost in the plain text; an unprintable word in a joke is rendered as a dash blank (L631); one apparent typo (L869) is not quoted. Long single-paragraph lines. The colophon and the Uncopyright page (L1098-1147) are boilerplate and not extracted.

### A. Arrival at the Base, Rouen, February 1917 (L560-607)

**[E26b-1]. Crossing back: relief at re-entering "the Machine," and the narrator's aside to the reader about "slow-motion wartime details."** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L560-562)
Sherston crosses to Le Havre in the second week of February 1917, and "As soon as the boat began to move I was aware of a sense of relief": responsibility for his future now lies in "the haphazard control of whatever powers manipulated the British Expeditionary Force." He says most men felt this and that the experience was called "being for it again." Of the thirty-three-hour journey to the Rouen base he notes in parentheses that he records the length "for the benefit of those who like slow-motion wartime details." The only other thing he recalls of the crossing is that someone stole his new trench-coat while he slept. Mechanism: the relief of surrendering agency is stated plainly and given a soldiers' catchphrase; the humor is the authorial wink at the reader and the petty-theft bathos. This is the narrator's account in a first-person novel.
*Candidate application:* A returning soldier can feel relief at being taken back into the institution's control, and the army's own slang ("for it") names the feeling. The dry aside to the civilian reader is a documented way of fencing off the boring and the awful in one parenthesis.

**[E26b-2]. The guard-room scene on arrival: a man in grief and rage, and the sergeant's weary formula.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L562)
Looking for the blanket store in the dark, Sherston opens a door onto a guard room where a man stripped to the waist kneels, clutching his chest and weeping, with the guard standing "embarrassed" and the sergeant "patient and unpitying." The sergeant explains: the man was held for assaulting military police and has just heard his brother was killed; he is "'Arf crazy," tore off his clothes and cursed the war and the Germans, and "It's his third time out." The sergeant's closing line is the explanation: "A Blighty one don't last a man long nowadays, sir." (A "Blighty one" is a wound that sends a man home.) Sherston goes off "into the gloom" still hearing the howling. No joke; the plain register is carried by the sergeant's dialect speech and an institutional shrug, and the narrator then comments only that this was "a damned depressing spot to arrive at."
*Candidate application:* The base is where grief and a disciplinary charge sit in the same room; the sergeant's weary, even kind, formula is a veteran's way of making a breakdown an ordinary camp event. *Cross-use:* A robot or exile community's version of a "detention room" scene could carry the same flatness.

**[E26b-3]. Two officers' talk through a blanket partition and the narrator's self-correcting disgust.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L564)
Lying awake in a four-foot-high canvas segment, Sherston overhears two officers "in rapid Welsh voices" comparing a squalid Rouen brothel. He calls it "the most disgusting little conversation I'd ever listened to," then immediately checks himself ("what right had I to blame the poor devils for trying to have a good time before they went up to the Line?") and ends: "Nevertheless, the War seemed to be doing its best to make me feel unheroic." Mechanism: the narrator's snobbery is exposed by his own second thought; "unheroic" is the dry label for the effect of crude company on his self-image. (Restrained handling: sexual talk, summarized; the ethnic tag "Welsh" is the narrator's, documented, not endorsed.)
*Candidate application:* The last-night-before-the-line coarse talk is judged by the narrator and then forgiven; the pattern of disgust followed by sympathy recurs through the book.

**[E26b-4]. The Base Depot notice board: names for the line, and three names for the firing squad, in the same bureaucratic hand.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L566)
The mess at the Infantry Base Depot is dispiriting: nobody knows anybody, they grumble about the "alcoholic R.C. padre" who runs the mess, the food, and "four and threepence a day," and they talk about the war "with an admixture of ineffective cynicism which hadn't existed twelve months before." After a notice goes up, officers crowd round to see whether they are on the list for the line next day; those who are "laughed harshly and sat down, with simulated unconcern, to read a stale picture paper." On the same board, three private soldiers' names are posted as shot for cowardice since late January, followed by the formula "The sentence was duly carried out." Mechanism: no joke from the narrator; the pairing is the point, and the "laughed harshly" is the officers' only humor. (Restrained handling: executions, summarized from a notice; no method is described. Compare E15c-5 and E15c-4 for the same institutional format in Graves.)
*Candidate application:* A "harsh laugh" plus a feigned interest in an old newspaper is a documented way a man receives his orders for the line; an execution notice on the same board makes the cynicism look cheap.

**[E26b-5]. The hospital tent: five officers, five self-presentations, and the humor of each.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L568-580)
Sherston, down with German measles, is moved into a tent of six already-settled officers. The narrator's studies: Parkins, a thirty-year-old Cambridge schoolmaster who uses stilted words, quotes the *Rubáiyát* at the nurses and calls the tent "this battered Caravanserai," amuses the others with "indecent rhymes and anecdotes," hates the front, and (the narrator predicts) "would get killed in some unutterably wretched attack after doing his best to dodge it." Holt, a balloon officer who "fancied himself" socially above the infantry, says there is "more tone in the R.F.A." and repeats "a stale joke": "I will arise and will go unto my father and will say unto him: Father, stand-at-ease!" (a parody of the Prodigal Son line). White, a sensible Territorial captain, is "sardonic about English methods" and respected by his men. Patterson, nineteen, a Scot, "made simple-minded war jokes, and then surprised me by quoting Milton and Keats"; the narrator ends, "Did he live, I wonder?" Strangford, nineteen, silent except about his father's harriers. The narrator's general observation: in the "accidental intimacy of army life, men were usually anxious to exhibit themselves to the best advantage, particularly as regards their civilian antecedents." Mechanism: the humor is observation of men's self-image; their own jokes are shown as stale (Holt's), naïve (Patterson's), or performed (Parkins's). (Restrained handling: "indecent rhymes" noted, not reproduced; the narrator gives none.)
*Candidate application:* A documented hospital-tent social comedy: each man's repeated joke is a status tool, and the narrator rates them by whether they are stale. The "Did he live?" line is the plain register arriving after a comic portrait.

**[E26b-6]. In the pinewoods: losing belief in the war, wanting to be like the young believer, and a verse of the *Rubáiyát* as prayer.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L582-585)
Slipping out through the hospital wire, Sherston walks among pines whose "surf-like sighing" calms him; "The pine-trees are patiently waiting for the guns to stop, I thought." At a shuttered house with an untidy garden the barnyard sounds are "comforting when one was in the exile of army life." He admits "I was losing my belief in the War, and I longed for mental acquiescence," to be like Patterson, who could still kneel and say simple prayers believing in the war. The only prayer that seems worth saying to him now is a quatrain of the *Rubáiyát* asking forgiveness for "all the Sin wherewith the face of Man is blackened." No joke; the register is plain and lyrical, and it marks the point where the narrator's belief starts to fail.
*Candidate application:* Documented from the inside: the first stage of private disaffection is a longing to be a believer again, not a wish to dissent. Quiet outdoor refuge and a borrowed text are what the narrator reaches for.

**[E26b-7]. The two doctors and the Major: a pessimist who enjoys predicting defeat, an optimist who proposes three more years, and the beating of conscientious objectors.** (PW-09, ch. VII §II, L589-591)
Back at the Base after ten days, Sherston waits nine mornings for his name to appear (it is "typewritten and slightly misspelt Sharston"). The pink-faced Medical Officer is "a profound pessimist" who tells him the Germans' retreat to the Hindenburg Line means they are "pulling our legs properly"; Sherston notes that the doctor "always looked his brightest when he was announcing that we were certain to lose the War." A Rifle Brigade Major, deaf in one ear and a "resolute optimist," says "Yes, things couldn't be much worse, but another two or three years ought to see the job finished." The Major then recounts with relish his dealings with two conscientious objectors at his depot: he had the educated one given two years' hard labor, and he had the other, a "humble inarticulate wretch" who refused to march, hauled behind a wagon along a road until badly injured, after which, the Major says, the man submitted, "made good" as a soldier, and was later killed in the trenches; "Discipline had to be enforced by brutality," says the Major. Sherston, who notes the Major "wasn't amenable to argument," gives no reply and writes only: "I hadn't formed any opinion about Conscientious Objectors, but I couldn't help thinking that they must be braver men than some I'd seen wearing uniforms in safe places and taking salutes from genuine soldiers." Mechanism: two opposite gallows temperaments (the doctor's cheer at bad news, the Major's cheer at worse) set side by side; the Major's story is told as a hearty anecdote with a happy ending, and the narrator's response is silence on the page followed by a private thought. This is the first place in this half of the book where the narrator thinks about dissenters at all. (Restrained handling: a man's being dragged behind a wagon and the narrator's own silence are summarized; the wording of the Major's account is not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* A man who will later dissent first sees how a hearty officer tells the story of breaking a dissenter, and responds by saying nothing and drawing a quiet comparison to the uniformed men in safe jobs. The institution's own account of the resister who "made good" and died is told as a success.

**[E26b-8]. A last haircut, and the officer whose scar is a "souvenir."** (PW-09, ch. VII §II, L595)
On his last day Sherston tells the barber to cut off "as much as he could" and stares at himself, "speculating prosaically on the probabilities of my head of hair ever needing another trim up." A captain in the next chair, wearing trench boots and heading for the line, says "Now I feel a quid better" and tells the barber that the Allies will "give them the knock all right this journey"; asked about a long scar on his head, he smiles: "A souvenir of Devil's Wood." Sherston wonders how long the man's enthusiasm will last. Mechanism: a deadpan, practical reason for a short haircut that carries a survival calculation inside it; the captain's cheerful, tourist-like word "souvenir" for a wound.
*Candidate application:* A character's pre-line ritual (the last haircut) is a place where the odds are calculated aloud by implication; the euphemistic "souvenir" for a wound is a standard understated form.

**[E26b-9]. The cathedral: the Church as "Benevolent Neutral Power" and the parenthesis that leaves the Germans out.** (PW-09, ch. VII §II, L597-601)
Going into Rouen Cathedral to "escape from the War for a while," Sherston adds that "the Christian Religion had apparently no claim to be regarded as a Benevolent Neutral Power." He watches a child who cannot understand the sermon "any more than it understood the War," and infers the preacher is telling people to love one another because "Jésu" died to save us, then adds in parentheses "(but not to save the Germans or the Austrians or any of that lot)." He admits "It was no good trying to feel uplifted, when such thoughts grimaced at me," but finds consolation in the building as the windows darken. Mechanism: a diplomatic metaphor turned on religion ("Benevolent Neutral Power"); a parenthetical sarcasm about who is excluded from salvation; the register turns lyrical in the last lines.
*Candidate application:* A soldier who is losing faith in the war expresses it as sarcasm toward the Church's silence on the enemy; a place of quiet is allowed to remain quiet.

**[E26b-10]. The Hôtel de la Poste: base staff as "London Clubmen" with "a conscientious objection to physical discomfort," and the narrator's wine-fed tolerance.** (PW-09, ch. VII §II, L605-607)
At the best hotel in Rouen, "frequented by every British officer employed in the district," senior officers had even suggested putting it out of bounds to infantry subalterns going up, since a "temporary gentleman" enjoying his last dinner behaved in a way "more suitable to a dugout than a military club". Sherston watches a green-tabbed Colonel scowl at the subalterns, imagines him supervising "an office full of clerks who made lists of killed, wounded, and reinforcements" (he himself was once received "with no civility at all" by such an office), watches young cavalrymen who "were still waiting for their chance," and ends by calling the dining room "full of London Clubmen dressed as Colonels, Majors, and Captains with a conscientious objection to physical discomfort." Then the turn: "somebody had to be at the Base"; they "were as much the victims of circumstances as the unfortunate troops in the trenches"; and "By the time I'd finished my dinner and a bottle of Burgundy, I felt softhearted about almost everything" and "there was a tolerant view to be taken about almost everybody, especially after a good dinner." Mechanism: the book's key sarcasm borrows the phrase "conscientious objection," which the Major used in the previous section for the men he beat, and applies it to the Base's comfortable officers; the narrator then deflates his own indignation with food and wine. This is a documented form of rank-and-front resentment ("the Base Details") softened by the narrator's self-mocking comfort.
*Candidate application:* Front-line resentment of the rear can be voiced in a borrowed phrase and then retracted in the same page; the reader sees the narrator as someone whose anger is easily dissolved by a decent meal. The reuse of "conscientious objection" across two adjacent passages is a link worth noting for the protest chapters later in this file.

### B. The Second Battalion in Corps Reserve, Camp 13 (L609-663)

**[E26b-11]. "Tedious and repetitional": the book's own deflation of the war's grandeur, and the boots.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §I, L615-617)
The chapter opens: "Although the War has been described as the greatest event in history, it could be tedious and repetitional for an ordinary Infantry Officer like myself." He is deposited at Corbie at midnight after eight hours in an unlit, overcrowded carriage with no glass in the windows; his valise rides off on the train and is returned a week later "with one sample of everything subtracted by someone at the Army Service Corps Dump." He walks seven miles in his greased marching boots ("my best friends"), and tells the reader that if their importance "sounds irrelevant," "a platoon commander's feet were his fortune." Mechanism: a mock-grand sentence reduced by "repetitional"; the petty-theft line by understatement; the boot passage by earnest self-defense against an imagined civilian reader.
*Candidate application:* Documented reflex: the narrator anticipates the civilian reader's impatience and defends the trivial. The boots passage is a plain example of what mattered to a platoon commander.

**[E26b-12]. General Whincop, the "No Rum Division," and the proposed "Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Great War Generals."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §I, L619)
The Second Battalion has come out of two months of severe frost at the Cléry sector: "no braziers in the trenches," wooden crosses taken from casual graves for fuel, mostly cold food. The divisional commander, Major-General Whincop, "had made himself obnoxiously conspicuous by forbidding the Rum Ration" (and earlier forbade steel helmets, objecting that they would "make them soft"), so the division was "the 'No Rum Division,'" though it "failed to appreciate their uniqueness." Sherston's reading: Generals, "like platoon commanders, were obliged to devise 'stunts' to show their keenness," and the helmet veto was "only a flash in the pan (or brainpan)." Then he turns: "since 1918 Generals have received their full share of ridicule and abuse," and he would "gladly contribute my modest half-guinea per annum" to a "Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Great War Generals," because "many an unsuccessful General had previously been the competent Colonel of an Infantry Battalion, thereby earning the gratitude and admiration of his men." Mechanism: a mock charity as a vehicle for a half-serious defense of generals; a pun; the turn from derision to a qualified sympathy. (The same divisional rum prohibition in the same sector and winter is reported as a plain fact in E15c-2, where Graves credits it with a heavier sick-list; the two accounts agree on the ban and its place, and Sassoon's narrator treats it as comedy and Graves's as a causal claim.)
*Candidate application:* A documented pattern for how veterans ridicule a commander, then temper the ridicule with the knowledge that commanders were men placed in the post by the same machine.

**[E26b-13]. Coke fumes, trench-feet, and a Colonel whose amiability "is not enough."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §I, L621)
Coal rationing at home meant coke for the army, and coke fumes poisoned men in unventilated dugouts; a thaw then produced "a hundred cases of trench-feet." The battalion is "in low spirits" because the popular colonel has been wounded; his successor "was indulgent and conciliatory" but "greater aggressiveness would have been preferable." Sherston's conclusion: "in a Commanding Officer, amiability is not enough." Mechanism: flat enumeration of a winter's casualties from administrative causes; a short aphorism. No humor beyond the closing maxim.
*Candidate application:* Evidence that men may miss the abrasive, efficient commander and judge the kind one negatively; the unit's loyalty tracks competence.

**[E26b-14]. Training the draft: "less dangerous in his preexisting ignorance," and hide-and-seek among the trees.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §I, L623-625)
The platoon includes eight Private Joneses, so the first difficulty is "to differentiate between them"; the draft is "undersized, dull-witted"; and in one case platoon training "began with the man being taught how to load his rifle," after which Sherston "felt that he would have been less dangerous in his preexisting ignorance." The manual is out of date, the sergeant "conscientious but unenterprising." So one gray afternoon he has a "bright unmilitary idea": the men play hide-and-seek among the trees. After a "self-conscious beginning they livened up and actually enjoyed themselves"; he sees "a sense of humanity had been restored to them." Mechanism: deadpan understatement about incompetence; then a deliberately childish game used as the one thing that works. He adds that he realized how intolerable the ordinary exercises were "unless the instructor was an expert."
*Candidate application:* A documented example of an officer using play as a restorative where drill has failed; the narrator names the effect himself ("humanity restored"), so the function is stated, not inferred.

**[E26b-15]. "Lots of Loving": a mess prank that used a worn gramophone record, and the victim's outraged literalism.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §I, L631)
On a Sunday walk to Heilly, Ormand recalls how two fellow officers used to "chip" him about his little gramophone, especially one record, "Lots of Loving." The joke was to pretend the record contained an unprintable epithet. On one occasion they recruited the Adjutant, who asked Ormand to play it and then feigned astonishment at an indistinct word on the worn disc; Ormand replied angrily, in the text: "I ask you, is it bloody likely that 'His Master's Voice' would send out a record with the word —————— in it?" (the dashes are the book's own). Ormand says "they overdid it." Mechanism: a conspiracy of officers (including a senior one) against a cheerful, hot-tempered young officer, relying on a deniable ambiguity in a worn record; the victim's reply invokes the company's reputation as proof. The narrator notes the joke as an item of the lost First Battalion's mess life, now remembered by a man who has just joined the Second.
*Candidate application:* A rank-crossing prank (the Adjutant joins in) shows that an adjutant may take part in the junior officers' teasing of one of their number; "overdid it" is the victim's own limit on the form. Pranks are told after the pranksters are gone.

**[E26b-16]. Ormand and the court-martial: the swerve from a death sentence to an injection.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §I, L633)
As they walk back, Ormand's cheer "diminished"; he doesn't "fancy his chance" in the spring offensive, longs for a civilian career, and hates "this silly stunt which the blasted Bishops call the Great Adventure." The day before he had sat on a court-martial, needed only "to make up the quorum," and yet was upset: "Some poor wretch had been condemned to be shot for cowardice," with a recommendation to mercy. Ormand then "relieved the situation" by saying "And tomorrow I've got to have my … anti-typhoid injection!" and Sherston answered by reminding him that he was "reducing his overdraft at Cox's by being at the Front." Mechanism: bathos by swerve, a minor dread named to replace a major one; the friend's reply is a joke about officers' pay. Cox's is the army agents' bank where officers' pay accrued. (Restrained handling: a capital sentence for cowardice, summarized; no execution is described. Compare E15c-4, the same charge seen from the other side of a court.)
*Candidate application:* The pattern: when a man is upset by a legal killing, he picks something small (an injection) to complain about, and the listener helps by supplying a second small, comic grievance (money). Neither names the thing.

**[E26b-17]. The Company Commander's "warts," and the M.O.'s double injection.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L641-643)
Captain Leake, a former Special Reserve officer, holds to a pre-war custom in which new second-lieutenants were "warts" whom seniors did not speak to except on parade; he opens by being rude to Sherston and remarks, for his benefit, that "these newly-joined warts were getting too big for their boots" (when in fact Sherston was grieving the loss of his valise); he also resents the narrator's service in the First Battalion, "a hated rival." He "thawed gradually." The M.O., Captain Munro, gives Sherston his anti-typhoid injection with the remark, "Hullo, here's Sherston, the man who did stunts with the First Battalion," giving "double injections, so as to save us the trouble of feeling unwell twice," and says, "That'll keep you quiet for forty-eight hours." Sherston "retired, with a sickly grin." Mechanism: peacetime-regiment rank etiquette surviving into war; the doctor's dry efficiency-as-kindness joke, with a mild dig at the narrator's reputation for recklessness. The narrator also supplies a coy aside: the M.O. is a "famous character," disliking applause in print.
*Candidate application:* A reputation for reckless "stunts" precedes a man to the new unit, and a doctor's first remark may both acknowledge and tease it. Two registers of dry humor (the C.O.'s snubs, the doctor's caring jokes) can sit in the same mess.

**[E26b-18]. The march out: German prisoners at work, and the troops' sense of luck.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L645)
A gang of red-and-blue-capped German prisoners at work on the road, with "sullen undernourished faces," makes the British column "look as if they were lucky in some sort of liberty." Then whistles blow and the march resumes. No joke; one sentence of plain observation.
*Candidate application:* A brief picture of how the enemy appears when captured (undernourished, working): the narrator does not comment on it as humane or otherwise, only on the relative luck.

**[E26b-19]. Wilmot's "bit of a jolly": champagne, an upright piano, and "every glass of wine made us dislike one another a little less."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L649-659)
Ralph Wilmot, a monocled veteran of dispatch riding, Gallipoli and France, who "liked to see life as a tragicomedy and himself as a debonair philosopher," and whose "unobtrusive jests were enunciated with a stammer which somehow increased their effect," arranges an evening in a wine-merchant's parlor ("buy some bubbly and tickle the ivories"). The three officers drink "bottle after bottle," "farewell to civilization with an air of finality," while Wilmot plays opera extracts and ballads and repeats one popular song until the room is "tropical" with smoke. The narrator's observation: "every glass of wine made us dislike one another a little less." The wine-merchant's wife takes the francs and gets all their names in her album, and he reflects she "must have made a handsome profit out of the War." Walking home, Sherston and Leake shake hands "sholemnly" and Sherston assures him he can rely on him to "blurry well do my damndest for him"; Sherston has trouble with the keyhole and says aloud in the greengrocer's dark shop, "Well, old boy, here you are, and now you gotter get up the stairs." Next morning "Leake was grumpy at breakfast," but "by the first halt I was feeling surprisingly clearheaded." Mechanism: the narrator uses drunk speech spelled phonetically for comedy; the evening's purpose is explicit, bonding with an antagonistic senior before the offensive. This is a documented farewell-ritual with a performer (the pianist) and a civilian profiting from it.
*Candidate application:* A sing-along farewell party before a push, organized by the unit's resident tragicomic philosopher, is the mechanism by which a hostile company commander and a subaltern are brought to good terms. The bonding is accomplished with alcohol and a repeated chorus, not by any speech.

### C. The march to Arras and the Hindenburg Line, 2-16 April 1917 (L665-799)

**[E26b-20]. The march north: a corps commander's tirade, the voice from the ranks, and "greater dignity" than a blusterer.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L665-673)
In a new Army Area the battalion sights a mounted Corps Commander by the roadside. The text gives the exchange in script form: Corps Commander, "Are you stuck to that bloody horse?"; Colonel Easby, "No, sir." (the text adds: dismounts hastily and salutes again). The General then yells about uncovered rifle muzzles (one of his "special ideas") and about the cookers carrying brooms. From the ranks comes the line "Pity he don't keep his own muzzle covered," which the narrator says voices "a prevalent feeling." He closes with the consolation that "Allenby, at any rate, could be rude to him if he wanted to." On the next stretch he decides, "a humble soldier holding up a blistered foot could have greater dignity than a blustering Corps Commander." Mechanism: an anonymous retort from within a marching column (no face attached, so no punishment), plus a sentence in which the narrator transfers the insult up the chain. The retort is a pun on "muzzle." Earlier on the same march a soldier had also nicknamed the signpost "Arras, 32 k" as "That way to the Sausage Machine!" (L671), the men's name for the coming battle.
*Candidate application:* The ranks' quip is voiced as a collective feeling, not as a person's act; the narrator's own aloofness from the General is expressed as a measure of dignity. The nickname "Sausage Machine" for a battle is an example of a gallows place-name coined before the event.

**[E26b-21]. "Any joke being better than none": "Fish Hooks," and the narrator's defense of dull marching notes.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II-III, L669, L705)
After a deliberately plain march entry the narrator addresses the reader: "There is nothing in all this, the reader will expostulate. But there was a lot in it, for us," because the approaching offensive gave the days "an ever intensifying significance"; for him "the idea of death made everything seem vivid and valuable." In a snowstorm on 11 April a signpost leads to a village that is no longer there; the men call the next place Ficheux "Fish Hooks," in the narrator's words "any joke being better than none in that snowstorm." Mechanism: a corrupted place-name as a cheap, shared joke whose worth is that it exists. The narrator also gives his own formula for what danger does to perception.
*Candidate application:* The best statement in this half of the book of the floor for joke quality: in misery, any joke is better than none. A nickname spoken by a column in bad weather is a unit-wide act, not an individual wit.

**[E26b-22]. At Saulty: the narrator decides to "make a little drama out of my own experience."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L681-685)
Sherston buys twelve dozen Woodbines for the company's "emergency," then sits on a tree-stump in a château park and examines why he is eager for the battle ("emotional satisfaction"). He recalls the newspapers' printed farewell letters from second-lieutenants ("Never has life brought me such an abundance of noble feelings") which he never believed, then catches himself "working myself up into a similar mental condition." He has lost faith in the war; the only thing left to believe in is "the Battalion spirit," and he knows its "humanities" are impermanent ("A single machine-gun or a few shells might wipe out the whole picture within a week"). Resolution: "to make a little drama out of my own experience—that was the way out. I must play at being a hero in shining armor, as I'd done last year; if I didn't, I might crumple up altogether." He adds (parenthetically) that self-inflicted wounds "weren't uncommon" and that "brave men had put bullets through their own heads before now, especially when winter made trench warfare unendurable." He ends: "Having thus decided on death or glory, I knocked my pipe out." Mechanism: the narrator describes his heroism as a deliberate act of self-theater with a protective function, and names its alternative as breakdown. (Restrained handling: a one-clause mention of soldiers' suicide and self-inflicted wounds, summarized; no method beyond "bullets" in the text and none reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* The soldier-narrator's self-diagnosed role-playing ("play at being a hero") is an explicit, source-attested mechanism: a deliberate performance that prevents collapse. For a dissenter, the same man's later refusal to perform is a change of script, not a loss of nerve.

**[E26b-23]. Arras preliminaries: "a fair bloody treat to listen to," forced optimism, and "something in his mind which he couldn't utter."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L689, L697)
Of the preliminary bombardment, someone in the ranks says it is "a fair bloody treat to listen to"; the narrator adds "We insisted on being optimistic" (Tanks, Cavalry, a divisional general's "walkover"). When the battle starts, he writes of "Military History being manufactured regardless of expense" (a parenthesis) and says that "With forced uneasy gaiety we talked loudly about the successes reported from the Line," while "every one of us had something in his mind which he couldn't utter, even to his best friend." Mechanism: the rank-and-file reaction to the guns is the connoisseur's idiom (a "treat"); the narrator's parenthesis puts industrial costing language on the making of history; the unspoken thought is named as the limit of speech between close friends.
*Candidate application:* Documented pattern: loud talk of successes is the group's cover for a private unsayable; even best friends do not trade it. "Manufactured regardless of expense" is the narrator's mock-commercial register for the battle.

**[E26b-24]. Aunt Evelyn's letter, and a gastric complaint met with an opium pill.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §II, L699)
During the first days of the battle the narrator is ill with a chill, a sore throat and festering scratches on both hands and cannot "feel like a happy warrior turning his necessities to glorious gain." His only letter is from Aunt Evelyn, who apologizes for having so little to say, has been reading *The Life of Disraeli* ("such a relief to get away from all these present-day horrors"), hopes he is "still in the Rest Camp," and reports spring-cleaning and "floods of conversation from the maids." He notes the letter "didn't help" the complaint, which an orderly's opium pill resolves with a pun on "open warfare." Mechanism: the contrast between the aunt's domestic news and the front, with no comment from the narrator. (Restrained handling: the pun on a bodily-function complaint is summarized; the wording is not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* A civilian relative's well-meant letter that cannot imagine the situation is a recurring comic device; here it arrives on the battle's second day, unremarked.

**[E26b-25]. The first dead man on the approach march, and the narrator's right to be "momentarily horrified."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §III, L709)
Entering a demolished village, Sherston sees an English soldier by the road with a badly smashed head; he says "this first one was perceptibly unpleasant," and defends the "right to be momentarily horrified by a mangled body seen on an afternoon walk," against people of "sound common sense" who say life is full of gruesome sights. He then states his own position: "I am no believer in wild denunciations of the War; I am merely describing my own experiences of it; and in 1917 I was only beginning to learn that life, for the majority of the population, is an unlovely struggle against unfair odds, culminating in a cheap funeral." He finishes with a dry note that the dead man "had achieved theoretical glory by dying for his country" and that the passing men "had an excellent chance of following his example." Mechanism: the register keeps a bureaucratic irony ("theoretical glory," "excellent chance") over a plain statement of horror; the self-disclaimer ("no believer in wild denunciations") is a stated limit on what the narrator will say. (Restrained handling: the head wound is summarized; the physical description is not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* A character can disclaim anti-war rhetoric while describing a corpse; the ironic phrasing "theoretical glory" is the narrator's way of speaking about patriotic death without preaching.

**[E26b-26]. Rabbit-hole headquarters: the lost bacon, and Rees and Shirley, two ways of managing fear.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §III, L711-717)
In a captured trench, Company Headquarters is a hole just wide enough for Leake, a stove and Sherston; the narrator drops his scrap of ration bacon into the stove, retrieves it despite his festering fingers and eats it "with great relish." He observes the two new officers: Rees, a "garrulous and excitable little Welshman" without "pretensions to being 'a gentleman,'" keeps up his courage "by talking incessantly and making jokes about the battle," and after a first shelling returns "noisier than ever," recounting his escapes with profanity and "roaring with laughter," because he "never minded making himself look ridiculous." Shirley, a Winchester and Oxford man, "simulated nonchalance," discussing Oxford colleges with Leake, "But he couldn't get the nonchalance into his eyes." Sherston later takes Shirley for a walk up the hill "to educate him in unpleasant sights"; Shirley says nothing, as if it were "part of the public school tradition," and when a distant explosion goes up Sherston remarks "sagely" that it was probably a German dump, while Shirley "watched it intently as though the experience would be of use." The narrator then flatly notes "Both Shirley and Rees were killed before the autumn." (Restrained handling: the dead on the hillside are summarized as arranged by stretcher-bearers in "happy warrior attitudes," with letters scattered; the narrator's phrase "a sort of visible fraction of the Roll of Honor" is quoted.)
*Candidate application:* A documented pair of humor styles split by class: the talker who laughs at his own fear and the officer who performs composure. The narrator rates the first as more reliable ("capable of taking care of himself"); the death note follows both without comment.

**[E26b-27]. The communiqué: institutional language over a failed attack.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §III, L721)
In a pocket, Sherston has "a recent communiqué (circulated for instructive purposes)" and quotes it: "That night three unsuccessful bombing attacks were made on the Tower at Wancourt. During the Battalion relief the next night, the enemy opened a heavy bombardment on the Tower and its immediate vicinity, following it up with an attack which succeeded, mainly owing to the relief being in progress. A local counterattack delivered by the incoming battalion failed owing to the darkness, pouring rain, and lack of knowledge of the ground. It was then decided that nothing could be done till daylight." His comment: "The lesson to be drawn from this episode was, I think, that lack of Artillery preparation is a mistake," and "anyone who has been in the front-line can amplify that communiqué for himself." Mechanism: a bland official sequence ("it was then decided that nothing could be done") left to stand for what it covers; the narrator's own "lesson" is an understated satirical summary.
*Candidate application:* Public-domain evidence for the "amplify for himself" device: the veteran reader is invited to fill in the report's omissions; a defector character could be written as a reader of such reports.

**[E26b-28]. Lost guides and the officer leading men "in the wrong direction."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §IV, L729-733)
Guides leading the company to relieve the Northumberland Fusiliers become less authoritative until they confess at midnight that they are lost. The narrator's parenthesis: "There were few things more uncomfortable in the life of an officer than to be walking in front of a party of men all of whom knew that he was leading them in the wrong direction." He blunders into a sunken road, finds sappers, and the exchange is "a case of 'Please, can you tell me the way to the Hindenburg Trench?'" He congratulates himself on his "cleverness." The rendezvous is hard to find because "wrong map-references had been issued by the Brigade Staff," and the companies file to their "ordained (and otherwise anathematized)" positions. Mechanism: farce of the front (a polite stranger's request in the wrong register for the setting); parentheses that carry the real feeling.
*Candidate application:* A comic set-piece in which the staff's error and the guides' bluster are shown from the point of view of the officer who has to bear the men's silent judgment.

**[E26b-29]. The arrival in the Hindenburg trench: the Field Cashier thought, "self-dramatizing desperation," and a platoon fleeing into the Tunnel.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §IV, L735-741)
The narrator describes the arrival as a dreadful place "which no imagination could have invented," where "a man of strong spirit might know himself utterly powerless against death and destruction, and yet stand up and defy gross darkness and stupefying shellfire, discovering in himself the invincible resistance of an animal or an insect, and an endurance which he might, in after days, forget or disbelieve." Interrupting himself ("But this is nothing new to write about, you will say"), he tells of feeling homeless in the unfinished trench and reminds himself of his remoteness from safety with the thought of the Field Cashier, whose fifty-franc notes were "no earthly use up here," so that he could not be sure he would be "alive and kicking the week after next." He credits "the self-dramatizing desperation which enabled a good many of us to worry our way through much worse emergencies than mine." Walking along to find the next company, he meets a thirty-to-forty-man platoon of a neighboring "amateur" battalion pushing through a doorway into the Tunnel because "the Germans were coming over"; he concludes only that the Tunnel was "having a lowering effect on their morale." (Restrained handling: men in the trench "huddled" in postures like death, summarized.)
*Candidate application:* The narrator's fear is carried by a pecuniary joke (cash that cannot buy safety). The flight of the neighboring unit is told as a dry observation about morale, and the contempt for "amateur" units is a documented attitude of the regular's account.

**[E26b-30]. The ordered patrol: a solitary stroll in No Man's Land, annoyance at Headquarters, and a pun on "reliefs."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §IV, L743)
A runner brings an order to patrol the whole company front; Sherston, with exhausted men and two novices, loses "my temper, quietly and inwardly" and goes himself, "more annoyed with Battalion Headquarters than with the enemy." He counts steps in the dark (there was no wire in front of a trench "constructed for people facing the other way"), loses confidence in his pistol, and finds the one reassuring action available is a private act on the ground which he treats as "my opinion of the War," with an aside that "there are other reliefs beside Battalion reliefs." He lays his pistol down to fix direction, finishes the patrol, and is "huskily challenged by an irresolute sentry, who, as I realized at the time, was the greatest danger I had encountered." Mechanism: a pun that sets a bodily act as a verdict on the war; a joke at the expense of his own side's sentry. (Restrained handling: a bodily-function joke, summarized; the wording of the act and the pun is not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* The narrator's resentment is aimed first at the headquarters that issues futile orders, and he carries it out in a way that remains his own; the nervous sentry as the real danger is an old and sourced soldiers' joke.

**[E26b-31]. The Tunnel, and the shave in the dregs of the tea.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §IV, L745)
He descends fifty steps into a tunnel whose "earthy smell" was "strongly suggestive of appearing in the Roll of Honor and being buried until the Day of Judgment," "that triumph of Teutonic military engineering." In a wire-netting bunk he envies his snoring companions, but three hours later is "gulping some peculiar tea with morose enjoyment" and shaves with "the dregs of my tea" because water is scarce. Mechanism: ironic praise of enemy engineering; the compact joke of a morning wash done in tea; "morose enjoyment" as the narrator's name for the stance.
*Candidate application:* A characteristic compressed contradiction ("morose enjoyment") is how a veteran narrator reports a small pleasure taken in the worst conditions.

**[E26b-32]. The fatigue party, the "impressive picture of 'Despair,'" and the thought "Who made the War?"** (PW-09, ch. VIII §IV, L747)
Carrying trench-mortar bombs for seven hours in rain, Sherston calls it "a typical infantry experience of discomfort without actual danger" and says "what we were doing was quite unexceptional." The party "would have made an impressive picture of 'Despair.'" Passing across the wrecked German outpost trench, he reports the dead lying in the postures in which they died, with different conditions of British and German bodies (restrained summary). One detail, a pair of hands protruding from the ground (nationality unknown), one pointing at the sky "with an accusing gesture," becomes for him, each time he passes, "the protest of those fingers" and "an appeal to God in defiance of those who made the War." He then thinks "Who made the War? I laughed hysterically as the thought passed through my mud-stained mind. But I only laughed mentally, for my box of Stokes-gun ammunition left me no breath to spare for an angry guffaw." And: "the dead were the dead; this was no time to be pitying them or asking silly questions about their outraged lives." (Restrained handling: dismembered and decomposing bodies, a human face detached from a skull in floodwater; summarized and not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* This is the nearest the book comes, before the hospital, to a dissenting thought: a question of guilt that provokes a laugh, which is suppressed by lack of breath and by a rule about not pitying the dead. The shape (a question, a hysterical laugh, a practical reason to stop) is a documented way of postponing dissent.

**[E26b-33]. Leake's breezy order: a hundred bombers and "underground attack along the Tunnel."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L751)
Back in the Tunnel, mud-plastered, Sherston is told by Leake, "breezily," that he is to command a hundred bombers next morning: "Twenty-five bombers from each Company; you're to act as reserve for the Cameronians." Leake adds, "Well, they're a bit hazy about it at Headquarters, but the General is frightfully keen on our doing an underground attack along the Tunnel, as well as along the main trench up above." The narrator already knows that the tunnel ends in a blown-in block with Germans on the other side and asks himself, "Had the War Office issued a textbook on the subject?" He lights his pipe but "failed to enjoy it, probably because the stewed tea had left such a queer taste in my mouth." Mechanism: the senior officer's offhand upper-class idiom ("a bit hazy," "frightfully keen") over a near-suicidal assignment; the narrator's mock-bureaucratic question; displacement of dread onto the taste of tea.
*Candidate application:* The tone of the order (cheerful understatement from the superior) matches the narrator's own habit; the subordinate's response is a question about a textbook and a complaint about tea.

**[E26b-34]. Bluffing with a Military Cross ribbon, and the beetle.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L753-755)
In a dugout with "friendly Scotch officers," Sherston, feeling incompetent in the face of their local detail, "puffed out my Military Cross ribbon (the dugout contained two others), nodded my head knowingly," and joined the discussion; the officers hope the underground attack will be dropped. He leaves "the desperation jollity of their little den" with only a "blurred notion" of the plan and admits: "I was, at the best of times, only an opportunist officer; technical talk in the Army always made me feel mutely inefficient." His main fear is "that I should make a fool of myself," and he now thinks the idea "a ludicrous one; for I see myself merely as a blundering flustered little beetle; and if someone happens to put his foot on a beetle, it is unjust to accuse the unlucky insect of having made a fool of itself." Mechanism: self-deprecation as a disarming reframe; the beetle image, which recurs in the next findings, removes agency and with it blame.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's anxiety is over ridicule, not death, and he relieves it with an image of himself as an insect with no dignity to lose. A character's humor may work by lowering his own stakes.

**[E26b-35]. Sleeping or dead: a kick in the Tunnel, "a bit too thick," and Wilmot's mock-authoritative document.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L757-759)
Going along the Tunnel by torch to find Headquarters, Sherston finds a figure under a blanket, kicks it and asks it for directions with an oath, then finds the man is a dead German (restrained summary: the face and the injury are not reproduced). He mutters that this "was really a bit too thick," and immediately corrects himself: "That, however, was an exaggeration; there is nothing remarkable about a dead body in a European War, or a squashed beetle in a cellar." The Adjutant, an accountant before the war ("most of his fighting was done in writing"), reports a "providential counter-order" canceling the underground attack. Wilmot, sitting calm, as though he "might conceivably have been twiddling a liqueur glass in a Piccadilly restaurant," puts on his monocle and writes a plausible-looking "Organization of F.F. Parties" in a few lines, adding "a little bit of skite about consolidation and defensive flanks." The narrator notes a stopped clock whose "dumb face" was "an idiot reminder of real rooms and desirable domesticity." Mechanism: understatement ("a bit too thick") undone by the narrator's own coda; paper authority produced by a veteran's style over thin content; the stopped clock as a plain image.
*Candidate application:* An action that shames the narrator (kicking the dead) is relieved by a deflating aphorism; Wilmot's skill is to fabricate the form an organization wants, which is how a veteran writes his way past nonsense.

**[E26b-36]. The chain of "hoping to satisfy": a satirical ladder from Brigadier to King and Country.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L761-763)
The two sit in silence about the attack: "The thing had to be attempted and there was an end of it (until zero hour)." The narrator then writes a chain: the Brigadier and Staff were hoping to satisfy General Whincop ("who'd got an unpopular bee in his bonnet about the rum ration, and had ordered an impossible raid, two months ago, which had been prevented by a providential thaw and caused numerous deaths in a subsequently sacrificed battalion"); Whincop, to satisfy the Corps Commander ("of whom we knew nothing at all, except that he had insulted our Colonel on the Doullens road"); the Corps Commander, the Army Commander, who "had as usual informed us that we were 'pursuing a beaten enemy'"; and he, the Commander-in-Chief, "with one eye on Marshal Foch," his "King and Country." A parenthesis: "the village which was now our Division's objective was still held by the Germans eight months afterwards." Wilmot and Sherston, he says, were "fully justified in leaving the situation to the care of the military caste who were making the most of their Great Opportunity for obtaining medal-ribbons and reputations for leadership," and if he is "caustic and captious" he pleads "the need for a few minutes' postwar retaliation. Let the Staff write their own books about the Great War say I." Mechanism: a ladder of deference as a satirical form; the narrator signals that his own hindsight is "retaliation." The corps commander of E26b-20 and the rum general of E26b-12 are recalled in this list. (Compare E15c-7 on the brigade's "straight lines" and the staff's salients in Graves.)
*Candidate application:* A usable model of soldier satire of the command chain: a ladder in which each level's goal is to please the next one up, ending in an abstraction.

**[E26b-37]. Homely talk as "a form of drug": the mental bicycle tour of the Weald.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L765-767)
Between ten and midnight the two talk only of peace: Wilmot knows Sherston's part of Kent and Sussex, so they make "a little tour of the Kentish Weald and the Sussex border, as though on a couple of mental bicycles," recalling a local squire addressing his golf-ball with a taciturn black retriever behind him, and the squire in his dogcart on his way to a county council meeting. Sherston writes: "Much has been asserted about the brutalized condition of mind to which soldiers were reduced by life in the front-line; I do not deny this, but I am inclined to suggest that there was a proportionate amount of simple-minded sentimentality. As far as I was concerned, no topic could be too homely for the trenches." The chatter, with working parties filing past the door, "challenged our surroundings with remembrances of parish names and farmhouses with friendly faces"; "A cottage garden was not an easy idea to recover convincingly." Mechanism: homely, whimsical reminiscence as the front's counterweight; the narrator calls the conversation "a form of drug." The humor here is gentle and local, not gallows.
*Candidate application:* An exact statement that sentimental English-countryside talk was as characteristic of the front as brutalization; the comic figures (a man hitting a golf-ball, a squire in a dogcart) are chosen for being absurd, ordinary and unreachable.

**[E26b-38]. The operation order and "my passport to the Stygian shore."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L769-771)
The book prints the order in full: "Secret. The Bombing Parties of 25 men will rendezvous at 2:30 a.m. tomorrow morning, 16th inst. in shafts near C Coy. H.Q.," with instructions on bomb dumps, silence and "a free passage way clear for runners," "under 2nd Lt. Sherston." The narrator's comment: "Such was the document which (had I been less fortunate) would have been my passport to the Stygian shore." His reflection in the foggy mirror, "with an effect of clairvoyance," he reads as "an omen of survival." Mechanism: a document in the army's flat register, followed by a literary-classical joke on its meaning; the mirror as a half-joking superstition.
*Candidate application:* Paper reduces an expected death to a rendezvous time and a clause about silence; the narrator's own phrase supplies the gap.

**[E26b-39]. Zero hour in the Cameronian dugout: "No harm in having a bit of cake, anyhow," the cascade of pots and pans, and an unspoken "I suppose you're for it now."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L773-777)
At zero Sherston sits among strangers in the Cameronians' headquarters, tidying his puttees and tie ("there was no rule against wearing a tie in an attack"). After twenty minutes the Colonel bursts out, "Good God, I wish I knew how they're doing!" and then, "as if regretting his manifestation of feeling," says "No harm in having a bit of cake, anyhow." A large homemade cake is on the table; Sherston eats a slice "with embarrassment." When, after about two and a half hours, a sergeant announces the attack has been driven back, he arrives down the stairs "in a clattering cascade of pots and pans" (headquarters stored its utensils on the steps). The narrator reads the Cameronian officers' "unuttered comment": "Well, old chap, I suppose you're for it now"; and "There was no question of another slice of homemade cake." Mechanism: a senior officer uses cake to cover a lapse in composure; slapstick noise breaks the suspense; the narrator voices the joke the officers do not say.
*Candidate application:* A Colonel's attempt at calm by offering cake is a model of the controlled-composure gesture; the unvoiced collective thought is the unit's humor being withheld out of tact.

**[E26b-40]. The bombing run: "But where are the Germans?" and the narrator's disliking of his own bravado.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L777-781)
Taking twenty-five bombers up the trench behind a guide, Sherston says he felt "quite confident" and (parenthetically) cannot believe from his "armchair" that he was there at all. He meets Captain Macnair and exhausted Cameronians moving the wrong way, who say the Germans are all around and they have no bombs. "Feeling myself to be, for the moment, an epitome of Flintshire infallibility," he assumes "an air of jaunty unconcern," tosses a bomb from hand to hand and asks, "But where are the Germans?"—adding "I can't see any of them." The Cameronian officers "recovered their composure." He writes that "(though for some reason I find it difficult to describe this scene without disliking my own behavior)" and calls the encounter "more absurd than impressive." With a silent Cameronian corporal he bombs along the trench without seeing more than "retreating field-gray figures"; he sees a wounded Scottish private and a German officer, which makes him "angry" (restrained summary); he peeps over a sap, is shot in the back by a sniper ("a stupendous blow"), and records that his "foolhardy attitude toward the Second Battle of the Scarpe had been instantaneously altered for the worse." Sergeant Baldock, "an admirably impassive little man," puts on a field-dressing. Mechanism: the narrator's performance of nonchalance as a rallying device, undercut by his later distaste; a deliberately tonal aside about "holes and ditches" at the moment of memory failure. (Restrained handling: the dead and wounded men seen in the trench, and the wound, are summarized, with no description of the injuries.)
*Candidate application:* The persona's reckless-comic style (jauntiness, a thrown bomb, a joking question) succeeds in the moment and is judged distastefully in retrospect; the narrator does not want the reader to take it as an ideal.

**[E26b-41]. The wounded hero's "superfluous sling," and the fizzling of ferocity.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L781)
After "a short spell of being deflated and sorry for myself," Sherston feels "rabidly heroical again, but in a slightly different style, since I was now a wounded hero, with my arm in a superfluous sling." He talks "volubly" to a laconic Stokes-gun officer and wants "one more onslaught before I turned my back on the War"; he is relieved by Dunning, whose "air of competent unconcern sobered me down," and stopped by the Cameronian Colonel's written order forbidding further advance. "My ferocity fizzled out then, and I realized that I had a raging thirst." The M.O. arrives "with the detached demeanor of a gentle botanist." Mechanism: comic deflation of the persona's pose through a sequence of small bathetic images (sling, thirst, botanist).
*Candidate application:* The narrator mocks his own heroics in sequence, with the next humbling image always ready; this is the mechanism that makes the later turn to dissent legible as a change in what he treats as comic.

**[E26b-42]. Leaving the line: "garrulous" farewell, "patriotic perforation," and "liberated eyes."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L785)
At Battalion Headquarters he is congratulated on his "supposedly dashing display" and takes leave "with voluble assurances that I should be back in a few weeks; but I was so overstrained and excited that my assurances were noises rather than notions." He says, "I would like to be able to remember that I smiled grimly and departed reticently. But the 'bombing show' had increased my self-importance, and my exodus from the front-line was a garrulous one." The Adjutant, whom he never sees again, "was killed in the autumn up at Ypres." The wound, "a neat hole near my right shoulder-blade" (his word), "this patriotic perforation had made a different man of me. I now looked at the War, which had been a monstrous tyrant, with liberated eyes. For the time being I had regained my right to call myself a private individual." Mechanism: the narrator mocks his own version of the stoic exit; a comic phrase ("patriotic perforation") turns the bullet hole into the pivot of his view of the war; the Adjutant's death is placed in an after-clause.
*Candidate application:* The narrator ties the first change of outlook to the physical exit from the line, not to an argument; his term "private individual" is the identity a soldier gets back when removed from the unit. Garrulousness after a wound is a documented reaction shown in the source.

**[E26b-43]. The Dressing Station, the padre, and the servant's handshake.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L787-795)
The walk to the Advanced Dressing Station crosses "a waste land where dead men had been left out in the rain after being killed for no apparent purpose." In the station "my egocentricity diminished among all that agony"; he listens to "an emotional padre who was painfully aware that he could do nothing except stand about and feel sympathetic" and observes "The consolations of the Church of England weren't much in demand at an Advance Dressing Station." He talks "nineteen to the dozen" until the Quartermaster puts him in a cart. Parting from his servant at a crossroads: "one couldn't wish a man luck when he was going back to the Hindenburg Trench," and he defends the intimacy of the handshake as between two humans, "one of whom was getting out of it comfortably while the other went back to take his chance in the world's worst war." At the casualty clearing station he "condoled with myself on having had no end of a bad time" while acknowledging "What I'd been through was nothing compared with the sort of thing that many soldiers endured over and over again." Mechanism: a dry line about the Church's irrelevance at the dressing station; the narrator makes fun of his own self-pity in a single clause.
*Candidate application:* The padre's helplessness is the narrator's observation, not a joke at his expense; the servant's handshake is a plain-register moment in which luck is named as the only distinction between the two men.

**[E26b-44]. The hospital train: facetious talk, "an aggregation of enormities," and "few among us would ever tell the truth."** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L797)
In the train carrying 500 men and 35 officers, "Although many of them talked lightly and even facetiously about it, there was an aggregation of enormities in the atmosphere of that train." Slightly wounded officers excitedly retell the bombing at Wancourt in "jargoning voices." The narrator's judgment: "We might be boastful or sagely reconstructive about our experience, in accordance with our different characters. But our minds were still out of breath and our inmost thoughts in disorderly retreat." Then: "We were the survivors; few among us would ever tell the truth to our friends and relations in England. We were carrying something in our heads which belonged to us alone, and to those we had left behind us in the battle." There are also dying men aboard, "men dying for their country in comparative comfort." Mechanism: a catalog of how survivors tell their stories (light, boastful, "sagely reconstructive") set against what they keep unsaid. This is the narrator's clearest statement on the gap between the frontline version and the civilian version.
*Candidate application:* Strong source for the civilian-facing register: facetiousness and boasting are two ways of managing the same unsayable. For the defector, the difference is that he now tells the truth to someone, or to no one.

**[E26b-45]. Charing Cross: flowers, and a leaflet from the Bishop of London.** (PW-09, ch. VIII §V, L799)
A diary entry written the day after reaching the base hospital says he is "still feeling warlike" and prepared to go back; he adds, of what follows: "But in spite of my self-defensive scribble I was in London on Friday evening, and by no means sorry to be carried through the crowd of patriotic spectators at Charing Cross Station." A woman hands him flowers and "a leaflet by the Bishop of London who earnestly advised me to lead a clean life and attend Holy Communion." Mechanism: the narrator marks his own diary as "self-defensive"; the civilian-welcome ritual ends on an inappropriate moral leaflet, without comment.
*Candidate application:* Documented civilian-facing absurdity: the returning wounded receive moralizing paper. The narrator's label for his own diary entry ("self-defensive scribble") shows he knows a diary can be a performance too.

### D. Hospital, the convalescent hotel, and Nutwood Manor, April-June 1917 (L801-919)

**[E26b-46]. "My privilege to be bitter": a ward routine and a catalog of imaginary visitors.** (PW-09, ch. IX §I, L807-821)
For about a week the narrator "could dream that for me the War was over, because I'd got a neat hole through me and the nurse with her spongings forbade me to have a bath"; then he "began to think," and decided "it was my privilege to be bitter," his attitude toward civilians implying "that they couldn't understand and that it was no earthly use trying to explain things to them." He then lists, in the style of a stage-direction table, how he would behave to four imaginary visitors. The senior officer: modest, "strongly imbued with the 'spirit of the Regiment' and quite ready to go out again," wanting to introduce him to "some of my people." The elderly civilian: is given a "haggard facial aspect of one who had 'been through hell'," wishes the wound "a bit worse than it actually was," has an "Inability to reveal anything crudely horrifying to civilian sensibilities," says "Oh yes, I'll be out there again by the autumn," with "Secret antagonism to all uncomplimentary references to the German Army." The charming sister of a brother officer: "Jocular, talkative, debonair, and diffidently heroic," wishful to wear "all possible medal-ribbons on pajama jacket," suppressing "all unpalatable facts." The hunting friend: "Deprecatory about sufferings," asks about last season's sport, "Jokes about the Germans, as if throwing bombs at them was a tolerable substitute for foxhunting," guffawing "(mitigated by remembrance that I'd got a bullet hole through my lung)." The narrator admits he can't keep these up; alone, he feels "self-pitying estrangement from everyone except the troops in the front-line." He adds that "(Casualties didn't count as tragic unless dead or badly maimed.)" He resents Aunt Evelyn's assumption that he has done enough, and knows he will go back: "A cushy wound wasn't enough to keep me out of it." Mechanism: a comic taxonomy of role-shifting performance for civilians, each visitor with a "script" that the narrator will play along with; the humor includes the narrator's own pose.
*Candidate application:* An explicit, source-attested demonstration that a soldier changes his register by audience (modest to the colonel, haggard to the elderly civilian, jocular to the sister, hearty to the hunter), and that he then hates the performance. For a defector, the version for each audience is a set of ready-made masks.

**[E26b-47]. The ward's talk and its two kinds of songs.** (PW-09, ch. IX §I, L823-827)
In a pleasant ward of forty beds, a "raucous gramophone" plays ragtime in the morning and "sentimental songs" in the evening; before midday no one talks "war shop," then fragments such as "Barrage lifted at the first objective," "shelled us with heavy stuff," "couldn't raise enough decent N.C.O.s" reach the narrator as he reads Lamb. The cardplayers include an airman held for three days by Prussians in a dugout who "hadn't treated him badly," and a Canadian whom the narrator "couldn't like," especially when he boasted of having killed prisoners. (Restrained handling: the boast of killing prisoners is summarized and its wording is not reproduced; the narrator's physical description of the Canadian, which is a facial caricature with an ethnic and class tinge, is not reproduced.) The narrator's response: "My brain was screwed up tight, and when people came to see me I answered their questions excitedly and said things I hadn't intended to say."
*Candidate application:* The ward as an echo chamber of fragments of jargon; the contrast of the airman's fair account of the enemy with the Canadian's boast is a documented small instance of how individual men's treatment of the enemy diverges.

**[E26b-48]. The ward at night: phantoms.** (PW-09, ch. IX §I, L829-831)
Bad dreams in the ward at night, with the noise of a munition factory across the road: a hallucination of mutilated men crawling over the floor, one handing him a letter. The narrator wakes to find the nurse making up the fire. Plain register, no humor. (Restrained handling: a graphic nightmare of maimed men and a fatal face wound; summarized, not reproduced.)
*Candidate application:* A reminder that the convalescent's comic daytime voice sits beside night terrors that the narrator reports without jokes, in a separate paragraph.

**[E26b-49]. "I began to think": criticism of the war, the vanity of a Bar to the M.C., and the young man who tells God He does not exist.** (PW-09, ch. IX §II, L835-837)
He became "definitely critical and inquiring about the War" without being "fully informed on the subject": "I generalized intuitively, and was not unlike a young man who suddenly loses his belief in religion and stands up to tell the Universal Being that He doesn't exist, adding that if He does, He treats the world very unjustly." He says his criticism was "queered" by the Adjutant's letter that his name had been "sent in for another decoration," and that his vanity pictured "a Bar" to the M.C. or "a red and blue D.S.O." On a bus, reading the "Unconservative Weekly" (the book's name for a weekly whose views run against the pro-war *Morning Post*), he agrees with every word and finds it "a sort of divine revelation," but would not be able to restate the arguments: "It's what I've always felt myself, though I couldn't exactly put it into words." Mechanism: an ironic self-portrait of the beginner dissenter whose positions are borrowed and whose ambition for a decoration survives alongside them. The narrator promises to "have more to say later on about my antagonism to the World War." This is the opening of the dissent arc in the book; the register is gently self-mocking.
*Candidate application:* The dissenter's early stage is shown as unsteady and vain; the narrator mocks his own convictions as borrowed and his medals-hunger as intact. This blunts any claim that the later stance rests on a single clean conversion.

**[E26b-50]. The Archbishop's Sunday fieldwork: private "Silly old fossil!"** (PW-09, ch. IX §II, L839)
"Smiling sardonically," Sherston reads the Archbishop of Canterbury's message that, given the emergency, "men and women may with a clear conscience do fieldwork on Sundays," and wonders "whether the Archbishop had given the sanction of the Gospel for that little bit of Sabbath fieldwork" (the bombardment on Easter Sunday at Arras). He glares toward Lambeth Palace and mutters "Silly old fossil!" The narrator adds that he did this heedless of whether the Archbishop was, "presumably, pained by the War and its barbarities." Mechanism: a sarcastic reply that treats the Archbishop's sanction of Sunday farm labor as the sanction of Sunday bombardment; the private mutter on a bus as the narrator's complete public act.
*Candidate application:* The first dissent in this stretch is a private mutter on a bus, aimed at the Church, with the narrator immediately conceding the target's decency. A pattern of safe, deniable, unattributed contempt.

**[E26b-51]. Luncheon with Durley: "old jokes and grotesquely amusing trench incidents were reenacted," and "his dangerous tendency to become serious."** (PW-09, ch. IX §II, L841-845)
With Durley, a friend from the battalion, Sherston "reverted automatically to my active service self": the war they discuss "was restricted to the doings of the Flintshire Fusiliers," a roll of who was wounded or killed ("poor old Somebody had been killed in the Bullecourt show") while "Old jokes and grotesquely amusing trench incidents were reenacted." The narrator's reading: "The Western Front was the same treacherous blundering tragicomedy which the mentality of the Army had agreed to regard as something between a crude bit of fun and an excuse for a good grumble," and the two were "remaining loyal to the realities of our war experience, keeping our separate psychological secrets to ourselves, and avoiding what Durley called 'his dangerous tendency to become serious.'" The waiter tells them the price of the hock has been "reduced since 1914," since "it was now an unpopular wine," and they laugh. Durley, whose voice is "a hoarse whisper," recounts a Flying Corps medical board where the doctor asked "angrily, 'Why the hell don't you stop that whispering?'" and says it would make little difference "whether you shouted or whispered when you were up in an airplane." The narrator "emerged from the restaurant without having uttered a single anti-war sentiment."
*Candidate application:* The best source in this half for humor as the language of two men who both want to keep from "becoming serious"; the code is explicit, and one of them names it as a danger. Jokes about the German wine's price and a whispering veteran are small and local. The narrator's silence on the war with the one friend who matters is the same silence as in E26b-7.

**[E26b-52]. Street names: Pledge, Money, Stone, Bernard Shaw, and Fudge, and the thought that truth-tellers are imprisoned.** (PW-09, ch. IX §II, L847)
Riding back, he amuses himself by reading shop names: "Pledge (pawnbroker), Money (solicitor), and Stone (builder)," an undertaker named "Bernard Shaw," and "perhaps the most significant name was Fudge (printing works)." The last sets off a thought: "What use, I thought, were printed words against a war like this? Durley represented the only reality which I could visualize with any conviction. People who told the truth were likely to be imprisoned, and lies were at a premium." He is tired and grateful for bed; "the War had taught me to be thankful for a roof over my head at night." Mechanism: wordplay on aptronyms (a distraction device) that turns on a single word ("Fudge") and brings the narrator to a dark political thought; the passage moves from comic to bitter in two sentences.
*Candidate application:* A direct bridge in the book from word-games to the thought that truth leads to prison; the humor is the narrator's way of reaching the thought without announcing it. Hindsight note: this thought precedes his own statement.

**[E26b-53]. News of the 23 April attack: the Doctor's published sentence, the "genial exculpatory Major-General," and Wilmot's lost piano hand.** (PW-09, ch. IX §II, L849-851)
Lying awake, Sherston recalls a letter (probably from Dunning) about the Second Battalion's attack of 23 April: ground gained and lost; four officers killed and nine wounded; about forty other ranks killed. "Such stories look straightforward enough in print, twelve years later; but their reality remains hidden; even in the minds of old soldiers the harsh horror mellows and recedes." He quotes the battalion doctor's later written verdict: "The occasion was but one of many when a Company or Battalion was sacrificed on a limited objective to a plan of attack ordered by Division or some higher Command with no more knowledge of the ground than might be got from a map of moderate scale." His own picture: an exhausted battalion congratulated by "a genial exculpatory Major-General, who explained that the attack had been ordered by the Corps Commander" (he adds the General was "blaming it on the Corps Commander"). Because he "knew for certain that Ralph Wilmot was now minus one of his arms," his "anti-war bitterness was enabled to concentrate itself on the fact that he wouldn't be able to play the piano again." Closing aphorism: "Altruism is an episodic and debatable quality; the instinct for self-preservation always got the last word when an infantryman was lying awake with his thoughts." Mechanism: a plain, quasi-official verdict from the unit's doctor set against the General's deflecting comfort; the narrator's grief selecting one concrete loss (the piano) as the object of anger.
*Candidate application:* The anger of a veteran goes to a specific, humane loss (a friend's music), not to a principle; the unit doctor's wry written judgment is a recurring authority in this book. Time course noted by the narrator: horror mellows even in old soldiers.

**[E26b-54]. The convalescent hotel: amputees and a blinded officer at a lecture on trench warfare, and the "sadistic" doctor.** (PW-09, ch. IX §II, L855-861)
At a London terminus hotel run by "a mind more military than therapeutic," convalescents must attend lectures including one on Trench Warfare; Sherston sees "several officers on crutches, with legs amputated," and at least one blind man; "They appeared to be accepting the absurd situation stoically; they were allowed to smoke." The Staff Officer's diagrams concern an obsolete engagement and end by urging "offensive ascendancy in No Man's Land," while some faces show "tortured nerves." The next day a "queer customer" of a doctor, whom a roommate calls a blighter who "seems to take a positive pleasure in tormenting people," refuses Sherston's country convalescence with a cruel face ("You'll stay here; and when you leave here, you'll find yourself back at the front in double-quick time. How d'you like that idea?"). Sherston, forewarned and armed with a letter from a Countess, "pretended to be upset by his severity" to give the man his satisfaction, and leaves; he hears officers "had been known to leave this doctor's room in tears." He says he does not regard this as "Army brutality," only a man who "craved for power over his fellow men," and ends: "I hope that someone gave him a black eye, and that he afterwards satisfied his desire for power over his fellow men in a more public-spirited manner." Mechanism: absurd official routine (blind men taught trench warfare) told with dry stoic details; the narrator's connections (a letter from a Countess) let him play along without cost. The next morning a "slightly higher authority" has him out in three nights.
*Candidate application:* Documented institutional absurdity and its rank-and-influence escape hatch: a man with social connections can sidestep the sadistic gatekeeper, and the narrator knows it. His joking wish is that the doctor find "a more public-spirited" outlet for his appetite.

**[E26b-55]. Nutwood Manor: an Earl's house, a Bible opened at random, and a 3 a.m. choice between the trenches and breakfast.** (PW-09, ch. IX §III, L863-873)
Sherston is sent to stay with Lord and Lady Asterisk, at a house "everything that a wounded officer could wish for" and "the first time I'd ever stayed with an Earl" ("Gosh! This is a slice of luck," he thinks). In his room he opens the Bible at random "to try my luck" and lands on the verse "The words of his mouth were smoother than butter, but war was in his heart," and remarks it was "an odd coincidence" but "the Old Testament's full of fighting." At three in the morning he cannot sleep; he thinks of friends in France, tries (the hostess being a spiritualist) to see whether concentrating on Ormand will bring "some reciprocal communication," and receives none. He recovers "an intense memory of men whose courage had shown me the power of the human spirit." He admits he wanted to be back for "a few hours" at "Arras and the Somme" "because the trenches really were more interesting than Lady Asterisk's rose-garden," and then deflates it: "But I also wanted to be back at Nutwood Manor for breakfast." Mechanism: bibliomancy as a mild joke; the 3 a.m. telepathy attempt; the bathetic breakfast at the end of a reverie of exciting exploits.
*Candidate application:* The comfortable-hospital stretch produces homesickness for the front, stated plainly and then undercut by an admission of appetite. A narrator who can mock his own wish for war is a precondition to the later turn against it.

**[E26b-56]. Lady Asterisk, the newspapers, and "what a company of men smelled like."** (PW-09, ch. IX §III, L877-887)
The hostess tells him "We want our soldier-guests to forget the War while they are with us." He resolves not to be "bluffed back into an easygoing tolerant state of mind" and deliberately reads press accounts. A dispatch from "War Correspondents' Headquarters" (summarized: a "mere boy" officer recounts killing enemy soldiers in a tone of sporting glee; the writer is "some amiable man who had apparently mistaken the War for a football match between England and Germany"). An illustrated weekly's review of "War Pictures at the Royal Academy" says of a battle picture, "the tips of their bayonets give one this impression—and it is a picture which makes one's pulse beat a lot faster." Sherston judges: "the woman journalist who wrote those words was deriving enjoyment from the War, though she may not have been aware of the fact," and asks "What was this camouflage War which was manufactured by the press to aid the imaginations of people who had never seen the real thing?" He imagines civilians who say "the lads from the front are inclined to be a little morbid" and add "something about 'the healing of Nations.'" Such people "needed to have their noses rubbed in a few rank physical facts" (he names the smell of a company after a week in action). (Restrained handling: the press anecdote of killing enemy soldiers is summarized; the term the dispatch used for the enemy is not reproduced.) Mechanism: reading aloud (to himself) as mockery; a quotation of a single sentence as the whole indictment.
*Candidate application:* A documented civilian-facing register: the press version converts a charge of bayonets into a source of pleasure for a reader. The returned officer's remedy is physical fact, not argument.

**[E26b-57]. "But death is nothing": Lady Asterisk's answer to a wounded officer asking whether to go back, and the Germans "up there."** (PW-09, ch. IX §III, L891-897)
Sherston enjoys "obliviously contented" days in the countryside, able to "discard my personal relationship with the military machine and its ant-like armies." One evening in the library, Lady Asterisk invites him to talk about his future, and he speaks "with self-indulgence in making a fine effect," putting his wish to live against sacrifice, noting that "most of my friends were assuring me that there was no necessity for me to go out for the third time"; he sees himself "as a noble suffering character whose death in action would be deeply deplored." He says nothing against the war: "it would have been bad form to be bitter about it at Nutwood Manor." She answers, putting aside her reticence "like a rich cloak": "But death is nothing... Life, after all, is only the beginning. And those who are killed in the War—they help us from up there. They are helping us to win." And, "with a sort of inflexible sympathy": "It isn't as though you were heir to a great name. No; I can't see any definite reason for your keeping out of danger. But, of course, you can only decide a thing like that for yourself." The narrator "couldn't answer that," goes upstairs "feeling caddishly estranged and cynical; wondering whether the Germans 'up there' were doing anything definite to impede the offensive operations of the Allied Powers." He decides "I began to wish that I could talk candidly to someone. There was too much well-behaved acquiescence at Nutwood Manor," where the officers "kept it to themselves... as if the eye of their Colonel was upon them." Mechanism: a patrician believer's comfort delivered as an order; the narrator's private joke imagines the enemy dead as combatants in the afterlife (a sardonic extension of her premise).
*Candidate application:* A source-attested example of the "ministering" patron class that asks soldiers to risk death with conviction, and of the soldier-narrator's silent gallows retort (the afterlife as a continuation of the war). The absence of anyone to talk to "candidly" is the stated condition immediately before the protest chapters.

**[E26b-58]. A fashionable guest and Lord Asterisk's after-dinner oration: "I'm only an old dotard."** (PW-09, ch. IX §III, L901-905)
A new guest, a young woman with pearl earrings, whose husband is campaigning in the Cameroons, tells him she is "dying to read Dante," exclaims "Life is so wonderful—so great—and yet we waste it all in this dreadful War!" and repeats gossip from Colonel Repington at the Ritz that Allenby's slow advance at Arras need not worry anyone; he concludes she is not "the stamp of woman for whom I was willing to make the supreme sacrifice." After dinner, Lord Asterisk (a former Colonial Governor, over eighty, crippled with rheumatism) tells the officers it is useless to wait for a military decision, that some colonies might as well be handed to Germany ("have you any predominating awareness" (pause) "of—Sierra Leone?"), and that Belgium's "redemption" was "patriotic obliquity" and its people would be as happy under German rule. He ends the discussion with "But I'm only an old dotard!" and the officers "pretended to laugh naturally, as if it were quite a good joke." Then, on bishops: "Oh what worlds of dreary (mumble) are hidden by the hats of our episcopal dignitaries!" Sherston concludes he was "a dear old chap who had done his level best to leave the world in better order than he'd found it." Mechanism: the old man's mumbling grandeur is mimicked with parenthetical stage directions; the young officers' enforced polite laughter covers a statement no one can answer. Later (E26b-62) the narrator recalls this skepticism when his own views are forming.
*Candidate application:* An establishment elder voices war-weariness and the junior officers manage it with a pretend laugh; the dissent here is aristocratic, mumbling and ignorable, in contrast to the protest of a soldier.

**[E26b-59]. Letters from the front: the Quartermaster's idiom, the discharged officer's black looks, and the servant's thank-you note.** (PW-09, ch. IX §III, L909-913)
Neighbors at tea "conceded full military honors to any officer who had been wounded" and talk of gardening, while the narrator reads a Quartermaster's letter: the battalion "has been hard at it again... as usual kept their end up well—much to the joy of the Staff, who have been round here today like flies round a jam-pot, congratulating the Colonel and all others concerned"; the padre was killed (the Quartermaster ("I haven't much room for his crowd as a rule") calls him "the finest parson I've ever known, absolutely indifferent to danger"); a bombing officer who had known Sherston at Clitherland died of wounds; two other officers killed; "All the best get knocked over.... The boys are now trying to get to Amiens to do a bit of courting." (Restrained handling: how the padre and the bombing officer were wounded and treated is summarized.) The narrator asks, "What earthly use was it, ordering boxes of kippers to be sent to people who were all getting done in, while everyone at home humbugged about with polite platitudes?" Birdie Mansfield, invalided out, writes that he is "fed to the teeth with wandering around in mufti and getting black looks from people who pass remarks to the effect that it's about time I joined up," on "3s. a day" pension, and that the talk in the munition areas is "the high cost of beer and the ability to evade military service by bluffing the Tribunals." His servant thanks him for the photo ("like life itself") and gramophone and apologizes for not packing the groundsheet. The narrator: "Nothing short of stopping the War could alter the inadequacy of kippers and gramophones or sustain my sense of unity with those to whom I sent them." Mechanism: the Quartermaster's folksy similes (staff "like flies round a jam-pot") and hard news in one breath; the servant's courtesy; the narrator's own phrase "stopping the War" is the first plain statement of that wish in this half. The "black looks" for a discharged officer in mufti is an example of the civilian-facing cost of leaving.
*Candidate application:* Strong source for "humor of the unit that stayed behind": idiom as a way of sending deaths in the same letter as courting plans. Also a documented cost for an ex-soldier out of uniform (stares and remarks).

**[E26b-60]. Dottrell's bad news, Dunning's "Cheerio," and "they are safe and happy now."** (PW-09, ch. IX §III, L917-919)
The last Nutwood letter from Dottrell reports that the Second Battalion attack was "quite a commonplace fragment of the War" ("Viewed broadmindedly," says the narrator), "a hopeless failure," with all but one officer a casualty and none of the bodies brought in. Ormand "looked pretty depressed, though outwardly as jolly as ever." Dunning "had been the first to leave our trench; had shouted 'Cheerio' and been killed at once." Dottrell adds thanks for a box of kippers. Told to Lady Asterisk, whose "face became self-defensively serene," she says "But they are safe and happy now." The narrator "did not doubt her sincerity" but "was incapable of accepting the deaths of Ormand and Dunning and the others in that spirit. I wasn't a theosophist." Mechanism: the narrator's "Viewed broadmindedly" is the dry opener of a casualty list; the Quartermaster's thank-you for kippers follows the deaths in the same paragraph.
*Candidate application:* The comfort of a believer is answered, in the narrator's mind, with a flat denial not spoken aloud. The friend's "Cheerio" at the instant of death is told without comment.

### E. Chapter X, "Independent Action": the decision to dissent and its outcome (L921-1096)

**[E26b-61]. Messines day and the editor's lunch: a mock clubland voice, a letter with a rude postscript.** (PW-09, ch. X §I, L927-931)
On 7 June (the day of the Messines mines, which the narrator reports with a flat "a great many Germans had been blown sky-high") Sherston lunches with Markington, editor of the "Unconservative Weekly." The narrator's own paraphrase of clubland talk about Kerensky gets a mock-pompous parenthetical ("Waiter, bring me a large glass of light port") and a hesitation, "humph," before "stick to its guns." Markington had been written to from Nutwood suggesting he publish "something outspoken" so people at home "know what the War was really like"; the letter, otherwise stilted, ended with a postscript: "I'm fed-up with all the hanky-panky in the daily papers." Markington is "a sallow spectacled man" with "earnest uncompromising eyes and a stretched sort of mouth which looked as if it had ceased to find human follies funny," yet "relaxing into ordinary humanity, he could enjoy broad humor," and asks for army anecdotes "which might be censored." Sherston "felt quite fond of Markington when he threw himself back in his chair in a paroxysm of amusement." Mechanism: the dissenting editor laughs at the soldier's stories; the narrator's note on the paper's own limits: Markington cannot print truthful infantry accounts because the paper "would be suppressed as prejudicial to recruiting."
*Candidate application:* A dissent-side humor channel: an embittered civilian opponent of the war who is nonetheless eager for a soldier's censored funny stories; the soldier's anecdotes are the entry ticket to the circle. The humor is exchange, not argument.

**[E26b-62]. Markington on patriotism and G.H.Q. visitors; Sherston's "single specimen" of eloquence.** (PW-09, ch. X §I, L931-939)
Markington: "The soldiers are not allowed to express their point of view. In wartime the word 'patriotism' means suppression of truth," and of club members who have visited G.H.Q.: "But their principles are purchasable. Once they've been invited to visit G.H.Q. they never look back.... And any lie is a good lie as long as it stimulates unreasoning hatred of the enemy." The narrator describes Markington "eyeing a small chunk of Stilton cheese on his plate as if it were incapable of agreeing with any but ultra-Conservative opinions." Sherston tells him the Second Battalion "had been almost wiped out ten days ago because the Divisional General had ordered an impossible attack on a local objective," and notes: "The phrase 'local objective' sounded good, and made me feel that I knew a hell of a lot about it." At the Cobden bust he "gazed fixedly at his marble whiskers, nodded gravely, and inwardly resolved to look up a few facts about him." He then quotes "a single specimen of my eloquence": the war "doesn't seem nearly such a bloody rotten show when one's out there as it does when one's back in England," "like being part of the Machine again," "I must say I've often felt extraordinarily happy even in the trenches... It's only when one gets away from it that one begins to realize how stupid and wasteful it all is. What I feel now is that if it's got to go on there ought to be a jolly sound reason for it, and I can't help thinking that the troops are being done in the eye by the people in control." He calls these "temperate remarks" and qualifies them. On hearing that the British refused the new Russian government's request to publish war aims and secret treaties: "How damned rotten of us!" with "a savage desire to hit (was it Mr. Lloyd George?) very hard on the nose." Markington says "if once the common soldier became articulate the War couldn't last a month." The narrator, leaving, is clamorous with ideas, wants to stop strangers in the street, and tells how the Mesopotamian oil-wells gave him the thought "A jolly fine swindle it would have been for me, if I'd been killed in April for an Oil Well!" but "I soon forgot that I'd been unaware of the existence of the Oil Wells before Markington mentioned them, and I conveniently assimilated them as part of my evidential repertoire." The narrator also recalls that old Lord Asterisk had been "skeptical" in the same vein about Belgium (E26b-58), which he now takes as support. Mechanism: the narrator's self-satire of an amateur convert (a bluff, a borrowed phrase, a borrowed grievance), alongside the editor's actual indictment, which the narrator does not mock.
*Candidate application:* Source-attested detail on the dissenter's early voice: tentative, colloquial, public-school (the "specimen"), and aware of its own weakness; its comic self-portrait is the narrator's way of disarming ridicule in advance.

**[E26b-63]. The decision: "the head was my own," "the call," and "the shortest road to martyrdom."** (PW-09, ch. X §I, L945-947)
After a fortnight in London his "mental inquietude achieved some sort of climax": "my aggregated exasperations came to a head; and, naturally enough, the head was my own." He went to Markington "Ostensibly... to ask his advice; in reality, to release the indignant emotions," and says, "It was a case of direct inspiration; I had, so to speak, received the call, and the editor of the Unconservative Weekly seemed the most likely man to put me on the shortest road to martyrdom." His "internal eloquence dried up" when he faced the editor. In the office: "I say, I've been thinking it all over, and I've made up my mind that I ought to do something about it.... I can't just sit still and do nothing.... I don't see why I shouldn't make some sort of statement—about how we ought to publish our War Aims, and all that, and the troops not knowing what they're fighting about." Markington goes to the window; Sherston watches a barge and traffic on Charing Cross Bridge, "My heart was beating violently. I knew that I couldn't turn back now." The editor: "I suppose you've realized what the results of such an action would be, as regards yourself?" Sherston says he did not "care two damns what they did to me as long as I got the thing off my chest." Markington, "with a gleam of his essential kindness": "As far as I am aware, you'd be the first soldier to take such a step, which would, of course, be welcomed by the extreme pacifists. Your service at the front would differentiate you from the Conscientious Objectors.... Such an action would require to be carefully thought out." The narrator: "His words caused me an uncomfortable feeling that perhaps I was only making a fool of myself; but this was soon mitigated by a glowing sense of martyrdom. I saw myself 'attired with sudden brightness, like a man inspired.'" Markington sends him to a Thornton Tyrrell, "the most uncompromising character I know." Sherston, looking up Tyrrell in *Who's Who*, reflects that his mathematics never advanced beyond "'six from four you can't, six from fourteen leaves eight'" and he knew "no more about the functions of a physicist than a cat in a kitchen." Leaving, he salutes a Major-General "rather grudgingly" and wonders "what the War Office would say if it knew what I was up to." Mechanism: every serious beat is accompanied by a deflating comparison (the cat in a kitchen, the arithmetic, the glowing martyrdom as a literary quotation); the narrator marks the exact moment he cannot "turn back." The text frames the decision as emotional and impulsive, not the result of an argument. Genre note: the persona narrates the decision, and the text presents it as his own inflated and impulsive mood. (Cross-use: for a defector, the first statement of intent is made to an outsider with no authority over the soldier.)
*Candidate application:* The decision to dissent is rendered as a spontaneous act and a mood followed immediately by self-mockery and by anxious second-guessing; the first reaction of the listener is concern for the soldier ("you'd be the first soldier to take such a step") and the distinction from conscientious objectors, who are the category the Major in E26b-7 had beaten. The narrator's earlier admiration of objectors (E26b-7) and this separation both appear in the book.

**[E26b-64]. Tyrrell: "You will be more alive in prison," and the laugh that was mistaken.** (PW-09, ch. X §II, L951)
In a Bloomsbury room Sherston finds a small, clean-shaven philosopher with "a powerful ironic mouth" reading Kropotkin's *The Conquest of Bread* (Sherston "wondered what on earth it could be about"). Tyrrell lights a pipe: "Well, I gather from Markington's letter that you've been experiencing a change of heart about the War." He cuts off the narrator's borrowed phrasing ("Never mind about what Markington told you. It amounts to this, doesn't it—that you have ceased to believe what you are told about the objects for which you supposed yourself to be fighting?"). Sherston agrees, saying it is "a bloody shame, the troops getting killed all the time while people at home humbugged themselves into believing that everyone in the trenches enjoyed it." Tyrrell proposes that he write a short statement on his conviction that "the War was being unnecessarily prolonged by the refusal of the Allies to publish their war aims," and says: "I have always regarded all wars as acts of criminal folly, and my hatred of this one has often made life seem almost unendurable. But hatred makes one vital, and without it one loses energy. 'Keep vital' is a more important axiom than 'love your neighbor.' This act of yours, if you stick to it, will probably land you in prison. Don't let that discourage you. You will be more alive in prison than you would be in the trenches." The narrator: "Mistaking this last remark for a joke, I laughed, rather half-heartedly. 'No; I mean that seriously,' he said." Tyrrell adds that by "thinking independently and acting fearlessly on your moral convictions you are serving the world better than you would do by marching with the unthinking majority," and that his circle "should print and circulate as many copies of your statement as possible," then retracts the push: "But I hadn't intended to speak as definitely as this. You must decide by your own feeling and not by what anyone else says." The narrator takes "a strong liking" to him and imagines him smiling "rather grimly" before bed. Mechanism: the mentor's epigram sounds like a joke and is not; the narrator's half-laugh is the exact point where gallows-style wit meets plain counsel. (Note: the book never names a real person behind Tyrrell or Markington, and I do not identify them; compare E15c-13 and E15c-14 for Graves's accounts of actual pacifist circles in Oxford.)
*Candidate application:* The dissenter's first adviser makes a paradoxical jest (prison as aliveness) that the soldier takes for a joke; the line between a witticism and a creed is crossed by the speaker saying "I mean that seriously." The adviser also refuses to own the decision.

**[E26b-65]. Writing the statement: "I say this War ought to stop," the Vickers gun comparison, and a draft that breaks off at "arrogance and incompetence of."** (PW-09, ch. X §II, L955-957)
Though Tyrrell said the statement need not exceed 200 words, it takes several days: "after several verbose failures it seemed as though the essence of my manifesto could be stated in a single sentence: 'I say this War ought to stop.'" He calls his state "a species of conversion which made the prospect of persecution stimulating and almost enjoyable," and his loss of confidence "in the same category as my diffidence when first confronted by a Vickers Machine-Gun and its Instructor." In a Jermyn Street hotel room at night he thinks, "I'll never be able to write out a decent statement and the whole blasted protest will be a washout! Tyrrell thinks I'm quite brainy, but when he reads this stuff he'll realize what a dud I am," and worries about "my candidature for a court martial." He considers going out again to "get killed just to show them all I don't care a damn," with the aside "(I didn't stop to specify the identity of 'them all'; such details could be dispensed with when one had lost one's temper with the Great War.)" and talks himself out of it: "getting killed on purpose an irrelevant gesture for a platoon commander." He tries to "think internationally" (the Germans "must be hating it just as much as we did"; he "couldn't propel my sympathy" as far as "the Balkan States, Turks, Italians, and all the rest of them," and the French were "too busy fighting and selling things to the troops to need my intervention"; these national generalizations are the narrator's period voice, documented, not endorsed) and returns to the dead of his battalions. He remembers Ormand's reply to a hypothetical question from his children: "No bullet ever went quick enough to catch me," and how Ormand sang "Rock of ages cleft for me, let me hide myself in thee" when they were shelled. He sets "the typical Flintshire Fusilier at his best, and the vast anonymity of courage and cheerfulness" against a profiteer overheard on a train saying that if the war lasted eighteen more months he could retire. His notes: "Fighting men are victims of conspiracy among (a) politicians; (b) military caste; (c) people who are making money out of the War," and "I believe that by taking this action I am helping to destroy the system of deception, etc.... through the arrogance and incompetence of" (the sentence breaks off); "Here it broke off, and I wondered how many c's there were in 'unnecessarily.'" He tries "I am not a conscientious objector. I am a soldier who believes he is acting on behalf of soldiers." and judges: "How inflated and unconvincing it all looked! If I wasn't careful I should be yelling like some crank on a barrel in Hyde Park." Tyrrell has written him to remember he must decide for himself. (Restrained handling: none beyond the ethnic generalizations noted.) Mechanism: the humor is entirely at the narrator's own expense (spelling, inflation, the Vickers comparison), while the friend's hymn and the dead friend's quip supply what the "statement" is for. The unit's gallows idiom is cited as a reason, not as a joke.
*Candidate application:* An unusually detailed picture of the process of composing a dissent: self-doubt in comic terms, a retrieval of the unit's own jokes as a motive, and a one-line statement of position that borrows a distinction from the Conscientious Objectors. The soldier's gallows humor appears as evidence of what is being defended.

**[E26b-66]. The draft's opening sentence, and the thought of a bomb falling on the author.** (PW-09, ch. X §II, L959)
Staring at the wallpaper "in an unseeing effort at mental concentration," Sherston produces the sentence that will open the statement: "I am making this statement as an act of willful defiance of military authority because I believe that the War is being deliberately prolonged by those who have the power to end it." "That would be all right as a kickoff, anyhow," he says, "So I continued my superhuman cogitations." Looking at the searchlights, he thinks "how ridiculous it would be if a bomb dropped on me while I was writing out my statement." Mechanism: the key sentence of the dissent is introduced with a throwaway sporting phrase ("kickoff") and "superhuman" mock-grandeur; the air-raid thought is a dry gallows turn on his own situation.
*Candidate application:* The formal text of the dissent is given plainly and in full-sentence legal register, and the narrator's comment on it is jocular; both registers coexist.

**[E26b-67]. The statement is finished; the Cambridge pretext, the Commanding Officer's praise, and the anti-war circle's dislike of uniforms.** (PW-09, ch. X §III, L963)
A week after their first talk Tyrrell approves the statement and helps with "a few minor crudities of expression." The plan is to wait until his leave ends, "then hurl the explosive document at the Commanding Officer at Clitherland (an event which I didn't permit myself to contemplate clearly)." Meanwhile the Commanding Officer, who knows only that Sherston has applied for a Cambridge cadet instructorship, "wrote that he would be sorry to lose me and congratulated me on what he was generous enough to describe as my splendid work at the front." Tyrrell introduces him to colleagues of the "Stop the War Committee" and the "No Conscription Fellowship," including "an intellectual conscientious objector (lately released after a successful hunger-strike)" and "a genial veteran Socialist (recognizable by his red tie and soft gray hat)." They welcome him, "but I couldn't quite believe that I had been assimilated," because of "their antipathy to everyone in a uniform. I was still wearing mine, and somehow I was unable to dislike being a Flintshire Fusilier." He adds: "I am obliged to admit that if these anti-war enthusiasts hadn't happened to be likeable I might have secretly despised them. Any man who had been on active service had an unfair advantage over those who hadn't. And the man who had really endured the War at its worst was everlastingly differentiated from everyone except his fellow soldiers." Mechanism: the narrator mocks the circle's costume (red tie, soft hat) with affection and records that the dissenter's loyalty to his regiment survives the dissent.
*Candidate application:* A documented identity split: a soldier who turns against the war still belongs to the soldiers, is a stranger to the pacifists, and holds an "unfair advantage" the pacifists give no credit for. The humor is affectionate at their expense and defensive at his.

**[E26b-68]. Hampstead, the Savoy Grill, and "By Jingo, I suppose you're right!"** (PW-09, ch. X §III, L965)
Tyrrell takes him to a Member of Parliament "interested in my case," who promises to raise the statement in the House once it has been sent to the Colonel. Walking with Tyrrell, Sherston feels "a pair of conspirators"; the philosopher's help "by his sense of humor, which he had contrived, rather grimly, to retain, in spite of the exasperating spectacle of European civilization trying to commit suicide." Between meetings he lives in "a world of my own" and tries "to keep my courage up to protest-pitch," visiting the Savoy Grill "to reassure myself of the existence of bloated profiteers," "nourished my righteous hatred of them" while "conveniently overlooking the fact that some of them were officers on leave." He admits "comfort-loving people are obliged to avoid self-knowledge—especially when there is a war on." Tyrrell says people who resolve the war's discords into "a higher harmony" are "merely enabling themselves to contemplate the massacre of the young men with an easy conscience." Sherston: "By Jingo, I suppose you're right!" (a jingoist's exclamation turned to agree with a pacifist), "wishing that I were able to express my ideas with such comprehensive clarity." Mechanism: a conspirator's feeling as self-description; the narrator's own hate-feeding is exposed in a parenthesis about officers on leave.
*Candidate application:* Humor that survives the protest: the mentor's grim humor is credited as a coping device; the narrator's self-righteous scouting of civilian excess is mocked by the narrator himself with a parenthesis.

**[E26b-69]. "What people say doesn't matter," and "Whincop's Bread Cards."** (PW-09, ch. X §III, L969-973)
Sherston plans to accept the Cambridge cadet post as insurance for what he called "strengthening my position," a safeguard against "what people would say." When he tells Tyrrell that "people couldn't say I did it so as to avoid going back to France if I had been given a job in England," Tyrrell, in the narrator's words, "pulled me up short": "What people say doesn't matter. Your own belief in what you are doing is the only thing that counts." He feels "abashed" but "couldn't help regretting that my second decoration had failed to materialize." All he has for the April action is "a gilt-edged card on which the Divisional General had inscribed his congratulations and thanks," "locally referred to as 'one of the Whincop's Bread Cards.'" He "did my best to feel that it was better than nothing." Mechanism: the soldiers' name for a general's printed thank-you (a "bread card," that is, a rationing token) as a satirical equivalence between congratulations and rationed food; the narrator's own regret about the missing medal sits against a statement that a medal is "a somewhat inadequate accretion to one's qualifications for affirming that the War was being deliberately prolonged."
*Candidate application:* A named instance of unit slang for an institutional gesture of thanks; "bread card" shows the troops pricing the General's praise. The narrator's vanity about decorations and his dissent are shown co-existing without resolution.

**[E26b-70]. The Old Broad Street air raid: the stoical cashier, "a bit steep," and a different kind of courage.** (PW-09, ch. X §III, L975-979)
On the way to Cambridge he stops to change a check ("Queer thing, having private means... They just hand you out the money as if it was a present from the Bank Manager"; "It was funny, too, to think that I was still drawing my Army pay"). An air raid is under way; women clerks run down a stairway while the cashier hands him five one-pound notes "with the stoical politeness of a man who had made up his mind to go down with the ship." Sherston leaves "with an air of soldierly unconcern" and tells the taxi-driver "I suppose we may as well go on to the station... it seems a bit steep that one can't even cash a check in comfort!" and "The man grinned and drove on." At Liverpool Street a bomb has hit the front carriage of the Cambridge train; the station clock reads 11:50 and is "a passive spectator"; a trolley carries an elderly man who is apparently dead, and wounded women groan on the platform. (Restrained handling: civilian casualties after an air raid, summarized; "The sight of blood caused me to feel quite queer" is the narrator's phrase.) He reflects: "This sort of danger seemed to demand a quality of courage dissimilar to front-line fortitude. In a trench one was acclimatized to the notion of being exterminated and there was a sense of organized retaliation. But here one was helpless." On the slow train he fantasizes: "It wouldn't be such a bad life... if one were a stationmaster on a branch line in Bedfordshire," or living in Trollope's Barsetshire. Mechanism: the front-line manner (soldierly unconcern, an understated complaint) transplanted into a civilian crisis and answered by a grin; the narrator's own commentary on how civilian helplessness differs.
*Candidate application:* A short, sourced account of the soldier's coping style (deflating complaint) meeting an air raid, and the stated difference between military and civilian terror: no "organized retaliation." Escape fantasies (stationmaster, commercial traveler) are comic and genuine.

**[E26b-71]. The Cambridge interview: a pretense so convincing the statement became "an improbability," and "a poor sort of joke on him."** (PW-09, ch. X §III, L981)
At Cambridge, in a college library converted to an orderly room, the Colonel (a History don) asks "Have you any specialist knowledge?" Sherston says he was Battalion Intelligence Officer for a time (suppressing that he "voluntarily relinquished that status after three days of inability to supply the necessary eyewash reports"). The Colonel hopes for "first-rate morale" and "the tradition of the prewar regimental officer," shakes his hand "rather as if I'd won a History Scholarship," and Sherston walks out "feeling that it was a poor sort of joke on him." While playing "the part of respectful young applicant" he did it "so convincingly that the existence of my 'statement' became, for the moment, an improbability." In King's Chapel he tries to recover "my conviction of the nobility of my enterprise" and recalls that Cambridge had dismissed Tyrrell from his lectureship because he disbelieved in the war: "'Intolerant old blighters!'... All they care about is keeping up with the other colleges in the casualty lists." Mechanism: a role-play with a trusting senior officer; the narrator's private moral accounting ("a poor sort of joke on him"); "eyewash reports" as slang for fabricated paperwork.
*Candidate application:* How an officer about to dissent deals with a courteous institution: he plays his part so well that he nearly forgets his plan; the humor is entirely private, and the target is shifted at the end from himself to the Cambridge elders.

**[E26b-72]. The double life at Butley: empty beehives "like bones," and underlining the sentences that did not need it.** (PW-09, ch. X §IV, L985)
Back home for a fortnight with Aunt Evelyn, he is "compelled to lead a double life, and the longer it lasted the less I liked it." In the garden at night three poplars "seemed like sentries guarding a prisoner" and the beehives (emptied by disease) "glimmered in the moonlight like bones." He tries to educate himself as "an intellectual pacifist" in a fortnight, reading Markington's weekly ("a tonic for disagreeing with organized public opinion") and Tyrrell's volume of lectures, the first page of which he quotes ("inspired by a view of the springs of action which has been suggested by the War"). He underlines one sentence ("What integrates an individual life is a consistent creative purpose or unconscious direction") "and then looked up 'integrate' in the dictionary," and decides "much time would be saved if I underlined the sentences which didn't need underlining." He admits nothing in the lectures could back him up "when I was being interrogated by the Colonel at Clitherland" and "had a vague hope that I could get myself arrested without going there. It would be so much easier if I could get my case dealt with by strangers." Mechanism: self-mockery over his intellectual unreadiness, the beehives and poplars carrying the dread.
*Candidate application:* Explicit statement of the preference for hostile strangers over familiar colleagues as the audience for a dissent; the narrator is honest that his "arguments" were scant and his anxiety was about facing his own colonel.

**[E26b-73]. The dinner with Aunt Evelyn: farce around an unconfessable plan, a parrot who will not say "Stop the War," and a glass of milk.** (PW-09, ch. X §IV, L989)
At dinner the aunt asks about azaleas at Nutwood, calls "Naughty, naughty, naughty!" at a cat on a chair, tells him "Don't eat so fast, dear; you're simply bolting it down. You'll ruin your digestion," and regrets the lack of cream for the strawberries, which sets him off on "a diatribe against profiteers and officials who gorged at the Ritz and the Savoy" which she joins "almost overdid her indignant ejaculations," adding a complaint about the "disgracefully immoral" behavior of young women war-workers. He is "grumpy" and refuses her cribbage; she calls Markington's paper written by people "mad with their own self-importance"; he spends "the next ten minutes trying to teach Popsy the parrot how to say 'Stop the War'" but the parrot "obliged me with his well-known rendering of Aunt Evelyn calling the cats." She brings a glass of milk and suggests golf by the sea; "Games of golf indeed!... I glowered at the glass of milk and had half a mind to throw it out of the window. Afterwards I decided that I might as well drink it, and did so." He had earlier reasoned that it would be "a relief to confide in her" but impossible: "it was obviously kinder to keep my aunt in the dark about it until she received the inevitable shock." Mechanism: comedy of absurd domestic detail, where each person's talk misses the other's; the protest slogan attempted on the parrot is the narrator's only rehearsal; the milk is the bathetic coda to his resentment.
*Candidate application:* An extended, source-attested instance of dissent comedy at home: the protagonist is funny to the reader because he cannot say the thing; the humor is the burden of the secret. The only audience for the slogan is a bird.

**[E26b-74]. Butley churchyard: tombstone epitaphs, a joke on Gray's Elegy, and "They would tap their foreheads."** (PW-09, ch. X §IV, L993-1001)
He sits on a tomb in the churchyard and finds "joining the great majority" "a homely—almost a comforting—idea," reading an epitaph for Thomas Welfare ("Respected by all who knew him") and for his wife ("Not changed but glorified"). He thinks of the church measuring local time while history passes, and remembers the day the old Bull Inn burned, and then the Somme: "There wouldn't be much Gray's Elegy atmosphere if Butley were in the Fourth Army area!" He recovers his resolve: "no right-minded Butley man could take it upon himself to affirm that a European war was being needlessly prolonged by those who had the power to end it. They would tap their foreheads and sympathetically assume that I'd seen more of the fighting than was good for me. But I felt the desire to suffer," and imagines "some new spiritual freedom." His monologue: "'They can all go to blazes.... I know I'm right and I'm going to do it'... 'It won't be any fault of mine,' I muttered." A shaggy farm horse "munching placidly" watches him climb a stile. Mechanism: the churchyard's sentimental epitaphs are used as a calm contrast to modern death; the narrator foresees the civilians' diagnosis of his dissent as a mental effect of war (tapped foreheads), which turns out to be the Army's own response (E26b-77 onward).
*Candidate application:* The soldier predicts that the home community will treat his dissent as an effect of too much fighting; that prediction is precisely what the institution does next. A character may foresee the pathologizing frame and go ahead anyway.

**[E26b-75]. The telegram, the courteous covering letter, and the statement in full: "It certainly sounds a bit pompous."** (PW-09, ch. X §V, L1009-1011)
A telegram from the depot, "Report how situated," ends his waiting; he writes the Colonel a letter "(brief, courteous, and regretful)," encloses the typed statement, apologizes "for the trouble I was causing him," and promises to return "as soon as I heard from him." He sends a copy to Dottrell hoping the First Battalion will not "entirely disapprove," and counts on Durley to be "sensible and sympathetic" and David (in a convalescent hospital) to be unable to dissuade him: "I didn't want anyone to begin interfering on my behalf. At least I hoped that I didn't; though there were weak moments later on when I wished they would." The book prints the statement (public domain; quoted in full because the text itself is the point):
> "I am making this statement as an act of willful defiance of military authority, because I believe that the War is being deliberately prolonged by those who have the power to end it. I am a soldier, convinced that I am acting on behalf of soldiers. I believe that this War, upon which I entered as a war of defense and liberation, has now become a war of aggression and conquest. I believe that the purposes for which I and my fellow soldiers entered upon this War should have been so clearly stated as to have made it impossible to change them, and that, had this been done, the objects which actuated us would now be attainable by negotiation. I have seen and endured the sufferings of the troops, and I can no longer be a party to prolong these sufferings for ends which I believe to be evil and unjust. I am not protesting against the conduct of the War, but against the political errors and insincerities for which the fighting men are being sacrificed. On behalf of those who are suffering now I make this protest against the deception which is being practiced on them; also I believe that I may help to destroy the callous complacency with which the majority of those at home regard the continuance of agonies which they do not share, and which they have not sufficient imagination to realize."

The narrator's comment, immediately after: "It certainly sounds a bit pompous, I thought, and God only knows what the Colonel will think of it." After posting the letters "with a feeling of stupefied finality" he lies down with a headache, compares "the prospect of being in a prison cell with the prosy serenity of this buzzing summer afternoon," and says he felt the same a year ago, "but going back to the War next day hadn't been as bad as this." Mechanism: the statement itself contains no humor and no irony; it is formal, abstract, and written in the first person of a soldier ("on behalf of soldiers"); the joking is only in the covering letter's politeness and the narrator's own gloss on it. The statement's key claim is a change in the war's character (defense to conquest) and a plea for negotiated aims, not an attack on how battles are fought ("I am not protesting against the conduct of the War").
*Candidate application:* The shape of a dissent text: no humor, an appeal to what soldiers were promised, and an explicit refusal to condemn the fighting itself. The humor lies around it (the narrator's "pompous," the courteous apology), which suggests a character may protest in plain words and keep the jokes for the margins.

**[E26b-76]. Tea with Captain Huxtable: dramatic irony, a private pun on "cashier," and the garden indictment that cannot include the guests.** (PW-09, ch. X §V, L1013-1019)
Huxtable, the retired captain who got Sherston into the regiment and whom Aunt Evelyn calls "waggish," comes to tea; he greets Sherston with a reference to Muhammad and the Mountain, inspects "the gilt-edged trophy" (the General's card), and is given it as a souvenir of "having got me into the Regiment—(bitterly aware that I should soon be getting myself out of it pretty effectively!)." He suggests the Army as a career, then says the Church "couldn't be too militant," and rejoices that "another of those cranky Pacifist meetings had been broken up by some Colonial troops" and that he would like to deal with a "Stop the War" meeting in Butley: "To him a Conscientious Objector was the antithesis of an officer and a gentleman." Sherston, "with forced facetiousness," tells him of the bank raid: "The cashier in the bank was as cool as a cucumber." The narrator explains the joke to the reader: there were cucumber sandwiches on the table, but "the implications of the word 'cashier' were stronger, since for me it was part of the price of martyrdom, while for the Captain it epitomized an outer darkness of dishonor. But the word went past him." (To "cashier" an officer was to dismiss him from the service in disgrace.) Aunt Evelyn agrees with Huxtable that "nothing could be worse than a patched-up peace." The narrator sketches Huxtable's family: a gouty father who "used to knock his coachman's hat off and stamp on it," a son who permits himself "nothing more sulfurous than 'confound.'" In the garden among the delphiniums, Sherston wonders whether he has "exaggerated the 'callous complacency' of those at home": "What could elderly people do except try and make the best of their inability to sit in a trench and be bombarded?" He recalls Dottrell's line "Men are beginning to ask for what they are fighting," and asks whether the war must go on "in order that colonels might become brigadiers and brigadiers get divisions, while contractors and manufacturers enriched themselves, and people in high places ate and drank well and bandied official information and organized entertainments for the wounded," but "was unable to include Captain Huxtable and Aunt Evelyn in the indictment." (Restrained handling: a pacifist meeting broken up by troops, summarized; no violence described.) Mechanism: a pun the speaker enjoys alone, because the listener lacks the vocabulary of disgrace; the dissenter's indictment is softened by fondness for those who cannot see it.
*Candidate application:* A source-attested form of private irony in front of an unaware audience: a joke whose meaning is entirely for the speaker, a preview of the future. The pun's content (cashiering) is the Army's own word for expulsion, which a defector character may mine.

**[E26b-77]. "Report immediately": packing, the Dumbridge pavilion clock, and a verse about Rousseau and the birds.** (PW-09, ch. X §VI, L1023-1034)
Two days later a second telegram, "Report immediately," "obviously a telegram which did not need to be read twice," ends the suspense: "All considerations connected with my protest were now knocked on the head. It no longer mattered whether I was right or whether I was wrong... zero hour fixed for tomorrow." Packing, he rejects "my automatic pistol, water-bottle, and whistle" and decides he "must walk into the Orderly Room neat and self-possessed and normal." Aunt Evelyn takes his abrupt departure "as a matter of course," gives him white pigeon feathers for pipe-cleaners and reminds him to take his golf clubs ("I shouldn't get any time for golf, I said"). Waiting at Dumbridge he finds the county cricket ground "blighted and forsaken" with "no one had even troubled to wind up the pavilion clock." On the bookstall he buys a small red volume of extracts, *The Morals of Rousseau*, and a couplet he finds to be Cowper's will not leave his head for ten days: "I shall not ask Jean Jacques Rousseau / If birds confabulate or no." It had "no apparent relevancy." He spends his last night of liberty in a vast hotel "alone with J. J. Rousseau," "going to Clitherland under my own escort, so to speak." Mechanism: the pattern of preparing to be arrested by packing (the trench kit is left, the golf clubs are urged on him) is a comic substitution of equipment; the nonsensical couplet that recurs is an involuntary mental tic at the moment of greatest dread.
*Candidate application:* A known pattern of nervous humor: an irrelevant verse or tune that will not leave the head during anxiety; the narrator names it as having "no apparent relevancy."

**[E26b-78]. The train to Liverpool: "a munching cow," a lost nerve, and "a harassed automaton."** (PW-09, ch. X §VI, L1038)
He writes that "a man can write too much about his own feelings," shuts his eyes, then looks out at fields that "only made me long to be a munching cow." He recalls his first journey to the depot in May 1915, "diffident" and keen to be "a passably efficient second-lieutenant," "beginning a fresh and untarnished existence." Alone in a first-class compartment, he "asked myself out loud what this thing was which I was doing; and my mutinous act suddenly seemed outrageous and incredible. For a few minutes I completely lost my nerve." Arriving at Liverpool he is "merely a harassed automaton whose movements were being manipulated by a typewritten manifesto," and "felt like nothing on earth." Mechanism: the self-deprecating cow image and "automaton" as images of lost agency; no one else is involved.
*Candidate application:* Documented before-the-act terror in a comic key: the dissenter's lost nerve is voiced aloud to nobody.

**[E26b-79]. Clitherland: the Major who will not arrest him, "the paper which you sent him," and the refused cigar.** (PW-09, ch. X §VI, L1040-1056)
At the depot, "a bored sentry was the only witness of my arrival," and Sherston has the feeling "I'd come skulking back to see how much damage had been caused by that egregious projectile, my protest." He straightens tie and belt and "saluted dizzily." In the orderly room the Colonel is away; Major Macartney, the acting commander, rises and shakes his hand: "How are you, Sherston? I'm glad to see you back again" (the narrator: "No one could have been less glad to see me back again than he was"). In the Major's hut "the unfortunate Major hadn't a ghost of an idea what to say," and offers a cigar; Sherston refuses it "with... regret," because accepting "would have been a sign of surrender": the two could have "debated—with all requisite seriousness, of course—the best way of extricating me from my dilemma. How blissful that would have been! For my indiscretion might positively have been 'laughed off' (as a temporary aberration brought on, perhaps, by an overdose of solitude after coming out of hospital)." The Major says the Colonel "deeply concerned about you, and fully prepared to overlook the—" (he hesitates) "the paper which you sent him. He has asked me to urge you most earnestly to—er—dismiss the whole matter from your mind." Sherston says he cannot. The Major: "But Sherston, isn't it possible for you to reconsider your—er—ultimatum?" The narrator notes it was the first time he had heard it so called and "the locution epitomized the Major's inability to find words to fit the situation." Sherston's own explanation of his views "I couldn't make it sound convincing," and he fears he seems "rather crazy": "To be telling the acting-Colonel of my regimental Training Depot that I had come to the conclusion that England ought to make peace with Germany—was this altogether in focus with right-mindedness?" He says, "Hadn't you better have me put under arrest at once?" and the Major recoils: "I'd rather die than do such a thing!" The narrator: "what fulminations could be effective against one whose only object was to be put under arrest?" and "As long as he doesn't really think I'm dotty!" The Major, "in confidential tones," sends him to the Exchange Hotel in Liverpool, where he is to "await further instructions"; Sherston returns "an immense load had been lifted from my mind" and thinks "how nice it was not to be under arrest." Mechanism: the officer's euphemisms ("the paper," "er—ultimatum," "dismiss the whole matter from your mind"), embarrassed silence and the refused cigar as the institution's soft way of declining to see a dissent; the dissenter's reversed wish to be arrested; the narrator's joke "ultimatumist." Note the DAA, a friend who had lost a leg at Gallipoli, sat at another desk "ostensibly busy with Army forms and papers" and says nothing.
*Candidate application:* The best sourced picture here of how a decent officer handles a dissenter: gentleness, euphemism, an offer to forget, and a refusal to apply discipline. The institution's reaction is to treat the act as a lapse in decorum or a temporary aberration. The dissenter's comic ambivalence (wanting arrest, relieved when it does not come) is the narrator's own account.

**[E26b-80]. Three days at the Exchange Hotel: a friend who does not mention it, and the Medical Board warrant.** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1060-1062)
In Liverpool Sherston sees only two depot subalterns who "cheerily inquired when I was coming out to the Camp," so the Orderly Room is evidently keeping the matter quiet. His one-legged friend, the Deputy-Assistant-Adjutant, visits and the two "managed to avoid mentioning everything connected with my 'present situation,'" while he "regaled me with the gossip of the Camp as though nothing were wrong"; on leaving he hands over an official instruction to attend a Special Medical Board at Crewe, with a railway warrant. Sherston weighs it: "Here was a chance of turning aside from the road to Court-Martialdom... Roughly speaking, two thoughts were exactly what I did give to it." One thought says drop it; the other says resist temptations "inflexibly"; he "called in pride and obstinacy to aid me, throttled my warm feelings toward my well-wishers at Clitherland Camp, and burned my boats by tearing up both railway warrant and Medical Board instructions." Mechanism: the whole institution's handling at this stage is silence, gossip and a form; the narrator's comic coinages ("Court-Martialdom") treat his own path as a destination.
*Candidate application:* The institution's second move after gentle persuasion is a quiet medical route; the friend who delivers it keeps up ordinary talk. The dissenter's refusal is a tearing-up of paper, a small physical act.

**[E26b-81]. Learning poems for prison, and a nightingale ode he cannot finish.** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1064-1066)
He spends Wednesday learning by heart poems from Palgrave's *Golden Treasury*, his idea being that they "would be a help to me in prison, where, I imagined, no books would be allowed," and tells himself he should get used to giving up tobacco but smokes "as many pipes as I could while I'd got the chance." A letter from the Member of Parliament urges him to keep his spirits up. When the Colonel arrives he finds Sherston learning Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale"; Sherston, caught mid-line at "Nor what soft...," wonders "What soft was it," reopens the book, and then greets the Colonel. Mechanism: preparing for a cell by memorizing verse and smoking, rendered as a mock-practical plan; the half-remembered line as a comic interruption at a solemn moment.
*Candidate application:* An act of preparation for punishment (memorized verse, hoarded pipes) is a documented comic-domestic form of facing imprisonment.

**[E26b-82]. The Colonel's visit: "to stop wanting to stop the War," the specially arranged Board, and "the whole British Empire."** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1066-1068)
The Colonel, "apparently unincensed," sits down "fussed and perplexed" and "with patient commonsense arguments, he did his best to persuade me to stop wanting to stop the War." What troubles him most is that Sherston skipped the Board: "Do you realize, Sherston, that it had been specially arranged for you and that an R.A.M.C. Colonel came all the way from London for it?" The narrator reads this as the anxiety of a man "whose existence was dominated by documentary instructions from 'higher quarters,'" for whom disregarding an order was "like a reversal of the order of nature." His arguments for "crushing Prussian militarism" are "stuttering" and civilian, as "the overworked superintendent of a reinforcement manufactory," who "had never had time to ask himself why North Welshmen were being shipped across to France" to be killed in various ways; he says the war cannot end "until it ended as it ought to end." His clinching appeal: "And surely it stands to reason, Sherston, that you must be wrong when you set your own opinion against the practically unanimous feeling of the whole British Empire." Sherston: "There was no answer I could make to that, so I remained silent and waited for the British Empire idea to blow over." The Colonel adds: "I told Mersey Defenses that you missed your Board through a misunderstanding of the instructions, but I'm afraid the affair will soon go beyond my control. I beg you to try and reconsider your refusal by tomorrow." He leaves "almost irately" without another word. Sherston, now more isolated, notes that Tyrrell "had never foreseen that my senior officers would treat me with this kindly tolerance which was so difficult to endure." (Restrained handling: the Colonel's list of how the men are killed is summarized.) Mechanism: a courteous officer's cover story, bureaucratic worry over a missed form, and a majority-opinion argument that the narrator meets with silence; the narrator's phrase "kindly tolerance" names the problem.
*Candidate application:* A precise, sourced description of how a decent commander handles a dissenter: arguments of the majority, a cover story to superiors, concern about the paperwork. The dissenter finds courtesy harder to bear than anger; there is no one to fight.

**[E26b-83]. Formby dunes: a "gleam of haggard humor," a fist shaken at the sky, and the ribbon that floated.** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1070-1074)
Two days in a "purgatorial limbo" in which his thoughts "became incoherent and even nonsensical," he takes a train to Formby for some fresh air, avoids the golf links "remembering, with a gleam of haggard humor, how Aunt Evelyn had urged me to bring my 'golf sticks,'" and, feeling "outlawed, bitter, and baited," shakes his fists at the sky in "the time-honored gesture." Then he "ripped the M.C. ribbon off my tunic and threw it into the mouth of the Mersey. Weighted with significance though this action was, it would have felt more conclusive had the ribbon been heavier. As it was, the poor little thing fell weakly onto the water and floated away as though aware of its own futility. One of my point-to-point cups would have served my purpose more satisfyingly, and they'd meant much the same to me as my Military Cross." Watching a ship on the horizon he decides "protesting against the prolongation of the War was about as much use as shouting at the people on board that ship." Mechanism: the narrator's comic annotation of his own symbolic gesture (a ribbon too light to sink), with a point-to-point cup as the preferred prop; the sequence ends in a plain, deflating simile. (Compare E15c-25, where Graves reports Sassoon telling him he "had just been down to the Formby links and thrown his Military Cross into the sea.")
*Candidate application:* The symbolic act of discarding a decoration is told as a joke at its own expense; the comic note is that the object was too light to carry the meaning. The simile of shouting at a ship is a plain statement of powerlessness.

**[E26b-84]. David Cromlech arrives: "Thank God you've come!" the smudge, and "talked nineteen to the dozen."** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1078-1080)
On the eighth day, a Sunday, Sherston is at "the end of my tether," having "survived seven days without hauling down my flag"; he thinks of going to church but finds "more real religion to be found in the Golden Treasury than in a church which only approved of military-aged men when they were in khaki." David Cromlech appears and Sherston cries "Thank God you've come!" David sits silent, anxious. Feeling "rather silly and hysterical," Sherston says "David, you've got an enormous black smudge on your forehead," and David "Obediently... moistened his handkerchief with his tongue and proceeded to rub the smudge off," his face "vacant and childish, suggesting an earlier time when his nurse had performed a similar service." Sherston then talks "nineteen to the dozen" and says, "When I started this anti-war stunt I never dreamed it would be such a long job, getting myself run in for a court martial," laughing "with somewhat hollow gaiety." Mechanism: a trivial, comic exchange (the smudge) as the discharge of tension; the dissenter calls his own protest a "stunt," the unit word for a reckless act, and laughs at the length of his own prosecution (compare E26b-17 and E26b-34, where "stunts" were the narrator's reputation).
*Candidate application:* An old friend arrives, and the first thing said is a joke about dirt on his face; the narrator's own word for his protest is the same slang the battalion used for his bravery. Humor returns with the first trusted person. (David Cromlech is not identified in the text; he is commonly identified in literary criticism with Graves. See E15c-23 to -27 for Graves's own account of the same days.)

**[E26b-85]. The argument over lunch and on the sands: loyalty, "skrimshankers," "cold feet," and "a man with a message."** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1082-1084)
David says: "I've come to tell you that you've got to drop this anti-war business." Sherston: "Don't you realize that I'm a man with a message? I thought you'd come to see me through the court martial as 'prisoner's friend.'" The narrator's retrospective gloss: "the verdict of posterity would be against us," since neither could "see beyond his own experience"; yet "we argued as though the secret confabulations of Cabinet Ministers in various countries were as clear as daylight to us, and our assumption was that they were all wrong, while we, who had been in the trenches, were farseeing and infallible." David: the war "was bound to go on till one side or the other collapsed" and "the Pacifists were only meddling"; "Tyrrell's only a doctrinaire," David replies, "though I grant you he's a courageous one."; "No one except people who've been in the real fighting have any right to interfere about the War; and even they can't get anything done about it. All they can do is to remain loyal to one another. And you know perfectly well that most of the conscientious objectors are nothing but skrimshankers." Sherston counters with a young doctor who "played Rugby Football for Scotland and was now in prison." When David says he has been "wire-pulling" for him, Sherston retorts that with David's "usual tact" he will get himself hard labor and perhaps worse (a joke about the penalty for desertion, summarized, not quoted). David "prodded disapprovingly at his food and then bolted it down as if it were medicine." On the shore at Formby, David says the front "would only say that I'd got cold feet"; Sherston replies, "Isn't that worse cowardice than being thought cold-footed by officers who refuse to think about anything except the gentlemanly traditions of the Regiment? I'm not doing it for fun, am I? Can't you understand that this is the most difficult thing I've ever done in my life? I'm not going to be talked out of it just when I'm forcing them to make a martyr of me." Mechanism: two friends who agree the world is mad dispute who has the right to speak; the narrator mocks both ("farseeing and infallible"); insults are the language of loyalty ("blighters," "skrimshankers"). (Compare E15c-13 and E15c-26 on "skrim-shanker" and "ratting"/"cold feet" as the army's readings.)
*Candidate application:* The soldier-friend's case against dissent has three parts: only fighting men have the right; the objectors are shirkers; the front will call it cold feet. The dissenter's reply is to name loyalty to his convictions as the greater cowardice to avoid. Neither side jokes about the matter except as a sting.

**[E26b-86]. The lie: "They won't make a martyr of you," the asylum threat, and the imaginary Bible.** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1084-1086)
David answers "They won't make a martyr of you": the Colonel told him to say that if Sherston keeps refusing to be "medically-boarded" they would "shut me up in a lunatic asylum for the rest of the War"; "Nothing would induce them to court martial me. It had all been arranged with some big bug at the War Office." Asked why he did not say so before: "I kept it as a last resort because I was afraid it might upset you." Sherston: "I wouldn't believe this from anyone but you. Will you swear on the Bible that you're telling the truth?" and David "swore on an imaginary Bible" that nothing would induce them to court martial him and that he would be treated as insane. "All right then, I'll give way." Sherston sits down on a breakwater; he feels "an enormous load had been lifted," thinks "I ought to have known that the blighters would do me down somehow," and replies to David's elation ("Then I'll tell Orderly Room they can fix up a Board for you tomorrow") "You can tell them anything you bloody well please!" The narrator's retrospective closing: "I was unaware that David had, probably, saved me from being sent to prison by telling me a very successful lie. No doubt I should have done the same for him if our positions had been reversed." Mechanism: the threat of the asylum is the weapon that ends the protest, since it denies the dissenter the martyrdom he wanted; the oath on a nonexistent Bible is a joke with real work to do; the narrator's last word on it is a retrospective that excuses the liar. Genre note: the text says the lie was a lie, and is narrated years later by the persona; whether the real events matched is outside this source. (Compare E15c-24 and E15c-26 for Graves's account of arranging the medical outcome and of reading the protest as a collapse; the novel and the memoir assign the persuasion and the evidence differently.)
*Candidate application:* The institution's most effective response to a principled dissent in this book is to refuse to prosecute it and to classify the dissenter as unwell. The friend's loving lie, sworn on an imaginary Bible, shows how humor and falsehood can serve loyalty; the dissenter is both relieved and defeated.

**[E26b-87]. The Medical Board: questions as a joke about the Crusades, an evaded answer, and a couplet about birds.** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1090-1092)
David gives "all the necessary explanations of my mental condition" while Sherston waits in an anteroom; Sherston now feels "an irresponsible person again, absolved from any obligation to intervene in world affairs," finds that "the present performance seemed rather ludicrous," and enters the "Bird Room" repeating to himself that he "should not ask Jean Jacques Rousseau if birds confabulated or no." The Board is a Colonel, a Major, and a Captain, the last "a civilian in uniform, and a professional neurologist"; the elderly Regulars' "acquaintance with Army Forms exceeded their knowledge of neurology." The Colonel asks, "Was it on religious grounds?" ("'No, sir; not particularly'... 'Fighting on religious grounds' sounded like some sort of a joke about the Crusades"), then "Do you consider yourself qualified to decide when the War should stop?" ("Realizing that he was only trying to make me talk rubbish, I evaded him by admitting that I hadn't thought about my qualifications, which wasn't true"), then "But your friend tells us that you were very good at bombing. Don't you still dislike the Germans?" (answer forgotten). Sherston feels he "simply must repeat that couplet out loud... it would prove conclusively and comfortably that I was a harmless lunatic." The neurologist's eye "signaled sympathetic understanding." The Captain suggests Slateford Hospital: "So it was decided that I was suffering from shell-shock." The Colonel "having demonstrated his senior rank by asking me an adequate number of questions" and Sherston repeats the couplet under his breath; "'Did you say anything?' asked the Colonel, frowning slightly," and he "disclaimed having said anything." Mechanism: a politely evasive performance for a board whose verdict has been pre-arranged; the nonsense couplet as a private joke and a potential insanity gag; the Captain's quiet role as ally.
*Candidate application:* The Army's last stage, in this account, is a board whose members are partly ritual and partly sympathetic, and whose questions (religious grounds, qualification to decide, do you still dislike the Germans) are meant to produce one of two verdicts. The dissenter's best weapon is polite evasion and a private joke he does not speak.

**[E26b-88]. The close: whistling, a missed train, and the volume ends on arrival.** (PW-09, ch. X §VII, L1094-1096)
Walking back from the Board Sherston overhears himself whistling: "Honestly, David, I don't believe I've whistled for about six weeks!" and looks at the blue sky, "grateful because, at that moment, it seemed as though I had finished with the War." Next morning he goes to Edinburgh; David, "who had been detailed to act as my escort, missed the train and arrived at Slateford War Hospital several hours later than I did. And with my arrival at Slateford War Hospital this volume can conveniently be concluded." The book therefore ends before any hospital scene, any doctor in the hospital, any return to the line or to civilian life; the last joke is the reversal of escort and patient. Mechanism: the narrator's comic voice returns at once with the verdict (whistling), and the ending is a deadpan, almost administrative closing sentence. (Compare E15c-25, where the same missed-train detail is told as "a great joke" between the two officers.)
*Candidate application:* The comic voice is not lost by the protest; it returns in the form of a whistle once the decision is taken out of his hands. The book ends with the dissenter safe and "finished" and with the friend late.

## Pattern roll-up

*(Narrator's account in an autobiographical novel; every pattern below is "as the persona tells it." Finding numbers in brackets.)*

**Functions**
- *Coping by self-theater.* The narrator names a deliberate role ("play at being a hero in shining armor") as the way to avoid collapse [22]; later versions are the "jaunty unconcern" in the trench [40] and the "soldierly unconcern" in the bank [70]. He mocks each in retrospect [40, 41].
- *Coping by displacement and small things.* A minor dread replaces a major one (the injection after a death sentence [16]; the taste of tea after a suicidal order [33]; the Colonel's cake [39]; an earworm couplet [77, 87]; learning poems for a cell [81]). The narrator's own formula: "any joke being better than none" [21].
- *Coping by retreat into the homely.* Rural-English reminiscence in the Tunnel is "a form of drug" and is as characteristic of the front as brutalization [37, 74].
- *Cohesion.* Farewell revel with a pianist-philosopher [19]; mess pranks that "overdid it" [15]; hide-and-seek that "restored humanity" to a platoon [14].
- *Boundary and rank.* "Warts" [17]; a voice from the ranks that cannot be traced [20]; "Whincop's Bread Cards" [69]; the satirical ladder of "hoping to satisfy" [36].

**Targets**
- Generals and Staff (the rum ban [12], the Corps Commander [20], the ladder [36], the "genial exculpatory" General [53]); the Base's comfortable officers, "London Clubmen... with a conscientious objection to physical discomfort" [10]; the Church (the "Benevolent Neutral Power" [9], the padre's helplessness [43], the Archbishop [50], Lady Asterisk's afterlife [57]); the press and its reviewers [56]; civilian visitors and relatives [46, 59, 73, 76]; the narrator himself, most of all [11, 34, 41, 49, 62, 65, 72, 83]; the anti-war circle, affectionately [67]; doctors who tyrannize or rubber-stamp [54, 87].

**Forms and mechanics**
- The parenthesis as the carrier of the true feeling [1, 11, 28, 36, 40]; understatement and deadpan [25, 33, 35]; mock-institutional language (a "Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Great War Generals" [12]; "candidature for a court martial" [65]; "ultimatumist" [79]); bathos by swerve [16, 55, 73]; the corrupted place-name ("Fish Hooks") and the pre-coined battle-name ("Sausage Machine") [20, 21]; a script format for a dialogue with a general [20]; a typology of imaginary visitors [46]; aptronym game that turns bitter [52]; phonetically spelled drunkenness [19]; the private pun on "cashier" [76]; the insect/animal self-image (beetle, "munching cow," "automaton") [34, 35, 78]; bodily-function puns [24, 30, summarized]; quoted official documents left to indict themselves (communiqué [27], operation order [38], the declaration [75]).

**Rules of license**
- Anonymous retort from within a column is licensed; naming oneself to a general is not [20]. The unsayable between best friends is not traded [23, 51]; "few among us would ever tell the truth to our friends and relations in England" [44]. Civilians receive the visitor-version appropriate to their type [46]. Joking about the dissent is the narrator's alone: the statement and the mentor's counsel are plain [64, 75]. A mentor's paradox is a joke only if the listener has not decided; Tyrrell's "You will be more alive in prison" is not [64]. A decent officer's license is to avoid the word: "the paper," "er—ultimatum" [79].

**Limits and failure modes**
- Humor stops at casualty lists and executions [4, 60], at night terrors [48], on the hospital train [44], and in the statement [75]. It misfires as stale jokes [5], overdone pranks [15], "forced facetiousness" [76], "hollow gaiety" [84], and a symbolic ribbon too light to sink [83]. It can hide a lapse in composure (the Colonel's cake [39]) or the narrator's own shame (kicking a dead man [35]). Comic self-image can displace conviction (medal-hunger beside anti-war convictions [49, 69]).

**Time course**
- The narrator, writing about thirteen years later, says that "the harsh horror mellows and recedes" even in old soldiers [53]; he dislikes his own jauntiness in retrospect [40]; he says the front's "endurance" is one a man "might, in after days, forget or disbelieve" [29]; every flash-forward death is a flat after-clause [26, 42, 60].

**Civilian-facing register**
- A catalog of masks by audience [46]; the press version of a bayonet charge [56]; a bishop's leaflet at the station [45]; a hostess's "they are safe and happy now" [60]; relatives who cannot hear [59, 73, 76]; the narrator's declared "attitude toward civilians" that "they couldn't understand" [46]; the later counter-thought that elderly people cannot be blamed for not sitting in a trench [76].

**Defector/dissent specifics (the dissent arc, in order)**
- *Seeds:* admiration for conscientious objectors after hearing how a hearty Major broke them [7]; a Base doctor's gloom [7]; "losing my belief in the War" [6].
- *Wound, bitterness, and doubt:* the wound gives "liberated eyes" and "a private individual" [42]; "my privilege to be bitter" [46]; criticism "not unlike a young man who suddenly loses his belief in religion and stands up to tell the Universal Being that He doesn't exist" [49]; humor as shared code with a friend who avoids "becoming serious" [51]; shop names that lead to "People who told the truth were likely to be imprisoned" [52]; "I began to wish that I could talk candidly to someone" [57].
- *The decision:* the editor's laugh at soldier anecdotes [61]; the narrator's borrowed phrases [62]; "the head was my own" and "a glowing sense of martyrdom" [63]; the mentor's "You will be more alive in prison" and the half-hearted laugh [64]; drafting with self-mockery and one retrievable motive (the unit's own courage and its dead friend's quip) [65, 66].
- *Division of loyalties:* welcomed by the anti-war circle, who dislike uniforms [67]; the narrator still "unable to dislike being a Flintshire Fusilier" [67]; "What people say doesn't matter" [69]; double life at home with the parrot who will not say "Stop the War" [72, 73]; civilians would "tap their foreheads" [74].
- *How the institution handled the dissenter:* decent officers decline to name or punish it [79, 82]; a form (Medical Board) is offered, then a threat of confinement as insane, delivered by a friend [80, 86]; the Board is a formality with one ally [87]; no court-martial, no prison [86, 87]. "Kindly tolerance which was so difficult to endure" [82].
- *The narrator's comic voice before and after:* before: jaunty, self-theatrical [22, 40, 41]; during: self-satirizing, nervous, domestic farce [65, 72-74, 77-78]; after: arrested humor returns as a smudge joke, a whistle, and a missed train [84, 88]; the declaration itself contains no humor [75].
- *Who jokes and who stops:* Markington laughs but speaks plainly [61]; Tyrrell's wit is a serious creed [64]; Major Macartney stammers and cannot find words [79]; the Colonel does not joke [82]; David's one joke is a lie sworn on an imaginary Bible [86]; Aunt Evelyn and Captain Huxtable are unaware [73, 76].

**Cross-cultural**
- British Army officer culture of 1917 only; the enemy is rarely a subject of humor [18, 29, 47].

**Craft observations**
- The author-narrator comments on his own technique ("a man can write too much about his own feelings" [78]; "I would like to be able to remember that I smiled grimly and departed reticently. But..." [42]); he lets official documents and a mock-heroic register do the satire, and keeps the casualty news in subordinate clauses [26, 42, 60].

## Gaps and cautions

- **Genre and persona.** *Memoirs of an Infantry Officer* is an autobiographical novel in which "George Sherston" narrates; units, places and people are renamed (the regiment, the depot, the hospital; Markington, Tyrrell, Cromlech and others). Nothing here is documentary testimony. The book states retrospection ("twelve years later," L849; the writer's armchair, L777). Where the persona claims he did not know something at the time (the lie, L1086), that is a later gloss. I do not identify real people behind the characters; the one exception is a flagged note at E26b-84 that criticism commonly identifies David Cromlech with Graves (not stated in the text).
- **The hospital and the doctor.** The brief's priority included the hospital. This volume ends when the narrator arrives at the war hospital (L1096), so there is no hospital doctor, no treatment, no return to the line and no later civilian life in my range. Everything that the narrator does with "the Army and a doctor" occurs in the depot Orderly Room, with the acting commander, the Colonel, and the three-man Medical Board (E26b-79 to -87), plus earlier encounters with other doctors at the convalescent hotel and the base (E26b-7, -54). Later events are in another book and not in PW-09.
- **Comparison with Graves.** E15c-23 to -27 give the same July 1917 events from Graves's side. Points of difference to be weighed by anyone using both: the declaration in E15c-23 and the one printed here differ in at least one word (here "callous complacency," there "callous complacence"), so neither is a verbatim historical source; the novel assigns the arrangement to a friend's threat and oath (E26b-86), while the memoir has its author "rigging" the board by performing a patriot's role (E15c-24); both include the missed train and the Formby ribbon or Military Cross in the water (E26b-83, -88; E15c-25).
- **Statement as printed.** The declaration here is the novel's version, printed once and also drafted in two earlier fragments (E26b-65, -66). The later drafts' wording ("an act of willful defiance...") is preserved; I spelled American per the project rule.
- **Class and rank.** The dissenter has private means, a regimental connection, a Countess's letter, a decorated record, and a colonel who is a friend; the gentle institutional response in E26b-79 to -87 should not be generalized to other ranks, to an enlisted man, or to a conscientious objector (compare the beating in E26b-7, where the same Army deals with objectors by a different method).
- **Period attitudes.** Ethnic, national, class and gender generalizations (Welsh, Scots, Irish, Canadian, French, "Boches," women war-workers) appear in the narrator's voice and in other speakers' mouths; they are documented, not endorsed.
- **Overlap and coordination.** My range began at L560, ten lines inside E26a's range (E26a ends at L570); E26a was unfinished when I finished, so its finding numbers and my cross-references to it are not given. E15c's finding numbers were read from the file as it stood.
- **Size.** This pass runs well over the 10-18 percent guideline (the range is roughly 43,000 words; the file is about 25,000). I gave full treatment to the dissent arc and the public-domain documents (E26b-49 to -88) as the brief prioritized; E26b-1 to -48 could be read as a skim for the front-line and convalescent humor.
- **Format.** The text file has no page numbers, and italics are lost; line numbers are the citation. A blank (L631) stands for an unprintable word in a joke. One apparent edition typo (L869) is not quoted. Standard Ebooks colophon and "Uncopyright" boilerplate (L1098-1147) was read, not extracted.
