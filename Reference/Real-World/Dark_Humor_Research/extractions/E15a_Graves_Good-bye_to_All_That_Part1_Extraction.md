## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-05 is.** Graves's best-known prose book, an autobiography written "at the age of thirty-three" (1929), told with a mock-businesslike framing (a "formal good-bye," "forgetfulness," "money"). It is a literary, partly comic memoir by a regular-class officer-poet: a Special Reserve second lieutenant, later captain, of the Royal Welch Fusiliers. The Gutenberg text carries transcriber marks (a diamond symbol for corrected misprints) and **end-of-book footnotes** (collected at L12313-12342, numbered [1]-[8]); the first three annotate passages in my range, and I read those (see E15a-22, E15a-42). There are **no page markers**, so I cite chapter numeral plus text-file line numbers ("L").

**Where the war narrative begins.** The childhood, school and Mallory-and-climbing material is **chapters I-IX (L168-2578)**. The war narrative begins at **chapter X (L2581-2584), "I was at Harlech when war was declared"**, and runs through enlistment and the Wrexham/Lancaster training period (to L3077); **chapter XI (L3081-3359)** is a stand-alone essay on Royal Welch regimental culture; **chapter XII (L3364-3877)** is a "reconstituted chapter" about his arrival in France in spring 1915 (written, he says, as a novel in 1916 and "retranslated into history"); **chapter XIII (L3882-)** is a run of letters home dated 21 May to early June 1915.

**Evidence base and method.** One narrator, one class, one regiment. The narrator is himself the audience's guide to officers' mess humor, regimental custom, and what the men said, with the men's voices reported in brief quoted bursts. The memoir is retrospective (1929) and says so about its own reliability: parts were first written as a novel; many fates "came to me merely as hearsay." The letters of chapter XIII are presented as contemporary extracts (which I cannot verify). Many anecdotes are second-hand or marked "it was said."

**What it does NOT contain (in my range).** No fighting narrative (my range stops before Loos, the Somme and Mametz). No extended other-ranks voice: soldiers speak in quoted lines of a sentence or two, mediated by the officer. No jokes by the narrator about named dead in his own voice. No explicit theory of humor; the claims about what humor does are occasional ("always joking," "a wonderfully witty answer," "kept them steady"). Almost no enemy-side voice except relatives' anecdotes and the German prisoners at Lancaster.

**Extraction scope.** Chapters I-IX are read in full but **extracted only for what bears on humor, class and institutional culture** (E15a-1 to E15a-13); the genealogy, religion, German childhood, family anecdotes and climbing technique are not extracted. Chapters X-XIII are extracted in full for humor-bearing passages (E15a-14 to E15a-53).

**Quotation conventions.** Quotations are exact except for American spelling (the project rule applies to quotations: "humor," "honor," "color," "pajamas," "armorer," "traveler," "gray"); square brackets mark my insertions; ellipses mark my cuts; the text's own dashes standing in for oaths are kept. Glosses not in the text are marked "my gloss." **Period national, ethnic and class caricature is documented, not endorsed** (Welsh, Irish, German, Italian, Indian-service slang, the French).

**Retrospective vs. period text.** Chapter XIII (letters) is the closest in time; chapter XII is a 1916 draft reworked in 1929; chapters X-XI are retrospective essay; the schooldays are told from 1929 and 1913 (one quoted school-magazine piece, E15a-9).

## Findings

### A. Front matter, childhood and school (chapters I-VIII): the class and institutional background (L1-2370)

*Scope note for this part: Graves's childhood and school chapters (I-VIII) are read in full but extracted only for what bears on humor, class and institutional culture. The war narrative begins after the school chapters (see the source-shape notes). Citations give chapter numeral and text-file line numbers ("L").*

**[E15a-1]. The narrator's stated purposes: a "formal good-bye," forgetfulness and money.** (PW-05, ch. I, L173-178)
The memoir opens by naming its own aims. Graves, writing at thirty-three, says the book is "an opportunity for a formal good-bye to you and to you and to you and to me and to all that," and then lists "forgetfulness, because once all this has been settled in my mind and written down and published it need never be thought about again" and "money." The tone is mock-businesslike: the writing is a settling of accounts so that the matter can be filed and dropped. The device of the semicolon list that slides from the solemn ("good-bye ... to me") to the mercenary ("money") is the book's basic register.
*Candidate application:* A veteran-narrator who treats the telling of his service as a discharge of obligation, with money named in the same breath as grief, is a documented pose (one author, one book); it does not show that the discharge worked.

**[E15a-2]. "Old Joe," the quartermaster, and the biographical slip he filled in with rum and khaki pants.** (PW-05, ch. I, L193-204)
Graves introduces the idea of a "proper chap" (a person with reality apart from official record) through a battalion quartermaster in France, "Old Joe," who won a D.S.O. for being "the only quartermaster in the Seventh Division to get up rations to his battalion in the firing line." Sent a slip to complete for a directory of distinguished persons, he ignored birth, place and campaigns and entered two items only. The example is the point, so, quoted (the form's printed headings are the left column):

> Issue . . Rum, rifles, etc.
> Family seat . My khaki pants.

*Mechanism and target:* deadpan literal-mindedness aimed at a civilian status register ("Who's Who" gazetteering). The joke is that he answers the form in the only currency he holds (supply, issue, his own trousers) and in doing so reports what the decoration was actually for. The rank of the joker is a quartermaster, not a combat officer; Graves reports it as an admired act, and uses it to sort "proper chaps" from "geographical features."
*Candidate application:* An old logistics soldier who answers an official form with an inventory of what he carries is a recognizable type of dry institutional insolence; it needs no enemy.

**[E15a-3]. Graves's own claims about a comic self and the army's watches.** (PW-05, ch. I, L284-303)
In a mock "passport description," Graves lists his crooked nose, uneven face and large hands, and names his "best comic turn" as a double-jointed pelvis that can "rap like the Fox sisters" (my gloss: a reference to the mid-nineteenth-century spirit-rapping mediums). He adds that he always magnetizes the mainspring of a watch, so that when "there was an army order that officers should carry watches and synchronize them daily," he "had to buy two new ones every month." The one reference to a war wound is dropped flatly into the same paragraph: one shoulder is lower "because of a lung wound in the war," followed by "Medically, I am a thoroughly 'good life.'" The wound shows up as an item in a list of physical eccentricities, at the same level as the nose; it is not dwelt on, and it is followed by an insurance-office phrase.
*Candidate application:* A wound reported in the register of a physical description, between a magnetized watch and an actuarial verdict, is one way the narrator's later humor handles the body: the wound is a fact about the shoulder, not a story.

**[E15a-4]. Class, "masquerade as a gentleman," and the cost of the "gentleman's education."** (PW-05, ch. I, L545-562; ch. II, L655-690)
Graves says his most useful and "most dangerous gift" is that he can, with officials and grudging institutions, "masquerade as a gentleman": his accent and gestures are "irreproachable," his clothes are not, and he has been "placed" as an eccentric heir to a dukedom. He says he "paid so heavily for the fourteen years of my gentleman's education" that he feels entitled "occasionally to get some sort of return." In chapter II he dates his own class consciousness to a scarlet-fever ward at age four and a half, where a clergyman's child was called "a fair toff" by the other children and he saw for the first time that "there were two sorts of people—ourselves and the lower classes." He says he accepted the separation "as naturally as I had accepted religious dogma, and did not finally discard gentility until nearly twenty years later" (by his own count, about the war years).
*Mechanism:* the narrator treats class as a performance he can run on demand, and dates the end of belief in it to the war. This is the frame for every later scene in which an officer and other ranks share a trench.
*Candidate application:* An officer-character who can still "pass" in rank-conscious company, and who knows it is a pass, has a built-in ironic distance from the class scaffolding that runs the unit.

**[E15a-5]. Family humor as background: the Latin jokes of two bishops, a practical joker who "never let us down in a joke," and the household's rules against slang.** (PW-05, ch. I, L403-414; ch. II, L597-610; ch. IV, L1072-1090)
(a) Graves's grandfather the Protestant bishop and the Catholic bishop of Limerick "cracked Latin jokes at each other" while the two were on "the very best terms"; the Catholic bishop once answered a text about a "quiver full" of children with "The ancient Jewish quiver only held six." (b) The father's educational rule was to insist on grammar, correct pronunciation, "and using no slang"; one child's printed nursery notice read "I must not say bang bust or pig bucket, for it is rude." (c) The German uncle Siegfried, heir to a castle, once ate pebbles on a garden path until the children copied him, then, to be rid of them, gave each a pebble that "crushed easily and had a chocolate center" on condition they leave; "He never once let us down in a joke." A parenthesis then says he "was killed in the war as an officer of the Imperial German Staff and I believe that they never found his body."
*Mechanism:* humor as a clever bargain (b), a straight-faced deception rewarded with a treat (c), and learned wit across a religious line (a). The parenthesis in (c) shows the memoir's habit: the dead are put in a flat subordinate clause after the joke, not before.
*Candidate application:* The narrator's later practical-joking and verbal sparring grow out of a house where slang was forbidden and wit was a family trait; the contrast with other-rank slang is built into him.

**[E15a-6]. Mallory's Pipe: the Climbers' Club entry as deadpan institutional record.** (PW-05, ch. V, L1496-1508)
Graves tells how George Mallory, having left a pipe on a ledge halfway down a precipice, scrambled back and up again by a short cut that the next day's inspectors found to be "an impossible overhang nearly all the way." The Climbers' Club's rule was that climbs are named for natural features, but "an exception was made," and the guide entry read: "_Mallory's Pipe_, a variation on Route 2; see adjoining map. This climb is totally impossible. It has been performed once, in failing light, by Mr. G. H. L. Mallory."
*Mechanism:* bureaucratic cross-reference language ("a variation on Route 2; see adjoining map") applied to a feat that contradicts the entry's own claim of impossibility. The contradiction is stated in a single dry sentence. Mallory is also the friend whom Graves names as a heroic exception; the same book will record his death on Everest.
*Candidate application:* The understated official-record voice that Graves admires (and that the later trench-report humor in the memoir uses) is already present in a mountaineering club's guide entry, a source in a different culture than the army.

**[E15a-7]. Charterhouse: the caste of "bloods" and the carnation demonstration by three sixth-formers.** (PW-05, ch. VII, L1776-1874)
Graves describes a strict dress-based caste system at Charterhouse: new boys had no privileges; a second-term boy could wear a knitted tie; colored socks, turned-down collars and the right to get up raffles came with years; and "very peculiar and unique distinctions were reserved for the bloods" (members of the cricket and football elevens): light gray flannel trousers, butterfly collars, coats slit up the back and "the privilege of walking arm-in-arm." After three sixth-form intellectuals (Mansfield, Waller, Taylor) were insulted by the bloods at a debate, they wrote a letter to the school magazine calling the offenders "certain First Eleven babies," and the next Sunday walked into chapel after the bloods' entrance "magnificent in gray flannel trousers, slit coats, First Eleven collars, and with pink carnations in their buttonholes." "It is impossible to describe the astonishment and terror that this spectacle caused," Graves says; the captain of the eleven "had gone quite white," and the three then kept a master in conversation after chapel so that no scene could be made. The headmaster, a scholar who "disliked the games tradition," refused the captain's request to discipline them and ruled that the sixth form was entitled to what it had assumed; "the prestige of the bloods declined greatly."
*Mechanism:* usurpation of insignia as a mock-ceremony (the carnations are the added insult), carried out in the one place where the offended group cannot retaliate, and then protected by an adult's presence. The target is a self-appointed elite whose authority rests on dress codes and ritual timing.
*Candidate application:* Wearing a superior caste's marks without leave, in a setting where the superiors cannot act, is a documented school-level model of insignia mockery; in this part of the memoir, the army's own distinctions (the regimental flash, E15a-29) are defended, not mocked.

**[E15a-8]. Bawdy humor as a weapon of exclusion, and Graves's later claim that the army "hardened" him to reply in kind.** (PW-05, ch. VI, L1638-1655; ch. VII, L1703-1705)
At Charterhouse an older Irish boy tormented Graves by "forcing his bawdy humor on my prudishness and inviting everybody to laugh at my disgust" and by building "a sort of humorous legend of my hypocrisy and concealed depravity," along with physical acts of spite (the text names them: ink on books, hidden clothes, water at night). Graves's resistance backfired: "violent resistance" "merely encouraged the ragging." He adds the claim that "I only got accustomed to bawdy-talk in my last two years at Charterhouse, and it was not until I had been some time in the army that I got hardened to it and could reply in kind to insults." In the following chapter, his last resort during the worst of the persecution was to "sham insanity," which "succeeded unexpectedly well": "Soon nobody troubled about me except to avoid any contact with me." (The same chapter says his parents' attempt to help, informing the house-master, led to his being treated as an informer.)
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* humor and insult are the same instrument in a closed male group; the victim's discomfort is the audience's entertainment; the skill of "replying in kind" is learned in the army. The stratagem of feigned madness works because the group avoids contact with the unfit.
*Candidate application:* The officer who comes into the army unable to trade insults, and who later can, is a documented progression for one man of one class; the memoir states it as a claim about the army's role, not as a dated scene.

**[E15a-9]. "My New-Bug's Exam": the school initiation interrogation, in school dialect.** (PW-05, ch. VIII, L2061-2148)
Graves quotes a piece he wrote for the 1913 school magazine *Green Chartreuse*. A newcomer's pajamas are "damp with the perspiration of fear" as the "Plead of Under Cubicles" examines first-year boys in the dark by lights-out candlelight, counting "One, two, three...." between a question and the deadline. The questions are deliberately trivial and unanswerable by anyone not already inside the institution (the colors of the House-Master's house, how to reach a town from a house, the age of the horse that "rolls Under Green" (a school playing field)). The answer "Six!" is "Wrong; thirty-eight." The initiate may "miss" three of ten; after eight questions and four misses he is sentenced to appear at the examiner's cubicle at seven the next morning, and the examiner "waved his hair-brush over the candle, and a colossal shadow appeared on the ceiling."
*Mechanism:* a mock-examination whose counting form imitates real tests, with sentence and shadow-theater as the menacing frame. It is a small hazing ritual that tests whether the newcomer will play along. Graves, who arrived at the school in 1909, writes it as a comic piece four years later.
*Candidate application:* The ritual interrogation of a new arrival with arbitrary knowledge questions, a count-down and a mock sentence is a school-level template of the "new man" ritual that later appears in units.

**[E15a-10]. School exits: the headmaster's farewell and a one-line telegram, both in institutional deadpan.** (PW-05, ch. VIII, L2303-2335)
(a) Graves's "last memory" is the headmaster's good-bye: "Well, good-bye, Graves, and remember this, that your best friend is the waste-paper basket." (b) An older boy, the Hon. Desmond O'Brien, "cheerfully disregarded all school rules," carried skeleton keys, lifted a memorandum from the headmaster's desk reading "Must expel O'Brien," and when threatened with the loss of a Saturday half-holiday over a cow killed with a catapult (his own act), sent the headmaster from a family wedding a telegram: "Killed cow sorry coming O'Brien." The next sentences: "At last, having absented himself from every lesson and chapel for three whole days, he was expelled. He was killed early in the war while bombing Bruges."
*Mechanism:* economy and a refusal to elaborate; the telegram is a confession and an apology at once, in a form that could be cabled. The death in the final sentence is given in one clause with no change of tone.
*Candidate application:* The earliest model in the book of the "reply by one line" and of an irreverent officer type who dies in a flat sentence.

**[E15a-11]. How the memoir marks its dead: flat parenthetical or clause-ending deaths, and a statistics paragraph.** (PW-05, ch. IV, L1078-1079, L1145-1147; ch. VIII, L1931-1934, L2278-2281, L2334-2335, L2337-2350)
The dead of school and family are recorded in a single subordinate sentence, usually after a lively anecdote: uncle Siegfried "killed in the war" and body not found; cousin Wilhelm "killed in an air-fight" (he was "shot down by a schoolfellow of mine"); Raymond (the school friend, an atheist and boxer) met in France in 1917 "was killed at Cambrai not long after"; a house-master with a malicious reputation "went into the army and was killed the next year"; O'Brien at Bruges. Then Graves states: "At least one in three of my generation at school was killed," followed by a plain arithmetic paragraph on infantry subalterns: average life "only about three months" at some stages, four wounded to one killed, and of the wounded one seriously and three lightly, who "returned to the front after a few weeks or months of absence and were again subject to the same odds."
*Mechanism:* the memoir's register for named dead is flat, brief and final, with the numbers given in the voice of an actuary. The wit is never turned on the dead themselves in this section.
*Candidate application:* An officer-narrator whose humor is lavish elsewhere and absent at the point of death, and who will state casualty arithmetic without comment, is a consistent register to compare with the other-ranks voice in Frank Richards (E13a/E13b).

**[E15a-12]. The O.T.C. camp and the anti-militarist debate.** (PW-05, ch. VIII, L2283-2301)
In the school's last debate, on compulsory military service, Graves was the principal speaker against the motion; it carried, with "only six votes out of one hundred and nineteen cast against." He had recently resigned from the Officers' Training Corps, "having revolted against the theory of implicit obedience to orders," and had been "frightened" at the summer camp by a display of "barbed-wire entanglements, machine-guns, and field artillery in action"; a general told the boys war with Germany was inevitable and they would lead the new forces. He adds, of the six who voted against: "Nevill Barbour and I are, I believe, the only ones who survived the war."
*Candidate application:* The pre-war mock of the military's implicit obedience, later reversed by joining, is the narrator's own route into the army; the survivor's count is stated, not joked.

### B. Chapter IX: Mallory, climbing and the end of school (L2371-2576)

**[E15a-13]. Mallory and the masters: a school code of license, and a grim joke about two dead house-masters.** (PW-05, ch. IX, L2409-2429, L2446-2450, L2542-2545)
Graves says Charterhouse had "a tradition ... of concealed warfare between the boys and the masters": cheating, lying and deceiving a master was "considered no shame," while doing the same to a schoolfellow "was immoral." The young master George Mallory refused this state of war and was despised for it. When two house-masters who had been unfriendly to him died within a short time of each other, Mallory joked to Graves: "See, Robert, how mine enemies flee before my face" (a psalm-style phrase). Two small climbing details belong to the same register: on a climb Mallory warned Graves not to chip a foothold because "It's got to last another five hundred years at least"; and the president of the Climbers' Club, Geoffrey Young, is said to have had four closest friends killed climbing, which Graves calls "a comment on the extraordinary care with which he always climbed" (Young later lost a leg with a Red Cross unit and climbed on with an artificial one, stated without comment).
*Mechanism and rule of license:* the rule that deception is legitimate against the institution's officers but not against peers is an early statement of an in-group/out-group license that carries into the army. The mock-biblical quip is a joke at the expense of dead men who were disliked, made in private by a friend.
*Candidate application:* The "lie to the authority, never to the group" code is a documented class-and-institution habit of this narrator's cohort; the quip about the dead enemies is a small example of the dead being joked about when the joker has a grievance against them.

### C. Chapter X: the war narrative begins: enlistment, the depot, Lancaster and Wrexham (L2581-3077)

*Scope note: the war narrative begins here, at "I was at Harlech when war was declared" (L2584). The rest of this chapter is the autumn of 1914 and the winter of 1914-15, all in England.*

**[E15a-14]. How the narrator came to the war: expecting a short one, then the newspaper chain (atrocity rumor) he later calls propaganda.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2584-2618)
Graves says he enlisted a day or two after the declaration, partly because a short war "might last just long enough to delay my going to Oxford in October, which I dreaded," and he expected garrison service at home. He says he "entirely believed" France and England had been drawn into an unwanted war and "It never occurred to me that newspapers and statesmen could lie," forgot his pacifism, and wrote a poem promising vengeance for Louvain. To show how far the atrocity stories ran ahead of the facts, he quotes four newspaper cuttings in order, in which a German report of church bells rung after the fall of Antwerp is successively reworked by French and Italian papers into a story of brutal punishment of priests. (The last cutting is a gruesome atrocity story; I summarize and do not reproduce it.)
*Mechanism:* the memoir's irony is created by arrangement: four cuttings, each citing the last, with no comment beyond "I discounted perhaps twenty per cent." The humor is the narrator's retrospective contempt for his own credulity.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-narrator who dates his loss of belief in the official story to a specific chain of rumor can be written as laughing at his earlier self; the memoir treats the rumor chain as the first proof that "official" and "true" differ.

**[E15a-15]. The company mess of officers with German blood, and a deadpan answer about enemy uncles.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2620-2631)
In the trenches "a few months later," Graves belonged to "a company mess in which four of us young officers out of five had ... either German mothers or naturalized German fathers." One of them said he was glad he joined when he did, because otherwise "they'd have accused me of being a German spy," and that his father kept his golf-club membership only "because he has two sons in the trenches." Graves answered: "Well, I have three or four uncles sitting somewhere opposite, and a number of cousins too. One of my uncles is a general. But that's all right. I don't brag about them. I only advertise the uncle who is a British admiral commanding at the Nore."
*Who tells, target, mechanism:* an officers' mess exchange between peers; the target is the suspicion of disloyalty that their own families face at home; the form is a straight-faced reversal ("I only advertise the uncle who...") that treats family on the enemy side as a matter of public-relations management.
*Candidate application:* Officers whose home status is shaky (German names, class marginality) turn the suspicion into an insider's joke at the mess table; the same speaker will later describe enemy relatives killed without any joke (see E15a-16).

**[E15a-16]. The German uncle's Rheims story, and the flat deaths of the cousins on the other side.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2633-2662)
Graves lists enemy-side relatives: cousin Conrad (a gentle naturalist whom he skied with in January 1914, later a holder of the "Pour le Mérite," "killed by the Bolsheviks after the war"), and a sixty-year-old uncle recalled as a Bavarian artillery lieutenant. The uncle, retelling a story to Graves's youngest brother, says a divisional general chose him to dislodge French observers from Rheims Cathedral because he was a Lutheran and not a Catholic; he fired two rounds and "down came the pinnacle and the Frenchmen with it." He called it "a very neat bit of shooting" and "was proud to have limited the damage like that," ending: "Really, you must go and have a look at it." (The general's contempt for the French and the details of the casualties are summarized, not reproduced.)
*Mechanism:* the enemy officer's pride in a proportionate act of destruction, told as a tourist recommendation; the understatement lies in treating a cathedral shelling as a craftsman's tidy job.
*Candidate application:* The memoir shows the opposite side's officer humor as the same pattern as its own (understated pride, matter-of-fact tone); Graves does not comment on the story.

**[E15a-17]. Joining the regiment: the depot's old soldiers and a medal called a record of "undetected crime."** (PW-05, ch. X, L2664-2702)
Graves reports his mother's reaction to his commission ("My race has gone mad," which she nonetheless treated as "a religious act") and his own beginner's mistakes in the officers' mess (saluting the bandmaster, not recognizing the colonel out of uniform, "talking shop in the mess"). He says his greatest difficulty was addressing "men of the company ... with the necessary air of authority," because many were re-enlisted old soldiers and "I disliked bluffing that I knew more than they did." The depot had old soldiers "usually also [wearing] the ribbon of the 'Rooti' or good service medal awarded for eighteen years of undetected crime." The depot's folk figure is "Jackie Barrett, a Kipling character," of whom it was said: "He and his mucking-in chum deserted the regiment in Quetta and went across the north-west frontier on foot. Three months later he gave himself up as a deserter to the British consul at Jerusalem. He buried his chum by the way."
*Mechanism:* the other ranks' idiom redefines the Long Service and Good Conduct medal as proof of not being caught; the Barrett legend is told with a deadpan final clause that turns desertion and a death into a travel anecdote.
*Candidate application:* Veteran enlisted humor in this memoir inverts the army's own moral categories (good conduct = not caught; deserter = long-distance walker), and a new officer is expected to hear it without comment.

**[E15a-18]. Lancaster internment camp: the staff as a gallery of mess types, and the prisoners' and guards' small comedies.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2704-2824)
Graves was on detachment at a camp for enemy aliens (waiters, seamen, band boys and commercial travelers). The details bearing on humor: (a) his guard of fifty Special Reservists, "Welshmen from the border counties," were "constantly deserting," made "nervous sentries" who shouted challenges with five live rounds in the magazine, and broke out through a sewer; one of them, Private Kirby, reported that "on the Sunday after we arrived, all the preachers in Lancaster took as their text, 'Mothers, take care of your daughters; the Royal Welch have come to town.'" (b) A prisoner explained a bloody mouth in broken English; I paraphrase rather than reproduce the dialect rendering. (c) The camp staff are given one-line portraits: a "fatherly colonel"; the secretary, an Oxford boxing coach, who sponsored Graves into the Royal Antediluvian Order of Buffaloes, whose Grand Marshal, after "four or five pints of 'bitter-gatter'" and a glass or two of "juniper" (secret words), "continually short-circuited in the ritual" by returning to one exchange ("Benevolence and joy ever attend us!"); the assistant-commandant who "made wistful jokes about his lost virility" with the recurring line "If you only knew how much that would mean to an old man like me"; an adjutant who, taken off a neutral liner by a German cruiser, had to give his parole not to fight against Germany and so was "forbidden the trenches" for the war; "a doctor who was the mess buffoon," on whose back Graves "broke the scabbard of my sword one night"; and an interpreter who, asked his nationality, would answer "I am Wagon-Lits" (a railway-sleeping-car company; the point is that he belongs only to the traveling trade). The same chapter notes that the commandant worried about the safety of a dozen German band boys among the prisoners, and that in 1915 *The Times* "found very amusing" a released prisoner's complaint that waiters had been taken to the camp handcuffed and fettered under a police escort, which Graves, who signed for them, says "was true."
*Mechanism:* the staff-as-types sketch (each in one line, each with one tag) is a mess-humor form; a ritual (the Buffaloes) is undermined by a drunk officiant; the interpreter's reply turns an official identity into a company's name.
*Candidate application:* Garrison-duty humor is relaxed, ritual-parodying and class-bound; it is the only setting in this part of the memoir where the officers' mess is entirely at ease.

**[E15a-19]. The adjutant's two verdicts, and the boxing match that got Graves sent to France.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2826-2874)
Back at the depot in October, Graves was stale and eager for France. The adjutant, a keen soldier, judged that he dressed badly (a poor tailor, a servant who did not polish his buttons) and that he was "not a sportsman," because on Grand National day he alone volunteered to be orderly officer. The company commander had reported him as "unsoldierlike and a nuisance." Then at a training camp he stepped into the ring with the regimental sergeant and welter-weight contender Johnny Basham, who was clowning for laughs: Graves "pretended that I knew nothing about boxing," Basham "dropped his guard and danced about with a you-can't-hit-me challenge," and Graves knocked him across the ring; "I laughed at him and he laughed too," and Basham "was decent in making it seem that I was a much better boxer than I was." When the adjutant heard, he said that an officer boxing like that "was a great encouragement to the men" and put Graves on the next draft to France.
*Mechanism:* an officer's feigned incompetence used to catch a professional playing the fool; the prize (France) comes through an institution's reading of "sportsmanship," not through anything Graves values. The ranks-and-officer laughing together in the ring is a rare cross-rank moment in this part.
*Candidate application:* The pretense of being worse than you are (a "trap" joke) is a documented device; so is the institution rewarding the wrong trait.

**[E15a-20]. "Glad Eyes" and the flat report of a nicknamed man's death.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2876-2890)
Of the officers sent to France before Graves, several were already dead or wounded. One was Second-Lieutenant W. G. Gladstone, "whom we called Glad Eyes" (a Liberal M.P. and grandson of the Prime Minister, who had spoken against the war, joined as a subaltern, refused a staff post and "took no care of himself"). "He was killed by a sniper when unnecessarily exposing himself. His body was brought home for a military funeral at Hawarden; I attended it." No joke attaches to the death; the nickname is given earlier in the paragraph as an affectionate tag of the mess.
*Mechanism and limit:* a nickname for a living colleague, then a plain clause for the death. The same narrator writes "unnecessarily exposing himself" as a judgment, not as sarcasm.
*Candidate application:* The mess's affectionate nicknames and the narrator's flat death notices belong to different registers in the same paragraph; the nickname is not used again after the death.

**[E15a-21]. "Dirty Williams": a nickname given as a sanction, and a drunk's confession told for effect.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2892-2918)
At Wrexham, the billet's landlord, a solicitor who overcharged the officers while "pretending amicability," had three wigs of progressive lengths and cycled through them to simulate hair growth until a drunken officer raided his room. That officer, Williams, "an extreme example of the sly border Welshman," told Graves drunken stories, one of them a grim joke about a fiancee in Dublin who, the day before the wedding, lost a foot to a tram, with the punch line "she was a lovely, lovely girl before that happened." Graves says Williams also gave Graves's name to women he met in the nearest town, so that the women wrote to Graves. Graves ended it in the mess: "In future you are going to be distinguished from all the other Williams' in the regiment by being called Dirty Williams." "The name stuck." Williams "escaped all trench-service except for a short spell in a quiet sector, and lasted the war out safely."
*Mechanism:* a mess nickname as a public penalty for private misconduct; it works because the group adopts it. The anecdote about the fiancee is the only joke in the passage that rests on a body injury, and it is the drunk's own. (Content handled in restrained terms; see the register.)
*Candidate application:* Naming-as-sanction is a documented officers' mess device; the man it was applied to is the one who avoided the front, and the memoir does not say whether the sanction cost him anything.

**[E15a-22]. Private Robinson and the "red waistcoat": shaming by costume, a song, and the sentence the narrator adds about the singers.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2920-2950; footnote [1] at L12313-12317)
When the whole battalion volunteered for overseas service, Private Robinson, a pre-war Special Reservist from Anglesey, refused ("I have a wife and pigs at home") and could not be "coaxed nor bullied." While the others were fitted in temporary navy blue, "they decided to shame him": he kept wearing the peace-time scarlet tunic and blue trousers, "a very dirty scarlet tunic," on the kitchen staff. His mates called him Cock Robin and sang a popular chorus at him, a line of which runs "In my old red vest I mean to cut a shine." Robinson answered in the same tune:

> For the more they call me Robin Redbreast
> I'll wear it longer still.
> I will wear a red waistcoat, I will,
> I will, I will, I will, I will, I will!

He was discharged in October as medically unfit: "Of under-developed intelligence, unlikely to be of service in His Majesty's Forces." Then the narrator adds the closing sentence: "While, of the singers, those who survived Festubert in the following May did not survive Loos in the following September." A footnote at the end of the book quotes a sixteenth-century jest of a cobbler who will not go to the King's wars: "it is good to sleep in a whole skin."
*Mechanism and what the humor does:* the group's shaming (costume plus song) is answered with a better verse by the shamed man, who wins by being unembarrassable; the story's ending reverses the roles by stating what happened to the mockers. The final sentence is plain, with no comment.
*Candidate application:* This is a documented case in which a refusal is met with ridicule that fails, and the author's closing sentence (the singers are dead) is the entire moral. Compare the sixteenth-century jest that Graves supplies as a precedent: refusal of war as a long folk-comic type.

**[E15a-23]. Orderly Room: pay-night license, the piccolo and the sergeant's bellow, and the nursery-offense trial Graves says his publishers advised him to omit.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2952-2987)
Graves describes Battalion Orderly Room as a four- or five-hour daily process of one "crime" every three or four minutes (desertion, disobedience, obscene language to an N.C.O., drunk and disorderly, robbing a comrade). On pay-nights "there was hardly a man sober; and no attention was paid so long as there was silence" when the company officer made his rounds before Lights Out (the standing license). A boy named Taylor, whom Graves had bought a piccolo, played jigs for the drunks and "gradually quickened it until he worked them into a frenzy," delaying the climax until the orderly-sergeant opened the door and bellowed "F Company, Attention!" at which Taylor hid the piccolo and "the drunks were left frozen in the middle of their capers, blinking stupidly." The narrator also reports hearing, at his first Orderly Room, a private soldier charged with "a nursery offense" on which expert evidence was "given and heard without a smile," and adds: "I have an accurate record of the trial but my publishers advise me not to give it here." (Nothing further is stated, and I do not guess.)
*Mechanism:* the piccolo player stages a joke whose punch line is the institution's own command; the withheld trial is a flagged self-censorship.
*Candidate application:* Pay-night drunkenness tolerated until the officer appears, and a joke staged for the sergeant's entrance, give the repertoire of the barrack-room before the war. The narrator marks where his own book was limited by a publisher, not by him.

**[E15a-24]. Orderly Room comedy continued: the stock four words, a threat in scriptural idiom, Boy Jones's catalog of charges, and the goat-major's charge sheet.** (PW-05, ch. X, L2989-3041)
Graves says Orderly Room "always embarrassed and dispirited me"; "even after sentencing thousands of men myself" it seemed "shameful." The only change the "civilian element" brought, half-way through the war, was an army order replacing "Prisoner and escort" with "Accused and escort." Obscene language, "always quoted verbatim," was "drearily the same," with "four stock words," and he says humor "only came from the very Welsh Welshmen from the hills who had an imperfect command of English." Items: (a) a man charged with threatening a lance-corporal who had a down on him had said to the corporal, "Corporal Smith, two men shall meet before two mountains." (b) A Welshman's tangled complaint about a sergeant's remark on guard duty (I summarize; the quoted version contains the very words charged). (c) Boy Jones, fourteen (looks thirteen), at Liverpool in 1917, faced five charges at once, including obscene language to the bandmaster (who, being squeamish, reported it in euphemism) and "absenting himself from the regiment until apprehended in the Hindenburg Line, France"; he had slipped out of detention, robbed a Bantam-battalion private of his kit and papers, gone to France in his place, deserted that battalion, and walked down the trench lines until he found his own regiment, which sent him straight home. "The punishment for all these offenses was ten days confined to camp and a spanking from the bandmaster." (d) The regimental goat-major, a corporal, was charged (first as lese-majesty, "later reduced to 'disrespect to an officer'"): "in that he, at Wrexham—on such and such a date—did prostitute the Royal Goat, being the gift of His Majesty the Colonel-in-Chief from His royal herd at Windsor, by offering his stud-services to ——, Esq., farmer and goat breeder, of Wrexham." He pleaded kindness to the goat, "to which he was much attached," and was reduced to the ranks.
*Mechanism:* the charge sheet's formal language is itself the joke (a mock-legal diction applied to a goat); the sentences are comically lenient (Boy Jones) or categorical (the goat-major's demotion). The narrator frames all of it as relief from the "drearily the same" routine.
*Candidate application:* Orderly Room offers a repertoire of institutional absurdity with real punishments behind it; the funny ones are the ones where the charge sheet cannot keep up with what the man did.

**[E15a-25]. The North Welsh recruits who "do curse and do swear" about their sergeant-major, and Welsh hymn-singing.** (PW-05, ch. X, L3043-3058; ch. XII, L3478-3481)
Graves says the regular battalions rarely contained "more than about one Welshman in fifty in the ranks" (mainly Birmingham recruits), and that after Lloyd George persuaded the chapels the war was a crusade there was "a sudden tremendous influx of Welshmen from North Wales," who "particularly resented having to stand still while the N.C.O.'s swore at them." A deputation told him: "Captain Graves, sir, we do not like our sergeant-major; he do curse and he do swear, and he do drink, and he is a maan of lowly origin, too." (Period dialect rendering; documented, not endorsed.) Later, in France, he says of a draft that sang Welsh hymns in parts on a night march: "The Welsh always sang when they were a bit frightened and pretending that they were not; it kept them steady. They never sang out of tune."
*Mechanism and rule:* a class-and-chapel complaint made with solemn politeness (the last item, "lowly origin," is the real grievance, ranked after swearing and drink); the hymn claim is the narrator's own generalization about what singing does for frightened men.
*Candidate application:* The memoir records a unit's whole-group coping act (singing in parts) as steadying and as a way of "pretending" calm; this is the narrator's claim, offered of one national group, not a general rule.

**[E15a-26]. The returned wounded officers' reticence, and the unit's accusation that one was "shamming."** (PW-05, ch. X, L3060-3076)
At Wrexham the recruit officers learned drill, musketry and regimental history but "dug no trenches, handled no bombs." Of the only two wounded officers back from Mons, neither would talk much about his experiences. One, Emu Jones, said only that the first queer sight in France was a grisly one in a butcher's shop (a mutilated-bodies image that I do not reproduce). The other said: "The shells knock hell out of a man, especially the big black ones. Just hell." He added that Jones "wasn't any good": "We marched and marched and he had a weak heart and used to faint and expect his poor, bloody platoon to carry him as well as the rest of their load. We used to swear he was shamming. Don't believe what old Emu tells you of the retreat."
*Mechanism and limit:* the veterans give the recruits almost nothing; what they do give is one grim, understated statement and one disparagement of a comrade as a malingerer. This is an early instance of the memoir's pattern in which the reports of the war come as terse remarks, not narrative.
*Candidate application:* Reticence plus mutual accusations of shirking among the returned is a plausible texture for new arrivals to the front; the weak-hearted man's own account is never given, only the other's version.

### D. Chapter XI: regimental culture as a humor-bearing institution (L3081-3359)

**[E15a-27]. The regiment's rivals: sneers, mottoes and a rhyming-slang challenge.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3084-3130)
Graves says he used to congratulate himself on his regiment by contrast with others: the Cheshires had been deprived of the title "Royal" for losing a battle; the Bedfords, though they had made a name in this war, were still called "The Peacemakers" because they had only four battle-honors and none more recent than 1711, and "it was a sneer that their regimental motto was: 'Thou shalt not kill.'" The Black Watch "had a stain on its record" (a lost square), and "If a Tommy of another regiment went into a public bar where men of the Black Watch were drinking, and felt brave enough to start a fight, he would ask the barmaid not for 'pig's ear,' which is rhyming-slang for beer, but for a pint of 'broken square.' Then belts would be unbuckled." The Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders were said to have gone into a battle "with nine hundred men" and come out "with nine hundred and one—no casualties, and a band-boy come of age and promoted a private." The Royal Welch, by contrast, had twenty-nine battle-honors and "a record ... beyond reproach."
*Mechanism:* inter-regimental disparagement by stock insult (a motto, a battle, a drink order that works as a challenge); the target is the rival's worst day, and the jokes are accepted currency in pubs and messes.
*Candidate application:* Rival-unit insults tied to a specific historical failure are a durable genre; one is a fighting word in a bar, and the narrator reports that all ranks knew them.

**[E15a-28]. The Sebastopol order-book: a plain register for burial and an auction of a dead officer's effects.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3132-3152)
In a mess cupboard Graves found the First Battalion's Daily Order-book from Sebastopol (the attack on the Redan): volunteers detailed for a storming party, ladders and ammunition, an "earnest Godspeed," then orders for burial of the dead, thanks for "gallantry vainly displayed," and "a notice that the effects of the late Lieutenant So-and-so, who had led the storming party, would be sold at public auction in the trenches next day." The next day's orders announce a Victoria Cross for a sergeant who lived to be the regiment's colonel. A sketch above his head showed the commanding officer sick in his tent "wearing a cap-comforter for the cold."
*Mechanism and limit:* institutional prose that moves from heroism to administration in a line. The memoir does not joke about the notices; the narrator says this was the moment he "caught the sense of regimental tradition."
*Candidate application:* A unit's administrative record as its own gallows-prose: the sale of a dead man's effects "in the trenches next day" is a documented routine that officers inherit.

**[E15a-29]. The flash: a regimental privilege defended against the Army Council, and a "shears" mission to a rival battalion.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3154-3223)
Graves explains that the Royal Welch alone wear the "flash," five black ribbons stitched to the back of the collar, said to commemorate the 1830s regiment's missing the order that abolished the queue; the colonel rode to London and won the privilege from King William IV. The Army Council, "usually composed of cavalry, engineer, and artillery generals," refused to sanction it on khaki and argued that it was "a distinctive mark for enemy marksmen." The regiment retorted "by inquiring on what occasion since the retreat from Corunna ... had any of His Majesty's enemies seen the back of a Royal Welch Fusilier?" The matter "was in abeyance throughout the war." In 1917, when a young officer of Graves's company was decorated, the King, as colonel-in-chief, had him do an about turn, looked at the flash, and said "Good, you're still wearing it, I see," then "in a stage whisper: 'Don't ever let anyone take it from you.' The regiment was delighted." After the war the Council sanctioned it, and also the spelling "Welch" with a "c," which "was as important to us as the miniature cap-badge worn at the back of the cap was to the Gloucesters"; Graves says he has seen a young officer sent off battalion parade because his buttons read "Welsh instead of Welch." Rumors that officers of the Eighteenth Battalion wore flashes "nearly down to their waists" caused "great consternation," and the adjutant sent the youngest subaltern to its Orderly Room "with a large pair of shears."
*Mechanism and what the custom does:* a minute sartorial and orthographic privilege is guarded as a matter of honor, in open defiance of the Army Council; the retort about enemies never seeing the regiment's back is a witty answer that turns the safety objection into a boast. The "shears" errand is a joke at the expense of an outsider battalion that cannot be corrected by anything but a comic enforcement.
*Candidate application:* Regimental distinctions defended with mock-seriousness against the central authority are a documented basis for in-group cohesion and for contempt toward newer or looser units.

**[E15a-30]. The Albuhera mess ritual, the Thomas Atkins legend, St. David's Night, and the Royal Goat.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3225-3262)
(a) In France a major of the Royal Fusiliers entered the mess of the Nineteenth (Bantam) Battalion of the Royal Welch Fusiliers, called for a drink, and asked "Do you know why I ordered that drink from the mess sergeant?" The senior officer answered, "Yes, you wanted to see if we remembered about Albuhera." The major: "Well, our mess is just along behind that wood there. We haven't forgotten either." After Albuhera (my gloss: the 1811 Peninsular War battle) the survivors of the two regiments had eaten together on the captured hill, and the regiments are honorary members of each other's messes forever. (b) A legend that a private of the First Battalion who served under Wellington, "Thomas Atkins," gave the generic name for the British soldier on the attestation form; Graves admits he cannot recall the deed that earned it: "And so here ends my very creditable (after eleven years) lyrical passage." (c) He lists the St. David's Night customs: raw leeks eaten "to the roll of the drum with one foot on a chair and one on the mess table," the Royal Goat "with gilded horns that once leapt over the mess table with the drummerboy on its back," and others he does not unpack here.
*Mechanism:* a ritual of allusion (a drink ordered to test a memory); self-mocking narrator breaking off a "lyrical passage." The customs named (leek-eating, a goat leaping the table) are traditions with a ceremonial-comic function.
*Candidate application:* The memoir shows how long, comic, allusive regimental rituals act as proof of membership for officers and men; I have not extracted their full description because it is not given in this part.

**[E15a-31]. "Dog-shooters": the regiment's contempt for the territorials, and the machine-gun spare-parts boxes.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3264-3292)
Graves says the war's swelling of the regiment weakened its spirit; the two line battalions accepted new-army battalions "one by one ... as they proved themselves worthy" but "the territorials it never accepted, disowning them contemptuously as 'dog-shooters.'" The reasons he gives are two failures: three of the four territorial battalions "failed signally in the Suvla Bay landing" (one "had offered violence to its officers; the commanding officer, a regular, had not cared to survive that day"), and the fourth "quite unnecessarily lost its machine-guns" at Givenchy. The machine-gun officer "had congratulated himself on removing the machine-gun bolts before abandoning the guns; it would make them useless to the enemy. But he had forgotten to take away the boxes of spare-parts," and a year and a half later the Second Battalion recaptured one of the guns "which had been busy against the British trenches ever since."
*Mechanism and limit:* the nickname is a class-and-competence insult; the machine-gun story is told as a dry joke with a grim sequel. The mutiny-and-suicide hint is stated in a subordinate clause and not elaborated.
*Candidate application:* The regular-versus-territorial contempt is a documented feature of regimental culture; the narrator reports it as received opinion ("it was known"), not as personal knowledge.

**[E15a-32]. Special Reserve officers told not to expect decorations, and the narrator's own flat count.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3294-3330)
Special Reserve recruits were told they did not belong to "the 'regiment' in the special sense" (which formerly required Sandhurst, two recommendations and a private income to "play polo and hunt"), and were warned they would not be recommended for orders or decorations, which "were not regarded in the regiment as personal awards, but as representative awards for the whole battalion" and so "reserved for the professional soldiers." Graves says this held: of perhaps two or three hundred Special Reserve officers overseas, with "about sixty or seventy killed," awards should have been "at least ten times that amount." He adds: "I myself never performed any feat for which I might conceivably have been decorated throughout my service in France."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's statement is plain, with no joke; the humor is the institution's own rationing logic (and the adjutant's "not more than two names" in E15a-38 shows the same logic in action).
*Candidate application:* Honors treated as a regimental quota, and the narrator's refusal to claim a feat for himself, are a documented officer-class stance toward decoration.

**[E15a-33]. Regimental spirit survives the annihilations, and the men know Minden better than the war's causes.** (PW-05, ch. XI, L3332-3359)
Graves reports that his First Battalion was "annihilated within two months of joining the British Expeditionary Force" and that "young Orme," fresh from Sandhurst, found himself commanding about forty rifles at First Ypres and "was himself killed"; the reconstituted battalion was nearly annihilated again at the Aubers Ridge and Festubert and again at Loos, "the one officer-survivor of the attack" being a machine-gun officer loaned from another regiment. He estimates fifteen or twenty thousand men passed through each line battalion whose fighting strength "was never more than eight hundred." Then: "In the First and Second Battalions throughout the war it was not merely the officers and non-commissioned officers who knew their regimental history. The men knew far more about Minden and Albuhera and Waterloo than they did about the fighting on the other fronts or the official causes of the war."
*Narrator's claim about what the tradition does:* when the unit is repeatedly destroyed, identity is kept by the traditions; it replaces both the cause and the other fronts.
*Candidate application:* A cohesion mechanism running on inherited ritual and battle-honor rather than on cause, attested here for one regular regiment by an officer of the Special Reserve.

### E. Chapter XII: arrival in France and the first days in the trenches (L3364-3877)

*Scope note:* the front-line narrative begins at chapter XII (L3364), which opens with Graves's statement that in 1916, on leave after being wounded on the Somme, he began an account of his first months in France, "wrote it as a novel," and now has "to retranslate it into history"; he gives "one reconstituted chapter."

**[E15a-34]. Harfleur and Le Havre: a base-camp comedy of touts, and the disappointment at being attached to a line regiment not their own.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3372-3384)
At the base camp the six new Royal Welch officers did route-marches and dock fatigues. Little boys accosted them offering their sisters, in pidgin English (I summarize and do not reproduce the lines). "We were glad when we got orders to go up the line. But disgusted to find ourselves attached not to the Royal Welch Fusiliers, but to the Welsh Regiment." The memoir treats the attachment as a regimental grievance from the first day.
*Candidate application:* The first comedy of the front is the base, and the first grievance is regimental.

**[E15a-35]. "The Ups and Downs": a regimental nickname that describes the unit's state, and the communiqué epitaph.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3386-3423)
The Second Battalion, Welsh Regiment (the old Sixty-ninth Foot), had the army nickname "The Ups and Downs," "partly because 69 is a number which makes the same sense whichever way up it is written. The 69 was certainly upside-down when we joined it." Its officers had been attached from seven other regiments; the quartermaster was from the Connaught Rangers; few time-serving N.C.O.'s remained; most men had under two months' training, some three weeks; the battalion had "in eight months lost its full fighting strength five times over." The division's disaster of 9 May is recorded in Graves's quotation of the official communiqué: "Meeting with considerable opposition in the direction of the Rue du Bois, our attacks were not pressed." He calls it "one of the worst disasters of the early part of the war" and the communiqué the division's "epitaph." The reserve of the depot was exhausted, "recruits ... over-age or under-age ... or men who had some slight physical disability," drawn by a Cardiff advertisement: "Enlist at the depot and get to France quick."
*Mechanism:* a palindrome-numeral nickname extended by the narrator into a pun on the unit's condition; the communiqué is the model of institutional understatement over a massacre; the slogan is grimly ironic (the quicker you get to France, the quicker you are at risk).
*Candidate application:* The official wording of a catastrophic failure ("our attacks were not pressed") is a documented object of later mockery by those who were there; the narrator quotes it and calls it an epitaph and does not add a joke.

**[E15a-36]. The old and the young in Graves's first platoon: Burford's rifle, Bumford's sleeping, and the hearsay of the dead.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3423-3462)
Graves still has the roll of his first platoon of forty. Fourteen gave their age as forty or over (and "these were not all"), and several had understated even that; James Burford, collier and fitter, when asked about the safety catch, said: "Excuse me, sir, will you explain what this here arrangement is on the side of my rifle?" He had last fired a rifle "in Egypt in 1882" and was sixty-three, "an old soldier when I was in Egypt." He spent summers as a tramp, and Graves overheard him and another old collier "talking about the different seams of coals in Wales ... one of the most informative conversations I ever heard." On the young side, William Bumford, a collier who gave his age as eighteen "was really only fifteen." He "used to get into trouble for falling asleep on sentry duty. The official penalty for this was death, but I had observed that he could not help it": Graves "had seen him suddenly go to sleep, on his feet, while holding a sandbag open for another fellow to fill," and got him a job as chaplain's orderly. Both were later combed out to the base; "neither escaped the war": Bumford returned and was killed, Burford was "killed, too, in a bombing accident at the base-camp." The narrator adds: "Or so I was told—the fate of many of my comrades in France has come to me merely as hearsay."
*Mechanism and limit:* the old man's question is a dry joke on the army's training system; the two deaths are told in one flat sentence. The mention that the sentry-sleeping penalty was death and that the officer arranged around it shows the narrator's mercy and the limits of the system's rules, without comment.
*Candidate application:* An officer's quiet circumvention of a lethal rule for a boy who "could not help it" is a documented kind of protection; the memoir does not make it a story.

**[E15a-37]. The first night march: the hymn broken by a salvo, the mock letter, the "musical instruments" and the standing sergeant.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3464-3510)
On the night march from Béthune the draft sang Welsh hymns in parts; a salvo of four shells from a battery 200 yards away "broke up *Aberystwyth* in the middle of a verse," the column tangled for a few seconds, and then "The men picked up their step again and began chaffing." A lance-corporal dictated a mock letter home: "Dear auntie, this leaves me in the pink. We are at present wading in blood up to our necks. Send me fags and a lifebelt. This war is a booger. Love and kisses." Soon after, a German shell burst twenty yards off; everyone fell flat; as pieces of shell-casing came down the sergeant said "They calls them the musical instruments." Frank Jones-Bateman, who "had a cut in his hand from a jagged little piece," said "Damn them, the devils have started on me early," and the sergeant answered, grinning, "Aye, they'll have a lot of fun with you before they're done, sir." When another shell burst two hundred yards behind them, only Sergeant Jones "had remained on his feet and laughed at us," and told the draft: "You're wasting yourselves, lads. Listen by the noise they make coming where they're going to burst."
*Mechanisms:* (a) the mock letter is a parody of the "leaves me in the pink" formula of soldiers' letters, with an exaggerated gore line and a request for a lifebelt, released as nervous banter immediately after the first shells; (b) the sergeant's coined euphemism for shell fragments; (c) the sergeant's grin at the young officer's first wound, a teasing prediction that is also a hazing; (d) the teaching joke delivered while standing.
*Candidate application:* The first exposure of a draft produces a recognizable cluster of humor: a parody letter, a nickname for the danger, a veteran's amused prediction to a new officer, and a lesson coded as a laugh; none of it is directed at the dead.

**[E15a-38]. Cambrin: early gas respirators, mice in the trench, and the recruit who asked "where's the battle?"** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3512-3566)
Graves reports that the first respirator in France, a gauze pad of chemically treated cotton waste tied over the nose and mouth, "It seems it was useless against German gas. I never put it to the test," and that the "smoke-helmet" that followed leaked at the talc. He keeps his torch pointed at the trench floor until he realizes that "hundreds of field mice and frogs" had fallen in and could not get out and "we could not help treading on them. So I put the torch back in my pocket." He recalls that a young soldier "a week or two later" called out to the old man cooking stew: "Hi, mate, where's the battle? I want to do my bit." In the trench the guide intones hazards ("Hole right." "Wire high." "Wire low."). Graves adds his own lore on danger: rifle fire "more trying than shell-fire" because "a rifle bullet even when fired blindly always had the effect of seeming aimed," and "we learned not to duck to a rifle bullet, because once it was heard it must have missed."
*Mechanism and limit:* the recruit's question is the standard greenhorn joke (compare the officer's "only a ricochet" in E11a-6); the rest of the passage is plain observation. The lore about not ducking is taught by experience.
*Candidate application:* The greenhorn's "where's the battle?" is a documented new-arrival joke; the narrator gives his own rule about not ducking as a piece of knowledge rather than as a joke.

**[E15a-39]. Battalion headquarters: a comfortable dug-out, a local armistice with the French predecessors, and the D.C.M. quota.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3568-3590)
The colonel, "a twice-wounded regular," offers whisky and hopes the newcomers will grow to like the regiment as much as their own. The dug-out has an ornamental lamp, polished silver, wallpaper, spring mattresses, a gramophone: "It was hard to reconcile this with accounts I had read of troops standing waist-deep in mud." The sector had been taken over from the French, "a territorial division of men in the forties who had a local armistice with the Germans opposite; there was no firing and apparently even civilian traffic through the lines." Posting him to C Company, the adjutant says: "Captain Dunn is your company commander. The soundest officer in the battalion. By the way, remind him that I want that list of D.C.M. recommendations for the last show sent in at once, but not more than two names, or else they won't give us any. Four is about the ration for the battalion in a dud show."
*Mechanism:* "ration" applied to gallantry awards, "dud show" for a failed attack, said as administrative routine. The local armistice reveals how little the opposing lines at this point shared of the later war's temper.
*Candidate application:* Bureaucratic quota talk about decorations, delivered in the supply-clerk's vocabulary, is a documented officer-level voice; it sits next to the memoir's statement (E15a-32) that decorations were reserved for regulars.

**[E15a-40]. The stretcher party and Sergeant Gallagher: callous economy of speech around a death by accident.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3601-3615)
On the way up, Graves's party met a stretcher; the guide asked the stretcher-bearer Dai who it was. Dai explained, in heavily oathed Welsh-English, that Sergeant Gallagher had thought he saw a German in No Man's Land, fired one of the new-issue percussion bombs, aimed too low, and the bomb hit the parapet and burst back on him; the speaker repeatedly called him "silly" and said he was "not worth sweating to get him back" and "put paid to." The wounded man "had a sandbag over his face" and "was dead when they got him back to the dressing-station." (The description of the injury, and the oaths, are summarized, not reproduced; see the register.)
*Mechanism and what it shows:* the other ranks' speech about a man killed by his own bomb is contemptuous and economical; the narrator reports it without comment, immediately after meeting men who did not salute and a fatigue party "cursing plaintively." This is the other-ranks voice in the memoir at its harshest.
*Candidate application:* Contempt for the careless, voiced about a man who is dying, is documented here in an other-rank voice; it is not softened, and the reader is left to judge.

**[E15a-41]. "A Christmas-tree": the officer's equipment, nicknamed.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3616-3623)
Graves, tired and sticky with clay, lists what hung from his belt: "revolver, field-glasses, compass, whisky-flask, wire-cutters, periscope, and a lot more. A Christmas-tree that was called." In a parenthesis he notes that officers had still been going out to France with swords and having them sharpened by the armorer, and that he was advised to leave his sword in the billet, "I never saw it again or bothered about it."
*Mechanism:* a nickname by resemblance (decorations hung on a tree) that the army applied to overloaded officers; the sword anecdote is a small joke on pre-war doctrine.
*Candidate application:* A one-line piece of jargon, a nickname by visual resemblance for the officer's burden.

**[E15a-42]. Captain Dunn's company mess: "only survivors," the mocking introductions, and "who's winning?"** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3625-3649; footnotes [2], [3] at L12319-12322)
The company commander, Dunn, was "two months younger than myself" and "one of the fellowship of 'only survivors.'" Graves gives a parallel case: Captain Miller of the Black Watch, who escaped the Rue du Bois "massacre by swimming down a flooded trench" and "has carried on his surviving trade ever since": "Only survivors have great reputations. Miller used to be pointed at in the streets when the battalion was back in reserve billets. 'See that fellow. That's Jock Miller. Out from the start and hasn't got it yet.'" (A footnote written in 1916 says, "I do not know what happened to Miller.") Dunn "had not let the war affect his morale at all" and greeted Graves with questions and teasing introductions: "Oh sorry, first I must introduce you. This is Walker—clever chap, comes from Cambridge and fancies himself as an athlete. This is Jenkins, one of those patriotic chaps who chucked up his job to come here. This is Price, who only joined us yesterday, but we like him; he brought some damn good whisky with him. Well, how long is the war going to last and who's winning? We don't know a thing out here." (A footnote adds, "Jenkins was killed not long after.")
*Mechanism:* the mess's introductions are small mocking portraits, delivered with affection; "Out from the start and hasn't got it yet" is the soldiers' idiom for a man whose survival is itself a status and whose luck is expected to end. The joke "who's winning?" treats the front's ignorance of the war as a gag.
*Candidate application:* Survival as a celebrity status ("hasn't got it yet"), and the practice of introducing newcomers by mock-faults, are both documented here; the footnote that the mocked man was killed "not long after" is another example of the memoir's habit of adding the death after the joke.

**[E15a-43]. Dunn on the territorials, and on how to handle Welshmen.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3651-3700)
Dunn gives Graves a summary of trench lore: the French will not share their inventions, the British "didn't think of [trenches] as places to live in, they were just temporary inconveniences," and now work all the time. His contempt is for a relieving territorial battalion: they sat down in the trench and said "Oh my God, this is the limit," wrote home about it, did no work on traverses or fire positions, "lost half their men from frost-bite and rheumatism," and one day "the Germans broke in and scuppered a lot more of them," and left the place "like a sewage-farm," and ignored latrines and food, which "encouraged rats"; a steel loophole they built "facing square to the front and quite unmasked" killed two of them: "absolute death-trap." He says "Awful chaps those territorial swine" and blames "Slack officers, of course." Of his own men: "These Welshmen are peculiar. They won't stand being shouted at. They'll do anything if you explain the reason for it. They will do and die, but they have to know their reason why. The best way to make them behave is not to give them too much time to think. Work them off their feet."
*Mechanism and limit:* the territorial contempt is a recurrent regimental theme (E15a-31) with the real reasons given (work discipline, health, death-trap loophole); the generalization about "Welshmen" is a regular officer's lore about management, not a joke, and is a stereotype held by an Anglo-Welsh officer class.
*Candidate application:* A company commander's practical doctrine of how to command a particular national stock, offered at the first meeting, is a documented officer-class habit; the memoir records it without endorsing or disputing it.

**[E15a-44]. The "Human Maxim": a new man's grief turned into noise, a nickname, and a corporal who could not stop laughing.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3687-3722)
Walker reports to Dunn that a man from the new draft, who had never fired the musketry course, fired live rounds for the first time that night: "He'd had a brother killed up at Ypres and he said he was going to avenge him. So he blazed off all his own ammunition at nothing, and two bandoliers out of the ammunition-box besides. They call him the Human Maxim now. His foresight's misty with heat. Corporal Parry should have stopped him; but he was just leaning up against the traverse and shrieking with laughter." Other new men joined in; the Germans answered with machine-guns and shells: "No casualties. I don't know why."
*Mechanism and what the register does:* a nickname by a machine-gun comparison; an officer's amused report of a waste of ammunition; the corporal's helpless laughter at a man who acts out his brother's death. The brother's death is stated once, plainly, as the reason.
*Candidate application:* The first-night nickname and the corporal's laughter show how a unit converts a recruit's raw grief into comic currency within hours; the grief itself is never joked about, only its noisy expression.

**[E15a-45]. First watch: the sentry who coaches the new officer, and the plain register of fear.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3757-3772)
Left in charge of the line, Graves looks over the parapet and sees the bushes and pickets seem to move. The sentry, Beaumont, says: "Aye, they do play queer tricks. Is this your first spell in trenches, sir?" When a flare drops behind the trench and Graves flinches, Beaumont says "It's bad to do that, sir," as a bullet cracks between them, and "Keep still, sir, and they can't spot you." The sentry's last remark is plain, not comic: a flare is "a bad thing to have fall on you. I've seen them burn a hole in a man." Graves spends the rest of his watch learning the trench maze, loses his way twice among the neighboring battalion, and falls into deep mud.
*Mechanism and limit:* the old hand teaches the officer by understatement and a note of danger, with no mockery; the narrator reports his own mistakes (the flinch, the mud) with equal flatness.
*Candidate application:* A sentry's quiet correction of an officer on his first night is a documented rank-crossing kindness that sits beside, and does not replace, the humor of the same night.

**[E15a-46]. The suicide at stand-to, and the officer's instruction to write the next-of-kin the "usual sort of letter."** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3782-3801)
At dawn stand-to Graves finds a man lying in a machine-gun shelter who does not answer; the machine-gunner beside him says "No good talking to him, sir." It is the first corpse Graves sees in France and a suicide (the method and the injury are described in the text and I do not reproduce them). The man belonged to the neighboring Munsters, had been in "the last push," and had received bad news from home about his girl. One of two Irish officers who come up says "We've had two or three of these lately," then tells the other: "While I remember, Callaghan, don't forget to write to his next-of-kin. Usual sort of letter, cheer them up, tell them he died a soldier's death, anything you like. I'm not going to report it as suicide."
*Mechanism and what the register does:* no joke is made; the dark note is the officer's instruction, in the brisk tone of a memo ("While I remember"), to substitute a standard consoling letter for the truth. The phrase "usual sort of letter" shows the formula already exists.
*Candidate application:* The institutional euphemism for a death (a "soldier's death") and the officers' decision to conceal a suicide to spare the family is a documented practice, told without comment; it is where the civilian-facing version departs from the event.

**[E15a-47]. Lice as "a standing joke": the funeral logic, and the answer that made Graves's platoon accept him.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3817-3835)
In the easy time between stand-to and breakfast the men "sat about talking and smoking, writing letters home, cleaning their rifles, running their thumb-nails up the seams of their shirts to kill the lice, gambling." "Lice were a standing joke." Young Bumford (the fifteen-year-old of E15a-36) handed Graves a louse and put an argument to him: "We was just having an argument as to whether it was best to kill the old ones or the young ones, sir. Morgan here says that if you kill the old ones, the young ones will die of grief, but Parry here, sir, he says that the young ones are easier to kill and you can catch the old ones when they come to the funeral." Appealed to as an arbiter ("You've been to college, sir, haven't you?"), Graves answered: "Yes, I had, but so had Crawshay Bailey's brother Norwich." Graves explains that "Crawshay Bailey is one of the idiotic songs of Wales" (in which the brother "was sent to Cardiff College, for to get a bit of knowledge"), and that the reply "was held to be a wonderfully witty answer." "After that I had no trouble with the platoon at all."
*Mechanism and narrator's claim:* a mock-parliamentary dispute over killing lice, using funeral and grief as the terms of the argument, put to the new officer as a test; he passes by answering from the men's own song and not from his education. The narrator states the consequence outright: it settled his standing with the platoon.
*Candidate application:* A new officer's acceptance can turn on a single reply that shows he knows the men's idiom (here a regional comic song); the lice-as-family joke parallels the lice-as-army and louse-hunting jokes in the other-ranks voice (compare Frank Richards, E13a-32; the Anzac Book's "chase" poem, E11a-15).

**[E15a-48]. Company breakfast as "board-meeting," and the gas alarm answered with a request for marmalade.** (PW-05, ch. XII, L3837-3877)
Graves is struck that Dunn settles the day's questions at meals "by a sort of board-meeting with Dunn as chairman" (unlike company commanders in England who did not take juniors "into their confidence"); the first debate is how to keep sentries awake, and Dunn decides to forbid them to lean on the traverse, since a firing flash from the same place lets the Germans "fix a rifle on the spot." He treats Graves's account of a near-miss as probably "a fixed rifle" of the Bavarian Guards Reserve, who "had complete control of the sniping situation." Then Dunn's servant "rushing in, his eyes blank with horror and excitement: 'Gas, sir, gas! They're using gas.'" "'My God!' said Price. We all looked at Dunn. He said imperturbably: 'Very well, Kingdom, bring me my respirator from the other room, and another pot of marmalade.'" Graves explains the alarm "originated with smoke from the German trenches where breakfast was also going on; we knew the German meal-times by a slackening down of rifle-fire." He adds: "Gas was a nightmare. Nobody believed in the efficacy of the respirators," and that "pink army forms marked 'Urgent'" kept arriving with instructions that "were all contradictory": soaking wet, then dry, then in a satchel, then the satchel not to be used. A boy of nineteen, Frank Jones-Bateman (nickname "Silent Night"), visited and "mentioned with a false ease that he had shot a man just before breakfast: 'Sights at four hundred.'"
*Mechanism:* Dunn's reply is imperturbability as a performance for his juniors (a model for the new officer); the "pink forms" are a joke on headquarters' ability to issue contradictory orders on a terror; the narrator's phrase "false ease" marks where the speaker's own humor is a cover.
*Candidate application:* The company commander's studied calm at the first false alarm, and the narrator's view that the junior officer's flat talk of a kill is "false ease," are documented officer-level behaviors; the officer's calm also sets the unit's tone.

### F. Chapter XIII: letters home, 21 May to 6 June 1915 (L3882-4152)

*Scope note:* Graves introduces the chapter with "Here are extracts from letters that I wrote at this time." His range of entries runs from 21 May to 6 June; my range ends at L4152, inside the 6 June entry.

**[E15a-49]. Letters of 21-23 May 1915: the kit list, the staff car, a pun, the old man who remembers the C.O. "in long skirts," and the Finnigan song.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L3885-4002)
Graves quotes his letters home. (a) Of poplars lopped off by shells he says "it was fun to see the poplar trees being lopped down like tulips." (b) He lists the soldier's "minimum load" (a list of thirty-four lines, including "Rounds ammunition 150") as weighing "about sixty pounds," before extras. (c) A staff officer in a Rolls-Royce "cursed us for bad march-discipline I felt like throwing something at him. Trench soldiers hate the staff and the staff know it." (d) During the French bombardment at Souchez a thunderstorm occurred in the middle; Walker, a fellow officer, said "Where the gunder ended and the thunder began was hard to say." (e) Old Burford "is so old that he refuses to sleep with the other men of the platoon" and sleeps on the fire-step in the rain; "he remembers the C.O. when he was in long skirts"; the platoon rags young Bumford for "childish simplicity" and "Bumford plays up to it and begs them not to be too hard on 'a lad from the hills.'" (f) On the march home from a night working party the men sang a song about Company Quartermaster-Sergeant Finnigan:

> Coolness under fire,
> Coolness under fire,
> Mentioned in dispatches
> For pinching the company rations,
> Coolness under fire.
>
> Now he's on the peg,
> Now he's on the peg,
> Mentioned in dispatches
> For drinking the company rum,
> Now he's on the peg.

with a nonsense chorus: "Whiter than the milky cokernuts, ... Wash me in the water / That you washed your dirty daughter in / And I shall be whiter than the milky cokernuts. / Nuts, / Nuts, / Oooooh nuts." Graves adds: "Finnigan doesn't mind the libel at all." (My gloss, not in the text: "on the peg" appears to mean under punishment; the chorus appears to parody a revival-hymn line about being washed "whiter than snow.")
*Mechanism and what the song does:* a satirical commendation of a quartermaster-sergeant for the very thefts of ration and rum, sung by the men as they come home exhausted; the target is named, tolerates it, and the song is a form of license ("the libel"). Walker's pun is officers' mess wit about a bombardment.
*Candidate application:* A named N.C.O. who is the butt of a unit song and "doesn't mind" is a documented sign of tolerated license; quartermaster figures appear twice in this part with opposite valence: Old Joe (E15a-2) is admired for his dry answer; Finnigan is lampooned for pilfering.

**[E15a-50]. The two miners' formal "report" about their sergeant-major, told from another company in the letter of 23 May.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4004-4023)
Graves relays an incident from another company: two young miners who disliked a sergeant "who had a down on them and gave them all the most dirty and dangerous jobs" decided to kill him; they then went to Battalion Orderly Room, saluted smartly, and told the adjutant they were "very sorry but we've shot our company sergeant-major," explained it as "an accident," and, asked whether they had mistaken him for a German, answered: "No, sir, we mistook him for our platoon sergeant." They were executed by a firing squad of their own company at Béthune; their last words were the battalion rallying-cry, "Stick it, the Welsh!"; and the French military governor made a speech "saying how gloriously British soldiers can die." (The killing and the execution are summarized in restrained terms here; the narrator's account is brief and unadorned.)
*Mechanism:* the confession in the form of a formal military report ("we've come to report, sir ... an accident") is the joke; the story's mood is the telling, not the outcome. Graves does not comment on the story, the execution, or the governor's speech.
*Candidate application:* The story is attributed by hearsay to another company; the narrator reproduces its dry formal register and the "glorious death" speech with equal flatness. The same pattern (humor in the report; plain in the outcome) will recur; note that the details are retold second-hand and may be embellished.

**[E15a-51]. "Waste" in the trenches: machine-gun tea.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4025-4033)
Graves says "ration biscuits are in general use as fuel for boiling up dixies," and that machine-gun crews boil water in their water-cooled guns "by firing off belt after belt of machine-gun ammunition at no particular target, just generally spraying the German line." "They say they make German ration and carrying parties behind the line pay for their early-morning cup of tea. But the real charge will be on income-tax after the war."
*Mechanism:* the cost of killing recast as a household bill, with the last sentence turning the real cost into the taxpayer's. The killing is a by-product of making tea and is joked about as such.
*Candidate application:* A documented example of a war's lethal routine being named in the idiom of a household economy; the narrator reports it as something "they say," not as his own act.

**[E15a-52]. The "cushy one": the old hands' wish for a wound, two stories told by the servant Fry, Haking's tears, and a sergeant's remark.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4035-4081)
(a) "The men are pessimistic but cheerful." They talk of getting a "cushy" one to send them back to "Blitey," which Graves glosses as "Hindustani for 'home.'" (my gloss: the usual spelling is "Blighty"). (b) Graves's servant Fry, a Cardiff paper-bag factory worker, tells two stories of men who wanted a cushy one. In the first, a man in the Munsters waves his hand, arms and legs above the parapet to draw a shot, gets none, concludes there is no one there ("Where's the German army to?"), looks over and is shot in the head; "Finee." In the second, a man in the Camerons loses a trigger finger and two more, goes "laughing through our lines" ("I'm aff to bony Scotland. Is it na a beauty?"), and on the way to the dressing-station forgets to stoop where the sniper was working and is also shot through the head. Fry ends: "We laugh fit to die." (The wording of the two men's deaths is summarized, not reproduced.) (c) Graves explains: "To get a cushy one is all that the old hands think of," only twelve men remain from the battalion's start, the old hands "don't believe in the war, they don't believe in the staff," but "they would follow their officers anywhere, because the officers happen to be a decent lot"; they look forward to a battle because it gives more chances of a cushy one "in the legs or arms," while in trench warfare "the proportion of head wounds is much greater." (d) General Haking, who wrote the standard textbook *Company Training*, "came round this morning to an informal inspection of the battalion and shook hands with the survivors. There were tears in his eyes." Sergeant Smith "swore half-aloud: 'Bloody lot of use that is, busts up his bloody division and then weeps over what's bloody left.'" Graves: "I didn't allow myself to feel either for the general or for the sergeant." He judges that the division "will fight all right but without any enthusiasm" and that "no one will mind smashing up over and over again the divisions that are used to being smashed up."
*Mechanism and what the humor does:* the stories turn the wished-for wound into a trap in which the man's wish is answered with a worse injury; "Finee" and the final line show the narrator and his servant laughing at the reversal. The sergeant's remark answers a general's weeping with contempt in a single sentence. Graves records the old hands' wish as a morale fact: it names the odds the men are working to, and is not a joke at the wounded in the first place.
*Candidate application:* The cushy-one wish (and its comic cautionary tales) is also attested in the other-ranks voice (Frank Richards, wound-as-prize, E13a-24, E13a-29). The officer-narrator's own stance toward both general and sergeant, refusing to feel, is a self-protective register, stated outright.

**[E15a-53]. Cuinchy brick-stacks, 28 May: "always joking," the rifle-grenade that "stood there looking at me," the sergeant-major's "pigeon-shooting," and joking with a dead man's arm.** (PW-05, ch. XIII, L4083-4147)
(a) The trenches around the brick-stacks "have made themselves rather than been made"; the Germans hold half the stacks, the British the other half; one trench's parapet "is built up with ammunition-boxes and corpses" (not elaborated here). (b) A rifle-grenade landed within six feet of Graves and, instead of exploding, "landed with its stick in the wet clay and stood there looking at me." (c) About ten casualties in the company that day were from German "sausage" mortar bombs; Graves says "I find that I have extraordinarily quick reactions to danger; but every one gets like that," and that the men "are much afraid, yet always joking": the company sergeant-major "stands behind Number Eleven brick-stack and shoots at the sausages with a rifle as they come over; trying to explode them in the air. He says that it's better than pigeon-shooting. He hasn't hit one yet." (d) After a shell burst overhead, Graves, his ears singing and his sense of balance gone, "was much ashamed when the sergeant-major came along the trench and found me on all fours, because I couldn't stand up straight. It was at a place where 'Petticoat Lane' runs into 'Lowndes Square.'" (e) A sanitary man killed in the open the night before lay on the fire-step waiting for burial: his arm was stiff and stretched across the trench; "His comrades joke as they push it out of the way to get by," or "shake hands with him familiarly. 'Put it there, Billy Boy.'" (The comrades' first remark is a mild oath; I quote only the second.) Graves explains: "Of course, they're miners and accustomed to death. They have a very limited morality, but they keep to it. They will, for instance, rob anyone, of anything, except a man in their own platoon; they will treat every stranger as an enemy until he is proved their friend, and then there is nothing they won't do for him." (f) A lance-corporal's letter to his wife, which Graves censored, said that French girls were nice to sleep with, so she should not worry, "but that he far preferred sleeping with her and missed her a great deal" (summarized in restrained terms).
*Mechanism and limits:* (b) personification of a dud as an observing creature; (c) the sergeant-major's make-believe sport is both brave and absurd; (d) the London street names on trenches ("Petticoat Lane," "Lowndes Square") supply a comic backdrop to the narrator's embarrassment at his own concussion; (e) the joke is directed at an unnamed dead man treated as a familiar figure, and Graves's own sentence ties it to the platoon's code ("will rob anyone ... except a man in their own platoon"). Graves does not condemn it and does not join in.
*Candidate application:* The unit's license to joke with a corpse is attested here as belonging to men "accustomed to death," in-group, in the same breath as a stated code of loyalty; the narrator's shame at his own physical collapse and his reporting of it (as "much ashamed") is a rare instance of an officer admitting a body reaction to blast.

*End of range.* My range ends at L4152, in the middle of the first sentence of the letter of 6 June 1915 (Graves describing billets in Béthune, "a swimming bath, all sorts of shops, especially a cake-shop"). The remainder of that letter, including the notice posted at the canal, belongs to the next pass (E15b) and is not extracted here.

## Pattern roll-up

*(What this unit establishes, from one officer-memoir by one narrator; "Graves" below means the narrator as he presents himself, 1914-15 events written up in 1916-29. Finding numbers are in brackets.)*

**Functions.**
- *Cohesion and identity:* regimental custom as the main cohesion device in this part: the flash (E15a-29), the spelling "Welch," the Albuhera mess ritual, the Royal Goat, St. David's Night (E15a-30), rival-regiment insult culture (E15a-27), the men's knowledge of regimental battles over the war's causes (E15a-33). The Finnigan song (E15a-49) and the Cock Robin episode (E15a-22) are sung cohesion with a victim in the middle.
- *Boundary:* regulars against Special Reservists (E15a-32), against territorials ("dog-shooters," E15a-31, E15a-43), against staff ("Trench soldiers hate the staff and the staff know it," E15a-49), against other regiments (E15a-27, E15a-34).
- *Coping:* the mock letter after the first shells (E15a-37); the Welsh hymn-singing when "a bit frightened" (E15a-25); "always joking" under mortar fire (E15a-53); Dunn's marmalade (E15a-48); the old hands' cushy-one stories (E15a-52).
- *Rank negotiation:* the lice answer that wins the platoon (E15a-47); the officer's staged calm (E15a-48); the sentry's coaching (E15a-45); the Welsh deputation's complaint about their sergeant-major's origins (E15a-25); the adjutant's reading of "sportsmanship" (E15a-19).
- *Narrator's own claims about humor and morale:* learning to "reply in kind" to bawdy insult only in the army (E15a-8); that the reply about Crawshay Bailey's brother meant "no trouble with the platoon at all" (E15a-47); that the Welsh sang to keep steady (E15a-25); that the men were "much afraid, yet always joking" (E15a-53); that regimental tradition outlasted repeated annihilation (E15a-33); that he "didn't allow myself to feel" for either general or sergeant (E15a-52). The memoir does not offer a theory of humor; these are statements on particular occasions.

**Targets.** Staff and generals (E15a-29, E15a-49, E15a-52); the official communiqué (E15a-35); the army's own regulations and forms (E15a-2, E15a-17, E15a-24, E15a-48); territorials (E15a-31, E15a-43); rival regiments (E15a-27); the quartermaster-sergeant (E15a-49); the unit's own failings in miniature (the goat-major, Boy Jones, E15a-24); the new man's naive question (E15a-38); lice (E15a-47); a general's tears (E15a-52); enemy relatives (E15a-15); and the dead only in the one platoon-license case (E15a-53) and in the fate of the mockers (E15a-22).

**Forms and mechanisms.** Deadpan and understated reply to a form or a crisis (Old Joe, E15a-2; Mallory's Pipe, E15a-6; Dunn's marmalade, E15a-48); mock-legal and mock-official language (the goat-major's charge sheet, E15a-24; the Climbers' Club entry, E15a-6; the contradictory "pink forms," E15a-48; the miners' "report," E15a-50); regimental nicknames and puns ("The Ups and Downs," E15a-35; "Dirty Williams," E15a-21; "Glad Eyes," E15a-20; "Silent Night," E15a-48; "Human Maxim," E15a-44; "Christmas-tree," E15a-41; "Cock Robin," E15a-22); parody of a formulaic letter (E15a-37); parody song (E15a-49); mock-ritual (the Buffaloes, E15a-18; the carnations, E15a-7; the New-Bug's exam, E15a-9); personification of ordnance (E15a-53); idiom that redefines official categories (the "Rooti" medal, E15a-17; "cushy one," E15a-52); the surprise-reversal tale (the cushy-one stories, E15a-52); the dark-comic closing sentence after an anecdote (Robinson's singers, E15a-22; Bumford and Burford, E15a-36; Jenkins, E15a-42).

**Rules of license.**
- Joking about institutions is licensed to an officer-narrator and to the men; joking about *superiors by name* appears in a sergeant's aside (E15a-52) and in a platoon song (E15a-49) tolerated by its target.
- Mess license: the officers' mess may name and mock a colleague as a sanction (E15a-21) and introduce a newcomer by mock faults (E15a-42).
- Pay-night license: drunkenness ignored until an officer appears (E15a-23).
- School code: deception of authority is no shame, of a peer is immoral (E15a-13).
- Platoon code: miners will rob anyone but a platoon-mate; a platoon's joking with a dead stranger is tolerated (E15a-53).
- An officer's acceptance by the men depends on speaking in their own idiom at a first test (E15a-47).

**Limits and failure modes (where the register turns plain).**
- *Named dead:* mostly in a subordinate clause at the end of the anecdote or paragraph (E15a-5, E15a-10, E15a-11, E15a-20, E15a-22, E15a-36, E15a-42), never as a joke target in the author's own voice.
- *Fear:* gas "was a nightmare" and respirators were not believed (E15a-48); rifle fire "gave us a worse feeling of danger" (E15a-38); "I lost my sense of equilibrium ... much ashamed" (E15a-53).
- *Shell-shock-adjacent:* the narrator on all fours after a shell (E15a-53); Bumford asleep on his feet and protected from a capital charge (E15a-36); a suicide whose family is told a different story (E15a-46); an officer who fainted and was accused of shamming (E15a-26).
- *Punishment and the firing squad:* a second-hand story of two executed men told in unadorned prose (E15a-50); sentry sleeping carried the death penalty (E15a-36).
- *Where humor is the vehicle of cruelty:* a boy's bullying at school through bawdy humor and a "humorous legend" (E15a-8); the shaming of Robinson (E15a-22); the stretcher-bearer's contempt (E15a-40); contempt for the territorials (E15a-31).
- *Humor as cover:* "false ease" about a kill (E15a-48); the unwillingness to feel for general or sergeant (E15a-52).

**Time course.** Within the range, from the school years through the first weeks of trench duty (August 1914 to early June 1915, with arrival in France in spring 1915): the school cruelty of Charterhouse (to 1914) as the training for later mess banter; the nervous chaff of the first night (E15a-37); the early mix of fun and fear (the poplars "like tulips," E15a-49). The memoir is retrospective (1929), and the passages that quote letters (ch. XIII) are the closest to the time. The narrator's own flash-forwards (the singers dead, Jenkins "killed not long after," Miller unknown) show the dry retrospective habit.

**Civilian-facing register.** The press chain of atrocity reports (E15a-14); the "usual sort of letter" to a suicide's family (E15a-46); the French military governor's "gloriously" speech (E15a-50); publisher's advice to omit a trial (E15a-23); the censor's role in the lance-corporal's letter (E15a-53); *Times* amusement at a camp story that Graves knows was true (E15a-18).

**Cross-cultural / regimental differences.** This unit is one British regiment (Royal Welch Fusiliers, Special Reserve officers attached to the Welsh Regiment's Second Battalion), Anglo-Welsh officers of the class that served in county regiments, and Welsh miners and colliers in the ranks. Welsh traits claimed by officers (needing a reason, hymn-singing, chapel scruples) are the narrator's and Dunn's generalizations (E15a-25, E15a-43). German-blooded officers' jokes (E15a-15) and the enemy uncle's story (E15a-16) are the only enemy-side material.

**Craft observations (what the memoir's own method shows).** The author places the joke before the death and the death in a subordinate clause; names the nickname, then the fate; uses a single plain figure for the numbers (E15a-11); admits when a story is hearsay ("Or so I was told," E15a-36); flags what he is withholding (E15a-23); and interrupts his own regimental lyricism ("here ends my very creditable ... lyrical passage," E15a-30).

**Contrast with the other-ranks voice (Frank Richards, E13a/E13b), where the text here supports it.**
- *Shared:* contempt for territorials (E13a-30, E13a-82) and for staff and orders (E13a-14, E13a-22); the Goat-Major and regimental pride; decorations as a target (E13a-12, E13a-77); lice as a joke (E13a-32); the wound as a prize ("cushy one," E13a-24, E13a-29); nicknames (E13a-17); humor that stops at named deaths (E13a-4, E13a-36).
- *Different:* Graves's officer voice is built on written and institutional forms (a quartermaster's directory slip, a charge sheet, a communiqué, a club guide, a letter) and is retold from a class position (a "gentleman's education," the "masquerade," the Charterhouse caste system) that Richards's voice does not have. The enlisted voice in Graves's own pages is quoted briefly (Fry, Bumford, Dai, Sergeant Jones, Sergeant Smith, the deputation) and never as an extended narrator; the officer-narrator reports it, so the memoir tells us what the officers *heard* and how they classified it, not how the men talked among themselves.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Range boundary.** My range ends at L4152 in the middle of the Béthune letter of 6 June 1915; the rest of that letter and everything after (including Loos, the Somme and the later war, the hospital, and the postwar chapters) belong to the other passes. Several footnotes to my range are collected at the end of the file (L12313-12342); I read footnotes [1]-[3] because they annotate passages in my range (E15a-22, E15a-42) and did not use the others.
- **Retrospective shaping and reliability.** The memoir is a 1929 reconstruction; the author says in chapter XII that his 1916 account was "written as a novel" and has to be "retranslated into history," and that "the fate of many of my comrades in France has come to me merely as hearsay." Dialogue is reconstructed, characters may be composites, and the book is known as a literary, partly comic autobiography. Do not treat any quoted line as a verbatim trench remark. The letters in chapter XIII are presented as contemporary, but I cannot verify that from the text file.
- **One narrator, one class, one regiment, one phase.** An Anglo-Irish-German public-school officer of a Welsh regiment, August 1914 to June 1915 (England until spring 1915, then the Cambrin-Cuinchy sector). Patterns here cannot be generalized to other armies, other ranks, or later years.
- **Humor mostly on the officers' side of the line.** The enlisted voice is mediated by the narrator (see the contrast above); the officer-narrator records and classifies, and selects for the anecdotal.
- **Hearsay inside the memoir.** Jackie Barrett (E15a-17), the miners' execution (E15a-50), the territorial failures (E15a-31), the Cheshires' loss of title and the Argyll "nine hundred and one" story (E15a-27) are told by others or as "it was said"; the last two are regimental folklore, not witnessed.
- **The school chapters are background only.** I did not extract the family history, religion, German childhood, the nervous breakdown, or the school-romance material, except for items bearing on humor, class and institutional culture. The school chapters also contain sexual and bullying content that I summarized only as needed (see the register).
- **Ethnic and national generalizations** (Welsh, Irish, German, Italian, French) are in the narrator's and officers' mouths and are documented, not endorsed.
- **Quotations** were checked against the text file only (British spellings converted to American per the project rule; the text file's own "♦" transcriber marks are dropped; one typo in the text, "around I us," is omitted by paraphrase); no printed edition was consulted.
