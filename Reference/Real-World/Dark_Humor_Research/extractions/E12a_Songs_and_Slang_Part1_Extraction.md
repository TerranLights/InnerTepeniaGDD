## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-08 is.** The archive.org OCR of the **second edition, "revised and enlarged"** (1930) of *Songs and Slang of the British Soldier: 1914-1918*, edited by **John Brophy** (the songs and the introduction) and **Eric Partridge** (the glossary; the press that published the book was Partridge's own company). The half-title says each edition was limited to 1,000 copies. The OCR front matter includes a library accession stamp. The structure visible in my range, by **printed page**: Preface to the First Edition (p. v; dated London, May 25, 1930) · Contents (p. vii) · **Introduction by Brophy (pp. 3-20)** · **Songs (pp. 23-86)** in three groups, *I. predominantly sung on the march* (pp. 23-53), *II. sung on the march, but more often in billets and estaminets* (pp. 55-73) and *III. chants and songs rarely, if ever, sung on the march* (pp. 75-86) · **Glossary of Soldiers' Slang begins at p. 93** (printed pp. 87-92 are not in the OCR text; the Contents list suggests they held the notes on *One-Eyed Riley* and *She Was Poor, But She Was Honest* and the glossary's title page; see Gaps) · my range ends in the entry "Issue," at **p. 130, line 5,000**. The Contents list also shows, beyond my range, a Note to Glossary (p. 185), Appendix A (Chants and Sayings), Appendix B (Songs Accompanying Bugle Calls) and a Postscript with additional songs and words; those are Part 2. So **the songs end and the glossary begins at the boundary between lines 2960 and 2963 of the text file.**

**What the editors say about method.** (Preface and Introduction; E12a-1 to E12a-4.) Both served "actively during the greater part of the War"; the collection depends on "memory and oral tradition" and consultation with "men of different ranks and widely varying experience"; they claim to include only songs "in use throughout the Army" (not regimental ditties); where versions differ they chose "the most pointed"; "propriety has suppressed one or two of the songs and amended a few others" and some bawdy verses are named but not printed; the glossary is "a record-by-glimpses" with fact and opinion distinguished; one editor is "a convinced pacifist." In my range there are about **50 songs and chants** (26 in group I, 16 in group II, about 10 in group III, with several only mentioned in notes) and **roughly 250 glossary entries** (A through "Issue").

**Evidence base.** Retrospective, twelve years after the Armistice, by two veterans; British Expeditionary Force; mostly the Western Front, with Mesopotamia, Gallipoli, Salonika and Egypt appearing in notes and entries. There is no recording method beyond recollection, no informant list, no frequency data, no dates for most songs, and no musical notation in the text (airs are named by title only). The first editor's claims are sociological and general; the glossary mixes definitions, etymologies (frequently speculative), and the editors' own commentary.

**What it does NOT contain** (in my range). No quoted speech from named soldiers; no officer-composed songs except one note that a song was "credibly said" to be by an officer (E12a-44); no songs about named battles or named dead comrades; no gore-level songs (the editors' own cleaning, E12a-1, E12a-19, E12a-35); the most obscene songs and verses are named as suppressed or are printed with words dashed out; no systematic account of Irish, Scottish, Welsh, Dominion or Indian troops' separate songs (Australian variants are mentioned only in passing); no defector, deserter, mutiny or shell-shock material beyond one-sentence mentions (Étaples mutiny, E12a-70; *Doolally*, E12a-66; *Conshie*, E12a-68). The editors' own prejudices appear in their notes (class remarks about "the working classes" and "illiterate" speech; period ethnic terms).

**OCR quality.** Mixed. Running text is clean enough to quote, but: song titles are lost or garbled at several places (*We Are the Boys Who Fear No Noise*; *Old Soldiers Never Die*; *Michael Finnigan*; *Greeting to the Sergeant*; I supply them from the Contents list and the introduction); some lines are partly lost (the air and last line of *Far, Far from Ypres*, the note on *Mother Hunt*, parts of *My Nelly* and *Michael Finnigan*); some proper names are mis-read (the OCR prints "Ferry" for "Jerry," "Fohnny" for "Johnny," "maze" for "mate"; I correct these only in my own text and say so where I quote); page numbers appear as bare numbers at the foot of each page; and the date of *Mademoiselle from Armenteers* is printed with a damaged digit ("191"). I cite **printed page plus text-file line numbers (L)**. I skipped nothing in lines 1-5,000 except the binding stamp text at L1-24 and the page-number-only lines.

**Quotation conventions.** The book is U.S. public domain, so I quote songs at length where the example is the point. British spellings in quotations are converted to American per the project rule (e.g., "favorite," "labor," "airplane"); the editors' original spellings of dialect words and slang (Gawd, civvy, blighter) are kept; square brackets mark my insertions or omitted words. ## Findings

### A. The editors' frame: preface and Brophy's introduction (printed pp. v, 3-20)

**[E12a-1]. The preface's claims about method: two veterans, memory and oral tradition, "propriety" as censor, and a refusal to make soldiers either saints or whiners.** (PW-08, Preface to the First Edition, p. v; dated London, May 25, 1930; L54-101)
Both editors say they "served actively during the greater part of the War" and that the book depends on "memory and oral tradition," so completeness cannot be claimed. They name their own censorship in the first paragraph: "propriety has suppressed one or two of the songs and amended a few others." They say they consulted "men of different ranks and widely varying experience" and aimed only for songs "in use throughout the Army during 1914-1918, and not restricted to particular formations." The glossary is described as "a record-by-glimpses of the British soldiers' spirit and life," with comments "far from 'official' in tone and in matter," and with fact and opinion separated. They disclaim speaking for everyone ("one of the editors is now a convinced pacifist") but claim these are "substantially ... the views of the majority of war-time soldiers," portrayed "neither as supermen or as whiners, as sots or as saints." The preface thanks T. E. Shaw (my gloss: T. E. Lawrence's service name), among others, for comments on the glossary.
*Candidate application:* A veteran-collected record that openly states its own cleaning and its own selection is a more honest model of "what the army sang" than a purely complete one; a character who sings or quotes such songs may likewise have a cleaned and an uncleaned version.

**[E12a-2]. Why the editors treat the songs as "folk-songs": anonymous, private-soldier in origin, rough in form, made by "homeless men."** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 3-5; L156-232)
Brophy opens by lamenting that earlier armies' songs (Odysseus' crew through Marlborough, Wellington and Napoleon) are lost, and that only music-hall songs survive from the Crimean and South African wars. Brophy claims this is "the first attempt to collect and publish the songs and parodies which the British soldier invented," that "eighty to ninety per cent." of the text "has never been printed before," that earlier "war books" printed only scattered, "bowdlerized" fragments mixed with author-made songs, and that where versions differ he chose "that version ... which in the opinion of the editors is the most pointed." Purely regimental ditties are excluded; only songs "universally sung in the British Expeditionary Forces at one time or another" are in. Brophy explains that one or more songs, including *Love My Girl*, were "entirely suppressed, as being merely lewd and not essentially soldiers' songs," and dates them to the pre-War period. The songs are "genuine 'folk-songs'"; "Nothing like them is found in civilian life"; they come "from the ranks, especially from the private soldiers without ambition to bear office or special responsibility"; the roughness of meter and rhyme shows "illiterate or semi-literate origin"; and they are "the songs of homeless men ... an itinerant community, continually altering within itself under the incidence of death and mutilation."
*Candidate application:* The sociological claim that songs come from the ambitionless private (not the NCO, not the officer) places this humor at the bottom of the rank structure; it is a useful constraint for who in a unit carries the repertoire.

**[E12a-3]. How a soldiers' song begins: the wit who twists a music-hall line, shouts it louder, and the crowd completes it (with two "aborted" parodies as evidence).** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 4-6; L238-295)
Brophy compares two theories of ballad origin (a single unknown poet; a community adding line by line) and says the same uncertainty applies here. He separates *unit* songs (made by the battalion's small journalist or "writer of Christmas card verses or parish magazine poetry," for the concert party; they are like "'family' jokes abhorred of visitors" and lack universality) from the *army-wide* songs, which are often parodies. The mechanism: a satiric man sees a chance "to travesty its sentiments or to satirize some aspect of the common lot" by twisting a line of a popular song, "shout[s] it aloud, louder than his comrades singing the original words"; if it is appreciated "it would be taken up generally," others improve and lengthen it. Evidence: during 1917-18 men sang for one couplet of the ragtime number *The Black Eyed Susans* "The Orderly Sergeant knows I'm coming, / I can hear him softly humming," and "the afflatus ceased" and the parody was never finished; and *Colonel Bogey*, "probably the most frequently heard march-tune in the Army," was fitted with a coarse word that could be hurled at any regiment whose name matched the meter, and no further words attached. Songs invented in melody as well as words were "snatches of nonsense or satire, or else ballads telling a story—usually a bawdy one."
*Candidate application:* The model of humor as a one-line interruption of a shared song, performed loudly against the group's grain and accepted or ignored by the crowd, is a useful mechanism for "who gets to make a joke in a unit and how it is voted in."

**[E12a-4]. How the repertoire spread: men from every unit mixed in billets, estaminets, hospitals and base camps, and after 1916 the "Base Details" system scrambled regiments.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 6-7; L296-337)
Some songs are inherited from the pre-1914 regulars, "perhaps the only descendants of a long oral tradition reaching back to the press-gangs and prisons of the eighteenth century"; but "the majority are War songs and became obsolete ... the moment the War ended." Dates given: *Mademoiselle from Armenteers* is a 1915 song (printed "191" with a damaged digit in the OCR), *I Wore a Tunic* 1917. No author has claimed any song, "perhaps because the author is dead, or did not realize his feat ... or is afraid of the outbreak of incredulity." Rapid propagation is attributed to the mixing of men "in billets and estaminets behind the line, and even more in hospitals, base-camps and troopships"; after 1916 invalided men no longer went back to their own battalions, so "a man who enlisted in the Devons" might end in the Border Regiment, then a "nominally Lancashire battalion composed of former Munsters, Scots Fusiliers, Middlesex, Norfolks and in fact every regiment in the Army."
*Candidate application:* A shared humor repertoire across a mass army is produced by the *breakdown* of unit loyalty (casualty replacement, hospitals), not by its preservation; a culture of broad, anonymous jokes is a sign of a worn-out unit system.

**[E12a-5]. Category 1, "Satire on War, and Mock-Heroics": the editors' functional theory of why men sang cynical songs.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 7-8; L342-399)
Brophy argues the "cold eye and the literal tongue" is not a 1914-18 discovery: the soldier's "stubborn, strictly agnostic spirit" is already in Shakespeare's *Henry V*, in the foot soldier Williams (Brophy cites C. E. Montague), and the idea that earlier warriors believed the patriotic songs and died in "very pretty attitudes" is a product of what history chose to record. The functional claim, in his words: "When the victims can cheek Juggernaut even as they writhe under the wheels, then by so much do they subtract from his victory." Singing *Hush! Here comes a Whizz-Bang* did not reduce the shell fire, "But the very knowledge of such songs reduced the emotional disorganization caused by fear, and aided him, after the experience, to pick his uncertain way back to sanity." Mocking all heroics (*I Don't Want to Die*, *I Don't Want to be a Soldier*, *Far Far From Ypres I long to Be*) "poked fun at the soldier's own desire for peace and rest, and so prevented it getting the better of him"; they were "strong bulwarks against défaitisme" (the editors' spelling and accent). The same note says "cheated hysteria with songs making fun of mud and lice and fear and weariness" is "the same ironic method of outwitting misfortune" the forefathers had.
*Candidate application:* The documented claim is not that the humor changes the danger but that it changes the *aftermath*, helping a man re-assemble himself; and that the target includes the soldier's own longing. A character's cynical songs may be doing a defensive job against his own wish to stop.

**[E12a-6]. Category 2, "Satire on the Military System": discipline itself kept dissent out of the songs.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 8-9; L401-436)
The private soldier thought Army organization "devised for ceremonial purposes": polishing buttons, presenting arms, alignment, saluting with the hand farther from the officer, keeping thumbs aligned with trouser seams. Told this had a "miraculous moral effect," the new soldiers believed "the professors" until experience showed "the old colonels and majors" to be "maunderers speaking from the book, blind to reality," but by then it was too late: discipline is "so hieratic and rigid that it can be altered only by revolution—and a revolution would have meant defeat." The soldier's spoken comments, "in private and occasionally sotto voce on parade," were "incisive and impolite," "But the very discipline against which he rebelled kept his opinions out of his songs"; only "faint ghosts" appear in *We Are Fred Karno's Army* and *At the Halt on the Left Form Platoon*.
*Candidate application:* This is a documented asymmetry: institutional grievance runs mostly in speech, not in song; a song-based record of a military culture will under-represent complaints against the system and over-represent complaints against persons.

**[E12a-7]. Category 3, "Satire on Superior Officers": who was a safe target and who was not.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 9-10; L438-469)
The resentment "often took the form of jeers at a particular person in authority," and certain ranks were targets "by their own nature": "colonels, sergeant-majors, quartermaster-sergeants and sergeants." The colonel is a target because he was "the supreme authority the private soldier knew," and "sometimes ... he took excessive care of his aging skin." **Officers generally escaped satire**: "not that the private failed to recognize that they had a much more comfortable time than he, but because they belonged to a race apart." For *staff officers*, "even his well-stocked invective failed. He merely had dark and dangerous emotions." The sergeant, sergeant-major and quartermaster-sergeant "were close at hand; and all the rant and bluster in the world failed to conceal a single defect." Songs named: *The Old Barbed Wire* (Colonel, sergeant-major, quartermaster-sergeant as "comic villains"), *The Sergeant-Major's Having a Time*, *We Haven't Seen the Sergeant*, and a "magnificent commination" the editors retitled *Greeting to the Sergeant*.
*Candidate application:* Target selection follows proximity and visibility: the near, disliked NCO gets the song; the distant staff gets only silence and hatred. A character from a far-from-command background may have songs about sergeants and none about generals.

**[E12a-8]. Categories 4 to 6: songs of civilian dreams (stated bluntly, then done with), songs of drink, and nonsense.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 10-11; L471-546)
Civilian life became "as incredible as the possibility of an undisturbed and liberated future" (Brophy quotes Sassoon's *Dreamers*); "*I Want to Go Home*, the soldier sang, mockingly perhaps, but without pretenses," and *When This Blasted War is Over* was the list of "ribald promises" that "kept the hard-flogged flesh going." There was "little envy of civilians avoiding the perils and discomforts of war. The soldier had his opinions and stated them bluntly. After that, the matter was done with"; *I Wore a Tunic* "stands alone, and it had but a 'limited run.'" Drink songs (*Here's to the Good Old Beer*, *Glorious!*) do not show "an army of drunkards"; "Remarkably few soldiers were reduced to bestiality." Nonsense and burlesque (*Inky-Pinky Parley-Vous*, *Skibboo*, *Down by the Sea*, *Wash Me in the Water*, *She was Poor But She was Honest*, *I Have No Pain, Dear Mother, Now*) are called "irrational fooling": "examine them and there is no content at all, but sing them in good company and they satisfy some deep, unsuspected thirst of the spirit."
*Candidate application:* A distinct kind of soldier song has no meaning by design, and the editors insist it still does a job; a character who hums or recites pure nonsense in company may be using it for exactly this reason.

**[E12a-9]. Categories 7 and 8: sex ribaldry (to be read "with the greatest reserve") and the rare sentimental songs; the soldier's "shyness" about sentiment.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 12-13; L552-607)
The editors say the bawdy songs (*My Nelly*, *Skibboo*, *Mademoiselle from Armenteers*) should be read with caution: "Many men refused to sing them in their bawdiest versions," and the English "pay little attention to the meaning of words" and let "a good tune ... excuse almost anything" (they compare it to a congregation's straight faces at the odd lines of *Hymns Ancient and Modern*). Sentimental songs are "very few": *Rolling Home* ("most of its sentiment and charm in the tune"), *The Old Barbed Wire* (pathetic "only in the last stanza, which is remarkable as almost the sole indication in song that the soldier was aware of his own dark fate"), and *Good-bye Nellie*. They say soldiers "invented few songs" for the parting mood "partly because he was shy of it, partly because he was so well supplied by the music-halls with tearful ballads." (Detailed content of the bawdy songs is summarized, not quoted; see the register.)
*Candidate application:* The single sentimental stanza that says "we know our own fate" is described as nearly the only one; the culture's grief is carried by borrowed music-hall songs, not by home-made ones.

**[E12a-10]. Why the soldier sang: physical elation, marching, fatigues, fear (not during a bombardment), shamefaced sentiment by proxy, sexual strain, revenge on the system, and amusement.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 13-14; L609-675)
Seven reasons, in the editors' order: (1) *physical elation* of a hardened body (the civilian clerk or mechanic seldom sings at work); (2) *marching*, where a band and singing relieve monotony; (3) *fatigues* (unloading, stacking) have their own chanty-like songs, whereas "long carrying fatigues went without musical honors, except now and again a derisive snatch—spirits were too low, and breath too scant"; (4) *the trench*, where "a tune, especially on a mouth-organ, would scare away fear itself, as well as the slow-settling bogey of depression," and some men had "an inborn genius, chivalrously cultivated, for cheering their companions"; (5) **a limit**: "need it be said, at this date, that no one chirruped under a bombardment or in the open exposed to machine-gun and rifle fire?"; (6) *indirect sentiment*: song expressed "in an indirect and impersonal fashion, affording good protection for his shamefacedness, his sentiment, especially his sentiment about his home and about his wife or girl," which he attributes to "the modern English terror of emotion"; (7) *satiric revenge*: as the soldier "found out" the Army and the War "he sang satirically and bitterly to revenge himself upon the system which held him prisoner and used him irrationally," and finally *amusement*: the songs "were to him what the theater, the twice-nightly local Empire or Hippodrome, the concert-hall and the cinema had been in civilian life."
*Candidate application:* The explicit rule that singing stopped when under fire, and that it was a camp-and-aftermath behavior, gives a reliable limit for when a soldier character's songs are plausible; the "shamefaced sentiment by proxy" device is a likely template for how a reserved character voices love or loss.

**[E12a-11]. Army obscenity as intensifier: omission of the expected expletive was the marker of real urgency.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 14-19; L677-877)
Brophy says only "a small proportion" of the songs are improper, in contrast with soldiers' ordinary talk, which "was technically obscene in almost every sentence." Brophy calls it "merely technical" because the words "were used habitually, almost mechanically, as mere intensives," with no thought of the literal meaning, and likewise blasphemy "without the slightest intention of blasphemy." Brophy identifies three sexual words around which "almost all Army obscenity revolved" (not named here), and offers a **concrete example of signaling by absence**: if a sergeant said "Get your [expletive] rifles!" it was "understood as a matter of routine. But if he said, 'Get your rifles!' there was an immediate implication of urgency and danger." Brophy argues the adjectival use became so common "the ear refused to acknowledge it and took in only the noun." (Brophy's long moral analysis of the three words, the comparisons with Joyce and Lawrence, and the claim that they are a product of "modern urban civilization" are summarized in the register, not reproduced.) His explanation of why: "It was a vent for the nervous exasperation set up by the unnatural strains of modern warfare"; "they chose obscene words whose sadism matched the sadism of war," and shell fire "batters the mind to a pulp of insanity." Brophy also separates the "technically improper" words for parts of the male body and excretory functions, which Brophy calls "coarse, but neither blasphemous nor sadistic."
*Candidate application:* Profanity as background noise whose *absence* conveys alarm is a specific, reusable detail for how an NCO or veteran signals danger; an ex-service character who drops swearing in a crisis (or, later, at a funeral) is using the same code.

**[E12a-12]. The editors' defense of collecting such songs: what the soldier found good in the War, and "Ah, things happened then!" as a condemnation of the civilian world.** (PW-08, Introduction, pp. 19-20; L879-946)
Asking why to record songs that "reflect the nature of war," Brophy says war created "a cosmos of its own" and cannot be sorted into wholly good and wholly bad. The soldier's list of what was good: "the simplification of his life, open air, the hard use of his body, the sense of a tangible, immediate and valuable task, the sense of being a member of a vast society organized to an ethical end, comradeship, uncertainty of the morrow, the possibility of adventure and an enhanced awareness of the mystery and precariousness of life." What "belongs properly to war—injustice, cruelty, maiming, the blasphemous undervaluation of life—was abhorred by the ordinary soldier." "When a man says of 1914-18, 'Ah, things happened then!' he is uttering a complete, if implicit, condemnation of our civilian world." The book ends its introduction on the image of "a thousand men, dead now or irrevocably changed, singing *Here We Are Again*, innocent of the Somme, Arras, Messines, Passchendaele." The editors claim the songs "perish, except as monuments, with our memories."
*Candidate application:* The documented ambivalence of veterans (nostalgia for what war gave, hatred of what it did) is the editors' own thesis; a post-service character who misses the service and despises the cause is not inconsistent on this account.

### B. Songs predominantly sung on the march (printed pp. 23-53)

**[E12a-13]. *Après la Guerre* (air: "Sous les Ponts de Paris"): the jilting-of-the-local-girl refrain, and the editors' own disclaimer about how seriously it was meant.** (PW-08, p. 25; L1006-1041)
A four-line stanza in pidgin French-English whose point is that the English soldier will leave after the war ends and the French girl will weep or be left pregnant, with a variation that dismisses her. (Content is of a callous sexual-abandonment kind; summarized, not quoted; see register.) The editors' note is the useful part: "Brutal and cynical: a traditional masculine jest. But it is not to be inferred that more than one or two men in a thousand of the millions who sang this song attempted to carry its implications into practice. The tune was delightful and gay; the words went without much thought." Mechanism: a gay French popular tune carries callous words; the editors say the words did not govern behavior.
*Candidate application:* A caution that cynical group songs about women or locals overstate actual conduct; a character may sing them with no corresponding record of action.

**[E12a-14]. *Fred Karno's Army* (air: "The Church's One Foundation"): self-mockery by a hymn tune, with the Kaiser's imagined verdict.** (PW-08, p. 26; L1047-1079)
Verbatim, public-domain: "We are Fred Karno's army, / The ragtime infantry: / We cannot fight, we cannot shoot, / What earthly use are we! / And when we get to Berlin, / The Kaiser he will say, / 'Hoch, hoch! Mein Gott, / What a bloody fine lot / Are the ragtime infantry!'" The editors say it varied by unit: Australians and New Zealanders sang "A.N.Z.A.C." for "ragtime infantry," and "rotten lot" often replaced the sarcastic "fine lot." Fred Karno was "a popular comedian whose performance, 'The Mumming Birds', was a crescendo of imbecility and absurd incompetence," with Charlie Chaplin and the late Harry Weldon among the performers, "both of whom continued and made famous the pathetic-comic tradition of ineffectiveness." Mechanism: a church-hymn tune and a claim of total uselessness, followed by a mock-triumph (the enemy commander is imagined admiring the unit), self-deprecation by a unit that is, at the time, in training. Target: self and the amateur "New Armies" (Brophy's introduction points to it as one of the faint ghosts of complaint about the system, E12a-6).
*Candidate application:* Self-deprecation of the group's competence, wrapped in a hymn, is the most "official-safe" kind of dark joke: it makes no accusation against a superior.

**[E12a-15]. *We've Had No Beer* (air: "Lead, Kindly Light"): a mock-lament, endlessly extensible, calibrated to how bitter the men felt.** (PW-08, p. 27; L1084-1111)
The text is "We've had no beer to-day!" repeated to a hymn tune. The editors: "sung lugubriously and low, usually at the end of a long route march"; the variation *We've had no duff* belongs to training in England; units "really indignant" would run "through the whole range of monosyllabic drinks, stanza by stanza" (stout, gin, ale, wine, port); in France it was usually forgotten "except when an expected issue of rum failed; then, if the men's feelings were not too bitter and circumstances not too depressing, the mock melancholy chant would be heard, *We've had no rum to-day!*" Mechanism: a solemn hymn tune for a trivial deprivation, and a practice of using the form only when the mood is not too bad.
*Candidate application:* The mock-lament is a barometer: it is sung when the grievance is small enough to be funny and not sung when the grievance is real; its silence in France is as informative as its presence.

**[E12a-16]. *At the Halt on the Left* (air: "Three Cheers for the Red, White and Blue"): a drill-instructor's exasperated question turned into a song.** (PW-08, p. 28; L1117-1140)
Verse: "At the halt on the left, form platoon! ... If the odd numbers don't mark time two paces, / How the hell can the rest form platoon?" The note says it "conveys with some precision the querulous tone as well as the drill-book phraseology of an exasperated officer or N.C.O.," and explains the maneuver. It was "popularized early in 1914" and "a pre-War Regular Army and Territorial song, to which the years 1914-1918 gave a flavor of their own." Mechanism: imitation of the instructor's own words to the instructor's recruits; target is the mechanics of drill, not a person.
*Candidate application:* Taking the instructor's catch-phrase and singing it back is a mild, deniable form of resistance.

**[E12a-17]. *Yes! And We Can Do It!* (air: a nursery game melody): the whole disciplinary sequence from offense to punishment, sung as a list with a proud refrain.** (PW-08, pp. 29-30; L1146-1206)
Each stanza gives a stage: "Breaking out of barracks! ... Parading all unbuttoned! ... Take his name and number! ... Up before the C.O.! ... Fourteen days detention! ... Pack-drill, bread and water!" to the refrain "As you have done before," and the last line turns to "Yes, and we can do it! ... As we have done before." The note defines the "naked" unbuttoned parade as a punishable offense, and "take his name and number" as the NCO's note-taking, "Trial and punishment followed the next day." Mechanism: a bureaucratic sequence enumerated with relish, then the refrain converts each punishment into a boast of the offender's record. Target: the disciplinary system, whose punishments are made into a collective badge.
*Candidate application:* Punishment-as-credential is a standard inversion; a character who recites a record of past offenses with something like pride is working in this vein.

**[E12a-18]. Songs the editors left out or doubted: cumulative marching songs, *Sammy Hall*, and the editors' admission that neither of them heard it.** (PW-08, p. 30; L1209-1231)
The editors omit "the old cumulative marching songs" (*One Man Went to Mow*; *Old King Cole* "in its parodied form") as "folk-songs without any reference to things military." They mention *Sammy Hall*, "a very old civilian song early adopted by the Army," each verse ending "So good-bye, and damn you all, / Damn your eyes," originally "a scoundrel's funeral oration," sometimes called *Captain Hall* in the Army; correspondents say it was sung "in 1914 and the earlier half of 1915," but "neither of us ever heard it sung in 1914-1918." Mechanism/caution: the editors expose a gap between what some informants claim and what the editors heard.
*Candidate application:* The repertoire shifts by phase of the war; early-war songs may not survive into later periods or be remembered by later men.

**[E12a-19]. *She Married a Man* and *Tiddleywinks, Old Man*: bawdy songs, one with an obscene longer version the editors would not print.** (PW-08, pp. 31-32; L1237-1277)
*She Married a Man* is a pre-War folk-song turned to Army use whose chorus turns on a deliberately omitted word (the editors print dashes); the note says a longer version has "great merits" but is "too obscene for us to attempt to give it." *Tiddleywinks, Old Man* (air: hornpipe) is a hornpipe chorus about the absence of women, with the note: "We have, to avoid the rigors of the law, slightly euphemized three lines." Both are summarized rather than quoted (sexual content; see register). The editorial notes are the finding: **the editors' own text records two levels of cleaning: the omitted word in print, and the longer unprinted version**, and a legal reason ("the rigors of the law") for the second.
*Candidate application:* The editors' acknowledgment that even the printed bawdy is already softened gives a baseline: real soldiers' bawdy was coarser than any printed record.

**[E12a-20]. *We're Here Because* (air: "Auld Lang Syne"): a one-word-repeated song of purposelessness, "sung with great gusto."** (PW-08, p. 33; L1283-1299)
Verbatim, public-domain: "We're here / Because / We're here / Because / We're here / Because we're here." The note: "Sung with great gusto because—ninety-nine times out of a hundred—the men who sang it had no idea why they were there, or where 'there' was, or how long they would continue at it." Mechanism: a famous sentimental parting tune, with a closed circular argument as the lyric; the gusto is the joke's second layer: the louder it is sung, the more it announces that nobody knows.
*Candidate application:* A tautology sung loudly is a precise model of "the war's pointlessness" humor with no accusation in it; a character may use "because we're here" as a closing phrase for any "why" question.

**[E12a-21]. Pure-repetition and echo songs: *King Charles*, *Down by the Sea*, *She Was So Good*.** (PW-08, pp. 33-36; L1301-1383)
*King Charles he was King Charles* "consists of just that one line, or, as some prefer it, just two lines, repeated till the singers wearied of it"; the editors date it as pre-War Regular Army. *Down by the Sea* (air unknown) is a call-and-echo piece ("Down by the sea / (Down by the sea!) / Where the water-melons grow ...") that ends on a non-sequitur question to a wife about "a cow with a green eye-brow"; the note says the echo lines "were intoned very softly, and the whole piece of nonsense was sung slow and sweet, till the last two lines, which went with speed and gusto," and that it was "almost certainly a pre-War American ditty" that most soldiers did not realize was American. *She Was So Good* is a single couplet ("She was so good and so kind to me, / Just like one of the family") broken off at "She was—" and restarted, "(and so on interminably, or as long as patience lasts)"; "almost certainly, in pre-War days, a folk-recitative." Mechanism: the song is a pure duration game, a held shared pattern with no content; the end is set by the singers' patience, not the words.
*Candidate application:* Group nonsense that can run indefinitely is a way for a unit to occupy time and voice together without anyone having to say anything; its authority comes from the group's refusal to stop, not from the text.

**[E12a-22]. *John Brown's Baby* (air: "John Brown's Body"): a pre-War parody whose gap is sung aloud as "shush!"** (PW-08, p. 35; L1338-1352)
Three repetitions of a line about a baby with a minor ailment in an embarrassing place ending "—shush!", then "The poor kid can't sit down." The note: "A pre-War parody adopted whole-heartedly by the soldiers, and by them granted a new and much more vigorous lease of life." Mechanism: the hymn-like martial tune of "John Brown's Body" with the bodily word withheld by a shouted hush; the crowd supplies the missing word. (The ailment is a mild bodily-function joke; summarized, not quoted; see register.)
*Candidate application:* A shared "censoring" noise inside a song, where everyone knows what is withheld, is a cohesion device, a joke the whole unit performs at once.

**[E12a-23]. *Barney* (air unknown; tune of "Bring Back My Bonnie to Me"): the sergeant-as-thief variation is what makes it a soldiers' song.** (PW-08, p. 37; L1389-1433)
The verse is a courting nonsense ("I took my girl for a ramble, a ramble ... She caught her foot in a bramble, a bramble, / And over she came") with a plaintive chorus "Oh, Barney, / Oh, Barney! / Oh, bring back my Barney to me." The *variation* is "Oh, Sergeant, / Oh, Sergeant, / Oh, bring back my rations to me." The editors say "it is really the variation which confirms this as an authentic soldiers' song, for the original form of this chorus is a well-known air found in many community-song books: *Bring back my Bonnie to Me*," and add that "Barney" was probably originally "Bonnie." Mechanism: a sentimental call for a lost lover is converted into an accusation that the sergeant has stolen rations; the same tender tune carries the grievance. Target: the ration-handling NCO (compare *The Old Barbed Wire*, E12a-49).
*Candidate application:* A one-word substitution that turns longing into accusation (the beloved becomes the thing the superior took) is a compact form of rank-directed grievance.

**[E12a-24]. *The Moon Shines Bright* (air: "Pretty Red Wing"): Charlie Chaplin as the ragged recruit, going to the Dardanelles.** (PW-08, p. 38; L1439-1455)
Verbatim, public-domain: "The moon shines bright on Charlie Chaplin, / His boots are cracking / For want of blacking, / And his khaki trousers / They want mending, / Before we send him / To the Dardanelles." Mechanism: a pre-War sentimental ballad tune turned into a recruit's dress inspection; the comic icon of the ill-fitting tramp is used as the type of the new army's ragged state, and the Dardanelles (Gallipoli) is named as the destination in an offhand "before we send him." Dating is by the place name (1915 campaign); the editors give no note.
*Candidate application:* The troop's most common self-portrait is shabbiness, and the joke points the soldier at the war by name only in the last line; the matter-of-fact "before we send him" is the dark part.

**[E12a-25]. *Rolling Home*: drinking-song words, but sung quietly, hummed or whistled; the tune carries the sentiment.** (PW-08, p. 39; L1461-1486)
Words: "Rolling home, / Rolling home ... By the light of the silvery moon! / Happy is the day / When you draw your buckshee pay / And you're rolling, rolling, rolling, rolling home." Editors' note: "In spite of the implications of alcoholic revelry, this charming air was often sung quietly and with much sentiment. In the evening or toward the close of the afternoon, the words would sometimes be omitted and the air whistled and hummed." (The editors' introduction earlier said "most of its sentiment and charm [is] in the tune," E12a-9.) Mechanism: the lyric is a cover story for a homing-wish; the humming that drops the words altogether is the point at which the feeling is allowed.
*Candidate application:* A character whose humming is the real emotion, while the words (if sung) are a drinking joke, is a documented pattern, and lets the humming be a plot-visible tell without a line of dialogue.

**[E12a-26]. *Here's to the Good Old Beer*: a toast cumulation through nine drinks, a pre-War community song "made their own."** (PW-08, pp. 40-41; L1495-1596)
A repeated toast ("Mop it down!") runs through beer, whiskey, porter, brandy, stout, rum, port and gin, each with a rhyming tag ("That never leaves you queer"; "That slips down as it oughter"; "That makes you feel blown-out"; "That makes you feel a sport"; "That fills you up with sin"). Two of the rhyme tags (brandy and rum) are bawdy and are summarized, not quoted. The editors' note adds that a sherry stanza ("That makes you feel so merry") also existed. Mechanism: the cumulative-list form; each new drink invites a new rhyme.
*Candidate application:* The joke here is the list; its value is in its endless extensibility (any drink, any rhyme).

**[E12a-27]. *I Have No Pain, Dear Mother, Now* (air: "My Love is like a Red, Red Rose"): the dying-soldier's last words to his mother, turned into a thirst.** (PW-08, p. 42; L1599-1612)
Verbatim, public-domain: "I have no pain, dear mother, now, / But oh! I am so dry. / Connect me to a brewery / And leave me there to die." Editors' note: the tune "is the regimental march of the Loyal North Lancashires, but to these words was known throughout the Army"; in the introduction they list it among "burlesque" at its best (E12a-8). Mechanism: the Victorian deathbed ballad ("I have no pain, dear mother") is rendered as a drink-wish; the wounded man's reassurance to his mother is flipped to an appeal for a brewery, with death being the cost the singer cheerfully accepts. Target: the sentimental dying-soldier genre of the home front and its music-halls.
*Candidate application:* The genre-flip of the "reassuring a mother" convention is a clear example of dark humor that treats the home audience's sentiment as the thing being mocked, not the death.

**[E12a-28]. *The Old Black Bull*: a Somerset tune sung "especially in Mespot," whose story ends with a broken-backed bull and a refrain "A long time ago."** (PW-08, p. 43; L1618-1677)
Stanzas: the old black bull comes down from the mountain; six heifers in the pasture; he "pawed on the ground and he [word missing in the OCR] in the fountain"; "Now the old black bull's gone back to the mountain"; "And his head hung low and his back was broken." The chorus "A long time ago" is repeated. Editors' note, in full: "Sung especially in 'Mespot.' Pre-War, when local to Somerset." The editors give no interpretation of the ending. (The missing word in the third stanza is probably a coarse one; it is an omission in the printed text or the OCR, not recorded as my doing; see register.) Mechanism: a pastoral animal ballad with a sexual middle and an exhausted ending, with a refrain that pushes the story into the past.
*Candidate application:* The ballad's last image is of a spent animal and its chorus is a shrug of time; a likely vehicle for men in a hot, static theater to sing about exhaustion without naming it. (Reading is mine; the source offers none.)

**[E12a-29]. *What Did You Join the Army For?* (air: "Here's to the Maiden of Bashful Fifteen"): the volunteer's own question, answered by "barmy."** (PW-08, p. 44; L1683-1696)
Verbatim, public-domain: "What did you join the Army for? / Why did you join the Army? / What did you join the Army for? / You must have been bloodywell barmy." Editors: the tune "is the regimental march of the King's (Liverpool) Regiment, with whose Regular battalions it may have originated." Mechanism: the self-addressed accusation; the group sings the rhetorical question to the volunteer, who is every one of them. The word used to describe the volunteer is "barmy" (mad).
*Candidate application:* Volunteer regret turned into a question the unit sings in unison fits the "left on principle" or "should have known better" register of a soldier who volunteered.

**[E12a-30]. *Raining* (air: hymn "Holy, Holy, Holy"): a hymn of four complaints, where grousing itself is one of the subjects.** (PW-08, p. 45; L1702-1731)
Stanzas: "Raining, raining, raining, / Always bloodywell raining. / Raining all the morning, / And raining all the night"; "Grousing, grousing, grousing ... Grousing at the rations, / And grousing at the pay"; "Marching, marching, marching ... Marching in the morning, / And marching in the night"; and the closing "When the war is over / We'll bloodywell march no more." Mechanism: a hymn tune and the triple-repetition form of hymn praise, set to weather, complaint and drill; the second stanza makes the grumbling a thing sung about, and the last stanza promises an end only to marching.
*Candidate application:* A unit that sings its own grumbling back to itself has found a way to share it without any individual complaining to a superior.

**[E12a-31]. *They Were Only Playing Leap-Frog* (air: "John Brown's Body"): a pre-War university innuendo parody, with a stanza for staff officers and one for the sergeant-major.** (PW-08, p. 46; L1737-1784)
Each stanza gives an innocent-sounding activity ("only playing leap-frog"; "only drawing water") performed in an obviously compromising way by a pair, sung fast (marked *prestissimo*), followed by the chorus "Oh, it's a lie ... For you know, you blighter, you're telling a lie." The pairs are grasshoppers, then "one staff-officer jumped right over the other staff-officer's back," then (in the water-drawing stanza) the sergeant-major who "came and stole the handle off the pump." Editors' note: "A pre-War University parody parodied by the Army." (The innuendo is sexual; summarized, not quoted. See register.) **Tension with the introduction (E12a-7):** Brophy says staff officers drew no satire, "his well-stocked invective failed"; here they are a verse's subject, but only as one of a pair of interchangeable characters in an inherited pre-War parody, not as a target of a song made for them.
*Candidate application:* Targets can be slotted into an inherited innuendo form; the songs of the most hated groups may appear in borrowed frames and not as dedicated pieces.

**[E12a-32]. *We Haven't Seen the Sergeant* (air: "He's a Cousin of Mine," a War-period music-hall song): the sergeant gone missing, and the hope that he went up with a mine.** (PW-08, p. 47; L1790-1811)
Verbatim, public-domain, short: "We haven't seen the sergeant for a hell of a time ... He came up to see what we were doin'; / Number Eight Platoon will be his bloody ruin. / ... Perhaps he's gone up with a mine. / He's a sergeant in the Rifle Brigade, / Well, strafe him, he's no cousin of mine." Editors' note: "Compare these sentiments with *Greeting to the Sergeant* (page 86). For 'Rifle Brigade,' of course, substitute the name of any other regiment, at will." Mechanism: a bright music-hall refrain used to say that a man in authority has not been seen, with the open hope that his absence is due to a mine; the last line disowns him as kin; the regiment line is a substitution slot (the same "slot" technique as in E12a-3 for *Colonel Bogey*).
*Candidate application:* The wish that an unpopular NCO or officer be hit, expressed as a mild observation that he has not turned up, is the usual register for what soldiers cannot say aloud.

**[E12a-33]. *I Wore a Tunic* (air: "I Wore a Tulip"): late-War resentment of civilians, and the editors' class condescension about its rhymes.** (PW-08, p. 48; L1817-1843)
Verbatim, public-domain, in part: "I wore a tunic, / A dirty khaki-tunic, / And you wore civilian clothes. / We fought and bled at Loos / While you were on the booze ... Oh, you were a-slacking / While we were attacking / Down on the Menin Road." The note: a "late-in-the-War song, popular for a short time when men who had been on leave or in hospital had seen the prosperity of many munition makers. It is, however, almost the only song which displays any resentment against able-bodied civilians." The glossary's *Civvy* entry (L3912-3916) calls the word "freighted with much emotion, envy, fond memories and eternal hope" and points to both this song and *When This Blasted War Is Over* (E12a-42). The editors add a remark about the "very rough approximations that serve for rhymes—clothes, knows; foe, road—due to the slovenly pronunciation of the working classes," which is the editors' own class judgment and not a finding about the song. (A middle line alludes to the civilian's alleged sexual company; summarized, not quoted.) Mechanism: a sentimental tune of loss turned on the home-front man; the grievance comes from direct contact with the civilian world on leave or from a hospital bed, i.e., it is conditional on being back in Britain.
*Candidate application:* Civilian resentment, in the evidence of this one book, arises on contact (leave, hospital) and not in the line; and the book's own editors said it was nearly the only such song.

**[E12a-34]. The *Mademoiselle from Armenteers* group: the refrain "Parley-vous," the editors' decision not to print most verses, and three variants that do different things.** (PW-08, pp. 49-52; L1849-1964)
*Mademoiselle from Armenteers* (air: a French music-hall tune): the opening stanza "She hasn't been for forty years" is printed; the editors say popular opinion treats the song as "almost endless" because it has been "lumped together" with the next three; the many vague stanzas "with the poor woman described as experiencing many vulgar mishaps" are withheld ("we do not propose to print them here"). *Madame, Have You... ?* is a call-and-response in the refrain's tune (the soldier asks the French landlady for wine, then for her daughter; the answers are "very good wine" and, for the daughter, "far too good for a bloke from the line"), i.e., the soldier is told he is not good enough. *The Sergeant-Major's Having a Time* uses the tune against the CSM (verses: "Swinging the lead behind the line" [shirking]; "Swigging the beer behind the line"; and a third on his women), a rank-directed accusation of comfort and shirking in the rear. The editors' note: "Inky-pinky parley-vous" is "actually an alteration" of the refrain of *Skibboo*, "which is earlier."
*Candidate application:* One tune serves a grievance (the CSM), a courtship rebuff (Madame) and a bawdy "lumped" legend; the reuse of one tune is the unit's way to put a new target on a known frame.

**[E12a-35]. *Skibboo*: a bawdy ballad with an endless line of descent, a self-censoring last stanza, and two stanzas the editors would not print.** (PW-08, pp. 52-53; L1970-2045)
A "German officer crossed the Rhine" looking for "women and wine," asks a landlord about his daughter; the sexual content (including a reference to the daughter's age and a boast) is summarized, not quoted. The song's last stanza is a plain piece of meta-humor: "It's a hell of a song that we've just sung / And the blighter that wrote it ought to be hung." Editors' note: they "slightly euphemized the refrain" (an uncensored variant ran, "Skibboo-skiatica-hell-of-a-skittika-dam'-gor-blimey"); "Before the closing stanza are two so obscenely graphic as to leave us no option but to suppress them." The note also claims "Skibboo may just possibly be the earliest part, the operative origin of the whole *Mademoiselle from Armenteers* group," sung before the War, in essentials "a parody of Uhland's *The Landlady's Daughter*," with the intermediate *Two German Officers* and a pre-War *Three German Officers* that may predate the Franco-Prussian War or reach back "to the Waterloo campaign, when English soldiers came into close friendly contact with the Germans." Mechanism: bawdy ballad as a vehicle of an enemy figure (a "German officer") in a stock role; ending by cursing its own author.
*Candidate application:* The way that the same anecdote is handed down and shifted from one enemy to another (and, in this case, from a literary ballad into the barrack room) shows that "enemy" figures in bawdy songs are slots, not portraits.

### C. "Songs sung on the march, but more often in billets and estaminets" (printed pp. 55-73)

**[E12a-36]. *Wash Me in the Water* (air: a Salvation Army hymn tune): hymn-parody whose target slot ("your dirty daughter") is swapped for "the Colonel's daughter."** (PW-08, p. 57; L2116-2152)
Verbatim, public-domain: "Wash me in the water / That you washed your dirty daughter, / And I shall be whiter / Than the whitewash on the wall." The note: "For 'your dirty daughter' read 'the Colonel's daughter'—according to taste and circumstances. This was a pre-War Regular Army song that was enthusiastically welcomed and continually sung by the whole B.E.F." It was usually sung without a chorus, but the Brigade of Guards, marching through Beuvry one night in 1915, was heard to sing it "with a chorus: 'than the wall, than the wall.'" The editors say its tune is the same as that of *Miles Behind the Lines* (Postscript, outside my range). Mechanism: a revival-hymn tune (washing and whiteness) with an insulting object slot; the indirect insult to the colonel runs through his daughter.
*Candidate application:* The "swap the name to taste" mechanism is how a common song becomes a local grievance without its writing being private to one unit.

**[E12a-37]. *Hush! Here Comes a Whizz-bang* (air: "Hush! Here Comes the Dream Man," a pre-War pantomime song): the shell warning sung as a lullaby, with "the wonders of No-Man's Land" as the euphemism for being killed.** (PW-08, p. 58; L2158-2177)
Verbatim, public-domain: "Hush! Here comes a whizz-bang, / Hush! Here comes a whizz-bang, / Now you soldiers, get down those stairs, / Down in your dug-outs and say your prayers. / Hush! Here comes a whizz-bang, / And it's making straight for you: / And you'll see all the wonders of No-Man's Land / If a whizz-bang (bump!) hits you." The "(bump!)" is a sound effect that stands where the death would be. Mechanism: a nursery "hush" tune; the instruction to "say your prayers" is the genre of a bedtime song; "see all the wonders" turns the trip across No-Man's Land into a sightseeing outing, and the stage-direction "bump!" does the work. The editors name this song twice in the introduction (E12a-5): they say a man who sang it did not change the shelling, but "the very knowledge of such songs reduced the emotional disorganization caused by fear." **Tension flagged:** the same introduction says "no one chirruped under a bombardment" (E12a-10); the editors do not say when this song was sung relative to shelling, only that it was known.
*Candidate application:* A children's lullaby tune used for incoming fire is a precise model of euphemism-plus-nursery rhyme; its punch is the invitation to "see the wonders" and a drum-beat for the hit.

**[E12a-38]. *The Bells of Hell* (air: a Salvation Army hymn tune, queried by the editors): Scripture on death parodied as a jaunty exemption, "for you but not for me."** (PW-08, p. 59; L2183-2206)
Verbatim, public-domain: "The bells of hell go ting-a-ling-a-ling / For you but not for me: / And the little devils how they sing-a-ling-a-ling / For you but not for me. / O Death, where is thy sting-a-ling-a-ling, / O Grave, thy victor-ee? / The bells of hell go ting-a-ling-a-ling, / For you but not for me." Editors' note: "In pre-War days this was a little-known Negro spiritual. In 1914-1918 it attained a new significance." (The editors' phrase is the period term; I record it as theirs.) Mechanism: a biblical line (the apostle's taunt at death, "O Death, where is thy sting?") is run through bell onomatopoeia, with the singer's exemption ("not for me") as a boast that is tempting fate; the object of the verse is the neighbor who is the one hit, so the song works whether it is a compliment or a death-wish for the listener.
*Candidate application:* The survivor's dark boast, sung toward the one who is hit or who is about to be, sits close to survivor guilt; a character who still hums it years later may be carrying exactly that.

**[E12a-39]. *I Don't Want to Die*: the plain statement of fear, and the editors' claim that ridicule "conquers" the war-weariness it expresses.** (PW-08, p. 60; L2212-2240)
Verbatim, public-domain: "I want to go home, / I want to go home, / I don't want to go in the trenches no more, / Where whizz-bangs and shrapnel they whistle and roar. / Take me over the sea / Where the Alleyman can't get at me. / Oh my, / I don't want to die, / I want to go home." The editors' note: "This was one of the most famous of war songs. It marks the first disillusionments of the 1914 volunteer when he discovered that war was not romantic and that the Germans were not subhuman. But it is very far from défaitisme. It certainly expresses the soldier's war-weariness, but it also conquers it with ridicule." ("Alleyman" is the soldier's German, from the French *Allemand*; my gloss.) Mechanism: a literal request in child's rhythm ("Oh my"), whose ridicule (per the editors) is in the childishness.
*Candidate application:* The editors' claim, that a flat wish to live is made bearable by being sung in a silly voice, is a testable pattern: the sillier the tune, the safer the plain fear.

**[E12a-40]. *We Are the Boys Who Fear No Noise*: mock-heroics with a confession omitted from the print.** (PW-08, p. 61; L2246-2254; title from the Contents list, since the OCR dropped it)
Verbatim, public-domain: "We are the boys who fear no noise / When the thundering cannons roar. / We are the heroes of the night / And we'd sooner [word omitted in print] than fight, / We're the heroes of the Stand-Back Fusiliers." Mechanism: a patriotic-boast pastiche turned by the third line into a confession of cowardice; the "Stand-Back Fusiliers" is an invented regiment of men who hold back. The omitted word in the fourth line is a coarse one. The editors do not annotate the song in the pages in my range.
*Candidate application:* Naming an imaginary regiment of those who "stand back" is a way to make cowardice a unit identity, available to a soldier who does not want to be accused of it individually.

**[E12a-41]. *If the Sergeant Steals Your Rum* (air: "Never Mind"): theft, shelling and death flattened to one refrain, and the pay that stops when you die.** (PW-08, p. 62; L2256-2295)
Verbatim, public-domain, all three stanzas in part: "If the sergeant steals your rum, / Never mind! ... Though he's just a bloody sot, / You can let him take the lot ... If old Jerry shells the trench, / Never mind! ... Though the blasted sandbags fly / You have only once to die ... If you get stuck on the wire, / Never mind! ... Though the light's as broad as day / When you die they stop your pay." The editors name the second stanza in the introduction (E12a-5) as an instance of songs that did not reduce shelling but reduced "emotional disorganization." Mechanism: a single understated refrain ("Never mind!") equates three events of very different weight; the last stanza supplies the record-keeping detail (pay stops at death) as the final indignity, in a place where the reader expects the horror.
*Candidate application:* The ordering (petty theft; shelling; dying on the wire) is a climb in danger set to a refrain that never changes; the bureaucratic last detail (the pay) is the form of the joke that works as a signature of institutional gallows humor.

**[E12a-42]. *When This Blasted War Is Over* (air: hymn "Take it to the Lord in prayer"): a post-service fantasy with rank reversal.** (PW-08, p. 63; L2301-2348)
Verbatim, public-domain, in part: "When this blasted war is over, / No more soldiering for me. / When I get my civvy clothes on, / Oh, how happy I shall be! / No more church parades on Sunday, / No more asking for a pass, / I shall tell the Sergeant-Major / To stick his passes [word omitted]. ... I shall sound my own revally, / I shall make my own tattoo: / No more N.C.O.'s to curse me, / No more bloody Army stew." An extra stanza "many units sang": "N.C.O.'s will all be navvies, / Privates ride in motor cars; / N.C.O.'s will smoke their woodbines, / Privates puff their big cigars. / No more standing-to in trenches, / Only one more church-parade; / No more shiv'ring on the firestep, / No more Tickler's marmalade." (A gloss: "revally" is reveille, "tattoo" is the evening call, "woodbines" are cheap cigarettes, "Tickler's" a jam maker; the last is my gloss from context.) Mechanism: a prayer-hymn tune used for a worldly wish-list; the second stanza's reversal puts former NCOs in laboring jobs and privates in cars and cigars. Note that the editors' introduction calls this list of "ribald promises" (E12a-8); what the printed text shows is mostly freedom from routine and a rank inversion, not the ribald; the one coarse item is the sergeant-major line.
*Candidate application:* The "world turned upside-down" stanza is the most specific documented form of the class-and-rank resentment in the book; its jibe is not at the officers.

**[E12a-43]. *When the Stew Is on the Table* (air: "When the Roll is Called up Yonder"): a hymn about the last-judgment roll-call, repurposed as a promise to turn up for dinner.** (PW-08, p. 64; L2354-2367)
Verbatim, public-domain: "When the stew is on the table, / When the stew is on the table, ... I'll be there. / When the beer is in the tankard, ... I'll be there." Mechanism: a famous hymn of the roll of the saved, with the soldier's appetite as the faith. *My observation, not the editors':* the glossary entry for "barbed wire" (E12a-60) records the roll-call after an attack as the moment when "hanging on the old barbed wire" was the survivors' ironic answer for a dead man, which gives the hymn's "roll" a second, local meaning. The editors make no such link.
*Candidate application:* Roll-call is a ritual that carries two meanings (attendance, and the count of the dead); jokes that use it have two layers by construction.

**[E12a-44]. *He's a Ragtime Soldier* and *Send Out the Army and the Navy*: the "(I don't think!)" tag as sarcasm marker, and a recruiting-song parody the editors say was credibly composed by an officer.** (PW-08, pp. 65-66; L2373-2423)
*He's a Ragtime Soldier* (air: "Ragtime Lover"): "Early on parade every morning, / Standing to attention with his rifle in his hand ... As happy as the flowers in May / (I don't think!), / Fighting for his King and his Country, / All for a shilling a day." *Send Out the Army and the Navy*: "Send out the Army and the Navy, / Send out the rank and file, / Send out the brave Territorials, / They'll face the danger with a smile / (I don't think!). / Send out my mother, / Send out my sister and my brother, / But for Gawd's sake don't send me!" Editors' note on the second: they omitted it in the first edition, taking it for a music-hall song; it is "credibly said to have been composed by a Scottish officer and first sung in October 1914; it was very popular until about the end of 1916," and is "the former of the two anti-heroic songs mentioned by Mr. J. B. Priestley" (quoted in the Postscript, outside my range). Mechanism: the parenthetical "(I don't think!)" is a spoken-in-song sarcasm tag that reverses the sentiment of the line it follows; the second song ends with a refusal of the very duty named, while naming relatives.
*Candidate application:* The attribution to a Scottish officer complicates the introduction's claim (E12a-2) that these songs are by private soldiers; officers also made and sang the anti-heroic songs, at least in 1914.

**[E12a-45]. *Old Soldiers Never Die* (air: "Kind Thoughts Can Never Die"): the veteran's proverb answered by a one-line second stanza.** (PW-08, p. 67; L2437-2454; title from the Contents, the OCR damaged the heading)
Verbatim, public-domain: "Old soldiers never die, / Never die, / Never die, / Old soldiers never die— / They simply fade away." And the second stanza: "Old soldiers never die ... Young ones wish they would." Mechanism: a pious proverb is followed by a wish that the senior men would; the hostile addendum is a one-line twist, the very mode of invention the introduction describes (E12a-3). Target: the old soldier or "old sweat" who lasts and lectures. No editorial note appears in the lines read.
*Candidate application:* A veteran-character is the target of exactly this joke from his own juniors, and he may know it; the targeting of the survivor is a different thing from the mockery of an officer.

**[E12a-46]. *Plum and Apple* (air: "A Wee Deoch an' Doris"): the jam ration as a front-versus-rear grievance.** (PW-08, p. 68; L2457-2482)
Verbatim, public-domain: "Plum and Apple, / Apple and Plum. / Plum and Apple, / There is always some. / The A.S.C. get strawberry jam / And lashings of rum. / But we poor blokes / We only get— / Apple and plum." Editors' note: "'Plum and Apple' in the early years of the War was the only variety of jam which reached the fighting troops. Later, a variety was provided, and so great had been the obloquy heaped on 'Plum and Apple' that it was rarely seen at all in 1918." Mechanism: a trivial ration is the object of a sustained song; the A.S.C. (Army Service Corps, the rear-echelon transport, named in the glossary as "Ally Sloper's Cavalry," E12a-55) are the people who "get" better; the final note says the grievance, sung, changed the supply.
*Candidate application:* An example of a unit joke that had effect (the jam changed), against the rule of thumb that complaints are only a vent.

**[E12a-47]. *Far, Far from Ypres I Long to Be* (air partly lost to OCR; a "sentimental ballad: pre-War"): the escape wish, with "whizz-bangs" in the sleep-song slot.** (PW-08, p. 69; L2488-2505)
Printed verse: "Far, far from Ypres I long to be, / Where German snipers can't snipe at me. / Damp is my dug-out, / Cold are my feet, / Waiting for whizz-bangs [last words badly damaged in the OCR; it appears to say 'send me to sleep']." Note: "Pronounce 'Ypres'—'Eepree.'" The editors cite the song with *I Don't Want to Die* and *I Don't Want to be a Soldier* as songs that "poked fun at the soldier's own desire for peace and rest" (E12a-5). **Caution:** the OCR for this page is damaged (the air's title and the last line are not clearly legible), so I do not quote the last line.
*Candidate application:* The wish to be elsewhere, said in a cozy lullaby register, with a wounding weapon as the sleep-bringer, is the same device as *Hush!* (E12a-37).

**[E12a-48]. *Glorious!* and *Good-bye, Nellie*: one beer-shortage joke and one song the editors say was sung with no irony at all.** (PW-08, pp. 70-71; L2548-2571)
*Glorious!*: "Glorious! Glorious! / One bottle of beer among the [number illegible in the OCR] of us. / Thank Gawd there are no more of us, / Or one of us would go dry." *My reading, not the editors':* where the group is small, the beer is enough; the joke is a gratitude for the smallness of the group, and in the context of a front-line unit a reader may also hear the thinning of a unit by casualties in the line, but the text does not say that. *Good-bye, Nellie*: "Good-bye, Nellie, / I'm going across the main ... I'm going to do my duty / For the girl I love." Editors' note: "Sung with great sentiment, and no notion that it was ridiculous. Possibly pre-War, but absolutely renewed by the soldiers." The introduction calls it "the slow, heavy melancholy of the soldier's sadder moods" (E12a-9).
*Candidate application:* The same repertoire holds a straight, sentimental song sung without any distance; the humor register has a seam, and the editors mark where the seam is.

**[E12a-49]. *The Old Barbed Wire*: a roll-call of who is where, climbing from the sergeant on the canteen floor to the battalion on the wire.** (PW-08, pp. 72-73; L2573-2671)
Five stanzas, each beginning "If you want to find the [X], / I know where he is, I know where he is": the sergeant ("He's lying on the canteen floor"); the quarter-bloke, i.e., the quartermaster-sergeant ("He's miles and miles behind the line"); the sergeant-major ("He's boozing up the privates' rum"); the C.O. ("He's down in the deep dug-outs"); and, in a different register, the old battalion: "They're hanging on the old barbed wire." The editors' note: "A significant variant in the last stanza was *your sweetheart* for *the old battalion*, with the corresponding grammatical changes." The introduction calls it "pathetic only in the last stanza," and "almost the sole indication in song that the soldier was aware of his own dark fate" (E12a-9), and puts the colonel, CSM and QMS as its "comic villains" (E12a-7). Mechanism: a cumulative accusation list in which each rank is located by a lapse (drunk, absent, sheltered), and then the same formula locates the dead; the refrain "I've seen him, I've seen him" is the witness voice. The last stanza exploits the pattern: the listener has been trained to laugh at the location and must stop.
*Candidate application:* This is the clearest documented example in the book of a song that builds a comic frame in order to break it; a character who knows this song knows that the laughter is a set-up.

### D. Chants and songs "rarely, if ever, sung on the march" (printed pp. 75-86)

**[E12a-50]. Folk-ballad recitations whose punchlines the print withholds: *It Was Christmas Day in the Workhouse*, *Old Mother Riley*, *My Nelly*, and the note on *Mother Hunt*.** (PW-08, pp. 77-81; L2705-2815)
The first recitation has two parts: the workhouse pauper's refusal of the Christmas pudding, and a harem version in which the sultan asks the eunuchs what they would like. In both, the final line is cut off in the print (the withheld answers are coarse and are summarized, not quoted; see register). Editors' note: "This in pre-War days was a kind of folk-ballad: in 1914-1918 it became essentially a soldiers' ballad ... the pre-War folk-ballad and folk-song element is very strong"; the soldiers of 1914-1918 "gave to numerous pre-War Regular Army words and phrases a new and more picturesque life, so they adopted or adapted many folk-songs ... and rendered them more vivid"; they also omitted *Twelve Are the Twelve Apostles*, "but we have to draw a rather rigid line in this group, for otherwise we should not know where to stop." *Old Mother Riley* (broken off in the print at the punchline "he had only one"): "Before 1914, a male folk-song; during the War, very definitely a soldiers' song." *My Nelly's a Goer*: a bawdy character-song (the printed refrain stops short of the key word). The note on *Mother Hunt* survives only in OCR fragments: it is a "gay and ribald commentary on an old lady's misfortunes" that "can hardly be printed here," and in pre-War days it existed as a ditty "sung occasionally by 'the [word lost in OCR] of the village.'" Mechanism: the unprinted punchline is the joke; the print withholds it in at least four places in one group.
*Candidate application:* Bawdy recitation is a participatory form: everyone knows the last line and the performer pauses, so that the audience says it.

**[E12a-51]. *Casey Jones* (air: a War-period music-hall comic song): the railway hero reassigned to a firestep.** (PW-08, p. 80; L2779-2798)
Verbatim, public-domain: "Casey Jones, / Standing on the fire-step, / Casey Jones, / With a pistol in his hand. / Casey Jones, / Standing on the fire-step, / Firing Very lights into No-Man's Land / (La-diddy-dah-dah— / Dah!—Dah!)." No editorial note. Mechanism: the engine driver of the music-hall song is set on the trench step in the unit's own chores (shooting flare-lights, "Very lights"), with a mock-bugle tag; the hero is a sentry doing a routine task. (The tag may imitate a bugle, but the text does not say so; my reading.)
*Candidate application:* A famous civilian hero is replaced by the humblest routine job at the front; the heroism of sentry duty is made comic and left unremarked.

**[E12a-52]. *I've Lost My Rifle and Bayonet* (air: "Since I Lost You"): a love-lament that is a confession of lost kit, ending in an appeal to the sergeant, and the editors' glossed inventory.** (PW-08, p. 82; L2818-2870)
Verbatim, public-domain: "I've lost my rifle and bayonet, / I've lost my pull-through too, / I've lost my disc and my puttees, / I've lost my four-by-two. / I've lost my housewife and hold-all, / I've lost my button-stick too. / I've lost my rations and greatcoat— / Sergeant, what shall I do?" The note explains each term: pull-through (a cleaning cord with a flannel "four-by-two" soaked in oil), housewife or "hussif" (a sewing compendium), hold-all (a linen pouch for knife, fork, spoon, toothbrush), button-stick (a brass strip under the buttons while paste is applied; "Soldier's friend—a pink metal-cleaning paste"), and the identity disc ("so that, if he were killed or rendered unconscious, he could be identified"). A popular variant: "I've lost the socks that you sent me— / They lasted the whole winter through. / I've lost the razor that shaved me, / I've lost my four-by-two, / I've lost my hold-all, so now I've got blow-all, / Since I've lost you." Mechanism: a ballad of romantic loss is run as an inventory of kit; the climactic "Sergeant, what shall I do?" puts the NCO in the role of confessor; the variant moves from issue gear to gifts from home and ends on a pun on "hold-all"/"blow-all."
*Candidate application:* The lost-kit list is a recognizable anxiety dream of the trained soldier and makes it communal.

**[E12a-53]. *Michael Finnigan*, *I Don't Want to be a Soldier*, *Can We Clean Your Windows?* and the sergeant chant: four short pieces with distinct mechanisms.** (PW-08, pp. 83-86; L2877-2960)
*Michael Finnigan*: nonsense about a man whose whiskers were blown in again by the wind; a pre-War folk-song that "the soldiers resuscitated" and gave "a new life much more vigorous." *I Don't Want to be a Soldier* (air: "On Sunday I Walk out with a Soldier," a revue song, "perhaps *Bric-a-Brac*"): a parody that names two feared wounds in plain terms (a bayonet in the belly and the loss of a body part to a shot; summarized, not quoted) and offers as the alternative to "live on the earnings of a lady-typist". *Can We Clean Your Windows?* (a chant): the window-cleaner's patter, the householder's "Not to-day. / Run away!," and the cleaner's disappointed "Drat it!" whose rhyme with "bucket" is deliberately withheld ("For *drat it* understand a dissyllabic phrase rhyming with bucket"), i.e., the audience is meant to supply an obscenity. *The sergeant chant* (title lost to OCR; from the introduction it is *Greeting to the Sergeant*, "magnificent commination," E12a-7; and cross-referenced as page 86 in the note to *We Haven't Seen the Sergeant*): four lines that open with a false compliment to the sergeant and then wish him a violent and lingering death (summarized; see register). Mechanism: three of the four rely on the unsaid or the unsinging word, and the last is a plain curse in a quatrain.
*Candidate application:* The editors' own selection puts the most hostile song (the sergeant's death-wish) at the end of the songs; it is also the one the editors praise most strongly.

### E. The glossary of soldiers' slang, entries A through "Issue" (printed pp. 93-130; L2963-5000)

*Arrangement note: the glossary is alphabetical and my range ends at the entry "Issue" (the definition is cut at the boundary of my range, L5000). Findings below group entries by theme and are keyed by headword and line number; they are not in alphabetical order. An asterisk before a headword in the book marks "a word not strictly slang: official, semi-official, or technical" (L2966).*

**[E12a-54]. The glossary's editorial voice: definitions that carry dry asides, which is itself a documented form of the humor.** (PW-08, Glossary, pp. 93-130; entries cited by headword and line)
The editors promised in the preface (E12a-1) "a record-by-glimpses" with opinions marked as such, and the entries do slip in sardonic commentary. Examples, each quoted from the definition itself: *Artillery Duel* (L3095-3102): "In practice, the German artillery battered the British infantry, and our artillery the German infantry, until supplies of ammunition ran short. It was a pleasant sport for the gunners." *H.E.* (L4887-4890): shells "for destroying fortifications and trenches, rather than for murder, though they performed this incidental office with great efficiency." *Hom Forty* (L4926-4931): a goods wagon marked "Hommes 40, Chevaux 8," with the parenthesis "(N.B.—The horses were an alternative, not an extra, load.)" *Blighty One* (L3351-3357): a wound serious enough to send a man to England, "a source of great joy to the receiver" and envy to comrades, "A sufficient commentary in itself on the glory of war." *Cadre* (L3596-3612): the editors' dry etymology (E12a-61). *Dixie* (L4197-4212): a cooking pot "devised to bite into the hands when carrying" whose lack of cleaning meant that "every meal brought ancient memories of its predecessors to the nostrils and the palate." *Duckboard* (L4297-4307): they "slip under your feet in the dark, so that the loose end rose and hit you violently on the head," but "made good firewood (illicit) and roof-props for shelters (semi-illicit)." *Fed Up* (L4573-4578): "A constant and recurring state of mind during a war irrational in itself and conducted irrationally." Mechanism: the editors use understatement, parenthesis and a turned phrase ("pleasant sport") in the same way the songs do, which makes the glossary a second, prose corpus of the same humor, from veteran editors writing for civilians twelve years after.
*Candidate application:* A veteran narrating for outsiders retains the deadpan of the line; a character who explains the army to civilians may do it in this tone without any intent to joke.

**[E12a-55]. Nicknames and entries for rear-echelon men, police, brass, and the safe: "Ally Sloper's Cavalry," "Base-Wallah," "Bull-Ring," "Brass Hats," "Big Noise," "Dug-out King."** (PW-08, Glossary; L2991-2999, 3030-3041, 3169-3172, 3191-3197, 3246-3249, 3486-3488, 3528-3533, 4062-4066, 4356-4375, 4599-4604, 4744-4747)
*Ally Sloper's Cavalry*: the A.S.C. (Army Service Corps, road transport), named by the infantry and artillery after a comic-paper character "because, with their good pay, comfort and comparative safety, they were not considered soldiers at all"; variant "Army Safety Corps." *A.P.M.* (Assistant Provost Marshal): "a harrier of too lively subalterns, an eagle eye (? or a vulture's) for omitted salutes, surreptitious drinks, over-stayed passes," and it is "said that A.P.M.'s often acted as guides for Generals desirous of seeing the night-life of the large towns: a suitable employment." *Base-Wallah*: a man "perpetually at the Base, and so living comfortably and safely." *Bull-Ring*: the training ground at the Base, "notorious for persecution of up-the-line soldiers by Base-Wallahs"; the most notorious was at Étaples. *Battle Police*: military police posted behind the line in an attack with revolvers and "authority to stop all unwounded stragglers and herd them back (if necessary with the revolvers) to the danger area where military police never appeared." *Brass Hats*: "Generals and Staff officers," for their gold lace, with a "less derisive and frequent variant: Tabs." *Big Noise*: a "Staff Officer, General, Politician, or Distinguished Foreign Personage visiting the troops. From the loud and important voices used by such persons." *Cushy*: "Soft; comfortable; luxurious. Especially, safe where danger was the general rule. Other people were always getting cushy jobs." *Dug-out King*: "an officer or N.C.O. who took advantage of his rank and office to remain in his dug-out as long as possible. Some of this genus never showed their heads above ground from the moment of entering a trench till it was time to leave" (variant: *dug-out warrior*). *Consolidate*: "To maintain an advantage, especially to curry favor with authority in order that a cushy job might be continued. From the technical consolidate—to fortify and make secure a trench or other position." *Flog* (verb): to sell Government property to civilians; "Quartermaster-Sergeants were popularly supposed to have large bank deposits as a result of successful and methodical flogging." *Gangway for a Naval Officer!*: a facetious cry used either to ask for passage or "of announcing sotto voce the approach of some self-important officer or N.C.O."
Mechanism: the nicknames sort the army by proximity to danger: the safe get either contempt or a comic-paper name; the staff gets a noise or a hat. Note the consistent pairing with *Introduction* (E12a-7), which says staff officers got silence in song but "dark and dangerous emotions"; the glossary shows that for them the slang channel (Brass Hats, Tabs, Big Noise) did the work the songs did not.
*Candidate application:* The slang channel took the hostility that the song channel left out; a character who never sings about generals may still carry a well-stocked set of names for them.

**[E12a-56]. Official language as deceit, and the soldier's names for it: "Eye-Wash," "Camouflage," "Divisional Comic Cuts," "Hot Air," "It's a Game," "Roll on, Duration," "Any Complaints?," "Green Envelope."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3618-3625, 4186-4191, 4387-4396, 4498-4516, 4739-4742, 3962-3974, 4836-4843, 4945-4950)
*Eye-Wash*: "Official deceit or pretentiousness, especially an appearance of virtue designed to conceal a disgraceful reality. Threats of punishment which could hardly be carried out were eye-wash, official reports of battles were eye-wash, the Orderly Officer's Any Complaints was often eye-wash. In fact much of the Regular Army's tradition of smartness and bustle came under this heading." *Camouflage*: the French term was taken over by soldiers "as a useful variant of eye-wash." *Divisional Comic Cuts*: "Soldiers' term for cheering communiqués issued from Divisional Headquarters, in which some of the information was known to be false and the rest was suspected—from experience" (named after a children's humorous paper). *Hot Air*: "Loose talk with less significance than is intended by the talker; unfounded rumors; bumptiousness; official phraseology; the whiting on the sepulchre." *Game*: "'It's a game!' meant, 'It's absurd! There's no sense in it.' Frequently applied to the war and to the military machine." *Duration*: volunteers enlisted for "four years or the duration of the war"; "'Roll on, Duration,' i.e. God end the war and make me a civilian again, was a frequent exclamation in moods of depression." *Complaints*: "A private soldier had to make his complaints through his platoon sergeant ... In practice, it was extremely unwise to make complaints"; the orderly sergeant's *Any Complaints?* was a standard round at dinner time and "Justifiable (i.e. very extreme) complaints about food were sometimes attended to." *Green Envelope*: an envelope exempt from regimental censorship in which the writer signed that the contents gave no information; "issued sparsely and infrequently, but could be obtained in number by bribery or barter." Mechanism: the soldier's terms for official speech define it by its effect on the listener (blurred sight, hot air rising) and treat it as a category of noise to be discounted.
*Candidate application:* A soldier-character's cynicism about communiqués may be sharply specific (named, pre-sorted) and not a general distrust; the existence of the censor-free envelope and the "unwise to complain" rule are plausible reasons letters and songs differ from each other in what they say.

**[E12a-57]. Discipline and punishment vocabulary: "Cap Off," "D.C.M.," "Dead Beat," "Dumb Insolence," "For It," "High Jump," "F.P. No. 1."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3666-3728, 3733-3737, 4083-4094, 4378-4384, 4641-4665, 4892-4895, 4041-4042)
*Cap Off* (the most detailed entry in my range): a man reported for something like "appearing on parade with a button unfastened or his hair more than one-eighth of an inch long" was "presented" to his company commander, "capless, beltless, flanked on either side by his escort, his heels together, his thumbs in line with the seam of his trousers," heard the charge read in "cumbersome and almost meaningless phrases," and was "usually too bewildered (or too old a soldier) to utter a word." The editors' judgment: "Once the machinery of military law was set in motion, there was little injustice done, except what is inherent in its rough-and-ready methods. Injustices began before that, with officers and N.C.O.'s who would invent crimes out of malice or wantonness. If a soldier incurred the dislike of an unscrupulous superior, his life was made a misery and there was no way of escaping a thousand daily vexations and petty punishments." They point to A. P. Herbert's *The Secret Battle* (English court-martial) and A. J. Evans's *The Escaping Club* (German). *D.C.M.*: "Most civilians knew that this stood for Distinguished Conduct Medal. To soldiers its first connotation, however, was District Court Martial." *Dead Beat* (exhausted, "past caring"; variant "Beaten to the Wide"): "Probably from the old days of penal flogging for slight offenses." *Dumb Insolence*: "A military crime. Allowing a derisive or disbelieving or amused or uninterested expression to appear on one's face when spoken to by a superior officer. An opportunity for exploiting personal dislike which, however, was rarely taken." *For It*: "Due for trouble"; *For the High Jump*: due for trial before the C.O. *F.P. No. 1* (Field Punishment No. 1): the offender was bound to a gun-wheel for an hour morning and evening for up to twenty-eight days; the editors state it "was to humiliate as well as to exhaust him, and his comrades were seemingly expected to jeer at him"; that some men were said to have been lashed to guns in action; but also that "by an open conspiracy of humanitarian origin," the period was arranged to fall while the battalion was in the line, "so that no punishment at all was possible." Mechanism: the vocabulary is a map of what could not be answered (a face, a button, a hair length), and the pun on D.C.M. is a one-word joke that swaps honor for punishment. This is also the system the song *Yes! And We Can Do It!* (E12a-17) recites.
*Candidate application:* A world in which the expression on one's face can be an offense explains why humor goes sideways into songs, nicknames and puns on initials; a unit's "open conspiracy" to make a cruel punishment not happen is itself a type of unspoken solidarity.

**[E12a-58]. *Après la guerre*: the editors' gloss gives the phrase two uses, one a joke about the indefinitely remote and one a "depository of secret sentiment."** (PW-08, Glossary, p. 94; L3044-3068)
The entry argues that the phrase "was so soon caught from the French" as "a sure indication of how much the thought and wish for peace was in everyone's mind," and "is not to be construed as défaitisme." Use 1: "jokingly for the indefinite and remote future," e.g. to the question "When will you marry me?" the answer "Oh, après la guerre," or "When's the Colonel coming up the line?—Après la guerre." Use 2: "a depository of secret sentiment, of longing for survival and for the return of decency, quiet and beauty." The editors point to both in the ribald ditty (E12a-13) and in a 1917-18 music-hall song whose refrain began "Après la guerre, There'll be a good time everywhere: smiling faces, gladsome missus." Mechanism: a loan-phrase that serves as both a refusal ("never") and a prayer ("when it is over").
*Candidate application:* A phrase that is simultaneously a deflection and a hope is a good model for how a post-service character deflects questions about the future.

**[E12a-59]. Shells, guns and bombardments as named things: "Archie," "Coal-box," "Crump," "Bump," "Dud," "Flying Pigs," "Footballs," "Grass-Cutters," "The Hate," "Iron Foundries," "Iron Rations," "What time does the balloon go up?"** (PW-08, Glossary; L3070-3071, 3123-3126, 3550-3554, 3949-3951, 4046, 4316-4320, 4612-4631, 4827-4833, 4883-4885, 4991-4995)
*Archie*: "facetious name for anti-aircraft gun; and also for its shell bursts." *Balloon*: "What time does the balloon go up? was a favorite way of asking the time fixed for any special parade or 'stunt'." *Bump*: "The near-by explosion of a shell. Used as a verb almost always in the passive voice, e.g. 'We didn't half get bumped last night.'" *Coal-box*: the shellburst of a 5.9 or heavier shell, "from the black smoke" (variants *Johnson*, *Crump*). *Dud*: a shell that failed to explode ("the subdued thud, heavy in itself but so much less than the expected explosion"), also an inefficient person ("The new sergeant's a proper dud on parade"). *Flying Pigs*: trench mortar bombs; *Footballs*: trench mortar bombs "because of their size and shape"; *Flying Darts*: airplane darts "falling in numbers with a skimming flight." *Grass-Cutters*: small air-dropped bombs scattering shrapnel pellets at low level "to kill rather than to destroy material things." *Hate*: "A bombardment, e.g. 'The morning hate is due now.' An allusion to Lissauer's Hymn of Hate, composed in August, 1914." *Iron Foundries*: "Heavy shelling." *Iron Rations*: the emergency ration, "also sometimes used for shells, e.g. 'There goes Jerry's iron rations!'" Mechanism: the weapons are named after domestic objects (pigs, footballs, coal, grass-cutters, rations) or after a social appointment (a morning "hate"; "the balloon goes up" as a way of asking when the day's chief event is due); the passive-voice grammar of *bump* ("get bumped") makes the shell the agent. (Further weapon entries continue in Part 2.)
*Candidate application:* Naming weapons for harmless household things, and putting the victim in the passive voice, is a regular pattern in this book; a veteran character's vocabulary for incoming fire is probably domestic.

**[E12a-60]. "Barbed Wire": the glossary entry that records "hanging on the old barbed wire" as an euphemism for a death, used at roll-call.** (PW-08, Glossary, p. 96; L3135-3149; and *Concertina Wire*, L3988-3990)
The entry first explains the entanglements both sides erected, that men were frequently caught in them at night and in daytime attacks, and then (in physical detail I summarize and do not quote; see register) what became of the dead who could not fall to the ground. "Hence it became a common euphemism for death, hard and bitter, to say that a man was 'hanging on the old barbed wire.' This was an ironic reply often given by survivors at roll-call after an attack, when the name of a man who had been killed was called." It cross-refers to the song *The Old Barbed Wire* (E12a-49). *Concertina Wire* is a barbless French wire that "when touched, it coiled about the intruder." Mechanism: a place-based phrase becomes a stock answer to a roll-call, i.e., the standard administrative question "present?" is answered with a joke that reports a death; the euphemism is delivered by the survivors at the moment of counting.
*Candidate application:* Roll-call responses are the right moment for such a phrase: a ritual in which the answer is mandatory and the euphemism lets a comrade say "dead" without saying it.

**[E12a-61]. Casualty, identity and "dock" language: "Cold Meat Ticker," "Dock," "C.C.S.," "Cadre," "Cannon-Fodder," "Fini Kapout," "Draft," "Gold Stripes," "went west."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3740-3743, 3596-3612, 3631-3634, 3958, 4220-4223, 4265-4277, 4584-4595, 4245-4250, 4789-4792, 3826-3833)
*Cold Meat Ticker*: "Identity disc." *Dock* (hospital, "used only in the phrase *in dock*"): "The metaphor is taken from the sea, ships going into dock for repairs." *C.C.S.* (Casualty Clearing Station): "Tents or huts behind the lines where operations were performed, and wounded were classified into dangerous, slight and 'return to England' cases." *Cadre* (the editors' entry, quoted at length because the example is the point): "As the war progressed it was found that a battalion put into an attack was almost sure to lose anything from fifty to ninety per cent of its strength in casualties (far more than the old-fashioned decimation—one in ten). It was extremely difficult to build up the shell-shocked and dispirited remainder ... Hence arose the practice of keeping a cadre out of the slaughter so that a continuity of organization could be preserved after the attack had failed or partially succeeded. This historical fact introduced into an argument will blunt at once all loose glorification of the war of attrition. By a significant coincidence the word cadre is closely related to cadaver = a corpse." *Cannon-Fodder*: "All combatant soldiers below the rank of, say, major: especially the infantry. A journalistic expression, only used with the implication of quotation, by soldiers." *Fini Kapout*: French slang for "finished"; the editors give the surrender plea "Kamarade, pas kapout" and the answer "perhaps ... 'Fini kapout'" (Dauzat's definition lists "tué" among its senses). *Draft* (reinforcements): "old soldiers were cynical and depressed, new soldiers curious and elated ... 'We, about to die, salute thee, O Bacchus!'" *Doolally* example: "Ever since his mate went west, he's been doolally tap" (the euphemism "went west" for death appears only in this example sentence in my range). *Gold Stripes*: thin gold stripes on the lower left sleeve, one for each occasion wounded (instituted 1916). *Chevron*: later-war sleeve chevrons for service years, red for 1914, blue later. Mechanism: the vocabulary around the dead and wounded is mostly mechanical (disc, dock, clearing station, cadre), and its dark joke is that it makes the person a thing being processed ("cold meat ticker"); the editors' *Cadre* entry turns the euphemism on its head by pointing to the corpse in the word.
*Candidate application:* "Cold Meat Ticker" is a precise example of gallows slang that names a safety device by the thing it will be used for; a soldier's name for the identity disc may be wry, unsentimental and entirely functional.

**[E12a-62]. *Blighty* and *Blighty One*: England as "faerie" and the wound that earns the trip.** (PW-08, Glossary, pp. 99-100; L3316-3357)
*Blighty*: "In this one word was gathered all the soldier's homesickness and affection and war-weariness"; the military machine "effectively stultified his desire to return, and his own sense of duty held him in France." The editors' longer comment: "He had entered another, insane, existence, ugly, precarious, fatiguing ... Blighty to the soldier was a sort of faerie, a paradise which he could faintly remember, a never-never land." The word is also an adjective of approval ("real Blighty butter"), from the Hindustani *bilaik* ("foreign country") and *bilaiti begun* ("foreign egg-plant, i.e. tomato"). *Blighty One*: a wound sufficient to take a man back to England: "Minor and mortal wounds were treated in France, but a Blighty One was naturally a source of great joy to the receiver, and aroused envy, mingled with congratulations, in his comrades. A sufficient commentary in itself on the glory of war." (Cross-reference to *Packet*, in Part 2.) Mechanism: the object of longing is a wound; congratulations and envy are the unit's response to a comrade who is injured.
*Candidate application:* The wish for a "nice" wound is a recurring soldiers' fantasy and an obvious contradiction; for a character with a chronic injury, the old "Blighty One" idea has a bitter second-order meaning (the wound that did not send him home).

**[E12a-63]. Drink, binge, cheer and small comforts: "Binge," "Blind," "Blotto," "Chin-Chin," "Cheero," "Be Good," "Bon Time," "Buckshee," "Any More for Any More," "Box Open, Box Shut," "Estaminet," "Eggs and Chips."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3005-3016, 3204-3210, 3271-3283, 3371-3389, 3430-3440, 3463-3479, 3510-3518, 3813-3823, 3847-3850, 4429-4437, 4455-4480, 4527-4532)
*Binge*: "An expedition deliberately undertaken in company for the purpose of relieving depression, celebrating an occasion or a spasm of high spirits, by becoming intoxicated. But music and singing are essentially part of a binge. More an officers' word than a private soldier's." *Blind*: a "more expert and brutal term than binge," derived from "the power of liquor to derange the eyesight." *Blotto*: "Perhaps facetiously derived from the absorbing qualities of blotting-paper." *Chin-Chin*: from the Chinese, said before drinking, "implying general good-will, satisfaction and hope." *Cheero*: "A modern unpretentious equivalent of the Roman Ave and Vale, as well as the Saxon Wassail!" *Be Good*: "An almost nonsensical exclamation, used in parting company, chiefly by officers ... 'Be good. And if you can't be good, be careful.'" *Bon Time*: "A period, brief or not, when unexpected happiness came the way of the soldier." *Buckshee*: "Surplus; free; adjective applied to anything come by with unexpected ease." *Any More for Any More*: "A jocular shout by the orderly man when he had given every man his share of a meal, and still had some stew or tea over remaining." *Box Open, Box Shut*: a phrase when offering cigarettes "The inference being that the donor's generosity was limited by hard circumstance. Those who were quick or near got a cigarette." *Estaminet*: "There is no equivalent in this country ... they furnished the jolliest and most heart-warming experience the private soldier met with." *Eggs and Chips*: "Probably the most popular dish in France ... a wartime invention due to the scarcity and high prices on one hand of bacon, and on the other of steak." Mechanism: the vocabulary of pleasure is made of exchanges (offers, shouts, toasts) rather than objects; a "binge" is defined by its purpose (to relieve depression) and by the necessity of singing.
*Candidate application:* The formal toast and the formal offer ("box open, box shut") are rituals of limited generosity; they let a hard-up man be generous within a joke.

**[E12a-64]. Food and ration jokes: "Bully-Beef," "Fray Bentos," "Hard Tack," "Emergency Rations," "Dixie," "Burgoo," "Duff."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3535-3541, 3557-3564, 4197-4212, 4323-4325, 4448-4452, 4671-4687, 4871-4872)
*Bully-Beef*: tinned beef, chiefly Argentine; "When the cooks got it, it was served in stew. When the cooks' 'services were not available,' it was eaten either cold or fried in a canteen." *Fray Bentos*: the prevalent brand, "so that it was often used as a synonym for the whole genus. Also, facetiously, as an approximation to 'très bien,' especially in reply to an inquiry about one's health and well-being. 'Oh, I'm Fray Bentos.'" *Hard Tack*: "Bully-beef and biscuits eaten uncooked and cold." *Emergency Rations*: a tin of bully and a few biscuits, "officially not to be touched except in emergencies ... Usually, an infernal nuisance." *Burgoo*: porridge (Turkish and Arabic *burghul*). *Duff*: pudding, also the verb "to do a job incompetently." Mechanism: a brand name is made into the answer to "how are you?" (a pun on *très bien*), i.e., the ration becomes a way of saying "fine" without meaning it.
*Candidate application:* A greeting formula built from the least-liked ration is a typical form of ritualized bitterness: it can be exchanged without any sign of complaint.

**[E12a-65]. Lice: "Chat," "Coot," "Crabs," "Crummy," "Hitchy-Koo," "De-lousing Station," and the editors' remark that chatting was "almost a social occasion."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3750-3807, 4016-4018, 4038-4045, 4116-4123, 4920-4923)
*Chat* (louse): "found (or it escaped) in hordes." The verb meant ridding oneself of some of them by searching seams or running seams over a candle flame, which "announced the death of the enemy—and eggs." "Living in the same clothes without a bath for weeks on end, it was impossible for a private soldier to be without them, and the most thorough chattings were little more than demonstrations of ill-will. Baths and a change of clothing at a delousing station were the only relief—and they happened rarely. As time went on, chatting became part of normal routine during quiet and rest periods; indeed, it was almost a social occasion, and in huts or dug-outs during the winter, and outdoors if there was summer sunshine, rows of men could often be seen with their shirts or trousers or tunics laid over their knees, cracking lice and jokes together." The editors quote Déchelette (French soldiers' slang) that lice "fear solitude and have a profound sense of family," multiplying "in their numerous leisure hours" (my translation; the book prints the French), and that lice leave a man soon after he is killed, once he is cold (summarized; see register). *Hitchy-Koo*: itchy from lice, "From the nonsense chorus of one of the earliest 'ragtime' songs, pre-war." *De-lousing Station*: baths where men exchanged "lice-ridden clothing, not for new but for old garments which had been baked in ovens." *Coot* (OCR; probably "Cooty" in the book) is "Pre-war cockney: said to be derived from the name of a titled lady who suffered in this way" ; *Crummy*: "Itchy because of louse-bites." *Crabs* (lice) is cross-referred to *Draw Crabs*, which in the glossary means to attract enemy artillery fire by exposing oneself on observed ground (L4279-4281).
*Candidate application:* The one place the book names a joke-telling ritual outright is the louse-hunt: a mundane task done in rows that provides the occasion for humor; for a soldier-character, a repeated, low-status chore may be where jokes live.

**[E12a-66]. Fear, madness and exhaustion named indirectly: "Cold Feet," "Get 'Em," "Dug-out Disease," "Funk-Hole," "Doolally," "Dingo," "Dippy," "Chronic," "Come in on your chin-strap," "Dead Beat," "Go Easy."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3954-3955, 4778-4779, 4364-4366, 4728-4730, 4245-4250, 4154-4173, 3885-3889, 3853-3864, 4091-4094, 4403-4414, 3382-3383, 4540)
*Cold Feet*: "Cowardice, temperamental and permanent. Not to be confused with wind-up" (the editors keep a distinction between a passing fright, *wind-up*, and a character fault). *Get 'Em* (to be scared): "'Them' probably referred to shaking fits." *Dug-out Disease*: "Chronic fear of death and danger which kept those, whose rank permitted any choice, safe in their dug-outs"; the editors make fear a rank-linked disease and a privilege (those with no choice could not catch it). *Funk-Hole*: "A dug-out or other shelter. No disparagement of the occupants is implied in this term." *Doolally*: "Mad, insane, not normal. More literally, sunstroke. From Deolali, a place in India where mental cases are segregated in hospital," with the example "Ever since his mate went west, he's been doolally tap." *Dingo* (slightly insane; from French *dingot*, and Dauzat's *dingue* = marsh fever); *Dippy* ("Temporarily insane; foolish; unduly simple"; perhaps from "dipsomania"); *Fanti* (OCR: "Fantr"; mad, from Hindustani); *Block*: to "do one's block" is "to lose one's senses." *Chronic*: "Very bad ... Taken probably from medical idiom, without any idea of its meaning, merely with a vague association of unhappiness"; "The pain's something chronic." *Chin-strap*: the strap on the steel helmet was often lowered round the jaw; "Many men, however, preferred to keep it hanging loosely on the back of the neck for fear" that a blow to the helmet "should cause the strap to choke them or to break their jaw"; "to come in on your chin-strap" meant finishing a march or carrying party so tired "that (figuratively) only the chin-strap kept the body upright." *Dead Beat* (above, E12a-57). *Go Easy*: "a typically English method of protesting against excess of labor, of speed, of eating or drinking, of swearing, of almost anything." *Deep End*: "To go off the deep end was to lose one's temper abruptly and violently." Mechanism: fear and breakdown are named by geography (Deolali), by medicine used wrongly (chronic), by body part (feet), or by a place (dug-out, funk-hole); the strongest group norm is the explicit statement that *funk-hole* is not an insult.
*Candidate application:* The vocabulary lets a soldier say "he's gone doolally" about a comrade's breakdown without a diagnosis; the rule that a shelter's name implies no disparagement of its occupants is a nice small taboo about what may not be teased.

**[E12a-67]. Dug-out hierarchy: who got deep protection and who got a hole in the wall.** (PW-08, Glossary, "Dug-out"; L4328-4358, 4053-4055)
Three kinds: *cubby-holes* dug into a trench wall, "in which one or at most two men could sleep without much comfort"; *shelters* "shored up with wooden props and corrugated iron," often "no more protection overhead than a layer of sandbags, or even a few duckboards or pieces of corrugated iron"; and *deep dug-outs*, "a real protection, but their construction was not encouraged except at H.Q. of various kinds," with stairways "six, ten, or more feet underground," rooms with "wire-netting beds—and even electric light." "It was always a great luxury to capture an undamaged German dug-out, even though its entrance faced the wrong way and might admit a shell-burst." The best had two entrances "so that if one collapsed, there was still an exit." *Dug-out* was also "a facetious name among officers for an oldish officer returning from retirement to active service and displaying little efficiency." This is the factual ground of the song's line "He's down in the deep dug-outs" (E12a-49) and the slang *Dug-out King* (E12a-55).
*Candidate application:* The protective hierarchy is a map that every private could read; the jokes about deep dug-outs are about where the body is placed, not about courage.

**[E12a-68]. Names for the enemy and for allied or colonial troops: which ones privates used, and the one the editors say they did not.** (PW-08, Glossary; L2974-2977, 3019-3028, 3105-3113, 3268, 3392-3428, 3524-3525, 3867-3878, 4161-4167, 4689-4700, 4796-4800, 4862-4864, 4916-4917, 4972-4978)
*Abdul*: "Turk, individual or collective" (cross-referred to *Johnny*). *Boche* (long etymology from French slang, *tête de boche*, *Alboche*, and Déchelette's anagram theory): "Practically never used by private soldiers, confined to officers." *Fritz*: "After 1915 used much less among private soldiers than Jerry except in referring to a near-by shell-burst." *Hun*: "German ... Occasionally applied in the vocative to an unpopular comrade," from a newspaper application after "the destruction of Louvain and other medieval buildings." *His*: "The enemy's. Used especially of shell bursts and airplanes." *Anzac* (the corps name made popular by journalists): "Rarely, if ever, used by English soldiers, who preferred *Aussie* or *Digger*"; *Billjim*: an Australian; *Dinkum*: good or genuine, and "among the Australian troops 'The Dinkums' were those soldiers who had been in Gallipoli"; *A.* "Worn on the regimental shoulder-colors by those Australian troops who had served at Anzac." *Conshie* (conscientious objector): "Some refused to fight, others went further and refused to participate in manual or clerical labor behind the lines or in their own countries," a neutral definition with no contempt in it (compare the contempt carried by *C3*, which the entry says was applied "not to a man who was C3, but, by analogy, to an action"). *Froggie* (a French soldier, from the English amusement at frog's legs); *Budoo* (Bedouin, used in Mesopotamia and Palestine); *Goody-La!* (Chinese Labor Corps for "Good!," "Taken over by the British for facetious use"). The entry for the Chinese Labor Corps (headword not reproduced here) contains period ethnic caricature, which I summarize rather than quote, and the entry for native Egyptians (*Gyppy*) is a bare familiar term whose headword I also do not repeat (see register). Mechanism: the enemy's name changes with the rank of the speaker (the entries say officers used *Boche* and privates did not, and that privates preferred *Jerry*, whose own entry is in Part 2), and the same word can be turned on an unpopular comrade (*Hun*), which makes enemy language a social weapon inside the unit; the Gallipoli-veteran label "Dinkums" is a status badge.
*Candidate application:* Which enemy name is used is a marker of rank and unit; a soldier's word for the enemy as a diminutive person (Fritz, Jerry) or an institution (the Hun) can tell the reader where the speaker stood.

**[E12a-69]. Nicknames by surname: a convention that renames men by who they are on the roll.** (PW-08, Glossary; L3504-3505, 4069-4071, 4240-4242, 4399-4400)
*Buck*: "Nickname for any man named Taylor." *Dolly*: "Nickname for anyone named Gray. From a popular song of the South African War period." *Dusty*: "All men named Miller could be addressed as Dusty." *Darky*: a prefix or synonym for the surname Smith (a convention that has a racial overtone; I summarize and do not repeat the entry's wording; see register). The entries cross-refer to *Nobby*, *Smudger* and *Spud*. Mechanism: the surname is the roll-call identity, and the nickname turns it into an everyday name a stranger could use at once.
*Candidate application:* A ready-made nickname for every surname lets a stranger be addressed instantly and takes the weight off first names; a defector who is addressed by a unit-style nickname by an ex-service peer will be instantly "placed" by it.

**[E12a-70]. Place-name jokes: "Étaples," "Hom Forty," "Funky Villas," "About Turn," "Iggry Corner."** (PW-08, Glossary; L2977, 4483-4495, 4731, 4926-4931, 4981-4983)
*Étaples* (the base town with infantry depots and hospitals): "The cemetery with its crosses reached to the horizon, and the tented depots in bad weather were even more depressing. Conditions at Étaples were so absurdly repressive that in 1917 a mutiny, provoked by the military police, broke out. The only published account of this will be found in the columns of the Manchester Guardian on several dates during February, 1930." The editors add that it was always pronounced "Eetapps" "For some occult reason." *Hom Forty*: a French goods wagon "Hommes 40, Chevaux 8" (E12a-54). *Funky Villas*: Fonquevillers. *About Turn*: Hébuterne. *Iggry Corner*: "Hurry up! (from the Arabic *iggri*). The Australians appropriately named a crossing in Bullecourt: Iggry Corner." Mechanism: a place name is replaced by an English pun or a drill command, so that a dangerous village can be said in a casual way; the account of the Étaples mutiny shows that a suppressed episode still reached print through a newspaper in 1930.
*Candidate application:* "About Turn" for a village known for danger is a clean example of a drill command that doubles as a warning; the Étaples entry is also one of the very few places the book names collective resistance, and it does so in a single sentence.

**[E12a-71]. Forms of address and talk in the unit: "Before You Come Up," "Chum" to a wounded man, "Chew the Fat," "Chew the Rag," "Argue the Toss," "Fair Dos," "Hipe."** (PW-08, Glossary; L3085-3088, 3216-3232, 3836-3844, 3896-3901, 4535-4538, 4897-4913)
*Before You Come Up*: "The classic crushing retort of the private soldier. The unanswerable argument from experience and seniority. It means—'I was in the army before you, I know more than you are capable of knowing, I've suffered more than you, I've done more than you, I'm a better soldier than you and a better man. And I refuse to believe a word you say.'" Later elaborations: "Before you knew what a button-stick was"; "Before you had a regimental number"; "Before you was breeched"; "I was cutting barbed wire while you was cutting your milk teeth." (The editors add a class remark, that "come" is "the normal illiterate error for 'came,'" which is their judgment on speech, not a finding on the retort.) *Chum*: "More popular, at least with non-Cockney troops, than either mate or pal ... Almost invariably used in the opening phrase, speaking to a wounded man" (cross-referenced to the Postscript, outside my range). *Chew the Fat*: "To sulk, to be resentful. Especially to murmur aloud." *Chew the Rag*: "To argue endlessly or without hope of a definite agreement." *Argue the Toss*: "To dispute loudly and long, especially when such argument was an annoyance to others." *Fair Dos*: "Justice, especially in sharing out rations, duties." *Hipe*: a rifle, "the word is derived from the parade-ground practice of malforming words in order to attain an effect of sharpness and authority" ("Slope-Hipe!"), used in "Get your bloody hipes!" (which echoes the introduction's example of how an expected expletive signaled routine and its omission signaled danger, E12a-11). Mechanism: the retort is a seniority claim in which duration of service is the only currency; "chum" to the wounded man is an emotional register that the headword defines by one use.
*Candidate application:* The retort "before you came up" is the perfect shut-down line from a veteran to a new man, and an ex-service character who has not used it in years may use it with mixed shame; the "chum to a wounded man" pointer is something to follow up in Part 2.

**[E12a-72]. What the book says soldiers would NOT sing or say (collected from my range).** (PW-08, various; cited by earlier findings)
(1) *No song about staff officers* (they gave rise only to "dark and dangerous emotions"; E12a-7); officers in general "belonged to a race apart" and "escaped satire" (E12a-7). (2) *The system largely stayed out of song*: "the very discipline against which he rebelled kept his opinions out of his songs" (E12a-6). (3) *No home-made songs of sentiment*: "he invented few songs to fit this recurring mood, partly because he was shy of it" (E12a-9); sentiment about home and wife or girl was expressed "in an indirect and impersonal fashion" through borrowed songs (E12a-10). (4) *No singing under bombardment or exposed fire*: "no one chirruped under a bombardment or in the open exposed to machine-gun and rifle fire" (E12a-10). (5) *No sincere use of "cannon-fodder"* by soldiers: "only used with the implication of quotation" (E12a-61). (6) *"Boche" was practically never used by privates* (E12a-68). (7) *Complaints in the formal channel were "extremely unwise"* (E12a-56). (8) *A facial expression could be a crime* (E12a-57). (9) *Resentment of civilians almost absent* (I Wore a Tunic, "almost the only song," E12a-33; and "after that, the matter was done with," E12a-8). (10) *Many men refused to sing the bawdiest versions* of the bawdy songs (E12a-9). (11) *Funk-hole implies no disparagement* of the men in it (E12a-66). (12) *Sung by whom is partly unknown*: the editors write that no author claimed any song (E12a-4).
*Candidate application:* The silences listed here are a better predictor of a character's speech than the content: a soldier-type may sing about the sergeant, the rations and the pay, and say nothing of the general, the cause or his own fear.

## Pattern roll-up

*(Devices are listed with the two best examples from my range, by finding number. Everything is for the British Expeditionary Force of 1914-18 as remembered in 1930 by two veteran editors; none of it is established for other armies or eras.)*

**Functions (the editors' own account).**
- *Coping after the fact, not during fire:* songs "reduced the emotional disorganization caused by fear" and helped a man "pick his uncertain way back to sanity," but "no one chirruped under a bombardment" ([E12a-5], [E12a-10]). Best examples: *Hush! Here Comes a Whizz-bang* ([E12a-37]); *I Don't Want to Die* ("conquers it with ridicule," [E12a-39]).
- *Mocking one's own wish for peace so that it cannot win:* "poked fun at the soldier's own desire for peace and rest, and so prevented it getting the better of him" ([E12a-5]). Best examples: *I Don't Want to Die* ([E12a-39]); *Far, Far from Ypres* ([E12a-47]).
- *Shy sentiment by proxy:* "affording good protection for his shamefacedness" ([E12a-10]). Best examples: *Rolling Home* hummed without words ([E12a-25]); *Good-bye, Nellie* sung straight ([E12a-48]).
- *Cohesion through shared form:* extensible lists, echo songs, censor-noises sung aloud. Best examples: *Down by the Sea* and *She Was So Good* ([E12a-21]); *John Brown's Baby*'s shouted "shush!" ([E12a-22]).
- *Status and boundary by seniority and badge:* Best examples: "Before You Come Up" ([E12a-71]); "Dinkums," gold stripes, chevrons ([E12a-61], [E12a-68]).

**Targets.**
- *Near, disliked NCOs and the quartermaster* (songs) versus *staff and "brass"* (slang only, and silence): "dark and dangerous emotions" ([E12a-7], [E12a-55]). Best examples: *The Old Barbed Wire* ([E12a-49]); *We Haven't Seen the Sergeant* ([E12a-32]).
- *The rear echelon and its comforts:* Best examples: *Plum and Apple* ([E12a-46]); "Ally Sloper's Cavalry" and "Base-Wallah" ([E12a-55]).
- *The volunteer himself and the unit's competence:* Best examples: *Fred Karno's Army* ([E12a-14]); *What Did You Join the Army For?* ([E12a-29]).
- *The old soldier:* *Old Soldiers Never Die* ("Young ones wish they would," [E12a-45]).
- *Civilians:* one late song only ([E12a-33]); the glossary's "Civvy" is "freighted with much emotion, envy, fond memories and eternal hope" ([E12a-33]).
- *The war's purpose:* *We're Here Because* ([E12a-20]); "It's a game!" ([E12a-56]).

**Forms and mechanics.**
- *Hymn or sacred tune with a trivial or grim text:* Best examples: *Fred Karno's Army* to "The Church's One Foundation" ([E12a-14]); *Raining* to "Holy, Holy, Holy" ([E12a-30]); also *We've Had No Beer*, *When This Blasted War Is Over*, *When the Stew Is on the Table*, *The Bells of Hell*.
- *Sentimental ballad flipped to a thirst or a loss of kit:* Best examples: *I Have No Pain, Dear Mother, Now* ([E12a-27]); *I've Lost My Rifle and Bayonet* ([E12a-52]).
- *One flat refrain that equates events of unequal weight:* Best example: *If the Sergeant Steals Your Rum* ("Never mind!"; "When you die they stop your pay," [E12a-41]).
- *Comic frame built in order to break:* Best example: *The Old Barbed Wire* ([E12a-49]); confirmed by the glossary euphemism ([E12a-60]).
- *Euphemism by domestic naming and by passive voice:* Best examples: "flying pigs," "footballs," "grass-cutters," "iron rations," "the morning hate," "bumped" ([E12a-59]); "see all the wonders of No-Man's Land" ([E12a-37]).
- *Euphemism by place or by institution's own words:* Best examples: "hanging on the old barbed wire" at roll-call ([E12a-60]); "in dock," "cold meat ticker" ([E12a-61]).
- *The withheld word:* print and song both leave the key word for the audience to supply. Best examples: *Can We Clean Your Windows?* ([E12a-53]); the workhouse and harem recitations ([E12a-50]); *John Brown's Baby* ([E12a-22]).
- *Substitution slot:* Best examples: "the Colonel's daughter" for "your dirty daughter" ([E12a-36]); the regiment's name in *We Haven't Seen the Sergeant* ([E12a-32]).
- *Sarcasm tag:* "(I don't think!)" ([E12a-44]).
- *Punishment as credential:* *Yes! And We Can Do It!* ([E12a-17]).
- *Deadpan editorial definition:* "It was a pleasant sport for the gunners" (*Artillery Duel*); "(N.B.—The horses were an alternative, not an extra, load.)" (*Hom Forty*), [E12a-54].
- *Pun on initials:* "D.C.M." = District Court Martial ([E12a-57]).

**Rules of license and limits.**
- *Who may be sung about:* sergeants, sergeant-majors, quartermasters, colonels (and the C.O. in the deep dug-out); not officers in general, and not staff in song ([E12a-7]).
- *The system stays out of song because discipline is real:* a face can be a crime ("Dumb Insolence"); complaint is "extremely unwise"; the green envelope has to be bought or bartered ([E12a-6], [E12a-56], [E12a-57]).
- *Obscenity as an intensifier:* its absence signals real danger (a sergeant who leaves the expletive out; "Get your rifles!"), [E12a-11].
- *No humor on the way out of a casualty:* no song in my range jokes about named dead; the one dead-comrade song is the pathos of the last stanza of *The Old Barbed Wire* ([E12a-49]).
- *Shelter and its name carry no disparagement* ("funk-hole"), [E12a-66].
- *The record is cleaned:* editors suppressed songs and verses and dashed words ([E12a-1], [E12a-19], [E12a-35]).

**Time course.**
- *Songs belong to a phase and die with it:* "the majority are War songs and became obsolete ... the moment the War ended"; *Send Out the Army* popular "until about the end of 1916"; *I Wore a Tunic* late and short-lived; *Sammy Hall* early-war ([E12a-4], [E12a-18], [E12a-33], [E12a-44]).
- *The grievance can change the ration:* *Plum and Apple* ([E12a-46]).
- *Retrospect:* "Ah, things happened then!" as a "complete, if implicit, condemnation of our civilian world" ([E12a-12]).

**Civilian-facing register.**
- Editors' bowdlerization and the legal reason for it ("to avoid the rigors of the law," [E12a-19]); the "greatest reserve" caution about bawdy songs' implications ([E12a-9], [E12a-13]).

**Cross-cultural notes (limited).**
- Australian and New Zealand variant of *Fred Karno's Army* ("A.N.Z.A.C.") and slang (*Digger*, *Dinkum*, *A.*, [E12a-14], [E12a-68]); French loan-phrases (*Après la guerre*, *comprez*, *san fairy ann*-type) and Hindustani and Arabic army slang absorbed from India and Egypt ([E12a-58], [E12a-68]); an American pre-War ditty sung unknowingly ([E12a-21]).

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Editors' filter, in three layers.** (a) *Selection:* the editors chose "the most pointed" version of each song and excluded regimental ditties and songs that were "merely lewd"; (b) *cleaning:* verses suppressed, words dashed, refrains softened, with "the rigors of the law" named as a reason; (c) *retrospective voice:* 1930, two veterans, one a pacifist, writing for readers. Nothing here measures frequency or how much of the real repertoire is absent. The claim that the songs come mainly from privates is the editors' and is contradicted by one note (an officer is "credibly said" to have written *Send Out the Army*, [E12a-44]).
2. **Internal tensions the editors do not resolve.** (a) Introduction says staff officers drew no satire ([E12a-7]); a leap-frog parody has a staff-officer stanza ([E12a-31]). (b) Introduction says no one sang under bombardment ([E12a-10]) and also that singing *Hush! Here comes a Whizz-Bang* did not alter the shell fire ([E12a-5], [E12a-37]). (c) Introduction calls *When This Blasted War Is Over* a list of "ribald promises"; the printed text is mostly about freedom from routine and rank reversal ([E12a-42]). (d) The editors say *Sammy Hall* was widely reported in 1914-15 but they never heard it ([E12a-18]).
3. **Class and ethnic condescension in the editors' notes.** Remarks about "the slovenly pronunciation of the working classes," "the normal illiterate error," and period terms for the Chinese Labor Corps, Egyptians, Jews and a surname nickname are in the glossary and in notes ([E12a-33], [E12a-68], [E12a-69], [E12a-71]); I record them as the editors' period language and as the reason some items are in the register below.
4. **OCR.** (a) **Pages 87-92 are not in the text file:** the Contents show *One-Eyed Riley* (a note) and *She Was Poor, But She Was Honest* in Group III, but the OCR runs from the sergeant chant (p. 86) straight to the glossary heading; check the page images or Part 2 for them. (b) Titles missing or garbled: *We Are the Boys Who Fear No Noise* (L2246), *Old Soldiers Never Die* (L2437), *Michael Finnigan* (L2877), *Greeting to the Sergeant* (L2956); I supply titles from the Contents and from the introduction. (c) Legible-but-wrong words: "Ferry" for "Jerry" (L4690-4692, 4863, 4917), "Fohnny" for "Johnny" (L2975, 4863), "maze" for "mate" (L3896), "Buppoo" for "Budoo" (L3524), "Fanti" printed "Fantr" (L4540), "Coot" likely "Cooty" (L4016); the number in *Glorious!* ("the ae of us," L2550) is unreadable. (d) The printed date of *Mademoiselle from Armenteers* is "191" followed by a damaged character (L309), so I do not give a year. (e) Lines L2488-2505 (*Far, Far from Ypres*) are badly damaged and I do not quote the last line. (f) Some songs that look "cut off" (*Old Mother Riley*, *Christmas Day in the Workhouse*) are cut off in the print on purpose.
5. **Cross-references to Part 2 and beyond my range.** The Postscript holds Priestley's quotation on the two "anti-heroic" songs ([E12a-44]), *Miles Behind the Lines* ([E12a-36]), a note on *Chum* "to a wounded man" ([E12a-71]), and additional songs and words; the glossary entries for *Jerry*, *Johnny*, *Packet*, *Napoo*, *San Fairy Ann*, *Wind-up*, *Tin Hat*, *Go West* and others sit in Part 2.
6. **Etymology.** Many glossary derivations are folk-etymology or speculation (*Binge*, *Doolally*, *Dingo*, *Cooty*, *Boche*); treat as period belief, not as lexicography.
7. **Not extracted (relevance, not content):** a number of ordinary glossary entries (kit, drill, rank insignia, rhyming slang for body parts, military terms) are in my range but do not bear on the humor questions; they were read and passed over. I did not extract entries for terms for women ("Bint," "Bird," "Click," "Hot Stuff," lines 3286-3297, 3918-3934, 4953-4966), though the editor's remark under *Bird* ("typical of a common male attitude to women") is a period critique of the vocabulary.
8. **Claims to check in a browser:** the book's statements about Étaples (1917 mutiny; the Manchester Guardian, February 1930); the dating and priority claims about *Skibboo* and the *Mademoiselle* family; any statement that a song was "universally sung" (the editors' claim; unverifiable).
9. **Direct bearing on the defector character** is only inferential: this is 1914-18 British infantry life as remembered by veterans; there is nothing about leaving the army on principle, about post-service humor, or about humor without a unit.
