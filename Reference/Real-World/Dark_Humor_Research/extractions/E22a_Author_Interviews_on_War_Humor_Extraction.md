**Lens for the whole unit:** what working writers (veterans among them) say about HOW war humor is built and when it fails. **Everything here is a craft statement by a writer, an editor or a critic.** It is evidence of how writers think about war humor, **not** direct evidence of how soldiers joke, except where a speaker is expressly reporting what they themselves heard or did as a soldier (marked "reporting" in the findings). Where a passage is the interviewer's or a critic's claim rather than the author's, the finding says so.

## Findings

### From WA-009 (Matt Gallagher, CNAS Q&A; veteran author, Army platoon leader, Iraq)

**[E22a-1]. A veteran-author's reason for putting banter in: humor is "one of a soldier's survival tools," and an old one.** (WA-009, answer 1, L9; reporting on his own platoon plus a reading of a WWII novel)
Asked by the interviewer how the back-and-forth smart-aleck dialogue of a combat platoon was captured so faithfully, Gallagher says it was important to include because it was vital to the platoon's experience and to Gallagher's own. He describes humor as "one of a soldier's survival tools" that predates the current wars, and says reading Norman Mailer's WWII novel *The Naked and the Dead* the summer before college was a shock, because it showed the Greatest Generation joking crudely, which on reflection was no surprise. His account of the function: it reminded them they were alive and that "their present wasn't their eternity." Gallagher says that their own generation did the same, in the irony and sarcasm of its era. The page gives only this one function and one comparison; no example, no count, no time-course. The framing premise is the interviewer's, not the author's: that earlier junior-officer memoirs were earnest and short on "the humor, the banter, and the absurdities" (L7).
*Candidate application:* Soldier humor can be written as a way of proving to oneself that the present is not permanent, and as era-specific in idiom (irony and sarcasm for a recent generation) rather than timeless gags. A character's register would date the character to when service happened.

**[E22a-2]. What fills the boredom, and the practice of writing real quips down.** (WA-009, answer 1, L11; reporting)
Gallagher paraphrases the adage that war is long boredom interrupted by brief terror and asks what fills the boredom. The answer given: not only guard duty, moving sandbags and cleaning weapons but a very large quantity of crude sexual joking. Gallagher says a point was made of "scrawling down" the funniest quips and events of the deployment, both for the blog and for posterity. **A caution on the brief's gloss:** the page does not say the quips were saved *because* soldiers detect fake dialogue. The "fraud" remark is in a different paragraph and is about leadership (E22a-3). Nor does the page say how much of any saved quip survived editing; in answer 3 Gallagher says only that the book allowed writing "more honestly and candidly" than the blog did and that editing improved the writing (L33). The blog's origin, per Gallagher: communication with family and friends, then "personal catharsis" (L31), with strict observance of operational security credited for not getting in trouble.
*Candidate application:* Realistic soldier talk is largely filler humor that accumulates in dead time, much of it sexual, and a writer who served can source it from notes taken in the moment rather than invention. The practice of keeping a private record of the funny moments is itself a soldier behavior worth giving to a character.

**[E22a-3]. Authenticity is judged by the troops: the leader (and by extension the joker) who is "a fraud" is detected.** (WA-009, answer 1, L13; veteran reporting on leadership, applied to humor by inference only)
Gallagher says the most important thing a platoon leader can do is be authentic with the troops because soldiers "can sniff out frauds and phonies like bloodhounds." Gallagher played to personal strengths, turned to NCOs for the weaknesses, and calls this a simple recipe, contrasting that style with the "gladiator" officers of two other well-known memoirs (the interviewer's earlier remark, L7, called those memoirs earnest and almost "too good to be true"). Gallagher then points, jokingly, to what each author's website shows: a video of a rival memoirist boxing a giant robot-major, versus a video on Gallagher's own site of a soldier teaching the author a pop-music dance. The page presents the dance video as a joke about self-presentation, **not** as a documented platoon custom. The page never applies the fraud test to joking or dialogue; that link is inference.
*Candidate application:* A leader's tolerated self-ridicule (letting a subordinate teach the leader something silly) can read as authenticity; an officer whose humor is performed will be caught. This is candidate material on rank negotiation, built from a single veteran's self-portrait.

**[E22a-4]. Detachment in the doing, reflection in the downtime, and "the dangers of thinking too much."** (WA-009, answer 2, L23; reporting)
Not about humor directly but about the conditions it works in. Gallagher says that while conducting operations you are forced into a "hyper-realistic mental state," detaching from the severity of what you are doing, and that in the moment you rely on the momentum of action; Gallagher still catches the memory of Iraq and has to remind the self that it "actually happened." There is also much time for reflection, on long missions and back at the outpost, and "anyone who's been deployed can attest to the dangers of thinking too much over there." Gallagher says these two modes explain the two styles of the book (hyper-realistic narrative and dialogue; stream of consciousness).
*Candidate application:* A veteran may remember the worst moments with a delayed sense that it really happened, and the quiet hours (not the firefights) are where thinking, and perhaps humor, does its work. Useful for the defector's relationship to the defector's own memory.

**[E22a-5]. Leaving: "I miss the people in the Army every day. I don't really miss the Army." Vernacular and reflexes that remain.** (WA-009, answers 4-5, L39-45; reporting plus the interviewer's anecdote)
Gallagher says that most veterans never accept civilian life's "banality" (a generalization, unsupported by data), and separates missing the people from missing the institution. The habit of swearing in any context survived, called a "universal gift from the military to its former soldiers and retirees"; the family has accepted it; strangers and "the meek" are shaken by it. Gallagher's partner (called "Lady Kaboom" in the interview, known to blog readers by another nickname) thinks Gallagher gives orders to civilians too much, especially slow-moving ones in the way. Gallagher adds, jokingly, a puzzled aside about civilians' body size ("Don't they have tape tests?", the Army's body-composition measure), and names the standard "combat veteran dislikes" (honking cabs, slamming dumpster lids). The **interviewer's** anecdote (L43, not Gallagher's): a fellow Iraq veteran at church, mid-conversation about a former battalion commander, swore about the commander and then asked whether that was allowed in church; the interviewer says "I was rolling with laughter" and concludes that "the old U.S. Army vernacular slips out" wherever you are.
*Candidate application:* The post-service residue is mostly small and audible: language, the habit of directing people, institutional yardsticks applied to civilians, and noise aversion, all delivered by the veteran with a smile. The distinction between the people and the institution is a ready position for a defector who left on principle.

**[E22a-6]. A banter-register interview that ends in elegy.** (WA-009, answer 6 and closing, L47-63; interview form, not soldier speech)
The whole interview runs on veteran-to-veteran ribbing (foosball, drinking, rival memoirists, pet-names). The last answer, a list of bars for a stout, turns without comment at one entry into a friend killed in Mosul in January 2007: the bar is where the friends tried to make sense of the wars, their futures and military culture, and where Gallagher last spent time with that friend; Gallagher associates the place with a particular folk-group recording of a song, and with the friend. The interviewer then closes by promising a toast. The page does not describe any rule about when the register may shift; it shows the shift happening, carried by a place, a drink and a song, in a few lines, with no signal that a joke is over.
*Candidate application:* In veteran-to-veteran talk the mourning is not marked off from the joking: a place and a drink can carry it, and the register change can be quiet. The memorial ritual here is not a joke at all, which is itself worth noting as the counterweight.

### From WA-010 (Garry Trudeau on *The Sandbox*; outside editor, NPR 2007)

**[E22a-7]. An outside editor's claim of function: "the thin membrane between these guys and insanity," and the editorial brief that kept the political rant out.** (WA-010, L17-27; editor's claim, not a soldier's)
Trudeau, who has never been to Iraq or Afghanistan (per NPR), says "that black, M*A*S*H-like humor has traditionally been the thin membrane between these guys and insanity," and "they have to look at horrible things and somehow detoxify them, and often they'll do that with humor." He said at the Pentagon signing that his aim was to give a general audience the "flavor" of life for troops, and that he asked contributors for writing that "spoke to the texture of quotidian, day-to-day activities" and **not** rants about the war or its politics. NPR (not Trudeau) adds that dark humor characterizes many of the posts. Trudeau's claim is a civilian-facing translation of soldier humor (that it is how they stay sane); the page offers no soldier's own statement and no example to test it. The editorial instruction is the more concrete datum: when a collection is asked for day-to-day texture rather than politics, humor is what is left standing.
*Candidate application:* The civilian-facing explanation of soldier humor is typically that it keeps them sane (a membrane, a detox). A defector or veteran character may find that explanation both true and a little too tidy, and know the audience wants it. Compare Gallagher's different, more local account (E22a-1).

### From WA-013 (NPR *Fresh Air* review; critic John Powers on Phil Klay's *Redeployment*)

**[E22a-8]. A joke whose punchline is the civilian-veteran gap.** (WA-013, L39; critic quoting a joke told inside the book)
The critic opens with an "old joke" which "gets told in" the collection: how many Vietnam veterans does it take to change a light bulb, answer: "you wouldn't know, you weren't there." The critic says the book itself "addresses the gap" between soldiers who fought in Iraq and "those of us back home." The mechanism is that the punchline refuses to answer the question; it is a boundary-marking joke that is about the outsider's exclusion. The page does not say in which story it is told, by whom, or how it is received there, and the critic is not a veteran, so whether soldiers tell it is not established here.
*Candidate application:* A short, rehearsed boundary joke (the answer is that you cannot know) is a ready tool for deflecting civilian questions with a laugh while enforcing the gap. Good for a veteran who wants to end a topic politely.

**[E22a-9]. The critic's theory of why the funny and the harrowing sit together in recent war fiction.** (WA-013, L43-45; critic's claim)
Powers says what makes Klay good is carrying the reader "from the battlefield to the strip bar" and "from the funny, to the harrowing, to the heartbreak," and offers a cause that is the critic's own, not Klay's: the recent wars (Vietnam, Iraq, Afghanistan) are not "conceptually clean" like WWII, because fighters move among civilians they are supposed to protect, who may try to kill them and whom they often kill back, "sometimes accidentally"; such war is "more surreal than clean," and further disorienting when "stoked by video games, knocked out by Ambien, and speaking in the obfuscating patois of military acronyms." He adds that soldiers are doing a dangerous mission they suspect their country does not really believe in and would rather not think about. These are the critic's generalizations about wars and a book; no soldier is quoted for them.
*Candidate application:* A veteran of a murky, civilian-embedded war may find absurdity in the mission's shape, not only its dangers, and acronyms as an opaque layer that both hides and carries meaning (compare E22a-13).

**[E22a-10]. A "blackly hilarious" story whose engine is aid money, corruption and a rich man's baseball project.** (WA-013, L41; critic's description)
The critic names "Money Is A Weapon System" as "blackly hilarious": a provincial reconstruction official tries to rebuild the area near Tikrit, only to see a fortune in American aid lost to local corruption and an American millionaire's "demented mission" of teaching Iraqis baseball. The humor, as described, is institutional and bureaucratic absurdity at a distance from combat. The page gives no excerpt, and does not say whether the humor is in the characters' speech or the narration. Klay himself, in WA-057, ties this story to bureaucratic confusion (E22a-13).
*Candidate application:* The target of soldier-adjacent humor can be the machinery of the mission (money, contractors, civilian experts) rather than the enemy or the dead.

**[E22a-11]. The returning-veteran gap, as the critic summarizes it.** (WA-013, L47-49; critic's summary of the book's characters)
Powers says some of the keenest moments show returning soldiers frustrated at trying to communicate their experience to people who greet them with cliches, "however well-meaning," and that because of the all-volunteer force, soldiers and veterans are "weirdly invisible." The critic quotes one fictional veteran: a perversity in the speaker makes the speaker want to bash the war with conservatives and defend it with liberals; and another: you risked your life for something bigger than yourself, "how many people can say that?" The critic says Klay's veterans "usually wind up feeling diminished, even soiled, by what they had to do in Iraq, but also superior to the America they were doing it for." These are statements by fictional characters as relayed by a critic, and are not documented veteran testimony.
*Candidate application:* A reflexive contrarian stance toward whichever civilian faction he is talking to is a plausible, specific posture for a veteran who feels misunderstood by all sides.

### From WA-057 (Phil Klay, *Image Journal*; veteran author, Marine, Iraq 2007-08, not in combat per the interviewer)

**[E22a-12]. "Both humorous and serious at once": the author's one stated principle on war comedy.** (WA-057, answer at L87; Klay speaking as writer, with a brief report on Marines)
Asked by the interviewer how he would describe the humor of Marines, and whether service gave him moments both humorous and serious (the interviewer also compared him to the poet Kenneth Koch, a claim that is the interviewer's), Klay says: "The comedy I like the best is always both humorous and serious at once." He cites Vonnegut's line that jokes are a viable form of literature and that critics fault him for making points with jokes rather than "oceanic tragedy." Klay then says there is an expectation that a war story will tell us somberly that war is hell, but "often in the worst of situations Marines are making the funniest (and darkest) of jokes." This last sentence is the closest to reporting in the page, and it comes with no example. He adds that bureaucratic culture has plenty of absurdity and the military more than its share, so "some degree of absurdist humor is inevitable." His final claim, a craft claim: "A really telling joke allows us to face things we'd rather avert our eyes from, and it makes us glad to do so."
*Candidate application:* The funniest material is the material that is also serious; a soldier character's jokes can be doing the work of looking at the thing, not of avoiding it. The relief Klay names ("makes us glad to do so") is part of the mechanism: the audience is relieved to have been allowed to look.

**[E22a-13]. Bureaucratic language as a vehicle: acronyms that obscure and also carry feeling.** (WA-057, answers at L25-29; Klay on his own stories)
Klay says two stories are tied by bureaucratic confusion: "Money as a Weapons System" (the widest look at policy) and "OIF" (the narrowest). In "OIF" every sentence has an acronym, by design; the acronyms come from different unit cultures (National Guard, Marine and others) so that even most Marines would not know them all, and they describe things outside the narrator at first, then midway the usage shifts. His claim: "Acronyms can be used to obscure, but they can also be emotive," and for what the narrator needs to say at the end, acronyms are "the only way to say what he needs to say." The other acronym story, "FRAGO," has an unapologetically Marine narrator who unloads acronyms and does not explain, expecting the listener to go along. He wants the reader to be startled by a different language and culture on turning the page. Note this is a statement about the **language of the institution**, not specifically about jokes, but it is the source's clearest craft statement on institutional language carrying horror or feeling.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may speak in institutional shorthand precisely when he is saying the thing he cannot say plainly; the jargon is not a barrier but the form of the disclosure. Listeners who do not know the code would hear only noise.

**[E22a-14]. A war story told in bars: the telling has a history, and without it "you've just got a simple story with a gross punchline."** (WA-057, answer at L17; Klay on his own story "Bodies")
On pacing, Klay says the opening of "Bodies" is, at heart, a relatively simple and somewhat grotesque story about two Marines collecting a corpse. The narrator heard it before he deployed and then told it in bars when people asked him about Iraq. The story opens with the narrator explaining how he used to tell it and "the ways in which he'd use it," because the meaning lies in the history of the telling and in the change in his relationship to the story. His craft claim: if you do not move through time, following the thoughts and emotional resonances for the character, "then you've just got a simple story with a gross punchline." He adds that the repeated story is understood in relation to every time it has been retold. The page does not describe the corpse-handling detail and I do not (register).
*Candidate application:* A rehearsed, funny war story for civilians has a shelf life and a function (it fills a silence, satisfies curiosity), and the teller's relationship to it changes. A veteran who still has the bar version ready is a character with something underneath it.

**[E22a-15]. Honesty is not the standard; the aim is something that "challenges the culture to see something new," and Heller is the example.** (WA-057, answer at L103; Klay's claim, with a Heller quotation)
Asked how to get closest to an honest representation of those who serve, Klay says he does not know that honesty is necessarily the standard; what we want is something that, in an honest way, "challenges the culture to see something new." He quotes Heller's claim that the antiwar and antigovernment feelings in *Catch-22* belong to the period after WWII (Korea, the Cold War, the fifties), and says that if we believe him, Heller did not strictly provide an "honest" account of WWII "and yet, he did provide us something invaluable." This is a craft position on the relation of comic war fiction to documentary truth, important because it licenses invention. Check the Heller quote against WA-061.
*Candidate application:* For design purposes, the relevant standard for a comic or satirical soldier culture is whether it makes the player see something new, not whether it matches any one army's record. Cross-use: a fictional faction's humor may express the author's present, not the faction's past.

**[E22a-16]. Telling civilians less: the writer's own policy of deflating civilian leaps.** (WA-057, answer at L99; Klay reporting)

### From WA-059 (David Abrams, *The Rumpus*; veteran author, 20 years in the Army, public affairs, Baghdad 2005, *Fobbit*)

**[E22a-23]. The headquarters-world war: expectation versus the cubicle, and the rear-area self as the comic subject.** (WA-059, bio L11-13; answers L19-31; Abrams reporting on his own tour)
Abrams was a public-affairs soldier with an infantry division headquarters in Baghdad in 2005, after seventeen years on active duty without a combat deployment. He expected tents and sand and found an air-conditioned building of cubicles and an office job "not unlike all the others," with gunfire and mortars audible at a distance. He says it took about a day and a half to settle into routine, that inside the wire "there's a tendency for the war to slip away into the background," that he rarely dealt with soldiers outside the headquarters except to interview them, and that his one regret is not going outside the wire. The interview does not say whether Abrams was himself called a fobbit by others or used the term of himself in his unit (in the answer he calls himself "just a Fobbit surrounded by other Fobbits").
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character with a staff, logistics or messaging background has a different humor from a combat-arms one: its targets are PowerPoint, the base's comforts, the distance from the fight and the guilt about it. If the defector served on the "safe" side, his jokes may be rueful about that.

**[E22a-24]. Sanitized language as both the daily job and the comic target ("Forbidden Words").** (WA-059, interviewer's question L45, Abrams's answer L47; interviewer's description plus Abrams reporting)
The interviewer (the page gives no name) says the novel contains a list of "Forbidden Words," usually funny, showing the distance between the public story and the real experience; the interviewer names three entries ("Iraqidocious," "pretzel logic," and a crude pseudo-measurement idiom that I paraphrase), and says the novel refuses to sanitize language, sex, video games and comfort. Abrams agrees it is an "unsanitary account of the 'business of war'" and says the careful choice of words, the scrubbing of language and the calculated images shown to external audiences were major parts of his daily life, and so seeped into what and how he showed in the novel. The page does not reproduce the list. The key datum: his job was to manage how the war sounded to civilians, which made the unmanaged speech a ready subject for comedy.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who worked in messaging or public affairs may joke most bitterly about vocabulary: words that must not be said, euphemisms that must be said. For a defector who left on principle, the euphemism machine is a plausible target of his dry humor.

**[E22a-25]. Processing comes later; the war in the middle was "surreal, often disconnected."** (WA-059, answer L35; Abrams reporting, with a generalization)
Abrams describes modern war as without a clear "front" and "rear," a 360-degree theater, and saturated by media: bloggers (he names Matt Gallagher, whose book is in WA-009) typing after a patrol, soldiers chatting online with spouses, and himself with a news channel on a TV beside his computer describing events as the headquarters' own activity reports arrived. He calls it "very surreal, often disconnected" and says it was hard to "process" the war while in the middle of it, and adds: "We only fully process what happened after we return home and see the war from the other end of the telescope." He adds "I think that's true for most soldiers," a generalization from his own case with no support on the page. The same answer is not about humor but about **time course**: understanding, and presumably the funny retelling, follows the experience.
*Candidate application:* A character who has been out ten to fifteen years may only recently have begun to "process" what he laughed through at the time; his humor may therefore have moved from in-the-moment to retrospective.

**[E22a-26]. Not a corrective: comedy as the "backstage of combat," and exaggeration as a tool.** (WA-059, answers L43 and L51; Abrams on craft)
Abrams says he did not write a corrective novel because that "would just end up tasting like medicine," and he tried to stay away from polemics; the book instead shows the "backstage of combat," a different picture from the word-association image of soldiers in a hail of bullets. Midway through his tour his literary agent wrote to him (quoted) that "only in fiction will this insane war finally reach an American reading public"; Abrams says this freed him to make the war as "loud and distorted" as he wanted and to paint it in images "as large as a billboard," because "sometimes you have to do this to get your point across." He says he already held a skeptical, cynical viewpoint from the start.
*Candidate application:* Exaggeration is a deliberate instrument of the comic soldier-writer, not a failure of realism; a character who tells his war as farce may be doing so because the sober version never reached anybody.

**[E22a-27]. Comedy is "possible—perhaps even necessary—to find" in any war, with a stated hedge and a model in religious satire.** (WA-059, answer L55; Abrams's claim)
Asked whether his sensibility or the later wars better suit the comic mode, Abrams says it is "possible—perhaps even necessary—to find comedy in any war", and that "seeing any war through the distortion of comedy is healthy" because there is "too much absurdity and irony at play in a combat zone not to pay attention to it." He adds, "others may have had an entirely different war experience." He names Heller, Richard Hooker (*M*A*S*H*) and Hašek (whom he says he has not read, only heard was funny) as models, and Flannery O'Connor as his main literary model, who "showed us how you can take something as sober and earnest as religion and punch holes in it until all the funny stuff leaks out"; he came at war "sideways and wearing a jester's hat." He expects critics for the light in which he casts the military. Note the interviewer's framing here (that the book belongs in the *Catch-22* tradition rather than the recent serious tradition) is the interviewer's.
*Candidate application:* Humor can be the character's chosen lens ("the distortion of comedy is healthy"), including a reason to keep looking at a thing that is absurd and not only terrible.

**[E22a-28]. An ending the interviewer reads as comic and morally weighty; Abrams's reply is about not meaning a message.** (WA-059, interviewer's quotation and question L57-69, answer L71; interviewer's claim and Abrams's response)
The interviewer quotes the book's final passage: the main character runs without helmet, vest or weapon toward the base's main gate while sentries raise rifles and shout, and a mortar approaches. The interviewer claims "Even this is comic in its way. Comedy usually springs from the darkest places," and reads the scene as raising the shelter of headquarters, disproportion between forces, robotic and joystick warfare, bureaucrats making slide presentations, and war as something like reality television, "nothing like Vietnam, where everyone at home was horrified." This is the interviewer's reading. Abrams answers that it would be disingenuous (his spelling in the text copy is "disingenous") to say he was not writing a moral novel, since any Iraq War novel enters the political conversation, but that he did not write with a particular moral intent; he ran his experience through the "sausage factory of fiction," hopes readers line up at opposite poles (some reading it as anti-military, some with empathy), and that a reader left in "bland in-between territory" means he has not done his job. He agrees the war reached the public as "just another TV show," and calls that disconnect dangerous.
*Candidate application:* A tragicomic scene works by letting the reader laugh and then feel the weight; the author does not pick the reading. For a character's own war story, the audience may split in the same way, with some hearing a joke and others hearing an accusation.

**[E22a-29]. Writing the comedy did not resolve the conflict.** (WA-059, answer L75; Abrams reporting)
Asked whether writing the book brought clarity, Abrams says he is "still just as conflicted" as before: he was a soldier doing his duty, with persistent unease ("What are we doing here?" "Are we really fixing this country or are we doing more harm than good?" and how to "pull ourselves out of this quicksand"). He says he is still in the "white space" the interviewer named after the last sentence. This is a data point, from one writer, that years of comic distance and a finished book are not the same as resolution.
*Candidate application:* A veteran whose humor is fluent and well-honed may still be exactly as unsettled as the day he left; the polish of the joke is no evidence about the state of the wound.

### From WA-060 (Abrams in conversation with Viet Thanh Nguyen, Literary Hub; two novelists, each interviewing the other)

### From WA-061 (Joseph Heller interviewed by Ramona Koval, New York, October 1998, broadcast 1999; WWII-era veteran-novelist at age 75)

**[E22a-34]. The absurdity belongs to the postwar period, not the war: Heller's own statement about where *Catch-22* came from.** (WA-061, answer L25; Heller's claim about his own book)
Asked whether he enjoys the phrase "Catch-22" entering the language (the page's own introduction, L9, explains the catch: a pilot can escape dangerous missions only by pleading insanity, but wanting out proves sanity), Heller says the novel "is really a post-war novel" and that "most of the attitudes and confusions that appear in it occurred to me as a result of conditions after the war, rather than my own experience in the war." He takes the phrase's growing use as evidence of a "timelessness" to the situation. This agrees with the Heller claim Klay cites in WA-057 (E22a-15), though the exact Klay-quoted words do not appear on this page. The page does not say which postwar conditions he means; the interviewer introduces Heller as "a master of the absurd," which is the interviewer's framing.
*Candidate application:* A comic soldier's absurdist sensibility may come as much from the peacetime institution and its aftermath as from combat. The defector who left ten to fifteen years ago may find the funniest material in the institution's logic, which he could see better from outside.

**[E22a-35]. The joking temperament predated the war; the political and critical outlook came after it.** (WA-061, answers L29-31; Heller reporting on himself)
The interviewer asks whether he found life absurd even growing up on Coney Island. Heller says no ("It would be very instructive and impressive to say yes"). He says he always had "the sense of humor," wisecracking and practical jokes, "a kind of perverse way with the wisecracks and jokes," and that personality "doesn't change much with the years." In the army he did not think about politics and did not even recall "hating the Germans even when they were shooting at me"; critical thinking began only after the army, at about 22, in college. Early short stories already fused the realistic and the fantastic. Source statement is one man's self-report 50 years after; the page does not describe any wartime joking by others.
*Candidate application:* Wit may be a pre-existing trait that service finds a use for, not something service produces. A character could joke the same way as a civilian, and what changes in service is the material and the stakes.

**[E22a-36]. Dark outlook as maturity: "realism is realism," and the virtuous usually do not triumph.** (WA-061, interviewer's question L33, answers L35-41; Heller's claim and interviewer's framing)
The interviewer quotes Heller's memoir about his youthful stories having been resolved by "ironic divine intervention on the side of the virtuous" and asks what changed to produce a book in which exploiters triumph. (The premise that a darkness suddenly came upon him is the interviewer's, and Heller rejects it: "I don't think so.") His answer: attitudes evolved; "realism is realism, and what happens does happen," and in life the virtuous usually do not triumph and the triumphant "usually lack virtue"; the change is "the difference between being very young and having a belief in the miraculous, and being a little mature and educated and knowing there is no such thing as the miraculous." He adds that with *Catch-22* he moved from a "fictitious cloud" of extravagant, miraculous or happy endings toward reality, relying more on style than on unusual content; and that his sympathies remain with the oppressed and exploited and his antipathies with the selfish and egotistical, which have not changed.
*Candidate application:* For a comic soldier-character, the dark humor may mark the loss of a belief in rescue or deserved outcomes rather than a love of the dark. Stable underlying sympathies (for the exploited) can sit under a hard comic surface.

**[E22a-37]. How a joke is built: "you have to lead up to it," and a run of wisecracks fails.** (WA-061, answer L171-173; Heller's craft claim)
Asked whether he laughs while writing, Heller says not much: he smiles when an idea occurs, laughs sometimes on rereading after weeks, and says writing is hard work whereas "talking is a cinch." His key sentence: "you can't be funny just by saying a joke, you have to lead up to it." Illustrating this, he says that when a film of *Catch-22* was being planned with Mike Nichols he sensed trouble, because a chapter the filmmakers liked was funny only because of what builds up to its final lines, and film has no room for extended dialogue; that chapter was among the last cut. He adds that "wisecrack is one thing, but you get three wisecracks in a row they're not going to be funny to a reader, there has to be a sense of the narrative progressing." (Text copy has a few garbled phrases in this paragraph; I have used only what is clear.)
*Candidate application:* Soldier humor realized in a game may need setup and a sense of progress to land; a stream of one-liners in a row does not read as funny. A character who tells a long, patient story with a late payoff is a different comic type than the quipper, and the build-up version is harder to deliver in a short dialogue exchange.

**[E22a-38]. Method: multiply the real, and take the target's own words.** (WA-061, answers L57, L63-65; Heller on *Closing Time*, a postwar satire, not on war)
Heller describes two techniques from his later novel: he took the menus and flower orders of a real museum society wedding and multiplied the quantities (a hundred dozen roses became a million), calling it "a Helleresque view of a reality, exaggerated to emphasize a number of things"; and he gave a fictional president a demeaning nickname and says many of that character's dialogues were taken from interviews given by a real vice president (I paraphrase the nickname). The page also shows he built the book by placing real and literary figures together in one underground space (including Hašek's Švejk, Vonnegut as himself, and Heller himself as a child under his own name). This is a satire on wealth, poverty and politics, **not** on war, and is included for the technique.
*Candidate application:* Satire of an institution can be built by taking its documents and its officials' own statements and scaling them; the humor is in the unaltered original plus an exponent. A veteran who mocks his old service may quote its own regulations and slogans back.

**[E22a-39]. The honesty contract with the reader: this didn't happen the way it is told.** (WA-061, answer L137; Heller's claim)
Heller says that in his novels, "even in Catch 22," he tries to make the reader understand that "this didn't happen the way it's being presented," unlike the old-fashioned novel presented as though it really happened; he refers to the novel as a novel in *Good as Gold* and introduces himself by name in *Closing Time*. The aim, in his words, is to "enter into an agreement with the reader" that the book is not to be taken all that seriously as an accurate representation. This is the craft counterpart to Klay's view that strict honesty is not the standard (E22a-15).
*Candidate application:* A comic war story told by a veteran can carry its own warning label, a wink that it is arranged, and this is what lets the audience take it as truth about the experience rather than a record.

**[E22a-40]. Constructed mentally, not emotionally; flat characters.** (WA-061, answers L123-129; Heller on his style)
Heller says he does not like realistic books: even when dealing with real situations his novels are "not realistic in that they construct, they're mainly mentally rather than emotionally." He says that in *Catch-22* you know nothing about Yossarian and there are almost no three-dimensional characters, and that the books "tend to violate basic laws of fiction writing"; he notes that he, among others, has moved away from the classical novel's detail of speech, dress and occupation, saying television and photography make that detail less necessary. A later novel (*Something Happened*) invests more in a single character.
*Candidate application:* Absurdist comedy about institutions can work with typed, thin characters (a function, a rank, a catchphrase), where the comedy is in the system. The fully drawn comic soldier is a different design choice.

**[E22a-41]. Joking in therapy: jokes displaced the free association.** (WA-061, answers L109-123; Heller reporting on himself, not on war)
The interviewer says Heller's memoir reports he told jokes through his analysis sessions. Heller says he dreamed jokes, and that the sessions aimed at complete free association four times a week, which he could "seldom achieve": he "just like[s] to talk and make jokes." He says he went into analysis to stay composed during a marriage breakup, that it "kept me busy," gave him a place to go and a stock of material for arguments with his then-wife, and that he is not sure it benefited him as much as the analyst expected (the interviewer remarks that his memoir says only people with no apparent need for analysis can benefit from it; Heller says most analysts would agree). He says the war had no effect on his writing, nor did a later serious illness. Treat as a single man's anecdote of joking as a style of resistance to talking directly.
*Candidate application:* A veteran in therapy or counseling may deflect with jokes the way Heller reports; the clinician may find the humor charming and unproductive, the same thing.

**[E22a-42]. A humor about an injury that is available only once the injury has been survived.** (WA-061, answer L163; Heller, on bad reviews)
Heller says he can remember good reviews of *Catch-22* but chose to put the vitriolic ones in his memoir and quotes them to audiences, and that "I can be good humored about it now because Catch 22 has endured, but I don't think I would have been that good a sport if the book had just dropped away." The reviews were very upsetting when they came out because they afflicted the ego and hurt sales. This is not about war; it is a clear first-person example of the benign-violation timing idea (E04-1): a hurt becomes laughable after the outcome is secure.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's ability to joke about a wound may depend on whether the outcome vindicated him; someone who has not had that vindication (a defector who left on principle and has no proof he was right) may not yet have the joke.

**[E22a-43]. Skepticism as the only wisdom, and the cost of having no conviction.** (WA-061, answers L153-159, L177-181; Heller on himself)
Heller says the only wisdom he has acquired is to be skeptical of other people's ideology and arguments and that he dislikes dogmatic, intolerant approaches; he describes himself as "very soft-hearted and sentimental" in his books, ruthless toward those who bother good-natured characters, "distressed by suffering, not to the point where I'm going to go into sulk about it." He also says he can't hold unquestioning beliefs and that he would be better off if he could (Yossarian's line in *Closing Time* that even a naive conviction is "more nourishing" than "the wasteland of none"), noting devout people seem content as they age. Much of this interview, not extracted here, covers religion, politics, determinism, free will, psychoanalysis and publishing; I skipped it where it is not about humor.
*Candidate application:* The comic skeptic pays for his stance with a lack of consolation, and he knows it. A defector who left an institution on principle and distrusts every ideology, including the one that sent him, may envy people who still believe.

### From WA-062 (Suzanne McConnell, essay on Vonnegut's "black humor," *The London Magazine*; excerpt from *Pity the Reader*, 2019; McConnell was Vonnegut's student and friend, so most claims are hers or reported by her)

**[E22a-44]. Vonnegut's theory: black humor's source is helplessness; laughing or crying is "what a human being does when there's nothing else he can do."** (WA-062, L11-15, L59-61; McConnell's frame, Vonnegut's quoted theory)
McConnell opens with a line from his novel *Bluebeard* (a husband making his wife so unhappy she developed a sense of humor) and says black humor's source is "helplessness and despair." The theory is Vonnegut's own words, quoted: "Laughter or crying is what a human being does when there's nothing else he can do." Elsewhere (a long quoted passage) he says laughter is a response to frustration just as tears are, and solves nothing; he borrows Freud's image of a dog that cannot get through a gate to bite or fight and digs dirt instead: a displacement activity. His "peak funniness" came at a Notre Dame literary festival, in a huge auditorium where the audience was so tightly tuned that even a cough got laughter, two days after Martin Luther King's assassination: an "enormous need to either laugh or cry as the only possible adjustment," because there was nothing to be done. His rule: "the biggest laughs are based on the biggest disappointments and the biggest fears." He also tells a cartoon of two men chained high on a cell wall, one saying "Now here's my plan," as an image of a situation one cannot get out of, and says American culture expects that a problem can always be solved, which is "so untrue that it makes me want to cry—or laugh."
*Candidate application:* The mechanism to write: a shared, unsolvable disappointment makes an audience over-ready to laugh, and the laughter is a release, not a solution. A veteran's humor may spike when the room is collectively helpless. The "plan" image fits a character who jokes about the absurdity of any plan.

**[E22a-45]. Naming: "black humor" versus "gallows humor," and two execution jokes.** (WA-062, L53-59; McConnell with Vonnegut quoted)
McConnell says Bruce Jay Friedman coined "black humor" for a 1965 anthology; Vonnegut at first objected to the grouping because the writers differed, then had "quite a lot to say about it." Vonnegut ties black humor to the Central European "gallows humor" that Freud discusses (Freud's example: a condemned man asked by the hangman if he has anything to say replies, "Not at this time"). Vonnegut also reports a line the writer Nelson Algren told him from a U.S. county jail: a man strapped into the electric chair says to the witnesses, "This will certainly teach me a lesson." Both are deadpan understatement by the condemned about the proceeding. Vonnegut's remark that the country's "one tremendous contribution to 'gallows humor'" occurred there is his quip as McConnell reports it. The page gives no information on whether the incident happened.
*Candidate application:* The classic form is the condemned person's dry understatement about his own situation, which turns the proceeding's formality against itself. For a character facing consequences, an understated retort is the natural register.

**[E22a-46]. A literary gallows line that migrated into real workplace use among aid workers.** (WA-062, L17-49; McConnell relaying a fan's e-mail)
McConnell says the quintessential Vonnegut "mousetrap" is a scene in *Cat's Cradle*: a doctor, overwhelmed and exhausted by an epidemic that killed over a thousand people in ten days at his hospital, starts giggling at the dead stacked outside and tells his son, "someday this will all be yours" (I summarize, and do not reproduce the description of the bodies; register). She then reports that a Vonnegut fan, an aid worker, wrote from a disputed border region, where he leads shelter planning for displaced people, that he quoted the line while orienting a new aid worker ("Someday this could all be yours"). This is a single anecdote about a humanitarian, not a military, workplace; the page does not report the new worker's reaction, nor whether the line is common in the field.
*Candidate application:* A workplace in which people handle catastrophe may adopt a literary or pop-culture punch line as its orientation joke, handed to new arrivals as the first lesson; the "someday this will all be yours" form (an inheritance of the mess) suits a unit that knows it is passing a burden down.

**[E22a-47]. Death as "the world's biggest joke," the refrain "up in Heaven now," and the jokes he tested in class, never at students' expense.** (WA-062, L65-107; McConnell and former students reporting)
A former student, Jim Siegelman, says Vonnegut taught that death was "the world's biggest joke, the ultimate punch line." Siegelman wrote a parody of the film/novel *Love Story* ending in a bleak punch line about a character's parents ("It's nobody's fault really... They're both dead"), and reports never having seen anybody laugh as hard as Vonnegut did at it. At the Iowa workshop Vonnegut tried out jokes on students, among them a religious-satire joke (the crucifixion story as a lesson that killing someone is fine if he is not well-connected, with a "revised" story in which God adopts a nobody before he dies), laughing until he wheezed; the class's response told him the joke and idea were good, and it appears in *Slaughterhouse-Five*. McConnell's observation: "One thing Kurt never tried on us was his own wit at our expense." She adds that he was direct and outspoken when angry. She also notes his favorite joke, which he asked graduates to say of him after his death ("Kurt's up in Heaven now"), and that in *Timequake* he repeats "is up in Heaven now" after the names of dead friends, including a war buddy, as a stock formula from a non-believer; she uses the same refrain for the dead in her own essay. Content note: the religion joke and the death jokes are described in summary; see register.
*Candidate application:* Two ritual devices: the formulaic mock-eulogy ("[Name] is up in Heaven now") said deadpan by someone who does not believe it, and the practice of testing a joke on a trusted audience and reading the reaction. And a license rule: a person with power over the room can joke freely about ideas and death but, by choice, never at the subordinates' expense.

**[E22a-48]. A self-directed joke about a self-destructive habit, turned outward at the cause.** (WA-062, L111-127; McConnell reporting Vonnegut's own text)
McConnell says smoking plagued Vonnegut all his life, that he regarded it as a form of suicide, and that late in life he made the self-accusation into a joke: a mock announcement that he will sue a tobacco company for a billion dollars because its package "promised to kill me" for decades, ending, "But I am now eighty-two. Thanks a lot, you dirty rats." The mechanism is a reversal: the habit's warning label becomes a broken promise, and survival becomes the grievance. McConnell adds that he did not "get off scot-free": emphysema, guilt, and a hospitalization after a fire caused by his cigarettes. The joke coexisted with the harm.
*Candidate application:* A veteran with a chronic injury or a vice may have a polished public joke about it that coexists with real cost; the joke is not a sign of the cost's absence.

**[E22a-49]. Native talent plus cultivation, and McConnell's claim that catastrophe or war "might help."** (WA-062, L131-137; McConnell's claims, with a report from Vonnegut's daughter)
McConnell says the knack for the blackly comic is "partly a native talent" (Vonnegut's daughter Edie reportedly said he showed that sensibility as a child) and can be cultivated by taking "a look around through that lens"; "You don't have to endure catastrophe or a war. But it might help." These are McConnell's claims; none is supported by data on the page. She then comments on a contemporary U.S. administration and its comedic effect, which is outside this lens and skipped.
*Candidate application:* The comic voice may be both temperament and trained habit; service can sharpen what was already there.

**[E22a-50]. Joking as the response to misery one cannot fix, and the friend who wept instead.** (WA-062, L139-171; Vonnegut quoted, McConnell reporting; restrained)
McConnell recounts that in 1970 Vonnegut and fellow WWII veteran-writer Vance Bourjaily were invited as witnesses to the secessionist state of Biafra during the starvation blockade. She quotes Vonnegut's first-person comparison of the trip to a visit to a Nazi extermination camp in operation and his description of the starving children; I do not reproduce either (register). Non-graphic details she gives: visitors found that children would grasp their hanging fingers and stop crying for a while, and when this happened Bourjaily "burst into tears." She describes the Biafran leader's remark (that if they go forward they die and if they go backward they die, "So we go forward") as gallows humor with a charismatic calm, and says his second-in-command was also a gallows humorist, about whom Bourjaily quipped that he should be the number-two man because he was "the second funniest man in Biafra." Vonnegut writes that the woman who had invited them told him scornfully, "You won't open your mouth unless you can make a joke," and he agrees: "Joking was my response to misery I couldn't do anything about." He recalled it ruefully, saying he could not stop himself. This is a first-person veteran account of his own reflex (WWII service stated on the page), but the setting is not combat and the passage is relayed by McConnell.
*Candidate application:* In the same scene one person jokes and another weeps; the two are different responses to the same helplessness, and each may bother the other. A character may be aware the joking reflex reads as callous to the person next to him and be unable to switch it off.

**[E22a-51]. The cost to the joker: the punch-line reflex, efficient closure, and avoidance.** (WA-062, L173-183; Vonnegut quoted, Thurber and a hypothetical therapist cited by McConnell)
McConnell cites James Thurber's point that a jokester will head for the punch line every time regardless of subject, and that Vonnegut said in a quote she gives that joking is "so much a part of my life adjustment" that he'd begin a story on any subject and "find funny things in it or I would stop." She states the writing problem: jokes deal so efficiently with ideas that there is little to say after the punch line, so one needs a new idea and another joke. She notes that "a therapist might say that to joke relentlessly is to avoid your real feelings" (a hypothetical she attributes to no one in particular), and says the page's point is that Vonnegut recognized the flaw in himself.
*Candidate application:* The reflexive joker's problem is that the joke closes the subject. A veteran may find that every conversation about the war ends because he made the joke that ended it.

**[E22a-52]. Time course: the gallows humorist grows bitter and less funny with age.** (WA-062, L187-199; McConnell with a quotation in Vonnegut's manner; one claim has no quotation marks in the text copy)
A sentence in the text copy, unmarked as a quotation but in the manner of Vonnegut, says American humorists "who choose to laugh rather than weep about demoralizing information, become intolerably unfunny pessimists if they live past a certain age." McConnell says this was true of Vonnegut himself: he became more pessimistic and less prone to joking as he aged, and she lists possible causes (aging; his physician son Mark's explanation of damaged brain chemistry; Vonnegut's own 1979 view that religious skeptics become very bitter toward the end because they finally realize there is no heaven or Judgment Day, as he says Mark Twain did). These are one writer's trajectory and a student's and relative's opinions.
*Candidate application:* The humor of a veteran in later life may thin and sour rather than ripen; the time-course picture is a decline in the ability to joke through, not just in the quantity of jokes. For the defector (10 to 15 years out and not yet old) this is an early-stage possibility, not a rule.

**[E22a-53]. The deadpan "I had to laugh like hell" that is never laughed.** (WA-062, L201-203; fictional passage from *Hocus Pocus*, quoted by McConnell as an example of a "sober jokester")
The quoted passage describes a professional-soldier character whose favorite expression is that he "had to laugh like hell" at serious, beautiful, dangerous or holy events, but who, the narrator says, kept a straight face when telling about it and was never heard to laugh. The mechanism is a verbal tag for an absurdity, delivered with no laughter, so the stated reaction (laughing) and the shown reaction (nothing) diverge. The passage mentions that the character's life ended in a named Vietnam battle, which I treat as fictional background.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may use a stock phrase for that-was-absurd, delivered flatly; the phrase signals the category of event without the performance of laughing. The gap between the saying and the face is itself characteristic.

### From WA-063 (The M*A*S*H Historian: a fan's review of Larry Gelbart's memoir, *Laughing Matters*, 1998; April 2024)

**[E22a-54]. The showrunner's stated stance, as relayed by a reviewer: anti-war rather than anti-military, and not just another war comedy.** (WA-063, L29-31; reviewer's summary of Gelbart's memoir, not Gelbart's own words)
The reviewer (an unnamed fan-historian) says Gelbart, the series' head writer, did not want the show to be anti-military but anti-war, understood that Vietnam-era sentiment was largely negative toward war and that the series, "like the original film and novel," could ride it, and did not want a war comedy in the manner of two named earlier military sitcoms; he wanted the series to confront the reality of war and show the humanity of people living through it. The reviewer adds that Gelbart and the producers did not want the reality of war overshadowed by the comedy, "and it didn't." The page also states that the first season struggled to find its voice, that "Dear Dad" was the first episode to run multiple storylines (over CBS's objections), and that the writers made a research trip to South Korea. The review gives no quotation from Gelbart and no description of how a joke is built.
*Candidate application:* A comic military piece can be pointed at the war and not the soldiers; the distinction between "anti-war" and "anti-military" is one a defector character may insist on in humor.

**[E22a-55]. What the broadcaster fought over: blood in the operating room, certain words, certain concepts.** (WA-063, L29; reviewer's summary)
According to the reviewer, Gelbart and the producers "had to fight CBS quite a bit" over blood in the operating room, the censorship of certain words, and the censorship of certain concepts (the page's example: virginity), and won more of these fights as the show succeeded. This is a data point on the gap between what a mass-audience gatekeeper allows and what a war setting contains.
*Candidate application:* Civilian-facing military comedy is shaped by gatekeepers; what reaches an outside audience is a negotiated, cleaned-up version, which is itself a reason for a veteran to distrust the "funny version."

**[E22a-56]. An unsourced claim about Korean War veterans and off-duty comedy.** (WA-063, L31; reviewer's assertion)
The reviewer says that comedy complemented the reality of war well "because many Korean War veterans have stressed how important comedy in their off hours was important to mentally surviving the war." No veteran, study or number is cited, and the sentence is the reviewer's own (the same page lists Gelbart's credited reasons for the show's success as the cast, writing, the Vietnam connection and the single-camera look, with no mention of this). Also note an internal inconsistency: the episode "The Interview" is listed once as season four episode 24 (L29) and once as season one episode 24 (L31). Treat the veterans claim as an unverified assertion.
*Candidate application:* None beyond a flagged lead: the claim that comedy "in their off hours" mattered for survival is plausible but not supported here, and should be checked against primary veteran sources before use.

### From WA-096 (Phil Klay on NPR *Fresh Air*, interviewer Terry Gross; KCUR page dated 2014-11-25; veteran author, Marine public affairs officer, Anbar Province, January 2007 to February 2008)

**[E22a-57]. The fictional narrator's practice: lie, size up the audience, pick the funny or the sad version, "Either way, it's the same story."** (WA-096, Klay reading from "Bodies," L23; fictional narrator's account, not Klay's)
Klay reads the opening of "Bodies" aloud. The narrator says that for a long time he was angry, did not want to talk about Iraq, and would not even tell people he had been; if they pressed, he told lies. He would then "size them up," to see whether they wanted him to continue, and, he says, a surprising number do. He describes the two versions, funny or sad, split by listener ("guys" like it funny with gore and a grin; "girls" like it sad with a thousand-yard stare; this is the narrator's stereotype, as in WA-058), and then adds a line that WA-058's excerpt does not carry: "Either way, it's the same story." The reading includes the start of the funny version (graphic, with a slur) which I do not reproduce. Terry Gross adds, after the reading, that the narrator works in mortuary affairs, and reads the passage as meaning it is hard to talk about what you see in war and sometimes you just lie; this gloss is the host's.
*Candidate application:* The same events are tailored by the teller to the listener's expected appetite, with the audience screened first, and the tailoring is a deliberate performance whose cost is that the real material stays unsaid. This is the mechanism behind a "funny version" and a "sad version."

**[E22a-58]. "Decoy stories": funny or interesting stories that are emotionally removed from what matters, and a way of keeping people at a distance.** (WA-096, Klay's answer, L31-33; Klay's explanation, crediting another veteran author for the term)
Asked about the passage, Klay says the narrator went through "some very hard things" but does not have "the war stories like people imagine," does not feel "unallied pride" (sic; probably "unalloyed") about what he did, feels conflicted and does not know how to feel, and that what he did does not fit what people expect, so he tells stories where "he knows how to get a reaction and how to fit into a particular type of narrative." Klay adds that "it's also a way of keeping people at a distance," and that another veteran author (the transcript spells the name "Michael Petrie," which may be a transcription error for a veteran-writer's name; unverified) writes of "decoy stories": stories about Iraq that are funny or interesting but, "at an emotional level," removed from what is really important to the teller. Klay reads the story as the narrator trying to get out of that mode and find the right audience for the stories of his deployment that really matter. In the same exchange Gross completes the thought that Klay himself lacks the stories people expect because he was a public affairs officer, and Klay agrees; he describes the job as a staff job with some time outside the wire, "a relatively safe job in a very dangerous place," and says he was not in combat.
*Candidate application:* This is the clearest documented statement of the funny version as a deflection device. A defector with a polished set of funny stories for civilians may be keeping the real material away from them, and be looking for one audience (a companion, another veteran) to whom the other stories can be told. The decoy is not a lie; it is a true-enough story chosen for its distance.

**[E22a-59]. Overload and deferral: you settle into the routine and "think about it later."** (WA-096, answer L63-65; Klay reporting; restrained)
Klay describes a mass-casualty night early in his deployment, when he carried a wounded child on a stretcher after a suicide truck bombing near a mosque; he thought he would never forget the child's face, "and then by the end of the night, I couldn't have picked him out of a lineup," because there were far too many injured. He says the environment is shocking, that you "look for a thing that you can do" (blood-donor lines, stretchers, supplies), settle into the routine and do your job, and that "it's a little bit too much to take in, and then you think about it later." I summarize and do not reproduce the injury description (register). This is a delayed-processing statement consistent with the Gallagher and Abrams accounts (E22a-4, E22a-25); it does not mention humor.
*Candidate application:* The first response to overload is task and routine; the weight arrives later. A character's joking may be located in the "routine" phase and the rest postponed.

**[E22a-60]. Why tell it, and the question civilians ask: "did you kill anybody."** (WA-096, answers L67-77; Klay's claims and one reported veteran view)
Gross quotes from Klay's New York Times piece that telling death experiences "tends to decay into a kind of pornographic voyeuristic experience." Klay replies that the question is why you are telling it: for voyeuristic shock, as the "Bodies" narrator does, or to work through grave issues. He says people have a fascination with war; sixth graders he taught asked whether he had killed anyone, and adults have too, "with a lot less excuse." He reports that some veterans call that "the most obscene question you can ask," and relays one veteran with extensive combat experience as saying it is not the question itself that offends but that "the people asking it don't seem to respect the moral seriousness of the question." Klay's test for his own stories: what are you trying to do, and what is the outcome, are you "enthralled to a particular kind of spectacle." The offense described is lack of seriousness in the asker, not the topic.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's reaction to a civilian's question is governed by whether the asker treats it with seriousness. The funny or gory version is what the spectacle-seeking audience gets; the serious asker may get something else.

**[E22a-61]. Civilian reactions: awe, then pity, then pathology; the dry arithmetic retort; and what the PTSD veteran said people never ask.** (WA-096, answer L157-163; Klay reporting, plus a reported veteran)
Klay says that earlier in the war, when it was more popular, he got a "you must be a super badass" reaction, and later, as the war became unpopular, people assumed he had post-traumatic stress and were sorry for him. He recounts a man in a bar telling him that all Iraq veterans would "snap" after ten years, so that with three back he had seven left; the text continues "so I better make them good years" and "it didn't affect me. I always kind of brushed it off" (it is unclear whether the "make them good years" line was said to the man or is Klay's afterthought; the page does not say). He says the PTSD trope is "a placeholder" and not about actual PTSD, that he has friends with it who are highly capable, that pity bothered him, and that a veteran who did have it told him "the people are only interested in the worst things that ever happened to me," and never ask about the people they loved in the unit. He cites Elizabeth Samet (who teaches at West Point) that it is easier to pathologize veterans than to examine our own relationship to violence, and says the individual veteran's experience gets conflated with the nation's feelings about the wars.
*Candidate application:* The civilian register runs from hero to victim, and both are ways of not hearing. A wry arithmetic retort is a possible response style; a veteran may bristle that the questions go only to the worst day and never to the unit he loved.

**[E22a-62]. Guilt of the staff job and of leaving: "there's always somebody who's risking more."** (WA-096, answers L79-87; Klay reporting; restrained)
Asked about watching fellow Marines go outside the wire while he had a staff job, Klay says there is "always somebody who's risking more," that this continues after leaving because the force is small and volunteer, that he feels guilt "that you didn't choose to do the harder thing" and respect for those who did. He tells of a Marine he knew who volunteered for Afghanistan after a friend died saving his life, in order to help others get through a hard deployment, and who died midway through that deployment; Klay says that if you know someone like that and chose instead to live in New York, "it's something that you think about." Gross labels this survivor's guilt; Klay agrees many veterans feel it, "even guys who have been through a tremendous amount of danger," and says the question is what you do about it (his example: veterans working with a refugee assistance project). The page does not link this to humor.
*Candidate application:* A staff or support veteran's humor may sit on top of a particular guilt at having had the safer job; a defector who left may have a corresponding thread of having left others to it.

**[E22a-63]. A cartoon name for a grim practice, and an ending that "doesn't close the circle."** (WA-096, Klay reading and answers, L95-119; Klay reporting what Marines told him; restrained)
In the title story's opening (read aloud), a unit's practice of shooting stray dogs during urban fighting is called, in the narrator's telling, by an operation name drawn from a children's cartoon; the narrator says he thought about it a lot, that at the time you do not think about it, and that you are thinking about who is in the house, what he is armed with and how he will kill you and your buddies. (I do not reproduce the trigger scene, which involves animals and blood; register.) Klay says it was an entry point for him: he knew a couple of Marines who had talked about watching dogs eat corpses in the Second Battle of Fallujah and one who talked about the operation by that name, and it seemed to crystallize "the strangeness of the actions that people take in a war zone" that "make a certain kind of sense in a war zone" and feel different when you come home. The story ends with the veteran, at home, insisting on personally ending his own old, sick dog; Klay says it is "a kind of complicated moment," "a little bit of all of those things" (responsibility, love, atonement, moral injury, as Gross proposes), and that the last line (the narrator not remembering what to do with the body) means the story "doesn't close the circle on any of those things." Whether the operation name was a real unit's wording, or how widely it was used, is not established by the page beyond Klay's report that a Marine mentioned it.
*Candidate application:* A cute, cartoon-derived name for a grim unit practice is a documented-in-fiction device of euphemistic whimsy over horror, one a veteran may still use, flatly, years later. The story's domestic ending shows that wartime habits come home and that the humor and the grief are not resolved by distance.

**[E22a-64]. Silence and stillness as the ritual for the fallen.** (WA-096, Klay setting up and reading the closing excerpt, L165-167; fictional passage; restrained)
Klay introduces and reads the end of an artillery story: after firing a howitzer round at human targets for the first time and never seeing the results, the narrator recalls that corpsmen carrying a fallen Marine past were met with everyone standing at attention in utter silence, and imagines the body's route home (a unit's mortuary handlers, then transfers through several countries) with Marines, sailors, soldiers and airmen standing silent and still at every stage until it reaches the family, "where the silence, the stillness would end." The passage contains no humor. It is included because it is the page's one picture of a collective ritual register for death (silence and stillness) set against which jokes about the dead would be read, and the page does not say whether jokes are or are not told elsewhere in that chain.
*Candidate application:* A unit can hold a strict ceremonial silence for the transport of the dead and a separate, ribald register for everything else. For a character, the rule is one of place and moment: not a ban on joking but a limit on where.

**[E22a-65]. The interview as a small enactment: the civilian host's discomfort, the laughter, and the invitation to ask.** (WA-096, L25-31; interview moment)
After the "Bodies" reading, Gross says, "you're a vet. And we both know what you've just written there," and the transcript marks laughter; Gross then says it feels almost uncomfortable to ask a veteran about the war, and about whether to ask or not ask. Klay answers, "Don't feel uncomfortable," because "that's part of why I wrote the book, to have conversations about it." The laughter is a release when the interviewer notices that the narrator's situation (what to ask, what to tell) is the host's too. The moment is brief and the page gives no further analysis.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who is secure enough may explicitly invite questions and defuse the civilian's awkwardness with ease; that confidence is itself a marker of someone who has found an audience (as against the decoy-story teller of E22a-58).

## Pattern roll-up

*(Distilled from craft statements by writers, editors and critics; labeled by what kind of speaker each pattern comes from. Only the lens headings the sources speak to.)*

**Functions**
- **Survival and proof of being alive:** humor as "one of a soldier's survival tools," reminding people their present is not their eternity (Gallagher, a veteran reporting on his platoon, E22a-1). The civilian-facing translation of the same function is "the thin membrane between these guys and insanity" and "detoxify" (Trudeau, an outside editor, E22a-7).
- **Response to helplessness:** laughing or crying is what a person does when nothing else can be done; the biggest laughs rest on the biggest disappointments and fears; a tightly tuned, collectively helpless audience over-laughs (Vonnegut, quoted; E22a-44). Vonnegut's own reflex: joking as his response to misery he could not fix, with a companion who wept in the same scene (E22a-50).
- **Facing what one would avert one's eyes from:** "a really telling joke allows us to face things we'd rather avert our eyes from, and it makes us glad to do so" (Klay, E22a-12).
- **A deflection device:** funny or interesting "decoy stories" that are emotionally removed from what matters, which keep people at a distance (Klay, via a veteran-author's term, E22a-58); joking that displaced free association in therapy (Heller, E22a-41).
- **Cohesion through in-group vocabulary:** rear-area slang in two wars ("fobbit," and a Vietnam-era acronym; E22a-23, E22a-32); acronyms as the language in which the speaker says what plain words cannot (Klay, E22a-13).

**Targets**
- **The institution and its machinery:** bureaucratic and acronym language, aid money and contractors, headquarters life and PowerPoint, scrubbed public language (Klay, Abrams: E22a-10, E22a-13, E22a-23, E22a-24); the target is "a question of competence," not the war itself (Klay, E22a-22).
- **The self and the rear-area self:** the "fobbit" author's own base-bound position (Abrams, E22a-23); self-directed jokes that coexist with real cost (Vonnegut on smoking, E22a-48).
- **The mission and the war's shape:** anti-war rather than anti-military (Gelbart as relayed, E22a-54); absurdity from civilian-embedded, murky wars (critic Powers, E22a-9).
- **Death, deadpan:** the condemned person's understatement (the hangman and electric-chair jokes; E22a-45); "up in Heaven now" as a stock mock-eulogy (E22a-47). Not at subordinates' expense (Vonnegut's teaching practice, E22a-47).

**Forms and mechanics**
- **Build-up over quipping:** "you can't be funny just by saying a joke, you have to lead up to it"; three wisecracks in a row fail without narrative progress (Heller, E22a-37).
- **Exaggeration and multiplication of the real:** scaling documented details; taking a target's own words (Heller, E22a-38); making war "as loud and distorted" as needed (Abrams, E22a-26).
- **Deadpan formulas:** "had to laugh like hell" said without a smile (Vonnegut's fiction, E22a-53); understatement from the condemned (E22a-45).
- **Cartoon or whimsical naming over grim practice:** the operation name in "Redeployment" (E22a-63).
- **A literary line adopted in the field:** "someday this will all be yours" used as an aid worker's orientation joke (E22a-46; humanitarian, not military, one anecdote).
- **Boundary-marking jokes:** the "you weren't there" punch line (E22a-8).
- **Honesty contract:** the comic war book signals that it is arranged (Heller, E22a-39); the standard is not strict honesty but making the culture see something new (Klay, E22a-15).

**Rules of license**
- **Audience screening:** the "Bodies" narrator sizes up the listener before choosing a version (E22a-57); Gallagher's authenticity rule is about leaders, not jokers, but is stated as "soldiers can sniff out frauds" (E22a-3).
- **Seriousness of the asker:** the offense in "did you kill anybody" is the asker's lack of moral seriousness (reported veteran view, E22a-60).
- **Power and the joke:** a teacher with authority jokes about ideas and death but never at his students' expense (E22a-47).
- **Strangers are easier:** "with a stranger there's less at stake" (E22a-20).

**Limits and failure modes**
- **The funny version is false to the unit:** the narrator admits his gory-funny story was "total bullshit" because no one in his unit talked about remains that way (fiction, E22a-19).
- **The joke closes the subject; relentless joking avoids feelings:** Vonnegut and Thurber on the punch-line reflex (E22a-51).
- **Joking is not resolution:** Abrams is "still just as conflicted" after a comic novel (E22a-29); Vonnegut's smoking joke coexisted with emphysema and guilt (E22a-48).
- **Civilian reactions of awe, pity and pathology** foreclose real conversation (E22a-61).
- **Some silences are ritual, not repression:** the chain of silent standing for a fallen service member (fiction, E22a-64).

**Time course and distance**
- **Processing comes after:** "we only fully process what happened after we return home" (Abrams, E22a-25); overload and "think about it later" (Klay, E22a-59); thinking too much is dangerous in the field (Gallagher, E22a-4).

## Gaps and cautions

- **Craft statements are not field evidence.** Almost everything here is a novelist, critic, editor or fan describing humor in books, with a few first-person reports by veterans (Gallagher on his platoon, Abrams on his headquarters tour, Klay on Marines he knew and on civilian reactions, Vonnegut on his own reflex).
