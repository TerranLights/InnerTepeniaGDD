## Source-shape notes (read first)

**Citation convention.** `[p.N]` is the PDF-page marker in the local text copy. For MV-B-001 the `[p.1-3]` markers are the poem's three pages (printed journal pages 82-84). For MV-B-002 and MV-B-003 the `[p.N]` markers are PDF pages of the JME Vol. 1 volume file and the printed page is N minus 11 (Martin runs [p.70-82], printed 59-71; Reichert [p.67-69], printed 56-58). The MEA web-site pieces have no page markers, so I cite **L = line number in the local .txt** and, where a story uses `***` scene breaks, the scene count. The O-Dark-Thirty files carry `[p.N]` markers (cited in their own entries). American spelling is used throughout, including in findings about sources that spell otherwise.

**Genre caution that governs the whole unit.** Nothing in the MEA text copies states "memoir" or "fiction" except where an author note says so. Where a piece has no author note, I label it **[NF?]** or **[FIC?]** from its internal evidence and say why. The index flags five MEA pieces (Watts, Whitehead, Bleuer, Laaser, McPadden) as genre-unstated and Davis as fiction per its bio; the O-Dark-Thirty items by Thompson and Rand are labeled fiction in their titles. No piece in this unit is independently verified as autobiographical. By my reading of the text copies, nine pieces carry no author note at all (listed under Gaps and cautions).

**MV-B-001 Sutton, "The Army (with apologies to Kipling)" [POEM].** One profane satirical poem (about 400 words; three PDF pages) printed in *Blue Streak: A Journal of Military Poetry* Vol. 1. Evidence base: one voice narrating an officer's career from platoon leader to battalion commander to retirement. **No author bio in the text copy**; the narrator and the author are not verifiably the same person. It borrows a canonical poet's frame ("with apologies to Kipling") without saying which poem. **Does NOT contain:** a setting, a date, a combat event, a named army (the staff-section vocabulary is U.S. Army). It contains profanity and one crude remark built on a personnel-office role and sexual orientation, summarized only (register below).

**MV-B-002 Martin, "One Night in Iraq" [NF?].** About 5,000 words, first-person present tense, one convoy-escort night "30 miles north of Najaf," narrated by the lead-truck gunner of a Humvee escorting mostly local-national drivers; interleaves banter, an escalation-of-force routine, and the gunner's long moral self-accounting. **No author bio and no genre label in the text copy**; the index descriptor "Army gunner, Iraq" is the index's, not the file's. The year is not stated. Names of crewmates (a sergeant and a captain) may be real or altered. **Does NOT contain:** a date, a unit designation, a bio, or any retrospective frame; everything is told as unfolding. It contains an intrusive imagined scene of the narrator's own death and passages about harm to civilians, summarized only (register below).

**MV-B-003 Reichert, "My Bosnian Deployment" [NF?].** About 1,100 words, a first-person retrospective ("I am out of the Army now"), January 2000, 3rd Armored Cavalry Regiment, Bosnia. **No author bio and no genre label.** It is organized by the narrator into three sections: "crazy and funny" pastimes, absurd missions, then "hellish" times. **Does NOT contain:** recorded dialogue beyond two short remarks, jokes in the sense of joke texts, or any post-service humor. Its closing passage is about a mass-grave guard shift and trauma symptoms, summarized only (register below). The narrator's statement that the war "was still going on" in 2000 sits against the official label "police action" that the narrator quotes; it is the narrator's own account and is not independently checked here.

**MV-B-004 Inks, "Love in the Time of Combat Injuries" [FIC?].** About 3,900 words, first-person, a medevaced sergeant at Walter Reed in 2010 who falls for a wounded Marine officer; ends years later at Bagram. **Has an author note** (Benjamin Inks: Purple Heart recipient, three years in the Army, later literature teacher, published in *Proud to Be* and *O-Dark-Thirty Review*) but **no genre label**; the narrator is introduced as "Sergeant Fischer" and "Jake," a different name from the author's, and the structure is romantic-comedy shaped, so I treat it as **fiction or autofiction** and not as a record of real conversation. Evidence of how a wounded veteran *writes* hospital humor, not of how patients spoke. **Does NOT contain:** any unit-level humor; it is a two-person (and nurse-and-visitor) hospital setting. It contains bodily-function and catheter humor and a sexual-innuendo idiom, summarized only.

**MV-B-005 Smiley, "Fifty Dollars Dead" [NF].** About 3,000 words; retrospective first-person account of 1968 in Vietnam (a 1st lieutenant, first on the DMZ in air defense artillery, then team leader of a mobile advisory team to a South Vietnamese Regional Force company in the Mekong Delta); written more than 56 years later ("to this day, more than fifty-six years later"). **Has an author note** (Richard H. Smiley, Ph.D., platoon leader and then team leader of MAT-65, retired psychologist) that matches the narrator's role, so I treat it as first-person nonfiction although the page does not label it. Evidence base: one man's single-tour narrative whose spine is a hunt for an informant. **Does NOT contain:** any joke texts, banter, nicknames or description of American unit humor; humor appears in exactly three places (the bounty frame, one slapstick moment, one "gruesome humor" episode among allied soldiers). It contains a mutilation episode and a racially coded comparison, both summarized only.

## Findings

### From MV-B-001 (Sutton, poem)

**[E25a-1]. [POEM] An officer's career told as a chain of humiliations, with the senior NCO as the real teacher.** (MV-B-001, [p.1-3])
Genre: profane satirical verse, parody of a canonical poet's frame. Author descriptor: name only (Geoff Sutton); no bio in the text. The narrator is an Army officer who recounts a career in stages, each ending in a rebuke from a sergeant: a platoon sergeant who cuffs the new platoon leader and tells him to clear things before doing anything dumb, and, at battalion level, a command sergeant major who pulls the new colonel out of a briefing to tell him his logic is thin. The refrain makes the lesson explicit: "And I learned 'bout the Army from him." The middle stanzas walk the staff sections one by one, each as a small tribe with its own grievance, using the acronyms as the joke's material (personnel, intelligence overlays, logistics, operations orders, courses of action): the officer is baffled by the personnel shop, dismissive of intelligence as nonsense, and ignorant of logistics ("food just ... appeared"), and relieved only at operations, "something real." One stanza makes a crude remark about the adjutant role and a sexual-orientation stereotype; another about shrunken swagger (restrained handling, see register). The humor's target is the narrator himself (officer self-satire), plus the staff branches as rival cultures; the NCO is the one figure treated with affection.
*Candidate application:* The officer-as-fool-corrected-by-the-sergeant is a standing comic structure that a former-officer or a former-NCO character could carry, and it shows that satire of one's own rank and career can be affectionate toward the people below it. The acronym-stacking is itself the register of insider humor: it only lands if the listener has lived the branches.

**[E25a-2]. [POEM] The poem stays comic to the end and lets a diagnosis ride in the same meter.** (MV-B-001, [p.3])
The last stanza leaves the career behind and moves to retirement: single malt at the end of the day, and then, in the same light rhythm, "And PTSD, I can't leave the basement, / At least there's calm in a Henry Clay" (a cigar brand). There is no tonal break and no explanation; the diagnosis is delivered as one more item in a list of comforts, with the calm of the cigar offered as the consolation. The preceding stanza before it, about being "saved by an old NCO," is the last sincere note, but it too is in the poem's comic meter. Nothing in the poem says who the audience for the joke is; its form (rhymed, regular, profane) addresses fellow officers and veterans.
*Candidate application:* A late-life or out-of-service character can state a serious condition inside the same joking cadence he used for the career, with the delivery unchanged; the absence of a tonal break is characterization (the habit is so formed that even the worst fact is metered).

### From MV-B-002 (Martin, "One Night in Iraq")

**[E25a-3]. [NF?] Night-convoy banter as relief from "nervous silence," with an unofficial scoring of wit.** (MV-B-002, [p.71-72])
Genre: first-person present-tense narrative, unlabeled; author descriptor from the index only (Army gunner, Iraq). The narrator, the exposed gunner of the lead Humvee, says the chatter of the sergeant and the captain in the cab below is "a welcome relief from the nervous silence accompanying night missions," and that over a 36-hour stretch the three men talk on the headsets about sex, insults and life. The narrator lays out the rules of the game without being asked: insults are not malicious but "a sign of camaraderie," the narrator has been "called every name in the book in the most loving of ways," and the men "judge each other based on the promptness and quality of our rebuttals." The narrator places himself as the weaker contestant (regionally, a Kentucky approach "slower and relaxed" versus two northerners who are "a little wittier"), says he is silent most of the time, speaks only when he has thought about what to say, and settles on a self-deprecating angle as his usual move. The narrator also lists topics he feels unqualified to join: sexual conquests and the "brutal" insult duel. The sergeant and the captain have, in the narrator's mock-solemn phrase, "reached a conclusion concerning the meaning of life" (a mock-grand framing of the cab chatter).
*Candidate application:* A character can be placed on a humor ladder inside a crew, with a defined reply-speed standard and a safe low-risk niche (self-deprecation) for the less quick member; the niche is a role, not a weakness. Insult-as-affection with a scoring rule is a reusable crew mechanic.

**[E25a-4]. [NF?] Call signs made up in the headset, with rank deciding how rude the nickname may be, and mock-formal radio procedure as comedy.** (MV-B-002, [p.72-73])
In a flash of invention while sleep-deprived, the narrator offers an entirely made-up personal anecdote (a mock origin story for a crude anatomical nickname, from "the guys in the showers" in high school), the sergeant and the captain bless it as a call sign and attach a mock-heroic legend ("the sickest, deadliest and craziest gunner in all of Iraq"), and the narrator extends the legend himself (enemies will lock their doors in fear). The others receive their own anatomical call sign (the sergeant) and a rank-abbreviation alliteration (the captain, a play on how a lieutenant is addressed); the narrator explains the captain's was "less severe" because "outside the humvee, he was an officer who commanded respect." The nicknames then move onto the convoy's radio net as call signs: the narrator and the sergeant make mock-formal radio calls to the rest of the convoy ("Roger ... over," "Actual," a report of "a brown box that looks suspicious"), and the other trucks answer with "chuckles overtake the static." The humor is a closed game among people who share the same long hours; the mock-procedure reuses the exact language of the dangerous job (suspicious object on the road) as the joke's skeleton.
*Candidate application:* Nickname creation as a spontaneous, group-authored event on a headset or radio net, where the severity of the nickname is modulated by the target's rank outside the vehicle. A comic call sign that doubles as a working call sign is a portable device for a crew culture with a radio.
*Cross-use:* Robots or crews on a shared comm channel who co-author each other's handles. The crude content of the nicknames is not reproduced here (see register).

**[E25a-5]. [NF?] Self-mockery as concealment: jokes keep the crew from seeing that the narrator has never fired to kill.** (MV-B-002, [p.77])
The most direct statement of function in the piece: "I use jokes to keep people from realizing I've never fired on anyone with the intent to kill." The narrator's jokes are a mock-title ("God's gift to war") and a latrine-wall saying he read in Kuwait, "Truck drivers pay no toll at the gates of Hell," which the narrator turns into an identity statement: "just truck drivers," not trained killers, pretending nightly to be infantry. The humor is thus aimed at the narrator's position (support unit in a combat role), and what it covers is a private moral stance (restraint) that the narrator believes would cost him standing. The narrator calls this a hidden asymmetry between how he is seen (confident, macho) and what he does (throws a rock instead of firing).
*Candidate application:* A character whose public persona is hard-edged joking, and whose private practice is mercy, with the jokes as the cover story. When that cover is penetrated, the cost is felt as lost standing, not as moral exposure.

**[E25a-6]. [NF?] The windshield rock: group laughter at a non-lethal act, and a mock-arithmetic that justifies it.** (MV-B-002, [p.76-78], [p.80])
Faced with an approaching car, the narrator has invented a third option to the rules of engagement: throwing a rock through the windshield. The crew laughs at the sound of breaking glass, the car stops, and later "everyone thinks it was the funniest thing in the world" (the narrator's phrase, [p.80]); a man outside his car cursing the convoy in Arabic draws chuckling on the radio. The narrator turns it into a private formula in which "each broken windshield and fit of ensuing laughter" is "equal to a life saved," and into a mock-demographic calculation (each driver not killed prevents at least nine future enemies) that the narrator says he tells himself; he adds that he knows the real sums are not that tidy, and the passage then drops into imagery of harm to a civilian (summarized, not reproduced; register). The laughter is the crew's response to the narrator's rule-bending; the narrator's rule-bending is itself an exercise in comic reasoning.
*Candidate application:* A character who devises a comic workaround to violence and is rewarded with laughter may become attached to the workaround because the laughter is the only recognition it gets. The mock-calculation is a form (absurd arithmetic over moral weight) worth keeping distinct from the quips.

**[E25a-7]. [NF?] Which killing may be funny: the narrator draws a line the Reserve unit does not, and satirizes the rules that created it.** (MV-B-002, [p.78-79])
The narrator describes a Reserve unit that followed the rules of engagement literally on the same route and left disabled civilian cars behind, and says the scary part is "I have heard people talk about how this kind of thing is funny and actually brag" and recount it at home as having "engaged the enemy." The narrator's own humor (self-mockery, rock-throwing) is set against this: laughing about restraint is acceptable to the narrator, laughing about the kills is not. The narrator also voices the satirical reading of the rules: the sequence of spotlight, flare and glow sticks makes any shooting legal, the brass "basically say what I have said happens through a wink and a nod," and "Lives are just winked and nodded away." A wall slogan ("Complacency Kills") is quoted straight at first and then ironized ("like a good little soldier"), and the narrator self-mocks his own ego ("there's that ego coming back into play"). The text is explicit that the line the narrator holds is his own, not the group's.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may keep a private rule about what is funny (restraint yes, bragging about civilian kills no), and the rule may differ from his unit's. Bitter satire of rules of engagement is a legitimate target of his humor, while the dead civilians themselves are not.

**[E25a-8]. [NF?] After a failed call, the crew's teasing enforces the standard and the narrator absorbs it by joking at his own expense.** (MV-B-002, [p.80-82])
When the narrator hesitates on a second car, the captain returns on foot and demands to know if the narrator is going to "sit there and let this guy blow us up," and there is a hard rebuke ("if you don't have what it takes then I'm going to find someone else"); the captain's anger, the narrator notes, is that the car got that close, not that it was not shot. On the ride home, in the narrator's words, "I get made fun of" ("that guy punked you out back there") with gendered insults about courage and being a bad gunner. The narrator's response: "I try, as usual, to turn the whole thing into a joke at my own expense," and then "I just take it. This is my cross to bear," because it denies "trigger-happy kids" their bragging rights. So the same joking layer carries two functions at once: discipline (the crew's teasing marks what counts as failure) and shield (the narrator's own self-mockery pre-empts the accusation). The narrator also says people "see through to my massive ego" and resent him, which positions the teasing as partly resentment and not only affection.
*Candidate application:* Teasing in a crew can be the mechanism by which standards are enforced without formal discipline, and a character who absorbs it by joking at himself is paying for a private moral choice with public status. The humor and the grievance travel together.

**[E25a-9]. [NF?] Where the piece goes plain: the dread passages carry no humor, and the ending is an unjoked collapse into sleep.** (MV-B-002, [p.74-75], [p.82])
The piece alternates registers. Banter and invention sit in the cab sections; the long sections about the narrator's moral burden carry no jokes. These include an intrusive, graphic imagined scene of the narrator's own death and the crew's, which the narrator says he runs "before every mission" (summarized only; register), a sober account of what the lead gunner must decide, and a gallows aside that a roadside decapitation hazard favors gunners who overthink, aimed at the narrator himself (summarized, not quoted; register). The final paragraph is flat and physical (tent heat, rifle under the cot, instant sleep) with no punchline. The narrator also says his war is "an algorithm" and that "The whole war thing is an equation": the analytic voice substitutes for the comic one when the stakes are highest.

*Candidate application:* Showing a character who performs the group's register on cue while somewhere else in himself. The refrain "wrong job" is an example of a short, repeated line used at the start and end of a scene to hold it shut.

**[E25a-56]. [NF?] After the crash: "you'll never be a civilian," group drinking, comic framing of violence, and an unfunny fall.** (MV-B-016, [p.14-17], "How to Be a Real Boy")
In the second person: keep your hair long to avoid being read as "Jarhead," do not shave on weekends, drive far from base to bars, and still "you'll never be a civilian," a hybrid, which the writer likens to stray dogs the unit shot (the writer's own self-label for the hybrid uses a term for mixed-ancestry people and is not reproduced; register). The nights out with platoon mates ("talk about fucking and fighting and shooting and drinking") end in a barroom brawl framed with comic devices ("There should be Benny Hill music playing on the juke," "a wild rumpus"), in which a bystander is kicked while the narrator watches (summarized; register), and then a drunken walk back, laughing and "trying to show swollen faces to one another in the dark." The section then leaves comedy: a naked jump from a high platform into a pool at night, friends singing cadence from the water, and a fall the narrator experiences as endless ("you keep falling"). The final sentence returns to the earlier solitary joke as a bleak summary of waiting for the next mission (summarized; register).
*Candidate application:* The transition to civilian life as a self-image problem (a permanent hybrid between soldier and civilian) managed through haircuts and distance from base; the unit's drinking-and-fighting night as a recurring ritual that the writer frames in cartoon terms. A single writer's account, aimed at the period around the end of the enlistment.

### From MV-B-017 (Weaver, "jokes with civilians")

**[E25a-57]. [POEM] The thesis in verse: the jokes are the vocabulary, belonging is the function, and there is "no signal" that tells an outsider when to laugh.** (MV-B-017, [p.142-143])
Genre: poem in a collective voice; author descriptor: name only. The first two lines state the function: "These are the things we laugh about. / It proves we belong." The poem's content is a list of in-group items rather than jokes: a vocabulary that renames daylight ("The Army has first light and before / that, stand to"); acquired tastes (field coffee) and basic skills that would be shocking elsewhere (tying a tourniquet); an argument over polishing boot leather; speaking "almost fondly" of field meals and riot-control gas; talk as "call and response," where one soldier drops a coin and the others follow "as if on command" ("We never disappoint"); remembering each other by last name, rank, squad and "stick" number; stories with sound effects (engine, rotor, shockwave, canopy, "the soft exhale before firing"); and memories with "cadence and caliber, sector and arc." The poem's claims about what cannot cross: "We cannot sanitize or explain"; "Our jargon has no synonyms"; "There is no signal to tell you when it's safe to laugh"; "Our jokes do not translate / into any of your languages." The nomenclature is described as "handles to carry our stories, keep from losing our grip."
*Candidate application:* A short statement of the boundary function (laughing at the right thing is the proof of membership), the form (ritual call and response, jargon, sound-effect storytelling) and the civilian-facing failure (no translation, no safe-to-laugh signal). The poem is a single writer's compression, with no jokes quoted.
*Cross-use:* Exile or defector communities with a private jargon could share the same belonging function; the poem does not address this.

## Pattern roll-up

**Read this first.** The unit is 17 single-writer pieces from literary journals. None is a sample of how veterans talk; each is a veteran *writer's rendering*, shaped by an editor and by the writer's craft ambitions. The patterns below are therefore separated by genre label. **Only Part A is first-person nonfiction or probable nonfiction, and even there the genre is unlabeled for most pieces.** Parts B, C and D show how veteran authors *depict* humor and must not be cited as evidence of how veterans actually joke.

### Part A. First-person nonfiction and probable nonfiction ([NF], [NF?]): Martin (E25a-3 to 9), Reichert (10-12), Smiley (18-20), Clifton (24-25), Sutherland (30-31), Whitehead (32-35), Laaser (39-41), Young (53-56)

*Functions.*
- **Relief from dread and silence:** banter on a headset fills "nervous silence" in a lead truck (3); off-duty play is "therapeutic" and "to get our minds off" separation and fatigue (10); a training memory of laughter is a comfort in a guard tower (24).
- **Concealment:** self-mocking jokes hide that the narrator never fires to kill, for fear of losing standing (5); a self-described darker sense of humor is called "just a coping mechanism" for an unnamed deeper hurt, while a specific event is claimed to be simply funny (31).
- **Worldview as coping:** the war as "a giant, asinine game played by a bunch of clowns," arrived at only when death became possible (32).
- **Discipline and enforcement:** after a failed call the crew's teasing sets the standard and the narrator absorbs it by joking at his own expense (8).
- **Boundary and test:** grotesque humor aimed at an embedded foreign advisor to watch for horror (20).
- **Performance while numb:** laughing and nodding "at the right times" at the aid station while elsewhere (55).

*Targets.* The narrator (self-deprecation) (3, 8); the new or green man (19, 34); a comic superior (33); the support unit's own status (5); institutions and their euphemisms ("police action," "staying hydrated," heat-casualty tape, intelligence "supply zones," the rules of engagement) (7, 11, 32); an allied general's photo-ops (32); the enemy's price on one's head (18); a frightened comrade after a false alarm (25); locals in absurd-mission anecdotes (11).

*Forms and mechanics.* Call signs invented on a headset and then carried onto the radio net with mock-formal radio procedure (4); mock-heroic legend built on a crude self-description (4); mock arithmetic that "justifies" restraint (6); bureaucratic-hyperbole punchline on compensation (11); dated-journal boredom voice with day count and euphemism critique (32); mock regulation voice with bracketed blanks (53); mock-scholarly argument with hyperbolic history (54); a short refrain that brackets a scene ("wrong job") (55); comic soundtrack or framing applied to a brawl (56); an officer's solemn report released by a grin (30); a bounty frame opening and closing an account (18).

*Rules of license.* Insults are affection and are scored on rebuttal speed and quality (3); how rude a nickname may be depends on the target's rank outside the vehicle (4); laughter at a close call is held for the debrief and aimed at the newcomer (19); a superior's joke cannot be refused and wears out as the stakes rise (33); the narrator's own line (restraint is funny, boasting about civilian kills is not) may differ from the unit's (7); no humor at one category (a mass-grave site and its aftermath) (12); a laugh at a frightened man is cut short by the one who recognizes his own fear (25).

*Limits and failure modes.* A superior's "funny" act becomes a hazard and a humiliation the audience does not stop (33, 35); laughter at another's expense after fear passes (25); gallows humor tolerated and left unreported for alliance reasons (20); laughter as symptom, not wit (39); humor as the only leak from "a pain so bad it rarely comes out, if but a chuckle" (40); performed laughter while dissociated (55).

*Time course.* Funny only "after the pain and excitement had faded" (31); "I still laugh about it today" for an absurd mission, while the grave memory returns in dreams and smells (11, 12); training dreaded and later longed for (53); the bounty joke told 56 years later (18); the sense of humor claimed to darken after service (31); in the transition, "you'll never be a civilian" and the long-hair, far-from-base management of identity (56); a happy laugh with children distinguished from the ironic and ward-door laughs (41).

*Settings beyond the unit.* Psychiatric ward: veterans address each other with military shorthand ("trooper") and trade a dry quip about being "back"; soldier-patients are quiet and contained (40). Aid station: a vehicle commander's sexualized banter against the narrator's silence (55). Neither is documented beyond one writer.

### Part B. Genre unverified or suspected fiction ([?], [FIC?]): Inks (13-17), Watts (21-23), Bleuer (36-38), McPadden (42-44)

*As rendered by the writer; not evidence of practice.*
- **Mutual license from shared injury** between two wounded patients, with joke-for-joke harshness and a stated "accountability" rule (13); rank as a play-script, with mock-formal address as flirtation and a salute used to test whether the other will "get weird" (14); the limit at the bereaved ("Gold Star" mothers) and a plain reframe as the turn (15); humor as a way to stay a person while being treated as a "Wounded Warrior" (16).
- **A short-timer's refrain** turned into a platoon laughter-suppression game, then reused at every register (comic, medic's comfort over a mortal injury, mess-hall promise, graffiti on a ritual of betrayal, unspoken last thought) (21-23).
- **Humor as armor in therapy:** a deflecting joke that misfires on a real loss; the loss of face-reading after blinding removes the feedback a joker depends on; the therapist's own matching joke breaks the armor and opens candor (36-37); veterans' talk in a garage remembered as "no glory, no bravado, no ego" (38).
- **A dark anecdote told aloud** where the listener's mild protest that the teller is always so dark is both rebuke and invitation, a sniper's "naughty laugh ... like in church" at a death, and a joking register that slides into contempt and institutional hush (42-44).

### Part C. Fiction ([FIC]): Davis (26-29), Thompson (45-48), Rand (49-52)

*As rendered by veteran authors; not evidence of practice.*
- **Dark humor as mask for pain:** a narrator's stated view that on submarines "often our only humor is dark humor" and that such "conversational mechanisms" mask pain (26); a rehearsed self-obituary in a mock-formal third person with bathos about the dead and a grudge kept in the margin (27); the "onions" deflection and its matching acknowledgment (28); a joke-filled afternoon that ends with a plain "maybe you should" (29).
- **Comic setup and plain collapse:** both Thompson and Davis build a funny, affectionate account and then drop the comic voice at the death or the disclosure (48, 29); the dead friend's son is told "the same lie" (48).
- **Naming and mock-titling:** nicknames from care-package contents, a borrowed celebrity name, a backronym for field rations, capitalized mock-titles, a dismissive name for the support unit, and the joke about who is placed where so an attack would hit them and not headquarters (46).
- **Rumor-chasing as a shared pastime** and tall-tale loyalty (45, 47); political questions about the war's purpose as the topic that "killed the mood" (47).
- **Rank-play and prank economies at a checkpoint:** jabs across rank, a joke that working dogs outrank corporals, a bug-zapper prank rewarded with tobacco (49).
- **Civilian-facing register:** a protagonist's theory that small physical details (a book whose glue melted in the heat) carry meaning civilians lack, and that the true account of a near-shooting cannot be told to parents, so he would "make it fiction" (50).
- **Shared laughter used to bury a moral discomfort:** a prank on a powerless queue of civilians; one member's silent dissent ("Yeah, for you."); a sing-along that makes the scene "more tolerable" and keeps it out of the written record (51-52).

### Part D. Poems ([POEM]): Sutton (1-2), Weaver (57)

- **Career satire in verse** that makes the officer the fool and the senior NCO the teacher, with acronym-stacking as the insider joke, and that lets a diagnosis ride in the same comic meter at the end (1-2). Narrator and author are not verified as the same.
- **A collective-voice poem** stating the function (laughing at the right things "proves we belong"), the form (call and response, jargon with "no synonyms," sound-effect stories) and the failure at the civilian border ("no signal to tell you when it's safe to laugh"; the jokes "do not translate") (57).

### Cross-genre observations (for use with the caution above)

- **A dissenting non-laugher recurs** in nonfiction (Clifton, 25; Martin's narrator, 7) and in fiction (Rand, 51; Thompson's narrator at the end, 48).
- **Humor and the plain turn are structurally paired.** In nearly every piece the comic voice is present in one stretch and absent in another, and the absent stretch is where the writer places the death, the atrocity, the assault or the grief (9, 12, 15, 29, 35, 48, 56).
- **Aftermath is rarely joked about in these pieces.** The post-service material that is not plain is either a single dry retrospective frame (18), a self-reported darkening (31), a stated no-translation boundary (57) or a happy laugh with children (41).

## Gaps and cautions

- **Genre is unverified for most of the unit.** Pieces with an author note that supports or states a genre: Smiley (platoon leader; narrator matches), Clifton (narrator addressed by the author's surname), Davis (fiction), Thompson and Rand (fiction by title). Pieces with **no author note in the text copy:** Sutton, Martin, Reichert, Sutherland, Whitehead, Bleuer, Laaser, McPadden and Weaver (nine). For Martin and Reichert the contributor notes may exist elsewhere in the JME Vol. 1 volume file but are not in the extracted pages; for Sutton the same may hold for *Blue Streak*. Inks and Watts have bios but no genre label. The index descriptors ("Army gunner, Iraq," "Marine, Iraq") are the harvest's, not the files'. Re-check in a browser before any genre-dependent use.
- **Internal tensions that suggest construction:** Whitehead's narrator has "five years" in the Army but is among the "privates" in the truck; Inks's narrator is "Jake," the author is Benjamin; Bleuer's narrator is "Kyle," the author is Seth; Thompson's narrator is an enlisted grenadier while the author served as an artillery officer; Smiley says he was not among those who adopted gallows humor, yet reports a shared "good laugh" the same week (a distinction between slapstick and mutilation humor, but still a tension); Reichert's statement that the war "was still going on" in January 2000 sits against the official "police action" label he himself quotes (the narrator's perception, not checked here).
- **Selection effect.** The harvest log (§3) says these 13 MEA and 4 O-Dark-Thirty items were chosen by humor-keyword density from about 840 MEA pieces and about 490,000 words of O-Dark-Thirty, then read in context. Humor is therefore heavily over-represented relative to what the journals publish, and journal editors and writers in or near creative-writing programs shaped the material (several authors say so in their notes: Clifton, Young, Rand, Inks, Watts).
- **One army and mostly one era.** All pieces are U.S. (Army, Marine Corps, one Air Force air-support squadron, one Navy submariner in fiction). Eras: Vietnam 1968 (Smiley, plus Vietnam characters in Davis and Bleuer), the 1990-91 Gulf War period (Thompson, fiction), Bosnia 2000 (Reichert), Iraq 2003-2009 (Martin, Clifton, Sutherland, Whitehead, Watts, Rand, Young), Afghanistan (Laaser, McPadden, Davis's bartender). No other armies, and no sample sizes: each claim rests on one writer.
- **No defector, exile or dissent-from-institution content in the strict sense.** The nearest items are the self-reported darkening after service (31), the transition-identity piece (56) and the poem on the civilian border (57). Nothing here concerns a person who left on principle.
- **Page and source mechanics.** MEA site pieces have no page markers, so citations use line numbers in the local text. The O-Dark-Thirty files were read through Internet Archive copies of the original PDFs because the journal's own domain no longer resolves; the printed-page offsets are stated in the source-shape notes. Several quotations cross line breaks or page breaks in the text copy; every quotation of three or more words was checked against the files by an automated comparison and corrected, shorter quotations were checked by hand, and the few residual non-matches were artifacts of line-break hyphenation or page markers.
- **Findings run long.** The findings total roughly 30% of the source word count, against the 10-18% aim, because each short narrative carries several distinct humor mechanics and the restrained handling required paraphrase rather than quotation. A later trim of the *Candidate application* lines would shorten the file without losing the documented specifics.
- **Items withheld for content reasons** (see the register) include several of the most extreme passages in the unit; the humor-relevant structure of each is described, but the wording is not. The developer's later re-run on a different model should extend these findings with exact wording only where it matters.
- **Not covered by this unit:** the other MEA and O-Dark-Thirty pieces the harvest log judged to hold only incidental humor, and the Sandbox, milblog and other Tier B material (separate units).
