## Source-shape notes (read first)

**Citation convention.** AC-03, AC-04 and AC-05 are JATS-converted text with **no section headings and no page markers** (AC-37 keeps its headings; AC-38 and AC-15 are PDF text with `[p.N]` markers). For the first three I cite the section I identified from the content plus **L = line number in the local .txt**. For AC-38 and AC-15, `[p.N]` is the PDF page marker: AC-38 printed page = N + 340 (article runs printed 341-353); AC-15 printed page = N + 73 (article runs printed 74-88). Throughout, "humor" is spelled American even where the source (AC-04, AC-15) spells it otherwise; file paths are left as they are on disk.

**The big caution for this whole unit: the six sources do not measure the same thing, and none measures what a soldier would call gallows humor.** AC-03 measures two Humor Styles Questionnaire subscales (aggressive and self-defeating) as a stand-in for "dark humor." AC-05 uses the Comic Style Markers' four "dark" styles (cynicism, sarcasm, satire, irony), which are styles of mockery, not humor about death or injury. AC-37 uses a single item on self-rated ability to keep a sense of humor in tense situations. AC-38 uses the Humor Styles Questionnaire's four styles (two "benign," two "injurious"). Only AC-04 (qualitative) and AC-15 (a few passages) touch the content and social handling of the humor itself. "Dark humor" in a finding below therefore always means the construct that source actually measured, and I say which.

**AC-03 (Yoshimura et al. 2024).** Cross-sectional online survey of **93 volunteer U.S. veterans** (recruited by social-media posts over 10 days in April 2022; no incentive; anonymous). Sample: 55 men (59%), 37 women (40%), 1 other gender identity; mean age 39.25 (SD 10.44); 51 Marines (54%), 28 Army (30%), 7 Air Force, 6 Navy, 1 Coast Guard; mean time served 9.96 years (SD 7.02); 87 enlisted (93%), 5 officers, 1 chief warrant officer. "Veteran" in the call includes active-duty, inactive-duty and discharged personnel, so the sample mixes in-service and out-of-service people with no time-since-separation figure. Regression analyses; Cantril ladder well-being; three connectedness ratings. **Does NOT contain:** a dark-humor-specific measure (the authors say none exists), humor content, who the humor is aimed at or used with, combat exposure, any PTSD measure, qualitative speech by veterans, or the descriptive means/SDs and correlation tables (Tables 1-2 are stripped from the text copy; I use only numbers given in prose). The only joke examples in it are two second-hand ones from firefighters and rural police, described below. Its review of other occupations (medical, police, firefighters, crime-scene and child-exploitation investigators) is a useful map but is second-hand and non-military.

**AC-04 (Mercer, Morgan & Lotto 2024).** Qualitative interview study, **10 UK paramedics** (HCPC-registered, more than 24 months' experience, operational in the past 12 months), recruited by word of mouth and snowball sampling from a university's module notice board; **three group interviews** (two of four people, one of two; about an hour each; via Microsoft Teams; October 2023 to January 2024); Braun and Clarke thematic analysis; four themes. The limitations section says the participants are all students or alumni of one university (Liverpool John Moores). **Not about soldiers.** **Does NOT contain:** jokes (beyond two shared category phrases), participant ages or sexes, the supplementary theme tables and fuller quotes (the text copy gives only short fragments, many of them clipped phrases rather than sentences), any quantification, or veterans. Group interviews mean peers heard each other's answers, and the question list was sent two weeks ahead, which the authors say kept sessions "free from surprises and any triggering conditions."

**AC-05 (Anders et al. 2025).** Cross-sectional online questionnaire (May-July 2022) of **2,291 Swiss German-speaking police officers** from 24 agencies, analyzed from 2,655 starters (excluded: 11 with under 1 year of service, 3 under 19, 29 embassy protection, 27 recruit trainers, 294 incomplete; 364 in all). Mean age 42 (SD 9.78, range 22-66); 79.3% men; 31.5% in management; 35.4% emergency police. Measures: PTSD (IES-R), burnout (OLBI), anxiety and depression (HADS), police stress (PSQ), trauma history (THQ), 14 coping strategies (Brief-COPE), eight humor styles (Comic Style Markers), personality (BFI-10). Methods: Welch t-tests, regression, spectral clustering, structural equation modeling. **Not about soldiers.** **Does NOT contain:** humor content or examples, the group or unit humor culture (the authors say this is untested), where or with whom humor is used, or any longitudinal data. Tables 1-4 and Figures 1-3 are not in the text copy (I use only numbers stated in the prose). The Big Five scale had an average Cronbach's alpha of 0.48, which the authors flag.

**AC-37 (Ward et al. 2021, author manuscript).** Secondary analysis of the **Army STARRS All Army Survey**: 21,449 U.S. Army active-duty soldiers, 20,901 after excluding 548 with all-missing data (only 9,616, or 45%, complete on every variable); mean age 28.66 (SD 7.41, range 18-61); majority cisgender male and White; 35% probable lifetime PTSD, 7% in the past 30 days; no rank or specialty data. Cross-sectional structural equation modeling. **This is the one in-service soldier sample in the unit.** **Does NOT contain:** dark or gallows humor of any kind (humor is one self-rating item), humor content, deployment history detail, or Tables 1-2, figures and appendices (absent from the text copy). The text copy repeats the hypotheses twice (a conversion artifact). About half the file is the reference list; I skipped it.

**AC-38 (Besser et al. 2015).** **105 Israeli married couples (N = 210)**, one spouse injured in a terror attack since 2001 and recognized as at least 20% disabled, drawn from the OneFamily organization's records (182 eligible couples; 23 unreachable, 48 declined, 6 incomplete). Survivors: 65 men, 40 women; mean age 49.32 (SD 11.05); spouses 48.47; mean 24.33 years married; mean 10.30 years since the attack (SD 2.55). Humor Styles Questionnaire, PTSD checklist (civilian version), PHQ-9, GAD-7; actor-partner interdependence model. **Not about soldiers** (civilian terror survivors and their spouses). **Does NOT contain:** gallows or dark humor content, any military sample, or a readable correlation table (Table 2 is badly garbled in the PDF text; I rely on the prose, Table 1 and Figure 1 values that were legible). The reference list was skipped.

**AC-15 (Morris & Hanna 2023).** Qualitative study: **20 British military veterans** (17 men, 3 women, all white British; left voluntarily or for medical reasons; early service leavers, the dishonorably discharged and the fully retired were excluded), recruited through Twitter/X posts and snowballing, interviewed by video or phone (40 to 105 minutes), reflexive thematic analysis, framed by William James's theory of the self. Three themes: destabilizing individualism, renegotiating the self, forging a self-understanding. **Humor is not the topic.** The honest inventory of what the article says about humor is: **one participant account of a misjudged joke and banter at a civilian workplace (Participant 15, [p.7]); one participant account of making "rooms go quiet" with speech they thought normal (Participant 19, [p.7]), which the text does not itself label as humor; one participant account of slang sayings that make military friends laugh and civilians frown (Participant 22, [p.9]); and two sentences of the authors' own gloss on "banter" and humor ([p.7-8]).** **Does NOT contain:** dark or gallows humor, humor as coping, any description of in-service humor, humor content, time-since-service data, or any analysis of how humor changes with years out. Participant numbers run up to 30 although only 20 were interviewed (the numbering is not explained). Branch, rank and combat history are not reported. Reference list skipped.

## Findings

### From AC-03 (Yoshimura et al. 2024, U.S. veterans survey)

**[E04-1]. Definitions, the benign-violation frame, and why a joke misfires.** (AC-03, Introduction, L15, L21-31, L47-49)
The authors define dark humor (their words, interchangeable with gallows humor) as treating "serious, frightening, or painful subject matter in a light or satirical way." They lean on benign violation theory (McGraw and Warren): something is funny when it breaks one norm but is acceptable under another, when the norm broken is at least somewhat acceptable to the receiver, or when the receiver feels psychologically distant from it. The implication they draw is that dark humor fails for people who do not accept the violation or cannot get distance from it. The misfire example they borrow (from a physician-patient study) is a doctor's retort to a worried patient: not unfunny in every possible setting, but unfunny to this patient at this moment, which the original authors called destructive physician-generated humor. The authors also note that dark humor is hard to separate from non-humor or malice because the distinction depends on the situation, so most studies rely on the researcher's or participant's own labeling. They add that humor of this kind need not be intentionally silly or cruel; they cite a view that it is an honest, transgressive reflection of a hard situation, and a view that it is "a way to maintain sanity under insane circumstances" more than a repertoire of jokes.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's joke can be written so the same words work in one room and wound in another, and the character may fail to notice the room changed. The misfire is the patient's lack of distance, not an absence of wit.

**[E04-2]. The insider code, and exclusion as a purpose.** (AC-03, Introduction, L53-57)
The paper reviews the argument that dark humor is a shared, backstage, somewhat secret code for people who have the same experiences: it conveys a shared reality inside the group and is not meant for outsiders, who often find it offensive. It cites medical studies in which each side (providers, patients) can hear the other's humor as disrespectful. It cites a crime-scene-investigator study in which users expect outsiders to be offended and sometimes use the humor as a shibboleth to sort who belongs. The paper also states the buffering logic: cohesion built around the code brings people closer, which lets them reappraise the situation and feel "I can make it through this." The authors treat this as a hypothesis their own data then fail to support (E04-6).
*Candidate application:* For a soldier-type character, offending outsiders can be a feature, not a side effect: the joke tests who is in. Written realistically, he may deliberately watch a civilian's face as a screen.
*Cross-use:* Exile or defector communities with their own coded humor could share the same sorting function; the source does not address this.

**[E04-3]. Content and target decide whether dark humor helps, and some events resist any joke.** (AC-03, Introduction, L59; second-hand and non-military)
The review cites a study of child-exploitation investigators (Craun and Bourke) where self-deprecating humor, sexual innuendo and humor at the expense of offenders were unrelated to work-related secondary stress, whereas humor at the expense of victims, and jokes about human behavior and society, were related to higher secondary stress, more alcohol use, less social support and more difficulty and frequency in working with disturbing media. A qualitative study of crime-scene investigators found that some investigations, child exploitation among them, allowed no attempt at laughter at all; the paper calls this a bind in which no amount of joking helped make sense of certain tragedies. A third study found that people reappraised hypothetical hard situations more positively when they used more good-natured humor and less dark humor. The authors conclude that dark humor's benefits may be limited to certain contents or styles. Two secondhand examples of ordinary occupational gallows humor are also reported, described only: a firefighter pun after an unsuccessful resuscitation of a man who died at an all-you-can-eat restaurant (the crew could not stop laughing and said it took the tension away), and rural police officers entering a suicide-call home, where one remarked on funeral-home music and the other answered that the person might be getting in the mood.
*Candidate application:* The target matters more than the topic: a character may joke freely about himself, the institution or the offender and still go silent on one category of victim or event. The category with no jokes is itself characterizing.

**[E04-4]. What was actually measured, and what the "self-defeating" style is in its own terms.** (AC-03, Introduction and Methods-Measures, L41-47, L81-83, L89)
The study had no dark-humor instrument, so it used the **aggressive** and **self-defeating** subscales (16 items) of the Humor Styles Questionnaire. Per Martin et al., aggressive humor lifts the self by putting others down (superiority, control); self-defeating humor tries to win approval and relationships by disparaging oneself, and is described in the scale's own definition as a form of defensive denial, a way of hiding underlying negative feelings. Sample items: for aggressive, a lack of concern about how others take one's jokes; for self-defeating, "I often go overboard in putting myself down when I am making jokes or trying to be funny" and "Letting others laugh at me is my way of keeping my friends and family in good spirits." Well-being was a Cantril ladder (0-10) rating of life now, combined with the same rating for five years ahead because the two were highly correlated, so the "life satisfaction" score includes a forward-looking element. Connectedness to civilian friends, veteran or active-duty friends, and family was rated 1-7. Reliability: self-defeating alpha 0.85, aggressive 0.76.
*Candidate application:* "Self-defeating" here describes a habit of joking at one's own expense to keep others comfortable, not a mood. A character who always deflates himself first may be performing reassurance for the room.

**[E04-5]. Design, sample and the authors' stated cautions.** (AC-03, Methods and Discussion, L73-77, L91-97, L123-129)
Cross-sectional survey, volunteer sample, anonymous. The authors say volunteer samples are nonprobability samples that may carry selection bias, and that the people who answered are those who use social media enough to see the call and chose to finish. A power analysis showed the study could detect only effects of about R-squared 0.10 for the two-predictor models (0.08 for one predictor), so smaller real effects could be missed; they call the findings descriptive and heuristic. They add that 93 people mostly from the Marines and Army cannot represent all service members; that there is no civilian comparison group, so it is unknown whether the humor styles are military-specific or a trait that predated enlistment; that the Humor Styles Questionnaire captures only a propensity, not dark-humor content; and that the questionnaire's construct validity has been questioned (less so for the two subscales used). They also explain their sampling aim by stating that dark humor is "relatively common in experience but rare in frequency of use" (asserted without a citation in the text copy).
*Candidate application:* Treat everything from this study as a weak signal about habitual style among online-active volunteers, not a rule about veterans.

**[E04-6]. No association between these two humor styles and feeling connected to anyone, including no sign of alienating civilians.** (AC-03, Results and Discussion, L99, L115)
The first hypothesis (more dark humor, more connection to veteran or active-duty friends) was **not supported**: overall model R = 0.17, F(2, 90) = 1.17, p = 0.28; aggressive humor beta = 0.24 (p = 0.20, CI -0.12 to 0.60), self-defeating beta = -0.15 (p = 0.21, CI -0.38 to 0.09). The research question (does it reduce connectedness to non-veterans?) also came back with no significant association: family members R = 0.22, F(2, 90) = 2.21, p = 0.12; civilian friends R = 0.18, F(2, 90) = 1.46, p = 0.24. The authors read this two ways: it contradicts the idea that humor of this type promotes cohesion by displaying shared understanding, but it also gives no evidence that it alienates people without military experience. They suggest content and type may leave listeners with different impressions of the speaker's motives, which these measures cannot see.
*Candidate application:* Do not assume that a veteran's rough humor is what isolates him; this study found neither bonding nor isolating signal from the two styles. If isolation is shown, the research supports sourcing it elsewhere (E04-7, E04-36).

**[E04-7]. Connectedness, not humor, tracks well-being, and it counts from veteran and civilian friends alike.** (AC-03, Results and Discussion, L101, L119)
Connectedness to veteran or active-duty friends predicted higher life satisfaction (R = 0.25, R-squared 0.06, F(1, 91) = 5.86, beta = 0.33, p < 0.05, CI 0.06 to 0.60); connectedness to civilian friends did too (R-squared 0.05, beta = 0.23, p < 0.05, CI 0.02 to 0.45); connectedness to family did not reach significance (beta = 0.23, p = 0.06). The authors read it as consistent with the wide literature that the quantity and quality of social relationships benefit everyone, veterans included. They conclude that good connections with friends are a route by which veterans thrive "whether through the use of dark humor or otherwise."
*Candidate application:* A character with civilian friends is not thereby worse off than one with only veteran friends in this data; the source supports no hierarchy between the two kinds of contact.

**[E04-8]. Self-defeating humor tracks lower life satisfaction, and survives controlling for friendships.** (AC-03, Results and Discussion, L107-109, L119-121, L131)
In a model with both styles predicting life satisfaction: R = 0.41, R-squared 0.17, F(2, 90) = 8.93, p < 0.001. **Self-defeating humor** was negatively associated with well-being (beta = -0.60, p < 0.001, CI -0.89 to -0.31), while **aggressive humor was unrelated** (beta = 0.05, p = 0.84). The note on the results table says coefficients are unstandardized, so the figure is on the original 0-10 outcome per scale point. When veteran-friend connectedness, civilian-friend connectedness and self-defeating humor were entered together (R-squared 0.22, F(3, 89) = 8.13), self-defeating humor (beta = -0.55, p < 0.01, CI -0.82 to -0.27) was the **only** significant predictor. The authors' reading: even among connected people, some feel dissatisfied and use self-defeating humor to manage that feeling. They state plainly that cross-sectional data cannot say whether such humor causes lower satisfaction (perhaps by suppressing factors that would raise it) or merely reflects it, and call for longitudinal work. They conclude that self-deprecating dark humor is unlikely to help service members adjust to overall life conditions, even when they feel connected.
*Candidate application:* A prominent habit of self-directed jokes is documented here as a marker of low satisfaction, not of ease. A character can be warmly connected and still use self-mockery as the visible tell that something is flat inside.

### From AC-04 (Mercer, Morgan & Lotto 2024, UK paramedics; not soldiers)

**[E04-9]. Audience reading is the explicit rule of license, and the fear is being overheard.** (AC-04, Results theme 1 and Discussion, L41-47, L103-105)
Asked what dark humor means, participants led with negative associations: anxiety that patients or families who overhear will infer a lack of empathy or indifference. The authors add that the fear extends to complaints, grievances or referral to the professional regulator, and that worry about this, especially when it surfaces in reflection after the fact, can raise stress and cancel the humor's benefit. Positive views were also reported: gratitude from patients and families when lightness appears in a grief-stricken situation, with recurring code words about reducing family grief and taking minds off it. Participants stressed "reading the room" and "knowing your audience." Fragmentary quotes: "Appropriately inappropriate for the correct audience"; "You have to be in the situation once or twice to appreciate the joke"; "Not funny for non-medical professionals." The authors say emergency workers develop intuition about when to use it and when to avoid it.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's license is situational and he knows it: the rule is not "never in front of civilians" but "judge who can hear." The documented cost of misjudging is delayed, after-the-fact worry.
*Cross-use:* The same room-reading rule would apply to any faction with a public-facing duty and a private joking register.

**[E04-10]. Coping function: laugh at the periphery, and use shared category phrases.** (AC-04, Results theme 2, L59-69)
Participants described dark humor as how they get through days of "life-changing things" and of death and dying, and said it builds resilience. One participant clarified the target: not necessarily the event itself but "events around the periphery, to make it bearable." Another described joking with colleagues about patients as a way to ease helplessness about long waits. Several comments called the humor taboo and centered on "experiences that are not normally considered humorous." As documented examples, participants described shared phrases for recurrent incident types: gardeners are said to be "kicking the bucket," and patients who die by suicide are described as "hanging around for us" (a wordplay label that evidently refers to hanging; the source does not explain it, nor say when or how it is said).
*Candidate application:* The humor can stay one step away from the wound, aimed at circumstance, logistics or wording rather than at the dead person; the coded category names are a compact way for a group to refer to something it cannot say plainly.

**[E04-11]. Failure mode: detachment, empathy fatigue, humor that "covers up processing," and creep into home life.** (AC-04, Results theme 3 and Discussion, L71-87, L111-115)
The third theme concerned long-term, chronic use. Participants said excessive use can start to affect emotional processing and spill past work: "I notice you start detaching from personal experiences"; empathy fatigue and burnout; humor that "Makes a barrier . . . but covers up processing"; and "Creeps into family and personal life." They also showed awareness of avoidance. One said, "We know it will affect us in the future, yet we still show up for work"; another used the image of Pandora's box ("it'll get us eventually"); and one said, in a clipped fragment, "We probably have PTSD ... will get us eventually." The authors tie this to a study of ambulance staff after suicide calls, where staff had vivid, lasting memories and appeared not to process the scenes formally, and to dissociation during incidents that may or may not be processed later. They add that PTSD is only one outcome in ambulance staff: depression and anxiety are more prevalent in a worldwide review, even though PTSD rates are very high.
*Candidate application:* The documented limit is not that the jokes stop but that they continue while something else goes unaddressed. A character may be fully fluent and funny about the worst material and know this is a deferral.

**[E04-12]. Concealment from family as compartmentalization, well-meant and costly.** (AC-04, Discussion, L115)
The authors report that participants seldom revealed distressing or emotionally charged parts of their work to family. They connect this to prior work (Regehr et al. 2023) that paramedics compartmentalize to shield loved ones, and add, from other literature, that although well-intentioned, this creates distance from the very relationships in which processing and healing could begin. The study itself asked about humor, so the link to humor-as-barrier is the authors' synthesis, not a measured result.
*Candidate application:* Protecting a partner from the material can be the mechanism of the distance. A character may think he is sparing someone and be experienced by them as sealed off.

**[E04-13]. Camaraderie, self-selection, and a proposed early-warning use.** (AC-04, Results theme 4 and Discussion, L89-99, L117-121)
Participants said colleagues share personalities and senses of humor, that the profession draws a specific kind of person ("A specific characteristic that the profession attracts"), that shared experience lets them lean on each other, and that the crew is "like a family." The authors cite studies that rapport with colleagues protects against PTSD and that camaraderie improves teamwork through trust built by shared experience and joking about it, while noting that long hours with colleagues may cost time with family. They suggest, as a future intervention, that teammates informally monitor each other's dark-humor use as an early warning sign of rising burnout or other mental health conditions. This is the same idea as AC-05's proposal (E04-20), reached independently from a qualitative direction, but here it is a research suggestion, not a tested result.
*Candidate application:* A unit's humor can double as a mutual check: people who know each other's usual register notice a change. Losing that audience after discharge also means losing the baseline against which anyone would notice.

**[E04-14]. A generational gate: humor is learned through exposure, and newcomers may be seen as too easily offended.** (AC-04, Results theme 1 sub-theme and Discussion, L49-57, L107)
A sub-theme the authors call unanticipated: experienced paramedics perceived students who began their education during the COVID-19 pandemic as less emotionally mature, less socially skilled, "naive and easily offended," and as having had less exposure to the crews' own humor because of reduced operational placement time. Participants framed the cohort with the label "COVID generation." The authors link this to literature on student mental health during the pandemic and recommend a comparison between recent graduates and experienced colleagues. It is the participants' perception only, not measured.
*Candidate application:* Veterans may see the humor as something one is enculturated into by exposure, and may judge those who were not exposed as having failed a maturity test. This is a prejudice in the group, not a finding about the newcomers.
*Cross-use:* A culture with a long apprenticeship in a humor register (robots trained on the unit's archive versus newly activated ones) would face the same gate; flagged as speculative.

### From AC-05 (Anders et al. 2025, Swiss police; not soldiers)

**[E04-15]. What "dark" means in this study, and how much distress the sample carried.** (AC-05, Introduction, Methods and Results, L27, L47-61, L85-89)
The study uses the Comic Style Markers: four "light" (benign) styles (Fun, Nonsense, Humor, and Jokes or wit) and four "dark" styles (Sarcasm, Cynicism, Irony, Satire), where the dark ones are defined as mockery and ridicule. It is a measure of how a person tends to joke, not of subject matter such as death or injury. Against screening thresholds (not diagnoses), 1,162 officers (51%) met the cutoff for probable PTSD (IES-R of at least 33), 827 (36%) for burnout, 501 (22%) for anxiety and 283 (12%) for depression (HADS of at least 8). Many more were just below the thresholds. PTSD, anxiety and depression did not differ by job role (all p > 0.05); burnout did, with small effects. The authors say the PTSD rate is higher than many earlier studies and attribute part of it to a broad span of exposure.
*Candidate application:* In occupations with high exposure, "carrying residual distress" may be the typical condition, not the unusual one; a soldier-type character who functions while symptomatic is normal for the population this literature describes.

**[E04-16]. Group comparisons: dark styles were never more common among the officers below the cutoff.** (AC-05, Results, L95-105, L149)
The abstract puts it flatly: "Dark comic styles were never associated with fewer pathological symptoms, only greater." In detail: the general light style ("Humor") was higher among officers below the cutoff for all four conditions; the light styles Jokes and Fun were significant only for depression; the largest light-style effects were for depression (Cohen's d 0.30 to 0.42). Of the dark styles, not one was used significantly more by the below-cutoff group for any condition. Use of all four dark styles was significantly higher among those over the cutoff for burnout, and in lesser proportion for anxiety, depression and PTSD. Cynicism was the only style tied to concern on all four conditions; sarcasm and satire on three; irony only on burnout. For PTSD specifically, the significant styles after correction were the general light style (lower) and cynicism and satire (higher). Cynicism and sarcasm showed the largest effect sizes of any style. (Effect-size tables are not in the text copy.)
*Candidate application:* If the character's habitual register is cynical and sardonic rather than silly or playful, this is the profile that tracks distress. A character who has lost the light, absurd or purely playful register entirely would be a stronger signal than one who is merely dark.

**[E04-17]. Regression: humor styles matter, but stress matters more.** (AC-05, Results, L109-115, L153-157)
With coping strategies, personality, stress and demographics all in the model, PTSD severity (n = 2,248; R-squared 0.36) was most strongly predicted by operational stress (B = 0.23). Next in size (B 0.09 to 0.11) came trauma history and the coping strategies behavioral withdrawal, distraction and denial (the text copy repeats "Operational Stress" in this list, likely a slip for another stress or history variable). Neuroticism, age, religion (B = 0.05) and substance use (B = 0.04) were weaker risks. Satire (B = 0.07) was a significant dark-style risk comparable to the other mid-sized predictors. The protective variables were the light styles Humor (B = -0.07) and Nonsense (B = -0.06), positive reframing (B = -0.05), having children and being male. For burnout (n = 2,233; R-squared 0.51), operational and organizational stress were equal and largest (B = 0.26), followed by neuroticism (0.15) and **cynicism (B = 0.09)**, ahead of substance use (0.08), planning (0.06), denial (0.05) and openness (0.05); protective were conscientiousness (-0.10), extraversion (-0.07), positive reframing (-0.07), the light style Humor (-0.06), and active coping and acceptance. The authors list behavioral withdrawal, distraction and denial as replicating their earlier French-speaking study.
*Candidate application:* No humor style is a main driver; the sources of distress are the job and the person's own coping patterns. Humor is better written as a visible expression of how someone is coping than as the cause of how they are.

**[E04-18]. Clusters: the same trauma exposure, very different symptom loads, depending on the humor profile.** (AC-05, Results and Discussion, L121-123, L159)
Six humor profiles emerged (cluster sizes 308, 442, 580, 210, 470, 281; mapping by the order listed). Cluster 4 (N = 210) used all four light styles, had above-average catastrophic trauma exposure and among the lowest symptom levels (z -0.25 to -0.50). Cluster 6 (N = 281) was the **most trauma-exposed**, used mainly Humor and Jokes, some satire, and had much lower symptoms (z -0.12 to -0.32) than the other exposed clusters. Cluster 3 used all four dark styles plus Humor and Jokes, had less trauma than Cluster 6 and higher scores on all four conditions. Cluster 2 favored Fun and Nonsense, had low exposure and low symptoms. Clusters 1 and 5 used the dark styles most; they had the **highest** symptoms; Cluster 1 was moderately exposed (about as exposed as Cluster 6) and Cluster 5 had low exposure but high symptoms. So: high exposure with mostly light humor went with low symptoms; comparable exposure with an all-dark profile went with the highest symptoms; and a dark profile with little exposure was still symptomatic. The authors treat this as corroboration that dark humor profiles identify likely candidates for intervention, and again warn that the data are cross-sectional.
*Candidate application:* Two characters with the same history can differ in how their humor sounds, and the difference is documented as informative. A character with heavy exposure and a mostly light, joking register is as plausible as one with light exposure and a mocking one.

**[E04-19]. Path model: demands lead to coping, coping to a humor style, humor style to symptoms.** (AC-05, Results and Discussion, L131-143, L163-169)
The best-fitting structural model ran Demands to Coping to Humor to Pathology, which fit better than Demands to Humor to Coping to Pathology, and better than treating them as simply correlated. Reported coefficients: neuroticism raised perceived work demands (B = 0.27); work demands were built far more from perceived occupational stress than from trauma exposure (trauma exposure loaded about 0.28 as much); demands drove maladaptive coping (B = 0.45) and, weakly, adaptive coping (B = 0.07); maladaptive coping predicted dark humor (B = 0.39); adaptive coping predicted benign humor (B = 0.33); dark humor predicted psychopathology (B = 0.36) and benign humor predicted lower psychopathology (B = -0.43), while work demands themselves had a direct path of B = 0.99. Agreeableness (-0.19) and conscientiousness (-0.14) predicted less dark humor; extraversion (0.20) and openness (0.07) predicted more benign humor. R-squared by outcome: burnout 0.75, anxiety-depression 0.87, PTSD 0.35. The "moderator" version (humor changes how stress converts to symptoms) was tested and was not significant; the mediator version was supported, with benign Humor and Jokes as a "suppressive mediator" and cynicism and satire as an "amplifying mediator." The authors read the model as a possible "upward or downward spiraling": people who lean toward adaptive coping turn to benign humor, which further helps; people who lean toward maladaptive coping turn to dark humor, which reinforces symptoms.
*Candidate application:* The model gives a character-building sequence: hard demands, then a coping habit, then a humor register that follows from the habit. A dark register is downstream of a coping style the character already had.

**[E04-20]. Humor as a signal: detect distress before the person is aware.** (AC-05, Abstract and Discussion, L9, L151, L165, L179)
The authors' practical proposal is that observing dark humor may be "a crucially convenient tool to rapidly and discreetly identify an officer carrying residual distress," possibly before the officer recognizes a problem, so that colleagues and leadership can intervene. They also propose encouraging light humor generously and dark humor less often, and note that practitioners of mockery and ridicule are likely "important candidates" for mental health interventions. In the Burnout models, stressors significantly predicted greater use of dark humor but not light humor, which they treat as a sign-of-pathology pattern. They propose conscientious and agreeable mindsets as protective against these styles. All of this is proposed, not tested, and depends on the direction question in E04-22.
*Candidate application:* In a culture that reads the register, a change in a person's humor is read as a symptom. A character could be known to a companion by a shift in how he jokes, before he has said anything.
*Cross-use:* A faction's medic or chaplain role might be built around reading the humor, which the evidence supports as an idea but not as a validated practice.

**[E04-21]. Dark humor is double-edged for the people the officer serves.** (AC-05, Discussion, L181)
The authors raise a second edge: mockery, cynicism, sarcasm, satire, irony and ridicule may harm traumatized victims who seek help, and dark humor at the expense of victims could be "a form of secondary victimization." They call humor style "a double-edged coping strategy" for officers and for the civilians they serve, and recommend studying the interaction between officer humor and service-user outcomes. No victim data were collected in this study.
*Candidate application:* A veteran with a mockery register may be a risk to vulnerable civilians in a way he does not see, a different problem from his own coping. This is the documented counterpart to the room-reading rule in E04-9.

**[E04-22]. Cautions: direction, measurement, sampling, and group norms.** (AC-05, Discussion, L171-177)
The authors name cross-sectional design as the key limit, and offer the counter-reading that dark styles may be manifestations of underlying psychopathology, revealing indicators of distress, not active coping. They acknowledge that role conflict, work meaning, organizational justice and decision latitude were not measured; that the number of invited officers is unknown, so selection bias is likely; that the study uses self-report screening, not clinical diagnosis; that the Big Five scale was unreliable (alpha 0.48); and that the sample is German-speaking Swiss (though it is consistent with a French-speaking cohort they studied earlier). They flag as a future direction the **fit between individual humor style and collective humor culture** (group norms for light versus dark humor, co-regulation, cohesion), which this study did not examine. Text anomalies to note: the concluding paragraph lists sarcasm among "benign" styles in a sentence that also lists it among the dark ones; the methods say officers were "randomly sampled" but the invitation went by email via 24 police chiefs with unknown reach; the text calls the humor instrument's validation study "THQ" (that is the trauma questionnaire's abbreviation, a likely slip for the Comic Style Markers).
*Candidate application:* Individual style is not the same as unit culture. A character's dark register may be the group's norm, in which case it signals less than it would in an outlier.

### From AC-37 (Ward et al. 2021, U.S. Army active-duty soldiers)

**[E04-23]. What "humor" is in this study, and the sample.** (AC-37, Method, L79-113)
The study draws on the Army STARRS All Army Survey (20,901 active-duty soldiers analyzed; 9,616 with complete data on every variable; mean age 28.66; 35% probable lifetime PTSD and 7% in the past 30 days). PTSD symptoms were the nine-item short form of the PCL (past 30 days). Deployment stressors were nine DRRI items (combat experiences, physical harm, exposure to death and destruction, assault, bullying) averaged across deployments. Unit cohesion was five items (including reliance on unit members, being able to talk to first-line leaders, respect for NCOs and officers, and leaders' interest in well-being). Morale was one item. **Humor was one item: how the soldier rates their ability to "Keep your sense of humor in tense situations" on a poor-to-excellent scale.** It is a self-rated coping ability, not a measure of dark or gallows content, nor of aggression, self-defeat, or audience.
*Candidate application:* "Keeps their humor under pressure" is the best-supported marker of resilience in this in-service sample; it is not evidence that dark jokes protect.

**[E04-24]. Results: all three factors overlap and each independently, modestly lowers PTSD symptoms over and above stressors; effects are small.** (AC-37, Results and Discussion, L125-129, L145)
The three resilience factors (unit cohesion, morale, humor) were positively associated with each other and each was negatively associated with PTSD symptoms after accounting for deployment stressors, which had the largest effect. The direct effects were small (the moderation model gave betas of about 0.07 to 0.20 in absolute value). A post hoc analysis found that deployment stressors were negatively linked to all three factors, so the factors mediated stressor effects, but the authors call these indirect effects "miniscule." Interaction tests showed that the three factors also buffered the stressor-to-PTSD link; each interaction was significant and small, and together they reduced the stressor effect to a minimal size. The authors say stronger buffering appeared from a combination of factors. They propose, as future work, resilience programs that build humor, morale and cohesion together and reward soldiers for healthy coping, and they add that morale is often neglected in existing programs.
*Candidate application:* In an active unit, a person's humor, morale and trust in leaders move together, so the loss of one often means the loss of others. A character who has humor but no unit around him lacks two of the three.

**[E04-25]. The authors' own limits: diminishing returns, humor as avoidance, thin measures.** (AC-37, Discussion and Future directions and limitations, L149, L155)
The authors caution that the benefits of these factors may be limited in acutely stressful or prolonged-stress situations and may decrease at higher levels of endorsement. They speculate that humor may serve as a resilience factor until service members begin to use it to avoid dealing adaptively with stressful situations, which could lead to higher, not lower, PTSD symptoms; they say future research should look for an optimal level. They flag a possible curvilinear relationship between cohesion and PTSD at high levels of stress, which an earlier study found and this study could not test. Stated limits: cross-sectional (no causal reading); self-report only, possibly affected by mental health stigma in military personnel; "relatively impoverished" measures (9 of 17 PCL items, single items for humor and morale), which constrain variance and may shrink effects; no rank or occupational specialty data. The sample is active-duty and cannot speak to the years after leaving. The "avoidance" idea is speculation, not a result.
*Candidate application:* The one in-service study states the same failure mode as the qualitative studies, in a hypothesis: humor that helps until it replaces dealing with the problem. For a character years out, that is a plausible description of drift, not a documented trajectory.

### From AC-38 (Besser et al. 2015, Israeli terror-attack survivors and spouses; not soldiers)

**[E04-26]. Who and what: injured civilians and their spouses, a decade on.** (AC-38, Abstract, Methods, [p.1, p.3-5], printed 341, 343-345)
The sample is 105 married couples, one spouse having survived a terror attack since 2001 with a recognized disability of at least 20%. Attacks: 42 suicide bombings or explosives, 40 rocket or mortar attacks, 23 shootings, stabbings or direct assaults. 59 survivors (and 61 spouses) described the injuries as severe and 78 survivors (and 69 spouses) said the attack had a major negative impact on their lives. The mean time since the attack was 10.30 years. Humor was measured by the Humor Styles Questionnaire: **affiliative** (humor to enhance relationships through wit and banter) and **self-enhancing** (keeping a humorous outlook in adversity to regulate emotion) are called **benign**; **aggressive** (sarcasm, teasing, ridicule to raise the self) and **self-defeating** (self-disparagement to enhance relationships at cost to the self) are called **injurious**. Survivors reported far higher symptoms than spouses (PTSD checklist 54.32 vs. 39.92, PHQ-9 depression 13.23 vs. 8.50, GAD-7 anxiety 12.02 vs. 8.47, all p < 0.001), and **spouses** reported **higher** affiliative (4.63 vs. 4.29) and self-enhancing humor (3.62 vs. 3.32; both p < 0.05) than survivors; aggressive and self-defeating were about equal.
*Candidate application:* Roughly a decade after an injury, the injured person scores lower on benign humor and much higher on symptoms than the partner who was not injured; a character in that position may joke less, not more, than those around him.

**[E04-27]. Benign humor tracks fewer symptoms in the person using it.** (AC-38, Results, [p.7], printed 347; Figure 1)
Using an actor-partner model with good fit (chi-square(21) = 27.88, p > 0.15; CFI 0.99; RMSEA 0.06), survivors' **affiliative** humor was negatively related to their own symptoms (beta = -0.33, p = 0.001) and their **self-enhancing** humor even more (beta = -0.42, p < 0.001). Spouses' affiliative humor was negatively related to their own symptoms (beta = -0.33, p = 0.01); spouses' self-enhancing humor was **not** significantly related to their own symptoms (beta = 0.10, p = 0.45). The model explained 53% of the variance in survivors' symptoms and 18% in spouses'. Symptoms in each spouse were positively linked with the other's, which the authors read as a bidirectional trauma effect in the couple.
*Candidate application:* The humor most linked to lower distress in survivors is the relationship-building (affiliative) and perspective-keeping (self-enhancing) kind, not the mocking kind.

**[E04-28]. Partner effects: each person's benign humor tracks the other's symptoms, but not symmetrically.** (AC-38, Results and Discussion, [p.7, p.9-10], printed 347, 349-350)
Survivors' **self-enhancing** humor was linked to lower symptoms in their spouses (beta = -0.28, p = 0.04). Spouses' **affiliative** humor was linked to lower symptoms in survivors (beta = -0.29; the text prints t = -2.04 with p = .003, which cannot both be right, so the p-value is probably .04). Survivors' affiliative humor showed no link to spouses' symptoms (beta = -0.03, p = 0.85). The authors offer explanations they could not test: survivors may withdraw from social activity after the trauma, so their affiliative humor reaches the spouse less; spouses with affiliative humor may maintain connection with a survivor who is focused on difficulties; a survivor's perspective-keeping humor may show resilience that spreads to the spouse. They recommend therapy that encourages the survivor's self-enhancing and the spouse's affiliative humor.
*Candidate application:* In a pairing like the defector and a close companion, the documented pattern is asymmetric: the one who was not wounded carrying warm banter, and the wounded one carrying a wry perspective, are each tied to the other doing better.
*Cross-use:* A companion's relationship role could be written around affiliative humor; this is from a married civilian sample and the source is silent on friends or comrades.

**[E04-29]. Injurious styles: almost no link either way, which differs from AC-03 and AC-05.** (AC-38, Results and Discussion, [p.7, p.9-10], printed 347, 349-350)
Out of 24 correlations between the injurious styles (aggressive and self-defeating) and the symptom measures, only one was significant: spouses' aggressive humor was associated with **lower** depressive symptoms in the survivor. The authors therefore left the injurious styles out of the main model. They stress that injurious styles were **not** associated with more symptoms; they simply had little connection to them, consistent with earlier work (which they cite) that injurious humor styles have not been tied to worse outcomes in trauma survivors but may not be particularly helpful either. They call for distinguishing benign from injurious styles when asking about humor and resilience.
*Candidate application:* This is a counterweight: in this sample self-mockery and teasing were neutral, not corrosive. Combine with AC-03 (self-defeating style linked to lower life satisfaction) and AC-05 (dark styles linked to more pathology) to see that the research does not agree on injurious humor.

**[E04-30]. The authors' cautions.** (AC-38, Discussion, [p.10], printed 350)
No data from before the attack, so no way to say what the survivors' state or humor was previously; the sample comes from one aid organization's records and may not represent all terror survivors; cross-sectional, so the underlying idea (humor styles shape symptoms) cannot be separated from the reverse, which they name directly (distress may shape the humor styles people use). They also say, because average time since the attack is about ten years, that symptoms have likely been part of daily life for a long time.
*Candidate application:* A decade of symptom-carrying is the realistic timescale in this study; the defector at 10-15 years out is in the same range, but this sample is injured civilians, and the source does not speak to veterans.

### From AC-15 (Morris & Hanna 2023, UK veterans after service)

**[E04-31]. Context: what the study is about, so the humor passages are read in place.** (AC-15, Introduction and Findings, [p.2, p.5-6], printed 75, 78-79)
Twenty British veterans were asked about the transition to "civvy street" and the self. The authors report that most leavers are described as faring well by employment, but a significant minority face stress and that anxiety and depression among veterans exceed PTSD in prevalence. In the findings, the military is remembered as a collective with a shared sense of worth, and civilian life is described as individualistic; most participants felt this contrast, and "camaraderie" is the word they used for what they left behind. Participants said they were not prepared for the individualism and felt let down by civilians who did not keep their word.
*Candidate application:* The reintegration problem in these accounts is trust and shared standards; humor is one small symptom of the mismatch, not its center.
*Cross-use:* A defector or exile moving between institutions of different norms would face the same switch from a collective code to an individual one; the source speaks only to UK service leavers.

**[E04-32]. A joke and banter misjudged at a civilian workplace, corrected gently, and not hard to fix.** (AC-15, Findings theme 2, [p.7], printed 80)
Participant 15 described, on first working purely with civilians, occasionally being able to "crack a tasteless joke" or to get "banter wrong." It "didn’t land me in trouble"; a colleague said, in effect, that "we don’t really talk like that in this office." The participant called the adjustment a warming-up period of a couple of months that was not difficult, with "a couple of learning points." The authors read this as feedback that teaches the new norms: the humor internalized in the military does not translate to civvy street and the participant adjusted their attitude to fit.
*Candidate application:* The documented civilian misreception here is mild and fast-correcting: a colleague names the norm, the veteran adjusts. This is a more modest picture than estrangement, and it is a self-report from one participant who describes the process as easy.

**[E04-33]. Speech that is normal to the speaker and quiets the room, and a resettlement course that does not cover it.** (AC-15, Findings theme 2, [p.7], printed 80)
Participant 19 said that, even at university, they had "made rooms go quiet" with things they said because they thought it was normal. They were told it was not normal to talk like that, and said no one had told them before because to them it was normal. They added that the resettlement program prepares you for work but "it doesn't prepare you for civilian life." **The text does not say the speech was humorous**; the authors place it in a discussion of how military norms of communication and attitude no longer fit, with the participant's self-consciousness following from the lack of the recognition they expected.
*Candidate application:* The register can be so habitual that the person does not hear it as a register; the signal is the room's reaction, not their own sense of having said anything unusual.

**[E04-34]. The authors' gloss: feedback to inappropriate banter produces a sense of being undervalued.** (AC-15, Findings theme 2, [p.8], printed 81)
In discussing Participant 1's phrase about extracting from the forces only what was needed to move forward, the authors add their own interpretation: what is useful depends on others' reactions, so "shocking" people or inappropriate banter may bring negative feedback and create a sense of self that is undervalued compared with others. This is the authors' inference and is not stated by the participant. In another place the authors assert that humor "expressed by most participants" in the military does not translate to civilian life, but only Participant 15's account is given in support.
*Candidate application:* Treat as a hypothesis from the researchers: that repeated social corrections about one's speech add to a lowered sense of worth. It is not shown by the interviews quoted.

**[E04-35]. Slang sayings land among veterans, not civilians, and feeling "understood" is the draw.** (AC-15, Findings theme 3, [p.9], printed 82)
Participant 22 explained that people in the services know what everyone else is about and what they have been through, whereas civilians would not understand. If they use slang words and slang sayings with military friends, "they laugh" and answer in kind; with civilians they would probably be frowned on. They reported feeling "more naturally relaxed really and more understood" with military people. The authors read this as mutual recognition that boosts esteem, and report that several participants worked to keep these contacts or build new networks, such as a veteran networking group started by one participant who had earlier not wanted to hear the word veteran. The passage does not say these sayings are dark or about death; it says slang.
*Candidate application:* The pull toward fellow veterans may be less about the content of the jokes than about not having to translate. A character can feel the relief of the shared register and still be wary of having only that.

**[E04-36]. Trust, isolation, and the authors' warning that veteran company can entrench the military self.** (AC-15, Findings and Discussion, [p.6-7, p.10-11], printed 79-80, 83-84)
Participants described civilians as self-interested, saying they were "lied to" more and not kept to their word. A flier in the sample described losing perspective on how "cutthroat, capitalist, money driven" the outside world can be. Most participants, whether or not they judged their transition successful, reported feeling isolated or "cast out into the wilderness," and Participant 30 described a continual rejection that left them feeling their "ego beaten to death." Another participant used the word "indoctrination" for how the military instilled a sense of self. The authors observe that reconnecting with veterans reduced the sense of being misunderstood, but, citing earlier work, caution that the sense of misunderstanding by civilians and the reconnection with veterans may reinforce a military sense of self that acts as a barrier to integration, and that civilian stereotypes do the same. They recommend community spaces that are **not** veteran-specific so that the disconnection between military and civilian society is not maintained.
*Candidate application:* For a veteran 10-15 years out, a plausible shape is people who still orient to the in-group register while having built a civilian life around it; the authors suggest that continued separation does not protect against, and may sustain, the isolation.

**[E04-37]. The authors' stated limits that matter here.** (AC-15, Limitations and Future Directions, [p.11], printed 84)
All participants were white British, as was the interviewer; only 3 of 20 were women; the study did not examine pre-service experiences, which may account for some difficulty; voluntary and medically discharged leavers were both included and their transitions may differ; participants' perceptions of societal attitudes were the data, and civilians were not interviewed (they suggest civilian perspectives as future research). No data on years since leaving or on principled exits appear in the text.
*Candidate application:* The source cannot be used to characterize a person who left on principle, or a woman or minority veteran's humor; it supports only the general outline of misfit and reconnection.

## Pattern roll-up (what this unit documents)

**Functions.** Coping and stress relief, with the target usually the periphery of the event (circumstance, logistics, wording), not the wound itself (AC-04). Cohesion through a shared experiential code (AC-03 review, AC-04). In an in-service Army sample, self-rated ability to keep humor in tense situations is one of three overlapping resilience factors with cohesion and morale, each with a small independent association with lower PTSD symptoms (AC-37). Warm, relationship-building humor and perspective-keeping humor are tied to lower symptoms in the person using it and, asymmetrically, in a spouse (AC-38).

**Targets.** Content and target change effects: humor at the expense of victims and about human behavior in general tracked more secondary stress than self-deprecation, innuendo or humor at offenders' expense, in one non-military study (AC-03 review). Some events resist any joke (AC-03 review). Dark humor aimed at help-seekers may secondarily victimize them (AC-05).

**Forms and mechanics.** Pun-based or euphemistic shared category phrases for recurrent grim incident types (AC-04). Slang sayings that function as recognition among service people (AC-15). Cynicism, sarcasm, satire and irony as the measured "dark" register (AC-05).

**Rules of license.** Read the room and know the audience; you have to have been in the situation to get the joke (AC-04). Benign-violation logic: acceptable in one frame, with psychological distance (AC-03). Complaints and regulator referral are the feared sanction for misjudged audiences (AC-04). The civilian correction in AC-15 was a plainly spoken norm ("we don’t really talk like that in this office").

**Limits and failure modes.** Chronic use that detaches the person from personal experience, covers up processing, produces empathy fatigue and creeps into family life (AC-04). Humor as avoidance, until it replaces dealing with the problem (AC-37, speculation; AC-04 participants' own awareness). Self-defeating humor tracks lower life satisfaction even among connected veterans (AC-03). Cynical, sarcastic, satirical and ironic styles tracked more distress in every comparison, with the largest effects for cynicism and sarcasm (AC-05). A dark humor profile identified symptomatic officers at the same trauma exposure as symptom-light ones (AC-05). Contradicting evidence: injurious styles had almost no link to symptoms in terror-attack survivors (AC-38), and the two HSQ styles tested in veterans were unrelated to connectedness (AC-03).

**Humor as a signal.** A change in humor register read as an early warning of distress, by peers or leaders (AC-05 proposal, AC-04 suggestion); both are proposals, not validated practices, and both depend on an untested causal direction. A misjudged joke is also a signal to the speaker about the new setting's norms (AC-15).

**Time course (in-service vs. after-service).** In service: humor, morale and cohesion move together and weakly buffer stressors (AC-37). After service: loss of the shared code and the unit; mild, fast-correcting misfires with civilian colleagues in one account; relief and recognition among veterans; authors' warning that exclusive veteran company may entrench separation (AC-15). Roughly a decade after trauma, symptoms remain a daily part of life for injured civilians and spouses (AC-38). There is no direct evidence in this unit about how a person's humor itself changes with years out.

**Civilian-facing register.** Paramedics keep dark humor away from patients and fear being overheard (AC-04); they compartmentalize, rarely telling family the distressing parts of the work (AC-04); a veteran's habitual register can silence a room without the speaker noticing (AC-15, Participant 19) or can be a recoverable mistake (AC-15, Participant 15). The study of veterans found no measurable alienation of civilian friends or family from the two injurious styles (AC-03).

**Defector/exile specifics.** Nothing in this unit addresses defection, dissent against the institution, or leaving on principle. AC-15 includes voluntary and medical leavers but does not analyze reasons.

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Constructs differ, so findings cannot be stacked.** AC-03 (aggressive + self-defeating HSQ), AC-05 (cynicism, sarcasm, satire, irony), AC-37 (one self-rating of keeping humor in tension), AC-38 (all four HSQ styles) and AC-04 (paramedics' own sense of "dark humor") are different things. In particular, "dark" in AC-05 means mockery styles, not gallows content, and no source measures the content of jokes about death, wounds or the enemy.
2. **All the quantitative studies are cross-sectional, self-report and non-random or of unknown reach.** All four authors of those studies say direction cannot be established (AC-03: cause or consequence; AC-05: styles may reveal distress; AC-38: distress may shape styles; AC-37: no causal reading).
3. **Sources disagree on injurious humor.** AC-03 found self-defeating humor linked to lower life satisfaction; AC-05 found mockery styles linked to more symptoms; AC-38 found injurious styles almost unrelated to symptoms; AC-37 found coping humor ability modestly protective. Outcomes (life satisfaction vs. PTSD, depression, anxiety, burnout), populations and measures all differ, so this is not a contradiction that can be resolved here.
4. **Only AC-37 and AC-03 involve military populations,** and AC-03 mixes active-duty, inactive and discharged with no time-out data. AC-04, AC-05 and AC-38 are paramedics, police and civilian terror survivors and are cross-use only. AC-15 is UK veterans after service but has almost no humor content.
5. **AC-15 contains very little on humor** (E04-32 to E04-35, E04-34 is the authors' inference). Do not cite it for "veterans' humor changes after service"; it documents a few self-reported misfits and a preference for veteran company.
6. **No source covers principled exit, defection, or an institution one left on moral grounds,** nor women's or minority veterans' humor specifically (AC-03 had 40% women but no sex analysis; AC-15 had 3 women).
7. **Text anomalies and damage.** AC-03: Tables 1-2 absent. AC-05: Tables 1-4 and figures absent; a likely duplicate "Operational Stress" in the PTSD risk list (L109); sarcasm listed as benign in the conclusion (L179); the instrument-validation sentence names "THQ" where the Comic Style Markers is meant (L61); the clustering cluster-to-N mapping is by listed order, only Clusters 4 and 6 are tied to N explicitly. AC-37: Tables 1-2, figures and appendices absent; the introduction says there was no demonstrated link between deployment stress and the resilience factors (L55) while the results report small negative links (L129); the model-comparison AIC/BIC values differ by about 320,000 between models (L129), which I cannot verify and which probably reflects different data handling, not a real difference in fit. AC-38: Table 2 garbled; p-value printed as .003 for t = -2.04 (should likely be .04). AC-15: participant IDs up to 30 for a sample of 20.
8. **Leads from reference titles (not read; flagged as titles only):** Henman 2001, "Humor as a coping mechanism: Lessons from POWs" (cited in AC-38 as evidence that injurious humor was not clearly harmful); Garrick 2006, "The humor of trauma survivors" (AC-37 reference list); Sliter et al. 2014, coping humor buffering in firefighters (AC-37 and AC-03); Craun and Bourke on child-exploitation investigators and Vivona on crime-scene investigators (AC-03); Smith & True 2014, "Warring identities," and Binks & Cambridge 2018 and Cooper et al. 2018 on British veterans' transition and cultural competence (AC-15); Dekel et al. 2005 and Galovski & Lyons 2004 on veterans' wives and families (AC-38).
9. **Original needed in a browser:** the missing tables for AC-03, AC-05 and AC-37, and the clean Table 2 for AC-38; the supplementary quote tables for AC-04.
