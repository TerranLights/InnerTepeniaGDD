## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What the source is.** A 2017 Master of Fine Arts thesis (University of Washington, Bothell campus, Creative Writing & Poetics) that the author calls "an experimental memoir about one Marine officer's experiences in Iraq and Afghanistan" (abstract, L80-82). It is a single author's book-length account, not an anthology: about 45,000 words, 281 page markers in the file, and a closing critical essay, "Afterwards" ([p.273-282], L8790-9361), in which the author explains the structure, sources and intentions. The file is a clean plain-text copy of an open-repository PDF; I found no OCR damage. There is no bibliography or reference list in the file to skip.

**The author's service, as the text establishes it.** Marine Corps officer: Officer Candidate School in summer 1999 and The Basic School afterward (L280, L1066); an artillery officer (L8280); a first deployment to Iraq in 2003 (the invasion by amphibious vehicle, then patrols in Baghdad), the closing essay's "Iraq in 2003" journals (L8920-8922); left active duty "after my first tour in the fall of 2004" for civilian construction work (L8274-8290, L847-849); a second deployment to Afghanistan beginning 5 May 2010 ("the second deployment for all of us, save one," L7759), as a Major in a fire-support liaison company (the text names 3rd ANGLICO in Los Angeles as the check-in unit, L1923-1925, and calls the Afghan role "supervisory," L9262), at Camp Dwyer, Marjeh, remote patrol bases and Camp Leatherneck in Helmand Province, advising and supporting an Afghan Army brigade (1/215 Corps, L2153); age 33 in mid-2010 (L5897). Back home by May 2011, with a marriage ending, a move to Colorado, nightmares and heavy drinking (L169-200, L1761-1801, L5904-5922). The text does not say why the author left in 2004 or how the author returned to a Marine unit, and gives no status for 2017. The author's name appears as given on the title page; the author's own first name appears once, in a handshake (L8414); the closing essay refers to the younger self once as "he (I)" (L8936), but I use "the author" throughout.

**Genre and structure.** Four formats by the author's account (L8962-8969): journal entries (single paragraphs on their own pages, the "connective through-line"), "short stories" (titled prose pieces of one to eight pages; about forty-three titled pieces in all, counting the four rants and the final text-message page, plus the closing essay), quotes from other people (single attributed lines, "-1stLt S"), and four "rants" (profane monologues, the speaker deliberately ambiguous: "Weapons," "Patrol Socks," "Pie Hole," "Suicide Prevention"). The order is deliberately non-linear and cyclical; the thesis "begins and ends with quotes from my time in Iraq" and "with stories about my life after Afghanistan" (L9257-9264). The first 25 pages were designed "to prime the reader" (L9357). Names are first names, ranks and initials; "Pookie," "Rollo," "Fat Jesus," "Captain the Hutt" and "Pancake Man" are the unit's nicknames.

**Evidence base.** One narrator, one service (U.S. Marine Corps), two wars, written roughly 2015-2017 from journals kept in 2003 and 2010 (the 2015 newspaper report cited in "Man-Love Thursdays" and a January 2017 text thread date the writing). The author says outright that dialogue and descriptions in some stories were altered to be more "palatable" to civilian readers, and that the civilians who read drafts still found them "too much" (E25e-81). So the stories are shaped composites; the journal pages are closer to the record but were selected and may have been trimmed.

**What it does NOT contain.** No theory of humor beyond the "Afterwards" statements; no jokes told to the author by a defector or an exile (the one exit from the service is a 2004 resignation with no stated reason); no extended combat narrative by design (the essay says the book is about "the in-between time"); no diagnosis beyond one therapy visit; no woman in the author's unit who speaks at length (three women appear: a linguist, a sexualized graffiti subject, and a telephone operator); Afghans and Iraqis speak only in quoted fragments or in the translated speech of an officer; little on the Iraq combat the author calls "more violently intense"; no songs or verse.

**Format and damage.** The `[p.N]` markers run from [p.1] to [p.282] with no [p.272]; the thesis's own page numbers, as the closing essay cites them, are one higher than the file markers (an entry the essay calls "p.43" is at [p.42]; "p.199" is at [p.198]). I cite file markers plus line numbers ("L"). A few quoted lines are split across line breaks; I quote across them without comment. The "Man-Love Thursdays" chapter cites a 2015 newspaper report by date only.

**Quotation conventions.** Short quotations only, exact (American spelling throughout in the original); square-bracketed material is mine. Graphic, sexual, slur-bearing and self-harm material is summarized (see the register at the end). Period ethnic, national and gender caricature in the author's voice and in the quoted speech is documented, not endorsed. "My gloss" marks any term the text does not explain.

**Retrospective vs. period text.** Journal pages are presented as contemporaneous (2003, 2010); chapter narration is retrospective, written years later and, by the author's account, adjusted. Where a journal entry and a chapter overlap (the sheep-and-child entry, E25e-62 and E25e-82), I rely on the entry for the wording and on the essay for the author's later reading.

## Findings

Citation convention: **[p.N]** is the text file's page marker, **L** is the text-file line number, the quoted title is the chapter heading. Chapters are the titled prose pieces; the untitled one-paragraph pieces are the author's "journal entries" (the abstract's term, L84) and attributed one-liners ("-1stLt S"); both are cited by page. The author refers to Marines by initials, rank-and-initial, or first names, as printed.

### A. Opening: frame, ceremony, news and the first journal entries (L1-L772)

**[E25e-1]. The book's frame: a thesis-memoir that says in its own abstract that dark humor, battlefield relationships and physical discomfort are connected "against a backdrop of unrelenting absurdity," opened by two contradictory one-line pages.** (title/abstract, [p.1-3], L1-98; epigraph pages [p.4], [p.5], L101-104)
The abstract (L80-98) describes "one Marine officer's experiences in Iraq and Afghanistan," woven from journal entries, short stories and private reflections, "non-linear," asking "how one's humanity can get sacrificed overseas and what burdens are carried home," and beginning and ending "in a way that speaks to the cyclical nature of American warfare." The first two pages after the abstract are single sentences: "I want this war to go on forever." and "I want it to end today." Neither is attributed or explained. Format signature of the whole book: untitled journal pages (a line to a short paragraph), single attributed quips, and titled chapters of two to eight pages; the author tells the reader in the abstract that some pieces are "short stories," so not every titled piece is claimed as literal memoir (see E25e-41).
*Candidate application:* A veteran's account can hold wanting-the-war and wanting-out as simultaneous, unreconciled states, set side by side rather than resolved.

**[E25e-2]. Opening pair of journal entries: a mock-congratulatory ceremony beside a flat radio-log casualty report.** ([p.6], L107-113; [p.7], L116-130)
Entry one: at a 1300 Purple Heart ceremony in the tents, the unit watched a corporal receive the medal "for getting shot" and the Marines congratulated the man by patting his back and saying "Smooth move." (L113). Entry two is in the compressed shorthand of a tactical log (counts of medevac and surveillance assets, detainees held for questioning) and reports a Marine from a supported platoon killed by a pressure-plate bomb, dying in the helicopter, and then an ambush where four friendly troops were killed and one wounded; the medevac "got canceled" because the searchers were still gathering all the casualties' remains. (Details of the injuries and remains are summarized, not reproduced; see register.) The mock-congratulation is the unit's rank-free dark humor: a medal is the institution's ceremony, the back-pat plus "smooth move" is the unit's reading of it. The log entry carries no joke at all; the horror sits in the acronym-dense deadpan, with the bureaucratic reason for the canceled medevac as the only editorial detail.
*Candidate application:* The institution's ceremony and the unit's mocking gloss on it can sit in the same moment; the gloss is affection and discount at once. A soldier character may default to logistical shorthand when something is too bad to narrate.

**[E25e-3]. "Osama" (May 2011): news of a milestone reaches the author at home as a text-message chorus, answered with a drink at ten in the morning and a list of hopes.** (chapter "Osama," [p.8-9], L136-200)
The chapter opens as bare dialogue in the voice of the author's Afghanistan friends: whooping texts ("Celebratory shots!"), then the question "Does that mean we won? Are we done over there?" and an angry aside about the country where the target was found. The prose then drops to the author alone: an empty Colorado apartment, CNN muted, a hawk on a wire, a gin and tonic made at the first news, "almost ten AM." The next paragraph lists what the news seemed to promise (that the war's costs were "worth it," that the "nightmares and the stress from coming home meant something," that a "pending divorce" might not be "a total loss," that the Marines might leave the desert, that the uniforms could "be put away"). Humor appears only as the hand-me-down banter in the texts; the author's own register is plain and solitary. It is the first place the book states the post-service condition (alone, drinking, divorcing, nightmares).
*Candidate application:* A group's celebratory shorthand ("Shots!") can reach a veteran who is alone, where it lands as a hope that the old costs add up to something. Good texture for a character who holds on to the unit by phone.

**[E25e-4]. Early journal entries: discomfort catalogs, vermin as roommates, and flat technical facts as the book's background hum.** ([p.10], L203-207; [p.11], L210-220; [p.12], L223-225; [p.19], L514-518; [p.24], L679-711; [p.26], L745)
Short entries run the same register: a newspaper article that the anti-malarial pills (mefloquine) can cause "psychotic episodes, depression, and suicidal thoughts," reported without comment; two rats, one of them (probably "Fat Jesus," the author is not sure) giving "the stink eye"; IEDs "sound like low whumps or thumps"; sand-fly bites or "fleas in my cot," with the dry hope it is not worse ("I wouldn't put it past this place to get even more miserable"); and a long "Things to remember" list (L679-711): cot moved from the tent wall, a cricket, a desert mouse hurdling sandbags, beetle tracks, moths bonking the headlamp, "the smell of cinnamon & pee in the port-a-shitters," a camp stool that makes the author's "taint go to sleep," pipe smoke on a Humvee hood "watching foreign stars," a satellite phone that drops calls, an e-mail network down five days, glue melting off helmet Velcro, an air conditioner that blows cold "for about 6 feet." One line, [p.26], is flat sarcasm: "Whomever is praying for my safety is doing an excellent job." (L745). The list form turns scattered irritations and small beauties into an inventory with no hierarchy; menace and comfort have equal weight.
*Candidate application:* A journal voice that lists the small and the tolerable beside the lethal (a mouse beside a bomb) is a way of keeping attention on what is survivable; humor appears as under-reaction. The one barbed prayer line shows the author's attitude to the home front's sympathy.

**[E25e-5]. "The Most Scared I'd Ever Been": the answer to the civilian question "was it scary?" is a story about wiping in front of other recruits, told so as to make the questioner flinch.** (chapter "The Most Scared I'd Ever Been," [p.13-17], L228-506)
The chapter begins in the civilian-facing register: people ask "Did you kill anyone? Is it like the movies? Was it scary?" and "They think they want to know but they don't really want to hear the true answers because the answers don't line up with their CNN or Hollywood version of war" (L232-238). The author and friends "talked about this phenomenon," and a friend, Tyler, had "the best answer to the being scared question I'd ever heard" (L246-250). The author's version is Officer Candidate School (summer 1999, Quantico, six weeks): the instructors' "unstated objective was to make us die," a list of injuries and dropouts, deliberate no-win tasks, and then the central comic episode, a long, mock-heroic account of the one-roll-per-two-places field latrines, the five-minute break for forty candidates, and the author's sudden realization that a stranger would see them wipe, with a paragraph of anxious questions about technique ("Was there a Marine Corps way to wipe my ass that I had not learned?") and fear of being given an embarrassing nickname. The pay-off is a deadpan list of trained-for catastrophes: the author "had trained to fight through the shock of traumatic amputations and potentially apply tourniquets to my own severed limbs, but I never trained for the scenario where I would have to wipe my ass in front of other fully-grown adult males" (L492-498). Function stated at the end: "whenever someone asks me if I was ever scared while I was in the Marine Corps, I say yes, yes, I was, and I tell them this story and then I laugh at their horrified expressions the entire goddamn time" (L502-506). (Bodily-function detail summarized, not reproduced; see register.) Mechanism: a true answer that is a decoy: the story answers the literal question about fear while refusing the real one, and the teller's enjoyment of the listener's discomfort is the point.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's stock deflection to civilians can be a rehearsed, funny, true-but-beside-the-point story that protects the real material; its working test is whether it makes the questioner stop asking. *Cross-use:* The same decoy-story mechanism suits an exile deflecting a newcomer's questions about the reason they left.

**[E25e-6]. Language fragments: the sardonic epigraph, the euphemism upgrade, and the found list of what Iraqi children said to Marines.** ([p.18], L509-511; [p.23], L672-676; [p.25], L714-742)
(a) An attributed quip from a first lieutenant: "So, what explodes around here? Everything? Oh, good." (L509). (b) A journal entry reporting that while "still in River City" because a Marine in Marjeh had been shot in the chest, the official term for a killed-in-action friendly was changed "from 'Angel' to 'Fallen Hero'" (L672-676): offered with no comment, the euphemism swap is the joke. (c) A "found" list, "Typical Iraqi streetside sayings, 2003" (L714-742): fragments such as "Bush yes!," "No Saddam," "Give me money," "Mister, give me pencil. For baby." Presented bare with no gloss; its humor, if any, is the compression of an occupation into begging-and-flattery phrases, and its darkness is the pencil request. (Ethnic or national caricature is by the author's selection of examples; not endorsed here.)
*Candidate application:* Official vocabulary revisions (the dead renamed) are a documented target of dry noting; a soldier-type's cynicism can be conveyed by simply reporting the new term.

**[E25e-7]. "Lose Yourself": the 2003 crossing of the line of departure is the book's most unironic passage, set to rap music, and it ends in a flat physical detail.** (chapter "Lose Yourself," [p.20-22], L525-669)
A close sensory account (Amtrac hatches, radio handset against the cheek, engines, grit) of the invasion's start: psychological-operations vehicles at the front of the column play a hip-hop track over the speakers; the Marines "collectively started to move to the rhythm," glance at each other and look away, and cross "feeling invincible" (L667-669). Two plain lines mark the register: "No one cheered" (L653) and "I couldn't hear my heartbeat anymore" (L587-588). No jokes; no irony. In the whole book it is the only passage where the author narrates the war as exhilaration, and it is placed before the long run of absurd and bleak pieces.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's earliest memory of combat may be the one memory that is clean and loud and not funny; subsequent humor is partly a response to having once felt this.

**[E25e-8]. "Pancake Man": a dead civilian run over by an entire convoy becomes a unit-wide joke, a "minor celebrity," and a photograph traded for; the chapter gives the author's own participation and does not apologize.** (chapter "Pancake Man," [p.27-28], L752-825)
During the 2003 push north, the column cannot pull aside "due to potential mine threats," so every vehicle passes over the body of an unnamed Iraqi man lying in the road. The narration says the Marines "laughed and cheered and pointed" at what the vehicles had made of him, nicknamed him "Pancake Man," and over the next days he is "a minor celebrity," "an excellent conversation starter for anyone you just met" (L802-804); the next page is an excited dialogue about who took a photo and whether it can be sent. The author includes themself in the "we." The chapter's own first line is "Everyone saw Pancake Man." (L752). Mechanism: the dead enemy-side civilian is reduced to a cartoon figure; the nickname makes a body into a landmark; photography and sharing turn it into currency. The piece neither condemns nor explains; its power is in the placement of the author's own laughter inside the account. (The physical details of the body are summarized, not reproduced; see register.)
*Candidate application:* Documented pattern of dehumanizing humor in the first days of an invasion: a dead stranger turned into a mascot and conversation piece. A veteran character who later cannot stand that memory is consistent with this; one who still laughs is also consistent.

**[E25e-9]. The title's source: a mock-lament over a deflated tiki god.** ([p.29], L828-836)
After an afternoon with one casualty, an inflatable "tiki god" melted by a cigar, the author said "This is why we can't have nice things," and the corporal responded "I knew you were going to say that." (L832-836). The catchphrase is a running in-unit reflex (the corporal predicts it), and it is the only place the title phrase is explained; its comic function is a mock-parental scolding of the unit by an officer who is also a member.
*Candidate application:* A unit's officer may have a signature mock-reproach that the troops can recite; its predictability is a mark of closeness.

### B. Gear, allies, officer school (L838-L1420)

**[E25e-10]. "Good Piece of Gear": a procurement officer's rangefinder demonstration, the old Marine-and-ball-bearings joke, and an order executed to the letter.** (chapter "Good Piece of Gear," [p.30-33], L843-1022)
The author's friend Lewis, an able infantry officer, transferred to the procurement side after both men had been through a tour where a thermal scope was useless for lack of its proprietary battery (L877-902); Lewis's stated reasons are to rest a punished body and to stop "the out of touch generals" and defense contractors from selling the Corps "the most cumbersome and pointless pieces of equipment." Six months into the job, a contractor demonstrates a laser rangefinder in a parking lot and calls it "unbreakable." Lewis first asks the author whether they remember "the old joke about the Marine and the ball bearings" (L936-938), which the author recites: a Marine left alone for a minute in a windowless room with five steel ball bearings will have "lose[n] one, break[n] one, eat[en] one, steal[n] one, and set one on fire" (L942-950). Lewis then calls over a passing corporal, returns his salute, hands him the device with "Break it," and the corporal throws it to the pavement: "Aye, aye, sir!" Lewis dismisses him with another salute and tells the shouting contractor, "Claims like that cost lives and I refuse to let people like you try to kill my Marines," that he will send the boss a report, and, to "You're paying for this!": "No. I'm not." (L1003-1022). Forms: a proverb-joke about the Corps' own destructiveness used as the setup; literal compliance as a weapon (the salute-and-dismiss ritual); targets: contractors and generals ("out of touch"). The stated function is moral: the joke and the stunt guard lives. Also notable: the author "got out to join the First Civ Div" (L847-849) while Lewis stayed in (the first mention of the author's break in service).
*Candidate application:* Marines' own folk joke about institutional chaos can be turned outward into a stunt against outsiders who sell to the institution; loyalty to the troops, not to procurement, is the stated ground. *Cross-use:* Fits a faction that treats suppliers and brass as the same adversary.

**[E25e-11]. Rear-area and allied targets: a cake-and-foam monument, ANA drill that cannot be laughed at, and a first sergeant's redundant insult.** ([p.34], L1025-1033; [p.35], L1036-1042; [p.36], L1045-1053)
(a) A homecoming and birthday dinner (Corps birthday, "235 years old," so November 2010): contract workers built a "life-sized Iwo Jima flag raising statue out of foam, cardboard, and cake icing" with a motto reading "Uncommon Valor With Common Virtue" (the stock phrase is "Uncommon Valor Was a Common Virtue"; the text prints it differently, which may be the author's note of the contractors' garble or a transcription difference; flagged): "We laughed" (L1033). (b) ANA troops marching in formation, with locked legs striking the ground, is "unbelievable but we can't laugh because we have to be professional" (L1036-1037): a clear statement of a rule of license (jokes about allies are suppressed on duty). (c) The embedded-training first sergeant who stays on base and enforces hair and uniform, mocking field teams as "female FET teams," which the author notes "literally translates to 'female Female Engagement Teams teams'," then: "So . . . sick burn, 1stSgt." (L1049-1053). Targets: contractors' garble of sacred iconography; allies' alien drill; the rear-echelon man's garrison pedantry; the author's own mock-praise of a failed insult.
*Candidate application:* Documented rule: laughter at allies is a contained, off-duty vice; the author records the suppression itself as the joke. Rear-area enforcers are the butt of the field team's dry commentary.

**[E25e-12]. "Ticks, Bugs, and Spiders": officer basic school told through acronyms, a hated-instructor set piece, and an author who whispers jokes and dares not grin.** (chapter "Ticks, Bugs, and Spiders," [p.37-41], L1056-1296)
The chapter opens with backronyms for the Basic School (TBS): "Taco Bell Supper. Teachers Being Stupid. Total Bull Shit." (L1060-1062), then the six-month curriculum, ending in "how to hate most of my fellow officers without making it look like I hated them" (L1075-1078). Hardships (heat-exhaustion "Silver Bullet" thermometers, ticks, sleet) are listed lightly. The instructors split into "volunteered" and "wanted nothing to do with TBS" camps; Captain Gant combines both ("he genuinely hated us but he was so damn excited about hating us"). The set piece: during a lecture he kicks over the podium, points at one bored lieutenant with a finger that the author calls "an area fire weapon" (L1157-1158) so that no one knows who is meant, the classmates "leaning away from each other, afraid of being collateral damage"; one lieutenant stands, wrongly; Gant races through the seats "like a selective tornado" (L1220), hauls up the right man, breaks his watchband and smashes the watch on the wall ("Guess what time it is"), then picks up the podium, aligns it to the carpet edge, and "resumed his lecture" (L1296). The author, far enough from the blast, whispers a "gleeful aside" to a neighbor ("Someone's about to get their shit pushed in," L1178-1180) and at the peak "didn't even dare think about grinning" (L1275). Mechanism: military metaphors applied to a human fit ("area fire weapon," "collateral damage," "selective tornado"); the ordinary resumption of the lecture is the punch line.
*Candidate application:* Training-pipeline abuse becomes folklore as a comic set piece that the audience enjoys partly because it was spared; rule of license: laughing aloud is dangerous, so the joke lives in a whisper and in the telling later. Officers share the same absurdity-recognition as the enlisted.

**[E25e-13]. A single-line puppy folder: the tenderness of an accident.** ([p.43], L1304-1316)
A lance corporal is seen looking at "collages and pictures of puppies" while pages load, and says "it calmed him." A sergeant explains that at a patrol base the team's explicit-material folder on their computers was labeled "puppies" as a cover word, so the young Marine "misinterpreted what they meant" when copying the team's talk. The comic mechanism is a euphemism that the newest man took literally. Two things are left unsaid: the author does not say what the folder contained beyond "the porn," and does not mock the Marine's actual calm.
*Candidate application:* An in-group code word that an outsider takes literally yields a gentle kind of joke, and an unintended comfort; neither is corrected by the author. (Sexual reference mild; summarized.)

**[E25e-14]. "Weapons": a cheerful, imperative how-to for maiming a person in hand-to-hand fighting, in the voice of a combatives instructor, with no frame around it.** (chapter "Weapons," [p.44-45], L1319-1386)
A page and a half of instructions delivered as encouraging advice ("Get creative," "Have some fun with it," ending "Your body is an amazing set of weapons limited only by your imagination," L1384-1386) and opened with a crude sexual simile for fighting. The content is graphic hand-to-hand mutilation described in the register of a fitness-class cue sheet. The piece has no narrator reaction, no setting and no attribution; it appears directly after the TBS chapter. The author's closing statement (see E25e-79 to E25e-81) classifies it as the first of four "rants," each "written as if a Marine spoke (or yelled) directly to the reader," here in "a calm, instructional voice," with the speaker ("me ... or someone else I overheard") "left intentionally ambiguous" (L9119-9131). The humor is the clash between the genial instructional tone and the content, and the refusal to comment. (Content summarized only; see register.)
*Candidate application:* The comic register of a drill instructor or combatives cadre over violent content is a recognizable voice; a character who still speaks in it in civilian life will unsettle civilians.

**[E25e-15]. Marginal command criticism: a forward base "a former drill instructor's idea of what a FOB should be," a pointless patrol the commander insisted on, and a disco-ball joke.** ([p.46], L1389-1399; [p.47], L1402-1404; [p.48], L1407-1431)
A sergeant H says of his forward operating base that it is "a former drill instructor's idea of what a FOB should be" (L1390-1392); the senior enlisted men and the master sergeant "fell out" of a long hump that "the CO insisted on," "a useless, senseless patrol that ... put the whole team in danger for no reason." A different entry reports that W urged trying another dining facility because it "might have disco balls and be full of women"; it "didn't ... and wasn't." (L1402-1404). The next page is a bare timetable for a convoy (departures, halts for cargo falling off a vehicle, "110 degrees in our Vic"), no commentary. Targets: a commander's ceremony-driven patrol, a base designed by garrison values; the dry negation "didn't ... and wasn't" as a one-sentence joke.
*Candidate application:* Disaffection with command is stated in a subordinate's compressed gripe, relayed second-hand by an officer who agrees but does not intervene; a timetable can be the joke's setting.

### C. Princess Jasmine; therapy; the language of the returned (L1434-L1830)

**[E25e-16]. "Princess Jasmine": a woman rated on a scarcity scale, an invitation declined, and a closing line that withdraws the joke.** (chapter "Princess Jasmine," [p.49-54], L1434-1747)
Set at Camp Dwyer, where women are rare. The chapter opens in the rating-talk of a men's camp: "Afghanistan changed the rating scales due to rarity and scarcity," so a "snarly three" at home becomes "a soft six or even a solid seven" there, and the Afghan linguist the Marines call "Princess Jasmine" was "a hard 10" and "a for-real nine back home" (L1458-1466). The next pages build her as dignified and watched: a male-interpreter retinue ringing her table; the author, a Major, six months on base, repeatedly brushes off messages from the author's own interpreters that she asks about them, assuming they are "messing with me." On returning late from a fourteen-hour convoy, grimy, the author "opted to give myself a pass" on the chow-hall dress rule as one of the base's senior officers and thereby gave the juniors permission (L1617-1625); the invitation comes in the chow line; the author declines for reasons stated plainly: a wife who moved states without saying so and went silent for three weeks, a belief that "Perception is reality in the Marine Corps," and a paragraph of desire (summarized, not reproduced). The chapter ends: "I still think about her from time to time. I wish I'd asked her her name." (L1743-1747). Mechanism: the book's most standard barracks humor (rating scales) at the front, a long unwinding into restraint and regret at the back; the ending retracts the nickname by admitting the author never learned her name.
*Candidate application:* The nickname and the rating system are the camp's folklore; the veteran's regret is that the folklore replaced a person. A "path not taken" with a named loneliness is useful for a character in exile.

**[E25e-17]. A major's slogan pun on "hearts and minds."** ([p.55], L1750-1754)
An attributed remark by Maj K recasts the counterinsurgency slogan as a shooting pattern ("Two in the heart, one in the mind"). The joke is a double-tap pun that turns the doctrine of winning over a population into the doctrine of killing it. Target: the counterinsurgency mission itself, voiced by a major. (Paraphrased, not reproduced, as it is a joke about killing; see register.)
*Candidate application:* Field-grade cynicism about the stated mission can ride on a pun about weapons drills.

### P. "Afterwards": the author's own poetics statement (L8790-L9361)

*Source note for this section: "Afterwards" ([p.273-282]) is the thesis's closing critical essay. It is the only place in the book where the author states method, intent, and the author's own account of what the humor is for. It also gives page numbers for the thesis's own pagination (see Gaps and cautions).*

**[E25e-79]. "Afterwards" I: the stated aim: a story about war that is not a war story, set in "the in-between time" when friends "joked with each other while we waited," against the hero film.** ([p.273-274], L8794-8889; influences L8871-8875, L9158-9160, L9180-9186)
"I set out to tell a story about war, not to tell a war story" (L8794). The material the author says is missing from the pop-culture war: "the boredom and the filth; the things that made me laugh or the things that made me question why we were there (even though those were frequently the same); the small moments of beauty; the jarring emotional shifts" (L8798-8806). The intent was "something that Hollywood could never film," about "the time where my friends and I sat around and joked with each other while we waited for something to happen" (L8810-8820); the working title was "Interaction." The author pushes back against "the red-blooded hometown hero idea" in three named war films, which "were not about anyone I had ever met, served with, or even heard about" (L8836-8842), and says the stories wanted were the satellite-phone call to an advertiser ("Glutes Magazine") and the jet-ski on a donkey cart, "not about my close calls with death or the rush of a firefight" (L8853-8863). Models named: *Catch-22*, Kent Anderson's *Sympathy for the Devil*, Henry Berry's collection of Korean War Marines' accounts, and two poets' use of white space. The structure, the author says, had to be non-linear because "my memories also did not come to me in a linear fashion... triggered by a smell or a sound" across "almost 20 years" (L8877-8883).
*Candidate application:* The key sentence for any soldier-type character's humor: what made the unit laugh and what made it question the war were "frequently the same" things.

**[E25e-80]. "Afterwards" II: four formats (journal, story, quote, "rant") that "soften the blow" of each other; the quotes as "punchlines to jokes that were never told"; format as comfort.** ([p.275-280], L8962-9249; stated purposes L8901-8914)
The formats: "journal entries, short stories, quotes from other individuals, and what I called rants, which were profanity-laden monologues, with each format informing, playing off, setting up, or softening the blow of the other" (L8962-8969). *Journal entries* are the "connective through-line," "events meant to anchor the reader in reality," a reminder "that the battles, fights, chaos, action, and destruction were all just a backdrop to the included stories"; the one-line entry about an aircraft's guns (a page-ID given as "p.43") is described as the tail end of "a pitched battle in Iraq" (L8977-8992), so some single-paragraph pages come from the 2003 journal. *Stories* were written with stated purposes: two, "Suicide Brief" (the text's other name for the "Suicide Prevention" rant) and "Beard Scalps," "for shock value, to show the reader how the horrible can become the mundane"; two, "Big T" and "Pancake Man," "to show the reader how emotionally or experientially extreme situations were normalized and accepted"; two, "Two Weeks" and "Rollo and Fat Jesus," as "situations that I felt no one would ever believe could be real" (L8901-8914). *Rants* are four, "placed in order of escalating intensity," each "written as if a Marine spoke (or yelled) directly to the reader," the speaker's identity "left intentionally ambiguous," "to elicit shock and discomfort ... as reminder that horrible things could (and do) appear without warning, much like they did in war, and that they can (and do) get so much worse" (L9115-9150). *Quotes* were pulled from "hundreds of quotes and snippets of conversations that I recorded over the years" and "mostly they were intended to be punchlines to jokes that were never told" (L9168-9176). White space is used so the reader can "sit and think about the absurdity of a situation, much like I did from time to time, while staring out across the desert horizon or looking deep into the flames of a trash fire" (L9190-9196), and chains of mundane entries show "a solid week where nothing exciting happened," with "a ladybug landing on my stomach" as the day's one event: "Sometimes war is boring" (L9203-9217). The author notes that the writing unconsciously reverted to the military's letter format, "familiar" and giving "visual comfort while I wrote about difficult things," a "bridge joining my Marine Corps life and my civilian life. Even now, one still impacts the other" (L9221-9249).
*Candidate application:* Documents a veteran-writer's own account of how soldiers' humor works on the page: the quip is a punchline whose setup was never told, and the form (format, spacing) is itself a coping habit carried from service.

**[E25e-81]. "Afterwards" III: the civilian audience and the "palatable" compromise: the true versions were darker; the readers still found the softened ones "too much."** ([p.277-279], L9066-9111)
The author "struggled between wanting to tell the stories truthfully or to tell them in a manner that would keep the reader from tossing away my thesis in disgust," reasoning that honesty might lose readers but glossing "the ugly parts, which was almost all of it," would betray "myself" and "the people who suffered alongside me." The compromise: "I retained the shocking and unbelievable subject matter but I altered some of the dialogue and descriptions." Four chapters are named as understated: "Man-Love Thursdays" and "WAG Bags" ("much darker and the attendant conversations more vulgar than what made it onto the page"), "The Most Scared I'd Ever Been" and "Self Love in the Porta-Shitter" ("do not fully do justice to the filth"), and "Final Exam" (does not "truly capture the raw violence of the execution video"). "Yet several people who read the final drafts ... (fellow cohort members, family, friends - i.e. a cross-section of my intended civilian readership demographic) felt like the descriptions were still too much" (L9095-9100). The rants were written "to further address this discrepancy between the flat reality of the journal entries and the more polished events in the stories" and "to recapture what I felt I sacrificed in the stories" (L9107-9116).
*Candidate application:* The most direct evidence in the file for a "funny version vs. sad version" and a "sanitized version": the author gives the civilian audience a toned-down account and measures the audience's tolerance in real readers. A veteran character who says "it was worse than that" has a documented analogue.

**[E25e-82]. "Afterwards" IV: the author's account of the journal pages: the shift "from tragedy to normalcy," "permission to react," and a stated wish that the reader feel "the same weariness and black humor."** ([p.275-277], L8920-8958; L8993-9052; L9343-9361)
The author rereading the 2003 and 2010 journals found their common tone to be "a sense of constant anticipation," more striking than the events: "the experience of seeing myself not knowing what was about to happen" (L8926-8940). The goal for the reader: "off-balance and wary, to feel the same weariness and black humor that I felt ... smell the smoke, taste the grit, and, at the end of it all, feel wrung out and exhausted" (L8950-8958). The sheep-and-child entry (E25e-62) is quoted in full to show "how fast the shift from tragedy to normalcy could occur" and "how I gave the reader permission to react to ... different deaths": the author's reaction to the sheep death "was one of incredulous hilarity. His death made me laugh until my stomach hurt," the girl's case went unresolved, and the entry's close was "how good it was to hear from my family again" (L9019-9044). The journals "go from dark humor to horrible catastrophe; from the tragic to the mundane; from intense to ridiculous" (L9044-9050). The closing paragraph lists the reading experience the book is built to produce: "Things come out of nowhere ... Dark scenarios turn light and funny," in service of "expect the unexpected" ("logically flawed, but still an ubiquitous Marine-ism") and "adapt and overcome," with the first 25 pages meant "to prime the reader" (L9343-9361).
*Candidate application:* The author states, as craft, that deaths in the same hour can draw laughter, nothing, and relief about mail, and that the permission to laugh is something the writer grants.

**[E25e-83]. "Afterwards" V: the institution, politics, cycles and the title: war as a racket, violence turned inward, no closure.** ([p.280-282], L9253-9339)
The thesis "begins and ends with quotes from my time in Iraq, which was more violently intense than my more supervisory role in Afghanistan," and with stories about life after Afghanistan; the cycle is the structure: "My wars ended but they weren't over. My deployments finished but there was never closure" (L9257-9274). On politics: "I felt like I lived through the physical embodiment of Smedley Butler's War is a Racket ... War is, and has been for some time now, a for-profit endeavor. I could see it manifested in every bloated government contract and redundant logistical activity aboard the camps and bases I visited"; the author "tried not to include journal entries that spoke directly to my political beliefs" so the reader could "reach their own conclusions" (L9278-9298). On violence: "Most of the violence that takes place in my thesis is imaginary and directed towards other Americans rather than towards the people who we are told are our enemies. The violence in Justifiable Fratricide never happened, despite how much I wanted to do it, while the potential for violence was established immediately in Big T, but then it never materialized because the people involved were tired of fighting" (L9302-9316). On the title: the working title "Interaction" was "very boring"; from "almost fifty possible titles" the author chose the present one because it "perfectly summed up the relentless absurdity and the fiscal and human waste of modern warfare. Plus, it made me laugh." (L9323-9339).
*Candidate application:* The one place where the author states an institutional critique in a sentence (profit and waste), and a plain statement that violence in the book is mostly inward-facing fantasy. A soldier-type character's humor may carry a political view that he declines to state.

### Q. Arc of the humor across the book, civilians, and the institution (synthesis; cites the findings above)

**[E25e-84]. The arc, by phase: the book is not ordered by time (the author says so, L8893-8899, L9253-9274), but the phases can be read off the dated and placed pieces.** (synthesis; chapter cites as given)
*Training (OCS summer 1999; TBS about six months):* comic set pieces told in retrospect: the latrine story that becomes the answer to civilians (E25e-5), the hated instructor and the lecture that resumed (E25e-12), the acronym backronyms. The one training piece with no humor is the institution's own desensitization exam, in which the instructors cheer (E25e-39); an earlier Corps ritual, the "Silver Bullet," appears as a passing detail. *Iraq 2003 (a junior artillery officer; the author calls it "more violently intense"):* the crossing of the line of departure is told without irony (E25e-7); the dead civilian turned into a mascot and photograph and "we laughed and cheered" (E25e-8); the attempt to "block out images from Iraq" and the kept comic image of the jet-ski (E25e-58). *Between wars (left active duty fall 2004):* the flinch at a blown tire and the first recognition scene with another veteran, "RPGs and clueless civilians" (E25e-74). *Afghanistan (second deployment, from 5 May 2010; a field-grade, "more supervisory" role):* the unit starts "ready to have some fun" (E25e-70); early-to-mid deployment is the high point of communal invention: the gross-out contest and its penalty court (E25e-35), the mock scientific survey (E25e-37), the pets (E25e-31), the clown prank (E25e-49), the sat-phone call (E25e-61), the movie-casting game (E25e-72); the same stretch carries the first plain verdicts ("This war is such a pointless waste," E25e-28; "just depressed & angry and tired of wasting my life here," E25e-34), the officer-hatred chapter and its sleepwalking near-miss (E25e-43 to E25e-45), and the whispered "I hate it here" that becomes a greeting (E25e-63); the late deployment shows numbing (laughter at animal-triggered deaths, a dying enemy's crawl, E25e-62, E25e-73) and hostility toward the allies (the chambered round, the formation of 200, E25e-55, E25e-73), and the last journal line is "I can't wait to get home and forget about all of this" (E25e-78). *After:* the news of a milestone alone at ten in the morning with a drink (E25e-3), the laughing-until-crying therapy hour (E25e-18), the cat shelter and the one-word page (E25e-52), the advice that no one will care (E25e-25), the night walk that feels like a patrol (E25e-77), the continuing text-thread jokes with a friend in 2017 (E25e-78).
*Candidate application:* A usable shape: eager and comic at the outset, a communal peak in the middle, fatigue and contempt after, numbing at the end of the rotation, then solitude and the small group of veterans afterward.

**[E25e-85]. What happens to the humor: it stays, it changes audience, it curdles in places, and it is never consciously abandoned.** (synthesis)
*Stays:* the author still tells the decoy story "and then I laugh at their horrified expressions the entire goddamn time" (E25e-5, L502-506); the 2017 text thread continues in the same register (E25e-78); the closing statement credits the title with making the author laugh (E25e-83); the chosen subjects are the funny ones (E25e-79). *Changes:* in service the humor is communal, rank-crossing and performed to an audience (the urinal banter, the survey, the call, the clown); after service it is either private (a journal, a Naval-letter-format habit), restricted to veterans (a deputy's advice, the toast with another Marine, the text thread), or a managed product for civilians (the decoy story, the "palatable" thesis, E25e-81). The book shows no civilian laughing with the author: the colleagues who see the flinch are "incredulous" and then "went back to talking about their trucks or us" (E25e-74), the decoy story's hoped-for reaction is horror (E25e-5), and the thesis's civilian readers judged the softened versions "too much" (E25e-81). *Curdles:* laughing at the sheep-triggered death "until my stomach hurt," at the dying enemy's crawl, and at the "Pancake Man" are the cleanest examples (E25e-8, E25e-62, E25e-73); a second sign is the translation of exasperation into murder fantasies (a colleague, an allied general's staff, a roomful of soldiers, E25e-43 to E25e-45, E25e-55, E25e-73); a third is a violent-dream appetite ("I have not felt like myself without them," E25e-69). *Fails:* the laugh-until-crying in therapy (E25e-18), the sat-phone prank that turns into homesickness (E25e-61), the post-service patrol walk where nothing is funny (E25e-77), and a Marine's own explanation that he laughs "because I don't want to cry" (E25e-67). *Not abandoned:* no passage renounces humor; the author instead states a willingness to reduce its rawness for readers (E25e-81) and a reluctance to say what it means: the "punchlines to jokes that were never told" (E25e-80).
*Candidate application:* For a former soldier ten to fifteen years out, the documented continuity is high: the humor persists but changes audience and register, and its failure points are specific (moments of stillness, being alone, being asked directly).

**[E25e-86]. Telling civilians: the book's rules, collected.** (synthesis; E25e-5, E25e-25, E25e-36, E25e-48, E25e-74, E25e-79, E25e-81, E25e-18, E25e-3)

Was it scary?") assumes a screen version; the author's answer is a rehearsed funny story that is true and beside the point, whose point is to watch the listener's face (L232-250, L502-506). (2) A friend who is a police deputy and combat veteran tells young Marines, who have just fought college students who insulted them about the war, "no one will ever care about you or what you did" and to "avoid talking with anyone who isn't a Marine"; the author calls this "a great kindness" and says "He and I had to learn that lesson the hard way" (L2540-2568). (3) A veteran's toast to "RPGs and clueless civilians" with a stranger who shares the flinch (L8449-8463). (4) The re-entry catalog in therapy (colors too bright, noises too loud, "My family kept telling me I wasn't over there anymore," L1779-1797), met by a clinician who says "Mmhmm." (5) The institution's own advice about the home, from a chaplain, is borrowed in the unit's register (E25e-36). (6) The "support the troops" gestures a unit sorts into a junk pile (E25e-48) and a child's note with an accidental vulgarity (E25e-19). (8) The author's attitude to the news and entertainment version is explicit: "Hollywood" and CNN are the foil in the first chapter and in the closing statement (L236-240, L8810-8832), yet the team plays the Hollywood-casting game itself (E25e-72).

**[E25e-87]. The narrator's relation to the institution: loyalty to the troops, contempt for the rear and for brass, a hidden mercy, an unprincipled-looking exit and no regret stated.** (synthesis)
*Loyalty:* to the team and the Marines under him (the procurement officer's stunt "to prevent the Marine Corps from being screwed over," E25e-10; care for a returning friend, E25e-47; protecting the bomb-planter secret and a worn-out sentry, E25e-72; the groomsman line, E25e-21). *Contempt:* for the rear-area enforcers, the sergeant major and the base commander's rules (E25e-63, E25e-65), for contractors (E25e-10, E25e-48), for a commander's Facebook posts and pointless patrols (E25e-15, E25e-54, E25e-72), for a bad officer who keeps being promoted (E25e-43, E25e-45), for the software and paperwork (E25e-38, E25e-64), and for the training pipeline's cruelty (E25e-12, E25e-39). *Dissent:* only small, covert and shared: whispered commentary in a formal briefing, a borrowed film line as countersign and greeting, an unreported decision (E25e-63, E25e-72); the one conscientious-objection item is a laugh line about someone else (E25e-50); the only structured statement of an institutional critique is the closing essay's "for-profit endeavor" line (E25e-83). *Orders to ignore:* the unit was told not to interfere with partner-force abuse (E25e-33). *Exit:* the author "got out" after the first tour in fall 2004 (L8274; the text says only that the author wanted "a change," L8284, and a job); the way back to a Los Angeles-based unit and a second deployment is not explained; there is no statement that the exit was on principle. *After:* the text gives no regret about leaving or staying beyond "I wish I'd asked her her name" (a different regret) and the wish in 2011 that "I could put my uniforms away and finally be done with it all" (L198-200), the last journal line "I can't wait to get home and forget about all of this" (L8787), and the closing "one still impacts the other" (L9248-9249).
*Candidate application:* This narrator is a model of an insider's loyalty-and-contempt split, not of a defector: the document is useful for the in-service half of the project's character (what he hated, how he said so quietly) and offers almost nothing on the exit itself.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions.** (1) *Coping and the explicit statement of it:* "I'm laughing at something I probably shouldn't simply because I don't want to cry" (a corporal, E25e-67); the unit's "Beats getting exploded, sir" for a ridiculous project (E25e-37); the author's "the things that made me laugh or the things that made me question why we were there ... were frequently the same" (E25e-79). (2) *Cohesion and recognition:* the first meeting with a sergeant who shares a sense of humor and "can laugh with you through the boobery and inhumanity" (E25e-21); a whispered "I hate it here" that becomes "our aloha" (E25e-63); the Hollywood-casting game as "more real" than a briefing (E25e-72); nicknames given by the troops (Pookie, Fat Jesus, the Hutt). (3) *Boundary against civilians and outsiders:* the decoy story (E25e-5), "clueless civilians" (E25e-74), contempt for contractors and rear-area staff. (4) *Resistance and rank negotiation:* the salute-and-dismiss stunt (E25e-10); the sergeant charging the captain for a lie and a soft drink (E25e-21); enlisted sabotage of a bad officer (E25e-45); the officer who "didn't officially condone" the rats (E25e-31). (5) *Morale:* the sat-phone call as "something hilarious to boost morale" (E25e-61); costumes at a hospital (E25e-76). (6) *Control and calibration of fear:* the pants scale (E25e-59); the log-style deadpan (E25e-2, E25e-20); mock reports (E25e-38, E25e-67). (7) *Permission:* the writer "gave the reader permission to react" (E25e-82).

**Targets.** Self (the clown, the failed lookout, the wiping panic, the 33rd birthday); the unit (rats, the survey, the contest); brass and staff (the sergeant major, the base commander, the CO's patrols, the Facebook posts); the institution's systems (software, the speeding rule, the care-package pipeline, the redundant resubmissions, the evaluation program); contractors and "support" gestures; the allied army (drill, drugs, volleyball, the map, the RPG repair; much of it ethnic or national generalization, recorded and not endorsed); the enemy (a bomb-planter's pretended zeal, a body, a crawl); animals (as survivors and as casualties); women (a rating scale, graffiti); the dead and the wounded (named friends only in plain voice, anonymous civilians and enemies as laugh lines); civilians; the weather and the base itself ("Place sucks, bro").

**Forms and mechanics.** Deadpan log language and acronym density as horror's carrier (E25e-2); order-of-mention cruelty (goats before the local, E25e-26); juxtaposition inside one entry (a postponed memorial beside a chair mishap, E25e-20); lists of "things to remember" as an inventory without hierarchy (E25e-4, E25e-60); mock-formal procedure over the intimate (socks, the survey, the penalty court, porta-potty etiquette, E25e-27, E25e-37, E25e-35, E25e-66); backronyms and wordplay games (TBS, "Lima Charlie," E25e-12, E25e-60); military metaphor applied to people and parties (the "area fire weapon," "going high and to the right," E25e-12, E25e-69); reported retorts and one-line epigraphs attributed by rank and initial (E25e-6, E25e-34, E25e-52); the long rant as a performed genre (E25e-80); the twist or hoax on the reader (Jenkins, E25e-41); the repeated-refrain slapstick ("Nothing happened," E25e-23); nicknames from film and comedy (the Hutt, Pookie, Fat Jesus, Pancake Man, Rollo); a pop-culture line used as a countersign (E25e-63); the hyperbolic murder-sentence as the last word of exasperation (E25e-55, E25e-65).

**Rules of license.** A man who has not deployed may not laugh at a death-themed toast (E25e-41). Laughing at the allies is suppressed when on duty (E25e-11). The senior man buys his place in a unit by paying for a lie told in his favor (E25e-21). An officer sets a floor (no degrading T-shirt slogans, a call that must not become phone sex, a refusal to let the team "develop a gross new perversion"; E25e-37, E25e-61) and otherwise supplies the method for a junior's absurd project. The enlisted may ridicule a bad officer and the officer may pretend not to notice (E25e-43). Insiders may joke about a thing the institution forbids them to act on (the abuse, E25e-33). A civilian is owed a story, not the answer (E25e-5).

**Limits and failure modes.** The joke registers its own limits ("We drew the line at taint scalping," E25e-29); the prank is designed around the target's real danger (disarm first, E25e-49); humor turns to contempt (the body-shaming, E25e-43), to numbness (the sheep, the crawl, the mascot, E25e-8, E25e-62, E25e-73) or to murder-in-the-head (E25e-44, E25e-55); it fails at the point of being asked directly (therapy, E25e-18) or of being alone (the walk, E25e-77); it can be a symptom ("a common joke" about abuse that the unit was ordered not to stop, E25e-33); it also masks fear and grief, and the places where either is named are few (the "nightmare" said by a prank's target, E25e-49; the aching empty hands, E25e-77; the laugh "because I don't want to cry," E25e-67).

**Where the writing turns plain** (a recurring move, usually in the last sentence): "Later, after the war, he asked me to be a groomsman at his wedding" (E25e-21); "I wish I'd asked her her name" (E25e-16); "Dinner ended earlier than anticipated due to an IED-related mass casualty event" (E25e-51); "His jaw clicked as he ate in silence" (E25e-65); "Everyone else quietly dispersed" (E25e-61); "He said, 'Mmhmm'" (E25e-18); "I sat in the truck. It was a long time before I could go home" (E25e-52); "He got promoted, last I heard" (E25e-45); "I kept walking. There was nothing else I could do" (E25e-77); "Hurt today ~ just depressed & angry" (E25e-34); "I did this every time" (E25e-75).

**Time course.** In training: comic after the fact. In the 2003 invasion: exhilaration then cheering at an anonymous body. Between wars: a flinch and a toast. In 2010: ready for fun, a communal peak, fatigue and contempt, numbing at the end. After: loneliness and drink, the veteran-only channel, humor kept in private and in texts, a thesis written to say what the in-between time was like; the war "ended" but "weren't over" (E25e-84, E25e-85).

**Civilian-facing register.** The decoy story, the palatable thesis, the advice to avoid non-veterans, the family's "you're not over there," "clueless civilians," the support-gesture junk pile (E25e-86).

**Defector/exile/dissent specifics.** Covert dissent inside a formal briefing, a pop-culture countersign, an unreported mercy, a refusal to do a killing as a civilian volunteer (the cat shelter, E25e-52), no principled exit stated, no after-exit jokes about the institution except "talk about how stupid everyone is" with another veteran (E25e-74) and the 2017 text thread about the ongoing war (E25e-78). Nothing here documents humor without a unit beyond the veteran pair or the text group.

**Cross-cultural.** Only one army (U.S. Marine Corps) and two wars; the allied force (Afghan National Army) is described entirely from outside, in a stereotyping voice that the book does not examine.

**Craft observations (the author's own).** Non-linear structure with white space; four formats that "soften the blow" of one another; quotes as "punchlines to jokes that were never told"; the flat journal entry beside the polished story; a deliberate priming of "expect the unexpected"; three stories chosen because "no one would ever believe" them; the compromise between truth and a civilian's tolerance (E25e-80 to E25e-82).

## Gaps and cautions

- **One narrator, one service, two wars.** A U.S. Marine artillery officer (later fire-support liaison role) who is explicitly not the "red-blooded hometown hero" the author says films present; the evidence is not a sample of Marines. Enlisted voices appear only as quoted lines and as characters the author writes.
- **Genre is mixed and the author says so.** The abstract and the closing statement describe journal entries, "short stories," quotes and "rants"; the author states that dialogue and descriptions were altered and that some chapters were softened; four rants have intentionally ambiguous speakers. Treat the stories as composites of memory and craft, not as testimony; the journal pages are closer to the record but were chosen and perhaps edited.
- **Page markers.** The text file's `[p.N]` markers are one lower than the thesis's printed page numbers cited in the closing statement (the closing essay calls an entry "p.43" that sits at file marker [p.42], and "p.199" for an entry at [p.198]); I cite the file markers and line numbers. There is no [p.272] marker (the file runs from [p.271] to [p.273]); I read the intervening lines and found nothing missing.
- **Dates.** Few pieces are dated; dated journal runs (25 May to 3 December in the officer chapter; a 6 January 2017 text thread; "5 May" for the flight; a 33rd birthday; the Corps' 235th birthday, so November 2010) support a 2010 deployment, but the year is inferred (the text gives "2003" and "2010" only in the closing essay, L8922). The book's wife-and-divorce material is non-linear and the two accounts of the return (an empty house in "Therapy"; a couch in "The Cat Shelter") are not reconciled.
- **Not in the text:** why the author left in 2004 and how the author returned; the author's rank on leaving; any statement of diagnosis; any account of the Iraq combat the closing essay calls the more intense; the "kid in Iraq" the author recalls in a late entry ([p.260], L8481); the author's sleepwalking-with-a-pistol episode is reported once with no sequel; no first-person voice of any woman in the unit; no account of how the unit's jokes were received by anyone outside it.
- **Possible transcription quirks.** A motto on a cake statue is printed in a form that differs from the common wording (E25e-11); a technical "BA5590" message is glossed from outside knowledge (E25e-67); the "Suicide Prevention" rant is called "Suicide Brief" once in the essay; one entry date is repeated out of order in the officer chapter ("14 Oct" appears twice, L5053 and L5083).
- **Content cautions.** Slurs, sexual and graphic material were summarized (see register). Stereotyping of Afghans, Iraqis, Asian travelers and women appears in the author's own voice and in the speech of others and is documented, not endorsed. The murder-fantasy material and the suicide rant are reported as the author's satire and imagined acts (the author says the violence "never happened"); do not mine them for method.
- **Original to check in a browser:** the thesis's own pagination, the verbatim wording of the four rants (the extraction intentionally omits them), and the exact wording of the "Man-Love Thursdays" chapter against its note about the NYT article.
- **Size.** The findings run longer than the 10-18% guideline because nearly every piece in a book of short pieces is a separate humor-bearing unit; no part of the source was skipped.
