## Source-shape notes (read first)

**Citation convention.** None of these web-essay text copies has page markers. I cite **L = line number in the local .txt** (each file opens with a SOURCE/AUTHOR/TITLE/PUBLICATION header block of five or six lines, then the essay, then an author bio; photo captions and "More by ..." lines are interleaved). Findings are in source order: The War Horse (MV-B-042 to 054), then Havok Journal (MV-B-019 to 023), then Task & Purpose (MV-B-035 to 041). "Humor" is spelled American throughout; file paths and URLs are left as they are.

**The big caution for the whole unit: selection.** These 25 files were found by domain-filtered searches on three outlets after un-filtered searches mostly failed (log searches 55, 67, 69-74, 84-85, 102-105); the log itself says the outlets may be over-represented. Everything here was **chosen by an editor** (The War Horse is an editorially staffed nonprofit newsroom that edits, fact-checks and copy-edits each "reflection"; Havok Journal and Task & Purpose publish contributors and staff, and some Task & Purpose items are as-told-to). Every essay is therefore a **veteran's published, shaped self-presentation**, written for civilian and veteran readers, usually decades after the events (Chetlain 1988, Lyons 1968-69, Meagher 1968, Van 1980; Jones's events in the 1980s written in 2026), and not a record of what was said in the room. Humor *claims* ("gallows humor is essential") and humor *scenes* (a recounted prank) are different kinds of evidence; I label which is which. **No essay contains a verbatim joke text beyond a few one-line remarks; the essays mostly describe humor, they rarely transcribe it.**

### From The War Horse (MV-B-042 to MV-B-054)

#### MV-B-042 (Chetlain, "Guard the Forks")

**[E25c-1]. A prank war as boredom engine: the brief is a prank that affects everyone and is 100% safe, and the nuclear mission is ambient.** (MV-B-042, L9-L21, L81)
Two sonar technicians, seven weeks into a Trident patrol, have run through the standard repertoire. Chetlain lists gags such as the "mail buoy" and "relative bearing grease" without explaining them, and notes an earlier coup of swapping the executive officer's stateroom door for the door of the officers' head. Their new brief is a prank that touches the whole crew and stays entirely safe. The scene opens on a deadpan inversion: told they need "something big," Chetlain points to 24 missiles aimed at the Soviet Union and asks "Isn't that big enough?"; the partner's answer is that this is not what he means. Real names could not be used in the sonar space, so the two address each other by nicknames (Chetlain's is "Eeyore"). Chetlain's own frame is that patrol is "long episodes of boredom interspersed with random moments of terror," that jokes "filled that space," and that they were "a rite of passage." The prank is the crew's, not the threat's, to have stakes.
*Candidate application:* For a sealed crew with a world-ending job, the humor aimed at the daily routine and the officers can be a deliberate refusal to give the mission any more air than it already has.
*Cross-use:* Any isolated community (polar winter-over crew, undersea or orbital habitat) can run a whole humor economy on shared boredom; the engine here is monotony, not fear.

**[E25c-2]. The prank is run through the target institution's own channels, so the countermeasures feed it; closure is a negotiated amnesty and a face-saving fiction.** (MV-B-042, L27-L79)
The first scheme (a toilet-paper shortage seeded by rumor) failed on logistics: the stock was in the chiefs' quarters and there was nowhere to hide hundreds of rolls, so the pair spent the rest of the patrol "reintegrating" it. The second (stealing every fork) worked: each sonar watchstander pocketed a fork after the pre-watch meal, forks were kept in a toolbox with a false bottom, over 400 were held after a month, and night reconnaissance of the mess decks added more. Each institutional reaction escalated the joke: a cook told the mess attendants to "guard the forks" and the crew began hoarding personal forks; the supply officer switched to plastic; the executive officer sent every officer through the whole boat with flashlights for a watch and missed the false bottom. Chetlain then wrote a fake "plan of the day" notice in bureaucratic voice about "renegade forks" and a team to apprehend them (the team's acronym is a flatulence pun). It ended when the senior enlisted man's plastic fork broke at pizza night and he demanded them back; the sonar boss offered "amnesty"; the forks were returned in a paper bag with an anonymous call to the mess decks that they had been "miraculously recovered." Neither prankster claimed it ("everyone knew"). About 50 days; Chetlain stresses that "Nobody was hurt."
*Candidate application:* A prankster's craft here is rumor engineering, patience, and writing in the institution's own register; the retreat is ritualized and no one is punished. A character who is good at this is reading a hierarchy, not just being silly.

#### MV-B-043 (Chetlain, "Splash")

**[E25c-3]. A nickname after a near-death: gallows humor arrives once everyone has survived, and sits beside gratitude.** (MV-B-043, L13-L60)
Chetlain narrates, in plain, procedural prose, a rough-weather helicopter transfer of an injured shipmate: a swell flooded the hatch, a crewman was washed overboard in 34-degree water for about two and a half minutes before a helicopter pilot pulled him out, and a hatch pinned another man's leg. Humor appears in one sentence, introduced as "In typical submarine gallows humor": the washed-overboard crewman was "immediately given the nickname 'Splash,'" the camera and a mailbag having gone to the bottom. The essay ends with the author's gratitude (the crewman had taken the camera and gone topside when the author, ordered up without a harness, would probably not have survived) and the line "But I'm still calling you Splash!" The humor is conferred on the survivor as a permanent label, fused with thanks. The same essay shows a conditional: the nickname follows a rescue, not a death. Chetlain's moral at the end is that "boring" is the best condition a submarine can have, and a recaptioned cartoon about the injured sailor's evacuation also circulated on the boat (wording not given).
*Candidate application:* The surest sign of affection in a crew may be the nickname attached to the worst moment someone survived. A character who keeps using it decades later is paying a debt in the only currency the group allows.

#### MV-B-044 (Flores and Tilton, "In on the [Racist] Joke")

**[E25c-4]. "It's always a joke": the claim of joking as a shield, and the penalty for not laughing.** (MV-B-044, L13-L65)
Written as a two-veteran dialogue (Flores, who is Filipino, an infantry medic; Tilton, mixed Pacific Islander and white, an infantryman and NCO), after an opening scene in which white soldiers whisper a slur about a Black soldier asleep in the field while NCOs hunt for his team leader to punish. The authors contrast it with the barracks claim, "Everyone is green," which they gloss as "Racism doesn't exist in the ranks." Examples, described only: a battle buddy confided a slur about the sleeper to Flores "as if I was in on a great secret," weeks after the same man had made a mocking gesture and gibberish phrase at Flores; Flores stayed silent and pretended to agree ("Hm, yes, I can see what you mean"). Tilton: an instructor who "jokingly" told Spanish-speaking soldiers to stop, misquoting an Army regulation; a platoon sergeant who repeatedly made racist remarks about Black and brown soldiers and answered Tilton's awkward looks with "just joking." Tilton drove off and left him on the other side of the base, reported him to the commander, and the platoon sergeant was relieved. Result for Tilton: a reputation with white NCOs as "that guy" who "couldn't take a joke," but respect from his own soldiers. Their summary exchange: "It's always a joke, isn't it?" / "It is always a joke!"
*Candidate application:* "Just joking" is a documented retreat that lets the speaker deny the target's reading, and the cost of refusing the retreat is social exile among the speaker's peers. A character who is the soldier who "couldn't take a joke" has a reputation, not a flaw.

**[E25c-5]. Dissent inside the veteran voice: laughing along as a purchase of belonging, and the argument that the jokes are not symmetrical.** (MV-B-044, L67-L89)
Flores admits participation: he let jokes about Asians "slide," played a Viet Cong role-play at the expense of a Vietnamese soldier with a thick accent (making him pose with AK-47s and say Vietnamese words) that he thought "all in good fun," and laughed at stereotype jokes and a slur used as a greeting. Looking back, he imagines that soldier saw him as "a jerk for reducing him to his ethnicity." Tilton argues that NCOs enforce a standard built in white supremacy, that acceptance requires being "not that guy," and that "if you can't take a 'joke,' people will isolate, target, and ostracize you." Flores's asymmetry argument: "white people" jokes lack the same power because there is no matching history of lynching, internment, exclusion from voting, property rights or the military, and racist jokes lower an individual's self-esteem and reinforce a stereotype that affects promotion. Their remedy is institutional (go to the equal opportunity or sexual harassment/assault prevention office, "because women are also 'in on the joke'") and the closing thesis is that "racist jokes are not jokes" and "Silence protects racism." **Editorial-slant caution:** it is an advocacy dialogue by two men of color; it records how it felt from the targeted and complicit side, and offers no view from the speakers who would defend the humor.
*Candidate application:* A veteran character from a minority group may have laughed along for years as the price of acceptance and later count that as a cost. A unit's "all green" humor can be a mask for a hierarchy it does not admit.

#### MV-B-045 (Hanf, "I'm Not a Sir")

**[E25c-6]. A woman officer's dry jokes as boundary management; the essay itself turns plain and normative.** (MV-B-045, L23-L65)
Hanf, trained at the Naval Academy to correct every lapse in greeting, finds Marines calling her "sir" at the gate, in the exchange, in email openings ("Gentlemen," "Good morning, sir") across her career, sometimes daily. Her habits: replying to an email that wrongly addresses her and adding a joke just before the signature block ("P.S. Not a 'sir'"), and a standing reply when asked if it bothers her, a quip about how many dollars she would have if she were paid for each "sir." She also did not berate the embarrassed junior Marine, since "his own embarrassment was sufficient shame." The essay then argues straight: Marines should use a "positive ID" step like the one before engaging a target (the author calls it "a slightly ridiculous combat comparison"), use rank-based or neutral greetings, and recognize that each snap assumption "reinforces the awareness of that individual as a minority." She estimates that perhaps one in 12 or so officers a young Marine meets is a woman. The jokes are what she did in the moment; the essay is what she wanted to be able to say.
*Candidate application:* A character in a minority within a unit can use light, self-owned humor so that the majority's default error is shown without a confrontation; the humor does not remove the grievance, and an essay-length plain statement lies behind it.

#### MV-B-046 (Jones, "Marine's Name Appears in Friend's Suicide Note")

**[E25c-7]. Banter as the whole of a friendship, a failed joke after a loss, and "sometimes it shows up as jokes."** (MV-B-046, L9-L21, L45-L55, L73-L75)
Jones, a young Marine at a first duty station overseas in the early 1980s, describes a friend, "William" (the author's pseudonym), as quick-witted and reckless; on duty "we joked, argued, and covered for each other," a friendship "forged in boredom, pressure, and shared volume." A week after the two competed for the same woman and she chose Jones, the friend died by suicide on duty and left a note that, the author was told, named him. Jones was not invited to the funeral and was given silence rather than a service; he kept showing up to formation, then went unauthorized-absent for nearly two weeks, was demoted and reassigned ("Weirdly, that saved me"). Years later, trying to "laugh it off," Jones tried a gallows line casting himself as the villain of someone else's note (paraphrased, not quoted; register); "None of that worked." What helped, "sometimes," was telling the story with the friend's name in it. For years he blamed the dead man as "weak." As a later police officer he noted that "not all trauma shows up as bruises or blood. Sometimes it shows up as jokes. Or silence."
*Candidate application:* Joking can be the only register two young men share, and then fail as a repair tool when the other is gone; the mask-and-silence reading ("jokes" listed beside "silence" as trauma presentation) is stated plainly by a veteran who also used the mask. His exclusion from the funeral (he does not know whether the family, the command or both decided) leaves any humor with no audience and the grief with no place to go.

#### MV-B-047 (Lyons, Vietnam)

**[E25c-8]. An explicit rule of license: joke about the wound a man walks away from, not about the grave ones.** (MV-B-047, L9, L15-L29, L49, L55-L63)
Lyons, a draftee infantry soldier in the Mekong Delta, 1968-69, says one of the major sources of unit casualties was the booby trap. He saw an X-ray of a GI hit by a homemade device, recognized the common construction hardware in the man's back from a lumber-yard job, and says "He and I later joked about it." The next sentence is the rule: "We did not joke about more serious injuries, of which there were far too many." His broader stance is stated as a plan: "Stay alert. Do things right. Have faith. And don't dwell on the bad stuff." The essay's own voice is wry throughout (mocking his failed typing test, the "green underwear," deadpan relief after kicking a live grenade whose handle was held by dry mud: "Thank God for dry weather"), and he now prefixes the grunt terms of endearment ("11-bravo, 11-bush, 11-ant bite, 11-jungle rot soldier") with "proud." Fifty-three years on, at reunions the stories "flow like water—or should I say beer?" and "I don't dwell on the bad stuff" is repeated as a refrain. **Caution:** the refrain is also a narrative policy; what is left off the page is not visible.
*Candidate application:* A realistic limit on gallows humor is a self-stated threshold of severity, set by the wounded man's own participation: the joke is allowed when the injured man joins in later.

#### MV-B-048 (Meagher, "Two Guys to a Bag")

**[E25c-9]. A casualty-handling detail: the one laugh is a reflex at a pratfall; then the essay stays plain.** (MV-B-048, L11-L51; **summarized, see register**)
A 1968 air-base radio operator and his night-shift crew (young airmen "not much older than boys") are sent to meet a helicopter carrying the dead and to carry the remains, "two guys to a bag, one bag at a time," in the crew chief's words, to the mortuary staff, who wait motionless. The scene contains the essay's only laugh: watching a fireman take "a massive pratfall" on the slick ramp, "We laughed instinctively." The detail then becomes a slapstick scramble up the ramp ("comically grabbing hold of each other") before the work, and an assembly line in which, Meagher says, "we each became experts in a macabre parade." #### MV-B-049 (Ore)

**[E25c-10]. Absurd-and-fun as a deliberate counterweight, and the rank-inversion getaway.** (MV-B-049, L9-L45)
Ore frames the whole essay against the combat-and-"embracing the suck" image: "Absurd and funny are definitely part of the story," many stories "didn't teach me a lesson," and "most of our service took place when we were kids." He says writing them reminded him how "absolute nightmares" the platoon was to its chain of command and apologizes to those people. First story (Fort Liberty, 2009): a platoon run goes too far into the woods, the platoon sergeant shouts "Zonk!" and everyone scatters; three runners break out onto the lawn of the division headquarters, forbidden to walk on, and are challenged by the command sergeant major ("Do you know who I am?"). One of them answers back, "Do we know who you are? Do you know who we are?" and then shouts "He doesn't know who we are! Run!" They run five more miles and are never pursued. Ore wonders whether the sergeant major would "take it for the loss that it was" or take it out on someone undeserving, "which is the custom in the military," and says that fifteen years later a knock on his door still prompts "the CSM finally found me!" Ore equates risk-taking in the barracks with parachute jumps ("what was the difference between jumping out of a two-story barracks window and jumping out of a plane?").
*Candidate application:* This is rank-satire as pure farce: the joke is a one-time win over authority that costs nothing, and the aftertaste is an affectionate, slightly anxious fear that never leaves the teller. Not all soldier humor is gallows humor; this is the other pole.

**[E25c-11]. The self-as-fool anecdote: mock-heroic narration of a near-catastrophe, disclaiming responsibility.** (MV-B-049, L49-L71)
Second story (2011): the author, a specialist who had repaired three belt-fed grenade launchers, tested them at a range he talked his way onto (seatbelt placed over his rank), fired several volleys with no idea where the grenades landed, and was then ordered, on the range loudspeaker, to clear the line while a tornado alert sounded and a tornado approached; he ran down the firing line pocketing live 40mm rounds ("the worst game of Frogger"), and later joked that the grenades "armed the oncoming tornado." The narration is built from a stock device: the sequence of regrets ("If only I had worked a little harder, I would have never dropped out of college," a recruiter who promised "stories to tell") and a closing mock-defense ("Definitely not mine") in which the weather takes the blame for any explosion. The author calls the result "a class on how to properly clear your Mk19s." No injuries are reported.
*Candidate application:* A character can tell the most dangerous story of his career as the one where he was the fool; the incompetence-and-luck frame makes the near-miss tellable at a dinner table.

#### MV-B-050 (Plummer)

**[E25c-12]. Humor register as a code-switch: "keep my jokes clean" at work, "military-grade humor" with veterans, and the question "Which one is the real me?"** (MV-B-050, L21-L47)
Plummer lists "dark senses of humor" among the traits service produces ("thick skins, sharp tongues, quick wits"), which he says help veterans "bond through shared misery and to cope," and which civilians read as "aggressive, abrasive, crude, and brutally honest." In law school and suburban life he tempers language and tone, keeps "my jokes clean and my humor light," covers tattoos, and bites his tongue during golf and poker "locker room" banter rather than unleash "military-grade humor" his neighbors are "simply not ready for." With old comrades the pattern reverses: "swapping crude memes and sarcastic insults" is cathartic but "no replacement for that missing bond"; hunting-trip campfires run on trading insults and an escalating game of one-upmanship in "profanity-laced" stories of fights and near-deaths (described only). The essay's resolution is "two faces," neither false: the professional restrains the "disgruntled Marine," and the "hardened realist" keeps the professional from being absorbed. **Caution:** the end of the essay ("I fit in everywhere and am accepted by everyone") reads as an editorially tidy resolution, a retiree two years out, and the effort of "minding everything I say" is named as a pressure.
*Candidate application:* Humor kept in reserve for the right audience is a labor cost; a character may be warm and gentle in public and need a regular, controlled outlet. Memes and insult-trading are named as a cathartic stand-in for a bond.

#### MV-B-051 (Searls)

**[E25c-13]. Pop-culture jokes as the command-center tension valve; the humor ends at a name.** (MV-B-051, L15-L19, L23-L75; **casualty detail summarized**)

#### MV-B-052 (Seymour, "The Military Funeral")

**[E25c-14]. Funeral humor is a family reflex that pride makes possible, not a coping craft: the essay's weight is that pride is a lid.** (MV-B-052, L9-L17, L29)
The index description (a funeral-detail officer's jokes about Jell-O cakes) over-reads the text. #### MV-B-053 (Van, chief initiation)

**[E25c-15]. A ritual comic trial: mock court, fines, ordeal tests, and the outsider made an honorary insider.** (MV-B-053, L7-L151)

**[E25c-16]. The same essay records, without comment, a disparaging code word for women in uniform and a sexualized finale.** (MV-B-053, L9-L15, L81-L85, L111-L135; **summarized, see register**)

#### MV-B-054 (Weiskopf, "The Stories I Share")

**[E25c-17]. The funny version travels; the frightened version does not: a filter on which stories go to civilians and spouses.** (MV-B-054, L9-L13, L35-L57)
Weiskopf opens with a dry remark made when mortars hit near her trailer in Mosul in 2004 (while her roommates hugged her): "Maybe we should spread out." She says this is one of the Iraq stories she "can lightheartedly share," that seems "to go over better than the ones I could tell while hardly breathing," because "no one really wants to hear what it is like to be terrified." The other funny stories she tells (an interpreter's persistent praise of a tall friend as a perfect bride because "her father owns a business"; an NCO who after meetings with locals reported how many goats and sheep he had "sold us for" and what great brides the women would make; a fun run interrupted by near-daily mortar fire) "strike the surface of funny," she says. The essay's end is the unshared stories, "not meant to be shared."
*Candidate application:* A veteran may hold a small, repeated set of safe funny stories as a presentable surface, with the sealed ones sitting behind it; the spouse's demand to be told in words is the step that opens it.

**[E25c-18]. Sarcasm that wears away with exposure, and a joke that comes true.** (MV-B-054, L9, L27, L49)
Weiskopf notes that every few months a new aid-station doctor arrived with "fresh uniforms and sarcasm, neither of which lasted for very long," and she sometimes watched both wear away and sometimes came back from a mission to find it already done. A joke about spreading out ends as a description of how three survivors live. 
*Candidate application:* A character's irony may have been a phase that burned out rather than a lasting trait, and a throwaway joke can be re-read years later as an accurate account of the group's fate.

### From Havok Journal (MV-B-019 to MV-B-023)

#### MV-B-019 (Chamberlin, "The Passover Party")

**[E25c-19]. A ritual that celebrates a loss: the "Passover Party" for an airman passed over for promotion.** (MV-B-019, L33-L35)
Chamberlin, a retired Air Force and Air National Guard chief master sergeant and crew chief, describes what he calls "a common Air Force tradition": Air Force promotions go in cycles by testing and points, only a set share are promoted, and those who miss out may get a group "Passover Party" (a pun on "passed over") with a cake iced to show the stripe they did not get, or a certificate or a humorous gift. In his words: "Celebrations for the winners and celebrations for the losers." His account of the mechanism: friends surround the person and talk about the failure and promise the next cycle; "Everyone laughs and jokes and then it's over"; an NCO tells everyone to get back to work because "Airplanes aren't going to fix themselves." He sets this against a corporate office where "everyone would be avoiding the person and uncomfortably mumbling some sympathetic words," and says civilians will read the party as "harsh, hazing, and uncaring" when "it's just the opposite." He says the same method is used for divorce, with no time given for moping: "we tell them it's not a big deal in the grand scheme of things." He concedes it is "a sad and frustrating time" for the passed-over. **Cautions:** the practice is attested only by this one author; the essay says nothing about how a passed-over airman feels, and its comparison with a corporate office of avoidance and mumbled sympathy is an assertion.
*Candidate application:* A unit can handle disappointment by making it public, comic and brief, then returning to work; the humor is a deliberate refusal to let the loser be isolated or to let the loss be dwelt on. A character raised in that custom may find civilian sympathy slow and uncomfortable.

**[E25c-20]. The argument around the ritual: toughening as ideology, and the author's own carve-out.** (MV-B-019, L7-L11, L29, L37-L45)
The essay's frame is hostile to outside reformers: "helicopter mothers" and "vote-pandering politicians" are accused of "diminishing and canceling traditions that build morale, esprit de corps, and a sense of belonging" and replacing them with "vanilla corporate values and slogans." He builds a case from the combat aircraft's weapon load list that the force is "a ravenous pack of angry apex predators," down to a junior finance clerk, and so toughness has to be built. A story about his four-year-old grandson (a minor fall escalated into wailing after a group of women asked if the child was hurt) is offered as a model: concern manufactures injury, and he told the group the child "wasn't in critical condition until you all convinced him that he was." He tells parents of recruits not to call a commander or senator, since the unit will find out and the recruit "will never live it down" (peer ridicule as enforcement), and to tell the child to "suck it up." **His one stated limit** is a parenthetical caveat that this is not meant to cover sexual assault and other crimes under the military code; he adds that writing requires "constantly qualifying things." **Editorial-slant caution:** this is the most polemical essay in the unit; it argues without evidence that removing such traditions "may end up contributing to the unnecessary deaths" of troops.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's defense of rough custom often arrives bundled with grievance at outsiders and a claim that toughness saves lives; both are positions to portray, not facts. The one carve-out he names (crime and assault) marks where even a defender places a limit.

#### MV-B-020 (Chamberlin, "Why Gallows Humor is Essential in the Military")

**[E25c-21]. Gallows humor as an outsider test in closed groups, with an occupational gradient inside one branch.** (MV-B-020, L7-L11, L23-L33)
Chamberlin says outsiders are "invariably" admitted to closed groups (the context is online groups where memes are posted) and then "quickly exposed when they react in abject horror"; when they voice indignation they are "treated as a failed act at Amateur Night at the Apollo" and jeered off the page. He cites the recent accident aboard the Bonhomme Richard in San Diego (named in the essay; the 2020 date is inferred from the essay's first-publication date): "Even while the fires were burning, the jokes were flying and the memes were being posted." Within the Air Force, he says "the biggest offenders are those in aircraft maintenance, and the Crew Chiefs are the worst," whose limits "are very fuzzy," and whose commanders "live in fear" of what they say. He extends the same humor to medical, firefighter, police and mortuary work and advises outsiders to "just stay outside." He addresses civilians with insider put-downs and says dismissively that they can "stay on their side of the fence." **Caution:** the evidence here is one man's assertion; the outsider test is a described pattern, not a recorded case.
*Candidate application:* Humor can be a gate: the outsider who reacts with horror is not corrected but removed. A character may carry this gate habit into civilian jobs.

**[E25c-22]. The stated origin of the humor in physical danger, scholarly claims at second hand, and a postscript disclaimer.** (MV-B-020, L13-L35; **injury detail summarized, see register**)
Chamberlin says the motivator is "ever-present danger around aircraft": a running jet engine kills in a moment without warning, he has seen students and friends killed and maimed (a crushed foot and a crushed head, which he describes plainly; not reproduced), and he declines to equate it with an infantryman's combat exposure. He decorates the claim with scholarship at second hand, none verified here: the term "gallows humor" as "black humor" coined by a surrealist theorist in 1935 (the text names Breton, tied to Swift), a 1942 sociology article by Obrdlik that he reports as saying such comedy strengthens the oppressed and undermines the oppressors, a Freud passage on the ego refusing distress (paraphrased by me), and a 2017 journal study that he reports as linking appreciation of dark humor with higher IQ, lower aggression and better resistance to negative feelings. He argues that a decade or more of a "nicey-nicey" culture has been cultivated in the military and asks, "are we doing our troops a disservice by taking away a coping mechanism?" His postscript: "p.s. We still get upset when people get hurt and killed."
*Candidate application:* The author's own limit is the postscript: the jokes are not a claim of indifference. Where the source at hand is a peer-reviewed or other scholarly one, check E01 and E05 first; the citations here are an enthusiast's.

### From Task & Purpose (MV-B-035 to MV-B-041)

#### MV-B-036 (Lutz as told to Clark, "There Are Some Things Only EOD Can Get Away With")

**[E25c-28]. License as a function of skill and autonomy: "we can get away with" it.** (MV-B-036, L9, L29, L35-L57)
Matt Lutz, a Navy explosive ordnance disposal technician (six years, to 2013; Iraq and Afghanistan), says EOD techs get a bad name for loose grooming and uniform standards but "are professionals when and where it counts." He says that because of "a very specific skill set," EOD "can get away with doing or not doing whatever we want": no working parties, a mutual-boss arrangement inside the team (an officer and a more senior technician each calling the other "my boss"), beards, and no weapon or uniform to the dining facility when the host army unit required both. In his account an Army first sergeant who complained about his sweatpants was answered, on Lutz's behalf and "respectfully," by his officer in charge; Lutz: "It was funny." When Army units demanded conformity he says the answer was in effect "I don't answer to you." The in-group humor he describes is lighter: the badge is called a "crab" because it looks like one; training is "beating on you" (PT) with "a jovial joking mentality" despite the instructors yelling; and sayings such as "tanned fat is muscle." **Caution:** the interview was shaped by a "Confessions Of" format that solicits "hilarious" stories; his dismissal of the "games" other services play over discipline is the interviewee's own.
*Candidate application:* The right to scorn regulation is conferred by the job's irreplaceability, and a specialist can turn that scorn on other services as a joke at their expense. A defector from a regimented force may know exactly which rules he was exempt from and which he was not.

**[E25c-29]. Pranks aimed outward with the tools of the trade, and a beer-fine custom for being in the news.** (MV-B-036, L59-L71)
Lutz says EOD techs never rigged each other but did it to Army and Marine units on deployment, in his words just to mess with them: small electric devices that pop loudly, rigged as a fake improvised explosive, such as a pressure plate under a doormat, with the culprits never identified; when turning over a building to Army EOD in Iraq, they wired it with a loud siren that the arriving unit then used as a doorbell. The tradition he confirms is that the first person to call out a teammate who appears in an article or on the news is owed beer by that teammate. His closing line, answering the interviewer's joke about owing beer, is a mock-defiant sign-off from a veteran who is "out" and so owes no one (the wording is profane; not reproduced).
*Candidate application:* EOD humor here is a trade parody: the prank's props are the very devices that kill, used on outsiders who cannot know. Teammates reserve for each other a lighter punishment (buying beer).

Sanders"; the text does not say who was calling, but it reads as a prank) and "didn't catch on" until the kangaroo-named one called twice, and quoted a film's "duality of man" speech at a sergeant major who was actually ordering him to remove a peace pin; an Army medic who was teased as a "POG" by the infantry platoon he was attached to and answered with crude graffiti insulting 11B anatomy on a portable toilet ("This wasn't really a prank, but it certainly ruffled feathers"), who also describes weekend hangovers cured by medics hooking each other up to IVs in the barracks as his MOS's tradition; an Air Force battle manager's long and scatological story of a three-week stomach illness during flying duty in Afghanistan, ending "No comment"; and a non-veteran editor who describes being punished with soap for misidentifying a Top Gun character. **Caution:** the set-up (humor-seeking prompts, drink, a column built to be funny) makes these curated, performative anecdotes, not sampled speech.
*Candidate application:* Inter-MOS teasing (infantry versus support) escalates by symbolic retaliation on a shared object; a specialty's perk is stated as a joke about what it can get away with. Not recorded here: any case of a joke that failed.

**[E25c-31]. Pre-invasion boredom, a disliked company commander, and a friendly-fire near miss told as "CLASSIC WINGER PRANK."** (MV-B-037, L63-L69)
Matt Ufford, a Marine tank officer (2000-2004), recalls the weeks before the 2003 invasion: the battalion's junior line officers in one circus tent with perhaps 90 minutes of work in 12 hours of daylight, so sleeping in was the main amusement. A company commander ("let's call him Capt. James," a pseudonym) who flipped on the lights at 6 a.m. as "god of the light switch" was harassed by taping the switch and removing the bulbs ("so much more satisfying to wake up to the sounds of him swearing"). The night before the invasion, an attack-helicopter pilot fired a missile at that officer's tank; it hit the engine and no one was hurt. Ufford labels it "CLASSIC WINGER PRANK" (the text does not define "winger"; it is presumably slang for aviators, with the pilot cast as the prankster) and says the group joked that it had taken the missile to remove the captain's rigidity; "James was a lot cooler after that." The text does not say whether the incident was investigated. **Caution:** the event as told is one man's retrospective in a humor column; "prank" here is a rhetorical frame for what the writer says was an accidental or unexplained shot.
*Candidate application:* A near-miss with a hated authority figure is told as comedy only because no one was hurt, and the comedy is credited with the man's improvement. Contrast E25c-3: the joke arrives after survival.

#### MV-B-038 (Doft as told to Clark, "Confessions Of A Tank Commander")

**[E25c-32]. Jokes at the perpetrator's expense in a cramped crew, and a hierarchy that needs no repetition.** (MV-B-038, L15-L53; **bodily detail summarized**)
Army Capt. Aaron Doft, in charge of 15 soldiers and four tanks at Forward Operating Base Rustamiyah east of Baghdad in 2006, says that on missions as long as 36 hours crews used bottles and MRE bags for bodily functions inside a tank where digital thermometers "stop at 150 degrees" (his claim). Asked whether it is "a point of humor," he says yes: "Lots of jokes at the perpetrator's expense," mostly about what the man ate and the texture of MREs. When asked how four men in a tiny space avoid friction, he says that "Everyone knows where they fit within the hierarchy," so "that's not a source of conflict," and that learning to communicate "to best amplify cooperation and reduce conflict" is both leadership and how people "just get along." On combat he is matter-of-fact: "in direct contact for more days than we were not," near-constant indirect fire at the base, an RPG that passed "about three feet" away, summed up as "So that was exciting."
*Candidate application:* The crew's humor is aimed at a fact of physical life that all share and none can avoid, and the target rotates; understatement ("exciting") is the register for being shot at.

**[E25c-33]. Initiation rituals for new arrivals: the officer's hat in the gun, the new man taped to the barrel.** (MV-B-038, L73-L85)
Two tanker customs: at qualification firing, a newly arrived platoon leader's cover is loaded into the main gun's chamber and the commander orders "Fire" so that the officer "sent his own cover downrange" (the text says it vaporizes in the tube; "it's fun to say"); and new, usually lower-enlisted arrivals are duct-taped to the main gun tube, which is elevated while the turret is spun "until the new arrival vomits, or starts giggling too much for anybody else to have fun." Officers and senior NCOs are exempt unless they "really feel like being a part of the unit and endearing themselves to the men." Doft participated in both: "Absolutely. Of course. It's part of the culture." He sets armor against infantry, who get their Combat Infantry Badges: "Tankers, we get to have fun."
*Candidate application:* Initiation humor here is a dose of physical discomfort with a clear end condition (the new man vomits or laughs), and officers earn standing by volunteering; the line "Tankers, we get to have fun" sets a self-image that trades prestige for play. *Dissent not present:* the interview does not ask the recipients' views.

#### MV-B-039 (Flowers, "Making Warriors Hard In An Age of Sensitivity")

**[E25c-34]. Advocacy: crude jokes and dressing-downs as "hardening," with a claim that jokes let troops laugh after recovering the dead.** (MV-B-039, L9-L37; **crude and body content summarized**)
Flowers, an Army veteran who rose from private to first lieutenant, praises the "smoke-sessions," the dressing-downs and the blunt leadership he received as a young soldier, remembering being told to be quiet in coarse idiom and never feeling the need to complain to an inspector general. He argues the force has become "an over-sensitive military of babies," claims that a "zero tolerance policy" toward "botched jokes because of audience taste in humor" creates "an unrealistic vacuum," and says crude sexual and bodily jokes "in the field or in the COC" (operations center) must be distinguished from "actual sexual harassment and hazing," which leaders "must confront." The central humor claim: "We need to empower our junior NCO's and junior enlisted to make jokes so that when they have to go pick up someone else's cadavers they can laugh afterward." He adds a stated skill: teach juniors "how to draw boundaries and stand up for what they are ok with joking about." He gives a skywriting stunt by pilots as a modern example of what should be tolerated. **Caution:** this is an opinion piece in a polemical idiom; the cadaver-duty link is asserted, not shown.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may argue that crude humor is training for the work with the dead; the same veteran's own limit (harassment and hazing are different) shows where the defense stops.

**[E25c-35]. Dissent from inside the outlet: an editor's note and a rebuttal headline.** (MV-B-039, L7, L41)
The page records that "The headline of this article has been changed to more accurately reflect the author's viewpoint" (the URL slug still carries the original, `theres-a-reason-for-all-that-hazing-and-ass-chewing-and-we-shouldnt-ignore-it`), and it ends with a link to a response piece headed "What Carries You Through The Hard Parts Of Combat Isn't Hazing — It's A Good NCO." The response's text is not in this file. The evidence of dissent is therefore the headline alone: a veteran-written response disputing that hazing, rather than leadership, is what carries people through combat. **Caution:** the dispute is visible only in titles and an editor's note; what the response says about humor is unknown.
*Candidate application:* Veterans publicly disagree about whether harsh humor is a tool or a crutch; a character's stance on that argument can differentiate him from comrades who share his history.

#### MV-B-040 (Mooney, "Sarcasm, Scotch, And Veteran Stereotypes")

**[E25c-36]. The civilian office as a place with nobody to joke with; humor turned into a counter-image of the veteran.** (MV-B-040, L11-L19, L25)
Mooney, a former Marine (Afghanistan, no combat stories by his own account), describes an office job in 2012 where coworkers first wanted "harrowing combat" stories, lost interest when he had none, and found his service "an uncomfortable subject." A colleague introduced him to new female staff as an Army veteran of Iraq who "blew people up" (wrong branch, wrong theater, and he says he never did); he stopped correcting it. He attributes the distance to media images of veterans ranging from "pitiable yet heroic" to "terrifyingly damaged." His reading: people at work "couldn't really joke around or relax with someone they couldn't picture doing either of those things"; his sarcasm made it worse, he became "moody, bitter, and snappy," which "a lot of people expect of a veteran." His friends outside work, nearly all veterans or pre-service friends, laughed at his tales of misadventure. The outlet was a weekend of writing a comedy pilot about veterans, "Vetted," with characters "not Michael Bay-esque super badasses or generic PTSD sob stories," aiming to show "the funny side, and even the darker side with a coating of comedy." **Caution:** it is a self-promotional piece (a crowdfunding appeal), and the author's stated humor type is sarcasm, not gallows humor.
*Candidate application:* Humor can stop being possible with civilians because they cannot picture the veteran as relaxed; the response here is a public, produced counter-portrait rather than a private joke.

#### MV-B-041 (Young, excerpt from *Eat the Apple*)

**[E25c-37]. A first homecoming: the funny and frightening stories fall flat, and a clean heroic lie replaces them.** (MV-B-041, L29-L57; **injury and detention details summarized**)
In a third-person present-tense memoir passage ("the boy"), a Marine infantryman returns from his first Iraq deployment in 2006 to family waiting at base. On 96 hours of leave in a hotel, drinking for the first time in eight months, he first tells true stories: a friend injured after the vehicle was blown up and struggling to speak, another falling down a dune while under sniper fire during a lieutenant-ordered trash cleanup (an absurd, dark-comic detail), a detention of a twelve-year-old during a raid, and "being ambushed in the back of a Humvee and laughing." Over the silent spaces the family drifts off to bed. He reads the women's faces as expecting more; he later understands they were "pleading, begging, and screaming for him to shut up." So he invents a battle story with imagined insurgents, one where he "gets to feel, for once, like a hero." The lie "becomes a scratch on the roof of the boy's mouth," later told to friends and other Marines, and he is "ashamed not to tell the story." The next morning an anxiety attack in the car follows a near miss; his aunt "tries to make a joke about the grandfather's driving. The boy cannot manage a laugh." **Caution:** the passage is literary memoir; the editor's note (the outlet's voice) frames the book as "self-lacerating wit," and the book's title is the author's explanation of a saying (digest the enlistment, then discard it and move on; the saying's second half is a vulgar rejection of the institution, paraphrased).
*Candidate application:* The jokes of the front, told to the home audience, are not received; the veteran then supplies the version that civilians recognize, and the lie becomes a second weight. The family's attempt to bridge with a light remark fails because he can no longer laugh at ordinary things.
*Cross-use:* The "eat the apple" saying (engage fully, then dispose of the institution) is a candidate exile's phrase for leaving.

## Pattern roll-up

Finding numbers in brackets. All patterns come from veteran self-presentations in three editorially gated outlets (see Gaps); read each as how a veteran chose to describe it to a general audience.

**Functions.**
- *Boredom relief and crew cohesion.* The most-stated engine of humor in the unit is monotony, not fear: sealed submarine patrols, quiet peacekeeping tours, idle pre-invasion weeks, long deployed downtime [1, 2, 23, 24, 31]. The boredom-and-terror formula is repeated almost verbatim by two authors [1, 24].
- *Tension valve under strain, with the content displaced.* A command center jokes about films and impressions, not the casualties on the screens [13]; a barracks fills time with eccentrics and storytime [23]; a crew jokes about each other's bodily functions in a tank [32].
- *Affection and debt.* A permanent nickname for a man who survived a near-death, used in gratitude [3]; honest, unflattering speech about a dead teammate that draws a roar of laughter from comrades [25].
- *Rank negotiation and license.* Rank satire as farce [10]; license conferred by competence [28]; an officer's standing earned by joining initiation [33]; the short-timer's stored credit [27]; a mock court that ends with an outsider made an honorary insider [15].
- *Boundary management.* A woman officer's dry jokes about a default she cannot change [6]; being "that guy" who cannot take a joke as a stance [4].
- *Identity and transition.* Humor register as a code-switch [12]; a produced counter-image of the veteran [36]; the missing audience [26, 27, 36].
- *Loss ritual.* A public, comic, brief ritual for professional disappointment, with a return to work [19].

**Targets.** Self and one's own incompetence [11, 17, 27, 30]; officers and senior NCOs, from a sergeant major's lawn to a light-switch captain to a new platoon leader's hat [2, 10, 31, 33]; other services and specialties (grunts and tankers, infantry and support, EOD and the host army, aircraft maintainers and everyone else) [26, 29, 30, 33]; shared bodily facts [32]; civilians as an outsider class [21, 36]; people with a minority position in the unit as targets of "jokes" [4, 5, 16]; the survivor of a near-death as the subject of a nickname [3]; the dead only in the honest-eulogy form [25].

**Forms and mechanics.** Deadpan understatement ("So that was exciting," "Maybe we should spread out") [17, 32]; mock-official register and a fake plan of the day [2]; rumor engineering [2]; nicknames, including security-driven ones [1, 3]; mock court with costumes, fines and ordeal stunts [15]; movie-quote private languages [27, 13]; pun-name prank calls [30]; mock-epic narration of trivial talk [24]; props from the trade used for a prank (fake explosive devices) [29]; initiation with a built-in end condition [33]; one-liner signature jokes in email [6]; the self-as-fool anecdote [11]; the funny version of a story prepared for civilians [17, 37].

**Rules of license.** A severity threshold set by the wounded man's own participation [8]; humor follows survival, not death [3]; humor ends at a known name [13]; outsiders are tested by their reaction and removed [21]; "just joking" as a retreat from accountability, and the price of refusing it [4, 5]; the target rotates (the perpetrator of the moment is joked about) [32]; the prank closes by amnesty and a face-saving fiction [2]; stated carve-outs: sexual assault and crimes [20], harassment and hazing [34], "we still get upset" [22]; a speaker's own boundaries are to be taught [34].

**Limits and failure modes.** Jokes that carry contempt for a minority soldier, and the pressure to laugh along as the price of belonging [4, 5]; a ritual's gendered code and sexualized finale remembered fondly by one who fled it [16]; a gallows joke after a friend's death after which "None of that worked" [7]; involuntary laughter and no debrief after casualty work [9]; pride as a lid on grief [14]; the unsaid stories behind the funny ones [17]; the inability to laugh at an ordinary joke after returning [37]; the cost of minding every word [12]; sarcasm that wears off with exposure [18].

**Time course.** In-service anecdotes are told with fondness 35 to 53 years later [1, 8, 15]; the same author can later revise an in-service joke into a regret [5]; a grandson-sized lesson in over-concern [20]; a 15-year-old flinch at a knock on the door after a prank [10]; sarcasm eroded in months, not decades [18]; reunions as the late-life site of humor [8]; a friend's loss still unresolved after four decades [7].

**Civilian-facing register.** The funny story that goes "over better" than the terrified one [17]; a clean heroic lie replacing true war stories at a family table [37]; kept-clean jokes at a law school with the real register kept for veterans and hunting trips [12]; a ritual that civilians would call hazing and the insider calls belonging [19]; the funeral as "b-roll" and merchandise [14]; misidentification by coworkers and the produced counter-portrait [36].

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics.** Dissent inside the veteran voice is concentrated in a handful of essays: [5] (the cost of being "in on the joke" and of silence), [7] (jokes as a mask; "none of that worked"), [14] (pride as a lid), [16] (the author's fondness and flight in one memory), [17] (the funny story as a shield), [35] (an outlet's editor and a rebuttal headline against the hazing-as-hardening argument), [37] (an inability to laugh). A departing-institution slogan ("eat the apple") [37]; the community left after service: hunting-trip comrades, reunions, an all-veteran circle of friends [8, 12, 36]; being excluded from a friend's funeral [7].

**Cross-cultural / army / era.** U.S. Navy submarine (1988) [1-3]; U.S. Navy chiefs' initiation (1980) [15]; U.S. Army and Marine Corps Iraq and Afghanistan (2003-2012) [4, 5, 10, 11, 13, 17, 26-37]; U.S. Air Force maintenance and air power [19-22]; Vietnam-era Army and Air Force (1968-69) [8, 9]; Canadian Army peacekeeping (1997) [23]. No non-Anglophone army and no pre-1968 setting.

**Craft observations (what the authors do on the page).** In most of the strongest essays the humor is a single line inside plain, procedural narration, and the humor is shown by its aftermath rather than a transcript [3, 8, 9, 13]; essays place the funny story beside the unsaid one so the reader sees the gap [17, 37]; the self-as-fool narrator is the safe protagonist of a near-disaster tale [11]; and the most credible limits are the ones an author states about their own jokes [7, 8, 20, 34].

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Selection and gatekeeping.** These 25 files are the product of domain-filtered searches (log searches 55, 67-74, 84-85, 102-105) and an editor's choice at each outlet. The War Horse essays are edited, fact-checked and copy-edited (and at least one author is a War Horse fellow), and their house style is the reflective personal essay, which favors an arc toward meaning, grief or critique; the Havok Journal items are opinionated pieces, three of them republished after years; the Task & Purpose items are staff-solicited humor and opinion ("hilarious, embarrassing, and revealing"). The result over-represents (a) polished retrospectives, (b) humor as a topic or a counter to civilian ignorance, (c) a handful of branches and eras. It under-represents spontaneous, in-the-moment gallows humor.
2. **Rare actual gallows humor.** Few pieces contain gallows humor in any strict sense (humor about death, injury or atrocity that the participants joke about): the "Splash" nickname [3], the bounded Vietnam rule [8], the Ranger's honest-insult laugh [25], a claim about cadaver duty [34], and Chamberlin's assertions [21, 22]. Much of the unit is pranks, rank farce, barracks absurdity, rivalry and initiation. The unit documents the frame and limits of gallows humor better than its content.
3. **Humor claims versus humor scenes.** Chamberlin [20-22] and Flowers [34] argue from assertion; Chetlain, Ore, Van, Weiskopf, Lajoie, Perna, Smith and the Confessions interviews recount scenes; Flores and Tilton [4, 5] argue and recount. Treat the assertions as positions of a defender, not data. The scholarly claims in [22] (origin of the term, a 1942 sociology article, a Freud passage, a 2017 study) are second-hand and unchecked; verify against E01 and E05 before reuse.
4. **Index descriptions that over-read the text.** The index and brief describe the Seymour funeral essay as funeral-detail jokes that make grieving families' hours bearable; the text has one sentence about a civilian teenager's family at a cousin's funeral [14]. The Smith "honest funeral" is a police funeral, and the laugh occurs at get-togethers [25]. Meagher's index line mentions "what humor did there" (index wording); the essay says only that a reflexive laugh happened and does not claim a function [9].
5. **Date and detail oddities.** Chamberlin's page shows 2025-07-04 but states first publication on July 15, 2020 [21]; Lajoie, Perna and Smith are also republished (first appeared 2022, 2015, 2022). Ore's caption puts him in Ramadi in "late 2019" while the story years are 2009 and 2011, and uses "Fort Liberty" for events before the name existed [10, 11]. Meagher's base name is spelled non-standardly in the text. Jones's pseudonym "William" is the author's. Van's initiation anecdote is a 42-year-old recollection of a drunken day, and the author says it is no longer practiced this way [15]. None of the essays can be independently verified from this folder.
6. **Race, gender and rank.** Voices are mostly officers and senior NCOs (Hanf, Seymour, Van, Plummer, Chamberlin, Doft, Flowers, Searls) or writers by profession (Clark, Mooney, Young); only one essay (Flores and Tilton) is directly about racial humor, and it is advocacy by two men of color; no essay gives the view of the people who tell the jokes it criticizes. Women's essays [6, 15, 16, 17] speak mostly of being addressed, included or filtered, not of an exclusion-by-humor argument, and the bride jokes in [17] are reported without evaluation. No Black, Hispanic or enlisted-woman voice appears first-hand except Weiskopf.
7. **Missing branches and settings.** The log records dead ends for medics, corpsmen, mortuary affairs and chaplains in this harvest, and the "Confessions Of" tag pages returned 410; a medic, corpsman or mortuary entry in the series may exist and is not here. No essay in this unit is by a person still in service.
8. **Items to check in a browser.** The recaptioned cartoon text on the submarine [3] (a photo caption only); the text of the response to Flowers [35]; the images attached to Chetlain's prank essay; page images in Van's essay (the pinned anchor, the initiation scrapbook); the Havok cake photograph referred to in [19]; the one missing closing quotation mark in [27].
9. **Internal tensions across essays.** Chamberlin says dark humor is a healthy coping mechanism that civilian sensitivity is removing [22]; Jones says of his gallows line, "None of that worked," and that what helped was telling the story [7]; Seymour finds pride the lid that allowed family jokes until Taps breaks it [14]; Weiskopf says the funny version works with others and the full version needs to be said aloud [17]; Lyons's rule is that the grave wounds are not joked about [8]. These are not contradictions so much as different jobs for humor (a social lubricant, a mask, a lid, a surface); the unit does not adjudicate.
10. **Quotation exactness.** The local text copies use typographic quotation marks; I quote in straight marks and checked each quotation against the files after writing. Where a source has profanity, I paraphrased rather than quoted (see register).
