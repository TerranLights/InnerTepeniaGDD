## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-08 is.** The archive.org OCR of the 1930 second edition of *Songs and Slang of the British Soldier, 1914-1918*, edited by John Brophy (songs, introduction) and Eric Partridge (glossary). The first half (lines 1-4,949, covered by E12a) holds the prefaces, Brophy's introduction, about fifty songs in three groups, and the first part of the glossary. **My range, lines 4,950 to the end (L9,562), is the second half of that structure, in this order:** (1) the **rest of Partridge's alphabetical slang glossary**, from "Hot Air" (printed p. 130) to "Zig-Zag" (p. 181), about 300 entries by a rough count of headwords, the OCR making an exact count unreliable; (2) the editors' **"Note to the Glossary"** (pp. 185-188; L7655-7795), a short essay on how the war changed the language, with a note on the dictionaries consulted (L7808-7842); (3) **Appendix A, "Chants and Sayings"** (pp. 193-197; L7857-8142), prose by the editors on catch-phrases, with texts; (4) **Appendix B, "Bugle Calls"** (pp. 198-200; L8145-8312), words fitted to bugle calls; (5) the **"For the Second Edition: Prefatory" note** (dated July 28, 1930, p. 203; L8323-8372) and a **Postscript** with ten new songs (L8375-8683, pp. 204-208), **about a hundred added glossary words** (L8689-9350, pp. 209-219), additions to Chants and Sayings (L9356-9482), three more bugle calls (L9488-9518), and a **"P.P.S. to Glossary"** (L9521-9543). There is **no index** in this file; the final lines (L9546-9562) are OCR noise from a damaged last page. So the **"remaining songs"** in my range are only the postscript songs, E12b-43 to E12b-49; all the other songs were in E12a's range, and the bulk of my findings are slang and the editors' commentary on slang.

**Evidence base.** As E12a states: retrospective (1930), by two veteran editors, British Expeditionary Force, mostly the Western Front with Gallipoli, Mesopotamia, Egypt and Salonika entering through single entries; recollection and oral tradition, no informant list, no frequencies, few dates; the glossary mixes definitions, usage sentences the editors composed, etymology (often speculative) and strong editorial opinion (against the Staff, Red Caps, M.O.s, the Quartermaster, "frightfulness" and the drill system). The postscript glossary and chants are supplements gathered after the first edition from correspondents (J. B. Priestley and others are thanked), and they cite trench newspapers (*The Wipers Times*, *The B.E.F. Times*) as sources.

**What my range does NOT contain.** No quoted speech from named soldiers; no joke told about a named dead man; no songs about particular battles; no explicit treatment of deserters, mutiny or shell shock beyond single words (the Étaples-area deserters appear in one entry, L9183-9189; mad-words are mild and graded); very little on officers' own humor except as flagged "officers' slang"; almost nothing from Irish, Welsh, Indian or Dominion troops except as loanwords (Arabic, Hindustani) and Australian or New Zealand slang; the most obscene songs are named but not printed; the sexual and scatological layer is described by the editors as large and is therefore mostly missing (see the register). The slang entries are of uneven value: roughly two thirds are technical or neutral (kit, organization, trench terms, place-names) and **I extracted only the revealing ones, as the brief instructs**; I do not list the rest.

**Overlap with E12a.** My range begins fifty lines before E12a's range ends (L4950-5002, from "Hot Air" to "Issue"); I extracted the entries from "Hot Air" to "Issue" only where they bear on my themes (E12b-1, E12b-2, E12b-3, E12b-9, E12b-13); E12a's own finding on those entries should be taken as primary.

**Format and OCR.** The text is a plain OCR. Printed page numbers are bare numerals on their own lines (e.g. "130" at L4962; "158" at L6443), and I cite **line numbers (L)** with printed pages in the source-shape notes. Headwords are often garbled and the leading asterisk on an entry means "technical, official, or semi-official" (the postscript says so, L8692). Quotation marks and apostrophes are curled. A few entries are cut off by the page break (e.g., "Touch" L7185; "Charlie Chaplin" L8777-8786). **A list of every OCR headword I had to read through is in Gaps and cautions item 7.**

## Findings

### A. Glossary, H through S (L4,950-6,490)

**[E12b-1]. The vocabulary of rumor: "hot air," "latrine rumor," "Mesopolonica," "furphy."** (PW-08, glossary: "Hot Air" L4945-4950; "Latrine Rumor" L5230-5259; "Mesopotonica/Mesopolonica" L5426-5431; "Peggies/Furphies" L5936-5943)
Partridge defines **hot air** as "loose talk with less significance than is intended by the talker; unfounded rumors; bumptiousness; official phraseology; the whiting on the sepulchre," from the image of hot air rising quickly: the same slang word covers a soldier's gossip and the brass's own prose. The longest rumor entry, **latrine rumor** (L5230-5259), lists five standard forms: (1) German plans, new weapons and traps; (2) changes of High Command, new theaters of war, "Napoleonic schemes of victory"; (3) "secret doings by one or other of the Allies which would relieve the pressure on the British front"; (4) spy and treason stories; (5) "auguries of a life of comparative ease and luxury in the near future." The editor's color examples: "Some battalions were always about to be moved to India, Egypt or even Samoa; others were to be employed sweeping the paths of G.H.Q. chateaux." The explanation is spatial: latrines were "practically the only places (except the grave) in which a private soldier could be comparatively secure from the eye and voice of authority," so men "sat there for long periods, reading newspapers, gossiping and exercising their imagination and credulity." The editor compares the scene in *All Quiet on the Western Front* and adds that British latrines were "slightly less open to the public view (only slightly) and not nearly so lugubrious." The hope-rumor of a posting to safety has its own compound word: "Rumor always had it in every battalion that the next move would be to the East and comparative ease and safety," so the imagined destination was named by fusing Mesopotamia and Salonica (text heading reads "Mesopotonica," the body "Mesopolonica"; the body form is the intended compound). The Australian counterpart, **furphy** (the OCR heading "Prerrizs" and the body spelling "Furphies"), is derived by one authority from a Melbourne maker of "sanitary carts" at a 1914 camp "when the destination of the A.I.F. was still unknown, and rumors abounded"; the editors' own guess is a made-word from "Murphies" and "potatoes," suggesting "the vividness of the Irish imagination."
*Candidate application:* Rumor is institutionalized into a named genre with predictable types, a sanctioned place (the latrine) and a fixed wish-fulfillment (posting somewhere easier). A soldier-type character's (or an exile community's) disbelief of the posting that is always about to come is a documented attitude, not a quirk.
*Cross-use:* Exile/closed communities: a standing rumor of relocation to safety, named and joked about.

**[E12b-2]. Naming the enemy: "Hun" (newspaper), "Jerry" (soldier), "Johnny," and the surrender word turned into a joke.** (PW-08, glossary: "Hun" L4972-4978; "Jerry" L5040-5046; "Jerry Up!" L5049-5054; "Johnny" L5095-5096; "Kamerad" L5117-5121; "Kiltie" L5151-5153)
**Hun** is defined as the German (singular and plural), "occasionally applied in the vocative to an unpopular comrade," and its origin is "a newspaper application" after the destruction of Louvain. **Jerry** is, by contrast, "a familiar expression almost of affection," "used constantly by private soldiers," and the example sentences show the pitying-neighbor register: "Jerry's damn quiet, to-night" and "Poor old Jerry, he's not half getting it from them heavies of ours." **Johnny** is short for Johnny Turk, "very rarely for a German." The exclamation **"Jerry up!"** warned that a German aircraft was overhead and might drop bombs; the editors add the gloss "or of faint interest," and that if the plane threatened a machine-gun attack "the remarks were wilder and stronger." **Kamerad**, defined as the German word used by prisoners "requesting mercy," was "often put to facetious uses by British soldiers among themselves, e.g. 'Kamerad!' when a story showed no signs of ending": the enemy's plea for his life becomes the joke-plea of a listener to a bore. A related note on journalism: **"kiltie"** (the OCR heading reads "Kittrz"; the definition is "a name for a Highland soldier, taken from popular journalism and used only mockingly for the purpose of starting a fight") shows a press coinage being weaponized among the troops themselves. The same distance between press vocabulary and soldier vocabulary appears in "Hun" versus "Jerry."
*Candidate application:* Documented pattern: the official or journalistic enemy-name is used by soldiers at most to insult a comrade, while the soldiers' own word for the enemy is familiar, even pitying. A character can sound like a veteran by which name he uses.

**[E12b-3]. Shells, bombs and strongpoints renamed from the household, the pantry and the hardware store.** (PW-08, glossary: "Iron Foundries" L4991; "Iron Rations" L4994-4996; "Jam-Tins" L5019-5022; "Johnson" L5091-5092; "Mills Bomb" L5443-5446; "Minnie" L5449-5450; "Non-stop" L5586; "Oil-Cans" L5676-5677; "Pill-Box" L5972-5974; "Pineapple" L5983; "Pip-Squeak" L6015-6016; "Plum Puddings" L6037-6038; "P.H. Helmet" L5945-5948)
The editors record a consistent method for naming the means of death. Heavy shelling is "iron foundries." **"Iron rations"** is the army's term for the emergency food reserve, and was "sometimes used for shells, e.g. 'There goes Jerry's iron rations!'": the shell is a ration delivered to you. The home-made bomb of 1915 (France and Gallipoli, before the Mills) is simply a **jam-tin**; the small German trench-mortar bombs are **oil-cans** ("from their shape") and **plum puddings**; the German mortar bomb with a wind-vane is a **pineapple** (and the British Mills bomb is described as "serrated like a pineapple"); the gas shell is a **pip-squeak** (loosely the whizz-bang); the **Minnie** is the *Minenwerfer* given a girl's pet name; the **coal-box**, the big high-explosive shell, has a synonym **"Johnson,"** named for the boxer Jack Johnson (the editors describe him in period racial terms, which I do not repeat; the point is a heavy shell named for a champion heavyweight). A shell that has passed well overhead is a **"non-stop,"** as of an express that does not call at your station. Even the early anti-gas flannel hood (**P.H. helmet**) is described by the editors with the one word "Mercifully" ("mercifully superseded by the box-respirator"). The concrete machine-gun post is the **pill-box**, "a German invention." Mechanisms: domestic and grocery words; diminutives and pet names; boxing metaphor for weight; the shell that missed as a train that did not stop.
*Candidate application:* A unit's names for incoming fire turn on ordinary objects, and the entries show that the objects are the army's own (rations, oil, jam), a clue that humor is built from the unit's daily world. Robots or exiles would naturally name hazards from their own routine materials.

**[E12b-4]. Dying and the dead: euphemism by property, by food, by rhyme and by irony.** (PW-08, glossary: "Landowner" L5226-5227; "Loaf o' Bread" L5339; "Napoo" L5525-5533; "Mafeesh" (OCR "Marrrsh") L5390-5394; "Next o' Skin/Next of Kin" L5539-5543; "Off It" L5646; "Packet" L5854-5868; "Part-Worn" L5902-5908; "Pipped" L6011-6012; "Pushing Up Daisies" L6198; "R.A.M.C." L6228-6240; "Rest Camps" L6319-6322; "Rooty Medal" L6353-6354)
Terms for being killed are overwhelmingly mild and often property-based. **"To become a landowner"** meant "to be dead and buried, the grave being the estate." **"Off it"** is "to die" (cross-referenced to Packet and West); **"pushing up daisies"** is "dead—and buried"; **"loaf o' bread"** is "dead" by rhyming slang. **"To cop a packet"** is explained at length: it "meant sometimes to be fatally wounded, sometimes simply to be wounded," while "Blighty or Blighty one was usual for a non-fatal wound," and the editors guess that *packet* may allude either to the missile or to the "gauze and lint of the First Field Dressing" applied to the wound. "Pipped" is to be hit, "especially by a bullet." **"Napoo"** (from French *il n'y en a plus*, first the estaminet keeper's reply when asking for drink, when he expected richer customers) "came to be used for all the destructions, obliterations and disappointments of war," and the examples place a loaf and a sergeant-major in the same slot: "The bread's napoo"; "The S.M.'s napoo." The Arabic equivalent **mafeesh** (the OCR heading reads "Marrrsh") is glossed "there is none: nothing; dead; finished" and used in "Egypt, Gallipoli, Mesopotamia and Palestine as *napoo* was used in France and Belgium." Irony works on official terms: **"rest camps"** are "camps behind the lines where troops returning weary from the line were harried with incessant parades and brass-polishing," and the term was "also used ironically for cemeteries." **"Part-worn"** is clothing "worn previously by another soldier, deceased, sick or otherwise having no use for it," and a man given it could usually exchange it later "by a judicious bribe to the [Q.M.'s] clerk" (the OCR prints "O.M.'s"). The **R.A.M.C.** (Royal Army Medical Corps) is expanded as "Rob All My Comrades," because the "private effects and souvenirs of an unconscious or sleeping man would sometimes disappear on the way to hospital." The **"rooty medal"** (rooty = bread) is the Long Service medal, "earned by eating army rations for a number of years." The "next of kin" entry (OCR heading "Next or Sxin," which reads as a pun on *next o' skin* but the editors give only the plain definition) explains that the soldier named on enlistment "the person to whom all effects and moneys due should be sent if the soldier died."
*Candidate application:* The dead are carried by words about land, bread, flowers, post and parcels; the grim is carried by the institutional: kit reissue, next-of-kin forms, medals made of rations. A character who speaks in these registers can say "dead" without ever saying "dead," and each term is also a small joke about bureaucracy.

**[E12b-5]. The language of attack: loss of the half-sentence, "mopping up," "one star, one stunt," "push."** (PW-08, glossary: "Over the Top" L5796-5807; "Push" L6183-6194; "Mopping Up" L5489-5496; "Pip" L5986-5998; "Runner" L6400-6411)
Four entries show the soldiers' (and editors') awareness of what attack-words concealed. **"Over the top"** was "originally *over the top and the best of luck*, but as it became increasingly obvious that there was no luck in frontal assaults, the latter half of the phrase was dropped"; the editors add that going over the parapet was "entering another world (and often literally, for the unlucky ones)." **"Push"** (the Somme's "Big Push" of 1916): "The word indicates the desired ideal rather than the actuality." **"Mopping up"** (clearing isolated machine-gun posts behind an assault) is described as a "domestic metaphor for a murderous business" that "is typically English" (the editor's generalization, not a documented soldier remark). **"Pip"** is the officer's star; because junior officers "became casualties in an attack in such numbers," a popular saying was **"one star, one stunt"**: that a one-pip officer was expected to last one attack. The entry **"runner"** records that the message-carrier's job, "though it offered relief from normal routine, was a very dangerous one," since runners were needed when shelling broke the phone lines.
*Candidate application:* The editors present a language that shed its hopeful tail, a euphemism built from housework, and a morbid actuarial proverb about junior officers: three different ways in which attack-language was edited by the men who used it.

**[E12b-6]. "San Fairy Ann" and "maalish": the book's explicit statement of how cynicism and fatalism form.** (PW-08, glossary: "San Fairy Ann" L6432-6467)
This is the most explicit editorial account of why the humor turned. The phrase (from French *ça ne fait rien*) was "extremely popular." "As the intelligence of the soldier penetrated year after year the infinite layers of bluff and pretentiousness with which military tradition enwrapped the conduct of the War, so his cynicism increased, became habitual. Men ceased to show initiative, not so much because they were dispirited by war, but because they realized that the stupidity of authority was impregnable." The editors add that "neither virtue, courage nor skill" could be shown to protect a man "from the quite impersonal violence of shells or bullets, from the malignance of a biased superior or from unnecessary and dangerous duties carried out by order from above," so the soldier "adopted a fatalism comparable to that of the Moslem murmuring his enervating 'Maalish'—It does not matter." The concrete list of what could be said "san fairy ann" about: the Quartermaster selling food and clothing on the side, the Divisional General ordering "a hopeless raid in order to win a C.M.G. for himself," and a man with cold feet getting "a cushy job at the Base." The phrase also had "lighter uses, especially in defiance of the warnings of friends," and variants (San Fairy, San Fairy Anna).
*Candidate application:* A clear causal model (random danger + authority's indifference = fatalism, expressed as one shrug-phrase) for a veteran character's characteristic "it doesn't matter." The phrase is a weapon against both fear and orders. Note this is the editors' interpretation (a participant-observer's, written in 1930), not a recorded soldier explanation.

**[E12b-7]. The faces of authority: Red Caps, the M.O., the Quartermaster, the Regimental, the padre, King's Regulations.** (PW-08, glossary: "K.R." L5212-5219; "Loot" L5355-5362; "M.O." L5456-5478; "Orders/On the Mat" L5707-5716 and L5780-5785; "Pack-Drill" L5847-5851; "Padre" L5871-5896; "Quarter Bloke" L6202-6212; "Red Caps" L6256-6272; "Regimental" L6278-6289; "Regimental Police" L6295-6299)
Partridge's glossary does not merely define; it states a view. **King's Regulations** were "designed to preserve tradition, not to increase efficiency." **Red Caps** (military police) are "the most hated and despised men in France," said to "revel" in "secret opportunities for cruelty" when they staffed military prisons; the editors say the job was voluntary and "no decent man would undertake it if he realized what it implied." The **Medical Officer**: some "brutes," some "weak underlings of the C.O.," some "undoubtedly human"; in the line the editors say they all worked hard, "many of them heroically," but in treating the sick "almost all M.O.'s exhibited neither intelligence nor courtesy," and as the war went on the medical profession "hurried the deformed, the epileptic and the aged to the shambles"; the same entry condemns the sending out of underage boys, ending "Surely the modern Moloch is a scientific god!" (one phrase in the middle of this entry uses a racial slur as a work simile; see the register). The **Quarter Bloke** (quartermaster): "It is possible (one must be open-minded) that there were some honest men among the Q.M.s, but the case has never been proved." The **"Regimental"** (the Regimental Sergeant-Major) is "tight" and as an adjective "excessively disciplinarian," with a speech example about the man who is silent up the line and "too bloody regimental for words" when the battalion comes out. **Padres** are sorted in three kinds (the man-of-the-world "despised and disliked"; the earnest but ineffectual "despised but tolerated"; the "spiritual but determined" first-aider and cigarette distributor, "respected as a person, but not as a parson"), with the exception that Roman Catholic padres "were respected, because they came into the line and because they lacked the haw-haw voice." Officers below captain were always addressed as "Mister" (the entry **"loot"** is for lieutenant; "Mr." was also a privilege of the R.S.M.). **"On the mat"** and **"Orders"** record the C.O.'s minor-offense court, held on a laid mat; **pack-drill** is "drill in full marching order performed continuously at high speed," a "severe" punishment. The **Regimental Police** (distinct from Red Caps) were "usually amenable to quite small bribes."
*Candidate application:* The institution has a stable cast of resented figures, and the text shows the *terms of the resentment*: not rank as such but arbitrary power, unfair distribution (food, medical treatment) and cruelty at a safe distance. A defector-type character would carry a precise typology of who deserved respect and why, and the religious figure gets a conditional respect ("as a person").
*Caution:* These are the editors' (Partridge's) judgments, strongly written and sometimes sweeping (e.g., "almost all M.O.'s"); the "opinion" register is the editors', not a recorded soldier consensus. The book was published in 1930, in the first wave of disillusioned war memoirs.

**[E12b-8]. The ethic of dodging: "lead-swinger," "old soldier," "old sweat," "laughing," "light duty," "M.D.," "mike," "lorry-jumping."** (PW-08, glossary: "Laughing" L5262-5267; "Lead-swinger" L5269-5272; "Light Duty" L5312-5330; "M.D." L5419-5423; "Mike" L5439; "Old Soldier" L5688-5693; "Old Sweat" L5696-5698; "Lorry Jumping" L5365-5374; "R.T.O." L6361-6383; "Knock Off" L5208-5211)
**"Laughing"** is an adjective: "comfortable, safe, fortunate, especially in contrast with others or with normal circumstances," e.g. "He's got a job at Brigade Head Quarters, so he's laughing"; "Once I get to the C.C.S. I'm laughing." (Here *laughing* names a position of safety and not a reaction.) A **lead-swinger** is "an habitual malingerer," a term "often used affectionately and with the faintest suggestion of reproach." **Old soldier** is "one grown old in sin," a term "of mingled admiration and indignation, implying cunning in the art of dodging work and trouble"; **old sweat** is the soldier of long service with "an accumulation of cunning." The sick-parade entries make an argument: the **M.D.** ("medicine and duty," a pill and orders to report for duty "and a cursing") and **light duty** (fatigues such as unloading lorries or shoveling coal) "broke down in the line," where genuinely ill men were evacuated and others "made shift as best they could," helped through their duties by their mates; it was "the absurdity and incessant reiterations of military routine which swelled the sick parades out of the line," and "Men's minds revolted from the constant nagging of parades and duties, and they went sick not to malinger but as a gamble (the odds were usually laid on the M.O.), hoping to break the monotony." **Lorry-jumping** (hitching rides behind the lines, stopping lorry drivers "with civil words (the only politeness ever extended to the A.S.C.)") and the **R.T.O.** excursions (returning soldiers deliberately catching wrong trains and persuading the Railway Transport Officer to date-stamp their passes, "a delightful holiday rich with new scenes and an air of personal freedom otherwise unknown in the Army") are given as skilled arts.
*Candidate application:* Dodging is not a moral failing in this lexicon; it is a craft with its own titles of respect, and the sick parade is read as a gamble against boredom. The affectionate reproach attached to the lead-swinger is a precise register: the group tolerates and teases the dodger so long as he does not endanger the group.

**[E12b-9]. Food, jam and the grumble as ritual.** (PW-08, glossary: "Jam On It" L5008-5016; "Issue" L4997-5002; "Jippo" L5074-5081; "Maconachie" L5381-5387; "Pozzy" L6123-6137; "Pozzy-Wallah" L6139; "Pork and Beans" L6099-6103; "Rooty" L6349)
**"Jam on it"** is a rebuke to a grumbler: the phrase "What do you want?—Jam on it?" mocks anyone complaining about "his lot or the scantiness of his blessings." **Pozzy** (jam) is the study case: in the first years "only one form of jam was available—plum and apple," which "became a standard grumble"; later issues brought "doubtful mixtures such as gooseberry and rhubarb" and cardboard cylinders replaced tins; "to the end an issue of strawberry or raspberry jam was an historic occasion." Someone "inordinately fond of jam" is a **pozzy-wallah** (wallah being the Hindustani suffix). **Maconachie** (the tinned vegetable-and-gravy ration) was "edible" warm but "cold, it was a man-killer," yet was welcomed as "a welcome change from bully-beef." **Jippo** is the juice or gravy of bacon, "greatly prized," with someone at the back of the queue always urging the orderly men to "steady on the jippo." **"The issue"** meant what was officially supplied, and could be used for "the whole lot": "There's no rum to-night. The sergeant's snaffled the issue." The allied nickname **"Pork and Beans"** (for the Portuguese) is a facetious approximation which the editors tie to the ration "with very little pork, if any."
*Candidate application:* Grumbling about food is an accepted, near-ritual genre with its own stock rebuke ("jam on it"), and it measures comfort in tiny differences (strawberry versus plum-and-apple). Another reminder that morale is carried by small supplies.

**[E12b-10]. Cohesion words: "mucking-in," "mob," "mate," "old bean," "one of the boys."** (PW-08, glossary: "Mucking-in" L5508-5522; "Mob" L5481-5486; "Mate/Matey" L5405-5409; "Mail Up" L5400-5401; "Old Bean" L5680-5686; "One of the Boys" L5703-5704)
**Mucking-in** is "a method of sharing rations, sleeping quarters and certain duties," "quite informal and arranged by the men themselves." The editor's claim: "A set of mucking-in pals, two, three or four, formed the real social unit of the army." The set received rations "in a lump" and divided them; brewed its own tea; "naturally, the members of the set were sworn friends: they would defend each other's property against scroungers, receive letters and parcels for an absent member of the group and—out of the line—drink and feast together"; the editors cite *Her Privates We* (Davies, 1930) as a faithful picture. **"Mob"** (battalion or unit) "implied no disrespect; in fact it was almost affectionate." **"Matey"** appears in the plea "Be matey! Lend us a fag" (the speech example shows the word as a request for small generosity). **"Mail up!"** is "a shout of joy and expectation when letters and parcels arrived from home." **"Old bean"** is "Familiar form of address, indicating affection. No meaning." (derived by the editors from the musical-comedy dude and "old man"). **"One of the boys"** meant to be "popular, cheerful and debonair."
*Candidate application:* The social unit that matters is two to four people sharing rations, not the platoon; its humor would therefore be private and in-pair. "Mob" and "old bean" show affection carried by meaninglessness.

**[E12b-11]. The editor's account of load, fatigue and "off duty" — states of the body that the slang does not joke about.** (PW-08, glossary: "Pack" L5810-5844; "Sanitary Fatigue" L6470-6477; "Off Duty" L5627-5643; "O'Grady" L5649-5667)
The long **pack** entry stops being a definition and becomes an argument: estimates of what the infantryman carried ran from "fifty-three to seventy pounds," "in practice, it was much more than this" (rifle nine pounds, extra ammunition, rations, "bags of bombs, sandbags and often picks and shovels, wire and picquets"). The editors write: "It is rarely understood that the war was fought by men who were always tired, because they got little and disturbed sleep in the line, and because they were, day in and day out, weighed down by appalling physical burdens. All this apart from the dangers and discomforts." Officers "carried no packs," though some disguised themselves as privates, and "many of them helped the weaker men by relieving them of part of the load—unofficially, of course." **Off duty** is described as "those periods, brief, uncertain and terminable at a moment's notice" when the private could "amuse himself," subject to prohibitions (hands in pockets, a tunic button fastened, a hat on, salute any officer within sight); the entry concludes that for a limited period and "to win the war" one could undertake "such a slavery," but "as a normal routine, it is unthinkable." **"O'Grady says"** (the P.T. instructors' version of Simon Says) is explained as a game that "may sound too simple to deceive even a plowboy," yet "when one has devoted years to acquiring habits of moving the body smartly in response to certain orders, it requires real restraint not to obey those orders when they are bawled with authority." **Sanitary fatigue** (latrine trenches, burying dung, and disinfecting "in the line" the places where old corpses were unearthed) is called "one of the most unpopular duties in the Army," although a few men "wangled it permanently as a cushy job." (Summarized; see the register.)
*Candidate application:* The text's clear message that exhaustion is the baseline, not the exception. A defector-type character's chronic physical injury can be placed against the editors' description of a body wrecked slowly and cumulatively by load before it was wrecked by anything else.

**[E12b-12]. Borrowed words: the empire's languages as the army's slang, and the French *poilu* as a counterexample.** (PW-08, glossary: "Iggry" L4981-4983; "Imshi" L4985-4986; "Jildi" L5066-5068; "Mafeesh" L5390-5394; "Pauny" L5923; "Pukka" L6176-6178; "Rooty" L6349-6351; "Saipa" L6425-6430; "Napoo" L5525-5533; "Poilu" L6041-6088)
Soldiers took commands and tags from wherever the army had been: **iggri/"iggry"** ("hurry up," Arabic; the Australians "appropriately named a crossing in Bullecourt: Iggry Corner"), **imshi** ("go! away with you," picked up in Egypt), **jildi** (hurry, Hindustani; "on the jildi"), **pani** (water, Hindustani), **pukka** ("real, genuine, correct, smart, soldierly," Hindustani), **rooti** (bread, Urdu), the greeting whose OCR heading reads "Saipa" (probably *saida*: "good day! greeting! Arabic, from Egypt, where so many English and Colonial troops were stationed at one time or another"), with the remark that "Arabic terms were very frequently used by Australians and New Zealanders and by the Regulars"; **mafeesh** (E12b-4), **maalish** ("It does not matter," E12b-6; the postscript glossary also lists "Ma'alesh—Never mind! It doesn't matter. From Arabic," L9042, and *maghnoon*, "fool, dolt, idiot. Also from Arabic," L9043), and from French **napoo**, **san fairy ann**, **promenade** and others. The editors also quote the French lexicographers on **poilu** (my gloss: "hairy one"), which Déchelette says "was more used at the rear than at the front, and at the front only by officers and non-coms," and conclude: "French soldiers didn't particularly like the word: they called themselves 'les hommes' or 'les bonhommes'." (A civilian name for a soldier that the soldier does not own; compare the "Hun"/"Jerry" split in E12b-2.)
*Candidate application:* Foreign-language slang is picked up from postings, by orders and commands first, and the army's lexicon becomes a map of where the unit has served. A veteran from a multi-theater service would speak in layers.
*Cross-use:* Exile communities and Outer Tepenia colonists with mixed-origin speech.

**[E12b-13]. Civilian-facing and officer-private contrasts: "leg-show," Kirchner pictures, "promenade," plus the sex-words handled restrainedly.** (PW-08, glossary: "Leg-Show" L5280-5305; "Kirchner" L5166-5175; "Promenade" L6142-6145; also "Hot Stuff" L4953-4966, "Jig-a-jig" L5057-5063, "Knocking-Shop" L5197-5205, "Piece" L5954-5957, "Red Lamp" L6273-6275; "Joy-stick" L5099-5101)
The editors use some entries to record the gap between the home front's and the soldiers' views. The **leg-show** (a vaudeville entertainment of chorus girls) "was considered by the civilian populace to be adequate compensation to any and all soldiers for the horrors of war—and many of the men found it so"; the editors add that "Probably no other generation will ever be able to guess at all the excitement and ... atmosphere of naughtiness generated between 1914 and 1918 by the word *legs*," and attribute the fuss to pre-war dress codes and "the hysteria of the times." **Kirchner** pictures (illustrations of young women in lingerie by a French artist) were pinned up by officers' orderlies in officers' billets and dugouts "to cheer his lonely heart"; "private soldiers, grosser in thought and conversation, took at the most a passing interest" (the class contrast is the editors'). The **promenade** entry shows the mock-courtship routine of a stock French phrase offered to a French girl, "very rarely a serious invitation: mere raillery." The remaining sex-related entries (a woman "willing to forgo the usual sexual reticences," a French-origin term for mercenary copulation, the slang for a brothel, for a girl and for a licensed brothel's sign, an aircraft control lever named for the "thrills" it could produce) are listed in the register: these are word-origin and usage notes whose mechanism is euphemism, onomatopoeia, and a code of knocks or lamps; their wording is not reproduced.
*Candidate application:* The editors depict erotic material as a civilian-supplied comfort and an officer-class decoration, with the private soldier's attitude to it as detached or blunt. This is useful as period class contrast only.
*Caution:* Not a representative record of sexual humor; the book handles that material delicately or omits it.

**[E12b-14]. Rhyming slang, tiny coinages and the pre-war Regular Army.** (PW-08, glossary: "Rhyming Slang" L6325-6337; "Pig's Ear" L5969; "Plates of Meat" L6019; "O My" L5700; "Nobby" L5564-5565; "Pedlar" L5931-5932; "Pull-Through" L6172-6174)
The rhyming-slang entry says the pre-war Regular Army private had "a most intricate system of slang in which a word was referred to obliquely by means of another word or phrase rhyming with it," probably "in origin, a device of thieves and other petty criminals for talking about their misdoings without incriminating themselves," and notes that "the rhymes chosen, oddly enough, often indicate a religious upbringing or the memory of innocent nursery rhymes" (the example "Cain and Abel" for "table"; the other example given is an ethnic-group rhyme, see the register). Short instances: "pig's ear" (beer), "plates of meat" (feet), "O my" (sword, from "Oh my Gawd"), "loaf o' bread" (dead; see E12b-4). The **nickname-by-surname** custom: "All men named Clark were by long tradition known as Nobby"; a man named Palmer was "Pedlar" "from a pugilist." A very tall thin soldier is a **"pull-through"** (a rifle-cleaning cord). The postscript glossary (L8751-9325) adds more rhyming-slang terms, e.g. "mince pies" (eyes), "strike me dead" (head), "war and strife" (wife), "Rory O'More" (door), "little gray home in the west" (vest), "pen and ink" (stink), and prints a rhyming-slang verse, *The Dream* (L9145-9161), of which the editors say they can quote only two of five stanzas; its text is nonsense-comic domestic narrative in rhymed substitutions and is not reproduced here. The editors' own jocular circumlocutions for the posterior and for two sexual terms, and the terms for a prostitute and a bodily function, are not reproduced (see the register). The editors add that rhyming slang, "though certainly much less interesting than cant, is more amusing" (L9143-9145).
*Candidate application:* Nicknames derive from fixed rules that outsiders cannot see (surname equals nickname), a mark of the long-service regular.

**[E12b-15]. Unit-cynical coinages: "ragtime" and "night ops."** (PW-08, glossary: "Ragtime" L6221-6226; "Night Ops" L5545-5548)
**"Ragtime"** is used "as an adjective for any special form of inefficiency or absurdity," e.g. "Oh, it's a ragtime camp, this is." The editors' neighboring entry **"night ops"** (night operations): "a part of training that was half romantic and half burlesque, but never realistic." Both entries mark how the troops filed the gap between the army's practices and the war.
*Candidate application:* A single borrowed word for an army doing something pointless is useful for a veteran's shorthand.

### B. Glossary, S through T (L6,486-7,186)

**[E12b-16]. Scrounging, "square" and "short hairs": the moral economy of petty corruption.** (PW-08, glossary: "Scrounge" L6497-6515; "Scrounger" L6518-6520; "Short Hairs" L6567-6576; "Snaffle" L6689-6691; "Square" L6780-6781)
The editors define **scrounge** as stealing "not personal belongings, but from a department or some other embodiment of authority," and claim: "More army property changed hands in France by scrounging than by legitimate means." The system is circular: "The unfortunate loser cursed a little, and then promptly set out to replace his loss by scrounging from someone else." The legend example: "a certain platoon once removed a whole elephant hut from a number of sleeping engineers. This feat in time became legendary." A **scrounger** is "an adept at the art," a word "Used as both insultingly and admiringly" (the grammar is the source's). The word came from North Country dialect, where it also meant "to wander about idly." **"To have a man by the short hairs"** means holding a leverage over a superior that prevents him from punishing you; the example sentence is "He daren't bring me up for orders because he's trying to dodge the Colonel just now. So I've got him by the short hairs." **"Square"** is to bribe, or otherwise secure "the favor or neutrality of a superior." (Earlier related entries, E12b-4 and E12b-7, show the same economy at the quartermaster's clerk and the regimental police.) 
*Candidate application:* Theft from the institution, not from the group, is a recognized competence, the ethic is stated, and the legendary exploit (a hut stolen from sleeping men of another unit) is the unit's version of a war story. A defector-type character may carry this competence as a habit he does not think of as a crime.

**[E12b-17]. Hope, duration and the end of the war: "it's all in the seven," "three blue lights," "sweating on the top line," "thumbs up."** (PW-08, glossary: "Seven" L6526-6530; "Sweating on the Top Line" L6922-6928; "Thumbs Up" L7068-7077; "Three Blue Lights" L7054-7059; "Time-Serving Man" L7109-7115)
**"It's all in the seven"** was "a philosophic exclamation used by Regular soldiers who had enlisted for seven years" and meant "I was a fool to enlist and now I've got to stick it." The **time-serving man** was a Regular who had enlisted "not for the vague and elastic duration of the War ... but for a definite period, usually seven years," and "needless to say, such men were not discharged when the period of their contract expired, but often easier and safer employment was found for them." The editors record a war-weariness joke, the **three blue lights**: "It was a standard joke among soldiers who despaired of the War ever ending that Peace would be announced by a rocket—three dark-blue lights, which would, of course, be quite undetectable against a night sky." (The Three Blue Lights are tied by the cross-reference to the Very Light and S.O.S. rocket entries, i.e., to the real signal vocabulary of the trenches.) **"Sweating on the top line"** meant "to be full of hope and reasonable expectation of happiness and success," possibly from the gambling game "House" (bingo), where the top line "nearly filled" meant "a sweat of exhilaration and anxiety." **"Thumbs up"** expresses "intense joy or gratification," with the examples "Thumbs up, lads. There's buckshee rum to-night" and "It's thumbs up in this company now the S.M.'s gone on leave." 
*Candidate application:* The joke form that tells the truth about a hope (the peace signal is by design invisible) is paired with hope-words borrowed from gambling and applause, a suggestion that hope itself was treated as a bet. The contract-versus-duration tension is documented as a Regular's grievance.

**[E12b-18]. Theatrical and journalistic vocabulary for battle: "show," "stunt," "strafe," "stuff," "shock troops," "stand to," "S.O.S."** (PW-08, glossary: "Shock Troops" L6547-6551; "Show" L6579-6591; "Stand To" L6830-6833; "Stuff" L6890; "Strafe" L6868-6873; "Stunt" L6893-6905; "Storm Troops" L6861; "S.O.S." L6713-6733)
**"Show"** is "a theatrical entertainment of the lighter sort" and, "hence, also, an attack, promoted (it often seemed) for the amusement of the Staff and newspaper magnates"; the phrase "to put up a good show" means "to fight a losing battle well; to acquit oneself honorably." **"Stunt"** starts as "any performance of outstanding skill or effectiveness" (an acrobatic performance, a clever football ruse, "a good stunt to fall out before the fatigue party's picked"), and "in time the quality of cleverness became inessential: all that was needed was the unusual element, the break in routine, and 'stunt' was applied especially to a big attack," e.g., "The Somme stunt," "The March 1918 stunt," or a small raid ("a bombing stunt at night"). **"Strafe"** (from the German "Gott strafe England") means either punishment ("I just heard the C.O. strafing the cooks like hell") or a bombardment; **"stuff"** is simply "shellfire." **"S.O.S."** was the "extreme appeal for help," signaled by rocket colors rather than by wire; the editors add that "sentry-work being nervy work, especially for tired men, S.O.S. rockets were usually entrusted only to officers and Company Sergeant-Majors." **"Stand to"** was the dawn and dusk vigilance on the firestep.
*Candidate application:* The theatrical words (show, stunt) name an attack as an entertainment staged by someone else; as with "push" (E12b-5), the vocabulary is the troops' own reading of who benefits. The stunt entry also documents semantic drift: a word loses its meaning of skill and keeps only "the break in routine."

**[E12b-19]. Absurdity in kit and parade: "Soldier's Friend," "swagger-stick," "spit-and-polish," "khaki blanco," and the medical inspection.** (PW-08, glossary: "Khaki Blanco" L5139-5143; "Soldier's Friend" L6707-6711; "Spit-and-Polish Parade" L6763-6766; "Swagger-Stick" L6916-6919; "Smutty" L6679-6686; "Short Arm Inspection" L6554-6564; "Sick" L6594-6620)
Several entries are one-line ironies. **Khaki blanco** (dressing for web equipment) was "known absurdly" by the name of the white dressing for canvas shoes. **Soldier's Friend** was the brand of brass polish (a pink tablet one spat on to make a paste), and "the manufacturer's name misinterprets the soldier's attitude to the commodity." The **spit-and-polish parade** was an inspection by the Colonel or a General, "an occasion for much application of soldier's friend, khaki blanco (cf.), and leather polish." The **swagger-stick** was "an absurdity, enforced by many colonels." The entry on what the men called obscene, **"smutty,"** observes that "the use of this word, and of dirty, in this sense, is a significant indication of a subconscious sense of shame even in the most degraded men," and that it was misapplied to "quite decent subjects, e.g. 'nudes' in art galleries" (an editorial reading; not a soldier statement). The routine medical inspection for venereal disease (a "short arm" parade) is described by the editors as "another example of the degrading slavery to which the necessities of war compelled decent, reticent men to submit" (summarized; see the register). 
*Candidate application:* A soldier who is marked by the unit's absurdities (polish, parades) may find these the easiest targets for dry irony because they are safe from punishment and shared by everyone.

**[E12b-20]. Sick parade, self-injury, "swinging the lead," and "working one's ticket": the code of malingering and what the joke covers.** (PW-08, glossary: "Sick" L6594-6620; "S.I.W." L6622-6636; "Skrim-Shanker" L6650; "Swinging the Lead" L6947-6971; "Ticket" L7079-7098; "Tapped" L7004-7009)
The **sick parade** is described as a ritual with a predictable outcome: the M.O. saw each man in turn, asked "a question or two," "invariably gave him a standardized purgative pill, known as Number 9, and marked him down as M.D.," Light Duty or Excused Duty; malingerers were reported "if the M.O. were sufficiently sure," those who "were unable to hold themselves upright" were in time removed to an ambulance, "But everyone got a Number 9." **Swinging the lead** (from the sailor taking soundings who let the lead swing and called out fictitious depths) is "a great source of pride with some soldiers and—in certain circumstances—with all soldiers," but the code has limits: "A lead-swinger who let other men do work he was able to do, or who practiced his craft when danger was threatened, was beloved of no one. But the man who swung it skillfully on experts, such as doctors, in England or behind the line, received genuine admiration and kept up no pretense before his comrades." The self-inflicted wound (**S.I.W.**) is treated in a separate, unjoking entry: "a most serious crime committed only by men temperamentally or physically unfitted for the line, or men so overstrained by service that they were no longer responsible for their actions"; many cases were "camouflaged by sympathetic superiors, but far from all," and there were special hospitals at the Base where "conditions were said to be severe to the pitch of persecution" (the entry cross-refers to novels about the subject; the act itself is not described). **"To work one's ticket"** (get discharged by scheming) is the pivot: "It would be noticed that a certain man kept complaining of chronic pains or began to behave peculiarly. Comments would be turned aside by old soldiers with the remark, 'Oh, he's only trying to work his ticket.' Thus the phrase in time became a genial and facetious suggestion that a man was mentally defective." Madness vocabulary is mild and graded: "tapped" is "slightly mad, below the normal in sanity" (a barrel from which part of the contents has been drawn); "loopy" (E12b-1's neighbor, L5341-5352) is "silly; slightly insane; unwise; eccentric."
*Candidate application:* A precise, documented ethic of who may joke about dodging, and a documented case in which the joking phrase for a faker became the genial phrase for a man who was unwell, a joke that works as a gate and can hide real distress. For a veteran with chronic pain, the phrase "working his ticket" is exactly what a skeptical peer would say.
*Caution:* The editors' wording is their own gloss on a phrase's drift; the book gives no instance of an actual man misread this way.

**[E12b-21]. Corpse words turned into friendly insult and warnings: "stiff," "snuff it," "stop one," "third man."** (PW-08, glossary: "Snuff It" L6697-6698; "Stiff" L6850-6853; "Stop" L6858-6859; "Third Man" L7043-7052)
**"Snuff it"** is "to die, not specially to be killed," from the old method of putting out candles. **"Stiff"** is a corpse, used "in friendly invective always with the prefix 'big,' e.g. 'You big stiff!' meaning, 'You are clumsy, stupid or incompetent.'" **"To stop one"** is "vivid for 'to be hit by a bullet or shell fragment'" (cf. packet, E12b-4). The "third man" superstition is the clearest ritual entry: "the most popular superstition in France was the firm belief that the third man to light a cigarette from the same match would inevitably be killed soon after. It was so strongly held that anyone inadvertently attempting to go third man would have his hand or his face pushed violently away—unless he was very unpopular."
*Candidate application:* A word for the dead is drained into a mild insult for incompetence; a shared superstition both protects the group and prices out the unpopular man, a documented piece of hidden social ranking inside a ritual of survival.

**[E12b-22]. Policing talk: "put a sock in it," "talk wet," "steady on."** (PW-08, glossary: "Sock" L6701-6704; "Talk Wet" L6979-6990; "Steady On" L6836-6840; "Tell the Tale" L7025-7027)
**"Put a sock in it"** was "a vivid and peremptory method of telling a man that he had talked too long, especially if he were complaining" (the entry points to the Postscript, which lies later in my range). **"Talk wet"** (talk nonsense unintentionally): "It was always the other person who talked wet and was told not to with considerable emphasis." **"Steady on"** is "a general cry of warning and (strictly moderate) indignation from the temperate English heart." **"Telling the tale"** is "explaining away military offenses and delinquencies—the offender doing the explanation." (Compare "Kamerad!" for the endless story, E12b-2, and "jam on it," E12b-9.)
*Candidate application:* Peer pressure against complaining is itself a joke form, with its own stock phrases, and it limits how long a grievance may be aired.

**[E12b-23]. "Souvenir": the word that made looting polite.** (PW-08, glossary: "Souvenir" L6736-6757)
The editors say "souvenir" "completely displaced" the English word keepsake "partly because *souvenir* could be used without sentimental associations, partly because it was *à la mode* to be French in France." Items collected include "pickelhaube helmets, revolvers, badges, knives, watches, razors (though these were prized for use, their edge being famous), nose-caps, bullets"; they "were picked up, taken from the dead, or prisoners, either forcibly, or more often, as a free gift or by barter or purchase," and "so many were collected that ex-R.A.M.C. men must have started many shops after the Armistice." The word also became a verb: "I'm going souveniring to-night"; "Harry's gone to souvenir [i.e. find] a brazier." A further use, a facetious offer made to French girls (a sexual jest), is mentioned in the entry and not reproduced.
*Candidate application:* A foreign loanword acts as a euphemism that strips the dead and prisoners of their status as persons, and then extends to ordinary finding. A morally ambivalent veteran could carry such a word.

**[E12b-24]. Drink: "S.R.D.," "squiffy," "tight," "tin-hat," "swig."** (PW-08, glossary: "S.R.D." L6816-6822; "Squiffy" L6809-6813; "Tight" L7105-7107; "Tin-Hat" L7118-7119; "Swig" L6942-6944)
**S.R.D.** (the initials stamped on rum jars) officially meant "Service Rum Diluted"; the editors comment, "The dilution must have been infinitesimal," and record a "popular but unauthorized amplification of the initials": "Soon Run Dry." Drunk is "squiffy" (from "askew" and "squint," the effect on eyesight) or "tight" ("a sufficiency of liquor"); "tin-hat" is "typical English facetiousness for a steel helmet" and "just occasionally for drunk"; **swig** is "especially of a hurried or illicit drink." (The OCR joins "Zig-zag, Blotto, Binge, Blind" as drunk-words by cross-reference.)
*Candidate application:* The drink vocabulary is mild, sight-based and hat-based, and the rum jar is the only item given an unofficial expansion of its stamped initials; a unit's rum is a standing joke about quantity and timing.

**[E12b-25]. Names for people: the rules of nicknames, rank forms and "Tommy."** (PW-08, glossary: "S.M." L6660-6665; "Sarnt" L6479-6483; "Skipper" L6642-6643; "Smudger" L6675-6676; "Spud" L6768-6777; "Squarehead" L6784; "Stout Fellow" L6864-6866; "Swaddy" L6908-6913; "Tails Up" L6974-6977; "Tassie" L7019-7023; "Tommy" L7135-7153)
The rules: the Company Sergeant-Major is "always introduced by 'the,' without the surname"; "Sarnt" is "a smart and soldierly pronunciation," used only before the surname; **"skipper"** is the officers' name for a company captain, "but not used as a prefix"; "stout fellow" and "tails up" are "officers' slang." Surname nicknames are fixed by custom (Smith is **Smudger**; Murphy is **Spud**, with the editors' note that potatoes, "like pigs," are "eternally associated in the English mind with the Irish, and an association being, to an Englishman, half-way to a joke"; Clark is Nobby; Palmer is Pedlar, E12b-14). **"Tommy"** (Thomas Atkins) was, the editors claim, "never used by English troops except derisively or when imitating the style of a newspaper or a charitable old lady," though used by Australians and New Zealanders; the French and Germans "picked up" the term "from newspapers." **"Tassie"** (soldier from Tasmania) was used by Australians and New Zealanders: "most English soldiers probably had no idea whether Tasmania was a country or a disease." **"Swaddy"** (a private) descends from an eighteenth-century word for "a country lout." **"Squarehead"** is a German.
*Candidate application:* Nicknames follow fixed rules by surname, region or rank, and the "official" nickname is the one soldiers refuse (Tommy). The editors' ethnic-stereotype note (Spud) is a period English attitude documented, not endorsed.

**[E12b-26]. Bayonets, cookers and balloons: household names for equipment.** (PW-08, glossary: "Sausage" L6486-6488; "Tin-opener" L7121; "Toasting Fork" L7126-7128; "Tooth-pick" L7168; "Toffee Apples" L7130-7132; "Tommy Cooker" L7156-7160; "Tanks" L6993-7001; "Torpedo" L7179-7182)
The bayonet has three facetious names, **tin-opener**, **toothpick** and **toasting fork** (the last because it "was often used for toasting bread"); trench-mortar bombs are **toffee apples** ("from the haft, like the skewer in a toffee-apple"); the observation balloon is a **sausage**; the small oil stove the army distributed when regular cooking could not continue is the **Tommy cooker** ("producing a very little heat very slowly"). The **tank** was so named "as a reference of innocent appearance, so that the enemy should not, through spies, anticipate their appearance in September 1916"; male tanks carried "six-pounder guns," female tanks "machine guns" (the editors' gloss).
*Candidate application:* The household-names pattern from E12b-3 continues: the weapon of close killing is named for a kitchen tool. This is a candidate pattern for how a unit (including robots) names its own tools.

### C. Glossary, T through Z (L7,184-7,646)

**[E12b-27]. "Wangle," "win," "touch," "up there you want it," "remember there's a war on": petty privilege as a semi-official craft.** (PW-08, glossary: "Touch" L7185-7188; "Touch Out" L7202-7204; "Up There" L7274-7279; "Wangle" L7379-7390; "Wangler" L7393-7394; "War On" L7402-7405; "Win" L7500-7502; "Weigh Up" L7427-7431)
**Wangle** is "to procure goods or an advantage of some kind illicitly but without punishment, by the exercise of cunning, moral pressure, blackmail or bribery," and the editors write: "Almost everything could be wangled in the Army by certain people, from a 'sleeping-out' pass in barracks to promotion. In fact, wangling became semi-official. A man who had lost say an entrenching tool or a blanket would not be punished, but would be told openly by his platoon commander to wangle or scrounge (cf.) another." A **wangler** is "one who habitually obtained privileges which he had not earned." **"Up there you want it"** (the speaker taps the forehead; "want" means "lack") was "an expression of pride ... to indicate intellectual superiority after one had 'wangled' extra leave or a cushy job." **"To touch" a superior** is "to ask for it ingratiatingly at a suitable moment" (example: "Wait till the canteen's open, and I'll touch the S.M. for a pass"); **"touch out"** is a stroke of good fortune. **"Win"** is "to acquire unlawfully," with the example "Won it from an Aussie when he wasn't looking" (a fountain pen taken from an Australian; the entry does not say who is speaking). **"Remember there's a war on"** (an authority's line) meant "Don't waste time; don't be frivolous; let's get back to our real job," and was "A popular admonition to scroungers (cf.) and gossips." **"To weigh up"** a man is "to estimate at the proper value and to be in a position to master the man or the situation," often in the passive: "The S.M.'s got you weighed up, my lad."
*Candidate application:* The documented norm is that a platoon officer may sanction replacement theft in the open, a tolerance structure that makes "wangling" a competence, not a crime, and a sign of cleverness to be displayed with a tap on the forehead.

**[E12b-28]. "West": the one euphemism the editors say reveals feeling.** (PW-08, glossary: "West" L7434-7451)
"To go west was the most popular euphemism in the Army for 'to be killed.'" The examples are "Oh, Bill went west at Ginchy" and a second sentence in which the speaker calls the dead man "poor b——" (blanked in the text). The editors claim it was "one of the few expressions which revealed the suppressed emotion of the soldier, and his secret sense of the tragedy in which he was caught," and give three origin theories: the setting sun (Weekley); American pioneer days, "when to go further west into unexplored country often meant death at the hands of Indians"; and thieves' slang for going to Tyburn, to be hanged. They add that the phrase "was also used trivially of things lost, stolen or strayed, e.g. 'My razor's gone west again.'"
*Candidate application:* A dead comrade's name is followed by a directional word, and the grief is carried by the small tag "poor" before an elided noun; the same word for a lost razor shows the scale-slide that lets the unit use one word for loss in general. The source calls the word revealing, not funny; it is the tender end of the euphemism scale.

**[E12b-29]. "Wind-up" and "windy": the license to talk about fear.** (PW-08, glossary: "Wind-Up" L7505-7527; "Windy" L7530-7538)
**Wind-up** is "fear," "used with *have*, not *feel*," and "to have the wind-up implied no disgrace, and could be mentioned casually in conversation, although usually in the past tense." The editors think it a wartime creation and offer two guesses at an origin: the end of the last hope of transported felons ("the ship could sail"), or the throat sensations of extreme fear resembling a baby's "wind." The verb phrase **"to put the wind up"** means to frighten ("Those minnies fairly put the wind up me"). **Windy** is the adjective: "apprehensive of personal danger or trouble," but "unlike that term could be used of others in a derogatory sense, e.g. 'He's too windy to stir out of his dug-out,'" and was "much more freely transferred to the uses of ordinary routine," e.g. "He's windy of going out without a pass."
*Candidate application:* A precise rule of license: the speaker may admit fear in the past tense and casually; fear in another man in the present is an insult; and fear spreads from shells to minor rule-breaking in the word's drift. A veteran's tone about his own old fear can be easy while his judgment of another's present fear is not.

**[E12b-30]. Exclamations of disbelief and delight: "what a hope!", "what about...", "you bet," "trez beans," "umpteen."** (PW-08, glossary: "What Hopes!" L7479-7484; "What About" L7462-7476; "You Bet" L7608-7612; "Trez Beans" L7242-7246; "Umpteen" L7258-7259; "Wash-Out" L7407-7419)
**"What hopes!"** was "a retort expressing utter disbelief in some promise or prophecy of a future good (or not so bad as at present) time," with the variation "What a hope you've got!" for a man confident of getting an advantage. **"What about..."** is "a peculiar idiom capable, according to the inflexion of the voice, of conveying many meanings," the examples including "What about the poor bleedin' sentries?," meaning "Don't forget those who are absent in sharing out rations." **"You bet"** is "almost a form of oath," its example the question "Would you like to get your own back on the Q.M.?" answered "You bet I would!" **"Trez beans"** is "a facetious and deliberate corruption of 'très bien,'" a general exclamation of pleasure ("My word, it's trez beans here"). **"Wash-out"** is to cancel ("Wash out that order for early parade") and, as a noun, "a failure, a disaster or an incompetent person": "This raid's going to be another wash-out," "The sergeant's a fair wash-out with a spade."
*Candidate application:* A flat verbal tool for puncturing a promise is part of the standard kit, as is an all-purpose word for "a failure" that is applied to raids and sergeants alike.

**[E12b-31]. Weapons and sounds the infantry disliked: trench mortars, whizz-bangs, Very lights, the "wonky" Lewis gun, the wooden track, the umbrella man.** (PW-08, glossary: "Trench Mortar" L7225-7240; "Whizz-Bang" L7489-7497; "Very Light" L7315-7333; "Wonky" L7557-7559; "Wooden Track" L7574-7578; "Town Major" L7207-7216; "Whippet" L7486-7487)
The **trench mortar** (also "footballs," "flying pigs," "toffee apples") is described as hurling bombs "quite slowly and visibly ... which spun unevenly in flight," and "caused as much trouble for their own infantry as for the enemy's, and were everywhere unpopular. When a T.M. battery had fired a few shots it departed with speed, and the infantry remained to await the inevitable retaliation from the enemy artillery." The **whizz-bang** "was applied to the shell only as a hostile explosion"; because of "short range and low trajectory, whizz-bangs arrived as soon, if not sooner, than you heard them." The **Very light** (flare) on a small parachute "illuminat[ed] the earth beneath for what seemed a geological age, during which no one dared move." **"Wonky"** (defective), with the example "The Lewis gun's wonky again," and the editors' parenthesis "(They always were.)" The **wooden track** was "unsheltered and extremely visible," so "most wooden tracks were heavily shelled and needed constant repair." The **Town Major** entry holds a single anecdote: "At one period Arras possessed a Town Major of eccentric character who used to stroll through the streets under shell-fire, serenely confident beneath the shade of an umbrella."
*Candidate application:* Resentment is directed at the friendly weapon that provokes retaliation, not just the enemy's; and the list of what could be seen or heard before it arrived is the list the infantry used to rank dangers. The umbrella man is a documented type of the officer eccentric.

**[E12b-32]. Civilian gifts and civilian stories: the Waacs' rumors, "Woodbine," "Yank," the YMCA, and "Wipers."** (PW-08, glossary: "W.A.A.C." L7345-7371; "Woodbine" L7561-7568; "Yank" L7599-7602; "Y.M." L7604-7605; "Ypres" L7615-7636; "Waza" L7422-7424; "War Babies" L7397-7399; "Wings" L7540-7543; "Winick" L7546-7548)
The editors record the rumor-mill around the women's corps: "Civilians were always ready to spread scandal about Waacs; ... most soldiers were ready to invent and pass on ribald stories, just as boys do about girls' schools," and "nine hundred and ninety-nine lurid stories about them can be entirely discredited" (the rest of the entry is summarized, see the register). **"Woodbine"** (a cheap cigarette) was imagined by "kind-hearted civilians" to be loved by all soldiers: "packets were often dropped on hospital beds—the poor patients groaning under the bedclothes. A most vile incense to offer to the god of slaughter." **"Yank"** was applied "by the British to all Americans, not always politely." **"Wipers"** gets an unusual pronunciation story: "According to the newspapers, the British soldier pronounced this word Wypers. He may have done so in 1914 or early 1915. After that, the majority of the troops seemed to harbor a suspicion that French was not properly pronounced by the same rules as English," and they used "Eepray" and "Eeps," while it is "known that Wipers ... was the pronunciation invented by Lord French, or someone else of high rank, at the very outset of the War" (the Wipers Times of PW-01 takes its name from this). **"Waza"** (a Cairo quarter): "The Battles of the Waza were two Australian brushes with the police in 1915." The **"winick"** entry gives "mad or foolish," from a Lancashire asylum town. "War babies" were "the illegitimate children with which, on the outbreak of war, sensational newspapers predicted England would be crowded" (summarized).
*Candidate application:* The gap between what civilians and newspapers thought soldiers liked or did, and what the soldiers did, is a rich seam: the press says Wypers while the men say Eeps.

### D. The editors' "Note to the Glossary" (L7,655-7,795)

**[E12b-33]. How slang is made, by the editors' own account: borrowed sources, irony of understatement, and the body's exhilaration.** (PW-08, "Note to the Glossary," L7739-7795)
The editors state the scale: "six hundred references" in the glossary, of which "only a small portion ... are pure creations out of nothing" (examples given: *wind-up, oojah-cum-pivvy, stunt, napoo, posh*); "the majority are common words adapted or extended in meaning. Thus *eye-wash*, *landowner* and *packet* each became charged with new content far removed from the original reference." They list three "main characteristics." **First, wide sources**: "Dialect and urban (especially Cockney) slang is resuscitated; and the Old Army slang draws upon Hindustani, Persian and Arabic"; French place and phrase names are "transformed into something neither English nor French: as in *Cumsah* and *Etapps*"; and "out of the forgotten depositories of eighteenth-century slang, an odd word or two turns up refurbished, like *binge* and *clink*." **Second, irony**, which is "frequent, almost prevalent. It is the typically English irony of understatement and ridicule. Some of the terror disappeared, together with the pomp, from war and military glory, when the soldier decided to call his steel-helmet a Tin-hat, his bayonet a Tooth-pick, his entrenching-tool handle a Piggy-stick, and a murderous bombardment a Strafe or Hate." **Third, bodily exhilaration**: "the exhilaration which most men felt in the hardening of their bodies and the sharpening of their physical faculties is clearly to be traced in the imagery, the onomatopoeia and the sensuous fidelity of many of these words" (examples: *brass-hat*, which "conveys so vivid an image of a general's resplendent cap that the mind easily sketches in the petulant wine-dark face beneath it"; *blimp*, "exactly the shape and the resilient flabbiness of the airship"; *coal-box*; and *dug-out*, which "is suggested all the impetus of the spade-edge and the stiff resistance of the clay"). On fear: "Physical fear became a commonplace of routine, but a commonplace that had to be conquered by private resolution and outward derision. It was made a guy, and Wind-up was the placard pinned upon it."
*Candidate application:* A compact, period-authored mechanism list that matches the entries: slang is mostly old words given new loads (landowner, packet), irony works by shrinking the thing (tin-hat, tooth-pick), and the specific work of "outward derision" is to turn fear into a figure of fun. The "guy" image (an effigy mocked in public) is the editors' own.
*Caution:* The editors' reading (an English-national irony, an "exhilaration" in the hardened body) is a 1930 literary judgment; it is a participant's thesis, not a measurement.

**[E12b-34]. The editors' frame: a million dead, a lost "cocksure-innocent" youth, and the daisies that "couple" with "push up."** (PW-08, "Note to the Glossary," L7655-7737)
The note opens with figures: "Seven million men before the end served in the Army, Navy or Auxiliary Forces; one million of them were killed, many millions were wounded at least once," and over a million women served as clerks, cooks, waitresses and nurses. The editors argue the experience altered the national character and that no one afterward would be "as innocent, as cocksure-innocent, as the youth which trooped into the World War of 1914 expecting something between a pageant-crusade and an imperialist picnic." Language, they say, had to respond: "The surface, at least, of all emotions must late or soon be expressed in words, for our own comfort." They illustrate English metaphor with one of the book's best examples: "Push up will couple with the ineligible daisies, and so form a new phrase, a new metaphor, and enrich the English consciousness by an illuminating flash of sardonic humor." They also describe the arrival of new recruits who "reached out eagerly for the traditional vocabulary of the old Army" (*dodging the column, rooti, swinging the lead, on the jildi*), "just as a new boy at school is all ears for the niceties of its special slang"; then the war "grew to vast proportions and assumed horrible aspects beyond the knowledge and capacity of the old Army. Another vocabulary was needed, and day by day it was supplied by inventive spirits responding to the onslaughts of circumstance."
*Candidate application:* A two-layer model of a veteran's speech: an inherited peacetime service layer (pride in the old words) and a war-made layer coined by "inventive spirits" in response to what the old layer could not name. 

### E. Appendix A: "Chants and Sayings" (L7,857-8,142)

**[E12b-35]. Catch phrases: wit ready-made, from the music-hall to the trench.** (PW-08, Appendix A, L7861-7892)
The appendix (unsigned; the editors' prose) argues that catch phrases "have always been popular; for wit, however much desired or striven for, does not rise to everyone's lips pat to the occasion," and that "an important function of the Victorian and Edwardian music-hall was to furnish phrases suitable for use in all the emergencies of life, and recognizable at once as humor." Pre-war examples: "Keep your hair on!", "Chase me, girls!", "Does your mother want a rabbit?", "Get your hair cut!", "Ginger, you're barmy!", "Have a banana!"; their "requisites ... were homeliness and directness, for ready-made wit always tends to become repartee." "When the War broke out the new armies subsisted for a time on what catch phrases the music-halls produced," "How's your father?" being "one of the most popular, turned to all sorts of ribald, ridiculous and heroic uses"; the editors add that this was "the last utterance of at least one dandified but efficient subaltern, dying of stomach wounds" (summarized only: a catch phrase as a dying officer's last words). "But as time went on the soldier began to produce his own sayings, even as he produced his own songs, to help him through his cataclysmic days."
*Candidate application:* Humor in the first months is borrowed (civilian music-hall tags); the unit's own is made later, from its own disillusion. A defector would carry both layers.

**[E12b-36]. Recruiting slogans turned back on their senders: "Kitchener wants you," "Remember Belgium," "What did you do," "Thank Gawd we've got a navy," "Are we downhearted? Yes!"** (PW-08, Appendix A, L7893-7946)
The editors record a systematic inversion. **"Kitchener Wants You!"** "became a method of telling a man he was selected for some especially disgusting, arduous or dangerous duty." **"Remember Belgium!"** "was heard with ironic and bitter intonations in the muddy wastes of the Salient," with the added tag "As if I'm ever likely to forget the bloody place!" **"What Did You Do in the Great War, Daddy?"** was "repeated scathingly in times of distress and misery" (the editors add an aside that "the post-war infant displays not the slightest interest in Daddy's martial past ... War-books have wrecked the private market in anecdotes"). **"Thank Gawd, we've got a navy!"**, "always 'Gawd,' even with north-country troops, to indicate the burlesque of the original sentiment," "was called into use whenever the incompetence of authority became more manifest than usual." **"Are we downhearted?—No!"** "soon became the vehement—Are we downhearted?—Yes! But this was intended as a humorous comment," and it was sometimes expanded into a call-and-response: "Are we downhearted? / No! / Then you damn (or bloody) soon will be!"
*Candidate application:* The recruitment poster is raw material for the soldier's irony; the cheerful call-and-response is inverted to its opposite and then expanded into a threat. A defector's attitude to his institution's slogans could draw on this pattern.

**[E12b-37]. The Staff as gods, the sergeant as visible and human: "Curse you, Jack, I'm all right," "Kiss me, sergeant."** (PW-08, Appendix A, L7947-7987)
Two sayings were "directed against the private soldier's superiors." The general one, **"Curse you, Jack, I'm all right!"** (the first word is a polite substitute), "typified concisely the implied and the often explicit arrogance of many senior officers toward the ranks" and was "a literal and true interpretation of the expression to be seen on the sleek faces of staff officers perched on their sleek horses or reclining in their sleek motor-cars (which, whenever possible, bespattered marching infantry with mud)." The editors continue: "To the private soldier the Staff were much what the capricious gods must have been to the Greek and Trojan infantrymen. They were known only by occasional glimpses (out of the line) and by the effects of their temperamental, inhuman and rarely intelligent fiats. The platoon-sergeant, whatever his defects, was visible and human. He might be fussy, easily scared and ill-tempered; but when he had taken too big a share of the rations and the next best dug-out to the officers', he had reached the limit of his power for evil. Besides, there were thousands of really good sergeants, respected and liked." Whether the privates shouted "Some say 'Good old sergeant!' / Others say '[blank] the old sergeant!'" depended on the man's conduct, "with rancor or with quite affectionate jocularity." **"Kiss me, sergeant!"** was "a common piece of facetiousness, uttered after a sergeant had been more than usually officious; and often in camps, to the orderly sergeant after he had commanded 'Lights out!'"
*Candidate application:* Distance, not rank, is what makes authority mythic: the nearest superior is a person with a limited reach, and teasing him with an affectionate impossible request is a documented register.

**[E12b-38]. Weather, duration, rumor, grumbling and seniority: "Send it down, David," "Roll on, Duration," "Tell me the old, old story," "Before you come up!"** (PW-08, Appendix A, L7988-8029)
**"Send it down, David!"**: "The Biblical David, for some unknown reason, was held responsible for rainfall, and drenched, shivering troops would apostrophize the dripping skies." **"Roll on, Duration!"** expressed "general fed-upedness and exasperation"; the 1914-15 volunteers enlisted for "four years or the duration of the war, whichever shall be longer," so the cry meant "May the end of the War come quickly and deliver us from these miseries!" **"Tell me the old, old story"** (the first line of a Nonconformist hymn) was sung at "all rumors of good times to come and promises from authority." **"What do you want?—Jam on it?"** was "the favorite reprimand" for chronic grousers (E12b-9) but "used just as often ironically, when extra fatigues or working parties took the place of a promised rest." **"Before you come up!"** was the "classic phrase" for repartee: "I enlisted before you, or I've seen more active service than you. The only answer possible was a citation of actual service and experience, or a fight."
*Candidate application:* The last item is a precise documented rule of license: seniority of service is a trump card in argument, answerable only by a counter-claim of service or a fight. Hymn lines as the response to a promise are a documented way of signaling disbelief.

**[E12b-39]. Shouted chants for amusement: mother jokes, "a soldier's farewell," "that's the stuff to give the troops," the crushing retort, and two long bathos routines.** (PW-08, Appendix A, L8031-8139)
The editors describe chants "shouted aloud on the march or in any place where soldiers congregated 'off parade,' purely in order to arouse amusement. The appreciative laugh never failed." The **mother jokes** include "Remember I'm your mother and get up those stairs!" (a nursery memory) and the famous opening of a letter home, "Dear Mother, I am sending you ten shillings—but not this week," which "retained to the end of the War its power of amusing simple men" (the phrase is the editors', in their period tone). **"You may have broke your mother's heart—you won't break mine!"** was "originally a stock phrase of drill-sergeants taking recruits in hand," "intended both to intimidate and to reassure the new soldiers that the tyrant of the barrack-square was human underneath," and "a jest widely appreciated and often repeated by privates." **"A Soldier's Farewell"** was "originally invective—the farewell being in full, 'Good-bye and you!'" but was chiefly "a jocular exclamation made very loudly for the amusement of others." **"That's the stuff to give the troops"** was heard "whenever rations or billets, rum or any other creature comfort turned out better or more plentiful than might have been expected," and in a variant "would often be shouted approvingly to artillery in action." The **retort** to an inspecting officer: a snatch dramatizing the officer finding an unshaven man who "was more than a match for him": "Ah, ah! no bloody shave this morning? / Ah, ah! no bloody razor!" "The headlong descent from the heroic strain" is a stock humor: the mock title "Napoleon's Greeting to his Troops—" with the punchline "Good morning, troops." The **two longest** chants were "also the best": (1) a rhymed metrical routine: "To-day's my daughter's wedding day, / Ten thousand pounds I'll give away (Three cheers!). / On second thoughts I think it best / To put it away in the old oak chest," then the crowd's "Yer mingy bastard! Chuck him out! etc."; (2) a melodrama exchange: "Help, help, there's a woman overboard! / Who will save her? / I will! / Who are you? / Barnacle Bill the sailor, just returned from sea," upon which "the whole assembly broke into the whistling of a hornpipe."
*Candidate application:* Documented performance forms: the repeated joke that "never failed," the call-and-response in which the crowd supplies the final insult (the mingy-benefactor routine), and the sergeant's reassuring tyranny. A humor culture is partly a repertoire of old routines, retold for the pleasure of the telling.

### F. Appendix B: "Bugle Calls" (L8,145-8,312)

**[E12b-40]. Words fitted to bugle calls: the day's routine mocked in verse.** (PW-08, Appendix B, L8151-8281)
The editors note that in the line "bugle calls, together with ceremonies and 'compliments,' were dispensed with," while in billets, rest camps and at the Base they were in daily use. The unofficial words: **Reveille** ("Get out of bed! / Get out of bed! / You lazy blighters: / I feel sorry for you I do"), after which a light sleeper would murmur it "before the Corporal found his boots and threw them at some snoring blanket-covered head"; **Quarter Dress** ("A quarter of an hour to do it in!"); **Fall in** ("Fall in A! / Fall in B! / Fall in every Companee!"); the **cookhouse** call ("Come to the Cookhouse door, boys ... When you see the Sergeant-Cook smile"); the dry-rations call ("How can I draw rations / When I'm not the orderly man?"); **Officers' Mess**, which went to "a very gross chant indeed," given in an amended form: "Officers' wives / Get puddens and pies, / A sergeant's wife gets skilly, / But a private's wife gets nothing at all / To fill her empty belly" (the editors observe that "all these ditties are the invention of the pre-War Regular Army, as this reference to wives 'on the strength' ... clearly shows"); the daily **Post** ("Letters from Lousy Lou, / Letters for you / And letters for me"); the **Defaulters** call ("You can be a defaulter / As long as you like / As long as you answer your name"); **Pay Parade** ("Fall in for pay, boys, / Fall in for pay. / You've bloody-well earned, boys, / Your shilling a day," sometimes varied to "Swinging the lead, boys ... Always remember / To work your head"); and **Retreat** ("You won't go to Heaven / When you die, Mary Ann").
*Candidate application:* The day's schedule is a set of tunes everyone hears, so the humor fitted to it is communal by design, and class (the officers' wives versus the privates' wives) is carried by a bugle call. A defector may still hum or hear a call in his head years later.

**[E12b-41]. What soldiers would not say: the Last Post was never given words.** (PW-08, Appendix B, L8295-8311)
The editors quote C. E. Montague's *Disenchantment* on the Last Post as "that most lovely and melancholy of calls, the noble death of each day's life," and add: "It was so beautiful and so moving that no one ever profaned it with words. Only here and there at the sustained repetition of the two notes which precede the concluding flourish, a man would murmur, quietly as if to himself, 'Lights out! Lights out!' and fall to sleep with the tune of it still echoing slumberously in his ears." The Retreat call (L8282-8292), by contrast, has a joke ("You won't go to Heaven / When you die, Mary Ann").
*Candidate application:* A source-documented boundary of the comic: one call in the day was left unprofaned, and the only words ever fitted to it were a quiet functional murmur. Humor is bracketed from the ritual of ending the day.

### G. "For the second edition": prefatory note and postscript songs (L8,323-end of my range)

**[E12b-42]. The second-edition prefatory note: how hard the text was to establish, and the *Wipers Times* link.** (PW-08, "For the Second Edition: Prefatory," L8329-8369)
The second edition (dated July 28, 1930) is "carefully revised and considerably augmented": two stanzas added to each of *Apres la Guerre* and *Raining*, a variant of *I've Lost My Rifle and Bayonet*, and a postscript with "ten songs (and a significant variant opening stanza to another); a hundred new words ... and further notes on fourteen others; many 'items' to Chants and Sayings; and three bugle-calls." The editors say the task was "infinitely more difficult than is commonly supposed" because "the mere collecting and sifting of the songs has proved very exacting, while the establishing of 'a sound text' has demanded patience and an assiduous discrimination, for there is no written authority in most instances." A footnote says that many of the new words "are omitted in 'Fraser & Gibbons,' large though that dictionary be. Some of these words are added to assist the civilian to a full enjoyment of *The Wipers Times*." Thanks go to J. B. Priestley and others for suggestions.
*Candidate application:* The editors themselves flag that these texts were oral, variant and unfixed, which is the proper caution for treating any one song text as canonical. 
*Cross-use:* Direct link to the sibling sources PW-01/PW-02 (the Wipers Times).

### H. Postscript songs (L8,375-8,683)

**[E12b-43]. Two song variants about money and kit: "Madame, I've got money to spend" and "I've lost my rifle and bayonet."** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8378-8401)
A variant opening stanza of *Madame, Have You...* (the first-half song of the estaminet) begins "Madame, I've got money to spend, / Cing sous, cing sous" (OCR "cing" for "cinq"); its remaining lines carry an innuendo about the proprietress's daughter, summarized here, not quoted (see the register). Mechanism: the "money to spend" is a boast whose refrain names the amount (five sous), so the boast is its own deflation. The famous *I've Lost My Rifle and Bayonet* gets a variant ending the editors call "very effective": "I've lost my rations and greatcoat, / I've lost my button-stick too; / I've lost my hold-all: and now I've got / Sergeant, what shall I do?" Mechanism: an escalating inventory of lost kit, then the helpless child-like appeal to the sergeant; the song's target is the regime of kit accountability (cf. E12b-16, where a platoon commander tells the loser to wangle a replacement).
*Candidate application:* The lost-kit refrain is a documented comic form in which the soldier performs helplessness before the nearest authority; the real-life answer (scrounge a replacement) is known to every singer, which is part of the joke.

**[E12b-44]. Songs of ironic contentment: "Oh what a life, living in a trench" and "I like to hear the news from the Dardanelles."** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8413-8449)
The editors note that the first, a marching song, "has a neater phrasing than most; there is certainly no folk nor Regular Army influence here": "Oh what a life, living in a trench, / Oh what a life, fighting for the French; / We haven't got a wife or a nice little wench, / But we're all quite happy in an old French trench." The second, "sung slowly and dolefully," is a "billets" song composed "in the summer or autumn of 1915" and tinkered with in 1916, a list of things the singer claims to like: "I like to hear the whistle of the Allyman's shells. / I like to hear the rifle-fire, / I like to see the blinking Allymans retire ... I like to hear the click-click of the pick and spade. / (The French, they are no bon.) / Look out, look out, the gas clouds are coming: / Go and get your respirator on." The editors judge it "banal generalization" in comparison to what follows (E12b-45). Mechanism: pretended contentment (the "all quite happy" claim) and a feigned taste for what is feared; the sudden gas warning punctures the list.
*Candidate application:* The pretended-contentment song is a documented genre; the editors rank it as weaker than the satirical-and-particular type, which tells a reader which songs they thought showed the soldiers at their best.

**[E12b-45]. "Miles behind the lines": a satire of a named type, adapted by each unit.** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8451-8464)
The editors call it "a totally different sort of song, satirical and particular (with variations as required by various units)." The sergeant-major refrain: "Miles behind the lines, / We've got a sergeant-major, / Who's never seen a gun; / He's mentioned in despatches / For drinking privates' rum, / And when he sees old Jerry / You should see the b——r run / Miles behind the lines!" Mechanism: the honor of being "mentioned in despatches" is turned into a reward for stealing rum; the final line (the blanked word is the text's own) pictures a brave-seeming man running at the sight of the enemy. Target: a rear-area sergeant-major who has never been in the line. The song is adapted per unit, so the target's name or rank changes while the structure stays.
*Candidate application:* A template song with a slot for "our" sergeant-major is a documented form: shared structure, local target.

**[E12b-46]. "Standing on the firestep": a song the editors say was actually composed by soldiers.** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8467-8496)
The editors say it "goes to two-four time, as we know from all we possess of the score (the first four lines), and ... is one of the few songs actually composed by soldiers." The verses: "Standing on the firestep at half-past nine, / Oh! Gorblimey, aint it cold! / Waiting for the whistle that the sergeant-major blows, / Oh! Gorblimey, aint it cold! // Nothing to drink but water, / No more rum from the Quarter, / We don't get half of what we oughter! / Oh! Gorblimey, aint it cold!" and a second chorus: "He drinks it all himself and gives us water." The editors add performance directions: "(with intensity)," then "(almost whispering)," then "(now completely frozen)." Mechanism: the weather as a sustained complaint; the refrain's volume falls as the cold deepens (the performers act out freezing); the rum grievance names the Quartermaster as thief, a familiar target (E12b-7).
*Candidate application:* The three performance directions show the song as a staged joke: the singers lowered the volume to simulate frostbite. A documented case of a shared complaint with a mimed body in it.

**[E12b-47]. "Has everybody got his iron rations?": a parody of a sentimental hymn-song aimed at red tape.** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8499-8550)
Sung "to the tune of *The Lost Chord*"; the editors call it "more 'literary'": "the satire, too, is against red tape and not against the wide-felt grievance of the rank and file at what they considered, often rightly, as the injustice of 'the Quarter Bloke.'" The lines: "Has everybody got his iron rations, / His bully, his biscuits and his tea? ... For he can't go to war without them, / In this war with Germany"; "We've only got to march fifty kilos, / And out of it we mustn't have a drink, / And we must be fresh when we get there, / As we always are—I don't think!"; and "Has everybody got his clean socks on? / Has everybody washed his feet? / Is everybody letting his mustache grow?— / Not very long but neat. / For these are the things that count most / In the war with Germany." The editors date it to 1916 "since in that year, for a while, it was deemed to be a crime to shave the upper lip." They also quote J. B. Priestley, who regretted two songs the first edition had missed: "that fine anti-heroic and ironical chorus which demands that everybody, including the women and the boys' brigade, shall be sent out to the Front except the singers" (the editors note that this has "now found: see p. 66," a first-half song) and a long one "which tells you what the private, corporal, sergeant, and other ranks said, and has the constant refrain, 'But there's none so rare, / As can compare, / With Kitchener's New Ar-mee.'" (a music-hall song).
*Candidate application:* The tune's solemnity ("The Lost Chord") is the vehicle; the list of the things that "count most / In the war with Germany" is the bathos. A useful example of the editors distinguishing two grievance targets (red tape and the thieving Quarter Bloke) and judging songs by which one they hit.

**[E12b-48]. "His comrades don't believe him" and "Don't go up the Line, Daddy": short and direct parodies.** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8568-8627)
"His comrades don't believe him, / His comrades don't believe him, / His comrades don't believe him, / He's such a liar" was "fairly bellowed" to the tune of *In and Out the Window*, "both on the march and in billets, often in humorously-caustic reply to an unusually picturesque story." The much longer *Don't Go up the Line, Daddy*, to the tune of *Don't Go down the Mine, Daddy*, "represents a parody of that song, like most Army parodies, very direct, very objective": "Don't go up the Line, Daddy, / Don't go up the Line, / Shells are coming over / Four at a time ... Some are four-point-two, Daddy, / Some are five-point-nine ... The mine goes up at nine, Daddy, / The mine goes up at nine." Mechanism: the child's plea of the original (a sentimental song of a child begging a father not to go down a coal mine) is kept as a refrain while the specific verses become a technical briefing of shell calibers and a timed mine detonation, so the plea is reduced to a list of facts. 
*Candidate application:* A singer pleads in the voice of a child while giving the artillery order of battle; the refrain is the sentiment, the verses are the intelligence. Also a short song that works as a "liar" tag for an exaggerating comrade, a documented form of peer correction (cf. E12b-22).

**[E12b-49]. "We pushed the damper in" (folk futility), "The roses round the door" (with the interpolated "without a shirt"), "Poor Kaiser Bill," and "Rogerum."** (PW-08, Postscript "Songs," L8633-8683)
*We Pushed the Damper In* is described as "very different and probably pre-War of the folk-song variety" and sung "to an air not unlike that of Bonnie Dundee": "Oh, we pushed the damper in, / Yes, we pushed the damper in, / And the smoke went up the chimney just the same. / So we pulled the damper out, / Yes, we pulled the damper out, / And the smoke went up the chimney just the same. / So we pushed the damper in ... and so on, interminably." Mechanism: a futility loop, an action and its reversal making no difference, repeated "interminably"; the editors give no commentary on what it says about the war. *The Roses round the Door* was "one of the most popular marching songs of late 1916 and early 1917"; the editors include it only for "a special effect," "second voices chiming in with the small symphonic movement, 'without a shirt,'" after lines of the sentimental text ("The roses round the door / (without a shirt) pp / Make me love mother more ... To my home in Tennessee / (Without a Shirt) ff"). Mechanism: a sentimental music-hall song is undercut line by line by a deadpan tag stating the singers' real condition (the editors do not explain the tag, so its exact meaning is not asserted here; the effect is clear). A 1914 marching song, sung "until about April 1915": "Poor Kaiser Bill is feeling ill, / The Crown Prince he's gone barmy, / And we don't care a [blank] / For old Von Kluck / And all his bleeding great army" (the blank is in the text). *Rogerum* is "a favorite," "was pre-War and partly civilian," and is "now the stock anthem of Toc H," with no text printed.
*Candidate application:* Early-war contempt for named enemy leaders gives way to more oblique irony; the interpolated-tag technique shows how the unit could sabotage a sentimental song with a chorus-line.

### I. Postscript glossary (L8,689-9,350)

The postscript glossary ("a hundred new words") is headed by the note that an asterisk means "technical, official, or semi-official" and a dagger means the word is also in the main glossary. The most revealing entries are grouped below.

**[E12b-50]. What soldiers would not say, and what they said instead: blasphemy, "tit-for-tat," and the intensifying euphemism.** (PW-08, postscript glossary: "Angels of Christ" L8703-8706; "Tit-For" L9247-9255; "Sock" L9223-9224; "Trip Wire" L9299-9301)
The entry for the Army Ordnance Corps nickname **"Angels of Christ"** (the OCR garbles the headword; the text reads "Ancets or Curist") is used by the editors for a general statement: "Blasphemy was very rarely employed in the soldiers' terminology, however much they may have blasphemed (for the most part technically but not morally) in their 'bad language.'" The entry **"tit-for"** records a euphemistic assent: the phrase "tit-for-tat" was "an unexpected euphemism to express enthusiastic assent: too bloody true," with two sample exchanges in which a man agrees with an insulting remark about the sergeant-major or with "We're for it to-night mate" by saying "Tit-for-tat!" The editors note: "Strangely enough, the evasion of the barred word led to additional emphasis: instead of saying 'too bloody true' or its euphemism, hard-bitten soldiers would often combine the two into 'tit-for-bloody-tat.'" **"Trip wire"** is "a frequent cause of strong language." **"Sock"** adds that "put the wood in the hole" was said when a man left a door open.
*Candidate application:* A precise boundary: soldiers swore a great deal but kept their slang names for institutions free of blasphemy; a euphemism meant to avoid an oath could become the intensifier. A veteran character's swearing can be technically coarse yet still avoid sacred names.

**[E12b-51]. Shell sizes, sound and fear: "five-nine," "four-two," "heavies," "dirt," "woolly bear," "Archie," "silent Percy" and the ambiguity of "wiped out."** (PW-08, postscript glossary: "Five Nine" L8899-8901; "Four Two" L8923-8925; "Heavy" L8975-8981; "Dirt" L8856; "Woolly Bear" L9328-9329; "Archie" L8709-8711; "Johnsons" L9008-9011; "Flammenwerfer" L8909-8912; "Wipe Out" L9540-9543; also main glossary "Rubber Gun" L6385)
The **five-nine** (5.9-inch) was "a much hated, very destructive German high-explosive shell"; the **four-two** was "an efficient German shell (and cannon), employed where it was foolish to send a 59 on a boy's errand" (the phrase "boy's errand" belongs to the editors). The **heavy** entry makes a fear argument: heavy guns, "owing to the shattering detonation, ... 'put the wind up the troops' far more than the amazingly swift 'whizz-bang' or '75' or the barking, vicious 18-pounder, even though the smaller shells came too quickly to be dodged, whereas one could judge the incidence of a 5-9 for example." Shells are "dirt" ("Jerry put over a lot of dirt last night"); the burst of a big H.E. shell is a **woolly bear**; the anti-aircraft gun is **Archie** (flagged as a feature of *The Wipers Times* No. 2, February 26, 1916, with the variant "Flaming Onion"); the **Johnsons** entry quotes a *Wipers Times* "advertisement" of February 12, 1916: "The Johnsons. A Shout. A Scream. A Roar." (the editors call it "a most apt description of their advent and explosion"); the long-range gun is a **rubber gun**, with the main-glossary definition "a big gun firing at a very long range" and the synonym **"silent Percy"** here (L9211). The **flame-thrower** (*Flammenwerfer*) is called by the editors "a devilish engine of war introduced in 1916," "very difficult to use." **"Wipe out"** means "to destroy a thing completely" and "'The battalion was wiped out' may mean 'to a man,' or 'almost to a man'" (L9540-9543, the P.P.S.). The **fixed rifle** (a sniper's rifle clamped to cover a given exposed point in the enemy trench) is summarized, not quoted (see the register).
*Candidate application:* A character could rank fear by acoustics: the shell that can be heard and judged is the most feared, the fast one cannot be dodged at all. "Wiped out" is a phrase the editors themselves mark as ambiguous in quantity, which fits a veteran's habit of questioning numbers.

**[E12b-52]. Turning official and journalistic words into jokes: "Jerry" origin, "frightfulness," "Der Tag," "offensive," "do one's bit," "toy-shop."** (PW-08, postscript glossary: "Jerry" L9002-9005; "Frightfulness" L8934-8937; "Der Tag" L8849-8853; "Do One's Bit" L8865-8866; "Dump" L8869-8872; "Mons Man" L9052-9053; "Offensive" L9527-9534; "Commandeer" L8836-8839)
The postscript revises the origin of **Jerry**: "possibly (even probably), however, the origin of the word lay in the appearance of a group of German tin-hats worn by their lessees: in outline they looked surprisingly like an array of chamber-pots, vulgarly known as 'jerries.'" (the editors' speculation; a household vessel as the enemy's name). **"Frightfulness"** was "originally German frightfulness and journalese; but adopted widely by officers, little by the ranks, to signify cruelty, tyranny, injustice, bullying, and cognate horrors. Sometimes used jocularly." **"Der Tag"** ("the Day") was "popularized by journalists; used by officers, often in derivative sense for any much-desired date or goal." **"Offensive"** was, besides "an attack on a large scale," a word "used by G.H.Q. in 1916-7 for a frame of mind that it desired to encourage in front-line troops in order to keep the enemy harassed. Enquiries were sent to commanding officers: did they consider themselves 'sufficiently offensive' that day. The word soon became a joke and—if one thought how those lived from whom it originated—an irritation." **"Do one's bit"** is "to enlist and, more especially, go to one of the fronts"; the **divisional dump** is jocularly "divisional toy-shop"; a **"Mons man"** is one of the original B.E.F., "popularized by journalists, who, however, got it from the troops"; **"commandeer"** is legitimate for an officer ("commandeering a billet") but, for a private, "bluffing himself regally into getting something by decisive 'command.'"
*Candidate application:* A pattern across the book: official or press phrases (Hun, Der Tag, offensive, do your bit, Kitchener wants you) enter soldier speech and come out as jokes about the institution; only a few (Mons man) travel the other way, from the troops to the press.

**[E12b-53]. The body and its institution: trench feet as a crime, "lousy as a cuckoo," "read a shirt," "hotpinc back," "ransom," "stick it."** (PW-08, postscript glossary: "Trench Feet" L9287-9289; "Trench Fever" L9295-9296; "Cuckoo" L8845-8847; "Read a Shirt" L9118-9119; "Louse-Trap" L9039; "Hotpinc Back" L8984-8985; "Hardening Squad" L8967-8968; "Ransow/Ransom" L9103-9104; "Stick It" L9227-9233)
**Trench feet**: "at one period it was a crime to get trench feet, but no humane commander enforced the penalty or even made the charge." **"Lousy as a cuckoo"**: "we got all cuckooed" means "we became lousy" (L8845-8847). **"Read a shirt"** is "Con a shirt for lice. The phrase, belonging to 1917-18, became generic for chat" (my gloss: *con* means to study closely). The woolen body-belt is a **louse-trap**. **"Hotpinc back"** (the OCR reading; the sense is "trying to avoid being cured of wound or sickness") names the dodge of a convalescent who delays recovery; the **hardening squad** is the group of "men being trained before returning to France after convalescence." The headword that the OCR renders **"Ransow"** (probably "Ransom") is "a recruit arriving just after a battle. A 1917-18 word," with no explanation of the metaphor. **"Stick it"** is "keep going, endure; e.g. ''E stuck it like a bleedin' 'ero'."
*Candidate application:* The institutional absurdity (a disease declared a crime by order and never charged) and the replacement soldier named by the battle he has missed are two documented ways the language tracked the system. The sample sentence about a man who stuck it like a hero places endurance, not valor, as the praised trait.

**[E12b-54]. Mockery of the institution and the self: the ceremonial raspberry, "the troops," mustaches, "sergeant-major" tea, "taped," "liver scraping," "come the acid," "come unstuck."** (PW-08, postscript glossary: "A Raspberry" L9108-9112; "Troops, The" L9304-9307; "Charlie Chaplin" L8777-8785; "Sergeant-Major" L9201-9208; "Taped" L9242-9245; "Wants His Liver Scraping" L9320-9322; "Come the Acid" L8826-8827; "Come Unstuck" L8830-8833; "On the Square" L9078-9079; "Bent" L8743; "Y'Ibn" L9348-9350)
The most vivid is **"a raspberry"** (blowing through the closed fist in contempt): "We have heard of a soldier who, every time that he went out of camp, turned round and solemnly—almost ceremonially, certainly ceremoniously—gave the Army a raspberry." **"The troops"** is "a semi-jocular form of referring to oneself; used instead of I, me; e.g. 'the troops could do with a leave', slang for a period of leave, furlough. Cf. the *we* of Royalty and newspaper editors." Mustaches carried rank: the **Charlie Chaplin** (a half-inch) was worn by officers, the **toothbrush** mustache "about twice the length" was "the 'property' of the rank and file, who would probably have been crimed for dumb insolence if they had dared to usurp the privilege residing in a Charlie Chaplin"; "only in 1916 that the men had to wear a mous[tache]" (the OCR line is cut off here and the remainder of the sentence, that officers "were intermittently encouraged" to do likewise, is partly lost). **Tea** was called **"sergeant-major"**: "on the theory or assumption that both officers' mess and the ranks' cook-house made tea for the S.M. whenever he wanted it. At first, *sergeant-major* was the term reserved for specially good and strong brews of tea; but afterwards it came to mean any cup or dixie of tea drunk between meals." **"Taped"** serves two masters: "'Jerry has us taped,' i.e. has the range; 'The S.M.'s got you taped,' i.e. weighed-up" (cf. "weigh up," E12b-27). A bad-tempered officer or N.C.O. **"wants his liver scraping,"** e.g. "The R.S.M. didn't 'arf want his liver scraping this morning." **"Come the acid"** is "to swank," and "put the acid on" means to ask for money or a favor; **"come unstuck"** is "to fail; to be reduced in rank" (the men use it generally, the officers "only in the second sense," perhaps influenced by French *dégommé*). **"Bent"** is "spoiled, ruined, e.g. 'a good man bent' or even 'good tea bent.'" **"Y'ibn"** is Arabic for "son of a dog," "among natives a deadly insult," used by the troops for "scoundrel, sometimes affectionately for scamp." 
*Candidate application:* Documented gestures: a personal ritual of contempt for the whole institution on every exit; the enlisted self as a collective "the troops" mimicking the royal "we"; the same slang word (taped) for the enemy's gun-range and the sergeant-major's judgment of a man; a brew of tea named for the strongest authority in the unit.

### J. Postscript: additions to Chants and Sayings, Bugle Calls, and the P.P.S. to the Glossary (L9,356-9,543)

**[E12b-55]. Additional chants and sayings: "Stand-to, boys, the Jocks are going over!", "I'm in the boat, push off," the girl overboard, "no names, no pack drill," "where did that one go?", "who pulled your chain?", "the first seven years are the worst," the murdered squire, and "you can have it."** (PW-08, Postscript "Chants and Sayings," L9356-9482)
**"Stand-to, boys, the Jocks are going over!"** belonged "to the latter half of the War" and was "a fairly kindly way of making fun of kilted soldiers and of the excessive popularity they enjoyed with journalists and women; spoken with exaggerated and comic respect on the advent of Highland troops—but without any mean feeling of jealousy." A saying that begins "Soldiers?" (the OCR damages the rest of the text; the gloss is "an expression of contempt for another unit (especially if slovenly)"). **"I'm in the boat, push off!"** "is equivalent to 'Curse you (or, you), Jack, I'm all right,'" and the editors give the original form of 1914, which paired the royal motto *Dieu et mon droit* with the same selfish refrain, "pronounced with a strong Cockney accent, *droit* rhyming with *right*" (the profane word in the refrain is summarized; see the register); "by 1916, the original saying had been almost completely forgotten." To the *Girl Overboard* chant (E12b-39) "men in some units added" a stanza: "But see, another lovely girl has fallen overboard: / Who will save her? / I will! / Who are you? / Tommy Atkins home on leave," upon which "as a rule, all those present would burst into the song of 'Tommy, Tommy Atkins, 'e's a good un, 'eart an' 'and.'" **"No names, no pack drill"** was "used by a soldier relating something and wishing not to involve another by mentioning his name; it was taken over from the Regular Army." **"Where did that one go?"** is "short for 'Where did that one go to, Herbert, / Where did that one go?'," from a popular wartime song, and was "sometimes followed up with: *Theirs or ours?*" **"It's a way they have in the Army"** was "a 1916-18 phrase and much more frequently employed by the officers than by the men." **"Who pulled your chain?"** is "the most witty of the 'sayings' recorded in this postscript," "a snub for anyone intruding into a conversation." **"The first seven years are the worst"** is "ironic with a jocular despair," "often prefaced with 'Cheer up!'," "usually either Job's comfort to a grouser or a whimsical encouragement to oneself; it was rarely heard before that 1916-17 winter which drove the iron fairly into men's hearts and souls." A **scripted exchange**, "rarely heard on the march," began when someone rushed in with "Have you heard the news?"; "it was 'the thing' for everybody to dovetail this with: 'What news?'" and the wag, "in a tone of either pained or triumphant surprise," said "The old squire has been foully murdered"; J. B. Priestley is quoted: "this was only a beginning. It was like a detective tale dramatized in the form of a classical Greek play, with messenger and chorus, speaking alternately." A terse dialogue: "A: This is a [bloody] war. What shall we do about it? / B: You can have it! (or, You can keep it!)" (the OCR garbles the adjective; the sense is the standard mild oath). **"The Mad Major"** was "applied to any very eccentric or excessively daring officer, especially if he were of that particular rank," from "a legend (current early in the War) about a bloodthirsty officer said to conduct, single-handed, astonishingly bold and successful raids on the enemy's line. Identity undetermined."
*Candidate application:* A tonal map of the sayings: kind teasing of the "Jocks" (a named class of popular fighters), contempt for the slovenly unit, a code of not naming comrades, a scripted call-and-response to share bad news that turns out to be a play; and the observation that the grimmest ironic saying ("the first seven years are the worst") arrived only with the hard winter of 1916-17. The "Mad Major" is a folk legend, not a person, which suggests units made their own eccentric heroes.
*Caution:* Several items are the editors' dating ("latter half of the War") without sourcing; treat dates as approximate.

**[E12b-56]. Additional bugle-call words: "Sick Parade" as a joke about the dead man, and the other three.** (PW-08, Postscript "Bugle Calls," L9491-9518)
Three calls "wrongly omitted from the essay on bugle-calls": **Pickets** ("Come and do a picket, boys, come and do a guard; / 'Tisn't very easy, boys, 'tisn't very hard"); **Dinner** ("generally known to the ranks as Hot Potatoes"; "Pick 'em up, pick 'em up, hot potatoes..."); and **Sick Parade**, "one of the most famous of all bugle-calls—part of it has been used by R. H. Mottram in one of his Spanish Farm Trilogy stories": "Sixty-four, ninety-four! / He'll never go sick no more: / The poor b——r's dead." (the blanked word is the text's own). Mechanism: the call that summons the sick and the tune that sounds the roll are fitted with words that announce a man's death as relief from the sick parade. The editors add that "nearly all, if not absolutely all, these words to bugle-calls ... existed in the Regular Army before the War, although very few existed before about 1890."
*Candidate application:* A documented bit of gallows humor tied to a routine call: death presented as exemption from a duty. The editors' note that the words date to the pre-war Regular Army means the jokes belong to a professional-soldier tradition, not only to the war's conscripts.

**[E12b-57]. The "P.P.S. to Glossary": "check," "offensive," "plague," "wipe out."** (PW-08, L9521-9543)
**"Check"** is "an alternative for Ticket" (E12b-20). **"Offensive"** and **"wipe out"** are treated in E12b-51 and E12b-52. The headword that the OCR renders **"Pracue"** is "mad; an analogy of stone mad, sometimes stone *piache*" and "frequently used by men with Indian service" (the OCR heading is uncertain; the Indian-service gloss suggests a Hindustani word).
*Candidate application:* Another entry in the mild "mad" vocabulary (batty, scatty, "gone dis," tapped, touched, winick, loopy, "plague"), where madness is graded and usually eccentric, not frightening.

## Pattern roll-up (how this unit says dark humor works)

*(Documented patterns, by lens. The bracketed numbers point to findings. Everything here is from one source: the 1930 British editors' record of British Army usage in the First World War, with some Australian and New Zealand material and Indian-Army and Arabic loanwords; it is not general to other armies or eras.)*

**Forms and mechanics of the language**
- *Domestic or household naming of the means of death.* Two best examples: "iron rations" for incoming shells ("There goes Jerry's iron rations!") (E12b-3); bayonet as "toothpick," "tin-opener," "toasting fork" (E12b-26, E12b-33). The editors explicitly name this as the main mechanism of irony: "Some of the terror disappeared, together with the pomp, from war and military glory, when the soldier decided to call his steel-helmet a Tin-hat, his bayonet a Tooth-pick ... and a murderous bombardment a Strafe or Hate" (E12b-33).
- *Euphemism for death by property, food, direction, or flowers.* "Landowner" (the grave as estate) and "go west" (with the editors' remark that "west" revealed "the suppressed emotion of the soldier") (E12b-4, E12b-28); "push up the daisies," given by the editors as a "flash of sardonic humor" (E12b-34).
- *Old words made to carry war's new load.* "Packet," "landowner," "eye-wash" as ordinary words "charged with new content" (E12b-33); "napoo" extending from an empty bottle to a dead sergeant-major (E12b-4).
- *Understatement and shrinkage.* "Non-stop" for a shell that missed; "stuff" for shellfire; "dirt" for shells; "woolly bear" for a heavy burst (E12b-3, E12b-18, E12b-51).
- *Borrowed words from the places the army had served.* Hindustani, Arabic and French: "pukka," "imshi," "napoo," "san fairy ann"; the editors' own comment that French became "something neither English nor French" (E12b-12, E12b-33).
- *Parody and inversion of official or sentimental texts.* Recruiting slogans reversed ("Are we downhearted? Yes!"; "Kitchener wants you" as a warning of dangerous duty) (E12b-36); *Don't Go up the Line, Daddy* retaining a child's refrain while the verses list shell calibers (E12b-48); *The Lost Chord* tune for the catalogue of kit (E12b-47).
- *Words fitted to bugle calls and to fixed formulas.* Reveille, pay parade, officers' mess, and the Sick Parade call "He'll never go sick no more" (E12b-40, E12b-56).
- *Scripted call-and-response routines.* The exchange about the murdered old squire; "Barnacle Bill" with the whistled hornpipe; the "mingy" benefactor routine (E12b-39, E12b-55).

**Functions and targets**
- *Coping with fear by outward derision.* "Wind-up" as "the placard pinned" on the "guy" that fear was made into; fear in the past tense is no disgrace (E12b-29, E12b-33).
- *Authority as target, graded by distance.* Staff officers compared to the gods of Troy; the platoon-sergeant "visible and human"; "Curse you, Jack, I'm all right" (E12b-37); the sergeant-major "mentioned in despatches for drinking privates' rum" (E12b-45); the Quartermaster who "drinks it all himself and gives us water" (E12b-46).
- *Institution as target.* King's Regulations "to preserve tradition, not to increase efficiency"; the ceremonial raspberry to the Army on each exit from camp; "offensive" as G.H.Q.'s requested frame of mind (E12b-7, E12b-52, E12b-54).
- *The war's pointlessness.* The repeated "pushed the damper in ... just the same"; "A: This is a bloody war. What shall we do about it? B: You can have it!"; the invisible peace signal of three dark-blue lights (E12b-17, E12b-49, E12b-55).
- *Cohesion.* The "mucking-in" set of two to four as "the real social unit of the army" (E12b-10); affectionate teasing of the sergeant ("Kiss me, sergeant!") and of the Highland "Jocks" (E12b-37, E12b-55).

**Rules of license**
- *Seniority of service trumps argument.* "Before you come up!" is answerable only by a counter-claim of service or a fight (E12b-38).
- *The lead-swinger's limits.* Admired if he fools doctors, "beloved of no one" if he lets others do his work or dodges when danger threatens (E12b-20).
- *Fear may be admitted in the past tense and casually; it is derogatory in the present of another man.* "Wind-up" versus "windy" (E12b-29).
- *The third man is pushed away from the match unless he is "very unpopular."* A ritual whose protection is allocated by social standing (E12b-21).
- *No names, no pack drill.* A rule for not naming comrades (E12b-55).

**Limits and what covers what**
- *What stops the joking.* The Last Post: "no one ever profaned it with words"; the S.I.W. entry has no joke; the "short arm" inspection and the unfit sent to the line are written as grievance, not comedy (E12b-41, E12b-19, E12b-20, E12b-7).
- *What a joke can hide.* "Working his ticket" drifting to mean a man is mentally defective, "in time ... a genial and facetious suggestion" (E12b-20).
- *Soldiers' own line on blasphemy.* "Very rarely employed in the soldiers' terminology," although the "bad language" was heavy; and a euphemism that intensified what it avoided (E12b-50).

**Civilian-facing register**
- *Press word versus soldier word.* "Hun" and "Tommy" (journalistic) versus "Jerry"; "Wypers" (newspaper) versus "Eeps"; "Woodbine" gifts that patients endured; the "leg-show" as compensation civilians thought adequate (E12b-2, E12b-25, E12b-32, E12b-13).

**Cross-cultural / era notes**
- *The editors' claim of a national style.* "The typically English irony of understatement and ridicule" (E12b-33); the French *poilu* a civilian name the French soldier did not own (E12b-12); Anzac and British exchange of theft jokes (E12b-27). Treat as one army's 1930 retrospective.

## Gaps and cautions

1. **The editors' voice is strong and partial.** Partridge's glossary entries and the unsigned appendices express a postwar disillusioned view (the M.O., Red Caps, Quartermaster, Staff). Statements such as "almost all M.O.'s exhibited neither intelligence nor courtesy" or the Red Caps' "secret opportunities for cruelty" are the editors' opinions in 1930, not soldier testimony or a measured fact. Likewise "the typically English irony," the soldier's "exhilaration" in "the hardening of their bodies," and "San Fairy Ann" as the product of cynicism are interpretive theses.
2. **No sources or dates for most entries.** Few glossary entries give a date or an informant; many examples are the editors' composed sentences. The postscript dates songs and sayings only roughly ("late 1916," "latter half of the War," "1916-17 winter").
3. **No jokes about the dead by name; no front-line verbatim.** The book is a collected lexicon, songs and chants, not a record of conversations; it shows the vocabulary, not who said what to whom, or the tone, timing and silence.
4. **Class and rank limits.** The record is overwhelmingly of the British other ranks of the Western Front plus the Regular Army tradition; officers' slang is flagged where distinct (loot, skipper, stout fellow, tails up). Almost nothing on Indian or colonial troops' humor except as loanwords; Australians and New Zealanders appear through their slang (furphy, "win," "dinkum").
5. **Content gaps by design.** The editors avoid obscenity (of one song they say it "can only be mentioned"), so the bawdy and obscene layer is mostly absent from my range of the record (E12b-13, E12b-43, E12b-49, register). The sexual-health inspection, the "souvenir" jest, and the "Waac" rumor entries are therefore summarized only.
6. **Historical claims to check.** The editors date the origin of "sweet Fanny Adams" to a murder in 1812 (summarized in the register); the usual account puts the case in 1867 and the tinned-meat issue shortly after (my gloss, not verified here; check in a browser before relying on it). The editors' note on dictionaries thanks Fraser and Gibbons "for a flood of light upon Sweet Fanny Adams" (L7835-7836), so the entry may have been revised in a form I cannot see here. The attribution of "Jerry" to German helmets resembling chamber-pots is the editors' speculation ("possibly (even probably)"), and some derivations (the "furphy" and "wind-up" origins) are offered as guesses.
7. **OCR damage.** Headwords are often garbled (e.g., "Hor Ar1r" for "Hot Air," "Kittrz" for "Kiltie," "Prerrizs" for "Peggies/Furphies," "Saipa," "Mesopotonica/Mesopolonica," "Ancets or Curist," "Hotpinc Back," "Ransow," "Pracue," "Rusger Gun" for "Rubber Gun," "Marftsh"); a few entries (the "Soldiers? ... them!" saying at L9370-9377, the "Hotpinc Back" entry) are damaged and I give only the editors' glosses. Quotation marks are often curled and unbalanced. Bare numerals are printed page numbers (about 130 to 221 in my range). A few asterisks look like footnote marks but are the editors' marker for "technical or official" terms. **The text file ends with garbled OCR noise (L9546-9562) and no index; nothing after the P.P.S. is recoverable from this file.**
8. **Items for original-page check in a browser:** the exact wording of the Sick Parade call (L9508-9512) and the "Soldier's Friend" brand claim; the "Hotpinc Back" and "Ransow" headwords; whether "Ancets of Christ" is the right reading; whether the "(without a shirt)" tag in *The Roses round the Door* has an editorial explanation in the unseen first half.
9. **Not my range, and cross-reference to E12a.** Lines 1-4,949 (the first-half songs, the editors' introductions to song groups and the first half of the glossary, A-"Hot Air") are covered by E12a; cross-references in my range to the *Pudden-basin*, *Chat*, *Elephant*, *Etaples*, *Dixie* and similar entries point to that half and are not checked here. Points of contact with E12a that a reader should join: E12b-43's lost-kit variant extends the first-half song *I've Lost My Rifle and Bayonet* (E12a-52); E12b-38's "Before you come up!" and the postscript's "Before your number was dry" variant (L8739-8740) extend E12a-71; and the euphemism entries here (E12b-4) continue the death-euphemism group E12a lists as E12a-59 and E12a-60. I did not re-read E12a's findings beyond its source-shape notes and pattern roll-up, as instructed, so I may repeat an item it already catalogs.
