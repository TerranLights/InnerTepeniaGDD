## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What it is.** A *keyword-and-phrase harvest*, not a sample of Australian veterans. The log shows 333 queries against the archive's full-text search (e.g., `"black humour"`, `"gallows humour"`, `"practical jokes"`, `nickname`, `larrikin`, `"laughed dead"~10`, `"stopped laughing"`, `"laugh civilians"~15`; query strings are given literally, with the archive's British spellings, inside code marks), plus theme restrictions for Vietnam, Korea, Malaya, Indonesian Confrontation, East Timor, Bougainville, UN Operations, Gulf War I and POW. 117 transcripts were downloaded; 47 were staged for reading; 42 were read at their keyword clusters; **40 were kept (the cap) and 172 passages cut from them as contiguous verbatim spans** (each at most 350 words, machine-checked against the saved transcript, 0 failures). Interviews were filmed 1999-2004; the saved set's interview dates run April 2003 to August 2004, so veterans of WWII were talking about events some 60 years old, Korea about 50, Vietnam about 35, and the 1990s peacekeepers about 4 to 12 years after the events.

**What the selection does NOT represent (log §§2, 5, 7; confirmed in the data).**
- **Keyword bias.** The search is whole-interview, Solr full-text, `AND` semantics; a hit means "this interview contains these words," not "this interview is about humor." A veteran who joked constantly but never used the words for humor, joke, laugh or nickname is invisible; one who said it once is overrepresented. The archive's transcripts use British spelling and American-spelled queries returned zero; **negative-humor phrases that begin "no" or "not" could not be searched at all** (stop words), so the *humor-stopped/limits* passages were reached by other routes (`"stopped laughing"`, `"nobody laughed"`, `"wasn't funny"`, `"lost my sense of humour"`) and by reading clusters. The log itself says the limits angle is "mostly implicit" in the passages and calls it a thin angle.
- **Not every narrator says "yes."** Many hits on `gallows humour` and `practical jokes` were the *interviewer's* own wording answered with "No, we didn't have that" (the log names interviews 69 and 849, **not saved here**); those negative answers are real data on limits but produced no passage, so the set leans toward veterans who confirmed the practice.
- **Excluded by the project's content boundary: bodily-waste material.** Passages built around latrines, toilets, vomit or urine were *skipped* (log §5 lists about a dozen items across 11 interviews: Fletcher, von Berg, Schunemann, Tolliday, Coultman-Smith, Fazackerley, Sly, Martin, Ramsey, Hamilton, Binstead). In at least von Berg's and Coultman-Smith's transcripts the log records that the veteran told comic stories in that register. **The set therefore underrepresents a large register of ordinary Australian army humor, and the omission is a boundary decision, not a finding about the veterans.** Three further borderline items were also set aside (a cook spitting on an officer's steak, a navy "turn in the barrel" gag, and a racial-slur nickname the veteran defended).
- **Narrators are unevenly drawn.** The 40 narrators supply between 2 and 9 passages each (Binstead 9 and Cavanough 9 at the top; Reiter 8; six narrators supply 2); the wars are tallied below. **Afghanistan and Iraq are absent** (archive filmed 1999-2004; every theme query for Gulf War II and War on Terror returned 0). The Gulf War I set is almost empty. **Peacekeeping is covered only by Somalia (2 narrators), Rwanda (1), East Timor (the pioneer sergeant Humphries plus Fraser), Bougainville (Andaloro) and Sinai (Hamilton-Smith).**
- **Sex and rank balance.** Women appear in three interviews only: Bruce (a civilian nurse who trained in Australia and worked in a Vietnamese hospital), Fazackerley (AWAS) and Andaloro (RAN nurse, later peace monitor in Bougainville), nine passages in all. Most narrators are other ranks or junior non-commissioned officers; five are officers (von Berg, Hamilton-Smith, Radford, McClean, Ramsey). Nothing in the harvest codes narrators by race or ethnicity.
- **Memory, not record.** The archive's own FAQ says the content is "memories and reflections" and does not claim to be an accurate historical record. A passage is a person *telling it now*, often 35-60 years later, to an interviewer on camera for a national archive: a civilian-facing, edited register.
- **Transcript conventions.** `Q:` and `A:` labels are the transcribers' and are sometimes wrong (the log notes answers under `Q:`); `[Tn mm:ss]` markers sit in the text; passages may begin or end mid-sentence; embargo notices (to 2034) appear in 7 files and no passage crosses one. Audio and video were never touched, so tone, delivery and laughter are not in the data.
- **Terms.** Reuse permitted with acknowledgment (see above). Passages here may be quoted up to two or three sentences with the interview ID and tape time.

**Heading counts (from the log, not recounted by me):** nickname 42, humor-as-coping-explained 59, humor-about-command/brass 50, gallows-humor 38, prank 37, humor-about-dead/wounded 30, humor-about-self 29, humor-after-service/civilians 28, other 27, content-note 26, humor-stopped/limits 24, humor-about-enemy 16, humor-in-captivity 16. ## Findings

### 1. Topics, forms and targets

**[E24d-1]. Nicknames are the commonest named form and are built from a short list of materials.** (42 tagged; #2 unsw-894 T6 18:30, #15 unsw-2152 T4 07:00, #26 unsw-1038 T9 20:30, #39 unsw-1749 T8 13:00, #63 unsw-2056 T3 10:30, #77-78 unsw-1975 T6, #101-103 unsw-761/1309, #107 unsw-716 T2 39:00, #120-121 unsw-607 T7, #142 unsw-104 T6 01:30, #153 unsw-614 T8 26:30, #159 unsw-235 T7 25:30)
The materials, with the narrators' own examples: (a) **the surname plus a stock suffix or partner** (Jonesy; any Rhodes or Miller becomes Dusty; clerks are Nobby; any Withers is Googy, #103 Bianchi, RAN; "mainly it was a nickname that came from your last name," #78 Seymour, 1 RAR); (b) **body and manner** (a small, strong man called Pup, #78; red hair makes a man Blue, a tall one Lofty and a short one Stumpy, #120-121 Reiter; a RAN friend whose leg injury left a limp and a head-bob was named for the motion, with the narrator's gloss that this is Australian humor, #102; a double amputee carried in a bamboo chair among shaved-headed prisoners on the Burma railway was named for a fruit, #107, restrained); (c) **one event that fixes a man** (Tangles, a trainee who kept falling in vines, #15; Bike a Day for the despatch rider who wrecked one a day, #121; Grovelly Joe for a repeat absentee who spent so long in detention that the detention barracks became his name, #142; Conchy for a RAN seaman trying to match a brother, #101; Psychedelic, then Psycho, for a new arrival in flamboyant civilian clothes, #63; Purple Pull-through for an officer left thin by New Guinea who ran in a purple jumper, #159); (d) **a borrowed screen character** (the hero of a migrant-comedy film for a sailor with an Italian surname, a Top Gun character for a look-alike, #103, #78); (e) **the role or rank itself** (a private soldier is a "baggy arse" after the oversized jungle greens, #2 Lampard; the rear echelon are "baseballers" and "blanket counters," #39; initials D.P. gave an officer "Drill Purposes," #153; a soldier who neither smoked, swore nor drank became "Padre," #135; and a man with a venereal disease could wear the name for it for a whole tour, #7, restrained); (f) **ironic inversion** (a man called Smiley, whose wife tells the narrator he has not smiled since New Guinea, #26).
*Mechanism:* a name compresses one observed fact into one word and the group then enforces it; event-names and inversions last longest. Several nicknames in the set rest on ethnicity or skin tone (#18, #23, #78, #146, #163-164); I do not reproduce them (see [E24d-3] and the register).
*Candidate application:* A character's nickname can be a one-word archive of a single incident or flaw; a companion may know the name and never the story.
*Cross-use:* Robot units could acquire event-names the same way.

**[E24d-2]. Names given upward are verdicts on officers, warrant officers, staff and nurses, and the rank line is enforced in the open.** (#29 unsw-2199 T3 04:30, #54 unsw-1249 T8 05:30, #57 unsw-2304 T8 26:00, #94 unsw-1376 T4 21:30, #98 unsw-1919 T4 08:30, #105 unsw-949 T8 13:30, #123 unsw-607 T6 03:30, #152 unsw-614 T8 23:30)
Documented targets: a RAN skipper called Two Gun, whom a sailor summed up as a hero in his own mind ("I would follow that man anywhere, just to see where he was going," #105 Goodall T8 14:00), and his first lieutenant, called Gentle Jesus; an officer the narrator had known in occupied Japan, whose nickname the narrator defended as neither swearing nor blasphemy when threatened with a charge, and which years later still drew a harsh reaction from the officer when the narrator prompted the officer's son to ask his father about it (#98 Martin); two officers nicknamed for their American cavalry costume (pearl-handled pistols, a regimental scarf) and a third called, apparently by nickname, Mr Wormhole as "absolutely useless" (#94 Ersser, British machine-gunner in Korea); a brigade staff captain who had been a tram conductor, "whittled him down to size" as Ding Ding (#152 Radford, WWII officer); a hospital ward's name for a nurse who gave painful injections (#123 Reiter); a company sergeant major called Pig, who is then taunted with a painted pig (#57); a battalion commander renamed by New Zealand troops, after they painted a target on the underside of his helicopter, with a crude nickname about it (#54). Radford, an officer, calls such names "absolutely devastating" and says never to have known one that was not relevant (#152 T8 25:00). The rank line: a newly promoted lance corporal who used a troop nickname for a sergeant was told "my rank is sergeant," while troopers could use it and say they could not be busted lower (#29 Jones, tank troop).
*Mechanism:* the nickname lets the group deliver a judgment the chain of command cannot formally hear; the license belongs to those below and rank, once gained, removes it.
*Candidate application:* An ex-soldier may still carry the private name he and his unit had for an officer, and may find he cannot say it to anyone who was not there.

**[E24d-3]. Rules of license for names: do not rebel; toughness limits a name; and names outlast the unit.** (#103 unsw-1309 T4 36:00, #78 unsw-1975 T6 04:00, #28 unsw-2199 T5 13:00, #77 unsw-1975 T6, #84 unsw-1607 T2 19:00, #106 unsw-949 T8 15:00; #23 unsw-1038 T4 07:30)
Bianchi (RAN) states the working rule: "the worst thing you can do about a nickname is rebel" (#103 T4 36:00), because showing annoyance fixes it. Seymour reports a nickname for a look-alike that "didn't last long" because the bearer was among the toughest men in the unit (#78, T6 04:00): the name's life depends on the bearer's capacity to enforce. Jones (tank driver, Vietnam) says names mattered *in* Vietnam because a shout had to bring an instant reaction in a danger setting, but not after (#28), while still insisting Australians abbreviate and bastardize names (Jones's own word). Fraser (army aviation) says "nothing's really called what it really is," and that callers asking for a man by his official name were met with "who?" (#84). A nickname can swallow the real name (Seymour, #77: back in the hometown after years, hearing the given name, Seymour has to remember it is theirs; Reiter, #120: it was the 1950s before Reiter learned another man's actual name; Goodall, #106: ex-sailors trying to support a veterans' affairs claim know mates only by nicknames). One narrator, speaking of a name given in BCOF Japan that rested on a cook's perceived ancestry, says "You know Australians, mad half the time tormenting people" (#23 Newell, T4 08:30), naming the practice as tormenting while treating it as national character; Japanese locals who heard it reacted with disbelief, which Newell reports with amusement. I do not reproduce the name.
*Candidate application:* A nickname someone wears for life was usually fixed by a first-week joke he took badly or well; the rule that showing annoyance fixes the name is an obvious reason a hardened character never reacts to his.

**[E24d-4]. Pranks on newcomers: the vehicle is usually a forged piece of authority (a form, a badge, a notice, a venue).** (#6 unsw-794 T2 11:00, #8-9 unsw-575 T7, #79 unsw-1975 T4 31:30, #141 unsw-104 T7 21:00, #172 unsw-253 T3 29:00)
The standing hoax for Vietnam arrivals was a weekly dance at a local town with women, which Leis (5 RAR rifleman) calls the biggest joke played on all new arrivals (#6). A RAN leading seaman (later a professional comedian) told a recruit that a sailor was needed in a nonexistent specialty with a six-month course; the recruit wrote it on his form and appeared before the board, where the narrator, who had only meant to tease, laughed in front of the commander and was in "deep trouble" (#8-9 Elliot). At basic training a recruit hoping for infantry was issued Catering Corps badges at the insignia issue, a "piss-take" lasting about half an hour that nearly reduced the recruit to tears (#79 Seymour). A soldier at a Sydney camp typed a notice ordering the uncircumcised to report for correction "for reasons of hygiene"; the unit "nearly mutinied" and three men went absent (#141 Medcalf, 15th Bn, before Bougainville; the author, McCann, has no rank given; wording restrained, see register). Years later, among old airmen, the hoax continued in peacetime: friends removed a "Women" sign from a lavatory and fixed it to a drunk friend's motel door (#172 Sly).
*Mechanism:* the joke borrows the institution's own form of command ("a notice," "a board," "a badge"), so the target's credulity tests how far the paperwork is trusted. The danger side shows in #8-9 and #141: the prank can reach the institution and rebound on its author.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who distrusts notices and forms on principle may have learned it as a joke played on him before it was a politics.

**[E24d-5]. Reprisal pranks between units, and what they cost.** (#3 unsw-894 T7 20:00, #41 and #46 unsw-1706 T6, #54 unsw-1249 T8, #55 unsw-1249 T8 33:30, #51 unsw-1399 T5 29:30, #67 unsw-1182 T6 17:00)
Documented items: departing SAS troops left a timer-fused CS (riot-control) gas grenade under an incoming squadron's tent (the recipients coughed for about eight hours; the narrator's verdict was that it was "a waste of a good CS grenade," while "they thought it was funny," #3 Lampard); a foot patrol (the passage does not say which unit) gave helicopter crews a long-dead deer carcass, which the crews flew over the SAS hill and shoved out the door (#41 Fletcher); when a crude sexual insult about 9 Squadron appeared on the SAS hill, the squadron's own CO told his crews to answer with condom water bombs and flour bombs dropped from aircraft ("Everybody had a laugh," #46, with the narrator's note that a hit "would have killed them"); New Zealand troops put on the dry fired empty cans from a gun into the officers' mess, and the officers restored their beer because it seemed "safer that way" (#54 Walford); a signaller was given electric shocks through the line held in the mouth while repairing cables under mortar fire, the other men all laughing "like mad" (#51 Schunemann); and a campaign of three uncoordinated attacks on an unpopular company commander (a thrown rock and a shouted "grenade," a bridge sawn half through, dye in his shower) ended with the company paraded, the perpetrators owning up, and fourteen days' field punishment for the narrator and others (#67 Humphries, 8 RAR, Vietnam; the narrator says the three groups did not coordinate).
*Mechanism:* reprisal has a grammar (an insult is answered in kind, sometimes with the commander's sanction, #46), and the costs are real: physical danger (#3, #46, #51), formal punishment (#67), and recipients who did not find it funny (#3).
*Candidate application:* Inter-unit rivalry can be written as a long exchange of increasingly dangerous "joke" reprisals that the officers permit until someone could be killed.

**[E24d-6]. Pranks aimed up: the test is whether the officer takes it in good part.** (#16 unsw-2152 T4 13:00, #20 unsw-2152 T6 35:00, #22 unsw-1038 T3 01:00, #57, #59 unsw-2304 T8 29:30, #81 unsw-2461 T8 32:00, #90 unsw-2021 T5 40:00, #130 unsw-1684 T2 37:30, #144 unsw-1186 T3 02:00, #154 unsw-614 T8 28:00)
Examples, told by the prankster, a witness or the officer concerned: recruits secretly pretending to be out of step when drill instructors called the time (#16); a man wiping his foot with a sock who dipped it into the platoon commander's mug while the commander told a joke, so the group's helpless laughter was at the tea and the commander thought it was his story (#20); an old soldier who, when the line was numbered off, shouted "Bingo" at his own number ("I've wanted to do that for five bloody years"), at which the officers "died laughing" and nobody was upset, which the interviewer ties to the war being over and Newell accepts with "Relaxed, yeah" (#22); a platoon commander whose painted pet pig followed him like a dog and who called it with a loud "here pig" outside the tent of a disliked CSM nicknamed Pig, who could do nothing: "A, I'm an officer" (#57); a half dozen men who cut down the single hilltop tree on a divisional exercise's final night, so the exercise was called off and the senior officers went out to inspect the bare hill: "Nobody got killed, no, and it was beautiful" (#130 Peirce, 2/2nd Bn, in Palestine); troops who stood and walked off the aircraft before a parachute drop and returned with "Only joking" (#90 Hamilton-Smith); a company that pretended not to hear a wheel order, which the company commander (Radford) classed as "one of the classic army jokes." Radford's account is the clearest statement of the rule: the order for an about-turn was given when it suited, with a curse in a good-humored voice, and "They've had their joke, you've taken it alright and everyone's happy" (#154 T8 29:30). Quips up the chain under orders also appear: a notorious thief caught by an officer with a bag from the cookhouse answered with a tally of his own detentions and an invitation to the officer, "Get in here and that will be 12" (he was charged), and a private ordered by a captain to "draw fire" answered that he was "no bloody artist" and the captain should write a letter (#150 Simmons, 2/25th Bn, T8 14:30-15:30). Counter-example: an older man, a World War I veteran who rode a horse in polished leggings (his rank is not stated; he was talking to an officer), whose saddle had been turned around; everyone burst out laughing and he "couldn't hack it as a bit of a joke, that was undermining his authority" (#144 Simmons, 2/25th Bn, T3 02:30).
*Mechanism:* a prank up the chain is a license test; the officer who accepts it buys goodwill and the one who cannot is exposed; the whole is policed by the target's response.
*Candidate application:* An officer's character could be sketched entirely by how he took being pranked, and that detail would be remembered by his troops for decades.

**[E24d-7]. Humor about command is aimed at the institution's voice: its documents, signs, briefers and recruiting.** (#37 unsw-1749 T7 05:00, #70 unsw-1679 T3 19:30, #50 unsw-1399 T3 39:00, #66 unsw-1182 T2 24:00, #12 unsw-575 T1 23:30, #65 unsw-2056 T6 07:30, #52 unsw-1399 T6 23:30, #87-88 unsw-2021 T3, #158 unsw-235 T6 21:30)
Heslewood (6 RAR) says "we defeated the Communist hordes" was a standard joke, because after a month out and with men killed or wounded, the battalion would receive a commander's letter next morning congratulating it for clearing an area that "you could walk out there straight away and get shot at," so that the men wanted to say "You go out and have a look" (#37). Thompson (Somalia-era 1 RAR) reports that recruits laughed at the recruiting video's golf course and cafeteria as pure fiction (#70). Schunemann (Korea) reports roadside signs on a front-line road read aloud by laughing men (three signs reading "Speed Brings Dust!", "Dust Brings Shells!" and "Shells Bring Misery!"), a joke shared with new men that was followed at once by a self-propelled gun opening fire (#50). Humphries, at an East Timor decompression briefing, laughed at a line about the wife and the lawns and was told to shut up (#66, see [E24d-23]). Elliot recalls a reunion in which men joked about the rumor that something (bromide, in Elliot's recollection) had been put in the navy's coffee, as each man announced how many daughters he had (#12). A Hong Kong R&R party was waved through a security check because the Americans took its lieutenant for a two-star general (#65 Coultman-Smith). Not all of it is upward: the commander is sometimes the joker, as when a colonel at an O Group answered an English captain's report of agricultural work with a one-line retort and the whole meeting "burst out into laughter. ... We couldn't go on" (#52 Schunemann), and when a platoon commander who fell off a cliff while saying "Everything's under control, men, follow me" was greeted for six months with "Follow you, sir?" (#87 Hamilton-Smith, SAS) and records that "if an officer or anyone takes themselves too seriously the Aussie soldier will soon let them know" (#88 T3 19:30). A platoon commander picking volunteers by pointing ("You and you and you") found that men laughed and joined in (#158 McClean, Kokoda officer). A hospital chaplain sharing a tent with a doctor put up a sign advertising "Souling" (spelled out letter by letter) and healing (#136 Rowell, T7 23:30).
*Mechanism:* the humor attacks the institution's *claims*, not its goals; the commander can be an audience, co-author or target.
*Candidate application:* Satirical letters and briefings from a command that is out of touch with what its troops are living are a documented raw material for a character's cynicism.

**[E24d-8]. Humor about the enemy is mostly spectacle, luck and propaganda, and the troops sometimes refuse it.** (16 tagged; #10 unsw-575 T6 02:30, #36 unsw-1749 T6 23:30, #117 unsw-607 T1 28:30, #139 unsw-104 T5 23:00, #4 unsw-794 T2 25:00, #61 unsw-2056 T7 15:00, #92 unsw-1376 T4 17:30, #147 unsw-44 T8 06:00, #25 unsw-1038 T8 22:30, #34 unsw-867 T5 22:30, #73 unsw-1679 T7 08:00)
The mild material: an enemy radio presenter reported a RAN ship sunk three times (#10 Elliot, HMAS Hobart). The hard material, which I summarize without its wording: a WWII platoon lay laughing at the sight of an enemy position wrecked by a bomb, after which the narrator says they developed a "weird" sense of humor (#117 Reiter); after a skirmish a unit joked about the way detainees had been treated (#4); a digger sat on a posed enemy corpse with a mug of tea (#61); a quip was made about a water supply fouled by enemy dead (#92, British narrator, Korea); a remark was made over a wounded enemy asking for water (#147 Blundell, Tobruk; the narrator labels it black humor); and a platoon leader's retort to an enemy "camp comedian" is retold (#139, secondhand). The refusal: at the clearing of a battlefield after a major action, Heslewood found papers and family photographs on the dead, "They were the same as us," and says there "was no taking the micky out of [teasing] them at all" (#36 T6 24:30). Locals as audience or butt appear too: Vietnamese women laughing at rear-echelon men who dived for cover (#25), Vietnamese colleagues falling about at Australians' mispronunciations (#34), and Somali women whose laughter served as an early warning ([E24d-36]).
*Mechanism:* ridicule falls on bodies, luck and the enemy's propaganda, rarely on ideology; the harder items are about the dead or helpless, and they are the ones narrators label or flinch at.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may carry a short list of "enemy jokes" he can still tell and a shorter one he never repeats.

**[E24d-9]. The wounded joke about themselves, and comrades tease them in a recognizable register.** (#36 unsw-1749 T6 23:30, #119 unsw-607 T7 02:00, #138 unsw-104 T2 11:30, #168 unsw-253 T5 28:30, #43 unsw-1706 T6 30:30, #97 unsw-1919 T7 07:00, #102, #107, #86 unsw-1607 T7 36:30, #151 unsw-1186 T8 16:00)
(i) *The injured own the first joke.* A badly wounded man playing dead and bleeding greeted the men who found him with a complaint about their lateness, which Heslewood reads as the man still keeping a bit of his sense of humor (#36 T6 24:00). A Korean War casualty who came round in a hospital found the padre, a priest, a minister and a physiotherapist around him, was told the padre had a box ready, and asked "Was that a joke?" ("No"), after which the physiotherapist wished the patient a happy birthday and Martin asked where the wine was (#97 Martin, 3 RAR). Two helicopter crewmen facing an engine failure exchanged a farewell line (#43). A man in hospital with a fractured pelvis found a visiting friend's jokes funny against Fraser's will ("You've got to shut up, I don't know if this is worth the pain," #86 Fraser). (ii) *Comrades answer in the same register.* A man shot in the buttock was consoled by his corporal with a cover story for his wife (#138 Medcalf); a RAAF pilot in a fight heard a screaming call for help answered by another Australian pilot's dry "Have you had it yet?" *Candidate application:* A character with a chronic injury may joke about it first and fastest, as a way of keeping the license to himself.

**[E24d-10]. Third-party jokes about the dead are mostly about the event, the ritual or the institution's wording; the dead comrade is almost never the butt.** (#1 unsw-894 T6 17:00, #99 unsw-761 T5 08:00, #137 unsw-1684 T6 08:00, #168, #170 unsw-253 T8 27:00; #62 unsw-2056 T7 16:30, #156 unsw-235 T5 22:30; context consulted for #156)

Joyce (RAN seaman, HMAS Murchison) describes a burial at sea in Korea where the ballast was rigged badly and the shrouded body moved upright on the way over; there was "a lot of giggling" under the captain's look, and Joyce calls it "tragic but humorous" (#99, restrained). A party of soldiers sheltering overnight in a Greek island morgue had one man lie on a slab as a corpse and raise and drop a booted leg in front of an elderly caretaker, who ran off into the snow ("a dreadful thing to do to him but you had to break the monotony," #137 Peirce; a hoax played on a living man, not a joke about a dead one). *Mechanism:* the butt is the ritual or the institution's phrase; the stranger or enemy dead are exposed to a harder register, the comrade dead are protected.
*Candidate application:* A character may recount a burial or death notice as a comic mishap yet refuse any joke about the specific man; the boundary between these is the character.

**[E24d-11]. Mock ceremonies: what the set actually has is thin.** (#6, #22, #33 unsw-867 T5 18:30, #59 unsw-2304 T8 29:30, #111 unsw-716 T7 12:00, #158, #168)

*Mechanism:* ritual is carried by repetition of one line at one occasion; it needs a witness group, not a rulebook.
*Candidate application:* A character's few surviving rituals may be one reunion line or one habit he cannot explain to anyone who was not there.

### 2. What the veterans say humor did

**[E24d-12]. The explanations cluster on a short vocabulary: coping, release, sanity, tension, mateship.** (59 tagged; #4 unsw-794 T2 27:00, #5 unsw-794 T5 27:00, #10 unsw-575 T6 03:30, #13 unsw-2152 T2 15:00, #21 unsw-2152 T3 02:00, #32 unsw-867 T3 29:30, #62 unsw-2056 T7 17:00, #72 unsw-1679 T7 23:30, #113 unsw-716 T5 40:30, #140 unsw-104 T5 30:00, #141 unsw-104 T7 22:30)
Leis (5 RAR) says the black humor "was a coping mechanism" and "a release" and "the biggest part of our survival" (#4, T2 27:00), and, retelling a firefight in which a burst tin of peaches felt like blood, lists what the laughter held: fear, anxiety, survival, support, achievement, being ready and disciplined, and handling shock (#5 T5 27:00). Elliot (RAN) says that in such situations people "jolly each other along," joking and cursing, and "that's how I got through it too" (#10). Cavanough (2 RAR) says Australians cope by having a go at a mate, and "that strengthens our mateship" (#13 T2 15:00). Bruce (student nurse, laying out a corpse at night) calls the helpless laughter "a sort of a release of emotion" (#32 T3 29:30). Medcalf (15th Bn, Bougainville) says men never talked about the worst with their mates and "covered it up by cracking jokes ... and seemingly being indifferent. That was the only way you stayed sane, I think" (#140 T5 30:00). Thompson (Somalia-era) says the Australian larrikin jokes about it "otherwise you would go insane" (#72 T7 23:30). Coultman-Smith (Vietnam, later emergency services) says "a coping mechanism, that's all" (#62 T7 17:00). Binstead, a Burma-railway prisoner, reports a surgeon's formula: humor and mateship together are what got men out ([E24d-33]).
*Mechanism:* no narrator offers a mechanism beyond relief, spirit, sanity and bonding, and none says humor made anyone braver. The terms "coping mechanism" and black humor recur in veterans filmed in 2003-04 and may owe something to the popular-psychology vocabulary of the interviews (in #30 it is the interviewer who raises the television show M*A*S*H, and Lennox answers that he has not seen it often).
*Candidate application:* A soldier-type character's own explanation of his jokes can be short and practical; a longer one would be learned later, from counselors or books.

**[E24d-13]. Fear is not spoken; it goes through a joke or through silence, and the straight face is a craft.** (#14 unsw-2152 T2 26:00, #53 unsw-1249 T6 39:30, #95 unsw-1108 T7 25:30, #125 unsw-1021 T8 14:30, #134 unsw-451 T6 26:00, #133 unsw-451 T6 22:00, #151 unsw-1186 T8 16:00, #145 unsw-44 T1 35:30)
Cavanough states the rule most plainly: you do not say you are scared to your mates; "You will make a joke or you'll say nothing because I think it's infectious" (#14 T2 26:00), and in terrible circumstances men pulled faces or did something ridiculous. Rowell (2/4th Bn), asked how humor helps if men will not show fear, answers that it is "the saving grace," then adds that one "may say something rather than actually laugh" (#134 T6 26:00). Older, Rowell says "I'm not a great laugher" and was telephoned after an Anzac Day broadcast showed the narrator laughing ("I don't laugh ... doesn't mean that I'm not laughing inside," #133). Hamilton (RAN gunnery instructor) states a performance rule: tell a joke with a deadpan face, "When a comedian laughs at a joke he's a failure in my book," and an instructor keeps a straight face even when not believing what is being said (#95 T7 25:30). Young, a prisoner leading a work party, had to keep a straight face while the men, out of the guards' line of sight, laughed at bowing to Young in turn ([E24d-32]). Walford (artillery sergeant), laughing in a pit at his first mortar attack, says it was "funny when you think about it but at the time it wasn't so funny" and describes the next morning, when Walford and a mate sat laughing over coffee while the rest of the battery, who had been slow to fill sandbags and now poked fun ("What's wrong with you blokes, frightened?"), dug frantically (#53 T6 40:30). Blundell (signaller, shelling): "a bit of nerves" (#145).
Walford's account of a battery sergeant major's inspection (a soldier wearing red football socks under boots and gaiters) ends with the whole parade "laughing inside but they didn't dare laugh out loud" (#56 T4 04:00). Fletcher, ordered to fly an SAS insertion the day before going home ("I am flying home tomorrow"), stacked extra armor plate and reports "if a leaf had barked at me in the tree I would have shot it" (#45 T8 30:00). Ersser (British gunner, pinned in a bunker under mortar fire) describes a pistol game of chance played with a pressured newcomer, a sergeant who threatened a charge only "for wasting ammunition," and the explanation "You had to have some sort of laugh otherwise you would have gone completely mad" (#91 T5 28:00; restrained: the nature of the game).
*Mechanism:* the joke is the permitted container for fear; the deadpan teller keeps the container shut while the listeners laugh.
*Candidate application:* A character who understates and never laughs at his own lines, and whose companions laugh a moment after he speaks, fits the documented craft.

**[E24d-14]. Relief laughter and the nobody-was-hurt story.** (#5 unsw-794 T5 25:30, #24 unsw-1038 T7 07:30, #40 unsw-1749 T9 25:00, #53, #87 unsw-2021 T3 17:00, #131-132 unsw-451 T3 18:00 and T6 28:00, #35 unsw-1749 T3 27:00)
The funny stories that veterans chose to tell mostly turn on a harmless accident in a dangerous moment: Leis's burst tin of peaches (#5); a column of infantry sliding down a snowy Korean ridge on their backsides, "laughing and joking" (#24 Newell); a rifleman who undressed in a wet pit to get warm, then was caught half-dressed by a negligent discharge (#40 Heslewood); Walford and a mate in a pit in the first mortar attack (#53); a platoon commander who fell off a cliff and lay at the bottom laughing while the whole platoon lay on their backs "like dead ants" above the narrator (#87 Hamilton-Smith; "That was the subject of many drinks in the bar," T3 18:30); a shell that landed between a section commander and a Bren gunner and tore only the seat of a man's trousers (#131-132 Rowell). Rowell supplies the explanation in the second telling: "you laugh in relief"; "a laugh of relief rather than anything else that he wasn't killed" (#132 T6 28:30), and agrees to the interviewer's phrase "tension release" (the word is the interviewer's). Heslewood: "There was always something to laugh at. It didn't matter how serious things were, we would laugh at it" (#35 T3 27:00).
A wisecrack from the ranks that "broke the ice" also appears in Reiter's account of the evacuation from Greece, where men boarding destroyers in dead earnest heard a shouted jibe at a man calling out that he could not swim, after which "everybody just roared laughin'" (#116 T1 09:00; restrained: the jibe and the man's description are not reproduced); Reiter generalizes: "somebody'd come up with a wisecrack like that and broke the ice and everybody laughed."
*Mechanism:* the retold-funny canon is accident-without-casualty slapstick that doubles as a near-miss statement; the story outlives the danger and becomes a bar story.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's repertoire of "funny war stories" may consist almost entirely of events in which no one died, so that the stories that include a death are the ones he does not tell.

**[E24d-15]. Cohesion: the clown as asset, ribbing as bonding, and a shared humor as a leadership trait.** (#75-76 unsw-1975 T5, #85 unsw-1607 T3 13:00, #2 unsw-894 T6 18:30, #58 unsw-2304 T8 31:30, #13, #39 unsw-1749 T8 13:00; #75 context consulted)
Seymour (1 RAR) says that being a "bit of a card" and a larrikin made the narrator "pretty safe" from the seniors, and "If your jokes are funny enough and other people laugh, well, you're an asset" (#75 T5 36:30; context consulted: the preceding question was about being initiated by senior soldiers). The same man, exhausted at the top of a long march in Townsville heat, threw off the pack and performed a mad guitar solo, which cheered the whole company; Seymour thinks that others would have been suspected of being on something or sick, "but because it was me it was all right" (#76 T5 39:00), and the OC and the CSM let it go. Fraser (army aviation) says infantry soldiers look like clowns and "when it comes down to do the job they're the guys you want watching your back" (#85 T3 14:00). Lampard says every platoon has one clown (#2). Von Berg (5 RAR platoon commander) says the corporals and sergeants of the leadership group shared the same sense of humor, "wasn't part of the selection process but it's the way it worked out," and that "we'd just laugh at the most crazy things and I think that in itself kept us going" (#58 T8 32:30), adding the rule "not at the expense of others in terms of those close to us" while laughing at friendly locals who did something silly. Rear-echelon ribbing ("good natured ribbing," #39) draws the line between those in the bush and those who were not. Three sergeants chosen by a marble draw named themselves "the three marbles," which Lennox says formed "a sort of bond or mateship which was important" (#31 T5 03:00).
*Mechanism:* humor is a trait that a unit sorts for (the clown who earns protection) and a bond the leaders share; ribbing marks both the in-group and its outer edge.
*Candidate application:* A character who was "the funny one" in his unit lost his protection and his audience together when he left it.

**[E24d-16]. Monotony, small comforts and deliberate lightness.** (#27 unsw-2199 T5 35:00, #104 unsw-1309 T7 16:00, #157 unsw-235 T2 13:30, #64 unsw-2056 T5 29:00, #55 unsw-1249 T8 34:00, #34 unsw-867 T5 22:30)
Not all of the humor is about danger. A tank driver walked the jungle each morning scolding an imaginary dog with a bowl and lead and "everyone used to crack up"; it "broke the monotony" (#27 Jones). RAN sailors pinned letters from home on the notice board for their unintended jokes (a mother who answered a question about two horse races by reporting that "a bloke called Galilee has won them both"; a letter about an aunt's teeth and new gas stove), which "broke the boredom up for a while" (#104 Bianchi). After a week without food a batman offered the narrator (a Kokoda-era officer) a fried egg and tomatoes that a sick captain could not eat, and a man living on boiled pumpkin was told to imagine himself in a boulevard restaurant eating soufflé: "Still tastes like bloody pumpkin to me. So we always had a laugh" (#157 McClean). On R&R in Vung Tau men compared themselves sexually, giggled and, in Coultman-Smith's account, held "no deep and meaningful discussions": "Life was serious enough. You didn't need to carry on those pressures on your off time" (#64 T5 30:00; sexual content paraphrased). On a fire support base where there was no alcohol "you had to lighten the circumstances," which meant a shaving-cream fight (#55 Walford, T8 34:00). Vietnamese colleagues fell over laughing at Australian nurses' tonal mistakes (#34 Bruce). Airmen in Tunis passed off a look-alike as a film star's brother to get a feast at a governor's palace, one of "a lot of things we never should have done, but that was the outlet," says Sly (#171 T2 28:00).
*Mechanism:* a deliberate shallow-play register is a protected off-duty space, used to keep the serious part from seeping in.
*Candidate application:* A character's insistence on the trivial in company may be a decision, not a lack of depth.

**[E24d-17]. Two timings, and narrators distinguish them: funny at the time (relief) and funny later (distance).** (#53 unsw-1249 T6 39:30, #69 unsw-1679 T3 28:00, #113 unsw-716 T5 40:00, #43 unsw-1706 T6 30:30, #42 unsw-1706 T6 25:00, #38 unsw-1749 T9 13:30)
Walford: it "really was funny when you think about it but at the time it wasn't so funny" (#53 T6 39:30; later-funny). Thompson, of a training accident in which a recruit's face was badly injured, says "it's not good to laugh about it, but it was funny at the time" (#69 T3 28:00; at-the-time-funny, restrained). Binstead, of the Burma railway: "We laugh now but it was serious at the time" (#113 T5 40:00). Fletcher (RAAF crewman), after a near-crash: "I think you need to look back and laugh about things, not to look at it with doom and gloom" (#43 T6 30:30). Fletcher on a medevac of a big man felled by heat exhaustion while the small man was fine: "sort of funny in a way but not funny," at pains to say it is not "funny" funny (#42 T6 25:00). Heslewood: at reunions "you tend to think of the funny things that happened rather than the bad things" (#38 T9 13:30).
*Mechanism:* the same event may be laughed at twice for different reasons (relief then reframing), and a narrator will mark which one is meant; the second laugh is a choice about what to dwell on.
*Candidate application:* A scene can show a veteran stopping himself mid-laugh to tell a listener that it was not funny at the time, which signals both the real event and his distance.

### 3. Limits and failure; and civilians, the funny version, and life after service

*(This section gets the fullest treatment because it bears most directly on a character living out of service. Findings 18-25 take the 24 "humor-stopped/limits" passages; 26-31 take the 28 "humor-after-service/civilians" passages.)*

**[E24d-18]. What the 24 "limits" passages actually establish: very little about jokes failing, a good deal about humor working too well.** (the 24: #11, #19, #30, #36, #38, #42, #55, #60, #69, #71, #72, #89, #93, #100, #113, #127, #143, #145, #146, #148, #155, #156, #166, #167)
The log warns the limits angle is "mostly implicit," and my read confirms it. Sorting the 24 by what each *shows* (a passage may fit two; I place it by its main weight):
- **A. The narrator's own frame breaks or is recognized as a symptom (4):** #30 (field-hospital radiographer), #11 (cartoon about a bombing), #19 (screams taken for clowning), #167 (a patient's giggling flashback).
- **B. A person present objects, or the target is a person (6):** #145 (a wounded officer's rebuke), #155 (laughing before a dead friend's mother), #89 (Sinai commander stopping ethnic naming), #55 (a man cocking a weapon behind the narrator and laughing), #60 (hazing at officer cadet school), #36 (no jokes about the enemy dead).
- **C. A category withheld by norm (1):** #156 (no joke about a dead comrade).
- **D. The conversation narrows to the funny, and the rest is not said (5):** #38, #148, #127, #143, #100.
- **E. Humor stands in for debriefing and the institution lets it (4):** #69, #71, #72, #166.
- **F. Qualified, weak or over-tagged (4):** #42 ("funny in a way but not funny"), #113 ("we laugh now but it was serious"), #93 (see [E24d-22]), #146 (an interviewer asks about failure and the answer describes a success).
**What the set does not contain:** no narrator says they *stopped being able* to joke after service (the nearest is a wife's report in #26 that a man had not smiled since the war); no passage shows a joke ending a friendship or a unit; only Lennox (#30) names a warning sign in the narrator's own humor, and Elliot (#11) says his stopped helping once he examined it; and apart from #145, #155, #89 and #55, there is no case of a joke landing on a hurt listener in real time. The interviewer's wording in #146 and #156 shows that when invited to confirm failure (asked whether there were times when the humor did not work), narrators tend to reaffirm the humor instead.
*Candidate application:* Written from this evidence, humor having stopped is less a veteran's statement than a thing seen in the silences, the selections and what others stopped telling the veteran. A companion who notices the change is doing the work the passages cannot.

**[E24d-19]. The self-recognized warning sign: when the joke starts to disgust the teller.** (#30 unsw-1669 T6 19:00-20:30; #11 unsw-575 T6 09:30-11:00; #19 unsw-2152 T6 17:30)
Lennox (national serviceman, radiographer in the 1st Australian Field Hospital) describes developing a black or sick kind of humor, detached, that Lennox did not like and was "not particularly proud of" (T6 19:00); the example given is a routine breath-holding instruction addressed to the body of a dead young man on the x-ray table (wording restrained). Looking back with "a degree of disgust," Lennox calls it "my warning sign that perhaps I'd been there too long" and a sign of needing "to get home fairly quickly," otherwise the outcome would have been "gone troppo in my underpants" (T6 19:30-20:00); the humor was "losing any contact with humanity" (T6 20:30). In the same family, Elliot (RAN, HMAS Hobart) says a cartoon about the bombing of a school bus helped the ship's company deal with it ("that's how you dealt with it ... it wasn't your fault"), but the thought "I'm responsible too" followed and "that makes it a little bit hard" (#11 T6 11:00): the humor works until someone examines it. Cavanough, hearing screams after an explosion nearby, assumed first that someone was clowning, since, in Cavanough's words, the men always had a warped sense of humor, then learned hours later of casualties, and says "we were robots ... just keep working" (#19 T6 18:30-19:00).
*Mechanism:* the humor is a tool the teller can watch being used; the signal of overuse is the teller's own disgust or the detachment it implies, and the three men above name the stop signal differently (disgust, second thoughts, a misread sound).
*Candidate application:* A veteran can know exactly which joke he made that marked the line, and may have told no one; the line is private.

**[E24d-20]. The wounded man's rebuke, and the answer "What do you want us to do, cry?"** (#145 unsw-44 T1 34:00-35:30; #146 unsw-44 T8 04:30)
Blundell (2/3rd Bn signaller) describes his first heavy shelling: men lay on the ground making wisecracks ("That was close"), a dud landed ten yards away, and a young officer who had been hit by a shell was brought back seriously injured while the group was "doing this bit of laughing." The officer was "very annoyed" and said: "If you'd seen what I had seen you wouldn't be laughing." The ringleader, Reggie Burns, answered "What do you want us to do, cry?" and Blundell says "That was the end of that bit" (T1 35:00). Asked what lay behind the humor: "a bit of nerves ... we were laughing. Somewhat nervously, but we weren't crying that's a certainty" (T1 35:30). This is the only passage in the 172 in which a person in the setting objects to the laughter and the reply is recorded; the answer does not apologize; it frames the alternatives as laughing or crying. Elsewhere in the same interview, asked directly whether there were times the humor did not work, Blundell says "if someone came out with an appropriate remark it would have an effect" and recounts a pinned-down exchange over a match with a soldier nicknamed for his coloring (restrained), which is an example of it working (#146 T8 05:00-05:30).
*Mechanism:* the license to joke next to a wound belongs to the exposed group, and a wounded outsider's objection is answered with the group's own necessity.
*Candidate application:* A scene in which a newly wounded man objects to the group's jokes and an old hand answers with a question, not an apology, would be consistent with this record.

**[E24d-21]. Caught laughing before the bereaved, and the code that the dead comrade is not joked about.** (#155 unsw-235 T9 24:30-26:00; #156 unsw-235 T5 22:30-23:00; #100 unsw-761 T1 18:00; context consulted for #156)
McClean (2/8th Bn and 39th Bn officer; Kokoda and Gona) was one of three young officers called the Three Musketeers; the other two were killed. Back home, at a hotel gathering of returned men, McClean says "I died a thousand deaths when I was caught laughing" at the party as the mother of one of the dead men came in to ask questions, and had to tell her how her son died, withering inside (T9 26:00) at being seen "carrying on as though nothing had happened." Asked, in another part of the interview, whether the death of a man killed by an air-dropped supply box would later raise a gallows laugh, McClean answered "No, never. ... There's a certain respect among the men for one another" (#156 T5 23:00; the same narrator jokes freely about hunger and forced volunteers, #157-158, so the refusal is specific to the dead). Joyce (RAN, Korea) recalls laughing as a child, with the siblings, whenever a thunderstorm drove their father, a First World War veteran, to hide under the bed, and being boxed on the ears by their mother; only later, at Anzac Day events where Joyce's father was an RSL president, hearing First World War men's stories, did "reality set in" and Joyce realized "that war is not a laughing game" (#100 T1 19:30).
*Mechanism:* the norm that the dead comrade is not a butt is an in-group norm; it is felt most sharply when an outsider who belonged to the dead man (a mother, a child) is present and the group's register is on show.
*Candidate application:* A character may carry a specific memory of the one time the register was visible to the wrong person. The shame is not that he joked but that he was seen.

**[E24d-22]. **[E24d-23]. Humor as the substitute for processing, and the institution that permits it.** (#69 unsw-1679 T3 28:00, #71 unsw-1679 T7 18:30, #72 unsw-1679 T7 23:00, #66 unsw-1182 T2 24:00, #80 unsw-2461 T8 31:30)

**[E24d-29]. Homecoming ironies and humor as a way to remember the dead as living.** (#122 unsw-607 T8 28:30, #96 unsw-1108 T3 21:30, #26 unsw-1038 T9 20:30, #68 unsw-1182 T3 16:30, #111 unsw-716 T7 12:00)

**[E24d-32]. **[E24d-33]. Captive humor as a survival doctrine, and its after-life.** (#113 unsw-716 T5 40:00, #115 unsw-716 T7 40:00, #108 unsw-716 T3 02:30, #111 unsw-716 T7 12:00, #128 unsw-1021 T9 32:30, #47 unsw-1399 T2 30:00, #167)

Asked how Binstead could laugh about it, the answer was "We laugh now but it was serious at the time" (#113 T5 40:00). Concerts were permitted from time to time, and English prisoners were "very good comedians" who played the women's parts (#113 T5 40:30). Afterward, Young says that thinking of prison life means only "the funny things," that Young meets fellow prisoners in Melbourne and they say "the funny things," with "never any thoughts of the horrible things," and that, for those now in very different civilian situations, they "remind them occasionally ... how they'd look when they were in prison again and bring them down to size" (#128 T9 33:00). *Mechanism:* humor is a taught doctrine in the captive community, paired with mateship, passed on by leaders; its after-life is the reunion funny-only register ([E24d-24]) and a leveling ritual among ex-prisoners.
*Candidate application:* A character who was held captive may have been taught, by a leader, a one-sentence doctrine about humor, which he repeats as if it were the whole of what he learned.

A few lines after the passage Thompson says the men wanted to try to have one of the local wild animals "like they do in the movies" and were refused as "too Australian, too politically correct" (T8 38:30-39:00).

*Candidate application:* A peacekeeper-type character may be most comfortable telling the vehicle-in-the-hole story and quietly steering away from the water-point story; the tell is a narrator announcing that the account will stay away from the bad stuff.

**[E24d-37]. Rwanda, East Timor, Sinai, Bougainville: pranks upward, register-policing downward, and the startle at home.** (#80-82 unsw-2461; #83-86 unsw-1607; #87-90 unsw-2021; #165-167 unsw-1764; #66)
*Rwanda* (Ramsey, colonel, medical contingent): the three passages contain no joke from the mission's central events; #80 says colleagues "reminisce on the good" and then bring out really dark humor ("do you remember?") without giving any ([E24d-23]); #81 recounts taking a twelve-seat mahogany table, in the dark, from a deputy force commander's bungalow (the contingent's own house had been stripped by looters) and hiding it at an open house by pulling a cover over it and sitting on it, with the interviewer asking whether they had owned up: "God no, we were Australians. Nothing wrong with that" (#81 T8 33:30); #82 recounts a regimental dinner after return where a gavel's crack made the narrator and the man beside jump "through the roof." *East Timor and aviation* (Fraser, army aviation loadmaster; later East Timor, though the passages are home service): foam lamingtons laid out for a colleague "renowned for being a pig" who ate three bites to avoid insulting the cook (#83), a nickname taken from a prime minister's name (#84), an infantry section's clown (#85), and a bedside visit on a fractured pelvis (#86); no passage is from East Timor operations. *Sinai* (Hamilton-Smith, contingent commander): the commander stops ethnic naming (#89, [E24d-22]); the earlier cliff-fall and the troops' mock refusal to jump are unit-level humor from the SAS career (#87, #88, #90). *Bougainville* (Andaloro): peace-monitoring commentary (#166) and naval hospital pranks (#165, #167).
*Mechanism:* of the 25 peacekeeping-era passages, only a handful carry humor from the operations themselves; the rest are unit culture. The "peacekeeping humor" in this set is therefore chiefly a commander's task of policing a register, a veteran's debrief, and a post-mission startle.
*Candidate application:* For a character from a peacekeeping-type unit, the on-mission humor to draw on is practical and local (looting a table, a pit trap), and the dark part may be implied as something the unit does not recount.

**[E24d-38]. How the 1990s-era humor differs from the war-fighting wars, where visible, and the limits of that comparison.** (counts from the Tallies table; #72, #74, #76, #89)
By the harvest's tags the 25 peacekeeping-era passages carry a smaller share of gallows humor (2 of 25, 8%, against 15 of 57, 26%, for Vietnam, 14 of 66, 21%, for WWII, and 7 of 17, 41%, for Korea) and of humor about the dead and wounded (1 of 25, against 10 of 57 and 13 of 66), and a similar or larger share explained as coping (12 of 25, 48%, against 25 of 57, 44%, and 19 of 66, 29%) and as limited or stopped (6 of 25, 24%, against 8 of 57, 14%). In content: (i) *low lethality is stated as the reason:* "No-one was getting killed" (#74); (ii) *the institution is present in the jokes:* debriefs and psychologists (#66), discharge for admitting problems (#72), drug tests (#76: other men would have loved to do the guitar solo but would not, "because they'd get drug tested or whatever," so only the known clown could), "politically correct" restrictions (#74 context) and a commander's ban on ethnic names (#89); (iii) *larrikin as an explicit self-label* (#72, #75). **Caution:** six narrators and 25 passages, selected by keyword, interviewed 4-12 years after events, with Somalia and Rwanda on a small base; the shares reflect what the harvest could reach and what narrators said when asked, not rates of joking; and the dead-and-wounded gap may simply reflect that the harvested peacekeepers were not asked, or did not say.
*Candidate application:* A character from a post-war peacekeeping force can be written with less gallows material and more institutional awareness, with the real weight placed in the debrief and the censoring of his own account.

### 7. Differences by war and branch (counts, with the caveats above)

**[E24d-39]. By war: what the counts show and what they cannot.** (the Tallies table; #4, #5, #10, #11, #30, #58, #61, #117, #147)
Harvest tags by group (shares are of passages in the group, not of speech): *coping-explained* is highest in the peacekeeping-era group (12 of 25, 48%) and in Vietnam (25 of 57, 44%), lower in WWII (19 of 66, 29%) and very low in Korea (2 of 17); *gallows humor* is 26% of Vietnam passages, 21% of WWII, 41% of Korea (7 of 17, four of them from the British gunner Ersser and Schunemann) and 8% of peacekeeping; *humor about the dead or wounded* is 18% of Vietnam, 20% of WWII, 29% of Korea, 4% of peacekeeping; *command/brass* is the largest target in Vietnam (19 of 57, 33%) and in the peacekeeping group (8 of 25, 32%), 26% in WWII; *pranks* are 26% of Vietnam passages and 17% of WWII. The *enemy* is a minor target in Vietnam (4 of 57, 7%); of the 11 WWII enemy-tagged passages, 8 concern captors in prison camps ([E24d-32]), leaving 3 about enemy troops in battle. Narrators' own words differ by war: "black humor" is used by Vietnam and WWII narrators (Leis, Elliot, von Berg, Blundell), "sick humor" by Vietnam narrators (Lennox, Coultman-Smith), a "weird" or "warped" sense of humor by a WWII sergeant (Reiter) and a Vietnam national serviceman (Cavanough), and "gallows humor" appears only after an interviewer supplies it ([E24d-43]). **What the counts cannot show:** the harvest's group sizes reflect how it was built. The log reports that about 626 of the 828 interviews hitting the search term `humour` are WWII, yet the harvest holds 66 WWII passages (38%), because the Vietnam, Korea, Malaya and peacekeeping material was reached by theme restrictions and each saved narrator was chosen for density of humor talk. Korea (5 narrators, 17 passages) and Malaya/Confrontation (3 narrators, 7 passages, 6 of them nicknames) are too small to compare.
*Candidate application:* The strongest contrast the data does support is between narrators who volunteer the language of "coping" (Vietnam and later) and those who tell stories and stop (WWII), which suggests a character's explanatory vocabulary should match the era and the decades since.

**[E24d-40]. By branch and rank: the officers' view of being the target, and the other ranks' view of the penalty.** (#154, #88, #87, #90, #81; #67, #3, #144; #101-106, #168-172)
By branch (my reading; see Tallies C): Army 130 passages from 29 narrators, Navy 18 from 6, air 17 from 3, a British unit 4 from 1, civilian nursing 3 from 1. The three branch contrasts I can support are modest. (a) *Navy:* the ship is a closed community, and narrators emphasize boredom humor (letters on the notice board, #104), naming rules by surname (#103, "The navy was just notorious for nicknames," T4 36:30) and the captain's nickname (#105); the RAN passages hold 5 of the 42 nickname tags (28% of RAN passages, against 24% overall), so this is no strong skew. (b) *Air:* the helicopter crews' prank war with the SAS (#41, #46), the fighter squadron's radio-net wit and beer-and-belongings routine (#168, Sly: the fate is individual, a voice that stops on the net), and the prisoners' bowing chain (#125). (c) *Army infantry:* the platoon is the unit, and the targets are the CSM, the company commander and the rear echelon. By rank, five officer narrators (von Berg, Hamilton-Smith, Radford, McClean, Ramsey; 19 passages) supply 12 of the 50 command-tagged passages (63% of their passages, against 25% for the other 153): officers tell of being pranked and of taking it well (#154, #87, #90, #81), whereas other-rank narrators tell of the reprisal and its cost (#67 field punishment; #3 CS gas; #144 the horseman who could not take it). The same behavior is narrated as affection from above and as risk from below. Medical narrators (Lennox, Bruce, Andaloro) give the most direct accounts of humor in the presence of a body or a patient ([E24d-19], [E24d-34]).
*Candidate application:* The same prank on an officer can be written as a story told warmly by the officer and as a charge sheet by the private who did it.

### 8. Australian specifics, as documented

**[E24d-41]. The self-image: dry, larrikin, mateship, and no tolerance for officers who take themselves seriously; and what the narrators say about others.** (#13, #14, #47, #75, #72, #88, #141, #152, #54, #166; #48 for "larrikinism")
Documented self-descriptions: Cavanough says the Australian way is to have a go at a mate, which "strengthens our mateship" (#13 T2 15:00), and contrasts it with American war films' fuss over calling a medic, whereas "our way" was to secure the area first and fetch the mate afterward (#14). Schunemann (Korea) calls the Australian humor "very good," "dry," "top class," with "no crummy stuff," and says dirty stories "don't really interest Australians, of that age anyway in that era" (#47 T2 31:00), yet Schunemann's own stories include an agricultural double-entendre at an O Group (#52) and the set as a whole is full of bawdy items (#5, #7, #64, #138, #141), so the self-description is an ideal rather than a record. Medcalf (Bougainville) says humor is "automatic and natural" to Australians and "any troops," and that German or British rigid discipline would not work on Australians (#141 T7 22:00-22:30). Hamilton-Smith: "never ever underestimate" the sense of humor of the Australian soldier, and if an officer takes themself too seriously "the Aussie soldier will soon let them know" (#88 T3 19:30). "Larrikin" appears as a self-label (Seymour, #75; Thompson, #72), as Schunemann's word for his generation (#48), and as the interviewer's remark in #152. "Mateship" is the explanatory frame in at least five passages (#13, #31, #40, #113, #115). Others' humor is described only in stereotype: Americans as having none (Schunemann, #47), New Zealanders as having a funny sense of it and doing things differently (Walford, #54), British-style humor as the dry model (#47). **These are narrators' self-stereotypes, not data on other armies**; Hamilton-Smith's Sinai passage (#89) shows the cost, a national sense of humor that "had to be pulled into check." The one public ritual that carries the register is Anzac Day: a drunk night in memory (#68), a father's veterans' stories (#100), a march broadcast (#133).
*Candidate application:* A culture's self-image about its own humor (dry, given to ribbing, indifferent to rank) can be written as a story it tells about itself, contradicted in the details by what its members actually do.

**[E24d-42]. Idiom, address, and humor across a language gap (who is laughing at whom).** (#48 unsw-1399 T2 25:30, #49 unsw-1399 T2 35:00, #34 unsw-867 T5 22:30, #25, #110, #112, #114, #51, #163-164)
*Idiom the narrators use unglossed* (the archive's brackets gloss some): "have a go at," "take the mickey out of," "piss-take" (the archive glosses it as a practical joke, #79), "pull the piss" (#85), "a card" (a comic, #75), "bloke," "digger," "nasho" (a national serviceman), "troppo" (driven mad by the tropics, #30, #151), "crook" (ill, #7; "go crook," angry, #112), "bludge" (to shirk, #112), "on the piss" (drinking, #75), "wag" (a joker, #11), "Pommy" (a Briton, #29). *Address:* aircrew used first names across ranks (#45), an artillery sergeant was "Serge" (#55). *Language play:* in occupied Japan an Australian turned Japanese politeness phrases into English-sounding nonsense ("Harry's gaiters," and a phrase about touching a mustache), and local Japanese adopted them back and learned "you beaut" and "bonza," which the narrator says they enjoyed (#48); Australians and Japanese in a village cinema laughed at opposite moments, which the narrator reports as "they were laughing when we felt sad, and we felt like laughing when they were sad" (#49 T2 35:30), after which the narrator mixed up the footwear left outside (#49). Locals laughed at Australians' mistakes: tonal Vietnamese (#34), rear-echelon men diving for cover (#25), a bilingual sister at a patient mix-up (#34). *Uncomprehending targets:* prisoners called a guard Boofhead and told him it meant "Japanese gentlemen" (#110); a local worker was taught an English insult for the RSM and recited it on parade without understanding it, and was named for a sexual-health term he did not know, which he "loved" (#163-164; restrained: ethnic and sexual wording); mock-Chinese babble was spoken into a tapped telephone line to a British officer (#51; restrained). Pidgin gave a comic line to the prisoners on the sandbank: "officer speak" (#114).
*Mechanism:* the language gap supplies a present audience that cannot understand; the joke's pleasure is that the target is in the room. The same gap makes the Australians the butt when locals laugh.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who served among a population that spoke another language may keep a stock of mangled foreign phrases that were in-group jokes, and may not recognize until later whom they were at the expense of.

**[E24d-43]. The interviewer's hand: some terms are supplied, some refused, and some answers pivot.** (#1 unsw-894 T6 18:30, #30, #61 unsw-2056 T7 15:30, #93 unsw-1376 T5 33:30, #113 unsw-716 T5 40:00, #132, #146 unsw-44 T8 04:30, #150 unsw-1186 T8 13:30, #156 unsw-235 T5 22:30)
The interviewer supplies the label gallows humor in #1 (Lampard repeats it with assent, T6 18:30); the phrase dark Aussie humor "that comes out in times of stress" in #150 (Simmons answers "Oh yes, it does," and a thief's stories follow); "tension release" in #132; black humor in #93 and #36; a sick sense of humor in #61 (after the narrator had already said "sick"); the television comparison in #30; "such a larrikin" in #152. Two narrators refuse the frame: McClean answers a question that invokes the Australian sense of humor and its penchant for gallows humor with "No, never" (#156); Heslewood, asked whether there were black jokes about the morbid scene of the battlefield clearing, says "No not really. I can't remember anything" (#36 T6 24:00). Others pivot: Blundell, asked whether humor ever failed, gives an example of it working (#146); Ersser, asked whether anyone was disturbed, names a man whose fear was about something else (#93). *Narrators' own words (my count, by passage):* "black humor" (6: Leis ×2, Elliot ×2, von Berg, Blundell), "sick humor" (3: Lennox, Coultman-Smith ×2), "dark humor" (1: Ramsey), "weird" or "warped" sense of humor (4: Reiter ×2, Cavanough ×2). **Two readings follow:** the harvest's *gallows-humor* tag (38) is the harvester's category, and at least five of its passages (#1, #91, #93, #150, #156) owe the tag to an interviewer's wording ("gallows," "dark," "black," "deathly"); and the log's note that many hits on the search phrases `gallows humour` and `practical jokes` were interviewer questions answered "no" (interviews 69 and 849, unsaved) means refusals are underrepresented here.
*Candidate application:* A character from a culture that does not name its dark humor may accept the label when a civilian offers it and wear it uneasily; the people who named it first were not him.

## Pattern roll-up

*Organized by the lens headings the extraction rules set; only those the 172 passages speak to. Numbers refer to the findings above.*

**Functions.** Release and sanity are the veterans' own words ([E24d-12]); fear passes through a joke or a silence ([E24d-13]); the relief-laugh canon is accident-without-casualty slapstick ([E24d-14]); the clown is protected and the leaders share the humor ([E24d-15]); humor fills monotony and is a deliberate off-duty lightness ([E24d-16]); in captivity it was a taught doctrine with mateship ([E24d-33]); nicknames fix a fact and a judgment ([E24d-1], [E24d-2]).

**Targets.** Nicknames, command and the institution's voice (documents, signs, briefers) ([E24d-2], [E24d-7]), rear echelon and other units ([E24d-5], [E24d-39]), the enemy mostly as spectacle or as captors ([E24d-8], [E24d-32]), oneself and the nobody-hurt accident ([E24d-14]), the wounded by their own and their comrades' license ([E24d-9]), the dead by event, ritual and euphemism rather than as a person ([E24d-10]).

**Forms and mechanics.** Nicknames made of surname, body, event, borrowed character, role or inversion ([E24d-1]); hoaxes that borrow the institution's own forms (a board, a badge, a notice) ([E24d-4]); reprisal pranks with a grammar ([E24d-5]); pranks up the chain tested by the officer's reaction ([E24d-6]); deadpan and the quip-in-place-of-a-laugh ([E24d-13]); institutional euphemism and signage as raw material ([E24d-7], [E24d-10]); parody songs, concerts, imaginary pets, reunion catchphrases ([E24d-11]); language mangling and the uncomprehending target ([E24d-42]).

**Rules of license.** Rank removes the nickname license ([E24d-2]); do not rebel against a nickname and the bearer's toughness limits it ([E24d-3]); the officer who takes a prank "in good part" is accepted ([E24d-6]); the wounded joke first and the dead comrade is not the butt ([E24d-9], [E24d-21]); weapons, singling out a person, ethnic naming in front of those named, and the enemy as a person are the edges ([E24d-22]); who may hear it is judged by the veteran ([E24d-27]).

**Limits and failure modes.** The 24 limits passages show humor working too well more often than failing ([E24d-18]); the warning sign is the teller's disgust ([E24d-19]); a wounded man's objection is answered with "What do you want us to do, cry?" ([E24d-20]); being caught laughing before the bereaved ([E24d-21]); humor substituting for debriefing, with discharge as the cost of disclosure ([E24d-23]); funny-only telling and the fading of detail ([E24d-24]); a laugh that is a symptom ([E24d-25]).

**Time course.** At-the-time and later laughter are distinguished by the narrators ([E24d-17]); at reunions only the funny is told ([E24d-24]); the older cohort modeled funny-only for their children ([E24d-21], [E24d-24]); nicknames outlive the unit ([E24d-3]); a few narrators reframe the dead as living through the funniest memory ([E24d-29]).

**Civilian-facing register.** The funny version goes outward and the serious goes unsaid ([E24d-26], [E24d-27]); a veteran says the register is what "in civvie street you'd be in jail for" ([E24d-27]); an official reads a laugh at a ritual as disrespect and the veteran answers with service ([E24d-28]); the register is preserved in emergency services, with colleagues, in a veterans' counseling group ([E24d-30]); an outsider's view that constant laughing may be a fault ([E24d-31]).

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics.** *The set has no defector or deserter.* The closest material: prisoners' covert insults and loss-of-face jokes aimed at captors ([E24d-32]); a unit's reprisal against an unpopular commander with a penalty ([E24d-5]); jokes at the institution's documents ([E24d-7]); a man whose only audience for the past is the comrades who agree not to ask ([E24d-24]); the "bring them down to size" ritual among ex-prisoners now in civilian life ([E24d-33]).

**Cross-cultural.** The Australian self-image ([E24d-41]), the language-gap humor with Japanese and Vietnamese hosts ([E24d-42]), and a commander's ban on ethnic naming in a Sinai peacekeeping setting ([E24d-22]).

**Craft observations.** The narrators' craft: the straight face, the quip in place of a laugh, understatement ([E24d-13]); the nobody-was-hurt story as the retold canon ([E24d-14]); the funny ones are protected by role ([E24d-15]); the interviewer's role in what a record looks like ([E24d-43]).

## Gaps and cautions

- **Keyword harvest, interview-level search.** The 172 passages are the humor-dense stretches of 40 interviews chosen from 117 downloaded, from a search whose terms are British-spelled and whose negative phrases failed. The set represents what narrators said about humor *when they said the words*, and over-represents those who answered "yes" to an interviewer's prompt. Many gallows-humor and practical-joke hits were interviewer questions answered "no," and those narrators (interviews 69 and 849, among others) are not in the set.
- **Excluded by project boundary: bodily-waste humor.** About a dozen items across eleven interviews were skipped (log §5). In several interviews that is the richest comic register, so the absence of waste-based humor from this file is a boundary decision, not a finding.
- **Afghanistan, Iraq and the Gulf War II are absent** (interviews filmed 1999-2004); Gulf War I is almost empty; the 1990s group is six narrators.
- **Women: three narrators, nine passages** (5%). Two further women-service interviews are noted by the log as unsaved (154, 826).
- **Context consulted (full transcripts) where a passage's meaning needed it:** #60 (von Berg, Portsea hazing), #74 and #73 (Thompson, Somalia), #75 (Seymour, the question was about initiation), #93 (Ersser, the identity of Hall), #143 (Medcalf, rest of the memory sentence), #156 (McClean, the supply-drop death and the casualty-letter euphemism). The lines they supply sit just outside the harvested window.
- **Hearsay and secondhand items:** #47's claim that Australian prisoners in Korea survived because of their humor (no source named; the number of Australian prisoners in Korea was small, but I have not checked the figure here), #96 (an uncle's story told by a nephew), #139 (an enemy comic shot, told of another platoon), #167 (the patient's Vietnam captivity, "I think ... or something"). **Do not cite any of these as an event.**
- **Duplicates:** #131 and #132 (Rowell's torn trousers) are very likely the same incident told twice; I counted them as two passages, not two events.
- **Tags overread:** #93's *limits* tag and #146's *limits* tag do not hold up on the transcript; several *gallows-humor* tags follow the interviewer's wording; one *humor-about-self* tag (#86) fits a hospital bedside, not the narrator's mockery of self.
- **Quoted ethnic and sexual wording.** Several narrators use racial or ethnic labels for the enemy and for locals, nicknames built on ethnicity or skin tone, and sexual wording; none is reproduced here (register). #68's narrator attributes a friend's humor style to the friend's Aboriginal descent, which is the speaker's own guess and is not evidence.
- **Memory and performance.** These are recollections 35-60 years old, told to an interviewer on camera for a national archive; the archive's own FAQ says they are not an accurate historical record. The audio and video, where tone and laughter would be, were never touched.
- **Spelling.** The transcripts use British spelling (the -our endings, for one); quotations here avoid the affected words or paraphrase them, and the only British spellings in this file are literal search strings inside code marks; no quotation has been altered except for straightening the archive's typographic quotation marks and apostrophes.
- **Source identifiers.** No `source_manifest` row existed for this source at the time of this pass; cited by the archive's own numbers.
- **Sex and pronouns.** The harvest gives no narrator's sex; I use no pronoun for narrators except for those in a women's service (AWAS, WRANS) and third parties whom the passages themselves label. In the "candidate application" lines, which describe fictional soldier-type characters, I follow the project's wording for the defector and use "he" for a generic soldier-type character.
