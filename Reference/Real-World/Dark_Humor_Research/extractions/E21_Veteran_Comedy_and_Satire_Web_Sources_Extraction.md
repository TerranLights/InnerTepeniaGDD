## Source-shape notes (read first)

**Citation convention.** The web-page text copies have no page numbers, so I cite the source ID plus the section heading or the subject of the passage. Each file starts with SOURCE / TITLE / FETCHED lines (all fetched 2026-10-05). Many copies end in site boilerplate (related-article lists, "Loading comments," store listings, subscription prompts, a long index of other VVAW essays); I read it and skipped it. Spelling in my own prose is American; quotations are given exactly as the copies read.

**The big caution for this whole unit.** Apart from one antiwar essay and one interview, these 27 pages are **journalism, promotion, reviews and personal essays; none is research.** Nothing here has a sample, a control, a measure or a method that this extraction can check. Their evidence value is as **testimony**: what veteran humorists, and the people who promote or write about them, say the humor does, with a few documented jokes. Where a page cites numbers (the ASAP evaluation in WA-053, the "14 percent" in WA-030, the WWP claims in WA-050), the numbers are second-hand, partial or unpublished, and I flag it each time. The programs described (ASAP Comedy Bootcamp, Comedy Warriors, Comedy Vets, Operation Comedy, GIs of Comedy, Wicked Comedy, Rapid Fire Comedy Tour) are **civilian-facing, performance-oriented, mostly post-service, mostly post-9/11 U.S. ventures**; they show how veterans package humor for audiences, not how a unit jokes in the field. The pages mostly cover Army and Marine veterans of Iraq and Afghanistan, with Vietnam only in WA-006 and WA-007 (WA-007 being the only non-American voice). Women, minorities and enlisted-versus-officer voices appear only where a page gives them a line, and I mark where.

## Findings

### From WA-006 (Levy and Erony, "War Jokes Wanted")

**[E21-1]. An open call for the most lawless war jokes mostly drew clean ones: the written, stranger-facing register is sanitized.** (WA-006, "Picture This")
Marc Levy (a Vietnam infantry medic, 1970) and Susan Erony (an artist and art historian) planned a book of combat jokes with essays on why soldiers turn to dark humor, and advertised on a veterans' watchdog site, in Army Times and through Iraq and Afghanistan veterans' associations. They wanted "tasteless, obscene, unforgivable lawless jokes," not the "smiley face on horror" of the Reader's Digest feature "Humor in Uniform." By their account the veterans "struggled with the concept"; most sent "cute vignettes, sanitized anecdotes," or clean bits on inter-service rivalry, and only about half a dozen items conveyed what the authors sought. No count, response rate or description of respondents is given: this is the authors' impression, not data.
*Candidate application:* What a soldier will offer a stranger who asks for a war joke in writing is the harmless kind; the rawest material lives in other rooms.

**[E21-2]. The authors' account of what the dark joke does, and what they say happens to it after the war.** (WA-006, "Drop Dead Funny," "Picture This")
In literary language: grim jokes "create and reflect a necessary distance" that lets soldiers keep doing their work; the joker "assumes power over the powerless dead"; "standing meaning on its head" wipes out the imprint of horror for a moment; men who have hunted and been hunted have had mercy "bled" out of them and can mock what they have become. They quote a Holocaust survivor ("view life as a black comedy") and a Vietnam-era Marine officer's summary of the cliche ("If you don't laugh, you'll cry"). On time course they assert that, carried home, the jokes may go to uncomprehending civilians, to other veterans, or be set aside, and that humor "misunderstood on the home front" and "freighted with secret guilt and shame" may over time "lose its healing cautery." That last point is asserted, not shown. The piece is antiwar commentary ("no laughing matter" is its title and last line), so the humor is used as evidence against war.
*Candidate application:* A veteran may feel his old jokes have stopped cauterizing: they still come out, but the relief is gone.

**[E21-3]. Documented example: a sergeant major's literal reply to a colonel's lament at a row of covered dead.** (WA-006, "Public Jester")
A joke from a Vietnam veteran and novelist (Larry Heinemann), summarized without its wording. A colonel and his sergeant major inspect the American dead laid out after a heavy fight; the colonel grows shaken, laments their youth and asks the sergeant major to guess their ages; the sergeant major answers the question literally, in a deadpan that ends the topic. The authors read it as Sancho Panza upending Don Quixote, with understatement as the weapon, and note "only veterans laughed." The target is the officer's idealizing sentiment and the rank gap in who may grieve aloud; the dead are treated as simply final, not mocked.
*Candidate application:* An enlisted move: answer an officer's rhetorical grief with flat literalism, which can also be a kind of respect that cannot be said directly.

**[E21-4]. Documented example: counting the enemy dead, and a fire-team leader's demonstration (restrained).** (WA-006, "Drop Dead Funny")
A Marine lieutenant-turned-author (Fred Tomasello Jr.), prefacing with "I never thought anyone would want to hear this," contributed an account from February 1968 near the Demilitarized Zone. After an enemy attack, the platoon had to count dead whose remains were badly damaged by artillery. A fire-team member balked, citing booby-trap fear and revulsion; the team leader shoved him, shamed him, and showed him how to do the job by handling one body in a way that ended in a gag offered to the lieutenant as a superstitious ritual. **The physical description, dialogue and punchline are deliberately not reproduced.** Civilians find it repugnant, the authors say, while combat veterans "belly laugh" at it forty years later. Their reading: the leader pokes fun at superstition and at himself, assumes the officer will laugh with him, and treats counting corpses as routine work that must be done right. Structure: fear is shamed by a peer, the shaming becomes a demonstration, the demonstration becomes a joke offered upward.
*Candidate application:* Humor around handling the dead is a work practice that removes the hesitation that would stop the job, and inviting the officer in is part of it.

**[E21-5]. Documented example: the "Little Bobby" joke, a home-from-war joke about an uncle (restrained).** (WA-006, "Little Bobby")
Contributed by the webmaster of a site for a light infantry brigade (the authors note that the brigade was one of several units at My Lai). A schoolboy offers a violent family story about an uncle in Vietnam as his classroom "fairy tale," a catalog of atrocity-type violence told with relish; asked by the teacher what moral could come of it, he answers with a one-line profane warning about provoking the uncle when drinking. **The details and the punchline are not reproduced.** The authors' reading: naive and prudish (female) teacher and classmates are pitted against an impish male braggart, and the obscenity "slaps you in the face" like a Zen master's blow, carrying the uncle's war-born malice while shielding the boy from the horror. The cast itself, women and children as the squeamish audience, is worth noting.
*Candidate application:* A joke circulated about the veteran, told from outside, with family as foil, is also how a community says a true thing about what he is like when drinking.

**[E21-6]. Documented example: a poem on a field incident, vehicle nicknames and a closing piece of advice.** (WA-006, "Let The Bad Times Roll")
The authors reproduce (by permission) "A Bummer" by Michael Casey, from *Obscenities* (Yale Younger Poets, 1972; reissued 2002), described here: a column of tracked vehicles moves single file through a farmer's rice paddies; the farmer begins hitting the lead vehicle with a rake and will not stop, and swings at the commander who comes to talk; the vehicles then go side by side through the field. The poem lists the vehicles' nicknames (some bawdy, some pop-culture jokes) and ends with flat mock advice to anyone with a farm in Vietnam and a house in hell. Mechanisms: a bureaucratic understatement for a ruined livelihood (the title), in-group vehicle naming, a closing ironic maxim. The authors call it mordant and timeless. It is a poem by one poet, not a joke told in a unit.
*Candidate application:* The humor can arrive as an understated caption over an act the speaker knows to be wrong; naming vehicles and kit is a unit habit worth giving a vehicle or robot culture.

### From WA-007 (Levy interviewing Bao Ninh, Part One)

**[E21-7]. A North Vietnamese veteran-author on unit talk: sex, food, home, regional teasing, and a rule of silence about battle.** (WA-007)
In 1998 at a writers' conference in Boston, Levy (a U.S. veteran of the 1970 Cambodia invasion) interviewed Bao Ninh, author of *The Sorrow of War* and a veteran of a brigade in which, by Levy's account, ten of five hundred survived. Three hours through a translator, reported in Levy's paraphrase. The humor-relevant content is three pieces. (1) Troops most often spoke about sex and food and "exchanged jokes"; soldiers came from different cities and provinces, and jokes grew out of that mix. (2) The one example: men teasing one another about the women of a particular province, comparing their bodies (restrained; paraphrase only). It is regional-stereotype banter aimed at women who are not present. (3) To keep morale up, the unit avoided talking about past or recent battles; Bao Ninh also would not speak of the marches, hunger or air attack, and Levy found him "remarkably reticent" and wondered whether his answer on morale (that the troops fought for their country's independence) was "doctrinaire." Mourning was done without religious ceremony, Bao Ninh said, because the troops were communists: "mourned and buried." The interview ends with an unprompted "We were human beings."
*Candidate application:* Talk in a unit under extreme risk can be organized by what it avoids: sex, food and home are allowed, and the war is the unspoken subject. Regional-origin teasing is a cheap, steady banter source in a mixed-origin force.
*Cross-use:* A faction with an ideological bar on religious rite may keep its grief to understatement.

### From WA-019 (Doctrine Man retires)

**[E21-8]. A staff-officer cartoonist on why the strip existed and what it was for.** (WA-019)
Retired Army Col. Steve Leonard (a logistician early on, the 1991 Gulf War, several Iraq deployments, then a strategist from 2006 until retiring at the end of 2014) began "Doctrine Man" at the Army's Combined Arms Doctrine Directorate at Fort Leavenworth, sharing it in cubicles. He describes it as a way to "make fun of ourselves," pointing to "the ludicrousness of the urgency of so much of what we did." Stick figures ("lousy artist"), two to three hours a day for 16 years, last strip timed to the D-Day anniversary in 2024. Task & Purpose says it combined the late Bill Mauldin's grasp of military minutiae (the article spells the name "Maudlin") with the group-dynamics insight of *The Office*, "politely" ridiculing the institution. Method: whichever general he worked for knew what he was doing; the only pushback came from people who missed the aim, which was not just to make people "laugh a little bit at themselves" but to "share key topics and drive discussions" the profession needed. The one cartoon described: two soldiers at Fort Drum, in summer, up to their necks in snow, one voicing a profane-initialism complaint and "I hate this place." A museum later displayed it. **This is satire of institutional absurdity from an officer's chair, not gallows humor about death or injury;** no joke text beyond that one.
*Candidate application:* Satire from within as a licensed channel: an insider has the institution laugh at its own urgency, tolerated because it feeds an argument the institution wants to have.

### From WA-020 and WA-032 (Comedy Warriors documentary: one film, a review and the film's own site)

**[E21-9]. What the film is, and how much of the "healing" claim is evidence.** (WA-020; WA-032, intro)
*Comedy Warriors: Healing Through Humor* (director John Wager; Emmy special recognition per the review) follows five wounded service members, Army Sgt. Joe Kashnow, Army Capt. Darisse Smith, Marine Sgt. Rob Jones, Army 1st Lt. Stephen Rice and Army Staff Sgt. Bobby Henline, coached by professional comedians (Lewis Black, Bob Saget, B.J. Novak, Zach Galifianakis and others) toward showcases at the Hollywood Improv and the Laugh Factory. WA-020 is a trade-press review around a benefit and Veterans Day 2014; WA-032 is the film's promotional page with biographies. **Neither is research:** the review says "we cannot laugh it off or away" but the jokes "do help the healing process," and the film's page says "emotional pain is lifted." Five self-selected, coached volunteers (one woman); no outcome measure. The film's page says the performers "disarm the stares" and make others comfortable.
*Candidate application:* Treat the program's claims as how the veterans and promoters want the story told; useful for how the humor is framed in public, not for how it works.

**[E21-10]. Documented injury humor: the pause, the opener, the family-shock prop, joking at the door of surgery.** (WA-020; WA-032)
- *The pause and the opener.* Henline (burned over a large share of his body, "almost half" in WA-020, 38 percent in WA-032; sole survivor in his vehicle) lets the audience take a long look, then opens: "You should see the other guy." Mechanism: it names the stare before the audience can and puts the wound in a fight-story form. He also jokes about skin grafts blurring which body part is which (paraphrased).
- *Managing family shock.* Jones (a landmine per WA-020; a roadside bomb with double above-knee amputation per WA-032) wore a pirate hat when his mother visited, which "would help with the initial shock": a prop resetting the room before anyone speaks.
- *Joking at the point of injury.* Kashnow turned to jokes heading into amputation surgery "to deflect from the pain and trauma." His biography uses the same register: he "zigged when he should have zagged" and was sent home for not getting along with flying debris; his deadpan focuses on "his injury, medical treatment, and subsequent amputation," and he wants to show it is "okay to laugh at someone with a disability." The promotional copy adds a "leg up" quip attached to an ethnic-stereotype aside about him (restrained; not reproduced).
- *Self-deprecation as brand.* Henline's persona is a pun on being burned; an occupational therapist made him promise to try stand-up after "laughter-filled" rehab sessions.

*Candidate application:* The documented repertoire for a visible injury: pre-empt the stare, make the wound the punch of a story, use a prop to control the first seconds, joke at the moment of peak fear. The humor concerns the injury's social cost, not its pain.

**[E21-11]. What the mentors and one participant say the humor does.** (WA-020)
Rice, who says his injury kept him in "a dark place" for a year, says veterans get "hung up" when they "define who they are by their injury," and that humor lets "that steam cap off" at certain times, instead of being beaten down and angry all the time. Lewis Black says each performer found a way to talk about their story "in a fashion that was funny." An unattributed line (it follows a list of mentors, so the speaker is unclear) says that if you keep talking about something that hurts, "it stops hurting." Novak's joke ("Haven't they been through enough?") models the permission to joke about the veterans' own situation, conferred by professional comics in a controlled setting.
*Candidate application:* A participant's own two-sided frame: joking to get through it (the steam release) versus being defined by it (the rut).

### From WA-021 (Task & Purpose on the ASAP Comedy Bootcamp show)

**[E21-12]. A free veterans' comedy course: two graduates on why jokes help, and how rough military humor is.** (WA-021)
Ten veterans took the free seven-week course (third class; Washington, D.C. and Hampton Roads; no year given) run by the Armed Services Arts Partnership (ASAP), ending in public shows. Marine veteran Michael Garvey (28; Iraq 2008, Afghanistan 2011, wounded 2011) built a routine on his PTSD, including a joke about searching a health website for the cause of his flashbacks and finding himself diagnosed with something unrelated. His statements: "things normally feel safer in a group, especially a whole group of veterans"; talking is easier with jokes in it because "it loosens up the other person, it loosens me up"; "I can talk about being shot and people will laugh at me instead of just stare at me." Margot Beausey (Navy veteran, former surface warfare officer) says group feedback is a mixed blessing because it comes from friends, that standing watch for long hours helped her stand-up, that military humor is "rough," producing thick skins and boundary-pushing jokes, and that "the military is a place where people talk a lot about race and gender," topics "really taboo" in civilian life. The co-founder says comedy offers confidence, catharsis and a way to "bridge the gap between civilians and veterans." **Journalism and program publicity; no outcome data; two participants as sources.**
*Candidate application:* The joke is the container that lets a veteran explain the trauma and keeps the listener from freezing. A veteran may also remember the service as the place where race and gender were discussed bluntly, against the civilian assumption that it is closed.

### From WA-022 (Sandboxx review of the documentary *Combat Obscura*)

Jenkins reports an 18-month mixed-methods evaluation (focus groups, interviews, surveys before and at three and six months, and a control group of people interested in the programs who had not yet taken them), results not yet published: "very clear evidence" of increased resilience, self-esteem, sense of purpose and social support, reduced depression and stress, "sustained over time." Pressler says rigor matters because they take money from donors and funders. **No numbers, effect sizes or sample size; the claims cover all ASAP arts programs (writing, improv, storytelling, music), not comedy or dark humor specifically.** WA-030 reports figures it attributes to ASAP's research (majority reporting higher self-esteem and confidence; 14 percent reporting less stress): not necessarily a contradiction (different data, no dates), but not the same size of claim. The page summary says ASAP has reached about 1,000 people through over 200 workshops and classes, 150 performances and about 15,000 audience members.
*Candidate application:* Do not cite the claim that veteran comedy programs reduce depression as established; it is an interested organization's claim with a promised report.

**[E21-36]. How the program says it manages the mix of play and tragedy, and who it brings in.** (WA-053)
Pressler names the hardest part as holding "a really hysterical silly improv class" and "a storytelling class where people are telling the most tragic and important story of their lives," and making sure classmates and instructors are ready when someone opens up. Both say it is "not art therapy" but a community, strengths-based arts program; instructors get an annual retreat with veteran-led military cultural competence, trauma foundations from social workers and art therapists, grounding techniques, and simulations with alumni as actors. Jenkins notes fewer than one percent of the country has served. The graduation show is the start of a path (performances at bakeries, breweries and corporate partners; mentoring; teaching), partly to avoid a "separate veteran artist community." Pressler says community arts skew "white, middle, and upper-middle class," and that free classes plus a scholarship fund diversify the arts organizations they partner with. Anecdotes: classmates officiating weddings; one visiting a participant's paralyzed husband in a VA hospital every weekend.
*Candidate application:* A veteran community can be organized around a skill (here joke-writing) that gives people a task and a role, with humor as the shared project. The unit disagrees with itself on treatment: program people say it is not therapy (WA-030, WA-053), a performer calls telling jokes his therapy (WA-031), and the two WWP veterans decline the word "cure" (WA-050).

### From WA-054 (NPR, Duffel Blog interview, 2015)

**[E21-37]. The satire's targets and the founder's statements: policy absurdity, civilian use of combat language, and "you make fun of what you know."** (WA-054)
In a Weekend Edition interview (copyright 2015; Szoldra says the site is a little over three years old), host Scott Simon reads headlines and Szoldra (former Marine, eight years, seven months in Afghanistan) comments. Examples: a headline about nudists allowed to serve openly; one about a militant group receiving a huge wireless bill; a mock announcement that the Pentagon would stop supplying a partner army and supply the enemy directly, which Szoldra explains as a comment on that army's collapse in a recent battle (a real air drop reportedly landed in enemy hands within a week); and a mock study claiming civilians who use combat phrases ("locked and loaded," "hit the beach on D-Day," "take no prisoners") feel a surge of self-importance while hiding emptiness. Szoldra: such phrases "often used in a civilian mindset" make little sense; "Politicians are not under fire. They're just dealing with bad press." **Targets documented:** military policy, allied incompetence, civilians who borrow combat language; not the dead or wounded. Reception: fooling readers is "a daily occurrence"; the biggest case, he says, was a U.S. senator's office writing the Pentagon to ask whether a story a constituent raised was true. On lineage, when Simon invokes Mauldin: "you make fun of what you know"; Vietnam veterans have written that they had underground newspapers "just like this"; the purpose, he says, "is just to highlight the absurdity of things and ultimately entertain veterans." Simon also raises a serious piece Szoldra wrote elsewhere on why his friends died in Iraq: the same man is satirist and mourner, in different venues.
*Candidate application:* Satire as an insider's routine outlet for absurdity, with grief written elsewhere in another register; a character may keep these in separate drawers. Mocking civilians who borrow combat language to feel heroic is a distinctive documented target.

### From WA-055 (listicle on humor in the infantry)

**[E21-38]. Six functions in an infantry veteran's list: relief after close calls, pressure release, pranks, rough horseplay as a bond, morale, and insult as intimacy.** (WA-055)
A short We Are The Mighty list (no byline or date; speaks as "we" for infantry troops, U.S. context assumed). (1) After a close call it is "natural" to crack a dark joke "to mentally settle" oneself; "It may not look healthy at first, but it works." (2) Dark humor is "therapeutic"; immaturity "takes some of the pressure off"; in remote places you have little but the comedy you invent; crude jokes are the staple (an unexplained caption about secretly filming a battalion commander in an undignified private moment; restrained). (3) Pranks pass downtime. (4) "Grab ass" and similar sexualized horseplay among men, which months of living together "earns us the right" to do; the cost of starting it is retaliation ("sleep with one eye open"). (Restrained; the page says nothing about limits, consent or who is excluded, and such horseplay can be hazing.) (5) Morale: days blend, "Fridays feel like Mondays." (6) "We constantly give each other shit because it's our way of bonding": infantrymen "don't typically volunteer to talk about their hopes and dreams that often," and trade stories of home, sexual exploits and beer plans instead; "Joking and shit talking builds us pretty tight bonds." **A listicle from experience; no evidence base.**
*Candidate application:* Insult-as-intimacy substitutes for emotional disclosure in a male infantry culture: affection is shown by the quality of the abuse. This page frames the earned right as months together, not shared trauma.

### From WA-056 (review of a National Guard and Army Reserve memoir)

**[E21-39]. A reviewer on a humorous war memoir: what the humor does for the telling, what it leaves out, what it gets across to outsiders.** (WA-056)
The reviewer (Katharine Petrich, a political-science doctoral candidate; the page does not say whether the reviewer served) reviews *Does My Suicide Vest Make Me Look Fat?* (John Ready, 2013), an Iraq memoir by a Guard and Reserve soldier, a title the review treats as deliberate provocation; the author promises an "irreverent, possibly offensive, definitely provocative" book from the "boots-on-the-ground, everyday soldier's view" of "the ridiculous absurdities" of institutionalized combat. The register: "uproariously funny, full of compound swearwords, rife with black humor, salted with tragedy"; vignettes out of order; closer to veteran social-media humor accounts than a war memoir. One line of the book is quoted (chalk commanders are usually majors, high enough to give orders and low enough for a colonel to use as the accountable one), a jokey job description carrying a real command-pressure lesson. The reviewer says the book will speak to anyone who had to tell their guys they were taking an "operational pause" because somebody forgot to pack enough batteries: a euphemism over a mundane failure. Substance: the best parts are the return home (60 days of adjustment for a reservist, "both generous and insufficient"; a near crash on a freeway from swerving away from a truck, fearing a roadside bomb, which the reviewer recognizes from a date who did the same); reservists return to communities "totally divorced" from the experience and lose informal support. Limit: the author says little about the family cost (oblique divorce, a few dating-while-deployed stories) though open about PTSD and survivor guilt; the reviewer calls it "wisdom-disguised-as-humor." The reviewer also argues that heroics-only accounts make a "one-dimensional image" of combat, and that this book "offers a necessary antidote to the lionization" of service. **One review of one memoir; the book is not in the set.**
*Candidate application:* A veteran-humorist may be funniest and most open on the institution and homecoming and silent on the family, and readers can see where the jokes stop. Euphemism over a banal failure ("operational pause") is the documented form.

### From WA-094 (Task & Purpose interview with a Ranger veteran-entertainer)

**[E21-40]. A veteran entertainer on the "dry" war-book voice versus how a team really jokes, and on laughing through the worst.** (WA-094)
Mat Best (former Army Ranger; four Iraq deployments and one to Afghanistan with 2nd Battalion, 75th Ranger Regiment; later contractor, online entertainer and businessman; interview around his memoir *Thank You For My Service*, year not stated) says many military books are "so dry," written in a clinical report voice (he parodies one: an enemy "presented himself," two rounds were fired, and "My training took over"), whereas "that's not really how we joked and talked in the team room" at 22. He wanted the book to hold the "macabre humor" that goes with war and nightly raids, "the stories that people are terrified to tell for some reason" (the one example, an unglamorous cold-weather episode in Ranger School, is not reproduced). He frames the unit as "just a bunch of guys and gals in a very small unit that are just surviving together," and keeps registers apart: funny war stories are about frustration, while a night of heavy casualties is written "in a very straight tone." Two aims: for readers who have not been to war, "the severity of sending young men and women to combat" (Hollywood "romanticizes" it; "people are dying and losing life, limb, and eyesight"); for veterans, "an outlet," so they can say "I never thought I could laugh about that" and "laugh through it." The title plays on the stock civilian thanks to veterans because he rejects the "mass narrative" that service members are victims to be pitied ("I absolutely loved it"). After leaving he was "a raging alcoholic, depressed," and pulled out with a support system; he calls himself a "stupid idiot infantryman." He also says the book took 17 months of Department of Defense review and that an expletive working title was dropped. **A commercial entertainer's promotion; his claim of an audience "outlet" is his own and nothing is measured.**
*Candidate application:* A character can tell macabre team-room material well while walling off the casualty nights, and can reject civilian pity as the frame. Self-deprecation as the way to tell a redemption story is a documented move, and reads differently beside AC-03's finding that habitual self-defeating humor tracks lower life satisfaction (a different population; do not equate).

### From WA-095 (ClearanceJobs essay on gallows humor between two old veterans)

**[E21-41]. A late-career veteran's essay: definition, a 1942 formulation, and two old men who never talk about what they saw.** (WA-095)
An essay (no byline in the copy) by a retired Army officer of nearly 30 years' service, retired about ten years earlier and a college-years firefighter. It opens with a Terry Pratchett line ("We who think we are about to die will laugh at anything"), defines gallows humor as comedy that "makes light of sensitive topics that are typically considered taboo," cites a 2021 health-site article that dark humor appears when one is having an overwhelming experience of grief, sadness or anxiety, and cites a 1942 sociology article by Obrdlik for a two-sided effect (it strengthens morale among those who share it and works against those it is aimed at), with outsiders reacting with horror at its "insensitive and heartless" look. The author's claim: "It doesn't dull the emotions, it funnels them. We still feel everything, we just cope with it differently," and "the dark humor that emerges from shared trauma forges our bonds, eases our pain, and keeps us sane." The story: the author's close colleagues were friends but "weren't really my tribe"; then an office across from a septuagenarian Navy veteran, Dan, produced a daily routine of trading insults and trash-talking whoever came to the door (a colleague likened them to the two old hecklers on a puppet show). **They "never talked about what we'd seen or experienced. That's not what old veterans do."** When Dan was dying, "we both knew it was coming, but we didn't talk about it. That's not how it works": "a silent acknowledgment" and "a brief moment of mutual affection." The morning after he died, the author made coffee from Dan's blend and, across the empty space between the doors, insulted it aloud: "Somewhere, I know he was laughing." **A personal essay on a career-advice site; one friendship; a claim of generality ("many veterans") with no evidence.**
*Candidate application:* The best documented portrait of humor after long service in this unit: affection delivered as abuse, no disclosure of the past, and grief handled by continuing the insult after the other is gone. Useful for an older veteran, or for the defector's late years.
*Cross-use:* Two aging former soldiers who never discuss the past but keep a daily ritual of insults could suit any long-serving robot veteran pair.

## Pattern roll-up

**Functions (all as stated by veterans or writers, not measured).**
- Relief and settling after a close call; a way "through" (WA-055, WA-050, WA-031, WA-025's "we survived that deployment... by sharing humor").
- A signal to the unit that one is still functional after being hit (WA-031, Tran).
- Bonding by insult in place of disclosure: infantrymen do not talk about hopes and dreams, they abuse each other (WA-055, WA-095); the same pattern in two old veterans who never discuss the past (WA-095).
- Distance that lets the work continue, including work with the dead (WA-006, the authors' interpretation; one documented fire-team example, summarized).
- Replacement topics in a unit under extreme risk: sex, food, home and regional teasing, with battle itself avoided to protect morale (WA-007, one North Vietnamese account).
- Institutional truth-telling: satire from inside that shows the institution its own absurdity, tolerated or absorbed (WA-019, WA-034, WA-035, WA-036, WA-054).
- After service: a substitute battle buddy and an antidote to isolation (WA-029, WA-053), a place where "both parts of me could exist" (WA-050), continuity of an NCO's duty to care (WA-031), a role-switch off the PTSD "uniform" (WA-027).

**Targets.**
- Self and one's own injury (WA-020, WA-032, WA-021); the self as incompetent trainee (WA-031).
- Superiors by mimicry or inversion: the team-leader costume (WA-025), the drill sergeant's open wish (WA-031), the officer called out in the audience (WA-052), the sentimental colonel (WA-006).
- Other services (WA-030, WA-034), junior officers and staff urgency (WA-019, WA-034), bureaucracy and the VA (WA-030).
- The Corps' own mythology (WA-035); the government's policy and allies (WA-054).
- Civilians who borrow combat language to feel heroic (WA-054).
- Regional and sexual teasing of absent women (WA-007) and a joke that casts women and a schoolteacher as the squeamish audience (WA-006).
- Not documented as targets in the public performance pages: the dead of one's own side and the wounded of one's own unit; the dead appear as the subject of joking only in the Vietnam-era items and, as an act of honor, in one comic's stated reason for suicide jokes (restrained).

**Forms and mechanics.**
- Deadpan literalism that answers an officer's rhetorical grief (WA-006).
- Understatement and euphemism over harm (a poem titled "A Bummer"; "operational pause" for forgotten batteries).
- The pause that lets an audience size up the wound, then a stock fight-story opener (WA-020); the pause that lets an audience breathe before a hard joke is turned (WA-030).
- Prop-first handling of family shock (WA-020), puns on the injury itself (WA-020, WA-032).
- The deniable order and the literal-minded subordinate, with an officer-production tag (WA-031).
- Rank inversion and impersonation (WA-025, WA-052).
- Institutional deadpan in fake news; vulgar content placed in a bland setting; satire grounded in reality (WA-034, WA-054).
- Vehicle and equipment nicknames as in-group naming (WA-006, WA-056, restrained).
- Clinical report voice as the foil for real team-room talk (WA-094).
- The stage persona as a garment (WA-027).

**Rules of license.**
- The explicit one: "we've kind of earned the right" because "we're the ones that went"; the voice of those who did it, not politicians; good faith (WA-030). Civilian listeners may still be amused, and a few civilian performers are tolerated (WA-033).
- Time together in a unit as the license to roughhouse (WA-055).
- Comedians can say what troops cannot say to their chain (WA-031, the writers' report).
- Audience reading and shared vocabulary: jokes need the shared experience (WA-052); the presence of one's boss changes what is laughable (WA-052).
- Only veterans laughed (WA-006); some jokes landed only with the veterans in the room (WA-030).
- The professional mentors' permission to joke at the wounded (WA-020, Novak).
- Internal limits: a veteran reviewer disciplines one "joke" (a weapon pointed at children) even while defending the unit (WA-022).

**Limits and failure modes.**
- A joke "might have flopped" with anyone else (WA-030); drug jokes flop in front of one's chain of command (WA-052).
- Strangers asked in writing for the worst jokes sent the safest ones (WA-006).
- Delayed collapse: the jokes in the firefight, the breakdown at home months later (WA-031).
- Silence as the other half: old veterans who never talk about what they saw (WA-095); the author who is open about PTSD but not family (WA-056); the "projected control" and hypervigilance behind the joking veteran (WA-050).
- A habit that outruns the occasion: jokes written into a eulogy (WA-051).
- Comedy as treatment disputed inside the same set: "not therapy, but therapeutic" (WA-030, WA-053) against "telling jokes is my therapy" (WA-031).
- Horseplay with no stated limits (WA-055) and commercial incentives of entertainers (WA-094).
- An unverified claim that the jokes "lose their healing cautery" over time (WA-006).

**Time course.**
- In combat: relief, signal, prank-passing of downtime (WA-055, WA-031, WA-025).
- Soon after: delayed recognition and breakdown (WA-031); isolation, then a first laugh as a hook (WA-051, WA-050).
- Early civilian life: avoidance of military material, then a return to it when it reaches civilians (WA-051, Potter).
- Long after: the humor persists as ritual abuse and replaces disclosure (WA-095); forty years on, veterans still laugh at the oldest joke (WA-006).

**Civilian-facing register.**
- Jargon is the first barrier; stories need translation (WA-052).
- The funny version explains the sad one: a joke "loosens up the other person" (WA-021); the program-level claim that humor bridges the gap (WA-021, WA-051, WA-053).
- Sanitized versions for strangers and official publications (WA-006, WA-031); raw material finds a home with troops but not with a civilian club (WA-028).
- A speaker may want civilians uncomfortable on purpose (WA-051, Pedersen).
- The stated duty to translate falls on the veteran (WA-035).

**Defector, exile and dissent specifics.** Nothing in this set addresses defectors or exiles. The nearest material: antiwar veterans (the VVAW authors of WA-006 and WA-007; the Veterans for Peace director in WA-025), insider satirists who criticize the institution and are tolerated (WA-019, WA-035), a comic discharged under "Don't Ask, Don't Tell" (WA-051), a veteran who refuses the victim story (WA-094), and community-without-a-unit built from performance programs (WA-029, WA-053).

**Cross-cultural.** Only WA-007 (a North Vietnamese unit; regional-origin teasing; battle-talk avoided) is non-American; WA-031 includes a Vietnamese-refugee-family soldier in a U.S. troupe.

**Craft observations.**
- Honesty over propaganda as the warrant for satire; the sharpest joke is "based in reality" (WA-035, WA-034).
- The dry report voice is not how the team talks, and the stories "people are terrified to tell" are the macabre ones (WA-094).
- Know the audience; a joke needs shared vocabulary (WA-052).
- Teach setup, punchline and tagline as a structure (WA-053); test a premise at an open mic (WA-051).
- Insiders do it better than civilians writing about the military (WA-034, WA-054).

**Claims made for comedy programs, and their evidence.** All claims are by organizers, promoters or participants. ASAP's own 18-month evaluation (WA-053) is unpublished in this set and covers all its arts programs; a figure attributed to it in WA-030 is modest (14 percent reported less stress). WWP's list of physiological effects and its report of an ASAP-led journal study (WA-050) carry no citation. Comedy Vets, Rapid Fire, Comedy Warriors, GIs of Comedy and Operation Comedy offer only testimony.

**Who is not in the room.** Women appear as: one of five in a documentary (WA-020, WA-032), a Navy veteran who says the military discusses race and gender more bluntly than civilian life (WA-021), a Marine who tells a joke about a violation of her own privacy to a partly veteran audience (WA-051), and absent targets or squeamish foils in two Vietnam-era items (WA-006, WA-007). Minority voices are individual (WA-050, WA-051, WA-030, WA-031, WA-028 on diverse comics). Most veterans do not perform stand-up, and every program page here describes performers; the non-performing majority, the silent, and those too injured or too unwell to join are not heard. Enlisted voices dominate; officers speak mainly in the satire pieces (WA-019) and in a retired colonel's essay (WA-095).

## Gaps and cautions

1. **Independence of the sources.** Six pages (WA-021, WA-027, WA-030, WA-050, WA-051, WA-053) concern **one organization, ASAP**, and one instructor (Dewayne White) speaks in three of them (WA-030, WA-050, WA-051). WA-020 and WA-032 are **one film**. WA-034 and WA-054 are **one satirist** at two dates. WA-035, WA-036 and WA-037 are **one cartoonist**. Counting pages therefore overstates how much independent evidence exists.
2. **Evidence status.** Nothing is a study. Verify before use: ASAP's promised public report (WA-053), the "14 percent" source (WA-030), and the humor-journal paper WWP refers to (WA-050).
3. **Small contradictions to check in a browser:** ASAP course length (six weeks in WA-050, seven in WA-021, eight in WA-027 and WA-053); the share of one performer's body burned (WA-020 vs WA-032); how another performer was injured (WA-020 vs WA-032); the date of the WHRO documentary (WA-027); when ASAP began (the host in WA-053 says 2015, the same page describes workshops in 2013); a quoted performer described as enlisting at 17 (WA-050) and as an Army major (WA-051), which may be consistent; the unit-leader's reported "never really forgave" yet "good sport" (WA-025).
4. **Unclear or damaged text:** WA-020's quote "when something hurts to talk about..." is not attributed to a speaker; WA-022's "puns about a level"; WA-036's "three squares"; WA-019's misspelling of Mauldin and a dropped word in the final quote; WA-031's character-encoding damage; WA-006 has typographic slips (missing apostrophes and one garbled sentence).
5. **Missing halves:** WA-007 is Part One only (Part Two, "Spring/Summer 2000," is not in the set). The podcasts (WA-028, WA-036, WA-037) and the films (WA-020, WA-022, WA-027, WA-032) were not heard or seen.
6. **Era and army skew.** U.S. and mostly 2000s-2020s; the only first-person field jokes are Vietnam (WA-006) and a few Iraq-era anecdotes (WA-025, WA-031). No source here gives a first-hand description of joking about the dead or suicide in the field; the suicide jokes in WA-030 are described by topic, not text.
7. **Authors' agendas.** WA-006 is an antiwar essay (the humor is evidence against war); WA-094 is a commercial promotion; WA-050 and WA-053 are an organization speaking about itself.
8. **Candidate applications** are my suggestions, never settled, and none is a line of dialogue.
