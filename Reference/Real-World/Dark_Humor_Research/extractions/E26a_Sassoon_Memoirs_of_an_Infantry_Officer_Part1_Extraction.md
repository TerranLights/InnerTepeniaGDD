## Source-shape notes (read first)

**What PW-09 is.** Sassoon's 1930 prose book, the second volume of the fictional "Sherston" memoirs (the first, *Memoirs of a Fox-Hunting Man*, is not in the collection), here in a Standard Ebooks plain-text edition of 1,147 long paragraph-lines, ten chapters. **It is an autobiographical novel**: the narrator is the fictional "George Sherston" of the series (in my range the text itself gives only "Mr. Sherston," "Kangaroo," "Kangar" and, once, "Master George"; the full name comes from the brief and the series, not from this stretch of text; the name Sassoon never appears in the narration), and the other officers, the colonel, the aunt and the servant are fictionalized figures. Every finding below is therefore **the narrator's account, not documentary testimony**; where the text itself blurs fact and fiction I say so (E26a-6, the narrator's own frame; E26a-41, the narrator on his failing recall; E26a-54, an outside-the-text identification of a character). The regiment is named in the text as the fictional "Flintshire Fusiliers."

**What my range covers (L1-570).** Chapter I, "At the Army School" (L15-70, April-May 1916, a month's course at Flixécourt); II, "The Raid" (L71-148, a failed trench raid at Fricourt/Mametz, with the communiqué); III, "Before the Push" (L149-202, dugout life, leave in England, return to the battalion); IV, "Battle" (L203-399: the eve of the Somme, 1 July seen from a support trench, the Mametz Wood assault and Quadrangle Trench, rest at Heilly, a lunch in Amiens, the reserve camp at Bazentin); V, "Escape" (L400-485: Cromlech's death reported and denied, enteritis, hospital at Amiens and Rouen, Oxford, leave at Butley, Dottrell's letters, the hunting field, a medical board); VI, "At the Depot" (L486-553: Clitherland Camp, December 1916-January 1917, Cromlech's banter, Durley's account of Delville Wood, the departure); VII, "Rouen in February" (L554-570, to the middle of a character sketch). **Another pass (E26b) covers L571-1,147.** Period covered: April 1916 to February 1917.

**Evidence base and method.** One narrator, one army (the British), one battalion, one rank for most of the range (a second lieutenant and platoon commander). The narrator is writing in 1930, about fourteen years later, and says that memory is partial ("Time seems to have obliterated the laughter of the war," E26a-41; a trench he "only this moment remembered"; at the start, "memory tries to misinform me"). The prose is interleaved with quoted **diary entries** (E26a-13, E26a-36, E26a-57) and quoted **letters** from the Quartermaster (E26a-73, E26a-74) that are presented as contemporary; I cannot verify them as such. Durley's long account of Delville Wood (E26a-83) is a speech the narrator reconstructs ("His narrative was something like this").

**What it does NOT contain (in my range).** No theory of humor beyond a handful of narrator remarks (E26a-22, E26a-41, E26a-82, E26a-87). **No extended other-ranks voice:** the men speak in a sentence or two, mediated by the officer (Flook, Kendle, Worgan, a sentry, a lance-corporal, a sergeant); the one long-service voice with a letter-writer's pen is the battalion Quartermaster, who speaks and writes as a staff-of-the-battalion veteran and mentions his pension (E26a-74), not as a private soldier. No enemy voices. No jokes the narrator claims to share with the other ranks. No dissent or defector content: the narrator's doubts are early and mostly suppressed in this half (E26a-76, E26a-87). The later protest, if the book has one, is in the other half and is not in my range. No page markers; I cite chapter, section and text-file line numbers ("L").

**Quotation conventions.** The source is public domain in the U.S. (published 1930), so some longer quotations are given where an example is the point (E26a-19, E26a-41, E26a-46, E26a-66). Quotations are exact except for American spelling (the project rule applies: "honor," "humor," "color," "defense," "favor," "gray," "traveler"); the text's own dashes standing in for oaths are kept; "my gloss" marks explanations not in the text. **Period national, ethnic and class caricature is documented, not endorsed** (Irish, Welsh, Australian, German, French, Staff).

## Findings

### A. Chapter I, "At the Army School" (L15-70): the rear-area month, the lecture room and the first mess talk

**[E26a-1]. The narrator's opening pose: a fatalist who treats the war as a personal quarrel and a month away from it as an interruption.** (PW-09, ch. I §I, L21)
The text file opens in medias res: the narrator refers back ("I have said," "I also stated") to a Mametz winter that is not in this file. He reports that he "had more or less made up my mind to die because in the circumstances there didn't seem anything else to be done," and then handles the orders that send him to a month's refresher course in the register of a man whose appointment has been interrupted. His "personal grievance against the Germans" is "interrupted for at least four weeks," and he speculates that the colonel (Kinjack) "had heard that I'd been looking for trouble." A bus then carries him "away from all possibility of dying a murky death in the mine-craters."
*Mechanism and target:* a mock-businesslike frame (the grievance, the interruption, the refresher course) over a plainly stated expectation of death; the narrator himself is the target, with the colonel's supposed wit as a light second layer. This is the narrator (the fictional "Sherston" persona) speaking; the text does not say whether the death-wish is the persona's or the author's own, and how the persona's attitude develops is outside what this pass can show at this point.
*Candidate application:* A character whose fatalism is voiced as a bureaucratic grievance, with an official order treated as a kind of rescue, shows how a resigned man can joke without claiming to be cheerful.

**[E26a-2]. Barton and the servant Flook: nicknames, mock-parental banter and an affectionate joke about a batman.** (PW-09, ch. I §I, L23, L35; ch. II §I, L79; ch. III §I, L167)
The narrator's company commander Barton sees him off to the Army School with a nickname joke ("Lucky Kangaroo," "hopping away for a holiday") and a mock instruction to the servant Flook to "keep Mr. Sherston well polished up and punctual on parade." Other officers use "Kangaroo" and "Kangar" too (the text does not explain the nickname in my range). Flook grins, then on the first morning remarks to his officer that "we'd come to the right place, for once," and wishes they could stay "for the duration" (reported in the narrator's paraphrase, not verbatim). Back in the billets the narrator says Flook's vigilance for his comfort was such that he "could more easily imagine him using his rifle in defense of my valise than against the Germans." In the front line Flook is overheard asking a bomber if he has seen his "officer-bloke."
*Mechanism:* officer-to-servant banter is warm and rank-aware: the officer jokes through the servant, the servant answers with a short dry remark, and the narrator turns the servant's devotion into a joke about priorities. Flook is the only other-ranks speaker in these chapters who gets repeated lines, and each is a sentence or two mediated by the officer.
*Candidate application:* The officer-servant pair as a small, warm, unequal comic unit (nickname, protective mock-orders, dry reply) is one documented form of cohesion across rank.

**[E26a-3]. The Army School as institutional satire: "Open Warfare," a commandant's motto, and the school as a holiday.** (PW-09, ch. I §I, L31-37, L41; §II, L69)
The narrator's account of Flixécourt, a quiet town thirty miles behind the Fricourt trenches, is a gentle comic indictment of instruction that does not match the front. Instructors, he says, "were all in favor of Open Warfare," learned in peacetime, so that students were to "think in terms of mobility" and solve tactical problems "in a land of field-day make-believe," while he would have welcomed "a few practical hints" on patrolling the mine-craters he actually knows. A big-game hunter demonstrates sniping, a luxury "seldom enjoyed by an infantry battalion in the trenches." The commandant's motto is "always do your utmost," but the narrator suspects he privately knew the school was "only a holiday for officers and N.C.O.s who needed a rest." On the last day a hundred English and French generals arrive: "it was not unlike an army of uniformed Uncles on Prize-giving Day. There were no prizes, naturally." Young Allgood remarks that the French generals look "brainier" than the British ones; the narrator answers "that they must be cleverer than they looked, and anyhow they'd all got plenty of medal-ribbons." Elsewhere the narrator notes that regimental badges "seemed somehow to affect the personality of the wearer," and that a Connaught Ranger and a Seaforth Highlander beside him were "likely to have been born in Middlesex."
*Mechanism and target:* understated contrast between instruction and experience, the school-day analogy (form-master, prize-giving) applied to generals, and a throwaway reply that converts decoration into the only available evidence of intellect. The target is the staff and training establishment, not the army's purpose; the narrator is amused, not bitter.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who jokes about the training establishment as a sheltered, ceremonial world, and who measures senior officers by their ribbons, is working in a documented register of rank-satire from below.

**[E26a-4]. Lecture-room parentheticals: gas "in its infancy" and "explosives often arrived from the wrong direction."** (PW-09, ch. I §I, L43)
The narrator describes the evening lectures. A gas expert from headquarters tells the 300 officers and N.C.O.s that "gas was still in its infancy." The narrator then adds in parentheses: "(Most of us were either dead or disabled before gas had had time to grow up.)" An urbane artillery general promises high explosive will be "our best friend," and his voice made them "unmindful, for the moment, that explosives often arrived from the wrong direction."
*Mechanism:* a retrospective aside, outside the scene in time (the narrator is writing in 1930 about spring 1916), that extends the lecturer's metaphor to its fatal conclusion. The death count is carried in the parenthesis, flatly, with no change of tone. The artillery sentence applies the same trick (friendly explosives arriving from the enemy's side) to the other arm.
*Candidate application:* The retrospective parenthesis that finishes an official metaphor with the casualty figure is a craft device a veteran narrator can use without addressing the dead directly.

**[E26a-5]. The bayonet lecture: mock-hearty jokes over a killing doctrine, and the narrator's verdict.** (PW-09, ch. I §I, L43-49; §II, L53) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
A massive Highland major lectures on "The Spirit of the Bayonet," quoting the training manual's emphasis on aggressive determination in bayonet assault. The narrator says he "spoke with homicidal eloquence, keeping the game alive with genial and well-judged jokes," and that though "at that time undecorated, he was afterwards awarded the D.S.O. for lecturing." A sergeant demonstrates with rifle and bayonet, "including facial expression," on the order to put on "the killing face." The narrator comments: "To hear the Major talk, one might have thought that he did it himself every day before breakfast." The lecture's slogans (summarized, not reproduced) urge fear and killing as a point-scoring contest for "our side," and give blunt, graphic advice about how little steel is needed; the narrator reports that the lecturer's voice "still battered on my brain" as he walks up to a beech wood at dusk. The next morning he practices bayonet drill on straw sacks as healthy exercise and says it would have been "futile and inexpedient to moralize" about it.
*Mechanism and target:* the narrator's irony works by reporting the lecturer's own genial-joking manner as the grotesque element (humor in the service of killing doctrine) and by one dry sentence puncturing the lecturer's personal claim to experience. The narrator's own response is private disquiet followed by compliance. **This is the lecturer's humor and the narrator's criticism of it, not a joke the narrator shares.**
*Candidate application:* Humor used by an instructor to normalize killing, and a listener's private revulsion followed by outward compliance, is a documented contrast with the gallows humor of the line (compare the front-line scenes below).

**[E26a-6]. The narrator's frame: "it is my own story," plus the comic holiday in France.** (PW-09, ch. I §II, L55-57)
Mid-chapter the narrator pauses to say that remembering a bath in a dyeing vat "may not be of much interest to anyone, but it was a good bath, and it is my own story that I am trying to tell"; readers expecting "a universalization of the Great War" should look elsewhere, for "here they will only find an attempt to show its effect on a somewhat solitary-minded young man." Earlier he says that on bright mornings, walking to the training ground with a rifle on his shoulder like a boy going to early school, he could "almost congratulate myself on having a holiday in France without paying for it."
*Mechanism and genre note:* this is the autobiographical-novel frame spoken aloud: the book disclaims documentary universality. It is also the narrator's own humor register for rear-area comfort, an understated self-mockery. It should not be quoted as Sassoon's own declared method (the text calls the narrator by the fictional name Sherston; the author's name does not appear in the narration).
*Candidate application:* A narrator who announces the limits of his account (one man's, solitary, not the war's) is a cue for how a character's stories should be framed as personal, not representative.

**[E26a-7]. Mock-heroic about the rear: the horses on parade, the Commander-in-Chief as an unimaginable body, and the Quartermaster's letter.** (PW-09, ch. I §II, L59, L63)
On the way to a bombing demonstration the narrator passes hundreds of draft horses lined up "shining in their summer coats" for a Commander-in-Chief inspection, calls them "the horsehood of England" and says the C.-in-C. was "a bodily presence which the infantry mind could not easily imagine." He notes mules were "mostly with the Artillery" and that he "was only a second-lieutenant" and could not spend the afternoon inspecting the horses. The Quartermaster Dottrell (the text also calls the Quartermaster "Joe"; it treats them as one man) writes to him: "the further you got from the front-line the further you got from the War," and reports he is eating the narrator's aunt's kippers and Devonshire cream, some "hadn't stood the journey well." The letter, the narrator says, put him "in the right frame of mind for returning to tours of trenches."
*Mechanism:* mock-heroic language for logistics and deadpan treatment of the highest rank as outside infantry experience. The Quartermaster's aphorism is an inverted proximity law about distance and truth, delivered as a throwaway observation.
*Candidate application:* The old logistics hand who frames distance from the front as distance from the war itself is a recurring figure of dry institutional realism.

**[E26a-8]. Allgood: a gentle named man, and the register that follows his death.** (PW-09, ch. I §II, L65)
Young Allgood, quiet, bird-watching, hoping to become a historian, is the narrator's companion at the school. The narrator reports that Allgood "said he'd always wanted to go to Germany, and there seemed nothing incongruous in the remark; for the moment I forgot that every German we killed was a point scored to our side" (the narrator is echoing the lecture's scoring slogan). Allgood "never grumbled about the war" but looked as if "inwardly warned that he would never see his home in Wiltshire again." The narrator ends: "A couple of months afterwards I saw his name in one of the long lists of killed, and it seemed to me that I had expected it."
*Mechanism and register:* humor is absent here; the only irony is the echo of the lecture slogan, which turns it against its own scoring logic. The death is reported as a retrospective fact in the book's flat register: a name seen in a list.
*Candidate application:* A gentle man's death recorded through a list, with the earlier jokes of the same chapter left behind, shows how an officer-narrator marks one loss without comment.

### B. Chapter II, "The Raid" (L71-148): mess talk, the brigade's raid, and the communiqué

**[E26a-9]. The mess table: newspaper tablecloth, whisky against lime-juice, and Mansfield's tirade about the planned raid.** (PW-09, ch. II §I, L83)
Returning from the school, the narrator finds the company mess glum; a new officer reads the newspaper that serves as tablecloth, one officer (Mansfield, nicknamed Birdie) lowers the whisky bottle while "the rest of us drank lime-juice." Told he is "the only human being in this Brigade who hasn't heard" about the planned raid, the narrator asks if it was a success. Mansfield answers with sarcasm: "Holy Christ! Was it a success? The Kangaroo wants to know if it was a success!" He then calls the planning "a funny story for the last fortnight" and complains "we've done everything except send word over to the Fritzes to say what time we're coming," adding that Barton hopes to win a D.S.O. out of it "for his executive ability." The narrator notes that Mansfield's remarks "were emphasized by the usual epithets." A fellow officer (Ormand) leaves the table announcing himself in the words of a music-hall song: "Gilbert the filbert, the Nut with a K, the pride of Piccadilly, the blasé roué."
*Mechanism and target:* mess-table sarcasm, with the target in order: the planning staff who have let the raid become common knowledge; the company commander seeking decoration through other people's risk; and the narrator, a newcomer to the news. Ormand's exit is comic escape by song, using a stage persona to leave a discussion he has "heard more than enough" of.
*Candidate application:* The mess scene shows swearing, sarcasm aimed at superiors' careerism, and music-hall quotation as the officers' way of absorbing dread about a pending raid; none of them states fear.

**[E26a-10]. The narrator's ambition and his night fear: where the comic register falls away.** (PW-09, ch. II §I, L87-93)
Lying in his sleeping bag, the narrator compares the raid to a horse he owns and sees someone else ride; he says he wants the war to serve the purpose that race-winning once did, and that a Military Cross would make him "comparatively safe and confident." Mansfield's gloomy remark (summarized in the register) was that if he got through Thursday night intact he would celebrate; the narrator notes Mansfield was "podgily built" and no crawler, yet more sensible than himself. Then, "warm and secure," listening to aspens, "the fear of death and the horror of mutilation took hold of my heart," followed by a short practical self-reassurance (revolver "wasn't so bad," bombs "you just chucked them and hoped for the best") and a drift into sleep.
*Mechanism:* self-comic naivete (the war as a racecourse, the medal as safety) is followed by a plainly stated fear in a single sentence; the narrator then reaches for the casual idiom of the mess ("hoped for the best") as comfort. The register turns plain when he is alone in the dark.
*Candidate application:* A character who maps combat onto earlier social ambitions (races, medals, standing among peers) and drops the mapping only when alone is a documented self-protective frame.

**[E26a-11]. The Quartermaster Joe on the Staff, the raid and "a few of our men."** (PW-09, ch. II §II, L97-101)
The narrator's friend the Quartermaster (Joe) is a pessimist who airs "customary criticisms of profiteers, politicians, and those whose military duties compelled them to remain at the Base." He says the permanent staff at Fourth Army Headquarters "numbered anything up to four thousand," and with "a ribald metaphor" (not reproduced) speculates on what they do all day; the narrator replies that some are busy at the Army School, and Joe supposes there is no prospect of their opening "a rest-cure for Quartermasters." On the raid he says it is "a baddish place for a show of that kind," that the Germans "would have come across themselves before now if they'd thought it worth trying" (my paraphrase of the sense: they would have raided first), and that "Brigade have got the idea of a raid hot and strong, and they've nothing to lose by it one way or the other, except a few of our men." Raids, he explains, had been revived after "some Canadian toughs" had pulled one off, "and since then such entertainments had become popular with the Staff." Of the volunteers: "It's always the good lads who volunteer for these shows"; he is quietly shipping a married transport man off on a cold-shoeing course to keep him from volunteering. The colonel (Kinjack) is reported to have "talked pretty straight to the Brigade Major" and never feared "giving the brass-hats a bit of his mind."
*Mechanism and target:* dry complaint about staff numbers and a deadpan summary of the brigade's cost accounting ("nothing to lose ... except a few of our men"). Joe's act of diverting a volunteer is the one practical kindness in the exchange, and is told without sentiment. The target is Brigade and Staff, not the colonel.
*Candidate application:* The quartermaster as the unit's sardonic ledger-keeper, who both jokes about the staff and quietly protects men from heroism, is a recognizable composite worth giving to a logistics character.

**[E26a-12]. Trench and road names, the personified road, and a train joke about a never-ending war.** (PW-09, ch. II §II, L105-109; ch. III §I, L167)
The narrator says the reserve position called "71. North" had been "christened it coldly" by some map-maker and "unexpectedly failed to get itself called the Elephant and Castle or Hampton Court" (the narrator evidently expected a London-style nickname of that kind to stick); it is nonetheless "a safe and busy suburb of the front-line." A communication trench, by contrast, is the "Old Kent Road." The road to Fricourt is personified: it "skulked along to the British Front Line, wandered evilly across No Man's Land, and then gave itself up to the Germans." Barton notes a single-line railway nearby and remarks that if the war lasted a few more years they would be coming to the trenches daily by train "like city men going to the office" (the narrator's paraphrase of Barton's remark).
*Mechanism:* the home-district naming habit (London streets for trenches) and the tone of suburban commuting turn the front into a familiar routine; the personified road gives the line a moral geography (it surrenders). The narrator does not claim the soldiers named the trenches themselves in my range.
*Candidate application:* Trench toponymy that borrows civilian suburbs, and the joke of the commuter war, are documented forms of domesticating the line.

**[E26a-13]. Diary entries: lyrical landscape and a one-line natural-history joke.** (PW-09, ch. II §II, L111-113)
The narrator quotes two diary entries (Tuesday evening, Wednesday evening, in his own pencil style) that are mostly observation: a ruined church, a pale sky, a Northumberland Fusilier officer who "drops aitches" (a class-and-region note), lark song, a thistle on the parapet. The Wednesday entry ends with a sudden dry line: "a large black slug out for his evening walk (doing nearly a mile a month)."
*Mechanism:* a one-clause, parenthetical joke on slowness inside a diary that otherwise describes the landscape without comment; the joke is addressed to the diarist himself.
*Candidate application:* A character who keeps a private record in which the only humor is a small deadpan aside about another living creature is a documented, quiet form of coping.

**[E26a-14]. The "happy warrior" farewell letter and the companion who blows out the candle.** (PW-09, ch. II §III, L117-119)
On the night of the raid the narrator writes a farewell letter to his aunt in what he calls "the 'happy warrior' style," and says "my own fine feelings took precedence of hers." He explains he could not imagine what the aunt was doing while he wrote, "to have done so would have cramped my style," then imagines it anyway (she drying her black Persian cat after it comes in from the garden). He adds that the aunt was "still comfortingly convinced that I was transport officer," though he had given up that job nearly three months earlier. His companion Durley "received it with appropriate gravity," wished him luck, "economically blew out the candle," and remarked "it was lucky the night was dark and rainy."
*Mechanism:* retrospective self-mockery (the heroic letter, the writer's feelings), civilian-facing evasion kept in the aunt's comforting error, and Durley's plain practical understatement ("lucky the night was dark and rainy") as the only blessing the situation allows.
*Candidate application:* The farewell-letter pose, mocked a decade later, and a companion's practical remark in place of a blessing, are two compact examples of officers' register before a raid.

**[E26a-15]. Raiders with blackened faces and the stock joke "Who's for a Blighty one tonight?"** (PW-09, ch. II §III, L121) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
In the large dugout twenty-five raiders blacken their faces with burnt cork "to avoid the danger of their mistaking one another for Germans," "exchanging boisterous jokes" and "pretending not to recognize one another." The narrator compares them to a minstrel troupe getting ready for a concert (the period's racially caricaturing wording is not reproduced), then lists what they actually carry: knobkerries (clubs), Mills bombs and hatchets. "'Who's for a Blighty one tonight?' was the stock joke (if such a well worn wish could be called a joke)." A "Blighty one" is a wound severe enough to send a man home.
*Mechanism:* a concert-party comparison for men preparing to kill, and a stock phrase that is a wish for a wound, which the narrator himself says is barely a joke. The humor is the raiders' own, reported from the outside.
*Candidate application:* The pre-raid stock phrase that wishes for a nonfatal wound is a documented ritual shape: a joke that functions as a shared hope, and which an observer recognizes as not really a joke.

**[E26a-16]. The operation orders, the colonel's veto and a sentry's compliment.** (PW-09, ch. II §III, L123-129)
The narrator, a second lieutenant who wants to go on the raid, is stopped by Colonel Kinjack: "your job is to stop in our trench and count the men as they come back." The narrator reports that the operation orders "had optimistically assumed that the hostile trenches would be entered without difficulty"; he adds that the object included capturing prisoners, "bomb dugouts, and kill Germans," and that a party of seven carried ladders because the German trench was believed deep. While he waits, one of two sentries remarks (with a censored oath, per the text) that the narrator would never be kept inside the trench, and the narrator says that "even at that tense moment I valued the compliment."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's dry commentary on the orders ("optimistically assumed") is a short rank-ironic note on planning; the sentry's remark is a backhanded other-ranks compliment about an officer who cannot stay out of danger.
*Candidate application:* Other ranks speaking of an officer in the third person, within his hearing, in a coarse way that is also praise, is a documented small mark of acceptance.

**[E26a-17]. The raid's casualties: the lance-corporal's gratitude, the search in the crater and the plain register.** (PW-09, ch. II §III, L129-139) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
When the raid fails against a second belt of wire, there is an exchange of bombs and the men scramble back, black faces showing "grotesque" in the flares. The narrator finds Mansfield badly hit and helps a gray-haired lance-corporal whose foot has been seriously wounded; seated on the fire-step, the man says: "Thank God Almighty for this; I've been waiting eighteen months for it and now I can go home." Then he mentions that the corporal Mick O'Brien is still in the craters. The narrator goes out alone, thinking "This is no fun at all," finds O'Brien gravely wounded and, with a rope and a helper, brings him in; "the Germans ... had stopped firing; perhaps they felt sorry for us." A stretcher-bearer examines O'Brien and takes off his helmet "with a gesture that vaguely surprised me by its reverent simplicity." The narrator says O'Brien "had been one of the best men in our Company" and notes how his face looked under last night's burnt cork (details not reproduced). The final tally: "Two killed and ten wounded was the only result of the raid."
*Mechanism and register:* there is no joking in this passage after the lance-corporal's line, which is itself the book's clearest example of a wound welcomed as an exit (the speaker's relief is sincere and is the only humor-adjacent moment). The narrator's own understatement ("no fun at all") and the stretcher-bearer's silent gesture carry the grief.
*Candidate application:* The welcomed wound as a tired man's release, spoken without irony, and the silent helmet-removal that replaces a joke, are two documented ways the register goes plain.

**[E26a-18]. After the raid: a colonel's brief equality, exultation, and the deflation of heroism.** (PW-09, ch. II §III, L141-143)
Reporting to Kinjack, the narrator finds him on his plank bed in a brown woolen cap with a tuft, haggard: "it was the first time I had ever shared any human equality with him." Kinjack "spoke kindly to me in his rough way." Walking back at dawn the narrator feels "a wild exultation" and the sense that the landscape "was life," then deflates himself: it was "only the sort of thing which people often did during a fire or a railway accident." Meanwhile Barton reports the doctor's view that Mansfield was "touch and go," that Mansfield was already "rejoicing at the prospect of getting across to Blighty," and that he was "cursing the bad wire-cutters which had been served out for the raid."
*Mechanism:* the rank gap closes in a shared exhaustion, and the narrator's comic self-deflation arrives in a single sentence after a paragraph of exultation. The wounded officer's complaint about the wire-cutters is the mess sarcasm of the earlier scene carried into the sick-bay.
*Candidate application:* A moment when rank becomes irrelevant after shared loss, followed by a tired officer cutting his own pride down, is a documented beat after a failed operation.

**[E26a-19]. The G.H.Q. communiqué: the official version beside the narrated event.** (PW-09, ch. II §III, L147)
The raid is reported in the communiqué, which the narrator quotes in full: "At Mametz we raided hostile trenches. Our party entered without difficulty and maintained a spirited bombing fight, and finally withdrew at the end of twenty-five minutes." Then: "This was their way of telling England." He adds that his aunt probably read it "automatically" in her newspaper, unaware that the event "had almost caused her to receive a farewell letter from me," and observes, "Curiously enough, I hadn't yet seen a German."
*Mechanism:* the official summary is allowed to stand against the narrated scene (raiders who never got through the wire, two dead, ten wounded). The single editorial sentence is a laconic reference to what is left out. The final remark, deadpan, restates how little contact the raid actually involved.
*Candidate application:* Quoting the official one-line version of an event the reader has just lived through is the most economical form of institutional irony and works without any joke.

### C. Chapter III, "Before the Push" (L149-203): the dugout, leave, the civilian world and the Stores

**[E26a-20]. Barton's dislike of the Irish, the mouse in the sugar-basin, and the Café Royal contrast.** (PW-09, ch. III §I, L155-157) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
In the dugout, Barton, back from leave, grumbles while filling in forms that "made Brigade think they were busy." When the narrator reads aloud the news of Lord Kitchener's drowning, Barton blames Irish people with an irrational hostility that the narrator describes as habitual ("he always blamed anything on them if he could"), including a remark about the nearest mouse (a mouse is standing on its head in the metal sugar-basin) which the narrator renders: he "eyed the mouse morosely, as though accusing it of Irish ancestry." The same speaker then lapses into a plain complaint about being at the Café Royal three nights earlier with his wife: "it's too much of a bloody contrast, coming back to all this." A shell then bursts and "both candles went out."
*Mechanism:* national prejudice is shown as a mess crank's tic and is gently mocked by the narrator through the mouse image; the underlying fact is Barton's loss of mood after leave. The shell extinguishing both candles is a stage-comic interruption in a dugout.
*Candidate application:* An officer's pet prejudice treated by his juniors as comic background, covering a homesick sulk, is a documented mess-room type. Period ethnic prejudice is reported here, not endorsed.

**[E26a-21]. The vocabulary of wounds: "cushy," "Blighty," and the "funny" first-day casualty.** (PW-09, ch. III §I, L157-159; also ch. II §III, L143)
In one dugout paragraph the narrator wonders if he will ever "get a Blighty wound" and lists recent casualties: an officer-scholar of Greek and Latin epigrams hit in the shoulder with muscles "damaged enough to keep him in England several months," a "nice bullet wound"; a shell splinter in another officer that is "cushy"; and a young Sandhurst officer, "Harris," with a seriously damaged knee whom the doctor thought "out of the war for good." The narrator comments: "It was funny to think of young Harris being hit in the first twenty-four hours of his first tour of trenches." The same hope appears in the raiders' stock phrase (E26a-15).
*Mechanism:* a wound is graded on a scale of convenience (cushy, nice, funny), the only comic register available for bodily damage; the dark irony ("funny") is the narrator's own, aimed at the first-day casualty's bad luck, not at the wounded man.
*Candidate application:* A working vocabulary that grades injuries by how far they take a man from the line is a documented soldier idiom, with "funny" marking the arbitrariness of timing.

**[E26a-22]. The narrator on the men's cheerfulness, the "number nine pill," and the gap between officers and ranks.** (PW-09, ch. III §I, L159)
The narrator thinks about how the war affects people "by weeks and months" and "boys under twenty stood it worst." He notes that a failing officer could be sent home by the colonel "with a confidential report," often to safe jobs in England, while a private "dud" stayed until he was "killed or wounded," and that "A 'number nine pill' was all they could hope for if they went sick." (My gloss: a stock, all-purpose laxative-type remedy issued by army medical orderlies.) He says Barton tells him he is too easygoing with the men, but he often feels he is asking them to do more than is fair; and: "I could never understand how they managed to keep as cheery as they did through such drudgery and discomfort." He also notes that in the line "one builds up a sort of comradeship with the tough and willing ones" but "back in billets the gap widens."
*Mechanism and officer-poet's relation:* this is the narrator's most direct statement about other ranks' humor in my range: he observes their cheerfulness from the officer's side of a rank gap, does not claim to share its jokes and says he does not understand its source. It is an observation by a sympathetic outsider, not a description of how the men would explain themselves.
*Candidate application:* An officer who notices and cannot explain his men's cheerfulness, and who sees that the army treats a failing officer and a failing private differently, supplies a documented frame for rank asymmetry in coping.

**[E26a-23]. Leave: the drawing-room waiting-room and the silent canary.** (PW-09, ch. III §I, L163-169)
The narrator walks into a quiet Kensington house on a Sunday evening in June with a piano playing next door and feels "detached from my surroundings," "rather as if I were in a doctor's waiting-room, expecting to be informed that I had some incurable disease." He notes that "the sound of the piano suggested that the specialist had a happy home life of his own, but it had no connection with my coming and going," and that a caged canary reminds him of the canaries the tunnelers carried out of their galleries. Earlier, on the line, he had mused about a German governess he once knew, and that the rumbling cartwheels beyond the line were "German cartwheels."
*Mechanism and register:* the comic image (the waiting-room, the specialist's happy home life) is a displaced fear, and the canary is the only object that links the two worlds. The tone is wry but the content is dread; the joke does not release it.
*Candidate application:* A man on leave who experiences comfort as a medical waiting room, and who finds one thing (a caged bird) that reminds him of the front, is a documented picture of the leave-man's displacement.

**[E26a-24]. Telling the family and the old neighbor: civilian-facing sanitizing and the lightning-conductor.** (PW-09, ch. III §II, L173-177)
On leave the narrator drops "a few heavy hints" about the trenches and notes that the earlier battles "hadn't divulged themselves very distinctly to anyone except the actual participators, who had so far proved inarticulate reporters." He had meant not to mention the raid to his aunt, but it "slipped out," along with the recommendation for a Military Cross. Her reaction ("But I thought you were only looking after the horses") makes him wish he had held his tongue; he later excuses himself by thinking "that she was bound to find out sooner or later, especially if I got killed." With an old neighbor, Captain Huxtable, a retired officer who thinks every fit man should serve, the narrator says "it was a jolly fine life out at the Front" and "for the moment, I probably believed what I was saying." At the gate, with "English reticence" forbidding anything direct, he mentions only that his aunt's lightning-conductor had been blown off in the spring and that she said it was not worth putting up again; Captain Huxtable laughs and says she "must be getting war-weary," and squeezes his hand "which meant more than he could say aloud."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's sanitized account to the civilians, the dark joke about being killed as the means of telling the truth, and a household anecdote standing in for what neither man can say. The comedy is displaced onto a lightning-conductor.
*Candidate application:* A trivial household anecdote used to carry a shared worry between a soldier and an old man who cannot go is a documented civilian-facing displacement.

**[E26a-25]. The leave-train platform and the absence of the cricket averages.** (PW-09, ch. III §II, L183-185)
At Waterloo the narrator watches people leaving after the first leave train: some "sauntered away with assumed unconcern," others "hurried past me with a crucified look," and one well-dressed woman was "biting her gloved fingers," returning "alone to a silent house on a fine Sunday afternoon." In the train he opens a Sunday paper containing "communiqués, casualty lists, and reassuring news from Galicia, Bukovina, and other opaque arenas of war," and says the absence of first-class cricket averages "was an apt epitome of the life we were condemned to."
*Mechanism and register:* the sentence about the woman is plain description with no comic frame; the cricket-averages remark is the narrator's usual dry compression of what the war removed from life.
*Candidate application:* The platform scene shows the civilian-side cost of leave stated without a joke; the comic remark follows only after the narrator is safely seated.

**[E26a-26]. The Army and Navy Stores shopping list: the salmon for the colonel, the "trench requisites," and the pistol.** (PW-09, ch. III §II, L187-189) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
Using his aunt's membership ticket, the narrator buys two pairs of rubber-handled wire-cutters (his "private contribution to the Great Offensive," bought because the company's cutters were savagely blunt), two bottles of old brandy, an automatic pistol and "a superb salmon" meant to placate Colonel Kinjack, since he overstayed his leave by a day: "the salmon spelled safety at Battalion Headquarters. Probably the word 'smelt' also entered my apprehensive mind." He rejects a periscope ("I shouldn't be in the trench long enough to need it"), "Mortleman's Patent Sound Absorbers" and a combined compass-barometer, and notes that the pistol was not "warranted to stop a man." His reasoning for the pistol includes, in summarized form, the thought that if he were lying wounded in a shell-hole with an injury worse than a Blighty one he might need a quick means of ending his own suffering, and a joke that the only target he could be sure of hitting was himself. He says he later stored the fish in the ship's cold-storage cupboard: "my fishy insurance policy."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's humor is a mock-practical shopping list with a pun (salmon/"smelt"), the comic weighing of civilian "trench requisites" against actual trench needs, and a grim practical reasoning about his own possible death delivered in the same comic-shopper's register. The blunt wire-cutters are the one purchase he treats as a real contribution; Mansfield's complaint about the issued cutters (E26a-18) is the context.
*Candidate application:* A comic shopping scene in which practical equipment is chosen for the narrator's own possible death, with no change of tone, is a documented way of making a contingency speakable.

**[E26a-27]. Back at the battalion: the salmon delivered, Fewnings's shirt, and the narrator's bathos before the Push.** (PW-09, ch. III §II, L193-201)
On the train a fellow passenger talks "irrepressibly" about his father's farm and a bull that "had produced sixty black and white calves"; "This information was received with apathy." At Bussy the narrator sees men bathing and thinks how young they look and "how difficult to embody them in the crouching imprisonment of trench warfare." He sends the salmon and a bottle of brandy to Kinjack's headquarters by Flook, and Barton, given the second bottle, calls him "a cunning old Kangaroo." The ex-schoolmaster Fewnings, now Lewis-gun officer, reports that the Brigadier ticked him off for a light-colored shirt, but that it "wouldn't make much odds what sort of shirt he was wearing in a week or two." The narrator adds that neither he nor Durley had ever had a word from the Brigadier ("perhaps because our shirts were the orthodox color") and that a few kind words from such "graduated autocrats" would make a platoon commander think them "jolly good Generals." In the mess, "harmony" reigns "as if our certainty of a volcanic future had put an end to the occasional squabblings"; the servants meanwhile are competing for a young woman in the farmhouse (a "comedy of primitive courtship"; see register). That night the narrator tries "to convert the idea of death in battle into an emotional experience," composes a fine-sounding private creed ("Courage, I argued, is a beautiful thing ... I am happy tonight, and I don't suppose I'll be dead in a month's time"), and then "almost fell over a goat which was tethered among some currant bushes."
*Mechanism:* two kinds of rank-comedy (the brigadier's attention to a shirt and not to the work; the colonel bribed with fish) sit beside the narrator's own self-staged exaltation, which a goat in the garden punctures at once. Fewnings's remark is the gallows version of the same point: the shirt will not matter.
*Candidate application:* The last days before an offensive as a period of comic, affectionate normality (sports day, a mess in harmony, a bribe), told by a narrator who is also quietly rehearsing a noble death and being embarrassed by a goat.

### D. Chapter IV, "Battle" (L203-335): the Somme preparation, July 1, Mametz Wood and the Quadrangle, and the laughter of the war

**[E26a-28]. Move-day stew, "lousy with guns," and the officers' name for a picture: "jocular Jesus."** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L209-211)
On the morning of a battalion move Barton is "always in such a stew" that the narrator keeps out of his way; when Barton emerges he is "hung like a Christmas tree" with equipment. Morlancourt is "lousy with guns," "as the saying was." The narrator and Durley share a billet room hung with a picture of the Eiffel Tower and "a ludicrous oleograph of our Savior preaching from a boat, which we always referred to as jocular Jesus." He ends the day sleeping in his fleabag with rain in the gutters ("it was no hardship; I have never slept more soundly in any bed").
*Mechanism:* the soldiers' idiom ("lousy with guns"), a shared pet name for a devotional picture (irreverent but not hostile in the text), and a flat comfort.
*Candidate application:* An officers' pair who give a recurring billet object an irreverent private name show how a billet's furniture becomes a set of in-jokes.

**[E26a-29]. "Comic Cuts": the Corps Intelligence Summary as official humor.** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L215)
The narrator copies the Seventh Division battle plan into his notebook; the objectives (Rose Trench, Orchard Alley, Apple Alley, Willow Avenue) have "gentler associations" and he calls the area "this topographical Arcadia." The officers decide "the Fourth Army was going to fairly wipe the floor with the Bodies" (sic; the text's own wording; possibly a transcription slip for the usual German label, flagged for the browser read). The Corps Intelligence Summary is known to the battalion as "Comic Cuts" (my gloss: the name of a popular British comic paper). He lists some of its claims: three enemy balloons destroyed, many enemy batteries silenced, a Russian capture at a place "forty-four miles northeast of Luck," and "At Martinpuich a large yellowish explosion had been observed." He calls the compiler "the anonymous humorist."
*Mechanism and target:* a nickname that turns the army's own intelligence product into a joke-sheet, and the narrator's deadpan quotation of its more vacuous lines. The target is the corps staff's writing, not any soldier.
*Candidate application:* A troops' nickname for an official bulletin as a comic paper is a documented small institution of its own, one that coexists with the real planning.

**[E26a-30]. Durley on the brass-hats: "regardless of expense," and the staff officer who does not nod.** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L217-219)
Going up to the line, the narrator and Durley pass the gas-shell smell at a heavy-gun position called Gibraltar (the narrator takes it for yellow weeds; Durley corrects him), and "a staff-officer who was wearing well-cut riding boots and evidently in a hurry to rejoin his horse" squeezes past "without so much as a nod." The narrator asks Durley whether the brass-hats "do know a hell of a lot about it all"; Durley replies he hopes they've learned something since Loos "when they'd allowed the infantry to educate themselves ... regardless of expense," and adds "sagely" that "They've got to learn their job as they go along, like the rest of us." Of the German balloons the narrator says it "was our duty to desire their destruction, and to believe that Corps Intelligence had the matter well in hand." Then, at 71. North, he remembers two privates "engaged in a good-humored scuffle; one had the other's head under his arm," and asks why he should remember that and forget so much else.
*Mechanism:* officers' irony toward the staff is dry and conditional (hope they've learned), the narrator's mock-dutiful sentence about the balloons mocks the official posture, and the private scuffle is the one scene he can keep.
*Candidate application:* Gallows irony about the cost of the staff's learning ("regardless of expense") as a way of naming a catastrophe without raising the voice.

**[E26a-31]. The postponement: "All Dressed Up and Nowhere to Go," and the officer's view of the men's concerns.** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L223-225)
On the wet Wednesday junior officers meet in the muddy street "to exchange cheerful remarks" because solitude "produced the sinking sensation appropriate to the circumstances"; the men "were keeping their spirits up as vocally as they could." When the attack is postponed forty-eight hours, "A popular song, 'All Dressed Up and Nowhere to Go,' provided the obvious comment, and our confidence in Operation Orders oozed away." On the march up, the narrator carries a Hardy novel and thinks of a familiar scriptural line ("we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out of it"), and notes that "the symbolism of the sunset was wasted on the rank and file, who were concerned with the not infrequent badness of their boots, the discomfort caused by perspiration, and the toils and troubles of keeping pace with what was required of them."
*Mechanism:* a song title used as collective commentary, groups of officers cheerfully talking to avoid solitude, and the narrator's own awareness that the men's concerns are practical where his are literary. The statement about the rank and file is his, not theirs.
*Candidate application:* A well-known popular song's title as a shared one-line verdict on an order, and the officer's honest awareness that his own aesthetic response is a private luxury.

**[E26a-32]. The wire-cutting party: Staff returns, the narrator's inflated "prestige," and Kendle's "cheeky jokes."** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L227-233)
Kinjack finds the wire gaps not cut; the narrator, huddled in a dugout reading Hardy, is fetched by Barton. He then reflects on the Staff: they "couldn't produce wire-cutters like a conjurer," and on the eve of battle Barton would probably be "filling in a 'return' stating how many men in his company had got varicose veins or married their deceased wife's sister." He imagines "our prestige as a Regular Battalion had been entrusted to my care on a front of several hundred yards." During the night cutting in shellfire, he works with Kendle ("a nineteen year old lance-corporal from my platoon") and Worgan ("one of the tough characters of our company"). The narrator calls the wire "a clawing thicket which couldn't have been more hostile if it had been put there by the Germans." At dawn "young Kendle was remarkable for his cheerfulness and courage, and his cheeky jokes. Many a company had its Kendle, until the war broke his spirit." As they come in, Kendle remarks "that my new wire-cutters were a fair treat." Barton, with "almost aunt-like anxiety," urges them in.
*Mechanism and relation to other ranks:* the narrator's mock-grandiose self-assessment (the regiment's honor on a few hundred yards) is the officer's own joke; Kendle's contribution is a single approving remark in the other-ranks idiom ("a fair treat"). The narrator gives Kendle a type ("many a company had its Kendle") and says what the war does to such men: it "broke his spirit" (a generalization that, in the next pages, the text enacts in another way: Kendle is killed).
*Candidate application:* The cheerful young lance-corporal as a unit's mascot of cheeky humor, praised by the officer for exactly that, as a figure who will not survive the book.

**[E26a-33]. Worgan's "haircut," the tiger among the gooseberry bushes, and the narrator's self-diagnosis of egotism.** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L237-239)
Returning in daylight to finish the wire, the narrator regards it "with personal enmity." Worgan, who stays with him, remarks that they are "giving it a proper haircut this journey" (the narrator reports this as Worgan's saying). The narrator finds the task "like going out to weed a neglected garden after being warned that there might be a tiger among the gooseberry bushes." He then judges himself: "I should have been astonished if someone could have told me that I was an interesting example of human egotism," and says he had "entirely forgotten that tomorrow Six Army Corps would attack, and whatever else happened, a tragic slaughter was inevitable." He adds that if he had been intelligent enough to realize all that his talents "would have been serving in some more exalted place, probably Corps Intelligence Headquarters." Back in the trench he says it "had been great fun," flourishing the cutters.
*Mechanism:* an ordinary soldier's metaphor from domestic gardening ("haircut"), the narrator's own more elaborate garden simile, then a sharp turn to self-criticism and a throw at the staff.
*Candidate application:* Garden and barber vocabulary for work done under fire, and the later turn on one's own excitement, document how a man can keep fear at a comic distance while knowing what the cost will be.

**[E26a-34]. The Military Cross ribbon, "medal-reflexes," and the infantry's joke about Base ribbons.** (PW-09, ch. IV §I, L243)
On 30 June the doctor "unpicked his own faded medal-ribbon, produced a needle and thread, and sewed the white and purple portent on to my tunic"; Barton's congratulations beam "homeliness and humanity." The narrator says that for the rest of his service "the left side of my chest was more often in my mind than the right," and that "Books about war psychology ought to contain a chapter on 'medal-reflexes' and 'decoration complexes.'" The decorations, he says, became "more and more fortuitous and debased as the War went on," and the infantry "rightly insisted that medal-ribbons earned at the Base ought to be a different color."
*Mechanism and target:* the doctor's act (sewing on a ribbon he himself wears) is warm; the narrator's coinage ("medal-reflexes," "decoration complexes") is a mock-clinical joke on the vanity of the decorated; the line attributed to "the infantry" is a class joke on rear-area honors. It is his report of what the infantry said, not a line he attributes to a named man.
*Candidate application:* A soldier's joke that rear-area decorations should be a different color documents decoration cynicism as a collective frontline position, and the narrator's honesty that he too watches his own chest.

**[E26a-35]. July 1 in the dugout: "Carpet slippers and kettle-holders," the Waverley Pen jingle, and Barton's "opera box."** (PW-09, ch. IV §II, L249-254)
During the forty-minute final bombardment Barton and the narrator sit "speechless, deafened and stupefied." Afterward the narrator asks Barton what he had been thinking about; Barton answers: "Carpet slippers and kettle-holders." The narrator's own mind had run on a stationers' advertising jingle he cannot complete: "They come as a boon and a blessing to men, / The Something, the Owl, and the Waverley Pen" (my gloss: a period pen-nib advertisement), and he agonizes over whether the first item was "the Shakespeare" or "the Dickens." He and Barton go up to watch the attack "in our fortunate role of privileged spectators"; Barton calls the support trench "our opera box." Jenkins stays crouching in a corner; the narrator says "His haggard blinking face haunts my memory," that Jenkins was "a good officer both before and afterwards," and "I felt no sympathy for him at the time, but I do now." He ends: "I cannot turn my field-glasses on to the past."
*Mechanism:* under extreme noise, both officers' minds retreat to domestic or commercial trivia (slippers, a pen advertisement); the comic detail (whether it was the Shakespeare or the Dickens) is the narrator's account of his own mind. Barton's "opera box" is a gallows-theater joke about observing an attack. The Jenkins passage turns plain with a confession of earlier unkindness.
*Candidate application:* Mind-flight into trivia during a bombardment, and the confession that one lacked sympathy for a man in terror and now has it, are documented moves of the officer-memoirist.

**[E26a-36]. The July 1 notebook: a spectators' tone, small private jokes, and the plain register of the wounded.** (PW-09, ch. IV §II, L258-280) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
The narrator gives his pencilled field notes from the support trench. Early entries mix landscape and watching ("Some Yorkshires a little way below on the left, watching the show and cheering as if at a football match"), a shave, a sunny view of Fricourt ("a sunlit picture of Hell, and still the breeze shakes the yellow weeds") and "Have just eaten my last orange." He notes, as the Manchesters wait, "Silly if I got hit!" When the Manchesters attack, the notes record a casualty total, a wounded man moving an arm, men lying still, and wounded left out in No Man's Land; the narrator does not joke. A gunner observer who had been in Fricourt tells him it is "full of dead" and, of one dead German officer (details not reproduced), says "a fine looking chap," "with some emotion, which rather surprised me." After Fricourt falls, the narrator lies in the long grass "basking in sunshine where yesterday there were bullets," sees troops "wandering about in search of souvenirs" and writes: "Feel a bit of a fraud."
*Mechanism and register:* the notebook keeps a diarist's dry brevity; the spectator comparisons (football match) are other men's, reported with distance. The only humor-like touches are two small self-deprecating or ironic fragments ("Silly if I got hit!"; "Feel a bit of a fraud"). The prose turns plain as soon as casualties appear.
*Candidate application:* The contrast between a spectator's sunny tone (football, oranges) and the casualty entries, in the same notebook, is the audience's own measure of how quickly the register changes.

**[E26a-37]. The concentration hollow: Kinjack's gruff order and the mouth-organ.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L284-288)
Resting before the Mametz assault the Brigade lies in a hollow; the narrator half-listens to the men's talk about souvenirs from the German trenches. Kinjack's order to a few men wandering about is shouted with a blunt remark (reported in the text with a mild oath) that they would be glad to lie down before they were much older. The narrator adds that Kinjack's "brusque manners had prevented him getting promotion, but everyone knew that it would be a bad day for the Battalion when Kinjack got his Brigade." A mouth organ "did its best to 'keep the home fires burning.'" He imagines his servant Flook, away on a carrying party, "awfully worried about not being with his officer-bloke."
*Mechanism:* the colonel's brusque joke-order is the gruff-affection register of command; the mouth-organ with a patriotic song is quietly ironic (the narrator says "did its best").
*Candidate application:* A commander whose abruptness is understood by everyone as care, and whose men's loyalty is stated indirectly (a bad day for the Battalion), is a familiar documented command voice.

**[E26a-38]. The road to Mametz and the first dead: "I wanted to be able to say that I had seen 'the horrors of war.'"** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L290-292) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
Marching up behind the Royal Irish, the narrator sees, in rows by the roadside, about fifty British dead, men of several regiments ("beyond regimental rivalry now"), with discarded equipment and dead horses, and smells a "sour pervasive stench" that he had not met before. Then he adds a dry, self-incriminating sentence: "I wanted to be able to say that I had seen 'the horrors of war'; and here they were, nearly three days old." Later, in a communication trench, he sees the first newly dead Germans he has seen in eight months (details not reproduced), who seem "unlike our own," and says they were "one with the little trench direction boards whose unfamiliar lettering seemed to epitomize that queer feeling I used to have when I stared across No Man's Land, ignorant of the humanity which was on the other side."
*Mechanism and register:* the grim joke is on his own curiosity (he collects the sight); the descriptions of the dead are in plain, uncomic language; the thought about unfamiliar lettering and the unknown humanity of the enemy is the most reflective moment of the chapter.
*Candidate application:* A character who remembers seeing "the horrors of war" as something he had wanted to see, and who now recoils at the memory, shows how curiosity can later become a source of guilt.

**[E26a-39]. The Staff discovers Mametz Wood is still full of Germans: mock-official summary and "did a guy."** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L292-296)
After four hours of delays the battalion moves forward, while the colonel's guide, "having presumably lost his way," is "having a much hotter time than we were." The narrator reports that the Staff had assumed the near side of Mametz Wood was unoccupied, and that "an uproar broke out ... which demonstrated with machine-guns and bombs that the Staff had guessed wrong." After eleven hours and under three miles, Kinjack describes the withdrawal as "did a guy" (slang for slipping away). The narrator's summary: the operation "had enabled the Staff to discover that Mametz Wood was still full of Germans," and "a few strong patrols could have clarified the situation more economically than 1,000 men with picks and shovels"; "The necessary information had been obtained, however, and the Staff could hardly be expected to go up and investigate such enigmas for themselves." He then adds a softening note that "the difficulties of extempore organization must have been considerable."
*Mechanism and target:* a mock-official summary in the passive voice of failure ("had enabled the Staff to discover") followed by a retreat to fairness, the narrator's own hedging; the target is the Staff's reliance on others for reconnaissance.
*Candidate application:* The deadpan "the operations had enabled the Staff to discover" as a form: stating the useful result of a failed operation in the Staff's own voice.

**[E26a-40]. Dottrell's rations, "Asbestos Bill," and the memory of jokes that cannot be recalled.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L300)
After four hours of rain the Quartermaster Dottrell arrives with rations and the rum issue: "He had a cheery word for everyone, and his jocularity was judicious. What were the jokes he made, I wonder? Their helpfulness must be taken for granted." The only one the narrator recalls is Dottrell's chaffing of an officer named Woolman, whose "dumpy figure had bulged abnormally" since he put on a bulletproof waistcoat sent by "Woolman's young lady in England"; "although reputed to be extremely vulnerable" it had made him perspire, and "a humorist in his company" had named him "Asbestos Bill."
*Mechanism:* the narrator admits he cannot recall the jokes, only their function ("helpfulness") and one nickname: a mock-heroic name for an officer in an impractical gift. The nickname is by one of his own men ("a humorist in his company"), which suggests other-ranks naming of an officer.
*Candidate application:* A quartermaster whose jokes work as ration-delivery morale, and a company wit who coins a nickname for an officer's civilian-bought gear, document the two forms of humor around supply and equipment.

**[E26a-41]. "Time seems to have obliterated the laughter of the war": the narrator's statement of what the laughter was.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L302)
The passage is the narrator's only explicit account of what laughter did before the Mametz Wood attack, and the example is the point, so it is quoted at length:

> Time seems to have obliterated the laughter of the war. I cannot hear it in my head. How strange such laughter would sound, could I but recover it as it was on such an evening as I am describing, when we all knew that we'd got to do an attack that night; for shortsighted Barton and the other company commanders had just returned from a reconnaissance of the ground which had left them little wiser than when they started. In the meantime we'd got some rum inside us and could find something to laugh about. Our laughter leapt up, like the flames of camp fires in the dusk, soon to be stamped out, or extinguished by our impartial opponent the rain. The consoling apparition of Dottrell departed, and I don't suppose he did much laughing once he was alone with his homeward rattling limbers.

*Mechanism and claim:* the narrator says that (a) the laughter belongs to a particular hour (a night before an attack, with rum and a visit from the Quartermaster), (b) it cannot be recovered in memory, (c) it was brief ("leapt up ... soon to be stamped out") and (d) the one who brings the jokes is probably not laughing himself once alone. It is the memoir's closest thing to a theory of the humor, and it is a retrospective loss, not a description of its content.
*Candidate application:* A veteran who can remember that there was laughter but cannot reproduce it, and who notices that the joker is not laughing when alone, is a useful model of how humor appears in memory: as function and mood without texts.

**[E26a-42]. The unnerved officer and the pistol bluff: a stage-managed threat, and "a bona fide manner."** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L310) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
In the dark of Bottom Wood the narrator and Kendle find an officer who has "lost all control of himself" on his way to tell the colonel that his company has not moved. The narrator, knowing Kinjack already thinks him "cold-footed," draws his pistol "with an assumption of ferocity," tells him to go straight back to "Asbestos Bill" (the nickname of Woolman, E26a-40; the text implies he is this man's company commander), and adds that Kinjack would take a worse view; the man calms and goes back. The narrator says "I have saved one of our officers from being court-martialled for cowardice," tells Kendle quietly it is the way to Headquarters, and quips to Barton that Woolman "seemed to be marking time, in spite of his bulletproof waistcoat." A parenthesis follows: "(Ten days later he was killed in what I can only call a bona fide manner.)"
*Mechanism:* a mock-theatrical act (the pistol bluff) performed to protect a man, the narrator's comic summary of it as his "queer" contribution, and the parenthesis that records the man's death by contradicting the colonel's earlier suspicion of cowardice. The humor and the death share a sentence.
*Candidate application:* A rescue disguised as a threat, and the later flat note that the "coward" was killed in earnest, show how a narrator can restore a man's honor with a deadpan parenthesis.

**[E26a-43]. Quadrangle Trench: the tired officers, Fernby, the field-glasses quarrel, and the dead German youth.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L316) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
In the captured trench the narrator finds three officers "with hunched shoulders and glum unenterprising faces" and, in contrast, a new officer, Fernby, "exulting in the fact that he didn't feel afraid." Two "tough-looking privates" are disputing the ownership of a pair of captured field-glasses; the narrator "pulled out my pistol and urged them, with ferocious objurgations, to chuck all that fooling and dig," then thinks "I seem to be getting pretty handy with my pistol." He then lifts one dead German from the trench floor, props him against the bank and wipes mud from his face with his sleeve (the dead youth looked about eighteen, with "a gentle face"); "this was the first time I'd ever touched one of our enemies with my hands." He concludes: "I hadn't expected the Battle of the Somme to be quite like this." A little later Kendle, who is with him, says of a helmet bobbing over the far bank "I'll just have a shot at him," fires once, "looked at us with a lively smile; a second later he fell sideways" (the wound is not described here: the text gives it in a clinical sentence not reproduced).
*Mechanism and register:* the comic touches in this passage (the field-glasses quarrel, the narrator's "handy" pistol) are small and brief; the register turns plain at the German youth and at Kendle's death. The narrator describes Kendle as having "a lively smile" at the moment he is killed, which is the pattern of the book's cheerful young men.
*Candidate application:* The cheeky young soldier's last act and expression recorded in a single plain sentence shows how the memoir marks a death without comment or consolation.

**[E26a-44]. The solo bomb attack: self-parody, "view-holloas," and the hysterical laughter.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L318-322)
After Kendle's death the narrator, in rage, discards his helmet and equipment and runs across a valley with bombs, pulling the second safety-pin with his teeth. At the top he finds "a well-conducted trench with a great many Germans in it" who are "already retreating," unaware "that they were being attacked by a single fool." He throws a few more bombs and, "idiotically elated," gives "a series of 'view-holloas' (a gesture which ought to win the approval of people who still regard war as a form of outdoor sport)" and says "Having thus failed to commit suicide, I proceeded to occupy the trench." Alone, he says he counted the abandoned sets of equipment: "between forty and fifty packs, tidily arranged in a row—a fact which I often mentioned (quite casually) when describing my exploit afterwards," and compares himself to "a boy who has caught a fish too big to carry home (if such an improbable event has ever happened)." He adds that apparently "no one was any the worse for my little bombing demonstration. Perhaps I was disappointed by this." Returning, he "flopped down beside him [Fernby] with an outburst of hysterical laughter."
*Mechanism and target:* a long run of self-mockery (a single fool, "view-holloas" as a fox-hunting cry, the bragged-about packs, the fish) at the expense of the narrator's own bravado and, in the view-holloa aside, at the civilians who see war as sport. The "hysterical laughter" at the end is the book's plainest instance of laughter as nervous release after extreme risk. The narrator, still in the register of understatement, calls the act a failure "to commit suicide," a dark phrase of the narrator's own that is quoted here only because it is non-graphic and is the narrator's joke at his own recklessness (noted in the register).
*Candidate application:* Comic self-description of one's bravest act, together with the later discovery that it was a blunder, is a documented officer-memoir device for not letting valor stand uncorrected.

**[E26a-45]. After Kendle: "attrition of manpower," the dying man, and the men who "weren't worrying about souvenirs now."** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L322) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
On his return, the narrator sees Kendle's body again and asks a bomber to cover the face (details of its color not reproduced). He passes the dead young German he had propped up; "someone had trodden on his face." He says "He and Kendle had canceled one another out in the process called 'attrition of manpower.'" Further on he sees one of his own men dying slowly of a head wound (not described), with two former mates beside him, one digging with an entrenching tool and the other scooping earth with his hands: "They weren't worrying about souvenirs now." He also says "a weary business it is, to be remembering and writing it down."
*Mechanism:* a staff euphemism ("attrition of manpower") used bitterly for a named dead British lance-corporal and an unnamed dead German; the comment on souvenirs recalls the looting talk of earlier pages, and the men's silence is the narrator's evidence of what has changed.
*Candidate application:* A euphemistic official phrase set against two specific deaths, and a practical gesture (digging) as the other ranks' expression of grief, are two registers the memoir uses side by side.

**[E26a-46]. "My courage was of the cockfighting kind": the bishop in the hospital ward.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L324)
Disregarding a written order from Barton, the narrator stays in the trench all morning and admits that "as the day went on, I definitely wanted to kill someone at close quarters" (the narrator's own phrase; I do not reproduce the surrounding action). He comments: "If this meant that I was really becoming a good 'fighting man,' I can only suggest that, as a human being, I was both exhausted and exasperated. My courage was of the cockfighting kind. Cockfighting is illegal in England, but in July 1916 the man who could boast that he'd killed a German in the Battle of the Somme would have been patted on the back by a bishop in a hospital ward."
*Mechanism and target:* a comparison to an illegal blood sport that turns into an ironic point about wartime civilian and clerical approval. The target is the pious, war-glorifying civilian (the bishop), and the narrator's own bloodlust. The example is the point, so quoted: the satirical hinge is "illegal in England" against "patted on the back by a bishop."
*Candidate application:* A veteran's dry insistence that his own berserk phase was a kind of fighting animal's courage, and that civilian praise was incoherent about it, is a documented critique that can live inside a funny sentence.

**[E26a-47]. Kinjack's anger, the Brigadier "in Morse Code," and the colonel's sense of humor.** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L328, L334)
At headquarters Kinjack, glowering, asks "why I hadn't come back with my company bombers" and why the narrator had not consolidated the captured trench; the narrator learns that the Corps artillery had been held up for three hours because Kinjack could not report that "my patrol" had returned, and that "he'd been ticked off over the telephone by the Brigadier (in Morse Code dots and dashes, I suppose)." The narrator "looked at him with a sulky grin." Two days later at Heilly, Kinjack makes "one further comment" and says "He would probably leave me out of the 'next show' as a punishment," whereupon the narrator remarks: "Some people asserted that he had no sense of humor, but I venture to disagree with them."
*Mechanism:* the narrator reads the colonel's one punishing sentence as a joke (the punishment is to be spared the next battle), turning command harshness into an exchange of humor between ranks. The remark about telephone "dots and dashes" is the narrator's joke at the Brigadier's expense, unspoken.
*Candidate application:* A colonel whose punishment is a deadpan joke about missing the next battle shows rank-humor as a private, understated contract between commander and subordinate.

**[E26a-48]. The relief by New Army Welsh troops: "I'm sorry for those poor devils!"** (PW-09, ch. IV §III, L332)
As the battalion is relieved, the narrator watches unseasoned Welsh New Army troops arrive, "mostly undersized men," and has "a sense of their victimization." A young platoon officer gives them orders "for the sake of appearances." Barton says "By God, Kangar, I'm sorry for those poor devils!" and the narrator says "dimly he pitied them, as well he might," foreseeing that two days later their division would be "involved in massacre and confusion" in Mametz Wood (the later fate is described, not reproduced).
*Mechanism and register:* no humor in the passage. The nickname "Kangar" appears in Barton's mouth in a serious sentence, and the narrator's sympathy for the unseasoned men is the only explicit pity in the chapter.
*Candidate application:* Veterans' pity for fresh troops, stated plainly by a gruff commander, is a documented register that sits outside the unit's jokes.

**[E26a-49]. Heilly: the troops "contrived to be cheerful," and the muttered grumble at the Army Commander.** (PW-09, ch. IV §IV, L338)
In the swampy camp at Heilly "the troops contrived to be cheerful," with the narrator noting that "to be away from the battle and in a good village was all that mattered." The Army Commander's brief visit makes the men feel they had "done creditably," yet "more than one voice might have been heard muttering, 'Why couldn't the old —— have dumped us in a drier spot?'" (the text's own dashes replace the oath). The narrator notes that the Army Commander "may well have been absentminded," since the Welsh Division had attacked Mametz Wood that day.
*Mechanism and target:* soldiers' muttered complaint against a general who has come to see them, with an oath in place, as an other-ranks form of affection or resentment that coexists with the visit's morale effect; the narrator's gloss undercuts the general with the reminder of the attack elsewhere.
*Candidate application:* The muttered complaint about a visiting senior officer's choice of ground documents how formal visits and informal grumbling coexist.

### E. Chapter IV continued (L336-399), with the opening of Chapter V (L400-408): Heilly, Amiens, the Transport Lines and the news of Cromlech

**[E26a-50]. Heilly mess talk: the grandfather clock, the barometer, and the order of things to say.** (PW-09, ch. IV §IV, L340-344)
On a sunny morning at Heilly the narrator, Barton and Durley relax; Barton asks, apropos of nothing, if the narrator always lights his pipe with his left hand. The narrator answers with a household anecdote: for years he thought the maker's name on the hall grandfather clock was "Thos. Verney. London," then found it was "Thos. Vernon. Ludlow!", and that the old barometer, which has always pointed to "Expect Wet from N.E.," really reads "Except" (which "goodness knows what that means"). Barton, who lives in Shropshire, declares "a square mile of Shropshire" worth the whole of France; Durley, reading Dickens, offers "a mild plea" for Stoke Newington. The narrator says his companions, "who were disinclined to be talkative, assured me that with such a brain I ought to be on the Staff." A paragraph of daydream follows: his aunt arranging flowers, a traction-engine, and a village cricket-ground mower who "didn't know that he would some day join 'the Buffs,' migrate to Mesopotamia, and march to Baghdad."
*Mechanism:* inconsequential, deliberately domestic anecdotes (clock, barometer, home counties) are the mess's way of filling a rest day with talk about a safe world; the narrator reads his own wandering as the stuff of which Staff officers are made. Only the last clause of the daydream (the mower's future) lets the war in.
*Candidate application:* The trade of homeland trivia in the mess (in effect, my county is better than your country: a paraphrase of the exchange, not a quotation) as a ritual of belonging, and the staff-barbed compliment that closes it, show how banal conversation functions as a refuge.

**[E26a-51]. Dottrell on the Quartermaster with two cars, and the General's staff's insomnia.** (PW-09, ch. IV §IV, L348)
Dottrell arranges a lift to Amiens through the "opulent" former Quartermaster of the battalion, now Quartermaster at Army Headquarters. En route the narrator sees "a handcuffed German prisoner" in a Staff car, "soon to provide material for an optimistic paragraph in Corps Intelligence Summary," and notes operation orders now assert that "we were 'pursuing a beaten enemy.'" At Querrieux, "as we passed the General's white château Dottrell speculated ironically on the average income of his personal staff, adding that they must suffer terribly from insomnia with so many guns firing fifteen miles away." At the Army Quartermaster's house, told he could spare a car "since he had the use of two," Dottrell "said he'd been wondering how he managed to get on with only one car."
*Mechanism and target:* two exchanges of rank-and-position repartee at the rear's expense: the staff's alleged insomnia (guns "fifteen miles away") and the Quartermaster's two cars. The latter is a sarcastic compliment delivered to the man's face.
*Candidate application:* A front-line quartermaster teasing a rear-area counterpart about privilege to his face, as a form of sanctioned license among men of similar rank, is a documented repartee.

**[E26a-52]. The Amiens lunch: "God only knows when we'll see a clean tablecloth again," Joe's riding joke, and the accidental good joke.** (PW-09, ch. IV §IV, L350)
In a green-shuttered private room at a famous restaurant the officers eat lobster, duck and champagne. Barton orders with the remark "God only knows when we'll see a clean tablecloth again." Dottrell tells of nearly being reduced to the ranks for "making a book" in Ireland before the war, and observes it was "lucky for some of us that horsemanship wasn't needed for winning the war, since most mounted officers now looked as if they were either rowing a boat or riding a bicycle uphill." As they leave he says he has "half a mind to go and look for a young lady to make his wife jealous"; the narrator suggests "there was always the cathedral to look at, and discovered that I'd unintentionally made a very good joke."
*Mechanism:* the lunch is a ritual of excess before a return to the line; Barton's remark carries the darkest note (the clean cloth as a last luxury); the riding joke lowers the peacetime elite; the narrator's cathedral line is a joke by accident, which he reports as a small triumph.
*Candidate application:* The luxurious lunch behind the lines as a gallows ritual, with the single dark remark about the cloth, and the officer who gets a laugh by accident, are two ordinary devices of mess humor.

**[E26a-53]. Revenge play in the old front line: Stokes shells down German dugouts.** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L360)
Before the Bazentin attack the narrator and Durley, free for a day, walk through No Man's Land in "a half-holiday mood." "A spirit of mischievous destruction possessed us"; they pitch unused trench-mortar shells down German dugout stairs and "reveled in the boom of subterranean explosions," feeling "as if we were getting a bit of our own back for what we'd endured." An indignant Salvage Corps officer shouts at them and they "decamped before he could identify us." The narrator notes that the 1st of July "had become an improbable memory, now that the dead bodies had been cleared away."
*Mechanism:* schoolboy-like destructiveness as release and revenge at an empty place that once menaced them; the comic element is flight from the officer in charge. The last sentence is plain.
*Candidate application:* Playful destruction of the enemy's abandoned works, with an escape from a salvage officer, is a documented small act of reclamation of a frightening place.

**[E26a-54]. David Cromlech's visit: "desperately cheerful ejaculations" and a meeting that cannot be about the war.** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L364) *(Plain register.)*
The narrator's friend David Cromlech, an officer of the Second Battalion, is heard among visitors "keeping up their courage with the volubility usual among soldiers who knew that they would soon be in an attack." The narrator recognizes his voice "with a shock of delighted surprise" and overhears "his desperately cheerful ejaculations with that indefinite pang of affection often felt by a detached observer." They walk to his bivouac and agree that "neither of us really wanted to talk about the Somme Battle"; they talk instead about England and what they will do after the war; Cromlech "had dropped his defensive exuberance now." The next morning the battalion has gone; the narrator can identify the spot only by "a scrap of silver paper" that might have belonged to their chocolate.
*Mechanism and register:* the loud, joking voice of the soon-to-be-attacked is named by the narrator ("defensive exuberance") and shown to fall away in private. There is no joke in the farewell. (Outside knowledge, not in the text: readers often identify Cromlech as a portrait of the poet Robert Graves; the text does not say so.)
*Candidate application:* The loud group voice before an attack, and the private quiet that follows when two friends are alone, show the two layers a single person can show in an hour.

**[E26a-55]. "A nice comfortable one in the shoulder," and the counting-down nickname.** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L366-370) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
The Transport Lines hear from the ration party that two officers were killed, several wounded, and that "Old man Barton had got a nice comfortable one in the shoulder" (a Blighty wound; E26a-21). The eight officers kept in reserve are, in the narrator's words, called by Durley after a counting-down children's rhyme whose period title carries a racial slur (the title is not reproduced); the narrator notes "there were now only seven of us," that is, the nickname is a running tally of the group's depletion.
*Mechanism:* a wound reported as an agreeable outcome ("nice comfortable one") and a nursery-rhyme counting game as a running tally of casualties. The two are the same register: a joke form that takes the count from the dead and wounded and keeps it as a game.
*Candidate application:* A reserve group's counting-down nickname, updated as members are called away, is a documented form of gallows tally; it should be handled with the slur removed.

**[E26a-56]. The tent argument about Cromlech: the colonel's remark, "pushing up daisies," and Perrin's "dry up."** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L372-376)
The reserve officers discuss "that queer bird Cromlech." The narrator reports that Cromlech was "a positive expert at putting people's backs up unintentionally," and that a colonel had said he "threw his tongue a hell of a lot too much, and that it was about time he gave up reading Shakespeare and took to using soap and water," then added "I'm agreeably surprised to find that he isn't windy in trenches." Mansfield once told him "Unless you watch it, my son, you'll grow up into the most bumptious young prig God ever invented!" In the tent Holman (a Sandhurst man new to the unit who looks down on temporary officers) complains of Cromlech's "hot air" and "brainwaves about who really wrote the Bible." Durley says he doesn't mind betting that "he'll go a long way—provided he isn't pushing up daisies when Peace breaks out." Holman retorts that "the sooner he's pushing up daisies the better." Perrin, a quiet older officer, ends it: "Oh, dry up, Holman! For all we know the poor devil may be dead by now."
*Mechanism and limit:* the whole exchange runs on the stock idiom "pushing up daisies." Durley uses it lightly about a friend; Holman uses it as an insult; Perrin ends the exchange by pointing out that the death is real. The narrator says Holman "soon became more democratic," which indicates that the rule about whom one can mock was learned.
*Candidate application:* A death idiom that is permissible as a mild joke between friends turns into cruelty when a hostile speaker uses it and a third officer ends the conversation: a documented illustration of limits of license (who may use the joke, about whom, and when a death is actually possible).

**[E26a-57]. The candle night: "I didn't want to die—not before I'd finished reading *The Return of the Native* anyhow."** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L380)
Late at night in the tent the narrator, unable to sleep, feels "a sort of numb funkiness" and says "I didn't want to die—not before I'd finished reading The Return of the Native anyhow." He notes that no one has mumbled "For Christ's sake put that light out." He judges his companions' outlook "mostly mechanical; they dulled everything with commonplace chatter and made even the vividness of the War ordinary," and that his meeting with Cromlech "had reawakened my relish for liveliness and originality." Looking at the sleepers (Fernby, Durley) he is released from his own despondency by the thought that "I couldn't save them, but at least I could share the dangers and discomforts they endured," and records a "little moment of magnanimity" in his diary, adding "rather portentously, perhaps."
*Mechanism and relation to the others' talk:* a comic reason for not wanting to die (a half-read novel); an explicit statement that the narrator finds his messmates' everyday chatter a dulling force while he values the wit of an eccentric friend; and a typical self-undercutting ("rather portentously"). This is the narrator's own view and is not corroborated by anyone's reported speech.
*Candidate application:* An officer who values wit and originality and finds ordinary mess talk dull is a documented perspective; it should be paired with the passages that show the ordinary talk doing real work (E26a-40, E26a-56).

**[E26a-58]. The camp: camouflage-stain pictures, "Nap," the *Daily Mail* and the servants' singing.** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L384-390)
In the reserve camp the narrator watches camouflage paint smears on the tent canvas turn into "ungainly birds," "monkeys in scarecrow trees" and a fight "in a Futuristic style" with a figure "losing an arm and a leg from a bomb explosion" (a canvas pattern, not a real event). At night "the others" play the card game "Nap" and talk "stale war stuff out of the Daily Mail," while the servants sing around a shell-box fire. Gunfire in the distance is "like someone kicking footballs." The last evening he spends "jawing to young Fernby about foxhunting," after reflecting that "a second-lieutenant could attempt nothing—except to satisfy his superior officers" and that "Armageddon was too immense for my solitary understanding."
*Mechanism:* idle imagination, card games and sports talk as ways to bear a period of suspense; the narrator's self-deflation ("a second-lieutenant could attempt nothing") answers his own apocalyptic mood.
*Candidate application:* The rear-area reserve as a time of enforced idleness whose comic coping (card games, daydreaming, talk of hunting) is shaped by its distance from the fight, with the news always bad.

**[E26a-59]. Dottrell at the crossroads, and the battalion's return as "an army of ghosts."** (PW-09, ch. IV §V, L394-398) *(Plain register.)*
Waiting six hours with Dottrell for the battalion, the narrator finds "the husky-voiced old campaigner was more eloquent than he realized," and that "in the simplicity of his talk there was a universal tone." The narrator reports that for Dottrell "it was a big thing for the Battalion to be coming back from a battle, though, as he said, it was a new Battalion every few months now." At dawn the returning troops pass: "shambling, limping, straggling and out of step," silent except for the clink of bayonets on petrol-cans and, at last, the bagpipes of Gordon Highlanders. The narrator: "it was as though I had watched an army of ghosts."
*Mechanism and register:* the narrator gives no jokes in this scene; the quartermaster's brief remark about the turnover of battalions is plain. The rum from Dottrell's flask is the only comfort.
*Candidate application:* The returning column as a silence in which no one jokes is a documented contrast to the jokes at the ration point (E26a-40), and a clear case of the register changing with the hour.

**[E26a-60]. Cromlech's death and the allowed comment; the "London editor" paragraph.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L406-408) *(Plain register.)*
On the march to rest, the narrator reports that he had heard "yesterday afternoon" that Cromlech "had been killed up at High Wood." "This piece of news had stupefied me, but the pain hadn't begun to make itself felt yet, and there was no spare time for personal grief"; "To have thought about Cromlech would have been calamitous." The only comment Durley, Dottrell and the others allowed themselves was "Rotten business about poor old 'Longneck.'" The narrator adds that "it was queer how the men seemed to take their victimization for granted," and imagines a London editor in a Staff car: "the spirit of the troops was amazing. And so it was. But somehow the newspaper men always kept the horrifying realities of the War out of their articles, for it was unpatriotic to be bitter, and the dead were assumed to be gloriously happy."
*Mechanism and register:* grief is deferred by working (no time) and compressed to one allowed comment, a nickname and an adjective. The newspaper paragraph is the memoir's direct complaint about the press's civilian-facing sanitizing.
*Candidate application:* A group's one allowed sentence for a named dead friend (a nickname and "rotten business"), with the narrator's private grief delayed, is a documented minimal ritual that complements the joking one (E26a-56).

### F. Chapter V, "Escape" continued (L410-485): sickness, the hospital, England and the letters from the Battalion

**[E26a-61]. The thermometer experiment and a joke about the departed Australians.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L410-412) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
The narrator blames Australians who left the La Chaussée billets in a bad state (neglected sanitation, furniture burned, details not reproduced) and wonders "Did the Australians leave anything else behind them, I wonder?", suggesting a disease from Gallipoli. Taken ill, he remembers the thermometer he inherited as Transport Officer: "I had never taken the temperatures of any of the horses, but I now experimented shakily on myself. When I saw that it indicated 105° I decided that the thing was out of order." Flook fetches the doctor and he wakes in a hospital in Amiens.
*Mechanism:* a dry joke at the expense of colonial guests, and a self-comic account of testing a veterinary instrument on himself and disbelieving the result. Both are told in the narrator's retrospective, comfortable voice. The remark about the Australians is period national banter, reported here, not endorsed.
*Candidate application:* The soldier's disbelief of the thermometer is a compact example of fever humor, where the joke is on the instrument.

**[E26a-62]. "Wangle my way home": the ethics of being ill, and the unsent prayer.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L416-418)
On his fourth night in the ward the narrator feels "almost too well" and recognizes that the change from active service to invalidism is "an acute psychological experience": "The door to safety was half open ... and the unutterable words 'wangle my way home' forced their way obstinately to the foreground, supported by a crowd of smug-faced excuses." Durley and the Adjutant had joked about how well he looked and "took my fortitude away with them." He reflects "I suppose they'll all think I'm swinging the lead" and prays: "'Oh God,' I prayed, 'do get me sent down to the Base!' (How often was that petition whispered during the War?)" He also reports seeing Allgood's name in the Roll of Honor and says "I sympathized with myself about Allgood, for I had been fond of him."
*Mechanism:* a self-lampooning confession of a malingerer's temptation (the "smug-faced excuses"), the parenthetical aside that makes the private prayer a universal one, and the odd phrase "sympathized with myself" for grief about a named dead friend. The visitors' jokes about his good looks are a cohesion gesture that he reads as a pressure.
*Candidate application:* The unspoken wish to be sent down as a respectable form of cowardice, and the jokes of friends that turn into pressure on the sick man, are documented pressures around illness.

**[E26a-63]. The delirious boy behind the screens, and the ward's verdict on a noisy man.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L418) *(Plain register.)*
A boy in the ward who calls continually to the nurse is judged by the man in the next bed (who "had himself got more than he wanted, in both legs") to be "making the most of" whatever wound he has; the narrator thinks "he must be jolly bad." In delirium the boy cries to someone called Dicky: "Don't go out, Dicky; they snipe like hell!" The narrator: "All the horror of the Somme attacks was in that raving." Next morning "the screens had vanished; the bed was empty, and ready for someone else."
*Mechanism and register:* the ward's earlier skeptical judgment ("making the most of it") is shown wrong by the delirium; there is no commentary on the correction. The register is plain; the narrator's own response is to fall asleep.
*Candidate application:* The group's quick suspicion that a loud patient is exaggerating, and the unnoticed correction by the next morning's empty bed, show a limit of ward humor: the benefit of the doubt is withheld until it is too late.

**[E26a-64]. Flook's farewell: dialect, a wink, and advice not to hurry back.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L422-424)
When the doctor orders the narrator on to the afternoon train, "my mind uttered a spontaneous Magnificat." Flook, who has come to the hospital with the officer's left-behind belongings, announces in "a hoarse undertone" in dialect: "Ah've brought the stoof." He says "the lads in C Company were hoping to see me back soon," and at parting "winked and advised me, confidentially, not to be in too much of a hurry about getting back." The narrator wonders when Flook "himself would get a holiday" and whether he "would ever return to his signal-box on the railway."
*Mechanism:* the servant's wink and whispered advice is a tacit permission to malinger; the narrator turns the thought back to the servant's lack of a holiday. This is one of only two places in my range where an other-ranks speaker is given dialect spelling (the other is the Base Depot sergeant, E26a-89).
*Candidate application:* A servant's covert solidarity (a wink, a whispered counsel against haste) as a warm, unequal comic exchange, with the officer aware of the inequality.

**[E26a-65]. "They call it enteritis," the Military Cross in *The Times*, and the lesser miracle.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L426)
Carried on a stretcher although he "could easily have walked," the narrator reaches a Red Cross hospital in Rouen. Asked what is wrong, he answers "They call it enteritis" "with an indefinite grin." The doctor, reading *The Times*, notices the narrator's name in a list of Military Crosses, pats his shoulder and says he will be sent to England. The narrator: "Good luck had 'wangled me home.'" He credits that doctor with saving him from "operations at Delville Wood, which might quite conceivably have qualified my name for a place on the Butley village War Memorial."
*Mechanism:* the grin and the deadpan ("They call it") acknowledge that the diagnosis is not wholly his; the medal that rescues him is the same one that he had mocked as "medal-reflexes" (E26a-34). The memorial line is the book's usual way of stating a fatality by hypothetical.
*Candidate application:* A decoration turned into a ticket home is a documented irony that sits close to what soldiers called wangling.

**[E26a-66]. The hospital label "Sick P.U.O." and the narrator's invented expansions.** (PW-09, ch. V §I, L430)
On the Hospital Ship the narrator looks at the luggage label attached to him, "Army Form W 3083," stamped "Lying Train and Ship" with "Sick P.U.O." on the reverse. The example is the point, so quoted: "On the boat, my idle brain wondered what 'P.U.O.' meant. There must, I thought, be a disease beginning with P. Perhaps it was 'Polypipsis unknown origin.' Between Rouen and Havre I devised several feebly funny solutions, such as 'Perfectly undamaged officer.' But my final choice was 'Poorly until October.'"
*Mechanism:* backronym play on an official abbreviation, produced idly in a state of relief; the narrator labels his solutions "feebly funny," which marks the humor as a private nervous pastime, and says he still has the label ("it is in my left hand as I write these words"), which I read as a small plain sign that the episode mattered. The final expansion, "Poorly until October," names the hoped-for length of his absence from the line.
*Candidate application:* Making up mocking expansions for an abbreviation on a medical label is a documented small invention of the patient on the way home.

**[E26a-67]. Oxford and Mr. Farrell: the old man who avoids the war, "sugar-teeth," and the incomprehensible disaster.** (PW-09, ch. V §II, L436-440)
Mr. Farrell, a retired civil servant and military-record historian, visits the narrator and keeps the conversation "safely on this side of the Channel"; the narrator "wouldn't have minded telling him a thing or two about the Somme." Farrell says sugar is scarce but "that doesn't affect me; my doctor knocked me off sugar several years ago." The narrator thinks of his childhood nickname for him, "sugar-teeth" (for which his aunt used to scold him), and averts his eyes "as if he could read my thoughts." He remembers eavesdropping as a boy on Farrell, Huxtable and his aunt "gassing about politics, or India," Farrell's habit of laughing "Her-her-her" after his own stories, and thinking it "rather fun" to fire a toy pistol outside the drawing-room door. He concludes that Farrell had been "almost as if he were talking to a survivor from an incomprehensible disaster."
*Mechanism:* a private nickname that is almost said aloud; a joke Farrell himself makes without knowing; and a childhood prank imagined. The civilian's careful avoidance is read by the narrator as respect and as a measure of the distance between civilians and survivors.
*Candidate application:* The civilian elder who avoids the subject out of tact, and the veteran who half-wishes he could say more, are a documented pair; the veteran's silence is a courtesy to himself as well.

**[E26a-68]. The father, the one-legged son and Haig's despatch: satire of the civilian view.** (PW-09, ch. V §II, L440)
In the hospital garden the narrator watches "that tall well-preserved man pushing his son very slowly across the lawn in a long wheeled bed"; the son "sallow and sulky, as he well might be, having lost one of his legs." The father tells a nurse how splendidly the boy had done at Gommecourt and shows a letter; the narrator wonders if he has ever learned that "the Gommecourt show had been nothing but a massacre of good troops," imagines him moving a flag on a war map, and says: "For him the Wood was a small green patch on a piece of paper. For the Welsh Division it had been a bloody nightmare." The scene then moves into the present tense: the father reads the son Haig's despatch aloud ("There is strong evidence that the enemy forces engaged on the battlefront have been severely shaken by the repeated successes gained by ourselves and our Allies") until the nurse arrives "brightly" with "Time for some more beef-tea!" and "Nourishment is administered under approving parental eyes."
*Mechanism and target:* a vignette of the civilian's war: map-flags, a quoted despatch, a spouted feeding-cup. The target is the proud, uncomprehending father (and the official prose he reads) and the son's unspoken dislike is the counterpoint. The narrator keeps his comment to a few dry sentences and lets the juxtaposition work (see the contrast note with E11b in the pattern roll-up).
*Candidate application:* The civilian reading official good news aloud to a wounded son while a nurse brings beef-tea is a documented image of home-front incomprehension that needs no joke from the veteran.

**[E26a-69]. Cromlech "risen again from the dead."** (PW-09, ch. V §III, L444)
During his last week at the hospital, canoeing on a weed-choked Cherwell, the narrator notes that his friend Cromlech "had risen again from the dead": he had seen the name in the newspaper list of killed, then received a telegram saying the man was in a London hospital and "going on well." For "fully a fortnight" he had accustomed himself to the idea of his friend's dead body "somewhere among the Somme shell-holes," and now had to be "disentangling myself from the mental obituary notices." He thinks, affectionately, "Silly old devil ... he always manages to do things differently from other people."
*Mechanism:* a comic reversal of a death notice, expressed in the language of the Resurrection and a tender insult. The earlier grief (E26a-60) is retroactively made premature, but the narrator does not make it cheaper.
*Candidate application:* A friend's false death notice and the affectionate insult at his return illustrate how relief takes the same form as the earlier mockery of a friend's oddness.

**[E26a-70]. Butley: the stables, Dixon, and the callback to "in its infancy."** (PW-09, ch. V §III, L446-450)
At home on sick leave the narrator cuts dead wood from a cedar while his aunt warns him that "it would be no joke to tumble out of such a big tree." In the stable harness-room, with rusting bits and a tin of hoof ointment from 1914, he thinks how long it would take Dixon to tidy it "forgetting for a moment that he'd been dead six months." Deciphering, with a pocket mirror, an old friend's postscript on the schoolroom blotting-paper, he reflects that his experience of such "poignant associations" was "still in its infancy," as someone had said "of Poison Gas when lecturing to cannon-fodder at the Army School."
*Mechanism:* the narrator's retrospective callback to the gas expert's phrase (E26a-4) turns a lecture phrase into a private motif; the death of Dixon is given in the same flat clause the book uses elsewhere; the aunt's remark is an unconscious joke about a danger that is not the real one.
*Candidate application:* A phrase from an official lecture that returns months later as a private bitter joke is a documented mechanism of memory, particularly for men who have heard few such phrases twice.

**[E26a-71]. Home-front jingoism: the prelate, the colonel's tree-trunks and the dachshunds.** (PW-09, ch. V §III-IV, L450, L476)
Of his aunt, the narrator says that she had "swallowed all the stories about atrocities in Belgium" (not elaborated), that it was her duty "as a patriotic Englishwoman, to agree with a certain prelate when he preached the axiom that 'every man who killed a German was performing a Christian act,'" but that she would have helped a wounded Prussian on her way to the post office. When she complains she has "no conversation," he assures her that after being "incessantly ordered about in the Army" it is "a great relief ... to be with someone who had no conversation." In the hunting field Colonel Hesmon declares that anyone under forty not in uniform is "nothing but a damned shirker" and regrets not having smashed the windows of a German count's house before war began; the master, Moffat, says the old man "was known to have practiced revolver-shooting in his garden, addressing insults to individual tree trunks and thus ventilating his opinion of Germany as a whole," adds "It's very odd; for Hesmon's an extraordinarily kindhearted man," and says "the Missus's dachshunds only just escaped being interned as enemy aliens."
*Mechanism and target:* satire of civilian hostility, delivered through two gentle portraits (the narrator's aunt, and Colonel Hesmon as reported by his friend Moffat); the target is the gap between the hatred preached and the kindness practiced. The narrator's reply to his aunt is an affectionate joke with a rebuke inside it.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's gentle teasing of a patriotic, kind relative, and the comic portrait of a colonel who shouts insults at trees, show humor as a way of keeping family love while refusing family rhetoric.

**[E26a-72]. Protheroe's parlor: the narrator's own hero-talk, and its self-exposure.** (PW-09, ch. V §III, L452-456)
Visiting a middle-aged neighbor and the village doctor, the narrator, "welcomed rather as a returned hero," slaps Protheroe on the back, tells him "he'd got the best dugout in Butley," admits "it was pretty bad out there, with an inward feeling that such horrors as I had been obliged to witness were now something to be proud of," asserts "I wouldn't have missed this War for anything," and, "frowning portentously as I lit my pipe, and forgetting for the moment what a mercy it had been when it brought me home myself," assures them "we should be in Bapaume by October." Walking home he feels "elated at having outgrown the parish boundaries," thinks "It was bad luck on Protheroe and the doctor; they must hate being left out of it," and decides "The War's all right as long as one doesn't get killed or smashed up."
*Mechanism and target:* the narrator mocks his own hero-talk by recording it with its inner contradictions; the target is his own vanity, and the civilian audience's complicity. The line "The War's all right as long as one doesn't get killed or smashed up" is the kind of casual remark the memoir returns to with irony after the next letter.
*Candidate application:* The wounded officer at home who performs the veteran for neighbors, and later sees through it, shows the pull of the civilian audience on the soldier's account of himself.

**[E26a-73]. Dottrell's two letters: "The Batt. is not now over strength for rations!"** (PW-09, ch. V §III, L460-468) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
Two letters from the Quartermaster, written on consecutive days, arrive together. The first reports that the battalion lost 200 men in three days, that an aid-post was blown in (details summarized, not reproduced), and that "The outlook is not rosy"; he is "Very glad to hear you are sitting up and taking nourishment," notes that "A lot of our best men have been knocked out recently," and adds: "We shall soon want another Batt." The second, which the narrator reads first and calls "the worst of the two," is addressed "Dear Kangaroo." It lists the battalion's losses at Ginchy: "Poor Edmunds was killed," "Also Perrin," Durley "badly wounded" (the sites of the wounds are not reproduced), "Asbestos Bill died of wounds," Fernby "very badly hit and not expected to live," C.S.M. Miles and Danby killed; and ends: "The Batt. is not now over strength for rations! The rest of the Brigade suffered in proportion. Will write later. Very busy." The narrator: "I walked about the room, whistling and putting the pictures straight. Then the gong rang for luncheon. Aunt Evelyn drew my attention to the figs, which were the best we'd had off the old tree that autumn."
*Mechanism and register:* the Quartermaster's gallows joke is his own accounting language (the battalion is no longer over strength for rations because so many are dead), placed after a list of names the narrator has known throughout these chapters (Perrin, Durley, "Asbestos Bill," Fernby, Miles) and before the closing "Very busy." The narrator's whistling and picture-straightening are his own displacement; the aunt's remark about the figs is the home counterpoint. The text gives no further comment.
*Candidate application:* The Quartermaster's ledger joke at the end of a casualty list is a documented form: the shortest and most bitter statement in the letter is an administrative fact about rations, and the reader supplies the rest.

**[E26a-74]. Dottrell's grievance, and the narrator's "selfish world."** (PW-09, ch. V §IV, L472-476)
In October another letter from Dottrell "let off steam in a good grumble": Kinjack has left to command a Brigade ("a great loss to the Batt."), but "They all come and go; stay in the Batt. long enough to get something out of it, and then disappear and will hardly give a thought to the men and officers who were the means of getting them higher rank. It's a selfish world, my friend." Commanding officers beg him to stay but "neither know nor care what happens to me ... when I leave the Service on a pension of 30s. a week." The narrator admits: "I am afraid I wasn't worrying overmuch about 'the dear old Corps' myself," while hunting with a friend's horses; hunting was said to be continued "for the sake of the boys at the Front." He notes that he was "armed with my uniform and the protective coloring of my Military Cross." He sees the hunting subaltern as "part of the 'selfish world' to which his attention had been drawn," and that the misty mornings "were simultaneously shedding an irrelevant brightness on the Ypres Salient and on Joe Dottrell riding wearily back with the ration-party."
*Mechanism and target:* the quartermaster's complaint is bitter and dry, and the narrator's retrospective response turns it on himself. The hunting slogan is quoted as a piece of civilian rhetoric.
*Candidate application:* The long-serving other-ranks-adjacent veteran who sees command as a ladder others climb, and the narrator who recognizes himself as one of the climbers, document the rank asymmetry in memory.

**[E26a-75]. The Caxton Hall waiting room: the artificial-limb price list and "the grim humor."** (PW-09, ch. V §IV, L480)
At a final Medical Board in London the narrator sits in an empty waiting room whose only ornament, above a fireplace with no fire, is a notice giving the scale of prices for artificial limbs and instructions on how officers can obtain them free. He says he was "adjusting my mind to what a journalist might have called 'the grim humor' of this footnote to Army life" when a Girl Guide announces that "Colonel Crossbones (or whatever his cognomen was) would see me now." He "pretended to be feeling pleased" on leaving.
*Mechanism:* the situation supplies the joke (an official price list as the room's only decoration), and the narrator declines to claim the phrase, assigning "grim humor" to the journalists. The nickname for the examiner (Crossbones) is his own.
*Candidate application:* An institution's own unintentional gallows joke, noticed and left alone, is a documented moment where the veteran refuses the commentary.

**[E26a-76]. The last day's hunting: a doubt "dismissed before it had been formulated," and the Romanian army.** (PW-09, ch. V §IV, L482-484)
After a good day's hunting the narrator decides that "the War was worth while if it was being carried on to safeguard this sort of thing. Was it? I wondered; and if a doubt arose it was dismissed before it had been formulated." He recalls enlisting "for 'three years or the duration'" and sees "a lifetime." A bugle seems to say "You'll all have to go in the end." On the train he reads about Romania and says: "I couldn't help feeling annoyed with the Romanian Army for allowing their country to be overrun by the Germans. They really might have put up a better show than that!"
*Mechanism and frame:* the first passage records the narrator's disillusion in its first, suppressed form, in the book's characteristic rhetorical question; the second parodies the armchair strategist's irritation, using the narrator himself as the target.
*Candidate application:* A man who has been in the line complaining mock-pompously that an allied army had not done better is a self-parodic form of the civilian's view; and a doubt that dissolves as soon as it is framed is the book's loyalty-and-disillusion pivot.

### G. Chapter VI, "At the Depot" (L486-553): Clitherland Camp, Cromlech's banter, Durley's account and the departure

**[E26a-77]. Clitherland Camp: the factory of Fusiliers, and a death in a parenthesis.** (PW-09, ch. VI §I, L492-496)
The narrator says the camp "for more than eighteen months ... had been manufacturing Flintshire Fusiliers, many of whom it was now sending back to the Front for the second and third time," and that it was "as much an essential cooperator in the national effort as Brotherhood & Co.'s explosive factory," which flared and reeked nearby. Among the officers he knows are Cromlech (recovered, "in spite of having had a bit of shell through his right lung," enough to play football), Eaves (who "was quietly making the most of his few remaining months") and Ormand. The narrator interjects, in parentheses, "(He was killed in February while leading a little local attack.)" They talk of "old times with the First Battalion" which "had already acquired a delusive unobnoxiousness," while "the 'Big Push' of last summer and autumn had now found a successor in 'the Spring Offensive' (which was, of course, going to 'get the Boches on the run')."
*Mechanism:* the camp-as-factory comparison, scare quotes round the staff's slogans, and a forward-looking parenthetical about Eaves's death: the same flat clause the book used for Dixon (E26a-70) and the "bona fide" officer (E26a-42).
*Candidate application:* The depot as a production line that is rated as a war industry, with a staff slogan renewed each season, is a documented target of mess-room irony.

**[E26a-78]. Ormand's overdraft, the troops' ditty, and the "droves of victims."** (PW-09, ch. VI §I, L498)
Ormand repeats his "periodic remark" that his only reason for wanting to go out again is that it would let him "pay off his overdraft at Cox's Bank." The narrator, looking at the senior officers' table, notes the Majors "might well have adopted as their motto the ditty sung by the troops: 'We're here because we're here because we're here because we're here.'" He observes that training is left to hard-worked regular sergeant-instructors, that recruits are "growing steadily worse" because most "had joined the Army unwillingly," and: "The war had become undisguisedly mechanical and inhuman. What in earlier days had been drafts of volunteers were now droves of victims. I was just beginning to be aware of this."
*Mechanism:* a dry repeated remark whose joke is the financial motive for returning to war; the troops' circular song, quoted as the Majors' "motto," is a documented soldiers' song used by an officer to mock the officers; the narrator's observation of conscript morale is plain.
*Candidate application:* The circular troops' song as a comment on aimless duty, and a young officer's repeated joke about the bank as his reason for going back, are two compact cohesion devices.

**[E26a-79]. Hunting as the antidote, and the loyalty of tradition.** (PW-09, ch. VI §I, L502-506)
The narrator spends weekly afternoons alone at a golf club ("I have always been considerably addicted to my own company") and arranges Saturday hunting with a care that was "an effective antidote to war-weariness." He says he "was readily given leave off Saturday morning duties, since an officer who wanted to go out hunting was rightly regarded as an upholder of prewar regimental traditions," and that on the morning train he was "encouraging as far as I could the illusion that I was escaping from everything associated with the uniform which I wore."
*Mechanism and tone:* mild self-irony about his own escapism and the way the regiment treats hunting as loyalty; the contrast between the camp and the Cheshire country is "like the difference between War and Peace."
*Candidate application:* An officer whose recreation is officially read as regimental virtue, and who knows he is escaping, shows how a private refuge can be institutionally endorsed.

**[E26a-80]. Cromlech's banter: Milton, Trollope and "a fad-ridden crank."** (PW-09, ch. VI §I, L508-510)
The narrator describes his friend David's literary demolitions ("that moribund academic concoction" for *Paradise Lost*), his dismissal of an orchestral concert as something that "upset him psychologically," and his claim that foxhunting is "the sport of snobs and half-wits." In a mock quarrel the narrator offers Trollope as a hunting man who was no half-wit; Cromlech answers that he was "probably a snob." The narrator "nearly lost my temper while refuting the slur on Trollope's character," David says the narrator "had no idea how funny I was when I reverted to my peacetime self," and, objecting to David's sneers at the hunt countries (Oakley, Grafton and others), the narrator says aloud "You're a fad-ridden crank," after which the matter "ended amicably" and "a minute later I was able to remind him that he was going on parade without a tie." The narrator adds that he "never tired of hearing him imitate the talk of excitable Welshmen" (period ethnic mimicry, not elaborated), and that the right thing to have said to a bad argument would have been "Oh rats, David!"
*Mechanism:* the friends' repartee is built on opposed tastes (literary fashion against sporting tradition) and on mutual teasing about identity, with the narrator as the straight man who knows he is being baited. It ends on a practical, domestic correction, the missing tie.
*Candidate application:* Friendship between officers conducted as mock argument about taste, and ended by a small mundane remark, is a documented non-combat register of cohesion.

**[E26a-81]. Two attitudes to the war, and Cromlech's "nose for anything nasty."** (PW-09, ch. VI §I, L512) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
The narrator says that "my attitude ... was that I wanted to have fine feelings about it. I wanted the War to be an impressive experience—terrible, but not Horrible enough to interfere with my heroic emotions," while Cromlech "distrusted sublimation and seemed to want the War to be even uglier than it really was," and "had no use for anti-war idealism." He tells Cromlech he has "a first-rate nose for anything nasty," after Cromlech's secondhand talk about the commercial vice establishments of French towns (summarized, not reproduced), imagining a "Puritan's Progress" through Havre or Rouen.
*Mechanism:* the narrator's gentle self-parody (the war as impressive but not too Horrible) is set against Cromlech's contrary insistence on squalor; the "nose" quip is a friendly insult from the narrator.
*Candidate application:* Two officers' opposed ways of making the war bearable (ennoble it, make it uglier), each one a defensive device, show that mutual teasing can operate across differing coping styles.

**[E26a-82]. The Olympic Hotel, the profiteers, and Durley's humor "suffered in proportion."** (PW-09, ch. VI §II, L516-518)
At a Liverpool hotel the narrator describes "an airtight Paradise made of imitation marble," and the noncombatants "doing themselves pretty well out of the War." When he points out to Durley a table of diners finishing a lavish meal with "stout and oysters" and says "they must be profiteers," Durley "regarded them with unspeculative eyes, but he made no comment; if he found them incredible, it wasn't surprising; both his brothers had been killed in action and his sense of humor had suffered in proportion." The narrator remarks "we weren't doing so badly ourselves" and refills Durley's glass. Durley, on sick leave, had been wounded in the throat and "spoke in a strained whisper."
*Mechanism and limit:* this is the memoir's plainest statement that bereavement erodes humor ("suffered in proportion"), and the narrator's own joke about the profiteers is deflected by his friend's silence. The narrator's second remark, that they themselves are doing well, is a self-implicating joke in the same mode.
*Candidate application:* A bereaved friend whose humor does not rise to a joke at the profiteers' expense is a documented marker of how loss narrows the license for jokes.

**[E26a-83]. Durley's account of Delville Wood: comic fragments inside a plain narrative.** (PW-09, ch. VI §II, L518-524) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
Durley tells the narrator of the attack of 3 September in a strained whisper (the narrator introduces it with "His narrative was something like this"; it is a reconstruction in the narrator's text, not a transcript). The details include small comic items: they left in such a hurry that "I'd got my puttees on over my slacks!"; the Adjutant "galloping up and down cursing everyone; I never saw him in such a state before—you know what a quiet chap he usually is"; "no one could say what all the windup was about"; the objectives, in a brewery-themed set of trench names (Pint Trench, Bitter, Beer, Ale, Vat, Pilsen Lane), which the text does not comment on; the Sergeant-Major's understatement on seeing the plan: "We'll have a rough house from Ale Alley"; and Durley's own verdict that "no one had any idea it was going to be such a schimozzle as it was!" In the attack, Durley says, the only thing in his head was "a tag from what some instructor had told me ... In an attack always keep going forward!" and later "another peacetime tag ... Never deprive a man of his weapon in a post of danger!" Of a man toppling over just ahead, he thought, "poor little chap, that's his last Cup Tie." The attack's violence (a runner shot, a man killed nearby, a German shot at close range, details not reproduced) and his own wounding (a heavy blow to the throat and shoulder, not detailed) are told in plain clipped phrases. Being helped out, he says men said "Good luck, sir"—"pretty decent of them under such conditions!"—and he recalls thinking "I must get back to Mother," and the Regimental Sergeant-Major leading him "very gently by the left hand."
*Mechanism and register:* the comedy lies in understatement and the retrospective absurdity of the training maxims; at the moment of the attack, the register is the plain flat of a survivor, and the only comparison with sport is one clause about a "Cup Tie." The sentence structure becomes fragmentary as consciousness fades.
*Candidate application:* A survivor's story that carries its funniest remarks in the setup (the puttees, the Adjutant, the Sergeant-Major's "rough house") and goes plain for the attack, and the rote maxims that haunt a man in action, are two features to compare against any account in which humor persists through the worst moment.

**[E26a-84]. The stretcher-bearers' note, "a gleam of his old humour," and the brigade's "holding attack."** (PW-09, ch. VI §II, L524-530)
At the dressing-station "they took a scrap of paper out of my pocket and read it to me. 'I saved your life under heavy fire'; signed and dated. The stretcher-bearers do that sometimes, I'm told!" (the text does not explain how the note got there or what the bearers meant by it). The narrator then reports: "He laughed huskily, his face lighting up with a gleam of his old humor." When asked if the attack was considered a success, Durley says that a brigade-staff man he met in hospital told him it was "only a holding attack in our sector," and that "we'd done all that was expected of us." The narrator comments that Durley "told it in a simple unemphatic way" with "unconscious gestures," and sums him up as "the shattered survivor of a broken battalion which had 'done all that was expected of it.'"
*Mechanism:* a joke about the stretcher-bearers' habit (told by the survivor, with a return of his earlier humor) and the official phrase "done all that was expected" reused bitterly by the narrator. The staff officer's phrase and the narrator's quotation of it are the memoir's usual way of letting an institutional sentence carry the verdict.
*Candidate application:* A survivor whose humor returns for one remark about the stretcher-bearers, and a brigade's justification repeated as an indictment, are two documented small beats in after-action talk.

**[E26a-85]. Fernby's death: "Silence was the only comment possible."** (PW-09, ch. VI §II, L532-534) *(Plain register.)*
Asked about young Fernby, Durley says he saw him in a hospital at Rouen: "They were trying to rouse him up a bit, as he didn't seem to recognize anybody," his head wounds are described (not reproduced), and "he died a few hours later." The narrator: "Silence was the only comment possible; but I saw the red screens round the bed, and Durley whispering to Fernby's bandaged head and irrevocable eyes, while the nurse stood by with folded hands."
*Mechanism and register:* the narrator states in a sentence the rule that governs the scene: no comment. The "red screens" echo the delirious boy in the Amiens ward (E26a-63). Nothing in the passage is comic.
*Candidate application:* The explicit statement that silence is the only available comment for a young officer's death marks the register change more clearly than any example.

**[E26a-86]. Cromlech's departure: "five to one against my ever using it."** (PW-09, ch. VI §III, L538-542)
David has himself passed for General Service. At the station "A glum twenty-one-year-old veteran" with "a certain philosophic finality," he tells the narrator: "I've reduced my kit to a minimum this time. No revolver. I've worked it out that the chances are about five to one against my ever using it." The narrator: "He hadn't explained how he'd worked the chances out, but he was always fond of a formula." Seeing him off, the narrator offers a hearty message for "old Joe" and receives "a crooked smile." Afterward he thinks "I ought to be going with him ... if I'd had the guts to ask for it," and weighs this against "a few more days with the Cheshire."
*Mechanism:* Cromlech's remark is gallows statistics (odds on a defensive weapon) delivered as practical packing advice; the narrator's guilt is plain, and set against his hunting.
*Candidate application:* A veteran's casual odds-making about his own survival, and the friend who stays behind weighing his hunting against his duty, document the way jokes and guilt coexist at a departure.

**[E26a-87]. The Copenhagen paper, the Army Commander and the "First Act of a play": the narrator's disillusion takes shape.** (PW-09, ch. VI §III, L542-546)
In Cromlech's left-behind propaganda weekly the narrator reads a translation from a Copenhagen paper: "The sons of Europe are being crucified on the barbed wire because the misguided masses are shouting for it." He asks "Was this really the truth, I wondered; wild talk like that was new to me." He recalls Dick Tiltwood, Fernby and Kendle, "and all those others whose violent deaths had saddened my experience," and that "so (I remembered it with a sick assurance) was I" a candidate for such a fate. Awake that night he remembers the "wine-faced Army Commander with his rows of medal-ribbons" at the Army School and asks "Did he know what he was doing, or was he merely a successful old cavalryman whose peacetime popularity had pushed him up on to his present perch?" He calls Flixécourt "the First Act of a play," Mametz the second, his leave the third; says "Bellicose politicians and journalists were fond of using the word 'crusade'" while "the 'chivalry' ... had been mown down"; and ends with a pun: "'Germinating and German-hating,' I thought, recovering my sense of reality with a feeble joke. After that I fell asleep."
*Mechanism and frame:* this is the first place in my range in which the narrator openly questions the Army Commander's competence and the crusade language. The pun on "Germinating" is a self-administered joke that returns him from lyrical grief to ordinary perspective, and the narrator himself calls it "feeble."
*Candidate application:* A man who has begun to doubt the senior command and who uses a pun to step back from the doubt is a documented first stage of disillusion; the joke is the way back into sleep.

**[E26a-88]. Dottrell's "mail and the rum ration," the final Medical Board and "Don't shake the table!"** (PW-09, ch. VI §III, L550-552)
Dottrell writes that the First Battalion "weren't thinking beyond the mail and the rum ration" and advises the narrator to stay away until the weather improves. At the final Medical Board a sallow youth confides that he'd "been out on the razzle the night before" and hoped to get another four weeks' home service. One of two silver-haired doctors asks "Feel fit to go out again?"; the narrator says "Yes; quite well, thank you"; the pen moves ("Has been passed fit for General Ser. ..."). The doctor says irritably "Don't shake the table!" (the narrator is tapping it); the narrator: "Shaking the table wouldn't stop that pen of his!"
*Mechanism:* the narrator's tapping and the doctor's irritation are the only visible friction in a routine that sends him back; his last line is a joke about the inertia of the pen.
*Candidate application:* The medical board as a ritual in which the only protest is a tapping finger, and the narrator's wry comment about the pen, is a documented bureaucratic image of the machine.

### H. Chapter VII, "Rouen in February" (L554-570): the Base and the hospital tent

**[E26a-89]. "Being for it again," a stolen coat, and the Guard Room.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L560-562) *(Plain register.)*
On the crossing the narrator feels relief at being "in the Machine again" and not responsible for his future; "the experience was known as 'being for it again.'" His only memory of the crossing is that "someone relieved me of my new trench-coat while I was asleep." He records a thirty-three-hour journey "for the benefit of those who like slow-motion wartime details." Looking for a blanket store at the Base Depot he stumbles into a guard room where a man bare to the waist kneels weeping, having just heard of his brother's death in the war. The Sergeant, "patient and unpitying," explains in dialect (with dropped aitches, as the text spells them) that the man had been under detention for assaulting the military police, that he has been half crazy (the sergeant's dialect, rendered here in standard spelling), and that it was "his third time out. A Blighty one don't last a man long nowadays, sir." The narrator goes on "into the gloom" and hears "uncouth howlings."
*Mechanism and register:* the stolen coat and the narrator's aside to readers who "like slow-motion wartime details" are the last light notes; the guard-room scene is plain. The Sergeant's phrase "A Blighty one don't last a man long nowadays" shows the earlier wound idiom (E26a-21) turned against itself by a sergeant. This is the second place in my range in which an other-ranks speaker is given dialect spelling (the first being Flook, E26a-64).
*Candidate application:* A sergeant's matter-of-fact formula for a grieving prisoner's state, and the old idiom of a "Blighty one" now accepted as short-lived relief, document how wound-humor wears out as the war lasts.

**[E26a-90]. The partition conversation, the Base mess and the notice board.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L564-566) *(Restrained handling; see register.)*
Lying awake in a canvas shed the narrator overhears two officers "in rapid Welsh voices" discussing a Rouen brothel (the conversation is not reproduced); he calls it "the most disgusting little conversation I'd ever listened to," then asks "what right had I to blame the poor devils for trying to have a good time before they went up to the Line?" and notes "the War seemed to be doing its best to make me feel unheroic." Next day the Base mess, which he finds "dispiriting," is full of officers "talking tediously about the War with an admixture of ineffective cynicism which hadn't existed twelve months before." They crowd round the notice board to see whether they are on the list for the line: "Those who were on the list laughed harshly and sat down, with simulated unconcern, to read a stale picture paper." The same board also lists three private soldiers who have been executed for cowardice since January (the official formula is not reproduced).
*Mechanism and register:* the harsh laugh of the officers whose names are on the list is a concealed-fear form of laughter that the narrator reads from outside; the "ineffective cynicism" is his name for the Base mess's humor. The notice about executions is mentioned in a single sentence without comment.
*Candidate application:* Laughter and a stale picture paper as the posture of the officers who have been told they are going up, and a notice board as the one place where both the list and the executed are posted, are two compact images of the Base's mood.

**[E26a-91]. The hospital tent: Parkins, the card players, and Strangford.** (PW-09, ch. VII §I, L568-570)
With German measles the narrator enters a hospital tent with six patients. The nurse asks "Mr. Parkins" to "shift round a bit to make room for the new patient," and Parkins "made it plain that six had been company in that tent and seven was an inconvenience"; "One of his opponents told him to stop chewing the rag and deal again." The narrator, content to read a book and "observe my fellow-invalids," does not offer to join the bridge. He then describes the quietest of them, Strangford, a nineteen-year-old in a kilt "of good North Irish stock," several months in the trenches, who talks happily of his father's harriers and otherwise "his brain appeared to cease working altogether." The description moves into the present tense: "After rubbing his knee, he takes a letter from his breast pocket ... once he smiles secretly, but when he has read it through he is solemn." (My range ends here, in the middle of the character sketch.)
*Mechanism and register:* the card players' grumbling about the extra bed is a comic cohesion of the patients (seen from outside); Strangford's sketch is plain and tender, with no comic frame.
*Candidate application:* A hospital tent as a small circle with its own grumbling rules, observed by a newcomer who does not join, and a very young patient's blank silence, are two documented sides of the same place.

## Pattern roll-up (what this unit establishes)

*All patterns are the narrator's account (the fictional "Sherston," a British second lieutenant, April 1916 to February 1917), one battalion, one army, officers' mess and billet; retrospective writing of 1930 with quoted diary and letters.*

**Functions.**
- *Cohesion and shared comedy of the mess:* sarcastic complaint about the planning of a raid at the mess table (E26a-9), nicknames and mock-orders between a company commander and a servant (E26a-2), a friendship conducted as mock argument (E26a-80), a quartermaster's morale-giving banter (E26a-40), a general topic of home counties and clocks to fill a rest day (E26a-50).
- *Coping before action:* mind-flight into domestic trivia ("Carpet slippers and kettle-holders," a pen-advertisement jingle, E26a-35); the song title "All Dressed Up and Nowhere to Go" as the whole company's verdict on a postponement (E26a-31); the stock wish "Who's for a Blighty one tonight?" (E26a-15); a joke-form counting-down game for a reserve group (E26a-55).
- *Laughter as release:* the "hysterical laughter" after the solo bomb attack (E26a-44); the loud "defensive exuberance" of friends before an attack (E26a-54); the narrator's pun to get himself to sleep (E26a-87).
- *Morale and discipline through gruff humor:* a colonel's punishment that is a joke ("leave me out of the next show," E26a-47); the narrator's mock-ferocious pistol to a man about to be damaged by the colonel (E26a-42, E26a-43).
- *Grief:* handled by suppression and a single allowed remark (E26a-60), flat parentheticals after a death (E26a-4, E26a-42, E26a-70, E26a-77), a statement that "Silence was the only comment possible" (E26a-85), and, once, a joke after a casualty list (E26a-73).

**Targets.**
- *Staff and brass:* Open Warfare lectures and "field-day make-believe" (E26a-3), the generals as "uncles on Prize-giving Day" with "plenty of medal-ribbons" (E26a-3), the staff's "returns" about varicose veins (E26a-32), the intelligence summary nicknamed "Comic Cuts" (E26a-29), the staff's discovery that a wood is full of Germans (E26a-39), the brigade's "nothing to lose ... except a few of our men" (E26a-11), the communiqué that "entered without difficulty" (E26a-19), the "holding attack" that did "all that was expected" (E26a-84), the Army Commander's competence (E26a-87).
- *Self:* the dying-fatalist pose (E26a-1), the "happy warrior" letter (E26a-14), the "tiger among the gooseberry bushes" and the "interesting example of human egotism" (E26a-33), the solo bomb attack (E26a-44), the hero at Protheroe's (E26a-72), the "selfish world" of a hunting subaltern (E26a-74). **The narrator is his own principal target**, which is the major difference from the satire in the verse (see the contrast note below).
- *Institutions and procedure:* the medical board's pen (E26a-88), the artificial-limb price list (E26a-75), the hospital label (E26a-66).
- *Civilians:* the preaching prelate, the colonel who shouts at trees, the proud father with the war map and Haig's despatch (E26a-68, E26a-71), the press that "kept the horrifying realities" out of its articles (E26a-60), the profiteers (E26a-82).
- *Training doctrine:* the bayonet lecturer's jokes (E26a-5), in which the narrator treats the humor itself as the grotesque element.
- *Enemy and national groups:* a few period national jokes (Irish, Australian, Welsh), documented not endorsed; the enemy is not a joke target in my range, and the German dead are treated gravely (E26a-38, E26a-43).

**Forms and mechanics.**
- *Understatement and deadpan:* "a nice comfortable one in the shoulder" (E26a-55), "We'll have a rough house from Ale Alley" (E26a-83), "no sense of humor" (E26a-47), "Curiously enough, I hadn't yet seen a German" (E26a-19).
- *Official language over horror:* the communiqué (E26a-19), "attrition of manpower" (E26a-45), "done all that was expected" (E26a-84), the "holding attack" (E26a-84), "the Staff ... to discover that Mametz Wood was still full of Germans" (E26a-39).
- *Nicknames and names:* "Kangaroo," "Kangar," "Comic Cuts," "Asbestos Bill" (E26a-40), "jocular Jesus" (E26a-28), "Longneck," "sugar-teeth" (E26a-67), "Old Kent Road" and "71. North" (E26a-12), "Crossbones" (E26a-75), the counting-down group nickname (E26a-55).
- *Parenthetical asides:* the retrospective parenthesis ("Most of us were either dead or disabled before gas had had time to grow up," E26a-4; "killed in what I can only call a bona fide manner," E26a-42).
- *Mock-heroic and mock-clinical coinage:* "the horsehood of England" (E26a-7), "medal-reflexes" and "decoration complexes" (E26a-34), "wangle my way home" (E26a-62), "Germinating and German-hating" (E26a-87).
- *Songs and ditties quoted:* "We're here because we're here ..." (E26a-78), "Keep the home fires burning" on a mouth organ (E26a-37), "All Dressed Up and Nowhere to Go" (E26a-31), "Gilbert the filbert" (E26a-9), a pen-nib advertisement (E26a-35).
- *Backronyms and wordplay:* "P.U.O." expansions (E26a-66), a pun on salmon and "smelt" (E26a-26).
- *Wound grading:* "cushy," "Blighty," "nice," "funny," "comfortable" (E26a-21, E26a-55, E26a-62, E26a-65).
- *Repartee between peers:* Dottrell and the Army Quartermaster's two cars (E26a-51), Cromlech and the narrator on Trollope (E26a-80), the accidental joke about the cathedral (E26a-52).
- *Pranks and play:* the Stokes shells down dugouts (E26a-53), the narrator's imagined toy pistol (E26a-67).

**Rules of license.**
- *Rank:* officers' sarcasm about Staff and Brigade in the mess is routine (E26a-9, E26a-30, E26a-51); a colonel's humor is read charitably by a junior (E26a-47); a servant jokes with his officer through gesture, not equal speech (E26a-2, E26a-64).
- *Death idioms:* a mild joke ("pushing up daisies") is licensed between friends about a possibly dead man and is cut off by a senior peer when it turns hostile ("For all we know the poor devil may be dead by now," E26a-56).
- *Bereavement narrows license:* Durley's "sense of humor had suffered in proportion" to his brothers' deaths (E26a-82).
- *Audience:* the narrator tells civilians the sanitized version (E26a-24, E26a-72) and says the "jolly fine life" line because "I wasn't going to wreck my leave."
- *Who is mocked by the men:* in the narrator's reports, the other ranks mutter about a visiting general (E26a-49), name an officer for his bulletproof waistcoat (E26a-40) and speak of the narrator in the third person within his hearing (E26a-16); the narrator does not show them joking about a dead man.

**Limits and failure modes.**
- *The register goes plain at the dead and wounded:* Allgood (E26a-8), O'Brien (E26a-17), Kendle (E26a-43, E26a-45), Fernby (E26a-85), the German youth (E26a-43), the lance-corporal's "Thank God Almighty for this" (E26a-17).
- *Humor that fails to hold:* the ward's quick judgment of a noisy patient (E26a-63), the mess argument that turns to cruelty (E26a-56), the narrator's own joke deflected by Durley (E26a-82).
- *Humor and fear coexist but do not cancel:* the night fear (E26a-10), the numb funkiness (E26a-57), the weeping man in the guard room (E26a-89).
- *Humor that normalizes killing:* the bayonet lecturer (E26a-5).
- *Laughter that is performance:* the officers on the notice board who "laughed harshly" while "simulated unconcern" (E26a-90).
- *Loss of recall:* "I cannot hear it in my head" (E26a-41), "I cannot turn my field-glasses on to the past" (E26a-35).

**Time course.**
- *In the war:* the comic register in rest and pre-action periods, plain after action.
- *After the war (1930):* the narrator cannot recover the jokes (E26a-41); his callbacks to lecture phrases (E26a-70) and his own earlier pieties (E26a-72) become jokes at his own expense.
- *Over the book's span:* the early stance is loyal and fatalist (E26a-1), the first doubt is suppressed ("dismissed before it had been formulated," E26a-76), and then admitted privately ("Did he know what he was doing," E26a-87); the rest of the arc is in the other half.

**Civilian-facing register.** The memoir shows the officer on leave telling the sanitized, enthusiastic version (E26a-24, E26a-72), civilians who avoid the subject (E26a-67), the home-front's flag-and-despatch war (E26a-68), the press (E26a-60), the home-front's jingoism and its kindness (E26a-71), and the leave-train platform (E26a-25). The narrator himself gives a funny, self-flattering version to Protheroe and a plain one to the reader.

**Defector, exile and dissent (limited).** Not in my range as such. What the range establishes is the **front end of the disillusion arc**: the narrator's loyalty and desire for a heroic experience (E26a-10, E26a-81), his unsayable wish to "wangle" home (E26a-62), his private doubts about the senior command and the crusade language (E26a-87), and the Quartermaster's grievance about officers who "come and go" (E26a-74). The narrator's humor about the institution is mostly bounded by loyalty to the battalion in this half.

**Cross-cultural.** One army (British), one era (1916-17), one class (junior officer). The narrator observes Australians, Welsh, Irish, Canadians and Germans, but each only through a line or two. The French generals appear once, in a joke (E26a-3).

**Craft observations.** (From the text, not from scholars.) The narrator states his own method: "it is my own story that I am trying to tell" (E26a-6), warns that memory removes the laughter (E26a-41), and calls his own puns "feebly funny" (E26a-66, E26a-87); he juxtaposes without comment (E26a-19, E26a-68, E26a-73); he marks the moment the register changes with a single plain sentence (E26a-85, E26a-17).

**Contrast with the verse (E11b, *Counter-Attack*).** *(Based on E11b's own roll-up and findings E11b-45 to E11b-60, not on a fresh reading of the poems.)* Both sources aim satire at the staff, the base, the home front, the press and the pulpit, and both name the dead only when the register has turned plain. Four differences are documented by the two extractions together. (1) The verse has almost no self-satire; the memoir's chief target is the narrator himself. (2) The verse names no comrades; the memoir names many (fictional) comrades and records their ends, usually in a flat clause. (3) The verse names jokes as a thing that needs company ("solitude annuls / Our earthly jokes," E11b-59) and calls the soldiers' songs "haggard mirth" (E11b-60); the memoir gives the same thought as a retrospective loss of recall ("I cannot hear it in my head," E26a-41) and as the narrator's guess about the Quartermaster: "I don't suppose he did much laughing once he was alone with his homeward rattling limbers." (4) The verse's other-ranks speech is overheard or remembered snatches inside the poems (E11b); the memoir gives a handful of lines to servants and sergeants and keeps the same mediated distance. The verse's recurring wound idiom ("Blighty wound so neat") has its prose counterpart in E26a-15, E26a-21 and E26a-89.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Genre.** An autobiographical novel with a fictional narrator; names of people and the regiment are changed; some characters are widely read as portraits of real people (I note one such outside identification, E26a-54, flagged as not in the text). Nothing here should be cited as documentary testimony without that caveat.
- **Mediated other-ranks humor.** The only other-ranks voices are one-sentence lines (E26a-2, E26a-16, E26a-17, E26a-32, E26a-33, E26a-37, E26a-49, E26a-64, E26a-89) and a Quartermaster's letters; no extended soldier monologue in my range. What the men "said" about officers or Staff is the narrator's report. The narrator's own view that the men's humor is something he "could never understand" (E26a-22) limits what the text can say about it.
- **Officer's class lens.** The narrator's relation to other ranks is sympathetic and distant (E26a-22, E26a-31, E26a-58); his own humor is literate, self-mocking and drawn from hunting, school and literature. This is one officer's register and is not a model of mess humor in general.
- **Retrospection and memory.** Written in 1930; the narrator says memory fails and quotes diaries and letters that I cannot verify. The comic reversals (Cromlech's false death, the communiqué, "Comic Cuts") are the text's own devices and may be shaped for effect.
- **Unexplained items.** The stretcher-bearers' written note ("I saved your life under heavy fire," E26a-84) is not explained in the text; "Comic Cuts" and the "number nine pill" are glossed by me from general knowledge, flagged as such; "P.U.O." is not expanded in the text. The "Bodies" in L215 (E26a-29) may be a transcription slip for another word and should be checked against a print or scan.
- **Period ethnic and national banter.** The Irish, Australian and Welsh remarks, the racial minstrel simile and the nursery-rhyme nickname (E26a-15, E26a-20, E26a-55, E26a-61, E26a-80) are recorded as period usage, not endorsed. The racial-slur items are not reproduced and are listed in the register.
- **Text format.** The Standard Ebooks plain text carries invisible characters around its em-dashes and ellipses; the dashes standing in for oaths are the author's (L129, L338). No page markers; citations are by chapter, section and L-number.
- **Range boundary.** My range ends at L570, in the middle of the Strangford sketch (ch. VII §I); E26b begins at L571. The narrator's later arc (hospital in England, "Independent Action") is not in this pass.
- **Not checked in a browser or against a print edition.** Nothing in this unit depends on a page number.
