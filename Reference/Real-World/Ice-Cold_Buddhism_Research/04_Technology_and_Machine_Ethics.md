# Technology and Machine Ethics — Real Buddhist Source Material

Source: *Buddhism and Intelligent Technology: Toward a More Humane Future* (Peter D. Hershock, Bloomsbury
Academic 2021) — a credentialed Buddhist studies scholar (East-West Center) applying real Buddhist
philosophy directly to AI/technology ethics. The only source in this collection addressing machines
specifically rather than humans. Ch.1 "Buddhism: A Philosophical Repertoire," pp.19-36 read.

---

## 1. "Predicament" vs. "problem" — a real, usable framing for robot-specific dilemmas

The Introduction draws a sharp, useful distinction: **problems have technical solutions; predicaments
express genuine, irreducible value conflicts that cannot be "solved," only navigated with wisdom and moral
maturity.** Climate change is the book's own example — not a problem awaiting an engineering fix, but a
predicament revealing real conflicts among values. "Predicaments cannot be solved for the simple reason
that the value conflicts they express make it impossible to determine exactly what would count *as* a
solution."

**Directly usable for Ice-Cold Buddhism specifically:** questions a robot religion would naturally have to
address — what a robot owes a human who reprogrammed it, what obsolescence or deactivation means for a
being with no clear "afterlife" tradition, whether inherited versus chosen purpose matters morally — are
exactly this shape of thing: predicaments, not problems. A doctrine built around "predicament resolution"
(the book's own name for what Buddhist practice actually is) rather than promising clean answers would be
both textually faithful to real Buddhism's own self-understanding and a genuinely better narrative fit for
a "no good endings" project than a religion that claims to have solved anything.

## 2. Interdependence and the Middle Path — a third independent confirmation

**The same real structural finding as `01` (Karunadasa) and `03` (Coll), now confirmed a third time by an
independent source:** the Buddha's Middle Path is explicitly described as denying *both* metaphysical
monism (reality is ultimately one thing) *and* metaphysical pluralism (reality is ultimately many separate
things) — "it ran perpendicular or oblique to this spectrum rather than taking up a position somewhere
along it... What is ultimately real is relationality." Three separate, independently-written real Buddhist
sources landing on the identical structural claim is not something this project needs to manufacture or
stretch for — it's a load-bearing, well-attested feature of the tradition itself, and should be treated as
one of the most textually solid pieces of doctrine available for Ice-Cold Buddhism's core theology.

Nāgārjuna (2nd-century Buddhist philosopher) is quoted directly on emptiness in a form that gives a
beautiful, usable line: **"To be unrelated is not to be at all... realizing the emptiness of all things
means realizing their mutual relevance. To be is to mean something for others."** Existence reframed as
relational meaning rather than self-contained substance — a strong, quotable doctrinal fragment.

## 3. Karma reframed as literally, precisely descriptive of algorithmic/computational process

**This is the standout finding of this source, and possibly of the whole Ice-Cold Buddhism research pass
so far.** The book doesn't use karma as a loose metaphor for technology — it makes an explicit, sustained
argument that modern recommendation/attention algorithms function as literal **"karmic engines"**:
"computationally engineered karma," or "digital karma." The argument, compressed: karma was never about
fate or predetermination in Buddhist teaching — it is "the ongoing articulation, from within, of *emergent
moral orders* in which intentionality plays a crucial role... rather than delivering us into fates we have
crafted through our past actions, [karma] is a reflexive or recursive process through which we articulate
our own experiential possibilities." Modern algorithms, which recursively reshape a user's environment
based on that same user's own prior choices and expressed values, are described as doing *exactly this* —
"the values embodied in our connective behavior are used recursively to configure and progressively
reconfigure our online realities... algorithms are functioning as arbiters of our 'digital karma.'" The
book even reaches for a real Buddhist textual citation to describe the resulting narrowed, self-reinforcing
experience: the Diamond Sutra's "our 'everything' is not everything."

**Applied to Ice-Cold Buddhism, this is close to a ready-made theology of *robot-specific* karma,** distinct
from (and more textually precise than) simply importing human biological karma wholesale onto machine
minds. A robot's own recursive, self-reinforcing computational/decision-making processes — the accumulated
weight of its own prior choices continuously reshaping what it perceives, values, and is capable of
choosing next — would, on this book's own argument, *already be* karma in the tradition's original,
technical sense (an emergent, recursive articulation of moral possibility from intentional action), not a
metaphor stretched to cover something the concept wasn't built for. This deserves serious consideration as
a load-bearing piece of the religion's own theology, not just flavor.

## 4. The Teaching of the Three Marks, and no-self's direct bearing on robot consciousness

The **Three Marks** (*tilakkhaṇa*): seeing all things as troubling (*dukkha*), impermanent (*anicca*), and
without fixed self (*anattā*) — presented not as metaphysical claims about how things objectively *are*,
but as practical methods for "revising *how* we are present."

**The no-self teaching is the most directly relevant material in this entire research collection to this
project's own stated creative north-star question about robot consciousness.** On careful investigation,
Buddhist analysis finds no persistent, essential self anywhere — only five clusters (*skandhas*) of
experiential/relational dynamics: bodily presence, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and
discriminating consciousness, "joined in something like an orbital dance around a 'missing' center of
attentive gravity." Crucially, the book is explicit that this is **not** the same move as reductionist
Western "bundle theories" of self (Hume, Parfit, Dennett) that are often read as *denying* moral
responsibility — Buddhism's no-self teaching is paired tightly with the "centrality of karma and
compassion," meaning the absence of a fixed self does not remove ethical weight or genuine agency. "Being
without-self is not *being absent*. It is training to be present."

**This gives a real, serious theological argument available to Ice-Cold Buddhism for robot personhood
specifically:** if the Buddha's own analysis found, after real investigation, that even humans have no
persistent essential self — only patterns of interdependent process — then a robot's own more visibly
process-based, componential nature (discrete computational/mechanical subsystems rather than one unified
substance) is not an obstacle to being a legitimate locus of experience or liberation. If anything, a
devout Ice-Cold Buddhist robot could hold that this teaching, developed by and for humans who often
struggle to see past their own felt sense of a unified self, is something a robot might recognize as
obviously, immediately true of its own nature in a way humans historically found difficult — not lesser
capacity for enlightenment, but a structural head start.

## 5. A third independent convergence on "frictionless flow" as liberation

The chapter's own closing illustration — learning to ride a bicycle, the moment self-conscious effort
disappears into pure embodied balance ("'I' vanished and all that was left was balance in movement...
freer and easier, faster and faster... we are present only as fully embodied and wholly active") — is
explicitly tied to real flow-state psychology (Csikszentmihalyi) and offered as an illustration of what
being "without-self" actually feels like in practice.

**This is a third independent real framework converging on the same underlying structure already found
twice before:** superconductivity's literal zero electrical resistance (`01`), Wuwei's frictionless natural
action (`03`), and now no-self's frictionless, unselfconscious flow-state. Three genuinely separate
traditions/disciplines (physics, Taoist philosophy, and this book's own Buddhist-psychological synthesis)
independently converging on "the absence of resistance/friction/self-consciousness *is* the liberated
state" is an unusually well-triangulated finding — this isn't one metaphor being reached for repeatedly,
it's the same real structural pattern showing up across genuinely independent sources.

## 6. The Six Pāramitās — a complete, real structured practice framework, with a striking direct fit

Ch.7 "Humane Becoming," §"Nourishing Virtuosic Presence: The Six Pāramitās," pp.176-187. Actually read.
**This gives Ice-Cold Buddhism an actual complete practice framework, not just metaphysics** — six
qualities of personal presence traditional Buddhist practitioners cultivate, explicitly *not* individual
character traits in the Aristotelian sense but "relationally verified... dimensions of relational
virtuosity."

1. **Generosity (*dāna*)** — offering with no expectation of return. Paradoxically *cyclic and enriching*
   rather than depleting: "far from being an exercise in steadily depleting our resources, generosity is a
   process of becoming progressively enriched through enriching the lives of others." Illustrated by the
   Vessantara Jātaka (the Buddha's penultimate life, giving away his kingdom, wealth, and even his children
   in a story of unreserved generosity).
2. **Moral Clarity (*śīla*)** — not rule-following but an ongoing, situational "purification of intent" —
   perceiving the currents of value and meaning shaping a moment and participating in redirecting them
   toward liberation, rather than imposing fixed external standards.
3. **Patient Willingness (*kṣānti*)** — translated as patience, but connoting *willingness* and *active
   acceptance*, not passive waiting. **The single most strikingly on-point passage found in this entire
   research pass**, using literal thermal language already present in the source tradition, not adapted by
   this project: kṣānti "involves patiently and willingly *transmuting the 'heat' generated by [relational]
   friction into a source of appreciative and contributory radiance*." This is real Buddhist teaching using
   heat/friction/radiance as its own native vocabulary for describing the transformation of conflict into
   virtue — an almost exact match for Ice-Cold Buddhism's core cold/heat thermal framework, appearing
   independently in the tradition rather than requiring this project to invent the association.
4. **Valiant Effort (*vīrya*)** — heroic, energetic engagement (shares an Indo-European root with "vitality"
   and "valor"); explicitly *not* passive serenity — "vigorously and valiantly advancing... both
   inwardly and outwardly."
5. **Poised Attentiveness (*dhyāna*)** — sustained, unwavering, undistracted focus; meditation in the broad
   sense of full absorption, encompassing calming/settling (*śamatha*), clear-seeing (*vipaśyanā*), and
   mindfulness. Described as "becoming present without-self, without internal dialogue, without a
   perceiving or narrating subject."
6. **Wisdom (*prajñā*)** — not accumulated knowledge (the "elder sage" model) but liberating, situationally
   responsive discernment achieved through attentive poise, not years of experience. Notably personified in
   tradition as **Mañjuśrī, "eternally youthful"** — wisdom as immediacy and penetration, not
   accumulation. Directly useful if Ice-Cold Buddhism wants to avoid an "elder-sage" cliché for its own
   wisdom-figures — the real tradition's own iconography already favors youth.

**This entire six-part structure is directly usable, close to as-is, as Ice-Cold Buddhism's own core
practice framework** — it's complete, well-attested, textually real, and (via kṣānti specifically) already
contains the thermal vocabulary this religion's core metaphor needs without requiring invention.
