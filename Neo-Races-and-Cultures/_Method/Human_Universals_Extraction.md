# Human Universals — Extraction Notes

**Source:** Donald E. Brown, *Human Universals* (McGraw-Hill, 1991), scanned copy at
`Reference/Materials/books/Human Universals - Donald E Brown [1991].pdf` (228 pages, image-only PDF,
no text layer — extracted via page-by-page reading, not `pdftotext`).

**Status:** working extraction, in progress. Page citations below are the book's own printed page
numbers, not PDF page numbers (PDF page = printed page + 10, due to front matter).

**Purpose:** raw material for two downstream uses — (1) grounding human city cultures in this GDD in
genuinely cross-cultural, non-stereotyped universals (supports [[feedback_no_national_stereotypes]] by
giving a baseline of what's true of *all* human groups, so city-specific variation reads as genuine
variation rather than invented difference), and (2) providing a structured basis for a
Human-Experience-vs-Robot-Experience divergence framework — which universals would/wouldn't carry over
to a robot culture, and why. See `Human_Universals_Culture_Framework.md` (once written) for the
synthesis; this file is the raw extraction only.

This book (1991 edition) does **not** contain the famous bulleted "List of Human Universals" checklist
that circulates widely online — that list is Brown's later contribution, reprinted as an appendix to
Steven Pinker's *The Blank Slate* (2002), not part of this original book. Instead, this book's core
content is delivered as **Chapter 6, "The Universal People"** — a composite, present-tense description
of a fictional culture ("the UP") that has every universal trait folded into a continuous narrative.
That narrative is paraphrased/reorganized by topic below, since the prose form doesn't lend itself to
direct quotation-as-reference.

## Chapter 6: The Universal People (pp. 130–141)

Brown's method: the UP is not any one real culture, but a composite standing in for what's true of
*every* human society. Compiled primarily from Murdock (1945), Tiger and Fox (1971), and Hockett (1973),
plus other cited sources; a few items added from Brown's own judgment where he felt existing references
were incomplete. Brown is explicit that this list is **provisional** — some items may not hold up as
truly universal with further research, and the ordering/clustering is somewhat arbitrary rather than
hierarchical (p. 140–141). He also flags that "surface" universals (this list) are distinguished from
possible "deeper" universals at the level of underlying process/mechanism — this book pursues the
former, not the latter (p. 141).

### Language
- All UP languages have phonemes (a closed set of contrastive basic sounds), with phoneme inventories
  never falling below ~10 or rising above ~70.
- All UP languages have grammar: nouns, verbs, and the possessive (distinguishing "intimate/inalienable"
  possession — body parts, kin — from "loose/alienable" possession — objects).
- Language undergoes change over time; UP do not speak the language of their remote ancestors.
- All UP languages redundantly encode some information (e.g., both subject and verb indicate number).
- All UP languages have figurative speech: metaphor (prominent) and metonymy (e.g., "crown" for king).
- All UP can speak onomatopoeically and have poetry, with poetic lines ~3 seconds long, marked by
  repetition of structural/semantic/auditory elements with free variation.
- Translation between languages is always imperfect but always basically possible — even words for very
  specific things ("chin") can be circumlocuted ("end of the jaw") and understood.
- All UP languages have semantic categories for: body parts, inner states (emotion/sensation/thought),
  behavioral propensities, flora, fauna, weather, tools, space/direction, motion, speed, dimension,
  giving/lending, and affecting things or people.
- Words are frequently polysemous (more than one meaning); more-used words are shorter, less-used words
  longer.
- All UP languages have proper names and pronouns; pronouns include at least 3 persons and 2 number
  categories; numeral systems exist even if limited (as few as "one, two, many").
- Universal logical notions across all UP languages: "not," "and," "same," "equivalent," "opposite,"
  general vs. particular, part vs. whole.
- All UP overestimate the objectivity of their own thought, especially when judging out-groups.
- All UP use "conjectural" reasoning — inferring unseen states (an animal's presence, a disease) from
  indirect evidence.
- Nonverbal communication (gesture, especially hand/arm) exists alongside language; some of it is sent
  unconsciously even when received consciously (e.g., a child's cry of fear).
- Facial expressions for happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust, and contempt are recognized
  across all UP groups; some (masking, mimicking) are voluntarily modifiable, some are involuntary.

### Cognition and Personhood
- All UP distinguish self from others, see the self as both subject and object, and see the self as
  neither wholly passive nor wholly autonomous — as *partially* responsible for one's own actions.
- All UP distinguish controlled from uncontrolled action, understand intention, know that others have a
  private inner life (memories, plans, feelings, choices), and distinguish normal from abnormal mental
  states.
- All UP have some form of naive "personality theory" — they can explain an individual's departures from
  the behavior expected of their social status in terms of that individual's character.
- All UP intuitively model other minds ("theory of mind" in modern terms) — imagining what others think
  and feel, without being explicitly taught to.
- All UP kin terminology distinguishes male from female and marks some generational distinctions.
- Time is at minimum implicitly durational; all UP have units of time (day, month, season, year) and can
  distinguish past/present/future; UP life has cyclicity/rhythm.
- Binary discrimination is a prominent feature of UP taxonomy/cognition (black/white, male/female,
  good/bad), but UP thought can also handle ordered continua (gradations between poles) as well as strict
  binaries.

### Emotion
- All UP are moved by sexual attraction and experience sexual jealousy.
- All UP have childhood fears, including a near-universal fear of loud noises, and fear of strangers
  peaking near the end of the first year of life (paired with strong caretaker attachment at the same
  age).
- All UP react with fear to snakes, though this fear is to some degree effortfully surmountable.
- Boys, given the presence of a man laying some claim on the mother, show an Oedipal dynamic —
  possessiveness toward the mother, coolness toward her male consort. Brown frames this narrowly (not as
  a universal complex in the Freudian sense generally, see Ch. 1's discussion of the Oedipus complex as
  one of the "six cases" that needed rethinking rather than outright rejecting).
- Envy is ubiquitous, and every UP culture has some symbolic means of coping with its consequences.
- All UP can express and recognize affection.

### Tool Use and Material Culture
- All UP are "quintessential" toolmakers — not merely tool users, but tool*makers* who make many
  different kinds of tools and are highly dependent on them (distinguishing humans from other
  tool-using species).
- UP tools include cutters (improving on what teeth/hands can do), pounders (improving on fists/feet),
  containers, and binding materials (string, cord, sinew, vine, wire); UP know and use the lever; some
  tools are weapons, including the spear.
- Tools are made with enough permanence to be reused, in patterned/somewhat-arbitrary styles specific
  enough that "we can often tell one people's tools from another's" — style persists beyond any one
  maker's lifetime.
- Most UP are right-handed (population-level trait, not universal-to-every-individual).
- UP may not know how to make fire from scratch, but always know how to use, sustain, and benefit from
  it — for cooking and for comfort/security.
- All UP have substances they use to alter mood/feeling: stimulants, narcotics, or intoxicants.
- All UP have some form of shelter from the elements.

### Reproduction, Family, Kinship
- All UP have distinct patterns for birth preparation, giving birth, postnatal care, and infant weaning
  (with a roughly standard weaning duration/pattern).
- UP are not solitary; they live at least part of their lives in groups, the family being one of the
  most important but not the only such group.
- UP groups are territorial in some sense — tied to locality or to a claimed territory, even nomadic
  groups that wander a defined range; they are materially, cognitively, and emotionally adapted to their
  home environment.
- A "sense of peoplehood" — being a distinct people, judging others by one's own terms — characterizes
  all UP groups.
- The core of a normal UP family is a mother and child(ren); the biological mother is *usually* the
  expected social mother (Brown notes this is usual, not absolute — footnote gives an example of an
  institutionalized exception, a woman taking the status of "man" and arranging for another woman to
  bear children to whom she is the social father).
- Marriage — a publicly recognized right of sexual access to a woman deemed eligible for childbearing —
  is institutionalized among all UP; the "person" holding this right is almost always male, but need not
  be a single individual (nor even necessarily always male, per the footnoted exception).
- Socialization of children is active, not left to unfold on its own: senior kin contribute substantially,
  children learn partly by watching/copying elders and partly by trial and error; toilet training is a
  universal part of this process.
- All UP distinguish one's own children/close kin from more distant relatives or non-kin, and favor close
  kin in various contexts.
- Sexual regulation sharply limits or eliminates mating among genetically close kin; mother-son mating in
  particular is unthinkable/taboo everywhere. Sex is a topic of great interest, though some UP groups have
  contexts in which they won't discuss it.

### Social Structure, Status, and Economy
- All UP societies have social differentiation by kinship, sex, and age at minimum, and statuses/roles
  beyond these categories too; statuses are mostly ascribed, but some are achieved, and some have rules
  of succession.
- All UP recognize social personhood distinct from the individuals who occupy it (i.e., abstract social
  entities that "act" — e.g., "the government" — as distinct from any one person).
- Prestige is unequally distributed in every UP society; economic inequality exists everywhere (Brown
  notes it's unclear whether this inequality is uniformly *approved of*, only that it's always present).
- Division of labor exists on the basis of sex and age at minimum; women in every UP society have more
  direct child-care responsibility than men; children are not expected to do the same work as adults.
- Men and women, adults and children, are seen by the UP as having different natures; men are, on
  average, seen as (and are) more physically aggressive and more likely to commit lethal violence; men
  dominate the public political sphere, with women/children correspondingly more submissive/acquiescent
  in that specific sphere (not necessarily elsewhere).
- Cooperative labor (joint undertaking of similar tasks), reciprocal exchange (of labor, goods, services,
  including negative reciprocity — revenge/retaliation), and non-reciprocal trade all exist in every UP
  society; food-sharing exists in at least some contexts everywhere.
- All UP plan for and attempt to predict the future in family/subsistence/social matters, using
  "triangular awareness" — the ability to reason not just about one's own relationship to others but
  about *others' relationships to each other* — a cognitive prerequisite for forming coalitions.

### Governance, Law, Conflict
- All UP have government in the sense of regulated public affairs and collectively binding decisions,
  including some framework of "corporate" (perpetually-membered) statuses.
- All UP have leaders (even if ephemeral/situational); generosity is admired/professed-to-be-admired in
  leaders specifically; no UP leader ever holds complete, unchecked power — every society is, de facto,
  some form of oligarchy (never complete autocracy, never complete democracy).
- All UP have law: rights/obligations attached to persons or statuses, binding within a perpetual social
  unit; law everywhere proscribes murder (unjustified killing) and rape, though both may be justified
  in specific in-group-sanctioned contexts (e.g., warfare, capital punishment); infractions are sanctioned
  by expulsion, incarceration, ostracism, or execution.
- Conflict is familiar to every UP society, and every society has customary (though imperfect) means of
  addressing it, including consultation and mediation in at least some cases; the proscriptions against
  violence never fully eliminate violence in practice.
- Conflict is structured everywhere around in-group/out-group distinctions; an "ethical dualism" applies
  different standards to insiders vs. outsiders (cooperation expected within the group, not necessarily
  extended to outsiders).

### Morality
- All UP distinguish right from wrong, recognize responsibility and intentionality (at least implicitly),
  recognize and employ promises, and treat reciprocity as a moral principle, not just an economic one.
- Empathy — the ability to understand another's inner state — is treated by Brown as a thread running
  through communication, morality, and personhood alike, appearing in multiple places in the UP
  description rather than being confined to one category.

### Etiquette, Ritual, Belief
- Etiquette and hospitality are UP ideals; all UP have customary greetings, customs for visiting kin or
  others, and standardized/preferred meal times/occasions.
- All UP maintain "normal" daily routines and are fundamentally diurnal (day-active).
- Standards of sexual modesty exist everywhere, even in societies that are customarily unclothed; public
  copulation and public defecation are avoided, or attempted to be avoided, everywhere.
- Food taboos exist everywhere (specific tabooed foods, tabooed utterances); some foods (sweets, in
  particular) are relished across all UP groups.
- Religious/supernatural belief exists in every UP society — belief in something beyond the visible and
  palpable, including some beliefs "demonstrably false" by outside standards; anthropomorphizing of
  non-human things/forces is universal.
- Magic is practiced everywhere, often oriented toward sustaining/increasing life and toward attracting
  the interest of the opposite sex; every UP society has beliefs about fortune/misfortune, and about the
  causes of disease and death; every UP group attempts to heal the sick (has some form of medicine) and
  attempts, in some fashion, to control the weather; divination is practiced everywhere.
- Rituals exist everywhere, including rites of passage that mark the transfer of an individual from one
  status to another, and mourning practices for the dead.
- Every UP group has a worldview — an understanding of the world and their place in it — partly
  structured by features of the mind itself (e.g., the ability, from early infancy, to recognize that an
  object known through one sense is the same object perceived through another sense — the world is
  experienced as unified, not as separate sense-specific worlds). Worldview is bound up with supernatural/
  mythological belief; folklore exists everywhere; dreaming and dream interpretation are universal.

### Property, Aesthetics, Play
- All UP are materialists in the sense of recognizing property — what belongs (even minimally) to an
  individual vs. a group vs. others — and have rules for inheriting property.
- All UP have aesthetic standards beyond pure utility: standards for personal adornment/hairstyling (even
  in minimally-clothed societies), standards of sexual attractiveness (including a near-universal
  preference for signs of youth/early nubility over signs of post-menopausal status), and standards of
  hygienic grooming distinct from purely aesthetic grooming.
- All UP know how to dance and have music; some dance/religious activity is music-accompanied; music
  everywhere includes melody, rhythm, repetition, redundancy, and purposeful variation, and is always
  understood as a created art form, not incidental noise; vocal music (including music that is a
  conjunction of music and poetry) exists everywhere; children's music exists everywhere.
- All UP, especially the young, play — play is understood as inherently enjoyable ("fun") but also
  functions as training for skills useful in adulthood.

### Brown's own caveats on this chapter (p. 140–141)
- This list draws heavily on Murdock (1945), Tiger and Fox (1971), and Hockett (1973), supplemented by
  Brown's own judgment in a few places (including some items included on the strength of his or a
  colleague's own field experience, where no citable reference existed yet).
- A few candidate universals were deliberately *omitted* despite appearing plausible in the literature —
  e.g., abortion and capital punishment were both considered and left out (or included only cautiously,
  capital punishment as a "near-universal") because Brown wasn't fully convinced the evidence supported
  a strict universal claim rather than a very-common-but-not-exceptionless one.
- Brown explicitly distinguishes "surface" (or substantive) universals — the kind catalogued in this
  chapter — from universals at a deeper, more mechanistic/process level, which this book does not
  attempt to fully catalogue. He also notes the list's internal ordering/clustering is somewhat arbitrary
  (he chose to start with culture and language, then proceeded roughly by convenience rather than strict
  logical hierarchy) and that a fuller account would show more interconnection between items than a flat
  list can represent (e.g., empathy recurring across the communication, morality, and personhood
  categories above is one such interconnection, not three unrelated facts).

## Chapter 7: Universals, Human Nature, and Anthropology (pp. 142–156)

Opens by framing the book's central thesis: universals exist, are numerous, and matter for anthropology,
but they're a *heterogeneous* set (cultural, social, linguistic, individual, unrestricted, implicational)
that resists a single overarching explanation. If there's one underlying source worth looking for, human
nature is the place — and, reflexively, universals can be used as evidence *for* what human nature is,
since human nature isn't otherwise directly observable.

### Brown's hierarchy of anthropological propositions
- **First-level (uncontroversial):** anthropology studies what humans are, what humans do, and how they
  got that way, comparatively across all peoples, times, places (and even species).
- **Second-level (also relatively uncontroversial):** humans are an evolved species with a distinct
  nature; everything humans do is a product of nature-in-interaction-with-environment; the human species
  shows only minor racial variation over its long evolutionary history; the human mind is fundamentally
  the same across all human populations (barring sex/age differences); humans live socially, with richly
  variable cultural traditions that are themselves products of human activity and thus part of each
  other's environment.
- **Middle-level (the load-bearing propositions Brown argues are mostly false or misleading — the crux of
  the chapter):**
  1. Nature and culture are two distinct phenomenal realms. *(False in its rigid form — culture must
     ultimately derive from nature; the interesting questions live in the interaction, not a strict
     binary.)*
  2. Nature manifests in fixed instincts, culture in learned behavior. *(False — many human behaviors,
     e.g., speaking, smiling, blend instinct and learning; genes can specifically shape *how* learning
     happens, not just whether it happens.)*
  3. Because human nature is the same everywhere, culture (not nature) must explain differences between
     populations. *(False — this is a non sequitur; nature having variable, input-sensitive responses to
     different environments is entirely compatible with nature also explaining differences, not just
     similarities.)*
  4. Human universals are likely to reflect human nature. *(Correct on its own — only misleading when
     paired with proposition 3.)*
  5. Except for its capacity to absorb culture, the human mind is largely a blank slate. *(False — flatly
     contradicted by the evidence in Ch. 4 and Ch. 6.)*
  6. Culture (because of 3 and 5) is the most important determinant of human affairs. *(Unsupported once
     3 and 5 fall — and anthropology has no actual method for quantifying "how much" culture explains
     relative to nature, so the claim of primacy was never empirically earned.)*
  7. Explaining human behavior in biological terms is a reductionist fallacy. *(False — reductionist
     explanation is routine and legitimate throughout science generally; there's no principled reason
     humans/the human mind should be exempt.)*
  8. Culture, being autonomous, has an arbitrary and highly variable character. *(A logical inference
     from 1/3/6/7, so it falls when they do — though Brown grants that *some* real potential for
     arbitrariness/variability is a genuine hallmark of the cultural; the error is treating that potential
     as boundless.)*
  9. Human universals are therefore few, and probably unimportant. *(Untenable given everything
     documented in Ch. 6 — this is presented as the practical stake of the whole book: proposition 9
     being taken for granted is what allowed universals to be neglected by the field for decades.)*
- Brown's own resolution is explicitly **interactionist**, not a swing to biological determinism: the
  fix isn't replacing "culture explains everything" with "biology explains everything," it's recognizing
  that particulars and universals, nature and nurture, are constantly in dialectical/feedback
  relationship, and that a mature anthropology needs a real (not hand-waved) method for telling where a
  given trait sits on that spectrum, case by case, rather than assuming the answer in advance.

### Closing argument (professional-responsibility framing, pp. 153–156)
- Brown closes by arguing that anthropology's near-monopoly on studying humanity *as a whole* has
  historically caused it to systematically overreport difference and underreport similarity — not out of
  malice, but because exoticism/anomaly is what gets published, funded, and taught, while broad
  cross-cultural regularity reads as unremarkable and goes uncited. He calls this bias toward the exotic
  a real professional distortion, not a neutral scientific outcome, and it's part of what let now-retracted
  or heavily-qualified findings (Margaret Mead's stress-free Samoan adolescence, sex-role reversal among
  the Chambri, Whorfian Hopi timelessness) become foundational anthropological teaching material for
  decades before being seriously challenged.
- Quotes Daly and Wilson's term **"biophobia"** for the social sciences' reflexive resistance to
  biological explanation.
- Final position: this is explicitly framed as **not a zero-sum contest** between universal/biological and
  particular/cultural explanation — the goal is a truer, more balanced account of humanity that
  recognizes both real similarity and real difference, because that balance is what actually matches the
  evidence, not because symmetry is aesthetically pleasing.

**Relevance note for this GDD:** Brown's own explicit rejection of "culture/nationality-of-origin as
sole causal explanation" in favor of an interactionist, environment-plus-nature account lines up cleanly
with this project's own binding [[feedback_no_national_stereotypes]] rule (nationality is never the
causal subject; a city's own conditions are). The parallel is worth keeping in the eventual synthesis —
Brown is independently arguing, from a completely different field and era, against exactly the kind of
essentialist "population X does Y because they're population X" reasoning this GDD's own compliance
framework was built to rule out.

## Chapter 4: Explaining Universals (pp. 88–117)

Brown surveys eleven distinct explanatory modes for why a given trait turns out to be universal, using
handedness, male dominance, kinship terminology, fire/cooking, and religious/aesthetic phenomena as
running examples. Not all modes are equally biological — but Brown's own conclusion (p. 117) is that
essentially **all non-formal explanations for particular universals turn out to be biological or
interactionist** (culture interacting with biological givens), with cultural-diffusion and "archosis"
(false-belief-that-spreads-for-no-adaptive-reason) as the only real non-biological alternatives, and even
those are minority cases. This chapter is the single most useful chapter in the book for a
Human-Experience-vs-Robot-Experience divergence framework, because nearly every explanation traces back
to a piece of human biology a robot in this setting's own established canon would not share.

### The eleven explanatory modes (summary)
1. **Explaining a universal with a universal** — e.g., explaining right-handed symbolic preference via
   universal dualistic thought. Methodologically weak on its own (every universal correlates with every
   other), but illustrative.
2. **Cultural reflection/recognition of physical fact** — culture doesn't invent the pattern, it notices
   and labels a real biological regularity (e.g., kinship terminologies universally track the real
   biological relationships involved in reproduction — see below).
3. **Logical extension from (usually biological) givens** — a universal follows more or less directly
   from the basic, unremarkable facts of being human: large-brained, slow-maturing, sexually
   reproducing, group-living, terrestrial, omnivorous, moderately sexually dimorphic, quarrelsome,
   without estrus.
4. **Diffusionist explanations resting on great age + great utility** — e.g., fire and cooking: ancient
   (fire-making ~40,000 years, opportunistic fire use back to 1,500,000 years), and useful enough
   (temperature control, food safety/digestibility, tool-shaping, protection) to have spread everywhere
   and never been abandoned once discovered.
5. **Archoses** — a bizarre subtype of (4): false beliefs that spread and persist across humanity despite
   *zero* or negative utility, simply because nothing corrected them before scientific method existed.
   Brown's detailed example: the "muelos" belief complex (La Barre) linking bone marrow, brain, spinal
   cord, and semen as "the same substance," underlying widespread headhunting-for-fertility practices.
   Presented as a genuinely rare case — most universals are *not* explicable this way.
6. **Conservation of energy** — e.g., linguistic "marking" patterns (the more frequently used word/form
   is shorter and less irregular) reflect a physiological tendency to conserve the energy speech costs;
   an interaction between human biology and cultural convention, not either alone.
7. **The nature of the human organism, emphasis on the brain** — specific, localized brain structures and
   functions (e.g., "cognitive imperative" — humans are driven to impose coherent explanatory order on
   unexplained stimuli, absent which supernatural first-causes get invented) directly produce or bias
   universal behaviors.
8. **Evolutionary theory** — the superordinate mode; see detailed subsection below.
9. **Interspecific comparison** — universal traits found in humans *and* our nearest relatives (e.g.,
   "triangular awareness" in chimpanzees, per de Waal) suggest deep phylogenetic roots rather than
   human-specific cultural invention.
10. **Ontogeny** — tracing how a universal trait reliably develops within each individual's lifetime
    (e.g., the well-documented "critical period" for first-language acquisition; the developmental
    timeline of infant facial recognition, tracked from birth) — compatible with both biological and
    cultural explanation, and often the only way to determine the actual mix of nature/culture at work in
    a given universal.
11. **Partial explanations** — some universals (religion, aesthetics) resist any single unifying
    explanation and are instead better understood as a disparate bundle of separately-adaptive or
    side-effect traits that happen to cluster together (e.g., religion's persistence explained partly by
    brain dysfunction/altered-states channeling, partly by innate snake-wariness feeding serpent
    iconography, partly by rhythmic/percussive effects on the brain, partly by the general "cognitive
    imperative" to explain the unknown — no one thread accounts for the whole phenomenon).

### Evolutionary theory in detail (the mode most load-bearing for robot-divergence framing)

- **Natural selection** operates on heritable variation (genotype) that produces differential reproductive
  success (fitness) via the resulting phenotype; it requires many generations and a relatively stable
  species-environment relationship to produce a true adaptation.
- Human nature is generally assumed (per the field, as of 1991) to have evolved chiefly during the long
  Paleolithic foraging period — modern/post-foraging environments are too recent and too unstable for
  substantial further evolution of universal traits to have occurred. **Humans now live in environments
  that differ, sometimes drastically, from the "ancestral" environment their universal traits were
  shaped by** — a discrepancy Brown treats as a research opportunity (novel environments make ancestral
  design stand out in bold relief) as much as a complication.
- Critical distinction: an adaptation's **function** (what it was selected for) is not the same as its
  **effects** (other things it incidentally causes/enables). Confusing effect for function is "a cardinal
  error" — e.g., human fingers were not adapted "for typing"; typing is an effect of a trait whose actual
  evolutionary function was something else entirely, and it takes rigorous comparative analysis to tell
  functions and effects apart (worked example given at length: whether female orgasm is itself an
  adaptation, i.e., function, or merely a nonfunctional side effect/"homologue" of male orgasm, i.e.,
  effect — Brown presents both sides as live debate, with practical stakes, e.g., for sex therapy).
- Adaptations can be **obligate** (always present, e.g., the thick skin on the soles of the feet, already
  present at birth) or **facultative** (conditionally triggered, e.g., a callus forming only where skin is
  repeatedly stressed) — facultative adaptations are complex to genetically program and function much
  like an "implicational universal" (present only given a triggering condition).
- **Sexual selection** is distinct from general natural selection: it reflects fitness specifically in
  obtaining mates, not fitness with the wider environment, and comes in two forms — intersexual (female
  choice of males) and intrasexual (male-male competition for access to females). It is the source of
  most within-species sex differences (as opposed to natural selection, which usually produces
  species-wide traits shared by both sexes).
- **Parental investment theory** (Trivers): the sex that invests more in offspring (in mammals, almost
  always the female — gestation, lactation) becomes the "limiting resource"; the sex investing less (in
  mammals, almost always the male) is freed to compete for/seek multiple mates. Greater disparity in
  parental investment between the sexes predicts greater sexual dimorphism and greater sex-difference in
  mating strategy generally. This single mechanism is presented as the root explanation for a whole
  cluster of separately-catalogued universals: male sexual jealousy being typically more violent (paternity
  confidence problem — a male can never be as certain a child is his as a female can), male preference for
  visual/youthful cues of fertility, women's reproductive potential being sharply capped by menopause
  (vs. men's much later and more gradual decline) explaining the near-universal preference for
  younger/postpubescent-but-youthful female partners, and the average husband being older than his wife.
- **Kin selection** (Hamilton) explains the evolution of altruism toward genetic relatives: a behavior that
  reduces one's own individual reproduction can still be selected for if it sufficiently increases the
  reproduction of kin who share copies of the same genes (formalized, though not consciously calculated,
  as inclusive fitness). Explicitly **not** a general-purpose "fitness calculator" running in every human
  mind — the criticism Brown surveys (Cosmides & Tooby et al.) is that minds evolve to solve *specific*
  ancestral problems, not to perform abstract fitness math; the evidence suggests kin-selected sentiment
  operates over a narrow, evolutionarily-tractable range of close kin (parent-infant bond, incest
  avoidance, sibling defense) rather than any general capacity to weigh "degree of relatedness"
  abstractly.
- **Reciprocal altruism** (Trivers) is the second major solution to the evolutionary puzzle of altruism:
  cooperative behavior that gets reciprocated has its cost effectively canceled over time, and individuals/
  coalitions that reciprocate reliably tend to outcompete non-reciprocators. Presented as a plausible root
  of reciprocity's status as a near-universal moral cornerstone (strong moral feeling attached to
  reciprocity, and close social monitoring of who reciprocates and who doesn't, are both treated as signs
  of genuine evolved psychology rather than pure cultural convention).
- **Group living** itself requires explanation (it has real costs, e.g., disease transmission) — cross-
  species comparison (Alexander) suggests predator protection, more efficient resource use, and highly
  localized resources are the three general factors that, singly or combined, make group living adaptive
  for a given species, including primates and humans.
- **"Clues" to phylogenetic (evolved) origin**, useful heuristically even without full causal proof:
  unusual ease/difficulty in learning a specific skill, a bounded critical period for its acquisition,
  emotionally-charged behavior that runs counter to a person's own consciously held ideals, intense
  fixation on specific topics, similar behavior recurring in other animals, and universality itself
  (especially *implicational* universality — where trait B reliably co-occurs with trait A).
- Evolution is also shaped by accident (random mutation supplies raw material selection can only pick
  among, never direct), phylogenetic constraint (a species' recent evolutionary history limits which
  directions are even available — e.g., humans plausibly rely on smell far less than fully engineered
  "ideal" sensory design might predict, purely because of primate ancestry), and trade-off/compromise
  (a trait beneficial in one respect can be a cost in another, so selection frequently settles for
  compromises rather than any theoretically "perfect" design).
- Distinguishes **"ultimate" explanations** (the evolutionary history that produced a genetic
  constitution) from **"proximate" explanations** (the actual anatomy/physiology/hormonal mechanisms that
  execute a trait day to day) — e.g., regardless of debate over the ultimate evolutionary cause of
  male-female psychological differences, the proximate cause is now uncontroversially understood to
  include differential hormone production between the sexes.

### Ontogeny highlights (developmental mechanisms behind specific universals)
- Facial recognition: newborns' focal distance (~8 inches) matches almost exactly the distance between
  a nursing infant's eyes and its mother's face — not necessarily coincidental; facial recognition
  develops through complex interaction between the infant's developing nervous system and caregiver
  interaction, and appears to have at least some innate component (primates raised without ever seeing a
  conspecific face still show some innate recognition of facial expressions).
  cross-sensory transfer of information develops very early (29-day-old infants can visually identify, by
  sight alone, a pacifier shape they have only ever felt, never seen) — proposed as part of the same
  mental mechanism that lets humans experience the world as a unified whole across different senses, and
  possibly part of the machinery underlying metaphor itself.
- Language: strong evidence (Lenneberg, Chomsky) for an innate "deep structure," acquired with a
  distinctive, age-bounded ease during a critical period, essentially like an organ developing via
  gene-environment interaction rather than "learned" in the way a fact is learned; deaf children not
  exposed to any sign-language model still spontaneously construct regular communication systems,
  reinforcing the innate-wiring interpretation over blank-slate/pure-learning accounts.
- The Oedipus complex (revisited from Ch. 1) is reframed ontogenetically by Spiro: not a fixed universal
  psychic structure, but a predictable outcome of a specific configuration of environmental conditions
  recurring at a specific critical developmental stage — remove the triggering configuration, and (per
  Spiro's account) the complex does not develop, which is presented as *consistent* with, not contrary
  to, its broad cross-cultural recurrence.

### Kinship terminology as "cultural reflection of physical fact" (detailed case, pp. 92–94)
Kinship terms are a clean worked example of explanatory mode #2. Brown's account: humans everywhere have
an innate ability/propensity to perceive the world largely as it actually is; brute, ubiquitous, and
important features of reality (like the objective facts of who-reproduced-with-whom) are especially
likely to get encoded directly into language and category systems. Every human society's kinship
terminology tracks the real biological relationships entailed in sexual reproduction (father, mother,
son, daughter, and combinations thereof) even though the terminology itself, and the social elaboration
built on top of it (rules of marriage, inheritance, obligation), is fully cultural. The universal is thus
neither purely biological (the *words* aren't in the genes) nor purely cultural (the underlying
relationships being labeled are not culturally invented) — it's a case of culture accurately tracking an
unavoidable biological substrate.

## Chapter 2: Conceptualizing, Defining, and Demonstrating Universals (pp. 39–53)

The methodological chapter — defines the vocabulary Brown uses everywhere else in the book. Several of
these distinctions are directly useful tools for the robot-culture-divergence synthesis, flagged below.

### Types of universals
- **Substantive vs. formal/process universals.** Substantive universals are the "surface" content-level
  traits catalogued in Ch. 6 (what anthropologists traditionally mean by "universal"). Formal/process
  universals (Chomsky's term in linguistics, Fox's in anthropology) sit at a deeper level — not the
  visible trait itself but the underlying generative mechanism that produces it, which may not be
  substantively universal on the surface even though the deep process is. Both are usually assumed to be
  rooted in human neurobiology.
- **Universal vs. near-universal, and the "statistical universal."** Ethnographic reporting is
  imperfect, and human behavior is complex enough that few traits are literally exceptionless. A trait
  present far more often than chance alone would predict, but not in literally every documented society,
  is a "statistical universal." Brown treats the universal/near-universal boundary as a matter of
  convenience along a continuum, not a meaningful hard line (he floats, without fully endorsing, a
  95%-distribution cutoff by analogy with statistical significance).
- **Implicational (conditional) universals.** Take the form "if A then B," where A is not itself a
  universal, society, or individual (e.g., "all societies with paved highways have centralized
  government"). These look relative/particular from one angle but are fully universal from another (every
  society that has A also has B, without exception) — Brown calls the apparent relativism here "a sort of
  optical illusion." Implicational-plus-statistical combinations ("if A then a *tendency* toward B") are,
  he argues, actually the single most common form of cross-cultural generalization in anthropology,
  though rarely recognized as a form of universal.
- **Universal evolutionary sequences.** A variant of the implicational universal: "A can only emerge
  after B" (e.g., unilineal stage-sequence models of cultural development), or "A and B emerge together as
  a result of some prior factor C."
- **The "universal pool."** Some universals take the form "if A, then some selection from a *finite,
  universally-fixed set* {B, C, D...N}" — e.g., all human languages draw their phonemes from a limited,
  universal pool of possible speech sounds; all human kinship terminologies draw from a universal pool of
  only about eight structuring semantic elements (Kroeber). **This is a directly useful concept for a
  robot-culture framework**: the question becomes whether a robot culture draws from the *same* universal
  pool available to humans, a *different* pool, or some overlapping subset — rather than a binary
  "shares the universal or doesn't."
- **Innate vs. "manifest" universals** (Tooby & Cosmides' distinction, applied by Brown). An innate
  universal capacity may not always be visibly expressed/"manifest" — Chomsky's example: metathesis is
  available in principle to any language's grammar without every language actually using it. Most
  explicit anthropological discussion of universals, Brown notes, is weighted toward the manifest kind,
  probably undercounting innate-but-not-always-expressed universals.
- **"Experiential" universals** (Yengoyan): universals rooted not in innate structure but in a shared
  experience every human has by virtue of being embodied and mortal in the same kind of world — his
  example: the universal experience of seeing that blood is red, feeding widespread (if not literally
  universal) symbolic equations of red with blood/life.
- **Emic vs. etic universals.** Etic = analyzed in cross-culturally valid, outside-observer scientific
  terms; emic = the way the people themselves conceptualize it, meaningful within their own worldview.
  Brown generally defines universals etically by default (an emic universal, additionally, is one that's
  a part of *every* people's own conceptual system — a stronger, rarer claim) but treats emic universals
  as worth noting when they occur. His own example: Ch. 4 is titled "Incest Avoidance," an etic label,
  rather than "The Incest Taboo," because incest avoidance is plausibly universal while explicit,
  verbalized incest *taboos* have real emic variation and aren't themselves universal.
- **Universals of essence vs. universals of accident** ("intrinsic"/"extrinsic" as possible synonyms).
  A universal of *essence* could not be eliminated from a given individual/population except by
  "unnatural intervention" — Brown's own examples are genetic engineering or concentration camps. A
  universal of *accident* is one we can realistically imagine being absent under different, still
  entirely natural conditions (his examples: the Oedipal dynamic, and the universal use of fire/cooking —
  both are near-universal not because they're logically inescapable but because the specific conditions
  that would eliminate them just happen never to have occurred across all known human populations). **This
  distinction is the single most directly transferable concept in the whole book for the robot-divergence
  framework**: a robot's entire existence, by this setting's own established lore, *is* the "unnatural
  intervention" case Brown uses to define the boundary of a universal of essence. Any universal that
  Brown would class as one of essence — rooted in evolved biology with no natural pathway to bypass it —
  is exactly the category most likely to *not* transfer to a robot culture at all (or to require a wholly
  invented functional analogue), while universals of accident (contingent on conditions, not on being
  a biologically-evolved organism per se) are much better candidates for still applying to robots, or
  applying in modified form.
- **"New" universals.** Traits that were not present in all societies known to ethnography historically,
  but that have since become effectively universal across all *currently extant* societies — Brown's
  examples: tobacco, metal tools, and (looking forward) items like plastic containers and
  machine-manufactured clothing, which he predicts will eventually qualify. **Also directly useful**: a
  robot culture, being a "new" population in Tepenia's own setting in exactly this structural sense
  (a population that didn't exist for most of the timespan the human universal-pool was assembled from),
  is a natural candidate for developing its own "new universals" that have no human precedent at all —
  not violations of the human list, just additions specific to a population category the original list
  was never trying to describe.
- **"Former" universals.** Traits that were once the common lot of *all* humanity but have since been
  eliminated in some (not all) populations — Brown's examples: high infant mortality, relentless
  childbearing and nursing for most women, transportation by foot only, and the near-total absence of
  impersonal (stranger) relationships. Useful reminder that "universal" is itself a claim indexed to a
  particular historical moment, not a metaphysical constant — which cuts against any temptation to treat
  the Ch. 6 list as eternally fixed rather than a snapshot of the conditions humans have mostly lived
  under.
- **Universals stated in the negative.** Some universals are best expressed as absences — e.g., "no
  society is a matriarchy," "no society's music consists exclusively of equal-length notes." Brown notes
  Chomsky in particular favors negatively-stated universals with the deeper implication that at some
  underlying level, the excluded case isn't just unobserved but *couldn't* occur.
- **Universals of classification vs. universals of content.** A universal of classification says a
  category exists everywhere (e.g., "religion," "gender") without specifying what fills it; a universal
  of content specifies the actual cross-cultural detail (e.g., specific universal facial expressions).
  Many universals sit somewhere between the two, and part of the ongoing work of the field (per Brown) is
  filling in more universals-of-content underneath categories that are so far only established as
  universals-of-classification.

### Methods of demonstrating universality (pp. 51–53)
No universal can ever be proven by exhaustive enumeration (there are always more unknown/poorly-recorded
societies than known ones) — every claim of universality is a hypothesis of varying evidential strength,
not a proof. Brown catalogues the main argument types anthropologists actually use:
1. Wide, systematic reading across the full ethnographic literature without finding a credible
   counterexample (Murdock's own method, using his own Human Relations Area Files database of ~400 coded
   societies — a >10% sample of all societies known to ethnography/history at the time).
2. A small number of very carefully controlled comparisons between maximally *different* societies —
   if a trait shows up even in societies chosen specifically for their dissimilarity, that's stronger
   evidence than a large but more homogeneous sample (Ekman's cross-cultural facial-expression studies
   among recently-contacted New Guinea highlanders are the book's running example).
2b. Related: single deeply-documented case studies can still be persuasive when the trait in question is
   *complex enough* that independent reinvention by chance is implausible (his example: a detailed
   African coyness display so specific in form that its independent parallel emergence elsewhere seems
   very unlikely to be coincidence).
3. Failed searches for counterexamples by researchers who were *specifically motivated* to find them —
   Brown's example: feminist anthropologists' diligent search for genuinely female-dominated societies,
   which came up empty and is treated as unusually strong negative evidence precisely because the
   searchers had every motivation to find a counterexample and didn't.
4. Providing a convincing causal/explanatory account for *why* a trait would be universal — understanding
   the generating conditions makes the universality of the result more credible on its own.
- Brown's closing caveat: even after every method above has been applied, universality is never strictly
  *proven* — only rendered highly probable, which is enough to treat the trait as a significant part of
  human nature or the human condition. He explicitly notes that many of the Ch. 6 items have not yet had
  all these methods applied to them, and invites further work.
