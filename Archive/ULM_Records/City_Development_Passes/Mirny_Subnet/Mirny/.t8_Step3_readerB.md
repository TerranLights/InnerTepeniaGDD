## 2 · The `[V]` physics, target by target

### T1 · Where drift settles — **QUALIFIED: the windward side fills first**

- **Solid barriers, and a building is one, deposit snow on the upwind side first.** *"For non-porous
  barriers, snow accumulates on the upwind side first. Deposition on the downwind side begins when the upwind
  drift reaches the top of the fence."* In the first stage a vortex keeps a cavity open right against the
  windward face; the lee then *"develops rapidly, filling in the circulating region behind the fence"* (Tabler
  2003, §3.8).
- **At equilibrium, both sides are buried:** *"Solid fences: The dimensions of the upwind and downwind drifts
  are similar. Both drifts have a length of 12-15H, and a maximum depth equal to H."* Porous fences behave
  differently: their lee drifts run to about 34H, deepest near 6H (UWyo summary of Tabler).
- **Past equilibrium the barrier stops catching snow:** *"Blizzards pass without losing snow"* (UWyo). For
  solid barriers the text says efficiency *"drops rapidly thereafter, which reflects the entrainment of snow
  particles in the accelerated flow over the top."*
- **Where the snow is not:** *"deposition occurs where surface shear stress decreases in a downstream
  direction, and erosion occurs where shear stress increases"* (Tabler §3.5). Accelerated flow scours: a tall
  fence's wake *"scoured out snow for nearly a kilometer downwind."*
- **Drifts harden fast:** bond strength *"doubles within 1 day and triples within 3 days"*, and wind-deposited
  snow *"can become quite resistant to subsequent erosion within only a few hours."*
- **At this site:** some buildings at the real site stand on 1.5 m piles *"to protect against snow drifts"*
  (ru.wikipedia). That's a real-world comparable of one response, not Mirny history.

> **For the spine:** *"Out of the wind is under the snow"* (§4) is **confirmed for the lee at equilibrium.**
> *"One building's shelter as another's drift"* (§9 shadow candidate) is **incomplete.** A solid building
> buries **its own windward approach first**, then its lee. The ground that stays clear is where the flow
> accelerates, so **clear ground marks fast wind.** The scale is set by barrier height, not by wind (12–15H).

### T2 · "Clear-sky whiteout" — **TERM CORRECTED; the hazard is confirmed, and there are two of them**

- **Whiteout proper needs an overcast sky.** AMS Glossary: whiteout *"occurs over an unbroken snow cover and
  beneath a uniformly overcast sky… Blowing snow may be an additional cause."* The Australian Antarctic Division
  gives the same definition: *"when the snow cover is unbroken and the sky is overcast."*
- **Loss of sight under a clear sky is a ground blizzard (blowing snow):** it *"can happen under clear
  skies"* and occurs *"in absence of falling snow"* (Wikipedia, *Ground blizzard*). WMO: blowing snow can
  *"obscure the sky and even the Sun"*; horizontal visibility at eye level (1.80 m) *"very poor."*
- **Drifting versus blowing:** drifting snow stays below about 2 m, and eye-level visibility is *"not
  noticeably diminished"* (WMO). Blowing snow reaches eye level and above.

> **For the spine:** the deficit *"No honest surface cues"* (§3 G2) **holds and is doubled.** Under a clear
> sky, wind-lifted snow blinds (ground blizzard). Under an overcast sky, flat light removes depth even in calm
> (whiteout proper). **Neither sky state is a safe cue.** The spec's phrase, as the spine quotes it, conflates
> the two (§6 C-2).

### T2b · Depth of the blowing-snow layer, and whether it can be seen coming

- **Depth:** CALIPSO lidar: layers from the detectable minimum (30 m) to about 1,000 m, **average about
  120 m**. Winter layers are deeper (100–500 m), summer shallower (search-returned summaries of the CALIPSO
  product description; primary page not fetched, see §8.10).
- **Near the ground:** most saltation happens within about 10 cm. At 100 km/h, *"50% of the total blowing snow
  is more than 1 m… above the surface, and 30% is above 2 m"*, and concentration above 5 m is *"negligible for
  purposes of drift control"* (Tabler §3.4). **A vantage above about 5 m sits above most of the moving mass in
  ordinary transport, but not in a deep event.**
- **When snow starts and stops moving:** fluffy snow moves at about 20 km/h (5.6 m/s). A wind- and sun-hardened
  surface *"can resist erosion at speeds in excess of 85 km/h"* (23.6 m/s). Snow *"typically ceases to blow"*
  below about 24 km/h (Tabler §3.4.4). Prairie dry-snow thresholds average 7.7 m/s (range 4–11) at 10 m, with a
  temperature dependence that reverses below about −25 °C (Li & Pomeroy 1997, via search summaries; primary
  blocked). **Mirny's mean wind (12.0 m/s, spine) sits above the average dry-snow threshold.**
- **Visibility against wind speed:** *"Visibility in blowing snow conditions is inversely proportional to the
  fifth power of wind speed"*: V = 1.1×10⁸ / U⁵ (m, m/s), *"for unlimited snow on the ground"* and without
  snowfall (Tabler §3.4.5). Computed from that law: **12 m/s → about 440 m · 15 m/s → about 145 m · 20 m/s → about
  34 m · 25 m/s → about 11 m.** Gustiness makes it worse: *"Over a period of 10 minutes or so, wind speed
  typically varies 30 to 50%… if wind speed averages 60 km/h… visibility could vary from 1100 m… to 16 m."*
- **The law is an upper bound on danger.** Its coefficient *"varies with snow availability."* One site data
  point (a storm article, physics only): at 25 m/s visibility was under 500 m, and at gusts of 33 m/s under
  200 m. That's far better than the unlimited-snow law predicts, **so the same wind gave very different
  visibility depending on the snow.**
- **Can it be seen coming? Partly.** Where a fast katabatic flow meets the slope break at the coast, it can
  form a hydraulic ("katabatic") jump: *"100-1000 m high, highly localized 'walls' of snow in the air in an
  otherwise cloud-free sky"* (CRYOWRF snow-wall abstract, via search summaries; primary fetch blocked).
  **The wall is a visible structure, but it forms at the slope break, which is where a coastal city is.** An
  upwind observer is in the fast flow, not in the wall. **From upwind, what can be read is the wind rising on
  the slope, not the wall itself.** Onset propagation speed was not found (§8.10).

> **For the spine:** the conflict *"each injury is read as the injured person's carelessness — the record had
> the right thresholds"* (§4) **meets a physical objection.** Visibility at a given wind depends on how much
> loose snow is available (the days since snowfall, given bonding that doubles in a day) and on gusts that swing
> visibility by two orders of magnitude within minutes. **A threshold written in wind speed alone cannot be
> right on both kinds of day.** It will be too cautious on hardened-snow days and not cautious enough the day
> after snowfall. The spine records that 60.6% of precipitation falls May–Sep, in the katabatic season.
> Handed forward to the shadow check (§7), not ruled on.

### T3 · Winter-solstice day length at 66°33′S — **CONFIRMED and QUANTIFIED**

**Method.** The generalized hour-angle equation, cos H₀ = (sin h₀ − sin φ sin δ)/(cos φ cos δ), is sourced

declination comes from a low-precision solar position. **Calibrated against eight published gaisma values for
Mirnyy (−66.55, +93.02); all eight reproduced to within one minute** (13:16, 13:23, 14:07, 15:00, 17:05, 21:41,
24:00, 10:54). Timeanddate refused both fetches (HTTP 403). No published solstice table was found (§8.8).

| Quantity (2026–27, φ = 66.55°S) | Value |
|---|---|
| **Solstice day length (21 Jun)** | **2 h 11 min** (standard refraction) · 17 min (geometric sun center only) · 2 h 37 min (strong refraction, h₀ = −1.2°) |
| June mean day length | **2.58 h** (spine: *"June still averages 2.4 h"*; see §6 C-4) |
| Sun continuously above horizon | **8 Dec → 5 Jan inclusive = 29 days** (standard refraction; **matches the spine**) · 16 days with no refraction · 35 days with strong refraction |

| No nautical darkness (−12°) | 24 Oct → 18 Feb |
| No astronomical darkness (−18°) | 7 Oct → 6 Mar |
| June bright sunshine (instrument, WMO ≥ 120 W/m² direct) | **1.7 h for the whole month** (Wikipedia climate box; WMO threshold per *Sunshine duration*) |

**The span depends on refraction.** Wikipedia gives about 12 June–1 July (about 20 days) for the Arctic Circle,
and the no-refraction case gives 16 days here. **The 29-day figure is a standard-atmosphere number. The real
count moves with the air.**

**Horizon geometry (derivation from sourced inputs; `[V]` for Step 4, not a finding):**
- **Midwinter noon sun:** due north, geometric center at about +0.01° and apparent upper limb at about
  +0.85°. **Haswell Island is 93 m high and about 2.5 km from the station site, nearly due north** (ASPA 127).
  Seen from a 35 m site it stands at **about 1.3°**, which would hide the solstice noon sun from ground where it
  lies due north. At 4 km (the minute-rounded coordinates) it would be about 0.83°, grazing. **Whether the
  midwinter sun is seen depends on where one stands relative to the islands.** The heights of the other
  Haswell islands were not found (§8.10).
- **Midnight sun:** due south at local midnight, over ice that rises inland. From an index-level figure
  (*"The ice surface south of the station elevates to a height of 1.5 km at a distance of 100 km"*, attributed
  to the AARI page, which could not be fetched), a linear rise gives a southern horizon of about 0.4–0.7°. The
  sun's upper limb at local midnight is about +0.8° at the solstice and about 0.0–0.1° at 8 Dec and 5 Jan.
  **So the midnight sun may be hidden behind the ice for much of the 29-day span, while the sky stays bright.**
  This rests on an unverified input and an assumed profile. It needs an elevation-model horizon (§8.10).

> **For the spine:** *"A sunrise every day"* (§3 G2 STRENGTH) is **astronomically true (2 h 11 min at the
> solstice) but thin on the ground.** June records 1.7 h of instrument-grade sunshine in the whole month, and

> the sun and understated for darkness: 80 days without civil darkness.** *"Day length is the one honest cue"*
> (§4) **holds for the calendar,** and is qualified at the scale of the street.

### T4 · Katabatic lulls by season and hour — **FOUND (general coastal statement), with a winter exception**

- **Summer and transition seasons have a daily cycle:** *"сток возникает или усиливается в вечерние часы,
  достигает максимума около 5-8 ч местного времени, ослабевает к 15-18 ч"* ("the drainage flow arises or
  strengthens in the evening hours, peaks around 5–8 h local time, weakens toward 15–18 h") (Repina,
  *Исследователь/Researcher*, 2025). That's a general coastal-Antarctic statement, not Mirny station data. Local
  solar time at 93°E is about UTC+6.2.
- **Winter has no daily lull:** *"В зимний период скорости стоковых ветров возрастают, и их ослабление или
  усиление зависит от изменения барического градиента при приближении циклона"* ("in winter katabatic speeds
  increase, and their weakening or strengthening depends on the pressure gradient as a cyclone approaches")
  (Repina).
- **Mechanism check:** solar heating disrupts the surface inversion, so summer katabatics weaken by day and are
  strongest in the early morning (English search summaries of the katabatic literature; AMS primary blocked).
- **Direction is steady, but the strongest wind broke the pattern:** Antarctic surface winds show directional
  constancy of about 0.9 over all but the gentlest slopes (search summary of Parish & Cassano 2003; primary
  blocked). At this site, storm winds come from the southeast quarter, maximum frequency in winter, 20–25
  hurricane-force days a year, **and the recorded maximum of 56 m/s came from the east, cyclonic.** The highest
  speeds come in the rear of cyclones *"when the vectors of katabatic and barometric winds coincide"*
  (search-engine summary of a forum page that could not be fetched). The 56 m/s magnitude is corroborated by
  ru.wikipedia (56–58 m/s), cited as physics only; the event itself is site history (withheld, §8.6).
- **Days above 15 m/s disagree across sources:** 200 (Repina), "more than 200" (en.wikipedia), 204 (forum
  summary), **220 (ru.wikipedia).**

> **For the spine:** the conflict *"people must stop (the lit month)"* versus *"the system can't"* (§4) gains
> an hour. **In summer the wind reliably eases in the afternoon (about 15–18 h), in the same season as the
> nightless weeks,** which gives outdoor work a daily window the light can't. **In winter the lull is not on
> the clock; it's on the barometer.** *"A hazard with a direction"* (§3 G2) holds for the katabatic regime,
> but **the strongest wind on record came from a different quarter.**

### T5 · The sea-ice window and wind closures — **CONFLICT WITH THE SPINE'S CITATION; window quantified**

Primary source: *ASPA No 127 Management Plan (Haswell Island)*, Measure 5 (2016), "Ice conditions":
- *"For the larger part of the year, the sea within the Area is covered with fast ice, whose width reaches
  30-40 km by the end of winter. **Fast ice breaks up between December 17 and March 9 (February 3, on average)
  and freezes between March 18 and May 5 (April 6, on average).** The probability that the ice-free period off
  Mirny Station will last more than 1 month is 85%, more than 2 months 45%, and more than 3 months 25%. The
  Area is always full of icebergs. In summer, when fast ice disappears, icebergs drift westward along the
  coast. Seawater temperature is always below zero. The tide has an irregular daily pattern."*
- **Fast ice is a traffic surface once it forms:** the plan places signs to prevent entry *"following the
  formation of fast ice which is safe for pedestrian and vehicle traffic."*
- Supporting, index-level only: first ice in early March, stable formation in the second half of March, about a
  month to establish fast ice at 20–40 cm thickness, and maximum width 30–40 km in Sep–Oct. Grounded icebergs
  limit maximum fast-ice extent, giving *"a broad maximum and an abrupt, relatively transient minimum"* (Fraser
  et al. 2012 abstract via search; primary blocked).
- **Wind inside the window:** the katabatic is offshore (from the S/SE toward the sea) and pushes pack ice
  away, opening polynyas (search summaries). Repina: *"В океан ветер далеко не уходит и затихает на расстоянии
  8-10 км от берега"* ("the wind does not go far into the ocean and dies out 8–10 km from shore"), a general
  claim. **Against 30–40 km of late-winter fast ice, the outer fast ice and its edge would lie beyond the
  katabatic's reach.** No Mirny-specific record of wind-suspended ship or boat work was found (query death,
  then thin sources; §8.8).

**Derived from the averages:** the open-water season averages about **62 days (about 3 Feb → about 6 Apr)**. The
breakup date spreads over **83 days** and the freeze date over **48 days.** In about 15% of years the ice-free
period is under a month.

> **For the spine:** *"No year-round sea — ice from March to October"* (§3 G2, citing `M` L109, L116, L133)
> **does not match the fast-ice record at this coast. The shore ice holds about April → February, and the
> open-water season is roughly February–April, not the warm months.** See §6 C-1. Real-world sources are not
> specifications, so the spec may diverge deliberately; **the decision is Step 4's.** Research also adds an
> **unlisted strength:** for about 10 months the sea is **a surface 30–40 km wide, safe for foot and vehicle
> traffic once formed.** And the window can't be scheduled: **the calendar is honest about light and not about
> the sea.**

### T6 · Whether storms affect relay equipment — **YES, conditionally; two mechanisms found, one open**

1. **Wind-driven triboelectric radio noise.** Wind over snow separates charge, and discharges emit impulsive
   radio frequencies (RF). Across experiments these backgrounds show *"a threshold wind velocity… typically
   O(10 m/s)"*, spectra *"shifted to the low-end… (100-200 MHz)"*, and *"for the strongest background signals,
   an apparent preference for discharges from above-surface structures"* (Aguilar et al. 2022,
   arXiv:2103.06079). Search summaries add fields up to 30 kV/m at 4 cm over eroding snow, and thresholds of
   6–8 m/s for some arrays. Amateur-radio sources describe "snow static" as worse in colder, drier snow
   (low-authority, snippet-level).
2. **Space weather (if the relay uses HF skywave).** The site is listed among **polar-cap stations at 77.2°
   corrected geomagnetic latitude** (LASP magnetometer table; epoch not given). Polar cap absorption (PCA)
   events black out high-latitude HF, with a mean of about 8 h for moderate events and about 1.6 days for
   severe ones, up to about a week (search summaries of the literature). **Geomagnetic coordinates drift over
   centuries, so this figure cannot be projected to 2564–2812. It's a HOLE, not an inference.**
3. **Rime icing and wind loading on masts:** not researched (TOOL-DEATH, §8.10).

> **For the spine (conditional on a radio relay in the VHF–UHF range on above-surface masts):** Mirny's
> *mean* wind (12 m/s) is above the triboelectric threshold, and it exceeds 15 m/s on 200–220 days a year.
> **The relay would carry its signal through wind-made noise most of the year, and that noise is worst in the
> storms that make the signal matter most.** That sharpens *"No relay redundancy"* (§6 #8): the single point's
> bad days and the city's movement-stopping days coincide.

### T7 · The ground between the coast and the quarries — **PARTIAL**

- **Inland rock south of the coastal strip: NOT FOUND.** Regional nunatak lithologies (gneiss, granite,
  charnockite, basic dikes) came back only in search summaries mixing distant localities, so they're unusable
  for "the quarries."

> **For the spine:** the quarry route (§4 *"The south sends the stone and the wind"*) crosses **moving ice
> whose dangers are inside it, not on it.** Derived, not measured: **a route marked on moving ice moves with
> the ice.** Velocity is unknown (§8.10). Where the quarries' rock is, in real-world terms, is **open.** The

### D4 check · "No felt turn of winter" — **CONFIRMED as a real phenomenon**

The **coreless winter** is documented for Antarctica: temperatures flat for 4–5 months (search summaries).
One plateau series quoted: *"the 2-m temperature drops by just 1.3 K between April and September."* At coastal
sites it's attributed to *"the mixing of air within the inversion air-masses flowing as katabatic winds and the
induction of warm oceanic air."* The spine's *"May–Sep means within 1.0 °C"* is consistent. **The deficit is
real physics: at this coast winter has no core; the turns of the year are marked by light and ice, not by
cold.**

### Deficit 5 (the SPINE) · No inherited judgment of the wind

**What it looks like where it exists (Gearheard et al. 2009, *Climatic Change*, Clyde River, Nunavut):**
- Judgment is **continuous and embedded in daily life.** Observations are made *"while traveling or hunting,
  while walking to the store, while enjoying a coffee at the window"*, and *"many elders and older Inuit
  immediately check the weather when they wake up each morning as they were trained to do as children."*
- **The snow is read as an instrument.** *Uqalurait* are *"tongue-like formations of snow, usually hardened by
  blowing snow… formed by the dominant wind… Because they are oriented to the dominant wind Inuit can use them
  as a navigational tool… especially useful in blizzard conditions or heavy snowfall when visibility is low."*
- **Trust in the wind's direction runs deep:** an elder explained that the prevailing wind *"is known to be so
  dependable, that the change in direction must be explained by a land shift, not a wind shift."*
- **It fails dangerously for the inexperienced.** When the wind shifts, uqalurait mislead: *"dangerous for
  younger or less experienced hunters who may be unaware of wind shifts."*

**The mirror case:** at Clyde River the living judgment and the station record showed *"limited agreement"*,
and the paper allows that they *"may not be of the same phenomena."* When local forecasts lost reliability in
the 1990s, forecasters said the **weather** changed, not their skill. Search summaries of related reports add
that people began *"relying on the weather forecasts on radio and TV"* (snippet-level).

> **What the contrast writes:** a culture **with** a teacher, facing a wind that stopped being persistent,
> drifted toward the broadcast signal. **Mirny is the inverse: its wind is highly persistent (constancy about
> 0.9; storms in the SE quarter) and it has no teacher.** **The wind here is the kind inherited judgment reads
> best. The environment is legible; the literacy is missing.** Mirny arrives by the opposite road at the same
> endpoint: trusting what is written and relayed.

### Deficit 7 · No living teacher (and *"the record… can't name what it left out"*)

**Fogbank (Los Alamos, *Nuclear Weapons Journal* 2009 no. 2, "Fogbank: Lost Knowledge Regained"; verbatim from
the extracted PDF):**
- Remanufacture was chosen *because* *"historical records of the production process were available."* But
  *"most personnel involved with the original production process were no longer available."*
- *"Some of the historical design records were vague and… some of the new equipment was equivalent, but not
  identical, to the old equipment."*
- **The decisive omission:** *"The historical Fogbank production process was unknowingly based on this
  essential chemical being present in the feed material. **As a result, only a maximum concentration was
  established** for the chemical and the resulting impurity."* Modern cleaning *"removes an essential chemical."*
- **The remedy was not a better recipe.** It was *"process controls designed to check both morphology of the
  material and the effectiveness of the downstream processes"*, checks on the **outcome.**
- The GAO wording (via search summary): knowledge lost because NNSA *"kept few records"* and *"almost all
  staff with expertise… had retired or left."*

**McArthur Forest Fire Danger Meter (CSIRO; museumoffire.net):** covered in §4 (Australian stock). **Key 3.1
contrast:** the device was calibrated on *"over 450 experimental fires… under a range of low to moderate weather
conditions,"* and its ceiling was anchored on a single worst day (1939). Reality exceeded it (Black Saturday,
2009, *"well over 200"*).

**Ise Jingu, Shikinen Sengu:** rebuilt every 20 years since 690 CE. **The official Mie page says the purpose is
uncertain** (*"no one knows for certain"*) and calls skill transmission only a benefit: rebuilding *"seems to be
the perfect opportunity to pass on such knowhow."* Tourism sites overstate it as the purpose (§8.9).

> **What the contrast writes:** every real record that stayed alive in this research had **a living layer
> writing back onto it.** Pomor lotsii were amended across generations, Genglubu carry "此条准" ("this entry is
> accurate") marks (§4), and Ise has periodic re-performance. **Mirny's record arrived without that layer.**
> Fogbank shows the failure shape: **an omission surfaces in a record not as a gap but as a limit set the wrong
> way**, a maximum where the practice needed a minimum. It also shows what replaced the lost teacher: **checks
> on outcomes, not fidelity to the recipe.** *(For Step 4 to test against Mirny; not a ruling.)*

## 4 · Borrowed Form — the three named stocks (the other eleven BLOCKED)

**Question (spine §9):** *which stock carried a way of passing on unwritten skill or reading weather —
researched, never recalled; divergence operator applied.* Every candidate below is a **documented, regional,
dated practice,** not a national character (No National Stereotypes). **Whether any 2564 exile carried it is
not established by anything in my admitted set.** These are candidates for the developer.

### Russian stock — three documented practices

1. **Pomor lotsii (pilot books).** *"Лоции передавались в семьях поморов по наследству и с годами дополнялись
   новыми наблюдениями."* ("Lotsii were passed down in Pomor families by inheritance and supplemented over the
   years with new observations.") They were small handwritten notebooks, one of *"24 листа, прошитых холщовой
   нитью"* (24 leaves sewn with canvas thread), holding about 400 place and water names and *"много емких, в
   два-три слова, описаний приметных мест, «глядений»"* (many terse, two-to-three-word descriptions of notable
   places and lookouts) (Artefact / culture.ru, Arkhangelsk museum exhibit). **A written record kept alive by
   inheritance and amendment.**
2. **Shelonik, a wind word that travels.** On Lake Ilmen (Novgorod) it names the SW wind *"дующий из устья реки
   Шелонь"* (from the mouth of the Shelon River). Pomors of the White Sea and Kola use it; on the Kolyma it names
   a SW wind that portends a sea storm; **on Baikal it names a S or SE wind** off the Khamar-Daban
   (dic.academic.ru, ruwiki). **The name moved with its speakers and was re-attached to whatever local wind
   filled the role. The word was portable; the reading was relearned.**
3. **"Sastrugi" is itself a borrowing from Russian:** *"Sastrugi (singular sastrug, from the Russian zastrug)"*
   (Tabler 2003, §3.4). **The founding stock's language already names the wind-carved snow ridge, the
   instrument §5 UI-C describes.** A word, not a skill.

### Chinese stock — the Genglubu (更路簿) of Hainan fishermen

- **Oral first, then written:** knowledge was *"在渔民中不断创造并口头流传，明代初年逐渐形成传抄本"*
  (continually created among fishermen and passed on orally, forming hand-copied books by the early Ming)
  (Zhou Weimin and Tang Lingling, *南海天书*, as described by China Daily 2016).
- **Contents** include *"观天知识、气象和水文知识"* (sky-watching, meteorological and hydrological knowledge),
  per Zhou and Tang. **Conflict:** Yan (2018), as fetched, shows no explicit weather content in the books
  themselves. Versions likely differ (§8.9).
- **Selective copying:** *"海南渔民并不是原封不动的抄写，而是根据自己的航海区域和线路需要有选择性的"*
  (fishermen did not copy verbatim but selectively, by their own waters and routes) (Yan 2018).
- ⭐ **Verification marks written onto the record:** *"彭正楷的《更路簿》在一些条目的上方有'此条准'的字样，
  意思是说：经过验证，此条是准确的"* (above some entries in Peng Zhengkai's book is written "此条准", meaning
  *verified, this entry is accurate*). Later users also *"不断地在后面增添一些条文"* (kept adding entries)
  (Yan 2018).
- **Terse by design:** *"用语非常精炼，精炼得字数少至不能再少"* (language as brief as it can possibly be), in
  Hainan dialect. ⚠ **The sources are heavily framed around South China Sea sovereignty claims. Only the
  transmission mechanism is taken; the politics is omitted.**

### Australian stock — two documented practices

1. **The McArthur Forest Fire Danger Meter:** field judgment of fire weather condensed into a **hand-held
   circular slide rule.** McArthur *"deliberately lit over 450 experimental fires between 1956 and 1961 under a
   range of low to moderate weather conditions"*. The Mk5 *"brought together the results of over 800
   experimental fires and wildfire observations into an easy-to-use system,"* with inputs of *"air temperature,
   relative humidity, wind speed and both the long and short-term drought effects"* (CSIRO). The top of the
   scale was anchored on **the worst day then known** (Black Friday, 1939 = 100), and the device was **scoped**
   to *"high eucalypt forest carrying a fine fuel load of 12.5 tonnes/hectare… over level to undulating
   topography."* On Black Saturday (2009) the index read *"well over 200"*; a *Catastrophic* category followed,
   and the whole system was replaced in 2022. The rating reached the public as **a roadside sign.**
2. **Surf lifesaving and the rip:** the dangerous water is the **calm-looking** water; *"almost 70 per cent of
   Australians visiting the coast cannot spot a rip"*; rips *"are difficult to spot without training and
   experience"*; about 30 of about 80 annual beach drownings; *"only four per cent of our beaches are
   patrolled"* (UNSW). Juniors (Nippers, ages 5–14) learn *"how to read the currents and the rips"*, and flags
   **place a watcher's judgment on the sand** (search summaries of SLSA and Wikipedia). **A practice built
   specifically around a surface that lies.**

## 5 · The Unrecognized Instrument (3.6)

*Run after Step 2 was finished (the spine is T8-cleared). Working reading as declared in §0.1.*

### UI-A · Quarry crews upwind + the relay = a warning line (spine candidate) — **HOLDS, with two stated limits**

- **What exists:** people on the upwind slope every working day (spine §4: *"The quarries lie upwind"*), and a
  signal path through Mirny (the relay).
- **What research says it could carry:** a persistent wind direction (constancy about 0.9; storms in the SE
  quarter), so upwind is a stable "where." Conditions can differ sharply over short distances (Norwegian
  convoy rationale). **A person on the land and a point station observe different winds** (*"may not be of the
  same phenomena"*, Gearheard), so an upwind human report is **not redundant** with any instrument at the
  coast.
- **Why it's unrecognized:** the crews are there for stone and the relay is there for the subnet. Each flow is
  counted as a cost *for others* (spine §5).
- **Limits:** (1) The **jump wall forms at the slope break**, which is where a coastal city is. Upwind crews
  would report **the wind rising**, a leading indicator, not a sighting of the wall. **Lead time is
  unquantified** (§8.10). (2) **In winter, changes follow approaching cyclones** (Repina), and the record
  maximum came **from the east** (snippet-level), which is not the quarries' quarter.

### UI-B · The harbor watch's live calls (spine candidate) — **HOLDS, and research widens it: ice calls, not only wind calls**

- **What exists:** a harbor watch (spine §3 G2 cost; `M` L143 as cited).
- **What research adds:** the sea window **can't be scheduled.** Breakup spreads over 83 days and freeze-up over
  48, and in about 15% of years the ice-free season is under a month (ASPA 127). **Fast ice becomes a traffic
  surface only after it forms, so someone must judge "safe for foot and vehicle"** every season. The katabatic
  may die 8–10 km out while blowing at the shore (general claim), so **the edge and the shore can disagree.**
- **Verdict:** the harbor watch is **the one role in Mirny that must make threshold calls on an unscheduled
  environment, on both ice and wind, every year.** It's the only place in the admitted set where judgment is
  **already practiced**, not inherited. Unrecognized because it's framed as port logistics.

### UI-C · The snow surface itself (research-surfaced, new) — **CANDIDATE**

- **What exists:** the drift the spine counts only as cost (*"Drift that regenerates"*; *"one building's
  shelter as another's drift"*).
- **What it already does:** it records the wind's direction. Sastrugi are *"tongue-shaped features 25 to 40 cm…
  tall that face into the wind… their orientation can provide an indication of wind direction for field
  observations"* (Tabler §3.4). Under a wind of about 0.9 constancy the record is consistent, and the Inuit
  precedent uses exactly this **"when visibility is low."**
- **Why it's unrecognized:** drift is booked as a standing cost, not as a record.
- **Limits:** a storm from another quarter (the cyclonic east) re-carves the surface, and then it **lies to the
  unpracticed** (Gearheard). **The instrument exists; its reader doesn't.** That agrees with the spine and does
  not solve it.

### UI-D · Wind readings as a visibility forecast (research-surfaced) — **CONDITIONAL**

- If Mirny records wind speed anywhere (not in my admitted set), Tabler's law means **it already holds a
  visibility forecast nobody decodes.**
- **Limit:** wind alone misleads. The same wind gives very different visibility depending on snow age (T2b), so
  the decoding needs a second input, **days since snowfall.**

**Considered and rejected as an Unrecognized Instrument:** day length. The spine already recognizes it (*"the
one honest cue"*). Research qualifies it (T3) but does not reveal it.
