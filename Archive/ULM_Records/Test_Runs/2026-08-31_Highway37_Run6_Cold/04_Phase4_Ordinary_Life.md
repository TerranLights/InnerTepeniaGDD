# Phase 4 — Ordinary Life

**Per `03` §0.1, a Corridor replaces Ordinary Life with the transit day** — there is no resident population's
ordinary day to write, only the day of whoever is actually moving along the road. **Two distinct populations
exist for this phase**, per Phase 2: the convoy crews who move cargo along the corridor (Band 0 — transit only,
no residency), and Mountain Pass Airport's own staff (Band 1 — genuinely resident, if only ever on rotation).
Both are written, kept separate, per the Band-1 inversion (`01` §2.3) applying to the second but not the first.

---

## A. The convoy crew's transit day (Band 0 substitute)

**Headline function, stated in one sentence: haulers move fabricated chambers from Mountain Pass Airport to
their destinations along Highway 37.** Writing away from it, per Phase 4's core move:

**Routines.** The overwhelming majority of a crossing is not cargo-handling — it is driving, monitoring vehicle
systems, and managing the crew's own physical condition against altitude and cold (Phase 1's continuous
standing cost, experienced here at the level of an individual person's actual day rather than as an abstract
capability-frame entry). **Rest happens in the vehicle itself for most of the crossing** — per the real-world
comparable researched in Phase 3, a convoy of this kind plausibly includes living-quarters sledges or cabin
space, not open-air camping, given the conditions.

**Mundane concerns.** Managing altitude symptoms is not a one-time acclimatization event but a **running,
low-grade concern for the length of the whole crossing** (per Phase 1's finding that the road never returns to
low altitude after its first quarter) — headaches, fatigue, appetite changes are simply part of the job, not an
emergency each time they occur. Equally mundane: vehicle maintenance in conditions that punish mechanical
failure severely, and rationing consumables across a crossing whose exact duration is not fixed in canon
(REQUESTED, see Phase 3 §E's sibling item).

**Personal struggles.** Extended separation from home-city social contact — for a Kunlun-origin robot crew
member, extended time away from an entirely-robot city's own social fabric; for a Vostok-origin crew member
(human or robot), separation from Vostok's own small, tightly specialized research community. **Neither
struggle is dramatic; both are simply what the job costs**, exactly the register Phase 4 asks for rather than
a crisis.

**Escapism / downtime — "Waypoint," a synthesized minigame, not a thin field left empty.** *(Added at the
developer's direct suggestion: this slot is exactly where a location's own established facts, run through the
generative techniques, should produce a real player-facing artifact rather than a placeholder note.)*

**Derivation, not invention from nothing — every piece traces to a fact already established earlier in this
pass:**
- **Materials: the corridor's own junction-marker tags**, per Phase 3 §D's Retroactive Mechanism finding — the
  physical, direction-independent markers this corridor's own junctions require in low-visibility conditions.
  **Off-duty, the same small, cheap, already-carried objects double as game pieces** — nothing new has to be
  invented or carried; the game exists because the equipment was already there for a different reason, which is
  the strongest kind of derivation this methodology can produce (`Cultural_Synthesis_Techniques.md`'s own
  Ambiguous Universal Object logic, applied to an object this pass already has rather than a new one).
- **Core mechanic: a route that cannot come back down.** Each of two players builds a personal line of tokens,
  played in strictly ascending value, from their own starting marker toward a shared center card. **A route
  whose accumulated value exceeds a fixed capacity threshold is "overloaded" and locked — it cannot be played
  on again for the rest of that hand.** This is not Caravan's ascend/descend-and-total mechanic transplanted
  wholesale; it is the single sharpest fact this whole pass has produced (Phase 1 §E: *this corridor climbs once
  and never comes back down*) turned directly into a bust condition, so the game's central tension — climb too
  eagerly and you're stuck, too cautiously and you never finish — **is the corridor's own physical signature,
  not a borrowed one.**
- **The bidirectional token, rare and prized:** per the same Phase 3 §D finding, a small minority of junction
  markers are the "bidirectional" type used at the Hwy 22 crossing. In the game, holding one lets a player
  reverse their own route's ascending order once per hand — a direct, legible echo of the one real junction on
  this corridor built to be read from either direction, without the game ever stating that connection outright
  (per the translation discipline — the source fact never appears in the game's own in-fiction vocabulary).
- **Win condition: three completed routes.** Not an arbitrary designer number — it is Concordia's own real
  highway count from Phase 5 §5a: **exactly three highways (37, 110, 183) terminate there.** A crew that has
  "reached Concordia" three separate ways has, in miniature, done what the real road network does at its own
  capital.
- **Token flavor, not rules, splits by origin.** Per Phase 2's composition asymmetry, a Kunlun-marked token set
  (precise, machine-uniform, reflecting an all-robot city's own manufacturing character) and a Vostok-marked set
  (visibly more improvised and hand-patched, reflecting a smaller, mixed, more resource-constrained research
  community) can be told apart on sight, without either deck playing differently — texture the crew's own
  founding-city split already supplies for free.

**Why this belongs in Ordinary Life rather than only in Phase 8 or Phase 10:** it is specifically what fills the
convoy crew's actual downtime, derived from what this pass already knows about them — **cross-referenced
forward to Phase 8 (Making → Play, sports, humor, leisure) for its fuller writeup, and to Phase 10 (Catalog →
Physical things) for the token set itself as a nameable, handleable object**, per the player-facing test's own
instruction to push a finding until it can be seen, handled, and done, not merely read about.

**Status: seed material, not a locked design.** The derivation above (materials, the bust mechanic, the
bidirectional-token rule, the win condition) is genuinely characteristic — every piece traces to a fact this
pass already established — but it has not been played, balanced, or run through an actual game-design pass.
**Treat it the way this whole file treats every finding: `Proposed:`, fertile, and a real starting point, not a
finished minigame spec.** A dedicated systems-design pass, if one is ever run against it, inherits this
derivation as its foundation rather than starting from nothing.

**Would this reasonably be someone's entire day, every day, forever?** No — per Phase 2, this is assigned,
rotation-clocked work, not a permanent existence. The transit day is bounded by design, which is itself
worth stating: **nobody's whole life is this crossing**, and the corridor's psychological weight is mitigated
precisely because everyone doing it knows it ends.

## B. Mountain Pass Airport's own day (Band 1, named-individuals-and-variance rule)

**Per `01` §2.3's Band-1 substitute rule: the correct answer is not a general outpost day but the actual
variance across its small crew.** This pass cannot name real individuals (Phase 10's role-placeholder rule),
but the composition asymmetry established in Phase 2 (an all-robot Kunlun-side delegation, a mixed Vostok-side
delegation) already supplies real variance to write from, rather than an averaged abstraction.

**Headline function: fabricating chambers.** Writing away from it: the crew's actual day includes equipment
maintenance during the outpost's dependence on Tower-grid overflow (Phase 1's G6 profile — a resource they
monitor but do not control), shared meals and off-duty time in an enclosed space that is the one warm interior
on the entire corridor (Phase 3 §D), and whatever a small, mixed-composition crew's daily coexistence actually
looks like.

**The composition asymmetry produces genuinely different daily needs, not merely a different roster.** Per
established project canon (`Robot_Biology_and_Culture/` — coolant maintenance, siligel, robot coffee as a
genuine social practice among robots), **the robot majority of the crew has maintenance routines the human
minority does not share**, and the human/mixed Vostok contingent has needs (food preparation, sleep cycles,
psychological support for prolonged isolation) the all-robot Kunlun contingent does not. **Neither need is
dominant or subordinate — they simply run in parallel**, which is itself the finding: a joint venture between
an all-robot city and a mixed city does not average into one uniform daily rhythm; it runs two overlapping ones
in the same small space.

**The Band-1 fragility rule, applied directly** (`01` §2.3: *"a Band 1 location should have at least one
practice that is visibly one person deep"*): **one Vostok-origin robot crew member is, at any given time, the
only person at the outpost who still brews real robot coffee from carried stock rather than the ration
synthesizer's standard output.** Nobody else has bothered to learn the process, because it is slower and the
synthesizer is adequate. **When that individual's rotation ends, the practice lapses entirely until, if ever,
someone else picks it up** — a small, genuinely one-person-deep custom, exactly the shape `01` §2.3 predicts
every Band 1 location should have somewhere.

**Would this reasonably be someone's entire existence, forever?** No, for the same rotation-clock reason as
§A — but here the answer matters more, because Mountain Pass Airport genuinely *is* where these people live
for the length of their posting, not merely somewhere they pass through. **The rotation clock is what keeps
this from being the sealed, claustrophobic outpost story it would otherwise become**: everyone here has a
return in view.

## D. Differentiation axis

**Named axis: "ordinary life here is always provisional."** Both populations this phase describes — the
transit crew and the outpost's own Band 1 staff — experience their day as bounded by a known return date,
never as an indefinite baseline the way a settled resident's ordinary life normally is. **This is what would
separate Highway 37's Ordinary Life entry from a settled city's**: not the specific content of the day (which
overlaps with many remote postings elsewhere), but the fact that *nobody here has an ordinary life without an
end already in view* — the rotation clock (Phase 2) is not incidental texture, it is the axis this whole phase
answers to.
