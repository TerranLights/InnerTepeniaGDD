# Phase 3 — Surface & Texture

**Asks:** What is this place made of, and what is it like to be inside it — to the eye, ear, nose, and skin?

---

## A. Formalize first

Admissible material already establishes: bedrock (Vesleskarvet) foundation; a two-tier vertical layout —
"lower city" (truckyards, warehousing) and "upper clifftops" (trucking/shipping depots, Arcanet nexus, business
district); rotating, freighter-tied crew schedule with no fixed day/night rhythm; the Shipyard Complex and the
physically demarcated, bounded-protocol Trade Yard. This phase formalizes and extends that, not invents from
nothing.

## B. The four sensory sub-fields, answered separately

| Sub-field | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Sound** | No single register — the port's round-the-clock, non-fixed-schedule operation (Phase 1, G2/G5) means there is no quiet hour and no rush hour either; machinery, freighter engines, and shift-change activity occur at genuinely unpredictable times relative to any solar day/night cycle, which itself is a texture finding distinct from an ordinary settlement's day/night sound rhythm — a place where "what time is it" is answered by shift and tide rather than by light. |
| **Smell** | Cold and machine-oil/diesel as a baseline (freighter engines, dockside equipment); imported cargo smell specifically tied to Sanay's own admissible import mix — "dried food items from South Africa" (Economy & Industry) — meaning the Trade Yard's bounded business zone plausibly carries a genuinely distinct olfactory signature (dried provisions, packing material) from the Shipyard Complex's own heavier industrial register. |
| **Feel** | Cold throughout, but NOT uniformly — per Phase 1's G2 finding, the port's position (inland-of-immediate-coast, partially sheltered) versus fully open dockside sections plausibly produces genuine micro-variation in wind exposure across the port's own footprint, distinct from a fully exposed coastal facility's uniform harshness. |
| **First impressions** | A place that is never actually idle — no visible "closed" state, no slack period a newcomer could catch it in. This is a direct texture expression of Phase 1's cost-dominant shape (§C): the port's identity as continuous maintenance/service is not merely an abstract capability-frame finding but something a visitor would physically perceive within minutes of arriving. |

## C. Public/institutional split

**⚠ Exposure flag, self-caught while drafting this section.** `SanayShipyard_ColdRun_Prep_2026-08-31.md`'s own
descriptive section (not its line-ranged reading sequence) states that the withheld material characterizes the
Trade Yard as *"a Guangzhou-Canton-System bounded-contact zone."* This session read that line as part of the
mandatory prep-document read before Phase 0. The finding below — that the Trade Yard reads as a formally
bounded, protocol-governed zone distinct from the wider Complex, reinforced by this pass's own fresh G7
research into Rotterdam/Durban/Guangzhou's real cargo-type zoning — is **independently grounded in admissible
material and genuine research**, but its resemblance to the withheld characterization cannot be claimed as
clean convergence, since the prep document's own summary put a version of this shape in front of this session
before Phase 1 was drafted. **Flagged here rather than left implicit; treat as corroboration-weight only, not
independent-derivation weight, at Gate 6.** Logged to the observations file as M-85.

**A genuine split exists, admissible and specific.** The Trade Yard is explicitly "physically demarcated"
with "defined, bounded protocols" for incoming freighter crews conducting business (`Sanay_Physical_
Infrastructure_Attributes.md` item 4) — distinct in register from the broader Shipyard Complex's general
freight-handling character. **This produces a real sensory/behavioral texture finding**: the Trade Yard reads
as a formalized, protocol-governed space (closer to Rotterdam/Guangzhou's differentiated-by-cargo-type terminal
zoning, per this pass's own G7 research) sitting inside a broader industrial complex that is otherwise far less
formally bounded. A person moving from the general Shipyard Complex into the Trade Yard crosses a genuine
register change — from ambient industrial noise/motion to something procedurally slower and more bounded.

## D. The seam — founding-era build vs. later expansion

**A real, admissible seam exists.** The original SANAE-era station infrastructure was "kept functional across
the interwar handoffs" and carried into the exile city (Founding section) — a compact, single-institution-scale
physical core. The Economy & Industry section's own "endless shipyards" language describes something
considerably larger: a mature, extensively built-out freight operation that grew well past the original
station's own footprint over the Second Interwar Period. **This is the two-stage lens** (`00b_Two_Stage_
Methodology.md`, generalized per `01` §4 rule 3) applied cleanly: a visible boundary should exist between the
compact, physically-inherited SANAE-era core and the much larger organic expansion the exile community built
around it once the port's economic role grew. **Candidate finding, REQUESTED for exact physical confirmation**:
older, denser, more conservatively-built structures near whatever the original station's own footprint was,
versus larger, more purpose-built industrial expansion elsewhere in the Shipyard Complex — a legible age-seam
in the built environment, not asserted as confirmed canon but as the characteristically expected shape given
the admissible founding-condition and economic-growth facts.

## E. Seasonal variance

Per Phase 1's both-are-true resolution on G2 (§B), the port's shipping season is real but its exact calendar
bounds are REQUESTED. What can be stated: whatever the open season is, the port's texture during it is the
continuous, unpredictable-rhythm character described in §B above; outside it (if a closed season genuinely
exists, per the real-world Antarctic resupply comparable), the port's texture would be markedly different —
quieter, defined by maintenance rather than freighter throughput. **This divergence is itself worth flagging
as a REQUESTED item with real texture consequences**, not a detail to skip: whether Sanay's port has a genuine
off-season or maintains its "at seemingly all hours" character year-round changes this phase's own answer
substantially, and admissible canon does not currently resolve it.

## F. Retroactive Mechanism

**The question:** what is established as simply true here that nothing explains?

**Candidate:** the vertical, two-tier layout itself (upper clifftops for admin/Arcanet/business, lower city for
truckyards/warehousing/heavy freight) is stated as fact in admissible canon but never mechanically justified.
**A physical cause that would produce exactly this:** Vesleskarvet's own bedrock stability is concentrated at
elevation (the nunatak's summit is explicitly its highest, most structurally sound point per Physical
Infrastructure Attributes item 2) — infrastructure most sensitive to structural reliability (the Arcanet relay
nexus, business/administrative records) would rationally be sited at elevation for exactly that reason, while
heavy industrial freight-handling, which needs direct water/dock access rather than structural delicacy, sits
low regardless of relative instability.

**The second-order consequence this generates for free:** administrative/business traffic and heavy-freight
traffic are physically separated by ELEVATION, not merely by function — meaning the port's own internal
logistics is a **vertical** chain (goods move up from dockside to clifftop administration for processing, or
paperwork moves down to dockside for cargo release) as much as a horizontal one. This is a genuinely new
finding this technique produced, not restated from any admissible source directly, and it is a strong
candidate for Phase 4 (Ordinary Life — what does moving between tiers cost an ordinary worker's day?) and
Phase 10 (a named vertical connector — a lift, a stair, a switchback road — as a catalog item).
