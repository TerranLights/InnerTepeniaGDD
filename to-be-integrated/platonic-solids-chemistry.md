# The Five Platonic Solids in Molecular Chemistry

Each Platonic solid corresponds to a specific coordination geometry — the way atoms or ligands arrange themselves around a central point or lattice site. Below is a full breakdown, solid by solid, with organic and inorganic examples for each.

---

## 1. Tetrahedron (4 vertices) — Coordination Number 4

The tetrahedron is the most common bonding geometry in chemistry, arising naturally whenever a central atom forms four bonds with equal repulsion between them (as in sp³ hybridization or VSEPR theory).

### Organic Examples
- **Methane (CH₄)** — the textbook example; sp³-hybridized carbon with four substituents at perfect tetrahedral angles (109.5°).
- Any sp³-hybridized carbon center — found throughout organic chemistry, including ethane, neopentane, and the individual carbons of larger cage molecules like adamantane.

### Inorganic Examples
- **Silicon tetrachloride (SiCl₄)** and **titanium tetrachloride (TiCl₄)** — simple tetrahedral molecular halides.
- **Sulfate (SO₄²⁻)**, **phosphate (PO₄³⁻)**, **perchlorate (ClO₄⁻)**, **ammonium (NH₄⁺)** — common tetrahedral polyatomic ions.
- **Diamond** — every carbon atom is tetrahedrally bonded to four neighbors, extending infinitely into a crystal lattice.
- **Zinc blende (ZnS)** structure — a classic crystallographic motif shared by many semiconductors, including GaAs, InP, and CdTe.
- **Silicate minerals** — the SiO₄ tetrahedron is the fundamental building block of nearly all silicate rocks and minerals, including quartz, feldspar, and mica.

---

## 2. Cube (8 vertices) — Coordination Number 8

Cubic coordination is rarer at the single-molecule level than tetrahedral or octahedral geometry, but it appears in a few notable structures.

### Organic Examples
- **Cubane (C₈H₈)** — a genuinely cube-shaped molecule where carbon atoms sit at the eight corners. It is famous in organic chemistry for its extreme ring strain combined with surprising kinetic stability.

### Inorganic Examples
- **Cesium chloride (CsCl)** crystal structure — each ion sits at the center of a cube formed by eight oppositely charged ions (8-coordinate).
- **Fluorite (CaF₂)** — calcium ions are cubically coordinated by eight fluoride ions.
- **Iron-sulfur cubane clusters (Fe₄S₄)** — biologically important clusters with a distorted cubic core, found in electron-transfer proteins such as ferredoxins.

---

## 3. Octahedron (6 vertices) — Coordination Number 6

Octahedral geometry is extremely common in inorganic and coordination chemistry, and appears in some of the most familiar crystal structures on Earth.

### Organic Examples
- Pure octahedral geometry is uncommon in simple organic molecules, but it appears in **organometallic compounds**, where carbon-based ligands surround a metal center octahedrally (see inorganic examples below).

### Inorganic Examples
- **Sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆)** — a perfect octahedral molecule, often used as the canonical example in VSEPR theory.
- **Hexafluorophosphate (PF₆⁻)** — another classic octahedral anion.
- **Transition metal coordination complexes** — including hexaamminecobalt(III) ([Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺), ferricyanide ([Fe(CN)₆]³⁻), and hexaaquairon(II) ([Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺).
- **Rock salt (NaCl)** structure — each Na⁺ ion is octahedrally coordinated by six Cl⁻ ions and vice versa; this structure is shared by MgO and many other alkali halides.

---

## 4. Dodecahedron (12 vertices, pentagonal faces)

**Important note:** in coordination chemistry, the term "dodecahedral geometry" often refers to a *different*, distorted 8-coordinate shape (a triangular-faced dodecahedron) — not the true 12-faced pentagonal dodecahedron of Platonic geometry. The examples below refer to the actual Platonic solid.

### Organic Examples
- **Dodecahedrane (C₂₀H₂₀)** — a genuine pentagonal-dodecahedron-shaped molecule. It was synthesized in 1982 after decades of effort and is considered a landmark achievement in synthetic organic chemistry.

### Inorganic / Structural Examples
- **Clathrate hydrates (gas hydrates)** — water molecules hydrogen-bond into pentagonal dodecahedral cages that trap small guest molecules like methane, forming so-called "flammable ice" found in permafrost and deep-sea sediments.
- Some **fullerene-related cage clusters** and boron cage compounds approximate dodecahedral symmetry, though true examples are rarer than icosahedral ones.

---

## 5. Icosahedron (12 vertices) — Coordination Number 12 / Cage Symmetry

Icosahedral symmetry is especially important in cage-like molecular structures and biological assemblies, since it's the most efficient way to enclose volume with a repeating structure.

### Organic Examples
- **Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀)** — technically a *truncated* icosahedron (like a soccer ball, made of hexagons and pentagons), not a regular icosahedron, but built directly on icosahedral symmetry. It is the most famous molecule associated with this shape.
- Larger fullerenes (C₇₀, C₈₀, etc.) — generally preserve icosahedral or near-icosahedral symmetry.

### Inorganic Examples
- **Closo-dodecaborate (B₁₂H₁₂²⁻)** — boron atoms sit at the twelve vertices of a near-perfect icosahedron; a classic example from borane cluster chemistry.
- **Icosahedral quasicrystals** — certain metallic alloys (e.g., Al-Mn alloys discovered by Dan Shechtman, who won the Nobel Prize in 2011) show local icosahedral atomic arrangements. This is notable because true icosahedral symmetry cannot repeat periodically like a normal crystal lattice — hence the term "quasicrystal."
- **Viral capsids** — many viruses (adenovirus, poliovirus, the HIV core) self-assemble their protein shells with icosahedral symmetry, since it is the most efficient way to enclose a volume using identical, repeating protein subunits.

---

## A General Caveat

Chemistry rarely produces *perfectly* regular Platonic solids. Real molecules often show slight distortions from bond angle strain, lone-pair repulsion, or Jahn-Teller effects. The tetrahedron and octahedron are the most commonly *near-perfect* geometries in real molecules; the cube, dodecahedron, and true icosahedron are rarer and tend to show up more clearly in crystal lattices or specially engineered cage molecules than in everyday compounds.
