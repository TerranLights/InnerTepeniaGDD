# The Five Platonic Solids — Forces, Pressures, and Natural Occurrence

**What this is:** a third reference layer for the flagged "original Platonic Solid symbol system" project (`TODO.md`), alongside `platonic-solids-chemistry.md` (coordination geometry) and `platonic-solids-mathematics.md` (per-solid and relational mathematical properties). This file covers a different axis again: what physical forces/pressures each solid is resistant or vulnerable to, and the real-world environmental conditions each shape naturally occurs in.

---

## Tetrahedron
- **Resistant to:** Shear and torsion, structurally — every face is a triangle, and triangles are the only polygon that can't deform without changing an edge length. This is why tetrahedral/triangulated frames are used in space trusses and engineering bracing wherever rigidity-per-material matters.
- **Vulnerable to:** Uniform external pressure from all directions (hydrostatic/isotropic compression) — it has the *worst* sphericity of the five, so it's the least efficient shape at resisting pressure applied evenly from every side. Sharp, acute vertices (its dihedral angle is the smallest of the five, ~70.53°) also concentrate stress, making it prone to fracture starting at an edge or point rather than failing gradually.
- **Natural occurrence:** The default, everywhere — sp³ tetrahedral bonding is the single most common coordination geometry in nature (diamond's bond network, nearly all silicate rock-forming minerals). But there's a real pressure story here worth flagging directly: **under extreme pressure, tetrahedral atomic coordination literally converts into octahedral coordination.** Ordinary quartz has tetrahedrally-coordinated silicon; stishovite — the high-pressure polymorph of SiO₂ found in deep mantle rock and meteorite impact craters — has *octahedrally*-coordinated silicon instead. Extreme pressure physically forces a tetrahedron to become an octahedron.

## Cube
- **Resistant to:** Direct axial/compressive loading — cubic packing is the only one of the five that tiles 3D space with zero gaps, so stacked cubic structures transmit compressive force cleanly face-to-face with no voids to collapse into (this is part of why brick/block construction works).
- **Vulnerable to:** Shear — square faces aren't inherently rigid the way triangular faces are; a cube can rack toward a slanted parallelepiped under lateral force unless braced (which is exactly why real-world square/cubic frames get diagonal — i.e., tetrahedral — bracing added). Cubic crystals also tend to have clean, well-defined cleavage planes: halite (rock salt) and pyrite both fracture predictably along their cube faces, meaning a low-effort fracture path already exists along specific directions.
- **Natural occurrence:** Common, ordinary, low-pressure conditions — halite, pyrite, galena, fluorite all form cubic crystal habits under everyday sedimentary/hydrothermal conditions, not extreme ones. Notably, diamond's *overall* crystal lattice is cubic (face-centered cubic) even though each individual carbon is tetrahedrally bonded — the local unit and the large-scale habit are different levels of the same structure, a literal physical instance of the tetrahedron/cube relationship, not just a mathematical one.

## Octahedron
- **Resistant to:** Higher pressure than tetrahedral coordination, directly — as noted above, 6-fold octahedral coordination is what silicon shifts *into* under mantle-level pressure, replacing the ordinary 4-fold tetrahedral arrangement. Octahedral geometry is, physically, the pressure-upgraded version of tetrahedral geometry.
- **Vulnerable to:** Still less pressure-efficient than the golden-ratio pair (sphericity ranks it below dodecahedron and icosahedron), and octahedral crystals have their own cleavage vulnerabilities along specific planes (fluorite, for instance).
- **Natural occurrence:** Spans both registers, which is unusual — natural diamond's classic uncut crystal habit is octahedral, formed under the mantle's extreme heat/pressure; but fluorite and spinel also commonly form octahedral habits under ordinary, low-pressure crustal conditions. It's the one shape that occurs comfortably at both ends of the pressure spectrum rather than being confined to one.

## Dodecahedron
- **Resistant to:** Once formed, genuinely good at holding its contents — the real-world dodecahedral cage (clathrate/gas hydrate) stably traps methane molecules without releasing them, so long as it stays inside its stability window.
- **Vulnerable to:** That window is narrow, and specifically vulnerable to **warming and depressurization** — clathrate hydrates decompose and release their trapped gas if the temperature rises or the pressure drops even moderately. (This is a real hazard: sudden clathrate depressurization is an actual danger in deep-sea drilling, and clathrate destabilization from ocean warming is a live climate concern.) The shape itself is also just difficult to bring into being at all — dodecahedrane, the pure molecular version, wasn't successfully synthesized until 1982 after decades of failed attempts.
- **Natural occurrence:** Narrow and extreme — specifically cold, high-pressure settings: deep permafrost and deep-ocean-floor sediment. No everyday, ambient formation pathway exists for this shape the way it does for the cube or tetrahedron.

## Icosahedron
- **Resistant to:** Uniform external pressure — it has the *best* sphericity of all five, the most efficient enclosure of volume per unit of surface area, which is a mechanical reason (not just an aesthetic one) that viral capsids evolved this shape to protect genetic material inside a minimal, resilient shell. Real icosahedral quasicrystals (certain rapidly-cooled metallic alloys) are also notably hard and wear/friction-resistant — hard enough to be used as low-friction, scratch-resistant coatings.
- **Vulnerable to:** Sudden shock — the same quasicrystal hardness comes paired with brittleness, so while it resists gradual wear extremely well, it tends to fracture rather than deform under sharp impact. Viral capsids have their own structural weak point too: the vertices are frequently where specialized portal proteins sit, functionally weaker points used for genome injection or release — the icosahedron's strength is in its faces, but its vertices are where it's built to open.
- **Natural occurrence:** Genuinely two different registers, and the contrast is worth keeping — viral capsid self-assembly happens spontaneously, constantly, under ordinary biological conditions (the default efficient way for many identical protein subunits to build a shell with no template needed). Icosahedral quasicrystals, by contrast, only form under rapid, non-equilibrium quenching — fast, violent cooling, not the slow equilibrium growth ordinary crystals need. One shape, two wildly different birth conditions: effortless and ubiquitous in biology, rare and violent in metallurgy.

## Connective Thread

The tetrahedron→octahedron shift under pressure (stishovite) is a literal physical instance of the Cube↔Octahedron duality identified in `platonic-solids-mathematics.md` — pressure is an actual, real-world mechanism that moves a structure from one side of that duality toward the other, not just a mathematical abstraction. The dodecahedron/icosahedron pair keeps splitting the same way it did in the meanings discussion: dodecahedron has *one* narrow, extreme pathway into existence, while icosahedron has *two* — one ordinary and effortless (viral capsids), one rare and violent (quasicrystals) — which tracks with the "sealed potential vs. unfolded potential" read those two solids were already given.
