what is a "capsule", and how can I give it as much prospective potential as possible?

A "capsule" on Steam is the main promotional image (or set of images) for your game — essentially your game's "box art" or thumbnail across the entire platform.It's the single most important visual asset for discoverability because:
    • It appears in search results, Discovery Queue, recommendation rows, wishlists, sales pages, and more.
    • Steam heavily scales it down (often to tiny ~120×45 px thumbnails), so if it doesn't work small, you lose clicks and impressions.

steamanalyser.com
Core Principles (aim for high scores on these 6 dimensions used by analyzers):
    1. Genre Clarity — Can someone name the genre in under 1 second at thumbnail size? Use strong visual cues (e.g., pixel art + sword for metroidvania, cute characters + farm for cozy sim).
    2. Title Readability — Bold, thick fonts with contrast/shadow/stroke. Keep it simple; test at tiny sizes. Place on a clean area (vignette, banner, or blurred zone).
    3. High Contrast & Color — Pop against Steam’s dark background (#1b2838). Check in grayscale. Strong focal point + value separation (bright subject, simpler/darker background).
    4. Single Focal Point — One main subject (character, key object, or scene). Avoid clutter—details get lost small.
    5. Uniqueness & Polish — Stand out from similar games. Match your in-game art style exactly.
    6. Brand Consistency — Feel like the rest of your page (screenshots, trailer).
What to Avoid:
    • Tiny details or fine lines (they vanish when scaled).
    • Review scores, awards, prices, dates, or extra text (banned or discouraged on base capsules).
    • Low-contrast/muddy art or busy compositions.
    • Generic stock-looking fantasy/sci-fi landscapes.
Actionable Tips to Make Yours Strong:
    • Start your design at 120×45 px and scale up.
    • Use bold silhouettes, iconic characters, or a single striking scene.
    • Test with friends: “What genre is this?” and “Can you read the title?”
    • Tools like free Steam Capsule Analyzers can score your draft (genre clarity, readability, etc.).
    • Hire a specialist if needed—capsule artists are common in indie circles and worth it for the CTR boost.

I've heard from a lot of places online that people tend to be very against games that use AI. How much validity is there to these claims?

There is real validity to the claims — especially for games that prominently use or disclose generative AI for visible content (art, writing, music, etc.) — but it's not universal hatred. Sentiment is polarized, with a vocal anti-AI segment creating a lot of noise online, while broader player data shows more nuance and tolerance depending on how AI is used. 
notebookcheck.net
Evidence of Backlash and Sales Impact
    • Sales/Review Penalty: A 2025 analysis of nearly 10,000 Steam games found that titles explicitly disclosing AI use got ~53% fewer reviews (a proxy for sales/engagement) compared to similar non-AI games, even after controlling for factors like developer experience and genre. They also had slightly lower positive review percentages (84.6% vs 88.3%). The effect was stronger for higher-quality or bigger projects. 
      notebookcheck.net
    • Vocal Opposition: On Reddit, forums, and social media, many players associate undisclosed or heavy AI use with "slop," laziness, or low effort. This leads to review-bombing, boycotts, and refunds in some cases. Steam's disclosure requirement (mandatory for generative AI content) makes it easy to filter or avoid. 
      insider-gaming.com
    • Next Fest/Indie Visibility: During events, a noticeable portion of demos use obvious AI assets, which draws criticism and can bury better games in the noise.
Counter-Evidence: Not Everyone Cares
    • Survey Data (2026): A GameDiscoverCo poll of ~3,800 engaged Steam players found:
        ◦ 43% okay with or accepting of AI use in games.
        ◦ 26% neutral.
        ◦ 31% negative (but only 8% said they'd never play a game with AI). 
          ai.ettoday.net
    • Many players are fine if AI is used transparently for non-core elements (e.g., coding assistance, concepting, or minor assets) or if the game is otherwise good. Acceptance is higher for tools vs. fully replacing human creativity.
Why the Split Exists
    • Vocal Minority Effect — Anti-AI voices (ethics, job loss, quality concerns, environmental impact) are louder online, especially in enthusiast spaces. This creates the perception of widespread hatred.
    • Context Matters — Heavy AI-generated assets in low-effort shovelware fuels resentment. Transparent, light, or high-quality use draws less fire.
    • Valve's Role — Steam requires disclosure for generative AI content (updated 2026 to clarify tools for "efficiency" don't always need it). This informs buyers but also flags games for skeptics. 
      eurogamer.net
Bottom line: The backlash is real and can hurt sales/visibility (particularly for disclosed generative AI in creative assets), but it's not a total dealbreaker for most players. A solid game with transparent, limited AI use can still succeed — quality and honest communication matter more than the tool itself. The loudest complaints often come from a minority, amplified by social media. If you're a dev considering AI, weigh disclosure carefully and prioritize human oversight for player-facing elements.

    • Be specific: Describe exactly what AI was used for (e.g., "AI tools assisted with concept art generation and texture upscaling; all final assets were manually edited and curated by the developer").
    • Don't disclose non-generative "efficiency" tools (code helpers, etc.) unless you want to — Valve clarified this in 2026.
    • Pro tip: Update the disclosure if your usage changes. This is the most reliable "official" channel and protects you from "hidden AI" complaints.

    • Dev Description / About Section — Add a clear, neutral paragraph near the top or in a dedicated "Development" tab:

    • Press Kit / FAQ — Include a clear AI section. Many journalists appreciate honesty and may highlight it positively if the game is good.
    • Response Strategy for Reviews — Reply calmly to negative comments: "I understand concerns about AI — here's exactly how I used it and why." Ignore or hide trolls; engage constructively. This often turns neutrals into supporters.

    • Patch Notes & Updates — Every major update can include a quick note on AI involvement (builds long-term transparency).

That's a great question — using AI for structural/narrative elements (story beats, questlines, dialogue trees, world lore, etc.) falls squarely under Steam's disclosure requirements because it's generative AI creating content that players consume. You should treat it the same as AI art: be fully transparent to avoid accusations of misleading players. 
eurogamer.net
How to Disclose It on SteamIn the AI Generated Content Disclosure section on your store page (required via Steamworks survey):Recommended wording example (adapt to your exact usage):
"This game uses generative AI tools to assist with narrative structure, questline drafting, dialogue generation, and lore development. All AI-generated text was reviewed, heavily edited, rewritten, and directed by the developer to ensure it fits the intended vision, tone, and quality standards. No raw AI output appears in the final game without human curation."
    • Be specific about what AI helped with (structural aspects, not just "story").
    • Emphasize human oversight — this is the #1 thing that reduces backlash ("I used it as a brainstorming tool, then shaped it myself").
    • If you used it for code/systems that affect quests (e.g., procedural generation), mention that too if it's player-facing.
You can also add a more detailed FAQ section on the store page or in devlogs.Broader Transparency Strategies (To Minimize Review-Bombing)
    • Devlog / Socials Early — Share your process openly: "I used AI to generate 50 quest outline variations, then picked the best 10 and rewrote them from scratch." Show before/after examples. This builds trust and turns potential critics into supporters.
    • In-Game or Menu Credit — Some devs add a subtle note in credits or options menu: "Narrative tools: AI-assisted drafting with full human editing."
    • Demo Feedback — Release a demo and explicitly ask for narrative feedback. Address concerns publicly ("Based on your comments, I rewrote Quest X without AI assistance").
    • Positive Framing — Position it as a solo-dev superpower: "AI let me experiment with way more story branches than I could alone, leading to richer quests."
Potential Risks & How to Handle Them
    • Narrative AI often draws stronger opinions than pure art because story feels more "creative/soulful." Expect some reviews like "feels AI-generated" even if heavily edited — respond calmly with facts.
    • Review-Bomb Risk — It's lower if you're honest from day one. The ~8% who refuse any AI will bomb anyway; the majority care more about whether the story is good.
    • Legal/Ethical Note — Ensure your AI prompts don't infringe on existing IP, and you're not feeding copyrighted material in.
Overall Advice: Full disclosure + emphasis on heavy human editing is the safest, most honest path. Many players respect devs who are transparent about tools (AI or otherwise).
