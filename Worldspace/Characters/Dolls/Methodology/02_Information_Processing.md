# Character Development Methodology — Stage 2: Information Processing

**Pipeline position:** how to process the information gathered in Stage 1 (`01_Input_Information.md`) — the
diagnostic toolkit for interrogating raw inputs until a specific, non-generic psychology falls out, before it
gets written down as structured Character Data in Stage 3.

**Status:** consolidation pass complete (2026-08-09) — independently-convergent techniques merged, distinct
techniques kept separate, governing principles (including a developer-refined Mystery Caution) stated up front.

---

## Governing Principles

Not techniques to run — standing checks on how much of the toolkit below to use, and what standard its output
gets judged against.

- **Backstory Proportionality Rule** (Kress) — processing effort should scale to how much a motivation would
  otherwise defy audience assumption. A motivation that already makes intuitive sense given who the character
  visibly is needs none of the toolkit below; a motivation that cuts against expectation needs real investment.
  This is Stage 2's own front-gate, parallel to Stage 3's Drift-vs-Drive question.
- **The Consistency Caution** (Maisel) — characters need to be *more* psychologically coherent than real people,
  not less. Every technique below should be in service of one legible, consistent throughline — not an
  ever-growing pile of realistic-but-contradictory quirks. This is the standard the toolkit's output gets judged
  against once processing is done.
- **The Mystery Caution, as refined by the developer (2026-08-09)** — Corbett's original claim was that a
  character shouldn't be fully resolved even by the writer, and that full transparency kills what makes her
  worth continued attention. **This project's version is stricter and different: full resolution belongs in the
  private design/character notes — nothing about a Doll's psychology needs to stay genuinely unknown to
  whoever's building her.** What stays withheld is specifically what surfaces *in actual media*: a fully-defined
  detail can be deliberately kept out of direct exposition and dialogue, and instead only gestured at through
  soft, ambient details embedded in the world around her — what drinks she favors, what routines she keeps,
  what she wears, what entertainment she owns in her own home, and so on. The mystery is a presentation-layer
  choice, not a design-layer one. This directly converges with and sharpens material captured elsewhere in the
  main DRAFT file: St. John's "Show the Symptom Before the Cause" and her *Longmire* cold-open case study (a
  character fully characterized through routine competence and small habitual gestures — a makeshift shower,
  scattered beer cans, a terse "Hurt it" — before a word of backstory is spoken), and St. John's point that a
  description of a dwelling or personal effects can carry the same characterizing weight as a line of dialogue.
  Treat this refined Mystery Caution as the umbrella principle those already-captured techniques are instances
  of: define everything privately, deliver almost none of it directly, and let soft ambient detail carry the
  weight instead.
- **The "Character Is Not You" Caution** (St. John) — watch for the same author's own reflexive instincts
  leaking into every character processed through this toolkit, a standing risk worth flagging explicitly given a
  single author generating companions across 35+ cities using the same methodology.

## Consolidated Techniques (independently convergent — same mechanism, two sources)

**NODE: The Why Chain / Interrogation Technique** — merges Card's Interrogation Technique and Boutros's Why
Chain. Both name the identical move: repeated causal "why does she want this" questioning, explicitly refusing
the first answer because the first answer is reliably a cliché (Card's "cliché shelf"), continuing until the
answer becomes primal (love, safety, identity, belonging, protecting family). Card's two supplementary moves —
**exaggeration** (push a trait further than realism strictly allows, with the caution that too much tips a
character into caricature) and **the twist** (invert the audience's working assumption) — survive as attached
refinements, as does his three-question trust checklist for the resulting scene (*So what?* — why should the
player care; *Oh yeah?* — would this character actually do that; *Huh?* — is it clear what's happening). Keep
Boutros's worked "Mike" example (*Breaking Bad* — laundering money, chased back to a murdered son and a
reshaped relationship to protecting family through money rather than trust) as the canonical illustration of
how far the chain should actually go before stopping.

**NODE: Self-to-Character Memory Mining** — merges Boutros's Mirror Interview and Corbett's defense-mechanism
excavation method. Both are the same underlying move: answer a question about a genuinely charged real moment
for *yourself* first, then ask the identical question of the character in an equivalent scene, rather than
deciding her reaction abstractly. Two use-modes worth keeping distinct within this one node rather than treating
as separate tools:
- **(a) Broad Flaw excavation** — Boutros's fixed question set: happiest memory, saddest memory, a time of
  genuine shame, a belief that regularly creates conflict and where it comes from, a time you were emotionally
  hurt. Answer for yourself first to prime honest, non-generic answers, then answer identically in the
  character's voice.
- **(b) Narrow defense-mechanism excavation** — Corbett's single-charged-moment version, scoped specifically to
  coping style: return to one genuinely charged personal moment (fear, shame, guilt), ask how you actually
  responded, then ask the same of the character in an equivalently charged scene, and watch what she does rather
  than deciding it abstractly.

## Standalone Techniques (distinct mechanism or distinct purpose — no merge)

- **The In-Voice Character Interview** (Boutros) — deliberately kept separate from Memory Mining above, despite
  serving the same broad goal (Flaw excavation), because the mechanism is genuinely different: a written
  in-character dialogue, starting deliberately banal ("why are you eating that sandwich") and letting the
  questioning drift toward more personal territory. Most of what comes out is expected to be useless — the
  value is specifically in the accumulation of small, half-defensive answers (the character resisting
  self-disclosure the way a real person would), not in a direct Q&A structure.
- **Obstacle Brainstorm-and-Triage** (Boutros) — generate a genuinely undisciplined list of 20-30+ internal and
  external obstacles standing between the character and her Want, quantity first, judgment later; then triage
  for which entries actually relate to the Want and produce real internal growth/conflict versus which are just
  situational texture. The payoff isn't the final kept list — it's that the volume surfaces obstacles (and often
  other characters) that reasoning forward from the premise alone wouldn't have found.
- **The Scenario Diagnostic Template** (this project's own synthesis of Maisel's mechanism) — the flagship tool
  in this whole stage, already the most fully built: a 9-step construction method (choose one axis → build a
  concrete populated situation → break it into 3-6 decision points → 4-6 concrete answer options per point,
  spanning genuinely different psychological types → one "consistent with" interpretation line per option →
  lightweight unscripted branch questions → 1-2 reflavored variants → ground the scenario in established canon
  → enforce the Consistency Caution across every choice made for one Doll), a full worked example ("Asked to
  Break Character"), and two seed lists of candidate axes (general-purpose and Tepenia-native) explicitly
  flagged in the DRAFT file for a dedicated future axis-brainstorming session. Two Maisel-sourced usage variants
  fold in *under* this tool rather than standing as separate techniques: running the same scenario with two or
  more companions together (to surface interpersonal dynamics rather than individual psychology), and running
  the same scenario at two different points in a Doll's arc — once "early," once "late" — to reveal both what
  she's learned and when the change actually began.
- **Compare-and-Contrast Self-Image** (St. John) — a character's self-image is only meaningful relative to a
  specific measuring stick (a social circle, a family member, a rival), not in the abstract. The generative
  question: who or what is this Doll's yardstick, and what does she tell herself about why she does or doesn't
  measure up to it?
- **Compelling Need via Maslow's Hierarchy** (Lisle) — which tier of unmet need (Physiological → Safety →
  Love/Belonging → Esteem → Self-Actualization) is most urgently driving her *right now*. Deliberately kept in
  Stage 2 rather than joining Enneagram/Direction in Stage 3, since this is explicitly situational — what's
  driving her in a given scene or arc-moment — not a fixed trait. Includes the pursue-vs-flee distinction (a
  need can be pursued toward something wanted, or fled from a remembered pain — not symmetrical, since fleeing
  implies a specific prior event that must eventually surface) and the "dart-throw" technique (pick an arbitrary
  point on the hierarchy and build backward from there) as a fast unsticking method.
- **The Public/Private Values Gap** (Lisle) — interrogate a character's private moral code (what she'll cheat
  at, lie about, or stay silent on when no one's watching) against her public moral code (what she'll publicly
  claim to believe or condemn), and treat the *gap* between the two — not either column alone — as the
  character detail worth writing.

## Troubleshooting Checklist

- **"Why is this character eluding me?"** (Maisel) — for when the toolkit above still isn't producing a
  workable psychology: Does she actually have a place in the story? Is she stuck one-dimensional because she's
  pegged too hard to an archetype? Is the real problem that the writer doesn't yet know what role she's meant to
  serve? Is the gap a knowledge gap about a type of person/background the writer doesn't understand well enough
  yet? Is she doing double duty — representing a whole group/city/faction *and* functioning as an individual —
  in a way that's pulling her in two directions? Is she over-defined *relative to* another character, existing
  only as someone else's foil?
- **Cross-reference, not duplicated here:** Seger's *Story Problem vs. Character Problem* test stays filed under
  Stage 4, not here — it diagnoses a different failure (a structural/narrative-function problem: does this
  character only exist to mechanically service the plot) from Maisel's checklist above (a psychological-
  processing problem). Worth knowing both exist and which one actually applies before assuming a stuck
  character needs more Stage 2 work when the real fix is structural.
