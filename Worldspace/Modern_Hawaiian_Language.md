# Modern Hawaiian — the national language of the Tepenian Federation

**Status:** reference note, created 2026-10-13 from the developer's draft (`to-be-integrated/Hawaiian_language.txt`) and the developer's answers the same day. **The language itself does not exist yet.** Its synthesis (vocabulary, grammar, register layers, slang) is the job of the separate slang-synthesis repo (`/home/kuroskalacs/Documents/Doll-Fi/media/Reference/slang-synthesis/`). This repo only needs to know *what the national language is and where concepts would derive from*. Nothing here is a Modern Hawaiian word; do not invent any.

---

## 1 · What is canon (developer statements)

**Origin.**
- Hawaii matters to every robot in the world. Doris Morikawa lives there and is involved in the International Archive, a vast library of human information that later becomes a major data source for building Arcanet in Antarctica. Hana Jinn lives there and works first at U.R.U.K., later at A.R.U.Ta.G.
- The Republic of Hawaii forms shortly after the Great War (2083). By the Falkland Treaty (2564) it is more than 500 years old.
- Hawaii is isolated for those ~500 years (the USA collapsed shortly after the Great War). In that time **an entirely new language takes shape.** It begins as a **pidgin of English, Japanese and Tagalog.** Korean and Spanish (especially Mexican Spanish) are supplementary influences.
- **Mandarin Chinese does not shape how the language works.** *(Developer's reasoning: it is written in glyphs and uses tones that no other major Hawaiian language accommodates. The developer notes that Japanese also uses kana, a regular, phonetically limited alphabet. Chinese may still reach the language as borrowed vocabulary; see §3.)*

**Names, by era.** *Each era calls its own language "Modern."*

| Era | Calls its own language | Calls today's real Hawaiian | Calls the Falkland-era language |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falkland Treaty and the Tepenian Federation | **Modern Hawaiian** | Old Hawaiian or Medieval Hawaiian | — |
| The Mars era (after the Tepenians colonize outer space) | **Modern Hawaiian** | Ancient Hawaiian | **Middle Hawaiian** |

**Role.** In the Federation era Modern Hawaiian is **the international robot language**, and it becomes **the national language of Tepenia**, known to robots and humans alike.

**Layered vocabulary.** Like English, which draws vocabulary and grammar from different languages at different levels (the developer's illustration from 2026 English: French for a higher register, Greek for mental and psychological contemplation, German for the common core), Modern Hawaiian would assign English, Japanese and the others to particular areas and levels. Which source language fills which level is **to be worked out in slang-synthesis.**

## 2 · Dome Fuji's official name

- Its proper name will be a **Modern Hawaiian vocabulary term**, in the way "Angkor Wat" is the title of the temple in Cambodia and also an ordinary Cambodian vocabulary term. Its colloquial name is "The Temple."
- **It comes from the national language** (known to robots and humans alike), not from the robots' own language alone.
- **Decision 2026-10-13: for now, refer to Dome Fuji as "The Temple." The proper name waits until the language has been synthesized.** No file is renamed; no placeholder word is coined.
- Whether today's Hawaiian (Old, Medieval or Ancient Hawaiian by era) contributes anything, such as a word for temple, is **open**; the developer said it is possible and wants it judged after sufficient research.

## 3 · Real-world reference points (researched 2026-10-13; a starting point, not the model)

*One round of searches. Everything below should be checked against primary sources before slang-synthesis relies on it. The developer's instruction: it will not be an exact one-to-one for Modern Hawaiian, but a good point of reference.*

**Hawaiʻi Creole English ("Pidgin") is the closest real analog, and it is already in Hawaii.**
- It began on the sugarcane plantations (reported start: 1835). Workers came from China, Japan, Korea, the Philippines, Portugal and elsewhere, with Native Hawaiian and English speakers. The pidgin grew into a creole that became many children's first language, in the early 20th century.
- Its main source of vocabulary is English, but it carries words and features from Hawaiian, Cantonese, Portuguese, Japanese, and later Ilocano, Okinawan and Korean. It differs markedly from English in sound, meaning and some grammar; it is a language, not careless English.
- Standard description: Kent Sakoda and Jeff Siegel, *Pidgin Grammar: An Introduction to the Creole Language of Hawaiʻi* (2003).
- **Useful implication for the canon:** the real pidgin stabilized into a first language within a few generations; 500 years of isolation is ample. The key design question is which source language supplies the grammar and which supply vocabulary at each level.
- **Correction to the draft:** Swahili is not a pidgin of Arabic, French and Persian. It is a Bantu language with heavy Arabic (and some Persian and other) vocabulary. The real Hawaiian Pidgin is the better comparison.

**Ilocano.**
- An Austronesian language of northern Luzon in the Philippines, related to Tagalog, Malay and Hawaiian (a distant relation). About 11 million speakers; agglutinative, with a Philippine-type voice system; historically written in the Roman alphabet, with an older indigenous script (kur-itan).
- In Hawaii it has been about as common as Tagalog. In the 2019–2023 American Community Survey data reported in the search results, roughly 58,000 people spoke Ilocano at home and roughly 59,000 spoke Tagalog or Filipino; in 2017–2021 Ilocano ranked first. The Ilocano-speaking population reportedly tripled between 1980 and recent years.
- **Implication:** Ilocano is a candidate Philippine contributor beside Tagalog, an open question for the developer and for slang-synthesis.

**Open research items.** Which Chinese varieties were present in Hawaii historically (the search results mention Cantonese); how kanji-derived vocabulary reaches Modern Hawaiian through Japanese; Hawaiian-language words in Pidgin and what a "temple" vocabulary term might look like (see §2); Okinawan and Portuguese as contributors.
