## B. The four sensory sub-fields, answered separately

**Sound.** Exterior: a near-constant wind presence — not an occasional storm sound but a baseline that is
simply always there when outdoors, such that true outdoor silence would itself be the unusual, notable
event (the inverse of most places, where wind is the exception). Interior: by direct contrast, given that
survival-grade structures here require "specialized orientation and design" against wind and icing (G2,
Phase 1), interiors are plausibly heavily sealed and insulated — producing a genuine, sharp acoustic
threshold between exterior and interior that a resident of a calmer climate would not experience as
dramatically crossing a doorway.

**Smell.** The Terra Nova Bay polynya is a permanent body of open water on an Antarctic coast otherwise
locked in sea ice — this is a genuine, differentiating smell source (brine, marine life, open water) that
an interior Tepenian city (Concordia, or an inland station) structurally cannot have. Rime ice itself — heavy
structural icing from sea spray meeting subfreezing air — carries its own cold, mineral, wet-metal-adjacent
character wherever it accumulates on exterior surfaces.

**Feel.** Wind chill, not ambient temperature, is the operative tactile fact outdoors — Specs' own note that
"an environment that is manageable in calm conditions at −5°C becomes impassable in a katabatic event at the
same temperature" means the *feel* of being outside here is fundamentally about wind pressure and cold
moving air against skin, not simply "how cold is it." Threshold crossings (entrances, doorways) are a
genuine physical event rather than an incidental one: any structure engineered against sustained high wind
plausibly requires a wind-break entry sequence (a vestibule or double-door arrangement) as an ordinary
engineering consequence of the same G2 constraint that demands "specialized orientation" generally — **this
is derived independently from the physical constraint itself, not from the excluded `_Physical_
Infrastructure_Attributes.md` file's "Threshold infrastructure" item (M-65)**, which made a similar-shaped
claim; the re-derivation here rests on ordinary engineering logic applied to the admissible climate facts.

**First impressions.** Arrival by sea (the polynya's own advantage, per G2/G5): a newcomer's first sight is
of a coastline where structures are visibly built *against* something — low-profile, wind-braced,
aerodynamically oriented rather than conventionally massed — a visually distinct architectural signature
from an enclosed/domed interior city. Arrival by Hwy 183: the highway itself is "a connecting road off"
the main corridor (Phase 1, G5's own deficit) — arriving overland is structurally a secondary, tributary
experience compared to arriving by sea, which is where Janbogo's real strength (G2/G5) actually lives.

## C. Public/institutional split

**A genuine, sharp split, falling directly out of Phase 1's G2 quadrants.** The exterior public register is
hazard-vigilant by necessity — wind-checking, shelter-routing, a heightened relationship to weather that
never fully relaxes (G2's own STANDING COST). The interior/institutional register, by contrast, is where the
STANDING COST's payoff lives: spaces engineered specifically to be stable, sealed, and calm precisely because
the exterior cannot be. **The split itself is characterizing** — Janbogo's civic life is legible as two
distinct registers separated by an engineered threshold, not a single continuous public sphere the way a
temperate city would have.

## D. The seam — the two-stage lens, and Phase 1's G4 finding pays off directly

**`01` §4 rule 3: is there a visible boundary between the founding-era build and anything added under later
pressure?** **Yes, and it is concrete rather than speculative**, because Phase 1's own researched G4 finding
supplies it directly: the real founding footprint — 16 buildings, ~4,000 m², three wings — is a small,
specific, boundable physical core. **Everything built beyond that original three-wing footprint, across two
and a half centuries of growth toward a population in the hundreds of thousands, is definitionally later
construction.** This is not a metaphorical seam; it is the literal, physical edge of what the founders
actually inherited versus everything the city became around it. **A player-facing consequence follows
immediately** (`Cultural_Synthesis_Techniques.md` §0b): the original three-wing core is a locatable,
enterable place — plausibly still recognizable by its construction era, materials, or scale relative to
everything around it, the way a real historic city center reads differently from its later sprawl. **Left
as a REQUESTED naming/detail question** (what the core is called now, whether it is still functionally
central or become a curiosity) rather than invented, per `05` §3's proper-name reservation and the general
discipline against inventing specifics research does not yet support.

## E. Seasonal variance — what this place becomes at its worst point

**The worst point compounds two hazards rather than trading one for the other.** Polar night (~May 20 – July
23, ~64 days) and the katabatic season's own peak (June/July, coldest months, most intense wind period per
the Monthly Summary) **overlap almost exactly** — meaning Janbogo's worst stretch of the year is
simultaneously its darkest and its most physically dangerous, rather than these being two separate seasonal
burdens that occur at different times. **This is sharper than it might first appear**: a place where the
dark season and the danger season were offset would let residents at least see clearly during the worst
wind, or endure the dark during calmer weather. Janbogo gets neither reprieve — full polar night and peak
katabatic intensity are the same six-to-eight-week stretch.
