# The Three Modalities — How Each District Relates to Change
**Status:** Design substrate only — NOT in-fiction. See `00_Method_and_Sources.md`.
**Sources mined:** Tierney, *All Around the Zodiac* (the dominant source — the only text in the corpus that treats modality as a mechanism rather than a label; OCR'd, quotations cleaned below); Clare Martin, *Mapping the Psyche* v1–v3 (psychological framing, the initiating/resisting/mediating triad, and the whole square/opposition geometry); Snodgrass (seasonal definition, the three "ritual crosses"); Emerson, *Behavioral Astrology* (Initiators/Workhorses/Finishers); Silva, *Zodiac Signs: The Ultimate Guide* and *Sun and Moon Signs* (the older term *quadruplicities*, per-sign application, Ophiuchus's modality-lessness).

> Framing guard: the four classical elements referenced below are an **out-of-fiction temperament grouping** used by the source books, unrelated to the in-fiction 8-element robot elemental system assigned to subnets. Never label a district by a classical element in any text a player sees.

---

## 1. What Modality Actually Describes

Modality is **position within a season**, not personality. Snodgrass gives the bare definition: the groupings by four determine "whether the house marks the beginning of a season (cardinal), the sun firmly fixed in a season (fixed), or the decline of a season (mutable)." Tierney runs it out month by month — cardinal opens (April, July, October, January), fixed occupies the middle (May, August, November, February), mutable dissolves the end (June, September, December, March) — and describes what each phase physically *is*. The cardinal month is when "the new qualities of that season rush in," a turning point that clears "the old, worn-out reminders of the previous season." The fixed month is when the season "settles into its most intense, concentrated expression," and — the detail that matters most for civic design — "the days can seem almost boringly similar to one another." The mutable month is when the season "starts to get fickle, weather-wise, a time when nothing is all that predictable"; the steady pattern "begins to break up and give way to the adjustments needed" for the next cardinal opening.

The three phases are therefore not three temperaments in competition. They are **one process in three stages, and each stage exists because the previous one exhausted itself.** Tierney is explicit about the direction of causation: mutability's job is to break down "forces that have already peaked in expression," because the concentrated energies of the fixed phase "have often become too crystallized — like a hardening of the arteries — to enhance the vital flow of the life force that was first animated by the cardinal signs."

Tierney's second image is the more usable one, and it is the cleanest mechanism statement in the entire corpus. The modes are **a breathing cycle.** Cardinal is inhalation — "an upward surge of rushing air," the thing that keeps us from passing out. Fixed is the pause at the top: "that stillness when we are momentarily not really breathing. Actually, holding our breath (fully possessing it) creates more of a static power than a gathering of energy." Mutable is exhalation, "an act that completes the cycle" — and critically, "what we are releasing at this point has been modified; it's not the same oxygen we first took in." **Cardinal takes in, fixed possesses, mutable returns something changed.** A district that only inhales suffocates on its own intake.

Martin supplies the abstract version: cardinal / fixed / mutable are **initiating, resisting, and mediating**. She grounds it in the number three — "the establishment of a relationship between two previously unrelated or opposing principles" — and in Plato's tripartite soul, where one part is spirited (cardinal), one "desires the pleasures of nutrition and generation" (fixed), and one is reasoning (mutable). The wheel demonstrates it four times over: "the primal force is Aries — Cardinal Fire, followed by the equally powerful resistance of the second sign of Taurus — Fixed Earth, creating a polarity which is only resolved by the third sign of Gemini — Mutable Air, with its ability to relate, communicate and mediate between the two opposing forces without taking sides."

Martin's boulder in the road is the single best behavioral test in the corpus, and worth stealing wholesale as a design instrument. Three travelers find the road blocked. **Cardinal** scales it — climbing equipment, straight over the top. **Mutable** goes around the outside, or changes direction entirely and finds another road. **Fixed** "will spend five years drilling a hole right through the center." Nobody in that parable is wrong. They are answering different questions: *how do I get past this*, *where else can I go*, and *how do I make this thing stop being an obstacle permanently*.

## 2. Cardinal — Aries, Cancer, Libra, Capricorn

**Core behavior toward change:** it starts the change and expects everyone else to arrive. Tierney's chapter title is *Gotta Get Moving.* Cardinal energy "supports taking direct action, with little hesitation or preplanning," and is "psychologically built to respond to situational challenges involving quick decisions and sudden changes." Martin: cardinal people "start ventures, they have a vision of what has to be achieved, they are self-motivated... they have the natural urge to instigate action and to initiate." Emerson calls them the Initiators, the ones who "can be counted on to get the energy flowing."

**Good at:** opening. Grabbing an opportunity in the window it exists. Forcing a decision that has been avoided. Tierney: they "won't stall or beat around the bush when something needs to be done soon, or when urgent, critical issues need to be squarely resolved." Also — against reputation — negotiation: "cardinality doesn't necessarily demand a noisy clash of opposing forces; lively, open negotiation is an option." It is upfront and straightforward in its dealings, "but it also expects fast results."

**Characteristic failure:** starting a fifth thing instead of finishing the first. "They do need to slow down before jumping into half-baked projects," and the drive to push "can be interpreted more as pushiness." Tierney's most quotable indictment: **"Even if cardinal signs are not all that productive and accomplished, they give the appearance of being in the thick of activity"** — the hustle is legible whether or not anything was built. And the closing line on Capricorn generalizes to all four: "cardinal signs sometimes never know when to quit pushing."

**Under pressure:** it manufactures a crisis to end the stalemate. This is the most important single sentence in the source for district design — "frustrated cardinal types will stir up a little commotion, now and then, that results in a temporary crisis where something's got to give." Cardinal "signs don't do well with prolonged deliberations, whereby dilemmas are drawn out. They seek a quicker release of tensions. They're also too impatient to cope with deadlocks or the foot-dragging tactics of others." And when genuinely cornered: "if unduly provoked, all cardinal signs feel the urge to attack others or defend themselves."

**What the four share despite different elements:** an intolerance of unresolved situations, and a reflex to be the one who acts on them. Not volume — this is the group's most misread feature. Tierney flags Cancer as the member that "seems oddly misplaced" among the assertive ones, and Martin corrects the misreading precisely: cardinal water "achieves its goals quietly and subtly. It is prepared to wait for the tide, or until everybody else has lost interest," then "get[s] what it wants to the surprise of all." **Cardinal means initiating, not loud.** A district can be soft-spoken, patient, and procedurally polite and still be an initiator; three of the four are.

## 3. Fixed — Taurus, Leo, Scorpio, Aquarius

**Core behavior toward change:** it does not refuse change; **it refuses to be the one who moves first.** Tierney states the actual rule outright — "their motivation to change is always internal" — and adds that "interference from the sidelines is very unwelcome." Fixed districts are not immovable objects; they are objects that will only be moved from inside. Martin's version: "if they feel pressurised or challenged they are likely to dig their heels in and not give an inch."

**Good at:** finishing, holding, and enduring. "What's so great about the fixed mode is that its inherent staying power allows us to finish what we or others start." They are "the builders of life, desiring to further construct and give substance to what the cardinal signs have initiated." Patience is a real capability here, not a virtue-signal: "they're less in need of immediately obtaining what they desire — they can wait, unlike the 'gotta have it now' cardinal group." Martin adds loyalty and "a tremendous capacity for endurance and perseverance." Emerson's label is the workhorses, "the project managers of the Zodiac."

**Characteristic failure:** the stalemate. Tierney: fixity "can lead to power struggles and no-win situations where all parties involved refuse to give in... Fixity is associated with stalemate conditions, where a battle of wills ensues." It becomes "outright pigheadedness at work, attempting to thwart the easier solutions of others" — note that the resistance is aimed at the *easier* solution specifically, because an easy solution proposed from outside is still a solution proposed from outside. The quieter failure is ritual: "being too habitual about adhering to one's daily rituals, especially at times when introducing a touch of variety would make so much more sense."

**Under pressure:** it holds, and the holding becomes the point. Fixed energy under load is not a gathering of force but the pause at the top of the breath — "static power." Martin, on aspect behavior, is blunt that this mode does not resolve tension, it outlasts it: a fixed opposition "will go on resisting" until something breaks it, and a fixed square becomes "very rigid and unyielding." Add pride, which Tierney names as universal to the group: "all fixed signs seem to have an inborn sense of pride."

**What the four share despite different elements:** the need to control what happens to them rather than "be swept up in a flurry of exciting but short-lived activity," plus an unstated conviction that their way is not stubbornness but correctness (Silva: "to Taureans, it's not about being stubborn because they wholeheartedly believe that their way is the right way"). Tierney flags Aquarius as the group's oddball — "all the other signs of this mode seem to want to hold on tightly to what they have," where fixed *air* holds an idea rather than a possession. Same immovability, different object.

## 4. Mutable — Gemini, Virgo, Sagittarius, Pisces

**Core behavior toward change:** it absorbs change and passes it on modified. Tierney gives mutability a job description, not a temperament: mutable signs "weaken the mighty resistances that fixed signs often put up to avoid being altered. Solid structure starts to lose its form as this process begins." Their task is "breaking down forces that have already peaked in expression, and that now need to be released and reformulated," and thereby "redistributing this energy, and preparing it for a future cycle of cardinal activity."

**Good at:** noticing that an adjustment is required before anyone else does — they are "highly alert regarding where and when such alterations are required" — and at going around obstacles rather than through them. Martin: because they have no motivation of their own "other than to remain fluid and flexible," they are "so good at mediating between the 'unstoppable force' of the cardinal signs and the 'immovable object' of the fixed signs." Also: they will "find a way around things and are generally not a bit bothered by changing situations," and are "more interested in the journey than in arriving." Emerson calls them the Finishers.

**Characteristic failure:** dispersal, and the broken promise. Tierney names the "wasteful scattering of mental energy — they can spread themselves too thin by trying to tackle too many things at once," and warns that "nothing is quite firm and absolute in the world of mutability... it's not even easy to have a clear direction in life, since nothing can be taken for granted," which makes long-range planning genuinely difficult rather than merely unappealing. The sharper failure is disclaimed commitment: mutable types are "prone to waver, waffle, switch sides, weasel out of things, pass the buck, or renege at the last minute — especially when starting to feel trapped by a commitment," because **"mutable signs always think they have a perfect right to change their minds, no matter what they've previously promised."**

**Under pressure:** it leaves, or it disappears into the role of go-between. Martin: mutable signs "avoid conflict at all costs because it is not in the nature of the mutable signs to engage in conflict, except as mediators or go-betweens." Silva's version of the same reflex, on mutable earth: it "deliberately takes the least resistant path... not keen on leading, but they can light the path." Two mutable poles under stress do not fight; they drift, and Silva's warning about a doubled mutable pairing is that they "never know where they are heading."

**What the four share despite different elements:** permeability and an aversion to monotony — they "cannot tolerate monotony," and they arrive at the same posture from opposite directions (two of them, Tierney says, run on "facts and figures" and things that can be "measured, tested, and confirmed as real"; the other two on "grand ideas and visionary ideals"). Tierney also grants the mode a genuine bonus worth using: resilience reads as youth. "Mutable signs certainly do not plod on, as if weighed down by gravity, but are light on their feet." Sagittarius is the group's oddball — the one mutable exception that will "plunge into experience" as assertively as a cardinal.

## 5. How the Modalities Collide

**The structural fact underneath everything in this section.** Signs three apart form a square; signs six apart form an opposition. Modality repeats every three signs. Therefore **every square and every opposition in the entire system falls between two signs of the same modality** — Martin states it plainly ("planets in square generally share the same orientation, since they will both either be in cardinal, fixed or mutable signs"), and Silva confirms the other half ("on the Western zodiac wheel, signs opposite one another share a quality"). Elements, meanwhile, repeat every four signs, so squares and oppositions land in *different* elements every time. Martin draws the conclusion: "although the planets will normally have the same modality, they will be in different elements, and so they will have a different way of processing information. This creates a friction which may never be entirely resolved, but this tension also provides energy and motivation."

**Read that as a design law: hostility is same-tempo, incompatible-aim.** Two parties grate when they want the thing at the same speed, in the same window, by the same means, and want different things. Nothing structurally hostile can exist between a district that initiates and a district that holds — those two are always at 30°, 60°, 120°, or 150° from each other. The soft aspects (trine at 120°, sextile at 60°) and the awkward ones (semi-sextile at 30°, quincunx at 150°) are *all* cross-modality. **Sworn enemies are always found inside your own modality; your natural allies and your merely-baffling neighbors are always outside it.** The four signs of a modality form what Snodgrass calls a ritual cross — the cardinal cross, the fixed cross, the mutable cross — a closed quartet of mutual squares and oppositions with no outsider in it.

**Cardinal vs. Cardinal — the welcome fight.** Martin: with two cardinal parties in square, "the challenge of the square may well be rather welcome, because each will stimulate the other to express itself in a dynamic, focused and goal oriented way." A cardinal opposition produces a felt obligation "to actively resolve the tension." These conflicts are loud, fast, energizing, and mostly survivable — they end, because both sides need them to end. Shared membership buys nothing: Tierney notes Aries and Cancer being "members of the same club doesn't mean they naturally see eye to eye," and Martin's Libra-vs-Capricorn example is exactly the pattern — same tempo, one side arguing from principle and the other from material result. Four such parties in one system is a permanent generator, not an occasional flare.

**Cardinal vs. Fixed — no aspect, and that is the point.** Structurally these two can only meet at soft or awkward angles, which is why the relationship is chronic rather than explosive. The friction is temperamental: Tierney says fixed signs "don't at all take kindly to pushy, aggressive people (cardinal types) or high-pressured situations." Martin's holiday scenario has cardinal doing all the research and booking while fixed "may have been to the same place for the last 25 years." The productive version is the standard pipeline — "it is often said that fixed signs finish off what cardinal signs start" — but Martin flags the trap in a chart weighted to both and empty of mutable: "a fairly driven and powerful combination, and there may be times when he needs the input of mutable people, to help him think of alternatives, or ways around difficulties he has come up against." **Cardinal plus fixed with no mutable can start things and complete things but cannot get around anything.**

**Fixed vs. Fixed — the real war.** Martin: a fixed opposition is "particularly resistant to integration" and "will go on resisting"; a fixed square means "each will resist the challenge of the other, and this can become very rigid and unyielding." Silva, on two fixed parties: "both always feel like they are undoubtedly right, and the other person involved is wrong," and "being fixed signs, both will struggle to adapt." This is the only collision in the system with no built-in exit. Neither side escalates to a crisis (that is a cardinal reflex) and neither side goes around (that is mutable). It simply persists, and it is inherited. Grudges on this axis outlive the people who formed them.

**Fixed vs. Mutable — erosion, not confrontation.** Also cross-modality, also never a square. Mutability's stated function is literally to dissolve fixity: mutable signs "weaken the mighty resistances that fixed signs often put up to avoid being altered," and what has become "too crystallized" gets broken down and redistributed. The fixed party never gets an argument it can win, because no argument is offered — the mutable party simply routes around the position until it is irrelevant. Fixed reads this as unreliability and bad faith; mutable reads fixed as an obstacle it politely declined to mention.

**Mutable vs. Mutable — the softest square, and the most useless one.** Martin: "a mutable square is much easier, since both are inherently more inclined to accommodate, adapt and adjust to each other," and a mutable opposition "isn't going to be quite so tense or fraught." The cost of that ease is that nothing is decided. Silva on doubled mutability: a recipe for difficulty "mainly because they never know where they are heading," and on the two Mercury-ruled mutables specifically, shared modality "is some common ground that might lead to solid communication, but the natures and lifestyles of the two are so fundamentally different that it probably won't matter in the end." **Two mutable parties in conflict produce indefinite accommodation, which is a different failure from deadlock and looks, from outside, like agreement.**

**Cardinal vs. Mutable — the coin-flip.** Martin: traveling together, "this could either work very well, with mutable perfectly happy to adapt and adjust to cardinal's plans, or mutable's lack of focus and commitment could just as easily infuriate cardinal." The variable is whether the mutable party is being asked to *follow* (fine, indefinitely) or to *commit in advance* (never reliably), since it holds "a perfect right to change their minds, no matter what they've previously promised."

## 6. The Governance Reading — DERIVED

*Everything in this section is derivation. The corpus treats modality as individual psychology or one-to-one relationship and never once as the politics of a resource-constrained polity. Nothing below is sourced; it is what the mechanism above implies when applied to Concordia's structure.*

Concordia is one sealed city, thirteen districts, one heat and power budget that cannot be grown. Under that constraint, modality maps directly onto **what kind of move a district is even capable of making.**

**Four Cardinal districts is a conflict engine, and naming it explains something the canon has left open.** Every one of the four holds a lever that unilaterally starts something the rest of the city must answer: the Power Core holds allocation, the Sanctuary holds intake, the Government District holds directive and procedure, the Yards hold production and construction capacity. Four initiators, one budget. Now apply Tierney's two lines together — cardinal parties are "too impatient to cope with deadlocks or the foot-dragging tactics of others," and "frustrated cardinal types will stir up a little commotion, now and then, that results in a temporary crisis where something's got to give." A city with four of them does not experience occasional emergencies; it **produces** them, on a cycle, as the standing method by which four impatient initiators break each other's deadlocks. Concordia's canon already records the symptom — no period longer than three years without an active emergency council session — and leaves open whether the Government District cultivates that condition or merely benefits from it. The modality reading offers a third answer that requires no villain: **nobody cultivates it. Four initiators sharing one budget generate it structurally, and the district that convenes the emergencies is simply the one holding the convening lever.** That is a strictly better answer for this project, because it is a non-malice mechanism with a real culprit.

**That the Government District is Cardinal rather than Fixed is the load-bearing detail.** A Fixed government would be a preserving institution: it would hold the status quo, arbitrate slowly, absorb pressure, and its power would consist of refusal. A Cardinal government **proposes**. It does not referee the other three initiators — it competes with them, using instruments they do not have. It issues directives and "expects fast results," and it cannot tolerate a drawn-out dilemma, which is precisely the temperament that writes a cap on how long grief may be resourced rather than sitting inside an unresolvable one. Martin notes that cardinal air is habitually mistaken for a water sign — assumed receptive when it is initiating — and that misread is the city's: every other district expects the Government District to be neutral ground, and it structurally cannot be. The actual neutral ground is the Hub, which has no modality at all.

**Who vetoes: the four Fixed districts** — Taurus, Leo, Scorpio, the Labs. Their power is not proposal; it is non-compliance, and it is unusually hard to break because "their motivation to change is always internal." A directive cannot move them and a crisis cannot move them; only a case made in their own terms, by someone they consider inside, can. Two behaviors follow. First, when a Fixed district loses an allocation fight it does not keep fighting — it withdraws from the fight and solves the problem privately, which is exactly the residential district's household-scale thermal self-sufficiency: not a victory, a secession from the argument. Second, a Fixed district that has been cut once will fight the *next* allocation with a ferocity that looks wildly disproportionate to outsiders, because fixed grievance does not decay and is inherited intact. The city's most intractable feuds will always be Fixed-vs-Fixed, and will always predate everyone currently arguing them.

**Who brokers: the four Mutable districts** — the Circuit, the Undergrid, the Frostlands, the Markets. They do not resolve the deadlock; they **route around it**, and the city runs on that routing far more than it admits. When official supply collapsed, the Markets became the shadow economy. When the war made the grid unholdable, the Undergrid kept life support running unwitnessed. The Circuit triangulates between accounts nobody can reconcile. The Frostlands maintain almost no written institutional culture and are therefore "enormously capable in ways that don't appear in [the Government District's] administrative records" — a mutable district deliberately evading a cardinal district's instruments, which the canon already notes it "has learned to use strategically."

**The cost of that, and it is the most useful line in this section:** brokering is invisible by construction. Cardinal action is legible ("they give the appearance of being in the thick of activity"), fixed refusal is legible, and mutable adaptation leaves no artifact — the whole mode's function is to dissolve structures, and dissolution has no monument. **Concordia's entire adaptive capacity is carried by the four districts with the least standing to demand credit for it,** which is the exact shape of the Undergrid's canonical wound and predicts the same latent grievance in the other three.

**The Hub has no modality.** The corpus's one relevant note is that the thirteenth sign "has no element and does not belong to a common modality," and "doesn't have an opposing sign." Derived: the Hub cannot initiate, cannot veto, and cannot broker on its own account — which is why it is permanently the venue and never the actor, and why nothing in the city is structurally aimed at it. It is the only place where all three tempos are neutral. Its contempt for district tribalism reads differently in this light: it is the one district with no tempo of its own to defend.

**Practical instructions this yields.**
- To break a Fixed district's refusal, never apply pressure and never route a directive — pressure hardens it. Supply an *internal* reason, through someone it already counts as its own.
- To pass anything through a deadlocked council, route it through a Mutable district; they are the only parties structurally capable of a way around, and the only ones with no stake in who wins.
- To manufacture a crisis, put two Cardinal districts on the same lever. To manufacture an unbreakable one, put two Fixed districts on it.
- Aries-vs-everyone resentment is broad, but Aries's *structural* conflicts are narrow: the other three initiators. The districts that merely consume its power are at soft angles to it and are, at worst, baffled by it.
- A quest that asks a Mutable district for a binding advance commitment is a quest designed to be broken, and should be written knowing that.

## 7. District Grouping

| Modality | Districts | Civic function |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardinal** — initiate | 5 The Power Core · 1 The Sanctuary · 8 The Government District · 6 The Yards | propose, convene, force decisions, produce crises |
| **Fixed** — resist | 2 Taurus · 3 Leo · 4 Scorpio · 7 The Labs | hold, finish, refuse, inherit grievance |
| **Mutable** — adapt | 9 The Circuit · 12 The Undergrid · 11 The Frostlands · 10 The Markets | route around, mediate, dissolve, go uncredited |
| **None** | 13 Axis Mundi (Hub) | venue; no tempo of its own, and nothing aimed at it |

Cross-check for use: each modality contains one district from each of the four out-of-fiction element groupings, so a modality group is never a temperament bloc — the four members share a *tempo* and share nothing else. That is exactly why they square each other.

## 8. Source Gaps

- **Nothing in the corpus applies modality to a polity.** Every treatment is individual psychology or a two-person relationship — charts, holidays, classrooms, marriages. Section 6 is entirely derivation and is marked as such.
- **Real source disagreement on who finishes.** Tierney and Martin have fixed finishing what cardinal starts, with mutable dissolving the result ("it is often said that fixed signs finish off what cardinal signs start"). Emerson and Silva both label mutable "the finishers" and fixed "the workhorses" / "doers." Not reconcilable. This file follows Tierney and Martin, because only their version derives from the seasonal mechanism rather than asserting a label; the disagreement is recorded rather than flattened.
- **Silva alone calls the cardinal signs "reacting or reactive signs,"** which flatly contradicts every other source's initiator framing and is never explained. Unused, but flagged in case it is a garbled reference to something real.
- **Etymology unavailable.** Silva glosses "cardinal" as from the Latin for *important*. No source in the corpus gives the standard derivation (*cardo*, a hinge — the point on which a thing turns), which would be the more useful image for a governance reading. Left unstated above rather than sourced from outside the corpus.
- **Tierney is OCR-damaged in exactly the wrong place.** His sign/element/mode reference table at the head of the chapter is destroyed — the columns have collapsed into bare word lists — and the fixed-mode section drops at least one full passage between the Taurus and Leo entries. All Tierney quotations above have been cleaned of scan artifacts; content was verified against the surrounding argument, but individual wordings should be sanity-checked before any verbatim reuse.
- **Modality-lessness rests on a single passage.** The claim that the thirteenth sign has no modality and no opposing sign comes from one Silva paragraph, which also says it "seem[s] to fit within the mutable modality most accurately." The Hub's structural neutrality in §6 and §7 is therefore thinly sourced and is a design decision as much as an extraction.
- **No source addresses a collective missing an entire modality.** Martin covers absence only for individuals, via a compensation theory ("we do tend to find ourselves forced to contend with anything which is missing in our charts") with no obvious civic analogue. Concordia has all three modalities present in equal fours, so the question is currently moot — but it would matter for any smaller settlement, subnet, or faction built on this substrate, and there is nothing to build that on.
