# Capricorn — Personality Substrate
**Maps to:** District 6 — Capricorn (The Yards) · industrial/ambition
**Status:** Design substrate only — NOT in-fiction. See `00_Method_and_Sources.md`.
**Sources mined:** Tierney (richest — structural + behavioral); Snodgrass (myth, history, pathology, shadow); Silva *Zodiac Signs: Ultimate Guide* (**complete** — personality, cusps, strengths/weaknesses, childhood, friendship/work, core need, same-sign friction); Silva *Sun and Moon Signs* (myth, self-isolation); Clare Martin *Mapping the Psyche* v1–v3 (Saturn geometry, Cancer axis, decay/pruning imagery); Emerson (decans, aspect tables, reverse-aging note).

**Revision note (supplementary pass).** The first extraction reported that Silva's *Ultimate Guide* Capricorn chapter "begins mid-book" and that its personality/dark-side chapters were absent. That was a slicing defect on our side, not a source gap: the slicer kept only the single largest contiguous block of Silva's Capricorn material and discarded the other ten. ## 1. Core Archetype & Driving Motive
The organizer who makes other people's visions structurally real. Tierney: Sagittarius supplies the ideals, "Capricorn provides the organizational know-how to make worthy social dreams come true" — and will only implement "those few broad-based concepts that have practical, timely value." Cardinal earth = duty plus the push to make things happen; the one temperament in the earth group "with the momentum to achieve prominence on a grand scale."

The motive underneath is not greed but **standing that cannot be revoked**. Martin: it takes "the hard and lonely road to the top, knowing that what it achieves has been hard won and truly deserved and can never be taken away." Nothing arrives free; "we will only reap what we have previously sown." Time is the measuring instrument for everything — Saturn is lord of time, and this temperament reads as fascinated by measurement itself (Martin literally suggests "a cartographer or clock maker").

Its stated civic vow (Tierney): "make society a safe and orderly place for its hard-working, law-abiding members." Its nightmare: "a lawless world run amok." Therefore **roles — especially professional roles — carry moral weight**: "Everyone must serve a needed social function and be productive if society is to keep from falling apart."

## 2. Light Expression (the district at its best)
- Builds things designed to outlast their builders. Martin's waning-square keyword for this sign: establishing "collective or social structures which will stand the test of time."
- Winter competence. Silva: "resourceful, and they always have a plan, just like winter has always dictated to all who wish to get through it." Martin: understands the necessity of "stripping things back to their bare bones, cutting back and pruning, in order that they can grow stronger in the next season."
- Genuine calculated courage — the one earth temperament "willing to venture forth into unknown territory to take a few calculated risks," but only "after it has carefully evaluated the situation." Never on faith.
- Integrity as reputation management that has become real: Tierney notes it knows brutal authorities historically "fall from power in a state of disgrace," and it wants no permanent stain, so it behaves honorably even when unwatched.
- Institutional memory as affection. Silva: remembers your tastes, your birthday, the names of every relative you ever mentioned; "loves to reminisce about the old days." Sentimentality here is archival, not effusive.
- Dry, load-bearing humor. Silva: the humor "serves not just to make people laugh but also to get the sea goat and those around them through hard times" — "the ace up Capricorn's sleeve and the secret to their awe-inspiring perseverance."
- Quiet authority: "tend to exert authority even when they aren't in an authority position on paper," and "inspire confidence in others making no conscious effort to do so."

## 3. Shadow / Dark Side
Priority section. The corpus is unusually rich here and the failure modes are *structural*, not malicious — which suits a district whose injustice is meant to be nobody's fault.

The recovered Silva chapters change the weight of this section. Snodgrass and Tierney supplied the *sycophancy*; Silva independently supplies the three things that make it run without anyone choosing it — a preference for legible proxies over actual fit, a stated preference for following the crowd because following is safe, and a felt need for schedules to never change. Those are, in order, how the sponsor chooses, why the sponsored defer, and why nothing is ever recalibrated. The first cluster below is the material that bears directly on "The Narrow Door" (see `../Deep_Dives/06b_Capricorn_Alternative_Conditions.md`).

**The patronage engine, from the inside**

- **Appearance over substance, named outright.** Silva's weakness chapter gives the mechanism in one clause: Capricorns "get stuck in a negative cycle when they focus more on how things look instead of how they feel." A temperament that grades on the visible surface and does not notice it is doing so.
- **The advice chapter *is* the injustice.** Silva's remedy for this sign at work: it "should work at looking more interested in their work," because "it is often seen that they are in a rush to leave when they get their work done even if colleagues are still at their desks. Changing this habit can be helpful and will make people notice their dedication." Nobody is told to fix the window through which dedication is observed — the worker is told to be more visible inside it. This is **nAlpha** stated as career self-help, by a sympathetic author, to the people it disadvantages.
- **Selection on credentials, sincerely mistaken for selection on merit.** Silva on how this sign picks a partner, and it reads identically as a description of how a master picks an apprentice: they "choose partners who appear to be perfect instead of taking time to see if they are really the right fit," they "use their business strategies to navigate their relationships," and they hold "a premeditated view about who would be the perfect partner for them, and this leaves them disappointed in the end." The verdict line is the usable one: "Someone who looks good and earns well is not necessarily the right partner if you just choose them based on these credentials, but Capricorn will learn this the hard way."
- **Conformity as a comfort, admitted.** "While they like the comfort and security in following others, they could achieve a lot more success if they walk the path less taken." Silva says plainly that this temperament follows the crowd because following is safe — the demand side of patronage, attested independently of Tierney's "kissing up." Paired with the same chapter's note that it must "learn to value their own decisions and thus gain respect from their colleagues at work": deference is the default setting, and it costs standing.
- **The schedule as a comfort object.** The single most useful new line for the calibration layer, from the child chapter: "Continuity comforts them. Capricorn children are better at sticking to schedules than their parents usually," and disruption to routine "causes Capricorn children to get off balance." A stale calibration is not left alone out of neglect. It is left alone because changing it is felt as a loss of safety, by people who never frame it that way.
- **Impossible standards, exported.** "They often hold other people to their own impossibly high standards." And the reason nothing is revisited: "Capricorns are strict and value tradition to the point where it is difficult for them to accept any new changes. They struggle with opening their minds to new things."
- **Saturn devours its successors.** Silva's aside on the planet is the darkest single line the corpus offers this sign: it "was named after the titan who tried remaining a ruler by eating his offspring." Set that against Pricus (§13) and the sign has exactly two paternal myths — the father who could not save his children, and the father who ate his. Both are about a senior generation and what becomes of the junior one.
- **Saturn's joy is the house of confinement.** Traditionally Saturn "finds its joy" in the twelfth house — "one of confinement, isolation, and solitude," which classical astrologers "also called the house of a bad spirit," connected to "anxiety and shadows which can cause mental anguish." The ruling planet of this temperament is most at home in the house of enclosure. Nothing else in the corpus makes the perimeter/fortress imagery (§12, §14.12) feel less like architecture and more like preference.

**The rest**

- **Boot-licking upward, whip-cracking downward.** Snodgrass, the sharpest line in the whole slice: the shadow "becomes a sycophant, snob, or abettor of illegal or ignoble activities. At the worst, the archetype never hesitates to cozy up to the brass, thus earning a reputation for boot-licking." Tierney's version is subtler and worse: it learned by kindergarten that "grownups are suckers for a well-behaved child," and it is "an expert at 'kissing up' to authorities" — and does **not** experience this as phoniness, only as strategy. Paired with Tierney's line that this sign is "so hung-up on glorifying authority (either by being one or bowing down to one)," you get patronage without anyone consciously choosing patronage.
- **The favor ledger.** Tierney: Capricorn and Libra "would both keep track of all favors asked and later expect others" to repay. Nothing is a gift; everything is an entry.
- **The stern parent in command.** "Work as hard as I do if keeping your position means anything to you!" Low tolerance for "incompetent folks who mess up and indirectly make the Goat look bad" — note the injury is *reputational*, not practical.
- **Exhaustion as evidence of virtue.** Tierney warns this temperament "needs to monitor workaholic tendencies"; Emerson: "outrageously responsible, so be careful you don't end up doing the work of others." Snodgrass's image is the definitive one: given a bad knee or gout, "to avoid losing out at work, the dependable goat prefers to balance a briefcase on a lapboard rather than rest and follow orders."
- **Guilt as the value system.** Silva: it introduces guilt into everything it touches — values "based on guilt," a standing sense that "nothing is good enough." Self-criticism is constant enough that Silva's advice chapter has to ask whether it would ever say those things to someone it loved.
- **Universalized private standards.** Silva: "Every Capricorn individual can have their own values, and these are set in stone... All their rules and values apply to any individual they come across." Personal conviction is applied as though it were law.
- **Moral policing beyond legality.** Tierney: it curbs behavior "it deems to be inappropriate or even a threat to conservative society — Capricorn targets not just lawlessness, but any actions it finds immoral."
- **Defensive conservatism disguised as stability.** Tierney: "Upholding the status quo may thus be done for defensive reasons, not simply for the purpose of stability." A fear of change wearing the costume of prudence.
- **Homogeneity pressure.** "Capricorn expects everyone to toe the line in ways that keep the community homogeneous — it distrusts 'outsiders' or those who threaten cultural cohesion."
- **Collapse on non-recognition.** If the dues are paid and the standing never arrives, it waits — patiently, for a long time — and then "a sense of being defeated by a cold, uncaring world can create bitterness and a slump of depression."
- **Inept before paradox.** Snodgrass, superb: "Faced with true paradox, Capricorn is inept, confounded by a deeper level of social or religious interpretation than precepts can explain. In these embarrassing situations, Capricorn confronts a shallowness in self that is likely to dismay or depress."
- **Silence, not lying.** Two of this temperament together: "Lies are never a real issue... The problem is the silence they leave between them." Debate becomes "a silent tournament that neither wins." They will refuse shared activity "out of spite. There is no other logical explanation for it."
- **The unforgetting.** Silva: it holds "on to a grudge for a long time," and "if you say something once to a Capricorn, they will always remember it. Even if it was an offhand comment, it leaves a deep impression on them." The same faculty that produces the institutional-memory virtue in §2 produces this. There is no forgetting mechanism anywhere in the temperament — only a ledger with two columns.
- **Isolation dressed as self-sufficiency.** Silva: when insecure or disappointed it "shut[s] the whole world out," hiding pain "behind a veil of humor and self-sufficient attitude," until life becomes "very lonely and isolated, even around people." Emerson names the endpoint: "the saturnine blues." The recovered weakness chapter closes the loop and makes it a trajectory rather than a mood: guardedness "can even leave them with no one to talk to, which can lead to depression over the years." The Moon's detriment supplies the trigger — wounded emotionally, it is "prone to become cynical," after which "it is difficult for a Capricorn to trust people again," and it will "take a long time to admit a need for emotional support."
- **Perfectionism with no floor.** Silva: "their need for perfectionism applies to everything in their life," and it "adopt[s] a negative view of their future when they have to face any failures or feel like things are not perfect." Failure is not a setback in this temperament; it is evidence about the future. Note the knock-on Silva flags: "this dejectedness and pessimism also have a negative effect on the surrounding people" — the mood is contagious and treated as realism.
- Snodgrass's blunter register, offered and then partly disowned by the author as overgeneralized: "drudges and crepehangers, perennially anticipating disaster," accused of "mean-spiritedness and inhibition." Also flags an upward-mobility shadow expressed as domestic supremacy — the elevated patriarch "flaunts supremacy, thus muddling relationships by becoming bossy, sexist, and judgmental," and reducing a competent colleague "to a needy suppliant."

## 4. How It Reads From Outside
Martin's bus-stop vignette is the single best recognition marker in the corpus: **"Capricorn waits with a grim sense of satisfaction and validation. He knew the bus would be late, since it validates his world view that everyone else is incompetent and irresponsible"** — and then, being a department head, decides to run a survey about it. Vindicated pessimism plus an immediate procedural response.

Other markers: courteous but never close — Silva warns you must "not mistake their default friendliness and courtesy with closeness... there is an undeniable distance that's always being maintained." Tierney's chameleon note: it slips convincingly into whatever demeanor an occasion demands and considers this strategy, not falseness, and "seldom is it ever truly caught off guard in a manner that exposes its hidden vulnerabilities." It seems "all-business" and somewhat flat, and is assumed uncaring. Silva's image of self-presentation: "a strong and independent core with a soft and approachable velvet cover." Compared to a fame-seeking temperament: "The Lion seeks fame while a Capricorn's main goal is a fortune... a Capricorn enjoys understated elegance," and saves obsessively where others spend. At a gathering: shows up when it matters, drinks "a few but never more than they can handle," makes polite conversation, and leaves "when it is acceptable to do so."

## 5. Core Need
To be **sincerely recognized for dedicated effort within a chosen field of contribution** (Tierney) — not applause, but the acknowledgment that the dues were real and were paid. Underneath that, Martin's Capricorn-rising material exposes the wound driving it: a "strong feeling of isolation, a sense of being alone in the world," a conviction "that there is something particularly unsafe about the world and that they are unlikely to get their needs met." Self-sufficiency is the armor over the belief that nobody is coming. The Cancer axis names the real question: "what is enough and what is not enough."

Silva's own "What Does a Capricorn Need?" chapter — the section the first pass could not reach — turns out not to be about achievement at all. It is a list of **permissions**, which is the more useful shape for district design. Confidence in its own judgment, so it can "value their own decisions and thus gain respect from their colleagues at work." Permission to pick work it actually loves rather than "following the crowd." A workplace separate from a spouse's, because "this sign is very competitive and controlling" and will otherwise turn a marriage into a promotion cycle. Exposure to unfamiliar things. The ability to let another person finish a sentence — "letting someone talk without interrupting is a skill this sign needs to acquire." Daily unproductive time. Permission to hope. And, last and sharpest, permission to stop: it "needs to stop criticizing themselves all the time," and should "think about the things they tell themselves and decide if it were something they would ever tell someone they loved."

The child chapter names the root of all of it. Capricorn children "tend to think that they have to prove themselves so they can earn respect and love," and Silva's instruction to the parent is to say the opposite out loud — that "they deserve love and respect even if they don't always come out on top." Underneath the need for earned standing is a conviction that **unearned standing does not exist**, and cannot be given. That is why recognition-denial (§3) is catastrophic here rather than merely disappointing: it is not a reward withheld, it is the disproof of the only theory of worth the temperament has.

## 6. Behavior Under Conditions
- **At work:** wants to own the organizing of its own task — "most comfortable when they oversee their work... organizing their time, environment, details of the project." Takes direction fine but performs best with autonomy. Detaches emotionally to decide raises and promotions, and considers that detachment a virtue. Chilling corollary from Silva: "Capricorns can also treat people like projects." Silva's recovered career chapter names five occupations as native to the temperament — teacher, professional manager/organizer, accountant or financial planner, business executive, and programmer/IT — and the shape they share is worth more than the list: every one is a role that **imposes order on someone else's material**. Silva's own flattery of the type gives the district its self-image in one line: people without earth in their charts "cannot lead organized lives, and they need a Capricorn's help to organize themselves." Note the one honest caveat the same chapter volunteers — "many Capricorns cannot relate to young children," which is a real limit on the district's teaching institutions and on how it treats its own apprentices.
- **Completion, not presence.** It leaves when the work is finished, colleagues still at their desks or not (Silva). Held against the workaholic portrait everywhere else, this is the corpus's most productive contradiction — see §15. Behaviorally it means the district measures a *task*, not a shift; the tension is that its promotion system measures the shift.
- **Under idleness:** destabilized, not rested. Silva's child chapter: they "like having tasks to do and often get listless when they are free or bored for too long." Unstructured time is a hazard here, not a reward — relevant to any Yards beat involving a work stoppage, a shutdown, or a strike.
- **Under ridicule:** the injury lasts. Silva, on the boy child but consistent with the adult portrait: "he hates being ridiculed or misinterpreted. This shakes the balance for a Capricorn boy. It can often affect him for weeks on end." Weeks, from a single instance of being *misread* — not attacked. This is a firmer number than the generic humiliation-withdrawal note below.
- **Against a deadline:** sovereign, and therefore late. Silva concedes that the Capricorn child "misses deadlines at times," because they like "doing things in their own time and with their own approach," take longer as perfectionists, and are "averse to any nagging or constant reminders to do things." Against the punctuality claim made elsewhere in the same book (§15), the reconciliation that actually serves design is: **punctual to other people's appointments, sovereign over its own deliverables.**
- **Socially / in public:** low profile by preference. Useful activity over leisure — Silva: propose a class or a hike, because "a coffee date or dinner will seem pointless to them most of the time." It maintains a private A-list; Tierney claims this temperament "created the concept of the 'in crowd,'" composed of those "going places and doing important things."
- **Under stress:** works more. Tierney's element note is that stressed earth *consumes* rather than burns out — it does not sense its own exhaustion the way fire does, but it also will not stop. Over-preparation (Emerson). Withdrawal after public humiliation: the first instinct is to disappear, though it may instead stand firm and defend its honor aggressively.
- **In crisis:** genuinely superb — patient, planned, unsentimental, still functioning when others quit. Silva: "When the going gets tough, and most other people are ready to throw in the towel," this is where it wins. Mars is exalted here: aggression under discipline is at maximum efficiency. The cost is that crisis *validates* the temperament's worldview, making the emergency posture hard to stand down from afterward.

## 7. Socialization (how newcomers are absorbed)
Slowly, and by demonstrated worth. Silva: to get close "you have to be persistent" — the distance is a test, and it takes time "to judge if a person is worthy of being part of their inner circle." What earns entry is loyalty, honesty, hard work, and visible skill: "display your skills to them since they are attracted to such things." Maintenance is a duty — you are expected to stay in touch, remember dates, and keep showing up; it dislikes people who disappear.

Children are raised into it early and deliberately. Tierney's "Born Old": this temperament arrives "possessing a wisdom normally reserved for the elders of any tribe," studies power roles from childhood, and "knows it has dues to pay before it will be allowed to become somebody important." Its protectiveness works "in the no-nonsense manner of a security guard on patrol," it feels responsible "for instructing people in how to conduct themselves," and it must be careful "not to overcontrol people in its attempt to provide them with safer and more structured ways to live." Snodgrass adds the shadow of it: as a parent it "may choose social status, prestige, wealth, or impressive genealogy over love and happiness" when setting a child's goals.

**The recovered childhood chapter** (Silva, wholly absent from the first pass) is the richest single block of new material in the supplement, and it is almost entirely about how a Yards child is made:

- **Continuity is the primary need.** Don't change their school, don't change their home; "continuity comforts them," and "Capricorn children are better at sticking to schedules than their parents usually." A generation raised to experience the timetable as safety is a generation that will not audit the timetable.
- **Respect is the currency, and it is understood to be purchasable.** They "dislike being treated disrespectfully" and "tend to think that they have to prove themselves so they can earn respect and love."
- **Rules are held in good faith — including by rule-breakers.** They have "inborn respect for rules" and "good intuition in judging what is right," but Silva's parenting instruction is the striking part: "if your child seems to break a rule, you should ask for an explanation. They usually have a very good reason for doing so." The cultural default here is that a person who broke a rule had a reason, and that the reason is worth hearing. That is a genuinely distinctive civic instinct for the Yards, and it cuts directly against the moral-policing shadow in §3. Keep both; the tension between them is the district's internal argument.
- **Play must have a purpose.** "Instead of playing tag, she would rather dig into your garden. Instead of playing in the sand, she would rather read a book. A game that helps her build or create something would be better suited." Competitive games are discouraged, because it is "competitive by nature, and it will often end in tears or sulking" when it loses; collaborative ones are prescribed instead. At the playground it "hang[s] back instead of rushing to play with the others."
- **The obsessive special interest is the standard childhood shape.** Trains means every book and every show about trains; dinosaurs means every species by name; and "don't be surprised if they often beg you to take them to the museum instead of wanting to go to the amusement park." Depth over breadth, from the beginning.
- **Sensory and social conservatism.** They "usually dislike loud noises," are uncomfortable being left with unfamiliar adults, and prefer the company of older children and of adults outright.
- **Thrift is taught, not discovered.** A piggy bank early; spend only "on things they need or want instead of splurging on useless things." The saving habit noted in §4 is trained, not innate.
- **Small wins are rewarded on purpose.** Silva's instruction: "praise their little achievements the same as you do their big ones," precisely because the child will not otherwise count them. Note this is *advice*, i.e. it is what the culture fails to do by default.
- **Reading is a recognized route into isolation.** "Don't let them spend too many hours doing this since it can make them grow into loners." The isolation failure mode (§3) is understood by this culture as a childhood risk with a known cause.
- **And the useful one:** the district's adolescents are its moral critics. Teenagers here become "often critical of you or others at home, but this is only because they have a strong sense of what is right and social justice. They are not shy about expressing differences in opinions over such matters." In a district whose injustice is structurally invisible to everyone inside it, the corpus names exactly one demographic constitutionally disposed to say something is wrong — and it is the one with no standing to be heard.

## 8. Conflict Geometry
**Opposition — Cancer (District 1).** Structural, not incidental: Martin frames Cancer–Capricorn as the **parent/child axis** (fourth house vs. tenth, Moon vs. Saturn) carrying "the twin core fears of engulfment and abandonment." The two are the same question asked from opposite ends — *who feeds whom, and what counts as enough*. The failure mode is documented precisely: "When this axis polarises, the Cancer end becomes increasingly needy and the Capricorn end becomes increasingly withholding and withdrawn," each escalation confirming the other. Note also that the Moon is in **detriment** here: the nurture function is at its structurally weakest exactly where the opposing district makes it a civic institution. Both sides are competent leaders; Silva's split is usable — one "is more gifted at working with different kinds of people while a Capricorn is better suited for working with products."

**Square — Aries (District 5).** Both cardinal, so both initiate hard and neither yields; incompatible elements make the collision permanent. Tierney's Aries chapter is explicit that Aries reads this temperament as "hung-up on glorifying authority" and defines itself against "a social system that tries to turn people into obedient sheep, not fighting rams." Aries pays for its defiance with a worse career — "at least nobody owns their soul." Emerson: "extremely hard. Both personalities couldn't be any more different... Pretty much a losing battle." The fight is over whether earned rank is legitimate at all.

**Square — Libra (District 8).** Also cardinal; the trap is that they *look* compatible. Silva: they share leadership ability and work well "on an intellectual level," and both keep favor ledgers. But "Capricorn is focused on facts while Libra plays with concepts," and Capricorn "hate[s] the inability of Libra to make decisions" — Emerson: "Capricorn will get fed up and hit the road." Tierney names the deeper split: Libra "knows how unfair society can be whenever it tries to regulate the private lives of its members," which sets it "at odds with rule-enforcing Capricorn." Procedure versus deliberation; a district that decides versus a district that keeps weighing.

Secondary friction worth having: Sagittarius (District 11) is where **Jupiter falls** here — Silva: this temperament "cannot comprehend the magic of life or any beliefs of a certain kind. They only trust in rational thought, hard work, and real results." And **Capricorn against itself** is a real conflict axis: two of them compete for first place, go silent instead of arguing, and Silva's verdict is harsh — "two negatives sometimes give a positive, but with Capricorns, it is far more likely to give another negative."

## 9. Affinity Geometry
- **Trine — Taurus (District 2):** shared material orientation and shared appetite for stability, profitability, and durable comfort; Emerson calls them complementary "in the most wonderful way." Taurus is also the corrective — "great at reining you in when you're going too hard and are at the risk of burning out."
- **Trine — Virgo (District 12):** instant mutual recognition, both cautious, both perfectionists; Silva: "you can never find a more hard-working and honest colleague." Division of labor is natural — Virgo takes day-to-day operations, Capricorn takes the long horizon. Risk: mutual suffocation, and Virgo burns out under Capricorn's standard.
- **Sextile — Scorpio (District 4):** the one relationship where trust runs deep. "The one sign in the zodiac that Scorpio can completely trust is Capricorn." Comfortable in silence together, shared dark humor, shared interest in digging up what really happened. Snodgrass's caution: these two "tend to tyrannize" when paired. Emerson: their danger is isolating together.
- **Sextile — Pisces (District 10):** the softening relationship. Pisces gets Capricorn to relax and open; Capricorn helps Pisces "articulate and materialize" its concepts. But Capricorn "will drain the magic out of Pisces," and its "simple disbelief" can wreck Pisces' faith in its own convictions.
- **Adjacent (semi-sextile) — Sagittarius and Aquarius:** mutual respect without comprehension. With Aquarius the odd fact is that both answer to **Saturn** in the traditional scheme yet want opposite things — freedom versus boundaries; Silva: "Capricorn precedes Aquarius, and there has to be some pressure for them to feel liberated."

## 10. Structural Position
- **Element — Earth** *(out-of-fiction temperament grouping only; unrelated to the project's in-fiction 8-element robot elemental system).* Behaviorally: sense-based realism, "less open to debate" than other modes of knowing; enduring, patient, tight-pursed; respects physical limits in principle but overworks in practice. Under stress, earth consumes and keeps going rather than flaring out.
- **Modality — Cardinal.** Initiating, goal-oriented, fast-tempo, self-motivated; "gotta get moving." Tierney's warning: cardinal signs "sometimes never know when to quit pushing." This is the sign of upward mobility and the ladder — "a strong instinct to climb."
- **Polarity — Negative / yin / receptive.** Energy directed inward and toward the material; reserved rather than projecting; the ambition is executed rather than announced.
- **Ruler — Saturn**, and specifically its **nocturnal** domicile (Aquarius is the diurnal one). Time, limitation, accountability, structure, and the fear of not being good enough. Silva's framing: this is where "Saturn's most authoritative and stern functions" get expressed and enacted. Martin's image: Saturn's rings as "protective moats surrounding a fortress." Tierney's distinction is useful for texture: Saturn "shies away from the limelight" and is "monkish," a "hard-working, blue-collar planet," whereas Capricorn "more actively creates the right climate for the recognition it craves" — the sign is *more socially ambitious than its own ruler*.
- **Houses — tenth, and (traditionally) twelfth.** The modern assignment is the **tenth house of career and public status**; Silva's summary card calls it the **House of Enterprise**, "which represents career, society, government, motivation, authority, and advantage." But the recovered chapter adds the traditional second placement, which is far more interesting and was entirely missing from the first pass: **the twelfth house is where Saturn finds its joy** — "one of confinement, isolation, and solitude," which classical astrologers "also called the house of a bad spirit," connected to "anxiety and shadows which can cause mental anguish," and felt as "being burdened with many concerns and responsibilities." Its redemption is stated in the same breath: the placement "is also linked to the theme of persevering until the defeat of all hurdles." The district's public face is the tenth house; its private weather is the twelfth.
- **Saturn return — 29.5 years.** The planet's orbit gives the temperament a hard, non-arbitrary age threshold: the return is "when you become your true self and gain control of your life," marking "true adulthood for a Capricorn in their late twenties." Silva's corollary is that the sign does not wait for it — "people from this sign feel obligated to work very hard from a very young age. They think that they must be responsible at every point in life." A culture with a real coming-of-age number that everyone starts serving two decades early.
- **Exaltation — Mars:** disciplined force at maximum effectiveness; strong initiative, strong libido, aggression that keeps its appointments. Silva's read is that this is Mars *subdued* rather than amplified — the placement "helps to subdue the rash qualities of Mars and allows its energy to be stabilized so its natives can persevere and endure well," producing "strategic and tactical minds."
- **Detriment — Moon:** the caretaking/feeling function at its structural worst. "More comfortable looking after others than being looked after." Silva supplies the mechanism and the failure state: a "protective barrier around them and their emotions," a long approach to vulnerability, "bouts of depression or sadness," and — the operative line for §3 — if wounded emotionally it is "prone to become cynical," after which "such experiences make it difficult for a Capricorn to trust people again."
- **Fall — Jupiter:** luck, faith, expansiveness, and open-handed generosity least available. Silva's phrasing is precise and useful: the result is not pessimism but **cautious optimism**, in which natives "find it difficult to believe that the universe is working in their favor or that they have good fortune." Countered by Martin's genuinely interesting positive reading: hard work and self-discipline are themselves "well recognised paths of faith," as in monastic traditions — this is a sign "where we are asked to 'bend the knee' to a higher authority."

## 11. Decans and cusps (candidate sub-neighborhood texture)
Emerson is the only decan source. Silva's recovered cusp chapter is a second, independent axis of intra-sign variation and is included below — the cusps are *not* decans (they are the sign's two borders, not thirds of its interior), but they serve the same design purpose and are considerably more usable for the Yards than the decans are.

### Decans (Emerson)
1. **First decan (Saturn-ruled, 0–10°):** "Your determination and patience are legendary. You are not the self-confident person others believe you to be, but your sheer capability and hardiness in everything you do are unrivaled." Can be "dictatorial, rigid, and inflexible," but keeps "a fine sense of humor" at the center. — The old core: hardest-working, least self-assured, most respected.
2. **Second decan (Venus-ruled, 10–20°):** "hard-working and patient, but you make time for rest, relaxation, and artistic pursuits. You slowly take in new experiences and can be hardcore about doing things correctly to see your projects all the way through." — The livable quarter: craft pride, aesthetics, actual leisure.
3. **Third decan (Mercury-ruled, 20–30°):** "a bit restless, but have a great need for efficiency and functionality. You are never impulsive, but you lack the patience that the first two Capricorn decans exhibit. You are highly intelligent, organized, and have a scientific mind. You make sure any evidence you see is empirically backed." — The metrics-and-instrumentation quarter; the people who would notice a calibration drift.

### Cusps — the two borders (Silva)

Silva treats these as full personality types with their own strengths, weaknesses, and compatibility, and is explicit that people on a cusp carry both signs at once: "inner conflict is a real issue in their personalities," and "such people have difficulty making decisions because their different traits are always clashing with each other." Read as district geography, these are the two *edges* of the Yards — the quarters that touch another district and are marked by it.

4. **Sagittarius–Capricorn border (Dec 18–24) — "the Cusp of Prophecy."** Jupiter over Saturn, and Silva insists the two are not really in conflict: "you have the best of both since they are not so contrasting as compared to the Capricorn–Aquarius cusp." The result is "not only a great visionary but also a practical realist who can inspire real changes" — fire's passion held up by earth's consistency, which "alone can die out quickly if not supplemented by the consistency and willpower of the Earth element." Socially this is the outlier quarter of an introverted district: "loyal, caring, and socially active," constantly surrounded by people who ask them for advice, and functioning as "a powerful leader and teacher." The failure mode is impatience with the slower — Silva's warning is "not everyone learns at the same pace" — plus a distaste for small talk and a standing risk that "isolation can happen inadvertently."
   — The Frostlands-facing quarter (District 11). Where the Yards keeps its optimists: the part of the district that still believes an expansion is fundable and can say so in the district's own vocabulary.
5. **Capricorn–Aquarius border (Jan 17–23) — "the Cusp of Mystery and Imagination."** Saturn against Uranus, and here Silva does call it a collision: "polarized and contrasting energies," "a hardworking idealist," "extremely grounded and determined" on one side and possessed of "a strong affection for spontaneity and variety" on the other. Strengths: "self-determination, creativity, wit, empathy, and idealistic thought processes." Weaknesses: "detachment, chaos, selfishness, critical thinking, and prejudice," a tendency to "close yourself off in your own world," and — uniquely in this sign — "the biggest procrastinators," a trait found nowhere else in the Capricorn material.
   The load-bearing passage is this one: "You can see the world for what it really is, and you are drawn to discuss its problems and find ways to fix it. While this gives you the ability to have intriguing and fascinating conversations, it can be isolating and intimidating when it comes to connecting with your close ones." And from the far side of the same cusp: they "hate the status quo, and desire to bring changes to the world," yet "on the one hand, these people like to hold on to customs and traditions while on the other hand, they want to transform the world around them" — they "tend to use their upbringing in a positive way and try to bring in changes using age-old traditions."
   — The Labs-facing quarter (District 7), and the most valuable new sub-neighborhood in the file. It describes, precisely, a group **native to the Yards, loyal to its customs, and nonetheless constitutionally able to see the status quo as a problem** — reform argued in the district's own traditional vocabulary rather than imported from outside. If a challenge to the Narrow Door originates internally rather than with the player or a Gemini archaeologist, this is the only quarter the corpus supports it coming from. It also supplies the reason such a movement stalls without outside help: procrastination, self-isolation, and conversations that intimidate the very neighbors they need.

## 12. Correspondences
- **Body / pathology:** the skeletal system, bones, skin, the body's structure and boundaries — and specifically **the knees**, which Martin reads as "an interesting symbol of our flexibility and of our ability to 'bend the knee' to a higher authority." Also the **chin**. Weak points "twinge from overwork, lifting, or a chill"; characteristic ailments are dislocated joints, knee degeneration, arthritis, gout, rheumatism, anemia, cough, dry mucosa, and **crystallization**. Snodgrass: cut off from its chief joy, it "falls into a decline that precipitates bodily ailments and despair."
- **Remedies:** knapweed, cinchona, elder, plantain, black hellebore; the cell salt **calcium phosphate** (bone salt); "milk is far better for the type than wine." Prescribed treatment is explicitly herd-animal treatment: "fresh air, broad-based diet, and social interaction."
- **Places ruled: fortresses.** (In the same list Scorpio gets caves and prison cells, Aquarius sewers and cesspools.) Terrestrial associations given: Albania, Bulgaria, the Orkney Islands, Oxford.
- **Shapes / forms:** the sea-goat — horned, fish-tailed, climbing a peak — which artists on "arras, heraldic shield, or pub sign" freely mutate into "a crocodile, dolphin, and sea serpent." The constellation is the smallest in the zodiac (fortieth in size) and among the oldest, "makes no spectacular display but has been called the smile in the sky."
- **Named stars (excellent signage/naming material):** Gieda, "the first goat," a multiple star colored **lilac, ash, and yellow**; Dabih, a double of one caerulean and one orange-yellow star, forming the goat's left eye; Armus, the heart; Bos, "the cow," on the face; Castra on the abdomen; Dorsum on the spine; Nashira and Deneb Algedi on the tail. The Hebrew name for the alpha star was **"the slain kid"**; the Arabic name for its cluster, **Alsa'd Aldhabih — "the lucky slaughterer."**
- **Colors — gap now closed.** Silva's introduction gives favorable colors as **brown, khaki, black, and purple**, and colors to avoid as **yellow and red**; her summary card compresses this to "**dark brown and black**." (Snodgrass's own color table is still cut — see §15 — but the values no longer have to be guessed.) The incidental star colors survive as accent material: Gieda's lilac/ash/yellow, Dabih's caerulean and orange-yellow. Note that the two avoid-colors are precisely the Leo and Aries palettes.
- **Stones, oil, chakra, tarot — gaps now closed.** Gemstone **blue sapphire** (Silva). **Tarot: The Devil** (Silva, stated directly — the first pass left this blank because Emerson's block was truncated). Crystal **jade**, essential oil **peppermint**, and chakra **root** are recovered from the known Emerson misalignment, where Capricorn's values print inside the Cancer block; see `04_Cancer.md` §15, which documents the same defect from the other side. All three sanity-check against earth and Saturn — jade is an earth stone and the root chakra is the grounding/survival/structure center — so the misfiling is safe to reverse here. **Metal and flower remain unattested.**
- **Numbers:** 1, 4, 8, 10, 13, 17, 19, 22 (Silva, "lucky numbers"). Usable for door, bay, shift, and workstation numbering.
- **Other:** the apostle Simon; the cardinal cross (with Aries, Cancer, Libra) associated with four archangels; the age band 63–70; the keyphrase **"I master,"** mode *perceptive*, faculty *discrimination*.

## 13. Myth, History & Cultural Associations
- **Pricus** (Silva) — the best myth in the slice for a district about legacy: a sea-goat who "was father to many sea-goats he tried to protect, and the story goes that Pricus failed to protect his children and was given a place to live in the sky after this great loss." A patriarch memorialized *for a failure of protection*.
  **The recovered chapter supplies the mechanism of that failure, and it is the single most on-target detail in the whole supplement.** Pricus could reverse time, and he used it: he "tried to protect the sea goats by turning back time when they wandered onto dry land and lost their ability to think and speak, but he had to give his children up to the wild." He kept resetting the clock to a configuration that no longer held, and every reset cost him the children again, until he stopped. Then he "made the constellation of Capricorn and gained an immortal home in heaven." Silva adds that this tale is "considered the reason why the melancholic and sad stereotype is associated with most Capricorns" — the sign's characteristic sadness is sourced, in its own myth, to *a father who could not accept that conditions had changed*. For a district whose core injustice is a founding-era calibration nobody ever revisited, this is not a loose thematic rhyme; it is the same story.
- **Saturn as Cronus** (Silva) — the planet "was named after the titan who tried remaining a ruler by eating his offspring." Held beside Pricus, the sign owns both halves of the generational problem: the father who could not save his children, and the father who consumed them to keep his seat. A district with a fixed number of master workstations and a master pool that alone can authorize an expansion is living inside the second one.
- **Pan and Typhon** (Snodgrass) — during the war with the giants Pan changed shape repeatedly to escape; leaping into the Nile, his upper half became a goat and his lower half a fish. Survival by improvised hybridization under attack.
- **Amaltheia** — the goat who nourished Zeus, whose horn was filled "with an inexhaustible abundance of fruits and vegetables"; Hercules presented that horn to the god of the underworld. The sign carries the cornucopia as much as the ladder: *provision earned by nursing something greater than yourself*.
- **Babylonian Ea**, god of subterranean waters, and the Tropic of Capricorn as "Ea's way." The sign is older than most: first observed 2449 B.C. Silva traces the lineage a step further back and a step wider: Ea is the Babylonian and Akkadian name for the Sumerian **Enki**, "the god of wisdom and water," later "the god of intelligence, magic, creation, and water" — and the sign's equivalent in Hindu mythology is **Makara**, represented by a crocodile rather than a goat. Three different civilizations put a *maker* at the bottom of deep water.
- **The Cancer polarity as a pair of named public spaces** (Silva): the axis is "known as Agora and Hestia in Greek philosophy" — the marketplace and the hearth, the world outside the door and the fire inside it. A cleaner one-line statement of the District 1 / District 6 opposition than anything in §8, and it comes with architecture attached.
- **Augustus.** His natal sign; his astrologer Theogenes proposed he adopt the horned goat; it flew "on the banners and standards of his personal legions," and in 19 B.C. he struck silver coins showing **Capricorn clutching the world's rudder.** An empire's founder chose the disciplined climber as his emblem.
- **Janus function.** As a calendar hinge the sign "surveyed the end of one year and the beginning of another," and therefore "represents gravity and sobriety, two emotions that assist self-assessment and the making of New Year's resolutions." Audit season made into a temperament.
- **Decay imagery.** Snodgrass: this sign "begins the process of decay or dissolution, which Pisces completes," and its planet's "divinity is represented by omega, the last letter of the Greek alphabet and a symbol of doom." Martin's cycle: the fruit forms in Sagittarius, **decomposes in Capricorn**; the corresponding alchemical stage "symbolises both putrefaction and fermentation." Both are true — rot and fermentation are the same process valued differently.
- **Cultural readings of the goat:** goodness and peace (Chinese); energy, divinity, and power (Hindu, Sumerian, Teutonic). Also "the sacred token of the moon goddess called Cybele or Selene and a promising sign to wayfarers."
- **The verse** (a 19th-century zodiac doggerel Snodgrass quotes) — worth keeping verbatim as a tonal target: *"Who climbs and schemes for wealth and place, / And mourns his brother's fall from grace— / But takes what's due in any case— / Safe Capricorn."*
- **Attributed voices and figures:** weighty, muscular prose with "economy of words and a sharp focus on substance"; Jack London named specifically, including "To Build a Fire," a story of a failed attempt to survive extreme cold, and characters "who succeed by extremes of self-control." Military exemplars offered: Stonewall Jackson, Robert E. Lee, Joan of Arc. In the Herculean labor scheme, Capricorn corresponds to the golden-horned stag — a labor of *capture*, not killing.

## 14. Design Hooks for District Use
1. **Patronage as sincere loyalty, not corruption.** The corpus supplies exactly the psychology the locked injustice composite needs: a temperament that "never hesitates to cozy up to the brass" *and does not experience this as phoniness* — Tierney calls it "a byproduct of its cautious disposition." Sponsors in the Yards should genuinely believe they promote on merit; the tell is that their merit judgments track the favor ledger. The supplement adds the sponsor's actual decision procedure, which is what was missing: Silva's account of how this temperament picks a partner is a description of selection on **legible credentials** — choosing "partners who appear to be perfect instead of taking time to see if they are really the right fit," running "business strategies" and "a premeditated view" over the choice, and only later learning that "someone who looks good and earns well is not necessarily the right partner if you just choose them based on these credentials." A master in the Yards should be able to state, honestly and in detail, the objective grounds for every sponsorship he ever made. Every one of those grounds should be a proxy.
2. **Visibility, not output, as the promotion currency — and the district's own advice literature says so.** Silva's remedy chapter tells the Capricorn to "work at looking more interested in their work," because "it is often seen that they are in a rush to leave when they get their work done even if colleagues are still at their desks," and changing that "will make people notice their dedication." Nobody is told to widen the observation window; the disadvantaged worker is told to stand inside it longer. That is **nAlpha** reproduced verbatim as sincere, well-meant career guidance. The Yards should have this in circulation as *helpful* material — a mentorship pamphlet, an apprentice's handbook, an older worker's kindly advice — and it should be the most damning single document in the district precisely because everyone who passes it along means well. It also gives the player an early, low-stakes artifact that reads as ordinary until the sponsorship records make it legible.
3. **The favor ledger as a physical artifact.** Nothing is given here; every favor is an entry that will be called. A sub-neighborhood, guild hall, or family that keeps literal obligation records is on-archetype and directly readable as evidence for the patronage layer.
4. **Exhaustion displayed as credential.** Snodgrass's briefcase-on-a-lapboard image translates straight into a worker who refuses medical stand-down. Bone/knee/joint degeneration is the sign's canonical pathology — a district where old workers walk badly and treat the limp as a service record.
5. **Calibration and time-keeping as identity, not bureaucracy.** Saturn is lord of time; Martin's suggested vocations are cartographer and clock maker; the third decan is the empirical, metrics-obsessed sub-flavor. The scheduling-drift layer of the injustice should sit inside the district's *proudest* institution, not a neglected one — the thing they measure best is the thing quietly measuring wrong.
6. **The ceiling that reads as prudence.** "Upholding the status quo may thus be done for defensive reasons, not simply for the purpose of stability." The infrastructure-ceiling layer is most on-archetype when residents defend it as caution, since revisiting it would require unearned faith — and faith is exactly what Jupiter's fall denies this temperament.
7. **Vindicated pessimism as the ambient civic mood.** The bus-stop vignette is the district's default emotional register: things fail, this was foreseen, and someone immediately opens a survey about it. Complaint here takes the form of procedure.
8. **Silence, not argument, as the local conflict style.** Two of this temperament in dispute do not fight — they go quiet and hold "a silent tournament that neither wins." Feuds in the Yards should be decades long, non-verbal, and invisible to outsiders. Silva's other note is a design gift: they only actually talk *while working on something together*.
9. **The Pricus register for local memorial culture.** Honor a founder for the children he failed to protect. A memorial to a preventable industrial disaster, maintained with total sincerity by the same institution that caused it, fits both the myth and the injustice. **Sharpened by the recovered myth:** Pricus's failure was not inattention, it was *turning back time* — repeatedly restoring a configuration that had stopped applying, at his children's expense, until he finally let it go. A founder-memorial in the Yards should honor a man who kept resetting something, and the district should not read that as the tragedy. Cronus, the sign's other father, supplies the counterweight (§13): a founder who kept his position by consuming his successors. One district, two founder statues, and the district can tell you why both were good men.
10. **"Born old, then young."** Tierney's children arrive already calculating; Emerson's adults get "increasingly playful, optimistic, and youthful" with age, and "inside of every serious Capricorn is a troublemaker." A district where the apprentices are grim and the retirees are the funniest people in the room — and where second-half-of-life joy is the reward structure.
11. **Recognition denial as the trigger for real bitterness.** The corpus is unambiguous that this temperament waits patiently and then collapses: "defeated by a cold, uncaring world," bitterness, depression. The district's dangerous figures should be people whose dues were paid and whose standing never arrived — not people who wanted a shortcut.
12. **Fortresses, and the moat.** The sign's ruled place is the fortress; Saturn's rings read as "protective moats surrounding a fortress." Architectural language for the Yards: enclosure, gatehouse, wall, permit, threshold — perimeter as civic virtue.
13. **Test-and-admit socialization.** Newcomers are held at a courteous distance until they show skill. A visible, culturally sanctioned proving process — and its shadow: outsiders who never pass it, since this temperament "distrusts 'outsiders' or those who threaten cultural cohesion."
14. **The timetable as a comfort object, and therefore untouchable.** "Continuity comforts them," and disruption to routine throws them "off balance" — from childhood. This is the missing emotional reason the founding-era calibration was never revisited: not neglect, not defense of an advantage, but the fact that in this district a schedule is what safety feels like. Anyone proposing recalibration is heard as proposing instability, by people who are not lying about that being how it feels. It also gives the district a sympathetic obstacle rather than a villainous one, which is what the "nobody's fault" brief requires.
15. **The adolescent as the only internal critic.** Silva: teenagers of this temperament turn "critical of you or others at home" out of "a strong sense of what is right and social justice." Combine with §11's Aquarius-border quarter — reformers who argue from the district's own traditions — and the Yards has a native, non-imported source of pressure on the Narrow Door, staffed entirely by people the district's own respect-must-be-earned logic gives no standing to. The complaint is available decades before anyone will hear it, which is a better and more painful shape than a district with no internal dissent at all.
16. **"They usually have a very good reason for doing so."** The one genuinely warm civic instinct in the supplement: this culture's default assumption about a rule-breaker is that the rule-breaker had a reason worth hearing, and the prescribed response is to ask. Hold it directly against the moral-policing shadow (§3) and it stops being a contradiction and becomes the district's live internal argument — the Yards as a place that will interrogate you fairly about why you broke a rule, and will not entertain the possibility that the rule is wrong.

- **Still open, and now the largest real gap — collective/crowd behavior.** Every source describes an individual, and the supplement did not change this: Silva's recovered chapters add a great deal about the Capricorn *child*, *friend*, *worker*, and *partner*, and nothing whatsoever about a Capricorn *population*. - **Real disagreements, left unflattened:**
  - Silva's *Sun and Moon Signs* contradicts itself: the sun-sign chapter says the best matches are water and earth and that air signs sour long-term, while the Moon chapter lists Capricorn Moon as compatible with "Air Moon signs (Aquarius, Gemini, Libra)" — after that same book names Gemini a poor match elsewhere.
  - Snodgrass contains a plain internal error, calling Capricorn "a water sign associated with fresh and salt water" in the same chapter whose own chart lists it as cardinal/earth/feminine. Almost certainly a slip about the sea-goat's tail and the Ea/subterranean-waters lineage, but worth knowing before quoting.
  - Silva's advice chapter says Capricorns "are in a rush to leave when they get their work done even if colleagues are still at their desks" — flatly against the workaholic portrait everywhere else in the corpus, including elsewhere in the same book. Usable as a genuine wrinkle: a culture that values *completion* over presenteeism, which would be a real and distinctive design choice for the Yards rather than a contradiction to resolve. **Upgraded by the full chapter, and now the single most valuable item in this list.** The line is not an isolated slip — it sits inside a paragraph that treats the behavior as a *defect to be corrected by performing visibility*: the sign "should work at looking more interested in their work," and changing the habit "will make people notice their dedication." So the corpus does not merely contradict itself about whether this temperament works late; it contains a completion-oriented worker and an observation system that rewards presence, and it sides with the observation system. That is the Narrow Door, stated by a sympathetic author as friendly advice. See §3 and §14.2.
  - **New — sociability, within a single book, six chapters apart.** Silva's strengths chapter opens: "the Capricorn has no trouble approaching strangers at parties and is comfortable chatting away to the staff at a store. They'll say yes when you propose to go out and don't have that 'I just want to stay home' default other signs have." Her friendship chapter says the reverse: "Capricorns are not the life of the party and like being in bed on time. They are not a fun friend." Every other source in the corpus backs the second version, so the first is the outlier — but it is a *useful* outlier, because it describes ease with strangers and service staff specifically, i.e. courteous public fluency without private closeness, which is exactly the §4 portrait. Recorded rather than resolved; the first reading is safe for public-facing Yards behavior, the second for private.
  - **New — a plain internal slip in Silva, two paragraphs apart.** The season paragraph says "the light and heat from the sun are at their lowest during this time of the year"; the mode paragraph immediately after says "the beginning of winter marks the Capricorn season when energy and light levels are at their highest." The first is correct (winter solstice) and is what the rest of the chapter's imagery depends on. Use the first; the second is an error.
  - **New — punctuality.** Silva calls this "the punctual Capricorn" when contrasting it with Pisces, and the child chapter concedes it "misses deadlines at times" because it likes "doing things in their own time and with their own approach." Reconcilable rather than contradictory, and the reconciliation is worth keeping: punctual to other people's appointments, sovereign over its own deliverables. See §6.
  - Tierney's "Born Old" versus Emerson's Benjamin Button reverse-aging read as opposed but are probably one arc, not two claims. Treated as a synthesis in §14.10; flagged here so it isn't mistaken for corroboration. Silva's Saturn-return material now supports the synthesis with a mechanism and a number: obligated to be responsible "from a very young age," reaching "true adulthood" only at the 29.5-year return.
