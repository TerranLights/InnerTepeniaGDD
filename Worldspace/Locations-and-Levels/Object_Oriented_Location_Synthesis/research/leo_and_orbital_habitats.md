# Low Earth Orbit and Rotating Orbital Habitats: physics and engineering research

Research date: 2026-10-06. Brief: `_BRIEF.md` (LAW 0-R; no figure from memory).

## 1. Scope

Low Earth orbit (LEO) as an environment, plus three engineering structure concepts: small robot-only staging stations (vacuum, solar power, shielding), O'Neill-cylinder-type rotating habitats, and Von Braun-wheel-type rotating wheels. Also orbit transfer delta-v and the published limits of space elevators and mass-flow towers. No societies or cultures.

## 4. Conflicts

**C1. O'Neill's own text versus his own Table 1 (same article).** Text: cylinders "about four miles in diameter, and perhaps about 16 miles in length," rotation period "two minutes." Table 1, Model 4: length 32 km (about 20 miles), radius 3200 m, period 114 s. Four miles of diameter is 6.4 km, so the radius agrees with 3200 m. At R = 3200 m the two periods give 0.895 g (120 s) and 0.991 g (114 s) (section 5). Likely reasons: the text rounds ("perhaps," "about"), and the 120 s figure is the nominal value; I could not tell which is intended. Neither is wrong as physics; both stay in the table.

**C2. Shielding mass for galactic cosmic rays: 4.5 t/m2, 10 t/m2, or a minimum near 20-40 g/cm2.** SP-413 (1975): 4.5 t/m2 (450 g/cm2) to reach 0.5 rem/yr in free space, and shows thin shielding of "a few tonnes" raises dose. Kalpana One (2007): 4.5 t/m2 "approximately," 10 t/m2 as a worst case below the Van Allen belts, and uses 10 t/m2 of regolith. Norman et al. (NASA Langley, 2017): model dose equivalent minimum near 20-40 g/cm2 of aluminum, rising beyond that. These differ in the dose target (0.5 rem/yr for a general population versus crew limits), geometry (slab versus enclosed habitat with shield on both sides), and model generation. The three do not contradict if the question is "how little can I get away with" (the Langley minimum) versus "what brings the dose to a general-population level" (SP-413). I have not reconciled the numerical gap of a factor of 10 or more.

**C3. ISS crew dose.** Cucinotta et al. (2008): 72 mSv per about 6 months (0.39 mSv/day), recorded in recent years of high solar activity. Shavers et al. (2023): 0.2 to 0.5 mSv/day over 20 years. These agree. A search summary attributed "80 mSv per six months at solar maximum and 160 mSv at solar minimum" to NASA/MSFC 2017; I did not open that page, so I treat that as unverified. If true it implies 0.44-0.88 mSv/day, higher than both measured values, and could reflect a different quantity (a design-basis value rather than a measurement) or epoch.

**C4. "Mass of the structure" for the Stanford torus.** Table 4-1(a) structural mass at 1/2 atm: 150 kt. Chapter 5: aluminum shell 156,000 t plus 48,000 t glass. Table 6-4: L5 colony structures 500,000 t. O'Neill's Model 1 total: 500,000 t. The first two count the pressure shell (and glass); the third counts everything fabricated at L5 including interior structures; O'Neill's 500,000 t counts soil, water and rock (420,000 t soil and construction material). Definitions, not errors.

**C5. Which shape is lightest: torus, cylinder, or sphere?** SP-413 Chapter 4 selects the torus because it "is economical in its requirements for structural and atmospheric mass" for a 10,000-person habitat; Chapter 7 says a Bernal sphere is "far more efficient in mass than the cylinder geometries" at a given diameter and takes shielding well. Globus et al. (2007) conclude the cylinder minimizes hull surface per unit of 1 g living area (stable cylinder about 1.45 to 1.77 versus at least pi for a torus), so it minimizes shielding mass. My derived total mass per floor area from SP-413's own Table 4-1(a) agrees with the cylinder view (14.8 t/m2 for the torus versus 1.19 t/m2 for the cylinder) but depends on how that table credits atmosphere and structure toward shielding. The disagreement is mostly objective function: structural mass versus shield mass, and habitat size. Kalpana One also gives 1.45 in the text and 1.77 in its appendix for the same ratio and says earlier papers had it as 2.2 and 1.45.

**C6. Rotation-rate limits.** Clark and Hardy 1960: about 0.1 rpm (from one subject; illusions threshold). Graybiel: 1 rpm symptom-free, 3 rpm tolerable, 10 rpm not fully adapted in 12 days. Hill and Schnitzer: under 4 rpm. Gilruth, Gordon and Gervais, NASA-STD-3000: under 6 rpm (with graduated exposure). SP-413: 1 rpm design cap. Kalpana One: 2 rpm, "not well supported." They differ in criterion (onset of illusions, nausea, adaptation time, performance), in subject counts, and in whether adaptation by gradual spin-up is allowed. Hall notes the trend toward looser limits from 1960 to 1980 partly reversed in 1983 (Cramer, 3 rpm).

**C7. Acceptable gravity range.** SP-413 Ch. 3: 0.9-1.0 g and design 0.95 +/- 0.05 g, "because there is no data" below 1 g; SP-413 Ch. 7: 0.7-1.0 g "assumed to be acceptable." Richter et al. (2017): below about 0.4 g seems insufficient for long-term musculoskeletal and cardiopulmonary health and no study of long exposure exists. These are an assumption, a design choice and a review of limited evidence; no measurement exists between 0.17 g (Apollo, days) and 1 g for long durations.

**C8. Launch cost.** SP-413 assumed $200/kg (1975 dollars) with a heavy-lift vehicle and $440/kg for the shuttle; O'Neill assumed $190-400/lb in 1972 dollars. Jones (2018, 2018 dollars) reports the shuttle at about $54,500/kg and Falcon 9 at about $2,720/kg. These differ because the earlier figures were projected costs of vehicles not built, while Jones divides total program cost by delivered mass.

**C9. Zimmerman's own losses.** The text says ascent losses "total about 4000 feet per second" and then uses 4,250 ft/s for the budget; at 30,000 ft/s total, 4,250 is consistent with the sum shown. I keep both quotes and treat the loss as about 1.2-1.3 km/s.

**C10. Atomic oxygen flux.** A search summary claimed an ISS-altitude flux of "1.0 x 10^14 atoms/cm2/s" with no source I could open. The only primary data I opened (MISSE-3/4 ram fluences of 1.2-2.14 x 10^21 atoms/cm2 over about one year) imply an average of roughly 4 to 7 x 10^13 atoms/cm2/s if exposed continuously for a year (section 5). The summary figure is not used.

**C11. Length of the longest Skylab mission.** SP-413 Ch. 3 says 89 days; Sibonga et al. list Skylab missions of 28, 56 and 84 days. Likely cause: a rounding or a different counting of mission days (launch to splashdown). Not material to any figure here.

## 5. Derived figures

All constants: GM = 398,600 km3/s2 and Earth equatorial radius 6,378.137 km (NASA Earth Fact Sheet); standard gravity g0 = 9.80665 m/s2 (stated on the same sheet); sidereal day 23.9345 h (same sheet); c = 299,792,458 m/s and sigma = 5.670374419 x 10^-8 W m^-2 K^-4 (NIST CODATA). Calculations were run in Python with these values.

**5.1 Circular orbit at 400 km.** r = 6,378.137 + 400 = 6,778.137 km. v = sqrt(GM/r) = sqrt(398,600/6,778.137) = 7.669 km/s. Period T = 2 pi sqrt(r^3/GM) = 5,553.6 s = 92.56 min. Orbits per day = 86,400 / 5,553.6 = 15.56. Basis: Keplerian circular orbit. (At 300 km: v = 7.726 km/s. At 350 km: T = 91.5 min.)

**5.2 Eclipse in a circular orbit, zero beta angle, cylindrical shadow.** Shadow half-angle = asin(Re/r) = asin(6,378.137/6,778.137) = 70.22 degrees; eclipse fraction = 70.22/180 = 0.390; eclipse time = 0.390 x 92.56 min = 36.1 min; sunlit = 56.5 min. Basis: geometric umbra only, no penumbra, no atmosphere refraction. Agrees with NASA JSC "up to 35 minutes." Eclipse shrinks and then vanishes as the beta angle increases (the TRMM paper reports 26 min at beta 58.5 degrees).

**5.3 Spin gravity (derivation).** A mass on the inner wall of a cylinder of radius R rotating at angular rate omega moves on a circle at speed v = omega R. Circular motion requires a net inward acceleration a = v^2/R = omega^2 R, supplied by the wall's normal force N = m omega^2 R, which the occupant feels as weight. So g_eff = omega^2 R (consistent with Hall 1994 eq. 3.16 as described in words). With omega = 2 pi / T: g_eff = 4 pi^2 R / T^2. Solving for the radius that gives g_eff = g0: R = g0 / omega^2.

| 1 rpm | 0.10472 | 894 m | 340 m | 148 m | 93.7 m/s |
| 2 rpm | 0.20944 | 224 m | 85 m | 37 m | 46.8 m/s |
| 3 rpm | 0.31416 | 99 m | 38 m | 16 m | 31.2 m/s |

(0.38 g and 0.165 g are NASA's Mars-like and Moon-like surface values: Moon surface gravity 1.62 m/s2 on the NASA Moon Fact Sheet is 0.165 g; the Mars value 0.38 is quoted from Richter et al.) Rim speed = sqrt(g R) for 1 g.

**5.4 Gravity gradient.** Weight scales with distance from the axis, so for a person of height h standing at floor radius R the head-to-foot fractional drop is h/R. For h = 1.8 m: R = 24.8 m, 7.3%; 55.9 m, 3.2%; 99.4 m, 1.8%; 224 m, 0.8%; 894 m, 0.20%; 3,200 m, 0.06%. (Gilruth's 1968 limit of 15% for a 6-ft person corresponds to R of about 12 m; I used 1.8 m for the person, not 6 ft.)

**5.5 Coriolis acceleration.** a_C = 2 omega v for a velocity v perpendicular to the axis (radial motion). For v = 1 m/s: 1 rpm 0.21 m/s2 (2.1% of g0); 2 rpm 0.42 m/s2 (4.3%); 3 rpm 0.63 m/s2 (6.4%); 0.5 rpm (R = 3,200 m) 0.105 m/s2 (1.07%). Check of the SP-413 jump example: v0 = sqrt(2 g h) = sqrt(2 x 9.80665 x 0.55) = 3.284 m/s; flight time t = 2 v0/g = 0.670 s; lateral displacement x = 2 omega (v0 t^2/2 - g t^3/6) = 2(0.10472)(0.7367 - 0.4911) = 0.0514 m at 1 rpm. SP-413 states 5.3 cm; agreement within 4%.

**5.6 O'Neill Table 1 gravity.** g = 4 pi^2 R / T^2: Model 1 (R 100 m, T 21 s) 0.913 g; Model 2 (320 m, 36 s) 0.994 g; Model 3 (1000 m, 63 s) 1.014 g; Model 4 (3200 m, 114 s) 0.991 g; text value (3200 m, 120 s) 0.895 g. Rim speed for Model 4: 176 m/s (O'Neill's inter-cylinder vehicle speed of 180 m/s matches this scale).

**5.7 Other published geometries.** SP-413 torus R 830 m at 1 rpm: 0.928 g, rim speed 86.9 m/s (SP-413 states 87 m/s). SP-413 sphere R 895 m at 1 rpm: 1.001 g. SP-413 relaxed torus R 209 m at 1.9 rpm: 0.84 g (stated 0.85 +/- 0.15 g). Von Braun 125 ft = 38.1 m: 1/3 g needs 2.80 rpm; 3 rpm gives 0.383 g. Noordung R 15 m at 8 rpm: 1.07 g. Tsiolkovsky 50 m at 2 rpm: 0.22 g (the "less than 1/4 g" reported by Hall). Kramer and Byers 49 ft at 7.5 rpm: 0.94 g. Kalpana One R 250 m at 2 rpm: 1.12 g (the 1 g minimum at 2 rpm is 224 m).

**5.8 Thin rotating ring limit.** Hoop stress in a free thin ring: sigma = rho v^2, so v_max = sqrt(sigma/rho), independent of radius (as stated in NASA TM-85686). Check of the TM's example: sigma = 27.7 x 10^6 lb/ft2 = 1.326 x 10^9 Pa (47.88 Pa per lb/ft2); rho = 95 lb/ft3 = 1,522 kg/m3 (16.018 per lb/ft3); v = sqrt(1.326 x 10^9 / 1,522) = 934 m/s = 3,063 ft/s (TM: 3,060 ft/s). For O'Neill's boron-aluminum (yield 322,000 psi = 2.22 x 10^9 Pa; density 4.1 g/cc = 4,100 kg/m3): v = sqrt(2.22e9 / 4,100) = 736 m/s; the radius at which 1 g needs that rim speed is v^2/g0 = 55 km (a self-weight limit for a ring with no load; real hulls also carry air, soil and shield, so the usable radius is much smaller). O'Neill's design, for scale, uses steel at 152,000 psi and R = 3,200 m with 7.5 t/m2 of hull-plus-load.

**5.9 SP-413 mass per area (Table 4-1a).** Torus: 150 x 10^6 kg / 6.8 x 10^5 m2 = 221 kg/m2 structure; cylinder: 42,300 x 10^6 kg / 5.5 x 10^7 m2 = 769 kg/m2 (3.5 times). Adding shield: torus (150 kt + 9,900 kt) / 6.8 x 10^5 m2 = 14.8 t/m2 (1,005 t per person at 10,000 people); cylinder (42,300 kt + 23,300 kt) / 5.5 x 10^7 m2 = 1.19 t/m2 (80 t per person at 820,000 people). The shield per unit of surface is 4.7 t/m2 for the torus (9.9 Mt / 2.1 x 10^6 m2) and 0.39 t/m2 for the cylinder (23.3 Mt / 60.3 x 10^6 m2), which is why the cylinder's tabulated shield is small: I could not find the credit rule in the appendices I opened. Torus floor strip check: 2 pi (830 m)(130 m) = 6.78 x 10^5 m2 (table: 6.8 x 10^5). Cylinder floor check: 2 pi (895)(8,950) = 5.03 x 10^7 m2 (table: 55 x 10^6, which adds the end caps).

**5.10 Earth-launched fraction in SP-413.** 156.7 kt from Earth / (500,000 t structures + 10,000,000 t shield + 272,600 t interior) = 156.7 / 10,772.6 kt = 1.45%. Basis: Tables 6-4 and 6-7.

**5.11 Orbit transfer Delta-v (vis-viva, two impulses).** Geostationary radius from sidereal day: r_GEO = (GM T^2 / 4 pi^2)^(1/3) = 42,164 km (altitude 35,786 km). Coplanar Hohmann from 400 km: 2.397 + 1.456 = 3.854 km/s; from 300 km: 2.426 + 1.467 = 3.893 km/s; transfer time 5.29 h (to the half-period). With a 28.5-degree plane change at apogee (from 300 km) 4.256 km/s versus Oleson's 4.234 km/s. Edelbaum low-thrust spiral, coplanar: sqrt(v0^2 + vf^2 - 2 v0 vf cos(0)) = v0 - vf = 7.726 - 3.075 = 4.651 km/s; with 28.5 degrees: sqrt(v0^2 + vf^2 - 2 v0 vf cos(pi/2 x 28.5 deg)) = 5.951 km/s versus Oleson's 5.958 km/s. Plane change of 28.5 degrees at GEO circular speed 3.075 km/s costs 1.51 km/s if done separately at GEO; 51.6 degrees would cost 2.68 km/s. Transfer to the lunar distance (apogee 384,400 km) from 400 km: 3.082 + 0.829 = 3.911 km/s, 4.98 days (SP-413: 4,100 m/s to lunar orbit, "about 5 days"). Escape from a 400 km circular orbit: (sqrt(2) - 1) x 7.669 = 3.176 km/s.

**5.12 Propellant mass ratio (Tsiolkovsky).** m0/mf = exp(Delta-v / (Isp g0)) with the specific impulses stated by Oleson. Chemical storable 340 s: exhaust speed 3.334 km/s; Delta-v 4.234 km/s gives ratio 3.56 (72% of departure mass is propellant). Cryogenic 460 s: 4.511 km/s; ratio 2.56 (61%). Hall thruster 2,000 s: 19.61 km/s; Delta-v 5.958 km/s gives ratio 1.355 (26%). Basis: single burn, no staging, no dry-mass fraction.

**5.13 Light time.** 400 km / c = 1.334 ms. Geostationary altitude 35,786 km / c = 119.4 ms; ground to GEO relay to LEO craft in the best geometry is at least about this long each leg, so about 239 ms there and back at minimum (path lengths in practice are longer). Moon 384,400 km / c = 1.282 s.

**5.14 ISS drag Delta-v.** Acceleration = F / m with m = 419,725 kg (NASA): 0.3 N gives 7.15 x 10^-7 m/s2 = 22.6 m/s per year; 1.1 N gives 2.62 x 10^-6 m/s2 = 82.7 m/s per year. Basis: the 1998 drag prediction applied to today's mass, so it is an order-of-magnitude indicator only.

**5.15 Atomic oxygen average flux.** Fluence / exposure time with an assumed continuous one-year exposure (3.156 x 10^7 s): 1.2 x 10^21 gives 3.8 x 10^13 atoms/cm2/s; 2.14 x 10^21 gives 6.8 x 10^13 atoms/cm2/s. The exposure duration was "about one year," not exactly.

**5.16 Radiator sizing in vacuum.** Radiated flux per unit emissivity: sigma T^4 = 5.670e-8 x T^4: 275 K (ISS ammonia supply temperature) 324 W/m2; 300 K 459 W/m2; 350 K 851 W/m2 (one face, perfect view of cold space, emissivity 1). ISS panel "1 kW for a 12 x 4 ft panel" is 4.46 m2, or 224 W/m2 per planform square meter (one cited figure, low confidence). Thermal power P needs area A = P / (epsilon sigma T^4): at 1 MW and 300 K with epsilon = 1, A = 2,180 m2 for one face.

**5.17 Space-elevator tether loading.** Tension per unit density along a tether from the surface (R_E) to GEO (r_G): the effective potential difference is the integral of (GM/r^2 - omega^2 r) dr = GM(1/R_E - 1/r_G) - omega^2 (r_G^2 - R_E^2)/2 = 48.42 MJ/kg (numerical integral agrees). For an exponential taper with area ratio A_max/A_0 = exp(rho x 48.42e6 / sigma), required strength per density sigma/rho = 48.42e6 / ln(taper): taper 2: 69.9; 3: 44.1; 5: 30.1; 10: 21.0 MN m/kg. Basis: tether supported at both ends in the equatorial plane, uniform strength, no payload, no tether self-mass beyond GEO accounted separately (a counterweight or extension past GEO is needed to hold tension; the exact taper includes that part).

**5.18 Self-supporting column.** h = sigma / (rho g0). Brick (70 MN/m2, 2,000 kg/m3): 70e6 / (2,000 x 9.80665) = 3,569 m (the course text: "over 3.5 km"). The built maximum is 829.8 m to tip (CTBUH). For a uniform column of height h the required strength per density is g0 h: 100 km needs 9.80665 x 100,000 = 0.98 MJ/kg (MN m/kg), 28 times the brick value of 0.035 MJ/kg (70e6 / 2,000), and a tapered column grows exponentially in area with exp(rho g h / sigma). A tether to GEO needs 21-70 MJ/kg (5.17).

**5.19 Atmosphere mass reference.** SP-413 and Kalpana both use Earth's 10 t/m2 atmospheric column; NASA gives total atmosphere 5.1 x 10^18 kg and I did not recompute. O'Neill's equivalent pressure 1.23 x 10^5 N/m2 minus the soil load (1.5 g/cc x 150 cm = 2,250 kg/m2, x 9.80665 = 22.06 kPa) leaves 101 kPa, which matches NASA's surface pressure of 1014 mb.

**5.20 Unit cross-check, rem to sievert.** By definition 1 rem = 0.01 Sv = 10 mSv. SP-413's unshielded free-space galactic dose of about 10 rem/yr is 100 mSv/yr = 0.27 mSv/day; its 0.5 rem/yr target is 5 mSv/yr = 0.014 mSv/day. The ISS crew measurements are 0.2-0.5 mSv/day (A2). These are the same order of magnitude despite the ISS sitting inside the geomagnetic field; the quantities differ (dose equivalent in a 1975 model versus effective dose measured on crew), so the comparison is a plausibility check only.

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

Each bullet is a condition a settlement in this environment has to cope with, tied to the findings above.

- **Heat leaves only by radiation.** In vacuum there is no convection or conduction to a medium; all waste heat must reach radiators and leave as infrared (A1, B). At 300 K a one-sided blackbody radiator sheds about 459 W/m2, so area scales with power (section 5.16). Radiators must avoid facing the Sun and the Earth, and the ISS rotates its radiators to seek shade. A machine or body inside a pressurized module has no convection in microgravity either (Astrobee finding, A1).
- **Each orbit is about 93 minutes with about 36 minutes of darkness at 400 km** (A1), so solar-powered systems need storage and take large thermal cycles; arrays degrade slowly (0.15-0.45 percent per year measured at the ISS, B).
- **Radiation is a continuous dose plus bursts.** At the ISS: about 0.2-0.5 mSv per day, mostly galactic cosmic rays, with a dose rate that roughly doubles between 360 and 460 km and a trapped-proton zone (the South Atlantic Anomaly) crossed several times a day; the outer electron belts reach the orbit at high latitude for about a fifth of each orbit; large solar events can reach as much as 40 percent of the orbit (A2). Free-space solar event doses fall by an order of magnitude between 1 and 10 g/cm2 of aluminum for the 1972 event (A2). Radiation shielding is not simply "more is better" for galactic cosmic rays (A2, D6).
- **Electronics see cumulative dose and random single-event upsets.** Example LEO year 2 krad(Si), a polar year 6 krad(Si), ISS background heavy-ion fluence about 9 per cm2 per year; destructive latchup and burnout exist for any complex part (A2, B).
- **Atomic oxygen erodes polymers on forward-facing surfaces** at about 10^-24 cm3 per atom, with fluence of 1-2 x 10^21 atoms/cm2 per year on a ram surface (A3).
- **Debris between about 1 and 10 cm is the main hazard** at ISS altitude; shields stop about 1 cm and below, tracked objects (10 cm and larger) can be avoided by maneuvering; debris dominates meteoroids from 250 to about 4,000 km (A3).
- **Drag is a continuous tax.** The ISS needs periodic reboost, 10-20 kg per month difference between lower and higher altitudes, drag of 0.3-1.1 N, and its attitude changes the drag (A4). Altitude trades drag against dose, debris and light-time.
- **Round-trip signal delays are milliseconds to a quarter second** for ground and relay paths in LEO, and 1.3 s one way to the Moon (5.13).
- **Microgravity degrades bone, muscle and the cardiovascular system on a months timescale** even with about 2 hours per day of exercise (C). Bone averaged 2-9 percent loss over 4-6 months; calf muscle volume fell 13 percent; recovery of bone took on the order of 9 months to half of the loss. The effect of fractional gravity is not measured over long durations (C).
- **Artificial gravity by rotation sets coupled limits.** Radius R = g/omega^2 (5.3). Higher spin rates shrink the structure but raise Coriolis forces, cross-coupled head-motion effects and gravity gradients; published comfort limits run from 1 to 6 rpm and are weakly supported (C, 5.4, 5.5).
- **Radiation shielding mass dominates a large habitat.** Published figures: 4.5 t/m2 for a general-population dose and 10 t/m2 as an Earth-equivalent; in SP-413 the shield was 98 percent of the torus mass (9.9 Mt of about 10.1 Mt) (D3, D6). The shield cannot rotate with the habitat at the same rate for large masses without exceeding material strength, so a rotating hull has little clearance to a fixed shield, which Globus et al. call a catastrophic failure mode (D6, D5).
- **A rotating hull is a pressure vessel plus a spinning ring.** Stress in a free ring depends on rim speed squared over specific strength; air pressure and loads add stress; mass of the wall grows with the radius of curvature (D1, D6).
- **A long cylinder is rotationally unstable by itself.** A single thin-shell cylinder with flat end caps is stable only for length up to about 1.3 radii; longer needs paired counter-rotation or active control (D2, D5).
- **Building a large habitat needs bulk material from the Moon or asteroids.** Of a first O'Neill colony, 2 percent of the mass came from Earth by plan; SP-413 lists 156.7 kt launched from Earth for a roughly 10.8 Mt colony (D7).
- **Moving a built structure is slow or propellant-hungry.** Chemical transfers take hours to days but need mass ratios of 2.6-3.6 for LEO to GEO; electric transfers take months; changing a spin axis or precessing it costs torque proportional to the angular momentum (E, 5.12).
- **Mechanisms in vacuum need vapor-pressure-proof lubricants and heating or insulation**; a heated, insulated actuator pays a mass and power cost, and unaccounted bearing forces killed an ISS coolant pump (B).
- **No propulsion from limbs in vacuum.** A human-shaped machine in vacuum or microgravity has no air to push against; it can only translate by contact with structure or by expelling mass (general physics, Newton's third law; no source needed).

## 7. Candidate handwaves

Flagged only; none accepted. The developer decides.

1. **A free-standing or compression-supported tower that reaches orbital altitude.** For any uniform column, h = sigma/(rho g); a brick tower fails at about 3.6 km and the tallest structure built is 830 m. Reaching 100 km with a uniform column needs strength per density of about 0.98 MJ/kg (derived, 5.18), 28 times the brick example, with exponential growth of cross-section for a taper; I read no strength-to-density value for any structural material that I can set against this, so I cannot say which real material would or would not suffice (open thread). The published routes are tension tethers (needs about 20-70 MN m/kg depending on taper, derived), active structures (launch loop, space fountain: power and mass-flow systems, not passive), or inflatable towers (Bolonkin, JBIS 2003, non-mainstream, not verified). A tower the setting treats as part of its geography needs one of these to be named.
2. **Material strength for a space elevator.** The 2003 concept report said a 100 GPa-class material "will be available in 2 years." I could not find evidence of that delivery; open thread. Any elevator in the setting is a material-science assumption.
3. **Plasma or magnetic radiation shields.** SP-413 calls the plasma core shield "speculative" (15 billion volts, 10^13 J stored); magnetic shields with low cutoff worsen dose through secondaries, and high cutoffs need structure that "precludes this design."
4. **Near-frictionless bearings between a rotating cylinder and a fixed frame.** O'Neill's 16 km-radius cylinders assume them (and 300,000 psi, 8 t/m2).
5. **Cheap launch to LEO and bulk lunar or asteroid supply on the assumed schedule.** O'Neill and SP-413 assumed $190-400/lb (1972) and $200/kg (1975); real shuttle cost was about $54,500/kg and Falcon 9 about $2,720/kg (2018 dollars). The mass-driver and L5 industrial base are unbuilt. The setting's launch-cost claim should be a stated assumption.
6. **Long-term health at fractional gravity or high spin rates.** No long exposure data exist at 0.17-0.9 g; below about 0.4 g the evidence is hedged; rotation above about 3-6 rpm is poorly tolerated without graduated adaptation. Choosing a spin rate or gravity level beyond the tested range is an assumption (C).
7. **A light, mobile wheel that also carries people and robots over long distances with minimal shielding.** The wheel is light only without the shield; SP-413's torus carried a 9.9 Mt shield, and a solar particle event like August 1972 gives crew 30.9 rem behind 10 g/cm2 of aluminum. A transit wheel above the belts must either carry shielding of that order (a few tonnes per square meter of crew-occupied surface for GCR, or a 10 g/cm2-class storm shelter for solar events) or accept dose. "Light" and "carries people over long distances" pull against each other (A2, D3, D4).
8. **"Low-tech" robot-only stations that survive indefinitely.** The environment requires radiators, radiation-tolerant or screened electronics with some mitigation of single-event latchup, vacuum-rated lubricants and thermal control for every mechanism (B). If "low-tech" means no radiators, heaters, shielded electronics or lubricated joints, it breaks physics. If it means robust but simple parts, it is allowed.
9. **Fast movement of a very large structure.** Hohmann transfers take hours to days but need mass ratios 2.6-3.6 for LEO to GEO at chemical specific impulse; scaled to megaton structures that is megatons of propellant. Low-thrust moves take months to decades (Oleson, SP-413). "Fast and cheap" requires an unspecified propulsion advance.
10. **Keeping a rotating structure pointed and spun while thrusting.** O'Neill's design relied on paired cylinders with zero net angular momentum and a compression tower; Kalpana One needs wobble control. A single spinning wheel under thrust has gyroscopic coupling to the thrust direction; no source I read quantifies it for a wheel (open thread).
