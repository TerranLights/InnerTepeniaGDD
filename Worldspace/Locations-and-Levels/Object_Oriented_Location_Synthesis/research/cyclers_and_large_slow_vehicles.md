# Cyclers and large, slow vehicles: physics and engineering data

## 1. Scope

Cycler and semi-cycler trajectories (Earth-Mars, Earth-Moon, Earth-Venus-Mars, planetary-moon systems), the taxi or ferry
problem at a cycler flyby, and large rotating structures used as slow vehicles (spin, thrust, loads, propulsion, transit
time); long-transit habitat shielding and mass; slow bulk cargo (electric tugs, sails, tethers); end-point infrastructure.

Evidence levels used in every table: **DEMONSTRATED** (flown or built), **ENGINEERING-STUDIED** (a quantitative design
study), **SPECULATIVE** (concept without a quantitative study, or extrapolation beyond any study).

## 4. Conflicts

1. **Aldrin cycler Mars V-infinity.** Nock 2003: Astrotel flyby speeds "7 to 12 km/s for Mars." Secondary pages (rows 8, 28) quote 9.75 and 10.69 km/s, and one says "in excess of 11.7." Likely cause: the Aldrin cycler is not exactly periodic in a real ephemeris; the Mars encounter speed changes from one synodic period to the next over the 15-year cycle, and single numbers come from different models (circular coplanar vs. real ephemeris) and different epochs. Landau and Longuski's averaged cycler (210-day limit, with DSMs) has a Mars arrival V-infinity of 5.8 km/s, because DSMs are traded for lower V-infinity. **The range, not a single value, is the defensible statement.**
2. **Propellant saving from V-infinity leveraging.** Rogers et al. 2012: 22.7 t for a 70 t cycler. A search summary of Rogers's later dissertation (row 78, secondary) quotes "as large as 24 metric tons ... for a cycler vehicle with 75 metric tons dry mass." Different assumed dry mass (70 vs 75 t); consistent in proportion (about 0.32-0.33 t per t of dry mass).
3. **Taxi shielding within the same NIAC report.** Nock 2003 gives the taxi crew module "about 3-cm thick polyethylene" (sec. 4.4.4 text), "~10 cm polyethylene" (sec. 4.4.4 assumptions), "equivalent to 10 cm water" (sec. 4.3.3) and "TBD [10] cm of water" (requirement 3.3.3.3). The report itself calls 3 cm "less than suggested thickness." Treat taxi shielding as unresolved, between about 3 and 10 g/cm2.
4. **What shielding level a long-transit habitat needs.** TM-4167 (1990): 3.5 g/cm2 of water for 0.5 Sv/yr BFO at solar minimum. Nock (2003) requirement: 30 cm of water (30 g/cm2), but designed as a storm shelter of about 31 m2 (section 5.6). SP-413 (sibling): 450 g/cm2 for 0.5 rem/yr (100 times lower dose). The differences are the dose target (worker or crew limit vs general population), the model generation, and whole-hull vs shelter-only protection.
5. **Solar maximum vs minimum dose.** TM-4167 Table I: unshielded BFO dose equivalent 45.1 cSv/yr at solar maximum vs 120.6 at solar minimum. Not a conflict between sources but an epoch effect of about 2.7 times at zero shielding, falling to about 2.1 times at 40 g/cm2; any single "annual GCR dose" must name the solar-cycle phase.
6. **Taxi rendezvous transit time.** Nock: up to 7-10 days in a small taxi; Genova and Aldrin (Earth-Moon): assumed one day. Different systems (interplanetary flyby at 5-12 km/s vs Earth-Moon cycler) and different design choices; both are design assumptions, not measured limits.

## 5. Derived figures

**5.1 Synodic period from the 15-year repeat.** 14.95 yr / 7 = 2.136 yr, matching "2 1/7 year" (2.143 yr) within 0.4%.

**5.2 Rocket-equation check on Rogers Table 6.** Propellant = m_dry (exp(Delta-v / (Isp g0)) - 1), m_dry = 70 t, Isp 450 s, g0 = 9.80665 m/s2. Aldrin 4:3(2)- direct, 4.510 km/s: 70 x (e^1.0220 - 1) = 124.5 t (table 124.5). S1L1, 3.806 km/s: 95.8 t (table 95.8). U0L1, 5.845 km/s: 193.2 t (table 193.2). The table is internally consistent.

**5.3 Spin energy of a large habitat.** For a body with angular momentum L spinning at omega: E = L omega / 2. O'Neill: L = 1.5 x 10^18 kg m2/s, period 114 s, omega = 0.0551 rad/s, so I = L/omega = 2.72 x 10^19 kg m2 and E = 4.13 x 10^16 J. O'Neill's "560,000 horsepower" over 3 years: 560,000 x 745.7 W x 9.47 x 10^7 s = 3.95 x 10^16 J (4% low; consistent). Rim speed and spin kinetic energy per tonne of rim mass at about 1 g: R = 56 m at 4 rpm: 23.5 m/s, 0.28 MJ/t; R = 250 m at 2 rpm: 52.4 m/s, 1.37 MJ/t; R = 830 m at 1 rpm: 86.9 m/s, 3.78 MJ/t; R = 3,200 m at a 114 s period: 176 m/s, 15.6 MJ/t. Basis: all mass at the rim (an upper bound).

**5.4 Thrust load compared with spin load.** A spin-gravity structure already carries its contents at about 1 g (9.8 m/s2) radially. A linear acceleration a adds a load of a/g of that, along the axis. At the electric-propulsion accelerations of 5.5 (2.5 x 10^-5 g or less), the axial load is about 10^-5 of the spin load. This is consistent with Joosten's analysis, where the spin case is "clearly the design driver" and a 100 N main-thrust load is minor. Basis: rigid structure, quasi-static thrust; it does not cover thrust transients, which Coyner (sibling) says "could generate loads exceeding steady-state conditions," or local load paths at the thrust attachment.

**5.5 Transit times for large masses under electric propulsion.** Thrust per unit power for an electric thruster: T/P = 2 eta / (g0 Isp). With eta = 0.6 (Joosten) and Isp = 5,000 s (Nock, Joosten): 24.5 mN per kW (40.8 at 3,000 s; 61.2 at 2,000 s). Propellant m_p = M0 (1 - exp(-Delta-v / v_e)), v_e = 49.0 km/s; burn time t = m_p v_e / T. Delta-v values: 1 km/s (a modest orbit change) and 6.4 km/s (LEO to Earth escape by low-thrust spiral, SP-428 IV-2).

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

- A cycler never stops: everyone and everything boards and leaves by taxi on a hyperbolic rendezvous, within days of departure, at relative speeds of about 3-8 km/s at Earth and 3-12 km/s at Mars depending on the cycler and the year (A, B). Missing the window means waiting a full synodic period (about 2.14 years) for the next pass (A, F).
- Taxi time is spent in a small, lightly shielded craft (2-3 m3 per person, 3-10 g/cm2) under up to about 5 g during aerocapture (B, Conflicts 3).
- On the Aldrin-type cycler the vehicle is empty for most of its orbit (more than 20 of 26 months in the NIAC design); on Earth-Moon cyclers there is an Earth pass every 7-10 days (F).
- A long-transit habitat without artificial gravity needs a storm shelter usable on as little as 30 minutes of warning; whole-hull shielding at worker-limit levels costs tens to hundreds of tonnes for a 10-50 m habitat, and at general-population levels thousands to over 10^5 t (D, 5.6).
- A spinning vehicle can thrust and steer without despinning at low thrust, but its steering is slow (about 2 deg/day cruise, 10-15 deg/day near planets) and large turns use rotation about the minor axis (C).
- A cylinder spinning about its long axis is passively stable only if it is short (length up to about 1.3 times radius with flat endcaps); longer cylinders need active control or a counter-rotating partner; all large rotating habitats need active wobble control as mass moves inside (C).
- Any large, slow vehicle moved by electric propulsion experiences accelerations of 10^-5 g or less: imperceptible, but transfers of 10^6 t or more take years to centuries at gigawatt power (5.5).
- Reaction-mass exhaust from a mass-driver engine or pellet launcher is a navigation hazard; it must be powder, liquid, or aimed so that it does not stay in orbit (C, E).

## 7. Candidate handwaves

1. **A spinning O'Neill-class or Stanford-torus-class habitat (10^5-10^7 t, rotating mass) as a cycler or as a vehicle that changes orbit on a useful timescale.** No engineering study was found that sizes this. The physics allows it (5.4, Kalpana stability rule), but at today's or projected electric-propulsion figures, 10^6 t needs about 10 GW of power and 0.1-0.8 years of thrusting per 1-6.4 km/s, with 2-12% of its mass as propellant and a 40,000-80,000 t power plant (5.5). Getting one onto an Earth-Mars cycler needs 3.5-5.8 km/s from LEO (Rogers, impulsive) — that is, 7-11% of its mass in propellant at Isp 5,000 s, or 35-52% with an 8 km/s rock-throwing mass driver; a low-thrust spiral needs more (6.4 km/s just to escape Earth, SP-428). **Flag: requires either multi-gigawatt in-space power and decades of construction lead time, or building the habitat already on the cycler orbit from material delivered there (as Simeoni and Casalino do for 1,256 t of asteroid shielding). The developer decides.**
2. **10^9 t-class structures moving at all.** Even with 10 GW, 1 km/s takes about 128 years (5.5). Treat such structures as effectively immobile.
3. **Solar sails with lightness ratio above 1 for bulk cargo** (SP-428's "lightness ratios ... of 5 or more"). A lightness ratio of 5 means the sail's radiation force is five times the Sun's gravity on it; no flown sail approaches this (NEA Scout: 86 m2, belt sibling). Treat sail-borne megaton cargo as SPECULATIVE.
4. **Full-scale rotating momentum-exchange tethers (skyhooks, rotovators, MXER).** Engineering-studied only; tether strength, debris survival and catch mechanisms are open requirements (MXER), and only small demonstrations were proposed (E). Using them as routine infrastructure is an extrapolation, not a handwave of physics.
5. **Mass drivers as reaction engines.** Studied in 1977 in detail (SP-428), never built; SCR (switch) mass is the stated problem for high-speed engines (C).
6. **Partial gravity sufficient for health.** Joosten: "essentially no data"; the study assumed 1 g. Any design that relies on lunar- or Mars-level spin gravity for long-term health is outside the evidence (D).
