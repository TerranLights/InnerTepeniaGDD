## 4. Conflicts

**C1. Altitude gained per unit of reboost delta-v (ISS, 2025).** NASA Glenn PIMS: 1.00 m/s gave "just under 1 km" (17 Mar 2025), 1.00 m/s gave about 1.68 km (14 Aug 2025), 0.30 m/s gave about 0.5 km (19 Jun 2025). That is 0.95, 1.68 and 1.67 km per m/s. A circular-orbit estimate gives 1.77 km per m/s (section 5 D2). Likely cause: the March figure is the flight controllers' planning number for a burn that was part of a phasing sequence, or it was rounded down; the report does not say. Use 1.7 km per m/s for planning, and treat the March value as unexplained.

**C2. ISS beta-angle cycle: 60 days or 72 days.** AMS-02 Collaboration: beta varies on "the 60-day precession period." A search summary quoted "about 72 days" for the westward shift of the orbit plane (not opened). These are two different periods, both correct: 72 days is the nodal regression relative to the stars; 60 days is the beta cycle relative to the Sun, because the Sun's apparent motion shortens it (section 5 D3).

**C3. Crew dose per six months.** Simonsen et al. (NASA, 2025): 40-90 mSv effective dose per mission. Cucinotta et al. (2008): 72 mSv average (19 astronauts). Berger et al. (2017): Columbus dose-equivalent rates of 591-647 microsievert/day in May 2016, which is 108-118 mSv per 182.6 days if held constant (section 5 D4), a different quantity (dose equivalent in a module, not effective dose to a person). The "80 mSv at solar maximum and 160 mSv at solar minimum" lead was repeated by news pages but was not found in any NASA document opened; it is consistent in ratio (a factor of two between solar maximum and minimum, which Simonsen, Cucinotta 2014 and the DOSIS series all support) but not in value. Differences arise from definition (effective dose versus dose equivalent versus absorbed dose), solar phase, ISS altitude (SAA share rose from 35% to about 50% of absorbed dose as altitude rose from about 350 to about 420 km), shielding of the sampled location, and sample (19 astronauts in 2000-2007 era versus 2016 hardware).

**C4. ISS array power: 248 kW or 75-90 kW.** Davies (NASA JSC): about 31 kW per wing at beginning of life, 8 wings, about 248 kW total. NASA Facts and Figures: "75 to 90 kilowatts." Delleur and Propp (NASA Glenn and Boeing): four modules "capable of producing a combined average power output of 75 kW." The first is peak rated output at start of life; the others are orbit-average power available to loads. The ratio is 30-36% (section 5 D5). The source documents do not itemize the difference (eclipse, sun-pointing angle, array shadowing and feathering, degradation, conversion losses).

**C5. Debris flux unit in the 2001 table.** Theall et al. print the unit "m-2 s-1" in the table and figures. Read literally, the greater-than-10 cm value (1.38E-07 to 5.51E-07) is 4.4 to 17 objects per square meter per year (section 5 D12), which cannot be right when about 25,000 such objects exist in all of Earth orbit (NASA ODPO FAQ). It is probably per year, but this was not confirmed. Use the table for ratios between sizes only.

**C6. NEP specific mass: 20, 3.2, 1 kg/kWe.** National Academies (2021): 20 kg/kWe for the whole NEP system (reactor, conversion, radiator, power management, thrusters). McGuire et al. (NASA Glenn, 2003): about 3.2 kg/kWe for the reactor power system at 8.2 MWe (excludes thrusters, PPU 1.25, structure and propellant tanks). A 2004 vapor-core-reactor paper: about 1 kg/kWe (concept level). The same 2004 record states less than 1 kg/kWe is unrealistic for the cycle it studied. Scope and technology level differ; none has been built.

**C7. Falcon 9 price per kilogram.** Jones (2018, earlier file): $2,720/kg ($62 million, 22,800 kg). SpaceX's own estimate quoted in an AIAA article (2022): $2,700/kg. This file: $74 million for the 2026 standard payment plan, which gives about $3,250/kg on the same 22,800 kg. The rise is the price increase from $62 million to $70 million (2025) to $74 million (2026). List price is not provider cost.

**C8. Unit printed in the NASA Moon-to-Mars GCR table.** Simonsen et al. Table 1 prints "mSv/yr" for free-space GCR dose rates of about 0.6 (solar maximum) and about 1.3 (solar minimum). The same table says these are "two to three times higher than ISS," the OCHMO 2021 requirement is "below 1.3 mSv/day," and the ISS rate is 0.2-0.5 mSv/day; so the unit is per day. The table's unit is a printing error.

**C9. Round-trip Mars dose.** BELT 3D: about 0.66 Sv round trip (MSL cruise measurement, 1.8 mSv/day). Simonsen 2025: 525 mSv (870-day mission, solar maximum) to 1,625 mSv (1,250-day, solar minimum) design values with shielding; Bailey et al. 2013: 394-456 mSv for one year behind ISS-module shielding at 1977 solar minimum. The ranges overlap; duration, shielding, solar phase and surface stay change the total.

## 5. Derived figures

Basis stated per item. Constants: seconds per year = 365.25 x 86,400 = 3.15576E+07 s; standard gravity g0 = 9.80665 m/s2 (defined constant); speed of light c = 299,792.458 km/s (SI-defined). ISS speed 7.67 km/s and orbit radius 6,778 km (400 km altitude plus 6,378 km Earth equatorial radius, the latter printed in SNUG Rev 10 Appendix T) are used where marked. Earlier-file values are cited by name.

**D1. Path length behind the TDRSS two-way delay.** 0.527 s x 299,792 km/s = 157,990 km; divided into four legs (ground to relay, relay to user, user to relay, relay to ground) = 39,498 km per leg. This is consistent with a geostationary relay (altitude about 35,800 km per search-summary mention of NASA text, not opened) seen at a slant range, and with the SNUG statement that the minimum round-trip propagation is "at least 0.5 seconds." The 1.0-1.1 s command loop (NTRS 20090022246) is about twice this; the source does not itemize the remaining roughly 0.5 s (it names landlines to the White Sands complex and onboard processing).

**D2. Altitude gained per reboost delta-v.** For a small prograde burn on a circular orbit, change in orbit radius = 2 a dv / v. With a = 6,778 km, v = 7.67 km/s, dv = 0.001 km/s: 2 x 6,778 x 0.001 / 7.67 = 1.77 km per 1.00 m/s. Measured (NASA Glenn PIMS 2025): 0.95, 1.67 and 1.68 km per m/s. Burn durations: 8.9 min for 1.00 m/s, 10.8 min for 1.00 m/s, 3.5 min for 0.30 m/s. Mean acceleration implied by 1.00 m/s in 10.7 min = 1.00 / 642 s = 1.6E-03 m/s2 = 0.16 milli-g (PIMS measured about 0.2 mg).

**D3. Nodal period versus beta period.** If the orbit plane regresses with a period of 72 days (search summary, not opened) and the Sun moves 360 degrees in 365.25 days, the plane-to-Sun angle repeats at 1 / (1/72 + 1/365.25) = 60.1 days, which is the AMS-02 "60-day" figure.

**D4. Six-month dose from rates.** Cucinotta/Simonsen range 0.2 to 0.5 mSv/day x 182.6 days = 36.5 to 91.3 mSv (matches "40 to 90"). Columbus dose equivalent (DOSTEL, May 2016) 647 and 591 microsievert/day x 182.6 days = 118.2 and 107.9 mSv (dose equivalent in a module with a quality factor applied; not effective dose; solar-cycle phase and altitude as of May 2016). SAA absorbed dose: (150 - 80) / 80 = +87.5% for +70 km (350 to 420 km), about +1.25 percentage points of the 350 km value per kilometer, valid only inside that altitude range.

**D5. ISS array power per area.** Cells: 82 strings x 400 cells = 32,800 cells per wing (matches Davies). Cell area per wing: 32,800 x 0.08 m x 0.08 m = 209.9 m2. Rated 31 kW per wing / 209.9 m2 = 148 W per m2 of cell area at beginning of life. Orbit-average 75-90 kW / 248 kW = 30-36%. For comparison the sunlit fraction implied by 90-min orbit and 30-min eclipse is 60/90 = 67%. If the Lockheed Martin press-release dimension (each wing 107 ft x 38 ft, seen only in a search summary, not opened) is right, gross wing area = 4,066 ft2 = 377.7 m2, giving 82 W per m2 of gross wing area and a cell packing of 209.9 / 377.7 = 56%. Atomic-oxygen check (item 3): flux = n v = 1.4E+08 cm-3 x 7.67E+05 cm/s = 1.07E+14 cm-2 s-1 (paper: 1.1E+14); annual fluence = 1.1E+14 x 3.156E+07 = 3.5E+21 cm-2 (ram surface, model conditions F10.7 = 150).

**D6. Launch cost.** $74,000,000 / 22,800 kg = $3,246 per kg (Falcon 9 list price, Jones-source capacity). $4.2E+09 / 27,000 kg = $155,600 per kg to the Moon (SLS Block 1 plus Orion, OIG launch cost, NASA payload to the Moon); with 38,000 kg (Block 1B figure) = $110,500 per kg (but the OIG cost is for the first four Artemis missions, not for Block 1B).

**D7. Time for commercial parts to reach their dose in LEO (basis PD-ED-1258 numbers).** Commercial 2 to 10 krad(Si); dose rates 100 to 1,000 rad(Si)/yr (low inclination, below 500 km) and 1,000 to 10,000 rad(Si)/yr (higher inclinations). 2 krad / 10,000 rad/yr = 0.2 yr; 10 krad / 100 rad/yr = 100 yr; 2 krad / 1,000 rad/yr = 2 yr; 10 krad / 1,000 rad/yr = 10 yr. Radiation-tolerant parts (20-50 krad) at 1,000-10,000 rad/yr: 2 to 50 yr. SEU example: a 1 Mbit memory (1E+06 bits) at 1E-05 errors/bit-day gives 10 errors/day; at 1E-07 gives 0.1 per day; at 1E-10 gives 1E-04 per day.

**D8. Rocket equation checks.** Delta-v = Isp g0 ln(1/epsilon) with Isp 465 s and epsilon 0.075 = 465 x 9.80665 x ln(13.33) = 11.81 km/s (Folta 2005 prints "approximately 11.8"). Four-stage payload ratio 0.071 versus single-stage 0.02 = 3.55 times, i.e., "355% greater" in the paper's wording. Fast round trip initial mass: 15,666 t versus 1,618 t = 9.7 times. Bailey et al.: 20 t less vehicle x 26 t propellant per tonne = 520 t, about 4.3 launches of 120 t class, which takes about 10 launches down to about 6, as the paper states.

**D9. Hall thruster thrust from power.** F = 2 eta P / (g0 Isp). XR-100: 2 x 0.63 x 100,000 W / (9.80665 x 2,570 s) = 5.0 N. AEPS: 2 x 0.67 x 12,500 / (9.80665 x 2,800) = 0.61 N. XR-5: 2 x 0.56 x 4,500 / (9.80665 x 2,020) = 0.25 N (efficiency and Isp from National Academies Table 3.2; total efficiency assumed to be the thrust efficiency).

**D10. Plutonium-238 supply in electrical terms.** GAO: one RPS uses about 3.5 kg of Pu-238 and gives about 120 We at start of life (6% conversion efficiency, so about 2,000 W thermal). That is 120 / 3.5 = 34 We per kg of Pu-238. 35 kg held = 10 units = 1,200 We at start of life; about half to specification (17.5 kg) = 5 units = 600 We. Production at the goal of 1.5 kg/year = 0.43 unit per year (51 We per year of production).

**D11. NEP mass scales.** 1 MWe x 20 kg/kWe = 20,000 kg; 2 MWe = 40,000 kg. Radiator 3,000 m2 at 7.0 kg/m2 = 21,000 kg; at 4.5 kg/m2 = 13,500 kg; at 3.5 = 10,500 kg. MARVL heat flux = 4 MWth / 3,000 m2 = 1,333 W per m2 (at about 550 K). ISS-type panel arithmetic for the slide's "about 11 kg/kWe" case: 2,500 m2 x 7.0 kg/m2 / 2,000 kWe = 8.75 kg/kWe for panels alone; the slide's 11 includes more than panels (trunklines, fluid, deployment; not itemized).

**D12. Harmonic drive and actuator life in other units.** 12 million input revolutions / 160 = 75,000 output revolutions. At 50 rpm input that is 12E+06 / 50 = 240,000 min = 167 days of continuous running. JPL/Aeroflex prototype: 120 million motor revolutions / 4 = 30 million gearbox output revolutions. Debris unit check (C5): 1.38E-07 per m2 per s x 3.156E+07 s/yr = 4.4 per m2 per yr (maximum 17.4 for 5.51E-07).

**D13. Crew dose per day in the lean Mars vehicle.** 394 and 456 mSv per 365 days = 1.08 and 1.25 mSv/day, under the OCHMO design target of 1.3 mSv/day for free space. Simonsen DRM totals divided by the 600 mSv career limit: 525/600 = 0.88; 1,625/600 = 2.7.

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

Each bullet names the finding it rests on (section 3 table letter).

- **Orbit decay is permanent work.** A structure at ISS altitude loses altitude every day and must be pushed back up; the three documented 2025 ISS reboosts (0.3 to 1.0 m/s on 17 Mar, 19 Jun and 14 Aug, 94 and 56 days apart; other reboosts may exist) used visiting-vehicle thrusters for 3 to 11 minutes at about 0.2 milli-g (A). The altitude chosen trades propellant (higher is cheaper at solar maximum) against debris and radiation exposure (lower is better at solar minimum); the planning difference was 10-20 kg of propellant per month (A).
- **Day and night are about 60 and 30 minutes, with a gap.** A low orbit gives about 90-minute cycles with about 30 minutes of eclipse; for roughly a week twice a year the orbit has no eclipse at all (beta above about 70 degrees), which swaps cold-soak problems for continuous heating on one side (A). Power must be stored for the dark part of every cycle (A).
- **Communication is relay-limited.** A relay satellite loop adds about 0.5 s of propagation each way and 1.0 to 1.1 s for a command round trip including processing (A). Real-time control of a remote machine through a relay is a conversation with a visible lag.
- **Radiation is a continuous dose, mostly cosmic rays, with a belt term that grows with altitude.** Crew dose 0.2-0.5 mSv/day at ISS (40-90 mSv per six months); the current career limit is 600 mSv; the South Atlantic Anomaly share of the absorbed dose rose from about 80 to about 150 microgray/day when the station was lifted from 350 to 420 km; no measured number exists for 600 km in the sources found, but crews at the Hubble-servicing altitude received the highest shuttle doses (B). Beyond the belts a transit adds 0.5-1.6 Sv per mission design (B); fast or heavily shielded transits are called "mass prohibitive" by NASA authors (B).
- **Solar storms and slot belts are episodic hazards.** A 2024 geomagnetic storm raised neutron dose equivalent inside ISS by a factor of two to three for a month (about 1.8 mSv extra) (B).
- **A large solar array delivers a third of its rated power on average.** 248 kW rated gave 75-90 kW delivered, from about 210 square meters of cell area per wing; arrays lose about 0.15-0.45 percent of short-circuit current per year in LEO, and a slow spiral through the Van Allen belts can halve array power (G, D).
- **Debris is dominated by small, fast pieces.** About 500,000 pieces of 1-10 cm and more than 100 million of 1 mm or larger orbit Earth; the threat to missions is mm-to-cm particles at about 10 km/s; ISS shielding is designed so critical components withstand about 1 cm; the models disagree for particles under 3 mm and there is no in-situ data above 600 km (C).
- **Atomic oxygen erodes polymers on forward-facing surfaces.** Ram flux at 400 km is about 1E+14 atoms per square centimeter per second (about 3.5E+21 per year) in the model used; it rises roughly fivefold per 100 km of descent between 400 and 300 km (C).
- **Large low-mass structures can have short orbital lives.** Lifetime at 400 km ranges from about 3 months to over 8 years depending on ballistic coefficient and solar activity (C).
- **Electronics wear out from total dose and glitch from single events.** Ordinary commercial parts tolerate about 2-10 krad(Si); LEO dose rates of 100-10,000 rad(Si)/yr mean 0.2 to 100 years to that dose depending on orbit and shielding; commercial single-event upset rates are of order 1E-05 errors per bit per day; hardened parts tolerate over 200 krad (D). Soft errors, resets and memory corruption are routine, and some single events are permanent (D).
- **Joints and gears have finite, lubricant-limited life in vacuum.** A vacuum-lubricated 160:1 harmonic gear lasted 12-13 million input revolutions (75,000 output revolutions) before efficiency fell to about 40 percent; a dry-film cold-vacuum actuator reached 30 million output revolutions before efficiency fell; lubricant that is consumed cannot be replenished in an open mechanism (D). Any human-shaped machine in vacuum or in space must count revolutions per joint per year against these figures.
- **Heat leaves only as radiation.** Radiators are heavy and large: 7 kg per square meter at the ISS, 1,300 square meters for 125 kW, and a 4 MW nuclear electric ship needs more than 3,000 square meters (H). A machine's waste heat must be carried to a radiator by a conductive or fluid path.
- **Human bodies change on schedules.** Space motion sickness in about 70 percent of people within 72 hours; plasma volume down 10-15 percent; the heart-rate response to standing stays elevated for at least two weeks after more than four months in orbit; eye changes in 81 percent of NASA long-duration flyers (69 percent with optic disc swelling), with structural changes persisting for years; persistent immune dysregulation through a six-month flight (E).
- **Launch mass is the constraint on everything above.** Falcon 9 list price is about $3,250 per kg (22.8 t basis); NASA's SLS plus Orion costs at least $4.2 billion per launch (about $155,600 per kg to the Moon) (F). A Mars round trip of about a year with a 50 t vehicle needed about 10 heavy-lift launches in the architecture studied; each extra tonne of vehicle adds about 26 t of propellant (I).
- **Plutonium-based power is scarce.** About 35 kg of Pu-238 was reserved for NASA (about half to specification), at about 120 We per 3.5 kg unit; the production goal was 1.5 kg per year (H).
- **Fast human transits require delta-v of tens of km/s with chemical propulsion.** 180-day Mars round trips need 36 to 45 km/s total; one-year trips with on-orbit staging need about 10 launches of 120 t; nuclear thermal rockets (Isp about 900 s) reduce the initial mass (H, I).

## 7. Candidate handwaves

Flagged, not accepted. The developer decides.

1. **Nuclear electric propulsion at megawatt scale and 20 kg/kWe.** No NEP component, subsystem or system was under development at the end of 2020 (National Academies). A 20 kg/kWe system needs a 1200 K reactor, 1150 K conversion, 4% burnup over four years, 5 kg/kWe thrusters and radiators well below 7 kg/m2. Any setting that assumes cheap, light megawatt-class electric ships is assuming hardware that exists only as requirements (H).
2. **Radiators at or below 3.5-4.5 kg/m2 with 3,000 m2 deployment.** State of the art is about 7.0 kg/m2 for 125 kW. The target has not been flown (H).
3. **Specific mass of 1 kg/kWe from vapor-core or similar reactors.** A concept-level claim in a 2004 abstract; the same record warns less than 1 kg/kWe is unrealistic for the cycle it studied (H).
4. **Any person-sized machine surviving years in open orbit on commercial parts.** At 2-10 krad(Si) tolerance, parts in higher-inclination LEO would reach their dose in 0.2-10 years; designs need hardened parts or shielding the sources do not quantify (D).
5. **A habitat orbit near 600 km with an unquantified radiation belt term.** The only measured altitude trend stops at 420 km; the 600 km value is unmeasured in the sources found (B).
6. **Solar sails with 0.3-3.7 mm/s2 acceleration.** Demonstrated or planned values are 0.075-0.17 mm/s2 and the proposing author says the field is "much slower than originally envisioned" (H).
7. **Large Pu-238 inventories.** The world supply for NASA was about 35 kg in 2017; assuming large radioisotope fleets would need far more than the roughly 35 kg held (H).
8. **Short Mars stays with one-year round trips.** Each requires a pre-positioned return stage and about 10 launches of 120 t; a crew of four on a 50 t vehicle (I). Reducing the crew or vehicle mass is the only lever the study offers.
9. **Falcon-class prices as the baseline for orbital construction.** Prices are rising ($62 million, $70 million, $74 million) and do not equal cost; SLS-class costs are two orders of magnitude higher per kg (F).
