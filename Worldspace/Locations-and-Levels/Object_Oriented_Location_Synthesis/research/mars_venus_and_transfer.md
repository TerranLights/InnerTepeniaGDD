## 4. CONFLICTS

1. **Mars surface gravity: 3.73 vs 3.71 m/s^2.** NSSDC Mars gives 3.73; JPL SSD and the HAVOC slide give 3.71. Cause: radius definition. GM/R^2 with the JPL GM gives 3.728 at the mean radius (3389.5 km) and 3.713 at the equatorial radius (3396.19 km). Neither is wrong.
2. **Mars temperature extremes.** NSSDC lists 184 to 242 K (-89 to -31 C) as the diurnal range; NASA's outreach page gives about +20 C and about -153 C; the REMS slides give Gale Crater air minima of 185 to 210 K and maxima of 230 to 280 K; the CO2 frost point near 148 K (secondary) sets the polar-night floor. They differ by definition (global mean vs site, air vs ground, any latitude vs one low-latitude crater, one season vs all). No single "temperature range of Mars" is correct; a design must name the latitude, season, elevation and air-or-ground.
3. **Mars surface pressure.** NSSDC: mean 636 Pa, range 400 to 870 Pa. Local values depend on elevation: Perseverance at Jezero saw 631 to 768 Pa in 2021; Gale Crater (-4.4 km) has a mean column of 21 g/cm^2 (about 779 Pa derived) and Guo et al. bracketed 700 to 970 Pa. Different sites and elevations.
4. **Mars GCR dose equivalent at the surface.** Hassler 2014 measured 0.64 +/- 0.12 mSv/day (near solar maximum, Gale); Guo et al. model 0.35 to 1.15 mSv/day over the solar cycle (lower at stronger solar modulation); NASA's technical brief uses about 0.5 mSv/day for a surface mission; a Hellas preprint quotes MARIE values of 273 to 547 uSv/day (in orbit, not on the surface, via a secondary route). Cause: solar cycle phase, column mass (elevation), shielding geometry (surface vs orbit vs spacecraft interior), and quality-factor conventions.
5. **Round-trip mission dose.** Hassler 2014: 662 mSv (2 x 180 days transit) plus 320 mSv (500 days surface) is stated as about 1.01 Sv in the text; the two table values sum to 982 mSv. The difference of about 28 mSv is within the quoted +/-108 and +/-50 mSv uncertainties, but I am recording it rather than choosing one. Zeitlin 2013 gives 0.66 +/- 0.12 Sv for "the shortest round trip with current propulsion systems," which is transit only. Guo et al. give 200 +/- 100 mSv for a 195-day trip at solar maximum, so mission architecture and solar phase move the dose by a factor of three to five.
6. **Cruise dose reference.** 1.84 mSv/day (Hassler, Zeitlin) vs about 1.6 mSv/day (LRO CRaTER, cited in Guo) vs "1.5 mSv/day" used as an example in the NASA brief vs 1 to 5 mSv/day for the range of solar conditions (Guo). Shielding, instrument and solar phase differ.
7. **Dust-storm severity.** Peak optical depth 4.3 to 5 (2007, MER), 3.7 to 9 (Viking 1977 second storm), 10.8 (2018, an estimate by JPL). Instruments, sites and methods differ (solar imaging vs array power vs estimates); all agree that a global storm can take solar output close to zero (DRA 5.0 quotes 15% on the worst MER days; Opportunity lost contact).
8. **Dust-storm frequency.** Zurek and Martin (1993): about one in three Mars years for a global event. A popular summary page claimed "one or occasionally two" per Mars year; I rejected it (secondary, unsourced). Regional and local storms are far more frequent than global storms.
9. **Lava tube size.** Sauro et al. (2020): Mars tubes are 1 to 3 orders of magnitude more voluminous than Earth tubes; a secondary summary of the same paper states "100 times wider" and gives Earth tubes as 10 to 30 m. I could not read the paper's tables, so I cannot reconcile "volume" with "width." A ResearchGate summary claiming "roofs 10 to 50 m thick, radiation reduced 10 to 20 times" was rejected.
10. **MOXIE output.** The paper (Hoffman et al. 2022) gives 6 to 8 g/hour, 6 g/hour at the reference condition, and 49.9 g over the seven 2021 runs. News pages say 6 g/hr and also 12 g/hr, and a later total of 122 g. The 122 g presumably covers runs after 2021 (not verified); "12 g/hr" I could not locate in the paper.
11. **Venus surface pressure and temperature.** 90 bar (Landis 2001), 92 bar (NSSDC, Gillmann), 93.3 bar (HAVOC slide), 96 bar (Landis 2003); 462 C, 464 C, 735 K, 737 K. Different elevations, sources and vintages; all within about 5%.
12. **Venus albedo.** 0.8 (Landis 2001; Anchordoqui), 0.65 (Colozza and Landis 2006), Bond albedo 0.76 (Howe). Geometric vs Bond albedo and different bands.
13. **Venus 50 km temperature and where "1 bar" sits.** HAVOC slide: 75 C and 1.05 atm at 50 km. Landis 2003: 1 bar slightly above 50 km, 0 to 50 C. Landis 2001: about 80 C at 45 km, about -35 C at 60 km. Anchordoqui: 0 to 100 C between 47 and 58 km. These are consistent with a lapse rate of about 8 C/km but disagree on which altitude is "Earth-like"; a design at 50 km sits at about 75 C, not at room temperature.
14. **Venera 13 survival time.** NASA's outreach page: "longest survivor: 2 hours." Secondary pages: 127 minutes (2 h 7 min), design life 32 minutes. The HAVOC proofs paper (NASA Langley) says 157 minutes. I could not reach an NSSDC mission page (error pages). 127 minutes is the most common figure, but all of its sources are secondary; the 157-minute figure may be an error or may count something other than surface transmission.
15. **Venus lander time and the "less than a week" statement.** The HAVOC proofs paper says total surface and atmospheric exploration time of the whole planet is under a week; not reconciled with anything, recorded as a stated claim.
16. **Landis (2003) envelope lift.** His text says a 1 km diameter envelope lifts 700,000 t and a 2 km envelope 6 million t. With his own lift value of about 0.5 kg/m^3 the arithmetic gives about 262,000 t and 2.1 million t. The text implies about 1.3 to 1.4 kg/m^3 of lift, which is above the lift of breathable air at 50 km (0.53 kg/m^3 derived). The figure may be a typographical or scaling slip, or use a different altitude. Treat the stated tonnages as unverified.
17. **Venus surface sunlight.** The HAVOC slide table lists "Solar Power 661 W/m^2" in the Venus surface column. Tomasko and Doose report about 2.5% of incident sunlight absorbed at the ground, and Landis 2003 calls the surface nearly dark. Derived: 2601.3/4 = 650 W/m^2 is the planet-average incident flux, and 2.5% of that is about 16 W/m^2. The slide's 661 W/m^2 is therefore probably a planet-average incident flux placed in the wrong cell; I do not use it as a surface value.
18. **Trace gases in the Venus atmosphere.** H2O 20 ppm (NSSDC, Colozza) vs 30 ppmv (Gillmann); He 17 ppm (Colozza) vs 12 ppmv (Gillmann). Differing compilations.
19. **Transfer time Earth-Mars.** Derived circular-coplanar Hohmann time is 259 days. DRA 5.0 reports 174 d out and 201 d back for a 2037 crew trajectory and 202 d for the quickest cargo trajectory it found. Real orbits are eccentric and inclined, and DRA optimizes departure speed and stay time; none of them is a contradiction.
20. **Mars CO2 inventory.** Zubrin and McKay (1993): enough volatile CO2 for a 300 to 600 mb atmosphere may remain. Jakosky and Edwards (2018): about 20 mbar is mobilizable and only about 1 bar remains in total, mostly locked in deep carbonates. Newer data (MAVEN, Mars Express, MRO, Odyssey) and a stricter definition of "accessible"; the later paper is peer reviewed and the earlier is a conference paper.
21. **Phobos size and density.** JPL mean radius 11.08 +/- 0.04 km and density 1.872 +/- 0.076 g/cm^3; a search summary of Willner et al. (2014) gave mean radius 10.993 km and density 1.860 +/- 0.013 g/cm^3; ESA gives 1862 kg/m^3 and 1.0603e16 kg. Different shape-model epochs.
22. **Wikipedia-sourced areostationary figures** (17,032 km altitude) match my derivation from JPL data; no conflict, but I did not use the Wikipedia pages as sources.

## 6. CONSTRAINTS FOR ANY INHABITANT (physics and engineering only)

Mars surface
- Pressure is about 0.6 kPa (3.1, 3.3): no unprotected exposure is survivable and every habitat is a pressure vessel that must resist an internal-to-external pressure difference of roughly 1 atm. The wind does little mechanical work (D19), but dust is the hazard, not wind force.
- The atmosphere is 95.1% CO2 with 0.16% O2 (3.1): it supplies carbon dioxide, nitrogen and argon, not breathable gas. Oxygen has to be made (MOXIE demonstrated 6 to 8 g/hour, 3.4) and nitrogen and argon are available as buffer gas (DRA 5.0).
- Gravity is 0.378 of Earth's (D3). The health effects of long-term partial gravity are outside the experience base (3.6, DRA 5.0 Table 6-1), so no one can state yet whether a person raised or living there for years stays healthy.
- Cold: ground and air temperatures commonly fall to about 170 to 210 K at night (3.1); polar nights approach the CO2 frost point (secondary). Heat rejection and heat retention are both design drivers because the thin atmosphere gives little convective help (this follows from the 0.016 kg/m^3 density).
- Light and power: the top-of-atmosphere flux is about 43% of Earth's (D26); global storms occur in about one Mars year in three and can cut solar output to a small fraction (3.2). Any solar-powered base needs storage or generating capacity for up to roughly 50 to 60 days of near-zero output, or a non-solar source; nuclear surface power is unaffected by dust (DRA 5.0). Solar is best between about 15 S and 30 N (3.2).
- Radiation: about 0.64 mSv/day on the surface (3.3), about 230 mSv/yr, so NASA's 600 mSv career limit is reached in under three years of surface time (D28). Regolith helps substantially: 2 m of cover cuts the modeled dose by about 15 times, 3 m by about 80 times (D29, model-scaled). Large solar particle events are unpredictable and were not sampled at the surface.
- Dust and soil chemistry: perchlorate at 0.3 to 1 wt% (3.4) is a toxic oxidizer in every handled soil; the fine dust is also expected to be a health hazard (secondary; the review was unreadable). Habitats need dust exclusion, filtration and decontamination on every transfer from outside.
- Water exists as 1.5 to 3 wt% bound in soil, and as ice at mid and high latitudes within meters (3.4); equatorial ice is not established. Choosing a latitude trades ice access against light and warmth (DRA 5.0 reasoning; Putzig et al.).
- Subsurface voids (lava tubes) are candidates, not confirmed habitats (3.4). A void gives mass shielding and thermal stability only if it is real, intact, and sealable.
- Atmosphere engineering is not an option on any planning horizon relevant to a settlement: only about 20 mbar of CO2 is mobilizable (3.11), and a breathable open atmosphere needs mass and energy scales of order 10^18 kg and 10^25 J (Turyshev preprint). Surface life remains inside pressurized or covered structures; regional covers (aerogel) are the only more plausible expansion (3.11).

Mars orbit
- Phobos and Deimos have escape speeds of 11 and 5.6 m/s (D14); a human or a machine standing on either has negligible weight, and any ground operation is anchoring and rendezvous, not walking. Phobos's orbit is decaying slowly (about 1.8 m per century) and is a loosely bound rubble pile (3.5).
- Low Mars orbit gives a period near 111 min (D11) and about 2 to 3 times the ISS dose equivalent (3.5); there is no magnetic field, and a solar particle event in 2002 took the measured dose rate up to about 40 times the quiet value.
- A relay or station at areostationary altitude sits about 17,000 km above the equator (D10); Phobos passes inside that radius three times per sol (D13, 3.5).

Earth-Mars transit
- Minimum-energy transit takes about 260 days (D4); real crewed concepts are 174 to 201 days per leg in the long-stay profile, with a 26-month cycle and a stay of 500+ days to wait for the geometry (3.6). Shorter total missions cost more propulsion: about 10 to 11 km/s for a 400-day round trip, over 20 km/s for 60-day outbounds (3.6).
- The dose inside a spacecraft is 1.84 mSv/day (3.3, 3.6), so a single 180-day leg already delivers about 330 mSv, and a round trip alone about 660 mSv, above NASA's career limit of 600 mSv; GCR shielding by ordinary materials is only about 7% (3.3).
- Communication is a 3 to 22 minute one-way delay, and a roughly 2-week command moratorium every 26 months at solar conjunction (3.6); voice conversation with Earth is impractical for most of the cycle (a physical consequence of c).
- Zero gravity for 6+ months causes bone loss that exercise plus diet can largely limit but not erase; some hip deficits persist at 3 years (3.6). A long confined mission produces sleep, activity and vigilance disturbances in most of a crew (3.6).

Venus surface and clouds
- The surface is 737 K at 92 bar with density about 65 kg/m^3 (3.7): electronics, seals, and every human-scale structure fail; the longest surviving lander lasted about two hours (3.7, with the 127 vs 157 minute conflict). Nothing inhabited can sit on the surface without extreme-temperature machinery that is not available in any source I read.
- The cloud layer is 45 to 70 km, with 1.05 atm and about 75 C at 50 km (3.8): pressure is Earth-like but temperature at 50 km needs cooling; the temperature falls by about 8 C/km, so about 55 to 58 km is warm-to-comfortable (3.8, from Landis; a design would still need cooling at the lower edge).
- Everything outside is a concentrated sulfuric acid aerosol, about 75 to 90% acid, in droplets with H2O only about 2% (3.8): every exposed surface needs an acid-resistant coating (FEP Teflon and polypropylene tested well at room temperature; PVC failed) and must hold up at 75 to 80 C.
- Breathable air is a lifting gas (0.53 kg/m^3 at 50 km, D21), so a habitat envelope can be mostly habitable volume; the mass it can lift is about a quarter million tonnes for a 1 km sphere (D22), not the 700,000 t that one source states.
- Winds of 85 to 100 m/s carry anything floating around the equator in about 106 to 110 hours (3.8, D24): a floating platform is constantly moving over the ground, has an effective light-dark cycle set by the wind (44 to 66 hours in HAVOC), and cannot hold a position over a surface site without propulsion. Wind loads on exposed structures are of order 3 to 8 kPa (D20).
- Sunlight above and in the upper cloud is plentiful (1418 W/m^2 at 50 km; reflected light of about 90% of the downward flux at 60 km allows two-sided panels) while the surface gets only a few percent (3.7, D25).
- Radiation at 50 km is similar to Earth's surface for the column mass (1.29 kg/cm^2) and simulated cosmic-ray flux; it rises about 40% near 60 km and is not measured in situ (3.8).
- Water is scarce as free gas (20 to 30 ppm) and is available chiefly as H2O in acid droplets; metals and silicon exist only at the surface, 45+ km down in 737 K gas (3.9).
- Leaving the cloud layer to orbit needs a two-stage rocket with about 9 km/s of delta-v (3.9); returning from Venus took about 300 days in the HAVOC trajectory (3.10).

Earth-Venus transit
- Windows recur every 584 days (3.10); a HAVOC-type round trip is 440 days, shorter than most Mars profiles but with a 300-day return leg (3.10).
- Communication delay is 2 to 15 minutes one way (D9).

## 7. CANDIDATE HANDWAVES (flagged, not accepted; the developer decides)

H1. **Fast, low-dose Earth-Mars transit** (weeks, not months): needs propulsion not in any source here with demonstrated performance (a nuclear thermal rocket has Isp 875 to 950 s in a study but has not flown; delta-v for a 60-day outbound exceeds 20 km/s). Anything faster than about 180 days per leg is a handwave unless the propulsion is specified.
H2. **Artificial gravity in transit or on a planetary surface** at a scale beyond a small rotating habitat: not tested; DRA 5.0 itself flags partial gravity as outside experience.
H3. **Reducing GCR dose in transit by shielding**: NASA's brief gives only about 7% reduction for GCR from aluminum and polyethylene; a thick consequent shield costs mass. Any story claim of "full shielding" in a small ship bends physics unless the shield mass is stated.
H4. **Open-air or pressure-suit-free Mars** (a thick breathable atmosphere): needs exaton-class volatiles and more than 10^25 J, or an unknown CO2 source; the peer-reviewed inventory says it is not available.
H5. **Large-scale Venus terraforming** (cooling, removing about 90 bar of CO2, importing water): every proposal is speculative; the best-case figure, about 200 years, rests on a sunshade or a planet-enclosing floating surface that does not exist.
H6. **Cheap surface access on Venus** (humans or ordinary machines on the surface, or large mining from the cloud layer): needs 737 K electronics and mechanisms and a tether or aircraft fleet; no source here shows a working design.
H7. **Floating "continents" or cities of kilometer size**: needs strong, light, acid-proof materials made in bulk (Howe states they cannot yet be manufactured at industrial scale); the lift arithmetic is fine (D21, D22), the structure is the unknown.
H8. **A human-shaped robot at either place**: a body with mass and joints has no special ability here. In Mars's 0.016 kg/m^3 air it cannot fly with arms, and in the Venus clouds it floats only on a buoyant envelope or aircraft. On Venus the body at 50 km sits at about 75 C and in acid; on the surface it would need 737 K parts. These are general constraints, not special cases.
