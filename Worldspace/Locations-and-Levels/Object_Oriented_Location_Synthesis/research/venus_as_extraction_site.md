# Venus as an extraction site: atmosphere and surface inventory, extraction, and export

*Research file, OOLSD library, tranche 7, section 2. Started 2026-10-11. Physics and engineering only.*

## 1. Scope

Venus as a source of material for the rest of the inner Solar System: the atmosphere (gas and cloud) and the surface as
inventories, extraction from floating platforms and from the surface, and export (ascent from the cloud deck, alternatives
to rockets, and Venus orbital infrastructure). The cloud-level environment is already in the sibling files and is cited,
not repeated: **MVT** = `mars_venus_and_transfer.md`, **GRV** = `gap_retry_mars_venus_jupiter.md`, **TRK** =
`space_trucking_and_transport_infrastructure.md`, **PRC** = `space_processing_and_fabrication.md`, **LSS** =
`life_support_lowg_power_structures.md`.

Evidence levels used in the tables: **DEMONSTRATED** (flown, or run in a test rig), **ENGINEERING-STUDIED** (a
quantitative design study), **SPECULATIVE** (a concept paper or thought experiment). **DERIVED** marks a figure calculated
here from sourced inputs; the arithmetic is in section 5.

## 3. Findings

**Reading in one paragraph.** Venus's atmosphere is the largest reachable reservoir of carbon and nitrogen in the inner
Solar System (about 1.3 x 10^20 kg of carbon and about 1.1 x 10^19 kg of nitrogen, DERIVED D1), and it can be processed
from a floating platform with hardware that is a scaled cousin of MOXIE, which has run on Mars. Two findings cut against
treating Venus as a general volatile source: **it is hydrogen-poor** (its whole atmosphere and clouds hold about
7 x 10^14 kg of hydrogen, less than Mars's polar deposits by a factor of about 400 to 530 and only 2 to 6 times Mercury's polar ice,
D2), and **getting anything off it is as hard as launching from Earth's surface**: about 9 km/s from the cloud deck,
with payload fractions in the studied vehicles of 0.15 to 0.56%, and no study found of any non-rocket export method
(TRK F1b; 3.7 below). The inventory is effectively inexhaustible at any extraction rate (3.10); the gravity well is the
limit.

## 4. Conflicts

1. **Nitrogen fraction: 3.5 +/- 0.8% vs 4.60 +/- 0.14%.** The compiled value (Johnson and de Oliveira 2019; NSSDC) is
   3.5%; the Pioneer Venus gas chromatograph alone gave 4.60% at 51.6 km. One instrument at one altitude against a
   multi-mission compilation; the compilation's error bar is wide enough to be the honest statement. Effect: the nitrogen
   inventory is 0.84 to 1.42 x 10^19 kg (D1), and every nitrogen ratio in 3.2 moves by up to about 30%.
2. **Water at cloud level: 700 to 2,000 ppm (Venera and Vega landers) vs about 30 ppm below and about 6 ppm above the
   clouds.** Rimmer et al. (2021) attribute the high lander readings to incidental sampling of droplets, which are 15 to
   25% water. Different quantities (gas plus droplet vs gas only); the gas-phase values are used here.
3. **ISRU production time for the Venus ascent vehicle.** Landis and Oleson (2024): 2 to 3 kg/hr produces the 2.35 t of
   CO/LOX in 16 to 25 days. DERIVED: 2.35 t at 2 to 3 kg/hr of total propellant takes 33 to 49 days; if the rate is oxygen
   only and the mixture is stoichiometric (oxygen 36.4% by mass), the oxygen takes 12 to 18 days. Neither reproduces 16 to
   25 days. The paper states neither the mixture ratio nor which product the rate refers to. Recorded, not resolved.
4. **Temperature at 50 km: about 107 C (Landis and Haag model table) vs about 75 C** (HAVOC slide, the VIRA-based table in
   GRV 3.10, and 340 to 350 K near 910 mbar in radio occultation). Landis and Haag quote a 7.7 C/km lapse from 462 C,
   which would give about 77 C at 50 km, yet their table lists 107 C; their altitudes are above the datum. The cause is not
   stated. The table's power figures depend on cell temperature, so its 50 km output (256 W/m^2) may be conservative.
5. **Cloud mass loading: about 24 mg/m^3 (derived here for the lower cloud) vs a 15 mg/m^3 model peak (GRV 3.15).** The
   derivation uses mean diameters and the disputed mode-3 population (50 cm^-3 at 8 um), which dominates; the model is
   reported to underestimate observed loading by about two. Same order; treat the column (0.1 kg/m^2) as order of
   magnitude only.
6. **Venus escape speed: 10.36 km/s (NSSDC) vs 10.46 km/s (Landis and Oleson 2024).** Already recorded as TRK C2.
7. **MOXIE rate: "up to 10 g/hr" (NASA instrument page), 6 to 8 g/hr (Hoffman 2022, runs through 2021), best 12 g/hr
   (JPL, Sep 2023).** Different epochs and operating points; the paper is the controlled figure.
8. **Argon and neon: Ar 70 +/- 25 ppm (compiled) vs 110 +/- 20 ppm (Venera 11/12, 23 to 1 km); Ne 7 +/- 3 vs 12 ppm.**
   Different instruments and altitudes. Argon mass in D1 uses 70 ppm and a mean atomic mass of 38 (Venus argon is roughly
   half 36Ar and half 40Ar in the compilation), an approximation.
9. **"Hydrogen from condensing sulfuric acid droplets" (Landis 2003) vs the inventory.** Not a contradiction of a number,
   but the derived budget (D3) puts only about 0.3% of the atmosphere's hydrogen in the clouds; the rest is water vapor,
   about 30 ppm below the clouds (in hot gas) and about 6 ppm above them. Either source is thin: roughly 10^5 m^3 of air per
   kg of droplets.
10. **Lightning.** The detections and non-detections are "mutually discrepant" (Lorenz 2017). The one LAC event (2020)
    carries a 0.1 to 8.3% meteor probability. The evidence level is DISPUTED and no flash rate is used here.

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant (physics and engineering only)

- **Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen are effectively unlimited; hydrogen is not.** The whole atmosphere holds about 7 x 10^14 kg
  of hydrogen (3.1, D1), mostly as below-cloud vapor; at cloud level it is about 6 ppm of vapor plus droplets at 0.1
  kg/m^2 of column. Any water, food or hydrogen-based propellant economy on a platform is hydrogen-limited and needs either
  very large air throughput (about 10^5 m^3 of air per kg of droplets, D3) or imports.
- **Every kilogram leaving Venus pays about 9 km/s from the cloud deck** (TRK F1b), with studied payload fractions of 0.15
  to 0.56% (3.7). With locally made CO/LOX (Isp 250 s) the lift-off mass is 39 times the burnout mass (D7). There is no
  studied non-rocket export method (3.7), and a stationary space elevator is geometrically impossible (D6).
- **Processing power at cloud level is solar and intermittent:** about 130 W/m^2 averaged over a cycle at 55 km for a flat
  array, with roughly 50-hour nights on a drifting platform (3.5). A 1 kg/hr oxygen plant needs tens of m^2 of array and
  hundreds of kWh of storage (D9).
- **The intake must reject 75 to 92% sulfuric acid droplets** (3.3, 3.11); the only flown envelope exposure is about two
  days (VEGA), and room-temperature lab exposures of 30 days (3.4).
- **A platform cannot hold position over a surface site** and moves round the planet in about 4 to 5 days (MVT 3.8);
  surface access from it means aircraft, droplines or tethers through about 50 km of increasingly hot gas, none of which
  has been built (3.6).
- **The surface is reachable only by machines rated for about 460 C and 9.2 MPa.** Demonstrated uncooled electronics are
  simple SiC circuits with months of life (3.6); keeping ordinary electronics at 30 C costs at least about 1.4 W of work per
  W of heat removed and about 5 W/W with realistic coolers (D8).
- **Surface rock is a basalt** with no measured metal enrichment (3.6); anything mined there is the same silicate,
  aluminum, iron and magnesium oxide mix available on airless bodies with far shallower wells.
- **A human-shaped machine** at cloud level stands on a buoyant structure or an aircraft; it works in 69 to 107 C (55 to
  50 km, Landis and Haag) or about 75 C at 50 km (other profiles), in acid aerosol, with gusts to about 3 m/s vertical and
  100 m/s zonal winds relative to the ground; its joints, seals and coatings face the same acid limits as an envelope.
  Below the clouds its body temperature, not its electronics alone, sets survival time.

## 7. Candidate handwaves (flagged, not accepted; the developer decides)

1. **Bulk export from Venus by anything other than rockets** (a mass driver, a skyhook, beamed propulsion from the cloud
   deck): no study exists; a launcher inside the atmosphere faces about 25 MPa muzzle dynamic pressure at 55 km (D6).
   A design that exports kilotonnes a year from Venus bends the demonstrated engineering by orders of magnitude.
2. **A Venus space elevator** anchored at synchronous orbit: the synchronous radius lies outside the Hill sphere (D6).
   This bends physics, not just engineering.
3. **Venus as a hydrogen, water or methane supplier:** the inventory is small (3.2) and dilute (D3).
4. **Long-lived surface mining machinery** (complex electronics, motors and processing plants working for years at 460 C):
   demonstrated SiC circuits are simple and last months (3.6).
5. **A tether lifting ore about 50 km from the surface** through 735 K gas and 100 m/s wind shear (Landis 2003 names a
   "fullerine tether"): no material data and no study.
6. **Multi-year acid-proof envelopes and intakes:** two days of in situ exposure and 30 days in the lab are all that
   exist (3.4).
7. **Kilometer-scale floating processing cities:** the lift arithmetic works (MVT D21, D22) but structure, materials and
   construction are unstudied (MVT H7).
