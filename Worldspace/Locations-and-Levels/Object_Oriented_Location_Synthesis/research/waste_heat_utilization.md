# Waste-heat utilization in sealed, vacuum-exposed structures (research)

Date opened: 2026-10-11. Research agent output. Real physics and engineering only; no worldbuilding.

## 1. Scope

Heat rejection and heat reuse for sealed structures in orbit (and later other bodies) that can reject heat only by thermal radiation. Covers thermodynamic bounds, heat pumps and thermal buses, power conversion, endothermic processes and their temperature requirements, thermal storage, actual uses of heat in space and analogous sites, and robot-body or battery heat collection. Checklist items A to G.

### 3D. Endothermic chemistry and physics: reaction heat, temperature required, can heat below about 100 C drive it?

Reading rule for this table: the **heat share** is T x (entropy change) / (enthalpy change). It is the most that can be taken from heat at the cell or reactor temperature; the rest of the enthalpy must arrive as electricity (electrochemistry) or as heat at the reaction temperature (thermochemistry). The reference values are NIST CODATA review values for formation enthalpies and entropies (R13) and NIST-JANAF for temperature dependence (R14). The Faraday constant used to turn kJ/mol into volts (96,485 C/mol) and the molar mass of water (18.015 g/mol) are standard constants I did not open a source for this session.

**A3. The three-quarters rule.** Q_c = x Q_h and A = Q_c / (sigma x^4 Th^4) = Q_h / (sigma Th^4 x^3). Work per radiator area: W / A = Q_h (1 - x) sigma Th^4 x^3 / Q_h = sigma Th^4 x^3 (1 - x). I checked the maximum numerically over x = 0.001 to 0.999.

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

- **Every watt a settlement uses becomes a watt it must radiate** (3A, R2), so power and radiator area are linked: at 300 K, 1 kW needs 2.2 m2 of ideal one-face radiator, and at the ISS ammonia supply of 276 K, 3.1 m2 (3A, section 5 A2). The ISS design capacity works out to 147 W per m2 of radiator planform (B-ISS).
- **The waste heat of a crewed or robot station is cold.** ISS cabin loops run at 4 and 17 C and the radiators run at or below 276 K (3B). Heat at that temperature has a Carnot factor of 5 to 25 percent against any realistic radiator, so nothing exotic can be made from it in quantity (3A, 3C).
- **Engines in vacuum are limited by their own radiators.** The cold side of any power cycle is a radiator whose area grows as 1/T^3 for a fixed Th; the area-optimal cold side is three-quarters of the source temperature, and ideal conversion of 300 K heat costs 2.6 times the radiator area of simply radiating it (3A, A4).
- **Hot heat, not warm heat, is what enables chemistry.** Reactions that make oxygen, hydrogen or metal need 800 C (MOXIE), 850 to 950 C (sulfur-iodine), 900 C (ilmenite), 1,600 C (molten regolith) or 1,900 C (carbothermal) (3D). A settlement with a reactor or concentrated solar furnace has such heat; one running on loop waste heat does not.
- **Electrolysis releases heat unless it runs very slowly.** At room temperature, 17 percent of the enthalpy of splitting liquid water can come from heat, but real stacks run at higher voltage and are net heat sources: the ISS electrolyzer keeps about a third of its input as stored enthalpy and releases the rest (3D, D4).
- **Distillation, evaporation, ice melting and drying are the processes low-grade heat can drive**; the ISS vapor-compression distiller recycles latent heat and reaches a COP of about 4. Heat is not the scarce input there; compressor power and bowl heaters are (3D, R22).
- **Thermal-swing CO2 beds need about 204 C.** Where vacuum is free, vacuum-swing beds avoid the heat entirely (3D, R23, R37).
- **Heat pumps trade electricity for radiator area and gain little when the sink is cold.** In vacuum against a 0 K sink the saving is a radiator area ratio of 0.4 to 0.9 for 0.5 to 1.2 kW of extra power per kW; against a hot lunar sink the saving is larger because the baseline radiator cannot work (3B, B-HP; R5).
- **Surviving a long dark period costs mass.** One kilowatt through a 354 h lunar night is 354 kWh of heat: 26 t as paraffin at system level, 1.6 t as a lithium-chloride absorber (concept), 3.8 t as melting ice (3E). A south-pole habitat concept plans for eclipses of up to 100 hours (R28).
- **Fixed heaters and mass dominate small systems.** The ISS runs 1.8 kW of electric heaters on a loop that rejects up to 70 kW; the distiller's bowl heaters use 40 to 50 percent of its power (3B, 3D).
- **A body or a pack gives low-grade heat in small amounts.** A Valkyrie-size body radiates at most about 1 kW at 300 K skin (sibling), its actuators run up to 70 to 90 C, and its cells lose 3 percent of charging power at the peak at 0.3C and under 15 to about 27 percent over a 1C to 2C-discharge cycle (3G). Any collected stream is below about 90 C.
- **A waste-heat-to-product claim needs an export path.** Absorbed heat leaves the radiator budget only with the product (3A).

## 7. Candidate handwaves

Flagged for the developer. I have not accepted any of these as physics.

1. **"A station runs its machines on its own waste heat."** Not available at room temperature: Carnot limits 300 K heat to 5 to 25 percent against realistic radiators, real devices convert about 1 to 12 percent of the heat, and the engine's radiator is more than twice the area of direct rejection (3A, 3C). Needs a hot source (reactor, concentrated solar) or an accepted loss.
2. **"Endothermic chemistry cools the station."** Only if the product is exported or stored out of the station; otherwise the heat returns when the product is used. Also see the ISS electrolyzer: it releases about 66 percent of its input as heat (3D).
3. **"Thermochemical splitting or solid-oxide electrolysis on loop waste heat."** Forbidden by temperature: 800 to 950 C required (3D).
4. **"Regolith ISRU on waste heat."** The reactions are at 900 to 1,900 C; at most 4 percent of an ilmenite plant's energy could come from a source below 100 C (D6).
5. **"A heat pump removes the radiator problem."** It reduces the radiator by a factor of at most 0.4 to 0.9 for the temperatures studied and adds up to 1.2 kW of heat per kW lifted; a lab heat pump with a 70 K lift gave COP 0.1 to 0.6 (3B).
6. **"Phase-change walls or tanks store a lunar night at room-temperature cost."** At system-level 48.8 kJ/kg the mass is 26 t per kW over 354 h (3E).
7. **"Robots shed their heat into the station loop for reuse."** The heat exists and can be collected by cold plates, but it is below about 90 C, mostly below 60 C, tens to hundreds of watts per body (3G); useful only for the low-grade uses in 3D.
8. **"Absorption refrigeration or a membrane-distillation unit runs on 40 to 60 C heat."** I found no opened primary source for either at that temperature; the one absorption concept I read regenerates at up to 120 C (R25). Open thread.
9. **"Heat of reaction from a vacuum-sealed process can be vented as hot gas."** Venting mass loses the inventory of a closed-loop station; radiation is the only heat exit that keeps mass (3A).

**What is forbidden or does not pay:** running a station's machines on its own loop waste heat (row 6); thermochemical splitting,
solid-oxide electrolysis or regolith processing on loop waste heat (row 8); venting hot gas to dump process heat (it loses closed-loop mass); a
heat pump "removing the radiator problem" (row 2). **The radiator stays.** A station's waste-heat reuse does not remove it.

## 10.2 Developer point: heat batteries for stations in orbit (2026-10-11)

The developer notes that **heat batteries (thermal energy storage) are a strong resource for stations in orbit** around the Sun, the Moon,
Mars, Jupiter or Saturn.

**What the research supports:** row 3 above. Heat storage is physically sound where the thermal environment is *periodic*: an eclipse, a
planetary shadow or a long night. The cost is mass, and §3E gives three storage approaches (paraffin phase change, a lithium-chloride
absorber concept, melting ice) with their system-level masses per kilowatt. **What the research does not give:**
- the actual eclipse length and frequency for each body's orbits (the LEO file has only the ISS case: about 36 min of darkness in about 93 min at 400 km);
- storage figures for the Sun-orbiting, Jovian and Saturnian cases (the file's cases are lunar night and ISS-class eclipse);
- a comparison of heat batteries against simply oversizing batteries or radiators for each case.

**Caveat on "orbit around the Sun":** a station in a heliocentric orbit has **no planetary eclipse**. For it a heat battery has no periodic
dark interval to carry the station through, unless the station spins, shades itself or changes distance from the Sun. That case needs its own
analysis.

## 10.3 Developer point: stations "grav-locked" at Lagrange points L4/L5 (for tomorrow, 2026-10-12)

The developer notes that stations at **L4/L5 will need other uses of heat**, and that this needs further research and/or calculation,
probably on 2026-10-12. **My framing of the question, not findings:**

1. **A heat battery earns its mass by carrying a station through an interval with no sunlight.** If a station at L4/L5 has few or no such
   intervals, the storage argument weakens, and heat has to be used (rather than stored) or rejected continuously. *To compute:* the fraction
   of the time a station at Earth-Moon L4/L5 (and at Sun-Earth L4/L5) is in the Earth's or the Moon's shadow. The LEO file's Lagrange-point
   section and the Moon/Lagrange file cover the positions and stability but I did not find an eclipse statistic there. *To verify before use.*
2. **The thermal cycle that does exist may be set by the Sun's apparent direction**, which changes over the synodic period for an
   Earth-Moon point. My understanding is that this is a monthly rather than a per-orbit cycle. *Unverified: confirm by calculation before relying
   on it.* If so, the relevant storage timescale would be days to weeks, not minutes, and the 354 h lunar-night figures above are the right
   order of magnitude to compare.
3. **Steady sunlight changes the problem into continuous rejection.** Every watt consumed is a watt to radiate (§6). *To compute:* the
   equilibrium temperature and the radiator area for a station of a given power at L4/L5, using the Stefan-Boltzmann arithmetic already in the LEO
   file (§5).
4. **Uses that need no cycle:** rows 1, 4 and 5 above (low-grade water and drying, collection of robot heat, a thermal bus), plus a heat pump
   (row 2). With a hot source such as a reactor or concentrated solar, rows 7–9 apply.
5. **No in-situ chemistry on loop heat:** the temperature requirements in row 8 do not change with location.

**Proposed work for tomorrow:** (a) compute the eclipse and Sun-angle statistics for Earth-Moon and Sun-Earth L4/L5 from orbital mechanics,
with the arithmetic shown; (b) size a radiator and a heat-pump case for a stated power at that thermal environment; (c) compare heat-battery,
oversized-battery and oversized-radiator options at the cycle length found in (a); (d) a short sourced search for how real L-point missions
(JWST at Sun-Earth L2 is a flown comparison; no mission has flown at L4/L5) manage their thermal environment. **The L4/L5 results
