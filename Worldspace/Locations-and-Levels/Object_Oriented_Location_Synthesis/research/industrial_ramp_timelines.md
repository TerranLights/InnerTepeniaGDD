# Industrial ramp timelines: build-out rates, learning rates, bootstrapping growth (research, 2026-10-11)

## Scope

Observed build-out rates and ramp histories of large terrestrial infrastructure (railroads, electrification, the
Interstate, container ports, submarine cables, internet backbone, solar and wind, chip fabs, Liberty ships), learning
rates (PV, Li-ion, launch, robots, Liberty labor), space-station and space-program assembly histories, published
space-industry schedules (SP-413, SP-428, space solar power, Mars DRA 5.0), self-replicating and bootstrapping
industry models, remote-settlement precedents, and the dependency lags that stall ramps. Rates and claims only, each
with its source; no timeline of this file's own is proposed.

## Conflicts

1. **NASA 1980 lunar factory growth: "10 years" in the text vs Table 5.2.** The text says output "could grow to
   approximately one million tons per year" in "just 10 years" and to >4 x 10^9 t/yr in 18 years. Table 5.2 lists
   two time columns, "Calendar years" and "Working years, T" (calendar = 2 x working). The fast-exponential column
   reaches 1.24 Mt/yr at 10 working years (20 calendar) and 3.7 x 10^9 t/yr at 18 working years (36 calendar). The text
   therefore counts working years. Anyone quoting "a million tons a year in ten years" from this study should say
   "ten working years, twenty calendar years, and only under fast-exponential cooperation."
2. **Hanford B Reactor construction start.** DOE fact sheet: "began in October 1943", fuel loaded 11 months later.
   Search summaries citing ASCE and others: 7 June 1943. Likely different definitions (site work and excavation vs
   reactor-block construction). The 1944 dates agree.
3. **Liberty ship totals.** USMC FY1945 report: 2,587 "regular Liberty dry cargo vessels" through 30 Jun 1945;
   Thompson (1997): 2,580 standard Libertys from 16 yards plus 119 Liberty-type tankers and transports (2,699); a
   teaching page gives 2,699. Scope (dry cargo only vs all Liberty hulls) and cut-off date explain the spread.
4. **Liberty building time minimum.** USMC report: fleet average lowest "39.2 days" (OCR "39 / 2"), Dec 1943; one

## Constraints for any inhabitant

- **Remote sites run on a supply chain with no slack.** McMurdo receives nearly all of its year's fuel and cargo in one
  sealift; missing one delivery breaks the chain because there are no on-continent reserves (F5). A settlement fed
  by launch windows (every ~26 months for Mars, F3 and F5) is in the same position with longer gaps.
- **Early colonies lose people before they gain independence.** Jamestown lost 80 to 90% of its settlers in its third
  winter and found its first export crop in year five (F5); export income came before anything like self-sufficiency.
- **Ramps are front-loaded with facility construction.** Calship's yard took ~13.5 months from groundbreaking to its
  first ship, and its first ship took 1.73 M labor hours against ~0.4 to 0.5 M later (F1). A first unit costs several
  times a mature one.
- **Learning is real but partly capital and quality.** Liberty ship labor fell 12 to 24% per doubling, but Thompson
  attributes almost all of it to capital deepening and lower quality, with over 10% of ships fracturing (F2). Fast
  ramps can trade away reliability.
- **The last 5% takes as long as the first 75%.** Interstate: 74% open 14 years after the 1956 Act, 99.98% after 41
  (F1). Electrification: 41 years from 8% to ~90% of dwellings (Derived D4).
- **Fabs, reactors, permits and grid connections each take years before the first output.** Greenfield fab ~1.9
  years from construction start (F1); reactor median 59 to 121 months by period (F6); US EIS median 3.5 years and
  interconnection ~5 years (F6).
- **Large programs slip.** About one megaproject in ten is on schedule; NASA's major-project portfolio carries 14
  years of cumulative delay (F6).
- **Self-growing industry depends on power duty cycle and closure.** In Metzger's model, cutting solar duty from 70% to
  40% cuts net growth by more than an order of magnitude; without local chip-making, Earth imports grow exponentially
  (F4). A power-limited model grows linearly, not exponentially (Ono 2025, F4).
- **A human-shaped machine that builds or repairs industry is part of the industry's mass to be reproduced.** In
  Metzger's baseline, three 135-kg "robonaut" assemblers are in each 7.7-t set (Table 2), and their electronics are
  the last thing closed (F4).

## Candidate handwaves (flagged, not accepted; the developer decides)

1. **Full closure, including chips.** Every bootstrapping model that grows without limit assumes eventual 100% local
   manufacture of electronics (Metzger Gens 5.0 and 6.0). No study read here shows how lithography reaches closure;
   CP-2255 recommends settling for 90 to 95% and importing "vitamins". Treating chips as locally made early is a
   handwave.
2. **Sustained exponential growth from a small seed.** CP-2255's fast-exponential table reaches 2.7 x 10^10 t/yr at 40
   calendar years; Metzger's abstract claims "millions of times the industrial capacity of the United States" within
   decades. The only simulation found with explicit power and processing bottlenecks grew linearly (Ono 2025). An
   exponential ramp beyond a few doublings needs its power, closure and labor assumptions stated, or it is a
   handwave.
3. **Additive manufacturing rate.** Metzger assumes 0.5 kg/h per printer; commercial printers cited at 0.10 to 0.15
   kg/h. At 0.1 kg/h each generation takes up to 10 years. A fast ramp built on the 0.5 to 1 kg/h figure assumes an
   unbuilt process rate.
4. **Learning curves extrapolated past the data.** Terzi and Nicoli's $300/kg by 2040 assumes the 21.2% rate holds;
   the authors list structural breaks, debris and single-provider risk. Using a historical learning rate as a forecast
   is an assumption, not a finding.
5. **Schedules taken at face value.** SP-413 (22 years), SP-428 (first SPS in 1990) and the UK SBSP study (operational
   by 2040) are plans. Reference-class data (F6) say most such schedules slip; the 1977 to 1979 plans assumed a
   Shuttle flying 60 times a year, which never happened.
