# Mars water budget and retention: inventory, losses, surface stability and needs

## 1. Scope

Mars only: how much water it holds now and where (and how much of it is cheap to reach), how much it once held and where that
went, how fast it loses hydrogen and oxygen to space today and how that would change with more water or a thicker atmosphere,
whether liquid water can persist at its surface, how much water stated uses would need, the magnetic-shield idea, and the
other volatiles (nitrogen, carbon) that "giving Mars water" would also require. Sibling files cited, not re-derived:
**MVT** = `mars_venus_and_transfer.md`, **GRV** = `gap_retry_mars_venus_jupiter.md`, **LS** = `life_support_lowg_power_structures.md`,
**VE** = `venus_as_extraction_site.md`, **ISM** = `inner_system_mining_synthesis.md`, **SLS** = `starlifting_synthesis.md`.
Calculation scripts: scratch folder `mars_water_budget_and_retention/` (`calc1.py`, `calc2.py`, and later ones), key output pasted in section 5.

**Cheap-to-reach water, as the sources describe it.** The water that is cheap to reach is ice within a few meters of the surface:

**What delivered water would do to the loss rate (DERIVED from the rows above; D2).** Because H escape is diffusion limited and the
limit is proportional to the total hydrogen mixing ratio above the cold trap, more water vapor reaching the upper atmosphere raises H loss
in proportion. At today's measured rates water is lost at 1.5 to 16 kg/s, so **1 m GEL lasts 0.28 to 3.1 billion years** and the present
atmospheric water column 2,800 to 31,000 years. If delivered water raised the upper-atmosphere total hydrogen mixing ratio to 300 ppmv,
3,000 ppmv or 3%, the diffusion limit rises to 71, 713 and 7,130 kg/s of water, and **1 m GEL would last 64, 6.4 and 0.64 million years**.
The limit is the ceiling; actual escape also needs the energy to lift the H (Jeans or non-thermal) and, over 10^5 years, the O to leave
too (Zahnle and Catling). **A thicker atmosphere does not by itself slow this:** the diffusion limit depends on the mixing ratio and
temperature, not the total pressure (Catling and Kasting eq. 5.53), while ion and sputtering losses (about 0.2 kg/s) are a smaller
fraction of a larger inventory. The cold trap is what keeps H low today; a warmer lower atmosphere (the stated purpose of delivering water
and greenhouse gas) weakens it, as the dust-storm data show (5 to 20 x more upper-atmosphere water when the lower atmosphere warms), and as the
high-obliquity model shows (a wetter, cloud-warmed atmosphere loses hydrogen 18.5 x faster; Gilli 2025). That 18.5 x is the only
modeled case found for a planet-wide wetter atmosphere; at that rate 1 m GEL lasts about 50 million years (D2: 6 x 10^27 H/s = 90 kg/s
of water; 1.44 x 10^17 kg / 90 kg/s = 5.1 x 10^7 yr).
Delivered water is therefore lost to space on **10^5 to 10^9 year** timescales depending on how much reaches the upper atmosphere, which
is long against any engineering horizon; the faster losses are to the ground (3.4).

**What the comparison says (D5, D6).** Settlement-scale uses are tiny against the planet: a million people living open-loop on the
mature-base allowance use 1 m GEL in 13 million years; a million people farming Earth-style use 1 m GEL in 80,000 to 200,000 years;
propellant for one ascent vehicle is 15.7 t. **No purpose short of a lake or sea needs water from off Mars.** The exchangeable
inventory (20 to 40 m GEL, 2.9 to 5.8 x 10^18 kg) already exceeds a 10^6 km^2, 1 km deep sea. What it lacks is not water but **access
and a place to keep it liquid**: the ice is at the poles or under meters of cover, and an open surface loses 0.3 to 6 m per year (3.4).
An ocean of the Villanueva minimum (137 m GEL) would need 1.4 to 1.7 x 10^19 kg more water than the surface reservoirs hold
(97 to 117 m GEL), unless deep crustal water (3.1) could be brought up. Against
delivery: Mars's own disk intercepts about 0.07 kg/s of solar wind (0.05 kg/s of hydrogen); capturing **the entire solar wind**
(1.3 x 10^9 kg/s) and burning all its hydrogen to water would make 1.9 m GEL per year and need 7.7 x 10^9 kg/s of oxygen; all of
Venus's atmospheric hydrogen as water is 0.043 m GEL; Mercury's polar ice is 0.007 to 0.021 m GEL (D6).

## 4. Conflicts

1. **Kilograms per meter of GEL.** Area-based: 1 m GEL = 1.44 x 10^17 kg (D1; Kurokawa uses 1.4 x 10^17). Terada et al. 2009 and
   Lammer et al. 2003 used 8 x 10^42 molecules = 2.4 x 10^17 kg per m (as footnoted by Kurokawa), 1.66 x larger. Their loss figures
   (18 to 78 m, 14 to 34 m) become 31 to 133 m and 24 to 58 m in the area convention. **Convert before comparing any two GEL numbers.**
2. **Polar deposits.** Plaut (2007) gives the SPLD as 11 +/- 1.4 m GEL treating the volume as water; as pure ice at 917 kg/m^3 it is
   10.2 m (D1). The pre-radar MOLA total for both caps was 16 to 22 m; Kurokawa's radar-era total is 20 to 30 m (2.8 to 3.2 x 10^6 km^3).
   Different instruments (topography only versus radar to the base) and the volume-as-water convention.
3. **Present inventory: 20 to 30, 34, or 20 to 40 m GEL.** Kurokawa 2014 and Jakosky and Hallis 2024: 20 to 30 (polar deposits, or
   "exchangeable" water); Carr and Head 2015 as quoted by Clifford and McCubbin: about 34 (adds mid-latitude mantles and ground ice);
   Scheller 2021: 20 to 40 (atmosphere and ice, citing several estimates). Definition (what counts as near-surface) and vintage.
4. **How much CO2 is available to warm Mars.** McKay and Marinova 2001 (and Zubrin and McKay 1993, 300 to 600 mbar) assume enough
   frozen and adsorbed CO2 may exist for a warm, thick state (about 200 kPa needed); Green et al. 2017 state a 4 C warming would release
   the north polar CO2 and start melting the water. Jakosky and Edwards 2018 (MVT 3.11 and its Conflict 20): about 20 mbar is mobilizable,
   giving under 10 K of the 60 K needed. The later, peer-reviewed inventory uses MAVEN, MRO, Odyssey and Mars Express data. **Use
   Jakosky and Edwards; the runaway scenarios in Marinova 2005 and McKay 2001 are conditional on CO2 that is not found.**
5. **Deep crustal water.** Wright et al. 2024: 1 to 2 km GEL at 11.5 to 20 km depth; Sun et al. 2025: 520 to 780 m GEL at 5.4 to 8 km;
   Jakosky and Hallis 2024: inferred 315 to 1,000 m, the upper bound being the pore space of the top 10 km. Different depth windows and
   one seismometer (InSight) for both seismic papers; both extrapolate a local inversion to the planet.
6. **Water lost to space.** Constant present-day rates: 3.6 to 25 m GEL over 4.2 Gyr (Jakosky 2018), 3 to 25 m (Scheller); MAVEN
   rates scaled with solar evolution: at least 23 m (Jakosky 2018); D/H minimum: 51 to 152 m over all history (Kurokawa, two stages
   summed); Jakosky and Hallis: 110 to 570 m; Scheller: negligible to about 70% of the initial inventory. The D/H method needs the size
   of the present reservoir and the fractionation factor; the escape-rate method needs the past Sun; the sink tally needs unobserved
   reservoirs. The spread is real and not resolved.
7. **Early water.** Villanueva 2015: at least 137 m; Kurokawa: 97 to 150 m at 4.5 Ga (minimum, polar reservoir only); Scheller: 100 to
   1,500 m exchangeable; Jakosky and Hallis: 685 to 1,970 m in all sinks; McKay and Marinova 2001: "a few meters" (noble-gas and older
   isotope arguments) to "up to 1 km" (geology). Method and what is counted (exchangeable versus total).
8. **Magnetic shield location.** Green et al. 2017: a compact 1 to 2 T dipole at Mars-Sun L1. Bamford et al. 2022: a narrow
   multi-coil L1 system "would offer a significantly lower mass option", but notes the shallow L1 well, the moving L1 point over Mars's
   eccentric orbit, and the undulating magnetotail. DuPont and Murphy 2021: superconductor mass scales as a^-3, so the compact L1 magnet
   is "unfeasible" (about 10^16 kg); a planet-encircling loop (about 10^9 kg) is the feasible design. DuPont and Murphy apply the
   superconductor critical-field limit explicitly; Bamford works to one significant figure without that constraint. **And whether a shield
   would retain water at all:** Green's premise (stop solar-wind stripping and the atmosphere grows) against MAVEN (the field-sensitive
   channels, ion escape and sputtering, are about 0.2 of 2 to 3 kg/s) and Gunell 2018 (a field can raise escape). See section 7, H3.
9. **Mars escape rate.** Jakosky 2018: 2 to 3 kg/s (H plus O, one Mars year, all processes); BIRA on Gunell 2018: Venus, Earth, Mars
   "similar (in the approximate 0.5-2 kg/s range)". Probably ion and heavy-species escape in the second versus total H plus O in the first.
10. **Evaporation of liquid water at Mars conditions.** Sears and Moore 2005: 1.01 mm/hr measured, 0.73 corrected to Mars gravity;
    buoyancy theory 2.56; Kuznetz and Gan data 0.25; Hecht data 1.9 (all at 7 mbar, 0 C). Kling 2019: 0.34 to 3.34 m/yr at 500 mbar and
    273 K with wind 1 to 10 m/s. Pressure, wind, humidity, gravity correction, and chamber versus model.
11. **Importing volatiles by impact.** Zubrin and McKay 1993: ammonia bodies steered from 12 AU, each adding volatiles and heat. McKay
    and Marinova 2001 (same second author): "no foreseeable technology" for impacts that add rather than remove volatiles, citing impact
    erosion (Melosh and Vickery 1989). The later paper reflects the impact-erosion argument; the earlier gives no erosion calculation.
12. **Liquid water at the south polar base.** Lauro et al. 2021: brine bodies; Smith et al. 2021: frozen smectite clays. Unresolved.
13. **Where liquid is allowed.** A search summary of Haberle 2001 says about 29% of the surface for up to 37 sols; Lobitz 2001 gives up to
    34% of the year at one site. Different measures (area versus fraction of the year) and inputs (GCM versus spacecraft maps).

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

- **Water is present but not where it is warm or easy.** Surface-accessible water is 20 to 40 m GEL, mostly polar; mid-latitude ice lies
  under 1 to 10 m of cover; equatorward of about 45 degrees the near-surface holds only 2 to 10% water-equivalent hydrogen, and loose soil
  1.5 to 3 wt% (3.1). Hundreds of meters GEL more is in minerals or 5 to 20 km down, beyond present drilling (3.1).
- **Exposed water does not last.** At 0 C and 7 mbar an open surface loses about 0.7 mm per hour (several meters a year if it stayed that
  warm); ice at -15 C sublimes at 0.3 to 1.0 mm per hour in a chamber; an open lake disappears in under 60 years at any pressure from
  7 mbar to 3 bar unless resupplied (3.4). Any open water body needs a cover (ice, structure or aerogel) or continual resupply.
- **Liquid is allowed only in narrow windows.** Pressure exceeds the 611 Pa triple point only in lowlands and basins, and only part of the
  year is warm enough; brines extend the window down to about -20 C (3.4).
- **Water vapor cannot pressurize the air.** Even at 0 C the vapor adds at most 612 Pa; at typical temperatures under 1 Pa (D3). Delivered
  water ends up as ice or liquid, not as atmosphere.
- **Water lost to space is slow on any engineering timescale.** Today 1.5 to 16 kg/s; 1 m GEL lasts 0.3 to 3 billion years; even a
  thousandfold rise in upper-atmosphere hydrogen leaves 1 m GEL lasting about 0.6 million years (3.3, D2). Losses to the ground (freezing,
  sublimation onto the poles, draining into pore space, binding in minerals) are the fast losses (3.4).
- **Dust storms and solar storms raise loss in bursts.** Regional storms raise hydrogen loss 5 to 10 x; the 2018 global storm put 20 x the
  water into the upper atmosphere; an ICME raised oxygen loss about 20 x (3.3, 3.6).
- **A settlement's water need is small against the planet's.** A million people on the mature-base allowance use 1 m GEL in 13 million
  years open-loop; one ascent vehicle needs about 16 t of water; Earth-style open farming is the largest use at 2,000 to 5,000 L per
  person-day, and on Mars that water leaves the field (3.5).
- **Breathable air is not a water problem.** It needs 13 to 30 kPa of O2 and at least 10 to 30 kPa of buffer gas; Mars has about 0.02 kPa
  of N2, and a 30 kPa N2 buffer is 1.2 x 10^18 kg, 2,800 times what the atmosphere holds (3.7, D8). Plants and microbes need far less:
  about 1 kPa total, 0.1 to 1 kPa N2.
- **The surface radiation environment is set by the thin air.** 0.64 mSv/day GCR now; an atmosphere of about 38.5 kPa would give an
  Earth-equal mass column (MVT 3.3, D7).

## 7. Candidate handwaves (flagged, not accepted; the developer decides)

- **H1. Making a sea or ocean by import.** A 10^6 km^2, 1 km sea is 10^18 kg; a 137 m GEL ocean needs 1.4 to 1.7 x 10^19 kg beyond what
  is on the surface. Capturing the entire solar wind and supplying its oxygen would add 1.9 m GEL per year (D6); all of Venus's hydrogen
  makes 0.043 m GEL. Any import scheme at ocean scale needs a source and a transport rate that no study found here supplies. Note that
  **Mars's own surface ice (20 to 40 m GEL) exceeds any lake or sea short of an ocean**; the limit is warming, not water (3.5).
- **H2. Open liquid water at today's pressure and temperature.** It boils, evaporates or freezes within hours to decades (3.4). Making it
  stable needs about 60 K of warming; the CO2 to do it with greenhouse effect is not available (MVT 3.11), and the alternatives
  (fluorinated gases needing about 100,000 Mt of fluorine; nanorods at 2.6 to 2.7 Mt/yr with an untested 10-year particle lifetime) are
  model results only (3.4).
- **H3. A magnetic shield as the means of keeping water.** On MAVEN numbers the solar-wind-driven channels are about 0.2 of 2 to 3 kg/s;
  thermal H escape and photochemical O escape continue under any shield, and a planetary field can raise escape (Gunell). The compact L1
  magnet is "unfeasible" on superconductor physics (about 10^16 kg); a planet-girdling loop is about 10^9 kg, 0.2 to 0.5 GA, 0.1 to 100 GW.
  No peer-reviewed simulation of retention with a shield was found (3.6). **A shield chiefly addresses storm-time bursts and surface
  radiation, not the steady water loss, which is already slow.**
- **H4. Nitrogen for a breathable atmosphere.** 1.2 x 10^18 kg of N2 for a 30 kPa buffer; no Mars reservoir of that size is identified
  (nitrate is hypothesized, not measured); import would be about 1.4 x 10^5 ammonia bodies of 10^10 t each (D8).
- **H5. Volatile delivery by impact.** Each 10^10 t impact releases about 10 TW-years (about 70,000 one-megaton equivalents); McKay and
  Marinova 2001 state that impacts adding net volatiles have no foreseeable technology because of impact erosion (3.7, Conflict 11).
- **H6. Raising deep crustal water.** The seismic reservoirs (0.5 to 2 km GEL) lie at 5.4 to 20 km depth under one seismometer; no
  extraction study exists (3.1).
- **H7. Delivered water staying put.** Warmed ground ice could drain into empty pore space (Ansari 2024); 30 to 99% of Mars's original water
  went into crustal minerals (Scheller 2021). Delivered water is subject to the same sinks.
