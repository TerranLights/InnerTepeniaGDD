## 2. Radio physics and "standard" frequencies for a Peninsula-built network (developer request, 2026-10-04) — **RESEARCHED 2026-10-04: `Research_Logs/Radio_Physics_Peninsula_2026-10-04.md`** (summary at the end of this section)

**Why:** the developer's idea is that people in the Peninsula region (the Palmer subnet) would naturally, perhaps some time later, begin building their own comms networks, and wants to know (a) the physics of radio broadcasting and propagation, (b) whether there are frequencies understood as "standard broadcast frequencies" (or similar: international calling, distress, broadcast, time-signal or common-use frequencies) that people could realistically converge on to communicate over long distances, and (c) how far those transmissions could actually travel.

**Scope given to the research agent:** propagation band by band (ground wave, NVIS, HF skywave, VHF line of sight, repeaters, satellite) with realistic ranges at modest power; ground conductivity of sea, ice, snow and rock; high-latitude and polar effects (auroral oval, polar cap absorption, solar cycle) and the Peninsula's geomagnetic latitude; noise; the real international framework of standard distress, calling, time-signal and broadcast frequencies, and which are actually used for Peninsula and Antarctic coordination today; why communities converge on a frequency (CB channel 9/19, marine channel 16, ham calling frequencies, RFDS); equipment a settled community could keep up; and a pairwise distance and link-feasibility table for the eight Palmer-subnet cities (Palmer City, Puerto Abrigo, Rothera, Esperanza, Marambio, Contrapunto, Pergamino, Signy). Output goes to `Research_Logs/` when it returns. Physics only; no lore. Note: Arcanet and the subnets already exist in canon; this research is about what a settled community could physically build and where.

### Result of topic 1 (agent report, 2026-10-04; spot-check before relying on it)
- **Documented, usable:** the first Antarctic radio link was Australian (Mawson's 1911 to 14 expedition: spark transmitter, Macquarie Island relay, wireless engineer Walter Hannam, a founder of the Wireless Institute of Australia). ANARE ran on HF for decades (Casey was a relay hub for Mawson and Davis in 1978; Australia's Antarctic HF was the last HF service the national carrier provided). The same Australian amateur community built both Antarctic wireless and the outback pedal-radio network (Kauper, Traeger; the Flying Doctor galah sessions). The AAD still recruits radio operators with outback or field-party experience. Field scheds are policy (twice daily; Macquarie nightly). Distinctive Australian items: the WYSSA telegraphic code, group-form call signs and SARTIME/SKED vocabulary in aviation, VK0 call signs; Australian-made Codan HF sets are issued by BAS.
- **International standard, NOT Australian:** the phonetic alphabet, digit pronunciation ("niner"), UTC, MAYDAY/PAN PAN, ROGER/WILCO/OVER/OUT, Q codes. **Australian, American and British military voice procedure is literally one document (ACP 125, shared by the CCEB).** The program differences (AAD, USAP, BAS) are program policy, not national culture.
- **Would be invention:** that Antarctic nets descend from Flying Doctor or Traeger procedure (no documented link beyond the shared amateur community; no Traeger use in Antarctica found); that Australians wrote a procedure that became the Antarctic or world standard (none found); that Australian manner causes procedure.
- **The developer's principle is real but has counter-cases:** Q code, Morse apparatus and allied procedure each became a standard; but two competing spelling tables coexisted until 1959, and the ITU's own figure words (Nadazero...) were ignored in practice in favor of ICAO digits. ITU explicitly allows national tables.
- **Dead ends:** the full COMNAP AFIM frequency tables are restricted; "ACOC" not found; the 1963 and 1969 Antarctic Treaty telecom recommendations not retrieved; AWS used as radio repeaters not found; no evidence the English dominance of Antarctic radio has been measured.

### Result of topic 2 (agent report, 2026-10-04; models and search summaries are flagged in the log; spot-check before relying on it)
- **The Peninsula is a good radio location.** Its geomagnetic latitude is only about -50 to -55 degrees (Palmer -51.4 AACGM; Casey -80.5; the south geomagnetic pole sits off the Wilkes Land coast), so it is sub-auroral and on the fringe of polar-cap blackouts, unlike East Antarctica. The aurora is overhead only at about G3-class storms. Lightning noise is scarce, so the settlement's own man-made noise is the limit (a quiet site is worth about 14 dB).
- **Range envelope for a modest 100 W station:** VHF is line-of-sight only (26 km with 10 m masts; every one of the 28 city pairs is blocked at that height; mountain-top repeaters work Palmer-Rothera, Palmer-Pergamino, Contrapunto-Pergamino; Signy is out of VHF reach entirely). **Regional (30 to 400 km): NVIS HF on 1.8 to 7 MHz** works 24 h in summer; in winter at solar minimum the usable band narrows to about 1.8 to 2.5 MHz at night. **400 to 1,400 km (Signy, Ushuaia, Punta Arenas): HF skywave 2.5 to 14 MHz**, feasible at 100 W with a simple antenna in quiet noise, marginal in rural noise, dependable at 1 kW or with +6 dBi antennas. **3,000+ km (Buenos Aires, McMurdo): needs 14 to 28 MHz in daylight and 1 kW**; Palmer-McMurdo needs both power and directional antennas. A real data point: Livingston Island to Spain, 12,710 km, 250 W into a 7.5 m monopole, over 95% availability on 8 to 10 MHz from 21 to 04 UTC in summer.
- **Broadcast reach:** AM at the water's edge by ground wave to the 54 dBuV/m contour: 1 kW 314 km, 10 kW 515 km, 50 kW 665 km over sea; only 13 to 35 km over ice and 35 to 84 km over rock, so siting matters more than power. FM from a mountain top covers about 140 to 200 km. A shortwave broadcast (LRA36 at Esperanza, 15 476 kHz) is rarely heard in North America.
- **Standard frequencies people could converge on:** the real anchors are VHF channel 16 (156.8 MHz) for local hailing; 2182 kHz and 4125/6215/8291 kHz marine HF distress; 3023/5680 kHz aeronautical SAR; 121.5 MHz; the time-signal bands (WWV 5, 10, 15 MHz, which also broadcast geophysical alerts); AM 525 to 1705 kHz and FM 88 to 108 MHz in Region 2 (the whole Peninsula and Signy are in ITU Region 2); amateur emergency centers of activity at 3750, 7060 and 14300 kHz; and the Royal Flying Doctor Service precedent of a continent-scale community working 2 to 8 MHz with a handful of shared channels (VKS-737). **Corrections to the developer's mental list: 500 kHz is NAVDAT-only since WRC-23 (no longer distress); 243 MHz is outside the distress appendix.** A natural regional calling band would be 80/75 m (3.5 to 4.0 MHz) and 40 m (7.0 to 7.3 MHz). Real Peninsula use today: Palmer field parties check in on 4125 and 11553 kHz and VHF channel 27; McMurdo air-ground 4770/5100/5400/7995/9032/11553 kHz.
- **Levers and caveats:** a +6 dBi antenna at each end is worth about an order of magnitude in power; weak-signal digital modes lower the needed signal by about 30 dB (the biggest technology lever); the solar cycle peaked at 160.9 (Oct 2024) and NOAA expects about 9 by 2030 (the harder case for upper bands); antennas on ice need elevated radials; VOACAP and P.533 disagree in winter.
- **Geometry:** Palmer to Puerto Abrigo is 27.4 km (not 30 to 50); Palmer to Rothera 361, to Esperanza 375, Marambio 361, Contrapunto 385, Pergamino 297, Signy 1,037; Rothera to Signy 1,319.
- **Dead ends:** the public AFIM has no frequency tables; Rothera and IAATO HF frequencies (summary only); Peninsula-specific VHF ducting, amateur records and repeater ranges; primary ice conductivity at MF/HF; sea-ice conductivity.

### Follow-up results (2026-10-04): `Research_Logs/Radio_Physics_Followup_PergaminoSigny_Relay_SantaLuce_2026-10-04.md` (spot-check before relying on it)
- **Pergamino to Signy (807 km, 96% sea): yes, holdable on HF.** Summer: 7 MHz around the clock, 10 MHz by day, 3.5 to 5 MHz at night; winter: 2.5 to 3.5 MHz overnight, 5 to 10 MHz about 12 to 21 UTC. Weak-signal digital works all 24 h even at 10 W; 100 W voice is fine in quiet noise and marginal in rural-noise winter (the two models bracket it: 3 to 4 h vs 24 h); +6 dBi at each end or 1 kW makes voice 24 h in all conditions. MF ground wave is useless at 100 W; AM reception at Signy needs a shoreline transmitter of at least 10 kW and is DX-grade only.
- **Elephant/Clarence relay:** a relay changes HF signal-to-noise by only 1 to 8 dB; its real gains are in the hardest case (rural-noise July voice: 3 to 4 h direct vs 10 to 18 h via the island) and for MF (an island AM relay at 10 kW would reach Signy at 55 to 57 dBuV/m, about 14 dB better than Pergamino direct). VHF line of sight to Signy exists from nowhere; Mount Irving (Clarence, about 1,950 m, unverified) has only marginal line of sight to the King George peaks. Access is poor (Point Wild is HSM 53 and landing is "opportunistic"; the only facility is the seasonal Brazilian Goeldi refuge at Cape Lookout); no quantified failure rate for unattended polar relays was found. **Esperanza as the Signy trunk gives about the same HF benefit without an island site.**
- **Santa Luce (1,918 km from Signy; dipole geomagnetic -67, AACGM -62):** weak-signal digital 24 h at 10 W; voice at 100 W with simple antennas is marginal (6 to 13 h/day); reliable at 100 W with +6 dBi at both ends or at 1 kW in quiet noise; rural noise needs both. The Santa Luce end is auroral and subject to polar-cap events (about 82 days/yr at Kp>=5, 33 at Kp>=6, 12 at Kp>=7; about 2.3 S2-class radiation storms/yr that can black out HF for days). The chain Esperanza-Signy-Santa Luce (2,581 km) is equal or better than the direct 2,025 km hop (up to +6 h/day) and keeps the first half sub-auroral.

### Floating relay in the Weddell Sea (agent report, 2026-10-04): `Research_Logs/Weddell_Floating_Relay_2026-10-04.md` (NSIDC sea ice, ERA5 waves and wind, AWI moorings; spot-check before relying on it)
- **Verdict:** no Weddell region is both calm and safe for an ordinary ship to float for weeks. Open summer water north of about 65 S is rough (mean Hs 1.4 to 2.2 m, P99 4 to 5.7 m, maxima 9 to 11 m) and refreezes from April; the interior pack is wave-free but drifts 3.75 to 6.2 km/day and crushes hulls (Endurance). The one sheltered open strip is the ice-front coast off Brunt/Halley (Hs about 0.6 m in DJF), open only about two to three weeks in late December and reached by icebreaker.
- **What survives:** purpose-built ice-class ships frozen in (Polarstern/MOSAiC 389 days at 15 t diesel/day with six support ships, about 200k euros/day; Ice Station Weddell 1992 floe camp, 4 months), subsurface moorings with the top instrument at about 150 m (AWI; about a third lost). No surface mooring is used in the Weddell ice zone; no floating HF relay precedent found.
- **"Hydroelectric" does not work (calc):** a platform drifting with the water sees no relative flow; measured Weddell currents give about 1 to 14 W from a 5 to 20 m2 rotor. Wind (about 140 to 330 W mean from a 2 m2 turbine) plus batteries plus diesel is the only workable mix (100 W mean load = 350 L diesel/yr); winter solar is nil May to July.
- **Radio:** a seawater-grounded monopole gains about +4 dBi at low angles, about 14 dB better than a monopole on ice, but a few metres of ice over water behaves like ice at 7 to 14 MHz. A floating relay adds only about -2 to +6 dB (P.533) or -1.5 to +12 dB (VOACAP) over the direct hop; the direct Peninsula-Belgrano hop is already 24 h at 1 kW or +6 dBi. Only the hard case (100 W, 0 dBi, voice, January) benefits.
- **Legal:** Antarctic Treaty Article VI keeps high-seas rights; Article VII.5 requires notice of expeditions; Madrid Protocol impact assessment; IMO Polar Code applies; radio licensing not researched.

### Australia and South America (agent report, 2026-10-04): `Research_Logs/Why_Australia_Links_South_America_2026-10-04.md` (hit the search cap partway; Southern Ocean weather and joint-research angles not searched; spot-check before relying on it)
- **Strongest reason is geometry (calc):** the shortest Australia to Atlantic-side South America paths cross Antarctica. Sydney-Santiago 11,350 km dips to about 62 S; Sydney-Buenos Aires 11,800 km reaches about 69 S; Perth-Buenos Aires 12,590 km reaches about 86 S, near the Pole. A settled Antarctica would turn the over-ice shortcut into a real corridor.

Casey is only about 2,180 km from McMurdo but about 5,700 km from the Peninsula, so the Australian sector is more naturally tied to the Ross Sea (and New Zealand) than to the Peninsula. Any Casey to Ross Sea to Marie Byrd Land to Peninsula chain passes through the NZ Ross Dependency and unclaimed Marie Byrd Land.

No bilateral Australia-Chile or Australia-Argentina Antarctic agreement found (the NZ-Chile 2018 arrangement is the only analogue).

### The Southern Hemisphere "ring" (agent report, 2026-10-04): `Research_Logs/Southern_Hemisphere_Ring_Australia_SouthAmerica_SouthAfrica_2026-10-04.md` (hit the search cap midway; spot-check before relying on it)
- **Headline: the real structure is a ring of five, not a triangle.** Five states hold Antarctic search-and-rescue responsibility (Argentina, Australia, Chile, New Zealand, South Africa) and five gateway cities carry the logistics (Punta Arenas, Ushuaia, Cape Town, Hobart, Christchurch). The Antarctic Treaty Secretariat is in Buenos Aires, CCAMLR in Hobart, COMNAP in Christchurch. A Christchurch/Ross Sea node is justified.
- **Why they talk (most durable first):** (1) search-and-rescue and medevac, since every settlement still needs a gateway hospital, a rescue center and a flight corridor, and even the shortest Antarctic rescues are 1,000 to 1,200 km, with 5 to 6 sailing days for some coasts; (2) gateway logistics; (3) great-circle geometry; (4) Southern Hemisphere weather-data scarcity and the ozone/Southern Annular Mode link; (5) HF space-weather forecasting (South Africa's SANSA and Australia's BoM already supply it); (6) SKA (Murchison and Karoo) and ESO/GMT. Weakest: direct trade and groupings (the Cairns Group is the only body spanning all three and it is a trade lobby).
- **Great circles (calc, spherical):** Perth-Cape Town 8,698 km stays over open ocean (southernmost 44.5 S); Cape Town to Buenos Aires, Santiago and Punta Arenas stay at 41 to 56 S. **Perth to Buenos Aires (12,591 km), Santiago (12,711) and Punta Arenas (10,529) cross East Antarctica**, passing 86 km from Casey, 243 km from Concordia and 97 km from Concordia respectively and 384 to 490 km from the Pole; Cape Town-Christchurch (11,015 km) also crosses Antarctica (passes 206 km from Concordia). Santiago-Sydney (11,346 km) stays at 61.8 S; Hobart-Ushuaia (8,642 km) crosses about 710 km of coast.
- **Worldbuilding reading (agent's inference):** the trans-Antarctic link is real only for Australia to South America; put polar relay capacity on the Casey-Pole-Weddell/Peninsula corridor (Concordia and the Pole are near-route sites). Perth-Cape Town and Cape Town-South America are ordinary Southern Ocean circuits needing only island or ocean relays. Radio-quiet zones (Murchison, Karoo) protect about 70 to 100 MHz and up, so they do not restrict HF. No source says Australia dominates Southern Hemisphere HF culture (the developer's premise stays an invention, plausible from Australia's 53 million km2 rescue region and Hobart's role).
- **Gaps:** no documented two-way HF contact among Perth, Cape Town and South America found; no single regional SAR agreement among the five found; sea-ice forecast producers and Montevideo's role not established.

### Drake Passage sea conditions (agent report, 2026-10-04): `Research_Logs/Drake_Sea_Conditions_2026-10-04.md` (altimetry, ERA5, NSIDC; may be interim; spot-check before relying on it)
- **Answer to "most violent water on Earth": no, on every wave metric computed.** Mid-passage mean Hs 3.6 m, P90 5.4, P99 7.9 (altimetry); the sectors south of Australia and Kerguelen average 4.4 m (P99 8.8 to 8.9); the Drake ranks 29th of 36 longitude windows at 55 to 58 S. The winter North Atlantic has about 1.5 to 2% of samples above 10 m versus about 0.3% in the Drake, and holds the 19.0 m record (Southern Ocean record: 14.9 m Hs, 23.8 m single wave, Campbell Island 2018). The "funnel" claim has no measurement behind it; mean Hs falls eastward through the narrowing.
- **What is true:** persistently rough and rarely calm. By month, mean Hs is about 3.1 m in January and about 3.9 m in Aug to Oct; storms of 14 to 16 m Hs occurred on 35 event-days in 1985 to 2026. Modeled 48 h crossing (calc): in Dec to Feb about 45% meet Hs above 4 m somewhere, 20% above 5 m, 7% above 6 m, and only about 9% stay under 2.5 m; Jun to Aug is worse (67% above 4 m, 22% above 6 m). Swell is about 73% of wave energy, mostly from the west (beam seas for a north-south route).
- **Wind and ice:** F8+ gales about 1.4% of summer samples mid-passage (5.8% in the Cape Horn approaches in summer, 9.2% in winter); sea ice essentially absent from the open Drake in summer, appearing on the South Shetlands side from about June; spray icing essentially absent Dec to Feb, 26 to 32% of winter hours in the South Shetlands/Bransfield.
- **Casualties:** the often-repeated "10,000 sailors, 800 ships" at Cape Horn has no traceable source; the Ushuaia museum's compiled list has about 54 vessels south of the Horn for 1815 to 1900 with about 1,200 stated deaths; the modern record is mostly broken windows, with one fatal wave strike (Viking Polaris, 29 Nov 2022: 11 to 16 m wave, 1 dead, 8 injured) in roughly 7,000 passenger crossings (an incomplete count, not a measured rate).
- **Trend:** altimeter Hs trends are not clearly significant in the Drake (P99 +1.8 cm/yr over 1993 to 2025, accuracy about plus or minus 0.2 cm/yr for means); projections point to Southern Ocean Hs increasing by 2081 to 2100.
