## 1. How Australian Antarctic and remote-area radio worked and grew

### 1A. ANARE radio history

**Pre-ANARE (Mawson, 1911–14)**
- The Australasian Antarctic Expedition set up a transmitter at Commonwealth Bay and a relay station on Macquarie Island. They used a rotary-arc transmitter and Morse to send weather and personal messages to Hobart [2][6][R].
- The AAD "Wireless of Wireless Hill" page says the equipment was a "German–made Telefunken 1.5 kilowatt 'spark' transmitter" [17][F].
- Macquarie Island first signalled the SS Ulimaroa on 13 Feb 1912. Commonwealth Bay reached Australia via Wireless Hill (Perth) in Feb 1913 [17][F].
- Mawson's own account describes the masts. Two masts were up by 1 Sept (1912). The structure was destroyed by wind on 13 Oct ("Black Sunday"). Receivers picked up "atmospherics" and drift-snow discharges [18][F].
- Walter Hannam was the wireless engineer, call sign 2YH, and a founding member of the Wireless Institute of Australia (WIA) [19][F]. A trade-magazine profile says he "connected Antarctica to stations in Hobart, Melbourne, Sydney, and Wellington" [20][F].
- The Hannam profile gives the transmitter as "1.5 W". See Contradictions.

**ANARE era (1947 on)**
- Heard Island opened Dec 1947 and Macquarie Island Mar 1948. Stations used 500-watt Morse transmitters, and "similar facilities were installed in 1954" at Mawson [2][R].
- AAD history page [1][R]:
  - In the 1950s, Morse linked ships, RAAF survey aircraft and ground stations.
  - "At each ANARE station, messages were exchanged with Australia, and other nation's Antarctic stations, via Morse code."
  - "Trained RAAF radio technicians maintained the High Frequency (HF) transmitter, receiver units and antenna systems, while radio operators used typewriters to record received messages, and Morse keys to transmit."
  - Early-1960s teleprinters "largely replaced" Morse to Australia, but "Morse code was still used to communicate with ships and field parties."
  - 10 km walkie-talkies were used for field work and ship-to-shore.
  - Voice "radphone" calls were booked with the station radio operator, routed through "Sydney Radio" and the International Telephone Exchange. They had no privacy and suffered fade-outs.
- The Mawson party's names are in [16][R]: Bob Dovers, Lem Macey, Bill Harvey, Bill Storer, Jeff Gleadell, John Russell, Bruce Stinear, Bob Dingle, Robert Summers and Georges Schwartz. A search summary says Macey was radio operator and second-in-charge [U]. [16] itself does not state his role.
- **WYSSA** was an ANARE-specific five-letter telegraphic code of set phrases, sent by teleprinter to families. Example from AAD: "WYNAN YIGUM YIKYR WYSWO, John" [1][R]. A search summary adds a limit of about 160 codes per year in the early days [U].
- AAD's Davis Station history page captions a 1957 photo "Radio operator A. Hawker … Davis (VLZ)" [1][R]. A 2024 AAD post says Davis still uses "VLZ Davis" for logging official communications [14][F]. The AAD 2006 magazine article names Mawson's station as "VLV" [3][F].
- Telex was introduced in 1962. 10 kW transmitters came in 1968. Radio-telephones were added at Davis and Mawson in 1969 [2][R].
- In 1978 a leased 50-baud telex circuit between Casey and Sydney opened. "Casey acted as a relay station for Mawson and Davis" [2][R].
- **HF problems** [2][R]:
  - Interference from other stations.
  - "Total blackouts due to polar cap absorption."
  - Low speed.
  - Australian Antarctic operations "were the last HF service provided by Australia's Overseas Telecommunications Commission (OTC, now part of Telstra), which wanted to close its HF service."
- Satellite transition [2][5][6][R/F]:
  - Inmarsat terminals went in through the mid-1980s.
  - A 1986 contract was let to OTC for ANARESAT (Intelsat, 7.3 m dishes).
  - Earth stations were commissioned at Davis (Mar 1987), Mawson (Jan 1988), Casey (Mar 1988) and Macquarie (Dec 1988).
  - Capacity was 64 kbps by 1992. It was 1.2 Mbps to Casey and Davis by 2015.
- **What HF does now.** HF "is still used for field parties, ships and aircraft out of VHF range with the addition of a SELCALL emergency calling system" [2][R]. Satellites "signalled the end of HF as the primary mode … though HF remained available for backup" [5][F].
- At AAD stations, radio base stations connect to operator positions over fibre. All radio traffic is digitally recorded for "more than three months", and operators can replay the last transmission [6][R].
- Station staff also run amateur stations, for example VK0D at Davis [14][F] and VK0WN at Casey [15][F].

### 1B. Remote-area Australian traditions and their documented lineage

**Documented links between Antarctic and outback radio**
- Hannam, the Mawson wireless engineer, was a WIA founder (2YH) [19][F].
- The WIA's history page says Flynn "studied wireless himself and enlisted the aid of amateurs and experts" [52][F].
- On the Flying Doctor radio, "South Australian amateurs, together with Harry Kauper 5BG and Alf Treager 5AX, provided the necessary 'knowhow'" [52][F].
- So both Antarctic radio (1911) and the outback network (1925 on) drew on the same Australian amateur community. This is a documented community overlap, not a documented protocol lineage.

**RFDS and outback radio**
- Silicon Chip [56][R, preview only]:
  - Flynn tested radio in 1925 with George Towns and Harry Kauper.
  - In 1926 Kauper built three sets for Arltunga, Hermannsburg and Alice Springs.
  - The first pedal radio, Nov 1928, was a "2-valve regenerative receiver" with a 1–1.5 W CW transmitter on 2230 kHz.
  - A 1930 pedal radio had two transmitters, on 2020 and 8630 kHz.
  - The same article says voice was "out of the question" for the portable gear at that stage.
- Voice sessions: "By 1935, voice transmission was possible and this was when the 'galah' sessions began" [U]. The Australian National Dictionary Centre entry confirms the term, citing F. Flynn, *Northern Gateway* (1963): "The women's radio hour, held regularly night and morning and referred to everywhere as the 'Galah Session'" [57][F].
- Traeger exports [U]:
  - Pedal sets reached Nigeria as late as 1962.
  - By 1970, "the School of the Air in Canada, and 20 developing countries" used Traeger sets.
  - Search summary only. I did not verify these at the primary level.
- School of the Air [U]:
  - It opened 8 June 1951 in Alice Springs, using the RFDS HF network.
  - Adelaide Miethke proposed it in 1950.
- Codan lineage [58][U]:
  - EILCO (founded 1959) released an HF set for the School of the Air network in 1961.
  - It won a contract to re-equip 12 RFDS bases in the 1970s.
  - It exported to Papua New Guinea, the US and Africa.
  - "Aussie selcall" in Codan radios is an Australian ecosystem; selcall itself derives from CCIR Rec. 493 [W/U].
- The RFDS still maintains HF as backup, "24 hours a day, seven days week, across all States" (Nov 2010 statement) [60][F]. Procedures differ by state and by RFDS base [60][F].

**Antarctic-to-outback links I found**
- The AAD's hiring page for Communications Operators asks for "radio communication experience with remote field parties, for example outback travellers or vessels at sea" [10][F].
- It also asks for an Aeronautical Radio Operators Certificate (AROC) and lists the Long-Range Operator Certificate of Proficiency as desirable [10][F].
- The LROCP is administered through the Australian Maritime College for the ACMA [U].
- This shows a current institutional connection between outback and Antarctic radio, but only at the level of recruitment criteria.

**Not found**
- Any documented use of Traeger or Flying Doctor equipment or procedure in ANARE. Multiple searches returned only Traeger/RFDS material and Antarctic material separately.
- Any evidence for an Overland Telegraph legacy in ANARE radio. Not found.
- I found no ANARE procedure document that cites RFDS procedure.

### 1C. Current AAD communications practice

- **Field scheds.** "When you are out in the field you will normally be required to make twice-daily radio scheds" [7][R, page last updated 12 Aug 2010].
- **Equipment** [7][R]:
  - "All long term field huts are fitted with VHF radios, and in some cases, HF antennas."
  - Portable VHF and HF radios are issued by the Telecommunications Section.
  - Long-term field groups may receive Iridium or Inmarsat-M suitcase phones.
- **Macquarie Island** [12][R]:
  - Field communication is mainly VHF. Four repeaters serve the island.
  - Each person carries a SPOT satellite tracker.
  - "Nightly radio scheds take place … at set times daily", giving forecasts and news.
  - A search summary gives the station call sign as VJM and the sked frequency as 3023 kHz [U]. The 3023 kHz figure also appears on a 2012 hobbyist list [49][F].
- **VHF repeaters.** "Solar and wind-powered VHF repeaters located on mountain tops extend the coverage around the main station areas" [2][R]. A Mawson example is the Mt Henderson repeater, tested alongside the VHF radios in the Henderson, Rumdoodle and Fang huts [13][F].
- **Roles** [9][10][11][F]:
  - Winter staff are Telecommunications Officers – Radio.
  - Summer staff are Communications Operators. They "will provide a safety radio net for helicopters, C212 aircraft, the new A319 Airbus, and scientific research parties" [11][F].
  - The TOR role lists experience in HF, VHF repeaters, satellite phones and PLBs.
- **Training.** AAD field training covers "Radio procedure" as a core competency, plus "remote area communication and power systems" as a specialist topic [8][R].
- **SAR coordination.** AMSA and AAD signed an MOU on Antarctic search and rescue on 23 Apr 2008 that includes "communications arrangements" [64][R].
- **Aviation.** A trade source states the Melbourne FIR runs to the South Pole [U, not independently verified].
- **ACOC.** The "Antarctic Communications Operations Centre" or equivalent: **not found**. The AAD's own pages never use the term.
- **Satellite.** Starlink or OneWeb at AAD stations: not found. A 2022 AAD/Speedcast item mentions the ability to add LEO services, but I did not read it directly.

### 1D. Call signs and licensing

- **Australian ITU blocks** are AXA–AXZ, VHA–VNZ and VZA–VZZ [32][R: Appendix 42].
- **VK0** is Australian amateur practice for Antarctic operation. ACMA says VK0 and VK9 call signs are "assigned for 12 months" and re-assignable [54][R via Wayback].
- A search summary adds that VK0 call signs "must only be used when the operator is located in the Antarctic" and need evidence of travel [U].
- The WIA asked ACMA in 2026 to restore VK0 for Heard and Macquarie Islands, which currently use VK9 [53][F].
- **VK1 → VK0 history:** Wikipedia [W][55] says VK1 was used before VK0 "c. 1955 based on Antarctic treaties". That date cannot be tied to the Antarctic Treaty, which was signed in 1959. See Contradictions.
- ITU "does not assign call blocks to Antarctica" [W][55]. This is unverified at the primary level.
- ITU Radio Regulations 19.67–19.69: amateur and experimental call signs have prefix and digit rules, and the prohibition on digits 0 and 1 does not apply to amateur stations [33][R].
- Station call signs: Davis uses VLZ for official logging [14][F]. Mawson is VLV [3][F]. Casey: "VNJ" appears only in a search summary [U]. I did not verify Casey's older call sign.

## 2. Real protocol versus culture: Australia, the US and the UK

### 2A. What is international standard

**ITU Radio Regulations (RR)**
- Article 57: radiotelephone procedure "detailed in Recommendation ITU-R M.1171 shall be applicable" [33][R]. M.1171 prescribes "THIS IS", "over" and calling forms [38][R].
- When spelling is needed, "the phonetic spelling tables in Appendix 14 shall be used" (RR 57.7) [33][R].
- Appendix 14 [32][R]:
  - Letters are Alfa, Bravo … Juliett … X-ray.
  - Figures are the compound words "Nadazero, Unaone, Bissotwo, Terrathree, Kartefour, Pantafive, Soxisix, Setteseven, Oktoeight, Novenine", with "Decimal" and "Stop".
  - Clause 3: "stations of the same country, when communicating between themselves, may use any other table recognized by their administration."
- Distress words (RR 32/33) [33][R]:
  - MAYDAY "pronounced as the French expression 'm'aider'".
  - PAN PAN "pronounced as the French word 'panne'".
  - SECURITE.
- Appendix 15 lists aeronautical SAR carrier frequencies 3023 kHz and 5680 kHz, 121.5 MHz and 123.1 MHz [32][R].

**ICAO**
- Annex 10 Vol II 5.2.1.2.1: "air-ground radiotelephony communications shall be conducted in the language normally used by the station on the ground or in the English language" [43][R].
- ICAO language proficiency level 4 became required in 2008 [43][R].

**Allied military**
- ACP 125(G) (Nov 2016) is a Combined Communications-Electronics Board (CCEB) publication. The CCEB comprises "Australia, Canada, New Zealand, United Kingdom and United States" [42][R].
- So Australian, American and British military voice procedure is literally the same document.

### 2B. Documented differences, with what is national

| Feature | Standard text | Australia | US | UK |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Digit pronunciation | ACP 125(G) §305: WUN TOO TREE FOW-ER FIFE SIX SEV-EN AIT NINE-ER [42][R] | CASA AC 64.B-02: same, 9 = "NIN-er" [39][R] | AIM examples use "Niner" [41][R] | CAP 413: same, "NINER" [40][R] |
| Decimal | ICAO: "decimal" | "DAY-SEE-MAL" [39][R] | AIM example says "One Two Two Point Three"; AIM also states "ICAO procedures require … DECIMAL. The FAA will honor such usage" [41][R] | "DAYSEEMAL" [40][R] |
| Time | UTC | "UTC must be referenced when transmitting time" [39][R] | "FAA uses Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) for all operations … 'Zulu' may be used" [41][R] | "UTC is to be used at all times, unless specified" [40][R] |
| OVER / OUT | ACP 125: every transmission ends OVER or OUT [42][R] | OVER "not normally used in VHF or satellite" [39][R] | AIM examples use "over" on VHF [41][R] | OVER/OUT marked "Not normally used in U/VHF" [40][R]; "OUT" usage is a listed UK difference from ICAO [40][R] |
| ROGER / WILCO | WILCO includes ROGER, "the two prowords are not used together" [42][R] | ROGER "under no circumstances … in reply to a question requiring read back" [39][R] | AIM lists "Wilco, Roger, Affirmative, Negative" [41][R] | "(CALLSIGN) ROGER is used in the UK"; CAP 413 discourages "callsign-copy the weather" and wants "Roger callsign" [40][R] |
| "Copy" | Not in ACP 125(G) proword list (searched text) [42][R] | Not in CASA word list [39][R] | Not verified | Discouraged in CAP 413 [40][R] |
| Readability | ACP 125 §632: "Reports such as 'five by five', 'four by four,' etc., will not be used" [42][R] | CASA: scale 1–5 ("Unreadable" … "Perfectly readable") [39][R] | Not verified | Not verified |
| Call-sign style | ICAO | "Within Australian airspace, 'group form' is the preferred means" for callsigns [39][R] | n/a | Several UK-specific variants listed [40][R] |
| Australian SAR terms | n/a | SARTIME; SARWATCH "based on … scheduled reporting times (SKED)" [39][R] | n/a | n/a |

Interpretation:
- Digits, alphabet, UTC, ROGER/WILCO and OVER/OUT are international or CCEB-shared. They are not Australian or British or American traits.
- Genuinely Australian items documented here are group-form call signs in Australian airspace, SARTIME/SARWATCH/SKED terminology, and AROC certification.
- The UK has a formal "UK Differences from ICAO" appendix. Australia files its differences on Airservices Australia's website [65][R]. I could not retrieve the Annex 10 Vol II file (404).
- "Over" on VHF, "point" for the decimal, and "five by five" bans are program or service conventions, not national character. They are not documented as cultural traits.

- These differences are program policy. They are not shown to be cultural.

### 2D. International Antarctic coordination

**COMNAP and the AFIM**
- The AFIM is produced by COMNAP. Subscribers are National Antarctic Programs, non-governmental operators through a program or competent authority, and Rescue Coordination Centres [46][R].
- It is "restricted" and tables include "Communication Centers" [46][R].
- The full AFIM frequency data are **not public** (restricted document).
- ATCM Resolution 3 (2022) recommends operators be provided the AFIM [46][R].

**Shared HF and VHF (what I could document)**
- USAP's US-17 "Antarctic Common" circuit uses 4.770/7.995/11.553 MHz for check-ins [44][R]. USAP 9.032 MHz is for ATC [44][R].
- McMurdo Air Traffic "Mac Center" has been described as covering "essentially all flights to and from Antarctica" [48][F, 2001 article].
- BAS lists 9032 and 11553 kHz as "Air Traffic Control — South Pole and McMurdo", 4125 as Palmer local, 6649/10024 as Punta Arenas ATC, and 4530 etc. as Union Glacier ALE [45][R].
- A 2012 hobbyist table of AAD frequencies lists 5726, 9032 and 11256 kHz USB as "Mac Centre" aircraft frequencies [49][F, third-party, dated 2012].
- Aero VHF: USAP 118.2 (approach), 121.5 (guard), 123.45 (air-to-air), 129.7 (Traffic Information Broadcast by Aircraft, "primary Antarctic operational frequency") [44][R]. BAS lists 118.1 and 121.5 [45][R].
- Together (inference): the programs share aviation HF and emergency channels, but use their own field-party sked frequencies.
- The 3023 kHz HF sked frequency used by AAD Macquarie (per [49]) is the same as ITU's aeronautical SAR reference frequency [32][R]. The two sources match, but whether it is licensed as such I do not know.

**Antarctic Treaty telecommunications meetings**
- ATCM Recommendation VI-1 (Tokyo, 1970), English text from the Dutch treaty bulletin (OCR'd by me) [30][R]:
  - Governments adopt "as guide-lines the Proposals contained in the Final Report of the Second Antarctic Treaty Meeting on Telecommunications held in Buenos Aires in 1969."
  - The Considering clauses say there is a need "to organize technically compatible routes for telecommunications both within, and out of the Antarctic Treaty area".
- Recommendation VI-2: annual exchange of information on telecommunications facilities in a standard tabular format by 31 October [30][R]. It also authorizes addressees to "correspond with other addressees … on the co-ordination of communications necessary for cooperative activities".
- A first Antarctic Treaty Meeting on Telecommunications took place in Washington in 1963 [31][F]. The Buenos Aires 1969 proposals were to supersede it [29][R].
- The text of the 1963 and 1969 recommendations is **not retrieved**. The UTAS PDF is gated.
- Kawahara (1970), Antarctic Record No. 38, reports that WMO asked for a revised coordinated schedule for meteorological data, and that SCAR was asked to continue studies of HF unreliability [29][R].

## 3. How weather-station data and field comms are relayed

### 3A. Automatic weather stations (AWS)

**AAD network**
- 20 sites in East Antarctica plus one at Heard Island; first station 1982 [21][R].
- All use ARGOS via NOAA polar-orbiting satellites. Three-hourly synoptic data from most stations go by Service Argos to the WMO Global Telecommunication System [21][F].
- "Many of the AWS are never revisited after initial deployment, and continue to operate without maintenance for a decade or more." As of late 2013, active stations included Amery G3, Dome A, Eagle and Panda [21][F].
- AntAWS (ESSD 2023) [25][F]:
  - Most AWS get annual summer visits.
  - GC41 operated from Oct 1984 without maintenance access.
  - Batteries are charged by a small solar panel near the mast top.
  - Failure modes: wind sensors seized by cold, rime and frost on sensors, snow.
  - Iridium is an alternative used on some stations.
  - AAD designed its own three AWS models over two decades.
- Law Dome summit AWS: "a propeller and wind vane on a pole with a solar panel and batteries"; "entire AWS had a 1in thick layer of frost all over it, even the guy ropes"; a four-day round trip from Casey (about 120 km) [23][F].
- Dome A AWS [22][24][F]: Australia/China cooperation; AAD partnered with Climetrics Pty Ltd on AWS redesign; 2004 news says "via satellite" without specifying a system.
- US: the University of Wisconsin network uses Argos and some Iridium; roughly 63 active AWS [26][F].
- **AWS as radio repeaters or relays:** not found. The AAD AWS metadata and the AntAWS paper describe data collection only [21][25].
- Separate from AWS, repeaters exist: AAD solar and wind-powered mountaintop VHF repeaters [2][R], and Mt Henderson [13][F].

### 3B. Traverse and deep-field comms

- Casey–Law Dome traverse: a nine-person party with two Hägglunds and a tractor [search summary, U]. The AAD page I fetched [Law Dome 2021 news] contained no radio details.
- Lambert Glacier Basin traverses ran 1989–1995 [U]. I found no communications description. A 2002–03 German-Australian Prince Charles Mountains party of 34 included "communications officer" among its roles [pcm02, read]; no equipment details.
- Wilkins Aerodrome and field camps use Inmarsat BGAN [6][R]. Iridium is "widely" used for remote field communications "utilising portable and vehicle mounted handsets" [6][R].
- Satellite phones as field kit: AAD began Iridium for the airfield AWS network investigations in 2001 [2][R].
- Relay examples from other programs:
  - Belgian Princess Elisabeth station added a relay antenna on Romnoes nunatak extending VHF "to 160 km even on a 5 W handheld"; HF links it to Novolazarevskaya 450 km away [61][F].
  - A US VHF repeater article (1993–94 season) says permanent mountaintop repeaters failed from temperature stress; replacement helicopter-deployed units were solar-powered and rated to -40 C [62][R, via Wayback, trade source].
- DROMLAN: eleven-country air network; HF used between hubs [search summary, U]. No frequencies found.

### 3C. Physical constraints

- **HF and ionosphere**
  - Polar cap absorption "results from the ionisation of the D-layer of the polar ionosphere by high energy protons" and "can last several days" [27][F].
  - Absorption "can also occur with increased auroral oval activity" [27][F].
  - AAD operates riometers [27][F].
  - BAS: "During periods of intense solar activity HF may not work well, or not at all" [45][R].
  - Kawahara (1970): HF unreliability "may not be cleared out" while links depend on ionospheric propagation, and the future is "satellite relay network" [29][R].
- **Geostationary visibility** (my calculation, spherical Earth, no refraction or terrain; (inference)). Satellite on the horizon at latitude 81.3°. Elevation to a satellite at the same longitude: Casey 66.3°S about 15°; Mawson 67.6°S about 14°; Davis 68.6°S about 13°; Dome A 80.4°S about 1°. At 60° longitude offset these fall to roughly 2–3° at Casey, Mawson and Davis. South Pole sees ordinary geostationary satellites at -8.6°, so it uses inclined-orbit satellites [63][F].
- **Iridium** at McMurdo: "coverage is not guaranteed in and around McMurdo Sound" [44][R].
- **Antennas, wind, ice**
  - Mawson's 1912 masts were wrecked by wind [18][F].
  - BAS: HF antenna length and tuning matter. A portable set is much weaker than the station set [45][R].
  - Rime and wind issues on instruments [25][F].
- **Power and cold:** USAP repeaters "operate on renewable energy sources and can become disabled in periods of poor weather" [44][R]. BAS Codan batteries last "up to 80 hours" [45][R]. AAD's GSM unit is rated to -40 C with 4 hours of battery [6][R].
- **Meteor burst** [28][R]:
  - Fukuda et al. (2003) tested it between Zhongshan and Syowa, about 1,400 km apart.
  - Non-meteoric propagation frequently dominated.
  - About 60% of packets arrived within two hours at five-minute generation intervals. (The PDF's font encoding garbles some digits. The 60% and 1,400 km values are my best decoding.)
  - US McMurdo–Byrd tests are reported in DTIC documents I could not open.
- **What a continental network needs** (inference): shared standards for relay chains, power, and an HF skywave fallback with a satellite primary where geometry allows. All the above elements appear in real programs, and none is Australian-specific.

## 4. How a dominant local standard spreads

### 4A. Historical standardization, with decision-makers

**Cases where a regional pattern became the standard**
- **Q code** [67][W/35][R]:
  - It originated in a British Post Office list for ships licensed by the Postmaster General (1909), per Wikipedia [W].
  - The ITU archive's 1912 service regulations contain Q-signals (QRA, QRB, QRK) [35][R].
  - The 1947 Atlantic City Radio Regulations carry Q codes in Appendix 9 [34][R].
- **Morse apparatus.** ITU's own history says the Austro-German Telegraph Union decided at Vienna in 1851 that international lines be physically interconnected, and "Morse telegraph apparatus was recognized as the accepted standard for all international lines" [37][R]. The ITU volume does not mention Gerke's code. Attribution of the 1848/1851/1865 code to Gerke and Paris 1865 is Wikipedia-level [W].
- **Joint US-UK procedure.** Wikipedia cites CCBP 3-2 "Combined Radiotelephone (R/T) Procedure" (1945) as a Combined Communications Board product [W]. ACP 125 is its CCEB descendant [42][R].

**Cases where there was no single winner**
- **Spelling tables.** The 1947 ITU radiotelephone spelling table was Amsterdam, Baltimore, Casablanca, Danemark, Edison … [34][R]. A 1959 French (O.P.T.A.) proposal to the Administrative Radio Conference states: "There are at present two spelling analogy tables in current international use: the I.C.A.O. table and the I.T.U. table appended to the Radio Regulations. Neither is fully satisfactory where the spelling out of figures is concerned" [36][R]. France proposed its own figure table in that document (e.g., "wun nona penta nona").
- The ICAO alphabet was adopted 1 Nov 1951 and finalized 1 Mar 1956, with C, M, N, U and X replaced; word choice required similar spelling in English, French and Spanish [unitingaviation, F]. NATO's page says NATO effective 1 Mar 1956 and that the ITU "subsequently" adopted it [F]. The ITU's adoption year (1959) is secondary [U]. I could not retrieve the 1959 Radio Regulations text.
- The ITU figure code (Nadazero…) is still in the Radio Regulations [32][R]. But CASA, CAP 413, FAA AIM, ACP 125 and the BAS manual all teach ICAO digits (tree, fife, niner) and none teach Nadazero [39][40][41][42][45][R]. (inference: the ITU figure words did not displace ICAO digit pronunciation in practice.)
- The ITU explicitly permits national alternatives: "stations of the same country … may use any other table recognized by their administration" [32][R]. The 1947 text had the same clause [34][R].
- **Time** converged on UTC across ICAO, FAA, CASA, CAP 413 and BAS (all above).

### 4B. Documented Australian contributions to international procedure

- **No** Australian-originated item in the ITU or ICAO procedures I read. The ITU and ICAO texts I read do not identify any Australian-authored procedure.
- Australian-specific items that exist but are not shown to have spread: group-form call signs; SARTIME/SARWATCH terminology; WYSSA; the AAD SELCALL emergency system; "galah session".
- Equipment spread is documented: BAS issues Codan 2110 HF radios for field parties [45][R]. A search summary says the Ranulph Fiennes "Coldest Journey" expedition chose Codan radios [58][U]. Codan HF sets are also reported in African and PNG use [U].
- Selcall: CCIR Rec. 493 "Digital Selective-Calling System" for maritime service is the basis [W/U]. Australia's Codan Selcall implementations are a national ecosystem on an international standard [U].
- School of the Air model: search summaries say Canada used it. Documentation is not primary [U].

### 4C. Network effects in Antarctica specifically

- Treaty text: "done in the English, French, Russian and Spanish languages, each version being equally authentic" [at_treaty, R]. So the Treaty system is four-language.
- ICAO language rule: ground-station language or English [43][R].
- **English dominance of Antarctic radio as a measured fact: not found.** I found only the ICAO rule, the Treaty's four languages, and generic "lingua franca" web claims [U].
- UTC for aviation is verified for FAA/CASA/CAP/BAS. USAP and AAD station sked times are not verified.
- Shared emergency channels: 121.5 MHz and 123.1 MHz (ITU Appendix 15) [32][R]; 3023/5680 kHz AERO-SAR [32][R]; 129.7 MHz TIBA [44][R].
- Antarctic Treaty telecom meetings (1963, 1969) and Recommendations VI-1/VI-2 are formal coordination [30][31][R/F].
- WMO Global Telecommunication System is the weather relay path (Argos to GTS) [21][F].

## WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS FOR THE DEVELOPER'S IDEA

**Documented, so a lore claim could rest on it**
1. The first Antarctic radio link was Australian (Mawson's 1911–14 expedition: Telefunken spark transmitter, Macquarie relay, Hannam as engineer) [2][17][R/F].
2. Australia's later Antarctic radio ran on HF for decades. Casey acted as relay hub for Mawson and Davis in 1978. It was the last HF service OTC provided [2][R].
3. The same amateur community (WIA) produced Antarctic wireless (Hannam) and the outback pedal-radio network (Kauper 5BG, Traeger 5AX) [19][52][F].
4. The AAD still recruits radio operators with outback or field-party experience [10][F].
5. Field-party skeds are routine policy: AAD twice daily [7][R], Macquarie nightly [12][R].
6. Distinctive Australian items exist: the WYSSA telegraphic code [1][R]; group-form call signs and SARTIME/SKED vocabulary [39][R]; the "galah session" word [57][F]; VK0 call signs [54][R].
7. Equipment can spread: BAS uses Codan HF radios [45][R].
8. Relay-chain and mountaintop-repeater practice exists, with documented failures from cold and wind [2][61][62].

**International standard only; not an Australian trait**
- Phonetic alphabet, digit pronunciation, UTC, MAYDAY/PAN PAN/SECURITE, ROGER/WILCO/OVER/OUT, Q codes, the 1–5 readability scale, call-sign blocks. All come from ITU, ICAO or the CCEB.
- Australian, American and British military voice procedure are one shared document (ACP 125) [42][R].
- Antarctic aviation HF/VHF channels and McMurdo's role are US-managed, not Australian [44][45][48].

**Would be invention**
- A claim that Antarctic nets descend from RFDS or Traeger procedure. I found no documentation linking them beyond the shared amateur community.
- A claim that Australians wrote a procedure that became the Antarctic or world standard. I found none.
- Any claim that Australian speech or manner is the cause of procedure. All documented differences are program policy or regulatory.
- "Dominant pattern becomes standard everywhere" is documented as a real phenomenon (Q code, Morse apparatus, Allied procedure), and has counter-cases (two competing spelling tables until 1959, ITU figure code ignored in practice, formally permitted national tables).

## Contradictions between sources

1. Hannam's transmitter: criticalcomms says "1.5 W" [20][F]; AAD says "1.5 kilowatt" [17][19][F]. The AAD figure is more plausible for a spark transmitter.
2. First Antarctic messages: AAD Hannam page says 25 Sep 1912 was the "first successful message transmission from Antarctica via Macquarie Island to Hobart" [19][F], while the Wireless Hill page says Commonwealth Bay reached Australia in Feb 1913 [17][F]. Possibly consistent (relay vs direct), but not reconciled.
3. VK0: Wikipedia ties the VK1→VK0 change to "Antarctic treaties" c. 1955 [55][W]; the Antarctic Treaty was signed 1959 [at_treaty].
4. Mawson radio: the AAD says the station was founded in 1954 [16][R]; the hobby-radio source dates the Mawson station call (0MA) to 1955 [50][F]. These may refer to different things (station versus broadcasting).
5. First satellite phone: Yates 2015 says first use "in the early 1980s" [6][R]; the AAD history page says Inmarsat was introduced mid-1980s [5][F]; Yates 2001 says Inmarsat terminals "through the mid-1980s" [2][R]. Compatible but not identical.
6. Antarctic Treaty Telecom meetings: the 1963 recommendations are described as superseded by Buenos Aires 1969 [29][R], but VI-1 adopts the 1969 proposals [30][R]. Consistent, with two different documents.
7. Satellite capacity: AAD 2015 says 1.2 Mbps to Casey and Davis [6][R]; Wikipedia says 9 Mbps per station under Speedcast [W]. Different dates likely; unverified.
8. ITU adoption of ICAO alphabet: 1959 (secondary) versus Wikipedia saying ICAO accepted a wartime alphabet in 1947. The ITU's own 1947 RR carried a different table [34][R], so the 1947 claim applies to ICAO, not the ITU.
