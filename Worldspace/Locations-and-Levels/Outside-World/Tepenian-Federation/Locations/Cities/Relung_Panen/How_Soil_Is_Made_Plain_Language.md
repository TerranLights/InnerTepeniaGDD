## What soil is

Soil is a mix of crushed rock, decayed living matter, living organisms, water and air. A good farm loam is roughly:

- **About 45% mineral particles**, from weathered rock, in three sizes: sand (coarse, drains fast), silt (medium) and clay (the finest, holds water and nutrients).
- **About 5% organic matter**: dead plant and animal material in all stages of decay. The finished, stable part is humus, which glues particles into crumbs, holds water and feeds the organisms.
- **About 25% water and 25% air**, in the gaps between the particles. Roots need both.
- **A small but essential living fraction**: bacteria, fungi, worms, mites and other organisms that break down organic matter and recycle nutrients. Some bacteria turn nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use.

## How it forms

Five things act together: the parent rock, climate, living organisms, the shape of the land, and time.

1. **Weathering breaks the rock down.** Physical weathering is freeze-thaw cracking, abrasion and glaciers grinding it. Chemical weathering is water and dissolved acids slowly changing the minerals, releasing phosphorus, potassium, calcium and magnesium.
2. **Life moves in.** Lichens, algae and mosses first, then plants. They add organic matter and acids that speed weathering.
3. **Organic matter builds up and decays.** Microbes turn it into humus. This is the slow step.
4. **Layers form.** A dark topsoil, a lighter subsoil, and weathered rock below.

It is very slow. In temperate climates a centimeter of topsoil usually takes on the order of centuries. In cold, dry places it takes far longer, because freezing, dryness and a lack of life slow every step.

## The key limit with rock

**Rock contains almost no nitrogen.** All of a soil's nitrogen comes from living things: bacteria that fix it from the air, or the decay of dead organisms. Crushed rock alone is therefore not soil. It still lacks organic matter, usable nitrogen, a microbial community and the right water behavior.

## How people make soil quickly

- Grind rock fine, to expose more mineral surface.
- Add large amounts of organic matter (compost, manure, or treated sewage sludge, called biosolids), which supplies carbon, nitrogen and microbes together.
- Add nitrogen-fixing plants or fertilizer.
- Control water, and flush out excess salt.
- Let microbes and worms do the rest.

The closest real example found: biosolids at about 220 dry tonnes per hectare took bare mine tailings to 77% grass cover in under 3 years. Iceland's reclaimed desert reached about 4.9% soil carbon after 11 years under trees. The research found **no case of crushed rock with no existing soil being turned into cropland.**

## What the Bunger Hills ground has and lacks (from the 2026-10-25 ground research)

| Has | Lacks |
|---|---|
| Gneiss bedrock and gravelly till, about 70 to 80% stones | Fine particles, organic carbon (very low), usable nitrogen |
| Mineral nutrients from the rock | A living microbial community outside the thin moss patches |
| About 36 km² of lakes; meltwater in summer | Steady liquid water: precipitation about 204 mm a year (likely overstated) against 450 to 600 mm potential evaporation; thaw only in December and January |
| Salt from sea spray in the north, lime and gypsum in the south | A neutral, low-salt rooting layer: bare ground is alkaline and salty |
| Permafrost thawing to about 0.8 to 1.6 m | Warmth: the annual mean air temperature is about −9 °C |

So the founders' job is to supply organic matter and nitrogen, flush the salt, and bring in life, or to grow food without soil at all. **Both routes are open; neither is ruled.**

## Words the founding story may use freely (plain meanings)

- **Till:** unsorted rock debris left by a glacier.
- **Biosolids:** treated sewage sludge used as fertilizer.
- **Humus:** the stable, dark end product of decay.
- **Technosol:** a soil built by people from rock, waste or other materials.
- **Hydroponics:** growing plants in nutrient water with no soil.
