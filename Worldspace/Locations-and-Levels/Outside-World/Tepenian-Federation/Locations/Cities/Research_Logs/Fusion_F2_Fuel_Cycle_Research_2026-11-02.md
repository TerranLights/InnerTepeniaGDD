## 1. Tritium inventory, production, decay, price

### 1.1 Decay
- **SOURCED (GAO-11-100, released 7 October 2010):** tritium has a "relatively short half-life of 12 years" and "decays at a rate of about 5.5 percent per year." (`gao.gov/assets/a311096.html`.)
- **DERIVED:** with a half-life of 12.32 years, the annual loss is 1 − 2^(−1/12.32) = **5.47%**. The 12.32-year figure appeared in search snippets that cite NIST (4,500 ± 8 days) and in Britannica; I did not reach NNDC or IAEA directly (**UNVERIFIED** at the primary level; the 5.5% is SOURCED from GAO).
- **SOURCED (War on the Rocks, 6 March 2026, by the NNSA's principal assistant deputy administrator for Defense Programs):** "Tritium's half-life is just over 12 years, so warhead reservoirs must be regularly replenished."

### 1.2 Civil (commercial) stock and production
- **SOURCED (Coleman and Kovari, UKAEA, IAEA-CN-258 conference paper, 2018):** "Heavy water (D2O) reactors are presently the only commercial source of tritium." Tritium production in HWRs estimated at "210 to 260 g/GWe/fpy" (full-power year); the paper assumes **228 g/GWe/fpy**. "A global T stockpile of 30-40 kg from commercial HWR operation will exist in the 2050's" (their modeled figure). Only Canada (Darlington Detritiation Facility) and South Korea (Wolsong) actively detritiate; Romania was considering it. **"Ontario Power Generation (OPG) in Canada sells approximately 100 g/yr of tritium to commercial users [McMorrow 2011], at a cost of 25-30 k$/g [Willms, personal communication, 2017]."**
- **SOURCED (Pearson, Kyoto Fusioneering, U.S. DOE Fusion Workshop, 1-3 June 2022, slides):** "All commercially available tritium is produced as a by-product in CANDU fission reactors. Current production rate is ~2 kg per year, with a ~30 kg (decaying) stockpile. The majority of the global stockpile is in Darlington, Canada, and is owned by Ontario Power Generation." Price: "approximate value is around $35,000 per gram [$35M per kg]."
- **SOURCED (NEI Magazine, "Answering the big tritium question," 17 July 2025):** "About 20 kg, according to ITER's estimate of the current global inventory." Price "$30,000-40,000 per gram, per Stephen Wheeler of the UK Atomic Energy Authority (UKAEA), in a February 2024 interview." "A CANDU reactor typically produces about 130 g of tritium per year (AECL)."
- **SOURCED (War on the Rocks, 6 March 2026):** "If all commercial tritium were combined, it would be about 25 kg." Commercial tritium "about $30,000 per gram." Private-sector (non-fusion) use "a few hundred grams per year worldwide."
- **SOURCED (El-Guebaly and Malang, U. Wisconsin, UWFDM-1372, July 2009):** "the total amount of T accumulated from all CANDU reactors at their peak, anticipated for 2025, amounts to ~30 kg."
- **Spread of the stock figure across sources (dated):** about 30 kg (2009 projection of the 2025 peak), 30-40 kg (2018 model, for the 2050s), ~30 kg decaying (2022), ~20 kg (2025, ITER estimate via NEI), ~25 kg (March 2026, NNSA author). **The sources do not agree on one number; the range of 20 to 30 kg is what they support.**
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippets only):** RSIS commentary, 29 October 2025, reportedly giving Canada's 17 operating CANDUs as yielding "up to about 2 kg per year," falling as units age, and a stock of about 14 kg by 2055. A Canadian Nuclear Laboratories slide (IAEA 2025 DEMO workshop) reportedly lists about 30 kg at Darlington's tritium removal facility and about 8 kg at Wolsong. Both pages returned HTTP 403 when fetched (see log), so I did not read them.
- **Alternative civil production:** **SOURCED (Coleman and Kovari 2018):** tritium-producing burnable absorber rods in a light-water reactor (the Watts Bar case) cost "40-60 k$/g [GAO 2000]," "approximately twice as high as for HWR T production," and need special licensing.

### 1.3 Weapons-program tritium
- **SOURCED (GAO-11-100, 2010):** NNSA meets its stockpile requirement by "harvesting tritium from dismantled warheads and irradiating a limited number of rods"; it "cannot meet its tritium needs in this manner indefinitely." Tritium-producing rods (TPBARs) are irradiated in TVA's Watts Bar 1; at the time TVA allowed 240 per cycle because of permeation into reactor coolant.
- **SOURCED (War on the Rocks, 6 March 2026):** Watts Bar first harvest 2007: 223 g. DOE's 2015 target: "2,800 grams per 18-month cycle by 2025"; raised target 3,300 g by 2027, "reached two years early"; spring 2025 estimate for the next harvest "about 3,400 grams"; designers' ceiling "about 5,800 grams per cycle." The exact 2025 yield "will be classified." **DERIVED:** 3,400 g per 18 months = about **2.3 kg per year**.
- **The weapons stockpile size is classified** (a search snippet from an IEER report says DOE classifies "production, inventory, and use" numbers; UNVERIFIED). I found no sourced figure for how much tritium is in weapons stocks. **NOT FOUND:** the size of any weapons stockpile; any statement on whether military tritium could be made available to civil fusion.
- **UNVERIFIED (snippet, IEER):** about 4 g per warhead; about 2.2 kg per year needed to offset decay for a 10,000-warhead arsenal. Treat as a lead only.

### 1.4 Demand and projected shortfall
- **ITER's lifetime need.** **SOURCED (Coleman and Kovari 2018):** "Unofficial estimates of tritium consumption by ITER put the figure at a total of 12.3 kg over its operational lifetime." JET's planned DTE2 campaign was expected to use 55 g; NIF's tritium inventory limit is 0.83 g. ITER D-T operations were then scheduled for about 2036.
- **ITER schedule now.** **SOURCED (ITER Organization press-conference summary, 3 July 2024):** the new baseline has the "Start of Deuterium-Tritium Operation Phase" "about 4 years delayed from the previous baseline, from 2035 to 2039"; start of research operation 2034; full magnetic energy 2036; first wall changes from beryllium to tungsten. Coleman and Kovari's own result is that **a delay in ITER's D-T start reduces the tritium available later: "ITER D-T operations taking place as presently scheduled in 2036 corresponds to 5.47 kg less T in 2055, whereas a five-year delay would result in there being 7.25 kg less tritium in 2055."** (More delay means more decay before use, and the CANDU supply tails off after 2040.)
- **Modeled availability for a demonstration plant in 2055 (Coleman and Kovari 2018):** from 0 kg (pessimistic scenario) to 27.7 kg (optimistic); the moderate scenario gives 12 to 14.3 kg, and "CANDU reactor tritium production in Ontario, Canada, will supply ITER's tritium demand (12.3 kg) but, following the delay in the ITER schedule, may only be able to supply 7.4 kg for a DEMO fusion reactor in 2055 (Scenario B)." India "could contribute almost 7 kg in 2055." Start-up inventory estimates for one demonstration reactor: "less than 1 kg to almost 20 kg for a ~2 GW fusion reactor." Their conclusion: "there are likely to be serious problems with tritium supply for the future fusion R&D programme."
- **Pearson 2022 (SOURCED, slides):** "Fusion demand likely to begin to deplete the available stockpile from as early as ~2035. Beyond 2050, it is unlikely that tritium will be produced by CANDU (end of life), so supply is highly uncertain." "Not enough external tritium to support any fusion program beyond the start-up of the first (or perhaps the first few) commercial reactors." Challenges named: export controls, international transport containers, competition for supply, cost.
- **Date caveat:** the Coleman and Kovari model dates from 2018 and assumed ITER D-T in 2036; the 2024 baseline moved it to 2039, so their absolute numbers are dated (the direction is stated in their own sensitivity above).

## 2. Breeding

### 2.1 Required tritium breeding ratio (TBR) and the published margin
- **Definition, SOURCED (Delaporte-Mathurin et al., MIT, arXiv:2412.02721, 2 December 2024):** "The TBR is defined as the ratio of tritium produced in the blanket to tritium consumed in the plasma. To achieve self-sufficiency, the TBR of a commercial fusion reactor must be greater than unity." "The TBR required by a given FPP design depends on the plant's performance requirements, the efficiency of its fuel cycle components, expected tritium losses, and reserve inventory needs."
- **SOURCED (Ward, Pearson, Scott, Lopes Cardozo, arXiv:2605.04707, 6 May 2026):** "In reality, the TBR must be greater (by some 20%) to account for tritons and neutrons lost during this process." The 6Li capture reaction releases 4.8 MeV; "blankets are often predicted to require 6Li enrichment levels of 60-90% (or potentially as low as 30% for solid-based breeders with neutron multipliers)." In a helium-cooled pebble-bed divertor case, "the assumed 60% enrichment adds about 25% to the TBR." Every centimeter of steel first wall reduces TBR "by some 7-10%."
- **Margin breakdown, SOURCED (El-Guebaly and Malang, UWFDM-1372, 2009):** the breeding margin (calculated TBR minus 1) is split into: known deficiencies in nuclear data **6-10%**; in modeling **3-7%**; unknown design uncertainties **0-3%**; tritium bred in excess of that consumed **1-2%**. "Fusion designs developed to date displayed a wide variety of calculated TBR, ranging from 1.05 to 1.8." For their advanced designs the net TBR needed could be as low as **1.01** against a calculated TBR of about **1.1**. Their stated conditions for net 1.01: burnup fraction in plasma above 10%, tritium-system repair in under 1 day, three or more processing systems, high recycle rates, low inventories, "extremely low T losses to environment (< 4 g/y)." They also write that "an uncertainty as small as 1% translates into 1-2 kg of T per FPY for 2-3 GW fusion power."
- **EU-DEMO, SOURCED at abstract level (Fischer et al., ISFNT-14 abstract, 2019):** "a net Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR) >= 1.0 is required ... a global TBR with some additional margin in excess of unity must be demonstrated to account for uncertainties in the neutronic simulations for DEMO and the loss budget expected for the processing of the tritium in the fuel cycle. Another margin has to be added when considering DEMO to provide the start-up inventory for a follow-up power plant." The abstract gives no numbers. **UNVERIFIED (search snippets of an ENEA repository record and an earlier KIT presentation):** required TBR of about **1.05**, design target of **1.15** with margins for calculation uncertainty (2020 paper), and an older design target of 1.10.
- **Recent modeling, SOURCED at abstract level (Meschini et al., *Nuclear Fusion*, 2025; the talk abstract at IPP, 12 March 2025):** including tritium trapping in components and their replacement, in an ARC-class plant "the first wall and vacuum vessel inventory rises by a factor of roughly 10^3-10^4," **doubling time increases by about 50%, start-up inventory by about 30%, and the required TBR rises by 2%-5%.**
- **What is and is not published as "the" margin:** the sources give a requirement of 1.0 (mathematical floor), about 1.01 to 1.05 (needed net, depending on assumptions), and calculated design values of about 1.1 to 1.15 or higher. There is no single agreed figure; the number depends on burn fraction, fueling efficiency, processing time, availability and losses (Abdou et al. below).

### 2.2 Conditions for self-sufficiency (Abdou et al., *Nuclear Fusion* 61, 013001, published 23 November 2020; SOURCED from the OSTI record's abstract and key numbers, not the full text)
- Authors: Abdou, Riva, Ying, Day, Loarte, Baylor, Humrickhouse, Fuerst, Cho. Conclusion: current physics and technology will not enable a demonstration plant and future power plants to meet the requirements of self-sufficiency, start-up inventory and tritium safety.
- Self-sufficiency is "impossible" if availability factor is below 10% for any value of (fueling efficiency × burn fraction); "possible" if availability exceeds 30% and the product is 1-2%; achievable "with reasonable confidence" if availability exceeds 50% and the product exceeds 2%. Processing time of 1-4 hours is required. ITER with a 50-50 mix has a tritium burn fraction of about **0.36%**, "too low for self-sufficiency." Tritium-only pellet core fueling gives 1.8-3.6%. Fueling efficiency extrapolated for DEMO is below 25% and "highly uncertain." A 24-hour reserve time leads to unacceptable inventories.

### 2.3 Start-up inventory
- **SOURCED (Abdou et al. 2020, via OSTI):** for a **3 GW** reactor: **about 11 kg** at (fueling efficiency × burn fraction) of 2% with 4 h processing; **under 5 kg** at 5% with 1 h processing. A **100 MW** facility: about 1 kg. "The requirement scales linearly with fusion power."
- **SOURCED (Coleman and Kovari 2018):** published estimates "less than 1 kg to almost 20 kg for a ~2 GW fusion reactor"; a start-up from D-D operation alone "would take years" and "does not appear to be economically sensible" with interest on capital; some argue "~5 years of D-D commissioning ... creating ~3-5 kg of T" (cited from Konishi et al.). "Commissioning of a fusion reactor in D-D plasmas will produce some tritium, provided a breeding blanket is installed."
- **SOURCED (El-Guebaly and Malang 2009):** ARIES-CS start-up inventory 4 kg, ARIES-AT 2 kg; tritium burn rates 135 and 97.8 kg per full-power year; decay losses 0.33 and 0.16 kg/y.
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippet):** a review noting estimates "from 0.5 to 25 kg for a 1-2 GW fusion power reactor."

### 2.4 Doubling time
- **SOURCED (El-Guebaly and Malang 2009):** their surplus calculation assumes "a doubling time of 5 years" for supplying the start-up inventory of a new plant; early plants "may require a Net TBR > 1.01 for shorter doubling time"; mature systems 1.002 to 1.01.
- **SOURCED at abstract level (Meschini 2025):** trapping lengthens doubling time by about 50%.
- **SOURCED (Ward et al. 2026):** industry build-out "involves 7 - 8 doublings" from a few demonstrators to 200 plants per year over roughly 10 years (this is the industry's doubling, not the tritium inventory's).
- **NOT FOUND:** a single sourced "doubling time in years" for a named design (ARC, EU-DEMO, STEP). The Abdou et al. paper identifies doubling time as a key parameter, but I did not read its tables.

### 2.5 Blanket concepts and calculated TBR
- **Concepts named in the sources (SOURCED unless noted):** helium-cooled pebble bed (HCPB, lithium ceramic pebbles with beryllium multiplier), helium-cooled lithium-lead (HCLL), water-cooled lithium-lead (WCLL), dual-coolant lithium-lead, and molten FLiBe (lithium fluoride + beryllium fluoride; the ARC concept is a "Liquid Immersion Blanket" in which "A full-scale LIB in an ARC-class FPP will require ~250.000 L of FLiBe" per Delaporte-Mathurin et al.; "the beryllium in FLiBe acts as a neutron multiplier"). The ITER test blanket modules cover HCLL, HCPB, "HCCB" and "WCCB" mock-up families in the MIT paper's description; ITER's current plan is four independent test systems.
- **HCPB calculated TBR, SOURCED (Hernandez et al., EUROfusion preprint WPBB-CPR(17)17877, 2017):** "The TBR values for the HCPB-long and HCPB-short are ~1.26 and ~1.15, which satisfies the current requirements"; 60% Li-6 enrichment.
- **UNVERIFIED (snippets):** WCLL about 1.14 against a target of 1.15 (2022 EUROfusion presentation); the 2025 WCLL redesign aiming at 1.15.
- **ITER test blanket modules, schedule:** **UNVERIFIED (snippet, ITER's Luciano Giancarli, IAEA talk, September 2025; the PDF returned 403):** TBS design phase to 2029, installation starting 2035, four D-T-phase systems operating from 2039 for about ten years. **SOURCED (Coleman and Kovari 2018):** "ITER will produce negligible amounts of tritium in the test blanket modules." So no blanket will deliver kilograms of tritium in the ITER era.

### 2.6 Measured TBR
- **Lab scale, SOURCED (Delaporte-Mathurin et al. 2024, BABY experiment, MIT):** 100 mL of molten ClLiF salt (a beryllium-free surrogate), two sealed-tube 14 MeV neutron generators. Result: **TBR = 1.17 × 10^10 tritons / 3.35 × 10^13 neutrons = 3.57 × 10^-4.** "At this scale, TBR is about four orders of magnitude lower than the value required by commercial fusion reactors," because only about 2% of the generator flux hit the salt. The measured TBR was **2.3 times less** than the OpenMC simulation, hypothesized to be permeation loss through the crucible wall. Tritium collected predominantly as HT, not the expected TF. The authors say "tritium self-sufficiency on a large scale has never been demonstrated." Planned follow-on (LIBRA): 1000 kg (~500 L) of FLiBe, long-term goal TBR at or above 1.
- **Mock-up neutronics, partly SOURCED:** a 2007 paper on an ITER test-blanket mock-up irradiated with 14 MeV neutrons at the Frascati generator (Klix et al., *Fusion Science and Technology* 52(4)) reports "the TPR calculation shows a tendency to underestimate" (calculated tritium production rate lower than measured); no numbers in the abstract I read. **UNVERIFIED (snippet, Batistoni et al., *Fusion Engineering and Design* 82, 2007):** HCPB mock-up calculation underestimated by about 10% on average, total C/E uncertainty 9-10% (2σ); a later HCLL mock-up within 5.9% (1σ). These are small-mock-up production-rate checks, not a breeding ratio of a blanket on a burning plasma.
- **UK program (SOURCED, UKAEA LIBRTI page, content dated up to 1 July 2026):** about £220 million planned 2023-2030, to "demonstrate controlled fusion tritium breeding" in an engineering-scale testbed using "a commercially available 14 MeV neutron source" at Culham; £8 million to 11 experimental and digital-simulation projects 2024-2026; **Commonwealth Fusion Systems named first international partner (news item of 1 July 2026)**; JET's main power building cleared 27 April 2026 for conversion to the facility; "no completed experiments" reported. **UNVERIFIED (snippets):** a FLiBe irradiation campaign scheduled for Q1 2026 under one project; a molten-salt mock-up breeder experiment at Culham scheduled for 2030.
- **NOT FOUND:** any measured breeding ratio from a blanket on a D-T burning plasma (none can exist yet: no D-T device with a blanket has operated); SPARC or ARC tritium inventory or TBR figures (queries in the log).

## 3. Lithium

### 3.1 Li-6 enrichment needed
- **Natural abundance:** the sources give "only 7.4%" (Pearson 2022), "approximately 7.5%" (Ward 2026; Polytechnique Insights 2025). The discussion file's 7.59% is GENERAL and consistent within rounding (**not verified at a primary isotopic-abundance source**).
- **SOURCED (Pearson 2022 slides, citing Bradshaw et al. 2011):** "Typically, a breeding blanket will require natural lithium to be enriched to 30-90% lithium-6." Beryllium-based blankets 30-60% enrichment, "in the order of 1-10 tonnes per reactor"; lead-based 60-90%, "in the order of 10-100 tonnes per blanket." "Lithium-6 supply is practically zero." "Hypothesis: the issue is not necessarily quantity (and cost) of lithium-6, but production of the material altogether."
- **SOURCED (Ward et al. 2026):** blanket inventory: "The designs for blankets in demonstration-scale devices (DEMO) contain roughly 50 tonnes of 6Li. For power plant blankets that must be replaced and reprocessed every 5 years ... approximately 100 tonnes of 6Li are required per GWth-scale reactor." Annual consumption "about 100 kg per year per GWth," about 0.1% of the site inventory. "Two blankets are effectively needed for a power plant to operate continuously: one for operation and at least one in standby." Costs: "today 6Li is sold at about $50k/kg, in small quantities only, mostly for medical purposes"; price floor via mercury process "near $1-2000/kg"; at $5k/kg, 100 tonnes of Li-6 "would contribute 25% to the total overnight cost" of an example plant.
- **Alternatives to enriched lithium:** Ward et al. note natural-lithium blankets exist in the literature, with trade-offs: reactors with thinner or no first walls (named: First Light Fusion, General Fusion, Zap Energy, Renaissance Fusion) have an advantage for natural-lithium breeding; "viability of these concepts has not yet been demonstrated." Inertial and pulsed devices may have higher solid-angle coverage.

### 3.2 Enrichment methods and the mercury legacy
- **SOURCED (Ward et al. 2026):** COLEX (mercury amalgam exchange) is the only proven industrial method, used in the U.S. at Oak Ridge (Y-12) and "presently ... in China, North Korea and Russia." "Between 1953 and 1963, this plant produced 442 tonnes of 6-lithium hydroxide - about 100 tonnes of 6Li, for which it used 11 thousand tonnes of mercury." That equates to "0.3 - 1 kt of mercury for the enrichment of 1 tonne of 6Li per year." About 10% of the mercury was lost, causing pollution; COLEX was banned in the U.S. and falls under the Minamata Convention. A blanket of 100 t of Li-6 would need an enrichment plant with "some 3 - 10 kt of mercury"; world mercury production is "2 kt" a year; China accounts for roughly 90%. Alternatives (lasers, crown ethers, electrochemical exchange, displacement chromatography, even microbes) "none of these have been publicly demonstrated at scale." The improved mercury-based ICOMAX process (Giegerich et al., *Fusion Engineering and Design* 149, 2019, 111339) is proposed to start enrichment by 2040 and complete a DEMO blanket supply by 2045. UKAEA's open call funded six companies for enrichment development. Highly enriched Li-6 "is a controlled substance" with export controls; the paper discusses "fusion grade" versus "weapons grade" enrichment levels (20% versus 60% as candidate cutoffs, not decided).
- **Discrepancy to flag:** Pearson's 2022 slide says Y-12 "produced approx. 442 tons of lithium-6 using COLEX (1954 to 1963)." Ward et al. (2026, with Pearson as co-author) say 442 tonnes was lithium **hydroxide**, containing about 100 tonnes of Li-6. The later, co-authored statement is the one to use. The slide also gives "est. ~$4000 per kg (von Hippel et al., 2012)" for COLEX and says COLEX is "Now banned under Minamata treaty (ban on new use of large quantities of mercury for industrial processes)."
- **Fission-side use of lithium isotopes:** Ault et al. (cited in Ward et al.) studied purifying Li-7 for fission plants; they favor crown-ether and laser methods over mercury. Not read at source.

### 3.3 Lithium demand per gigawatt-year of fusion
- See section 6 for the working. In brief: burning 1 Li-6 atom per tritium atom bred gives **112 kg of Li-6 per GWth-year (DERIVED)**, which agrees with Ward et al.'s "about 100 kg per year per GWth" (SOURCED). Expressed as natural lithium (7.5%) that is about 1.5 t.
- **Other published "per 1 GW" figures, which do not agree with each other:** **SOURCED (Polytechnique Insights, 2 December 2025, Treiner and Bonhomme):** a "1 GW plant would consume about 167 kg of deuterium per year" and "about 7 tonnes of natural lithium per year." **UNVERIFIED (snippets):** 100 kg deuterium and 3 tonnes natural lithium per GW(e)-year (an IAEA INIS record on the UK fusion programme); about 100 kg D and 150 kg lithium (a Leibniz Society paper). **DERIVED:** the Polytechnique values correspond to about 4.5 GWth (167/37.5) and 4.75 GWth (7 t / 1.47 t) per "1 GW," i.e., about 21-22% efficiency if the "GW" is electric; the article does not state its basis. The 3-tonne figure corresponds to about 2 GWth per GW(e). The spread (1.5 to 7 t of natural lithium per GW) is explained by what "GW" means (thermal or electric, and at which efficiency) and what enrichment is assumed, not by disagreement on the reaction.

### 3.4 World lithium supply for scale
- **SOURCED (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries, February 2026, lithium chapter):** world mine production **222,000 t (2024) and 290,000 t (2025 est.)** of contained lithium; "Global consumption of lithium in 2025 was estimated to be 263,000 tons." **World reserves 37,000,000 t** (Chile 9.2 Mt, Australia 8.4 Mt, China 4.6 Mt, Argentina 4.4 Mt, U.S. 4.4 Mt, Canada 1.6 Mt). **Identified resources about 150 million t** (measured and indicated; 30 Mt U.S., 120 Mt elsewhere; Argentina 28 Mt, Bolivia 23 Mt, Chile 13 Mt). End uses: batteries 88%. Lithium carbonate averaged $9,000 per ton in 2025 (battery grade, annual average); spot in China about $9,300 to $10,300 per ton over the year.
- **Older figures in circulation:** Polytechnique Insights (December 2025) gives "about 115 million tonnes total, with about 30 million tonnes currently exploitable as reserves" (USGS) and IEA demand of 95 kt (2021), 205 kt (2024), up to 928 kt projected for 2040. The 2026 USGS edition read above supersedes the 115/30 Mt pair (reserves 37 Mt, resources about 150 Mt). Ward et al. say demand is "expected to strongly increase ... to more than a million tonnes per year by 2040."
- **Scale (DERIVED, section 7):** the discussion file's 430 t/yr of natural lithium is about 0.15% of 2025 world production.

### 3.5 Lithium from seawater
- **SOURCED (Frontiers in Chemical Engineering review of direct lithium extraction from dilute solutions, 2022):** seawater "0.1-0.2 ppm" (table values 0.17-0.26 mg/L by ocean); "seawater contains nearly 230 Gt of Li+." The review states "techno-economic and life cycle analyses are needed" before large-scale evaluation, and its energy table has no seawater values (I read only the first 100,000 of 265,000 characters).
- **DERIVED (check):** 0.178 mg/L × 1.335 × 10^21 L (ocean volume: my assumption, **UNVERIFIED**) = 2.4 × 10^11 t, same order as 230 Gt. (One search snippet's "231.4 trillion tonnes" is a units error.)
- **Cost or energy per kilogram from seawater: NOT FOUND.** A Green Chemistry 2025 techno-economic study of electrochemical membrane recovery (403 on fetch) was seen in a snippet saying seawater recovery "remains costly"; a desalination-focused study argued seawater is "inherently uncompetitive against terrestrial brines" (snippet). A news summary of a Stanford process quoted about $4,500 per tonne of lithium carbonate versus $9,100 from brine, with "stability problems" for seawater (snippet, UNVERIFIED). None of these is a seawater cost I could read.

## 4. Deuterium

- **Abundance, SOURCED (ITER Organization, "Deuterium: a precious gift from the Big Bang," 11 March 2011):** seawater holds **33 mg of deuterium per liter**. Heavy water is separated by chemical exchange, then electrolyzed to deuterium gas. Price in that article: "around EUR 4,000" per kilo, "the price Tore Supra pays a commercial supplier" (a research-scale price; the article's consumption figure of about 3 kg a year is for Tore Supra). Polytechnique Insights (2025) gives 33 g/m^3, the same value. A snippet of an arXiv review gives "about $2 per gram" (UNVERIFIED; the PDF exceeded the fetch size limit).
- **Method and world production, SOURCED (IAEA Technical Reports Series 407, "Heavy water reactors: status and projected development," 2002):** "In the sixty-five years since its discovery, deuterium as heavy water has been produced in ten countries. ## 5. Tritium handling

- **ITER inventory limits.** **SOURCED (Taylor and Cortes, "Lessons learnt from ITER safety & licensing for DEMO and future nuclear fusion facilities," arXiv:1309.6179, 2013):** "for the in-vessel inventory of tritium and activated erosion dust (assumed to be up to 1 kg and 1000 kg respectively) the first confinement system is the vacuum vessel"; "maintaining a restriction on the quantity of tritium that may be vulnerable to release in an accident scenario such as a fire is a key provision ... This implies a strict control on the inventory of tritium in each process and storage system, as well as within each room (or fire sector), including that of trapped tritium"; tritium accountancy at ITER "will be based on maintaining a number of mass balance areas." Earlier power-plant studies concluded "emissions would be very low, with a maximum public dose impact around 1 μSv/year. This may now appear to be an under-estimate." **UNVERIFIED (snippets):** ITER's site tritium inventory is **4 kg** (a Basic Nuclear Installation in France); an early safety option file assumed 3 kg.
- **Plant-scale permeation.** **SOURCED (Emmerson, Shackleford, Lefebvre and Brown, UKAEA, *Fusion Engineering and Design* 220, 2025, 115351):** "The total EU DEMO tritium exhaust limit is estimated at 2 mg/day or 1 g/yr. In contrast, depending on the breeding blanket concept, tritium permeation from the blanket into the coolant has been estimated anywhere between **1 and 50 g/day**." Solubility uncertainty in lithium-lead "leads to a variation in estimates of the calculated tritium losses up to 90%." "Estimates suggest a Permeation Reduction Factor (PRF) between 100 - 1000 is needed in the breeder blanket." Reduced-activation steels at about 500 °C "have a high permeability for tritium." Tritium permeating through the heat exchanger into steam is named as a contamination risk. Coatings "have been developed with a PRF as high as 10^3 - 10^4" in the lab; aluminum oxide on Eurofer "PRF of ~1000" in one test; effects of irradiation on coatings are open.
- **Releases in normal operation, SOURCED (Taylor and Cortes 2013):** sources include leaks from fuel-cycle equipment, outgassing from components removed from the vessel and stored, leaks from the tritium recovery station, and vessel venting. Mitigations at ITER: filtered venting and detritiation systems at sub-atmospheric pressure; baking the divertor at 350 °C before venting. Waters with intermediate tritium content have "no on-site destination."
- **Other limits found:** NIF's tritium inventory limit **0.83 g** (SOURCED, Coleman and Kovari 2018, citing the NIF user guide). El-Guebaly and Malang's design condition: environmental losses below 4 g/y (SOURCED).

## FINAL: what the sources support, do not support, and NOT FOUND

### The sources support
1. **Tritium is scarce and decays.** Commercial stock is on the order of 20 to 30 kg (no source says more), produced only as a by-product of heavy-water reactors at about 130 to 260 g per reactor-GWe-year (about 2 kg/yr in Canada by one source), losing 5.5% a year to decay, priced at $25,000 to $40,000 per gram. The weapons side has a classified stockpile and a supply of about 2.3 kg/yr from Watts Bar (NNSA author, March 2026). Fusion demand begins to consume the civil stock around the mid-2030s in published models; ITER's lifetime need is about 12.3 kg; ITER's D-T start moved to 2039 (July 2024 baseline).
2. **A D-T plant must breed its own tritium.** Net TBR must exceed 1; the published requirements and margins run from about 1.01 (idealized net with a >10% burn fraction) through about 1.05 to 1.15 and up, depending on losses, burn fraction, processing time and uncertainty margins. Calculated blanket values of about 1.1 to 1.26 appear in EUROfusion work (HCPB 1.15 and 1.26). The 2020 Abdou et al. review concludes that present physics and technology do not meet the self-sufficiency, start-up-inventory and safety requirements (ITER's burn fraction is about 0.36% against a needed 2% or more). Start-up inventory is about 11 kg per 3 GW of fusion power at poor performance, under 5 kg at better, scaling linearly; extremes from under 1 kg to about 20 kg per 2 GW appear in the literature.
3. **No blanket has demonstrated breeding at power-plant scale.** The only direct TBR measurement I read is 3.57 × 10^-4 in a 100 mL salt cell (MIT, 2024), four orders below what a plant needs, with the measurement 2.3 times below simulation. The UK LIBRTI program (£220 million, 2023-2030) and a 1 July 2026 partnership with Commonwealth Fusion Systems are building the first engineering-scale tests; no results yet.
4. **Lithium: the bottleneck is Li-6 enrichment, not lithium.** Plants need about 112 kg of Li-6 burned per GWth-year (about 100 kg in the cited paper), but blankets hold about 100 t of Li-6 per GWth-scale reactor. The only proven industrial enrichment is the mercury COLEX process (Oak Ridge: about 100 t Li-6 from 11,000 t of mercury over 1953-1963), which is banned in the U.S., subject to the Minamata Convention, and unscalable by the May 2026 analysis; other methods are unproven at scale; enriched Li-6 is export-controlled. World lithium: 290,000 t mined in 2025, reserves 37 Mt, resources about 150 Mt (USGS, February 2026). Seawater holds about 230 Gt at roughly 0.1 to 0.2 mg/L.
5. **Deuterium is not a constraint.** 33 mg/L of seawater; produced as heavy water by the Girdler-sulfide or ammonia-hydrogen processes; about 30,000 Mg made worldwide through 2001, 60% in Canada; a book value of about $300 per kg of D2O.
6. **Tritium containment is a design driver.** ITER limits in-vessel tritium to about 1 kg (4 kg site total, snippet); the EU-DEMO exhaust limit is estimated at 2 mg/day (1 g/yr) against blanket-to-coolant permeation estimates of 1 to 50 g/day, so a permeation reduction factor of 100 to 1000 is needed.
7. **The discussion file's three GENERAL figures are confirmed:** 3.4 × 10^14 J/kg (derived 3.37), 93 kg per GWth-year (93.5; tritium 56.1 vs El-Guebaly's 55.6), and 1.5 t of natural lithium per GWth-year as the natural-lithium equivalent of 112 kg of Li-6 burned (Ward: about 100 kg Li-6), with the caveat in section 6.

### The sources do not support
- A claim that tritium supply is adequate for a national fusion fleet from external sources: every source read says the external stock supports at most the first plant or the first few plants.
- Any single figure for required TBR, start-up inventory, doubling time or world tritium stock; the sources give ranges and the ranges are stated above.
- That any breeding blanket has demonstrated a ratio above 1, in any size, or that tritium self-sufficiency has been demonstrated (the MIT authors: "has never been demonstrated").
- Cheap or scalable Li-6 enrichment today.
- A tidy per-GW lithium number: published values from 1.5 t to 7 t differ because of unstated bases.

### NOT FOUND
- Size of any weapons tritium stockpile; whether military tritium could be released to fusion.
- Measured TBR of any blanket under D-T burning plasma conditions; SPARC or ARC tritium inventory and TBR; ITER test-blanket module current schedule at a readable source (only a 403-blocked snippet).
- Cost or energy per kilogram of lithium from seawater; cost or energy per kilogram of deuterium (only a research-scale price, a book value for D2O, and an unread $2/g snippet).
- Regulatory plant tritium inventory limits and release limits (other than ITER's) in regulator documents; ITER's French release limits.
- A primary-source (NNDC/IAEA/NIST) page for the 12.32-year half-life; a primary isotopic-abundance page for natural Li-6 (sources give 7.4 to 7.5%).
- A sourced doubling time in years for a named plant design; the full text of Abdou et al. 2020, Meschini et al. 2025, Fischer et al. 2020 and Kovari et al. 2018 (read at abstract or summary level only).
- Girdler-sulfide energy consumption per kilogram of deuterium.

### Surprises worth noting
- The 25 to 30 kg world stock is small enough that the national burn in the discussion file (16.3 t/yr) is hundreds of times the whole stock each year; the start-up inventory alone, at the lowest published performance, would be 19 to 36 times the stock.
- The enrichment problem is about the **inventory** (about 100 t Li-6 per GWth-scale plant held in the blanket), not the annual consumption (about 0.1% of it). A May 2026 analysis (Ward et al., with Pearson) treats this as a capital-cost and mercury-supply problem, and discusses non-proliferation (export controls on enriched lithium).
- A slide in a June 2022 presentation overstates Oak Ridge output by a factor of about four (442 t of lithium hydroxide, not lithium-6); the 2026 paper corrects it.
