## Item 1: Cities grown around great machine and metal works

### 1.1 The Ruhr (Ruhrgebiet), Germany

**Already settled in T10 (not re-checked):** Zollverein, Essen: mine 1847 to 1986, *"up to 8,000 miners working in
shifts,"* coking plant closed 1993, UNESCO 2001, now the Ruhr Museum, Red Dot Design Museum and about 60 creative
firms on about 100 ha. German hard-coal mining ended on 21 December 2018 (Prosper-Haniel, Bottrop). Duisburg
water-side port handling 41.7 Mt (2024).

**Workers' colonies (Zechenkolonien, Werkssiedlungen).** All SOURCED to German Wikipedia, "Zechensiedlung," unless
marked:

- **1830s:** the first large ironworks and machine plants housed single workers in *"barracks-like quarters"*
  (kasernenartige Unterkünfte), for example the barracks of the Paulinenhütte.
- **1844:** the Gutehoffnungshütte began the **Eisenheim** colony (Oberhausen) for married foremen and masters:
  two-story houses, each with *"two apartments with separate entrances."*
- **Cross-plan house (Kreuzgrundriss):** first built in Mulhouse (Alsace) for potash miners, shown at the 1855 Paris
  World Exhibition; **first Ruhr settlement of this type 1858, Bochum-Stahlhausen**: two-story houses with four
  apartments, each with a garden and a shed used as a stall and toilet.
- **Migration:** after 1871, *"over 700,000 migrants"* came to the Ruhr over 40 years, mostly landless laborers from
  West and East Prussia, Silesia and Poland.
- **Overcrowding and lodgers:** several families shared one apartment and took in lodgers, called *Kostgänger*
  (boarders) and *Schlafburschen* (bed lodgers). Kitchens became living kitchens; room access was rearranged so
  that lodgers reached their rooms without passing through the family's.
- **Shared beds by shift:** an LWL regional-geography text (search 1 snippet, "Werkssiedlungen") says a lodger's bed
  was sometimes rented to several bed lodgers (Schlafgänger) who slept in it one after another as their shifts
  changed. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only; both LWL PDFs returned HTTP 404).
- **Stall and garden as a tie to the job:** a local-history article on miners' small-animal keeping (Bönen, 2013;
  search 1 snippet) says the mine owners provided stalls deliberately to bind miners to the pit long term; the
  LWL text adds that garden and stall improved family food and attracted migrants from the countryside. **UNVERIFIED**
  (snippets; pages not read).
- **Housing as a labor tie:** a 1978 Oxford D.Phil. thesis on Bochum-area miners (search 2 snippet) says company
  housing tied workers to their employer, colonies were built separately by origin, and miners in company housing
  changed jobs much less often than other Ruhr workers. **UNVERIFIED** (the thesis file exceeded the fetch limit).
  In the 1891 strike, the Hannover colliery management threatened miners with dismissal **and eviction** (search 2
  snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **From about 1905:** garden-city settlements (Robert Schmohl's Krupp settlements; Georg Metzendorf's
  **Margarethenhöhe**, Essen; Gartenstadt Hüttenau, Welper).
- **1960s:** the planned **Neue Stadt Wulfen** was laid out for up to 60,000 residents; because its pit (Zeche
  Wulfen) was not productive enough, the plan was cut to 20,000. **SOURCED.**
- **From the 1960s:** company housing was privatized as mining shrank, which caused social problems and resident
  resistance; owners' individual alterations broke up uniform streets; some colonies became protected heritage
  areas (Denkmalbereich). **SOURCED.**

**Neighbor: Krupp's Margarethenhöhe, Essen** (a works-founded "small town," not a works village). SOURCED to the
Margarethe Krupp Foundation unless marked:

- Foundation deed **1 December 1906**; Margarethe Krupp gave about **50 ha** and **one million marks**.
- **1913: about 1,500 people in 360 apartments.** By 1913 it had restaurants, bakers, butchers and hairdressers; a
  Catholic school (1914) and a Protestant school (1916); a tram stop (December 1914); a 172 m pedestrian bridge (1910);
  by **1945, 1,681 apartments**. A weekly market of 18 stalls has run since 15 September 1947.
- It was intended as *"a self-contained small town with its own market, inn and consumer cooperative, rather than
  an 'industrial village'"* (search 4 snippet, regional heritage route). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- Why it is a neighbor: the same company that ran the works built a whole town quarter with its own shops,
  schools and transport.

**Workforce over time (SOURCED, bpb, Czierpka):**

- 1929 to 1932: the Ruhr mining workforce *"shrank from almost 353,000 to 190,000"* (Depression).
- **1957: 397,000** people employed at the Ruhr collieries; **1961: 296,000; 1966: just over 200,000.**
- First lost shifts (Feierschichten, unpaid idle days) at Ruhr pits in **February 1958**; closure premiums paid from
  **December 1962** sharply raised the number of closures; last colliery closed **December 2018**.
- Labor recruitment: in World War I, Dutch and Belgian workers and prisoners of war; in World War II, *"forced
  laborers and prisoners of war were used massively."*
- **Conflict:** a popular source in search 5 says "over 600,000 miners" in 1957; Planet Wissen (search 5 snippet)
  gives 505,000 (1960) to about 242,000 (1970), apparently all-German scope. bpb's Ruhr-only 397,000 (1957) is used
  here. See Unresolved conflicts.

**Guest workers (Gastarbeiter) in mining:** the German–Turkish recruitment agreement was signed on **30 October
1961** (bpb, search 7 snippet). A Bochum university note (search 7 snippet) says that by the 1980s foreigners were
**about 20% of colliery workforces, about 80% of them Turkish nationals**. A bpb photo caption (search 7 snippet)

redirects to a homepage; the Friedrich-Verlag article is behind a login).

  the concert series "Miners Sing for Miners" (Bergleute singen für Bergleute) in Dortmund. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).

## Item 4: Industry set deep in the ground or in remote interiors; guarding a sensitive product

### 4.1 China's Third Front (Sanxian, 三线建设), 1964 to 1980

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Covell F. Meyskens, "The Third Front Campaign," in *Proletarian China* (Made in China
Journal / Verso, 2022), https://madeinchinajournal.com/download/24094 (text extracted locally) · English Wikipedia,
"Third Front (China)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Front_(China) (first 100,000 characters) · Sixth Tone,
13 October 2021, https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1008681. Dead ends: the *China Quarterly* "Afterlives of the Third
Front" introduction (Westminster repository HTTP 405; Cambridge HTTP 403 and unreachable hosts).

- **Why and where (SOURCED, Meyskens):** on 27 May 1964 Mao rejected a coast-first Third Five-Year Plan; the country
  was divided into three fronts, the Third in central and western China, where the Party *"had to secretly build a
  large military-industrial complex"* as a backup economic motor; *"all Third Front projects had to be dispersed in
  hidden mountain locations."* Leaders endorsed it in **August 1964** after the Tonkin Gulf incident. The siting
  formula is given in English as *"close to the mountains, dispersed, and hidden"* (Wikipedia; the Chinese wording
  kào shān, fēnsàn, yǐnbì 靠山、分散、隐蔽 appears in search snippets; a Chinese page adds "into caves" (进洞);
  UNVERIFIED in a page read).
- **Scale (SOURCED, Meyskens):** about **40% of the national construction budget, 1964 to 1980**; two waves (from 1964
  in the southwest: three railroads, **the steel town of Panzhihua**, the coke town of Liupanshui, a weapons complex in
  the mountains around Chongqing; from 1969 in central and northwest China); *"roughly fifteen million people took
  part in the campaign, with about one million laborers coming from urban areas and the rest mobilized from the
  countryside."* Wikipedia: about **1,100 projects, 1,945 enterprises and research institutions**; **205 billion
  yuan, 39.01%** of national investment in basic industry and infrastructure (1964–1980); from 1964 to 1970 about
  380 large projects, **145,000 workers** and 38,000 equipment units moved inland. **Conflicts:** urban workforce of
  about 1 million (Meyskens) vs **4 million urban workers with families** (Cambridge introduction, search 42
  snippet; UNVERIFIED) vs "over 200,000 workers who migrated with their work units" from more than 100 relocated
  factories and mines (Sixth Tone) vs 145,000 moved 1964–1970 (Wikipedia). Different scopes; see Unresolved
  conflicts.
- **Who was sent (SOURCED, Meyskens):** a **political background check** excluded anyone classed as landlord, rich
  peasant, counterrevolutionary or rightist or with foreign contacts, so that recruits *"would not disclose its
  existence"*; selection was presented as a political privilege. For urban workers it was a *"socioeconomic
  demotion."* Rural laborers hired out earned about **32 yuan a month**, of which the home work unit took a share for
  food and lodging, leaving **about 6 yuan** in hand (rural average earnings **11 to 15 yuan a year**).
- **Life on site (SOURCED, Meyskens):** sites reached by truck rides *"for hours, if not days, up dusty mountain
  roads"*; heavy machinery installed by hand; work *"militarized"* (military units; colleagues called
  "comrades-in-arms" (zhànyǒu, 战友); morning calisthenics, long hours, readings of Mao); early recruits slept in
  tents or under the stars; then **rammed-earth huts with thatched roofs**; stream water, rice porridge and pickled
  vegetables, meat about once a month, austerity *"by design"* to keep resources for heavy industry. The official
  order of priorities: *"First build the factory and afterward housing"*; some workers received a **"subsidy to keep
  secrets"**; about 75% of sampled work units paid above the national industrial norm (Wikipedia).
- **The work-unit "small society" later (SOURCED, Sixth Tone, about the children of such plants):** *"limited
  communication between factories and the outside world"*; each plant had a **code number**, and work IDs carried
  only that number; *"complete sets of facilities including hospitals, public spaces, staff canteens, recreation
  centers"*; free care at the factory hospital; factory schools to college age; one plant (Factory 401) had its own
  dairy farm with monthly milk tickets; a group wedding at which a leader announced *"you are a revolutionary couple
  from now on."* A former resident: *"Your factory was your home."*
- **What failed (SOURCED, Meyskens):** a **1984 State Council report** found *"only 48 percent of all projects were
  worthy of further development; the other 52 percent were abandoned"*; about **one-quarter** of funding went to war
  materiel plants; rushing caused shoddy construction and years of repairs. Workers' memoirs recall material
  privation, separation from family, and fear that poor work-unit schools would keep their children *"forever in
  China's mountainous backwoods."* Wikipedia adds: loss-making plants could close under the 1986–1990 plan; many were
  privatized 1980–2000; some moved out of caves to cities; old sites are now museums, hotels and leisure complexes.
  Relocation from mountain valleys to cities in the "adjustment" stage, and city land later taken for redevelopment
  (Cambridge introduction and a Hu Yuehan study, search 52 snippets; UNVERIFIED). Search 44 (Chinese, on the 1980s
  adjustment, the "devoted our youth" slogan and transfer of factory-run schools and hospitals) **died at the query.**
- **Second angle (search 57, Chinese, on the 1984 adjustment office and relocations): died at the query** for the
  office's founding and the "121 enterprises" figure; one return (Aisixiang, snippet) quotes a 1991 leadership remark
  that moving all Third Front plants was wrong and that military plants should open "windows" in coastal or central
  cities. **UNVERIFIED.**

**Sarov (formerly Arzamas-16), Nizhny Novgorod Oblast (SOURCED, Kommersant 2020, translated):**

- About **96,000** permanent residents, growing about **500 a year**; average age **41**; **1,038 women per 1,000 men**.
- City area **230 km²**, of which the built-up town is about **10%**; the rest is federal nuclear center (RFNC) and
  technical sites. *"The length of the city zone guarded to the standard of the Russian border is more than 100
  km."*
- **Entry:** *"An ordinary resident of the Nizhny Novgorod region or of Russia cannot get into Sarov"* without a
  special pass. Valid grounds: business trips, **visiting relatives who live there**, "socio-cultural needs"
  (athletes, artists, scientists, including foreign). Buying property, owning a business or temporary registration
  can bring a **permanent pass**. Entry is barred to people with convictions for serious crimes and to **dual
  citizens**; citizens of CIS countries cannot enter even for work. About **4,000 workers enter daily from 6:00 and
  must leave by 22:00.** The National Guard (Rosgvardia) inspects incoming cargo.
- **Residents:** those with access to state secrets (mainly RFNC staff and security-service and police employees)
  face **limits on private foreign travel**. Graduates of the local physics institute with good grades are
  **guaranteed jobs** at the RFNC. Residents leave freely.
- **Attitude:** *"The population of Sarov is in favor of the city remaining closed."* Opening was said to require
  costly extra security around RFNC sites.
- Search 45 snippet: population **94,084 (2024)**; a pass can take **up to three months** (travel source).
  **UNVERIFIED.**

**Seversk (formerly Tomsk-7), Tomsk Oblast (SOURCED, Novaya Gazeta 2016, translated):** about **108,000** people; built from the early 1950s around the **Siberian Chemical Combine**;
not on maps until perestroika. In 2016: all construction-installation departments bankrupt; staff cuts at the clinical
hospital and other public bodies; a combine subsidiary bankrupt with all its workers given notice; *"about one-third
of the working-age population"* said to be unemployed; residents commute to Tomsk by expensive bus; the young go into
security work, *"they have no other future"*; the largest rally in the city's history (**250** by police count, twice
that by organizers) demanded a halt to moving gas-centrifuge equipment away and a jobs program. A 2019 priority
development territory (TOSER "Seversk") was created to attract investment; population 94,500 in an undated ZATO
association presentation (search 46 snippets; UNVERIFIED).

**Habits of a closed production town (sourced as marked):**

- **Non-disclosure:** residents signed a non-disclosure undertaking covering the facility and their place of
  residence; residents **could not travel abroad or invite relatives**; relatives visiting Sarov today need a special
  pass (spetspropusk); hiring was strict even for cleaners (Zagorsk-6) if there was *"the slightest flaw"* in one's
  biography; perks: good services, no shortages of food or household goods, *"zero crime and high salaries"*;
  neighbors made shopping trips into Zagorsk-7; Arzamas-16 was surrounded by armed guards and barbed wire and its
  residents were *"not allowed to leave"* (citing the book *Sarov – eto my*). Russia now has **23** such settlements,
  **10 under Rosatom** and the rest under the Defense Ministry. **SOURCED** (Rambler 2021, secondary).
- **The workplace secrecy office:** the First Department (Pervyi otdel) was in charge of *"secrecy and political
  security of the workplace"* at every Soviet enterprise handling technical or scientific information; it was *"a
  part of the KGB"* and *"not subordinated to the management"*; it controlled *"access to information considered state
  secret,"* foreign travel and publications, and tracked copying machines and typewriters. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). A
  "Form 41" non-disclosure statement was signed on leaving a job with secret access (OSA Archivum catalog, search 51
  snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **The bargain (Ozersk, a closed plutonium town):** living there was *"akin to winning the golden ticket"*; high
  living standards; *"the exchange of political for consumer freedom"*; after the Soviet collapse, *"Ozersk residents
  voted to keep their fences and guards"*; temporary workers brought in by the tens of thousands were not monitored
  for radiation. **SOURCED** (Wilson Center summary of Kate Brown's talk on *Plutopia*, 2013).
