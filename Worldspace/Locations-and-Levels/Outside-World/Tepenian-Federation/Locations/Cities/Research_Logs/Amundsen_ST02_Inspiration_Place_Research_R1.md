## 1 · S2-04 Hakka earthen roundhouses (tulou), Fujian (e.g., Chengqi Lou)

**Claim to test (from the input):** single ring buildings housing hundreds of people of many households, inward-facing, with shared courtyards, wells and stores. Care: verify room counts and populations; distinguish famous from typical.

**Verdict on the claim:** CONFIRMED WITH CORRECTIONS. (1) Not all tulou are rings: many (in Yongding most) are square or rectangular, and some are "five-phoenix" mansion types; round ones cluster in particular valleys. (2) The well-known peak figures (Chengqi Lou "up to 800" residents, "400 rooms") are peak or upper-bound figures repeated across tourism and press sources, with differing counts (370 vs. 400 rooms; peak 600 vs. 800 people). Typical tulou are much smaller (for example a 16-bay single ring with six households, or a two-story building with "more than a dozen rooms"). (3) "Many households" is right, but historically they were usually one patrilineal clan (shared surname), not unrelated families. (4) Inward-facing with shared courtyard, wells, granary floor and ancestral hall: CONFIRMED. (5) Since the 1980s to 2010s, many are partly or wholly emptied; populations today are a fraction of the peaks.

**Research approach (tradition, not one place):** the building tradition itself, plus three documented places: Chengqi Lou and the Gaobei cluster (Yongding, Hakka); Hongkeng village with Zhencheng Lou and Fuyu Lou (Yongding, Hakka); and the Nanjing County clusters (Tianluokeng, Huaiyuan Lou, Yuchang Lou) with Hua'an's Eryi Lou (Minnan-built). One Guangdong (Raoping) example appears where the source covers it, labeled as such.

### A. Snapshot

- **What a tulou is:** a large enclosed, fortified earth building, usually round or rectangular, with load-bearing rammed-earth walls three to five stories high; "can house up to 800 people." Over 20,000 tulou-type earth buildings exist in Fujian, of which about 3,000 count as "Fujian Tulou" in the southwest. Walls up to 1.8 m thick on a 1-2 m stone base; walls lean slightly inward; lowest two stories had no windows; one main gate with iron-plated wooden doors, some with water tanks above to quench fires set at the door; gun ports on the top level. The term appears in a 1573 Zhangzhou record. [K+ defensive dwelling] [P3] — SOURCED: Wikipedia, "Fujian tulou," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian_tulou (read 2026-11-21; first 100,000 of 116,091 characters read).
- **UNESCO listing:** 46 buildings inscribed July 2008 (32nd session, Quebec City), spread over more than 120 km in southwest Fujian; built between the 15th (UNESCO's current text) or 12th (2008 text) and 20th centuries. — UNVERIFIED (UNESCO page returned HTTP 403; seen in search snippets of whc.unesco.org/en/list/1113 and Wikipedia). Of the 46, 23 are in Yongding (China Daily HK, 2024-09-10, https://www.chinadailyhk.com/article/592700, SOURCED; note this article misdates the listing as 2018).
- **Counts of tulou differ by source:** "over 23,000" in Yongding alone (The Paper, 2016-09-30, SOURCED); "about 30,000" in Fujian (Beijing Review, 2023-12, SOURCED); researcher Huang Hanmin's count of 2,812 large tulou (National Geographic, 2015, SOURCED); "Yongding has 1,768 tulou" (China Daily HK 2024, SOURCED). These figures plainly use different definitions (all earth buildings vs. large fortified tulou); none is wrong on its own terms.
- **Defining feature:** "closed outside, open inside": living rooms ring the outer wall, opening onto a central courtyard with the ancestral hall at the center (used for worship, festivals, weddings, funerals and meetings) and usually two or three public wells. [P5] — SOURCED: Wikipedia (above); National Geographic, "Hakka tulou" (2015-01-02), https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/150102-hakka-china-tulou-fujian-world-heritage-culture-housing (read 2026-11-21).
- **Vertical use of each household's slice (Chengqi Lou and many Yongding tulou):** ground floor kitchen and dining (no outer windows); second floor grain store (no outer windows); third and fourth floors bedrooms. A family therefore owned a vertical column of rooms. [P4] [K5] — SOURCED: Wikipedia; National Geographic 2015; Zheng Shanyun, 《福建土楼》 (Chinese Culture Net, Hong Kong, PDF, 86 pp.), https://chiculture.org.hk/sites/mainsite/files/2019-04/30025_fujiantulou_pdf_cn.pdf (saved PDF read locally 2026-11-21; undated; author is head of the feature desk, China News Service Fujian bureau).

### B. What else exists there (economy, institutions, services, who lives there)

**Social organization (historical):**
- Usually one multi-generational patrilineal clan per tulou; residents shared a surname except in-marrying wives. Each son's branch received a unit; rooms identical in size and decoration "whether they belonged to the Hakka clan leader or a common pig farmer." Shared property: wells, ancestral hall, bathrooms, weapons, land and orchards. [K4] [P7] — SOURCED: Wikipedia; National Geographic 2015; China Digital Times excerpt of NYT, 2011-03-22, https://chinadigitaltimes.net/2011/03/monuments-to-clan-life-are-losing-their-appeal/.
- **Rotating duties:** festivals, cleaning of public areas, and opening and closing of the main gate were assigned to family branches in rotation. [K1] [K6] [R] — SOURCED: Wikipedia. Gate closed at night, late returners needed a password to be let in: UNVERIFIED (search snippet of an HKU thesis, hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/181560/2/Content.pdf; the PDF returned HTTP 403).
- **Self-sufficiency under siege:** with food stockpiles people "could live for months without leaving" (NYT via CDT, SOURCED); wells, granaries and animal pens inside (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). [K5]
- **Clan elder leads the New Year rite:** on the first day of the lunar year the clan head (族长) leads all residents in worship at the ancestral hall; on the 1st and 15th of each lunar month elders and women burn incense there (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). Described as places and practices only. [K2]
- **Mutual aid:** "if one family has trouble, everyone helps," including between tulou and between villages (Zheng PDF, SOURCED, a descriptive and somewhat idealizing source). [K1]

**Economy (historical and present):**
- Construction was financed by clan pooling or by wealthy individuals. Fuyu Lou (1880) was built by three Lin brothers who grew rich from a workshop making tobacco-shredding knives (条丝烟刀); Zhencheng Lou (1912) by descendants of a tobacco-knife/cigarette-cutter business, at a cost of 80,000 silver dollars over five years. [K8] — SOURCED: Zheng PDF; National Geographic 2015.
- Farming: rice, persimmons and tea on limited farmland (Beijing Review 2023, SOURCED); collectivized farming continued in the 1960s commune era (Wikipedia, SOURCED). In one Guangdong (Raoping) tulou, about 400 people in three production brigades lived there in the 1950s (The World of Chinese, 2024-02, SOURCED).
- **Today:** tourism is the main cash economy around the listed clusters. Yongding Tulou Scenic Area: 4.6 million visitors and 182.37 million yuan revenue in 2023; more than 20% of tulou operating income goes to a preservation fund (China Daily, 2024-06-06, https://global.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202406/06/WS6661a968a31082fc043cb49e_3.html, SOURCED). National Day/Mid-Autumn 2025: 284,100 visits, 13.37 million yuan in ticket revenue, 420 performances in seven days, nearby homestays 95% full (China Daily via chinaservicesinfo, 2025-10-10, SOURCED). Peak daily visitors near 30,000 (The Paper 2016, SOURCED). [K8]
- **Ground floors as shops:** many ground-floor rooms in Chengqi Lou now sell souvenirs and tea (Beijing Review 2023, SOURCED); residents sell tea, tobacco, herbs and handicrafts; some charge about US$1 to look inside (Gulf News/LA Times 2018, SOURCED).
- **Hotels and homestays:** Fuyu Lou (19 rooms as hotel, 2018); a Kanxia tulou as hotel at US$15-20/night (2018); Shengheng Lou converted for nearly 12 million yuan into 14 suites, about 5,000 guests in 2023 (China Daily HK 2024, SOURCED); Huanxing Lou (about 500 years old, two past fires, used as a warehouse) renovated from July 2022 for 120 million yuan with elevators, supported by Tencent (Beijing Review 2023, SOURCED). A Nanjing County tulou homestay association exists; an intangible-heritage market near Huaiyuan Lou drew about 700,000 visitors from May 2025 to April 2026 (Fujian provincial government English site, 2026-04-23, https://www.fj.gov.cn/english/cultureandtravel/attractions/202604/t20260423_7130613.htm, SOURCED).
- **Ticket revenue sharing (Hongkeng):** villagers receive 8% of ticket revenue, reported as "600-plus to around 900" (unit not stated) per year per person or household (unclear) (The Paper, 2016-09-30, https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1536701, SOURCED). [K8] [K9]

**Who lives there now:**
- Hongkeng village: "more than 600 households, over 3,000 people, all of the Lin clan" (Zheng PDF, undated, SOURCED); "over 910 households and nearly 3,000 people, about 1,500 living in the scenic area" (The Paper 2016, SOURCED).
- Many tulou now hold only five or six residents, mostly frail elderly people; parents work in cities while grandparents raise children (National Geographic 2015, SOURCED). Young couples with children leave for concrete houses with privacy, indoor plumbing and air conditioning; some tulou have become de facto homes for seniors (Gulf News/LA Times 2018, SOURCED). [K9] [P9]
- Gaotou township (where Chengqi Lou stands): 5 administrative villages, 38 natural villages, 5,219 resident population in 2010, about 90% surnamed Jiang (UNVERIFIED: search snippet of an academic paper on Gaotou Jiang genealogy). Current Chengqi Lou household count: NOT FOUND (see F).

**Institutions (governance and law):**
- **Regulation:** 《福建土楼世界文化遗产保护条例》 (Fujian Tulou World Cultural Heritage Protection Regulations), adopted by the Fujian Provincial People's Congress Standing Committee 2011-09-29, in force 2011-12-01. Owners/users sign a protection-and-use responsibility letter with the county heritage department (Art. 20); owners do daily upkeep and must report danger (Art. 21); urgent repairs are organized by the county and paid for by provincial and higher governments (Art. 21); only qualified heritage firms may design or carry out repairs, keeping original structure, materials and craft, with plans approved by the national heritage department (Art. 22); tourism revenue must go to protection and to compensating owners, with the method negotiated among county, township, village committee and owners, audited and reported to owners (Art. 26); village committees may write village rules (村规民约) and set up community protection groups (Art. 4). [K1] [K6] [K8] — SOURCED (reprint): https://www.fadada.com/article/detail-9380 (official gazette text not read).
- **Fire rules (Art. 18):** county organizes security and fire facilities, inspects electricity, gas and fire use, runs self-rescue training; owners must provide fire equipment and manage open flame; volunteer fire teams are encouraged. [K1] [K6] [R] — SOURCED (same).
- **Prohibitions in the core area (Art. 17):** no mining, quarrying or sand extraction, no graves or waste sites, no storing of flammable, explosive or corrosive materials, no defacing, no outdoor advertising or artificial attractions; fines 2,000-20,000 yuan (Art. 28). [K6] — SOURCED (same).
- **Scenic-area management:** Fujian Hakka Tulou Tourism Development Co. (a company running the Yongding scenic area); in August 2016 the National Tourism Administration gave the Yongding 5A scenic area a severe warning (unlicensed guides, unsafe walkways, poor toilets and sanitation, disorderly vehicles, vendors blocking roads); rectification included 32 upgraded public toilets with 24-hour staff, 175 street stalls removed, 1 person detained (The Paper 2016, SOURCED). [K1] [K9]
- **Schools:** many larger tulou had a dedicated schoolroom or used the ancestral hall as a school: named examples Yijing Lou's private school (Yongding), the Hu clan's Tangzi Ge private school (Xiayang), Huaiyuan Lou's "Sishi Shi" school (Nanjing), the school-building of Yulong Lou in Gaopi town (高陂镇), Yongding (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). Today: Chuxi has a primary school; Hekeng's enrollment is shrinking and kindergarten children take a small bus (National Geographic 2015, SOURCED). In Neilong village (coastal Fujian), villagers donated spare rooms in Taoshu Lou as classrooms for a community academy that opened May 2017 (Sixth Tone, 2017, SOURCED). [K10] [K13]
- **Resettlement (Hongkeng):** resettlement housing began 2008; four phases covering nearly 400 households, nearly 100 resettled by 2016; survey standard 6 m² per person; relocation cannot be forced and some residents oppose it (The Paper 2016, SOURCED). [K4] [K9]

**Daily logistics and services:** [K5]
- Transport: Longyan-Xiamen railway opened 2012; Nanjing Station designed to resemble three tulou; Shancheng (Nanjing County) about 2 hours by car from Xiamen (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Shenzhen to the area about four hours by train (China Daily HK 2024, SOURCED).
- Water: public wells in the courtyard; wealthier tulou had private kitchen wells (Yuchang Lou: one by each kitchen stove, a well "only about a meter" deep because of springs under the hill side, Zheng PDF, SOURCED).
- Chimneys built into the earth walls kept kitchens free of smoke (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). [R]
- Sanitation: rooms had no sanitary facilities (Zheng PDF, SOURCED); Qingxing Lou added bathrooms on each floor, but many residents still used chamber pots (Gulf News/LA Times 2018, SOURCED). Where toilets and animal pens were located in older tulou: NOT FOUND (see F).
- Electricity reached one Guangdong (Raoping) tulou at night only about two decades before 2024 (The World of Chinese, 2024, SOURCED).

### C. Cultural locations (names, what each is)

- **Ancestral halls inside each tulou** (worship, weddings, funerals, meetings); today some serve as bike parking (Gulf News/LA Times 2018, SOURCED). [K10]
- **Earth-god shrines at every village entrance in Yongding**, visited on the 1st and 15th of the first lunar month with offerings (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). Described as places only.

- **Stone brush-pillars (石笔)** set up before ancestral halls for clan members who passed imperial exams; the Hu clan temple in Zhongchuan village, Xiayang, once had 25, now 15 (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). [K13]

Hua'an traditional houses turned into galleries, cafés and libraries (Fujian gov. 2026, SOURCED). [K10] [K13]
- **Performing arts of the region:** Minxi Han opera (originally called luantan; named Han opera in the early 1930s) and Minxi marionette theater (puppets about 77 cm tall, each worked by more than ten strings, several hundred plays) (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). Shifan ensembles play at rites (Zheng PDF; ihchina, SOURCED).

**Festivals and customs:** [K2]
- **Minxi Hakka Lantern Festival celebrations** (national intangible heritage, 2008, no. X-71), 15th of the first lunar month, in Liancheng, Yongding, Changting, Wuping and Shanghang. Components: Gutian dragon parade (22 generations; dragon segments made by each household, each surname in rotation one year at a time), Luofang "walking the ancient affairs" (走古事, 28 generations), Zhixi lanterns, Fushi (Yongding) lantern stories, Xinquan firecrackers. Protecting body: Liancheng County Cultural Center. — SOURCED: https://www.ihchina.cn/project_details/15078.html; Zheng PDF (Luofang: young men train their legs on the mountain on the 3rd-4th of the first month, fast three days from the 12th, then carry roughly 200 kg tableau frames, about 20 bearers each, up a riverbed against the current on the 15th).
- **Hukeng "Zuo Dafu" (作大福)**, a thanksgiving rite after the Double Ninth Festival honoring the deity Baosheng Dadi, with opera, a procession and "five sacrifices" offerings set out by each family; origin told as a late-Ming plague; villagers divide the work (booking opera troupes, collecting fees, building the stage). Frequency conflicts: one source says yearly (the Aoyao village event, Sohu, 2020-12-03, https://www.sohu.com/a/436233812_100218260, SOURCED); snippets say the Li clan holds it every three years, lunar 9th month 11th-16th (UNVERIFIED: Sohu 202300912 and Baidu Baike snippets; Baike returned 403). Described as a custom and place only. [K1] [K8]
- **Spring Festival in a tulou:** red lanterns in the courtyard, couplets on doors, home-made rice wine given and served (CGTN, 2019-01-27, SOURCED). Hakka New Year runs from the 24th of the 12th lunar month to the 15th of the first; on the 2nd day of the year each household kills a capon, then the family visits the ancestors' graves (Zheng PDF, SOURCED). Former residents return for national holidays (May Day, National Day, New Year) and weddings (National Geographic 2015; Gulf News 2018, SOURCED). Hotel owners report slow business outside those holidays (Gulf News 2018, SOURCED). [K7]
- **Mazu procession** with a dragon parade through Meilin's tulou (National Geographic 2015, SOURCED).
- **Funerals:** at Yangzhao Lou professional mourners joined a funeral (National Geographic 2015, SOURCED). Recorded as a fact only (deferred mortuary question).
- **Festivals created on purpose:** [K3] the 2025 Yongding "Intangible Cultural Heritage Carnival" (420 performances, a 3D light show of released lanterns, dragon dances); Hua'an night parades of "intangible heritage" (Spring Festival 2026: 100,000+ tourists, ticket revenue up 23% to 6.2 million yuan, night visits up 73.72%); "thousand lanterns" garden events at Spring Festival (China Daily 2025; Fujian gov. 2026, SOURCED; thousand-lantern event UNVERIFIED snippet). **Food and drink:** [K12]
- Hakka rice wine made in a tulou: steamed rice cooled on bamboo, put in a jar with water, red yeast (hongqu) and white yeast (baiqu), fermented about a month; golden, sweet and strong (CGTN 2019, SOURCED; a guide calls Yongding tulou wine low-alcohol: UNVERIFIED snippet).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- The enclosed ring/square with one gate and a central hall produced clan self-government in one building: rotating gate and cleaning duty, posted conduct rules, the clan head's New Year rite, equal identical rooms, and shared wells, granary floor and weapons (Wikipedia; National Geographic; Zheng PDF; all SOURCED). [K1] [K6] [P7]
- Vertical household slices (kitchen below, grain in the middle, bedrooms above) meant every family handled its own food storage inside the wall, and siege-proofing was built into the floor plan (sources above). [K5]
- The thick earth walls are described as keeping a "cool in summer, warm in winter" microclimate and buffering humidity in a region with about 1,800 mm of annual rain (Zheng PDF, SOURCED; residents' statement in Beijing Review 2023, SOURCED). Measured data: a summer study of Chengqi Lou's storage rooms reportedly found the walls moderate temperature and moisture, with winter performance unstudied (UNVERIFIED: snippet of MDPI Sustainability 4(11):2803; page returned 403). [R]
- The same features now drive departure: shared corridors, connected doors and no sanitary facilities clash with younger residents' wish for private households (Zheng PDF; NYT 2011 via CDT; Gulf News 2018; all SOURCED). Building a new tulou costs about five times a concrete house (Zheng PDF; National Geographic 2015, SOURCED), so few new ones are built: "every one that falls is one fewer" (Zheng PDF, paraphrase). [K9]
- Fame of a few listed tulou channels money (tickets, hotels, preservation fund) to them, while unlisted ones lack funding: Sixth Tone (2017) reports more than 3,000 tulou lacking funding and official protection; government involvement generally requires heritage designation (SOURCED). [K8] [K9]
- Earth walls are vulnerable to soaking: a Hakka rammed-earth ancestral hall complex (Guantian Li family hall, Shanghang County, built 1836) partly collapsed (about 1,000 m²) after record 24-hour rain in June 2024 (China Daily, 2024-06-18, SOURCED). Collapse of "more than 20" tulou after typhoons Meranti and Megi (2016): UNVERIFIED (ScienceDirect abstract snippet citing a press report; not confirmed in a separate search).

### E. Structural features bearing on industry, economy, arts and work culture

- **Rammed-earth craft as a competence community:** Hakka tulou construction technique is national intangible heritage (2011, third batch, VIII-28; applicant Nanjing County): tools are formwork boards, rammers, shovels and large and small beating boards; steps are soil selection, proportional mixing, piling and curing, moisture control, a ramming sequence, and wall repair; carpenters design the timber frames, dividing the ring evenly by room (https://www.ihchina.cn/project_details/14308.html, SOURCED). The page says modern materials have pushed the craft close to being lost. ## 2 · S2-03 Fez el-Bali (the old medina of Fez)

**Claim to test (from the input):** a very large car-free medina of lanes, dead ends and courtyards: a whole working city as a maze. Care: verify size, population and how goods and services move without vehicles.

**Verdict on the claim:** CONFIRMED, with corrections on population. (1) Size: about 2.2-3.0 km² (Wikipedia infobox 2.20 km²; car-free area "about 300 hectares," about 2,400 m by 1,600 m). (2) Car-free: confirmed for the interior; one road enters from the south along the river to Place R'cif for public transport and emergency vehicles, and trucks serve only areas near the gates. (3) Population: the widely repeated "about 156,000-160,000" is a 2002-era figure (and Guinness "largest car-free urban area by population," record dated 2009-10-16, UNVERIFIED snippet). Census figures for the Fès-Médina arrondissement fall steeply: 109,137 (1994), 91,473 (2004), 70,592 (2014), 49,775 (2024). The arrondissement boundary is not necessarily identical to the walled Fes el-Bali, so the two series should not be equated without checking (boundary NOT FOUND). (4) "Working city": confirmed: crafts employ over 40,000 people in the medina (2023). (5) Maze: confirmed by street widths down to 60 cm, cul-de-sacs, and bent-axis entries, but the layout is also a deliberate hierarchy (house, house compound, quarter, city).

### A. Snapshot

- **Location and origin:** on both banks of the Oued Fès (Wadi al-Jawahir) in northern Morocco; Idris I founded a town on the right bank in 789, Idris II a second on the left bank in 809; joined under the Almoravids in the 11th century; refugee settlers from al-Andalus and Kairouan produced two quarters, the Andalusian Quarter (east bank) and the Qarawiyyin Quarter (west bank), each with its own walls, mosques and markets. [P3] — SOURCED: Wikipedia, "Fes el Bali," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fes_el_Bali (read 2026-11-21); Said Ennahid, "The City in the Islamic World: Medieval Town Planning & the Preservation of Architectural Heritage in Fez," IEMed, 2025-05-02, https://www.iemed.org/publication/the-city-in-the-islamic-world-medieval-town-planning-the-preservation-of-architectural-heritage-in-fez/ (SOURCED).
- **Size and form:** car-free area about 300 ha (about 2,400 m E-W by 1,600 m N-S); some lanes as narrow as 60 cm, few reach 5 m; 13,380 historic buildings and 10,539 retail businesses (as of 2002); density about 550 inhabitants per hectare (2002) — SOURCED: J. Crawford, "The Medina, Fes-al-Bali, Morocco," Carfree.com, 2002, https://www.carfree.com/fes. A guide snippet gives 279 ha and "9,400" lanes: UNVERIFIED.
- **Population history (sources and dates):** 1921: 70,645 (1,500 Europeans); 1952: 179,400 (15,800 Europeans); late 1970s: nearly 180,000 at about 650/ha (IEMed 2025, SOURCED; these early counts may cover the wider historic city). 2002: 156,000 (Carfree 2002, SOURCED). Arrondissement Fès-Médina: 1994 109,137; 2004 91,473 (20,088 households); 2014 70,592 (17,342 households, 212 foreigners); 2024 49,775 — SOURCED: City Population (citing HCP Morocco), https://www.citypopulation.de/en/morocco/fes/2310107__fès_médina/ (read 2026-11-21); household counts UNVERIFIED (HCP snippet). [P9]
- **Layout as hierarchy:** four levels: courtyard house, house compound, quarter, city; access at each level controlled by bent-axis entries and cul-de-sacs; "a distinct separation between the public sphere and the family sanctum" (IEMed 2025, SOURCED). Early 20th century: 18 neighborhoods (12 Qarawiyyin side, 6 Andalusian side) per Roger Le Tourneau (Wikipedia, SOURCED). [P5]
- **Walls and gates:** a long circuit of walls still largely encloses it; main historic gates Bab Mahrouk, Bab Guissa, Bab Ftouh; Bab Bou Jeloud is the best-known entry today (Wikipedia, SOURCED). The French-built Bab Boujloud gate's doors carried a large outer bolt "used to lock in the inhabitants at night or during unrest" (Khalid Bekkaoui, "Fez Medina: Creation, Conquest and Contraband," Open University Ferguson Center, https://university.open.ac.uk/arts/research/ferguson-centre/node/127, SOURCED). Night closing of quarter gates as well as city gates (a 19th-century traveler's account): UNVERIFIED (search snippet).
- **UNESCO:** inscribed 1981 (with Fes Jdid) as "Medina of Fez"; UNESCO describes it as "dangerously overpopulated" with uncontrolled development and building deterioration (Wikipedia citing UNESCO, SOURCED secondhand). A 1976 UNESCO general assembly in Nairobi had declared it a "cultural treasure of humanity" (Cour des comptes, below, SOURCED).

**Daily logistics (how goods and services move without vehicles):** [K5] [R]

## 3 · S2-05 Matera's Sassi

**Claim to test (from the input):** a cave town layered into a ravine, emptied by government order in the mid-20th century and later revived. Care: displacement history, report neutrally; verify evacuation dates and law.

**Verdict on the claim:** CONFIRMED, with precision. (1) Law: Legge 17 maggio 1952, n. 619 ("risanamento dei rioni Sassi"), followed by further special laws (n. 299 of 21 March 1958; n. 126 of 28 February 1967; n. 1403 of 15 December 1971). (2) It was not a single order to empty everything: the 1952 law provided for vacating dwellings declared uninhabitable, repairing those that could be repaired, and building rural hamlets; in practice, because of residents' poverty, sources describe it as a forced evacuation. Relocation ran in phases from 1953 (first La Martella houses handed over 17 May 1953) into the late 1960s and early 1970s. (3) Numbers: sources give about 13,000 (1937 survey), 15,000 (1950s), "about 17,000" and "almost 20,000" Sassi residents; the 1951 census gives the whole comune 30,390. (4) Revival: Legge 11 novembre 1986, n. 771 (recovery of national interest), UNESCO listing December 1993, European Capital of Culture 2019. (5) "Cave town": partly; in 1937 1,646 of 2,997 Sassi dwellings were rock-cut, the rest built or mixed.

### A. Snapshot

- **What and where:** two districts, Sasso Caveoso and Sasso Barisano, with the Civita between them and the "Piano" above, forming Matera's old town in Basilicata, on one slope of the Gravina ravine on the Murgia plateau; houses dug into calcarenite ("tufo"); streets often run on the roofs of houses below. UNESCO 1993 with the Park of the Rupestrian Churches; criteria (iii), (iv), (v); 1,016 ha, buffer 4,365 ha. [P3] — SOURCED: Wikipedia, "Sassi di Matera," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassi_di_Matera (read 2026-11-21). (One source, Architectural League 2020, gives the UNESCO year as 1996; Wikipedia and Internazionale give 1993.)
- **Sub-districts:** Sasso Barisano had five rioni (Casale, Vetere, Lombardi, Fiorentini, Casale di San Pietro Barisano), mostly tailors and artisans until the 1950s; Sasso Caveoso six rioni (Capone, Pianelle, Casale del Seminario, Malve, Casale del Monte Errone/Idris, Casalnuovo), artisans, carpenters, tailors, shoemakers and mostly peasants. The two Sassi reportedly also differed in speech — SOURCED: Michele Valente, *Evoluzione socio-economica dei Sassi di Matera nel XX secolo* (Energheia, 2021), https://www.energheia.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Evoluzione-socio-economica-dei-Sassi-di-Matera-nel-XX-secolo-Michele-Valente.pdf (saved PDF read locally). [P9]
- **Conditions before evacuation (1937 health survey by Matera's health officer Luca Crispino, presented March 1937):** 2,997 dwellings in both Sassi; 1,646 dug into rock, "without air, without light, soaked with damp"; 501 beyond economic repair; 850 in good condition; 2,147 (71.6%) uninhabitable. Matera then had 23,040 inhabitants, about 13,000 in the Sassi in 2,910 families; most families had one room, often shared with animals; about 1.2 rooms per dwelling and about 4 persons per room (Valente 2021, SOURCED). A 1938 municipal hygiene office statistic: 1,641 cave dwellings of 2,997 (54.85%) and infant mortality of 44.42% (Casa Grotta museum page, https://www.casagrotta.it/storia-della-casa-grotta/, SOURCED; other popular sources give different infant-mortality figures, e.g. 463 per 1,000 vs. a national 112, UNVERIFIED). In the early 1950s about 15,000 people lived in the Sassi and there was "not even one school" there (Valente 2021, SOURCED). [K9] [P9]
- **Population of the comune:** 30,390 (1951), 38,562 (1961), 44,502 (1971), 59,748 (2021 census) (Wikipedia "Matera," SOURCED; 1971 from Valente). Current residents of the Sassi: "about 3,000" (Visitare Matera, citing no census for the Sassi; UNVERIFIED, page 403); older estimates "over 4,000" (2007) and "4,000-5,000" (UNVERIFIED snippets). No official count found (see F).

### B. What else exists there

**The evacuation and resettlement (reported neutrally):** [K9] [K4] [P7]
- **Political background:** Carlo Levi's *Cristo si è fermato a Eboli* (1945) made the Sassi known nationally; Communist leader Palmiro Togliatti visited in 1948; Prime Minister Alcide De Gasperi called the old town "una vergogna nazionale" and accelerated its emptying through special laws (Alessandro Leogrande, *Internazionale*, 2015-09-13, https://www.internazionale.it/weekend/alessandro-leogrande/2015/09/13/matera-capitale-cultura, SOURCED). De Gasperi visited in 1950 (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Law 619/1952, as written** (text read at Edizioni Europee, https://edizionieuropee.it/law/html/1/zn14_04_010.html, SOURCED): Art. 1 methods: (a) transfer of parts of the districts whose dwellings are declared uninhabitable, (b) repair of dwellings that can be made fit, (c) building rural hamlets under the land-reclamation framework. Art. 4: approval of the transfer plan counts as a declaration of public utility, with works declared urgent under the 1865 expropriation law. Art. 5: the State pays for infrastructure, for permanently sealing uninhabitable dwellings, and for rural hamlet services. Art. 6: the Ministry of Public Works builds public housing for those who must leave. Art. 7 (later repealed in 2008): the Mayor, with the Genio civile, declares dwellings uninhabitable, orders them vacated by a deadline, and gives eligible household heads a right to a public housing unit. Art. 8: owners ordered to repair; if they fail, the State does the work and recovers the cost over ten years without interest. Art. 9: tampering with sealing works is fined. Art. 10: a commission chaired by the Mayor assigns housing. Art. 11: assignees must move in within one month or lose the unit; subletting and transfer forbidden. Art. 12: Matera's public housing institute (IACP) manages the new buildings. Art. 13: annual rent set by the Ministry. Art. 14: assignees may buy, in 35 interest-free annual installments. Art. 15: about 700 million lire (1951-1955) for expropriation, works and advances; about 4.5 billion lire (1951-1957) for housing.
- **New districts built to 1965** (Valente 2021, SOURCED): La Martella rural hamlet (160 buildings, 183 dwellings, 1,350 rooms), whose first houses De Gasperi handed to 50 families on 17 May 1953; Venusio (66 dwellings); Picciano (62); Agna semi-rural (175); Serra Venerdì quarter (828 dwellings, 47 shops); La Nera (353 dwellings, 13 shops); Spine Bianche (667 dwellings, 32 shops). La Martella was built 1952-1954 by UNRRA-Casas (Adriano Olivetti was its commissioner from 1947) with architects including Ludovico Quaroni and Federico Gorio, designed to keep the "values of relationship and proximity" of the old neighborhoods (UNVERIFIED: Olivetti Foundation and other snippets; the Olivetti Foundation PDF was too large to read).
- **Experience and effects:** after the new quarters were built, sources note a contrast between their planning quality and the "socio-cultural poverty" of the former Sassi residents, with no economic program beyond agriculture to support the move (Valente 2021, SOURCED). How individual families experienced the move: NOT FOUND in a read source (see F).
- **Individual case:** the Vizziello cave at 11 Vico Solitario was walled up on 16 April 1956 under the law; the Genio Civile took permanent possession and paid 42,800 lire compensation; it is now the Casa Grotta museum (Casa Grotta page, SOURCED).
- **After the evacuation:** emptied houses were sealed; April 1965 saw the first major collapse of evacuated houses, and 14 families still living nearby were moved out (Valente 2021, SOURCED). A December 1967 conference on the "risanamento" heard Italia Nostra's president call himself "extremely pessimistic"; the Sassi were partly inhabited until the mid-1960s (Internazionale 2015, SOURCED). Law 1403 of 15 December 1971 reopened the way to a design competition for recovery (Valente 2021, SOURCED); an international competition followed in 1974 (UNVERIFIED snippet).

**Recovery (Law 771/1986, as written; Edizioni Europee, https://edizionieuropee.it/law/html/34/zn5_07_009.html, SOURCED):** [K1] [K8]
- Art. 1: conservation and recovery of the Sassi of "preeminent national interest." Art. 5: 100 billion lire to the Comune for 1986-1989. Arts. 2-3: biennial recovery programs approved by the council, listing properties, uses, infrastructure and costs. Art. 6(4): up to 5% for temporary "parking" homes and premises during works. Art. 4 and 8: sub-concession of restored properties to individuals, associations or housing cooperatives meeting public-housing criteria, with at least ten years' residence; commercial and service uses with grants up to 60% and a ten-year direct-use commitment. ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- Cut-rock and stacked houses on a dry limestone slope made water the shared problem: rain ran from roofs through terracotta gutters into rock-cut cisterns plastered with cocciopesto, serving homes, cellars, oil mills, cheese workshops and stables; cistern water had to be boiled; drinking water came from springs, aqueducts (an underground aqueduct to a public fountain from at least 1351) and public fountains; large public cisterns ("palombari") from 1565; in dry summers people drew water from a year-round pool in the Gravina; the Apulian Aqueduct arrived in 1926 with cast-iron fountains in the Sassi; rock-cut silos stored wheat for up to fifteen years (Matera Welcome, "Matera and water," https://www.materawelcome.it/en/?p=12102, SOURCED). A neighborhood (vicinato) bell cistern served 4-6 houses, 30-80 m³; private ones 5-15 m³; about 2,200 cisterns in all (UNVERIFIED: ENI page 404; Geopop snippet). [K1] [K5] [R]
- **The vicinato** (neighborhood courtyard) was the "molecule" of settlement: groups of poor families around a shared courtyard, often with a well and common oven, bound by "singular traditional laws" amounting to "exasperating mutual control"; artisans dominated peasants within them; envy and hypocrisy coexisted with mutual aid born of need; relationships between neighbors mattered more than kinship. A 1950 sociometric study (Lidia De Rita, University of Bari) found residents described the vicinato as "all united," "like a family" (Valente 2021, SOURCED). [K4] [K6] [K10] [P5]
- Cave living with animals and no sanitation produced the hygiene crisis that drove the 1952 law and the evacuation (Crispino survey; Valente; SOURCED). [K9]
- Once emptied, the same rock fabric became an asset for heritage tourism, film sets, cave hotels and festivals (sources above). [K8]

## 4 · S2-11 Shibam, Hadhramaut

**Claim to test (from the input):** a dense walled city of tall mud-brick tower houses, close-packed and connected. Care: conflict history, report neutrally; verify current condition and population.

**Verdict on the claim:** CONFIRMED, with corrections. (1) Walled and dense: about 465-500 tower houses inside a mud-brick wall roughly 330 m by 250 m (one source). (2) Heights: most 5-8 stories; "5 to 11" in Wikipedia; one 2010 news article says "up to 16 stories" (an outlier, not supported elsewhere). (3) "Connected": bridges and doors between buildings for escape are reported (National Geographic 2017), but the extent of connection is not documented in any read source. (4) Population: widely cited "about 7,000" (Wikipedia, Al Bayan) vs. "about 3,000" (GOPHCY via Asia Times, 2019) vs. "16,094" (2004, a popular Arabic site, probably the wider town or district: UNVERIFIED). Current population: NOT FOUND in an official source. (5) Condition: on UNESCO's List of World Heritage in Danger since July 2015; repeated house collapses (2008 flood; 2019 wall section; 2020 rains: at least four buildings fully collapsed, 15 damaged; a house collapse in January 2026), and ongoing restoration programs.

### A. Snapshot

- **Location:** Wadi Hadhramaut, eastern Yemen, Hadhramaut Governorate; seat of Shibam District; Seiyun and Tarim lie to the east; road west to Mukalla (Wikipedia, "Shibam," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibam, read 2026-11-21, SOURCED). About 990 km from Sana'a and about 700 m above sea level (Holm Akhdar, "Old Shibam: an environmentally advanced mud city," published 2019-11-15, re-edited June 2026, PDF read locally, SOURCED). Hot desert climate; about 64 mm annual rain (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Form:** a walled city on a raised earth dais above the wadi floor (Aga Khan Award page, SOURCED); trapezoid close to a rectangle (Al Bayan, undated, SOURCED); wall about 330 m by 250 m (Al-Ain, 2020-11-02, SOURCED); wall 5-9 m high; the only entrance is on the south side, the gateway at the meeting point of flood channels (Al Bayan, c. 2021, https://www.albayan.ae/world/arab/2021-07-09-1.4204110, SOURCED). Site of the Aga Khan-awarded project: 81,000 m² (Aga Khan, SOURCED).
- **Houses:** "about 500" mud-brick tower houses (Wikipedia; Al Bayan 2012); "more than 465 houses and buildings, most five to eight floors" (Al Jazeera Net, 2026-01-14); "470" (Asia Times 2019); "400 inhabitable clay towers" (Yemen Times 2007). Foundations 1-2 m deep; ground-floor walls about 2 m thick, thinning upward; walls whitened with lime or ash (Al Bayan c. 2021, SOURCED). Average house: six stories, about 500 m² total surface, 225 m² usable (Aga Khan, SOURCED). Present town dates from 1533 after floods in 1532-1533 destroyed much of the earlier city (Wikipedia; Al Bayan; SOURCED). [P3] [P10]
- **History:** capital of the Kingdom of Hadhramaut around 300 CE; in the 20th century one of the three main cities of the Qu'aiti Sultanate (Wikipedia, SOURCED); historically a caravan stop on the incense route (National Geographic, 2017-04-03, SOURCED). Merchants returning from Asia in the 19th century renewed the walled city and expanded it into a southern suburb, al-Sahil (Al Bayan c. 2021, SOURCED). [K8]
- **UNESCO:** inscribed 1982; danger list 2015 (Wikipedia, SOURCED); added on 2 July 2015 "with the outbreak of war" (Holm Akhdar, SOURCED). UNESCO pages themselves were refused (HTTP 403).

### B. What else exists there

**The tower house (floor-by-floor use):** [P4] [P5] [K4] [K6]
- Ground and first floors: dark, lofty, narrow storerooms for grain and staple food with few openings; sheep and goats kept in adjacent rooms and on first-floor terraces at night. Second and third floors: several living rooms used by the men. Fourth and fifth floors: women's living areas with kitchens, washrooms and toilets. Sixth floor and above: children, or newly wed couples of the extended family. Upper terraces stand in for the courtyards the houses lack — SOURCED: Salma Samar Damluji, "Earthen wear," *The Architectural Review*, c. 2020 (PDF from the Dawan Architecture Foundation, read locally).
- Other accounts: ground floor tools and animals, second floor food, third floor usually the elderly, fourth for receiving guests (ArchDaily, 2022-07-13, SOURCED); ground floors windowless for livestock and grain, upper floors communal social space, with connecting bridges and doors between buildings for escape (National Geographic 2017, SOURCED). The accounts differ in detail; Damluji's is the fullest.
- Orientation: service spaces face north; main facades and windows face south to catch winter sun; decoration limited to doors, windows and interior columns; facades plastered with lime (nura) on the lower and upper parts, the middle left mud-colored (Shiban and al-Saqqaf, "Mud architecture in Wadi Hadhramaut," *Hadhramout University Journal of Natural and Applied Sciences* 16(1), June 2019, PDF read locally, SOURCED). [R]

**Materials and building craft (K14):** [K14] [R] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- Mud brick (madar): field mud mixed with straw (wheat or barley chaff) to reduce cracking and shrinkage, left to ferment one to two weeks, poured into wooden molds of various sizes, sun-dried; limestone foundations; lime (nura) made by burning limestone in kilns, crushed to powder, mixed with sand, and applied to roofs and facades as a moisture barrier (Shiban and al-Saqqaf 2019, SOURCED).
- Alluvial mud is collected from the fields around the city after floods; renders are mud with local hay and straw; lime waterproofing in two layers plus a final wash; the traditional ash-based binder (ramad) was hard to reproduce, so retired master builders trained younger builders to make it; one old master builder trained two apprentices to stabilize the al-Hara madrasa minaret (about 20 m); hauling materials through narrow streets and up to roofs is the main constraint (Aga Khan Award page, https://the.akdn/en/how-we-work/our-agencies/aga-khan-trust-culture/akaa/rehabilitation-old-city, SOURCED).
- **Maintenance cycle:** annual lime-wash on roofs; mud and lime render on roofs and terraces every 20 years; mud render on exterior walls every 40-50 years; periodic repainting and plumbing repair (Aga Khan, SOURCED). Residents could not repaint facades in 2020 because of lack of resources and the war (Al-Ain 2020, SOURCED). [K1] [R]
- Thermal behavior: a 40 cm mud wall delays heat transfer by about 15 hours vs. about 5 hours for a 20 cm cement wall (Holm Akhdar, citing Hussein 2018, SOURCED as reported).
- Builders: a 61-year-old builder who helped replace the city wall in 1985 is quoted; when outside funding stopped after late 2014, local builders lost income and could not pass on skills (Asia Times, Saeed Al-Batati, 2019-02-21, https://asiatimes.com/2019/02/yemens-manhattan-of-the-desert-at-risk-of-ruin/, SOURCED). A mud-building restoration association exists (its head is quoted in Al-Ain 2020, SOURCED).

**Economy, work and who lives there:** [K7] [K8] [P9]
- Residents work as farmers, government employees, or in manual labor including construction (Asia Times 2019, SOURCED).

Palm groves and gardens on three sides of the town (Al Bayan c. 2021, SOURCED). ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- Vertical, walled housing on a raised base kept the city out of floodwater and off the farmland (National Geographic 2017; Holm Akhdar; SOURCED), placed storage and animals at the bottom and family life above (Damluji, SOURCED), and gave defense against raids (Wikipedia, SOURCED). [P5]
- Mud construction makes maintenance a permanent duty (annual roof lime-wash; 20- and 40-50-year render cycles), so the city depends on master builders, materials from its own fields, and money; when funding stopped after 2014, upkeep lapsed and collapses followed (Aga Khan; Asia Times; Al-Ain; SOURCED). [K1] [K9] [K14] [R]
- Floodwater is both the material source (alluvial mud collected after floods) and the main threat (2008, 2020, 2026 seepage) (sources above). [K9]
- The small one-gate walled city concentrates heritage value and international funding (GTZ, UNESCO-EU, ALIPH, Prince Claus/Dawan), while the city's own office has six staff and no budget (Al Jazeera Net 2026, SOURCED). [K8]

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life culture)

- Agriculture (spate irrigation, palms, clover), government jobs and construction labor are the economic base; tourism collapsed after 2009 (sources above). [K7] [K8]
- Restoration programs are themselves an employer of builders and young people (Cash for Work; ALIPH: employment for local craftsmen; SOURCED). [K14]
- Social organization: the wider Hadhramaut has a historic status hierarchy (sada, mashayikh, tribesmen, and marginalized groups), on which a 1965 study of the village of Hureidah is the standard reference (UNVERIFIED: SOAS and Wikipedia snippets; Shibam-specific data NOT FOUND). [K4]
- **Speech (K11, loose note only):** Hadhrami Arabic is spoken in the Wadi (with urban varieties in Tarim, Seiyun and Shibam); valley and coastal varieties differ; vocabulary shows Southeast Asian and East African emigration influence (UNVERIFIED: Wikipedia and other snippets).

### Most useful finding per pick
1. **Tulou:** clan self-government inside one building: rotating gate, cleaning and festival duty by family branch, posted conduct rules, identical rooms for all, and each family owning a vertical slice (kitchen, granary, bedrooms); today governed by a 2011 provincial regulation with fire duties, banned acts and a tourism-revenue compensation rule.
2. **Fez el-Bali:** the gate "utility areas" where trucks hand goods to donkeys, mules, handcarts and the Zarzaya porters' corporation (internal rules, trainers, a weekly mutual-aid fund); the arrondissement population fell from 109,137 (1994) to 49,775 (2024) while buildings at risk peaked at 4,207 (2004).
3. **Matera's Sassi:** the full text of Law 619/1952 (vacate-by-deadline, sealing, one-month occupancy rule, no subletting) and the 1937 survey (2,997 dwellings, 1,646 rock-cut, about 13,000 people, about 4 per room); the vicinato's "traditional laws" and mutual surveillance.
4. **Shibam:** the floor-by-floor tower house (storage and animals below, men's floors, women's floors with kitchens, newlyweds on top) and the fixed maintenance cycle (annual roof lime-wash, 20- and 40-50-year renders), which lapsed when funding stopped after 2014; the gate was closed nightly until 1967.
