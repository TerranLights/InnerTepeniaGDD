### A. Other settings (neighborhoods, housing, workplaces, outdoor spaces)
- **Scotland Road (north end, Vauxhall/Everton):** laid out as a turnpike in the 1770s and partly widened in 1803, with working-class housing on both sides; the center of working-class life for Everton and Vauxhall; large Irish population, many having fled the Famine; associated with poor housing, poverty, violence and sectarian division; over 200 pubs once operated in the area, and by 2022 only the **Throstles Nest** (opened 1804, beside St Anthony's Church) remained; many dwellings demolished in the 1930s and replaced with corporation flats; after 1945 residents were rehoused in council estates at Croxteth, Halewood, Huyton, Kirkby, Norris Green and Stockbridge Village; building of the second Mersey road tunnel (Kingsway) cleared more housing; 2001 population 6,699. T. P. O'Connor, an Irish Nationalist, held the Liverpool Scotland seat for 44 years to 1929, the only constituency outside Ireland to vote continuously for an Irish nationalist. A democratic free school (Scotland Road Free School) ran from 1970. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland_Road. [K4, K9, K10, P3, P9]
- **The Granby "Four Streets" (Liverpool 8):** Beaconsfield, Cairns, Jermyn and Ducie Streets, built in the 1870s (designer Richard Owens), joined by Granby Street, which had about 100 shops in the 1950s; after the war one of the UK's earliest multicultural neighborhoods; designated a "twilight area" in the 1960s; Shelter's Neighborhood Action Project 1969–72 bought and renovated homes; after the 1981 riots shops closed and housing associations withdrew; the council's policy was demolition until only four streets remained; by the mid-2000s one street of 68 households had 8 occupied; in 2003–04 the council bought and boarded up most remaining houses and stopped street cleaning; in 2010 residents were pressed to sell for £8,000 each. Residents' responses: the Granby Residents Association (1993, disbanded about 2010); residents painted curtains on boarded windows and put out benches and plants; **Granby Four Streets Community Land Trust** formed November 2011; council "Homes for a Pound" scheme 2013 (2,500+ applicants); the CLT bought 13 Cairns Street houses for £1 each; the collective Assemble won the 2015 Turner Prize (£25,000) for the work, the first group to win it; **Granby Winter Garden** (two derelict houses made into an indoor garden and community space, Arts Council capital funding) opened March 2019. **Granby Market**: began about 2007 as a table sale in Cairns Street when only 8 households lived there; first Saturday of each month on Granby Street; up to 70 stalls by 2018. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granby_Four_Streets. [K1, K9, K10, K4, SM:man versus society]
- **The seafarer's home (wives and families).** An oral-history summary from LJMU's *Homeward Bound* project (Elder Dempster Lines, interview 2016-04-13) records an Elder Dempster master's working pattern on the West Africa run as **31 days at sea, 11 days at home, 2 days of home leave spent in the office**; wives were not allowed to sail on the mail ship *Aureol* but could go on cargo voyages (she made three); she wrote letters daily so one waited at each port; the daughter sent drawings; when the husband was home "life continued as normal"; she worked (St John's Hospice) and described herself as self-sufficient; there was "little contact with the company" while he was away; she likened the master's role to the loneliness of "a parish priest"; retired officers kept up contact by playing bridge and going to luncheons. SOURCED: LJMU, Homeward Bound interview summary, Marie Therese O'Sullivan (PDF, read via text extraction). An LJMU 2025 article studies the Royal Liverpool Seamen's Orphan Institution (formally opened 1874) as a case of the "maritime family" (UNVERIFIED, search listing). [K7, K4, P4]
- **Dock workforce shrinkage (the workplace that emptied):** registered dock workers in the Liverpool and Birkenhead scheme fell from **10,449 in 1970 to 1,244 in April 1989**; across all scheme ports, 71,846 (1959) to 9,319 (May 1989). SOURCED: Hansard, written answers, HC Deb 16 May 1989 (api.parliament.uk). Total decasualization of dock work came in September 1967 (UNVERIFIED, thesis snippets; Greig Taylor, MMU thesis on Liverpool dock labor 1967–89 located, not read). [K7, K9]
- **Seafarers ashore today:** the Liverpool Seafarers Center and the cruise terminal jointly run a **Crew Welfare Hub** at the cruise terminal (first set up in 2018 in a converted shipping container); free wifi, refreshments, local information, a parcel service and ship-welfare visitors; summer 2024: 97 vessels and 55,575 crew, with 20 volunteers. UNVERIFIED (snippets: Church Times 2024-11-01, which refused fetching; cruise-terminal report; crew-center.com, which redirected). [K1, K10, R]

### B. Subcultures and communities (how each lives, its own spaces)
- **Irish Catholic and Orange Protestant Liverpool.** Orange lodges spread to Liverpool in the early 19th century; the first Liverpool parade was in 1819; 1909 sectarian rioting (an inquiry witness claimed 3,200 Catholics fled Everton). Today: on **12 July** marches start at the Orange Lodge on Mill Street (the Dingle) and at the lodge on Everton Park, converge in the city center, then go by coach to Southport; described as the only remaining 12 July procession on the British mainland; flute bands, drummers, banners, uniforms; several generations of the same families; membership has fallen sharply; members may not marry Catholics (members have been expelled for doing so, per historian Mervyn Busteed); the Order runs socials, family events and charity; the Provincial Grand Orange Lodge occupies a pub-style building at the top of Everton Brow; a 2017 Apprentice Boys parade passing the Liffey pub on Renshaw Street saw violence, condemned by the lodge. SOURCED: *The Post* (Liverpool), "The strange life of Loyal Liverpool," 2024-07-13. In 1876 7,000–8,000 Orangemen marched, the largest Orange procession in English history; marches moved out to the countryside after 1852; 1960s slum clearance moved Everton's lodges away with their members. UNVERIFIED (snippets, isismagazine/livpost). The popular idea that Everton FC is the Catholic club and Liverpool FC the Protestant one (Liverpool's founder John Houlding was an Orangeman) is disputed by researchers, who find little evidence of a faith link in support. UNVERIFIED (Irish Times and fan-history snippets). [K4, K2, K9, K6]
- **Black Liverpool's own institutions.** The community is described as Britain's oldest Black community, some families tracing Black heritage back about ten generations to the 1730s; mostly settled in Toxteth; three nights of racial violence 31 July – 2 August 1948; 2011 census 2.8% of the city Black; the 1981 riots left one man dead, 500 arrested, 70 buildings destroyed, about £11m damage. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people_in_Liverpool. Riots: trigger the arrest of Leroy Cooper on 3 July 1981; peak 5–6 July; long-standing tension with police, the "sus" stop-and-search law, CS gas used on the British mainland for the first time; aftermath the Scarman Report (November 1981), the Merseyside Task Force, enterprise zones. SOURCED: ourhistory.org.uk (2011-08-27). Institutions: the **Charles Wootton Center** (adult education, 1974, named after the Bermudian ex-sailor drowned by a mob in 1919; Black history courses as well as maths and English); **Elimu Wa Nane** (after-school activities, 1979, in the Methodist Center); the **Liverpool 8 Law Center** (1982, 34–36 Princes Road, set up after 1981); the Kuumba Imani Millennium Center (2004); Liverpool Black Sisters. UNVERIFIED (writingonthewall.org.uk and other snippets). [K1, K9, K4, P9]
- **Liverpool-Yemenis.** Early records put the first Yemenis in Liverpool in the late 19th century, as merchant seafarers, but few early arrivals settled there; many later came from Sheffield's steel industry after the 1970s–80s redundancies, attracted by cheap property and a gap in the market: South Asian families had not taken up newsagents and grocers in Liverpool, so Yemenis did; the community is in its third and possibly fourth generation, centered in Toxteth (L8), keeping ties through trips to Yemen and community organizations; one young participant described a double Arab and Liverpool identity. SOURCED: David Harrison, "Liverpool Yemenis: a sketch of a hidden community," University of Leeds *Religion in Public* blog, 2018-03-27. About 400 Yemeni-owned newsagents (UNVERIFIED, Wikipedia snippet). Al-Rahma (Liverpool Central) Mosque on Hatherley Street, Toxteth (UNVERIFIED). **Liverpool Arab Arts Festival**: founded 1998 by Liverpool Arabic Center and Bluecoat; first festival 2002 and yearly since; partners include Unity Theater, FACT, National Museums Liverpool, Liverpool Philharmonic; funded by Liverpool City Council and Arts Council England; Arab British Center Culture & Society Award 2010. SOURCED: arabartsfestival.com/about/history. Bluecoat's archive says the partnership was with the Liverpool Yemeni Arabic Club and grew from a first "Arabic weekender" in August 1999 (UNVERIFIED, Bluecoat archive snippet). [K3, K4, K8, P9]
- **Somali seamen:** Somali seamen settled in Cardiff, Liverpool and east London and joined the National Union of Seamen (UNVERIFIED, snippet); a Heritage Fund project records Horn of Africa people living in Liverpool (UNVERIFIED). Liverpool-specific Somali history beyond Round 1's Somali Club: NOT FOUND.
- **Chinese Liverpool today:** the **Chinatown arch** (paifang) on Nelson Street was planned from 1992 under a city regeneration scheme, its design chosen by the local Chinese community; parts made in Shanghai and erected by Shanghai craftsmen for Chinese New Year 2000; about 13.5 m high; wartime bombing had moved the quarter from Pitt Street inland to Nelson Street. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia and guide snippets; sources differ on build dates). **Wah Sing Chinese Community Center** (registered charity 513857, 149 Duke Street; education and relief of poverty) and **Pine Court** housing association (1 Nelson Street; said to date from 1986; multilingual housing officers). UNVERIFIED (Charity Commission listing snippet; the register page refused fetching). [K1, K4]
- **The bohemian art-school circle (Liverpool 8, 1950s–60s):** **Ye Cracke** (Rice Street, off Hope Street), a 19th-century pub where Lennon and Stuart Sutcliffe drank as students of the nearby Liverpool College of Art. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ye_Cracke. Writers describe the area round the art school as the cheap place for painters, poets and musicians, with Ye Cracke as their most regular haunt; Lennon, Sutcliffe, Rod Murray and Bill Harry formed a student group there; the "Liverpool poets" (Roger McGough, Brian Patten, Adrian Henri, a College of Art lecturer) published *The Mersey Sound* in 1967; Henri said the area was "murdered by planners" (1971). UNVERIFIED (snippets). [K13, K10]
- **Folk revival and sea songs.** **The Spinners** (formed September 1958 from the Gin Mill Skiffle Group; Tony Davis, Mick Groves, Hughie Jones and Cliff Hall, who brought traditional Jamaican songs) began with American skiffle, then added sea shanties and English folk songs after a seaman, Redd Sullivan, suggested it; they founded Liverpool's **Triton Club** folk club; BBC1 series 1970–77; retired 1988; last concerts as "Spinners Legends" 2–3 October 2021 at the Philharmonic Hall. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spinners_(English_band). The club opened in a basement in September or October 1958 and is called Liverpool's first folk club; Merseyside once had 28 folk clubs. UNVERIFIED (Telegraph obituary via pressreader, not fetched; sixtiescity.net; Liverpool Philharmonic snippet). [K13, K14]
- **K11 (speech, loose):** English in the Liverpool regional dialect; Irish, Arabic (Yemeni), Somali and Chinese (Cantonese) communities present. Nothing further recorded.

### C. Leisure industries (how free time was organized and sold)
- **Dance halls:** the **Grafton** (West Derby Road) opened 9 February 1924 as a purpose-built hall for 1,200 dancers, beside the Locarno (now the Liverpool Olympia, under the same ownership); bands from Joe Loss and Victor Silvester to the Beatles; closed as a dance venue September 2008; facade partly collapsed 2013-01-27; demolition and 80 apartments approved March 2025. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grafton_Ballroom. A memoir blog says jive was not allowed in Liverpool dance halls in the 1950s (UNVERIFIED, anecdotal). [K10, K6, K9]
- **Across the river:** the **Tower Ballroom**, New Brighton (beneath a tower opened 1900 and dismantled by 1921) hosted the Beatles 27 times, last on 14 June 1963; destroyed by fire 1969; New Brighton ferries ended 1971. The ferry *Royal Iris* hosted the Cavern's riverboat concerts in the 1960s; Mersey Ferries still run Merseybeat tribute cruises. UNVERIFIED (Wirral map PDF, Beatles Bible, Mersey Ferries listing snippets). [K10, K3]
- **Football terraces as mass leisure:** the Kop at Anfield once held about 27,000 standing people; 1964 TV footage shows about 28,000 singing together, songs including Beatles and Cilla Black chart hits ("performances of pride in the city's musicians"), a crowd that appears entirely male, swaying only possible on terraces; Gerry and the Pacemakers' recording of a *Carousel* show tune went to No. 1 in 1963 and became the club's anthem after a copy was given to manager Bill Shankly, and it is central to the annual Hillsborough commemorations; an LP of the crowd alone was released in 1972; the last standing game on the Kop was 30 April 1994; since all-seater stadiums the crowd is more affluent and ticket prices are up about 300%, with locals increasingly excluded and a gray economy of touting. SOURCED: Martin Cloonan, "Songs to Learn and Sing: Understanding the Use of Singing at Liverpool FC Football Matches," *Lähikuva* 3–4/2023, pp. 109–128 (PDF, read via text extraction). Lyrics are not recorded here. [K10, K13, K9, SM:man versus society]
- **Social clubs and cabaret:** a typical clubland night was bingo, raffles, live music and a star turn, with 1960s investment behind a 1970s boom (national accounts); Liverpool venues such as the Shakespeare Club turned to cabaret in the early 1960s. UNVERIFIED (snippets). Liverpool-specific working men's club lists: NOT FOUND (one Liverpool Echo feature refused, see log). [K10, K8]
- **Heritage tourism events:** **International Beatleweek** is organized by Cavern City Tours; bands from over 20 countries, fans from over 40; Event/Festival of the Year at the Liverpool City Region Tourism Awards, March 2026. SOURCED: cavernclub.com/beatleweek. Its origin in a 1977 Beatles Convention at Mr Pickwick's, Fraser Street, and the convention's move to the Adelphi Hotel in 1981 under Liz and Jim Hughes. ### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Irish:** the **Liverpool Irish Festival** is an annual ten-day festival in October and custodian of the **Liverpool Irish Famine Trail** and memorial; the memorial (St Luke's Church gardens) was designed by Eamonn O'Doherty and commissioned by the Great Famine Commemoration Committee of Liverpool, about 1998; the trail, created in the 1990s with a volunteer history research group, was revived by the festival from 2021; in October 2024 a bronze-shoes sculpture was walked from Clarence Dock, where about 1.3 million Irish migrants arrived during the Famine, to the memorial. - **Orange 12 July** (B above). **Liverpool Arab Arts Festival** (B above).
- **Food (K12):** scouse is a stew of usually beef (sometimes lamb), potatoes, carrots and onions; the name is a shortening of lobscouse, a sailors' dish of meat and ship's biscuit ("a dish much eaten at sea," 1785); potato-based lobscouse was a regional dish by the late 18th century; a meatless version is recorded for people who could not afford meat; served with pickled red cabbage or beetroot and bread; since 2000 bars, cafés and restaurants put scouse on the menu once a year to raise money for charity (Global Scouse Day; calendar date not given on the page read). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouse_(food). [K12, K2]

SOURCED: University of Liverpool news, 2009-07-10. This closes a Round 1 NOT FOUND. A BA in popular music followed in 1995 (UNVERIFIED). [K14, K13]
- **K6, everyday rules (new in Round 2):**
  1. **Victorian licensing as social control:** licenses were renewed yearly, letting magistrates threaten cancellation; magistrates limited the number of pub doors and when they could be used so staff, police and customers could see women drinkers; snugs were criticized as places where immorality went unseen; pubs clustered where low-paid dockers and casual laborers (many Irish) lived; from 1872 publicans had to limit the time reputed prostitutes spent in pubs, and in 1909 one license was renewed on condition that such women's drinking time be limited to four minutes; in some streets one pub per 13 people was estimated. SOURCED: University of Cambridge research feature on David Beckingham, *The Licensed City: Regulating Drink in Liverpool, 1830–1920*, 2017-07-04.
  2. **National hours from 1964:** the Licensing Act 1964 set general weekday hours ending at 10:30 p.m. with a two-and-a-half-hour afternoon break from 3 p.m.; local justices could vary hours within limits. UNVERIFIED (legislation.gov.uk PDF snippet).

### A. Other settings
- **St. Pauli as a residential district:** about 22,500 residents (2016 profile); about 69% single-person households; households with children 12.8% (Hamburg-Mitte 16.8%); 22.3% foreign nationals and 36.3% with a migration background (2015); about 15.0% receiving basic income support (SGB II) against 9.9% citywide; unemployment 8.1% of those aged 15–64 against 5.6% citywide. UNVERIFIED (search summary of Statistikamt Nord district profiles 2015/2016; the 2016 PDF returned 404 on fetching). [P4, P9]
- **The harbor edge and its quarters.** The **Landungsbrücken** (first landing stage 1839) were a working place of goods handling (a 1932 photograph shows a freight business with wheelbarrows and sacked goods) and turned mainly touristic from about the 1950s, as the resident fish and boatmen's trades gave way to excursion restaurants and souvenir stalls; a fish-roll stall owner told a researcher in 2011 that nearly all her customers were tourists. SOURCED: Svea Scheel, "In Hamburg isst man Fisch?! Konstruktion von Raum und Regionalität: Fischbrötchen an den Landungsbrücken," *vokus* 2010/2 (University of Hamburg; PDF read via text extraction). The **Portugiesenviertel** (southern Neustadt, center on Ditmar-Koel-Straße, between the Landungsbrücken and the Michel) was already marked by restaurants and harbor pubs in the 1950s–60s and today has about 40 food businesses, busiest in summer. ### B. Subcultures
- **The FC St. Pauli fan scene.** In the mid-1980s the club was in sporting and financial trouble (promotion to the 2. Bundesliga 1986; 3,000–4,000 spectators at the run-down Millerntor); squatters from the Hafenstraße and their sympathizers began to attend, drawn by the atmosphere and the absence of violence and neo-Nazis, and right-leaning fans were pushed out; a squatter brought the pirate skull flag into the stadium; the fanzine *Millerntor Roar!* ran 1989–93; the **Fanladen St. Pauli** was founded by fans in 1989/90, independent of the club, later a fan project under the 1993 national sport-and-security concept; in 1989 fans stopped a planned "Sport-Dome" with a shopping center, described as the first time German fans opposed their own club's leadership; this inspired nationwide fan associations (BAFF, Pro 15:30); in 2003 a "Retter" shirt campaign sold about 130,000 shirts for a net €1,149,000; members' meetings banned selling the stadium name and banned striptease dancers in a stadium box, citing the stadium rules against sexism. ### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **The 1896–97 dockworkers' strike (the root of port labor institutions):** began 21 November 1896 and lasted about eleven weeks, nearly 17,000 strikers at the peak (16,960 receiving strike pay in late January 1897); dock work was casual, with men hired in port taverns, which were "the actual centers of employment," by intermediaries (Baase and their foremen); demands: higher wages, shorter hours, collective agreements, abolition of the intermediaries; trigger: a planned cut in bonuses for handling hazardous goods; about 2,000 strikebreakers by 7 December, about 1,000 Italians refused entry; police occupied the port from 26 November; the Senate banned door-to-door collections for strikers on 14 December and imposed a minor state of siege on the port in mid-January; strike pay began at 8 marks a week; small shopkeepers and peddlers backed the strikers; ended about 6 February 1897 with defeat (66% vote), followed by clashes in the Neustadt with about 150 injured. Consequences: wages were thereafter paid in wage offices rather than pubs, a central employment agency and a port inspector were created, ferry fares were cut, collective agreements covered the whole port by 1913, and the Senate announced redevelopment of the Gängeviertel slums. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1896–97_Hamburg_dockworkers'_strike. [K1, K7, K9, K6, SM:man versus society]

### A. Other settings
- **The Battery** (on the narrow harbor entrance below Signal Hill): first settled for the fishery; once called Crow's Nest Cove, renamed for the gun batteries installed from the 17th century against the French; stayed a fishing community through the wars; the cod moratorium left fishing stages abandoned, and very little fishing now goes on there, though a few residents (named on the page: fisherman Jack Wells, who still salts and dries fish on flakes; Charlie Pearcey's family twine store) keep it up; later the area went from "challenging to live in" to sought after, with soaring property prices and large residential developments, and some residents work to preserve its vernacular buildings. SOURCED: Memorial University Intangible Cultural Heritage inventory, "The Battery" and "The Battery – Fishing" (mun.ca/ich, updated 2022-07-27). Artists and intellectuals came in the 1970s for the setting and cheap housing; the city has studied rockslides and avalanches there since 1995. UNVERIFIED (MUN folklore exhibit snippet). [K9, K14, P3]
- **Quidi Vidi Village** (a small-boat harbor east of the city): West Country migratory men established fishing rooms and went home each fall, a handful overwintering to guard the rooms and cut timber; settlement into families took over 150 years after about 1650; as late as 1835 migratory men from southwest England and southeast Ireland still fished there; 1680: fifteen shallops and 84 men, almost all by-boat keepers; 1751: eight wharves, ten flakes, about fourteen buildings; a fishing room held wharf, store, flake, dwelling or cook room, a stable and root cellar; Christ Church (1842, architect James Purcell) was given up by the Anglican Church in the 1960s and later converted to a home by a heritage carpenter; in one family house the kitchen is "the most used room," for card games, music and family gatherings, with a chair kept by the stove for visitors. SOURCED: Memorial University Department of Folklore, *The 2013 Quidi Vidi Field School* booklet (2014; PDF read via text extraction). Quidi Vidi Brewery (1996) occupies the former village fish plant; Mallard Cottage (built 1820–40) has been a restaurant since 2013; an artisan-studio incubator operates there. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia and Parks Canada snippets). [K9, K10, K7, P3]
- **Schools under the churches (K1, K6):** from 1949 Term 17 of the Terms of Union guaranteed a publicly funded school system run by denominations; no non-denominational public system existed; by the 1990s there were four systems (Integrated, Pentecostal, Roman Catholic, Seventh-day Adventist), 27 denominational boards, about 550 schools and 140,000 students; denominations controlled trustee appointments, school locations and teacher hiring; a 5 September 1995 referendum (55% yes) gave the legislature more power; a 2 September 1997 referendum (72.7% yes; 149,315 to 56,020) replaced the system with a single one, with non-denominational religion courses, observances where parents request them, and the right to withdraw. SOURCED: Government of Newfoundland and Labrador, "Education Reform in Newfoundland and Labrador: Term 17" brief (releases.gov.nl.ca, 1997; a government advocacy text). [K1, K6, K4, P7]
- **Weather as a closure of the city (K5):** on 17 January 2020 a blizzard dropped more than 76 cm of snow; the city declared a state of emergency, ordered all businesses closed and told residents to stay home; it lasted more than a week and was lifted at 6 a.m. on 25 January; the military helped clear snow; free transit ran to 7 February to keep cars off the roads. ### A. Other settings
- **Neighborhoods and who lives where:** the colonial center is ringed by relatively well-off residential areas; to the north, large and sometimes luxurious homes; **Monte** and **Alto de São Nicolau** are affluent, with many returned emigrants; **Chã de Monte Sossego** is mostly apartment blocks of civil servants and young families; farther out (**Espia, Ribeirinha, Lombo de Tanque**) modest brick houses stand beside emigrants' villas and very precarious dwellings; **Alto Solarino, Fonte Filipe, Bela Vista** and **Monte Sossego** are described as among the poorest; the city's population doubled between 1960 and 1980 and again by 2005; remittances from emigrants in Europe and the United States improved living conditions from 1968; schools include Escola Camões (1880) and the old liceu (now Escola Jorge Barbosa), long the only liceu in the northern islands, attended by Amílcar Cabral and Pedro Pires; the hospital's first stone was laid in 1880; Porto Grande's quays were modernized by December 1997. SOURCED: pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindelo_(Cabo_Verde). **Ribeira Bote** had 3,956 residents in 2010 and holds the old hospital and the church of Nossa Senhora Auxiliadora (UNVERIFIED, Wikipedia snippet). [P3, P4, P9, K4]
- **Self-built hillside housing:** families who cannot pay rent build houses of zinc sheets cut from drums, wood, some stone and a cement base, often meant as temporary but becoming near-permanent; residents mark plots on steep slopes themselves and build first, then seek legalization from the municipality (one family bought dug-out land from neighbors and then bought the plot from the council); sanitation has not reached these areas, people dig pits and buy water from neighbors; mud enters houses in the rainy season; the council gave one family aid for a wall against flash floods; preliminary 2021 census: São Vicente had 23,976 buildings, 68.5% complete, and 7.3% "non-classic" (shacks or drum-sheet houses), the highest share in the country; nationally such buildings rose 85.7% since 2010 (1,603 to 2,977). SOURCED: RTP, "Sonho de casa própria ainda se faz com bidão e 'tambor' em Cabo Verde" (undated, 2021). Neighborhoods named: Ribeirinha and Espia. [K9, K5, K14, R]
- **Upgrading by residents:** the **Outros Bairros** program (architect Nuno Flores, for the Ministry of Infrastructure, Spatial Planning and Housing; grew out of a design initiative at a local arts institute) rebuilt public space, drainage, electricity, water and sanitation in **Alto de Bomba**; residents were trained and built with local quarried stone; over two years it gave work experience to 25 architects and about 60 residents and reached 171 households and 1,500 people; completed 2020; shortlisted for the 2022 Aga Khan Award; it aims to make informality a recognized method of urbanization in national policy. SOURCED: e-architect.com. [K1, K14, R]
- **Water (Round 1's NOT FOUND, closed in part):** in 1879 water was mostly imported from Tarrafal on Santo Antão or drawn from 13 public and 22 private wells; in 1886 spring water from Madeiral was piped into the city; about five years later a city water company built a reservoir at Largo do Madeiral; today the desalination plant averages 2,900 m³ a day and covers about 65% of the island; supply is by the private company Electra. SOURCED: pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindelo_(Cabo_Verde). Mindelo pioneered desalination in Cape Verde; the first plant stood at **Matiota** (its two boilers later sold as scrap); residents recalled households keeping two water supplies, one for washing and one for drinking and cooking, women carrying water in cans of about 25 liters, women queuing for water at Vascónia and Madeiral, piped water later topped up by a tanker-filled 3,000-liter tank, small boats bringing water from Tarrafal through a pipe from Miller's wharf, and showers limited to three a day in one household. SOURCED (as residents' recollections in a blog): Joaquim Saial and Zeca Soares, *Praia de Bote* blog, 2012-08-13, with reader comments. A desalination agency (JAIDA) was formed in 1969, with the plant and network built 1969–71; Electra formed 17 April 1982 (UNVERIFIED, snippets). [K1, K5, R]
- **The fishing villages:** **São Pedro** (southwest coast) and **Salamansa** (about 1,170 people) are artisanal fishing villages; with Calhau and Mindelo they make up the island's four fishing communities; Salamansa's fishermen's association ran an underwater ecotourism trail with GEF small-grants support that later stopped. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia, GEF snippets). [K7, K14]
- **The August 2025 floods (K5, K9):** on the night of 10–11 August 2025, rain linked to Tropical Storm Erin dropped 192.3 mm on São Vicente in about five hours, far above the island's yearly average; at least nine died; about 1,500 people were forced from their homes; residents had no warning (the government said it lacked forecasting equipment); a state of emergency was declared; roundabouts and areas were destroyed, streets split, houses filled with mud and the main city market largely destroyed; a private chef cooked meals for the displaced; a party-organizing group turned into an emergency volunteer group, bought mattresses and pillows and ran a crowdfunding page; well-known artists dug mud from houses with shovels; the diaspora sent aid. SOURCED: Dennis Ade Peter, OkayAfrica, 2025-08-20. Over 2,500 buildings damaged, five bridges collapsed, the central hospital and health centers flooded, water and sanitation networks failed; national mourning; deaths reported up to 12 (UNVERIFIED, ReliefWeb/WHO/Renascença snippets; ifrc.org refused). ### B. Subcultures
- **Women traders (transnational and street):** in Mindelo "everyone trades," from street fish-sellers to well-off people returning from Europe with full suitcases; in one national list of occupations, those in trade numbered 6,822 and women outnumbered men four to one (2010 census); fish sellers depend on brothers, partners and sons who fish; produce comes mainly from Santo Antão because São Vicente is too dry for much farming; most goods in boutiques and kiosks are brought by transnational women traders from Portugal, the United States, Italy and Brazil, or sent by emigrant relatives in barrels; barrels arrive monthly at Porto Grande, and traders pack the costlier goods at the bottom to avoid high duties; Chinese goods imported by Chinese traders are gaining ground by price; shops and markets close on Sundays, when goods from the barrels are sold at house doors; older women with straw baskets sell sweets and cigarettes in the streets, more on festival days and at carnival; women who travel to import prefer to be called importers or traders, distinguishing themselves from door-to-door and street sellers, some of whom come from Santo Antão and Santiago; poorer sellers display food and second-hand clothes on the street, better-off ones rent space; continental Africans sell sunglasses, phone covers and other manufactured goods. SOURCED: Vinícius Venancio, "Rabida bô: retratos das práticas comerciais em Mindelo, Cabo Verde," *R@U* 11(1), 2019, pp. 683–695 (fieldwork January–March 2017; PDF read via text extraction). The Creole term for these traders and its meaning are recorded in the slang file only. [K4, K5, K8, K6, P9]
- **Fish sellers organized:** the **Associação Peixeiras do Mindelo** (APM) proposed a National Fishmongers' Day, officially established by the government in 2024; on Saturday 17 May 2025 it held an exchange among São Vicente's four fishing communities at the Calhau community space, calling it a "dignified job, with a woman's face" and demanding better conditions and recognition. SOURCED: Inforpress, 2025-05-16. Wholesale fish sellers buy more and have more capital than retail sellers (UNVERIFIED, University of the Algarve study snippet; the repository page was behind a bot check). [K1, K14, K7]

### A. Other settings
- **The September 1922 arrival (Round 1's NOT FOUND, closed):** between 1 and 30 September 1922, 35 steamships and one ocean liner carried more than 40,000 refugees from the Asia Minor coast to Piraeus and Keratsini bay; some voyages lasted 12 days in ship holds with little food or water; on 4 September over 8,000 came on four Italian and one English ship, and two or three ships at Keratsini were not allowed to land; on 5 September ships were radioed not to approach Piraeus for health reasons; Piraeus (131,170 in the 1920 census) grew by about a third in a month. **First shelters:** quayside sheds and warehouses, the Karaiskaki square sheds, railway wagons, the Tzelepi shore, church and school courtyards, squares; requisitioned buildings included the Municipal, Olympia, National and Royal theaters and even gambling clubs; on 12 September the vegetable market was fitted out for 1,000 people. **Response:** fundraising drives, communal kitchens, a 30-drachma allowance per refugee, ten employment offices from 9 September (one in Piraeus) that placed 1,028 men and 1,095 women in jobs between 27 September and 31 December 1922; newspapers ran "refugee columns" where refugees advertised skills; on 15 September requisition of property for housing was authorized and on 30 September Apostolos Doxiadis was given emergency powers to house refugees within eight days; employers needed a certificate of good character to hire refugees as domestic workers; forced sharing of requisitioned homes was the biggest disruption for locals. SOURCED: Eleni Kyramargiou, "Ο Πειραιάς των προσφύγων," *Efimerida ton Syntakton*, 2016-06-12. [K1, K4, K5, K9, P9]
- **Drapetsona's Pontic settlement:** Pontic refugees had settled from 1919–21 on empty land beside the chemical-and-fertilizer factory and the Heracles cement works; by 1922 the shack-and-tent settlement was overcrowded, with no plan, roads, sewers, light or water; men and many women worked as factory laborers for 10 drachmas a day; three local associations merged in 1923 into the Union of Pontians of Piraeus, which in 1924 obtained 160 wooden shacks for 160 families (against about 5,000 families in need); with no school, the association rented a building and hired refugee teachers, some of whom had been laborers, for a night school, later taken over by the Ministry of Education; in 1926 the municipality set up local offices in Drapetsona, Kokkinia and Tambouria. SOURCED: pontosnews.gr, 2018-06-11 (memoir text of Dimitrios Misailidis, from a 2016 book by the Union of Pontians of Piraeus–Keratsini–Drapetsona). [K1, K4, K14, P9]
- **The fertilizer plant and its afterlife:** the plant (AEECHPL) began operating in October 1910 and closed for good in 1999 after liquidation in 1993; a 2015 law zoned the 640-stremma site for green space, recreation, culture, education and health and barred housing; in July 2016 Parliament transferred the port zone from the Piraeus Port Authority to the municipality; after residents' long demands the municipality built an open-air theater, three football pitches and a seaside promenade on about a third of the site. SOURCED: parapolitika.gr, 2019-08-18. The park is named "Park of Labor" (Πάρκο Εργατιάς) (UNVERIFIED, efsyn snippet). [K9, K1, K10]
- **Perama's ship-repair zone:** refugees from Constantinople and Pontus settled Perama in the early 1920s; the first shipyards opened in 1928 and life has been tied to ship repair since; most residents work in shipyards, boatyards and slipways; repairs can take up to five months, starting with hauling the ship out, then hydroblasting, painting and plate work; holds have little oxygen, toxic fumes from burning old paint and extreme welding heat; until 2009 the zone employed more than 5,000 skilled workers, and by 2016 local unemployment was put at about 90% as ships went to Asia or Turkey; young people stay away because of hard conditions, accidents and unstable work; an explosion on the tanker *Anangel Greatness* (1987) killed 16, and one in the hold of the *Frentsip Gaz* (July 2008) killed eight. SOURCED: Vasilis Tsiolis, "Μια μέρα στο Πέραμα," *To Vima* (BHMAgazino), 2016-04-08. The free ship-repair zone and an improvised settlement of internal migrants date from the 1960s; a fatal crane accident in May 2023 led to 24-hour strikes by the metalworkers' union. UNVERIFIED (snippets; the helios.eie.gr history PDF exceeded the size limit). [K7, K9, K14, SM:competence shown under pressure, R]

### B. Subcultures
- **Refugee associations in Nea Kokkinia (Nikaia):** of 150 organizations identified in the interwar settlement, 17 were tied to refugees' places of origin; examples: Refugee Union of Nea Kokkinia (1924), Progressive Youth (1925), Association of Merchants and Shopkeepers (1928), building cooperative "Kronos" (1928); they supported widows and orphans, helped find work (including for women in carpet weaving), collected housing installments and negotiated prices with the settlement commission, ran a night school and a night commercial school, restored a temporary church holding icons and relics from the lost homelands, organized music, theater, excursions, sports and dances, issued refugee-status certificates used as identity documents (until a Metaxas-era ban), backed compensation claims under the motto "what we had – what we lost – what we claim," placed newspaper ads to find missing relatives, and lobbied for fountains and street cleaning; leaders entered local government; rivalries fragmented the movement; a Bithynian union helped rename the area Nikaia. - **The port's nightlife economy, as a researcher of it describes it:** records from about 1840 show brothels along the Troumba waterfront; authorities later moved them to the walled Vourla block in Drapetsona, the first state-regulated brothels, with the first venereal-disease hospital inside; Vourla became a prison under the German occupation; a 1956 law ended the madam system; when the US Sixth Fleet anchored off Faliro, touts who spoke some English met sailors at the stone clock tower and led them to Troumba, where smuggled transistor radios, lighters and cigarettes were sold to them; cabarets (Maxim, Brazil, Tonis) were mostly on Filonos Street; the 1967 closure was ordered with a six-month deadline; Troumba was also residential, filled by day with shipping firms and workers; Asia Minor people ran eateries where hashish was also traded; Piraeus had "unwritten rules" that kept its mixed population's coexistence stable; locals long treated the subject as a "miasma" omitted from local histories; the researcher argues rebetiko was not "hashish music." ### A. Other settings
- **La Huaca (the barrio outside the walls):** in the 17th century a hut settlement beside a paupers' cemetery, home to about 500 enslaved Black and mulatto people, who built homes along the Tenoya river with timber from shipwrecks; after the city's fortifications were completed in 1790 it became a neighborhood of fishermen, laborers, porters, artisans and musicians; its 19th-century wooden **patios de vecindad** (tenement yards) include three recognized by INAH (San Nicolás, San Salvador and La Favorita, the last considered among the oldest in Latin America, with wooden units of 3.15 × 7 m built for laborers and enslaved people); a local man pioneered carnival comparsas in the 1930s; musicians linked to the barrio include Toña "La Negra" and Agustín Lara; the **Asociación de Vecinos del Barrio de La Huaca** has had a cooperation agreement with the Universidad Veracruzana since 2005 to protect its heritage (a tourist corridor on Doblado Street, restoring the yards, a traditional food venue, a social hall and an on-site museum; a university urban brigade has worked there since 2007). SOURCED: Fernanda Melchor, *UniVerso* (Universidad Veracruzana) no. 305, 2008-04-14. The World Monuments Fund dates the barrio to 1870 as housing for migrant workers outside the walls (UNVERIFIED, conflicting snippet); residents face insecurity, precarious housing and eviction threats (UNVERIFIED, UNAM thesis snippet). [K4, K9, K1, K13, P3, P9]
- **The emptying historic center:** the center underwent nearly 20 years of rapid depopulation, ruinous buildings and abandoned heritage; a Universidad Veracruzana study identified about 200 vacant buildings, most in the core perimeter; academics propose housing as the means of rehabilitation. UNVERIFIED (UNAM thesis and UV press snippets). [K9, P3]
- **Dock labor after 1991:** the federal takeover ("requisa") of the port under President Salinas dismissed the unionized dockers and was the first step toward a port administration company (APIVER) and the model for privatizing Mexico's port system; former maneuver-workers and stevedores long sought pensions (a presidential promise covered 518). UNVERIFIED (UNAM thesis and press snippets). A new terminal in the northern expansion zone (Grupo CICE) foresees about 500 direct and 1,200 indirect jobs (UNVERIFIED, Expansión snippet). Total port employment: still NOT FOUND (press figures are operators' projections). [K7, K9, K1]
- **The Hidalgo market and the 2002 fire:** on 31 December 2002 an explosion at a fireworks stall started a fire that killed 30 people shopping for year-end purchases at the market; afterward fireworks sales were banned in the municipality, and authorities run operations against fireworks at the market every year; no ceremonies mark the event, but a cross stands at the corner of Juan Soto and Hidalgo streets. SOURCED: N+ (nmas.com.mx), "Se cumplen 21 años de la tragedia en el Mercado Hidalgo" (2023). The market (built 1960; the Unidad Veracruzana market 1946) sells fish and seafood, regional food and herbs (UNVERIFIED, UV journal and tourism snippets). [K9, K6, K10, K12]
- **The 2011 violence:** in 2011 five journalists were killed in the state and twelve reporters fled, others no longer signing articles with their real names; on 20 September 35 bodies were dumped in Boca del Río; two weeks later federal forces took over security under "Veracruz Seguro" and 850 municipal police were dismissed; local press counted about 350 organized-crime killings in the state that year; the US issued a travel alert; in the port, trash went uncollected (274,000 tons). SOURCED: Gardenia Mendoza, *El Diario NY*, 2011-12-27. [K9, SM:man versus society]

### D. Third places

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **La Rama (16–24 December):** for eight nights, groups of children and adults carry decorated branches (colored paper, tinsel, balloons, LED lights) from house to house, singing to guitars and tambourines; songs praise the host's generosity, joke about stingy previous stops and the Nativity, and ask for a gift (candy, coins); the farewell song depends on whether a gift is given; one account traces it to colonial missionaries using the evergreen branch, with processions led by Nahua people, turning playful and competitive between neighborhoods in the 19th century and a neighborhood festival in the 20th. SOURCED: Stephen Randall, *Mexico News Daily*, 2024-12-24. Its origin (Indigenous, African, Spanish or Caribbean) is debated in other sources (UNVERIFIED). Lyrics not recorded. [K2, K4, K6]
- **21 April (the 1914 defense):** at 11:20 a.m. on 21 April 1914 US Marines began landing to seize the customs house over an arms shipment for the Huerta government; Commodore Manuel Azueta rallied cadets at the naval school; cadet Virgilio Uribe was shot dead loading his weapon; Lieutenant José Azueta kept firing a machine gun at the corner of Landero y Coss and Esteban Morales despite wounds and died on 10 May 1914; US forces left on 23 November 1914; the naval school was declared "Heroic" by presidential decree of 20 December 1949. SOURCED: Secretaría de Marina, "Reseña histórica del 21 de abril de 1914" (gob.mx PDF, read via text extraction). The anniversary is commemorated each year by the Navy (in recent years led by the President at the naval school at Antón Lizardo) (UNVERIFIED). [K2, K9, SM:competence shown under pressure]
