### Forth Bridge (rail, Scotland)
- SOURCED (Network Rail media center, https://www.networkrailmediacentre.co.uk/news/finished-on-the-forth-bridge-repainting-now-complete, announced 9 Dec 2011): refurbishment and repainting ran 10 years, GBP 130m; up to 4,000 tonnes of scaffolding encased the bridge; over 230,000 m2 of steel painted (a caption gives 255,000 m2) and all 6.5 million rivets treated; about 240,000 liters of paint; three-layer system (primer, glass-flake epoxy, top coat) supplied by W.J. Leigh of Bolton, designed to last 25 years; "more than 1,500 people worked on the project, with up to 400 on the bridge per day at the peak"; an abseiler team worked throughout in hard-to-reach areas; "No full repaint is expected for at least 20 years. A small team of specialists and engineers will continue to monitor and maintain the bridge." Contractors: Balfour Beatty (main), Pyeroy, Harsco Infrastructure, RBG, ThyssenKrupp Palmers. The page names no injuries and gives no worker quotes. It states 57 lives were lost in the original construction.
- Search-snippet conflict: crew "200" vs "400" is explained by the primary page (400 is the daily peak; 1,500 is the total across the project). Mark: the 200 figure is NOT FOUND in a primary source.
- NOT FOUND: the headcount of the permanent post-2011 "small team". Dead end died at the sources (Network Rail gives no number).
- SOURCED (wordhistories.net, https://wordhistories.net/2022/09/10/paint-forth-bridge/, quoting the 1901 West Lothian Courier): "painting had run continuously since the bridge opened"; the 1901 article says the bridge was receiving its fourth coat since erection, workmen started at the south end and took about three years to cover the whole length, so "no sooner are they finished than the men have to begin again". Earliest metaphorical use of "paint the Forth Bridge" cited: Nonconformist & Independent, 3 April 1890 (ink "sufficient to paint the Forth Bridge"); earliest use for a never-ending task: Port-Glasgow Express, 1923; a 1939 Manchester Evening News piece shows "when they stop painting the Forth Bridge" as a common way of saying "never".
- SOURCED (search summary quoting a 2004 New Civil Engineer report via a forum, so second-hand; mark UNVERIFIED): the "endless repainting" gang never existed in the literal form, although "under British Rail management, and before, the bridge had a permanent maintenance crew". The Scotsman article (https://www.scotsman.com/news/end-of-the-metaphor-at-least-until-2031-1661497) returned HTTP 403 and was not read.
- Worker voice (search snippet only, page 403): Panos Pictures feature "Painting the Forth Bridge" (https://library.panos.co.uk/features/stories/painting-the-forth-bridge.html) quotes a sprayer/painter, John Quinn, 61, who had been on the bridge over half his working life, calling it "a huge part of our lives"; it shows numbered name badges from hundreds of workers. UNVERIFIED (snippet only, page not opened). Francis Frith memories page: a contributor's father worked on the bridge for years "six days a week in all weather" (snippet only).

### Golden Gate Bridge (road, California)
- SOURCED (Golden Gate Bridge district, https://www.goldengate.org/bridge/bridge-maintenance/painting-the-bridge/): current crew per its FAQ is 13 ironworkers, 3 pusher ironworkers, 28 painters, 5 painter laborers, 1 chief bridge painter (= 50 people, DERIVED: 13+3+28+5+1). Ironworkers replace corroded steel and rivets with high-strength bolts, fabricate small parts, help painters rig, and remove plates and bars so painters can reach the interior of columns and chords. Painting is continuous; priorities come from inspections tracking where corrosion advances. Early crews (1937-38) were "a small crew of painters, some with prior bridge experience". FY 1938/39: painting cost $112,431.84 (labor $86,589.13, paint $20,412.64, brushes/tools $5,430.67); over 30 percent of working hours lost to weather. Since 1970 corroded rivets replaced by A-325 high-strength bolts (galvanized from mid-1970s). Lead-based paint removal completed 1995 (page gives conflicting dates 1965/1968 for earlier steps). 2012: repainting of the main cables and north approach viaduct.
- Myth-handling: the district describes the work as continuous touch-up driven by inspection, not an end-to-end cycle (SOURCED in substance; secondary sites with crew figures 28/38/42 are lower quality and are superseded by the district FAQ).
- Construction-era culture (search snippet, UNVERIFIED beyond snippet): safety net under the span saved 19 men who called themselves the "Halfway to Hell Club" (district exhibit "All in a day's work", https://www.goldengate.org/exhibits/all-in-a-days-work/); oral history book by Harvey Schwartz, "Building the Golden Gate Bridge: A Workers' Oral History". No painter-pride interviews found in this pass (died at the sources).

### Eiffel Tower
- SOURCED (ipcm.it, https://www.ipcm.it/en/article/the-corrosion-of-the-eiffel-tower.aspx): repainted roughly every seven years since construction; 20th campaign began 2019, ran late; campaigns "18 months to over three years" (article also gives an average of 16 months); about 50 specialist painters "experienced with metal structures at height and unaffected by vertigo"; about 60 tonnes of paint per campaign, about 15 tonnes eroded between campaigns; tower weighs 10,000 tonnes (7,300 tonnes metalwork); painted mainly by hand with guipon brushes from harnesses; three shades, darkest at the base; weather halts work; the lead-bearing old layers were removed by "piquage" into a net, workers wear masks and shower to decontaminate each shift; the 20th campaign was delayed by a nine-month pandemic pause and by high lead levels. Color history: Venetian red lead 1889, ochre brown 1892, yellow 1899, yellow-brown 1907 (kept 47 years), brownish-red 1954, "Eiffel Tower Brown" 1968.
