### Golden Gate Bridge (road, California)
- SOURCED (Golden Gate Bridge district, https://www.goldengate.org/bridge/bridge-maintenance/painting-the-bridge/): current crew per its FAQ is 13 ironworkers, 3 pusher ironworkers, 28 painters, 5 painter laborers, 1 chief bridge painter (= 50 people, DERIVED: 13+3+28+5+1). Ironworkers replace corroded steel and rivets with high-strength bolts, fabricate small parts, help painters rig, and remove plates and bars so painters can reach the interior of columns and chords. Painting is continuous; priorities come from inspections tracking where corrosion advances. Early crews (1937-38) were "a small crew of painters, some with prior bridge experience". FY 1938/39: painting cost $112,431.84 (labor $86,589.13, paint $20,412.64, brushes/tools $5,430.67); over 30 percent of working hours lost to weather. Since 1970 corroded rivets replaced by A-325 high-strength bolts (galvanized from mid-1970s). Lead-based paint removal completed 1995 (page gives conflicting dates 1965/1968 for earlier steps). 2012: repainting of the main cables and north approach viaduct.
- Myth-handling: the district describes the work as continuous touch-up driven by inspection, not an end-to-end cycle (SOURCED in substance; secondary sites with crew figures 28/38/42 are lower quality and are superseded by the district FAQ).
- Construction-era culture (search snippet, UNVERIFIED beyond snippet): safety net under the span saved 19 men who called themselves the "Halfway to Hell Club" (district exhibit "All in a day's work", https://www.goldengate.org/exhibits/all-in-a-days-work/); oral history book by Harvey Schwartz, "Building the Golden Gate Bridge: A Workers' Oral History". ### Eiffel Tower
- SOURCED (ipcm.it, https://www.ipcm.it/en/article/the-corrosion-of-the-eiffel-tower.aspx): repainted roughly every seven years since construction; 20th campaign began 2019, ran late; campaigns "18 months to over three years" (article also gives an average of 16 months); about 50 specialist painters "experienced with metal structures at height and unaffected by vertigo"; about 60 tonnes of paint per campaign, about 15 tonnes eroded between campaigns; tower weighs 10,000 tonnes (7,300 tonnes metalwork); painted mainly by hand with guipon brushes from harnesses; three shades, darkest at the base; weather halts work; the lead-bearing old layers were removed by "piquage" into a net, workers wear masks and shower to decontaminate each shift; the 20th campaign was delayed by a nine-month pandemic pause and by high lead levels. Color history: Venetian red lead 1889, ochre brown 1892, yellow 1899, yellow-brown 1907 (kept 47 years), brownish-red 1954, "Eiffel Tower Brown" 1968.

### Cologne Cathedral (Kölner Dombauhütte)
- SOURCED (cathedral's own history sheet, https://www.koelner-dom.de/fileadmin/media/PDF/newsroom/Geschichte_der_Koelner_Dombauhuette.pdf, text extracted): "Anzahl der Mitarbeiter: ca. 100 (davon ca. 80 hochqualifizierte Handwerker und Restauratoren: Steinmetzen, Steinbildhauer, Steinrestauratoren, Glasmaler, Kunstglaser, Glasrestauratoren, Gerüstbauer, Dachdecker, Maler, Schreiner, Elektriker, Schlosser, Schmied, Installateur, Gold- und Silberschmied, Metallrestauratorin" plus about 20 in management, administration, archaeology and art history, archive and publishing). Owner is the Metropolitan Cathedral Chapter; until 1948 the Dombauhütte was a Prussian state enterprise. Zentral-Dombau-Verein (a citizens' support society founded 1842) paid about 60 percent of construction costs then and "still carries about 60 percent of the annual budget".
- SOURCED (same): more than 500 craftsmen worked at once during the 1842-1880 completion, in workshop buildings roughly where the Roman-Germanic Museum stands; 38 years to finish. The 1880 "completion" was followed by about 20 more years of work; the master builder declared the work finished shortly before his death in 1902; on 20 May 1906 an angel's wing fell from above the main portal after Sunday High Mass, stopped only by an umbrella, nobody hurt; the restoration then began and never stopped. Almost the whole choir flying-buttress system was renewed by the late 1930s. Post-war rebuilding of the nave was finished in 1956. A stone restoration workshop was set up 2013. The medieval workshop archive was taken to Paris by French revolutionary troops in 1794 and is lost. Building stopped about 1520 for over 300 years; the wooden crane on the south tower was the city's landmark.
- Cross-check: History Hit (https://www.historyhit.com/locations/cologne-cathedral/) says "over 80 stonemasons, glaziers, roofers, and other specialists" work constantly on maintenance; consistent with the cathedral's own count of about 80 craftspeople.
- SOURCED (Dombaubericht 2024, text extracted from PDF https://www.koelner-dombauhuette.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Dombaubericht_2024.pdf; this is the 65th annual report): the cathedral fire brigade runs regular drills including high-angle rescue for the workshop staff working on the scaffolds; scaffold crews (Gerüstbauer) are in-house; the in-house paint master (Malermeister) repaints the workshop's freight elevator and the carpentry shop renews its woodwork. 2023-24 marks "200 years since the re-founding of the Cologne Dombauhütte".

### Bauhutten as an institution (UNESCO)
- SOURCED (UNESCO decision 15.COM 8.C.3, https://ich.unesco.org/en/decisions/15.COM/8.C.3, December 2020): cathedral workshops of Germany, Austria, France, Norway and Switzerland were inscribed on the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices (not the Representative List; one trade site wrongly says the List). Knowledge passes "orally and in writing" via an apprenticeship and journeyman system dating to medieval times, on-site training, a coordinated European certification for master craftspersons, preserved centuries-old documentation, and "festive rituals and customs". The decision gives no number of workshops (a search summary says 18 workshops across five countries; UNVERIFIED).

### Sociology of maintenance
- SOURCED (Aeon essay "Hail the maintainers", Russell and Vinsel, https://aeon.co/essays/innovation-is-overvalued-maintenance-often-matters-more): inventors and innovators are "perhaps somewhere around one per cent" of the workforce; maintenance is "undervalued and unappreciated", the authors argue for "the dignity of being a maintainer"; low-wage maintenance work is tied to African-American, Latino and other minority workers; domestic labor sits outside GDP; most human labor (laundry, trash, janitorial, food preparation) is upkeep; examples of maintainers named include Blue Plains wastewater plant workers and graveyard-shift security guards. - SOURCED (abstract, Graham and Thrift, "Out of Order: Understanding Repair and Maintenance", Theory, Culture and Society 24(3):1-25, 2007, via https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/31938; full text not available): repair and maintenance are "central to an understanding of modern societies and, particularly, cities", a "missing link in social theory, which is usually overlooked or forgotten"; cultural imaginings of infrastructure "actively work to push repair and maintenance activities beyond the attention of social science"; cases: computer communications with electricity, and automobility. The abstract does not mention workers as a group.

The Jackson chapter PDF was not opened (HTTP 404).

### Naval dockyards
- SOURCED (Kent Archaeological Society, "The Dockyard Work-force: A Picture of Chatham Dockyard c. 1860", https://www.kentarchaeology.org.uk/journal/97/dockyard-work-force-picture-chatham-dockyard-c-1860): total workforce 2,782 (about 1,000 shipwrights, 605 labourers, 1,735 "established" permanent staff); shipwright apprenticeship seven years, bound to the Master Shipwright but learning from the whole gang, often bound to a father, uncle or brother, entry by public examination after 1859; workers present 10 hours in summer, 8 in winter, muster four times a day by the dockyard bell; established workers had lifelong security and pensions (from age 60 under the 1859 Superannuation Act) and were paid slightly less than hired men to fund them; family networks officially tolerated; shipwrights an "elite artisan corps"; engine fitters claimed a rival "counter-elite" status; canteens and tap houses banned from 1833; a mutual-aid club founded 1865 had 1,200 members and paid GBP 12 to a deceased member's family; traditions included St Clement's Day for smiths, St Katherine's for ropemakers, and group cricket and outings.
