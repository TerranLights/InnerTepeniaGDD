## 1. Physiology and medicine of pressure altitude

Earlier file (Amundsen_Station_Real_World_Research_2026-10-29.md section 1.8) already holds: station pressure average 681.4 mb, pressure altitude average 3,223 m (range 2,801 to 3,690 m); ASAP study (Anderson et al. 2011) headline figures (52% AMS, 7 HAPE evacuations 2006, 40% Diamox, no difference). ### 1.1 ASAP study: detail beyond the headline (SOURCED, PMC3114308 opened and read via WebFetch)
- Sample: 246 adults, 37 +/- 11 yr, 30% female, recruited over two summer seasons 2006-07 and 2007-08. (The earlier file says ~70% male; consistent.) Flight McMurdo (sea level) to Pole "about 3 hours."
- AMS (Lake Louise) by day: Day 1 24%, Day 2 33%, Day 3 15%, Day 4 9%, Day 5 6%, Day 6 4%, Day 7 4%.
- Symptoms in the whole group: shortness of breath with activity 87%; sleeping difficulty 74% (78% of non-Diamox users vs 75% of Diamox users); headache 66%; fatigue 65%; dizziness 46%; general-health limitation 41%; mental-status changes 37%; shortness of breath at rest 31%; cough 30%; GI upset 24%; peripheral edema 9%. More than 20% still had exertional dyspnea, fatigue and sleep problems through Day 7.
- Physiology: resting SpO2 97.3% +/- 1.3 at sea level fell to 89.1% +/- 3.2 at the Pole; resting heart rate 71.5 to 82.8 bpm. Ambient about -42 C, humidity "0.03%."
- Dehydration: hematocrit and hemoglobin rose slightly with "a small estimated reduction in blood and plasma volumes consistent with dehydration" (per the fetch tool's paraphrase; exact sentence not captured).
- Diamox: users started 24-48 h before departure, twice daily, reduced to 125 mg BID on side effects; users were older, had less altitude and Pole experience and more prior altitude problems (self-selection). Authors: benefit "appeared negligible, though it may have prevented more severe symptoms in higher risk subjects"; late starts caused by uncertain flight schedules are named as a reason.
- Sleep: authors say jet lag and perpetual daylight may have inflated sleep complaints; "two weeks of acclimatization at McMurdo and darkened quarters at the Pole" likely limited these.
- 2007: "more aggressive treatment with medications and oxygen" for the most symptomatic. Conclusion: equivalent air altitude alone is an inadequate predictor of hypoxic stress at the Pole.

### 1.2 USAP arrival protocol: the UTMB Center for Polar Medical Operations FAQ (SOURCED, primary; https://www.usap.gov/USAPgov/travelAndDeployment/documents/MA-FRM-0007.pdf, form MA-FRM-0007 dated 10 June 2012; text extracted with pdftotext and read in full)
- "residents are flown directly from sea level to approximately 10,000 feet of altitude. This requires vigilance to prevent overexertion in the first days at altitude."
- "Significant adaptations occur within the first four days at altitude. It may take a month or more to completely adapt." Conditions screened in the Physical Qualification process may bar a person from Pole employment.
- Periodic breathing: "multiple breaths followed by pauses"; worst at night; "repeated awakenings, leading to poor or disrupted sleep and subsequent daytime fatigue." Alcohol and sleeping medications "worsen" it and "are therefore not recommended while acclimatizing." Treatment: Diamox 125 mg at bedtime for the first three to four days.
- AMS "impacts approximately 30% of people traveling to high altitude," normally days one to three. Prevention: "at least four liters of water per day, a low-salt diet, and ... no heavy physical exertion for the first two to four days." Diamox 250 mg twice a day, starting the day before ascent, continuing three to four days. "Gingko ... ineffective." "Using supplemental oxygen, especially at night, can also help reduce symptoms."
- HAPE: "Three percent of people going to altitude are expected to develop HAPE," usually day two to three; symptoms: shortness of breath at rest and when lying flat, cough dry/wet/pink-frothy/bloody. Reduce risk with no heavy exertion for three to four days, Diamox, and keeping warm "to include breathing through a neck gaiter outside to prevent cold-induced spasms of blood vessels in the lungs." Treatment list: Diamox, "possible blood vessel dilators like nifedepine or Viagra, inhalers such as albuterol, dexamethasone, oxygen and possible descent."
- HACE: "rare at South Pole's altitude, but can be seen when oxygenation is worsened by the presence of HAPE." Treatment: Diamox, dexamethasone, oxygen, descent.
- Prior experience does not protect: "If you've gone to altitude 99 times, you may get altitude illness on your hundredth ascent." Time in New Zealand and McMurdo "is sufficient to allow you to lose your previous altitude acclimatization." "The only predictor that you will get sick is that you've been sick before."
- Where Diamox comes from: McMurdo medical (a member of the McMurdo medical team meets direct arrivals, gives an altitude briefing and offers Diamox before the Pole flight); also available from the South Pole Clinic.
- Note the two documents disagree on the Pole AMS rate: this FAQ cites about 30% (general high-altitude figure) while ASAP measured 52% by Lake Louise criteria (see Unresolved conflicts).

### 1.4 Other plateau stations: pressure altitude, sleep and blood oxygen (SOURCED via search-tool summaries; PMC4928492 and journalpulmonology.org items not opened)
- Concordia (3,233 m): mean air pressure 482.5 mmHg for Feb-Dec 2011 "corresponding to 3880 m along the Equator"; another source gives corrected altitude 3,800 m. Nocturnal SpO2 averaged 85.1-87.9%, diurnal 89-94% in one study; a 12-month study (journalpulmonology.org, S2531043723000491) found daytime SpO2 96.6% at baseline, 89.7% at one month and 90.6% at twelve months (Dumont d'Urville, sea level, 97.6% and 98.0%); more light-sleep stage 2, less deep sleep, more micro-arousals, elevated night blood pressure over 12 months at Concordia. A Concordia vs Dumont d'Urville comparison concluded altitude, not season, drove most sleep differences while season affected fragmentation.
- Vostok (3,488 m): barometric pressure 460 mmHg, "comparable to about 4,000 m" (Russian Institute study, Springer doi 10.1134/S0362119720070063, via search summary); winter crews stay indoors much of the time because of low temperatures (reduced activity).

### 1.6 Pregnancy and children (general altitude medicine; no children live at the Pole)
- SOURCED (search-tool summary; Moore et al. review at repositorio.umsa.bo, not opened): Colorado birth weight falls about 100 g per 1,000 m gain, curvilinear with a breakpoint near 2,000 m (6,600 ft); preeclampsia incidence higher at altitude and accounts for about half the birth-weight decline; Bolivian cohort preeclampsia/gestational hypertension 1.7 times more frequent (95% CI 1.3-2.3) at high altitude. 2022 meta-analysis (Grant, Giussani, Aiken; 59 studies): birth weight falls 54.7 g (+/- 13.0) per 1,000 m with gestational age not significantly different; Bolivian study (Julian et al.): 70 g, 56 g and 39 g per 1,000 m for Europeans, mestizos and Andeans. A 2015-2024 Andean systematic review found gestational hypertension rises at altitude while preeclampsia was variable. Tibet-specific figure NOT FOUND at this query (re-queried under item 2).
- SOURCED (UIAA, https://www.theuiaa.org/children-at-altitude/, opened; Meijer & Jean 2008): for a preschool child "it is generally recommended not to ascend to a sleeping altitude of higher than 3,000 to 4,000 m" and "prefer a sleeping altitude of <2,500m"; "no conclusive scientific data on the matter with young children"; children "need a similar time for acclimatization to that of adults"; ear pain is "the most common topic concerning children at altitude"; "a child's thermal regulation is immaturely developed"; young children may "become very quiet and still" rather than report cold. The page treats travel, not children raised at altitude.
- SOURCED (search-tool summary, Frontiers in Pediatrics 2021, 10.3389/fped.2021.798310, not opened): normal apnea-index parameters from low altitude cannot be applied at high altitude; periodic breathing in early infancy is more marked with altitude and declines with age.
- UNVERIFIED: a Colorado ski-resort blog claims a 2024 Wilderness Medical Society pediatric dosing revision; not checked, not used.

## 4. Cold injury and cold-work practice

### 4.1 Frostbite
- SOURCED (American Family Physician, 1 April 2020, summarizing the 2019 Wilderness Medical Society guideline, https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2020/0401/p440.html, opened): prevention: protect skin from moisture, wind and cold; avoid sweating and wet extremities; mitts rather than gloves; avoid alcohol, drugs and perfusion-reducing medications; avoid cold exposure during illness; chemical or electric hand and foot warmers; skin emollients "do not protect against frostbite"; maintain hydration and nutrition; minimize constriction; supplemental oxygen advised only in extreme hypoxia above 25,000 ft (7,500 m); the page does not mention a buddy system. Rewarming: in the field only if definitive care is more than 2 hours away and refreezing can be prevented; water at 37-39 C (98.6-102.2 F) with a thermometer, or test an uninjured extremity for 30 seconds; complete when the extremity is red or purple, soft and pliable, typically within 30 minutes; ibuprofen up to 600 mg four times daily; do not use a frozen extremity for walking; if refreezing is possible keep the tissue frozen; tPA for frostbite reaching at least the proximal interphalangeal joints, given within 24 hours of thawing, with "each hour of delay" decreasing salvage by 28% (3 mg bolus plus heparin 500 units per hour); iloprost (up to 72 hours) first-line for grade 3-4 when tPA is contraindicated; amputation should wait up to 3 months for demarcation unless sepsis; tetanus prophylaxis, no routine antibiotics. Classification: frostnip (superficial, no lasting damage) and degrees one to four.
- SOURCED (PubMed 38577729, Wilderness Medical Society frostbite guideline 2024 update, abstract opened): recommendations were updated and graded by the American College of Chest Physicians method; the abstract does not list them. A search-tool summary of secondary pages says the 2024 update recommends ibuprofen 12 mg/kg/day in two divided doses as a weak recommendation, conditionally recommends thrombolytics within 12 hours of rewarming, and says IV iloprost appears to reduce amputation rates in severe limb frostbite (not FDA-approved in the U.S. per the AFP summary). The 12 h vs 24 h thrombolytic window differs between the 2024 summary and the 2019 AFP text: UNVERIFIED which is current without the full text.
- SOURCED (Cattermole 1999 BAS retrospective study, abstract at https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/503074, opened): 61 cold-injury consultations in 1986-1995 (about 2.5% of all new consultations), incidence 65.6 per 1,000 people per year; 95% frostbite, 3% hypothermia, 2% trench foot; superficial frostbite 74%, face 47%; no frostbite caused permanent tissue loss; 78% occurred during recreation, skiing and snowmobile driving often involved; "an entirely preventable occurrence." NOT FOUND: a USAP-specific incidence study (anecdotal clinic accounts only; died at the sources).
- SOURCED (Environment Canada wind-chill bands as reproduced by CCOHS, https://ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/cold_working.html, opened): -28 to -39 high risk, skin can freeze in 10-30 minutes; -40 to -47 very high, 5-10 minutes; -48 to -54 severe, 2-5 minutes; -55 and colder extreme, under 2 minutes; faster in sustained winds over 50 km/h.

### 4.2 Hypothermia
- SOURCED (summary of the Wilderness Medical Society 2019 accidental-hypothermia guideline, https://www.outdoored.com/articles/summary-wilderness-medical-society-clinical-practice-guidelines-out-hospital-evaluation-and/, opened; the guideline PDF copy at femecv.com returned a graphic, not text): staging by core temperature: mild 35-32 C, moderate 32-28 C (shivering may persist; handle carefully), severe below 28 C (marked loss of consciousness, no shivering, cardiac instability); protect from the environment, remove wet clothing safely, active rewarming with an insulative layer, a vapor barrier and an external heat source (chemical heat packs, forced air, hot water bottles) to the axilla and chest with burn precautions; small chemical hand warmers "are not recommended for core rewarming"; warm-water immersion or shower not recommended (shock from peripheral vasodilation); moving a patient from horizontal can trigger afterdrop and circumrescue collapse; check for a pulse for at least one minute before compressions; one defibrillation attempt below 30 C; withhold resuscitation drugs until above 30 C; moderate and severe cases to a capable hospital, unstable cases to an ECLS/ECMO-capable facility; heat the transport vehicle to 28 C.
- SOURCED (WHSC Ontario "Cold Stress" bulletin v2.0, read in full from the PDF): the body can lose 25-30 times more heat in contact with cold wet objects than dry; hypothermia occurs most often in spring and fall rather than winter, with most cases in air between -1 and 10 C; maximum severe shivering at 35 C core.

### 4.3 Work/warm-up limits, buddy rules and the law
- SOURCED (WHSC Ontario, PDF read in full): Ontario's Occupational Health and Safety Act is "largely silent" on cold; Regulation for Industrial Establishments s.129(1)(b) requires an enclosed workplace no less than 18 C; the work/warm-up schedule was developed by the Saskatchewan Department of Labor and adopted by ACGIH as TLVs for working outdoors in the cold; the schedule applies to any 4-hour work period of moderate to heavy work with 10-minute warm-ups in a warm location and an extended break (lunch) at the end; for light-to-moderate work apply the schedule one step lower; applies only to dry clothing; assumes individuals in top condition; continuous work below freezing needs heated warming shelters; new workers need time to accustom; buddy system; avoid bare-skin contact with metal below -7 C and with liquids (gasoline, alcohol, cleaners) below 4 C.
- SOURCED (CCOHS, opened): at or below -7 C wind chill during continuous work: work-warming regimen with heated shelters; at or below -12 C: constant observation by a supervisor or buddy, pace work to avoid sweating; if skin is wet and exposed to wind reduce the air temperature used for the wind-chill calculation by 10 C; gloves below 4 C for light work and below -7 C for moderate work; mittens below -17 C; at least one trained person per shift for emergencies; "Some Canadian jurisdictions have legislated temperature ranges for specific circumstances; others use ACGIH TLVs as limits or guidelines." The page names no province's numbers.

### 6.7 Appetite and body mass at altitude and in polar residence
Neighbors followed: the laboratory and expedition literature (because no Pole-specific series exists), Concordia (the only high plateau station with a published winter body-mass series), Rothera (a measured year at a coastal station, followed to separate cold and isolation from altitude without comparing the two here), and Indian Army posts at 3,750-4,100 m (the only long sojourn data found).
- Meta-analysis. SOURCED (Matu, Gonzalez, Ispoglou, Duckworth & Deighton 2018, "The effects of hypoxia on hunger perceptions, appetite-related hormone concentrations, and energy intake: a systematic review and meta-analysis," Appetite 125:98-108, doi 10.1016/j.appet.2018.01.015; accepted manuscript at https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/126213/ read in full via pdftotext): 28 studies (54 fasted and 22 postprandial comparisons); 407 participants, 86% men, mean age 29; hypoxia from 2,134 to 7,753 m (mean 4,302 m), exposure from 50 minutes to two months (mean 10 days); hypoxia produced "a trivial but significant decrease in postprandial hunger scores (SMD: -0.15, 95% CI: -0.29 to -0.01; n=14; p=0.043) and a moderate decrease in energy intake (SMD: -0.50, 95% CI: -0.85 to -0.15; n=8; p=0.006)"; postprandial acylated ghrelin fell and fasted insulin rose, both more as severity increased; no effect on total ghrelin, GLP-1, leptin or PYY; "a shorter duration of exposure tended to be associated with a larger decrease in energy intake" (p = 0.056), "signifying a possible acclimatization response during prolonged exposures." ### 7.3 Materials at low temperature
Neighbors followed: U.S. Army cold-regions engineering (the most complete primary statement found), European steel grades, hydraulic-fluid and cable makers' data, and rail breaks (the best-recorded structural failure that rises with cold).
- Primary. SOURCED (Deborah Diemand and James H. Lever, "Cold Regions Issues for Off-Road Autonomous Vehicles," ERDC/CRREL TR-04-6, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory, April 2004, read via pdftotext):
  - Lubricants "represent the single most critical problem encountered by vehicles in cold regions"; the military's "Oil, Engine, Arctic (OEA), a 0W20 synthetic lubricant ... for ambient temperatures from 40°C to -50°C"; "all machinery in cold regions is equipped with engine heaters, and both block and pan heaters are highly recommended"; 75W90 synthetic gear oil is common; "Even low-temperature lubricants will thicken at sufficiently low temperatures"; arctic hydraulic and brake fluids are advisable: "Water absorption, leakage, excessive pump water, and stiffening are the main concerns" [sic in the extracted text; "pump wear" is probably meant, but that reading is mine and UNVERIFIED]; "it is best to warm hydraulic systems at no load before subjecting them to full-load application."
  - Fuel: the cloud point of DL-2 diesel is "about -12°C. That of JP-8, on the other hand, is lower than -60°C, making it a better choice for use in the extreme cold. However, even light fuels will ignite with greater difficulty as the temperature falls, and heating the fuel tank (or at least the fuel lines) should be considered." Coolant at a proper ratio "will not freeze above about -40°C" and below that freezes "into a slurry rather than a solid block."
  - Cold soak: "Stiffened lubricants, including hydraulic fluids, can place abnormally high loads on joints and linkages. Seals can break. Structural elements can bend or crack. Drivetrain elements can snap. It is crucially important to initiate operation slowly in very cold temperatures."
  - Metals: "High-strength steels generally become brittle as temperature drops, making them susceptible to fracture"; welds, sharp corners, cracks and holes act as stress concentrators, and shock loads worsen as rubber mounts stiffen; "It is good practice to choose steels with brittle transition temperatures lower than expected minimum air temperatures"; "Most aluminum and stainless-steel alloys do not become brittle with decreasing temperature but usually have lower strengths"; high-tin plain bearings and solder (above 35% tin) should be avoided; "Zinc galvanizing on wire rope can also become brittle and crack below -20°C."
  - Elastomers "become increasingly brittle as temperature decreases, and failures often occur before the material has warmed to operating temperature"; "choose silicone products whenever possible ... They are the seal of choice at low temperatures"; "Fan belts are the most common casualties among elastomers"; rubber shock mounts stiffen; tires develop temporary flat spots overnight and should be driven slowly until warm.
  - Plastics: "it is not a good idea to use a plastic component in any situation where it is likely to be exposed to significant vibration or sharp blows at temperatures below -20°C." Below -20 C differential contraction matters, for example steel shafts in aluminum housings.
  - Ice fog "occurs as moisture in the air freezes directly to ice crystals at temperatures about -40°C" and can be produced at warmer temperatures by vehicle exhaust, "especially at start-up."
  - Batteries and electronics: see 7.4 and 7.7.

### 7.4 Batteries
- SOURCED (UNAVCO Knowledge Base article 386, Beth Bartel, "SLA Battery Cold Performance Tests: Gel vs. AGM," 25 Feb 2016, https://kb.unavco.org/article.php?id=386, opened; filed under Polar Programs, and it refers to "a system on the Antarctic Plateau which turns off during the winter"): one new Deka Gel 8G31 (98 Ah) and one new Deka AGM 8A31 (105 Ah) in a cold chamber; capacity as a share of 25 C capacity: -20 C gel 24% / AGM 23%; -30 C 10% / 13%; -35 C 3% / 4%; -40 C 1% / 2%; -45 C 0% / 1%; -50 C 0% / 0%. After a 48-hour soak at -70 C and a retest at 25 C, the discharged gel battery kept 97% and the discharged AGM 88%; both recovered fully after the series; "These results do not show a significant difference in the cold performance between Gel and AGM batteries"; charging had no temperature compensation, so cold capacities are lower than makers' figures; a larger sample would be needed.
- SOURCED (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, "Technology Developments to Advance Antarctic Research: Proceedings of a Workshop," 2022, chapter 4 "Power and Energy for Polar Research," https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26699/chapter/5, opened): lithium thionyl chloride cells have "excellent energy densities and very good low-temperature performance" but "poor peak current output capabilities," so many cells run in parallel; lithium iron disulfide performs well cold with lower energy density; "Alkaline batteries have very poor low-temperature performance"; Iridium transmission draws about 2.5 W against 0.4 W for routine operation (ice-tethered buoys rated to -25 C). East Antarctic plateau instruments (Robert Clauer, Virginia Tech) run on solar panels and lead-acid AGM batteries; when voltage drops "the systems hibernate and wait for sunlight," then "heaters warm the batteries and the system wakes up"; they have "performed consistently well since their first deployment in 2008"; tower sections joined with pins so installation "can be performed with gloves on." Automatic weather stations (Matthew Lazzara, UW-Madison) use lead-acid and gel batteries with solar, "generally only need one watt or less"; batteries are "thermally stable, but snow accumulation can often make retrieval of batteries difficult"; limited wind installations "did not hold up well"; lithium-ion "can be a real challenge to operate in colder temperatures" and has shipping restrictions. Low temperatures make lubricants and fuels viscous, causing "excessive maintenance requirements" for generators; "Diesel engines need maintenance, which is not always possible," so one team runs redundant small single-cylinder diesels "enclosed in insulated boxes, with some success"; proton-exchange-membrane fuel cell membranes "can freeze and cause a catastrophic system failure"; polar wind turbines must survive windstorms of about 120 kt; CRREL has several cold chambers for battery testing; "More open access to cold testing facilities would be a great value."
- SOURCED (CRREL TR-04-6, 7.3): lead-acid batteries "become vulnerable to freezing when discharged, do not charge well when cold, and lose power at low temperatures"; good practice is to warm them before cold starting or charging; nickel-cadmium performs better than lead-acid; nickel-metal hydride loses more capacity and should not be charged below 0 C; lithium-ion has "relatively little capacity loss at low temperatures" and charges at normal rates above 0 C, at reduced rates down to -30 C (2001-era sources); a thermoelectric generator burning propane could warm and trickle-charge batteries.
- Search summaries only (vendor pages, not opened): charging lithium-ion below 0 C plates metallic lithium on the anode, permanently; battery-management systems block cold charging; a battery maker's paper says a fully charged lead-acid battery delivers about 75% of room-temperature capacity at 32 F and about 40% at 0 F.
