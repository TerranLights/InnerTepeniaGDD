## 1. Matanuska Valley Colony / Palmer / Mat-Su Valley, Alaska

### A. Other settings (kinds of space and what they are like)
- **The fairgrounds as a year-round venue, not a two-week site.** The Alaska State Fair, Inc. describes itself as a nonprofit that "provides a center for the Alaskan community to gather ... throughout the year." Besides the fair (Aug 16 – Sept 2 in 2024), the grounds host:
  - a **Holiday Bazaar** over two October weekends with 100+ vendors;
  - **Mighty Monster BooFest**, a Halloween beer festival endorsed by the Brewers Guild of Alaska, with costume contest and silent auction;
  - **Bright Up the Night**, billed as Alaska's largest drive-through holiday light display, nearly a mile long, with music broadcast to car radios;
  - the **Yuletide Festival** (new 2024): carousel, bonfire, food trucks, ornament decorating;
  - **Stallions of the North** (June 1, 2025).
  - SOURCED: Alaska State Fair 2024 Annual Report, https://www.alaskastatefair.org/site/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/2024-Annual-Report-For-Web.pdf (PDF read in full; published 2025).
- **Rebarchek Farm**, on the fairgrounds. One of the first colony farms (Ray and Edna Rebarchek), on the National Register. Now runs hands-on classes in cold-climate gardening, growing a prize cabbage and keeping backyard chickens, and rents out as a meeting and event space. SOURCED: same report.
- **The fair's own spaces:** the **Borealis** concert venue (ConocoPhillips Alaska Concert Series; five sold-out shows in 2024), **Pioneer Plaza** (where the head gardener's award-winning "Magic Train" living mosaiculture display stood), a **fairground rail depot** (the Alaska Railroad "Fair train" returned in 2024 for the first time since 2019), and free general parking lots named by color (purple, red, yellow, brown). SOURCED: same report.
- **Hermon Brothers Field**, the ballpark at the fairgrounds. Built in 1976 by volunteers "out of a thicket of pine trees and alder bushes," named for the local construction firm that built it. An all-grass infield, a dugout slightly below ground where local 9-year-old Little Leaguers gather, and Pioneer Peak behind the left-field pole. Two volunteer residents keep the grounds. Games start 6–7 p.m. in July. Capacity CONFLICTS: 1,300 (ADN 2019) vs 1,700 (Wikipedia). SOURCED: Anchorage Daily News, 2019-07-10, https://www.adn.com/sports/alaska-baseball/2019/07/10/alaskas-ballparks-palmers-hermon-brothers-field-is-an-oasis-of-green (read); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mat-Su_Miners (search summary).
- **U-pick farms and farm stands.** Pyrah's Pioneer Peak Farm (mile 3 Bodenburg Loop) is called the largest pick-your-own place in the valley, with up to 35 kinds of produce picked July to early October and paid by the pound. The Bodenburg Butte area stays rural, with farming and farm-product distribution as its main industry. SOURCED: alaska.org, https://alaska.org/detail/pyrahs-pioneer-peak-u-pick-farm; Lonely Planet (search summaries).
- **Family farm stands as institutions.** Bushes Bunches (Bruce and Vickie Bush, Palmer), a family farm and produce stand since 1956, was named 2024 Farm Family of the Year, an award run 25 years by the state Division of Agriculture and the Fair. SOURCED: 2024 Annual Report.
- **Lake and float-plane landscape.** Wasilla has eight public seaplane bases on area lakes; FAA records list 43 private land airstrips, eight more private seaplane bases, two heliports and a STOLport registered in Wasilla. The city runs a campground, boat launch and dog park on Lake Lucille, Newcomb Park on Wasilla Lake, and a skate park. The Little Susitna River Public Use Area is the gateway to a 300,800-acre public game reserve. An "anti-moose" electric mat was laid around the Wasilla runway in 2005. SOURCED: English Wikipedia "Wasilla, Alaska," raw wikitext read in full (citing AirNav 2008, City of Wasilla), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasilla,_Alaska.
- **Big Lake (15 mi west of Wasilla)** is a recreational lake district of rental cabins, vacation homes and private docks, described as "the go-to destination for Anchorage and Mat-Su families who want a lake experience without a long drive or a flight." Winter brings snowmachining and skating. SOURCED: lake.com / Big Lake listings (search summaries; commercial sources). UNVERIFIED in detail.
- **Commuter-suburb strip.** Wasilla's commercial life runs along the Parks Highway (mi. 39–46), with a smaller older Main Street; "nearly one third" of Wasilla people drive the ~40-minute commute to Anchorage (city profile, 2008). SOURCED: Wikipedia Wasilla.

### B. Subcultures
- **"Valley trash" (a reclaimed insult).** In early July 2004, State Senator Ben Stevens dismissed a Wasilla road flagger who had emailed him criticism as "just more Valley trash." Robert Baker, president-elect of the Wasilla Chamber of Commerce, sold about 100 black "Proud to be Valley trash" T-shirts at $22. SOURCED: Anchorage Daily News, 2004-07-21 (republished URL dated 2019-08-01), https://www.adn.com/alaska-news/2019/08/01/entrepreneur-cleans-up-with-valley-trash-shirt-after-mudslinging/ (read). The Christian Science Monitor (2008-09-04) reports that residents "proudly call themselves 'valley trash'," that bumper stickers followed, and that locals call Anchorage "**Los Anchorage**." It describes an "antigovernment, independent spirit" and fierce opposition to zoning ("Lock and load!"). SOURCED: https://csmonitor.com/USA/Politics/2008/0904/palin-s-wasilla-a-small-town-with-attitude (read). Note: these are journalists' characterizations, not survey data.
- **Colony speech.** University of Wisconsin–Madison linguists (Raimy, Salmons, Purnell; *Journal of English Linguistics*, Nov 2009) found Upper Midwestern dialect features still audible in the Mat-Su because of the 1930s colonist influx ("the echoes of Midwestern speech remain"), mixed with western U.S. features. Features examined: vowels, dropped g's, and expressions like "you betcha," "heck," "darn." SOURCED: On Wisconsin (UW alumni magazine), Spring 2010, https://onwisconsin.uwalumni.com/speakin-palinese/ (read); Wikipedia Wasilla (citing Milwaukee Journal Sentinel 2009-11-15). Note: the study's data was one speaker's debate speech; it is not a population survey.
- **Colonist selection (partly answers a round-1 gap).** Wikipedia says the colonists were "deliberately selected from Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Michigan due to their comparable cold climates." SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matanuska-Susitna_Valley (read). The individual selection criteria for families remain NOT FOUND.
- **North Slope rotational workers.** About 8% of valley residents work on the North Slope, bringing in about $220M in Slope payroll; Mat-Su is the second-largest source of Slope labor after Anchorage. Workers also commute to Red Dog mine and Bristol Bay. SOURCED: Alaska Public Media, 2013-03-20, https://alaskapublic.org/2013/03/20/borough-exports-include-housing-labor (read). Slope rotations are generally one-week-on/one-week-off or two-on/two-off, with free transport and room and board; workers described family strain and common divorce. SOURCED: Univ. of Minnesota Duluth student archive page "Life on the Slope" and ADN sponsored content 2020-10-13 (search summaries; the first is undated and student-made). UNVERIFIED for Mat-Su specifically.
- **Commercial fishermen.** 120 Wasilla-area residents held commercial fishing permits (as of the 2011 Trends citation); they work seasonally in Lower Cook Inlet, Bristol Bay, the Gulf of Alaska or Prince William Sound, since there is no commercial fishery in Upper Cook Inlet. SOURCED: Wikipedia Wasilla.
- **Mushers.** Willow calls itself "the dog mushing capital of the world"; many Iditarod mushers live and train there and around Big Lake (e.g., Martin Buser's Happy Trails Kennel, Big Lake). SOURCED: alaska.org dog-mushing page (search summary). In April 2026, 25 dead sled dogs were found at a kennel in the Caswell Lakes area after months of complaints to the borough. SOURCED: ADN, 2026-04-16 and 2026-04-21 (search summaries).
- **Snowmachiners.** The **Iron Dog** (since 1984), billed as the world's longest snowmachine race (over 2,000 miles), has a ceremonial start in Wasilla and the official start at Big Lake in mid-February. Todd Palin of Wasilla is a four-time champion. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Dog (search summary); Wikipedia Wasilla.
- **Climbers and the Talkeetna crowd.** The **Fairview Inn** (1923) is Talkeetna's bar and eight-room lodging for Denali climbers going and returning; it holds climber memorabilia, is the town's tallest building (zoning bars anything taller), and carries the airport windsock. Owned since about 2022 by Henrik Wessel and partners. SOURCED: Alaska's News Source, 2024-05-29, https://www.alaskasnewssource.com/2024/05/29/roadtrippin-2024-visiting-historic-fairview-inn-talkeetna (read).
- **The Talkeetna Bachelor Society** (1981, at the Fairview Inn). Its **Wilderness Woman Contest** (since 1986, created by Pam Rannals) is held the first weekend of December for single women 21+: timed events such as hauling firewood and water, driving a snowmachine, harnessing a dog team, and serving a sandwich and beer to a "bachelor" watching football. The **Bachelor Ball and auction** follow that evening, and proceeds go to women and children in crisis. Prize: a fur hat, a plaque, sometimes a trip to Europe. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilderness_Woman_Contest (read); KTNA (search summary).
- **4-H and FFA youth.** The 4-H Junior Market Livestock Auction at the fair sold 141 animals for $589,306 in 2024, up from 82 animals / $352,000 in 2021 and $185,000 in 2020. Palmer FFA is a named contributor to Rebarchek Farm. Fair scholarships include a 4-H/FFA/Vendor award. SOURCED: 2024 Annual Report.
- **Giant-vegetable growers.** A small group of named families dominates: in 2024 Scott Robb (Palmer) won the 28th Giant Cabbage Weigh-Off at 97.35 lb, with Paul and Keevan Dinkel (Wasilla) second and third; the Dinkels also placed in the 18th Great Pumpkin Weigh-Off (won with a 2,035-lb pumpkin). Eight new state crop records were set, e.g., a 25-ft-9-in tomato plant. SOURCED: 2024 Annual Report.
- **Homeschool / correspondence families.** About 3,000 Mat-Su students attended statewide correspondence schools outside the district in 2023; IDEA (Interior Distance Education of Alaska) alone had about 2,600 Mat-Su students; the district's own Mat-Su Central had about 2,803 by one count. SOURCED: KTNA and ADN (search summaries, 2023–2026; the KTNA page fetched did not contain the article). UNVERIFIED by direct read. Raven Homeschool is a 2024 fair sponsor (SOURCED: annual report).
- **Alaska Native communities.**
  - **Eklutna**: St. Nicholas Russian Orthodox church (built 1830s, reconstructed 1970) and a still-active cemetery of 100+ painted **spirit houses** over graves, mixing Orthodox and Dena'ina practice; the village is described as the area's oldest continuously inhabited Dena'ina settlement (c. 1650). SOURCED: Atlas Obscura; Frommers (search summaries).
  - **Chickaloon**: the **Ya Ne Dah Ah School** (1992), the first tribally owned and operated school in Alaska, teaching Ahtna language, drumming, singing and basket weaving alongside state standards. Its site description uses Ahtna names for local places, e.g., Palmer = *Nuutah* ("between or among islands"), Sutton = *Ts'es Tac'ilaexde*. SOURCED: https://www.chickaloon.org/ya-ne-dah-ah-school/; Harvard Honoring Nations (search summaries).
  - Wasilla's Dena'ina name is *Benteh* ("among the lakes"). SOURCED: Wikipedia Wasilla.
  - The Knik Tribal Council is a named contributor to Rebarchek Farm. SOURCED: annual report.
- **Churches.** Wasilla has 51 churches on record across 25 traditions; the largest tradition is non-denominational (directory site, search summary, UNVERIFIED). Wasilla Bible Church (1977, nondenominational evangelical, up to 1,000 attendees, new building 2006); Wasilla Assembly of God (Pentecostal, founded 1951, first met in the Wasilla Community Hall). SOURCED: Wikipedia entries (search summaries).

### C. Leisure industries
- **Visitor volume (2016 study).** About 391,000 out-of-state visitors, 89% (348,000) in May–September; Talkeetna drew 239,000 and Palmer/Wasilla 174,000. Direct spending was nearly $100M, supporting 1,350 jobs, plus $1.2M+ in bed tax. Biggest sectors: food and beverage, then lodging and tours. Cruise-linked hotels and tours added $16.4M and 134 jobs. Study by McDowell Group for the Mat-Su Convention and Visitors Bureau. SOURCED: KTNA, 2017-12-07, https://ktna.org/valley-tourism-report-2016-released/ (read); akcruise.org (search summary for the cruise figures).
- **The fair as the main leisure business.** 370,485 visitors in 2024 (record), up from 303K (2019), 305K (2021), 340K (2022), 356,179 (2023). Economic impact $50.9M (2024) vs $21M (2014). It takes no state funds. 7,660 entered exhibits. SOURCED: 2024 Annual Report. **Note:** round 1 cited the 1998 figure of 361,804 as the peak; the 2023 and 2024 figures exceed it. 275 employees (2022) (search summary, UNVERIFIED). A two-week stint can provide 75% of a vendor's annual revenue, and Palmer sees a sales-tax bump of "probably up to 25%" during the fair. SOURCED: ADN 2020-05-31; Alaska's News Source 2022-09-03 (search summaries).
- **Sponsors show who the fair's customers and backers are:** oil (ConocoPhillips, Alyeska Pipeline, Alaska Oil and Gas Association), shipping (TOTE Maritime, Lynden Transport, sponsoring touring exhibits since 2017), credit unions, local radio ("Valley Radio Q99.7, the Voice of the Valley"; "K-HITZ 107.1 Your Valley"), the Wildflower Garden Club. SOURCED: annual report.
- **Summer collegiate baseball.** The **Mat-Su Miners** (Alaska Baseball League; founded 1976 as the Valley Green Giants, renamed 1980 by the people of Palmer). ABL titles in 2025 and 2026, among others. SOURCED: Wikipedia; ADN 2026-08-03 (search summaries).
- **Fishing tourism.** The **Deshka River** (boat access only, west of Willow) is described as the favorite "remote" fishery for Anchorage-area anglers, where "tens of thousands flock to fish"; the mouth of Willow Creek is fished "combat-style," anglers "lined up in there like cordwood." King salmon seasons have repeatedly been closed or catch-and-release for low returns. SOURCED: Craig Medred, 2018-03-13; KTNA (search summaries).
- **Nightlife in Palmer** is a few bars: **Klondike Mike's Saloon** (820 S Colony Way; family-friendly until evening, live music) is described as one of three bars on the main drive "where the nightlife happens"; **Palmer City Alehouse** (320 E Dahlia Ave; 44 taps, live music Friday and Saturday all summer). SOURCED: travel and listing sites (search summaries). UNVERIFIED in detail.

### A. Other settings
**The kibbutz dining hall (חדר אוכל), as a space with a history.** SOURCED: HE-WP חדר אוכל (קיבוץ).
- It began as the workers' kitchens of the Second Aliyah. Degania built its first purpose-built dining hall only 16 years after founding. The first purpose-designed kibbutz dining hall opened at Ginegar in 1928 (architect Richard Kauffmann).
- Early halls were temporary huts: long tables for 8–10 on wooden benches, sometimes broken crockery, crowding and hygiene problems. A **bell** at the entrance called members to emergencies, holidays, assemblies and the morning wake-up for work.
- A bowl in the middle of each table for peels and scraps was nicknamed the **"kolboinik"** (כולבויניק).
- Hall size was set by the **Passover seder**, when all members and guests sat at once and the meal could not be run in shifts.
- In the 1950s knives were **chained to the tables** because cutlery was short: one or two knives per six or eight diners.
- After 1948 many halls were large **"Swedish huts"** (wooden barracks from postwar Germany or Sweden). Kfar Giladi's kitchen equipment came from a decommissioned ship's galley.
- Late 1960s–1970s: shifts ended, self-service began, benches became square and round tables, and people sat in fixed places — at lunch by origin or work branch, in the evening with family. The separate children's kitchens were abolished. The 4 p.m. family meal at home marked the shift to family culture.
- From the mid-1980s crisis, many halls closed. Some became restaurants open to the public; others became offices. In 2009 artist Michal Shachnai-Yaakobi staged "The Dining Hall as Parable" with 40 artists in the abandoned hall of Kibbutz Yifat. She told *Haaretz* (2012-09-13): the hall is "a very loaded place for kibbutz members and certainly for those who left ... for some it is quite a traumatic place," and a privatized community has to keep it from standing "like a white elephant in the middle of the kibbutz."
- Halls were decorated with work by the kibbutz's own artists, with lawns spread outside the exit.

**Sde Boker (kibbutz).** SOURCED: HE-WP שדה בוקר.
- Began on 15 May 1952 as a cooperative "farm for raising livestock," with ten named founders (the name was proposed by Yaakov Orev, "Buda"). It voted to become a kibbutz in October 1955 and formally joined the Ihud movement only in April 1962. 200 sheep came from Turkey in January 1953, and an artificial lake holding 200,000 m³ opened in 1955.
- The early security situation shaped it. Barbara Proper, a founding member, was murdered while herding goats in September 1952; the first tree was planted 30 days later. Yosef Yairi was killed in an ambush in December 1952. A Nahal platoon of about 60 soldiers then arrived, and later a Scouts (Tzofim) gar'in that built the garage, metal shop, carpentry shop and peach orchard. The military government removed the Bedouin tribes from the area when the kibbutz was founded.
- **Today:** "one of a small group of kibbutzim that still keep their traditional kibbutz way of life." About 450 residents in the early 21st century, about 190 of them members. In 2021, 24 people were accepted as "new members with economic independence" — the first such intake since founding — as part of a long effort to grow and rejuvenate. Farm of about 1,300 dunam: the Davik adhesive-tape factory, orchards, chicken coops, irrigated crops, a kennel, vineyards, the Sde Boker winery and tourism. A gallery caption notes a neighborhood of houses designed by architect Vittorio Corinaldi and the founders' original huts near Ben-Gurion's hut.
- A **service-year "commune"** (komuna, through the kibbutz movement) lives on the kibbutz: young people doing a pre-army year of informal education, Ben-Gurion heritage work and labor on area farms.
- A French report (Slate.fr, story 112647) said Sde Boker members still have no personal bank accounts or private cars, with 180 members and nearly 450 people including volunteers and employees. UNVERIFIED: search summary only (the page returned 403), and its date is not confirmed.

**Midreshet Ben-Gurion** (the community settlement 3 km south). SOURCED: HE-WP מדרשת בן-גוריון.
- Ben-Gurion's vision at the 6 October 1963 cornerstone ceremony: "a kind of Hebrew Oxford in the Negev." It was owned and run by a government corporation until 2003, when it became an ordinary settlement in the Ramat HaNegev Regional Council. It is the largest settlement in that council.
- Who lives there: residents, university students, researchers, boarding-school pupils and pre-army academy cadets.
- Institutions: the Blaustein desert-research institutes; the Ben-Gurion Heritage Institute; a solar-energy research institute; the **Gal Center for Leadership** (2007), which runs the **Negev Mechina** (pre-army academy), the "Kav HaZinuk" leadership program for young people from the periphery, and "Bein HaShitim," a workshop and challenge-activity center; the Sde Boker field school; the Zin primary school; a **Gadna** (youth pre-military) base; and the IDF's Negev geography education unit.
- **Desert architecture as a setting.** The first buildings borrowed from tropical rather than desert prototypes. After the desert institutes opened, an experimental earth-brick solar building, the **"Adobe House" / "Mud House"**, was finished in 1981, and its principles became the basis for climate-adapted neighborhoods. Named neighborhoods: Moshe Safdie's "Lego" neighborhood, Neve Zin, and a newer neighborhood "where people live without air conditioners — and it isn't hot" (Ynet, 2015-07-21, cited in HE-WP). There is also a commercial center and the "Hexagons" hostel.
- **Ben-Gurion Promenade** (dedicated 2023), on the cliff over the Zin valley.
- Student housing (BGU Sde Boker campus): 198 units (single, double, couple and family), in the heart of the campus. SOURCED: BGU dormitories page (search summary).
- **Shabbat rhythm.** From Friday afternoon to Saturday evening classes stop, and the one small market and the four restaurants close; "the community revives on Sundays." English is the working language of the international university community, which draws students from about 20 countries. SOURCED: Medill News Service (Northwestern), 2019-04-13, https://news.medill.northwestern.edu/chicago/life-at-a-research-lab-in-israels-negev-desert (read).

**Ein Gedi (kibbutz).** SOURCED: HE-WP עין גדי (קיבוץ).
- Founded as a Nahal outpost in 1953 and civilianized as a kibbutz in 1956, mostly by youth-movement and Nahal gar'in graduates. Before 1967 it could be reached only from Arad, hours away on dirt roads, and it ran on its own generator for 12 years until connected to the grid in January 1968 by a 53-km line from the Dead Sea Works.
- After the 1967 war a new Dead Sea road made it a stop for travelers. In 1968 it began a 100-room guest house, a bathing beach and a gas station, and tourism became a main livelihood.
- The site moved uphill in July 1959. A small youth hostel, Beit Sarah, stood between the old site and the shore.
- **The botanical garden** grew out of an ordinary kibbutz ornamental garden. It was registered in 1994 with the international botanic-gardens association, but the Ministry of Agriculture does not recognize or fund it because of unresolved zoning. It has baobab trees.
- **Water dispute.** In 2004 it emerged that the mineral-water plant, permitted at first to pump 6,000 m³ a year, had increased to over 120,000 m³ a year. The Society for the Protection of Nature in Israel (SPNI) and nature activists said this dried the stream. A charter with the Nature and Parks Authority (29 April 2007) committed the kibbutz to return 100% of the natural flow to the Arugot and David streams. SPNI still opposes it. Rare species were reported returning by 2009.
- **Youth on the kibbutz:** the **Ein Gedi Boarding School**, in the heart of the botanical garden, houses about 80 boys and girls in grades 9–12 from across Israel. They study at the regional six-year **Ein Gedi high school** with an emphasis on art, music and sport, and take part in kibbutz holidays, ceremonies, branch work and volunteering, alongside the youth movement (HaIhud HaHakla'i). The secular pre-army academy **"Melach HaAretz"** ("Salt of the Earth") has operated there since 2008.

**Ein Gedi's privatization.** The kibbutz ended its nearly 50-year system of equal pay and moved to pay by productivity. "The first thing" it did was make members pay for their food and electricity, then it tackled "hidden unemployment," telling members working at the farm, spa and guest house who were not really needed to find other income. SOURCED: JPost "Kibbutzim change with the times" (search summary; the page returned 404 on fetch). UNVERIFIED by direct read; date unknown. Across the movement, about 75% of kibbutzim adopted a differential-salary "safety net" model from the late 1990s to the early 2010s. SOURCED: Diao, SIOE conference paper 2019/2020 (search summary).

**Central Arava: the moshav as a built setting.**
- **Hatzeva** (moshav ovdim; Nahal outpost 9 Dec 1965, civilianized 21 Nov 1967). SOURCED: HE-WP חצבה.
  - During the transitional **"moshbutz"** stage (Nahal outpost to moshav) everything was shared, kibbutz-style: dining hall, laundry, a small supply store. When houses were ready, the farm was split into family units, the dining hall and laundry were taken apart, and the supply store became the grocery or *tzarchaniya* (co-op shop). Hatzeva split its common vegetable garden into family plots in September 1971 and moved to its permanent site, under 1 km from the Jordanian border, on 18 January 1972, with 26 married couples, 10 singles and 12 children.
  - Until 1990 the moshav treasury acted as a common bank: each family's produce was marketed through the association, and families drew a living budget and credit line from the treasurer. That system was dismantled in 1990 in the moshav debt crisis. Since then each family markets independently, sharing only land and water — "but the social framework has remained as it was."
  - **2020:** about 190 families (about 150 member families). About 80 are second-generation families who came back, 58 of them farming. About 850 people in all. A "continuing sons" neighborhood was added in the 2000s and a community expansion with 86 plots in 2018.
  - **Spaces:** four kindergartens and nurseries; a swimming pool used by residents and tourists; a library named for member Nadav Harari, killed in 1973; an archive of the moshav's history; a clinic with a resident nurse seven days a week and a doctor from Sapir three times a week; 13 tourism compounds with about 65 guest rooms, three khan-style lodgings, catering and a grocery.
  - Farming: about 8,000 dunam of drip-irrigated vegetables and orchards, a goat herd and an ornamental-fish farm. The TV series *Yellow Peppers* (about parents of an autistic child on a southern moshav) was filmed there.
  - Youth institutions: the Arava pre-army academy (2009, about 40 cadets), the **"Adam VeAdama"** high school (54 pupils), and **"Derech Lotan"**, a desert center for at-risk youth founded by the parents of Lt. Lotan Slavin, killed in the 2006 Lebanon war.
- **Sapir** (community settlement, 1979). SOURCED: HE-WP ספיר; English Wikipedia Sapir, Israel (read). Built to house the regional council's municipal workers and now mostly professionals, a mixed religious and secular "rural" community. It hosts the council offices and the **regional school "Shitim."** Groundwater found at construction was drained into an artificial lake, around which landscape architect Shlomo Aronson designed **Sapir Park**, with a grove and rest areas. A *Haaretz* piece (2022-12-07) asks why the Israeli film industry is drawn to this tiny settlement. The **Arava Aquatic Sports Center** at the foot of Mount Darga is a multi-pool sports facility and community center replacing the single outdoor pool in each village, used for lessons, competitions, classes and public events; it won the Rechter Prize for architecture (*Haaretz*, 2023-12-27). SOURCED: HE-WP; archdaily / jidipi (search summaries).
- **Ein Yahav** (the first and largest Arava moshav). SOURCED: HE-WP עין יהב.
  - Founded by a Nahal gar'in on 23 Nov 1959, after the founders appealed directly to Ben-Gurion, who approved it on 7 August 1959. It went from moshbutz to moshav shitufi to moshav ovdim.
  - In 1995 its farmers formed the marketing association **"Yofi shel Yerakot"** ("Beauty of Vegetables"), which by 2013 was Israel's largest fresh-vegetable marketer (120M NIS in 2012). From about 2010 many residents shifted to tourism, and it now has over 100 guest units, plus workshops, art, restaurants and catering.
  - Its youth movement is **Bnei HaMoshavim**, and a branch of the Arava pre-army academy opened there in September 2017.
  - **Population CONFLICT:** HE-WP says about 900 residents (2026); English Wikipedia (round 1) gave 1,654 (2024). Not resolved.
- **Tzukim** (community settlement, 2004). SOURCED: HE-WP צוקים.
  - Founded by people from central Israel seeking a change of life, on the site of the former Nahal outpost Tzofar (1968 to the mid-1970s), formerly called "**Camp Bildad**." About 90 families by January 2019, out of 155 planned units. Livelihoods: tourism, education, art, freelance work, farming-related jobs and the council.
  - Two kindergartens, one a mixed-age anthroposophic (Waldorf) one. About 80 teenagers in the HaIhud HaHakla'i youth movement.
  - Seven desert-lodging compounds, from mud huts to luxury suites.
  - The abandoned Camp Bildad became an **artists' village** with ceramics, painting, sculpture, woodwork and jewelry workshops, a nature shop and a coffee roastery, open Fridays, on the intermediate days of festivals, and during the film festival.
- **Arava farm-tourism spaces (2021):** the **Bein HaShitim farm** (2017) near the Vidor Center, offering hands-on farming and a weekly **Friday market** of vegetables, sandwiches, baked goods, coffee and local crafts; home catering from Hatzeva that began for residents and grew with tourism; and the Prem Ursula restaurant in Tzukim with an adjacent craft brewery. Tzukim is described as about 100 families. SOURCED: Maariv travel, 2021-03-19, https://www.maariv.co.il/lifestyle/travel/Article-828618 (read).

### B. Subcultures
- **Boarders vs. locals (Midreshet Ben-Gurion slang).** The environmental high school, founded 1965 as "Har HaNegev High School," has about 100 pupils per grade, 9–12 (2018). Boarding pupils from all over the country are called **"pnimistim"** (פנימיסטים, "boarders"); children of local staff and residents who live at home are **"midrashnikim"** (מדרשניקים). Pupils may go on personal hikes without adult supervision, called **"shiluchim"** (שילוחים, roughly "sendings-out") in local jargon. Five units of environmental science are compulsory, and more than a hundred graduates have moved to the Negev after school. SOURCED: HE-WP מדרשת בן-גוריון.
- **Pre-army academy cadets (mechina).** Central Arava has four branches, all under the Ruach Arava association: Hatzeva (Sept 2009, the mother branch, about 40 cadets), Paran (Sept 2012, about 40), Ein Yahav (Sept 2017, 44), and Tzofar (2021, about 35, one-year). It is mixed religious and secular. Cadets volunteer a day and a half a week in regional schools and in welfare centers in Be'er Sheva, Yeruham, Dimona and Eilat; train three times a week for army service; study Zionism, Judaism, philosophy and history; and work on Arava farms. SOURCED: HE-WP מכינה קדם-צבאית ערבה. Ein Gedi has "Melach HaAretz"; Midreshet has the Negev Mechina (see A).
- **The service-year commune** at Sde Boker (see A).
- **Foreign volunteers (historical, movement-wide).** Few came before 1967. In the 1970s about 12,000 arrived a year, and a total of 350,000 from 35 countries came after 1967: UK about 50,000, South Africa about 40,000, Sweden about 25,000, Denmark about 20,000, Germany about 15,000. Over time most were non-Jewish Western Europeans. They usually stayed 2–3 months for food, board and sometimes pocket money, working in agriculture, kitchens, gardens and factories, and some studied in kibbutz ulpan. Kibbutzim worried about drugs, drinking and members marrying volunteers and emigrating. Numbers fell to a low of about 100 in 2001 (Second Intifada) and recovered to about 1,500 in 2007. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kibbutz_volunteer (read). Note: another source summary claims "a half-million volunteers" from 1967 to 1985, which CONFLICTS with Wikipedia's 350,000. Not resolved.
- **The bomb-shelter pub.** After the dining hall closed in the evening, volunteers gathered, smoking and gossiping, then went down to **bomb shelters that doubled as pubs**, where kibbutz men arrived to meet them. In the 1980s volunteer bands toured neighboring kibbutzim's shelters. Shelters used as discotheques had to be cleared for military use in the 1973 war. SOURCED: Lilith magazine "Truth or Dare" and other summaries (search summary only; Lilith returned 403). UNVERIFIED as to which kibbutzim, and not specific to the three named places.
- **AICAT trainees (Arava International Center for Agricultural Training, Sapir).** About 1,000 students a year (2016) on a 10-month work-study program, arriving each August, 70% male, from Nepal, Vietnam, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, Ethiopia, South Sudan, East Timor, Thailand, Indonesia, Uganda, Rwanda and Nigeria. Over 10,000 in 20+ years. Each is assigned to an area farmer who becomes "their mentor ... their family away from home," and they are paid by the farms. Their leisure is organized as field trips around the Arava, visits to agri-tech companies, the annual Arava Open Day, and a trip to Mount Hermon. SOURCED: ISRAEL21c, 2016-05-09, https://archive.israel21c.org/turning-israels-desert-into-a-teaching-experience-2/ (read). Hatzeva's farmers use these trainees alongside Thai workers. SOURCED: HE-WP חצבה. A Hebrew headline gives 1,200 students; that link is broken (UNVERIFIED).
- **Nepalese students and Thai workers on 7 October 2023.** Of about 22 foreign workers killed at Kibbutz Alumim (Gaza border, not the Arava), 12 were Thai and 10 Nepalese agricultural students; Thai workers there lived in mobile caravan dormitories near the kibbutz entrance. SOURCED: JNS, 2025-01-23, https://www.jns.org/feature/thai-farm-workers-attacked-on-oct-7-return-to-negev-farms (read, first 100k characters). Nationally, 34 Thai nationals were killed and over 7,000 Thai farmworkers left Israel after 7 October; returns began in May 2024; about 38,000 Thai farmworkers were in Israel more recently. SOURCED: search summaries of Times of Israel, Arab News and Tribune India. UNVERIFIED by direct read. Arava-specific effect: NOT FOUND.
- **Thai workers' internal life.** Workers arrive on five-year visas that cannot be renewed; married couples are barred from coming together; employers hold quotas called "visas." There is an internal hierarchy by age and seniority. Farmers practice what Kaminer calls "disavowal" ("they don't feel" Thai presence). SOURCED: Kaminer, MERIP 2016 (re-read for this round; round 1 covered the Songkran parade). Kaminer's book *Capitalist Colonial* (Stanford UP, 2024) is based on fieldwork from autumn 2015 on a Central Arava moshav and in Isaan; a reviewer notes it only briefly treats the difference between Thai behavior at work and in leisure hours at night. SOURCED: Asia by the Book review, 2025-08-26 (read); SUP pages (search summary; the SUP excerpt returned 429).
- **Buddhist presence (attempted).** In January 2011 the Central Arava Regional Council signed an agreement with the nonprofit "Wisdom and Intelligence" (led by Lama Dvora Tzvieli, Tibetan Buddhist) to allocate "hundreds of acres" for a Buddhist "spiritual university." The anti-missionary group Yad L'Achim campaigned against it. SOURCED: Israel National News, 2011-01-13, https://www.israelnationalnews.com/news/141713 (read). Outcome: NOT FOUND.
- **Second-generation returnees.** At Hatzeva about 80 of 190 families are children of founders who came back (2020). SOURCED: HE-WP חצבה.

## 3. SEKEM, Egypt (the Belbeis farm, Heliopolis University, and the villages around them)

### A. Other settings
**On the farm (as visitors saw it, Feb 2025; HKS 2025):**
- The farm is enclosed by a **mud (Lehm) wall** inside its belt of eucalyptus and casuarina trees. The first wells were 50–80 m deep; today they are 100–120 m. "A delicate, very fine scent lies over SEKEM, coming from the herb fields."
- **Livestock pens:** cows, donkeys, sheep and chickens in open enclosures roofed with palm leaves against the sun. Fields of vegetables, herbs and clover-grass for fodder.
- **Mud dovecotes** with clay pots built into the walls for pigeons to nest in; the droppings are collected for compost.
- **The compost yard**, with "a mighty mango tree" for shade. Compost is 30% manure and 70% green matter plus shredded branches, and it is shipped to the satellite farms in Faiyum and Wahat.
- **The "preparation house"** (*Präparatehaus*): a round mud hut with a half-round roof, peat-lined walls and round benches that open to store biodynamic preparations in large jars, dark and cool.
- **The herb-drying building**, where dried herbs wait for collection.
- **The Waldorf kindergarten** next to the school: small mud houses with low door handles and international children's books. Children wear **uniforms in different colors by "temperament"** — stronger or paler green, red or blue for more temperamental or more melancholic children.
- A **school for children with disabilities**, with shell necklaces hung over its entrances.
- The smell of freshly cooked jam.
- **An art-therapy studio:** "a bright, light room at the very top of a building," where two art therapists, Nihal and Petra, regularly make art with the workers.
- **The SEKEM Guest House**, where visiting groups eat and sleep; the students ate lunch and a festive dinner there. Visiting groups under 2025 conditions could not move outside SEKEM without a registered itinerary, tourism-police checkpoints, a driver and a security escort ("outside SEKEM nothing happens without a security person and a timetable"). The students described SEKEM and the university as a "clearly European-shaped and protected" space.

It uses solar panels, greywater systems and natural materials and cools without energy-intensive air conditioning; staff are "many of whom are community residents"; evenings may include storytelling. Limstrand on 25 May 2025: https://www.kirken.no/globalassets/fellesrad/svalbard/dokumenter/%C3%A5rsm%C3%B8te%202024/%C3%A5rsrapport%202024%20-%20svalbard%20kirke.pdf (21-page PDF downloaded and read in full; translations mine). ### A. Other settings
- **Housing moved by avalanche.** On 19 December 2015 an avalanche from Sukkertoppen hit 11 homes and killed two people (a two-year-old girl and a man). Since 2018, 107 homes have been demolished and replaced; demolition of 140 homes in the danger zone began in December 2019; and the 2023 avalanche plan calls for 61 more in **Lia** to be demolished. A new residential area in **Gruvedalen** (east of the mouth of the Longyear river) has added 132 units since 2018, with 27 more due spring/summer 2024. Avalanche protection works were completed after eight years (on an April 24; year per TU, not confirmed). SOURCED: Longyearbyen lokalstyre, *Boligbehovsutredning 2023*; Teknisk Ukeblad (tu.no); DSB 2016 report (search summaries). UNVERIFIED by direct read.
- **Student housing.** Until 2021 most UNIS students lived in five renovated **mining barracks in Nybyen** (30–40 minutes' walk from the center). The **Elvesletta** student housing in the center opened in summer 2021, and Nybyen was dropped because of avalanche risk. Barracks life had a "kitchen to kitchen party," where kitchens competed on themes and partygoers were led from kitchen to kitchen. SOURCED: Wikipedia Nybyen; High North News; FU Berlin student letter (search summaries).
- **The church as a 24-hour building.** Svalbard kirke is open day and night, with a fireplace lounge (**peisestua**); visits are estimated at about 45,000 a year (the door counter was removed during renovation). The parish house is inside the church. In January–February 2024 the church had **no water or sewage for 30 days** because of frozen pipes; the priest had to move out, fire rounds were run daily, and the church closed one weekend. Frozen water to the **Skjæringa** area is "a recurring problem through several decades." SOURCED: Kirke 2024.
- **The church cabin ("Kirkehytta")** in **Todalen**, used for outdoor services and by scouts and folk-high-school groups. In 2024 there were six outdoor services: at the cabin, at **Hiorthhamn**, at **Vindodden**, and at the Longyearbyen cemetery. SOURCED: Kirke 2024.
- **The closed cemetery.** It has been closed to new urn graves since slush avalanches in **October 2016**; a new burial ground was still awaiting regulation in 2025. "It is a sign of civilization that one takes care of one's dead. We shall do that here on Svalbard too." SOURCED: Kirke 2024.
- **The town square** with its Christmas tree (see E). SOURCED: Kirke 2024.
- **Cabin areas** that depend on snowmobile access: Reveneset – Hiorthhamn – Adventdalen – Foxdalen, and **Vindodden**. SOURCED: Longyearbyen Jeger- og Fiskerforening hearing statement (search summary; PDF did not download cleanly). **Bjørndalen** cabins face the sea; UNIS has a **student cabin in Bjørndalen**. SOURCED: UNIS "11 things," 2022-12-07, https://www.unis.no/news/11-things-you-need-to-know-about-studying-in-longyearbyen/ (read); listings (search summary).
- **Dog yards** outside town: **Green Dog Svalbard** (Bolterdalen, about 250 dogs, called the biggest yard on Svalbard), **Arctic Husky Travellers** (Bolterdalen, 10 km out, 56 huskies), **Svalbard Villmarkssenter** (Adventdalen, about 40 Alaskan huskies). SOURCED: Visit Svalbard / visitnorway listings (search summaries; commercial self-descriptions).
- **Svalbard Folkehøgskole** (folk high school). Opened fall 2019; owned by YMCA-YWCA Norway (KFUK-KFUM) and Longyearbyen Local Council. The new campus at **Sjøskrenten** (built for Store Norske by Hæhre Arctic, due February 2023) has capacity for 125 pupils and reuses an industrial kitchen and interior elements from the closed **Svea** mining settlement, plus a rooftop solar park. SOURCED: High North News, 2021-12-21, https://en.highnorthnews.com/arctic-living/building-the-worlds-northernmost-folk-high-school-in-svalbard/228105 (read). About 75 students on seven lines, including outdoor life, climate, environment, research, photography and art. SOURCED: search summary. UNVERIFIED.

### B. Subcultures
- **Filipinos.** 127 at 1 January 2025 (round 1). Interviewed in late 2024, Filipino resident **Vernon Alvez** (arrived late 2021, after seasonal farm work in Trøndelag; now a taxi driver, his wife works in a café) said: "We fill a lot of essential roles here — in cleaning, transport, and service. I'm pretty sure we're needed." He coaches basketball with local youth and his daughter joined them for secondary school. The essay says many Filipinos avoid speaking publicly for fear for their jobs, and that stricter immigration rules and the invalidation of foreign driver's licenses created uncertainty. SOURCED: *Artica Writings* essay 4 (interview by Elida Høeg), https://www.articasvalbard.no/news/svalbard-is-home-not-norway-third-essay-in-artica-writings-series-now-available-ddl68 (read; publication date not shown). On 29–30 August 2025 the Philippine embassy held a mass at Svalbard kirke (60+ attendees, said by Fr. Johan Dumandan, described as the first Filipino mass in English there "in recent memory"), followed by an evening gathering of 100+ Filipinos and a meeting with Mayor Terje Aunevik. SOURCED: ScandAsia, https://scandasia.com/philippine-embassy-strengthens-ties-with-svalbard-community/ (read). Name of the community association: NOT FOUND.
- **Non-EU service workforce in crisis.** In June 2020 the government proposed NOK 7 million to send home laid-off non-EU/EEA workers who were not eligible for benefits (expiring 20 June 2020). LO and Visit Svalbard objected that cleaners, waiters, hotel and shop staff are "a key group," some resident "for decades." SOURCED: High North News, 2020-06-05, https://en.highnorthnews.com/business/fears-consequences-for-tourism-if-foreign-workers-are-sent-away-from-svalbard/163158 (read).
- **UNIS students.** Everyone takes a mandatory first-week safety course with rifle shooting, and those who pass may rent, buy or borrow a rifle. Rifles and signal flares are required outside settlements. SOURCED: UNIS pages (search summaries; UNIS A–Z read). Customs listed by UNIS (A–Z guide, 2021-11-01, https://www.unis.no/news/a-to-z-student-guide-to-longyearbyen-3/, read):

- **The shoes-off custom.** "The Longyearbyen tradition is to take your shoes off before entering a building." It began as a way to keep coal dust out and persists at UNIS, hotels, libraries and restaurants. SOURCED: UNIS "11 things," 2022-12-07 (read). After February 2022, people-to-people cooperation with **Barentsburg** changed. - **LGBT community and allies.** **Longyearbyen Pride** was founded in **2019** by Lene Jeanette Dyngeland (she stepped down in 2024). Nearly 600 attended in 2019, including the governor, police leaders, firefighters, teachers, and priest Siv Limstrand. ### E. Cultural activities and civic life
**Church year and civic ritual (Kirke 2024):**
- **Solfesten, 8 March 2024:** after trying to "sing the sun forth" at the **old hospital steps** with the Polargospel choir, 200 people got **"solvafler"** (sun waffles) in the church's fireplace lounge.
- **1 May service with LO Svalbard** (the trade-union federation), a long-standing tradition, with the mixed choir **"Kullstrupene" — Longyearbyen Blandakor** (literally "the coal throats") and a speech by an MP.
- **17 May** service and parade, with Barentsburg children taking part.
- **Christmas Eve:** the church filled for two services, with a broadcast link to the Troll station in Antarctica followed by a greeting from the governor. At 22:00 there was a ring-dance around the tree on the town square, **"Christmas on the square"** (third year): about 100 people formed three rings singing acoustically "in ice-cold wind and drifting snow," people coming "out of apartments, houses, hotels and bars."
- **Christmas Day:** an international service, "Unto us a child is born," with readings in six or seven languages.
- **Pre-Christmas visits** by the priest with the governor to Barentsburg, Ny-Ålesund, Hopen, Bjørnøya and Hornsund ("an established and important tradition"). Spring and autumn helicopter visits to the Polish Hornsund station with a Catholic priest from Tromsø. Six Catholic masses in 2024.
- **Rites, 2024:** 99 services (127 events in all, 5,702 attendances); 5 weddings (3 outdoors); 7 memorial gatherings plus 2 funerals **streamed from the mainland** (there are no burials in town); 3 baptisms; 15 confirmands.
- **Children's and youth groups:** choirs **Polargospel** (ages 7–13, Wednesdays, 22 members) and **Polarknøttene** (4–7, Thursdays); **scouts** (25 members; trips to the church cabin and **Isfjord Radio**; three went to camp in Iceland); weekly **baby singing** (Thursdays) and **baby meet-ups** (Tuesdays, explicitly "to break isolation" and to puncture "the myth that there is no help to be had up here"); a weekly **healthy-living group** with walks, training and dog-yard visits for people at home in the daytime.
- **Confirmands** fundraise for Norwegian Church Aid (NOK 61,020 in 2024) and take a long weekend in Oslo to "get to know a larger and more complex Norway."
- **About 60 volunteers** served the church in 2024. "Volunteering and **dugnad** [communal work bees] play an important role in Longyearbyen, as social glue." The Svalbardbutikken Coop sponsors children's treats.
- **Crises in 2024:** a large fire in February left several families suddenly homeless, and an evacuation and next-of-kin center was opened. In September a permanent resident took his own life, "a shattering event." The church sits on the governor's emergency council and the local psychosocial crisis team.

#### E. Cultural activities and civic life (municipal fixed calendar; HE-WP רחובות)
- **February:** the Rehovot march and the Rehovot race.
- **Purim:** a municipal **Adloyada** parade.
- **Passover:** "Dancing Spring," a dance festival. **Mimouna** celebrations.
- Holocaust Remembrance Day and Memorial Day ceremonies.
- **Independence Day:** the family bike ride "**Sovev Rehovot VeHakifuha**" ("go round Rehovot").
- **June: Pride parade** (since 2018).
- **July:** the three-day evening **International Live Statues Festival** along Yaakov Street (round 1), and "**Sharim Lach Rehovot**," a public sing-along led by Shlomo Toledo.
- **August:** summer events in the parks. **Sukkot:** "celebrating in the sukkah."
- **October:** a night race.
- **Hanukkah:** an annual musical staged by the Bamat HaNoar youth.
- **Neighborhood rabbis** (2024): East, Kfar Gvirol, Kiryat Moshe and Sela, and Oshiyot, plus community rabbis for Chabad and for the Ethiopian community. The late chief rabbi Simcha HaKohen Kook (served 1972–2022) was regarded as acceptable to both Haredi and religious-Zionist circles. SOURCED: HE-WP.
