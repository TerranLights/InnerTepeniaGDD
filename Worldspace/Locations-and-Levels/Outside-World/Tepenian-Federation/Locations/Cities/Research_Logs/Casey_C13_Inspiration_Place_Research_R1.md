## 1 · #60 Kashgar (Kashi, Qeshqer), Xinjiang, China

**Claim to test:** "An oasis city where routes converged and a great market drew traders of many origins (K8, K4)."
**Verdict:** CONFIRMED, with two corrections. (1) The convergence is real and well documented: the northern and southern Tarim Basin routes rejoined at Kashgar at the foot of the Pamirs, and westward branches left from it for the Ferghana Valley and for Termez/Balkh. Today the same geometry is formalized as four land ports plus an air port within reach of the city. (2) "A great market" is true but now plural and changing: the famous Sunday market split into a daily bazaar and a separate livestock market on the city's edge, and the big central bazaars were demolished or rebuilt in 2022-2023 (reported by RFA). The "traders of many origins" part holds historically (Russian-Turkestan traders, British-Indian presence, consulates) and in a narrower present form (Uyghur, Tajik, Kyrgyz and Kazakh livestock sellers; a community of Pakistani traders from Gilgit-Baltistan).

### A. Snapshot
- County-level city in the Tarim Basin, southern Xinjiang, near the Kyrgyz and Tajik borders; elevation 1,270 m; 39°28′N 75°59′E. Seat of Kashgar Prefecture. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashgar (read 2026-11-21).
- **Population:** 782,662 (seventh census, 1 Nov 2020); 584,035 (2010) and 408,577 (2000) on the same boundaries; 2020 urban 392,730, rural 389,932. SOURCED (search return citing citypopulation.de / NBS; the Wikipedia infobox gives the same 782,662). Prefecture: about 4.50 million (2020). UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Ethnic makeup (2018):** Uyghur 85.8%, Han 13.5%, Hui 0.2%, Uzbek 0.2%, others 0.3%. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Kashgar, Demographics.
- **Climate:** cool arid (BWk); annual mean 12.6 °C; January mean −5.2 °C, July 25.9 °C; 85.2 mm precipitation a year; record −24.4 °C (1959) and 40.1 °C (1958); 215 frost-free days; 2,862.6 sunshine hours. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Kashgar, Climate. [P3]
- **Defining feature:** an irrigated oasis at the western end of the Taklamakan where the two desert-rim routes rejoin before the mountain passes; watered by the Kashgar River system and wells; the city stands on the south bank of the Tümen (Tuman) River, a 77.6 km tributary of the Kashgar River. SOURCED (Tuman River and Britannica text seen in search return; Wikipedia page lead). Partly UNVERIFIED: Britannica text seen in a snippet only.

### B. What else exists there
- **Economy and status.** Declared a Special Economic Zone in May 2010 (China's sixth; the first in the far west). Critics noted its distance from hubs and its farm economy. Kashgar Prefecture has the third-lowest GDP per head among Xinjiang's prefecture-level units. 2019 nominal GDP CN¥22.8 bn. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Kashgar (Economy). [K8]
- **Trade through ports (official figures).** Kashgar Prefecture has five first-class open ports: Hongqilafu (Khunjerab), Karasu, Torugart, Irkeshtam and Kashgar air port. Jan-Jul 2024 prefecture foreign trade CN¥63.1 bn (+50.1%); the Kashgar Comprehensive Bonded Zone (under 4 km²) CN¥35.89 bn (+64.7%), 56.9% of the prefecture's trade, first of Xinjiang's four bonded zones. Truck clearance from Kashgar to exit was cut "from 3-5 days to 6-8 hours" (official claim). Bonded-zone sectors: farm-product processing, textiles and apparel, electronics assembly, new energy, pharmaceuticals, equipment; services: logistics, cross-border e-commerce. The 14th China-Xinjiang Kashgar-Central and South Asia Commodity Fair was held 17-21 Aug 2024. SOURCED: chinanews.com/cj/2024/09-11/10284233.shtml (official media). Full-year 2024 prefecture trade CN¥99.44 bn (+18.8%): UNVERIFIED (search return only). [K5, K8]

Karakoram Highway to Islamabad over the Khunjerab Pass; Kyrgyzstan via Torugart or Irkeshtam passes; national highways G314 and G315. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Kashgar (Transport). The China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway (just over 500 km; ceremonial start in Kyrgyzstan late Dec 2024, full-scale works from June 2025, opening targeted 2030-31) begins at Kashgar. ### C. Cultural locations
- **Id Kah Mosque** (city center). Built 1442 by Saqsiz Mirza; rebuilt 1798; enlarged to its present size 1838; renovated 1981; façade tiled 2004-05. About 16,800 m²; capacity about 20,000 in the mosque and courtyard. Friday attendance: nearly 10,000 (2009), 4,000-5,000 (2011), 800-900 (2021). Mostly closed for worship since 2016, opening for elderly worshippers on select holy days; open to visitors at set hours. Imam Jume Tahir was killed on 30 July 2014; his successor was later jailed for 15 years (date disputed in sources). RFA reported the Quranic plaque removed (2018, 2020). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Id_Kah_Mosque. [K9] [P6]
- **Afaq Khoja Mausoleum** (Haohan/Ayziret village, about 5 km NE). Built about 1640 as the tomb of Naqshbandi Sufi master Muhammad Yusuf; his son Afaq Khoja buried there 1694; 72 members of five generations entombed; called the holiest Muslim site in the region; designated a tourist attraction; a tourist park opened beside it in 2015; the adjoining cemetery still used by Uyghurs. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afaq_Khoja_Mausoleum. [P6]

### D. How the defining feature shaped these things
- Route convergence made Kashgar the place where caravans from the desert rims re-formed for the passes; long-distance trade made it a city of consulates: the British consulate (Chini Bagh, 1890-1948 per Wikipedia; home of George Macartney for 28 years; host to Stein, Hedin, von Le Coq) and a Russian consulate whose courtyard filled with callers every morning (19th-century account). SOURCED: Wikipedia Kashgar and Chini-Bagh; Russian consulate detail UNVERIFIED (Digital Silk Road snippet). 19th-century Russian trade was "in the hands of several hundred natives of Russian Turkestan," with regular agencies between Kokand, Almaty and Kashgar. UNVERIFIED (Digital Silk Road / Toyo Bunko snippet). From 1880 to 1949 Chinese, Hindus, Armenians, Jews, Russians and Badakhshanis married local Uyghur women. SOURCED: Wikipedia, History of Kashgar. [K4]
- Earlier religious plurality: Marco Polo (c. 1273-74) recorded Nestorian Christians with their own churches; under the Kara-Khitans Kashgar was a Nestorian metropolitan see. Kashgar was for a time the Kara-Khanid capital after Satuq Bughra Khan's conversion (10th c.); destroyed 1514 and rebuilt with a new fort on the Tuman River; ruled by Yakub Beg from 1865 to 1877. SOURCED: Wikipedia, History of Kashgar. [P6]
- The modern port network, bonded zone, freight-rail plans and Shenzhen aid all build on the same position. The trade-market and livestock-market pattern (a weekly peak market fed from the surrounding oasis villages and the mountain pastoralists) follows from the oasis-and-pasture setting. ### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life) — including sensitive history
- **Old City demolition and rebuilding (K9).** Kashgar was named a national historic and cultural city in 1986. A 2001 reinforcement plan (CN¥600 m) stalled. After the May 2008 Sichuan earthquake, a "reconstruction" project launched in late February 2009: about 50,000 households (more than 200,000 people, roughly half the city) to be resettled; CN¥3 bn from central and regional governments over five years; official reasons were earthquake and fire risk, infrastructure, plus "economic development, ethnic unity, and the reinforcement of Xinjiang's borders." Residents told reporters of inadequate compensation and no consultation; a Kashgar relics official said preservation got too little attention. SOURCED: CECC analysis, 7 Apr 2009. Over 4,700 homes demolished in 2009 (CECC later report, UNVERIFIED snippet). About 85% of the old quarter demolished since 2008 (UHRP, Jun 2020); the remaining area leased to Beijing-based Zhongkun Investment Group and marketed as a "living Uyghur folk museum." On 31 Mar 2024 the regional legislature passed the Regulation on the Protection of the Ancient City of Kashgar (in force 1 May 2024), forbidding demolition or alteration of designated features and protecting crafts, customs and rituals; critics called it tourism-led. SOURCED: RFA, 28 Apr 2024. [K9] [SM: man versus society]
- **Detention and surveillance (K9, K6).** RFA (22 Jan 2018), citing an anonymous township security chief, reported about 120,000 Uyghurs held in "political re-education" camps in Kashgar Prefecture, with four camps in Kashgar city, the largest set up May 2017 at No. 5 Middle School. SOURCED (the figure is the source's, unverified by RFA). The OHCHR assessment (31 Aug 2022) found "serious human rights violations" and said arbitrary detention "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity"; China called the facilities "learning facilities established in accordance with law intended for de-radicalization" and rejected the findings. SOURCED: news.un.org/en/story/2022/08/1125932. ASPI (Sep 2020) estimated about 16,000 mosques destroyed or damaged across Xinjiang, with "more damage outside the urban centers of Urumqi and Kashgar"; China's foreign ministry said there are about 24,000 mosques. SOURCED: HKFP, 25 Sep 2020. Press reports (AFP, Sep 2023) describe a relaxation of checkpoints and body scans alongside a state-backed tourism push. UNVERIFIED (snippets; the full AFP pages were refused, see log).
- **Everyday behavior rules (K6). Yes, there are formal rules, set by the state, not local custom:**
  - *Xinjiang Regulations on De-Extremification* (in force 1 Apr 2017, region-wide; Kashgar included): forbid "abnormal" beards, robes covering the whole body and face, refusing state radio/TV, religious-only marriage, using halal "to interfere in others' secular life," keeping children out of state schools, and names that "exaggerate religious fervour." Enforcement mechanism not stated in the article. SOURCED: Al Jazeera, 1 Apr 2017.
  - *Minors and religion*: the 1993 regional measures implementing the minors' protection law forbid guardians to let minors take part in religious activity; the 2008 regional list of "23 illegal religious activities" includes letting students fast. Ramadan directives in Kashgar Prefecture counties (2011) ordered restaurants to stay open (Jiashi/Peyziwat; license revocation threatened in Zepu/Poskam), told teachers to visit students' homes to dissuade fasting (Yengisar) and barred students and teachers from religious venues. Enforced by county and township governments, education bureaus, schools, work units and inspection teams. SOURCED: CECC, 11 Oct 2011.
  - *Unwritten / customary*: meshrep is described by UNESCO as a setting where the host enforces moral standards (SOURCED above). Handshake-closed livestock deals (UNVERIFIED). No other customary house rules found.
- **Language (K11):** Uyghur (Turkic) is the majority's language; Mandarin is the state language and, per RFA/UHRP reporting, has displaced Uyghur as the language of instruction in schools; Tajik (Sarikoli/Wakhi), Kyrgyz and Urdu/Wakhi speech among border traders also present. Mostly UNVERIFIED (search returns).

## 2 · #61 Dunhuang (Shazhou), Gansu, China

**Claim to test:** "An oasis where the overland route split into northern and southern branches, the last supply point before the desert (K5)."
**Verdict:** CONFIRMED in substance, with refinements. (1) The split is documented: Dunhuang stood at the western mouth of the Hexi Corridor where the routes divided around the Tarim Basin; the Yumen Pass (Jade Gate, about 90 km NW, built 111 BC) controlled the northern route and Yangguan (Yang Pass, SW; distance given as 70-110 km by different sources) the southern; Wikipedia adds that under the Sui and Tang it was the intersection of all three main routes, and that by the 6th century caravans favored a northern route through Hami. (2) "Last supply point before the desert" is supported by secondary sources (travelers loaded water and food there before the Lop Nur crossing; a prefect of Dunhuang supplied the pilgrim Faxian with the means to cross) but no primary source read this round states it in those words; it is marked UNVERIFIED. The strongest SOURCED evidence of Dunhuang's supply function is the Han relay station Xuanquanzhi (below).

### A. Snapshot
- County-level city in NW Gansu under Jiuquan; area 31,200 km²; elevation about 1,140 m; seat Shazhou Town; county-level city since 1988; nine towns, 56 village-level units. **Population 185,231 (2020 census); 186,027 (2010).** SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunhuang.
- **Climate:** cool arid (BWk); January mean −8.3 °C; July 24.6 °C; annual precipitation given as 67 mm in the text and 44.6 mm in the climate table; about 3,260-3,300 sunshine hours, among China's sunniest. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Dunhuang. [P3]
- **Defining feature:** a river oasis (Dang River) between the Kumtag Desert and the Qilian foothills, at the point where the corridor road divides to go round the Tarim Basin.

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Supply and control:** the Han garrison and commandery system (Dunhuang Commandery c. 104 BC; Yumen 111 BC) and the relay-station network existed to feed, mount and pass officials and envoys on the road west; ordinary private traders do not appear as routine guests in the Xuanquan records (UNVERIFIED). Population was moved in by the state to hold the oasis, repeatedly (SOURCED, Gansu Daily).
- **Medieval associations (K1, K6, K10):** Dunhuang manuscripts preserve nearly 500 documents of grassroots associations (*she* 社/*yi* 邑), late 9th-10th c. Each association had three officers (president, manager, secretary) and a written regulation; the founding "great regulation" was **sealed and could be opened only for a dispute the members could not settle**; "separate ordinances" handled small matters. **Circulars** were passed hand to hand: each member wrote "understood" or put an ink dot by his name and passed it on; the last returned it to the sender; circulars stated **penalties for being late, not coming, or being slow to pass the notice on**, and the office marked who arrived with his offering. Activities: **funeral mutual aid** (the most important in practice), spring and autumn banquets, Buddhist feasts, lamp-lighting and sand-printing of Buddhas; associations kept common stores (noodles, oil, millet, wheat, jute) that members could borrow, and records of fines. There were women's associations (regulation of 959, S.527) and **"canal people" and canal associations**: commoners responsible for flood prevention and repairing canal dams and bridges, called out by circular. ## 3 · #62 Jiayuguan (Jiayu Pass and Jiayuguan City), Gansu, China

**Claim to test:** "A fortified pass remembered as the last gate before leaving for the open west (K9)."
**Verdict:** CONFIRMED, and widened. (1) The memory is documented: the fort was the westernmost pass of the Ming Great Wall at the narrowest point of the western Hexi Corridor; banished people went west through its outer gate; in the 1920s Mildred Cable found the archway covered with writing by exiles and disgraced officials and recorded popular names for the gate (moved to the slang file under the language caution). (2) It was also a working **border checkpoint**: Ming officials counted and registered foreign embassies on entry and checked every name against that register on exit (Timurid embassy, 1420/1422). (3) One correction: the usual founding date (1372) is contested by recent scholarship (Zhou Song, 2020), which argues for 1390-94. (4) The modern city is a separate, newer thing: a steel company town founded on the Gobi from 1958, which is now the place's main fact.

### A. Snapshot
- **The pass:** about 6 km SW of the city between two hills at the narrowest point of the western Hexi Corridor; trapezoid fort, perimeter 733 m, over 33,500 m², walls 11 m high; east and west gates with gate buildings; inner fort, outer fort and moat; described as the most intact surviving military building among the Great Wall passes; part of the Great Wall World Heritage Site. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiayu_Pass; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiayuguan_City.
- **The city:** prefecture-level city (county-level 1965, prefecture-level 1971), formed from parts of Jiuquan County and Sunan Yugur Autonomous County, and named after the pass. Area 1,224 km². **Population 312,663 (2020); 231,853 (2010)**; sex ratio 108.02 (2010: 114.59); 2020 ages 0-14: 49,732, 15-64: 227,996, 65+: 34,935. Two urban subdistricts (Xiongguan 167,883; Gangcheng — literally "steel city" — 130,590) and three small towns (Xincheng 6,632; Wenshu 4,134; Yuquan 3,424). SOURCED: Wikipedia, Jiayuguan City; citypopulation.de (NBS 2020 census). About 315,000 residents (2026). SOURCED: Gansu Daily via ifeng, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Climate:** cool arid (BWk); January daily mean about −16 to −2 °C; July about 15 to 29 °C; about 86 mm precipitation a year, almost no winter snow (Wikipedia); official figures 85 mm rainfall against 2,149 mm evaporation, a "seriously desertified" area (chinanews 2019). SOURCED. [P3]
- **Defining feature:** a narrow gap (about 15 km between the Qilian range and the Black Mountains) that any east-west traffic must pass; the Yuquan spring supplied men, horses and fields. SOURCED: The Paper, 20 Mar 2023 (Jiayuguan Culture and Tourism Group).

## 4 · #93 Lanzhou, Gansu, China

**Claim to test:** "A river city at the mouth of the corridor west, where rail lines and roads meet and split, and whose everyday travelers' noodle dish became known across the whole country (K12, K5)."
**Verdict:** CONFIRMED with two corrections. (1) River and junction: true. Lanzhou lies on the Yellow River; it was the Han seat of Jincheng commandery (81 BC) on the Northern Silk Road; today the Longhai (east), Lanzhou-Xinjiang (west), Lanzhou-Qinghai (to Xining and Lhasa), Baotou-Lanzhou (north) and Lanzhou-Chongqing (south) railways meet there, plus high-speed lines at Lanzhou West. The Hexi Corridor proper begins farther west, at Wushaoling ridge; Lanzhou is the river crossing and junction before it. (2) The noodle: Lanzhou beef noodles are an everyday **breakfast** dish of the city's residents (shops open before dawn), not specifically a travelers' dish. And the "Lanzhou lamian" shops found across China are, by Wikipedia's account and Chinese press, mostly a different, Qinghai (Hualong) halal-style noodle sold under the Lanzhou name since 1988; there are also many Linxia-run shops. Who made the dish "known across the whole country" is therefore partly migrants from neighboring Hui and Dongxiang areas.

### A. Snapshot
- Capital and largest city of Gansu; on the Yellow River; elevation about 1,517-1,600 m. **Population (2020 census): 4,359,446 (prefecture-level city), urban area 3,474,858**; first time above 4 million. Area 13,086.9 km² (prefecture). Main groups: Han, Hui, Tibetan (no shares given). Climate semi-arid; mean 10.9 °C; 309 mm rain, almost all May-October. GDP 2020 CN¥209.6 bn (CN¥56,972 per head). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanzhou.
- **Defining feature:** a narrow Yellow River valley ringed by mountains (which also traps winter smog), at the crossing point between the eastern loess lands, the Qinghai-Tibet plateau road, the Ningxia/Inner Mongolia road and the Hexi Corridor west.

### C. Cultural locations

"The Lanzhou lamian commonly found outside Lanzhou is not the same dish." SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanzhou_beef_noodles. **Breakfast rhythm:** in 2010 over 700,000 people a day ate beef noodles for breakfast in a city of about 3.6 million; about 780 noodle shops in the four urban districts; annual sales about CN¥700 m. ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The river made the city a crossing (pontoon bridge, then the 1909 iron bridge), a river-transport point (skin rafts downstream), and an irrigated valley (waterwheels). The junction position made it the hub of the 1950s rail network into the northwest and the site of First Five-Year Plan heavy industry. The Hui and Dongxiang presence in and around the city tied the everyday food to halal practice, and the migrant networks from Hualong and Linxia carried a "Lanzhou" noodle out along the same routes.

## 5 · #59 Caravanserais and Anatolian hans (e.g., Sultanhanı)

**This pick is a tradition/institution.** Researched as: the institution itself (Seljuk road caravanserais; Ottoman road and city hans; the Persian caravanserai as a parallel inscribed system), then three well-documented places: **(a) Sultan Han, Sultanhanı (Aksaray)**, the named example; **(b) Karatay Han (Kayseri)**, because its endowment deed survives and shows how one actually ran; **(c) Koza Han (Bursa)** and **Büyük Valide Han (Istanbul)**, the city hans where the institution became a trading and living house. Ibn Battuta's Akhi lodges are added as the urban hospitality institution that worked alongside the road hans.

**Claim to test:** "Fortified roadside inns spaced along caravan routes, where people, animals and news rested together (K4, K5)."
**Verdict:** CONFIRMED and sharpened. (1) Fortified: yes; Seljuk hans were built like castles, with cut-stone walls and buttress towers, and also served as army quarters, lodgings for foreign rulers, and at times prisons or refuges. (2) Spaced: yes, at about one day's march; sources give 30-40 km (Osman Turan) or about 40 km (UNESCO), and one study warns the day's-march rule does not fit everywhere. (3) "Rested together": yes and literally; most travelers slept beside their animals in the covered hall or stables, prestigious guests in cells. (4) What the claim leaves out: the road hans were **charitable endowments (vakıf)** giving **three days of free lodging, food and fodder to every traveler**, with a farrier, veterinarian, cobbler and (as needed) doctor on the endowment's payroll; and the state reportedly **compensated merchants from the treasury** for goods lost to robbery. "News" resting there: NOT FOUND as a documented function (no source read addresses information exchange specifically).

### A. The institution (snapshot)
- **Terms:** *kervansaray*, *han* and *ribat* are used in inscriptions and sources; *han* later meant an urban lodging-and-trade building; *ribat* originally a frontier fort. Earliest Turkish example given: Ribat-i Mahi (1019-20, near Mashhad). SOURCED: Şebnem Akalın Eryavuz, "Kervansaray," *TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi* vol. 25 (2022), islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/kervansaray.
- **Anatolian Seljuk building wave:** from the late 12th c., when Anatolia became a center of international trade; sultans and statesmen built hans along main and secondary routes; the "sultan han" plan (open courtyard in front, a vaulted 3-, 5- or 7-aisle covered hall behind, often a small central dome; side rooms, a *mescit* or bath) first at Alay Han (completed 1192); nine surviving sultan-built hans; large-han building stopped in the second half of the 13th c.; under Mongol rule hans became smaller, often courtyard-less, rubble-built. SOURCED: TDV "Kervansaray." Totals: "roughly 300" (Cambridge chapter) or "over 200" (dissertation proposal); Duvar English (2024) says over 250 Seljuk-era caravanserais in Turkey. UNVERIFIED except the Duvar figure (SOURCED, read). Kurt Erdmann's 1950s field survey (volumes 1961, 1976) is the foundational architectural study. UNVERIFIED (search return).
- **Spacing:** "nearly every *menzil* (about 30-40 km)" — Osman Turan, "Selçuk Kervansarayları," *Belleten* (Türk Tarih Kurumu; read from the saved PDF; the article cites works to 1941; publication year not printed in the extracted text, NOT VERIFIED). About 40 km, the most a camel or mule went in a day (UNESCO Silk Roads text, UNVERIFIED, page redirected). Archnet gives 30 km. A study of Ayyubid/Mamluk Syria says the day's-march theory does not fit there. UNVERIFIED (search return).
- **Ottoman period:** caravanserais were built mostly in towns, in trade districts or within *külliye* complexes; the state founded *menzil* complexes to settle routes; typical two-story arcaded courtyard with rooms having platforms and hearths, stables, storage and offices around; some had shops on the facade or a *bedesten*; military caravanserais (e.g., Ulukışla, 1616-19); they lost importance as international trade declined; some Seljuk hans (e.g., Karatay) became dervish lodges. SOURCED: TDV "Kervansaray." Road security was also kept by the *derbend* organization: semi-civilian guard communities at passes and on roads, in groups of 30, tax-exempt in return for the duty, developed from an Ilkhanid system; some people sought the status only for the exemption. UNVERIFIED (search return: Özcan 2018, Orhonlu 1990).
- **The Persian parallel:** UNESCO inscribed "The Persian Caravanserai" in 2023 (ref. 1668): 54 caravanserais (two of 56 proposed left out), criteria (ii) and (iii). SOURCED: Wikipedia, The Persian Caravanserai (UNESCO page itself refused, 403). Shah Abbas I (from 1588) ordered a network of caravanserais; road security declined after the 1660s; Tavernier described routes kept in good order with guard posts close enough to relay alarms. UNVERIFIED (search return: RFE/RL; Lisbon CHAM volume).

### B. What went on inside (who, work, daily life)
- **Free hospitality:** travelers were guests of the founder for three days and paid nothing; lodging, meals, bath, mosque, pharmacy; free when needed: a doctor; free shoes for poor travelers; fodder; a farrier; a veterinarian; cart repair. SOURCED: TDV "Kervansaray." Turan: the hans held dormitories, kitchens, food stores, goods depots, stables, straw lofts, mosque, bath, fountains, a hospital and even pharmacies, a cobbler to mend shoes and make new ones for the poor, farriers, and a *divan* (office) with officials to run income and spending; "merchants and every kind of traveler, rich or poor, could have all their needs met free." SOURCED (Belleten PDF). [K1, K5] [R: rest, repair and shared-store place]
- **The Karatay Han endowment deed (645 AH / 1247 CE), the best-documented case (Turan, Belleten):** a three-person board (*mütevelli*, *müşrif*/inspector, *nâzır*) ran it, one-sixth of income going to the founder's family as trustees; the inspector paid 500 dirhams and 50 *mudd* of wheat a year. Staff and annual pay: imam 200 dirhams and muezzin 150 (plus 24 mudd wheat each); a *muzîf* (head of guest reception, food and lodging) 200; a *hancı* (stable and animals) 150, raised to 200 in an addendum of 646; a stores officer 200; a **permanent veterinarian** 100; a mounted officer for the han's estates 100; a skilled cook 200; the village bath-keeper 120; a **cobbler** 100 plus leather, and nails and horseshoes as needed. **Every traveler — "Muslim or non-Muslim, free or slave" — was to receive equally, each day, about 1 kg of bread, 250 g of cooked meat and a bowl of food; honey halva was made every Friday evening and shared equally among all travelers**; barley and straw for the animals; kitchenware to be bought (50 large bowls, 20 copper plates, 100 large and 50 small wooden bowls, copper pots and two cauldrons); sick poor travelers to be treated with medicine and drinks until well. Turan notes an Egyptian author's contrary report that guests were treated by rank. SOURCED (Belleten PDF, read). [K1, K6, K12] [P7] [R: shared energy/rest ritual — the Friday halva]

SOURCED (Belleten PDF). [SM: a dangerous or fascinating system]
- **Who slept where (K4):** most travelers slept alongside their animals in the stables; prestigious travelers had cells. UNVERIFIED (UNESCO text via search return). ### D. How the defining feature shaped these things
- Spacing by a day's travel fixed where villages, guard posts and later towns grew (Sultanhanı and Karadayı are villages named after or wrapped around their hans). The endowment model meant each han was a small economy (estates, staff wages in coin and wheat, kitchens, a cobbler and vet) whose purpose was to keep traffic and trade moving; when trade shifted away (after the 16th c.), hans turned into dervish lodges, villages, ruins, or, in cities, permanent trading houses and workshops with resident communities (Iranians in Istanbul, silk dealers in Bursa).

## 6 · #109 Nizhny Novgorod Fair (Makaryev Fair), Russia

**Claim to test:** "A great seasonal fair at the meeting of two rivers, where traders from many directions gathered during the navigation season (K2, K8)." Care note: dates of its peak to check.
**Verdict:** CONFIRMED, with dates corrected. (1) Place: the fair stood on the low left bank of the Oka at its confluence with the Volga (the Strelka/Kunavino side, opposite the old city). (2) Season: officially from 15 July to 25 August (Old Style; flags raised and lowered), with trade often running into early September, i.e., the height of the navigation season. (3) Traders: Russians, Europeans, Armenians, Persians, Bukharans, Khivans, Tashkent traders, Tatars, and Chinese tea via Kyakhta. (4) **Peak:** the move to Nizhny was in 1817 (first trading in the new stone bazaar 15 July 1822); deliveries grew from about 36 million rubles (1817-26 average) and 57.2 million (1846) to 246 million (1881); the 1851 railway to Moscow is credited with opening the heyday of the second half of the 19th century; Fitzpatrick finds turnover still rising into the 1880s while the fair lost relative importance; a delivery figure of 195 million (1912) is also reported. (5) **Closure (verified, not taken from the brief):** trade slumped in the First World War; on 17 Jan 1918 the Nizhny Novgorod Soviet attached the fair grounds to the city and abolished the fair committee; revived under the NEP 1922-1929 (turnover 31 → 300 million rubles; over 2,500 firms in 1928); the last fair was held in **1929**; the fair was **abolished by a decree of 6 February 1930** in the campaign to end the NEP. The issuing body of the 1930 decree was NOT FOUND.

### A. Snapshot
- **Origins:** a fair near the Makaryev (Zheltovodsky) Monastery on the left bank of the Volga from the mid-16th c. (official founding by decree of June 1641, when the monastery got the right to collect duty; at first a one-day fair on 25 July, St Macarius' day); foreign merchants by 1666; delivery about 30 million rubles by the end of the 17th c. (as given). After a fire on 16 (28) Aug 1816 destroyed the bazaar (loss over 2 million rubles), it moved to Nizhny Novgorod. SOURCED: ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Нижегородская_ярмарка (read 2026-11-21); 1641 decree detail UNVERIFIED (znanierussia snippet). Construction started 15 Feb 1817, 6 million rubles allotted, directed by Agustín de Betancourt; site nearly 8 km². SOURCED: ru.wikipedia.
- **Scale:** 60 blocks with 2,530 shops; up to 200,000 visitors a year when the city had under 20,000 people (ru.wikipedia, SOURCED); "about 200,000 people traveled to sell goods" (Harvard Imperiia, SOURCED). A 1905 visitor counted 60 buildings, 2,500 bazaars, 8,000 exhibits (en.wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** a temporary city of trade that existed for about six weeks a year on low ground at a river confluence, emptying for the rest of the year.

### B. What else existed there (economy, institutions, infrastructure, people)
- **Goods:** cotton goods, tea, metals, woolen and silk goods (ru.wikipedia, SOURCED); 13 million pounds of tea passed through in 1856, carried from Kyakhta near Lake Baikal; Russian tea merchants were the largest buyers and paid in wool, cloth and cotton, so their tea prices set most other prices; Ural iron was unloaded on a sand island and taken by tramway to the railway; the "Bourse" traded everything but grain (Harvard Imperiia, citing Henry Johnstone's 1876 journal; SOURCED). The tea monopoly weakened after trade with Chinese merchants was allowed on 30 Apr 1861 (ru.wikipedia, SOURCED). [K8]
- **Persian trade:** in the 1870s the fair received on average up to 95% of all Persian goods brought to Russia (dried fruit, cotton, rice, carpets) and shipped Russian textiles, metals, furs, leather, wool, grain; one Persian grocery firm moved 553,000 poods through the grounds in 1914; Russian goods to Persia 32,000 poods (1911), 53,000 (1914); Persian merchants expanded after the 1860s cotton crisis; the Caspian shipping monopoly of the "Kavkaz i Merkuriy" company delayed deliveries (an 1889 complaint by an Isfahan merchant). SOURCED: Kornoukhova and Tsareva, *RUDN Journal of World History* 13/2 (2021): 167-176 (based on the Archive of Foreign Policy of the Russian Empire). [K5, K9]
- **Tatar merchants (K4, K11):** Tatar was used at the fair alongside Russian, German and French, and some fair orders were translated into Tatar and posted; Tatars held a near-monopoly in down and feather, sold "Asian" footwear and "Tatar wares," and were strong in carpets, leather goods, haberdashery, cotton, headwear, tea and sugar; Tatar owners ran ordinary (not first-class) lodging rooms; a Kazan merchant ran one of three commission agencies at the fair; a Tatar firm sold Tatar-language books there from 1875; merchants funded a Muslim primary school and an imam's house by the fair mosque (1,000 rubles each from several merchants, early 1900s); in the mid-1880s Tatar merchants trading in Tashkent asked the fair committee to petition against the post-1866 rule limiting property purchase in Central Asia to Orthodox Christians and natives; the petition failed. SOURCED: N. I. Tairov, "Tatar entrepreneurs at all-Russian fairs," *Uchenye zapiski Kazanskogo universiteta* 150/1 (2008) (read from the saved PDF).
- **Infrastructure (K1, K5):** a 100 m-wide bypass canal (Betancourt's) and a 3.5 m raised base against floods; a 510 m pontoon bridge over the Oka; **two underground sewer galleries 640 m long, the first in Russia**; four "Chinese rows" for Asian traders (tea the main good); an Armenian church (round, 21.5 m) and a mosque (decree 1825); the Spassky Cathedral (consecrated 25 Jul 1822) and the Alexander Nevsky Cathedral on the Strelka (1868-81). SOURCED: ru.wikipedia. The main fair house had a 3.5 m flood dam; rebuilt in the late 1880s-1890 in Russian Revival style. SOURCED: en.wikipedia (it credits Montferrand). Railway to Moscow 1851. SOURCED (ru.wikipedia).
- **Administration and order (K1, K6):** from strict state management after 1817 the fair grew into an institution with some local autonomy; by 1890 at least three elected bodies (including an Exchange Committee and a General Meeting of fair traders) and municipal services. UNVERIFIED (abstract of Fitzpatrick, ch. "The Nizhnii Novgorod Fair Administration," via RePEc). A fair office monitored trade and turnover and its reports fed the governor's and ministers' reports. UNVERIFIED (search return). **Policing:** the province lacked police for the fair, so Cossack units were sent each season; a commission with the Interior Ministry then created mounted police constables for the fair under a written instruction setting their rights and duties. SOURCED: Akhmedov, *Vestnik of the St Petersburg University of the MIA* 2021/2: 27-32 (abstract read; body not available). City police and Cossacks were sent in 1817 and 1820. UNVERIFIED.
- **Kunavino, the host suburb (K4, K10, K7):** a ferry settlement on the Oka; 32 households in 1621; it suffered from Oka floods and was granted a marsh outlet in 1624 "for pasture and as refuge during the high water"; boats bound up the Volga were masted, rigged and provisioned there, and crews celebrated a safe passage there. In 1817 about 400 houses; with the fair, residents rented houses to visiting merchants as lodgings, taverns and inns at high prices; before the railway **Kunavino was "quiet and deserted" for about ten months a year**, lively only in fair time; a 6-7 room furnished flat rented for 3 rubles a month off-season but brought 300-500 rubles for the two fair months; after the railway it was busy year-round. In the mid-1860s it had 515 houses, 30 shops, 10 taverns and hotels, 30 drinking houses, 5 bakeries, 2 public baths, 3 guardhouses, 100 cabmen, and about 4,914 permanent residents. Local holidays: two processions with an icon credited with ending the 1771 plague; on the second Sunday of Lent townspeople spent the day in Kunavino, with dog-baiting and goose- and cock-fighting; fist-fighting had died out. A merchant founded the parish school in 1825 (about 80 pupils by 1865). An Armenian section in the Kunavino cemetery. SOURCED: N. Khramtsovsky, "Historical sketch of Kunavino and its present state," *Nizhegorodskie gubernskie vedomosti* 1865 (reprint, Nizhny Novgorod library; read locally from the saved .doc). [K2, K10] [R: off-season emptiness, seasonal rest]
- **Entertainment:** hotels, taverns, restaurants, baths, a theater and a circus were built for visitors; wooden show booths with traveling troupes; later "mechanical and electric theaters," among Russia's first cinemas; horse racing. UNVERIFIED (culture.ru and putevka snippets; culture.ru page failed to load). Kunavino had many brothels in the 19th c.; local historian figures give 900-1,000 officially registered prostitutes in the whole city; an uncheckable claim attributed to Dumas (1858) is repeated. UNVERIFIED (popular article, sobaka.ru, snippet). [K6, K9]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The confluence put the fair where Volga, Oka and (from 1851) rail traffic met; the low ground forced the canal, the raised base, the flood dam and the sewer galleries; the six-week season produced a twin settlement (Kunavino) that lived on rents for two months and idled for ten, plus an apparatus brought in only for the season (Cossacks, then mounted constables). Asian trade routes produced dedicated quarters (Chinese rows, a mosque, an Armenian church) and multilingual notices.
