# Town design research digest, physical side: above and below a polar ice plateau · 2026-11-21

**What this is.** A digest of eight real-world research logs in this folder, written for a design team. The team is designing a town of about 7,000 residents on a high polar ice plateau: about 2,835 m up, on about 2,850 m of ice. The ice drifts about 10 m a year, about 75 mm of snow a year lands and stays, cold and altitude (not wind) are the hazards, the year is six months of night and six of day, everything is supplied from outside, and the town exists to keep one very large remote technical installation running. The team wants two settings, one above the surface and one below it, fundamentally different from each other. This digest says what the real world shows about building, running and living in each.

**Rules kept.** Real world only. Every fact keeps the mark it has in its source file: **SOURCED** (an opened page, URL given where the source file gives one), **UNVERIFIED** (search snippet, secondary page, or unresolved), **DERIVED** (arithmetic or plain inference on sourced figures, method shown), **NOT FOUND**. No mark is upgraded. Where a source file gives no URL for a SOURCED item, this digest says "URL not given in the file." Nothing is added from memory; gaps go to section 9. Places are not ranked against each other except inside tables of facts. No design is recommended. "What it causes" chains are given only where the files' facts support them, each step marked.

## 1 · Living and building ON the surface of a polar plateau / ice sheet

### 1.1 The site itself (South Pole as the closest match)
- **Elevation and ice.** About 2,835 m (NSF station page, https://www.nsf.gov/geo/opp/support/southp.jsp) or 2,836 m (met handout, https://amrdc.ssec.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/met-handout.pdf); both **SOURCED** [A2] §1.1. Ice under the IceCube array "approximately 2,880 m" by radar, **SOURCED** (Hills et al. 2022, https://benhills.github.io/uploads/Hills2022_GRL.pdf) [A2] §1.2; COMNAP gives "about 2,700 m," **SOURCED** (https://static1.squarespace.com/static/61073506e9b0073c7eaaf464/t/611497cc1ece1b43f0eeca8a/1628739608968/COMNAP_Antarctic_Station_Catalogue.pdf) [A2] §6.7. Conflict, see section 10.
- **Ice motion.** "10 m a⁻¹ in the direction of 40° W," **SOURCED** (Fudge et al. 2020, https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/16/819/2020/); a mean 9.3 m/yr rising "from 8.5 to 10.3 m/yr in the direction of flow" over one survey area, **SOURCED** (Hills 2022); rate "slightly erratic from year to year," **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/pole/survey.html). All [A2] §1.3. The geographic marker is relocated every 1 January because the ice and everything on it moves, **SOURCED** (USAP news, https://www.usap.gov/news/4561/) [A2] §1.3, [C2] §5.7.
- **Accumulation.** "Average Annual Snow Accumulation: 9 inches (23 cm); Average Annual Liquid Equivalent: 3.4 inches (8.6 cm)," **SOURCED** (met handout); 84.5 ± 8.9 mm water equivalent a year in 1992–97, about 30% above the 1960s, **SOURCED** (https://www.usgs.gov/publications/late-20th-century-increase-south-pole-snow-accumulation); about 8.7 cm/yr and 0.080 m/yr ice equivalent, **SOURCED** (Hills 2022; Fudge 2020). All [A2] §1.4. No melt is recorded: the all-time high is −12.3 °C, **SOURCED** (https://cimss.ssec.wisc.edu/satellite-blog/archives/9445); "with no thawing," **UNVERIFIED**. [A2] §1.4–1.5.
- **Drift, not snowfall, is what buries things.** "Drifts still build up around objects on the surface," **SOURCED** (CIRES 2019, URL not given in the file) [A2] §2.2. Blowing snow starts cutting visibility above 15 knots and whiteout usually comes above about 25 knots; the average wind is 10.7 knots, peak gust 48 knots, **SOURCED** (met handout) [A2] §1.6.
- **Temperature.** Daily means about −27 to −28 °C in December–January and a flat −57 to −60 °C from April through September; record low −82.8 °C (June 1982), **SOURCED to Wikipedia only (secondary)** for the monthly table, and the record low also **SOURCED** (met handout). "The temperature often increases by over 25 °C in the lowest few hundred meters" in winter (surface inversion), **SOURCED** (met handout). [A2] §1.5.

**Chain (burial by drift).** 1st: drift around objects, not the 23 cm of open-field snow, buries structures (**SOURCED**, CIRES, [A2] §2.2). 2nd: at the Pole, "snow around the major buildings is close to two stories above the 2007 level" (**SOURCED**, Polar Journal, https://polarjournal.net/problems-at-the-pole/, [A2] §2.3). **DERIVED:** two stories at the design's 3.7 m story height ([A2] §2.3) is about 7.4 m over about 17 years (2007 to the 2024 article), about 0.44 m a year around buildings, against 0.23 m a year of open-field accumulation. 3rd: "snow hauling is a near full-time job" (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi, https://brr.fyi/posts/south-pole-topography, [A2] §2.3), and at the old Dome the hauled snow was "burning up precious fuel and crew time" (**SOURCED**, NSF 2010, https://phys.org/news/2010-03-dome-home.pdf, [A2] §2.2).

### 1.2 Structures: what has been built on the surface, generation by generation
- **Ground-level huts and tunnels (1956–57, "Old Pole").** Prefabricated buildings linked by tunnels, delivered by airdrop, **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/igy1/igy1.html) [A2] §2.1. "Wind-blown snow began covering it after several years," **SOURCED** (NSF 2022, https://www.nsf.gov/science-matters/how-human-presence-south-pole-has-evolved-over-past-century). By 1974–75 the crew lived "20 to 30 feet below the snow surface," **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/history/tunnel.html). Demolished about 2010 for safety, **SOURCED** (NSF 2022). [A2] §2.1. **DERIVED:** 20–30 ft (6–9 m) over about 18 years (1957–1975) is about 0.34–0.51 m a year.
- **A shell over buildings (1975 Dome).** A geodesic dome "164 feet wide" (NSF 2022) or 50 m wide and 16 m high (NSF station page), conflict noted, both **SOURCED**; modules inside for about 30 people; steel arches outside for garage, fuel and power plant, **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/history/dome/dome2.html). Designed for snow load, winds of about 100 mph and −80 °F, **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/drygulch/drygulch.html). [A2] §2.2.
- **Raised building on jackable columns (2008 elevated station).** "Shaped as an airfoil and sits on elevating columns. It faces the wind, which compresses and accelerates air to scour away snow. The columns can lift it two additional stories," **SOURCED** (NSF 2022) [A2] §2.3. Foundation detail, **SOURCED** (engineers' article reprinted at https://www.southpolestation.com/foundationdesign.html) [A2] §2.3, [B2] §4.1: 910 mm columns on steel box-girder grade beams bearing on snow; columns 5.3 m, 12.8 m at full height; "designed to be jacked twice, each time a full story height of 3.7 m," in 250 mm increments, "using four 57 mm rods at each foundation column," the whole structure or pod by pod; design bearing pressure 48 kPa long-term; the snow pad is built "by backfilling snow in 300 mm lifts and driving over it with front-end loaders," compacted to 0.55–0.60 g/cm³; leveling by hydraulic jacks and steel shims; predicted 50-year settlement about 840 mm. Two pods (A and B) joined by an elevated walkway, B-Pod able to serve as an emergency "lifeboat," **SOURCED** (USAP webcam page, URL not given in the file) [A2] §2.3. A 45-year theoretical life and a 2-inch limit between columns, **UNVERIFIED** (CRREL 2006 report not opened) [A2] §2.3.
- **Plateau neighbors on legs.** New Vostok complex: 140 × 13.5 m, walls insulated 80 cm, "on 36 supports, each 3 m high," **SOURCED** (AARI, https://aari.ru/science-and-projects/new-wintering-vostok-station) [A2] §6.4. Neumayer III (ice shelf): building on a platform "21 m above the ice," 16 hydraulic columns, "the whole station raised hydraulically," annual lift 80–100 cm, **SOURCED** (IPY, https://www.ipy.org/news-a-announcements/item/2133-germanys-antarctic-neumayer-station-iii-starts-scientific-operation; Structurae, https://structurae.net/en/structures/neumayer-station-iii) [A3] §4.5. Halley VI on hydraulic legs with skis, moved 23 km in 2016–17, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §6.2, [B2] §4.3. Summit Station (Greenland): science buildings sledded between locations "to limit drifting and scouring," the Mobile Science Facility leveled to within 0.5 degree after each move, **SOURCED** (geo-summit.org, URL not given in the file) [B2] §4.2; a 1989 building "elevated to minimize snow drifts," **SOURCED** (Wikipedia) [B2] §4.2.
- **Surface-scale settlement with many buildings.** CFS Alert (on rock, not ice): "approximately 90 buildings, with some 19 major structures," about 22,500 m², **SOURCED** (statement of work, https://canadabuys.canada.ca/documents/pub/att/2021/04/20/994ff5f23c52a5e8976207d32d987f82/alert_sow_en.pdf) [A3] §4.6. The Pole has "about 47 buildings," **SOURCED** (NSF station page) [A2] §2.4. Station areas are divided into sectors (Dark, Clean Air, Quiet, Downwind), **UNVERIFIED** except that the Dark Sector is kept "free of both light and Electromagnetic Interference" about 1 km away, **SOURCED** (southpole.era.gs, URL not given in the file) [A2] §4.7.

**Chain (raise or be buried).** 1st: the elevated station was designed to be jacked up two stories (**SOURCED**). 2nd: designers planned to raise or move buildings periodically, which "has never happened"; NSF began addressing raising in 2023, work not before 2025 (**SOURCED**, Polar Journal) [A2] §2.3. 3rd: stairwell bottoms and the main entrance stairs are buried and vault hatches are extended "multiple times" as snow rises (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi topography) [A2] §2.3. Any actual post-construction jacking, its crew size or days: **NOT FOUND** [B2] §4.1.

### 1.3 Materials and temperature limits on the surface (detail in section 7)
- Tape and adhesives "lose effectiveness in the cold," **SOURCED** (Blue Ribbon Panel 2012, https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/files/antarctica-07232012.pdf) [B2] §4.1.
- "High-strength steels generally become brittle as temperature drops"; most aluminum and stainless alloys do not; plastics should not take vibration or blows below −20 °C; zinc galvanizing on wire rope can crack below −20 °C, **SOURCED** (CRREL TR-04-6, URL not given in the file) [C4] §7.3.
- Plasticized PVC cable "becomes stiff below −40 °C," silicone rubber rated to −60 °C, PTFE to −200 °C, **SOURCED but VENDOR** (SEA Wire, URL not given in the file) [C4] §7.3.

### 1.4 What fails on the surface (record)
- The Dome sank: "each year the structure sunk deeper into the ice," and its entrances were covered by drift by the 1990s, **SOURCED** (NSF 2010; NSF 2022) [A2] §2.2. Ring beams broke in 1988, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §2.2.
- The elevated station settled "nearly four times faster than originally predicted" at one stage, **UNVERIFIED** (Wired 2002 snippet) [A2] §2.3.
- Outdoor storage gets buried: bulk items on elevated snow berms need "substantial maintenance ... annually to keep the berms free of snow ... and locate items," **SOURCED** (BRP 2012) [B2] §4.1; backlogged material on berms "is being buried ('bermageddon')"; buried at the Pole over about 70 years: "the original station, construction equipment, and a cargo plane," **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §2.4; cargo "buried by wind" when aircraft "could not fly to the South Pole station in 2021 and 2022," **SOURCED** (https://polarjournal.net/waste-disposal-at-the-south-pole/) [B2] §7.1.
- Fire on the surface in deep cold: Old Pole garage fire on 13 Oct 1964 at −67 °F destroyed three tractors, **SOURCED** (igy1) [A2] §2.1.
- At Halley III (ice shelf), buildings inside steel tubing were "buried 12–15 m below the surface" in 10 years and abandoned over access and ventilation, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §6.2.
- Non-polar surface failures that bear on any town site: an avalanche at Longyearbyen (19 Dec 2015) hit 11 homes and killed 2 after wind deposited up to 4 m of snow, **SOURCED** (https://www.unis.no/news/force-deadly-polar-bears/) [A3] §3.2. Ground deformation from work below forces a town to move: Kiruna, "Even if we stopped the mine today, the deformations would continue for many years," **SOURCED** (https://lkab.com/en/press/continued-mining-affects-more-of-kiruna/) [A3] §3.1.

### 1.5 What a surface day looks like (record)
- **Shared chores.** "House mouse" cleaning shared by all on rotation; volunteers wash dishes; "Summer residents are restricted to two, two-minute showers per week"; one load of laundry a week, **SOURCED** (USAP Participant Guide ch. 6, URL for ch. 7: https://www.usap.gov/USAPgov/travelAndDeployment/documents/ParticipantGuide-Chapter7.pdf) [B1] §2.3. Showers "restricted to two minutes," **SOURCED** (brr.fyi water post, 2024-06-07, URL not given in the file) [A2] §4.2.
- **Rounds.** Maintenance specialists walk daily rounds "of several miles, in all weather," **SOURCED** (memoir *Cold*, https://erenow.org/common/cold-three-winters-at-the-south-pole/3.php) [A2] §3.2.
- **Work week.** "Nine hours a day, minimum, six days a week" (an IT manager), **UNVERIFIED** [B1] §2.3.
- **Outbuildings.** "Outbuildings have no plumbing (porta-potties, dry toilets, buckets, bottles)," **SOURCED** (brr.fyi water post) [A2] §4.3; staff use "pee bottles," **SOURCED** (https://www.yahoo.com/news/2-workers-remote-antarctic-research-154109475.html) [C4] §6.2.
- **Clothing.** A required multi-layer Extreme Cold Weather system; first-year participants and South Pole winter-overs "must take the full complement of USAP-issued clothing," **SOURCED** (NSF policy, https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/files/AIL-POL_1000.06_ECW82.pdf) [C4] §4.4.
- **Time outdoors in a cold town.** At Yakutsk, "Five to 10 minutes outside can cause fatigue, facial stinging ... Twenty minutes is the point when even hardy residents go indoors," **SOURCED** (Slate/Atlas Obscura 2014, URL not given in the file) [C4] §3.1.
- **Indoor dryness.** Humidity at the Pole "0.03%" in the ASAP study, **SOURCED** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3114308) [C4] §1.1; the greenhouse lobby is "the only humid place on station," **SOURCED** (brr.fyi "The last egg," URL not given in the file) [B1] §4.2.

### 1.6 Light and dark at the surface
- 2024: sunset 22 March, sunrise 20 September; full astronomical darkness 11 May to 31 July, **SOURCED** (Oesper, https://cosmicreflections.skythisweek.info/tag/south-pole/); **DERIVED** 182 days with the Sun down and 81 days of full darkness; civil twilight about 13 days after sunset and 14 before sunrise. Maximum Sun elevation 23.5° at the December solstice, **SOURCED** (met handout). [A2] §1.7.
- Aurora "can be monitored around the clock" in winter because the station sits at about 74.2° magnetic latitude, **SOURCED** (Doolittle & Mende 1994, https://s3.amazonaws.com/Antarctica/AJUS/AJUSvXXIXn5/AJUSvXXIXn5p349.pdf) [A2] §1.7. How often aurora is visible: **NOT FOUND**.
- The nearest artificial light in winter is McMurdo, about 1,355 km away, **SOURCED** (southpole.era.gs) [A2] §4.7.
- Polar-night towns: midday light on Svalbard "reaches several hundred lux, sometimes over 1,000 lux," "usually not enough to reliably set the body's internal clock"; streetlights, reflective vests, illuminated windows and headlamps shape the townscape, **SOURCED** (https://polarjournal.net/polar-night-how-darkness-shapes-rhythm-psyche-and-community-the-example-of-longyearbyen-in-svalbard/) [C4] §3.6. Longyearbyen kindergarten children wear reflective vests and sometimes headlamps, **SOURCED by search summary** (as marked in [C4] §5.3).
- Clocks: McMurdo and the Pole keep New Zealand time because their supply base is Christchurch, **SOURCED** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_in_Antarctica) [C2] §5.7.

## 2 · Living and building BELOW the surface

### 2.1 Polar under-snow and under-ice precedents: how they were dug
- **Camp Century, Greenland (1959–1966/67).** Cut-and-cover trenches for 100 people year-round, "maximum use ... of snow as a construction material," design life 10 years, standard trenches about 24 × 24 ft, **SOURCED** (CRREL TR-174, https://icedrill.org/sites/default/files/477706.pdf) [A2] §6.1. "Backbone with pairs of ribs" plan; tunnels about 7 m wide, reactor trenches 12 m; buildings "about the size of a trailer home" from prefabricated panels; Peter plows cut the tunnels; "snow dumped onto steel arches that were removed after it hardened," **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine, https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-october-1-1960-camp-century-cold-war-ice-fortress-built/) [A3] §4.1. "26 tunnels totaling almost two miles," **SOURCED** (Atomic Heritage Foundation 2018, URL not given in the file) [A2] §6.1.
- **Camp Fistclench (Site 2), Greenland.** "Only low chimney tops show above the snow"; trenches dug by a 13-ton Peter plow; "snow becomes solid ice about 15 ft down"; 24 insulated Jamesway huts, 20 × 40 ft, in trenches roofed with timber trusses and covered deep with snow; roads as 20-ft trenches roofed with curved corrugated metal, covered with snow, the metal removed "once the snow hardened, leaving a snow arch," **SOURCED** (TIME 1957, https://time.com/archive/6612233/science-fist-clench-under-ice) [A3] §4.2. Five shallow tunnels with bolted timber roofs; quarters 50–102 sq ft per person, **SOURCED** (veterans' site, https://thuleab.dk/index.php/other-sites/camp-fistclench; figures not traced to a primary report) [A3] §4.2.
- **New Byrd Station, Antarctica (1961).** "Located underground except for some scientific structures"; trenches with timber sills, "roofed with corrugated steel arches, snow backfilled to the arch peak"; buildings "free from the heavy drifting which is crushing the old Byrd station," **UNVERIFIED** (snippets) [A3] §4.4.
- **Camp TUTO ice tunnel (Greenland ice edge).** A 1,100-ft tunnel carved into the foot of the ice cap, with living quarters and diesel generators, interior 17 °F (−8 °C), **UNVERIFIED** (Wikipedia) [A3] §4.3.
- **Thule ice warehouse.** A chamber 65 ft square and 25 ft high, 150 ft below the surface and 500 ft back from the glacier face, **SOURCED** (TIME 1957) [A3] §4.2.
- **The Pole's arches today.** Metal semicircular buildings holding "the power plant, water plant, primary cold/warm storage, fuel storage, carpenter shop, and vehicle maintenance," entered by a vehicle ramp and "completely buried under decades of accumulated snow," **SOURCED** (brr.fyi topography) [A2] §2.3–2.4. The logistics arch (1970s) holds 45 × 10,000-gallon fuel tanks and "nearly two years of food"; the power plant and vehicle-maintenance arches were "added in the late 1990s below grade, which shortened their lifespan," **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §2.4. Under-snow utility tunnels of 3,071 ft carrying fresh-water and wastewater piping, excavated with a CRREL subsurface tunneling system, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §2.4.
- **Neumayer III garage.** The platform building is "connected to a garage dug into the snow"; garage and technical equipment in "a subsurface snow cavern at the front," about 8.20 m below the station, **SOURCED** (IPY; Structurae) [A3] §4.5.

### 2.2 How ice tunnels deform, sag and close (the record)
- **Camp Century.** Snow to be "trimmed and removed ... each year in order to maintain the original cross section of the trenches is estimated to be on the order of 20,000 cubic yards," about 0.173 yd³ a minute over a 240-day, 8-hour work year; trimming over 12-ft-wall buildings began "even before the rest of the camp had been completed"; "Wonder Arch" steel arches distressed, **SOURCED** (TR-174) [A2] §6.1. "Regular trimming because the moving ice distorted the tunnels," **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine) [A3] §4.1.
- **TR-174's conclusions (verbatim points, SOURCED [A2] §6.1).** "Subsurface camps on ice caps are feasible and practicable, but only in environments as cold or colder than Century"; "Roughly 10 years is the maximum feasible design life for an undersnow camp in environments similar to Century"; "Means for a continuous program of trimming the snow trenches should be provided in initial planning"; sites should be crevasse-free with surface slopes not over 2%, "as greater slopes could be indicative of rapid visco-elastic flow rates." Advantages it claimed: lower initial cost, avoided surface environment, less heating fuel, fewer imported materials, minimized drifting.
- **Fistclench.** "As the surrounding snow warmed and new snow built up on the roof, the tunnel closed rapidly"; rigid timber roofs took large stresses and several bents cracked; Jamesways "not thermally effective," **SOURCED** (veterans' site) [A3] §4.2.
- **Old Pole.** Tunnels crushed by snow: in 1958–59 a tunnel was shored with stacked fuel drums "that began to crumple"; steel arches replaced wood in 1963–64; the station was "completely dug out for major reconstruction of tunnels," **SOURCED** (igy1). The 1974–75 crew were "always wary of the stresses and strains evident throughout," **SOURCED** (tunnel page). [A2] §2.1.
- **The Pole arches now.** "Ice is encroaching from below, pushing interior facilities upward"; the gap between arches and interior buildings has closed "from three feet to 'mere inches'"; some arches buckle under snow pressure; NSF studies called them "closer than ever to failing"; fuel pipes turned horizontal and the top of the vehicle shop cut off "have not solved the problem"; "no replacement project exists" (Feb 2024), **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §2.4.
- **Neumayer I and II (ice shelf).** "Annual snow accumulation of 80 cm has buried the Neumayer Station about 15 m beneath the snow surface"; "Ice movement and pressure have heavily deformed the steel tubes," **SOURCED** (IPY) [A3] §4.5.
- **TUTO.** The ice camps suffered "gradual deformation, compression, and subsidence, requiring annual repair"; the ice ramp has since collapsed, **UNVERIFIED** [A3] §4.3.
- **New Byrd.** "Lasted ten years until it too succumbed to ice, snow and internal moisture," **UNVERIFIED** [A3] §4.4.
- **After abandonment.** Camp Century "No one has visited since," buildings likely being crushed, **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine) [A3] §4.1; the tunnels "quickly collapsed," **SOURCED** (AHF 2018); the site is now about 35 m deeper under snow, **SOURCED** (AGU 2016, URL not given in the file) [A2] §6.1.

**Chain (closure → labor → life span).** 1st: moving, warming or loaded snow closes trenches (**SOURCED**, Fistclench; TR-174). 2nd: keeping the cross section means removing about 20,000 yd³ of snow a year at Camp Century (**SOURCED**, TR-174). 3rd: TR-174 sets "roughly 10 years" as the maximum feasible design life and says trimming must be planned from the start (**SOURCED**). **DERIVED:** Camp Century (10 years designed), New Byrd (about 10 years, UNVERIFIED), and the Pole arches (1970s/1990s, "closer than ever to failing" in 2024) bracket the recorded life of under-snow structures at roughly one to five decades, with the longest lives carrying ongoing failure reports.

### 2.3 Heat, cold and the host material
- "Wooden foundations kept building heat from melting the snow below" at Camp Century, **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine) [A3] §4.1.
- At the Svalbard Seed Vault (in permafrost rock) water entered the access tunnel in October 2016; afterward heat sources were removed from the tunnel and technical installations moved to a separate service building "to avoid unwanted heating," **SOURCED** (https://www.seedvault.no/?p=2435) [A3] §3.2.
- Snow at depth at the Pole is about −60 °F, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi water post) [A2] §4.2.
- Cooling with stored ice: 200,000 kg of ice in basins at Katarinaberget (Stockholm), **SOURCED** (https://www.subbrit.org.uk/sites/katarinaberget-nuclear-shelter/) [A3] §2.4. Deep mines: rock walls about 60 °C at Mponeng, more than 6,000 t of ice slurry pumped in, **SOURCED** (https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/66169-deepest-mine) [A3] §3.6.
- Heat from equipment: Cheyenne Mountain's "computers generate the heat required to keep building thermostats at 70 to 72 degrees," **SOURCED** (AFPS 1998, https://nuke.fas.org/guide/usa/c3i/9804241.html) [C2] §S5.6.

**Chain (heat melts the host).** 1st: building heat melts snow below (**SOURCED**, Camp Century wooden foundations). 2nd: in the Pole's late-1990s arches, "ice is encroaching from below, pushing interior facilities upward" (**SOURCED**). 3rd: no replacement project exists (**SOURCED**). Whether heat is the cause at the Pole: the source does not say; the link is **UNVERIFIED** as an inference.

### 2.4 Ventilation and air below
- Passive shafts: Coober Pedy ("Ventilation shafts run up to the surface to ensure the supply of oxygen and to release moisture," **SOURCED**, https://science.nasa.gov/?p=1073347); Derinkuyu ("more than 50 additional ventilation shafts," **SOURCED**, https://www.history.com/articles/derinkuyu-turkey-underground-city); Beijing (2,300 sealable shafts, **UNVERIFIED**). [A3] §6.3.
- Mechanical: SURF fresh air via the Oro Hondo and #5 Shaft fans, down two shafts, with redundant power and ventilation, **SOURCED** (https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1401.0861) [A3] §3.4. Helsinki requires ventilation "designed to also function in fire situations," **SOURCED** (https://www.hel.fi/hel2/ksv/julkaisut/esitteet/esite_2009-8_en.pdf) [A3] §2.3.
- Polar: Fistclench showed only chimney tops above the snow (**SOURCED**, TIME); Halley III abandoned partly over ventilation (**UNVERIFIED**). The Pole's power plant uses CO₂ fire suppression that would make the space "Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health," so entry requires sign-in and tag-in, **SOURCED** (https://brr.fyi/posts/south-pole-electrical-infrastructure) [A2] §4.1.
- Vehicle exhaust: ice fog forms at about −40 °C and "can be produced at warmer temperatures by vehicle exhaust, especially at start-up," **SOURCED** (CRREL TR-04-6) [C4] §7.3.
- Depth and pressure: travel to SNOLAB (2 km down a mine) "puts about 25–30 percent more pressure on your body," and underground is "warm and humid," **SOURCED** (visitor guide, https://indico.cern.ch/event/1345184/attachments/2747100/4780269/SL-MCS-OTR-20-001-F03-Visitor-Information-Guide_Rev_15%20(2023-09-20).pdf) [A3] §3.3.
- Bad air as a living condition: Beijing basement rooms with "no windows, chilly drafts, damp and moldy in summer," **SOURCED** (https://www.chinafile.com/node/9226) [A3] §5.3.

### 2.5 Light below
- No daylight: Beijing basements, no windows (**SOURCED**, ChinaFile); Naours, electric lighting (**SOURCED**, https://www.showcaves.com/english/fr/subterranea/Naours.html); GPS Master Control's room is windowless (**SOURCED**, https://www.sciencefriday.com/articles/behind-the-master-controls-of-gps/). [A3] §6.4, [C2] §4.4.
- Courtyards bring light into below-grade homes: Matmata pit courtyards; sunken yaodong; Setenil's two quarters, one lit by sunlight reflected off the cliff, one always shaded, **SOURCED** (https://showcaves.com/english/es/subterranea/Setenil.html) [A3] §1.6, §6.4.
- Lighting levels in polar ice tunnels: **NOT FOUND** in the files.

### 2.6 What life below is like (record)
- **Camp Century's public life below.** Mess hall, theater, dispensary, emergency room, hobby shop, recreation spaces, labs, crew quarters, storage, **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine); a library of 672 sq ft, ten-bed infirmary with operating room, "Main Street" over 1,000 ft, **UNVERIFIED** [A3] §4.1.
- **Fistclench.** Kitchen 384 sq ft, dining 960, exchange 512, plus library and club, **SOURCED** (veterans' site); residents "would hardly feel 100-mph gales overhead," **SOURCED** (TIME). [A3] §4.2.
- **Fuel argument for going below.** "Fuel is about three-quarters of the cargo sent to an Arctic base," and underground shelter was expected to save much of it, **SOURCED** (TIME 1957) [A3] §4.2.
- **Windowless work.** Underground office workers "wanted windows more strongly," rated lighting "significantly more negatively" though measured lighting was "very similar," and rated their offices "less pleasant, less stimulating, and more enclosed"; the author attributes this to knowing one is underground without daylight, **SOURCED** (BRI Research Paper 138, https://www.kenken.go.jp/english/contents/publications/paper/138.html) [A3] §6.4. Singapore cohort: no association with distress after adjustment (2020) vs an association with distress and worse sleep (2021 thesis), **UNVERIFIED, conflict** [A3] §6.4.
- **Mountain installations.** Cheyenne Mountain: 15 buildings on 1,319 steel springs under 1,800 ft of rock; the main work center "looks like the inside of a ship," **SOURCED** (https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/fifty-years-of-mission-in-the-mountain/); long stretches without natural light compared to submarine duty, **UNVERIFIED** (low-authority page). [C2] §4.5.
- **Shelters built without daily needs.** Sonnenberg (Lucerne) "fails to fulfill its intended purpose because planners ignored everyday human needs," **SOURCED** (https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/en/2022/04/shelters-a-swiss-phenomenon/); the kitchen fed only staff and patients, residents had to bring two weeks of food, about 20 toilets per block, water limited occupancy to about two weeks, **SOURCED** (https://www.thelocal.ch/20161020/inside-switzerlands-largest-nuclear-bunker-40-years-on). [A3] §5.1.

### 2.7 Non-polar underground towns and installations (precedents in the files)
- **Homes below, work above.** Coober Pedy: about half of residents underground, interior "an almost constant 23 °C" against summer highs reportedly 52 °C, **SOURCED** (NASA EO) [A3] §1.1. Guadix: about 2,000 cave houses at 18–20 °C, a "forest of white chimneys" marks them from above, **SOURCED** (eldiario.es, https://www.eldiario.es/viajes/municipio-granada-convertido-capital-europea-cuevas-miles-casas-subterraneas-pm_1_12113078.html) [A3] §1.7. Naours: 9.5 °C, 33 m below the town, **SOURCED** [A3] §1.5.
- **Homes above, work below.** Kiruna (mine to a main level 1,365 m below ground, **SOURCED**, https://lkab.com/en/events/the-moving-of-kiruna-church/), SNOLAB (lab 2 km down, 5,000 m² clean space, staff 150, **SOURCED**, https://www.snolab.ca/about/about-snolab/), SURF (Davis Campus certified for 62 occupants, **SOURCED**, Heise). [A3] §3.1, §3.3, §3.4.
- **Services below, people above (cities).** Helsinki: about 9 million m³ in about 400 facilities (2009); "common-use utility tunnels" carrying district heating and cooling and power and telecom cables, "300 kilometers" of lines; a binding underground master plan, **SOURCED** (Helsinki plan PDF). About 10 million m³ in nearly 500 facilities and about 5,500 shelters (2026), **SOURCED** (https://finland.fi/life-society/helsinki-underground-where-the-city-plays-swims-and-shelters/). [A3] §2.3. Singapore: utilities and transport placed underground "to lessen effects on the surface," Common Services Tunnels, deep rock caverns, **SOURCED** (https://www.ura.gov.sg/land-planning/master-plan/master-plan-2025/themes/strengthening-urban-resilience/an-underground-of-possibilities/) [A3] §5.4.
- **Walkway networks in cold cities.** Montreal RÉSO, 32 km, "Frais en été et chaud en hiver," about 500,000 users a day, **SOURCED** (https://montreal.ca/articles/reso-le-reseau-souterrain-de-montreal-13713); Toronto PATH, "each segment is owned and controlled by the owner of the property it runs through," **SOURCED** (https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/visitor-services/path-torontos-downtown-pedestrian-walkway/); Minneapolis skyways "sucked life up and away from the streets" (historian Larry Millett), **SOURCED** (https://www.minnpost.com/minnesota-history/2013/07/minneapolis-oldest-skyway-still-use-turns-50/). [A3] §2.1, §2.2, §2.6. Norilsk: "long underground corridors link the factories," residents spend most of their time indoors, **SOURCED** (Meduza 2015, URL not given in the file) [C4] §3.4.

### 2.8 What fails below (record)
- **Water from above.** Seed Vault 2016 (**SOURCED**); Wieliczka 1992, "Water is the basic threat to a salt mine," surface subsidence over 2 m (**SOURCED**, https://nbi.com.pl/content/uploads/assets/NBI-pdf/2006/1_4_2006/06_4_3_wieliczka.pdf); Homestake flooded to 1,381 m below surface after pumps stopped in 2003 (**SOURCED**, Heise); Zhengzhou metro 2021, 14 dead after floodwater entered a tunnel (**SOURCED**, https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1009524); Utqiagvik's utilidor nearly flooded in 2015 and 2017 (**SOURCED**, Alaska Public Media 2023, URL not given in the file). [A3] §6.6, [C4] §3.5.
- **Fire in a sloped tunnel.** Kaprun, 11 Nov 2000: "Air rising through the tunnel fed the fire"; 155 died; "Most passengers fled uphill toward the toxic smoke"; the 12 survivors went downhill, **SOURCED** (https://hdgoe.at/kaprun_funicular_disaster) [A3] §6.1.
- **Rock burst.** Kiruna, 18 May 2020, Mw 4.2 about 1,146 m down; mine evacuated; no injuries, **SOURCED** (https://lkab.com/en/news/analysis-of-the-seismic-event-in-kiruna-on-may-18th-completed/) [A3] §3.1.
- **Shelters not ready.** Sonnenberg 1987: "The doors did not shut properly," defects "never fixed for financial reasons," **SOURCED** (https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/lucerne-says-goodbye-to-the-cold-war/685910); Oslo 2023 audit: shelters not kept in proper condition, no maintenance routines, **SOURCED** (https://www.oslo.kommune.no/politikk/budsjett-regnskap-og-rapportering/rapporter-fra-kommunerevisjonen/rapport-19-2023-tilfluktsrom/). [A3] §2.4, §5.1.
- **Ice closure.** See 2.2.

## 4 · The connective tissue: how surface and subsurface parts are connected in real places

- **Stair tower.** At the Pole the elevated station connects to the buried arches by an enclosed stair tower ("Beer Can") descending about 50 ft, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi topography) [A2] §2.4; water is lifted about 50 ft up the Beer Can, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi water) [A2] §4.2. The main entrance stairs ("Destination Alpha") and stairwell bottoms are buried, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi topography).
- **Vehicle ramps.** The Pole arches are entered by a vehicle ramp, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi). Camp Century had "two ramped entrances for people and machinery," **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine) [A3] §4.1. TUTO had a gravel ramp more than a mile onto the ice cap, since collapsed, **UNVERIFIED** [A3] §4.3.
- **Escape hatches.** Camp Century had 16 emergency escape hatches straight up through the ice, **SOURCED** (Earth Magazine). Pole vault hatches are extended "multiple times" as snow rises, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi). Porthcurno's wartime tunnels had a 120-step escape staircase to the cliff top, **SOURCED** (https://www.warhistoryonline.com/war-articles/bunker.html) [C2] §1.1 (119 vs 120 steps, minor conflict).
- **Platform to cavern.** Neumayer III's platform building is "connected to a garage dug into the snow," **SOURCED** (IPY) [A3] §4.5.
- **Shafts, cages and lifts.** SNOLAB: mine cage, then a 1.4 km walk "over uneven terrain"; visitors must be able "to scale a 50-foot ladder in case of an emergency evacuation," **SOURCED** (visitor guide) [A3] §3.3. SURF: two primary shafts; the Yates cage is 1.4 × 2.7 × 3.8 m, max 10,000 lb, three science shifts a day; redundant power, fiber and ventilation through both shafts; a standby diesel for safety systems for up to 24 h, **SOURCED** (Heise) [A3] §3.4. Pyhäsalmi: elevator to about 1,400 m in about 3 minutes; an 11 km decline driven in about 30 minutes, **UNVERIFIED** [A3] §3.7.
- **Clean/dirty boundary at the connection.** At SNOLAB everyone showers on arrival underground; "after showering, you are considered clean and cannot pass back onto the dirty side without showering again"; "The shower cannot be bypassed for any reason," **SOURCED** (visitor guide) [A3] §3.3.
- **Rail through rock.** Jungfraujoch: 7.6 km of the route in tunnel to 3,454 m; the research station reached "by a short walk from the railway station through a tunnel" (**UNVERIFIED**) and the Sphinx lab by elevator (**UNVERIFIED**); the railway itself **SOURCED** (https://www.jungfrau.ch/de-ch/unternehmen/jungfraubahnen/jungfraubahn-holding-ag/jungfraubahn-ag/) [A3] §4.7.
- **Utility tunnels as connectors.** Pole under-snow utility tunnels for water and wastewater, 3,071 ft, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §2.4. Alert lists "utility tunnels and cabling" among maintained systems, **SOURCED** (statement of work) [A3] §4.6. Utqiagvik's temperature-controlled utilidor carries about a third of the town's water, wastewater and electrical lines and stopped at 3 miles because extension was too expensive; the rest is buried with thaw risk, **SOURCED** (Alaska Public Media 2023, URL not given in the file) [C4] §3.5. Inuvik: 16 km of above-ground utilidor, "circulation and heating stations," an after-hours utilidor operator on call, **SOURCED** (https://www.inuvik.ca/living-here/water-sewer-and-utilities/) [B2] §1.2. Helsinki: common utility tunnels, 300 km of lines (**SOURCED**).
- **Hidden or domestic entrances.** Derinkuyu entrances inside houses (**SOURCED**, History.com); Kaymaklı tunnels reached from residents' yards (**SOURCED**, newgoreme.com, https://www.newgoreme.com/about-turkey/cappadocia-region/kaymakli-underground-city-3726/); Naours entrances in farmhouses and a windmill (**SOURCED**); Katarinaberget's 11 pedestrian access points, some hidden under soil in public squares (**SOURCED**). [A3] §6.1.
- **Doors that must close.** Sonnenberg's 350 t, 1.5 m blast doors did not shut properly in the 1987 exercise (**SOURCED**, swissinfo) [A3] §5.1. Cheyenne Mountain's 25-ton doors "now remain open around the clock" (**SOURCED**, Air & Space Forces) [C2] §4.5.
- **Many owners, one network.** Toronto PATH segments are owned by about 35 corporations; the City became coordinating agency in 1987 and paid for signage (**SOURCED**) [A3] §2.2. Minneapolis skyways have no uniform design (**SOURCED**) and no uniform hours (**UNVERIFIED**) [A3] §2.6.
- **What failed at connections.** Kaprun: the sloped tunnel acted as a chimney (**SOURCED**). Montreal 2007: a 7-m corridor fissure halted a metro line for months (**UNVERIFIED**) [A3] §2.1.

**Chain (the connection is the weak point in cold and fire).** 1st: rising air in a sloped tunnel fed the Kaprun fire and passengers fled uphill into smoke (**SOURCED**). 2nd: Helsinki requires smoke exhaust, fire compartmentation and "clearly indicated emergency exit routes" to the surface (**SOURCED**). 3rd: at the Pole the main entrance stairs and stairwell bottoms are buried and hatches must be extended repeatedly (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi). **DERIVED:** in the polar record, exits to the surface are the part that burial attacks first.

## 5 · Utilities and systems at this scale

### 5.1 Power
- **South Pole.** Three Caterpillar 3512B engines, each 750 kW "after de-rating for altitude and fuel blend"; normally one runs, one is on standby, one is down for maintenance; a 250 kW peaking set built in but unused; 480/277 V, outlying buildings fed at 4,160 V; Emergency Power Plant in the B-Pod "Lifeboat," two 250 kW sets, black-start capable, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi electrical) [A2] §4.1. New science "must fit within the plant's capacity," **SOURCED** (same). A model of replacing 95% of the diesel for "170 kW of power," **SOURCED** (Bender et al. via techxplore, URL not given in the file; whether 170 kW is whole-station is not stated) [A2] §4.1. IceCube's lab used "about 50kw out of 650+250kw available station power" (2009), **SOURCED** (https://sundowner.colorado.edu/seefeldt/ptc-2005_2016/2009_pdf/2009PTC_Soundarapandian.pdf) [C4] §7.7.
- **Camp Century (nuclear).** PM-2A: 10,000 kW thermal, 1,560 kW net electric, crew of 18; 74.1% of time on line in 1962 with average gross demand 830 kW; three 300-kW diesels covered shutdowns and "provided power for the entire camp for more than fifty percent of the time" during shakedown; removed when year-round operation was dropped, **SOURCED** (TR-174) [A2] §6.1. Radioactive liquid waste discharged into the ice cap under an agreement allowing "up to 50 millicuries per year," **SOURCED** (TR-174).
- **Bilibino (nuclear, Chukotka).** The plant was the town's "city-forming enterprise" and produced "about 75%" of electricity in the isolated grid, **SOURCED** (https://1prime.ru/20111201/753292964.html); it "supplied the settlement with heat"; a coal plant would need about 200,000 t of coal a year against about 40 t of nuclear fuel, **SOURCED** (https://lenta.ru/articles/2016/08/05/bilibino_aes/); decommissioning "about eight years," site rehabilitation from around 2054, **SOURCED** (https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/bilibino-units-being-shut-down-for-decommissioning); the town now takes heat and power from a diesel energy center and a floating plant, **SOURCED** (https://www.neimagazine.com/news/bilibino-fuel-unloading-starts/). [BG] §3.
- **Other cold towns and stations.** McMurdo: five 1,400 kW diesel units plus three 330 kW wind turbines; overhead radial distribution "less than reliable"; 800,000 gal of fuel a year for electricity and 500,000 for heat, **SOURCED** (Master Plan 2.1, https://www.usap.gov/news/documents/McMurdoMasterPlan_2.1.pdf) [B1] §7.1. New Vostok: four 200 kW diesels plus two backups, "double or triple redundancy" (**SOURCED**, AARI) [A2] §6.4. Neumayer III: three diesels, a standby, a 30 kW wind turbine (**SOURCED**, IPY) [A3] §4.5. Longyearbyen: about 70 GWh of district heat and 40 GWh of electricity a year for 1,850 consumers (**SOURCED**, TU.no); **DERIVED** about 44 MWh per resident a year [B2] §2.1. Nunavut: 25 isolated diesel plants, "There is no back-up grid" (**SOURCED**, https://www.assembly.nu.ca/sites/default/files/2026-06/TD-025-7%282%29-EN-QEC_Annual_Report_2024-2025.pdf) [B2] §2.3.
- **Power failures.** Vostok, 12 April 1982: a powerhouse fire of about 15 minutes destroyed "all three diesel engines and their backups"; without heat the crew could not melt snow for water; "rescue was impossible for eight months"; 19 survivors relieved after 227 days, **SOURCED** (https://theconversation.com/building-a-telescope-in-the-coldest-place-on-earth-21482) [A2] §6.4, [B2] §6.1. Nuuk, 28 Dec 2024: skewed voltage meant "our emergency power plant did not automatically start up," **SOURCED** (https://nukissiorfiit.gl/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/ENG-Nukissiorfiit-Arsrapport-2024.pdf) [B2] §2.2. Utqiagvik, 11 Feb 2026: outage of heat, power, phone and internet; crews went door to door "to identify homes that may be experiencing freeze-ups," **SOURCED** (https://www.north-slope.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/PSA-Utqiagvik-Power-Outage_asc-v3_021126.pdf) [B2] §2.4. NIST Boulder, Dec 2025: utility outage, a crucial generator failed, the atomic time scale "has failed," **SOURCED** (https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/21/nist_ntp_outage_warning/) [C2] §5.1.

**Chain (power is heat is water).** 1st: "The power plant must run continuously, since its diesel engines supply the station's heat"; staffed 24 hours (**SOURCED**, memoir) [A2] §3.2, §4.1. 2nd: generator waste heat also maintains the water supply (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi) [A2] §4.1. 3rd: when Vostok lost its generators it lost power, heat and light, and the crew "could not melt snow for water" (**SOURCED**, The Conversation).

### 5.2 Heat
- Station heat comes from power-plant waste heat by glycol loop, AN-8 boilers as backup, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi electrical and water) [A2] §4.4. A heat-load figure in kW for the elevated station: **NOT FOUND** [A2] §4.4.

### 5.3 Water (snow melters and Rodriguez wells)
- **Rodwell principle and operation.** Water heated "with the generator exhaust manifold and muffler," circulated through the power-plant heat exchanger; the well starts "at a depth of about 80 to 100 feet (the firn layer)"; at "500 feet deep" a new well is started because pumping costs rise; yield "about 14 gallons of water per minute"; the old well becomes "the next sewer outfall"; the melt is nearly deionized and leaches copper and lead from pipes, so sodium and calcium are added; abandoned-well bottom ice "is full of meteorites," **SOURCED** (LBNL 2003, https://glacier.lbl.gov/gtp/SouthPole/rod_well.html) [BG] §2.3. **DERIVED:** 14 gal/min × 1,440 = about 20,160 gallons a day at full yield.
- **Current system.** Rodwell 3 in use, Rodwell 4 planned; wells drilled "several hundred feet," used until they reach "millions of gallons," then converted to wastewater disposal; raw water about 36 °F, return water heated to about 84 °F; limestone contactors, filters, soda ash, calcium hypochlorite; a separate pressurized fire-sprinkler tank, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi water, URL not given in the file) [A2] §4.2. "The current well should last three more years"; without a new well water use would be limited, possibly cutting summer staffing, **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §4.2. Wells last about seven years, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §4.2.
- **Camp Century's well.** The first well passed 500 ft and was relocated in May 1962; water "better in quality than water obtained by triple distillation in glass," **SOURCED** (TR-174) [A2] §6.1.
- **Capacity limit.** At the Pole, with summer-only accommodation, "the overall population approaches 250, limited by the operational capacities of the water and sewage systems," **SOURCED** (BRP 2012) [B2] §1.8.
- **Snow melters.** Concordia: two snow melters produced 80 m³ of water; the melter must be refilled several times a day or the water overheats; heat came directly from the generator; two of three water pumps froze when air intakes were opened to cool an overheating generator; recycling membranes were damaged by freezing; urine kept out of recycling because ammonia damages membranes, **SOURCED** (first-person account, https://www.gdargaud.net/Antarctica/WinterDC7.html) [C4] §6.2. "All water is recycled," **SOURCED** (https://www.khaleejtimes.com/lifestyle/food/meet-the-chef-who-cooks-gourmet-meals-in-the-most-remote-corner-of-the-world-at-51-3c) [C4] §6.2.
- **Melting energy.** **DERIVED** (standard constants): about 0.51 kWh per US gallon from −51 °C snow to 10 °C water, about 69% of it latent heat [A2] §4.2.
- **Water failures.** 1972–73 experimental Rodwell at Old Pole "was not deep enough or adequately winterized, so it froze up"; the Dome's snow melter and conveyor "did not work," causing "a shortage of water for the first winter," **SOURCED** (https://www.southpolestation.com/trivia/trivia87.txt) [BG] §2.4. Longyearbyen: a single ice-pond source; Legionella, manganese, sulfate and nickel events 2024–25; advice not to drink tap water, **SOURCED** (council report, https://www.lokalstyre.no/download/18.5626f86419ddcc34b6b3d6/1777558120002/Vedlegg%20sak%2009-26%20%C3%85rsberetning%202025%20-%20Longyearbyen%20lokalstyre.pdf) [B2] §1.1.

### 5.4 Sewage
- Pole: "Wastewater is not treated. Raw sewage is injected into old Rodwells"; "millions of gallons of raw, untreated wastewater are stored underground beneath the station"; two holding pits force-discharge at high flow; heat-traced, insulated lines; no treatment plant budgeted as of June 2024, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi water) [A2] §4.3. Who runs it day to day: **NOT FOUND** [B1] §2.3, [BG] §2.5; it sits with the facilities-maintenance group under a PM foreman, **UNVERIFIED/DERIVED** [BG] §2.1.
- Camp Century left about 240,000 L of wastewater and 200,000 L of diesel in the ice, **SOURCED** (AGU 2016) [A2] §6.1.
- McMurdo treats sewage (macerators, activated sludge, UV, outfall), solids shipped to the U.S.; "If the grinder pumps failed, untreated discharge could reach the environment within 60 seconds," **SOURCED** (TPO Magazine 2019, URL not given in the file) [B1] §7.2.
- Vacuum sewers in flat Arctic towns are "the most costly and energy intensive form of sewer system," **SOURCED** (https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2019/06/f63/ANTHC-final-report.pdf) [B2] §1.7.

### 5.5 Waste
- "All non-sewage waste must go back to McMurdo," **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §4.3. "There are no dumpsters at the South Pole"; a four-person team packed 137 pallets in about five weeks; lashing at −40° was hard, **SOURCED** (https://polarjournal.net/waste-disposal-at-the-south-pole/) [B2] §7.1. Waste berms "require annual maintenance so that the waste does not become permanently buried by drifting snow," **SOURCED** (BRP 2012) [B2] §7.1.
- LC-130 cargo costs "$5.25 per pound," **SOURCED** (BRP); **DERIVED** about $11,570 per metric ton [B2] §7.1.
- Australian stations returned 153 t in 2024–25 (**SOURCED**, https://www.antarctica.gov.au/site/assets/files/116146/aad_waste_management_strategy_and_rta_plan_-_2026_-_2031_-_final.pdf) [B2] §7.2.
- McMurdo stopped incineration on 22 March 1993 after "an environmental lawsuit and the Madrid Protocol," **SOURCED** (southpolestation.com incinerator page, URL not given in the file) [B1] §7.3.
- Pole waste staffing: four waste sorters in summer, one in winter, **SOURCED** (https://reachtheworld.org/13-months-bottom-world-images-1) [BG] §2.2.

### 5.6 Fuel storage
- Pole fuel arch: 45 × 10,000 gallons = 450,000 gallons, **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §4.1. Fuel is AN-8, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi); liquid to about −72 °F, **UNVERIFIED** [A2] §4.1, [C4] §7.2. Diesel cloud point about −12 °C; JP-8 below −60 °C, **SOURCED** (CRREL TR-04-6) [C4] §7.3.
- McMurdo stores "a three-year supply of fuel"; the fuel team is "constantly struggling to keep equipment from weeping," **SOURCED** (https://www.aviationpros.com/gse/fueling-equipment-accessories/quality-control/article/21092749/fueling-aviation-in-antarctica) [B1] §7.1, [C4] §7.2.
- Failures: McMurdo, 19 Feb 2003, about 6,500 gal of diesel leaked from a tank, **SOURCED** (NSF release via EurekAlert, URL not given in the file) [B1] §7.3. Norilsk, 29 May 2020: a diesel tank ruptured, more than 20,000 t spilled; the operator blamed melting permafrost under the supports, **SOURCED** (Al Jazeera, URL not given in the file); cause unsettled, **UNVERIFIED** [B2] §6.3. During an eight-day early-July cold snap near −100 °F, warm fuel was moved in drums to outlying buildings, **UNVERIFIED** [C4] §7.8.

### 5.7 Fire
- Pole winter: a volunteer brigade with one week of training is "the only firefighting force during winter," **SOURCED** (memoir) [A2] §3.2. Summer: seven firefighters and two dispatchers; foam sleds kept heated because "foam must stay above −40 °C," **SOURCED** (Hakai 2024, URL not given in the file) [A2] §4.8. Dry chemical used because "liquid water is often hard to obtain in large quantity," **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, as marked in [B2] §6.1).
- Lessons stated in sources: emergency supplies kept in a separate hut "well away" from the station; buildings spaced apart to limit spread, **SOURCED** (coolantarctica.com/Antarctica fact file/science/antarctica_fire_history.php; Wikipedia) [B2] §6.1.
- Fire water in cold towns: Longyearbyen's pipe network "is not sized" for current firefighting-water needs, a problem at two fires, **SOURCED** (council report) [B2] §1.1. McMurdo's system was "not capable of delivering the required fire flow," **SOURCED** (Master Plan 2.1) [B1] §7.2.
- Maintenance hazard: two McMurdo fire technicians died in December 2018 doing maintenance on a generator building's fire-suppression system, **SOURCED** (ScienceAlert, URL not given in the file) [B1] §7.3.
- Station fires listed with dates (Hope Bay 1948, Mirny 1960, Vostok 1982, Rothera 2001, Progress 2008, Ferraz 2012 and others), **SOURCED** (coolantarctica) [B2] §6.1.

### 5.8 Medical
- Pole: a physician year-round plus a PA or NP; COMNAP lists two winter medical staff, capability "Basic," equipment "Altitude medicine, Diagnostic ultrasound, Diagnostic X-ray, Telemedicine," medical facility 84 m², **SOURCED** (COMNAP; Participant Guide) [A2] §6.7, [B1] §2.2. On-site gallbladder surgery in 2003 was judged too complex for the clinic, **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/news/medevac2003/medevac2003.html) [A2] §3.4.
- Three midwinter evacuation flights in about 70 winters (2001, 2003, 2016), all Twin Otters from Rothera, **DERIVED** [A2] §3.4. "Seven workers were evacuated from the South Pole in 2006" with HAPE-like symptoms, **SOURCED** (ASAP) [A2] §1.8.
- Telemedicine ultrasound "made intercontinental medical evacuation unnecessary in one-quarter of potential cases" (2002–04), **SOURCED** (BRP 2012) [B2] §5.6.
- McMurdo: 7 of 88 study participants were evacuated over one winter, 2 for psychiatric or behavioral reasons, **SOURCED** (https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/89724/1/evacuee_paper_AAM.pdf); **DERIVED** 8.0% [BG] §13.5.
- A 2,500-person cold town: Longyearbyen hospital, 23 staff (4 doctors, 6 nurses and others), "Minor surgery room, no planned surgeries," serious cases flown to Tromsø, **SOURCED** (https://en.visitsvalbard.com/dbimgs/LongyearbyenHospital2026.pdf); **DERIVED** 9.2 staff per 1,000 [B2] §5.1.
- At Concordia, CPAP could not be used on a HAPE patient "because of limited oxygen and pressurized air supply at the station," **SOURCED** (https://explorersweb.com/altitude-sickness-in-antarctica/) [C4] §1.3.

### 5.9 Communications

## 6 · Transport and supply

- **Season.** Planes to the Pole fly roughly late October to mid/late February; "usually between February and October planes don't fly in or out because it is too dark and cold," **SOURCED** (AP 2016, URL not given in the file); the Pole "is reachable by plane for only about three months per year," **SOURCED** (NSF 2022) [A2] §5.3. Construction had "only about 100 building days per year," **SOURCED** (NSF 2022) [A2] §5.5.
- **Aircraft.** LC-130 from McMurdo in 3–4 hours (**SOURCED**, ASAP); 26,000 lb allowable cabin load (**SOURCED**, Thur 2012, https://sundowner.colorado.edu/seefeldt/ptc-2005_2016/2012_pdf/PTC_2012_Thur.pdf); "an LC-130 burning about 1.33 lb of fuel to deliver 1 lb to Pole" (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi electrical) [A2] §4.1, §5.3. The elevated station took about 24 million lb in more than 900 flights over 10 years (**SOURCED**, NSF 2022) [A2] §2.3.
- **Cold limits for aircraft.** LC-130 "rated safe down to −55 Celsius," Twin Otter "rated to −75 °C," **UNVERIFIED** (search summary of an NSF release) [C4] §7.6; other figures conflict (section 10). "With temperatures plummeting below −50 °C ... These temperatures risked hydraulic failure and fuel gelling in aircraft," **SOURCED** (phys.org 2024 on Tomanek et al., URL not given in the file) [C4] §7.6. On skis, "If the aircraft stops, it freezes fast to the surface"; "Several takeoff runs may be required to pack down and harden the snow," **SOURCED** (Lockheed Sky Power 1996, URL not given in the file) [C4] §7.6. "There are no hangars or aircraft shelters at the South Pole"; a 2022 LC-130 repair was done outdoors at −40 °F and below, **SOURCED** (https://taskandpurpose.com/news/air-force-lc-130-skibird-antarctica) [C4] §7.6.
- **Traverse.** South Pole Traverse: 1,030 miles one way; season 25 Oct–10 Feb (about 110 days); round trip about 55–58 days; 4 Case and 4 Caterpillar tractors per platform; planning figure about 28 gallons per mile; delivered efficiency 0.21 to 2.37 lb per lb of fuel burned by season, against 0.88 for the LC-130, **SOURCED** (Thur 2012) [A2] §5.2. Early limit set by "early season extreme temperatures on the Polar Plateau (<−40 °F until mid Nov.)," **SOURCED** (Thur 2012). Recent seasons about 300,000 gallons, **SOURCED to a secondary article** (ScienceBlog 2026) [A2] §5.2. Tractor modifications: enclosed hoods, added heaters, cold-viscosity fluids, shorter grousers "to flex more easily in cold temperatures"; summer working temperatures −20 to −40 °F, **SOURCED** (https://www.grainews.ca/machinery/belted-ag-tractors-see-use-in-the-antarctic/) [C4] §7.5.
- **Other plateau traverses.** Vostok 2022–23: 1,480 km from Progress, 45 vehicles, 17 traverses, 2,486 t; the last left "when night temperatures were already reaching −50 °C," **SOURCED** (ATCM45 BP27 via southpolestation.com, URL not given in the file) [A2] §6.4. Concordia: about 1,100 km; three traverses delivered about 470 t in 2002–03; "altitude reduced tractor power," **SOURCED** (AAD magazine 2003, URL not given in the file) [A2] §6.3.
- **Demand.** "South Pole requires ~550,000 gallons of fuel delivered per year; represents ~148 LC-130 missions," **SOURCED** (Thur 2012); summer resupply "up to 450,000 gallons," **SOURCED** (NSF station page) [A2] §5.1.
- **Food pipeline.** The annual shipment "provides approximately a 13-month food supply"; food arrives "six to eight months old and will be stored for as much as a full year"; **DERIVED** up to about 18–20 months old when eaten, **SOURCED** (https://www.army.mil/article/120005/Warrant_officer_makes_trip_to__the_ice_/) [C4] §6.8. "Shipping from the US to McMurdo takes about a year," **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §5.5. At the Pole "the last whole egg" was fried on 6 June 2023; "We won't have a resupply plane until November," **SOURCED** (brr.fyi, URL not given in the file) [B1] §4.2. Warehouse space for "nearly two years of food," **SOURCED** (Polar Journal) [A2] §2.4.
- **Airdrops.** A C-17 dropped four pallets, "just under 70,000 pounds," on 20 Dec 2006 as a test of airdrop "as a viable emergency relief option for winter-over personnel," **SOURCED** (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/00s/airdrop06.html) [A2] §5.4.
- **Removal of people.** No winter flights for months at the Pole; three midwinter flights in about 70 winters (**DERIVED**). Australia's law lets a station leader arrest, designate a building as a "place of detention," seek a warrant by telecommunications and report every 28 days, **SOURCED** (https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2008B00478/asmade/1993-11-17/text/original/pdf) [BG] §13.1. At Bellingshausen (2018) a suspect "could not fly until 20 October" and was held at the station's Orthodox church "because the continent has no jails or police," **SOURCED** (https://www.vice.com/en/article/an-attempted-murder-at-a-research-station-shows-how-crimes-are-prosecuted-in-antarctica) [BG] §13.3. How a person is held at the Pole in winter: **NOT FOUND** [BG] §13.6.

**Chain (season → stock → fresh food).** 1st: no planes from February to October (**SOURCED**). 2nd: food is "ordered years ahead, shipped in bulk, and deep-frozen" (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi) and the station stores nearly two years of food (**SOURCED**). 3rd: fresh eggs end in early June and "the greenhouse is the only source of fresh food" in winter (**SOURCED**, brr.fyi) [B1] §4.2.

## 7 · Altitude and cold effects on daily physical life

### 7.1 Body
- "Residents are flown directly from sea level to approximately 10,000 feet"; "Significant adaptations occur within the first four days ... It may take a month or more to completely adapt"; drink "at least four liters of water per day," low-salt diet, no heavy exertion for the first two to four days; alcohol and sleeping pills worsen periodic breathing, **SOURCED** (https://www.usap.gov/USAPgov/travelAndDeployment/documents/MA-FRM-0007.pdf) [C4] §1.2.
- 52% of 246 arrivals met acute-mountain-sickness criteria, peak on day 2; resting SpO₂ fell from 97.3% to 89.1%; sleeping difficulty 74%; exertional breathlessness 87%, **SOURCED** (ASAP) [A2] §1.8, [C4] §1.1. Pressure altitude averages 3,223 m, highest 3,690 m, **SOURCED** (met handout) [A2] §1.8.
- Concordia: daytime SpO₂ 89.7% at one month and 90.6% at twelve months; more light sleep and micro-arousals, **SOURCED via search summary** (as marked in [C4] §1.4). Concordia winter crews lost body weight (mean −4.5% ± 6.1%; 47% lost more than 5%), **SOURCED** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12715607/) [C4] §6.7. Hypoxia moderately decreases energy intake, **SOURCED** (Matu 2018, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/126213/) [C4] §6.7.
- Children: "it is generally recommended not to ascend to a sleeping altitude of higher than 3,000 to 4,000 m" for a preschool child; "no conclusive scientific data," **SOURCED** (https://www.theuiaa.org/children-at-altitude/) [C4] §1.6. Birth weight falls about 54.7 g per 1,000 m, **SOURCED via search summary** (as marked in [C4] §1.6).
- Oxygen in rooms at altitude: China's GB/T 35414-2017 standard for diffused oxygen indoors above 3,000 m, **SOURCED** (https://www.chinesestandard.net/AMP/English.amp.aspx/GBT35414-2017) [A3] §4.8; Nagchu piped liquid oxygen to more than 15,000 people in 23 institutions, **SOURCED** (https://chinadailyhk.com/article/200371) [C4] §2.5; at ALMA "No one is allowed to sleep at the high site," **SOURCED** (https://eso.org/public/blog/breathless-science/) [C4] §2.9.

### 7.2 Cooking
- **Boiling point.** **DERIVED** (Antoine equation): about 88.9 °C at the Pole's average station pressure, 87.3 °C at the lowest, 90.4 °C at the highest [C4] §6.1. A Pole baker says "The best you'll get is about 93 C," **SOURCED but ANECDOTAL** (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47313934) [C4] §6.5; conflict in section 10.
- **Effects.** Boiled and simmered foods cook at lower temperature and need longer; oven temperatures "are not affected by altitude"; yeast bread rises faster; the guide stops at 10,000 ft, **SOURCED** (https://routt.extension.colostate.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2018/05/High-Altitude-Food-Perperation-Guide.pdf) [C4] §6.1. Lower pressure "can cause cakes to rise too much in the oven," **SOURCED** (https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/learn/high-altitude-baking.html) [C4] §6.5. At the Pole "cakes and bread rise far too rapidly and then ... fall," **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of a Pole baker's blog) [C4] §6.5. Pole galley: "yeast works really well at altitude," a full proving cabinet, steam-injector ovens, "a massive pressure cooker but it's a pain to use and clean," **SOURCED but ANECDOTAL** (Hacker News) [C4] §6.5.
- **Pressure cookers on a plateau.** Water boils at about 85–90 °C on the Tibetan plateau; traditional pressure cookers were over-pressurized and "may even explode"; a plateau-specific cooker sold about 690,000 units by end-2023, **SOURCED** (https://regional.chinadaily.com.cn/tibet/2024-09/10/c_1020289.htm; state-media source) [C4] §6.6.
- **Cold storage.** Ice cream stored outside at about −70 °F "takes multiple days" to warm to serving temperature, **SOURCED** (brr.fyi) [B1] §4.2. "The extreme temperatures can cause meats and other foods to dehydrate," **SOURCED** (army.mil) [C4] §6.8. At Concordia "Even the yeast is frozen," **SOURCED** (Khaleej Times) [C4] §6.2.

### 7.3 Equipment and materials
- **Lubricants and fluids.** Lubricants "the single most critical problem encountered by vehicles in cold regions"; "Stiffened lubricants ... Seals can break. Structural elements can bend or crack. Drivetrain elements can snap. It is crucially important to initiate operation slowly," **SOURCED** (CRREL TR-04-6) [C4] §7.3. Arctic hydraulic oil pour point −62 °C, **SOURCED but VENDOR** (https://phillips66lubricants.com/product/arctic-low-pour-hydraulic-oil/); **DERIVED** warmer than the Pole's July extreme minimum of −80.6 °C [C4] §7.3.
- **Batteries.** Lead-acid gel and AGM capacity as a share of 25 °C capacity: −20 °C about 23–24%; −30 °C 10–13%; −40 °C 1–2%; −50 °C 0%, **SOURCED** (https://kb.unavco.org/article.php?id=386) [C4] §7.4. Lithium-ion "can be a real challenge to operate in colder temperatures"; PEM fuel-cell membranes "can freeze and cause a catastrophic system failure," **SOURCED** (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26699/chapter/5) [C4] §7.4.
- **Electronics.** A Pole telescope became "non-operational" below −95 °F; "Between the dryness and the cold, static electricity is always present"; "Thousands of dollars worth of equipment have been lost to static electricity"; o-rings "freeze solid"; equipment must be serviceable by someone "wearing a huge parka, mittens," **SOURCED** (https://passporttoknowledge.com/antarctica/researchers/mikemasterman/mikemasterman_journals.html) [C4] §7.7. IceCube's data center listed "Low humidity = high ESD," cooling and power stability as challenges, **SOURCED** (PTC 2009) [C4] §7.7. Temperature cycling causes condensation and shorts "when a cold vehicle is brought indoors," **SOURCED** (CRREL TR-04-6) [C4] §7.7.
- **Vehicles.** Vehicles used only in emergencies below about −65 °F; hydraulics need above −70 °F, **UNVERIFIED** [C4] §7.2, §7.5. At Concordia vehicles are stored in a cave in winter "due to the fuel freezing," **UNVERIFIED** [C4] §7.5. At −45 °C on the plateau a differential was replaced overnight under a heated tarp, **SOURCED** (IPY blog 2007, URL not given in the file) [C4] §7.5. Electrical equipment is "removed, packaged, and stored in heated boxes over the winter," **SOURCED** (Reach the World) [C4] §7.5.

**Chain (cold soak → slow start → time).** 1st: lubricants and hydraulic fluids stiffen and seals can break (**SOURCED**, CRREL). 2nd: operation must start slowly and engine heaters are standard (**SOURCED**, CRREL). 3rd: at the Pole, vehicle use is limited in the deepest cold (**UNVERIFIED**), and in Oymyakon engines are left running "from Autumn all the way to Spring" (**SOURCED**, autoevolution, URL not given in the file) [C4] §3.2.

### 7.4 Cold injury and outdoor work
- Wind-chill bands: −40 to −47 skin can freeze in 5–10 minutes; −48 to −54 in 2–5 minutes; −55 and colder under 2 minutes, **SOURCED** (https://ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/cold_working.html) [C4] §4.1. At or below −12 °C wind chill, "constant observation by a supervisor or buddy," **SOURCED** (CCOHS) [C4] §4.3. Avoid bare skin on metal below −7 °C, **SOURCED** (WHSC Ontario, URL not given in the file) [C4] §4.3.
- British Antarctic Survey 1986–95: 65.6 cold injuries per 1,000 people a year; 78% during recreation, **SOURCED** (https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/503074) [C4] §4.1.

## 10 · Unresolved conflicts among the files that matter to the design

1. **Snow accumulation units.** 23 cm of snow (met handout), 8.6 cm liquid equivalent (met handout), 84.5 mm water equivalent (Mosley-Thompson 1999), about 8.7 cm/yr (Hills 2022), 0.080 m/yr ice equivalent (Fudge 2020), all SOURCED [A2] §1.4.
