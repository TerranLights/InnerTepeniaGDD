## 1 · #111 Yokohama and its port jazz bars

**Round 2 focus:** the districts and groups around the jazz rooms (Noge, Kotobuki, Koganecho, Chinatown, Honmoku), the amateur and volunteer side of the city's jazz, port festivals, and the open threads from Round 1 (the JAZZ Association's founding, the Jazz Promenade's budget, audition grading, Bar Bar Bar and Motion Blue status).
**Headline:** the jazz scene sits inside a ring of working districts that each grew from the port and the occupation: **Noge**, the one Japanese district left unrequisitioned in 1945, which became the day laborers' drinking quarter; **Kotobuki**, the port day-labor lodging district, now a welfare district; **Koganecho**, a former illegal red-light street turned artists' district (2005–2009); and **Chinatown**, whose schools and festivals split along the 1949 divide. The city's jazz is carried today largely by **amateurs and volunteers**: several ward-level outdoor jazz festivals (Honmoku from 1981, Asahi 30+ years; Kōnan and a "Summer Jazz" committee also sat on the 2004 festival committee), ~300 volunteers at the Jazz Promenade, and ~160 amateur bands a year who pay to play its street stages.

### A. Other settings (districts, housing, work and school spaces)
- **Noge after 1945 — the "only Japanese town" (SOURCED):** residents' recollections collected by Yokohama Central Library (90th-anniversary panel record, "あの頃の、ヨコハマは・・・", updated 2025-07-15, https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/kurashi/kyodo-manabi/library/100th/90years/6-2.html ): in Sept–Oct 1945 central Yokohama's commercial districts were requisitioned, leaving Noge as the only Japanese town; street vendors lined the road from Noge Ōdōri to Miyakobashi (~60 stalls in one account) selling almost everything except food; US soldiers sold chocolate and gasoline there and came back as MPs to crack down; manual laborers injected methamphetamine on the street; the nearby Koganecho area was known for drugs and prostitution; around 1965 day laborers gathered between Sakuragicho and Miyakobashi, trucked to the wharves for cargo work, paid about ¥1,500 a day; those who found no work drank at standing bars; the job-placement office was in the area. "Once the vendors were gone, fewer customers came to Noge." [K4, K7, K9, P4]
- **The Toshibashi shopping-street building (SOURCED):** built **1964** for the Tokyo Olympics to take the street stalls off the road; a curved two-story building ~100 m long on the Ōoka River; units of roughly 3 tsubo (~10 m²), ground-floor units facing the street, upper ones the river; about 60 small bars and eateries (Toyo Keizai Online, Mayuko Igarashi, 2018-07-27, https://toyokeizai.net/articles/-/231051 ). [K10, K4]
- **The City History Archives on occupied Yokohama (SOURCED, 市史資料室たより / "Yokohama City History Archives newsletter", issue of 2012-07-31, "占領された街", read locally from the city's PDF https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/city-info/yokohamashi/gaiyo/shishiryo/jyohoshi/1-25.files/0134_20180918.pdf ):** as of Oct 1951 Yokohama held about **62.3% of all requisitioned land in Japan outside Okinawa** (Tokyo ~2.2%), and ~61.3% of requisitioned residential land (Tokyo ~19.4%) (figures from the city's 1958 report *横浜市の接収と復興*); Isezakicho 1–2-chōme was filled with PX, clubs and souvenir shops for soldiers; Japanese drinking streets grew in Noge, and these Japanese streets were in turn designated **off-limits to US soldiers** in principle. Text extracted from a vertically typeset PDF; quotations reassembled from fragments. [K4, K6, P5]
- **Repatriates' food stalls (SOURCED, Sanpo no Tatsujin, Fleet Yokota, 2025-03-02, https://san-tatsu.jp/articles/382217/ ):** residents displaced from the requisitioned center crossed the Ōoka River into Noge; day laborers gathered; drinking alleys grew to serve them; a Noge gyoza house dates itself to **1949**, founded by a woman repatriated from the former colonies who first ran a food cart; repatriates started stall businesses because they needed little capital; prices in 2025: fried gyoza ¥440, bottled beer ¥704. [K12, K4, K9]
- **Kotobuki-chō — the port's day-labor lodging district (SOURCED, Naka Ward page, updated 2024-03-29, https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/naka/kenko-iryo-fukushi/fukushi_kaigo/chiikifukushi/minsei/20230829111108650.html ; Kanagawa Prefecture civic-group profile, 2025-02-28, https://www.pref.kanagawa.jp/osirase/1305/saponavi-kanagawa/initiative-details/0013.html ):** the ward describes it as formerly a "town of day laborers such as port workers", now a "welfare town" centered on cheap lodging houses; about **5,700 residents, ~90% on public assistance**, aging, with some lodgings now used as language-school dormitories for young foreigners; one of Japan's three major day-labor hiring grounds. Lodging houses built on land released from requisition, and the job office's move there from Sakuragicho — UNVERIFIED (snippet of a city research journal). 8,000+ port workers at the height of cargo handling; container and mechanized handling in the 1970s shifted day labor to construction — UNVERIFIED (snippet of a Kanagawa Shimbun article; that site is refused). Kotobuki-chō General Labor Welfare Hall completed 1974 — UNVERIFIED (same). [K4, K7, K9, P9]
- **Koganecho — from illegal red-light street to arts district (SOURCED in part):** the NPO Koganecho Area Management Center works in the Hatsunecho–Koganecho–Hinodecho area; founded **2009** after the first **Koganecho Bazaar (2008)**; mission "art-based community development" so that a town "once lined with illegal sex businesses" never returns to that state; partners include city bureaus, the ward office, Yokohama City University, the prefectural police and Keikyu railway (https://koganecho.net/about/ , SOURCED). Dozens of small studios and galleries now line the two narrow streets on either side of the elevated railway between Koganecho and Hinodecho stations; the Bazaar runs each autumn, 2024 edition 33 artists, admission ¥1,000 (Time Out Tokyo, 2024-03-29, SOURCED). Police operation against the district's illegal businesses began **11 Jan 2005** after a residents' clean-up campaign from 2003; ~257 such shops at peak — UNVERIFIED (Kanagawa Shimbun snippets). [K9, K13, K1]
- **Honmoku (SOURCED, *Naka Ward History*, ch. 8 "Honmoku district", city PDF https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/naka/kusei/koho/reference/nakakushi50.files/0014_20200129.pdf , OCR text of a scanned book, read locally):** before and just after the war Honmoku's shore was a clam-digging and sea-bathing coast (a 1950 *Mainichi* item quoted: a beginner could dig about a shō of clams in 30 minutes; a simple bathhouse charged adults ¥20); US forces requisitioned schools (one became an American middle school, another a naval hospital) and Western-style houses; in **March 1953 a "Honmoku District Requisition-Release Return League" (本牧地区接収解除返還同盟)** was formed by 120 landowners (Honmoku residents and owners from elsewhere) to press for the land's return. (OCR text is imperfect; figures read as printed.) [K1, K9, P5]
- **Schools of the Chinese community (SOURCED, Arisawa Tomono, "中華学校における音楽教育", *Bulletin of Tokyo Gakugei University, Humanities and Social Sciences II* 66, 2015, pp. 205–215, read locally):** after 1949 an ideological split ran through Yokohama's overseas-Chinese community and in **1952 the "school incident"** divided the original Yokohama Chinese School into **Yokohama Yamate Chinese School (mainland-aligned)** and **Yokohama Chinese School / 横浜中華学院 (Taiwan-aligned)**; Yokohama is the only city where a school split. Aug 2014: Yamate 541 pupils (overseas Chinese 30%, ethnic Chinese mostly with Japanese nationality 66%, Japanese 4%), 39 teachers of whom 11 are alumni; 中華学院 362 pupils (12% / 42% / 46% Japanese), 18 teachers, 16 from Taiwan. Both schools have strong lion-dance, dragon-dance and folk-dance clubs whose pupils and alumni perform at Chinatown events. Wikipedia (SOURCED) adds that the original school was founded under Sun Yat-sen's influence and gives Yamate 413 pupils in 2008. [K4, K14, K11]

### B. Subcultures
- **Occupation-era bandsmen (SOURCED, ja.wikipedia "進駐軍クラブ", citing Uchida Kōichi and Tōya Mamoru):** a rating committee formed mainly of Japan Musicians' Union officers held the **first grading audition in July 1947 at Shiba Kōkaidō, Tokyo**, then judged in Yokohama, Nagoya, Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe, Kure and Fukuoka; ~200 bands graded that year. Grades S·A, S·B, A, B, C, D (1947), widened to nine (SA–F) in 1951; pay per person, first hour: S·A ¥380, S·B ¥320, A ¥290, B ¥180, C ¥150, D ¥110; each further hour ¥90–110. In Tokyo and Kanagawa the Central Liaison Office handled selection and payment. Club staff were fingerprinted, carried ID and worked two shifts. **Yokohama club list:** Zebra Club, Sea Side, Cliff Side (all NCO), **400 Club (NCO, Black-only)**, **Club 45 (EM, Black-only)**, Sakura Port (EM), **New Yorker (cabaret, Black-only)**; Zebra Club white-only until its 1955 move; from Sept 1951 Yokohama's four officers' clubs hired Japanese managers. [K4, K6, K8, K14]
- **Bandsmen's practice culture (SOURCED, Tōya Mamoru, "ポピュラー音楽にみる「アメリカ」", *グローカル研究* no. 1, Seijo University, 2014, pp. 43–60, read locally):** because auditions ranked them, bandsmen practiced "day and night" to climb a grade; jobs and audition news traveled by word of mouth among peers; one large Tokyo agency (GAY Company) also sent troupes to clubs as far as Misawa, Hachinohe and Hokkaido. Vibraphonist Sugiura Ryōzō began in the clubs as a university student and stayed based in Yokohama for the rest of his career. [K14, K7, SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Dancers and hostesses:** **Cliffside**, Motomachi — in its heyday a full band and "over 100 hostesses"; interior kept as in the late 1950s; described in 2005 as "now the only dance hall in Yokohama" (TV Tokyo *Adomachi*, broadcast 2005-09-17, SOURCED). Opened 1946 as the Yamate Butōjō with ~200 dancers at its peak; now used mainly for jazz concerts and ballroom dancing; a volunteers' committee once staged a one-night event to keep it alive — UNVERIFIED (Kanagawa Shimbun snippet; refused site). The Recreation and Amusement Association's segregated venues for GIs near Yokohama — UNVERIFIED (snippets of University of Maryland exhibition pages; the exhibition page returned 400). [K4, K9]
- **Day laborers and port workers:** see Noge and Kotobuki above. **Support activists in Kotobuki (SOURCED, prefecture profile 2025):** the Kotobuki Supporters' Exchange Group (formed **Jan 1993**, ~300 correspondence members, office in the Kotobuki Seikatsukan) runs a **Friday soup kitchen**, the year-end **Kotobuki winter struggle** (soup kitchen and patrols over New Year), a summer festival, night patrols to people sleeping rough (including a Christmas patrol), study sessions, and life-history interviews (since 2000, 80+ people, three 3-hour sessions each). [K1, K10, K4]
- **The overseas-Chinese community:** see schools above and festivals below. Chinatown's Taiwanese-run eateries in Noge (e.g., a Chinese restaurant founded **1959** by Taiwanese grandparents, now run by a grandchild, serving offal stir-fry and night-market snacks — Nikkan Gendai, 2023-09-14, SOURCED). [K4, K12]
- **Generational change in Noge's drinkers (SOURCED, Nikkan Gendai 2023-09-14):** once called a place women should stay out of; now crowded with young men and women and with groups of mothers drinking in the daytime; the district mixes sex businesses, a cinema for gay audiences and "snack" buildings beside new bistros and a food-stall village. ~840 establishments by an organizer's count — UNVERIFIED (Kanagawa Shimbun snippet). [K4, K10]
- **Artists:** Koganecho's studio community (above). **Amateur jazz players:** see C and E. [K13]
- **K11 (loose):** Japanese throughout; Chinese varieties and Mandarin in the Chinese schools (the Taiwan-aligned school teaches mainly in Mandarin; the mainland-aligned one in both Chinese and Japanese); English in the occupation-era clubs.

### C. Leisure industries
- **Amateur stages are a paid-entry system (SOURCED, Jazz Promenade 2023 street-stage entry rules, PDF https://jazzpro.jp/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/2ab32023815219dfee4c6aa1f9cc9812.pdf , read locally):** 40-minute sets plus 15-minute changeover, 12:00–19:00 on both days; **jazz only** (set list submitted in advance); big bands (11+) get PA and an operator; combo and drumless venues get only a drum kit, keyboard and amps with no PA or engineer, and some venues cap volume so drums and horns are not miked; **fee ¥2,000 per performer**; bands made up entirely of high-school-age or younger club members play free but enter the lottery; places, venues and running order are **decided by lottery** and cannot be changed; bands haul and set up their own gear; attendance at the briefing is compulsory; on-the-day venue management by the certified NPO Arcship; a festival T-shirt for players at ¥2,000. 2026: ~160 bands on ~12 street stages over two days (official announcement snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K6, K8, K14, R — shared backline and self-managed changeovers]
- **Ward-level outdoor jazz festivals:** **Honmoku Jazz Festival began in 1981** (Yokohama Arts Foundation press release for Jazz Promenade 2026, https://san-tatsu.jp/pressrelease/559006/ , SOURCED); held the last Sunday in August at the Honmoku Citizens' Park baseball ground, started by citizen volunteers who answered a call from the Naka Ward Office, with citizens taking the lead from the second year — UNVERIFIED (snippet of a city research journal; the PDF could not be fetched). **Yokohama Asahi Jazz Festival** (Asahi Ward): "over 30 years", vol. 35 on **30 Aug 2026** at the Kodomo Shizen Park baseball ground; executive committee with the ward office's culture section; advance ¥5,000, door ¥6,000, students ¥3,000, junior-high and under free; opened by a kindergarten marching band; amateur stage chosen by public audition (31 May); a youth big band and a junior-high brass band featured (city page, updated 2026-07-31, SOURCED). [K3, K8, K14]
- **The Jazz Promenade's money and scale, 2004 (SOURCED, Yokohama Keizai Shimbun, 2004-10-07, https://www.hamakei.com/special/28/ ):** budget about **¥50 million**, mostly performers' fees; city subsidy plus sponsors and ticket sales; 2003 attendance ~98,000; day pass ¥5,000, elementary pupils free, junior-high and high-school students ¥1,000; ~2,000 musicians at ~40 venues; ~320 "Jazz Crew" volunteers (about 260 recruited publicly, mostly residents, some from as far as Sendai; ~60 staff lent by other Yokohama jazz festivals), many returning year after year. 2026: Naka Ward's 100th-anniversary stage; 2 halls, ~15 street venues, 16 jazz clubs (Arts Foundation release, SOURCED). [K8, K3, K10]
- **Clubs:** **Bar Bar Bar**, Kannai — founded **1984**, live jazz almost nightly, 18:00–23:00, closed Sundays; **moved in March 2023** to a basement two minutes away (Time Out Tokyo, 2023-03-06, SOURCED) — resolves Round 1's "reported closed". Its owner founded the JAZZ Association (below). **Motion Blue Yokohama** closed in 2022 (jazzinjapan listing snippet, page 404; UNVERIFIED); the Red Brick Warehouse closed for renovation from June 2022 and Building 2 reopened 6 Dec 2022 (city press releases, snippets, UNVERIFIED). **Windjammer** founded **1972** with interior modeled on a 19th-century sailing ship's cabin; its house cocktail invented by the first owner was popular with sailors calling at the port; live music from about 20:00 nightly (Kanagawa Shimbun 2016 snippet, UNVERIFIED; some listings mark it closed, conflict unresolved). **Cliffside**: see B. [K10, K13]
- **What locals do (beyond jazz):** Noge's bar-hopping by office workers on the way home and daytime drinking by groups of mothers (SOURCED, Toyo Keizai 2018; Nikkan Gendai 2023); clam-digging, sea-bathing and boat fishing off Honmoku before reclamation (Naka Ward History, SOURCED); the street-performance festival (Round 1). [K10]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Port Opening Day and festival (SOURCED, city bulletin *広報よこはま*, May 2026, p. 26 PDF, read locally):** celebrated since **2 June 1860** (first anniversary of the port) with floats and hand-dancing through the town; from **1982** "Yokohama Dontaku" on 2 June under the theme "celebrate the opening, thank the port"; renamed **Yokohama Port Opening Festival in 1995**; ~750,000 visitors a year; 45th edition **1–2 June 2026** at Rinkō Park; a mass choir of 300+ ("Dream of Harmony"); light-and-sound show over the harbor; run by the Yokohama Port Opening Festival Executive Committee. The **International Costume Parade** began **1953** "to broadcast the port's revival", renamed **The Yokohama Parade** in 2002, 300,000+ spectators. The same page notes Yokohama ranked first in Japan for cruise-ship calls in 2025. [K2, K3, K1]
- **Postwar civic festivals on film (SOURCED, city newsreel list https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/city-info/seisaku/torikumi/kichi/sesshu/eizo-rekishi.html ):** a **Neon Festival** on Isezakicho street (14 Apr 1951); a **US–Japan summer joint concert** at the Yokohama Park music hall (1954); a **port festival celebrating the return of Shinkō Pier** (12 May–3 Jun 1956); the opening of Negishi Forest Park on the former racecourse (2 Oct 1977). [K2, K9]
- **Chinatown festivals (SOURCED, city column Jan 2025):** the Spring Festival has been documented since 1883 (*Japan Punch*); the modern tourist-facing **Spring Festival from 1986** (lion and dragon dances, stage); city-supported as the **Yokohama Spring Festival from 2022** with giant lanterns at ~32 places in central Yokohama; New Year countdowns at both temples (Gregorian) and in Yamashita-chō Park (lunar). **Kanteitan** (Guan Yu's birthday): held from early Meiji; a large celebration in July 1910; its big procession lapsed after 1923 and returned in **August 1991** as a festival for all Chinatown. Organizer of the Spring Festival: the Yokohama Chinatown Development Association cooperative (SOURCED as "since 1986", same). **Parallel national-day observances:** a Double Ten (10 Oct) parade by the Taiwan-aligned side and 1 Oct observances by the mainland-aligned side, with past clashes and heavy policing on both dates — UNVERIFIED (snippets of ROC office pages and a Taiwanese education site; the latter returned an empty page). [K2, K3, K4]
- **Kotobuki's yearly round (SOURCED, Naka Ward 2024):** April carp-streamer event with nursery children; July Tanabata with ~10 m bamboo in Kotobuki Park; autumn community sports day with a long tug-of-war (a tradition already held in the late 1960s–70s); autumn welfare festival rolling a **20 m futomaki** together. Winter struggle and summer festival by the support group (above). [K2, K10, K1]
- **Yokohama JAZZ Association (resolves Round 1 NOT FOUND):** founded **1991** as a voluntary group by Tsuruoka Hiroshi, owner of Bar Bar Bar (and an executive of Yokohama Stadium), who reached out to jazz fans and club owners because "Yokohama's musical culture was being lost"; ~200 members in 2004; office then inside the Kanagawa Shimbun's cultural business division; the Jazz Promenade began in 1993 as a joint committee of the Association (which wanted a simultaneous multi-venue festival) and the city's cultural foundation (which wanted an event for the city's own arts); chief producer Shibata Kōichi, a founding member who had worked behind the Chigusa counter as a student (Yokohama Keizai Shimbun 2004, SOURCED). Incorporated as a general incorporated association c. 2018 (2020 was its "third year since incorporation"; chair Nakagawa Akira; first formal logo July 2020) (Town News, 2020-08-06, SOURCED); membership ¥6,000 a year plus ¥1,000 joining fee (association site snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K1, K8, K13]
- **Arts governance of the festival:** Naka Ward Office, Arts Foundation, the city's Bureau of Vibrant Sports and Culture (2026 release, SOURCED). [K8]

## 2 · #112 Shanghai's ballroom and jazz era (1920s–1930s)

**Round 2 focus:** who lived around the dance floors (dancers and their recruitment, Russian émigrés, Filipino bandsmen, Jewish refugees, the Japanese community), the leisure economy of ordinary residents (the Great World, opera and storytelling), third places (cafés, bar strips, parks), rules that sorted people, and the open threads from Round 1 (ticket prices by class of hall, a musicians' union, the 1949–1954 closures).
**Headline:** the dance-hall world was the top layer of a city sorted by nationality and money. **Ticket prices ran from two or three dances for a dollar in better halls to ten for a dollar in Hongkew's waterfront dives** (1937), and dancers were mostly teenage girls from poor hinterland families, some bound to older women who took their earnings. Around them lived stateless Russians (~21,000 by 1936, 100+ shops on one avenue), ~17,000 German and Austrian Jewish refugees (1938–39) in a district of cafés later confined by the Japanese, and a Japanese community that grew from ~20,000 to over 100,000 after 1937. Ordinary Shanghainese leisure ran through the Great World amusement center (from 1917) and women's opera fandom rather than the ballrooms.

### A. Other settings
- **The dancers' world (SOURCED, *Current History*, Oct 1937, pp. 100–101, crediting the *Manchester Guardian*; reprinted Shanghai Sojourns 2018-08-02 at https://andrew-field-05n7.squarespace.com/shanghais-dancing-world/2018/8/2/a-good-summary-account-of-shanghais-dance-hall-industry-in-1937 ):** 100+ dance halls and cabarets employing about 5,000 professional partners; some opened in the afternoon, one or more at the lunch break; most partners Chinese girls aged about 15–25, mostly in their late teens; ten years earlier nearly all were Russian, mostly from Harbin; about 80% of the Chinese partners were illiterate though many spoke English well; most came from poor hinterland families, and **some were bought outright or held under contract by older women, to whom they handed their earnings in return for food, clothing and lodging**. [K4, K7, K9, P9]
- **Russian émigré quarter (SOURCED, Shen Jialu, *Shanghai Tan* magazine via The Paper, 2021-01-25, https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_10905316 ; English version, Sixth Tone, 2022-01-24):** ~210 Russians in the French Concession in 1920, ~21,000 in Shanghai by 1936; a refugee fleet of 30 vessels brought 1,900+; most lived in the French Concession, a smaller group around Sichuan Road Central and Wujin Road; their commercial street was **Avenue Joffre** (now Middle Huaihai Road between Chongqing and Shaanxi Roads): 100+ Russian shops, about 85% of the street's foreign businesses, and 40+ Russian restaurants and cafés (Tkachenko Brothers Café on Sinan Road the oldest and largest); a sausage factory, a brewery and a vodka distillery nearby; a Pushkin monument (1937); stateless after Soviet revocation of citizenship, some with Nansen passports, **without the extraterritorial privileges of treaty nationals**; the French authorities allowed them autonomous bodies and gave free medical care and job help. ~1,100 left on the Soviet ship *Ilyich* on **6 Aug 1947**. [K4, K9, K1]
- **Jewish refugee district (SOURCED, US Holocaust Memorial Museum encyclopedia, "German and Austrian Jewish Refugees in Shanghai", https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-and-austrian-jewish-refugees-in-shanghai ):** ~1,500 refugees at the end of 1938, nearly 17,000 a year later; no visa needed to land until August 1939; the poorest crowded into Hongkew (under Japanese control after 1937); the Committee for Assistance ran five group shelters (the Ward Road shelter opened Jan 1939 in a converted barracks), ~2,500 living in them by end-1939 at six to 150 per room, ~4,500 more eating at their soup kitchens; JDC funding rose from $5,000 (1938) to $100,000 (1939); by 1940 the Chusan Road area had European-style cafés, delis, nightclubs and bakeries. In **February 1943** the Japanese ordered stateless refugees who had arrived after 1937 into a "designated area" in Hongkew, with exit passes from a refugee bureau (UNVERIFIED snippets of USHMM and USC Shoah pages). [K4, K9, K1]
- **Japanese community (SOURCED, Tanaka Hitoshi, Zou Can, Maeda Teruhito, "1930年代上海日本居留民社会的变迁", Osaka University Forum on China Discussion Paper 2015-10, read locally):** over 10,000 by WWI (Japanese textile mills; then the largest foreign group), ~20,000 by the mid-1920s, slow growth, then a jump after August 1937 to **over 100,000 in 1943–44**; a class structure described as a company-employee elite (~3%) and middle layer (~40%) living in the concessions and upper Hongkew, and the rest small traders, craftsmen and service workers based in Hongkew; the residents' association was contested by "textile", "company" and "local" factions; street federations and neighborhood associations; the Japan Club and a Japanese youth club. [K4, K1]
- **Waterfront bar districts (SOURCED, Paul French, "Into the Shanghai Trenches", *Los Angeles Review of Books* China Blog, 2015-04-14):** a pre-WWI brothel district centered on Scott Road in the Northern External Roads (~300 foreign and Chinese brothels at its height; robbery of newcomers by mixed-nationality gangs; under Japanese gendarmerie control in the late 1930s; mostly destroyed by bombing by 1945); sailor bars and brothels along Wayside and Broadway in Hongkew from about 1850, joined in the 1930s by Jewish-run bars and cabarets on the edge of the refugee district, some lasting into the early 1950s; in Frenchtown, a bar strip on **Rue Chu Pao San** (now Xikou Road, just off the Bund) for sailors, soldiers and slummers, with the Palais Cabaret, the 'Frisco, Mumms, the Crystal, George's Bar, Monk's Brass Rail, the New Ritz and The Manhattan, lasting into the 1940s. Its popular name came from fist fights between foreign sailors and soldiers (Bristol Historical Photographs of China caption, UNVERIFIED snippet). ### B. Subcultures
- **Dancers:** see A. In better halls management usually let a dancer keep **half** her ticket earnings, with a lower commission on drinks; most barely made a living; a few with rich patrons up to £100 a month; the larger halls' average about £10 a month and the minimum ~£2, "above the average factory girl's wage"; much of it went on cosmetics and clothes (*Current History* 1937, SOURCED). A 2017 popular-history piece gives the top dancers' share as 70–80% with a fixed monthly salary, ordinary dancers 50/50, dancers hired publicly, trained and issued a hall pass (Phoenix News, 2017-06-14, SOURCED as a claim; secondary). **Recruitment and segregation (SOURCED, James Farrer & Andrew Field, "From Interzone to Transzone", *Intersections* 31, ANU, http://intersections.anu.edu.au/issue31/farrer-field.htm ):** the first paid partners (late 1910s–early 1920s) were Russian and Japanese; Chinese hostesses appeared in **1928** and soon outnumbered them; tens of thousands of young women, mostly from the hinterland, worked as dancers in the 1930s–40s; Chinese elites at first avoided the ballrooms as un-Confucian. [K4, K7, K8]
- **Russian émigrés' work (SOURCED, The Paper 2021):** light-music and jazz bands at the Lyceum, Astor House, Cathay, Metropole and Paramount; many joined the Municipal Orchestra; women taught dancing in clubs, worked as cinema ushers and hotel hostesses; strong men worked as bodyguards; poorer men repaired shoes, ground knives, pulled rickshaws or busked; 10 of the 17 first professors at the National Conservatory were Russian; ~200 Russian newspapers and magazines over the decades; a Russian theater troupe at the Paris Theater; a retired officers' association; church schools and a Russian law-and-commerce school. A 1929 Settlement police estimate attributed 85% of foreign crime to Russians, and a 1935 League of Nations survey figure (22% of Russian women aged 16–45 in sex work) is widely quoted without stated method — both reported as the source gives them. [K4, K14, K9]
- **Filipino bandsmen:** a Filipino bandleader, Jose Contreras, led a "Greater East Asia Concert" at the Queen's Theater under Japanese occupation, mixing Japanese, Chinese, Indian and Filipino songs; dance bands took up Chinese songs after Anglo-American films were banned (*Times Week*, 21 Mar 1943, reprinted 2018, SOURCED). A **Filipino Musicians' Union of Shanghai** existed with named officers and a 1942 music committee and a 1944 general meeting — UNVERIFIED (snippets of a Cambridge book glossary, refused 403, and of an ABS-CBN article, refused in Round 1); founding date and size NOT FOUND. [K1, K14]
- **Women's opera audiences (SOURCED, Fujino Mako, "越劇の受容者とテクスト", *言語と文化*, Kwansei Gakuin University, c. 2019–20, read locally):** Yue opera, staged from the 1930s by all-female troupes, built a mainly female audience of migrants from northern Zhejiang (Shaoxing, Ningbo, Hangzhou); middle-class and lower-middle-class women formed its most devoted fans and patrons (citing Jiang Jin 2010); radio broadcast it all day; libretto booklets and fan publications sold widely (a 1939 collection listed the radio stations that carried it); top actresses began reforming the repertoire in the 1940s. [K4, K13, K11]
- **Students and families in the halls (SOURCED, *Current History* 1937):** families with grandmothers and infants rented tables by the hour; patrons in better Chinese halls drank little liquor, preferring tea or soft drinks; less reputable halls targeted high-school and university students; some husbands danced while wives sat on the sidelines. [K4, K12]

### C. Leisure industries
- **Price ladder (SOURCED, *Current History* 1937; resolves Round 1's open thread):** in better-class halls $1 bought two or three dance tickets; in Hongkew's dives $1 bought ten; better halls added vaudeville acts between dances; the largest new Chinese-run hall covered several acres with a swimming pool, a restaurant and 100+ partners (mainly Chinese, some Russian and Eurasian); the first Chinese-owned hall opened around 1926. [K8, K12]
- **Hotel and club floor shows, 1933 (SOURCED, *The China Press*, 9 Feb 1933, reprinted 2018):** Cathay Ballroom (a tango orchestra and two Black American entertainers newly arrived); the Plaza Hotel's Rose Room revue; the Little Club on Bubbling Well Road with a "symphonic orchestra" at dinner, an all-American dance band and a weekly College Night; the Canidrome Ballroom with a manager, MC, tap dancers and singers in a revue. [K13]
- **The Great World (大世界) (SOURCED, Shao Dong, The Paper, 2016-12-28, https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1587526 ):** opened **14 Jul 1917** by Huang Chujiu; admission two jiao, then free movement between all theaters until midnight (opening at noon, 9 a.m. on Sundays); regional and Peking opera, "civilized drama", comic shows, storytelling and pingtan on the second floor, acrobatics, magic, puppets, martial arts, films day and night with a rooftop open-air screen; taken over in **1931** by the gangster Huang Jinrong's company after the founder's death and bank collapse, after which fights were frequent; taken over by the city culture bureau **2 Jul 1954**, renamed People's Amusement Park 1955, back to Great World 1958, a warehouse and then Youth Palace (1974) in the Cultural Revolution period, reopened 1987, closed 2003, reopened Dec 2016 as an intangible-heritage center; ~12,000 visitors a day in the 1950s, capped at 15,000 tickets. A 1953 account (Yang Li-hsin, *China Monthly Review*, 1 Jun 1953, reprinted on Field's site, SOURCED): renovated 1951; four floors, three or four theaters of 400–500 seats per upper floor; ping-pong, billiards, a ballroom, a roof garden, a summer skating rink and open-air cinema; a free clinic in a former shrine room; admission ¥2,000 (old currency, under 10 US cents); ~800 staff; 45,000+ visitors a day at Spring Festival; performers attended union-run study groups; **all brothels closed November 1951**. [K8, K10, K9, K13]

## 3 · #41 Hofbräuhaus, Munich

**Round 2 focus:** the house's regulars and staff, its neighborhood (the Platzl), the city's beer-hall rules and the institutions that grew around beer (beer-garden law, a pouring-watchdog association, beer-price riots), the folk-dance and tavern-music calendar it hosts, post-1945 history, and Round 1's open threads (Stammtisch signage and rules, staff working life, the Bergwacht founding date).
**Headline:** Munich treats the price and measure of beer as public business. A government-set beer tariff provoked the **1844 riots**, which ended when the king had the Hofbräuhaus sell at a lower provisional price; a **citizens' association against short pouring** has existed since 1899 (banned by the Nazis, refounded 1970, ~4,000 members); a **royal decree of 1812** let brewers serve over their cellars but not sell food, which is why guests may still bring their own food to a beer garden, a right written into a 1999 state ordinance after **~20,000–25,000 people marched in 1995** against a court-imposed early closing. Inside the house, the city's cultural department and the Hofbräuhaus jointly run Bavarian folk-dance nights four times a year plus the **Kathreintanz** that closes the dance season in late November.

### A. Other settings
- **The Platzl (SOURCED, muenchen.de "Platzl", © 2026):** not a planned square but a widening of Orlandostraße, named since at least 1780; the Hofbräuhaus fills almost the whole east side; the **Orlandohaus** (1900) to the north with a ground-floor arcade; burghers' houses at Platzl 2 and 3 (cores c. 1565 and medieval; renovated 1987/88 with heavy loss of fabric); several student corps and a Burschenschaft have been based on the square since the 19th century (three corps remain); cobblestones, a pedestrian zone. **The Volkssänger (folk-singer) stage** opposite the Hofbräuhaus in a 600-seat hall converted in 1888 ran from **1906 to the mid-1990s**; Weiß Ferdl joined in 1907 and directed it for decades; it shaped visitors' picture of Bavaria through costume, dialect and Alpine settings; Weiß Ferdl showed early sympathy for the NSDAP and appeared at its events from the early 1920s (same page; muenchenwiki snippet adds 36 years as director and the 1953 fountain at the Viktualienmarkt, UNVERIFIED). About 800 full-time folk singers were registered in Munich in 1905 (munich.travel snippet; page refused 403, UNVERIFIED). [K13, K9, P3]
- **Staff spaces and work (SOURCED, hofbraeuhaus.de "Jobs and careers"):** staff work across the Schwemme, beer garden, Bräustüberl and Festsaal; pay advertised as "above the collective agreement" with training on offer; applications by email or phone. ## 4 · #42 Australian pub rock and the suburban "beer barns" (1970s–1980s)

*(As in Round 1, the large venues are called "large suburban hotels" here; colloquial terms are filed in the slang file.)*

**Round 2 focus:** who could and could not drink in a pub (women, Aboriginal people, under-18s) and how those rules changed; Indigenous bands and women performers on the circuit; the licensed clubs that competed with pubs; pay and booking; the 2010 Melbourne licensing fight and youth all-ages music; pub-room design; and Round 1's open threads (band fees and door deals, Musicians' Union rates).
**Headline:** the pub-rock room was the end point of a long sorting of who was allowed in a bar. **Aboriginal people could not lawfully be supplied alcohol in NSW until 1963** (South Australia 1965; Queensland reserves 1971), except with an exemption certificate; **women were barred from public bars in Queensland until 1970**; under-18s were and are excluded, which produced a separate government-funded all-ages circuit in Victoria from 1986. Bands earned roughly **$200 a night at the bottom and ~$3,000 at the top (1983)**; the door deal displaced fixed fees in the late 1970s. In NSW, **mutual clubs funded by poker machines (legal in clubs from 1956)** were a parallel mass-leisure system with their own rooms.

### A. Other settings
- **Pub-room design (SOURCED, Tanja Luckins, *The Conversation*, 2022-12-21):** six o'clock closing ran roughly from WWI to the 1960s in the south-eastern states; little renovation in its first decade (licensing uncertainty, fear of prohibition, local wet-or-dry polls); major remodeling in the later 1930s, pushed by Licensing Boards (kitchens, toilets, bars resurfaced in rubber, linoleum and tiles); rebuilt pubs were usually brewery-owned, with Tooheys and Tooth & Co competing in NSW; tiles followed a wider hygiene trend rather than being simply for hosing down after the closing-time rush (a common belief the author qualifies). [K6, K8, P3]
- **The sorting of drinkers (SOURCED, Matt Allen, *The Conversation*, 2016-06-03):** NSW adopted six o'clock closing by referendum in **1916** after a drunken soldiers' riot; the closing-time rush "pushed women out of the public bar and into ladies' lounges"; 10 p.m. closing followed a **1954** referendum; reduced Sunday trading is a legacy. Western Australia restored 10 p.m. in 1959, Victoria 1966, South Australia last (first legal after-six beer 28 Sep 1967) (Wikipedia article on six o'clock closing, search snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K6, K9]
- **Licensed clubs (SOURCED, abstract of J. Cornwall, *For members and their guests: a history of clubland in New South Wales 1880–1980*, PhD, UTS, 2010, https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/37840 ; full text closed):** clubs became a popular movement in the 1930s and "mass leisure institutions" after WWII; the postwar suburban club grew from six o'clock closing and the anti-brewery, anti-hotel sentiment it fed, from new attitudes to drinking and leisure, and from **poker-machine revenue** (machines present in clubs from the early 1900s, legalized by the Cahill Labor government in **1956** in the belief that they gave working men access to club life); the clubs' decline began in the 1970s. [K8, K10, K4]
- **The large suburban ring (SOURCED, Sarah Taylor, "Lost Venues, Long Nights", *Cordite*, p. 2):** 1983 maps show few inner-city clusters and a ring of suburban venues, especially in Sydney; large Sydney suburban rooms often ignored fire rules, so crowds exceeded capacity; new Sydney fire regulations a few years into the 1980s and random breath testing (all states, 1982 per this source) shifted the circuit; in the 1990s live music clustered more than before. [K5, K6, P5]

## 5 · #43 The British folk club movement (pub back rooms)

**Round 2 focus:** the older pub-singing traditions the clubs grew beside (East Suffolk Saturday sings, Sheffield pub carols, London's Irish sessions), the seasonal and festival calendar, the morris world that shares the same pubs, Scottish and Welsh beginnings, youth and education, club economics today, and Round 1's open threads (current admission prices; the end of the MusTrad chapter).
**Headline:** the folk club sits among older, rule-bound pub singing customs. In **East Suffolk** a chairman kept order with a tap on the table, singers were called by name, men avoided songs "owned" by another singer, and **a man who would not sing paid for a gallon of beer**. Around **Sheffield**, carols dropped by the churches are still sung in a dozen village pubs **from the week after Remembrance Sunday to Christmas**, with soloists heard in silence. **Irish building workers** brought sessions into London pubs from about 1946. Today a guest night costs about **£7–£18**, a singers' night £0–£1; organizers lose rooms when pub managers change (one club left its hotel of **39 years and 1,598 concerts** after new room charges). Morris dancing, which uses the same pubs, has **~13,600 dancers in ~812 sides (2020), average age 55, now gender-balanced**.

### A. Other settings
- **The East Suffolk country pub (SOURCED, Keith Summers, "Sing, Say or Pay! ## 6 · #115 Barrowland Ballroom, Glasgow

**Round 2 focus:** the dance-era rules (Round 1's main NOT FOUND), the market traders and the founder's barrow business, the street outside (the Gallowgate's football and Irish pubs, the Calton weavers' memory), the city's holiday calendar, newer communities using the market, nearby civic institutions, and Round 1's open threads (fire date, the 1970s, how bookings work).
**Headline:** for most of its dance years the Barrowland, like most Glasgow halls, **sold no alcohol** — patrons drank in pubs first and some smuggled bottles in handbags — and in **1962 Parliament debated, and rejected (35 to 130), a Glasgow MP's clause to ban liquor certificates in all Scottish dance halls**, argued on the grounds that parents let daughters go unaccompanied because no drink was sold. The market began as **a barrow-hire business (300 barrows) for hawkers moved on by police**, serving Highland, island and Irish incomers; the Gallowgate outside is the traditional walk to Celtic Park, lined with supporters' pubs; and Calton's weavers, shot by soldiers in **1787**, are still commemorated each 3 September.

### A. Other settings
- **The barrow business (SOURCED, Robbie Armstrong & Stuart Edwards, *The Bell*, 2024-12-24, preview text; rest paywalled):** the founder began minding a friend's fruit barrow at Parkhead Cross at 12; married in 1902; she and her husband ran a fruit shop in Bridgeton and she sold fruit to theater-goers; seeing the need, she bought barrows to rent to other traders, **building a stock of 300 at a yard on Marshall Lane, Calton**; in 1920 they acquired the lot between Kent Street and Moncur Street and opened it as a market in 1921 for **hawkers who had been moved on by police** from crowded streets; customers and traders were working-class incomers from the Highlands, the islands and Ireland; after six weekends of heavy rain in 1926 she roofed the Moncur Street side, and by 1928 it was enclosed on all sides, allowing permanent shops; widowed in 1930 with nine children.
