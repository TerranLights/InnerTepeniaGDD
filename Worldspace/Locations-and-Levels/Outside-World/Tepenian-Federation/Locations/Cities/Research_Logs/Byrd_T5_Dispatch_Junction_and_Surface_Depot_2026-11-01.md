**What failed: the GTH land deal**

- The province, through the GTH, paid two Regina businessmen **$103,000 an acre for 204 acres**, about
  **$21 million**, land linked to the Regina Bypass. Provincial Auditor **Judy Ferguson's report (30 June 2016)**

  of fraud or wrongdoing"*; she stressed it was a process audit, not a forensic one. The RCMP closed a *"highly
  complex"* two-year investigation with **no charges**. The information and privacy commissioner later said the
  deals rested on *"factually wrong"* information (Regina Leader-Post, 12 September 2017). Wikipedia notes

**The port's own districts: logport, built on dead steelworks**

- **logport I, Rheinhausen: 265 ha, the former Krupp steelworks, commissioned 1998, "over 5,000 direct jobs
  created since 1999."** Later sites: logport II Wanheim 35 ha (2006), III Hohenbudberg 15 ha (2013), IV
  Kamp-Lintfort 30 ha (2014), V Oberhausen 25 ha (2021), VI Walsum 40 ha (2022). *"More than 7,000 jobs have
  already been created on the logport sites."* **SOURCED** (duisport, logport page; operator's claim).
  DERIVED: total logport area 265 + 35 + 15 + 30 + 25 + 40 = **410 ha**, of which logport I is 65%.
- **The steelworks that died there:** *"Es war 1993, als auf diesem Gelände in Duisburg-Rheinhausen die Lichter
  endgültig ausgingen"* (it was 1993 when the lights finally went out on this site); *"über 5000 Menschen verloren
  ihren Job"* (over 5,000 people lost their jobs). About **100,000 people** joined the protest, *"den größten und
  mit 160 Tagen längsten Streik in der Stahlindustrie"* (the largest and, at 160 days, longest action in the steel
  industry), which only delayed the closure. **SOURCED** (Böckler, 2011). German History in Documents and Images
  says the closure threatened **over 6,000** jobs (search 9 snippet). **Conflict noted.** The protest's dates
  (commonly given as 1987 to 1988): **UNVERIFIED** (the fetch summary placed it "about ten years before"; not
  resolved).
- **What the new jobs are (2011):** *"50 Transport- und Lagerunternehmen angesiedelt, in denen rund 3500 Menschen
  Arbeit gefunden haben"* (50 transport and warehousing firms, about 3,500 people); *"Gabelstaplerfahrer, Packer,
  Kommissionierer - all das sind meist Anlerntätigkeiten"* (forklift drivers, packers, order pickers: mostly
  semi-skilled jobs); these workers *"verdienen ... deutlich weniger als die Stahlarbeiter von damals"* (earn much
  less than the steelworkers did). About **40,000** jobs then depended on the port. **SOURCED** (Böckler, 2011).
  DERIVED: logport I jobs grew from about 3,500 (2011) to "over 5,000" (operator, undated page); the operator's

- **Pay and bargaining (2011):** not all firms had a collective agreement, and those that did used the
  freight-forwarding (Spedition) agreement; the port company (Hafen AG) left the municipal employers' association

  froh, dass sie überhaupt einen Job haben"* (many came out of unemployment and are glad to have any job at all);
  a works-council member at Wincanton: *"Streiken könnten wir nicht"* (we could not strike). **SOURCED** (Böckler,
  2011).

**The old port quarter: Ruhrort**

- **Schifferbörse (shipping exchange), opened 1901**, to bring order to cargo trading: *"Before this, business
  transactions used to be conducted in the open streets, with boatmen frequently being cheated with ploys like
  false freight charges or excessive commissions."* Around 1900 Ruhrort had *"more millionaires per capita living
  here than in Berlin"*; *"the pub scene and nightlife were dominated by boatmen and sailors"*; Ruhrort was
  *"mentioned in the same breath with the likes of St. Pauli, Hamburg until far into the middle of the last
  century."* The port tour ends at *"one of the quaint boatmen pubs, where boat owners used to pick up their mail
  in former times."* **SOURCED** (Duisburg Kontor leaflet, 2020).
- **Decline:** *"When a wave of rationalization and job-cutting hit the shipping industry, boaters were able to
  enjoy shore leave less and less, leaving this district of the city mired in economic slump,"* the setting for
  the TV detective Horst Schimanski; today *"artists and creative spirits liven up the pubs and shops"*; a
  "Kreativquartier." The Museum of German Inland Navigation sits in a former Art Nouveau swimming pool; the last
  paddle steamer, *Oscar Huber*, is a museum ship. **SOURCED** (same leaflet).
- Early residents were *"predominantly skippers who earned their money through coal trade on the Ruhr and onward
  transport on the Rhine"* (KuLaDig heritage inventory, search 10 snippet). **SOURCED (snippet).**

### 2.2 SubTropolis, Kansas City, Missouri: the warehouse city entered by truck

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Hunt Midwest, SubTropolis page, https://huntmidwest.com/subtropolis · National
Association of Letter Carriers, *The Postal Record*, February 2023, "SubTropolis" (PDF saved by WebFetch, text
extracted locally), https://www.nalc.org/news/the-postal-record/2023/february-2023/document/Subtropolis.pdf ·
English Wikipedia, "SubTropolis," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SubTropolis.
**Dead end:** Axios Kansas City, 3 February 2026, HTTP 403 (died at the source).

**What it is and how it was made**

- Lateral limestone mining began in **1945** (Midwest Precote, an asphalt producer); the first underground
  commercial storage opened **1964**; Lamar Hunt's company bought the miner in **1970**. Up to **160 ft**
  underground in the Bethany Falls limestone; tunnels **16 ft high, 40 ft wide**, between **25-ft-square
  pillars**; facilities are **addressed by pillar number** and streets are mostly **named for rock layers**;
  *truck idling is prohibited* (fumes); AM-FM radio does not reach inside. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- The miners left **55 million sq ft** of space, of which **14 million** is suitable for offices and warehouses;
  the limestone dug out *"was used to pave roads and airport tarmacs in Kansas City."* *"The pillars also function
  as address markers to navigate the cavernous space — SFS is located at Pillar 210."* **10 miles of roads and two
  miles of railroad track**, **500-plus truck docks**; rent about **half** of surface rates. **SOURCED** (Postal
  Record, 2023).
- Operator figures: **14 million sq ft, 10 million developed since 1964**; **13 miles of lighted, paved roads**;
  clear heights 14 to 17 ft by building; **55+ tenants, 2,500+ tenant employees**; tenant types *"food and
  beverage, records storage, data center, pharmaceutical, distribution, and automotive"*; **24/7 armed security**;
  a new warehouse of up to 200,000 sq ft in **as little as 120 days**; energy savings **50% to 70%**; a tenant:
  *"70 and overcast every day."* **SOURCED** (Hunt Midwest; marketing claims).
- **Conflicts:** road length 10 miles (Postal Record) vs 10.5 (Wikipedia introduction) vs 6 (Wikipedia layout
  section) vs 13 (operator); developed space "about half" of 14 million (Postal Record) vs 10 million (operator) vs
  "more than 9 million" (Axios snippet). Unresolved.

**Who works there and how many**

- About **1,300** employees and **50 tenants (around 2000)**; **55 tenants (2010)**; **over 2,000 employees and
  6 million sq ft leased (2024)** (Wikipedia); operator says 2,500+. **SOURCED.**
- **The postal stamp center:** USPS Stamp Fulfillment Services, arrived **1982**, **180 employees**, **520,000 sq
  ft**; *"At any given time, we have about $3 billion worth of stamps within the facility"*; about **59,000 orders
  a day** in peak season; *"Besides the SFS employees, 2,000 other office and warehouse workers at SubTropolis
  toil under the rock each day."* Workers call it **"the caves."** Letter carriers deliver daily to an
  **above-ground mailroom**. **SOURCED** (Postal Record, 2023; Bill Gicker, USPS Director of Stamp Services).
- **Other named tenants:** National Archives Federal Records Center; EPA Region 7 Training and Logistics Center;
  Underground Vaults and Storage (*"distributor of last resort"* for film studios); Ford (leased **25 acres** in the
  early 1970s for vehicle inventory); early food tenants Pillsbury and Russell Stover; **W. W. Grainger**, a
  facility *"set to become the world's largest underground distribution center upon completion in summer 2026."*
  **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). Whether the Grainger center opened: NOT FOUND.

**What it is like, and what goes wrong**

- **Floors tilt** from erosion and settling; automated machinery stands on bedrock *"so that nothing moves under that
  machinery"*; in one conference room staff joke about getting a seat *"so they won't roll away from the table on
  the tilted floor."* **Blasting every day around 5 p.m.:** *"You can hear the explosions as they make the caves
  bigger ... It's a little unnerving."* *"If we get tight on space ... they just blast out a wall and we expand
  into it."* **SOURCED** (Postal Record, 2023, quoting Gicker). So the mine still operates beside the tenants
  (DERIVED from the blasting account; Wikipedia also calls it a working mine).
- **2001 anthrax scare:** 50 postal employees in the complex were tested after handling mail from the Brentwood,
  D.C., center; antibiotics given; no infection. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- **Neighbors:** Kansas City's caverns total about **25 million sq ft, roughly 10% of the metro's industrial
  space**, and mine storage in the 1950s and 1960s made the city a cold-storage center (Wikipedia); Louisville
  Mega Cavern (Kentucky) and a former mine at Butler, Pennsylvania (Corbis, federal records) are named as similar.
  **SOURCED.**

- The **dispatch center** is set up in **mid-December** each year; in 2013 it was run by retired police officers
  (dispatch supervisor David Madder; assistant supervisor Richard Bulley, a retired Ontario Provincial Police
  hostage negotiator): *"a unique job, a unique location and a really good people to work with"*; *"You form a very
  close bond with the guys if you work around them for three months."* **SOURCED** (Sevunts, 2013).
- **Trip:** about **15 hours to Ekati**, with a sleep at **Lockhart Camp** (hot meals, laundry, showers); driver
  Dave Cook (Nova Scotia, six seasons) rejects the TV picture of drivers staying awake for long stretches:
  *"They don't do that."* In storms trucks are funneled into the camps (*"We have 98 trucks at Lockhart Camp"*).
  Three maintenance camps (Dome Lake, Lockhart Lake, Lac de Gras) house **49 personnel each**. Loads: diesel,
  ammonium nitrate for blasting, earth-moving machines. Pay is now similar to southern hauling, which has cut the
  draw; some drivers prefer to stay home. **SOURCED** (Sevunts, 2013).
- One carrier's Yellowknife terminal needs **about 200 drivers each season**, planned from November, drawn from
  returning drivers, other terminals and outside carriers; U.S. drivers cannot be used without special Canadian
  work permits. **SOURCED (search 21 snippet of a KAG Canada page; the page returned 404).**

**What fails**

- **2006** was *"catastrophic"*: *"one lake's ice disintegrated so quickly that trucks were trapped at mine sites"*

  because of warmth (Sevunts sidebar). The table shows 2010 at **33 days and 3,508 loads**. **SOURCED.**
- **Climate:** the road runs *"for an average of around two months each year"* and *"costs more than $20 million
  to build and operate annually"*; a study found viability *"slipped noticeably at 2C"* with *"barely any chance
  of getting to a 50-day season at 4C."* Operations director Mike Lowing (Rio Tinto): *"We worry more about the
  portages going before the ice does"*; *"We go over 55 portages"*; *"This year we punched just a tad under 6,200
  loads"* (2021); all three active diamond mines were scheduled to close by the **mid-2030s** with *"nothing yet
  lined up to replace them."* **SOURCED** (Cabin Radio, 2021).
- **2026:** a record warm winter reportedly cut weight limits by mid-March and closed large sections to heavy loads
  by 30 March 2026; the season has reportedly shortened by about two weeks on average over 20 years (Skillings,
  an industry analysis site); a May 2026 Cabin Radio story says the road enters a *"new, uncertain era"* after Rio
  Tinto stepped back as lead party (headline via ilrtoday.ca). **UNVERIFIED** (weak or unread sources; same status
  as in T10).
- **Conflict:** length 400 km (Joint Venture "About," T10) vs "+600 km" (Joint Venture poster, including the
  Lupin end) vs 568 km (Wikipedia, T10); portages 65 (poster) vs "over 55" (Lowing, 2021).

### 3.2 Driver hours, rest and the radio

- **Australia (Heavy Vehicle National Law, standard hours, solo driver):** in any 5½ hours, at most 5¼ hours' work
  and a 15-minute rest; in any **24 hours, at most 12 hours' work and at least 7 continuous hours of stationary
  rest**; in any 7 days, at most **72 hours' work** with 24 continuous hours off; a **work diary** is required once
  a driver goes **more than 100 km from base**; work time rounds **up** to the next 15 minutes and rest **down**;
  *"outer limits only."* **SOURCED** (NHVR, search 30 snippets).
- **The Northern Territory is outside it:** the NT *"has chosen not to adopt"* the national fatigue provisions
  *"until they are shown to work in remote Australia"*; a work diary is *"not specifically required"*; operators
  answer instead to NT WorkSafe health-and-safety duties; trips into Western Australia follow WA's own system.
  **SOURCED** (NT Government, "Fatigue management for operating across borders," search 31 snippet).
- **United States (FMCSA, property carriers):** **11 hours' driving** within a **14-hour** on-duty window; a
  **30-minute break after 8 cumulative hours of driving**; **60 hours in 7 days or 70 in 8**; a **34-hour restart**.
  **SOURCED (search 37; mostly training sites; the official current text was not read: treat as UNVERIFIED
  against 49 CFR Part 395).**
- **Canada (federal regulation):** **13 hours' driving, 14 hours on duty** in a day; cycle 1 **70 hours in 7
  days**, cycle 2 **120 hours in 14 days**, reset after 36 or 72 hours off; a higher cycle (80 hours in 7 days)
  north of 60°N is reported. Daily off-duty: **8 consecutive hours** (one source) vs **10 hours** (another).
  **SOURCED (secondary; conflict noted; regulation text not read).**
- **Radio:** UHF CB **channel 40** is *"the primary road safety channel Australia-wide, most commonly used by trucks
  including pilot/escort vehicles for oversized loads"*; **channel 29** on the Pacific Highway (New South Wales and
  Queensland); caravanners use channel 18; drivers wanting to overtake a road train, or pulling over to let one
  pass, are told to call the truck on 40; keep language clean *"since anyone can tune in."* **SOURCED**

### 4.2 Laredo, Texas: a truck inland port with an office economy

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Texas A&M International University, A.R. Sanchez School of Business, Texas Center for
Border Economic and Enterprise Development, *Economic Outlook Report*, vol. 18, no. 1, May 2024 (published by the
Laredo Chamber; PDF saved by WebFetch, text extracted locally),
https://laredochamber.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/202405-Vision.pdf · Cargado, "Transloading vs Through-Trailer,"
https://cargado.com/insights/transloading-vs-through-trailer · NCBFAA, reposting Spectrum News 1 (5 June 2025),
https://ncbfaa.org/news-advocacy/ncbfaa-in-the-news/2025/12/02/changing-tariffs-increase-workload-for-laredo-customs-broker.
**Dead ends:** Overdrive, "Laredo's shuffle," HTTP 403; TAMIU presentation of 20 October 2025, HTTP 404 (both died at
the source); a count of Laredo's licensed brokers from the brokers' association (search 40; died at the query).

**The office cluster**

- *"This network organically developed within a 40-mile radius spanning the border, comprises over **500 customs
  brokerage firms**, in excess of **500 freight carriers**, and more than **300 warehousing and storage
  facilities**. This dynamic ecosystem supports the livelihoods of over **30,000 individuals employed across the
  border**."* **SOURCED** (TAMIU, May 2024). It counts both sides (Laredo and Nuevo Laredo).
- **The city:** population **256,187** (2022 estimate); 74,380 households, **3.39** persons per household; median
  household income **$60,928**; per-capita income about $25,000; 21% with a bachelor's degree; civilian workforce
  **115,726 (2023)**; unemployment about **3.4% to 4.5%** across 2021 to 2023 after **11.6%** in the second
  quarter of 2020. **SOURCED** (TAMIU, May 2024; read from charts, so exact quarter values are approximate).
- **Volumes:** **2,936,130 inbound truck crossings (2023)**, *"almost two times that of the next busiest truck
  crossing in Detroit"*; *"over 18,500 truck crossings daily between north and southbound traffic, with over 15,000
  crossing through the World Trade Bridge and about 3,500 via the Colombia Solidarity Bridge"*; **5.84 million**
  truck crossings in all (2023); **4,415** inbound trains (2023), about **12 trains a day** carrying about
  **1,456 containers**; the World Trade Bridge *"alone processes nearly 40% of all trade between the U.S. and
  Mexico."* **SOURCED** (TAMIU, May 2024). Trade value **$353.94 billion (2025)**, the leading U.S. land port and
  third among all U.S. ports in 2025 after topping the list in 2023 and 2024 (Laredo EDC, KGNS, FreightWaves, search
  41). **SOURCED (snippets).** One figure of "roughly 20,000 trucks per day" (September 2025 report): conflict with
  18,500 noted.
- **Laredo's occupational fingerprint (BLS, above):** highest concentration in the nation of cargo and freight
  agents (**LQ 11.53**) and of dispatchers (**LQ 3.60**). DERIVED: 840 + 510 = **1,350** people in those two
  occupations alone in the Laredo metro (May 2023; excludes self-employed brokers).

**What the work is: freight changes hands three times**

- *"A Laredo truckload is three legs with up to four companies"*: a U.S. carrier to a Laredo yard; a **transfer
  (drayage) carrier** across the bridge to a Nuevo Laredo yard (the transfer driver is called a **transfista**); a
  Mexican carrier to the consignee. **Through-trailer** moves keep the loaded trailer intact while tractors and
  drivers swap around it; **transloads** unload and reload at the border (two extra handlings and a broken chain of
  custody). *"The yard-to-yard transfer ecosystem there is the deepest on the border."* **A customs broker on each
  side** clears the freight (the *agente aduanal* in Mexico and a U.S. counterpart). *"The standard practice is one
  to two days for the border, with a quiet buffer day."* Border trailer detention runs on its own clock. **SOURCED**
  (Cargado, a broker's guide).
- Very few U.S. trucks enter Mexico and very few Mexican trucks travel beyond a narrow commercial zone near the
  border; more freight moves north than south, so northbound loads compete for trucks (search 46 snippets).
  **SOURCED (secondary).**
- **A broker's voice:** JD Gonzalez, licensed 30 years: *"My grandfather was a Mexican customs broker"*; *"We have a
  Mexican division and then we all have a duty-free store that we work with"*; *"If you see where it's from, if
  it's another country, a customs broker handled that."* Changing tariffs increased the workload (headline).
  **SOURCED** (Spectrum News 1 via NCBFAA, 2025).
