## 1 · #4 Ogden, Utah, and its 25th Street

**Claim to test:** a rail-junction town whose station-front street of bars and jazz clubs served train crews and travelers changing lines (K13, K10). **Care:** structure only; record the rougher history neutrally; verify how central the jazz clubs were and in which decades.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED IN STRUCTURE, NARROWED ON JAZZ.** 25th Street is the three-block street running east from Union Station; it grew up to serve passengers forced to change trains at the junction (and train crews who changed there) and became a saloon, gambling and brothel district from the 1870s/1880s to the mid-1950s. **Jazz was not the street's main business**: the documented jazz scene is essentially one venue, the basement club of the Black-owned Porters and Waiters Club/hotel, from the later 1940s to 1959 (with one source dating its open, desegregated phase to the early 1960s), plus one resident saxophonist who played "nearly every club" on the street. Touring musicians reached it because they were changing trains at Ogden. No other named 25th Street jazz club was found (see F).

### A. Snapshot
- **Ogden:** population 87,321 (2020 census), Utah's eighth-largest city; metro 694,863 (2020); area 27.55 sq mi; elevation about 4,341 ft (1,323 m). Settled 1846 as Fort Buenaventura (trapper Miles Goodyear); incorporated 1851. Hispanic or Latino residents 30.8% (2020), up from 23.64% in 2000; median age 31.8. Snow typically late Oct/early Nov to about April; record low −23°F. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogden,_Utah (read 2026-11-20). [P3, P9]
- **The junction:** in 1869 Union Pacific (from the east) met Central Pacific (from the west); Ogden was chosen (1874) as their junction city. Because the two railroads used different gauges [as the Tribune states it], passengers had to change trains here. SOURCED: planning.org/greatplaces/streets/2014/25thstreet.htm; sltrib.com (2023-06-18, "Main Streets Utah: how Ogden's 25th..."). Crews also changed here: UP crews ended their runs at Ogden; trains went on with Southern Pacific crews west or Oregon Short Line crews to Salt Lake City. SOURCED: utahrails.net/ogden/ogden-up-1900-1982.php. [K7, P5]
- **The street:** first called 5th Street, renamed 25th Street 1889; three blocks from Wall Avenue (west, at Union Station) to Washington Boulevard (east); city's first graveled (1885) and paved (1893) street. Listed on the National Register as a historic district 31 Jan 1978 (ref. 78003260); local Ogden register 1981. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_25th_Street; planning.org page above.
- **Defining feature:** a station-front street whose customers were the people stuck between trains. Union Station: wood depot 1869; brick Romanesque station 1889 built by the jointly owned Ogden Union Railway & Depot Co.; burned 1923; present Spanish Colonial Revival building dedicated 22 Nov 1924, 56-ft lobby ceiling. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogden_Union_Station.

### B. What else exists there
- **Rail traffic and its fall (figures conflict):** "as many as 120 trains a day" moved through in the war years, and one station porter recalled 62 passenger trains leaving daily; for a long stretch "a passenger train arrived or departed about every five minutes, around the clock"; 17 passenger tracks and an underground passage. SOURCED: historytogo.utah.gov/trains-helped-win-war. Wikipedia gives "more than 60 daily passenger trains" at peak and about 20 by 1950; Weber State's exhibit says 25 passenger trains stopped daily in the 1940s. SOURCED (both). By the late 1960s Southern Pacific ran three passenger trains a week (Weber) / two per direction daily (Wikipedia). Amtrak took over 1971; California Zephyr and Desert Wind dropped Ogden 1983; the last long-distance train (the Pioneer) left in May 1997. Ogden City took the building in 1977 and in Dec 2022 agreed to buy the land under it from UP for $5.5 million. SOURCED: Wikipedia Ogden Union Station. **The figures disagree; treat all as approximate.** [K5, K9]
- **Rail work:** terminal switching by the jointly owned OUR&D; Riverdale (East) Yard built 1942, expanded 1954; new roundhouse and Pacific Fruit Express ice plant and icing platforms built in the 1920s (ice house leased to PFE, burned 1919, rebuilt); SP machine shops 1908; Ogden Union Stock Yards handled $40 million of livestock (mostly lambs) in 1964; four packing plants and three grain mills were major customers in 1965. PFE stopped all icing 2 Sep 1973. After the 22 Dec 1982 UP merger with Western Pacific and Missouri Pacific, most California traffic moved via Salt Lake City instead. SOURCED: utahrails.net/ogden/ogden-up-1900-1982.php; PFE date UNVERIFIED (search snippet, utahrails.net/industries/pfe-in-utah.html). Rail employee headcount: NOT FOUND. [K7, K14, P8]
- **Station porters:** until desegregation in the 1960s nearly all station baggage porters were Black; these jobs helped create a Black middle class. 18 worked "around the clock" in the war (historytogo); named porters served 1910s-1970, and the last two retired in 1970; the NAACP honored the last four retirees for a combined 155 years of service. SOURCED: exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/redcaps; historytogo page. [K7, K14, P9]
- **WWII economy:** Utah General Depot (opened 1941; peak over 7,500 civilians; German and Italian POW camp from 1942); Hill Field, Clearfield Naval Supply Depot, expanded Ogden Arsenal; Ogden named one of 58 overcrowded war-production areas (1942), with federal worker housing (Grandview Acres, Washington Terrace); some workers lived in roadside tents. Over 1 million service members passed through the station; the volunteer Red Cross canteen (1942-1945/46) was among the busiest in the Pacific area (one source claims 1.6 million served: UNVERIFIED). SOURCED: nps.gov/articles/000/ogden-city-utah.htm; exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/world_war. [K5, K9, P9]
- **Economy today:** IRS regional center is the city's largest employer (over 5,000); McKay-Dee Hospital, Weber State University, Ogden City School District, Autoliv, Fresenius; Business Depot Ogden (the former depot, now a 1,000+ acre business park). SOURCED: Wikipedia Ogden. Hill Air Force Base (outside the city): about 20,000-23,000 personnel, $3.7 billion economic impact (2019 study), the state's largest single-site employer: UNVERIFIED (search snippets: ksl.com, deseret.com, sltrib.com 2025). [P8]
- **Transport today:** FrontRunner commuter rail (2008) from Ogden Central Station, just north of Union Station, with no direct access between them; no Amtrak (bus link from Salt Lake City); Ogden-Hinckley Airport. SOURCED: Wikipedia pages above. [K5]
- **Ownership of the street:** the city, not the state DOT, owns 25th Street, which keeps heavy trucks off it; it is designed to be slow (angled parking, wide sidewalks, early 1980s), with faster cross streets. SOURCED: sltrib 2023-06-18. [K1, P5]

### C. Cultural locations (names)

Browning Firearms Museum; Utah Cowboy and Western Heritage Museum; Browning-Kimball Classic Car Museum; Myra Powell Gallery; Union Station Research Library; Browning Theater (rentable); hosts Hostlers Model Railroad Festival, weddings, marathon expo, fairs. A 2005 building on the site houses a record shop. SOURCED: exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/porters; standard.net 2016; sltrib 2023. [K4, K10, K13, P6, SM:man versus society]
- **Royal Hotel** (Wall Avenue, a block west): one of two Black-owned hotels near the station accepting Black travelers. SOURCED (sltrib 2023); "two Black-owned hotels... south side of the street" only for Black business: UNVERIFIED (snippet, kjzz.com/thesignpostwsu.com). [K4]
- **Jazz resident:** a saxophonist arrived 1945 for a two-week gig, stayed about 70 years, played "nearly every club" on 25th Street, refused to play segregated clubs; touring musicians changing trains called him for jam sessions at any hour; died 7 Dec 2019 aged 100; Utah's governor named 18 April a day in his honor (2002) with concerts held that date. SOURCED: visitutah.com/articles/ogden-joe-mcqueen-utah-jazz; standard.net 2016; visitogden.com/blog/live-in-ogden. [K13]
- **Brothel and vice sites (neutral record):** a madam active from the 1880s/1889 owned a large share of the street by about 1908; her ice-cream parlor fronted a brothel (its sign survives); a block-long alley of very small rooms behind the north side of the street (about 50 women at a time) ended with police raids in 1912; she later ran Salt Lake City's 1908 consolidated district. In 1947-1950s a second-floor brothel at 201 25th St earned about $30,000 a month; its operators were repeatedly arrested and fined, then convicted of federal tax evasion. 201 25th St now houses a bar and dance floor. SOURCED: sltrib.com 2023-06-18 and 2023-06-21 ("Meet two of Ogden's infamous madams"); 1912 raid date UNVERIFIED (snippet, Wikipedia Belle London). [K9, K6]

SOURCED: ilovehistory.utah.gov/japanese-american-communities (north side of 25th near railyards); rest UNVERIFIED (snippets: kutv.com, intermountainhistories.org). The railroad drew Chinese, Japanese, African American, Greek, Italian and Latino residents. SOURCED: sltrib 2023. ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Forced transfers made a customer base.** Passengers had to change trains here, and crews ended runs here; the street at the station door filled with hotels, lodging houses, restaurants, saloons, cigar shops, clothing stores, gambling and brothels (SOURCED: planning.org; sltrib). Soldiers on WWII stops went up 25th Street for drinks (SOURCED: historytogo). [K7, K10]
- **The porters' lodging became the jazz club.** The jazz basement existed because a rooming house for Black railroad porters and waiters existed, and musicians came because they were switching trains (SOURCED: Weber porters page; visitogden). [K4, K13, SM:competence shown under pressure]
- **Segregation sorted where people could sleep and gather:** two Black-owned hotels near the station; Black war workers' arrival "threw a spotlight on Ogden's unspoken segregation rules," leading to the NAACP branch (founded early 1944); its later president (from 1963, 33 years) worked on minorities' difficulty buying homes outside established minority neighborhoods. SOURCED: exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/world_war and /NAACP. [K4, K9, K6]
- **The street declined when the trains did** (1950s-60s), then was rebuilt as a slow, city-owned heritage street (1977 Master Plan, 1979 redevelopment district, 1980 streetscape, 1996 design guidelines, 2000 amphitheater; landmarks-commission approval for buildings). SOURCED: planning.org; sltrib. [K1, K9]

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)
- **Everyday rules (K6):** YES, several, mostly state-made.
  - Utah statewide liquor ban from 1917 (stricter than federal); national Prohibition 1920-33; Utah allowed beer 1934 and ended prohibition 1935, creating the State Liquor Commission, which still regulates sales; enforcement on the street was undermined by bribery (1933 indictment of the mayor, police chief and a county commissioner on 1,000+ counts; all acquitted). SOURCED: exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/prohibition.
  - Postwar: alcohol legally only from state liquor stores and licensed bars/clubs; many 25th St businesses sold illegally or resold store liquor at a markup after hours. Late-1940s-1950s cleanup (new mayor gave the county attorney free rein; narcotics office 1952; brothel raids; gambling machines destroyed, sometimes publicly; the military's concern about venereal disease near reactivated bases in 1950); 1954 citywide crackdown. SOURCED: exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/vice; Wikipedia Historic 25th Street. The cleanup's lasting effect is judged "hard to judge" by the Weber exhibit.
  - Brothel keeping was handled for decades by repeated arrest and fine; lasting convictions came via federal tax charges. SOURCED: sltrib 2023-06-21.
  - Unwritten segregation rules (lodging, clubs) until clubs dropped them when they "were losing customers" (Standard-Examiner, around the early 1960s). SOURCED.
  - Today: redevelopment governed by landmarks commission design approval. SOURCED: sltrib.
- **Tunnel legend vs record:** the legend of bootlegger tunnels from Union Station to the hotel at the east end is unproven. Weber State's special-collections curator has found no evidence and attributes it to connected basements along a block; the 1979-80 rebuild of the street found no tunnels; several businesses show sealed basement openings, some pointing under the street, and one owner describes moving illegal whiskey between connected basements. SOURCED: ksl.com 2016-05-17. [K9]
- **Speech (K11, loose):** English; a large Spanish-speaking population (30.8% Hispanic/Latino; a Spanish-speaking civil-rights organization chapter founded 1968). Historically Japanese, Greek and others near the street. SOURCED: Wikipedia Ogden; exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/SOCIO.
- **Arts from cheap space (K13):** artist studios in a former parking garage with no commission; record shop and galleries in rebuilt buildings. SOURCED: sltrib 2023.
- **[R]** flags: rail repair shops, icing platforms, terminal switching company jointly owned by rival railroads (maintenance and handover culture); porters' lodging between shifts (rest between runs).

## 2 · #84 Moose Jaw, Saskatchewan

**Claim to test:** a rail divisional point whose station-side district of hotels and bars served crews and travelers (K10, K7). **Care:** its tunnels and gangster legends are partly folklore; separate documented tunnel history from legend.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with a sharper shape than claimed.** The CPR chose the site in 1882 and made it a divisional point in 1883 with freight yards and repair shops; the station (1920-22) stands at the head of Main Street; River Street, running past the station end of downtown, held most of the city's hotels (seven of nine by 1924: UNVERIFIED) and was the city's tolerated vice district under a police chief (1905-1927) whose documented policy was containment rather than suppression. **The tunnels-and-Capone story is not documented history**: scholarship (Androsoff 2021) and local historians treat it as a tourism construction; what is documented is connected basements and at least one short sealed passage, a 1985 brick chamber, and a 1986 business-district study whose findings are reported in two contradictory ways (see E).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location and size:** about 160 km north of the US border and 65 km west of Regina, at the confluence of the Moose Jaw River and Thunder Creek; population 33,665 (2021), 33,910 (2016); incorporated as a city 1903 (community 13 Feb 1884); the province's fourth-largest city. Fastest growth 1911-21 (about 40%), the 1940s (about 19%), the 1950s (36%). SOURCED: thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/moose-jaw; mjpolice.ca/about/history (incorporation date). [P3]
- **Before the railway:** Cree and Assiniboine seasonal (winter) encampments; Métis traders and buffalo hunters built the first permanent settlement. SOURCED: Canadian Encyclopedia; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moose_Jaw. [P9]
- **Climate:** transition between semi-arid and humid continental; long cold winters, short warm summers; record high 43.3 °C (July 1937). SOURCED: Wikipedia (first 100k chars). [P3]
- **Defining feature:** the CPR divisional point (1883) plus the Soo Line link to Minneapolis/Chicago (completed 1893), making it the leading railway center of the future province. SOURCED: Canadian Encyclopedia; historicplaces.ca (Old CPR Station). [K7, P5]

SOURCED: Canadian Encyclopedia. Photos record a CPR night shift (1912) and a roundhouse mechanical staff (1937): UNVERIFIED (snippets, memorysask). Shop/crew headcounts: NOT FOUND. Designated Heritage Railway Station 10 June 1991; municipal heritage 1999. SOURCED: pc.gc.ca DFHD id 2014; historicplaces.ca id 7778. It now houses a liquor store: UNVERIFIED-as-current (virtualsk.com, undated feature; read on page). A round-the-clock station restaurant served CPR passengers. SOURCED (virtualsk.com). [K10, K5]

Its size: about 1,000 jobs and a $30-million payroll (Encyclopedia of Saskatchewan, undated); 1,390 total employment in 1994 and 920 FTE direct and indirect jobs from the NATO program (mid-2000s consultant report); a 25-year, $11.2 billion RCAF training contract awarded later: all UNVERIFIED (snippets). - **Policing:** first constable 1884 (also license inspector; in 1899 also volunteer fire chief); first motor car 1911; 52 officers and 20 FTE support staff now. SOURCED: esask.uregina.ca/entry/moose_jaw_police_service.html; mjpolice.ca. [K1]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Rail made the vice district and its management.** The police history itself ties the city's red-light era to the CPR moving its divisional headquarters to Moose Jaw and the Soo Line's arrival; police work was keeping gambling, drinking and brothels confined to River Street and breaking up fights between CPR and Soo Line workers; Regina residents could ride the CPR round trip for $1.00 to visit River Street. SOURCED: mjpolice.ca/about/history. Chief Walter P. Johnson was hired 5 Sep 1905 with orders to remove brothels from the parks; served to 1927; later elected mayor (1937). SOURCED: mjpolice.ca. Historians describe his approach as confinement to the River/Manitoba/View Streets area with occasional brothel raids; View Street marked the boundary between vice and respectability. ## 3 · #34 Kansas City, 18th and Vine

**Claim to test:** a rail-hub city where a dense jazz district grew from the music traveling musicians brought through it (K13).

**Verdict on the claim: PARTLY CONFIRMED, CORRECTED ON TWO POINTS.** (1) Kansas City was a rail hub and the base and last stop of touring circuits for territory bands and Black vaudeville, and touring bands came there to rest, hire players and play; so traveling musicians did feed it. (2) **But the sources give two other causes as at least equal:** a political machine (about 1925-1939) whose tolerance of liquor, gambling and all-night cabarets kept several hundred venues open, and housing segregation (covenants and deed restrictions) that concentrated Black residents, businesses and nightlife in this district. (3) **Geography correction:** the famous club strip ran between 12th Street (north) and 18th Street (south); several of the best-known clubs (the Reno at 12th and Cherry, the Sunset at 1715 E. 12th) were on 12th Street, not at 18th and Vine. 18th and Vine was the Black commercial "downtown" (600+ businesses), home of the musicians' union hall, theaters, the newspaper and the founding meeting of a Black baseball league.

### A. Snapshot
- **City:** Kansas City, Missouri: 508,090 (2020), metro 2,253,579; African Americans 26.5% (2020), down from 29.9% (2010). Rail growth followed the Hannibal Bridge over the Missouri River (opened 1869); railroads and the stockyards made it a transport and livestock center. Troost Avenue is today one of its sharpest racial and economic dividing lines. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri (first 100k chars). [P3, P5, P9]
- **The district:** east of downtown, roughly bounded by 18th St, Woodland Ave, 19th St and The Paseo; historic district about 9 acres, 35 contributing buildings, listed on the National Register 9 Sep 1991 (part of the "18th and Vine Area" multiple property submission). The 12th and Vine intersection six blocks north no longer exists (a housing project was built over it). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18th_and_Vine. [P5]
- **Defining feature:** the historic center of the metro's Black culture and commerce; developed as a Black settlement area in the 1880s-90s as families moved from decaying neighborhoods near the river; building boom from WWI to about 1930. SOURCED: aahtkc.org/18thandvine; historicmissouri.org/tours/show/16; scalawagmagazine.org (2020-12).
- **Rail:** Union Station opened 30 Oct 1914 (then third-largest US station), built by the Kansas City Terminal Railway, a joint company of 12 railroads; 850,000 sq ft; passenger peak 678,363 in 1945 [as given]; 32,842 in 1973; closed 1983; restored with a 1996 bi-state 1/8-cent sales tax (just under half of $250 million), reopened 1999; Amtrak back 2002. Before it, over 180 trains a day passed the older Union Depot. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City_Union_Station. A WWI peak of 271 trains in a day and "over 72,000 trains" in 1945 come from the station's own site: UNVERIFIED (snippet). [K5]

## 4 · #7 Fort Worth Stockyards and Billy Bob's Texas

**Claim to test:** a cattle-drive stopover whose stockyards barn became a huge honky-tonk, a single venue big enough to be a city's calling card (K13, K8).

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with corrections to the building's story.** Fort Worth was a drovers' stop on the trail north (1866-1890), then a rail stockyard and (from 1903) a packing center. Billy Bob's occupies the city-built 1936 Cattle Exhibit Building (on a site with covered livestock exhibit space from about 1910), which held stalls and a 1,200-seat auction arena; after the stock show left (1943/44) it was used as an aircraft factory in WWII and then as a department store, and was **vacant** when it was bought (1980) and opened as a club on 1 April 1981. **Correction:** the club came into an empty retail building, not straight from a working barn; and the "world's largest honky-tonk" billing is the venue's own claim (100,000 sq ft interior, 6,000+ capacity). ### A. Snapshot

## 5 · #8 Gilley's, Pasadena, Texas

**Claim to test:** a giant roadhouse bar, long billed as the largest of its kind, whose fame spread far beyond its town. **Care:** closed decades ago; a historical case. Verify the closing date and how far the "largest" claim held.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with dates and size pinned down.** A metal-building beer joint on Spencer Highway reopened as Gilley's in 1970 or 1971 (sources split), grew by additions into a compound with a 6,000-person capacity, and was listed in the *Guinness Book of World Records* as the world's largest nightclub/honky-tonk (cited to the 1987 edition). Its core customers were Pasadena residents who worked in the Houston Ship Channel refineries and petrochemical plants. A 1978 *Esquire* article and the 1980 film made from it, shot largely at the club, spread its fame nationally and started a national country-dancing and mechanical-bull fad. **Closing date:** closed by court order in (March) 1989 after the 1987/88 partners' lawsuit; burned in a fire ruled arson/suspicious in July 1990; remaining arena demolished November 2005. **How far "largest" held:** the size usually given is 48,000 sq ft (a 70,000 sq ft figure appears only in low-reliability sources); the Guinness listing is real per the state handbook; by the early 1980s a Fort Worth club built to ride the same fad claimed 100,000 sq ft and the same 6,000 capacity, so the claim was contested by the early 1980s.

### A. Snapshot
- **Pasadena, Texas:** Harris County, second-largest city in the county; north border on the Houston Ship Channel, southeast corner on Galveston Bay; founded 1893 by a Galveston man, incorporated 1928. Population 58,737 (1960), 89,957 (1970), 112,560 (1980), 119,363 (1990), 151,950 (2020); Hispanic or Latino 67.81% (2020), 48.24% (2000). Refineries from about 1917-1920; economy tied to the Ship Channel, Bayport terminal and industrial district; petroleum refining and petrochemicals remain key. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasadena,_Texas (first 100k chars). Nearly half of US petrochemical processing and about 30% of US refining along the channel: UNVERIFIED (snippet, sah-archipedia.org). [P3, P8, P9]
- **The club:** 4500 Spencer Highway (SOURCED: Austin Chronicle 1999-10-15); 13.5-acre site (SOURCED: bloghouston.com 2005-11-09); about 48,000 sq ft and 6,000 capacity (SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherwood_Cryer, which carries a conflict-of-interest notice; capacity also SOURCED: tshaonline.org Handbook of Texas "Gilley's"); open seven days, 10 a.m. to 2 a.m. (SOURCED: TSHA; Wikipedia Cryer). [K10]
- **Defining feature:** a giant working-class dance hall in a refinery suburb that national media turned into the model for a new kind of giant honky-tonk. SOURCED: Austin Chronicle; TSHA.

### B. What else exists there
- **Who came:** many Pasadena residents who worked at Houston-area petrochemical plants used it as their social venue (SOURCED: TSHA); refinery workers and others came after work to dance and drink (SOURCED: houstonpress.com 2015-05-08); the owner said troublemakers were mostly local refinery and plant workers, tourists rarely (SOURCED: Austin Chronicle); showers for truckers suggest truckers among patrons (SOURCED: kutx.org; TSHA). - **Owner's business model:** the owner was a former Shell Oil welder who owned convenience stores, honky-tonks and bars; in 1971 (or 1970) he hired a country singer to play piano at his Spencer Highway club, then reopened it under the singer's name as a 50-50 partnership; he air-conditioned the metal building instead of replacing it; he added on piece by piece; he bought the patent tied to the mechanical bull and ran three bulls continuously, later making and selling bulls (shipped to Oregon and Latin America) from an ice-house bar in Deer Park; he collected discarded mattresses around Pasadena for under the bulls; he opened a second club down the road for the house bandleader. ## 6 · #38 Armadillo World Headquarters, Austin (1970-1980)

**Claim to test:** a large converted hall that hosted rock, blues and folk together and made its city's music reputation (K13).

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, and broader than claimed.** A former National Guard armory (est. built 1948) on Barton Springs Road, leased cheaply in 1970 by a band manager and collaborators from the just-closed countercultural venue downtown; open 7 Aug 1970 to 31 Dec 1980; eventual capacity 1,500 (Handbook of Texas). A peer-reviewed calendar reconstruction (Mellard, *Journal of Texas Music History* 10:1, 2010) counts about 900 acts over a little more than 2,000 evenings and shows the hall was not only rock/blues/folk: country-identified acts were 55% of 1973 bookings (the progressive country peak, 1972-1974), and by the late 1970s it was Central Texas's jazz hub; it also hosted gospel, honky-tonk, Western swing, conjunto, Tejano, zydeco, Cajun, R&B, punk and a ballet company (which played it more often than the most famous country star). The same study warns that the popular memory of the hall (counterculture and traditional working-class Texans united by country music) is partly myth. Sources credit it as arguably the origin of the modern Austin music scene, which the city later branded with an official slogan (adopted 1991).

### A. Snapshot
- **Austin:** population 253,539 (1970) and 341,665 (1980), +34.8% (Imagine Austin table citing the Census) or 251,808 (1970, timeline): UNVERIFIED (snippets). State capital; home of the University of Texas (the hall's audience drew on students; UT's student paper is one of the calendar sources). SOURCED (Mellard 2010). Neighborhoods south of the Colorado River historically housed working-class Anglos and Mexican Americans and their businesses. SOURCED: Mellard 2010. [P3, P9]
- **The hall:** 525½ Barton Springs Road at South First Street, south of the river and downtown; former National Guard armory (no construction records; estimated 1948); site 5.62 acres (Wikipedia) or a 76-acre landlord property (Alcalde 2017: **conflict**, possibly the whole tract vs the lot); "poor acoustics and uncomfortable, but its rent was cheap and its location central." SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armadillo_World_Headquarters. It stood in an auto repossession lot beside a cafeteria and a roller rink. SOURCED: Mellard 2010. Capacity 1,500. SOURCED: tshaonline.org Handbook "Armadillo World Headquarters" (John Wheat, rev. 2020). [K10]
- **Defining feature:** a cheap, oversized, ill-suited building run as a "community arts laboratory," modeled by its lawyer co-founder on a London arts lab combining film, dance, theater and music. SOURCED: Mellard 2010; texasstandard.org.

### B. What else exists there (how it worked)
- **Founding and money:** conceived after the downtown countercultural venue (Vulcan Gas Company, on Congress Avenue, which had faced "daunting community opposition") closed in 1970; seed money from a band member's father and an Austin literary group (Wikipedia), and from the band's record deal with a major label (Texas Standard). Opened 7 Aug 1970 with three bands. SOURCED: Wikipedia; texasstandard.org; Mellard. [K8, K13]
- **Operations:** a large open hall with inadequate roofing; a ring of side rooms that kept changing use (offices, makeshift apartments, art galleries, recording studios, bakeries, arcades); the artistic director lived in an apartment he built just offstage; a kitchen known to national touring acts for hearty meals; an outdoor beer garden (1972, funded by a musician who later ran the place) with its own stage that raised capacity. Beer garden and food were added to build steady cash flow; at its peak it sold the second-highest volume of a Texas brewery's draft beer in the US, behind the Houston Astrodome; a Dallas department store sold a branded line. SOURCED: Mellard 2010; Wikipedia. Much volunteer labor; month-to-month "perpetual financial crisis." SOURCED: TSHA. [K8, K10, K12, R]
- **Phases (Mellard's counts):** 1970-1972 experimental arts-lab period, 200+ performances; 1973-1976 progressive-country peak with national profile, 1,000+ performances; 1977-1980 under new management, more musically diverse and more financially stable, nearly 900 performances. Most frequent performers were local and regional bands (top band 123 nights), used as openers and as headliners with their own following; they "structured the day-to-day experiences." SOURCED: Mellard 2010. [K7, K13]
- **Finances and management:** large guarantees to acts, low ticket prices, weak promotion; staff laid off late 1976; bankruptcy filed in 1976 (Texas Standard) or Chapter 11 in 1977 (Wikipedia) (**conflict**); founder left 1 Nov 1976 and handed over to a musician-investor who restructured it on a firmer footing and briefly returned it to profit. SOURCED: Wikipedia; texasstandard.org; alcalde.texasexes.org 2017-07. [K8, K9]
- **End:** the business owned the venue but not the land; rising real estate values (partly caused by the music scene's own success) led the landlord to sell (estimated $4-8 million); lease expired; final sold-out New Year's Eve show 31 Dec 1980 ran until about 4 a.m.; contents auctioned January 1981; armory razed 1981 and replaced by a 13-story office building (One Texas Center); a plaque in its parking lot (dedicated 19 Aug 2006). SOURCED: Wikipedia; texasstandard.org; alcalde; TSHA. [K9]

### C. Cultural locations (names)
- **The hall's art department:** a group of a dozen poster and mural artists produced hundreds of concert posters, defining the venue's look and seeding Austin's poster art scene; a de facto house photographer. SOURCED: Wikipedia; TSHA. Posters held by the Austin History Center (e.g., a 64-poster collection by 15 artists, 1972-1978; the 1980 last-show poster). SOURCED: Austin History Center live-music subject guide (PDF, read via text extraction). [K13, P10]
- **Live recordings made there:** country-rock, blues, jazz and rock albums (1974-1975 recordings by a touring country-rock band, a Texas blues guitarist and a rock composer raised its national profile). SOURCED: texasstandard.org; Wikipedia. [K13]
- **Other venues of the same scene (named in Mellard 2010):** Soap Creek Saloon (locally oriented progressive country; its top acts played nearly 900 evenings vs about 400 for the hall's top five); Antone's (blues, downtown, founded 1975 partly in reaction to the hall's turn toward country); Raul's (punk); the One Knite (blues); the Texas (later Austin) Opry House (favored by the decade's best-known country star after a falling-out with the hall over "financial, personnel, and firearm-related misunderstandings"). Threadgill's (a 1930s-60s gas station that sold beer on North Lamar; the hall's founder bought it in 1977 and opened his restaurant there; the hall's crew were its first customers, 1 Jan 1980). SOURCED: Mellard; alcalde (Threadgill's date 1 Jan 1980); purchase 1977 UNVERIFIED (snippet). Broken Spoke (opened 1964, South Lamar): UNVERIFIED (snippet). [K10, K12]
- **City institutions that followed:** Austin City Limits (PBS series created 1974 by the local station; pilot featured the decade's best-known country star; 43 years on PBS); Austin Music Commission (1989); the city's official music slogan (adopted 1991, after it was found Austin had more live venues per capita than anywhere in the US); SXSW (1986); ACL festival (2002, Zilker Park). SOURCED: Austin History Center guide. [K1, K3, K8]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Cheap space made the experiment possible** (Wikipedia: cheap rent, central location); its awkwardness was absorbed by turning side rooms into whatever was needed (Mellard). [K13]
- **Size and cost shaped its role:** compared with smaller clubs, the hall acted as "connective tissue" between Austin's artists and audiences and the national music industry, while smaller venues nurtured local acts. SOURCED: Mellard 2010. [K13]
- **Its success raised the land value that ended it.** SOURCED: texasstandard.org. [K9]
- **Myth vs record (care):** the two best-known country stars of the period played only about a dozen times combined (at least 7 and 5), mostly late 1972 to early 1974; a rock guitarist and a punk band each played more often than one of them; a ballet company played more often than the other. A 1960s musician, asked how counterculture and traditional working-class Texans interacted then, answered that they did not, and recalled harassment and violence. SOURCED: Mellard 2010. [K4, K9]

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)
- **Everyday rules (K6): YES, informal and state-level.**
  - House: inexpensive admission; cannabis use tolerated; police reportedly never raided (SOURCED: Wikipedia); an undercover narcotics agent reportedly joined the crew socially (SOURCED as an anecdote: alcalde).
  - State: liquor by the drink became legal in Texas only from 1971 (mixed-beverage permits, local-option elections; Austin's first legal mixed drink 1 June 1971); before that, drinkers bought bottles and paid for setups at beer halls. UNVERIFIED (snippets: Texas Monthly; TABC history; towers.net). The hall's documented drink trade was draft beer (SOURCED).
  - Pre-1970 countercultural venues downtown met community opposition (SOURCED: Mellard).

- **Speech (K11, loose):** English; South Austin historically Anglo and Mexican American; Spanish-language genres (conjunto, Tejano) booked. SOURCED: Mellard.
