## SECTIONS

### 1. Isolated and confined environment (ICE) psychology

**1.1 The review baseline (Palinkas and Suedfeld 2008).**
- SOURCED (PubMed 17655924 via Europe PMC; abstract read as paraphrase by the fetch tool): Palinkas LA, Suedfeld P. "Psychological effects of polar expeditions." Lancet 2008;371:153-163, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(07)61056-3. Symptoms (disturbed sleep, reduced cognitive ability, negative mood, interpersonal tension and conflict) follow seasonal patterns; authors propose three overlapping syndromes: winter-over syndrome, polar T3 syndrome, subsyndromal seasonal affective disorder. About 5% of expedition members meet DSM-IV or ICD criteria for a psychiatric disorder. Many also gain positive outcomes (better stress coping, self-sufficiency, improved health, personal growth). Authors recommend psychological and psychiatric screening, support such as telephone counseling, and training in coping, group interaction and team leadership.
- UNVERIFIED (press only, https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna19975250 and others): "40 to 60 percent may suffer negative effects like depression, sleep disruption, anger, irritability and conflict." Not checked against the paper. Press also quotes the authors that interpersonal conflict is the greatest source of tension.
- SOURCED (Sandal, van de Vijver, Smith 2018, intro, via Europe PMC PMC6256132): "Reduced mood, adaptation problems, and sleep difficulties account for 60% of all diagnoses in Antarctica" (citing Palinkas et al. 1995; Lugg 2005); the cluster is called winter-over syndrome because it is most prevalent in the cold, dark season (Palmai 1963).

**1.2 Seasonal depression and the isolation-versus-darkness question.**
- SOURCED (PubMed 7676841): Palinkas, Cravalho, Browner 1995, Acta Psychiatr Scand 91:423-9. 119 men and women who wintered; depression scores rose significantly across the 12 months; summer depression and marital status independently predicted winter depression; a winter-over symptom factor appeared in winter but not summer. Authors concluded social isolation during winter may explain seasonal mood shifts better than lack of sunlight.
- SOURCED (PubMed 11259861): Palinkas et al. 2001 Psychoneuroendocrinology. 12 participants (10 men, 2 women) at McMurdo, 1997 or 1998 winter, monthly serum over 10 months. TSH and mood showed a circannual pattern (peaks November and July, trough March-April). Falls in free T3 and T4 preceded high tension-anxiety and confusion; rises in tension-anxiety and total mood disturbance preceded a fall in free T3 ("feedback of mood on T3"). Authors suggest winter-over syndrome may reflect relative CNS hypothyroidism.
- SOURCED (Arendt 2012, Europe PMC PMC3793275): true SAD appears rare in temporary polar residents although subsyndromal low mood is reported; "no overt SAD requiring treatment among Halley personnel since 1984" per the author.

**1.3 Third-quarter phenomenon: contested.**
- SOURCED (Sandal 2018 intro): resilience tends to decrease around the third quarter regardless of stay length, as people realize the mission is only half done (Bechtel and Berning 1991); for winter-over crews the third quarter coincides with midwinter; "empirical support remains equivocal."
- SOURCED (Bell, Brown, Mitchell 2019, Front Psychol, Europe PMC PMC6530432; 72 sources, 253 effect sizes, 1,150 data points): "team mood data did not consistently support" the third-quarter effect; 3 of 7 teams showed increased negative affect (PANAS) in the third quarter; mood disturbance spiked around the one-year mark in both Mars-500 and an Antarctic winter-over; by 40% of mission completion (at least 90 days in the data) every team had reported at least one conflict; no team exceeded six conflict instances per month with a given target; longer missions (Concordia, Tara Drift, Mars-500) had less social time together than 6-, 10- and 12-day simulations; cohesion tended to decline over time; the cohesion-performance link in ICE settings was imprecise (one Antarctic estimate mean -0.10, 95% credible interval -0.46 to 0.28) whereas in non-ICE teams it was positive (small to moderate in firehouses, moderate to large in special-operations teams).
- SOURCED (Stuster, French remote stations diary analysis, MIT OCW slides https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-423j-aerospace-biomedical-and-life-support-engineering-spring-2006/fb2f71dd65435b099ea319f3a0512221_icestuster2006.pdf; 4 expedition leaders and 5 physicians at Dumont d'Urville, Kerguelen, Crozet, Amsterdam Island): "All expeditions, short and long, Antarctic and insular, decline in overall net positivity/negativity (NPN) during the third quarter"; physicians show the third-quarter dip, leaders their greatest decline in the second quarter. Entries coded 39% positive, 46% negative, 15% neutral overall. Net positivity: long missions -0.015, short missions -0.210 (short missions substantially more negative); Antarctic -0.029 vs insular -0.042.
- SOURCED (Stuster 2010, NASA, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100026549/downloads/20100026549.pdf, ISS journals, 10 crew members, 4,247 entries per a secondary summary): "evidence of a decline in morale during the third quarters of the missions."
- SOURCED (Dunn Rosenberg et al. 2022, Front Physiol, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.898841/full): 18 individuals across three HI-SEAS missions (8, 12 and 8 months, 2014-2017; six mixed-gender international crews). Four-stage model: eustress of initial adaptation (first quarter), deprivation (second), disruption of team dynamics with rising oxidative damage, reduced activity and conflict (third), asynchronous coping (fourth). Adverse conditions most likely after the halfway point (roughly six months for 8-12 month missions); mid-mission (4-6 months) proposed as the point where interventions matter most. The article names no statistically tested predictors of resilience.

**1.4 "Psychological hibernation" at Concordia (primary read).**
- SOURCED (Sandal, van de Vijver, Smith 2018, Front Psychol 9:2235, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02235/full, Europe PMC PMC6256132): two Concordia winter crews, N = 27 (crew 1: 14 men, ages 23-58; crew 2: 13 including 3 women, ages 22-51), about 10 months, nine measurement occasions. Positive affect, active problem solving, palliative reactions, avoidance and comforting cognitions all reached a low around midwinter (quadratic effects: PA 0.43, active problem solving 0.75, avoidance 0.82, palliative 0.77, comforting cognitions 0.70); negative affect stayed low and unchanged (authors suggest reluctance to report distress); subjective sleep quality declined mainly linearly (0.19); coping and PA recovered in the second half. Authors argue the dip "may represent an adaptive way of coping" and question whether it is reduced resilience; they link it to the "Antarctic stare" (Barabasz et al. 1983: mild fugue, absentmindedness, drifting, around the third quarter). Limits: three women, self-report, small sample. Station: 3,232 m, mean temperature -51 C, lowest -85 C, winter crew 15-16.

**1.5 Mars-500 (primary read).**
- SOURCED (Basner et al. 2014, PLoS One 9(3):e93298, Europe PMC PMC3968121): six men (3 Russian, 2 ESA, 1 CNSA), mean age 32, 520 days, 3 June 2010 to 4 Nov 2011, 550 cubic meter chamber at IBMP Moscow; communication delay days 54-470. Crew averages for depression and mood disturbance were low (BDI-II mean 2.2/100; POMS total mood disturbance 10.4/100); BDI-II by quarter 1.5, 1.4, 3.1, 2.8 (quarters 3 and 4 significantly higher). Individual differences were large and stable (average intra-class correlation about 55%): one man had worsening sleep-onset insomnia and chronic partial sleep deprivation (mean sleep 6.54 h vs crew 7.39 h; 64% of vigilance lapses); one free-ran on about a 25-hour period; two others had altered sleep-wake timing; two showed neither behavioral disturbance nor distress. Two crew members were asleep while the others were awake (or vice versa) for 20.1% of the mission. Conflicts with mission control: 41 reports; crew-to-crew: 8; two crew members made 85% of reported conflicts. Activity fell steeply early, declined slowly in quarters 2-3, rose at the end. The abstract's "93% of mission weeks" figure conflicts with the body text (8 of 74 weeks, about 11%): flagged under Unresolved conflicts.
- SOURCED (Europe PMC PMC4529101 abstract, via search result): wearable proximity sensors in the 520-day simulation; "crew relation time index" correlated with a sociometric questionnaire (r = .35 to .55).

**1.6 Cohesion decline measured by sensors (Concordia).**
- SOURCED (Europe PMC search result, Basner et al., Front Psychol 2026, PMC13283885, abstract only): wearable proximity sensors in two Antarctic winter-over missions of 10-14 months (13 participants in 2015, 12 in 2016); cohesion declined as missions progressed and people spent more time alone; Year 1 scores higher than Year 2. Full text not read.

**1.7 Sleep and circadian effects of polar night and midnight sun.**
- SOURCED (Zivi, De Gennaro, Ferlazzo 2020 review, Front Neurosci, Europe PMC PMC7433404; first 100,000 characters read): polar sleep studies generally show lower slow-wave sleep and efficiency, more fragmentation, lighter sleep, but results are inconsistent and effects of season, confinement and light "cannot yet be separated." Examples: Halley (10 subjects) sleep timing delayed in winter, fragmentation up; Maitri (6 men) lower total sleep time and efficiency in winter; Belgrano II (12 men) mean sleep duration fell significantly during the polar night (July); Neumayer III (54 participants) total sleep time declined linearly over the year, arousals up in women; Zhongshan (11) sleep onset delayed in mid-winter but total sleep time unchanged; Halley and Concordia (20) bed, wake and sleep times delayed in winter and day-to-day circadian stability declined after two months without direct sunlight; Dumont d'Urville (sea level) had longer, more efficient sleep than high-altitude Concordia. Midnight sun: "no included study directly isolates midnight-sun effects"; a 12-day summer trip (14 students) found no significant sleep effects.
- SOURCED (Arendt 2012, PMC3793275): winter artificial light: maximum personal exposure about 500-700 lux, workspaces rarely exceed 300-500 lux, Dome Fuji living spaces under 300 lux; outdoors with sun below horizon usually below 1 lux and not above 100 lux; summer sunny day over 40,000 lux, heavily overcast about 1,000 lux. At Halley (75 S) sun does not rise for about 100 days and does not set for about 110 days. Dome Fuji study: melatonin delayed about 4 h in winter vs summer. Extra white or blue-enriched light at 75 S raised midwinter maximum exposure to about 1,000-2,000 lux and blue-enriched light advanced sleep onset by about 19 minutes vs white (2006), but extra light "did not fully prevent winter sleep problems." Skeleton photoperiod (1 h of 2,000 lux in the morning plus 1 h in the evening) restored summer-like melatonin phase. Summer: some people free-run or delay in summer, especially in field camps without scheduled sleep or blinds; recommendation: blinds and eye masks plus scheduled sleep. On Svalbard 81% of Russian men and 77% of Russian women reported sleep problems of at least 2 weeks, nearly four times the Norwegian rate; in Tromsø 17.6% of women and 9.0% of men reported dark-season insomnia (as cited by Arendt).
- SOURCED (Sletten et al. 2022, J Pineal Res, Europe PMC PMC9541096): 51 expeditioners (41 men, 10 women; mean age about 45.5) at Davis, Mawson and Casey over two winters, 54% of 95 invited, about six months. Mean sleep about 7.2 h but 41.4% of episodes under 7 h and about 19% under 6 h. Circadian phase highly variable (range about 17 h); 34 of 50 had some sleep misaligned with their melatonin phase; misaligned sleep averaged 6.1 h vs 7.3 h aligned (p < .0001). Cognitive performance fell with time awake and at biological night. Depression, anxiety and affect scores did not change across the season; team cohesion declined and conflict rose, peaking in September; these tracked time in expedition more closely than daylight. Authors cite Halley VI's lighting system designed to maintain circadian synchrony, and say "provision of a 24-h light-dark cycle in an operational environment does not guarantee entrainment."
- SOURCED (Zivi 2020, on submarines): French strategic submarine, 19 marines, 70 days: no significant change in recorded sleep between days 21 and 51 and no change in night-time cortisol, authors credited scheduled activity and controlled light and temperature. Work schedule acts as a strong time cue.
- SOURCED (Concordia polysomnography, secondary summary via search result): 13 men, 13-month winter-over, measured every 6 weeks; high-altitude periodic breathing, longer sleep onset latency, slower psychomotor speed; apart from obstructive apneas, measures stayed stable over time. Primary paper not opened (UNVERIFIED beyond the summary).

**1.8 Cognitive effects.**
- SOURCED (Barkaszi et al. 2016, Front Hum Neurosci, PubMed 27445768 abstract): Concordia winter-over crew, six measurement periods, EEG/ERP and behavioral attention tasks; reaction-time and N1 changes attributed mainly to practice; "Antarctic conditions did not negatively affect cognitive activity despite the stressors."
- SOURCED (Europe PMC search result, npj Microgravity 2025, PMC12254294, abstract only): MRI before, after and 5 months after a 12-month Concordia stay, 25 crew, 25 controls, 4 "flying phantom" participants; gray matter fell in hippocampus, thalamus, temporal and parietal regions and white matter declined; all regions except the thalamus returned to baseline at 5 months; ventricle volume increased; better sleep linked to smaller loss.
- SOURCED (Sletten 2022): performance on memory tasks worse with longer time awake and at biological night (circadian effect, not a season effect).

**1.9 What predicts good and bad adjustment.**
- SOURCED (Palinkas 2003 Am Psychol, PubMed 12971084, abstract paraphrased): mood shifts with daylight and mission phase; personality, social needs and coping measured during the mission predict mood and performance better than pre-deployment measures; members of low-cohesion expeditions report more depression, anxiety and anger; depression lower at physically more severe stations; wintering over linked to fewer later hospital admissions.
- SOURCED (Palinkas et al. 2000, Aviat Space Environ Med, PubMed 10870821): 657 American men who wintered 1963-1974; social/demographic, personality, interpersonal-need and station-environment variables together explained 9-17% of variance in performance measures; independent predictors across more than one measure: military service, low neuroticism, low extraversion (direction not stated in the abstract), low conscientiousness (same caveat), low desire for affection from others.
- SOURCED (Stuster slides): "Some individuals adjust quickly to isolation and confinement; others adjust slowly; a few fail to adjust and remain miserable." "Trivial issues are exaggerated." "Subgroups will form and can negatively affect group harmony, if permitted to develop to an extreme."
- SOURCED (Basner 2014): about 55% average intra-class correlation: stable trait-like individual differences; the crew member with the lowest social desirability score was the only one to report depressive symptoms (possible reporting bias).
- NOT FOUND: a quantified relation between crew size and morale in these studies other than the Palinkas 2000 note that crew size was among predictors tested (direction not given in the abstract).

**1.10 SIRIUS.**
- SOURCED (IAC-24 abstract https://iafastro.directory/iac/paper/id/88196/abstract-pdf/IAC-24,A1,1,2,x88196.brief.pdf, as summarized by the search tool; abstract not opened directly): SIRIUS-19 (about 4 months, March-July 2019, mixed-sex crew of six, IBMP Moscow) and SIRIUS-21 (8 months, started 19 November 2020); SIRIUS-21 showed a moderate rise in negative emotions (stress, sadness) after isolation, linked by authors to weaker group cohesion than SIRIUS-19; both kept generally good well-being. **2.4 International Space Station (crews of 3-7, sealed, 6 months).**

### 3. Recreation and leisure infrastructure at remote sites; private space; third places; light

**3.1 McMurdo Station (US; winter about 150-200; summer 800-1,000).**
- SOURCED (USAP Participant Guide 2016-2018, ch. 7, https://www.usap.gov/travelanddeployment/documents/participantguide-chapter7.pdf, read in full via pdftotext): austral winter population 150-200, summer 800-1,000; about 90% of USAP participants reside in or pass through McMurdo. **3.7 Evidence on private space and the use of places.**
- SOURCED (Carrere and Evans 1994, Environment and Behavior 26(6):707-741, abstract via search snippet): study of one Antarctic station over one winter with self-reports and video records: residents chose to spend much time alone and valued control over social contact; flexible rooms supporting both work and leisure were preferred; residents personalized the station; ratings of environmental qualities stayed neutral across the winter. UNVERIFIED in detail (abstract-level).
- SOURCED (Weiss, Feliot-Rippeault, Richard 2007, Environment and Behavior 39(2):147-164, DOI 10.1177/0013916505285934, via search snippet; station not confirmed): one year at a French Antarctic station; young scientists valued privacy and invested in their work areas, technicians preferred the shared main hall; flexibility characterized all preferred places; after midwinter preference moved from private places toward working areas, and near the end of the mission the need for privacy rose again.
- SOURCED (search snippets, UNVERIFIED): a study of 14 winter-over crew across five stations found lack of privacy associated with sleep disturbance; a 1988 Palmer Station study (Carrere, Stokols, Evans) recommended flexible spaces with visual and auditory privacy separated from social areas.
- SOURCED (Li and Zou 2025, Front Psychol, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1567378, read): Chinese Arctic Yellow River Station, Ny-Alesund, about 500 square meters, 20-25 people, most share bedrooms of about 10 square meters (manager has own room and bathroom); 12 interviews and 63 valid questionnaires; "space privacy" had the strongest correlation with restorative perception (r = 0.685, p < 0.01), room height 0.669, color 0.628, space interactivity 0.609, sports equipment 0.605, recreational facilities 0.472 (mean satisfaction 3.351/5, among the lowest); neatness of environment lowest satisfaction (2.769/5); temperature highest (4.411). Limitations: retrospective self-report; all Chinese expeditioners.
- SOURCED (NASA OCHMO-TB-016, above): individual quarters with auditory isolation; visual separation of private spaces for the perception of privacy.
- SOURCED (Doll and Gunderson 1970, US Navy Medical Neuropsychiatric Research Unit, catalog summary via search snippet https://openpolar.no/Record/ftdtic:AD0740348): 20-activity leisure questionnaire given twice in a year to 171 Navy enlisted men and 103 civilian scientists at stations of 8 to 30 members; "the most striking finding was the relatively low level of leisure activities at the smaller stations"; larger stations offered more facilities and more chances for social contact. UNVERIFIED in detail (full report not opened; openpolar fetch failed on a certificate error). **3.9 Light therapy rooms.**

**4.5 Social support and belonging: evidence.**
- SOURCED (Bell, Anderson, Roma, Landon, Dev 2024, Front Psychol 15:1350630, read): 64 crew members, HERA campaigns 4-6 (45 days) and SIRIUS-19 (120 days) and SIRIUS-21 (240 days), communication delay up to 5 minutes one way; perceived support declined over mission days across all four sources (public, analog organization, family/friends/colleagues, crew); crew support had the highest mean (34.7 on a 6-42 scale) and declined least (public 17.9 and steepest); overall support was unrelated to stress but crew support was related to lower evening stress (beta = -0.42, 95% CI -0.58 to -0.26); authors recommend screening and training for crew dynamics. Limitations: single-item stress measure; analog crews.
- SOURCED (Palinkas 2003): low-cohesion expeditions report more depression, anxiety and anger.
- SOURCED (Stuster): subgroups can form and harm group harmony if they develop to an extreme.
- SOURCED (Polarforschung 2022): recommended leadership: minimize hierarchies, build team identity, structure routines, reflect on individual needs.
- SOURCED (Basner 2015 PLoS One abstract via Europe PMC search result): proximity sensors in the 520-day simulation measured relations among six men; and (Basner 2026, Concordia) cohesion decreased over missions.

### 5. Conflict, discipline and misbehavior in remote communities

**5.1 The 2022 NSF/USAP sexual assault and harassment needs assessment (primary read).**
- SOURCED (NSF, Office of Polar Programs, "Sexual Assault/Harassment Prevention and Response (SAHPR) Final Report, Volume One: Needs Assessment," 22 June 2022, prepared by Team LDSS under an Interior Department Federal Consulting Group interagency agreement; downloaded from https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/files/USAP-SAHPR-Report.pdf and read via pdftotext): data collected from April 2021 by key stakeholder interviews (NSF, Leidos as lead of the Antarctic Support Contract, military, grantees), focus groups, an online survey open for two weeks in October-November 2021 (sent to 3,846 current or former community members within the last three years; 179 bounced; 880 completed, a 24% response rate of 3,667), plus 11 emails and 5 additional individual conversations; military personnel's survey responses arrived too late to be included.
- SOURCED (same report): "Nearly three-fourths of female respondents (72%) and almost half of all male respondents (48%) believe that sexual harassment is a problem within the USAP community" and 47% of women and 33% of men agree sexual assault is a problem; about 40% of all focus-group participants discussed a personal negative experience with sexual assault or harassment (footnote 25: this counts personally experiencing, personally witnessing, or being personally involved in the reporting process, so it is NOT a victimization rate); only 23% of "leadership" (older, higher-paid, higher-status) agree sexual assault is a problem and 40% agree harassment is; more than one-fourth of survey respondents do not believe or do not know if their organization cares whether they are safe, higher among women, seasonal employees, younger workers and lower earners; 52% say they understand the sexual assault reporting process and 61% the sexual harassment reporting process because of employer training; 48% find employer-provided information valuable; fewer than half of women understand how to report.
- SOURCED (same report, structure): USAP is multi-employer (contractors under Leidos and subcontractors, grantees, military); each employer holds discipline authority, so "NSF has limited ability to conduct comprehensive policy enforcement"; "the risk of removal from the ice is the only practical sanction within NSF's authority" (the 2018 Polar Code of Conduct lists removal from station, termination by the employer, or civil or criminal enforcement); a senior NSF person is quoted: "primarily NSF has the authority to remove people from the ice. It doesn't give us authority to tell a private company or an institution to discipline or fire anyone. It doesn't give us any authority over military personnel."
- SOURCED (same report): findings of distrust in contractor human resources (victims "dismissed, minimized, shamed, blamed" and retaliated against); a quoted contractor: "if they are harassed or assaulted and report it, they will be the ones who will be going home ... 'don't report it or you will go home and be blacklisted from the program'"; a grantee quote: "there is a perception that if a report is filed, the harassed will be removed, rather than the harasser"; in a team of six, losing one person is a serious cost so "people let things go"; accused persons removed from the ice were sometimes described as leaving for "family or medical needs," leaving victims unsure of outcomes; known offenders could return on another contractor with no formal process to prevent rehire; NSF "is not informed as to the frequency, scope, severity, or outcomes" of incidents; training seen as ineffective; prevention infrastructure "nearly absent"; the report recommends a coordinated community response team, victim support, a communication plan, and restructured policy and oversight.
- SOURCED (same report, structural risk factors): the isolated setting "blurs the boundaries between personal and professional life," making it harder to intervene as a bystander, hold peers accountable or report; male-dominated workforce and leadership (skilled trades) raises risk; the entity that pays and fires is not always the entity that enforces policies.
- SOURCED (same report, alcohol finding #7): "Alcohol contributes to sexual misconduct, but it is not the sole source"; "Drinking at the bars and 'pre-drinking in the rooms' are staples of community life at McMurdo"; supply planes deliver cases of beer or wine to field researchers and "cocktail hour every evening continues to be a tradition in the field for some"; senior administrators saw alcohol as "the number one risk" ("3 bars, 24 hours a day of light in the austral summer ... an incubator of trouble"; "work for 8 hours and party and drink for 16"); but many community members and focus groups reported incidents in the galley, machine shop, field camps, transport vans, immediately after harassment training, and on dry research vessels and alcohol-free field camps, none involving alcohol; one interviewee cited a Department of Justice list of workplace-violence factors (understaffing, frustration, downsizing, management style, inconsistent discipline) and said "when we blame alcohol, we don't acknowledge the real problem"; incidents of groping, harassment and stalking at or when leaving the McMurdo bars were also reported.
- UNVERIFIED (press): AP and others report "59% of women had experienced harassment or assault on the ice" and a "40.7% experienced at least one instance of sexual assault or harassment among 521 who completed a victimization inventory"; these numbers were NOT FOUND in Volume One of the Needs Assessment as read, so they may come from another volume or another metric (see Unresolved conflicts).

**5.2 What followed (2022-2025).**

### 6. Contact with home and the outside world; return and re-entry

**6.1 Bandwidth, by place.**

**6.3 What outside contact does to morale (evidence).**
- SOURCED (Stuster, French stations, slides): personal communications were roughly net-neutral (positive 46.2%, negative 43.7%, net +0.025) whereas communications with headquarters were strongly negative (positive 21.2%, negative 64.7%, net -0.435) and general administration -0.340; "communications with loved ones also can be problematic ... (misunderstandings, negative news, stimulating longings for home)"; outside communications increased in net positivity during the third quarter "at the same time the values of other behavioral categories decline"; frequent visits by outsiders at insular stations were disruptive and raised negativity; "headquarters does not respond in a timely fashion" and "angry or confused by message from headquarters" are recurring themes.
- SOURCED (Stuster 2010, ISS): Outside Communications was the second most frequent category (1,456 of the entry assignments) after Work (1,523).
- SOURCED (Basner 2014, Mars-500): conflicts with mission control outnumbered crew-crew conflicts five to one (41 vs 8) and peaked around the Mars-landing period; communication delay in effect from day 54.
- SOURCED (Bell et al. 2024): support from the public, the organization, and family declined across the mission, with the public declining fastest; crew support declined least.
- SOURCED (NASA OCHMO-TB-016): in-flight psychological support includes private family conferences; "communication system should be provided in each private quarter"; repatriation briefings with astronauts and their families normally 6 weeks before landing.
- SOURCED (Japanese Syowa study, NIPR 2021 release, https://www.nipr.ac.jp/english/info/notice/20210330.html): 172 participants from five JARE wintering teams (station typically about 30 people), six surveys; most coped either by keeping a strong internal connection to home or by adapting flexibly; no personality traits distinguished better from worse adaptation; readjustment after return not yet reported.
- SOURCED (Royal Navy/Forces News): weekly one-way family messages for submariners, 120 words (conflicting figures; see 2.2).

**6.4 Return and re-entry.**
- SOURCED (Australian Antarctic Division news, 2023, https://www.antarctica.gov.au/news/2023/fresh-berries-and-a-dog-to-pat-antarctic-expeditioners-pack-up-and-look-forward-to-home): end-of-season debrief by the program's psychology team with tips for readjusting; expeditioners can feel overwhelmed by smells, crowds or the sensation of driving fast; families go through a readjustment too and "roles and responsibilities sometimes need renegotiating"; returning by ship gives a gradual shift while returning by air is abrupt: "Don't schedule too much, you'll be more tired than you think"; expeditioners miss the close bonds, the simpler pace of station life, and cite things to get used to again (cooking for themselves, paying for groceries, locking up the house); a station leader: "We don't know how we're going to react to being in civilisation after a short 4.5 hour flight."
- SOURCED (Royal Navy/Forces News): submarine returnees describe fresh air, walking, "adjusting to silence": "People do struggle with adapting to the silence of it all."
- SOURCED (search snippets, DTIC 1985 US Navy report ADA161773 and others, UNVERIFIED): earlier studies found adjustment problems and "reverse culture shock" common among returning winter-over personnel; a Navy comparison found winter-over was not associated with higher later hospitalization; Palinkas 2003 (above): winter-over linked to fewer later hospital admissions.
- SOURCED (search snippet, UNVERIFIED): Norris's University of Tasmania 2010 doctoral thesis "Breaking the ice: developing a model of expeditioner and partner adaptation to Antarctic employment" (single expeditioners, partnered expeditioners, partners; reintegration over 12 months; ship-based vs air-based movement) and a related 2015 Polar Journal paper tracking 383 Australian expeditioners at 2 and 12 months, which reportedly found that psychological change was predicted by pre-departure and post-return factors rather than experiences on the ice; a 1987 Polar Record study (Taylor and McCormick) found couples' relationships improved slightly overall at 16 months after separation with individual exceptions. None of these primary sources was opened (all fetches failed) and the 2015 paper's details conflict between snippets.
- SOURCED (Balderrama-Durbin et al. 2015, Psychological Services 12(3):213-221, PMID 26213790, via Europe PMC): 76 partnered US Air Force members before, during and after a year-long Iraq deployment; about 20% (nearly 1 in 5) reported moderate to severe difficulties in family reintegration; predicted by pre-deployment relationship distress and shared commitment to the military, and post-deployment PTSD symptoms, alcohol misuse, reluctance to discuss combat, and relationship distress. This is war-zone deployment and not directly comparable to a posting.
- SOURCED (search snippets, UNVERIFIED): longitudinal study of 555 military couples for seven months after return found changes in emotional and sexual intimacy, readjustment to daily life and conflict, with the frequency of these changes declining over the transition (Knobloch-Fedders et al. 2020).

**8.4 Humor as mechanism (evidence).**
- SOURCED (Nautilus, https://nautil.us/why-we-need-court-jesters-in-space-237446, reporting Jeffrey Johnson, University of Florida anthropology): at Antarctic research stations "the most effective crews had members who took on informal roles that supported group cohesion"; at the South Pole "the clown bridged social relations between the scientists and the trades crew—the plumbers, electricians, and mechanics"; less effective groups lacked a clown or other informal leaders; the same report relays HERA findings (nine four-person crews, 30-45 days): collective performance improved with practice but the longer the isolation the worse the crews became at thinking together, combining expertise and solving problems creatively. ### 9. Meaningful work and purpose

**9.1 Work as the primary source of satisfaction; too little and too much.**
- SOURCED (Stuster, French remote stations, slides): "Work is a primary source of satisfaction. Too much or too little work results in negativity and can threaten individual adjustment. Helping others with novel tasks provides welcome variety in a monotonously routine schedule." Workload ranks third among salient categories (after group interaction and outside communications).
- SOURCED (Stuster 2010, ISS): Work is the most-mentioned category (1,523 assignments); "The importance of meaningful work to adjustment and morale is evident"; "all participants complained about trivial, redundant, and tedious tasks, but few complained about the low workload periods that are typically experienced during the latter stages of transferring responsibility for the ISS to a replacement crew"; the high tempo of scheduled work usually limited leisure; Earth photography completely eliminated boredom as an enemy for most.
- SOURCED (Basner 2014, Mars-500): workload highest in the first quarter and lower afterward, consistent with growing sedentariness; activity fell steeply early and rose at the end.
- SOURCED (Van Puyvelde et al. 2022, 24 interviews): high workload can amplify ICE impacts and, citing HERA, worsens the cycle in which relationship conflicts and individual strain feed each other; external work planning in analog crews did not account for sleep deprivation or actual workload; monotony was among the most frequently cited environmental stressors ("a blank page"); the authors cite literature linking prolonged boredom to lack of motivation, risky behavior such as alcohol use, and slower emergency response; strong job devotion was a common coping strategy (one participant never skipped checking equipment and data); prior research links job performance and satisfaction to feelings of usefulness and self-esteem; devoted people may be over-represented in post-mission studies. The paper does not address task variety directly.
- SOURCED (search snippets, UNVERIFIED): occupational stressors in ICE include monotony and boredom, alternating high and low workloads, and lack of separation between living and working spaces.
- SOURCED (Sletten 2022): circadian misalignment with work and sleep schedules reduces alertness and performance; regular work schedules act as time cues (Duplessis 2007, in Zivi 2020).
- SOURCED (USAP SAHPR 2022, Section on risk factors): a DOJ-derived list of workplace-violence factors quoted by an interviewee includes understaffing, frustration, downsizing, management style, inconsistent discipline, frequent grievances.

**9.2 Role and status.**
- SOURCED (search snippet of a US Navy report, DTIC AD0766452 "Factors Affecting the Stability of Job Attitudes in Long-Term Isolated Groups"; abstract only): job attitudes were measured three times over 18 months in 231 Navy enlisted men and 140 civilian scientists and technicians; the Navy men's morale deteriorated through the winter while the scientists' did not; "occupational role was seen to be an important determinant of job attitudes during long-term isolation." UNVERIFIED (full report not opened; fetch failed).
- SOURCED (Weiss et al. 2007, above): within one French station young scientists and technicians used space differently (scientists invested in work areas and privacy; technicians in the main hall).
- SOURCED (SAHPR 2022): contractor/subcontractor employees and grantees had different employers and discipline systems; lower-status, seasonal, younger and lower-paid workers had less trust in leadership; "four separate elements, each with its own rules and group culture" (Navy, contractor, scientists, NSF managers) is a historian's description per a search snippet (UNVERIFIED).
- SOURCED (search snippet from a 2025-26 account, UNVERIFIED): of a McMurdo summer population about 735 were support staff (electricians, plumbers, cooks, helicopter pilots, cargo specialists, IT, medical).
- SOURCED (Polarforschung 2022): Neumayer's 9 winterers: 4 scientists, 3 technicians, 1 physician-leader, 1 cook; recommended minimized hierarchy.
- NOT FOUND: a systematic job-satisfaction survey of Antarctic support contractors versus scientists.

**9.3 Evidence from rotation workers (offshore, mining, maritime).**
- SOURCED (Asare et al. 2021, "Health and well-being of rotation workers in the mining, offshore oil and gas, and construction industry: a systematic review," BMJ Glob Health, PMC8311322, first 100,000 characters read): 90 studies (64 offshore oil and gas, 14-16 mining, 5 FIFO/DIDO, 3 construction), average 91.2% male; psychological distress 10.0-36.3% onshore workers and 8.0-14.0% offshore; depression 28.3-32.3% onshore and 16.7-28.0% offshore; anxiety 22.3% onshore and 11.4-15% offshore; job demands linked to poorer mental health (one OR = 6.2, 95% CI 1.8-21.2); job control (4 studies) and job clarity (2 studies) and social support (5 studies) linked to better mental health (perceived supervisor support RR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.63-0.92); roster effects: 2 weeks on/1 off had OR = 2.4 (1.7-3.4) for high distress vs 4 on/1 off; 56 on/28 off had OR = 1.53 (1.15-2.04) for anxiety vs 28/28; shifts over 12 hours OR = 1.61 (1.17-2.30); remoteness-related stress OR = 3.7 (1.6-8.6); living-condition stress including shared accommodation and lack of privacy linked to anxiety. Boredom not found in the part read.
- SOURCED (Brooks SK et al. 2022, "Mental health and psychological wellbeing of maritime personnel: a systematic review," BMC Psychol, PMC9150387, first 100,000 characters read): 63 studies; loneliness, homesickness and separation from family were frequent; high social support linked to lower depression (n = 917); long daily hours (more than ten) associated with emotional exhaustion and sleepiness; shorter time on board (around two months) associated with better mental health and job satisfaction (n = 298); temporary contracts linked to depression and job insecurity the strongest predictor of fatigue in one study; inadequate telecommunication a strain; recreational areas valued for cohesion; only two intervention studies; 35.8% of 530 seafarers named living and working conditions as main source of job dissatisfaction.
- SOURCED (search snippets, press on an industry-funded Robert Gordon University report, UNVERIFIED): North Sea workers on three-weeks-on/three-off rotas were nearly twice as likely to report ill health as those on two-on/two-off.
- SOURCED (search snippet, International SOS Foundation survey, UNVERIFIED): 29% of remote rotational workers met benchmark for clinical depression on rotation; strong sense of community and support from co-workers and managers.
- NOT FOUND: evidence for 14/14 rotations specifically; studies on boredom as a distinct factor for rotation workers; evidence for postings of one to several years.

### Unresolved conflicts
- Mars-500 depressive symptoms: abstract says the most affected man reported symptoms in "93% of mission weeks"; body text says 8 of 74 weeks (about 11%).
- Third-quarter phenomenon: supported (Stuster French stations, ISS journals, HI-SEAS model, Sandal 2018 midwinter dip) vs not consistently supported (Bell 2019 review: 3 of 7 teams; Kjaergaard cited as equivocal); Sletten 2022 found conflict peaking in September and changes tracking time on expedition more than daylight.
- Darkness vs isolation: Palinkas 1995 attributes winter depression more to social isolation than to darkness; Palinkas 2001 ties T3/thyroid and mood to season; Arendt 2012 finds true SAD rare; Cochrane evidence on light therapy preventing winter depression is "very low" quality while single Halley trials show circadian benefits.
- Cognition: Barkaszi 2016 found no negative cognitive effect at Concordia; a 2025 npj Microgravity abstract found transient gray-matter loss; Sletten found circadian-misalignment performance effects.
- Midwinter origin: USAP says first celebrated by the Belgica crew in 1898; Wikipedia says Belgica had no celebration and credits Scott's expeditions; another source says 1902 Discovery.
- 300 Club origin: 1979 Pole adjunct to Scott's Hut Race vs 1950s Little America V "over 300 club".
- Submarine family messages: Royal Navy weekly 120 words vs two 60-word messages weekly (Jersey Evening Post) vs 4 x 20-word messages per patrol (US account) vs 40-word limit attributed to US boats; SSBN patrol length 70-77 days vs 90.
- South Pole: winter population 39 (Yahoo) vs about 45 (USAP guide); internet about 5 hours a day (2021 report) vs about 12 hours across three systems (USAP page snippet).
