## Pick 1 — Shouguang, Shandong (passive-solar winter greenhouses)

**Claim to test:** "A national vegetable base built on low-power earth-walled greenhouses that grow through freezing winters (K14, K8)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with narrowing.** (a) The greenhouses are passive-solar "winter-warm" (冬暖式) lean-to houses whose 1989 design was coal-free, relying on a heat-storing north wall and a night thermal blanket; early walls were rammed or piled earth, later brick plus foam and then aerated concrete, so "earth-walled" is true of the original type and not of every current generation. (b) "National" is correct in two senses: Shouguang supplies about a third of the vegetables sold in Beijing markets (2018 figure), and it exports the greenhouse method itself (8,000+ technicians working across China, 2025). (c) It sits inside a national policy, the Vegetable Basket program (from 1988), but the 1989 greenhouse was a village initiative, not a state design. (d) "Low-power" now needs narrowing: 7th-generation "smart" greenhouses use IoT controls and motorized blanket rollers (electricity use not quantified in sources read). (e) It is also a big brine, salt, bromine and chemical town; vegetables are not its only industry.

### A. Snapshot
- County-level city under Weifang prefecture, north-central Shandong, on the southwest shore of Laizhou Bay (Bohai Sea). Area 2,200 km². Population 1,139,454 (2010 census); 1,103,110 (2018). 5 subdistricts and 9 towns (2012). — Wikipedia "Shouguang", read 2026-10-29. **SOURCED** (encyclopedia, secondary).
- Defining feature: the "winter-warm" passive-solar vegetable greenhouse, first built at Sanyuanzhu village in 1989. — Xinhua 2018-12-09 (http://www.xinhuanet.com/english/2018-12/09/c_137660983.htm); China Daily/chinaservicesinfo 2018-12-25 (https://chinaservicesinfo.com/s/201812/25/WS5c218c2c498ee2f0291e4690/greenhouse-vegetables-show-quality-is-king-amid-upgrade.html). **SOURCED.** [K14]
- Origin story (state media accounts, both read): the Sanyuanzhu village Party secretary (elected 1978) visited six provinces and cities in 1988 and early 1989 to learn techniques; the makeshift greenhouses then in use burned "around five tons" of coal each winter; he built a coal-free design heated only by sunlight and in mid-1989 persuaded 16 villagers to join, starting with cucumbers; the 17 households "earned much more" that winter and the whole village followed. — same two sources. **SOURCED.** [K14, K8] A third report (China Daily 2011-02-24) is a dead link (404). A snippet-only figure: "By 1990, some 5,130 greenhouses were set up" in the county, earning "over 60 million yuan" a year. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of a page that returned 404).

### E. Structural features
- **Single-point fragility (K9):** Typhoon Rumbia (Aug 18–19, 2018) plus upstream reservoir releases into the Mi River damaged "nearly 200,000 vegetable hothouses (half of Shouguang's total)," drowned about 25,000 hogs; rebuild cost CNY 150,000 per greenhouse; farmers had no insurance and had to wait until late fall to rebuild, "almost a year" without income; cucumbers rose from CNY 1.2–1.7 to 3 per half-kilo and Shandong's vegetable price index jumped 16% in a week; officials were criticized for not discharging water in advance. — Yicai Global 2018-08-27 (https://www.yicaiglobal.com/news/flood-sets-china-veggieville-back-two-years-greenhouses-are-still-waterlogged); China Daily 2018-08-28 (https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/201808/28/WS5b8493d4a310add14f388109.html). **SOURCED.** Total losses 21.5 billion yuan, 10,000 houses collapsed (snippet). **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Soil exhaustion (K9):** a study of 72 Shouguang solar greenhouses found soil quality "improved at first and then sharply declined after 10 a," with high P and K, low organic matter, and high nitrate infiltration that "poses risks to underground water safety." — Li, Xu & Liu 2019, IJABE (https://ijabe.migration.pkpps06.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/ijabe/article/view/4092). **SOURCED** (abstract-level).
- **Construction lineage (K14):** walls of dry-rammed or piled earth and brick, back wall 1.5 m high (early); 1970s–90s types with backfill and 1.5 m-thick bases; 1990s "Liaoshen I" double clay brick with 100–200 mm polystyrene; clay brick dropped in 2005 for aerated concrete; research put ideal earth-wall thickness at 1.5 m and ranked perlite, then cinder, then sawdust as fill. — HortiDaily 2019-06-10 (https://www.hortidaily.com/article/9111101/from-ancient-to-now-the-history-of-the-chinese-solar-wall-greenhouses). **SOURCED.** Working principle: transparent south-facing film roof, opaque insulated north roof, a heat-storing north wall, ground storage, and a thermal blanket rolled down at sunset and up in the day; used across China north of the Yangtze. — Sun et al. 2023, Frontiers in Energy Research (https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2023.1247153/full). **SOURCED.** "Usable where night temperatures can be below −20 °C," and an unheated house held 10–15 °C above outside on a February night. — search snippets (thermalscience.vinca.rs; resilience.org). **UNVERIFIED.**

## Pick 2 — Singapore's food and water security ("30 by 30," the Four National Taps, rooftop and vertical farms)

**Claim to test:** "A state that imports most of its food has set a national target for growing its own in enclosed spaces, and treats water as a strategic system (K1)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED in part, CORRECTED in part.** (a) Imports: over 90% of food is imported, from 187 countries and regions (2024). **Confirmed.** (b) The national target existed ("30 by 30," set 2019: 30% of nutritional needs produced locally by 2030 on under 1% of land), but it was **not a target for enclosed growing as such** (it covered eggs, fish and vegetables, by any method) and it was **dropped on 4 November 2025**, replaced by "Singapore Food Story 2": by 2035, 20% of fiber (vegetables, beansprouts, mushrooms) and 30% of protein (eggs, seafood), with resilience in a disruption as the stated aim. The enclosed-farm push largely failed commercially: of 16 rooftop car-park farm sites tendered in 2020–21, 7 remained in Nov 2025, producing under 1% of local output; several large indoor farms closed or shrank 2023–25. (c) Water as a strategic system: **confirmed strongly** (Four National Taps, a tax on water from 1991, a 99-year import agreement expiring 2061, a long public campaign to make recycled water acceptable). (d) Food stockpiling is a fourth, older pillar (rice stockpile rule since 1968).

### A. Snapshot
- City-state; food is over 90% imported, from 187 countries and regions in 2024 (140 in 2004). — SG101 "Singapore's Food Challenge" (https://www.sg101.gov.sg/economy/case-studies/sg-food-challenge/), read 2026-10-29. **SOURCED.** [K5]
- Defining feature for this pick: a government-built system of food and water security in a state with almost no farmland and limited natural water: four water sources, food-import diversification, stockpiles, and local production. [K1]
- Water demand about 440 million gallons a day, expected to almost double by 2065; two-thirds of the land area is water catchment; 17 reservoirs; 8,000+ km of drains and canals; five NEWater factories and five desalination plants (the fifth, on Jurong Island, opened 17 April 2022). — Ministry of Sustainability and the Environment (https://mse.gov.sg/policies/water), read 2026-10-29. **SOURCED.** [K1]

### B. What else exists there
**Food policy (K1, K5):**
- Primary Production Department founded 1959; self-sufficiency in eggs, poultry and pork reached by the 1980s; mid-2000s price volatility prompted a rethink. In 2021 local farms met 30% of egg needs, 8% of fish, 4.5% of leafy greens. Stockpiles widened after COVID (2020+) to frozen protein and canned vegetables; eggs imported from Poland from June 2020. 2025 partnerships: an Agreement on Trade in Essential Supplies with New Zealand and a rice-trade memorandum with Vietnam (both October 2025). — SG101. **SOURCED.**
- Why 30 by 30 was dropped (Minister Grace Fu, 4 Nov 2025): supply-chain disruption, energy and labor cost inflation, tighter financing, "delays in farm development and some exits," weak scaling and consumer acceptance of alternative proteins. Local shares then: hen eggs over 30%, beansprouts over 50%. New four-pronged strategy: diversify imports, local production, stockpiling, and global partnerships; government to help with costs and build multi-tenanted "plug-and-play" farm facilities. — Mothership 2025-11-04 (https://mothership.sg/2025/11/singapore-30-by-30-food-replace/). **SOURCED.** Overall self-production "below 10%" in 2025; Agri-food Cluster Transformation fund S$60 million (2021), S$28 million to 43 companies by end-2024; two large Lim Chu Kang high-tech hub projects delayed from 2024. — Eco-Business 2025-03-05 (https://www.eco-business.com/news/singapores-30-by-30-food-security-goal-currently-under-review-confirms-government). **SOURCED.**
- **Rice Stockpile Scheme**: from March 1968, rice importers must hold a buffer of two times their average monthly imports in a government-designated private warehouse; importers own the stock but the government can acquire it in an emergency; stock must rotate within a year; administration moved from Enterprise Singapore to the Singapore Food Agency on 1 April 2023. — search summary of Enterprise Singapore / customs pages. **UNVERIFIED** (pages not opened). The "two times average monthly imports" rule and a government-held stockpile expanded since 2020 (levels not disclosed, "assessed and re-calibrated periodically"). — written parliamentary reply, 4 July 2022 (https://www.meti.gov.sg/newsroom/written-reply-to-pq-on-stockpiling-of-key-food-items). **SOURCED.** [K1, K5, K6]

## Pick 3 — Qatar's food build-out after the 2017 blockade (Baladna dairy)

**Claim to test:** "A closed door forced a country to build domestic food production fast, in a desert (K5, K9)."

## Pick 4 — West Berlin's Senate Reserve and the Airlift

**Claim to test:** "A city whose land access could be cut kept large stockpiles, and its people lived with that knowledge for decades (K5, K6)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with one narrowing.** The Senate Reserve (Senatsreserve) existed from about 1950 to 1990, ordered by the three Western Allied commandants after the 1948–49 blockade, sized to keep "normal" life going for at least 180 days, and paid for by the federal government. **Narrowing:** the reserve was general public knowledge, but its **locations and contents were kept secret** (700+ mostly secret sites; "very few people" had detailed knowledge). Ordinary contact with it came through the **sale of rotated old stock at low prices** to residents. The knowledge was lived with as a background fact plus a periodic cheap-goods experience, not as detailed awareness. A second precaution of the same kind is documented: a gas store built under the Stößensee lake when Soviet gas began flowing in October 1985.

### A. Snapshot
- **Blockade** 24 June 1948 – 12 May 1949; **airlift** 26 June 1948 – 30 September 1949. 2,334,374 tons delivered on 278,228 flights (US 76.4%, RAF 23.3%); nearly two-thirds of tonnage was coal, about 1,500 tons a day was food; peak "Easter" effort 15–16 April 1949, 12,941 tons on 1,383 flights. At the start, West Berlin held "at least 18 days' supply per major food type." — Wikipedia "Berlin Blockade," read 2026-10-29 (first 100k of 390k characters). **SOURCED** (secondary). [K5]
- Deaths: Wikipedia gives 101 (40 British, 31 American, 15 German civilians); the Tempelhof-Schöneberg district office and the Airlift Gratitude Foundation give 78; a berlin.de listing snippet gives 76. Figures differ by what is counted. — Wikipedia; berlin.de district page "75 Jahre Luftbrückendenkmal" (https://www.berlin.de/ba-tempelhof-schoeneberg/ueber-den-bezirk/gedenken/artikel.1688741.php). **SOURCED** (both); 76 **UNVERIFIED**. [K9]
- **Senate Reserve**: required by the Western Allied commanders-in-chief, with the Allies keeping oversight; aim at least 180 days of "normal" life, making another blockade "economically pointless"; federal government paid for goods and turnover, 30 million DM a year (1957) rising to 100 million DM (1990); ~4 million metric tons in 700+ mostly secret facilities (624,000 m² open storage, 423,000 m² indoor); value 2,000 million DM (early 1980s), 1,600 million DM (1990); about 1,000 item types, including 189,000 t of grain, 44,000 t of meat, 18 million rolls of toilet paper, 25.8 million cigars, 291,000 pairs of children's shoes; replaced old stock "sold at low prices to the population"; on dissolution, 90,000 t given to the Soviet Union as humanitarian aid. — Wikipedia "Senate Reserve," read 2026-10-29. **SOURCED** (secondary). Coal to last about a year, medicine six months; the ration to be 2,900 calories a day (blockade level 1,800). — search snippets of the same and related pages. **UNVERIFIED.** [K1, K5, K8]
- The order dates from 1950 (the three Western commandants); listed contents also included ~50,000 kg of pickled cucumbers, mustard, cigarettes, bus brake pads and "19 cows." — Berliner Zeitung, Susanne Lenz, 2026-03-28 (https://www.berliner-zeitung.de/article/die-west-berliner-senatsreserve-symptom-des-kalten-kriegs-das-in-der-ddr-fuer-schadenfreude-sorgte-10025836) (paywalled; read the open introduction only; its headline says the reserve caused **Schadenfreude** in the GDR, body not read). **SOURCED** (intro only). ## Pick 5 — Hong Kong's Dongjiang water supply and the express food trains

**Claim to test:** "A large city whose water and fresh food arrived through a few dedicated lines from outside (K5)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED for water; CONFIRMED historically and NARROWED today for food.** (a) Water: since 1 March 1965 an 83 km supply line from the Dongjiang (East River) has provided about 70–80% of Hong Kong's fresh water (annual ceiling 820 million m³, extendable to 1,100); since 2003 it runs in a dedicated closed aqueduct. (b) Fresh food: from 1962 to 2010 three scheduled daily express freight trains (Nos. 751, 753, 755; from Wuhan, Shanghai and Zhengzhou) carried live pigs, cattle and other fresh goods; by the 1990s they supplied "more than 99%" of Hong Kong's live poultry and livestock (Shenzhen government account). **Narrowing:** the trains ended in 2010 and fresh food now arrives mainly by **road** through a few border crossings (above all Man Kam To / Wenjindu), plus water routes and, since 2023, a night "green channel" over the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge; staples such as rice come mostly from Southeast Asia, not from the mainland lines. (c) The 2022 COVID wave showed the fragility: when truck drivers fell ill and the crossing closed briefly, fresh-produce supply dropped by as much as 70% and vegetable prices rose about threefold; a rail freight link was reopened on 3 March 2022.

### A. Snapshot
- About 7 million people; about one-third of the 1,098 km² land area is water catchment, including three reservoirs built in the sea. — DEVB blog 2023-12-10 (https://devb.gov.hk/en/home/my_blog/index_id_1546.html); Wikipedia "Water supply and sanitation in Hong Kong," read 2026-10-29. **SOURCED.**
- Defining feature for this pick: dependence on a small number of physical lines from the mainland for water and fresh food. [K5, K1]

### B. What else exists there
**The 1963 water crisis (K9, K6):** drought from Nov 1962 to Dec 1963 (901 mm of rain against 2,220 expected); rationing tightened from 3 hours a day (2 May 1963) to 4 hours every two days (16 May) to **4 hours every four days (1 June)**; people, "mostly the women and children," queued at street standpipes; hillside squatter settlements carried water uphill; thousands of hectares of paddy abandoned; water-heavy industries needed special permits; Buddhist monks held three-day prayer vigils for rain; 13 chartered tankers made 1,371 round trips to the Pearl River (June 1963 – June 1964), bringing 4,288 million gallons (about one-third of consumption) at a cost of about $67 million; old wells reopened; cloud seeding tried (October 1963, unsuccessful); a Water Supply Emergency Committee met 40 times; relief came with Typhoon Viola on 27 May 1964. — Industrial History of Hong Kong Group (https://industrialhistoryhk.org/hong-kong-water-supply-the-1963-water-famine/). **SOURCED** (well-documented local-history site). **The express trains (K5, K7, K14):** Hong Kong asked for help with fresh-goods shortages in 1959; the Ministries of Foreign Trade and Railways set up the service; the first train (No. 621, later 751) left Wuhan at midnight on 20 March 1962 with 30+ wagons of pigs, cattle and sheep; Nos. 753 (Shanghai Xinlonghua) and 755 (Zhengzhou North) were added on 11 December 1962; they ran on fixed schedules three times daily; escorts ventilated wagons constantly in summer to keep pigs alive, separated huddling pigs in winter to stop crushing, and swept manure several times a day; by the 1990s the trains supplied "more than 99%" of live poultry and livestock and "more than half" of frozen meat, fish, fruit and vegetables; retired in 2010 after 48 years. — Shenzhen Municipal Government, "Shenzhen Story" 2022-12-21 (https://www.sz.gov.cn/szstory/202212/content/post_10348350.html). **SOURCED** (official account). ## Pick 6 — Pan Am's hydroponic garden on Wake Island and WWII hydroponic farms (Ascension, Chōfu)

*Researched as a tradition (soilless growing to feed isolated outposts) with three documented places, per the brief.*

**Claim to test:** "Food grown without soil on coral and bare ground, in tanks, to supply bases that could not import fresh produce."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED for Wake and Ascension; CORRECTED for Chōfu.** (a) **Wake** (1937–41) was a commercial airline stopover, not a military base: a coral atoll with no natural soil, resupplied by ship about every six months; Pan American grew vegetables in redwood and then concrete tanks for about 35 residents and the weekly Clipper passengers. (b) **Ascension** (1945 – mid-1946): a US military farm for about 2,000 servicemen, growing in beds of local black volcanic clinker, closed about a year after the war. (c) **Chōfu** (from 1946) was **not** a base that could not obtain produce: Japan grew plenty of vegetables, but they were fertilized with night soil and carried parasites and bacteria, so the occupation army grew its own "clean" salad vegetables in gravel beds on a former Japanese Army airfield. The motive was hygiene, not access. It also shaped Japanese farming: the government issued national guidelines for "clean vegetables" in 1955. (d) "In tanks" fits all three, though the media differed (water culture at Wake; volcanic clinker at Ascension; washed gravel at Chōfu).

### A. Snapshot
- **Wake Island (Pan Am, 1935–41):** construction began 1935; airmail from 22 Nov 1935; passenger service from 4 Nov 1936; two scheduled flights a week in 1937; a prefabricated 48-room hotel with hot showers; Chamorro workers from Guam as kitchen and service staff; the freighter SS *North Haven* brought workers, materials and six months of provisions (arrivals 9 May 1935, 5 Feb 1936); "resupply ship came only once every six months"; on 8 Dec 1941 Japanese bombing destroyed the hotel and killed employees, the *Philippine Clipper* evacuated about 40 passengers and Pan Am staff, and the Chamorro workers were left behind (5 of 45 killed in the first strikes, 5 more when the hospital was bombed). — Wikipedia "Peale Island," read 2026-10-29. **SOURCED** (secondary). [K5, K9]
- **Ascension Island:** first hydroponic farm built by the US military in 1945 "for the c.2000 servicemen on the island"; believed to be only the third large-scale production plant of its kind; crops at first cucumbers, tomatoes, radishes, green peppers and lettuce in beds of local black clinker (rock), which a 1985 trial found superior to commercial perlite; featured in National Geographic (Moore, 1945); closed mid-1946. — Leigh Morris, *Hydroponic Crop Production on Ascension Island: History, Current Situation and Potential*, Blue Marine Foundation, January 2019 (https://www.bluemarinefoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Ascension-Hydroponics-Report-Jan-19.pdf), pp. 3, 5. **SOURCED** (read pages 1–14).
