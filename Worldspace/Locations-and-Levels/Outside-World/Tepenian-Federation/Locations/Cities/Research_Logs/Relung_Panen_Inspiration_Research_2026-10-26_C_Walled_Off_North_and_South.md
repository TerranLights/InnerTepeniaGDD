## 1. Skagway and Haines, Alaska

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked.

### A. Snapshot

**Skagway**
- Head of Taiya Inlet, northern end of the Alaska panhandle. Borough area 464 sq mi (452 land, 12 water); "currently the smallest borough in Alaska." Population 1,240 (2020 census), 968 (2010); 2025 estimate 1,104. Population "doubles in the summer tourist season." SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skagway,_Alaska
- Founded 1897 in the Klondike gold rush; 8,000 to 10,000 people in 1898, about 3,000 at the 1900 census, below 1,000 by 1910, near 500 by 1920. First first-class city in the Territory of Alaska (1900); in June 2007 voters dissolved the City and incorporated the Municipality of Skagway (a borough). SOURCED: Robinson and Shanks, "Skagway: Gold, a railroad, and lots of big ships," Alaska Economic Trends, June 2008, https://laborstats.alaska.gov/trends/jun08art1.pdf
- Name: from Tlingit "Shgagwei," "roughed up water," per Skagway Traditional Council. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of skagway.org; council page not opened).
- **The blocker:** the Coast Mountains rise straight from the inlet. Two passes lead to the Yukon headwaters lakes:
  - **White Pass**, 873 m / 2,864 ft (Wikipedia, White Pass, via search result). The railway's summit is given as 2,885 ft (879 m), 20 mi from Skagway. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Pass_and_Yukon_Route . The highway summit is given as 3,292 ft at Milepost S 14.3 of the South Klondike Highway. UNVERIFIED (search snippet, passbagger.org; not opened). Note the three figures differ because rail line, highway and pass saddle are different points.
  - **Chilkoot Pass**: Wikipedia's Chilkoot Trail page gives 1,067 m / 3,525 ft and a trail of 33 mi (53 km); Wikipedia's Chilkoot Pass page gives 3,759 ft (1,146 m). The two figures conflict and I did not resolve which is the summit saddle. SOURCED (trail page read): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilkoot_Trail ; 3,759 ft UNVERIFIED (snippet).
  - Crossing in 1897-98: Canada required "at least one ton of gear, enough to supply a prospector for one year"; five tramway operations eventually competed to haul goods over the Chilkoot. Trail's Tlingit name "Chilkoot Dei-yi," "Chilkoot-owned Trail," reflecting Tlingit control of the trade route. Trail "became obsolete in 1899" when the railway was built from Skagway over White Pass. SOURCED: Chilkoot Trail page above.
  - Railway: White Pass & Yukon Route, 3 ft narrow gauge, 107 mi Skagway to Whitehorse; construction began May 1898, final spike July 29, 1900; "450 tons of explosives" to reach the summit; one tunnel. Closed October 7, 1982 after metal prices collapsed; reopened as a heritage line from 1988 (to Fraser 1989, Bennett 1992); now runs 67.5 mi to Carcross. Bought July 2018 by Klondike Holdings and Carnival Corporation in a joint venture. SOURCED: WP&YR Wikipedia page above.
  - Road: Klondike Highway, officially opened 1979 (Trends June 2008 footnote); Wikipedia says completed 1978. 108 road miles to Whitehorse; 98 mi to the Alaska Highway. SOURCED: https://themilepost.com/communities/skagway/ . Open year-round (NPS winter page below).

**Haines**
- Chilkat Peninsula between the Chilkoot and Chilkat rivers, at the head of Lynn Canal. Town population 1,657 (2020), 1,713 (2010). SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haines,_Alaska . Borough 2,530 per The Milepost. SOURCED: https://themilepost.com/communities/haines/ (year of figure not stated).
- Borough covers nearly 2,620 sq mi; 40-mile drive from Haines to the Canadian border; Lynn Canal is "North America's longest glacial fjord," extending 90 mi south. 2009 estimated population 2,286, median age 46.4 (state 33.5). SOURCED: Wilkinson, Baron and Krieger, "The Haines Borough," Alaska Economic Trends, Sept 2010, https://live.laborstats.alaska.gov/sites/default/files/trendsArt/sep10art3.pdf
- Native name "Deishú," "end of the trail." SOURCED: Haines Wikipedia page.
- **The blocker and the gap in it:** the Haines Highway runs about 152-153 mi to Haines Junction, Yukon, crossing **Chilkat Pass at 3,510 ft (1,070 m)**. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of Wikipedia Chilkat Pass and The Milepost; pages not opened). Highway completed 1943, following the Dalton Trail, itself a Tlingit trade route called the "grease trail" (after candlefish oil) controlled by the Ganaxtedih Ravens and Deklawedih Eagles of Klukwan; Jack Dalton ran it as a toll road from 1899. SOURCED: Trends Sept 2010. U.S. Customs open 7 a.m. to 11 p.m.; highway maintained year-round. SOURCED: The Milepost Haines page.
- Skagway and Haines are about 15 miles apart by water across Lynn Canal / Taiya Inlet but have no road between them. Passenger fast ferry about 45 minutes, May to September, up to about 10 sailings a day on peak days. UNVERIFIED (ferrygogo.com and alaska.org snippets; not opened). State ferry (Alaska Marine Highway) links both. The Haines ferry terminal is 4.5 mi out on Lutak Road; Haines airport 3.5 mi from downtown. SOURCED: The Milepost Haines page.
- 1948: two Haines residents started a ferry to Skagway and Tee Harbor near Juneau; the Territory bought it in 1951 and it later became the Alaska Marine Highway System. SOURCED: Trends Sept 2010.

### B. What else exists there

**Skagway economy**
- Tourism dominant: 2,025,841 visitors in 2025, about 1.3 million by cruise ship. SOURCED: Skagway Wikipedia (cites 2025 figure; underlying source not checked).
- 2019: 1,035,800 cruise passengers stopped in Skagway (78% of all Alaska cruise passengers). Skagway and Denali workforces are "67 percent nonresident in a typical year"; in season Skagway employment "more than triples." SOURCED: Wiebold, "Has tourism fully bounced back?", Alaska Economic Trends, April 2023, https://live.laborstats.alaska.gov/sites/default/files/trends/apr23art1.pdf
- 2007 (state labor data): average monthly employment 880; Retail 197; Transportation 222 (scenic and sightseeing 164); restaurants and bars 77; accommodations 15; government 168 (federal 50, state 14, local 104). White Pass & Yukon Route the largest employer, 144 summer jobs in 2007. Retail shrinks from ~350 summer jobs to under 100 in winter; 67% of retail employers are clothing, jewelry and gift shops. Sales tax $5.3 million in 2007, about $6,000 per capita. Only 23.3% of residents born in Alaska (2000). Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park employs about 20 year-round and 30 more in summer. Tourism is "pretty much the only game in town." SOURCED: Trends June 2008.
- Sales tax 3%, rising to 5% in summer; $7 million in 2013, $6,996 per year-round resident. Scenic and sightseeing transportation employment 432 times more common than nationally. SOURCED: Bell, "Tourism in Southeast," Alaska Economic Trends, Aug 2015, https://laborstats.alaska.gov/trends/aug15art1.pdf
- Ore terminal: 2007 the rehabilitated Skagway Ore Terminal took its first concentrate since 1997 (Yukon copper-gold, trucked down, shipped to Asian smelters) (Trends 2008). Minto mine shipped through it from 2008; Minto Metals stopped in 2023 and went bankrupt; in 2026 Selkirk Copper Mines began a scoping study with the Municipality on shipping concentrate for a Minto restart (up to $200,000 funding). UNVERIFIED in detail (search snippets of miningnewsnorth.com, 2023 and 2026-08-21; not opened).
- Port: at midnight March 18, 2023 port management reverted to the Municipality, ending a 55-year lease to the railway's Pacific and Arctic Railway and Navigation Company. UNVERIFIED (snippet, skagwaynews.com).
- Rockslides: June 23 and Aug 3/5, 2022, rockslides hit the Railroad Dock (normally 4 ships at once, reduced to 3); about 100,000 passengers lost for the rest of 2022; a tour operator laid off 4 drivers; a gallery owner's sales fell over one-third; assembly member: the downturn hits "the sales tax money that runs our government here." SOURCED: Stremple, Alaska Public Media, 2022-08-22, https://alaskapublic.org/news/2022-08-22/rockslides-are-battering-a-skagway-cruise-ship-dock-and-crushing-the-towns-economy

**Skagway institutions, services, supply, housing**
- School: Skagway School (single K-12 school); enrollment passed 100 (100.85) in fall 2015 for the first time in about seven years, bringing about $280,000 more state funding and three teachers. UNVERIFIED (KHNS/KTOO snippets, 2015).
- Clinic: Dahl Memorial Clinic; pharmacy being opened. UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- Groceries: one store, Fairway Market IGA, about 5,000 sq ft, five aisles; 22 workers in season, 10 in winter. Order Saturday; Centralia, Washington warehouse picks Monday; Alaska Marine Lines loads Tuesday; barge leaves Wednesday; arrives late the following Monday; restocked Tuesday-Wednesday; about 10 days. Bread ships frozen; once a six-week bread shortage; "You get whatever shows up." SOURCED: Munson, Skagway News, 2020-10-10, https://skagwaynews.com/?p=40340
- Housing: seasonal workers arrive with no housing and couch-surf, sleep in cars or tents or crowded unheated bunkrooms ("the Skagway shuffle"); Garden City RV Park served as de facto worker housing until it closed May 2021. SOURCED: Swasey, KHNS via KTOO, 2021-09-02, https://www.ktoo.org/?p=256616 . 2024 reporting: businesses buying residential properties and converting them to housing for temporary workers, displacing long-term residents; assembly formed a housing ad hoc committee. SOURCED: Fund for Investigative Journalism summary of McCluskey's KHNS reporting, 2024-10-02, https://fij.org/grantee-uncovers-causes-of-housing-shortage-in-small-alaskan-cruise-ship-town-prompting-changes . "Roughly 2,000 seasonal workers" figure: UNVERIFIED (snippet only).
- Winter: limited visitor services Oct 1 to Apr 15; "Skagway's population is very small in the winter"; Klondike Highway open year-round; ferries on winter schedule from Haines and Juneau. SOURCED: NPS, https://home.nps.gov/klgo/planyourvisit/wintervisits.htm . Many businesses close and owners leave town in winter. SOURCED: Trends 2008 (via search summary; consistent with read text).

**Haines economy and institutions**
- 2009: leisure and hospitality 20.7% of jobs; local government 14.7%; retail 14.7%; health care 12.0%; construction 7.5% of jobs but 18.2% of wages. Seafood processing mostly July-September. 2009: over 80 resident commercial permit holders made landings, about $5.1 million, over 6.1 million lb. Top employers 2009: Ocean Beauty Seafoods; Haines Borough School District; Haines Borough; Southeast Alaska Regional Health Consortium (SEARHC); Southeast Road Builders; State of Alaska; Alaska Mountain Guides; Chilkat Guides; Cornerstone Home Health; grocery. Wage and salary jobs 882 (2001) to a high of 1,061 (2007). SOURCED: Trends Sept 2010.
- Cruise: 63,400 passengers in 2019 (5% of Alaska total). SOURCED: Trends April 2023.
- Mining: the Palmer Project (zinc, copper, gold, silver, barite), about 14 mi upstream of Klukwan; under exploration nearly two decades; DOWA held the majority until Nov 2024; American Pacific Mining sole owner, then sold 100% to Vizsla Copper in Nov 2025 for $15 million in stock. Chilkat Indian Village leadership opposes it. SOURCED: Ellfeldt, KHNS via Alaska Public Media, 2025-11-17, https://alaskapublic.org/news/alaska-desk/2025-11-17/a-mineral-exploration-project-near-haines-has-changed-hands-again
- Access cut, Dec 2, 2020: 10.26 in of precipitation in two days at Haines airport; Beach Road landslide killed two; "every major road had a significant damage or a landslide"; washed-out roads isolated downtown from the dock where fuel arrived; 40 homes on Lutak Road lost power and water; 50 families evacuated; some isolated three or more days; state disaster Dec 3, federal Feb 17, 2021. SOURCED: UAF ACCAP Extreme Events Library, https://uaf-accap.org/projects/extreme_events_library/2020-southeast-landslides/ (page dated 2026-03-23). "160 landslides": UNVERIFIED (NOAA repository snippet).

### D. How the blocker shaped these things
- Skagway exists because it is the sea end of the shortest passes to the Yukon: gold-rush port, then 80 years as the railway's tidewater terminal, then mine ore terminal, then heritage tourism built on the same route. SOURCED: Trends June 2008.
- The Tlingit trade routes over the passes (Chilkoot "owned" trail; Klukwan's "grease trail" over Chilkat Pass) were controlled monopolies before the gold rush, and the roads and the railway follow them. SOURCED: Chilkoot Trail page; Trends Sept 2010.
- Year-round road to Canada through the mountains means winter cultural life faces inland (ski race on the Canadian side; Whitehorse competitors); summer life faces the cruise ship. SOURCED: Yukon News 2020; Trends.
- Freight comes in by barge from Washington roughly weekly; one grocery store; delays visible on shelves. SOURCED: Skagway News 2020.
- Narrow valley floor plus seasonal labor surge produces a chronic seasonal housing crisis. SOURCED: KTOO 2021; FIJ 2024. (That the cause is the valley's narrowness: NOT stated in the sources read; not asserted.)
- Haines: when slopes failed in 2020, the town split into pieces cut off from each other and from its fuel dock. SOURCED: ACCAP.

### E. Structural features bearing on industry, economy, arts and work-life
- Extreme seasonality: Skagway's jobs triple in summer; two-thirds of the workforce nonresident; retail of gifts and jewelry; town government funded by summer sales tax at up to 5%. A rockslide at one dock directly hits the municipal budget and school funding.
- Haines: older population (median 46.4 in 2009), mixed economy (fish, tourism, health care, local government, construction); recurrent conflict over mining vs. salmon.
- Arts infrastructure in Haines grew from repurposed military buildings (Fort Seward hospital, the arts center on Fort Seward Drive) and a 1950s revival of Tlingit carving and dance; the radio station shares the arts building.
- Fraternal halls (Eagles, Elks, Arctic Brotherhood) are core gathering places and theater venues in Skagway.

## 2. Seyðisfjörður, Iceland

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked.

### A. Snapshot
- Town at "the innermost point" of the fjord of the same name, East Iceland; part of Múlaþing municipality, formed 2020 by merging Fljótsdalshérað, Seyðisfjörður, Borgarfjarðarhreppur and Djúpavogshreppur. Peaks: Bjólfur 1,085 m (west), Strandartindur 1,010 m (east). SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyðisfjörður (merger detail UNVERIFIED, snippet of Múlaþing Wikipedia page).
- Fjord about 17 km long. SOURCED: east.is blog, 2022-01-24, https://www.east.is/en/blog/seydisfjordurs-celebration-of-light
- Population: 676 on 1 Jan 2018 (+26 on the previous year); through most of the 20th century steady at 750-1,000; fell about 15% over the 20 years to 2018; residents 50 and under fell from ~65% (2008) to ~57%; 65 and over rose from 17% to 21%. SOURCED: government working-group report "Seyðisfjarðargöng: Valkostir og áhrif á Austurlandi," June 2019, with KPMG annex, https://www.stjornarradid.is/library/02-Rit--skyrslur-og-skrar/Sey%c3%b0isfjar%c3%b0arg%c3%b6ng_sk%c3%bdrsla.pdf . "Under 700" in 2025: SOURCED, Grapevine 2025-04-08 (below). Average growth about 0.3% a year over the last 10 years: SOURCED, Múlaþing housing plan 2026, https://www.mulathing.is/static/files/aaetlanir/husnaedisaaetlun-2026-mulathing.pdf
- **The blocker:** the only road out climbs Fjarðarheiði, a mountain pass of about 600 m (Wikipedia gives 600 m), to Egilsstaðir, about 27 km by Route 93. SOURCED: Seyðisfjörður Wikipedia.
  - 2010-2017 the pass was impassable for part of a day on average **17 days a year**; 31 days in 2014, at least 7 in 2012; it has occasionally been closed several days in a row, "though that is rare." The road is kept open 06:30-22:00 (07:00-21:30 weekends), so closures outside those hours are extra. SOURCED: 2019 report, KPMG annex, p. "Ófærð á vegum."
  - Traffic 2017: average 601 vehicles/day over Fjarðarheiði; summer 1,083, winter 262, so winter traffic is only 24% of summer (much of summer traffic is the Norröna ferry). 2018: 640/day average, 1,145 summer, 284 winter. Both tourists and locals avoid the pass in winter because of uncertainty about conditions; tourism operators name that uncertainty as a barrier to year-round tourism. SOURCED: 2019 report, Table 3 and KPMG annex.
  - The planned tunnel (Fjarðarheiðargöng): about 13.3-13.4 km; portals at about 130 m above sea level; estimated 33.5 billion ISK at 2.5 billion ISK per km (2019 prices); would shorten the road by about 6 km; expected 1,000-1,500 vehicles/day once in full use. SOURCED: 2019 report Table 4 and section 4/10. The regional association of municipalities (SSA) called it the "priority project" because "the circumstances of residents in Seyðisfjörður in winter are such that they can no longer be accepted" (my translation). SOURCED: same.
  - Status: in 2024 construction was scheduled to begin 2025 (~7 years). In 2025 it was pushed back in the national transport plan and replaced by "Fjarðagöng" (tunnels to Mjóifjörður and Neskaupstaður); Fjarðarheiðargöng now "some time after 2040." SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjar%C3%B0arhei%C3%B0arg%C3%B6ng . The municipality "has protested strongly" against the transport plan; the tunnel is "fully designed." SOURCED: Múlaþing housing plan 2026. (mbl.is Iceland Monitor story of 2025-12-03 on the postponement returned 403; not read.)
- Ferry: "Every week the car ferry MS Norröna of Smyril Line comes to Seyðisfjörður from Hirtshals in Denmark and Tórshavn in the Faroe Islands"; "the only car ferry between Iceland and other countries." SOURCED: Seyðisfjörður Wikipedia. Norröna sails all year, but in winter mostly for freight; it also carries fresh fish exports; serving the ferry is "one of the pillars" of the town's economy. Passengers about 12,000 at 2000, about 28,000 in 2017. SOURCED: 2019 report.

### B. What else exists there
- History: foreign (mainly Danish) merchants founded the trading place mid-19th century; Norwegian herring fishing 1870-1900 drove growth. SOURCED: AECO community guidelines, https://www.aeco.no/guidelines/seydisfjordur-community-guidelines/ . Norwegian fishermen settled from 1848; "world's first modern industrialized whaling station" at Vestdalseyri (1864-1866); telegraph cable landed 1906; SS El Grillo sunk by bombing in WWII, now a dive wreck. SOURCED: Seyðisfjörður Wikipedia.
- Fish processing: Síldarvinnslan announced on 2023-09-16 the closure (end of November) of the former Brimberg groundfish plant, about 30 jobs; reasons: rising costs, cod quota cuts, plant too small for the needed investment; fishmeal production continues; trawler Gullver's crew kept on, landing at other ports. SOURCED: Fiskerforum, https://fiskerforum.com/groundfish-processing-in-seydisfjordur-to-shut-down/ . Gullver (built 1983) taken out of service 2025: UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- Salmon farming dispute: Kaldvík has applied for (Grapevine: license under review) open net-pen salmon farming in the fjord; the group VÁ has led opposition since 2020; "75% of population opposed"; concerns include pens inside the avalanche danger zone, the FARICE telecom cable to Scotland and the ferry lane; the fish factory closure removed 33 jobs while the farm would offer 10-15. SOURCED: Garen, Reykjavík Grapevine, 2025-04-08, https://grapevine.is/mag/interview/2025/04/08/a-disaster-waiting-to-happen/ . Application "in process": SOURCED, Múlaþing housing plan 2026. A 10,000-tonne figure: UNVERIFIED (snippet of MAST/Skipulag documents).
- Service geography: Seyðisfjörður residents get about 38% of their services in town, 32% in Egilsstaðir, 16% in the capital area, 0.8% in Reyðarfjörður; about 21% of residents work outside the town. SOURCED: 2019 report, citing Byggðastofnun's 2018 survey. No university in East Iceland. SOURCED: same.
- LungA School: experimental art folk high school (lýðháskóli) founded 2013, two 12-week terms a year, international students of all ages. SOURCED: 2019 report. Added a new study line in 2023, creating some jobs. SOURCED: Múlaþing housing plan 2026.
- Disaster, December 2020: 569 mm of rain 14-18 December, "the heaviest rainfall measured over a five-day period in Iceland"; largest landslide on 15 December, the most damaging ever to hit an Icelandic urban area; scar up to 20 m high; geological surveys had not shown prehistoric slides there. SOURCED: Icelandic Met Office, 2021-01-08, https://en.vedur.is/about-imo/news/the-landslide-in-seydisfjordur-is-the-largest-landslide-to-have-damaged-an-urban-area-in-iceland . On 18 December the whole town was evacuated; 39 houses damaged; loss over 1 billion ISK. SOURCED: Wikipedia. Residents returned (where homes survived) October 2021: UNVERIFIED (snippet). Afterwards: an incentive fund for businesses; avalanche/landslide barrier works in Bjólfur "well advanced," barriers below Botnar being designed; harbor-area expansion planned. SOURCED: Múlaþing housing plan 2026.

### C. Cultural locations
- **Skaftfell Center for Visual Art**, Austurvegur 42: founded 1998 by friends of Swiss-German artist Dieter Roth (1930-1998), who spent his last decade partly in the town; 1907 timber house, 420 m², three floors, Norwegian-pattern neoclassical; once a goldsmith, restaurant, guesthouse, shops, carpentry; now gallery, art library, residency, print workshop, bistro; state and municipal funding; director Celia Harrison since Jan 2024. SOURCED: https://skaftfell.is/about-us/
- **LungA Art Festival** (2000-2024): "Listahátíð ungs fólks á Austurlandi," youth art festival founded by 15-year-old Björt Sigfinnsdóttir, her mother and friends, because art was hard for local young people to reach; mid-July week of workshops ending in exhibitions and concerts; performers included Pussy Riot, Daði Freyr, Sóley, Ragnar Kjartansson; ended after 2024 because costs were "no longer manageable given the limited funding available." SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LungA_Art_Festival
- **List í Ljósi** ("Art in Light"), mid-February, founded 2016 by Celia Harrison and Sesselja Jónasardóttir; marks the sun's return over the mountains after months of absence; the power company turns off central streetlights; installations on buildings, hillsides and the Blue Church; Flat Earth Film Festival screenings; talks at Herðubreið community center; Eyrarrósin award 2019. SOURCED: east.is blog 2022-01-24.
- **Blue Church** (Seyðisfjarðarkirkja): moved to its present site 1920, damaged by fire 1989. SOURCED: east.is historical sites page, https://east.is/en/communities/visit-mulathing/visit-seydisfjordur/historical-and-cultural-sites-in-seydisfjordur . Summer concert series, Wednesday evenings July to mid-August, jazz, classical, folk; 2026 "Sumarkvartettinn" with singer Björt Sigfinnsdóttir. UNVERIFIED (Lonely Planet and east.is snippets).
- **Rainbow Street** (Norðurgata): first painted on a summer night in 2016 by residents and town employees. SOURCED: https://www.east.is/en/place/rainbow-street
- **Tvísöngur**: concrete sound sculpture by Lukas Kühne, opened 5 Sept 2012, five domes 2-4 m high, each resonating to a tone of the Icelandic five-tone "tvísöngur" harmony tradition. UNVERIFIED (guidetoiceland/skaftfell archive snippets). Its existence and maker: SOURCED, east.is sites page.
- **Technical Museum of East Iceland** (Tækniminjasafn Austurlands): covers about 1880-1960 in the Wathne estate buildings (Wathne House, Jóhann Hansson's machine shop, the shipyard, Angró); most buildings destroyed or badly damaged 18 Dec 2020; the 1894 Wathne house / old telegraph station survived. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia snippet). Museum destroyed in the slide: SOURCED (Wikipedia Seyðisfjörður read). "Ambitious rebuilding" under way: SOURCED, Múlaþing 2026.
- **Fjallkonustígur** trail in the Vestdalur nature reserve; cave where human bones and more than 400 beads and pins from about 940 were found in 2004. SOURCED: east.is sites page.
- Telegraph phone-booth sculpture by Guðjón Ketilsson; Herðubreið community center; Tourist Information Center. SOURCED: AECO guidelines.
- Planned viewing platform on Bjólfur. SOURCED: Múlaþing 2026.

### D. How the blocker shaped these things
- The pass is the reason the region's leaders treat winter isolation as unacceptable and the tunnel as the top priority; services and shopping are pulled toward Egilsstaðir over the pass. SOURCED: 2019 report.
- The international ferry lands here because of the deep fjord; the pass is why it matters so much (ferry traffic is most of the summer road traffic). SOURCED: 2019 report.
- The mountains block winter sun for months; the town built a festival around its return. SOURCED: east.is 2022.
- A youth art festival began because art was "difficult to access" for young people there; it spawned a school. SOURCED: LungA Wikipedia.
- The steep walls behind the town produced the 2020 landslides; recovery funds, barriers and museum rebuilding followed. SOURCED: IMO; Múlaþing 2026.

### E. Structural features
- Small aging population; fish processing shrinking; ferry, tourism, arts and an art school as the other legs; a live dispute over aquaculture as a job source. The arts life is institutional (Skaftfell, LungA School, List í Ljósi, Blue Church concerts) and tied to a few founders and artists, with festivals ending when funding runs short.
- Winter traffic one-quarter of summer: seasonality is set partly by the pass, not only by weather.

## 3. Ísafjörður and the Westfjords, Iceland

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. The key primary source is the Westfjords regional plan's status assessment: "Svæðisskipulag Vestfjarða 2025-2050, Stöðumat - fylgirit," Feb 2026, https://www.vestfirdir.is/static/files/Skipulagsmal/Svaedisskipulag/fylgigagn-svaedisskipulags-vestfjarda_stodumat-februar_2026.pdf (cited below as "Stöðumat 2026"; my translations).

### A. Snapshot
- Ísafjörður: 2,744 people on 1 Jan 2023; largest settlement of the Westfjords and seat of Ísafjarðarbær (also Hnífsdalur, Flateyri, Suðureyri, Þingeyri). Built on a sand/gravel spit ("eyri") in Skutulsfjörður, an arm of Ísafjarðardjúp. Trading charter 1786. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ísafjörður . Ísafjarðarbær "just over 3,800" (Q4 2024). SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026. (4,130 on 1 Oct 2025: UNVERIFIED, snippet.)
- Region: eight municipalities on 1 Jan 2025 (35 at the mid-20th-century peak; 32 in 1986, 12 in 1996); the smallest, Árneshreppur, has 60 people. Population fell almost without break from 1940; since 2016 it has risen 6.4%, driven by in-migration to jobs at the biotech firm Kerecis, fish farming and tourism. Foreign citizens 20.5% of Westfjords residents (Jan 2025), 36.1% in Súðavík. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- **The blocker:** steep basalt fjord walls and high plateaus between every fjord. The Westfjords is "the region with the highest share of mountain roads and/or avalanche slopes inside work and service areas or on links to other regions"; snow line lower than elsewhere in Iceland; winter road service "has not been frequent enough and has stood in the way of the community's development." SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
  - Before 1996, Ísafjörður reached Flateyri and Suðureyri over passes such as Breiðadalsheiði, summit 610 m, "formerly the highest pass in regular use in the Westfjords," "frequently blocked by snow and prone to avalanches." **Vestfjarðagöng** (opened Sept 1996, work from 1991): 9.12 km, Y-shaped, three arms (Breiðadalur 4,150 m; Botnsdalur 2,907 m; Tungudalur 2,103 m); mostly single-lane with passing places and traffic lights; ~700 vehicles/day. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestfjarðagöng
  - **Bolungarvíkurgöng** (opened 25 Sept 2010): 5,426 m; replaced the Óshlíð shore road under "steep and unstable" slopes, "subject to frequent avalanches and rockfalls," "one of Iceland's most dangerous roads"; ~800 vehicles/day. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolungarvíkurgöng . (Vegagerðin English booklet URL returned an HTML page; dead end at source.)
  - **Dýrafjarðargöng** (opened 25 Oct 2020): 5.6 km; ISK 9.2 billion (2016 prices); shortened Route 60 by over 27 km; replaced an unpaved pass "closed most of the year"; links north and south Westfjords. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dýrafjarðargöng . Dynjandisheiði road works and crossing of Gufufjörður/Djúpifjörður expected done 2027. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
  - The 2024 "Vestfjarðalínan" call from the regional infrastructure association asks for five new tunnels plus doubling of the Breiðadalur arm within 10 years. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
  - To Reykjavík: 455 km paved via Steingrímsfjarðarheiði; winter snow clearance seven days a week 11:00-19:00. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of guidetoiceland.is; not opened).
- **Avalanches:** Súðavík, 16 Jan 1995, 14 killed (8 children); Flateyri, 26 Oct 1995, 20 killed; afterwards a large national program of avalanche dams; Flateyri's A-shaped deflecting dams (1998) 600 m long, 15-20 m high. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia/Amusing Planet snippets; pages not opened).
- **Air:** Ísafjörður Airport, 1,400 m runway in the fjord; approaches "generally cannot be straight-in," landing northeast needs "a full 180 degree turn" before touchdown; Icelandair only, to Reykjavík; passengers 51,791 (2008 peak), 33,076 (2016), 14,710 (2020). In 2025 Icelandair said it would end the route; per Wikipedia the government reached a subsidy deal in April 2026 keeping flights to at least 2030. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ísafjörður_Airport . "Just under 30,000" passengers in 2024; domestic flying in a "negative spiral" of falling supply and demand; Icelandair announced in March 2025 it would end Ísafjörður flights in autumn 2026. Bíldudalur about 4,000 passengers (2024); Gjögur about 100, "important as freight flight in winter months." SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.

### B. What else exists there
- **Economy (region):** Westfjords residents are under 2% of Iceland's population but earn over 3% of export income (KPMG). Excluding the public sector, fish processing is the largest industry by hours worked and fishing the largest by income. Tourism has doubled to 9% of wage earners on average over the year. Aquaculture grew fastest: from 25 workers in 2011 to about 250 in 2024; about 6% of wage earners, 8% of income; fish-farm jobs have partly replaced lost jobs in traditional fisheries. Most wage earners work in public services, education, care and administration. Kerecis (fish-skin medical products) created many specialist jobs in Ísafjörður; sold to Coloplast in July 2023 at a valuation of USD 1.3 billion with no change to its Icelandic operations. Also: a lactose-free dairy in Bolungarvík; two salt works using geothermal energy (Reykjanes and Reykhólar); seaweed processing. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- Hraðfrystihúsið-Gunnvör (HG), founded 1969, based in Hnífsdalur, "the largest fisheries company in the Westfjords," about 250 employees. UNVERIFIED (westfjords.is and CB Insights snippets).
- **Services:** Ísafjörður and Bolungarvík have the most services, trade and administration; Ísafjörður, Patreksfjörður and Hólmavík host police, district commissioner and road administration offices. Only Ísafjörður has discount grocery chains (Bónus, Krónan, Nettó). Three commuting zones (Strandir/Reykhólar; south; north). Old parts of Ísafjörður are designated protected built areas. Limited flat land has fixed the towns' shapes and growth limits; Ísafjörður is the clearest case of a dense harbor core with linear growth along the shore. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026. Hospital and junior college exist in Ísafjörður: SOURCED as "a modern hospital" (Ísafjörður Wikipedia); details NOT FOUND.
- **University Center of the Westfjords** (Háskólasetur Vestfjarða), founded 12 March 2005; master's programs in Coastal and Marine Management, Marine Innovation and Regional Development with the University of Akureyri; Icelandic courses for foreigners; UArctic member. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Centre_of_the_Westfjords . About 20 students admitted per program per year: UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Cruise:** Ísafjörður 184 ship visits and 143,119 passengers in 2023, Iceland's third-largest cruise port; eight Westfjords ports take cruise ships (Ísafjörður, Patreksfjörður, Suðureyri, Flateyri, Þingeyri, Bolungarvík, Hólmavík, Djúpavík). SOURCED: https://www.westfjords.is/en/travel-trade/cruise-ships-travel-trade . Some places now limit how many cruise ships can berth at once "to reduce the load on the harbor communities." SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026. A 5,000-a-day passenger limit and a pier extension to 500 m (ISK 1 billion): UNVERIFIED (cruisemapper/nauticalflock snippets; mbl.is 403).

### C. Cultural locations
- **Aldrei fór ég suður** ("I never went south," after a Bubbi Morthens song): Easter-weekend music festival since 2004, founded by Mugison and his father Guðmundur Kristjánsson; never an entry fee, bands unpaid; broadcast on Rás 2/RÚV; acts have included Sigur Rós, Of Monsters and Men, Hatari. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldrei_f%C3%B3r_%C3%A9g_su%C3%B0ur . "The best-known festival in the Westfjords"; draws tourists, musicians and "Westfjorders who have moved away"; called the "wish-child" of Ísafjörður people; music event of the year at the Icelandic Music Awards 2019; Eyrarrósin 2008. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- **Ski Week (Skíðavikan)** at Easter and **Fossavatnsgangan**: Iceland's oldest ski race still held, first run 1935 with 7 skiers (~18-20 km from near Fossavatn to Seljalandsdalur); now 1 km to 50 km (50 km since 2004) plus night races; FIS listed 2005; Worldloppet since 2014; held around the April-May turn. SOURCED: https://fossavatn.is/en/um/ (Ski Week link: UNVERIFIED, snippet).
- **Westfjords Heritage Museum** (Byggðasafn Vestfjarða), founded July 1941; since Fishermen's Day 1988 in Neðstikaupstaður, Iceland's oldest cluster of houses: Krambúðin 1757 (store), Faktorshúsið 1765 (manager's house), Tjöruhúsið 1781 (storehouse, now a seafood restaurant), Turnhúsið 1784 (museum); about 190 accordions (oldest 1830); about 15 restored boats; 12,000 visitors summer 2013. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westfjords_Heritage_Museum . One of three Westfjords museums with formal Museum Council recognition. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- **Music schools:** Tónlistarskóli Ísafjarðar founded 1948 by the Ísafjörður Music Society in the home of Jónas Tómasson, run in Ísafjörður, Flateyri, Suðureyri and Þingeyri; Listaskóli Rögnvaldar Ólafssonar (1992), tied to **Edinborgarhúsið**, a music and performing-arts house; Tónlistarskóli Bolungarvíkur (1964). Theater: Litli leikklúbburinn (Ísafjörður), Leikfélag Flateyrar, Leikfélag Hólmavíkur; **Kómedíuleikhúsið** (1997), the only professional theater in the Westfjords, based in Dýrafjörður, specializing in one-person plays. SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- **Act Alone** solo-performance festival: founded 2004 by actor Elfar Logi Hannesson; moved from Ísafjörður to Suðureyri (~270 people) in 2012; main venue the communal hall (under 200 seats); free to audiences; 4 events in 2004 to almost 20 in 2017; just over 3,000 visitors in 2017. SOURCED: Jónsdóttir, The Theater Times, 2017-09-10, https://thetheatretimes.com/politics-unaided-act-alone-solo-performance-festival-sudureyri-iceland/
- **Region-wide:** over 30 museums and exhibitions; museum visitors just over 100,000 in 2019; odd centers: Sea Monster Museum (Bíldudalur), Museum of Sorcery and Witchcraft (Strandir), Samúel Jónsson's art museum (Selárdalur), a new Basque center (Djúpavík), Arctic Fox Center (Súðavík, exhibit plus research); Skjaldborg documentary film festival (Patreksfjörður). SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- Weaknesses named in the regional cultural plan: little cooperation, "few artists work at their art all year," young artists don't return after study, culture rests "on few shoulders." SOURCED: Stöðumat 2026.
- Swamp soccer (Mýrarboltinn): NOT confirmed (dead end at query).

### D. How the blocker shaped these things
- Tunnels are the decisive factor in ties within the region (Stöðumat 2026); before them, villages a few km apart were separated by avalanche-prone passes; each tunnel retired a deadly road (Óshlíð) or a seasonal pass.
- Narrow strips of flat land on gravel spits fixed town shapes and growth limits (Stöðumat 2026).
- The 1995 avalanches led to a national dam-building program (UNVERIFIED as above).
- A festival named "I never went south" (i.e., never left for Reykjavík) is the region's flagship and a homecoming for those who did leave (Wikipedia; Stöðumat 2026).
- Air links are fragile: a hard approach, falling passengers, and in 2025-26 the threat of losing scheduled flights, met by state subsidy (Ísafjörður Airport Wikipedia; Stöðumat 2026).
- Music schools founded early (1948, 1964) and run in every village form the base of music life (Stöðumat 2026).

### E. Structural features
- Decline for most of a century, then a turn since 2016 on new industries (aquaculture, biotech, cruise tourism) and many immigrant workers (one in five residents a foreign citizen).
- Fisheries still the private backbone; public sector the largest employer group.
- Free festivals with unpaid or modestly paid artists (Aldrei fór ég suður, Act Alone) as a model; volunteer-run sports (ski club, ski marathon since 1935).
- Seasonality: cruise season crowding, with berth limits; tourism operators want more winter road service to make tourism year-round.

## 4. Baracoa, Cuba

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Source note: Cuban state media and independent/exile media (CubaNet) report on the same events from opposed positions; where possible I cite the state or UN/NGO source for figures.

### A. Snapshot
- Municipality in Guantánamo Province at Cuba's eastern end; 78,056 people (2022) on 977 km². Founded 15 August 1511 by Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar as Nuestra Señora de la Asunción de Baracoa, Cuba's first capital; Columbus visited 27 Nov 1492. On the Bay of Honey (Bahía de Miel); Sierra del Purial behind; El Yunque, a table mountain, 575 m; Salto Fino waterfall 305 m. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baracoa . Town (urban) population: NOT FOUND.
- **The blocker:** the Sagua-Baracoa mountain massif (Nipe-Sagua-Baracoa range). Before the mid-1960s "Baracoa was only accessible via a long, still unsurfaced route to Holguín and by sea." **La Farola** (the mountain section of the Guantánamo-Baracoa road) was built in 20 months, April 1964 to December 1965; it "made it possible to reach Baracoa ... easily by land from Guantánamo for the first time in its history." Today about 96% of people traveling to or from Baracoa use it, and 83% of goods traffic to Baracoa goes by road. SOURCED: Cubaheute (citing the provincial paper Venceremos), "After 59 years: first major renovation for Cuba's most important mountain pass," c. late 2024/2025, https://www.dac27.ch/CubaHeuteEng/After%2059%20years_%20first%20major%20renovation%20for%20Cuba's%20most%20important%20mountain%20pass.pdf
  - Length figures conflict: the whole Guantánamo-Baracoa "Vía Azul" is 154 km; the mountain section crosses the Sagua-Baracoa massif south to north "in a zigzag of 30 kilometers" from Las Guásimas. SOURCED: EcuRed, https://www.ecured.cu/La_Farola . Richès Karayib gives 36 km and says it was named one of the seven wonders of Cuban civil engineering in 1997. SOURCED (minor site): https://richeskarayib.com/baracoa-1511-1965-454-years-of-isolation-farola/ . Wikipedia gives "120 km" with "11 bridges" and heights over 600 m; a search summary gave heights "sometimes reaching 450 m." Maximum elevation and bridge count: UNRESOLVED.
  - The 1964-65 road and the Moa route: when La Farola was damaged after Hurricane Oscar (Oct 2024), a World Food Program convoy of five trucks reached Baracoa by "the alternative route between Baracoa and Moa." SOURCED: OnCuba, 2024-10-30 (below).
- Air: Gustavo Rizo airport near town; runway about 1,850 m, unlit (daytime only); two flights a week from Havana (Thursdays, Sundays); plans for international flights. UNVERIFIED (search snippets of Wikipedia and Directorio Cubano; not opened).

### B. What else exists there
- **Economy:** bananas, coconuts and cacao; "Cuba's main chocolate manufacturing area." SOURCED: Wikipedia. The cacao plant ("David Suárez Abella"/"Rubén David Suárez" cocoa derivatives enterprise) was opened by Ernesto Che Guevara, then Minister of Industries, on 1 April 1963, capacity to process 40,000 quintals of cacao; described as the only cocoa processor in Cuba. SOURCED: Rodríguez Milán, OnCuba, 2012-10-23, https://oncubanews.com/en/chronicles/baracoa . 2011: nearly 200 workers, capacity 2,000 t/yr with 80% original equipment; director complained of theft and black-market demand. Coconut-oil plant (German equipment, 30+ years old): 26 workers; 200 t of oil in 2010 vs a 1990 peak of 1,450 t; boiler and raw-material transport problems. SOURCED: González, Havana Times, 2011-04-25, https://havanatimes.org/?p=42373 . French migrants from Haiti influenced cacao growing (date given as 1668 in the article; I could not check it). Baracoa produces "85%" of Cuba's cacao: UNVERIFIED (search snippet).
- **Hurricane Matthew (Oct 2016):** in the municipalities of Yateras, San Antonio del Sur, Baracoa, Maisí and Imías, more than 176,000 people "were isolated due to the collapse of bridges, falling trees, rivers and water walls, coastal flooding and landslides"; over 95% of houses totally or partially destroyed; over 74,000 people unable to return home; almost 200 schools affected in Maisí and Baracoa with over 15,300 children out of school; "Coffee, coconut, banana and cocoa plantations, the main economic sources of livelihood in the area, were destroyed." SOURCED: ACT Alliance appeal CUB161, Nov 2016, https://actalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Appeals_11_2016_Hurricane_Matthew_Response_in_Cuba_-CUB_161.pdf . "90% of homes in Baracoa damaged": UNVERIFIED (ReliefWeb snippet; page 403).
- **Hurricane Oscar (Oct 2024):** "catastrophic flooding"; La Farola had landslides, rockfalls, blocked drains and undercut road edges; heavy vehicles restricted; communities cut off; at least seven dead and over 10,500 houses damaged (province); plantain, vegetables, coffee and cacao damaged. SOURCED: OnCuba, 2024-10-30, https://oncubanews.com/cuba/lluvias-y-deslizamientos-siguen-afectando-zonas-azotadas-por-oscar-en-guantanamo . Then the first major repair of La Farola in 59 years: 40 trucks and bulldozers needed but largely idle for lack of tires, batteries and lubricants; vehicle weight to be limited permanently; a dedicated maintenance brigade to follow. SOURCED: Cubaheute PDF above.
- Alejandro de Humboldt National Park (Holguín/Guantánamo provinces, ~710 km², UNESCO World Heritage, high endemism), home of Polymita painted snails. UNVERIFIED (national-parks.org snippet; UNESCO page 403).

### C. Cultural locations
- **Catedral de Nuestra Señora de la Asunción**, which holds the **Cruz de la Parra**, a wooden cross traditionally said to have been planted by Columbus (Las Casas records Columbus placing "a large cross at the entrance of that port" on 1 Dec 1492); considered the only survivor of 29 Columbus crosses; offered to Pope John Paul II on his 1998 visit, who declined to take it. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruz_de_la_Parra . Carbon dating to the late 1400s and native Cuban wood: UNVERIFIED (snippet; not in the read text).
- **Forts:** Matachín (now the Municipal Museum: history, culture, traditions, natural heritage; listed as "currently not available") and La Punta. SOURCED: Wikipedia; https://www.cuba.travel/en/destinations/baracoa-guantanamo/what-to-do/cultural-spaces
- **Cultural spaces** (official tourism site): Ranchón del Nengón y Kiribá (bearer group of the local genre; plays, sings, dances; dining); Casa de la Trova Victorino Rodríguez (trova: loud singing with solo guitars, serenades, duets, trios); Casa de Cultura Cecilio Gómez Lambert (music, dance, literature, theater, visual arts); Galería de Arte Eliseo Osorio; Centro Cultural El Yunque (ARTEX open-air terrace; "currently not available"). - **Music:** nengón and kiribá, peasant-festivity genres of the Baracoa area regarded as ancestors of changüí and son; call-and-response between soloist and chorus; the community of El Güirito keeps them alive against modern music and youth migration. UNVERIFIED (EcuRed page returned 502; Villa Paradiso/salsablanca snippets).
- **Cueva del Paraíso**, an archaeological museum in caves above town with displays, rock paintings and a burial site; Taíno statue overlooking town. UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **April 1 / Duaba:** on 1 April 1895 Antonio Maceo and about 20 others landed at Duaba on the schooner Honor to restart the independence war; "since 1903 this civic march has been a Baracoa tradition, considered one of the oldest in Cuba"; in 2025 the president led the march from Baracoa to Duaba. SOURCED: Prensa Latina, 2025-04-01, https://www.prensa-latina.cu/?p=1545027 . Nightly street parties that week: UNVERIFIED (Lonely Planet-type snippet).
- **Food:** cucurucho (grated coconut cooked with sugar, sometimes with papaya, guava or orange, in a cone of palm leaf); bacán (grated green banana with sauce, sometimes crab, boiled in banana leaf); guanimo (unstuffed bacán); frangollo (dried ground banana with sugar, pressed in squares in banana leaf); tetí (a ~2 cm transparent fish caught at river mouths on the waning moon, July-January); calalú; lechita (coconut-milk sauce for fish); chorote (homemade chocolate drink from cacao balls). Also "riding oxen" listed among customs. SOURCED (enthusiast site run by Nigel Hunt): https://www.baracoa.org/customs.html

### D. How the blocker shaped these things
- 454 years with access by sea and a long unpaved northern route; the source asserts that "relative isolation helped ... strengthen the role of cacao and coconut in the local economy," still visible in recipes and landscapes. SOURCED (minor site, claim, not proven): Richès Karayib.

- The 1963 cacao factory sits at the source of the beans rather than at a central port. SOURCED (fact of location): OnCuba 2012. (Why it was placed there: NOT FOUND.)

### E. Structural features
- An agricultural-processing economy (cacao, coconut, coffee, bananas) in a state system, with chronic equipment, spare-parts and transport shortages that show up in output (coconut oil 1,450 t to 200 t).
- Repeated hurricane destruction of homes, crops and the one road.
- A strong civic calendar (April 1 march since 1903), state cultural institutions (Casa de la Trova, Casa de Cultura, galleries, museum in a fort), and a distinct food culture built from the three main crops.

## 5. Juneau, Alaska

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Main data source: Juneau Economic Development Council, "Juneau by the Numbers" (2024, 2023 data), https://jedc.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Choose-Juneau-Juneau-by-Numbers.pdf (cited "JEDC 2024").

### A. Snapshot
- City and Borough of Juneau: 32,255 (2020), 31,609 (2025 estimate); 3,254.70 sq mi, "the second-largest municipality in the United States by area"; on Gastineau Channel at the foot of Mount Juneau, Douglas Island opposite; "one of only two U.S. state capitals without roads connecting it to the rest of the state" (the other Honolulu); "a de facto island city ... all goods coming in and out must be transported by plane or boat." Gold found 1880 by Joe Juneau and Richard Harris. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juneau,_Alaska . 2023 population 31,549; median age 39.9; about 900 air miles from Seattle, 600 from Anchorage. Juneau "resides on the unceded territory of the Áak'w Ḵwáan on Lingít Aaní." SOURCED: JEDC 2024.
- **The blocker:** the Juneau Icefield, 3,900 km² (1,500 sq mi) across Alaska and British Columbia, 140 km north-south by 75 km east-west; source of the Mendenhall and Taku glaciers; "over 40 large valley glaciers and 100 smaller ones"; the Juneau Icefield Research Program has monitored it since 1948. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juneau_Icefield . Behind it the Coast Mountains; in front, open water.
- **The road that was not built:** the Juneau Access / Lynn Canal Highway, a proposed 47.9-mile road north toward Skagway at an estimated $574 million, which would still have needed a ferry link (to a new terminal at the Katzehin River mouth); cancelled by Governor Bill Walker in December 2016 citing the state fiscal crisis; formally ended by the federal government later after almost 25 years of work. UNVERIFIED in detail (Wikipedia/Juneau Empire snippets; not opened).

SOURCED: Moore, "Connecting the Region," SE Conference 2024, https://archive.seconference.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Connecting-the-Region-Kelly-Moore-AML.pdf . ## 6. Cordova, Alaska

### A. Snapshot
- Port city "near the mouth of the Copper River, at the head of Orca Inlet on the east side of Prince William Sound"; population 2,609 (2020), 2,239 (2010); "no roads connect Cordova to any other Alaskan communities"; reached by ferry or plane. Organized 26 March 1906 by Michael James Heney as the terminus of the Copper River & Northwestern Railway; 1911-1938 more than 200 million tons of copper ore (Kennecott) moved through it. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordova,_Alaska . (The "200 million tons" figure is as Wikipedia states it; not checked against a primary source.)
- **The blocker:** the Chugach Mountains behind and the glacier-fed Copper River delta to the east. The **Copper River Highway** (begun 1945) runs 49.5 mi from the ferry terminal along the old railbed toward the Million Dollar Bridge at Miles Glacier, intended to reach the state highway system at Chitina; the 1964 earthquake halted construction and dropped the bridge's north span (permanent repair 2005); first 12 mi paved, rest gravel; "the highway past mile 36 has been de facto abandoned since 2011 due to erosion of the approach to Bridge 339." SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_River_Highway . 2011 scour of about 50 ft from shifting delta channels; since then Childs Glacier reachable only by guided charter; some tourism businesses reported losses of 30% or more. UNVERIFIED (Alaska DOT white paper/snippets; ADN 2013 article returned 404).
- Air: Alaska Airlines jet service to Merle K. "Mudhole" Smith Airport, 11 mi out on the highway. Ferry: state ferry to Valdez and Whittier. SOURCED: Cordova Wikipedia; Copper River Highway Wikipedia.

### B. What else exists there
- **Fishing town:** "Half of all households in Cordova have at least one person involved in commercial fishing or processing"; salmon is "the most economically important" fishery. SOURCED: Cordova Wikipedia. Cordova brings in about 90% of the Valdez-Cordova Census Area's gross commercial seafood earnings, mainly salmon, over $38.3 million (2015); 373 permits fished in 2015, which if counted as jobs would be about 18% of the city's July jobs; processing pays less, so the average wage ($46,382) was below the state's ($54,191). 2010-2014 estimates: unemployment 4.4%, poverty 2.4%, median household income $93,750; 8.4% Asian (processing workforce not stated as the reason). SOURCED: Rodrigues, "Vast census area covers towns with broad range of identities," Alaska Economic Trends, Aug 2016, https://laborstats.alaska.gov/trends/aug16art3.pdf

- **Fresh-fish air freight:** season-opener jets carry Copper River salmon to Seattle (e.g., up to 160,000 lb in a day, 2007). SOURCED: Alaska Airlines press release, 2007-05-11, https://news.alaskaair.com/newsroom/alaska-airlines-to-fly-up-to-160000-pounds-of-copper-river-salmon-to-seattle-for-season-opener . - Strong local organizing (blockade, festivals, tribal cultural programs, a science center born of the spill era).

## 7. Caleta Tortel, Chile

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Key primary source: Consejo de Monumentos Nacionales (Chile's National Monuments Council), "Apuntes y actividades sobre monumentos nacionales: Caleta Tortel" (educational notes; file dated 2024-12; text by Claudia Silva Guzmán), https://www.monumentos.gob.cl/sites/default/files/2024-12/Caleta%20Tortel_Apuntes_0.pdf (cited "CMN"; my translations).

### A. Snapshot
- Comuna de Tortel, Capitán Prat Province, Aysén Region; commune 20,390 km² (CMN) or 19,710.6 km² (Spanish Wikipedia); **523 inhabitants (2017 census)**; between the Northern and Southern Patagonian Ice Fields at the mouth of the Baker River, "the most voluminous in Chile"; temperate rainy climate, about 1,849 mm of rain a year; vegetation includes ciprés de las Guaitecas, coigüe, mañío, canelo, tepú. SOURCED: CMN. Over 80% of the commune is protected area (national parks/reserves). SOURCED: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caleta_Tortel ; 81% per UN Tourism, https://tourism-villages.unwto.org/en/?p=16161
- **The blocker:** islands, channels and estuaries on one side and "steep mountain ranges" on the other, with the two ice fields north and south. Until 2003 there was no road; "In 2003 a new section of the Carretera Austral was inaugurated, leaving Caleta Tortel at last joined by land to the rest of the country." SOURCED: CMN. Even now there are no streets: more than 6 km (CMN) / "more than eight kilometers" (newer press) / "5 miles" (Ruta de los Parques) of walkways, bridges and stairs of ciprés de las Guaitecas connect landings, houses, shops and public services; the length figure varies by source and date. Cars stay at a parking lot built in the newer, upper zone (approved by the Monuments Council so as not to alter the landscape). SOURCED: CMN.
- Distances: 78 mi (~125 km) from Cochrane; 287 mi south of Coyhaique; Puerto Yungay 27 mi further on the Carretera Austral. Bus some weekdays; small aerodrome; Entel the only mobile network; Caja Vecina for banking. SOURCED: Ruta de los Parques, https://www.rutadelosparques.org/en/tortel/ and https://www.rutadelosparques.org/en/puerto-yungay/ . From Puerto Yungay: the Padre Antonio Ronchi barge across to Río Bravo (route on to Villa O'Higgins) and the TABSA ferry (Crux Australis) to Puerto Natales, about 41 hours, stopping at Puerto Edén. SOURCED: Ruta de los Parques Puerto Yungay page (ship name and capacity ~150 passengers/~15 cars: UNVERIFIED, snippet).

### B. What else exists there
- **History (CMN):** Kawésqar canoe people in pre-Hispanic times. 1888 Chilean Navy survey of the Baker fjord. 1901 concession to Juan Tornero to bring 1,000 European settler families, leading in 1903 to the Compañía Explotadora del Baker, a timber company based at Puerto Bajo Pisagua; late 1905 over 200 workers from Chiloé arrived; in 1906, 120 Chilote workers died, probably of hunger and scurvy, and were buried on the **Isla de los Muertos** (3 km NW; Monumento Histórico 2001; 33 crosses visible); work stopped and the company went bankrupt. 1940-44 much of the territory burned and Tortel was nearly empty. 1954 settlers asked the Navy for help; 1955 a Navy lookout and signal post was created (a radio-telegraphist/nurse and his wife), and settlers built "El Galpón Rosado" and the first house. 1966 the Empresa de Comercio Agrícola set up to buy cypress posts (estacones) and sell food, consolidating the settlement. 1974 Tortel became a commune; 1978 the Navy built a school; 1980 the Public Works Ministry built an aerodrome; 1981 the municipality building. From the 1980s: municipal buildings, a library, police post (retén de Carabineros), radio, social housing, a new school and health post (posta) outside the historic center, a drinking-water network and a hydroelectric plant. SOURCED: CMN. (Wikipedia gives the official founding date as 28 May 1955, celebrated as the town anniversary. SOURCED: Wikipedia; Ruta de los Parques.)
- After the 2003 road: population and infrastructure grew, producing two zones: the old one (older houses, school, library, churches, stores, lighthouse, covered plazas) and a newer one (gym, a second school, housing estates, the parking lot). SOURCED: CMN.
- **Economy:** tourism, cypress timber extraction, fishing. SOURCED: Spanish Wikipedia. Community tourism: rowboat trips on the Baker with pioneer families; cypress raft-building demonstrations; beekeeping; carpentry and shingle (tejuela) workshops; a PRODEMU rural-women program; women 69.2% of the formal tourism workforce. SOURCED: UN Tourism page. Shingle-making expertise and teaching workshops: SOURCED, Illesca Cabrera, "Un paseo por la arquitectura de Caleta Tortel," *Somos Cultura Aysén* (Museo Regional de Aysén magazine), c. 2025, https://museoregionalaysen.gob.cl/sites/www.museoregionalaysen.gob.cl/files/2025-01/SCA%202.pdf
- **Housing:** most houses are simple wooden gable-roofed structures facing the sea, of cypress, raulí or pine, "no more than 25 m²"; newer ones have zinc roofs, few have shingles. SOURCED: CMN.
- Services (Ruta de los Parques, 509 residents): rural health service, police, tourism office, auto repair. SOURCED.
- Ciprés de las Guaitecas (*Pilgerodendron uviferum*): "the southernmost conifer in the world," up to 20 m; its wood is rot-proof and "can last more than a hundred years under water"; protected under Law 20.283 on native forest. SOURCED: CMN.
- Prince William of the UK spent about 10 weeks volunteering in the commune in 2000. SOURCED: CMN; Wikipedia.

### C. Cultural locations
- **The walkway network itself** and the **Zona Típica** (2001): an 80 m strip of the cove, the landings, walkways, houses and hillside vegetation, including the peninsula by the Baker delta; any intervention there needs Monuments Council approval. SOURCED: CMN. Origin: the Chilote system of axe-cut wooden paths (envaralados, log roads over bog) was replaced by more durable cypress walkways on minimal supports over the vegetation. SOURCED: CMN.
- **Isla de los Muertos** (Historic Monument 2001), 33 crosses. SOURCED: CMN.
- **El Galpón Rosado** (the first community building, 1955). SOURCED: CMN (current use NOT FOUND).
- **Festivals:** Timber Festival (Fiesta de la Madera), first week of February: local artists, artisans and timber workers, ending in a community dance. SOURCED: UN Tourism. Second week of February: annual traditional festival and a festival of the "Baker song." SOURCED: Ruta de los Parques. Anniversary 28 May. SOURCED: Ruta de los Parques.
- **Song and poetry:** Nicasio Luna's verse "Señores yo soy del Baker, / esa geografía tan dura / mezcla de adversidad y belleza ... donde muchos perdieron la vida / sin tener una sepultura" opens the regional museum magazine's piece on Tortel; the author sums up life there as "paisaje hermoso, vida dura" (beautiful landscape, hard life) and says the low connection to the rest of the country made residents build with what was at hand. SOURCED: Illesca Cabrera, *Somos Cultura Aysén*.
- **Cultural governance:** a "cultural consortium of musicians, artists, and artisans" and community artistic committees. SOURCED: UN Tourism.
- Old-zone institutions: library, churches, lighthouse, covered plazas (plazas techadas, i.e., roofed gathering spaces for the rain). SOURCED: CMN.

### D. How the blocker shaped these things
- No road until 2003 and boggy, rocky ground produced a town of walkways rather than streets; cars still stop at the edge. SOURCED: CMN.
- The town grew around buying and shipping cypress posts by water, then around state outposts (Navy, school, aerodrome) because no road reached it. SOURCED: CMN.
- The 1906 deaths of over 100 imported timber workers, far from supply, are a monument and a theme of local song. SOURCED: CMN; Somos Cultura Aysén.
- The road's arrival split the town into an old heritage zone and a new zone and brought tourism, now its main stated economy. SOURCED: CMN; UN Tourism.
- Roofed plazas and tiny houses respond to ~1,849 mm of rain a year. (Plazas techadas named in CMN; the causal link is my inference, marked as such.)

### E. Structural features
- Tiny public-sector-anchored town (municipality, school, health post, police) plus family-scale tourism and woodcraft; women dominate the formal tourism workforce.
- Heritage regulation (Zona Típica) shapes every building decision in the old zone.
- Crafts as economy and teaching (shingles, rafts, carpentry workshops).

## 8. Ilulissat, Nuuk and Tasiilaq, Greenland

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Key primary sources: Statistics Greenland, *Greenland in Figures 2025*, https://stat.gl/publ/en/GF/2025/pdf/Greenland%20in%20Figures%202025.pdf (cited "GiF 2025"); and two Government of Greenland answers to parliament (Inatsisartut § 37 questions) on East Greenland supply: 2020-092 (26 June 2020), https://ina.gl/media/xrme5rai/092_2020_forsyningsskibe_jnapp_svar.pdf , and 2025-169 (28 Oct 2025), https://www.ina.gl/media/rq1jqvoh/169_2025_fly_og_transportforbindelserne_lapou_svar.pdf (cited "INA 2020" and "INA 2025"; my translations from Danish).

### A. Snapshot (country-level blocker)
- Greenland: 2.2 million km², 81% ice-covered, population "just about 57,000"; "All towns and settlements are located along the coast line. No roads exist between towns, and travel is by sea or air only." Over 65% live in the five largest towns. Colony from 1721, Danish county 1953, self-government 21 June 2009; left the EU 1985. Danish krone is legal tender. SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- Populations (GiF 2025, 2025 figures): **Nuuk 19,905; Ilulissat 5,087; Tasiilaq 1,758**. (Wikipedia gives Nuuk 20,113 Jan 2025, Ilulissat 5,149, Tasiilaq 1,830; differences reflect date/definition.)
- "All passenger and freight transport takes place by ship, plane, or helicopter. Most towns have paved roads and cars ... Royal Arctic Line has a monopoly on freight routes to towns and settlements"; Arctic Umiaq Line's *Sarfaq Ittuk* runs West Coast passenger routes most of the year; "transportation to the northernmost regions of North Greenland and East Greenland is impossible in winter due to sea ice." Royal Arctic Line: four larger Atlantic vessels and six smaller settlement vessels. Registered vehicles 2024: Nuuk 4,760 cars/vans, 360 snowmobiles, 39 buses, 62 taxis; Ilulissat 743 cars, 382 snowmobiles; Tasiilaq 96 cars, 197 snowmobiles. SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- Ice-sheet crossings by travelers "typically between Kangerlussuaq and Tasiilaq ... require special permission from the Self-Government." SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- Economy: over 40% of jobs are public; more than 60% of employed women work in the public sector; average monthly employment 2023: 29,339, of which fishing/hunting/agriculture 4,600; "a large seasonal variation in employment, due to the climate and the geographic dispersion, which limits mobility." Fishing is "Greenland's single most important trade"; land-based processing dominated by state-owned Royal Greenland (the largest company) and private Polar Seafood. Danish block grant 4,141.5 million DKK (2023, preliminary). Hunting license mandatory; professional hunters "usually do not make a full living from hunting." Sled dogs may not be moved between the North/East and West/South dog regions without a permit, to keep the breed pure. Five hydropower plants since the 1990s supply Nuuk, Qaqortoq/Narsaq, Sisimiut, Ilulissat and Tasiilaq. Regional hospitals in Nuuk, Qaqortoq, Sisimiut, Ilulissat and Aasiaat. Prisons in Ilulissat, Aasiaat, Nuuk, Qaqortoq and Tasiilaq. SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- Culture (national): "Most towns have their own museum"; "Most towns have their own choir, and folk dancing is popular"; traditional dances "of European origin, introduced by whalers in the 17th century and early colonists in the 18th century, while drum dancing and singing represent the original Inuit musical tradition"; "a relatively large number of musicians, primarily in the rock and pop genres." KNR is the public broadcaster; two national weeklies, *Atuagagdliutit* and *Sermitsiaq*. SOURCED: GiF 2025.

### B/C/D/E by town

#### Nuuk
- **A.** At the mouth of Nuup Kangerlua fjord; Sermitsiaq (1,210 m) visible from almost everywhere; "no roads connect Nuuk with other parts of Greenland"; founded 1728 by missionary Hans Egede and governor Claus Paarss as Godthaab; renamed Nuuk 1979. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuuk . New international airport opened **28 November 2024** with a 2,200 m runway. UNVERIFIED (Copernicus/EU Space snippet; consistent with GiF 2025's "opening of Nuuk's international airport in 2024"). International passengers at Nuuk: 4,720 (2016) to 11,306 (2024). SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- **B.** Public sector is the largest employer; Royal Greenland plant (>DKK 50 million a year, 80% shrimp, also cod, lumpfish, halibut). Inatsisartut (31 seats); University of Greenland (Ilisimatusarfik, founded 1987); Queen Ingrid's Hospital (central hospital). City: 80 km of local roads (2017), "three traffic lights, 12 roundabouts, and one road tunnel" (the only ones in Greenland); Nuup Bussii since 1980; Buksefjord hydropower since 1993, line across Ameralik fjord with a 5,376 m span; Nuuk Center (2012), Greenland's first shopping center. SOURCED: Nuuk Wikipedia. University offers 12 bachelor and 4 master programs; about 30% of Greenland's students study abroad. SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- Housing: in 2002, about 2,000 people were on Nuuk's waiting list (4,250 Greenland-wide; Tasiilaq about 100), some waiting over 10 years. SOURCED (old): Nunatsiaq News, 2002-01-25, https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/housing_crisis_growing_in_greenland/ . - **C.** **Kalaaliaraq** ("the little Greenlander," Danish *Brædtet*), Old Nuuk, ~150 m from the cathedral: Greenland's largest fresh food market; hunters and fishers sell fish, whale, seal, reindeer, musk ox directly; dried/salted meat allowed since 2018; a social meeting place "comparable to a village pump"; a rare polar bear sale becomes a public event; recorded hygiene issues (2007; 2016 trichinella from polar bear meat). SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalaaliaraq_Market
  - **Katuaq** cultural center (Schmidt Hammer Lassen, opened 15 Feb 1997): film, concerts, theater, exhibitions, conferences; partners from Greenland, Canada, Alaska and the Nordic countries; Wikipedia lists two auditoria (1,008 and 508 seats as stated there), art school, library, café; façade inspired by the northern lights (snippet).   - **Nunatta Isiginnaartitsisarfia**, the National Theater (est. 2011), stage art based on Greenlandic culture and also the country's acting school; 3-5 productions a year (snippet). SOURCED: GiF 2025.

- **D/E.** The capital concentrates a third of the population, the public jobs, the higher education, the national cultural institutions and the only real road grid; freight is a Royal Arctic Line monopoly; the market is where hunting economy meets city.

#### Ilulissat
- **A.** Seat of Avannaata municipality, Greenland's third-largest town; trading post founded 1741 by Jacob Severin's company. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilulissat . 250 km north of the Arctic Circle; no sun 26 Nov to 13 Jan; midnight sun 21 May to 24 July. SOURCED: AECO Ilulissat community guidelines (2023, with Avannaata municipality), https://aeco.no/guideline/community-and-research-station/ilulissat-community-guidelines . Ilulissat Icefjord (Sermeq Kujalleq), UNESCO World Heritage since 2004, "the size of 66,000 soccer fields"; daily ice output equal to New York's yearly water consumption (as GiF states it). SOURCED: GiF 2025. Expanded airport with a 2,200 m runway due to open October 2026. UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **B.** Fishing for Greenland halibut and shrimp; tourism. SOURCED: Wikipedia. Halibut in Disko Bay: commercial fishery since about 1910 with longlines from small open boats, small vessels, or "through holes in the sea ice and transported by dogsledge"; the Icefjord fishery is mainly in winter when the glacier is less active and the fjord is frozen; more than a third of Disko Bay catches come from the small area by the fjord mouth; landings 11,435 t (2023), 9,644 t (2024). UNVERIFIED (NAFO scientific council papers via snippet; not opened). "Traditional fishing town specializing in halibut." SOURCED: AECO. International passengers at Ilulissat airport 3,816 (2024). SOURCED: GiF 2025. "Almost as many sled-dogs as people." SOURCED: Wikipedia. Dog areas zoned in the municipal plan (e.g., 3.9 ha by Sermermiut Aqqutaa, ~6 ha by Sisorarfik); dogs are "working animals, not pets"; keep 5 m from moving sleds. SOURCED: AECO; zoning UNVERIFIED (kommuneplania snippets).

UNVERIFIED (Realdania/guide snippets). **Sermermiut**, protected ancient settlement used by Saqqaq, Early Dorset and Thule cultures over 4,000+ years ("keep silence in Sermermiut"). SOURCED: AECO (silence, protection); cultures UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia snippet). **Knud Rasmussen's Museum** (explorer's birthplace), **Ilulissat Art Museum**, **Zion's Church** (late 18th century). SOURCED: Wikipedia.
- **D/E.** The ice that blocks land travel is also the fishing ground (winter fishing on fjord ice by dog sled) and the tourist draw; dogs are infrastructure with their own land-use zones.

#### Tasiilaq
- **A.** On Ammassalik Island, East Greenland; the most populous community on the east coast; Danish trading station founded 1894, after Gustav Holm's 1884-85 expedition; formerly Ammassalik, renamed 1997; main place where East Greenlandic (Tunumiisut) is spoken. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasiilaq . Access: helicopter to Kulusuk airport; seasonal cargo ships; "no roads far outside Tasiilaq." SOURCED: Wikipedia. Kulusuk international passengers 5,359 (2024). SOURCED: GiF 2025.
- **B. Supply and the ice:** Royal Arctic Line (2020): Tasiilaq, Ittoqqortoormiit, Uummannaq and Upernavik are "cut off from ship transport for large parts of the year"; Tasiilaq's first ship usually arrived in early June, with a plan from 2021 to try mid-May; calls every second week instead of every third, 11 scheduled calls a year plus "red calls" (made only if ice and weather allow), up to 13; routing via Reykjavík. SOURCED: INA 2020. In 2025: the last sailing to East Greenland is planned for early November because the settlements in the Tasiilaq district begin to freeze in and ice and storms increase; chartered ships leave for other work; the waters off Tasiilaq "continue to be marked by winter storms and ice." Icelandair's Kulusuk-Iceland route stops in winter; residents petitioned; the government says it cannot contract the route because Nuuk-Kulusuk flights run year-round and can carry freight including food.
