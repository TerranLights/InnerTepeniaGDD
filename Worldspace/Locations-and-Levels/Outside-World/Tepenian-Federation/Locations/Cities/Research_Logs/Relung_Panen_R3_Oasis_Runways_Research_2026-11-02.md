### 1.2 Summer surface and meltwater behavior (what exists)
- The 2019 survey was in late January to mid-February; the surface was "ideally level" with cryoconite pits; the 30 cm infiltration layer is snow that "begins to melt in the summer season" and refreezes; "signs of spring decay in the ice cover are not observed" (all from Paper A, earlier file).
- **Why the gulf keeps its ice (climate frame, from the neighboring lake):** Doran, McKay, Meyer, Andersen, Wharton and Hastings, "Climatology and implications for perennial lake ice occurrence at Bunger Hills Oasis, East Antarctica," *Antarctic Science* 8(3), 1996, pp. 289-296 (the author list in the earlier file differs: the 2026-10-29 file names Lyons and Des Marais, who are on the 2000 paper). An automatic weather station stood on **White Smoke Lake** (not on the gulf) from January 1992 to July 1993. Abstract as returned by the search tool: mean annual solar flux about **115 W m⁻²**, mean wind **4.6 m s⁻¹**, mean air temperature **-11.2 °C**; summer degree-days above freezing **71 °C-days** ("similar to ... McMurdo Dry Valleys"), winter freezing degree-days **3,987 °C-days** ("much smaller" than there); "The Bunger Hills Oasis seems to be in a marginal climatic region for the persistence of thick lake ice. We propose that this is either through the heat sink the glacier offers, and/or the positive feedback for ice growth provided by the high albedo of the adjacent glacier"; "the extent of glacier ice contact becomes the controlling factor in maintaining an ice cover all year." The repository page (repository.lsu.edu/geo_pubs/692) returned the same abstract in paraphrase without the numbers, so the numbers are **UNVERIFIED** at the digit level; the qualitative conclusion is SOURCED (fetch extract).

### 1.3 Cryoconite, dust and what dust does to a runway (comparator data)
The oasis cryoconite is rock dust brought by the prevailing easterly wind (Paper A, earlier file). On a runway, CRREL's Pegasus monograph says: "Nearly any matter other than snow that lies on the surface will promote deterioration. Common contaminants include fuel and lubricants, ice chunks, dirt and other mineral matter, trash ... and soot," and at Pegasus "winds from the southwest carry sand particles from Black Island over the runway"; cores showed "numerous horizons of concentrated mineral particles ... (on the order of less than once per year to several times a season)." **SOURCED**, Blaisdell, Lang, Crist, Kurtti, Harbin and Flora, *Construction, Maintenance, and Operation of a Glacial Runway, McMurdo Station, Antarctica*, CRREL Monograph 98-1 (March 1998), pp. 70-71 (full text read). No source found ties cryoconite pits specifically to aircraft damage.

- **Aircraft that used it:** none found. **Summer behavior:** "cracks and water-logged areas" mapped in late January-February 2019 (Paper A, earlier file). A measured ablation rate, glacier ice thickness at the strip, and stake results: **NOT FOUND** (searches 36, 37, 39; the stake data are unpublished in everything reached).
- **Physical setting (UNVERIFIED, from a Wikipedia mirror snippet):** the glacier extends about 40 km along the south flank of Bunger Hills and ends in the Edisto Ice Tongue. **White Smoke Lake** lies beside it (Doran 1996).
- **Comparators for what a blue-ice strip needs** (these are about *other* sites, SOURCED):
  - *Wilkins* (French inspection report, full text): a runway "usable ... 3 kilometres in length and approximately 300 meters in width," 750 m above sea level, 67 km from Casey; "an inability to use the runway in the heart of summer, namely the months of December and January, due to surface deformation of the runway at ground temperatures above -5 °C"; "continuous snow and ice management" with snow groomers, "whose sub-base is mainly marked by the presence of blue ice"; "white-out" conditions and ice movement (the site moves about 12 m per year) require differential GPS to secure the runway position. Operation: late October to early March; the aerodrome is evacuated for about four months in winter.
  - *Pegasus (natural ablation-zone concerns)*: cuspate exposed blue ice "will generate noise and high frequency vibration in fast-moving aircraft but this is nearly unavoidable"; filling the cusps with snow at a windy blue-ice site "would be difficult and the snow could not be kept in place anyway"; grading or grinding "may not make the ice any smoother on a small scale" (CRREL Monograph 98-1, p. 69-70).
  - The earlier file's Mellor and Swithinbank (1989) findings (no bearing limit; limits are roughness, crevasses, melt) are unchanged.

## 3. Lake Figurnoye and the other oasis lakes

### 3.1 Figurnoye as measured in 1987 (Klokov et al. 1990, full text read)
- Station: "Oasis-2" seasonal station (given in the paper as 66°16'S and "110°45'E", evidently a misprint for 100°45'E); the paper says the lake's "station bay" is where oxygen was measured, so the station stood on Figurnoye (called there "Figurnoe Lake (Algae Lake)").
- A **transit-flow** lake ("Figurnoe and Dalekoe Lakes are slightly mineralized (17-18 mg l⁻¹)"), with "a strong vertical and horizontal mixing"; fed by glacial melt (pH about 6.9); "after releasing from the ice cover the water had pH of 7.2-7.4"; samples were taken "either from the ice or from the boat" between 19 January and 15 March 1987. So the lake was ice-free or had open water at some of its sampling in the January-March window. (SOURCED.)
- Oasis lake totals: "hundreds of large and small lakes ... the total area of the lakes is about 36 km² covering 6-7% of the oasis bedrock"; the southern ice-free bedrock is 263 km² with a maximum altitude of 165 m; mean annual air temperature -9.1 °C; "the lowest registered [air humidity] value was 12 per cent"; potential evaporation 450-600 mm a year; ground temperatures "above freezing point during four months" (Korotkevic 1958, Rusin 1961 as cited). (SOURCED.)
- **Winter ice thickness on Figurnoye: NOT FOUND** (queries 41, 42 and the Polish and Russian lakes papers reached; Klokov gives no ice thickness). Freeze-up "within about 10 days" from late February/early March (Filcek and Zieliński 1990, earlier file) is still the only seasonal statement.
- Holocene history of the lake exists (Journal of Paleolimnology 2002, "Holocene environmental changes and development of Figurnoye Lake in the southern Bunger Hills," record at epic.awi.de/id/eprint/8151; page blocked by an Anubis "Access Denied"). A search summary says lake-ice conditions in a colder, drier early Holocene were "more severe than present" (UNVERIFIED). No modern ice thickness is in it as far as the snippet shows.

### 4.3 Friction, melt and surface rules from the field programs
- **Friction (CRREL, Pegasus):** "In general, a coefficient of at least **0.25** will be present on any glacial ice runway surface when the temperature is less than -10 °C. Concern for low levels of friction should arise at temperatures warmer than **-5 °C**. Snow is capable of providing a higher coefficient of friction than ice at warm temperatures"; a thin impermeable surface "sheen" must not be allowed to form; the snow cover must never exceed **6 cm** "to avoid overstressing landing gear" and should be dragged or planed "once a day or after every aircraft operation." (full text; SOURCED)
- **Friction figures from Wilkins:** "Blue ice ... a co-efficient of friction of 0.1. The snow pavement increases it to 0.3" (ABC/Crikey-derived search snippet; UNVERIFIED) and the earlier file's Studd (2008) "around 0.20 or better" criterion with measurements every 500 m. These differ by method and are not reconciled.
- **Melt protection:** Pegasus graded ice was covered with 25 cm of snow "in place by the end of November" and compacted through December and the first week of January, to guard against melt pools that "could destroy the possibility of using a runway before complete refreezing in March or later"; proof rolling after the 1991-92 summer found about 30 weak spots (average 2.8 m², 15-46 cm deep) tied to refrozen melt pools. (full text; SOURCED)

### 4.4 Wilkins, 2023-24 (the only recent public numbers for a blue-ice wheeled-jet runway)
All from the French inspection report (ATCM 46, attachment 104; full text read; SOURCED) unless marked.
- Runway: usable length about **3 km**, width about **300 m**, at about **750 m** elevation, 67 km southeast of Casey. Operational from "late October to early March on average," with "an inability to use the runway ... December and January ... due to surface deformation ... at ground temperatures above -5 °C." (Section 4.1 of the report also says "early November to late February on average"; the report is not internally consistent.)
- Traffic in 2023-24: "23 intercontinental flights: either by military aircraft C-17 (7 flights) ... or by Airbus A-319 (15 flights)" (7 + 15 = 22; the report says 23, **unreconciled**). A319: up to 32 passengers Hobart to Wilkins and 38 on return.
- Staff: **8 people in summer and 4 in winter**, one a site manager; "the presence of a doctor on-site is mandatory for each flight"; a medical tent; the site can accommodate up to 40 people; evacuated for about four months of winter. (Section 4.2.2-4.2.3.)
- **The aircraft are not refueled at Wilkins.** The two on-site tanks hold fuel from Casey "used solely to power the buildings and equipment on-site," about **30,000 to 40,000 liters a month** from September to March. Snow-clearing at the runway "can lead to over-consumption" of Casey's fuel in some seasons.
- Biosecurity: "No specific biosecurity measures are implemented at Wilkins ... the cargo has been fully bio-secured either departing from Hobart or before departure from Casey."

### 4.5 What limits wheeled freighters (767F, A330F, 777F) on ice: what the sources say
No source found treats a 767F, A330F or 777F on ice. The following limits are stated for the wheeled jets that have used ice (all SOURCED, from the items above):
1. **Ice contact stress, not total weight:** CRREL ties acceptance to the ratio of ice compressive strength to the aircraft's maximum tire pressure (at least 2.0), and to gear arrangement and "gang" load; Pegasus proof-tested at 260 psi for a 200 psi aircraft. Lindstrom: standard PCN/ACN methods "doesn't apply at all to ice"; the 787 team wrote a bearing-strength study reviewed by the Norwegian authority and Boeing, and found strength about 20 times the requirement. No tire pressure for the 767, A330F or 777F was looked up in this round (not found in any source read).
2. **Surface temperature:** friction concern above -5 °C (CRREL); Wilkins unusable at ground temperatures above -5 °C (French report); Pegasus summer closure.
3. **Surface roughness and melt features:** cuspate blue ice (vibration), melt pools (weak spots), dust and fuel contamination (CRREL).
4. **Braking and directional control:** "braking action is only slightly worse than landing on a wet runway - our biggest challenge is lateral control and handling the aircraft on the ground, especially in strong winds" (Studd 2008, earlier file); Lindstrom: "The braking action is quite good. It's similar to what you have on a wet runway"; "you don't aim for a smooth landing. You aim for a firm landing."
5. **Length:** every jet case found used about 3.0-3.35 km; FAA multipliers and the RCAM table of the earlier file; Wang: compacted snow on ice needs 1.6 times land length.
6. **Radio altimeter reliability over ice:** the 787 crew practiced the flare without it because "we weren't 100 percent certain that the radio altimeter was going to be reliable on ice." (Lindstrom.)
7. **Fire and rescue category:** Troll's rescue cover was Level 6 while the 787-9 needs Level 9; Norse was approved down to Level 8 with a documented risk assessment.
8. **Fuel:** the 787 carried enough to return to Cape Town "because refueling in Antarctica is very expensive"; Wilkins does not refuel aircraft; an A319 uses extra tanks to avoid the need (Studd 2008, earlier file).
9. **Turnaround and weather:** the crew would not depart unless "100 percent certain that the weather was acceptable"; the 767 turned around in about 2 hours.

## FINAL: what the sources support, do not support, and NOT FOUND

### What the sources support
1. **A heavy wheeled landing on the gulf's ice has happened:** two Il-18Vs on 3 December 1963, on a strip prepared in advance by the 8th Soviet Antarctic Expedition "in one of the lagoons of Transcription Gulf" (Lukin; Dubrovin gives the same wording), 80 people carried; and a 1961-62 runway at the oasis that refueled an Il-18V and An-12 for the Christchurch leg (two Russian chronicles agree; a third conflicts on 1961 details). (SOURCED)
2. **The gulf's ice and water:** 2.95-3.20 m ice (2019), a fresh layer 85-88 m thick on three sources (70 m on a fourth), saline anaerobic water below, tides of about 1.3 m, a connection to the sea through channels in Edisto Glacier inferred by the 1987 authors. (SOURCED/inferred)

(SOURCED rules; DERIVED application; extrapolation beyond Sharp's range flagged)
4. **Blue ice, melt and temperature are the limiting variables in the Antarctic record**, not total weight: Wilkins is unusable at ground temperatures above about -5 °C; Pegasus closed for melt and dust. (SOURCED)
5. **Australian flights to Bunger Hills are routine in recent summers** with Baslers and Twin Otters, a 1.5-hour leg, 39 t of cargo moved to Wilkins and by road to Casey for one season, fuel by drum, camps of 27-37 people. (SOURCED)
