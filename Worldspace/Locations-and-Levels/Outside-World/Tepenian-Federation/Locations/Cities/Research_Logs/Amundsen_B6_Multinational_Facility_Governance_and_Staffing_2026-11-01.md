#### 1.1 CERN: Convention, Council, Director-General, contributions
- SOURCED (CERN Convention text, https://council.web.cern.ch/en/content/convention-establishment-european-organization-nuclear-research): "The Council shall be composed of not more than two delegates from each Member State who may be accompanied at meetings of the Council by advisers." #### 1.3 CERN: discipline and disputes (staff)
- SOURCED (CERN annual report on Chapter VI of the Staff Rules, 2022, via Wayback copy of https://home.cern/news/official-news/cern/2022-annual-report-chapter-vi-staff-rules-and-regulations-settlement): "Behavioural concerns or administrative disputes ... are addressed in a timely manner using, whenever possible, informal resolution mechanisms such as mediation." Formal route: request for review, then internal appeal with the Joint Advisory Appeals Board (JAAB) consulted by the DG; discipline under S VI 2.01 to 2.05: sanctions in order "a warning; a reprimand; suspension without remuneration ... not exceeding six months; downward adjustment of the staff member's salary; demotion; dismissal"; "The Director-General shall consult the Joint Advisory Disciplinary Board (JADB) prior to taking any disciplinary action other than a warning or a reprimand"; for particularly serious misconduct the DG may dismiss without notice and without the JADB. External: complaint to the Administrative Tribunal of the International Labor Organization (ILOAT) after internal remedies.
- SOURCED (same report, 2022 counts): seven internal appeals introduced in 2022; five warnings (reasons listed: a disrespectful e-mail to the medical service; a user assaulting a colleague verbally and physically in an office altercation; inappropriate comments about management in a performance form; a fellow abusing IT administrator privileges to read colleagues' private communications; a drunk and inappropriate user at the public entrance requiring the Fire and Rescue Service); no reprimands; two JADB-based decisions (a user's contract of association terminated for fraudulent registration of associated personnel and misappropriation of funds; a staff member demoted plus downward salary adjustment for fraudulent violation of financial rules and conflict of interest); one staff member and two fellows terminated in probation for insufficient performance; one user's contract terminated for communications asserting as fact that CERN had illegally appropriated the user's work. ILOAT: many pending complaints (about performance qualification, tax certificates for associated members, pay review, harassment-appeal rejections); footnote 4: "the recent increase in ILOAT litigation against CERN continues."
- SOURCED (CERN Ombud, 8th annual report, 2018, via Wayback copy of https://ombuds.web.cern.ch/sites/default/files/2022-10/2018%20CERN%20Ombud%20Report.pdf): the Ombud function was created in July 2010 "at the same time as the publication of the CERN Code of Conduct"; "zero-barrier, informal, impartial and confidential channel for all Members of the Personnel"; "There have been on average 100 visitors/year since the Office was established in 2011"; 104 visitors in 2018; most outcomes through "advice or coaching", the rest through mediation, referral or formal action; "the majority of colleagues chose discussion or coaching over intervention, continuing to cite a fear of disclosure or retaliation"; most issues fall in "Evaluative relationship" (supervisor-subordinate), then peer relationships, then safety/health/physical environment; "Since 2011, the Ombud has also been in charge of dealing with the informal resolution of the various types of harassment ... At any moment that a formal complaint is lodged, the Ombud is no longer involved." Observation on mixed origins: sexual harassment cases where "Sometimes the fact of belonging to the same nationality reinforces common behavior, when the female colleague is of another nationality." On users (visiting researchers): "The issues concerning Users are amplified by the flat structure within the community, which sometimes leaves them without any clear framework of accountability"; their "contractual status is more fragile" and "scientific competitive pressure can sometimes be extremely high."

#### 1.5 ESA and ESTEC

- SOURCED (same, Art. XVII): disputes between Member States, or a state and the Agency, "which is not settled by or through the Council, shall, at the request of any party to the dispute, be submitted to arbitration" (three-member tribunal).
- SOURCED (same, Annex V Art. The phrase "juste retour" does not appear in the Convention text (searched: "geographical distribution", "return coefficient" only).

### 2. International Space Station: partner division, code of conduct, language, who decides, Mission Control shift communities

- SOURCED (IGA Article 22 text, https://www.jaxa.jp/library/space_law/chapter_3/3-2-2-9/3-2-2-922_e.html): "Canada, the European Partner States, Japan, Russia, and the United States may exercise criminal jurisdiction over personnel in or on any flight element who are their respective nationals." For on-orbit misconduct that affects the life or safety of a national of another Partner State, or occurs in or damages another Partner's flight element, the perpetrator's state must consult; an affected state "may, following such consultation, exercise criminal jurisdiction ... provided that, within 90 days ... the Partner State whose national is the alleged perpetrator either: (1) concurs in such exercise of criminal jurisdiction, or (2) fails to provide assurances that it will submit the case to its competent authorities for the purpose of prosecution." Para. 5: the article does not limit "the authorities and procedures for the maintenance of order and the conduct of crew activities ... which shall be established in the Code of Conduct pursuant to Article 11."
- SOURCED (Wikipedia, "Politics of the International Space Station", secondary, citing IGA Art. 5): "There is no fixed percentage of ownership for the whole space station. Rather, Article 5 of the IGA sets forth that each partner shall retain jurisdiction and control over the elements it registers and over personnel in or on the Space Station who are its nationals." Same page: "Crew time, electrical power and rights to purchase supporting services ... are divided 76.6% for NASA, 12.8% for JAXA, 8.3% for ESA, and 2.3% for CSA" (this is the share of the US Orbital Segment utilization, not of the Russian segment; the page's wording does not state "US segment" in that line, so the scope is UNVERIFIED from a primary document). Module-level shares: Columbus 51% ESA / 46.7% NASA / 2.3% CSA; Kibo 51% JAXA / 46.7% NASA / 2.3% CSA; Destiny 97.7% NASA / 2.3% CSA. Barter between partners is allowed (search summary of an AviationWeek/NASA Ames source, not opened); NASA-Roscosmos seat barter exists (search summary, UNVERIFIED detail).
- SOURCED (Crew Code of Conduct, 14 CFR 1214.403, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/1214.403): established "pursuant to Article 11 (Crew)" of the IGA and the NASA bilateral MOUs; its purpose: "establish a clear chain of command on-orbit; establish a clear relationship between ground and on-orbit management ... set forth disciplinary regulations; establish physical and information security guidelines; and define the ISS Commander's authority." Conduct standard: "to maintain a harmonious and cohesive relationship among the ISS crewmembers and an appropriate level of mutual confidence and respect through an interactive, participative, and relationship-oriented approach which duly takes into account the international and multicultural nature of the crew and mission." The Commander "is the highest authority among the ISS crewmembers on-orbit"; main responsibilities include "(5) maintain order; (6) ensure crew safety, health and well-being including crew rescue and return"; the Commander "shall have the authority to use any reasonable and necessary means to fulfill his or her responsibilities," extending to elements, crew, activities of any kind and "data and personal effects ... where necessary to protect the safety and well-being of the ISS crewmembers"; "Any matter outside the ISS Commander's authority shall be within the purview of the Flight Director"; "Issues regarding the Commander's use of such authority shall be referred to the Flight Director as soon as practicable"; the Commander is "solely accountable, to the Flight Director." The Commander "is entitled to change the daily routine ... where necessary to address contingencies"; otherwise implements the mission as directed by the Flight Director; Flight Rules "outline decisions planned in advance ... designed to minimize the amount of real-time discussion." The Multilateral Crew Operations Panel (MCOP) decides who commands: "nothing in this Section shall affect the ability of the MCOP to designate the national of any Partner State as an ISS Commander," and sets the succession order before flight.
- SOURCED (same, Section IV): discipline: "ISS crewmembers will be subject to the disciplinary policy developed and revised as necessary by the MCOP and approved by the Multilateral Coordination Board (MCB)... The disciplinary policy is administrative in nature ... Such violations may, inter alia, affect flight assignments as an ISS crewmember. The disciplinary policy does not limit a Cooperating Agency's right to apply relevant laws, regulations, policies, and procedures to the ISS crewmembers it provides." Medical privacy: all personal medical information "shall be treated as private medical information." NOT FOUND: the text of the MCOP disciplinary policy itself (not in the CFR).
- SOURCED (NASA Facts IS-1999-05-ISS023, May 1999, Wayback copy of http://spaceflight.nasa.gov/spacenews/factsheets/pdfs/is199905iss023.pdf; early-operations snapshot, now historical): "primary oversight for all operations resting with NASA"; Zarya's command and control at the control room in Korolev; "NASA flight control operations maintain oversight and approve all plans while the Russian flight control team directs real-time ISS operations based on the approved plans"; "A small NASA flight control team, designated the Houston Support Group, is stationed at the Korolev control center to facilitate communications and information exchange"; Houston's station control room, "When fully staffed ... about a dozen flight controllers, led by an International Space Station flight director"; the Flight Director has "Primary decision-making authority for station operations"; permanently staffed "by a full Flight Control Team when required, and by a Duty Officer at other times"; position list includes ADCO "works in partnership with Russian controllers" and TOPO "works in partnership with Russian controllers."

### 3. Antarctic Treaty governance in practice

- SOURCED (Antarctic Treaty text, Wikisource copy, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Antarctic_Treaty): Art. VII: each consultative party "shall have the right to designate observers to carry out any inspection"; "Observers shall be nationals of the Contracting Parties which designate them"; each observer "shall have complete freedom of access at any time to any or all areas of Antarctica"; "All areas of Antarctica, including all stations, installations and equipment ... and all ships and aircraft at points of discharging or embarking cargoes or personnel in Antarctica, shall be open at all times to inspection"; aerial observation allowed; parties give advance notice of expeditions and of "all stations in Antarctica occupied by its nationals." Art. VIII: observers and exchanged scientific personnel "shall be subject only to the jurisdiction of the Contracting Party of which they are nationals in respect of all acts or omissions occurring while they are in Antarctica for the purpose of exercising their functions"; for other persons the parties' positions on jurisdiction are reserved, and in a dispute the parties "shall immediately consult together with a view to reaching a mutually acceptable solution." Art. IX: consultative meetings decide by approval of all consultative parties ("approved by all the Contracting Parties whose representatives were entitled to participate"); status as a consultative party requires "substantial research activity there, such as the establishment of a scientific station or the despatch of a scientific expedition." Art. XI: disputes: "negotiation, inquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement or other peaceful means of their own choice"; referral to the International Court of Justice only "with the consent, in each case, of all parties to the dispute."

### 4. Svalbard Treaty: equal access, the Governor, Longyearbyen and Barentsburg

#### 7.4 Althing at Thingvellir (documented facts only)

#### 7.5 How respect rather than force is sustained (observed mechanisms, from the sources above)
- Consent to the process before the dispute: parties sign the LINX Terms of Agreement and may withdraw at any time; PCA parties submit a "compromis"; ITER Art. 8 and ESA Art. XVII require consultation, mediation or arbitration first; the Antarctic Treaty Art. XI requires consent for ICJ referral.
- Confidentiality as the price of participation: LINX mediation keeps no records; CERN Ombud confidentiality exception only for imminent serious harm.
- A fallback to a third forum rather than to force: LINX may refer to CEDR; CERN staff go to the ILO Administrative Tribunal; ICJ judgments may go to the Security Council (never used per an unopened source).
- Reputation and reciprocity as the sanction: the AMTI report shows that where a party refuses, only pressure remains; the Treaty inspection system relies on open access and published reports rather than penalties; Svalbard's Governor uses the police and the right to reject residents without income (a state's force, not the treaty's).
- Documented failure modes: non-participation (China, PCA); low attendance defeating a vote (LINX EGM 2017); fear of retaliation keeping people away from formal complaint (CERN Ombud 2018: "fear of retaliation"); feuds continuing despite courts (Althing).

### 8. Quotas and balance in practice (UN, CERN, ESA, ITER) and documented problems

#### 8.1 United Nations Secretariat: geographical distribution
- SOURCED (UN Charter Art. 101(3), as quoted in UN General Assembly documents; search summary of A/72 and JIU material, the Charter page itself not opened for Chapter XV; UNVERIFIED wording): efficiency, competence and integrity are the "paramount consideration," with due regard to recruitment "on as wide a geographical basis as possible."

The report says a review of recruitment methods "to potentially eliminate unconscious, unintended bias" was forthcoming.

- SOURCED: see 1.5 (Annex V, Art. IV). This is an industrial-contract quota, not a staff quota; the staff clause (Art. XII) says only "an adequate distribution of posts among nationals of the Member States."
- SOURCED (UK government, "Evaluating the benefits of the UK's investments in the European Space Agency," executive summary, https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evaluating-the-benefits-of-the-uks-investments-in-the-european-space-agency/evaluating-the-benefits-of-the-uks-investments-in-the-european-space-agency-executive-summary): "convention mandates equitable participation among its member states, proportional to their financial contributions. This principle, known as geo-return"; the UK's "geo-return coefficient stood at 0.99, meaning that for every EUR 1 invested, the UK received EUR 0.99 back in contract value, resulting in a deficit of EUR 41.18m" and this is "a reduction in the UK's geo-return deficit compared to historical figures" (the page gives Q4 2024 per a search summary).
- SOURCED (Advanced Television, 21 June 2024, https://www.advanced-television.com/2024/06/21/esa-contemplates-major-rule-change): "the geo-return has frequently forced prime contractors to restrict their subcontractors to those from a specific country, and this is often cited as the reason for manufacturing delays and unwanted complications"; the ESA council decided to test a streamlined approach on pilot projects; protectiveness: "if - for example - Italy is funding 65 per cent of the Italian-made Vega rocket system, then it is likely to seek a return that's commensurate to its funding"; trade-press, not ESA. Search summaries (not opened): before the November 2025 ministerial meeting (CM25, Bremen, 26-27 November 2025) a member-state group proposed fewer return lines and more flexible envelope programs, and the Director General reaffirmed geo-return as "fundamental"; UNVERIFIED what CM25 decided (no confirming snippet found).

#### 8.4 ITER: see 1.7
- Art. 7: "adequate distribution of posts among the Members in relation to their contributions"; 2024 staff pool from 30 countries, 64% from Europe (the host Member), with "efforts to improve the distribution of the representation of the ITER Members," feedback sessions with underrepresented populations and Domestic Agencies; Members nominate candidates ("nominated applications"). Problem: 1,850 applications for 45 external recruitments in 2024 and a staffing cap of 1,170 nearly reached, so there are few openings with which to rebalance.

#### 8.5 Cross-cutting documented problems (restated from the sources, no comparison across sites)
- A balance rule rests on a second principle that overrides it (competence/excellence first) at CERN, ESA and the UN, so shortfalls persist for decades (CERN: "this imbalance has continued to grow over the past 20 years" per the Staff Association at TREF 2020).
- Funding states treat balance as a return on investment and tie support to it (CERN, ESA); observed consequences: tension with pay and career issues (CERN Staff Association), supplier restrictions (ESA), a small pool of eligible posts (UN: 3,611 geographical posts out of 36,574 staff, DERIVED about 9.9%: 3,611 / 36,574 = 0.0987).
- The mechanism changes who applies more than who is chosen (UN: unrepresented-state applicants are selected at comparable rates but form the smallest pool; CERN Staff Association: more applications do not mean more staff).
- Turnover limits integration regardless of balance (CERN survey: "High turnover -> Social environment changes very often. Establishing a social network can be challenging.").
