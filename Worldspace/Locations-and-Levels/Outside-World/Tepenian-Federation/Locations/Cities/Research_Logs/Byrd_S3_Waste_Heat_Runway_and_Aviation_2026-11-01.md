## 1. Rejecting industrial heat in extreme cold

### 1a. Dry coolers and closed-circuit coolers in deep cold (vendor practice)

- **SOURCED (vendor summary, secondary):** a technical note says 19% glycol protects only to about −3 °C and recommends
  45–50% glycol for winters reaching roughly −27 °C to −33 °C.
  https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/plate-frame-glycol-concentration-for-winter-freeze-protection
  *(search snippet only; page not opened)*
- **SOURCED (snippet):** EVAPCO gives a 30% glycol solution a freezing point of about −14 °C and a burst point of about
  −51 °C; de-icing can reverse fan direction for a set period. https://evapco.com/media/130356 *(snippet only)*
- **SOURCED (snippet):** Baltimore Aircoil lists temperature setting, fan control and dry operation as the main icing
  protections, chosen by expected climate extremes and load variation; Marley warns that prolonged fan reversal in
  subfreezing weather can damage fans. https://www.manualslib.com/manual/2866975/Bac-Hxv.html?page=31 ·
  https://manualsonline.com/manuals/mfg/spx/mhf702.html?p=18 *(snippets only)*
- **UNVERIFIED (engineering general knowledge, not found in a source this session):** in very cold air the problem for an
  air-side heat rejecter flips from "not enough capacity" to "too much capacity": the fluid can be overcooled and freeze,
  so fans are slowed, cycled or stopped and coils are bypassed. No source found giving a −40 °C to −60 °C design procedure.
- **NOT FOUND:** a published design procedure, minimum flow or leaving-fluid limit for dry coolers at −40 °C to −60 °C air.

- **Amundsen-Scott South Pole elevated station (2000s).** **SOURCED** (Berry and Braun, "Foundation Design for the Elevated

### 1d. Keeping frozen ground frozen under a heat source

- **Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS), Alyeska Pipeline Service Company.** **SOURCED** (Alyeska fact page): "Some 420 miles
  of the 800-mile-long pipeline is elevated on 78,000 vertical support members due to permafrost"; "There are over 124,000
  heat pipes along the pipeline. These pipes transfer ground heat into the air to ensure soil remains stable and able to
  support the pipeline"; "Miles of buried pipeline: 380"; "The pipeline project involved some 70,000 workers from 1969
  through 1977." https://alyeska-pipe.com/fact_tidbits
  - **SOURCED (snippet, conference paper):** the heat pipes use anhydrous ammonia and sit in VSMs where the line is
    elevated in warm, non-thaw-stable permafrost; some developed "cold topping" from non-condensable gas (a by-product of
    corrosion or dissociation of ammonia), repaired with "getter" devices or by recharging with CO2.
    https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/9780784483589.031 · https://inl.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/thermal-performance-of-taps-heat-pipes-with-non-condensable-gas-b/
    *(snippets only)*
  - **SOURCED (PBS, paraphrase returned by the fetch tool):** USGS geologist Art Lachenbruch warned in 1970 that a hot oil
    pipe would thaw the permafrost around it; three designs followed — buried where thaw-stable, elevated on H-shaped
    supports with sliding shoes where not, and a short refrigerated buried stretch using chilled brine.
    https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/pipeline-designs-protect-permafrost/
  - **What failed — SOURCED:** "The slope of permafrost where an 810-foot section of pipeline is secured has started to
    shift as it thaws"; "57 miles northwest of Fairbanks near the Dalton Highway"; "Alyeska is installing approximately
    100 free-standing thermosyphons 40 to 60 feet into the ground"; "There are about 124,000 thermosyphons arrayed along
    the path of the pipeline"; "Construction is expected to take 120 days." https://insideclimatenews.org/news/11072021/thawing-permafrost-trans-alaska-pipeline/ (2021-07-11)
    - **SOURCED (snippet):** a later Petroleum News report says the count was later dropped to 60.
      https://www.petroleumnews.com/pnads/795874619.shtml *(snippet only)*
- **Inuvik, Northwest Territories (Canada).** **SOURCED:** "The town of Inuvik was built to replace the flood-prone community
  of Aklavik"; "The design and construction occurred in the late 1950s and early 1960s"; "most structures are constructed
  on timber piles drilled five meters into the ground"; "Water and sewer services are provided using heated
  above-ground 'utilidors'"; "Engineers and contractors expected the permafrost depth to be one meter (3.3 ft.)"
  but it was about 350 m. https://legacy.csce.ca/en/historic-site/town-of-inuvik-infrastructure/ (Canadian Society for
  Civil Engineering historic site, plaque 2012)
  - **What failed — SOURCED (snippet only):** Inuvik still has 6,400 m of original utilidor in service, some nearly 65
    years old; "The original wood pilings ... are now heaving and sinking as the permafrost melts"; replacement estimated
    at $80 million. https://nnsl.com/inuvikdrum/80-million-to-replace-utilidor-system-already-30-years-past-service-life-7262961
    *(the page redirected to yellowknifer.com and returned 404; died at the source)*
  - **Second round — SOURCED (snippets only):** the expected service life is 30 years against nearly 65 in service; the next
    replacement phase is about $20 million; the town set aside $3 million for the following year and was applying to the
    federal disaster-mitigation program; an earlier (2017) project replaced 540 m of utilidor at an estimated $5,729,334, with
    up to $4,297,000 from the federal Small Communities Fund. https://www.cbc.ca/1.5391835 ·
    https://ca.news.yahoo.com/inuviks-utilidor-nearly-65-years-120000979.html (404 — died at the source)
    *(snippets only; the CBC date was not shown)*
  - **Conflict:** the CSCE plaque page says the utilidors were "replaced in 2009" (fetch-tool summary), while the news reports

- **Yakutsk, Sakha Republic (Russia).** **SOURCED** (AFP, 2018-12-05): "With a population of about 300,000 Yakutsk is the
  world's largest city built on permafrost"; "Most Soviet-era apartment blocks in Yakutsk are made of concrete panels and

## 3. Clearing snow with waste heat

### 3a. Holland, Michigan — downtown snowmelt (power-plant waste heat)

- **SOURCED (City of Holland):** "approximately 690,000 square feet of heated sidewalks and streets"; another 190,000 square
  feet of privately owned snowmelt is heated from City snowmelt mains; started 1988; "190 miles of tubing laid underneath
  the pavement and sidewalks"; a closed system pumping "over 4,700 gallons of water per minute at 95 degrees"
  [Fahrenheit]; design capacity about one inch of snow per hour at 20 °F with 10 mph wind; waste heat from power generation,
  water returned to the Holland BPW plant; since 2016 the loop uses potable water instead of Lake Macatawa water;
  expanded to the Civic Center market area, the sidewalks to Herrick District Library, and the ramps of the 7th Street
  parking deck. https://cityofholland.com/879/Snowmelt-System
- **SOURCED (Plumbing & Mechanical, 2024):** "they can use the circulating water from the waste heat rather than a boiler
  system"; "Water initially came from Lake Macatawa, but now the city uses potable water from Holland Energy Park, which
  opened in 2017"; the water "returns about 20° cooler"; "the current expansion includes nearly 807,000 square feet and

### 3e. Heated bridge, apron and runway experiments

- **SERSO, Därligen bridge, A8, canton of Bern, Switzerland (seasonal storage, 1994–).** **SOURCED** (Eugster and Schatzmann,

## 6. Heat that makes a place visible

### 6a. Ice fog, Fairbanks, Alaska

- **SOURCED (Ned Rozell, Geophysical Institute, University of Alaska Fairbanks, 1997-01-08, reporting Carl Benson's 1965 work):**
  power plant cooling water "accounted for 64 percent of the manmade water sources that could end up as ice fog"; "Gasoline
  combustion made up only 3 percent"; cars and trucks are "the most visible culprits"; "people in the Fairbanks area exhaled
  and sweated about 58 tons of water vapor into the air each day in 1964"; "about 2,000 outdoor dogs in Fairbanks in 1964"
  exhaled "a little more than a half ton of water each day"; total about 4,000 tons a day in 1964, perhaps more than 6,000
  then; ice fog forms "when the temperature drops below about minus 30 degrees Fahrenheit"; "inversions form at the top of
  the ice fog layer". https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/ice-fog-product-temperature-topography-dogs
- **SOURCED (Anchorage Daily News, 2024-02-17, on Lea Hartl, Alaska Climate Research Center, 2023 paper):** 1950–1980 averaged
  "more than two weeks' worth of days with ice fog each year"; 1990–2020 about six days a year; "Ice fog has a magic number:
  minus 30 degrees Fahrenheit"; the decline is because "there are many fewer days in which Fairbanks experiences
  temperatures below that magic minus 30 degrees"; a major source today is an open stretch of the Chena River warmed by
  power-plant cooling water; "the actual mass of water ejected as vapor from the exhaust is 1.3 times greater than the mass
