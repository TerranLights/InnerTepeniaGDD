## 1 · #6 Clarksdale, Mississippi

**Claim to test:** a small city at a famous highway crossing, known worldwide for blues and its juke joints, a reputation carried outward by travelers and musicians.
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with narrowing.** (1) The "highway crossing" is real (old US 49 x US 61), but its status as *the* blues crossroads is a **designation made in 1999 by state resolution**, chosen because the town was already a blues center and tourism destination; the devil-bargain story is **folklore** attached to it, and several other Delta sites were also proposed. (2) The town was a crossing of transport long before highways: the head of navigation on the Sunflower River, a railroad town from 1879, and the site where two Native trade paths crossed. (3) The worldwide reputation was carried outward first by **people leaving** (the Illinois Central depot as a main departure point of the Great Migration; Muddy Waters left by train in 1943) and by **radio** (WROX, from 1944), and since about 2000 by a deliberate **tourism-and-festival economy** organized by a handful of incomers and locals. (4) Juke joints: one long-running original (Red's) survives; most of the present venue roster is post-2000.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Mississippi Delta, on the Sunflower River; county seat of Coahoma County. Elevation 174 ft (53 m). **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Clarksdale, Mississippi," read 2026-11-20). Area is given inconsistently on that page (18.79 sq mi in infobox vs 13.9 sq mi in text). **SOURCED (the inconsistency).**
- **Population:** 14,903 (2020 census). Peak 21,673 (1970); 21,105 (1960). 2024 ACS estimate 14,231. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia; census figure also in Census QuickFacts snippet, **UNVERIFIED** there). Racial makeup 2020: Black 81.49%, White 14.46%, other/mixed 1.95%, Hispanic 1.37%, Asian 0.66%. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- **Founding:** John Clark, 1848, bought land and started a timber business; the town became a trading center. Incorporated 1882; railroad (Louisville, New Orleans and Texas Railway) reached it 1879. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). The city's own history page: Coahoma County chartered 9 Feb 1836 from the 1830 Choctaw Cession (Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek); the county name is from a Choctaw word for "red panther"; the town site lay where two Native trade routes crossed (Chakchiuma Trade Trail, Lower Creek Trade Paths); Clarksdale sits at the head of navigation on the Sunflower River and early businesses faced the river; an 1889 fire, floods and poor roads slowed early growth; county split into two judicial districts in 1892 (seats Friars Point and Clarksdale), abolished 1930 leaving Clarksdale sole seat. **SOURCED** (cityofclarksdale.org/?p=469, read 2026-11-20). [K+ transport-crossing history, P5]
- **Defining feature (as the place presents it):** blues heritage and live music. The state designated the old 49/61 junction "the Crossroads" in April 1999; Vic Barbieri's three-guitar metal sculpture (over 1,000 lb) installed May 1999; Highway 61 through town renumbered 161 in 2004 when bypasses opened. **SOURCED** (Mississippi Blues Trail marker #222 "Crossroads of 49 & 61," msbluestrail.org, text by Jim O'Neal, dedicated 9 Apr 2026). [K2, K13, P6]

### B. What else exists there
**Economy and employment**
- **Cotton was the historic base.** The city's history page records merchants of Lebanese, Italian, Chinese and Jewish origin in the early 1900s, when the city was promoted as a cotton capital. **SOURCED** (cityofclarksdale.org/?p=469). [K+ merchant communities, P9]
- **Mechanization:** In 1944 the Hopson Planting Company, just south of town, grew a cotton crop produced entirely by machine (planting to baling) on 28 acres; International Harvester had worked with Howell Hopson II from 1935-1944; first public demonstration of the picker reported as 2 Oct 1944 (crowd sizes reported variously as 300, "several hundred," 3,000). **SOURCED** for the 28-acre crop and its effect on Black field labor (cityofclarksdale.org; Wikipedia); demonstration date and crowd **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; SAH Archipedia and HMDB pages returned 403). The city page states plainly that mechanization made many Black field workers expendable and, with postwar changes, accelerated the Great Migration north. **SOURCED.** [K9, K7, P8, SM:humanity-and-technology]
- **Tenancy:** Mississippi Encyclopedia snippet says Coahoma County had more than 2,600 sharecropping households, the second-highest figure in the state. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only; the encyclopedia's pages returned no body text through the fetch tool).
- **Today:** Coahoma County's largest industries (2024, Data USA snippet): health care and social assistance (1,198 workers), educational services (1,089), retail (748). In Clarksdale: health care 887, education 851, manufacturing 575. Poverty: county 35.5% (2020-2024 ACS), city 40.3% (2024). **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets of Data USA / USAFacts). Largest employers (2025 county business-climate profile, snippet): Clarksdale HMA LLC (495), Clarksdale Municipal School District (427), Coahoma County School District (400), Coahoma Community College (362), Northwest Mississippi Regional Medical Center (185); unemployment 6.5% (Nov 2024); health care the largest share of county GDP (15.2%, 2023). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; the search tool itself warned the PDF layout was jumbled).
- **Tourism as a policy bet:** Next City (Yonah Freemark, 23 Aug 2010): population about 18,000 and falling; downtown "half empty"; median household income just over half the U.S. average; industrial jobs declining, farm jobs scarcer, young people leaving; the Delta Blues Museum drew 25,000-30,000 visitors a year; the article doubted tourism was producing good jobs. **SOURCED.** PBS NewsHour (14 Jun 2019): an economist who studied downtown redevelopment observed that visitors and most new downtown businesses are overwhelmingly white in a majority-Black town; a Black-owned pancake house owner said many people depend on the festival and that what happens "inside the fence" needs to reach the wider community. **SOURCED.** [K8, K9, P9]
- **Tourism tax:** Coahoma County levies 2% on lodging and 1% on prepared food and beverages; most of the revenue goes to marketing and promotion (Kappi Allen, Coahoma County Tourism Commission, quoted in Delta Business Journal, 13 Jul 2017). **SOURCED** (hottytoddy.com/?p=225470). The Tourism Commission was created by late-1990s legislation. **UNVERIFIED** (bill snippets). In 2025 the commission copyrighted the Crossroads logo. **SOURCED** (Blues Trail marker #222). [K1, K8]

**Institutions**
- **Government:** mayor-led city; the current mayor, Orlando Paden, is the son of the late Red's Lounge owner and now owns Red's. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets: Afar, AOL/Tour, Garden & Gun).
- **Flood control (shared-problem institution):** The Yazoo-Mississippi Delta Levee District was created by act of 28 Feb 1884 in reaction to the 1882 flood, first seated at Austin, Miss.; its headquarters were moved to Clarksdale by legislation of 19 Jan 1886 and it remains domiciled there. **UNVERIFIED** for the Clarksdale move (snippets from leveeboard.org and MDAH); the district's own timeline page **SOURCED** for: 1884 creation; 1917 first Flood Control Act; 1927 flood ("the greatest peacetime disaster" per Hoover) leading to the Flood Control Act of 1928; 1937 flood passed without a levee break; long floods 1973 and 1982-83; 2011 flood the largest on record between Cairo and Baton Rouge (leveeboard.org/history14.html). [K1, P7]
- **Schools:** Clarksdale Municipal School District (nine schools, about 3,600 students); private Lee Academy, Presbyterian Day School, St. Elizabeth's; Clarksdale Collegiate charter school (opened 2018). **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). **Coahoma Community College** began as Coahoma County Agricultural High School (1924), described as the first agricultural high school in Mississippi for Black students under "separate but equal"; college division added 1949; opened to all races 1965; the high school was dissolved in 2018 and replaced by Coahoma Early College High School. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of the college's history page, Wikipedia and MDAH). [K+ segregation-era institutions]
- **Hospital:** Northwest Mississippi Regional Medical Center (also called Merit Health Northwest), about 181 acute + 14 rehab beds (195 total) in older state filings; operator Curae went bankrupt 2018; Community Health Systems managed it from May 2019; Delta Health System took over the lease 1 Feb 2021. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Media:** WROX radio, first broadcast 5 Jun 1944, 250 watts (1,000 W from 1964); at 257 Delta Ave from July 1945, about 40 years inside the Alcazar Hotel; since 2005 at 628 DeSoto Ave; FM simulcast from 2010, now 97.5 FM. Early Wright, an auto mechanic who managed a gospel quartet from Helena, Ark., bought a 15-minute Sunday slot in 1947, was offered a DJ job (accepted after his pastor approved), and broadcast 1947-1998, playing blues/R&B and gospel; described as the first Black disc jockey in Mississippi. Live in-studio guests included Sonny Boy Williamson II, Sam Cooke, B.B. King, Muddy Waters, Elvis Presley and others; a young Ike Turner, who ran the Alcazar Hotel elevator, learned the control room there and later hosted a show and played live on air. **SOURCED** (wroxradio.com/history, read 2026-11-20). Also the *Clarksdale Press Register* newspaper and WAID radio. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). [K13, K14, P8, SM:power-rising-step-by-step]
- **Civil rights:** Martin Luther King Jr. held the first major SCLC meeting here 29 May 1958 and returned 1962. Aaron Henry, a local pharmacist (Fourth Street Drug Store), became state NAACP president in 1960. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). Christmas 1961: bands from two Black high schools, which had marched in the city Christmas parade since the late 1940s, were barred after NAACP activism; Henry and the county NAACP organized a boycott of downtown stores, which also protested white-owned stores' refusal to hire Black workers beyond menial jobs; slogan "If we can't parade downtown, we won't trade downtown"; Henry and about six others were convicted of "conspiring to withhold trade," his business was attacked and his wife fired from her teaching job; the boycott was economically devastating to white store owners. **SOURCED** (Zinn Education Project, "Christmas Shopping Boycott"). The drugstore burned in 1993; one account says the boycott ran over two years and won the return of the Black bands to the parade. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). August 1951 rape case and local activism. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). [K9, K6 (segregation as enforced rule), P7, SM:man-versus-society]

- Highways US 49, 61 and 278. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). Local airport Fletcher Field. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). No passenger rail in Clarksdale today; the nearest Amtrak stop found is Marks, Miss. (City of New Orleans line, one train each way daily, opened 4 May 2018). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; distance from Clarksdale NOT FOUND).
- **Historic rail and migration:** the Illinois Central depot was the main departure point for Black residents leaving for Chicago after cotton prices fell post-1920. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia; city history page). Muddy Waters left from the Clarksdale platform in May 1943 after a wage dispute at Stovall plantation (he earned 22 cents a day driving tractors, asked for 25; others earned 27). **SOURCED** (WBEZ). The Delta Blues Museum occupies a former railroad depot (built c. 1918 for the Yazoo and Mississippi Valley Railroad, later Illinois Central freight depot; build date given variously 1910/1918/1926). **SOURCED** (Next City gives 1918; others **UNVERIFIED**).

### C. Cultural locations
- **Delta Blues Museum** (1 Blues Alley). Founded 1979 in a library branch because tourists kept stopping at the library asking for blues sites; moved into the city-renovated depot in 1999; Muddy Waters Wing (2012) holds the remains of his cabin from Stovall plantation; collection of instruments, recordings, folk art and costumes. **Funding:** City of Clarksdale, Mississippi Arts Commission, NEA; city funding gives Coahoma County residents free admission. **Arts and Education Program:** free after-school lessons led by four local musicians; started from Johnnie Billington, a garage repair-shop owner who taught local youth guitar, hired by the museum with a late-1980s MAC/NEA grant; two of his former students now teach; instruments supplied, students rotate instruments to learn as a band; a student band has released two CDs; 2014 National Arts and Humanities Youth Program Award; a high-school docent program began when one senior asked to earn service hours as a guide and recruited 26 others. **SOURCED** (NEA, "The Delta Blues Museum: Bringing the Blues to the Next Generation," 25 Sep 2017). [K13, K14, K8, R (repair-shop owner as teacher), P8]
- **Red's Lounge** (Red's Juke Joint). Opened early 1980s by Red Paden; small room, red lights, low ceiling, a refrigerator of beer, a smoker and picnic benches out front; cash only. Paden died December 2023; son Orlando owns it. **SOURCED** for interior and Paden quotes (WGBH/NPR, 26 Feb 2017); dates and ownership **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). Paden organized an Old-Timers Blues Festival with a barbecue contest ($1,000 first prize, 2017). **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Ground Zero Blues Club:** opened May 2001 in a former cotton warehouse near the museum; co-owned by actor Morgan Freeman, Bill Luckett (d. 2021) and partners. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). Went from one to four live-blues nights a week. **SOURCED** (jsjbf.org article).
- **Cat Head Delta Blues & Folk Art** (Delta Ave): record and folk-art store opened by Roger Stolle, an Ohio-born fan who moved in 2002 (PBS); acts as an informal welcome center; hosts the Cat Head Mini Blues Fest (Sunday of Juke Joint weekend, and again in October) and keeps the town's live-music calendar. **SOURCED** (PBS 2019). One article (jsjbf.org) gives the move as 1995; conflict noted, PBS preferred. [K13]
- **Other named venues (2024 calendar):** Bluesberry Cafe, Hambone Gallery, Hooker Grocer + Eatery, Old Madidi Bar (Auberge Hostel), Hopson Commissary, The Den Again, Delta Blues Alley Cafe, Studio 305, Bad Apple Blues Club, Levon's Bar & Grill, Ooze-Bar, Shack Up Inn. **SOURCED** (msbluestrail.org/?p=15834, calendar Sep-Dec 2024).
- **Riverside Hotel** (615 Sunflower Ave): built as the G.T. Thomas Afro-American Hospital, where Bessie Smith died 26 Sep 1937 after a car accident; converted to a hotel 1943-44 by Mrs. Z.L. Ratliff Hill (20 rooms on two floors), who bought it in 1957; still Ratliff-family owned; in the African American Civil Rights Network since 2023. **SOURCED** (NPS article, updated 8 Mar 2024). It housed traveling Black musicians in the segregation era and is listed in the Green Book. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K4, K5, P9]
- **New World District** (south side, Issaquena Ave and 4th St): the historic Black business and entertainment district, named for Nelson Jones's New World saloon and rooming house (by 1890); described as a red-light district until a fire about 1920, after which brick commercial buildings were built; W.C. Handy's orchestra played there 1903-05; active to the 1940s; now largely vacant. The **New Roxy** theater (363 Issaquena), vacant from the mid-1980s, under renovation from 2008. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of Delta Blues Museum, Visit Clarksdale heritage map, SAH Archipedia). [K9, K4]
- **Hopson Commissary / Shack Up Inn** (Hopson plantation, south of town): plantation buildings turned music venue and lodging of old sharecropper shacks (one an 1883 cabin moved from Rich); opening date given as 1988 or 1998. **UNVERIFIED** (SAH Archipedia 403; snippets conflict). [K+ re-use of labor housing as tourist lodging, K9]
- **Tennessee Williams Rectory Museum** (St. George's Episcopal Church rectory), where the playwright lived as a child; his grandfather Rev. Walter Dakin was rector 1917-1933; rectory named a Mississippi Literary Landmark 2003. **Cutrer Mansion** (festival headquarters since 2005). **SOURCED** (Coahoma Community College, festival "About" page).
- **Hot tamale makers:** Abe's Bar-B-Q (616 N. State St, same site since 1937) and Hicks' tamales (305 S. State St); Mexican vendors sold tamales downtown within living memory; the Southern Foodways Alliance documents a Hot Tamale Trail. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; Mississippi Today page returned 403). [K12]
- **Chinese grocery families:** Delta Chinese came after the Civil War (first as recruited cotton pickers), opened groceries mostly in Black neighborhoods, lived in rooms behind the store, were socially placed "in-between" Black and white residents; families traveled long distances to regional Chinese dances. The Chow family of Clarksdale (children of grocers) are profiled; a community member estimates the Delta Chinese population fell from about 2,500 (mid-1970s) to about 500. **SOURCED** (NPR/WBEZ, 18 Mar 2017). [K4, P9]
- **Clarksdale Walk of Fame** (2008). **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).

**Festivals** [K2, K3]
- **Juke Joint Festival** (April): run by the Clarksdale Downtown Development Association (501(c)(3)), which also runs the Clarksdale Film Festival; the 2027 edition is the 24th; Saturday daytime free on about 17 stages over 8 downtown blocks (10am-6pm); Saturday night about 25 indoor venues, 45+ acts, entry by a limited wristband with no separate covers, about 6pm-1am; free Thursday kickoff and Sunday mini fest; over 100 performances in the week; non-music attractions include monkeys riding dogs and racing pigs, crafts, workshops, children's events; sponsors include Visit Clarksdale, Southern Bancorp and the Walton Family Foundation; attendees from all 50 states and about two dozen countries; called the county's "biggest blues and business week of the year." **SOURCED** (jukejointfestival.com/about.php, read 2026-11-20). Founded (by Stolle and businessman Bubba O'Keefe) c. 2004. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). Created to revive a downtown Stolle described as "really winding down." **SOURCED** (PBS 2019). [K3, K8]
- **Sunflower River Blues & Gospel Festival** (first weekend of August, free): began 1988 as a promotion to draw shoppers downtown, funded by downtown merchants and organized by Jim O'Neal and Dr. Patricia Johnson, grown from free holiday music festivals in 1986-87; merged in 1993 with a gospel festival begun in 1992. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). Stages on the Sunflower River bank (acoustic stage beside a canoe company, whose owner supplies watercolor paints for festival-goers' paintings hung on the stage), inside the old station, and a main stage at the museum; musicians also play in businesses and on sidewalks. **SOURCED** (Mississippi Folklife photo essay, 2013, photos 2009). [K3, K13]
- **Mississippi Delta Tennessee Williams Festival** (October): run by Coahoma Community College; each year on one play; lectures, readings, porch plays performed on front porches in the historic neighborhood; a high-school acting contest with over $3,000 in prizes for school drama departments; Saturday finale with supper and a dance to a Delta blues band. Inaugurated 1993 (college page) or 1992 (other sources). **SOURCED** (CCC "About"). [K3]
- **Others:** Clarksdale Film & Music Festival (January); Mighty Roots Music Festival (Stovall Farm, September); Hopson Pumpkin Pickin' Festival (October); Hambone Festival (late October); Deep Blues Fest (October); Cruz'n the Crossroads Car & Truck Show (October); Super Blues Sunday; New Year's at the Crossroads. **SOURCED** (msbluestrail.org calendar 2024). Claims of "live blues 365 nights a year" and "about a dozen" to "more than 20" festivals a year come from promoters. **SOURCED as claims** (PBS 2019; msbluestrail.org; jsjbf.org).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The museum exists because visitors arrived asking for blues sites before any museum did (founded in a library to answer them). **SOURCED** (NEA 2017). [K13]
- The "Crossroads" was designated in 1999 to fix a folklore claim to one site; the logo was copyrighted in 2025; the designation was chosen partly because the town was already a tourism destination. **SOURCED** (marker #222). [K8, P6]
- The blues economy re-used buildings left by the cotton economy: a cotton warehouse (Ground Zero), a freight depot (museum), plantation commissary and sharecropper shacks (Hopson/Shack Up Inn), vacant downtown storefronts (Cat Head, Hambone, Yazoo Pass placed in vacant buildings). **SOURCED/UNVERIFIED** as marked above. [K9, K+]
- Festivals were created by merchants and downtown development groups to bring shoppers and visitors downtown (Sunflower 1988; Juke Joint c. 2004). [K3]
- Two economies sit side by side: a visitor economy whose customers and new owners are mostly white, and a resident population that is about 81% Black with poverty around 35-40%. **SOURCED** (PBS 2019; Wikipedia) / poverty **UNVERIFIED**. [K4, K9]

## 2 · #35 The Chitlin' Circuit

**Claim to test:** a network of venues along travel routes that carried musicians and their reputations from town to town (K13, K8).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with important corrections.** (1) It was less a fixed "network of venues" than a **booking and promotion system** layered on top of the segregated Black business districts of many cities and towns: a central booking agency (Ferguson Brothers, Indianapolis, from 1940/41) coordinating local promoters, whose capital often came from the **numbers game** and other informal economies. (2) Reputations were carried not only by the touring bands but by the **Black press** (a bandleader's column in the *Chicago Defender* served as "road intelligence" for bands and promoters) and later by Black radio. (3) It was the successor of a formal theater circuit (TOBA, 1920s, collapsed 1930). (4) The name itself was a vernacular term, popularized only in 1966-67; the Black press called it the "one-nighter" or "theatrical" circuit, and some performers rejected the name. (5) It declined after desegregation (1964 onward) and urban renewal, but a smaller network survives, and the name was later applied to touring Black stage plays.

### A. Snapshot (the tradition itself)
- **What it was:** venues in the eastern, southern and upper-Midwest U.S. where Black musicians, comedians and entertainers could perform safely and commercially under segregation, roughly 1930s-1960s; ranged from rural juke joints to city theaters. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Chitlin' Circuit," read 2026-11-20).
- **Name:** from chitterlings (pig intestines, a soul-food dish); one historian (Frederick Douglass Opie) says club owners sold chitlins and other soul food from their kitchens; also a play on the "Borscht Belt." Lauterbach: the Black press called it the "one-nighter" or "theatrical" circuit; the name implied "second-classness," and performers such as Little Richard and James Brown did not want to be grouped under it. Earliest examples all come from Lou Rawls interviews (*Billboard* 3 Dec 1966; *Los Angeles Times* 8 Jan 1967). **SOURCED** (GBH/NPR Code Switch, Tanya Ballard Brown, 16 Feb 2014). [K+ naming, P6]
- **Predecessor:** Theater Owners Booking Association (TOBA), a white-run vaudeville circuit for Black performers, about 80-100 theaters at its peak in the early-mid 1920s; founded 1920 or January 1921 (sources differ); performers mocked its initials (the joke is in the slang file, per the language caution); collapsed 1930. **UNVERIFIED** for founding details (snippets); TOBA formed 1921 and collapsed 1930 **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, Chitlin' Circuit). Scholarly source: Michelle R. Scott, *T.O.B.A. Time* (University of Illinois Press, 2023). [K8]
- **Scale:** Wikipedia lists about 45 named venues in about 40 cities, from Annapolis to Phoenix, plus seasonal Black beach and farm venues (Carr's and Sparrow's Beach, Md.; Rosedale Beach, Del.; John Brown's Farm near Sharpsburg, Md.). **SOURCED.** Georgia's Historic Preservation Division identified 89 locations in Georgia that served as vaudeville or circuit venues (20 of unknown status) in its historic context *On the Circuit*. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; the PDF exceeded the fetch size limit).

### B. How it worked (the system)
**Founders and money**
- **Denver D. Ferguson** (1895-1957), from Brownsville, Ky., moved to Indianapolis in 1919; founded Ferguson Printing Company on Indiana Avenue (1920), which printed tickets for the numbers or "policy" games, disguised as baseball scorecards; brought in brother Sea Ferguson as public face when authorities took notice; both among the city's wealthiest residents by the late 1930s, known for loans and charity in the Black community. Clubs: Cotton Club (1931, Sea), Trianon Ballroom (1932), Sunset Terrace (1938) and Royal Palm Gardens on Indiana Avenue; Ferguson Hotel; Fun Bowl bowling alley (1941). After TOBA collapsed, they used bandleader Walter Barnes and his contacts to bring top Black acts to Indianapolis. In 1940 a clampdown under Mayor Reginald H. Sullivan revoked their business licenses; they then opened **Ferguson Brothers** booking agency (1941; some sources 1940), called "the most powerful Black-owned talent agency in the country," booking territory bands and touring them through the South for Black audiences. Denver went bankrupt in 1953. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Denver D. Ferguson"; Indianapolis Encyclopedia, read 2026-11-20). [K8, K14, P8, SM:power-rising-step-by-step, SM:dangerous-or-fascinating-figure-or-system]
- **The model (per Lauterbach, summarized in a Vassar review):** Ferguson applied numbers-game principles to booking: each Black customer had little money, but nickels and dimes across many bands and venues added up; he took a percentage of each show. His contracts had a guaranteed performer fee, a deposit, and typically a 50/50 split of the surplus; a road manager counted attendance and collected the cash; this replaced the older "first money in the door" arrangement. He coordinated regional promoters into one circuit: Don Robey (Houston), Frank Painia (New Orleans, Dew Drop), Clint Brantley (Macon), promoters in Florida, Sunbeam Mitchell (Memphis). **SOURCED** (pages.vassar.edu/musicalurbanism, review of Lauterbach). Graft and bribery, numbers, bootlegging and commercial sex as parts of the underground economy behind it. **SOURCED** (Reason, 2011, summarizing WSJ review). Local venues were often controlled by one local entrepreneur who also dealt in real estate, gambling, liquor and entertainment. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet). [K8, K6]
- **Information channel:** Walter Barnes (Royal Creolians, Chicago) toured the South half of each year (November-April by 1936) and wrote a *Chicago Defender* column that described the self-sustaining Black business districts of Southern cities (the column's own term for them is in the slang file) and reported where bands were touring, functioning as "road intelligence"; he solicited promoters and clubs through the paper. **SOURCED** for the column's role (Vassar review); tour season **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K5, K13]
- **Black business districts as the base:** segregated cities had districts each with a main street of bars, restaurants and barbershops; Madam C.J. Walker's hair business brought millions of dollars into Indianapolis's Black community; Lauterbach profiles people, including women, who ran bars, agencies and clubs. **SOURCED** (KCUR/NPR, Ed Ward review, 10 Jan 2012). [K4, K8]

**Venues, travel and daily work** [K5, K7]
- Venue types: dance halls, roadside shacks, storefront back rooms, converted churches; wooden buildings on the edge of town holding about 600, with food and drink sold on site; many with no indoor toilets. Bands worked one-night stands, hundreds of miles a day, in cheap buses or cars with instruments tied to the roof. In towns with no Black hotel, Black families put up touring musicians in their homes. Memphis's Mitchell Hotel became an informal musicians' hiring hub by 1949. Wartime factors (1943): shellac rationing halted record manufacturing and a bus ban hit big bands. **SOURCED** (Vassar review). [K7, R (no-fixed-rest travel; homes as rest stops)]
- **Pay:** small venues paid poorly (Lighter, *Historical Dictionary of American Slang*). **SOURCED** (GBH 2014). Bobby Rush (a long-time circuit headliner) has said pay depended on how hungry the performer was, that sometimes pay was a plate of food, that promoters sometimes ran off with the money, and that he once paid sidemen $5-5.50 a night. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of USA Today 2021, Jazz Weekly).
- **Lodging network:** *The Negro Motorist Green Book* (Victor and Alma Green, Harlem, 1936-1966; paused during WWII rationing; circulation over 2 million by 1962; over 10,000 businesses listed; over 1,400 tourist homes, i.e., private homes renting rooms; sold through Esso stations; postal workers solicited listings along their routes). Sundown towns barred Black people after dark. At least half of listed businesses closed within ten years of integration. **SOURCED** (NPS, "The Green Book: An Historic Context"). Musicians, including circuit musicians, passed lodging addresses by word of mouth. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of a Smithsonian page). [K5, K6, K9]

### C. Places where it was practiced (three documented examples, plus others)
**(i) Indiana Avenue, Indianapolis (the hub)**
- Black commercial and entertainment corridor; the Walker Building (1927; Madam C.J. Walker company) is among few survivors; Walker Theater reopened 1988 and hosts jazz and theater. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of Clio, IUPUI, IBJ). The numbers game thrived on the Avenue; a local operator was known as its leading numbers man. **SOURCED** (WFYI, *Echoes of Indiana Avenue*, 11 Nov 2022). Decline: 1950s urban renewal led by the Indianapolis Redevelopment Commission cleared residents and sold land to Indiana University (IUPUI campus); Interstate 65; integration. Sources date the decline variously from the 1950s to the late 1960s. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; IUPUI page 404, Indiana Public Media redirect not followed). The Fergusons closed their clubs as the Avenue declined in the 1950s. **SOURCED** (Indianapolis Encyclopedia). [K9]

**(ii) Club Ebony, Indianola, Mississippi (small Delta town; Indianola about 9,000 people)**
- Built by Johnny Jones, who bought the property in November 1945 with his wife Josephine; opened about 1948; Jones called it "the South's largest and finest night club"; built for big bands (Jimmie Lunceford, Count Basie); Jones let plantation workers in on credit and collected after payday; Jones paid high fees for big names, contributing to his financial trouble; after his death (May 1950) it was run under a white bootlegger from Leland who had lent him money; Ruby Edwards bought it 1958; Willie and Mary Shepard rented it 1974, bought 1975, Mary ran it 34 years until 2008; B.B. King bought it 2008 and gave it to the B.B. King Museum and Delta Interpretive Center; since 1980 it hosts the closing night of King's annual homecoming festival. **SOURCED** (Mississippi Blues Trail marker No. 84, msbluestrail.org/?p=329). Social role: homes were small and families large, so the club was where people ate, danced and socialized; bonded and bootleg whiskey sold. The nightly format stopped being viable; structural damage after 2021; about $800,000 in repairs funded by a South Arts grant and the City of Indianola tourism tax; reopened May 2023. Context: the Ludlow textile plant brought postwar money; a nearby street was known for gambling. **SOURCED** (ArtDaily, May 2023). [K8, K10, K12, K4, P4]

**(iii) Royal Peacock, Auburn Avenue, Atlanta (big-city stop)**
- Formerly the Top Hat Club (185/186½ Auburn Ave); bought in early 1949 for $31,000 by Carrie Cunningham, a former entertainer with the Silas Green tent show, who also owned the Royal Hotel and housed distinguished guests there; peacock decor, floor show, house band, emcee; rented for anniversary dances, Sweet 16 parties, Christmas gatherings, and fundraisers for the Boy Scouts and the waiters' union; promoted through posters, WERD radio, the *Atlanta Daily World* and *Atlanta Inquirer*; popular acts played two shows a night; Henry Wynn (Supersonic Attractions) acquired it 1959. Decline: by the 1970s integration meant it was no longer one of few places Black audiences could see live acts; the community shifted west; parking shortages as car ownership grew; night-time crime. **SOURCED** (Atlanta History Center, "Vanishing Black Atlanta," 6 Jul 2022). Auburn Avenue: renamed from Wheat Street in 1893 at white petitioners' request; Atlanta Life Insurance (1905), Standard Life (1911), Citizens Trust Bank; *Atlanta Daily World*; Ebenezer Baptist and Big Bethel AME; NAACP, Odd Fellows, Masons, Urban League offices; split by the Downtown Connector freeway; National Historic Landmark 1976. **SOURCED** (New Georgia Encyclopedia). [K4, K8, K9, K10]

**Other documented places (briefer)**

### D. How the defining feature (segregation and mobility) shaped those things
- The circuit existed because Black performers were barred from white venues and lodging. **SOURCED.** Enforcement at mixed shows: police strung a rope down a Raleigh dance floor (early 1950s) and the larger crowd's race decided whose dance it was; balconies reserved by race; armed police at a Birmingham show; a whites-only second show refused by Jackie Wilson in Little Rock (1960) led to his entourage being chased out by armed residents; New Orleans police arrested a biracial jam session (1957). Black performers often could not use the front entrance (Atlanta). **SOURCED** (Rolling Stone, Steve Knopper, updated 23 Feb 2021). [K6, K9, SM:man-versus-society]
- The circuit's venues doubled as the community's general social hall (Club Ebony; Liberty Theater; Royal Peacock's private-event rentals). [K10]
- Its capital came from informal economies (numbers printing and gambling, bootleg liquor). [K8]

### E. Structural features
- **Central agency + local promoters + percentage deals** (guarantee, deposit, split, road manager counting the house). [K8, K14]

## 3 · #36 Pullman porters as carriers of news and music

**Claim to test:** railway workers who carried newspapers, records and word of opportunity along the lines, so reputations traveled with the traffic (K14, K13).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED for newspapers and word of opportunity; CONFIRMED but less firmly documented for records and music.** (1) Newspapers: well documented. The *Chicago Defender*'s publisher arranged an informal distribution system with porters who carried the paper south, sometimes against Southern laws; Southern cities banned the paper; porters also passed on papers passengers left behind and told Southern Black communities how to travel north and what to expect. (2) Records and music: the main source is one book (Larry Tye, *Rising from the Rails*, 2004), which reports porters buying records in Chicago and New York and reselling them in Southern towns where stores did not stock Black artists, and porters mixing with traveling musicians; this is anecdotal, not quantified. The company also ran porter vocal quartets that sang on trains and radio and recorded in the 1920s. (3) Beyond the claim: the porters' greater historical weight was as an **organized labor network** (the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, 1925; first major contract between a Black union and a corporation, 1937) whose members and wives founded community institutions in the rail-terminal neighborhoods (Chicago, West Oakland, Montreal, Vancouver) and whose leaders later organized civil-rights actions. One historian cautions that the real recruiters for northern migration were Black migrants themselves, not porters.

### A. Snapshot (the occupation itself)
- **What:** sleeping-car attendants employed by the Pullman Company (and in Canada by the CPR and others), carrying baggage, making berths, shining shoes, serving passengers; maids served women passengers and children. George Pullman hired formerly enslaved men from the late 1860s and by then hired only African Americans as porters. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Pullman porter," read 2026-11-20). Pullman service in the U.S. ended 31 Dec 1968. **SOURCED.**
- **Scale:** 1926: over 10,000 porters and about 200 maids (Wikipedia); a 1926 union survey counted 12,000 porters and waiters. **SOURCED.** Pullman was the nation's largest employer of African Americans; porters were 44% of the Pullman rail-car operation workforce. **SOURCED** (NPS, Pullman National Monument, "Pullman Porters"). Over 20,000 Black workers at peak including about 12,000 porters. **SOURCED** (NPCA, "The Women Behind the Brotherhood"). 1969: 325 porters, average age 63. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- **Pay and hours (1926 union report):** minimum $72.50/month, average $78.11, average tips $58.15/month; porters paid their own meals, lodging, uniforms and shoe-shine supplies (average $33.82/month); 400 hours or 11,000 miles a month for full pay; overnight sleep of 3-4 hours deducted from pay; unpaid early arrival to prepare cars; charged for towels or pitchers passengers stole. Pullman paid over $10 million in dividends in 1925. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). 1937 contract: guaranteed 240-hour month, overtime after 260; porters' minimum $77.50 to $89.50; maids' $75.00 to $87.00. **SOURCED** (Driehaus Museum blog). Contract date given as 25 Aug 1937 (Driehaus, WTTW) or 25 Apr 1937 (Britannica snippet). [K7, K8]

### B. What else exists around the occupation (institutions, rules, work and life)
**Rules and discipline** [K6]
- Every porter was required to answer to the company founder's first name while working, whatever his own name; passengers used it as if porters were the founder's servants. A union-linked society formed in protest (the Society for the Prevention of Calling Sleeping Car Porters "George," claimed 31,000 members) and in 1926 Pullman installed racks showing each porter's real name; in Canada name plaques were won in the 1945 contract. **SOURCED** (Newberry Library, Pullman digital collection, "On the Trains"; Wikipedia; Canadian Encyclopedia, "Sleeping Car Porters in Canada"). (The forms of address themselves are recorded in the slang file.)
- Porters were held to strict standards of dress, conduct and bearing; could be fired without appeal if white passengers, especially white women, complained they were too familiar; signed loyalty oaths never to join a union; the company followed individual workers and spied on union meetings; a discharge registry (1907-1912) and car service rules (1893) survive. **SOURCED** (Newberry). Former porters (1982 film) said a complaint about shoe-shining or a missed greeting meant reprimand or about ten days' suspension, with no hearing; a company instructor reported on porters and recorded who attended union meetings. **SOURCED** (Folkstreams transcript of *Miles of Smiles, Years of Struggle*, 1982). A 1925 maids' instruction banned rouge and powder; maids could be fired for pregnancy and were barred from mixing with male staff. **SOURCED** (Newberry interview with Miriam Thaggert, 2022; NPCA).
- **The company town:** Pullman, Ill. (Chicago) had no saloons; the Hotel Florence held the only bar, for hotel guests not residents; 3,000 workers struck 11 May 1894 when wages were cut but company rents and prices were not; the national boycott was broken by federal troops; the town had very few, if any, Black residents. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of WTTW, Streetwise, Gizmodo); strike sequence **SOURCED** (NPS, "The Pullman Story Part 2"). [K6, K9]

**Labor institutions** [K1, K14]
- **Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters (BSCP):** formed 25 Aug 1925 in Harlem (500 porters; motto "Fight or Be Slaves"), led by A. Philip Randolph for four decades; first Black union chartered by the AFL (1935); first major labor agreement between a Black union and a corporation (1937), with grievance procedures; acted as a civil-rights organization into the 1960s; merged 1978. **SOURCED** (NPS; Wikipedia). White railway brotherhoods and the American Railway Union barred Black members. **SOURCED** (NPS) / ARU exclusion **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Women's side:** organizing was done largely by porters' wives because open supporters were fired; one wife visited more than 300 porters' homes around Washington, D.C., held meetings at her house with the blinds drawn, and some wives signed their husbands up and paid the dues; Ladies' Auxiliaries ran dinners and conventions; the union dropped "& Maids" from its name in 1929; the 1937 contract raised maids' pay but cost them seniority. **SOURCED** (NPCA). The union's social program (debate clubs, lectures, summer socials, balls and dances) was as important as petitions and helped fund it. **UNVERIFIED** (Newberry snippets). [K4, K10]
- **Civil rights:** E. D. Nixon, a porter and head of the Montgomery BSCP branch, helped organize the 1955 Montgomery bus boycott and, being often away on runs, enlisted a young minister new to the city to lead it in his absence. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of Encyclopedia of Alabama, NPR). Former porters include Thurgood Marshall, Malcolm X and Gordon Parks. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). [SM:competence-under-pressure, SM:man-versus-society]

**Carrying news** [K5, K13]
- Robert S. Abbott (founded *Chicago Defender* 1905) arranged with Pullman porters to carry the paper south by rail, sometimes against Southern state laws; classified ads offered jobs, housing and transport north; copies were read aloud in barbershops and churches; Southern authorities detained would-be migrants at platforms on "vagrancy" grounds or tore up tickets. **SOURCED** (Driehaus Museum blog). Porters tossed bundles from moving trains into Black neighborhoods and told communities how to travel north and what they would find; some towns banned the paper because it threatened cheap Southern labor. **SOURCED** (America's Black Holocaust Museum, "How the Pullman Porters Advanced Black Culture"). After a 1915 anti-lynching slogan many Southern cities banned it; by 1919 the South supplied three-fourths of about 130,000 circulation; a 64-page list of distribution points was obtained by Military Intelligence in 1919; copies reached over 1,500 Southern towns. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of Encyclopedia of Chicago; its page failed with an SSL error). Porters also passed on papers and magazines passengers left behind. **SOURCED** (Syncopated Times review of Tye, 1 Feb 2018). A working paper (A. Moorthy) studies the paper's effect on migration using porter distribution along the Illinois Central. **UNVERIFIED** (PDF too large to read). Counterpoint: historian James Grossman says the true northern labor agents were Black migrants themselves. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of History.com).

**Carrying music** [K13, R]
- Per Tye: porters in Chicago and New York bought new records by Black blues singers and resold them in the South, often at twice the price, where local stores did not stock Black artists; they mingled with traveling musicians in club cars, listened and learned; they heard blues in rural places and carried it back to the cities. **SOURCED as Tye's claims** (Syncopated Times, 2018). The company gave in-house a cappella quartet lessons; several porter quartets performed under the company name at once, on long-haul trains and on radio; a Chicago porter quartet recorded for Paramount in the late 1920s. **SOURCED** (The Conversation, "The forgotten voices of race records"). Songs about the job were recorded as early as June 1921 (OKeh). **UNVERIFIED** (discography snippets). [K13, K14]

**Layovers and daily life** [K7, K10]
- At the end of the line porters stayed in company quarters, often poor, and passed the time sleeping and playing cards (bid whist). Early porters got about two and a half hours' sleep. The job carried status with family and friends; wages helped buy homes and send children to college. **SOURCED** (Folkstreams 1982 transcript). Porters spent overnight stops in segregated Black districts where lodging, restaurants and entertainment would serve them. **SOURCED** (ABHM). Porters' extra wages sent children to historically Black colleges. **SOURCED** (ABHM). [R (rest quarters at the end of the line)]

### C. Places where porters lived and built institutions (three documented examples, plus others)
**(i) Chicago (Pullman company headquarters; Bronzeville)**
- Porters and maids lived near the rail lines in neighborhoods such as Bronzeville; the director of the A. ### D. How the defining feature (mobility under segregation) shaped those things

## 4 · #37 New Orleans and Congo Square

**Claim to test:** a port where many origins met and music grew from that mixture in a public gathering space (K13, K4).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with two corrections.** (1) The gathering space was not freely chosen: Sunday rest came from the 1724 Code Noir, but there was **no right to assemble**; in 1786 the governor pushed gatherings to Sunday afternoons after Mass, and an **1817 city ordinance confined** enslaved people's dancing to the one site the mayor appointed. Congo Square was thus a space of permitted, watched assembly that was also a market. (2) The "mixture" was layered in waves that are well documented: Senegambians under French rule, Kongo-Angolans under Spanish and American rule, about 9,000-10,000 refugees from Saint-Domingue via Cuba in 1809-10 (roughly doubling the city), then Irish, German and Sicilian immigrants through the second-busiest U.S. immigrant port before the Civil War, and later Hondurans and Vietnamese. (3) Jazz did not grow from one square: historians and the National Park Service stress neighborhood institutions (benevolent societies and their halls, brass bands, parks, dance halls) rather than the Storyville legend. The square's dances ended after Reconstruction; the space has had a revival since the 1970s-1990s (Armstrong Park, Jazz Fest's first year, Sunday drum circles).

### A. Snapshot
- **New Orleans:** population 383,997 (2020 census); metro 1,271,845 (2020). **UNVERIFIED** (Census QuickFacts / Wikipedia snippets). Largest resident industries 2024: health care (25,858), education (23,813), accommodation and food (17,889). **UNVERIFIED** (Data USA snippet). Visitors 2024: 19.08 million (2023: 17.93 million; 2019 peak 19.75 million); visitor spending about $10.0-10.4 billion; tourism and hospitality about 80,000-85,000 jobs. **UNVERIFIED** (New Orleans & Company release snippets). Port: 1.2 million cruise passenger movements in 2024 (a record); cargo activity estimated at $101.5 billion total U.S. economic value ($31.5 billion in Louisiana) in a 2024 study. **UNVERIFIED** (Port NOLA snippets).
- **Congo Square:** a public plot on North Rampart Street between St. Ann and St. Peter, now the southwest corner of Louis Armstrong Park in the Tremé; 2.35 acres, about half its 19th-century size; known by many names over time (official name Beauregard Square from an 1893 ordinance; "Congo Square" made official by unanimous City Council ordinance in 2011); National Register 1993. **SOURCED** (64 Parishes, "Congo Square," read 2026-11-20). [K+ naming, P6]

### B. What else exists there (history, economy, institutions)
**The Sunday gatherings** [K2, K4, K6, K12, K13]
- 1724 Code Noir made Sundays non-work days for everyone, including the enslaved; no law granted a right to assemble; before 1817 people gathered on Sunday afternoons at levees, backyards, plantations and other squares; the 1817 ordinance restricted enslaved people's dancing and gatherings to one location appointed by the mayor. Origins of participants: under French rule about two-thirds from Senegambia, others from the Bight of Benin and Kongo-Angola; under Spanish and American rule mostly Kongo-Angola; also arrivals via Saint-Domingue and Cuba; attended by field hands, domestic workers, hired-out enslaved people and free people of color; crowds in the hundreds, some reports thousands. Instruments: drums of barrels and hollow logs, banza, kalimba, panpipes, gourd rattles, balafons, jawbones, horns; later fiddles, triangles, tambourines. Dances included the Calinda, Bamboula, Congo (Chica), Juba. **Sunday market:** the enslaved earned money on Sundays and bought from market women: pecan pies, pralines, roasted peanuts, molasses candy, calas (rice cakes), coffee, lemonade, ginger beer. The square also hosted ball games, horse shows, bullfights, cockfights, circuses, military drills, public executions and slave auctions. 1864: more than 20,000 gathered there to celebrate the Emancipation Proclamation. **SOURCED** (64 Parishes).
- 1786: the first known reference to the place as "Congo" is a bishop's complaint that performances began before Sunday Mass ended; Governor Miró then ruled gatherings could not start until Sunday afternoon after services. Benjamin Latrobe (1819) saw 500-600 people in circles of dancers and musicians and sketched instruments. Accounts survive mostly from white writers who, per Freddi Williams Evans, documented people they did not understand. Per Jerah Johnson (1995), gatherings ran regularly until the mid-1800s, revived briefly after Union occupation (1862), ended in the repression after Reconstruction, and continued in private settings and on plantations for about 30 more years. **SOURCED** (Robert H. Cataliotti, "Crossroads at Congo Square," Smithsonian Folkways magazine, Winter/Spring 2015; PDF read via local text extraction).

**The port and the mixture** [K9, P9]
- **Slave trade:** New Orleans was the largest slave market in the U.S. by most accounts (one count: more than 135,000 people sold; a marker: more than 100,000 between 1804 and 1862); sales were spread across the city rather than one market building; demand tracked cotton and sugar. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of HNOC "Purchased Lives," Smithsonian, 64 Parishes). Enslaved Africans brought from 1721 by the Company of the Indies, mostly Senegambian. **SOURCED** (HNOC, "Coming to New Orleans" series).
- **Saint-Domingue refugees:** a 1810 wave from Cuba roughly doubled the enslaved population and nearly tripled the free population of color within months. **SOURCED** (HNOC). About 9,000-10,000 people (2,731 white, 3,102 free people of color, 3,226 enslaved in one count). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Later waves:** second only to New York in international arrivals in the 30 years before the Civil War; Irish and Germans (1830s-1850s, famine and upheaval); Central European Jews; Sicilians (large wave from the 1880s; a merchant class shaping the lower French Quarter); Chinese and Filipinos dominating Gulf dried-shrimp work in the late 1800s; Hondurans via the banana trade (by 1962 the largest Honduran population outside Honduras); Lebanese from the 1880s; Catholic Vietnamese after 1975 (New Orleans East, West Bank); Mexicans doubled after Katrina (rebuilding work). Indigenous Chickasaw, Chitimacha, Choctaw and others lived in and traveled through the area before 1718/1722; many were enslaved. **SOURCED** (HNOC). Irish worked as draymen, screwmen, canal and rail builders. **UNVERIFIED** (64 Parishes snippet).

**Institutions** [K1, K14]
- **Benevolent and mutual-aid societies:** Perseverance Benevolent and Mutual Aid Association (1783, earliest recorded); Société d'Economie et d'Assistance Mutuelle (1836, free Black leaders, many educators; minutes in French). In 1875 the Economie required members to parade in black-and-white attire with music for special events and funerals (minutes used the English term "brass band"); from 1883 it held annual anniversary brass-band parades, which HNOC identifies as the origin of today's second lines. By 1900 more than 200 Black organizations, with about half of the Black population belonging to at least one. Mid-20th century: neighborhood-based social aid and pleasure clubs (SAPCs) emerged, focused on charity, social events and parades. **SOURCED** (HNOC, Eric Seiferth, 24 Feb 2021). Dues served as health and burial insurance because white insurers refused Black customers; the Depression and Black insurance companies reduced benevolent societies by the 1930s; SAPCs hold Sunday second lines in fall and spring; about 45-70 clubs today; four months after Katrina a coalition of 29 clubs held a large second line in Tremé supporting displaced residents' right to return. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Second-line permits and fees (K6, K8, K9):** after shootings in crowds at two 2006 second lines, police raised escort fees from about $1,200 to $3,760 plus a $10,000 state-law bond; the police chief had broad discretion; the SAPC Task Force sued (ACLU of Louisiana, 16 Nov 2006). **SOURCED** (ACLU press release). A 2007 court-accepted agreement let one club parade for $2,400 instead of $7,500. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Mardi Gras Indians (Black Masking Indians):** origins contested (one account: refuge of escaped enslaved people with Houma, Chitimacha and other tribes; another: first accounts after Buffalo Bill's Wild West show visit, 1884-85); a tribe's hierarchy has a leader with deputies and queens, a scout who walks blocks ahead, a signaler with a flag, and a path-clearer (the role titles are in the slang file); weekly practices in neighborhood bars; each leader sews a new beaded and feathered suit every year, unveiled Mardi Gras morning; Uptown pictorial panels vs Downtown three-dimensional designs; parade on Mardi Gras Day, St. Joseph's night (19 March) and a spring Sunday; violent clashes between rival tribes declined in the later 20th century under police pressure and chiefs who redirected rivalry into suits, songs and language, notably Allison "Tootie" Montana (a plasterer by trade) of the Yellow Pocahontas. **SOURCED** (64 Parishes, "Mardi Gras Indians," updated 21 Feb 2020). A Mardi Gras Indian Council formed 1985; suits take a year and cost thousands. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K2, K14, K12, SM:competence-under-pressure]
- **Baby Dolls:** women's masking tradition that emerged about 1912 among sex workers in the segregated Black red-light district (boundaries set about 1897 by ordinance); first group led by Beatrice Hill, Leona Tate, Althea Brown; costumes of bonnets, short skirts, bloomers; on Mardi Gras they sang bawdy songs, smoked cigars, staged boastful confrontations and threw money; spread to other women (especially in Tremé) and men; declined mid-century; revived (several current groups); 2013 Presbytère installation. **SOURCED** (64 Parishes, "Baby Dolls"). [K2, K4]
- **Storyville (1897-1917):** legal red-light district of about 38 blocks created by ordinance; closed under Navy pressure in 1917; most structures removed for the Iberville housing project in the 1940s. The NPS states that jazz was not born in Storyville, though the district exposed it to a wider audience. **SOURCED** (NPS, New Orleans Jazz NHP "Jazz Map"); dates and Navy role **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K6, K9]
- **Musicians' support:** New Orleans Musicians' Clinic (affordable medical care for musicians; founding year NOT FOUND). Musicians' Village (Upper Ninth Ward; Habitat for Humanity with two musicians; 81 houses, five duplexes and the Ellis Marsalis Center for Music, for musicians who lost homes in Katrina); in Dec 2025 the City Council asked for transparency after reports that Habitat sold several properties there. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). ## 5 · #113 Filipino house bands across Asia

**Claim to test:** musicians who for a century traveled from port to port and hotel to hotel as house bands, so a whole region's live music was carried by people in transit (K13, K7).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED in outline; the "century" is accurate (late 1800s to now), but the tradition is a sequence of distinct circuits, each with its own rules and end.** (1) **Colonial-era port cities:** Filipino players were recruited by the Shanghai Municipal Orchestra from 1878 (cheaper than Europeans, close by) and by the 1920s-30s dominated Shanghai's cabaret jazz bands; also hotel and dance-hall work in Tokyo (1920s), Singapore (1930s-50s) and colonial dinners across Asia. (2) **Postwar Cold War circuit:** after Shanghai's clubs closed (1949) musicians moved to Hong Kong, where they founded the musicians' union (1948) and arranged and played for the city's pop and film industry; U.S. base clubs (Clark, Subic/Olongapo, Okinawa, Japan) and Vietnam-war R&R economies (Bangkok) multiplied jobs. (3) **State-managed export era:** from 1994 the Philippine government required performers to hold an Artist Record Book; Japan's "entertainer" stream (tens of thousands a year, many of them actually club hostesses) collapsed after 2005 trafficking reforms. (4) **Today:** smaller flows (about 1,500-2,000+ musicians deployed a year) to Gulf hotels, cruise ships, Chinese and Southeast Asian hotel lounges, under host-country sponsorship systems. **Narrowing:** the music carried was mostly the current Western repertoire (jazz, then rock, then pop covers) rather than Filipino music; the bands' selling points as stated by employers and agents were skill, versatility and **low cost**. Local musicians in several cities learned from them, and in some cities local unions complained about the competition.

### A. Snapshot (the tradition itself)
- **Who:** Filipino instrumentalists and singers working abroad on contract as resident (house) bands in hotels, nightclubs, cabarets, base clubs, cruise ships and lounges, typically six nights a week. **SOURCED** for six nights a week in Gulf hotels (The New Arab/AP, 15 Nov 2020) and Mui Ne, Vietnam (Tuoi Tre, 29 Apr 2018).
- **Scale (estimates, not official):** a Manila agent estimated 25,000-30,000 Filipino musicians and singers performing abroad at any one time in about 3,000 clubs, hotels, cruise ships and restaurants; overseas pay generally $800-1,500 a month. **SOURCED** (AFP in Gulf News, updated 15 Sep 2018). Official deployments: more than 2,000 musicians a year 2015-2019 (POEA, per Rappler). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; Rappler 403; POEA table not reached). NYT (31 Jan 2013, via a secondary page): over 40,000 entertainers left in 2002, mostly for Japan; departures fell from 43,818 (2004) to 4,050 (2006), then about 1,500-2,000 a year; pay from about $400 a month to $2,000 in five-star hotels, $800-1,500 on cruise ships; top destinations Japan, Malaysia, South Korea, China. **UNVERIFIED** (secondhand via factsanddetails.com; original not read).

### B. What else exists around it (origins, institutions, rules, work and life)
**Origins at home** [K14]
- **Philippine Constabulary Band:** formed under the U.S. colonial constabulary (active 1901/1902 to 1942), first directed by Walter Loving, an African American army bandmaster; he recruited from community ensembles in small Luzon towns, some members having played in Spanish Army bands; about 300 clarinetists auditioned for eight places; 85 players (1909), 100 (1937); played the 1904 St. Louis fair, led Taft's 1909 inaugural parade, gave 115 concerts at the 1915 Panama-Pacific Exposition; most members killed during the Japanese occupation; reconstituted 1946. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Philippine Constabulary Band"). [K14, P8]
- Commentators trace musicians' versatility to Spanish-era church and military music and U.S.-era records and dance music. **SOURCED as claims** (New Arab/AP 2020; Globe and Mail, 8 Mar 2012). A 19th-century source called Philippine bands "one of the chief articles of export from Manila." **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of an academic paper).
- **Home training grounds:** U.S. base clubs at Clark (Pampanga) and Sangley Point (Cavite) in the 1940s-50s; Manila hotel and club venues (Here's Howe at the Shellborne Hotel, Manhattan Club, Club Celebrity, Café Indonesia, Manila Hotel and others); singers rose through radio and TV talent contests; first-night pay of 10 pesos when the minimum wage was 6. **SOURCED** (Krina Cayabyab, "Filipina Singers and Jazz in Post-Colonial Manila (1946-1970)," *Jazz and Culture* / Rutgers JJS). Olongapo's clubs around the Subic naval base were home to many bands for about two decades. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). In 2012 Manila club members earned under 600 pesos (about $14) a night for up to four sets, plus tips of 100-200 pesos per request. **SOURCED** (Globe and Mail 2012). Agents scout village fiestas, singing contests and karaoke bars. **SOURCED** (AFP/Gulf News).

**Regulation and recruitment** [K6, K1]
- **Artist Record Book (ARB):** POEA Department Order 3-C (May 1994) defined it as a certificate of competence; issued only to performers granted a competency certificate (fee P300); mandatory for contract processing from 1 Oct 1994; only musicians, singers, dancers and novelty acts holding an ARB could be deployed; minimum age 21 (1996 rules); three-year validity; later replaced by an Artist Accreditation Card. A 1991 circular already limited deployment to legitimate performing artists with a one-year local track record. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of Philippine Supreme Court e-library documents). [K6]
- **Japan:** Filipino "entertainers" entering Japan rose from the late 1970s; 80,048 (2003), 82,741 (2004), about 60% of all foreign entertainers; most worked as club hostesses (serving drinks, talking, singing with customers, required to date customers by day) rather than stage performers, which the Immigration Act did not permit; from early 2005 Japan required two years' study or experience abroad and stopped accepting the ARB/AAC; from June 2006 employers had to pay at least 200,000 yen a month under written contract. **SOURCED** (HURIGHTS Osaka, *FOCUS* vol. 43, March 2006). Entries fell from nearly 80,000 (2004) to about 8,000 (2005). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets citing U.S. TIP reports). After the crackdown POEA stopped holding auditions and relied on licensed agencies; Japan found some women deployed as musicians could not play instruments. **UNVERIFIED** (NYT 2013 via secondary page). [K6, K9]
- **Host-country rules:** UAE kafala ties residency to the job, so job loss cancels the visa. **SOURCED** (New Arab/AP). Malaysia requires PUSPAL approval before a professional visit pass for any foreign performer, up to 90 days per application, with 2024-25 conduct rules (e.g., no cross-dressing by foreign male performers, limits on Islamic holiday eves). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). Singapore: a 1985 change made it easy for foreign musicians to work there, bringing in many Filipino entertainers and making work harder for Singaporean musicians. **SOURCED** (Esplanade, "A Jazz To Call Our Own," Sinclair Ang, 20 Jul 2018). Cruise ships: musicians sign ship's articles under flag-of-convenience law and the captain's discipline; blood-alcohol limit 0.05%; passenger relationships forbidden. **SOURCED** (David Cashman, *Ethnomusicology Review* 19, 2014). [K6]

**Work and life** [K7, K5, R]
- **Gulf (Dubai):** bands in hotel bars and lounges, many on six-night contracts; housed in employer-controlled dormitories, sometimes eight to a room; in 2020 a band advocacy group (UAE Filipino Bands Alliance) said about 80% of Filipino artists had visas canceled and 70% never got promised end-of-service pay; British vocalists reportedly earned in days what Filipinos earned in a month; a scholar said Filipino bands are cheaper to hire; one manager locked 11 performers in their dorm for months, then the landlord evicted them; returning musicians took pay cuts of 65%. **SOURCED** (New Arab/AP, 15 Nov 2020). [K9, K7]
- **Cruise ships:** contracts about four months; bands of 5-9; production shows twice a night; shared cabins; crew bar as the social center; musicians rank as "staff" between officers and crew; cruise lines began hiring Filipino and Eastern European musicians because they cost less; Filipinos described by informants as strong improvisers, weaker sight-readers. **SOURCED** (Cashman 2014). [K10, K7]
- **Vietnam:** one family band has played six nights a week at a resort in Mui Ne for about ten years; the parents had worked Dubai, Japan, Italy and Europe in the 1980s, came to a Saigon hotel in 1997, and moved to Mui Ne in 2007 after the Saigon scene grew competitive; peak arrival of Filipino musicians 2001-02 amid economic and political trouble at home; children trained from about age six. **SOURCED** (Tuoi Tre, 29 Apr 2018). [K4, K7]
- **South Korea:** a band manager sending about two bands a month since 2008; demo tapes reviewed by a board in Seoul (about a month); a six-month booking at $800 a month each vs $3.50 a night at home. **SOURCED** (AFP/Gulf News).

### C. Places where it was or is practiced (three documented examples, plus others)
**(i) Shanghai (1878-1949)**
- Shanghai Municipal Orchestra began recruiting Filipino players in 1878 because of proximity and lower fees than Europeans; by the mid-1920s most of its players were Filipino; some moved into jazz; Filipino bandleaders were the mainstay of the cabarets; dance halls were often divided by class and race; a Filipino bandleader's act once ranked above an American band in a *China Press* poll; a Chinese bandleader deliberately recruited tall northern Chinese musicians to distinguish his band from the mostly Filipino ones; Filipino musicians formed closer ties with local artists and audiences than many Americans did; after Shanghai's nightclubs closed in 1949 they moved to Hong Kong. **SOURCED** (Sixth Tone, "The Ups and Downs of Shanghai's 'Saint of Songs'"). A Filipino Musicians' Union committee existed in Shanghai in 1942; Filipino bands in cabarets were usually five or six players; a 1928 *China Weekly Review* credited Filipino musicians with launching the Chinese jazz age; a historian wrote that Filipino musicians were "synonymous with jazz in old Shanghai." **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of ABS-CBN, Andrew Field's site, Cambridge). A Philippine Musicians Association of Shanghai was formed in 2007. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; consulate page 403). [K13, K8, K1]

**(ii) Hong Kong (1948-present)**
- Hong Kong Musicians Union founded 1948 as Filipino musicians grew in number (first led by a British musician, then a Filipino musician); musicians rented flats on Hillwood Road, Tsim Sha Tsui, for jam sessions; union bought a Union House in Mirador Mansion in 1957; general assemblies at the Filipino Club, Tsim Sha Tsui; an annual Concert in the Park with the Philippine Consulate and the HK government (Kowloon Park, later the Cultural Center piazza). **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Filipino NGOs in Hong Kong"). Filipino arrangers filled a gap for Shanghai songwriters who could not arrange their own songs in 1950s Hong Kong; a local lyricist praised their sight-reading and speed; a leading Hong Kong composer was trained by Filipino musicians. **SOURCED** (Sixth Tone) / composer **UNVERIFIED**. 1960s Filipino rock bands were central to the scene; work dried up in the 1970s as disco records replaced live combos; later karaoke and Canto-pop displaced lounge acts; Filipino cover bands were welcome partly because they cost less than Western or Chinese bands; Wan Chai house bands play up to six nights a week. **UNVERIFIED** (SCMP and Time Out snippets; both 403). In 2020 the union's representative said work collapsed with the protests and then the pandemic. **UNVERIFIED** (NYT via secondary page). Scholarship: Lee William Watkins, *Humanities Diliman* (2010), on Filipino musicians' everyday life and racial slurs from hotel clients. **UNVERIFIED** (abstract snippet; journal PDF certificate expired). [K1, K4, K10, K13]

**(iii) Dubai / the Gulf (late 20th century-present)**
- See B above: hotel bars and lounges, dormitory housing, kafala sponsorship, the 2020 collapse. Dubai "drew legions of Filipino cover bands" as it grew from a pearling port into a regional party capital; a Manila agent said up to 60 Dubai venues sought cover groups (2012). **SOURCED** (New Arab/AP 2020; Globe and Mail 2012). [K9, K4]

**Others (briefer)**
- **Japan:** an all-Filipino jazz band at the Imperial Hotel, Tokyo (1924 photo); Filipino musicians brought by American recruiters before WWII and again in the 1950s; a Filipina singer played the Golden Gate Club (1955) with Manila musicians to Japanese and American audiences. **SOURCED** for 1955 (Cayabyab); others **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Okinawa:** Filipino players were role models, teachers and rivals for Okinawan musicians in base clubs; a local pianist learned jazz from a Filipino player in a base club. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of a KAKEN report and CS Monitor).
- **Singapore:** jazz played mainly by Caucasian and Filipino émigrés in hotels, cabarets and dance halls (1930s-50s); local players learned from them; a Filipino bandleader (Alfredo Libio) led the Singapore Musicians' Union and its dance orchestra and negotiated wages for unionized musicians in 1949. **SOURCED** (Esplanade) / Libio **UNVERIFIED** (Roots.gov.sg snippets).
- **Bangkok:** several Filipino members in a 1940s state-linked Thai jazz band; one Filipino guitarist had earlier played in Cebu, Madagascar, Colombo, Bombay and Singapore; Filipinos were valued as exotic and cosmopolitan. **SOURCED** (Benjamin Tausig, *Bangkok After Dark* newsletter, 20 Jul 2021). Filipino musicians dominated Bangkok's hotel and nightclub music in the 1960s; presence declined after 2000. **UNVERIFIED** (Bangkok Post snippets; page behind a paywall redirect, not followed). Filipina singers held long Bangkok hotel residencies 1962-71 and played Sunday jam sessions at jazz clubs; Intercontinental hotel contracts took one couple to Afghanistan, Beirut and Tehran. **SOURCED** (Cayabyab).

### D. How the defining feature (musicians in transit) shaped these things
- Each circuit followed a power and money structure: colonial port elites (Shanghai), U.S. military bases and war economies (Okinawa, Clark, Subic, Vietnam-era Bangkok), then tourism and hospitality growth (Gulf, cruise ships, Southeast Asian hotels). [K8]
- Musicians' own institutions appeared where they concentrated: unions in Shanghai (1942), Hong Kong (1948) and Singapore (Filipino-led, 1949), a Shanghai association (2007), a Gulf advocacy alliance (2020). [K1, K14]
- The home state built a certification and deployment bureaucracy around them (1991-1994 onward). [K6]
- Host musicians both learned from them and saw them as cheaper competition (Singapore 1985; Okinawa; Hong Kong). [K4, K9]

### E. Structural features
- **Contract rhythm:** short contracts (four to six months common), six nights a week, several sets a night, renewal or move on. [K7]
- **Repertoire:** current Western popular music performed close to the record; covers on request. [K13]
- **Housing tied to the employer** (dormitories, ship cabins). [K5, R (shared rest quarters)]
- **Family bands and child training** as a way to keep work in the family. [K14]
- **Exposure to host-country sponsorship systems** (kafala, entertainer visas, performer permits). [K6, K9]

**K6 rules of everyday behavior:** home-country certification (ARB/AAC) and deployment rules; host-country visa and permit regimes; cruise-ship conduct rules (alcohol limit, no passenger relationships); Malaysian conduct rules for foreign performers. As recorded above.
**K11 speech:** musicians speak Philippine languages (Tagalog, Cebuano and others) among themselves and perform mostly in English; in Shanghai and Hong Kong they worked alongside Chinese (Mandarin, Cantonese) musicians. No further note.

## 6 · #110 Daejeon and the song "Daejeon Blues"

**Claim to test:** a city grown at a railway junction where two main lines split, whose best-known song is a blues about parting at the station late at night (K13). Song's year and details to check.
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with corrections.** (1) **Junction:** Daejeon was a small farming area until the Gyeongbu Line (Seoul-Busan) opened a station there on 1 Jan 1905; the Honam Line (to Mokpo) branched there from 1912-1914 (dates differ by source). The junction was **moved to Daejeon from the planned site at Geumsan** because there was no time to bridge the Geum River before the expected Russo-Japanese War; the lines were built to move Jeolla rice to Busan for Japan. The town was in large part a Japanese settlers' railway town (over half of residents Japanese in 1910 and 1925, per a secondary source). (2) **Song:** "대전부르스" ("Daejeon Blues"), 1959; lyrics by Choi Chi-su, a sales staffer of Shin Segi (신세기) Records on a business trip, who saw a couple parting at Daejeon Station before the 0:50 a.m. train to Mokpo (a Honam Line train) and wrote the poem at his inn; music by Kim Bu-hae (about three hours' work); sung by Ahn Jeong-ae; the label's best seller to that date; revived by Cho Yong-pil in 1980. The 0:50 train was moved to 3:05 a.m. the following February. **No lyrics are recorded here.** (3) **Beyond the claim:** the song is now an instrument of civic policy: a 2009 district festival and, from 2023, the city's large "Daejeon 0 o'clock Festival" were built on it, and the melody plays on subway trains arriving at Daejeon Station. The same rail role also made Daejeon the distribution point for U.S. aid flour after the Korean War, which underlies its noodle and bakery culture.

### A. Snapshot
- **Daejeon Metropolitan City:** about 1.44 million people (Feb 2026, Wikipedia infobox); area about 539.85 km²; fifth-largest metropolis; nominal GDP 2023 about KRW 54 trillion; Chungcheong dialect region. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Daejeon," read 2026-11-20). City-reported population 1,439,157 (2024) to 1,440,729 (Dec 2025), the first rise after 12 years of decline, driven by in-movers in their 20s-30s. **UNVERIFIED** (Kukinews snippet).
- **Daejeon Station:** Jeong-dong, Dong-gu; opened 1 Jan 1905 as an ordinary Gyeongbu Line station; junction operations from the Honam Line opening on 6 Mar 1912; station rebuilt 1919; in May 1920 an underpass described as the first in a Korean railway station; a new three-story concrete station completed 28 Dec 1958 during postwar reconstruction; new building 2004 with the high-speed line; SRT from 2016; 14,771,245 passengers in 2008 (KTX about 55%). **SOURCED** (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, "대전역," encykorea.aks.ac.kr). - **Why here:** the junction was moved from Geumsan to Daejeon by Shibusawa Eiichi (Gyeongbu Railway Co. president) because war with Russia was expected and there was too little time to bridge the Geum River; Japan built the line to carry Jeolla rice (Mokpo lacked port facilities) to Busan; Gyeongbu Line groundbreaking 20 Aug 1901, opened 1 Jan 1905. **SOURCED** (Korea JoongAng Daily opinion, Shin Bok-ryong, 29 Oct 2026). - The town itself exists because of the 1905 station and the junction choice. **SOURCED.**
