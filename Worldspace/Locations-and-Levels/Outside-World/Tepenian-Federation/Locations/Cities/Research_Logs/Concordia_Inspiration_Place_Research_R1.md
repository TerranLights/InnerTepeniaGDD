## 1 · Moscow, Russia [PRIMARY]

### A. Snapshot

- **Location / status:** Capital of Russia, a federal city on the Moskva River. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow (read 2026-10-26).
- **Population:** 13,010,112 (2021 census, city proper); urban area 19.1 million; metropolitan area 21.5 million. **SOURCED** — same page. 2025 estimate of about 13.1–13.4 million appears only in search snippets (Rosstat municipal files) — **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Area:** 2,561.5 km² city proper (Wikipedia); the city grew by 1,490 km² on 1 July 2012 when territory was taken in from Moscow Oblast. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_Moscow.
- **Structure:** 12 administrative okrugs. Central okrug 701,353 people in 10 districts; Southern okrug largest at 1,593,065 in 16 districts; Zelenogradsky, Novomoskovsky (113,569, 11 settlements) and Troitsky (86,752, 10 settlements) are the detached or annexed okrugs (figures as of the 2010 census / 1 July 2012). Kapotnya's page gives 125 raions (districts) in total. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_Moscow ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kapotnya_District.
- **Plan:** radial-concentric. Rings, inside to outside: Boulevard Ring, Garden Ring, Third Ring Road, Central Ring Road, and the MKAD (Moscow Ring Road, 108.9 km, completed 1962), which was the city's administrative boundary 1960–1984 before the city annexed land beyond it. MKAD widened to ten lanes 1995–1999. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Ring_Road.
- **Defining feature:** national capital and the country's dominant economic concentration. In 2018, regional GDP was 17.9 trillion rubles, about a fifth of Russia's GDP, with GDP per capita about 2.5 times the Russian average. **SOURCED** — https://wko.at/wien/wko/cx-network/moscow (Austrian Chamber of Commerce country page, figures for 2018/2019). Wikipedia gives 2024 GDP of 41 trillion rubles. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow.

### B. What else exists there

**Economy and industries**
- Sector shares of regional GDP (2019): trade 26.1%, industry 15.7%, real estate 11.7%, science and research 8.5%, transport 6.7%, IT and telecom 6.6%. Unemployment 1.4% (2019). 720,312 registered companies (January 2020). 16.6 million m² of office space, 4.4 million m² of it class A. 36 technoparks and industrial parks, 48 industrial complexes, and the "Technopolis Moscow" special economic zone. Consumer spending about 10 trillion rubles a year. **SOURCED** — https://wko.at/wien/wko/cx-network/moscow.
- City government figures for 2024: industrial production index up 17.8% on 2023. About 370 technology enterprises. Electrical engineering: over 130 enterprises, about 17,000 workers. Machine building: over 260 enterprises, over 17,000 workers, over 13,000 elevators a year (more than half of Russia's output). Medical instruments: over 300 enterprises. Garment output up 22.5% in 2024. **SOURCED** — https://www.mos.ru/en/mayor/themes/12388050/ (official city site; promotional figures, so treat as the city's own claims). Search snippets add that computers, electronics and optical products were nearly a quarter of manufacturing (420 billion rubles), food industry 186.8 billion rubles (11%), pharmaceuticals 106 billion (6.2%). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only, gxpnews.net).
- **Moscow International Business Center ("Moskva-City"):** 60 ha in Presnensky district, about 4 km west of Red Square on the Presnenskaya Embankment. Conceived 1992, building started 1995. Tallest tower, Federation Tower Vostok, 374 m and 101 floors. Planners estimate 250,000–300,000 people working, living or visiting at any time. About $12 billion invested by 2014. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_International_Business_Center.
- **Remaining heavy industry inside the city:** the Moscow Refinery (Gazprom Neft) in Kapotnya district (8.06 km², about 27,200 people in 2010), described as one of the few industrial facilities left within the city since most were moved out on environmental grounds. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kapotnya_District.
- **Electronics town:** Zelenograd, founded 3 March 1958, refocused in 1962 to electronics, microelectronics and computing. It lies 37 km northwest of the center as an exclave inside Moscow Oblast, 37.22 km², 221,712 people (2010) and an estimated 270,527 (2024). It hosts Mikron and Angstrem (integrated-circuit makers), the National Research University of Electronic Technology (MIET), and a special economic zone from 2005. About 30% green space. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zelenograd.

**Who lives and works there**
- **Ethnic make-up (2021 census):** Russians 69.7% (9,074,375); Tatars 0.6%; Armenians 0.5%. Wikipedia also cites "1.8 million official 'guests'" (temporary residents) and an estimated 1 million irregular migrants. Religion (2020): Russian Orthodox 55%, irreligious 28%, Muslim 8%. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow.
- **Labor migrants:** about 631,000 migrants worked in Moscow on a labor patent in 2025 (TASS, cited 25 Jan 2026), out of 2.3 million in Russia. **SOURCED** — https://360.ru/news/obschestvo/liderami-po-kolichestvu-trudovyh-migrantov-okazalis-moskva-i-peterburg/. A city minister (Jan 2023) named construction, housing and communal services, retail, transport and delivery as their main sectors. In 2022 they paid about 32 billion rubles of income tax to the city budget (about 2% of income-tax receipts). The Multifunctional Migration Center issued 3.8 million patents in 2015–2022 and processed about 1.2 million people in 2022 (biometrics and medical checks). **SOURCED** — https://www.mos.ru/news/item/119194073/. Nationally, the typical migrant is described as a 28-year-old married Uzbek man living in or near Moscow. **UNVERIFIED** (kun.uz snippet). After the March 2024 Crocus attack, migrants nationally faced document checks, workplace raids and firings. **SOURCED** — https://carnegieendowment.org/russia-eurasia/politika/2024/07/russia-central-asia-migrants?lang=en.
- **Housing — the Renovation program:** approved in 2017 under a federal law of 1 July 2017. It covers about 5,171–5,176 mainly five-story Khrushchev-era blocks (khrushchevki), replaced by 6–14-story (up to 20) towers, with completion planned for 2032. About 500 documented protests by 2023, and supportive rallies of about 35,000. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Urban_Renewal_Initiative. Progress as of Dec 2025: over 250,000 residents rehoused and 7.9 million m² built; resettled numbers are expected to pass 450,000 by 2029. **SOURCED** — https://realty.rbc.ru/news/6989dcea9a794764fe7bf482 (dated Feb 2026 by its content; the fetch tool returned a date of 9 Feb 2025, which conflicts with its "as of December 2025" figures). In 2025 about 17,500 families moved; 897 five-story blocks (148,500 people) fully resettled. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Riot history:** on 13 October 2013, in Biryulyovo Zapadnoye (southern Moscow), about 600 people rioted after a local man was killed, allegedly by a migrant. Rioters targeted a vegetable warehouse (ovoshchebaza). Police detained about 200 rioters and, the next day, over 1,200 migrant workers in the area. An Azerbaijani citizen was arrested as the suspect on 15 October. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Biryulyovo_riots.

**Daily life patterns**
- **The dacha cycle:** in the mid-1990s about 25% of families had a dacha, often shared, and it served as a "safety net" through the crises of the 1980s–90s. Over 40% of Russians have some suburban home, and 11% live in it year-round (Levada 2017). Retirees and grandmothers with grandchildren are the core summer residents; the Soviet dacha was a place of work, and today it is becoming a year-round suburban home. **SOURCED** — Polukhina, E. (2018), *Urban Studies and Practices* 3(4):152–163, DOI 10.17323/usp342018152-163 (PDF read). A 2022 Promrating survey found 70.1% of Moscow dacha owners planned to spend significant summer time there. The most-planted crops were cucumbers (72.2%), tomatoes, onions, zucchini and potatoes, and the article ties this to 1990s shortage memories. **SOURCED** — https://moskvichmag.ru/gorod/ogorod-vmesto-morya-7-iz-10-moskvichej-provedut-leto-na-dache/ (28 Mar 2022). "More than 3 million Muscovites have dachas" and a summer-weekend suburban population of over 11 million: **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).

### C. Cultural locations (names, by kind)

**Industrial sites turned creative clusters**
- **Winzavod**, 1/8 4th Syromyatnichesky Lane: seven late-19th-century buildings of a brewery (malt brewing from 1810; "Moscow Bavaria" in the 1870s–80s) and later a winery. Opened as an art center in 2007 by Roman Trotsenko; holds 13 galleries. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winzavod ; https://discover.moscow/galleries-and-urban-vibes-moscows-art-districts.
- **Artplay**, 10 Nizhnyaya Syromyatnicheskaya: founded 2003 (the first creative quarter). It moved after its earlier site at the "Red Rose" factory, which later housed banks and Yandex. Now in a former manometer factory near Kurskaya: 75,000 m², three exhibition halls, 300+ showrooms, the British Higher School of Art and Design. It is self-financed by rents. **SOURCED** — https://arterritory.com/en/visual_arts/interviews/4775-how_creative_quarters_can_survive (16 Oct 2012).
- **Flacon**, 36 Bolshaya Novodmitrovskaya: a former crystal-bottle plant for a perfume house, now design studios and fashion showrooms. **Khlebozavod No. 9**, 1 Novodmitrovskaya: a 1930s bread factory, now Russian fashion brands, food and a concert space. **Supermetal**, 2nd Baumanskaya: a former metallurgy institute, now design and digital studios. **Krasny Oktyabr** (Red October), 6 Bersenevskaya Embankment: a former chocolate factory, now galleries, clubs and rooftop bars. **GES-2 House of Culture**, 15 Bolotnaya Embankment: a 1907 power station, now exhibitions, theater, cinema and a bakery. **SOURCED** — https://discover.moscow/galleries-and-urban-vibes-moscows-art-districts (undated city tourism page).
- **Garage Museum of Contemporary Art:** founded 2008 by Dasha Zhukova and Roman Abramovich in Melnikov's 1926 Bakhmetevsky Bus Garage. In 2015 it moved to Gorky Park, to the 1968 Vremena Goda restaurant building rebuilt by Rem Koolhaas. It has archives of Russian contemporary art from the 1950s on and has collected Russian art of the 1980s–2020s since June 2024. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garage_Museum_of_Contemporary_Art.

### D. How the defining feature shaped these things

- **Capital concentration → in-migration labor economy:** construction, utilities, retail, transport and delivery depend on patent-holding migrants (631,000 in 2025), processed through a dedicated migration center. Tension has surfaced in incidents such as Biryulyovo 2013 and the post-Crocus checks of 2024 (sources above).
- **Capital concentration → office and finance cluster:** the 60-ha Moskva-City tower district and 16.6 million m² of offices; trade is the largest GDP share (26.1%).
- **Soviet planned-capital legacy → the shape of daily life:** the radial-ring road plan; the deep Metro built as an architectural showcase; mass five-story housing now being demolished under Renovation; VDNKh as an exhibition of the planned economy; the house-of-culture and library network now being "rebooted"; Zelenograd as a planned single-industry science town inside the city's borders.
- **De-industrialization of the core → cultural reuse:** breweries, bread, chocolate, bottle and gauge factories and a power station became art clusters (Winzavod, Khlebozavod 9, Krasny Oktyabr, Flacon, Artplay, GES-2). Artplay's forced move shows rent pressure pushing creative tenants out once a site gains value.
- **Scale of supply → industrialized food logistics:** Food City (since 2014) replaced dispersed informal wholesale.

### E. Structural features bearing on industry, economy, arts and music, work and life

- Very low recorded unemployment (1.4%, 2019) with a highly educated workforce (50% of professionals with degrees, per WKO).
- Long commutes as a norm (two-thirds commute over an hour), with outer districts as dormitory zones.
- A summer outflow to dachas, especially retirees and children with grandmothers.
- State-dominated cultural infrastructure (over 50% of about 1,800 organizations), with private art clusters alongside. The city's own document calls state institutions "uniform."
- The city's ongoing physical remaking: Renovation through 2032, new hospitals, Metro expansion.
- An older population served by organized municipal leisure (Moscow Longevity).
- Free mass public festivals throughout the year (Moscow Seasons, City Day).

## 2 · Los Angeles, California [SECONDARY]

- **What / where:** the largest city in California, seat of Los Angeles County. Founded 4 September 1781 by 44 settlers as El Pueblo de Nuestra Señora la Reina de los Ángeles; incorporated 4 April 1850. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles.
- **Population:** 3,898,747 (2020 census); 3,869,089 (2025 estimate). Metro area 12.9 million (2024); combined region over 18 million. County about 9.86 million (2022). Land area about 469 sq mi (1,210 km²). **SOURCED** — same page.
- **Make-up (2023):** Hispanic/Latino 47.2%, non-Hispanic white 28.3%, Asian 12.0%, Black 8.5%. 56.8% of residents aged 5+ speak a language other than English at home (2021). Main foreign-born origins are Mexico, El Salvador, Guatemala, the Philippines and South Korea. **SOURCED** — same page (second segment).
- **Neighborhoods / governance:** the *Los Angeles Times* "Mapping L.A." project (2009) drew 114 neighborhoods inside the city; a 2017 alternative map by Eric Brightwell counted 472. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapping_L.A. There are 99 advisory neighborhood councils, set up by the 1999 charter, each covering about 40,000 residents, with about $25,000 a year in public funds; non-citizens and formerly incarcerated people can take part. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_councils_of_Los_Angeles.
- **Defining feature:** an imported-water, multi-industry metropolis whose world-known identity is the entertainment industry. Key industries are film and TV, the port, aerospace and defense, fashion, technology, petroleum and tourism. Metro GDP was $1.355 trillion in 2024. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles.

**Infrastructure**
- **Water:** the Los Angeles Aqueduct, begun 1908 under William Mulholland and completed 1913, runs 233 miles from the Owens River (system total 419 miles). Groundwater pumping had dried most Owens Valley springs by 1970 and ended it as a farming area. During the "California Water Wars," farmers dynamited the aqueduct several times. A Mono Basin extension was added in 1940–41 and a second aqueduct in 1970. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Aqueduct. 2023 supply: Metropolitan Water District imports 51%, LA Aqueduct 38%, local groundwater 9%, recycled 2%. **SOURCED** — https://dwqr.ladwp.com/2023dwqr/water-sources.
- **The aqueduct shaped the city's borders:** the San Fernando Valley joined Los Angeles in 1915 because of aqueduct water. It now has about 1.8 million people on 260 sq mi (42% Latino, 39% non-Hispanic white, 11% Asian, 2023). It holds studios (Warner Bros., Disney, Universal), aerospace history (Lockheed, Rocketdyne) and an adult-film industry now in decline, and it voted on secession in 2002 (Measure F, failed). **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Fernando_Valley. San Pedro and Wilmington were consolidated in 1909 via the 1906 "Shoestring" annexation of a connecting strip. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Pedro,_Los_Angeles.
- **Transit:** LA Metro had 311,261,332 boardings in 2024 (+8%), with over 1 million average weekday boardings in Sept–Oct 2024. **SOURCED** — https://www.metro.net/about/media-relations/la-metros-2024-ridership-soars-to-more-than-311-million-marking-significant-growth/ (21 Feb 2025). Who rides (Fall 2023 On-Board Survey, N = 21,880): 66% Hispanic/Latino, 16% Black, 9% white, 7% Asian. Median household income $17,246, and 84% are at or below HUD's "very low income" line. 54% of trips are work commutes and 15% school commutes. Only 18% had a car available for the trip. 69% answered in English. **SOURCED** — https://cdn.beta.metro.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/26132406/System-Results-Fall-2023.pdf (PDF read via pdftotext).

**How food and goods arrive**
- By sea through the port (above) and by rail from the port to downtown. The Los Angeles Terminal Market (1917–1923, John Parkinson) was built at the Southern Pacific terminus, and "at one point the majority of the region's produce business" passed through it. The Seventh Street and Ninth Street wholesale markets were consolidated in 1986 as the Los Angeles Wholesale Produce Market. The old site became ROW DTLA in 2017 (30 acres, 100 shops, 1.3 million ft²), was American Apparel's base, and has hosted Shein's US headquarters since 2021. The 7th Street Produce Market still runs and becomes Smorgasburg on Sundays. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ROW_DTLA.
- **Street vending:** the city legalized sidewalk vending on 28 Nov 2018, and the state Safe Sidewalk Vending Act (SB 946) took effect 1 Jan 2019, with permits and placement rules. Estimates range from 20,000 to nearly 50,000 vendors. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; the CUNY scholar page returned 403).

**Who lives and works there; housing**
- **Housing:** LA County homeownership is 45%, its lowest in over 50 years. Median home values are over 10 times incomes. Over 90% of the county's 734,000 renter households earning under $50,000 are cost-burdened. Since 2015 the county lost over 500,000 people, including 280,000 foreign-born. Families with children fell from about a third of households (2010) to a quarter (2023). In the city of LA, permit-to-occupancy takes 18 months on average (35 for 5+ unit projects). Subsidized units are 6% of county rentals. **SOURCED** — USC Lusk Center, *State of Los Angeles Housing and Neighborhoods* 2025 executive summary, https://lusk.usc.edu/sites/default/files/2025-08/2025%20SOLACHAN%20Executive%20Summary%20CMYK.pdf (PDF read). City renter share 63–63.7%, and about 651,000–661,851 rent-stabilized (pre-Oct-1978) units: **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; the controller page returned 403).
- **Homelessness (point-in-time count):** in the city, 43,669 in 2025 (from 45,252 in 2024), with unsheltered down 7.9%; county 72,308. Tents, RVs and makeshift shelters in the city fell from 12,717 to 11,010. **SOURCED** — https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/los-angeles-2025-homeless-count/3744593 (14 Jul 2025). A RAND study found the official count missed up to 32% in study areas and captured only 61% of Skid Row's unsheltered population. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Skid Row** (officially Central City East): about 50 blocks between 3rd and 7th Streets and Main and Alameda, with a stable homeless population of over 4,400 since at least the 1930s (about 10,000 then). About 7,500 residential-hotel rooms were lost in the 1960s. The city designated it an informal "containment zone" in 1976, and the 2006 Safer Cities Initiative brought intensive policing. Main services: Union Rescue Mission, Los Angeles Mission, Midnight Mission, and the county Central Health Center. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skid_Row,_Los_Angeles.

**Districts and distinct urban settings (a selection)**
- **Boyle Heights** (Eastside, 6.5 sq mi, 92,785 in 2000, about 94–95% Latino): historically Jewish and Japanese as well as Mexican, and one of the few areas without race-restrictive covenants. It is now mainly a cultural, commercial and religious center, with the Japanese American National Museum (1992), the JACCC, and the Nisei Week festival in August. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Tokyo,_Los_Angeles.
- **Leimert Park** (South LA; 9,880 people in 2000, 79.6% Black): planned 1928 with an Olmsted Brothers plan. Leimert Park Village is the center of African-American art and music in LA. - **San Pedro** (83,556 people in 2022, about 12 sq mi): the port neighborhood. - **Arts District** (eastern edge of downtown): citrus packing and Santa Fe rail warehouses, then artist lofts made legal by the 1981 Artist-in-Residence ordinance (LA Artcore 1976, Al's Bar). Rising prices are displacing artists (median property price $714,500 in 2016). **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_District,_Los_Angeles.
- **Chavez Ravine** (now Dodger Stadium): the Mexican-American communities of Palo Verde, La Loma and Bishop were cleared by eminent domain in the 1950s for a public-housing plan (Elysian Park Heights) that was then dropped. The 352 acres went to the Dodgers after a 1958 referendum; the evictions are known as the "Battle of Chavez Ravine"; the stadium opened 1962. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chavez_Ravine.

### D. How the defining feature shaped these things

- **Imported water → the city's territory:** aqueduct water was the reason the San Fernando Valley joined (1915), and it made suburban sprawl possible across 469 sq mi. The cost fell on the Owens Valley (water wars, dried springs).
- **Entertainment → districts and workforce:** studios in the Valley; a creative labor market of freelance and crew jobs that shrank by roughly a third in 2022–24 when production left for other tax-credit regions. The Hollywood Bowl and the Hollywood Forever festival sit in the industry's namesake district.
- **The port → harbor neighborhoods:** the 1906–1909 annexations of a land strip, San Pedro and Wilmington; port-linked jobs (1 in 14 in the city); immigrant fishing communities (Italian, Croatian, Norwegian, Japanese on Terminal Island); a downtown rail-terminus produce market.
- **Immigrant labor and trade → garment district, street vending, ethnic districts:** Fashion District piece-rate work (now banned), legalized sidewalk vending, Koreatown, Little Tokyo, Boyle Heights, Mariachi Plaza, and a Catholic Church holding Mass in 42 languages.
- **Car-and-freeway form → transit use split by class:** Metro riders are mostly very-low-income Latino and Black riders without a car for the trip. The East LA Interchange cut through Boyle Heights. Lowrider cruising culture grew on the boulevards.

### E. Structural features

- Very high housing cost, a renter-majority city, falling homeownership, and population loss since 2015, especially of immigrants and families with children.
- Large visible homelessness concentrated in Skid Row, a 1976 containment-zone policy area.
- An economy of a few large employers (health care, universities, aerospace) beside a freelance entertainment labor market in contraction (2022–2025).
- Fire exposure at the wildland edge (Palisades 2025).
- Civic structure: 99 advisory neighborhood councils open to non-citizens.
- Imported-water dependence: 89% of 2023 supply came from aqueducts and imports.

### C. Cultural locations (gathered so far)

- **Tepito** (Colonia Morelos, Cuauhtémoc): about 25 streets with 35,000–120,000 residents (estimates vary). Its tianguis (street market), dating from pre-Hispanic times, has about 12,000 merchants in sections for food, shoes and secondhand goods. The name is Nahuatl for "small temple." It produced the boxers Kid Azteca and Raúl "El Ratón" Macías. The Santa Muerte shrine on Alfarería Street, kept by Enriqueta Romero, draws thousands of devotees a month. Counterfeit trade and organized crime have earned it the name "Barrio Bravo." **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tepito.
- **Plaza Garibaldi** (Eje Central near Bellas Artes): formerly Plaza Santa Cecilia, renamed 1920. Salón Tenampa was opened in 1923 by Juan Indalecio Hernández of Jalisco, and Cirilo Marmolejo's band played there by 1927. Mariachis, trios and jarocho groups are hired around the clock, busiest on weekend nights after 11 p.m. A 2007 renewal added the Museum of Tequila and Mezcal and a Mariachi School. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Garibaldi.
- **Xochimilco** (borough of 442,178 people, 122 km²): chinampa raised fields about 1,000 years old, with about 5,000 still in original form; UNESCO 1987. Trajinera boat rides on the canals (Wikipedia's text gives "approximately 170 km2" — probably canal length; unconfirmed). Isla de las Muñecas (Island of the Dolls). It sinks about 18 cm a year, and pollution has led UNESCO to threaten its listing. Only about 600 wild axolotls in 2003. The "Flor más Bella del Ejido" pageant has run since 1955. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xochimilco.
- **Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe** (Tepeyac, Gustavo A. Madero): the new basilica (1976, Ramírez Vázquez) holds 10,000 and was built because of subsidence and crowding at the 1709 church. About 20 million pilgrims a year, about 9 million of them around 12 December. **SOURCED** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Our_Lady_of_Guadalupe.
