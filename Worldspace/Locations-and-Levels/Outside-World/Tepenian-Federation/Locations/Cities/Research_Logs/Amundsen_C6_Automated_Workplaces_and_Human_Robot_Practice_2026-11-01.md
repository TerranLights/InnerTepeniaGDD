## 1.1 Rotterdam — ECT Delta (1993) [neighbor added: the historical first version]
- SOURCED (ECT, https://www.ect.nl/en/news/fast-forward/30-years-ago-ect-opened-very-first-automated-terminal-world): "a crane team at the automated terminal consisted of just three people" (a conventional terminal: eight). ## 1.2 Rotterdam — APM Terminals Maasvlakte II (MVII) and RWG
- SOURCED (APMT, https://www.apmterminals.com/en/maasvlakte/about/our-terminal): "the world's most advanced fully automated terminal," automated areas "fenced off from staff"; roughly 80% of crane movements automated, the rest run remotely; dual-spreader cranes; "73 Lift Automated Guided Vehicles (L-AGV)" run on electricity; each AGV runs ~8 hours then drives itself to a station for automatic battery swap. Page does not give headcount.
- SOURCED (Marketplace, Stam): MVII "they still have 600 people working there." Jobs listed in the article: remote crane drivers, mechanics, lashing gangs, supervisors, troubleshooters; "there are plenty of jobs that humans still need to do." Port innovation director Oscar van Veen: goal is "getting the data in" rather than "getting the people out." Albert Veenstra (Erasmus): newer terminals built for ships whose cranes don't allow "a physical person in the cabin." Stam: "Particularly the first phase, it was a strong fight."
- SOURCED (search summary of Maritime Executive/ABB pages; training count only): since early 2014 APMT trained 45 remote operators on simulators; ABB system places STS operators in a control room in the terminal building, first in the world with no one in the crane cab. ABB: control room may reduce fatigue and neck/back stress; cameras often give a wider view than the cab (search summary only).
- SOURCED (ABB operator video, via search summary): operator Ben den Otter described a mental adjustment: after doing everything manually, trusting the crane to do it automatically took a different mindset.
- UNVERIFIED (kitalent.com, promotional-looking, search summary only): "180 specialized automation technicians" at MVII; "40% fewer dockworkers than a conventional terminal."
- RWG (Rotterdam World Gateway, DP World + four shipping lines): SOURCED (Port Strategy, https://www.portstrategy.com/?p=229099, c. March 2015): unions FNV Havens and CNV Vakmensen wanted a collective labor agreement (CLA); RWG said a CLA "is not representative of the workforce at the terminal," would not impose one without employees' "express consent"; unions alleged RWG tried to exclude "various groups including office workers"; striking dockers disrupted RWG. ## 6.4 Ships with periodically unattended machinery spaces (UMS) (resume session)
**The regulation text**
- SOURCED (Danish Maritime Authority, Notice D II-1 E, 1 October 2002, "Technical regulation on the construction and equipment, etc. of passenger ships on domestic voyages," Part D "Additional requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces," https://admin.dma.dk/Media/637743811611429569/DII1E-01102002.pdf, extracted with pdftotext; "Only the Danish version is authentic"). Each regulation is cross-referenced to SOLAS chapter II-1 regulations 46 to 54 (marked "R 46" to "R 54"); the SOLAS consolidated text itself was not read (IMO sells it; died at the sources), so this is the SOLAS-derived wording as adopted by one flag state:
  - R 46: the arrangements must ensure "that the safety of the ship in all sailing conditions, including maneuvering is equivalent to that of a ship having the machinery spaces manned"; "satisfactory arrangements are made for regular inspections and routine tests"; "Every ship shall be provided with documentary evidence of its fitness to operate with periodically unattended machinery spaces."
  - R 47: early fire detection and alarm in boiler air casings and uptakes and in scavenging air belts; engines of "2,250 kW and above or having cylinders of more than 300 mm bore" need crankcase oil-mist detectors or bearing temperature monitors.
  - R 48: bilge wells "large enough to accommodate easily the normal drainage during the unattended period"; sea-inlet valves operable from a floor high enough that, after the largest inlet pipe breaks, "the water will not reach their height within ten minutes from the time when the flooding alarm ... has been received."
  - R 49: speed, thrust direction and propeller pitch "fully controllable from the navigating bridge"; a bridge emergency stop independent of the bridge control system; control from "one location at a time"; the number of consecutive automatic start attempts limited to save starting air.
  - R 50: "A reliable means of vocal communication shall be provided between the main machinery control room or the propulsion machinery control position as appropriate, the navigating bridge and the engineer officers' accommodation."
  - R 51: the alarm system shall "have a connection to the engineers' public rooms and to each of the engineers' cabins through a selector switch, to ensure connection to at least one of those cabins"; sound on the bridge "for any situation which requires action by or attention of the officer on watch"; "activate the engineers' alarm ... if an alarm function has not received attention locally within a limited time"; "the acceptance of any alarm shall not inhibit another alarm."
  - R 52 and R 53: automatic shutdown on serious malfunction; automatic start and connection of a standby generator on loss of the running one.
- What the text does not say: it sets no maximum unattended period, no duty-engineer roster and no number of seconds for the "limited time" (NOT FOUND in the regulation as read). SOURCED-SECONDARY (search summaries; UNVERIFIED): a Marine Insight Q&A says "16 consecutive hours"; a DNV rule is quoted as requiring unattended operation "for 24 hours or for the maximum continuous operation time when less than 24 hours" (the DNV page renders with no body text; died at the source); US Coast Guard Marine Safety Center guidance (PRG E2-17, 2021): a deadman (personnel) alarm must alarm on the bridge if not routinely acknowledged, "SOLAS uses 30 minutes," and an unacknowledged engine-room alarm escalates to an engineers' assistance alarm (USCG PDF returned 403).

## 7.7 Safety standards for robots working near people
- SOURCED (SICK, Markus Bechtel, 8 September 2025, https://www.sick.com/br/en/more-safety-for-humans-and-machines/w/blog-robotic-norm-iso10218): the 2025 edition of ISO 10218 replaces the 2011 edition; "ISO 10218-1:2025 distinguishes between two robot classes" (large industrial robots and smaller ones for collaborative applications); it fully incorporates the former ISO/TS 15066 including "the biomechanical limits that define the permissible forces and pressure loads" in a human-robot collision; each safety function gets an application-specific performance level instead of a blanket PLd; cybersecurity is addressed in line with the EU Machinery Regulation. SOURCED-SECONDARY (search summaries; UNVERIFIED): both parts published February 2025; "collaborative" is now a property of the application, not of the robot; end-effector guidance from ISO/TR 20218-1 and -2 is folded in.
- SOURCED-SECONDARY (search summaries of research papers and vendor guides; the standard itself is sold and was not read): ISO/TS 15066 (2016) names four collaborative modes (safety-rated monitored stop, hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, power and force limiting); its body model gives, for example, 130 N maximum permissible force for skull and forehead and 140 N for hands and fingers; limits are tied to the onset of pain and distinguish transient from quasi-static contact.
- SOURCED (Behrens, Pliske, Umbreit, Piatek, Walcher, Elkmann, "A Statistical Model to Determine Biomechanical Limits for Physically Safe Interactions With Collaborative Robots," Frontiers in Robotics and AI 8, 2022, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2021.667818): ISO/TS 15066 "provides a list of biomechanical limits for 29 different body locations"; part of them come from a study with 100 subjects (Melia et al., 2019, pinching), the rest were estimated from literature: "Most of the limits in ISO/TS 15066 are preliminary, since they are based on unverified data from a literature survey"; the authors tested 112 subjects at 28 body locations with an algometer (pinching) and a pendulum (impacts), raising force "until the load evoked a slightly painful feeling"; their proposed limits are the 75th-percentile pain-onset threshold of a group that is 70% male; examples: forehead pinching 110 N (blunt), impact 150 N (blunt); palm impact 330 N (blunt).
