## Pick #70 — Hội An (Vietnam)

**Claim to test:** "A trading port where merchants of several origins waited for the seasonal wind to change, so access shaped who lived there and when (K4, K5)."

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with one correction of emphasis.** Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century sources describe a yearly trade season governed by the monsoons, an annual fair of roughly four months, a town physically split into separately governed Chinese and Japanese quarters, and a resident population that roughly doubled during the trade season. Correction: the "waiting" was not mainly merchants idling until the wind turned; it was a whole seasonal economy (warehouses, floating markets, temporary marriages, local women as brokers, towing villages) organized around the wind schedule, and a permanent mixed population (the Minh Hương) grew out of merchants who stayed. Monsoon timing is given differently by different sources (see A and D); no single month-by-month schedule was found in a primary source.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location and administration today:** Old town on the north bank of the Thu Bồn River, about 3 km upriver from the sea (Wheeler 2003, SOURCED). Since **1 July 2025** the former Hội An city (formerly in Quảng Nam province) is part of the merged **Đà Nẵng** municipality (Resolution 202/2025/QH15 merged Quảng Nam into Đà Nẵng). The former city was split into **three wards (Hội An, Hội An Đông, Hội An Tây) and the island commune of Tân Hiệp** (Cù Lao Chàm) (VOV, 29 Jun 2026, https://vov.vn/xa-hoi/hoi-an-1-di-san-4-don-vi-hanh-chinh-cap-xa-va-bai-toan-khong-gian-phat-trien-moi-post1310791.vov, SOURCED). The ward of Hội An (old town core) was formed 16 June 2025 under Resolution 1659/NQ-UBTVQH15 from Minh An, Cẩm Phô, Sơn Phong, Cẩm Nam wards and Cẩm Kim commune; **37,222 people (2024), 10.81 km²** (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hội_An,_Da_Nang, SOURCED). A ward official gives "over 37,000 residents, 7,450 permanent households, 561 temporary households" (Tuổi Trẻ, 4 Apr 2026, SOURCED). Former-city population: ~98,599 (2019 census, citypopulation.de, UNVERIFIED snippet). [K1, P9]
- **Heritage management:** the Hội An heritage management center was renamed the **Hội An World Cultural Heritage Conservation Center** (Trung tâm Bảo tồn Di sản văn hóa thế giới Hội An) and now sits under the Đà Nẵng Department of Culture, Sports and Tourism (VOV 2026, SOURCED). [K1]
- **Defining feature:** UNESCO World Heritage Site since December 1999 (4 Dec 1999 per UNESCO nomination record; criteria ii and v, "an exceptionally well-preserved example of a traditional Asian trading port") (search snippets of UNESCO archive, UNVERIFIED; the UNESCO list page returned 403, NOT READ). Over **1,200 old relics, ~70% privately owned; only ~20–30% still lived in**, most owners having moved out and leased to outside businesses (Tuổi Trẻ, 15 Sep 2024, SOURCED). [K9, P3]

### B. What else exists there
**History of the trade season and the waiting port (core of the claim).**
- Formally established as the Nguyễn lords' foreign trading port in the late 16th century (Looram thesis, Flinders, search snippet, UNVERIFIED; the PDF exceeded the size limit, NOT READ). Earlier Champa-era port (9th–10th c., Arab, Persian, Chinese merchants) (Hội An heritage center history page, https://hoianheritage.danang.gov.vn/en/nature-and-history/nature-and-history/history-culture-1.html, SOURCED). [P6]
- **Borri (Jesuit, in Hội An c. 1618–1622), as quoted by Nguyen Thi Vinh Linh (Vietnam Social Sciences, 2021, vjol.info.vn VSS article 74525, read from the saved PDF, SOURCED):** "The Chineses and Japoneses, drive the chief trade of Cochinchina, which is managed at a fair held yearly at one of the ports of this kingdom, and lasting about four months." And: "the king of Cochinchina assigned the Chineses and Japoneses a convenient spot of ground, to build a city for the benefit of the fair. This city is called Faifó… they are divided from one another, each having their distinct governor, and the Chineses living according to the laws of China, as the Japoneses do according to those of Japan" (Dror and Taylor 2006, pp. 132–133). [K4, K2, P5, P7]
- **Season timing (same article, citing Hoang Thi Anh Dao 2017):** Portuguese ships came from Macau on the northeast monsoon in March–May; left in July–August on the southwest monsoon before the storm season, a 20-day voyage to Malacca; "trade activities in Hoi An started around the time of the Tet holidays and lasted six to seven months (from February to September)." A cited Nguyen Chi Trung (2016) passage: goods were gathered and "a fair was held for nearly four months every year." Large ships moored at outports (Touron/Đà Nẵng, Trà Nhiêu, Trung Phương) "waiting for completing customs procedures." First Portuguese contact 1516: a fleet arrived in August and "due to headwinds, they stayed there for a few days." SOURCED. [K5, K7]
- **Port-tax schedule (Lê Quý Đôn, Phủ biên tạp lục, 1776, as cited there):** each Portuguese ship paid 8,000 quan on arrival and 800 on departure, the highest of any origin. SOURCED (secondhand via the article). [K8, K1]
- **Charles Wheeler, "A Maritime Logic to Vietnamese History? Littoral Society in Hoi An's Trading World c.1550–1830" (AHA/History Cooperative 2003, SOURCED via webdoc.sub.gwdg.de mirror):** "between the northern and southern monsoons, roughly February through July," the town's population of about 5,000 roughly doubled, crowding warehouses, markets and floating markets; with the August winds ships left for China and Japan; around the lunar new year ships returned. Arriving ships waited off Cù Lao Chàm until Nguyễn inspectors issued the clearance; fishing villages held charters to tow ships in, in exchange for remitted tribute; others gathered birds' nests or salvaged wrecks. Piracy followed the season ("from the second or third month to the ninth month all the bandit boats make their sweeps", Lan Dingyuan quoted). Foreign merchants took temporary local wives who acted as their trade agents (monk Da Shan: they "dispatch their wives to conduct trade, and cannot do business without them"); markets were "in the hands of women" (Poivre). [K4, K7, K5, K14, SM:dangerous system]
- **Heritage center:** "During the typhoon season (August to October), Chinese traders often remained for prolonged periods" (SOURCED, history page). Note this differs from the departure-in-August accounts; recorded as a discrepancy.
- **Japanese quarter:** Red-seal ship system 1604–1639: ~350 licensed voyages, **130 to Vietnam**; 34 letters exchanged between the Tokugawa and the Nguyễn; the 1635 ban stopped Japanese ships; Japanese "continued to live in the Japanese town within Hội An for several decades after the ban" (National Archives of Japan exhibition, https://www.archives.go.jp/event/jp_vn45/english/ch02.html, SOURCED). A 1642 Dutch report estimated 4,000–5,000 Chinese and 40–50 Japanese (search snippet, UNVERIFIED). Japanese-Vietnamese excavations (Kikuchi Seiichi, Showa Women's University) recovered large quantities of Japanese (Imari) and Chinese ceramics documenting the residents' lives (JSPS KAKENHI News 2014 vol. 3, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). About 40 Japanese tombs are counted; three in Cẩm Châu (Tani Yajirobei d. c.1647; Banjiro 1665; Gusokukun 1629); "the last ship to repatriate Japanese traders left in 1673" (Viet Nam News, 15 Aug 2024, SOURCED). [K9, K4]
- **Minh Hương:** legitimized by the Nguyễn in **1679**; Ming-loyalist and Zheng-network merchants who settled; most customs posts reserved for them (170+ individuals in customs in the 1700s) (Wheeler, SOURCED). Quan Công temple built 1653 by the Minh Hương with Vietnamese (snippet, UNVERIFIED); Tụy Tiên Đường (Minh Hương ancestral hall) national relic 29 Mar 1993 (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K4, K1]
- **Decline:** Tây Sơn and Trịnh fighting from 1775 damaged the town; silting of Cửa Đại and Cổ Cò made it hard for large ships; trade shifted to Đà Nẵng (heritage history page SOURCED; 1775 detail UNVERIFIED snippet). French Resident's office in Hội An in 1892 (heritage center "Dragon years" page, SOURCED). [K9]
- **Highland supply chain:** cinnamon, agarwood, honey, forest medicines came down the Thu Bồn, Vu Gia and Chiên Đàn rivers via midland hubs (Kim Bồng, Trà Nhiêu, Phú Chiêm); upstream tax stations in 1830; "Under the Nguyễn lords, Hội An port annually sold up to 2,000 tons of various types of cinnamon" (Nguyễn Văn Thịnh, heritage center, 19 Mar 2026, SOURCED). [K5, K8]

A street-food seller of 30+ years: customers in the early morning are all locals, done by midday. Breakfast dishes 25,000–40,000 VND. ### C. Cultural locations (named)
- **Chùa Cầu (Japanese Covered Bridge):** built 1593 by Japanese per heritage center (other sources: 17th c.); national relic 1990; reopened after a 19-month restoration on 3 Aug (2024, at the 20th Hội An–Japan exchange) (SOURCED/UNVERIFIED mix as marked above; vnexpress/nhandan snippets for restoration). [P6]
- **Assembly halls (hội quán):** Trung Hoa / Dương Thương (64 Trần Phú; shared hall of the five congregations, c.1741, once a school teaching Chinese to children); Phúc Kiến (Fujian; Thiên Hậu, busiest on her feast, 23rd of 2nd lunar month); Quảng Triệu / Quảng Đông (176 Trần Phú); Triều Châu (Âm Bổn, Mã Viện; 1845–1852 by stele); Hải Nam (Quỳnh Phủ). - **Museums:** Museum of Trade Ceramics (1995), Sa Huỳnh Culture Museum (1994 or 1995), Folklore Museum (2005), Precious Heritage Museum (private photography museum) (travel guides, UNVERIFIED). **Traditional Art Performance House**, 66 Bạch Đằng; **Japanese Culture Gallery**, 06 Nguyễn Thị Minh Khai (SOURCED, ticket notice).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The two-quarter town with separate governors is a direct product of the seasonal fair (Borri). The bridge sits between the quarters. [K4]
- Waiting ships created waterfront work: towing charters, floating markets, warehouses, provisioning; locals joined commerce only in certain parts of the year (Wheeler). [K7, K14]
- Temporary marriage and women as brokers were the social form of waiting for the wind (Wheeler). [K4, SM:man versus society]
- Merchants who stayed became the Minh Hương and the congregations, whose halls still run festivals (Nguyên Tiêu, Thiên Hậu). [K2]
- The highland-to-coast river trade (cinnamon) made the midland villages (Kim Bồng, Trà Nhiêu) satellites of the port; Kim Bồng carpenters once built boats and houses and now make handicrafts (baovanhoa snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K14, R]

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)
- **Heritage-tourism monoculture inside a ticketed zone:** ticket revenue funds restoration; houses leased to outsiders; only 20–30% of relics lived in; residents' share of the old town shrinking (SOURCED, Tuổi Trẻ 2024). [K8, K9]
- **K6 — everyday behavior rules (FOUND, several):** (1) Walking and Cycling Town hours, 9:00–21:30 summer / 21:00 winter; Phan Châu Trinh St. 17:30–21:30 (heritage center, SOURCED); motor vehicles banned in set windows (travel guide, UNVERIFIED). (2) **"Old Town Night" (Đêm phố cổ),** first trial **8 Sep 1998** on the 14th lunar night: all modern and public lighting turned off, pedestrian only, goods moved inside the threshold, residents asked to hang lanterns, wear traditional dress and not park on sidewalks; ward teams visited homes to encourage compliance; residents resisted at first. 230+ nights and 1 million+ visitors in 20 years (SGGP, 2 Oct 2018, SOURCED). (3) **Street-vending rules (Apr 2026):** no selling from carts anywhere in the old town; one sign per business; foreign text at most one-third the size of Vietnamese; no mobile loudspeakers; enforced by ward forces, education first then penalties "to make an example" (Tuổi Trẻ, 4 Apr 2026, SOURCED). (4) **Homestay rule (2024):** only native residents who live in the house and hold a "cultural family" recognition (or three consecutive years of it) may host guests in the old town (Tuổi Trẻ, 15 Sep 2024, SOURCED). (5) **Cù Lao Chàm plastic-bag campaign** from Aug 2009: bags banned from the island, inter-sector inspection team (2010) checks arrivals and swaps bags for net bags; fines 1–2 million VND for businesses, 300–500 thousand for small traders; households lose their "cultural family" title (Báo Văn hóa, 5 Aug 2019, SOURCED). [K6, K1, P7]
- **Floods as the annual hardship:** October–November floods enter the old town; in late Oct 2025 the water reached 3.4 m, matching the 1964 record; 5,800+ households under 0.5–2 m of water; boats the only transport; tourism suspended (Tuổi Trẻ News, 30 Oct 2025, SOURCED). Some years the water rises 8 times; boatmen earn 300,000–500,000 VND per short trip in floods, but in 2025 core-zone boatmen lost income because of safety closures (Nhân Dân, 10 Nov 2026, SOURCED). Ticket revenue partly reserved for house repairs after floods (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K9, K5, K7]
- **Quiet season (Nov 2026):** markets near empty; restaurants open mainly "to count the days"; buy-one-get-one days (Nhân Dân, SOURCED). [K10, K3]
- **Merger friction:** tours split across wards; old-town ward generates the most waste but has no landfill and must ship it to Hội An Tây (which sometimes refused); no land for parking; unclear who licenses repairs or answers a fire; sister-city ties and the creative-city title attached to a city that no longer exists (VOV, 29 Jun 2026, SOURCED). [K9, K1]
- **Postwar near-loss:** in 1976 a crowd of 500+ assembled to demolish temples and halls (Khổng Miếu, Phúc Kiến hall, Ông Bổn) in an anti-superstition campaign; provincial party secretary Hồ Nghinh stopped it; in 1979 a petition to close Ông Bổn was defused (Tiền Phong, 16 Jun 2013, SOURCED; recollection-based). Tết 1968: Hội An was a primary target in Quảng Nam (USMC history snippet, UNVERIFIED). Large-scale Hoa emigration from Vietnam 1978–79 (~450,000 nationally; snippet, UNVERIFIED; no Hội An figure found). [K9]
- **Festivals:** Nguyên Tiêu (15th of 1st lunar month), run by the communal houses and the Chinese halls; national intangible heritage 2023; street processions since 2024 (Đoàn Cẩm Giang, heritage center, 24 Mar 2026, SOURCED). Cầu Ngư whale festivals at four shrines on fixed lunar dates (16/1, 10/2, 16/2, 4/4); stranded whales buried and later exhumed into the shrine; eleven Nguyễn decrees 1826–1911 (SOURCED). Long Chu procession 15/1 and 15/7 (UNVERIFIED). **Hội An–Japan Cultural Exchange** (created festival): first 2003 per one source, 1998 per another; 21st edition 26–28 Dec 2025; 22nd 22–24 May 2026 (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Japanese residents clean the Japanese tombs at Vu Lan/Obon (Viet Nam News 2024, SOURCED). [K2, K3]
- **K11 (languages, loose):** Vietnamese (Quảng Nam regional speech); historically Chinese varieties of the congregations (Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew, Hainanese, Hakka) and Japanese, Portuguese among traders; Chinese once taught to children at the Trung Hoa hall. Whether Chinese varieties are still spoken daily: NOT FOUND.

## Pick #71 — Malacca (Melaka, Malaysia)

**Claim to test:** "A port where traders waited between monsoons, building a many-origin city around waiting and transshipment (K4, K8)."

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED in its historical form; a present-day echo also found.** Under the Sultanate (c.1400–1511) the port sat where the monsoon systems met, so cargo from one direction had to be stored until traders from the other direction arrived; foreign traders were divided among four harbor masters by origin; over eighty languages were reported. Several mixed communities that still exist (Chitty, Baba-Nyonya, Kristang) descend from traders and settlers who stayed and intermarried. The present-day echo: merchant ships and tankers anchoring off the Melaka coast to wait crowd out local fishermen (2020–2023 reports). What was NOT found: a primary-source statement of how many months traders typically stayed (Pires's Malacca book, Hakluyt vol. II, could not be reached; see WANTED).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** west coast of the Malay Peninsula on the Strait of Malacca; capital of Melaka state; Melaka Historic City Council (MBMB) governs the city (SOURCED, Star 2025). State population about 1.03 million (2023, citypopulation.de citing DOSM, UNVERIFIED snippet); Kota Melaka parliamentary constituency 215,409 (MyCensus 2020, OpenDOSM snippet, UNVERIFIED). [P9]
- **Defining feature:** joint UNESCO World Heritage Site "Melaka and George Town, Historic Cities of the Straits of Malacca," inscribed 7 July 2008, criteria (ii)(iii)(iv), on the basis of their "multi-cultural trading heritage" (snippets of UNESCO decision pages, UNVERIFIED). Melaka core zone 45.3 ha, buffer 242.8 ha (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K1]
- **Founding:** c.1400 by Parameswara, a prince from Singapura who later converted to Islam (OpenStax World History vol. 2, §2.2, SOURCED). [P6]

### B. What else exists there
**The waiting port (core of the claim).**
- Ships from the west and the Far East arrived **December–March**; ships from Java and eastern Indonesia **May–September**; "goods coming in from the east or west had to be stored awaiting the arrival of other traders"; cargo carried by elephants to warehouses; customs duty **6% of cargo value** paid before trade with resident merchants; **four syahbandars** (harbor masters): one for Gujaratis; one for South Indians and Bengalis; one for local merchants (Java, Palembang, Borneo); one for the Chinese; the trading community spoke "more than 80 languages"; port governed under the Hukum Kanun Melaka (speech by Nor Mohamed Yakcop, The Edge Malaysia, issue 805, 10–16 May 2010, SOURCED; a ministerial speech, secondary). [K4, K5, K8, K1, P5]
- A second account puts the sailing pattern as November–April downwind toward the Strait and May–October with the reversed monsoon (snippet of a secondary source, UNVERIFIED). Another notes departures "sometimes delayed" by changes in the monsoon and by merchants staying to finish their business (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Pires** (in Malacca 1512–1515; wrote most of the Suma Oriental there): Malacca "is at the end of the monsoons, where you find what you want, and sometimes more than you are looking for"; "no trading port as large as Malacca is known" (Suma Oriental, Hakluyt ed., vol. I, p. 228, archive.org text, SOURCED). Pires on traders from Borneo: "They do not pay duties in Malacca; they hand over a present, which comes to the same thing," and "They have every year two monsoons to bring them and two others to take them back"; the Chinese, Ryukyu, Cochin China and Champa traders fell under the syahbandar titled the Laksamana (same, vol. I, OCR text, SOURCED). Pires's own Malacca book (vol. II, pp. 229–278) NOT READ. 84 languages attributed to Pires (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K8, K1]
- Rice supply: about 50–60 junks of rice a year from Java, 30 from Siam, 20 from Pegu (snippet of a secondary source, UNVERIFIED). [K5]
- **Maritime law (Undang-Undang Laut Melaka):** covered sailors' conduct toward the captain, the order of trade on arrival ("the captain was allowed to trade his goods first"), and penalties for quarrels and theft among passengers; the land code (Undang-Undang Melaka) punished robbery, adultery and drunkenness (OpenStax, SOURCED). [K6, K1]
- **Population c.1510:** "approximately ten thousand homes"; author infers at least 40,000 people (Katrina Gulliver, "European Perceptions of Malacca in the Early Modern Period," BiblioAsia, 10 Oct 2009, SOURCED). [P9]

**Conquests and colonial periods (sensitive history, reported plainly).**
- **1511:** Afonso de Albuquerque arrived with over 1,000 armed troops, waited a week, then bombarded the city; Hindu, Chinese and Burmese merchants helped the Portuguese land; within a month the Portuguese held the city and Sultan Mahmud Shah fled; a fortress was built and the Hindu merchant Nina Chatu made chief administrator (OpenStax, SOURCED). Portuguese restricted Muslim trade and never regained the earlier prosperity; c.1615 about a hundred Portuguese families, a bishop and a Jesuit college (Gulliver 2009, SOURCED). [K9]
- **1641:** VOC and Johor took the city after a siege of roughly five to six months (August 1640–January 1641); the city was weakened by disease and starvation; most Portuguese left; many Indian Hindus who had worked with the Portuguese also left (snippets, UNVERIFIED). The Dutch brought few civilian settlers; a 1719 account called the trade "entirely decayed" yet the place still the "magazine of the Eastern trade" (Gulliver, SOURCED). [K9]
- **British:** briefly from 1795; transferred under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 17 March 1824 (in exchange for Bencoolen/Bengkulu); part of the Straits Settlements 1826–1946 (Gulliver SOURCED; dates UNVERIFIED snippets).
- **Japanese occupation:** Malacca fell 15 January 1942; after the war Chinese residents gathered victims' remains and buried them at the foot of Bukit China; the Melaka Warrior Monument there was unveiled in 1948 (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Malaya-wide Sook Ching deaths are estimated at 25,000–55,000 ethnic Chinese (snippet, UNVERIFIED; Melaka-specific figure NOT FOUND). [K9]
- **20 Feb 1956:** Tunku Abdul Rahman announced the date of Malaya's independence at Padang Pahlawan, Bandar Hilir, before a reported 100,000 people; 20 February is now a state holiday, replacing the "Historical City Declaration Day" holiday of 15 April (snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K2]

**Economy and work today.**
- 2024: over 15 million visitors and RM12.68 billion tourism revenue (one count), or 13,584,042 tourists by November under Visit Melaka Year 2024 (another count); state GDP RM48.9 billion in 2024 (+4.4%); Chinese arrivals 204,818 (2023) → 664,687 (2024); 143,218 cruise passengers Nov 2024–Oct 2025 (snippets, UNVERIFIED). - **Trishaws:** about 300 riders in 2024; **260 riders licensed for 2025** after interviews and health screening; unique ID numbers and QR codes; zone rotation every three months; booking via the council app; earlier complaints about loud music led the council to make music choice and volume a criterion for license renewal (snippets of Star/Bernama/Sinar, UNVERIFIED; Star 23 Jun 2024 SOURCED only for the reason: older riders struggling to pedal, some asking passengers to pedal). [K6, K14, R]
- **Fishermen:** about 1,000 registered fishermen given state aid (GPS, fish finders, social-security cover) (snippets, UNVERIFIED). National monsoon aid RM300/month to fishermen who cannot go to sea in the northeast monsoon (Nov–Mar) (snippet, UNVERIFIED; general, not Melaka-specific). [K7, K1]
- **Present-day waiting ships:** close to 250 fishermen from seven coastal areas (Kampung Hailam, Pantai Puteri, Balik Bukit, Tanjung Kling, Sungai Lereh, Klebang, Perigi) lost fishing grounds to large merchant ships and tankers anchoring along the coast; the state said it would reorganize mooring off Tanjung Kling (one ship per designated area, ships in one line) and review scheduling; fishermen asked to report anchoring ships to the Marine Department (snippets of Star/Sinar/Malay Mail 2023, UNVERIFIED; Star 4 Oct 2023 SOURCED only for the quote: "The growing maritime traffic along the route has reduced areas for merchant ships and oil tankers to anchor"). ## Pick #72 — Khao San Road, Bangkok (Thailand)

**Claim to test:** "A street that became a meeting and information hub for travelers on their way elsewhere (K10, K4)."

## Pick #73 — Lloyd's Coffee House, London (England)

**Claim to test:** "A coffee house where shipping news collected because everyone connected to the sea passed through it (K10, K8)."

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with a sharpening.** The coffee house drew ship owners, captains, merchants, brokers and underwriters, and Edward Lloyd deliberately supplied shipping news to keep them: a pulpit from which news and auction prices were read, his own news-sheet (1696–97), a network of port correspondents, candle auctions of ships and cargoes. News did not only "collect"; the house actively built an information service, which later became formal institutions (Lloyd's List 1734; Lloyd's Register 1760/1764; the Lloyd's Agents network 1811; the insurance market itself). The same network also served the transatlantic slave trade (insurance of slaving voyages; the coffee house named as a return point in runaway-slave advertisements), reported below plainly.

### A. Snapshot
- **What and where:** coffee house opened by Edward Lloyd (c.1648–15 Feb 1713) on Tower Street (first record: London Gazette, February 1688, a notice offering a reward for five stolen watches; other sources give 1686 or 1687); moved December 1691 to 16 Lombard Street, opposite the Royal Exchange; Lloyd probably came from Canterbury c.1680 (lloyds.com Edward Lloyd page; lloyds.com "Coffee and commerce"; en.wikipedia.org Lloyd's Coffee House; all SOURCED). [P3]
- **Plaque today:** "Site of Lloyds Coffee House, 1691 - 1785. Corporation of London," 15 Lombard Street (londonremembers.com, SOURCED); ground floor now a supermarket (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K9]
- **Defining feature:** the place in the City where maritime information was gathered and traded; London then had over 80 coffee houses, each serving a different group (lloyds.com, SOURCED). [K10]

### B. What else exists there (then and its successors)
**How the house worked.**
- Staff "three men and two girls" serving coffee, tea, sherbet and fruit punch and supplying pens, ink and paper; regular candle auctions of ships' cargoes; a news sheet on ships, cargo and foreign events; correspondents in European ports; Lloyd was a sidesman at St Mary Woolnoth and served as constable and questman (lloyds.com Edward Lloyd page, SOURCED). [K14, K7]
- "Lloyd had a pulpit installed in the new premises, from which maritime auction prices and shipping news were announced" (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Addison (The Spectator) describes the "Auction Pulpit" from which the house boy read a lost paper aloud "to the whole Room" (quoted in *Merchants, mariners and mavericks – Lloyd's Agents, the first 200 years*, Lee Coppack and Lucy Jolin, for Lloyd's, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). "The coffee house had no entry requirements or restrictions. Debate was robust in the crowded and smoky atmosphere ... at times it was more like a gaming house" (same, SOURCED). [K10, K6]
- Brokers rented "boxes" (tables) to sell insurance; the name "box" survives in today's Underwriting Room (lloyds.com, SOURCED). [K10]
- **Candle auctions:** a pin set about an inch below the wick; lot sold when the candle burned down to the pin; Lloyd's sold ships, tobacco, coffee, timber, sugar, cloth and wine; under Queen Anne so many auctions were held there that it "developed into one of London's major sales rooms"; the slaving vessel *James and Frances* was auctioned there in 1700; no evidence found of enslaved people sold at Lloyd's candle auctions; auctions continued to the early 19th century (John Lane, Insurance Museum, "Candle Auctions," 30 Mar 2024, SOURCED). [K8, K9]
- **Lloyd's News:** three times a week from 1696; ended February 1697 after a libel dispute over text his printer added (Lloyd's Register "Edward Lloyd coffee house" page, SOURCED); Britannica 1911 says a paragraph about the House of Lords required a public apology and he chose to stop (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K6]
- **Succession:** codicil 28 Jan 1713 gave the lease to head waiter William Newton, who married Lloyd's youngest daughter Handy on 30 Jan; Lloyd died 15 Feb 1713; Jemsons took over 1727; **Thomas Jemson founded Lloyd's List in 1734**; Samuel Saunders proprietor until 1763; New Lloyd's Coffee House opened 21 Mar 1769 at 5 Pope's Head Alley, with a New Lloyd's List a week later; 1771, 79 members agreed to pay £100 each toward a building; 1774 members moved to the Royal Exchange and became underwriting-only (lr.org history page, SOURCED). [K1]
- **Lloyd's List:** weekly 1734; twice weekly 1737; earliest surviving issue 2 January 1741 (modern dating); daily Monday–Saturday from 1 July 1837; first annual index 1838; movements and casualties withheld in both world wars and issued confidentially (Royal Museums Greenwich research guide H1, SOURCED). Final print issue (No. 60,850) in December 2013, then online only (snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K5]
- **Lloyd's Register:** Society for the Registry of Shipping formed in 1760 by underwriters, brokers, shipowners and merchants who associated through the coffee house; first Register Book 1764, yearly since; recorded name, rig, master, tonnage, decks, place and year of build, owner, class, voyage; subscriptions paid surveyors who inspected hull and equipment and assigned a class (heritage.lrfoundation.org.uk "18th century register books," SOURCED). A rival shipowners' register appeared in 1799; the two merged in 1834 (snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K14, K1]

**The intelligence network (K5, K14).**
- 1739: Richard Baker, master of the coffee house, carried news of the Porto Bello victory to 10 Downing Street; 1794: Lloyd's learned of a British ship's capture off Lowestoft before the Admiralty (lloyds.com, SOURCED). [SM:competence under pressure]
- 1805: Lloyd's appointed Goodwin, Curling & Co. at Deal (Goodwin Sands, "more than 2,000 ships") as agents; first five months saved owners and underwriters about £2,000 in 32 cases. 1771: 79 subscribers; 1809: 1,400–1,500, two-thirds underwriters. 1811 inquiry under Joseph Marryat recommended "a regular and universal system of intelligence and superintendence"; trust deed binding subscribers; Committee of 12; 140 places named (59 in Great Britain); first 20 Agents approved 13 Nov 1811; agents unsalaried, earning commission; first duty "prompt and regular advices of the arrival and sailing of vessels, of their being in circumstances of danger and distress, of the appearance of the enemy's cruisers or privateers" (*Merchants, mariners and mavericks*, ch. 1, SOURCED). [K5, K14, K1]
- London coffee houses generally kept bags for letters to be forwarded by merchant ships, a private traffic "contrary to the law"; the Post Office opened a Ship Letter Office in 1798/1799 and admitted in 1809 it could not enforce the law; an 1818 prosecution of the New England Coffee House keeper was settled by a £5 penalty (Royal Philatelic Society London display, Ian Marshall, 2016, p. 85, SOURCED; Lloyd's not named on that page). Letters found on captured ships were often addressed to individuals at London coffee houses (Anna Brinkman-Schwartz, IHR blog, 28 Sep 2021, SOURCED). [K5]

**Lloyd's today (successor institution).**
- Building at 1 Lime Street, Richard Rogers Partnership, opened 1986, Grade I listed 19 Dec 2011 (snippets of Historic England list entry, UNVERIFIED). Gross written premium £55.5bn in 2024 (+6.5%) (Lloyd's press release snippet, UNVERIFIED). Names peaked at about 32,000–34,000 in 1988; corporate capital admitted 1994; Names' losses in the asbestos and catastrophe years (c.£8bn) led to Reconstruction and Renewal and Equitas (snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K8, K9]
- Underwriting Room: ground floor and galleries 1–2; clear-desk policy (no marketing material at the boxes beyond digital signage); visitors need photo ID; dress "to generally accepted City standards," with religious and cultural dress supported; weekend access only for pass holders with advance notice; coffee "to the box" by app; liveried waiters (Lloyd's Underwriting Room Guide, Sept 2023, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). Waiters in red or blue uniforms call brokers with messages, ring the Lutine Bell and write the Loss Book (kept since 1774) with quill and ink (lloyds.com "Lloyd's in 14 floors," SOURCED). The tie requirement was relaxed in recent years (Insurance Journal 2021 snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K6, K7]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Because shipping people gathered there, the house became a sales room (auctions), a newsroom (pulpit, Lloyd's News, Lloyd's List), a registry (Lloyd's Register) and a risk market (boxes, subscription book "at two guineas a year" in 1750, formal subscription market 1771) (lloyds.com, SOURCED). [K8, K1]
- Its information edge mattered in wartime: Britain was at war for 58 of the years 1700–1820; Lloyd's underwrote British ships against French capture and French ships against British capture (*Merchants, mariners and mavericks*, SOURCED); Patriotic Fund 1803; £38,000 raised after the Nile (1798) (lloyds.com, SOURCED). [K8]
- Speculative wagering on the lives of public figures in the 1750s–60s led professional underwriters to break away in 1769 (IRMI, John Bogardus, 1 Sep 2007, SOURCED); the Life Assurance Act 1774 required an insurable interest (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K6, K9]

## Pick #74 — Post Office Bay, Floreana (Isla Santa María, Galápagos, Ecuador)

**Claim to test:** "A barrel where passing sailors leave letters for others to carry home, a mail custom built only from traffic (K2, K5)."

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with corrections.** (1) The container was first a **box nailed to a post** under a sign reading "Hathaway's Postoffice" (Porter's crew, 1813), later a terrapin-shell-roofed box, and the present barrel tradition dates to HMS *Leander* (1897–98) and later restorations; the popular "James Colnett, 1793" origin is disputed (Colnett wrote that he never landed). (2) It worked because there was **no regular mail service** to Pacific whalers: outbound whalers left letters, homeward-bound ships checked the addresses and carried them. (3) Its letters were also **intelligence**: Porter used them in 1813 to locate British whalers. (4) Once the island was settled (1832) the box went unused for a time, as settlers held letters at the settlement (FitzRoy, 1835). (5) Today the custom is maintained by tourists, stampless, with hand delivery expected.

### A. Snapshot
- **Island:** Floreana (official name Isla Santa María; formerly Charles Island), 173 km²; more than 98% national park (del Campo Tejedor & Ruiz-Ballesteros, *Journal of Marine and Island Cultures* 10(1), 2021, SOURCED). Parish (parroquia rural) Isla Santa María, canton San Cristóbal, province of Galápagos; only settlement **Puerto Velasco Ibarra**; **145 inhabitants (INEC census 2022)**; 49 households (2022; 41 in 2010) (PDOT Isla Santa María 2023–2027, Registro Oficial Edición Especial Nº 315, 24 June 2025, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). [P9]
- **Post Office Bay:** north coast; Galápagos National Park "Managed intensive use site"; permitted uses: interpretive group tours with a naturalist guide, photography, guided walks, activities on authorized vessel itineraries; also the remains of a 1920s Norwegian settlement and a lava tube; warning against fragranced sunscreen because of introduced wasps (*Polistes versicolor*) (GNP site description via whalesite.org, SOURCED). [K6, P3]

### B. What else exists there
**History of the post (core of the claim).**
- **1813:** Lt. Downes of USS *Essex* returned with papers "taken from a box which he found nailed to a post, over which was a black sign, on which was painted Hathaway's Postoffice"; the papers told which British whale-ships had put in; whalers "generally take on board from two to three hundred" tortoises; a donor had left "a cask of water" near the post for people in distress (Porter, *Journal of a Cruise*, via galapagos.to, SOURCED). [K5, K1, SM:dangerous system]
- Colnett misattribution: "Post Office" absent from Arrowsmith's 1798 chart, added c.1820 (probably from Fyffe's 1815 chart); Colnett wrote "we then shortened sail, and stood off and on during the night." FitzRoy (24 Sept 1835): a box on a post; "since the island has been peopled the box has been empty." Davis (Dec 1835): a large terrapin shell roofed the box. Petit-Thouars (1838): whalers left notes in a bottle near a tree. HMS *Leander* placed a barrel in 1897–98; 1924 sign of the British St. George expedition; 1934 crew of the Polish *Dar Pomorza* restored the barrel; tourism from c.1960 (whalesite.org "POBARREL," SOURCED). National Geographic: origin "opaque," first barrel "may have come from a crew in the 1890s" (Nina Strochlic, 18 Sep 2017, SOURCED). [K2, K9]
- **Why it existed:** no regular mail service reached Pacific whalers; letters passed ship to ship ("gams") or via the Charles Island drop (Stony Brook LIHJ and other snippets, UNVERIFIED; Philip K. Allan blog, 6 May 2019, SOURCED but uncited). Voyages lasted two to three years (Allan, SOURCED). [K5, K7]
- **Whaling and tortoises:** logbooks of 79 whaling vessels, 189 visits, 1831–1868; 13,013 tortoises recorded or estimated; Charles Island recorded catch 1,775 (+531 estimated); "the tortoises practically disappeared during that decade" (the 1830s), after colonization in 1832; logs record anchoring at Post Office Bay in 1832–33 and 1834; tortoises stored alive in holds for months (C. H. Townsend, *Zoologica* IV(3), 1925, via ploughboy anthology, SOURCED, first 100,000 characters). [K9, K12]

**Settlement history (sensitive, reported plainly).**
- **12 Feb 1832:** Col. Ignacio Hernández took possession of the archipelago for Ecuador at Floreana; expedition organized by José de Villamil left Guayaquil on 22 Jan 1832 on the schooner *Mercedes*; President Flores also intended the islands to hold political prisoners, criminals and mutinous soldiers; first confined people reached Floreana in April or mid-May 1832; Villamil issued property titles by late 1832; penal use of the islands continued into the 20th century (El Comercio, 2021/updated, SOURCED). Settlers of both sexes arrived April–June 1832 (Charles Darwin Foundation "Galapagueana," SOURCED).
- **Darwin's diary, 23–27 Sept 1835:** settlement "of only five to 6 years' standing," about 4.5 miles inland; "The inhabitants are in number 200-300; nearly all are people of color & banished for Political crimes"; sweet potatoes and plantains; "The main evil under which these islands suffer is the scarcity of water"; tortoise meat the main animal food; Englishman Lawson acting governor (galapagos.to DIARY, SOURCED). [K9, K5]
- **1925–1927 Norwegian colony at Post Office Bay:** arrived 10 Aug 1925 on the schooner *Floreana*; built a main building and power-plant shed; income from cargo and whaling never came; supplies and mail irregular; by Oct 1926 demoralized; last three men left January 1927; concrete posts, tanks and a slab remain (Lundh, "Galápagos: A Brief History," galapagos.to, SOURCED). [K9]
- **1929–1934:** Friedrich Ritter and Dore Strauch (1929); the Wittmer family (1932), who farmed near the main spring, "Asilo de la Paz"; a baroness and companions (1932). The baroness and one companion disappeared in March 1934, fate unknown; Ritter died in November 1934 (food poisoning per Lundh); accounts by survivors contradict each other (Lundh SOURCED; other details snippets UNVERIFIED). Later families: Cruz (1939), Salgado (1946); new generations from 1974 (PDOT, citing Claudio Cruz, SOURCED). [K9]

**Life today (PDOT 2025 unless noted; SOURCED).**
- Work: 91 in the occupied labor categories (24 state employees, 30 private employees, 10 day laborers, 14 self-employed…); working-age 112, 79% employed. Tourism and farming are the livelihoods; crops maize, cassava, plantain, citrus, coffee, pineapple, banana, sugarcane, legumes, pasture; Asociación de Productores Agropecuarios Verde Floreana (36 members, 2024). In 2020: 46 of 147 people had state jobs (paying about double mainland rates), 35 earned from tourism, 13 construction, 13 agriculture (JMIC 2021, SOURCED). [K7, K8]
- **Water:** springs at Asilo de la Paz (~0.5 L/s, main year-round source) and La Baronesa; untreated water piped down; in scarcity, 46 households supplied on set hours and days; 21 households catch roof rain; a desalination plant with a 250 m³ tank near the cemetery; Hotel Wittmer runs two small desalinators; tests in 2017/2021 found consumption water incompatible with health standards. A 7-km pipeline built by collective labor in the 1970s; consumption limited to about 30 liters per week per person (JMIC 2021). [K1, K5, K9]
- **Supply and access:** no airport (nearest San Cristóbal and Baltra); fiberglass passenger launches mostly from Santa Cruz; heliport for park and science use only; cargo from Guayaquil by M/N *Paola* once a month and the *Gabarra Isabela* once a month, otherwise bought via Santa Cruz. Electricity: 100% coverage; hybrid system of a 21 kWp solar plant with batteries and 0.29 MW dual-fuel (jatropha oil/diesel) generators from 2010; Starlink internet. Passenger movements to Floreana: 10,132 in 2023; 3,119 in 2021 (Biosecurity Agency data). [K5]
- **Services:** Unidad Educativa Amazonas, 31 students (Inicial II to 2nd year of Bachillerato, Sept 2024); level-1 health post with a rural doctor, dentist and nurse, 131 permanent residents attended in 2023; Catholic church and an evangelical church (Hosana); no culture house. [K10]
- **Community tourism:** CECFLOR (Centro Comunitario Floreana), created 2014, 77 participants, seven guesthouses (≈3 rooms each), five restaurants; shared governance with the National Park; received 1,283 overnight tourists in 2023 (PDOT). Before, day tours on up to 10 boats a day, about 20,000 visits a year, and only about 20% of revenue stayed on the island; in 2018 the community chose tourism degrowth; the community is split on visitor numbers (JMIC 2021). Only CECFLOR is authorized to run daily tours to Post Office Bay with local guides (Moon snippet, UNVERIFIED; page 403). [K1, K8, K9]
- **Restoration:** two rodent species and feral cats targeted in late 2023; 98% of cats and nearly 60% of dogs sterilized in a July 2022 campaign (snippet, UNVERIFIED); **158 juvenile tortoises of Floreana lineage released the week of 20 Feb 2026**, bred from Wolf Volcano animals with Floreana ancestry, the first of 12 species to return; the island's ~160 residents took part in planning, biosecurity and monitoring (Charles Darwin Foundation newsroom, 20 Feb 2026, SOURCED). [K1, K14]

**Verdict so far: PARTLY CONFIRMED, with a correction about who created it and why.** Elko is a railroad town (Central Pacific townsite laid out 29 Dec 1868) and the Gathering is held in late January and is now the town's best-known event. But the Gathering was **not created by the town**: it was organized in 1985 by folklorists (Hal Cannon and colleagues, later the Western Folklife Center) with local buckaroo poet Waddie Mitchell, and the stated reason for late January is that **ranch work slows then**, so working cowboys could attend (LARB 2019); Elko was chosen because it had a convention center and inexpensive lodging (snippet). The town and county later came to treat it as an economic event (County Commission asked the organizers not to cancel in 2020; ECVA co-plans; hotels fill). ### A–B. Additions: work, money, people
- **Mine work rhythm (K7):** Nevada Gold Mines is the Barrick–Newmont joint venture, announced 11 March 2019, with Barrick in control. It employs more than 7,000 people. Shifts are often 12 hours, and a workday can approach 15 hours with the commute. Crews rotate between day and night shifts. Coach buses collect miners from lots around town before sunrise, seven days a week. Average pay was claimed at $94,000 (a company figure, not independently checked). Operating Engineers Local 3 contract (about 1,300 workers, early 2019): the company stopped recognizing it in late 2019, then settled in August 2020 after National Labor Relations Board action, with new charges filed in 2021. A former employee calls Elko "a company town." Company spending in town has included $5 million in pandemic loans to local businesses, about $30 million for area broadband, and $8.4 million in social investment in 2020 (Nick Bowlin & Daniel Rothberg, *High Country News* with *The Nevada Independent*, 1 Jan 2022, SOURCED). Rotations in job postings include 5-on/4-off and 14-on/14-off with camp housing (search results only, UNVERIFIED). A contractor bus carrying mine workers collided with an ore truck on State Route 766 in 2019 (Mining Weekly snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K7, K8, K9, SM:a dangerous or fascinating figure or system, P4, P8]
- **Boom and bust (K9):** The city's census population was 1,160 in 1870, 752 in 1880, 6,298 in 1960, 8,758 in 1980, 14,736 in 1990, 16,708 in 2000, 18,297 in 2010 and 20,564 in 2020. The economy follows gold prices: a 1980s boom ended in a late-1990s bust, and a new boom began in 2009 (en.wikipedia.org "Elko, Nevada," SOURCED; the bust years themselves were not checked against a primary source). Elko County grew from 17,269 to 33,530 between 1980 and 1990 (Wikipedia snippet, UNVERIFIED). The Carlin discovery dates to spring 1961 (Newmont geologist John Livermore). Drilling began in 1962, and the first gold bar was poured on 4 May 1965 (republicofmining.com reprints of the Elko Daily Free Press, May 2015, snippet only, UNVERIFIED; page refused 403). [K9, P6]
- **Ranch calendar (K7):** UNR Extension modeled a representative Elko County cow-calf ranch: 700 cows; one hired hand plus the owner, with summer help from local workers and the family. Federal grazing runs mid-April to mid-November; hay and grain are fed through winter into early spring; cows are pregnancy-checked in November; calves are sold by summer video auction for November/December delivery ("Elko County Cow-Calf Production Costs & Returns," Curtis, Brough, Torell & Riggs, UNR Extension FS-07-08, 2006, SOURCED). On the Spanish Ranch (Elko County), the spring works begin in early May (Western Horseman snippet, UNVERIFIED). This fits the Gathering's stated reason for late January: winter is feeding time at the home ranch, not the season of moving or branding cattle. That link is drawn from the sources here; no source states it for Elko in those words. [K7, D]
- **Chinese Elko (K9, K4):** Elko's water supply came out of the Chinese gardens. Chinese immigrants Hop Sing and Hi Loy held the town water system from 1883. In 1888–89 they rebuilt it with a new main from Kittridge Canyon. Hi Loy, a merchant, and his foreman Le Hi are reported to have built a wooden flume from the canyon to a small reservoir above the China Ranch, now Elko City Park. A later ditch, about nine miles long, ran from Osino on the Humboldt River (the "China Ditch"). Hi Loy's store was where water bills were collected in 1885. Hi Loy left for China in 1898. In 1898 the Hop Sing Tong sold a half interest in the water company for $1,000, per a 2011 account citing county deeds. Hop Sing sold his remaining share in 1903. The city bought the works after a bond election on 11 January 1921, passed by 25 votes, and completed the purchase in 1922. Sources disagree on whether Hop Sing and Hi Loy were two people (waterworkshistory.us "Elko, Nevada," citing the *Elko Weekly/Daily Independent* 1875–1917 and Chung 2011 / Chang & Fishkin 2019, SOURCED). An "Early Inhabitants" marker reportedly places two joss houses and homes near Fifth and Silver Streets (search result only, UNVERIFIED; hmdb page 403). [K9, K1, K14, R]
- **Western Shoshone (K9), treaty history in more detail:** The following comes from the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, *Mary and Carrie Dann v. United States*, Report 75/02 (2002), as hosted at humanrts.umn.edu (SOURCED; first 100,000 of 181,380 characters read). The Treaty of Ruby Valley was signed 1 Oct 1863, ratified in 1866 and proclaimed 21 Oct 1869. The Temoak Band filed its claim in 1951. In 1962 the Indian Claims Commission found Western Shoshone aboriginal title to 24,396,403 acres, extinguished by "gradual encroachment." In 1966 the parties stipulated an average extinguishment date of 1 July 1872. The award was $26,145,189.89, about $0.10–0.15 per acre, without interest, and the Court of Claims affirmed it in 1979. In 1974 the United States sued the Dann sisters for grazing trespass. The Supreme Court ruled against them in 1985, holding that "payment" occurred when the award was appropriated to the Treasury. The award was put in an interest-bearing Treasury account and had not been distributed. Many Western Shoshone refused the money. In 1992 the BLM impounded horses (161 in March, 269 in November), and in 1998 it imposed a $288,191.78 fine. The Commission found that the United States had failed to ensure the Danns' right to property under conditions of equality. A 2004 Western Shoshone Claims Distribution Act was mentioned only by an advocacy source (UNVERIFIED). [K9]
- **Treaty commemoration (K2):** In 2023 the Noowuh Knowledge Center (executive director Mary Gibson, Te-Moak/Elko Band) held a three-day conference for the treaty's 160th anniversary, with Great Basin College as partner. Friday and Saturday were held at GBC and Sunday at the Elko Indian Colony Gymnasium. Gibson called it a treaty "of peace and… friendship" and said the conference honored the ancestors rather than the treaty itself (Noah Glick, *Sierra Nevada Ally*, 28 Sep 2023, SOURCED). The state's 2026 tribal calendar lists a "Ruby Valley Treaty Conference, Elko & Ruby Valley, Oct 1-4," which suggests it recurs (Nevada Dept. of Native American Affairs, "NV Tribes Important Dates," Sept 2026 PDF, SOURCED, read locally). [K2, K9, P6]
- **Elko Band events (K2, K10):** The "28th Annual Elko Band Powwow" was held 9–11 October 2026 at 2250 Indian View Heights (the Colony gym) (NV DNAA calendar, SOURCED). Search results also list a Western Shoshone fandango (Sept 2025), a spring flea market at the Colony gym and a social powwow for fentanyl awareness (search result only, UNVERIFIED). [K2, K10]

### C. Additions: cultural locations (named)
- **Star Hotel**, near Silver and 2nd Streets: opened 1910 by Pete and Matilde Jauregui for Basque sheepherders between stints with the flocks. It was enlarged under demand. Basque ranch women gave birth there with Matilde helping the doctor, and dances and weddings were held there. It has been owned only by Basque families for 100 years (owners in 2010: Scott and Tricia Ygoa), and a list of the owning families hangs on the bar wall. In 2010 six boarders lived upstairs, and the hotel closed about a week each April for maintenance. Its centennial was marked on 4 July 2010 during the 47th National Basque Festival, with mus played in the dining room (*Euskal Kazeta*, 28 Jun 2010, SOURCED). Meals are served family-style with no menu, called by a dinner bell (Travel Nevada snippet, UNVERIFIED). Picon punch is served there (Allison Williams, *Seattle Met*, 26 Feb 2020, SOURCED). [K10, K12, K4]
- **Ogi Deli** (opened 2017): pintxos and Basque cider; mus card game on Fridays (*Seattle Met*, SOURCED; opening year from a snippet, UNVERIFIED). **Toki Ona** is named in a visitor guide only (UNVERIFIED). [K10, K12]
- **Elko Ariñak Dancers**: founded 1966 with 25 members; first president Rossana Aguirre. They first practiced in a backyard or patio, then in the Catholic church basement, dancing at first to a cassette by Jimmy Jausoro. Today there are three age groups (4–6, 7–12, 13–18) and a singing instructor; the first out-of-town show was in Ely (euskalkultura.eus, SOURCED). The Elko Euzkaldunak Club (1959) runs the National Basque Festival at the Elko Basque Clubhouse on Flagview Drive (search results, UNVERIFIED). [K2, K13, K14]
- **Silver State Stampede** at the Elko County Fairgrounds: started in 1912 by saddle maker G.S. Garcia as "a community gathering and competition for local ranch cowboys" (1913 in some sources). It calls itself the oldest rodeo in Nevada and joined the PRCA in 1988. It is the only PRCA rodeo to award handmade Garcia spurs, and paid out $180,675 in 2024. It is usually held the second weekend of July, with a parade, kick-off concert, trade show, dances after the Friday and Saturday shows, and a local Grand Marshal each year (Becky Lisle, *Ropers Sports News*, 11 Sep 2024, SOURCED). It is run by a volunteer non-profit association (chamber listing snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K2, K14, SM:competence shown under pressure]
- **Elko County Fair**: Labor Day weekend at the fairgrounds (the former China Ranch). Live Thoroughbred and Quarter Horse racing with pari-mutuel betting, 4-H livestock show, stock-horse events, Nevada Stallion Stakes. The first fair was held 16–18 Sep 1920 (search results, UNVERIFIED; exploreelko and onlyinyourstate pages truncated or refused). [K2]
- **Great Basin College**: founded 1967 by ten Elko businessmen, who raised $45,000 in 30 days. First classes were held in the basement of Grammar School #1 as Elko Community College. A gift from Howard Hughes kept it open after 1969 budget cuts. It moved in 1973 to the former Ruby View Golf Course as Northern Nevada Community College. Diesel technology and nursing were added in the 1970s, and electrical systems, industrial maintenance and welding in the 1980s "as mining grew." It was renamed in 1995 and began a B.A. in Education in 1999. It now serves Nevada's 10 most rural counties, 86,514 square miles (GBC catalog "History at a Glance," SOURCED). [K14, K1, R]
- **California Trail Interpretive Center**: run by the BLM, 8 miles west of Elko at I-80 exit 292, free entry. It covers 1841–1869 and the Indigenous cultures the trail affected. Its winter schedule cuts opening to Thursday–Monday, 9:00–4:30 (BLM Elko District release, 25 Oct 2019, SOURCED); seven days a week from May (BLM 2024 snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K7, K13]
- **Commercial Hotel / Stockmen's**: the Commercial began as the Humboldt Lodging House in 1869 beside the tracks. Newt Crumley Jr. booked Ted Lewis in spring 1941 (opening night given as 26 April 1941), and it is reported as the first Nevada resort to hire big-name entertainers, a practice that lasted over two decades. Stockmen's was a rival hotel-casino (search results only, UNVERIFIED; NPR 2008 and GBC "Howard Hickson's Histories" pages could not be read). [K13, K8]

### D. Additions: how the defining feature shaped things

**Unit `Casey_C18`, Round 1: all six picks done.** Finished 2026-11-21 MST.

**Claims tested (one line each):**
- **#70 Hội An:** confirmed, with a correction. A yearly monsoon-governed trade season of about four months, with separately governed Chinese and Japanese quarters and a seasonal doubling of residents. The "waiting" was a whole seasonal economy, not idle merchants (details in its section).
- **#71 Malacca:** confirmed in its historical form. Goods were stored between the two monsoon systems, and foreign traders were split among four harbor masters by origin. Today ships anchor offshore to wait, crowding out local fishermen (2020–2023).
- **#72 Khao San Road:** confirmed for about the 1980s–2000s (onward-travel agents, tickets, visas); narrowed for today, now mostly a nightlife strip shared with young Thais.
- **#73 Lloyd's Coffee House:** confirmed and sharpened. Edward Lloyd deliberately built a news service (a pulpit, a news-sheet, port correspondents, auctions), and it became formal institutions. The same network also served the slave trade.
- **#74 Post Office Bay:** confirmed, with corrections. A box on a post (1813) before the barrel (from 1897–98). It worked because whalers had no mail service, and its letters were also used as intelligence (Porter, 1813).
- **#33 Elko / National Cowboy Poetry Gathering:** partly confirmed. Elko is a railroad and I-80 stop town, and the winter quarter yields the least room tax. But folklorists created the Gathering, timed to the ranch-work lull; the town did not. The town's tourism body backed it later.
