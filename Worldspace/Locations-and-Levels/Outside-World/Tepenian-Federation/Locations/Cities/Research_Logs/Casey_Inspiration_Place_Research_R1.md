## 1 · Flagstaff, Arizona [PRIMARY]

### A. Snapshot

- **Location:** Coconino County, northern Arizona; about 146 mi north of Phoenix; 35°10′50″N 111°37′12″W. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Flagstaff, Arizona," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagstaff,_Arizona (accessed 2026-10-26).
- **Elevation:** 6,821 ft (2,079 m) official; "approximately 7,000 feet" in common use. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, same page.
- **Area:** 66.08 sq mi total (66.03 land). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Population:** 76,831 (2020 Census); metro (Coconino County) 145,101. Up 16.6% from 65,870 in 2010. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia. City's own series (Arizona Dept. of Administration / AZ Commerce Authority): 70,643 (2016) → 80,566 (2024) → 81,447 (2025, estimated). **SOURCED** — City of Flagstaff, *Annual Comprehensive Financial Report FY 2024-25*, Schedule 18, https://public.destinyhosted.com/flagsdocs/2026/CCMTG/20260421_4656/9952_FY_2024-25_ACFR_-_Final.pdf (council agenda 2026-04-21; read via local PDF-to-text).
- **Median age:** 26.3 (2020, Wikipedia); 25.9–26.0 in the city's 2022–2025 table. Very young because of the university. **SOURCED** — both above.
- **Ethnic composition (2020 Census):** White 66.2%; Hispanic/Latino 19.7%; American Indian/Alaska Native 11.2% (8,615); Asian 2.6%; Black 2.1%; two or more races 10.6%. "About 5,500 people of Navajo ancestry." **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Climate:** cold semi-arid / hemiboreal; avg precipitation 21.6 in/yr; avg snowfall 97 in/yr; record daily snowfall 35.9 in (2019-02-21); sunshine ~78% of the year; record low −30°F (Jan 1937). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **What it is:** a mountain college-and-railroad town on a high plateau inside a ponderosa-pine forest, at the junction of I-40 (east–west) and I-17 (to Phoenix), with the BNSF Southern Transcon main line and historic Route 66 running through downtown. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Founding:** first permanent settlement 1876 (Thomas F. McMillan's cabin); name from a stripped-pine flagpole raised at the spring on 1876-07-04; incorporated as a town 1894, city 1928. By 1886 it was "the largest city on the railroad line between Albuquerque and the West Coast." **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Defining feature (as the sources frame it):** altitude and a dark, clear sky in a pine forest on a transcontinental corridor. Specifically: a rail-and-highway junction town whose high, dry, dark site produced an astronomy cluster (Lowell Observatory 1894; Pluto found there 1930; world's first outdoor lighting ordinance 1958; world's first International Dark-Sky City 2001-10-24). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia; Lowell Observatory, "Dark Skies," https://lowell.edu/about-us/dark-skies/.

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

All sourced above; stated as the sources state them, not inferred further.
- **Altitude + clear dark air → astronomy institutions and law.** Lowell sited there in 1894 for the sky; Lowell scientists and the city wrote the world's first outdoor-lighting ordinance in 1958 (banning searchlights); an Astronomical Zone system centered on Anderson Mesa and the Naval Observatory; the 2001 Dark-Sky City designation; the Dark Sky Coalition; a brewery named for it; a $53 M public astronomy center. (Lowell; Wikipedia; Discover Flagstaff.)
- **Altitude + trails → a professional endurance-running population** and a university running dynasty; athletes "live high, train low" by driving down to Sedona/Cottonwood (3,300–4,300 ft). (Cronkite News.)
- **Ponderosa forest on the railroad → the lumber economy** (Ayer Lumber Co. 1882 → Riordan brothers' Arizona Lumber & Timber Co. 1887, the town's largest employer for ~50 years, which also brought electricity and the first library) and with it the segregated Southside/Los Chantes mill-worker housing south of the tracks. The forest-products thread survives as Restoration Forest Products (275 employees, 2025) and NAU's forestry school (1958). (NAU Review 2024-10-14; Wikipedia; KNAU 2017; ACFR.)
- **Transcontinental corridor → successive pass-through economies:** railroad town (1880s), Route 66 (1926; locals collectively funded the Hotel Monte Vista, opened 1927-01-01), I-40; tourism "took over from traditional industries" by the late 1920s. Today half of visitors are on their way somewhere else, mostly the Grand Canyon (80 mi). (Wikipedia; NAU tourism study.)
- **Snowy high peaks → a ski economy and a sacred-site conflict.** Snowbowl began snowmaking with treated municipal wastewater in December 2012 under a 2002 city contract; the Navajo Nation and others lost a federal suit; the Hopi Tribe won the right (Arizona Supreme Court, 2014-01-07) to pursue a public-nuisance claim against the city. (HCN 2014; Wikipedia.)
- **Proximity to the Navajo and Hopi Nations → a regional hub** for health care (only Level I trauma center north of Phoenix), shopping, the Native heritage festivals, an urban Indian center, and the historic All-Indian Pow-Wow. (NAH; Grand Canyon Trust; MNA; NAU.)

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)

- **No single dominant employer:** the top ten together hold only 17.3% of city employment (2025), down from 22.7% (2016). Largest three are a university, a hospital system and a medical-device maker, each ~3,000. (ACFR.)
- **Young, student-heavy population** (median age ~26); seasonal swings tied to the academic year and to the ski / summer / fall-color tourism seasons. (ACFR; Wikipedia.)
- **A dedicated tourism tax funds arts, parks and beautification** (BBB, voter-renewed to 2043) — the arts sector is partly paid for by visitors' lodging and bar tabs. (ACFR.)
- **Housing cost is a declared civic emergency** (2020) with half of households low-income — a constraint on who can live and work there. (KJZZ.)
- **The railroad is physically central and lethal:** a quiet zone through downtown, a federal safety review in 2024–25, and a yearly toll of pedestrian deaths. (City presentation.)
- **Arts and science are run by many small nonprofits** (50 in the 2016 study) rather than a few large institutions. (AEP5.)

## 2 · Memphis, Tennessee [SECONDARY]

*Developer's note on this pick: "genuine multi-modal crossroads — two interstates (I-40/I-55), five converging rail lines, and the Mississippi River all meeting at once, with Beale Street's live-music scene right at the junction." Research below covers the crossroads and Beale Street first, then the rest of the city.*

### A. Snapshot

- **Location:** Shelby County, southwest Tennessee, on the Chickasaw Bluffs above the Mississippi River; 35°8′46″N 90°3′7″W; across the river from West Memphis, Arkansas, and just north of Mississippi. Land area 302.57 sq mi. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Memphis, Tennessee," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee (accessed 2026-10-26).
- **Population:** 633,104 (2020 Census); 610,919 (2024 estimate, declining); metro ~1.345 million (TN-MS-AR). Shelby County 919,173 (2024). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia; Tennessee Dept. of Economic & Community Development (TNECD), Shelby County profile, https://tnecd.com/counties/shelby/.
- **Composition:** Black (non-Hispanic) 62.7% (2023 ACS) — **UNVERIFIED** (Data USA snippet). City shifted from 60.8% white (1970) to majority Black. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Founding:** 1819-05-22 by John Overton, James Winchester and Andrew Jackson; named for the ancient Egyptian capital; site chosen on the bluffs above flood level. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **What it is:** a river, rail, highway and air freight junction city that became "the world's largest spot cotton market" and a hardwood center, then a global air-cargo hub (FedEx) and a birthplace of blues, rock and roll, soul and Memphis rap. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Defining feature (developer's framing, confirmed by sources):** four freight modes meeting at one bluff — the Mississippi (port), five of the seven Class I railroads, I-40 × I-55, and the FedEx air hub. **SOURCED** — see B.

### B. What else exists there

**The crossroads, mode by mode**
- **Rail:** five Class I railroads — Union Pacific, Norfolk Southern, BNSF, CSX, Canadian National; ten freight facilities: Leewood Yard (CSXT), Sargent Yard (UP), Forrest Yard (NS), Yale Yard (BNSF), Harrison (Johnston) Yard (CN), Memphis Intermodal Facility – Tennessee Yard (BNSF), CN supply-chain facility, Port of Memphis (CN), Intermodal Gateway (CN/CSXT); only Forrest Yard, Intermodal Gateway and the Memphis Intermodal Facility handle containers. Four Mississippi bridges: Frisco Bridge (1892, rail), Harahan Bridge (1916, rail), Memphis & Arkansas Bridge (1949, I-55), Hernando de Soto Bridge (1973, I-40, "M-shaped" arch). Amtrak *City of New Orleans* (Chicago–New Orleans) daily at Central Station. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Transportation in Memphis, Tennessee," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_in_Memphis,_Tennessee. BNSF Tennessee Yard: $200 M rebuild begun 2005 on a 185-acre footprint, 600,000 lifts/year; CN's $100 M Harrison Yard rebuild (2009) is CN's second-largest U.S. classification yard; CN/CSX Intermodal Gateway ($35 M, opened 2005) in the port's Frank C. Pidgeon Industrial Park — **UNVERIFIED** (trade-press snippets: rtands.com, ajot.com). "One of only three U.S. cities with five of the seven Class I railroads" — **UNVERIFIED** (Inbound Logistics snippet; page 403).
- **River:** Port of Memphis covers 15 river miles (mile 725–740) on both the Tennessee and Arkansas banks; 68 waterfront facilities, 37 of them terminals; petroleum, cement, steel, fertilizers, grains; President's Island (developed 1946, with a closure dam and dredged harbor) holds 95% of the port's industries. Port describes itself as second-largest inland port on the shallow-draft Mississippi and "sixth-largest" U.S. inland port. **SOURCED** — Port of Memphis, "About," https://portofmemphis.com/about/. (Other sources say fourth or fifth — Wikipedia says "fifth-busiest"; rank depends on year and measure, unresolved.) 8.2 million short tons (2022) — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of Wikipedia "Port of Memphis").
- **Highway:** I-40 and I-55 converge; also I-240 loop, I-269 outer loop (completed 2018), I-22 to Birmingham. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia. "Two-day truck drive to 70% of the U.S. population," "America's Distribution Center" (chamber slogan) — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Air:** Memphis International Airport (FedEx's global "SuperHub") cargo, per Airports Council International via Wikipedia list: #2 2019 (4.32 M t), **#1 in the world 2020 (4.61 M t)**, #2 2021 (4.48 M t), #2 2023 (3.88 M t), #3 2024 (3.75 M t), **#6 2025 (2.97 M t, −20.9%)**. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "List of busiest airports by cargo traffic"; 2025 figures cross-checked in *International Airport Review*, https://www.internationalairportreview.com/article/107921/top-10-busiest-airports-world-cargo (ACI data). Cause of the 2025 drop: NOT FOUND (not researched this round).
- **FedEx World Hub, the night sort:** "Each evening, 7,000 Memphians descend upon the Hub"; 862.8 acres; 42 miles of conveyors; capacity 160,000 packages + 265,000 documents an hour; a plane lands every 90 seconds during the night sort (2013 figures). **SOURCED** — *Memphis Magazine*, "FedEx at Forty: Night Moves," Greg Akers, 2013-05-01, https://memphismagazine.com/features/fedex-at-forty-night-moves. ### C. Cultural locations (names, what each is)

*Beale Street (at the junction the developer names)*
- **Beale Street** — ~1.8 mi from the river to East St.; historic district 27 acres. Laid out 1841 (developer Robertson Topp); Black traveling musicians from the 1860s; Young Men's Brass Band formed 1867; **Church's Park** (1899) with a 2,000-seat auditorium, built by Robert Church, the South's first Black millionaire; W. C. Handy arrived 1903 and wrote "The Memphis Blues" (as "Mr. Crump," 1909) and "Beale Street Blues" (1916); 1920s–40s Louis Armstrong, Muddy Waters, B.B. King, Memphis Minnie; National Historic Landmark 1966; urban renewal in the 1960s–70s demolished surrounding buildings; Congress designated it "Home of the Blues" 1977-12-15; Beale Street Development Corporation (1973) secured $5.2 M in grants. Today: B.B. King's Blues Club, Rum Boogie Café, A. Schwab's (dry goods), Memphis Rock 'n' Soul Museum, FedExForum at the east end. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Beale Street," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beale_Street. Urban-renewal demolition (June 1965) of every historic building on Beale between Main and Second except Lansky Brothers — **UNVERIFIED** (Memphis Heritage snippet).
- **Beale Street Music Festival** — first weekend of May in Tom Lee Park; "typically hosts over 100,000 people during the weekend"; the only Memphis in May program run continuously since 1977. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Memphis in May," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis_in_May.

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- **River bluff + cotton → the market city.** "World's largest spot cotton market"; Cotton Row on Front Street; the Exchange floor now a museum. (Wikipedia; Cotton Museum.)
- **Migration along the river and rail into the city → the music.** Black musicians on Beale from the 1860s; rural-to-urban migration nearly quadrupled population 1900–1950 (102,350 → 396,000); the same migration moved rural pitmasters into city barbecue joints. (Wikipedia; Beale Street; Edge 2014.)
- **Central location + all freight modes → FedEx and the logistics cluster.** The SuperHub's night sort brings thousands of part-time overnight workers; ~90,000 in the distribution & logistics cluster; the barbecue joint ships ribs by FedEx. (*Memphis Magazine*; *Marketplace*; TNECD; Edge.)
- **Junction economy → the festival calendar is placed on the river and on Beale.** Memphis in May (1977) was built to bring people back downtown to the riverfront; Beale Street Music Festival is at the foot of Beale in Tom Lee Park. (Wikipedia, Memphis in May.)
- **Labor in a freight-and-service city → the civil-rights landmarks.** The 1968 sanitation strike and Dr. King's assassination produced Clayborn Temple's role, I AM A MAN Plaza and the National Civil Rights Museum. (Civil Rights Trail; NCRM.)

### E. Structural features

- **A night economy as a structural fact:** the city's largest employer runs its core operation overnight with a mainly part-time workforce. (*Memphis Magazine*; *Marketplace*.)
- **Very concentrated employment in logistics and health care**; a single employer (FedEx, 30,000) at more than twice the size of the next. (TNECD.)
- **Deep poverty alongside a large tourism economy:** 22.6% city poverty, 36.3% child poverty, with sharp racial gaps; 13.1 M visitors and 44,100 hospitality jobs. (Poverty Fact Sheet; Memphis Tourism.)
- **Music heritage is the main tourist motivation**; three historic studios still record, and the music economy is split between heritage tourism (Graceland, Sun, Stax, Beale) and living scenes (Memphis rap, jookin'). (Memphis Tourism; Oxford American.)
- **Population decline in the city** (633K → 611K, 2020–2024) while it remains a three-state metro center. (Wikipedia.)

## 3 · Reno, Nevada [SECONDARY]

*Developer's note on this pick: "historic transcontinental-railroad stopover that pivoted into a genuine destination with its own full civic identity, not just a waypoint reputation." Research below follows that arc: river crossing → railroad town → divorce and gambling destination → highway gateway → present-day logistics, tech and arts city.*

### A. Snapshot

- **Location:** Washoe County, northwest Nevada, in the Truckee River valley (the Truckee Meadows) at the eastern foot of the Sierra Nevada, on the California line, ~23 mi northeast of Lake Tahoe; elevation 4,505 ft. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Reno, Nevada," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reno,_Nevada (accessed 2026-10-26).
- **Population:** 264,165 (2020 Census); 283,621 (2025 estimate); metro 490,596. City area 111.70 sq mi. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Founding:** a toll bridge on the Truckee (Charles Fuller, 1859), bought by Myron C. Lake (Lake's Crossing); Lake and Fuller had roughed out a road toward the Comstock Lode at Virginia City (~25 mi south) — today's Virginia Street. The Central Pacific Railroad bought ~107 acres north of the river from Lake and auctioned the first townsite lots on **1868-05-09**; named for Union Gen. Jesse Lee Reno (killed at South Mountain, 1862). **SOURCED** — Alicia Barber, KUNR, 2018-05-10, https://www.kunr.org/time-place-with-alicia-barber/2018-05-10/150-years-later-the-story-of-how-reno-came-to-be. (Wikipedia gives Fuller's bridge as 1859; a search snippet gives Lake's purchase as 1861. Snippets also say 400 lots were offered and that Charles Crocker named the town — **UNVERIFIED**.)
- **What it is:** a casino-and-university city on the transcontinental rail line (Union Pacific, formerly Central Pacific) and I-80, which since 2014 has also become a logistics, manufacturing and data-center region. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia; *Nevada Independent* (below).
- **Defining feature (developer's framing, as sources describe it):** a railroad stop that made itself a destination through a series of deliberate attractions — fast divorce (1931 six-week residency), legal gambling (1931), the Arch and its slogan (1926/1929), and a calendar of large staged events. **SOURCED** — see B–D.

### B. What else exists there

**Economy, then and now**
- **Divorce trade:** residency for divorce cut to 3 months (1927) and 6 weeks (1931), the fastest in the U.S.; divorcing visitors (mostly women) stayed at downtown hotels near the Washoe County Courthouse (the Riverside, the El Cortez) and at guest "divorce ranches" in Washoe Valley and near Pyramid Lake; tossing wedding rings off the Virginia Street Bridge was real ("a lot of locals… have found rings in the river" — historian Alicia Barber). **SOURCED** — KUTV / Sinclair "Amazing America," 2026-02-26, https://kutv.com/amazing-america/renos-divorce-boom-the-industry-that-built-the-biggest-little-city. Wikipedia dates the "divorce capital of the world" era to the 1930s–1960s. **SOURCED**.
- **Gaming:** legalized statewide 1931. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia. **Washoe County gaming win FY2024: $1,051,650,280 = 6.7% of Nevada's total** (Clark County 87.0%); state gaming taxes from Washoe $78.95 M (6.4%). **SOURCED** — Nevada Gaming Control Board, *Information Sheet FY24*, https://www.gaming.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/gamingnvgov/content/about/info-sheet/IS%202024.pdf (read via local PDF-to-text).
- **Largest employers, Washoe County (Nevada DETR / Data Axle, "2026 – 1st Release," size classes only):** 1,000–4,999: Nugget Casino Resort (Sparks), Renown Health, Peppermill, Blackhawk Network Holdings, Eldorado Reno, Silver Legacy, University of Nevada, Reno, UPS Customer Center (Sparks), Circus Circus, LEGACY Supply Chain Services, North Reno VA Clinic, Hamilton Company. 500–999: Arrow Electronics, Washoe County Sheriff's Office, JC Penney Distribution Center, Hyatt Regency Lake Tahoe (Incline Village), Renown South Meadows, City of Sparks, Western Village Inn & Casino (Sparks), Mt. Rose Ski Tahoe. **SOURCED** — https://nvlmi.mt.gov/_docs/Top-Employers/2026-1-Counties/Top-Employers-in-Washoe-County. Note: four of the top six are casinos. Tesla does not appear because its Gigafactory is in **Storey County**, not Washoe (see next); a 2024 Sparks list (snippet) ranks "Tesla Gigafactory" at 5,000–9,999 — **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Tahoe-Reno Industrial Center (TRIC), Storey County, ~20 mi east:** 107,000 acres; Tesla's Gigafactory 5.4 M sq ft; the park draws ~30,000 people a day; Tesla's 2014 deal carried ~$1 B in tax incentives, $6.2 B invested, $5.7 B in wages 2015–2022; a 2023 expansion (semi-truck and cell factories) planned 3,000 more jobs with $330 M in abatements. Reno-area median home price went from under $300,000 (2014) to ~$600,000 (2022). **SOURCED** — *Nevada Independent*, 2023-03-02 (updated), https://thenevadaindependent.com/article/what-teslas-multibillion-dollar-gigafactory-expansion-means-for-nevada. (Wikipedia gives 5.8 M sq ft and ~11,000 employees — figures differ by date.) Other TRIC tenants named in snippets: Switch data centers, Google (1,210 acres bought), eBay, Rackspace, Amazon fulfillment — **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Burning Man (Black Rock Desert, north):** "conservatively estimated at $60 million" a year to Nevada (~$50 M participants, ~$10 M organization); hotels, grocers, costume shops, ice distributors, RV dealers, hardware/box stores, auto repair and car washes plan their year around it; it brings "the largest influx of passengers to the Reno-Tahoe International Airport — more than Christmas and Thanksgiving"; Reno has Burner-linked maker spaces (The Generator, Artech) and the Morris Burner Hostel. **SOURCED** — Marnee Benson, *Burning Man Journal*, 2018-02-07, https://journal.burningman.org/2018/02/news/brc-news/my-how-youve-grown-the-burning-man-effect-in-nevada/ (the event organization's own publication — a self-interested source).
- **Education:** University of Nevada, Reno (~21,353 students; mining engineering, agriculture, journalism; "one of only two Basque Studies programs in the nation"). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia. UNR's William A. Douglass Center for Basque Studies holds 55,000+ volumes. **SOURCED** — UNR, 2021-08-27 (below).
- **Health:** Renown Health; Saint Mary's Regional Medical Center (snippet); North Reno VA Clinic. **SOURCED** — DETR list (Renown, VA).
- **Transport and how goods move:** Union Pacific main line (former Central Pacific) through downtown in the **ReTRAC trench** — 2.25 mi of double main line lowered into a 1.75-mi trench 54 ft wide × 33 ft deep, built Oct 2002–spring 2006 for $279.9 M (city bonds $111.5 M and a $50.5 M federal TIFIA loan, both backed by hotel-room and sales taxes; UP $17 M; federal grants $21.3 M), eliminating 10 street crossings and creating 120 developable acres. **SOURCED** — FHWA project profile, https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ipd/project_profiles/nv_retrac.aspx. Train counts were disputed at the time (city: 17–19/day; a critic citing a UP map: 12) — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet). Amtrak *California Zephyr* daily; RTC Washoe buses; I-80 and US 395/I-580. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia. **Reno-Tahoe International Airport:** 4.8 M passengers in 2024 (+6%), busiest since 2008; 11 airlines; $238 M bond issued Aug 2024. **SOURCED** — RNO release, 2025-01-16, https://www.renoairport.com/newsroom/release-reno-tahoe-international-airport-celebrates-record-breaking-year-in-2024/.

**Who lives there**
- **Reno-Sparks Indian Colony (RSIC):** began as a settlement of landless Washoe, Paiute and Shoshone people along the Truckee next to Reno (1880s per a snippet; "early 1900s" per Wikipedia); federal government bought 20 acres for $6,000 in 1917 (+8.38 acres 1926); IRA government and federal recognition 1934; 1,134 members; 1,920 acres at Hungry Valley (19 mi north) placed in trust 1984; income from five smoke shops and commercial leases (Walmart and car dealerships); a $20 M tribal health center on Kuenzli St.; 350+ employees, over half tribal members. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reno-Sparks_Indian_Colony.
- **Basques:** Basque men came to herd sheep on surrounding ranches; at least 10 Basque boardinghouses in the 1920s–30s around Lake St. and East 2nd St., a block from the train station (Santa Fe, Indart, Toscano, Martin, Hotel Español, French Hotel, Star, Alturas, Altona, Commercial) — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of euskalkazeta / thisisreno). UNR confirms boarding houses "established near the railroad stations" offering banking, medical and translation help, and names Louis' Basque Corner and the Santa Fe. **SOURCED** — UNR *Nevada Today*, 2021-08-27, https://www.unr.edu/nevada-today/blogs/2021/commemorating-nevadas-rich-basque-heritage.
- Newcomers: Washoe County expected ~18,000 more residents by 2032 from Tesla; "high-income California transplants" — **UNVERIFIED** (AP/Inc. and commercial snippets).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- **River crossing on the road to the Comstock → the town's spine.** Virginia Street began as the toll-bridge road to Virginia City; the Arch spans it; MidTown is its southern stretch. (KUNR 2018; Wikipedia, Reno Arch; *Tahoe Quarterly*.)
- **Railroad stop → arrivals, boardinghouses and a Basque quarter by the depot.** Basque herders arrived by train and lodged in boarding houses near the station. (UNR 2021.)
- **Being a stop people pass through → deliberate "reasons to stay."** The six-week divorce residency (1931), legal gambling (1931), the highway-exposition arch and its 1929 slogan, the Nevada Round Up (1919, "to get people to town"), Artown (1996, "to draw people into downtown"), Hot August Nights (1986) and the balloon race (1982) are each documented as created to bring visitors or residents into town. (KUTV; Wikipedia; KOLO; UNR 2015, 2025; NNBW 2011.)
- **The rail line through the middle of downtown → the ReTRAC trench,** paid for largely with hotel-room taxes — the visitor economy financed burying the railroad that founded the town. (FHWA.)
- **Interstate + rail + cheap desert land east of town → the logistics and battery-factory era** (TRIC/Tesla, distribution centers among the top employers), with a doubling of home prices 2014–2022. (*Nevada Independent*; DETR list.)
- **Gateway to the desert and the lake → event-season commerce**: Burning Man's supply run through Reno and its airport peak; Lake Tahoe and ski employers in the county list. (*Burning Man Journal*; DETR.)

### E. Structural features

- **Gaming remains the largest single employer category** in the county's top-20 list, but Washoe takes only 6.7% of Nevada's gaming win; a long sequence of downtown casino closures and conversions to housing and non-gaming hotels. (NGCB; DETR; Mike's Reno Report.)
- **A regional economy split across county lines:** the largest factory sits in Storey County while its workers and housing pressure fall on Reno. (*Nevada Independent*.)
- **Event-driven calendar:** July (Artown, Dancing in the Streets), early August (Hot August Nights), late August (Burning Man supply surge), September (balloon race), June/July (rodeo). (Sources above.)
- **Public infrastructure financed by visitors** (room tax backing the rail trench bonds). (FHWA.)
- **Small-business, low-rent revitalization** in MidTown after highway rerouting emptied it. (*Tahoe Quarterly*.)
