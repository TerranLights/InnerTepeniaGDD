## 1 · Grange halls, United States

**Round 1 covered:** the institution's founding, structure, ritual outline, political work, co-ops, Washington State Grange, La Prairie hall, Maine halls, Centre County Grange Fair, Oregon hall-use rules. **Round 2 chases:** the open "school" question, the inside of a meeting (degrees, door rules), what a hall's year looked like (suppers, dances, children's events), who was inside and who was not (women, youth, race), the generational split of the 2010s and the California hall-property fight.

### A. Other settings
- **The hall as the schoolhouse (Round 1 NOT FOUND, now partly found):** **Monticello Grange No. 338**, Main Street (US Route 1), Monticello, Maine: built **1922** to replace a hall lost to fire in 1921; the town's "only performance space, hosting social events, town meetings, and school graduations"; **school graduations and events were held there until 1974**, when the school built its own large hall; town offices were in it until **1980**; Grange chartered 1899; NRHP 5 July 2000. [K8, P4]
- **Inside a meeting (K6, P6, P7):** a **gatekeeper** controls the door by degree; members must give **passwords** to enter; "the fourth degree is necessary to take part in most Grange meetings"; after business the meeting can "drop in degree" to admit lower-degree members or open to the public; symbolic farm tools are displayed at the opening; ceremonies draw on the Bible, farm analogies and the seasons and teach "temperance, humility and hard work." Degrees: the first four (conferred in the local Grange) follow spring, summer, autumn, winter; the fifth (county Pomona Grange) teaches perseverance; the sixth (State Grange) concerns death; the seventh (National Grange) dramatizes entry into heaven. SOURCED: Kathy Mulady, "Grange rituals offer lessons," *Spokesman-Review*, 10 Nov 1996, https://www.spokesman.com/stories/1996/nov/10/grange-rituals-offer-lessons (read 2026-10-29). ### B. Subcultures
- **Women:** founding rules required four women on the officer roster to form a Grange; a St. Louis Grange adopted the demand that women have "equal voice and vote"; women voted in meetings and found them "a real source of social enjoyment." **Youth:** founder Kelley wanted members as young as fourteen so the young would not leave for the cities. **Non-farmers:** early members included town-dwellers, part-time farmers, judges, lawyers and schoolmasters. - **Race:** most members were white; most Granges had no written exclusion clause, but the Alabama State Grange urged recruiting white European immigrants to compete with "African bone and muscle" (Corso 2021, citing Rogers pp. 16–17, SOURCED as the article's claim). That the Grange became "a refuge for rural white power across the former Confederacy," that Louisiana alone admitted members regardless of race, and that African American farmers were organized into Granges in the North: UNVERIFIED (search-result summary attributed to Wikipedia; not read on the page). ## 2 · Danish cooperative dairies and folk high schools

**Round 1 covered:** co-op dairy rules and counts, Hjedding, the separator, folk high school calendar and counts, assembly halls (Nyvang), railroads. **Round 2 chases:** conduct rules at folk high schools and halls (Round 1 NOT FOUND), daily life inside a folk high school, the assembly hall today, the religious split that divided villages into parallel institutions, the gymnastics and rifle-club movement that filled the halls, and the dairyman's training (still NOT FOUND).

### A. Other settings
- **Inside a folk high school today (P4):** residential; most students 18–24; typical stay 4 months; students "live, eat, study, party and clean together"; shared or single rooms; weekly joint and small-group meetings; "Everybody has the same duties and the same rights – regardless of sex, social range, religion, nationality, ethnicity etc."; "You usually sing every morning – and whenever there is an occassion" [sic]; the shared songbook (*Højskolesangbogen*) holds **572 songs** in Danish, English, German and Swedish, including pop and religious songs; teaching built on "the free word, dialogue and an open curriculum which can be changed during the course." SOURCED: Danish Folk High Schools (the schools' association), "What is a folk high school," https://danishfolkhighschools.com/about-folk-high-schools/what-is-a-folk-high-school (read 2026-10-29; no date shown). Morning assembly as the day's start, e.g. 9:00–9:30 at Engelsholm, and meals three times a day cooked by in-house chefs: UNVERIFIED (snippets from engelsholm.dk and rondehojskole.dk). [K7, K10, K12, K13, P4]
- **Askov Højskole** (near Vejen): founded **1865** as the continuation of Rødding after the loss of South Jutland in **1864**, when principal Ludvig Schrøder and the teachers moved north of the new border; from **1878** the "expanded high school" offered further study to men and women who had already attended other folk high schools; physicist and inventor **Poul la Cour** made his windmill experiments there; winter courses of three to six months and shorter summer courses; students from beyond the peasantry and from Norway and Iceland; a venue for "numerous meetings and conferences"; today Askov Højskole og Efterskole, with a lower-secondary boarding school added in 2001. SOURCED: Hans Henningsen, "Askov Højskole," *Lex*, last modified 11 Aug 2026, https://lex.dk/Askov_H%C3%B8jskole (read 2026-10-29). Rødding staff and dates (Johan Wegener principal 1844; Rødding students sent to Scotland to learn farming in December 1851): UNVERIFIED (snippets; Danish Wikipedia mirror refused 403). ### B. Subcultures: two parallel villages (K4, K9, P6, P9)
- **Grundtvigians and Indre Mission:** the revivalist **Indre Mission** (lay association 1853, reorganized 1861 as Kirkelig Forening for den Indre Mission i Danmark) went from **10 mission houses in 1880 to 399 in 1900**, strongest in **Central and West Jutland, South Jutland and Bornholm**; mission houses carried biblical names (e.g. Sarepta, Tabor); no church rites were performed in them. SOURCED: Overgaard, Bach-Nielsen & Holst, "Indre Mission," *Lex*, last modified 16 Oct 2026, https://lex.dk/Indre_Mission (read 2026-10-29).
- **How the split ran through daily life (SOURCED, translated from Danish):** where Indre Mission set up its own businesses "such as dairies and hotels" in many communities while Grundtvigians set up their own church and school, the division deepened: "You delivered your milk at different places, drank your brandy (or precisely did not) at different places, and perhaps even had your children educated at different places"; the two groups demonstrably named their children differently; the division continued after death, visible in gravestones marked as given by the faithful (examples in the two churchyards at Vorupør, Thy). A mission house was "in principle open to all, but in reality you did not go there unless you already belonged." A 1918 program by Indre Mission chairman Carl Moe set the line on games, **dance and above all alcohol**; under Christian Bartholdy (1934–59) the tone softened. Sunday school was once the only leisure offer for children in a parish, later an internal activity. Case place: **mission house "Tabor," Harring parish, Thy**, standing alone in open, windswept fields: a small hall with biblical wall paintings, a large hall with benches along the walls, a parlor organ, a lectern and a painting like an altarpiece, and a kitchen upstairs; many mission houses are being sold or pulled down. SOURCED: Michael Riber Jørgensen, "Fællesskab i forandring: Om Indre Mission, identitet og interaktion – set fra Thy," *Historisk Årbog for Thy* 2020, pp. 119–138, https://arkivthy.dk/images/Aarbog/2020a/Faellesskab%20i%20forandring%20af%20Michael%20Riber%20Joergensen.pdf (PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29). [K4, K6, K9, K12, P6, P9]

## 3 · Prairie wheat pools and grain elevators, Saskatchewan

**Round 1 covered:** the Pool's founding, governance, local committees, elevator counts and form, heritage elevators, the elevator office as news exchange, the permit book (Alberta source). **Round 2 chases:** the *Western Producer* question (Round 1 NOT FOUND), the Pool's fieldmen, the other buildings of an elevator town (hotel beer parlor, rink, church hall), seasonal workers, Hutterite colonies, First Nations farmers under the permit system, and fall suppers.

### A. Other settings
- **The hotel beer parlor (K6, K4, P4, P7):** under the 1935 law the legislature approved separate **women-only beer parlors** (passed **22 January 1935**): in cities separate entrances and "no means of communication" between the men's and women's rooms; in smaller centers one entrance but separate parlors; MLA Omer Demers objected that refusing to let men and women drink together admitted "the beer parlor is not going to be a decent place to go"; in **March 1935** Regina hotelmen said they would not provide women's parlors and hotels elsewhere followed, so in practice women stayed out. SOURCED: Joan Champ (historian, former Western Development Museum CEO), "Women-only beer parlors," blog *Railway & Main: Small-Town Saskatchewan Hotels*, 20 Mar 2011, https://hotelhistories.blogspot.com/2011/03/women-only-beer-parlours.html (read 2026-10-29; cites the Regina *Leader-Post* Jan–Feb 1935, Heron 2003, Campbell 2001). **The Liquor Licensing Act of 1 April 1959** created licensed dining rooms, cocktail bars and **beverage rooms** (converted parlors) where women could drink, while men-only parlors could continue; it applied town by town through **local option votes**, e.g. Prince Albert on **29 June 1959** (about 25% turnout, approved two to one); Attorney-General Robert A. Walker said it would end "the obscene kind of drinking" in beer parlors; male patrons and hotelmen feared women would cut beer sales and inhibit male company. ### B. Subcultures
- **Seasonal workers: the harvest excursions, 1890–1930:** railways sold tickets at **$15 one way or $20 return** from anywhere in Canada; about **14,000** arrived in the peak year **1908**; harvesters earned **$1.75–$2.25 for a 10–12 hour day**, threshing crews **$2–$3.25 a day with board**; work lasted about **15 days**; trains crowded and crude; ended with the wheat economy's collapse and the **combine (from 1930)**. - **First Nations farmers (K6, K9, P9):** the 1870s treaties included requests for the means to farm (tools, seed, animals), but implements were inadequate ("ten families, for example, were to share one plow"); government farm instructors from 1879, abandoned by the early 1880s; from **1881** Indigenous farmers **could not sell any grain or other produce without a permit**, and after **1885 a pass system** confined movement off reserves; the **1889 peasant farming policy**, adopted after non-Indigenous farmers complained, made reserve farmers cut acreage, grow root crops rather than wheat, and use hand tools; by the mid-1890s per-capita cultivated acreage on reserves had fallen to about half its 1889 level.
