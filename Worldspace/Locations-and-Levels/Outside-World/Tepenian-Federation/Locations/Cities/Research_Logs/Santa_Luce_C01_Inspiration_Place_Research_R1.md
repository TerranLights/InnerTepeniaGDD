## 1 · Gibraltar

**Claim to test (from input):** a dense town built on and into one rock, with tunnels cut through it, whose population descends from many origins with none dominant and lives at close quarters.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with two narrowings.** (a) Density and tunnels: confirmed (about 5,000 people per km² on 6.8 km²; about 55 km of tunnels, nearly twice the road network). But the tunnels were cut by the military, and civilian life is lived *on* the Rock's western slope and on reclaimed land, not inside it; the tunnels' civilian roles today are water storage, desalination, two road tunnels, data storage and tourism. (b) "Many origins, none dominant": confirmed by a 1995 surname study (largest share 27%), but the modern population counts itself overwhelmingly as one identity (79% "Gibraltarian" in the 2012 census), so the many origins have fused rather than stayed apart. A third layer the claim misses: about 45% of employee jobs are held by people who sleep across the border in Spain.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location and size:** British Overseas Territory at the southern tip of the Iberian Peninsula. Area 6.8 km²; land border with Spain 1.2 km; shoreline 12 km; highest point 426 m (the Rock). SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibraltar (accessed 2026-10-29). [P3]
- **Population:** 2022 census 37,936 usually resident (18,891 male, 19,045 female, census date 14 November 2022); density about 5,000/km². SOURCED for 37,936 and density (Wikipedia infobox); the sex split and date are UNVERIFIED (citypopulation.de table seen in search summary only). Earlier census: 32,194 (2012), about 4,959/km². SOURCED (Wikipedia). Preliminary 2022 figure reported as "about 38,000" by the Gibraltar Chronicle. UNVERIFIED (snippet). [P9]
- **Defining feature:** a limestone rock with a town on its western slope and on reclaimed land. Reclaimed land is about one-tenth of the territory's area but houses over 40% of the population. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). [P3, P5]

### B. What else exists there
- **Economy:** main sectors financial services, online gambling, maritime services and tourism. Military share of the economy about 7% today against over 60% in 1984. Manufacturing a little under 2%. Corporate tax fixed at 10%. GDP (nominal, 2024 est.) £3.089 billion. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). [K8, P8]
- **Labor market:** around 30,000 workers, 80% private and 20% public; unemployment around 1%. 53% of the labor force live in Spain or are non-Gibraltarians. Frontier workers hold about 45% of all employee jobs and 58% of private-sector jobs; 63% of frontier workers are Spanish nationals. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). Government analysis of October 2015 data: 10,473 of 26,144 employee jobs held by frontier workers; in online gaming, 1,761 frontier workers, about 60% of that sector. UNVERIFIED (seen in a search summary of a government document; not opened). Large gambling firms employ "over 3,000" in Gibraltar (Racing Post, Brexit period). UNVERIFIED (snippet). [K7, K4, P8, P9]
- **Governance:** Parliament of 17 elected members, term up to four years; Government of 10 elected members. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P7]
- **Schools:** 15 state schools, two private schools, Gibraltar College (further education); government secondary schools Bayside Comprehensive and Westside; independent Prior Park School Gibraltar. University of Gibraltar opened September 2015; before that there was no full-time higher education locally. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P4]
- **Water (the Rock's oldest shared problem):** historically, rain was collected on concrete and rock catchments and in wells in the north. Today drinking water comes entirely from desalination; a reverse-osmosis plant is built inside a tunnel in the Rock and a multi-stage flash distillation plant stands at North Mole; drinking and sanitary water are stored in large reservoirs excavated under the Rock. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). Underground reservoirs dug 1898–1900 (four), 1911–15 (one), 1933–38 (four), and two more in 1956–68. SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunnels_of_Gibraltar. Victorian houses: tiled roofs collected rainwater into underground tanks used for drinking, washing, cleaning and plants. SOURCED, https://www.ministryforheritage.gi/heritage-and-antiquities/a-gibraltar-patio-in-victorian-times-1726 (page undated; © 2021 footer). [K1, K5, P3] [R]
- **Transport and access:** the airport runway crosses Winston Churchill Avenue, so traffic is stopped by movable barricades for flights; a road tunnel (Kingsway) under the new terminal opened officially 31 March 2023. Traffic drives on the right and speed limits are in km/h because of the shared land border. Cable car from near the Botanic Gardens to the top of the Rock, intermediate stop at the Apes' Den. Algeciras ferry reopened 16 December 2009. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). [K5, P5]
- **How goods arrive:** NOT FOUND as a sourced account of daily supply. What is sourced: the land border was closed by Spain in 1969 with all communication links cut, partially reopened 1982, fully reopened 1985; a 2006 agreement relaxed controls but motorists and pedestrians still face occasional long delays. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). [K5, K9]

### The people and how they got there
- **1704:** after the capture, "all but 70 of the existing inhabitants … elected to leave." SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibraltarians. [P9, K9]
- **Who came:** Genoese and Catalans formed the core of the first civilian population; historian William Jackson names Genoese, Spaniards, British, Portuguese, Jews and Maltese as the main groups, which by the 20th century had formed a separate Gibraltarian identity. Menorcans from the 18th century; Maltese workers from 1888 drawn by harbor construction. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltarians). Early census, 1753: 434 British, 597 Genoese, 575 Jews, 185 Spaniards, 25 Portuguese (government census history page); a National Archives document gives 414 British and 572 Jews. UNVERIFIED (search summary; neither page opened). [P9]
- **Surname study (1995 census, share of family names):** British 27%, Spanish (excluding Menorcan) 24%, Italian 19%, Portuguese 11%, Maltese 8%, Jewish 3%, Menorcan 2%, other 4%, unassigned 2%. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltarians). [P9]
- **2012 census by nationality group (32,194 usually resident):** Gibraltarian 79.0% (25,444); UK and other British 13.2% (4,249); Spanish 2.1% (675); other EU 2.4% (785); Moroccan 1.6% (522); other 1.6% (519). SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltarians). [P9]
- **Religion (2012):** Christian 83.6% (Roman Catholic 72.1%, Church of England 7.7%), Islam 3.6%, none 7.1%, Judaism 2.4%, Hinduism 2%, other 1.3%. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltar). [P6]
- **Moroccan workers (1969 on):** the 1969 closure cut off 4,600 Spanish workers; Gibraltar recruited laborers from Rabat, Tangier and Tetouan; nearly 3,000 Moroccans had moved by 1970. They received renewable one-year residence permits with no route to settlement; family reunification was blocked; until 1986 Moroccan mothers had to return to Morocco at seven months pregnant to give birth; after the 1985 reopening anyone unemployed six months could be deported; citizenship applications were refused into the 2010s; affordable housing was restricted to citizens, pushing many into rent-controlled housing, often (per a 2019 Environmental Agency report as cited) without toilets, showers or kitchens. Most of the original workers now hold dual British Gibraltarian and Moroccan citizenship; many elderly men live alone in the Upper Town with spouses and children in Morocco. SOURCED, John David, "From Guest Workers to Citizens?", University of Westminster RCPP blog, 25 February 2025, https://blog.westminster.ac.uk/rcpp/from-guest-workers-to-citizens-the-lasting-impacts-of-anti-settlement-policies-on-gibraltars-moroccan-workers/ (an academic blog; figures not cross-checked against official data). [K4, K6, K9, P9] [SM: man versus society]
- **The 1940 evacuation:** most civilians were evacuated in 1940; about 13,500 shipped first to Casablanca; by end of 1940 roughly 10,000 in London (many in Kensington, under the Blitz), about 2,000 in Jamaica (Gibraltar Camp, built for 7,000; over 100 babies born there), a smaller number in Madeira (about 200 Jewish evacuees in Funchal). Those with essential jobs stayed. First return group of 1,367 arrived 6 April 1944; 500 were re-evacuated to Scotland and 3,000 to camps in Northern Ireland when London housing was reclaimed; the last returned in 1951. The article states the evacuation strengthened British national identity; commemorated by a statue by Jill Cowie Sanders; Funchal and Gibraltar twinned 2009. SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evacuation_of_the_Gibraltarian_civilian_population_during_World_War_II. [K9, P9, P6]
- **K11 (loose):** English official and used in schools; most Gibraltarians bilingual in Spanish; a local vernacular rooted in Andalusian Spanish with English, Genoese, Maltese, Portuguese and Haketia elements; Hebrew, Arabic, Hindi, Sindhi and Maltese spoken within specific communities. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Gibraltarians).

### Living at close quarters (the patio)
- **Victorian patio housing:** most homes were built around a central patio shared by several families, with a shared water pump and outside toilet; a roof *lavadero* for washing; about 15,000 people in roughly one square mile from Casemates to the Piazza and up toward the Moorish Castle by 1860. Neighbors decorated patios with plants and spent time together over cards, dominoes and the *tablita* (a game). SOURCED, Ministry for Heritage page (above). [K4, K10, P4]
- 1868 census: 720 patio buildings housing over 13,000 in the Town; average 4.7 families per patio building; overcrowding, poor sanitation and the daily influx of transients cited as factors in the 1865 cholera outbreak. UNVERIFIED (search summary of a ScienceDirect article, *Social Science & Medicine*; page refused, HTTP 403). 1937 Gibraltar Housing Commission (in Hansard): 36% of the working-class population in single-room tenements. UNVERIFIED (snippet). [K4, K9]
- **Today:** the Upper Town is the historic residential area of narrow, stepped streets; Moorish Castle Estate, Glacis and Laguna are government estates; activists describe urban decay in the Upper Town; a housing waiting list "reaching the 1000 mark" (Chronicle opinion piece). All UNVERIFIED (snippets). [K9]

### D. How the defining feature shaped what exists
- The Rock's lack of fresh water and flat land produced: roof and slope catchments, underground tanks and reservoirs, a desalination plant inside a tunnel; reclaimed land housing over 40% of people; the road crossing the runway. SOURCED (above). [K1, P3]
- The military fortress produced the 55 km of tunnels: 1782 Great Siege Tunnels (begun 25 May 1782 by the Soldier Artificer Company under Sergeant-Major Henry Ince); WWII tunnels by four Royal Engineers and Canadian companies, able to hold the 16,000-strong garrison with 16 months' food, with telephone exchange, power station, distillation plant, hospital, bakery and workshop; Stay Behind Cave (Operation Tracer, six men, rediscovered 1997); tunnelling ended April 1968; the Admiralty Tunnel is now secure data storage; Keightley Way and Dudley Ward Way carry civilian traffic. SOURCED (Tunnels of Gibraltar). [K14, P3, P8] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- The single land border produced: frontier labor as about 45% of jobs; the Moroccan worker regime after 1969; National Day as a political-then-civic festival. SOURCED (above). [K4, K9]

## 2 · Juneau Icefield Research Program (Alaska and British Columbia)

**Claim to test (from input):** a long-running summer field school whose camps sit on nunataks inside an icefield, so research life is literally lived on rock islands in ice. Care: seasonal camps, not a town; verify which camps are on nunataks.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED.** The program's own historian describes "nunatak base camps located on bedrock ridge tops" and camps "on emergent bedrock ridges," with students moving "onto and off of the glacier surfaces from these bedrock glacial refugia" (Connor 2009, SOURCED). Camps specifically confirmed on rock: **Camp 18** ("a group of tin buildings on a rock," overlooking the Gilkey Trench; SOURCED, Orion), **Camp 26** (on a nunatak on the north side of the Llewellyn Glacier; SOURCED, Wikipedia geospatial table), **Camp 10** (on a nunatak/rock outcrop above the Taku Glacier, across from the Taku Towers; UNVERIFIED, search summaries of a JIRP blog post (404 when opened) and a student blog; the Vantage Peak weathering report says the camp's spur is part of that nunatak, UNVERIFIED). **Camp 17** sits above Lemon Creek Glacier, reached by a 1,200 m climb from sea level (SOURCED, Connor 2009); whether it is on a nunatak: NOT FOUND. Camp 30 is a building in the town of Atlin, BC, not on ice. It is a summer field camp, not a settlement; nobody winters.

### A. Snapshot
- **What it is:** an eight-week summer field school in glaciology and earth-systems science on the Juneau Icefield, which straddles the Alaska–British Columbia border in the Coast Mountains; glacierized area given as 3,176 km². Students ski about 320 km across the Lemon Creek, Taku and Llewellyn glaciers (upper snowfields to 1,980 m) from Juneau, Alaska, to Atlin, British Columbia. SOURCED, Cathy Connor, "Field glaciology and earth systems science: The Juneau Icefield Research Program (JIRP), 1946–2008," GSA Special Paper 461 (2009), pp. 173–184 (read as PDF text). [P3]
- **Founding and age:** reconnaissance from 1946; "Project on the Taku Glacier," a 10-year Office of Naval Research contract to the American Geographical Society, ran eight field seasons from 1948 through the International Geophysical Year (1957–58); the nonprofit Foundation for Glacier and Environmental Research (FGER) was established in 1955 to support JIRP. Founder and long-time director Maynard M. Miller (led it until his death in 2014). SOURCED (Connor 2009; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juneau_Icefield_Research_Program). Surface mass-balance data since 1946, mainly on Lemon Creek and Taku glaciers; described as the longest-running icefield research program in North America. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P6, P8]
- **Size:** 32 students per year (2026 page); historically 15–50 per summer depending on funding; about 1,300 individuals 1946–2008 (about 2,500 if counted per summer). SOURCED (https://www.juneauicefield.org/students/fees-financial-aid; Connor 2009). About 50 people eat together at a major camp. SOURCED (JIRP Food page, https://www.juneauicefield.org/field-season/food). [P9]
- **Run by:** FGER, with the University of Colorado Boulder, the University of Maine and the University of Alaska Southeast (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Earlier academic home: the Institute of Glaciological and Arctic Environmental Sciences, which moved from Michigan State University to the University of Idaho in 1975 (Connor 2009, SOURCED). [P7]

### B. What else exists there (the camps and how they work)
- **Camp layout:** each major camp (17, 10, 18) has a cookshack, a lecture building with projector and a library of icefield history (textbooks, meteorological reports, theses, photographs), a generator/maintenance building, several bunkhouses and several outhouses. Buildings are permanent but uninsulated and unheated (about outside temperature; they keep off rain, snow and wind). Single bunks with foam mattresses; bunkhouses divided between faculty/staff and students; students choose single-gender or gender-neutral housing. Camp 10's five bunkhouses and each camp's outhouses carry individual names (the names are informal coinages and go to the slang file, not here). SOURCED, https://www.juneauicefield.org/field-season/camps. [P3, P4, K4]
- **Power:** a gasoline generator runs each evening until lights-out at 23:00; the cookshack has the charging outlets. SOURCED (Camps page). Students learn to run diesel generators, Coleman lanterns and gas stoves. SOURCED (Connor 2009). [K5, K7] [R]
- **Water:** rain and snowmelt from protected collection areas, no plumbing; when meltwater runs low the Camp Manager may limit showers and laundry; one gravity-fed shower per permanent camp, filled by carrying heated buckets; laundry by hand, dried on lines over the generators. SOURCED, https://www.juneauicefield.org/field-season/hygiene and Camps page. At Camp 18, water from a small snowmelt pond and a square cut into a snowbank used as a refrigerator. SOURCED, Lizzie Kenny, "Camp 18, Juneau Icefield, Alaska," *Orion*, 27 October 2016, https://orionmagazine.org/place/camp-18-juneau-icefield-alaska/. Walk-in freezers dug in snowbanks. SOURCED (Connor 2009). [K1, K5, P3] [R]
- **Supply:** grocery helicopters roughly once a week, also carrying mail; fresh produce limited and items run out between deliveries; daily radio or satellite contact with Juneau logistics; students have no internet or phone except in emergencies. SOURCED (Camps and Food pages). Faculty rotate in and out by helicopter every 1–2 weeks. SOURCED (FAQ page, https://www.juneauicefield.org/field-season/faqs). [K5, K7]
- **Medical:** field staff trained to Advanced Wilderness First Aid, many as Wilderness First Responders; the program tries to keep a doctor, PA or NP with the expedition, possibly hours away; anything beyond field care means helicopter evacuation "as soon as weather permits." SOURCED (FAQ page). [K1, K5, K14]
- **Cost:** 2026 total $11,900–$12,900 (field fee $11,900 to FGER; tuition $500–$1,000 to CU Boulder or UAS for six upper-division credits); partial scholarships up to $3,000 from the Joan W. Miller Fund (est. 2013, alumni-supported). The fee covers meals, lodging, instruction, logistics, fuel and transport between Atlin, Skagway and Juneau. Participants reach Whitehorse or Skagway at their own cost; JIRP carries them to Atlin for orientation. SOURCED (Fees page). [K8]
- **Funding history:** NSF awards (post-Sputnik science-education funding), NSF REU support 1987–1995 (98 undergraduates from 74 universities by 1997), NASA Alaska Space Grant scholarships via UAS from 1996, thousands of donated hours from the Miller family, faculty and staff, and alumni donations to FGER. SOURCED (Connor 2009). [K8]
- **Access rule:** the camps are private, operated under a U.S. Forest Service Special Use Permit for JIRP educational and research use; all non-JIRP visitation and use is prohibited. SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geospatial_Summary_of_the_Research_and_Recreational_facilities_within_the_Juneau_Icefield_area. [K6, K1]

### Work and daily life [K7, K14]
- Undergraduates are on the icefield for the middle six weeks of eight and do not leave except in emergencies. SOURCED (FAQ).
- A Cook Crew of 3–4 students cooks for the whole camp for a full day; breakfast and dinner eaten together; field parties pack their own trail lunch; an all-day snack table (pilot bread, peanut butter, jam, trail mix, dried fruit, coffee). SOURCED (Food page). [K12, K10]
- Daily meteorological observations at each camp; snow pits for mass balance at annually revisited sites; GPS velocity surveys; camps about one day's travel apart. Students must be formally assessed as safe for glacier travel by staff safety trainers before beginning glacier mass-balance work; a Safety Week teaches ropes, ice axes, crampons, self-arrest and crevasse rescue; students also learn to load and unload helicopters safely. SOURCED (Connor 2009; FAQ). [K14] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- Field travel groups are chosen for team strength and skill diversity after the first week's assessment. SOURCED (Connor 2009). [K14]
- Final research reports are written at Camp 18; the traverse ends by crossing Atlin Lake by boat to Atlin (population given as 400), where students present at an annual JIRP Science Symposium for residents and visitors. SOURCED (Connor 2009). [K2, P5]
- Daylight: in June sunset about 23:00 and sunrise about 03:00; dark-ish from about 21:00 to 05:00 by late summer; aurora usually seen at Camp 18 or in Atlin later in the season. SOURCED (FAQ). [P3]

## 3 · Kangerlussuaq, Greenland

**Claim to test (from input):** an inland settlement at the ice-sheet edge that exists as a logistics and science gateway for a coastal country. Care: former air base; site history is context, not a driver.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED for its past role; NARROWED for the present.** It sits at the head of a long fjord about 35 km by road from the ice-sheet edge, and for decades it was Greenland's main air hub (the only civilian airport able to take large long-range aircraft) and a science-support base. But the gateway role is being withdrawn: when the rebuilt Nuuk Airport opened in November 2024, Air Greenland's hub moved there, and the population fell from 564 (2024) to 399 (2025), about 30%. The present-day story is a hub town losing the function it exists for, while officials look for replacements (military use, testing, research, road and port). The "inland" part is partly true: it is at the inner end of a fjord, with a harbor, not landlocked.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** western Greenland, Qeqqata municipality, at the head of Kangerlussuaq Fjord (given as 190 km in one section and 160 km in another of the same article); about 50 km north of the Arctic Circle. Gravel road to the ice-cap viewpoint "Point 660" about 35 km; Russell Glacier about 20 km. SOURCED, https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Kangerlussuaq (updated markers March 2024). [P3, P5]
- **Population:** 399 (2025), down about 30% from 564 (2024). SOURCED (Wikipedia). About 500 (2023). SOURCED, Michael Wenger, *Polar Journal*, 27 April 2023, https://polarjournal.net/kangerlussuaqs-future-gets-flashes-of-light-but-remains-unclear/. [P9, K9]
- **Climate:** subarctic bordering on tundra, very dry: annual precipitation 167.7 mm on about 43 days; February daily mean −19.8 °C, July 11.2 °C; record low −47.2 °C, record high 26.6 °C. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P3]

Balseiro; grew from the facilities of the Perón-era Huemul Project; 160 students (90 undergraduates) in the infobox; degrees in physics and nuclear, mechanical and telecommunications engineering; admission after two years of university physics or engineering through a "rigorous admission procedure"; graduates to April 2013: 963 first-degree, 234 master's, 460 doctorates; the CAB's work led to Latin America's first research reactor (1957 per Wikipedia) and Atucha I (1974). SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balseiro_Institute (article flagged as needing citations). RA-1 switched on in 1958 as the first reactor in Argentina and Latin America (date differs from Wikipedia); early contributors Enrique Gaviola, Manuel Balanzat (Spanish), Gino Moretti (Italian); INVAP founded 1976, linked to the CAB and the Institute, built the RA-6 research reactor; Altec SE spun off with provincial and CNEA support. SOURCED, Edith Cabrera, *Diario Río Negro*, 31 May 2025 (updated 27 November 2025), https://www.rionegro.com.ar/historias-de-la-patagonia/hito-historico-los-inicios-del-desarrollo-nuclear-en-argentina-4150452/. [P8, K14]
- **INVAP:** fiscal year July 2021–June 2022 closed with a record 1,518 employees; more than 1,600 at headquarters and offices per the CEO; average tenure 9 years; about 59% university graduates and 31% technicians; lines include nuclear-medicine plants (Mo-99 plant in India), air-traffic-control radars (about two dozen for Argentina's air navigation agency), radar exports (Nigeria); a new building in Bariloche's industrial and technology park. SOURCED, Martín Belvis, *Diario Río Negro*, 21 September 2022, https://www.rionegro.com.ar/economia/invap-da-mas-empleo-y-amplia-el-horizonte-de-negocios-en-el-mundo-2507409/. Turned 50 on 1 September 2026. UNVERIFIED (snippet). [K7, K8, K14, P8] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Universities:** National University of Comahue, National University of Río Negro, National Technological University. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P4]
- **Tourism economy:** Cerro Catedral, the largest ski resort in South America; most visitors June–September; a traditional destination for high-school graduation trips and for *quinceañera* trips; named "National Capital of Adventure Tourism" by Congress in 2012. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [K7, K3]
- **Student graduation trips:** on average almost 30% of the city's tourists each year are graduating students; 44.6% in the winter high season and 45.1% in the spring low season; more than 120,000 students a year before the pandemic; student hotels had the year's highest average occupancy (75%) and employ about 600 a year (about 60% permanent, 40% temporary); controls include a municipal registry of trip coordinators, transport inspections on arrival, and joint work between the municipal Tourism Secretariat and the Asociación de Turismo Estudiantil de Bariloche (ATEBA). SOURCED, *Diario Río Negro*, 11 April 2021, https://www.rionegro.com.ar/por-que-el-turismo-estudiantil-es-estrategico-para-bariloche-1765140/. For 2023: about 120,000 students expected from June, more than 90,000 Argentine (June–January), 18,000–24,000 Chilean (November), about 3,000 Uruguayan (July). SOURCED, Lorena Roncarolo, *Diario Río Negro*, 3 June 2023, https://www.rionegro.com.ar/sociedad/unos-120-mil-estudiantes-desembarcan-en-bariloche-a-partir-de-junio-2947348/. Peak of nearly 160,000 in 1997. UNVERIFIED (snippet). [K7, K3, K4, K8]
- **Transport:** San Carlos de Bariloche International Airport; the Tren Patagónico links the city with Viedma. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [K5]
- **Architecture:** Civic Center (opened 1940, Ernesto de Estrada, national monument 1987); Alejandro Bustillo designed the Llao Llao Hotel and the Neo-Gothic cathedral (structure completed 1947); the center was redesigned in an alpine style in the 1930s. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P3]

### Friction and loss
- **Postwar refugees:** in 1995 Bariloche drew international attention as a refuge for Nazi war criminals including Erich Priebke (who had directed the German School there) and Reinhard Kopps. SOURCED (Wikipedia). Reported neutrally; claims about Hitler are presented in the article as unproven. [K9]
- **Poor outer neighborhoods:** Vivero Municipal and El Alto, on the outskirts, are among the city's poorest areas (partly built houses, unpaved streets, cardboard and sheet-metal shelters); Mapuche families pushed off their territories moved to these margins; the Mapuche share there is significant though poverty is not exclusive to them; a lonko (community head) of the Lof Che Collihuen, living in Vivero Municipal, describes discrimination that led many to lose their language, surname and identity, and states his community's demands are territorial and peaceful. SOURCED, Juan Federico, Cadena 3, 12 October 2022, https://www.cadena3.com/noticia/tension-en-el-sur/ser-mapuche-hoy_338888. People scavenging at the landfill are mentioned. SOURCED (Wikipedia, in passing). [K9, K4, P9] [SM: man versus society]
- **Villa Mascardi:** a Mapuche group occupied National Parks land at Villa Mascardi (about 35 km from Bariloche, inside Nahuel Huapi National Park); federal forces (Prefectura, PSA, Federal Police, Gendarmería, special groups GEOF and Albatros) evicted on 23 November 2017 under a federal judge's order; Rafael Nahuel, 27, was shot and died at hospital; two others injured. SOURCED, *El Cronista*, 25 November 2017, https://www.cronista.com/economia-politica/un-mapuche-murio-tras-un-enfrentamiento-con-prefectura-en-villa-mascardi (the article names the community as "Remu Lafken Mapuche"; other sources name the group Lof Lafken Winkul Mapu). In 2020 Bariloche residents protested an occupation by the Lafken Winkul Mapu; the Coordinadora Mapuche de Río Negro spoke for the Mapuche side. SOURCED, *La Nación*, 1 September 2020, https://www.lanacion.com.ar/sociedad/por-que-hay-conflicto-mapuche-villa-mascardi-nid2437524.
