## 1. Terrestrial supply, price and uses

### 1.1 Where terrestrial helium-3 comes from

- **SOURCED** (isotopes.gov, DOE Isotope Program page "Supply and Demand of Helium-3", undated page, read 2026-11-02): "The DOE's He-3 comes only from NNSA Defense Program operations, as a byproduct of tritium decay separated at the Savannah River Site in South Carolina." The page says "thousands of liters" are made available each year, "Demand as high as 70,000 liters per year was outpacing government supply in 2008," and "projected Federal demand for He-3 is now less than 6,000 liters per year." DOE chairs the White House He-3 Inter-Agency Group, which sets priorities and allocations. The page gives no stockpile size, production volume, auction history or price.
- **SOURCED** (CRS Report R41419, "The Helium-3 Shortage," 6 Oct 2011): "approximately 8,000 liters of new helium-3 per year" from decay of the US weapons-program tritium. **DERIVED:** 8,000 L = 1.08 kg per year.
- **DERIVED (tritium physics):** tritium half-life 12.32 yr gives a decay fraction of 1 - 2^(-1/12.32) = 5.5% per year. One kilogram of tritium that decays yields about 1 kg of helium-3 (atoms are 1:1; masses 3.016 vs 3.016), which is about 7,430 liters. The CRS 8,000 L/yr therefore corresponds to about 1.1 kg of tritium decaying per year, which at 5.5% per year implies roughly 20 kg of tritium in the feeding stock (my inference, not in any source).
- **SOURCED** (Wittenberg, Santarius, Kulcinski, *Lunar Source of He-3 for Commercial Fusion Power*, UWFDM-676, Fusion Technology 10, 167, March/April 1986; scanned PDF, read via OCR): in 1986 they estimated about 300 kg of tritium in US weapons, "5.6% of the tritium decays per year, there should be approximately 15 kg of 3He produced per year," a total from all tritium sources of "approximately 18 kg/yr" after 2000. **Compare:** the 2011 CRS figure (about 1 kg/yr) is about 14 times lower than that 1986 projection. The sources do not explain the gap; the 1986 text itself calls its weapons figure "very approximately estimated."
- **SOURCED** (CRS 2011): stockpile "about 140,000 liters in 1990," "about 235,000 liters in 2001," "about 50,000 liters in 2010." Before 2001 demand was about 8,000 L/yr, "less than new supply from decay," so stock grew; the draw-down accelerated after about 2005. **DERIVED:** 50,000 L = 6.7 kg.
- **Russia:** CRS 2011: Russia "exported about 25,000 liters per year to the U.S. from 2004 to 2008" and then suspended exports. **SOURCED.**
- **Canada:** CRS 2011: Ontario Power Generation's stored tritium "could yield about 130,000 liters over ten years." The 1986 paper estimated CANDU tritium at 30 kg by 2000, giving at least 10 kg of He-3 and about 2 kg/yr afterward (**SOURCED**, scanned). World Nuclear News (16 Sep 2021, **SOURCED**): OPG's subsidiary Laurentis Energy Partners "plans to extract and commercialise helium-3 starting at the end of 2021" from tritium stored at Darlington (four units, 3,512 MWe), and describes itself as "the first civilian, non-military source of helium-3." The article gives no output figure. **No current Laurentis volume was found.**
- **Natural gas and the atmosphere:** the 1986 paper (Table 1, **SOURCED**, OCR): helium in US natural gas has a 3He/4He ratio of only 0.2 parts per million (atoms); US stored helium then held about 29 kg of He-3, known natural-gas reserves with more than 3,000 ppm He about 187 kg; the atmosphere about 4 million kg (ratio 1.4 ppm), recoverable only as a by-product of air liquefaction; mantle venting about 3 kg/yr. CRS 2011 (**SOURCED**) says "U.S. natural gas reserves may hold 1 to 5 million liters" (**DERIVED:** 135 to 670 kg). Wilkes (cited in the 1986 paper) estimated 99% pure He-3 from natural gas at "$156/liter (STP)" in 1986 dollars. The CRS gives "$34 to $300 per liter" incremental energy cost for natural-gas extraction plus infrastructure. **SOURCED.**
- **Interlune's terrestrial separation (company claim):** press release of 20 July 2026 (**SOURCED**, interlune.space): its "Cold Capture" process demonstrated "99% pure helium-3" from Grade A helium; "In 2025, the U.S. produced approximately 81 billion liters of gaseous and Grade A helium"; Cold Capture "could generate up to 2.5 kilograms of helium-3 per year if deployed across U.S. helium facilities," which the company says "would roughly triple current domestic production." The company also says helium-3 is currently produced mainly from aging tritium stockpiles, "limited and costly to expand." **DERIVED check:** 81 x 10^9 L x 0.2 x 10^-6 (the 1986 natural-gas ratio) = 16,200 L = 2.2 kg, which agrees with the company's 2.5 kg. **Claim not independently verified**; the technology "has only been demonstrated on Earth." The earlier AFWERX release (Nov 2025; via a trade-press snippet, **UNVERIFIED**) quoted the CEO as saying the company could "double" US supply, a different multiplier from "triple." **DERIVED:** if 2.5 kg is the added amount and "triples" production, current production is 1.25 kg/yr; if the new total is triple, current is 0.83 kg/yr. Either reading is close to the 8,000 L (1.08 kg) of 2011. A July 2026 commentary (newspaceeconomy.ca, no byline) says Interlune states "U.S. annual production of about 1 kg."
- **World total:** estimates found: "22,000 to 30,000 liters per year" (a market report citing Edelgas Group data, via a Lowy Institute 2025 synthesis, and repeated by postquantum.com, April 2026 updated Oct 2026, **UNVERIFIED**: I did not reach Edelgas or the Lowy original); "near 40,000 liters" (a USGS count mentioned in a search snippet, **UNVERIFIED**, could not be traced). **DERIVED:** 22,000 to 30,000 L is 3.0 to 4.0 kg per year. **No primary source for a world total was found.**
- **A statement by Olson (NASA KSC, AIAA ASCEND 2021 paper 211018, read in full):** "There is only about 100 kg of presently available 3He on Earth," citing a secondary source. **SOURCED as a statement; the stock figures above (6.7 kg in 2010, 13.4 kg reserve in 1986) do not support 100 kg as a stockpile**, so I treat it as a loosely defined figure.

### 1.3 Uses and demand

- **SOURCED** (CRS 2011): the post-2001 shortage was driven by neutron detectors at US borders (radiation portal monitors, "each ... about 50 liters"; the Spallation Neutron Source "received 35,000 liters" in 2008), polarized helium-3 lung imaging, and oil-and-gas well logging. Demand was "about 80,000 liters" in 2008. Federal allocations: 8,767 L (FY2009), 13,225 L (FY2010), 15,294 L (FY2011).
- **SOURCED** (isotopes.gov): peak demand 70,000 L/yr in 2008; "projected Federal demand ... now less than 6,000 liters per year." GAO-26-108053 (published 23 Jun 2026, released 23 Jul 2026) lists He-3 uses as "medical, national security, quantum science, and research," records "583 shipments from fiscal years 2020 through 2025" (third among 30 isotopes), and notes the 2011 shortage response "was delayed because no DOE entity had stewardship responsibility." It gives no liters or dollars. **SOURCED.**
- **Quantum cooling:** Maybell release (7 May 2025, **SOURCED**): "Current dilution refrigerators each use a few dozen liters of helium-3. Larger future systems may need hundreds or thousands of liters." Bluefors (16 Sep 2025, **SOURCED**) says it has "delivered over 1,500 dilution refrigerators." Postquantum.com (**UNVERIFIED**): about 40 L (5 g) per refrigerator; demand "40,000 to 60,000 liters" including quantum; annual deficit "20,000 to 40,000 liters." The Pulsar page (**UNVERIFIED**) cites a quantum-industry projection "above 50,000 L per year" attributed to Bluefors and Interlune announcements.
- **The 2008 to 2011 shortage:** CRS 2011 causes: post-9/11 detector deployment, production tied to weapons needs not demand, auctions ended "in September 2009" in favor of allocations; GAO found weak DOE forecasting and management (GAO-11-472, 2011, as summarized by GAO-26-108053).

## 2. Lunar helium-3

### 2.1 Solar-wind source and how it is stored

- **SOURCED** (Kulcinski lecture slides, Univ. of Wisconsin FTI, 16 Feb 2004, read via `pdftotext`): solar wind is "96% H, 4% He"; 3He/4He in the solar wind is "4 x 10^-4 atomic, 3 x 10^-4 by weight"; average flux "about 3 x 10^8 cm^-2 s^-1"; "4 x 10^25 particles/cm^2" hit the Moon over 4.5 billion years, "equal to the number of atoms in the first 2 meters of lunar regolith"; "at the present SW flux, over 500 million tonnes of 3He hit the Moon over 4.5 billion years"; the near side "collects only about 1/3 that of the far side (Swindle, 1992)." **Discrepancy noted:** Olson (2021) gives the flux as "3 x 10^10 ion/(cm2-s)," two orders higher than Kulcinski's 3 x 10^8; I did not resolve it (not needed for the Moon figures, which rest on measured sample concentrations).
- **SOURCED** (Olson 2021): 3He is "approximately 0.002%" of the solar wind (the Kulcinski ratio 4 x 10^-4 times a 4% helium fraction gives about 0.0016%, consistent); implantation depth about 26 nm in ilmenite; helium is "preferentially retained in titanium dioxide rich minerals, like ilmenite"; "approximately 88% of the helium in Apollo 11 sample 10084 was found in particles smaller than 100 um"; helium concentration "does not appear to be correlated with depth."
- **SOURCED** (Crawford, *Lunar Resources: A Review*, Progress in Physical Geography, preprint arXiv:1410.6865, 2015, read via `pdftotext`): "its average abundance in the regolith is about 4 parts-per-billion (ppb) by mass, with the highest measured concentrations in lunar regolith samples being about 10 ppb (Fegley and Swindle, 1993)." Fegley and Swindle's global average is 4.2 ppb.

### 2.4 Regolith per gram, heating temperature, equipment

- **DERIVED (100% recovery):** tonnes of regolith per kg of He-3 = 1 / concentration. 4 ppb: 250,000 t/kg (250 t per gram). 10 ppb: 100,000 t/kg (100 t/g). 20 ppb: 50,000 t/kg (50 t/g). 30 ppb: 33,000 t/kg. The discussion file's 240,000 t per kg at 4.2 ppb checks. At 20 ppb one tonne of regolith holds 0.02 g, about 0.15 L. Interlune's "three liters" (0.40 g) at 20 ppb needs 20 t and at 4 ppb 100 t; the company says it needs "enough regolith to fill a large backyard swimming pool" (press release, 7 May 2025, **SOURCED**), a statement of order, not a calculation.
- **Process losses, 1986 (UWFDM-676, OCR, **SOURCED**):** beneficiate to the under-20-micron fraction (16.5% of soil mass, "about 80% of the He"), heat to about 600 C ("greater than 90% of the 4He evolved"), overall efficiency 72%, so a 40% excess of soil is mined. "In order to produce 1 kg of 3He, approximately 1.2 x 10^5 Mg (tonnes) of soil must be mined" (the exponent is garbled in the OCR; I recovered 10^5 by cross-check: 1.12 kg of He-3 sits in 2 x 10^4 t of beneficiated soil, and 2 x 10^4 / 0.165 = 1.2 x 10^5 t). The same paper gives the mining equipment of Williams et al.: 965 t of equipment mining 737 t/hr using 2,918 kW; 14 MJ per tonne; processing 1.3 kg/s of soil needs 410 kW of solar power and 29 t of equipment (7 t collectors, 22 t furnaces); cryogenic distillation separation factor 3.0 at 2 K, with a "superleak" stage to pre-enrich; one shuttle-sized vehicle carries 30 t of liquid He-3 per round trip. Their energy accounting: about 2,200 GJ per kg to mine, process and ship; 6 x 10^8 GJ per kg fused; energy payback "about 270," against "about 20" for U-235 fuel and "about 16" for coal.
- **Heating (Apollo-sample release curves, in Olson 2021, **SOURCED**):** "about 50% of embedded 3He is released by 500 C and 95% is released by 800 C" (Apollo sample 10084). Mark-III design heats the under-100-micron fraction to 700 C; a dome concept with buried 800 C heaters reaches about 627 C after 320 hours and releases about 80% of the He-3. Crawford: heating to about 700 C "would itself consume about 5% of the energy obtained from the fusion of the released 3He unless efficient ways of recycling heat ... can be implemented"; about 80% recovery "may be reasonable."
- **Mark-III miner (Univ. of Wisconsin FTI, completed 2006), per Olson 2021 (**SOURCED**):** designed to collect **33 kg/yr** at 10 ppb (66 kg/yr at 20 ppb), "enough 3He to fuel one ~400 MW fusion power plant per year"; 4 m bucket-wheel excavator to 3 m depth over 1 km^2 per year; excavate **1,258 t/hr**, heat 556 t/hr, move 23 m/hr, consume about **350 kW** electric; a 12.3 MW solar collector (10 m dish fed by a 110 m heliostat) heats 157 kg/s of regolith with 85% heat recovery (82 MW without recovery); the heat-pipe heat exchanger weighs about 9 t, 21,500 heat pipes; whole mining operation about **50 t**; "nearly five million tonnes of regolith" excavated per year. **DERIVED checks:** 1,258 t/hr x 3,942 h = 4.96 x 10^6 t; 1 km^2 x 3 m x 1.5 t/m^3 = 4.5 x 10^6 t; 33 kg at 10 ppb and 100% recovery needs 3.3 x 10^6 t, so the design assumes about 67% overall recovery. It also yields other volatiles per tonne at 700 C (Apollo 11 sample 10086.16): H2 43 g, 4He 22 g, 3He **0.007 g** (that is 7 ppb for this sample), H2O 23 g, N2 4 g, CH4 11 g, CO 13.5 g, CO2 12 g.
- **Domed in-situ release (Wittenberg), per Olson:** three 100 m domes moved every lunar day, 30 kg/yr at 10 ppb, mass 174 t, only about 5 t of regolith disturbed (one millionth of the excavator case). **Lab agitation tests (NASA KSC, 2018, simulant implanted with 4He):** 70% of implanted helium removed at 1.5 g/s and 96% at 9 g/s with no heating; real regolith not tested.
- **By-product framing (Olson, Crawford):** a pilot that mines about 400 t of regolith for 15 t of water "could also demonstrate the ability to extract about 6 g of 3He" (assuming equatorial concentration); Crawford notes He-3 "will automatically be released as a by-product of extracting other, more abundant, solar wind-implanted volatiles."
- **Present-day large excavation:** Olson cites coal mining machines (TAKRAF SR 8000) at over 16,000 t/hr of overburden; Interlune/Vermeer's prototype is rated 100 t/hr (below).

### 2.5 Published economics

- **1986:** energy payback about 270 (above); natural-gas He-3 fuel cost "14 mills/kWh" at $156/L (Wilkes).
- **Crawford 2015 (critical):** for 10% of 2040 world electricity (1.4 x 10^20 J world total) at 50% end-to-end efficiency, "about 500 km^2 of high-concentration (i.e. 20 ppb 3He) regolith would need to be processed every year down to a depth of 3 meters"; 5,000 km^2/yr for all of it, with accessible reserves lasting "about 200 years"; D-3He energy density 5.8 x 10^14 J/kg; value "several million US dollars per kg at current wholesale energy prices." He concludes it is "at least premature to identify lunar 3He as a solution to the world's energy needs," but allows "relatively small-scale" uses and "a shortage of 3He on Earth for non-fusion applications." **DERIVED check:** 0.1 x 1.4 x 10^20 J / 0.5 / 5.8 x 10^14 J/kg = 48,300 kg (48 t) per year; 48,300 kg / (20 ppb x 3 m x 1,500 kg/m^3) = 540 km^2/yr, which matches his 500.
- **Schmitt 2007 (AAPG abstract):** "In 2006, the energy in helium-3 was worth about $1.4 billion per metric tonne when compared with industrial coal at $2.50 per million BTU"; "100 kg could run a 1,000 MW fusion plant for a year." Book *Return to the Moon* (Springer/Praxis, 2006): reviews quoted by search snippets give conflicting startup costs ($2.5 billion for a base, $5 billion for reactors), **UNVERIFIED**.
- **2026 commentary (newspaceeconomy.ca, 13 Jul 2026, no byline):** "The original fusion-centered business case for lunar helium-3 mining does not exist in investable form"; "Lunar helium-3 is a physically real resource with a developing niche market"; its arithmetic: at 100 t/hr and 20 ppb, about 2 g/hr; 10,000 L (1.3 kg) needs 65,000 t or 650 operating hours. **DERIVED, my check:** 100 t/hr x 20 ppb = 2 g/hr; 1.346 kg = 673 hr at 100% recovery.
- **Cost of landing equipment:** Aerospace America (2026): delivery to the Moon "$500,000 to $1 million per kilogram" (**UNVERIFIED**, quoted from the article's text as relayed by the fetch tool).
- **DERIVED, what a single 100 t/hr excavator could supply (my arithmetic, 100% recovery, running 8,760 h):** 876,000 t/yr gives 17.5 kg at 20 ppb, 8.8 kg at 10 ppb, 3.5 kg at 4 ppb. Interlune's stated target of 20 kg/yr (Aviation Week via snippet, **UNVERIFIED**) needs 1 million t at 20 ppb and 2 million t at 10 ppb.

## 4. Legal status

All texts quoted from the page named. Whether helium-3 "can be owned" is a legal question the sources do not settle.

- **Outer Space Treaty (opened for signature 27 Jan 1967), Article II (**SOURCED**, NASA History SP-4225 copy):** "Outer space, including the moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means." **Article I:** "Outer space, including the moon and other celestial bodies, shall be free for exploration and use by all States without discrimination of any kind, on a basis of equality and in accordance with international law, and there shall be free access to all areas of celestial bodies." **Article VI:** activities of non-governmental entities "shall require authorization and continuing supervision by the appropriate State Party." The treaty says nothing explicit on private ownership of extracted material.
- **Moon Agreement (opened 18 Dec 1979, in force 11 Jul 1984), Article 11 (**SOURCED**, UNOOSA):** (1) "The moon and its natural resources are the common heritage of mankind"; (3) "Neither the surface nor the subsurface of the moon, nor any part thereof or natural resources in place, shall become property of any State, international intergovernmental or non-governmental organization, national organization or non-governmental entity or of any natural person"; (5) States Parties "undertake to establish an international regime ... to govern the exploitation of the natural resources of the moon as such exploitation is about to become feasible"; (7)(d) "an equitable sharing by all States Parties in the benefits derived from those resources." **Parties:** about 17 to 18 (**UNVERIFIED**; secondary sources of 2019 to 2026 say 18 then 17; I did not reach the UN depositary list). Search results say the United States and Russia are not parties and cite the common-heritage clause as the reason.
- **US Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act, Pub. L. 114-90, 25 Nov 2015, Title IV, 51 U.S.C. 51303 (**SOURCED**, Cornell LII):** "A United States citizen engaged in commercial recovery of an asteroid resource or a space resource under this chapter shall be entitled to any asteroid resource or space resource obtained, including to possess, own, transport, use, and sell the asteroid resource or space resource obtained in accordance with applicable law, including the international obligations of the United States." (The congress.gov text of the full Title IV, including the sovereignty disclaimer, returned 403 and was not read.)
- **Artemis Accords (13 Oct 2020), Section 10 (**SOURCED**, NASA PDF via `pdftotext`):** "The Signatories affirm that the extraction of space resources does not inherently constitute national appropriation under Article II of the Outer Space Treaty, and that contracts and other legal instruments relating to space resources should be consistent with that Treaty." Para. 3: signatories commit "to informing the Secretary-General of the United Nations as well as the public and the international scientific community of their space resource extraction activities." Para. 4: they intend "to contribute to multilateral efforts to further develop international practices and rules applicable to the extraction and utilization of space resources, including through ongoing efforts at the COPUOS." NASA's Artemis page states **78 signatories**, the 78th being Vietnam on 28 October 2026 (**SOURCED**, page read 2026-11-02).
- **Luxembourg, Law of 20 July 2017 on the exploration and use of space resources (published Mémorial A n. 674, 28 July 2017):** "Art.1. Space resources are capable of being owned" (**SOURCED** from a law-firm page, JD Supra; the official Legilux text was not reached; one other translation says "appropriated"). The law sets authorization and supervision procedures "largely inspired by those for the financial sector" (same page). Luxembourg's later submission (quoted in The Space Review, 20 Feb 2023, D. O'Brien, **SOURCED**) says the treaties "have not yet been tested with regard to the rights over resources found in space."
- **UAE, Federal Law No. 12 of 2019 (issued 19 Dec 2019)** on the regulation of the space sector: the law's existence and date are **SOURCED** from search snippets only; its space-resources provisions and the 2023 instruments (Federal Decree-Law 46 of 2023; Ministry of Cabinet Affairs Resolution 19 of 2023, which define space resources as "any non-living resources present in Outer Space, including minerals and water") are **UNVERIFIED**. The legislation portal and the UAE Space Agency PDF returned 403 and one blog hit an SSL error; **I did not read the operative text.**
- **What the literature says (partly sourced):** The Space Review (2023) records Russia's position that extraction "does not give rise to ownership of these resources" and "the national law of any State cannot extend to territories outside its jurisdiction." The IISL "Space Resources" collection (undated; individual authors, not an IISL position, **SOURCED**): M. Sundahl reads the Hague Building Blocks as treating extraction as permissible "in accordance with the principle of non-appropriation under Article II"; A.-S. Martin says the UN treaties "remain silent concerning who would own the rights to minerals, gases and water" and that US, Luxembourg and UAE laws "do not pave the way for any national appropriation by claim of sovereignty"; M. Hanlon calls the OST internally contradictory and notes that the Moon Agreement says resources "in place" may not become property. A search summary (UNVERIFIED) reports that Gorove read Article II as barring only national, not private, appropriation, and that the Hague International Space Resources Governance Working Group adopted its Building Blocks in November 2019; I could not open the Building Blocks text (404 on both copies tried). **NOT FOUND:** a source that settles whether a private company owns extracted He-3 under international law; the sources show a divided reading, with national statutes (US, Luxembourg, UAE) affirming it, and the Moon Agreement (few parties) and some commentators dissenting.

## 5. Scale check against the setting (DERIVED, my arithmetic; no recommendation)

Using the discussion file's 54 kg of helium-3 per gigawatt-year of fusion heat, and independent working: He-3 per kg fused with its deuterium = (1,000 g / 3.016 g/mol) x 6.022 x 10^23 x 18.35 MeV x 1.602 x 10^-13 J/MeV = 5.87 x 10^14 J = 18.6 MW-years, so **53.8 kg per GW-year of heat**. This agrees with the figure in the discussion file (54 kg) and with Olson/Crawford (about 19 MW-yr/kg and 5.8 x 10^14 J/kg); the reaction energy is 18.35 MeV (Crawford says 18.4; Olson gives 14.68 + 3.67 MeV).

|---|---|---|---|
| Discussion file: 290 GW heat (101.7 GWe at 35%) | 290 GWth | 15.6 t | about 15,000 times |

At 9.1 t/yr and 20 ppb with 80% recovery, the regolith to process is 9,100 kg / (20 x 10^-9 x 0.8) = 5.7 x 10^8 t/yr, or about 126 km^2 per year to 3 m at 1.5 t/m^3 (DERIVED). ## FINAL

### What the sources support
- Terrestrial He-3 is a by-product of tritium decay (US: Savannah River; Canada: Darlington). The best sourced US figure is about 8,000 L (about 1.1 kg) per year (CRS 2011). Federal demand is now under 6,000 L/yr (DOE page, undated). The 2008 to 2011 shortage came from a demand surge on a supply set by weapons policy. Quantum cooling is now a named demand driver, with Bluefors (up to 10,000 L/yr, 2028 to 2037) and Maybell ("thousands of liters" per year, 2029 to 2035) contracts.
- The Moon's He-3 is solar-wind-implanted, held mostly in fine ilmenite-rich soil, and measured at about 4 ppb average, up to about 10 to 18 ppb in samples; maps suggest more than 20 ppb in two big maria; the whole-Moon inventory is estimated at about 10^9 kg. About 600 to 800 C releases it. Published mining concepts need millions of tonnes of regolith per year per 30 kg (Mark III: 1,258 t/hr, 350 kW, about 50 t). A critical review finds the fusion case small and premature.
- Interlune's contracts, the NASA payload (2028) and the 100 t/hr excavator prototype are real and dated. As of 2026-11-02 nothing lunar has been extracted or delivered.
- Legal: Article II bars national appropriation by "any other means"; the US (2015) and Luxembourg (2017) statutes and the Artemis Accords (78 signatories) say extraction is not appropriation; the Moon Agreement says resources "in place" cannot become property but has few parties; scholars are divided.

### What the sources do not support
- No source shows lunar He-3 has been returned, sold or delivered. No source shows D-3He net energy. The "abundant" description is contradicted by the ppb figures. No source gives a current DOE price; the 2024 to 2026 prices ($2,000 to $4,000+/L) are the company's and commentators' claims.
- The 1986 weapons-derived estimate of 15 to 18 kg/yr is not borne out by the 2011 CRS figure (about 1 kg/yr); no source reconciles them.
- Olson's "about 100 kg available on Earth" has no support in the stockpile figures found.
- The 22,000 to 30,000 L/yr and 40,000 L/yr world production figures rest on unreached or untraceable sources and are about 3 to 5 times the US-only figure.

### NOT FOUND
- A numeric Chang'e-5 He-3 concentration or extraction temperature (queries in the log; the official release gives none).
- A primary 2024 to 2026 DOE He-3 price or allocation list.
- Laurentis (Canadian) current output in liters.
- The Interlune dollar terms of the Maybell, Bluefors and DOE contracts (only secondary "$300 million" and "~$500 million total").
- Official Chinese or Indian plan documents on He-3 extraction (403 on the plan sources).
- The UAE law's operative space-resources text; the Hague Building Blocks text; a UN depositary count of Moon Agreement parties.
- The USGS "probably unmineable" assessment at a source.
- Any Interlune press release or news dated after July 2026.

### Surprises
- Interlune's "three liters" is 0.40 g, about 20 tonnes of regolith at 20 ppb (a few hundred tonnes at the global average), against its "swimming pool" description.
- The 1986 paper's tritium-based projection was about 14 times the 2011 actual rate.
- The Artemis Accords count reached 78 on 28 Oct 2026, five days before this record.
