### 2.3 How the Rodwell is run (engineering description, adds to the 2026-10-29 file)
- **SOURCED (G. Przybylski, LBNL, "Water at the South Pole," 7 March 2003, https://glacier.lbl.gov/gtp/SouthPole/rod_well.html):** water is heated "with the generator exhaust manifold and muffler" and circulated through the power-plant heat exchanger; the well starts at "a depth of about 80 to 100 feet (the firn layer)"; when it reaches "500 feet deep" a new well is started because "the elevated cost of pumping favors starting a new well"; when it works well it yields "about 14 gallons of water per minute"; the old well becomes "the next sewer outfall"; the melt is nearly deionized ("DI water wants to have ions dissolved in it") and leaches copper and lead from pipes, so sodium and calcium are added; when a well is abandoned a crew goes in to chip out bottom ice that "is full of meteorites which settled to the bottom." The page does not name the operator.
- **DERIVED:** 14 gal/min × 1,440 min = 20,160 gallons a day at full yield (the page gives no daily figure).

### 2.4 What failed (history)
- **SOURCED (southpolestation.com trivia file, https://www.southpolestation.com/trivia/trivia87.txt, read 2026-11-01; an old-timer's account):** "There really was a small Rodrigues well at Old Pole in 1972-73," put in by NCEL "on an experimental basis"; "Unfortunately it was not deep enough or adequately winterized, so it froze up"; at the Dome (1975-77) "the small original snow melter (stainless steel tank) and its rather complex conveyor system, did not work," requiring "a hurriedly contrived makeshift winterover project" and causing "a shortage of water for the first winter"; a new melter was airlifted in the following year and installed outside the power plant; the loop was redone in 1990 with new generators. No crew is named.

### 3.1 What the plant was to the town
- **SOURCED (Prime news agency, 1 December 2011, updated 11 December 2012, https://1prime.ru/20111201/753292964.html, read in Russian):** the plant is the town's "city-forming enterprise" (*gradoobrazuyushchee predpriyatie*); it produced "about 75%" of the electricity in the isolated Chaun-Bilibino power system; director Farit Tukhvetov said a decision on new heat and power sources for the town "will be made by 2014"; the article's own narration (not a named speaker) says that for about 20 years after shutdown "a significant part of the personnel will work in practically the same mode" (monitoring shut-down reactors, removing fuel, dismantling).
- **SOURCED (Mikhail Karpov, "The First Arctic Circle Plant: How and Why the Bilibino NPP Was Built," Lenta.ru, 5 August 2016, https://lenta.ru/articles/2016/08/05/bilibino_aes/, read in Russian):** the plant "supplied the settlement with heat," replacing several boilers with a single heat-and-power center; all technical structures were put under one roof partly to simplify keeping indoor temperatures in the Arctic climate; in 1966 the Komsomol declared it a "shock" construction project and young people came on Komsomol vouchers, with student construction brigades from across the USSR; prefabricated houses for the young builders arrived in September 1966; an An-2 airstrip was built early on; town status 1993; output fell from 350 million kWh (mid-1980s) to 215.9 million kWh (2015) after local enterprises and gold mining closed; operating cost much higher than at NPPs in accessible regions; a coal plant would need about 200,000 metric tons of coal a year versus about 40 metric tons of nuclear fuel. No staff count in the article.
- **UNVERIFIED (search summary, attributed to Rosatom):** the plant was "the only source of heat" in the town and also heated the adjoining industrial complex and housing.

### 3.2 Shutdown, decommissioning, and what happens to staff
- **SOURCED (World Nuclear News, "Bilibino's units being shut down for decommissioning," Thursday 4 December 2025, https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/bilibino-units-being-shut-down-for-decommissioning):** Unit 2 was taken offline first of the three still running; "Unit 3 is scheduled to be shut down on 11 December," "Unit 4 following on 22 December"; capacity "has been replaced by the floating nuclear power plant" (*Akademik Lomonosov*, 70 MW); director Konstantin Kholopov: "electricity will be supplied to us from Pevek"; "Decommissioning is expected to last about eight years"; "Unloading of the used nuclear fuel is expected to last about two years"; "The beginning of full site rehabilitation is expected to occur from around 2054"; chief engineer Andrey Kuznetsov: the work "will require decades." No staff numbers.
- **SOURCED (Nuclear Engineering International, "Bilibino fuel unloading starts," 22 July 2026, https://www.neimagazine.com/news/bilibino-fuel-unloading-starts/):** "Most of the jobs at the station still remain"; after 2027 a "roadmap for transfer to other divisions of Rosatom will be developed"; the town now takes heat and power from "the diesel energy center and the Akademik Lomonosov floating NPP based at Pevek"; unloading from the cores up to two years; moving the fuel to Mayak (Ozersk) at least eight years in total; site rehabilitation "planned to be completed in 2054–2055."

### 3.3 The town now: services and what is failing
- **SOURCED (Viktoriya Lazareva, "Life at the Edge of Russia: How Bilibino Copes with High Prices and Isolation," Peterburg2.ru, 23 May 2026, https://peterburg2.ru/news/zhizn-na-krayu-rossii-kak-bilibino-spravlyaetsya-s-dorogoviznoy-i-izolyaciey-237752.html, read in Russian; a light consumer article):** the population "has shrunk threefold over recent decades and continues to fall"; after the plant closed "the city switched to diesel heating" and the energy center moved to Pevek; the economy rests on "rotational workers and northern salaries" that look large only on paper; butter 660 rubles a kilogram (citing banki.ru); food dearer than in Moscow; in summer the route from Anadyr "turns into impassable mud," in winter an ice road for heavy machinery only; most goods arrive by winter roads open a few months a year, after which only aviation remains and prices rise; town streets are compacted dirt; no chain stores, pharmacies on a schedule, taxis rare; "jobs are shrinking" and the young leave. ### 4.1 Who ran the powerhouse, 1936-1987: the utility operators
- **SOURCED (Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, "LADWP's Presence in Boulder City," history page, https://acquia.ladwp.com/who-we-are/our-history/power-past-present/ladwp-boulder-city/ladwps-presence-boulder-city, read 2026-11-01):** the powerplant was "operated and maintained by LADWP and Southern California Edison (SCE) personnel" "under contract with the Bureau of Reclamation from 1936 to 1987"; "SCE operated four units on the Arizona side for itself and all the private utility participants." LADWP figures: "Beginning with about 85 employees in the initial years of operation"; "The normal staffing level was 125"; wartime peak "as many as 150 LADWP personnel to reside in Boulder City"; "There were 16 LADWP operators assigned per shift for a total of 48," later "reduced to seven per shift when some manual functions were eliminated"; "These personnel covered three shifts 24 hours a day for 50 years"; "Another LADWP on-site group was responsible for maintenance and repair of the generating equipment"; "Major overhaul work required as many as 60 employees to be temporarily brought in from Los Angeles"; "On June 1, 1987, the 50-year contract expired and LADWP's staffing in Boulder City was reduced to just 45 personnel," with every employee given "an opportunity to transition to other roles"; "Today, about two dozen Nevada-based LADWP employees are assigned to the Boulder City area."
- **Housing and social life, same page. SOURCED:** a severe wartime housing shortage in Boulder City "had forced LADWP employees to find housing in Las Vegas"; LADWP built "12 employee cottages ... over five separate blocks" and "20 temporary employee cabins and one larger structure"; "In 1939, Southern California Edison (SCE) constructed its Hoover Dam operator homes on the west side of Cherry Street"; LADWP later left the landlord role "by auctioning off nearly all 80 remaining Boulder City residential properties"; "the LADWP Boulder Picnic ... was regularly attended by 400-500 LADWP employees and their families."
- **DERIVED:** 16 operators × 3 shifts = 48 (as stated); at 7 per shift, 7 × 3 = 21 operators on the rota (minimum, before relief and leave cover, which the page does not give). LADWP's on-site total fell from a normal 125 to 45 at the 1987 handover, a 64% cut (1 − 45/125).

### 8.1 Chuquicamata: the company town at its height
- **SOURCED (Elena Mayorga Marnich, architect and Calama municipal works director, "Sueño de una integración patrimonial o el traslado de Chuquicamata a Calama" (Dream of a heritage integration, or the move of Chuquicamata to Calama), *Urbano*, Universidad del Bío-Bío, November 2004, pp. 4-8, https://revistas.ubiobio.cl/index.php/RU/article/download/518/481/2109; WebFetch could not read the PDF, so the saved copy was converted to text locally and read in Spanish):**
  - Founding and layout: from 1917 a camp was built about 3 km from the mine "to form a self-supporting unit of exploitation"; it had two groups of housing, the "American camp" (*campamento americano*), which "housed only engineers and executives from the United States," ranked by elevation on a hill, with the manager's house ("Special A") at the top (imported materials, five family bedrooms and two for service staff), then sub-managers, superintendents, heads and engineers lower down; the hospital, fire station, police post (*carabineros*) and bakery that supplied the whole camp were in that sector; 3 km away the workers' camp, separated from the foremen's, "likewise hierarchical."
  - Early conditions: worker housing in rows of "Latas" (tin) houses, "Adobe" houses, and "Los Buques" (the ships), long corridors of rooms for single men; "every 12 houses there was a common bathroom" consisting of a long wall 35 cm high and 10 cm wide "without separation"; a 1929 government commission found single men's rooms "miserable hovels where up to 16 individuals sleep on cots," no sewerage, wastewater thrown on the ground; in the New Camp tin section, no sewerage, light, patio or drain, "even the dog and the cat live and sleep there together with the children and the parents in one or two beds."
  - Control: "The employees' behavior was constantly watched in the camp, basically to control theft and alcohol consumption"; shops operated on land the company granted in concession, the trader paying for the building.
  - Peak: "In the sixties 24,000 people were settled in the camp, among workers, their families, police, public servants, teachers and traders," and living conditions "had improved markedly."
  - Services: "The camp of Chuquicamata was able to supply almost all the functions a small town could boast of," naming the Teatro de Variedades, Cine Chile (1943), Club de Empleados (1947), Club Chuquicamata, Liceo América B-10, a maternity hospital, the union auditorium (*Auditorio Sindical*), Club Chuqui, Hospital Roy H. Glover, Club Social Obrero (1967).
  - After nationalization (completed 1971) and the departure of the Americans, many precarious settlements were demolished and new homes built.
  - The move: the idea took shape in 1998 for three reasons: (1) Chilean environmental law, because shared residential and industrial ground meant urban air-emission standards "could not be met," leaving Codelco the choice of cutting production or moving the people; (2) workers' quality of life in a city; (3) cost containment by extending waste dump 95 over the camp. The plan built "3,206 homes" in Calama, "which would allow the descent from the camp of some 10,000 people"; the last stage was due in 2006; the author argues the move had run gradually since 1967, because between 1967 and 1992 nothing could be built in Chuquicamata (mining concessions), pushing housing into Calama; in 1998, with the copper recession, "the application of Outsourcing" and environmental policy, urban development of affected cities was promoted; the Hospital Roy H. Glover was by 2004 "buried under tons of rubble."
- **SOURCED (Codelco, "Una vida centenaria," https://codelco.com/una-vida-centenaria/prontus_codelco/2011-02-16/121822.html, read in Spanish; page date not shown, URL dated 2011-02-16):** Chuquicamata "reached nearly 20,000 inhabitants, surpassing the population of Calama"; schools, clubs, a theater, businesses; the Roy H. Glover hospital, "in its time, the most modern hospital in the country." No date for the peak.
- **SOURCED (Minería Chilena, "Sin agua y sin luz, los últimos 13 habitantes que se resisten a abandonar Chuquicamata," 18 February 2008, https://www.mch.cl/sin-agua-y-sin-luz-los-ultimos-13-habitantes-que-se-resisten-a-abandonar-chuquicamata/, read in Spanish):** the camp "was definitively closed in August 2007" after 92 years for environmental and economic reasons; the relocation of **3,343 families** to Calama is complete and officially "there is no one there"; 13 people remained, rejecting Codelco's compensation and waiting for the courts; Codelco cut electricity and water ("they cut off my electricity and water so I'll leave," Alcides Lira, 81, who carried water in drums from neighboring houses); a shop owner was offered 10 million pesos for her store; another resident said a 2004 agreement (11 million pesos and the option to buy commercial premises in Calama by April 2005) was never honored. **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** official closing ceremony 31 August 2007; moves began in 2004 once new housing in Calama was finished.

### 12.1 SIRIUS-19 (2019): the primary psychology paper
- **SOURCED (Supolkina N., Yusupova A., Shved D., Gushin V., Savinkina A., Lebedeva S.A., Chekalina A., Kuznetsova P., "External Communication of Autonomous Crews Under Simulation of Interplanetary Missions," *Frontiers in Physiology* 12:751170, published 9 November 2021, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.751170/full, read):**
  - Design: 120 days, March to July 2019, at the Institute of Biomedical Problems (IBMP), Moscow; six crew: three women aged 29-33 (all Russian) and three men aged 31-44 (one Russian, two American); real-time phone contact in the first and last 10 days only, and on days 11-108 only delayed contact with a 5-minute one-way delay; a six-day simulated lunar landing by four crew members around weeks 9-10, splitting the crew in two. Comparison: SIRIUS-17 (17 days, October-November 2017, three Russian women aged 28-37, two Russian men and one German man aged 33-44) and earlier Mars-500 findings.
  - Data: "320 audio sessions" in the first 10 days (11 hours) against 34 in the last 10 days (1 hour 17 minutes); 2,399 crew video messages (38 h 35 min) and 1,047 Mission Control video messages (18 h 40 min); 145 crew text messages and 1,035 from Mission Control; crew video messages fell from about 200 in the first week to 115-120 in the final weeks; private communication excluded.
  - Findings: the "detachment" seen in Mars-500 was confirmed (communication volume fell, read as growing autonomy), with a temporary rise during the landing simulation; women's statements were more emotional, with more humor and positive reappraisal, and mentioned sleep quality more; men's showed more cognitive content and more anger, with a non-significant tendency toward confrontation (p = 0.084); by the end the sexes' styles had converged in volume, emotionality and some acoustic measures; sociometric cohesion rose and perceived similarity among crewmates rose; cohesion on the leisure criterion dropped after the landing split (p = 0.042); crew with medium reactive anxiety used more self-control and informing statements, those with low anxiety more requests, criticism and initiative.
  - Limitations stated by the authors: six men and six women across both studies, "pronounced individual differences," "too early" for general conclusions; nationality and sex were confounded (all women Russian); SIRIUS-17 too short for trends; the "third quarter" explanation needs testing; landing-period recordings incomplete through technical problems.
- **DERIVED:** audio sessions fell 89% between the first and last 10 days (1 − 34/320); audio time fell 88% (from 660 to 77 minutes).

### 13.1 Australia: the law a station leader works under (primary text)
- **SOURCED (Australian Antarctic Territory, Criminal Procedure Ordinance No. 2, 1993, Federal Register of Legislative Instruments F2008B00478, https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2008B00478/asmade/1993-11-17/text/original/pdf; the as-made text, read after local conversion of the PDF; later amendments not checked):**
  - Who: "'special constable' means: (a) leader; or (b) police officer"; "'field leader' means person appointed by the Commonwealth to be the leader of the members of an Australian expedition in the Territory, except those on a ship"; "'leader' means field leader, voyage leader or deputy voyage leader." The Minister issues each leader an identity card.
  - Where to hold someone (s. 6): "A field leader may designate in writing a building, or part of a building, to be a place of detention in the Territory"; a voyage leader may so designate "a ship, or a part of a ship."
  - Arrest (s. 10): a special constable, "with such assistance as is necessary and reasonable and if necessary and reasonable by force, may arrest a person without a warrant" on reasonable grounds that the person is committing or has committed an offense and that a summons would not secure their appearance in court, prevent repetition, or protect evidence. Section 13: the arrested person must be placed "in custody in a place of detention as soon as practicable," with others' help and reasonable force if needed.
  - Remote handling: oral communication and documents may pass "by means of telecommunications" (s. 4).
  - Detention (s. 30): "As soon as practicable after a person is charged ... a leader must apply to a Magistrate for a warrant to detain the person," in writing or "orally, if it is impracticable to apply in writing."
  - Long holds (s. 31): "A detainee must be brought before a court as soon as practicable," and "As soon as practicable after the end of each period of 28 days during which a detainee is in the custody of a leader, the leader must report to a Magistrate the fact that the detainee remains in custody."
  - Removal (Part 8, s. 42): a detainee may be "conveyed by a custodian to and from a prison" in an Australian State or Territory, "by whatever route is reasonable," including transit between the Territory and a State or Territory.
