## 2. Safe return and alternates

### 2a. The terms and the rule (Australian regulation)

- Australian civil rules for larger aeroplanes do not use the phrase "point of safe return" in the text read; they use **"critical point"** together with the category **"isolated destination aerodrome."** **SOURCED** — Part 121 (Australian Air Transport Operations—Larger Aeroplanes) Manual of Standards 2020, as made, F2020L01561, https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2020L01561/asmade/2020-12-10/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html
- **Definition (s 4.03), verbatim in substance:** a planned destination is an *isolated destination aerodrome* if the fuel to (i) fly a missed approach there, (ii) climb, (iii) fly to "the nearest aerodrome that would meet the requirements for a destination alternate aerodrome," (iv) descend and (v) land there, plus final reserve fuel, "is greater than" — for a turbine-engine aeroplane — "the fuel required to fly the aeroplane for 2 hours at the planned cruising level above the planned destination aerodrome, including final reserve fuel." **SOURCED** — MOS s 4.03(1)-(3).
- **Fuel when the destination is isolated (s 7.02(4)(b)):** destination alternate fuel is the fuel "to fly for 2 hours at normal cruise consumption above the isolated destination aerodrome, including final reserve fuel." **SOURCED** — MOS s 7.02(4).
- **The go/no-go point (s 4.12):** "A critical point must be calculated before the commencement of the flight" (and entered on the operational flight plan). "The flight must not be continued beyond the critical point unless: (a) an assessment of the authorised weather forecast relevant to the aerodrome; and (b) the aircraft traffic and other operational conditions relevant to conducting an approach and landing at the aerodrome; indicate that a safe landing can be made at the estimated time of use." Ceiling/visibility minima for continuing are those of table 4.11 (or, on an area forecast, lowest safe altitude for the last segment plus 500 ft and visibility not less than 8 km). **SOURCED** — MOS s 4.12(2)-(5).
- **Critical point definition (s 1.04):** "a point en route ... at which the aeroplane can: (a) if it arrives at the point with adequate fuel to complete the flight to the planned destination aerodrome while maintaining the fuel required by subsection 7.05(3)—continue to that aerodrome; or (b) otherwise—divert to an en-route alternate aerodrome while maintaining the fuel required." **SOURCED** — MOS s 1.04.
- **Alternate minima depend on the alternate's approach aids (table 4.11):** e.g. a single-runway aerodrome with one instrument approach must be forecast at least 400 ft above the second-lowest minimum and 1,500 m more visibility; an aerodrome with **no** authorised instrument approach needs lowest safe altitude plus 500 ft and 8 km visibility. **SOURCED** — MOS s 4.11 and table 4.11.
- Whether the Skytraders A319 Hobart-Wilkins service is flown under Part 121 as written, or under an operator-specific approval/variation (s 4.21 allows "operational variation approvals—alternate aerodrome requirements"), was **not found**. The RAAF C-17 is a state aircraft and outside CASA's civil rules (**UNVERIFIED**, general knowledge, not checked).
- Wilkins itself is "a Civil Aviation Safety Authority (CASA) certified runway on natural ablated glacial ice at ~740 m ASL, ~70 km SE of Casey." **SOURCED** — AAD Draft IEE, Aviation Operations 2020-2025, s 2.5, https://www.antarctica.gov.au/site/assets/files/54769/draft_aviation_iee_-_2020-25.pdf

### 2d. What a second airfield on the same coast would or would not change (about 780 km apart)

This subsection applies the rule text above to the question. **The rule text is SOURCED; the application is DERIVED (my reasoning), not stated by any operator or regulator. No real document was found that analyzes a pair of East Antarctic coastal runways as mutual alternates.**

- Under s 4.03, a destination stops being "isolated" only if a **qualifying** destination alternate exists and the fuel to go around, climb, fly there, descend, land, plus final reserve, is **not more than** 2 hours of cruise fuel above the destination. About 780 km at a jet's cruise speed is roughly one hour of cruise; with the missed approach, climb, descent and final reserve added, it **may** fall under the 2-hour figure. **DERIVED, not checked against any aircraft's fuel data.** If it did, flights could plan to that alternate instead of carrying 2 hours' holding fuel or turning back at the critical point.
- But "qualifying" carries conditions that the sources show are hard to meet in Antarctica: (1) an authorised weather forecast for the alternate at the estimated time of use, at or above the table 4.11 minima (for an aerodrome with no instrument approach, lowest safe altitude plus 500 ft and 8 km visibility) (**SOURCED**, MOS s 4.11); (2) the alternate must meet the other aerodrome requirements of the civil aviation legislation (**SOURCED**, note to s 4.03); (3) prior agreement from the operator of that airfield, which COMNAP says exists only for its own use (**SOURCED**, AFIM Procedures s 4.2); (4) the alternate must actually be open (prepared surface, staffed) at that time; Wilkins is staffed by up to eight people only in the flying season and closes about six weeks in midsummer (**SOURCED**, AAD).
- Whether weather at two coastal sites about 780 km apart is independent enough for one to serve as the other's alternate was **not found** in any source read. No claim is made either way.
- **One historical precedent found later (while researching item 4), SOURCED:** the Soviet Il-18D flight Moscow to Molodezhnaya, February 1980: "Для обеспечения безопасности полета в районе предполагаемой «точки возврата» на дежурстве находилось НИС ААНИИ «Профессор Визе». С его борта осуществлялась оперативная передача сводок авиационной погоды. Запасной ВПП была выбрана площадка на станции Новолазаревская." (To ensure flight safety, the AARI research vessel *Professor Vize* stood on duty near the planned "point of return," transmitting aviation weather reports; the site at Novolazarevskaya station was chosen as the alternate runway.) — Lukin (2022), *Российские полярные исследования* no. 48, pp. 13-17 (full citation in item 1b). By my calculation Molodezhnaya to Novolazarevskaya is about 1,370 km great-circle (**DERIVED**, haversine from station coordinates). So a second coastal Antarctic runway **has** been used as a planned alternate, once, for a one-off state expedition flight with a weather ship at the point of return; it is not a scheduled-service precedent. The same source records that in 1961-62 the aircraft took on full fuel for the return to Christchurch at a runway in the Bunger Hills oasis (not at Mirny), and that in 1963-64 the Il-18s first landed on a runway prepared in a Bunger Hills lagoon before flying on to Mirny, then left on 3 January 1964 without a Bunger stop; it concludes that safe heavy wheeled-aircraft operations on runways prepared at coastal stations are "весьма сложно" (very difficult).

### 3a. The runway's own weather (Wilkins)

All from Carpentier, S. (2010), "Weather and forecasting at Wilkins ice runway, Antarctica," *IOP Conf. Ser.: Earth Environ. Sci.* 11 012001, doi:10.1088/1755-1315/11/1/012001 (Bureau of Meteorology, Hobart). **SOURCED** (read in full):
- Setting: Wilkins at about 750 m ASL, 63 km SE of Casey, "on the western slope of Law Dome, which rises to 1395m ASL and is about 150km in diameter." The weather is "complicated by interactions between the strongly contrasting airmasses of cold/dry 'continental' and warm/moist 'maritime' that meet in the area."
- **Katabatic winds:** "katabatic flow is limited to a few hundred metres above the surface; consequently katabatic outflow off the continent rarely reaches the elevation of Wilkins." Southeasterlies, the commonest direction at Haupt Nunatak on the Vanderford Glacier below, are "quite rare at Wilkins runway."
- **Prevailing wind:** "the prevailing wind is from the northeast through east to east-southeast. Apart from rare northwesterlies ... other wind directions are practically non-existent." In summer Wilkins "typically lies in the wake of Law Dome."
- **Blizzards and their onset:** forecast challenges "include the sudden onset of blizzards associated with the formation of an internal gravity wave." Low-pressure systems and warm fronts "typically affect the area on a weekly to daily basis" and "tip the balance from blocked flow to flow-over regimes," producing a gravity wave "which leads to the bulk of strong wind events at Wilkins runway and at Casey." The swing can be "as markedly different as sunny with light winds versus blizzard conditions." Example given: a gale at Casey lasting 24 hours.
- **Turbulence:** "Turbulence in the wake of Law Dome is a real concern for both forecaster and pilot."
- **Low cloud and fog:** with westerlies, "Forced ascent ... against the slope of Law Dome invariably leads to the formation of low cloud or fog"; whether the runway is in cloud depends on the height of the subsidence inversion. Steaming fog can wrap onto Casey with a Vincennes Bay mesolow (example: fog for one hour, 21 December 2005).
- **Forecast data gap:** the nearest upstream upper-air station is Dumont d'Urville "over 1000km away," so the key flow regime is estimated from models; "Direct observations of the airmass to the east ... could only improve the chances of a successful forecast."
- **Temperature limit:** "a temperature at or below -7°C at the runway is necessary to achieve the surface friction constraints imposed by the Australian Civil Aviation Authority. Therefore flights typically leave Hobart at 5am for a 9:30am (2230UTC) arrival at Wilkins, coinciding with the start of warming of the runway surface." (AFP 2012 gives "below minus 5 degrees Celsius"; the two figures conflict and are recorded as found.)
- **Precipitation:** the western slope of Law Dome, including Wilkins, is in a "rain shadow"; the eastern side is modeled at >2.5 m water equivalent a year.
- **Service frequency in 2010:** "The weekly service is provided by an Airbus A319 jet."

Other Wilkins weather facts:
- Runway designation 09T/27T. **SOURCED at Wikipedia.** That an east-west runway lines up with the prevailing NE-E-ESE wind, so that most strong winds are head- or tailwinds rather than crosswinds, is **DERIVED** (my reading of two sources together); no source states it.

### 4d. How Soviet and Russian aviation operated at Mirny

All **SOURCED** from Lukin (2022), *Российские полярные исследования* no. 48, pp. 13-17 (AARI; read in full; translations mine), unless marked:
- First Soviet intercontinental flight: Il-18V and An-12 left Moscow 15 December 1961, routed Tashkent - Delhi - Rangoon - Jakarta - Darwin - Sydney - Christchurch - McMurdo - Mirny, and "26 декабря самолеты успешно совершили посадку на подготовленной снежной взлетно-посадочной полосе (ВПП) станции Мирный" (landed on a prepared snow runway at Mirny on 26 December). The Il-18 flew several missions in Antarctica, including carrying a sick Australian from Mawson (first flown to Mirny) to McMurdo on 7 January 1962. Full fuel for the return to Christchurch was taken on at a runway in the Bunger Hills oasis.
- Second flight, November 1963 to January 1964: two Il-18Vs carrying an 80-person air expedition (69 expedition members) landed first on a runway prepared in a Bunger Hills lagoon, then flew to Mirny.
- The author's conclusion from these flights: "стало очевидно, что обеспечить безопасность полетов с посадками и взлетами тяжелых самолетов на колесных шасси на ВПП, подготовленные на прибрежных антарктических станциях, весьма сложно. Требуется серьезная подготовительная работа по созданию ВПП из уплотненного снега." (It became clear that safe landings and takeoffs of heavy wheeled aircraft on runways prepared at coastal Antarctic stations are very difficult; serious preparatory work is needed to build runways of compacted snow.) The Soviet program then moved its heavy-aircraft gateway to Molodezhnaya (compacted-snow runway 2,560 × 42 m, completed 1979) and later Novolazarevskaya (blue-ice runway accepted 1984).
- End of air service at Mirny: "From the earliest days of its existence until the 1990/91 summer field season of the 36th Soviet Antarctic expedition (SAE), the employed air service ceased its operations due to the increasingly worsening economic situation in Russia." **SOURCED** — Popov et al. (2019), *Earth's Cryosphere* 23(1): 69-77, doi:10.21782/EC2541-9994-2019-1(69-77), https://pureportal.spbu.ru/files/52258533/ . Russian Wikipedia instead says the aerodrome was closed in 1990 because of ice cracks (**SOURCED at Russian Wikipedia; conflicts with Popov et al.**; not resolved).
- Current landing strip (operations-relevant facts only; ground conditions are the other file's subject): a Basler BT-67 (DC-3T) of ALCI landed on 10 February 2016 on a new strip; it is marked (centerline, shoulders, threshold) with "a wind cone"; ten crevasses 10-20 cm wide were found between 787 m and "the end (1300 m) of the landing strip"; it was built "on a poorly investigated dynamically active glacier" with flow of about 6-80 m/yr; its steepest segment averages 1.66° (29 m/km), parameters "close to the limit values" of Russian civil aviation norms. **SOURCED** — Popov et al. The paper states the strip's purpose as a stop for medium-range aircraft between stations "east of Casey (Australia) and to the west of Davis (Australia)," about 1,500 km apart: "an intermediate airport is critical for ensuring the flights safety." **SOURCED.**

### 5c. What each leg asks of each class (DERIVED arithmetic; not an operator's or manufacturer's analysis)

The planning rule in item 2 requires, for an isolated destination, fuel to hold two hours over the destination (turbine aircraft) and a critical point beyond which the flight may not continue unless the destination forecast supports a landing; a flight that turns back at that point must then reach its origin. In practice the real links carry round-trip fuel (item 2b). The figures below set great-circle round trips (no reserves) against the published ranges in 5b, which assume typical payload and standard reserves at a normal destination, so a real Antarctic round trip with holding fuel and cold-weather margins would do worse. Each destination is stated on its own.

**Casey / Wilkins.** Hobart-Wilkins-Hobart is about 6,844 km (3,695 nm); Perth-Wilkins-Perth about 7,750 km (4,185 nm).
- Standard-tank narrow-bodies: A319neo 6,850 km, A320neo 6,300 km, 737-8 6,480 km published, i.e. at or below the Hobart round trip before any reserve. The AAD's A319 flies this round trip because it is a long-range variant ("over 5,000 nautical miles") carrying about 38 passengers (**SOURCED**, AAD).

### 5d. What an Antarctic runway has offered each class, in real operations

- **Wide-body (Boeing 787-9, 767; Airbus A340):** Troll, blue ice, 3,000 m × 60 m (or 3,300 × 100 m per Wikipedia; the two figures conflict); Norse 787 carried 45 passengers and 12 t in 2023. **SOURCED** (airdatanews; avweb; Wikipedia). - **General:** "Because of ice's low coefficient of friction, planes tend to decelerate with reverse thrust, as opposed to traditional means of braking the wheels, and so runways are often several miles long." **SOURCED at Wikipedia** ("Blue ice runway"; tertiary).

## 6. Passenger processing and border clearance

**What this item covers:** how people bound for Antarctic programs are cleared at a gateway airport (border, customs, quarantine and biosecurity, medical), and what a gateway terminal physically has. Each gateway is described on its own. "Clearance" here means four separate things that the sources treat separately: (a) border exit and re-entry; (b) biosecurity (keeping non-native species out of Antarctica, an Antarctic Treaty obligation, and also the gateway country's own import rules on return); (c) medical fitness; (d) operator pre-flight processing (baggage, cold-weather clothing, briefing).

### 6a. The treaty-level rule all gateways serve (biosecurity)

- **Annex II to the Protocol on Environmental Protection, Article 4 (as attached to Measure 16 (2009)):** "No species of living organisms not native to the Antarctic Treaty area shall be introduced onto land or ice shelves, or into water, in the Antarctic Treaty area except in accordance with a permit"; "Dogs shall not be introduced onto land, ice shelves or sea ice"; food is exempt "provided that no live animals are imported for this purpose" and plant and animal products are "kept under carefully controlled conditions"; "Each Party shall require that precautions are taken to prevent the accidental introduction of micro-organisms (e.g., viruses, bacteria, yeasts, fungi) not present naturally in the Antarctic Treaty area"; "No live poultry or other living birds shall be brought into the Antarctic Treaty area." **SOURCED** — https://documents.ats.aq/recatt/Att432_e.pdf . (Whether and when the 2009 revision of Annex II entered into force was not checked: **not verified**.)
- **CEP Non-native Species Manual (July 2011 edition):** guidance, explicitly "recommendatory" and not "mandatory." Prevention "should focus on pre-departure measures within the logistics and supply chain" — at the point of origin, "at gateways to Antarctica (ports, airports)," on "means of transport (ships, aircraft)," and at Antarctic stations used as departure points. Listed practices include: clothing for Antarctica laundered unless new, pre-worn footwear "cleaned thoroughly before arrival in Antarctica"; cargo checked "before loading on board the aircraft or vessels"; cargo packed, stored and loaded "in an area with a clean, sealed surface (e.g. bitumen, concrete ...)"; "Intercontinental aircraft are checked and treated as necessary ... to ensure they are insect free before departure to the Antarctic." **SOURCED** — https://www.env.go.jp/nature/nankyoku/kankyohogo/database/visit/pdf/non_native_species_en.pdf (2011 edition; later editions not read).

### 6b. Hobart (Australian Antarctic Program, to Wilkins)

**Border.** "The Australian Antarctic Territory is not considered part of Australia under the Migration Act 1958, so all travelers have an international movement recorded"; a valid passport is required, valid six months past the scheduled return. **SOURCED** — AAD "Passenger flight information" (item 1a). Australian Border Force involvement is shown by its handling of Tourist Refund Scheme claims at its Hobart office by appointment before travel day (**SOURCED**, same page). Who staffs the outbound border check on the day at Hobart airport, and where in the airport it happens: **not found**.

**Medical and personal clearance (before the airport).** "Every expeditioner must meet strict medical standards": "A full physical examination by an AAD-approved doctor"; "Blood tests, heart and lung function tests, hearing and vision tests, and dental checks"; "Screening for underlying health conditions that could cause problems in a remote environment." Expeditioners deploying "for a period of 3 months or longer" complete a "Psychological (Adaptability) Assessment" (online questionnaire plus interview with a registered psychologist). "All successful applicants must undergo a National Police Check." Mandatory pre-departure training in Hobart takes "between two and twelve weeks" (workplace health and safety, field survival, technical training, station operations, community living). **SOURCED** — AAD Jobs, "Essential information for all applicants," https://jobs.antarctica.gov.au/living-and-working-in-antarctica/application-process/ . **Health quarantine (one season, 2020-21).** "All our expeditioners will have a compulsory isolation period of two weeks in Hobart immediately before going south ... whether they come from interstate or are already in Tasmania"; COVID-tested "three times"; isolation at a Hobart hotel; masks and gloves "after leaving the hotel and during transport to the airport"; "The air crew will also be in isolation prior to travel." **Biosecurity (cargo and aircraft).**

### 6d. Punta Arenas (Chile), to King George Island (Teniente Marsh / Frei) and to Union Glacier

**Gateway role.** INACH: "Punta Arenas (Chile) is the gateway city to Antarctica that most countries (between 19 and 23 depending on the season) use to access that territory." **SOURCED** — INACH, "COVID-19 Protocol for control and monitoring of passengers and cargo entry to Antarctica through Chile," https://www.spp-antarktisforschung.uni-rostock.de/storages/uni-rostock/Alle_MNF/Antarktisforschung/Dokumente/Aktuelles/COVID-19_PROTOCOL_INACH_english.pdf (undated; written for the 2020-21 pandemic period).

**How Chile coordinated program passengers (pandemic period).** INACH was named "national technical coordinating agency" for passenger and cargo traffic "between Punta Arenas and the Antarctic Peninsula," working with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, operators, the health and maritime authorities. ### Addendum to item 3 (Casey / Wilkins): crosswind and the turned-back record

All **SOURCED** from the operator's own statements unless marked. They date from the first two seasons (2008-2010) and may not describe current limits.
- **Landing criteria stated by Skytraders (2008):** "We will only land if there is less than 5 knots of cross-wind, we have 'primary' GPS navigation and if the runway friction coefficient is 0.2 or better"; "we are looking for a cloud base of 2,000 feet or more, and a visibility of at least 10 kilometers, before we will commit to a landing"; "one of the biggest dangers is the lack of surface definition ... exacerbated by a phenomenon known as whiteout, which can occur even when there is a high cloud-base and, sometimes, unlimited visibility"; "We tend to go only when the weather is good ... in the Antarctic the weather is usually either very, very good, or very, very bad." — Garry Studd (Skytraders), Airbus *FAST* 42, July 2008, https://skytraders.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/FAST42.pdf

## 7. What the facts imply

**Factual only; no recommendation.** This is the only section that sets the two places side by side. Each point says what it rests on, and where the sources stop.

### 7a. Distance and aircraft

- From Hobart, Mirny is about 750 km farther than Wilkins one way (4,171 km against 3,422 km), about 1,500 km farther on a round trip (8,342 km against 6,844 km). From Perth the difference is about 260 km (4,139 km against 3,875 km). (**DERIVED**, item 5a.)
- The only real scheduled jet link in this sector (Hobart to Wilkins) is flown with round-trip fuel on purpose: the operator chose an aircraft "without a point-of-no-return" because "there is no nearby alternate" (**SOURCED**, item 3 addendum). On published manufacturer ranges, standard-tank narrow-bodies are at or below the Hobart round trip to Wilkins and below the round trip to Mirny before any reserve; the A321XLR clears the Wilkins round trip by about 1,850 km and the Mirny round trip by about 360-420 km before reserves; the wide-bodies listed clear both by a wide margin. (**DERIVED** from sourced ranges, item 5c; no payload-range chart was read, so payload at these ranges is unknown.)
- So, on the distance facts alone: a round-trip-fuel narrow-body service is a real, working pattern for the Wilkins site (with a long-range variant and about 38 passengers); for Mirny the same pattern would leave little or no margin in any narrow-body class whose range was read, and points toward wide-bodies or toward refueling at the Antarctic end, which item 2 shows does not remove the need to carry fuel to turn back at the critical point. (**DERIVED.**)

### 7b. Weather

- The two weather records are **not like for like**: Casey's long-run statistics are for the coastal station, not for Wilkins (about 65-70 km inland and 700-780 m higher), while Mirny's are for a coastal station at 35 m. (**SOURCED**, items 3-4.)
- On the station records, Mirny's long-run mean wind (23.1 kt, 1991-2020) is about two-thirds higher than Casey station's (13.9 kt); in the October-March flying months Mirny ranges 15.7-24.7 kt against Casey's 10.5-13.5 kt; Mirny's calmest months (December, January, 15.7-15.9 kt) are still above Casey's annual mean (13.9 kt). (**SOURCED** data, **DERIVED** means, items 3b and 4a.) Secondary sources give more than 200 days a year above 15 m/s at Mirny (item 4a).
- Wilkins sits above the katabatic layer, with winds "practically" confined to northeast through east-southeast and a runway aligned with them; the operator stated a landing limit of under 5 knots of crosswind (2008). (**SOURCED**, item 3 and addendum.) For Mirny **no wind direction was found**, so **no statement can be made about crosswind exposure on any runway alignment there.** This is the largest gap in the weather comparison.
- Visibility: Wilkins has documented low cloud and fog with westerlies and sea fog carried inland from Vincennes Bay, sudden-onset blizzards, and turbulence in the wake of Law Dome (**SOURCED**, item 3). Mirny has documented storms with visibility under 200 m, AARI's statement that storms confining staff indoors are "frequent" (**SOURCED**, item 4 and addendum), but **no visibility or blizzard-day statistics**. The sources do not support a numerical visibility comparison.
- Temperature: the Wilkins operation needs runway temperatures at or below −7 °C (or −5 °C per AFP) and closes about six weeks in midsummer for melt (**SOURCED**, items 1 and 3). Mirny's mean daily maxima are above freezing in December and January (**SOURCED at Wikipedia** from AARI data, item 4b). Whether that would close a runway at Mirny depends on the surface type, which is the geology file's subject.

### 7c. Operating history at each place

- Wilkins: a CASA-certified blue-ice runway with jet service since 2008 (131 landings to January 2018), short of the planned 20 or more flights a season, chiefly because of melt; one early season had 2 weather turn-backs in 12 flights. (**SOURCED**, items 1 and 3.)
- Mirny: heavy wheeled aircraft (Il-18) landed on prepared snow in 1961-62 and 1963-64, refueling or staging at Bunger Hills; AARI's own historian concluded that heavy wheeled operations on runways prepared at coastal stations were "very difficult," and the Soviet program moved its heavy gateway elsewhere; air service at Mirny ended around 1990-91; today's strip is about 1,300 m on an active, crevassed glacier, used by a Basler since 2016. (**SOURCED**, item 4d.) No source records jet operations at Mirny after 1991.

### 7d. Alternates and a second runway on the same coast

- Under the Australian rule read (Part 121 MOS 2020, as made), a destination stops being "isolated" only if a **qualifying** alternate is within the 2-hour fuel figure, with a forecast, minima, approval and an open, staffed runway (**SOURCED** rule; application **DERIVED**, item 2d). A runway about 780-815 km away may fall inside the fuel figure, but nothing found shows that weather at the two sites is independent enough, and COMNAP says Antarctic facilities are for their operator's own use unless agreed in advance (item 2).
- The real history points both ways: Australia considered and **did not build** an alternate aerodrome at Bunger Hills, about 250 nm from Wilkins, choosing round-trip fuel instead (**SOURCED**, Colborne 2010); the Soviet program used Bunger Hills for refueling and staging in 1961-64 and named Novolazarevskaya as the alternate for one 1980 flight (**SOURCED**, Lukin 2022). Bunger Hills lies between the two sites, about 480 km from Wilkins and about 350 km from Mirny (**DERIVED** from approximate coordinates 66.25°S 100.75°E, which are **UNVERIFIED**; the Wilkins figure matches Colborne's 250 nm). No source analyzes a two-runway system on this coast for scheduled service.

### 7e. Passenger processing

- In every real gateway found (Hobart, Christchurch, Punta Arenas), border exit, operator check-in, baggage, cold-weather clothing and most biosecurity are handled **at the gateway city before departure**, and medical clearance **before travel** (**SOURCED**, item 6). No border, customs or immigration presence at any Antarctic airfield was mentioned in any source. So, on the facts found, **where the border is cleared does not depend on which Antarctic site is chosen**; it happens at Hobart (or Perth) either way. (**DERIVED** from item 6.)
- What the Antarctic end does need, in every real case, is **capacity to absorb weather waits and transfers**: Wilkins' transit rooms and emergency shelter for 40+, the AAD's wish to stop overnighting transit passengers at Casey, USAP "boomerang" bags, DAP's two spare hotel nights per package. (**SOURCED**, items 1 and 6.) The scale of that need would follow the weather-loss rate, which was **not found** for either site.
- Real volumes for scale: Wilkins under 45 passengers a flight and under 800 return passengers a season (planned); Christchurch over 2,000 passengers in a season over 100+ flights; DAP 70 passengers a flight in Antarctic configuration. (**SOURCED.**) Nothing found describes an Antarctic terminal at scheduled-airline volumes.
- One fact of relation: both real sites lie in the sector Australia claims (the Australian Antarctic Territory, 45°E-160°E excluding Adélie Land: **UNVERIFIED**, general knowledge, not checked), but Mirny is a Russian station and Casey an Australian one; the AAD lists Mirny only as a place to "access Mirny (Russia)" and a depot for Casey-Mawson flights (**SOURCED**, item 2b).
