## 1 · #26 Iowa 80, Walcott, Iowa

**Claim to test:** "A truck stop large enough to hold a full set of services for people on the road, a small town built entirely for transit (K5, K10)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with one correction.** Iowa 80 does hold a near-complete set of services for long-haul drivers (food 24 hours, showers, laundry, barber, dentist, chiropractor with DOT physicals, chapel service, theater, gym, truck repair, truck wash, certified scale, pet wash, a museum), and it has its own security staff, snow removal and water wells. **Correction:** it is not "a town built entirely for transit." It sits inside, and was annexed by, a real farm town (Walcott, platted 1854, 2020 pop. 1,551) with its own German-heritage civic life, and its workforce is drawn from that town and its neighbors. The truck stop is a single family-owned business, not a settlement: no one is recorded as living there; drivers sleep in their own trucks.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Interstate 80, Exit 284, 755 W. Iowa 80 Road, Walcott, Iowa, just west of Davenport (Quad Cities). Walcott lies in Scott and Muscatine counties. SOURCED (Municipal 2019, https://themunicipal.com/2019/02/worlds-largest-truck-stop-walcott-iowa ; Wikipedia "Walcott, Iowa", read 2026-11-21).
- **Size:** about 220-225 acres owned, of which about 80-90 developed (80 in Municipal 2019; 85 in University of Iowa *Iowa Now*, 2013-02-18, https://now.uiowa.edu/node/25406 ; "over 220 acres" in *Rootstalk*, Grinnell College, https://rootstalk.grinnell.edu/issues/volume-x-issue-1/johnson/). SOURCED; figures vary by source and date.
- **Main building:** 100,000 sq ft in 2017 with a 23,000 sq ft addition under way (WVIK, 2017-11-03, https://www.wvik.org/economy/2017-11-03/worlds-largest-truckstop-growing-again); 130,000 sq ft in Municipal 2019. SOURCED. (Wikipedia's figures, 67,000 and 225,000 sq ft, are internally inconsistent; not used.)
- **Parking:** 900 tractor-trailers, 250 cars, 20 buses. SOURCED (Municipal 2019; HDT 60-years article, https://www.truckinginfo.com/news/iowa-80-truckstop-celebrates-60-years-of-serving-the-professional-driver, 2024).
- **Traffic:** about 5,000 customers a day, 45% of them driving big rigs (Municipal 2019). SOURCED. *Iowa Now* (2013) states "about 64 million customers a year," about 20% long-haul drivers; that figure does not square with 5,000 a day and is recorded as the source states it, not adopted.
- **Staff:** about 500, on three shifts (WVIK 2017; Municipal 2019; *Iowa Now* 2013; *Rootstalk* "over 500"). Wikipedia says 450. SOURCED.
- **What it is / defining feature:** a single, privately owned, 24-hour truck stop that has not closed since opening ("the equivalent of being open more than 473,040 consecutive hours," Municipal 2019; "has never been locked," HDT 2024). SOURCED. Its "World's Largest Truckstop" title began when Standard Oil named it the "World's Largest Amoco" in the 1980s; it has never been verified, and Guinness told the family it would not know how to measure it (*Iowa Now* 2013). SOURCED. [K+: a self-claimed superlative as identity]
- **History (SOURCED unless marked):**
  - I-80 near Walcott: segment from the IA 38 split to US 61 in Davenport opened **1960-12-01**; segment from east of Iowa City to IA 38 completed **1962-08-24** (iowahighways.org I-80 page, read 2026-11-21). The 1964 stop was the first at the Walcott exit on a newly opened stretch that ended at Iowa City (*Iowa Now* 2013).
  - Bill Moon, working for Standard Oil, chose the site before the interstate was finished; **Standard Oil built and opened the stop in (June) 1964**: a small white enamel building with six diesel pumps (HDT 2024) or ten pumps (*Iowa Now*) and a 50-seat restaurant, surrounded by cornfields. Moon took over management in **September 1965**. In **1984** Standard Oil (Amoco) sold it, and Bill and Carolyn Moon bought it, "using everything they had and borrowing from friends" (HDT 2024). Municipal 2019 says they bought it "the following year" (1965); Wikipedia says 1984 without a reference. The 1984 date is the trade press's and the better-supported one.
  - Expansions: a maintenance bay for semis in the 1970s, a gift shop in the 1980s, a food court in the 1990s (*Iowa Now*); "28" (Municipal 2019), "more than 35" (WVIK 2017) or "32" (HDT 2024) expansions and remodels. A $10 million food court and retail expansion began 2017 (WVIK).
  - Bill Moon died in **1992**; the stop became a TravelCenters of America franchise; the second generation (Delia Moon Meier and Will Moon) runs it (Wikipedia; *Iowa Now*).
  - **Owner:** Iowa 80 Group, Inc., Walcott, which also owns truck stops elsewhere (Joplin 44, Missouri; Kenly 95, North Carolina; "11 other truck stops in seven states," Municipal 2019), the Truckomat truck washes and the CAT Scale Company (HQ on the Iowa 80 grounds). UNVERIFIED for the current location count (sources conflict: 11 to 13).

- **The town of Walcott** (Wikipedia "Walcott, Iowa", SOURCED; its census table and civic history are cited there): platted 1854; rail service 1855; named for William Walcott, a Chicago and Rock Island Railroad director, who gave $500 in 1855 for a school on condition the town take his name; incorporated 1894 (pop. 354). Land sold for as little as 75 cents an acre in 1857, drawing German and Scotch-Irish settlers; most Germans came from **Schleswig-Holstein**; by 1910 nearly 90% of Scott County farmland was owned by German immigrants or descendants. The land was earlier inhabited by the Kaskaskia people (Wikipedia, unsourced there; UNVERIFIED). Water system and tower 1897; volunteer fire department by 1897; fire station and city hall 1931 (same building still). A 1911 Dun & Co. report called Walcott the richest town in the U.S. relative to bank deposits per head. Population: 664 (1960), 989 (1970), 1,425 (1980), 1,551 (2020); median age 43.2; 94.2% White, 2.8% Hispanic. Schools: Davenport Community School District (Walcott Elementary, Walcott Intermediate, Davenport West High). **Annexations:** 1962 Highway 6; **1973 the I-80 interchange area**; early 1990s further interchange quadrants, which "significantly" increased valuation. Sister city: Bredenbek, Germany (1997, per Wikipedia; not confirmed elsewhere). [K9, K8]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Being open always and sited at a fixed interstate exit made the stop the place of refuge in the 1978-79 closure, and made its Kitchen the town's only open restaurant on holidays (sources above).
- The founder's habit of asking drivers what they lacked "if we can't get home" (Delia Moon Meier, *Iowa Now*) produced the dentist, barber, chiropractor/DOT physicals, showers and theater: services from a town, but laid out for people who cannot leave their vehicle for long. 
- Federal HOS rules shape what is sold: fast food for 30-minute breaks (WVIK 2017).
- Its scale drew the company into adjacent trades it then founded (CAT Scale 1977, Truckomat), and into civic acts (Jamboree, museum, annexations).

### E. Structural features
- A **private company provides what a town would**: water wells, security, snow removal, a chapel hour, a museum, a festival; while the municipality (pop. ~1,550) supplies annexation, water connection for new building and the volunteer fire department.
- **Work culture split**: local three-shift service workforce, family-run concessions over three generations; transient customers governed by federal clocks.
- **Culture as display**: the Jamboree beauty contest and the showroom make the truck itself the art object.

## 2 · #29 Wall Drug, Wall, South Dakota

**Claim to test:** "A roadside stop that turned passing traffic into a destination in its own right (K8)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, and sharpened.** Wall Drug turned a Depression-era drugstore in a town of 326 into an attraction drawing more than 2 million visitors a year to a town of fewer than 700. The mechanism was a **free give-away (ice water, later 5-cent coffee and free coffee and donuts for the military) announced by hundreds of roadside signs**, then a family program of expansion that turned the stop into a "show business" stage. Two corrections: (1) it never stopped being a real drugstore: its tiny pharmacy is the town's only one and is **cross-subsidized by the tourist shops** (K8 confirmed in a specific form); (2) the business was repeatedly threatened by changes to the road itself (the I-90 bypass of Route 16A in the late 1960s, the 1965 Highway Beautification Act, the 1979 fuel crisis), and its responses to those are part of what it is.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** 510 Main Street, Wall, Pennington County, South Dakota, on the north rim of the Badlands, off I-90 Exit 110; about 54 miles (Encyclopedia of the Great Plains) southeast of Rapid City; Badlands National Park's entrance a few minutes south. SOURCED (walldrug.com/about-us; Encyclopedia of the Great Plains entry by Sheila R. Aaker, BHSU, https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.ii.057.html; SD News Watch 2025-05-26).
- **Size:** a 76,000 sq ft complex with two malls, occupying almost an entire block and most of downtown (EGP; KFF Health News 2024-07-24; Municipal 2018). SOURCED.
- **Visitors:** more than 2 million a year; up to 20,000 a day in summer (walldrug.com; KFF); 16,000-18,000 on busy summer days (Ted H. Hustead, NPS oral history, January 2003, https://www.nps.gov/people/tedhustead.htm). SOURCED.
- **Staff:** about 70 year-round (mostly living in Wall) plus about 120 seasonal (SD News Watch, 2025-05-26, https://www.sdnewswatch.org/south-dakota-tourism-national-parks-badlands-wall-drug/); 185 in summer 2025 including **95 foreign workers on temporary visas** and 20 other seasonal staff (SD News Watch, Bart Pfankuch, 2025-07-14, https://www.sdnewswatch.org/wall-drug-badlands-south-dakota-population-housing/). SOURCED. HR estimate: about 30% J-1, about 10% H-2B, the rest American (search snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Town of Wall:** platted 1907 when the Chicago and North Western Railroad reached it; incorporated 1908; named for the natural "wall" of the Badlands escarpment; Lakota name Makȟóšiča Aglágla Otȟuŋwahe, "Town alongside the Badlands"; elevation 2,815 ft. Census: 167 (1910), 326 (1930), 556 (1950), 786 (1970), 834 (1990), 818 (2000), 766 (2010), **699 (2020)**; Native American share 7.0% (2010). (Wikipedia "Wall, South Dakota", read 2026-11-21.) SOURCED. Census figures in SD News Watch differ slightly (807 for 2000, 774 for 2010, 688 for 2023).
- **Defining feature:** the give-away and the signs. **December 1931**: pharmacist Ted Hustead (graduated 1929) and Dorothy Hustead bought the only drugstore in Wall (pop. 326) with a $3,000 inheritance and gave themselves a five-year trial. **Summer 1936**: Dorothy proposed signs offering free ice water to travelers on the hot highway (Route 16A / US 14-16); Ted and a high-school boy put up signs modeled on Burma-Shave serial signs; cars turned in within days (walldrug.com; EGP). By summer 1937 the store hired eight girls (walldrug.com) or five extra workers (*International Directory of Company Histories* vol. 40, 2001, via fundinguniverse.com). SOURCED. The Husteads chose Wall partly because its Catholic parish (St. Patrick's, founded 1919) offered daily Mass (National Catholic Register, 2022-05-30). SOURCED.

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The give-away made the store a destination; its revenue built the art collection, the chapel, the Backyard and the malls, and keeps the pharmacy alive for ranch families (KFF).
- Its scale created a seasonal labor demand the town of 700 cannot fill, so the company became a landlord and an importer of international summer workers, and the town has a structural housing shortage.
- Its dependence on road traffic made each change to the road (bypass, billboard law, fuel crisis) a crisis, answered each time by more signs and more spectacle.
- The town's tax base and civic amenities (sales tax, foundation-funded pool and golf course, free gym) rest on tourist spending.

### E. Structural features
- A single family firm is the town's largest employer, its main landlord for seasonal staff, its pharmacy, and the host of its winter social events.
- Year-round life runs on cooperatives (power, phones) and pooled local money; summer life runs on visitors and visa workers.
- Surrounding land is mostly federal or tribal: national park, national grassland, Pine Ridge, missile sites.

## 3 · #30 Tucumcari, New Mexico (Route 66 motel strip)

**Claim to test:** "A highway town whose motels and lights existed to serve those passing through (K8, K9)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with two corrections.** (1) The town did not begin as a highway town: it was founded in **1901 as a Rock Island railroad construction camp** and was a railroad division point and transcontinental junction before Route 66 existed; the motel strip was a second layer on a railroad town, and irrigated farming (from 1946) and ranching were a third. (2) The "lights" have a specific local cause: a **local neon sign plant opened in 1939** (*Tucumcari Daily News*, March 1939), the only one in that part of the state. The bypass decline is real: population peaked at 8,419 in 1950 and was 5,278 in 2020, with a fifth of housing units vacant; **I-40's Tucumcari bypass opened in 1981** (best-supported date; the NPS's "late 1960s" refers to I-40 generally). The town now actively preserves its motel and neon heritage with a first-in-the-nation sign ordinance.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Quay County seat, northeastern New Mexico high plains; elevation 4,032 ft; about 180 miles east of Albuquerque and 110 west of Amarillo (Rogers, *Tucumcari Project*, Bureau of Reclamation, 2009/2013, saved PDF read); "60 miles from the nearest significant town" (Route 66 News, Ron Warnick, 2022-02-11, https://route66news.com/?p=41397). SOURCED.
- **Population** (Wikipedia "Tucumcari, New Mexico", census table, read 2026-11-21, SOURCED): 2,526 (1910); 4,143 (1930); 6,194 (1940); **8,419 (1950, peak)**; 8,143 (1960); 7,189 (1970); 6,765 (1980); 6,831 (1990); 5,989 (2000); 5,363 (2010); **5,278 (2020)**. 2020: **57.5% Hispanic or Latino** (51.41% in 2000); median age 41.2; 2,304 households, 37.2% one person; 2,895 housing units, **20.4% vacant**. Route 66 News notes the population never passed 9,000.
- **Founding:** 1901 construction camp of the Chicago, Rock Island and Pacific; called Ragtown, then Six Shooter Siding; briefly Douglas; renamed Tucumcari in 1908 after Tucumcari Mountain; name likely from a Comanche word for lying in wait (Comanche used the mesa as a lookout); the "Tocom and Kari" Apache love-story etymology is described as "historically suspect" (Rogers 2009; Wikipedia). Quay County created 1903 from Guadalupe County. SOURCED.
- **Defining feature:** the Route 66 motel and neon strip (Route 66 Boulevard; named Gaynell Avenue before 1970 and Tucumcari Boulevard 1970-2003, Wikipedia). Billboards "TUCUMCARI TONITE!" along the highway invited travelers to the town's "2000" motel rooms, later changed to "1200" (Wikipedia, snippet of the history section; UNVERIFIED for the exact numbers' dates). The slogan was dropped for "Gateway to the West," then restored on **2008-06-24** by the **Lodgers' Tax Advisory Board**, which runs the billboards (Wikipedia/snippet, UNVERIFIED for the date). About 25 neon signs remain on Route 66 in various states (Route 66 News 2022, SOURCED).

### B. What else exists there
- **Railroad:** Tucumcari became a division point (crew changes, servicing) and the transfer point where the Rock Island and Southern Pacific pooled transcontinental trains (the Golden State; the Choctaw Rocket); the 1901 link between the SP at El Paso and the Rock Island at Liberal, Kansas put it on a transcontinental line; depot built 1926 (Mission Revival), passenger service ended 1968; Union Pacific gave the depot to the city in 2002; the city and Tucumcari MainStreet Corp. spent $1.8 million restoring it; it now houses the **Tucumcari Railroad Museum** (Union Pacific "Train Town" page, SOURCED; Wikipedia, SOURCED). By 1910 the town had a roundhouse, depot and water tower (snippet, UNVERIFIED). The Rock Island was ordered liquidated in 1980 (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Irrigation (Tucumcari Project):** Conchas Dam (Army Corps of Engineers, completed 1939, $15.8 million per a snippet) feeds the Conchas and Hudson canals (110 miles combined). The project was "a work relief project during the Great Depression and an emergency food project during the Second World War," built partly with WPA labor; authorized for about 45,000 acres but never watered more than about 34,000 (7,000 acres in 1946; 33,616 in 1955) because of drought and canal seepage. The **Arch Hurley Conservancy District** took over operation in 1954. Settlers came as "land-hungry families" (*Reclamation Era*, 1947), some from Oklahoma; some grew cotton unsuited to the climate; many lacked irrigation experience or capital, and loan agencies hesitated. Since drought began in 2002, about half the project water is lost to seepage; farmers shifted to sprinklers and mostly alfalfa for livestock feed; the district's 2007 crop census reported **14,759 acres irrigated** (Rogers, *Tucumcari Project*, USBR 2009, ed. Gahan 2013, https://www.usbr.gov/history/ProjectHistories/Tucumcari%20Project%20D2.pdf, saved PDF read; SOURCED). [K1, K9]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Distance (60 miles to the next town) made it a natural overnight stop; the railroad and then Route 66 brought the traffic; a local neon plant (1939) made lit signs cheap to get, which produced the "neon strip."
- The motel economy created the town's main civic money machine (lodgers' tax), which in turn funds festivals meant to fill motel beds.
- The bypass turned the strip into heritage: preservation grants, a museum, a sign ordinance and a Route 66 identity now sustain it, while abandoned motels became targets for arson.

### E. Structural features
- Three overlapping economies, each with its own institution: railroad (division point, now museum), irrigated agriculture and ranching (Arch Hurley district), road tourism (lodgers' tax board, MainStreet).
- Large public-sector share (21% of jobs) and farm share (13.6%) under the visible tourist strip.

## 4 · #67 Michi-no-eki (道の駅, Japanese roadside stations)

**Claim to test:** "Roadside stations combining rest, local markets and road information, which made stops into local destinations (K8, K5)." Care: verify the number of stations and when the program began.
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, and the government says so itself.** The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) defines three functions: **rest** (24-hour free parking and toilets), **information** (road, tourist and emergency-medical information) and **regional cooperation** (local promotion facilities). MLIT's own staging names the shift the claim describes: **Stage 1 (from 1993) "a place serving passing road users"; Stage 2 (from 2013) "the michi-no-eki itself as the destination"; Stage 3 (2020-2025) "a base to accelerate regional revitalization and tourism."** Additions to the claim: they have also become **disaster bases** (39 designated "disaster-prevention michi-no-eki" from 2021; used as relief and road-clearing bases after the 2024 Noto earthquake) and **community centers for aging rural towns** (day care, bus hubs, meal delivery). They can also fail: about 30% are said to run deficits and seven have been deregistered, including one killed by a parallel toll-free expressway.
- **Count (verified):** **1,234 stations as of 2026-09-04 (65th registration)** (MLIT list page, https://www.mlit.go.jp/road/Michi-no-Eki/list.html, read 2026-11-21, SOURCED). Earlier: 1,231 after the 64th registration on 2025-12-19 (Trafficnews, 2025-12-26, SOURCED); 1,230 on 2025-06-13 (Kinki bureau, snippet).
- **Start (verified, with nuance):** the idea was proposed at a 1990 regional-development meeting in the Chūgoku region by a Yamaguchi participant ("why not have a station on the road?"; MLIT credits Sakamoto Kazuaki [坂本多旦] of the Funakata Farm group, snippet); **social experiments at 12 sites in Yamaguchi, Gifu and Tochigi prefectures in 1991** (October 1991 per English Wikipedia; Chūgoku Regional Development Bureau document, snippet); registration and signage system created by the national government in February 1992/1993 (sources differ); **first registration of 103 stations on 1993-04-22** (snippets of MLIT/Yamaguchi documents; MLIT's 2024 document confirms "1993 system creation" and 103 stations in 1993; SOURCED). Yamaguchi Prefecture calls itself the "birthplace of michi-no-eki"; the station at Abu town (阿武町) opened as an experimental facility in 1991 (snippets, UNVERIFIED).

### A. Snapshot (the tradition)
- **What it is:** a government-registered public facility on an ordinary (non-expressway) road, set up by a municipality or a body that can stand in for it (a prefecture; a corporation at least one-third owned by local government; a public-interest corporation the municipality recommends; or a corporation leasing municipal land and buildings under a management agreement); registered by MLIT on the mayor's application; built either jointly with the road administrator ("integrated type") or by the municipality alone ("standalone type") (MLIT outline page, https://www.mlit.go.jp/road/Michi-no-Eki/outline.html, SOURCED). **Registration requirements:** free 24-hour parking of sufficient capacity, clean toilets (Western-style in principle), child-care facilities (baby corner), road and regional information, regional promotion facilities, and barrier-free routes (MLIT outline, SOURCED); a 2006 MLIT release gave roughly 20 or more parking spaces and about 10 or more flush toilets (snippet). Basic concept: "a lively place with individuality, built together with the region"; in disasters it takes on a disaster-prevention function (MLIT 2024, SOURCED).
- **Growth** (MLIT Road Bureau, *Status of Stage 3 Efforts*, April 2024, saved PDF read, SOURCED): 103 (1993), 701 (2003), 1,005 (2013), 1,204 (2023); municipalities with a station: 90 (1993), 527, 706, 827 (2023), i.e., about half of all municipalities; about half the stations contain public road land. Within 30 minutes' drive of a station live about 77% of Japan's population (97.06 million); within 60 minutes 99.3% (125 million). National average 1.07 stations per 100 km of national and main local roads, far lower in Tokyo, Kanagawa, Osaka and Aichi. Facilities within stations (2023): municipal office branches (14), hospitals or clinics (8), lodging (71), hot springs (112), footbaths (89), campgrounds (63), experience farms (36), overnight parking (33).
- **Scale of trade:** in 2015, 210 million visitors and ¥210 billion in sales (Nippon.com, Matsuo Ryūsaku, 2019-09-27, SOURCED); a different private source says about 240 million visitors and about ¥250 billion (snippet, UNVERIFIED). MLIT's primary figure: NOT FOUND.

Shippers' councils at farmers' markets (Karari). Local governments use stations for the problems of depopulation and aging (day care, bus hubs). [K1] [SM: competence under pressure]
- **[K2] Festivals and customs:** anniversary festivals (周年祭) and harvest festivals keyed to the new rice: e.g., Kisarazu Umakuta no Sato's 5th anniversary with new Koshihikari rice and red-and-white mochi given out (2022); Ōki (Fukuoka) "autumn harvest festival" in November 2024 tied to the town's 70th anniversary; Rice Land Fukagawa (Hokkaido) annual "great harvest festival" with Fukagawa new rice (October 4-5, 2025); Imari (Saga) renewal anniversary run by the local agricultural co-op (March 2026); Kurukuru Naruto (Tokushima) sweet-potato and new-rice festival (October 2026) (event-site snippets, UNVERIFIED). **Stamp rallies:** each regional council runs a booklet stamp rally, with about 10,000 finishers (by region) a year, described as having "taken root as michi-no-eki culture"; a national "conquest" award requires finisher certificates from all nine blocks (FY2026 deadline 2027-02-28); a national digital stamp trial began July 2026 with Autobacs Seven, for three years (council PDF and press-release snippets; partly UNVERIFIED). Overnight car-campers versus locals and operators is the main friction (K6). [K4, K10]

- **[K6] Rules of everyday behavior:** **Napping is allowed; staying the night is "basically declined."** MLIT's official answer: "since michi-no-eki are rest facilities that drivers can use 24 hours a day to prevent traffic accidents, it is fine to take a nap in the car to recover from fatigue during the drive. However, overnight use of public spaces such as parking lots is basically declined." ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Making rest facilities a registered national network gave municipalities a reason and a template to build a market and information center at the roadside; the 24-hour rest requirement created the car-camping problem and the nap/stay rule; the free toilets and parking made stations natural disaster bases.
- Turning the stop into a destination (Stage 2) depended on local produce and processing, which tied farmers (often elderly) into a direct-sales economy.
- Dependence on road traffic means a new parallel road can kill a station (Marusep).

### E. Structural features
- Public-private hybrid: municipal ownership, MLIT registration, designated managers or third-sector companies, farmers as shippers.
- Exported as a development model: a 2004 World Bank report said michi-no-eki "have great potential as a tool for reducing poverty" (Japan Times 2020, quoted in a snippet; page 403); JICA studies in Vietnam (2009 master plan; national standard 2011; about 25 stations), Thailand (about 19, community-run), Indonesia (Michi-no-Eki Pakewa, Tomohon, North Sulawesi, opened 2023-05-26) (MLIT/JICA snippets, UNVERIFIED).

## 5 · #69 Korean expressway rest areas (고속도로 휴게소)

**Claim to test:** "Rest stops known for their own foods, so the stop itself becomes a reason to travel (K12)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED in part, with an important correction.** Korean expressway rest areas are known for food, and the Korea Expressway Corporation (KEC, 한국도로공사) has deliberately built that reputation (annual signature-dish selection since 2015; 155 rest areas hosting branches of famous local and century-old restaurants by 2024); individual menus draw people who go there on purpose. **But** the dominant public view of rest-area food, stated by the Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport himself, is that it is "expensive and not tasty," and the reason is structural: a multi-layer lease system in which middle operators took an average of about 33% (up to 51%) of tenants' sales, with long monopolies, including subsidiaries of an association of KEC retirees. In 2026 the government began replacing that system with direct contracts. The "destination" quality is real but uneven, and it rests on a contested business structure.

### A. Snapshot (the tradition)
- **Count:** 211 rest areas as of October 2025, 194 of them leased (search summary of 2025 reporting, UNVERIFIED); "208 in total last year, 205 outsourced and 3 directly run" (earlier article, snippet); **236 nationally in a 2024 MOLIT survey (incl. private expressways), 100 (42.4%) over 20 years old** (snippet). Counts differ by date and by whether private-capital expressways are included; an official KEC table was NOT FOUND.
- **Origins:** the Gyeongbu Expressway (Seoul-Busan) was begun 1968-02-01 and fully opened 1970-07-07 (National Archives theme site, snippet; Newsis 2020). Expressway rest areas began in **1970 with 4 simple rest areas** and grew to 135 by 2008 (최윤혁·백승걸, KEC Road and Transport Research Institute, *Journal of the Korean Society of Transportation* 27(6), Dec. 2009, saved PDF read; the numerals were partly lost in extraction, so the "4" is from the search snippet: partly SOURCED). **Chupungnyeong rest area** (추풍령휴게소), at the midpoint of the 428 km Seoul-Busan route (214 km from each end), on the Baekdudaegan ridge at the Gimcheon/Yeongdong border, is commonly called Korea's first; it opened in 1970 (Maeil Shinmun, 이홍섭, 2011-07-14, SOURCED for "first" claim and setting; opening month from snippets). It once had a small zoo (National Archives photo caption, SOURCED) and a cloud bridge linking its two sides, removed when the road was widened. Counts: 99 (1999), 115 (2002), 141 (2004); sales ₩472.8 billion (1999), ₩668.1 billion (2002), ₩662.1 billion (2004) (Weekly Kyunghyang, 최성진, 2005-09-27, SOURCED).
- **Scale of trade:** total rest-area sales **₩1.4456 trillion in 2023** (up 14% from ₩1.2677 trillion in 2022); restaurant sales ₩400.4 billion (up 26.7%) (Seoul Shinmun, 2024-03-20, SOURCED).
- **How it is run:** on KEC-built ("financed") expressways, KEC builds rest areas and leases them to private operators, who sublet shops to tenants; private-capital (민자) expressways have their own rest areas. Middle operators' fees: average 33%, maximum 51% of tenant sales; 53 leased rest areas had the same operator for 20+ years, 11 for 40+ years (snippets of 2026 coverage). A government audit found an association of former KEC employees (도성회) ran rest areas through subsidiaries, some for about 40 years; its chairmanship was passed among former KEC presidents (Newsis 2026-07-09, SOURCED; chairmanship detail from snippet).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- A closed expressway system with KEC as sole landlord created captive customers, which allowed high rents and middle-operator fees, which produced the "expensive and not tasty" reputation; the destination-food reputation grew from KEC's deliberate counter-programs (signature dishes, famous-restaurant branches) and from a few standout sites (Deokpyeong, Haengdamdo).
- National holiday migrations make rest areas a seasonal national ritual space, with toll waivers and best-seller lists.

### E. Structural features
- State-owned infrastructure company + private operators + small tenants; retired officials' networks in the middle; reform underway in 2026.
- Food linked to place names (Cheonan walnut cakes) whose origins go back to colonial-era railway station vendors.

## 6 · #122 Ekiben (駅弁, railway station lunch boxes)

**Claim to test:** "Each station sells its own boxed meal made from local foods, so a stop in transit becomes a reason to travel (K12)." Care: food lead only; the "first ekiben" claim is disputed.
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED historically, and qualified for the present.** Ekiben began in the 1880s as plain rice balls sold at railway starting points; from the late Meiji era stations developed regional specialties (Shizuoka sea-bream rice, Toyama trout sushi, Miyajimaguchi conger-eel rice), and from the 1950s-60s "local" ekiben became a reason to travel and a souvenir. **But** the station-bound trade has shrunk sharply: faster trains, shorter stops, sealed windows, the end of many on-board sales and convenience-store competition cut the number of member vendors from about 400 in the late 1960s (405 in 1967) to **about 83 on 2024-03-31**. Much of the trade now happens away from the station: at department-store ekiben fairs (Keiō in Shinjuku since 1966), big station shops (Tokyo Station), mail order and frozen exports. The "reason to travel" now often runs in reverse: the ekiben travels to the buyer.

### A. Snapshot (the tradition)
- **What it is:** a boxed meal sold at or near a railway station, typically made by a licensed station-concession vendor. There is no legal definition; the "ekiben mark" (set in 1988 by the Japan Railway Station Concession Business Central Association, 日本鉄道構内営業中央会) may be used only by member vendors, without menu or quantity standards (Mynavi/Trafficnews snippets, UNVERIFIED). In the Meiji era, the menu, price, sales method, and even the number and dress of vendors needed railway approval (nlab snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Origin (disputed; reported as competing claims):**
  - **Utsunomiya, 1885-07-16** (the day the Ōmiya-Utsunomiya line opened): the inn **Shirakiya** sold two rice balls with black sesame and pickled plum plus takuan pickles, wrapped in bamboo sheath, for 5 sen. This is the "standard theory," also presented by JR East (Trafficnews, 2025, "駅弁140年史," p.1, SOURCED; JR East page, snippet). Shirakiya's owner was reportedly reluctant because the area was undeveloped and trains few; some accounts say a Nippon Railway executive staying at the inn persuaded him (Trafficnews SOURCED; executive account snippet). Shirakiya's claim rests on oral history because its records were destroyed in WWII air raids (English-language snippet, UNVERIFIED). Utsunomiya was briefly a starting point while passengers crossed the Tone River by ferry, so a meal service was needed (Trafficnews, SOURCED). Today Matsunoya (founded 1893) is the only ekiben maker left at Utsunomiya Station, which once had three; its president says no old photos or records survive (nlab snippet).
  - **Competing claims:** Kobe or Osaka (Umeda) 1877/1878 (sold to soldiers going to the Satsuma Rebellion, per one account); Nagahama 1882; Ueno or Kumagaya 1883 (an 1883 Nippon Railway record); Tsuruga at the Hokuriku line's extension; Takasaki and Oyama also claimed (Trafficnews SOURCED for Kobe 1878, Ueno 1883, Tsuruga; others from AtHome/nlab snippets). Trafficnews concludes supply most likely began "simultaneously in many places"; all the claimed sites were starting stations. A Japanese wiki corpus states the Utsunomiya theory is "effectively denied" by later research (snippet). **Not resolved; both the standard theory and the challenges are recorded here.**
  - **Makunouchi-style box (rice plus many side dishes):** Himeji Station, by **Maneki** (まねき, now Maneki Shokuhin): 1888 at the Sanyō Railway's Hyōgo-Himeji opening (Trafficnews, SOURCED: grilled sea bream, rolled omelet, grilled kamaboko, rice with black sesame; the railway asked for meals and tea for passengers), or 1889 (Kobe Shimbun 2021, SOURCED: rice and 13 side dishes, the association's "origin of the makunouchi ekiben"; Maneki founded 1888 by Takeda Mokuhachi, snippet), or 1890 (another snippet). Dates conflict.
  - **Commemorative days:** **"Ekiben Day," April 10**, set in 1993 by the Central Association (the character 弁 resembles "4" and "十," and 当 is read "tō," ten; spring travel season); registered by the Japan Anniversary Association in 2024 (snippets). **July 16 "Ekiben Memorial Day"** (Utsunomiya), with JR East events at Utsunomiya Station (2022) (snippets; one source says MLIT set it in 2001: UNVERIFIED).
- **Scale and decline (SOURCED unless marked):** association members **405 (1967) → about 83 (2024-03-31)** (MAFF export plan by "EKIBEN WORLD TEAM," saved PDF read); 349 at the 1987 JNR privatization → 86 (Tōyō Keizai snippet); 87 in late January 2021, about one-fifth of the late-1960s figure (Kobe Shimbun, 2021-03-09, SOURCED); "about 20% of the peak of about 400 in the 1960s" (Hokkaido Shimbun, 2025-12-30, lead only).

### B. What else exists there (three documented makers and their stations, plus others)
- **Ogino-ya, "Tōge no Kamameshi" (峠の釜めし), Yokokawa Station, Gunma** (Shin'etsu Line, foot of the Usui Pass): Ogino-ya began selling rice balls at Yokokawa in 1885; the kamameshi (rice cooked with toppings in a small Mashiko-ware earthenware pot) was devised by fourth-generation head **Takamizawa Mineji** in **1957** (Bunshun Online, 坂崎仁紀, 2022-07-05, SOURCED) or **1958** (several snippets) because passengers wanted a warm ekiben; the pots reportedly came from a Mashiko pottery peddler whose unsold small pots the company bought up (snippet). Around 1975, passengers crowded the vendors and bought through windows while helper locomotives were coupled for the Usui climb; eating it while watching the pass was customary (Bunshun, SOURCED). In 1962 it opened a drive-in on National Route 18 (snippet). On **1997-10-01** the Hokuriku (Nagano) Shinkansen opened and the Yokokawa-Karuizawa line was abolished, cutting Yokokawa's traffic; Ogino-ya moved into Shinkansen on-board sales and Tokyo Station (Bunshun, SOURCED); most sales now come from other stations, drive-ins and fairs (snippet). The pot maker later went into restructuring; Ogino-ya said the kamameshi would continue (Trafficnews headline snippet). [K9, K12]
- **Abe Shōten, "Ikameshi" (いかめし), Mori Station, Hokkaido** (Hakodate Main Line): squid stuffed with glutinous and regular rice and simmered; first sold **1941**; maker has sold on site at the Keiō fair since the first one (1966) and was the fair's top seller **50 times in a row from 1971**, entering its hall of fame in 2021; now run by the third generation (Trafficnews, 2022, SOURCED). Created in wartime food shortages: squid abundant in Uchiura Bay, rice-saving was encouraged, and it was filling (Trafficnews p.2 snippet, UNVERIFIED as read). After expresses stopped calling at Mori, station sales collapsed, and sales moved to department-store fairs (snippet). During COVID-19, fair overstock forced deep discounts (Trafficnews 2020-05-23, SOURCED). [K9, K12]
- **Kiyōken, "Shiumai Bentō" (シウマイ弁当), Yokohama** (roots in the concession at the original Yokohama station of Japan's first railway, 1872; business from 1908; shiumai completed 1928): launched **1954-04-01** at ¥100 when coffee cost ¥50; its first president was a former Yokohama stationmaster who developed it with Chinatown cooks to strengthen local character; still packed in thin-wood (kyōgi) boxes to keep the rice's moisture; about 30,000 made a day (2024) (Walker+, All About, Getnavi snippets; UNVERIFIED as read); can make up to 63,000 meals a day at three plants (Trafficnews 2019, SOURCED). [K12, K14]
- **Others (SOURCED unless marked):** late-Meiji regional ekiben: Shizuoka "tai-meshi," Yamakita "ayu-zushi," Ōfuna sandwiches, Shizuoka "Western bentō," Tsuchiura eel rice, Toyohashi eel rice and inari-zushi, Toyama trout sushi, Miyajimaguchi anago-meshi (Trafficnews 2025). **Tōchikuken "kashiwa-meshi," Orio Station, Kitakyūshū** (rice cooked in chicken broth with chicken, egg and seaweed): platform vending since the Taishō era, revived by one salesman who joined at 51 and became the "oldest new salesman" at 53, selling about 30 boxes a day from a wooden box hung from his neck, 08:00-16:00, closed Wednesdays (President Online, 内藤慧, 2020-05-05, SOURCED); described elsewhere as the only platform vending left on regular trains (snippet). Hitoyoshi Station (Kumamoto) had a vendor for half a century (snippet, status unknown). **Gyūniku Domannaka** (Yonezawa, Shinkineya, launched with the Yamagata Shinkansen) is the top seller at **Ekiben-ya Matsuri**, Tokyo Station's ekiben shop, which sells about 10,000-15,000 a day from about 150-200 varieties (record about 22,000) (snippets). Hiroshima Ekiben supplied 48,000 meals a day during the 1994 Asian Games (Trafficnews 2019, SOURCED).
- **Business loss and succession (Trafficnews, 宮武和多哉, 2019-05-19, SOURCED):** Shinagawa's Tokiwaken was bought by a conference-hall company and left the bentō business; Kuroiso's Futaba Shokuhin halted ekiben after its ice dessert became a hit; Matsuyama's "shōyu-meshi" ended when its maker closed and was revived by another maker; Kuroiso's "Kyūbi-zushi" returned at expressway service areas and Utsunomiya station building; Hamamatsu's Jishōtei runs burger franchises to keep its ekiben going. On-board sales on many JR limited expresses and Shinkansen were cut sharply in February-March 2019. Causes named: convenience stores, aging owners, lack of successors.
- **COVID-19 (Trafficnews, 2020-05-23, SOURCED):** Kanaya (Oshamambe) about 4,000 tour and meal reservations canceled; Kamio Bentōbu (Niitsu) "station sales zero"; Maneki (Himeji) zero tour sales in April and May; Arataké (Matsusaka) lost tour-train orders. Vendors turned to mail order, drive-through and frozen ekiben.
- **Workforce:** hand-plating of side dishes in factories (JR Central Passengers' Tokyo plant, snippet); vendors cite labor and ingredient costs as the main pressure (snippet). Start times and worker ages: NOT FOUND.

### C. Cultural locations (named)
- **Keiō Department Store "Genso Yūmei Ekiben to Zenkoku Umaimono Taikai"** (Shinjuku; 1st in February 1966; 61st opened 2026-01-06 with 380+ ekiben; about 300,000 ekiben and ¥613 million in 13 days around 2017) (snippets; the first department-store ekiben fair is attributed to Takashimaya Osaka in 1953). **Ekiben-ya Matsuri** at Tokyo Station. **JR Kyūshū Ekiben Grand Prix** (since 2004) and **JR East "Ekiben Aji no Jin"** (snippets).
- **Ekiben wrappers (掛け紙) in museums:** the **Fukuoka City Museum** collection holds wartime wrappers: an Okayama Station "tea-included bentō" wrapper urging "let us all cooperate in saving rice," printed diagonally with the Sino-Japanese War slogans "Building a New Order in East Asia," "Japan-Manchukuo-China Cooperation" and "Perseverance and Endurance" (jpsearch item fcm_db-310580, SOURCED); other wrappers (Osaka, Nagoya, Onomichi) call on workers and women to support the war, urge savings targets, show soldiers with comfort bags, and ask that rail capacity be reserved for the war (jpsearch snippets). Izumi Kazuo, *Ekiben Kakegami no Tabi* (Kōtsū Shimbunsha Shinsho, 2017), on prewar wrappers (snippet). [K13, K9]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Long stops at particular stations created a market for food at exactly those stations, which became their identity (Yokokawa, Mori, Orio); when the stops ended, the identity moved into the box itself and traveled to fairs and shops.
- Regional ingredients and the competition among stations produced the "local specialty" ekiben, and later the department-store fair as a national tasting circuit.

### E. Structural features
- A licensed concession system tied food vendors to railway companies (JNR, then JR); privatization in 1987 coincided with continued decline.
- The trade's survival now depends on off-station channels (fairs, mail order, frozen exports).

## FINAL

**Finished:** 2026-11-21. All six picks researched at Round 1 depth (A to F, K1-K14).

### Claims tested, in one line each
1. **Iowa 80:** confirmed as a full service set for drivers (food, showers, dentist, chiropractor with DOT physicals, chapel hour, theater, repair, scale, museum; its own wells, security and snow removal), corrected: it is one family business annexed into a real farm town (Walcott, pop. 1,551), not a town; nobody lives there.
2. **Wall Drug:** confirmed and sharpened: a give-away (free ice water, 1936) announced by hundreds of signs made a Depression drugstore a 2-million-visitor stop; its tourist shops cross-subsidize the town's only pharmacy; the town of about 700 depends on its sales tax and on about 95 visa workers a summer housed by the company.
3. **Tucumcari:** confirmed, corrected: a 1901 railroad town before it was a Route 66 motel town; neon came from a local sign plant (1939); I-40 bypassed it in 1981; population fell from 8,419 (1950) to 5,278 (2020); heritage preservation now includes a 2020 landmark-sign ordinance.
4. **Michi-no-eki:** confirmed by MLIT's own staging (1993 rest stops → 2013 "the station itself as destination" → 2020 regional-revitalization bases); verified count **1,234 on 2026-09-04**; experiments 1991, first 103 registered 1993-04-22; also disaster bases (Noto 2024) and community centers; seven deregistrations.
5. **Korean expressway rest areas:** food reputation confirmed and deliberately built by KEC, but the prevailing view is "expensive and not tasty" because of a multi-layer lease system (middle-operator fees about 33%, up to 51%), now being replaced (2026 direct-contract reform).
6. **Ekiben:** confirmed historically (local specialties from late Meiji); the first-ekiben claim is disputed (Utsunomiya 1885 standard theory vs. Kobe/Osaka 1877-78, Nagahama 1882, Ueno/Kumagaya 1883, Tsuruga); vendors fell from 405 (1967) to about 83 (2024); trade has moved to fairs, big-station shops, mail order and exports.
