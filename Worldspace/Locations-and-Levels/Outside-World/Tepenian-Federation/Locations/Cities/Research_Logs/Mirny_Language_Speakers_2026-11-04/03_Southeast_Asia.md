**Reading the figures.** "Speaker" is the developer's definition: native plus fluent second-language speakers who could conduct business, social talk and general dealings in the language. Indonesia's census and survey threshold for "can speak Indonesian" is lower than that (BPS: the person *understands what others say in Indonesian and can say words others understand*), so the census figure is used as the HIGH end and the BEST is set slightly below it. Every LOW/BEST/HIGH that is not a source number is my reasoning from the sources named, and says so in the note.

## Gaps and conflicts

- **Indonesian ability, national: 97.96 % vs 97.24 % vs 98.12 %.** The BPS long-form table gives 97.96 % (my computation from its counts, aged 5+, 2022 data); Databoks reports 97.24 % from the same long form but its base or measure is unclear; SUPAS 2025 gives 98.12 % (aged 5+). The differences are small and BEST is set below all three because the census bar ("understand and be understood") is lower than the developer's.
- **West Papua Indonesian ability is the weakest of the census cells.** 96.62 % for Papua province seems high for a region where the highlands are overwhelmingly Papuan with low schooling; I could not reach the 2010 provincial table (sensus.bps.go.id returned 404 / aborted) or a regency-level figure to check it. LOW is set at 88 % on reasoning, not data.
- **Javanese and Sundanese: denominator of the 2020 figures.** detik.com gives Javanese 42.12 % and Sundanese 18.40 % "of people over 5"; these cannot be shares of all people (the top ten sum to 82.7 %, above the 73.87 % who use any regional language), so I treated them as shares of regional-language users. If that reading is wrong, the 2020 numbers are unusable and the 2010 census figures (31.79 %, 15.14 %) stand alone.
- **English, Indonesia: 30.8 % (Wikipedia list, citing a company blog) vs at most 6.28 % (BPS SUPAS 2025, all foreign languages).** BPS used; the blog figure rejected.
- **English, Thailand: 27.16 % (Crystal 2003, expert estimate of any competence) vs "very low" EF band and about 0.5 % at home (2010 census).** No population survey found; BEST 10 % is a judgment, low confidence.
- **English, Vietnam: no population-share source exists in what I could reach.** The only "percentage" (53.81 %) is an EF EPI score misreported as a share.
- **Mandarin, Thailand: 8.8 million "native Chinese speakers" (WorldData via seasia.co) vs 111,866 Chinese home speakers (2010 census).** The first counts all Chinese varieties and probably ethnic heritage; the census counts home use. Neither measures Mandarin; Mandarin is placed at about 1.5 %, under the 2 % line.
- **Thai ability in Patani: no measured figure.** The RILCA 2007 survey (1,255 residents) holds proficiency data but its PDF returned 403; the ANU (Suwilai 2012) PDF was unreadable. The 60–90 % range is reasoning.
- **Patani Malay share: 2000 census is the latest provincial figure found;** the 2010 NSO provincial reports are scanned images.
- **Acehnese: the 60–65 % home-use figure** rests on a recalled 2010 provincial population used as the denominator; the ethnic figure (71.72 %) and the academic 56.7 % bracket it.
- **Javanese in West Papua is borderline (about 8–10 % by ethnicity, my estimate):** Papua Barat alone is 14.64 % Javanese (2017), but no Papua-province figure was found.
- **Several "recalled, not verified" items are flagged in the table** (Chinese ethnic share in Indonesia, Aceh's 2010 population used as a denominator, size of Vietnam's largest minorities and of the Hoa). They only bound negligible or HIGH cells.
