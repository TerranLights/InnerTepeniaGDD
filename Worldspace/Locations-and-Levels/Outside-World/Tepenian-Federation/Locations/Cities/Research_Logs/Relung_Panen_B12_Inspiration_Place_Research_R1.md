## 1 · Ord River Scheme and Kununurra, Western Australia

**Claim to test:** "A remote irrigation region built ahead of its market, long argued for and against (K9)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and sharpened.** The scheme was built on state and Commonwealth money for crops whose markets repeatedly failed (cotton in the 1960s, sugar 1995–2007); critics in 2013–2016 put its return far below cost, and the 2016 WA Auditor General found the latest expansion had no detailed costings and had not delivered the expected benefits. The scheme still runs and is still argued over. Note for accuracy: the "built ahead of its market" framing is the critics' framing; supporters (the WA government in 2016) pointed to visible town growth. Both are recorded below.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** East Kimberley, far north of Western Australia, at the confluence of the Ord and Dunham rivers; about 45 km from the Northern Territory border; 834 km from Darwin; 3,214 km from Perth by the Great Northern Highway; nearest town Wyndham, 100 km. Elevation 47 m. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Kununurra, Western Australia" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Population:** 5,494 (2021 census; urban centre 4,515); 24.9% Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander; 67.1% born in Australia. Described as transient: an estimate of over 7,000 (2006) if itinerant residents, outlying farms and communities were counted, and around 10,000 in the dry season (April–September) with tourists and itinerant farm workers. **SOURCED** — same page.
- **What it is:** a town founded **1961** (gazetted 10 February 1961; post office opened 1 September 1960) on land resumed from Ivanhoe Station pastoral lease, **specifically to service the Ord River Irrigation Scheme**. Name from the Miriwoong word for "river" (Goonoonoorrang), with a "K" added to match other East Kimberley names. **SOURCED** — same page.
- **Defining feature:** a state-built irrigation district fed by two dams. **Stage 1:** the Diversion Dam at Bandicoot Bar, started late 1960, opened by Prime Minister Menzies 20 July 1963; it holds Lake Kununurra, which gravity-feeds the irrigation area through the Main Channel. **Stage 2:** the Ord River Main Dam, 55 km upstream, work from 1968/1969 (sources differ), opened by Prime Minister McMahon 30 June 1972; it holds **Lake Argyle** (741 km² per the Ord River article) and flooded the Argyle Downs homestead. A 36 MW hydroelectric station was added 1995–96. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Kununurra" and "Ord River" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Climate:** semi-arid bordering tropical savanna; dry season April–September (no August rainfall over 5 mm in 53 years of records); October–December a hot, humid pre-monsoon period with storms; monsoon January–March with heavy rain and road closures. Mean daily maximum 35.1 °C; record high 45.3 °C. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Kununurra".

### B. What else exists there
**Origins of the scheme** [K1, K8, P8]
- The idea grew out of a drought hitting Kimberley cattle stations; Minister Frank Wise proposed experimental areas on Ivanhoe Station in 1937; the Carlton Reach Research Station was set up 1941 by Kimberley Michael Durack for the WA Department of Agriculture; it moved downriver in 1945/46 as the **Kimberley Research Station**, a joint CSIRO–state facility (later the Frank Wise Institute of Tropical Agriculture). In 1951 its committee recommended sugar and rice as the cash crops that could justify a dam; by 1953, 150 rice varieties were under test. In August 1959 the Commonwealth granted WA £5 million, mainly for the scheme. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Ord River" and "Kununurra".

**Crops over time — a record of failures and switches** [K9, P8]
- Rice on the pilot farm from October 1960. First farmers received plots in **1964** (UNVERIFIED — search snippet only); 31 farms by 1966 (**SOURCED**, Wikipedia). Stage 1 farms (about thirty) grew mainly **cotton** 1962–66; the cotton caterpillar *Helicoverpa armigera* became pesticide-resistant, and low yields plus falling world prices led to suspension of the commercial cotton industry. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Ord River".
- **Sugar:** the Ord sugar mill was built by CSR and opened 1995; it was later sold to growers (Ord River Canegrowers Pty Ltd) with a $4 million state loan; the 2007 crush was the last. Low world sugar prices, shrinking production and uncertainty about expansion were the reasons given; sugar had been the dominant crop, and its end was "a devastating blow." **UNVERIFIED** (snippet from the WA Department of Agriculture page; the page itself returned **404** when opened). The 1995–2007 dates are **SOURCED** from Wikipedia "Ord River" ("late 1990s until 2007").
- Current: melons, mangoes, pumpkins, chia, and **Indian sandalwood** — "the largest commercial Indian Sandalwood plantations in the world"; more than 60 different crops grown by 2009. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Ord River" and "Kununurra". MG Corporation began growing cotton again in 2020. **SOURCED** — mgcorp.com.au "Our History".

**The arguments for and against** [K9, K8]
- 2013: the Wilderness Society estimated $1.45 billion spent for a return of 17 cents per dollar. The Australia Institute: "currently supports just 260 jobs despite $2 billion spent." **SOURCED** (as reported in Wikipedia "Ord River"; the original reports were not opened).
- **WA Auditor General, Report 20, tabled 7 September 2016**, on the "$500 million Ord-East Kimberley Development Plan": "Social and irrigation infrastructure have been delivered, and are being used, but it took longer and the cost to the State has been higher than planned." **SOURCED** — audit.wa.gov.au report overview page. SBS News the same day: announced 2009 at $415 million over two years; actual $529 million over seven years ($114 million over); "no detailed costings to support the decision"; local and Aboriginal employment during construction "not sustained"; 8,000 ha allocated to the Chinese-owned developer Kimberley Agricultural Investments, of which about 1,600 ha was farmed (mainly chia). The Regional Development Minister defended it: "You have only got to walk around the streets and see what is happening on the ground." **SOURCED** — SBS News, 7 Sep 2016. A 250-person workers' camp in Kununurra was part of the extra cost. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; businessnews.com.au article paywalled).
- A 2014 critical essay (Jessica McLean, republished on farmlandgrab.org, 10 Dec 2014): the Ord Final Agreement "says nothing about water"; the Main Dam holds "hugely more water than is needed for present irrigation"; Aboriginal people are 40–50% of the lower Ord basin population; a Shanghai-owned company was given preference for a 50–75 year lease. **SOURCED** (opinion piece; figures are the author's).
- 2012: release of a further 74 km² of Stage 2 "Goomig" lands approved. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Ord River".

**Water institutions** [K1]
- **Ord Irrigation Co-operative**, formed **1996** "to operate and manage the business of providing water and drainage services to the farms within Stage 1," as part of the transfer of the irrigation assets from the State to the growers. **SOURCED** (businessnews.com.au company profile). Stage 1 covers 15,150 ha with over 300 km of earth-lined, gravity-fed channels and drains, over 120 regulators, 1,200 service meters and 61 customers. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).

**Aboriginal land and the Ord Final Agreement** [K1, K9, P9]
- Town and scheme are on Miriwoong country (Miriwoong and Gajerrong / Miriuwung Gajerrong peoples). Courts recognized MG native title in **2003 and 2006** over two adjacent areas. The **Ord Final Agreement** (negotiated 2005; signed as an Indigenous Land Use Agreement, which MG Corporation dates to 2006) settled native title over the area of the Ord Stage 2 expansion, with compensation, transfer of freehold parcels and new conservation areas. **MG Corporation** (Yawoorroong Miriuwung Gajerrong Yirrgeb Noong Dawang Aboriginal Corporation) was created to receive and manage the benefits; the state's Ord Enhancement Scheme (about $10 million, ended 2015) funded health, housing, education, training, employment, environment and heritage programs. MG Corp runs construction, maintenance, housing, agriculture and ranger divisions. **SOURCED** — mgcorp.com.au "Our History". The land holding is described as a ~700,000 ha mosaic. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- Equal wages for Aboriginal pastoral workers were phased in in the Kimberley from **December 1968**; many families were turned off stations; per one source, many Miriwoong and Gajirrawoong people were already in Kununurra after the flooding of Lake Argyle. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets; the National Museum of Australia transcript page returned **403** and was not retried).

**Mining** [K7, K9]
- **Argyle diamond mine**, about 185 km by road from Kununurra (UNVERIFIED for the distance), discovered 2 October 1979, mining from 1985, closed **3 November 2020** after 37 years and more than 865 million carats. Most of its 520 workers commuted from Perth (over 2,000 km) on alternating two-week shifts, living in an on-site residential camp; a large number of local Indigenous people worked there. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Argyle diamond mine". Rio Tinto put its direct contribution at about 6% of the region's gross regional product. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).

**Institutions and services** [P4, P7]
- Schools: East Kimberley College (K–12, just over 900 students), St Joseph's Primary, Ngalangangpum School (both Catholic); a North Regional TAFE campus. Kununurra District Hospital (32 beds plus 10 aged-care beds). Courthouse opened 26 October 2014. East Kimberley Regional Airport, 3.7 km west, "crucial during wet seasons." Leisure Centre (25 m pool), Neighbourhood House, Youth Hub (PCYC). Shire of Wyndham–East Kimberley. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Kununurra".
- Media: the *Kimberley Echo* (in Kununurra since 1980); ABC Kimberley; **6WR Waringarri Radio** (Aboriginal community radio); Reach Beyond Australia shortwave station. **SOURCED** — same.

**Workforce and seasonal labor** [K7, K4]
- The melon-picking season draws migratory farm workers. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia. Harvest work (watermelon, mango, pumpkin, chia weeding, sandalwood pruning) is a destination for Working Holiday visa holders doing the 88 days of regional work that extends the visa; Kununurra has working hostels and a job-shop waiting list. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets from backpacker job sites; not opened). September marks the melon and mango harvest. **UNVERIFIED**.

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The town exists only because of the scheme: founded 1961 as its service town, on resumed pastoral land. [P8] **SOURCED**.
- The dams created the main tourism assets (Lake Argyle, Lake Kununurra, the Diversion Dam), and the Lakes Argyle and Kununurra Ramsar Site. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Ord River".
- The sugar era produced a mill and a distillery; when the mill closed, the distillery outlived it. **SOURCED / partly UNVERIFIED** as above.

- Irrigated harvests create the seasonal workforce of backpackers and migratory pickers, so the town's population swings with the dry season. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, population swing) / **UNVERIFIED** (backpacker detail).

## 2 · Fordlândia, Pará, Brazil

**Claim to test:** "A transplanted company town for one crop that failed in a hostile setting (K9)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with two corrections of emphasis.** (1) The failure was not only the setting: sources agree it came from both natural causes (densely planted rubber trees fell to leaf blight and insects) and imposed working and living rules that local workers rejected (time clocks, cafeteria meals, a Midwestern diet, a nine-to-five day through the hottest hours), producing a riot in 1930 and very high turnover. (2) The town did not die: after Ford left in 1945 it was run by Brazil's Ministry of Agriculture and is today an inhabited district of Aveiro with some 1,750–3,000 people (sources differ), whose old company buildings are the subject of a heritage fight that a court ruled on in May 2026.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** a district of the municipality of **Aveiro, Pará**, on the east bank of the **Tapajós River**, about 300 km south of Santarém. District area given as 14,268 km². **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Fordlândia" (read 2026-10-29).
- **What it was:** a prefabricated company town and rubber plantation of the Ford Motor Company. Concession of about 1 million ha (2.5 million acres) granted **21 July 1927**; the first expedition left in August 1928; Fordlândia operated 1928–1934 as the main plantation, then work shifted to **Belterra**, a second site closer to Santarém (Wikipedia: "40 km south" — note: Belterra is downriver, toward Santarém; the direction given by Wikipedia is not confirmed here). Both sites were handed to the Brazilian government in **1945**. **SOURCED** — The Henry Ford (museum) research page; Wikipedia.
- **Terms:** tax exemption in exchange for 9% of profits (7% to the Brazilian government, 2% to local municipalities). Intended for 10,000 residents. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Money:** Henry Ford page: initial investment $2 million, final total $20 million; surrendered for $250,000. Al Jazeera (29 May 2026) and others: sold for **$244,200**. Wikipedia: "a loss of over US$20 million." **SOURCED** (figures differ slightly by source; all three recorded).
- **Defining feature:** an attempt to grow plantation rubber in the Amazon, the rubber tree's home region, using Midwestern industrial organization. "Not one drop of latex from Fordlândia ever made it into a Ford car" (Wikipedia's wording). **SOURCED**.
- **Population today:** "About 3,000" (2017, Wikipedia; previously ~90 residents until the early 2000s — as stated by Wikipedia); "approximately 1,750" (O Liberal, undated page); "approximately 2,000" (99% Invisible); "thousands" (Al Jazeera 2026). **SOURCED** (all on opened pages; they disagree — no census figure for the district was found).

### B. What else exists there
**Under Ford (1928–1945)** [K4, K7, P4, P8]
- **Facilities built:** hospital (Wikipedia; later a ruin — the hospital "designed by architect Albert Khan [sic — Kahn]" with 100 beds is now "brush and ruins," IHU Unisinos 2015; it **burned down in 2012**, Al Jazeera 2026), school, swimming pool, golf course, tennis courts, movie theater, a 50 m water tower (still standing), sawmill, workshop, warehouses. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, IHU, Al Jazeera.
- **Segregated housing:** an "American Village" (Vila Americana) for managers and separate areas for Brazilian workers. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia.
- **Pay and benefits:** 35 cents a day plus free food, shelter and medical care. **SOURCED** — The Henry Ford.
- **Turnover:** for every 1,000 positions the company had to hire 4,000 workers (**UNVERIFIED** — snippet attributed to Jalopnik/WoodTV, not opened). Grandin (via 99% Invisible): workers "would work a few weeks or a few months and then they would disappear and … go back into the jungle to work their plots, to produce their own food, and maybe they come back the following year." James C. Scott (LRB review of Grandin): turnover "many times higher than in Dearborn." **SOURCED** — 99% Invisible; LRB vol. 31 no. 19.
- **Work rhythm clash** [K7]: local workers were "used to working in the early morning, then taking a break during the hottest parts of the day, and later coming back to work"; Ford imposed a nine-to-five day with time clocks and eight-hour shifts. Workers "bitterly resented whistles, badges, time-clocks and surveillance" (Scott). **SOURCED** — 99% Invisible; LRB.
- **The planting failure** [K9]: trees planted close together became "easy prey for tree blight, Saúva ants, lace bugs, red spiders, and leaf caterpillars"; wild rubber grows scattered through the forest, which protects it. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia; The Henry Ford names leaf fungus, saúva ants and lace bugs.
- **Belterra figures (for context of the company's later model):** by 1940, 7,000 inhabitants and 2,000+ workers (including 261 women and 60 boys); 844 houses plus barracks for 950 men; 3+ schools with 1,000+ students; churches, stores, recreation building, golf course, library, radio stations, power plant; a hospital of 120+ beds. Fordlândia had 500 people in 1940. 3,651,500 rubber trees planted by 1941; first commercial tapping 1942 gave 750 tons of latex against a need of 38,000 tons a year. **SOURCED** — The Henry Ford.

**After 1945** [K9, P9]
- Federal installations and farms were set up, with houses occupied by Ministry of Agriculture employees; the site remains federal (Union) property while the municipality of Aveiro manages the district, which complicates protection. **SOURCED** — IHU Unisinos, 3 Aug 2015.
- **Livelihoods today:** fishing, agriculture and some heritage tourism; the region is seeing soy and cattle expansion. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets; not on a page opened).
- **Utilities:** running water is unreliable; residents reported going 30 days without water. **SOURCED** — Al Jazeera, 29 May 2026.
- **Access** [K5]: by river only in practice, about four hours by boat from Santarém (boats also from Itaituba); not reachable by large ships in the dry season. **SOURCED** — Portal Amazônia, 7 Jun 2022 (river access; dry-season limit). A road route via the Transamazônica exists but is described as harder. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The whole built fabric — hospital, water tower, American-style wooden houses, segregated manager village, golf course — exists because a car company imported a Michigan town plan to produce one raw material. **SOURCED**.
- The company's moral and dietary rules produced their own counter-places (the island bar and brothel) and their own conflict (the 1930 riot). **SOURCED**.
- The heritage dispute today exists because of that imported fabric: since 1990 a listing petition sat with IPHAN; residents are divided because listing brings maintenance obligations, and many have replaced wood with masonry for durability, eroding the original character; land titles were passed informally between residents. **SOURCED** — Portal Amazônia, 7 Jun 2022. Federal prosecutors sued IPHAN and Aveiro in **2015**; in **mid-May 2026** a court ordered federal, state and local authorities and IPHAN to produce and carry out a recovery plan, with fines for noncompliance. **SOURCED** — WTOP/AP 29 May 2026; Al Jazeera 29 May 2026.

### E. Structural features
- **One crop, one company, one remote river:** all supply depended on the river; when the company left, the town lost its reason for being and passed to a ministry. [K5, K9] **SOURCED**.
- **Imposed time vs. local time:** the clash between a factory schedule and a heat-and-season-based local work rhythm is the best-documented structural fault. [K7] **SOURCED**.
- **Federal land, municipal management, informal titles:** a governance tangle that blocks both restoration and private repair. [K1, K9] **SOURCED**.

## 3 · Las Vegas and the Southern Nevada Water Authority (SNWA), Nevada, USA

**Claim to test:** "A large desert city that lives by water rules and a dam's power (K1, K6)."
**Verdict: HALF CONFIRMED, HALF CORRECTED.**
- **Water rules: CONFIRMED and strongly so.** About 90% of the valley's water is Colorado River water drawn from Lake Mead under a fixed 300,000 acre-feet state allocation; a regional water authority enforces seasonal watering days, a year-round Sunday ban, a summer daytime spray ban, escalating water-waste fees, roving investigators, a statutory ban on watering decorative grass, pool-size and cooling limits, and rebates for removing grass.
- **"A dam's power": CORRECTED.** Hydropower is a small part of the city's electricity — about four percent (WorldAtlas); Reclamation's list of Hoover power recipients does not include Las Vegas itself (Boulder City is the one Nevada city listed); Nevada receives about 23–25% of Hoover's output through the Nevada Power system. The valley's utility generates most of its power from natural gas and solar (snippet). The dam's real role for Las Vegas is **historical** (the 1931–36 construction boom) and **as the creator of Lake Mead**, the reservoir the city drinks from — and Las Vegas draws that water through its own intakes, not through the dam.

### A. Snapshot
- **Las Vegas city:** 641,903 people (2020 census); metro about 2.4 million; seat of Clark County. Founded **1905** by a railroad land auction of 110 acres; incorporated 1911; a Mormon fort was built there in 1855; Spanish explorers noted the natural springs about 1829. Nevada legalized casino gambling in **1931**. Demographics (2020): 40.4% non-Hispanic white, 33.31% Hispanic/Latino, 12.33% Black, 7.01% Asian. Summer heat reached 120 °F in July 2024. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Las Vegas" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Economy:** "tourism, gaming, and conventions, which in turn feed the retail and restaurant industries." 2024: about 41.7 million visitors (up 2.1% from 40.8 million), about 6.0 million convention attendees (LVCVA figures). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia; Calculated Risk blog quoting LVCVA. 150,211 hotel rooms, 83.6% occupancy, Clark County gaming revenue $13.567 billion, 58.4 million airport passengers (2024). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **SNWA:** founded **1991**; a seven-member board representing seven member agencies (Big Bend Water District/Laughlin, Boulder City, Clark County Water Reclamation District, Henderson, Las Vegas, Las Vegas Valley Water District, North Las Vegas). Two treatment plants (Alfred Merritt Smith; River Mountains); fluoridation since 2000; ozone treatment. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Southern Nevada Water Authority".
- **Defining feature:** a desert metro whose water is almost entirely a share of one river, stored behind one dam, under one interstate allocation. **SOURCED** (below).

### B. What else exists there
**Water supply** [K1, K5, P8]
- "About 90 percent" from the Colorado River; about 10% from Las Vegas Valley groundwater (LVVWD rights 40,760 af; North Las Vegas 6,201 af). "Nevada has the right to consumptively use 300,000 acre-feet of water per year." About 40% of water is used indoors, and about 99% of that is recycled. Lake Mead has fallen about 160 feet since 2000. **SOURCED** — snwa.com "Where water comes from".
- **Return-flow credits:** treated wastewater returns to Lake Mead through the Las Vegas Wash; each gallon returned lets the valley withdraw another, so withdrawals can exceed 300,000 af as long as *consumptive* use does not. The valley is said to depend on over 200,000 af of return-flow credits. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; the mechanism is consistent with SNWA's "99% recycled" statement, which is SOURCED).

## 4 · Hungarian agro-towns and their tanya farmsteads (Kecskemét, Hódmezővásárhely)

**Claim to test:** "Large towns whose people were farmers, with homesteads scattered across open land (K7, K4)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with the mechanism made precise.** The Great Plain market towns (*mezőváros*) held huge municipal territories after the Ottoman-era destruction of surrounding villages; their farmer-citizens kept a house in town and a farmstead (*tanya*) out on the town's land. The split between the two homes followed age, season and the week (see K7/K4 below). The system peaked 1850–1949, was deliberately broken by building bans, forced resettlement into planned centers and collectivization (1949–1961), and survives today mainly in horticultural sand-soil districts, with a state-funded caretaker service for those who remain. Kecskemét and Hódmezővásárhely are both confirmed as classic examples.

**Main source for this pick (opened and read in full):** Bray Ágnes – Kovács Teréz, "Alföldi tanyák és tanyapolitikák a múltban és a jelenben" (Great Plain tanyas and tanya policies, past and present), *Acta Sociologica* (Pécs), 2021, pp. 4–22 — PDF downloaded from journals.lib.pte.hu and read as extracted text (Hungarian). Cited below as **[B&K 2021]**. It summarizes Erdei Ferenc (1939, 1942), Győrffy István (1937), Balogh István (1965), Beluszky Pál (2001), Duró Annamária (2000, 2005, 2018) and others.

### A. Snapshot
- **Kecskemét:** 110,813 people (2016), 321.36 km², Hungary's eighth-largest city, seat of Bács-Kiskun county. Named an *oppidum* (town) in a 1368 charter of King Louis I; under Ottoman rule its people arranged "to pay tax directly to the pasha in Buda," gaining protection and special status. Held nearly 30,000 cattle in the early 18th century; after phylloxera, vineyards were planted on the sandy plains around the town, founding its wine and brandy trade; known for apricot brandy. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Kecskemét" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Hódmezővásárhely:** 44,009 people (2017); municipal territory 487.98 km², "one of the largest municipal territories" in Hungary; formed in the 15th century from the merger of four villages (Hód, Vásárhely, Tarján, Ábrány); 150 years of Ottoman occupation from 1542 (another source: Csongrád County taken 1552); by 1890 "the fourth largest Hungarian town" with 55,475 inhabitants; an agrarian town with a peasant citizenry surrounded by tanyas; Cittaslow member. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Hódmezővásárhely". (The 1552 date is **UNVERIFIED**, snippet.)
- **The tanya:** a residential and farm-building unit on the outer territory of a settlement; the word replaced the older term *szállás* in the 19th century [B&K 2021]. Győrffy (1937), quoted: the Great Plain farmer "has two settlements: one is his town or village house, the other is the tanya in the outer lands serving as summer or winter quarters." [B&K 2021] **SOURCED**.

### B. What else exists there — how the system formed and worked
**Origins** [K1, P5, P8] — all **SOURCED** [B&K 2021]
- During and after the Ottoman period, **70–80% of Great Plain settlements disappeared**; the surviving market towns and the free royal town (Szeged) leased or annexed the lands of the vanished villages, gaining enormous pastures (Debrecen's territory reached nearly 170,000 *hold*). The market town organized farming itself; neither the absent lord nor the Sultan intervened, giving the towns and their citizens unusual autonomy, looser feudal ties and a free-peasant way of farming (the "Great Plain path" of Márkus and Beluszky, linked also to the Reformation).
- Livestock: privately owned herds grazed on distant pastures from St. George's Day to St. Michael's Day under herdsmen hired by the community; in winter each farmer had to house his own animals, so individual winter quarters were built on the pastures. Hungarian Grey cattle exported west from the Plain played a large role in the national economy from the 13th to 18th centuries.
- In the 18th century grain grew in importance; farmers stayed temporarily on the outer lands during autumn sowing and summer harvest, building modest shelters for themselves and their draft animals; "the true home of the tanya farmers remained the mother settlement."
- Late 18th / early 19th century: semi-free animal keeping and maize lengthened the time needed on the tanya; servants (various kinds of hired, mostly in-kind-paid farm hands) were settled there permanently, but stayed tied to the town house and the owner's family.
- **Mass tanya farming came after 1848**: the lifting of bans on permanent moves to the tanya, rising grain demand, and especially the **parceling out of common pastures** for plowing. Pastures on the Plain belonged to the towns, and each town's magistrate set its own policy: **Szeged** leased pasture for 10 or 30 years in 5-*hold* units; **Kecskemét** gave small plots **free to the town's poor** and also sold outer pasture; Debrecen allowed tanya land only to those who owned a town house. 1850–1870: Szeged's tanya population doubled and **Kecskemét's tripled**; the two became the most tanya-heavy towns on the Plain. In the Kiskunság, migrants "got 20 *hold* of sand for two *hold* of black earth." Some settlers were German- or Slovak-speaking.
- **1910:** 11% of Hungary's total population and **one third** of the Plain's lived on tanyas. **1930:** about 30% of the Plain's people, nearly a million, lived in outer areas, about 800,000 of them on tanyas (Beluszky). **1949:** tanya residents peaked at **1.1 million**, after the 1945 land reform (about 650,000 poor peasants and landless laborers received on average 5.6 *hold*), when new owners built **75,000 tanyas** on the Plain, largely by their own labor.

**Erdei's three types** [K4, K7] — **SOURCED** [B&K 2021]
- *Large-farmer tanya*: servants lived there; the farmer lived in town with his family and came out for a few days at major work times.
- *Small-farmer tanya* (Erdei's ideal and the dominant type): the working-age young farmer lived out on the tanya all year with his family and a furnished household, but his real home was in town, where his **old parents lived**, where he **came in on Sundays and market days**, where **holidays were spent**, and where he **moved in old age**. Work and civic life were thus separated.
- *Farm-type tanya*: a permanent, sole home "from cradle to grave," with no town house; Erdei ranked it below even the village.
- Mendöl Tibor (geographer) disputed Erdei: most tanyas were isolated settlements with little link to the town; both, per Csatári (2005), were partly right. Families split between the "outside" tanya and the "inside" town house never broke from the mother settlement (Duró 2005).

**Destruction (1949–1961)** [K9, P7] — **SOURCED** [B&K 2021]
- A **Tanya Council** (*Tanyai Tanács*, chaired by Erdei Ferenc) designated **236 tanya centers** (*tanyaközpont*); **Government Decree 1300/1949** restricted and banned building in outer areas — from then on, building was allowed only in the designated centers. The large territories of Plain towns were split up; **Szeged and Kecskemét each lost their historic territory to 9 future tanya villages**. Promised buildings in the centers (council hall, school, surgery, pharmacy, bored well, concrete road, electricity) came slowly; by 1953 only 82 centers were thought worth developing; the council dissolved itself in 1954. **The building ban stayed in force until 1986.**
- **Collectivization:** two waves, 1948–1953 (20% of farmland) and 1959–1961 (96%); peasants joined under psychological pressure and sometimes physical force; tanya farmers often lost the tanya too. Some burned their tanyas; some took their own lives. A former member, 1983: "it was like when the Sunday table is set beautifully, and when we sit down to eat, they take everything away from in front of us."
- A March 1960 decree required each member to get **one *hold* of household plot**, measured out **around the tanya** for tanya dwellers; work in the cooperatives was paid in work units valued only at year-end, so the household plot was farmed with great care.
- In horticultural sand districts (Danube–Tisza interfluve) simpler "specialist cooperatives" let members keep using their own land for a levy (often 10% of sales); **tanyas survive today mostly where these existed.**
- Tanya population in the Plain counties: 694,000 (1960) → 279,000 two decades later (Romány); about **200,000 by 1990**. **Tanya schools were closed in the early 1970s**; in the 1980s cooperative geese were kept in the former school buildings. Parents advised their children to leave; the tanya population aged. Under late socialism, generations sometimes reversed: the young stayed in the tanya center and the parents moved out to the tanya.

**After 1989** [K9, K8] — **SOURCED** [B&K 2021]
- Compensation laws from 1992 allowed land to be measured out around one's own tanya. Many small producers failed in the market economy; impoverished families pushed out of towns moved to tanyas; subsistence crime appeared; new villas rose beside old tanyas near towns. Since 1990 the census counts tanya dwellers only within "outer-area" population (about 200,000 in 1990 and 2001, half in the Homokhátság sand ridge).
- Policy: Parliament resolution **49/2009 (V.27.)** on preserving and developing tanyas; tanya development grants from 2011; the **Tanyafejlesztési Program** (Tanya Development Program) from 2015. **"At the end of 2017 the Great Plain tanya was officially declared a Hungaricum, as cultural heritage"**; the agriculture ministry gives an annual "Tanya of the Year" award in four categories.
- New uses: rural tourism, guest rooms, gastronomy, horse riding, second homes, some telework; "nearly a third of tanyas" now house only old people (Matula 2005).

## 5 · Solo road-travel music (developer-added, subnet-wide)

**Claim to test:** "Music for traveling alone on the road: whatever songs, genres or listening habits belong to long solo drives and the stops along them" — not necessarily country music; research the kind of music and listening culture around solo road travel in real places (long-haul and other solo drivers), not one genre.
**Verdict: CONFIRMED as a real, documented listening culture, with three distinct layers** — and the research supports the developer's caution that it is not one genre:
1. **Songs about the road** (a song genre): in the US, truck-driving country songs from 1963; in Australia, a body of truck songs centered on Slim Dusty and Joy McKean.
2. **Radio as company** (a broadcast institution): overnight shows built for drivers (from 1969/1971 in the US), later satellite talk radio; in remote Australia, radio coverage itself is patchy, and the loss of a remote shortwave service in 2017 was fought by outback workers and travelers.
3. **Listening habits and their function**: surveys and studies frame in-cab listening as fatigue and loneliness management — music, talk radio, podcasts and audiobooks mixed; an experimental study found medium-tempo music best for long-distance fatigue. Mental-health programs now use the in-cab radio itself.
Plus **gatherings**: a memorial convoy named after a truck song, and a country-music festival whose first award went to that song.
**Not found:** research specifically on solo drivers who are *not* truck drivers (commuters, remote workers, travelers) beyond one sociological article seen only as an abstract snippet.

### A. Snapshot — what the "practice" is, and where it is documented
- **United States** — truck-driving country music as a named sub-genre (from 1963); overnight trucking radio (WBAP Fort Worth from 1969; WWL New Orleans from 1971); satellite trucking talk channel (from 2001/2003). **SOURCED** (below).
- **Australia** — truck songs by Slim Dusty (written mainly by Joy McKean and others), the Lights on the Hill memorial and convoy at Gatton, Queensland, the Tamworth Country Music Festival, and the radio-coverage problem of the outback. **SOURCED** (below).
- **Function** — fatigue, alertness and isolation. **SOURCED / UNVERIFIED** (below).

### B. What exists — the institutions of the practice
**1. Songs (US)** [K13, K7]
- **"Six Days on the Road"** — written by Earl Green and Carl Montgomery, first recorded by Paul Davis (1961); **Dave Dudley's 1963** recording (Kay Bank Studios, Minneapolis) reached No. 2 country and No. 32 Hot 100, 21 weeks on the country chart; lyrics about highway patrolmen, weigh scales, loneliness and staying awake on long drives. Historian Bill Malone: not the first trucking song, but it "set off a vogue for such songs" for decades, as country became identified as "working man's music in the 1960s." A 1997 cover changed a line to remove a drug reference. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Six Days on the Road". Dudley is called "the father of truck driving country music." **UNVERIFIED** (American Songwriter, snippet).
- **"Convoy"** (1975, C. W. McCall — a character created and voiced by Bill Fries with Chip Davis) — No. 1 on US country and pop charts, born of the mid-1970s CB-radio craze; Fries: truckers "were forming things called convoys and they were talking to each other on CB radios … Chip and I bought ourselves a CB radio and went out to hear them talk." It boosted CB sales beyond truckers. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; Wikipedia page not opened).

**2. Songs (Australia)** [K13, K9]
- **"Lights on the Hill"** — written by **Joy McKean**, recorded by her husband **Slim Dusty**: a driver with a heavy load at night is blinded by lights on a hill, hits a pole and goes off the road edge. It won the first-ever Golden Guitar for Song of the Year at the first Tamworth festival, **January 1973**; Slim's recording won Best EP or Single; certified Platinum (70,000 units). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Lights on the Hill (song)".
- **Trucks on the Track** (album, May 1984, EMI/Columbia; No. 50; Gold) with truck-themed tracks ("Trucks On The Track," "From Here To There And Back," "Dieseline Dreams," "Trucks, Tarps And Trailers," "Dogs, Dust And Diesel" and others). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Prime Movers** (compilation, EMI, 15 July 2016), 20 truck songs recorded before Slim Dusty's death in 2003. VICE (14 July 2016) interviewed drivers in Queensland, NSW and WA: a driver of 40+ years: "If I had a dollar for every mile I did listening to Slim, I could pay off the national debt"; another recalled older rules requiring 800–1,000 km overnight runs, with amphetamine use common; the songs are described as romanticizing freedom, honor and community and presenting worker stoicism, uniting drivers who disagree on safety, unions and conditions. Songs named: "Names Upon The Wall," "The Lady Is a [truck driver]" (title as given by the source uses the Australian colloquial word for truck driver), "Trucks Tarps and Trailers," "Long Black Road," "Pushin' Time." **SOURCED** — VICE, 14 Jul 2016.

**3. Radio as company (US)** [K10, K1, K7]
- **WBAP Fort Worth:** Bill Mack began an overnight (midnight–6 a.m.) country show in **1969**, which became a favorite of long-haul drivers; he took on-air calls from drivers nationwide, monitored CB radio, and sometimes interviewed drivers who stopped by the station; later syndicated (*Country Crossroads*, 200+ stations) and on satellite radio until retirement in 2012; died 31 July 2020. **SOURCED** — The Trucker, 3 Aug 2020. (His on-air handle is in the slang file, not here.)
- **WWL New Orleans — *The Road Gang*:** created by Charlie Douglas, on air from **1971**: country music, weather, road-condition reports and talk, 7½ hours a night, six nights a week; WWL's clear-channel AM signal carried it to drivers across the country at night. Historian Errol Laborde: listeners heard "there was a world out there that we didn't hear much about" — truck-stop showers, bunk rooms, 24-hour cafés, road conditions from places like Butte, Montana; WWL is credited with creating a new radio genre. **SOURCED** — Audacy/WWL, 29 Mar 2022. Dave Nemo co-hosted for 13 years and took over full time in 1984. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Satellite:** a truckers' talk channel on Sirius from 2001/2003 (Sirius Trucking Network), renamed in March 2006, merged with XM's trucking channel on 17 February 2009; now SiriusXM channel 146 (US and Canada); topics: politics, economics, social issues, regulation; AP news and weather; hosts included Dave Nemo and Bill Mack; a daily hour from the owner-operators' association (OOIDA). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Road Dog Trucking" (flagged there as under-cited).

**4. Radio coverage and its loss (Australia)** [K5, K9]
- The ABC shut its Northern Territory shortwave service (transmitters at Katherine, Tennant Creek and Roe Creek/Alice Springs) at the **end of January 2017**; those affected included cattle-station workers, mustering camps, outstations, rangers, remote residents and **travelers in outback regions**; AM/FM lack reach across the sparsely populated NT; streaming and satellite radio work only at home near infrastructure; the ABC redirected $1.2 million to digital radio for Darwin and Hobart. A cattlemen's association CEO described people outdoors "100% of waking hours" with "very minimal contact." **SOURCED** — swling.com summarizing *The Guardian*, 18 Jan 2017. Large parts of the Stuart Highway have no ABC radio coverage; FM is lost within about 10 km of some towns on country roads; FM reaches about 150 km from the strongest transmitters. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; Barkly Regional Council page **404**).

**5. Listening habits and function** [K7, P4]
- **Experimental study:** Li, Chen & Zhang, "Effect of Music Tempo on Long-Distance Driving," *i-Perception* 2019 (China): 16 drivers, real-road 140-minute monotonous highway drives, EEG and eye tracking; slow (42.6 bpm), medium (91.7), fast (122.1) tempos and no music. "Medium-tempo music is the best choice to reduce fatigue and maintain attention for a long-distance driving"; slow music helped attention at first but fatigue worsened after 30 minutes; fast music relieved fatigue but hurt attention. Background cited: about 68% of British drivers listen to music while driving; 62% say it relaxes them, 25% that it improves vigilance. **SOURCED** — PMC6636224.
- **Driver survey (US carrier):** 85% of drivers listen to traditional radio, 66% stream music, 14% audiobooks. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; the Matheson page failed: **expired certificate**). Drivers mix playlists, talk radio and sports to avoid tiring. **UNVERIFIED** (industry blogs, snippets).
- **Australia, isolation:** "Open Road, Open Up" — GME (radio maker) with Rural Outreach Counselling and driver-advocate Rod Hannifey: free mental-health support from trained advocates and drivers on **UHF CB Channel 24**, with signed trucks rolling out nationally; "one in two truck drivers report some level of psychological distress"; "For truck drivers in remote and regional areas, staying in touch can mean the difference between life and death" (GME); "I know firsthand how hard it can be out there on the road alone" (Hannifey). **SOURCED** — gme.net.au (launch 17 July; year not on page — 2023 per Big Rigs URL, which returned **403**). 57% of drivers use podcasts and audio against loneliness; half of truck and warehouse workers use digital radio and podcasts to manage mental health. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; Healthy Heads campaign).
- **Sociology of the solo car:** Michael Bull, "Automobility and the Power of Sound," *Theory, Culture & Society* 21(4–5), 2004, 243–259 — on solitary car habitation and the radio, cassette, sound system and phone as ways drivers transform the space they move through. **UNVERIFIED** (abstract seen only in snippets; Sussex repository **403**).
