## 1 · Inuvik Community Greenhouse, Inuvik, Northwest Territories, Canada

**Claim to test:** "A former arena turned into a shared greenhouse in the Arctic, run as a civic garden (K10, K1)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with narrowings.** (a) It is a former hockey arena (a Quonset rink built 1971 for Grollier Hall, the Catholic residence attached to Sir Alexander Mackenzie School, i.e. part of the Indian Residential School system). (b) It is run not by the town but by a non-profit, the Community Garden Society of Inuvik (CGSI, formed November 1998). It is a seasonal (May to October) sunlight greenhouse, not a heated year-round one; a separate heated hydroponic container was added in 2021 for winter greens. (c) It combines community plots (ground floor) with a commercial greenhouse (upper floor), so "civic garden" is half the picture.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Inuvik, NWT, on the East Channel of the Mackenzie Delta, about 2 degrees (roughly 200 km) north of the Arctic Circle (68.36° N). SOURCED (Guinness World Records page; Globe and Mail 2004-07-12; sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca 2019-12-24).
- **What it is:** a 16,000 sq ft (1,486 m²) Quonset-style former ice-hockey arena whose tin roof was replaced with translucent polycarbonate glazing; ridge vent opens automatically. Main floor = community and family plots; second floor = about 360 m² commercial greenhouse (bedding plants, hydroponic vegetables). SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca, read 2026-10-29; PHAC profile canada.ca, undated, read 2026-10-29).
- **Record:** Guinness World Records lists it as "Largest Arctic communal greenhouse," 16,000 sq ft, achieved spring 2000. SOURCED (guinnessworldrecords.com record 772963). [Note: the Guinness text says "year round"; every other source, including the society's own operating pattern, says May to October. Treat "year round" as wrong for the main building.]
- **Timeline:** 1998 CGSI formed, feasibility study and fundraising; 1999 Aurora College donated the arena to CGSI; 2000 official opening (some sources say production began spring/early summer 1999; Western Producer 2024-05-16 says "operating since 1999, 25 seasons"). SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca; producer.com 2024-05-16).
- **Defining feature:** a building tied to the residential-school era turned into a shared food-growing space that runs on the midnight sun. Plaques at the entrance acknowledge former Grollier Hall students; the case-study authors describe it as functioning as "a healing program" for survivors. SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). [K9, K10] [P6]
- **Town population:** 3,137 (2021 census), down from 3,243 (2016); 65% Indigenous (of whom ~64% Inuvialuit, ~26% First Nations, mainly Gwich'in, ~6% Métis). SOURCED (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuvik, read 2026-10-29, citing Statistics Canada). The PHAC profile gives 3,700 (older figure). [P9]

### B. What else exists there (greenhouse and town)
**The greenhouse as an institution [K1, K14]**
- **Plots, over time (numbers changed, all SOURCED, each dated):** 2004: 88 raised plots, large 16×4 ft at $50/yr, small 8×4 ft at $25/yr, ~18 in deep, waiting list (Globe and Mail 2004-07-12). PHAC profile (undated, ca. 2008-09): 74 full plots, half plots available, up to 170 members, 100+ members. 2024: 188 plots, of which 39 are "market plots" whose produce is sold at the weekly greenhouse market or the weekly community Arctic Market (Western Producer 2024-05-16).
- **2024 terms:** membership $25 per season (1 April to 31 March), covers insurance; benefits include 24/7 access, discounts, newsletter. Large plot $60 + 5 volunteer hours; small plot $30 + 2 volunteer hours; elders (60+) get a large plot free with membership. 120 members signed by early May 2024; 150 volunteer hours logged by 1 May. Staff: a greenhouse manager and an event/activity coordinator. SOURCED (producer.com 2024-05-16; membership $25 also in NWT News/North 2021-02-25).
- **Earlier volunteer rule:** 15 volunteer hours per plot per year (tours, watering, tending elders' and children's plots). SOURCED (PHAC profile; sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). Sponsored plots for elders, group homes, children's groups, people with disabilities, local charities. SOURCED (same).
- **Programs:** gardening classes; gift shop for local artists' crafts; composting facility; commercial bedding-plant and hydroponic production; Saturday community market; children's carnival; school tours and workshops; weekly plant sale; flower baskets for the town. SOURCED (PHAC profile; sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). Chicken coop and egg program; an outdoor garden maintained by the Inuvik Native Band (2021). SOURCED (yellowknifer.com / NWT News/North 2021-02-25). 2025: a seasonal composting-program job posting. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of goodwork.ca job posting).
- **Year-round add-on [R]:** April 2021, a heated hydroponics container (repurposed trailer, built by ColdAcre Food Systems) arrived; 500 to 1,000 lb of greens per month; ~20 hours/week maintenance; sold via Northmart and a $20/week member veggie box, "now available all year long." SOURCED (hortidaily.com 2021-04-12). A later report says solar power was added to it. UNVERIFIED (headline only; the NNSL page died at the source, 404 after redirect).

Original floors, windows, doors and materials were reused. SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca).
- **Funding [K8]:** Government of Canada; GNWT (Northern Foods Development Program); corporate sponsors ConocoPhillips, Shell Canada, CIBC, ExxonMobil, Imperial Oil; aboriginal groups. Grant and donation money "is not eligible to pay for salaries," so the coordinator post was part-time/seasonal. SOURCED (PHAC profile; sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). 2024-25: CanNor contribution of $24,199 (21 Oct 2024 to 31 Mar 2025) for "Renovating the Inuvik Greenhouse" (roof, walls, ceiling panels). UNVERIFIED (search snippet of open.canada.ca grants record).
- **Recorded problems [K9]:** high volunteer, membership and board turnover; insufficient operating funding; a "community perception" issue about inclusivity; in 2021 the executive director said the building's association with Grollier Hall still creates barriers to membership. SOURCED (PHAC profile; sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca; NWT News/North 2021-02-25).
- **Regional role:** gives advice and seedlings to Fort McPherson, Tuktoyaktuk, Tsiigehtchic; inspired the Iqaluit community greenhouse. SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca).

**The town [K5, K7, K8]**

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The light regime sets the greenhouse calendar: about 56 days of continuous daylight and 30 days of polar night per year; the unheated main building runs mid-May to early October, peaking in July. Sources explain the winter closure as too cold and dark: artificial heat and light would be needed. SOURCED (Wikipedia climate; Globe and Mail 2004; sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). [K7] [P4]
- Early-season water is snowmelt, collected until the water pipes thaw; in spring 2024 gardeners were wheeling snow into the building to moisten soil. SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca; producer.com 2024-05-16). [K5] [R]
- Permafrost: plots are raised and insulated, built over gravel, to stop heat moving into the frozen ground. SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). [K14]
- Interior runs about 10 °C warmer than outside in summer; upper level reached 30 °C from sun alone on 2 May 2024. SOURCED (same; producer.com).
- Gardening supplements, not replaces, hunting and fishing. SOURCED (sustainableheritagecasestudies.ca). [K12]

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)
- A volunteer-hours economy: plot rent is paid partly in labor (15 h/plot in the 2000s; 2 to 5 h in 2024), and that labor covers tours, watering and the plots set aside for elders and children. [K1, K7]
- Funding cannot pay wages, so the institution runs on volunteers and a seasonal coordinator and suffers turnover. [K8, K9]
- The commercial upper floor and market plots tie the shared garden to retail (Northmart, Arctic Market). [K8]
- Trades students did the conversion: the building is also a training site. [K14] [R]

## 2 · Hveragerði, South Iceland

**Claim to test:** "A greenhouse town in a cold climate that exists because heat is cheap." (Input care note: use as a counterexample on power.)
**Verdict: PARTLY CONFIRMED, NARROWED.** (a) The settlement formed around 1929 from several heat-using enterprises at once, not greenhouses alone: the dairy co-operative Mjólkurbú Ölfusinga (and its skyr plant, 1930), geothermal wool washing, seaweed drying, and greenhouse growing (first greenhouse 1923; oldest station Fagrahvammur, 1929). The state bought the estate shortly before 1930. (b) Heat is cheap (steam from hot wells), but **electricity for winter lighting is the growers' largest cost**, so "cheap heat" alone does not make the greenhouses viable. (c) Greenhouse area has fallen by almost half from its peak and greenhouse sites are being turned into housing as the town grows as a commuter town.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** South Iceland, 45 km east of Reykjavík on Route 1, at the foot of the Hengill central volcano; the Varmá river runs through town. SOURCED (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hveragerði, read 2026-10-29).
- **Population and area:** 2,657 (Wikipedia figure, date not stated on the read page); 9 km². Growth from 1,757 (2000) to 3,342 (2025). UNVERIFIED (citypopulation.de snippet). Historical: 121 (1940), 399 (1946), 1,593 (1989). SOURCED (Wikipedia). [P9]
- **Status:** separate municipality from 1946 (the President of Iceland spoke at its 60th anniversary on 20 April 2006); town status 1 July 1987. SOURCED (olafur.forseti.is speech PDF, read via text extraction; Wikipedia).
- **Defining feature:** a geothermal field (hot springs, fumaroles) in the middle of the town, used from the start for production. A 2016 regional source calls a geothermal area in a town center "unique nationwide." SOURCED (south.is food-and-production page; Wikipedia).

### B. What else exists there
**Origins [K8, K14]**
- "Around 1929, the village Hveragerði began to form after a co-operation for the dairy Mjólkurbú Ölfusinga was established." Skyrgerðin (the skyr plant), built 1930, "the first of its kind in Iceland," also made Iceland's first yogurt-type product. Green farming began at the same time. SOURCED (south.is, food and production).
- The President's 2006 speech: Hveragerði people were the first in Iceland to harness geothermal heat to drive production machinery, to wash wool, dry seaweed and make consumer goods from milk; they made flower growing a trade, grew vegetables in greenhouses, and sold vegetables at scale for the first time in Iceland; a natural-food society (NLFÍ) built a clinic there. SOURCED (olafur.forseti.is, 2006-04-20; read in Icelandic, my translation).
- Hot-spring water used for space heating since 1929. First greenhouse 1923. SOURCED (Wikipedia).

**Greenhouse industry, then and now [K9]**
- Oldest horticultural station: Fagrahvammur, established 1929; the 1929 houses are demolished, its oldest surviving buildings date from 1961. 2020 survey (by Landform, Selfoss, for the municipality): **18 active stations, and about the same number closed in recent years; housing is being built on former station sites.** SOURCED (bbl.is Bændablaðið, 2020-03-12).
- 1980s-1990s: about 40 stations, ~50,000 m² of glass. Early 2026: ~27,000 m², a 46% drop from the peak and 20% since 2006. Causes named: housing pressure, hard operating conditions, competition from larger producers, changing growing methods. The Eden station burned in 2011. The 2025-2037 master plan aims to support growing, preserve historically valuable greenhouses and fold greenhouse heritage into new building; on 9 April 2026 the council approved a committee on a greenhouse-preservation strategy. SOURCED (visir.is, 2026-05-11, opinion piece by a municipal councillor).
- In 2017 the municipality itself offered a central greenhouse station (Þelamörk 29) for rent, asking bidders to show how their operation would benefit "the town's character and image." SOURCED (hveragerdi.is, 2017-03-10). [K8]
- **Energy costs:** Horticultural College campus heated with 110 to 130 °C steam from hot wells; "the highest cost factor for growers is electricity since we need to light our crops from October until the end of March," and on overcast summer days. National self-sufficiency (as stated by the college): cucumbers 100%+ (exported to Denmark, Faroes, Greenland), tomatoes 55-60%, peppers 20-30%. SOURCED (hortidaily.com, 2023-07-07). Growers' prices held flat into 2026 because aluminum and silicon smelters cut demand (Norðurál technical problems; PCC Bakki halted). SOURCED (hortidaily.com, 2026-01-29). Growers' complaint that farmers get ~0.5% of national power and pay more than aluminum smelters: UNVERIFIED (snippet, source article not opened). [K8] [R]

## 3 · Japanese plant factories (Kameoka and others)

**Claim to test:** "Fully enclosed, stacked lettuce production where electricity is the main cost (K7)." (Input care: Japan nation note.)
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in shape, NARROWED on cost.** Fully artificial-light plant factories (完全人工光型, PFALs) in Japan are indeed enclosed, multi-tier, and overwhelmingly lettuce. But electricity is **one of two or three large costs, not reliably "the" main one**: a 2016 industry article says power "generally" takes **about one third** of production cost; a 2026 summary of the national survey says electricity takes **20-35% of annual sales**; a 2022 Chiba University cost model for lettuce puts **labor (US$279/m²/yr) above electricity (US$166/m²/yr)**. Most daily work is still manual. Only **~40-43% of artificial-light operators are profitable or break even**. The Kameoka plant (Spread Co.) is one real example; its current operating status was not found.

### A. Snapshot (the type)

Two types: fully artificial-light (closed, no sunlight) and sunlight-using (greenhouses with supplementary light), plus a combined type. Research in Japan goes back about 40 years (from c. 2016). ## 5 · Biosphere 2, Oracle, Arizona

**Claim to test:** "A sealed growing world whose crew learned what enclosed farming costs (K9)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and sharpened.** In the first two-year closure (26 Sep 1991 to 26 Sep 1993) eight people in a ~1.27 ha sealed structure grew about 80% (reported as 83%) of their own food on ~2,000 m² of farmland, were hungry much of the first year (10-20% weight loss), worked ~66 hours a week with ~45% of all work time going to food (farming, processing, animals, cooking), lost oxygen from 20.9% to ~14-14.5% (oxygen had to be injected), and split into two hostile factions. Light (glass cut sunlight by 50-60%), an El Niño winter, mites, and a no-pesticide rule set the farm's limits. The second crew (1994) reached full food sufficiency but the mission ended early amid a management takeover. Since 2007/2011 it has been a University of Arizona research and visitor site.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** near Oracle, Pinal County, Arizona, at the base of the Santa Catalina Mountains, ~1,200 m elevation, ~58 km (36 mi) north of Tucson. SOURCED (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere_2, read 2026-10-29).
- **What it is:** 3.14 acres (1.27 ha) under glass on a ~40-acre campus; built 1987-1991; Ed Bass funded ~US$150 million to 1991; operated by Space Biospheres Ventures. Biomes: rainforest, ocean with coral reef, mangrove marsh, savanna, fog desert, an agricultural area (~27,000 sq ft per Wikipedia; 2,000 m² cropping per Nelson), and the human habitat. Atmospheric leak rate about 10% per year or less. SOURCED (Wikipedia; Nelson & Dempster 1996, "Living in Space: Results from Biosphere 2's Initial Closure," read via PDF text extraction from gwern.net).
- **Today:** owned by the University of Arizona (research operations from June 2007, full ownership 1 July 2011); ~100,000 visitors a year; in 2017, 55 staff including 25 research faculty, budget ~US$8 million; Bass's Philecology Foundation gave US$30 million in 2007 (which created the visitor center) and US$30 million more in 2017; research grants from NASA, NSF and DOE. SOURCED (tucson.com / Arizona Daily Star, 2017-09-20; Wikipedia, which gives the 2011 pledge as $20 million: the two sources differ on the earlier gift).

### B. What else exists there
**Life inside, Mission 1 [K7, K12, K14] [R]**
- **Food:** ~2,000 m² cropping area produced ~80% of needs over two years; with three months of food grown inside before closure and some seed stocks, average intake ~2,200 kcal, 73 g protein, 32 g fat per person per day; intake lower in year one; most of the 10-20% weight loss came in year one; blood cholesterol fell sharply. Over 86 crops including herbs. Main crops: rice, wheat, sorghum, sweet potato, taro, lablab bean, peanuts (the key fat source), beet, chili, tomato, squash, greens, lettuce, banana, papaya. Animals: African pygmy goats (milk, "the outstanding producers"), chickens (eggs, meat), Ossabaw feral pygmy pigs, tilapia in rice/azolla paddies. A computer program tracked nutrient intake and planned plantings. Crew squeezed ~200 m² of extra planters into every spare spot, adding hundreds of kilograms of food in year two. SOURCED (Nelson & Dempster 1996).
- **Food as social life:** "The standard of the cooking on a particular day would have an effect on the general spirits of the crew... much of the crew's social life became centered around food. Holidays were celebrated with huge feasts." SOURCED (Nelson & Dempster 1996, quoting Silverstone and Nelson). [K2, K12]
- **Work:** each crew member averaged **66 hours a week**; agriculture and food (processing, animal care, cooking) took ~45% of all work hours; two-thirds of time went to operations and one-third to research, against a planned 50/50; cooking rotated, each person cooking all three meals one day in eight (~8 hours); **Sundays and outside holidays were days off, but food preparation, animal care and system checks were needed every day**; midway, some lab equipment was moved out and scheduled sample exports allowed to save crew time. SOURCED (Nelson & Dempster 1996). [K7]
- **Pests and the no-pesticide rule:** pesticides and herbicides could not be used without health hazards in a closed air and water cycle (CO₂ residence ~4 days); control was by beneficial insects (ladybugs, praying mantis, parasitic wasps), intercropping, hand control, and "safe sprays" (soap, light oil, Bacillus thuringiensis). Problems: two mites, mealybug, aphid, powdery mildew, root-knot nematode, cockroaches; the broad mite forced a switch from white to sweet potatoes and more lablab bean. SOURCED (Nelson & Dempster 1996). [K6, K14]
- **Light:** glazing and structure cut incident light 50-60%; the two closure years had strong El Niño storm fronts that lowered yields; artificial lights were installed afterwards. SOURCED (Nelson & Dempster 1996). [R]
- **Atmosphere:** CO₂ ranged from under 1,000 to over 4,000 ppm (Wikipedia: 4,000-4,500 in winter); oxygen fell from 20.9% to ~14% (Wikipedia: 14.5% after 16 months, comparable to ~4,080 m altitude), traced to soil organic carbon and to CO₂ reacting with exposed concrete; oxygen injected January and August 1993. SOURCED (Nelson & Dempster 1996; Wikipedia).
- **Ecology:** ant and cockroach explosions; morning glories overgrew the rainforest; most vertebrate species and nearly all pollinating insects died; the crazy ant *Paratrechina longicornis* came to dominate. SOURCED (Wikipedia). [K9]

**People and conflict [K4, K9] [SM:man versus society]**
- The crew split into two factions, "implacable enemies, barely on speaking terms," over research versus closure integrity; part of the Scientific Advisory Committee resigned in February 1992. In November 1992 the crew began eating seed stocks not grown inside; an order to remove the PR director who learned of it "could never be enforced without effectively terminating the closure." SOURCED (Wikipedia).
- Mission 2 (6 March to 6 September 1994, seven crew) achieved complete food sufficiency and needed no oxygen injection, but on 1 April 1994 Bass brought in new management under a restraining order served by federal marshals; on 5 April two Mission 1 crew members opened an airlock door and three emergency exits for ~15 minutes (~10% of the air exchanged); Space Biospheres Ventures dissolved 1 June 1994; the mission ended early. ## 6 · Eden Project, Bodelva, near St Austell, Cornwall

**Claim to test:** "Enclosed growing domes built in a spent clay pit, now a visitor and festival site (K3, K13)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** The biomes sit in the former Bodelva/Carvear china-clay pit (worked from the 1820s, "all but exhausted" by the mid-1990s, sold to Eden). It opened 17 March 2001 with Millennium Commission (National Lottery) money, runs a summer concert series in the pit (Eden Sessions, since 2002), a winter ice rink, and founded a national community-meal day (The Big Lunch, 2009). Narrowing: it is an **educational charity and visitor attraction, not a food producer**; visitor numbers have fallen from 1 million+ (2019) to 543,000 (year to March 2025), with repeated job cuts. Since June 2023 a ~5 km deep geothermal well heats the biomes.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Bodelva, near St Austell, mid-Cornwall, in the china-clay district. SOURCED (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eden_Project).
- **The pit:** originally two pits, Bodelva (from 1825) and Carvear (working by 1827); Bodelva worked through into Carvear in 1955; clay often discolored pink, limiting markets; used for newsprint; "by the mid-1990s the pit was all but exhausted"; it stayed in production until sold to Eden. SOURCED (wwjohnston.net family-history page on Bodelva/Carvear, read 2026-10-29; secondary). Wikipedia: in use "for over 160 years." SOURCED. [K9]
- **What it is:** two main enclosures of ETFE inflated cells on geodesic steel frames (Grimshaw Architects): Rainforest Biome 55 m high, 100 m wide, 200 m long; Mediterranean Biome 35 m high, 65 m wide, 135 m long. Construction from 1998; opened 17 March 2001; conceived by Tim Smit with Jonathan Ball. SOURCED (Wikipedia).
- **Structure and money:** educational charity, The Eden Trust (charity no. 1093070); income from tickets, memberships, donations; part-financed by EU Objective One. SOURCED (edenproject.com "our funding" page). Millennium Commission total £56 million. SOURCED (Hits Radio Cornwall, 2026-03-05). Total build cost: sources give £80 million (RGS schools page, read) and £141 million (snippet, UNVERIFIED); an initial £37.5 million Millennium grant (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Visitors:** over 1 million in 2019 (Wikipedia, SOURCED); 604,000 (year to 31 March 2024) and 543,000 (year to 31 March 2025); more than 25 million since 2001. SOURCED (Irish News / PA, 2025-09-26; Hits Radio 2026-03-05).
