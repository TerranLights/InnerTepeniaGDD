## 1 · #12 Dawson Creek, British Columbia (Mile 0 of the Alaska Highway)

**Claim to test:** "A town whose identity rests on being the origin point of a long highway, marked by a civic milepost and a traveler culture (K2)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with corrections.** (a) The town's own tourism plan states that Mile "0" is "foundational for the area's tourism industry" and that the highway is "the primary trip motivator for touring visitors" (Dawson Creek Community Tourism Plan, Draft 2, March 2024, SOURCED). (b) But identity does **not** rest on the highway alone: the city's own investment guide says there are more businesses in the city servicing agriculture "than any other single industry," and oil and gas, construction and trucking are the largest employers (Community Profile 2024, SOURCED). The town was founded as a **railhead** (1931), not a highway town; the highway came 11 years later. (c) The "civic milepost" is real but is a **1946 civic replacement placed at the town center, not at the true start of the road**; the true start is marked separately by a stone cairn. The traveler culture is real but short-stay: in 2023 just over 70% of visitor-center visitors were same-day visitors (tourism plan, SOURCED).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** northeastern British Columbia, Peace River Country, about 72 km SE of Fort St. John and 134 km NW of Grande Prairie, Alberta; elevation 665 m. (Wikipedia "Dawson Creek," read 2026-11-20, SOURCED.)
- **Population:** 12,323 (2021 census), up 1.2% from 12,178 (2016); 10,740 in 2001. Peace River Regional District 61,532 (2021). (City of Dawson Creek, *Community Profile & Investment Guide 2024*, Quick Facts table, read from the PDF, SOURCED.) Nearly 16% of the 2016 population belonged to an Aboriginal group (Métis or First Nations) (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Size:** land area 26.72 km² (Wikipedia infobox) or 22.32 km² (tourism plan 2024). Sources differ; both SOURCED.
- **Climate:** subarctic (Dfc), bordering humid continental; January average high −7.2 °C / low −19.0 °C; July 22.2 °C / 8.9 °C (Community Profile 2024, SOURCED). Record low −49.2 °C; record high 38.9 °C on 2021-06-29; "very strong winds year-round" (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **What it is:** a farm-service and resource-service city, the terminus ("end of the line") of the Northern Alberta Railways from 1931, and the start of the Alaska Highway since 1942. Hub of four routes: Highway 97 north (Alaska Highway), Highway 97 SW (John Hart Highway, opened 1952), Highway 2 to Grande Prairie, Highway 49 to Peace River. (Wikipedia; On This Spot walking tour stop 33, SOURCED.)
- **Defining feature:** Mile "0" of the Alaska Highway, which runs 2,237 km to Delta Junction, Alaska (tourism plan 2024, SOURCED). [K+ origin-point identity] [P3]

**History in brief (all SOURCED unless marked):**
- The Peace River region was home to Sekani and Beaver (Dane-zaa) peoples and later Cree (City of Dawson Creek, "Our History," read 2026-11-20). The town is named for the creek, named for geologist George Mercer Dawson, whose survey party passed in August 1879.
- A first hamlet grew ~1919–1930 about 1.5 miles SW of today's center; settlers were "mainly British war veterans." When the Northern Alberta Railways put its terminus to the NE, nearly every building was **dragged across the fields** to the new townsite in the mild winter of 1930–31 (the Co-op store took about two and a half weeks on rollers: On This Spot stop 7). The golden spike was driven 1930-12-29; the first regular passenger train arrived "at 6:30 pm, Thursday, January 15, 1931." Five grain elevators stood at the new townsite. (City "Our History"; South Peace Historical Society [SPHS] "History of the N.A.R. Park," calverley.ca.) [K9 relocation]
- Village charter 1936-05-26 (pop. >500); city 1958. 518 inhabitants at the 1941 census (SPHS "The Alaska Military Highway," calverley.ca 03-015).
- **1942 boom:** construction began in March 1942; the first 35th Engineers arrived 1942-03-09; Dawson Creek was HQ of the southern (largest) sector under Maj. Gen. James A. O'Connor; seven regiments, ~11,000 soldiers; after the Japanese invasion of the Aleutians (June 1942) the US Public Roads Administration hired 5,000 civilian contractors, three-quarters Canadian (On This Spot, "Peace Country & the Second World War," SOURCED). Peak resident population ~5,000 (SPHS 03-015) or "a rip-snorting frontier town of ten thousand" (1944 description quoted by On This Spot). The figures differ.
- **Pay gap:** Canadian workers earned $0.86/hour vs $1.57 for Americans; on a 77-hour week a Canadian trucker made $69, an American $136; turnover ran up to 100%; the Canadian government put its workers in separate camps (On This Spot WWII page, SOURCED). [K7, K9] [SM: man versus society]
- **1943 explosion:** on 1943-02-13 a fire in a downtown livery barn holding US Army road-building supplies, including dynamite, exploded and destroyed a whole block; five killed and 150 injured (Wikipedia; City "Our History"). The walking tour gives "164 injured" and says a fire ignited "a truck carrying TNT" (On This Spot stop 9). There was no town water system; martial law was declared and stock was piled on the golf course; US Army personnel saved the Co-op main store (SPHS 05-011, SOURCED). Merchants rebuilt in permanent materials, which the city says is why few "western" storefronts survive (City "Our History"). Cause never established (search snippets; UNVERIFIED). [K9] [SM: competence under pressure]
- Natural gas arrived in 1950, the first BC town to get it; oil was struck at Fort St. John in 1951 and that town's oil industry later outgrew Dawson Creek's (On This Spot stop 37, SOURCED). Population ~7,500 by 1956 (stop 14).

### B. What else exists there
**Economy (SOURCED, Community Profile 2024 unless noted):**
- Agriculture: the Peace Region has >2 million acres in production and >1,300 farms producing "nearly $200 million" a year; it grows almost 90% of BC's grain, 95% of its canola, 90% of its dry field peas and 95% of its forage seed. Groups listed: Dawson Creek Exhibition Association, BC Grain Producers Association, BC South Peace River Stockmen's Association, Peace Region Forage Seed Association, South Peace Feeder Cooperative, South Peace BC Bred Heifer Cooperative Association, Sweetwater Parkland Farmers Institute. [K1, K14] [P8]
- Labor: the largest employment groups are construction, retail trade, other private services, food and beverage services, truck transportation and oil and gas extraction with its support services. NE BC unemployment 4.3% in 2021 (vs 6.5% province). Dawson Creek's own unemployment 9.8% in 2021, down from 12.1% in 2016 (search summary of city workforce page; UNVERIFIED).
- Oil and gas: the BC portion of the Montney play holds an estimated 271 trillion cubic feet of gas (search summary of city page; UNVERIFIED). Workers are housed partly in industrial camps; Encana's Sunset Prairie Lodge, ~55 km away, was planned for up to 2,500 people, later halved (Times Colonist / BIV snippets; UNVERIFIED). Officials have described transient workers as a "shadow" population straining hospitals; Northern Health once estimated 1,636 work camps in the region against an official regional population of 66,167 (BIV 2014 snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K4, K7]
- Wind: Bear Mountain Wind Park, 102 MW, called the first fully operational wind park in BC, a partnership including a local co-operative and AltaGas (Community Profile 2024, SOURCED). Northern Lights College runs wind-turbine maintenance training on a 150 kW turbine that had run in a Danish wind park (NLC news snippets; UNVERIFIED; program reported both suspended and resumed).
- Trucking: the sector grew from highway construction; one firm began when its founder came in 1942 "with one truck"; Dawson Creek carriers ran terminals at Fort St. John, Fort Nelson, Watson Lake and Whitehorse (SPHS trucking articles, snippets; UNVERIFIED). Super-B long-combination trucks are allowed from Alberta as far as Dawson Creek; CN freight links to Fort Nelson, Prince Rupert and Vancouver (Community Profile 2024, SOURCED). [K5, K14]
- Retail leakage to Grande Prairie, Alberta, is a named concern (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Since the 1970s Fort St. John took much industrial development and Grande Prairie became the commercial hub (search summary; UNVERIFIED). [K9]
- Tourism: 18 hotels/motels with 986 rooms; RV parks with ~350–360 sites (Mile "0" Campsite 80, Northern Lights RV Park 92, Tubby's RV Park & Car Wash 96, etc.). Business travel "motivated primarily by resource extraction" is "a major visitor segment," and hotels catering to it lose occupancy when the resource cycle turns down. The Municipal Regional District Tax (hotel tax) has **not** been adopted; the City funds Tourism Dawson Creek directly. (Tourism plan 2024, SOURCED.) [K8]

**Institutions (SOURCED unless noted):** City council; RCMP policing since 1948; fire brigade organized 1938 (On This Spot stop 17). Northern Lights College, est. 1975 on the former RCAF airfield site, with its regional administration in Dawson Creek (Wikipedia; On This Spot WWII page). School District 59: four elementary schools, Dawson Creek Secondary; Notre Dame School (Catholic, founded 1944 by the Sisters of Providence); Mountain Christian School. Dawson Creek and District Hospital is being replaced; the new hospital is targeted to open in early 2027, emergency exam spaces rising from 10 to 15 (Northern Health project page, snippet; UNVERIFIED). Nawican Friendship Center (Indigenous): family wellness, youth resource center, street outreach (BC HelpStartsHere listings, snippets; UNVERIFIED).

**Infrastructure and how goods arrive (SOURCED, Community Profile 2024):** Dawson Creek Regional Airport (5,000-foot runway; no scheduled flights; scheduled flights are 50 minutes' drive away at Fort St. John; a "lifeline" for BC Emergency Health Services patient transfers). BC Transit municipal buses; two taxi companies. Passenger rail ran 1931–1974 (Wikipedia). [K5]

**Water (K1, K5, K6, K9) — the town's live shared problem (SOURCED):**
- The only drinking-water source is the Kiskatinaw River. The city reports persistent drought since 2020; in January 2024 the river froze and intake stopped (Level 4 drought); a second Level 4 came in summer 2025; a State of Local Emergency was declared 2025-10-14 and later ended (City "Drought Emergency" page, read 2026-11-20). The Tyee (2025-09-26) reported the river lost a further 40% of flow between late August and early September 2025; reserves last ~150 days without pumping; the city applied for a 36-inch pipeline from the Peace River near Taylor (>50 km), up to 10 million m³/yr, est. $100 million. Average water use per Montney fracking well rose from 7,000 to 22,000 m³ between 2012 and 2019.
- Earlier answer to the same problem: after a 2006 drought the city built a Reclaimed Water Facility with Shell Canada that sells treated wastewater to industry (4,500 m³/day; Shell up to 3,400 m³/day, piped to its gas operations) and stopped industry from using potable water; 23 reclaimed-water sales contracts by spring 2022; a 1-million-m³ raw-water reservoir (2017) gives 270 days of off-river storage (Community Profile 2024).
- **Rules (K6):** staged water-conservation measures; Stage 2 limited lawn watering and industrial bulk use; Stage 3 (from 2025-09-11) barred hauling bulk water for fracking, including filling ponds and dugouts (city news and search summaries; UNVERIFIED in detail).

**Who lives and works there:** settler farm families (British veterans first, 1919–30); wartime newcomers who stayed; oil-patch and construction workers, many rotating; Indigenous residents (Métis, Cree, Dane-zaa and others); recent immigrant communities (a 2026 multicultural festival listed Brazil, India, Norway, the Philippines and Ukraine plus Indigenous entries: Energeticcity 2026-06-20 snippet; UNVERIFIED). Nearby, 518 Sudeten German refugees, mostly anti-Nazi Social Democrats, were settled at Tupper (now Tomslake), ~20 miles SW, in 1938–39 (City "Our History"; Wikipedia "Tomslake" snippets). [K4] [P9]

### C. Cultural locations (names; SOURCED unless marked)
- **Mile "0" Post** (10th Street and 102nd Avenue): first post ~4 ft high at 8th Street and Alaska Avenue; hit by a car and broken in 1946; the Jaycees had member Ellis Gislason, a sign painter, build a ~10-ft wooden replacement, presented to the city on **Christmas Day 1946** and set at the town center ("not the exact start of the highway"). It was not fixed down: on Halloween youths stole it, put hay on it, tried to burn it, once swapped it for a wooden privy, and it was found under the Pouce Coupe bridge. Replaced by a metal post fixed in place in the early 1960s. It became "the traditional place for tourists to have their pictures taken before setting out." (SPHS, "The story behind the Dawson Creek milepost," calverley.ca 05-006.) [K2, K6]
- **Miniature mileposts:** Gislason made a replica for a visiting national Jaycees president; the village commission and most civic groups adopted **giving miniature mileposts to visiting dignitaries**; he made >75,000 over ~30 years (smallest 7.5 in., local wood and rock), ~50 cents profit each; a rival firm sold imported Japanese copies (same source). [K2, K8, K14]
- **Mile "0" Cairn** at the east end of NAR Park, marking the start of the highway; the City's 2024 guide calls it "the stone cairn that marks the beginning." Erection date NOT FOUND (one blog says late 1950s; UNVERIFIED). Next to it, SPHS's "Dawson Creek Historical Site" plaque at 900 Alaska Avenue gives "11,000 troops and 16,000 civilians," "133 bridges, 8,000 culverts" and "1,523 miles" (Waymarking transcription, SOURCED as transcription).
- **Surveyor statue** at the Mile Zero traffic circle: 16-ft scrap-metal sculpture by local artist Karl Mattson of a surveyor pointing down the highway, unveiled 2004-04-27 during BC Arts Week (SPHS bn07-69). A 75th-anniversary (2017) sculpture was made from a 6-tonne 1942 ditch-digging machine (CBC snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **NAR Park:** the 1931 NAR station, bought by SPHS for $1 (1977), restored with a $250,000 BC Lottery grant, opened as the **Station Museum** 1983-06-17; it also holds the Visitor Information Center. **Dawson Creek Art Gallery** in a 1949 Alberta Wheat Pool elevator annex the city bought for $1 and moved in November 1982 (opened 1983-10-21; run by the South Peace Art Society under lease). Weekly Farmers' Market in the park (SPHS "History of the N.A.R. Park"; City heritage page). Sources disagree on the elevator's age (1949 vs "before the railway").
- **Walter Wright Pioneer Village** in **Mile "0" Rotary Lake Park** (1901 Alaska Highway, at the Alaska–Hart junction): buildings set up 1969, opened 1976, renamed 1981 for SPHS president Walter Wright, moved in the early 1990s; SPHS owns the historic buildings and leases them to the Mile 0 Park Society. Hosts the **Annual Pioneer Village Jamboree** (June) and the **Multicultural Fusion** festival (City heritage page; tourism plan).
- **Alaska Highway House** (downtown, next to the post): closed 2021; artifacts moved to the Pioneer Village (City heritage page).
- **Calvin Kruk Center for the Arts** (former federal building/post office; completed 2015), housing the **Kiwanis Performing Arts Center** (since 1986), a café, artists' guilds and the SPHS archives (City heritage page). **Unchagah Hall**, 630-seat theater in the high school.
- **Downtown murals:** "1940s Street Scene" (students, 2000, designed by Inez Demuynck), "People of the Community" (2001, Calvin Kruk), "Memory Lane" (Shelly Mowat, Holly Squires), "Trail of '42" (2003) (On This Spot).
- **Historic businesses:** Alaska Hotel (1931 as the Dew Drop Inn; "a major social hub for US soldiers"; burned 2012); Mile 0 Hotel / Blue Bird Café; Lawrence Meat Market (1931, family-owned); Bill's News (1949); New Palace Hotel opened 1943 by Mah Show and Mah Fong (On This Spot). Dawson Co-operative Union (farmers' co-op founded 1921; "4th largest in Western Canada" decades later per SPHS snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Kiskatinaw Bridge,** ~28–32 km north on the Old Alaska Highway: a ~58 m timber trestle on a banked nine-degree curve, built in nine months by a Canadian contractor, "one of the first curved wooden bridges built in Canada" (BC Parks page, SOURCED). Listed as closed for disrepair in the 2024 tourism plan (SOURCED).
- **South Peace Community Multiplex** (2008): Ovintiv Events Center (4,500 seats, 6,500 for concerts; managed by OVG360), Ken Borek Aquatic Center, Lakota Agriplex (110 indoor stalls, rodeo arena). In 2019 events supported 69 local jobs and $6.7M visitor spending; concerts were 59% of event visitor spending (tourism plan; Community Profile 2024).
- **Media:** CJDC radio since December 1947 (890 AM); the *Peace River Block News* (from Rolla 1930) and its successors ended with the closure of the *Dawson Creek Mirror* and *Alaska Highway News* in October 2023 by Glacier Media, leaving the region without a local paper (On This Spot stop 32; CTV/Global search summaries; UNVERIFIED in detail). [K9]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The railhead created the town; the highway made it a **base camp** and paymaster (the CIBC branch became US Treasury depository 1942-03-06: SPHS 03-015) and seeded the trucking industry (SOURCED / UNVERIFIED as above). [K14]
- The highway start produced a **civic marker economy**: the post, the cairn, the surveyor statue, the miniature-milepost gift custom, the photo ritual, the Mile "0" names on a park, a campground, a hotel, a car club and a market (sources above). [K2, K8]
- The traveler flow is seasonal and brief: June–August peak; >40% of visitor-center check-ins in 2022 were from the US/Mexico; ~39,456 visitors in 1998 and ~40,000/yr in 2004 (SPHS and Government of Canada snippets; UNVERIFIED). In 2026 the main center counted 922 (Apr), 2,148 (May), 3,282 (June) plus a mobile center opened May 13 (Tourism Dawson Creek July 2026 newsletter, snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K7]
- Highway construction and the following settlement opened Dane-zaa land: Doig River First Nation says the highway "brought in settlers and developers, and our lifestyle changed dramatically"; the 1914 Montney reserve was surrendered in 1944–45 so it could be sold to Veterans Affairs for returning soldiers; members lost traplines after 1911 trapline registration; a 1978 suit over lost oil and gas royalties led to a 1995 Supreme Court of Canada ruling that the Crown had breached its fiduciary duty (Doig River FN "History," read 2026-11-20, SOURCED). Parks Canada's plaque text says the road "changed ... Native ways of life" (Parks Canada DFHD 1677, SOURCED). [K9] [P9]
- **Segregation (K4, K6, K9):** Black engineer regiments (93rd, 95th, 97th, 388th battalion) were, by US Army policy, kept out of towns: the 95th, arriving in late May 1942 (~1,200 men), was held in a camp away from Dawson Creek and "banned from entering Dawson Creek itself"; Black units were "typically not permitted to use heavy machinery" (NPS "Black History in the Last Frontier: Alaska Highway Engineers," SOURCED; On This Spot WWII page, SOURCED; Washington City Paper snippet, UNVERIFIED). Their history is now shown at the Visitor Center, gallery and Pioneer Village (CBC 2023 snippet; the CBC page returned 403, NOT READ).

### E. Structural features
- **Two economies, two calendars:** an agricultural year (seeding, harvest, the August Exhibition) and a resource cycle (winter drilling season; hotels "serve oil-patch workers in winter": Wikipedia). Tourism adds a summer layer. [K7]
- **Short-stay pass-through:** >70% same-day visitors; the town calls itself a regional "base camp" for Tumbler Ridge, Chetwynd, Hudson's Hope, Pouce Coupe, Taylor (tourism plan). [K5]
- **Event economy built in on purpose (K3):** 15 annual events, two "signature": the **Mile "0" Cruisers Summer Cruise / Show and Shine** (second weekend of July; 30th in 2026; cars across downtown blocks with prizes at the Mile "0" marker; snippets UNVERIFIED) and the **Dawson Creek Exhibition, Stampede and Parade** (second weekend of August; agricultural fair, pro rodeo, chuckwagons, Indian relay racing; ~50,000 attendance in 2023, snippet UNVERIFIED; first stampede 2–3 July 1922, first "Exhibition and Fall Fair" 7–8 August 1953 per SPHS snippets, UNVERIFIED; the 2026 edition was called the 104th). Others: Family Day (Feb); **Spring Breakup Rodeo and Dance** (April; Dawson Creek Exhibition Association, CPRA-sanctioned, with a post-rodeo dance; named for the thaw season); South Peace Oilmen's Lobster Fest (May); South Peace Art Society Auction Gala (May); Peace Art Festival, Indigenous Peoples Day, Pioneer Village Jamboree, Multicultural Fusion Fest (June); Dare to Dream Music Festival (July); Oktoberfest (October); a Rotary and South Peace Oilmen's Christmas Tree Light-Up (tourism plan 2024 list, SOURCED; others snippets). [K2, K3, K8]
- **Arts from volunteer societies and donated buildings:** almost every cultural venue is a $1 or surplus building (station, elevator, post office, school) run by a society (SPHS 1952, South Peace Art Society, Kiwanis, Mile 0 Park Society) (sources above). [K13, K8]
- **Water scarcity vs industry** is the present-day shared problem (above). [K1]
- **Local news loss** (2023). [K9]

**K6 summary (everyday behavior rules):** (1) Historical: US Army policy barred Black soldiers from the town (1942); martial law after the 1943 explosion. (2) Current: staged water restrictions, with Stage 3 aimed at fracking water hauling; liquor at events needs a council letter before a provincial Special Event Liquor Permit (City community-event permit page, snippet UNVERIFIED); bylaw enforcement says its aim is "compliance through education" (snippet UNVERIFIED). **K11 (speech, loose):** English throughout; Indigenous languages of the area include Beaver (Dane-zaa) and Cree (Doig River FN; City history). No further note.

## 2 · #13 Delta Junction, Alaska

**Claim to test:** "A small town at the official end of one highway and its junction with another, defined by where roads meet."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with additions.** Delta Junction sits at "the Triangle," where Milepost 1422 of the Alaska Highway meets Milepost 266 of the Richardson Highway (City/visitor page, SOURCED). The state labor department writes that Delta Junction and Tok "both began as highway construction camps" and are "organized around travel," offering gas stations, mechanics, motels and convenience stores "rare in most of Alaska" (Alaska Economic Trends, Sept. 2025, SOURCED). **Additions:** (a) the "official end" is a negotiated status: an "End of the Alaska Highway" monument first stood in Fairbanks (erected by the Fairbanks business community) and was moved to Delta Junction around 1991, on the reasoning that the Richardson Highway already linked Delta and Fairbanks when the Alaska Highway arrived; Fairbanks is still called the "unofficial end" at Historic Mile 1520 (ExploreNorth "Alaska Highway signs," SOURCED). (b) The town is defined as much by a **military post** (Fort Greely), **gold mining** (Pogo), a **state farm project** and a **bison herd** as by the road meeting.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Southeast Fairbanks Census Area, Interior Alaska, on the Delta River near its meeting with the Tanana, about 99 mi (160 km) SE of Fairbanks (Wikipedia "Delta Junction," SOURCED).
- **Population:** 703 (1970), 945 (1980), 652 (1990), 840 (2000), 958 (2010), 918 (2020), 977 (2024 est.) (Alaska Economic Trends, Sept. 2025, table, read from PDF, SOURCED). Big Delta (CDP) 421 in 2024 (same). Surrounding Deltana CDP covers 562 sq mi of land (ExploreNorth "Deltana history," SOURCED).
- **Climate:** subarctic (Dwc), bordering semi-arid; record low −63 °F (1947-01-30), record high 92 °F (1969-06-15); average snowfall 54 in. (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Deltana: January average low −11 °F, July average high 69 °F; 12 in. precipitation (ExploreNorth Deltana, SOURCED).
- **Government:** city incorporated 1960-12-19 (Wikipedia, SOURCED; "second-class city" per a snippet, UNVERIFIED); mayor–council; the region is in Alaska's unorganized borough area (Census Area is a statistical unit only: Alaska Economic Trends footnote, SOURCED). Combined sales tax 0% (Avalara snippet, UNVERIFIED). Property-tax status NOT FOUND.
- **Defining feature:** the end of the Alaska Highway and its junction with the Richardson Highway. [K+ endpoint identity] [P3, P5]

**History (SOURCED unless marked):**
- Tanana Athabascan people occupied the site through most of the 19th and early 20th centuries (ExploreNorth "Delta history"). The Big Delta landing was a chum-salmon fish camp and Athabascan wintering place; Lt. Henry Allen found Native structures there in the late 1800s; nearby archaeological sites date back more than 11,000 years (Alaska Science Forum, "Roadhouse provides rest along trail," 2017, SOURCED). The Delta-Goodpaster band's area covered Big Delta, the Delta River and the Goodpaster River (Wikipedia "Tanana Athabaskans" snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- 1904: telegraph station (Wikipedia). The Army's Trans-Alaska Military Road (by 1901) and, after the 1902 Tanana Valley gold strike, the Valdez–Fairbanks Trail (completed for wagons 1910; motor traffic in the 1920s) brought **roadhouses** (ExploreNorth; Alaska Economic Trends).
- 1928: bison from the National Bison Range, Montana, released for hunters (23 animals per Wikipedia; 19, 23 or 28 in other sources); the area was renamed Buffalo Center in 1927 (ExploreNorth Deltana). Sources differ on numbers and year.
- 1942: Alaska Highway construction; an airfield (Big Delta Army Air Field, later Allen Army Airfield) served Lend-Lease aircraft ferried to the Soviet Union; it later became **Fort Greely**, a cold-weather training post, "the coldest installation in the country" (Alaska Economic Trends; search summaries of army.mil, UNVERIFIED for details).
- 1946 dairy farm; 1953 homesteaders brought beef cattle; Trans-Alaska Pipeline construction 1974–77 brought "a dramatic upswing to the population and economy" (ExploreNorth).
- 1962–1972: Alaska's first and only nuclear power plant (the SM-1A) heated and powered Fort Greely; planned demolition by 2029 (Alaska Economic Trends, SOURCED). [K+]
- 1990s: Fort Greely declined after the Cold War; placed on the 1995 base-closure list; plans for a private prison "never materialized"; reopened in 2001 by the Alaska Army National Guard for missile defense (Alaska Economic Trends; Wikipedia). 2002–2005 contractor boom (Boeing, Bechtel) (Wikipedia). [K9]

### B. What else exists there
**Economy (SOURCED unless marked):**
- "Military facilities, mining, agriculture, and tourism" (Wikipedia).
- **Fort Greely missile defense:** Ground-based interceptors run by the Alaska Army National Guard's 49th Missile Defense Battalion, which also provides site security; soldiers recruited nationally to permanent active-Guard roles (army.mil and National Guard snippets; UNVERIFIED). Interceptor counts differ by date (18, 30, 44) (snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Gold:** Pogo Mine just north of Delta Junction and Manh Choh near Tok "together employ more than 1,000 people"; mines lift the area's average wage to $7,655/month in 2024, second only to the North Slope (Alaska Economic Trends, SOURCED). Pogo work rotations posted as 2 weeks on/1 off, 4 on/2 off, 28/14 or 15/13, living at a company camp (250-person upper camp and 203-person lower camp per state DNR), with buses to and from Fairbanks, Delta Junction and Tok (job postings and DNR snippets; UNVERIFIED). [K7, K4]
- **Agriculture:** state Delta Agricultural Project I (August 1978): 22 farms averaging ~2,700 acres sold by **lottery**; Delta II (1982): 15 more farms. Barley was meant to be exported through Valdez; "the railroad never came" and the project was defunded after a change of governors (Wikipedia, SOURCED); "inconsistent yields eventually sunk the pilot project. Debt piled up, and many farmers had to sell or walk away" (Alaska Economic Trends, SOURCED). Legacy: >80,000 acres developed (alaska-highway.org "Farming," SOURCED); Alaska Flour Company (Wrigley family; stone-ground barley flour) and Alaska Range Dairy, the state's only Grade A dairy (Alaska Economic Trends). Delta Meat & Sausage processed reindeer, bison, elk and yak (trade article snippet; status uncertain; UNVERIFIED). [K9, K12, K14] [SM: man versus society]
- **Bison and farms (K1, K9):** the 70,000-acre **Delta Bison Range** was created in 1980 "to confine the bison and keep them out of the barley fields" (ExploreNorth Deltana, SOURCED). Bison damage dropped after forage was planted on the range in 1985; a Delta Bison Working Group recommended raising the hunt-application fee to fund damage mitigation; a 2013–14 bill raised it to $20 (ADF&G/legislative snippets; UNVERIFIED). The permit drawing drew ~23,700 applicants in 2015; residents may draw once in ten years, nonresidents once in a lifetime (ADN 2015 and ADF&G 2024 snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Cost of living:** Southeast Fairbanks Census Area median home price $262,800 in 2024; residents drive to Fairbanks to buy at non-rural prices (Alaska Economic Trends, SOURCED). [K5]

**Institutions and services (SOURCED unless marked):** City Hall; Delta Community Library; landfill; airport (D66) (City homepage). Delta/Greely School District: Delta Elementary, Delta Junior/Senior High, Fort Greely School, New Horizons, a homeschool program; ~830–970 students by source (snippets; UNVERIFIED). [K1, K5, K8, K9]
**Media:** *Delta Wind* weekly, from 1992 (Library of Congress record via snippet; UNVERIFIED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Two travel routes stacked on one spot: the older Valdez–Fairbanks wagon trail made the **roadhouse** culture (two roadhouses now museums); the 1942 highway made a **construction camp** that became the town; the junction then attracted the airfield, the pipeline crossing and the visitor economy (sources above). [K5]
- The "end" is administered as a **certificate and a photo marker** by a farm organization, which ties the endpoint ritual to the farm community (City visiting page). [K2, K8]
- **Segregation on the northern route (K4, K6, K9):** the 97th Engineers (Black enlisted men under white officers) landed at Valdez in April 1942, worked north and then built 130 miles of what is now the Tok Cut-off; Gen. Simon Buckner wanted Black troops "kept far enough from the settlements" (Washington City Paper snippet; UNVERIFIED). On 1943-03-29 at the Gerstle River camp (between Delta and Tok) a white lieutenant ordered ten Black soldiers into an unheated truck for a 130-mile ride to Fairbanks at about −34 to −36 °F; hesitation led to mutiny convictions and long sentences at hard labor (BlackPast and Yukon News snippets; both pages refused or 404, NOT READ; UNVERIFIED). In 2017 Alaska set aside October 25 to honor the Black soldiers (NPS, SOURCED).
- The highway's construction camps seeded roadside Athabascan settlement (Dot Lake) and drew survivors of the Healy Lake epidemic to highway villages (above). [P9]

## 3 · #11 "Parting of the Ways," Wyoming (Oregon Trail)

**Claim to test:** "A trail junction remembered precisely as the point where travelers had to choose between two routes." (Care: a site, not a town; use for the decision-point idea.)
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with two corrections.** (a) "Remembered precisely" is ironic: the 1956 state marker on Wyoming Highway 28 stands at the **wrong place** (now called "False Parting"); the true fork lies ~9.5–10 miles west on open BLM land, and in 1988 the Oregon-California Trails Association placed a second marker beside the first to say so (WyoHistory.org "Parting of the Ways"; NPS "True/False Parting of the Ways," SOURCED). Researchers also identify an earlier first fork (1844–45) a few miles away, just west of the Big Sandy crossing (trailresearch.org, SOURCED). (b) The choice was **not final**: "Travelers could rejoin the trails farther on" (WyoHistory, SOURCED); the Sublette Cutoff rejoined the main Oregon Trail at the Bear River near Cokeville (NPS, SOURCED).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** ~18 miles west of the Continental Divide crossing at South Pass, on an open sagebrush plain; true site GPS 42.257561, −109.228253, about 15 miles NE of Farson and 3 miles north of WY-28; false site 42.284464, −109.05896 (NPS place pages, SOURCED). Sweetwater County (NRHP nomination; Sublette County per one marker listing).
- **What it is:** the fork where the Sublette (or Greenwood) Cutoff left the main Oregon Trail. Right fork: west across the "Little Colorado Desert" toward Fort Hall/Oregon (the cutoff). Left fork: southwest to Fort Bridger (est. 1843 by Jim Bridger and Louis Vasquez), and from 1846 on to Salt Lake City and California (NPS true parting, SOURCED). The site is on the Oregon, California, Mormon Pioneer and Pony Express National Historic Trails (same).
- **Physical remains (NRHP nomination, BLM, 1973, listed 1976; ~5 acres; read from PDF, SOURCED):** the Oregon Trail branch "has been bladed some years ago," with ruts visible to the south; the Sublette Cutoff "has not been improved and is probably near its original condition." A small reddish sandstone ~15 in. high, carved with arrows "F, Bridger" and "S, Cut Off," was placed by L.C. Bishop and Paul Henderson (not historic). The 1988 OCTA quartzite post reads "←Fort Bridger S. Cut-off→" (HMdb snippet; page refused; UNVERIFIED).
- **Defining feature:** a choice between "a long way" (the Fort Bridger route) and "a hard way" (the cutoff) (geologist Keith Meldahl, quoted by NPS, SOURCED). [K+ decision point] [P5]

**The two routes (SOURCED unless marked):**
- **Cutoff:** opened 1844 by the Stephens–Townsend–Murphy party, guided by Caleb Greenwood (trailresearch.org). Saved about 46 miles and 2.5 days (WyoHistory; NRHP: "saving 46 miles"); trailresearch.org measures 111.6 vs 180.3 topographic miles (68.7 miles, 4–5 days). Cost: a waterless stretch between the Big Sandy and the Green River, given as 40.5 topographic miles (trailresearch.org), "about 50 waterless miles" (NPS) or "forty miles" with "but little grass" (Joel Palmer, 1845, quoted by WyoHistory). Livestock could go 2–3 days without water, or wagons drove continuously for 15–25 hours (trailresearch.org).
- **Naming:** called the Sublette route because Joseph E. Ware's *Emigrants' Guide to California* (St. Louis, 1849) "through an error listed the old Greenwood Cutoff west of South Pass as the 'Sublette Cutoff'" (NRHP nomination, SOURCED). Ware compiled the guide from Frémont and Bryant without having made the trip; he later attempted it, fell ill, was left by a wagon train beside the road, and died (Donner Summit Historical Society review, citing Alonzo Delano, SOURCED). [K9]
- **Use:** "About two-thirds of the emigrants that season took the Sublette Cutoff" (NPS, of 1849, SOURCED). J.M. Hixson (1849): emigrants "had mostly taken the right-hand road for the past two years" (WyoHistory). Use declined later as other cutoffs and Mormon resupply changed traffic (trailresearch.org).

### B. What else exists there (the site and its surroundings)
- **No settlement at the site.** Land: federal, BLM-managed public land; reached by unsigned BLM Road 4106 and then 4 miles on the historic trail; high-clearance 4WD recommended; "energy developers have built extra roads in the area" (NPS; WyoHistory, SOURCED). Regional land uses: livestock grazing (the Rock Springs grazing allotment covers ~2.1 million acres, with landscape health standards rated "not met — livestock" on 950,000 public acres in 2024: WyoFile snippet, UNVERIFIED); wild horses (dispute between the Rock Springs Grazing Association and BLM since 2010; 2025 court ruling: Wyoming Public Media snippets, UNVERIFIED); sage-grouse priority habitat (BLM snippets, UNVERIFIED); natural gas (the Jonah Field, ~59,600 acres between Pinedale and Farson; a 3,500-well expansion halted by a federal court in January 2026: Federal Register 1998 and Cowboy State Daily 2026 snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K9]
- **Nearest community: Farson** (Sweetwater County CDP), at US 191 / WY-28: 313 people in 2010 (242 in 2000); 6,595 ft; cold semi-arid; record low −52 °F (1978-12-31); 7.83 in. precipitation. Founded on Carey Act irrigation: John Farson, a Chicago financier, backed the Eden Irrigation and Land Company (1907); post office 1909. Farson-Eden School (K-12, Sweetwater County School District #1); county library branch; Eden-Farson Fire District (Wikipedia "Farson," SOURCED; one Wikipedia figure, a 105 °F record high dated 2026-07-14, looks doubtful and is not used). The Eden Project irrigates 17,010 acres; dams first built ~1907, rebuilt by 1959 (USBR snippet, UNVERIFIED). The **Farson Mercantile** is known as an ice-cream stop (WY State Historical Society snippet; page 404, NOT READ). A fifth-generation family put 1,709 acres of ranch land near Farson under a conservation easement (snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K1, K8]
- **Eden-Farson archaeological site:** a Native campsite where pronghorn from a communal hunt were processed: at least 12 lodges, a bone bed of at least 212 pronghorn, Protohistoric (WY SHPO and Wikipedia snippets; UNVERIFIED). Whether it was a single mass kill is debated (American Antiquity snippet).

### C. Cultural locations (within the trail corridor; SOURCED unless marked)
- **False Parting wayside** (BLM interpretive site on WY-28) with the 1956 state marker ("right to Oregon, left to Utah and California") and the 1988 OCTA marker (WyoHistory; NPS).
- **South Pass overlook** (BLM), **Oregon Buttes** (BLM Wilderness Study Area), **Pacific Springs**, **Dry Sandy Crossing** (emigrant-dug water holes still visible; a 1860–61 Pony Express relay station, traces gone), **Little Sandy Crossing** (BLM; 13 emigrant graves nearby), **Big Sandy Crossing / Big Sandy Station** (Farson) (NPS and WyoHistory snippets; UNVERIFIED except Pacific Springs/Oregon Buttes via WyoHistory).
- **Simpson's Hollow** (~10–12 mi W of Farson): on 1857-10-05 (or 10-06) Mormon militia (Nauvoo Legion) under Lot Smith burned a US Army supply train of ~26 wagons during the Utah War (NPS/Wyoming marker snippets; UNVERIFIED). Nearby marker for "graves of the unknown emigrants" (HMdb snippet).
- **South Pass City State Historic Site** (gold camp from 1867; >1,000 people and 250 buildings within a year, a few hundred by 1872; Esther Morris served as the first woman justice of the peace from 1870-02-14; 17 of 23 original structures restored; open Memorial Day to mid-October) and **Atlantic City** (NPS and Wyoming State Parks snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Mormon handcart sites** further east on the Sweetwater: **Sixth Crossing** and **Rock Creek Hollow** (Willie handcart company, 1856), run by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints with summer missionaries; ~70,000 youth "trekkers" pulled handcarts between them in 1999–2006 (USGS report summary; full page timed out; UNVERIFIED). [K2, K3]
- **Independence Rock** (further east), the emigrant signature rock: >5,000 names, carved or painted with wagon grease, tar, or buffalo grease and glue; defacing it is now unlawful (Wyoming State Parks snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K2, K6]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
**How emigrants decided (K2, K6, K14) (SOURCED: NPS true-parting page; WyoHistory):**
- "Decisions depended on the condition of livestock and reports about grass along each route."
- **Message posts at the fork:** Elisha Perkins (1849-07-25) saw "40 or 50 notes" on forked sticks; J. Goldsborough Bruff (1849-08-02) saw a notice asking travelers to pile stones against the sticks to keep them up. Messages were also written on bones and skulls along the trail (BLM National Historic Oregon Trail Interpretive Center, snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- "One company let the oxen choose the route." "Some groups with divided opinions split up, while others voted." Edward Jackson (1849-07-01): they "could scarcely choose," so they voted for the Mormon road to Salt Lake City (NPS).
- **Touts at the fork:** John Hawkins Clark (1852-07-02) met a man urging emigrants to the right-hand trail and concluded he was a ferry operator seeking business (WyoHistory). Green River ferries charged ~$7.00 per wagon on Sublette's route in 1850; rights were granted by the State of Deseret on 1850-02-12; Mormon operators bought out (or forced out) the mountain-men ferrymen in 1850 (WyoHistory/Heritage Gateways snippets; UNVERIFIED). [K8]
- John Steele (1850-07-15) described a night march "stumbling over dead oxen and horses" before taking the cutoff (WyoHistory).
- **Company government (K6):** emigrant companies commonly wrote constitutions and bylaws until about 1850 (ad hoc agreements after): elected captains and officers (one constitution re-elected them every four weeks), a guard roster in alphabetical order, trials for sleeping on guard, extra guard duty or expulsion as punishments, and in some, bans on gambling or drinking; some trains stopped every Sunday (search summaries of the Oregon Encyclopedia and Oregon Historical Quarterly vol. 16 transcriptions; the Oregon Encyclopedia page returned 403; UNVERIFIED). "Very few companies journeyed from the Missouri to the Sierras without either total division or drastic alterations in numbers or management" (scholarly article snippet; UNVERIFIED). [SM: man versus society; competence under pressure]
- **The cost of choosing wrong (K9):** the Donner–Reed group left the main company at the Little Sandy around 1846-07-20 to take Hastings's untried cutoff via Fort Bridger, on Hastings's letters and promise to guide them; Hastings had gone ahead when they arrived (Utah History to Go / Wikipedia timeline snippets; UNVERIFIED). Ware's own death (above).

**Shoshone lands (K9, P9) (SOURCED: WyoHistory, "Coming to Wind River: The Eastern Shoshone Treaties of 1863 and 1868"):** Eastern Shoshone bands gathered in late August–September at the head of the Sweetwater for a fall buffalo hunt and held a summer Sun Dance near Fort Bridger. Trail traffic "quintupled in 1849 and doubled again in 1850"; emigrant livestock left grass scarce and game harder to find. After troops left for the Civil War (1862), Shoshone raiders burned stage stations between the North Platte and Bear rivers. The 1863 Fort Bridger Treaty recognized ~44 million acres and paid $10,000 a year in goods for 20 years; its Article V acknowledged "the driving away and destruction of game along the routes traveled by whites" (treaty excerpt via WyoHistory snippet). The 1868 treaty made a ~3.2-million-acre Wind River reservation, now ~2.3 million acres. In January 1863 US troops attacked a Shoshone village on the Bear River, killing at least 250 people.

**Death on the trail (K9; recorded as fact only, per the deferred mortuary note):** nearly one in ten emigrants did not survive; of ~350,000 emigrants disease may have killed up to 30,000, ~10–15 deaths per mile; cholera killed more than all other ailments combined (NPS "Death on Trails," SOURCED). Single graves were often dug in the trail itself so wagons would compact them; most were left unmarked against grave robbers (same).

**Traffic:** peak 1852 (marker text: "1852, the peak year, estimated 40,000 emigrants"); 1849 ~27,000–30,000; 1850 ~52,000–55,000 (various snippets; figures disagree; UNVERIFIED).

### E. Structural features (the decision-point tradition)
- **A choice made with partial, traded information:** notes at the fork, guidebooks (some written by people who had not traveled), touts with a stake, and reports from passing travelers (above). [K14]
- **The decision unit was the company,** governed by a written or improvised compact, which often split at such points (above). [K4, K6]
- **Later cutoffs repeated the pattern:** the federal **Lander Road** (built 1858–59; left the main trail at Burnt Ranch, the ninth Sweetwater crossing; ~60 miles shorter; 13,000 users in 1859) (NPS snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Other documented "partings" on the same trail system** (tradition rule: two further places):
  1. **Raft River Parting of the Ways, Idaho** (near Delco, Cassia County): until 1849 California-bound emigrants shared the Oregon road to the Raft River, where the California Trail turned south; marker C-1 by Trails West Inc., 1999; after 1849 most used Hudspeth's Cutoff instead (HMdb snippets; UNVERIFIED; sources disagree on the exact spot).
  2. **The Dalles, Oregon:** the overland trail effectively ended here before 1846; emigrants chose between rafting the Columbia (rafts, Hudson's Bay Company bateaux or Native-built craft; deadly rapids) and, from 1846, the **Barlow Road** toll road around Mount Hood (built by Samuel Barlow with Philip Foster; ~145–150 wagons the first year); about one in four still chose the river (NPS/OCTA/Oregon Encyclopedia snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Memory work today:** the corridor is marked by BLM concrete posts placed by OCTA (BLM brochure snippet), state markers, and church historic sites; youth handcart treks reenact the Mormon trail east of South Pass (above). The official 1993 Oregon Trail sesquicentennial wagon train ran only in Idaho and Oregon (CS Monitor 1993 snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K3]

**K6 summary:** emigrant company constitutions (above); modern: defacing Independence Rock is unlawful; motor traffic is discouraged on wagon swales (NPS asks visitors to walk at Little Sandy/Dry Sandy; snippet). No rule specific to the Parting site found.
**K11 (speech, loose):** emigrants mostly English-speaking (with others, not itemized); Shoshone (Eastern Shoshone) on the land. No further note.
**K10/K12/K13:** not applicable to an unpeopled site; Farson's Mercantile is the local gathering stop (UNVERIFIED).

## 4 · #94 Vladivostok and the kilometer 9288 marker

**Claim to test:** "A port city that marks itself as the end (or beginning) of the longest railway with a marker on the station platform, an origin-point identity like a highway's mile zero (K2)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with corrections.** (a) The marker exists: a stele "9288" on the platform of Vladivostok station, **installed in 1996** for the 100th anniversary of the Far Eastern Railway and the completion of station repairs; a column topped by a double-headed eagle, the number 9288 engraved on the four sides of a marble pedestal (Primorye regional tourism site visit-primorye.ru, SOURCED). Russian Wikipedia gives the distance to Moscow's Yaroslavsky station as **9288.2 km** (SOURCED). (b) **Both "end" and "beginning" are historically true:** construction of the Great Siberian Way was ceremonially begun **in Vladivostok** on 19 (31) May 1891 at Kuperovskaya Pad, with Tsesarevich Nicholas present (Vokrug Sveta, 2004/2022, SOURCED). Vladivostok was decided as the terminus only in 1905, after the fall of Port Arthur (same, SOURCED). (c) The railway is not the city's main identity: Vladivostok defines itself first as a **port and naval base** (main base of the Pacific Fleet) (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED). No custom tied to the marker (such as a ritual photograph) is documented beyond tourism-site mentions; NOT FOUND as a documented tradition.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** southern tip of the Muravyov-Amursky Peninsula, Primorsky Krai, on the Golden Horn Bay and Amur and Ussuri bays of the Sea of Japan.
- **Population:** 594,201 (city, 2025); 625,630 (urban okrug, 2025); peak 648,000 in 1992; 28,993 at the 1897 census (Russian Wikipedia "Владивосток," SOURCED). Primorye lost 12,500 people in the year to 1 Jan 2024, more than any other Far Eastern region (Rambler/Rosstat snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Area:** 325.99 km² (city); 590.14 km² (okrug) (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Climate:** monsoon-influenced; mean annual 5.1 °C; January −11.9 °C, August 20.0 °C; extremes +33.6 / −31.4 °C; ~840–855 mm precipitation (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **History (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED):** military post founded 20 June (2 July) 1860 by troops landed from the transport *Manjur*; first civilian settler, merchant Yakov Semyonov, 1861; city status 1880. Civil War: Czechoslovak troops overthrew Soviet power 1918-06-29; Japanese troops entered 1920-04-05; the Far Eastern Republic's army took the city in October 1922. **Closed city:** a special regime by Soviet Council of Ministers decision of 1951-08-11, effective 1952-01-01; opened by Yeltsin's decree of 1991-09-20, in force 1992-01-01 (Russian Wikipedia; Rossiyskaya Gazeta via vfokuse.mail.ru, SOURCED; Wikivoyage gives 1958, UNVERIFIED). After the decree ordinary Soviet citizens could enter without special passes; foreign ships from 1992-01-01 (RG). Free port status from 2015-10-12; Eastern Economic Forum annually from 2015 (the RG piece says 2014); capital of the Far Eastern Federal District from 2018 (RG, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** the end (and starting point) of the Trans-Siberian Railway, at a Pacific port. [K+ terminus identity] [P3, P5]

### B. What else exists there
- **Port and trade:** cargo turnover 21.2 million t (2018); foreign trade >$11.8 billion (2015) with 104 countries, chiefly China, South Korea, Japan, the US, Germany, Taiwan; ~40% of organizations in trade and services (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED). Vladivostok Sea Fishing Port container throughput record of 32,000 TEU in April 2023 (PortNews snippet; UNVERIFIED). Freight transport and construction named the fastest-growing sectors (T-Bank via TASS snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **The used-car economy (K9, K14, SM):** in the 1990s the surviving fish firms exported to Japan, and the city became a center for illegal fishing, timber smuggling and resale of Japanese cars (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED). The **Zelyony Ugol** car market opened in September 1993 (after starting on the "Stroitel" stadium); in 2003 the magazine *Za Rulyom* called it probably the largest in Russia, with 10,000–12,000 cars on weekends; car trading was said to feed "about half" of the city's residents (Za Rulyom/VZ snippets; UNVERIFIED). In 2021 it had shrunk; the flow of drivers who ferried cars west overland had nearly stopped because rail tickets and cars had become too expensive; right-hand-drive headlights needed refitting (40,000–50,000 rubles) to pass inspection (Rossiyskaya Gazeta 2021-04-08, SOURCED). A park was planned on the site in 2025 (snippet; UNVERIFIED). **December 2008 protests:** a decree of 10 Dec 2008 raised import duties on cars by up to 200%; Primorye protests began 14 Dec; about 500 gathered on the main square on 21 Dec and riot police (OMON, flown in from Moscow) detained up to 100; at least a dozen journalists were detained and beaten (Moscow Times archive, SOURCED; CPJ snippet). A regional motorists' leader said a train of Russian-made cars "would probably be set on fire" if it reached Vladivostok (Moscow Times, SOURCED).
- **Navy:** main base of the Pacific Fleet (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED). Pacific Fleet headcount NOT FOUND.
- **Industry:** Sollers car assembly, 31,800 cars in 2015 (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Education and science:** Far Eastern Federal University (FEFU), with its campus on Russky Island built for the September 2012 APEC summit (the state invested ~$20 billion in preparations; the Golden Horn bridge and the cable-stayed Russky Island bridge were built then) (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED). Far Eastern Branch of the Russian Academy of Sciences.
- **Transport and logistics (K5):** the station has 4 platforms and 8 tracks; electrified 1962; transfer to the adjacent **sea terminal** (Russian Wikipedia "Владивосток (станция)," SOURCED). Ferries to South Korea and Japan (Eastern Dream, from 2009; suspended 2019; partly resumed to Donghae 2023) (snippets; UNVERIFIED). 422,600 registered cars (2015); tram lines cut from 18.4 to 5 km and trolleybus from 45 to 4.5 km before APEC; a funicular; suburban trains inside the city (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED). Group visa-free travel with China resumed 2023-08-01 (snippet; UNVERIFIED).

### C. Cultural locations (SOURCED unless marked)
- **Vladivostok railway station:** first station designed by P.E. Bazilevsky, foundation stone 19 (31) May 1891, consecrated 1893 (or delivered 1894); rebuilt and enlarged 1910–12 by V.A. Planson in a style matched to Moscow's Yaroslavsky station, so both ends of the line look alike; from 1924 the eagles and coats-of-arms mosaics were removed or plastered over (Russian Wikipedia card; Project Baikal and Wikipedia-mirror snippets; UNVERIFIED for 1910–12 and 1924 details). On the platform: the **9288 stele** and the steam locomotive **Ea-3306** (visit-primorye.ru, SOURCED). Nearby: monuments to Yul Brynner and Lenin, the "City of Military Glory" stele (same).
- **Arseniev Museum** (State United Museum-Reserve of the History of the Far East named after V.K. Arseniev): founded 1884 as the museum of the Society for the Study of the Amur Region; opened 1890; nationalized 1925; ~600,000 items; branches include the City Museum (from 2014), the House of the official Sukhanov, the Arseniev House (snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Theaters and arts:** 4 theaters and >30 museums; Primorsky Picture Gallery (>5,000 items); **Primorsky Stage of the Mariinsky Theater** (opera and ballet, opened 2013); Gorky Primorsky Drama Theater; Vladivostok Biennale of Visual Arts since 1998 (Russian Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Millionka** (central quarter): the former Chinese quarter (named for its population); "Big" and "Little" Millionka on Kolkhoznaia, Pekinskaia and Koreiskaia streets; buildings meant for 150–200 people held 400–500 (Wilson Center, Jersild, SOURCED). Today a heritage quarter of cafes and fashion (snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Sinhanch'on Memorial** (1999) and small park on Khabarovskaya Street, at the former Korean quarter; taken over by the city in April 2023 (English Wikipedia "Shinhanchon," SOURCED).
- **Zelyony Ugol** car market (above). **Berezka** fish market, Russkaya St. 16 (gastro-guide snippets; UNVERIFIED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The rail terminus made the city the **transshipment gate** between Russia's interior and the Pacific: cars from Japan came in by sea and went west by rail or were driven west by hired drivers (RG 2021; Moscow Times 2008). [K5, K14]
- The station sits beside the sea terminal, so the end of the line is a ship-to-train interface (Russian Wikipedia station page). [P5]
- The railway ran on Moscow time even in Vladivostok, seven hours off local time (Vokrug Sveta, 2004 text, SOURCED; may have changed since). [K7] [K+]
- **Sensitive histories (K9) — reported plainly:**
  - **Chinese (Millionka), 1936:** in March 1936 an NKVD official claimed 12 Chinese in the Millionka were spying for Japan; on 17 April 1936 the Politburo decided to liquidate the quarter (snippet, UNVERIFIED for the date); the NKVD arrested and deported thousands, sending 4,202 people to China and dispersing others; in December 1936 the official reported that Big and Little Millionka "no longer exist." In 1937 about 24,600 Chinese lived in the Russian Far East, at least half in Vladivostok (Wilson Center, citing the State Archive of Primorsky Krai and E.N. Chernolutskaia, SOURCED).
  - **Koreans (Sinhanch'on), 1911–1937:** the enclave formed in 1911 after authorities cleared the older Korean area citing cholera; ~10,000 people by 1915; a Korean school (1912), Korean-language newspapers, a theater company and a People's Assembly; a base for the Korean independence movement; in April 1920 Japanese soldiers killed Korean civilians there (toll unknown). In **1937** residents were reportedly given 24 hours' notice before deportation to Central Asia; 3,408 of Vladivostok's 7,994 Koreans lived in the enclave (English Wikipedia "Shinhanchon," SOURCED). The decree was dated 21 August 1937; ~172,000 Koreans were deported from the Far East, nearly half to Uzbekistan and the rest to Kazakhstan; one estimate puts deaths in 1937–38 at ~40,000; the 1993 Supreme Soviet resolution rehabilitated them (search summaries; UNVERIFIED). From 1990 to 1998 the Korean population of Primorye grew by several thousand through migration from Central Asia (Tajik civil war, Fergana conflicts), mostly to Ussuriysk and villages rather than Vladivostok (Park Hyun-gwi, 2020, CyberLeninka, SOURCED).
  - **Closed city 1952–1992** (above).
  - **2000–01 heating and power crisis:** fuel (mazut) shortages and rotten pipes left Primorye homes cold; Vladivostok homes averaged 14 °C; in January 2000 all Vladivostok schools canceled classes because classrooms were unheated; residents of 19 blocks in Uglovoye blocked a highway; eight criminal cases opened against local officials; Governor Nazdratenko lost his post in February 2001 (Russian Life Jan/Feb 2001, SOURCED; Lenta.ru snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K1, K5]

## 5 · #15 Watson Lake Sign Post Forest, Yukon

**Claim to test:** "A roadside tradition where passing travelers leave signs from home, so a transit town's landmark is made by the people passing through (K2)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with refinements.** (a) The tradition began in 1942 with a soldier, not a tourist: Private Carl K. Lindley of the US Army's 341st Engineers (Company D), on light duty after an injury near Lower Post, was ordered to repair an army directional signpost damaged by a bulldozer and added a sign "Danville, Illinois, 2835 miles"; others followed (English Wikipedia "Sign Post Forest"; Travel Yukon; signpostforest.com, all SOURCED). (b) The landmark is made by travelers but **kept by the town**: Travel Yukon says "the town maintains a miniature maze of towering signposts"; sources say the town adds rows of posts as they fill (Travel Yukon, SOURCED; snippets for new rows, UNVERIFIED); earlier caretakers were "ordinary citizens, the Lions Club, the Hippie Club and finally the Town of Watson Lake" (HMdb marker snippet; UNVERIFIED). (c) It is a designated **Yukon Historic Site** (ceremony 5 June 2014; >75,000 signs then) (Travelweek, 2014-06-11, SOURCED).

### A. Snapshot
- **Town:** Watson Lake, Yukon, at Historic Mile 635 of the Alaska Highway, near the BC border; at the junction of the Robert Campbell Highway and the Alaska Highway; the north end of the Stewart–Cassiar Highway is ~22 km west (English Wikipedia "Watson Lake, Yukon," SOURCED). Population 1,133 (2021 census), up from 1,083 (2016) (Wikipedia/StatCan via search summary; the Wikipedia page read gives 1,133, SOURCED). Two Mile and Two and One-Half Mile Village were annexed on 2016-01-02 (same).
- **Climate:** subarctic; average January lows ~−26.7 °C; record low −58.9 °C (January 1947) (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Name:** Frank Watson, one of ~1,500 people who tried the overland Klondike route from Edmonton, stopped in the Upper Liard area in spring 1898, trapped and prospected, married a Native woman from Lower Post, and built on "Fish Lake," now Watson Lake (Yukon Nuggets, SOURCED). In 1941 airport construction began (two runways, DOT and RCAF housing); in 1942 the highway passed 10 miles from the lake and a community grew at the junction; a post office opened 1942; Watson moved his family north to Winded Lake to get away from the activity (same, SOURCED).
- **The forest:** "a couple of acres" (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Sign counts by date: >65,000 (2008, snippet), >76,000 (Aug 2010, Wikipedia), >75,000 (2014), >80,000 (end of 2017 season, signpostforest.com), >90,000 (Travel Yukon historic sites page), "almost 100,000" (Travel Yukon) or ">100,000" (Wikipedia). All SOURCED or as marked; counts are approximate.
- **Defining feature:** a landmark built by people passing through. [K2] [P6]

### B. What else exists there
- **Economy:** "the main center of the small forestry industry in Yukon"; has served the Cassiar asbestos mine (BC, closed 1992) and the Cantung tungsten mine (NWT border; mining stopped October 2015; permanent closure planned from April 2022) (Wikipedia, SOURCED; CIRNAC snippet, UNVERIFIED). Regional hub for territorial services, mining, forestry and tourism (archived Yukon communities profile, snippet; archive copy not opened per instructions). The **Yukon Forestry Summit** was held in Watson Lake on 2026-08-06 with Liard First Nation, industry, the town and the territorial government; joint Liard First Nation–government timber harvest planning at Scoby Creek, ~78 km east (Yukon government news and plan snippets; UNVERIFIED). Cassiar's ~1,100 residents scattered mostly to Terrace, Smithers and Prince George when the mine and town were liquidated and auctioned in September 1992 (CBC/Wikipedia snippets; UNVERIFIED). [K9]
- **Airport (K5):** a 1930s civilian field taken over by the RCAF in 1941 for the **Northwest Staging Route**; the log-sided **Air Terminal Building** (1942) is a Yukon Historic Site and the only NWSR terminal still in use in Yukon; the BC-Yukon Air Service hangar is called "the last military hangar of its kind in Canada"; the **Air Force Lodge**, a wartime log barracks moved into town, is the oldest building (Travel Yukon historic sites page, SOURCED; Yukon release snippet, UNVERIFIED). Scheduled service today by Air North (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Institutions:** Town of Watson Lake (mayor–council); RCMP detachment (Together for Justice Protocol with the Liard Aboriginal Women's Society, 2012: snippet, UNVERIFIED); a six-bed hospital that residents have publicly distrusted, and a coroner's finding that doctors misdiagnosed a patient as being in alcohol withdrawal when she had a bowel obstruction (CBC snippets; UNVERIFIED). **Morgan Chaddock Recreation Center**: pool (Dennis Ball Swimming Pool), ice rink, one-sheet curling on hockey ice on Friday nights, youth center, community hall (town site snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Liard First Nation (Kaska Dena):** home in Watson Lake and the neighboring Upper Liard settlement; the Two Mile area north of the core has a concentrated First Nations population; a member of the Kaska Dena Council (Wikipedia, SOURCED). ~1,400 members; administration in Watson Lake (BC government listing snippet; UNVERIFIED).

### C. Cultural locations (SOURCED unless marked)
- **Sign Post Forest** (Mile 635/634.3): road and street signs, license plates, laminated pages, dinner plates, handmade plaques (Wikipedia; Travel Yukon). - **Northern Lights Center** (Northern Lights Space and Science Center): a tilted digital dome (~15.2 m, ~100 seats) explaining the aurora's myths and science; opened 1996 or 1997 (signpostforest.com SOURCED for the dome; snippets for dates).
- **Wye Lake Park** (Parks Day events with the town) (Yukon government snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Lower Post, BC** (~a few km south of the border): the **Lower Post Indian Residential School** (1951–1975); ceremonial demolition on **30 June 2021**, 46 years to the day after it closed, attended by Daylu Dena Council, Liard First Nation, Taku River Tlingit, Tahltan and Kaska people and survivors from across the North; a cultural center with gymnasium and traditional-cooking space was funded ($11.5M federal, $1.5M BC, $538,960 Daylu Dena) and opened in 2024 (BC/Canada releases and Kaska Dena Council snippets; UNVERIFIED). Families relocated to be near the school, and many Pelly Banks members were absorbed into Liard First Nation (APTN snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K9]

## 6 · #14 Coldfoot Camp, Dalton Highway

**Claim to test:** "A truck stop that is the last services before a remote pass, where drivers check conditions and rest before committing (K5, K10)." (Care: very small; use for the "last services" role, not scale.)
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with refinements.** Coldfoot (Mile 175 of the Dalton Highway) is the last services before ~240 miles without services to Deadhorse, a stretch that crosses the Brooks Range at **Atigun Pass** (4,800 ft), where avalanches can close the highway and strand travelers for days (Coldfoot Camp FAQ; Wikipedia "Coldfoot"; Alaska DOT&PF Dalton page, all SOURCED). In 2001 Heavy Duty Trucking called it "the only place to stop" on a ~470-mile stretch between the Hilltop truck stop north of Fairbanks and Deadhorse (SOURCED; Yukon River Camp at Mile 56 also sells fuel, so this is true for full food-and-lodging service in winter, not for fuel alone). "Check conditions" is supported mainly by the CB-radio and convoy culture (below) and by the visitor center's role; no specific "check before the pass" ritual was documented. **Addition:** it is a **truckers' building** literally: truckers built it from pipeline-insulation crates and carved their names on its center pole (Coldfoot Camp history, SOURCED).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Coldfoot CDP, Yukon-Koyukuk Census Area, Alaska; on the Middle Fork of the Koyukuk River, north of the Arctic Circle; elevation 1,014 ft; Mile 175 of the Dalton Highway; ~240 miles south of Deadhorse/Prudhoe Bay; ~260 miles north of Fairbanks (Wikipedia; NPS "Coldfoot and Wiseman"; Alaska Public 2011, SOURCED).
- **Population:** 34 (2020), 10 (2010), 13 (2000) (Wikipedia "Coldfoot, Alaska," SOURCED). In fall 2011 only "a small crew of workers" lived there (Alaska Public Media, SOURCED).
- **Climate:** subarctic (Dfc); January mean −18.9 °F; record low −74 °F (−59 °C), with an unofficial −82 °F never recognized; record high 88 °F (Wikipedia, SOURCED; much of the article is tagged "citation needed"). Alaska's official record (−80 °F) was set at nearby Prospect Creek Camp on 1971-01-23 (search summaries; UNVERIFIED).
- **History (SOURCED unless marked):** founded 1898 in the Koyukuk gold rush as "Slate Creek" (gold found there 1899 per NPS); renamed Coldfoot ~1900 after prospectors who "got cold feet" about wintering and went south; at its peak "a gambling hall, two roadhouses, seven saloons, and ten 'working girls,' for whom several local creeks are named"; mail once a month from Fort Yukon by dogsled or on foot (Coldfoot Camp history). Abandoned when gold was found at Wiseman (1908; miners moved ~13 miles north by 1912), its buildings taken for building material or firewood (Coldfoot Camp; NPS). Post office 1902–1912, reopened 1984 (Wikipedia). A 1970s Trans-Alaska Pipeline construction camp (built 1970 for up to 260 highway workers and up to 450 pipeline workers, per an HMdb marker snippet, UNVERIFIED); some original shop bays survive at the DOT yard (Coldfoot Camp). On **1974-09-29** crews from the Coldfoot camp working south met crews from Prospect camp working north, completing the Haul Road in 154 days (Wight et al., BTS/ROSA P report "Access, Equity, and Safety in Rural Road Development... the Dalton Highway, 1974–2024," read from PDF, SOURCED).
- **The truck stop:** in 1981 Iditarod champion Dick Mackey parked an old school bus and sold hamburgers (or coffee) to haul-road truckers; truckers treated it as the midway stop and over time built a permanent building from packing crates that had held pipeline insulation, adding work each time they passed; to mark completion they raised a **center pole**, still behind the front desk, and engraved their names on it; it now serves "as a message board for truckers, miners, and locals" (Coldfoot Camp history, SOURCED; 1980 in one source). Mackey sold in 1990 (snippet, UNVERIFIED). **Sukakpak Inc.** of Fairbanks bought the truck stop in May 2001; it also owns Northern Alaska Tour Co. (HDT 2001-11-15, SOURCED). Today staffed under the "Work Arctic" employer brand, which also runs Yukon River Camp and Deadhorse Camp (Coolworks profile, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** the last full services before ~240 miles of empty road and a high pass. [K5] [P5]

### B. What else exists there
- **Coldfoot Camp facilities (SOURCED: camp FAQ/history; HDT; Expeditioner 2018):** Trucker's Cafe (kitchen 5 a.m.–midnight; buffets; a "Trucker's Favorite" of biscuits and sausage gravy, hash browns and eggs); the **Frozen Foot Saloon** (domestic and Fairbanks-brewed beer and wine; liquor may not be sold or brought into the saloon, but guests may drink their own in their rooms); the **Slate Creek Inn** / "Coldfoot Inn" (two long white buildings built in the 1970s as pipeline crew housing); fuel and minor tire repair; a gift shop. Groceries: none; the nearest stores are ~240 miles away in either direction; kitchen staff may sell milk, eggs, bread and coffee at higher prices because goods ship from Anchorage (FAQ). [K5, K12]
- **Government presence:** an Alaska DOT&PF maintenance camp (one of seven year-round camps on the Dalton, each covering 60–70 miles; crews work 6 a.m.–6:30 p.m. on two-weeks-on, two-weeks-off) (DOT&PF Dalton page, SOURCED; the Coldfoot camp's existence from Wikipedia, SOURCED). The **Arctic Interagency Visitor Center** (BLM, US Fish and Wildlife Service, NPS), summer only, with an 8 p.m. evening program daily, free bear-canister loans and backcountry orientations for Gates of the Arctic; a BLM winter contact station (BLM and NPS pages/snippets; partly SOURCED). Coldfoot Airport: 4,000-ft gravel strip, air taxi and general aviation (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Post office (NPS, SOURCED). A State Trooper post: NOT FOUND (searched).
- **Workforce and staff life (K4, K7):** Work Arctic camps have 5–35 staff each; housing and meals provided; all camps are drug-free workplaces with pre-employment, random and for-cause testing (including marijuana); limited cell service, intermittent internet; "'working arctic' is not for everyone" (Coolworks profile, SOURCED). A 2018 first-person account: staff lived in ATCO units behind the café with thin walls; winter nights to −40°; no more than ~4 hours of midwinter daylight; staff life "like life on a ship"; some coworkers dated and married; some isolated themselves in the dark months; a cell tower had recently been installed (The Expeditioner, 2018-07-11, SOURCED). In 2001 the owner managed winter by cutting to fuel-only hours for a time, then reopening the kitchen as winter **aurora tourism** grew (HDT 2001, SOURCED). Camp says the aurora is visible on most clear nights (~80%) from late August to mid-April (FAQ, SOURCED). [K7, K8]
- **Neighbors:** **Wiseman** (~13 miles north): gold camp founded ~1908; population 5 (2020) or 12 full-time (2022); peaked at 58 in 1930; connected to the road only in the early 1990s; Bob Marshall's base in the 1930s (*Arctic Village*), who called it "the happiest civilization of which I have knowledge" and shared the book's proceeds with residents (Wikipedia "Wiseman"; NPS, SOURCED). Wiseman (not Coldfoot) is one of 11 resident-zone communities with subsistence privileges in Gates of the Arctic, with Alatna, Allakaket, Ambler, Anaktuvuk Pass, Bettles, Evansville, Hughes, Kobuk, Nuiqsut and Shungnak (NPS snippet; UNVERIFIED). [P9]
- **Indigenous peoples (K9, P9):** the Koyukuk gold rush "brought major changes" to Koyukon Athabascan and Kobuk Iñupiaq life (NPS snippet; UNVERIFIED). Anaktuvuk Pass, settled in 1949 by Nunamiut from Chandler Lake and the Killik River, is the last Nunamiut (inland Iñupiat) settlement; caribou remain the staple (NPS snippets; UNVERIFIED). In July 1973 Doyon, Ltd. wrote that its 41 Interior villages were "opposed to the construction of the Trans-Alaska pipeline and the construction of all road systems west and north of Fairbanks that would connect our villages with urban centers" (BTS report, SOURCED). Tanana Chiefs Conference and the North Slope Borough sued to keep the road closed (*Turpin v. North Slope Borough*, 1994) and lost (BTS report, SOURCED). Residents of Koyukuk-side villages reported caribou stopped coming, perhaps repelled by truck lights (High Country News snippet; UNVERIFIED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
**The road and its access fight (SOURCED: BTS report; DOT&PF):** Alyeska built the Livengood–Yukon "TAPS Road" in 1969–70 and the Yukon–Deadhorse Haul Road in 1974 (~390 miles in ~5 months; $258M from Alyeska plus $27M federal, mostly for the Yukon bridge). Oil flowed 1977-06-20. The state took over in 1978–79; in October 1978 DOT closed the road to vehicles without permits (permits for state, industrial and commercial vehicles; ~200 trucks a day in 1981 per the New York Times). In 1981 the road was named for James Dalton and a court ordered it opened year-round to **Disaster Creek (MP 211)**. In 1991 Governor Hickel's DOT moved to repeal the permit rule; DOT stopped checking permits in 1992; after *Turpin v. North Slope Borough* (Alaska Supreme Court, 1994) the road became fully public in **January 1995** (DOT says 1994). After September 11, 2001 the state closed the northernmost section and set up security checkpoints, later run 12 hours a day and then abandoned; access through the oil fields to the Arctic Ocean stays restricted. [K9] [SM: man versus society]
**Traffic:** ~160 trucks a day in summer and ~250 in winter (Wikipedia via snippet; UNVERIFIED); "at least 100 truckers" on an average day (ADN 2015 snippet; UNVERIFIED).
**Truckers' practice (K14, K6, SM: competence under pressure):**
- CB **Channel 19** is the road's communication line, monitored by truckers, travelers, pipeline security and troopers (Wikivoyage/dangerousroads snippets; UNVERIFIED). BLM: always drive with headlights on; stay in lane in construction zones; watch for wildlife (BLM Dalton Highway Recreation page, SOURCED); yield to oncoming trucks and oversized loads (BLM guide snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- The camp's operator in 2006: truckers "enjoy stopping to talk and socialize"; "no trucker would pass a driver stuck along the Haul Road"; most travel in groups of three or four; some make the run three times a week; he favored concentrating services in nodes rather than spreading them along the road (UAF Project Jukebox interview with Brett Carlson, 2006-11-17, SOURCED).
- A Lynden driver in 2001 carried tools for hoses, lines, filters and motors, two or three days of food, arctic gear and an $800 sleeping bag; inexperienced drivers riding their brakes on steep descents leave polished stripes that cost traction; troopers had recorded 30 fatalities in three decades (HDT 2001, SOURCED).
- Crashes 2000–2021: highest in 2013–2014 (mostly property-damage only); July–August highest months; semi-trailers most often involved; fatal crashes "evenly distributed" over the period (BTS report, SOURCED). Pay: one Carlile driver said a round trip fell from ~$5,000 (Anchorage–Prudhoe, heyday) to ~$2,000 (Fairbanks round trip) (Overdrive snippet; UNVERIFIED).
**Closures (K5, K9):** Sagavanirktok River flooding closed the north end for a week in spring 2015 and then 18 days in May 2015 (MP 334.5–413 at peak); state response cost $15.5M; repeated washouts in 2023 and 2025 (DOT/ADN snippets; UNVERIFIED). Avalanches at Atigun Pass hit four trucks in March 2017 (two buried; drivers escaped) (ADN snippet; UNVERIFIED).

### E. Structural features
**Rules of everyday behavior (K6) — the corridor is a rule-dense place:**
- **Hunting and vehicles (federal era):** from the Yukon River north, firearm hunting prohibited within 5 miles of each side of the highway (bow hunting allowed in most of it); state law prohibits off-road vehicle use within 5 miles from the Yukon River to the Arctic Ocean; all hunting prohibited in the Prudhoe Bay Closed Area (BLM page, SOURCED). Hunters must stop at any ADF&G check station in the corridor (ADF&G regulation snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **2026 change:** PLO 7966 revoked the 1971 withdrawals (PLOs 5150 and 5180) effective **2026-02-25**; ~1.4 million acres were conveyed to the state on 2026-05-06 (snippets). The DNR fact sheet (March 2026, read from PDF, SOURCED): conveyed lands leave federal subsistence units; ADF&G method-and-means rules apply; under **AS 19.40.210** snowmobiles and long guns within five miles of the corridor "will no longer be allowed" for federally qualified subsistence users on those lands; the state committed to authorizing winter snowmachine access on ten existing RS 2477 trails. Tanana Chiefs Conference condemned the transfer as a loss of the ANILCA rural subsistence priority; BLM's Alaska director said residents "will no longer have the federal rural subsistence priority" (Alaska Public Media, 2026-02-24, SOURCED). A federal judge declined to halt the transfer (snippet). [K1, K9]
- **Camp rules:** liquor not sold or carried into the saloon; drug-free workplace with testing (above).
- **Camping:** dispersed camping limited to 14 days at one spot; do not block pipeline access roads (BLM page, SOURCED).
**Seasonality (K7):** summer: visitor center, BLM campgrounds (May–Sept), bus tours that overnight in Coldfoot (Wikipedia), Dalton Highway Express vans for hikers and paddlers (snippets); winter: truckers, aurora tourists, dog-sled and snowshoe outings (camp site snippet). Winter conditions can start in August and last through June (DOT&PF, SOURCED).
**K10 (third places):** the café and the saloon are the only gathering rooms on a 240-mile stretch; the center pole serves as a message board (above).
**K12:** trucker buffet food (above). **K13:** none found. **K11 (speech, loose):** English; Koyukon and Iñupiaq are the region's Indigenous languages (sources above; no further note).
**Wider "last services" network on the road:** Yukon River Camp (Mile 56; fuel, lodging; closed mid-September to mid-May per one snippet), the Hot Spot Cafe (~Mile 60), Coldfoot (Mile 175), then Deadhorse (Mile 414) (snippets; UNVERIFIED except Coldfoot).

### Claims tested (one line each)
- **#12 Dawson Creek:** confirmed with corrections. Founded as a 1931 railhead; the Mile "0" Post is a 1946 civic replacement at the town center (the true start is a separate cairn); agriculture and the oil patch are as large as the highway in its economy.
- **#13 Delta Junction:** confirmed. The "official end" moved there from Fairbanks around 1991; a farm organization sells the end-of-highway certificates; the town is also shaped by Fort Greely, gold mines, a failed state barley project and a bison herd.
- **#11 Parting of the Ways:** confirmed, with an irony: the 1956 state marker stands ~9.5 miles from the true fork, and OCTA corrected it in 1988. The choice could be undone further on.
- **#94 Vladivostok 9288:** confirmed. The stele went up in 1996; the city is also where the line's construction began (1891); its main identity is port and navy.
- **#15 Watson Lake:** confirmed. Begun in 1942 by a soldier; kept up by the town; a Yukon Historic Site since 2014.
- **#14 Coldfoot:** confirmed. Last full services before ~240 miles and Atigun Pass; built by truckers out of pipeline crates.
