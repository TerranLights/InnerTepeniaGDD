## 1 · Salinas Valley, California

**Round 2 focus:** Round 1 covered the cold chain's two eras, the strikes, Chinatown's history, the rodeo, Spreckels and H-2A dormitories. This round goes to the Indigenous Oaxacan arrivals, the Filipino and Japanese community institutions, the clinic network, the winter off-season and the Yuma rotation, leisure (fair, soccer), and everyday rules in worker housing.

### A. Other settings
- **The Alisal (East Salinas).** A neighborhood east of downtown, ZIP 93905, elevation 89 ft; a post office opened as "East Salinas" in **1940**, renamed Alisal in 1947; the unincorporated community was **annexed to Salinas in 1963**; Alisal High School is there. SOURCED (Wikipedia "Alisal, Salinas, California," read 2026-10-29). It began as housing for Dust Bowl migrants and became a settlement of Mexicans, Chicanos, Filipinos and Dust Bowl arrivals. UNVERIFIED (search summary of the City of Salinas Alisal District Identity Master Plan page; not opened). **Alisal Center for the Fine Arts**: youth classes (seven in dance, four in music, one in visual arts) mostly at the **Breadbox Recreation Center, 745 N. Sanborn St.**, more than 100 young people. UNVERIFIED (search summary of a Voices of Monterey Bay feature). [K4, K13] [P3] [P9]
- **Hotels and motels as worker housing.** Until 2019 labor contractors could rent motel and hotel rooms in Salinas for only **28 days**; contractors used different names and reservations to get around it; on **21 March 2019** the City extended the limit to **six months, renewable for six more**, for all hotels and motels in city limits, with city inspections, parking for agricultural companies and a required management plan; aim stated by a councilmember: get workers "out of residential homes and into organized housing," since up to **15 farmworkers** were living in single homes. SOURCED (KION, 2019-03-21). [K4, K6, K1] [P4]
- **A downtown Salinas hotel used for H-2A workers (2020):** workers employed by **PGM Packing** (supplying Driscoll's strawberry operations) slept on bare metal cots without mattresses; dozens shared **two and a half bathrooms**, waiting in line until midnight; rats, cockroaches, bedbugs. State housing inspectors (HCD) visited in summer 2020 and found "no violations," stating the department "does not regulate housekeeping"; a second probe in November 2020 closed in days. A **2022 settlement** required PGM to stop using the hotel and provide beds and bedding (no admission of liability). SOURCED (Felicia Mello and Wendy Fry, CalMatters, 2024-07-01). [K4, K9] [SM:man versus society]
- **A grower's purpose-built complex:** The Growers Company built a **600-bed** housing complex in Salinas (about 2018) plus apartments in Huron; of its H-2A crews, **242 return in June and 303 stay in Salinas until 30 November**. SOURCED (Samantha Byrd, KYMA, 2022-04-14). [K4, K7]
- **Clinics.** **Clínica de Salud del Valle de Salinas (CSVS)**, founded **1980** by community members for farmworker families; Monterey County's first federally qualified health center (**1989**); first school-based clinic **1998**; first mobile medical and dental van (for homeless people) **2004**; **13 clinics from Pajaro to King City** by 2019; over **50,000 patients and about 200,000 visits a year (2018)**; more than **10,200 babies delivered since 1986**; sliding fees; English–Spanish staff living in the communities served; an outreach program for farmworker families. SOURCED (csvs.org "Our story," undated). First clinic run by volunteer nurses and doctors **out of two houses in old Chinatown**: UNVERIFIED (search summary of a Monterey County Weekly piece). [K1, K5] [P4]
- **Schools as language sites:** CBDIO runs a **Mixtec after-school program at Santa Rita Elementary School** (reading and writing in Mixtec, Indigenous music, art and food) and gives daily in-language help to Indigenous parents in the Santa Rita School District; office at 921 B South Main Street. SOURCED (centrobinacional.org/salinas, undated). [K1, K11] [P4]

### B. Subcultures
- **Indigenous Oaxacan arrivals.** The Central Coast is "perhaps the most diverse California region in terms of different indigenous languages spoken," with Mixtec, Triqui and Zapotec groups; many newer arrivals are monolingual in languages without a standard written form and face discrimination carried over from Mexico and isolation within the existing (mestizo) farmworker community. SOURCED (Middlebury "Language attitudes in Monterey" site, "Indigenous language use in the Salinas Valley," undated, author not named). The shift toward Indigenous migration is dated there to after 1994 (SOURCED as the page's claim). About a quarter of all Indigenous Mexicans in California live on the Central Coast: UNVERIFIED (search summary). **Organizations:** **CBDIO** (Centro Binacional para el Desarrollo Indígena Oaxaqueño), Salinas office: language justice, interpreting, labor-justice help with stolen wages, interpretation in Indigenous languages, an inclusive economic-development initiative (SOURCED, centrobinacional.org); Indigenous residents with CBDIO raised rent concerns at the Salinas City Council on **24 September 2024** (UNVERIFIED, search summary). **MICOP** (founded 2001, Ventura County based) states that half of California's 120,000 Indigenous migrant farmworkers live on the Central Coast (UNVERIFIED, search summary). Triqui pea pickers in the valley protested a work quota (UNVERIFIED, search summary of a David Bacon piece). [K4, K1, K9] [P9] [SM:man versus society]
- **Filipino community (historical).** Labor contractors (Faustino Fabros, Pablo Tangonan and others) ran Filipino crews out of Salinas; crews traveled as far as Florida (about 70 men left Salinas in late November 1930; in July 1932 white Floridians forced Filipinos out, and 60 went on to Cuba); workers slept in bunkhouses and, in one 1929 case, a barn. Leisure: Filipino social clubs, **taxi dance halls** (ten cents a dance of about 90 seconds; dancers kept five cents), weekly **cockfights** drawing up to 900 spectators, gambling houses with Chinese games plus craps and roulette. A newspaper, ***The Philippines Mail***, was published in Salinas from about **1933 into the 1990s** (publisher Delfin Cruz). Associations: **Filipino Community of the Salinas Valley** (president Alfonso Castillo, a labor contractor, who later turned a Salinas house into an eight-bedroom retirement home for former workers), **Monterey Bay Filipino Club**, **Filipina Women's Club of Salinas** (1930s, wives of labor contractors). Racial violence: a mob of over 300 attacked the E.J. Firebaugh Ranch at Exeter, **29 October 1929**; Fermin Tobera, 22, shot at Watsonville, **22 January 1930**. SOURCED (Alex S. Fabros Jr., *Positively Filipino*, 2024-10-09; a first-person family history). [K4, K9, K10, K7] [P9]
- **The Filipino Hall, Cebu Street (near Chinatown):** bought by a group of community members in the **1940s**; banquets, dances, picnics and birthday parties held there "kept the community close." SOURCED (Dennis Ben, oral history interviewed by Corey Fernandez, 2012-10-22, CSU Monterey Bay Digital Commons, Chinatown oral-history collection, item 44; description page read). [K10, K4]
- **Japanese American community.** The **Salinas Buddhist Temple**, 14 California Street (Chinatown), holds an annual **Obon** festival (2018: bonsai and ikebana demonstrations; nine hours; held in late July). SOURCED (KION, 2018-07-30) and UNVERIFIED as to address and length (search summaries). Japanese growers built a cut-flower trade on the Central Coast: one family's Onitsuka Greenhouse was among the "initial eight families" who started with Yoshimi Shibata; about **80 flower growers** at the peak, about **two** by 2018 after Colombian imports from the 1980s; Onitsuka closed **2014**. SOURCED (KION 2018-07-30). [K2, K9, K14] [P9]
- **K11 (speech, loose):** Spanish and English dominant; Mixtec, Triqui, Zapotec and Chatino in daily use among Oaxacan arrivals, some monolingual (SOURCED, Middlebury; CBDIO); historically Ilocano and other Philippine languages among Filipino crews (inferred from the contractor names and the Positively Filipino account; no language survey opened).

SOURCED (KION, 2017-09-14). Who plays (by occupation or hometown) was not stated. [K10] [P4]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **La Guelaguetza Salinas** (Oaxacan cultural event): **annual, August** (2026: 16 August, 10 a.m.–5 p.m.), Natividad Creek Park; in its **17th year in 2026** (so begun about 2009); president **Sandra Iveth Santos**; over **2,000** attend; folkloric dance groups from Oaxacan regions (many dancers come from Southern California), regional food handed out, immigration information and food-distribution partners; it "grew out of a need to preserve culture and confront discrimination" after locals conflated Oaxacan and Mexican identity; the word is given as Zapotec for "to share"; Salinas is one of 12 registered host cities; named a Champion of the Arts honoree by the Arts Council for Monterey County in 2026, its first outside recognition. SOURCED (Royvi Hernandez, Monterey County Now / Salinas Valley Now, 2026-08-14). [K2, K3, K4] [P6] [P9]
- **Labor of Love** (end-of-season thanks to crews): begun **2015** by the **Yuma Fresh Vegetable Association** and extended to the Salinas Valley about 2015–16; surprise meals delivered to crews in the field as the Salinas harvest closes (October), gifts and raffles; at a Dole field in 2017 the team handed out breakfast burritos and airshow tickets and honored a celery box closer employed since 1985 who had not missed a day in three years; workers return each June. SOURCED (Perishable News, 2017-10-02). [K3, K7, K8] [SM:competence shown under pressure]

### Winter, off-season and the Yuma rotation [K7, K5, K9]
- **The equipment move:** processors shut down in Yuma on a Thursday; about **60 truckloads** (dryers, scales, baggers, cutters, palletizers) leave by midnight; install Friday, test Saturday, sanitize Saturday night, start production Sunday; Salinas runs **mid-April to 30 November**; crops are planted 130–140 days before harvest. SOURCED (KYMA, 2022-04-14; companies named: Green Gate Fresh, JV Smith Companies, The Growers Company). **Correction to R1's "November to April" Yuma window:** these companies give Salinas until 30 November; Yuma November to March or April (both appear; SOURCED for the KYMA dates only).
- **The winter workforce in Yuma (for the Salinas firms):** at peak, H-2A guest workers are about **75%** of JV Smith's harvest labor; an estimated **30–40%** of the workforce migrates seasonally from California; the rest are local residents, daily cross-border commuters from **Sonora**, or harvest hires; a field supervisor "recently returned from Salinas." SOURCED (Alexandra Rangel Moran, KAWC, 2025-11-21). [K4]
- **For those who stay in Salinas:** about **70%** of workers surveyed in a California study reported not working for an average of **nine weeks**, often around the holidays, living on savings (SOURCED, Anne Marshall-Chalmers, Civil Eats, 2023-03-29; statewide survey including Monterey County). A five-year UC Santa Cruz–CBDIO study (published 2026-03-12) found a 2025 median wage of **$18.50/hour**, shared rents because one family cannot pay alone, and that seasonal gaps empty savings within a month; some workers migrate seasonally and leave family behind. SOURCED (UC Santa Cruz News, 2026-03-12; authors Chris Benner, Gabriella Alvarez, Sarait Martinez, Yesica Guzmán Rodríguez). Unemployment in Salinas doubling from about 10% in October to 20% in February: UNVERIFIED (search summary; the page carrying it was not identified). [K7, K9] [P4]

## 2 · Totara Estate and the first frozen-meat shipment from Port Chalmers, New Zealand

**Round 2 focus:** Round 1 covered the 1882 operation, the estate's buildings, Port Chalmers' container and cruise figures, the maritime museum, National Lamb Day and Ōamaru's precinct. This round goes to Port Chalmers' everyday town (pubs, market, waterfront workforce, artists, music venue), its festival and its liquor history, the freezing-works tradition the shipment produced (Ōamaru's first works, Pukeuri's workforce and seasons), Totara's heritage programs, and Ōamaru's festival and alcohol rules.

### A. Other settings
- **Port Chalmers' main street and bays.** George Street (numbered in the 5000s to avoid confusion with Dunedin's George Street) holds all shops, cafes and services; small shops include vintage clothing, handmade jewelry and a children's-book illustrator's shop; **Flagstaff Lookout** at the end of Constitution Street overlooks the port. **Carey's Bay** (about 15 minutes' walk) is the fishing harbor. SOURCED (Wikivoyage "Port Chalmers," read 2026-10-29). Ship servicing and building grew in Port Chalmers while Carey's Bay became a fishing port; the community is "a mixture of families long associated with the fishing or shipping trades" and "more recently arrived creatives and intellectuals." UNVERIFIED (search summaries; the ODT "Port life never boring" page returned 404). [K4, K7] [P3] [P5]
- **The seasonal town.** Cruise season **October to March or mid-April**; 2019 peak **229,000 passengers on 115 vessels** (Wikivoyage figure; R1 recorded 104 ships and 153,000 passengers for 2018–19 from Wikipedia; the two do not reconcile and are both secondary). SOURCED as each page's claim. Some retailers open only a few days a week, propped up by cruise days. UNVERIFIED (search summary of a travel feature). [K7, K8] [P4]
- **Ōamaru's first freezing works (the land side of the cold chain).** Esplanade Road, Ōamaru; built **1885–86** by the New Zealand Refrigerating Company, designed by Thomas Forrester, of North Otago limestone; opened **2 February 1886**; one Bell-Coleman machine driven by a water motor, with a steam engine and boiler as backup; capacity **300 sheep a day**, storage **12,000 carcasses**, raised to **20,000** after the 1886–89 extension; sheep came from the Eveline works 5 km north; the company merged with the Christchurch Meat Company in **1905** and killing moved to Pukeuri in **1914**; now private storage; Category 2 (List 3217, 1983); thought to be **New Zealand's oldest surviving freezing works**. SOURCED (Heritage NZ List 3217). [K14, K9] [P8] [R]
- **Pukeuri (the works that followed).** In 2017 the plant ran **four chains**; traditionally it worked **eight months a year**, extended to **ten months for at least two chains** after bobby-calf processing began in the mid-1990s; in the off-season workers shifted to the **Waitaki instant mashed-potato factory**; halal slaughter introduced; a major fire in **2006**; the workforce became "much more diverse," with Pacific Island, Filipino and South African staff; a foreman butcher retired in May 2017 after **46 unbroken years**, describing the plant as a place where "everybody knows everybody"; the NZ Meat Workers Union is present. SOURCED (NZ Herald / The Country, 2017-05-28). [K7, K4, K14] [P9] [SM:competence shown under pressure]

### B. Subcultures
- **Waterfront workers.** A **Port Chalmers Marine Labourers' Union** formed about 1907, about 250 members by 1913, affiliated with the Federation of Labour (UNVERIFIED, search summary of a union-badge history site). In March 2004 the **Maritime Union** had about **150 members and 40 casual workers** at Port Chalmers and planned pickets if a non-union stevedoring firm (Mainland Stevedoring) worked a fishing trawler; the national president, a Ngāi Tahu member, objected that Sealord (part-owned by Te Ohu Kai Moana) was contracting with it. SOURCED (Maritime Union media release, Scoop, 2004-03-02). [K1, K9, K14]
- **Fishing families of Carey's Bay** (see A; UNVERIFIED as to the "Friday evening" pub gathering, search summary only). [K10]
- **Artists and musicians.** An artists' colony from the 1970s (R1). **Chick's Hotel** (built 1876 of bluestone and brick for former mayor Henry Dench; bought 1879 by carrier George Chick and his wife Ellen): a sailors' pub that closed in **2006** in near-ruin; revived in **2008** by Hector Hazard, an ex-bicycle courier from England; taken over about 2013 by musicians (Mike McLeod, Tom Bell, Morgan Oliver) who financed it while holding other jobs; hosted local and international acts; the **Amped Music Project** ran all-ages Sunday-afternoon shows there for young Dunedin musicians; closed as a venue at the end of **March 2016** because the owners' motivation "had peaked"; lease kept two more years for recording and art. SOURCED (John Bollen and Loulou Callister-Baker, RNZ The Wireless, 2016-03-17). Last show by The Clean and The Shifting Sands: UNVERIFIED (search summary). [K13, K10, K9] [P6]
- **Freezing-works workforce (the tradition, documented for Hawke's Bay works).** A workforce of "Māori, Pākehā, as well as Islanders, Chinese, Dutch, Arabs, Muslims, Irish, Italian…"; workers "travelled the country socially playing sports under the banner of their freezing works name" and supported each other through weddings, christenings and funerals; women were confined (by the 1970s) to casings, lamb cuts and offal; a **1978 Human Rights Commission complaint** challenged Whakatū's ban on women butchers; Whakatū closed **10 October 1986**, Tomoana **19 August 1994**. SOURCED (Te Hira Henderson, MTG Hawke's Bay, in Hawke's Bay Today, 2022-05-27). The chain system replaced solo butchers after a 1930s strike: UNVERIFIED (search summary). Workplace extended-family networks in which Māori workers were pivotal, and Māori roles in 1970s meat strikes: UNVERIFIED (search summaries of Cambridge IRSH article, which refused with 403). **Note:** these are Hawke's Bay sources describing the tradition, not Totara or Port Chalmers specifically. [K4, K10, K9, K14] [P9]
- **K11 (speech, loose):** English; te reo Māori in place names and in Ngāi Tahu and union contexts; no survey opened.

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Port Chalmers Seafood Festival** (resolves an R1 gap): **biennial since 2011** (R1's "biannual" means every second year); first held in the port's **"Shed A"** on 1 October 2011 during the Rugby World Cup (UNVERIFIED, search summary); founders **Robbie Ballantyne and Peter Cole**, a core committee of about 20 (UNVERIFIED, search summary). **2015** (26 September): held on the wharf and inside a port shed; a **50-strong volunteer team**; proceeds to local charities and groups including **Otago Community Hospice**, **Port Chalmers Rowing Club** and Lions clubs; transport by two ferries (from Dunedin's Steamer Basin and from Portobello), a heritage train from Dunedin Railway Station, and a free bus. ## 4 · Hawaiian sugar plantation camps

**Round 2 focus:** Round 1 covered the arrival sequence, the 1850 contract law, the camp layout by origin and rank, the numbered tags, the perquisite system, the strikes, the plate lunch, the posted fines and curfew, and three heritage sites (Plantation Village, Kōloa, Hanapēpē). ### A. Other settings
- **The camp day (Waipahu, Oʻahu, as recalled).** Women up at **4 a.m.** to cook breakfast and lunch; wake-up whistle at **5**; crews gathered at the train at **5:45**; work from **6**; a **10-minute** break at 8; **half an hour** for lunch at 11:30, back at noon; the end-of-work whistle at **4:30**; **lights out across the camp at 8 p.m.**; hours rose "from eight hour… to ten hours." SOURCED (four Plantation Village interpreters, Esperanza Gabriel Garcia, Domingo Los Baños, Moses Pataki, Amy Sekuma, interviewed 2004-11-16, Crossing East archive, crossingeast.org, posted 2017-03-26). [K7] [P4]
- **Home ground.** Backyards with tomatoes and chickens for eggs and meat; Japanese households used **a large shared bath** (UNVERIFIED for others: mothers heating the water and late bathers getting cold water, search summary); irrigation ditches served children as swimming holes and fishing spots; families mailed one package a month to each family back in Japan. SOURCED (Crossing East 2004). [K4, K12] [P3]
- **The company store and pay.** Workers were known by tag number at the store; "You get paid in the office and you go right across pay your bill"; charging all month "and you had to survive." SOURCED (Crossing East 2004). [K8, K5]
- **Camp schools.** Children went to the **public school** ("the great equalizer… we grew up together") and then to a **Japanese language school** at the other end of town; some attended English Standard schools (Lincoln, Roosevelt named); teachers gave English names to children whose names they could not pronounce. SOURCED (Crossing East 2004). **Hakalau (Hawaiʻi Island) example:** a Japanese language school opened in **July 1904** in a rented bakery room in the lower camp with **50 pupils** (3 November 1904); a dormitory held over a dozen boarders by 1905; a kindergarten served over 30 preschoolers at Honohina by 1906; four school buildings in all by **October 1927** on a one-acre site in the upper camp; a girls' dormitory in 1918; the **Hakalau Jōdo Mission** (1904–07) on an acre provided rent-free by the plantation, with a Young Men's and a Young Ladies' Buddhist Association formed 1912–20. SOURCED (hakalauhome.com, "The early years," a community history site, undated). [K4, K11, K13] [P4]
- **Plantation medicine.** Plantations gave free care to employees and dependents in hospitals and dispensaries; the first plantation hospital was at **Makaweli, Kauaʻi, in 1886**; named plantation doctors served several plantations at once (Līhuʻe, Grove Farm, Makee). UNVERIFIED (search summaries; the one source opened, E. S. Goodhue, "The Physician in Hawaii," *Texas Medical Journal* 30(11), 1915, is page images only on PMC; no text read). [K1, K8] [P4]
- **A camp at its size (Puhi Camp, Grove Farm, Kauaʻi):** at its peak **600 homes and 1,200 residents** from China, the Philippines, Japan, Puerto Rico and Portugal; its own gas station, slaughterhouse, Chinese laundry, **three stores** and recreation grounds; cane, pineapple and other crops **until 1968**; its cemetery holds **97** named on stones (20 in Japanese) and **52** unknown, cleaned in 2015 by about **50** Kauaʻi Community College staff and volunteers (the college stands on the camp's land). ### B. Subcultures
- **Filipino contract workers (1906–1946).** The Hawaiian Sugar Planters' Association recruited about **125,000** from the Ilocos and Visayas regions; mostly single men, three-year contracts, **10-hour days, six days a week, 90 cents a day**, the lowest paid of any group, living in barracks for which they paid rent; at first most meant to return home; the final 1946 cohort included more women, children and relatives of earlier workers and some with professional schooling. SOURCED (Wikipedia "Filipinos in Hawaii"). Within Filipino camps there were separate Ilocano and Visayan camps; each camp held several hundred workers with few families: UNVERIFIED (search summary). **Sakada Day** was enacted by the Hawaiʻi Legislature in **2015**; the second observance, **20 December 2016**, honored living workers who came in 1946. UNVERIFIED (search summaries; the Philippine Consulate page refused with 403). Rizal Day programs at the Waipahu ballpark: UNVERIFIED (search summary). Dime-a-dance halls with saxophone bands for single men: SOURCED (Crossing East 2004). [K4, K7, K2] [P9]
- **Portuguese (from 1878).** Nearly **16,000** arrived by end of 1911, from Madeira and the Azores; unlike Chinese laborers they **could bring families** (about 3,300 contracted men 1878–87 but about 10,700 people in all), which planters hoped would bring stability; 11.6% of the population by 1910; spoke Portuguese, English and Hawaiian; three Madeiran cabinetmakers arriving in **1879** brought the small guitar the ukulele was adapted from; faced discrimination and many moved on to California. SOURCED (Wikipedia "Portuguese immigration to Hawaii"). Portuguese, Spanish and Japanese foremen had better houses than ordinary Japanese and Filipino workers: UNVERIFIED (search summary of a crossingeast page). [K4, K13] [P9]
- **Puerto Ricans (from 1900).** First group of **56** men left on **22 November 1900** (San Juan → New Orleans → rail → California → ship); **5,883** came in 13 groups; mistreated and underfed in transit; by 1902 **34 plantations** had 1,773 on payroll (1,734 field hands, 39 clerks or overseers); 4,890 in Hawaiʻi by 1910; brought the cuatro, maracas and güiro, from which a local Puerto Rican string-band style grew in the early 1900s; pasteles made with green bananas in place of plantains, wrapped in banana or ti leaves; godparent ties kept as a binding institution; Puerto Rican and Portuguese workers at ʻEwa joined the **1920** strikes over pay and discrimination; "Europeans were paid more and received better quarters." SOURCED (Wikipedia "Puerto Rican migration to Hawaii"). Puerto Ricans "did a lot of the music" in the camps. SOURCED (Crossing East 2004). [K4, K12, K13] [P9]
- **Japanese camp institutions:** **kumiai**, neighborhood mutual-aid associations, organized funerals (cooking, cleaning, burial) and helped at weddings, since most first-generation arrivals had no relatives in Hawaiʻi; the **Wailea–Hakalau kumiai** still exists: on a 20 July gathering, its first since 2019, **51** residents met for a potluck, raffle and talk about common concerns; president Akiko Masuda, also president of the Wailea Village Historic Preservation Community; its stated rules: "Engage in collective effort. Look out for each other"; "Consistently show up." SOURCED (hakalauhome.com, Wailea–Hakalau Kumiai page, year of the gathering not stated). Rotating credit circles among camp families built on trust without collateral: UNVERIFIED (search summary; the Camp 5 Maui page returned 404). [K1, K10] [P7]
- **Children across the lines:** slingshots, war comics, games with the single men; ethnic ball teams (Filipino, Japanese, Puerto Rican) but inter-plantation games picked "the best" from all; cockfights and gambling with marbles and bottle caps; neighbors brought food across groups for birthdays; "you touch hands, you touch hearts," ending in intermarriage. A separate clubhouse with a tennis court for white staff and a Japanese clubhouse were named. SOURCED (Crossing East 2004). [K4, K10] [P4]
- **K11 (speech, loose):** each group's own language (Japanese, Ilocano, Visayan, Portuguese, Spanish, Korean, Chinese) plus a shared creole that grew from the mix and that mainland teachers could not follow (SOURCED, Crossing East 2004; Wikipedia Puerto Rican migration). Noted only.

SOURCED (Karleen C. Chinen, Discover Nikkei, 2016-03-02). The 1925 Hawaii Baseball League of ethnic teams (Asahis, All-Hawaiians, All-Chinese, Portuguese Braves, Caucasian Wanderers, later All-Filipinos): UNVERIFIED (search summary). ## 5 · Israeli absorption centers and the ulpan

**Round 2 focus:** Round 1 covered the ulpan's origin and scale, the 1950s transit camps, the support package, Ulpan Etzion at Beit Canada, the Mevaseret Zion center, the kibbutz ulpan and its rules. This round goes to the inside of an ulpan class, a rural absorption village, the end of Mevaseret Zion, the kibbutz host-family program, a residential Arab–Jewish ulpan, and what arrival groups built for themselves: their own festivals made national, a theater, a chess club, and the fight over religious leaders.

### A. Other settings
- **The ulpan classroom (Ulpan Etzion, Jerusalem, 2004).** "Five-month, five-days-a-week, five-hours-a-day"; over **200** young adults from **28** countries, a class of about **35**; teacher originally from Morocco, raised in Jerusalem; lessons from grammar, vocabulary, newspapers, radio news and political argument ("the political climate served as our daily lesson"); at breaks students **separated by nationality** (Argentines with their tea gourds, French students smoking, Russians laughing together, Americans studying Talmud); ceremonies included a mock Passover meal at which classes presented their own traditions. SOURCED (first-person account, JTA, 2004-09-02, author not named on the page as read). Formal class ending at 1 p.m. daily: UNVERIFIED (search summary). [K4, K11, K7] [P4]
- **A rural absorption village: Beit Alfa (north), opened 2007.** **141** ground-level homes for Ethiopian arrivals; typical stay **three years**: the first year for Hebrew and Judaism, the rest for working and saving; first jobs in factories, shelf-stocking, cleaning and farm work; coffee and Ethiopian bread served; a community garden for spices; children's soccer; earlier residents mentor newcomers; the rural siting deliberately chosen to resemble life in Ethiopia rather than Jerusalem or Tel Aviv; the article's sponsor (ICEJ) pays flights and aid. SOURCED (Annaliese Johnson, ICEJ Aliyah blog, 2023-05-29; a sponsoring organization's own account). **The same center, earlier:** about **150** Ethiopian residents demonstrated in Jerusalem over economic distress and harsh conditions, saying the remote site kept them from finding work; "The Jewish Agency and the absorption center didn't live up to the agreements"; a review committee had a 10-day deadline; seven protesters were detained trying to enter the Prime Minister's Office compound. SOURCED (Yael Branovsky, Ynet, article 3619861; date not shown on the page as read). [K4, K9, K7] [SM:man versus society]
- **The end of Mevaseret Zion (update to R1).** The High Court rejected residents' petition; residents had to leave by **31 October 2017**; about **500** affected, sent to absorption centers in **Ashkelon and Be'er Sheva**; one resident had immigrated at 14 after both parents died, served as a military fitness instructor and worked in security on a minimum wage of about **NIS 3,000** that would not cover rent; a lawyer said most had tried to leave but were refused mortgages or public housing unless they first moved south. SOURCED (Adi Rozenberg, Ynet, article 5013674, 2017). 650 tenants petitioned and 660 were evacuated; 900 housing units planned on the site: UNVERIFIED (search summaries). Ethiopians at Mevaseret had no synagogue of their own, and doubts over the newcomers' Jewish status were raised at Saturday services: UNVERIFIED (search summary of a JPost item). [K4, K9] [P5]
- **Earlier trailer parks** used as absorption sites for the 1990s arrivals: Hazerot Yasaf (1991–2001), Givat HaMatos (1991–2000), Hulda (1992–99); a Jerusalem hotel (Shalom Hotel, 1991–93); **Canada House, Jerusalem, active since 1949**. SOURCED (Ethiopian Jewry Heritage Center, "Absorption Centers" archive map, ethiopianjhc.org.il, undated). [K4, K5]
- **The kibbutz host-family route: First Home in the Homeland** (Jewish Agency, Negev and Galilee): a kibbutz-based program for young immigrant families; **30** kibbutzim (one Jewish Agency page) or **44** (search summary of another); several hundred a year; parents study Hebrew for the first **six months** while children attend local schools; afterward families may stay as members or leave. SOURCED (Jewish Agency, "First Home in the Homeland for Ukrainian Jews," 17 November, year not shown). Founded **1989**, each family adopted by a kibbutz family: UNVERIFIED (search summaries). [K1, K4] [P4]
- **A residential ulpan for Jews and Arabs: Ulpan Akiva, Netanya**, founded **1951** (one of the first three ulpanim) by **Shulamit Katznelson**, who directed it for nearly 50 years; Jews and Arabs "encouraged to get to know each other, speak each other's language, and learn about each other's history and culture"; Katznelson was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize in 1992 and 1993. SOURCED (Wikipedia "Shulamit Katznelson"). ### B. Subcultures
- **Ethiopian Israelis and their religious leaders.** The **kessim** led Ethiopian Jewish communities for centuries; their role shrank after immigration from **1984 (Operation Moses)**; the Chief Rabbinate refused for decades to recognize their authority over marriage and divorce (UNVERIFIED as to details, search summaries of JTA archive pieces); on **19 February 2018** the **Ministerial Committee for the Integration of Israeli Citizens of Ethiopian Descent** granted kessim official status, the page stating this covers religious functions and places them in state religious councils; a kes: "recognition that we are a community with values, with traditions, with leaders." SOURCED (Andrew Friedman / TPS, Jewish Press, 2018-02-19; the marriage-and-divorce scope is the page's statement and is not confirmed elsewhere). [K1, K9] [P6] [P7]
- **Russian-speaking Israelis** (about **1.2 million**, mostly arriving in the 1990s): see **Novy God** (E), **Gesher Theatre** and **Be'er Sheva chess** (C, D). ### E. Cultural activities and civic life

Gatherings at Armon Hanatziv since the mid-1980s: UNVERIFIED (search summary). Attendance figures: NOT FOUND. [K2, K4] [P6]
- **Mimouna** (Moroccan Jewish): the night after Passover, marking the return to leavened food; tables set with luck and fertility symbols in fives, a layered pastry, mint dipped in milk as a blessing, visits to the sea; in Israel first organized as a mass picnic by activist **Shaul Ben Shimhon** at **Ben Shemen** in **1966** (**300** people), **3,000** the next year, tens of thousands in Jerusalem by **1970**; the central event in **Sacher Park** draws politicians; an estimated **two million** Israelis took part in **2012**; Israeli law gives employees **unpaid leave** for it on request. - **Da Lang, Longhua (a migrant district).** About **500,000** people, of whom **8,200** registered and **491,800** "floating"; over **50%** aged 20–29 (born after 1980); nearly half had arrived within the past year, about a quarter stay four to five years; just outside the old Special Economic Zone line; made a sub-district in **2011**; urban villages plus factories; road widening and a "Fashion Valley" meant to draw creative industries. **Labor Square** (built **2007**) is "the vibrant heart of the district" because people spend free time outside crowded dormitories and shared flats: weekend gatherings of hundreds, roller skating, dancing, singing, a big TV screen. SOURCED (Linda Vlassenrood, ed., *Archis* / "Da Lang Fever," 2014-04-07, from the INTI "New New Towns" research program). [K4, K10] [P4] [P9]
- **Baishizhou, the end (update to R1).** Demolition announced summer **2019**; a scholars' **Baishizhou Committee** survey estimated nearly **28%** of residents would leave Shenzhen, over **51%** would face longer commutes, almost **50%** risked losing work, and about **4,000** children would need new schools. SOURCED (Yang Qian, Sixth Tone, 2019-10-28). Enrollment quotas tied to school districts meant Baishizhou leases did not qualify children elsewhere; **85%** of **1,031** surveyed households were highly concerned; registered residents about **83,000** (unofficial up to 150,000); a 25-year resident's two-bedroom rent of **2,500 yuan** would double on moving; on **4 August 2019** an artist had an excavator drop **400 dolls** collected from local families into a river outside the city as a protest piece. SOURCED (Fan Liya, Sixth Tone, 2019-08-06). [K9, K4] [SM:man versus society]
- **Urban villages generally:** more than **10 million** people, about half the city's residential land (2018); in 2018 the city promised to preserve a share of remaining villages; a developer proposed low-impact renewal in 2017. SOURCED (Sixth Tone, 2019-10-28). Village buildings on collectively held rural land escape urban building rules, which is why they stand close enough for neighbors to reach across. SOURCED (Brandon Fuller, NYU Marron Institute blog, 2013-06-03). **Gangxia**: 580 buildings and about 70,000 people by 1998, mostly migrants rather than land-holding villagers; **Hubei village** (about 500 years old) slated for a mall: UNVERIFIED (search summaries). [K4, K8] [P3]
- **Factory dormitories:** up to **24** to a room; kettles and hair dryers banned, with written confessions required for breaking the rule; overtime far above the legal **36 hours a month** (one payslip **98 hours**). SOURCED (SOMO, 2011-05-07, summarizing a SACOM/SOMO investigation of Foxconn's Shenzhen and Chengdu plants; the schedule details given were for Chengdu). [K4, K6, K7] [R]

### B. Subcultures

A government community center with **17** staff supports five volunteer teams; a young migrant performance troupe sings and dances at district events. SOURCED (Archis 2014). [K10, K1, K13] [P4]
