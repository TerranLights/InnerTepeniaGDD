## Pick 1 · Subak, Bali (Indonesia)
*Care note from the input file: founder nation, structure only. This section records structure (how the institution works) and its physical and economic setting.*

### A. Snapshot
- **What it is:** a subak is a self-governing association of the farmers whose paddy fields are irrigated from one shared irrigation structure (one weir and its canal). "Between 50 and 400 farmers manage the water supply from one source of water." The word first appears in royal inscriptions in the 11th century; the system "dates back to at least the 9th century AD." [K1] [P7] — SOURCED: ICOMOS Advisory Body Evaluation No. 1194rev, 14 March 2012, pp. 170–172 (https://webarchive.unesco.org/web/20160811074155/http://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1194rev.pdf, read via extracted PDF text 2026-10-29).
- **How many:** ICOMOS 2012: "about 1,200 of these water collectives." Wikipedia (citing 2019 figures): "estimated at 1,559"; older 1996 estimate "about 1,300 subak … approximately 110,000 hectares." — 1,200: SOURCED (ICOMOS). 1,559 and 1,300: SOURCED on Wikipedia "Subak (irrigation)" (read 2026-10-29), underlying sources not opened.
- **World Heritage site:** "Cultural Landscape of Bali Province: the Subak System as a Manifestation of the Tri Hita Karana Philosophy," inscribed 2012 (deferred in 2008, revised nomination 28 January 2011, ICOMOS mission 12–19 October 2011), criteria (iii), (v), (vi); 19,519.90 ha plus 1,454.80 ha of buffer zones; five components: Pura Ulun Danu Batur; Lake Batur; Subak Landscape of the Pakerisan Watershed; Subak Landscape of Catur Angga Batukaru; Royal Water Temple of Pura Taman Ayun. — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012). UNESCO's own list page (whc.unesco.org/en/list/1194) returned HTTP 403.
- **Defining feature:** rights to draw water are tied to rituals at water temples; temples mark water sources and the points where water passes downhill, and regional water temples host the meetings where staggered irrigation schedules are set. [K1, K2] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012; Lansing et al. 2017, PNAS 114(25):6504–6509, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5488911, read 2026-10-29).

### B. What else exists there (structure, economy, institutions)
- **Physical system:** weirs "spaced a few kilometers apart" divert water from rivers flowing from volcanic lakes into irrigation tunnels, "many over a kilometer in length," feeding canals around the terraces; "allows the delivery of small quantities of water with remarkable accuracy." Traditional production of two rice crops a year. [K14] [P8] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012, p.171–172).
- **Office-holders inside a subak** (as listed on Wikipedia, read 2026-10-29): a chairman (pekaseh or kelihan subak), and in larger subaks a vice-chair, secretary, treasurer, a messenger, a religious assistant and a water-distribution supervisor; very large federations are headed by a senior chairman. The members' assembly decides when water flow begins and plans work. [K1, K7] — SOURCED on Wikipedia; primary sources not opened.
- **Written rules:** "Most subaks possess written legal codes, called awig-awig, which detail the rights and responsibilities of subak membership"; protected under Bali Province Regulation No. 5 (2005) Section 19. [K6] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012, p.178).
- **Sanctions under those codes:** warnings, fines, cutting off a member's water, and seizure or auction of property; fines for dumping garbage in channels around Rp 500,000. [K6] — UNVERIFIED (search snippets only; the three Indonesian journal PDFs that state this, at ejournal.warmadewa.ac.id and core.ac.uk, all returned HTTP 403).
- **Elected head; water does not follow rank:** "Each subak has an elected head, the pekaseh, who organizes all duties and obligations"; the subak "avoids the danger that a rich and noble landowner may appropriate a greater share of water than … poorer peasants and landless tenants." [K1, K4] — SOURCED: Hauser-Schäublin, B. (2011) "Land Donations and the Gift of Water," *Human Ecology* 39:43–53 (open access PDF, read 2026-10-29), p.45.
- **Scheduling:** "Irrigation schedules are chosen by consensus in subak meetings"; for multi-subak coordination "these meetings take place in regional water temples and make use of a sophisticated permutational calendar to plan and implement staggered irrigation schedules." A 2017 survey of 150 farmers in 10 subaks found "upstream farmers worry more about pests, whereas downstream and middle farmers are more concerned with water shortages." [K1, K7] — SOURCED (Lansing et al. 2017, PMC5488911).
- **Pest control via synchronized fallow:** temple-coordinated schedules control pests "by inducing synchronized fallow cycles." [K1, K7] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012, p.175–176).
- **Batur temple congregation and tribute:** Pura Ulun Danu Batur's "congregation appropriately includes all subaks"; it is "managed by the people of Batur village, supported by contributions from more than 250 subaks." Temple and village were destroyed by the 1926 eruption and rebuilt higher on the caldera rim. [K1, K8] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012, p.172).
- **Historical economics of the supreme temple (a narrowing of the "temple as neutral scheduler" view):** palm-leaf manuscripts kept at Batur show the temple in the 18th–19th centuries was "one of the most important land owning and tribute-collecting bodies in Bali"; villages delivered rice (about 5 kg of unhulled rice per land unit) at the 10th-month festival, and subak heads were asked to bring money, sometimes assessed per weir; building a new dam was taxed. Peasants feared the priests' refusal of holy water "if they do not fulfill their obligations for three successive years." In water-short regions such as North Bali, downstream subaks disputes were often referred to the king or his official, who "set up a written regulation … and a schedule as to which subak was allowed to make use of the water, when and for what purpose." Rulers could also use water as a weapon (diverting it, or damming and breaking a dam). [K1, K8, K9] [SM: a dangerous or fascinating figure or system] — SOURCED (Hauser-Schäublin 2011, pp. 45–49).
- **Recent support payments:** since 2018, monthly incentives of Rp 1,500,000 (~US$100) for subak chairmen and Rp 650,000 (~US$40) for vice-chairs and water supervisors. [K8] — SOURCED on Wikipedia (read 2026-10-29; the article shows a wrong currency symbol); primary source not opened.
- **Governing Assembly:** Bali Governor's Regulation No. 17/2010 created the Governing Assembly for the Cultural Heritage of Bali; monthly general meetings; six working groups with subak representatives on all; promised US$2,200 per subak for one year to 17 subaks transitioning to organic farming. [K1, K8] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012, pp.178–179). A 2016 assessment says the body operated "only in name," dominated by officials with "token representation" of farmers. [K9] — SOURCED: G. MacRae, "Good intentions, mixed realities," *Inside Indonesia* 125, 5 July 2016 (read 2026-10-29).

### C. Cultural locations (named)
- **Pura Ulun Danu Batur** (Kintamani): supreme water temple, five courtyards, shrines to "some 45 deities," foremost the Goddess of the Lake. — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **Lake Batur:** crater lake regarded as the abode of the Goddess of the Lake and ultimate source of subak water; "no overground outlets." — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **Tirtha Empul** (Pakerisan): 10th-century water temple around a revered spring; outer yard with communal ablution area, inner pool for purification; a royal inscription dated 962 AD refers to a dam here. — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **Pura Mengening, Pura Pegulingan** (9th century, octagonal stupa, keeps an ancient royal inscription), **Gunung Kawi** (11th-century rock-cut royal tombs and monasteries along the Pakerisan, with carved water channels). — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **Pura Luhur Batukaru** (11th century, apex of the Batukaru temple system) and **Lake Tamblingan.** — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **Pura Taman Ayun** (Mengwi): royal water temple built early 18th century, moat-surrounded, 29 shrines in the inner yard; distributes holy water to downstream subaks; its moat irrigates the small Subak Batan Badung; managed by the Royal House of Mengwi. — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **Pura Jati** (Batur area): where the New Year on the full moon of the 1st Balinese month is still celebrated; historically its priests kept the calendar that set the timing of water movement. — SOURCED (Hauser-Schäublin 2011, p.47).
- **Museum Subak** (UPTD Museum Subak, Sanggulan, Tabanan Regency): inaugurated 13 October 1981 by Governor Ida Bagus Mantra; owned by Tabanan Regency, run by its Cultural Office; tools, documentation, audiovisual material. — SOURCED: indonesia.travel/gb/en/destination/bali-nusa-tenggara/bali/uptd-museum-subak (read 2026-10-29). "250 collections" of tools and a miniature farmhouse: UNVERIFIED (snippet only).
- **Ngusaba Kadasa** (festival, see D). [K2]
- **Village architecture:** in subak interiors, family houses of single-story structures around the family granary (lumbung), "a steeply thatched roofed structure built on stilts"; thatch and wood now scarce and building craft "vanishing." [K14] [P3] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012, p.177).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Ngusaba Kadasa at Pura Ulun Danu Batur:** held at the full moon of the 10th Balinese month; in 2024 the main day was 24 March, with rites through 5 April; over 40 groups took part, subak representatives making separate offerings; offerings included crops, animals, cakes, fruit and flowers; the main procession circles the village. [K2] — SOURCED: detik.com Bali, "Semarak Prosesi Mapepada Agung Jelang Puncak Karya Ngusaba Kadasa di Batur" (2024, read 2026-10-29). Historically "the final festival that celebrates the end of the agricultural and annual cycle," scheduled after harvest because tribute in rice was delivered then. [K2, K8] — SOURCED (Hauser-Schäublin 2011, pp.47–48).
- **Water-sharing that crosses kingdoms:** at Taman Ayun, downstream farmers "may 'borrow' water from subaks far upstream," with weirs managed by "thousands of farmers" to allow the flow. [K1] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012).
- **What breaks it:** the Green Revolution (BIMAS program reached Bali 1971); by 1977, 70% of southern Bali's terraces were planted with Green Revolution rice; within two years, reports of "chaos in water scheduling" and "explosions of pest populations." [K9] — SOURCED (Lansing et al. 2017). Wikipedia adds: 1988 World Bank finding of pervasive pesticide pollution and restoration of subak scheduling authority — SOURCED on Wikipedia only.

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)
- **Tourism and land:** ICOMOS names the main threats as the move away from traditional organic rice and "tourism pressures upon farmers to sell land for villas"; rising land prices and living costs "tempt them to sell their land." [K9] — SOURCED (ICOMOS 2012). Denpasar's farmland fell from 5,753.43 ha (1993) to 2,444 ha (2018); about 1,000 ha/year lost island-wide. — SOURCED on Wikipedia only.
- **Jatiluwih after listing:** visitors rose from 44,000 a year to nearly 98,000 within a year of listing and 165,000 by 2014; 45% of entry fees to government, 55% to the local community, with the subak farmers receiving "less than a quarter" of the community share. [K8, K9] — SOURCED (MacRae 2016). (The article's 2014 revenue figure, "over Rp.3 trillion," appears to be an error in the source and is not used.)
- **December 2025 dispute at Jatiluwih:** authorities sealed 13 restaurants and shops built on protected farmland; landowning farmers covered viewpoints with corrugated iron and black film in protest, arguing that without tourism income young people leave and fields are abandoned. [K9] — SOURCED: bali.live, "Jatiluwih Dispute: Farmers Shut Off Views in Protest" (read 2026-10-29). A zero-tax policy on Jatiluwih rice fields from 2026: UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Water competition:** tourism uses about 65% of Bali's water (IDEP Foundation figure); 260 of 400 rivers dry; Lake Buyan down 3.5 m; Jatiluwih farmers reported buying water. [K5, K9] — SOURCED: Al Jazeera, "Bali: The tropical Indonesian island that is running out of water," 1 Dec 2019 (read 2026-10-29). The figures are attributed by the article to NGOs and to Stroma Cole's research; not independently checked.
- **Work rhythm:** water released to upstream, middle and downstream groups in turn over about two weeks; rainy-season and dry-season rice plantings, with dry-season alternative crops. [K7] — SOURCED on Wikipedia only.
- **Speech (K11, loose):** Balinese and Indonesian. — general.

### Verdict on the claim to test
**Confirmed, with a narrowing.** Water temples do anchor scheduling for whole watersheds, but sources show the schedules are set by consensus in subak meetings held at regional temples (Lansing 2017), not dictated by priests alone; and historically the supreme temple was also a major landlord and tribute collector, with kings imposing written schedules in water-short regions (Hauser-Schäublin 2011). "Water as the organizing institution of the farming year" holds: the annual cycle ends with the 10th-month festival at Batur, timed after harvest.

## Pick 2 · Aflaj of Oman
*Language note: local customary unit names and local terms that the sources describe as "locally known as" have been kept out of this file and written to the separate `slang_found/Relung_Panen_B04_R1_slang.md`. Office titles (wakil, areef) and the general time-share unit named in UNESCO texts (athar) are kept as institutional terms.*

### A. Snapshot
- **What it is:** a falaj (plural aflaj) is a complete gravity-fed irrigation system serving a settlement; the classical Arabic root means "to divide into shares." Three types: ghaili (diverted from a wadi's base flow; 48% of systems), aini (from perennial springs; 28%), daoudi (underground tunnels from a "mother well" up to 60 m deep at the mountain foot; 24%). [K1] [P8] — SOURCED: ICOMOS evaluation in UNESCO nomination file 1207 (https://webarchive.unesco.org/web/20180108235914/http://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1207.pdf, downloaded and text-extracted 2026-10-29), pp.47–48.
- **Numbers:** "over four thousand systems have been identified in a large-scale survey completed in 2001"; about 3,000 still functioning. National inventory (1997–98, published 2001): 3,648 demand areas; 26,498 ha dependent on falaj water (17,561 ha cropped); annual demand about 459 million m³, 99.8% agricultural. — SOURCED (nomination file 1207, ICOMOS section and §2/§5). By 2015, "a quarter of the aflaj had stopped flowing" and "a third had seen a sharp decline" since the 1990s inventory. [K9] — SOURCED: Fred Pearce, "Oasis at Risk: Oman's Ancient Water Channels Are Drying Up," Yale Environment 360, 1 June 2015 (read 2026-10-29), quoting Slim Zekri (Sultan Qaboos University).
- **World Heritage site:** "Aflaj Irrigation Systems of Oman," inscribed 2006; five systems: Falaj Al-Khatmeen and Falaj Daris (Nizwa), Falaj Al-Malki (Izki), Falaj Al-Jeela (Sur), Falaj Al-Muyassar (Al Rustaq). — SOURCED (nomination file 1207). (UNESCO list page itself not opened.)
- **Defining feature:** water is owned and traded as time shares separate from land. [K1, K6]

### B. What else exists there (structure, economy, institutions)
- **Administration:** "The executive authority in each system is the falaj agent, or wakeel, appointed by the local sheikh in consultation with the stakeholders and advised by a technical expert." The wakil keeps the funds, regulates sale and rental of shares, and oversees daily operation; subordinates (areefs) distribute the water shares. "The system is not based on any form of written or statute law, but rather on a traditional system of time-sharing that is passed from one generation to the next." Larger aflaj have two or three wakils (Al-Malki, Daris and Al-Khatmeen each have two). [K1, K6, K7] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207, ICOMOS p.47 and §5.j).
- **Time shares:** rotation of 7 or 10 days (UNESCO text); shares range "from between 12 hours down to as little as 1.25 minutes." The athar is commonly 30 minutes; "during droughts the athar may be reduced to 15 minutes." Timekeeping by day was by a tall stick's shadow falling on rows of small stones; by night, by "close observation of a total of 24 stars." "Several settlements still preserve their sun-dials." [K1, K7] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207). Bjornlund (IWRA 2023 congress paper) gives a weekly cycle of 336 half-hour shares for one falaj — UNVERIFIED (snippet only; PDF not opened).
- **Ownership, inheritance, rent and auction (the claim):** "The aflaj are owned by the individual shareholders, with certain shares allocated to the mosque … and others to the religious school." Title is recorded in a registration deed; "ownership of these shares is absolute, and they can be inherited." The wakil records all transactions in a register. Some shares "are owned communally by all the shareholders in the falaj, and these are available for rent": weekly rental auctions at a set time and place; bidders place a stick behind the auctioneer (who may be an areef) and withdraw sticks as the price rises until one remains; the wakil collects the money "for overall falaj management purposes." Individuals may also buy or sell shares (at athar level or below); average athar price at nomination (c. 2005) US$8,290. In wet periods, surplus water is diverted out of the settlement to recharge the aquifer. [K1, K6, K8] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207, §5, p.~40 of the dossier).
- **One falaj in detail, Falaj Daris (Nizwa):** built under the Ya'ruba Imamate in the 17th century; according to its current wakil, the Imam merged three smaller aflaj into it and substituted their rights. Total 17 day-and-night units of flow distributed over an eight-day rotation through four named areas; each area has an areef appointed by owners who delivers each holder's turns, then releases water to the next area; each 24-hour turn is split into equal night and day halves "since it is more convenient to irrigate during day time than night." Rights breakdown (by the author's table): endowments (waqf) 50%, state treasury (Bayt al-Mal) 24%, private 14%, falaj-owned rental shares 12%. The falaj-owned day is auctioned weekly for maintenance; a recorder attends and writes bidder, time slot and price in a book. In abundant flow, 32 rental units are offered; in scarcity, 16. Flow: 276 L/s (2004) vs 47 L/s (2009). Endowed shares fund the poor, religious students, local students and foreign students in Nizwa, about 300 mosque-attached farms, a cemetery and walls; endowment revenue for students "has been accumulated in an account of the Bank of Muscat under governmental control." Private rights serve "more than 205 farmers": 89 hold less than half an athar, 90 hold half to one, 26 hold more than one. A rule that only half the nominal flow may be traded in drought is unwritten: "Writing this down may confuse people and it is rather better to know it customarily as the water is fluctuating." The "falaj Daris book," written in 1672 under Imam Sultan bin Saif Al-Ya'rubi, records all rights and is kept by a named family. [K1, K6, K8, K9] [SM: competence shown under pressure] — SOURCED: A.S. Al-Marshoudi (2018), "Water Institutional Arrangements of Falaj Al Daris in the Sultanate of Oman," *International Journal of Social Sciences and Management* 5(1):31–42, DOI 10.3126/ijssm.v5i1.19006 (PDF read 2026-10-29).
- **Order of uses in the settlement:** at the point where the falaj surfaces (often next to a fort or watchtower), water is first taken for drinking and cooking, the next stretch is reserved for ablutions, then it passes the mosque and fort to clothes-washing places; "only after these uses have been satisfied" is it divided to palm groves and crops. [K6, K4] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207, ICOMOS p.47).
- **Falaj Al-Khatmeen's three-way split:** at the town entrance the channel splits into three equal parts, one for local holdings and two for State Treasury land; "if three balls of the same size and weight are thrown into the channel before it splits into three, each will run automatically into a separate branch channel." [K14] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207, ICOMOS p.48).
- **State role and law:** the Ministry took responsibility for maintaining main underground channels in 1981; Royal Decree No. 29/2000 (Water Wealth Protection Law) requires permits for works between mother well and settlement; no new wells within 3.5 km of mother wells. Ministerial Decree 219/2003 formed an inter-ministry committee. [K1] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207).
- **Labor and age (current):** at Al Farfarah village (about 30 minutes inland from Muscat), about 50 owners hold shares from half an hour to 15 hours a week; a 30-minute weekly right costs "a bit over $3,000"; auctions average "$5 for 30 minutes of water"; "The auction fees pay for [the manager's] work, running repairs, and charitable activities." "With young Omanis moving to the cities for work, the old men left in charge employ contract workers from South Asia." Main cause of decline: private pumped boreholes upland lowering the water table below the aflaj. [K7, K8, K9] — SOURCED (Pearce 2015).
- **Community volunteer repair:** in August 2017, local youths in Wadi Al Meh (Al Amerat, Muscat) cleared about 4 km of a falaj blocked by rockfall "with their own resources," citing that "there has been no specific entity to check for defaults." [K14, K1] — SOURCED: Oman Observer, "Youths strive to bring back ancient falaj to its former glory," 7 Aug 2017 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Research body:** University of Nizwa hosts a UNESCO Chair on Aflaj Studies and Archaeo-Hydrology; a 2025 project ("Aflaj Systems and Social Cohesion in Oman, Al Dakhiliyah Governorate," lead Dr Majid Labbaf Khaneiki) studies how shared aflaj link tribes. [K1] — SOURCED: Oman Observer, 23 June 2025 (read 2026-10-29). The Al-Wasit detail (waqf about a quarter of shares, some auctioned weekly for repairs; five tribes around one falaj) — UNVERIFIED (snippet only).

### C. Cultural locations (named)
- **Falaj Daris shari'a and park** (Nizwa): landscaped park around the falaj's surfacing point, with interpretation boards; "among the most visited sites in the Sultanate" with the renovated fort (2005 dossier). — SOURCED (nomination file 1207).
- **Nizwa Fort and Nizwa Souq:** souq in the shadow of the fort, restored. — UNVERIFIED (snippets only).
- **Nizwa Friday livestock market:** sheep, goats and cattle auctioned by walking them in circles; a bookkeeper records sales "as a percentage goes to the state"; "small livestock farming in Oman is usually a woman's business." [K12, K8] — SOURCED: oman.de, "Friday market Nizwa" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Bait Al-Redadah Fort** (Al-Khatmeen's channel runs beneath it; Ya'ruba period, 1649–1711). — SOURCED (nomination file 1207).
- **Per-site buildings:** watchtowers, mosques, ablution blocks, sundials and "small buildings were auctions of the water shares are held" (e.g., at Al-Muyassar, "a sundial and auction building"). At Al-Jeela, the mosque is "the symbol of the unity and place of gathering of this small community." [K10] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207).
- **Traditional mortar workshops** still burn clay cakes with palm wood to make water-resistant mortar. [K14] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207, ICOMOS).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Settlement plan follows the water: houses sited by proximity to the falaj; watchtowers placed "to insure a clear view of the whole demand area"; water's order of use fixes where drinking, washing and the mosque sit along the channel. [K4] [P5] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207).
- Endowed water shares fund mosques, schools and the poor, so religious and educational institutions are partly financed by water auctions. [K8] — SOURCED (Al-Marshoudi 2018).
- Night irrigation turns and star-reading as a skill. [K7, K14] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207; Pearce 2015: "eight villages still distributed water according to the stars" about a decade before 2015).

### E. Structural features
- **Crops:** date palms dominant; lemons, fodder, sugar cane, seasonal crops; goats, sheep, cows, poultry. [K12] — SOURCED (nomination file 1207).
- **Cost and scarcity:** auction prices rise and fall with flow; administrators restrict the volume offered to keep demand. [K8] — SOURCED (Al-Marshoudi 2018).
- **Settings in decay:** "mud forts watchtowers and traditional houses are mostly neglected and abandoned"; palm groves replaced by houses; roads cut channels. [K9] — SOURCED (ICOMOS, nomination file 1207).
- **Speech (K11, loose):** Arabic (Omani dialects). — general.

### Verdict on the claim to test
**Confirmed and sharpened.** Water from one channel is divided by time shares that are inherited (absolute, deeded), bought and sold (at athar level), rented annually, and auctioned weekly (communally owned shares, proceeds to maintenance). Two additions the claim missed: large blocks of shares are religious endowments and state-treasury property (at Falaj Daris, 74% together), and drought rules shrink what can be traded, by unwritten custom.

## Pick 4 · Valencia's Water Tribunal (and Murcia's Council of Good Men), Spain
*Language note: the court's spoken ritual formulas (in Valencian) are recorded only in the separate slang file; this file gives their sense in English.*

### A. Snapshot
- **What they are:** customary irrigators' courts on Spain's Mediterranean coast, inscribed together in 2009 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity as "Irrigators' tribunals of the Spanish Mediterranean coast: the Council of Wise Men of the plain of Murcia and the Water Tribunal of the plain of Valencia." UNESCO: "customary legal institutions for water management" dating from al-Andalus (9th–13th centuries); both recognized by Spanish law; proceedings oral, marked by "speed, transparency and impartiality"; members "democratically elected." Murcia's council has "seven members" with authority over a community of 23,313 members; Valencia's tribunal has eight elected syndics representing nine irrigation communities totaling 11,691 members. [K1, K6] — SOURCED: UNESCO ICH page, element 00171 (Spanish, read 2026-10-29).
- **Defining feature:** a weekly public court that settles irrigation disputes orally, on the spot, with no appeal. [K1, K6]

### B. What else exists there (structure, institutions)
**Valencia, Tribunal de las Aguas de la Vega de Valencia**
- **When and where:** every Thursday at noon (12:00) at the Door of the Apostles of Valencia Cathedral; "simplicity in functioning, without complex protocols." — SOURCED: tribunaldelasaguas.org (home and "Funcionamiento" pages, read 2026-10-29); El Debate, 12 Nov 2023 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Who:** nine irrigation communities: Quart, Benàger i Faitanar, Tormos, Mislata, Chirivella, Mestalla, Favara, Rascanya and Rovella. — SOURCED (tribunaldelasaguas.org). Syndics must be farmers who cultivate their own land and have "the reputation of an honest man"; they wear the traditional black farmer's smock. — SOURCED (El Debate 2023). The president has traditionally been one of the syndics of the Favara or Tormos communities. — UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Procedure:** the defendant is summoned by the canal's guard and may be called up to three times before judgment in absentia. The bailiff (Alguacil), carrying a two-pronged gilded brass hook, calls the accused of each canal in the order in which the canals draw water from the river, from Quart to Rovella. The guard (or a private accuser) states the case; the president asks the accused for his answer. "Entirely verbal, in Valencian, with no lawyers or written documents"; witnesses and on-site inspection are allowed. The syndic of the defendant's own canal does not take part; if the defendant is from a right-bank canal, the left-bank syndics propose the sentence, and vice versa. Guilty verdicts condemn to "penalty and costs, damages and losses, according to Ordinances." "No appeals exist"; the sentence is enforced by the syndic of the accused's canal. [K1, K6, K4] — SOURCED (tribunaldelasaguas.org "Funcionamiento").
- **Typical cases:** water theft or misuse, breaking the irrigation turn, failure to clean channels, damage to works. [K6, K7] — SOURCED (El Debate 2023).
- **After the court:** the syndics move to the Casa-Vestuario (a building by the cathedral) to deal with common water business, including river flow and canal gate settings. [K1, K10] — SOURCED (tribunaldelasaguas.org).
- **Scale and history:** "17,000 hectares" of irrigated land served by "eight mother canals" from the Turia, divided into 138 shares of water; origins in al-Andalus, "perfected" after the conquest by King James I; survived the Bourbon centralization and the 1812 Cadiz Cortes; recognized by the 1978 Constitution and the Valencian Statute of Autonomy. — SOURCED (tribunaldelasaguas.org "Historia"; the 17,000 ha appears to be a historical figure and is not dated on the page).
- **Recognition:** Valencia's tribunal was already listed as a Bien de Interés Cultural under Valencian Heritage Law 4/1998 by 2008. — SOURCED (Montaner Salas 2008, below).

**Murcia, Consejo de Hombres Buenos de la Huerta de Murcia**
- **Origins:** said to derive from irrigation administration after the Islamic founding of Murcia city in 831; Alfonso X's privileges of 1266 authorized the city's officials to "choose each year two good men" to have the main drains of the huerta cleaned; huerta ordinances compiled in a 14th-century codex (punishing "the man who takes water that is not his"); recompilation under Charles II in 1695 with procedural guarantees; in the 18th century a Water Judge took over dispute jurisdiction; landholders formed a representative commission in 1785; the council itself is first regulated in the 1849 "Ordinances for the Regime and Government of the Huerta of Murcia," reprinted by Pedro Díaz Cassou as the "Ordinances and Customs of the Huerta of Murcia," chapter 17. [K1, K6] — SOURCED: Elena Montaner Salas, "El Consejo de Hombres Buenos, patrimonio oral e inmaterial de la Región de Murcia," *Papeles de Geografía* 47–48 (2008):185–191 (PDF read 2026-10-29).
- **Sessions:** the 1849 ordinance text (art. 165) says the council meets in public at the town hall every Thursday (and, in the old text, Sunday) from nine to twelve; chaired by the mayor or his delegate, who votes only to break a tie (art. 166). Since 21 October 1999: a president, secretary, 5 member-judges and 5 substitute members. [K1] — SOURCED (Montaner Salas 2008). Current practice: Thursdays in public in the town hall's main hall; oral, public sentence. — SOURCED in summary form on Wikipedia "Council of Wise Men of the plain of Murcia" (read 2026-10-29), which also gives 1848 as founding date (conflicts with 1849 above).
- **Legal standing:** under Organic Law 13/1999, a Murcia High Court ruling of 30 May 2003 held that the council's decisions "cannot be reviewed" by ordinary courts because they are "authentic rulings" of a tribunal with its own jurisdiction; its brief oral procedure still guarantees hearing, contradiction and evidence. [K1, K6] — SOURCED (Montaner Salas 2008).
- **Threat named by the source:** "if the huerta does not exist, the mission of these customary courts will also disappear and the Council of Good Men will never meet again." [K9] — SOURCED (Montaner Salas 2008, p.190, translated).

### C. Cultural locations (named)

## Pick 5 · Acequias of New Mexico (United States)
*Language note: statutory office titles (mayordomo, commissioners) and the member term used in statute-adjacent official documents (parciantes) are kept. ### A. Snapshot
- **What they are:** community irrigation ditches (acequias), each a self-governing association of the landowners with water rights from that ditch ("parciantes"), with three elected commissioners and a mayordomo. [K1] — SOURCED: NMSA §73-2-21 text via FindLaw (read 2026-10-29); New Mexico Magazine, Stanley Crawford, "Why We Love NM: Our Acequias Feed the Land," 12 July 2023 (read 2026-10-29).
- **How many:** "~700 Acequias in New Mexico" across the Rio Grande and tributaries, San Juan, Rio Chama, Nambe/Pojoaque/Tesuque, Pecos (incl. Gallinas), Canadian (Mora), Rio Pueblo/Rio Embudo, Jemez, Rio San Jose, Hondo, Gila and Mimbres. — SOURCED: New Mexico Acequia Association (NMAA), "Overview of Acequia Policy Issues," presentation to the Land Grant Legislative Interim Committee, 12 July 2023 (PDF on nmlegis.gov, text extracted and read 2026-10-29). Other figures: "up to 1,000" (Crawford 2023), "some 800" (snippet, UNM Utton Center). Counts differ by source.
- **Legal status:** practices "crystallized into law" by territorial assemblies of 1851–52 (act of 7 January 1852); pre-1907 water rights recognized by the state constitution; acequias governed by statute as special districts and "defined as political subdivisions of the state in 1965" (NMAA 2023). [K1] — 1965 and constitution: SOURCED (NMAA 2023). 1851–52 act: UNVERIFIED (snippet of a court case at cite.case.law, not opened).
- **Defining feature:** a ditch owned and worked in common, run by an elected ditch boss, opened each spring by collective cleaning. [K1, K7]

### B. What else exists there (structure, institutions)
- **Statutory powers:** commissioners may "assess fatigue work or tasks of all parties owning water rights," "make all necessary assessments to provide funds for the payment of the salary of the mayordomo," and "receive money in lieu of such fatigue or task work." The mayordomo has "the superintendence of all work thereon, the distribution of the waters thereof and the collection of fines, if any," and reports in writing to the commissioners on the first Monday in June and the last Monday in September. Commissioners' proceedings must be written in books that are "public property." [K1, K6, K7] — SOURCED: NMSA 1978 §73-2-21 (FindLaw text, read 2026-10-29). Officers: three commissioners and one mayordomo, each owning an interest in the ditch; commissioners elect a chair, secretary and treasurer on or before the first Monday of January (§73-2-12). — UNVERIFIED (Justia page 403; seen in snippet).
- **Pay:** the mayordomo receives "a modest monthly salary." — SOURCED (Crawford 2023).
- **Spring cleaning:** in late March or early April "when water resumes flowing," overseen by the mayordomo; everyone works as a ditch digger in sections of six to eight feet marked by the mayordomo (enough room to shovel "without banging into each other"). — SOURCED (Crawford 2023). Workers use shovels, chainsaws and rakes, and cut and burn overgrown grasses and brush; "Women also had the duty on these days of providing meals for the workers"; families without available labor "could contract with a day laborer"; it "served as a rite of passage" for young men; from the 1930s, as men migrated for work, women "took up the management of the farms" and hired substitutes; today the communal cleaning is "waning" as acequias hire firms with machinery. [K2, K7, K14, K4] [SM: competence shown under pressure] — SOURCED: Dabi García, "La Saca y la Limpia: Some Thoughts on a Springtime Practice, Ritual and Rite of Passage," NMAA blog, 22 Mar 2024 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Fine for absence:** a fine "imposed on an irrigator who fails to show up for the" annual ditch cleaning; "monies collected enable the mayordomo to hire" substitute workers. [K6] — SOURCED: UNM Utton Center, "Glossary – Land Grants, Acequias and the Law" CLE workshop (PDF, extracted and read 2026-10-29).
- **Sharing in shortage:** "in times of shortage it is not possible to get water to every field"; some communities prioritize "kitchen gardens, then … larger gardens and orchards, then … alfalfa"; on the Gallinas River "eight acequias take turns diverting"; in some acequias members call the mayordomo to ask for water, others keep rotation schedules; "Mayordomos are on the front line … dealing with complaints." [K1, K6] — SOURCED: Janice Varela, "Acequia Customs and Water Management," NMAA blog, 26 Apr 2012 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Control over transfers:** under a 2003 law an acequia with the right bylaw language "has authority to deny transfers" of water rights into or out of it; NMAA reasons that transfers out mean "fewer parciantes for collective work and governance." [K1, K9] — SOURCED (NMAA 2023).
- **State money:** Acequia and Community Ditch Fund (created 1988) for legal defense in water-rights adjudications (one adjudication "Filed in 1966, Settled in 2013," with members "named as defendants"); ACDF at $830,000 with a request for $1 million; Acequia and Community Ditch Infrastructure Fund (SB 428, 2019; first full cycle FY23) receiving $2.5 million a year from the Irrigation Works Construction Fund; the Interstate Stream Commission created an Acequia Bureau (five staff). NMAA assists "over 100 acequias per year with governance and financial compliance." [K8] — SOURCED (NMAA 2023).
- **Disasters:** 2022 wildfires: Hermits Peak/Calf Canyon (341,735 acres, 45+ damaged acequias), Black Fire (325,136 acres, 24), Cerro Pelado (45,605 acres, 1); burn-scar floods destroyed diversions and choked ditches with silt (e.g., Acequia del Lado Sombrillo, Gascon, Mora County). [K9, K5] — SOURCED (NMAA 2023).
- **Use it or lose it:** four years of non-use can forfeit a water right in New Mexico; newcomers unfamiliar with acequia duties. [K9] — SOURCED: Ilana Newman, Daily Yonder, reprinted in Durango Telegraph "Ditch democracy," 22 May 2025 (read 2026-10-29), quoting a Taos-area parciante: "The governance of the acequias is the longest historical practice of democracy in the U.S."
- **Advocacy body:** New Mexico Acequia Association, Santa Fe; working since 1989; programs include the Congreso de las Acequias, Sembrando Semillas, Acequia Youth Conservation Corps, Acequia Culture Youth Leadership Institute, Los Sembradores Farmer Training Project. [K1, K14] — SOURCED (NMAA 2023; lasacequias.org/about, read 2026-10-29).

## Pick 6 · Bisses of Valais, Switzerland
*Language note: "bisse" (French) and "Suone" (German) are the regional names for the channels, and "consortage" is the legal form of the water association; these are kept. Local names of offices, devices and a local saying are recorded only in the slang file.*

### A. Snapshot
- **What they are:** gravity irrigation channels in the canton of Valais, many cut into or hung on cliff faces, bringing glacier and river water to dry slopes. "More than 600 bisses" are registered in Valais; the longest active one is 14 km; the highest intake is at 2,890 m; the oldest are attested in the 10th century; the bisses appear on the Swiss 100-franc note issued 12 September 2019. [K14] — SOURCED: MySwitzerland (Switzerland Tourism), "Un patrimoine précieux" (French, read 2026-10-29).
- **Recognition:** "traditional irrigation: knowledge, technique and organization" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage on 6 December 2023 (18th session, Kasane, Botswana), a seven-country nomination coordinated by Austria; Swiss participants include water-channel cooperatives in the Oberwalliser Sonnenberge, Ayent, Lens, Trient, Nendaz and Grächen, and the irrigated meadows of Bern and Lucerne. Communities developed written rules: rods, "water letters," bulletins, sweeping/rotation regulations and water registers. [K1, K6] — SOURCED: Swiss Federal Office of Culture, "Traditional irrigation" (bak.admin.ch, read 2026-10-29). (Other sources give 5 December; the BAK date is used.)
- **Defining feature:** channels on dangerous ground that need yearly rebuilding by the shareholders. [K14, K1]

### B. What else exists there (structure, institutions)
**Savièse and the Bisse du Torrent-Neuf (main worked example)**
- **Place:** Savièse, on the right bank of the Rhône above Sion, plateaus at 700–1,400 m below Mount Prabé; dry climate, about 600 mm of rain a year and about 2,000 hours of sunshine. — SOURCED: Rose Marie Roten Dumoulin, "La quête de l'eau à Savièse" (PDF hosted by the Musée valaisan des Bisses, bisses-valais.ch; undated; text extracted and read 2026-10-29).
- **Construction contract:** in 1430 the people of Savièse, backed by men of Sion, hired an engineer to build a channel along the north face of the Prabé, about 200 m above the old one; price "72 fichelins of wheat, 32 setiers of wine and 800 florins"; about 7 km, "two thirds cut into and hung on the rock"; the old channel was retired only in 1448. Larch beams (about 1.5 m, 20×20 cm) were set in holes drilled in the rock to carry fir planks; from the 18th century each beam bore the date it was installed, to aid maintenance. [K14] — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin).
- **Shareholders over time (Torrent-Neuf):** 1450: 107 owners, 22,237.5 ares irrigated; 1594: 196, 57,613.5; 1671: 291, 59,219.3; 1772: 292, 59,640.0. The author reads stagnation in owners and irrigated area as a sign of growing poverty through successive inheritance divisions. Shares rose from 800 to 830 after 19th-century works. [K8, K9] — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin).
- **Administration:** a communal affair until 1810, when Savièse created a consortage; turns continued to be set by quarter (by storage pond). The chief officer (from the 15th century) was often elected from the richest owners "because he must advance the money for the annual maintenance"; a registrar kept the book of rights and transfers; elected pond-keepers released water to shareholders who had first shown their right slip to a distributor at the main division point; about two-thirds of water was taken there and one-third at the ponds; from the 18th century a patrol officer walked the channel almost daily and handled opening and closing. Six main storage ponds around 1,000 m held water overnight to even out flow; they were cleaned of silt yearly. A sawmill was installed in the 19th century at Brac on the Prabé's north face to supply rebuilding. [K1, K7] — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin).
- **Yearly work rhythm (until 1936):** inspection in early April by a small team climbing the Prabé face; water in by 15 April; for two weeks "200 workers" on site while "about fifty women and children" carried sacks of fir twigs and moss to caulk the plank joints; men took turns standing in the current to slow it while forest humus sealed the gaps; the parish priest blessed the workers at the start and the water at the chapel of Ste-Marguerite. [K2, K7, K14, K4] [SM: competence shown under pressure] — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin).
- **Community statutes (K6):** 1447: no diverting water in irrigation season; turns set by elected pond-keepers; gardens watered only from an hour and a half after sunset; fine 10 sous ("in 1929, 20 francs"); no selling or alienating water without the syndics' consent; everyone must attend maintenance work "at the stated hour except for valid excuses," fine 3½ sous. 1619: no outsider or resident may use any water without the keepers' consent and turn, fine 20 sous; selling water to an outsider forfeits all water rights; workers report at sunrise; half of a theft fine goes to the person harmed. A woman marrying outside the community had to sell her water and alpine rights to a shareholder. From the 15th century no more cattle on the alp than one had fed through winter. — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin).
- **Conflict:** water from the Sionne brought lawsuits with Sion (1576 ruling cited); the Morge intake led to violent disputes with neighboring Conthey (under Savoy) until the war of 1475. [K9] — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin).
- **End of the cliff channel:** a 4,800 m tunnel through the Prabé, begun after 1929 and finished 1935, supplied drinking and irrigation water; the commune guaranteed the consortage the same flow from 10 April to 20 September, raised to 0.40 L/s per right; the cliff section on the north face "will be dynamited to avoid accidents." [K9] — SOURCED (Roten Dumoulin). Restoration as a hiking route by an association in 2005 with suspension footbridges — UNVERIFIED (snippets; the association's site gives no history).
- **Today:** trail of 9 km (2.4 km along the channel), from Les Biniis (1,037 m) to Tripon (955 m), between Ste-Marguerite and the Brac refreshment bar. — SOURCED: valais.ch "Bisse du Torrent-Neuf" (read 2026-10-29). Closed from 1 November and "totally forbidden in case of rain." — SOURCED: torrent-neuf.ch (read 2026-10-29).

**Other channels and general practice**
- **Bisse d'Ayent:** built 1442; source at the Tseuzier reservoir; the wooden section on the Torrent-Croix cliff was abandoned in 1831 when a tunnel was dug and was restored (not reused) in 1991; "Near the entrance of the tunnel is a hammer which allowed the watchman of the bisse to know if the watercourse continued to flow normally"; in water June to October; about two-thirds of its length still carries water. — SOURCED: Wikipedia "Bisse d'Ayent" (read 2026-10-29). Water-driven hammer as an alarm (a slowed or silent hammer meant a blockage, heard even at night): UNVERIFIED in detail (snippet).
- **Upper Valais (Sonnenberge: Naters, Ausserberg, Eggerberg, Baltschieder):** spring work parties need 10 workers for one channel and 20 for another; a 14-day rotation on one; water costs 5 francs a year per irrigation hour on that channel; "Who has the right to water, at what hour and for how long: this has always been recorded in writing." Named Landscape of the Year 2020. [K6, K7, K8] — SOURCED (MySwitzerland).
- **Danger:** a 2022 Swiss article: those who built and maintained the channels on cliff faces risked their lives and "many found death"; it repeats a tradition that prisoners who volunteered for dangerous wall work were pardoned afterward. — SOURCED: Üsé Meyer, "Heilige Wasser am Abgrund," *Der Hauseigentümer*, 1 Sept 2022 (read 2026-10-29); the pardon story is reported as tradition, not documented. Modern hiking deaths on bisse paths: Bisse d'Ayent, 24 Jan 2015 (snippet: Rhône FM); Bisse du Ro, Crans-Montana, 2 May 2024 (snippet). — UNVERIFIED (snippets only).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

### Verdict on the claim to test
**Confirmed.** Channels hung on cliffs were maintained by shared, compulsory duty (attendance at the stated hour, fines for absence, rebuild every spring), and the work was dangerous enough that the cliff section at Savièse was dynamited after replacement "to avoid accidents." Additions: rights were recorded in registers and traded within the community but not to outsiders; the chief officer was often the richest man because he advanced maintenance costs; and the craft ended on the main example in 1935, when a tunnel replaced it.
