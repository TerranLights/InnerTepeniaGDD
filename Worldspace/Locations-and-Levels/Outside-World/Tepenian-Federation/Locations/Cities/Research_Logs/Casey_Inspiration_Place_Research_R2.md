*Round 1 covered the economy, employers, tourism figures, major museums, venues, breweries, the Snowbowl dispute, altitude running and the Southside's origin as mill-worker housing. This section adds neighborhood life, subcultures, festivals and civic bodies Round 1 did not reach.*

### A. Other settings (partial)

- **The Southside (south of the tracks and Route 66).** "One of Flagstaff's oldest neighborhoods," settled by "African American lumber mill workers, Basque sheepherders, and Italian and Mexican immigrants." **SOURCED** — NAU Stories, "Black history in Flagstaff's Southside neighborhood," 2023-02-28, https://in.nau.edu/stories/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/5431. Black, Mexican and Indigenous (Diné and Hopi) residents lived there together while white residents lived north of the tracks; the area long lacked sidewalks and paved streets. **SOURCED** — NAU Center for Health Equity Research (CHER), *Fairness First* podcast page, "Deborah Harris," 2022-09-06, https://in.nau.edu/center-community-health-engaged-research/blog/fairness-first-podcast-deborah-harris/.
  - **Flooding:** Harris states the Rio de Flag "was purposefully diverted into the Southside" so that more affluent areas would not flood; the Southside Community Association now works with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and the city on the Rio de Flag Flood Control Project. **SOURCED** — CHER page above (her account, as quoted; not independently checked against Corps or city records).
  - **Dunbar School → Murdoch Community Center**, 203 E. Brannen Ave. Dunbar was the segregated school for Black children; teacher and principal **Cleo Wilson Murdoch** (died at 39) is its namesake after it became a community center. Principal **Wilson Riles**, one of the first Black students at the college that became NAU, worked with superintendent Sturgeon Cromer to desegregate Flagstaff's public schools in **1953**, a year before *Brown v. Board*. **SOURCED** — NAU Stories 2023-02-28. Address **UNVERIFIED** (Coconino County calendar snippet; page 403).
  - In the 1920s more than 500 Black workers were moved by the Cady Lumber Company from Louisiana to McNary, Arizona; some later moved on to Flagstaff, 1940s–50s. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippet attributed to NAU pages; not opened).
  - **Green Book sites:** Flagstaff had Green Book–listed lodging, including the **Motel DeBeau** downtown. **SOURCED** — KNAU, 2021-06-18, https://www.knau.org/knau-and-arizona-news/2021-06-18/flagstaff-expo-highlights-black-owned-businesses-on-juneteenth. An NAU undergraduate (Ty Holliday) built a digital "Green Book Passport" of Southside sites. **SOURCED** — NAU Stories 2023-02-28.
- **Hispanic neighborhoods (old names).** Our Lady of Guadalupe parish tied together *plaza nueva* (New Town), *plaza vieja* (Old Town), *la loma* (the hill) and *Los Chantes*; Los Chantes was built by the Arizona Lumber and Timber Company for mill workers, and "the original houses are now a shopping center"; La Plaza Vieja is described as a remnant of the town's first neighborhood. Named institutions: La Ciudad de México Grocery; La Iglesia Metodista El Divino Redentor (1892). **UNVERIFIED** — search snippets of NAU Special Collections, *Los Recuerdos del Barrio en Flagstaff* (oral-history project begun 1997 by Delia Ceballos Muñoz); the pages failed to load (SSL error and no HTTP response), as in Round 1.
- **Sunnyside (east side, beyond McMillan Mesa).** Bounded by McMillan Mesa, East Route 66, Fourth Street and Cedar Avenue; 1925 subdivision of 160 acres of the former L. E. Johnson ranch, sold by Alf Dickinson & Son; the city neighborhood with the highest Hispanic share (40.7%, a commercial data site). **UNVERIFIED** — all from snippets (*Arizona Daily Sun* "100 years ago" column, paywalled; skyscraperpage forum; simplemaps). Deborah Harris is described as modeling neighborhood organizing for Sunnyside. **SOURCED** — CHER page.
- **Museum of Northern Arizona campus**, 3101 N. Fort Valley Rd., two miles from downtown on US 180: the **Easton Collection Center**, designed with a Native American Advisory Committee, faces east to the rising sun; a glazed solar aperture marks the equinoxes on its inner door and the solstice path on the vestibule wall; Native artists "regularly visit the collection center to learn from the art of their ancestors"; MNA holds 800,000+ objects and specimens. **SOURCED** — MNA, *Summer Heritage Festival returns July 2 & 3, 2022* (press release PDF), https://musnaz.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2022-Heritage-Festival-Press-Release.pdf (read via local pdftotext).
- **NAU Native American Cultural Center** — opened October 2011; 12,540 sq ft; $7 M ($3 M donations including $2 M from the San Manuel Band of Mission Indians, $4 M university funds); "the only facility of its kind serving all Arizona tribes"; houses student organizations, recruitment and retention; at opening NAU had "1,000+ Native American students from 105 tribes." **SOURCED** — NAU News, "NAU unveils noteworthy Native American Cultural Center," https://in.nau.edu/news/nau-unveils-noteworthy-native-american-cultural-center/.
- **Fire-crew bases:** "Hotshot Ranch" (formerly the Ski & Spur Ranch) at the base of the San Francisco Peaks, home of the Flagstaff Hotshots; the Mormon Lake Work Center on the south end of Flagstaff. **SOURCED** — Coconino National Forest, "Fire Resources," https://www.fs.usda.gov/r03/coconino/fire/resources.

### B. Subcultures (partial)

- **Wildland firefighters.** The **Flagstaff Interagency Hotshot Crew** began 1972 as the Coconino Hotshots, "one of the first three Hotshot crews established in the Arizona and New Mexico region," formed from "a 10-person Hopi suppression module combined with another 10 local individuals"; renamed Flagstaff Hotshots 1973. The **Mormon Lake IHC** was formed 1976 (moved to Flagstaff's Mormon Lake Work Center 1979); the **Blue Ridge IHC** (1973) is at Happy Jack. The forest also runs three 10-person hand crews, 12 engines, four water tenders and four dozers. **SOURCED** — Coconino NF "Fire Resources" (above).
- **Native students.** NAU's fall 2024 entering class had its "highest percentage of new Native American students ever (4.2%)" and of new Hispanic students (29%); 40% of all undergraduates are first-generation; 41% of new students Pell-eligible. **SOURCED** — NAU News, 2024-08-26, https://in.nau.edu/news/class-of-2028-numbers/ (preliminary, pre-census counts). Total fall 2024 American Indian enrollment 1,109 (4% of 28,468) — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of an NAU fact sheet; the flyer PDF returned 404).
- **Black community.** Organized around the Murdoch Center and the **Southside Community Association** (executive director Deborah Harris, NAU staff 25 years until 2017); the SCA runs "Lived Black Experience" town halls (social justice, criminal justice, institutional racism, mental health, economic impact, inclusion). **SOURCED** — CHER page. The **Coconino County African Diaspora Advisory Council** (chair Khara House in 2021: "The history of the Black community of Flagstaff is Flagstaff's history"). **SOURCED** — KNAU 2021-06-18.
- **Diné and Hopi shoppers and residents.** Navajo name recorded as *Kin Łání Dookʼoʼoosłííd Biyaagi*, "Many Houses Below San Francisco Mountains" — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of a UNM Hillerman site). Around 1969, Diné complaints about disrespectful treatment by businesses in border towns (Gallup, Farmington, Holbrook, Winslow, Flagstaff) led Flagstaff to form an **Indian Relations Committee** (chair Frank Dickerson) meeting six times a year; members of **15 western-reservation chapters** shopped frequently in Flagstaff. **SOURCED** — Bill Donovan, "50 Years Ago: Flagstaff responds to complaints from Diné," *Navajo Times*, 2019-04-25, https://navajotimes.com/50years/50-years-ago-flagstaff-responds-to-complaints-from-dine/.
- **Hispanic heritage organizers.** **Flagstaff Nuestras Raíces**, a 501(c)(3) "promoting Hispanic culture and history through events, gatherings, the performing and visual arts"; runs the **Flagstaff Summertime Tardeada** (mariachi and folklórico, from 2014), **Celebraciones de la Gente** (Día de los Muertos at MNA, from 2003), an Our Lady of Guadalupe heritage project, Hispanic veterans slideshows, sugar-skull and *calavera literaria* workshops. **SOURCED** — https://flagstaffnuestrasraices.org/tag/news/.

### C. Leisure industries (partial)

### E. Cultural activities and civic life (partial)

- **MNA Heritage Festival of Arts and Culture** (the Fourth of July–weekend festival). MNA has held a festival that weekend since the **first Hopi Craftsmen Show (1930)**, created "to give artists the chance to sell their unique artwork for a fair price"; "by 1932 a festival was added for Navajo artists"; Zuni festival from 1987; canceled only during World War II and in 2020; in 2021 it was rebuilt as one festival open to Native artists from all Colorado Plateau tribes. The 2022 edition had nearly 200 artists (34 first-timers; four returning for the first time since MNA's last Pai Festival in 2003), mainstage dance groups (Olla Maidens, Zuni; Polequaptewa Dance Group, Hopi; Dineh Tahʼ Dancers, Diné), piki-bread baking (Evelyn Numkena), Zuni games (Elroy Natachu), natural dyeing (Roy Kady), a group painting raffle by Art of the People for the Buddy Whitethorne Foundation; free shuttle from Flagstaff High School parking courtesy of Twin Arrows Resort and Casino; sponsors NEH, Arizona Tourism, Creative Flagstaff, Garlands, Bashas'. Arizona Office of Tourism award 2018. **SOURCED** — MNA 2022 press release (above). MNA's stated reason for one festival: "Culture-specific festivals that feature only the Hopi, Navajo, and Zuni left out many other Native people of the region," plus strain on staff, volunteers and budget. **SOURCED** — MNA Artist FAQs, https://musnaz.org/all-upcoming-events/heritagefestival/artist-resources/artistfaqs/. 2026 dates June 26–28, admission $10–25. **SOURCED** — https://musnaz.org/events/2026-06-26/.
  - ⚠ **Conflict with Round 1:** Round 1 recorded (UNVERIFIED snippet) that the Navajo Festival began in **August 1949**; MNA's own 2022 release says Navajo artists were added "by **1932**." Both left in place; the 1949 date may refer to a separately organized Navajo show (not checked).
- **Hopi Arts & Cultural Festival** — "the only all-Hopi arts and cultural festival in Northern Arizona"; run by the **Hopi Arts and Education Association** (501(c)(3)); 14th edition Aug 23–24, 2025, at Flagstaff Mall; pottery, baskets, paintings, jewelry, katsina dolls, Hopi dances, silent auction, frybread; free; proceeds fund scholarships for Hopi students and artists. **SOURCED** — KNAU calendar, https://www.knau.org/community-calendar/event/14th-annual-hopi-arts-amp-cultural-festival-09-04-2025-12-52-27. 2026 edition Aug 22–23 — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Celebraciones de la Gente** — Día de los Muertos at MNA, from 2003 (above); 2026: 22nd edition, Oct 25–26, with Mariachi Agave (Tucson) and Ballet Folklórico de Colores (Flagstaff) — **UNVERIFIED** for the 2026 details (snippet).
- **Flagstaff Juneteenth Festival & Celebration** — Southside Community Association with the County African Diaspora Advisory Council; 2026: Sat. June 20, 10 am–4 pm, Murdoch Center parking lot; free; food, live music, prizes, vendors, educational performances. **UNVERIFIED** (county calendar snippet; page 403). ## 2 · Memphis, Tennessee [SECONDARY]

*Round 1 covered the freight crossroads, FedEx's night sort, the employers, Beale Street's history, the studios and museums, Memphis in May, barbecue, jookin', the civil-rights sites and COGIC. This round adds neighborhoods, migrant and occupational groups, how Beale Street is run today, fan and youth culture, and further festivals and third places.*

### A. Other settings

- **Orange Mound** (southeast of Midtown, beside the Liberty Bowl stadium). Built from 1890 on the former Deaderick plantation by developer Elzey Eugene Meacham (spelled "Meachem" on Wikipedia) as a subdivision for African Americans, lots selling for under $100; early homes were narrow shotgun houses. Residents owned rather than rented. Billed in the 1970s as "the largest concentration of blacks in the United States except for Harlem." Population loss came with desegregation, and crack cocaine hit in the 1980s–90s. Revitalization since then has "substantially reduced crime." About 14,400 residents, 95% African American. Anchors: **Melrose High School** (renovated stadium 2009), **Mount Moriah Missionary Baptist Church** (1926), **Mt. Pisgah CME Church**. Rap duo 8Ball & MJG grew up there. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Orange Mound, Memphis," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_Mound,_Memphis. Annexed by Memphis 1919. Present-day arts groups include **TONE** (Black arts and community development nonprofit), **Unapologetic** (music/arts collective founded by Orange Mound native producer IMAKEMADBEATS) and **Mama Sundry** (co-founded by singer Talibah Safiya). There is a neighborhood Juneteenth whose attendance "nearly doubled" in its third year, and the *Brick X Brick* exhibition was part of the Tennessee Triennial. **SOURCED** — Alex Greene, "Memphis Neighborhoods: Orange Mound," *Memphis Magazine*, 2023-08-08, https://memphismagazine.com/features/memphis-neighborhoods-orange-mound/. Lot sizes of 25×100 ft at $40 with a 50-cent deposit, and the claim that Mattie Deaderick asked Meacham not to sell to Black buyers, are **UNVERIFIED** (snippets, BlackPast / Atlanta Black Star).
- **Binghampton** (Midtown–East Memphis edge): a mixed neighborhood of long-time Black residents and resettled refugees. **Caritas Village**, 2509 Harvard Ave., is in a former Masonic building and opened December 2006. It has a restaurant serving about 20 free meals a day, community meals, a Rhodes College after-school theater program, a children's reading group, a free medical clinic on some Tuesdays, rotating art shows, and Centro Cultural Latino workshops. Founder Onie Johns moved there from suburban Germantown in 2000: "Often there are people in suit and ties, neighborhood folks and homeless folks all eating in there together." **SOURCED** — Lance Wiedower, *High Ground News*, 2016-09-01, https://fromcommonground.com/features/CaritasVillage.aspx (the site has moved from highgroundnews.com). Closed June 2017 for renovation after Johns stepped down; reopened after 14 months — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Soulsville (South Memphis), East McLemore Ave.:** the **Stax Music Academy** began June 2000 in an elementary-school cafeteria with 125 children and moved in 2002 into its own building near the Stax Museum. **SOURCED** — *American Songwriter*, "A Legacy Lives On at Stax Music Academy," https://americansongwriter.com/legacy-lives-stax-music-academy/ (undated on the page read). Soulsville Charter School opened July 2005 with 60 sixth graders and had about 626 students in grades 6–12 — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Crosstown Concourse** (verifies Round 1's snippet). This was the 1927 Sears retail store and catalog plant (640,000 sq ft at opening, 1927-08-27), closed 1993. It reopened 2017-08-19 after a $200 M conversion and is now 1 M sq ft over 14 stories. At opening it was 96% occupied with 200+ residents and 41 tenants: Crosstown High (charter), Memphis Teacher Residency, Church Health, Crosstown Arts residencies and performance spaces, a YMCA, and restaurants. About 400,000 sq ft is apartments, and the project created 700+ jobs. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Crosstown Concourse," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosstown_Concourse.
- **Shelby Farms Park** (east). About 4,500 acres, owned by Shelby County and run since 2007 by the Shelby Farms Park Conservancy. The land held the Nashoba Community (1825), described as an education experiment for formerly enslaved people. **From 1929 to 1964 it was the county penal farm**, worked by incarcerated people. In 2016 the $52 M "Heart of the Park" project enlarged Patriot Lake (renamed Hyde Lake), added trails and a visitor center, and planted 1 M+ trees, after which visits "more than doubled." A bison herd of about 45 lives on 56 acres. The **Shelby Farms Greenline** is a 12.49-mile rail trail to Midtown, extended October 2025. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, "Shelby Farms," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelby_Farms. Annual visitors "1 million+" vs "3 million": **UNVERIFIED and in conflict** (snippets).
- **Hickory Hill** (southeast): middle- and lower-income, majority-Black, with the city's second-largest Hispanic community (Hispanic population up "nearly 700 percent" over a decade). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of Wikipedia / *High Ground News*).

## 3 · Reno, Nevada [SECONDARY]

### A. Other settings

- **Sparks, the rail-yard town (Round 1 open thread).** In 1904 the Southern Pacific moved its division point from Wadsworth to the eastern Truckee Meadows. "Moving day from Wadsworth came on July 1, 1904": houses were dismantled and carried on flatcars. The yard had "a roundhouse — the largest west of Chicago" (40 stalls), dismantled in 1959. The last remnant is an acre-sized machine shop near the Nugget Casino, with an uncertain future. The town was nearly named for Union Pacific president E. H. Harriman, who declined. Lillard Park/Victorian Square displays Southern Pacific Locomotive No. 8, a historic schoolhouse and markers. **SOURCED** — Michael Misanik, Truckee Meadows Parks Foundation blog, "Sparks Railroad History: Then and Now" (updated 2023-09-08), https://www.tmparksfoundation.org/single-post/sparks-railroad-history-then-and-now. ⚠ A snippet (kupi.com) dates the move to **July 4**, 1904, and says houses, trees, chickens and people were moved free 30 miles west. **Conflict with the July 1 date; snippet UNVERIFIED.**
- **Weekly motels, "housing of last resort."** About **2,550 people lived in 50+ Reno motels** (2021). Casino developer **Jeffrey Jacobs** (Jacobs Entertainment) cleared 14 motel properties over five years for a proposed **$1.8 B entertainment district**. These included the 46-room **Castaway Inn** and the **7/11 Motor Lodge**. The city had no policy requiring replacement of lost units or targeted relocation help, and approved incentives for the project. Residents named include a retired maintenance worker 20 years in motels, a woman who moved into her car, and a former maid planning to sleep by the river. Rents ran from under to "north of $1,000 a month." **SOURCED** — Anjeanette Damon, ProPublica with the *Reno Gazette Journal*, 2021-11-15, https://www.propublica.org/article/chaos-and-uncertainty-as-developer-plans-to-demolish-motel-that-serves-as-housing-lifeline.
