## 1 · S2-01 The Icehotel, Jukkasjärvi, and other snow and ice hotels (e.g., Kemi SnowCastle)

**Claim to test:** rooms of snow and ice where people sleep, kept below freezing on purpose and rebuilt each year; how heat, light, air and comfort are managed so the structure does not melt. **Care:** verify the rebuild cycle, room temperatures, and which parts are permanent and heated.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with corrections.**
- The rebuild cycle is real: the winter hotel is harvested, built and melted every year (SOURCED, icehotel.com/environmental-cycle; icehotel.com/making-icehotel; press.visitsweden.com 2025-12-15).
- Corrections: (a) not all of it is seasonal: since 2016 a permanent, mechanically cooled ice building, ICEHOTEL 365, stands beside it (SOURCED, icehotel.com/icehotel-365); (b) the cold rooms are not where people live: everything warm (toilets, showers, saunas, luggage, changing rooms) sits in a heated building next door, and most guests sleep only one night on ice (SOURCED, icehotel.com/about-icehotel/how-it-works); (c) the hotel is not kept cold by machines in winter: the outdoor cold does it; only the year-round building and the ice store need cooling (SOURCED, icehotel.com/icehotel-365, environmental-cycle).
- Room temperature: about -5 °C (official how-it-works page, SOURCED); -5 to -8 °C (Michelin Guide article 2024-12-20, SOURCED). Snow hotels elsewhere hold similar temperatures: Hôtel de Glace -4 to -5 °C (SOURCED, valcartier.com); Kemi's year-round indoor snow space -5 °C (SOURCED, Yle 2020-10-19).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** village of Jukkasjärvi on the Torne River, Kiruna Municipality, Norrbotten, Sweden; about 17 km from Kiruna (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Icehotel (Jukkasjärvi)", read 2026-11-21); about 200 km north of the Arctic Circle (SOURCED, Michelin Guide 2024-12-20).
- **Village population:** 548 in 2010 (Statistics Sweden via en.wikipedia.org "Jukkasjärvi", SOURCED); "about 800 inhabitants" (kirunalapland.se/en/jukkasjarvi, SOURCED, undated); "about 900 residents" (Michelin 2024, SOURCED). Figures disagree; no current official count read.
- **What it is:** a hotel built every winter of river ice and "snice"/"snis" (a snow-and-ice mix used for walls, floors and ceilings), plus a permanent cooled ice building and warm hotel rooms and cabins. Winter hotel 2025/26 (ICEHOTEL 36): 2,800 m², 550 tons of ice, roughly 30,000–32,700 m³ of snis (the press release gives both figures), 12 art suites, a Main Hall, a Ceremony Hall, about six weeks to build, 89 builders, artists, lighting designers and art support staff, 33 artists from 12 countries (SOURCED, press.visitsweden.com 2025-12-15). Older Wikipedia figure of about 6,000 m² (SOURCED as what Wikipedia says; not current).
- **Whole operation 2025:** 44 warm hotel rooms, 28 warm cabins, 18 year-round art and deluxe suites, 36 winter art suites and ice rooms, 3 restaurants, 5 wilderness camps, ICEBARs in Jukkasjärvi and Copenhagen (SOURCED, Visit Sweden press release 2025-12-15). More than one million visitors since it began (same, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** a building that is borrowed from the river each year and returned to it by melting (SOURCED, icehotel.com/environmental-cycle).
- **Founding:** 1989, Yngve Bergqvist built Arctic Hall, an ice-and-snow art gallery by the frozen river; when village cabins were full, visitors slept in it, and that night is counted as the hotel's start (SOURCED, icehotel.com founder page). Wikipedia gives a slightly different origin: Japanese ice artists' exhibition 1989, then a 1990 cylinder igloo exhibition by French artist Jannot Derid where the first guests slept (SOURCED as Wikipedia's account). Bergqvist had arrived in the mid-1970s and opened a canoe center (SOURCED, Michelin 2024).

### B. What else exists there; how it works [K5, K7, K1]
- **Ice tonnage is reported inconsistently** (flagged, not resolved): "around 5000 tons" harvested (icehotel.com/environmental-cycle, SOURCED); "around 2,000 tons" each spring, of which about 50 tons go to ICEBARs elsewhere (icehotel.com/harvest-time-swedens-30th-icehotel, SOURCED); 550 tons in the 2025/26 winter hotel itself (Visit Sweden 2025, SOURCED); production hall holds over 900 t of ice and 27,000 t of snow (Wikipedia, SOURCED as Wikipedia's claim); 4,000 t harvested March–April (search snippet only, UNVERIFIED). The likeliest reading is that harvest totals and the ice actually built into one winter hotel are different quantities, but no source says so outright.
- **Harvest and storage:** ice harvested from the Torne in spring "when the ice is at its thickest"; ICEHOTEL 36's ice was cut in March (SOURCED, Visit Sweden 2025); blocks weigh "up to two tons" and are stored cold through summer (SOURCED, icehotel.com/making-icehotel) in an "ice storage warehouse" by the river; 800 m² of solar cells were put on that warehouse roof in 2016 (SOURCED, environmental-cycle). Euronews (2025-12-17, SOURCED) says the ice was harvested "before ICEHOTEL 35 had melted" and stored in a "solar-powered hangar". [R]
- **Build:** about six weeks; molds shape arches, removed after a few days (SOURCED, making-icehotel); construction begins around mid-November when temperatures allow snow-gun operation, snow sprayed on steel forms, the hotel opens section by section weekly from early December to early January (SOURCED as Wikipedia's account). A warm November 2024 delayed the start of ICEHOTEL 35 (UNVERIFIED, search summary of an Icehotel press release; page not opened). [K9]
- **Selection of artists:** call for proposals opens at the start of each year; about 15 creators chosen from about 150 proposals; jury balances gender, origin, age and experience; selected creators gather at an art symposium in November (SOURCED, making-icehotel). 2025/26: 33 artists, 12 countries (SOURCED). [K13, K14]
- **Interior upkeep:** interior ice creations replaced on site about every six months for quality and safety (SOURCED as Wikipedia's claim); "Ice is a living material, so maintenance or repairs may happen while the hotel is open"; early guests may find parts unfinished (SOURCED, how-it-works). Rooms "can move or sink up to half a meter during the season due to heat from occupants" (UNVERIFIED: search snippet from Lighting Journal Mar 2023; page returned 404). [K14, R]
- **Lighting and power:** no electrical outlets inside ICEHOTEL (SOURCED, how-it-works). Early history of lighting: candles, then halogen and fluorescent, later LEDs and fiber optics fed through holes drilled in the ice so the light source sits away from the ice; cabling sunk in tubing in walls and floors (UNVERIFIED: search snippet of a Live Design article; page refused 403). Current designers find the ice so reflective that the problem is getting light levels low enough (UNVERIFIED, same Lighting Journal snippet).
- **ICEHOTEL 365 (permanent):** opened 2016; 2,100 m²; 18 art and deluxe suites; constant -5 °C; cooled by solar panels (875 m², designed with Hans Eek) using midnight-sun daylight May–July; houses the ICEBAR (drinks in glasses of Torne ice), and by day the suites work as an art exhibition (SOURCED, icehotel.com/icehotel-365). The company says it uses "100% renewable energy" and has installed energy-saving systems in all buildings since 2008 (SOURCED, environmental-cycle). [R]
- **Warm/cold split (the house logic):** warm check-in from 15:00; overnight guests get the ice rooms from 18:00 when the hotel closes to the public; by day all cold rooms and suites are open to day visitors; each guest gets a locker or changing booth in the warm Riverside building (toilets, showers, sauna, sleeping-bag pickup, outerwear lending, luggage store); ice rooms and art suites have no toilets or luggage storage; winter rooms have drapes, not doors; 365 suites have doors; recommended one night on ice and the rest warm; children recommended over seven (SOURCED, how-it-works). Sleeping bag rated to -25 °C; beds are ice frames or wooden frames with mattress and reindeer hides (SOURCED, how-it-works). Warm lingonberry juice is served in the morning (SOURCED, icehotel-365 page). Morning wake-up by staff with lingonberry juice then sauna (UNVERIFIED, travel-review snippets). [K4, K6, K12, R]
- **Workforce:** "over a hundred seasonal workers" come north each year as guides, waitstaff, cooks and hosts, recruited "from all over the world"; permanent staff could be hired for ICEHOTEL 365; seasonal staff work about five months; winter activity is four times the bare-ground season (SOURCED, FAR Online, *Resultat* no. 1 2017). ~110 seasonal and 50 year-round staff (UNVERIFIED, search summary of the same article; the opened text was truncated before those numbers). Job ads say staff housing "can be arranged" or is "a possibility" and several require EU citizenship or a Swedish work permit (UNVERIFIED: search snippets; the careers site karriar.icehotel.com refused connection with a certificate error and was not retried). A permanent "Maintenance Ice" job exists to build, maintain and care for ice and snow rooms and sculptures (UNVERIFIED, snippet). The company register lists Icehotel Aktiebolag at about SEK 188 million turnover and 100 employees for 2025 (UNVERIFIED, snippet from upplysningar.syna.se; not opened). [K7, K4]
- **Ownership/alliance:** in May 2020 Icehotel joined Petter Stordalen's Nordic Hotels & Resorts alliance, keeping its own brand (UNVERIFIED, search summary; page not opened).
- **Pandemic:** the year-round section was closed mid-April to 30 June 2020 (UNVERIFIED, snippet of an Icehotel press release 2020-05-20). [K9]
- **Food:** ICEHOTEL Restaurant uses reindeer, moose, Arctic char and bramble berries; The Veranda seats 16 and works with curing, smoking, drying, pickling; a restaurant is in the "Old Homestead", a timber building dating to 1768; breakfast includes reindeer-meat sandwiches (SOURCED, Michelin 2024-12-20). [K12]

### C. Cultural locations (Jukkasjärvi and surroundings)
- **Jukkasjärvi Church** (Jukkasjärvi kyrka, Church of Sweden): central section c. 1607–08, rebuilt 1726, bell tower 1740s, organ gallery 1785; red-painted log building in timber-crib construction, the only one preserved in Sweden; oldest church in Lapland county; 87 grave sites found under the floor in a 1907 repair; altarpiece by Bror Hjorth (teak triptych donated by mining company LKAB, 1958, for the 350th anniversary), side panels depicting the life of priest Lars Levi Laestadius; font by local craftsman Vilhelm Isaksson made with only an axe; organ (1997, Grönlunds orgelbyggeri, Gammelstad) of masur birch, reindeer horn and rye-tanned skin with Sámi mythological symbols on the stop panel; open daily 09:00–15:00 (SOURCED, svenskakyrkan.se/jukkasjarvi). Toilet closed "due to vandalism" except during services (SOURCED, same) [K6, K9].
- **Márkanbáiki Sámi open-air museum** (Marknadsvägen 2), linked to Nutti Sámi Siida: exhibits on Sámi culture, garments, tools, nomadic life and reindeer-herding history; restaurant Ovttas; Sámi handicraft shop; signs in Swedish and English; reindeer graze on the river shore in autumn. "Márkanbáiki" is North Sámi for assembly or marketplace, and the site is described as the origin of the village (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/?p=4574).
- **Hembygdsgården** (local heritage farm museum), Marknadsvägen 11, timber homestead buildings dating to 1736 (UNVERIFIED, Wikivoyage mirror snippet). Oldest building in village: timber cottage from 1736 (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/jukkasjarvi).
- **Ceremony Hall** (formerly the "Ice Church"): redesigned each winter, 35–40 guests, December–April, about 80 ceremonies a year, civil or religious; international couples need a certificate of no impediment 14 weeks ahead (SOURCED, icehotel.com/weddings). Historically run by the Jukkasjärvi parish and consecrated at Christmas Day service each year; its founding priest married about 1,500 couples over 17 years (SOURCED as Wikipedia's account; Wikipedia flags one count as uncited). 2025/26 hall named ECHO (SOURCED, Visit Sweden 2025). [K2]
- **ICEBAR** and the ice gallery in ICEHOTEL 365 (SOURCED). First Absolut Icebar opened in the hotel 1994 (SOURCED as Wikipedia's account).
- **Puimonen** hill viewpoint; illuminated ski trails 5 km and 10 km; dog-sled kennels (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/jukkasjarvi). The village has "about 800 inhabitants plus more than 1,000 dogs" (SOURCED, same page; tourism-board figure, not a census).
- **Kiruna Snow Festival** (Snöfestivalen), Kiruna town center: last weekend of January "since 1986" (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/snow-festival); LKAB's own news article says the festival has run "since 1999" (SOURCED, lkab.com 2026-01-26). The two disagree. Centerpiece: the International Snow Sculpture Competition on City Hall Square, sculptures left until they melt in spring; also street hockey, a snow playground, children's dog sledding, reindeer in the town center, Sámi food tastings; free; run by Kiruna Lapland (earlier by Tusen Toner) with Icehotel, associations, the culture school and high-school students; works with Nutti Sámi Siida; LKAB a sponsor (SOURCED, both pages). 2027 dates 27–30 January (SOURCED). [K2, K3, K8]
- **Kiruna European Capital of Culture 2029**, opening week 22–28 January 2029 (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se snow-festival page). [K3, K8]

### D. How the defining feature shaped what exists
- An ice gallery begun to fill the winter led to a business that brings over a hundred seasonal workers north each year and runs at four times its summer volume in winter (SOURCED, FAR Online 2017). [K7, K3]
- The river's seasonal cycle sets the year: harvest in March, store through summer, build in November, open December, melt April (SOURCED across making-icehotel, Visit Sweden 2025, Wikipedia). [K7, K2]
- A spin-off craft economy: an ice-sculpture workforce that also exports work (40 ice sculptors hired to build an iceberg for a 2010 Paris fashion show, pre-carved at -40 °C on a staff parking lot, shipped in 11 trailers; SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/borrowing-a-river). Sámi artists have made recent suites (e.g., "Áhku Fáhcat", ICEHOTEL 35, by a Norwegian/Sámi artist; SOURCED as search summary of icehotel.com pages, page not opened: UNVERIFIED). [K13, K14]

### E. Structural features
- **Sámi land and herding:** Kiruna Municipality has seven samebyar (Könkämä, Saarivuoma, Lainiovuoma, Vittangi, Laevas, Gabna, Talma); a sameby is both a grazing area and an economic and administrative unit; herding year from calving (April–May) to winter grazing (November–December) (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/reindeer-management). Talma's winter center is at Jukkasjärvi (UNVERIFIED, snippet). Gabna ended its cooperation agreement with LKAB in December 2025 over a planned mine (UNVERIFIED, Cultural Survival snippet). [K9]
- **Visitor behavior rules around reindeer** (published by the tourist board): stay on existing trails; stand still if in a herd; do not approach or photograph herds on slopes; adapt snowmobile speed; follow dog rules (SOURCED, kirunalapland.se/en/reindeer-management). [K6]
- **Kiruna town relocation:** the town center is being moved because of ground deformation from LKAB's iron-ore mine; about 10,000 people affected over the years, completion expected about 2035 (UNVERIFIED, search summaries; LKAB page redirected and was not re-fetched). [K9]
- **Languages [K11]:** Kiruna is an administrative municipality for three minority languages, Sámi, Finnish and Meänkieli, besides Swedish (UNVERIFIED, Sveriges Radio and Swedish law snippets). The village name comes from North Sámi; Finnish-speaking settlers arrived in the 17th century (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Jukkasjärvi").

### Other snow and ice hotels (two well-documented examples)
**Kemi SnowCastle (LumiLinna), Kemi, Finland**
- Kemi: 19,339 people (31 Dec 2025); port town on the Bothnian Bay at the Kemijoki mouth; two big pulp and paper mills and Europe's only chromium mine (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Kemi"). Metsä Fiber's bioproduct mill (started 2023, EUR 2.02 billion, 1.5 million t pulp/yr) also supplies district heat to the city (UNVERIFIED, Metsä search summaries). [K8]
- First castle 1996, about 300,000 visitors; was in the harbor, moved to a park in 2017; areas of 13,000–20,000 m², towers over 20 m (SOURCED as Wikipedia's claims, page flags citations needed). Contains in various years a SnowChapel (50–100 seats), SnowRestaurant with ice tables and reindeer-fur seats, SnowHotel (SOURCED, Wikipedia).
- **Who builds and pays [K8, K1]:** first built by the city itself; one family firm (IP Heikkilä) built 13 castles; Frozen Innovation Oy built 2018–2019; from 2019 the city-owned tourism company Kemin Matkailu Oy hires contractors itself, renting molds and expertise from Lainio Snow Village; cost once described as "about that of building a large detached house" (SOURCED, Yle 2019-11-14). An earlier operating company, Lumilinna Oy, went into liquidation with about EUR 250,000 of debt and lawsuits among shareholders (SOURCED, Kaleva 2002-01-10). [K9]
- **Snow:** made from seawater; needs seven degrees of frost to be usable (SOURCED, MTV Uutiset 2020-02-08). In 2020 a mild early winter meant too little snow: the snow hotel was smaller and took no overnight guests (SOURCED, same). Outdoor castle not built in 2020/21 for the first time in 25 years, because of the pandemic (UNVERIFIED, Yle headline snippet; Yle 2020-10-19 SOURCED says it "may not be built"). A year-round indoor snow space opened 2019 at Mansikkanokka: 400 m², -5 °C, restaurant, slide, sculptures (SOURCED, Yle 2019-11-14 and 2020-10-19). [K9, R]
- **Rules [K6]:** the snow hotel must follow the same fire rules as an ordinary hotel, with inspectors checking; extinguishers are kept in the snow castle because beds, blankets and smoke are the hazard (SOURCED, MTV Uutiset 2020-02-08).
- 2025: Kemin Matkailu runs the year-round LumiLinna and the icebreaker *Sampo* trips; record visitor numbers but few stay overnight; city plans a hotel by the castle; worker apartments in Kemi mainly serve Metsä Fiber staff (SOURCED, Yle 2025-08-01). [K4, K5]
- Kemi's snow castle in 1997 hosted the City Theater's *Jääsatu* inside a snow structure built around the Meripuisto song stage, heated with infrared heaters (SOURCED, Yle article on the song stage, 3-12270428). [K13]

**Hôtel de Glace, Saint-Gabriel-de-Valcartier (near Quebec City), Canada**
- Created 2001 after its founder visited the Swedish Icehotel in 1996; four years of development; moved sites before settling at Village Vacances Valcartier in 2016; 2025/26 was its 26th winter; about 50 people build it each year (about 30 production, 15 sculptors), 3,000 ice blocks, 35,000 or 45,000 t of snow (page gives both), six weeks of day-and-night work, interior -4 to -5 °C, construction starts once temperatures stay below zero for a week (SOURCED, valcartier.com). Partnership with Tourisme Wendake with three cultural days and a Shaputuan tent (SOURCED, same). [K7, K14]
- **Fire, January 2018:** fire in a guest room in the early hours; 13 of 14 rooms occupied; minor smoke inhalation, no structural damage; cause not officially determined, one hypothesis a lit candle falling on bedding; closed some days to clear smoke (SOURCED, globalnews.ca 2018-01-09). [K9, K6]
- **Lapland Hotels SnowVillage, Lainio, Kittilä (Finland):** rebuilt yearly; artificial snow from Lainio River water; construction starts when it reaches about -10 °C; team of about 50; interior -2 to -5 °C (UNVERIFIED, search snippets; fact sheet refused with HTTP 429 and not retried).

## 2 · S2-15 Snow-sheltered and earth-sheltered winter dwellings (sod houses; Inuit winter houses (qarmaq) and snow houses; Norse longhouses)

**Claim to test:** traditional ways of keeping people warm in snow and ground with small, spread heat sources. **Care:** a tradition pick; research the techniques and two or three well-documented sites or reconstructions; use the communities' own names; describe Indigenous heritage respectfully.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with a correction about "spread".** In all three traditions the heat sources were small (a seal-oil lamp, a hearth, human and animal bodies, ground heat), but the heat was *concentrated*, not spread: people gathered in one warm room or on one raised platform and let the envelope (snow, turf, earth) do the work. The Icelandic household lived in one room, the baðstofa; the Inuit household on one sleeping platform at the warmest level of the snow house; the Norse household around one long hearth.

### Part A · Inuit snow houses (iglu / igluvigaq, plural igluvigait) and winter houses (qarmaq, plural qarmait)

**A. Snapshot / technique**
- **Forms:** permanent all-season qarmait were often semi-subterranean, of stone, whale bone and sod, sometimes insulated outside with snow; summer shelters (tupiit) of pelts or later canvas; snow houses (igluvigait) used in winter on hunting trips because quick to build; both qarmait and igluvigait could be lined with a tupiq inside, "which kept the insulating snow cold and the inside of the house dry"; a porch for food storage or for the sled dogs (qimmiit); a communal sleeping platform at the back; furnishings limited to a few benches; snow houses could join a large main room to smaller rooms for individual families; when walls blackened with soot from the qulliq they could be scraped clean; an unsound house was simply rebuilt (SOURCED, Qikiqtani Truth Commission, *Igluliriniq: Housing in the Qikiqtani Region, 1950–1975*, revised report 2024, pp. 3–4). [K14, R]
- **Thule (early Inuit) winter houses, from c. 1000 CE:** oval, dug up to 1 m into the ground, 3–9 m external diameter; stone, earth, moss and whalebone, sometimes driftwood and sod; whale-bone roofs in the central and eastern Arctic; a narrow underground entrance passage angled up into the floor works as a **cold trap**; at Brooman Point (Nunavut), each house was used a few years at most, three to five at once, likely autumn to spring, early 12th century; the snow house later became the main winter dwelling, possibly linked to Little Ice Age cooling (stated as a possibility, not a finding) (SOURCED, The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Thule Winter House").
- **Spring transition:** the qarmaq was traditionally built in spring after rising temperatures and the returning sun made snow-house roofs melt and drip (UNVERIFIED, search snippet of a conference paper). [K2]
- **Measured performance (the strongest source found):** Kershaw, Scott and Welch, "The Shelter Characteristics of Traditional-Styled Inuit Snow Houses," *Arctic* 49(4), Dec. 1996, pp. 328–338 (SOURCED, PDF read in full via the saved file). Two iglus built at Resolute (Qausuittuq), Feb. 1993, furnished on the advice of Resolute residents who had lived in iglus:
  - Large iglu 4.1 m diameter, 72 blocks averaging 23.6 kg, 9 person-hours; small iglu 3.05 m, 46 blocks averaging 28.2 kg, 7 person-hours. Blocks about 15 cm thick, 50 × 75 cm. Lifting the blocks alone roughly doubles a person's daily energy use. [K14]
  - Heat came from kudliks (seal-oil lamps; wicks 35 and 58 cm); fuel was frozen seal fat pounded and put behind the wick; lamps sat on three wooden pegs in the platform. People came in about 22:00 and used the lamps until about 24:00. [K7, R]
  - With heat, the chamber reached up to 45 °C above outside air (outside -28 to -41 °C). Even unheated and empty, the large iglu was 5 °C above ambient from ground heat. Warmest air at the ceiling; the coldest at the door well. Hanging caribou skins inside raised temperatures (gradient across the lining up to 4.9 °C) and reduced heat loss by at least 50 W/m². Moisture from breath, cooking and combustion froze onto the inner wall as a thin icy seal.
  - Fuel: small iglu needed the fat of one ringed seal every 6.3 days, large one every 3.7 days; body heat supplied 8–14% of the needed energy. The snow wall's insulation equaled roughly a 10 cm insulated (R12) wall. Snow good enough to build with covered only 3.6% of the surveyed 4 km² area. [K5]
  - Historical temperature records collected in the paper (Table 1): skin lining, practiced on Baffin Island and in the Iglulik area, raised chamber temperature from 1–3 °C to 10–20 °C (Boas 1888; Mathiassen 1928); lamps varied from 12 to 136 cm long, small ones for travel, large (≥60 cm) for semi-permanent winter houses (Jenness 1946); Boas reported a 25-block traveling iglu built by two people in two hours.
- **The qulliq (seal-oil lamp):** wick of Arctic cotton and moss laid along the lamp's groove; tended with a hook-shaped tool (taqquti) that a husband made for his wife, marking her as the keeper of the home's flame; used for cooking, drying clothes, melting ice, boiling water, light for sewing and warmth (SOURCED, "Lighting the Qulliq," Ilinniapaa Skills Development Center page on arcticdata.io, undated). Many events in the North open with a qulliq lighting today; missionaries had tried to ban the practice (UNVERIFIED, search snippets). [K2, K14, R]

**B. Who lived in them and how they got there / institutions**
- **Settlement period, 1950–1975 (sensitive history, reported neutrally):** the Qikiqtani Truth Commission (established 2007) found that Inuit were "enticed, and often coerced" into government-supported settlements, the 13 communities of the region today, for jobs, schooling and health care; the federal Eskimo Housing Loan Program (1959) and the Eskimo Rental Housing Program (1965) followed (SOURCED, QTC *Igluliriniq* 2024). The Department bought and built 1,200 one-room "matchbox" houses (Style 370, 370 sq ft) and resold them to Inuit 1959–1965, sized in multiples of 4×8 ft plywood sheets and kept small so "less fuel would be required to heat the houses"; prices rose from $400–500 to $1,500–1,800; a 1960 estimate found only 6% of Inuit had ever had steady wage work; fuel costs were higher than many could pay; initial rent about $25 a month plus $5 furniture and $10 services (SOURCED, same). In the early 1950s kerosene heating left soot on walls, clothes and bedding (SOURCED, same). Some camps were bulldozed or burned after families moved (SOURCED as search summary of QTC findings; the housing report mentions the bulldozing of a family's qarmaq, SOURCED). The Government of Canada formally apologized on 14 August 2019 (UNVERIFIED, search summary of canada.ca). [K9, K5, K8]
- The same report notes Inuit "continued to live in multigenerational families, and to share food, chores, stories, and laughter together in a single room" after moving, and many still live on the land part of the year (SOURCED). [K4, K10]

**C. Cultural locations and practices today**
- **Qaggiq** (large communal snow house / gathering institution): historically used to perform, celebrate, compete, share knowledge and resolve disputes (UNVERIFIED, search summary of Nunatsiaq/CBC). Qaggiq 2026, Iqaluit, 27–29 March: first since 2021; free; foreman plus 15 apprentices built the main qaggiq and two small iglus in about two days; designed without a roof this time because the 2021 structure was "caving in very early"; drum dancers from the Inuvialuit Region and Cambridge Bay, throat singers from Arviat, theater from Pond Inlet; organized by Qaggiavuut (Nunavut Performing Arts Center) (SOURCED, Nunatsiaq News, 25 March 2026). A 2025 scholarly essay describes the snow qaggiq as a dome with interior diameters upward of twenty feet, where people gathered for storytelling, singing, drumming and dancing, and stresses that snow settlements were "often rebuilt at the same sites year after year" (SOURCED, Phoebe Springstubb, NiCHE, 17 Nov. 2025). [K2, K3, K10, K13]
- **Teaching iglu building:** Kugluktuk High School with Nunavut's Department of Culture and Heritage, Jan.–Apr. 2015: over 30 youth built an iglu colony 10 km out of town; the land expert taught probing snow for hidden layers and finding consistent snow about 18 inches deep; he said few hunters still use iglus as regular shelter and now often pitch a tent and wall it with snow blocks (SOURCED, Nunatsiaq News, 4 May 2015). Nunavut's 2008 Education Act lets qualified elders teach such skills in schools (UNVERIFIED, snippet). Parks Canada at Sirmilik ran an elder-led workshop in 2018 (UNVERIFIED, snippet). [K14, SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Qaummaarviit Territorial Park,** 12 km west of Iqaluit: 11 Thule sod-house remains, occupied from as early as 1200 CE into the 1800s (dates vary by source), declared a historic park 1985; boardwalk; descendants took part in a recent excavation aiming at a full reconstruction (UNVERIFIED, search summaries; pages not opened).

**K6 (rules of everyday behavior):** a 1970s ethnography of Qipisa describes visitors waiting at the front door to be greeted unless close relatives (UNVERIFIED, snippet). Inuit customary law (maligait) and the concept of piqujaq, a "thing that had to be done," exist (SOURCED as terms explained in the NiCHE essay; Government of Nunavut's Inuit Maligait publication seen only as snippet, UNVERIFIED). No general rule set for snow-house household behavior was read. In 19th-century Greenlandic communal houses (below) privacy was a negotiated norm.

**Greenland (Kalaallit Nunaat) winter houses**
- Winter houses mainly of rock and turf, often against a hill; driftwood and whale ribs and jaws for the roof; windows of stretched seal or animal gut; some for one family, larger ones for several; igloos used mainly in the far north (Qaanaaq area); peat houses still lived in "as late as the 1940s" in many places including Old Narsaq; a 1948 commission worried about tuberculosis in damp housing; Greenland became a Danish county in 1953 with a housing-benefit scheme; Block P in Nuuk (1965–66) was reportedly Northern Europe's largest apartment block and was demolished in 2012 (SOURCED, visitgreenland.com/activities/architecture). [K9]
- 19th-century communal winter houses around Disko Bay and West Greenland: rectangular, stone and turf, low entrance tunnel, up to sixty people; each nuclear family a compartment on the raised sleeping platform separated by skin or fur hangings, put up only for sleeping or privacy; side platforms for unmarried men, widowers, visitors; soapstone lamp for heat and cooking; families who hunted together wintered together; summer in tents; Danish administration later split households and pushed "westernized" homes, and iron stoves "did not support gathering around the lamp"; houses partly dismantled and aired after winter (SOURCED, University of Copenhagen, Center for Privacy Studies, 24 Sept. 2025). [K4, K6, R]

### Part B · Icelandic turf houses (torfbæir)

**A. Snapshot / technique**
- Built since settlement in the 9th century; large flat-stone foundation, wooden frame (birch, driftwood, imported timber), turf blocks laid around it, often herringbone; longhouses replaced from the 14th century by many small interconnected rooms; the gabled *burstabær* style from the late 18th century (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Icelandic turf house"). Before 1910 most Icelanders lived in turf houses; by the 1930s concrete and stone had replaced them (SOURCED, JSTOR Daily, 15 July 2026, citing Gavin Lucas 2012). Between 1910 and 1940 turf dwellings fell from 52.4% to 18.6% of housing stock (SOURCED, van Dijken et al., *Gerontechnology* 3(3), 2005, Table 1, from *Hagskinna*).
- **Heat:** the baðstofa (communal room) was where everyone except master and mistress slept, two to a narrow bed, including children, grandparents, servants and paupers placed with the household; sheep were sometimes moved into the lower level in the coldest weather (SOURCED, JSTOR Daily 2026). At Glaumbær in 1931, 11 occupants spent most of their time in one 52 m² baðstofa, timbered with a raised wooden floor while kitchen, stores and corridor had bare turf; walls up to 3.5 m thick; simulated indoor climate "cool in winter"; air change only about 0.4 per hour, so smoke, kitchen gases, drying fish, whey-preserved food and ammonia (urine used for cleaning) were not cleared (SOURCED, van Dijken et al. 2005). The baðstofa was often built over the cattle shed for the animals' warmth (UNVERIFIED, snippets). [R, K4]
- **Fuel:** settlement-era fuels included birch and willow, peat, mineral turf and cow dung; a 10th–12th-century hearth at Reykholt burned peat and dung only (UNVERIFIED, search summaries of Simpson et al., *J. Archaeological Science* 30(11), 2003; PDF not opened). Meat smoked over sheep dung (hangikjöt) (UNVERIFIED). [K12, K5]
- **Health (friction):** the 2005 study found occupant health "was not negatively influenced by building properties that are characteristic to turf structures"; disease was linked to poor living conditions and crowding (SOURCED). Moisture from turf and ground means turf houses need constant renewal (UNVERIFIED, snippet). Danish-era nationalists from the 1700s on saw turf houses as backwardness (SOURCED, JSTOR Daily, citing Sigurjón Baldur Hafsteinsson 2019). [K9]

**B/C. Sites**
- **Glaumbær, Skagafjörður:** 13 interconnected buildings, about 730 m²; a passage runs from the front door through to the baðstofa at the far end; kitchen and pantry mid-18th century, front gables 1879; turf main material because stone is scarce; last residents left 1947, protected the same year; £200 donated for conservation by Mark Watson in 1938; Skagafjörður Heritage Museum (Byggðasafn Skagfirðinga) founded 1948, exhibition "Life in Turf Houses in the 19th Century" opened 15 June 1952; part of the National Museum's Historic Building Collection; Áshús Café (SOURCED, glaumbaer.is). Exhibition: open hearth kitchen, food in barrels and tubs, people ate sitting on their beds in the baðstofa (SOURCED).
- **Keldur, Rangárvellir:** described by the National Museum as Iceland's oldest existing farmhouse, late 12th century, the only remaining farmhouse of the "old passageway type", stone, timber and turf; underground tunnel believed 12th–13th century, possibly from a time of conflict; open 1 June–31 Aug. (SOURCED, thjodminjasafn.is/en/soguleg-husasofn/keldur). [K9]
- **Laufás, Eyjafjörður:** church recorded from 1047; most of farmstead 1840–1877; last inhabited 1936; National Museum since 1948; run by the Akureyri Museum (UNVERIFIED, snippets; the museum page failed with an SSL error).
- **UNESCO tentative list:** "The Turf House Tradition", with Glaumbær, Laufás, Keldur, Árbær and others (UNVERIFIED: snippets; the UNESCO page refused 403).
- **Kvöldvaka** ("evening wake"): for centuries household members worked at crafts in the baðstofa while one recited poetry, told a story or read aloud (SOURCED, National Museum of Iceland, Museum Night event page, 6 Feb. 2026, a recreated evening with the Iðunn folk-poetry society, visitors seated with carders and spindles). Its length was set by how long outdoor work was possible (UNVERIFIED, snippet). [K2, K10, K13]
- **Turf craft today [K14]:** the Heritage Craft School (Fornverkaskólinn) at Glaumbær/Tyrfingsstaðir has run courses "over the past two decades"; 2026 turf course 3–5 June, 6–12 participants, ISK 70,000; taught by a turf craftsman's firm (Fornverk ehf.); over 500 participants in all; union education funds may pay fees (SOURCED, glaumbaer.is courses page). Only a few people in Iceland can do turf-heritage work, skills passed on through repair projects (UNVERIFIED, snippet of an Althingi answer; page refused 403). [R]

**K6 (rules of everyday behavior): FOUND.** *Vistarband*: from the Píningsdómur of 1490, anyone without property worth at least three cows had to be a farm laborer (vinnuhjú), contracted usually for a year, unable to leave the farm without the farmer's permission; disputes went to the district head (hreppstjóri); being a free worker (lausamaður) was banned 1783–1863; about 25% of the population were bound this way in the 19th century; in 1894 free-worker status became open to most landless people after small-scale fishing grew (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Vistarband"; abolition-year details vary). This is what filled the turf houses' baðstofa with servants. [K6, K7, K9]

### Part C · Norse longhouses

### D. How the defining feature shaped things
- Small heat sources forced **one warm room** for everyone: one platform (Inuit), one baðstofa (Iceland), one hearth hall (Norse), and with it shared evening work and storytelling (kvöldvaka; qaggiq gatherings) (SOURCED as above). [K4, K10, R]
- Cheap, local, renewable envelopes meant **rebuilding as routine**: snow houses rebuilt each winter on the same sites; turf houses constantly renewed; Glaumbær's buildings span 1750–1879 (SOURCED). [K14, R]
- Fuel equals food in the Inuit case: one seal's fat heated a small iglu for about six days and its meat fed two people four to five days (SOURCED, Kershaw et al. 1996). [K5, K12]

- State housing programs replaced these dwellings within a generation in both Iceland (1910–1940, by choice and modernization) and the Qikiqtani Region (1950s–1970s, under federal programs the QTC found coercive) (SOURCED). [K9]

## 3 · S2-16 Distributed underfloor heating (Roman hypocausts; Korean ondol)

**Claim to test:** heat spread under floors through many small channels rather than one hot room; how much heat, where it goes, how it is fed and maintained, what daily life it shaped. **Care:** a technique pick; research the technique and two or three well-documented places.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED with two corrections.** (1) Neither system spreads heat evenly: both have a hot end and a cool end, and both societies turned that gradient into social order (Korean seating by rank from the warm end; Roman bathers moving room by room from cool to hot). (2) The Roman hypocaust was above all a bath and reception-room technology for the wealthy, the army and the public baths, not general housing; Korean ondol, by contrast, became universal housing from the 17th century. A third tradition, the medieval Baltic heat-storage hypocaust, stored heat in stones and released it later, which is a different principle (store then release) from both.

### Part A · Korean ondol (온돌; native word gudeul, 구들)

**A. Snapshot / technique**
- **Status:** "Ondol culture" (온돌문화) was designated a National Intangible Heritage of Korea on 30 April 2018, classed as traditional living custom / residential life; defined as "Korea's comprehensive residential culture centered on floor heating" (SOURCED, encykorea.aks.ac.kr E0077489). Designated as a custom shared across the peninsula, so no individual holder or group was recognized (UNVERIFIED: search summary of the designation notice). The Cultural Heritage Administration announced the plan on 16 March 2018 (SOURCED, korea.net 20 March 2018). [K1, K2]
- **Parts:** firebox (agungi, 아궁이) → flues (gorae, 고래) under thin flat floor stones (gudeuljang, 구들장) → chimney (gulttuk, 굴뚝); fuel traditionally firewood or charcoal, later coal briquettes (yeontan), then gas, oil and electricity with hot-water pipes (SOURCED, encykorea E0077489). Two furnace types in heritage repair practice: the stove-furnace that also cooks (부뚜막아궁이) and the furnace directly below a room that does not cook (함실아궁이); fan-shaped flue layouts (부채고래) and others; a sump (개자리) before the chimney (SOURCED, Lim Jun-Gu and Kim Young-Jae, "Proposing the Revision of Ondol Construction in the Standard Specification for Heritage Repair," *Journal of Architectural History* 32(5), Oct. 2023, PDF read locally). The Cultural Heritage Administration publishes a standard repair specification for ondol work, one of 20 trade sections (SOURCED, same). [K14, R]
- **Deep history:** Neolithic pit-house hearths; Bronze Age separation of heating hearth and cooking stove; early Iron Age flues and chimneys; full-floor ondol completed in late Goryeo; most active spread in the 17th century, driven by war damage and drought and cold; 18th-century rooms made smaller with buffer spaces and layered windows (SOURCED, encykorea). Early L- and U-shaped stone-tile ondols at Neukdo and Bangiri (49 dwellings analyzed) date to about 200 BCE–100 CE, linked to the Krounovka culture of Primorye, where similar ondols appear by the 3rd century BCE (SOURCED, abstract: Kim Jae Youn, *Archaeology, Ethnology & Anthropology of Eurasia* 54(1), 2026).
- **Measured performance:** a traditional sensible-heat ondol held a comfortable floor surface (23–29 °C) for only about 1 hour at 16–18 °C room temperature, versus 5 hours for a latent-heat test unit; a conventional hot-water ondol needed 12 hours to reach steady state (UNVERIFIED: search summaries of KoreaScience abstracts, 1995). [R]

**B. Who uses it, how it spread, institutions**
- **Spread across classes:** in early Joseon heated sitting floors were a commoner's way of life and the upper classes used braziers; from the late 16th century, with the Little Ice Age, ondol spread to the yangban and the royal family, reaching the king's bedchamber by the 17th century; firewood use rose sharply, Hanseong (Seoul) had a serious firewood problem, the hills around the capital were stripped, and heavy rains flooded the Cheonggyecheon; colonial-era Japanese authorities blamed ondol and demanded its removal (SOURCED, Seoul Shinmun, 10 Jan. 2024, reporting an essay by Prof. Kim So-ra in the Korean Studies Promotion Service webzine *Dam*). Firewood came to Seoul by river and was sold by riverside merchants and firewood shops (UNVERIFIED, snippet). [K5, K9, K8]
- **Coal briquettes (yeontan) and carbon monoxide (friction; reported neutrally):** briquettes became the leading fuel in 1965 (1961: firewood 57.8%, briquettes 31.9%) (SOURCED, Seoul Shinmun column, 1 May 2017). Government policy from 1953 promoted anthracite as household fuel; mid-1950s 19-hole briquettes; recurring winter shortages (1966; 1974 oil-shock hoarding); from the 1960s, families died each winter from briquette gas; the state trained and certified plasterers, required inspections of multi-family housing and penalized owners and builders after accidents; poisoning disappeared in the 1990s with gas and oil boilers (SOURCED, National Archives of Korea theme page "briquette"). Deaths: about 350 (1968), 580 (1973), 1,013 (1976 peak); gas seeped from the ondol through floor-paper gaps, wall cracks and door gaps; from 1980 a nightly briquette-gas warning was broadcast with the TV news; stove builders and plasterers were sometimes arrested and landlords punished; only six hyperbaric chambers in the country in 1973 (SOURCED, Seoul Shinmun 2017). [K9, K6, K1]
- **Modern housing:** early apartments (Mapo complex, 1961–64) used radiators; hot-water ondol became standard in apartments from the 1970s on (UNVERIFIED, search summaries of Korea Times and Wikipedia). [K+ housing]

**C. Cultural locations**

### Part B · Roman hypocausts

**A. Snapshot / technique**
- **Vitruvius, *De architectura* 5.10 (written about 15 BCE):** baths placed as warm as possible, sheltered from north and north-east; men's and women's hot baths adjoining so one furnace serves both; three caldrons over the furnace (hot, tepid, cold) refilled in sequence; floor base tiled "inclining towards the furnace" so a dropped ball rolls back, letting flame spread under the floor; piers of eight-inch bricks two feet high laid in clay mixed with hair; two-foot tiles on top carry the pavement; double vaults keep steam off timber; laconicum with a bronze shield on chains in the dome opening to regulate heat (SOURCED, Morgan 1914 translation on penelope.uchicago.edu). [K14, R]
- **Routine and temperatures:** unheated room → warm (tepidarium, about 30 °C) → hot (caldarium, about 40 °C, nearest the furnace) → cold plunge; floors too hot to touch, so wooden sandals; wall flues of box tiles; invented probably in the 1st century BCE; used in baths and some reception rooms (SOURCED, English Heritage, "Baths and Bathing in Roman Britain"). Carnuntum reconstructions measured floors at 20–50 °C, walls up to 30 °C, caldarium air about 32 °C with very high humidity, bath water up to 48 °C; long heat-up times, little control, high fuel use; large baths often fired continuously; the cheaper channel heating (Kanalheizung) was common in the northern provinces (SOURCED, Carnuntum magazine, 13 Jan. 2026). The NOVA replica bath at Sart, Turkey (1998, with bath historian Fikret Yegül): water heated to about 100 °F and the floor so hot the men wore sandals (SOURCED, pbs.org NOVA slide 13).
- **Efficiency and fuel:** a recalculation of the Saalburg test-room firing of 29–30 Dec. 1951 gives 33% system efficiency (UNVERIFIED, snippet of a Propylaeum volume); a modeling study estimates 4.6 t of wood per day for the baths of Sagalassos; a study of Ostia's Terme del Foro estimates fewer than 150 trees a year; a villa furnace at Groundwell Ridge burned mostly oak at 330–410 °C (all UNVERIFIED, snippets; the Virginia dissertation page refused 403). [K5, K8]
- **Staff:** the balneator collected the fee (a quadrans, the smallest coin; children free); fornacatores tended the fires; capsarii guarded clothes (SOURCED, Smith's *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, 1875, "Balneae"). Children may have cleaned the narrow heating spaces at Wroxeter (UNVERIFIED, English Heritage display caption via Google Arts & Culture snippet; an interpretation). [K7, K14]

**B/C. Places**
- **Chesters (Cilurnum), Hadrian's Wall:** military bath-house outside the fort by the North Tyne, changing hall with niches for gods' statues, warm, hot and cold rooms and a sweat room; served about 500 soldiers (from the late 2nd century the second Asturian cavalry); the changing hall was used for gambling, socializing and exercise; may have been open to locals (SOURCED, English Heritage). [K10, K4]

[K4]

**K6 (rules of everyday behavior): FOUND.** Baths opened at sunrise and closed at sunset; readiness was signaled by a bell; Hadrian and Marcus Aurelius forbade mixed bathing and Alexander Severus banned it in Rome; theft in the baths was made a capital crime in the capital; baths closed in public misfortunes (SOURCED, Smith's *Dictionary*, 1875, "Balneae"; a 19th-century reference, treat as secondary). ### Part C · Medieval heat-storage hypocausts (a related third form)
- An underground vaulted furnace piled with granite stones 40–50 cm across; perforations in the room floor closed while the fire burned and smoke left by a flue; after the fire and cleaning, the flue was dampered and the vents (10–12 cm) opened, releasing stored hot air; Malbork Castle (Marienburg) test, 1822: 3.5 hours burning 0.7 m³ of spruce raised the 850 m² hall from 6 °C to 22.5 °C in 20 minutes; next morning opening the vents gave 14 → 19 °C without a new fire; six days later still 8 → 10 °C; about 500 documented in the Baltic region, perhaps 800–1,000 by 1500; 95 listed in Estonia (2009), at least 54 in Tallinn; replaced by tile stoves in the 16th century in Livonia (SOURCED, Kris De Decker, Low-Tech Magazine, republished Resilience.org 22 Mar. 2017, citing Bingenheimer 1998, Tvauri 2009, Spiegel 2016, Truu 2016). [R, K14]

- **Rome:** the gradient was spatial (a sequence of rooms), making the bath a timed social circuit with staff, fees and hours (SOURCED). [K7, K10]

## 4 · S2-02 Ghadames old town, Libya

**Claim to test:** a desert town of covered, shaded passages with rooftop routes: a labyrinth designed for climate control, later largely left for a new town. **Care:** recent conflict history, report neutrally; verify the abandonment and any return or conservation.

**Verdict on the claim: CONFIRMED, with three refinements.** (1) The covered maze served defense and privacy as well as climate: the plan is described as made "to confuse and slow down potential invaders" (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Old town of Ghadames"). (2) The rooftop routes were not general circulation but a **women's** network; men used the covered alleys below (SOURCED, same; en.wikipedia.org "Ghadames"). (3) The abandonment was gradual and is not total in use: the state built a new town from the 1970s (families moving from 1981 per one source), the last family left in the 1990s, but owners keep returning in summer and for the October festival (sources below). Conservation: UNESCO World Heritage 1986; List in Danger 2016; **removed from the Danger List in July 2025** (SOURCED, KCL news 31 July 2025; decision ID 8710 seen only as search result).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** oasis in Nalut District, Tripolitania, northwestern Libya, about 462 km southwest of Tripoli in a straight line (about 600 km by road), near the Algerian and Tunisian borders; elevation about 589 m; hot desert climate, July highs about 41 °C, about 73 mm rain a year (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Ghadames"; road distance SOURCED, Al Ain 2 Nov. 2020).
- **Population:** about 10,000 (2011 estimate, Wikipedia, SOURCED); about 11,000 (Reuters via Arab News, 24 Apr. 2013, SOURCED); about 15,000 with Tuareg an estimated 30% (Al Jazeera, 10 Aug. 2012, SOURCED); "up to 25,000" (Al Ain, 2020, SOURCED). Figures disagree; no census read.
- **Old town:** 38.4 ha; fortified wall with gates (Bab al-Burr, Bab al-Fars, Bab al-Atshan); seven tribal neighborhoods (mahallas): Tesku, Ait Welid, Ait Waziten, Ait Mazigh, Ait Ouligh, Ait Fersan, Ait Darar; houses typically four stories of mud brick, limestone and palm wood; ground floor storage, first floor the family's main room (wast ad-dar), upper levels and roof for daily life, especially women's; covered semi-dark alleys (sabat) for men at ground level, open rooftop terraces as women's exclusive domain; thick walls, openings and light wells for ventilation and light (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Old town of Ghadames", citing UNESCO and a 2023 HBKU thesis). Up to 30 of the old town's 1,250 houses are used during the festival (SOURCED, World Nomads/Lonely Planet, 16 Sept. 2009). [R]
- **Defining feature:** a covered, interconnected town around the Ain al-Faras (Ghasouf) spring.

### B. What else exists there
- **Water (the shared problem) [K1]:** the spring feeds five water tunnels running through the old city to homes, mosques and farms (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 27 May 2022, citing a local guide). Ghadamsis divided water in shares based on farmland area, using a time-measuring device called the **qadus** (القادوس); to prevent waste, water carried a price except the water set aside for mosques, drinking by all living things, and washing; the channel layout shaped where mosques sit, with ablution places at the front (SOURCED, al-Darawi, Aqil, al-Aswad and Zrayqan, "The impact of Ghasouf on the urban environment of old Ghadames," *International Science and Technology Journal* 33(2), Univ. of Elmergib, PDF read locally). A French study notes irrigation measured by fractions of the day or by a water clock called gadûs from Ghadames to the mountain oases, possibly from Latin *cadus* (UNVERIFIED, snippet of a Trousset article). The spring began drying up in the 1980s and an artesian well was dug for farmers; a UNDP–Libyan tourism and antiquities authority project (c. 2003) studied reviving it and employed over 60 workers repairing adobe buildings with traditional materials (SOURCED, Al Bawaba/Mena Report, 16 Jan. 2003). [K1, K5, K9, R]
- **Economy:** historically a trans-Saharan trade base until the 19th century and "an important site in the trans-Saharan slave trade" (SOURCED, Wikipedia "Ghadames"); routes Tripoli–Ghadames–Ghat–Aïr to Kano and Katsina, and to Timbuktu (UNVERIFIED, scholarly snippets); a British consular post opened at Ghadames in 1850 and was closed with Murzuk's in 1860–61 after the trade proved hard to stop; Ghadames slave statistics survive only as reconstructions from consular despatches (UNVERIFIED, snippet of a *Libyan Studies* article). Today: many residents on the state payroll or running their own businesses; tourism before 2011 about 30,000–35,000 visitors per season (Oct.–May), seven hotels, two open full-time in 2013; three flights a week to Tripoli (SOURCED, Reuters/Arab News 2013). Airport flights to Tripoli and Sabha; an older French-era airport and a newer one (SOURCED, Al Ain 2020). Dates, olives and vegetables grown in the oasis (UNVERIFIED, travel-guide snippet). [K5, K8, K9]
- **Institutions:** Ghadames City Promotion and Development Authority (also rendered Urban Development Authority) maintains the old city; it says it receives about 1% of its requested budget and recently restored one of the oldest girls' schools in North Africa (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2022). Municipal council and mayor (SOURCED, AFP via Al-Monitor 4 Feb. 2023). [K1, K8]
- **Languages [K11]:** Ghadamès (an Eastern Berber language, Ethnologue status "threatened"; speaker numbers uncertain, estimates from about 4,000 to 17,000; dialects of Ayt Waziten and Ayt Ulid) alongside Arabic; Tuareg also live in and around the town (SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org "Ghadamès language"; Al Jazeera 2012).

### C. Cultural locations
- **The old town itself** with its covered streets, gates, squares (each clan district had a public space for festivals, SOURCED, Wikipedia "Ghadames"), the spring and its pool, mosques.
- **House interiors:** red designs on whitewashed walls (triangles, diamonds, suns, moons, palms, hands), mirrors, palm-straw food covers, brass and copper (SOURCED as stated on temehu.com, a site with a polemical tone; treat as its account). Wall painting is done by women (UNVERIFIED, snippets). **Zanjafor/Zanjafoor** mural painting is a women-led craft at risk, revived by the MaLiCH project (SOURCED that it was revived, KCL 2025; the dedicated article returned 404).
- **Leather crafts** (shoes, bags) historically (UNVERIFIED, garbled snippet of a Libyan academic paper). Basket weaving shown at the 2023 festival (SOURCED, AFP 2023). [K13, K14]
- **Festivals [K2, K3]:** a three-day festival each October at the end of the date harvest: day one in the modern town; day two in the old city, where residents return to family homes, open their doors, sing and dance, hold weddings and coming-of-age ceremonies; day three at a Tuareg camp in the dunes (SOURCED, World Nomads/Lonely Planet 2009). A three-day October festival with singing, street dancing and dune sunsets, which officials hoped to revive in 2013 (SOURCED, Reuters/Arab News 2013). The 14th "Ghadames International Tourism Festival" was held in 2010 with an open exhibition in the old city on Tuareg heritage (UNVERIFIED, Arabic snippet; page returned 410). A crafts and heritage "shopping festival" featuring Tuareg traditions in Feb. 2023 drew visitors from across Libya and Tunisia (SOURCED, AFP via Al-Monitor 4 Feb. 2023).
- **Civic role:** Ghadames hosted the first UN-facilitated Libyan political dialogue on 29 Sept. 2014 (House of Representatives members and boycotting members; call for a nationwide ceasefire) (SOURCED, UN News 29 Sept. 2014), and the Joint Military Commission (5+5) on 2–4 Nov. 2020, its first meeting inside Libya (SOURCED, Al Ain 2 Nov. 2020). [K+ neutral meeting ground]

### D. How the defining feature shaped what exists
- **Two-level town:** men's covered alleys below, women's roof network above, with houses joined so that "roofs attached to each other" form paths (SOURCED, Wikipedia; Ghasouf paper figure caption "women's passages on the roofs"). [K4, K6, R]
- **Water time as law:** spring shares measured by time with the qadus, priced except for mosque, drinking and washing (SOURCED). [K1, K6]
- **Seasonal use after abandonment:** owners return in summer because the old architecture is cooler (SOURCED, Wikipedia "Ghadames"; Al Jazeera 2012) and for the October festival (SOURCED). [K10, K7]

### E. Structural features (incl. abandonment, conflict, conservation)
- **Abandonment:** in the 1970s the government built new housing outside the old town (SOURCED, Wikipedia); families began moving in 1981 (SOURCED, Al Ain 2020); the town "began to crumble" after people moved in the 1970s–80s to homes with better sanitation and modern amenities (SOURCED, Al Bawaba 2003); "officially uninhabited since the mid-1980s" (SOURCED, World Nomads 2009); the last family left in the 1990s for lack of water and electricity (UNVERIFIED, Al Jazeera 2014 snippet). Dates differ by source. A 1943 air strike flattened a group of homes never rebuilt (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2022). Rain has caused collapses (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2022; Arkeonews 28 Jan. 2022; AFP 2023 says authorities called heavy rain "a new climate phenomenon in the region"). [K9]
- **Conflict since 2011 (reported neutrally; accounts differ):** Ghadamsi townsfolk and Tuareg, both Amazigh, had lived together for generations (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2012). In Aug. 2011 opposition forces entered without a fight; after a pro-government counterattack about a month later that involved Tuareg leaders, about 200 Tuareg families left the next day; local officials said reprisals were isolated acts against militiamen and that families were never forced out, while displaced Tuareg said they were scapegoated (SOURCED, The World/PRX, 15 Aug. 2013). Clashes in Sept. 2011 and May 2012 caused deaths on both sides; about 2,200 displaced Tuareg were in Dirj by Aug. 2012 (UNHCR estimate) and Tuareg leaders proposed a new town (Alwaal) about 50 km away, which Ghadamsi negotiators called unrealistic and UNSMIL opposed (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2012). Wikipedia states Tuareg troops controlled the town until 2011 and that, after a later change of control, the Tuareg community was forced to flee, using the term "ethnic cleansing" (SOURCED as Wikipedia's wording; not confirmed in a primary source here). [K9]
- **UNESCO:** inscribed 1986; Danger List 2016 because of conflict damage and the threat of more (SOURCED via search summaries of UNESCO; the UNESCO pages themselves refused 403); Libya sought removal from July 2021 (SOURCED, AFP 2023); removed July 2025 at the 47th session after a monitoring mission in Feb. 2025; the KCL-led MaLiCH project (from 2020, funded by ALIPH) worked with the Department of Antiquities, the Ghadames City Promotion and Development Authority, Libya's UNESCO delegation, Bayt Alyakaniya (Cairo) and local civil society on emergency stabilization, women-led crafts (Zanjafor painting), a new Visitor Center, youth engagement and community-led management planning (SOURCED, KCL news 31 July 2025). [K1, K8, K14]
- **Building science:** traditional houses rely on thick walls, compact form, internal courtyards/light wells and natural ventilation; contemporary houses depend on mechanical cooling (SOURCED, abstract: Bugaldaen and Alghabaile, Misurata Univ. journal, Jan. 2026). Field measurements in 21 traditional dwellings in summer 2013–2014 and a 2001 *Building and Environment* comfort study exist (Ealiwa et al. 2001, DOI 10.1016/s0360-1323(99)00071-2) but their numbers were not read. [R]

### Key findings, one per pick
- **S2-01 Icehotel / snow hotels:** the cold rooms work only because every warm function (toilets, showers, saunas, luggage, changing) sits in a heated building next door and most guests sleep one night on ice; the winter hotel is borrowed from the Torne River each March and melts back in April, while a permanent solar-cooled ice building (ICEHOTEL 365, 2016, -5 °C) runs year-round. Kemi's snow castle shows the fragility (mild winters, a liquidated operating company, a pandemic gap) and that snow hotels follow ordinary hotel fire rules.
- **S2-15 Snow/earth-sheltered dwellings:** measured iglu data (Kershaw et al. 1996): with seal-oil lamps the chamber reached up to 45 °C above outside air; a small skin-lined iglu used one seal's fat every 6.3 days; heat was concentrated on one platform or in one room (baðstofa), not spread. Iceland's vistarband labor law (1490–1894) filled that room with bound servants; Qikiqtani settlement housing (1959–75) replaced iglus and qarmait with small plywood houses sized to save fuel.
- **S2-16 Underfloor heating:** both systems have a hot end and cool end that society turned into order (Korean seat of honor at the warm end; Roman room-by-room bathing circuit with hours, staff and fees). Ondol's spread in the 17th century stripped the hills around Seoul; the coal-briquette era killed about 1,000 people in 1976 alone. Medieval Baltic heat-storage hypocausts stored heat in stones for days.
- **S2-02 Ghadames:** a two-level town (men's covered alleys, women's rooftop network) organized around one spring whose water was shared by time with the qadus and priced except for mosques, drinking and washing; left for a state-built new town from the 1970s–80s, still used in summer and for the October date-harvest festival; on the UNESCO Danger List 2016–2025, removed July 2025 after a community-led conservation project.
