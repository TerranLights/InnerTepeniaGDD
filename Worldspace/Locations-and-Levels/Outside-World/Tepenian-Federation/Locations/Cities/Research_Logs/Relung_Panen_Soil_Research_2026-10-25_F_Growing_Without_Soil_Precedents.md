## 1. Findings summary

### 1.1 What was found, in brief

1. **Antarctic plant growing is small, salad-scale, and soilless by law.** At least 46 facilities have operated in Antarctica since 1902; nine were operating in 2015 (Bamsey et al. 2015). The best-measured ones (EDEN ISS at Neumayer III; the South Pole Food Growth Chamber) produce on the order of 1 to 4 kg of fresh vegetables per day for crews of about 10 to 50, on 12.5 to about 23 m² of growing area. None grows staple calories (grain, tubers, legumes) at any meaningful scale. The Madrid Protocol's revised Annex II prohibits deliberate import of non-sterile soil and requires permits for cultivated plants.
2. **Measured energy cost in Antarctica is very high.** EDEN ISS (2018) used about 205 kWh of electricity per kg of fresh food for the plant-growing hardware alone, about 256 kWh/kg including the facility's other equipment, and averaged 19.2 kWh per m² of growing area per day. Its crew time was 6.31 crew-member-hours per kg of food in 2019.
3. **The only measured "whole diet" closed-system figures come from life-support research.** NASA and BIOS-3 work puts the figure at about **50 m² of crop area per person** for 2,500 kcal/day under electric light (Wheeler, NASA). Biosphere 2 fed eight people about 80% of their nutrition from 2,000 m² of sunlit, soil-based farm (including animals) for two years. BIOS-3 regenerated 50 to 70% of its crew's diet in the plant compartments, on 63 m² for three people, at about 400 kW of lamp-driven power.
4. **Scaled up with stated assumptions** (COMPUTED, Section 2.2): at 50 m²/person and lighting energy of 3.4 to 7.4 kWh/m²/day, 100,000 people need about 5 km² of lit growing area and about 0.7 to 1.6 GW of average lighting power; 1,000,000 people need about 50 km² and about 7 to 16 GW, before heating, cooling, dehumidification, pumping or processing.
5. **Making soil from sterile rock is documented, but as revegetation, not as farmland.** Biosolids at about 220 dry t/ha took bare copper tailings (0% organic matter) to 77% plant cover in under three years (Arizona). Basalt rock dust at 50 t/ha/yr and 10 kg/m² raised yields 12 to 21%, but only as an amendment to existing farm soil. Constructed soils ("Technosols") made from waste show soil-like horizon development within about three years. Reclaimed Icelandic basalt desert held 4.91% soil carbon after 11 years under trees, against 0.76% on eroded ground. Natural soil formation runs at about 1 t/ha/yr or less (Verheijen et al. 2009).
6. **Regolith studies are the nearest analog for growing in sterile crushed rock, and they show the limits.** Plants germinated and grew for 50 days on Mars and Moon simulants with no nutrients added (Wamelink 2014). Real Apollo regolith grew Arabidopsis, but the plants were stunted and stressed (Paul et al. 2022).
7. **Failure cases are well documented.** They include springtail infestations at Scott Base (2002, 2004) and Davis (2014). Davis also suffered snow and ice damage and heat loss. Halley VI cancelled its planned 11 m² unit on cost-benefit grounds. Australia suspended all station plant growing in 1989. In Biosphere 2, the rich soils drew oxygen down from 20.9% to about 14%. EDEN ISS lost temperature control in midwinter, and its pumps lasted about a year. Arctic community greenhouses ended for lack of committed operators and because the produce could not match import prices. In 2023, AeroFarms filed for Chapter 11 and AppHarvest was liquidated.

## 2. Findings by question

### 2.1 Question 1 — Antarctic and polar precedents

#### 2.1.1 The master inventory (Bamsey et al. 2015)

**Source:** Bamsey, M.T., Zabel, P., Zeidler, C., Gyimesi, D., Schubert, D., Kohlberg, E., Mengedoht, D., Rae, J., Graham, T. "Review of Antarctic Greenhouses and Plant Production Facilities: A Historical Account of Food Plants on the Ice." ICES-2015-060, 45th International Conference on Environmental Systems, Bellevue, WA, July 2015. PDF: https://elib.dlr.de/96573/1/ICES-2015-060_Antarctic%20Plant%20Production%20Facility%20Review.pdf (read in full).

SOURCED facts:
- "More than 46 distinct plant production facilities have, at one time or another, operated in Antarctica." These were operated by 13 nations plus one NGO (Greenpeace). In 2015, **nine** were operating: Casey, Davis and Mawson (Australia), Great Wall (China), Syowa (Japan), King Sejong and Jang Bogo (South Korea), Scott Base (New Zealand), and Amundsen-Scott South Pole (USA).
- Countries that confirmed they did not operate facilities in 2015: India, Norway (which had never operated one), Poland, and Russia. "Russia does not presently operate any specifically designed plant production facilities at its Antarctic stations nor do they have plans to reinitiate" the closed ones. (This changed later; see Vostok, 2.1.6.)
- Pre-Madrid-Protocol facilities often used non-native soil (Polish soil at Arctowski, later "local sand (flushed with fresh water) and diluted guano"; US soil at Cape Hallett 1958; New Zealand soil at Lake Fryxell 1979; Polish soil and penguin guano). One pre-protocol facility is hypothesized as a vector for *Poa annua* establishing in Antarctica. "Currently operating facilities no longer import non-native soils nor utilize local soils. Rather, they are required to use hydroponic systems."
- Container form factor: seven Antarctic facilities have been built into shipping containers (Casey, Davis ×3, Mario Zucchelli, King Sejong, Scott Base).
- Psychological effect, King Sejong: "83% of station crew members found that the produced vegetables were either 'very helpful' or 'somewhat helpful' to their mental health."
- Historic substrates used in soilless growing: scoria at Casey (1980); sand, gravel and vermiculite at Davis (1969); local volcanic ash and sand at Stonington Island (1946); perlite and shale at Syowa (1966); gravel and vermiculite at Halley I (1962); later rockwool, perlite, expanded clay and vermiculite.

#### 2.1.2 EDEN ISS (Neumayer III, Germany / DLR)

**Sources (all opened):**
- Zabel, P., Zeidler, C., Vrakking, V., Dorn, M., Schubert, D. (2020). "Biomass Production of the EDEN ISS Space Greenhouse in Antarctica During the 2018 Experiment Phase." *Frontiers in Plant Science* 11:656. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2020.00656/full
- Zabel, P., Vrakking, V., Zeidler, C., Schubert, D. (2022). "Energy and Power Demand of Food Production in Space based on Results of the EDEN ISS Antarctic Greenhouse." ICES-2022-87, St. Paul, MN, 10–14 July 2022. https://elib.dlr.de/186936/1/EDEN%20ISS%20Energy_final_v2.pdf
- DLR press release, 23 Aug 2019. https://www.dlr.de/en/latest/news/2019/03/20190823_project-eden-iss-presents-results
- DLR "ANT-Land 2019/20" project report. https://elib.dlr.de/142073/1/ANT-Land%20Report%20-%20EDEN%20ISS%2019-20_final%20v2.pdf
- Crew-time paper abstract (2019 season), elib.dlr.de/143406.
- Bunchek, J. (2022). "2022 EDEN ISS Antarctic Greenhouse Project Report" (NASA/DLR). https://elib.dlr.de/197091/1/Bunchek_NASA%20PDF184_2022%20EDEN%20ISS%20Antarctic%20Greenhouse%20Project%20Report.pdf

**Facility (SOURCED):** two conjoined 20 ft containers with 10 cm insulation, on a platform 400 m south of the station, holding 12.5 m² of cultivation area. Power and IT come by buried cable, but there is **no water connection**: "All fresh water must be prepared inside the station and manually brought to the greenhouse, and all waste water must be manually removed" (Bunchek 2022). Fresh water is prepared at the station "at a rate of 1 L/min" and then transported. Growing conditions in 2018: 330–600 µmol/(m²·s) LED, 21 °C, ~65% RH, 1,000 ppm CO₂, 17 h photoperiod.

**Station power (SOURCED, Zabel 2022):** Neumayer III has four diesel combined heat and power plants of up to 150 kW electric each, with up to three running on demand and one as emergency backup. Waste heat from power generation heats the station. A wind turbine adds up to 30 kW.

**Yields (SOURCED):**
- 2018 (7 Feb – 20 Nov, 286 days): >268 kg edible. Breakdown: cucumbers 67 kg, lettuces 56 kg, leafy greens 49 kg, tomatoes 50 kg, herbs 12 kg, radish 8 kg, kohlrabi 19 kg. Total biomass was 517 kg, of which 268 kg was food. 26 crops were grown. Productivity was 27.4 kg/m²/yr, or 0.075 kg/(m²·d); excluding underperforming peppers (11% of the area), 0.089 kg/(m²·d). Most of the harvest was consumed by a winter-over crew of 10.
- Crop-specific rates in 2018 (kg/m²/d): cucumber 0.321, mizuna 0.194, arugula 0.188, kohlrabi 0.141, Red Giant mustard 0.130, Swiss chard 0.102, Waldmann's Green lettuce 0.080, radish 0.078, tomato 0.052.
- 2019: 106.5 kg, harvested June–November after standby mode February–May. Monthly: Jun 4.87, Jul 14.74, Aug 25.29, Sep 26.67, Oct 23.97, Nov 11.02 kg.
- 2021 (2 Mar 2021 – 15 Jan 2022, 319 days): 315 kg of fresh edible biomass from 37 cultivars, averaging 0.99 kg/day and about 110 g per crew member per day. The report states "25.3 kg per m2, which was 790 g per m2 per day." **COMPUTED check:** 315 kg ÷ 12.5 m² ÷ 319 d = 0.079 kg/m²/d = **79 g/m²/d**, so "790 g" appears to be a typographical error in the report.

**Energy (SOURCED, Zabel 2022):**
- Monthly electrical demand was 6,600–7,800 kWh. The total for February–November 2018 was 68,516 kWh, which equals 5,481 kWh per m² of cultivation area.
- Average power was 11.23 kW during the 17 h photoperiod and 8.21 kW during the 7 h dark period, or 898.4 and 656.8 W per m² of cultivation area. DLR's press release rounds this to "0.8 kW per square meter," against a prior aerospace assumption of 2.1 kW/m².
- Daily demand per area was 19.2 kWh/m²/d for EDEN ISS and 12.8 kWh/m²/d for the SPFGC. Illumination alone was 5.7 kWh/m²/d for EDEN ISS (LED) and 9.5 kWh/m²/d for the SPFGC (HPS). Illumination was 28% of EDEN's total demand.
- "Common Equipment" (room heaters, water boiler, room lights, kettle) used 13,461 kWh, 20% of the total, averaging 2,280 W.
- Plant-cultivation hardware used ~55,055 kWh, giving "~205 kWh electrical energy per kilogram produced food" in 2018.
- After a free-cooler fan failed in mid-May, the remaining fan was shut down June–September because Antarctic air alone cooled the fluid. Demand still rose June–October, "most likely associated with the dropping temperatures during the winter months and the corresponding need for additional electrical heating."
- **COMPUTED:** including Common Equipment, 68,516 kWh ÷ 268 kg = **~256 kWh/kg** of food.

**Crew time (SOURCED):**
- 2018: "an average of three to four hours per day to grow the plants," of which about two-thirds went to operating and maintaining the technology and one-third to sowing, harvesting and care. Experiments took another 4–5 hours per day (DLR press release 2019). **COMPUTED:** 3–4 h/day × 7 = 21–28 h/week for cultivation alone.
- 2019: total crew time was 694.5 crew-member-hours, or **6.31 CM-h/kg** (elib.dlr.de/143406 abstract). The monthly maximum was 102.5 h (July and August), and remote support peaked at about 50 h (August) (ANT-Land report).
- 2021: "an average of 4 h 50 minutes needed to be spent in EDEN each day," not counting most support work inside the station. Harvesting was the largest task at 254 h, then cleaning 160 h, repair 117 h, nutrient mixing 105 h, and routine maintenance 103 h (Bunchek 2022). "In its current iteration, EDEN ISS requires too much crew input." **COMPUTED:** 4.83 h × 319 d ≈ 1,541 h ÷ 315 kg ≈ **4.9 h/kg** (in-greenhouse time only).

**Water per kg: NOT FOUND.** Fresh and waste water were measured for the first time in 2021, but the results were not published in the 2022 report (Bunchek 2022, "Consumables"). The 2020 biomass paper does not report water.

#### 2.1.3 South Pole Food Growth Chamber (SPFGC), Amundsen-Scott

**Sources:** Patterson, R.L., Giacomelli, G.A., Kacira, M., Sadler, P.D., Wheeler, R.M. (2012). "Description, operation and production of the South Pole Food Growth Chamber." *Acta Horticulturae* 952:589–596 (abstract opened at experts.arizona.edu); Bamsey et al. 2015; Zabel et al. 2022.

SOURCED:
- Built by the University of Arizona CEAC with Sadler Machine Co., under an NSF contract from 2001. Operating since 2004.
- Plant production room of 5.60 × 3.91 × 2.50 m, plus an environmental room of 2.35 × 3.91 × 2.50 m. Cultivation area ~22.7 m² (Zabel 2022); Bamsey gives a 50 m² facility footprint.
- Light from HPS lamps; NFT and deep-flow hydroponics. Station interior at ~68 kPa, ~16 °C, ~10% RH.
- Daily inputs (2006 data, Jan–Oct): 1.1 kg CO₂, 0.21 kg dry fertilizer salts, and **1,012 MJ (281 kWh)** of electricity. Daily outputs: 0.52 kg O₂ and **2.8 kg edible fresh vegetables**.
- Yields "as high as 45 kg of fresh vegetables per week with typical yields of approximately 27 kg per week" (Bamsey, citing Patterson). Power draw in 2006 was ~16 kW over the 17 h photoperiod and ~3 kW in the dark.
- Productivity 0.13 kg/m²/d (Zabel 2022).

**COMPUTED and a conflict:**
- 281 kWh/d ÷ 2.8 kg/d = **~100 kWh/kg**. Zabel et al. 2022 instead state "the SPFGC used around 22.8 kWh/kg," citing Patterson 2012. The two cannot both describe the same quantity. The 22.8 figure may use a different basis (for example, total biomass, or the 45 kg/week peak). This is unresolved. Do not use either figure without checking the full Patterson paper, which was not opened (paywalled).
- Cross-check: 16 kW × 17 h + 3 kW × 7 h = 293 kWh/d, consistent with 281 kWh/d.

#### 2.1.4 Other Antarctic stations (SOURCED from Bamsey et al. 2015 unless noted)

- **Casey (Australia):** Two 20 ft containers with stainless-steel lining, in use from 2000–01. Crops include tomatoes, lettuce, herbs, cucumbers, onions, snow peas and silver beet. First season: "64kg of vegetables and herbs were produced in 5 months," including "38.6kg harvested from 24 plants" of tomato (AAD *Australian Antarctic Magazine*, 10 Oct 2001). An earlier 1980 room-scale system using scoria and fluorescent light failed through insufficient light and water supply.
- **Davis (Australia):** The 2001–02 facility was two 20 ft containers. It produced 237 kg edible between 1 Dec 2012 and 16 Oct 2013, while "420 kg of total green matter and an additional 240 kg of clay balls and perlite were put through the station's incinerator." It closed in 2014 (see failures, 2.6). The interim 2015 unit had a ~9 m² footprint with NFT and fluorescent plus HPS light, operated only in winter.
- **Mawson (Australia):** An L-shaped building (6 × 3 m and 4 × 2.5 m rooms) in use from 2001–02. In 2023 it was volunteer-run: "a dedicated team of nine," "an hour a day or thereabouts and a few hours each weekend," at a station of 19 (AAD, 31 Mar 2023). Kg harvested: NOT FOUND.
- **Australian practice (AAD hydroponics pages, opened):** "As soil is prohibited as a growing medium, seeds are propagated in Rockwool propagating cubes. Once germinated, the seedlings are transferred into perlite or expanded clay." Pollination is done by hand with a brush. "Non-fruit bearing plants are not allowed to go to seed. Instead the flowers are removed and incinerated." Rooms are kept at 25 °C and 60–70% RH. Macquarie Island has a more restrictive seed list approved by the Tasmanian Parks and Wildlife Service.
- **Syowa (Japan):** In 1966–68, plant boxes of 0.2–0.29 m² each sat under fluorescent light in a 60:40 perlite/shale medium, and a 2.7 m² growth unit was carried on the ship *Fuji*. From 2008, a 5-tier enclosure (~3 m²) at 1,500 ppm CO₂ produced 18.8 kg of lettuce from April 2008 to January 2009.
- **King Sejong (Korea):** A 20 ft container plant factory (PARU Co.) with 15–20 cm polyurethane insulation, 3 levels, and 72 trays of 60 × 30 cm. It provides "an average of more than 1 kg of fresh vegetables per day." **COMPUTED:** 72 × 0.18 m² = 12.96 m² of tray area, so ≥1 kg/d ÷ 12.96 m² ≈ ≥0.077 kg/m²/d.
- **Jang Bogo (Korea):** 35 m² inside the main building; one overwinterer is placed in charge each year.
- **Great Wall (China):** 36 m², PMMA skin on steel frame, LED plus HPS lamps, stacked ebb-and-flow. Planned output ~60 kg/month, design life 15 years, maximum electrical load 8.5 kW, with condensate recovery. HortiDaily (11 Aug 2016) reports "600 square meters of 16 millimeter thick multi-skin sheets." That is the area of cladding material, not floor area, and should not be read as a 600 m² greenhouse.

#### 2.2.4 Biosphere 2

**Source:** Asseng, S. et al. (2020). "Wheat yield potential in controlled-environment vertical farms." *PNAS* 117(32):19131–19135 (full text via Europe PMC, PMC7430987, opened).

SOURCED:

**COMPUTED (lighting energy, measured-basis wheat):**

SOURCED: "In 2014, the best LED fixtures had an efficacy of 1.7 µmol J⁻¹." #### 2.2.8 Area and energy to supply one person's calories for a year; 100,000 and 1,000,000 people (COMPUTED)

**Assumptions (each stated, each sourced where possible):**
- A1. Calorie target: **2,500 kcal/person/day** (the basis of Wheeler's 50 m² figure). For reference, WFP and UNHCR use **2,100 kcal/person/day** as the emergency ration planning figure, following the 1995 Committee on International Nutrition report and WHO (search summary of UNHCR/ENN sources: UNVERIFIED in detail, widely cited).
- A2. Crop mix: Wheeler's figure rests on BIOS-3 plus NASA chamber crops (wheat, potato, soybean, lettuce, tomato) under electric light. No animal products are included.
- A3. Area: **50 m² of growing area per person** (Wheeler, SOURCED).
- A4. Light: the NASA chamber used 510–930 µmol/m²/s for wheat. Taking 20 h/day at 510 and at 930 µmol/m²/s gives 36.7 and 67.0 mol/m²/d.
- A5. Fixture efficacy: 2.5–3.0 µmol/J (Kusuma 2020, SOURCED). All emitted photons are assumed to reach the crop (optimistic).
- A6. Only lighting energy is counted. Heating, cooling, dehumidification, pumping, CO₂ supply, harvest, processing and storage are excluded, so these are **lower bounds** for lit systems. For scale, EDEN ISS's total demand was about 3.4 times its lighting demand (19.2 ÷ 5.7).

**Lighting energy per m² per day:**
- Low: 36.7 mol ÷ 3.0 µmol/J = 12.2 MJ = **3.4 kWh/m²/d**.
- High: 67.0 mol ÷ 2.5 µmol/J = 26.8 MJ = **7.4 kWh/m²/d**.
- Cross-check: EDEN ISS measured 5.7 kWh/m²/d for LED lighting of salad crops, inside this range.

**Per person (50 m²):**
- Area: 50 m².
- Lighting: 50 × 3.4 = 170 kWh/day up to 50 × 7.4 = 370 kWh/day. That is **62–135 MWh/yr**, or **7.1–15.4 kW** of continuous average power.
- With the EDEN ISS whole-facility ratio (19.2 kWh/m²/d, salad crops, stand-alone Antarctic container): 50 × 19.2 = 960 kWh/day = 350 MWh/yr = 40 kW average. This is an illustration of the measured Antarctic benchmark, not a design figure.

**100,000 people:**
- Growing area: 100,000 × 50 m² = 5,000,000 m² = **5 km² (500 ha)** of lit growing area. With 10 stacked layers, floor footprint ≈ 0.5 km² (50 ha), ignoring aisles and plant rooms.
- Lighting: 100,000 × 62–135 MWh = **6.2–13.5 TWh/yr**, or **0.71–1.54 GW** average.

**1,000,000 people:**
- Growing area: **50 km² (5,000 ha)**; with 10 layers, ≈ 5 km² of floor.
- Lighting: **62–135 TWh/yr**, or **7.1–15.4 GW** average.
- At the EDEN ISS whole-facility rate: 350 TWh/yr, or ~40 GW average.

**Sunlit comparison (Biosphere 2 basis, soil, includes animals, about 80% of nutrition):** 250 m²/person → 100,000 people need 25 km², and 1,000,000 people need 250 km². Energy for supplementary light and heat at a polar site: NOT computed (no sourced per-area figure exists for a polar sunlit food-crop greenhouse feeding people at full diet).

### 2.3 Question 3 — Making soil

#### 2.3.1 Crushed rock (basalt rock dust): an amendment, not a soil

- **Kelland et al. 2020, *Global Change Biology* 26:3658 (DOI 10.1111/gcb.15089; PDF via White Rose, opened):** "amending a UK clay-loam agricultural soil with a high loading (10 kg/m²) of relatively coarse-grained crushed basalt significantly increased the yield (21 ± 9.4%, SE)" of *Sorghum bicolor* under controlled conditions, "without accumulation of potentially toxic trace elements in the seeds," after 120 days without P or K fertilizer. Modeled CO₂ sequestration was 2–4 t CO₂/ha, 1–5 years after a single application. **COMPUTED:** 10 kg/m² = 100 t/ha.
- **Beerling et al. (US Corn Belt; arXiv 2307.05343, opened; published in *PNAS* 2024):** "we applied crushed basalt on an annual basis for four years (at a rate of 50 tons per hectare)." "Maize and soybean yields increased 12-16%." Each 50 t/ha application "released an average of at least 7 kg P ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ and 23 kg K ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹." Cumulative carbon removal was 15.4 ± 4.1 t CO₂/ha over four years.
- **What was not found:** any published case of crushed rock alone, with no existing soil, being turned into productive cropland. Every rock-dust trial read was on existing agricultural soil. Searched: enhanced weathering field trials, rock dust crop trials. NOT FOUND.

#### 2.3.2 Biosolids (sewage sludge) on sterile mine tailings

- **Pepper, I.L., Bengson, S.A., Rao, P.R., Josephson, K.L. (2003),** "Revegetation of Mine Tailings through the Use of Biosolid Amendment," ASMR/Billings Land Reclamation Symposium, June 2003 (PDF, opened):
  - "Tailings are in essence crushed rock with 0% organic matter, and can be layered to depths of 30–36 m."
  - A 2 ha copper tailings plot near the Mission Mine, Arizona, received "approximately 220 dry tons per hectare of biosolids," incorporated in December 1998.
  - Heterotrophic bacteria quickly reached ~10⁷ per gram. "By September 2001 vegetative cover had increased from zero to 77%." Cover was 18% at 14 months and 78.2% later. The species were bermudagrass and Russian thistle at first, later buffalo grass and Lehmann lovegrass.
  - The tailings, not the biosolids, were the major source of metals. No metal leaching was seen under low rainfall without irrigation.
  - Arizona's lifetime limit for biosolids on tailings is 336 dry t/ha, which can be applied at once. "At least 250 metric tons (dry) per hectare may be necessary to promote active vegetation of mine tailings in the desert Southwest USA" (citing Bengson 2000). Greenhouse tests used 67 dry t/ha (Thompson & Rogers 1999).
  - This is revegetation with grasses, not food-crop land.
- Other rates (search summaries only, UNVERIFIED): 62, 77 and 179 dry t/ha trials, with 77 best (5,500 kg/ha first-season yield); trial plots of 20–80 dry t/ha, with 80 best for germination; an Ontario approved maximum of 150 dry t/ha; 224 t/ha with lime at California Gulch.
- **Biochar caution:** Jeffery et al. 2011 found that biosolids were "the only feedstock showing a statistically significant negative effect (−28%)" on crop yield when made into biochar (search summary of the meta-analysis: UNVERIFIED). See 2.3.5.

#### 2.3.4 Reclaimed land: timescales

- **Iceland (basalt desert reclamation).** Nyirenda, H. (2015), "Assessment of soil carbon in reclaimed land in South-West Iceland," UNU Land Restoration Training Program final project (PDF, opened):
  - Soil carbon in natural woodland was 9.32%, tree-revegetated plots 4.91%, grass-revegetated plots 1.12%, and eroded ground 0.76%.
  - The tree plot had been under restoration for **11 years** and the grass plot for **13 years**.
  - The grass plot sat below the 1.5% C minimum expected for Icelandic Andosol under vegetation.
  - Systematic Icelandic reclamation began in **1907**. The "Farmers Heal the Land" project started in 1994, with 370 participating farmers in 1994.
- **Reclaimed US Midwest prime farmland** (search summaries of ASMR/ASRS papers: UNVERIFIED): corn yields on soils with replaced B horizon were 1,800 kg/ha higher than on graded spoil in 1980, while undisturbed soil yielded about 10% more than reconstructed soil. Poor soil physical condition (compaction) is the most limiting factor. SMCRA (1977) requires restoration to equivalent row-crop productivity, tested by multi-year yields.
- **Natural soil formation rate (SOURCED, abstract page):** Verheijen, F., Jones, R.J.A., Rickson, J., Smith, C. (2009), "Tolerable versus actual soil erosion rates in Europe," *Earth-Science Reviews* (JRC49530). "The upper limit of tolerable soil erosion, as equal to soil formation, is approximately 1.0 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹" including dust deposition. The separate weathering and dust components were not shown on the page: NOT FOUND there.

#### 2.3.5 Peat and biochar

- **Biochar (UNVERIFIED in numbers; title and authors confirmed via OpenAlex, abstract not available):** Jeffery, S., Verheijen, F.G.A., van der Velde, M., Bastos, A.C. #### 2.3.6 Lunar and Martian regolith (nearest analogs for soil-making from sterile rock)

- **Wamelink, G.W.W. et al. (2014),** "Can plants grow on Mars and the moon: a growth experiment on Mars and moon soil simulants," *PLoS ONE* 9(8):e103138 (abstract via Europe PMC, opened). "Plants are able to germinate and grow on both Martian and moon soil simulant for a period of 50 days without any addition of nutrients." Growth on the Mars simulant was "much better than on moon regolith simulant and even slightly better than on our control nutrient poor river soil." Tomato, wheat, cress and field mustard did well; cress and field mustard set seed. Open questions remain about water-holding capacity and how representative the simulants are.
- **Wamelink et al. (2019),** "Crop growth and viability of seeds on Mars and Moon soil simulants," *Open Agriculture* 4:509 (DOI 10.1515/opag-2019-0051). Per the news summary (sci.news, 15 Oct 2019, opened): "The simulants were mixed with organic matter to mimic the addition of residues from earlier harvests." Mars simulant JSC-1A was used with ten crops; nine grew and spinach failed. Biomass on the Mars simulant was comparable to Earth soil, and significantly lower on the Moon simulant. **The percentage of organic matter added: NOT FOUND.** The paper itself could not be opened: De Gruyter PDF empty, HTML returned 405, WUR edepot empty, DOAJ 403.
- **Paul, A.-L., Elardo, S.M., Ferl, R. (2022),** "Plants grown in Apollo lunar regolith present stress-associated transcriptomes that inform prospects for lunar exploration," *Communications Biology* 5:382 (full text via Europe PMC, PMC9098553, opened). The test used 900 mg of regolith per well, in 48-well plates on rockwool wicks, against a JSC-1A control. Germination was ~100% in all samples, but "lunar regolith plants were slow to develop and many showed severe stress morphologies." All showed salt-, metal- and ROS-stress gene expression, and Apollo 11 samples did worst. "In situ lunar regoliths... are not benign substrates."

#### 2.3.7 Antarctic composting experience

- **NOT FOUND:** no documented composting of food waste or plant residues into growing media at any Antarctic station. Searched in-vessel composters at Antarctic stations (Bharati, Princess Elisabeth, King Sejong, McMurdo) and the AAD waste pages.

### 2.5 Question 5 — Seed stock, livestock and amendment logistics; biosecurity (real-world constraints only)

**Protocol text (SOURCED):** Measure 16 (2009), "Amendment of Annex II to the Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty," Baltimore, 17 April 2009. Text: Australian Joint Standing Committee on Treaties tabling, https://aphref.aph.gov.au/house/committee/jsct/2february2010/treaties/antarctic_text.pdf (opened). Entry into force: "came into force for Australia on 8 December 2016" (Commonwealth of Australia Gazette C2017G00205, 17 Feb 2017, opened). Article 4:
- (1) "No species of living organisms not native to the Antarctic Treaty area shall be introduced onto land or ice shelves, or into water, in the Antarctic Treaty area except in accordance with a permit."
- (2) "Dogs shall not be introduced onto land, ice shelves or sea ice."
- (3) Permits are issued "to allow the importation only of cultivated plants and their reproductive propagules for controlled use, and species of living organisms for controlled experimental use."
- (4) Permitted species must, "prior to expiration of the permit, be removed from the Antarctic Treaty area or be disposed of by incineration or equally effective means."
- (6) Food import is allowed "provided that no live animals are imported for this purpose."
- (8) "No live poultry or other living birds shall be brought into the Antarctic Treaty area." Unconsumed poultry products must be removed or incinerated.
- (9) "The deliberate introduction of non-sterile soil into the Antarctic Treaty area is prohibited."

**Waste (Annex III, as practiced by Australia; SOURCED via the AAD waste page):** Putrescible waste is incinerated and the ash returned. Metals, plastics, paper, cardboard and glass are returned for recycling. Sewage sludge is removed to Australia.

**How seeds and plants move in practice (SOURCED):**
- Australia propagates from seed in rockwool, with no soil, and seed lists are restricted (Macquarie Island lists are approved by Tasmanian Parks and Wildlife) (AAD hydroponics pages).
- Scott Base's 2012 benchtop unit was assembled "only after the departure of the last flight of the summer season," then disassembled, cleaned with sodium metabisulfite and stored over summer (Bamsey 2015).
- Several systems were built and tested in containers before shipping (PULSA, King Sejong, the SPFGC, the O'Higgins module, EDEN ISS). Casey and Davis used containers already on site (Bamsey 2015).
- Syowa's 1965–66 growth unit was operated aboard the resupply ship *Fuji* during the voyage (Bamsey 2015).
- Historically, plants survived transfer badly in cold: at Maitri in 1986, "approximately half of the plants perished due to the freezing temperatures" while seedlings were moved between stations (Bamsey 2015).

**Supply-chain biosecurity checklists (SOURCED, COMNAP/SCAR "Inter-continental Checklists," PDF opened):**
- "The majority of seeds that become entrained in cargo come from areas immediately surrounding storage facilities."
- Wooden packaging should meet IPPC ISPM 15. Vehicles should be hot-washed or steam-cleaned, and containers pressure-washed inside and out.
- Fresh produce on aircraft calls for insect precautions.

**Documented incursions via the food and plant pathway (SOURCED):**
- The springtail *Hypogastrura viatica* was "detected live in the unpacking of imported fresh produce at an Antarctic research station" in East Antarctica in 2020, "the first record of the transfer of *H. viatica* to Antarctica via fresh food." Identification did not happen until May 2024 (Onley, Houghton, Liu, Shaw 2025, *Biological Invasions*, DOI 10.1007/s10530-024-03525-w; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "Some non-native species have colonized Antarctic station buildings and hydroponic facilities." *Trichocera maculipennis* "rapidly colonized the sewage treatment plants of multiple stations on King George Island" (Hughes et al., Antarctic Environments Portal, updated 10 May 2019).

**Livestock historically (UNVERIFIED in detail):** Byrd's cows, 1933–35 (see 2.2.9). Dogs were banned under Annex II Article 4(2) (SOURCED). The sled dogs' final removal date was not checked: NOT FOUND in this pass.

**Soil amendments to Antarctica:** NOT FOUND as a current practice. Non-sterile soil is prohibited (Art. 4(9)), and only sterile inert media (rockwool, perlite, expanded clay, vermiculite) are documented post-Protocol.

### 2.6 Question 6 — Failure cases in cold regions

All SOURCED unless marked.

1. **Australia, 1989: all station plant growing suspended** "due to concerns over the introduction of non-indigenous species and ever more stringent environment regulations." About 20 past expeditioners signed an open letter protesting it. The ban was lifted in 1991 under a standardized program (Bamsey 2015).
2. **Scott Base, 2002 and 2004: springtail infestations.** After the 2002 detection, "all plant material was destroyed, the hydroponics unit was dried and then disinfested with bleach, and cooled down rapidly." After the second infestation, the unit "was not reinitiated because of two concerns: the risk of introducing exotic species into the environment, and for energy consumption considerations" (Bamsey 2015).
3. **Davis, 2014: springtail infestation in winter, then retirement.** The facility "had, over years, suffered snow/ice damage and had a number of cold paths that resulted in heat loss and condensation issues" and was returned to Australia rather than recommissioned (Bamsey 2015).
4. **Halley VI: planned 11 m² hydroponics "terminated based on cost-benefit grounds"** (Bamsey 2015).
5. **Casey, 1980s room system:** "insufficient light and water supply," then closed (Bamsey 2015).
6. **Overheating of sunlit structures in polar summer:**
   - Halley I, 1962: "Even with outdoor temperatures well below freezing, temperatures inside the greenhouse rose above 35 °C," with low humidity (Bamsey 2015).
   - Iqaluit, 1974: "The 24-hour daylight was a problem. It got too hot."
   - Pond Inlet, 1980–81: "It did get really hot, about 120 F" (Nunatsiaq News, 26 Nov 2004).
7. **Generator fumes:** Signy Island, 1963 — poor growth of radish, lettuce and spinach was attributed possibly to "fumes from the diesel generators or high temperatures" (Bamsey 2015).
8. **India, 1986:** experiments "confirmed that neither sunlight nor the electric lighting systems were adequate for productive plant growth under the configurations used." Dakshin Gangotri closed in 1990 from excessive snow load (Bamsey 2015).
9. **EDEN ISS (Neumayer III):**
   - 2018: temperature control failed "during the Antarctic winter season, at very low external temperatures," dropping to 16 °C. Humidity reached 86% (April–July). A free-cooler fan failed. Peppers used 11% of the area for minimal fruit. The nutrient solution needed mid-mission rebalancing (Zabel 2020, 2022).
   - 2019: remote-start germination failed because rockwool did not absorb enough aeroponic spray. Microbial blooms followed sterilization, which was ascribed to reduced microbiome diversity (ANT-Land report).
   - 2021: high-pressure pumps "last on average for about a year, but often not"; many sensors failed; leaks could not be repaired in place; "EDEN ISS requires too much crew input" (Bunchek 2022).
10. **Biosphere 2 (1991–93):**
    - Oxygen fell "from an initial 20.9% (ambient) to around 14% after 16 months of closure" through "reactions with organic C in the systems' soils," and oxygen had to be injected.
    - CO₂ ranged from under 1,000 to over 4,000 ppm.
    - Food met only about 80% of needs, and crews lost 10–20% of body weight.
    - Broad mite forced a shift from white to sweet potatoes. Other pests were mites, mealybug, aphids, powdery mildew, root-knot nematode and cockroaches.
    - El Niño cloudiness reduced light (Nelson & Dempster).
11. **BIOS-3:** "the failure of four of six lamp[s]" is mentioned in one experiment (OCR fragment; UNVERIFIED in detail). High irradiance brought "air temperatures that are too high (approximately 27-30 °C)" (Salisbury et al. 1997).
12. **Arctic community greenhouses (Nunatsiaq News, 26 Nov 2004):**
    - Iqaluit (1974): "It needed full-time looking after and we couldn't give it that time."
    - Pond Inlet co-op (1985–86): only Chinese cabbage and lettuce could match import prices, and the project was not profitable.
    - Potatoes failed in the cool greenhouse.
    - "Key obstacle": finding committed operators.
13. **Commercial CEA collapse, 2023** (Food Technology magazine, Dale Buss, 26 Oct 2023):
    - AeroFarms canceled a planned US$1.2 billion IPO and filed for bankruptcy.
    - AppHarvest ousted its CEO, cut expected sales in half, and did a US$127 million sale-leaseback.
    - Infarm laid off more than half its workforce after raising more than US$600 million.
    - Causes cited include "surging energy costs for LED lighting," high interest rates and food inflation.
    - AppHarvest's liquidation and AeroFarms' exit from Chapter 11 (Sep 2023) come from the FoodNavigator headline: the article page returned 404 and redirected; SOURCED for the headline only.
14. **Midwest mine reclamation:** "poor soil physical condition is the most limiting factor to successful row crop production on reclaimed mined land" (search summary: UNVERIFIED).
