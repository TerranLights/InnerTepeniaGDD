## 1 · #76 Warsaw Old Town reconstruction (Stare Miasto, Warsaw, Poland)

**Claim to test:** "A historic center rebuilt from paintings, drawings and records, a city reviving its past from documents (K9, K14)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with corrections.** The Old Town was rebuilt 1945–1953 (Market Square reopened 22 July 1953; reconstruction of the wider historic center declared finished 1966; Royal Castle 1971–1984) from (a) interwar measured drawings made by Warsaw Polytechnic architecture students as a compulsory part of their training, rescued during the war; (b) prewar photographs and inventories; (c) 18th-century paintings (Bellotto's views) used as one reference among several; (d) salvaged fragments and rubble reused in place. **Corrections:** paintings were not the main source and were not followed exactly; facades were reproduced fairly faithfully but interiors, courtyards and plot structure were deliberately modernized (sewers, central heating, bright flats, green courtyards; 19th-century outbuildings removed); the rebuilt district was given a chosen "historic" appearance (largely 17th–18th c.) and some facades were "improved" or reflect earlier periods. The reconstruction was also a political decision with propaganda use, and it was funded partly through a payroll-linked national fund and partly by voluntary donations and volunteer labor (sources differ on the mix; see B).

### A. Snapshot
- **Location / size:** Stare Miasto, Śródmieście district, central Warsaw, on the Vistula escarpment. Area of the Old Town neighborhood (City Information System unit) **25.93 ha**; UNESCO buffer zone **666.78 ha**. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Town,_Warsaw (read 2026-11-21).
- **What it is:** 13th-century town around the Mazovian dukes' castle (later the Royal Castle); town rights c. 1300; brick walls before 1339; Market Square laid out late 13th/early 14th c.; houses Gothic until a great fire in 1607 (22 houses burned), then late-Renaissance. Square c. 90 × 73 m. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia (Old Town) and pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rynek_Starego_Miasta_w_Warszawie (read 2026-11-21).
- **Defining feature:** near-total destruction in 1944 and near-total reconstruction from records. UNESCO World Heritage "Historic Center of Warsaw," inscribed **1980**, criteria (ii) and (vi), after deferrals in 1978 and 1979 (ICOMOS had concerns about a reconstruction being listed); minor boundary modification 2014. Described as "an outstanding example of a near-total reconstruction of a span of history covering the 13th to the 20th century." — SOURCED (criteria, 1980, 2014, quote) en.wikipedia Old Town; deferral history UNVERIFIED (search snippet summarizing whc.unesco.org documents; whc.unesco.org/en/list/30 returned **403**, not retried). Note: figures of 4,500 (2004) → 3,400 (2014) that surface in searches are for **Kraków's** Old Town, not Warsaw — not used.

### B. What else exists there (economy, institutions, how it works)
**The destruction (context, plainly).** [K9] [P6]
- Warsaw Uprising began **1 August 1944, 17:00**; capitulation **2 October 1944** (63 days). Polish forces took the Old Town in the first days; Germans surrounded it; on **2 September 1944** the last Polish soldiers left the Old Town through the sewers. Over 180,000 victims in the Uprising overall; c. 40,000 people murdered in Wola 5–7 Aug 1944. Himmler ordered the city destroyed; in late September 1944 German forces systematically destroyed most of Warsaw, including the Royal Castle. — SOURCED, encyclopedia.ushmm.org "The Warsaw Polish Uprising" (read 2026-11-21). Sewer evacuation total c. 5,300 people including 150 German POWs — UNVERIFIED (warsawuprising.org snippet).
- Citywide: c. **70%** of Warsaw's buildings (of 103 million m³) and c. **80%** of dwellings destroyed; of 987 listed monuments 64 survived, none of the 141 most valuable; losses >16 billion prewar złoty vs a 1939 state budget of c. 2.5 billion; c. 40% of the destruction came after the Uprising by deliberate burning and blasting. — SOURCED, dzieje.pl (PAP, A. Kondek-Dyoniziak, 4 Jul 2023) and oko.press (A. Węgłowski, 20 Jul 2024) (both read 2026-11-21).
- Royal Castle: shelled and burned **17 Sept 1939**; Hitler ordered it blown up (4 Oct 1939); from 10 Oct 1939 German units with art historians stripped floors, marble, sculpture; **Polish museum staff, at risk of being shot, secretly removed stucco fragments, parquet, paneling and artworks** (rescue led by Stanisław Lorentz); remaining walls blown up after the Uprising in 1944. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Castle,_Warsaw (read first 100,000 chars) and IPN communiqué 203879 (19 Jul 2024, read locally from saved PDF). [K14] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- Rubble: c. **22 million m³** lay in streets and squares, plus c. 6 million m³ in ruins to be demolished; an engineer in *Stolica* (1945) reckoned removal would take two million railway wagons. — SOURCED, Museum of Warsaw exhibition text "Sala 3: Wielkie porządki" (*Zgruzowstanie Warszawy 1945–1949*, 2023; read locally from saved PDF). (oko.press gives 20–30 million m³.)

**The rebuilding body and its records.** [K1] [K14] [P7] [P8]
- **Biuro Odbudowy Stolicy (BOS, Warsaw Reconstruction Office):** predecessor office set up 22 Jan 1945 (mayor Marian Spychalski; Jan Zachwatowicz); KRN resolution to rebuild the capital 3 Feb 1945; BOS operating from **14 Feb 1945**. Director Roman Piotrowski; deputies Józef Sigalin, Witold Plapis, Szymon Syrkus. Staff c. **1,500 by mid-1945** (early 1946: 350 engineers, 534 technicians, plus doctors, nurses, 49 journalists), falling to 193 by June 1948; at end of 1945 it supervised several hundred building sites with c. 10,000 workers. 13 departments in 1945 incl. Historic Architecture. Ran its own canteen (Feb 1945), sports club (May 1945) and a staff holiday house at Świder; launched the weekly *Skarpa Warszawska* (Oct 1945, later *Stolica*); co-organized the exhibition *Warszawa oskarża* (3 May 1945); "red plaques" campaign marked c. 800 historic structures (Mar–Apr 1945). Lost powers stepwise from 1945; dissolved 1951 under a 6 Sept 1950 regulation (sources differ 1950/1951/1952). — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Reconstruction_Office; dzieje.pl (2023); przystanekhistoria.pl art. 106260 (all read 2026-11-21).
- Early staff reportedly hired first those who owned boots; staff slept on parachutes and worked from unheated offices. — SOURCED (as reported), dzieje.pl 2023; oko.press 2024.
- Damage inventory 1945–early 1946 on a **nine-grade scale** (1 = undamaged, 9 = nothing left), engineers walking assigned districts. — SOURCED, dzieje.pl 2023.
- **Archive:** c. 11,700 file units (c. 50 linear m; c. 70% of BOS output survives) per the State Archive; English Wikipedia gives c. 14,679 files (1945–1953) — figures differ by counting. Held at Archiwum Państwowe w Warszawie; being digitized; inscribed on UNESCO **Memory of the World** register **2011**. — SOURCED, dzieje.pl 2023; en.wikipedia BOS. [K1]
- **The prewar student surveys (the "records" of the claim):** in the interwar years every first-year architecture student at Warsaw Polytechnic had to measure and draw a wooden vernacular building (plans, elevations, details); after the second year the same for monumental architecture (palace, castle, walls, most often a church). By this means the Department of Polish Architecture (Zakład Architektury Polskiej, Prof. Oskar Sosnowski) held correct plans of almost all of Warsaw's historic churches. Zachwatowicz, with Sosnowski (killed during the 1939 siege), saved this archive from destruction; Zachwatowicz had himself uncovered and partly reconstructed the Old Town walls and the medieval bridge with a fragment of the Barbican in 1935–1938. — SOURCED, *Ochrona Zabytków* 37/2 (145), 1984, pp. 100–102 (bazhum.muzhp.pl; image-only scan, read by local OCR of the saved PDF; author of the church-reconstruction piece not legible in OCR; the volume also carries S. Lorentz, "Wspomnienie z dawnych lat"). Sosnowski's death 24 Sept 1939 after being wounded in the School of Architecture courtyard while trying to save archives; the surviving collection (>35,000 drawings) "provided the information needed to reconstruct Warsaw's Old Town" — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Sosnowski (secondary). [K14] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Paintings:** Bellotto's 18th-c. *vedute* and prewar students' drawings were "key sources" (en.wikipedia Old Town, SOURCED); oko.press (SOURCED) names Canaletto/Bellotto and Vogel as sources for the Baroque-style rebuild. Bellotto's paintings now hang in a room of the rebuilt Royal Castle; his artistic liberties mean the rebuilt district departs from them in places — UNVERIFIED (travel/press snippets: atlasobscura, notesfrompoland 2022).
- **Method:** bricks and decorative rubble reused (anastylosis). Facades reproduced fairly faithfully; interiors modernized (sewerage, sanitary fittings, central heating), small flats merged, 19th-century back outbuildings removed, ceramic roofs added. Surviving original elements reused, e.g. portals at nos. 21 and 31, Gothic niches at 31 and 40, polychromes at 20 and 40. Dekert side houses partly survived thanks to prewar fire-resistant reinforced-concrete ceilings (engineer Stanisław Hempel's 1938 work). Main work 1947–1951; Market Square 1949–1953. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia Old Town; pl.wikipedia Rynek; dzieje.pl 2023; Museum of Warsaw "Warsaw Reconstruction Route" page (muzeumwarszawy.pl/en/?p=29457, read 2026-11-21). [K14] [P3]
- **Conservation contractor born of the rebuild:** *Pracownie Konserwacji Zabytków* (PKZ), state enterprise created by ministerial ordinance **25 Aug 1950**, idea credited to Zachwatowicz; predecessors: conservation workshops of 1945 and a special conservation works unit (Oddział Robót Konserwatorskich nr 10) of a Warsaw state builder from 1947. PKZ became general contractor for the Royal Castle. — UNVERIFIED (search snippets of *Wiadomości Konserwatorskie* 31/2012 and *Ochrona Zabytków* articles; not opened). [K14] [R]

**Money and labor.** [K8] [K1]
- **Społeczny Fundusz Odbudowy Stolicy (SFOS)**, created **1948**; renamed SFOKiS in 1958 and its money shared with other cities (SOURCED, Museum of Warsaw Reconstruction Route page). Mechanism: one source says 0.5% of the pay of all working citizens; culture.pl (snippet only; page 403) says voluntary donations — **conflict unresolved** (UNVERIFIED both).
- Slogan of the period: "Cały naród buduje swoją stolicę" ("The whole nation builds its capital"). — SOURCED, przystanekhistoria.pl 106260.
- **Social rubble-clearing actions** from 1946: organized first by BOS, then by political parties (PPR, PSL, PPS), large workplaces, and guilds of craftsmen, artists and actors; concentrated in **September ("month of rebuilding the capital") and on Sundays**; groups were taken to the ruins to clear streets and pull bricks for reuse; volunteer brigades came from Yugoslavia and visitors from Britain and the US; the museum text states plainly these were also propaganda events. Before that (1945–46) a private market of **carters from surrounding villages** hauled rubble daily for pay — and often dumped it a few streets away. In the Three-Year Plan (1947–49) large state builders took over with bulldozers from US surplus and Soviet deliveries, converted tram wagons and narrow-gauge railways; rubble went to BOS-designated dumps that formed lasting landforms. — SOURCED, Museum of Warsaw "Sala 3" text (2023). [K7] [K14] [SM: man versus society]
- Bricks: Wrocław became a central brickworks in the early 1950s (up to 165 million bricks a year), but only c. 200 wagons reportedly went to Warsaw. — SOURCED (as reported), oko.press 2024.
- **Royal Castle (1971–1984):** a BOS castle-design studio existed from June 1945; the Sejm voted in 1949 to rebuild; design work halted 1961; decision **20 Jan 1971** (Gierek era); Civic Committee (Obywatelski Komitet Odbudowy Zamku Królewskiego) first met **26 Jan 1971** (chair: party official Józef Kępa; deputy Prof. Stanisław Lorentz); financed largely by public contributions; 500 million złoty and 1,000+ donated artworks by May 1975, with committees abroad and foreign governments donating; shell complete July 1974; interiors opened **1984**. — SOURCED, IPN communiqué 203879 (2024); en.wikipedia Royal Castle. Today a state museum under the Ministry of Culture.

**Who lived in and lives in the rebuilt Old Town.** [K4] [P4] [P9]
- The rebuilt Old Town was given a **residential function**. Postwar architects designed "modern, bright flats with central heating and spacious green courtyards"; the Museum of Warsaw's 2026 press text calls the Old Town, behind its historic facades, a living 1950s housing estate where neighbors greet each other on the way to the shops. — SOURCED, Museum of Warsaw press release "73. Urodziny Starówki" (16 Apr 2026, read locally from saved PDF); Reconstruction Route page.
- Flats in the Old and New Town were allocated through state ministries ("resorty"); workers and builders of the Old Town received flats there; an artists' colony is mentioned. — UNVERIFIED (snippet of *Ochrona Zabytków* 2017 special supplement pp. 150–155, KARTA-related; the PDF returned **403**). State allocation of flats in general was introduced by a December 1945 decree on public management of premises — UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- Residents' association exists: Stowarzyszenie Mieszkańców Warszawskiego Starego i Nowego Miasta (chair named in 2017 as K. R. Górski) — UNVERIFIED (snippet); activities NOT FOUND.
- **Land ownership:** the **Warsaw Decree of 26 Oct 1945** transferred all land in the city to the municipality; owners had six months to apply for perpetual lease of their plot, a 3,000 zł filing fee was charged, compensation rules were never issued, and many applications were rejected; c. 80% of the city's real estate was affected; the decree remains in force and Poland never passed a general restitution law, so heirs litigate case by case. — SOURCED, przystanekhistoria.pl art. 45166 (read 2026-11-21). [K9]
- Short-term rentals: across Śródmieście c. 3.5 short-term-rental flats per 100 dwellings (DELab UW, Dec 2023) — UNVERIFIED (snippet; delab.uw.edu.pl page returned a bot-check wall). Old-Town-specific figures NOT FOUND.

**Access and daily logistics.** [K5] [K6]
- The Old Town (zone SM), New Town (NM) and Castle Square (PZ) are a **no-entry zone for vehicles** (B-1 signs at every entrance). Exceptions: municipal services; **deliveries unloading up to 30 minutes between 06:30 and 11:30**; vehicles with a type I pass (works, events, removals, weddings; apply at least 7 days ahead) or type B pass (residents; businesses; free, issued by the Zarząd Terenów Publicznych, Podwale 23); type B holders may stop up to 3 minutes outside delivery hours. Cycling only on set New Town streets. Basis: Mayor's Order 4143/2010; project ZTP/45/K updated 11 Sept 2025. — SOURCED, warszawa19115.pl/en/-/organizacja-ruchu-na-starym-miescie (read 2026-11-21).

### D. How the defining feature shaped what exists
- The destruction-and-reconstruction story is itself the main cultural product: the Museum of Warsaw's exhibitions, the Heritage Interpretation Center, the Reconstruction Route, the cellars trail, the annual Old Town Birthday, and the BOS archive's World Register listing all exist because of it. [K13] [P6]
- The rebuild produced a **residential estate inside a historic shell** (1950s flats behind 17th–18th-c. facades), which shapes who lives there (allocated tenants, later owners) and the present tension between residents and visitors (cultural park, delivery windows, vehicle passes). [K4] [K9]
- It created durable **competence institutions**: the conservation enterprise PKZ (1950), the conservation doctrine credited to Zachwatowicz (General Conservator), and a habit of documenting heritage by measured drawing. [K14] [R]
- Memory practices tied to exact times (castle clock at 11:15; sirens at 17:00 on 1 Aug). [K2]

### E. Structural features
- Land municipalized by the 1945 decree; housing allocated by the state after the war → a residential population not formed by a market; unresolved restitution claims persist citywide. [K8] [K9]
- Tourism economy concentrated on the Market Square (restaurants, souvenir trade), museums financed by city and state; the Royal Castle is a national museum drawing c. 2 million visitors a year. [K8]
- Recognized authenticity debate (reconstruction vs original; 1950s fabric now itself heritage). [K9]

## 2 · #77 Shakespeare's Globe, London (1997), with its setting on Bankside, Southwark

**Claim to test:** "A performance venue rebuilt from historical records and scholarship, reviving a lost building's name and form (K13)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with corrections.** The building is a scholarly *reconstruction* of the 1599 Globe, built c. 230 m (one street nearer the river) from the original site, not on it. The evidence was thin and indirect: no interior view of the first or second Globe exists; designers used exterior views (chiefly Hollar's 1647 *Long View*), the Fortune playhouse building contract, the de Witt sketch of the Swan, surviving 16th-c. buildings for joinery, and — late in the design — the 1989 excavated foundations of the Rose and a corner of the Globe. Some features were deliberately taken from the 1614 second Globe (external staircases) for practicality, and modern safety concessions were made. The companion indoor **Sam Wanamaker Playhouse** (2014) is described by the Globe itself as "reimagined, rather than reconstructed." The "name" revived is the original's, and the institution's own label for the building is "the third Globe."

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** 21 New Globe Walk, Bankside, London SE1 9DT, south bank of the Thames, London Borough of Southwark; c. 230 m from the original site, center to center. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare%27s_Globe (read to 100,000 chars, 2026-11-21).
- **What it is:** open-air timber amphitheatre with thrust stage, yard and three covered galleries; thatched roof; c. **873 seated + c. 700 standing** (Wikipedia; its own total of "about 1,400" conflicts). Operator: **The Shakespeare Globe Trust**, a charitable company; subsidiaries Shakespeare Globe Productions Ltd and SGT Trading Ltd (commercial). — SOURCED, Wikipedia; SGT Annual Report and Financial Statements 2024 (cdn.shakespearesglobe.com, read locally from saved PDF).
- **Opened:** first performance 1993 (in German, during construction); "prologue" seasons; official opening **1997** with *Henry V* (Mark Rylance), attended by the Queen. Theater opened 22 May 1997 per EBSCO snippet — UNVERIFIED. — SOURCED, shakespearesglobe.com "The Third Globe"; Wikipedia.
- **Scale (year to 31 Oct 2024):** >**750,000 visitors** to the Bankside venues across 363 days; 617 performances and events with **429,156 attendees** and c. **£13.5m box office**; **74,242 people bought £5 groundling tickets**; >150,000 tickets at £10 or less; 51% of tickets at £30 or less; >1/3 of visitors international (147 countries); **9,740 guided tours**; income **£28.8m** (charitable activities £22.6m: theater £15.7m, education £3.1m, tours £3.6m); shop turnover £2.2m; staff costs 46% of expenditure; **471 freelancers incl. 147 actors**; **671 volunteers** working 13,000+ shifts / 53,836 hours; **"no regular Government subsidy"**, relies on Theater Tax Relief; 3,000+ members, c. 70 patrons; Living Wage Employer. — SOURCED, SGT Annual Report 2024.
- **Defining feature:** a lost 1599 building brought back as a working theater from documents, archaeology and period craft.

### B. What else exists there
**How it was made (the competence story).** [K14] [K13] [R] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Builders:** McCurdy & Co (Peter McCurdy); architect Theo Crosby (Pentagram); engineers Buro Happold; QS Boyden & Co; historical adviser **John Orrell** (*The Quest for Shakespeare's Globe*, 1983); academic advisory committee chaired by **Andrew Gurr**. — SOURCED, Wikipedia; "Building Shakespeare's Globe" (shakespearesglobe.com blog, 12 Jun 2017).
- **Timber:** unseasoned ("green") English oak from the Forest of Dean, West Hertfordshire, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; frame needed straight single logs c. 32 ft tall; each stage pillar carved from one tree, worked from square to 256 facets; every beam **hand-cut in workshops in Berkshire** and assembled on site with c. **12,000 hand-carved oak pegs**; traditional joints, period tools for the pillars; cranes and scaffolding used for safety. Build took four years. — SOURCED, Globe 2017 blog and "The Third Globe."
- **Walls:** lime plaster to a 17th-c. recipe, lime-washed. **Thatch:** water reed (and Norfolk sedge for ridges); described as the first thatched roof permitted in London since the Great Fire of 1666; required special permission; lined with fire-retardant, plus concealed roof sprinklers. Thatch in London's air lasts about half the rural 40–60 years; ridges, valleys and whole slopes have been replaced; a rethatching program is scheduled for 2027/28. In Oct 2008, master thatchers Phil Campbell and Kit Davis (both on the original thatch) led ten thatchers over four weeks re-ridging with 800 bundles of Norfolk Broads sedge while the theater stayed open. — SOURCED, Globe 2017 blog; WhatsOnStage 16 Oct 2008; SGT Annual Report 2024. Thatching and lime plastering could only be done in summer (1993). — SOURCED, Hansard 19 Jul 1993.
- **Modern concessions:** more exits, fire doors, stewards, numbered bench seating, concrete pit, fire-retardant thatch, sprinklers. — SOURCED, "The Third Globe"; Wikipedia.
- **Sam Wanamaker Playhouse** (opened **January 2014** with *The Duchess of Malfi*): based on two drawings found in the 1960s in a book at Worcester College, Oxford, once credited to Inigo Jones, attributed in 2005 to John Webb (c. 1660); architects Allies + Morrison with "reconstruction architect" Jon Greenfield; timber by McCurdy & Co (made in Oxfordshire workshops); oak panels, Scots pine floor and ceiling; six chandeliers of 72 beeswax candles each plus sconces; chandeliers raised and lowered on pulleys by staff, sometimes between acts to trim wicks; budget £7.5m (2010); an anonymous donor pledged £1.5m matched funding conditional on the Wanamaker name. — SOURCED, "Building the Sam Wanamaker Playhouse" (shakespearesglobe.com blog, 2020). [K14] [R]
- **Original Practices** (Rylance era 1995–2005): period clothing researched and made with period materials and techniques (Jenny Tiramani), reconstructed period instruments without amplification (Claire van Kampen), men in female roles, then all-female productions from 2003 (*Richard III*); program credits such as "Master of Plays." One failure: hazelnut shells strewn on the yard floor (copying the Rose finds) **blocked the yard drains**. The actor-audience relationship was judged the main success. — SOURCED, "Original Practices at Shakespeare's Globe" (Globe blog, 30 Apr 2020). From 2016 Emma Rice added lighting/sound rigs; Michelle Terry (from 2018) restored original playing conditions and introduced the **Globe Ensemble** (repertory company model). — SOURCED, Wikipedia; Ensemble 12-week rehearsal, 11:00–17:00 days UNVERIFIED (ArtsJournal 2018 snippet).

**How the project came about (money, friction).** [K8] [K9]
- **Sam Wanamaker** (US actor-director) stayed in Britain in the early 1950s after learning he risked the McCarthy-era Hollywood blacklist (UNVERIFIED, snippets; dates 1951/1952 differ). In 1949 he found only a blackened plaque on a brewery wall marking the Globe site (SOURCED, Hansard 1993). Globe Playhouse Trust formed 1970 (Hansard) / Shakespeare Globe Trust 1971 (Globe site) — sources differ. Inspiration: a mock Globe at a 1930s US world's fair (Chicago 1934 per Hansard; Cleveland 1936 per the Globe site; the Globe blog says Cleveland 1949 — **conflict**).
- **Site and dispute:** 125-year lease with Southwark Council signed **1982** for a riverside site; a new council leadership then tried to break the lease, calling the project and Shakespeare study "elitist"; after c. four years the site was "won back" when the council concluded it could not defend its actions in court; ground-breaking 1987 (Prince Philip); construction in earnest 1989. — SOURCED (as stated by an MP in debate), Hansard HC 19 Jul 1993.
- **Money (1993):** c. £7.5m raised and spent on foundations; £2.5m more needed; English Tourist Board's £200,000 (1989) withdrawn when work had not started; donations from the governments of New Zealand, Denmark, Canada, Japan and Bremen; oaks from Windsor Great Park; timber from the New Forest and Forest of Dean (Forestry Commission); steel from British Steel and a California firm; the UK government repeatedly declined direct funding. — SOURCED, Hansard 1993.

- Wanamaker died 1993, before completion. — SOURCED, "The Third Globe."

**The original Globe and its district (history).** [K9] [P6]
- The 1599 Globe was built from the timbers of **The Theater** (Shoreditch, 1576/77), dismantled by the Burbages' company around Christmas 1598 while the landlord was away and carried across the Thames; burned **29 June 1613** when a stage cannon set the thatch alight during *Henry VIII*; rebuilt on the same foundations by June 1614 (tiled roof); closed by Parliament's ordinance of September 1642; pulled down 1644–45 for tenements. — UNVERIFIED for the dismantling details (snippets: Wikipedia, SHaLT); fire and 1642 closure SOURCED, London Museum "The outdoor theaters of Tudor London" (read 2026-11-21).
- **Why Bankside:** a law barred playhouses in the City three years before The Theater opened, so they were built outside the walls (London Museum, SOURCED). Bankside's **Liberty of the Clink** (sold to the Bishop of Winchester c. 1149) lay outside the City and the county sheriff's authority; it allowed theaters, licensed brothels (grant of 1161) and bull- and bear-baiting; abolished 1889. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_of_the_Clink.
- **Neighbors then:** the Rose (1587; c. 500 in the yard, c. 1,100 in three galleries; demolished 1606), the Swan (1595; called the largest, 3,000), the Hope (1614; plays and bear-baiting). Excavation finds at the Rose include money pots (likely for fees or snack money), a finger ring, dagger guard, fork. — SOURCED, London Museum. Food remains (oysters, mussels, hazelnuts, walnuts, dried fruit; crab, sturgeon and imported fruit associated with galleries) — UNVERIFIED (NPR 2016 snippets; the NPR page failed to load: socket hang-up). [K12]
- **Thames watermen** ferried playgoers; ferrying to the playhouses was a large share of their income until coaches and the theaters' move north reduced it (UNVERIFIED, snippets). Company of Watermen and Lightermen (dated 1514 by the London Museum); apprentice race **Doggett's Coat and Badge** since 1715, still run. — SOURCED, London Museum "Who were the River Thames watermen?" [K14] [K5]
- **Rules of the period:** the Privy Council ordered playhouses closed when weekly plague deaths exceeded about 30 (decree after 1603), often disregarded by players. — UNVERIFIED (snippets). [K6]
- **Archaeology 1989:** Rose foundations found during a routine pre-development dig in Park Street; "Save the Rose" campaign; the office building was redesigned so the remains survive beneath it. A corner of the Globe's foundations was found behind **Anchor Terrace** (1834, built for senior staff of the Anchor Brewery); scheduled monument 13 Dec 1989; most of the Globe lies under listed houses and cannot be excavated; its outline is marked in the paving. — UNVERIFIED (snippets; Historic England / Wikipedia). The **Rose Theater Trust** (incorporated 1988) preserves the Rose; in 2022–23 it had only limited site access. — UNVERIFIED (charity filings snippet).

### D. How the defining feature shaped what exists

## 3 · #78 Raleigh Tavern, Colonial Williamsburg (Williamsburg, Virginia, USA) — structure only

**Claim to test:** "A tavern reconstructed from documentary evidence and returned to use as a public gathering place (K10)."
**Verdict:** **HALF CONFIRMED, HALF CORRECTED.** (1) *Reconstructed from documentary evidence:* **confirmed** — rebuilt 1930–31 (opened 16 Sept 1932) from excavated foundations (dig from 1928), Benson Lossing's 1848 drawings of the facade and Apollo Room, estate inventories, insurance/period descriptions and the 1782 "Frenchman's Map." Public gathering happens around it: ticketed tours, Revolutionary-era street theater using porches as stages, the adjacent **Raleigh Tavern Bakery** (takeaway), anniversary events of societies founded there, and Colonial Williamsburg's top donor tier named after it. ### B. What else exists there
**The original tavern's life (history; what a tavern was here).** [K10] [K12] [P4] [P7]
- One of more than a dozen taverns for most of the 18th century; in the 1760s up to 21 taverns for c. 1,500 residents (CW magazine, UNVERIFIED snippet); twelve on the eve of the Revolution. - **Rooms and uses:** public dining room open to all; private rooms for elites; **Apollo Room** (addition c. 1749; balls, concerts, lectures; a Cherokee delegation hosted in 1751; Latin mantel motto roughly "cheerfulness is the offspring of wisdom and good living"; part of the original fireplace survived and is built into the reconstruction); **Daphne Room** (clubs, refreshments during balls); billiard room (a 1771 inventory valued the billiard table at £500); bar likely staffed by a white barkeeper serving imported wine, brandy, gin, rum, ale, porter, cider and punch. - **Keepers:** cabinetmaker **Anthony Hay** acquired it 1767 and kept it until his death (1770); **James Barrett Southall** bought it from Hay's estate in 1771 and owned it c. 30 years until his death in 1801 (he also kept a 920-acre livestock farm, "Southall's Quarter"). — SOURCED, W&M "Southall's Quarter"; Wikipedia.
- **Political use:** 1769 burgesses met in the Apollo Room after Governor Botetourt dissolved the House and adopted the Non-Importation Agreement; 1773 Jefferson, Henry and others planned intercolonial committees of correspondence; May 1774 after Dunmore's dissolution, 89 former burgesses signed an Association and called for a "general congress." 1779: first public celebration of Washington's birthday in Williamsburg held there. 1824: banquet for Lafayette. — SOURCED, Wikipedia; Google Arts/CWF.

## 4 · #118 Shuri Castle (首里城), Shuri, Naha, Okinawa, Japan — structure only

**Claim to test:** "A royal building rebuilt from records, surveys and old images after its destruction, then burned and rebuilt again, the past revived from paper (K9, K14)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with dates and nuances.** Destroyed in the Battle of Okinawa (ablaze by 27 May 1945); site used by the University of the Ryukyus 1950–1984; main hall (Seiden) rebuilt 1989–1992 (opened **3 Nov 1992**, 20th anniversary of reversion) from: the 1768 royal repair record with drawings and timber dimensions (the "Sunpōki"), the Shō family's 1846 repair drawings, the 1928–33 dismantling-repair drawings made when the hall was the Okinawa Shrine worship hall, and prewar photographs and field notes (notably Kamakura Yoshitarō's). **Burned again 31 Oct 2019** (cause never determined); rebuilt 2022–2026 with new research (deeper red from a historically documented pigment, Okinawan timber); completion ceremony set for **22 Nov 2026** (tomorrow at the time of writing) and public opening of the interior from 23 Nov 2026. **Nuances:** (1) The castle had burned and been rebuilt at least three times before 1945 (1453, 1660, 1709); the "reference period" for reconstruction is the hall as rebuilt c. 1712. ## 5 · #79 Lüderitz and Kolmanskop, ǁKaras Region, Namibia

**Claim to test:** "A living port with an abandoned settlement a short distance away that became a local curiosity and visitor site (K9)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, and larger than stated.** Lüderitz (2023 population **16,125**) is a working fishing, mariculture and (formerly) diamond port; **Kolmanskop**, c. 10 km inland, is a 1908 diamond town abandoned by 1956 and since 2002 a ticketed visitor site inside the restricted diamond area, run under the diamond company Namdeb with a private concessionaire. Additions the claim omits: (1) Lüderitz's waterfront holds a second, darker "ruin" — **Shark Island**, a 1905–1907 German concentration camp for Nama and Herero prisoners (estimated 1,000–3,000+ deaths), now a campsite and contested memorial; (2) the diamond fields ran on Ovambo migrant contract labor under lethal conditions; (3) a planned green-hydrogen megaproject and port expansion are now reshaping the town and are opposed by Nama leaders because of Shark Island.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Atlantic coast of southern Namibia, between the Namib Desert and the sea; c. 700 km south of Windhoek; ǁKaras Region; constituency renamed **ǃNamiǂNûs** in 2013. Climate: cold desert, **c. 17 mm rain a year**, frequent strong wind, cold overcast days. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCderitz (read 2026-11-21).
- **Population:** **16,125 (2023 census)**; 12,537 (2011); 13,295 (2001). GIZ (Dec 2023) says "about 20,000." — SOURCED, Wikipedia; giz.de "All eyes on Lüderitz" (4 Dec 2023).
- **Governance:** seven-seat **Town Council**; mayor Phillipus Balhao; town administration built for a small town, budget c. EUR 9m (2023). — SOURCED, Wikipedia; GIZ.
- **Defining feature:** an isolated harbor town whose history (colonial founding, genocide camp, diamond rush and its ghost town) lies visibly around it, now facing a new resource boom.

### B. What else exists there
**Founding and colonial history (plainly).** [K9] [P6]
- Bartolomeu Dias reached the bay in 1487/88 ("Angra Pequena") and raised a stone cross at Diaz Point (replica stands there). — SOURCED, Wikipedia; az.com.na (5 Nov 2009).
- **1883:** Heinrich Vogelsang bought Angra Pequena and land for Bremen merchant Adolf Lüderitz from the ǃAman (Bethanie) Nama chief **Josef Frederiks II**; the contracts used German geographical miles (c. 7.4 km), which Frederiks did not understand — "Vogelsang misled Fredericks about the land's size." German flag raised 7 Aug 1884; bay renamed Lüderitzbucht after Lüderitz vanished in 1886. — SOURCED, Wikipedia; az.com.na 2009; NYRB (J. Hammer, 18 Nov 2021). A University of Pretoria chapter argues the acquisitions were void under Nama law — UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- 1915: South African takeover; many Germans deported (incl. the Felsenkirche's pastor and congregation); later apartheid-era segregation, which older residents still recall (CS Monitor 2016, snippet UNVERIFIED; page 403).

**Shark Island concentration camp (1905–1907).** [K9] [P6] [SM: man versus society]
- Operated by the Imperial German Army; first references March 1905; closed April 1907. Herero prisoners from 1905; Nama from August 1906 (c. 2,000 Nama by late 1906); Nama leader Samuel Isaak's protests were ignored. Rations of raw rice, salt and water; shelters open to wind, fog and spray; typhoid and scurvy; beatings with sjamboks; public executions in view of the town; rape widely reported. **Forced labor** on the harbor, the Lüderitz–Aus railway and leveling the island with explosives; a 1,600-strong Nama work force shrank to 30–40 able workers by late 1906. Remains were sent to Berlin for "racial" research (Eugen Fischer worked there); skulls including that of Nama captain Cornelius Fredericks. Deaths: estimates **1,032 to 3,000** at the camp, possibly >4,000 around the bay; 1,203 Nama dead by March 1907. Closed after Maj. Ludwig von Estorff's visit. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_Island_concentration_camp (citing Erichsen 2005; Erichsen & Olusoga 2010; Sarkin 2011; Forensic Architecture 2024).
- **Today:** a state-run holiday resort with **20 campsites and a refurbished lighthouse** rented as accommodation; a genocide memorial tombstone unveiled at a three-day descendants' commemoration (April 2023); the Ovaherero Genocide Foundation's head: it "should not be a place where people can vacation." Germany acknowledged genocide in 2021 and offered €1.1bn in development aid over 30 years; descendants and parties want talks reopened. — SOURCED, The Citizen/AFP (22 Apr 2023). Annual remembrance by the **Nama Traditional Leaders Association** and Ovaherero Traditional Authority (April 2023: Gaob David Hanse; Prof. Paul Isaak). — SOURCED, New Era (26 Apr 2023). Declared a national heritage site 15 Feb 2019 (per an op-ed) — UNVERIFIED.
- **Port expansion dispute:** Namport's planned Lüderitz port expansion (to serve green hydrogen exports) adjoins Shark Island; Forensic Architecture found evidence of burial places and possible remains in surrounding waters; Nama leaders rejected the plan (April 2024) and objected again in the EIA (Jan 2026); Namport says the site will not be harmed and promised an underwater study; the Town Council backed the extension. — SOURCED (risk, campaigners) namibian.com.na (The Conversation, 19 Feb 2025); rest UNVERIFIED (VOA, Namibian Sun snippets). [K9] [K1]

**Diamonds, Kolmanskop and contract labor.** [K9] [K14] [K7] [P8]
- **Discovery:** April 1908, railway worker **Zacharias Lewala** found a diamond while shoveling sand near the Grasplatz siding and showed it to his supervisor August Stauch. Kolmanskop ("Coleman's hill," after a transport driver who abandoned his ox wagon there in a sandstorm). — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmanskop; Lewala date UNVERIFIED.
- **Sperrgebiet:** from September 1908 a vast "forbidden zone" (nearly the size of Belgium) given mostly to the German Colonial Corporation; colonial secretary Dernburg set up the **Regie** cartel (Walther Rathenau) with output quotas, prison terms and fines for trespass or trading unregistered stones, and a spy network. — SOURCED, NYRB 2021 (review of S. Press, *Blood and Diamonds*, 2021).
- **Kolmanskop at its height (c. 1910–11):** electric power, stone houses, theater, ice factory, casino, school, hospital, ballroom, sports hall; railway to Lüderitz and electric lines to Pomona/Bogenfels (destroyed 1915) and Charlottental, powered from a 1.5 MW station in Lüderitz; skittle alley; an X-ray station reportedly used to detect swallowed diamonds (UNVERIFIED). Decline from WWI; richer deposits found 1928 near the Orange River (Oranjemund); **abandoned 1956**. — SOURCED, Wikipedia (Kolmanskop); atlasandboots.com (2018/2021).
- **Ovambo migrant labor (1908–1914):** thousands came each year from the Ovambo region on **six- or nine-month contracts**, returning home to farm; because the Ovambo region was never formally occupied, companies had to pay monthly wages and competed via a **Mining Chamber** of all Lüderitz companies that paid four recruiters (bonus per contract — so they also signed children and the sick). Travel mostly on foot, with two days in open freight cars (many died) and, from 1912, a ship from Swakopmund; many died of thirst on the road. On the fields: flour, rice, fat and sugar eaten as porridge three times a day between shifts; **scurvy** the most common disease and cause of death (22 of 25 cases at one company); workers built their own quarters of tarpaulin, jute and rusted iron ("small dirty holes without air and light"), rebuilt whenever a field was exhausted; dormitories 8 × 3.5 m with stacked bunks, no windows or floors; white supervisors lived in mobile houses. Workers used group bargaining and headmen to press for higher wages. — SOURCED, M. M. Rudolph, "The labor migration of the Ovambo workers to the diamond fields in German South West Africa," *Esboços* 31(57), 2024, 220–236 (read locally from saved PDF).
- Mortality figures: 10% of African workers at Kolmanskop in one year (official report), 331 of 2,200 Ovambo in a similar period, unofficial claims of 45%; ordinances banned "salubrious accommodations"; overseers whipped slow workers and forced enemas to recover swallowed stones. — SOURCED (as reported), NYRB 2021.
- Later system: **SWANLA** (1943–1972) contract labor, compounds, criminal penalties for breaking contracts; abolished after the **1971–72 general strike**. — UNVERIFIED (snippets). Oshiwambo-speaking workers from the north remain a large share of the mining and fishing workforce — UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Diamonds now:** Namdeb (Namibian government + De Beers) closed the nearby **Elizabeth Bay** mine in Sept 2018 (c. 160 employees) and sold it in 2019 to a Namibian consortium; mining now mainly offshore (Debmarine). — UNVERIFIED (mining press snippets). **Diamond Act 13 of 1999** (in force 1 Apr 2000) criminalizes unlawful possession of unpolished diamonds (s. 30(11)). — UNVERIFIED (court-judgment snippets). ## 6 · #80 Old Sarum and Salisbury, Wiltshire, England

**Claim to test:** "An abandoned earlier settlement standing beside the city that replaced it (K9)."
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED.** Old Sarum (hilltop c. 3 km north of the city center) was an Iron Age fort, Roman Sorviodunum, Saxon mint town, Norman royal castle and the first cathedral city (see moved there after 1075). The bishop and clergy moved to a planned new city in the valley — New Sarum, i.e. Salisbury — with the foundation ceremony in **1220**, the bishops' tombs moved **14 June 1226** (the "formal dissolution" of the old cathedral), and a royal city charter on **30 January 1227**. Old Sarum's houses were gone by 1540; it stayed a "borough" sending **two MPs** until the **1832** Reform Act, with no resident voters. **Additions:** the abandonment was gradual (castle used to the 15th c.); the old cathedral's stone was reused; and today a new suburb named Old Sarum (on and beside a 1917 airfield) houses several thousand people next to the monument, so the "abandoned" hill again has neighbors.

### A. Snapshot
- **Salisbury:** population **41,820 (2021 census)**; 18.5 km²; at the confluence of the Avon, Nadder and Bourne; laid out on a grid in 1220 on Bishop Richard Poore's estate; unitary authority Wiltshire Council plus **Salisbury City Council** (parish-level, created 2009). — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury (read to 100,000 chars, 2026-11-21).
- **Old Sarum (monument):** c. **29 acres** of rare chalk grassland in English Heritage care; Iron Age fort c. 400 BC. — SOURCED, english-heritage.org.uk (Old Sarum history; facilities pages).
- **Defining feature:** a deserted predecessor on the hill, visible from a living cathedral city in the valley — with seven centuries of legal fiction (the rotten borough) between them.

### B. What else exists there
**Old Sarum's history and abandonment.** [K9] [P6]
- Iron Age hillfort (c. 400 BC); Roman Sorviodunum (three roads met at the east gate; two Romano-British settlements outside the ramparts; a temple precinct inside); Saxon mint by 1003. After 1066 a motte with large outer bailey; sheriffs of Wiltshire based there; keep under Henry I; Bishop Roger's courtyard house (c. 1130); Eleanor of Aquitaine held there 1173–1189. Cathedral: see moved from Sherborne after the 1075 Council of London; built under Osmund (1078–99), extended by Roger (1102–39), furnished by Jocelyn (1142–84). — SOURCED, English Heritage "History of Old Sarum."
- **Why they left:** "dissatisfaction with the site and poor relations with the castle garrison"; royal approval 1194; foundation ceremony at New Sarum 1220; tombs of Osmund, Roger and Jocelyn moved 14 June 1226, then demolition began. Edward III spent >£700 on castle repairs; administrative center into the 15th c.; 1514 Henry VIII granted the castle stones to Thomas Compton to remove; by 1540 John Leland found no houses inside or outside the walls. — SOURCED, English Heritage. Stones reused at the new cathedral — UNVERIFIED (EH snippet).
- **2014 survey** (University of Southampton: K. Strutt, T. Sly, D. Follow-up Easter 2015. — SOURCED, southampton.ac.uk news (3 Dec 2014).
- **Rotten borough:** constituency **1295–1832**, **two seats**, burgage franchise (votes attached to plots; no resident voters from at least the 17th c.; landowners nominated nonresident tenants); **eleven voters at the last election (1831)**; elections held on a mobile hustings under the "Parliament Tree" in the "electing acre" (tree died 1905); the Pitt family owned it as a pocket borough (William Pitt, later Earl of Chatham, MP 1735); sold in 1802 to the Earl of Caledon for £60,000 (£65,000 in other sources); abolished 1832 and merged into Wilton. — SOURCED, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Sarum_(UK_Parliament_constituency).
