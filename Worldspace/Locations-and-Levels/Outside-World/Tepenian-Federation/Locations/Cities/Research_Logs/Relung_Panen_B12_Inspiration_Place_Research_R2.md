### A. Other settings
- **Mirima community** — a small Aboriginal community on the edge of Kununurra; the Mirima Council was set up in **1971** under Catholic priest Peter Willis; Miriwung people occupy the southern section near the Ord River and Gajerong people the northern; incorporated as Mirima Council Aboriginal Corporation on **25 November 1986**; "Mirima Layout Plan No. 1" endorsed by the WA Planning Commission **15 April 2003**; covered by the Miriuwung–Gajerrong native-title determination (WC94/2). Population **NOT FOUND** on the page. [K4, P9] **SOURCED** — Wikipedia "Mirima community" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Neighborhood House** (7 Chestnut Avenue) — runs Parent & Baby classes, a playgroup, Young at Heart (older residents), a Makers Circle, a charity (second-hand) shop, community workshops and food relief. The town has one bank (Commonwealth Bank), two supermarkets (Coles and Tuckerbox), **no mains gas** (LPG cylinders from local hardware stores), Horizon Power and Water Corporation utilities. The wet season (November–April) brings cyclones and flooding that "potentially" isolate the town for days. [K5, K10, P4] **SOURCED** — kununurraneighbourhoodhouse.org/community.html (undated).
- **Aboriginal health clinic** — the **Ord Valley Aboriginal Health Service (OVAHS)**, incorporated **1984** as the East Kimberley Aboriginal (Medical/Health) Service; marked 30 years on **1 September 2014**; that year it became the first Aboriginal medical service in regional and remote WA with ISO 9001:2008 certification; maternal and child health, social support, specialist transport. **SOURCED** — ORIC "Where accreditation is due" (2014). ### K6 — Rules of everyday behavior (chasing Round 1's NOT FOUND)
- **History, resolved** — **SOURCED** — Codeswitch, *Takeaway Alcohol Management System (TAMS): A Review into the Effectiveness of the Trial System, January–October 2016* (October 2016), published by the Shire of Wyndham East Kimberley (PDF read in full as extracted text):
  - Section 64 of the Liquor Control Act 1988 (WA) has been used **since 1992** to impose conditions on licensees in northern regional towns.
  - **March 2009**, Kimberley-wide (Director of Liquor Licensing): takeaway sale of liquor over 6% in containers over 1 L prohibited (cask wine), and takeaway beer in glass containers over 400 ml prohibited.
  - Before that, Kununurra and Wyndham licensees had **voluntary** limits: no cask or fortified wine after 5 p.m.; no 750 ml bottled beer; no packaged liquor during funerals and special events at police request.
  - "Prior to the mandated restrictions of February 2011, there was no limit on the amount of take-away alcohol that could be purchased by an individual. Take-away alcohol was available on a Sunday and full-strength alcohol was available from 10:00am at the bar and from 12:00pm in take-away form."
  - "The restrictions were originally introduced on **7 February 2011** … modified in **November 2012**." Set by the Department of Racing, Gaming and Liquor.
  - **TAMS** (ID-scanning daily limit across all outlets) began as a 12-month trial on **Monday 14 December 2015**, introduced by the Accord; the review found people confused it with the Commonwealth **Cashless Debit Card** trial publicized from September 2015. A red screen at the counter means service is refused. **SOURCED** — same, and swek.wa.gov.au TAMS page. (The Shire's 2020 letter says TAMS had been "in place since 2014" — a discrepancy with the review's December 2015; both recorded.)
- **April 2026 — tougher limits proposed:** WA's Director of Liquor Licensing, Lainie Chopping, began a formal inquiry into Kununurra (and Newman); proposed: takeaway outlets closed **Sunday and Monday**, 7 p.m. closing, and per-person daily limits of one case of beer, two bottles of wine or one bottle of spirits; exemptions for tourists, station residents and event permits; reason given: family and domestic violence in Kununurra "58 times what it is in the metro Perth area." Shire President David Menzel: "The answers to family and domestic violence are not to do with liquor restrictions." A six-week comment period for vendors was planned. **SOURCED** — ABC News, 17 Apr 2026. Whether these were adopted: **NOT FOUND**. [K6, K9, P7, SM: man versus society]
- **Who enforces:** the state liquor regulator sets the restrictions; licensees enforce at the counter through TAMS; police are the Accord's partners. **SOURCED** (TAMS review; council letter).

## 2 · Fordlândia, Pará, Brazil — Round 2

**Main source this round (opened and read as extracted text, Portuguese):** Zâmara Elaíne Anunciata Lima, *A patrimonialização de company towns: o caso de Fordlândia e Belterra*, master's dissertation, Programa de Pós-Graduação em Arquitetura e Urbanismo, Universidade Federal do Pará, Belém, defended 30 Aug 2019 — ethnography with field visits 2014, 2017 and 2018 and interviews with residents. Cited as **[Lima 2019]**.

**What Round 1 left open and what this round settled:** present-day festivals — still **NOT FOUND** after four Portuguese searches (a parish church exists; no feast or event was found); an official population — **partly FOUND** (the *district* had 15,849 people in the 2010 census, but that is a large rural district, not the village); livelihoods — **FOUND** (fishing named as one of the main economic activities); recruitment origin — **partly FOUND** (Northeast/Ceará claim is in an opinion column only; a 1993 economic history gives 3,100 men recruited at most against 30,000 planned).

### A. Other settings
- **Named sub-areas of the old company town:** Vila Americana (managers' houses; four houses left by 2010–11), Vila Matinha (a house there extended in masonry at the back, wooden façade kept, seen 2014), and a "Vila Chapéu" named in an interview; the old hospital, the cemetery, the *grupo escolar* (school), and the fenced compound (*o cercado*) with the warehouses and machinery. Streets are steep and graveled, so some visits need transport. **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, pp. 27–28, 56].
- **Visual landmarks from the river:** the **Igreja do Sagrado Coração** (Sacred Heart church), the iron water tower and the warehouses. **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, image 6 caption]. The church is listed as a Roman-rite parish church, **Igreja Sagrado Coração de Jesus**, Metropolitan Archdiocese of Santarém. **SOURCED** — gcatholic.org (no dedication date or feast given).
- **Access in practice (field diary, 2014 and 2017):** three river options — the Santarém–Itaituba–Santarém line boat leaving each city at 4 p.m.; a smaller boat direct to the village leaving Itaituba at 11 a.m.; and a faster *lancha* on the Santarém–Itaituba line leaving at 1 p.m.; about four hours from Santarém. Road access is by the "Rodovia Transfordlândia," described as precarious, largely unpaved, and a worry in the rains. **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, pp. 29–30]. [K5]
- **Under the company (memory):** a resident who moved there in 1954 at 16 recalled street inspection of everything — no litter thrown outside, a bin or drum at each house, garbage collection — "a regime"; another (interviewed in Belterra, speaking of Fordlândia) recalled lights switched on at 6 p.m., daytime power only for the hospital, part of the commerce and the managers' village, the generator stopped at about 11 p.m. and restarted at 5 a.m., and a signal at 7 a.m. to start work, with "a lot of inspection." **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, p. 56, interviews 13 Jul 2017 and 24 Sep 2018]. [K6, K7]

### B. Subcultures
- **Elderly former company and ministry families:** residents interviewed were mostly elderly, some born under the company-town regime and/or former Ministry of Agriculture employees; they describe the present's precariousness as the absence of the past's "organization and rigor," which ended, in their account, with the transfer to the municipality of Aveiro. **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, pp. 28, 56]. [K9, P9]
- **Newcomers seeking land:** one of the people who occupied a Vila Americana house came "from another state looking for land to farm"; the rest of the community at first warned of Federal Police action, then accepted the family after it repaired the house. **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, p. 71, citing Camargos 2015]. [K4, K9]
- **Riverside fishing community:** "Fordlândia é uma comunidade ribeirinha, à margem do rio Tapajós do qual é totalmente dependente, seja para pesca uma das principais atividades econômicas ou como via de deslocamento." **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, p. 30]. [K7, P4]
- **Historical workforce origin:** Ford planned 30,000 men for the plantations and recruited at most **3,100**; the economist Francisco de Assis Costa (*Grande capital e agricultura na Amazônia: a experiência Ford no Tapajós*, UFPA, 1993) attributes this to a labor force that did not exist as a wage market; after the meals revolt the company planted **manioc** to supply food better suited to local diets. **SOURCED** — book review, *Cadernos de Ciência & Tecnologia* v.13 n.3 (1996), pp. 411–414. That the workers were mainly from Ceará/the Northeast: **UNVERIFIED** (opinion column by Xico Sá, *Diário do Nordeste*, 22 Aug 2025, which gives no source). [K7, K12, P9]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Emancipation campaign (failed):** residents campaigned to become a separate municipality; a former leader: "we fought a lot to emancipate … it was shelved in Brasília … the government vetoed and we did not emancipate." Fordlândia was a rural area of Aveiro until **Municipal Law no. 134/2017** made it a **district**, which had **15,849** inhabitants in the 2010 census (IBGE, as cited). Residents name the failed emancipation as the main cause of local decline. **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, pp. 57–58]. [K1, K9, P7]
- **Self-help heritage occupation, 2010–2011:** a retired Ministry of Agriculture employee and former community president moved into a Vila Americana house to stop termites, bats and vandals, after writing to IPHAN and the federal property office without reply; by 2010–11 all four remaining Vila Americana houses were occupied, against the mayor's and IPHAN's approval but not wholly condemned locally; occupants maintain them (cleaning, replacing timber, painting) without IPHAN guidance; the houses were in good condition in 2014. A former school director: "Many people are nostalgic and wanted the houses untouched, but they are also looking after the heritage … as long as they don't take permanent possession." **SOURCED** [Lima 2019, pp. 70–72, citing Camargos 2015]. [K1, K9, K14]
- **Federal listing denied 2024**, with courts still recognizing a duty of preservation. **UNVERIFIED** (Olhar Digital snippet; not opened). (The May 2026 court order is in Round 1.)

### A. Other settings
**Hódmezővásárhely's four former tanya centers** [K4, K9, P5, P9] — **SOURCED** [GH]
- In 1949–50 the Tanya Council marked out sites 7–10 km (never more than 15 km) from town; five centers were planned around the town — **Batida, Erzsébet, Kútvölgy, Rárós and Szikáncs** — and four were built within 2–3 years (Rárós was started and abandoned; the most common explanation, from local scholar Szenti Tibor: "people simply did not move there"). None grew into a village or commune, unlike the centers around Szeged. Each has the same plan: public buildings on a main square ringed by streets of houses. Residents worked in the cooperative and state farms.
- Each once had a well-equipped kindergarten, a school and a culture house with a cultural organizer running programs; children went to secondary school in town and mostly did not return. Workers first commuted to town factories and farmed their household plots in the afternoons and weekends, fattening pigs for extra income.
- After 1990 the cooperatives closed or were transformed; local schools and kindergartens were closed and **school buses** take children to Hódmezővásárhely; population aging; houses and land for sale. Since 1990 they are called "outer-area settlements" (*külterületi települések*).
- **Population (KSH 2005):** Szikáncs 595 people / 215 households; Erzsébet 178 / 57; Kútvölgy 170 / 71; Batida 238 / 89; plus Kishomok (a newer district that was never a tanya center) 464 / 180; town total 49,382.
- **Services in 2008** (town's Field Guard Service and Outer-Area Coordination Center survey): every center had a village hall (*faluház*), library, internet point, public catering, municipal office hours and social care; a **mobile post office** in Batida, Erzsébet and Kútvölgy (fixed in Szikáncs); health care only in Batida and Szikáncs; a **village caretaker** (*falugondnok*) in Batida, Erzsébet and Kútvölgy; no nursery or youth institution anywhere; kindergarten places 15/15/15/25 with 11/9/6/18 children enrolled.
- **Kishomok**, along road 47 toward Szeged: former tanyas whose land could not be farmed at scale were parceled into enclosed gardens in the 1960s; vines and fruit at first, then leisure gardens with tool sheds and weekend houses in the 1970s; first permanent residents in the 1980s "mainly for social reasons"; now a district of its own. **Batida**, on the road to Makó and near the new M43 motorway, saw a slight population rise as mainly professional families bought cheap plots.
- **Earlier outposts:** from **May 1892** two outer-area administrative posts (at Vásárhelykutas, today Székkutas, and Pusztaközpont near today's Kardoskút) had a town official, doctor and veterinarian come out one or two days a week to a rented tanya, followed by a gendarmerie post; closed around the First World War; re-established September 1921 (with one also at Mártély), staffed by low-ranking clerks helped by tanya teachers. Székkutas (on the Szeged–Békéscsaba railway) parceled 26 house plots around its station in 1914. Székkutas, Mártély and Kardoskút later became independent municipalities — the ones, the author notes, that the tanya population itself had called into being.

### B. Subcultures
- **Farmers vs. landless laborers — the reading-circle split (1869–1900)** [K4, K9, K10] — **SOURCED** [Bezdán]: the first "people's circle" (*népkör*) formed in Hódmezővásárhely in **1869**; close to **fifty** reading circles are traceable by 1900 (some library histories say over fifty). Peasant society was splitting: in 1894 there were 9,540 landholders, 9,510 in 1896, with 1,026 more holding under 20 *hold* within those two years. Circles divided along class lines — "1848" independence circles under peasant leadership, a young farmers' self-education society (1880s) that banned "political debates, political papers and the serving of drink" and was led by the town's two biggest taxpayers, and in the 1890s land-laborers' and poor peasants' agrarian-socialist circles; the **General Workers' Reading Society** (*Általános Munkás Olvasó Egylet*, earlier a land-laborers' trade society) was the local branch of the Social Democratic Party of Hungary, and János Szántó Kovács and his associates attended the nearby circles. In response, a Catholic Reading Society, a Reformed reading society (Belsőerzsébet) and a reorganized Liberal circle were founded. The mayor wrote that the circles were operating "more as political associations" than as reading societies; "police persecution" is recorded.

### D. Third places
- **Tanya reading circles as the outer-area meeting places** — **SOURCED** [Bezdán]: in the town's huge territory (132,260 *hold*), 21 circles formed in the 19th century, beginning with **Barackos (1884)**, whose founders wrote that its aim was reading papers and recommended books, "to awaken and spread social life," promote rational farming and keep school-leavers' reading from being forgotten; then Gorzsa and Kutas (1886), Szőrhát (1887), Mártély and Földeákszél (1889), Pusztafeketehalom (1890), Pusztakutas, Pecercés, Mártélyfeketehalom and Külsőerzsébet (1892), Kútvölgypart and Belsőerzsébet (1893), Kenyerepart and **Kopáncs (1894)**, Kardoskút and Rárós (1895), Mátyáshalom and Csicsatér (1898), Hatrongyos (1900). Most were named simply after the field-district or road. Already in **1869, 37.3%** (16,517 people) of the town lived in outer areas. The Kutas society moved in January 1886 from the school to an inner room of the roadside inn (*csárda*) for its general meeting, and in autumn 1890 held a **dance evening in a rented room on a member's tanya to raise money for its library**. Rooms were usually rented in a leader's house; the 12-member committee met regularly and general meetings were held **in the winter months**; in some outer-area circles leadership fell to "one big family." Rules said that, because papers were few and many members could not read, "an intelligent member who reads clearly shall read aloud the best and latest newspaper articles" at members' request, and that "debates arising from differences of opinion may have their place in the circle." Town circles held 100–200 volumes; tanya circles often fewer than 100. Average assets of the first four circles in 1878: 174 forints (furniture, flag, pictures, books), funded by dues, patrons and event income. [K10, K13, K14, P4]
