## 1 · #111 Yokohama and its port jazz bars

**Claim to test:** a treaty port where records and ship musicians brought jazz ashore, leaving a district of small jazz bars and a city jazz festival (K13, K3). Festival founding year to check.
**Verdict:** **Largely confirmed, with corrections.** (1) Ship musicians: confirmed — a five-man band on a Toyo Kisen liner from Yokohama (1912) bought sheet music in San Francisco and became the Hatano Orchestra, which played Japan's first commercial dance hall (Kagetsuen, Tsurumi, 1920). (2) Records arriving by ship: plausible but **only UNVERIFIED** (a single personal wiki page); the sourced record story is different — V-Discs given by a US soldier to the Chigusa jazz kissa after 1947. (3) The larger driver was not the treaty port as such but the **1945–1955 US occupation**: about 94,000 troops, ~30 clubs and soldier-only cabarets in central Yokohama, and a booking system that pulled Japanese musicians in. (4) "District of small jazz bars": confirmed for **Noge** (with Kannai/Isezakicho); city-wide ~15 jazz clubs and ~10 jazz kissa in 2025. (5) Festival: **Yokohama Jazz Promenade, founded 1993** (SOURCED, official site); annual, two days in October.

### B. What else exists there (jazz-relevant economy and institutions)
- **Occupation footprint (SOURCED):** MacArthur arrived 30 Aug 1945 and stayed at the Hotel New Grand; US Eighth Army HQ in the Customs House; ~90% of port facilities requisitioned; ~100,000 US troops arrived; enlisted men in prefabricated Quonset-type barracks in Isezakicho and Kannai; 1,000+ officer houses in Yamashita-cho, Honmoku and Negishi (35 in Yamashita Park). — Kobari Toshirō, "横浜JAZZ100年物語" ch. 2, jazzpro.jp, 2025, https://jazzpro.jp/yokohama-jazz100/jazz100/jazz100-02 . City page: city center "fenced with wire mesh, a no-entry zone for Japanese" (金網で囲まれて日本人立入禁止区域), returns gathered pace after 28 Apr 1952 — https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/city-info/yokohamashi/gaiyo/shishiryo/showa/digital-archives/saisei/comment4.html [K4, K9, P5]
- **Scale (SOURCED, academic):** Japanese estimate of **94,000 US troops in Yokohama by end-Dec 1945, ~25% of all occupation troops**, in a city of 625,000; requisitioned land **9.2 million m² by Sept 1946 = 2.3% of the city**, 40% of it in the center. Street names given by the US Army (KENTUCKY AVE., TEXAS ST.); the Kanagawa Shimbun ran ~30 instalments of an "ABC of the street" English glossary (Sept–Oct 1946). — Tōya Mamoru 東谷護, "占領期日本におけるポピュラー音楽文化", *Mixed Muses* no. 16 (Aichi Prefectural University of Fine Arts and Music), PDF via https://ai-arts.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/864/ [K4, K11, P3]
- **Return of land (SOURCED, city):** Negishi Racecourse returned 23 Nov 1969 (now Negishi Forest Park); Yokohama Enlisted Men's Club returned 30 Jun 1969; Honmoku/Shin-Honmoku beach housing District 1 agreed 1969, District 2 returned **31 Mar 1982**; Yamate housing Feb 1972. — https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/city-info/seisaku/torikumi/kichi/sesshu/yokohhama-rekishi02.html [K9]
- **Arts governance (SOURCED):** the Jazz Promenade is co-hosted by the city's Bureau of Prosperity, Sports and Culture (にぎわいスポーツ文化局), contact office its Creative City Promotion Division (創造都市推進課). 2026 dates 10–11 Oct; venues include Kannai Hall and the Red Brick Warehouse. — https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/kanko-bunka/bunka/sozotoshi/sozotoshi/coope.html [K8]
- **Yokohama Nigiwai-za** (Noge-chō): municipal hall for popular performing arts (rakugo, manzai), opened **2002**; designated-manager term 2022-04-01 to 2027-03-31 — UNVERIFIED (search snippet of city page). [K10]

### C. Cultural locations (named)
**Pre-war**
- **Kagetsuen Dance Hall** (花月園舞踏場), Tsurumi hilltop, opened **1920** (March) as an addition to Kagetsuen amusement park (1914), owned by Hiraoka Hirotaka and his wife; the city's history newsletter calls it "Japan's first" as a commercial dance hall; closed in the early Shōwa years and reopened 10 Mar 1929. The **Hatano Orchestra** played there from 1921 (6 players, later up to 25). Novelist Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (then living in Honmoku) was a regular. — jazzpro ch. 0 https://jazzpro.jp/yokohama-jazz100/jazz100/jazz100-00 ; *市史通信* no. 10 (Yokohama City History Archives), "横浜のダンスホール", PDF https://www.city.yokohama.lg.jp/city-info/yokohamashi/gaiyo/shishiryo/jyohoshi/1-25.files/0174_20180918.pdf (SOURCED, read locally) [K13]
- **Kiraku-za** (喜樂座), Isezaki-chō: the magician Shōkyokusai Tenkatsu's troupe opened 1 Jul 1925 with a jazz band accompanying dancers; this date is the basis of the 2025 "Yokohama Jazz 100" celebration (research of the late Shibata Kōichi). — jazzpro ch. 0, SOURCED. [K3, K13]
- **Grand Hotel** (Yamashita-chō; predecessor of the Hotel New Grand): a 1922 *New York Times* travel account (Burnet Hershey, 25 Jun 1922) said jazz could be heard within hours of arrival and Yokohama had about half a dozen jazz bands. — jazzpro ch. 0, SOURCED (secondhand via that page). **Hotel New Grand** opened Dec 1927 as an earthquake-recovery symbol, architect Watanabe Jin — UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Yamashita-chō dance halls, 1928–1932 (SOURCED, 市史通信 no. 10 table):** Yokohama Butō Kenkyūjo (Yamashita-chō 123, c. Oct 1928), **Union** (Yamashita-chō 88→83→82, 15 Nov 1928; closed 20 Mar 1930), **Rotary** (Yamashita-chō 86, 25 Feb 1929), **Metropolitan** (Aioi-chō 2-44 → Benten-dōri 1-2, Feb 1929; licensed Apr 1930), **Cherry** (Kaigan-dōri 3-12, c. Feb 1929), Gekkōpen (closed by police almost at once, Nov 1929), Caravan, **Napoli**, Carlton, Kei, **Bluebird**, **Taiheiyō (Pacific)**, Yokohama Dance Hall (Dec 1930), Social (1931), Pharos Dancing Palace (Motomachi), Platanus, Mankarō (Tsurumi). Halls began as "research societies" (研究会) teaching dance, then turned into commercial halls where paid dancers partnered customers by ticket. [K13, K4]
- **Drinking houses peculiar to Yokohama** (the source names the type by its local term; the term is filed in the slang file, not here): houses that served alcohol where women partnered guests in dancing. Dancing in bars and cafés was banned and occasionally raided. SOURCED (市史通信 no. 10). [K6, K4]

**Occupation era (1945–55)**
- **Clubs and soldier-only cabarets, ~30 in central Yokohama** (Tōya, SOURCED). Camp Yokohama clubs: **Zebra Club** (NCO), **Seaside Club** (NCO), **Club 45** (EM), **Crossroads** (NCO), **Golden Dragon** (Officers). Also Olympic (cabaret, Tokiwa-chō), **Sakura Port** (EM club below Momijizaka; housed a booking office that supplied Yokohama's clubs), New Yorker and 400 Club, Seamen's Club and Colonial Club (Yamashita-chō). — Tōya; jazzpro ch. 2. [K4, K13]
- **Zebra Club (SOURCED, Tōya):** the US Army requisitioned the Bank of Tokyo building opposite Kaga-chō police station (Yamashita-chō 176) on 16 Feb 1946 and opened it about three months later as an NCO club; returned 9 Sep 1955, then moved to the site of today's Kanagawa Kenmin Hall. Until the move it was off-limits to Japanese **and to Black soldiers**. Open 11:00 to ~23:00. All staff were Japanese: interpreters, accounting, bartenders, cooks, waiters, boilermen, cloakroom, cashiers, janitors, guards, drivers; staff underwent background and ideological checks. Name said to come from the NCO chevrons' zebra-like stripes. Military scrip used at first (tickets bought at the cashier). [K4, K6, K7, K9]
- **Cliffside** (クリフサイド), Motomachi: one of three large late-1950s nightclubs (with Blue Sky and Night and Day), "still exists" — jazzpro ch. 5, SOURCED. Opened 1946 as "Yamate Butōjō", up to ~200 dancers at its peak — UNVERIFIED (Kanagawa Shimbun snippet; page refused).
- **Mocambo** (モカンボ), Isezaki-chō 2-chōme: three after-hours jam sessions (11 p.m. to morning), organized by guitarist Ueki Hitoshi, drummer Hana Hajime and guitarist Sawada Shungo; 500-yen fee covering the room; the last, 27–28 Jul 1954, recorded and issued as *幻のモカンボ・セッション'54*. Pianist Moriyasu Shōtarō died in Sept 1955. — jazzpro ch. 4, SOURCED. [K13, K7]
- Other late-1950s rooms: **Wals** (Isezaki-chō 1-chōme), **Gogo** (behind Yūrindō bookstore; from 1959 the Cozy Quartet led by Watanabe Sadao), **Tris Club** (Chōja-machi). — jazzpro ch. 5, SOURCED.

**Jazz kissa and bars (today and recent)**
- **Chigusa** (ちぐさ), Noge: opened **1933** by Yoshida Mamoru; ~6,000 SP records by 1942; under the January 1943 ban he surrendered ~500 and hid the rest; site burned in the war; reopened **1947** after his return from China (1948 in one TV listing — conflict noted); started again with ~1,000 SPs and ~300 V-Discs from a US soldier; served as "hangout and contact office" for musicians working the US clubs; Akiyoshi Toshiko transcribed records there 1954–56. Yoshida died 1994; family/regulars ran it to **2007**; reopened about five years later with regulars' help; closed its building **10 Apr 2022** (narrow, earthquake-unsafe) for a planned "Jazz Museum Chigusa" (~¥100 million, fund-raised). **Status (SOURCED, 20 Dec 2024 notice):** first construction phase done, but "negotiations with the landowner have not concluded"; 3,000+ LPs stored in a Noge warehouse, moved to a Kannai space run by "Music Chronicle Yokohama · Club Save Chigusa"; the operating body is 一般社団法人ジャズ喫茶ちぐさ・吉田衛記念館. — jazzpro ch. 1, 2, 4; https://www.noge-chigusa.com/?p=1771 ; TV Tokyo listing (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K13, K9, K14]
- **Downbeat** (ダウンビート), Hanasaki-chō 1-43, Miyamoto Bldg 2F (Noge side): opened **1956** in Wakaba-chō, moved to Noge after ~10 years; founder was a bassist in a jazz band for the occupation forces (d. 1995); current owner the third generation (Yoshihisa Shūhei, 40s, a regular of ~20 years before taking over); ~3,500 records (+~1,500 added); Altec speaker; open 16:00–24:00, closed Mondays, no cover charge. — Sanpo no Tatsujin, 2026, https://san-tatsu.jp/articles/546884/ and /546886/ (SOURCED; part 1 calls the owner "second generation", part 2 "third" — part 2 is the interview and is taken as correct). [K13, K14]
- **dolphy**, **Noge Junk** (Noge), **Bar Bar Bar** (Kannai; reported closed for relocation 2023 — UNVERIFIED), **Windjammer** (Yamashita-chō 215; ship-cabin interior; nightly live jazz, 5 sets, live charge ¥600–800, no under-20s — SOURCED rurubu; founded 1972 — UNVERIFIED), **Motion Blue Yokohama** (Red Brick Warehouse No. 2, 3F; closed 2022 — UNVERIFIED), **Golden Cup** (Honmoku-dōri; opened **1964** in the US-requisitioned zone — UNVERIFIED, Walker Plus page timed out; house band became The Golden Cups, formed Nov 1966, influenced by the Far East Network broadcasts from the Honmoku base — SOURCED, Wikipedia).

**Festivals and other**
- **Yokohama Jazz Promenade** (横濱 JAZZ PROMENADE): founded **1993**, slogan "街全体をステージに" (the whole city as a stage); two days each October; >100,000 visitors a year; formats: hall concerts, club shows, free street-corner stages (mostly amateurs), satellite events, and a **jazz competition** (held 17 times since 1993). Run by an executive committee of 11 bodies: the Honmoku, Asahi and Kōnan local jazz festival committees, the Chamber of Commerce and Industry, Yokohama JC, the Convention & Visitors Bureau, the International Association, KAAT, the Kanazawa Hall operator, the Yokohama JAZZ Association and the Yokohama Arts Foundation; ~300 public volunteers ("Jazz Crew") a year. Its stated policy is to stage as many Japanese artists as possible rather than rely on famous US names; ~3,000 professional musicians have appeared. — https://jazzpro.jp/about ; jazzpro ch. 6 (SOURCED). 2025: 11–12 Oct, ~30 venues (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K3, K8, K14]
- **Noge Daidōgei** (野毛大道芸, street-performance festival): begun **1986** by local shopkeepers to revive Noge after the shipyard moved in **1983** and customers fell sharply; four stages on Noge Hondōri; 40th edition 18–19 Apr 2026 with 40 groups; run by an executive committee of shopkeepers and youth-association members. Samba team Escola de Samba Saudade formed in Noge in 1986. — Sanpo no Tatsujin, 2026-04-10, https://san-tatsu.jp/news/517149/ (SOURCED). [K3, K9]
- **Minato Mirai Super Big Band:** junior big band of middle- and high-school students, formed **2013** at Yokohama Minato Mirai Hall with support from Nettai Jazz Gakudan; weekly practice (47 sessions in FY2025); 53 members in FY2025; plays the Jazz Promenade; alumni have turned professional; organized by the Arts Foundation with the Yokohama JAZZ Association, funded by an Agency for Cultural Affairs grant. — FY2025 report, https://yokohama-minatomiraihall.jp/static/file/archive/houkoku_sbb_2025.pdf (SOURCED, read locally). [K14, K8]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **The liner route** gave the first trained jazz band (1912 ship band → Hatano Orchestra → Kagetsuen 1920) and the foreign clientele that made Kanagawa prefecture license dance halls more leniently "in consideration of attracting foreigners" (SOURCED, 市史通信 no. 10). [K13, D]
- **The occupation** created mass demand that outran supply: big bands and combos nightly; ~500 US clubs nationwide at peak; ex-military-band players switched to jazz because their brass-heavy line-ups matched the big-band format; students and amateurs, even non-players, entered "because it paid"; "even the cheap ones got double a normal salary" (army bandsman Takazawa Tomomasa). Payment was at first in food and cigarettes; later the Japanese government paid fees through the Central Liaison Office; ~170 booking agencies by the late occupation. — Tōya (SOURCED). [K7, K8, K14, SM:competence shown under pressure]
- Musicians' own accounts: Hara Nobuo (b. 1926): "In those days Yokohama was the mecca of jazz"; he attributed bebop's early hold to the many Black soldiers who wanted jazz-style playing, versus Tokyo clubs that wanted "elegant dance music" (jazzpro ch. 3/4, SOURCED). [K13]
- **Jazz kissa** existed because imported LPs cost ¥2,500–3,000 against monthly salaries of ~¥20,000 (late 1950s); "jazz kissa was truly a place to study jazz" (jazzpro ch. 5, SOURCED). [K13, K14]
- After 1952 the clubs closed with the land returns; musicians moved to bases elsewhere; late-1950s Yokohama "sparked the spread of jazz clubs offering regular live performance" (jazzpro ch. 5). [K9, K13]

### E. Structural features
- **K6 — rules of everyday behavior (found):**
  - *Kanagawa Dance Hall and Dancer Regulation* (舞踏場及舞踏手取締規則), prefectural ordinance promulgated **21 Jan 1930**: governor's permit required; location and building restrictions; limits on sharing a building with restaurants or inns; **students and minors barred; no drinking; dancer licensing**. Considered more lenient than Tokyo's rules of Nov 1928 "for attracting foreigners", but **business limited to the Kannai district**. Entrance: ¥1 weekdays, ¥2 Sundays, women half price. Police lectured Metropolitan dancers on morals. — 市史通信 no. 10 (SOURCED).
  - National: Home Ministry ordered **all dance halls closed 31 Oct 1940**; after Dec 1941 jazz labeled "enemy music"; **January 1943** jazz record performance banned and records surrendered for destruction; saxophones, brass mutes and English notation banned (jazzpro ch. 1, SOURCED).
  - Occupation: US clubs were **off-limits to Japanese** except staff, performers, brokers and guests brought by soldiers; there were also **Black-soldier-only clubs**, and the Zebra Club excluded Black soldiers until 1955 (Tōya, SOURCED).
  - Jazz kissa etiquette (general, Japan): listening in silence; 1960s style of dim light, very loud sound, talking banned; the master chooses records, requests mostly from regulars; one LP side played in full; patrons come alone or in pairs — Wikipedia "Jazz kissa" citing Atkins 2001, Novak 2008, Derschmidt 1998 (SOURCED, secondary). In Yokohama today: Chigusa — music first, "not a place to discuss business", soft voices (Metropolis 2015, SOURCED); Downbeat's owner talks only when asked, keeps volume loud, and steps in when a customer lectures others about what "real jazz" is (SOURCED). Windjammer: no under-20s (SOURCED).
- **K1 — shared-problem institutions:** dancers' labor union "Yokohama United Dancers' Self-Governing Association" (横浜連合ダンサー自治会) formed **20 Jun 1931** (mutual aid and "improvement of character"); owners' **Yokohama Dance Hall Association** formed Apr 1931 to "avoid useless conflict among operators" and "purify dancers' morals"; **strike** of 20 Metropolitan dancers on 17 Jan 1931 won 7 of 12 demands; disputes at Yokohama Dance Hall (July) and Carlton (Aug) followed. Teachers' body Imperial Social Dance Teachers' Association formed July 1931. — 市史通信 no. 10 (SOURCED). Modern: Noge's 1984 groups "野毛文化を育てる会" and "野毛地区まちづくりを考える会" (UNVERIFIED snippet of a city journal). [K1, K9, K14]
- **K7 — work rhythms:** clubs 11:00–23:00; after-hours jam sessions 23:00–dawn; individual brokers (known by a colloquial job name, filed in the slang file) waited at terminal stations (Tokyo, Shinjuku) where club trucks collected musicians, picked that day's players and formed an instant band — "in effect, recruiting day laborers" (Tōya, SOURCED). [K7, K14]
- **K8 — money:** 1954: each member of George Kawaguchi's Big Four reportedly earned ~¥400,000/month vs an average salary of ~¥17,000 (jazzpro ch. 3, SOURCED). Noge clubs today are small family businesses that "earn little money"; musicians often play for almost nothing; most rooms have no PA (Japan Travel, 2024-10-16, SOURCED). Jazz Promenade: city co-host, Arts Foundation, local business and railway supporters; budget NOT FOUND.
- **K9 — friction and loss:** May 1945 air raid; ~2,000 days of occupation; land returns stretched to 1982; shipyard move 1983 emptied Noge's bars; Chigusa's 2007 and 2022 closures and stalled museum. Occupation-era bandsmen: young (mid-teens to mid-20s), better paid than their peers, some fell into gambling and drugs (Tōya); stimulants were quietly sold at the Mocambo sessions (jazzpro ch. 4). [K9]
- **K10:** Noge's 600+ bars and eateries (postwar black-market origin) form the year-round base; the jazz rooms sit inside this drinking district (Sanpo no Tatsujin 2026, SOURCED).
- **K11 (loose):** Japanese; in the occupation years English signage and US Army street names dominated the requisitioned center (Tōya, SOURCED).
- **K12:** Noge standing bars and yakitori (general, Japan Travel); Windjammer's 200+ cocktails; hotel-born dishes (seafood doria, spaghetti Napolitan, pudding à la mode) at the Hotel New Grand — UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **K14:** occupation-era players learned from the "Hit Kit" song folios, pirated "1001" fake books and stock arrangements, lent among bandsmen or transcribed from records and radio (Tōya, SOURCED). [R] — swap-and-copy culture of scarce scores and records.

## 2 · #112 Shanghai's ballroom and jazz era (1920s–1930s)

**Claim to test:** a many-origin port whose large ballrooms and hotel bands made it the jazz center of its region, with fame carried by sailors and travelers (K13, K4). Context: foreign concessions and inequality, reported plainly.
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with three corrections.** (1) Scale: by 1936 "over three hundred cabarets and dancehalls of various sizes" (Wakeman 1995, cited in Chen 2007; SOURCED secondhand); 1933, ~175 cabarets and ~5,000 paid dancing partners (an operator's estimate, China Press). (2) The bands were overwhelmingly **foreign contract labor** — Filipino above all, then White Russian, American (white and Black), with Chinese bands entering only from 1934 and still a minority; the ballrooms ranged from elite hotel rooms to **taxi-dance halls** where the business was the paid female partner, not the band. (3) "Fame carried by sailors and travelers" is supported: tourists were "the greatest patrons", spending peaked "when the United States fleet is in town", and waterfront cabarets held "sailors from the ships of a dozen nations" (1933 and 1937 press; SOURCED). The era ran past the 1930s: wartime enclave boom 1937–41, Japanese control 1941–45, the 1947–48 ban crisis and dancers' riot, and final closure in **November 1954** (UNVERIFIED snippet of a China Quarterly article).

### A. Snapshot
- **International Settlement:** formed 1863 by merging British and American concessions; the French Concession remained separate. Area 22.59 km² (1925); population 501,561 (1910), 1,137,298 (1925); 1,040,780 Chinese residents (1932); ~400,000 more fled in after August 1937. Land legally "remains Chinese territory" (Land Regulations art. 28). Japan overtook Britain as the largest foreign group in 1915. — Wikipedia "Shanghai International Settlement" (SOURCED, secondary; article flagged for thin citations; first 100,000 characters read). [P5]
- **Extraterritoriality:** citizens of treaty powers tried in their own consular courts; Mixed Court (1864–1926) handled cases against Chinese defendants with foreign assessors; Provisional Court 1927; Chinese Special Courts 1930. Shanghai Municipal Council elected by ratepayers (~3% of residents eligible); no Chinese councillors until 1928 (3 seats), 5 from 1930; early-1930s seats: British 5, Chinese 5, Japanese 2, American and others 2. Shanghai Municipal Police; Shanghai Volunteer Corps (萬國商團); British battalion and US Marines after 1927. — same (SOURCED, secondary). [K9, K6]
- Whole-city population and a 1935 census total: **NOT FOUND** in a readable primary (Virtual Shanghai holds the tables as spreadsheets; original 1935 census forms are lost).
- **Defining feature:** an enclave economy of hotels, department stores and entertainment that imported both its musicians and, at first, its dancing partners, then localized both.

### B. What else exists there (relevant economy and people)
- **Who played (SOURCED):** "fifty to sixty places, entertainment establishments, most of which are part of hotels... hardly any Chinese jazz musicians"; bands of 5–6 for small rooms, 10–15 for large (bassist Da Ren Zheng, quoted in Chaichana 2024). Contract musicians were "American, Austrian, Gypsy, Jewish, Russian, and Filipino." — Tanarat Chaichana, "The Sinicization of Jazz", *Humanities, Arts and Social Sciences Studies* 24(1), 2024, pp. 256–263, PDF https://so02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/hasss/article/view/265028 (read locally). [K4, K14]
- **Filipino musicians (SOURCED):** a 1928 *China Weekly Review* article (5 May 1928) said cabaret music came from "hard-working Filipinos who have a monopoly of modern music". — reprinted on Andrew David Field, *Shanghai Sojourns*, 2018-07-12. Filipino bandsmen came "of their own accord", trained in jazz under US colonial rule; White Russian classical musicians, refugees of the revolution, came "unwillingly" and some played cabarets only to earn a living (Szu-Wei Chen, *The Music Industry and Popular Song in 1930s and 1940s Shanghai*, PhD, University of Stirling, 2007, https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/202/1/shanghai.pdf , read locally). Shanghai Municipal Orchestra began recruiting Filipinos in 1878 because they cost less than Europeans; majority Filipino by the mid-1920s — UNVERIFIED (snippet of a Cambridge chapter; page refused). Filipino musicians moved to Hong Kong after 1949 (e.g., trumpeter Silverio Yaneza, bandleader Lobing Samson) — UNVERIFIED (snippets; ABS-CBN page refused). [K4, K14, K9]
- **White Russians:** ~25,000 émigrés in Shanghai (UNVERIFIED snippet); Russians "more than half" of the Municipal Orchestra by the 1920s (UNVERIFIED snippet). Oleg Lundstrem's band (formed in Harbin 1935 with eight young Russian amateurs) came to Shanghai 1936 and played there until 1947, when Lundstrem returned to the USSR (Wikipedia, SOURCED). [K4]
- **American Black musicians (SOURCED, China Press 7 Nov 1934, "Shanghai Show World" by Max Chaichek, reprinted Shanghai Sojourns 2018-07-14):** Frank Augustine led the first Black band in Shanghai; Jack Carter's orchestra; pianist Teddy Weatherford, brought from Java to the Canidrome by Hal P. Mills (his band of "negroes, Filipinos and Hawaiians"); Buck Clayton's 14-man band at the Canidrome (1934); Earl Whaley's 8-man band at a Chinese-owned cabaret on Bubbling Well Road; Jimmy Carson at Lafayette Garden. [K13]
- **Whitey Smith** (drummer, US): came 1922, citing better pay than in the US and Prohibition; led the Majestic Hotel band (400 people queued for its Sunday afternoon dance); played the 1927 wedding of Chiang Kai-shek; left 1937 with the war (Chaichana 2024 citing Smith's memoir; SOURCED). [K13, K7]
- **Record and song industry:** Li Jinhui (b. 1891, Xiangtan) founded the Chinese Song and Dance Institute (1927), toured Southeast Asia; songs recorded by Pathé-EMI and Great China Records ("Drizzle" 1927, "Express Train" 1928) (Chaichana 2024, SOURCED; Li's death year given there as 1967, elsewhere 1968 — conflict). ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Imported labor, local hierarchy:** high-class rooms served Westerners and the Western-educated Chinese elite; Clayton: "Poor people couldn't even look in the Canidrome" (Chaichana citing Clayton 1986, SOURCED). A 1934–35 guidebook graded cabarets "high class, low class and no class" — in the first, patrons brought their own partners; in the second they paid hostesses (Chen 2007 footnote, SOURCED secondhand). [K4, K6]
- **Racism at work (SOURCED):** Buck Clayton's band lost its Canidrome contract after a white patron insulted and punched Clayton (his memoir, quoted in Chaichana 2024); *That's* (2021) names the assailant as the slot-machine operator Jack Riley. [K9]
- **Localization as a cost and audience strategy (SOURCED, Chen 2007):** in **1934** the gangster Du Yuesheng commissioned Li Jinhui to form an all-Chinese band for the new Yangzi Hotel dancehall — tall northern Chinese "so that they would not be mistaken for Filipinos"; it filled the room nightly; because Chinese bands were cheaper, other halls followed, Li supplying players and music free. **1936:** the Three Surplus Cantonese Music Club began a residency at the Lunar Palace; **1942:** bandleader Chen He replaced the huqin with accordion, violin, piano, guitar, bass and drums, and other halls made their Cantonese bands do the same. A dancehall using a gramophone instead of a band "would be considered inferior". [K13, K8]
- **Hostess labor replaced the banquet singer (SOURCED, *China Weekly Review* 1928):** Russian refugees introduced the paid-partner cabaret; "more than 1,000 Russian dancing girls" at one time, earning $200–500/month from tickets and commissions on wine; by 1928 Chinese hostesses were displacing them; at least two cabarets used only Japanese dancers and a Japanese orchestra. 1933 estimate: ~5,000 partners, >60% Russian, ~30% Japanese, ~10% Chinese (operator John H. Browning, *China Press* 11 Jun 1933; Field doubts the percentages). 1936: ~3,000 licensed Chinese dancers and 300 foreign (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K4, K7, K8]
- **Sailors and tourists (SOURCED):** Browning, 1933: tourists "spend much more than the local residents"; Americans spend most, sailors slightly more than Marines; "When the United States fleet is in town, money flows freely"; drinks: U.B. beer, sloe gin, rickeys, gin fizzes, whisky; average hostess ~25 dances a night, up to ~100 at peak. Jim Marshall, *Collier's*, 10 Jul 1937: tickets "three for a Shanghai dollar" at the Santa Ana; a popular dancer earned $100–200 a week but paid for about three new gowns a week and a maid; Venus held Japanese cruiser sailors, US destroyer crews, French, German and British sailors. [K12, K8, SM: a dangerous or fascinating figure or system]

### E. Structural features
- **K6 — rules (found):** dog racing barred in the International Settlement but allowed in the French Concession (SOURCED); Japanese-licensed cabarets in Hongkew/Yangtszepoo kept Japanese closing hours, not the Council's (US consular dispatch 1933, UNVERIFIED snippet, history.state.gov); Chinese and Japanese governments tried to ban dance halls in the late 1920s–30s; the New Life Movement (mid-1930s) targeted them as vice; they survived "protected by the city's international zones" (Field, *Intersections* 31, Dec 2012, SOURCED). After the Japanese took the whole city (Dec 1941 / 1942), Hollywood songs and big-band numbers were withdrawn as enemy products (Chen 2007, SOURCED). **National ban:** Executive Yuan austerity measures (July 1947) prohibited commercial dance halls, deadline 1 Oct 1947 (*Time*, 22 Sep 1947, SOURCED); Shanghai negotiated closure by lottery in batches; the ban was withdrawn 31 Mar 1948 (The Paper, 2021-11-22, SOURCED). Post-1949: the songs of the jazz-Chinese fusion were condemned as "decadent sounds" and banned; dance halls finally banned Nov 1954 (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K6]
- **K1 / K9 — the 1948 dancers' riot (SOURCED, The Paper 2021-11-22, "四马路" column; *Shanghai Women's Gazetteer*):** 1946: 29 dance halls (MGM, Ciro's, Paramount, Vienna, Metropole, Lido, New Fairy Forest, Yangzi and others), ~3,000 dancers (registered 3,300+ per a 1946 Social Bureau count, UNVERIFIED; *Time* 1947 estimated 20,000 "dancing girls" — figures conflict). Autumn 1947: a Dancers' Friendship Association formed under the Shanghai Women's Association, led by Jin Meihong and Sun Zhimin, offering sewing, cooking, typing, bookkeeping and embroidery classes (500+ enrolled). **31 Jan 1948:** dancers, musicians and staff met at the New Fairy Forest; the Social Bureau drew lots early (14 top halls, including the Paramount, to close first); ~6,000 people stormed the Social Bureau on Linsen Road; armored police units; **797 arrested** (gazetteer; "700+" in The Paper); Jin Meihong sentenced to 3 months, suspended 2 years; the Friendship Association was shut. The Dance Hall Trade Association's chair argued dancers "have no other skills". Entertainment tax on dance halls once 50%, later 25%. Dec 1949: police counted 1,422 dancers (gazetteer). [K1, K9, K8, SM: man versus society]
- **K7 / K8 — pay system:** dancers paid by tickets, one per dance; generally **70% handed over, 30% kept**; the hall's supervisor of dancers collected the tickets for the owner (gazetteer, SOURCED). Field gives "half the revenues of the dance tickets" (SOURCED) — conflict noted. Wartime refugee influx after Aug 1937 lowered entry barriers and ticket prices (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **K9 — war:** **14 Aug 1937** bombs aimed at the Japanese cruiser *Izumo* fell on Nanking Road between the Cathay and Palace hotels and on crowds outside the Great World amusement center; "approximately 2,000" killed (Bristol Historical Photographs of China, Hn-n11, SOURCED); ~1,200 dead per other accounts (UNVERIFIED). A taxi-dancer, Chen Manli, was shot dead on a dance floor after refusing a Japanese occupier (*That's Shanghai* 2015, SOURCED). After 1949 many foreign musicians left; some local players went to Taiwan, Hong Kong and the West (Chaichana, SOURCED).
- **K10:** the dance halls themselves were the city's third places across classes, from department-store halls to waterfront alleys; elite hotel ballrooms survive as fixed spaces (Field 2019).
- **K11 (loose):** English, Shanghainese and other Chinese varieties, Russian, Japanese, Tagalog/English among Filipino musicians; signage and press in English and Chinese.

## 3 · #41 Hofbräuhaus, Munich

**Claim to test:** a single enormous beer hall famous far beyond its city, with long shared tables and a stage (K10, K12).

## 4 · #42 Australian pub rock and the suburban "beer barns" (1970s–1980s)

*(The pick's own label uses a colloquial term; in this section the venues are called "large suburban hotels". The term and other nicknames found are filed in the slang file.)*

**Claim to test:** very large pubs built to hold big crowds for loud live rock, which shaped a whole national sound (K13, K8). Care: find which venues are best documented, and their capacities.
**Verdict:** **Confirmed in structure, corrected in cause.** (1) The large hotels were not purpose-built for rock: they were built in the late 1960s as **mass drinking venues** — e.g., the Matthew Flinders Hotel, Chadstone (opened 8 Apr 1968 by Commodore Motels and Carlton & United Breweries on the edge of a "dry" district, main bar ~1,000 + Flagship Room 500, beer piped to 40 outlets). **Bands came after**, once 1960s licensing reforms (10 p.m. closing in Victoria from Feb 1966; women in public bars; Sunday trading; drinking age 18) and baby-boomer numbers made free live music a way to fill them. (2) Best-documented capacities: Bombay Rock, Brunswick (upstairs room "over 1,000"); Matthew Flinders (~1,000 + 500); Selina's, Coogee Bay Hotel (1,750 per one source; 2,000–2,500 per Time Out). (3) The "sound" link is reported by the music press and Wikipedia (loud, simple, riff-based rock suited hot, crowded rooms) — a plausible, widely repeated claim, not a measured one. (4) Decline: random breath testing (Victoria 1976, NSW Dec 1982 — UNVERIFIED snippets), poker machines in hotels (Victoria 1992, NSW reforms 1996–97 — partly SOURCED), fire and building rules, rising costs.

### A. Snapshot
- **What:** Pub rock — loud, hard-edged rock played in inner-city and suburban hotels, peaking in the 1970s–1980s; before the 1970s most live pop was staged in unlicensed halls as all-ages, alcohol-free events; in the 1960s states repealed Sunday-observance rules, extended hours and lifted the ban on women in public bars; in the 1970s the age of majority fell from 21 to 18; "pub owners soon realized that providing live music (which was often free) would draw young people to pubs in large numbers." — Wikipedia "Pub rock (Australia)" (SOURCED, secondary; history section tagged as under-cited). [K13, K8]
- **Peak:** about 1978–1988 (Sarah Taylor, RMIT, *Cordite Poetry Review*, 1 Feb 2015, SOURCED); "golden age" mid-1979 to mid-1983 per a former band manager (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Licensing timeline, Victoria (SOURCED, State Library Victoria "Pubs and publicans" guide):** 1916 six o'clock closing and Licensing Court; **1966 hotel hours extended to 10 p.m.**; 1968 Liquor Control Act (Liquor Control Commission replaces licensing courts); 1986 Nieuwenhuysen Report ("restrictive and outdated"); 1988 Liquor Control Act 1987 in force; 1991 Gaming Machine Control Act; 2012 live music made an objective of liquor law for the first time; 2015 late-night ban on small (≤200) venues lifted. NSW ended six o'clock closing on 1 Feb 1955 (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K6, K1]

### B. What else exists there (the venues and their economics)
- **Matthew Flinders Hotel, Chadstone (SOURCED, Sarah Craze, 23 Mar 2023):** corner of Batesford and Warrigal Roads; opened 8 Apr 1968; CUB owned land and buildings, Commodore held the license (lease-back), sold its stake 1970; "one of several large" such hotels built around Melbourne; **sited on the edge of the dry area around Ashburton, Ashwood and Chadstone, "no rival pub for miles"**; main bar ~1,000, Flagship Room 500; beer piped ~7 miles to 40 outlets; within a few years ~250 18-gallon kegs and 3,000 dozen bottles a week, incl. a drive-through; ~$54,000–57,000 a year in license fees; 1970s bands (The Angels, Cold Chisel; AC/DC Jan 1975); underage drinking tolerated, failing building inspections (seating, exit signs, inward-opening doors), frequent fights, notorious door staff (a 1974 lawsuit alleged a broken ankle), motorcycle gangs; 1986 new owners, ~$11 m renovation, nightclub; **poker machines from 6 Aug 1992 (about 400 queued by noon)**; machines cut from 80 to 46 under 2007 restrictions; part of the site became a liquor superstore. [K8, K9, K6]
- **Melbourne's dry zones (SOURCED, Dr Elizabeth Taylor, RMIT News, 23 May 2018):** created by the October **1920 local option poll** (≥60% "no license" closed every hotel in a district): 2 districts voted no-license, 71 reduction, 143 continuance; earlier the License Reduction Board had closed 1,300+ hotels; compulsory ballots for restaurant/café licenses in Melbourne's two dry zones removed in 2015. (Which districts: Boroondara and Nunawading — UNVERIFIED, 1930 NZ press snippets.) [K1, K6]
- **Bombay Rock (SOURCED, Ian McFarlane, *Rhythms* #289, reprinted Third Stone Press 2018-10-15):** late 1977 at 287 Bourke Street (CBD); relaunched 10–11 Mar 1978 in a former Greek nightclub at Sydney Road and Phoenix Street, Brunswick; promoter Joseph Gaultieri ran the door, Greek owners the bar; upstairs room "over 1,000", smaller downstairs room for lesser-known acts; billed as an "international 3AM rock 'n' roll club"; almost every major Australian act within a year, plus touring overseas bands; live albums recorded there; wound up late 1983 after a dispute over renovations. [K13, K7]
- **Croxton Park Hotel, Thornbury (SOURCED, Darebin Libraries heritage page):** licensed 1844 as the Pilgrim Inn (Plenty Road), rebuilt 1897; **by 1979 live bands seven nights a week and "the highest beer consumption of any Victorian hotel"**, slipping to no. 3 in alcohol sales in the early 1980s; sold in 1979 for "the 22nd time"; licensing history includes an 1875 charge over dancing (ruled a licensing matter) and a 1936 fine for an open bar door after hours. Band room renovated and relaunched Oct 2015 (Tone Deaf, SOURCED). Other Melbourne large suburban hotels named: South Side Six (Moorabbin), Village Green (Mulgrave), Doncaster Inn, Pier Hotel (Frankston), Whitehorse Inn, Waltzing Matilda (UNVERIFIED snippets); Station Hotel, Prahran (Wikipedia, SOURCED). - **Sydney:** **Selina's, Coogee Bay Hotel** — 1,750 capacity (UNVERIFIED snippet) or 2,000–2,500 (Time Out Sydney, 1 Sep 2021, SOURCED); "came to epitomise the pub rock era" (former band manager quoted); not a regular concert venue since 2009 (UNVERIFIED); demolition and a ~$111–112 m redevelopment proposed 2021, approved 2024 (UNVERIFIED). **Royal Antler, Narrabeen** — renamed from Royal Narrabeen Hotel in 1963; bands drew people north of the Harbor Bridge in the 1970s; lease bought 1985, renovated, renamed Narrabeen Sands (Pittwater Online News, 2022, SOURCED); ~170 known gigs (Australian Music Database, UNVERIFIED). **Rydalmere Family Inn** — Cold Chisel, winter 1980: poor acoustics, rowdy crowd (VICE, Vincent Dwyer, 25 Sep 2017, SOURCED). **Comb and Cutter, Blacktown** — ~160–170 listed gigs 1978–83 (UNVERIFIED). Sydney's large suburban venues sat in a ring: Caringbah, Blacktown, Mona Vale, Parramatta, Dee Why; Melbourne's in Frankston, Ferntree Gully, Mulgrave, Essendon, Doncaster, Armadale; "large-capacity (or, at least, very crowded, since fire regulations were often flouted)" (Taylor, *Cordite* 2015, SOURCED). **Bondi Lifesaver** (Bondi Junction, closed 31 Aug 1980) was a small specialist room, not a large hotel (Colin Baldwin, SOURCED). - **Newcastle — Star Hotel (SOURCED, Wikipedia "Star Hotel riot", citing Newcastle Herald and SMH):** owned by Tooth and Co. since 1934; popular with visiting seamen; back bar open to gay and lesbian patrons from the late 1960s with drag shows; in the 1970s a venue for young people, students and local bands; in 1979 the licensee banned gay patrons, prompting a picket. [K4, K9]
- **Adelaide — Largs Pier Hotel:** opened 1882, drive-through bottle shop from 1953 (Wikipedia, UNVERIFIED snippet); a former lifesaver recalls that **lifesaving clubs booked the interstate bands, kept the door and paid bands and bouncers, while the pub kept the bar** (UNVERIFIED, single recollection; page under maintenance). ## 5 · #43 The British folk club movement (pub back rooms)

**Claim to test:** acoustic folk nights held in rooms of ordinary pubs, run by volunteers and open to any performer (K13, K10).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with two qualifications.** (1) "Open to any performer" holds for the **singers' night / singaround** (everyone gets a turn by default; 61–87% of those present sang at clubs measured 2017–18) but not for guest nights, where paid guests do two sets and organizers choose a limited number of floor spots; and in the movement's first decade, **"policy clubs"** (led by Ewan MacColl's) restricted singers to songs in a language they spoke and to traditional styles. (2) Most clubs were and are in pub back or upstairs rooms, but some key early venues were **not pubs** (temperance-hotel rooms, union halls, coffee-house cellars such as the Troubadour, 1954). Volunteer-run: yes — "predominantly voluntarily run", with no umbrella body and no registration.

### A. Snapshot
- **What:** weekly or monthly gatherings, "usually in the back or upstairs room of a public house"; members mostly urban middle class, repertoire focused on rural and industrial working-class song; amateur "floor singers" sing one or two songs; "residents" are regular local performers; touring guests form a circuit (Wikipedia "Folk club", citing Sweers 2005, Finnegan 2007, Boyes 1993 — SOURCED, secondary). "There is no umbrella organization governing folk music activity in the UK, and no defining rules. Predominantly voluntarily run, events typically take place weekly or monthly, and each series is self-contained" (Fay Hield & Paul Mansfield, "Anything goes? Recognizing norms, leadership and moderating behaviors at folk clubs in England", *Ethnomusicology Forum* 28(3), 2019 [pub. 2 Jun 2020], accepted manuscript read locally via https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/162128/). [K1, K10]
- **Numbers over time (conflicting estimates, all SOURCED as stated):** Sept 1959 *Sing* listings: only nine English clubs (Liverpool, Manchester, Bradford, six in London) — probably incomplete (MusTrad MT020, ch. 3, c. 1998, https://mustrad.co.uk/articles/topic.htm); MacColl claimed 1,500 clubs following his policy and 11,000 Singers Club members (called a "probable gross exaggeration"); mid-1960s "probably over 300" (Wikipedia); "1960s heyday of around 3000 clubs" (Bean 2014, cited in Hield & Mansfield); 500–900 in 1987 (MacKinnon 1993, same); "over 160" in the UK after stabilizing (Wikipedia); ~35 in Greater London and 26 in Yorkshire by an online-listings survey (Hield & Mansfield); *The Living Tradition* listed **105 clubs in all of England in 2021** (Butler & Butler, *Int. J. of Traditional Arts* 5, 2026, read locally). Scotland: from three clubs in 1960 to 100+ by 1970 (single memoir source, UNVERIFIED snippet). [K9]
- **Defining feature:** participatory singing in a borrowed room of a working pub, organized by volunteers.

### B. What else exists there (institutions and the founding clubs)
- **Ballads and Blues (London):** co-founded by Ewan MacColl; at the Round House, Wardour Street, Soho; founding date and nature disputed (1953 often given; relaunched as a club Nov 1957 per J. P. Bean); met mostly at the Princess Louise, High Holborn; MacColl and Peggy Seeger left to found the **Singers Club** in 1961; Ballads and Blues continued to May 1965; the Singers Club closed in 1991 (Topic FC history) or 1993 (Wikipedia) — conflict. — Ken Hunt, review of J. P. Bean, *Singing From The Floor: A History of British Folk Clubs* (Faber), kenhunt.doruzka.com, 19 Feb 2016 (SOURCED); Wikipedia (SOURCED). [K13]
- **Topic Folk Club, Bradford (SOURCED, club history https://www.nawaller.com/topicfc/Site/history.html):** founded **Sept 1956** by Alex Eaton (after leaving the local Young Communist League choir) and friends, in "dingy, dusty" rooms with no rent at Laycock's Rooms (a temperance hotel); about **20 venues** since — union hall, pubs (Fox and Goose 1959, Sun Inn 1963–68, **Star Hotel 1969–1991**, Melborn Hotel 1995–2005, etc.), an Irish club, bars; **~3,000 club nights**, fewer than 50 missed apart from Covid; Fridays for ~40 years, Thursdays since 1994; claims to be "certainly the oldest continuously-operating weekly folk club". Early admission one shilling; early funds went to Hungarian refugees (1956) and to records and books for research; **raffle started 26 Aug 1966 when funds ran low** (first prize a 15-shilling record token) and "has continued ever since"; moving into a pub in 1959 ended the admission of under-18s. Today about two-thirds guest nights; singers' nights when no guest is booked; a written constitution and safeguarding policy. [K8, K6, K10, K14]
- **Black Diamond Folk Club, Birmingham (SOURCED, Phil Cross, historyofbrumfolkclubs.uk, page undated, latest event Nov 2024):** formed **June 1964** as a singers' club by Stan Burgess, Chris Scott-Warwick and Ron Wheeler; strong chorus singing (many sea songs); booking guests from 1966; **19 venues in 55 years**, almost all pub rooms (Old Crown, Digbeth 1971–85 the longest; upstairs rooms, an underground room); over 55 years ~1,350 guest and ceilidh bands booked at a cost of over £125,000, ~94,000 attendances, 1,200+ floor singers; about nine residents at one stage; reasons for moving: a new landlord banning door admission charges (2007), karaoke nights, a fire officer closing a clubroom, a pub sold for redevelopment, sudden pub closure (2014), a landlord's death (2024); in 2024 the club visited ~30 premises before finding an upstairs room "every Friday". Attendance after Covid: 11 at the first guest night, 13 at the first singers' night (Aug 2021); 25–30 by the 59th season. [K9, K8, K10]
- **Bridge Folk Club, Newcastle:** at the Bridge Hotel (Grade II listed, c. 1899), Monday evenings, upstairs function room; founded 1958 (Newcastle University Co-Curate, SOURCED); Wikipedia gives 1953 and calls it the oldest club in its original venue (conflict). [K10]
- **Black Swan Folk Club, York (SOURCED, Richard Catton, *English Dance and Song*, Spring 2016, folkengland.org):** began at York University in the mid-1970s, moved within weeks to the Black Swan Inn (c. 600 years old; panelled upstairs room of the late 1600s), where it stayed ~40 years through six landlords; three guest weeks then one singers' week; bought its own PA because of traffic noise through open windows; won the BBC Radio 2 Folk Club of the Year; runs a Winter Folk Day and a free City of York Folk Weekend; organizers in their 60s worry about succession. Landlord: "The folk club is as much a part of the pub as the pub is part of the folk club." [K10, K3, K9]
- **Hull:** the Watersons ran Folk Union One in "the city's biggest pub room" at the Old Blue Bell, Market Place (founded 1963 per one source; UNVERIFIED snippets); a 1969 LP *Blue Bell Folk* by singers of "Hull Union One Traditional Folk Club" is held at Hull History Center (catalog C DSHF/4/11/2, UNVERIFIED snippet). [K13]
- **Sheffield:** Barley Mow club above the Three Cranes pub; Highcliffe club (now the Greystones) (Hunt/Bean, SOURCED). **Manchester:** an early club opened 1954 from a guitar session at the Guitar Circle pub (MusTrad, SOURCED). **Scotland:** St Andrews Folk Club began 1962–63 in the interval of hotel jazz nights, then fortnightly Sundays at the Star Hotel; residents 30 minutes, floor spots, then the guest; door takings paid a guest fee of about £5 and "just about broke even" (Soundyngs, St Andrews, 5 Jan 2026, SOURCED); Scottish clubs "less dogmatic", mixing Scottish, Irish, English and American material (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **English Folk Dance and Song Society (SOURCED, folkengland.org "Our history"):** Folk-Song Society 1898 + English Folk Dance Society 1911 merged **1932**; **Cecil Sharp House**, Camden, opened 1930 ("the first dedicated folk arts center in the UK"); 1935 first International Folk Dance Festival; **1967 National Folk Week, "more than 1,000 events"**; Arts Council Regularly Funded Organization 2009, National Portfolio Organization 2012; National Youth Folk Ensemble from 2016; trading name changing to **Folk England** from autumn 2026. In the 1950s the society was seen as dance-focused; after its October 1957 festival a dispute over presentation and accompaniment split it from the magazine *Sing*; from 1947 its organizer in the north-east and West Country encouraged "singarounds" at barn dances (MusTrad, SOURCED). [K1, K8]
- **Coffee-house cellars (not pubs):** the **Troubadour**, Earl's Court, founded 1954 by Michael and Sheila van Bloemen, "a cornerstone venue in the British folk revival"; still a live venue (troubadourlondon.com, SOURCED). Les Cousins and Bunjies (Soho) closed (Wikipedia, SOURCED).

### C. Cultural locations (by kind)
Pub back rooms and upstairs rooms (named above); temperance and union halls (Bradford, 1956–63); university folk societies (Cambridge St Lawrence Folk Song Society 1950, Oxford Heritage Society by 1956 — Hunt, SOURCED); Cecil Sharp House and its Vaughan Williams Memorial Library (1930); coffee-house cellars (London); festivals spun off from clubs (City of York Folk Weekend). [K10, K13]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **The pub room shapes the economy:** admission at the door + raffle + guest fee; the pub gains bar trade; a landlord can end the arrangement at will (Black Diamond: door-charge ban, karaoke, closure) (SOURCED). Moving into pubs ended under-18 attendance (Topic FC, SOURCED). [K8, K9]
- **The pub room shapes the law:** from 1964 to 2005 pubs needed a separate Public Entertainment License for live music, except under an exemption for no more than two performers; "particularly stringent observance" meant an audience singing along, or successive performers, were in breach, so **a singaround in a pub without a license was technically illegal**; the Licensing Act 2003 (in force Nov 2005) required a single premises license covering regulated entertainment, applications had to be advertised and could be blocked by neighbors; morris dancing alone won an exemption; the **Live Music Act 2012** (in force 1 Oct 2012) freed unamplified live music 8 a.m.–11 p.m. in all venues; a Sheffield session that moved to a central pub returned when staff insisted it stop by the 11 p.m. curfew. — Tim Knowles, "English Folk Law: A Brief Introduction to Pub Licensing", *Int. J. of Traditional Arts* 1, 2017 (read locally). Some clubs became private members' clubs requiring joining 24 hours in advance (Wikipedia, SOURCED). [K6, K1]
- **Participation over polish:** post-1945 revivalists' aim was "simply to get people singing ... Participation was the thing to aim for" (Armstrong & Pearson 1979, quoted in Hield & Mansfield, SOURCED). In two East Midlands clubs (2018–2023) 10–30 attend, 10–15 perform, two songs each, 20–30 songs a night; singers mostly men of retirement age (20–25% women); "all performances, even those which are technically poor or break down midway, are listened to with respect and given applause"; a visitor under 30 gets "a possibly intimidating level of welcome"; ~half the performers over five years were transient, many dropping in from other clubs (Hugh Miller, "What Are People Singing in Folk Song Clubs?", *Int. J. of Traditional Arts* 5, 2026, read locally). [K4, K7, SM: competence shown under pressure — singing unaccompanied before a room]

### E. Structural features
- **K6 — rules of everyday behavior (found, and the core of this pick):**
  - **Policy rule (late 1950s):** residents, guests and floor singers were to sing only "songs which were in a language the singer spoke or understood"; aimed mainly at floor singers; a reaction to skiffle fans arriving and to a generation becoming "quasi-Americans"; "American vocal inflection, syncopation, or instrumental accompaniment" counted against a performance; MacColl allowed only three floor singers and could cancel them at will (a participant's recollection); attendances dipped briefly then recovered; MacColl and a colleague formed a "Critics Group" that analyzed members' singing. Club structure resembled Scout or Boys' Brigade meetings, with subscriptions, lists and announcements. — MusTrad MT020 ch. 3 (SOURCED).
  - **Today's unwritten rules (Hield & Mansfield 2019, SOURCED):** rules "are rarely explicit" and are "incrementally learned"; at a singaround everyone has a turn by default and must actively decline; order goes round the room (one club kept anti-clockwise from the MC's corner for 11 years) or by a sign-up list ("one song each until we reach the end of the list"); MCs quietly reorder to balance quiet and loud, chorus and solo, gender and instruments, and to put strong singers first; guest nights limit floor spots, first-come or curated; a resident is expected not to take offense at being left out for a newcomer, and giving up one's turn for a visitor shows regard for the club's ethos; in unled sessions, taking four or five songs when others have one or two causes resentment. Chorus songs let "the whole room" join; participation rates: 84%, 61%, 71% on three nights at one club (2017), average 76% at another (2018). Drums and full electric line-ups remain rare (Wikipedia).
  - **Who enforces:** the organizer/MC, without formal authority — "moderating behaviors" rather than rules.
- **K9 — friction and loss:** pub closures and landlord changes as the main cause of moves (Black Diamond, Topic, Black Swan, SOURCED); "pubs have just disappeared and most of the clubs were in pubs" (interviewer, Hunt 2016); aging audiences and fewer volunteer organizers ("more and more people trying to make a living from the folk scene and less and less people behind the scenes", *The Living Tradition* editorial 2018, quoted in Hield & Mansfield); Covid closures of 18–20 months (Topic, Black Diamond, Miller). Smoking banned in pubs 1 Jul 2007 (Topic FC page).
- **K8:** door admission, raffles, guest fees (≈£5 in 1960s St Andrews; £7.50 per song for a 1975 radio feature; £125,000+ in guest fees over 55 years at one club); EFDSS state arts funding from 2009; clubs themselves largely unfunded (no source found for club grants).
- **K10:** the club as a weekly fixed point that follows its members from pub to pub; the same core people cross between clubs (Miller; Hield & Mansfield).
- **K11 (loose):** English; Scots and Scottish English in Scottish clubs; the 1950s policy turned on singing in one's own language.
- **K12:** real ale is cited as a reason for choosing a venue (Black Diamond: "about a dozen real ales"); the bar trade underwrites the free or cheap room (SOURCED/inferred from the Black Diamond history — the second half is an inference, not a source statement).
- **K13:** the scene grew from skiffle's decline, university societies, Communist and labor-movement cultural groups (Workers' Music Association, Young Communist League choir), and cheap borrowed rooms.
- **K14:** apprenticeship by floor spot — novices sing beside professionals; some residents turned professional (Black Diamond). [R] — none plainly applicable.

## 6 · #115 Barrowland Ballroom, Glasgow

**Claim to test:** a large dance hall above a market that became a venue touring bands seek out, with a reputation spread by performers and crowds (K13, K10). Build and rebuild dates to check.
**Verdict:** **Confirmed.** Built **1934** by Maggie McIver above the Barras market she and her husband founded (land bought 1921), as a hall for her stallholders' Christmas dance; **extended 1938**; **burned 19 Aug 1958** (date UNVERIFIED snippet) months after her death (June 1958); **rebuilt and reopened 24 Dec 1960** (one source says 1959 — outlier); a dance hall into the 1960s; largely unused by the early 1980s (closed in the early 1970s and used as a roller disco per one source); **reborn as a concert venue in 1983** when Simple Minds filmed a video there with ~600 invited fans and returned for sell-out shows. Capacity ~1,900 standing (2,000–2,100 in some sources; ~1,500 dancers in 1960). The reputation claim is supported: artists voted it the UK's best venue in a Time Out poll (May 2023) and a 2005 BBC Radio 1 poll of 60 touring bands also recognized it (Pollstar 2025). Still owned by the McIver family's company.

### A. Snapshot
- **Where:** 244 Gallowgate, Calton, Glasgow G4 0TS, east of the city center; street-level market halls with the ballroom above; large animated neon sign (Wikipedia, SOURCED; Celtic Connections venue page, SOURCED).
- **Calton and Bridgeton (SOURCED, Understanding Glasgow neighborhood profile):** population 21,776 (2022 Census); minority ethnic population 26% (2022) up from 3.1% (2001); 45% socially rented households, 27% owner-occupied, 52% of householders living alone; life expectancy below the Scottish average (2020–2024); women live ~5.5 years longer than men. A 2008 WHO report put male life expectancy in Calton at 54 (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K9, P9]
- **Defining feature:** a family-built hall above a family-run market, which has survived fire, the end of the dance-hall era and a notorious unsolved murder case, and now serves touring rock.

### B. What else exists there
- **The Barras market (SOURCED):** Maggie McIver (born Margaret Russell, 1879, daughter of a policeman) started trading with a fruit barrow at age 12; she and her husband James rented horses, carts and then static barrows to traders in Calton (Moncur Street); land on the Gallowgate bought 1921; the market covered/enclosed from 1926 to 1928–31 (dates vary by source); she rented many stalls to women; widowed in 1930 (malaria) with nine children; died 1958 a millionaire. — Glasgow Women's Library blog, 9 Dec 2014; STV News 24 May 2016 and 11 Mar 2016 (all SOURCED). Market heyday the 1950s; busy on Sundays in the 1960s when other shops shut (ScottishNews 2026, SOURCED). [K8, K4]
- **Market today:** weekends, 10:00–16:00 (house site, UNVERIFIED snippet); 80+ traders (undated, UNVERIFIED); post-pandemic mix of sustainable fashion, a second-hand bookseller, food trucks and containers on "Maggie's Way" (Suffolk Street) with a stage and busking space; Glasgow Pride's route now ends there (ScottishNews, 4 Sep 2026, interview with managing director Dugald McArthur, great-grandson of the founder, SOURCED). Food: a seafood stall run by one family for almost 50 years, doughnuts fried to order, shellfish bars since the 1950s–60s, chestnut carts historically, and several Hong Kong-rooted food stalls; the first Glasgow Hong Kong Market was held there in 2023 (The Skinny/GNAW, 22 Jul 2024, SOURCED). Centenary 2021. [K12, K3, K4]
- **Ownership and management (SOURCED):** owned by the McIver family's company (Margaret McIver Ltd per a registry snippet, UNVERIFIED); "independently owned, family-run"; general manager of ~40 years (Pollstar, 25 Apr 2025). Shares passed down the family; the previous managing director, the founder's grandson, is credited with reviving the ballroom in the early 1980s (ScottishNews 2026). 2023–24 new bars named for family members; accessible lift installed 2024. [K8]
- **Barrowland Park and Album Pathway:** a 100 m strip by artist Jim Lambie listing 2,000+ acts who played the Barrowland since 1983, opened July 2014 as a Commonwealth Games-period project funded by the council's regeneration services (~£700,000 — UNVERIFIED snippets); intended to be temporary; residents and fans petitioned to keep it (Glasgow Music City Tours, 14 Aug 2015, SOURCED; STV 2016 confirms 2,000+ names, SOURCED). [K3, K9]

### C. Cultural locations (named)
- **The ballroom:** sprung maple floor (reinforced c. 1988 for crowds — Pollstar 2025), barrel-shaped ceiling with acoustic tiles credited for the sound (house site); the tiles were requested by the resident bandleader (Big Issue, Alison Irvine, 23 Aug 2019, SOURCED); a dressing room with stars above the mirrors; no lift for gear — equipment goes up several flights with a hoist; a local crew unloads two articulated lorries from 7:30 a.m. (Irvine, *Barrowland: The Inside Story of Glasgow's Beloved Ballroom*, Luath, extract on Books from Scotland, SOURCED). **Barrowland 2**, a smaller room/bar (Wikipedia). [K14, R — load-in crews and their gear routines]
- **Resident band** Billy McGregor and the Gaybirds, from 1934 and again from 1960 (house site, SOURCED).
- **Annual events tied to the place:** Stiff Little Fingers' sold-out **St Patrick's Day** concerts since 1992 (Wikipedia, SOURCED); Celtic Connections festival concerts each January (2027: six nights) (SOURCED); the Scottish Music Awards since 2020 (Wikipedia); the Barrowland Hall of Fame from 2015 (house site); the 1985 Margaret McIver Festival at which the neon sign was relit (house site). [K2, K3]
- **Glasgow's other dance halls:** Plaza, Eglinton Toll (1922, steel sprung floors), Locarno (1926), Green's Playhouse Ballroom (1928); eleven first-rank "Palais de Dance" opened in the 1920s; "at least eighty dance halls" during WWII; Scottish Country Dance Society formed in Glasgow in 1923 (Irene Maver, TheGlasgowStory, SOURCED). Glasgow had 256 dance halls in 1937 and the Barrowland is the only one still operating under its original name (ScottishNews 2026, SOURCED — figure conflicts with Maver's). [K13, K10]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Market → hall:** built so stallholders could hold their Christmas dance; opened Christmas Eve 1934 with a "Barras night out" that became a yearly tradition (STV 2016, SOURCED). [K2, K4]
- **Big room + good floor + good sound → touring destination:** after the 1983 video and shows, 2,000+ acts played by 2013; artists call it a "rite of passage" and "bucket list" venue (Pollstar 2025); Time Out's artist-curated ranking (16 May 2023) put it first; one artist explained his attachment "because of the people who go to gigs there ... more ready to let themselves go" (SOURCED). The venue's capacity is such that playing it marks a band as having "made it" (Big Issue 2019, SOURCED). [K13, SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Wartime:** the neon sign was taken down in 1940 as a bombing precaution after the ballroom was mentioned in German propaganda broadcasts; dancing continued (house site; STV 2016, SOURCED); a 1941 newsreel shows the hall packed with jitterbugging dancers (UNVERIFIED snippet; NLS film page refused). [K9]

### E. Structural features

- **K9 — friction and loss (reported neutrally):**
  - **1968–69:** three young women were murdered after nights out at the Barrowland — found 23 Feb 1968, 15 Aug 1969 and 31 Oct 1969; all three had been at the ballroom that night; more than 100 detectives and 50,000+ statements; a widely circulated police likeness based on the one witness; the case is unsolved and Police Scotland says such cases "are never closed"; speculation about a later-convicted murderer could not be confirmed because DNA samples had deteriorated (STV News, 23 Feb 2018, SOURCED). - **K8:** a single family company running both market and venue; the venue is hired by promoters (mechanism NOT FOUND); post-Covid rescheduling and rising touring costs (Pollstar).
