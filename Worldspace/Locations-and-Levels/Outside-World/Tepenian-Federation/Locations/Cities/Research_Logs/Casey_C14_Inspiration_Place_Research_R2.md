## 63 · Timbuktu (Tombouctou), Mali · Round 2

**Scope of this round:** the settings of daily life inside the earthen town (the house, the quarter, the river port), the groups who live there and their own ceremonies, the town's third places and media, food culture, and the everyday rules now in force under blockade. Round 1's open threads were chased (curfew hours, hospital, university, salt volumes, Maouloud, Bella Farandi, hotels, tourism figures); results are stated under F.

### A. Other settings
- **The house (K4, K6, P3, P4).** A resident's site (exploretimbuktu.com, post of 19 Oct 2008, no author named; footer "© 2008 Miranda Dodd") describes the earthen house: walls of *banco* (clay brick set in mud) about half a meter thick that "acts like a thermos"; roof beams once of palm trunk, now mostly eucalyptus; wealthier families face outer walls with desert limestone; high ceilings. A simple house is a day/guest sitting room plus a storeroom that doubles as the parents' bedroom, children sleeping in the sitting room; better-off homes add bedrooms, a second sitting room, roof access or a second floor. **Large family homes (the source links the style to the Moroccan period) separate men's areas at the front from women's areas at the back, with two staircases: a front one and a "hidden" interior one leading to the women's upstairs; a man's locked private room holds his accounts and prayer materials.** People take mattresses out to the yard, street or roof to sleep in the cool night air; kitchens are separate, in a shaded corner of the yard or under a pole-and-mat shelter; cooking on charcoal stoves; drinking water cooled in unglazed clay jars; nomadic households use goatskin bags; pit latrines in smaller solid houses; outer banco walls are re-coated after rain damage, roofs sealed with banco. SOURCED. Beehive earthen bread ovens stand "near house entrances" (SOURCED, Afrik.com, 15 Aug 2017; see E). [K4, K6, K14, R]
- **Quarters and who lives where (K4).** A Timbuktu writer (Salem ould Elhadj, *Les arts à Tombouctou*, first published in *Tapama* no. 1, 1996; reprinted *Mots Pluriels* no. 12, Dec 1999) names the medina quarters Badjindé, Sankoré, Djingareïber and Sareïkeïna plus Djamaï Kounda and Bellé Farandi, and places the masons in Djingareïber and the carpenters in Sareïkeïna; the farmers' *Hala* dance belongs to the Ag Welène farmers of Sareïkeïna. SOURCED. A second Timbuktu writer in the same issue (Abdourahamane Hassèye Touré, *Les universités tombouctiennes: grandeur et décadence*) says descendants of the Wangara traders still live in Badjindé (SOURCED, 1996/1999). [K4, P5]
- **The river port setting (K5).** Pinasses (long motorized wooden boats with raised ends, usually two open decks and a roof) carry passengers and goods every few days on the Mopti–Koriomé axis; Kabara is usable only at high water because of silting (UNVERIFIED, search summaries of Wikipedia "Kabara, Mali" and REACH; REACH itself read in R1). The 8 Jan 2026 capsizing (R1) killed traders, women and very young children among others (UNVERIFIED, AllAfrica snippet of RFI). [K5, K9]
- **Water and power when the plant stops (K5, K9).** From Tuesday 23 June 2026 the city had no electricity or piped water: the EDM thermal plant was shut for lack of fuel and the state water company Somagep could not pump; a few boreholes "usually built by NGOs" stayed open but were overwhelmed; **women queued for hours and carried buckets over long distances**; hygiene "exécrables"; the hospital kept running on solar panels and generators; black-market petrol from Algeria sold at 2,500–3,000 FCFA/l against a regulated 875 FCFA, and many traders had stopped bringing it for fear of army drone strikes; the municipal special delegation announced a water-distribution campaign with civil protection from Friday 26 June. SOURCED (RFI/David Baché via AllAfrica, 26 Jun 2026). [K1, K5, K9, R]
- **Schools (K9).** National suspension of all classes from 26 Oct 2025 (fuel shortage), resumption planned 10 Nov 2025; over 2,000 Malian schools closed for insecurity in June 2025 (600,000+ pupils) (SOURCED, HRW, 28 Oct 2025; no Timbuktu-specific figure on that page). A 2022 REACH assessment found 49% of 205 surveyed schools in Timbuktu region closed at least once in the three school years to 2021 (UNVERIFIED, search summary). On 2 Jun 2025 schools near the military camp were evacuated during the attack (SOURCED, Afrik.com, 3 Jun 2025). A 2022 report says a cleric close to the armed groups set conditions for reopening schools in jihadist-influenced parts of the region, including Arabic in the curriculum (UNVERIFIED, Koaci snippet, 11 Nov 2022). [K6, K9]

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Occupational corporations with their own ceremonies (K14, K2).** The **butchers** (drawn from Songhay, Peul, black Kel Tamasheq and Arma families; four great families; a chief butcher who approves the event and gives kola nuts to the four families) dance the **Abarbarba**, which grew out of the Maouloud: from about 17:00 in a set square, women clap, a singer leads, a male and a female dancer compete in a turban-and-scarf dance, masked figures made of horns and hides mime driving off a sorcerer; instruments are a large round drum, a calabash drum and three clay pots; it ends at the Djingareyber call to prayer at dusk. The carpenters' **Djam Tanda** door-making family is consulted on ceremonies. SOURCED (ould Elhadj 1996/1999). The Abarbarba is still danced at weddings, baptisms, religious feasts and receptions of dignitaries (UNVERIFIED, Vikidia snippet; page refused). [K2, K14, SM:competence]
- **Masons' corporation (K14, R).** Governed by orally transmitted principles; formerly father-to-son only, now open to anyone, but any young person from a mason family is still expected to learn early (UNVERIFIED, search summary of a heritage source on Djenné/Timbuktu). The Aga Khan Trust for Culture began comprehensive conservation of Djingareyber at the end of 2006 (roof, minaret, facades, interior with electrical, ventilation and sound systems), training local craftsmen and apprentices on the job (SOURCED, the.akdn earthen-architecture page, undated; no Timbuktu trainee count given there). Trainee counts of "more than 140" or "more than 180" community masons are UNVERIFIED (search summary; the Archnet page returned 429).
- **Noblewomen's music (K13).** The **M'Baga**: a one-string fiddle (calabash body, horsehair string), traced by the author to Andalusian Muslims and to the patron saint Sidi Yahia al-Andalusi; noblewomen once played it at home, improvising genealogical songs; later a public dance in the four medina quarters at weddings, turban ceremonies and circumcisions; dancers in embroidered boubous with gold and silver jewelry; gestures address co-wives and a rivalry dance follows; already declining in 1996 because girls preferred *takamba* and modern dance. SOURCED (ould Elhadj). [K13, K2]
- **Black Kel Tamasheq** dance the **Diaba** after big harvests and at social ceremonies: a basin of water with an inverted calabash struck with cloth-tipped sticks, pentatonic song, a courtship dance with coded gestures. **Farmers (Ag Welène, Sareïkeïna)** dance the **Hala** with hoes and songs invoking Saint Boukouri Attahir for farmers' protection. The notables hold an annual singing contest in Sankoré (name recorded in the source; not a slang term). SOURCED (ould Elhadj). [K2, K13, K7]
- **Fishers (K7, K14).** For the Niger bend generally (not Timbuktu-specific): some Bozo fishers stay near home all year; others run seasonal fishing campaigns tied to the farming calendar, often several pirogues together with wives who help fish and process; long-distance migrants leave their village for 8–10 months (UNVERIFIED, IRD study snippets, 1980s–90s fieldwork). Masons are said to come largely from the Bozo (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Christians (K4, K9).** A Protestant pastor on the edge of town, about 100 m from the desert; his congregation fell from about 150 before 2012 to about 20; about 2,000 Christians in Timbuktu overall (his estimate); of four former churches one in use; Muslim neighbors hosted returning Christians after 2013; many returnees "keep their bags packed." SOURCED (evangelisch.de, 15 Sep 2014). Christians were about 1% of the population before 2012 and waited about a year before returning (UNVERIFIED, Christian press snippets). A journalist of the Christian radio Tahanint was killed in Timbuktu (UNVERIFIED, Afrik.com snippet, undated). [K9]
- **Arab and Tuareg residents after 2013 (K4, K9, sensitive).** Many left when French forces drove out the armed groups in January 2013, fearing reprisals for suspected association with the occupiers; some who stayed were killed; returnees met distrust and in one case a mob burned about a dozen Tuareg homes after a gun attack on security forces; a Tuareg school director in Mbera camp said in 2013 no one there was ready to go back. UNVERIFIED (AP/Fox News and IRIN snippets, 2013–2014; The New Humanitarian refused every page tried, 403). UNHCR (2016) said about 41,000 refugees had returned to Mali after the mid-2015 peace agreement (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Former servile groups (K9, sensitive).** No current source on Bella Farandi quarter was found (see F). Bella/Ikelan are exonyms the group does not use for itself and which carry negative connotations; after WWII French officials reported about 50,000 people under direct control of Tuareg masters in Gao and Timbuktu regions (UNVERIFIED, search summary of academic sources). The 2011 wedding account below names Haratin "family retainers" running errands (SOURCED).
- **Youth (K10).** Informal circles of young male friends meet in the street (the local term for them, and one circle's name, are in the slang file); in Hamabangou young men volunteer to clean and repair their quarter's streets and infrastructure (UNVERIFIED, UNICEF story snippets; pages refused 403). A youth center, the "Café jeunesse" (Maison des jeunes), was rehabilitated with UNDP support as a place to meet, train, get information and relax (UNVERIFIED, UNDP snippet; page refused 403; date unknown). A youth-association president was quoted in Sep 2023: "no truck enters Timbuktu today" (UNVERIFIED, AFP snippet).
- **Languages (K11, loose):** Songhay (Koyra Chiini) carries much Arabic vocabulary in Timbuktu (UNVERIFIED snippet); otherwise as in R1.

### C. Leisure industries
- **Tourism is effectively absent.** Routard (undated online guide) says the region's hotels, agencies and guides "are no longer operational" and any return depends on lasting stabilization (UNVERIFIED snippet). A 2016 Le360 piece says hoteliers, guides, agencies and artisans were put on "forced leave" and some switched trades (UNVERIFIED snippet). The EU Trust Fund reported four craft workshops reopening in October 2019 (UNVERIFIED headline). Festival Vivre Ensemble (27–29 Dec 2024) and the December 2025 Biennale are the only recent visitor events found (R1). No 2024–2026 arrival figures were found. [K8, K9]
- **Wedding economy (K8, K13).** Griots are paid for almost every service at a wedding (see E); in 2011 one wedding involved 400,000 FCFA at the outset, 120,000 FCFA on the trousseau and 100,000 FCFA for a cow. SOURCED (exploretimbuktu, 26 Apr 2011).
- **What locals do:** evening sitting outside, roof sleeping, street circles of friends, draughts games in the evening (UNVERIFIED, Le Temps snippet, date unclear; page refused 403), radio listening (see D).

### D. Third places
- **Mosques and saints' tombs (K10, K2).** Sixteen mausoleums in and around the city hold the 333 saints, including women saints Nana Mariam and Nana Fatouma; residents ask favors through the saints; popular belief holds that they form an invisible protective barrier around the city. SOURCED (DakarActu, page dated 7 Jul 2026, by an intern; mostly historical). Descendants of the saints are consulted with the traditional masons and customary and religious chiefs on the mausoleums (UNVERIFIED, UNESCO study snippet). A cemetery is known locally as that of "the three saints"; military vehicles were seen there the night after the 2 Jun 2025 attack (SOURCED, Afrik.com 3 Jun 2025).
- **Wells as gardens:** the town's old wells serve as small gardens (SOURCED, DakarActu 2026).
- **Markets** as in R1; for Kabara and Koriomé landing places see A.
- **Radio (K10, K13).** Timbuktu stations named by MINUSMA (equipment donated Oct 2016) and by RSF's 2024 appeal of 547 Sahel community radios: Bouctou (Buktu), Jamana, Tahanint, Fréquence Santé, Alfaida, Al Farouck, Kalémé, Lafia, Beeray, Albilad Soudan, Walfadjri, plus ORTM rural radio. SOURCED for the 2016 list (IBTimes UK, 21 Nov 2016); UNVERIFIED for the RSF list (snippet). The regional URTEL coordinator said radio "occupies a special place in our habits, and in our lives" (SOURCED, IBTimes). [K10, K13]
- **Sport:** a Stade Municipal (about 5,000 seats, on the Kabara road) used by a club named Avenir de Tombouctou (UNVERIFIED, generic venue listing); match records of a club "Tombouctou" against Bamako clubs 2014–2020 (UNVERIFIED). No neighborhood league found.
- **Women's work groups (K1, K14).** About ten women, mostly war widows, weave wire fencing on two machines (bought for 1,650,000 FCFA) with ICRC material; sold on the local market to farmers and herders; the workshop head trains the others. SOURCED (ICRC, 7 Aug 2015). Hammabangou village association Alkara Gourey (27 women; sewing, leather, smithing, knitting equipment) and a women's associations' coordination farming a 12-ha "Jardin de la Paix" (UNVERIFIED snippets). [K1, K14, R]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Maouloud (K2).** The writer of 1996 calls it "une fête capitale pour Tombouctou": on the eve, people across the city, at markets, homes, workshops and wells, strike noisy objects and sing religious songs in Songhay (SOURCED, ould Elhadj). In November 2025 residents hung colored lights around Sankoré mosque (SOURCED, Africanews 16 Nov 2025; no program given). A stampede at a narrow passage to a mosque during Maouloud is reported (UNVERIFIED, undated snippet: "at least 26 killed"). Katta pasta is the feast-day dish (below).
- **Weddings (K2, K6, K12).** A first-person account of one wedding (exploretimbuktu, 26 Apr 2011) lists: a negotiation visit by the groom's people with a milk drink, dates, roasted peanuts and cakes, and money with the date announced; a trousseau suitcase bought by the groom's female relatives; a short prayer and the imam's blessing at the bride's home; the bride washed, dressed, veiled and driven in a horn-blowing procession to a separate house; breakfast of stew and bread from the bride's family to the groom's group; braiding with gold on the third day; an unveiling at dusk under a tent, with a tip to the older woman behind the bride and a female griot taking the money; the move to the groom's home; later a party by the bride's friends with the in-laws as honored guests. Rules recorded: the bride keeps face covered and eyes lowered and is held to be especially vulnerable to spirits; she should look sad to leave her family; the groom must not be left alone; days are counted dusk to dusk. A female "aunt" acts as counselor and organizer. SOURCED. The account's food and dress vocabulary suggests an Arabic-speaking (Moorish) household; it is one wedding, not a general rule. The Songhay bride's hairstyle is braided at collective ceremonies as the mark of married status (UNVERIFIED, Vikidia snippet). [K2, K6]
- **Food culture (K12).** **Katta**: thin short strands of local-wheat pasta rolled between two fingers by women, dried a day in shade, toasted brown-yellow, stored in bags or pots; cooked in a sauce of dried fish, tomato, spices and mutton or beef; made for important guests and Muslim feasts (Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Mawlud); a Slow Food Presidium launched 2011 with FAO and Italian funding aimed at a women's cooperative of katta and bread producers in Timbuktu. **Breads**: wadjila, tukasu (steamed), takula (flat, from earthen ovens). A garlic-spiced meat sausage is also made. SOURCED (Afrik.com, 15 Aug 2017). **Widjila**: bread balls steamed in a couscoussier, served with mutton in sauce; "the dish par excellence of northern Mali," especially in Ramadan. SOURCED (Le360, 7 Mar 2022). Wedding guests get tea, milk and sheep's meat (SOURCED, exploretimbuktu 2011). [K12]
- **Scholarship as livelihood (K14, K9).** The scribe's trade is "obsolete"; the writer contrasts the old fee system for Quranic teaching with what he calls the material exploitation of pupils by some Quranic masters today (SOURCED, Touré 1996/1999). [K9]
- **Civic response:** municipal water distribution with civil protection (June 2026, above); youth volunteer street work (UNVERIFIED).

It was later extended (UNVERIFIED, APA snippet). Journal du Mali (Dec 2025, R1) still described the city as under curfew. Current hours after mid-2025 NOT FOUND.
- **Armed-group rules on the roads:** in October 2025 JNIM reportedly announced rules for all travelers on Malian roads, notably veils for women (UNVERIFIED, RFI via AllAfrica headline and snippets; page refused 403). OFPRA (2023) describes zakat imposed and strict rules affecting women in areas the groups control (UNVERIFIED, snippet). No music or wedding ban specific to the Timbuktu region in 2024–2026 was found.
- **Household rules:** gendered house spaces and the wedding rules above. A night custom of boys catching and cooking cats is reported as an "old tradition" of the town (UNVERIFIED, AFP 2022 snippet via SNRT; page refused 403).

## 64 · Agadez and the Cure Salée (In-Gall), Niger · Round 2

**Key new source:** the Niger Ministry of Culture's participatory intangible-heritage inventory *Inventaires communautaires: Boubon, Agadez et pratique de l'Habbanaé* (Niger 2014–2015; fieldwork Oct–Dec 2015; UNESCO Dakar project; 61-page PDF read locally with pdftotext). It inventories Agadez practices with the Sultan, the chief of the Sultanate's masons, the chief of the Sultanate's griots, the east and west festival organizers, tanners, smiths and others as participating community members. Cited below as "ICH inventory 2015." All SOURCED unless marked.

### A. Other settings
- **The city as administered space (K1, P7).** Agadez region (2023 projection): 691,415 people; Tchirozérine department (which contains the city) 346,978; organized into 6 departments, 4 urban and 11 rural communes, **1 sultanate, 4 cantons and 13 nomadic groupings**. Under-15s 49%; urbanization 44.7% (2012). Livestock 2022 (thousands of head): goats 1,076.9; sheep 616.9; camels 166.8; donkeys 114.9; cattle 49.4. Health 2022: 19,676 inhabitants per doctor (WHO norm 10,000), 1,827 per nurse. Agadez commune gross primary enrollment 96.96% (2020–21). Hotels 2022: 11 in the region, 194 rooms, 295 beds (one 3-star, two 2-star, one 1-star, seven unclassified). Water points 2022: 2,621 modern points, 928 cemented wells, 533 pastoral pumping stations. Paved roads 826.3 km (2022), of which 165 km in good condition. SOURCED (INS Niger, *Agadez en chiffres*, edition 2023, dated February 2024, PDF read locally). [K1, K5, K8]
- **Municipal profile (K1).** City 137,354 (2017) over 213 km², growing 3.17%/yr since 2000; 40% aged 15–39; many residents nomadic or semi-nomadic; many internal migrants arrived in the 1970s–80s fleeing drought; municipal council of 20 elected every five years, 15% women, six committees (finance, development and environment, economic affairs, land affairs among them); decentralization since 2002 hampered by lack of staff and tax base. About 5,000 tourists a year 1985–2007; the 2015 law cut migrant transit by 75%; in 2016 an estimated 5,000 people a week left Agadez for Libya. SOURCED (ODI, *Mayors Dialogue: City profile Agadez*, Nov 2020, citing INS and Clingendael 2017). [K1, K8, K9]
- **The hospital (K5).** The regional hospital (CHR) was built in 2007 with Islamic Development Bank financing; WHO installed an oxygen plant there and in April 2024 donated 7.5 t of medicines and equipment to the region; a US field hospital added 30 beds; a WHO mission noted lack of ambulances and motorbikes and missing birth kits. UNVERIFIED (WHO Afro and ADF snippets).
- **The Tuareg bride's house (K4, K14).** In Eladab, a village on the edge of Agadez commune, **women and girls build the *ehan* (a domed Tuareg tent-house) for a new bride**: stakes at each corner, wood bent round, a palm-rib frame tied together, large mats laid over and roped down as roof, a side mat around the stakes; inside, two posts for decoration, the bed, the trunk, the provision bag; taught mother to daughter, with prayers, songs and dances. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). [K4, K14, R]
- **Tannery quarter (K14).** Traditional tanning is done in the peripheral quarter of **Abalan**: hides from the abattoir soaked two days in lime and natron, then in pits with *Acacia nilotica* pods, washed in salt water, dried, oiled with groundnut or palm oil, dyed in a pit about 1 m deep, then stretched with a stick. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). [K14]
- **Gold camps (K7, K9).** At Tchibarakaten businessmen built water towers, shops, restaurants, a field hospital and lighting (UNVERIFIED, Vice snippet); about 4,000 miners were said to be still working, numbers falling as surface deposits ran out (UNVERIFIED, Small Arms Survey snippet); on 11 Apr 2023 five soldiers escorting a miners' convoy from Tchibarakaten to Arlit were killed in an ambush (UNVERIFIED, Koaci snippet). GI-TOC/ISS (2023) studied recruitment at Tchibarakaten and Djado from 65 interviews (identified, not read).
- **Salt town of Bilma (sub-area of the caravan system, K5, K14).** Roughly half of Bilma's people still work the salt pits; "every family has its own salt pan," worked with wife and children; salt pressed in date-palm moulds; a traditional authority (the *mai*) sets the sale price; buyers are few; caravans still stop over; people travel armed and in military-escorted convoys when possible. SOURCED (AFP via Al Jazeera gallery, 12 Jun 2023). Small Taghlamt camel trains still leave each November (UNVERIFIED, Wikipedia-derived snippet, undated).

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Ethnic economic niches (K4, K7).** An informal understanding among groups: **Toubou** run trade and transit toward Libya and transport to the Djado gold mines; **Tuareg** do the same toward Algeria and the Tchibarakaten mines; **Arabs of Libyan descent** handle cross-border agro-food trade from Libya and Algeria and have moved into transport, construction and public works. SOURCED (ODI 2020 citing Clingendael 2017; the Clingendael PDFs were refused, 403). [K4, SM:system]
- **Smiths and the cross (K14).** The Agadez cross is cast only by men from established jeweler families (mold, molten gold, silver or nickel, demould, decorate, polish; 2–3 days per cross). The men's Tuareg leather purse is made exclusively by women, mostly of smith families, taught from childhood; 10–20 days per piece. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). Tourism raised smiths' status (a newer term for "artisan" came to be preferred; recorded in the slang file); at the peak the artisanal center federated **up to 1,500 members in 72 cooperatives** with German project support; women's crafts (leather, basketry, shoemaking, weaving, dyeing, knitting, sewing) sold for less, men controlling raw materials and marketing; a women's union of six groups with NGO equipment charged members 3,000 FCFA every four months; one craftswoman's daily takings fell from over 100,000 FCFA to under 1,000 after the tourist collapse; at the peak three women headed travel agencies (2000) and one owned a hotel. SOURCED (Ouassa Tiekoura, "Tourism and gender identities in Agadez, Niger," *Via Tourism Review* no. 2, 2012; fieldwork 2010–11, 40 interviews). [K8, K9, K14]
- **Women in the migration economy (K7).** For decades hundreds of Agadez women lodged and fed migrants in transit, often as wives or sisters of drivers or shelter managers (UNVERIFIED, photo-project description; page refused 403).
- **Migrants (K4, K9).** Locals and migrants "clash frequently"; locals say migrants encourage smoking and drug use; migrants say locals do not accept their differences; nationals of countries deemed hostile (e.g., Nigeria) have passports held for a few days on arrival. SOURCED (Al Jazeera, Shola Lawal and Hannane Ferdjani; page date read as 29 Jul 2025, though the URL path carries 2024/7/29). Earlier reporting (2016–19): shelters are walled plots, sometimes with a hut, tarps, no water or electricity; men do odd jobs, some women sex work; only 7% of non-Nigerien migrants surveyed by IOM tried to work in Agadez (UNVERIFIED, Reuters Foundation and IOM/Samuel Hall snippets). A second-division club, **Nassara Agadez**, run by a former migrant transporter, has signed stranded migrants as players (UNVERIFIED, AFP via ModernGhana snippet; page refused 403). A Nigerian resident attended Bianou in 2019 (SOURCED, IOM, below).
- **Guitar bands (K13).** Agadez has dozens of bands hired for weddings, baptisms and political events, and a living can be made on that circuit; Etran de L'Aïr (formed 1995), sons of nomad families settled in the 1970s, say they play for Tuareg, Toubou, Zarma and Hausa alike; they began with one acoustic guitar and a calabash floating in water as rhythm, later amplifying with a transducer, then electric guitars and drums. UNVERIFIED (Music in Africa and band-page snippets).
- **Hip-hop (K13).** The Sahel Hip Hop caravan for peace (left Niamey 22 Apr 2018) performed on the "immense stage" of the **Maison des Jeunes et de la Culture** around the 24 April Concord Day; local musicians shared the bill. SOURCED (Music in Africa, 3 May 2018). [K10, K13]
- **Languages (K11, loose):** Friday Quran reading is in Arabic, Tamasheq and Hausa (ICH inventory 2015); otherwise as in R1.

### C. Leisure industries
- **Tourism collapse and domestic-tourism turn (K3, K8).** About 5,000 tourists a year 1985–2007 (ODI). The **17th Festival de l'Aïr** opened at Iférouane on Fri 5 Dec 2025 for three days under the theme of domestic tourism and crafts ("Valorisation du tourisme interne et de l'artisanat…"): camel races and harnessing contests, equestrian parades, dances, concerts, exhibitions of leather, metal, jewelry and textiles; prime ministers of Niger, Chad and Burkina Faso attended. SOURCED (Sidwaya). Thousands attended and a site excursion was organized (UNVERIFIED, voyagesafriq snippet). Old-town souvenir shops are almost all closed; one remaining shop sells silver knives in leather cases and imported beads (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2025).
- **Sport (K10).** The 44th **Sabre National** (national traditional wrestling championship) was held in Agadez for ten days around the New Year 2023/24 (semi-final 31 Dec 2023); venues decorated in the colors of Niger, Burkina Faso and Mali; prizes up to USD 16,500 funded by businesses and patrons; *sogolo* music and team dances; a spectator called it "the cement of unity." SOURCED (VOA/AFP, 2 Jan 2024). The arena is the Arène des Jeux Traditionnels Aboubacar Djibo, with 80 wrestlers (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K10]
- **Wedding circuit:** see bands above; weddings are public and the main band income (R1).

### D. Third places
- **Friday mosque circuit (K10, K2).** After Friday prayer at the Grand Mosque, the Sultan, notables and designated scholars go to the adjoining **Albohari mosque** for the reading of a sixtieth of the Quran, led by the Sultanate's official in charge of Quran reading; prayers for peace and unity; the Sultan then receives greetings, and the head of the Sultanate's band sings his praises as he leaves. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015).
- **Thursday dusk praise round (K2, K13).** When the Sultan is in town, every Thursday at dusk (after the night prayer) the Sultanate's griot band, with *algaita* oboes and drums in **twelve rhythms each with its own meaning**, praises and gives counsel to the Sultan and notables (security chief, chief butcher, the women's representative), moving from the palace to the deputy sultan's, the Kel Ewey chief's and the Sultan's secretary's houses; it also reminds the town that Friday is coming. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). [K2, K13]
- **Quarter mosques named in Maouloud rounds:** Fanna (Akanfaya), Larabawa (Oungoual Bayi), Adani (Obitara), Ayouba, Abadede (Amarewat), Malam Ousseini, Tandé, and the Quranic school of Amdit. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015).
- **Maison des Jeunes et de la Culture** (above); the bus station and passeurs' offices as the busiest scenes (SOURCED, Al Jazeera 2025); a roaming tea seller serving sweet green tea with mint (SOURCED, same). [K10, K12]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Bianou, in full (K2).** The Agadez carnival under the Sultan's authority, **24 days**, "the whole community takes part"; origin uncertain (hypotheses: Noah's flood; the first year of the Hijra; the arrival of Libyans from Ghat). Run by two groups, **West** and **East**, under the principal organizer (*tambari*), with a youth leader and a materials officer for each side. **A week after Eid al-Adha** the West organizer and his officers visit the patriarch, who after a Fatiha strikes the drum three times to open; each side then parades its own route through the old town singing and dancing; on the day of the new crescent the two groups meet at the Kel Ewey chief's house in **Akanfaya** and compete in song and dance; on the **8th day** ("evening of beauty") men present themselves to the Sultan in their finest clothes and ride round town until the sunset prayer, then ride to **Alerces village (5 km)** for a night vigil; on the **9th** they return in full regalia and are welcomed with dancing; the **10th** is for Quran reading, prayers and joking-kinship exchanges. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). IOM's 2019 account: 18 Aug–11 Sep 2019, "30 days after Tabaski," 23 days; warriors of East and West with spears and daggers cross the city to tambourines; the once-hostile meeting of the two sides now symbolizes peace; at night the convoy goes 5 km out and returns next morning dancing, singing and waving palm branches; the Sultanate worries it is "slowly losing its popularity among the new generations"; IOM support was EU-funded stabilization. SOURCED (IOM West and Central Africa, 10 Sep 2019). Note: start "a week after" (ICH) versus "30 days after" Eid al-Adha (IOM); both recorded. A traditional date-millet-cheese ball is prepared for Bianou (below). [K2, K1, SM:competence]
- **Maouloud (K2).** Celebrated from **27 Safar to 10 Rabi' al-Awwal** across old-town mosques; only men read the Quran in the mosques; opening readings at Abadede mosque (Amarewat) 27–30 Safar; on the 7th day (the Prophet's naming day) a large gathering at Tandé mosque, and that night men and women meet at the Sultanate square and sing praises until dawn to close the festival. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015).
- **Replastering the minaret (K1, K14, R).** The Grand Mosque's 27 m minaret is maintained under the Sultan's responsibility: the Sultanate's chief mason informs the Sultan, who mobilizes resources; Quranic schools, young boys, masons and elders are called; the Sultan launches the work after prayers; about **fifteen days** of work; *banco* of water, straw and laterite prepared days ahead, applied from the traditional scaffold; **east face three coats, north and south two, west one**, against the rain. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). [K1, K14, R, SM:competence]
- **Food (K12).** A ball of **millet, dates and dried cheese**, pounded together by Aïr Tuareg women (who pray, sing and recite poems while making it), prepared for Bianou, weddings and naming ceremonies, and as travel provisions because it **keeps about a month**; on Saharan journeys men prepare it. SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). Sand-baked bread and three-glass tea as desert hospitality are documented mainly for Algerian Tuareg (UNVERIFIED for Agadez).
- **Livestock solidarity (K1).** *Habbanaé*, a Wodaabe/Fulani practice now extended to Tuareg and others: loan of a pregnant female animal for one to three years to someone whose herd was lost; a milk variant lends a newly calved animal until weaning; given at post-harvest celebrations or on Wednesdays or Sundays (inventoried in Tahoua, Maradi and Zinder regions, not Agadez). SOURCED (ICH inventory 2015). [K1, R]
- **The Cure Salée in depth (K2, K1, K7).** Herders' *tenekkert*, July to December in good years; more than 10,000 people and thousands of animals on the Ighazer plain each season; each group keeps its own route and window: Ouelleminden camel herders arrive from the west at the end of July and leave end of August; Kel Gress (from Damergou and the Tarka valley) pass east of In-Gall and stay about two months; Bororo Fulani take long-horned cattle to the salt springs avoiding the others; Illabakan rarely leave before 15 August; Kel Fadey occupy the whole plain; Kel Ahaggar and Kunta fall back to Tegidda n'Tesemt. **Tegidda** has three brackish springs (salt, doum palm, mountain); natron waters purge and deworm animals. It is a time of reunions, marriages, palavers and settling disputes; a legend says married women who do not follow the transhumance become sterile. Only the Kel Gress still run the Taghlamt caravan to Fachi and Bilma. **Since 1976** the state has used the gathering to meet herders (the minister opened it, toured camps; vaccinations and gifts of sugar, tea and tobacco); now the Ministry of Livestock sets the date; a 600-seat grandstand and, since 2009, an arch mark the site; program: speeches, beauty contest, camel races, music contest, health awareness; interrupted 2006–2009 by the rebellion; mining has closed routes at Tegidda n'Tesemt, Azelik and Gélélé, concentrating herds, causing conflicts and dispossession without compensation, and pushing some herders to settle. SOURCED (ingall-niger.org, "La cure salée," undated association page). **Colonial era:** a French post set up at In-Gall 23 Sep 1904; Captain Laforgue met Ouelleminden and Kel Gress chiefs there during the cure, 3–10 Sep 1907; in 1908 a Tahoua camel corps section (59 riflemen, 190 camels) oversaw the cure; no gathering in 1916–17 after the Kaocen revolt; resumed 1919 under guard of 40 riflemen; fort built 1917; post dissolved 1927. SOURCED (ingall-niger.org, colonial-period page). The 2025 preparations: the regional governor launched works (entrance gate, latrines, palm-frond huts, grandstand) and a small-ruminant vaccination campaign (SOURCED, Journal du Niger, 23 Sep 2025). [K1, K2, K5, K9]
- **Civic dates (K2, K3).** 24 April, National Concord Day (since the 1995 peace accord with the Tuareg rebellion); a Flame of Peace weapons-burning was held in Agadez on 25 Sep 2000 before about 10,000 people. UNVERIFIED (snippets).

## 65 · Zagora and the "Tombouctou 52 jours" sign, Morocco · Round 2

**Key new source:** Simon Martin, *Influence du tourisme sur la gestion de l'eau en zone aride: exemple de la vallée du Drâa (Maroc)*, Université de Lausanne (IGUL), June 2006 (licentiate thesis; PDF read locally; fieldwork in Zagora hotels, campsites and the ORMVA; it relies heavily on L. Ouhajou 1996). Cited as "Martin 2006." Its figures are 2004–2005 and are dated as such.

### A. Other settings
- **The tourist strip south of the wadi (K4, P5).** In 2004 several hotels stood south of town across the wadi near Amezrou but apart from the village, forming "a kind of tourist quarter" near the palm grove and Jbel Zagora and at the junction of desert routes (south to Tagounite and M'hamid, east to Merzouga); land there was cheap agricultural or unused land. Zagora had 9 classified hotels (of 14 in the province) and 2 unclassified; the provincial tourism council (CPT) decided in 2003 that all hotels must be classified by 2006; riads in the palm grove escaped classification; **21 campsites, only 10 authorized**, most equipped for camper vans; more than 1,100 hotel beds plus about 80 in riads; three campsites visited held about 800 places. Named hotels with rooms/beds (2004): Palais Asmaa 96/121, Riad Salam 120/240, Club Reda 156/310, Ksar Tinzouline 88/180, La Fibule du Draa 24/48, Kasbah Asmaa 32/64, Sirocco 20/40, Zagour 18/40, La Palmeraie 56/110, La Rose des Sables 25/54, Les Amis 18/28. Fixed and temporary **bivouacs** in the dunes are called a "new, much-demanded tourist product" (provincial monograph 2004). SOURCED (Martin 2006). [K4, K8, K10]
- **Ksar quarters by status (K4, sensitive).** Traditional Drâa society (after Ouhajou 1996) ranks three groups: **religious lineages** (chorfa, claimed descendants of the Prophet, and marabout families descended from a saint) at the top, traditionally handling religion and **settling disputes**; former **protector tribes** (once nomads who settled on their protégés' land; politics, war, herding); and **Haratin** (Black sedentary farmers), "the major part of the disinherited population, forced into farm work or emigration." The division is "marked clearly in the sharing of residential quarters in the ksour" and in irrigation. Martin observed that in Amezrou the Haratin "live in a separate place" and meet "at best the indifference" of the Berber or Arab population. The state officially promotes equality, though the order "was never officially questioned" (Ouhajou, quoted). SOURCED (Martin 2006). [K4, K9]
- **The Jewish kasbah of Amezrou (K4, K14).** Described as one of the oldest Jewish kasbahs of the Drâa; a synagogue "in better shape than others in the Draa Valley"; narrow streets and covered passages; **Jews used the west door, Muslims the east door**; Jews "left the country around 1958"; jewelers, smiths and carpenters work nearby, and smiths trained by Jews still use the tools and models they left to make fibulae and medallions. SOURCED (moroccanjews.org, Zagora page, undated). Fodor's dates the silversmiths' departure to "after 1948" (UNVERIFIED snippet); both dates recorded. [K14, R]
- **M'hamid El Ghizlane (sub-area, end of the road).** Rural commune 6,664 (2024 census), 7,590 (2014) (UNVERIFIED, citypopulation snippet citing HCP). Ksar Ouled Driss dates to the 17th century with about 150 families still living in it (UNVERIFIED, Lonely Planet snippet). A nomad school set up by an association closed, and locals formed their own association (UNVERIFIED, TelQuel 2015 snippet; telquel.ma refuses fetches). Morocco's nomad population fell from about 70,000 (2004) to about 25,000 (2014 census); herders go into debt to buy fodder in drought; settling is the most common response (UNVERIFIED, TelQuel Oct 2022 snippet). Afropop (2018) put the remaining nomad families around M'hamid at "roughly 4,000 families (about 25,000 people)" (SOURCED as the article's statement).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Water law of the canals (K1, K6, R).** 89 canals (*seguias*) along the valley, traditionally each with its own intake, now 78 intakes including five modern diversion dams. Two regimes: **collective water**, where every plot gets water in turn from top to bottom until flooded (in practice favoring upstream plots, often held by dominant groups); and **owned water**, where water is property separate from land, used in **rotations of 8 to more than 15 days divided into water-days of 12 or 24 hours**, the order fixed by rules or **drawn by lot**, each day split into time-shares originally allotted by each user's contribution to building the canal and since sold, rented, given or inherited. Maintenance: under collective water everyone, even the landless, owes **collective work days** (because they use water domestically); under owned water each water-day's group sends an equal number of workers, each owner contributing in proportion to his shares; the religious authority mediates canal disputes; state intervention pushed paid work in place of collective labor. Collective water dominates upstream, owned water downstream. SOURCED (Martin 2006, citing Ouhajou 1996 and Hamza 1988). [K1, K6, K14, SM:system]
- **Water conflict now (K1, K9).** Civil groups estimated in Aug 2024 about **10,000 unauthorized wells out of 20,000** in the province; 80–85% of residents are small farmers living on dates; palms fell from 5 million (1930) to under 800,000; the governor rationed watermelon after a meeting on 18 Oct 2023; there is no water police in Zagora and the nearest basin-agency office is in Guelmim; demands included a ban, disaster-zone status and more water police. SOURCED (Hespress, 23 Aug 2024, Arabic). A governor's decision no. 20 of **14 Oct 2026** regulates red and yellow watermelon for the 2026/27 season (UNVERIFIED, snippet). [K1, K6, K9]
- **Festivals and saints:** the Moulay Abdelkader Jilali moussem date remains NOT FOUND (Arabic and French). Achoura is celebrated nationally with children's bonfires and water-throwing (UNVERIFIED; no Drâa-specific source).
- **Food (K12):** a wedding tagine (meat with apricots, prunes, almonds), Drâa vegetable soup and stuffed pigeons are cited as local dishes; bread baked in hot sand (UNVERIFIED, tourism-site and restaurant snippets).
- **Henna (K7):** henna is grown at Tazarine, Aït Boudaoud, Aït Ouallal, Nkob and Taghbalte in Zagora province; a "Henna of the Maïder Basin" geographical indication (2021) covers 17 communes in Errachidia, Zagora and Tinghir provinces (UNVERIFIED snippet; page truncated).

## 108 · Tamanrasset, Algeria · Round 2

### A. Other settings
- **Quarters and who settled where (K4, P5, sensitive).** From 1975 the town became "a vast building site that attracts many people"; the Kel Adagh (Tuareg from the Malian Adrar des Ifoghas side) settled in **Tahaggart**, on the periphery, organized by region of origin, which the authors call a kind of ghetto; many arrived after the 1963–64 revolt in Mali and the 1970s droughts (via Timiaouine); in 1975 local authorities suspended civil registration for these border residents and set up a selection commission. In April 1965 the town's special municipal delegation had 7 Tuareg, 1 Haratin and 2 traders. Around 1973–74 Haratin who had earned wages at the In Ekker nuclear-test site bought old trucks and opened a Timiaouine–Tamanrasset passenger line at 150 DA a person. SOURCED (Rachid Bellil and Badi Dida, "Évolution de la relation entre Kel Ahaggar et Kel Adagh," in H. Claudot-Hawad (ed.), *Le politique dans l'histoire touarègue*, IREMAM 1993, pp. 95–110, read on OpenEdition). [K4, K7, K9]
- **Outreach geography (K4).** A local NGO's 2015–16 outreach counted people met by quarter: Tahaggart 1,599, Gataâ El Oued 42, Sersouf 11, Matnatalat 10, Amechouen 1, Assoro 1; its women's-health center stands at the entrance of Sersouf Ferraille (the scrap quarter). SOURCED (Algerie360, 9 Jun 2016). New quarters named in other sources: **Taberkat** (site of the new hospital, below), **Tafsit** (site of a new 156-unit market, 2013), cité **El Wiam** (a 58-stall fruit-and-vegetable market opened at the start of Ramadan 2013). SOURCED (Radio Algérie 11 Aug 2025; Algerie360 23 Jul 2013). Note: Round 1 searched "Tafsit" as a spring festival; in Tamanrasset sources it appears as a place name. [K4]
- **Hospital (K5, K9).** After a fire at the old hospital in late July 2025, the new **240-bed hospital "Défunt Moudjahid Guemama Mohamed"** in the Taberkat quarter entered service on Sunday **10 Aug 2025** (Ministry of Health communiqué via APS). SOURCED (Radio Algérie, 11 Aug 2025). The minister launched rehabilitation of the old hospital in September 2025 (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Schools (K9).** In one school year (report on 2018–19) several hundred pupils were turned away for lack of classrooms; the new lycée at Gataâ El Oued (800 places) had 244 pupils (UNVERIFIED snippet). Schooling in the Ahaggar "seems to give few results" compared with the Ajjer (UNVERIFIED, IREMAM chapter snippet).
- **Old Assihar market and its replacement (K5, K10).** The central Assihar market burned on a Thursday in July 2013; it had **more than 419 traders, 182 of foreign nationality, 140 with a trade register**; the communal assembly president ordered it closed; legal traders were to be compensated and moved to the new **Tafsit** market (about 90% built, 156 units); the wilaya set aside 120 million DA to rehabilitate the site, which must keep hosting the annual Assihar fair. SOURCED (Algerie360, 23 Jul 2013). This dates Round 1's undated fire. In 2012 the market was described as run mostly by Malian and Nigerien traders selling counterfeit electronics, cosmetics, herbs and a Malian beer, while grilled-meat restaurants serving Malian dishes were spreading in the center (SOURCED, Algerie360, 6 Mar 2012). [K5, K6, K10]
- **Life texture, 2010 (K10).** Many children leave school early and beg; children play on a pile of tires; the amusement park is crowded and run down; tea drinking is "a constant social ritual"; bottled water nearly unavoidable; frozen fish a treat (SOURCED, Algerie360, 21 Jan 2010; same reportage as R1, further details).

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Kel Ahaggar and Kel Adagh (K4, K9, sensitive).** Historically Kel Ahaggar men married Kel Adagh women but rarely the reverse; later, mixed couples formed (Kel Ahaggar–Kel Adagh and Chaamba–Kel Ahaggar). A 1975 brawl led to burned tents and an order to send Kel Adagh back to Timiaouine; the authors record that a phrase naming someone as Kel Adagh came to be used pejoratively among Kel Ahaggar (recorded in the slang file). Young Kel Adagh who worked in Libya sent money home that funded festivities. After the death of Amenokal Bay ag Akhamuk (1973) no new amenokal was named; El Hadj Moussa was elected a national deputy in 1976. SOURCED (Bellil and Dida 1993). [K4, K9]
- **The Amenokal today (K1).** The office was not recognized after independence and was abolished in 1977 (UNVERIFIED, Wikipedia snippet); press reports a rivalry between cousins over representing the Tuareg tribes and contested legitimacy since the death of El Hadj Moussa Akhamoukh; an APS communiqué of April 2025 was signed by an "Amenokal N'Ahaggar et Tamesna" (UNVERIFIED snippets). [K1]
- **Malian Tuareg refugees (K9, sensitive).** In October 2024 Algerian authorities forced several thousand Malian Tuareg who had sheltered near Tinzaouatène back across the border; tens of thousands of Malian Tuareg remain in Algeria, "tolerated" rather than recognized, with no camp opened (UNVERIFIED, RFI via AllAfrica snippet; page refused 403). Drone strikes at Tinzaouatène killed at least 21 civilians in August 2024 per a Tuareg-led group (UNVERIFIED, AP snippet).
- **Youth (K9, K10).** In 2012 a young Tuareg man said the state treats the Tuareg "as folklore"; a taxi driver listed what young people want: work, leisure, housing, marriage; informal taxis take anyone who raises a hand for a flat 50 DA; most shops are run by people from other regions (Kabyles in food, easterners in other goods); one youth called the festivals a distraction from real problems. SOURCED (Algerie360, 6 Mar 2012). Unemployed rallies (about a thousand at the main square; a sit-in at the town hall; demands for jobs reserved for "children of the South"; local unemployment given as 13.49%) are reported, undated (UNVERIFIED snippets, probably 2013). The whole city had four cybercafés at one point (UNVERIFIED, undated snippet). [K9, SM:man vs society]
- **Civil society (K1).** The association **Green Tea** (president named; center for HIV information since July 2015; 2,311 tests of 2,659 people met; a women's-health center with psychological consultations and community educators; outreach to migrants in restaurants and gathering places; 16 nationalities, 32% women). SOURCED (Algerie360, 9 Jun 2016). It also counted 2,724 displaced migrants in July 2016 (R1).
- **Christians (K4).** A Tamanrasset parish holds Sunday Mass (18:30 in a 2024 pilgrimage program); Little Brothers of Jesus live there; the feast of St Charles de Foucauld (1 December) is celebrated in the diocese (UNVERIFIED, diocesan PDF snippets).
- **Languages (K11, loose):** Tamahaq, Arabic and Hausa/Sahelian languages in the markets; nothing beyond R1 read.

### D. Third places

SOURCED (Algerie360, 27 Apr 2022). The tea-table names are in the slang file.
