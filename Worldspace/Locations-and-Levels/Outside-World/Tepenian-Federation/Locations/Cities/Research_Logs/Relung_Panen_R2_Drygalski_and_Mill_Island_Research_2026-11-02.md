### The 1960 station "Mir" and the person who ran it

- Wikipedia (SOURCED): "Mir," a temporary Soviet meteorological field station, 20 May to 6 August 1960, 65°45'S 92°26'E, elevation 327 m, "for one winter," citing *Scientific Stations in Antarctica 1882-1963* (New Delhi, 1971).
- **Who:** a German biographical notice of **Günter Skeib** (Polarforschung 81(2), 2012, and a 1999 birthday notice) says he led an East German research group inside the **5th Soviet Antarctic Expedition**, wintering at Mirny in 1959-60, and that the group set up and ran an outlying station on Drygalski Island for meteorological and radiation measurements. **UNVERIFIED** (search-tool summaries of the notices; the PDFs returned Anubis access-denied pages or SSL errors).
- **Skeib's paper (above) says he visited the island in February 1960 and stayed there from May to August 1960** (UNVERIFIED, abstract snippet). This matches the 20 May to 6 August dates of "Mir" and attributes the station to the East German observer. It does not explain the identical dates that Wikipedia also gives for the Druzhba station on Zavadovskiy Island (open in round 1); that remains **unresolved**, now with one more fact on the Mir side.
- **Weather numbers attributed to the paper (UNVERIFIED, one search summary only, not repeated by a second query):** on the island during the observing period, 36 storm days (Sturmtage) and 5 hurricane days (Orkantage); 34 days with fog, "a phenomenon extremely rare at Mirny." A second search summary of the same paper said the island's July 1960 mean air temperature was about 1.9 °C colder than Mirny's, with a range that suggested a more continental climate. **Treat all of these as unconfirmed until the paper is read.** If correct, the fog-day count would be the only direct visibility observation found for either island in this unit.

# 6. Bedmap3 ice thickness at both islands (DERIVED, model values, not measurements)

My understanding that thickness is built from radar surveys plus interpolation, and that mask value 1 means grounded ice and 3 floating ice, comes from recall and is **UNVERIFIED**; the survey-count grid is the file's own evidence of where surveys exist. # FINAL

## What the sources support

**Drygalski Island**
1. It is an island of ice about 205 to 220 km², dome 325 to 327 m, sheer ice cliffs 23 to 46 m high, 78 to 85 km from the mainland, resting on a bank of the Davis Sea (the bank statement from an unopened-paper abstract). The REMA cliff-top height from round 1 (median 41 m) agrees with the cliff range.
2. People have been on it: Soviet parties landed in 1956 or 1957 (sources differ), and an East German observer attached to the 5th Soviet Antarctic Expedition wintered there from May to August 1960, running the temporary station "Mir" (the second fact is from an abstract and notices that could not be opened).
3. Monthly mean sea-ice concentration 25 to 45 km off the island is about 75 to 82% in July to September and about 4 to 14% in February (1991-2020, 25 km grids), and was at or above 15% in all 30 years from April to November at every cell computed.
4. There is a direct observation of fog (34 days in 1960 and a storm-day count) attributed to the Skeib paper in one search summary; it is unconfirmed.

**Mill Island**
5. Two Australian papers give the field data for the 2008-2010 cores: summit elevation 503 m; firn-ice transition 83.4 m; borehole −13.86 °C at 19 m; mean accumulation 1.35 m ice equivalent per year (0.79 to 2.04 over 1913-2009), about 1,312 kg/m² of precipitation recently; marine fog and rime over the summit in summer from sea breezes; no in-situ weather observations; ice thickness assumed, not measured, with a maximum of about 400 m on the assumption that the bed is at sea level.
6. The two papers disagree on summer melt on the summit (minimal versus occurring on cloud-free days).
7. An aircraft has landed on Mill Island: a C212 in January 2009 (the shallow-core site at 338 m elevation). The 2009/10 party spent three weeks drilling, dug a 2 m trench and measured the borehole again in 2010/11.
8. Sea-ice cover off Mill Island's north coast (1991-2020 monthly means at two cells 25 to 40 km out): 46 to 72% in January to March and 69 to 80% from May to October.

**Bedmap3 grid (model values, not measurements)**
9a. The grid has no radar-survey count on either dome. Its thickness is 115 to 128 m (median 122 m) at Drygalski, with a stated uncertainty median of 187 m and a model bed up to 208 m above sea level, and 38 to 419 m (median 370 m within 20 km of the centroid; 400 m at the core site) at Mill Island, with an uncertainty median of 195 m and a model bed +110 m at the core site. The Drygalski value conflicts with the Skeib abstract's "bank of the Davis Sea."

**Comparables**
9. The closest documented analogue found for a dome-top landing is Roosevelt Island (an ice dome about 500 m above its shelf): in one season a Basler flew 9 early-season missions and an LC-130 landed once on the ungroomed surface; Twin Otters failed on payload, fuel and rolling fog. No source describes a prepared runway on any island dome.
