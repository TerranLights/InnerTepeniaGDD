**Scale and rhythm (the 2010 city baseline).** SOURCED [MTL-PIARC]:
- 19 boroughs since 2006; population 1,626,000; "4100 km de rues"; "6500 km de trottoirs" (4,100 km of streets, 6,500 km of sidewalks).
- "Budget de déneigement annuel de 136 millions de dollars canadiens" (annual snow budget C$136 million).
- Climate at the airport: snow "225 cm / hiver"; "8 précipitations de neige de 10 cm et plus par hiver" (eight snowfalls of 10 cm or more per winter); about −5 °C during a storm and −10 °C after.
- Per winter, on average: "140 000 tonnes métriques de sel" (140,000 metric tons of salt); "13 500 000 mètres cubes de neige éliminés" (13.5 million m³ of snow disposed of); "65 opérations d'épandage" (65 spreading operations); "15 opérations de déblaiement" (15 plowing operations); "5 à 6 opérations de chargement" (5 to 6 loading operations); "30 sites d'élimination utilisés" (30 disposal sites).
- The four stages: (1) spreading de-icers and abrasives; (2) plowing streets and sidewalks, then "Décider si la neige doit être chargée ou non" (decide whether the snow must be loaded) and, if so, post the parking-restriction signs; (3) loading; (4) disposal.
- Disposal is coordinated centrally by the Cleanliness and Snow Removal Unit, which draws up the disposal strategy, "Réassigner les camions en cas d'urgence" (reassigns trucks in an emergency), builds new disposal sites, writes the road-salt management plan, gathers and analyzes weather data and gives technical support to the boroughs.
- **DERIVED:** C$136,000,000 ÷ 1,626,000 residents ≈ C$84 per resident per year (2010). 13,500,000 m³ ÷ 1,626,000 ≈ 8.3 m³ of snow hauled away per resident per winter.

**Thresholds (current).** SOURCED [MTL-PAGE]: spreading salt and crushed stone "dès que la chaussée et les trottoirs sont glissants" (as soon as roads and sidewalks are slippery); "Déblayage de la neige à partir d'une accumulation de 2,5 cm" (plowing from 2.5 cm); "Chargement de la neige à partir d'une accumulation de 10 à 15 cm" (loading from 10 to 15 cm). Priority order: (1) major arteries, access to health facilities and schools, priority bus routes; (2) collector streets and other bus routes; (3) local streets and industrial areas. Narrow streets: when "une chute de neige de 15 cm ou plus" is forecast, parking is banned on the right side and the left side is reserved for narrow-street permit holders "de 19 h à 9 h." Residents may not push snow into the street, near hydrants or onto the sidewalk.

**A full loading operation.** SOURCED [MTL-2023]: "Près de 3000 personnes et 2200 véhicules de déneigement prendront part aux opérations de chargement" (nearly 3,000 people and 2,200 snow vehicles); the network is "quelque 10 000 km de rues, trottoirs et pistes cyclables" (about 10,000 km of streets, sidewalks and bike paths; note this total mixes the 4,100 km and 6,500 km lines of the 2010 baseline with bike paths); start "à partir de mardi 5 décembre à 7 h," rolling out borough by borough; arteries, transit lanes and bus routes first; "7971 places de stationnement incitatif gratuit, majoritairement disponibles la nuit" (7,971 free parking spaces, mostly at night) for residents displaced by the bans; "chaque remorquage ralentit les opérations d'une dizaine de minutes" (each tow slows the operation by about ten minutes); residents asked to park "à 30 cm" from the curb with mirrors folded.

**Cost.** SOURCED [NOOVO-2023]: 2023 to 2024 snow budget "estimé à 197 millions $," about C$10 million more than the year before; "environ 1 million $ par centimètre de neige enlevée" (about C$1 million per centimeter of snow removed); the article says no other Canadian city spends as much on snow and gives, for comparison as published (not computed here): Toronto "plus de 100 millions $ par an," Winnipeg C$36.26 million budgeted for 2023 against C$87.18 million actually spent in 2022. **UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia citing the city's 2021 budget highlights, not opened):** cost above C$170 million, about 3% of the city budget, in 2021. **DERIVED:** C$197 million ÷ 225 cm ≈ C$0.88 million per cm of average snowfall, consistent with the article's "about 1 million per cm" (uses the 2010 snowfall normal; the two figures are from different years).

**Fleet and crews (2026).** SOURCED [MTL-2026] (15 October 2026): C$15.8 million in new snow investment, of which C$13.3 million renews the fleet with 43 machines ("19 tracteurs-chargeurs," "13 têtes de souffleuse," "11 camions épandeurs": 19 loaders, 13 snowblower heads, 11 spreader trucks); C$2.5 million more transferred to boroughs for "plus de 16 équipements additionnels" for salting; "8 nouvelles équipes de cols bleus" (8 new blue-collar crews); two new "expertise hubs" from 1 January 2027; a tracking tool, Go-Géo, rolled out during the winter. The release gives no total budget, headcount or contractor share.

**Where the snow goes.** SOURCED [MTL-PIARC]:
- 30 disposal sites: "16 chutes à l'égout (30% de l'élimination)" (16 sewer chutes, 30% of disposal); "1 carrière (20%)" (one quarry, 20%); "13 sites de surface (50%)" (13 surface sites, 50%). "Aucun site de déversement au cours d'eau" (no dumping into watercourses); "Aucune fondeuse à neige (mobile ou fixe)" (no snow melters, mobile or fixed).
- A sewer chute: snow is tipped into an opening at pavement level, drops down a shaft into a collector or interceptor carrying "un débit d'eau usée d'au moins 1,5 mètre cube par seconde" (at least 1.5 m³/s of wastewater). North interceptor: 14 m³/s, 5 m diameter, pumped from 35 m below ground, "3100 mètres cubes de neige / heure durant le jour et de 2400 mètres cubes / heure durant la nuit." South interceptor: 16 m³/s, 5.5 m diameter, 45 m deep, 7,800 m³ of snow per hour by day and 6,100 by night. Condition: water must reach the treatment plant at "une température minimale de 2°C" (at least 2 °C). (The lower night capacity is stated without a reason; UNVERIFIED that it reflects lower night sewage flow.)
- Saint-Michel quarry: acquired 1981 and used for snow since; trucks tip snow from the top of a cliff "d'une hauteur d'environ 65 mètres" (about 65 m); 13 tipping platforms, 3 cantilevered; a continuous 105 m surface along the cliff edge; an emergency stacking area at the bottom; capacity "environ 5 millions de mètres cubes."
- Surface sites: "Capacité entre 50 000 et 1 700 000 m3."
- Every disposal site needs a provincial environment ministry certificate of authorization: melt water drained and treated before discharge, no soil contamination, "Aucune nuisance aux citoyens," annual sampling of melt water. C$20 million for new chutes, site corrections and a continuous quay at the quarry.
- **What fails (the city's own words):** the sites "suffisent pour éliminer la neige provenant d'un peu plus de 50% des hivers" (are enough for the snow of just over half of winters); "les chutes à l'égout, avec leur capacité horaire, sont moins fiables" (sewer chutes, limited by their hourly capacity, are less reliable); the city needs to cover 75% of winters and to "remplacer des sites qui seront perdus au profit du développement" (replace sites lost to highway, industrial and commercial development), about C$40 million. Conclusion slide: disposal "semble une activité de second rang" (looks like a second-rank activity) but "peut grandement influencer la vitesse des opérations de chargement" (can greatly affect the speed of loading).
- SOURCED [MM-2023] (14 March 2023): the Saint-Michel depot "peut recevoir environ six millions de mètres cubes de neige" and "est actuellement rempli à 85 %"; the former Francon quarry "stocke près de 40 % de la neige de la métropole"; eight snowfall events that winter, twice the normal; gusts above 50 km/h are dangerous for trucks tipping at the dump edge; mechanical shovels cut into the top of the pile to make room before spring storms. **Conflict logged:** 5 million m³ and 20% of disposal (city, 2010) against about 6 million m³ and about 40% (MétéoMédia, 2023).
- **UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia, its citations not opened):** 15 sewer chutes and 11 snow depots; chutes take 25% of the snow; a man-made glacier often survives the summer at the quarry; loads are checked at dumps by camera and GPS; until the 1980s most snow was dumped into the St. Lawrence River from wharves.

**Labor and integrity (Montreal-specific; the general treatment is in item 2).**
- SOURCED [CSE-1999]: in 1999 the City of Montreal had "700 cadres et 12 801 employés syndiqués"; CUPE Local 301 represented 4,213 blue-collar employees (3,804 permanent, 409 auxiliary), with an employment floor of 3,580 permanent jobs, so numbers were falling by attrition. "Le déneigement est fait à 50 % par les cols bleus et à 50 % par l'entreprise privée" (snow removal was done 50% by blue-collar city workers and 50% by private firms). Spreading abrasives and de-icers on sidewalks and streets was done by city crews "pour tout le territoire de la ville"; crews also clear and thaw "12 950 bornes-fontaines" (12,950 hydrants). "Lors de l'opération neige, les cols bleus de ce service qui n'exécutent pas d'autres tâches essentielles sont dégagés de leur travail habituel et sont affectés aux opérations de déneigement" (during a snow operation, every blue-collar worker of the department not doing another essential task is taken off normal work and assigned to snow). A further 427 permanent and 7 auxiliary blue-collar workers in the supply department maintained and repaired the snow, de-icing and spreading equipment.
- SOURCED [CSE-1999], the incident: on 2 February 1999 several hundred blue-collar workers left work for a sympathy demonstration; the 3 a.m. bulletin forecast light snow (1 to 2.5 cm) with freezing rain from 8 a.m., but freezing rain began at about 6:55 to 7:00 a.m.; supervisors had a spreading guide that sets the share of crews and machines to send out by forecast, and for that forecast it called for "la sortie de 100 % des effectifs et des équipements"; crews had been kept on overtime overnight to prepare spreaders and tracked sidewalk machines (local nickname logged in the slang note); most crews returned only by about 10 a.m., and "le retard pris allait prendre un certain temps pour être comblé, avec ses inévitables conséquences pour la population." The Council found an illegal strike. The remedy the union undertook: unpaid community work by "1 000 salariés cols bleus pour une durée de six (6) heures consécutives," 900 on the work and 100 coordinating, with the city naming "un contremaître par 25 salariés cols bleus, soit 40 contremaîtres" (one foreman per 25 workers, 40 foremen).
- SOURCED [Noovo Info, 4 February 2026, https://www.noovo.info/nouvelles/societe/article/les-cols-bleus-de-montreal-sont-en-greve-mercredi-avec-des-services-essentiels/]: CUPE Local 301 (FTQ), "about 6,000" blue-collar workers, struck for 24 hours (6:00 a.m. Wednesday to 5:59 a.m. Thursday) over wages (the union rejected an 11% offer and sought a 7% catch-up; it also cited a lack of budget and of equipment in good condition); collective agreement expired 31 December 2024. Snow was an essential service under a labor tribunal order: salting when 1 cm or more is forecast; clearing of sidewalks and roads with grades of 7% or more from 2.5 cm; loading only from 17.5 cm of accumulation. Garbage collection was suspended, the tribunal ruling that this did not threaten public health or safety.

**The road network and its service standards.** SOURCED [SPK-WS]:
- Plowed roadway length (about, km): main arterials 90; arterials 390; secondary arterials 1,710 (arterial total 2,190); residential roads ("生活道路") 3,270; total 5,460. Of about 5,546 km of managed road, about 86 km are not plowed because they are too narrow for machines.
- Targets for packed-snow thickness and usable width (excluding abnormal weather): main arterials within 3 cm, four lanes or more; arterials within 5 cm, three lanes or more; secondary arterials within 25 cm, 1.5 lanes or more; residential roads within 30 cm, "1車線+歩行空間以上" (one lane plus walking space or more). **Note:** on residential roads the city's own target is a packed-snow surface up to 30 cm thick, not bare pavement.
- New-snow plowing ("新雪除雪") is done "０～６時ごろ" (about midnight to 6 a.m.), to finish before the commute and avoid jams and accidents; dispatch when, in near-continuous snowfall, depth exceeds 10 cm and traffic or walking is expected to be hampered. Snow is pushed toward the sidewalk side on arterials and toward sidewalks and houses on residential roads; sidewalk crews pile it toward the roadway. A typical arterial crew: two graders and two loaders ("グレーダ２台・ショベル２台," for two-to-four-lane roads); a loader on residential roads; a rotary blower on sidewalks. Sidewalk clearing covers only sidewalks with at least 2.0 m usable width in busy areas such as JR and subway stations and public facilities.
- Other operations: surface leveling ("路面整正") when ruts and slush form, shaving the surface and pushing the spoil to the roadside (with snow pushed into house entrances reduced); widening ("拡幅除雪") when roads narrow, cutting the foot of the roadside snow banks and piling it on top.
- Haul-away ("運搬排雪"), done by the city: on arterials and some school routes, "原則シーズン１回" (in principle once a season), bus routes one or two extra times, intersections as needed; early January to early February; snow banks loaded into dump trucks and carried to snow-disposal sites. Residential-road haul-away is done jointly with residents (see below).

**Season counts and costs (fiscal 2024), SOURCED [SPK-WS]:**
- Arterials: new-snow plowing about 20 times, about ¥1.1 billion; widening about 5 times, about ¥0.5 billion; sidewalk plowing about 18 times, about ¥0.9 billion (plowing total about ¥2.5 billion); planned haul-away once, about ¥4.0 billion. "対象路線全線を１回排雪するには、約30日を要する" (one haul-away of every target route takes about 30 days).
- Residential roads: new-snow plowing about 16 times, about ¥1.1 billion; surface leveling about 3 times, about ¥0.8 billion ("１回実施するには３日程度の日数が必要," each round takes about three days); widening about twice, about ¥0.2 billion (plowing total about ¥2.1 billion); partnership haul-away once, about ¥3.7 billion city share plus about ¥1.0 billion community share; one pass over all applied-for routes takes about 30 days.
- Comparison on an arterial: haul-away needs "約4倍の人員" (about four times the crew) of plowing per crew, and "約64倍の費用" (about 64 times the cost) per km.

### 2a. Politics: mayors judged by snow

- SOURCED [Huma Khan and Devin Dwyer, "Do Mayors' Political Fates Hang in the Outcome of Snowstorms?," ABC News, 11 February 2010, https://abcnews.com/amp/Politics/politics-snow-washington-dc-mayor-adrian-fenty-spotlight/story?id=9809564]: Chicago Mayor Michael Bilandic "was handed most of the blame for the mishandling of the blizzard" of January 1979 and lost the following primary though he "had long been expected to win"; political scientist Andrew McFarland: "A mayor is expected to do this [efficient snow removal] and see that it's done, and if he doesn't, it's expected that he will be in real political trouble in the next election," and "To the extent that you can point to one cause for a candidate losing, that would have been the one cause." New York Mayor John Lindsay "faced major backlash for giving Manhattan the preferred treatment and abandoning Queens in the snow-removal process" (1969); he was reelected. Historian Vincent Cannato: "The thing about snow, it is symbolic," and the 1969 storm "kind of capsulated a lot of people's belief that the city in general wasn't working." Counter-examples in the same article: Washington, D.C., Mayor Marion Barry, away at the Super Bowl during a 1987 storm ("We're not a snow town"); Boston Mayor Kevin White was on vacation in 1978, yet "a majority of Bostonians approved of the way he handled the situation." The article gives no statistical evidence that snow decides elections; its case rests on examples and expert opinion.
- SOURCED [CBS Chicago, 13 January 2022, https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/chicago-blizzard-1979-43rd-anniversary/]: the official total for 12 to 14 January 1979 was 18.8 inches, later revised to 20.3 inches (about 52 cm), on 7 to 10 inches already on the ground; it was not a record (23.0 inches in 1967). Failures: the Red Line shut north of Belmont, the Purple and Yellow lines shut altogether; express trains bypassed West Side Black neighborhoods, angering community leaders; garbage piled up; some parked cars stayed stuck "for the rest of the winter"; fire equipment struggled to reach a fire on an "unplowed side street" (one dead, 20 injured); many roads still not clear by 18 January. Columnist Mike Royko argued city crews' skills were built around Election Day vote-getting. Jane Byrne made the snow central to her campaign and won the primary "in a landslide," then the general election; Bilandic later said, "We all learn from our mistakes. I've made them, and I freely admit it."
- **UNVERIFIED (search summary, not opened):** Bilandic himself argued the storm, not his administration's response, cost him the election; a transit-fare dispute was another factor. Denver's 1982 Christmas blizzard and Mayor William McNichols's 1983 defeat: NOT FOUND in this pass (died at the query; no result addressed it).

### 2b. The people: status, pay, shortage

**North America, public-agency plow operators.** SOURCED [AASHTO Journal, 25 February 2022, https://aashtojournal.transportation.org/?p=21086]: an AASHTO survey run in January and February 2022 drew "responses from 31 state departments of transportation and 51 public works agencies"; "84 percent of respondents indicated they are experiencing higher than normal vacancies of snowplow operators"; "81 percent of those higher-than-expected vacancies were due to a lack of full-time employees"; of the rest, 44% reported trouble finding seasonal or temporary employees and 31% trouble securing private contractors; 25% reduced their winter level-of-service targets and 6% more were considering it; 15% used tools such as "tow-plows," which clear two lanes at once; some state DOTs offered "mission-critical" incentive pay, stipends and in-house training for the commercial driver's license (CDL), walk-in interviews and social-media advertising. Respondents believed (the article calls it anecdotal) that many CDL holders leave for higher-paid private jobs. **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** Connecticut DOT said drivers can work up to 17 hours straight, enough for a short storm, with outside contractors needed for longer events; officials said they compete with school-bus and delivery employers for the same CDL holders. **New York City: a sanitation department that becomes a snow department, plus casual labor.**

**Japan: contractors paid by the hour of work, in a climate where work is uneven.** SOURCED [Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT), maintenance subcommittee material 4, "道路除雪工における積算方法の改善" (improving cost estimation for road snow clearing), 15 pages, PDF created 16 December 2020, hosted by the National Institute for Land and Infrastructure Management, https://www.nilim.go.jp/lab/peg/img/file1691.pdf; read by local text extraction]:

National direct works had no rule for fixed costs.

### 3i. Infrastructure that is buried every winter and dug out every spring: the Tateyama Kurobe road

- SOURCED [Tetsudo Channel, 25 March 2023, https://tetsudo-ch.com/12877858.html]: clearing of the alpine bus road runs "藤橋周辺(標高475m)から室堂(標高2,450m)まで、総延長31.3km" (31.3 km from around Fujihashi at 475 m to Murodo at 2,450 m); in 2023 work began on 7 February; by 20 March one lane was open to Mimatsu (2,030 m) and two lanes from Midagahara (1,930 m) to Mimatsu; snow at Murodo was 560 cm (760 cm a year earlier); the route opened on 15 April and runs to 30 November.
- SOURCED [Impress *Digital Camera Watch*, 14 February 2025, https://dc.watch.impress.co.jp/docs/news/1662818.html]: clearing began in early February near Tateyama Station and takes "約2カ月" (about two months) to reach Murodo; the "Otani" snow walls are what the cleared road leaves standing; snow depth "740cm" (at Murodo) on 10 February 2025; progress is posted online from March.
- **UNVERIFIED (a 2018 personal blog, quoted in the search summary, not opened):** before about 2000 the cleared road was only one bus wide; after GPS was fitted to the snow machines the buried road could be found without marker poles, to the centimeter, and two lanes could be cut; the Otani section takes about a month.
- **DERIVED:** 31.3 km in roughly 60 to 67 days (7 February to mid-April) ≈ 0.5 km of road recovered per day, as a whole-route average (the true rate varies with depth and lane count).
- **NOT FOUND:** crew numbers, machine counts and cost (died at the sources).

### 3j. The same cycle on a drift-driven road: Going-to-the-Sun Road, Glacier National Park

- SOURCED [Micah Drew, "A Monumental Task," *Flathead Beacon*, 14 June 2021, https://flatheadbeacon.com/?p=94542]: the west-side plow crew is "a 12 person crew, which includes two USGS avalanche technicians and a heavy mechanic"; dozers cut the snowpack down to about four feet, the height rotary plows can take; excavators and small loaders take the deepest drifts; the whole job takes "roughly three months"; Logan Pass can lie under "up to 80 feet of snow" (about 24 m); in heavy years progress can slow to "a quarter mile a week" (about 0.4 km); every morning forecasters check overnight temperatures, dig snow pits along the Garden Wall and read remote sensors and weather stations, and "any sign of loosening snow" ends work for the day; operators must protect guardrails and historic rock walls; history: in 1929 a crew of six worked 10-hour shifts with a newly bought 3/4-yard gasoline shovel; in 1933 drifts were 30 feet high and the shovel ran double shifts.
- SOURCED [SnowBrains, 21 May 2026, https://snowbrains.com/going-to-the-sun-road-2026-big-drift/]: the Big Drift is "a one-mile stretch" just east of Logan Pass where wind-driven snow builds to "50 to 80 feet each winter" (about 15 to 24 m); spring plowing began 1 April 2026; east and west crews meet at the drift; that winter an avalanche buried the restroom building at Big Bend, and about 135 feet of rock wall was lost at The Slopes; "The average opening date for vehicles to travel the full 50-mile corridor is June 11"; the road "has not opened to vehicles in May since 2005."
- **UNVERIFIED (search summaries):** a crew usually takes about a week to get through the Big Drift (70 to 90 feet in 2017); in 2024 crews dug the drift from the top down because it cannot be plowed from the bottom.
- **Point of fact for this unit:** here the deepest accumulation is wind-made (drift), not snowfall-made, and it sits in one known place every year.
