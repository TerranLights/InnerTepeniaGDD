## Pick 1 · Subak, Bali (Indonesia)
*Care note from the input file: founder nation, structure only. This section records structure (how the institutions work, who does what, which rules bind) and the physical and social settings, not national character.*

### A. Other settings (sub-areas and spaces of daily life)
- **The subak's own buildings and shrines, by level:** farm-plot bamboo shrines (sanggah catu) at each field; Pura Bedugul at the water-diversion point; Pura Ulun Swi (or Ulun Empelan) at the original dam; lake temples above. A subak also keeps a meeting pavilion (bale subak), which members are obliged to build, maintain and repair together with the subak temple, paths and fences. [K1, K10] [P3] — SOURCED: shrine hierarchy, E. Speirs, "Explaining Bali's Subak System," NOW! Bali, 14 Jul 2021 (https://www.nowbali.co.id/balis-subak-system-irrigation/, read 2026-10-29); bale subak duty, N.A. Naris, "Penerapan Subak dalam Institusi Sosial Petani Transmigran Bali," *Jurnal Sosialisasi* 9(3), Nov 2022, pp.64–75 (https://ojs.unm.ac.id/sosialisasi/article/download/38437/18458, PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29), p.70; temple names also in I.G.A.P. Eryani, *Pengelolaan Air Subak untuk Konservasi Air dan Lahan*, Jayapangus Press, Denpasar, 2020 (http://book.penerbit.org/index.php/JPB/article/download/461/456, read 2026-10-29), p.11.
- **Boundaries are hydrological, not administrative:** a subak's edge is "where the flowing water can no longer reach a field" (blocked by river, ravine, canal or village land), so subak areas overlap village boundaries; several subaks fed from one division structure usually form a coordinating body (subak-gde) "to make it easier to coordinate the mutual lending of irrigation water." The subak is autonomous and "not under the village government," but coordinates with the village "especially in carrying out rituals." [K1, K4] [P5] — SOURCED: W. Windia, Sumiyati and G. Sedana, "Aspek Ritual pada Sistem Irigasi Subak sebagai Warisan Budaya Dunia," *Jurnal Kajian Bali* 5(1), April 2015, pp.23–38 (https://ejournal1.unud.ac.id/index.php/kajianbali/article/download/5611/3192, PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29), pp.24–26 (translated).
- **The village hall alongside the subak (the banjar, a separate institution):** each banjar adat (customary hamlet association) has a Bale Banjar: a semi-open building with a shrine (Pura Pengulun Banjar), a meeting floor, a pavilion for ritual equipment, a wooden slit-drum tower (bale kulkul) "for community communication," a kitchen and storage. In three Kuta banjars studied in 2025, tourism-era meetings had moved to every two weeks; "economic discussions (management of tourism levies and homestays) dominate up to 60%" of sessions; LPG kitchens and served food extend attendance "30–40% per session"; the slit-drum persists as the calling signal alongside banjar WhatsApp groups. [K10, K4, K9] [P4] — SOURCED: N.M.M. Mahastuti, N.R.P. Salain, N.M.A.M.B. Zakharia, "Bale Banjar as a Space for Deliberation and the Preservation of Balinese Culture in Kuta," *Architectural Research Journal* 5(2), 2025, pp.85–89 (https://www.ejournal.warmadewa.ac.id/index.php/arj/article/download/14214/7183, PDF read 2026-10-29).
- **The wantilan:** the largest Balinese pavilion type, wall-less under a multi-tiered roof, "usually located at a village's main square or main junction," used for communal meetings, sports, dance classes, gamelan performances, and (in temple outer courts) ritual cockfights. [K10] [P3] — SOURCED: Wikipedia "Wantilan" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Off-season:** Bali's paddy year has no closed season in the visitor sense; the subak year is set by planting turns and the ritual cycle (see E). An off-season in the sense of access closing: none found.

### B. Subcultures
- **Occupational work groups inside a subak (the claim's "structure"):** officers besides the head: a secretary, a treasurer, a messenger or announcer, and a temple priest (pemangku) for ritual matters. Work groups (sekaa) by task: soil working; water handling; a watch group "tasked with guarding water from theft" and driving off birds and rats; transplanting; weeding; reaping; and carrying the cut sheaves to the granary. [K1, K7, K14] — SOURCED (Eryani 2020, pp.9–10, citing Sutawan 2008). A three-way membership division (those who work the fields; those who fund farming; periphery including temple priests): SOURCED on NOW! Bali (2021) only.
- **Exempt members:** in the transmigrant subak studied (below), members are either active labor members or exempt members (the subak priest and officers); officers pay only the temple-festival dues; the head is exempt from festival dues, compulsory levies and communal work. [K1, K6] — SOURCED (Naris 2022, p.70).
- **Generational:** in the World Heritage subak area of Tampaksiring (Gianyar), the average farmer age was 62.59 years (range 40–77) (Sarita 2013, cited); agriculture's share of Bali's regional GDP fell from 59.07% (1971) to 19.98% (2003) and 17% (2013); food-crop farmers' terms-of-trade index was below 100, "meaning … in a loss-making condition"; the authors expect aging and falling income to reduce ritual activity. [K9, K7] — SOURCED (Windia et al. 2015, p.33, citing Bank Indonesia 2014).
- **Migrant subak (Balinese transmigrants, West Sulawesi):** at Desa Tommo (Mamuju Regency, West Sulawesi), 87 household members keep a subak organized around a Pura Ulun Suwi, though fields are rain-fed and need no water-sharing rules. Officers: head, secretary (keeps the written code and attendance), treasurer, a ritual section (prepares offerings), a building section (bridges, culverts, fences, temple cleanliness). Officers serve "according to ability," whereas in Bali officers are elected every five years. Planting outside the auspicious window: "in Bali … it may not pass 14 days; if it passes 14 days it is fined by the subak"; in Tommo planting can run two months late because of rain. Four subak officers were also schoolteachers, one a primary-school head. "Most sanctions are usually in money." [K4, K6, K7] [P9] — SOURCED (Naris 2022, pp.67–71, translated).
- **Religious and ritual specialists:** temple priests (pemangku) at subak level; regional and lake temples run by others, to which subaks only contribute dues (see D). — SOURCED (Windia et al. 2015, p.33).
- **Gender:** meetings of the hamlet association (banjar) are attended by married men; wives have a say "only indirectly … through her husband." — UNVERIFIED (search summary of general guide pages; not opened). In the transmigrant subak, the start ritual is held only once there is agreement "on the readiness of the wives to prepare the ritual." — SOURCED (Naris 2022, p.68). A documented women's duty roster for subak ritual or work in Bali itself: NOT FOUND.
- **Speech (K11, loose):** Balinese and Indonesian; Balinese in subak meetings and ritual. — general (a 2024 Warmadewa community-service page titled "Balinese language conservation in kesubakan activities" was seen in search only, UNVERIFIED).

### D. Third places
- **Bale subak, subak temple, bale banjar, wantilan** (see A). [K10]
- **Contributions without organizing:** for festivals at temples tied to water sources (Pura Ulun Danu Batur; Pura Beratan at Lake Beratan), "the subak only gives dues, not as the organizer." [K8] — SOURCED (Windia et al. 2015, p.33).
- **Temple upkeep under shrinking land:** conversion of farmland "very fast (average 750 ha/year)" shrinks subak areas, so dues "to care for and worship at" subak temples fall; "many subak temples appear neglected"; friction arises over whether the village should take them over. [K8, K9] — SOURCED (Windia et al. 2015, p.26).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **The individual ritual cycle (16 rites, per field, by crop stage):** at first going down to the field; after seeding the nursery; at planting; after planting if plants are damaged; at 12 days; 15 days; one month (35 days); 42 days; two months (70 days); when flowering is even across the terraces; before harvest (the rite for the rice goddess); at harvest; after harvest (thanks and readiness for the next season); when rice is stored in the granary; when pests or disease strike (pest-warding rite); and when going to fetch water at its source. [K2, K7] [P4] — SOURCED (Windia et al. 2015, Table 1, pp.31–32, citing Sutawan 2008).
- **Subak-level rites, all members together "on a day agreed by the subak":** (i) the fetching-the-water rite at the subak's source (dam, division structure or spring); (ii) the temple anniversary festival at the subak temple. [K2, K1] — SOURCED (Windia et al. 2015, pp.32–33). Communal channel cleaning is "generally fitted to" the water-fetching ceremony. [K7] — SOURCED (Eryani 2020, p.30).
- **Season meetings set the calendar:** subak meetings held "routinely before the planting season" set the auspicious day (from the Balinese calendar) to begin soil work and planting, sometimes the crop to be planted, and the communal work on channels. [K1, K7] — SOURCED (Eryani 2020, p.30). The rules: written code (awig-awig); long-standing customs functioning as conventions; and unwritten decisions of meetings (perarem). [K6] — SOURCED (Eryani 2020, p.29).
- **Water proportional to dues and labor:** "irrigation water obtained by farmer-members is proportional to the dues paid and the labor that must be provided"; mutual lending of water is customary. [K1, K8] — SOURCED (Eryani 2020, p.28).
- **A festival created on purpose (K3):** the Subak Spirit Festival, held 9 November 2024 at the Subak Monument, Jatiluwih, led by Indonesia's Ministry of Culture (opened by the deputy minister) with the Bali provincial cultural office; farm-produce exhibitions, small-business food and souvenir stalls, a sacred harvest dance, a concert, traditional bamboo instrument demonstrations, and launch of a web portal. [K3, K8] — SOURCED: tatkala.co, 9 Nov 2024 (read 2026-10-29). Whether it is annual: NOT FOUND.
- **Provincial grants (K8):** under Governor I Wayan Koster, Bali province grants Rp 10 million per subak per year, administered by the Department for Advancement of Indigenous Communities; 526 subaks in Buleleng Regency received it in 2023 (504 in villages, 22 in urban wards), 26 more than in 2022; funds are for temple, community and environmental purposes. [K8] — SOURCED: tatkala.co, 14 Feb 2023 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Present-day counts (open thread from Round 1):** 1,596 subaks (Unud LPPM Subak Unit data, 2023); elsewhere 2,711 in total, of which 1,604 wet-rice subaks and 1,107 dry-field subaks. — UNVERIFIED (search summary only; the underlying pages were not opened). Membership island-wide: NOT FOUND.

### K6 · Rules of everyday behavior
- **Subak sanctions (open thread from Round 1):** violations of irrigation-water use are punished by "payment of a fine at each members' meeting," and a repeat offender "can be expelled from subak membership"; channels are kept clean by communal work against household waste. [K6] — SOURCED (Eryani 2020, p.34). Members who break duties such as communal work are sanctioned by the subak; enforcement by the subak head; "most sanctions are usually in money." — SOURCED (Naris 2022, pp.67–68). Rupiah amounts in any named subak's code: NOT FOUND (see F).
- **Hamlet-level sanctions (banjar, not subak; recorded because the same written-code system governs daily life around the fields):** a Badung hamlet's code (article 63) lists sanctions as: penalty labor (communal work); money or goods fines; fines in produce; loss of the house plot held from the village; ostracism ("not spoken to," excluded from village membership); and a purification ceremony at the three village temples, paid for by the offender. Light offenses such as "not attending communal work or meetings" draw money fines; staged process: advice, warnings, fine, apology, then exclusion from notices ("no slit-drum call," no village services). Ostracism is imposed by the hamlet head after a meeting; the author notes it lacks procedure and is "often imposed without giving the person a chance to defend himself." [K6, K9] — SOURCED: A.A.M.A.T. Dewi, "Penerapan Sanksi Adat Kasepekang di Banjar Tegal Gundul, Tibubeneng Kabupaten Badung," *Jurnal Komunikasi Hukum* 8(1), Feb 2022, pp.28–40 (https://ejournal.undiksha.ac.id/index.php/jkh/article/download/43871/20913/109197, PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29), pp.30, 34–36 (translated).
- **The island-wide Day of Silence (Nyepi):** on 1 Kedasa of the Saka calendar, from 6 a.m. to 6 a.m., four prohibitions: no fire or light, no work, no travel, no entertainment; customary village guards (pecalang) patrol; exceptions for emergency vehicles and women in labor; the day before, youth groups build and parade effigies that are burned; the day after, families gather to ask forgiveness. — SOURCED: Wikipedia "Nyepi" (read 2026-10-29). In 2025 the national ministry suspended mobile telecommunications, internet and TV/radio broadcasting "from 6 a.m. until 6 a.m. the next day" at the provincial government's request. — SOURCED: Antara News, 20 Mar 2025 (read 2026-10-29). Ngurah Rai airport closed 24 hours: UNVERIFIED (Antara headline in search only). [K6, K2]

## Pick 2 · Aflaj of Oman
*Main new source this round: S. Bandyopadhyay and B. Mershen, "Falaj Communities in Oman: A Case for Local Governance? Ibāḍī Legal Rulings and Spatial and Ethnohistorical Observations," *Journal of Material Cultures in the Muslim World* 3 (2022): 6–47, doi:10.1163/26666286-12340028, open access (https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3169173/1/26666286_003_01_s002_text.pdf, PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29), cited below as "B&M 2022." Its three case studies are Birkat al-Mawz, al-Hamra and Misfat al-Abriyin. Transliterated institutional terms are kept only where they name an office, a building or a legal zone.*

### A. Other settings
- **The protected strip along the channel (harim al-falaj):** a legal "zone of protection" (300 to 500 cubits in the legal compilations cited) inside which owners are protected "from illegal tapping of water or encroachment of buildings" and keep "sufficient space for cleaning the water channels"; it is left unfenced so that "legitimate public access for domestic purposes is assured" and the supply stays "open to inspection" (quoting Wilkinson 1978). Unauthorized new channels, private basins and wells that could reduce flow "were strictly avoided." [K1, K6] [P5, P7] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, pp.26–27).
- **The civic strip that grows along the water:** in Birkat al-Mawz the surfacing point, the mosque and, farther downstream, "the women's bathing and prayer enclosure" form the civic core; in one quarter the channel became "a narrow passage accommodating several communal bathing enclosures, including for women," plus at least three men's meeting halls, two of them belonging to named tribal groups and built into the upper floors of dwellings. In Misfat al-Abriyin, along the main channel: first the drinking-water collection point, then the women's bathing and washing facilities; two small men's ablution and bathing rooms built over the channel below the storage basin; a madrasa, the main mosque, a meeting hall and the original souq at the old village entrance; a room for "washing the deceased." [K4, K10] [P3, P5] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, pp.27–29, 35–36).
- **Women at the channel head (Hamra oasis, 1970s fieldwork):** "For most of the day, the head of the Falaj is reserved for women"; women fetch water, wash pans, pots and clothes, and socialize along the channel; houses have separate entrances and separate guest rooms for male and female guests. Status differences: women of the shaykh's families "do their washing and chores more privately than women of the lower classes"; non-shaykhly women run small trades (sewing, embroidery, rosewater) that are not acceptable for shaykhly women. [K4, K6, K7] — SOURCED on a review: "Women and Community in Oman," *Al-Raida* no.57 (Spring 1992), p.22–23 (https://alraidajournal.lau.edu.lb/images/issue057-page022.pdf, read 2026-10-29), reviewing Christine Eickelman, *Women and Community in Oman* (New York University Press, 1984; book itself not opened).
- **News at the water and the mosque:** women gathering at the falaj to wash "exchange news"; men meet at the five daily prayers; "A cannon or rifle would be fired to convey urgent information such as fires and deaths." [K4, K10] — SOURCED: R. Al Kindi, "From sablah to written letters," Oman Observer, 20 Jan 2025 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Depopulated old quarters:** at Misfat al-Abriyin, "Residents began moving to a mountain across the valley in the 1990s," with only five original families living permanently in the old village by 2015; others return for Eid. [K4, K9] — SOURCED: A. Zacharias, "An Omani village reunited for Eid," The National, 27 Sep 2015 (read 2026-10-29).

### B. Subcultures
- **Falaj office-holders (beyond Round 1):** the administrator is "chosen by the shareholders" and can consult the main shareholders for important decisions; larger systems add a bookkeeper who records changes in ownership by inheritance, sale or lease; one or two technical overseers (one for tunnels, one for open channels); an auctioneer; and in some communities "an official stargazer." Officials are "financially compensated … usually from the proceeds of auctioning falaj shares." [K1, K14] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, p.12–13, citing Wilkinson 1977, Al-Ghafiri et al. 2003, Nash 2007).
- **Field and irrigation laborers:** farming is done "by both male and female members of the farmer's family or by farm workers … also responsible for irrigation activities, who are usually paid in kind." [K7] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, p.13). On historical slavery (neutral): enslaved labor grew in importance in the 19th century as dates became a cash crop exported to the USA, but according to the sources cited this applied "almost exclusively" to the Batinah coastal plain with its labor-intensive well irrigation, "rather than the falaj communities of the interior." [K9] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, note 7).
- **Shareholders who are not residents:** "Water rights mostly are not connected to land titles, nor are they confined to falaj community residents"; owners may live in other villages or abroad; legal rulings say owners living overseas must still pay part of repair costs. [K1, K8] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, pp.12, 32).
- **Hydro-tribalism:** a 2025 University of Nizwa project describes the falaj as "a unifying force, facilitating intertribal cooperation through shared water resources — a structure the researchers term 'hydro-tribalism'"; funded by the Ministry of Higher Education, Research and Innovation. [K1, K4] — SOURCED: Oman Observer, 23 Jun 2025 (https://www.omanobserver.om/ampArticle/1172404, read 2026-10-29). The Al-Wasit details remain UNVERIFIED (see F).
- **Youth volunteers reviving dry channels:** in the wilayat of Manah (rendered "Munh" by the translation), a group of young men began restoring the long-dry Falaj al-Faraskhi (over 3 km of tunnel, up to 13 m deep); the article reproduces two water-share sale deeds dated 1183–1193 AH (1769–1778 CE) priced in silver coin. [K14, K1] — SOURCED: M. bin S. al-Hadhrami, Oman Daily (Arabic), 6 Apr 2024 (read 2026-10-29, via translation by the fetch tool). At al-Khali village (Wadi Fada, Dhank), residents cleared silt, rocks and dead wood when the falaj ran again after 27 dry years. — SOURCED: Al Roya (Arabic), dated 4 Oct 2026 on the page (read 2026-10-29).
- **Generational:** in Wadi Bani Kharus (al-Hijir), the current administrator keeps the share schedule from memory: "All of this is registered in my head; nothing is written." [K1, K7] — SOURCED: Oman Observer, 14 Oct 2023 (https://www.omanobserver.om/ampArticle/1144205, read 2026-10-29).
- **Speech (K11, loose):** Arabic (Omani dialects). — general.

### D. Third places
- **The village meeting hall (sablah; also called majlis or barzah):** listed in Oman's national intangible-heritage inventory (compiled 2010 by the Ministry of Heritage and Culture with the Public Authority for Craft Industries, Ministry of Social Development, Ministry of Sport Affairs, Sultan Qaboos University, the Association of Omani Women and others). "An independent building or a place where there is shade and water," in the middle of the neighborhood, belonging "to all people of the village"; formerly "a headquarter of the administration where people meet in their sorrows and joys"; a destination for guests "who have no relatives or acquaintances in the village." Dates, water and coffee utensils are always kept there; food is not cooked there but brought from homes in turn: by the first person to meet the guest, by a daily house rota, or "the turns are exchanged according to the rotation of Falaj in some places." Funding: the state treasury in big towns, an endowment "as … in the mosques," day rental, villagers' contributions, charity, or income property (shops, buildings); villagers collect donations if income falls short. Historically at most two per village, built of mud, pebbles and palm trunks. Men met there after work until dinner; men sometimes did palm-frond craft work together in front of it; a group of men roasted and ground coffee for villagers and guests, and a youth passed with water for hand-washing. Practitioners: "inhabitants of the same village (men)." [K10, K8, K4, K12] [R] — SOURCED: Oman national inventory submission to UNESCO, received 1 Feb 2015 (https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/30396.pdf, PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29), entry on the sablah. Every Omani house also "has a designated place for men to gather and another for women." — SOURCED (Al Kindi 2025).
- **Endowments that fund meeting places:** at Misfat al-Abriyin, the eighth day of the eight-day falaj rotation belongs to "the partners," including endowments "for the maintenance of the communal meeting hall sabla and for the madrasa"; the seventh day belongs to endowments for the falaj, mosque, cemetery and mountain cisterns; a third of that day (16 half-hour units) is auctioned "every 4–5 years" for maintenance. Endowment holdings there also include palm plots, single palms, and a room for making and storing vinegar for the meat cooked at religious festivals. [K8, K10, K12] — SOURCED (B&M 2022, Table 2 and pp.39–40).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Eid communal pit oven (Misfat al-Abriyin, 2015):** about 40 large meat parcels, one per family, cooked two days underground in a communal pit; each sack marked with a metal object ("a bit of rebar, a faucet, a door knocker … a flattened Fanta can") to identify its owner; women prepare spice marinades for up to four weeks, men slaughter and butcher; young men dig the parcels out after midday prayers on the third day of Eid; boys beat drums to announce the collection; a family bought a young bull at Nizwa market for 350 rials; the man interviewed had managed the village irrigation as mosque caretaker for 30+ years. [K2, K12, K4] — SOURCED (Zacharias, The National, 2015).
- **Festival prayer ground and sundial:** at Misfat the sundial that timed falaj shares stood "next to the old musallat al-'id" (the open-air Eid prayer ground); the mosque terrace was the preferred star-watching place for night shares; stone pillars about 1.5 m high on the eastern ridge served as star markers. ## Pick 4 · Valencia's Water Tribunal (and Murcia's Council of Good Men), Spain

### K6 · Rules of everyday behavior (open thread: fines and labor per acre, now answered for a named acequia)
- **Labor duty scales with acreage:** "Each parciante is obligated to two hours of labor per acre for each day that the mass Acequia cleaning is in session" (raised in December 2017 from one and a half hours); acreage rounds up to the next whole acre; a member who cannot work must hire a laborer; unmet hours cost "$15.00 for each hour," offset against banked hours. Annual assessment $8.00 per acre. [K6, K7, K8] — SOURCED (Alcalde By-Laws, Art.5 §1 and assessments article).
- **Who may vote:** only members not delinquent in assessments and who "have not defaulted on their work assignments"; no proxy votes; commissioners serve staggered three-year terms (changed from two years in 2007). [K1, K6] — SOURCED (Alcalde By-Laws, Art.3).
- **The mayordomo here is appointed, not elected:** appointed yearly by a majority of the commissioners after the annual meeting, paid $1,200 a month for nine months (raised from $600 in 2003 by stages; $1,200 since December 2020); has "full authority to shut the private head gate" of members not in good standing "and to secure them by any available means," may suspend water use until dues and fines are paid, and may sue for delinquencies. A bookkeeper (flat $1,200 a year) records each member's labor hours at the cleaning. [K1, K6, K8] — SOURCED (Alcalde By-Laws, Art.2 and Art.8). *(Round 1 found the statute allowing election or appointment; this acequia appoints.)*
- **Shortage sharing:** in water shortage, farms of 25 acres or more irrigate Monday 06:00 to Wednesday 06:00 and smaller farms Wednesday 06:00 to Monday 06:00 (adopted May 2013, replacing a four-section, two-days-each rotation); the mayordomo may grant water out of turn in "dire need"; out-of-turn use brings a warning, then "a citation and a fine and prosecuted via an official complaint in magistrate court." [K6, K1] — SOURCED (Alcalde By-Laws, Art.5 §3).
- **Night rule:** no member may leave a check-dam in the canal "unattended after 6:00 pm"; overflowing banks or obstructing flow is prohibited; the mayordomo may remove unauthorized structures after two days and bill the owner. [K6] — SOURCED (Alcalde By-Laws, Art.5 §4).
- **Pollution:** dumping sewage or trash brings a report to the state Environment Department and police and a fine of "$500.00 for the first offense and $1,000 for each offense thereafter," plus legal costs; since December 2018 a $100 reward to anyone giving evidence (photos) of pollution. Other breaches of members' duties: automatic fines of $50 then $100. State law (NMSA §73-2-64, as quoted): interference with a ditch or taking water contrary to an official's order is a misdemeanor, fine $100–$250 plus damages. ## Pick 6 · Bisses of Valais, Switzerland
*Language note: as in Round 1, "bisse," "Suone" and "consortage" are kept. Local titles of bisse and village officers, the names of the work roles at the spring water-in, a work call and a village nickname were written only to the slang file; this file uses English descriptions.*

### A. Other settings
- **The water-in day on the Savièse cliff channel, 28 April 1934 (the last one):** announced from the pulpit the Sunday before; the work was led by the bisse's chief officer with two deputies and two inspectors; his election "gives rise to fierce rivalries, like that of the municipal authorities, but once chosen his supremacy is recognized by all, and his orders obeyed with absolute discipline." "While it is difficult in Savièse to find workers at 10 or 12 francs, the chief has as many as he wants at 4 francs a day. Each family considers it a duty to send one or two, men or women"; up to 200 gathered at the chapel of Ste-Marguerite. Upkeep cost about 10,000 francs a year (avalanche, rockfall and ice damage; sun-warped beams). At dawn: Mass "with a special prayer invoking divine protection on the men, to keep them from any accident"; a mule brought kegs of white wine and cheese for raclette; at noon a fire on the intake dam, raclette for all, the priest's blessing with the men kneeling on a ledge over the gorge. Then four men took turns blocking the advancing water front with their bodies in the channel while others churned brown forest humus into it, so the silt sealed the joints, repeating every 50 to 100 m for nearly three hours, in snowmelt water at about 4°C "up to the chest"; young women carried fir branches and sacks of humus and brought "large containers of boiling coffee" from the Brac chalet. Afterward wine was opened under the larches; "the priest is happy to have no accident to deplore." [K2, K7, K14, K12, K4] [SM: competence shown under pressure] [R] — SOURCED: Louis Seylaz, "Adieux au bisse de Savièse," first published in the *Gazette de Lausanne* (1934) and reprinted about 1961 in the *Bulletin de la Murithienne*, pp.118–122 (PDF hosted by the Musée valaisan des Bisses, https://bisses-valais.ch/app/uploads/medias/adieux-au-bisse-de-saviese-seylaz-louis/seylaz_louis_adieu_bisse_de_saviese.pdf, text extracted and read 2026-10-29). *(The reprint's editor dates the last blessing "Saturday 25 April 1934"; Seylaz's text says "Saturday 28 April." - **Storage ponds and the ban on feast-day watering (Savièse):** water from two channels was divided among six quarters, each with a pond; the ponds stored water "when the water is not used for irrigation, namely on Sundays and feast days," when "it was forbidden to water." Rights on the Torrent-Neuf were divided into 830 shares, each giving three hours of water; two counters checked use. [K6, K1, K7] — SOURCED: Jean-Nicolas Héritier, *Les eaux saviésannes: évolution du réseau d'irrigation et gestion de l'eau potable*, mémoire de licence, Institut de Géographie, Université de Lausanne, June 1998 (PDF hosted by the bisse museum, https://bisses-valais.ch/app/uploads/2020/06/Heritier-Jean-Nicolas-Les-eaux-saviesannes.pdf, text extracted and read in relevant sections 2026-10-29), pp.10–11, 15–16.
- **Village-level water societies (Savièse, 1998):** each of Savièse's six villages ran its own drinking-water consortage until the commune joined the networks from 1957. At Saint-Germain, the general assembly is "all households domiciled in the village," with a committee of four and two auditors; connection fee "100 francs for a villager and 800 francs for a non-villager"; villager status "is inherited from father to son with the obligation for descendants to reside in the village," and this applies across all Savièse villages; household dues 50 francs a year from 1976 to 1996. At Drône: 500 francs entry, 100 francs a year, set to avoid borrowing for repairs; the consortage also appoints the chapel bell-ringer, the keeper of the village flag, and those who wash the basins and keep the chapel; a separate men's society with "very restrictive" entry handles vineyard work and the distribution of Easter bread. At Granois: 350 francs (villagers) or 850 (non-villagers) entry, annual dues raised from 100 to 200 francs to clear about 30,000 francs of debt. Roumaz's village president opposed merging into a commune network because "the general assembly is a moment when all the villagers can meet … If it were to disappear … people would no longer know each other," and because villagers repair the network as volunteers or for small payments. [K1, K4, K8, K10] [P7] — SOURCED (Héritier 1998, pp.38–49). *(Round 1 open thread on present-day consortage fees: answered for Savièse drinking-water consortages as of 1998; irrigation-bisse fees in a French-speaking consortage: still NOT FOUND.)*

### B. Subcultures
- **Village men's societies:** in Savièse, the "society of men" (also called the society of the privilege), formed in the Middle Ages around village chapels under the village patron saint, "became states within the state": they kept chapels and paid the churchwarden, answered the call in case of danger, administered the village, named police agents and organized fire defense; by 1998 some restored village heritage. Villages "have their chapels, their schools, their cafés, their shops and organize Corpus Christi in turn." [K1, K4, K10, K2] — SOURCED (Héritier 1998, pp.34–35, citing Roten-Dumoulin 1990 and de Rivaz 1928).
- **Status of channel workers:** Suonen workers "held an important function and corresponding standing in the village community." — SOURCED: German Wikipedia "Suone" (read 2026-10-29, via the fetch tool's quotation).
- **Consortages as a legal and social form:** members' duties are communal work and holding offices; rights are use of common goods; written statutes were codified mostly in the 16th and 17th centuries; today governed by the Valais law implementing the Swiss Civil Code of 24 March 1998 (arts.126–131), with statutes approved by the cantonal government; with the end of traditional farming, "time for communal work ran short and mutual dependence disappeared"; some communes took over old consortages' duties; one vineyard irrigation consortage (Leytron) took on pest control from 2000. Robert McC. Netting's 1970s fieldwork at Törbel informed Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990). [K1, K14] — SOURCED: Thomas Antonietti, *Consortages en Valais*, Swiss intangible-heritage portal text, 2012 (PDF on vs.ch, read in full 2026-10-29).
- **Scale today:** "the canton today counts 1,500 consortages"; "no fewer than 190 of the 300 bisses recorded are still managed collectively"; they irrigate "three quarters of the crops of Valais"; the power company at the Rawyl dam "still pays" the Ayent bisse consortage for the water it uses. [K1, K8] — SOURCED: Céline Duruz, "Ancestrale, la gestion collective des bisses reste un exemple pour l'avenir," *Terre & Nature* no.24, 17 Jun 2021, p.4 (PDF hosted by the museum, read 2026-10-29), quoting museum director Gaëtan Morard. A 2018 survey counted 188 bisses longer than 1 km (55 of cantonal, 101 regional and 32 local importance); "Each bisse is supervised by a guard who sometimes stays in a cabin along the watercourse." — SOURCED: aboutswitzerland.eda.admin.ch (Swiss Federal Department of Foreign Affairs), "The bisses of Valais" (undated; read 2026-10-29).

1. **Subak:** a subak's boundary follows where its water stops, not village borders, and its members' water share rises with the dues and labor they give.
2. **Aflaj:** the village meeting hall, funded by endowments or rent, where hosting guests can rotate in step with the falaj rotation, and where one rotation day's endowment pays for the hall and the school.
3. **Ghadames:** the 1986 ICOMOS description of women's rooftop circulation and men's covered streets, and the 2018 reconciliation roadmap that lists drinking water, the airport and the Awal school as local priorities.
4. **Valencia / Murcia:** the canal guard is the usual accuser, fines run a few hundred to a thousand euros, and the guards and syndics measured the 2024 flood damage.
5. **New Mexico acequias:** a full set of bylaws that ties labor, voting rights and water access to acreage and good standing, enforced by an appointed, paid mayordomo who can lock a head gate.
6. **Bisses:** the 1934 eyewitness account of the last cliff water-in: families sending workers, Mass for protection from accidents, men blocking the water with their bodies, and wine afterward. Also village water societies that charge non-villagers up to eight times the entry fee.
