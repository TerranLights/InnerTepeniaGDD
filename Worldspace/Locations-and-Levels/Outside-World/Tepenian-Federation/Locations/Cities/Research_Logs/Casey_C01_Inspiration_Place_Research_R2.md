## 1 · #4 Ogden, Utah, and its 25th Street

**Round 2 focus:** the people around the street (Black, Mexican American, Japanese American, Greek and railroad families), their own spaces, the city's event machine and who runs it, everyday rules today, and the open threads from Round 1 (other jazz venues, rail headcounts, Black population by decade).

### A. Other settings (sub-areas, housing, schools, workplaces, off-season)
- **The segregated Black neighborhood:** Black residents were "generally confined to living in a 12-block area" between 24th and 30th Streets and Grant and Wall Avenues; Black-owned businesses sat mostly at its north end on or near 25th Street; restrictive covenants kept non-white residents out of newer subdivisions in the more affluent parts of the city. SOURCED: archives.utah.gov 2021-02-19 ("A Glimpse into Ogden's Black Community"). The same area was redlined (HOLC maps, 1933 on) and described by a preservationist as "industrial and long overlooked." SOURCED: fox13now.com (Wall Avenue Baptist Church story, undated). [K4, K9, P5, P9]
- **The neighborhood school:** Pingree School (30th Street between Lincoln and Wall Avenues) was not officially segregated, but served most of the city's Black children (the archive judges no other Utah school likely had a higher concentration), had "a handful" of Black teachers among the first in Utah, and also Hispanic children; it closed and was demolished about 1971. SOURCED: archives.utah.gov 2021. [K4, P4]
- **The Jefferson neighborhood today:** the Marshall White Center (opened 1963; named for a Black Ogden police detective killed on duty) serves "the predominately Black Jefferson neighborhood." SOURCED: standard.net 2022-06-18. [K10, P4]
- **Railroad family housing (Mexican American):** a Southern Pacific track laborer (later lamp tender and temporary foreman, 40+ years' service) first lived with his family in "a basic company-provided home"; they then moved from an apartment on Grant Avenue to a house on Stevens (later condemned), and then to a mostly white area west of Wall Avenue, where a neighbor organized a collection to pressure the family to leave. SOURCED: digital.weber.edu id 162193 (Union Station Oral History Project, interview of 23 Sep 2023). Families settled near relatives in a neighborhood with other Hispanic families; owning a home was the measure of success. SOURCED: digital.weber.edu id 162209 (interview 4 Oct 2023). [K4, K9, P4, SM:man versus society]
- **Living over the shop:** a Japanese American family running a noodle restaurant at 225 25th Street (opened about 1944; closed 2007) lived above it in the former movie-theater projection room; the restaurant was open about 5 p.m. to 2 a.m. weekdays and 3 a.m. weekends; a Judo club met in a basement that had once held a gambling room. SOURCED: digital.weber.edu id 104307 (oral history, 18 Sep 2013). Building chronology (movie theater from 1912; restaurant from 1948 per the tourism board, "around 1944" per the family; neon dragon sign removed 2008, restored 2015). SOURCED: visitogden.com "History Through Architecture" (the two opening dates **conflict**). [K4, K7, K12, P4]
- **The shops as a workplace:** Southern Pacific's Ogden shops (machinery first ran 31 Dec 1893; 33-stall roundhouse 1906) had "almost 800 employees" after the 1906-1908 additions (local press, as quoted); SP had about 2,000 people in the Ogden area at its post-WWII peak and 80 by 1987; the roundhouse was torn down October 1954; all remaining SP locomotive and car repair facilities were retired 31 Dec 1988; SP offices in Ogden closed 9 Oct 1989; shop buildings leveled June 1999. SOURCED: utahrails.net/ogden/ogden-sp.html. The railroad remained Northern Utah's largest employer until WWII and the rise of Hill Field (UNVERIFIED: snippet, standard.net 2019-05-05). Approximately 3,900 railroad workers in 1915 (UNVERIFIED: snippet only; the University of Utah collections page returned HTTP 418 and was not retried). [K7, K9, K14, P8] **(Closes part of R1's headcount NOT FOUND.)**
- **Railroad work rhythm in one family's account:** regular hours 7:00 to 3:30, but call-outs at any hour of night and in bad weather; early assignments took track men to other Western states for two weeks to a month; the daughters filled out their father's time cards, which he checked from memory; Hispanic workers estimated at about 10% or less of the railroad labor force early on; foremen mostly white. SOURCED: digital.weber.edu ids 162193, 162209 (memory, as told). [K7, K14, P8, SM:competence shown under pressure]
- **A shelter at the rail edge:** the Lantern House (successor to St. Anne's Center, which had served Weber County's homeless population since 1981) opened 16 June 2015 on a 5-acre site at 33rd Street and Pacific Avenue after a $7 million campaign (the LDS Church gave $1.5 million); 2017: almost 53,000 shelter nights for about 1,500 individuals and 500 families; beds cut from 300 to 211 in 2019 under code requirements; 50 extra winter cots in its soup kitchen (Feb 2025). UNVERIFIED (search snippets: deseret.com 2011/2015, icatholic.org, fox13now 2019, standard.net 2025; pages not opened). [K9, K5, P4]
- **The off-season street:** winter programming on and around 25th Street: Christmas Village (four weeks), the Holiday Electric Light Parade (est. 19,000 attendance, 64 floats, 115 volunteers, 2025), a winter edition of the farmers market, the First Friday Art Stroll continuing monthly (est. 8,000-10,000 over the year; 29 stand-alone locations plus 40 studios in one building), a Winter Solstice Celebration at the Dumke Arts Plaza. SOURCED: Ogden City Arts, Culture & Events Division Annual Report 2025 (PDF, text extracted locally). A January "Winterfest" that closes downtown streets and covers them with snow is reported (UNVERIFIED: snippet, year not shown). [K10, K3, P4]
- **The housing stock:** most units built before 1950; median rents rising about 1% a year faster than incomes over two to three decades. UNVERIFIED (snippet, Ogden City Council housing presentation). Displacement reporting for East Central Ogden: NOT FOUND. [K9]

### B. Subcultures
- **Black railroad families (K4, P9):** 1900: 51 African Americans in Ogden; "less than one hundred" before the early 1900s; 200-300 between 1910 and 1940; 1,106 by 1950 (wartime industry and government jobs); for many years the state's second-largest Black population. Railroad work (often as porters and waiters) drew them. SOURCED: standard.net 2018-02-26 (quoting a 1994 USU thesis and a Weber State historian). **(Closes R1's "Black population by decade" NOT FOUND, in part.)**
  - *Churches:* Embry Chapel AME (2718 Pingree Ave.) and Wall Avenue Baptist (2701 Wall Ave.) served the community through the first half of the century (SOURCED: archives.utah.gov). Wall Avenue Baptist's building was raised in 1918 (dedicated May 1919) by about 100 members who funded and built it themselves; the congregation moved to New Zion in 1956. SOURCED: fox13now.com. Founding dates of both congregations differ across sources (1908-1916): UNVERIFIED.
  - *Veterans' club:* the old church then became Pioneer Post 66 of the American Legion, described as the only all-Black veterans' post in Utah when posts were segregated; it hosted fashion shows, dances and family gatherings, was a civil-rights hub, and stayed until the early 2000s; the building is now empty and in disrepair. SOURCED: fox13now.com. A resident who arrived in 1951 (a key-punch operator at Hill) said it was often the only place Black residents could socialize, because white clubs excluded them. SOURCED (same). [K10, K9]
- **Mexican American railroad families (K4, P9):** Southern Pacific track work (laying ties by hand) was an entry job; families recall exclusion from theaters and restaurants, signs barring Mexicans, being refused by a doctor and a dentist, and traveling to Salt Lake City for Spanish-language films. Social life: St. Mary's Catholic Church, folk-dance groups that kept the heritage, weddings, quinceañeras and fiestas held at Union Station (weddings needed hired security), at a building on 25th Street and at the Berthana; a summer "Hispanic day" at a regional amusement park and Father's Day outings there. SOURCED: digital.weber.edu ids 162193, 162209. Latinos are 32% of Ogden's roughly 87,000 (2021 estimate). SOURCED: standard.net 2022-10-12. [K2, K12]
- **Japanese American families (K4, P9):** Issei men gathered at the noodle restaurant on Mondays when it was closed; a Japanese-owned grocery next door, an electronics shop, a café and a bar; a cousin served as reverend at the Buddhist church; Japanese was the owner's son's first language until about age five; the father gave police discounts and officers were regulars (the son credits this with the restaurant never being robbed); a family whose farmland was taken during the wartime removal later received restitution. SOURCED: digital.weber.edu id 104307. A family that left California to avoid internment opened a market on 25th Street around 1943 (UNVERIFIED: snippet of another Weber oral history). The Buddhist temple was organized 1 March 1913, first meeting in a store at 236 24th Street; a 1915 plan to build at 2840 Washington Boulevard drew citizen protests (UNVERIFIED: snippet of exhibits.weber.edu SC50 "buddhist"). The Buddhist Church of Ogden still holds an annual Obon festival with dancing, food and taiko (UNVERIFIED: ksl.com snippet). [K2, K12, K14]
- **Greek railroad laborers (K4, K7):** Greeks came to Utah through a Greek American labor agent working with railroad companies; they lived in tents or railroad cars, were paid as little as $20 a month, and were segregated on railroad gangs and often given the more dangerous work (UNVERIFIED: snippets of Utah state history pages). Weber State's "Immigrants at the Crossroads" project holds oral histories of Ogden's Dutch, Italian, Greek and Japanese railroad immigrants (UNVERIFIED: snippet). No Ogden Greek coffeehouse or "Greektown" on 25th Street was documented: NOT FOUND.
- **Railroad craft unions (K14, K1):** city-directory listings show the Brotherhood of Railroad Trainmen's Ogden Lodge No. 68 meeting Thursdays at the I.O.O.F. Hall, the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers' division meeting Mondays at the Knights of Pythias Hall, a carmen's lodge and other craft unions meeting at a Union Labor Hall at 362 24th Street, and a trainmen's ladies' auxiliary. UNVERIFIED (snippets of Weber State digitized directories; years not shown). The 1922 shopmen's strike in Ogden: NOT FOUND.
- **All-ages and DIY music (K13, youth):** an all-ages café-gallery venue since 2004 (its owner says it has long been the city's only all-ages venue); an all-ages basement venue at 329 E. 24th St. for local Utah music; a warehouse DIY venue for punk, metal and hardcore that moved to Ogden from Salt Lake City in fall 2023; a print shop turned punk merchandise store that keeps shows all-ages "because so many venues around here are 21 and over." UNVERIFIED (snippets: slugmag.com, thesignpostwsu.com, visitogden.com). Covers bands and originals in bars of 50 to hundreds. SOURCED: visitogden.com/blog/ogdens-music-scene. [K13, K6]
- **Outdoor-industry people (K+ "outdoor-brand town"):** after the 2002 Winter Games, Ogden shifted economic focus toward outdoor sports; 40-50+ outdoor-gear companies have a footprint in the city (figures differ by source); national ski media named it an "emerging ski town" (UNVERIFIED: snippets: siteselection.com, nationalgeographic.com, ksl.com). Some residents feared the tourism push would further strain the environment (UNVERIFIED: planetizen snippet). [K8, P8]
- **Speech (K11, loose):** English; Spanish in daily use among the Mexican American community; historically Japanese as a first language in some 25th Street families. SOURCED (oral histories above).

### C. Leisure industries and what locals do
- **Concerts as a city product:** the Ogden Twilight series sold 58,734 tickets across 10 shows in 2025 (individual shows 4,331-7,586). The 7,500-capacity amphitheater held 25 events over 59 days in 2025 (MLK march, Cinco de Mayo, Twilight, the marathon finish festival, Juneteenth over three days, an orchestra pops concert, Shakespeare, Pride over two days, a 9/11 memorial fire ride, a recovery-days event, Día de los Muertos, Christmas Village). The city issued 144 special-event permits covering 311 days of programming in 2025. SOURCED: Ogden City ACE Annual Report 2025. [K3, K8]
- **Rodeo season:** Ogden Pioneer Days, started in 1934 by a newly elected mayor during the Depression to draw tourists and the spending of livestock-show visitors; the first edition had a midway, dances, beard-growing contests with cash prizes, parades, a pageant sponsored by the Weber County Daughters of Utah Pioneers, and three rodeos ("120 events in 120 minutes"), $0.50 tickets sold at local stores, a full 10,000-seat stadium. SOURCED: exhibits.weber.edu/s/SC50/page/Pioneer_days. Today a month-long July program (parades, a pops concert and fireworks, a devotional, an art show, a special kids' rodeo, a rodeo queen contest) run by the Ogden Pioneer Days Committee and Foundation with the city; the rodeo draws more than 30,000 a year and is ranked among the PRCA's top outdoor rodeos. SOURCED: ogdenpioneerdays.com/about. Separate from the Salt Lake City Days of '47 (24 July 1847). [K2, K3, K14]
- **What locals do when the visitors leave:** winter downtown events (see A); First Friday art stroll; bowling and day trips (one family's weekly day off was bowling or the regional amusement park). SOURCED (report; oral history 104307). [K10]
- **Dance halls of the big-band era:** the Berthana (opened 26 May 1915; second-floor ballroom 120 x 80 ft; built by two business estates and named for the two women credited with it, meant as a gathering place for Ogden's young people; hosted conventions, concerts, dances, beauty pageants and boxing; roller rink from about 1940; largely unused from the 1970s; renovated 2012 for $750,000; a restaurant moved in 2016). SOURCED: intermountainhistories.org/items/show/682. A rival White City Ballroom on 25th Street drew touring big bands (UNVERIFIED: snippets). [K10, K13]

SOURCED: digital.weber.edu 104307. Día de los Muertos events in 2022: at Ben Lomond High School by Ogden Friends of Acoustic Music (OFOAM), with an ofrenda, student art and makeup; at Union Station by a youth-arts nonprofit. ### Everyday behavior rules (K6)
- **Bar density, 1980s to 2021:** after more than 35 bars were found in one small area of the street, the city in the 1980s allowed no more than two 21-and-over drinking establishments per block (both sides) to reduce crime, blocking new bars on the street (SOURCED: fox13now.com, undated; standard.net 2021-01-06). On a 4-3 council vote (reported 6 Jan 2021) the limit rose to three per side of a block in the Central Business District Intensive Zone (20th-27th Streets, Wall to Adams), counting corner businesses and any business with a public door on the block. Supporters (a developer who first petitioned in 2009, a lounge owner) cited growth and nightlife; a council member who voted no said 25th Street had been unsafe in his youth and "It has taken decades for us to get the genie back in the bottle." SOURCED: standard.net 2021-01-06. The current city code also bars converting an existing private club into a tavern in those zones (UNVERIFIED: amlegal snippet). [K6, K9, K1]
- **State drinking rules that shape the bars:** last sale 1:00 a.m. under any circumstance; bars, clubs and taverns may sell 10 a.m.-1 a.m.; restaurant drinks must be ordered with food and be under 30% of sales; bars and clubs stopped having to charge a membership fee or cover in June 2009; a required barrier between drink preparation and restaurant diners was introduced, removed (2009), restored (2010) and replaced in 2017 by a rule keeping minors 10 ft from drink preparation; photo ID for every purchase from 1 Jan 2026. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_laws_of_Utah. [K6]
- **Gang injunction (2010-2013):** a court declared a local street gang a public nuisance (Sept 2010) and barred alleged members (estimates 315-500) from gathering or associating in a 25-square-mile "safety zone" covering much of the city, even without criminal activity; made permanent August 2012; overturned by the Utah Supreme Court on 18 Oct 2013 on service-of-process grounds, the court confirming that gangs can be sued. SOURCED: ksl.com/article/27299251 (2013-10-18). An 11 p.m. curfew for named members is reported (UNVERIFIED: snippets of ACLU of Utah). [K6, K9, SM:man versus society]
- **Unwritten exclusion:** signs barring Mexicans; exclusion from theaters, restaurants and white clubs; a neighbor's collection to push a Mexican American family out. SOURCED (oral histories, fox13now). [K6, K9]
- **House rule at one restaurant:** police ate at a discount and were regulars. SOURCED (oral history).

## 2 · #84 Moose Jaw, Saskatchewan

**Round 2 focus:** what replaced River Street, the military community, winter sport and halls, the festival calendar and who runs it, the Chinese community after the head-tax era, newcomers, the 1935 drinking rules, and R1's open threads (CPR headcounts, River Street hotel list, Sidewalk Days founding, Hometown Fair).

### A. Other settings
- **River Street's disappearance, dated (K9):** the original Brunswick and Ottawa hotels (both early 1880s) were torn down in 1939; no other major demolitions until the Cecil Hotel burned in 1975 (the first major loss); the Alexandra Block soon after; the Royal Block about 1979; the Academy Block (last a hardware store) and the Lyric Theater building (last a billiard hall) about 1980; the Empress Hotel gutted by fire in 1987 (the article's own list says 1978: **conflict**); a New Year's Day 2004 fire at Main and River; a 2007-2008 redevelopment plan approved by council stalled in a slowdown; in 2009 the last three multi-storey brick buildings (the Brunswick Hotel, the old City Hotel, the Royal Hotel) were demolished for a redevelopment that was never completed, leaving mostly parking lots. SOURCED: discovermoosejaw.com 2019-07-19 (local historian, author of five local history books). The Brunswick (82 River St. W., built 1903) is where the city incorporation papers were signed on 20 Nov 1903 (UNVERIFIED: snippet). **(Closes most of R1's River Street hotel-list NOT FOUND.)** [K9, P5]
- **The military housing community:** Bushell Park, about 5 km south of the city on Highway 2, was opened 19 Sep 1952 to house staff and students of the air base; it holds Residential Housing Units and a small retail center. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushell_Park (a stub). ## 3 · #34 Kansas City, 18th and Vine

**Round 2 focus:** the residential neighborhood under the music district, its housing, schools, lodges, churches and clubs, its leisure under segregation, the cooperative that replaced a cleared neighborhood, today's rules and redevelopment, and R1's open threads. **R1's main NOT FOUND (the National Register nomination) is closed:** the 1989 Multiple Property Documentation Form was read in full from the NPS copy (npgallery.nps.gov NRHP 64500294; prepared 21 Dec 1989 by a preservation consultancy; WebFetch could not parse the PDF, so its text layer was extracted locally with pdftotext). ### A. Other settings
- **The residential neighborhood (MPDF 1989):** the area began as a densely populated Black residential section; surviving houses are modest one-story and larger frame single- and multi-family houses in the 1800 block of Highland Avenue (the oldest at 1820 and 1822 Highland). Residents' occupations in city directories: maid, laborer, porter, janitor, warehouse and factory workers, plus attorneys, physicians and teachers; several shopkeepers lived above their shops. 1900-1910 the city's Black population rose 54% to over 23,000; 1900-1920 from 17,567 to 30,719; 38,574 by 1930. In 1913 the 22 blocks around 18th and Vine held 4,295 residents. SOURCED: MPDF 1989. [P4, P9]
- **Worker housing (MPDF 1989):** 1902 rental apartment buildings on E. 19th Street (four apartments each, built close on narrow lots; early tenants a porter, a laborer, a maid, a clerk, a Pullman Company employee); 1910-1911 fourplexes in the 1700 block of E. 19th built by a prominent Black realtor, with shops on the ground floors; the Western Apartments (1919-1920, 24 units; tenants in 1925 maids, laborers, porters, section hands; after 1940 a hotel renting by the day, week or month). Most development was financed and built by white investors: in 1913 only 800 of the city's 23,566 Black residents (3.4%) owned property, and in 1924 the Black newspaper ran an editorial titled "Keep Renting and Stay Poor." A 1913 housing study found many buildings substandard; one at 19th and Woodland had 48 rooms plus a 10-room rear apartment divided into two-room suites with no sinks or baths. In 1913, of 8,100 Black male workers in the city, 5,006 were low-paid laborers and 600 porters or waiters; there were 23 Black physicians and 4 dentists. SOURCED: MPDF 1989. [K4, K9, K7, P4]
- **Hotels:** the Street Hotel (1508-1512 E. 18th; razed) was the major Black hotel before 1920 and "the best hotel for blacks in the city," with short- and long-term lodging; the Booker T. Hotel (1929, 1821 Vine; razed). SOURCED: MPDF 1989. The Street Hotel's 1930s club gave the name to today's museum jazz club; the hotel was listed in the Green Book. SOURCED: americanjazzmuseum.org/the-blue-room. [K10, K5]
- **Schools:** Attucks School (formed 1893; present building 1905; 1922 wing with gymnasium and auditorium because of overcrowding; 560 students and 13 teachers in 1911; almost 1,000 students in 1950; integrated in the 1960s; the oldest continually occupied school built for Black students in the city). SOURCED: MPDF 1989. Lincoln High School opened 1890 at 19th and Tracy as the city's first all-Black high school (UNVERIFIED: kcur.org snippet). [P4]
- **A school that seeded the jazz scene (K13):** a bandmaster and military instructor joined Lincoln High on 5 Sep 1916 and stayed to 1922; three days a week of military drill and two of music; built the band from 5 to 30 players in a year, plus a 30-member girls' glee club, a 24-member boys' glee club and a 15-piece orchestra; a strict disciplinarian who used a ruler and made students recite the elements of music; jazz was not in the curriculum, but he did not discourage it, and the 1919 yearbook praised a student's jazz orchestra that played during lunch; his pupils included several who became leading Kansas City jazz musicians (including the bassist of the most famous territory band). Earlier he had led the band at a local amusement park (1915) and directed a Black music settlement school (summer 1916). SOURCED: libweb.umkc.edu/spec-col/local627/text/introduction/smith.htm. [K13, K14, SM:competence shown under pressure]
- **The cleared neighborhood and the cooperative (K1, K9):** Attucks, a Black neighborhood of about 54 acres with more than 600 families (houses lacking running water and electricity), was razed in the 1950s under urban renewal; a University of Richmond study found over 3,000 families displaced by urban renewal in the two Kansas Citys 1950-1966, more than half of them households of color. On the land, Parade Park Homes (groundbreaking 11 Aug 1961; first residents about a year later; 510 townhomes) became one of the nation's oldest Black-owned housing co-ops: federal below-market financing required co-op form; residents bought a share tied to an occupancy agreement for a unit and paid monthly charges set from the co-op's expenses ($66 a month for a two-bedroom in the 1960s); residents set policy, rules and budget; in 1975 the board required a couple to show a marriage license before moving in together. Residents describe a close-knit place where neighbors watched each other's children ("like a cocoon"). By 2019-2022 it had some of the worst HUD inspection scores, board feuds and about half its units occupied. SOURCED: kcur.org 2022-08-10. The city bought it at foreclosure (2024: UNVERIFIED snippet); after more than a dozen fires since Thanksgiving 2025, demolition began in February 2026 for a $300 million project of 1,100+ units (490 for households at 30-60% of median income, 535 at 60-80%, 60 for-sale later), first phase due 2027. SOURCED: kcur.org 2026-02-09. [K1, K4, K9, P4, SM:man versus society]
- **The redeveloped street today:** the 18th Street pedestrian mall between the Paseo and Woodland (curbs, gutters and parking removed; closable to cars for events; $4 million federal, $2 million+ city) opened May 2026 as part of a $400 million program (theater restoration, museum expansions, an outdoor roller rink in a park, a 480-space, $20 million garage). SOURCED: kcur.org 2026-05-28. In 2016 residents of the co-op asked whether a planned $28 million of improvements and a youth baseball academy would serve them; a council member spoke of "fear that what's being built isn't being built for the population that lives there now"; newer apartments were nearly full. SOURCED: kcur.org 2016-05-12. [K9, K8]

### B. Subcultures
- **Musicians (K14, K13):** the Black musicians' union (founded 1917 by 25 musicians; first headquarters on 18th Street) bought the duplex at 1823 Highland for $4,750 in $35 monthly installments at 6% and funded its conversion with a December 1929 ball (SOURCED: theclio.com/tour/2424/7; the MPDF gives 1928 for the purchase: **conflict**). It became a dance hall and clubhouse where musicians met, rehearsed and performed (e.g., a 1 May 1932 open house and smoker for a touring bandleader, to which all union musicians were invited) (SOURCED: MPDF 1989). A historian estimated it was "a second home, a training ground and a source of jobs" for about 90% of the era's musicians; the white local repeatedly urged a merger from the 1930s and the Black local resisted, keeping the best opportunities for its members as clubs closed; the national federation forced the merger in 1970 and the local had to leave the building, which the Foundation (created 1958) kept as a social club. SOURCED: theclio.com/tour/2424/7. In 2017 the Foundation was officially a fraternal social organization with about 60 dues-paying members; its president called the late-night crowd one of the most diverse in town; younger members planned spoken-word and hip-hop nights and a low-power radio station; a former director's lawsuit and a 1949 expulsion of a president for misappropriating funds are part of its record. SOURCED: kcur.org 2017-05-26. [K1, K10]
- **Railroad workers' club:** the Railway Men's Social Club, of porters and workers of the Missouri Pacific and other lines, had a meeting hall on the second floor of 1600 E. 19th St. in the late 1930s. SOURCED: MPDF 1989. [K7, K10, K14]
- **Lodges and aid societies (K1):** a three-story Masonic building at 18th and Woodland (razed) was the center for the area's "large number of black Masons" into the 1940s; an Elks lodge (formed 1906, "designed to help others") met above the 18th and Vine stores, later in the Lincoln Building; a boys' club founded 1926 raised money for the needy; the NAACP branch (formed 1915) and the Urban League (1919) had offices in the Lincoln Building in the 1930s. SOURCED: MPDF 1989.
- **Jewish merchants:** many stores were owned by Jewish merchants (e.g., a grocery at 1513 E. 18th, a market at 1725 E. 18th); the relationship is described as largely harmonious, with the Black newspaper writing in 1924 against those who "speak harshly of our Jewish neighbors ... whose stores we patronize, and whose money we borrow"; by 1940, 57% of the area's businesses were Black-owned. - **Packinghouse workers:** Kansas City is said to have hired Black workers in its slaughterhouses earlier than other cities, in relatively well-paid jobs; occupations were rigidly segregated by the 1920s; a 1938 strike at the Armour plant solidified the union's interracial alliance. - **Black women's social clubs as organizers:** in 1958 a teacher in a Black women's social club, refused lunch-counter service downtown despite spending heavily there, formed with her club president a committee that grew (with other Black clubs, ministers, the NAACP, the Black newspaper's editor, a church women's group and white pastors) into the Community Committee for Social Action; tactics: protest stickers mailed back through charge-account bills, canvassing, a consumer boycott of five department stores, pickets from 19 Dec 1958 in the holiday rush, a threatened mass march, and "diner tutorials" by women members to prepare Black customers; picketing suspended 9 Feb 1959; three stores desegregated their dining rooms 27 Feb 1959 and the others about two months later. - **Youth (1968):** on 9 April 1968 (the funeral day of the assassinated civil-rights leader) Kansas City, Kansas closed schools but the Missouri side kept them open; students of Central, Lincoln and Manual high schools walked out and marched downtown; police used tear gas (including in a church basement where a youth dance was planned); three days of rioting followed; six Black residents died, hundreds were injured or jailed, more than a dozen officers were injured; the National Guard was called in. SOURCED: kcur.org 2018-04-04. [K9]

## 4 · #7 Fort Worth Stockyards and Billy Bob's Texas

**Round 2 focus:** the packing-town that surrounded the yards and the Mexican American Northside that outlasted it; the 1921 strike (R1 NOT FOUND); the district's festival calendar and who runs each; crafts; the giant club's house rules and workforce; and how the district lives beside its neighborhood today.

### A. Other settings
- **The company town:** the one-square-mile industrial community around the yards and plants incorporated as Niles City in 1911 "to escape Fort Worth jurisdiction" and was annexed in 1922/1923 (sources give both) after a long legal fight under a new state annexation law; it had its own fire department, schools and police and was promoted as one of the wealthiest small cities per capita (its promotional nickname recorded separately). The city paid each packer a $100,000 bonus; plants began building in 1902 and opened in 1903; Swift's plant opened during the 1904 fat stock show. SOURCED: City of Fort Worth historic context chapter "Early Twentieth Century Urbanization, 1900-1945" (PDF excerpt, text extracted locally); fwweekly.com 2010-01-13. **(Closes R1's "$100,000 bonus" UNVERIFIED.)** [K1, K8, P5]
- **Packing employment (R1 NOT FOUND, partly closed):** Swift employed about 1,700 "during the area's heyday" (SOURCED: fwweekly.com 2010, undated figure); Swift opened with about 1,000 and doubled; the yards and plants together were the workplace of up to 5,000 people over the decades (UNVERIFIED: snippets of a local-history blog that refused Round 1). Swift offered medical care, emergency leave and a pension; workers did not join the 1949 and 1952 strikes and in the 1960s took voluntary pay cuts to keep the plant open; Armour closed 1962, Swift 1971; fires gutted the empty Swift buildings in 1971 and 1973 (the second burned three weeks), and a wrecking firm broke three wrecking balls on the brick and gave up. SOURCED: fwweekly.com 2010-01-13. A streetcar ran beside Packers Avenue for plant workers (UNVERIFIED). [K7, K9, P8]
- **The 1921 strike and a lynching (K9; reported neutrally):** in late 1921 a national packinghouse strike reached the Niles City plants; the striking union members at Swift were white-only and the owners brought in Black replacement workers; on 6-7 Dec 1921 strikers attacked a 53-year-old Black meatpacker who crossed the picket line, he fired and wounded two strikers, and a mob beat him; on 11 Dec 1921 about 25-30 men took him from the segregated ward of the city-county hospital and hanged him at NE 12th Street and Samuels Avenue. The union disavowed the killing; six suspected mob members, including two Niles City policemen, were indicted and none went to trial. On 11 Dec 2021 a local peace-and-justice coalition affiliated with a national memorial initiative placed a historical marker at the site. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching_of_Fred_Rouse. Some sources place him at Armour rather than Swift (UNVERIFIED: snippet, mappingviolence.com): **conflict**. The strike ran from late 1921 to February 1922 and was seen as a failure (UNVERIFIED snippet). **(Closes R1's 1921 strike NOT FOUND.)** [K9, K4, SM:man versus society]
- **Population context:** Fort Worth's Black population was 16-18% of the city in 1900-1920 (4,219 of 26,688 in 1900; 15,896 of 106,482 in 1920); "Mexican" counted separately only in the 1930 census. SOURCED: city historic context Table 3-3. [P9]
- **The Northside today:** the Historic Northside has about 11,000 residents, nearly 97% Hispanic or Latino; property values rose about 60% 2016-2021 (an Urban Land Institute panel); the city's Main Street pilot (since August 2022, run by the Fort Worth Hispanic Chamber of Commerce) reports 440+ small-business assistance efforts and $84 million in corridor projects, plans a Hispanic small-business hub, a missing-middle housing study and heirship legal help, and seeks "neighborhood stability as development accelerates." SOURCED: historicfortworth.org/historic-northside-project. The chamber's CEO has said North Main has many restaurants but many empty storefronts (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K9, K1, P4]
- **Schools:** North Side High School (first called North Fort Worth High; the first building called North Side High in 1914, prompted by the stockyards' growth; first football team 1909; the steer became the mascot by October 1923, with a school paper named for a rope; present 15-acre campus built 1937 with PWA and WPA money; about 1,600 students). UNVERIFIED (snippets of Wikipedia and Waymarking; the article is flagged for missing citations). [P4, K2]
- **The district as workplace now:** drovers arrive at 6:45 a.m. daily to ready horses and steers; the drive runs every day except Easter, Thanksgiving and Christmas, weather permitting; a seasonal ice rink outside the Exchange Building; visitors tripled to about 9 million a year in a decade; a $145.7 million second phase was approved in June 2024 (300,000 sq ft commercial, new hotels, 295 apartments). SOURCED: tcu360 "From trails to tomorrow" (late 2024). [K7, K14, K3]

### B. Subcultures

## 5 · #8 Gilley's, Pasadena, Texas

**Round 2 focus:** the plant town the club served — its shared-risk institutions, shift work and labor disputes, what young people and adults did for fun, the regulars after the club, exclusion rules, the city's festivals and the club site's fate. **The club closed in 1989 and burned in 1990 (R1); most of this section is about the town around it.**

### A. Other settings
- **The plants as workplace and hazard:** on 23 Oct 1989 at a Phillips 66 chemical complex in Pasadena, during contractor maintenance to clear settling legs on a polyethylene reactor, a release ignited about two minutes later in a vapor-cloud explosion, followed by more explosions and fires: 23 dead and 130-300 injured. Findings: a single isolating ball valve instead of the double-block or blind-flange practice; cross-connected air hoses that opened the valve; a permit-to-work system not enforced; no fixed gas detection; no dedicated firewater system (pumps failed; a standby pump under maintenance, another out of fuel); a siren possibly inaudible; the planned control center destroyed; occupied buildings with air intakes near the process. SOURCED: hse.gov.uk COMAH case study "Phillips 66, Pasadena, 23rd October 1989." Most of the dead were Phillips employees, two were from the six-man contract crew (UNVERIFIED: snippet); OSHA proposed about $6.4 million in fines (UNVERIFIED: snippet, Oil & Gas Journal). [K9, K7, K14, SM:humanity and technology questions]
- **Shift work:** refinery workers describe 8-hour days changed to 12-hour shifts in the 1980s and runs of 8-16 straight 12-hour days (UNVERIFIED: snippets of union and labor-press accounts; not Pasadena-specific). The club's 10 a.m.-2 a.m. daily hours carried the motto recorded separately (SOURCED via a blog quoting the state handbook: southbelthouston.blogspot.com 2015-06). [K7]
- **A five-year lockout (K9):** Crown Central Petroleum locked out 252 union members (Local 4-227) at its Pasadena refinery on 5 Feb 1996; the union ran an international campaign with labor, civil-rights, religious and environmental allies; a Norwegian state oil company canceled its crude-processing contract (35% of the refinery's capacity) in October 2000; the settlement after five years gave about 11.5% raises over 13 months, protected seniority and kept the contract if the plant were sold; locked-out members lived on international union support and a hardship fund from other locals. SOURCED: industriall-union.org archive ("Five-Year US Oil Lock-Out Ends", undated) and ibiblio.org/prism Nov 1997 (boycott call; the company had demanded permanent replacement of nearly half the workforce). ## 6 · #38 Armadillo World Headquarters, Austin (1970-1980)

**Round 2 focus:** the hall's economics (R1 NOT FOUND: rent, staff, the brewery tie), the festival it spawned that outlived it, the city's other gatherings and leisure places, who lived where and on how little, the rules (drugs, segregation), and the neighboring scenes (blues, punk). Real-world facts only.

### A. Other settings
- **The building and its terms:** a 30,000 sq ft former armory leased for $500 a month; about $4,000 of start-up money from a founder's own funds, a band member's and a record contract; folding chairs, a concrete floor, high windows, a tin roof, and no air conditioning or heating, which hurt attendance in summer and winter. SOURCED: theclio.com/entry/25497 (a student-authored entry listing the state handbook, a 1986 magazine retrospective and the 2010 scholarly article among its sources). **(Closes R1's rent NOT FOUND.)** Staff went 13 weeks without paychecks in 1976 (UNVERIFIED: snippet). [K8, K10, P4]
- **The neighborhood:** the hall opened "in a South Austin neighborhood populated mainly by lower-income Anglos and Mexican Americans." SOURCED: Fox, *Journal of Texas Music History*, article on the Lone Star Brewing Company and Texas culture (PDF docs.gato.txst.edu/56051, text extracted locally). A Mexican American community grew in South Austin in the 1940s, and some white residents put up yard signs telling Mexican families to leave (UNVERIFIED: snippet, historynewsnetwork.org). A federal labor study called Austin the cheapest city of its size in 1969 and again five years later; a small house rented for about $50 a month (UNVERIFIED: snippets of Texas Monthly and other accounts). [K4, K9, P4, P9]
- **The audience's origins:** youths who had left rural or suburban homes for the university (Fox); a 1973 observer described the crowd as middle-class youth from Texas cities "rarely more than two or three generations removed from more rural times" (SOURCED: Ruch, "'Far Out in Texas': Countercultural Sound and the Construction of Cultural Heritage in the Capital City," *Journal of Texas Music History*, PDF via docs.gato.txst.edu, text extracted locally). - **The East Side:** under segregation the 1928 city plan pushed Black residents into East Austin (SOURCED: kut.org 2025-05-22); there a grill opened on VJ Day 1945 in a converted icehouse at 1104 E. 11th St. by a booking agent and band manager, for returning Black servicemen who had few places to eat and drink; it became a stop on the Black touring circuit and by the 1960s drew white students; integration and suburban moves emptied it; the club closed in the mid-1970s (the café stayed), burned in 1988, reopened 1996, and was listed on the National Register on 16 Oct 1998. ### B. Subcultures
- **The beer distributor as insider (K8, K14):** a brewery district manager transferred to Austin in 1973, where a national rival was courting students; he rode the delivery trucks and found that "the real fertile market was the youth market and the on premise market"; the hall was the brewery's second-biggest keg account in Texas and its largest in Austin; the brewery gave the hall a volume discount; he pledged to raise district sales 30% "if he could do it his way," traded shirt and tie for cutoffs and a beard, and visited clubs several times a week; he told the hall's resident artist why brewery workers preferred returnable long-neck bottles (filled to the bottom, less carbonated); the artist's first poster for the brewery sold for $1,000 and paired the armadillo with the long-neck bottle; the hall's own ad agency had already approached the brewery's marketing director. SOURCED: Fox (JTMH). **(Closes R1's NOT FOUND on the brewery relationship: no formal sponsorship found, but a discount, a key-account relationship and a shared poster campaign are documented.)** [K8, K13, SM:power rising step by step]
- **Brewery plant workers' custom:** union brewery workers were given free beer on breaks and chose the returnable long-neck bottles over cans (SOURCED: Fox). [K12, K7, R]
- **Poster artists:** the armadillo motif began with one artist's late-1960s posters (his first armadillo poster was for a 1968 park concert); some students campaigned to replace the university's longhorn mascot with the armadillo (SOURCED: Fox). Lamar in 2014 (UNVERIFIED: snippets). [K13, K14]
- **Psychedelic and drug culture (K6):** LSD reached Central Texas in 1964 and was made illegal in 1968; some bought peyote at local nurseries or picked mushrooms in cow pastures; a retired police officer recalled the force was "overwhelmed" and believed courts were sympathetic to the college-student users. The downtown psychedelic venue (opened 1967) never got a beer license, lived on door charges that went uncollected from friends, drew police attention for open drug use, and closed in 1970. SOURCED: Ruch (JTMH). Marijuana passed openly at the hall (SOURCED: Ruch, quoting contemporaries). [K6, K9]
- **Blues players and the white student audience:** a blues club opened on a then nearly vacant Sixth Street on 15 July 1975 to link veteran circuit players with young white musicians; it drew the young white crowd away from East Side clubs, which lost money. SOURCED: Watson (JTMH). [K4, K9, K13]
- **Punks:** a bar near the university, opened by two Mexican American owners meant as a base for Tejano music, booked punk bands on Monday nights from early 1978 (first shows 50-70 people); in autumn 1978 an exchange between a singer and a police officer started a small riot with six arrests, which national press coverage turned into a draw; it closed in 1981. SOURCED: Watson (JTMH). [K9, K13]
