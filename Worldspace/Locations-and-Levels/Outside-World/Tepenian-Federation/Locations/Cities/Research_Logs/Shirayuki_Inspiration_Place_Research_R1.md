## Pick 1 · Shibuya District, Tokyo, Japan [PRIMARY]

*Scope note:* "Shibuya" names both a special ward (Shibuya City, 渋谷区, which also contains Harajuku, Ebisu, Yoyogi, Sendagaya, Hatagaya and more) and the station district around Shibuya Station. Ward-level figures below are for the whole ward; the cultural items are mostly the station district (Dogenzaka, Udagawacho, Jinnan, Shibuya 1–3 chome, Maruyamacho, Sakuragaokacho). Harajuku/Jingumae items are held for Pick 2.

### A. Snapshot

- **What it is:** a special ward of Tokyo in the Kanto region; incorporated as a ward of Tokyo City on 1932-10-01. The name comes from the Shibuya clan, who held the area from the 1160s. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya (read 2026-10-26).
- **Population:** 230,609 residents in 142,443 households (estimate, 2024-01-01); area 15.11 km²; density about 15,000–16,000 per km² (Wikipedia gives two density figures on different pages; not resolved). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, as above. 2025 Basic Resident Register: 231,402 (111,018 male, 120,384 female); ages 0–14 = 10.1%, 15–64 = 71.2%, 65+ = 18.7%; population was 195,980 in 2010. **SOURCED** — opengov.jp/en/population/estimates/tokyo/shibuya/ (read 2026-10-26). Registered-population series 2019–2024: 226,594 → 229,671 → 230,506 → 229,013 → 229,412 → 230,609 (Jan 1 each year, from Shibuya Open Data). **SOURCED** — Tokyu Livable, *Research Shibuya* brochure, data as of 2025-05-07 (PDF, livable.co.jp/assets/file/Research-Shibuya_EN.pdf, read via pdftotext 2026-10-26).
- **Foreign residents:** 11,935 of 230,609 (5.2%), 2024. **UNVERIFIED** (search-engine summary of a ChildFund Japan PDF; not opened). Nationality breakdown **NOT FOUND**.
- **Daytime vs. resident population (2020 census):** day/night population ratio **259.7** (4th highest of Tokyo's 23 wards); **505,111 daytime workers** in the ward (5th of the 23 wards; 485,339 in 2015). **SOURCED** — Tokyo Metropolitan Government Bureau of General Affairs, 令和2年国勢調査 昼間人口 概要 (toukei.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/tyukanj/2020/tj20gaiyou.pdf, tables 2 and 6, read via pdftotext 2026-10-26). The ward's absolute daytime population was not in the ranked tables I read: **NOT FOUND** as a stated figure.
- **Defining feature:** the Shibuya Station hub and the scramble crossing in front of it. Shibuya Station opened 1885-03-01 on the Shinagawa Line; about 3 million people a day use it (2025), described as the world's second-busiest station; FY2024 daily passengers: JR East 648,828; Tokyu and Tokyo Metro combined 1,770,430. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya_Station (read 2026-10-26). The scramble crossing was laid out in **1973**; 1,000–2,000 people per green cycle (some estimates 3,000); about 260,000 pedestrians on weekdays, 390,000 on non-working days, up to 500,000 on peak days. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya_Crossing (read 2026-10-26).
- **Terrain:** Shibuya is a valley (谷地形); historically a quiet area beside the Shibuya River. **SOURCED** — Tokyu Corp., 渋谷の歴史 (tokyu.co.jp/shibuya-redevelopment/history/past/, read 2026-10-26).

### B. What else exists there

Today: lines of JR East (Yamanote, Saikyo, Shonan-Shinjuku), Tokyu (Toyoko, Den-en-toshi), Keio Inokashira, Tokyo Metro (Ginza, Hanzomon, Fukutoshin). A Taro Okamoto mural, *The Myth of Tomorrow*, was installed in the station in 2008. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, Shibuya Station. Travel times (weekday commuting hours): Shinjuku 7 min, Ikebukuro 10, Shinagawa 13, Ginza 17, Tokyo Sta. 19, Yokohama 33, Haneda 48, Narita 81. **SOURCED** — Tokyu Livable brochure.

### C. Cultural locations (named)

*Shrines and ritual*
- **Konnō Hachiman-gū (金王八幡宮)** — founded 1092-01-15 by Minamoto Yoshiie; deity Hondawake-no-mikoto; prayer hall 1612; red gate 1769–1801. The Shibuya clan settled around it and built a castle there; the shrine is tied to the origin of the place name. **SOURCED** — rodsshinto.com/13-113-04 (read 2026-10-26). Its annual September festival, the **Shibuya Matsuri**, includes a joint procession of **14 neighborhood mikoshi** from Aoyama through the scramble crossing to SHIBUYA109 (Sunday ~2 p.m.); kagura hall programs include taiko and folk dance; children's games (yo-yo fishing, shateki). **SOURCED** — timeout.com/tokyo/things-to-do/shibuya-matsuri (read 2026-10-26).
- **Shibuya Hikawa Shrine (渋谷氷川神社)** — called Shibuya's oldest shrine (legend: founded by Yamato Takeru); grounds about 4,000 tsubo with "Hikawa no Mori Park," which keeps a **sumo ring** where the Edo-period votive **Konnō sumo (金王相撲)** — one of the "three great suburban sumo" of Edo — was held at the festival. September festival with stalls, ohayashi, kagura and mikoshi at night; a matchmaking prayer every month on the 15th at 15:15. **SOURCED** — tokyu.co.jp/area/shibuya/article/arti-01KE8BVM4Z275S4KXBP11FM08V/ (read 2026-10-26). *(A search summary attributed Konnō sumo to Konnō Hachiman-gū; the page I read places it at Hikawa Shrine.)*
- **Shibuya–Kagoshima Ohara Festival (渋谷・鹿児島おはら祭)** — dance parade on Dogenzaka and Bunkamura-dori, started **1998** with ward cooperation because the Shibuya clan moved to Satsuma (Kagoshima) in the Kamakura period; 29th edition 2026-05-17, about 2,300 dancers. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets of asoview.com, timeout.jp, toretabi.jp; not opened).

*Drinking, eating, meeting*
- **Nonbei Yokocho (のんべい横丁, "Drunkards' Alley")** — formed **1950** when station-area food stalls (yatai) were gathered together, later moved to the present site by land readjustment; **39 shops** (2021) along two lanes beside the tracks at Shibuya 1-25-9; most bars seat about five; all shops closed voluntarily in the 2020 emergency and the alley crowdfunded with T-shirts, towels and lanterns bearing supporters' names. **SOURCED** — tv-tokyo.co.jp/adomachi/backnumber/20210313/141519.html (read 2026-10-26); seats and two-lane layout **SOURCED** — blog.urbanadventures.com/eat-drink-yokocho-alleyway-tokyo/ (read 2026-10-26).
- **Statue of Hachikō** — original by Teru Andō, April 1934 (the dog attended); melted for the war effort; replaced August 1948 by Takeshi Andō (commissioned by the Society for Recreating the Hachikō Statue); the "Hachikō exit" is the standard meeting point. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Hachikō (read 2026-10-26).
- **Center Gai (センター街)** — narrow pedestrian street in Udagawacho over the culverted Udagawa River; fast food, brand shops, clubs; a 4-ton entrance arch collapsed in a typhoon on 1997-06-20 (one death). **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_Gai (read 2026-10-26). Renamed "Basketball Street" in 2011 partly to shed a reputation for youth gangs ("teamers"); the name did not catch on. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets, muza-chan.net and others).

*Music*
- **Spotify O-East complex**, 2-14-8 Dogenzaka — opened as "On Air" in Feb 1991; renamed several times; three venues: O-East (1,300 standing), Duo Music Exchange (700), O-Crest (200); operated by Shibuya-O Group (Shibuya Television). **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya_O-East (read 2026-10-26).

- **Bunkamura** — opened 1989-09-03 by Tokyu; Orchard Hall (2,150 seats), Theater Cocoon (747), The Museum, Le Cinéma; from 2023-04-10 all venues except Orchard Hall closed for redevelopment of the adjacent Tokyu Department Store main store site. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunkamura (read 2026-10-26). Full reopening "end of FY2027": **UNVERIFIED** (search summary).
- **Record shops, Udagawacho** — the area near Shibuya PARCO became a dense record-shop district from the 1990s to early 2000s. **Manhattan Records** founded 1980 behind Shibuya Police Station, at 10-1 Udagawacho since about 1993; hip-hop vinyl; mural by TABOO1, TENGA one and Mark Bode; opened a Seoul branch Feb 2025. **SOURCED** — deeptokyo.jp/en/spot/culture/5175/ (read 2026-10-26). **Disk Union** (Antenna 21 Bldg B1F, 30-7 Udagawacho, floors by genre) and **Recofan** (BEAM Bldg 4F). **UNVERIFIED** (Time Out snippet). **Tower Records Shibuya** — flagship opened 1995, nine floors. **UNVERIFIED** (japantravel snippet).
- **Shibuya-kei** — a late-1980s/1990s pop microgenre named for the district (term adopted c. 1993), built on Shibuya's concentration of import record shops (HMV Shibuya opened 1990, closed 2010; Wave closed 1999; Manhattan Records; Zest); artists Flipper's Guitar, Pizzicato Five, Cornelius; labels Trattoria, Readymade, Crue-L, Escalator. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya-kei (read 2026-10-26, first 100k characters).

*Fashion and youth*
- **SHIBUYA109** — opened April 1979, operated by SHIBUYA109 Entertainment (Tokyu group); designed by Minoru Takeyama as a "fashion community" of small shops; aimed first at women in their early 30s, it became a refuge for the **gyaru** subculture. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya_109 (read 2026-10-26).
- From the 1980s youth culture centered on Center Gai: **"teamers" (チーマー)** groups in American-casual "Shibukaji" dress, then Shibuya-kei and **kogal** high-school girls; earlier the street's main users had been university students, later high-schoolers. **UNVERIFIED** (Japanese search snippets: gotokyo.org/jp, bunshun.jp, tokyu.co.jp); the Tokyu history page **SOURCES** the sequence "teamers, Shibuya-kei, kogal" from the 1980s and "Bit Valley" around 2000.

*Parks, museums, quiet places*
- **Miyashita Park** — opened 1953; in 1964 the Shibuya River beside it was turned into a drainage conduit and the park was lifted onto an artificial deck over a parking lot; a futsal court was added 2006; Nike's 2008 naming-rights plan ("Miyashita Nike Park," skate facilities) involved removal of homeless residents and drew protest; closed 2017; reopened July 2020 as a ~330 m, three-story mall (about 60 shops) and hotel with a rooftop park (lawn, skate park, bouldering wall, sports court). **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyashita_Park; metropolisjapan.com/miyashita-park/ (both read 2026-10-26). The volunteer group **Nojiren** ran a soup kitchen based in the park from 1998; "The Coalition to Protect Miyashita Park from Becoming Nike Park" opposed the rebranding; a 2019 survey by the Advocacy and Research Center for the Homeless estimated actual homeless numbers at least 2.8 times official counts. **SOURCED** — Metropolis (an opinion-leaning article; its claim that ownership passed to Nike Japan is **UNVERIFIED** and conflicts with Wikipedia, which describes naming rights).
- **Shoto Museum of Art** (ward museum, building by Seiichi Shirai) and **Toguri Museum of Art** (Asian ceramics, Imari and Nabeshima; founded Nov 1987) in Shoto. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- The station's growth line by line (1885–1938) and Tokyu's department stores, PARCO, 109 and Bunkamura built a district where a private railway company is also the main landlord and cultural operator. **SOURCED** (Tokyu history page; Bunkamura and 109 pages).
- The huge daytime inflow (ratio 259.7; 505,111 daytime workers) against ~231,000 residents means most people present are commuters, shoppers and visitors. **SOURCED** (Tokyo census tables).
- The station's crowds and the scramble crossing produced the meeting-point culture (Hachikō), the street-crowd events (Halloween, New Year), and then the year-round drinking ordinance. **SOURCED** (nippon.com; Wikipedia).
- Postwar black-market stalls near the station were gathered into Nonbei Yokocho (1950), which survives beside the redevelopment. **SOURCED** (TV Tokyo).
- Import-record shops clustered near PARCO in Udagawacho fed Shibuya-kei and a hip-hop record scene. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia Shibuya-kei; deeptokyo).

## Pick 2 · Harajuku District, Tokyo, Japan [PRIMARY]

*Scope note:* Harajuku sits inside Shibuya ward (Pick 1 holds the ward-level statistics). Officially it is Jingūmae 1–4 chōme; the name "Harajuku" was dropped from addresses in 1965 but survives through JR Harajuku Station. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harajuku (read 2026-10-26).

### A. Snapshot

- **Location and extent:** Jingūmae, Shibuya ward, between Sendagaya (north) and Shibuya (south); "Harajuku" in common use takes in Takeshita Street, Omotesandō and backstreets such as Cat Street. Stations: JR Harajuku (Yamanote Line) and Meiji-jingumae (Tokyo Metro Chiyoda and Fukutoshin). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, Harajuku.
- **Population of Jingūmae:** **NOT FOUND** (searched `渋谷区 神宮前 町丁別 人口 世帯数 2024`; results were real-estate listings; the ward's town-by-town tables were not reached).
- **What it is:** a youth-fashion and shopping district beside two large green spaces, Meiji Jingu and Yoyogi Park; earlier a small post town of the Edo era. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia; fun-japan.jp/en/articles/11873 (read 2026-10-26).
- **Defining feature:** street-fashion youth culture, which grew from the meeting of the postwar U.S. military housing (Washington Heights) and the shrine-avenue shopping street, and was later concentrated by the Sunday pedestrian zone (Hokoten). **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, Harajuku and Yoyogi Park; euronews.com (2020-12-23) article on Harajuku (read 2026-10-26).

### B. What else exists there

**History that set the layout.**
- Site of Japan's first powered flight, 1910-12-19 (Capt. Yoshitoshi Tokugawa), on the army parade ground that is now Yoyogi Park. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoyogi_Park (read 2026-10-26).
- **Washington Heights:** U.S. officers' family housing during the Allied occupation, on the land now occupied by Yoyogi Park and the National Gymnasium; Wikipedia gives 1945–1967. It became the **1964 Olympic Village**; Yoyogi Park opened to the public in **1967**. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, Yoyogi Park; Harajuku; euronews. *(Metropolis' Cat Street article says Washington Heights lasted "until 1964"; the dates are not identical across sources.)*
- **Yoyogi National Gymnasium** (Kenzo Tange) — 1964 swimming, diving and basketball; 2020 Olympic handball. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia, Yoyogi Park.
- Iga ninja were said to have been granted land here in the late 1500s for protecting Tokugawa Ieyasu. **SOURCED as a claim** — metropolisjapan.com/scratching-the-surface-a-history-of-cat-street/ (read 2026-10-26); the source says "allegedly."

**Retail and the fashion economy (named places).**
- **Omotesandō** — the approach avenue to Meiji Jingu, lined with zelkova (keyaki) trees; luxury flagships. **Omotesando Hills** (Mori Building; opened 2005/2006 — sources differ; ~250 m frontage; 130+ shops and 38 apartments; Tadao Ando involved, Studio PDP named as designer) replaced the **Dōjunkai Aoyama Apartments** (Bauhaus-inspired, built 1927 after the 1923 Kantō earthquake); a small section was reconstructed at the southeast end; the demolition raised preservation questions. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omotesando_Hills (read 2026-10-26).
