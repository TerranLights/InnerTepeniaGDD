## Item 1: Cities that grew on top of themselves

### 1.1 Çatalhöyük (Konya Plain, Türkiye): houses rebuilt on their predecessors, entered from the roof

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Ian Hodder, "More on history houses at Çatalhöyük: a response to Carleton et al"
(PDF from Hodder's site, saved by WebFetch, text extracted locally),
https://hodder.squarespace.com/s/More_on_history_houses_at_Catalhoyuk_a_r.pdf. Dead ends: project guidebook PDF
(https://catalhoyuk.ku.edu.tr/sites/default/files/Catalhoyuk-Guidebook-ENGLISH.pdf, "socket closed" twice: died
at the source); UNESCO list page (HTTP 403: died at the source).

**Size, date and population. SOURCED (Hodder):**
- *"The closely packed agglomerated settlement at Çatalhöyük is 12.5 ha in size and the population has been
  estimated at between 3,500 and 8,000 at the height of its occupation (Cessford 2005)."*
- East Mound *"dated from 7100 to 6000 cal BC (Bayliss and Hodder 2015)."* UNESCO's nomination document (search
  46 snippet) gives **21 m of Neolithic deposits dated 7,400 to 6,200 BC**; travel and press sources give about
  20 m and "18 layers" (search 46). Project archive reports (search 1 snippet): houses *"razed and rebuilt in more
  than thirteen construction phases, eventually creating the 20m high mound."* UNESCO World Heritage inscription
  **2012** (search 46, Daily Sabah and others; not read on a UNESCO page).

**Rebuilding on the predecessor. SOURCED (Hodder):**
- *"houses are built up against each other with access through the roofs."* No streets between house clusters
  (UNESCO, search 46 snippet). One 1996 report found roof entry was not the only access at lower levels (search 1
  snippet; UNVERIFIED in detail). A fragment of an **8,500-year-old wooden ladder** above an oven is reported
  (Ancient Origins, search 46; secondary, UNVERIFIED).
- *"James Mellaart (1967), recognized that some buildings were rebuilt many times on the same footprint, reusing
  the stubs of earlier walls."*
- *"The habit of building houses exactly onto the firm foundations of the walls of earlier tightly packed buildings
  meant that house buildings got 'stuck' with a particular plan that continued through time."*
- Houses were lived in **45 to 90 years**, with occupants re-plastering walls and floors (guidebook, search 1
  snippet). Hodder: *"evidence of wall painting in at least one of the up to 450 fine plaster layers on the interior
  wall surfaces."*

**What the layers show about remembering. SOURCED (Hodder):**
- Four building types: *"history houses, multiple burial houses, elaborate houses, and other houses."* History
  houses are *"defined as having at least three phases of rebuilding, and in at least one phase there are large
  numbers of burials (over 10)."* *"There can be between 0 and 62 burials in one building. The size of houses varies
  between 12 and 70 sq m."*
- *"There seem to be two main types of history making ... The first involves repetitive practices in which the same
  activity occurs in the same place in a building over time. The second involves the curation and retrieval of
  objects from earlier buildings and their deposition in later buildings."*
- Examples: a wall painting in one building (B.60) in the same place as red painting on the same wall of the house
  above it (B.59); *"pairs of leopards repeated in consecutive levels"*; in the 65-56-44-10 sequence, *"the repeated
  setting of pots in floors at the base of ladders"*; *"In B.1, a pit was dug down to retrieve an installation or
  relief from the west wall of the main room"*; *"bones from a burial in Building 65 had been retrieved and
  redeposited in a grave in the following Building 56."* Skulls and jaws *"were removed, circulated and deposited
  (for example in other graves or in foundation pits for support posts)."*
- Who was buried under a floor: *"biological affinity played only a minor role in interment location. To some degree
  those that were buried in houses were 'practical' rather than biological kin."* Houses with many burials *"acted as
  preferential burial locations for the inhabitants of other buildings."* Of 31 buildings dug 2000 to 2008, *"only 3
  had no burials."*
- **Lost records (what failed in the excavation of the layers). SOURCED (Hodder):** Mellaart *"dug for 229 working
  days, often with a team of 35 men ... dug a trench 20m deep and excavated 144 complete buildings and 351 complete
  rooms"*; *"his paper record was lost"*; he reported *"over 400 skeletons ... but only 297 reached"* the
  osteologist, and *"27 had no labels."* The later project: *"a team of about 160 researchers and local staff
  excavating for 20 years ... 53 buildings."* **DERIVED:** 229 ÷ 144 = **1.59 working days per building** (Hodder
  gives "1.6").

### 1.2 Basilica di San Clemente, Rome: three stacked buildings

**Pages read (WebFetch):** basilica website history page, https://www.basilicasanclemente.com/?p=1996 · English
Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Clemente,_Rome (first 100,000 characters).

- **Levels. SOURCED (basilica site):** *"two 1st century buildings, separated from each other by a narrow laneway"*;
  the smaller, brick one *"was most probably a Roman house, a domus"*; the larger *"was part of the imperial mint"*;
  around the end of the 2nd century the vaulted lower rooms of the house were *"transformed into a temple complex"*
  (a Mithraeum); around 400 the building became a basilica with a nave and two aisles; the 12th-century basilica
  was built on *"the old walls rising above the rubble."* Wikipedia: *"a three-tiered complex of buildings."*
- **Depth. SOURCED:** the 1st-century street level is *"some 20 meters below that of the present-day"* (basilica
  site). No depths given for the middle level.
- **Why the lower church was buried. SOURCED:** abandoned around 1100 when *"the street level had risen
  considerably"*; *"the old basilica was filled with rubble to the height of the capitals"* (basilica site).
  Wikipedia: *"A now-outdated hypothesis held that the original church had burned out during the Norman sack"*
  (1084); *"no evidence of fire damage in the lower basilica has been found to date."*
- **Rediscovery. SOURCED:** *"in 1857 Father Joseph Mullooly, the prior of San Clemente, realized that the ground
  level was much deeper in the past"*; excavation to 1870 with Giovanni Battista de Rossi (basilica site).
  Wikipedia says *"discovered in the 1860s"* (conflict logged).
- **Water below. SOURCED:** Father Louis Nolan *"built a drainage tunnel between San Clemente and the Colosseum"*
  in 1912 to 1914 to remove water flooding the lower levels (basilica site). The Mithraeum's main room *"was
  discovered in 1867 but could not be investigated until 1914 due to lack of drainage"* (Wikipedia).
- **Who keeps it. SOURCED:** the Irish Dominicans, *"From 1677 to today"* (basilica site); Wikipedia: *"since 1667"*,
  running a residence for priests (conflict logged).

### 1.3 Naples underground: quarries, then cisterns, then shelters, then dumps and tours

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Rosario Varriale, "A new artificial cavity in the subsoil of Naples. The tuff quarry
and the cistern of Corso Vittorio Emanuele," *Opera Ipogea* 1/2024 (first page PDF, text extracted locally),
https://www.operaipogea.it/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/OI-1-2024_Varriale-prima-pagina.pdf · Hydria Project,
Naples aqueducts, "People / Culture,"
https://hydriaproject.info/en/case-studies/naples-ancient-aqueducts-between-civil-uses-and-imperial-grandeur/people-culture
· Galleria Borbonica, official history, https://galleriaborbonica.com/en/history · Il Post, 6 March 2024, "Why
the streets of Naples keep collapsing" (Perché le strade di Napoli continuano a collassare),
https://www.ilpost.it/2024/03/06/perche-napoli-strade-voragini/. Dead end: Tufano 2022 (iris.unina.it PDF, SSL
handshake failure: died at the source).

- **Why there are voids. SOURCED (Varriale 2024, abstract):** the Yellow Neapolitan Tuff *"was the only natural
  stone utilized since the foundation of the town"*; *"The tuff quarries in the town of Naples represent the main
  typology of the cavities explored."* Quarrying under the city is dated by some authors to the 11th century; the
  author's studies put the first underground tuff quarries in the **16th century**, tied to *"laws on the
  prohibition of building around the town promulgated since 1555"* and the **1588** law of the Spanish viceroy
  Count of Miranda. Speleological exploration began **1955**; *"hundreds of artificial cavities have been explored"*
  by the Southern Speleological Center (Centro Speleologico Meridionale) and the speleological group of the Italian
  Alpine Club, Naples; *"Since 1990, the underground cavities of Naples represented an important tourist
  attraction."*
- **One cavity, as found today. SOURCED (Varriale, Italian abstract, translated):** found *"during some works
  inside an apartment located on Corso Vittorio Emanuele"*; entered *"through a single shaft about 16 meters
  deep"*; part used as a rainwater cistern, part an extraction gallery about 10 m long and almost 7 m high,
  through which the tuff for the building above was probably taken; area **136 m²**, void about **700 m³**;
  *"Some sectors have been completely filled by debris dumped illegally through a second shaft that can no longer
  be inspected"* (Alcuni settori sono stati completamente riempiti dai detriti sversati abusivamente); probably
  dug after 1770, at the same time as the building. **DERIVED:** 700 m³ ÷ 136 m² = **about 5.1 m mean height.**
- **Water from below the house. SOURCED (Hydria):** *"All the buildings in the oldest part of the city had wells to
  draw water from well pipes,"* which *"in time were walled over and served as cisterns"*; water was drawn *"from
  public wells and cisterns, in palaces, even in peoples' yards and flats"*; in a building on vico Pazzariello a
  well shaft opened on each floor *"at least until the mid 1960s"*; *"The reservoirs were supposed to be kept clean
  by well workers but this was not always assured."* Search 6 snippet (Hydria): the shaft men or well workers
  (pozzari) were *"the only real experts about the water tunnels"* (UNVERIFIED against the page read, which does not
  use the word).
- **Cholera. SOURCED (snippets, conflicting):** *"Between August 23d and November 9th some 12,345 cases and 7,086
  deaths occurred"* in 1884, and *"in the following year Naples was supplied with pure water from a distant
  mountain stream"* (Popular Science Monthly vol. 43, via Wikisource, search 6); Hydria's timeline says the 1884
  outbreak *"kills 14,000 people in Naples, mostly poor."* The date water abstraction from the old wells stopped is
  UNVERIFIED.
- **Shelters. SOURCED (Hydria):** in World War II *"well shafts were enlarged and spiraling stairs constructed for
  public entry"* into *"air raid shelters 30 to 60 meters below the surface."*
- **A sealed royal tunnel, reused, buried and reopened. SOURCED (Galleria Borbonica history page):** decree of
  **19 February 1853** by Ferdinand II, architect Errico Alvino, an underground viaduct linking the Royal Palace with
  Piazza Vittoria, *"a military passage, in defense of the Royal Palace"*; opened in 1855 *"for public transit for
  only 3 days"*; 1939 to 1945 an air-raid shelter where *"between 5,000 and 10,000 Neapolitans"* found refuge,
  *"equipped with electrical systems and restrooms,"* walls whitewashed with lime, entered by a spiral staircase
  and a passage from the Prefecture; afterwards a *"Police Car Pound"* until **1970**, and a dump for *"all that had
  been extracted from the rubble caused by the two hundred bombings"*; vehicles, motorcycles and statues found
  *"under 8 meters of debris"*; in **2007** *"geologists working in the tunnel discovered a walled
  passage."* Run by a cultural association (Associazione Culturale Borbonica Sotterranea). The year of public
  reopening: NOT FOUND (secondary sources say 2010 or 2014).
- **How many voids, and what goes wrong. SOURCED (Il Post, 2024, translated):** in 2010 the city's geological
  safety service *"counted about 900, for a total area of 60 hectares"* (ne censì circa 900 per una superficie
  complessiva di 60 ettari); the city has *"commissioned a new one to obtain a three-dimensional mapping of the
  subsoil"*; *"a leak from the sewer network or a water pipe is enough to erode the ground"*; risk is highest in
  heavy rain; the first recorded collapse was in **1728** in the Sanità district during a flood; in **1966 to 1969**
  *"over 60 sinkholes, collapses and subsidences were reported"* and *"11 people died in total, there were also
  400 injured"*; on 21 February 2024 a sinkhole in Via Morghen (Vomero) about 10 m wide and 5 m deep swallowed a tree
  and two cars. Older counts (search 13, university thesis snippet): 366 cavities (203 aqueduct, 163 extraction),
  and **461 censused in 1988**. Tufano 2022 (search 5 snippet, *Natural Hazards*): **458 sinkhole events,
  1880 to 2021**, concentrated in the historic center. **DERIVED:** 60 ha ÷ 900 = **about 670 m² per cavity** on
  average.

### 1.4 Matera's Sassi: cave dwellings emptied by law, then revived

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Giuseppe Cosentino, "La Martella, an Architecture of Feeling," *OASE* 117 (PDF, text
extracted locally), https://oasejournal.nl/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Pages-47-61-oase-117-lr-spread-5.pdf ·
Initaly, 20 May 2026,
https://www.initaly.it/en/articolo/concessioni-nei-sassi-di-matera-bennardi-chiede-un-dibattito-pubblico-15484.

- **Who lived there. SOURCED (Cosentino):** *"During the first half of the twentieth century, more than 15,000 people
  lived in such caves (or sassi) mostly peasants in a state of total destitution."* Quoting Carlo Levi (*Christ
  Stopped at Eboli*, 1944): *"Every family generally had only one of these caves as their dwelling, and men, women,
  children and animals all sleep together."* Sociologist Friedrich G. Friedmann (1949 study, Fulbright grant funded
  through the Marshall Plan) found *"a predominantly peasant population who in the mornings traveled to work in the
  countryside around Matera, yet returned every evening to inhabit the Sassi."*
- **How the neighborhoods worked. SOURCED (Cosentino):** the basic unit was *"the 'enclosures', or neighborhood
  units. These elaborate settings form a distinct public space overlooked by eight to ten cave dwellings ... a dense
  web of social and family relationships that made it possible to lend mutual aid or share essential services such
  as an oven for baking bread."*
- **The emptying. SOURCED:** the De Gasperi government passed *"a law on 17 May 1952 that determined the
  redevelopment and closure of the Sassi"* (Cosentino). Numbers moved conflict: about 15,000, 16,000, or 17,000 and
  "two-thirds of the city's inhabitants" (search 8 snippets); "nearly 20,000" (search 48 snippet). New quarters
  named: La Martella, Venusio, Picciano, Spine Bianche, Serra Venerdì (search 8 snippets). Relocation 1952 to 1968,
  or into the 1970s (snippets).
- **La Martella. SOURCED (Cosentino):** Adriano Olivetti (vice-president of UNRRA-Casas) promoted *"the construction
  of 200 dwellings for the displaced population of the Sassi,"* designed by Ludovico Quaroni; an interdisciplinary
  study commission (established 1951, led by Friedmann, with a psychologist, a social worker, a geographer, a
  hygienist, a historian and an anthropologist) supplied *"economic, demographic and psychological insights into the
  needs of the families to be relocated."* Inaugurated **May 1953**, when *"the agrarian world was in decline."*
- **Revival. SOURCED (search 9 snippets, legal texts and UNESCO):** Law 771 of 11 November 1986 declared the
  conservation and recovery of the Sassi of *"overriding national interest"* (preminente interesse nazionale); most
  of the area is State-owned and leased to the Municipality, which manages it; UNESCO World Heritage **1993**.
  Initaly (2026): sub-concession renewals *"could extend up to thirty years"*; a councilor sought public
  consultation over automatic renewals, citing residents' quality of life, *"tourism monoculture"* and
  repopulation incentives for the young. European Capital of Culture **2019** (search 48 snippet).
- **Current residents:** about 3,000 people in the Sassi, about half the dwellings occupied (search 48, undated
  secondary snippet): **UNVERIFIED.**

### 1.5 Chicago: grade-raising, 1850s to 1870s

**Pages read (WebFetch):** English Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_of_Chicago · WBEZ, "Raising
Chicago," https://www.wbez.org/shows/wbez-news/raising-chicago/aae67013-459b-424d-850e-8d9cb6dcadbf · WGN, "Ask
WGN: Chicago's sunken homes,"
https://wgntv.com/news/ask-wgn/chicagos-sunken-homes-are-remnants-of-a-bold-effort-to-raise-the-city-out-of-the-mud/amp.

- **Why. SOURCED (Wikipedia):** drainage was inadequate for two decades, *"culminating in the 1854 outbreak of cholera
  that killed six percent of the city's population."* Ellis Chesbrough's 1856 sewer plan was *"adopted"* by the
  Common Council. WGN: *"the City made a bold decision in 1855: raise the roads four to 14 feet to make room for
  sewers."* WBEZ: *"the sewers were constructed on top of the old street level"*, then *"covered over and the land
  filled in."* *"Raising the city took nearly twenty years."*
- **How buildings went up. SOURCED (Wikipedia):** a Lake Street block raised 4 ft 8 in *"by a team consisting of 600
  men using 6,000 jackscrews"*; *"Businesses operating in these premises were not closed down during the
  operation"*; workers *"permitted to walk at the old ground level among the jacks"*; the Tremont House raised 6 ft
  in **1861** by a consortium led by Ely, Smith and Pullman, *"500 men ... 1,000 jackscrews,"* *"business as usual
  was maintained,"* a guest noticing *"the front steps leading from the street into the hotel were becoming steeper
  every day"*; the Robbins Building raised 27.5 in with *"1,584 jackscrews ... over a three-day period"*; frame
  buildings were more often moved, *"so common as to be considered nothing more than routine traffic."* **DERIVED:**
  6,000 ÷ 600 = **10 jackscrews per man** on Lake Street; 1,000 ÷ 500 = 2 at the Tremont.
- **Who paid. SOURCED (WGN):** the City raised the roads; for buildings, *"the City left it up to building owners to
  either move them (yes, really), leave them below street level or physically lift them up and add a new
  foundation."* *"Instead of paying for the costly process, many homeowners (especially in working-class
  neighborhoods) opted to build a bridge or stairs from their second story leading to the new road."* Bridgeport and
  Pilsen named. Dollar split: NOT FOUND.
- **What was left below. SOURCED:** "vaulted" sidewalks over the void between building and raised street; *"the steel
  beams supporting the tops of the vaulted sidewalks began to crumble and the concrete began to crack. So the costly
  process of filling them in began"* (WGN). Over 2,000 vaulted sidewalks remained as recently as 2001, and the
  Chicago Department of Transportation has an Emergency Vaulted Sidewalk Program (search 11, Wikipedia "Vaulted

### 1.6 Galveston: grade-raising after the 1900 storm

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Galveston Monthly, "The Seawall Chronicles," chapter 4,
https://www.galvestonmonthly.com/seawall-chron/Chapter4.html · American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE), landmark
page,
https://www.asce.org/about-civil-engineering/history-and-heritage/historic-landmarks/galveston-seawall-and-grade-raising
· Bullock Museum Texas Story Project page (held no text: died at the source).

- **Context. SOURCED (ASCE):** the 1900 storm killed *at least 6,000* in a city of *about 44,000*; a Board of
  Engineers (Henry Martyn Robert, Alfred Noble, Henry Clay Ripley) reported on 25 January 1902; seawall 1902 to 1904,
  17,593 ft long, 17 ft above mean low tide (now over 10 miles); grade raising **1903 to 1911**, about **2,000
  buildings** with hand-turned jackscrews, about **500 city blocks**, **16.3 million cubic yards** of sand. Raised
  8 to 17 ft (search 12 snippet). **DERIVED:** 17,593 ft × 0.3048 = **5.36 km**; 16.3 million yd³ × 0.7646 =
  **about 12.5 million m³** of fill.
- **How. SOURCED (Galveston Monthly):** Goedhart & Bates of New York (P. C. Goedhart and Lindon W. Bates) used four
  self-propelled hopper dredges, *"They insisted that the fill be taken from the harbor"*; *"Fill from the harbor would
  be 80-90 percent water"*; a canal *"three miles long, twenty feet deep, and two hundred feet across, with two
  turning basins"* about 100 ft behind the seawall; districts enclosed by levees *"that varied from two to ten feet
  high"*; *"Boardwalks were built all across the city to facilitate pedestrians,"* some *"through the front parlors of
  people's homes"*; residents lived with draining water under their homes, blocked drains, flash floods and
  mosquitoes; sewers, water lines, streetcar tracks and sidewalks were torn up and replaced, and *"Bushes, trees,
  topsoil, and fences all had to be torn up and then replaced."*
- **Who paid. SOURCED (Galveston Monthly):** *"Homeowners were responsible for the task and expense of raising their
  houses"*; total cost to *"the city of Galveston and her residents six million dollars"*; *"Out of 2,156 houses that
  were raised, not one condemnation suit was filed against the city."* Goedhart & Bates lost *"over a quarter of a
  million dollars"* and gave up the job; the American Dredging Company finished in late 1911; the whole took *"over
  nine years."* No federal assistance (search 12 snippet, Waymarking transcription of the plaque).

## Item 2: Sealed and forgotten sections beneath living cities

### 2.1 The Seattle Underground (Pioneer Square)

**Pages read (WebFetch):** English Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle_Underground · Seattle
Department of Transportation (SDOT) blog, 25 February 2010, https://sdotblog.seattle.gov/2010/02/25/sdot-work-down-under/
· SDOT blog, 23 April 2019,
https://sdotblog.seattle.gov/2019/04/23/changes-under-way-new-rules-for-large-trucks-in-pioneer-square/ · Walt
Crowley, HistoryLink essay 3086 (posted 2001, revised 2017), https://www.historylink.org/file/3086 · Priscilla Long,
HistoryLink essay 418 (1998), https://historylink.org/File/418.

- **How it came to be. SOURCED:** after the 1889 fire, *"city planners raised Pioneer Square streets by one to two
  full stories to address chronic drainage problems"* (HistoryLink 3086); about 12 ft, up to 30 ft in low spots, on
  retaining walls (search 14 snippets). *"Retaining walls were constructed along the new roadway grades and the
  areas between them were filled"*; *"pedestrians climbed ladders to go between street level and the sidewalks"*
  (Wikipedia; the ladder sentence is uncited). *"Merchants on the original ground floors survived for a while with
  access via sidewalk stairways, but these were eventually sealed off"* (HistoryLink 3086).
- **What it became. SOURCED (HistoryLink 3086):** *"The 'underground' was abandoned to transients and the occasional
  urban spelunker."* Wikipedia: *"The basements were left to deteriorate or were used as storage"*; flophouses,
  gambling halls, speakeasies and opium dens (tagged "citation needed": UNVERIFIED).
- **The 1907 condemnation: UNVERIFIED.** Wikipedia: *"In 1907, the city condemned the Underground for fear of bubonic
  plague"* (no citation); no primary source located (search 47). **What is SOURCED (HistoryLink 418):** Leong Sheng
  died of plague on 19 October 1907, with *"two other plague deaths during 1907"*; the U.S. Public Health Service
  *"inspected for plague 15,475 trapped rats, and policed sanitation, mainly in the 218 blocks along and behind the
  city's waterfront"*; *"Ordinance #17391 was one of the first rat proofing ordinances enacted anywhere in the United
  States."* The state health department gives "three, possibly seven" human deaths (search 47 snippet).
- **Reopening as a tour. SOURCED (HistoryLink 3086):** a *Seattle Times* item of 3 August 1964 invited readers to
  contact Bill Speidel; *"Speidel will be operating a regular 'Underground Tour' by May 1965"*; the tours built support
  for preservation, and *"The 30-acre neighborhood was designated as a national historic district in 1969"* (a 1970
  National Register date appears in search 24 snippets: conflict logged). A second company, Beneath the Streets,
  began in **2013** (Wikipedia).
- **Who maintains the buried level now. SOURCED (SDOT):** *"Most of the 115 areaways in downtown were created when
  City engineers raised Pioneer Square's streets a full story following the Great Seattle Fire of 1889"*; they lie
  *"generally in the street right-of-way"*; *"In 2000, SDOT undertook a thorough study of the condition of the Pioneer
  Square areaways"* and made emergency repairs; one *"cobblestone retaining wall is in poor condition and has bulged
  out laterally"* (2010). In 2019 vehicles over 10,000 lb were barred from the curb lane of First Avenue between Marion
  Street and Railroad Way South *"due to concerns about the underground road support structures"*; the support
  walls, of brick, rubble and mortar, over 100 years old, sit *"right under the outer edge of the sidewalk"* and in
  places *"could not continue to hold the weight of large trucks and buses"*; the city was *"continuing to conduct a
  thorough inspection of every underground areaway and road support wall in Pioneer Square,"* with monitoring
  equipment and relocated loading zones.

### 2.2 Edinburgh: Mary King's Close and the South Bridge vaults

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Showcaves, Mary King's Close,
https://www.showcaves.com/english/gb/subterranea/MaryKingsClose.html · English Wikipedia, Mary King's Close,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_King%27s_Close · The Real Mary King's Close blog, 17 February 2026,
https://www.realmarykingsclose.com/blog/chesney-grave-marker/ · English Wikipedia, Edinburgh Vaults,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_Vaults. Dead ends: realmarykingsclose.com/history (HTTP 404); Canmore and
Trove.scot site 117430 (HTTP 403); Edinburgh Evening News history article (HTTP 403). All died at the source.

**Mary King's Close (built over, not sealed by plague)**
- **SOURCED (Showcaves):** in **1753** *"the Burgh Council decides to build the Royal Exchange"*; the close *"was one
  of four which had to be destroyed to build the Royal Exchange, now the City Chambers"*; *"At the lower end, the
  lower floors of the buildings were left, strengthened by additional arches."* The top four stories were demolished
  and the rest used as the foundation (search 16 snippet). The popular story that it was sealed during a plague is
  disputed; parts were gradually enclosed and built over (search 16 snippet).
- **Who lived and worked there. SOURCED (Wikipedia):** *"one of the city's busiest trading areas"*; named for Mary
  King, *"a merchant burgess who resided on the Close in the 17th century."* In the 17th century it housed the city's
  poor (search 54 snippet: UNVERIFIED); about a quarter of the city died of plague in that century (search 54
  snippet: UNVERIFIED).
- **The last residents. SOURCED (operator's blog, 2026):** the Chesneys, *"working as part of Chesney & Son Sawmakers
  in the underground workshop,"* *"the final family who lived and worked beneath the Royal Mile"*; *"Their reluctant
  departure in 1902 after the City Chambers served a compulsory purchase notice."* Wikipedia: a compulsory purchase
  order *"for £400"* in **1897** (citation needed). Search 19 snippets give 1903 and a death on 3 October 1906, aged
  74, with conflicting cemeteries.
- **Reopened.** The operator's general manager: *"Back in 2003, we opened our doors under Continuum Attractions"*
  (search 54 snippet: SOURCED, snippet). Tours every 20 minutes, 60 minutes long (Showcaves). Reported use as a
  bomb shelter in World War II (search 54 snippet: UNVERIFIED). Ownership and lease terms: NOT FOUND.

**The South Bridge vaults (built, abandoned, filled, forgotten). SOURCED (Wikipedia unless noted):**
- Work *"did not begin until August 1785"*; completed **1788**; *"approximately 120 rooms or 'vaults' beneath the
  surface of the South Bridge"*; only the Cowgate arch is visible today, the other 18 arches enclosed by tenements
  (search 17 snippet).
- Uses: *"taverns, workshops for cobblers and other tradesmen, as well as storage space."*
- Failure: *"Construction of the bridge had been rushed and the surface was never sealed against water"*; *"The vaults
  began to flood"*; abandonment began as early as **1795**; businesses left in the **1820s** *"mainly because of damp
  and poor air quality"*; *"The vaults also served as additional slum housing for the city's poor."*
- Sealing: *"It is not known when the vaults complex was closed down, with some suggesting as early as c. 1835 and
  others as late as c. 1875."* Rubble was dumped in to make them unusable (search 17 snippets, reason undocumented).
- Rediscovery: *"That people had lived there was only discovered in 1985 during an excavation"* (middens with toys,
  medicine bottles, plates: search 17 and 18 snippets); Norrie Rowan found a tunnel in the 1980s and he and Norman
  Rowan excavated in the 1990s. A 2015–16 recording at 85–87 South Bridge found *"domestic fireplaces and staircases
  dating from c.1790"* (Canmore record, search 18 snippet; page HTTP 403). The north-side vaults are mainly used for
  ghost tours. **DERIVED:** 1795 − 1788 = **abandonment began within 7 years** of completion.

### 2.3 Cincinnati: Over-the-Rhine lagering cellars

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Katie Coburn, *Cincinnati Magazine*, 19 March 2020,
https://www.cincinnatimagazine.com/article/the-brewing-history-that-lurks-beneath-our-feet/ · Stephanie Vermillion,
*National Geographic*, 5 September 2024,
https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/cincinnati-oktoberfest-underground-beer-history.

- **What they were. SOURCED:** lagers needed *"a fermentation temperature of 45 to 55 degrees"* (°F); every
  pre-Prohibition brewery aged lager underground (Coburn). Lager production *"required hand-carving below-brewery
  caves"*; *"The deepest ones we've found go about 40 feet deep, so four stories"* (Michael D. Morgan, in Vermillion).
  Brewing employed *"tens of thousands"* by the mid-1800s (Vermillion). One cellar described as 35 ft wide, 200 ft long,
  with 3-ft walls (search 20 snippet: UNVERIFIED).
- **Why sealed. SOURCED:** *"artificial refrigeration made lagering cellars virtually obsolete"* by the late 1800s
  (Vermillion); after Prohibition ended in 1933 the tunnels were filled, destroyed or covered because of *"chemical
  refrigeration"* (Coburn); many *"sealed up and forgotten"*; owners walled up cellars, sold buildings, and tunnels
  were buried under new construction and trash (search 20 snippet).
- **How they are found again. SOURCED:** the Linck tunnels, *"Forty feet below"* 1818 Race Street, *"10,000 square
  feet of lagering tunnels"* built in 1855, were found by the Model Group in **January 2018** *"during building
  renovations"*; American Legacy Tours began seasonal tours two months later and runs year-round tours under the
  Kauffman Brewery, 1622 Vine Street (Coburn). The Kauffman tunnels (brewery 1860 to 1919) were found in the 1990s
  during renovation into apartments (search 20 snippet). In **2006** local beer experts led the first tours of known
  cellars *"to raise neighborhood funds,"* then searched for sealed cellars using *"blueprints, literature, old news
  mentions, and sledgehammers"*; *"more than a dozen known cellars,"* with *"dozens more"* expected (Vermillion).
  No collapse caused by a cellar was found (search 21: died at the query).

### 2.4 How cities seal, condemn, map, reopen or police old underground space, and what goes wrong

Documented mechanisms, each with the place it is sourced from (no comparison intended).

- **Owners abandon, the public body inherits the structure.** Seattle: stairways *"eventually sealed off"*; the road
  authority now inspects 115 areaways and restricts trucks (2.1). Chicago: vaulted sidewalks filled by the city at
  cost as beams decayed (1.5).
- **Compulsory purchase, then building over.** Edinburgh: the Royal Exchange (1753) built on the lower stories of
  four closes; the last family bought out (1897 order, left 1902) (2.2).
- **Obsolescence, then sealing by owners.** Cincinnati: refrigeration and Prohibition (2.3). Edinburgh vaults:
  flooding and damp, then rubble infill, date unknown (2.2).
- **Reuse as shelter, impound and dump.** Naples: cisterns and a royal tunnel became shelters (1939 to 1945), then a
  police car pound to 1970 and a store for bomb rubble (1.3).
- **Reopening by citizens and associations.** Seattle (Speidel, 1964–65), Cincinnati (neighborhood fundraising tours,
  2006), Naples (a cultural association), Edinburgh (Continuum, 2003). **Neighbor: Berliner Unterwelten (Berlin
  Underworlds Association)**, why: a membership association that researches, documents, opens and runs a city's
  underground. **SOURCED** (https://www.berliner-unterwelten.de/en/the-association/general-information.html):
  founded **1997** *"by people from all walks of life and professions"*; *"more than 520 members (January 2021)"* in
  *"several independent sections"*; a general meeting, a board elected for three years, and a five-member members'
  representation; it documents *"caverns, air-raid shelters, disused railway tunnels, derelict brewery vaults"*;
  active at *"12 of its own predominantly underground locations"*; investors and public bodies approach it on
  underground questions. Founded in response to the post-reunification building boom that was destroying or
  filling bunkers; first Gesundbrunnen tours **1999** (search 22 snippets). Visitor numbers: NOT FOUND.
- **Discovery by renovation.** Naples apartment works (1.3); Linck tunnels 2018 and Kauffman 1990s (2.3); Edinburgh
  vaults 1985 (2.2). **Neighbor: Nottingham Caves Survey**, why: a living city's systematic count and 3D survey of
  hand-cut caves under its center, with caves still turning up under building sites. **SOURCED**
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottingham_Caves_Survey): *"700+ man-made sandstone caves that are known to be
  present"*; *"In total 64 caves were surveyed"*; 2010 to 2012; funded by the Greater Nottingham Partnership and
  English Heritage; owners' permission sought, then laser scanning (billions of points) and fish-eye photography,
  equipment moved by bicycle trailer; built on a British Geological Survey register of the 1980s and the city's Urban
  Archaeological Database. A cave found under a university building site was to be excavated and 3D-mapped *"before
  being filled in"* (search 52 snippet). Other counts: about 450 or 500 (search 52 snippets).
- **Policing and occupation of drains.** **Neighbor: Las Vegas storm drains**, why: present-day occupation of a
  city's underground by people with nowhere else to live, and the agencies that deal with it. **SOURCED** (Geoff
  Schumacher, *Desert Companion*, 1 August 2019,
  https://knpr.org/magazine-desert-companion/2019-08-01/danger-in-the-drains): *"An estimated 300 people live in the
  hundreds of miles of storm drains"* (HELP of Southern Nevada); *"Three people drowned in them in 2016"*; the flood
  district does not fit grates because they *"would block debris"*; before a storm a mobile crisis team goes in *"to
  post warning signs."* Later estimates **1,200 to 1,500** (an outreach director; FOX5, September 2023); Shine A
  Light *"saved 375 people from the washes"* in 2023; **722 miles** of drains, channels and washes plus 113
  detention basins (2026); **27 deaths from local flooding since 1990** (Regional Flood Control District) (search 23
  snippets).
- **What goes wrong, as recorded:**
  - *Collapse:* Naples, 11 dead and 400 injured in 1966 to 1969; a 2024 sinkhole in Via Morghen (1.3).
  - *Lost records:* Çatalhöyük's first excavator's *"paper record was lost"* (1.1). (Lost mine plans in the Ruhr:
    see 3.3.)
  - *Illegal dumping:* a Naples cavity filled through a shaft *"that can no longer be inspected"* (1.3).
  - *Flooding and damp:* the Edinburgh vaults within seven years (2.2); San Clemente's Mithraeum unreachable until
    1914 for lack of drainage (1.2).
  - *Overload of old supports:* Seattle's 1890s walls and modern trucks (2.1).
  - *Getting lost, and legend replacing record.* **Neighbor: Odesa catacombs**, why: the largest-cited urban quarry
    labyrinth, where the mapped share is smaller than the claimed one and stories of the lost outrun the evidence.
    **SOURCED (snippets, search 53; UNVERIFIED as a set):** claimed length 2,000 to 3,000 km, *"Actually surveyed are
    1,700 km"*; dug for limestone, from which most 19th-century houses were built; most entrances closed off, *"it is
    impossible to secure, or even find, all of them"*; reports of people getting lost and dying of dehydration; the
    best-known story (a teenager lost at a New Year party in 2005) is publicized from a 2009 claim and called a hoax
    by one source.

## Item 3: The authority that maps and shores a city's voids

### 3.1 Paris: the General Inspectorate of Quarries (Inspection générale des carrières, IGC)

**Pages read (WebFetch):** IGC, *Activity Report 2024* (Bilan d'activités 2024), Hauts-de-Seine edition, 62 pages
(PDF saved by WebFetch, text extracted locally),
https://cdn.paris.fr/paris/2025/08/07/rapport-d-activite-2024-hauts-de-seine-VBkg.pdf · City of Paris, "The
quarries of Paris, a visit with experts," https://www.paris.fr/pages/les-carrieres-de-paris-visite-avec-des-experts-18949
· City of Paris, "Who was Charles-Axel Guillaumot,"
https://www.paris.fr/pages/qui-etait-charles-axel-guillaumot-l-homme-qui-sauva-paris-de-l-effondrement-5150 · City of
Paris press invitation, 4 October 2017 (PDF, text extracted locally),
https://cdn.paris.fr/presse/2020/03/23/7805e962fda0f0b27d0fe3034e45656e.pdf · Sortir à Paris, updated 20 August 2026,
https://www.sortiraparis.com/en/what-to-visit-in-paris/history-heritage/articles/251018-under-paris-a-team-surveys-300-kilometres-of-tunnels-to-avert-collapses
· English and French Wikipedia, "Inspection générale des carrières."

**Founding. SOURCED:**
- A section of Rue d'Enfer *"caved in on December 17, 1774, 'for several hundred meters'"* (Sortir à Paris); *"a
  series of old underground quarries in Paris collapsed"* in 1774 to 1776 (City of Paris, translated).
- Founded *"by royal decree of Louis XVI on 4 April 1777 as the 'Service des carrières du département de la
  Seine'"* (English Wikipedia); first Inspector Charles-Axel Guillaumot (1730–1807), in office 1777 to 1791 and
  1796 to 1807. Its three original functions: *"to search for underground voids under the city of Paris, to map
  them,"* and to consolidate the ground under public roads and royal property (City of Paris, translated). The City
  calls it *"the first service in the world charged with consolidating a city 'built on a void'"* (le premier
  service au monde chargé de consolider une ville « bâtie sur du vide ») (press invitation, 2017). The first bone
  transfers to the ossuary took place under his supervision (City of Paris).
- In **1899** it had *"eighteen members"* (dix-huit membres); **since 1968** attached to the City of Paris; now part
  of the City's Roads and Mobility Department (Direction de la Voirie et des Déplacements) (French Wikipedia). It
  consolidates **only public land**; a separate Versailles unit created in **1967** covers the outer departments
  (French Wikipedia). Its action in Hauts-de-Seine is governed by a **convention of 21 March 1969** (Activity Report).
  Offices near the Porte de Vanves (14th arrondissement); historic seat at Place Denfert-Rochereau (City of Paris).

**Staff and organization today. SOURCED (Activity Report 2024):**
- *"48 AGENTS de la Ville de Paris"* serving Paris and the inner suburbs, working on **801 ha** of old gypsum
  quarries, **2,349 ha** of old limestone quarries, **35 ha** of old chalk quarries, and a gypsum-dissolution risk zone
  of **about 7,800 ha**. **DERIVED:** 801 + 2,349 + 35 = **3,185 ha of old quarries.**
- Four divisions: (1) **inspections, cartography, research and studies** (collect and keep information on quarries
  and gypsum dissolution, update and reissue quarry and geological maps, inspect accessible quarries under public land
  and by agreement, *"first-urgency expertise"* when damage is reported, monitor groundwater levels, prepare hazard
  maps for risk-prevention plans); (2) **technical-regulatory** (opinions on building applications in Paris and the
  inner suburbs, special prescriptions for building stability and public safety, opinions on local land-use plans,
  information to the public); (3) **studies and works** (soil surveys and consolidation on City land, quality control,
  project-management help to the State and local authorities); (4) **general administration** (telephone reception,
  mail, permit files, budgets, contracts, conventions, staff, training, logistics).
- Teams combine *"engineers, geologists, surveyors,"* technicians and inspectors (Sortir à Paris). A **brigade of
  well-sinker miners** (brigade des puisatiers mineurs): *"Eleven workers of the brigade of well-sinker miners have the
  task of visually inspecting, very regularly, the condition of the galleries"* (City of Paris, translated; undated
  page).

**What they did in 2024. SOURCED (Activity Report 2024):** **287** inspection visits in old quarries under public
land; **31,693** written information requests answered for the four departments *"with an average response time of
1 day"*; **1,047** members of the public received and advised; **2,500** opinions on building applications;
**85** worksites inspected and **79** receipts issued for reinforcement works; **99** incidents linked to the subsoil
(60 in Paris); in Paris only, **25** soil-survey worksites, **6** groundwater-gauge (piezometer) installations and
**9** consolidation worksites. Hauts-de-Seine alone: 614 opinions, 59 inspection visits; a sinkhole (fontis)
surfaced in 2024 in the Parc Rodin, Issy-les-Moulineaux. Yearly series 2020 to 2024 (charts, read as text): visits
68, 276, 268, 211, 287; building opinions 2,614, 2,468, 2,938, 2,542, 2,500 (the pairing of labels to years in the
extracted chart text is uncertain: UNVERIFIED order). An older City page: in **2015** the IGC *"intervened 73 times"*
for incidents (City of Paris). **DERIVED:** 31,693 ÷ about 250 working days = **about 127 written requests a working
day**; 287 visits ÷ 52 = **about 5.5 a week**; 300 km ÷ 48 agents = **6.25 km of gallery per agent.**

**Mapping. SOURCED (Activity Report 2024):** the atlas began in **1859** under Inspector General Eugène de Fourcy
with *"17 sheets at 1/1,000"*; *"Today, the atlas has 454 sheets (general format 80×60 cm)"* showing quarry levels and
how they were worked, consolidations, foundations of buildings that had works, and incidents, *"set against the
cadastre"*; the maps *"are in permanent evolution."* In 2024 sheet 20-41 (Paris 16th, Iéna–Kléber) was reissued to
separate the upper, middle and lower working levels and to show new quarry pockets found during consolidation. The
City page gives **458** maps (conflict logged). Early survey plans were drawn at 1/216 (French Wikipedia).

**Inspection routine. SOURCED (Activity Report 2024):**
- The brigade watches *"300 km of galleries"* under the public land of Paris and the neighboring departments, and,
  by agreement, under the land of the Paris transit operator (RATP), the national railway (SNCF), the Ministry of the
  Armed Forces and the Catacombs of Paris. *"The brigade and the engineers of the IGC monitor the aging of these
  quarries and, when needed, report accelerating degradation and threats to public safety."*
- The IGC checks that galleries under public roads are not clogged by grout overflowing from consolidation of
  private property; the contractor is ordered to restore the gallery.
- *"In 2024, during an inspection, an IGC team detected carbon monoxide fumes in a gallery where workers were using a
  jackhammer powered by a generator. Alerted by the 4-gas detector, the team cut the power, ventilated the quarry,
  warned the workers of the danger and allowed their safe evacuation."*
- The brigade also does small maintenance and consolidation work, maintains the remote sensors, and takes manual
  groundwater readings in Paris. Teams check *"pillars, vaults and consolidations"* for cracks, water ingress and
  ground movement, and watch heavy rain, rising groundwater and drought (Sortir à Paris). The City page gives
  **280 km** of galleries (conflict logged).

**Consolidation methods. SOURCED:**
- Historic: masonry pillars, with *"the intermediate space systematically backfilled"* (French Wikipedia).
- Today (Activity Report): filling of underground limestone or gypsum quarries; injection of gypsum dissolution
  pockets; pressure injection of cement grout; and *"treatment by injection of loosened ground ... can only be done
  below 5 meters of depth so as not to damage the utility networks located in the upper layer."* Surveys use two
  geotechnical firms on framework contracts: microgravity and ground radar first, then destructive drilling with
  parameter logging, pressuremeter tests and laboratory tests.

**How it works with builders. SOURCED (Activity Report 2024):**
- *"Under prefectoral orders, planning services consult the IGC"* for applications in risk zones. Opinions scale with
  risk: prescriptions or recommendations for a soil study, shallow or deep foundations, or underground consolidation
  *"by masonry or injection."* If knowledge of the subsoil is insufficient, *"the IGC issues an unfavorable opinion and
  states what must be gathered"* for a new one. Prescriptions are *"an indivisible part of the planning permit"*;
  works inspectors visit the sites; at completion the applicant submits an as-built file (dossier de récolement), and
  the works are then added to the maps. *"The IGC has received building permits since 1881"* (City of Paris).
- For incidents on any land, private or public, an agent can visit and give a preliminary diagnosis; studies and
  repairs *"must be carried out by specialized firms chosen and paid by the owner of the affected property."*
- Large public projects (transit, developers) get dedicated support to adapt technical notices.
- About **20%** of Paris buildings stand above a quarry (City of Paris); *"Nearly one in five"* (Sortir à Paris).

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Korena Di Roma Howley, *Eos*, 2 March 2026,

- **Where and how deep. SOURCED:** *"Nearly 200 meters (650 feet) below the grassland near Hutchinson"* (Eos);
  *"Fifty-four stories underground!"* (FOX). Salt laid down about 275 million years ago (Permian) (Wikipedia).
- **The company. SOURCED:** UV&S *"was incorporated on June 11, 1959"*, chosen from many sites, privately owned (Hutch
  Post); first stored oil and gas records (Eos); president Jeff Ollenburger: *"We will store anything that's not
  illegal, flammable, or explosive"* (Eos). Holdings: film reels and props, government records, data cards, paper
  (Eos; Hutch Post); *"over 6 million boxes of films, data, scripts, costumes"* and, for one TV station, *"over six
  hundred boxes of news footage"* (FOX, quoting partner Lee Spence). Other clients: banks, Fortune 500 companies, oil
  and gas firms, foreign governments, private citizens and a few preppers (Vice).
- **Space. SOURCED (Eos):** *"UV&S currently occupies 50 of approximately 900 available acres,"* in bays *"each about
  the size of a football field"*; *"We will never run out of space."* The mine covers about **980 acres**; its chambers
  end to end would run about 150 miles (Wikipedia). **DERIVED:** 50 ÷ 900 = **5.6%** of available space; 980 acres ×
  0.004047 = **3.97 km²**.
- **Climate. SOURCED:** *"around 20°C (68°F) and 45% humidity"* (Eos); refrigerated panels built for material that
  needs lower temperature and humidity (Eos).
- **Getting down. SOURCED:** an elevator *"approximately 2.5 × 1.3 meters (8 × 4 feet)"* (Eos); *"a hoist a
  minute-and-a-half down a mine shaft"* (FOX); *"The main elevator lifts for the mine operate just on the other side
  of a plastic curtain"* (Hutch Post). Visitors to Strataca: *"a 90-second trip"* in a double-deck elevator holding
  fifteen per deck; *"Hard hats and rescue breathers have been required since 1954"* and have never been needed
  (Wikipedia). UV&S shared the cost of the new shaft with the museum (Wikipedia).
- **Work below. SOURCED:** workers barcode reels so staff can find them and ship them back to studios (Vice);
  generators underground hold fuel *"for only a couple of months"* (Vice). Employees are trained in confidentiality
  (company text, search 35 snippet). **Staff count: NOT FOUND** (searches 35 and 49).
- **The working mine alongside. SOURCED:** Hutchinson Salt Company (began 1923 as Carey Salt Company) still mines rock
  salt *"primarily used for deicing roads in winter"* (Eos); a loaded skip lifts four tons per round trip of about
  three minutes (Wikipedia).
- **Strataca. SOURCED (Wikipedia):** opened **1 May 2007** as the Kansas Underground Salt Museum, renamed **4 June
  2013**; galleries on mining, geology, the miners' transport cars ("mantrips") and a replica UV&S vault; a "Dark

  about nuclear threats"* the Bank kept *"Paper copies of essential files from the head office and all three branch
  offices"* in the mine; the space was *"equipped to serve as a bomb shelter, with food and supplies to accommodate
  400 people"*; *"Strict protocols controlled access"*; used until **1997**, then moved *"closer to Kansas City."*

### 4.2 Iron Mountain, Boyers, Pennsylvania: a limestone mine as a records city

**Pages read (WebFetch):** Center for Land Use Interpretation (CLUI), https://clui.org/ludb/site/iron-mountain-boyers ·
*Butler Eagle*, 24 March 2015, https://www.butlereagle.com/?p=328422 · Iron Mountain, "Stories from the

- **History and size. SOURCED (CLUI):** U.S. Steel dug limestone here *"from 1902 to 1952 ... for use in steel mills

- **Who works there. SOURCED, conflicting:** *"About 2,500 people work there"* (*Butler Eagle*, 2015, with about

- **Cooling by the mine's own water. SOURCED (*Butler Eagle*, 2015):** a $200 million data-center expansion to use

- **The retirement office in the mine. SOURCED:** *"600 people who work in a limestone mine"* for OPM, staffing up *"by
  at least 200 people in the past five years"*; room for **28,000 file cabinets**; the work runs *"at the speed of
  human fingers and feet"*; a former worker: *"On a daily basis, we would get from five to 50 e-mails"* asking staff to
  search their desks for case files (*Billfold*, quoting the *Washington Post*, 2014). In 2025: *"more than 700 people
  worked in the Boyers mine"* (DOGE), *"like a thousand"* (Musk); average processing since October 2023 of *"47 to 73
  days"*; the claim that the elevator limits throughput is unverified; a 2019 GAO report cited *"continued reliance on
  paper applications and manual processing"* (Snopes). More than $100 million spent on failed modernization since
  the late 1980s (GAO via an outlet, search 37 snippet: UNVERIFIED).
- **Other holdings. SOURCED (CLUI):** the Corbis image archive (now part of Getty Images); films and documents for many
  companies.

### 4.3 Barbarastollen, Oberried (beyond T10): how rarely it is opened and how the containers are made

**Pages read (WebFetch):** CHEManager, 4 December 2017,
https://chemanager-online.com/de/themen/edelstahl-rostfrei-bewahrt-das-gedaechtnis-der-nation-im-barbarastollen ·
Sebastian Stoll, evangelisch.de, 30 May 2011, https://www.evangelisch.de/inhalte/105035/30-05-2011 · English
Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarastollen_underground_archive. Dead ends: Bundestag paper 18/5147 and
the parliamentary press service item (SSL errors: died at the source).

- **How often. SOURCED:** *"Four times a year, new deliveries of cultural property arrive at the tunnel"* (Vier Mal im
  Jahr kommen neue Lieferungen von Kulturgütern im Stollen an) (evangelisch.de, 2011); *"Every year, a good two dozen
  more are added"* (Jedes Jahr kommen gut zwei Dutzend hinzu) and *"At least 1,000 more stainless steel containers
  still have room here"* (CHEManager, 2017). **DERIVED:** 1,000 ÷ about 24 a year = **about 42 years** of remaining
  space at that rate.
- **How a container is made. SOURCED (CHEManager, translated):** the film is *"35 mm wide polyester film,"* welded into
  large reels *"each 1,320 m long"*; *"16 of these large reels are stacked one above another in the lower part of a
  stainless steel container"*; after conditioning, the two halves are joined in a climate chamber and *"sealed airtight
  with a copper ring and fixed with 18 screws,"* giving *"a dust- and pollutant-free microclimate"* at 10 °C and 35%
  relative humidity inside. Early containers were welded and rusted at the seams, so production switched to seamless
  deep drawing (search 38 snippet). **DERIVED:** 16 × 1,320 m = **21.1 km of film per container**; 1,500 containers ×
  21.1 km = **about 31,700 km**, consistent with the reported 31,347 km (2016).
- **The tunnel. SOURCED:** *"The passage is 700 meters long and was once driven into the middle of the mountain to
  search for iron ore"* (evangelisch.de, translated); two storage galleries of 50 m branch off the main tunnel behind
  pressure doors, with containers on double-tiered shelving; tunnel air a constant 10 °C at about **70%** relative
  humidity (CHEManager). Cover: *"buried under 400 meters of rock"* (Wikipedia) versus *"almost 300 m"* of gneiss and
  granite (Vogt et al., cited in T10) (conflict logged).
- **Who films and why. SOURCED (evangelisch.de):** the federal office photographs documents *"that the state
  archivists consider important enough"* with a camera worth €40,000; *"Production is exclusively analog."* A federal
  civil-protection official (Lothar Porwich) on the 500-year rating: *"Presumably it's even more likely to be 1,000"*;
  on whether anyone will be able to read the film: *"Personally, I trust in people's curiosity."*
- **Status. SOURCED (Wikipedia):** one of **five** sites worldwide under special protection of the Hague Convention
  (the others Vatican City and three refuges in the Netherlands). Microfilmed items listed include the Peace of
  Westphalia, the papal bull threatening Luther, Otto I's coronation document, Cologne Cathedral plans and the Basic
  Law. Staff count and access rules: NOT FOUND.

### 4.4 The working life and rules of people who keep records deep underground (neighbors)
