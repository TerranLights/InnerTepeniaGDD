## 1 · #5 Winnipeg and The Forks (Manitoba, Canada)

Round 2 goes into the neighborhoods, halls, clubs and groups that make up ordinary life, and chases Round 1's open threads (rail-shift culture, off-season third places, present-day everyday rules, food, festival volunteer counts and funding, Indigenous organizations' own sources).*

### A. Other settings (sub-areas and what daily life is like in them)

**The community-center network: the shared building of every neighborhood [K1, K10, K14, P4, P7]**
- **63 independent community centers operating 81 recreation facilities.** The **City owns the buildings and grounds; volunteer boards run the centers**, hire staff and deliver programs. In **2025 over 8,000 volunteers** worked across the 63 centers (Tanis Knowles Yarnell, Manager of the Community Centers Division). Facilities include arenas, gyms, commercial kitchens, sports fields, **outdoor ice rinks**, basketball and tennis courts, spray pads, wading pools, off-leash areas; a few host food-waste drop-off stations (winnipeg.ca, "Celebrating the volunteers who help local community centers thrive", SOURCED).
- The **General Council of Winnipeg Community Centers** (GCWCC) was created in **1971** when the 13 municipalities and the old City merged into the amalgamated city; it co-administers city renovation grants, and a 2006 management agreement formalized its role; City Council appoints three community representatives to its board (winnipeg.ca / downtownwinnipegbiz snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- Older pattern (1930s, North End): **neighborhood rinks were volunteer-built**, some from a log structure and one from a CPR boxcar; children played street hockey with frozen horse droppings as pucks and old magazines as shin pads (Jerry Szach, "Playing in the Shadow of the Ukrainian Labor Temple", *Manitoba History* No. 60, 2009, SOURCED). [K14, R]

**The North End in the 1930s, as remembered (Szach, MHS No. 60, SOURCED) [P3, P4, P9]**
- Unpaved streets, gapped wooden sidewalks, horse-drawn delivery wagons stuck in summer mud; children sucked ice slivers from ice-company trucks and chewed warm street tar; peddlers sold watermelons, live chickens and bought rags.
- A **Saturday promenade along Selkirk Avenue**, with Ukrainian shops to the west and Jewish businesses toward Main Street.
- Grocers ran **tabs**; families shopped daily in small amounts; coal grades were bought by season (poplar, tamarack, Souris, Drumheller anthracite). Bread came from the **Winnipeg Workers Bakery**, described as a legacy of the General Strike; milk by horse-drawn delivery from co-ops and creameries. [K5, K12]
- Lemko house parties with violin and button accordion, children sleeping on the coats.
- Health: a neighborhood drugstore supplied cheap care and home remedies, including leeches; an unlicensed healer treated the author's leg after a specialist proposed amputation. [K14]
- **Selkirk Avenue c. 1900s–1910s** (Abe Padolsky memoir, MHS No. 26, SOURCED): "the heart of the Jewish North-end", with the Hebrew School and the chief rabbi's house nearby; fur and cap stores, two grocers, a five-cent store, butchers, chicken-and-egg dealers, barbers; a footpath over the CPR tracks used by "those who worked in the needle trades"; the **Queen's Theater** "played to capacity with Jewish talent"; Selkirk Park's four gates were "closed at night and Sundays with a chain and lock" [K6]. Later: one synagogue was described as one of a dozen in the North End and now one of two; the **Holy Ghost Polish parish** (1898) still operates though the Polish community has largely moved away, and Polish grocers and travel agents remain on Selkirk (bikewinnipeg.ca snippet, UNVERIFIED); "decades of commercial decline" on Selkirk (Winnipeg real estate news snippet, UNVERIFIED). The North End's Jewish population is described as growing from 3 (1886) to 11,746 (1916) (Daniel Stone, MHS, snippet, UNVERIFIED; figure as reported). [K9]

**Railway shop towns inside the city (Round 1 NOT FOUND: rail-shift culture) [K7, K14, P8]**
- **Transcona** (now a Winnipeg ward): founded around a central repair-and-maintenance shop shared by the Grand Trunk Pacific and National Transcontinental railways; work began **1908** on flat land east of the city; incorporated **6 April 1912**; by 1916 the second-largest town in Manitoba; **construction camps first, then family houses** built by men expecting years of work; boarding houses (the Queen's Court hotel had 60 rooms for boarders; the Palma Hotel rented rooms mainly to CN employees); **at the 4 p.m. shift change CN workers poured out of the shops down Bond Street and stopped traffic**; a café whose walls were covered in team photos was where people gathered after games; Ukrainian Catholic parish from 1913; a Scout hall built by volunteers through a "buy-a-brick" campaign; St. Michael's Parish Hall housed over 450 evacuees in the 1950 flood; amalgamated with Winnipeg 1972; almost 30,000 people by 1985 (MHS No. 13, "A Trip Through Time in Transcona", 1987, SOURCED). The shop whistle marked start of work, breaks and shift ends (Transcona Museum snippet, UNVERIFIED). About **33 steam locomotives built there 1926–1931** (Transcona Museum snippet, UNVERIFIED). CN's Transcona Shops still operate; current employee numbers NOT FOUND.
- **Weston** (CPR Weston Shops): a neighborhood that grew with the shops and declined as shop jobs fell; the Weston yards separate south Winnipeg and the West End from the North End (Wikipedia "Weston, Winnipeg" snippet, UNVERIFIED; article flagged as needing citations). The CPR announced 2,000 new shop jobs during the 1880s boom; a Great War memorial tablet at the shops was dedicated 28 April 1922 (MHS snippets, UNVERIFIED). Present workforce: NOT FOUND.

**The Forks in the off-season (Round 1 NOT FOUND: off-season detail) [K10, K3, P3]**
- Winter program "Learn to Winter" (2026): every weekend in January–February, free; warming-hut tours; drop-in skating lessons; DJs on the ice under the canopy; a winter workout; **storytelling and drumming around the fire in a teepee**; ice basketball on the river trail; sponsored by Manitoba Liquor & Lotteries; an Elder is among the named instructors. Winter hours: Market shops 10–6 (to 7 Fri–Sat); food hall 11–9 (to 10); the bar-and-food-hall space to 10 (11 Fri–Sat); weather cancellations posted online (theforks.com "Learn to Winter Together", SOURCED).
- Money: The Forks Market revenue about **$7.76 million** (year to 31 March 2025, up from $7.27 million); parking revenue fell from about $6.16 million to $5.56 million (audited statements, snippet, UNVERIFIED; PDF not opened). This extends Round 1's note that parking is the main income.

**Naawi-Oodena (the former Kapyong Barracks) [K9, K1, P5, P9]**
- Land of the former Kapyong Barracks repatriated in **2022** to a joint reserve land base owned by the **seven Treaty One First Nations** (Brokenhead Ojibway, Long Plain, Peguis, Roseau River Anishinaabe, Sagkeeng, Sandy Bay Ojibway, Swan Lake) (treaty1.ca, SOURCED for the list; CBC/CTV snippets, UNVERIFIED for 2022). Described as Canada's largest urban reserve; 2021 master plan for up to 3,000 homes, over a million sq ft of commercial space, sports and community spaces and a Treaty One administration center, about $1.2 billion; the **Gaawijijigemangit Agreement** (municipal development and services agreement) signed with the City; construction began 2024; first business a gas bar (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Treaty No. 1 signed **3 August 1871** at Lower Fort Garry "after several days of discussions and ceremonies" (treaty1.ca, SOURCED).

### B. Subcultures

**Indigenous urban institutions (Round 1 open thread: Indigenous organizations' own sources) [K1, K4, P9]**
- **Indian and Métis Friendship Center**: opened **1959** at 376 Donald Street after a 1958 conference motion by Mary Guilbault of the Winnipeg Urban Indian Association; early work centered on group programs; described as the start of the national Friendship Center Movement; the Manitoba Historical Society dates informal operation to 1951; funding suspended by the provincial association in early 2017, the center closed, and it was announced (2023) as returning as the **Winnipeg Indigenous Friendship Center** at 45 Robinson Street (search snippets of Canadian Encyclopedia, MHS, VNFC, Global News, UNVERIFIED). Canada's History confirms the old and new names and ties friendship centers to post-war First Nations migration to cities; by 1971 there were 43 in Canada (canadashistory.ca, SOURCED). Thesis: "A place of awakening: the formation of the Winnipeg Indian and Metis Friendship Center, 1954–1964" (University of Manitoba, 1993; record only).
- **Ma Mawi Wi Chi Itata Center**: begun in **fall 1984** by community members, primarily Indigenous women working through Indigenous women's organizations, to "reclaim Aboriginal people's inherent role and responsibility as the caregivers" of Indigenous children and families; the name is given as Ojibway for "we all work together to help one another"; after 1997 community consultation it repositioned itself from "expert" to "helper"; about 200 staff (CCPA 2009); about 50 programs from 11–18 sites (snippets of archived site, Justice Canada, CCPA, CanadaHelps, UNVERIFIED). This is the organization whose workers founded the Bear Clan Patrol (Round 1).
- **Thunderbird House** (Whaka Pimadiziiwii Pinaysiiwigamic / Circle of Life Thunderbird House), 715 Main Street at Higgins: opened at the **vernal equinox, March 2000**; Douglas Cardinal design with **four entrances aligned to the cardinal directions** and a copper roof suggesting an eagle with spread wings; "spiritual anchor" of the **Neeginan** project; houses counselling, healing, workshops and celebrations; the site previously held outreach programs and cheap housing in former hotels (Winnipeg Architecture Foundation, SOURCED). Sweat lodges, sharing circles, drum and powwow workshops and sacred fires on request (archived site snippet, UNVERIFIED). Long-running funding trouble: leaking roof; a 2015 CCPA/Free Press column says it "isn't used nearly enough", does not earn enough to be self-sustaining, and lacked city support; community groups rent it for events (policyalternatives.ca, SOURCED); city funding halted for 2017 (CBC snippet, UNVERIFIED; cbc.ca not fetched). [K8, K9]
- **Rooster Town to the North End:** in 1959 the City offered remaining Rooster Town families **$75 to move by May**, threatening eviction otherwise; social-services departments pressed families to relocate to the North End, where housing was cheaper (Canadian Encyclopedia / City of Winnipeg Rooster Town materials, snippets, UNVERIFIED). Adds to Round 1's Rooster Town note. [K9]
- Indigenous population growth is usually credited to off-reserve migration, but the City Archives research guide notes academic work also points to birth rates and rising self-identification (snippet, UNVERIFIED). Historical neighborhood shares for the 1950s–60s: NOT FOUND.
- **Métis music and dance:** Métis jigging is described by the Manitoba Métis Federation as created in the Red River area in the mid-1800s; the most-cited origin story is an 1860 wedding dance where a fiddler composed a tune a priest named; the tune is played at almost all Métis functions; Red River Jig contests are held at Festival du Voyageur (February 2025) and at a Métis pavilion at Folklorama; **Asham Stomperfest** is a Manitoba jig festival (CTV/CBC/MMF snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K2, K13]

**Ukrainian halls and their split (K4, K13) (Szach, MHS No. 60, SOURCED)**
- The Ukrainian Labor Temple (Round 1) ran **Ukrainian night school**, a drama group staging plays **every Saturday for over twenty years**, **Friday films** (Eisenstein, Chaplin), a **Sunday-morning Junior Section** (woodcarving, model building, chess, Easter-egg painting, embroidery), and **Sunday concerts** of mandolin orchestras, choirs and folk dance with admission by silver collection; it printed a Ukrainian daily; the federal government **closed the Ukrainian Labor Temples in 1941**, while the Polish Labor Temple stayed open.
- The community was divided between the pro-Soviet labor halls, the nationalist halls (Prosvita Hall; a nationalist paper) and the churches (Orthodox vs. Catholic). Families chose churches partly on politics; baptism could be arranged to satisfy school registration. [K9]
- **Ukrainian Christmas**: Orthodox and Ukrainian Catholic churches keep Christmas on **7 January** (Julian calendar); a **12-dish Holy Supper** on Christmas Eve honors the apostles and contains no meat or dairy (Nativity fast); New Year on **13 January** with Malanka dances at halls through January, dates staggered between communities (CBC/Manitoba Co-operator snippets, UNVERIFIED; cbc.ca not fetched). [K2, K12]

**Filipino Winnipeg [K4, K7, P9] (MHS No. 77 "Stories of Bayanihan and Belonging, Part 2", SOURCED; Canada's History "Tailor Made", SOURCED)**
- Arrival waves: nurses first (Misericordia General Hospital, 1950s–60s, snippet); about 100–150 Filipino families in the late 1960s; **Filipina garment workers recruited from October 1968**, when Winnipeg's garment trade had about **5,200 textile workers** and firms were running at about half capacity; the program accepted only single applicants, so many women hid partners and children; most were paid **piece rates**; one firm (Silpit) arranged apartments, gave loans of $150–200 forgiven after two years of work; jobs were so plentiful a worker who quit in the morning could be hired by afternoon; workers already in Winnipeg met new arrivals at the airport; later family sponsorship of spouses, parents and siblings (Canada's History, SOURCED). 450 garment workers came in 1968 and 1,211 more by 1972 (parliamentary debate snippet, UNVERIFIED). Winnipeg twinned with Manila 1979 (Canada's History, SOURCED).
- Neighborhoods: 1970s arrivals moved into the **West End** or the **Maples and Tyndall Park** area (MHS, SOURCED).
- Organizations: Filipino-Canadian Association (1960); a social club formed by 1967 that merged with it in 1970 as the **Philippine Association of Manitoba**; Filipino Domestic Workers' Association of Manitoba (1988, 150 members then); Philippine Heritage Council of Manitoba; Filipino Nurses' Association (~100); **Migrante Manitoba** (office at the UFCW Training Center, $10 monthly membership); Knights of Rizal scholarships since 1997; **Dr. José Rizal Park** opened 21 June 2014 (MHS, SOURCED).
- Customs: **Philippine Heritage Week** each June: mass, religious parade (Santacruzan, Santo Niño), Independence Day Ball, picnic in Assiniboine Park, about 500 attend, usually at least five roast pigs; **Manitoba Filipino Street Festival** in August; newcomers housed, employed and connected through relatives (the article frames this as bayanihan, a Filipino word for communal help); typhoon relief fundraising (MHS, SOURCED). [K1, K2, K12]
- The Provincial Nominee Program (below) names Filipino and German Mennonite networks as key sources of nominees (IRPP, SOURCED).

**Mennonites [P9]**
- Winnipeg is described as the largest urban concentration of Mennonites in the world: 19,105 in the 1981 census; more than 20,000 and about 45–50 Mennonite churches more recently; six churches existed by 1940 and many more came with the 1950s–60s rural-to-urban migration; Mennonite Brethren Bible College and Canadian Mennonite Bible College were founded in Winnipeg and later joined in **Canadian Mennonite University** (Canadian Encyclopedia / University of Waterloo / Plains Humanities snippets, UNVERIFIED). Farmer sausage is attributed to Manitoba Mennonites (food-blog snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K12]

**Newcomers: the Provincial Nominee Program [K1, K8, P9] (IRPP study, SOURCED)**
- Canada–Manitoba Immigration Agreement 1996; nominee annex **1998**; first nominees **1999** (418 per CIC News snippet, UNVERIFIED). Why: Manitoba's population grew only 16.2% in 1971–2006 vs. 46.6% nationally; small communities were shrinking. Before the program about **90% of new arrivals settled in Winnipeg**; in 2000–2008, 32% of nominees planned to settle outside it. One-year retention above 80% (1999–2005 arrivals). Existing family and friends act as "magnets"; Manitoba took over **settlement and adult language services** under the 1998 annex; 52% of surveyed nominees used agencies, 39% family and friends, 9% employers. More than 130,000 immigrants via the program by 2018 (provincial release snippet, UNVERIFIED).

### C. Leisure industries and what locals do when visitors leave

- **The lake:** in summer residents leave the city for cottage country: Lake Winnipeg beaches (Grand Beach, about 80 km north on PTH 59, with three km of white sand; Gimli and Winnipeg Beach on the west shore, about an hour away; **Victoria Beach**, a private resort hamlet operated by cottage owners) and the Whiteshell (Falcon and West Hawk lakes, about 126 km east on Highway 1) (Travel Manitoba / Manitoba Parks / Frommer's snippets, UNVERIFIED). Weekend traffic data and the history of cabin culture: NOT FOUND.
- **Ice fishing:** heavy winter traffic on the Red River near Lockport and Selkirk and on Lake Winnipeg's south basin; temporary **ice-shack villages** form near Gimli; an ice village at Lockport; a portable village moved to FortWhyte Alive inside the city; target walleye, perch, pike; warm autumns delay the season (CBC/Travel Manitoba/Interlake Tourism snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K10, K14]
- **Curling:** the **Granite Curling Club** (founded 1880 or 1881; oldest club in western Canada and parent of other clubs, which split off from it) used only granite stones and "old country" rules; first clubhouse with an indoor rink 1892; current building opened for 1913–14 on the Assiniboine with **nine sheets of ice**, spectator space on four sides, a members' lounge with a big fireplace, a games room for billiards and cards, and a separate waiting room for women; women and youth admitted later "as the times changed"; first local club with artificial ice (1953) (MHS No. 14, SOURCED). The annual **Manitoba Bonspiel** dates from 1884 or 1889 and drew a couple of hundred rinks by 1900, heavily from rural Manitoba (heritage report snippets, UNVERIFIED; its "world's largest" claim is the source's). [K10, K2]
- **Hockey as civic gathering:** the **Whiteout Street Party** on Donald Street beside the arena during Jets home playoff games; capped at 5,000 people; $10 tickets in 2024 with $5 to United Way Winnipeg; the custom of fans wearing white is dated to 1987 (CBC/CTV/Global/NHL snippets, UNVERIFIED; the CTV retrospective returned only its headline). [K3, K10]
- **Late-night food:** **Salisbury House**, founded **1931** by Ralph Martin Erwin as a 10-seat all-night counter café on Fort Street; a red-peaked roof became its mark ("Red-peaked roof now at 17 sites", 1963 headline); 45 locations listed over time (40 in Winnipeg; others in Brandon, Flin Flon, Norway House, Selkirk, Steinbach), many now closed; sold 1979 to a Montreal company; bought 2001 by a Manitoba consortium; 2017 partly sold to a group including the **Métis Economic Development Fund** (MHS "Manitoba Business: Salisbury House", SOURCED; the all-night origin from MHS/Wikipedia snippets, UNVERIFIED). **Kelekis' Restaurant**, 1100 Main Street, opened 1946 by Chrys Kelekis (an immigrant who began with a pushcart of sweets and popcorn), run by his six daughters, closed 2013 (MHS Historic Sites, SOURCED). [K10, K12]

### D. Third places

- **Community centers** (above): the citywide shared building, volunteer-run.
- **Halls and the fundraising dance.** A ticketed fundraising dance in a community center or Legion hall is described as "a particularly Manitoba-grown concept", held for a wedding (traditionally run by the wedding party, letting people who could not be invited to the wedding support the couple), a charity or a community group; tickets about $10, sold person to person; cheap hall bar with budget beers and one brand each of rye, rums and vodka; a **midnight buffet of Ukrainian-style garlic sausage, cold cuts, rye bread, pickles and cheese**; prize tables, 50/50 draws and a grand prize drawn about 11 p.m.–midnight; prizes often donated by local businesses (socialsguide.com, SOURCED; the local name for the event type is in the slang file). **Rules:** a **social occasion liquor permit** is required whenever liquor is sold or served outside a licensed premises or private home; apply at least **four weeks** ahead; **non-profits may hold at most 12 permits a year, individuals and businesses at most 2**, with matching raffle-license limits; raffle licenses free with the permit and no financial reporting (LGCA "Social Occasion Liquor Permits", SOURCED). Guests generally must be invited or hold advance tickets, not pay at the door; charitable fundraisers are open to the public and must file a financial statement within 14 days (LGCA terms PDF snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K6, K8, K10, K12]
- **Curling clubs** (above). **The Forks** in winter (above). **Thunderbird House** and the friendship center (above). Transcona's café with team photos; Selkirk Avenue's shops and synagogues; the **Hebrew Sick Benefit Hall** (named in Szach, SOURCED).
- **Le Marché St. Norbert Farmers' Market**: began July **1988** with eight vendors on a lawn in St. Norbert, at the Red and La Salle confluence south of the city; proposed by **Heritage St. Norbert** (formed mid-1970s to protect the area's French-Métis heritage) to keep public land from becoming a strip mall and "bring people into the community"; organized as a **non-profit co-operative** on a Saskatchewan model; member vendors elect the board; casual vendors may apply for membership after a year; vendors must make, bake or grow what they sell; 130 full-time and up to 50 casual vendors; no disposable bags provided (Canadian CED Network profile, c. 2010, SOURCED, read from the PDF). ### E. Cultural activities and civic life

- **Festival du Voyageur** (Round 1 NOT FOUND: volunteer counts and funding): Georges Forest proposed a winter festival in **1967**; Festival du Voyageur Inc. incorporated 18 December 1969; first festival 26 Feb–1 Mar 1970 at Provencher Park (est. 50,000), moved to Whittier Park in 1978 (Fort Gibraltar reconstruction); 10 days each February; school "voyageurs" have visited schools since 1977; 2025: 181 artists on 7 stages; about **10 permanent and 200 temporary staff**; volunteers gave **12,946 hours in 2025**; **expenses about $3.6 million (2025)**; attendance 96,000 (2019), 16,470 (2022), 76,000 (2023), 74,975 (2024), 67,380 (2025), 70,051 (2026) (Wikipedia, SOURCED as read). 922 volunteers in 2024 (snippet, UNVERIFIED); its volunteer guide says over 350 individual volunteers plus community groups supplying several hundred more (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K2, K3, K8]
- **Folklorama** (Round 1 NOT FOUND: volunteers and funding): **43 pavilions**, **361,703 visits**, 3–16 August 2025 (CTV snippet, UNVERIFIED); **communities themselves run their pavilions** and carry the financial risk (CTV "Folklorama pavilions require financial gamble, hard work, volunteers", headline/snippet, UNVERIFIED); volunteers get pavilion passes, and meals after a full shift (pavilion registration form snippet, UNVERIFIED). Volunteer headcount conflicts (8,000 to 20,000; 40,000+ shifts in 2026) (snippets, UNVERIFIED). The 2025 annual report returned 404. Funding: NOT FOUND. [K3, K4, K8]
- **Louis Riel Day**: Manitoba's February holiday, the **third Monday of February**, first observed 18 February 2008; 114 schools sent names; eleven schools submitted the winning name and each got a **$1,000 library grant** (gov.mb.ca "Louis Riel Day", SOURCED). Created to fill the long holiday gap between New Year's Day and Good Friday (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K2, K3]
- **Winnipeg Fringe Theater Festival** (July): founded **1988** under the Manitoba Theater Center, modeled on Edmonton's, non-juried, venues centered in the Exchange District; the festival calls itself the second-largest fringe in North America (Wikipedia/Canadian Encyclopedia/festival snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K3, K13]
- **Arts from cheap space (K13 origin):** the warehouse district held "a large percentage of the city's artists and small arts organizations" because underused heritage buildings were affordable in the 1960s–70s; the tri-level **$90 million Core Area Initiative (1980)** included arts accommodation after lobbying; **Artspace Inc.** formed **1984**; the 55,000 sq ft **Gault Building** opened 30 October 1986 with 14 founding tenants after a two-year renovation; the provincial Centennial Center Corporation leases it to Artspace for **99 years at $1 a year**; it now houses 18 arts organizations, studios, a gallery and a cinema; its success helped turn the area into "the Exchange District" (art-space.ca "History", SOURCED). [K8, K13]
- **Strike memory:** the 1919 centenary (2019) brought a conference (8–11 May, University of Winnipeg), a gala dinner on 15 May, archive exhibits, Exchange District walking tours and a musical at Rainbow Stage (snippets, UNVERIFIED). An annual commemoration or Labor Day picnic: NOT FOUND. [K9]

### K6 · Rules of everyday behavior (present day; Round 1 found none beyond parking bans)
- **Liquor store controlled entrances**: from late 2019/2020 Manitoba Liquor Marts with controlled entrances admit customers through a locked inner door only after staff scan original photo ID (no photos or photocopies; sunglasses and face coverings removed); photo, name and birthdate kept **21 days** on an encrypted server, used only for incident investigations; entry refused to people involved in an incident (violence, theft, fraud, robbery, property damage), to holders of IDs reported lost or stolen, and to anyone without accepted ID; children 11 and under may enter with a caregiver but may not handle products. Reason given: thefts and robberies had become a safety burden for staff, customers and police; result claimed: robberies "virtually eliminated", thefts from hundreds a week to a handful (mbll.ca "Controlled Entrances", SOURCED). Thefts had risen from 658 (2017) to over 2,600 (2018); the first such entrance followed a violent attack on workers at the Tyndall Park store; 43 installations under a $2.5 million budget (CBC/CTV/Global snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K6, K9]

## 2 · #53 Port Augusta, South Australia

*Round 1 covered the junction, the rail and power eras and their closures, the 2021 census, the prison, RFDS, Pika Wiya, Lakeview, Umeewarra Mission and Davenport, native title, the liquor rules, the botanic garden, Pichi Richi and the main events. Round 2 chases its NOT FOUNDs (origin of the "crossroads" title before 1993; workshop employment; food culture; off-season third places; Wadlata's opening year; current liquor-restriction status) and goes into schools, youth, clubs, halls, Aboriginal organizations and the seasonal rhythm.*

### A. Other settings

**The settlement's oldest shared buildings [K10, K13, P4]**
- **The Institute** (52 Commercial Road): attempts from 1863; land granted 1 December 1873; built with £837 raised locally plus a £750 government grant; a hall for 400, reading room, museum and library; opened January 1876; 4,000 volumes by 1909; the library closed in 1971 and the building became a **teenage club** (Flinders Ranges Heritage Survey vol. 2, City of Port Augusta, Austral Archaeology / Donovan & Associates, 1995, SOURCED, read from the downloaded PDF). In 1950, seventeen men met in the Institute Hall to revive the golf club (pagc.org.au, SOURCED).
- **Yarta Purtli / Port Augusta Cultural Center**: the name is given as a Nukunu word meaning "place of the stars"; the complex began as the troopers' and police barracks and the **waterworks reserve** (a public bath-house, the Green Room, from 1875; workshop, stables and blacksmith's shop 1882); used by the Engineering and Water Supply Department to 1972; part of the local court complex 1976–2006 (Gallery 2 was the Magistrates Court); State Heritage listed 1981; turned into a cultural center in **2008** under the state **Regional Center of Culture** program with money from all three levels of government; now an art gallery, the Barracks entertainment venue, the Institute Theater and meeting rooms (yartapurtli.sa.gov.au "History", SOURCED). Hosts the annual **Malka Aboriginal Art Prize** and regional Tarnanthi exhibitions (2025: "Woven within Stars", 20 Sep–14 Nov) (snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K13, K8]
- **Town Hall** (1887; gutted by fire 17 May 1944; reopened 30 August 1946 with a high tea and concert) (Heritage Survey 1995, SOURCED).
- **Wadlata** (Round 1 NOT FOUND: opening year): the Wadlata Outback Center occupies the former **St Joseph's Catholic convent and school** (school opened 1927); the survey says the building "is currently used as offices by the Wadlata Outback Center Administration which opened in 1988" (Heritage Survey 1995, SOURCED; the sentence leaves it unclear whether 1988 is the center's opening or the administration's). Port Augusta was the seat of a **Catholic diocese** from 10 May 1887 (All Saints' Cathedral) until the seat moved to Port Pirie in 1951 (Wikipedia / Catholic encyclopedia snippets, UNVERIFIED). [P6]
- **The wharf** (1880s): about 1,118 ft long with two rail lines; State control passed to the Commonwealth in 1914; enlarged early 1940s; most of the district's produce once crossed it (Heritage Survey 1995, SOURCED).

**The marine edge closed (K9, K10, a major Round 2 finding)**
- Port Augusta "once boasted at least nine working jetties" and by 2024 had **none**: the east-side jetty was dismantled in **2019**, the council-owned west-side jetty closed the same year, and most of the state-owned wharf has been **fenced off since 2019**; the Great Western Bridge was being dismantled. Mayor Linley Shine: "The culture of this community, and our young people, has been to utilise our marine infrastructure"; children had lost the chance to "swim, jump or fish off the town's jetties", pastimes of earlier generations. Council CEO John Banks tied the lack of marine access to the town's youth-crime problem: the wharf had been used by parents "for jumping off, or fishing, for cheap recreational purposes". Teenagers surveyed by SA's Commissioner for Children and Young People (November 2023) put reopening the wharf high on their priorities; one Year 9 student: "A good place to live has wharves and jetties in use." The council allocated $150,000 for west-side jetty concepts (rebuild about $4 million); the state funded a pontoon to replace the east-side jetty; timber assets "designed with a 50-year lifespan" were "154 years into their life". A tackle-shop owner of 30 years said a generation of children was missing out (*Sunday Mail*/*The Advertiser*, Paul Ashenden, "Let them jump, fish", 14 April 2024, SOURCED, read locally from the PDF reprint hosted by the Commissioner for Children and Young People). [K9, K10, P3, SM: man versus society]
- **East Side Foreshore** has pontoons for swimming and mooring; a beach-safety listing describes a lit jetty for night fishing and swimming (council/beachsafe snippets, UNVERIFIED; may predate the 2019 removal).

**Waterworks camps (historic) [K4, K7]**
- At the Davenport Reservoir works in the early 1890s, up to 140 men (later 72 men and 15 horse teams) lived in "an interesting little canvas town", with "the customary boarding-houses [three]" and children among the tents; completed May 1894 (Heritage Survey 1995 quoting a contemporary report, SOURCED).

**Health travel and the town as a place of treatment [K5, P5, P9]**
- Before the Purple House dialysis unit at **Pukatja (Ernabella)** opened in **2019**, APY Lands patients with kidney failure had to relocate to Adelaide, Alice Springs or Port Augusta; by 2019 seven patients formerly living in those places for treatment had gone home (Guardian/press snippets, UNVERIFIED). A 2014 count found nine APY dialysis patients based in Adelaide and two in Port Augusta; a 2014 ministerial statement said people preferred Port Augusta or Adelaide because they could bring family "and do other business while they are here" (Hansard snippets, UNVERIFIED; hansardsearch refused the fetch). SBS (8 Nov 2019, SOURCED): the four-bed Pukatja unit cost about $2.5 million (mostly federal; the local arts center raised $170,000); dialysis is three sessions a week of four to five hours; a patient in Adelaide: "I feel lonely"; some who refused to leave country for treatment died; the APY Lands have about 3,000 people in about a dozen communities. [K9]

### B. Subcultures

**Aboriginal community institutions [K1, K4, P9]**
- **Umeewarra Radio 89.1 FM** (Umeewarra Aboriginal Media Association): founded **1987**; named after nearby **Lake Umeewarra**, "a gathering place for Aboriginal peoples for thousands of years", where groups gathered in summer, with the gulf, to escape the heat; posters of country singers in the studios; a meeting place for country-music performers and fans; messages on culture and reconciliation (indigenous.gov.au "Umeewarra Radio: meeting place for country music legends", SOURCED). The idea came from a feasibility study by a co-founder of CAAMA in Alice Springs; 12 hours a day of local content, then CAAMA; reaches Davenport, Whyalla and Port Pirie; produces a national program for the National Indigenous Radio Service; runs Umee TV; supported 25+ NAIDOC events; an SROI study says it brings people together given "the fractious relationship between Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal residents" (NIAA SROI summary / umeewarramedia snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K13, K10]
- **Davenport Community Council** (Aboriginal community council; 18 Simmons Street per a directory); Davenport became a suburb on 8 July 2014 at the Council's request; the old Davenport municipality was annexed by Port Augusta in February 1933 (Wikipedia snippets, UNVERIFIED). Other corporations on the ORIC register: **Bungala Aboriginal Corporation** (registered 20 June 1994, Young Street), Barngarla Determination Aboriginal Corporation RNTBC (2017, Whyalla Norrie) (ORIC snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Port Augusta and Davenport Community Alliance**: Traditional Owner groups, Aboriginal community-controlled organizations, young people, service providers, business and police; tied to a **$12 million Community Safety and Wellbeing Plan** funded by state and Commonwealth (DHS snippets, UNVERIFIED; dhs.sa.gov.au refused in Round 1, not fetched; NIT article refused, 403).
- **Substance-misuse services**: a 24-hour **Sobering Up Unit** (5 Elizabeth Terrace; showers, clean bed, washed clothes, hot meal) and a **Mobile Assistance Patrol** van that takes intoxicated people there; a $1.2 million state outreach program (SAPOL, SA Health, Pika Wiya, SA Housing Authority) with street and roving teams replaced the axed City Safe program in 2020 and helps people stranded in town get home (council/inkl snippets, UNVERIFIED). The Aboriginal Drug & Alcohol Council SA lists a Port Augusta branch (12 Young St) of its sobering-up and day-center services for Aboriginal people of Port Augusta, Ceduna and outlying areas; free; no alcohol allowed in the van (sacommunity.org listing, SOURCED). [K1, K6]
- **Languages in school:** Port Augusta Secondary School and Carlton (B-9) School appear on the state list of schools teaching **Adnyamathanha**; no school Barngarla program found; Barngarla revival is run through community workshops in Port Augusta, Whyalla and Port Lincoln and a dictionary app (education.sa.gov.au / Port Lincoln Times snippets, UNVERIFIED; education.sa.gov.au refused, 403). [K11]

**Cameleers (historic) [K5, K9, P9]**
- **31 Afghan cameleers** arrived on the *Blackwell* at Port Augusta, and on New Year's Eve 1865 a crowd watched **124 camels** lifted ashore for Elder & Co.'s Beltana station; camel trains carried wool and stores between Spencer Gulf and the mines and stations; Afghan-owned carrying businesses from the late 1880s; decline with motor transport in the 1920s, settlements deserted by the 1940s (SA History Hub "Afghans", SOURCED). In 1884 nearly 300 camels and 56 cameleers landed at Port Augusta (snippet, UNVERIFIED). An 1893 election speech at Port Augusta opposed importing more cameleers (snippet, UNVERIFIED). Commerce: by 1909 Young & Gordon employed about 70 people and sent goods to Queensland by camel team (Heritage Survey 1995, SOURCED). The **Standpipe Hotel** at Port Augusta West (licensed 16 September 1882, open about March 1883) stood at the end of the water pipeline under the gulf, where teamsters watered bullocks and camels; it had a billiard room, skittles, stockyards and bullock pens; the license lapsed in 1901 "when declining teamsters forced the hotel to close"; plaques there (1986, 1988) honor the cameleers and a re-enactment trek (Heritage Survey 1995, SOURCED). [K10]

**Youth [K1, K9]**

**Tennant Creek, the services town 25 km south [P4, P9]** ("The Crowded Desert", Debra Jopson and Ella Rubeli, *The Global Mail*, c. 2013, SOURCED as archived)
- About 3,000 official residents with a small Aboriginal majority in 2011 (many Aboriginal people avoid the census); many young people; visitors from remote communities come for **dialysis, school, work, family events or funerals**, and many stay on.
- **Eight town camps** on the edge of town house about half the Aboriginal population in tin, wood and concrete houses, some holding 20 or more relatives sleeping on floors, porches and in yards; poor plumbing in older homes.
- Some families keep Warumungu beliefs; **initiation is still held at the edge of town**; children move between town and bush life; weekend hunting for goanna, kangaroo and bush turkey and gathering witchetty grubs.
- A new **$3.7 million police station** (January 2013), about 40 officers who patrol football crowds, enforce drink limits and breath-test drivers; on one grand-final weekend: **14 arrests, 186 people taken into protective custody, 188 liters of alcohol seized or poured out**; a takeaway queue outside the Tennant Creek Hotel on grand-final day.
- Anyinginyi Health runs local health services including dialysis; kidney disease, scabies and childhood anaemia are common and linked to overcrowding. A girls' program raises money by café, disco and car-wash fundraisers.
- Housing governance: **Julalikari Council Aboriginal Corporation** is the Aboriginal housing organization for the camps; long (99-year) sub-leases of the camps to government were agreed as part of upgrade funding; a 16-bed youth accommodation service (NT/DSS snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K1, K4, K9]
- **Barkly Work Camp**: a low-security correctional work camp on Peko Road, about 2 km from the town center; opened 8 September 2011 with 50 places; prisoners do community work for local organizations and events (13,370 hours between 1 July 2025 and 31 March 2026, valued at about $333,582); a prisoners' football team has reached a local grand final and the camp has supplied players to remote clubs; parts were unfenced and there have been walk-outs (Wikipedia / NT Corrections / Alice Springs News snippets, UNVERIFIED; nt.gov.au pages not fetched). [K7, K10, K14]
- **Mining revival**: the **Nobles** gold project (Tennant Mines, acquired by Pan African Resources for US$54.2 million) poured its first gold on **13 May 2025**; 15,560 oz in the first seven months; "over 160 jobs" with one in six held by Aboriginal Territorians "almost all from the Barkly"; 126 Barkly suppliers and 33 Indigenous-owned businesses listed in a government case study (mining press / NT government snippets, UNVERIFIED). ### A. Other settings

**The first company cottages [K4, K6, P3, P7]** (Crewe Heritage Center "Virtual Museum: Railway Cottages", compiled with the Railway Cottages (Crewe) Residents Association, SOURCED)
- Built from **1842** by the Grand Junction Railway; first group around Moss Square (where Christ Church later stood), designed by Liverpool architect John Cunningham; Betley, Tollitt and Dorfold Streets built to the same designs; two house types.
- Inside: American spruce floors, pitch-pine stairs, a corner cupboard in the living room, a kitchen fireplace with an iron bar, shelves under the stairs; **gas lighting from the company's own gasworks** in the main room ("relatively rare in working-class housing"); some houses had piped water, others used a pump; a reservoir near Mill Street (1842) pumped and filtered water for works and residents.
- Early residents came from Bristol, Manchester, Liverpool, Staffordshire, Lancashire, Wales, Scotland and Ireland.
- **Company rules for tenants:** board minutes said tenants who were "filthy and disreputable" should be watched closely, and employees who brought discredit on the establishment could be dismissed. [K6]
- 70 houses in this group; some demolished by 1876 for LNWR general offices; **57 threatened with demolition in the early 1970s, saved by a local campaign**, renovated and listed in 1976. [K9]

### B. Subcultures

**The co-operative town [K1, K8, K12]**
- **Crewe Co-operative Friendly Society**, 1845, brought by Crewe Works employees; its first food store was in an LNWR-owned cottage on Earle Street, which the source calls the Co-op's second UK store after Rochdale (the article's claim); first secretary James Ainsco (retired 1852); moved to Market Street in **1866**, where it made and sold its own bread, meat, tailoring, drapery and furniture; Co-operative Street was demolished in the 1980s for the Market Shopping Center; the article says Crewe became "the most Co-operative town in the country" relative to population (claim); absorbed into the North Midlands Co-operative Society in **1969**; the Co-op's **99 tea factory** opened off Weston Road in **1976** (Crewe Nub News, 2023, SOURCED). Records 1845–1946 are at Cheshire Archives (D 4807) (National Archives catalog snippet, UNVERIFIED); a 150th-anniversary plate (1845–1995) (WCML catalog snippet, UNVERIFIED).

**The Polish parish [K4, P9]** (St Mary's Crewe, "My story: the Polish community", SOURCED)
- In **1947** Polish soldiers and families, mostly of the 5th Kresy Infantry Division of General Anders's II Corps, some war orphans, were resettled in a former American base at **Doddington**, near Crewe. The parish of **Our Lady of the Gate of Dawn** began there with an army chaplain; its altar image was made from soldiers' eagles and mess tins. The camp had a nursery, school, kitchen, canteen and infirmary that employed Polish women; courses in English, cooking and tailoring helped people find work; Corpus Christi processions, dances, a choir and a scout troop.
- As the camp shrank by 1960, many emigrated to Canada, the US and Australia; others moved to Crewe for work. A Polish Mass at St Mary's from 1958 continues; a Saturday school taught religion.
- Fundraising bought **71 West Street** (chapel, hall, Polish Club with bingo); the club went bankrupt in **2006**, leaving the parish its debts.
- The parish was near closure until the **2004** migration from Poland revived it; a second weekly Mass was added; renovations from 2011 and 2022.
- A 3,500-strong Polish community and three Polish shops reported in the late 2000s; fortnightly Saturday police sessions at Polish shops; a Polish pub-restaurant opened in an existing pub; **20+ Slovak families** at Ruskin School in a 2016/17 cohesion plan (Daily Post / Crewe Chronicle / council snippets, UNVERIFIED).
