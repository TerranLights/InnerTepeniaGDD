## Pick 1 · NOIRLab [PRIMARY] — "(under authority of the NSF)"

**Name check:** matches a real institution exactly. Full name: **NSF NOIRLab** (U.S. National Science Foundation National Optical-Infrared Astronomy Research Laboratory). It is not one place; it is an organization with a headquarters and three regional bases. This pick is researched as the organization, its headquarters campus in Tucson, its Chilean base (the AURA Recinto in La Serena) and the mountain sites and towns those staff use. Its Hawai'i base (Hilo) and its Arizona mountain (Kitt Peak) are covered under Pick 2, which names them directly, to avoid writing them twice.

### A. Snapshot

- **What it is:** "As of 1 October 2019, all National Science Foundation-funded ground-based nighttime optical and infrared astronomical research facilities operate together in one organization called NSF's NOIRLab." It runs five Programs: Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory (CTIO), the Community Science and Data Center (CSDC), Gemini Observatory, Kitt Peak National Observatory (KPNO), and Vera C. Rubin Observatory operations. **SOURCED** — noirlab.edu/public/about/faqs/ (read 2026-10-26).
- **Who runs it:** managed by the **Association of Universities for Research in Astronomy (AURA)** under a cooperative agreement with NSF. Director: Patrick McCarthy. **SOURCED** — same FAQ page.
- **Where:** headquarters at **950 N. Cherry Ave., Tucson, Arizona 85719**, "in the same building where offices for Kitt Peak Operations, Vera C. Rubin Observatory Operations in the US and the Community Science and Data Center are located. The building formerly housed NOAO." Telescopes on Iolkam Du'ag (Kitt Peak, Arizona), Maunakea (Hawai'i), Cerro Tololo and Cerro Pachón (Chile). Chilean offices for Gemini South, Rubin and CTIO are in La Serena; Gemini North offices in Hilo. **SOURCED** — same FAQ page.
- **HQ setting:** the HQ "is on Cherry Avenue, just across the road from the University of Arizona and the Steward Observatory building." HQ visitors park in University of Arizona garages (Cherry Avenue Garage, Second Street Garage). **SOURCED** — noirlab.edu/public/about/broader-impacts/arizona/ and noirlab.edu/public/visits/noirlab-hq/ (read 2026-10-26).
- **Size, staff:** the Arizona page says NOIRLab "employs approximately 250 staff based in Arizona out of a total of 450." A 2023 NOIRLab blog says it "hosts 70 of the most diverse and innovative ground-based telescopes in the world across three observing sites ... and employs around 500 full-time staff members." Chile page: "NOIRLab and AURA in Chile currently employ approximately 230 staff in the local area." Hawai'i page: "about 25% of NOIRLab's staff work in Hawai'i." **SOURCED** (all four, noirlab.edu, read 2026-10-26). *These figures come from undated pages and a 2023 post and do not add up cleanly; recorded as each page states them, not reconciled.*
- **Budget:** AURA's FY2025 annual report gives total AURA revenue of **$463 million** (NSF $182M, NASA $275M, DOE+other $7M) and expenses by center: STScI $274M, **NOIRLab $118M**, NSO $40M, Rubin construction $32M. AURA employs "approximately 1700" people across its centers. **SOURCED** — AURA Annual Report FY2025, aura-astronomy.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/AURA-Annual-Report-2025-05272026.pdf (downloaded and read 2026-10-26).
- **Defining feature:** a national, federally funded but university-consortium-operated lab whose work is split between city offices and remote high mountains in three countries/territories, with telescopes increasingly run remotely from the city bases.

### B. What else exists there

**The organization's history (sets up the bases):** from NOIRLab's timeline — March 1956, the Tohono O'odham Nation agrees to a test site on Kitt Peak; October 1957, AURA founded; February 1958, "The Tucson Base building land is acquired"; March 1960, the Kitt Peak (later NOIRLab) HQ in Tucson completed; November 1962, Cerro Tololo selected; January 1963, AURA buys "El Totoral," "close to 30,000 hectares with Cerro Tololo near its center" (the name refers to totora reed used for roofing); October 1963, a plot in La Serena bought "for construction of the CTIO offices, laboratories and staff housing," extended in 1966 and 1968; October 1977, the Chilean government declares the CTIO lands a "Privileged Scientific Sanctuary," where mining is prohibited without the President's consent; February 1984, NOAO formed by combining CTIO and KPNO; 1987, Maunakea accepted as the Gemini North site; 1989, Cerro Pachón chosen for Gemini South. **SOURCED** — noirlab.edu/public/about/timeline/ (read 2026-10-26).

**Work patterns (all sites):** from a 2023 NOIRLab staff-profile blog — the CTIO site manager runs a daily morning communication meeting and oversees 20 staff (maintenance, facility crews, engineers, electricians) "to have everything ready to do science at night"; a CTIO mechanical engineer works 8–5, mostly "at her office in the coastal town of La Serena," about 80 km west and 2,000 m lower than CTIO, going up the mountain for installations, with a hard stop: "We need to plan everything ... to finish by 4 p.m. because the telescope needs to be prepared for that night. Astronomers now rarely operate or even attend: they submit proposals and a team of operators and observers collects data. ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

- **City base + remote mountain** is the organizing shape everywhere: La Serena holds offices, labs, the Instrument Shop, a data center, staff housing and a motel inside one walled compound beside the university; the mountain holds dorms, a 24-hour cafeteria and night crews. Remote observing has moved more work off the mountain and into the city (Gemini South Remote Observing room in La Serena; Hilo remote ops). **SOURCED** (sources above).
- **Dark sky as a protected resource** produced laws and land rules: Tucson/Pima County outdoor lighting ordinances since **1972**, a joint code in 1994, revised 2006 ("to preserve the relationship of the residents ... to their unique desert environment through protection of access to the dark night sky"); the codes cap lumens per acre, force billboard lights downward and set curfews for lit signs; measured sky brightness over Kitt Peak was stable 1999–2010 despite Tucson's growth. **SOURCED** — archive.ilsr.org/?p=2466 (ILSR, undated, quoting the 2006 code) and Scientific American blog, 2010. In Chile: the 1977 "Privileged Scientific Sanctuary" decree (no mining without presidential consent), the title restriction of AURA's land to astronomy support, and the 2015 sanctuary designation. **SOURCED.**
- **Astrotourism** as a regional industry grew alongside the research sites; NOIRLab itself says it "foster[s] the development of astrotourism in the Coquimbo Region" and calls astrotourism "a key ally in the protection of dark skies." **SOURCED** — NOIRLab Chile page.
- **Outreach programs named for the sites:** Viaje al Universo and AstroDay (Chile); the **Padre Picetti Award** for Chilean science teachers, named after Father Juan Baptista Picetti of the Seminario Conciliar school in La Serena; the annual 10-day **La Serena School for Data Science**; Globe at Night (citizen light-pollution science, originated at KPNO); partnership with the AISES chapter at Tohono O'odham Community College (water-rocket launches with glow sticks). **SOURCED** — NOIRLab Arizona and Chile pages.

### Kitt Peak — findings so far

**A. Snapshot**
- KPNO is on Kitt Peak in the Quinlan Mountains, Sonoran Desert, **on the Tohono O'odham Nation**, 88 km (55 mi) west-southwest of Tucson; altitude **2,096 m (6,877 ft)**; ~260 observing nights a year; founded 1958; "more than twenty optical and two radio telescopes," one of the largest gatherings of astronomical instruments in the northern hemisphere. Largest: Mayall 4-m (now carrying DESI) and WIYN 3.5-m; the McMath–Pierce solar telescope was for decades the world's largest solar telescope; also the ARO 12-m radio telescope and one dish of the Very Long Baseline Array. Climate: subtropical highland (Cfb), much cooler and wetter than the surrounding desert. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitt_Peak_National_Observatory (read 2026-10-26).
- NOIRLab: "over 20 facilities" operate under "a lease with the Tohono O'odham Nation," less than an hour's drive from Tucson; Kitt Peak chosen in 1958 as the national observatory site. **SOURCED** — noirlab.edu/public/about/broader-impacts/arizona/ (see Pick 1).
- Name: NOIRLab's timeline says Kitt Peak "is a sacred mountain to the Tohono O'odham Nation who call it I'oligam Du'ag which means 'Manzanita Bush Mountain'" (the FAQ spells it "Iolkam Du'ag"). #### Maunakea Observatories

**Name check:** the picks file says "Mauna Kea observatories." The real collective name is **Maunakea Observatories (MKO)**. The University of Hawai'i at Hilo's Hawaiian-language college recommends the one-word spelling "Maunakea" as the proper noun; "Mauna Kea" is kept only in legal names such as the "Mauna Kea Science Reserve." **SOURCED** — hilo.hawaii.edu/maunakea/culture/ (read 2026-10-26).

**A. Snapshot**
- A group of independent observatories at the summit of Maunakea, Hawai'i Island, ~4,205 m (13,796 ft). They sit in a 525-acre "Astronomy Precinct" (set up 1967) inside the 11,228-acre Mauna Kea Science Reserve. Telescopes listed: CFHT 3.58 m, Gemini North 8.1 m, IRTF 3.0 m, JCMT 15 m, Subaru 8.2 m, SMA (8 × 6 m), UKIRT 3.8 m, a VLBA 25 m dish, Keck (2 × 10 m), UH88 2.2 m, UH Hilo Hōkū Kea 0.9 m; the CSO closed in 2015. Site strengths: dark skies, steady air, low humidity, height above most water vapor, low cloud, low latitude. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maunakea_Observatories (read 2026-10-26).
- **Defining feature:** among the best ground astronomy sites on Earth, on a mountain that is the most sacred place in Native Hawaiian tradition. Its management and future are a long-running public conflict.

**B. What else exists there**
- **Origins:** Gerard Kuiper tested sites and persuaded Governor John A. Burns to bulldoze a dirt road; he set a small telescope on Pu'u Poli'ahu, avoiding the highest peak as "holy ground." UH physicist John Jefferies won the NASA contract for a 2.24 m telescope (built 1967, first light 1970); the state agreed to build an all-weather road. **CFHT** agreed in 1973. Management plans followed in 1977, 1980, 1982 and 2000 (the 2000 plan created an Office of Mauna Kea Management, set 525 acres for astronomy and moved 10,763 acres to "natural and cultural preservation"); in 1998, 2,033 acres went to the Mauna Kea Ice Age Reserve. More than $2 billion invested. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia MKO. *Wikipedia's Hilo article (uncited on this point) says astronomy on Maunakea "was developed at the invitation of the Hawaiʻi Chamber of Commerce following the collapse of the sugarcane industry."*
- **Lease:** the Reserve is leased from the state Department of Land and Natural Resources (General Lease S-4191, June 21, 1968) and managed by the University of Hawai'i. The lease expires in **2033**. **SOURCED** — Wikipedia MKO; the lease number and date are **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of Act 255; the capitol PDF returned a bot-block page).
- **New governing authority:** **Act 255 (2022, HB 2024)** created the **Mauna Kea Stewardship and Oversight Authority**. It declares astronomy a state policy, gives an 11-member voting body "majority authority" over land management, and requires the "timely decommissioning" of some telescopes. Its voting seats must include a "lineal descendant" of a practitioner of Native Hawaiian traditions tied to the mountain and a recognized practitioner. UH has until **2028** to hand over management (joint management from July 1, 2023). The act puts a moratorium on new leases or lease extensions. **SOURCED** — popsci.com, "A new law is putting astronomy back in the hands of Native Hawaiians" (2022, read 2026-10-26).

### B. What else exists there
- **The site:** Wilson Laboratory. In the 1960s Cornell built "the most powerful accelerator ever located on a university campus," a 75,000 sq ft NSF-commissioned facility. To save money, physicist **Robert Rathbun Wilson** and architect **Ian Mackinlay** put the synchrotron **under Cornell's main athletic field** (Upper Alumni Field). A used sewer-tunneling machine with a 10-foot bore dug an 800 m-diameter ring 60 ft down. The complex is 20% above ground and 80% below. It has a six-story equipment tower (the linear-accelerator injector plus power and cooling), a 10,000 sq ft experiment hall, and a three-story lab and office wing. It was dedicated Oct. 10, 1968 as "The Robert Rathbun Wilson Synchrotron Laboratory." - **Layers of history:** **CESR** (Cornell Electron Storage Ring, from 1977–79) was a collider for particle physics. **CHESS** was built 1978–80 for $1.3M as a small facility running "parasitic" on CESR, with three beamlines (A, B, C) and six stations; first director Boris Batterman. **CHESS East** (1988–89) added lines D and F and an NIH-funded biohazard level 2 lab. **G-line** (from 1999) has a station with toxic-gas handling. **SOURCED** — CHESS history page. Cornell's nuclear physics dates to 1934, when M. Stanley Livingston came from Stanford to build the second cyclotron. The CLEO particle detector ran from 1979 to its last run on **March 3, 2008**; it drew 42 institutions and 400+ physicists and produced 530 papers. Its 26-ton magnet was removed **Sept. 6** (year of the article, ~2008–09) and shipped to Jefferson Lab. That cleared the way for a **$15 million** upgrade to a single positron beam optimized for X-rays. **SOURCED** — Cornell Chronicle, "With CLEO detector gone, CHESS facility looks back, ahead" (news.cornell.edu/node/286845, read 2026-10-26; exact year not printed in the text captured). The 2018 **CHESS-U** upgrade replaced one-sixth of the ring with six new double-bend achromats and raised energy from 5.3 to 6.0 GeV. **UNVERIFIED** — snippet of arXiv 1810.06557. Kathy Hochul), leaving CHESS "a true third-generation (state-of-the-art) light source." **SOURCED** — Cornell Chronicle 2019.

- **Work rhythm:** "When CHESS is producing X-rays (about 6 months per year), we operate 24 hours a day. Technical staff "design, build, troubleshoot, and maintain the majority of our experimental equipment."
