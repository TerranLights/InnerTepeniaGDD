## 1. Skagway and Haines, Alaska

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Round 1 already covers museums, the Days of '98 show, the Fourth of July, the Buckwheat Ski Classic, the housing crunch, the grocery barge, Haines' arts center, the Bald Eagle Festival and the fair's founding. Nothing below repeats those.

### A. Settings (spaces of daily life)

**Skagway**
- **Population swing.** Year-round population about 900. It triples in April and May when about 2,000 seasonal workers arrive. SOURCED: Munson, KHNS, 2024-05-03, https://khns.org/boot-camp-for-newcomers-schools-seasonal-workers . In 2020 the Skagway News gave "about 1,000 year-round residents" and 12,000-15,000 people a day in town during the cruise season. In 2019 it counted 983,917 cruise passengers from 446 ships plus nearly 398,000 crew and other visitors. SOURCED: Wehmhoff and Munson, Skagway News, 2020-04-25, https://skagwaynews.com/?p=39439 . The 2019 passenger figure differs from round 1's (1,035,800, Alaska Economic Trends). The two sources count differently; this is not resolved.
- **Broadway, in and out of season.** "Once the cruise ships leave, a majority of the businesses on Broadway will close for the winter." SOURCED: KHNS 2024-05-03. The 2023 season set a municipal record of about 1.2 million cruise passengers. Broadway was congested enough that officials considered limiting buses for pedestrian safety. Tours sold out, so more visitors went onto the trails. One shop owner: "Although the town was certainly busy, my average sales were lower than 2019." SOURCED: d'Epremesnil, KHNS, 2023-10-23, https://khns.org/?p=63710
- **Winter food.** In winter 2022-23 there was one full-service restaurant, The Station Bar and Grill (owner Beth Smith: "a very quiet winter"). The other food came from the two private clubs, the Elks Lodge and the Eagles Aerie. SOURCED: Swasey, KHNS, 2022-12-23, https://khns.org/?p=60768 . (Round 1 had this only as UNVERIFIED; it is now confirmed.)
- **Skagway Community Recreation Center**, 455 13th Ave. It is a former school gymnasium converted by the municipality. It has exercise bikes and treadmills, a climbing wall, an outdoor skate park, weights, public showers, and classes (yoga, spinning, Pilates, Zumba). It takes bookings for the public-use cabin at Upper Dewey Lake and gives out trail information. SOURCED: https://alaska.org/detail/skagway-community-rec-center (undated listing). The Skagway visitor department lists yoga at the Rec Center among winter activities. SOURCED: https://skagway.com/plan-your-trip/winter-in-skagway (2026).
- **Dahl Memorial Clinic** is a Federally Qualified Health Center. It offers family medicine, x-ray, lab, a limited pharmacy, and 24-hour emergency care including medevacs. Visiting specialists (dentists, physical therapists, public-health nurses) come in rotation. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of jedc.org provider listing; not opened).
- **Winter season.** Snow starts in October and winter activities run through April. From October to April, business hours are posted monthly on the town's Facebook page, updated on the first Friday. Northern lights can be seen in town and at the White Pass summit. SOURCED: skagway.com winter page.

**Haines**
- **The P Bar (Pioneer Bar), downtown.** "One of the most filmed bars in the ski industry." During ski season "a potpourri of locals and a veritable 'who's who' of big mountain athletes convene at the P Bar at night." Hand-drawn art on the ceiling records visiting mountaineers. SOURCED: Herran, Alps & Meters journal, 2016-04-07, https://journal.alpsandmeters.com/journal/36hoursinhaines
- **Bamboo Room** (attached to the Pioneer Bar). It serves breakfasts and burgers. Its daily special is "a favorite for the local seniors, who gather each day to talk small-town life and politics." UNVERIFIED (search snippet, alaska.org listing).
- **Mountain Market** (3rd and Haines Highway). It is a coffee roaster and organic grocery, "a nearly daily stop" for residents. **Sarah J's Cafe** (2nd Ave), espresso. **Haines Brewing Company** (Main St), with a tasting room where locals gather. **Port Chilkoot Distillery**, in a converted 1904 military bakery in Fort Seward. **Fireweed Restaurant**. SOURCED: Alps & Meters 2016. **Fogcutter Bar**: "bustling local hangout." UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Haines Borough Public Library**, 111 3rd Ave. It was started in 1931 by the Haines Women's Club as the Haines Library Association, moved in 1959, and was extended in 1979 and 1987. Its new 7,300 sq ft building (MRV Architects) opened in early 2003, after about eight months of construction using volunteer labor. Library Journal named it "Best Small Library in America" in 2005, citing over 60,000 visits a year, 28,000 computer sessions and 75+ volunteers. Its Dragonfly Project has teenagers teaching older residents to use technology. Collection 33,657 items; circulation 55,888; population served 2,520. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haines_Borough_Public_Library
- **Letnikoff cannery**, used as a gathering site for fishing families (see E). SOURCED: KTOO 2018 below.
- **Haines School Pool** ("where almost all Haines swimming takes place"). **High School Gymnasium**: "almost every evening of the week" various age groups and leagues play basketball in winter. UNVERIFIED (snippet of a hainesalaska.gov document; page 403).
- **Klukwan** is 18-20 mi up the Chilkat River. Its Tlingit name, "Tlakw Aan," is given as "Eternal Village" / "The Village That Has Always Been." It has the Klukwan School, a community library and the Klukwan Mercantile store. SOURCED: Chilkat Indian Village site, https://chilkat-nsn.gov/?p=465 (undated).

### B. Subcultures

- **Seasonal cruise workers (Skagway).** About 2,000 come each spring. Since about 2022 the Skagway Development Corporation (program manager Jacob Watson) has run a week of orientation events for them, informally the "boot camp for newbies." The April 22-28, 2024 program included free STI testing at Dahl Memorial Clinic, a National Park Service bear-safety talk, history talks, a brewery tour with an indoor aeroponic farm, and a business mixer. Recurring recreation: cornhole every Wednesday, co-ed softball on Mondays, Duff's Marathon and the Klondike Road Relay. SOURCED: KHNS 2024-05-03. Live music, karaoke and trivia nights at several bars: UNVERIFIED (snippet, reddit/coolworks).
- **Year-round business owners** stay through the dead season. In 2020 Mike Healy of Skagway Brewing Company laid off 75% of his staff, his "toughest day as a business owner." Greg and Billi Clem of Klondike Tours kept their business open. SOURCED: Skagway News 2020-04-25.
- **Catholic parish, St. Therese of the Child Jesus** ("The Green Church"), 9th Ave and State St. It has about ten households. The present church was built in 1979 with the Christian Brothers and the Catholic Extension Society. One priest, Fr. Perry Kenaston, served both Skagway and Haines and commuted weekly by ferry or plane. Winter attendance drops; summer visitors support the parish financially; visiting priests serve "the thousands of summer visitors." History: Mass in homes from fall 1897; a store was converted into St. Mark church, full on Christmas Eve 1898. SOURCED: Scime, North Star Catholic, June 2021, https://northstarcatholic.org/parish-profile-st-therese-skagway/ . Other current congregations: NOT FOUND.
- **LGBTQ community and allies.** A grassroots Pride Planning Committee has run Skagway Pride every year since 2019. In 2024: a drag show at Seven Pastures (June 1), a "Rainbow Roll" at the Recreation Center (June 14), Drag Story Time at the library (June 15), "The Gays of '98 Show" at the Eagles theater (June 28), and rainbow cupcakes all month at Lucy's Bakery. The municipality gave $5,000 in grants and the mayor signed a proclamation. SOURCED: Munson, KHNS, 2024-06-07, https://khns.org/?p=66201
- **Skagway Tlingit.** Skagway Village (Skagway Traditional Council) is a federally recognized tribe with an estimated 57 citizens in 2025. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia snippet). NPS history says the area was traditionally Chilkoot Tlingit territory, with Dyea a seasonal fishing camp and staging place for trade. "Dayéi" is said to mean "to pack." UNVERIFIED (snippets of NPS/Wikipedia; not opened).
- **Heli-ski guides and athletes (Haines).** The season "officially" begins at the end of February. Three companies charge $6,000-8,000 per client for a week. Alaska Heli Skiing normally employs about 15 (only 7 in the poor 2018 season). Southeast Alaska Backcountry Adventures (SEABA) had "a hundred landings a year." Local borough permits limit skier-days and federal BLM permits limit landings. Habitat concerns for mountain goats and bear dens have blocked expansion of the terrain. Insurers offer refunds for weather days. SOURCED: Wilber, KHNS, 2018-03-12, https://khns.org/?p=24214
- **Commercial gillnetters (Lynn Canal).** For over a decade they have held a salmon barbecue at the start of the season. In 2018 king salmon were replaced by sockeye and rockfish because kings were scarce. The Haines King Salmon Derby, run for 40 years, had not been held for three years. A Friday gathering at the Letnikoff cannery was added for fishermen and families, since crews leave for the opening on Saturday. Ocean Beauty donated 1,000 lb of sockeye and Haines Packing 500 lb of rockfish. SOURCED: Leasia, KHNS via KTOO, 2018-06-12, https://www.ktoo.org/2018/06/12/gillnetters-barbecue-replaces-kings-with-sockeye-adds-event-for-fishermen-families/
- **Chilkat Tlingit (Klukwan).** The village was founded by Gaanaxteidi (Raven) men and their Kaagwaantaan (Eagle) wives. It has a matrilineal clan system with Eagle and Raven moieties. Songs, stories, dances and the Tlingit language are taught to the young. SOURCED: chilkat-nsn.gov. Seasonal Klukwan Traditional Knowledge Camps teach salmon harvesting, moose preparation and hooligan (eulachon) processing, with Tlingit language worked into each activity. They are hosted by the Jilkaat Kwaan Heritage Center in partnership with the school library. UNVERIFIED (IMLS grant snippets).
- **Community theater people (Haines).** The Lynn Canal Community Players were founded in 1956 and called "the oldest continuously operating community theater group in Alaska" (UNVERIFIED, Juneau Empire snippet). They have put on at least two shows a year for nearly 60 years, and anyone who works on a show as cast or crew becomes a member. Their 2015 "Dancing at Lughnasa" was directed by Tod Sebens, a Glacier Bay tour-boat captain, and featured an auto-shop worker acting for the first time after 50. The Chilkat Center building began as an 1800s cannery that the Army moved to Fort Seward in 1924. Founder Mimi Gregg pushed two upgrades of the building. SOURCED: Lende, ADN, 2015-03-22, https://www.adn.com/we-alaskans/article/onstage-haines-rekindling-spirits-our-tribe/2015/03/22 . The Players also produce the "River Talk" storytelling series. UNVERIFIED (Juneau Empire snippet).

### C. Leisure industries

- **Skagway cruise retail and tours:** busy is not the same as lucrative. In 2023 the record passenger count came with lower average sales per shop than 2019. Tourism director Jaime Bricker heard "mixed sentiments" and saw a shift toward experiences over goods. SOURCED: KHNS 2023-10-23.
- **2020 collapse:** ships cut by about 60% and Holland America and Princess cancelled land excursions and closed wilderness lodges. Some businesses did not open; others expected 75% revenue losses. SOURCED: Skagway News 2020-04-25.
- **Locally run events with public money:** the North Words Writers Symposium (May 29-June 1, 2024, keynote Jamie Ford) received $25,000 in municipal grants. SOURCED: KHNS 2024-06-07. The visitor department's calendar lists a Spring Festival (Skagway Arts Council: winter art showcase, town cleanup, spring market, folk festival), the Fran DeLisle Cancer Awareness Walkathon, Duff's Marathon, the Elks Summer Solstice Party, Blues, Brews & BBQ (Arts Council), the Klondike Road Relay to Whitehorse (Sport Yukon, September), Fall Festival (October), and Yuletide (tree lighting, bazaars, quilt show, dances, music). SOURCED: https://www.skagway.com/events/annual-events/ (2026). Winter: the White Pass & Yukon Route "Santa Train" produced an exceptionally busy day (2022). SOURCED: KHNS 2022-12-23.
- **Haines heli-skiing** (spring, high-end international clients; see B). Its outdoor economy also includes ice climbing, fat biking, cross-country skiing, ice fishing, and salmon "through November." SOURCED: https://visithaines.com/things-to-do/in-the-winter (undated).
- **Southeast Alaska State Fair, Haines.** The 47th fair (July 30-Aug 2, 2015) charged $40 adult admission and had nine headliners and 35+ acts on three stages. Other features: a "Southeast's Got Talent" show ($400 prizes), a Logging Show, a Fisherman's Rodeo, horseshoe and disc-golf tournaments, fiddle and dog shows, a Wearable Art Review, and home-arts entries in Harriett Hall (pickled beach asparagus, bull-kelp pickles). There were entries from Whitehorse and Wrangell, and a shuttle from the ferry dock for out-of-towners. SOURCED: Alaska Public Media, 2015-07-29, https://alaskapublic.org/2015/07/29/southeast-alaska-state-fair-readies-for-lift-off . The fair's year also includes a Spring Fling (April) and the gillnetters' free salmon barbecue (June). UNVERIFIED (snippet).

## 2. Seyðisfjörður, Iceland

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Translations from Icelandic are mine. Round 1 covers the pass, the tunnel, the ferry, the landslide, Skaftfell, LungA, List í Ljósi, the Blue Church concerts, Rainbow Street and the salmon-farm dispute. Nothing below repeats those.

### A. Settings

- **The school campus around Sólveigartorg** (Suðurgata 4). Seyðisfjarðarskóli has been a single combined preschool, primary and music school since 2016. Regular schooling in the town dates from 1881-82. The "Old School" (Gamli skóli) was a prefabricated building cut in Norway and put up in three months in 1907. All ordinary classrooms are still in it. The "New School," the red building on the east side of Sólveigartorg, opened in 1986. It holds the arts and crafts rooms (an "art department") and **the combined town and school library**. A newer **sports hall and swimming pool** also stand on the square. Autumn 2020: **63 pupils** in grades 1-10 (between 3 and 10 per grade), taught in combined-grade groups. **21 pupils of foreign origin**; 6 learning Icelandic as a second language. 22 staff, including 14 teachers. An after-school club (Skólasel) for grades 1-3. Children can take music lessons during the school day. Parents in a focus group said the school lacked enough "support in the community" and that indiscipline among pupils was persistent. The school won the national parents' association award (Heimili og skóli) for its "Viskubrunnur" ("well of wisdom") project, which involves the whole school community and residents of the town. SOURCED: Menntamálastofnun, external evaluation of Seyðisfjarðarskóli, October 2020, https://stjornarradid.is/library/03-Verkefni/Menntamal/Ytra-mat/Grunnskolar/seydisfjardarskoli_.pdf
- **Herðubreið community center / Herðubíó.** A local group had the idea for a cultural hub in 1923. Building began in 1946 and it opened on 16 December 1956, holding regular film screenings and dances. The cinema closed in 2008 (old equipment, no money) and reopened in 2020. Sesselja Hlín Jónasardóttir (born in the town; her grandfather did the original woodwork) and Celia Harrison (from New Zealand) took the building over in 2017 and renovated it, with Sesselja doing much of the painting herself during the pandemic. It has one screen with 115 seats and shows films on Fridays and Sundays, mixing Hollywood with art-house titles. It hosts the Flat Earth Film Festival and film clubs. The building also holds concerts, dances, theater, **weddings and funerals**, and LungA School works upstairs. A Minions screening drew 250 people: 150 extra chairs were brought in and the show started an hour late. In one spring, visitors were stuck in town for four days by mountain closures. SOURCED: Zubenko, Reykjavík Grapevine, 2024-08-16, https://grapevine.is/?p=10077052 . "The only cinema in the whole of East Iceland": UNVERIFIED (snippet, east.is/islandshotel).
- **Stafdalur ski area**, on Route 93 up on Fjarðarheiði, 8 km from the center of Seyðisfjörður and 21 km from Egilsstaðir. It has a beginner rope lift (100 m, weekends and holidays), a lower lift (900 m, 190 m vertical), an upper lift (700 m, 160 m vertical), a 5 km cross-country trail groomed when weather allows, rentals and a lodge. SOURCED: https://east.is/en/service/stafdalur-skiing-area (undated). Floodlit; run jointly by the Hérað area and Seyðisfjörður; snowmobile trails toward Dyrfjöll: UNVERIFIED (east.is snippet). Note: the ski area sits on the same pass whose closures cut the town off.

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Translations from Spanish are mine. ### A. Settings

- **Parque Independencia (Parque Central).** It was built in the early 20th century to mark the founding of the republic in 1902, on the site of Baracoa's first cemetery (17th-19th-century burials by the church). It "became the central plaza where retretas [band concerts] and paseos [evening strolls] were held." By the 20th century it was ringed by the Teatro Encanto (finished 1931), the Lyceum (1927-31), hotels, bars and shops. A 1950s study counted thirteen bars and restaurants around it. Today it hosts craft fairs, book events and political events. Its central fountain shows indigenous Baracoa mythology. SOURCED: EcuRed, "Parque Central (Baracoa)," https://www.ecured.cu/Parque_Central_(Baracoa) (undated wiki entry, Cuban state encyclopedia).
- **Cine-Teatro El Encanto**, facing the park; opened as a theater on 2 May 1932; the Hotel Plaza is on its upper floor. UNVERIFIED (EcuRed snippet; dates differ from the park entry: 1931 vs 1932).
- **Manuel Fuentes Borges baseball stadium.** National Series games were played here "year after year." It has been abandoned for years, with parts in danger of collapse. Baracoa is described as a municipality with "enormous baseball tradition." UNVERIFIED (CubaNet snippet; site 403).
- **Toa river crossings (Cayo Güín and other river communities).** The about 2,813 residents of Cayo Güín cross the Toa, Cuba's highest-flow river, several times a day on bamboo rafts pushed with long poles (cayados), to take children to school, go to work and shop. Courtship and life events are tied to the crossing. SOURCED: Paz, OnCuba, 2012-08-23, https://oncubanews.com/tendencias/los-navegantes-del-rio-toa . The rafts ("cayucas") are lashed with palm leaf or wire. A rafter from Quiviján learned to steer by age nine: "the cheapest and fastest" way across. SOURCED: Rodríguez Milán, OnCuba, 2014-08-20, https://oncubanews.com/en/cuba/society-cuba/cuban-traditions/the-other-rafters-yemaya-and-a-pig/
- **Cacao farms in the hills.** Families keep thatched drying structures with sliding trays for cacao and coffee and make chorote at home. Hamlets such as El Güirito receive visitors for tastings. UNVERIFIED (villaparadisobaracoa snippet). In 2011, Baracoa produced about 75% of Cuba's cocoa and 70% of its coconut. Output fell from nearly 1,500 t to 1,300 t (2010) because of low state prices. Farmer Osvaldo Hernández (eight in his family): "We've gotten to the point of sowing root vegetables on cocoa plantations because we have to survive." The Cocoa Research Station keeps 256 genotypes. The region gets over 90 in of rain a year and has been nicknamed **"the Urinal of the Sky"** since the 19th century. SOURCED: González, Havana Times, 2011-04-18, https://havanatimes.org/?p=41855 (note: round 1's "85%" share stays UNVERIFIED; this source gives 75%).

### B. Subcultures

- **Toa rafters (balseros / cayuqueros).** They are an occupation (moving people, produce and animals across and along the river) that has become a festival competition (see E). Women and men both compete ("for ten years" as of 2012). SOURCED: OnCuba 2012, 2014.
- **Cacao and coconut campesinos** (above), under state purchase prices; they intercrop bananas and root crops to survive. SOURCED: Havana Times 2011.
- **Indigenous-descendant families.** Jose Barreiro, writing for Cultural Survival, says indigenous settlements at Tiguabos, Palenque and the San Andrés valley were dispossessed in the 19th century. Families named Rojas and Ramírez resettled in the Yateras valley and formed the community **Caridad de los Indios**. Along the Toa valley down to Baracoa and Yumurí, and along the coast to Los Arados (Punta Maisí), the Rojas, Ramírez, Romero, Cobas, Rivero and many Jiménez, Hernández, Veloz and Cabrera families "retain history, identity, and customs." Practices he describes: planting yuca and boniato by the moon (otherwise plants "rot early"); casabe (cassava bread) and ajiaco; grandmothers as herbal healers; tobacco given as "an offering to the mother plant" before gathering medicinal herbs; thatched bohíos of royal palm; shells (guamo) on graves. Anthropologist Manuel Rivero de la Calle's studies (1964, 1972-73) found at least 1,000 people with Arawak physical traits in Yateras alone. Official scholarship long held that the indigenous people were extinct by 1550 (Fernando Ortiz). One scholar quoted refuses to call them Indians and uses "descendants." SOURCED: Barreiro, "Indians in Cuba," Cultural Survival Quarterly (online 2010-03-02), https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/indians-cuba . Reported as the author presents it.
- **Catholic parishioners.** The parish church of Nuestra Señora de la Asunción was made a **minor basilica**; the decree was read at Mass on 15 Aug 2013, the city's founding day. Restoration from 2009 replaced the roof and reinforced the structure. The Cruz de la Parra, "slightly over two meters" when found, now measures 67 x 57 cm "due to pieces taken by devotees over the centuries"; it is a National Monument. SOURCED: ACI Prensa / EWTN News, 2013-08-16, https://www.ewtnnews.com/es/mundo/america/cuba-iglesia-parroquial-de-baracoa-es-elevada-a-basilica-menor

## 8. Ilulissat, Nuuk and Tasiilaq, Greenland

All pages read 2026-10-26 unless marked. Round 1 covers the transport blockers, the economy, populations, Kalaaliaraq, Katuaq, the National Theater and Museum, the Egede statue, the Icefjord Center, Sermermiut, the Ammassalik Museum, drum-song duels, East Greenland supply and the sled-dog zones. ### A. Settings

**Nuuk**
- **Blok P**, built 1965-66: about 320 apartments, five stories, over 200 m long, "cut right across Nuuk in an east-west direction," and home to about 1% of Greenland's population. It was designed without regard for Inuit ways of living. Narrow doorways were hard to pass in heavy winter clothes, and European-size wardrobes could not hold fishing gear, so residents kept gear on balconies, "blocking fire exits." Early on, fishermen processing catch in bathtubs caused drainage problems. Demolition was proposed in 2010 by the government and city council, and the building came down in stages from 2011 to 2014 (final demolition 19 Oct 2012). Residents were mostly rehoused in **Qinngorput**. The north wall once carried the largest known Greenlandic flag, made from discarded clothing by artist Julie Edel Hardenberg with schoolchildren. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blok_P . Concerns of rehoused tenants about higher costs and poor transport in Qinngorput; **Nuussuaq** built in the late 1980s: UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia snippets of other pages).
- **The old colonial harbor** is the focus of National Day in Nuuk (E). UNVERIFIED (visitgreenland snippet).
- **Katuaq forecourt**: the Nuuk Snow Festival moved there in 2025 (E). SOURCED: visitgreenland event page.

**Ilulissat**
- **Harbor and fish plant.** With boat fishing now possible through winter, larger halibut catches land in the harbor, a shift from the era of stable sea ice. SOURCED: Murray, Marketplace, 2022-11-25, https://www.marketplace.org/story/2022/11/25/ilulissat-greenland-fast-climate-changes-are-altering-a-way-of-life . ### B. Subcultures

- **Mushers and the Greenland Dog.** The national race, **Avannaata Qimussersua** ("The Great Dog Sled of the North"), was founded in 1989 by musher Ole Qvist as a regional race in Uummannaq and became national by 2021. It is held in March or April in West Greenland north of the Arctic Circle, at a different town each year (Ilulissat in 2024). About 40 km over land and ice; purebred Greenland Dogs only; up to 12 dogs per team; traditional low sleds; vaccination required. Some towns' mushers were banned in 2019 over non-purebred dogs. Winner 2025: Henrik Jensen of Ilulissat. In 2025 a U.S. grant supported mushers' participation, and Usha Vance cancelled a planned public appearance amid controversy over U.S. talk of controlling Greenland. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avannaata_Qimussersua . About 37 mushers and 444 dogs; a "rite of passage for local teenagers," with winning or starting rewarded by "free beer and long-lasting honor": UNVERIFIED (mushing.com / Air Greenland snippets).
- **Sled-dog decline (national).** The number of sled dogs halved over 20 years to about 15,000 (2017). Reasons given: climate change and poor sea ice, snowmobiles, dearer dog food (fish waste now exported for human food), and disease (10 million DKK over three years for vaccination). Scientists made 22 recommendations, including UNESCO protection; one called it "the biggest working dog culture in the world." SOURCED: Drivsholm, ScienceNordic, 2017-08-12, https://sciencenordic.com/greenlands-sled-dog-population-decreasing-rapidly . A team of 12-14 dogs costs DKK 3,000-4,000 a month, and the dog-hunting season shrank from five months to about six weeks: UNVERIFIED (snippet).
- **Young bilinguals and "Nuuk-Danish."** Birgitte Jacobsen ("Colonial Danish," *International Journal of the Sociology of Language* 159, 2003) described a Danish variety with a "peculiar staccato rhythm" and "monotonous intonation." It is spoken mainly by young people with Danish as a first language, or monolinguals close to Greenlandic, who use standard Danish formally and Nuuk-Danish in their groups as an "in-group marker." The reviewer reads it as a sign that young people have moved past a "neither-nor identity crisis." SOURCED: Fermaud, review, Univ. of Zurich "Skandinavien postkolonial," 2020-11-25, https://dlf.uzh.ch/sites/skandinavien-postkolonial/review-birgitte-jacobsen-colonial-danish/ (secondary; the 2003 article itself not read).

Hosts have included Ammassalik (Tasiilaq), Nanortalik and Kangaatsiaq. Hence "the season lasts only a week," true only of the final, because teams must fly or sail. ### E. Cultural activities and civic life

- **Mitaartut** (around Epiphany, January 6). Silent masked visitors (one is a *mitaartoq*) go house to house, communicate only by gesture, try to stay anonymous, and are rewarded with "cakes, candy, and cigarettes." The name comes from *mitaarneq*, "cutting faces," from smearing soot on the face when there is no mask. It is "a blend of indigenous Inuit culture and Danish influence." Masks mix "traditional scare spirits and contemporary themes" (one copied Munch's *The Scream*). SOURCED: Visit Greenland, "Mitaartut - The silent masked performances," https://visitgreenland.com/articles/mitaartut/ (undated). East-West differences: NOT FOUND.
- **National Day (Ullortuneq), 21 June.** Introduced in 1985 by Home Rule; self-government began on this date in 2009; a Danish flag day since 2016. "Celebrated in all Greenlandic settlements": morning singing, coffee, flag raising, speeches, folk dance, music, kayaking. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_National_Day . **In Tasiilaq**: speeches, choirs and a shared breakfast; a morning **seal-hunt competition**; a **women's flensing competition**; an outdoor gathering at **Ittimiini** (weather permitting) with choirs, **drum dancing**, games and traditional food; an evening at the **sports hall** with **polka**, live music and dancing. Some families instead sail to favorite places to eat Greenlandic food. A resident: "I like that we come together as people on the National Day, and show how proud we are of ourselves and our land." SOURCED: Destination East Greenland, https://eastgreenland.com/celebrating-greenlands-national-day-in-east-greenland/ (undated).
