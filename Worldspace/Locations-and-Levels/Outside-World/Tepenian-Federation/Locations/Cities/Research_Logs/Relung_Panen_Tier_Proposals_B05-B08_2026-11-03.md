**Kept as first-pass, with the reason noted** (these are the places a reader might expect to change):
- **Ladakh (#51), PRIMARY kept.** The institution half holds: villages owe labor or cash for the channels (355 households in nine neighborhoods at Phyang), a village water officer rotates the water, and a dispute ended in a written agreement. Two cautions go with it: only 2 of the 10 artificial glaciers one NGO built still worked in 2013, and the water problem is scarcity timed by ice, where the city's is melt and too much water (the same difference that moved the Negev farms down). I kept PRIMARY because the timing-by-ice mechanism bears on F13 and F4 together; the developer may prefer SECONDARY on the scarcity point.
- **Iquitos (#55), PRIMARY kept.** Round 1 added that power also arrives by river as fuel for an isolated, failing thermal system; Round 2 added a national law exempting fuel consumed in Loreto from sales and excise tax and committing the state to reinforce Loreto's thermal power rather than connect it to the grid. That strengthens F5 and F12.
- **Japanese plant factories (#59), SECONDARY kept.** Electricity is one large cost, not always the largest, and under half of operators are profitable; the flagship Kameoka plant stopped in 2024. It stays the only researched case of fully enclosed stacked growing at commercial scale, which F2 and F12 need.
- **Sundrop Farms (#60), SECONDARY kept.** It still relied on the grid and on mains-water backup; ownership changed repeatedly. It is the one researched case of a desert greenhouse making its own water and heat.
- **Biosphere 2 (#61), SUPPORTING kept.** Its numbers on the cost of enclosed farming are the best documented (66-hour weeks, about 45% of work time on food, oxygen to about 14%), but eight people is far from the city's scale. An upgrade to SECONDARY is a fair call if the developer values the cost-of-enclosure data over scale.

## 3 · Corrections the research made to the first-pass reasoning

- **Ladakh:** there are two techniques by two different people, horizontal artificial glaciers (from 1987) and vertical ice stupas (from 2013–14), not one. The stored ice is not neutral: at least one documented case set an upstream village against a downstream one and the district administration ordered the diversion stopped; a written agreement followed in 2017–18. Sowing is April–June; natural glacier melt arrives only from late July.
- **Siwa:** "one route" holds only for the paved era (first asphalt road, to Mersa Matruh, in the mid-1980s; the Bahariya road needs a permit). The salt lakes are partly a modern product, swollen by drainage from wells drilled since the 1980s. The Siyaha festival is first a reconciliation festival (October full moon), tied to a Sufi order; its claimed age conflicts (160 years versus 700 years), left open. The oasis is three populations, not one (Siwi tribes, about 2,000 Shahibat Bedouin in their own villages, and Egyptian incomers). Population: 28,088 in the 2017 census (2023 estimate 36,574), which explains most of the 25,000–60,000 spread.
- **Baikal:** for Listvyanka, on the mainland at the end of a road from Irkutsk, the ice shapes winter tourism more than access; the three-regime finding is Olkhon's. Off-season crossings are commercial and paid (700–1,000 rubles each way in February 2026), not a free resident service as far as any opened page shows.
- **Norilsk:** "no road to the outside" holds for the rest of Russia, but there is an internal railway to its own port, Dudinka; supply is by river and sea as well as air. The K6 side (foreigners need special permission) is documented.
- **Iquitos:** "largest" is qualified in the source ("the largest city in the world that cannot be reached by road that is not on an island"); one road exists but ends about 100 km south at Nauta.
- **Tristan da Cunha:** the land is communally owned, but the potato plots are held and worked by individual families, not farmed in common. Sheep limits conflict between sources (two per person versus seven per household), left open.
- **Inuvik:** it is a former hockey rink of the residential-school system (care note), run by a non-profit and not by the town; seasonal rather than year-round.
- **Hveragerði:** heat alone does not make the greenhouses viable; winter-lighting electricity is the largest cost. The settlement formed around several heat-using trades, not greenhouses alone.
- **Japanese plant factories:** electricity is about a third of production cost (or 20–35% of sales), not clearly "the main cost"; labor is larger in one cost model (US$279 per m² per year against US$166 for electricity). Most daily work is manual.
- **Sundrop:** it is backed up by grid power and mains water, and the land and glasshouses are now owned separately from the growing business.
- **Biosphere 2:** the first crew grew about 80% (reported as 83%) of its food; it split into two factions; the second crew (1994) reached full food sufficiency but its mission ended early.
- **Eden Project:** an educational charity and visitor attraction, not a food producer.
- **Leamington:** the workforce is year-round employer-tied labor rather than purely seasonal.
- **Cameron Highlands:** its share of national vegetable supply has fallen to about 15%; the farm-level share under rain shelters was NOT FOUND.
- **Đà Lạt:** the move under plastic is now the object of an official rollback target (under 40% greenhouse cover per ward by 2025, and zero in inner wards by 2030).
- **Souss-Massa:** the deficit is now partly offset by seawater desalination.
- **Ica:** asparagus is no longer the defining crop; the claim fits Ica's asparagus period (about 1995–2015).
- **Naivasha:** the lake now rises and floods worker estates (since 2020) as well as running low (2009 drought).
- **Salinas:** the cold chain had two eras and two workforces, not one; trucks replaced rail from the 1970s. A first-written Salinas 2020 population was corrected to UNVERIFIED in the log.
- **Totara / Port Chalmers:** the meat was air-cooled on land and **frozen on the ship** (a Bell-Coleman machine), not frozen on land; the two sites are a rail journey apart. Cargo counts (4,311 versus 4,331 sheep) and the Burnside opening year (1882 versus 1883) are unresolved.
- **Lānaʻi:** the island was not only one farm: one company built town, harbor and fields together; the single-owner pattern outlived pineapple (final harvest October 1992).
- **Sugar camps:** separation by origin was deliberate control; the shared culture formed where the separation broke.
- **Absorption centers and ulpan:** the method changed (institutional housing, then cash-based direct absorption from about 1989–90); results are documented as uneven.
- **Shenzhen:** "no inheritance" is corrected: the original Cantonese and Hakka villagers kept land rights and house the arrivals as tenants. Two 1979–80 population figures (about 314,000 and about 30,000) are left unresolved in the log (the town proper versus the county area).
