## 1 · #6 Clarksdale, Mississippi

### A. Other settings
- **Brickyard neighborhood:** built after the Second World War to house Black veterans; one resident's father laid brick to tell the nearly identical houses apart. **SOURCED** (Addie Citchens, "Giving Context to the Blues: A Walk with Brenda Luckett," *Mississippi Folklife*, 11 Aug 2024). [K4, P4]
- **New World neighborhood as a working neighborhood, not only a heritage zone:** Messenger's pool hall sat among a barbershop, a Black dentist's office and a funeral home in buildings the Messenger family owned; St. James Temple (Church of God in Christ) is down the block; children went after school to St. Francis Catholic Center. **SOURCED** (Citchens, "Where the Locals Go," *Mississippi Folklife*, 3 May 2020). A circle of churches in the district sheltered civil-rights activists; Haven United Methodist held meetings in its basement, entered through a door marked "Elevator"; Centennial Baptist was a SNCC meeting place. **SOURCED** (Citchens 2024). [K4, K10, P4]
- **Old Fourth Street (renamed Martin Luther King Boulevard in the 2000s):** for many years the center of the Black business district (restaurants, funeral homes, churches, nightclubs; Aaron Henry's pharmacy operated into the 1980s); by 2013 Red's and Messenger's were the only businesses the writer could identify as still operating there. **SOURCED** (John Shaw, *The Delta Review*, 17 Apr 2013). Wade Walton's barbershop, in a building that had been W.C. Handy's house, was where boys read the *Chicago Defender* on Saturdays. **SOURCED** (Citchens 2024). [K9, K10]
- **Segregated spaces still visible:** the Paramount theater's back staircase (the former Black entrance); the former Greyhound station (site of sit-ins, now a restaurant). Aaron Henry's house was bombed twice and neighborhood men took turns guarding it. **SOURCED** (Citchens 2024). [K6, K9]
- **Downtown building stock (as the city markets it):** fewer than 250 downtown properties, about 30% (70-plus) for sale, lease or partnership; prices from under $50,000, many under $100,000; one 7,549 sq ft building listed at about $23/sq ft; some leases under $1/sq ft a month; average building date about 1932 (oldest 1890); most former retail, plus department stores, a hotel, a theater and a six-story office building; many with apartments above. **SOURCED as city claims** (cityofclarksdale.org, "Arts & Culture District Buildings," read 2026-11-20). [K8, K9]
- **Schools as a divided setting:** Clarksdale High (white) and Higgins High (Black) merged on 1 Feb 1970 after *Alexander v. Holmes County* (29 Oct 1969); when schools reopened on 2 Feb 1970, 574 of Clarksdale Junior High's 585 white students did not return; Lee Academy was created that year (about 500 at the first meeting, 115 registered on the spot), moved that fall to a campus on a former cotton field, staffed by former public-school teachers; Presbyterian, Baptist and Methodist schools also opened or expanded; Higgins's principal was demoted to assistant, Clarksdale High's kept his post. In fall 2015 Coahoma's three private schools were 7% African American, the public districts over 90% Black; Lee Academy tuition about $6,900. **Clarksdale Collegiate** (Mississippi's first rural charter school, fall 2018, in a former church youth room): 145 students in year one, 93% Black; extended day over eight hours, school year about three weeks longer; about $17,000 revenue per student including philanthropy; more than 1,300 residents petitioned against it; the Clarksdale school board backed a lawsuit against the charter law. **SOURCED** (Danielle Dreilinger, *The Hechinger Report*, 19 Sep 2019, updated 21 Oct 2020). Lee Academy's initial enrollment of 654 and "never had a Black student" as of 1986 **UNVERIFIED** (Wikipedia snippet). [K4, K9, K8, K6]
- **Clinics:** Aaron E. Henry Community Health Services Center, a federally qualified health center founded in Clarksdale in 1979 and named for the pharmacist and NAACP leader; CEO since 1989 Aurelia Jones-Taylor; expanded to Tunica; for over 30 years it has run the Delta Area Rural Transit System (DARTS), which began as rides to medical care and became the public transit service Delta Rides, used by hundreds of riders to reach jobs, childcare and services; a workforce program trains residents (some became nurse practitioners and billing staff); a 2023 program with Southern Bancorp refers patients to financial advisers and credit counselors. **SOURCED** (CHC Chronicles, "Community Health Center Initiatives Advance Empowerment," read 2026-11-20). School-based clinics in Clarksdale, Marks and Tunica and a mobile unit **UNVERIFIED** (NACHC listing snippet). [K1, K5, P7]
- **The river as a setting:** Quapaw Canoe Company (founded about 1999 by John Ruskey; also in Memphis and Vicksburg; about a dozen guides) takes Clarksdale children from Griot Arts and the Spring Initiative onto the Mississippi; apprentices learn to build canoes and to guide; one guide who leads most youth work began as an apprentice; a former apprentice said she grew up hearing that the river was a place where people die; Ruskey said the aim is youth "who have fun in a place that they've been told to fear their whole life." **SOURCED** (WWNO, 30 May 2024). **Moon Lake**, an oxbow of about 2,300 acres outside the main levee about 15 miles north of town, with three public ramps, picnic shelters and a restaurant; hunting clubs keep lodges along it. **SOURCED** for ramps, size and distance (Visit Clarksdale, "Moon Lake"); hunting lodges and summer crowds of "hundreds" **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of mydeltaworld and others). [K14, K10, SM:competence-under-pressure, P4]

### B. Subcultures
- **Jewish merchants (a community that has almost ended):** Jews were in Coahoma County by the late 1860s (first known Jewish birth 1868, Friars Point); started as peddlers, then dry-goods and general stores supplied by Memphis wholesalers; in the 1916 directory at least two-thirds of 15 dry-goods stores were Jewish-owned; about 400 people in the 1930s (then the largest Jewish community in Mississippi), nearly 300 at the end of the Second World War, 100 families in 1970, 20 members in the early 2000s, one young family in 2020. Congregation founded 1896 by five families (services in homes or the Knights of Pythias hall); first synagogue 1910 (Delta Ave.); Orthodox and Reform factions kept together in the 1929 building by holding services on separate floors; Ladies' Aid Society (1916) ran an annual New Year's Ball; B'nai B'rith (1910); a rabbi of the 1930s founded a short-lived institute of Jewish studies (1938); the rabbi from 1962 to 1978 opposed the civil-rights movement and was valued for it by segregationist supporters; one memoir reports the sheriff supplied prison labor, almost certainly Black prisoners, to build the 1910 synagogue. Decline causes given: New Deal acreage cuts and mechanical pickers (from 1947) removed sharecropper customers; chain discount stores. Deconsecrated 3 May 2003; Torahs sent to a North Carolina camp and to a new congregation in Poland; the cemetery remains. **SOURCED** (ISJL, *Encyclopedia of Southern Jewish Communities*, "Clarksdale, Mississippi," read 2026-11-20). [K4, K9, P9]
- **Lebanese families:** arrived from the late 1870s into the 1960s; nearly all began as peddlers selling dry goods to Black sharecroppers, on foot (one man walked about 20 miles a day) and later by Model A; then grocers and restaurateurs. Abe's Bar-B-Q opened in 1924 by an immigrant from Zahlé; the owner grew up in Riverton, a settlement of Italian, Lebanese and African American residents; Lebanese dishes are cooked there on Sundays; in 1965 Abe's and the restaurant that became Chamoun's Rest Haven were the only two local restaurants that served Black customers in a test of segregated dining. **SOURCED** (The Kitchen Sisters / NPR, "Kibbe at the Crossroads," WUSF mirror, 31 Jan 2008). Chafik Chamoun (b. 1932 Zahlé, arrived 1954) sold household products door to door in rural Coahoma for 19 years for a national products company; bought a grocery in 1968 (NPR gives 1960) that added kibbe sandwiches to compete with supermarkets; bought Rest Haven (American, Italian, Lebanese) in 1990; his daughter runs it. **SOURCED** (University of Mississippi eGrove, "The Lebanese in Mississippi," Chafik Chamoun profile; interview 6 Apr 2007). A Lebanese social club (Clarksdale Cedars Club) and Catholic faith as common among Delta Lebanese **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K4, K12, P9]
- **Chinese families (update to Round 1):** a Clarksdale-born grocer whose father came in 1920 ran a store in the heart of the Black neighborhood and sat on the Chamber of Commerce, the Exchange Club and the arts council; the community had kept to itself until the early 1990s, when the city asked him to start a Chinese New Year celebration open to all, with a parade and a sold-out banquet; a retired merchant's garden fed the family year-round. **SOURCED** (Stephen Flinn Young, "Chinese in Mississippi," *Southern Exposure*/Facing South, 1 Oct 1994). The Delta's Chinese church and mission school (1937-1951) were in Cleveland, not Clarksdale; the Chinese cemetery is in Greenville. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K4, K2, P9]
- **Black church life:** Clarksdale holds the Church of God in Christ state headquarters (Northern Mississippi Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction) on Sunflower Ave.; its jurisdictional bishop has pastored St. James Temple since 1972; the city installed a street sign in his honor in Oct 2017, mounted by the municipal utility. **SOURCED** (cityofclarksdale.org, "Bishop T.T. Scott"). [K10, P6]
- **Mennonites:** a Mennonite community that moved to the Delta in the 1960s started a downtown restaurant (The Dutch Oven). **SOURCED** (Jack Lawton, HottyToddy.com, 12 Feb 2014). A Clarksdale Mennonite Church cemetery is listed in a directory. **UNVERIFIED.** History of the congregation **NOT FOUND**. [K4]
- **Newcomers to downtown:** from 2002 a wave of incomers (record-store owner, a blues museum owner, a gallery-owning musician couple, a farm-supply store owner) came for about five years, drawn by blues history; at first the downtown street was nearly empty by 5:30 p.m.; returning locals then opened restaurants; loft apartments were made above a restaurant. **SOURCED** (HottyToddy 2014). (The local word for these incomers is in the slang file.) [K4, K9]
- **Youth:** Griot Arts (after-school and summer arts for grades about 4-12: visual arts, theater, choir, instruments, dance, a rock band; tutoring; mentors; grew from 12 to 45 students in about four years; Walton Family Foundation support); its founder writes that many students have known abuse, gangs or an absent parent and that it often takes more than a year for them to trust staff. **SOURCED** (Walton Family Foundation, "Awakening Opportunity Through the Gift of Creativity," Oct 2016). Founded 2011; a coffee-shop work-experience program **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K13, K14]
- **Elders:** Red's Old Timers Blues Festival (founded 2018 by Red Paden; free; at the MLK Park stage across from Red's; for musicians 60 and over; held 31 Aug 2025) aims to honor older players while aging musicians die and jukes close, and to strengthen the Black community downtown; the current organizer is Paden's son, now the city's mayor. **SOURCED** (Aallyah Wright, Capital B News, via Yahoo, 5 Sep 2025). [K2, K13, P9]
- **Speech (K11):** English; earlier Jewish settlers were German-speaking (about 1870); Lebanese families brought Arabic; Delta Chinese spoke Cantonese (Round 1). **SOURCED** (ISJL; NPR 2008). No further note.

### C. Leisure industries and what locals do
- **Size of the visitor economy:** 2024 direct visitor spending in Coahoma County $85.8 million (lodging $22.8M, food and beverage $20.3M, recreation $19.2M, retail $15.3M, transportation $8.3M); total impact $128.1M; 676 direct and 236 indirect tourism jobs (912), $32.6M income; $6.8M state and local taxes ($1.7M direct local). **SOURCED** (Visit Mississippi / Tourism Economics 2024 county table, mstourism.com Coahoma PDF, text extracted locally). [K8]
- **Juke Joint Festival in numbers (2015):** daytime crowd guessed at about 7,000, night crowd about 3,200 by wristband sales; visitors tracked from at least 28 countries, 46 states plus D.C., about 54 Mississippi counties; volunteers collect visitor data and sign-in sheets sit in museums and restaurants; every hotel within an hour booked; day stages grew from about 3 to 12, night venues from about 5 to over 20; local performers are booked first. **SOURCED** (Rachel West / Delta Business Journal, HottyToddy, 12 Mar 2015). [K3, K8]
- **Night-life calendar (2016-2019 guide):** Red's (Wed, Fri, Sat, Sun nights; cash; beer sold; barbecue grilled on the street); Bluesberry Cafe (Monday nights, weekend "blues breakfast"; local musicians sit in); Ground Zero (Wed-Sat; Thursday jam with a local house band); Hambone gallery (Tuesday); Hopson Commissary (Monday free home-cooked food; "locals for many decades"); Levon's (Sunday afternoon session where locals and visitors mingle); New Roxy (open-air stage, theater, film; renovated by a merchant mariner). **SOURCED** (*Delta Bohemian*, "A Clarksdale Live Music Venues Guide," updated 14 Nov 2019). [K10, K13]
- **What locals do instead:** Messenger's on ordinary nights is a locals' room (pool, dominoes, a jukebox playing from open to close), crowded with tourists only at festival time. **SOURCED** (Citchens 2020). Moon Lake boating and fishing (above). The nearest casino resort (Lula) is about 20 minutes away **UNVERIFIED** (travel listing snippet). (Round 1's addendum already records that many locals go to Tunica casinos.) [K10]
- **Sister-city tourism:** Clarksdale and Notodden, Norway, have been sister cities since 1996; the Notodden blues festival is held in August a week before Clarksdale's Sunflower festival; a 10-member Notodden delegation was welcomed in May 2016 with a porch party in the historic district. **SOURCED** (Delta Blues Museum press release, 10 May 2016). Clarksdale blues students attend a student seminar in Notodden **UNVERIFIED** (snippet). [K3, K8]

### D. Third places
- **Messenger's** (New World): opened 1910 by Edward Messenger (liquor license from 1907), the town's oldest continuously running business; first a pool hall, domino den, juke and café for sharecroppers coming to town after payday, near Issaquena and the railroad, with three or four stools and hamburgers and bologna sandwiches; moved to a larger building in the mid-1940s; a gathering place for poor Black residents "away from work pressures and police harassment"; a grandson took over in the 1960s and added ping-pong; the kitchen now serves one item (a Polish sausage with grilled onions and peppers); beer only, with mixers supplied for liquor customers bring. **SOURCED** (Citchens 2020). [K10, K12, K6]
- **Red's:** in a building that was formerly a music store where an early rock-and-roll band bought instruments; posted rule "Cash only and no videos allowed." **SOURCED** (Visit Clarksdale blog, 2013 with 2024 update). [K6, K10]
- **Riverside Hotel (corrections and additions to Round 1):** opened 12 Jul 1916 as the Clarksdale Colored Hospital (later the Ancient Order of Watchmen hospital, at one time run by G.T. Thomas and Charles Haffer Jr.), serving Black patients until 1942; opened as a hotel on 9 Nov 1944 by Mrs. Z.L. Hill (license applied 30 Jul 1945); guests included Sam Cooke, Howlin' Wolf, John Lee Hooker, the Staple Singers, the Five Blind Boys and Rev. C.L. Franklin as well as local musicians who roomed there; Ike Turner's band rehearsed in the basement in the early 1950s and Mrs. Hill embroidered their neckties; a 2021 city resolution names it the site where blues gave birth to rock and roll; her son Frank Ratliff (d. 28 Mar 2013) and his wife ran it; granddaughters own it and in 2025 opened a Living Blues and Civil Rights Museum. **SOURCED** (Mississippi Blues Trail marker No. 4, msbluestrail.org/?p=448; Delta Blues Museum obituary notice). Green Book listing still **UNVERIFIED**. [K4, K5, K10, R (lodging as rest stop for touring players)]
- **Abe's and Rest Haven** as family Sunday tables for Lebanese families and, from 1965, places that served Black diners (B above). [K12, K10]
- **Wade Walton's barbershop** (Saturday reading of the *Defender*, above). [K10, K5]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Sunflower River Blues & Gospel Festival (chased from Round 1's UNVERIFIED):** grew from free Holiday Music Festivals of 1986-87 sponsored by the Downtown Association to bring shoppers; first full festival 4 Jun 1988 (named Sunflower Riverbank Blues Festival), funded mainly by downtown merchants and organized by Jim O'Neal and Dr. Patricia Johnson; 1989 funded largely by individual donors; a biracial group of volunteers formed the nonprofit Sunflower River Blues Association in 1990; North Delta Academy of Gospel Music held a gospel festival from 1992 and joined in 1993 (the chronology gives 1994 as the merger year); gospel staged on Sunday and in the Civic Auditorium; a student-run electric stage from 2012; canceled 2020; the city "adjusted local laws to accommodate festival and nightclub activity"; Coahoma County created a tourism commission in 1991; annual awards named for the WROX deejay (since 1991) and for a gospel-festival co-founder. **SOURCED** (Delta Blues Museum, "Sunflower Chronology"; Blues Trail marker, msbluestrail.org/?p=472). (Round 1's "late-1990s" date for the tourism commission is contradicted here by 1991.) [K3, K1, K6, K13]
- **Delta Blues Museum (corrections):** founded 31 Jan 1979 by the director of the Carnegie Public Library, starting in a room at Myrtle Hall Elementary School, moving into the main library in 1981 (the director took the collection home each night for security); city took over from the library board and moved it to the depot in 1999; attendance began at about one visitor a month and reached an estimated 25,000 in 2012; Delta Blues Education Program begun 1992. **SOURCED** (Blues Trail marker, msbluestrail.org/?p=331). [K13, K14]
- **Juneteenth / BAM Fest:** a June 19-21 downtown festival of local artists and a collective of jukes and clubs, broadcast on local radio; disrupted by the pandemic at the city's first Juneteenth celebration (2020). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of Visit Clarksdale and Press Register). [K2, K3]
- **Chinese New Year** (city-requested, early 1990s; above). [K2]
- **Youth curfew:** under-18s may not be on public streets after 9 p.m. Sunday-Thursday and after about 11 p.m. Friday-Saturday until 5:30 a.m., under an ordinance of about 2011; police stepped up enforcement in Oct 2021 against rising juvenile crime, with citations, fines and possible jail; exceptions for a minor with a parent, legally employed or in an emergency; a Griot Arts staffer said youths out at night often lack a safe home. **SOURCED** (WREG, Mike Suriani, 28 Oct 2021). An earlier summer curfew in 2024, expiring 31 Aug 2024 **UNVERIFIED** (Press Register snippet). [K6]
- **Levee-camp labor (Round 1 thread, Delta-wide, not Clarksdale-specific):** camps ran from late fall to early winter at low water; the workforce was mainly Black sharecroppers valued for mule-driving skill; 12-16 hour days; $1-2 a day, Black workers about $1.50 less than white; irregular pay periods pushed workers into commissary debt; contractors organized gambling, drinking and prostitution after hours; about a third of camp residents were Black women working as cooks or prostitutes; convicts built levees until the early 1900s; the NAACP sent two investigators undercover for three weeks in Dec 1932. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Mississippi Delta levee camps," read 2026-11-20). [K7, K9, K1]
- **1941-42 study of local life:** the Fisk University-Library of Congress Coahoma County study includes a manuscript section titled "Saturday: Gambling in the Delta," a chapter on religious life in transition, and an appendix listing the records on the machines in Clarksdale amusement places (that is, what the jukeboxes held). **SOURCED** for the contents list (Project MUSE, *Lost Delta Found*, Vanderbilt UP 2020); the texts themselves were not read. [K10, K13]

## 2 · #35 The Chitlin' Circuit

### A. Other settings
- **Seasonal tobacco-warehouse dances (closing Round 1's NOT FOUND):** formal dances were held in many North Carolina tobacco towns in warehouses big enough for large crowds, first tied to county fairs and the opening of tobacco markets. In Rocky Mount the dance began in 1870 and took the name June German in 1903; from at least 1918 a dance for Black residents was held the Monday after the white one, in the same warehouse and reusing the white dance's decorations and stage; by the 1930s it drew upwards of 8,000; in the 1940s Louis Armstrong and Count Basie played it; bands alternated through all-night dancing, and people from all over eastern North Carolina dressed up to go. **SOURCED** (North Carolina Arts Council, "Featured Stories from the Region's Musical History," African American Music Trails). In Wilson, an Army veteran converted a tobacco pack house into a nightclub from 1947 that ran over 45 years (James Brown, Little Richard, Al Green played). **SOURCED** (same). [K2, K4, K7, K10, P4]
- **Hotels above and beside the clubs:** in Memphis, Andrew and Ernestine Mitchell leased two floors above a drugstore on Beale Street in 1946: a 30-room hotel (managed by Ernestine) on the third floor, where they lived, and a lounge below; they fed and sheltered broke traveling musicians (one later famous guitarist recalled getting a room and a bowl of chili); a singer stayed for weeks with no money; another was a frequent resident whose belongings were left with them after his death in 1954; hotel damaged by fire Oct 1957; Mitchell's agency booked dates for touring blues acts in the 1950s and he let organizations hold benefits at his club. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Sunbeam Mitchell"). In New Orleans, a barber from Plaquemines Parish opened the Dew Drop Inn at 2836 LaSalle St. in 1939 as a hotel (first a sandwich shop for workers from the nearby Magnolia housing project), added a nightclub in 1945; his brother ran the restaurant; the barbershop was part of the complex; he let stranded or out-of-work musicians stay and found them gigs to repay him; he also ran a booking service assembling bands for New Orleans and the Gulf Coast; musicians gathered there before and after work. **SOURCED** (64 Parishes, "Dew Drop Inn"). In Macon, the Douglass Theater stood in a complex with the owner's Colonial Hotel for Black patrons (before 1909). **SOURCED** (Digital Library of Georgia, Douglass Theater records essay). [K4, K5, K10, R (hotels as rest and recovery points for touring players)]
- **Fraternal lodge halls:** the Black Elks (IBPOEW, formed Cincinnati 1898 because the white Elks barred Black members; 12 lodges in 8 states by 1899; women's auxiliary 1902; Civil Rights Commission 1927) and the VFW gave halls to touring blues and R&B acts; members sold tickets to guarantee a turnout; **Pullman porters helped form chapters, especially in the South**; Elks Hart Lodge No. 640, Greenwood, Miss., was one of the Delta's main R&B venues in the 1940s-60s and in 1962 SNCC's first meeting place in town. **SOURCED** (Mississippi Blues Trail marker, msbluestrail.org/?p=339). [K1, K10, K4]
- **Seasonal resorts as circuit stops:** **Carr's Beach**, Annapolis (bought 1902 by a formerly enslaved man and his wife; resort opened 1926; Sparrow's Beach added 1931 by a daughter); after the operator's death in 1948 her son formed an amusement company with a Baltimore businessman who invested $150,000 (pavilion, a nightclub, a midway with slot machines); a team of Black special deputy sheriffs kept order; a July 1956 Chuck Berry show drew an estimated 70,000 though only about 8,000 fit; 11,000 for James Brown in 1962; a local Black-format station's DJ broadcast events live from 1952; roadside jukes and barbecue stands grew around it; decline after Jim Crow ended; last show 1973; the city bought the remaining land in 2022 for a heritage park. **SOURCED** (Ann Powell, Visit Annapolis, 1 Feb 2024). **Idlewild, Michigan** (2,700 acres in Lake County; lots sold from 1912 for "$1 down and $1 a month" by white investors; advertised in the Black press; over 25,000 vacationers a summer; Flamingo and Paradise clubs; a Saginaw businessman ran a bar, a numbers business, a booking agency and a touring revue; the only Black resort with its own church; a Chautauqua with Black speakers); decline after the 1964 Civil Rights Act. **SOURCED** (Bill Castanier, *Lansing City Pulse*, 16 Feb 2022). [K2, K4, K8, K9, K10]
- **City dance halls with a white section:** East Austin's hall at 817 E. 11th St. (Royal Auditorium from 1929, later Cotton Club; two stories, balcony, 1,500-2,000 capacity) hosted Earl Hines, Louis Armstrong, Jimmie Lunceford and others 1932-42; Armstrong and Lunceford sometimes played a second show there after segregated bookings at the university gym or a downtown theater; a rope across the floor marked off a white section, and white people could enter freely while Black people were excluded from white venues; nearby Black colleges were a reason bands came, and one college played basketball games in the hall; raided in 1935 at the governor's direction over gambling and liquor; the overcrowded balcony collapsed during an Armstrong show in the early 1940s; closed by 1943; became a college dormitory in 1944; a new city auditorium in Rosewood Park took over as the segregated venue for large touring acts for about 20 years. **SOURCED** (Michael Schmidt, *Local Memory: A History of Music in Austin*, 12 Jun 2019). [K4, K6, K9, K10]
- **Victory Grill (chased from Round 1):** opened on VJ Day 1945 by a booking agent and band manager as a restaurant and bar for Black servicemen who could not easily get a beer elsewhere; first a lean-to, then the building next door; leased out in 1952; nightclub closed mid-1970s, restaurant stayed; fire 10 Oct 1988; reopened 1996; National Register 16 Oct 1998; a large Juneteenth reunion revived the street in 1987. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Victory Grill"). [K9, K2]

### B. Subcultures
- **Women who ran the business:** Evelyn Johnson (b. 1920 Thibodaux, La.; trained as an X-ray technician; hired at a Houston record store in 1947) ran day-to-day operations of a Houston record company, kept the books at the Bronze Peacock club, and ran the Buffalo Booking Agency from 2809 Erastus St. in the Fifth Ward for over 25 years; her routing built the Southwest extension of the circuit and of the touring circuit for gospel groups; she taught one star singer to read; recording artists got no royalties but a new car if records sold well, so she sent them on the road to earn. **SOURCED** (michaelcorcoran.net, 6 Oct 2022). License to manage union artists in 1950 **UNVERIFIED** (KUTX snippet). Carrie Cunningham (Royal Peacock), Ernestine Mitchell (hotel manager) and the Carr sisters (beach resorts) appear above and in Round 1. [K14, K8, SM:power-rising-step-by-step]
- **Women's bands on the road:** the International Sweethearts of Rhythm began in 1937 as a fundraising swing band of the Piney Woods Country Life School (Mississippi), turned professional in 1941, and crossed the country for nearly a decade by sleeper bus; a white saxophonist who joined in 1941 sometimes wore makeup to pass as Black and kept a low profile around sheriffs; under 1940s Mississippi law a member of Chinese ancestry counted as nonwhite, so she did not draw attention; two members were refused service at a Baltimore lunch counter. **SOURCED** (John McDonough, NPR, via WRTI, 22 Mar 2011). [K6, K4, SM:man-versus-society]
- **Tent-show troupes (the circuit's touring predecessor):** the Rabbit's Foot Company, organized from 1898-1900 by a Black former string-band guitarist from Jacksonville who already ran a small theater chain; by 1904 over 60 performers in three Pullman cars, reaching up to 16 states a season; ran an all-Black baseball team that played local teams in each town (to at least 1916); a 1908 railcar fire in Shelby, N.C., killed several sleeping performers; sold in 1912 to a white carnival operator who moved the base in 1918 to his 1,000-acre plantation near Port Gibson, Miss., where musicians gathered each spring to build the season's show; two cars, an 80 x 110 ft tent, a brass band parading through town, gas lanterns as footlights, no microphones; small towns on weekdays, cities on weekends; 1956: one-day stands, a payroll of 50 including a ten-piece band; last show 1959; vehicles seized and sold for taxes and debt in 1960. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "The Rabbit's Foot Company"). [K7, K5, K13, K14]
- **Territory bands:** dance bands that worked a region on one-nighters (1920s-60s) in VFW halls, Elks lodges, Lions clubs and hotel ballrooms; in the 1920s they traveled by station wagon with an equipment truck, from the mid-1930s in sleeper trailers. **UNVERIFIED** (Wikipedia snippet). The Alphonso Trent orchestra (Fort Smith, Ark.) began as a cooperative in which all members got equal pay and contributed to arrangements, a common Southwest arrangement; a hotel bellman's tip led to a two-week booking in a Dallas hotel ballroom that lasted about 18 months and put it on a 50,000-watt station; it played street corners for coins before that; stranded in Cleveland in 1930 after a fire destroyed its instruments and library. **SOURCED** (CALS *Encyclopedia of Arkansas*, "Alphonso E. Trent"). [K14, K7, K1]
- **Gospel groups (a parallel circuit):** Detroit quartets of the 1930s-40s, some starting as church usher-board quartets, became full-time touring groups on national circuits and broadcast weekly; female groups with piano or organ emerged in the late 1940s-early 1950s; one broadcast Sunday evenings from a Baptist church and was among the first to take gospel into lounges. **SOURCED** (Joyce M. Jackson and James T. Jones IV, "Good News for the Motor City," Smithsonian Festival of American Folklife program book, 1987; PDF text extracted locally). [K13, K14]
- **Segregated musicians' unions:** the American Federation of Musicians (founded Cincinnati 1896) encouraged separate Black locals from the start because most locals barred Black members; Chicago's Black Local 208 was strong and its members felt well represented, distrusting the national body, which treated Black locals as subsidiaries; after 1964 the AFM ordered locals in the same city to merge (Chicago became 10-208); Philadelphia's Black local resisted for fear of losing power and was expelled in 1971. **SOURCED** (Martha Hyde and Gregory Riley, *Allegro*, Local 802, Sep 2023). Los Angeles locals merged 1953, Seattle 1958, San Francisco 1960, Boston 1970. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K1, K14, K9]
- **Speech (K11):** English; performers and audiences African American; Creole and Cajun French influence in the Louisiana trail-ride scene (below). No further note.

### C. Leisure industries and audiences
- **Who promoted:** the Ferguson agency (from late 1941) ran about a dozen bands in touring cycles and trained barbers and bartenders to promote shows in out-of-the-way places where tax collectors would not count patrons; after 1940 the city revoked liquor licenses for Black-owned clubs on Indiana Avenue only. **SOURCED** (Preston Lauterbach, "Chitlin' Circuit Operative: Denver D. Ferguson," prestonlauterbach.com, 12 May 2011). [K8, K14]
- **Pay at the bottom (closing Round 1's UNVERIFIED):** about 1949-50 a club paid one bandleader $3 a night and fed him; he sold the extra food for another $3, and the $6 was split among four musicians (about $2 each). **SOURCED** (NPR, Noah Caldwell and Tinbete Ermyas, via WUSF, 18 Aug 2023). [K7, K8]

### A. Other settings
- **The smoking room as office and bedroom:** the porter shined shoes nightly there, pressed clothes, and cleaned toilets, washbowls and mirrors repeatedly; for most of the Pullman years he slept there on a sofa behind a thin black curtain on a thin mattress while passengers came and went; the rulebook said the porter must "under no circumstances" ask passengers to vacate the smoking room so he could retire; a conductor could give him an empty upper berth in special cases, and tourist cars without a smoker reserved one upper for him; porters got passengers out by saying they had to clean up; an experienced porter made a lower berth in three to five minutes (some claimed 90 seconds); at night he stayed on call, adjusting heat, helping passengers up ladders, caring for the sick, infants and pregnant women; an 1888 instruction book said he "should be almost constantly on his feet"; he kept contagious passengers off; toilets were not flushed in or near stations. **SOURCED** (Larry Tye, "The Work of a Pullman Porter," Alicia Patterson Foundation, 30 Jul 2004). [K6, K7, R (rest taken in a shared work space on sufferance; cleaning and repair rounds)]
- **Before and after the 1937 contract (one museum's summary):** up to 400 hours a month became up to 240; no paid vacation became twelve days; no guaranteed sleep on long or overnight trips became a guaranteed 3-4 hours on trips of 12 hours or more; grievances became possible. **SOURCED** (NMAAHC Searchable Museum, "Pullman Porters and Maids"). An AP account gives the contract's starting pay as $150 a month, which conflicts with Round 1's figures ($77.50-89.50 minimum); the merger date is given as 1979 (AP) against 1978 (Searchable Museum, Round 1). **SOURCED (conflict recorded)** (Herbert G. - **Railway "quarters" (Montreal):** visiting porters generally stayed in small, isolated boarding houses run by the railways in the St. Antoine district, segregated from the housing market. **UNVERIFIED** (Canadian Encyclopedia snippet; that site is on Round 1's refused list and was not opened). The district (named about 1887) housed Black workers from the U.S., the Caribbean and the Maritimes kept near the rail yards by housing discrimination; about 14,000 residents, most Black, were displaced when the city demolished it in the late 1960s-1970s as "urban renewal"; the later name was coined by officials. **SOURCED** (Canadian Museum of History, "St. Antoine (Little Burgundy)"). [K4, K9]
- **The YMCA as a rest stop:** Chicago's Wabash Avenue YMCA (opened 1914 with Julius Rosenwald's backing; housing and job training for migrants; the Association for the Study of Negro Life and History was founded there in 1915; closed 1981; restored for about $9 million in the late 1990s). **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Wabash Avenue YMCA"). ### B. Subcultures
- **Filipino attendants:** the company hired about 400 Filipinos as club-car attendants in 1925, a few months into the union drive, and a dozen Chinese; the company printed articles meant to set them against Black coworkers and tried to create a separate Filipino union, which Randolph refused; many Filipinos became dues-paying BSCP members, some holding staff posts in Chicago; one organizer (born 1896, Laoag, Ilocos Norte; earlier a Hawaiian sugar-plantation laborer) served on the Chicago division's executive board and grievance committee for over 25 years; in 1938 the union won back a Filipino attendant whom managers had moved to the kitchen on the claim his English was poor; half of the most active Filipino BSCP members married Black women (per a 1982 study); some Filipino and Black-Filipino families shared homes in Bronzeville. **SOURCED** (Ari Antonio, Chicago History Museum, 18 Oct 2023; Don Villar, Chicago Federation of Labor, n.d.). [K4, K1, P9]
- **Dining-car crews:** the steward who ordered supplies and ran the crew was always white; chef, cooks (first to fourth) and waiters almost always Black; 1927 Southern Pacific pay: chef $150 a month, fourth cook or dishwasher $60, waiters under $80 and mostly tips; 12-14 hour days (about 5:30 a.m. to 9 p.m.) before the union, which won the eight-hour day, overtime and a pension; cooks owned their own knives and learned to sharpen them on a yard grindstone, and were skilled enough for fine restaurants that would not hire Black chefs; the Black-founded Dining Car Cooks and Waiters Union (mainly Southern Pacific) could not get an AFL charter and was held as a non-voting auxiliary of the bartenders' union; its office was on 7th Street, Oakland, on the same block as the porters' union; mostly Black yard crews in West Oakland cleaned, oiled, iced and coaled the cars. **SOURCED** (Thomas C. Fleming, "Fourth Cook on a Railroad Diner" and "Joining the Union," *Columbus Free Press*, 24 Jun and 1 Jul 1998). [K14, K1, K7, K12, R (yard maintenance crews)]
- **Redcaps (station porters):** before about 1938 paid by tips alone; legally treated as trespassers with privileges rather than employees until they won employee status before the Interstate Commerce Commission, which brought minimum-wage coverage; then a 10-cent-per-bag fee with a guaranteed $2.40 a day (1940); 1947: $50.96 for a seven-day, eight-hour week plus tips; the union (from 1937, led by a Chicago redcap, renamed when it took in Pullman laundry workers) merged into the railway clerks in 1972; one Portland redcap served about 50 years and put six of seven children through college; Portland's redcaps later became airport skycaps. **SOURCED** (Maine AFL-CIO 2023). In Ogden, Utah, the last four redcaps (combined 155 years' service) were honored by the NAACP in 1970 as passenger trains fell from 25 a day (1940s) to three a week. **SOURCED** (Weber State University exhibit, "Last of the Ogden Redcaps, 1970"). In St. Paul, redcaps also cut grass, shoveled snow and ran elevators at Union Depot (over 200 trains a day at peak); workers typically spent five days on a train and five at home; tips of 5-10 cents a bag. **SOURCED** (MPR News, 9 Mar 2023). [K14, K1, K7]
- **Maids:** one maid per train; childcare, care of the sick, helping women bathe and dress, manicures; unpaid for delays or illness; sometimes harassed by male passengers. **SOURCED** (Searchable Museum). (A maid's phrase for early unassigned service is in the slang file.) [K7]
- **Student and seasonal porters:** college men took porter jobs to pay for school (a 1965 Nebraska student); the company hired 3,000-4,000 extra men each summer (1924 report) **UNVERIFIED** (garbled newspaper scan; page fetched empty). [K7]
- **Canadian porters' families:** recruits came from the Caribbean (St. Kitts, Barbados, St. Croix, Jamaica) and from Black families who left Oklahoma and Texas; runs kept men away 15-20 days a month (a Vancouver run: ten days out, turnaround on the eleventh, about four days' layover); some porters were university-educated men shut out of other work in the Depression; Vancouver's BSCP division signed up 96% of the city's CPR porters (certified 1944, first contract 1945); the Ladies' Auxiliary collected dues, ran dances, banquets, teas (a Mother's Day tea; a spring tea), annual picnics and porters' balls (Winnipeg's drew guests from Minneapolis and Iowa), and gave its first scholarship in 1944; wives raised children largely alone and held family celebrations days late when husbands came home. **SOURCED** (Library and Archives Canada, *Porter Talk* podcast, episode 5, 12 Jun 2025). [K4, K10, K2, P4]
- **U.S. women's councils:** porters' wives and maids first organized Women's Economic Councils to raise union money (the Chicago leader elected 1931), since the company had spies in the neighborhood; bake sales, card games and dances kept families going in the Depression. **SOURCED** (Ivan Phifer, *Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder*, 2 Jun 2017). (The same article's claim that the auxiliary "organized the National Council of Negro Women" is recorded as the article's claim only.) [K1, K10]
- **Speech (K11):** English; Spanish-speaking (a Mexican-born cook-turned-porter in Boston); Ilocano/Tagalog among Filipino attendants; Caribbean English among Canadian porters; Montreal porters lived in a French-speaking city. No further note.

### C. Leisure and the ways porters spent time and money
- Porters' wages and tips bought homes and college for children; status high in the Black community and low among white passengers, in one former porter's words. **SOURCED** (CSM 2008). One former porter called it "the second best job I ever had." **SOURCED.** [K8]
- Boston's "Knights of the Rail" oral histories (Robert C. Hayden, for a Back Bay Station exhibit; held at UMass Boston, digitized): porters, cooks, waiters, cleaners and an all-woman crew; themes of long absences, strain on families, little formal training, discrimination from employers and passengers, and most saying they enjoyed the work. **SOURCED** (UMass Boston Archives blog, 8 Apr 2020). [K14, K7]

### D. Third places
- **Union United Church, Montreal:** porters and their wives, refused at other churches, met in July 1907 and founded it on 1 Sep 1907 (26 at the first service; treasury about $2), first meeting in a CPR-owned Welsh hall; one minister served 1923-1968; joined the United Church of Canada in 1925; the **Negro Community Center** began in its basement in February 1927 (averaging 1,000 attendees a month by 1931); the basement also held a debating and dramatic club, a theater guild and an art club; today about 50 nationalities. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Union United Church"). The UNIA's Liberty Hall in St. Antoine (from 1919) held weekly concerts, dances and socials. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet). [K10, K4, K13]
- **Hogan's Alley, Vancouver (chased from Round 1):** an alley in Strathcona near the Great Northern terminus; about 300 Black residents by 1914; a Pullman Porters' Club on Main Street; chicken restaurants that doubled as speakeasies, including one (1948-1980) visited by Louis Armstrong and Count Basie, where Jimi Hendrix's grandmother cooked; an AME chapel (1918-23, bought with matching funds from the U.S. head office; sold in the 1980s); under a 1920s-30s mayor, police regulated vice rather than suppressing it; rezoned industrial 1931; called a slum in 1950; much of it razed for the Georgia Viaduct (built 1967-71) before residents halted the freeway; a 2014 stamp; a planned Black cultural center after the viaducts' removal. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Hogan's Alley, Vancouver"). [K9, K10, K12]
- **Seventh Street, West Oakland:** West Coast headquarters of the BSCP (1716 7th St.); the dining-car union's office on the same block; the BSCP's first vice president owned a pool hall there to supplement his porter's pay **UNVERIFIED** (Berkeley archive snippet); a local producer recorded most of the street's blues musicians; a 1960s club; BART's elevated line down the street in the 1960s is named as a main cause of decline. **SOURCED** (UC Berkeley 7th Street archive, "Learn About 7th Street"). [K9, K10, K13]
- **Rondo, St. Paul:** railroad work was the main employer of Black St. Paul; while men were away women ran household and civic life (scouting, children's activities); I-94 (1956-68) demolished about 433 houses and closed or removed 300 businesses there, 72% Black-owned or occupied **UNVERIFIED** (MnDOT snippet); railworker exhibit "Twin Tracks" at the Minnesota Transportation Museum curated by a co-founder of Rondo Avenue, Inc., whose grandfather was a Great Northern porter and whose father was a Northern Pacific waiter and private-car steward. **SOURCED** (MPR 2023). [K9, K4]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **News carrying, more firmly sourced:** porters smuggled the *Defender* into the South, handing bundles to pickup contacts at risk to jobs and lives; copies were read aloud in churches, barbershops, homes and streets, up to four readers per copy; by 1920 the paper had 563 newsboys; a Mississippi county banned it as "German propaganda"; Pine Bluff, Ark., sued for an injunction naming 18 Black leaders including two ministers. **SOURCED** (Erick Johnson, *Chicago Crusader* via *Washington Informer*, 25 Apr 2016). The porters had an informal alliance (1914-1939) with five Black papers (*Afro-American*, *Defender*, *New York Age*, *Amsterdam News*, *Pittsburgh Courier*); a St. Paul paper was the official organ of an earlier railway porters' brotherhood; some porters were required to sing and dance for passengers. **SOURCED** (Jennifer Hasso, Jim Crow Museum, Ferris State, Aug 2021). [K5, K13]
- **Records and music (Round 1's open item):** the record-reselling account traced again only to Tye's book (porters bought Bessie Smith and Ma Rainey records in Chicago and New York and resold them South at about double the price). **SOURCED as Tye's claim** (KCCK radio page, c. Feb 2025). Porter quartets: a Chicago porters' quartet recorded for Paramount in the late 1920s, billed as the company's premier lineup, and several groups sang under the company name at once **UNVERIFIED** (Urban Faith/Local News Matters snippets); an instrumental group named for porters recorded for Gennett in April 1927 and a Memphis porters' chorus recorded gospel for Victor in 1930 **UNVERIFIED** (Discography of American Historical Recordings snippets; detail page 403). [K13]
- **E.D. Nixon (chased from Round 1):** porter from the early 1920s; joined the BSCP and followed Randolph; co-founded the Alabama Voters League in the 1940s; led a march of about 750 on the Montgomery County courthouse (1944); Montgomery NAACP president 1945, state president 1947; first Black candidate in the 20th century for the county Democratic executive committee (1954); posted Rosa Parks's bail with his house as collateral; MIA treasurer; his home was bombed 1 Feb 1956; left the MIA in 1957 over its split between educated leaders and working-class members and women; later a public-housing recreation director; died 25 Feb 1987. **SOURCED** (F. Erik Brooks, *Encyclopedia of Alabama*, updated 1 Mar 2024). Organizing the boycott "while on runs" **NOT FOUND**. [SM:man-versus-society, SM:power-rising-step-by-step]
- **Commemoration today:** the National A. Philip Randolph Pullman Porter Museum (founded 1995) holds an annual black-tie Gentle Warrior Awards gala each February (30th in Feb 2025, adding a "Descendants of Distinction" award), an annual Juneteenth celebration, a Labor Day program (6th Urban Renaissance event, 2022, with groundbreaking for an expansion) and an "Honoring the Brotherhood" event each 25 August (BSCP centennial 25 Aug 2025, with the National Park Service and the Historic Pullman Foundation). **SOURCED** (Citizen Newspaper Group, Jan 2025; CBS Chicago, 5 Sep 2022). Amtrak honored three former porters at Washington's Union Station in 2008. **SOURCED** (CSM 2008). [K2, K3]

**K6 rules of everyday behavior:** (1) Smoking-room rule: the porter may not ask passengers to leave so he can sleep; uppers assigned only by the conductor. **SOURCED** (Tye 2004). (2) Shoes: only black and tan, one pair at a time; no toilet flushing near stations; contagious passengers barred. **SOURCED.** (3) A rule against weapons and against carrying liquor or beer to sell to passengers, and against "habits of dissipation" off duty. **UNVERIFIED** (secondhand transcription on a reading-group page, undated). (4) Company tactics against the union: spies in neighborhoods, wives' tea parties as cover for meetings. **SOURCED** (AP 1997; Phifer 2017). (5) Inns legally refusing Black guests (Maine until 1971). **SOURCED.** (6) Redcaps' legal status as non-employees until the ICC ruling. **SOURCED.**

## 4 · #37 New Orleans and Congo Square

**What Round 1 already holds (not repeated):** the Sunday gatherings and their regulation (1724, 1786, 1817), the waves of arrivals, benevolent societies and the origin of second lines, the 2006-07 permit-fee fight, Mardi Gras Indians and Baby Dolls in outline, Storyville, the NPS neighborhood music map, the drum circle's revival, Jazz Fest's first year, the Claiborne/I-10 loss, and K6 items (street-music curfew, open containers).
**Round 2 focus:** the neighborhoods and their working settings (Tremé, the riverfront, Central City, New Orleans East), the city's distinct communities (dockworkers, Sicilians, Hondurans, Vietnamese, school bandsmen, masking Indians), how culture is paid for and governed today, and the city's annual cycle of rites. ### A. Other settings
- **Armstrong Park and the clearance of Tremé (closing Round 1's NOT FOUND, with conflicting figures):** a square block was condemned in the 1920s for the Municipal Auditorium, which excluded Black residents until 1953 and kept segregated seating about 11 years more; 1949/1954 Housing Act clearance razed more homes; in the early 1960s a cultural-center project and the elevated Claiborne expressway displaced residents (about 500 live oaks lost); the city renamed the 30-acre site for Louis Armstrong in 1971; since the National Park Service took over in 1999 the park was "under lock and key" most of the time, and on one visit the author counted six visitors. **SOURCED** (Lawrence N. Powell, *Southern Spaces*, 17 Jan 2012). Nine of 14 blocks cleared; displacement given as 178 families, "more than 400 families," or "more than 1,900 people" by different sources. **UNVERIFIED (conflicting snippets)**. [K9, P5]
- **The riverfront as a workplace:** before containers, gangs of about 20 men unloaded breakbulk cargo (sacks, barrels, bales; cotton, rubber, grain, bananas; 250-lb sacks) day and night, hired and fired by the job at shipping-line offices at the foot of Canal Street; over 150 shipping companies at midcentury; dockworkers lived in shotgun doubles in the first blocks from the river; seamen's bars and corner joints ran around the clock; in 1935 the ILA set up a white local (1418) and a Black local (numbered 1919 in the page as fetched; the number was not checked against a second source) splitting work roughly equally for most of the century; the Black local's Claiborne Avenue hall hosted Martin Luther King Jr. in 1961; the locals merged by 1980; over 8,000 longshoremen before containerization, under 500 now; bananas left for Gulfport in the 1970s. **SOURCED** (Justin A. Nystrom, "The Vanished World of the New Orleans Longshoreman," *Southern Spaces*, 5 Mar 2014). The 1892 general strike brought 43 unions (about 27,000 members) together across race and skill. **UNVERIFIED** (University of Chicago thesis snippet). [K7, K14, K1, K10, P8]
- **Musicians' housing:** Musicians' Village (Upper Ninth Ward; 8.2-acre former school site; 81 houses, five duplexes for elders, a pocket park and a music center opened Aug 2011) was built with Habitat for Humanity for musicians who lost homes in Katrina. In December 2025 the council said it was "blindsided" when Habitat sold several units without affordability protection; tenants, many elderly musicians on fixed incomes, faced about triple their rent; the council had in 2024 given $2 million to help over 120 Habitat homeowners facing insurance increases. **SOURCED** (New Orleans City Council release, 11 Dec 2025). [K9, K8, K1]
- **Short-term rentals:** from 1 Dec 2019 hosts had to live full-time at properties they rent; a councilmember said Tremé had lost "upwards of 20%" of its Black population since the platforms arrived; reassessments doubled or tripled some home values. **SOURCED** (Tracey Lindeman, *Fortune*, 30 Nov 2019). [K9, K8]
- **Schools and bands:** high-school marching bands carry the Carnival parades (one school band scheduled for six parades in 2023, 90 marchers, about 50 freshmen); after Katrina the schools became a decentralized charter network with no district music supervisor, long bus trips limit after-school band, and feeder programs shrank; bands are a route to college scholarships. **SOURCED** (Nick Weldon, HNOC *First Draft*, 2 Feb 2023). [K14, K13, P4]

### B. Subcultures
- **Mardi Gras Indians, the bar as practice hall:** in Central City, the H&R Bar (2425 Dryades; sign "Established 1966") held Sunday-evening drumming and chanting sessions for months before Mardi Gras; the corner of Second and Dryades became a gathering place for nearly all Uptown tribes; the bar burned in 2001 and a neighboring bar remains the center of Uptown Indian activity. **SOURCED** (*A Closer Walk*, "H&R Bar"). A chief (1922-2005; a lather by trade, building frames for plaster) masked from the 1920s; a 1987 NEA Heritage Fellow; suits of hand-sewn beads, plumes and rhinestones cost hundreds to thousands of dollars; his wife helped make them but never masked. **SOURCED** (NEA). After police clashed with Indians on St. Joseph's night (about 2005), a special council session was held where that chief spoke on police treatment and collapsed and died (27 Jun 2005); afterward the police included Indians in academy training; a youth tribe uses social media and crowdfunding; camera crowds now follow parades in Central City. **SOURCED** (Eve Troeh, WWNO via KUNC, 19 Mar 2015). [K2, K14, K6, K9, SM:competence-under-pressure]
- **Social aid and pleasure clubs as organizations:** clubs need two years of street parades before appearing at Jazz Fest; Jazz Fest fees help offset parade costs; a fund created in 1994 helps pay police details for anniversary parades; clubs have ladies', youth and kids' divisions; the oldest Black parading organization still active first paraded at Jazz Fest in 1975. **SOURCED** (Judy Cooper, HNOC *First Draft*, 5 May 2022). Parades run every Sunday September-May, often on a club's anniversary, stop at members' homes and barrooms, last about four hours; dues and fundraisers pay for permits, bands and outfits; one club organizes traditional funerals and holds a parade requiring uniforms and traditional music. **SOURCED** (Matt Sakakeeny, 64 Parishes, 2017). Season opened 24 Aug 2025; about 45-58 clubs **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K1, K2, K8, K10]
- **Sicilians:** after the Civil War, mostly male Sicilian farm workers came for plantation work and later sent for families; St. Joseph altars began as private home altars, open to those who knew the sign of a palm frond over the door, then were advertised in newspaper classifieds; three types (home, commercial such as bakeries, and large church or institutional altars); built in three tiers; meatless Lenten foods; guests leave with a fava bean (kept in a wallet for money luck), cookies, a prayer card and bread (thrown into storms to calm them); altars before 19 March. **SOURCED** (64 Parishes, "St. Joseph Altars," 14 Jul 2024). Nearly 60 parishes and schools build altars. **UNVERIFIED** (CNA snippet). [K2, K12, P6]
- **Hondurans:** linked to the city by the United Fruit banana trade; migrants arrived on refrigerated steamships from the 1910s for fruit-company jobs, Catholic schooling and escape from discrimination (including Garifuna and Black West Indians); a Lower Garden District community grew in the 1950s-60s with restaurants, nightclubs, a Spanish-language radio station and a church with Spanish masses (St. Theresa of Avila, 1401 Erato St.) and the feast of Honduras's patron saint; the largest U.S. Honduran population in 1962; moved to Kenner, Gretna and Terrytown from the 1970s; largest Hispanic group until Mexican arrivals after Katrina. **SOURCED** (Emily Perkins, HNOC *First Draft*, 12 Sep 2023). Honduran food trucks near a home-improvement store fed day laborers after Katrina. **UNVERIFIED** (NPR snippet). (Self-names and a neighborhood nickname are in the slang file.) [K4, K12, P9]
- **Vietnamese:** from 1975, Catholic Charities settled refugees in New Orleans East (half of the first 200 families in the Versailles apartments) and the West Bank; over 14,000 in the metro (2012); Mary Queen of Vietnam church at the center; backyard gardens (mangoes, mint, taro, squash, bananas); a Tet festival; a Blessing of the Fleet for Vietnamese shrimpers in Plaquemines each May; a youth organization runs a low-power FM station. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Vietnamese in New Orleans"). [K4, K12, K2]
- **Speech (K11):** English; Spanish (Honduran, Cuban, Mexican communities); Vietnamese in New Orleans East; Italian/Sicilian heritage. No further note.

### C. Leisure industries
- **Drinking hours as a leisure institution:** Louisiana sets no last call and New Orleans none in normal times; bars close at varying hours; drinks can be taken out in plastic cups; morning drinking draws no judgment. **SOURCED** (Aislyn Greene, *AFAR*, updated 23 Jan 2024). During COVID the city capped sales at 11 p.m., then 6 a.m.-1 a.m. from 9 Apr 2021 (entertainment district bounded by the river, Esplanade, Rampart and Common). **SOURCED** (City of New Orleans release, 9 Apr 2021). [K6, K10]
- **Outdoor music as a regulated product:** a pre-pandemic interpretation of the zoning ordinance barred most businesses from outdoor live music without a special-event permit; the pandemic administration lifted restrictions and created an extended permit; a planning study recommended permanent change, but a councilmember's office declined to proceed (2021); busking is protected expression within the noise ordinance; porch concerts were briefly charged a $100 permit; porch concerts in the 7th Ward and Gentilly are said to have fed gentrification while Black residents face complaints for their own gatherings. **SOURCED as MaCCNO's account** (MaCCNO column, *ANTIGRAVITY*, Jul 2021). [K6, K9, K13]
- **Who pays for culture:** the Jazz & Heritage Foundation has put Jazz Fest proceeds into community grants since 1979; Dec 2025 round $2 million to over 600 recipients ($1,000-$6,500); it also owns or runs the community radio station, an archive, a heritage center, a music school, and smaller festivals (blues and barbecue, Creole gumbo, Cajun-zydeco, Congo Square rhythms). **SOURCED** (jazzandheritage.org, 2 Dec 2025). [K8, K3]

### D. Third places
- **Dooky Chase's** (2301 Orleans Ave.): began 1939 as a sandwich shop and lottery outlet on Claiborne; moved 1941; from 1946 the young couple who took it over made it an upscale restaurant for the Black community; met labor leaders in the 1955 Godchaux sugar strike; planning sessions for lunch-counter protesters; a secret meeting place for King and the Freedom Riders despite ordinances against most interracial assembly; bombed May 1965 (no injuries); holds an art collection of African American artists. **SOURCED** (*New Orleans Historical*, 5 Apr 2017). [K10, K12, K6]
- **St. Augustine Church (Tremé):** founded 1841 by free people of color on former plantation land, dedicated 9 Oct 1842; free people of color bought more pews than white parishioners and bought the side-aisle pews for enslaved people; a Tomb of the Unknown Slave on the grounds; parishioners included Homer Plessy and Sidney Bechet; after Katrina the archdiocese moved to close it and parishioners barricaded themselves in the rectory for about two weeks (2006); off probation March 2009; a weekly Gospel Jazz Mass; placed in Chapter 11 in Nov 2025 under the archdiocese's abuse settlement. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "St. Augustine Church (New Orleans)"). (The historians' name for the pew contest is in the slang file.) [K10, K9, K4]
- **The Musicians' Clinic and St. Anna's:** the clinic (conceived 1995, opened 1998 with LSU Health Sciences Center) spawned a foundation after Katrina with a gig fund, an emergency fund and cancer screenings; with St. Anna's Episcopal Church in Tremé it runs a weekly jam session with a free meal and a "resource hall" (acupuncture, legal advice, housing help, screenings); moved in 2023 to Johnson and Gravier streets. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "New Orleans Musicians' Clinic"). Opened 1 May 1998; $10 minimum sliding fee **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K1, K14, K10]
- **Congo Square drum circle today:** sponsored weekly on Sundays by the Congo Square Preservation Society; the series dated to 1988 (another source 1984); the society got the square on the National Register (1993), placed the marker (1997), and runs school field trips with drum and dance workshops (2013). **SOURCED** (Tulane *Music Rising*, CSPS entry). Afternoon hours vary by source (2-4, 3-5, 3-6 p.m.). **UNVERIFIED.** [K2, K10]

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Funerals with music (recorded as fact only, per the mortuary caution):** procession from service to cemetery with dirges, then up-tempo music once the body is laid to rest; traditionally for prominent Black men, often musicians; benevolent societies arranged them and offered a band for an extra fee; recently extended to young people who died young, with fewer dirges and everyday clothes; staged parades for tourists since the 1970s. **SOURCED** (Sakakeeny, 64 Parishes). [K2, K1]
- **MaCCNO:** formed Sept 2012 at a trumpeter's club after long-time music bars were told they lacked live-entertainment permits; in January 2014 a second line led from outside City Hall into council chambers over a noise ordinance; meets Wednesday evenings in a restaurant back room; helped win "culture friendly" changes in the 2015 zoning rewrite. **SOURCED** (Eve Abrams, WWNO, 15 Apr 2014). [K1, K6, K9]
- **Domestic slave trade remembered:** after 1808 the city held the nation's largest domestic slave market; over 50 sale sites marked on an 1849 map (business district, boats, French Quarter courtyards, the St. Louis Hotel rotunda, now a hotel without a marker); a prime field hand cost about $1,500 in the 1850s; the trade was tied to cotton, banking, insurance and clothing; post-war newspaper ads sought separated family members into the early 1900s; HNOC exhibition 17 Mar-18 Jul 2015. **SOURCED** (Eve Abrams, WWNO, 3 Jun 2015). ### A. Other settings
- **The six-month cycle and the shared apartment (Japan, before March 2005):** a "promotion" agency recruits women, gives pre-departure singing and dancing lessons (usually not Japanese), advances the air ticket and first month's pay, and places them in clubs across Japan on six-month contracts; the other five months' salary is paid only at the end, minus airfare and document costs; a weekly cash allowance of about ¥3,500-5,000 and free rice; housing in one apartment room shared by several, sometimes a dozen, women; work from 6-7 p.m. to 1-2 a.m. with two to four days off a month, combining hostessing, singing, dancing, waitressing and after-hours dishwashing and cleaning; 1990s salaries US$250 to over $1,000 a month plus tips and a 10% share of drinks and food ordered by their customers (¥1,000-5,000 a day); then about six months at home preparing the next visa and contract, often with required lessons; the ideal was six months in Japan, six at home, repeated.
