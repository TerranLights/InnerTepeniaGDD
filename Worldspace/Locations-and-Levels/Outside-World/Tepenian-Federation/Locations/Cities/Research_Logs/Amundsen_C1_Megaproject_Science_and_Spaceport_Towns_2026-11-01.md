# SECTION 1 — Boulder City NV, Panama Canal Zone towns, Suez Canal towns

## 1A. Boulder City, Nevada (Hoover Dam)

**Why built.** SOURCED (bouldercity.com/a-planned-community-boulder-city/): urban architect Saco Rienk DeBoer designed it as a company town for Hoover Dam workers; plan submitted 1930, scaled down after cost estimates; layout symmetrical and formal with a large park and the Bureau of Reclamation building on the hilltop. "The Bureau of Reclamation estimated that about 5,000 men would need housing in the residential areas." Originally planned for single male employees only. SOURCED (PBS American Experience, hoover-building-boulder-city): built on a ridge above Hemenway Wash "as a government reservation under the Reclamation Service." SOURCED (Review-Journal "Built by the dam, for the dam", via search summary): the arrangement kept the Bureau in control of worker housing and "prevent Nevada politicians from exercising any authority over the area"; the town was built in 15 months (Boulder City Review, bouldercityreview.com/news/boulder-city-owes-its-existence-to-dam/).
- Predecessor: SOURCED (PBS) workers and families first lived at "Ragtown", "There was nothing green around here." SOURCED (Wikipedia) tent camp.
- Wikipedia states: "built in 1931 by the Bureau of Reclamation and Six Companies, Inc. as housing for workers"; "sole reason for existence was the need to house workers contracted to build the Hoover Dam."

**Population over time.**
- Dam peak workforce: 5,251 (SOURCED via search summaries; sources differ on month, June/July/September 1934; a Colorado state document says 5,218; ~21,000 men worked on the dam in total). The 5,000 figure for Boulder City is a housing-need estimate or capacity, NOT a census count (SOURCED search summary: "about 5,000 men in 1,050 wooden structures").
- Census (SOURCED, Wikipedia): 1950 3,903; 1960 4,059; 1970 5,223; 1980 9,590; 1990 12,567; 2000 14,966; 2010 15,023; 2020 14,885. Census.gov history page: 1940 Boulder City counted "as part of Clark County, Nevada" (Clark County 16,414 in 1940); 2024 estimate 15,029.
- NOT FOUND: Boulder City census figure for 1930 and any 1935 peak town population (three searches; the census.gov history page and PBS both silent).
- Post-dam: SOURCED (PBS incorporation page) "The Bureau of Reclamation planned to keep only a small operations crew and tear down the rest of the town. The Six Companies expected to leave for another project." SOURCED (Boulder City Review) the town was slated for demolition but "many of the people who came to work on the dam decided to stay"; many took jobs with Reclamation or other federal agencies, state agencies, or companies tied to hydropower; "Six Companies sold homes to residents."

**Who lived there and on what terms.** SOURCED (PBS): dormitories for single men; one-, two-, three-room cottages for families. African American, Native American and Hispanic dam workers were barred from living inside the gates. Visitors by permit only (bouldercity.com); gatehouse 1932 (bouldercity.com); SOURCED (Review-Journal via search) "Visitors were allowed to enter by permit, and by 1932, had to pass through a gatehouse." SOURCED (Boulder City Review): a gated fence and checkpoint were erected at Railroad Pass after the 1931 strike to "prevent union organizers from entering the federal reservation" (note PBS says a security gate was built in 1931 during labor unrest; the two sources agree on the cause and differ slightly on the year of the gatehouse: 1931 vs 1932, see Unresolved).

**Housing and services (company side).** SOURCED (PBS): mess hall "served 6,000 meals a day"; "$1.50 a day, a man received three meals"; weekly deliveries "12 tons of fruit and vegetables, five tons of meat, and two and a half tons of eggs." SOURCED (search summary of Weber State/UNLV captions): River Camp dormitories for ~480 single men; eight similar dormitories each housing 172 men (UNVERIFIED as to whose dorms: captions mix Six Companies and government). Hospital: SOURCED (PBS) opened 1932, underwritten by Six Companies, served only dam workers; spouses and children had no provided care. bouldercity.com says no hospital was planned and the nearest was Las Vegas, 33 miles away (CONFLICT, see Unresolved). Library and a church (PBS). Boulder Theater built 1931 (bouldercity.com). Train station and a graded road to Las Vegas (Boulder City Review).

**Schools.** SOURCED (PBS): "The town had no formal education system. The government provided no school funds, and Six Companies' donation covered only makeshift schoolhouses." SOURCED (Wikipedia): makeshift schoolrooms "until the city won the right for state-funded schools" on the federal reservation.

**Rules and governance.** SOURCED (PBS): Sims Ely, "a 69-year-old former newspaper editor and Justice Department employee," held legal, moral and judicial authority; "iron-fisted gentleman"; resident Mary Ann Merrill "a hard man." Wikipedia: Ely screened business owners for character and financial viability (title "city manager" per Wikipedia). SOURCED (PBS; bouldercity.com): gambling, drinking and prostitution banned. Enforcement: "They would search the cars entering the city, and any liquor found was confiscated" (Review-Journal via search). Bootlegging: SOURCED (bouldercity.com) illegal sales took place in what is now Bootleg Canyon; Boulder City Magazine (via search) notes a failed bootlegging raid on March 24, 1931. Prohibition in the nation ended 1933 but liquor remained barred on government land (Las Vegas Sun, via search).
- **Alcohol dates.** Ban from the town's start in 1931 (Wikipedia gives no explicit start date; search-summaries date it to the founding). SOURCED (Wikipedia; Review-Journal; Boulder City Review: "The sale of liquor was finally legalized in Boulder City in 1969.") End: 1969. NOT FOUND: the vote tally or the mechanism in 1969 (two searches; sources died at the sources, paywall/403).
- **Gambling dates.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): "Gambling has been prohibited since the city's outset"; the charter approved by residents at incorporation prohibited it, and the provision still exists. Never legalized within city limits. Hoover Dam Lodge has a Boulder City mailing address but is outside city limits (Wikipedia). CONFLICT: the search summary put it on a parcel inside Lake Mead NRA; Wikipedia says only "outside city limits."
- **Other controls.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): growth limited to 120 residential building permits per year; hotels capped at 35 rooms. Boulder City Review: the growth-limit ordinance dated 1979.

**Incorporation / end of federal control.** SOURCED (PBS incorporation page): for over a decade after the dam's completion, residents argued over incorporation; 1946 first bill (Rep. Berkeley Bunker), died; 1949 Reclamation hired Henry Reining (USC); June 1950 report proposed gradual release; July 1951 Interior Secretary Oscar Chapman executive order released Boulder City from the Boulder Canyon Project; "The city had no tax base and no democratic institutions, and had never held an election." 1951 vote created a citizens advisory committee, most elected members opposed separation. 1953 and 1955 Senate hearings. 1958: anti-incorporation side sent one man, Bruce Eaton, who pushed Congress to keep bans on gambling, prostitution and liquor; September 2, 1958 Eisenhower signed the Boulder City Bill; January 1960 formal incorporation ceremonies. (Wikipedia says control relinquished 1959; Britannica via search says 1958 ceded; one source says 1960 — CONFLICT, resolved in practice as law 1958, local control 1959, ceremonies January 1960.) NOTE: the PBS page says Eaton lobbied to keep the bans on gambling, prostitution and liquor and "could not stop the bill's final passage"; whether his amendment passed is not stated (UNVERIFIED). Liquor was legalized 1969.

**Current status.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): 2020 population 14,885; median age 54.3; median household income $69,746; council-manager government. Four public schools (Boulder City High, Garrett Junior High, King Elementary, Mitchell Elementary) in Clark County School District.

**What failed.** (a) No school provision and no family health care for years (PBS). (b) Racial exclusion from the town; non-white workers lived outside the gates (PBS). (c) The plan to demolish the town after the dam was reversed by residents staying. (d) Alcohol ban held about 38 years (1931 to 1969).
**Neighbors followed:** Henderson NV (Basic Magnesium) — NOT yet followed; see Section 5 neighbors if time. Ragtown and Williamsville (below Lake Mead) noted: SOURCED (historiclasvegasproject.com via search) Williamsville housed workers and families and was submerged by Lake Mead after 1935.

## 1B. Panama Canal Zone towns

**Governance.** SOURCED (Wikipedia "Panama Canal Zone"): Isthmian Canal Commission under 1902 and 1904 Acts; Executive Order 1885 effective April 1, 1914 placed it under the Secretary of War as "The Panama Canal"; from July 1, 1951 the Canal Zone Government governed and the Panama Canal Company operated the canal until 1979; the governor was also company president (usually an Army Corps of Engineers general); from 1952 the company had to break even. Own police force, courts (US District Court for the Canal Zone 1914 to 1982). Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty November 18, 1903; Torrijos–Carter treaties took effect October 1, 1979; canal handed over December 31, 1999.

**Population over time.** SOURCED (census.gov history stories, August 2024): 1904 9,742; 1912 62,810 (counted Commission employees and laborers, railroad employees and military in Panama, inside and outside the Zone); 1914 45,296 (police census); 1916 31,048; 1920 22,858; 1930 39,467; 1940 51,827 ("Nearly 46 percent of inhabitants lived on a military base"); 1950 52,300 (preliminary); 1970 44,198. NOT FOUND: a verified 1960 figure (a garbled 1970 table suggests ~42,122 — UNVERIFIED). The 1950 largest towns: Silver City 5,729; La Boca 4,246; Balboa 4,117 (census.gov). Construction labor: ~42,000 workers hired 1904 to 1915, mostly from Barbados; ~8,300 from Spain 1906 to 1908; fewer than 2,000 Italians (Linda Hall Library exhibit).

**Who lived there and on what terms.** SOURCED (Wikipedia; Linda Hall): gold roll (mostly white Americans) and silver roll (Caribbean, Spanish, Italian, others) — "The system was a color line" with separate privileges, eating facilities and building entrances. Gold roll housing was free (Linda Hall; Wikipedia: houses assigned mainly by seniority; residents did not own their homes; one square foot of housing per dollar of monthly salary, doubled if married). Single white men: private ~220 sq ft rooms in two-story dormitories; silver families ~240 sq ft; single silver workers in barracks on cots. In November 1906 Chief Engineer John Stevens ordered most Black workers moved from the gold roll to the silver roll; February 8, 1908 no more non-Americans on the gold roll (search summary of one source, UNVERIFIED on the 1908 date). Pay in gold or silver coin until 1918.

**Housing.** Early: four-unit two-story apartment structures; bachelors in hotel-like buildings (Wikipedia). Balboa: North American suburban style, frame houses on paved streets (panamapassage.com, lower-quality source: UNVERIFIED). Silver-roll towns Rainbow City, Silver City, Paraiso, Margarita built denser (panamapassage; also mentioned in Wikipedia town list).

**Services.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): Canal Zone Commissary established after Stevens ordered food at cost in 1905; by 1913 it had 22 general stores, 7 cigar stores, 22 hostels, 2 hotels, a mail-order division. Panama restricted the commissary to military personnel, canal employees and dependents. Two high schools (Balboa, Cristobal); Canal Zone Junior College from 1933 (night classes; over a thousand students and dormitories in the 1960s). American school system transferred to DoDDS in 1972 (panamapassage, UNVERIFIED). Clubs: SOURCED (Linda Hall) YMCA clubhouse at Culebra with bowling alley, billiards, gymnasium, library, bandstand, $10 a year; Masons and Knights of Columbus; "each town had women's clubs, art and literary societies, and music and drama clubs." Rules: most arrests were intoxication or disorderly conduct; idle workers could be arrested for vagrancy; unpaid fines could mean chained penitentiary labor on roads (Linda Hall).

**Individual towns.**
- Gamboa (SOURCED Wikipedia, whose history section is flagged uncited): built 1911 for about 700 silver roll workers; 173 residents by 1914; 251 in 1933 (10 Americans); the Dredging Division moved there in 1936, population 1,419 within a year, peak 3,853 in 1942. Commissary, post office, school, five churches, railroad station, fire station, gas station; residents built a civic center (USO, later Civic Council); golf club opened January 1, 1939 (9 holes); remote and "less desirable than other Canal Zone towns", newcomers housed there until eligible elsewhere; non-US non-white population lived in "Santa Cruz." After 1979: school enrollment 115 (1979), 53 (1987-88), closed June 1988; commissary and theater boarded up through the 1980s; Panama Railroad's demise in the late 1980s cut the town off; today "inhabited, but as a shadow of its former self"; ecotourism.
- Gatun (czbrats.com): old Gatun village (flooded by Gatun Lake) had a church, school, about a dozen shops, 90+ houses, "served a population of 600"; buildings moved to the new site. panamapassage: lock town with commissaries, schools, YMCA (UNVERIFIED).
- Cristobal: main Caribbean port town and Panama Railroad terminal; Cristobal High School passed to Panama under the 1955 treaty (czbrats); high school reopened at Coco Solo in 1959 (czbrats); Colon Hospital closed and was torn down. Merged into Colon's urban grid after 1979 (panamapassage, UNVERIFIED).
- Balboa: named in 1909; administrative seat; 4,117 in 1950 (census.gov).
- Margarita: third-locks town, approved January 1940, first residents December 1940 (czbrats).
- Red Tank abandoned and overgrown around 1950; Rosseau torn down after about 20 years; Brazos Heights privately owned (Wikipedia).
- Construction-era railway towns 1908 (czbrats): Gorgona 2,750 (1,065 whites, 1,646 Blacks, 39 Chinese), Las Cascadas 2,425 (once above 5,000), Bas Obispo 1,744, Matachin 2,042, Frijoles 784.
- Gorgas Hospital in Ancon now houses Panama's Ministry of Health and the oncology institute (Wikipedia).

**After 1979.** Treaty took effect October 1, 1979; 35 enclaves emerged on that date, nine more later (Wikipedia). Many commissaries, schools, clubs closed or transferred (panamapassage). 
**What failed:** the color line itself; Gamboa's dependence on rail and dredging; towns abandoned (Red Tank).

## 1C. Suez Canal Company towns (Ismailia, Port Said, Port Fouad)

**Port Said (SOURCED, Wikipedia):** founded April 25, 1859 by Said of Egypt, de Lesseps swung the first pickaxe; first 150 laborers in tents 1859; ~2,000 in 1860; 10,000 permanent in 1869 when the canal opened; 1907 about 50,000 including 11,000 Europeans; early Europeans lived in "wooden bungalows imported from northern Europe"; everything including fresh water imported until the Sweet Water Canal, "Giant water storage containers were erected"; Port Fouad founded December 1926 by the company, planned with "300 houses for its laborers and functionaries" in a French style; Company's Port Said office built 1893. Nasser nationalized the company on July 26, 1956. 2024 population 790,000 (infobox) vs 794,720 (history section): internal Wikipedia conflict.
**Ismailia (SOURCED):** founded 1863 on Lake Timsah (Wikipedia, "by Khedive Ismail"; the OpenEdition CNRS chapter says built "in 1862 to oversee canal works and house the administration"; CONFLICT 1862 vs 1863). Company headquarters, later Suez Canal Authority. Search summary: a city of three quarters, a European quarter used essentially by company employees, an Arab quarter for workers from all over Egypt (the third quarter name not captured). Designed by Company architects with separation between European and local districts (OpenEdition CNRS chapter). The plural society existed until 1956. Pre-war (1967) populations: Suez ~250,000, Port Said 280,000, Ismailia 143,000 (Aramco World, May 1977). Evacuation: about a million civilians evacuated; by 1971 only ~5,000 remained in Suez; 1973 war left a fifth of Ismailia and of Port Said destroyed or damaged beyond repair; 85 percent of Suez; return: 850,000 to 900,000 of the million returned; Ismailia 220,000 in 1977, Port Said 360,000 in 1977 (Aramco World). 2024: Ismailia 380,000 (Wikipedia). Suez Canal Authority holdings (Wikipedia, citation needed): 12,000 housing units, a hospital in Ismailia, emergency hospitals at both ends, four schools (UNVERIFIED, concerns post-1956 authority). Evacuation dates: 1968-1970 (CNRS) vs 1967-1969 peak migration (migration study via search) vs 1967 to 1975 canal closure (Wikipedia) — see Unresolved.
**NOT FOUND:** Suez Canal Company 1950s headcount, its pilot numbers, its Ismailia hospital/school/club inventory (four searches; Piquet PDF returned 404, UN letter only a dispute claim; died at the sources).

# SECTION 2 — Manhattan Project towns (Los Alamos, Oak Ridge, Hanford/Richland)

## 2A. Oak Ridge, Tennessee
**Why built; scale.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): government bought nearly 60,000 acres in 1942 for a planned city; grew from about 3,000 to 3,750 in 1942 to about 75,000 by 1945 (ORNL blog also: "At its peak, Oak Ridge was home to 75,000 men, women and children"). Employment peaked at over 80,000 in June 1945 (NPS via search summary). Name chosen partly because the rural-sounding "Oak Ridge" "held outside curiosity to a minimum" (Wikipedia). Three plants: X-10 (later ORNL), Y-12, K-25 (ORNL blog).
**Secrecy, fences, passes.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): "The Secret City"/"The City Behind a Fence"; guard towers and fence with seven gates; all workers wore badges; no airport built for security. SOURCED (ORNL blog "When Oak Ridge was gated and muddy"): security fencing, a curfew, armed guards at each entrance, its own military intelligence force. Residents often did not know what was being produced (Kiernan book summary, via search; WBIR oral history). NOT FOUND: the badge/pass system detail (two searches; the OSTI pages fail with an SSL error in the fetch tool, which is a tool dead end, not a source gap).
**Housing.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): trailers, barracks, "hutments" (prefabricated five-person huts heated by a central coal-fired furnace); Gamble Valley up to 4,000 trailer spaces; Happy Valley from about 5,000 to about 15,000 people; permanent housing = prefabricated modular homes, apartments, dormitories, many from cemesto panels. ORNL blog: "victory cottages" "bunked five workers around a coal stove," shared bathrooms. Teachtnhistory curriculum PDF (via search): housing allocated by a lettering system by worker status and family size.
**Managing company, quantities.** SOURCED (New York Times, August 20, 1945 "New York Concern Directs Oak Ridge," reproduced in a teachtnhistory.org curriculum PDF, text extracted): Turner Construction Company, asked in September 1943 by the Corps of Engineers "to relieve the armed forces of all burdens of operation and community relationships." Managed 12,000 homes, 10,000 apartments, police, fire, water supply and sewage systems, roads, elementary and secondary schools, stores and supermarkets, a 250-bed hospital and movie theaters. Roane-Anderson Company (a wholly owned Turner subsidiary) managed a farm with 3,000 cattle, a chicken ranch, an electric power station, the town's laundry, domestic help. "A million and a quarter meals a month were served in cafeterias seating 10,000 persons at a time." The ninth-largest bus system in the country, 2,400,000 miles a month, average 120,000 passengers a day. "10,000 employes of the construction company" ran the town, "twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week." DERIVED: 10,000 town-operating employees / 75,000 inhabitants = about 1 town-operations worker per 7.5 residents (company staff, including those serving contractor workers; the quoted article does not separate dependents).
**Services at the early-years level.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): ~300 miles of roads, ten schools, seven theaters, 17 restaurants and cafeterias, 13 supermarkets, a library with 9,400 books, a symphony orchestra, church services for 17 denominations. Recreation: SOURCED (NPS via search) Jackson Square and Grove Center community centers, community swimming pool (white residents only), sports list (badminton, baseball, basketball, bowling, handball, horseshoes, shuffleboard, soccer, softball, table tennis, touch football, volleyball). Wartime letter (Frances Carroll, July 16, 1945, in the same PDF): "never-ending carnival," felt it would be "rather depressing to live there permanently"; drinking water "heavily chlorinated"; no butter, dark bread, chocolate ice cream, but excellent steak; night shift "practically nothing to eat"; shifts rotating "7 days on days, a day off, seven days graveyard, day off, & 7 swing shift & then 80 hours off"; swimming at Big Ridge. Long lines for groceries and cigarettes (WBIR oral-history, via search). Mud: "the lack of sidewalks and constant construction" (NPS via search).
**Rationing.** NOT FOUND as an Oak Ridge-specific system (two searches). The letter shows food-supply gaps (butter, bread) consistent with national wartime rationing: UNVERIFIED link.
**Segregation and schools.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): segregated at the insistence of Southern Democrats in Congress; Black workers in hutments near Y-12; before 1954 elementary segregated, Scarboro Elementary; Scarboro High opened 1950; 1955 85 Black students entered Oak Ridge High and Robertsville Junior High; Scarboro Elementary closed 1967; pool and public accommodations integrated over several years.
**Governance transition.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): formally named in 1949 and shifted to AEC civilian control; incorporated 1959 with a city manager and council. SOURCED (ORNL blog): on March 19, 1949, at Elza Gate, a magnesium ribbon was electrified by the Graphite Reactor, opening the city to the public; the three plants' future was not secure until the AEC guaranteed their operation in 1949. Later census: 27,169 (1960), 28,319 (1970), 27,662 (1980), 27,310 (1990), 27,387 (2000), 29,330 (2010), 31,402 (2020) (Wikipedia). Atomic Energy Community Act of 1955 (LoC blog): local self-government, transfer of municipal functions and utilities to local government, orderly sale of community property; federal funds continued to help operate the communities, citing "fiscal problems peculiar to the communities by reason of their construction as national defense installations."
**What failed.** Housing never matched demand; segregation; "Dogpatch" mud; the post-war fear that the plants would close (secured 1949).

## 2B. Los Alamos, New Mexico
**Why built; growth.** SOURCED (EdWeek, "Los Alamos: the making of a community," 1996): War Department approved the site November 25, 1942; Army bought the Ranch School and about 54,000 surrounding acres, mostly Forest Service land. Population "roughly doubled every nine months until the war ended"; average resident 24 years old; 80 babies born in the first year with P.O. Box 1663 on birth certificates. Population figures: 1,500 in January 1943 and 3,500 by year's end (search summary, source unnamed); NPS: "Los Alamos grew to 6,000" by 1945; Vice (via search): over 8,000 at the war's end; a 1953 personal account gives 12,000 (all UNVERIFIED; see Unresolved). Army had planned for about 300 temporary residents (atomicarchive.com).
**Secrecy, passes.** SOURCED (Wikipedia; atomicarchive): closed city, "accessible from the outside world only through two gates"; newcomers reported to the Santa Fe office (Dorothy McKibbin); early passes were letters signed by J. Robert Oppenheimer, later security passes; mail went to a Santa Fe P.O. box and was censored; "Laboratory members were not allowed personal contact with relatives" and "not permitted to travel more than 100 miles"; "Outgoing mail was censored and long distance calls were monitored"; residents could not name fellow residents' occupations; barbed wire and armed guards.
**Housing.** SOURCED (atomicarchive): Ranch School buildings converted; masters' houses became residences for top administrators ("Bathtub Row" for the only houses with tubs instead of showers); "The four-family Sundt apartments and the McKee houses were built and occupied at a frantic pace"; "For more than 20 years the housing never quite managed to catch up with the demand." Overflow: Frijoles Canyon Lodge at Bandelier requisitioned from June 1943 to January 1946; some families in tents there as late as 1945 (NPS).
**Services.** SOURCED (atomicarchive): "There was never enough water"; one telephone line (Forest Service) when 1943 began and only three until 1945; dry cleaning sent to Santa Fe until a laundry concession in summer 1944; "A 12-grade school system with 16 teachers was established in 1943"; nursery school for working mothers; Commissary "groceries to Project residents at cost plus ten percent"; maid service of "Indian women from nearby pueblos" rationed by the number and ages of a woman's children and her hours; "The first resident dentist arrived in 1944 and a Project hospital was established the same year."
**Civic and social.** SOURCED (atomicarchive): "A town council was formed the same year [1943], its members elected by popular vote" to advise the community administration; "More than 30 recreational and cultural organizations were formed during the army years"; Los Alamos University founded 1945 (Fermi, Bethe among others) for lectures; home-grown concerts and theatrics, movies several times a week. University of California operated the lab from January 1, 1943 under a nonprofit contract with the Manhattan Engineer District.
**Schools.** SOURCED (EdWeek): Central School opened September 1943, curriculum by Walter W. Cook (University of Minnesota); "military officials were reportedly angered by the elaborate Central School building"; federal government ran the school until 1946 and funded the system entirely until 1949; 1950 to 1955 joined New Mexico public system, federal payments still about one-third of district budget; 1967 school land/buildings transferred to the school board; 1981 six-year contract with the Department of Energy.
**Post-war.** SOURCED (EdWeek) the security gates and checkpoints came down in 1957; (LoC blog) "front gate" opened to the public in February 1957; Los Alamos was not included in the 1955 Atomic Energy Community Act because it was still a security risk; Pub. L. 87-719 (September 1962) amended it to provide for sale of residential properties once classified; the author's own family bought half a duplex in April 1966 (house sales began about 1965 or 1966: CONFLICT inside the blog). EdWeek: the 1955 Act authorized federal payments "until they became self sufficient"; the 1962 amendment set payments for ten years fiscal 1967 to 1976, renewed 1986, due to expire fiscal 1996, extended by one year in 1996. Incorporation: Britannica (via search) says consolidated city-county government in 1969 (UNVERIFIED; one search). Census: 1970 11,310; 1980 11,039; 1990 11,455; 2000 11,909; 2010 12,019; 2020 13,179. LANL "approximately 10,500 employees," annual budget about $2.45 billion; about 40 percent of lab employees live in Los Alamos, the rest commute from Santa Fe, Española, Taos, Albuquerque (Wikipedia).
**What failed.** Water, phones, housing; the federal subsidy that had to be renewed repeatedly; commuting majority today.

## 2C. Hanford construction camp and Richland, Washington
**Scale.** SOURCED (NPS, about-hanford): the construction camp "housed 50,000 people at its peak"; the Corps and DuPont "recruited nearly 100,000 workers from 1943 to 1945." Search summaries: about 51,000 in June 1944 (archaeology report); another source 45,000 employed; 131 barracks for 24,892 men, 64 barracks for 4,357 women, 880 hutments, 3,639 trailer lots (UNVERIFIED, source unnamed in search snippet); camp built over the old Hanford townsite from early April 1943; closed 1947; demolished in part by Camp Columbia prisoners in 1945 (Wikipedia: Camp Columbia). About 10 percent of workers were women (clerical, food service, domestic); about 14 percent African American men, mostly low-skill construction; Black workers pushed to the east side of Pasco "lacking basic city services" (NPS). The camp itself was segregated with a separate area (76 trailer lots plus barracks) per a search summary (UNVERIFIED).
**Displacement.** SOURCED (NPS): in early 1943 about 1,500 residents of White Bluffs, Hanford, Richland and nearby communities were told the government had acquired their land under war powers; 30 to 90 days to leave; tribes that used the area barred from returning. (Wikipedia: Richland 300+ residents evicted March 6, 1943; HistoryLink says about 250 residents and 428,000 acres: CONFLICT on numbers.)
**Camp Columbia.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): federal prison camp near Horn Rapids; opened February 1, 1944, run by the Federal Bureau of Prisons until October 1947; 1,300 prisoners passed through, up to 290 at one time; up to 40 guards; no fences; prisoners processed more than 5,600 tons of fruit; shut down October 10, 1947; then housed Hanford rail and site workers until 1949; abandoned 1950, some Quonset huts to Richland.

# SECTION 3 — Science cities and science parks

## 3A. Tsukuba Science City (Japan)
**Why built.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): September 1963 national government ordered a science city around Mount Tsukuba; reasons "overcrowding in Tokyo" and excess applicants to elite universities; land purchases from 1966 by negotiated prices; facility construction from June 1969; by March 1980 the planned facilities were operating "to some degree." Government-owned land is an irregular strip so "walking is uncommon"; more than 100 parks joined by pedestrian and bike paths.
**Who and how many.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): by 2000, 60 national research institutes and two national universities in five zones, surrounded by more than 240 private research facilities; about 7,500 foreign students and researchers from up to 133 countries at any time; nearly half of Japan's public R&D budget spent there over decades. Glasgow paper (Tsukuba Science City as in-between place, 2016 interviews; eprints.gla.ac.uk/146659): today ~150 private labs vs over 200 earlier (Castells and Hall 1994); about a quarter of private firms that relocated have retrenched; of 1,781 foreign researchers 33.7% Chinese and 53.5% East Asian, UK and US 4.8%; foreign researchers "rarely blended into the Japanese community on a more informal/personal level."
**Population.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): 127,402 (1980); 168,466 (1990); 191,814 (2000); 214,590 (2010); 241,656 (2020); 256,526 (January 2024). Glasgow paper: 225,000 by 2015 "a scenario far from how it was conceived at the beginning"; ~20,000 people commute in daily.
**Early residents' complaints.** SOURCED (Wikipedia): families from Tokyo experienced "culture shock" from "dirt roads and open fields"; built around car travel, no train station; residents' complaints about uncollected garbage led first to a dump pit before collection service. SOURCED (Glasgow paper, summarizing another study): many people assigned to Tsukuba refused; the site was "wild dogs and mosquitoes" "far from being suitable for scientists and their upper-middle-class wives"; settlers complained the city would never be a real neighborhood. Critics called it a waste of taxpayer money, government control of academic research, and lacking traditional Japanese culture (Wikipedia). From 2006 onward the city worked to build more city life after critics called it "flat" (Wikipedia). Expo '85 drew about 20 million visitors (Wikipedia).
**Housing and the loss of it.** SOURCED (Glasgow paper): "Previously Tsukuba was self-sufficient as a result of research institutes providing housing for staff and the proximity of residence to work, but as this subsidized housing has been terminated... many employees in Tsukuba have moved out to its outskirts or along the TX line." National-officers' housing complexes built in the 1970s in the very center now "stand largely or entirely empty" awaiting disposal by the Ministry of Finance, "weeds grow among these boarded up houses and apartments." The built-up core is a garden-city/new-town form planned by the Urban Renaissance Agency but "much of it remains in the hands of individual central government ministries"; surrounding area regulated by Ibaraki Prefecture in low-density US-suburb form.
**Transit.** SOURCED: Tsukuba Express opened 2005, Akihabara in about 45 minutes (Wikipedia; Glasgow paper says "almost half of that taken by the Joban line and highway bus"); Tsukuba is the 20th and last stop; stops 17 to 19 were rice paddies when the interviewee arrived (Glasgow paper).
**What residents say is missing / what failed.** Glasgow paper: gaps in communication among institutes and groups remain; institutes are "effectively branches responsible to their 'head offices' (the Ministries) in Tokyo"; several institutes (AIST, NIMS) opened Tokyo branch offices. NOT FOUND: any spouse-specific data, a named "Tsukuba syndrome" or suicide statistic (my own guessed query found nothing: UNVERIFIED that any such phenomenon exists; do not cite). The Tsukuba Science City Network has run a living-conditions and workplace stress survey every five years since 1986 (search summary); the PDF of the 7th survey returned 404 (died at the source).

SOURCED (search summary of koreascience.kr papers and Wikipedia): planned 1967, master plan approved 1973, construction from 1974, renamed Daedeok Innopolis January 2005; government institutes 5 (1979) to 21 (2007), private institutes 3 to 43; the original plan envisaged a science town of 50,000 residents (snippet cut off, UNVERIFIED); "over 20 major research institutes and over 40 corporate research centers" and "more than 17,000 PhD-level researchers" (Wikipedia); one paper 22,000 researchers and employees (UNVERIFIED). People arrived from the west by the trainload, "but not everyone held out; the most resilient stayed."
- **Who came and how many (SOURCED, V. S. Nikiforov, *Irkutsk Historical-Economic Yearbook*, pp. 340 to 344, https://history.bgu.ru/dl.ashx?id=1468, read via pdftotext, translated; it cites the 1967 document collection *Bratskaya GES* vol. II and the State Archive of the Russian Federation, f. R5451):** more than 300 Komsomol members came in 1955 on Komsomol vouchers; before handing over a voucher, organizers interviewed people and warned them that some sites would have to be built from nothing. The first insulated tents went up at the end of January 1955 and the builders called the first settlement "Green Town" (Wikipedia: Green Town was flooded by the reservoir in 1961).
- **Housing in 1959 (same source):** "In 1959 there were only 283,000 m² of living space for 27,900 builders, on which more than 50,000 people lived." DERIVED: 283,000 / 50,000 = about 5.7 m² per person. 2,120 families urgently needed housing and 2,420 families lived in cramped conditions; 660 workers lived in 95 insulated tents and 568 in railcars and other adapted premises; 80,000 m² were needed, 47,000 m² were planned for 1959, and 23,000 m² were built in eight months (just under 50 percent of plan). 5,528 young builders lived in dormitories with 29,556 m² of space (DERIVED: about 5.3 m² each). In eight months of 1959, 6,446 builders left, 25 percent of the workforce (4,337 of their own will), while 9,621 were hired. The February 1960 Irkutsk regional party conference named poor housing and poor supply as main causes. In 1961, 1,247 left, "or 24%": 298 at their own wish, 614 left without permission, 32 committed crimes, 103 dismissed for repeated breaches of discipline, 200 for other reasons (DERIVED: 1,247 / 5,385 = 23.2 percent). Some recruits had low skills, inflated pay grades, and expected high earnings and "immediate receipt of a well-appointed apartment." From April 1962 every dismissal was to be reviewed by the work collective. Over 12,000 people gained new trades or raised their qualification in 1957 to 1959.
- **Construction-trust staffing, 1959 (SOURCED, N. N. Naumova and K. A. - **The reservoir and the people moved (SOURCED, M. A. Planning in 1956 to 1957 replaced 63 old settlements with six new large ones. Moving and rebuilding a house was the owner's own job at the owner's cost; if a house was more than 50 percent worn or had to go more than 50 km, a new one of equal volume was built from the dam budget; war and labor invalids, families of fallen servicemen, pensioners and the elderly whose houses could not be moved got new houses from the city council. Houses were taken down and rebuilt "by help": three or four owners agreed among themselves and helped each other. Today (search summary of a June 2026 Siberia.Realities report, UNVERIFIED): descendants of the resettled in Bratsk and Ust-Uda districts remain cut off after regional authorities found no money for the "Meteor" passenger boat.

Census: 51,455 (1959); 155,362 (1970); 213,725 (1979); 255,705 (1989); 259,335 (2002); 246,319 (2010); 224,071 (2021); 219,365 (2025 estimate). Michigan State (https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1961/bratsk/) gives 43,000 in 1959 (CONFLICT with 51,455) and "averaging 27 years of age in 1970"; the village that gave the name was submerged. ## 6A. Pullman, Illinois (Pullman Palace Car Company)
- **Who and how many (SOURCED, Richard T. Ely, "Pullman: A Social Study," *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* 70, February 1885, pp. 452 to 466; full text at https://web.viu.ca/davies/H321GildedAge/Pullman.SocialStudy.htm):** "a town of eight thousand inhabitants," founded less than four years earlier; employment "about four thousand, of whom over three thousand are employed by the Palace Car Company." The 1894 strikers' statement gives about 4,000 workers and about 20,000 residents (Report on the Chicago Strike, excerpt at https://history.hanover.edu/courses/excerpts/336pullman.html) (CONFLICT with 8,000; different years, and the strikers' count may include the surroundings). Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman,_Chicago): about 900 rowhouses with indoor plumbing, gas and sewers; architect Solon Spencer Beman.
- **Ownership, leases, rent (SOURCED, Ely 1885):** "No private individual owns to-day a square rod of ground or a single structure in the entire town"; "every tenant holds his house on a lease which may be terminated on ten days' notice"; usual rents $14 to $25 a month plus about 80 cents for water; a five-room cottage $17 a month; "Rents are probably about three-fifths what they are in Chicago"; the company expected at least a six percent return. "The annual charge for the use of the Public Library, for nothing in Pullman is free, is three dollars." The company church stood empty "because no denomination can pay the rent"; "The Presbyterians offered $2000 a year for it, and this was refused."
- **Alcohol (SOURCED, Ely 1885):** the company hotel held "the only barroom allowed in Pullman, though there are thirty on the outskirts of the place in Kensington."
- **Surveillance and speech (SOURCED, Ely 1885):** "The men believe they are watched by the 'company's spotter'"; "not one single resident dare speak out openly his opinion about the town in which he lives." Verdict: "the idea of Pullman is un-American"; "benevolent, well wishing feudalism."
- **Pushback, 1894 (SOURCED, strikers' statement, Hanover excerpt):** five cuts in wages and conditions May to December 1893, the last near 30 percent ("from 30 to 70 percent" overall, as the statement puts it), while "our rents had not fallen," and rents across Chicago had fallen, "in some cases to one-half"; a house costing $15 in Pullman leased for $8 in Roseland; rent was deducted from wages; the company bought water from the city at 8 cents per thousand gallons and resold it at a 500 percent markup; gas that cost 75 cents per thousand feet in Hyde Park cost residents $2.25; "no man or woman of us all has ever owned or can ever hope to own one inch of George M. Pullman's land"; the trigger was the firing of two grievance-committee members. **CONFLICT on rents:** Ely 1885 (about three-fifths of Chicago's) vs the strikers 1894 ($15 vs $8 in Roseland) vs Eugene Debs's claim that rents were "fully 25 percent higher than elsewhere" (search summary, UNVERIFIED).
- **Strike and aftermath (SOURCED, NPS, https://www.nps.gov/pull/learn/historyculture/the-strike-of-1894.htm):** strike from May 11, 1894; American Railway Union boycott of Pullman cars; injunction under the Sherman Act; "thousands of U.S. Marshals and U.S. Army troops were deployed"; clashes "left dozens dead and more wounded"; in October 1898 the Illinois Supreme Court ordered the company to sell all non-industrial land; the town "mostly changed hands in 1907, with residents given the first option to buy." The Strike Commission said the aesthetic features admired by visitors "had little monetary value for employees" (as quoted by Wikipedia; the report's own text was NOT reached: died at the sources).
- **Later (SOURCED, Wikipedia):** decline after 1950s industrial restructuring; a 1960 plan to demolish the town for an industrial park was stopped by residents, who formed the Pullman Civic Organization; population peak 10,965 in 1970; National Monument 2015, National Historical Park 2022. Annexation to Chicago: 1899 (Wikipedia as read) vs 1898 (search summary) (CONFLICT; a third date, 1889, is often given for the annexation of Hyde Park township, but no source read this session states it).
- **What failed and when:** rents held while wages fell (1893 to 1894); a town no resident could own; the corporation's charter did not allow it to run a town (court, 1898); sale 1907.

## 6B. Hershey, Pennsylvania (Hershey Chocolate)
- **Founding and provision (SOURCED, American Heritage, "Sweetest Place on Earth," https://www.americanheritage.com/sweetest-place-earth):** site chosen 1901; town named by public contest 1906; residents mostly factory workers; houses with lawns; a trolley, bank, library, shops, the Hershey Industrial School for orphan boys, an amusement park (from 1907, at first playground equipment for employees), a zoo (1916), a $3 million Community Center with a 1,904-seat theater and the $2 million Hotel Hershey (1933, 190 rooms). Depression: Hershey vowed his "Utopia would never see a breadline" and funded building that employed more than 600 workers: "If I don't provide work for them, I'll have to feed them." Control: unannounced inspections of milk cans at the soda shop; residents told to paint chipped fences. Early photos show only white workers; the school enrolled no minority pupils until 1968.
- **Homes and utilities (UNVERIFIED, search summaries):** the companies sold home sites to employees and underwrote their mortgages; the Hershey Improvement Company was the main utility provider. The Hershey Estates history page returned 404.
- **Trolley (SOURCED, Hershey Community Archives, https://hersheystory.org/how-trolleys-helped-hershey-thrive/ and https://hersheyarchives.org/?p=834):** chartered January 1903, before the factory groundbreaking; service from October 15, 1904; 35 miles of track and 34 cars at its height; fare 5 cents, "Heavy subsidies kept the basic fare at $.05"; about 50,000 gallons of milk a day hauled to the factory in the late 1910s and early 1920s; workers commuted from neighboring towns; last run December 21, 1946.
- **Pushback, 1937 (SOURCED, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_chocolate_workers%27_strike,_1937; Hershey Community Archives, https://hersheyarchives.org/?p=7793):** a 60-hour week cut to 40 hours; from 1930 to 1936 the company spent more than $10 million on the town, cut hours and stopped annual bonuses while earning more than $37 million after tax; hiring and firing by favoritism; about 3,000 factory workers plus nearly 2,000 elsewhere; a CIO local formed early 1937; wages agreed at 60 cents an hour for men and 45 cents for women; organizers then laid off. Sit-down from April 2, 1937 (organizers claimed 1,200, executives 400, the Archives about 500: CONFLICT); dairy farmers lost 800,000 pounds of milk a day; April 7, more than 3,000 loyal workers and farmers stormed the plant, outnumbering strikers four to one; strikers "were forced to run a gauntlet and emerged beaten and bloody." Vote, April 23, 1937 (worker Raphael Eckenroth's journal): "C.I.O. had 786. Loyal 1542." Federal authorities ordered a second election; in 1939 the AFL Bakery and Confectionery Workers won, gaining overtime rates, paid vacations and grievance arbitration.
- **What failed and when:** the bargain of paternal care for loyalty broke in the Depression (1930 to 1937); the trolley ended 1946; in 2002 residents "raised a furor" against the controlling trust's plan to sell the company, and a judge blocked it (American Heritage).

## 6C. Port Sunlight, England (Lever Brothers, later Unilever)
- **Timeline (SOURCED, Port Sunlight Village Trust, Conservation Management Plan Appendix 1, https://www.portsunlightvillage.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/PSVT-CMP-Appendix-1_Timeline-of-Key-Dates-v3.pdf, read via pdftotext):** first sod March 3, 1888; first tenants September 1889; Gladstone Hall 1891; "More than 400 houses" by 1900; schools, Technical Institute and Lever Library 1903; Christ Church 1904; more than 700 men from works and village volunteered 1914 to 1918; village "considered complete" 1938; severe air-raid damage 1939 to 1945; indoor toilets, upstairs bathrooms and modern kitchens planned 1954 to 1959; managed by Unilever Merseyside Ltd from 1960; renovation 1963 to 1980; Grade II listing 1965; Conservation Area 1978; "**1980 The first houses are sold in the village**"; the Village Trust formed 1999 to take over communal buildings, landscape and unsold houses.

## 6D. Saltaire, England (Titus Salt)
- **SOURCED (Saltaire Collection, https://www.saltairecollection.org/saltaire-history/foundation-of-saltaire/):** Salt left a Bradford that was "hugely overcrowded and insanitary"; Salts Mill opened September 20, 1853, with 3,000 workers producing 30,000 yards of cloth a day; 850 residences on just over 25 acres; houses from two to seven bedrooms, each with drainage, water, gas, a back yard and a private lavatory; occupancy depended on "the ability to pay the graded rents" (the page calls it a "common myth" that larger houses were for senior staff); 4,389 residents in 1871; a dining hall for 500; Congregational church (1859), but Salt "did not compel his workforce to attend a church"; factory schools for 750 pupils (1868); a six-bed infirmary (1868, closed 1979); 45 rent-free almshouses with a weekly pension; the Club and Institute (1871) to "supply the advantages of a public house without its evils"; a park (1871).
- **SOURCED (Saltaire Village volunteer FAQ, https://saltairevillage.info/Saltaire_WHS_01_FAQ.html, which warns it "may be inaccurate"):** "There are 824 houses in the village, accommodating 4,500 people"; "If you lost your job, you lost your house"; "always a dry village," with no pubs or beer-houses allowed; the company went into liquidation in 1892 and consortia took over the mill; in 1933 the houses were sold to a Bradford estate agent, who added electric light and bathrooms and sold them to owner-occupiers: "The link between the Mill and the houses was broken"; the mill business ended by 1986, and Jonathan Silver bought the empty building in 1987 for art and retail.
- **CONFLICTS:** 850 vs 824 houses; 4,389 (1871) vs 4,500; tenancy by rent alone (Collection) vs tied to the job (FAQ). Search summary: Salt died in 1876 (my query wrongly said 1892; that error died at the query).
- **What failed and when:** the founding company failed in 1892; the housing link to the mill ended in 1933; the mill closed by 1986.

## 6E. Bournville, England (Cadbury)

## 6F. Fordlândia, Brazil (Ford Motor Company)
- **SOURCED (The Henry Ford, https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/popular-research-topics/ford-rubber-plantations-in-brazil):** a free concession of 2.5 million acres (July 1927); wages 35 cents a day plus free food, shelter and medical care; "Dearborn-style" rules: a Midwestern diet, square dancing, poetry readings, two-family houses; shoes and quinine required against hookworm and malaria; 1933, a second site (Belterra) chosen; 1940: 500 employees at Fordlândia and 2,500 at Belterra (Belterra: 7,000 inhabitants, 844 houses and barracks for 950 men, schools for over 1,000 pupils); the two hospitals had over 120 beds; 1942 output 750 tons of latex against a need of 38,000; 1945 handed to Brazil for "a mere $250,000" after about $20 million invested; causes of failure: leaf blight and pests, factory men instead of horticulturists, an imposed lifestyle and high turnover, the riot over a self-serve food line.
- **Rules and revolt (SOURCED, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fordl%C3%A2ndia):** alcohol, tobacco and prostitution banned, "even inside workers' homes," with managers inspecting houses; an "Island of Innocence" about 8 km upstream had bars and brothels; Brazilian workers and American managers lived in separate areas; the 1930 "Breaking Pans" (*Quebra-Panelas*) revolt over food: workers cut telegraph wires and drove managers into the jungle until the army arrived; moved to Belterra 1934; sold 1945; about 3,000 people lived there in 2017.
- **The riot in detail (SOURCED, James C. Scott, review of Greg Grandin's *Fordlandia*, London Review of Books 31:19, https://lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v31/n19/james-c.-scott/duas-cervejas):** December 22, 1930; a Dearborn supervisor replaced waiter service with a cafeteria queue; a worker shouted "We are not dogs that are going to be ordered by the company to eat in this way"; rioters smashed the dining hall, power house, office, sawmill and radio station, burned records and smashed the time clocks; no one was killed. Unmarried workers had to eat in the company hall and were charged even for skipped meals; workers were examined for venereal disease and given anti-malarial pills at clock-out; turnover was "many times" Dearborn's; grievances centered on "the right of free movement"; managers had lawns, a clubhouse, pool, golf course and cinema; the plantation closed in November 1945 and workers were not told until the Americans boarded their ship.
- **What failed and when:** the crop (blight, 1930s); the imposed way of life (riot 1930); the whole venture (1934 move, 1945 exit).

## 6G. Kohler, Wisconsin (Kohler Company)
- **Village and the American Club (SOURCED, National Register nomination, American Club, 1977, https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/f7f28fe7-ec0d-4ed6-842e-fc0b893c5d0f, read via pdftotext):** the company moved four miles west of Sheboygan in 1899; the village was planned with architect Richard Philipp and the Olmsted Brothers; Walter J. Kohler: "a worker deserves not only wages but roses as well." The American Club was dedicated June 23, 1918, "primarily as a home for single men" and a community recreation center, named for Kohler's wish to Americanize foreign-born workers (Dutch, German, White Russian); portraits of Lincoln and Washington and American flags; classes in English and citizenship. Kohler's dedication speech: to "hygienically house single men of modest means, giving them high standards of living and clean, healthful recreation that would inculcate in them a desire for Americanization"; not to be run for profit. As described in 1977: eight double and two single rooms with bath plus 85 dormitory rooms; a cafeteria, laundry, lounge, tap room, bowling alleys and barber shop. Remodeled into a village inn in the 1940s and a hotel in 1981 (search summary). Village incorporated 1912 (Wikipedia, Kohler strikes).
- **Pushback (SOURCED, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohler_strikes):** 1934: after a company-controlled workers' association was formed, the AFL Federal Labor Union 18545 asked for recognition; the company shut the plant; on July 27 special deputies fired on strikers; Lee Wakefield and Harry Englemann were killed and 47 people wounded, gassed or injured; 250 National Guard arrived; the company union won the supervised election. 1954 to 1965: about 2,800 of 3,300 employees struck from April 5, 1954; production resumed with non-union workers; more than 1,000 alleged acts of vandalism and at one point more than 300 arrests; the UAW paid strikers $12 million; in 1960 the NLRB ordered 1,700 workers reinstated; in December 1965 the company agreed to $3 million in back wages to about 1,400 former employees and $1.5 million in pension contributions: "The longest major strike in American history had ended."
- **NOT FOUND:** company-owned family housing, home loans and evictions in the village (search 81; died at the query).
- **What failed and when:** the company's control of labor relations (1934 deaths; 1954 to 1965 strike).

## 6I. What failed (facts gathered above, listed without ranking)
- Rent and pay set by the same employer, so wage cuts became rent crises: Pullman 1894; Lowell 1836 board increase.

- Armed suppression: Kohler 1934 (two dead), Pullman 1894 (dozens dead).
- Owner distress passed to the town: Scotia (1986 takeover, 2007 bankruptcy, town owned by a hedge fund from 2008); Saltaire (company liquidation 1892).
- Resource exhaustion: Kennecott 1938.
- Where the link was cut by design: Bournville (Trust, 1900; open to non-employees); Port Sunlight's Trust (1999).
