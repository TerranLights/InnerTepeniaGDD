## 1 · The place

### 1.1 Position and elevation
- **Position:** 90°S; the Amundsen-Scott station sits at the geographic South Pole. **SOURCED** (NSF station page, https://www.nsf.gov/geo/opp/support/southp.jsp , published 2024-12-05, updated 2026-06-05).
- **Elevation:** about **2,835 m (9,301 ft)** above sea level (NSF page above). The station Meteorology Department handout gives **2,836 m / 9,306 ft** ASL (USAP/SSEC "Meteorology Department, Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station" handout, dated "kh 8-05", i.e. August 2005, https://amrdc.ssec.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/met-handout.pdf , text read via pdftotext). **SOURCED** (both; the 1 m difference is between sources, not resolved).
- **Surroundings:** the handout states that "toward grid north to grid east of the Pole, the East Antarctic Plateau climbs to over 4,000 meters / 13,000 feet" and "toward grid west to grid south, the Transantarctic Mountains rise from the ice." Grid north is defined as the prime meridian (0°). Distance from McMurdo Station: **847 statute miles**. **SOURCED** (met handout).

### 1.2 Ice thickness and bed
- **Ice thickness under the IceCube array (beside the station):** "Recent 7-MHz impulse radar data yields an ice thickness of approximately **2,880 m** within the IceCube array ..., about 70 m deeper than previous estimates (Price et al., 2002)." **SOURCED** (Hills, Christianson, Hoffman, Fudge, Holschuh, Kahle et al. 2022, "Geophysics and thermodynamics at South Pole Lake indicate stability and a regionally thawed bed," *Geophysical Research Letters* 49, e2021GL096218, doi:10.1029/2021GL096218; PDF https://benhills.github.io/uploads/Hills2022_GRL.pdf , read via pdftotext).
- **Older figures in circulation:** about 2,700 m (SSEC/AMRDC dataset page, snippet only), "2,820 m" (an IceCube-related thesis chapter, arXiv 0910.2555, snippet only), "nearly two-mile-thick" (USAP news 2018), "two-mile (3 km)-thick" (Wikipedia). **UNVERIFIED** as exact values; they are the range of popular statements. The radar figure above supersedes them in the one source that compares (Hills et al. say the new figure is ~70 m deeper than Price et al. 2002).
- **South Pole Lake:** a subglacial lake "constrained to a 50 km² area, 15 km from the geographic South Pole"; ice thickness above it **2,852 ± 10 m**; an earlier seismic study "showed a 32-m deep lake underlain by 150 m of sediment"; the authors infer "a consistently thawed ice-sheet bed within the surveyed area" and that the lake "has been thermodynamically stable for at least 120,000 years." **SOURCED** (Hills et al. 2022).
- **Regional bed:** a 2017 EGU abstract (snippet) describes bedrock uncertainties of over a kilometer near the Pole and the "Pensacola Pole Basin," generally ~650 m below sea level. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only; not opened).

### 1.3 Ice flow, direction, and the marker reset
- **Speed and direction:** "the surface velocity is **10 m a⁻¹ in the direction of 40° W**" (Fudge, Lilien, Koutnik, Conway, Stevens, Waddington, Steig, Schauer, Holschuh 2020, "Advection and non-climate impacts on the South Pole Ice Core," *Climate of the Past* 16, 819–832, https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/16/819/2020/ , citing Casey et al. 2014). Upstream measured velocities "range from 3 to 10 m a⁻¹." **SOURCED**.
- Hills et al. 2022 measured a **mean 9.3 m/yr** over their survey near South Pole Lake, "with a slight increase from 8.5 to 10.3 m/yr in the direction of flow," and state flow is "to the grid northwest." **SOURCED**.
- The met handout: "Ice Movement: 33 feet/year (10 meters/year)." NSF station page: the station "drifts about 10 m (33 ft) per year." **SOURCED**.
- southpolestation.com survey page (Bill Spindler's historical site, https://southpolestation.com/pole/survey.html): about 10 m/yr at a bearing "between 37 and 40 degrees west of grid north," rate "slightly erratic from year to year," toward South Georgia in the South Atlantic. **SOURCED** (page read; degree symbols garbled on the page, values read from context).
- USAP news (2018-02-02, https://www.usap.gov/news/4561/): the ice is "continuously sliding towards the Weddell Sea at a rate of about ten meters (33 feet) a year." **SOURCED**. (The 40°W meridian from the Pole runs toward the Weddell Sea / South Atlantic, so these are consistent statements of one direction. **DERIVED**, geographic reading only.)
- **Holocene history:** Lilien et al. infer a ~**15% speed-up through the Holocene** (0% at present, 15% at 10 ka) "with constant patterns of accumulation and flow direction." **SOURCED** (Lilien, IceCube collaboration talk slides 2021-01-19, https://events.icecube.wisc.edu/event/128/contributions/7311/attachments/5723/6707/lilien_icecube_20210119.pdf , read via pdftotext; the paper is Lilien et al. 2018, GRL, doi 10.1029/2018GL078253, not opened).
- **Marker reset:** the geographic pole stays fixed; the ice and everything on it moves. The marker for "the precise geographic South Pole" is relocated annually; "the crew spending the Southern Hemisphere winter at the station designs a marker," NSF approves the design, it is "machined on site," and unveiled on **1 January** (USAP news 2018). **SOURCED**. Historical method (1970s): movement computed from satellite tracking of the station's sattrack antenna; surveyors "turned an angle from the previous year's location and measured off the distance" (southpolestation.com survey page). **SOURCED**. Earlier positions were marked with stakes or copper pipes; the 1994 and 2003 markers were stolen (same page). **SOURCED**. The tradition of a new marker each year "appears to date back to the 1980s" (USAP/Antarctic Sun, snippet only). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Antarctic Sun pages:** `antarcticsun.usap.gov` failed DNS (`ENOTFOUND`) for every fetch; web.archive.org is blocked to the fetch tool. Every Antarctic Sun fact in this file is therefore from search snippets or secondary pages and is marked UNVERIFIED unless confirmed elsewhere.

### 1.4 Snow accumulation and burial
- **Accumulation:** met handout: "Average Annual Snow Accumulation: 9 inches (23 cm); Average Annual Liquid Equivalent: 3.4 inches (8.6 cm)," measured "via an array of snow stakes." **SOURCED**.
- Mosley-Thompson, Paskievitch, Gow & Thompson 1999 (*JGR Atmospheres*, doi 10.1029/1998JD200092): a network of **236 accumulation poles** gave a 1992–1997 average of **84.5 ± 8.9 mm water equivalent** per year, about **30% above** the 1960s (~65 mm w.e.); 1965–1994 had "the highest 30-year average of this millennium." **SOURCED** (USGS publication page, https://www.usgs.gov/publications/late-20th-century-increase-south-pole-snow-accumulation).
- Hills et al. 2022: "the known accumulation rate (**~8.7 cm/yr**)" (ice-equivalent context). Fudge et al. 2020: upstream oldest layer averages **0.080 m/yr** ice equivalent, with spatial variation "approximately ±20%"; Titan Dome 0.074 m/yr. **SOURCED**.
- **No melt:** "with no thawing" (Wikipedia, citing History Channel *Modern Marvels* 2005 and an NSF 1993 study). **UNVERIFIED** as worded; consistent with the record high of −12.3 °C (§1.5), which is far below freezing (**DERIVED**).
- **Burial of structures:** see §2 (the 1957 station was under ~1.8 m of snow by 1960; the Dome's drift problem; the elevated station's jacking design). Drift around obstacles, not the 23 cm/yr of open-field accumulation, is what buried the earlier stations; that sourcing is in §2.
- **Ice-core context:** SPICEcore, 1,751 m long, drilled at the South Pole 2014–2016, spans **54,300 years** (bottom age 54,302 ± 519 years, SP19 chronology). **SOURCED** (Fudge et al. 2020 for depth and 54,300; the ± figure from a search snippet of the SP19 chronology paper, Winski et al. 2019, *Climate of the Past* 15, 1793, **UNVERIFIED**).

These measure different things (gauge catch vs. stake accumulation, which includes drift); **not resolved**, all stated.
- **Precipitation form:** "Ice crystals ('diamond dust') are the most common form of precipitation at South Pole, and are observed on the majority of days ... often fall out of a clear sky"; branched snowflakes are rare, mostly in summer. **SOURCED** (met handout).

### 1.6 Wind
- Met handout (2005): average **10.7 knots** (12.3 mph); text says "averaging about 11 knots annually, wind speeds rarely exceed 40 knots"; prevailing direction **020° (grid northeast)** in the table, "from grid north" in the text; **peak gust 48 knots (55 mph), August 1989**. Blowing snow "begins to significantly reduce visibility" above **15 knots**; "dangerous whiteout conditions (zero visibility with total loss of the horizon) usually occur when sustained winds exceed about **25 knots**." Down-slope conditions with grid NE–SE winds; cloud and precipitation with grid N or NW winds; grid S and SW winds "rarely observed." **SOURCED**.
- A fifty-year climatology (Lazzara et al. 2012, *Atmospheric Research*) reports a significant downward trend in mean wind speed of **0.28 m/s per decade**. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only; not opened).
- A 2017 climate summary: prevailing grid NE (030°), average ~9.2 knots. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only).

### 1.8 Pressure altitude and its physiological effect
- Met handout (2005): station pressure average **681.4 mb**, highest 719.0 mb (Aug 1996), lowest 641.7 mb (Jul 1985); **pressure altitude average 10,576 ft (3,223 m)**, highest **12,107 ft (3,690 m)**, lowest 9,190 ft (2,801 m). **SOURCED**.
- The thin polar atmosphere is the cause: "The Earth's atmosphere is substantially more compact at cold, high latitudes" (met handout, in the clouds section). **SOURCED**.
- **ASAP study:** Anderson, Miller, O'Malley, Ceridon, Beck, Wood, Wiste, Mueller, Johnson & Johnson 2011, "Incidence and Symptoms of High Altitude Illness in South Pole Workers: Antarctic Study of Altitude Physiology (ASAP)," *Clinical Medicine Insights: Circulatory, Respiratory and Pulmonary Medicine* 5:27–35, doi 10.4137/CCRPM.S6882 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3114308). **SOURCED**:
  - average barometric pressure **512.7 mmHg (2006)** and **513 mmHg (2007)**, equivalent to ~3,184–3,197 m; lowest daily averages 509.8 mmHg (~3,242 m, 2006) and **501.8 mmHg (~3,365 m, 2007)**; physiological altitudes "approached 3,400 m."
  - **246** medically screened adults (~70% male, mean age 36.8); **52%** met Lake Louise criteria for acute mountain sickness at some point; peak on **day 2 (33%)**; ~**40%** used acetazolamide, with no difference in incidence (51% vs 52%).
  - **Seven workers were evacuated from the Pole in 2006** with symptoms consistent with high-altitude pulmonary edema; life-threatening symptoms in ~2% of subjects.
  - Workers fly from McMurdo (sea level) to the Pole in ~3–4 hours and often begin hard work soon after.
- USAP deployment material gives a South Pole pressure-altitude range of **9,000 to 12,500 ft**. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only, https://usap.gov/travelanddeployment/1819 not opened).

## 2 · The station's history and form

**Key sources opened for this item:** southpolestation.com "South Pole Station — the first 10 years" (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/igy1/igy1.html), "Building the Dome (continued)" (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/history/dome/dome2.html), "Dry Gulch" (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/drygulch/drygulch.html), "Foundation Design for the Elevated Station" (https://www.southpolestation.com/foundationdesign.html), "A Tunnel Runs Through It" (https://southpolestation.com/trivia/history/tunnel.html) — Bill Spindler's historical compilation site, which reprints primary documents and participants' accounts; NSF "How human presence at South Pole has evolved over the past century" (Lauren Lipuma, 2022-12-01, updated 2026-03-09, https://www.nsf.gov/science-matters/how-human-presence-south-pole-has-evolved-over-past-century); NSF "Dome away from home" (2010-03-09, via phys.org PDF https://phys.org/news/2010-03-dome-home.pdf); CIRES South Pole Ozone blog (2019-10-09); Polar Journal "Problems at the Pole" (Seth Pilgrim, former South Pole Area Manager / Winter Site Manager, 2024-02-15, https://polarjournal.net/problems-at-the-pole/); brr.fyi "South Pole Topography" (anonymous 2022–23 winter-over IT worker, 2023-01-27, https://brr.fyi/posts/south-pole-topography); NSF master-plan news (2024-05-16).

### 2.1 Generation 1 — the 1956–57 IGY station ("Old Pole")
- **Purpose:** built for the International Geophysical Year (1957–58); research on snow and ice, ice-sheet movement, air temperature, auroras; "never meant to be permanent." **SOURCED** (NSF 2022).
- **Construction dates:** first cargo airdrop (C-124) 25 Oct 1956; R4D *Que Sera Sera* with RADM Dufek landed 31 Oct 1956; construction crew under LT Dick Bowers arrived **20 Nov 1956** (landing ~8 miles from the Pole); galley occupied ~27 Dec 1956; flights resumed 10 Feb 1957; **last airdrop 21 Feb 1957** closed the station; the berthing building from that drop was finished in early March. **SOURCED** (southpolestation igy1). Wikipedia: last construction crew left 4 Jan 1957. **UNVERIFIED** (secondary).
- **Form:** prefabricated Clements and T-5 buildings, a Jamesway berthing hut, garage, science building, galley, balloon inflation building, RAWIN dome, aurora tower, a 4×8 ft astronomical hut, linked by **tunnels**. **SOURCED** (igy1). Wikipedia: prefabricated 4×8 ft panels. **UNVERIFIED** (secondary).
- **Delivery:** entirely by **airdrop** (C-124) and small ski-plane landings through 1957; heaviest C-124 airdrop a ~9-ton D-4 tractor (21 Oct 1959) on five 100-ft G-11 parachutes; a D-2 tractor airdropped 10 Nov 1957 "buried itself more than 45 feet deep." First LC-130 landing at Pole **30 Oct 1960** (1 h 54 min from McMurdo). Drummed fuel was airdropped until 1962–63, when LC-130 bulk fuel began; "the first fuel arch had three 25,000-gallon bladders." **SOURCED** (igy1).
- **First winter parties:** 1957 population **18** (OIC LTJG Jack Tuck Jr.); 1958 18; 1959 17; 1960 19; 1961 20; 1962–64 22 each. **SOURCED** (igy1). CIRES blog says the first winter crew was **16** (8 scientists, 8 Navy). **SOURCED** (CIRES). Conflict **not resolved**.
- **Early measurements recorded:** first −100 °F on 12 May 1957; −102.1 °F (−74.5 °C) on 18 Sep 1957; a Russian traverse of 1960–61 measured the ice at **9,219 ft** (2,810 m) thick at the Pole; a 1961–62 USGS survey gave ice motion of "about 20 meters per year" (an early figure, twice the modern 10 m/yr). **SOURCED** (igy1, as historical records; not modern values).
- **Burial and why it was replaced:** "wind-blown snow began covering it after several years" (NSF 2022); "within a couple of years, snow had completely buried it" (CIRES); Wikipedia: under 1.8 m of snow by 1960 (secondary). Tunnels were crushed by snow: in 1958–59 a tunnel was shored with stacked fuel drums "that began to crumple"; steel arches replaced wood-supported tunnels in 1963–64; the station was "completely dug out for major reconstruction of tunnels" in 1963–64; a June 1963 NCEL study recommended "an elevated structure and underlying tunnel"; a 1961 study planned replacement from Deep Freeze 64; a garage fire on **13 Oct 1964** (−67 °F) destroyed three tractors. **SOURCED** (igy1). In the final years (~1974–75) the crew lived "20 to 30 feet below the snow surface," "always wary of the stresses and strains evident throughout"; old-station power was shut off **3 Feb 1975**. **SOURCED** (tunnel page).
- **Demolition:** demolished in the 2009–10/2010–11 summer for safety; one article gives the station as ~30 ft (9 m) below the surface; method included dynamite and a heavy machine breaking the roof. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; the Live Science article returned only its headline, "South Pole's First Building Blown Up After 53 Years"). NSF 2022 says "deconstructed in 2010." **SOURCED**.

### 2.2 Generation 2 — the geodesic Dome (built 1971–75, occupied 1975–2008/2010)
- **Builders and dates:** Navy Seabees of NMCB-71; a construction camp ("Dry Gulch") built 1972–73 for ~110 Seabees (average ~120; 146 construction personnel cited for 1973–74); NMCB-71 left February 1974; the project completed at the end of 1974–75 with military then civilian workers. **SOURCED** (Dry Gulch page). Station structures (modules) designed and built by ATCO; NMCB-71 built the arches and above-surface structures; Holmes & Narver did utilities in the utilidor and the fuel system. **SOURCED** (dome2). Dedicated January 1975 (snippets); moved into in 1975. **SOURCED** (NSF 2022 for 1975).
- **Form:** NSF 2022: geodesic dome "**164 feet wide** at the base"; Wikipedia: 160 ft (50 m) wide, 52 ft (16 m) high, with 46×79 ft (14×24 m) steel archways; NSF station page: 50 m wide, 16 m high. **SOURCED** (NSF for 164 ft and 50 m × 16 m; the two NSF pages differ by 4 ft, not resolved). Under the dome: modular buildings with single rooms for ~30 people, library, recreation, labs, galley, post office, meeting space. **SOURCED** (NSF 2022). Outside the dome, steel arches: Biomed, garage, helium/cargo, **fuel**, and **power plant** (diesel engines with a snow-harvesting conveyor intended to fill the water tank, which "did not work"). **SOURCED** (dome2). All construction cargo had to fit inside an LC-130; more than 50 crates/modules delivered by VXE-6. **SOURCED** (dome2).
- **Designed to withstand:** snow, winds of about **100 mph**, and temperatures of **−80 °F or lower** (Dry Gulch page). The dome "was sufficiently strong to withstand the weight of snow that would blow onto it, and its round shape helped deflect the fierce winds"; it needed no internal columns; it "was designed to be flown to the South Pole with relative ease in small pieces and then assembled using a system of struts, bolts and gusset plates." **SOURCED** (NSF 2010).
- **Why replaced:** "The dome could no longer accommodate the demands of research activities ... and each year the structure sunk deeper into the ice it was built on. **Blowing snow that collected on top of it had to be removed and hauled away, burning up precious fuel and crew time** during the short austral summer." The Antarctic Treaty regime "requires that buildings and equipment no longer in use be removed and the site remediated whenever possible." **SOURCED** (NSF 2010). Drifting snow covered its entrances by the 1990s. **SOURCED** (NSF 2022). Ring beams broke in winter 1988 from overstress (Wikipedia citing the Antarctic Sun). **UNVERIFIED**. "Designed as a stopgap... to last about a decade" (snippet from file770.com). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Drift, not snowfall:** the Pole gets only a few inches of precipitation a year, "but drifts still build up around objects on the surface"; the Dome sheltered modules from drifting snow. **SOURCED** (CIRES 2019, paraphrase from the opened page).
- **Deconstruction:** began 18 Dec 2009 with the crown, finished 15 Jan 2010 (Antarctic Sun snippet). **UNVERIFIED** (site unreachable). The materials went to the Navy at Port Hueneme, Calif., with the top sections preserved for a possible Seabee museum exhibit. **SOURCED** (NSF 2010).

### 2.3 Generation 3 — the elevated station (built ~1997/1999–2008, in use)
- **Dates:** design began 1992 (Wikipedia, secondary); construction began **1997** (NSF 2022) or **1999** (CIRES, Wikipedia) — **conflict not resolved**; dedicated **12 January 2008**. **SOURCED** (NSF 2022 for the 10-year construction; CIRES for the date). The Dome was decommissioned the same day (CIRES). **SOURCED**.
- **Construction logistics:** "It took 10 years and required about **24 million pounds** of cargo from the U.S. **More than 900 flights** moved materials from McMurdo Station. Crews had only about **100 building days per year**." **SOURCED** (NSF 2022). Wikipedia: 26,000 lb per LC-130 flight. **UNVERIFIED** (secondary). DERIVED: 24,000,000 lb ÷ 26,000 lb ≈ 923 flights, consistent with "more than 900."
- **Cost:** about $150 million (Wikipedia, citing NSF FY2008 budget request); a House Science Committee transcript gives "$153 million"; an NSF-linked release, "$152.9 million budget"; an earlier NSF estimate $127.9 million. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets and secondary).
- **Size:** **65,000 sq ft** and 124 × 45 m (408 × 150 ft) per an NSF poster (snippet; the poster URL returned HTTP 404); **80,000 sq ft across two stories** per CIRES and Wikipedia. **Conflict not resolved; both UNVERIFIED as to which is the built figure.** Capacity **150 beds** (NSF master-plan news 2024: "keeping the station's existing 150-bed capacity"). **SOURCED**. One usap.gov piece (snippet): private quarters for 154 in summer and 50 beds for winter. **UNVERIFIED**. Layout: four modular wings with bedrooms plus a full gymnasium extending downwind (CIRES); an **A-Pod and B-Pod** joined by an elevated walkway, with **B-Pod able to serve as an emergency "lifeboat"** (USAP webcam page). **SOURCED**.
- **Designed to withstand / anti-burial design:** "shaped as an airfoil and sits on elevating columns. It faces the wind, which compresses and accelerates air to scour away snow. The columns can lift it **two additional stories** to prevent burial." **SOURCED** (NSF 2022). Foundation (BBFM Engineers, Anchorage; snowdrift studies by RWDI, Guelph): **910 mm diameter columns** cantilevered from steel box-girder grade beams (710 × 610 mm) bearing on the snow; design bearing pressure 48 kPa long-term / 96 kPa full; governing long-term pressure 34 kPa; pad 1.8 m above the existing snow; column height **5.3 m, reaching 12.8 m at full height**; "designed to be jacked twice, each time a full story height of **3.7 m**," in 250 mm increments; predicted 50-year settlement ~840 mm (990 mm with the building); design differential settlement 150 mm. **SOURCED** (southpolestation foundation-design page, reprint of the engineers' article). A 2006 CRREL report (Blaisdell & Weale, "Settlement of a Foundation on a Permanent, Deep Snowpack," ERDC/CRREL) analyzed settlement Nov 2000–Jan 2005: 36 columns, ~13 ft (4 m) tall, a 2-inch limit between adjacent columns, predictions matching a **45-year** theoretical lifespan; a 2019 ERDC follow-up found shimming adequate. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; the DTIC PDF returned HTTP 403 and the OpenPolar record failed a certificate check). Wired 2002: settling "nearly four times faster than originally predicted" at that stage. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Burial in practice:** "Snow around the major buildings is close to **two stories above the 2007 level**"; designers planned to raise or move buildings periodically, which "has never happened"; NSF began addressing raising/relocating in 2023, work not before 2025. **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024; author was a station manager, so first-hand but one person's account). Stairwell bottoms and the main entrance stairs ("Destination Alpha") are buried; "snow hauling is a near full-time job"; vault hatches are extended "multiple times" as the snow rises; the arches are "completely buried under decades of accumulated snow," entered by a vehicle ramp. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2023).

### 2.4 Current infrastructure (as reported 2023–2024)
- **The arches:** metal semicircular buildings from before the elevated station, holding "the power plant, water plant, primary cold/warm storage, fuel storage, carpenter shop, and vehicle maintenance"; connected to the elevated station by an enclosed stair tower ("Beer Can") descending **about 50 ft**. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi). The **logistics arch** (1970s) holds **45 × 10,000-gallon fuel tanks** and warehouse space for "nearly two years of food"; the **power plant and vehicle-maintenance arches** were added in the late 1990s below grade, which shortened their lifespan; "ice is encroaching from below, pushing interior facilities upward"; the gap between arches and interior buildings has closed "from three feet to 'mere inches'"; some arches buckle under snow pressure; NSF studies called them "closer than ever to failing"; mitigations (fuel pipes turned horizontal, top of the vehicle shop cut off) "have not solved the problem"; "no replacement project exists" as of Feb 2024. **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024).
- **Utility tunnels:** "3,071 feet of under-snow tunnels... utility tunnels, housing the piping that supplies us with fresh water and removes wastewater" (Bangor Daily News, 2009, snippet); excavated with a CRREL subsurface tunneling system (ASCE *J. Cold Regions Eng.* 2001, 15(4):231, snippet). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Station sectors:** Operations Sector ("backyard": logistics, storage, outbuildings), an RF sector, and others. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi). The "Dark Sector" for astronomy and "Clean Air Sector" for atmospheric sampling: not on any opened page in this item; see §4.7. 
- **Site counts:** "About **47 buildings**, including housing, offices, labs, a store, a food growth chamber, dining, recreation, and medical services." **SOURCED** (NSF station page).
- **Buried material:** "the original station, construction equipment, and a cargo plane" lie buried at the Pole; backlogged material stored on berms is being buried ("bermageddon"); a 2022–23 cleanup was suspended for budget in 2024; all non-sewage waste must return to McMurdo. **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024).
- **Planning:** South Pole Station Master Plan (planning began 2020; >175 participants at a June 2023 meeting; final plan expected fall 2024; keep 150-bed capacity); 30–50 year horizon. **SOURCED** (NSF 2024-05-16; Polar Journal 2024 for the horizon).

### 3.2 Roles in a winter crew
- **A winter station manager's account** (memoir *Cold: Three Winters at the South Pole*, chapter text as reproduced at https://erenow.org/common/cold-three-winters-at-the-south-pole/3.php ; the page does not give the author's name; crews of **42–46** across three winters). **SOURCED** (as one account; the erenow reproduction is a third-party copy):
  - **Science:** 8 positions at the large telescopes, caretakers who answer alarms at any hour.
  - **Winter Site Manager:** leads without direct reports, since crew work for different companies and institutions.
  - **Facilities:** foreman, facilities engineer, two maintenance specialists, usually one plumber, one carpenter, at least one electrician; a fire-systems technician (two of three winters).
  - **Power plant:** lead, mechanic, and water-plant technician; the plant is staffed **24 hours a day** because "its diesel engines supply the station's heat."
  - **IT/communications:** four (systems administrator, network engineer, two satellite-communications engineers).
  - **Logistics:** four (senior materials person, two materials persons, inventory data specialist).
  - **Medical:** a physician plus a physician assistant or nurse practitioner. **Safety:** one engineer. **Meteorology:** two meteorologists, later one plus two winter research associates.
  - **Vehicle maintenance:** shop foreman, heavy-equipment mechanic, heavy-equipment operator. **Galley:** food-service supervisor, two production cooks, a steward.
  - **Shared duties:** scientists join all crew duties, "dishpit and house mouse"; the fire brigade is "a volunteer force of crewmembers who completed one week of training" and "the only firefighting force during winter"; maintenance specialists walk daily rounds "of several miles, in all weather."
- **Telescope and detector teams:** the 10-m South Pole Telescope keeps a **two-person** winter crew; IceCube a **two-person** detector team (one also systems administrator for the South Pole System). **SOURCED** for SPT (Ferguson 2023: "the only two winterovers working on" SPT); IceCube and BICEP job ads seen in snippets only, **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Shared duties (2023 account):** weekly "House Mouse" cleaning (toilets, snow shoveling), fire team trained by the Denver Fire Department, dishwashing; winter ran "mid-February to late October" with no flights. **SOURCED** (Ferguson 2023).
- **The Winter Site Manager position:** a contractor listing describes a senior site lead for the full austral winter, 10 months. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only).

### 3.3 Selection and medical screening
- **Legal basis:** 45 CFR Part 675, "Medical Clearance Process for Deployment to Antarctica" (Federal Register 62(111), 10 June 1997, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1997-06-10/html/97-14700.htm). **SOURCED**:
  - Winter-over candidates "are subject to additional evaluation": psychological evaluation "performed by a qualified team of USAP-designated physicians/clinical psychologists," for "a determination of psychological adaptability for such an isolated assignment."
  - The rationale: during the austral winter "timely evacuation to more comprehensive medical facilities off the Antarctic continent may be impossible."
  - Screening examines ten systems: lungs and chest wall; heart and vascular; abdominal and GI; endocrine/metabolic; genitalia and urinary; musculoskeletal; skin; neurological; psychiatric or psychological; **dental**.
- **Current PQ rules (NSF FAQ, https://www.nsf.gov/od/opp/soh/polar-physical-qualification/faq):** tests no more than six months old; PQ valid 12 months; candidates 45 and under may do the full PQ only every other year, **but anyone wintering at the South Pole in the second year must repeat the complete PQ**; non-disclosure of any medical history from birth leads to "Not Physically Qualified." Allow ~12 weeks. **SOURCED**.
- **Psychiatric screening outcome:** "Those with a history of psychiatric disorders, current symptoms of psychosocial distress, or considered to be at risk for a psychiatric disorder and/or poor performance on The Ice are disqualified from winter-over duty." **SOURCED** (Palinkas c. 2000). 1963–1974 Navy screening teams were "a clinical psychologist and psychiatrist." **SOURCED** (same).
- **Selection in practice (memoir above):** face-to-face interview; full PQ; a psychological exam (in the author's first year); 3 days teambuilding (Estes Park YMCA); one week fire school (Aurora, Colorado) or a wilderness first-aid course; trainers watch for negative traits and "some candidates are dropped before deployment." **SOURCED** (one account).
- **Not found:** the current psychological-evaluation instrument used by USAP (the 2024 policy SOH POL 2000.10 Vol. 2 surfaced only as a table of contents). **NOT FOUND**.

### 3.5 Mental-health and winter-over research (real studies)
- **Palinkas, "On the Ice: Individual and Group Adaptation in Antarctica"** (L. A. Palinkas, UC San Diego; manuscript c. 2000; PDF https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Palinkas_On_The_Ice.pdf , read via pdftotext). Draws on "over 1,100 Americans who wintered-over between 1963 and the present." **SOURCED**:
  - **Isolation window:** "Between the months of February and August at McMurdo and **February and October at the South Pole**, the stations are physically isolated."
  - **Confinement:** "as much as **60%** of one's leisure time is spent alone in a dorm room" (Carrere et al. 1991, McMurdo and Pole fieldwork 1988–89).
  - **Winter-over syndrome:** depression, irritability and hostility, insomnia, cognitive impairment, "long-eye" or the "Antarctic stare"; symptoms rise to mid-winter, decline in the third quarter, rise again at the end. At McMurdo 1989: 64.1% sleep problems, 62.1% depressed, 47.6% more irritable, 51.5% concentration/memory problems. "Approximately **5%** of winter-over personnel experience symptoms that fulfill DSM criteria for a psychiatric disorder" — despite prior screening.
  - **South Pole–specific:** POMS mood disturbance at the Pole, winters 1991–1994, differed significantly between the second half of winter (July–October) and the first (March–June) — a "**third quarter phenomenon**." Pole crews 1992–1994 showed "a significant decline in the extent to which individuals asked others for advice or provided advice."
  - **Crew structure (South Pole, 1992–94):** one crew had a **clique** structure ("Biomed," "Library" and "Bar" groups of five each, six unaffiliated); one a **core-periphery** structure (16 core, 5 semi-periphery, 6 periphery); one a hybrid. The clique crew had significantly higher tension-anxiety, depression and anger all winter.
  - **Polar T3 syndrome:** circannual mood-disturbance peaks in November and July and trough in March matched seasonal serum TSH in a 22-person cohort (1996–97), linking cold-related thyroid change to the winter-over syndrome.
  - **Salutogenic findings:** among 657 men who wintered 1963–1974, non-sleep mood symptoms were *inversely* associated with station altitude, latitude and cold; 63.0% rose, 8.5% unchanged, 28.4% fell over winter. In 1991, McMurdo personnel scored significantly higher on depression measures than South Pole personnel. 328 Navy winter-overs had **20% fewer** first hospital admissions afterward than 2,396 screened volunteers not sent (neoplasms −73%, endocrine −60%, musculoskeletal −44%; mental disorders −36% and injuries −27%, not significant) (Palinkas 1986).
- **Palinkas et al. 1995**, "Seasonal variation of depressive symptoms in Antarctica," *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica* 91(6):423: depressive symptom scores rose over 12 months; summer depression and marital status predicted winter depression. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; the PDF found under that name was a different 1995 Palinkas paper on a Canadian Arctic expedition, not used).
- **2017 meta-analysis** of time-dependent mood in Antarctic personnel (*Polar Record*): no validated scale for the winter-over syndrome; several studies found no detrimental effect. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only).
- Other stations' studies (Maitri, Zhongshan, Halley: melatonin phase delay in winter) surfaced but are not South Pole studies and were not opened. **Out of scope / not used.**

## 4 · Systems

### 4.1 Power and fuel
- **Main plant (2023):** three **Caterpillar 3512B** engines, each driving a generator rated **750 kW** "after de-rating for altitude and fuel blend"; normally **one runs, one is on standby, one is down for maintenance**, rotated; a fourth 250 kW "peaking" generator was built in but is unused because one main set covers demand ("fits comfortably" within 750 kW). Output 3-phase 480/277 V, 60 Hz; outlying buildings (e.g. SPRESSO ~5 miles out) fed at 4,160 V. Some critical loads on UPS. The power plant uses a CO₂ fire-suppression system that would make the space "Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health," so entry requires sign-in and tag-in. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi "South Pole Electrical Infrastructure," 2023-08-20, https://brr.fyi/posts/south-pole-electrical-infrastructure). An older Raytheon Polar Services procedure (snippet, undated) gives the same three 3512B/750 kW sets and a 205 kW Cat 3306B peaking set. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Emergency Power Plant:** in the "Lifeboat" inside the elevated station; **two Cat 3406B engines, 250 kW each** (de-rated); black-start capable; own AN-8 day tanks and an external fueling connection; separate feeds to major load centers. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2023). B-Pod "can serve as an emergency 'lifeboat'." **SOURCED** (USAP webcam page).
- **Fuel:** primary fuel **AN-8** aviation fuel (an Antarctic blend); gasoline ("mogas") for snowmobiles and light vehicles; bulk AN-8 in the fuel arch; two large day tanks at the plant. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2023). Fuel arch: **45 tanks × 10,000 gallons = 450,000 gallons** (Polar Journal 2024). **SOURCED**. AN-8 "formulated to remain liquid at low temperatures"; "to withstand temperatures as low as −72 °F" (snippets). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Annual fuel:** "Summer resupply (November to mid-February) brings **up to 450,000 gallons** of fuel" (NSF station page). **SOURCED**. Other statements: "nearly 450,000 gallons ... annually"; "over half a million gallons"; a 2012 planning slide ~550,000 gal/yr (all snippets). **UNVERIFIED**. **NOT FOUND:** an official annual consumption figure split by use (power plant vs vehicles vs aircraft refueling).
- **Load:** an Argonne/NREL study (Bender et al., *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews*, reported 2024-04-08 by Nick Oakes, via techxplore PDF) modeled replacing "95% of the diesel fuel needed to supply **170 kW** of power at the South Pole station," saving ~$57 million over 15 years after a $9.7 million investment, payback "just over two years." "All of the energy at the South Pole currently is generated by diesel fuel and a generator" (Amy Bender). **SOURCED**. (Whether 170 kW is the whole-station average or a science load is not stated in the article; **not resolved**.)
- **Cost of air-delivered fuel:** "an LC-130 burning about **1.33 lb of fuel to deliver 1 lb** to Pole." **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2023, as the author's statement).
- **Heat recovery:** "Generator waste heat directly to heat the station" and to maintain the water supply. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2023). "The power plant must run continuously, since its diesel engines supply the station's heat"; staffed 24 h. **SOURCED** (memoir, §3.2). Lifeboat heat from emergency-plant waste heat plus AN-8 boilers. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi).
- **Power budget as a limit:** new science projects "must fit within the plant's capacity," a concern for large projects. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2023).

### 4.2 Water (the Rodriguez well) and melt
- **Rodwell principle:** a meltwater cavity in the ice, heated with generator waste heat; concept developed by Army engineer **Raul Rodriguez for Camp Century, Greenland, 1960–61**; wells last ~seven years, ending when the cavity becomes too deep to heat and recirculate efficiently. **UNVERIFIED** (Wikipedia "Rodriguez well" via snippet; LBNL page https://glacier.lbl.gov/gtp/SouthPole/rod_well.html not opened).
- **Current system (2024):** the current well is **Rodwell 3**; **Rodwell 4** is planned for freshwater, after which Rodwell 3 becomes the sewer outfall; wells are drilled "several hundred feet" into the snow; each is used for several years until it reaches "millions of gallons," then converted to wastewater disposal. Raw water ~36 °F out, ~42 °F at the plant; return water heated to ~84 °F; heat from the power plant via a glycol loop, with backup boilers. Treatment: limestone contactors, sediment and carbon filters, soda ash, calcium hypochlorite. Two unpressurized bulk tanks of several thousand gallons each; a pressurized distribution tank; a separate pressurized fire-sprinkler tank in the elevated station; water lifted ~50 ft up the "beer can." **Showers are restricted to two minutes.** Snow at depth is about −60 °F. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi "South Pole Water Infrastructure," 2024-06-07). (The page's figure for the energy to heat one gallon, as returned by the fetch tool, was garbled and is not used.)
- **Well life and risk (2024):** the well "supplies thousands of gallons of water daily" by melting "a bulb in the ice hundreds of meters below the surface"; "the current well should last three more years"; replacement at least 2–3 years away; without a new well, water use would be limited to essential functions, possibly forcing a cut in summer staffing. **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024).
- **Earlier method:** the Dome-era power plant had a "snow harvesting conveyor" to keep the plant's water tank full, which "did not work." **SOURCED** (dome2). A "snow mine" was the water source in the 1990s (spores from snow-mine water caused mold in early greenhouse attempts). **SOURCED** (greenhouse1 page).
- **DERIVED (standard physical constants, not a source figure):** melting 1 kg of snow from −51 °C (−60 °F) to water at 10 °C takes about 2.1 × 51 + 334 + 4.19 × 10 ≈ 483 kJ, i.e. ≈ 0.51 kWh per US gallon (3.785 kg). Most of it (~69%) is the latent heat of melting.

### 4.3 Waste and sewage
- **Sewage:** "Wastewater is not treated. Raw sewage is injected into old Rodwells," the "sewage outfall"; "millions of gallons of raw, untreated wastewater are stored underground beneath the station"; no treatment plant planned or budgeted as of June 2024, though the 2024 draft master plan recommends one; two holding pits (A and B pods) force-discharge at high flow; B-pod pit can discharge to a small emergency outfall; heat-traced, insulated lines. Outbuildings have no plumbing (porta-potties, dry toilets, buckets, bottles). **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2024). A new sewer-outfall project scheduled for 2024 was behind schedule. **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024).
- **Solid waste:** "All non-sewage waste must go back to McMurdo for shipment off the continent"; backlog on berms being buried ("bermageddon"); buried at the Pole over ~70 years: "the original station, construction equipment, and a cargo plane"; 2022–23 cleanup suspended for budget in 2024. **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024).
- **Utility tunnels:** fresh-water and wastewater piping run in under-snow utility tunnels (~3,071 ft in 2009). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).

### 4.4 Heating
- Station heat comes from **power-plant waste heat** (glycol loop), with AN-8 boilers as backup (brr.fyi 2023, 2024). **SOURCED**. **NOT FOUND:** a heat-load figure in kW for the elevated station.

### 4.5 Food
- **Stores:** the logistics arch holds warehouse space for "**nearly two years of food**." **SOURCED** (Polar Journal 2024). In winter "crew eat frozen food stockpiled over the summer"; fresh salad crops are not supplied because they cannot be frozen or dried. **SOURCED** (University of Arizona CEAC page, https://www.ceac.arizona.edu/research/south-pole-growing-chamber). NSF: 24,354 meals served annually. **SOURCED** (NSF station page; the figure is as printed). Galley staffing in winter: supervisor, two production cooks, a steward. **SOURCED** (memoir, §3.2).
- **Greenhouse (South Pole Food Growth Chamber):** built by the University of Arizona's Controlled Environment Agriculture Center; completed and delivered **February 2004**, assembled by Raytheon Polar Services; a volunteer-run chamber existed since 1992; it supplements the frozen diet with salad crops and serves as "a humid, bright space where crew can relax." **SOURCED** (CEAC page). Facility: **14 × 28 ft (≈392 sq ft)**; two-thirds production room with enriched CO₂, wall-to-wall hydroponic tanks on sliding drawers, water-cooled sodium lamps at ~⅓ of full sun; one-third "enviro-room" with higher humidity; earlier attempts: Siple's hydroponics 1957 (a philodendron survived, edible crops molded), 1960–61 bean plants, a converted paint locker 1990, an annex-roof structure 1994–95. **SOURCED** (southpolestation.com/trivia/00s/greenhouse1.html). Other size figures in circulation: 250 sq ft, 20 × 30 ft, 45 m² (snippets). **UNVERIFIED**; conflict not resolved. Output: 375 lb in one season (2011), "a little more than a half-pound per person per week"; a record 167 lb harvest in one week (snippets). **UNVERIFIED**. The station page calls it "a food growth chamber." **SOURCED** (NSF).

### 4.6 Communications
- **Why it is hard:** equatorial geostationary satellites are below the horizon at 90°S; "the extreme geographic latitude makes maintaining continuous communication links impossible." **SOURCED** (Lockheed Martin, 2016-10-11).
- **Main link:** U.S. Air Force **DSCS III B7** (launched 31 Jul 1995, 10-year design life), operational for the Pole in **June 2016**, replacing NSF's retired **GOES-3**; military X-band; **30 Mbps down / 10 Mbps up**; a daily contact window of **3.5 hours**, "increases annually"; 2.4 m VSAT terminals at Pole and Christchurch; traffic to the USAP hub in Denver; an upgrade would convert the inactive 9 m antenna to X-band DSCS. GOES-3 gave ~1.5 Mbps. **SOURCED** (USAP "DSCS Satellite," https://www.usap.gov/technology/4298/; Lockheed Martin release).
- **User experience (Oct 2023):** "a few hours a day" of link, the window shifting about 4 minutes earlier per day; individual speeds from a couple kbps up to ~2 Mbps; ~750 ms latency; jitter sometimes several seconds; operational traffic prioritized. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi "Engineering for Slow Internet," 2024-05-30). USAP webcam page: satellite coverage about 9 hours per day, shifting ~4 minutes earlier each day. **SOURCED**. (The 3.5 h DSCS window and the 9 h total coverage are different measures — the main high-bandwidth link vs all satellites; the pages do not reconcile them.)
- **Other paths:** TDRS satellites, Iridium (email under 100 KB in earlier years), Skynet MILSATCOM (named on the USAP DSCS page). **SOURCED** for Skynet (USAP); others **UNVERIFIED** (Wikipedia).
- **Starlink:** NSF tested Starlink at McMurdo (Sept 2022) and announced it for McMurdo and Palmer; as of Oct 2023 "no similar announcement for South Pole Station." **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2024). **NOT FOUND:** any confirmation of Starlink service at the Pole (searched 2026-10-29; future.usap.gov **dead at the source**, DNS failure).

### 4.7 Science facilities
- **Sectors:** the area around the Pole is divided into **Quiet** (grid southeast; seismology and radio), **Clean Air** (grid northeast, upwind; NOAA's Atmospheric Research Observatory), **Dark** (grid northwest, ~1 km; kept free of light and EMI; telescopes), and **Downwind** sectors. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets from several pages; the one opened page, southpole.era.gs, confirms only that the Dark Sector is kept "free of both light and Electromagnetic Interference" and that MAPO is about 1 km from the main station). NSF lists the Martin A. Pomerantz Observatory, the Atmospheric Research Observatory, the South Pole Telescope, seismic instruments, and IceCube. **SOURCED** (NSF station page).
- **IceCube Neutrino Observatory:** **5,160 DOMs** (10-inch PMTs) on **86 strings** between **1,450 and 2,450 m** depth, 125 m string spacing, 60 DOMs per string, 17 m vertical spacing; DeepCore (8 central strings) lowers threshold to ~10 GeV; **IceTop** 81 surface stations; built over seven austral summers 2004–2011, last string lowered **18 Dec 2010**; holes ~60 cm wide melted by hot water at ~2 m/min, one hole about every two days, ~11 h to deploy each string; 2,500 m of 10 cm high-pressure hose; construction cost **$279 million** (NSF ~$242 million). **SOURCED** (https://icecube.wisc.edu/science/icecube/detector). Winter crew: two (see §3.2).
- **South Pole Telescope:** 10 m dish (an "8 m" figure also appears in a snippet); two-person winter crew; duties include cryogenic fridges, optics, motor replacement, bearing greasing in the cold. **SOURCED** for crew and duties (Ferguson 2023); dish size **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **BICEP/Keck:** BICEP3 in the Dark Sector Lab and the Keck Array in MAPO (2014 description, snippet). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Why here:** "Clear, dry air and the absence of light pollution make unique astronomical observations possible"; research spans astronomy, astrophysics, aeronomy, auroral studies, geospace, meteorology, geomagnetism, seismology, earth tides, glaciology. **SOURCED** (NSF station page). The nearest artificial light in winter is McMurdo, ~1,355 km away. **SOURCED** (southpole.era.gs).

### 4.8 Fire and emergency practice
- **Winter:** the fire brigade is a volunteer force of crew members with one week of training — "the only firefighting force during winter" (memoir); 2023 fire team trained by the Denver Fire Department (Ferguson 2023). **SOURCED**.
- **Summer:** a professional crew of **seven firefighters and two dispatchers**, October–February, mainly on standby for aircraft (sometimes 12+ hours at the runway); two covered sleds carrying ~7,500 L of foam and Purple-K dry chemical, towed by a tractor, kept heated because foam must stay above −40 °C; the summer crew trains the winter brigade. **SOURCED** (Hakai Magazine, 2024-04-01). Professional firefighters ("Station 3") added in the 21st century; dry chemicals preferred over water in the cold (Wikipedia citing Antarctic Sun 2007, 2011). **UNVERIFIED** (secondary).
- **Refuge:** B-Pod / the "Lifeboat" with the Emergency Power Plant, independent heat and fuel. **SOURCED** (USAP webcam page; brr.fyi 2023). A fire-sprinkler tank serves the elevated station. **SOURCED** (brr.fyi 2024).
- **Historical fires:** garage fire 13 Oct 1964 destroyed three tractors (igy1); two construction-camp fires 30–31 Oct 1976, one destroying the galley/head complex (Dry Gulch). **SOURCED**.
- **Medical:** a physician and a PA or NP in winter (memoir); telemedicine consultation (2003 medevac page). **SOURCED**. On-site surgery capacity is limited (2003: gallbladder surgery "judged too complex" for the clinic). **SOURCED**.

## 5 · Supply

**Key primary source for this item:** Paul Thur (Traverse Operations Manager, Antarctic Support Contract), "Traversing in the Antarctic," USAP Polar Technology Conference, 5 April 2012 (https://sundowner.colorado.edu/seefeldt/ptc-2005_2016/2012_pdf/PTC_2012_Thur.pdf , read via pdftotext). Cited below as **Thur 2012**.

### 5.1 Demand
- "South Pole requires **~550,000 gallons** of fuel delivered per year; represents **~148 LC-130 missions**." **SOURCED** (Thur 2012). NSF (2024/2026): summer resupply "brings up to **450,000 gallons**." **SOURCED**. (The two differ by date and definition; not reconciled.)
- **DERIVED:** 550,000 ÷ 148 ≈ **3,716 gallons per LC-130 fuel mission**; Thur's near-term plan of "220,000 to 250,000 gallons = 59 to 67 flights" gives the same ≈3,730 gal/flight. A snippet stating that LC-130s "can only deliver 1000 gallons of fuel per flight" (CIRES UAV blog) conflicts with this and is **UNVERIFIED**.

### 5.2 The South Pole Traverse (SPoT)
- **Route:** **1,030 miles** one way (Thur 2012, with segment lengths **27 mi McMurdo Ice Shelf · 621 mi Ross Ice Shelf · 88 mi Leverett Glacier · 296 mi Polar Plateau**); the Leverett Glacier climb is **87.66 miles with 6,903 ft of elevation gain**; ~10% grade at the Leverett headwall requires shuttling loads. **SOURCED**. Wikipedia gives 995 mi (1,601 km) from the 2008 Antarctic Sun and notes growth to 1,028–1,030 mi as ice moves. **UNVERIFIED** (secondary).
- **Terrain named in Thur 2012:** the **McMurdo Shear Zone** — a 3.5-mile crossing remediated by CRREL in 2002–03, "~40 crevasses," "blasting and filling of 2 to 3 crevasses each year is required prior to crossing"; the two sides move ~2.3 ft/day (McMurdo Ice Shelf) and ~3.75 ft/day (Ross Ice Shelf), so the trail has moved 1.45 and 2.37 miles downstream in nine years and lengthened 0.31 mi; "Shoals of Intractable Funding" (where Reedy Glacier outflow merges); "Dornick region" (1–2 ft sastrugi at 3–4 ft wavelength, "extremely rough on equipment"); "RIS swamp" (several hundred miles of soft snow, 6–12 in ruts); "Lakes district" (hard-packed); Leverett headwall (accumulation >2–3 ft/yr); "Sastrugi National Park" (4–5 ft sastrugi, 15–20+ ft wavelength); "Plateau swamp" (several hundred miles of soft snow, >12 in ruts). **SOURCED**.
- **History:** proof-of-concept 2002–2006; first arrival at the Pole **23 December 2005** (Wikipedia citing Antarctic Sun). **UNVERIFIED** (secondary). On the 2004–05 attempt the team met crevasses "as many as 10 in a single mile" near the Transantarctic Mountains; ASTER satellite imagery was used to find safe waypoints; tractors reached the plateau 4 January 2005 and turned back; one crevasse ("Mongo") was 9.8 m wide and 25 m deep. **SOURCED** (NASA Earth Observatory, Michon Scott, 2005-08-30). Earlier, a 1991 tractor fall into a hidden crevasse is cited as the reason all Pole supply was flown (snippet). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Season window:** "Oct. 25 to Feb. 10 — deep-field traverse season ~110 days." The early limit is rigid because of the "on-continent fixed-wing Medevac requirement" and "early season extreme temperatures on the Polar Plateau (<−40 °F until mid Nov.)"; the late limit is flexible (fixed-wing medevac on continent until ~18 Feb; ~2 weeks of winterization can be done by the winter crew). **SOURCED** (Thur 2012).
- **Trip timing:** planned round trip **~58 days** (30 southbound, 7 turnaround at the Pole, 21 northbound); actual 2012–13: SPoT1 57 days (31/7/19), SPoT2 55 days (29/7/19); turnaround maintenance between traverses up to 2 weeks. Speeds: with ground-penetrating radar 7 mph max; heavily loaded without GPR ~8–10 mph; 10 mph max even northbound. **SOURCED** (Thur 2012). A 2026 secondary article (ScienceBlog "The Long View," 2026-09-17, citing NSF documents) gives an average round trip of ~52 days, "about three round trips per typical season," 8–10 people per trip. **SOURCED to a secondary article** (its underlying NSF figures were not opened).
- **Crew (2012):** first traverse 10 (field supervisor, mountaineer, shop foreman, 3 heavy-equipment mechanics, 4 operators); second traverse 8. **SOURCED** (Thur 2012).
- **Fleet:** each traverse platform: **4 Caterpillar and 4 Case agricultural tractors**, 1 snow-groomer for GPR crevasse detection, 2 generators; support modules (kitchen/berthing, power/ablution, freezer, tool shed) need 2 tractors. Tractor fuel burn: **Case 3.46 gal/mile heavily loaded / 2.26 light**, ~25,000–29,000 lb drawbar pull; **Caterpillar 2.95 / 1.94 gal/mile**, ~25,000 lb; PistenBully/Prinoth ~1 gal/mile; generators run ~13 h/day; overall planning figure **~28 gallons per mile** for a traverse. Modifications: Arctic seals and fluids, plug-in and Espar heaters, double-pane windows, blades, cranes, winches. **SOURCED** (Thur 2012).
- **Sleds and bladders:** steel tanks replaced by plastic (HMW polyethylene) sleds and flexible fuel bladders; challenges: "breakages of plastic sheets in the field during each traverse," "fuel bladder reliability"; air-cushion pontoon cargo platforms tested — off-the-shelf pontoons' seams failed after 100 miles (2010–11), a custom system ran 1,600+ miles without failure (2011–12). **SOURCED** (Thur 2012). Two 3,000-gal bladders per sled, four sleds = 24,000 gal per group; bladder sleds about one-sixth the cost and one-tenth the weight of steel sleds (ScienceBlog 2026, secondary). **UNVERIFIED** at primary level.

### 5.3 LC-130 ski-aircraft
- Operated by the **New York Air National Guard's 109th Airlift Wing**; missions per season reported variously as ~400 (older), 200–250 (2017), 120 (a later season). **UNVERIFIED** (snippets; amc.af.mil **dead at the source**, HTTP 403).
- Flight McMurdo → Pole about 3 hours (Wikipedia), "3–4 hours" (ASAP study §1.8). **SOURCED** for 3–4 h (ASAP). First LC-130 Pole landing 30 Oct 1960, 1 h 54 min from McMurdo (igy1). **SOURCED**.
- **Payload:** elevated-station construction: ~24 million lb over 900+ flights (NSF 2022) → **DERIVED** ≈ 26,000 lb per flight; Thur uses a 26,000 lb allowable cabin load. **SOURCED** (Thur 2012).
- **Cold limits:** an NSF account (Antarctic Journal, NSF 98-41, snippet) describes the Pole "near −55 °C" as too cold for LC-130s at season opening; at about −60 °F the aircraft's ground contrail stops cargo loading (snippet). **UNVERIFIED** (the NSF page returned HTTP 404). **NOT FOUND:** a published LC-130 minimum operating temperature.
- **Season:** flights to the Pole roughly late October to mid/late February; "usually between February and October planes don't fly in or out because it is too dark and cold" (Kelly Falkner, NSF, 2016). **SOURCED** (AP 2016). NSF 2022: the Pole "is reachable by plane for only about three months per year." **SOURCED**. The 2003 medevac flight was "the first aircraft to arrive since February." **SOURCED**.
- **Incident:** an LC-130 stranded at the Pole in December 2022 after a tail-elevator control cable snapped; repaired on site (snippet, *Stars and Stripes* 2022-12-22). **UNVERIFIED**.

### 5.4 Airdrops
- **IGY era:** the 1956–57 station was built almost wholly by C-124 airdrop (first 25 Oct 1956; last 21 Feb 1957); a ~9-ton D-4 tractor dropped on five G-11 parachutes in 1959. **SOURCED** (igy1).
- **Midwinter airdrops:** "first midwinter airdrop at Pole" 1981; "these resupply drops lasted till 1995, and were discontinued due to email, supply system improvements, greenhouses, and budgets" (southpolestation.com snippet). **UNVERIFIED**. 1983 and 1984 midwinter drops by C-141B to McMurdo and the Pole (snippets: *Air & Space Forces*; *The Press*, 23 Jun 1984). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **July 1999:** medical supplies for Dr. Jerri Nielsen airdropped by a C-141 (Lt Col Greg Pike). **SOURCED** (southpolestation airdrop06 page).
- **20 December 2006:** first airdrop at the Pole since July 1999 — a **C-17** (446th Airlift Wing, 14 crew) from Christchurch via McMurdo dropped four pallets of food, "just under 70,000 pounds," in one pass from ~1,000 ft, as a proof of concept for C-17 grid-mode navigation and of airdrop "as a viable emergency relief option for winter-over personnel." Summer 1991–92: a C-141 drop of construction material. **SOURCED** (southpolestation.com/trivia/00s/airdrop06.html).

### 5.5 Season windows (summary of sourced statements)
- Station: summer operations "begin in October and run until about early February, when the roughly eight-month winter begins" (USAP webcam page); "February and October at the South Pole, the stations are physically isolated" (Palinkas c. 2000); 2023 winter "mid-February to late October" (Ferguson). **SOURCED**.

**Key cross-station source:** COMNAP, *Antarctic Station Catalog*, version August 2017 (PDF https://static1.squarespace.com/static/61073506e9b0073c7eaaf464/t/611497cc1ece1b43f0eeca8a/1628739608968/COMNAP_Antarctic_Station_Catalogue.pdf ; 20.8 MB, downloaded to the scratchpad with curl because it exceeded the fetch tool's size limit; text read via pdftotext). ### 6.1 Camp Century, Greenland (U.S. Army, 1959–1967) — the under-ice precedent
**Primary source:** E. F. Clark, *Camp Century — Evolution of Concept and History of Design, Construction, and Performance*, CRREL Technical Report 174, 1965 (PDF https://icedrill.org/sites/default/files/477706.pdf , OCR text layer read via pdftotext; some OCR noise). Cited as **TR-174**.
- **Design criteria:** "a subsurface camp (constructed by use of the cut-and-cover trenching technique), with a capacity to house **100 personnel** on a year-round basis"; a nuclear plant "to provide electrical power and steam to operate the water well"; "maximum use ... of snow as a construction material"; "a design life of **10 years** (with appropriate maintenance)." Standard trenches ~24 × 24 ft. **SOURCED** (TR-174).
- **Reactor PM-2A** (ALCO Products): installed summer 1960; critical and operational **2 October 1960**; ran (apart from maintenance) until **9 July 1963**. Gross thermal **10,000 kW**; gross electric **2,000 kW**; net electric **1,560 kW**; process steam 1,000,000 Btu/hr; plant weight 310 short tons in 27 packages (each ≤ 9 × 9 × 30 ft, ≤ 30,000 lb); core life 17 months at full power; **crew 18 men**; plant cost $5.7 million through FY61; O&M 65 mills/kWh. In 1962: gross 5,388,900 kWh, **74.1% of time on line**, average gross demand **830 kW** while on line. An auxiliary plant of three 300-kW diesel generators covered shutdowns — and during shakedown "provided power for the entire camp for more than fifty percent of the time." Removal approved 2 Aug 1963 "primarily from plans to discontinue year-round operations ... to reduce Greenland R&D support costs." Radioactive liquid waste was discharged into the ice cap under a Danish-American agreement permitting "up to 50 millicuries per year." **SOURCED** (TR-174).
- **Water — the original Rodriguez well:** installed summer 1960; a diesel-fired steam generator (standby) and the reactor's 10⁶ Btu/hr as primary heat; "easily produced sufficient water to satisfy all camp requirements"; the first well passed **500 ft** depth (near the pump's maximum head) and was relocated in May 1962 after ~3.5 × 10⁶ gallons (OCR shows "3.5 x 10 gallons"; exponent lost); "more than 5 × 10⁶ gallons" from the new well by Sept 1964; water "better in quality than water obtained by triple distillation in glass." **SOURCED** (TR-174; the monthly consumption figure is garbled in the OCR and is not used).
- **Snow trimming — the cost of living in ice:** "the amount of snow which has to be trimmed and removed from Camp Century each year in order to maintain the original cross section of the trenches is estimated to be on the order of **20,000 cubic yards**," ≈0.173 yd³/min over a 240-day, 8-hour work-year; a mechanical system was estimated at $0.4 million. Trenches deformed, "Wonder Arch" steel arches distressed; trimming over 12-ft-wall buildings began "even before the rest of the camp had been completed." **SOURCED** (TR-174).
- **Conclusions (verbatim points):** "Subsurface camps on ice caps are feasible and practicable, but only in environments as cold or colder than Century"; "Roughly **10 years is the maximum feasible design life** for an undersnow camp in environments similar to Century"; "Means for a continuous program of trimming the snow trenches should be provided in initial planning"; sites should be crevasse-free with surface slopes not over 2%, "as greater slopes could be indicative of rapid visco-elastic flow rates." Advantages claimed for subsurface camps: lower initial cost, avoided surface environment, less heating fuel, fewer imported materials, minimized drifting. **SOURCED** (TR-174).
- **Later record:** construction June 1959 – October 1960, ~$8 million; ~6,000 tons of supplies; heavy loads hauled from Thule at 2 mph (70-hour trip); "26 tunnels totaling almost two miles"; a well giving "10,000 gallons of fresh water daily"; the Army stopped operating it in 1966 and the tunnels "quickly collapsed"; Project Iceworm (planned missile tunnels) canceled 1963 partly over ice instability. **SOURCED** (Atomic Heritage Foundation, 2018-07-19). AGU press release (2016-08-04) on Colgan, Machguth, MacFerrin, Colgan, van As & MacGregor 2016 (*GRL*, doi 10.1002/2016GL069688): housed **85 to 200** soldiers; main trench 400 m, more than 12 side trenches of ~150 m; now ~35 m deeper under snow; waste over 55 ha: **200,000 L diesel**, **240,000 L wastewater**, suspected PCBs, radioactive coolant of unknown volume; the site could turn from net snowfall to net melt "as early as 2090." **SOURCED**. A 2021 reassessment found meltwater never penetrated more than ~1.1 m and remobilization before 2100 is not expected (Wikipedia citing it). **UNVERIFIED** (secondary).

### 6.3 Concordia (Dome C; France–Italy)
- **Position/elevation:** 75°05′59.9″S 123°19′57.4″E; **3,233 m**; ~1,100 km from the coast; one of 3 inland year-round stations (with Amundsen-Scott and Vostok). **SOURCED** (COMNAP 2017).
- **Climate:** mean annual −52.1 °C; February −43.7; July −64.2; mean wind 10.8 km/h, max 114.8 km/h; "temperatures hardly rise above −25 °C in summer and can fall below −80 °C in winter with record of **−84.6 °C reached in 2010**." **SOURCED** (COMNAP 2017). A newer reading of −84.1 °C on 18 July 2026 (Discover Magazine, snippet). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **People:** summer 35 staff + 35 scientists, **max 80**, 80 beds; **winter 8 staff + 5 scientists (13)**; medical: 2 in winter, capability "Basic, Dental, Surgery," 120 m² medical facility; distance to hospital 5,000 km; closest emergency facility in Antarctica 1,100 km. Area under roof 3,605 m²; labs 748 m²; logistics 2,856 m². **SOURCED** (COMNAP 2017).
- **Built and powered:** IPEV and PNRA built it 1999–2005; "continuously occupied since that time"; power "fossil fuel," 230 V, 24 h. Vehicles: one 4WD, two snow groomers, five skidoos, a tracked loader, a telehandler, a summer tractor, bicycles. **SOURCED** (COMNAP 2017). **NOT FOUND:** Concordia generator capacity and annual fuel use.

### 6.4 Vostok (Russia)
- **Position/elevation/ice:** 78°28′S 106°48′E; **3,488 m**; at "the 'Pole of Cold'"; ice **3,700 m** thick with ~120 m of snow-firn; bed ~200 m below sea level; precipitation 50 mm ("ice needles, diamond dust"); mean annual **−55.4 °C**; mean wind 19.44 km/h. Opened **16 December 1957**. Old infrastructure: "four capital structures with the design DES power of **270 kW** and the oil tank capacity of **200 tons**." Summer 30, **winter 15**, 30 beds; no showers (2017); medical: 2 staff in winter, capability "Basic, Dental, Surgery"; access "Airplane, Sledge tractor traverse"; runway 3,000 × 80 m; flights Jan–Mar and Oct–Dec. **SOURCED** (COMNAP 2017).
- **Record cold:** **−89.2 °C at 02:45 UT on 21 July 1983** (Turner et al. 2009, *JGR*, via snippet; Guinness via snippet). **UNVERIFIED** at primary level (widely consistent).
- **The 1982 fire:** at 4 a.m. on **12 April 1982** a fire swept the powerhouse (~15 minutes); "all three diesel engines and their backups were destroyed"; one person killed; the station lost power, heat and light, and without heat the crew could not melt snow for water; an old diesel from an ice-coring project powered a radio; a scrapped diesel dug out of the snow was restored over ~two months; "rescue was impossible for eight months"; after **227 days** the **19 survivors** were relieved by tractor from the coast; 20 people were on station. **SOURCED** (Michael Ashley, UNSW, *The Conversation*, 2013-12-15). Cause a short circuit; victim named as mechanic Alexey Karpenko (Russian-language snippets). **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Supply history:** historically by tractor traverse from **Mirny** ("the Mirny-Vostok-Mirny route"). **SOURCED** (COMNAP 2017, Mirny entry). Now from **Progress**: the 2022–23 season used a **1,480 km** route, **90 drivers and 45 vehicles (40 PistenBully Polar 300, 5 Challenger MT865)**, **17 traverses** (12 construction, 5 fuel), **2,486 tons** delivered; the last traverse left Vostok 14 Feb 2023 "when night temperatures were already reaching −50 °C." 166 construction workers flew in by Il-76 from Cape Town to Progress's airfield; 22 workers wintered in 2023. **SOURCED** (Russian Federation, ATCM45 Background Paper 27, 2023-04-28, PDF via southpolestation.com, read via pdftotext). 2021–22: 1,200 t of construction material; 30 people at the station in winter 2024; dedication planned for January 2025. **SOURCED** (southpolestation.com "The new Vostok," compiling ATCM reports). About one-third of fuel towed from Progress to Vostok is burned by the tractors (2018 expedition diary, snippet). **UNVERIFIED**. **NOT FOUND:** confirmation of the new station's first full wintering (2025).
- **The new wintering complex:** 140 m long, 13.5 m wide, max 17.5 m high; >1,911 m²; walls insulated 80 cm; **on 36 supports, each 3 m high**; capacity **15 winter / 35 summer**; five modules; **four 200-kW diesel generators** plus two separate backups; solar panels under test; "a **two-year supply of fuel and food**"; "all systems have double or triple redundancy." The old station was reconstructed in 1974 and 1982 and "conserved three times." **SOURCED** (AARI, https://aari.ru/science-and-projects/new-wintering-vostok-station).

## FINAL · Factual summary and the NOT FOUND list

### Factual summary (real world only; each point carries its mark in the section cited)
1. **The place (§1).** The station sits at 90°S on ice ~2,880 m thick under the IceCube array (radar, Hills et al. 2022), at ~2,835 m elevation. The ice moves ~10 m/yr toward 40°W (grid northwest, toward the Weddell Sea), so the geographic-pole marker is reset every 1 January; the winter crew designs it. Open-field accumulation is ~23 cm of snow (~8.5 cm water equivalent) a year, but drift around structures is what buries things. Daily mean temperatures run about −27 °C in December–January and a flat −57 to −60 °C from April to September; record low −82.8 °C (June 1982), record high −12.3 °C (25 Dec 2011). Average wind is ~11 knots; blowing snow starts at ~15 knots and whiteout at ~25 knots; peak gust 48 knots. The sun sets ~22 March and rises ~20 September, with ~81 days of full astronomical darkness (2024). The station is at ~74° magnetic latitude: polar cap at magnetic night, cusp at magnetic midday, aurora can be watched around the clock. Average pressure altitude is ~3,200 m (to ~3,690 m), and 52% of 246 arriving workers met acute-mountain-sickness criteria.
2. **History and form (§2).** There have been three generations. (a) The 1956–57 IGY station was built by airdrop and buried within years; by 1975 its crew lived 20–30 ft down, and it was later demolished. (b) The 1975 Dome, 164 ft across, was flown in in small pieces and built against snow load and ~100 mph winds. It was replaced because it sank, had to be dug out every year at the cost of fuel and crew time, and was too small. (c) The 2008 elevated station is an airfoil on jackable columns, designed to be raised two full stories (3.7 m each). It took ~24 million lb of cargo, 900+ flights, ~10 years and ~100 building days a year. Snow is now ~two stories above its 2007 level and it has never been raised. The 1970s and late-1990s arches (power, fuel, logistics) are buried and deforming, with no replacement project (2024).
3. **People (§3).** Up to ~150 people in summer; 39–50 in recent winters (45 in 2026; peak 86 in 2005). 1,802 people have filled 2,252 winter positions since 1957. Winter roles and selection follow 45 CFR 675: a psychological evaluation for winter candidates, and a full PQ before each Pole winter. Three midwinter evacuation flights have been made (2001, 2003, 2016), all Twin Otters out of Rothera; the 1999 and 2011 cases waited for October. Palinkas's research documents a winter-over syndrome, a South Pole "third-quarter" effect, worse moods in clique-structured crews, and some salutogenic findings.
4. **Systems (§4).** Three Cat 3512B / 750 kW generators run one at a time, plus a 2 × 250 kW emergency plant in the B-Pod "Lifeboat." The fuel is AN-8, with 450,000 gal of storage and a summer resupply of up to 450,000 gal. Generator waste heat warms the station and melts the Rodwell water. Showers are limited to 2 minutes. Raw sewage goes into old Rodwells, and all other waste returns to McMurdo. Food: frozen stores of nearly two years, plus a 14 × 28 ft hydroponic chamber. The main link is DSCS-3 B7: 30/10 Mbps for ~3.5 h a day, ~750 ms latency for users, and no confirmed Starlink. IceCube has 5,160 sensors at 1,450–2,450 m, with sectored dark, clean-air and quiet zones. In winter a volunteer fire brigade is the only fire force.
5. **Supply (§5).** The South Pole Traverse runs 1,030 miles over the shelf, the Leverett Glacier and the plateau. It operates 25 Oct – 10 Feb, takes ~55–58 days per round trip, uses 4 Case and 4 Cat tractors per platform, and burns ~28 gal/mile. It delivered 56,000–933,000 lb per season (2005–2012 table), and recent seasons average ~300,000 gal (secondary source). Delivered per lb of fuel burned: ~1.6 by traverse vs 0.88 by LC-130. Historical demand was ~550,000 gal/yr, or ~148 LC-130 missions. Planes don't fly February–October. Midwinter airdrops ran ~1981–1995 (UNVERIFIED); there were medical drops in 1999, and a 70,000-lb C-17 food drop in 2006 as an emergency-relief test.
6. **Ice and altitude installations (§6).** Camp Century (1959–67) was designed for 100 people and a 10-year life. Its reactor gave 1,560 kW net, and it had to trim ~20,000 yd³ of snow a year; the conclusion was that "roughly 10 years is the maximum feasible design life." The other plateau stations, all diesel and traverse-supplied: Concordia (3,233 m, 13 winter, ~470 t/season by traverse in 2003); Vostok (3,488 m; a fire in 1982 destroyed all generators, and 19 survived 227 days until relief; a new complex on 36 × 3 m supports with 4 × 200 kW diesels, supplied by 17 traverses / 2,486 t from Progress in 2022–23); Dome Fuji (3,810 m, intermittent winters); and Kunlun (4,087 m, summer only).
