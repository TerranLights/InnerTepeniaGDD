## 1. Submarine-cable and telegraph relay stations as communities

### 1.1 Porthcurno, Cornwall (Eastern Telegraph Co. -> Cable & Wireless)
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://pkporthcurno.com/discover-pk/our-story/): shore end of first cable laid 7 June 1870; Eastern Telegraph Company formed 1872; Cable & Wireless Ltd formed 1934; by the start of WWII "14 cables coming ashore, carrying some 70% of all communications"; wartime traffic rose from "approximately 231,000,000 words" (1938) to "705,000,000 words" (1944); telegraph operation ceased 1970 ("100 years after it had started"); training college operated "until 1993"; access to the valley in wartime restricted: "only holders of a Porthcurno Pass could come and go."
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PK_Porthcurno): station "started with a staff of 16 young men," described as "often badly behaved and ill-disciplined" (Wikipedia wording; not independently verified).
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porthcurno): "the largest submarine cable station in the world, with the capacity to receive and transmit up to two million words a day" (inter-war); Cable & Wireless engineering college opened 1950; closed 1993 "because of its isolation"; the claim that over 90% of inhabitants worked for or depended on Cable & Wireless is tagged "citation needed" in the article, so UNVERIFIED. Tunnels: two parallel main tunnels with two cross-tunnels, dug by local tin miners from June 1940, about 15,000 tons of rock removed; a fifth tunnel is the escape route.
- SOURCED-SNIPPET (search, extended mode, pages: atlantic-cable.com/CableCos/Porthcurno via search result summary; pkporthcurno.com oral-history items): in 1877 the station employed "four married men and 32 bachelors"; around 1900 "some 150 staff and trainees"; permanent clerks lived in houses adjoining the station, "given to them rent-free by the Company" (an 1885 clerk's account); a tennis court was provided; a cricket and football field was described in 1885; children of overseas staff attended the local school and there were Christmas parties (oral history, Barbara Lever, PK Porthcurno). Operators trained at Porthcurno were "dispatched overseas at a salary of 52 pounds per year." A training school for telegraph operators existed by 1909. Trainees: "mainly men from across the world."
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://www.warhistoryonline.com/war-articles/bunker.html): about 200 Cornish tin miners built the tunnels; cost "around 21,000 pounds"; 120-step escape staircase to the cliff top; armed soldiers guarded the entrances. (Slow Travel, https://www.slow-travel.uk/post/porthcurno-bunker, opened): staff "working in shifts for 24 hours a day"; side rooms were "workshops, sleeping and washing accommodation"; staff kept their roles secret and locals were told "a shortcut to the local pub was being built"; a 500 lb bomb that fell on a nearby farm in May 1941 is displayed. Historic England listing (via search summary, page 403): tunnels declared open 31 May 1941; Pillbox Study Group (via summary, page failed on SSL): operating space about 150 ft x 26 ft x 23 ft. Step count differs by source (119 vs 120): minor conflict.
- NOT FOUND: any wartime or 1920s operator headcount at Porthcurno; any figure for how many people slept underground.

## 2. Optical telegraph and relay-runner systems

### 2.1 Chappe telegraph (France, from 1794)
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chappe_telegraph): towers "every 5 to 15 kilometers"; first Paris-Lille line 230 km, "fifteen towers"; Paris-Brest 58 stations; by 1844 "534 towers... linking the 29 most important cities... more than 5,000 km"; "The tower operators accounted for more than 90 percent of the system's personnel"; originally two operators per tower (one read signals, one ran the mechanism), later "often only one operator" because of funding; operators "worked 365 days per year" and were paid the same as a construction day-laborer (no amounts given); operators did not have the code book and could not decipher what they relayed; had to check that the next tower did not mistransmit; sanctioned for mistakes; inspectors oversaw "about ten towers", used military telescopes; Paris-Lille message "within 9 minutes"; Strasbourg-Paris 4 days by horse vs 2 hours by telegraph; early local towers were "destroyed during popular uprisings."
- SOURCED-SNIPPET (French-language search summary citing geneawiki, https://fr.geneawiki.com/wiki/Claude_CHAPPE): surnumeraires (last-grade auxiliaries) learned the trade unpaid and sometimes waited long for a post; Paris-Strasbourg line 52 stations in 1798; replaced by electric telegraph 1847-1854. A French-language source gives 535 stations in 1845.
- NOT FOUND (two searches, English and French): pay amounts; log-book formats; per-station headcount beyond the two-then-one pattern. Died at the sources (no digitized regulation text surfaced; the regulations likely sit in French archives).

### 2.2 Neighbors followed: other optical telegraphs
- **Prussian semaphore, Berlin-Koblenz (1832-1849).** SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_semaphore_system): 62 stations, six-armed masts; average 11 km apart (max 15, min 7.5); first section of 14 stations done November 1832; "frequent interruptions in visual contact during drizzly and gray weather" led to an added station in 1842; synchronization round trip Berlin-Koblenz-Berlin under two minutes in good weather. SOURCED-SNIPPET (heritage study via search): living quarters usually on the south side of stations; operated by day and in good visibility only; three dispatch offices (Berlin, Cologne, Koblenz) did encoding and decoding. NOT FOUND: staff per station, ranks and pay (Wikipedia says "not stated").
- **British Admiralty shutter telegraph, London-Portsmouth (1796-1814/15).** SOURCED-PAGE (https://www.alresford.org/displayed/displayed_21_3.php): "A journal was kept at each station, which was usually manned by two glass men and two ropes men"; another description "four or five men"; some shutters under a Royal Navy lieutenant, others a Foreman; "At important points such as the terminals there would be an extra hand"; "No living accommodation seems to have been provided"; speed: time signal London-Portsmouth "one minute," coded messages "15 and 17 minutes" in good visibility; in poor visibility urgent messages went by horse and rider between stations; night lanterns "conspicuously unsuccessful"; shutters dismantled 1814 (Napoleon to Elba) and replaced by semaphore after 1815. SOURCED-SNIPPET: a later semaphore station crew was a Royal Navy lieutenant and a handyman/signalman, often a retired sailor; semaphore line 1824 to December 1847.

### 2.3 Inca chasqui and tambo
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chasqui): relay houses (chaskiwasi) about 2.5 km apart; chroniclers conflict (Garcilaso one quarter league, about 1.25 km; Polo de Ondegardo one and a half leagues, about 7.5 km); "four to six slim, fit and young chasquis would stand in wait" per station; minimum four runners; Guaman Poma: runners on call day and night, "not to be missing over one hour"; Murua: runners tested by running across a plain, down a hill and up a rough slope, trained daily, lazy runners punished with "fifty blows"; patrol exchanged for a fresh one every month; fed from the Inca's storehouses; runners' families exempt from other duties (Guaman Poma); quipus carried "mainly numbers in an ordered form" and "There is no evidence that the chasquis could read the quipus"; speed estimates "up to 300 km per day," Betanzos news "five or six days"; tambos at "a day's journey interval" stored goods and lodged travelers. Speed estimates conflict across sources (150-300 km/day or more: UNRESOLVED as a range).

### 2.4 Mongol Yam

### 2.5 Persian chapar
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapar_Khaneh): Herodotus: 111 stations between Sardis and Susa (20 in Lydia and Phrygia, 28 Cappadocia, 3 Cilicia, 15 Armenia, 4 Matiene, 11 Cissia); "so many horses and men are set at intervals, each man and horse appointed for a day's journey"; Susa to Sardis 2,700 km in "nine days" vs about 90 days on foot (the "seven days" figure in some secondary sources is not confirmed in Herodotus); guard posts at river crossings and the Cilician gates. No staff counts per station in the article.

### 2.6 Roman cursus publicus
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursus_publicus): mansiones about 25 Roman miles apart with about two mutationes between; one stage about 8 and one third Roman miles; core personnel limited to administration and operation: veterinarians, wagon-wrights, grooms; couriers and drivers not part of the service; most recruited from the military (often ex-speculatores); Procopius: each stage had "forty horses and a number of grooms in proportion"; costs first compensated, later charged to inhabitants along routes as a munus, then moved to the fiscus around Nerva; Julian's pass limits (twelve to vicars, two to governors, four each to three proconsuls); diplomas (passes) listed holder, validity, means of travel, route, lodgings; abuses: free transport for families, forged and stolen passes; typical 38-62 miles (61-100 km) per day, emergency rider "160 km or more in a day"; Pertinax's accession news took over 63 days to reach Egypt; bad news marked with a feather on the messenger's spear, good with a laurel; Diocletian/Constantine split into cursus velox and cursus clabularis; Justinian dismantled most except the Persian route. SOURCED-SNIPPET: mutatio staff a stabularius and grooms (secondary site; unconfirmed).

### 5.1 NIST, Boulder (UTC(NIST))
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://www.nist.gov/node/1644636): "More than 20 atomic clocks are measured and tracked at the NIST laboratories in Boulder, Colorado"; typically "10 to 15" clocks in the ensemble; about two-thirds hydrogen masers, one-third cesium beam; one clock is a pivot and the others are compared every 12 minutes by multi-channel measurement systems; the AT1 algorithm gives no clock more than 30% weight and needs at least four clocks with non-zero weight; output is an external synthesizer steered from the pivot clock, 5 MHz and 1 PPS, distributed to the laboratories through pulse distribution amplifiers; Internet Time Service provides time of day; holdover about 1 ns per week for a hydrogen maser and 10 ns per week or less for high-performance cesium; leap seconds enter the time codes but not the physical signal. ### 5.6 Leap-second decision as governance

## 6. Ground stations and deep-space networks as shift communities

### 6.1 NASA Deep Space Network: operating model across the three complexes

## 7. Reference-standard custody

### 7.1 The International Prototype of the Kilogram (IPK) and its vault
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Prototype_of_the_Kilogram): IPK and six sister copies (K1, 7, 8(41), 32, 43, 47) are kept in a basement vault at the BIPM's Pavillon de Breteuil in "a controlled environment"; "Three independently controlled keys are required to open the vault"; the IPK sits under three nested bell jars, the copies and replicas under two, all stored in air; ten working copies in the BIPM calibration lab (eight routine, two special); verifications: "only three verifications," in 1889, 1948 and 1989, with a periodic verification campaign 1988-1992; the IPK "lost perhaps 50 micrograms of mass over the last century" relative to its official copies (a relative drift, not an absolute one: the article stresses that either could have changed); cleaning method developed 1939-1946: chamois soaked in equal parts ether and ethanol, then steam cleaning with bi-distilled water, settle 7-10 days before verification; cleaning removes 5 to 60 micrograms; after cleaning masses regain about 1.11 micrograms per month for three months then about 1 microgram per year; check standards such as K4 are not cleaned for routine calibrations; "Great care is exercised when transporting prototypes" (1984: K4 and K20 hand-carried on separate commercial flights); kilogram redefined in 2019 on physical constants. The article does not say who holds the keys now.
- SOURCED-PAGE (Physics World account of the 2010 opening, https://physicsworld.com/a/au-revoir-kilogram/): vault in the basement of the main lab building, three locks and a safe inside; "Three antique keys, held by citizens of different countries"; in 2010 the holders were the BIPM director (UK), the CIPM president (Germany) and a representative of the French Archives (who arrived an hour late because of a strike); attendees: CIPM members (18), BIPM staff and a few invited outsiders; stored in air because it may outgas in vacuum; the cylinder is about 39 mm high and 39 mm in diameter; visitors exclaimed "It's so small!"; at the end the three key holders recorded temperature and relative humidity and signed a document confirming they had verified the prototype and its official copies; the opening is a "century-old tradition" and the cylinder had defined the kilogram for 121 years (as of 2010).
- SOURCED-SNIPPET (LNE paper in French via search, partly garbled): in 1889 the three keys were assigned to the director of the Archives de France, the director of the BIPM and the president of the CIPM. SOURCED-SNIPPET (Wikipedia mirror summaries): the vault is opened at the annual CIPM meeting (not confirmed on the main Wikipedia page, which does not mention it: UNVERIFIED as "annual").
- SOURCED-PAGE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_Convention): Meter Convention signed in Paris 20 May 1875 by 17 states, created the BIPM as an intergovernmental organization; Pavillon de Breteuil "enjoys privileges and immunities"; as of 16 October 2024 the CGPM had 64 members and 37 associates; CIPM has eighteen members, each from a different state, France always one seat; BIPM employs "around 70 people"; custody of the historical IPK and the former international prototype of the meter (retired 1960). (Location is given as Saint-Cloud in Wikipedia, and as Sèvres by the BIPM itself: the Pavillon de Breteuil stands in the Parc de Saint-Cloud in the commune of Sèvres; naming conflict is cosmetic.)
