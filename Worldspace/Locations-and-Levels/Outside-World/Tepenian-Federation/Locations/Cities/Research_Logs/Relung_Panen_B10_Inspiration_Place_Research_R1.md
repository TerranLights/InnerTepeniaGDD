## 1 · Grange halls, United States

**Kind of pick:** an institution (the Order of Patrons of Husbandry, "the Grange") and its building type (the local Grange hall). Researched as the institution, plus three documented halls and one Grange-run fair: La Prairie Grange Hall No. 79 (Rock County, Wisconsin), the Maine halls, the Washington State Grange, and the Centre County Grange Encampment and Fair (Pennsylvania).

### Verdict on the claim
**Claim:** "Farmers' halls that served as the meeting place, dance hall and school of farm districts."
- **Meeting place and dance hall: CONFIRMED (SOURCED).** The La Prairie hall's upper room "is used for Grange meetings, for 'old time' parties with dances (it has historically boasted a good floor for dancing), and for 4-H plays" (NRHP nomination, 1976). It also held card parties, Sunday church services, Town Meetings and voting.
- **"School": NARROWED.** No source found shows Grange halls generally serving as the district's schoolhouse. What the sources do show: (a) the Grange's own education work happened in the hall: an elected Lecturer runs a program at every meeting (PA and NY State Grange lecturer pages, snippets only, UNVERIFIED), the La Prairie hall kept a library in the 1880s (its bookcases later became kitchen shelves, SOURCED), and Junior Grange (ages 5 to 15) meets there (SOURCED); (b) the traffic often ran the other way: over 30% of Washington's 200+ active halls in 2012 were buildings first built as schools, churches or community halls and taken over by a Grange (Taylor 2013, SOURCED from the abstract); (c) the Grange lobbied for school funding, for example Washington's Barefoot Schoolboy Act of 1895, which equalized school funding among districts (HistoryLink, SOURCED). Better wording: *a meeting hall, dance hall, polling place, and the place of the farm district's adult education.*

### A. Snapshot
- **What it is:** The National Grange of the Order of Patrons of Husbandry, a fraternal farm organization founded **December 4, 1867** in Washington, D.C., by seven men and one woman (Oliver Hudson Kelley, William Saunders, Francis M. McDowell, John Trimble, Aaron B. Grosh, John R. Thompson, William M. Ireland, Caroline Hall). First local Grange: Fredonia, New York, April 1868. [K1] SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Grange_of_the_Order_of_Patrons_of_Husbandry (read 2026-10-29).
- **Size:** peak **858,050 members in 1875**; about **140,000 members in 1,400 communities** in 2025; first net membership gain in nearly seven decades reported in 2022. SOURCED (same page).
- **Structure:** subordinate (local) Grange → Pomona Grange (usually county) → State Grange → National Grange (35 State Granges plus Potomac Grange No. 1). [K1, P7] SOURCED (same page).
- **Defining feature:** the local hall as a fraternal lodge for whole farm families, women included as full members and officers. Founding rules required that four of the elected offices (one third) be held by women; women paid a 50-cent initiation fee against three dollars for men. [K4, P9] SOURCED (same page).
- **Ritual:** seven degrees of membership with ceremonies built on the seasons and farm symbols; oaths, secret meetings and passwords borrowed from Freemasonry, partly to keep railroad agents out; officers in sashes and "jewels," the presiding officer titled "Worthy Master." Motto: "In necessariis unitas, in dubiis libertas, in omnibus caritas." [K2, K6, P6] SOURCED (same page).

### B. What else exists there (the institution's works)
- **Political work:** railroad rate regulation ("Granger Laws"); *Munn v. Illinois* (1876) held grain warehouses to be private businesses in the public interest and open to regulation (later narrowed by *Wabash v. Illinois*, 1886); Rural Free Delivery; Farm Credit System; temperance; the Seventeenth Amendment; women's suffrage. [K1, P7] SOURCED (Wikipedia, above).
- **Cooperatives:** about **30,000 mill, elevator and warehouse co-ops in 1877**, run on Rochdale principles; most failed by the 1880s through "incompetent and inexperienced management, a lack of capital, and embezzlement scandals." [K1, K9, P8] SOURCED (Wikipedia, above).
- **Washington State Grange (example of a strong state body):** founded September 10, 1889, at the Pioneer Store in La Camas (now Camas). Members: 1,219 in 36 chapters (1892) → fewer than 500 (1898, after the Panic of 1893) → 9,000 (1909) → about 17,000 (1912) → record 490 chapters (1937) → nearly 50,000 (1945) → 72,806 in 413 chapters (1982) → nearly 50,000 in 290+ Granges (early 2000s). Built a cooperative fire-insurance association (1894), the Grange Wholesale Warehouse Company (1918), and eight statewide co-ops over 20 years (mutual life insurance, a milling company, a livestock-marketing association); some ventures (chemurgy, i.e. potato and wheat processing; a property investment called the Rustic Inn) lost money. Political results it backed: direct primary (1907), woman suffrage (approved 1910), initiative/referendum/recall (1912), Public Utility Districts Act (1930) to bring rural electricity, Family Farm Water Act (1976). [K1, K8, K9, P7, P8] SOURCED: https://historylink.org/File/10717 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Grange Insurance Association:** traces to the 1890s Washington fire-insurance co-op; took that name in 1944; rebranded Granwest Insurance effective July 1, 2026. UNVERIFIED (search snippets from insurancebusinessmag.com and insurancejournal.com; not opened).
- **Decline factors** named by HistoryLink: farm consolidation; competition from other activities; Grange seen as the preserve of white Christian farmers, limiting minority membership; aging membership (members averaged over 50 in the mid-1960s). Wikipedia: membership fell 40% from 1992 to 2007 after the National Grange stopped offering insurance; farm population went from about one third of Americans in the early 1900s to under 2% by the 2010s. [K9] SOURCED.

### C. Cultural locations (named halls and events)
- **La Prairie Grange Hall No. 79**, Town Hall Road, near Janesville, Rock County, Wisconsin. Built **1874**; two stories, 32 × 50 ft, white clapboard. Ground floor: open room with collapsible voting booths on the wall, tables for Junior Grange and card parties, and the kitchen at the back. Upper floor: meeting and dance room, low stage built 1921 and enlarged 1929, and a painted "play curtain" covered with local advertising. Horse sheds and a seven-foot fence once stood outside. Hardwood floors "laid by the boys" in 1899. Indoor latrines added 1931. Oldest standing Grange hall in Wisconsin. **A joint Grange hall and town hall:** the Town gave the land and $700; the Grange built the hall; the Town owned a ground-floor room "to front on the road," which the Grange used as its dining room when the Town did not need it. In 1960 the town deeded its share to the Grange, keeping the right to hold town meetings and voting there. About 60 adult members in 1976, plus a Junior Grange; some children were fifth-generation members. [K4, K10, K13, P3, P4, P7] SOURCED: NRHP nomination by Nancy Belle Douglas, Rock County Historical Society, 31 Aug 1976, https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/ec5baa62-925b-4abc-9161-13b72459eb54 (PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29).
- **Maine Grange halls:** Maine had about **55,000 members** at the early-20th-century peak (more per capita than any other state) and **about 600 halls**; now **about 90 Granges and fewer than 3,000 members**, with **4 to 5 Granges closing each year**. Current uses: Dexter Grange and Trenton Grange as community theaters with concerts and open mic nights; Victor Grange (Fairfield) with 6,000+ participants at events in 2022–23; Valley Grange giving dictionaries to third-graders; Halcyon Grange (Blue Hill) with a commercial kitchen; **Starling Hall (Fayette)**, the first Maine building built specifically as a Grange, kept up by the nonprofit Friends of Starling Hall since 2014. Other halls named: Lamoine Grange, Fairview Grange (Smithfield). [K9, K10, K13, P4] SOURCED: https://www.bangordailynews.com/2024/08/04/homestead/preserving-maine-grange-halls-joam40zk0w/ (read 2026-10-29).
- **Washington:** 200+ buildings in active use as Grange halls in 2012; purpose-built halls "often constructed on donated land, with materials and labor contributed by grange members." [K8, K14, P8] SOURCED (abstract): Holly Anne Taylor, *Grange Halls in Washington State: A Critical Investigation of a Vernacular Building Type*, M.A. thesis, University of Washington, 2013, https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/5810254b-33c7-444a-affe-6d659e8ba230/full.
- **Happy Valley Grange (Redmond, Washington):** first organized in 1909 at the Happy Valley School House; the hall stands next to that schoolhouse site; a social center into the 1970s and still politically active (salmon, flood control) in 2003. [K10] SOURCED: https://historylink.org/File/7516 (read 2026-10-29).
- **Centre County Grange Encampment and Fair, Centre Hall, Pennsylvania** (a Grange-founded fair): first held **September 24, 1874** in Leech's Woods as the "Granger's Pic-nik" (about 3,000 people), organized by Leonard Rhone; tents from 1877 (lent by the Pennsylvania National Guard; 28 tents in 1877, 200 by 1910); moved in 1890 to Grange Park, bought by the Centre County Pomona Grange (26 acres, 70 by 1923, now 264); now about **1,000 tents and 1,500 RVs**, 7,000 exhibit items, livestock sales; first amusement ride 1915; cancelled 1943 (one-day picnic held instead) and 2020. A permanent committee has run it since 1890; from 1914 county Granges and rural groups could appoint committee members. Several sources call it the last remaining tenting fair in the U.S. (UNVERIFIED as a superlative; snippets only). [K2, K3, K4, K12, P4] SOURCED: https://centrehistory.org/article/grange-fair/ (read 2026-10-29).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Because the Grange was a lodge for whole families with women as officers, the hall has a kitchen and dining room, a dance floor and a stage for plays, and a children's branch (Junior Grange), not only a meeting room (La Prairie, SOURCED). [K4, K10]
- Because the Grange's first enemy was railroad and warehouse pricing, it built co-op elevators, mills, stores and insurance, and its halls doubled as the place where members bought into these (Wikipedia; HistoryLink, SOURCED). [K1, K8]
- Because a rural township often had no other public building, the hall became the polling place and town hall (La Prairie, SOURCED). [K1, K10, P7]
- Because the Pomona Grange owned land for a picnic, a week-long tent camp and fair grew on it, with families keeping tents across generations (centrehistory.org, SOURCED for tents and land; generational tent-keeping UNVERIFIED, snippets from statecollege.com and NPR not opened). [K2, K3]

### E. Structural features
- **Self-built, self-funded buildings:** donated land and member labor (Taylor 2013); town–Grange cost sharing (La Prairie: $700 and land from the town). [K8, K14] SOURCED.
- **Hall-use rules (K6):** Grange halls set their own conduct rules for renters. The Oregon State Grange's standard hall-use agreement makes the renter choose either "NO ALCOHOLIC BEVERAGES WILL BE SERVED OR ALLOWED ON THE PREMISES" (any appointed Grange representative may inspect at any time, stop the event, close the hall and keep all fees and deposits) or alcohol with state liquor-commission licenses and $1,000,000 host-liquor coverage naming the Grange and the Oregon State Grange; it also has a no-smoking clause and a clause barring conduct harmful "to the good name of the Grange" and "indecent, or offensive acts contrary to good standards of moral conduct." **Enforced by:** the local Grange's appointed representative. **Origin:** not stated in the form; the National Grange historically supported temperance (Wikipedia, SOURCED). SOURCED: https://orgrange.org/res/forms/hall/sub_hall_use_agreement.pdf (PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29). Individual halls differ: Leedy Grange (Oregon) reportedly bans alcohol on the grounds with no exceptions (UNVERIFIED, snippet; page refused with 403); Oshtemo Grange Hall (Michigan) allows alcohol only with an approved Alcohol Agreement (UNVERIFIED, snippet).
- **Ritual secrecy and degrees** (above) are themselves rules of membership. [K6] SOURCED.
- **Revival:** news reports (2014–2024) describe halls refilled by young farmers and homesteaders, and some halls turning into general community venues for concerts and open mics (Bangor Daily News, SOURCED for Maine; other revival reports UNVERIFIED, snippets only). [K9, K13]
- **Speech (K11):** English. No other note.
- **[R]** flag: members building and repairing their own halls by donated labor (hardwood floors laid by members; member-built halls) bears on shared maintenance cultures.
- **[SM: man versus society]** applies plainly to the Grange's fight with railroad and warehouse monopolies.

## 2 · Danish cooperative dairies and folk high schools

**Kind of pick:** two linked institutions (the cooperative dairy, *andelsmejeri*, and the folk high school, *folkehøjskole*), plus their companion building, the village assembly hall (*forsamlingshus*). Documented places: Hjedding Andelsmejeri (near Ølgod, West Jutland), Rødding Højskole (South Jutland), and the Sandby/Nyvang assembly hall.

### Verdict on the claim
**Claim:** "Farmers organized processing and export collectively and built schools that shaped their civic culture."
- **Collective processing and export: CONFIRMED (SOURCED).** Cooperative dairies (from 1882) and cooperative bacon slaughterhouses (from 1887, Horsens), plus a cooperative egg-export body (1895), feed and fertilizer co-ops and a cooperative bank (1914). By 1939 co-ops handled **91% of milk production and 85% of export slaughterings** (lex.dk, Claus Bjørn).
- **"Built schools": NARROWED.** Folk high schools were not built *by the cooperatives*. The first (Rødding, 1844) predates the first co-op dairy by 38 years and was founded by Christian Flor as a Danish-language school against German influence in Schleswig. Lex.dk states the folk high schools "did not directly address" cooperation as a subject; but many first-generation cooperative leaders of the 1880s–90s had attended one, and the link became an ideological story in the early 1900s; **the causal link is debated** (lex.dk, SOURCED). A 2025 economic-history paper finds both creameries and folk high schools spread faster in railway-connected parishes (Görges, Rove, Sharp, Vedel, SOURCED), i.e. the two spread side by side along the same channels.

### A. Snapshot
- **Hjedding Andelsmejeri**, Hjeddingvej 2, 6870 Ølgod, Varde Municipality, West Jutland. Founded **10 June 1882** by local farmers (a snippet says 26 farmers; UNVERIFIED, landbrugsavisen.dk not opened); bylaws largely drafted by farmer Niels Hansen Uhd of Ølgod (UNVERIFIED, snippet). Original red-brick building of about 90 m² in three rooms: one for the dairyman, a cooling and kneading room, and the dairy room for weighing-in, skimming, churning and cheesemaking, with a steam engine, hot-water vat and pump. Extended in the 1880s–90s with an ice house, cheese store, and engine and boiler rooms; **the dairyman and his family lived on site**. Moved into Ølgod in 1905; museum since 1948. [P3, P8] SOURCED: Slots- og Kulturstyrelsen, "25 fantastiske industrier" guide no. 08, https://slks.dk/fileadmin/user_upload/industriensminder/PDF-guides/08Hjedding_Kor2.pdf (PDF text extracted and read 2026-10-29).
- **Defining rules of the cooperative dairy** (all SOURCED, same guide and lex.dk): members bound themselves to **deliver all their milk to the dairy for a fixed number of years** (delivery obligation); members **jointly carried the financial liability** ("one for all, all for one"); **surplus paid out by the amount of milk delivered**; but **one member, one vote** at the general assembly regardless of size; open membership. **Hjedding's bylaws became the template for about 1,400 cooperative dairies** (slks.dk). [K1, K6, K8, P7]
- **Technology that made it possible:** the Maglekilde continuous cream separator, launched 1878, gave more butter per kg of milk and cut skimming time; at Hjedding it and the churn were driven by a steam engine. SOURCED (slks.dk). [P8]
- **Rødding Højskole**, Rødding, South Jutland: the world's first folk high school, opened **7 November 1844** (date UNVERIFIED, snippet), founded by Christian Flor to counter official German influence in the Duchy of Schleswig (SOURCED, lex.dk "folkehøjskole"). Today takes students mostly aged 18 to 25, teaching in Danish (UNVERIFIED, snippet from rhskole.dk).

### B. What else exists there
- **Growth of the dairies:** a founding wave in 1886–89, with **244 new co-op dairies in 1888 alone**; about **one third of all farms were members by 1890**. Counts: **1,046 dairies (1903) → 1,399 (1939) → 904 (1964) → 23 (1992)**; co-op slaughterhouses 27 → 61 → 62 → 5; feed co-ops 1,505 (1939) → 83 (1992). SOURCED: Claus Bjørn, "andelsbevægelse," *Lex – Danmarks Nationalleksikon*, https://lex.dk/andelsbev%C3%A6gelse (last modified 1 Oct 2024; read 2026-10-29). [K1, K9]
- **Why the switch:** after 1870 cheap grain imports from Russia, Ukraine, Poland and the U.S. collapsed grain prices; farmers moved to dairy and pigs, which needed shared processing plants. Export market for bacon was Britain. SOURCED (Wikipedia "Danish cooperative movement"; lex.dk). [K1, K9]
- **Consumer co-op stores (*brugsforeninger*):** first was Thisted Arbejderforening (1866) on the Rochdale model; umbrella body FDB founded 1896. SOURCED (lex.dk). [K1, K5]
- **Consolidation:** from the 1950s; MD Foods (later Arla Foods, with Swedish co-ops from 1997) and Danish Crown; by 1993 only 22 dairy co-ops and 6 slaughterhouses. SOURCED (lex.dk). [K9]
- **Daily milk run:** example of the Båring cooperative dairy: about 120 milk suppliers and 6 milk wagons collecting every day including Saturdays and Sundays, because cows must be milked daily. UNVERIFIED (search snippet; source not opened). [K5, K7]
- **Folk high schools:** about 350–400 have existed in Denmark in all; 21 by 1867 (after the 1864 war), 68 by 1918, about 70 today. Christen Kold (school at Ryslinge from 1851) set the farm-calendar timetable: **men's courses November to March, women's courses May to July** (women admitted from the 1860s; Askov mixed from 1885; mixed courses standard after WWII). Boarding schools; **no exams** ("Exams were prohibited," danishfolkhighschools.com). State subsidies from 1851, fixed rules from 1892; 2010 state funding 548.5 million kr. Large early schools: Askov, Testrup, Vallekilde. Danish folk high schools were copied in Norway (1864), Sweden (1868), Finland (1890) and among Danish-Americans (from 1878). SOURCED: Poul Dam, "folkehøjskole," *Lex*, https://lex.dk/folkeh%C3%B8jskole (read 2026-10-29); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_high_school; https://danishfolkhighschools.com/about-folk-high-schools/history. [K7, K13, P6]
- **Railroads:** a railway connection raised the likelihood of a parish having a co-op creamery by about 10.8 percentage points (1915 data) and of a folk high school by about 1.7 points; by 1920, 4.5% of rail-connected parishes had a folk high school against 2.5% of never-connected ones. SOURCED: Görges, Rove, Sharp & Vedel, "Tracks to Modernity: Railroads, Growth, and Social Movements in Denmark," arXiv 2502.21141 v2 (2025), https://arxiv.org/html/2502.21141v2 (read first 100,000 characters). [K5]

SOURCED: Oplevelsescenter Nyvang, "The Heart of the Village," https://www.oplevelsescenternyvang.dk/en/nyvangs-bygninger/community-hall (read 2026-10-29). [K4, K8, K10, K13]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The continuous separator plus a steam engine made a central plant pay only if many farms delivered daily, which is why delivery was a binding duty and why milk wagons ran seven days a week (slks.dk SOURCED; Båring UNVERIFIED). [K1, K5, K7]
- Equal votes regardless of herd size meant small and large farmers met as equals in the general assembly; the assembly hall and the folk high school gave them the habit of meeting and speaking (lex.dk notes the folk high schools gave rural people "self-respect" and fed co-op and democratic participation; SOURCED as a claim of the article). [K1, K4, P7]
- The farm calendar set the folk high school calendar (winter for young men, summer for young women). SOURCED. [K7]
- Losing the war of 1864 and the German pressure in Schleswig drove the first wave of schools and halls (SOURCED: Wikipedia, lex.dk). [K9]

### E. Structural features
- **Rules of everyday behavior (K6):** the binding rules found are institutional: the delivery obligation, joint liability, one-member-one-vote, and the folk high schools' ban on exams. No source found on conduct rules (alcohol, curfews) at folk high schools or assembly halls (looked: Nyvang page, lex.dk, danishfolkhighschools.com).
- **Who paid:** members (co-op shares and joint liability), residents (hall shares), and the state (folk high school subsidies from 1851/1892). SOURCED. [K8]
- **Competence (K14):** the dairyman (*mejerist*) was a trained resident specialist who lived at the dairy with his family (slks.dk SOURCED); training details NOT FOUND in this round.
- **Speech (K11):** Danish; regional Jutlandic dialects are in daily use in West and South Jutland (general note; no source opened on dialect use).

## 3 · Prairie wheat pools and grain elevators, Saskatchewan

**Kind of pick:** an institution (the Saskatchewan Wheat Pool and its farmer movement) and a building type (the country grain elevator), researched through the Pool's own structure and four documented elevator sites (Hepburn, Truax/Elmsthorpe, Keatley/North Battleford, Val Marie).

### Verdict on the claim
**Claim:** "Farmer-owned co-ops controlled the export chain, and the elevators became each town's marker."
- **Controlled the export chain: NARROWED.** The Pool owned country elevators and export terminals (Thunder Bay, bought 1959; Vancouver, built 1968; Prince Rupert, 1982) and at its 1971 peak handled **67% of Saskatchewan's grain** (8.9 million tonnes) through 1,224 elevators (esask, SOURCED). But **selling** was not the Pool's alone: its own central selling agency collapsed after the 1929 crash, the federal government took over for 1930–31 and paid $22 million in guarantees to banks, and from **1935 the Canadian Wheat Board** marketed prairie wheat (esask, SOURCED). So farmer co-ops controlled *handling* (elevators and terminals), and a federal board controlled *marketing* for most of the 20th century. From 1996 the Pool was a publicly traded company; in 2007 it bought Agricore United and became **Viterra** (Wikipedia, SOURCED). Its share of provincial grain fell from over 60% (early 1970s–1992) to 22% by 2004 (esask, SOURCED).
- **Elevators as each town's marker: CONFIRMED (SOURCED),** with the K9 half the claim names: elevators once rose "every 12 to 16 kilometres" along the rail lines (Canadian Encyclopedia); towns were laid out around them (hotels across from the elevator, per Western Producer 2014); heritage designations now cite "the central role" of a single elevator in its community (Truax).

### A. Snapshot
- **Saskatchewan Wheat Pool:** incorporated **25 August 1923** as Saskatchewan Co-operative Wheat Producers Ltd. after U.S. co-op organizer Aaron Sapiro's campaign (joint conference, Saskatoon, 6 August 1923); renamed Saskatchewan Wheat Pool in 1953. The 1924 sign-up drive succeeded with **45,725 original contract signers** (over half the province's wheat acreage). First elevator: **Bulyea, 1924**. Bought the Saskatchewan Co-operative Elevator Company's **451 elevators** (1926) and Federal Grain's 217 (1972). Peak **1,224 elevators (1971)**; closed 600 traditional elevators 1971–82; Project Horizon (1997) put $270 million into 22 inland terminals. Over 71,000 farmers held Class A (voting) shares in 2003. Board presidents from A. J. McPhail (1924–31) to Terry Baker (2004–). [K1, K8, K9, P7, P8] SOURCED: Kathy Lang, "Saskatchewan Wheat Pool," *Encyclopedia of Saskatchewan*, https://www.esask.uregina.ca/entry/saskatchewan_wheat_pool.html (read 2026-10-29).
- **Defining feature:** a farmer-owned, farmer-governed handling system with an elevator in nearly every railway town.

### B. What else exists there

Outside that chain sat the **elected local committees** in each town: "All of Saskatchewan Wheat Pool's public policy comes from local committees. Almost all of our operating policy as a company comes from local committees." Delegates debated the resolutions sent up from local committees at the annual meeting. Abandonment sped up with rail deregulation, branch-line closures and the **end of the Crow Rate in 1996**; demolition crews use track hoes and sometimes explosives. ## 5 · Tamworth Country Music Festival, Australia

## 6 · Nenana Ice Classic, Alaska

**Kind of pick:** an event (a charitable lottery on the moment the Tanana River ice breaks at Nenana) and its town (Nenana, Interior Alaska).

### Verdict on the claim
**Claim:** "A whole territory bets on the moment the river ice breaks, making the thaw a civic event."
- **CONFIRMED, with two narrowings (SOURCED).** (a) "Whole territory": tickets are sold across Alaska (over 200 outlets) and only in Alaska; at least 300,000 tickets were sold in 2019 (ADN). The contest began in **1917**, when railroad workers building the Alaska Railroad bet on the breakup (first jackpot about $800); Wikipedia also reports an informal pool from 1906 (not confirmed by any other source opened; UNVERIFIED). (b) "Civic event": in the town itself the season opens with **Tripod Days**, a weekend festival in early March, and the Ice Classic hires **70–100 local people** each spring to sort tickets and enter guesses (Alaska's News Source 2025, 2026). Under Alaska law an "ice classic" is a defined form of **charitable gaming**, and the Nenana pool is named in the statute (UNVERIFIED, snippet of AS 05.15.690; page refused, see F).

### A. Snapshot
- **Nenana:** on the south bank of the **Tanana River** at the mouth of the Nenana River, mile 412 of the Alaska Railroad, 55 road miles southwest of Fairbanks on the George Parks Highway (304 miles to Anchorage). Population **358 (2020 census)**, down from a peak of about 5,000 in the early 1920s; about **41% American Indian and Alaska Native**, mainly **Lower Tanana Athabascan**. Older name Tortella, from Toghotthele ("mountain that parallels the river"). Growth came after the 1902 Fairbanks gold strike and with railroad construction from 1915; **President Warren Harding drove the golden spike in 1923** at the 700-ft Mears Memorial Bridge; Nenana was the starting point of the **1925 serum run to Nome** (diphtheria antitoxin by dog sled). Over 40% of year-round jobs are government jobs; Crowley Marine supplies river villages by barge. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nenana,_Alaska (read 2026-10-29). [K5, P3, P9]
- **Services:** health clinic, mental health clinic, fire department, public library, State Troopers office, district court; the **Nenana Student Living Center**, a statewide boarding facility for high school students. Archaeological sites nearby dated 11,000–12,000 years old. "The majority of residents participate in subsistence activities"; several Iditarod mushers and champions live there. Climate extremes recorded: 98 °F and −69 °F. SOURCED: Tanana Chiefs Conference community page, https://www.tananachiefs.org/about/communities/nenana/ (read 2026-10-29; population figure there is a 2012 estimate of 408). Federally recognized tribe: Nenana Native Association (UNVERIFIED, snippet). [K1, K4, K14]
- **River freight:** Nenana is the hub of tug and barge shipping on the Interior river system; **May to September** is peak season for villages to get supplies by barge rather than by more expensive air; summer population grows by about 150–200 (UNVERIFIED, snippets). [K5, K7]

### B. How the Ice Classic works
- **The tripod:** a 26-ft wooden tripod (it actually has four legs, per Wikipedia) is raised on the river ice by volunteers with ropes during Tripod Days; a line runs from it to a clock on shore, which trips when the ice moves the tripod about 100 ft downriver, recording the official breakup time. SOURCED: Alex Bengel, "'A community affair': Nenana Ice Classic prepares annual Tripod Days event," *Alaska's News Source*, 3–4 Mar 2026, https://www.alaskasnewssource.com/2026/03/04/community-affair-nenana-ice-classic-prepares-annual-tripod-days-event (read 2026-10-29); Wikipedia. [K2, P6]
- **Tickets:** on sale **1 February to 5 April** each year at over 200 locations in Alaska; each ticket is a guess of month, day, hour and minute; $2.50 per ticket in 2019 (ADN 2019, SOURCED). Alaska gaming rules require tickets to be sold in the state (UNVERIFIED, snippet). [K6]
- **Money (K8):** **40–50% of the money raised goes to the jackpot**, the share set by board vote; the rest pays expenses and supports organizations in Nenana and Alaska: scholarships, the library, the senior center (Bengel 2025 and 2026, SOURCED). Record jackpot **$363,627 in 2014** (Wikipedia, SOURCED); "more than $14 million" paid out in its history (UNVERIFIED, snippet). The 1917 pool: $800/$801 (sources differ by $1). Operates as "a non-profit charitable gaming organization" (Wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Winter jobs (K7):** after sales close, **70–100 people** are employed to sort tickets and enter guesses into a database; this "helps those without employment during the winter season and sometimes serves as the first job for students at a local school" (Bengel, "'Part of our history': The tripod is up for the 2025 Nenana Ice Classic," *Alaska's News Source*, 6–7 Mar 2025, https://www.alaskasnewssource.com/2025/03/07/part-our-history-tripod-is-up-2025-nenana-ice-classic, SOURCED). Manager Megan Baker: "everyone gets involved by working and helping, sorting the tickets and putting information in the database and of course celebrating Tripod Days." [K7, K8]
- **Breakup record:** typical freeze-up October–November, average peak ice thickness about 41 inches by 1 April (Wikipedia, SOURCED). **Earliest breakup: 12:21 a.m., 14 April 2019** (six days earlier than the previous record of 20 April, 1940; ice had measured only 16 inches in January against a typical 30-some). SOURCED: Michelle Theriault Boots, *Anchorage Daily News*, 14 Apr 2019, https://adn.com/alaska-news/2019/04/14/nenana-ice-classic-tripod-goes-down-setting-record-for-earliest-river-break-up (read 2026-10-29); the 1940 year of the old record is from a snippet (UNVERIFIED). **Latest: 20 May** (1964 and 2013) (Wikipedia; RealClimate, SOURCED).
- **Climate record (K+ science):** the contest's minute-exact record since 1917 was used by Raphael Sagarin and Fiorenza Micheli (Stanford) in *Science* 294:811 (26 Oct 2001) as evidence of earlier spring (UNVERIFIED, snippets; paper not opened). Gavin Schmidt (RealClimate, 7 Mar 2014) computed a trend of about **6 days per century earlier** (±4) over 1917–2013. SOURCED: https://www.realclimate.org/index.php/archives/2014/03/the-nenana-ice-classic-and-climate/ (read 2026-10-29). [K+]
