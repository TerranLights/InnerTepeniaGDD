## 1. Tunnels, trenches and rooms cut into ice sheets and deep snow

### 1.1 Why build under the snow at all: the engineers' own trade-off list (Camp Century program, 1950s)

- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 4 to 6]:** The Army weighed above-surface against subsurface construction and wrote out both lists. Above-surface: lower maintenance cost, better for radomes and antennas, less power for lighting, and "psychological advantages (i.e., many people have an aversion to living in subsurface camps)"; but higher first cost, exposure to the surface climate, more imported materials, "a severe snow-drift removal problem," and it "only delays eventual burial; hence, provides no permanent solution." Subsurface: lower first cost, less heating fuel, fewer imported materials, less drift, easier to camouflage; but "Maintenance cost is higher," "Requires year-round power for lighting," "Has limited useful life," and "Is psychologically undesirable."
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 2]:** "above-surface structures soon become subsurface as a result of new accumulation and the continuous drifting of snow. This is a condition for which they were not designed; hence, under the stresses of the ever-increasing overburden, ultimately they fail."
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 4]:** an "optimum zone" exists where closure is slowest: below the depth where snow densifies quickly and above the depth of heavy overburden. At Site II and Camp Century it was deduced to extend "from approximately 60 to 120 feet below the surface." **DERIVED:** 60 to 120 ft = about 18 to 37 m.
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 6]:** a network of **subsurface roads** was considered so that winter traffic would be independent of weather, but "a system of subsurface roadways would be economically feasible only if major installations were envisioned." The reason given: for weeks at a time Thule Air Base could not get mail, passengers or supplies to the ice-cap radar sites by air in winter.

### 1.2 The construction method: Peter snow millers, undercut trenches, snow arches, metal arches (CRREL / SIPRE)

- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 8]:** The machine was the **Peter snow miller**, "used by the Swiss to clear roads of avalanche snow in the Alps." Capacity "approximately 780 yd³/hr in 0.4 g/cm³ snow"; it could cut dense snow with ice deposits; its milling produced "a high density (0.55 g/cm³) granular material" called **Peter snow**, which "hardens with time into a material of significant mechanical strength." **DERIVED:** 780 yd³/hr = about 596 m³/hr. Its introduction is credited to Dr. Henri Bader, then Chief Scientist of SIPRE (Snow, Ice and Permafrost Research Establishment, CRREL's predecessor).
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 9]:** age-hardening is fastest when the processed snow is laid in layers with large temperature differences and "only when the snow was colder than the air"; the best unsupported arches used a broad grain size (0.1 to 2.0 mm). Milling gave no stronger snow in the end than natural snow of like density; the benefit was reaching high density and hardness fast.
- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 9 to 17], the sequence of trials:**
  - 1955, Site II (later Camp Fistclench, 220 miles east of Thule, 6,800 ft elevation): first trenching trials.
  - 1956: "The first 500 feet of trench was produced and covered with an arched snow roof composed of Peter snow," laid on "a specially designed removable metal form."
  - 1957: "the first known deliberately designed subsurface camp" (Camp Fistclench); straight-walled trenches "roofed with conventional structures of wood and metal in all cases where the spans were greater than 8 feet."
  - 1958: Bader's **undercut trench**, "a width of 8 feet at the top and 18 feet at the floor"; a depth of 10 ft "provided sufficient overhead clearance to assure at least a 5-year life before settlement of the roof would begin to crush the enclosed framed structures." Roofed with an **unsupported snow arch**: metal forms over the open slot, Peter snow backfilled over them, forms removed "as soon as the snow had age hardened sufficiently to be self-supporting."
  - Lessons from Fistclench: "flat structural roofs over snow trenches were incompatible with the ever-increasing snow load," waste could be put into melted shafts, and supports "must be spaced symmetrically with respect to the trench center line" because the floor arches upward as the trench deforms.
- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 29, 36, 53]:** for spans wider than the undercut method allowed, **corrugated metal "Wonder Arches"** (16-gauge) over straight-wall trenches up to 40 ft wide. Clark's rule after four years: "For trenches wider than 24 ft at the floor, straight-wall trenches, covered with metal arches, are preferable; for trenches up to 24 ft wide at the floor, undercutting and covering with unsupported (metal forms removed) snow arches is considered preferable." Arch forms should stay until a ram hardness of 100 is reached at 15 cm depth.
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 17], foundations:** Costes concluded 50 psi point bearing (safety factor 2) was a conservative pile value if piles were spaced at least three diameters apart and set 30 ft below trench floors; later Camp Century tests (Kovacs, 1964) indicated skin friction may matter more. Spread-footing settlement "is strongly dependent on temperature and initial snow density," linear with load up to 2,000 psf.
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippet only; record AD0613266 at openpolar.no failed TLS):** a CRREL production study of cut-and-cover work at Camp Century reports a 1,600-ft trench with horizontal curves built in the 1961 and 1962 seasons, "a total of 290 man-hr" for arch installation, and production estimates based on a five-man crew.

### 1.3 Camp Century, Greenland (1959 to 1966/67)

**Site.** 138 miles east of Camp Tuto on the Tuto-to-Site II trail (SOURCED [TR174, p. 25]). Average annual accumulation "approximately 4 feet of snow"; "average annual mean temperature is around −10°F" (SOURCED [TR174, p. 53, footnote]). **DERIVED:** −10 °F = −23.3 °C; 4 ft = 1.22 m of snow per year. The name: the camp was first meant to be 100 miles from the ice edge; Danish representatives disliked maps that read "like an obituary column," so no person's name was used (SOURCED [TR174, p. 25]).

**Design brief (SOURCED [TR174, p. 27]).** "a capacity to house 100 personnel on a year-round basis"; design life of 10 years "(with appropriate maintenance)"; parallel main trenches crossed by a central "main vehicular access trench, large enough to accommodate tractor-drawn sled trains"; heated buildings on timber cribs or piles with about 2 ft of clearance above the snow floor; buildings heated electrically; water well at least 1,000 ft from the sewage sump; sump at least 500 ft from the nearest building; standard trenches about 24 ft wide at floor level; fire walls between buildings; two ventilation systems (one for buildings, a separate one "to expel heat and gases from the trenches").

**Services specified (SOURCED [TR174, p. 27], list verbatim):** living quarters; kitchen and mess hall; latrines and showers; recreation hall and theater; library and hobby shops; "Dispensary, emergency operating room, and a 10-bed infirmary"; laundry; post exchange; scientific laboratories; cold storage warehouses; POL (petroleum, oil, lubricants) storage tanks; communications center; equipment and maintenance shops; supply rooms and storage areas; nuclear power plant (with standby diesel-electric plant); administrative buildings; utility buildings; chapel.

**As built.**
- **Trenches:** "21 tunnels with a total length of 9,800 feet (3.0 km)"; "The longest trench extended to 1,100 feet (340 m)" (SOURCED [WP-CC]). The trench numbers in [TR174]'s deformation table and layout run up to 21, which agrees.
- **Buildings:** Army T-5 arctic buildings narrowed from 20 ft to 16 ft to fit the trenches, "grossly over-designed for use in Camp Century," built with "only fifty percent of the normal number of laminated plywood roof trusses" (SOURCED [TR174, p. 29]).
- **Schedule and haul:** construction June 1959 to October 1960; in 1959 only "10% of the permanent camp (5 trenches and 5 buildings)"; materials hauled the 138 miles from Tuto "approximated 1000 short tons in 1959 and 4600 short tons in 1960," on 20-ton cargo sleds, with special sleds built in Greenland for heavier items (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 31 to 32, 57 to 58]). Peter miller operators were trained at Houghton, Michigan, in December 1959 (SOURCED [TR174, App. A]).
- **Cost:** estimate $7,920,000, of which the PM-2A reactor $5,700,000 and the non-nuclear camp $1,820,000; footnote: "The actual total cost of Camp Century was held to this figure" (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 30 to 31]). The non-nuclear line items include prefabricated buildings $700,000, roofing forms $150,000, ventilation systems $200,000, "Alarm systems (fire & carbon monoxide)" $10,000, fire fighting system $50,000.
- **Deviations forced by the short season (SOURCED [TR174, p. 32]):** steel arch forms left in place in many trenches (unsupported snow arches whose forms came off early "deformed rapidly"); planned sliding portal doors never installed; the sewage sump built "only 150 feet from the nearest building" instead of 500; "The ventilation systems called for in the original plans were not installed"; fire walls not installed (plans for 1961 canceled because sprinklers were judged adequate).

**The reactor, PM-2A (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 47 to 51]).** Gross thermal power 10,000 kW; gross electric 2,000 kW; net electric 1,560 kW; process steam 1,000,000 Btu/hr; plant weight 310 short tons in 27 packages (each "9 ft x 9 ft x 30 ft or less, 30,000 lb or less"); core life 17 months at full power; "Crew size 18 Men"; plant cost through FY61 $5.7 million; operating and maintenance cost 65 mills/kWh. Critical and operational 2 October 1960; ran "except for down-time ... until 9 July 1963." In 1962: 6,491 hours on line, 2,269 off (74.1 percent on line), average gross demand 830 kW; most downtime was "the necessity to cease operation of the plant for several weeks while USAPR&DC crews rehabilitated the metal arch roofs covering the reactor building trench." Turbine-generator faults in 1961 (traced to a thrust-bearing failure in November 1960) stopped electrical output for about 2.5 months in total. Backup: "three 300-kw diesel powered generators"; during shakedown the diesels powered the camp "for more than fifty percent of the time." Radioactive liquid waste went into the ice under a Danish-American agreement allowing "up to 50 millicuries per year"; 1962 discharges: 47,078 gallons carrying 11.333 mCi. Removal approved 2 August 1963 "primarily from plans to discontinue year-round operations at Camp Century in order to reduce Greenland R&D support costs"; dismantled and shipped out summer 1964, with residual radiation "considerably higher than had been anticipated," so more crew were needed for the work.

**Abandonment and what failed.**
- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 36 to 40]:** the wide reactor trenches' Wonder Arches were left partly uncovered in the rush before the dark season, took uneven loads and "by the end of the first year of occupancy showed severe distress"; "Twice during the 3-year life of the PM-2A at Century, sections of these arches were raised approximately 6 feet."
- **SOURCED [WP-IW], citing Petersen 2008, p. 79:** by mid-1962 the reactor room ceiling "had dropped and had to be lifted 5 feet (1.5 m)"; in July 1963 the Army decided to run Camp Century as a summer-only camp. "The facility was evacuated in 1965"; "Project Iceworm was canceled, and Camp Century closed in 1966" (uncited in the article), while the same article also says "decommissioned in 1967." [WP-CC] gives operation 1959 to 1967, "manned for five years and abandoned after eight." (Conflict logged in FINAL.)
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 52]:** Clark's two unsolved problems: "stabilization of snow trench floors for tracked vehicles and prevention of drifting snow from closing ramp entrances." Conclusion: "Roughly 10 years is the maximum feasible design life for an undersnow camp in environments similar to Century," and subsurface camps are feasible "only in environments as cold or colder than Century."

**Project Iceworm, the scale it was a prototype for.** **SOURCED [WP-IW]:** "a system of tunnels 4,000 kilometers (2,500 mi) in length," "up to 600 nuclear missiles," over "52,000 square miles (130,000 km2)," with launch clusters "spaced 4 miles (6.4 km) apart" and launch-complex floors "28 feet (8.5 m) below the surface" (area and spacing cited to Danish documents of 1997 and a 1960 Army report; length and missile count uncited). **UNVERIFIED (secondary sources via search summary, primary 1997 Danish report not found):** a staffing figure of about 11,000 soldiers to live under the ice. **SOURCED [WP-IW], tagged citation needed in the article:** ice-core work showed the ice "would destroy the tunnels and launch stations in about two years."

### 1.4 Camp Tuto and its ice tunnel (1954 to 1966)

- **SOURCED [WP-TUTO]:** Camp Tuto stood at the edge of the ice cap 18 miles east of Thule Air Base, elevation 1,500 ft, with a 2,000-ft runway; the access ramp onto the ice was "over 1 mile" long. "Camp Tuto was reportedly home to 450 military personnel, although some were there seasonally" (September 1958). Services listed: mess hall, supply center, communications center, machine shop, an ionosphere station, "two doctors and a dentist." Buildings: first Jamesway huts, later "over a hundred" aluminum-sandwich buildings in a grid, some joined by egg-shaped corrugated steel tunnels. Survival cabins every 30 miles along the ice trail to Century and Fistclench.
- **SOURCED [WP-TUTO]:** "a 1,100-foot (340 m) long ice tunnel was carved into the foot of the ice cap," about half a mile from camp; "The temperature inside was found to be 17 °F (−8 °C)"; "officially opened in October 1961" (sources: *New York Times*, October 1961; Walter Wager, *Camp Century: City under the Ice*, 1962). It held temporary living quarters and diesel generators.
- **SOURCED [TIME57]:** "Near Thule," engineers had dug a chamber "65 feet square and 25 feet high" into a glacier, "150 feet below the ice surface and 500 feet back from its face," with built-in refrigeration. (Whether this is the same excavation as the Tuto tunnel is not stated: NOT FOUND.)
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippets only; the primary pages were blocked):** SIPRE did ice-tunneling at Tuto in the summers of 1955 to 1958 (onemine.org abstract, "Arctic Survival Study: Ice Tunneling in Greenland"); Landauer (1959, *Journal of Glaciology* 3(26)) measured closure in the Red Rock and Tuto tunnels, both excavated in summer 1955; Butkovich and Landauer described a grid method used in a 366-m tunnel. The October 1961 "opening" and the 1955 excavation are compatible only if the 1961 date is a later public or extended opening (conflict logged).

### 1.5 Camp Fistclench, Site II (1957 to 1960)

- **SOURCED [WP-FC]:** 76°10′N 55°21′W, 6,800 ft; "a crew of one officer and 27 men" arrived May 1957; "The largest of five trenches, 600 feet long," held living quarters and utilities, 20 ft wide and 8 ft high; corrugated steel arches with blown snow over them; two diesel generators; "it housed several dozen during the summer"; it "was not used to overwinter troops"; "The large amount of fuel needed to operate Fist Clench influenced the choice" of a reactor at Century.
- **SOURCED [TIME57] (a 1957 briefing, before outcomes):** trenches dug with "a 13-ton, Swiss-built Peters plow"; "Twenty-four insulated Jamesway Huts" (20 ft by 40 ft) set in trenches, roofed with timber trusses and covered with snow; about 60 scientists and soldiers "will winter" there; fuel is "three-quarters" of the cargo to an Arctic base; and "One hundred miles of under-ice highway are now under construction between Thule and Fist Clench." **UNVERIFIED:** no other source found confirms any length of under-ice highway was ever built; [TR174] treats subsurface roads only as a concept. Wintering conflicts with [WP-FC] (logged).
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 25]:** Fistclench was "austere," "had not been designed to accommodate" a reactor, and at 218 miles from Tuto "imposed an unnecessary logistical burden"; hence the new camp nearer the ice edge.

### 1.6 South Pole: buried first station, the dome, the utilidor and the new tunnels

- **Original station (1956/57 to 1975).** **SOURCED [WP-SP]:** first winter party "eight IGY scientists" plus eight Navy support men; "By 1960, three years after the construction of the station, it had already been buried by 6 feet (1.8 m) of snow"; abandoned 1975, its mostly wooden roof caved in under the load; demolished December 2010. **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** demolition followed an incident in which an equipment operator fell through during snow-stability testing.
- **The dome (1975 to 2009/10).** **SOURCED [WP-SP]:** "160 feet (50 m) wide by 52 feet (16 m) high, with 46 by 79 feet (14 m × 24 m) steel archways"; one arch was the entry, a transverse arch held maintenance buildings, fuel bladders, the power plant and the snow melter; the dome base, first at grade, was slowly buried; in winter 1988 "a loud crack" was heard and the foundation base-ring beams were found broken from overstress; the 1981 to 1982 summer camp had 11 Jamesway huts of about 10 people each; dismantled late 2009.
- **The first utilidor (1970s).** **SOURCED [SPS-UT1]:** egg-shaped corrugated metal arch sections placed under the snow beneath the dome foundation, carrying "electrical, communications, water, and sewer lines between buildings"; sewage was meant to flow by gravity to an outfall outside the dome. 1977: cracking in the dome floor began the outfall problems (a drift pushed the station end down; snow over the sewage bulb settled). 1979 to 1980: leveling failed, "the bulb filled and flooded the end of the utilidor"; fix: a lift shaft and pumps lifting sewage to a higher gravity line and a new bulb, later grinder pumps.
- **The CRREL tunneling machine and the new tunnels (1990s to 2002).** **SOURCED [SPS-UT1]:** NSF tasked CRREL in January 1992; machine was "a modified Melroe Bobcat" with a rotating cutter and a snowblower; spoil moved through ducts by "a 50 HP fan rated at 2,500 CFM," about 8 lb/s of snow; standard cross-section "6 feet wide by 10 feet high"; a 380-ft sewer tunnel, last stretch hand-dug with chain saws; escape hatches included. **SOURCED [SPS-UT2]:** main production over three summers, 1999 to 2000 through 2001 to 2002; the main tunnel "about 1900 feet," L-shaped, plus cross tunnels A to E leading to water wells and sewer bulbs; 228 ft hand-dug in one week while the machine was down; machine output "about 125 feet per week"; breakdowns "every week or two"; an access ditch "35' deep, 30' wide and 200' long"; tunnel E, the emergency sewer tunnel, was hand-dug with chain saws; the new tunnels were near ambient "-60°F," about 20 degrees colder than the 1996 to 1997 tunnel (the author suspects the old sewer bulbs warmed it); a board at the entrance shows who is inside "in case of an emergency."
- **People and use.** **SOURCED [IRIS]:** "1,000's of feet of ice tunnels deep under the surface where the water pipes and electric lines run"; "a consistent -56 to -58 degrees" (unit not stated; context is Fahrenheit); cut with electric chainsaws; workers have carved niches into the walls "to mark seasons, big events, and other occasions"; fuel stored under the snow "something like 450,000 gallons." **UNVERIFIED (snippets only, primary blocked):** 3,071 ft of under-snow tunnels (*Bangor Daily News*, 2009); the 2014 restoration aimed at "roughly 10 feet high by 6 feet wide," with the tunnel at about −55 °C at its deepest point (*Antarctic Sun*, 11 July 2014, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/4042 , DNS failure).

## 2. How fast openings in ice close

### 2.1 Measured closure in snow trenches: Camp Century, 1962 to 1963

- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 33 to 39]:** the only failure mode observed was slow viscous flow, not collapse. "Although there is considerable variation, the average approximates 30 inches of gross vertical closure a year." The summary of Tobiasson's 1962 to 1963 measurements: "Main Trench: 16 in./year (average)"; side trenches "34 in./year (average)"; "Overall average throughout camp: 29.66 in./year." **DERIVED:** 16, 34 and 29.66 in/yr = about 0.41, 0.86 and 0.75 m/yr.
- **SOURCED [TR174, Figs. 28 and 29]:** Main Trench (no heated buildings; air "varies with season between 14°F and −40°F"): "Reduction in cross sectional area = 12% per year." Trench 20 (camp headquarters and gymnasium; air "between 23°F and 5°F"): "Reduction in cross sectional area = 17% per year."
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 36, footnote], the temperature effect:** "the creep rate is twice as fast at 14°F as at 2°F and one hundred times as fast at 28°F as at −40°F." **DERIVED:** 14 °F = −10 °C, 2 °F = −16.7 °C, 28 °F = −2.2 °C, −40 °F = −40 °C. Where heat collected, the snow warmed "several degrees" and deformed "more rapidly than predicted by several fold."
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 36]:** closure was lower in trenches where metal arch forms stayed in place, but Clark judged the bigger factor was that "most of the trenches retaining the in-place metal arches contained no buildings or other sources of heat."
- **SOURCED [WP-CC]:** the 1959 trenches "compressed within four years, to the point that many had reached their design margins."
- **SOURCED [TR174, Table II, sample rows]:** centerline floor-to-apex height, 1962 to 1963: Main Trench at station 0+21, 15.80 ft to 14.70 ft (−1.10 ft); Trench 20 at 1+50, 19.4 ft to 15.3 ft (−4.1 ft, arch removed in 1963); Trench 21 at 1+50, 21.8 ft to 21.5 ft (−0.3 ft). Single-year drops ranged from about 0.3 ft to 4.1 ft.

### 2.2 Measured closure of a deep hole in cold ice: Site II, Greenland, 1957 to 1958

- **DERIVED:** 1,135 ft = 346 m. 2.704 / 5.8375 = 0.46, so the hole lost 54 percent of its diameter in about 10 months. As a log strain, ln(5.8375 / 2.704) = 0.77 in 10 months, about 0.92 per year. At 185 ft (56 m) the change was within measuring error.

### 2.3 The flow law and what it implies with depth

- **SOURCED [Wikipedia, Glen–Nye flow law, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen%E2%80%93Nye_flow_law]:** effective strain rate = A × (effective stress)^n; "the most commonly used assumption" is n = 3; A depends on temperature by an Arrhenius relation, A = A₀ exp(−Q/RT); "Estimates of A vary by orders of magnitude." The article cites Nye (1953), "The flow law of ice from measurements in glacier tunnels, laboratory experiments and the Jungfraufirn borehole experiment." (Nye 1953 itself: NOT READ, blocked.)
- **SOURCED [icedrill.org abstract of Talalay and Hooke 2007, "Closure of deep boreholes in ice sheets: a discussion," *Annals of Glaciology* 47, https://icedrill.org/library/closure-deep-boreholes-ice-sheets-discussion]:** "Laboratory experiments yield values of A₀ = 9.514 MPa⁻³ a⁻¹ for secondary creep"; "Typical borehole closure experiments then give E ≈ 0.16"; "Closure data from Vostok hole 3G yield E ≈ 0.7" (E is the enhancement factor multiplying A).
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippet of the same paper; full text blocked at Cambridge):** "We thus used Paterson's values of A at −22.8 °C (0.486 MPa⁻³ a⁻¹), n (3)."
- **UNVERIFIED (standard glaciology result, recalled, not read in a primary source this session):** Nye's solution for a long circular opening in ice under overburden pressure p gives a relative closure rate (dr/dt)/r = A (p/n)^n. It contains no term for the opening's size: on this theory a large hall and a small borehole at the same depth and temperature close by the same fraction per year.
- **DERIVED (illustration only; rests on the two UNVERIFIED items just above; overburden pressure p = ρ g h with assumed column-mean densities of 780, 840, 870, 890, 905 and 912 kg/m³ for 100, 200, 300, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 m to allow for lighter firn near the top; ice at −22.8 °C; nothing inside the opening pushing back):**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 m | 0.77 | 0.008 | about 124 years | 0.001 | about 775 years |
| 200 m | 1.65 | 0.081 | about 12 years | 0.013 | about 78 years |
| 300 m | 2.56 | 0.30 | about 3.3 years | 0.048 | about 21 years |
| 500 m | 4.37 | 1.5 | about 8 months | 0.24 | about 4.2 years |
| 1,000 m | 8.88 | 12.6 | about 29 days | 2.0 | about 6 months |

  Arithmetic for the 300 m row: p = 870 × 9.81 × 300 = 2.56 MPa; rate = 0.486 × (2.56 / 3)³ = 0.486 × 0.62 = 0.30 per year; e-folding time = 1 / 0.30 = 3.3 years. Two cross-checks against measured data: (a) at 346 m the Site II hole (§2.2) closed at about 0.92 per year, about twice the E = 1 line (the line gives 0.47 per year at 346 m), so the E = 1 column is not an overestimate for that site; (b) the cube law makes each doubling of depth multiply closure about eightfold, which agrees with the practical dry-hole limits in §2.4. Warmer ice closes faster: the rate factor rises steeply with temperature, and in thick ice sheets the ice usually gets warmer toward the bed (that last point is general knowledge, UNVERIFIED here).

### 2.4 Practical depth limits found in real practice

- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 4]:** for snow trenches, the optimum (slowest-closure) zone at Site II and Camp Century was about 60 to 120 ft (18 to 37 m) below the surface. **SOURCED [TR174, p. 52]:** "Roughly 10 years is the maximum feasible design life for an undersnow camp in environments similar to Century"; for Byrd Station's under-snow trenches, colder and drier, Clark judged 20 years reasonable (ENGINEERING DATA).
- **SOURCED [Zagorodnov et al. 2014, *Journal of Glaciology* 60(223), https://bpcrc.osu.edu/sites/default/files/2020-12/Zagorodnov_JGlac_2014.pdf , read via text extraction]:** "The depth of dry and semi-fluid borehole drilling is limited due to the risk of losing the drill as a result of rheological closure of the borehole. The rate of closure increases with borehole depth and increasing ice temperature." "The deepest dry borehole so far in a shelf glacier reached 330 m depth in a 416 m thick shelf glacier (Rand, 1977). When the drill was at the borehole bottom, it was seized by the ice and lost during a 30 min intermission." Two Ross Ice Shelf dry holes "experienced difficulties due to borehole closure below 200 m (T = −10°C)." The paper's own working limit: "the maximum depth of a dry borehole is 300–400 m."
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippet, Cambridge full text blocked):** "drilling in ice without fluid can be carried out safely to depths of 300-350 m."
- **UNVERIFIED [WP-IW], tagged citation needed:** ice-core data showed the Greenland ice at Camp Century "would destroy the tunnels and launch stations in about two years."
- **NOT FOUND:** any record of a person-sized opening kept open for years at depths of several hundred meters in an ice sheet. Closest found: 346 m borehole (§2.2), Vostok deep drilling (fluid-filled holes only), and the subglacial lake access holes (also fluid-filled).

### 2.5 Trimming: who, how, how much, how often

- **Camp Century (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 41 to 46]):** "the amount of snow which has to be trimmed and removed from Camp Century each year in order to maintain the original cross section of the trenches is estimated to be on the order of 20,000 cubic yards. Assuming a work-year of 240 days of 8 hours each, snow trimming and removal would have to be at the rate of approximately 0.173 cu yd/min." "Even for a camp no larger than Century, the snow trimming effort is burdensome, and particularly so if done with hand tools alone." A photo shows "a soldier using an electric chain saw to trim snow away from the building eave." A mechanized trimming-and-removal system was estimated at $0.4 million, "not justified unless a number of subsurface installations such as Century were planned." **DERIVED:** 20,000 yd³ = about 15,300 m³ per year; check: 240 × 8 × 60 = 115,200 minutes; 20,000 / 115,200 = 0.174 yd³/min.
- **What made trimming hard (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 44 to 46]):** buildings with 12-ft walls in 24-ft trenches left 4 ft less headroom, so "trimming of the snow arches above the 12-foot wall buildings had to be commenced even before the rest of the camp had been completed"; overhead utility lines and a plank floor in the main trench meant trimming "could not be accomplished without removing either the utility lines or the plank floor," so in summer 1963 "the floor was removed and an additional 4 feet of snow was excavated"; metal arches in Trenches 7, 9, 16, 18 and 20 were removed in June and July 1963 "to permit overhead trimming." Clark's fix: put utilities "in a sub-floor utility duct," which "permits trimming of the snow roof without removal of a great amount of hardware and the resulting disruption of power and sewage services."
- **Floors and ramps (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 46 to 47]):** under vehicles the snow floors disaggregated "to depths of 12 or more inches"; oil and fuel drips prevented recompaction; each bulldozing of a ramp made it deeper and longer. - **South Pole (SOURCED [southpolestation.com 2013 to 2014 photo page, https://southpolestation.com/1314/14photos3.html]):** the tunnels are "slowly being compressed by the deepening snow"; squares of snow were cut from the walls with a chain saw by a carpenter and hauled out "with a sled and pulley system" through the power plant arch; caption: "we used to have to do this in the fuel arch" (a recurring job); a project to enlarge the emergency escape raises was "still out in the future somewhere."

### 2.6 Refrigerating or ventilating to slow closure

- **Air wells, Camp Century 1959 to 1960 (SOURCED [TR174, p. 41]):** "14-inch diameter holes (air wells) approximately 40 feet into the snow mass beneath the trench floors"; fans drew air through the colder permeable snow below, cooling it "several degrees." Yen and Bender concluded air wells were "a practical and economical means of cooling snow trenches, but only if the wells were spaced at least 80 feet apart and were operated on a year-round basis," and recommended "that all portals and ventilation shafts leading to the outside be left open during the winter season to facilitate the natural convection flow of wintertime cold air through the trenches."
- **What was skipped (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 30, 36 to 40]):** convection barriers between heat sources and snow arches were dropped from the plans; Clark: "there can be little doubt that the use of convection barriers would have retarded the closure rates significantly," and "any system which would have dissipated or otherwise expelled the heat from the trenches would have extended their useful life span, without extensive maintenance." "Failure to install a system to expel heat from the trenches became a serious problem." Heat sources named: buildings, the reactor, "vehicles, electric lights, and even people."
- **Refrigerated ice storage chamber near Thule (SOURCED [TIME57]):** 65 ft square, 25 ft high, 150 ft below the ice surface, "with built-in refrigeration."
- **Jungfraujoch:** artificial cooling from 1997 (SOURCED, §2.5).
- **Byrd Station under-snow trenches, ENGINEERING DATA: UNVERIFIED (snippet of a 1967 Navy technical note):** deformation "attributed in part to above 0°F tunnel temperatures, threatens the structural integrity and useful life of the undersnow camp"; a 1965 heat-load survey existed.

## 3. Building against snow accumulation

### 3.1 The basic finding: anything on the surface makes drifts

- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 20 to 22]:** "any object, obstruction, or depression on the surface results in the formation of snowdrifts at a steadily increasing rate"; "surface structures must be elevated 10 to 20 feet above the surface on piles, if rapid burial is to be avoided"; snow fences are "only a temporary expedient" (they are buried, must be dug out and reset, and their mounds trap still more snow); structures should be laid out "so that no structure is located in the area of the snowdrift pattern caused by another structure." Methods studied 1954 to 1964 ranged from fluid-dynamics theory through full-scale field tests to wind-tunnel scale models of blowing snow (Gerdel 1961). Inflatable air locks and air curtains at portals were proposed but "never actually tested in the field."
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 47]:** at Camp Century "Portal ramps to the camp filled with drifting snow within a few hours during frequent storms," and anything protruding above the surface built drifts that added overburden and so sped the closure of the trenches below.
- **SOURCED [TR174, App. B, by Wayne Tobiasson]:** CRREL's 1964 covered-entrance test: two processed-snow abutments 14 ft high and 12 ft apart, poured in plywood forms from Peter-miller snow, forms stripped after 24 hours; a 14-ft-span corrugated metal arch on timber seats; 6 to 12 in of milled snow blown over the arch; a three-piece plywood door at the open end of the ramp "to provide personnel and vehicular access while preventing drift snow from depositing in the end of the ramp."
- **UNVERIFIED (search snippet of a 2016 ERDC/CRREL report on drifting at Summit Station; full text failed on SSL):** about 25 percent of the snow the wind carries into Summit each winter is deposited as drifts, "mostly in close proximity to the structures."

### 3.2 The DEW Line ice-cap radar stations, DYE-2 and DYE-3, Greenland (1959/60 to 1988)

- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 5, Fig. 5]:** the DEW Line ice-cap stations used the "Texas Tower" concept, a "Built-in jacking device permits periodic raising of structure as new snow accumulates"; Clark: initial cost "far greater than that of constructing beneath the surface," and the building is "readily visible for miles."
- **SOURCED [lswilson.dewlineadventures.com DEW Line historical description, https://lswilson.dewlineadventures.com/?p=100]:** DYE-2, about 100 miles inland at 7,600 ft; the Composite Building is "6 stories high and is hung 30 feet above the snow on 8 support columns"; "Annual accumulation of ice and snow is approximately 3 feet"; −40 °F "is normal during winter"; water from melted snow scooped by "a 300 foot, variable height cable-way extending from the side of the Composite Building."
- **SOURCED [ERDC/CRREL TR-11-10, Barna et al. 2011, https://geo-summit.org/sites/default/files/docs/Summit-Station_ERDC-CRREL_TR-11-10.pdf , read via text extraction]:** the two DEW stations built 1959 to 1960 were "massive structures, elevated above the snow surface, and required periodic lifting. While the design life was 10 years, they remained operational for 30 years until they were abandoned in the early 1990's."
- **SOURCED [Wikipedia, DYE Stations, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DYE_Stations]:** "Snow accumulation of about 1 m (3 ft 3 in) per year caused subsidence which eventually necessitated a re-foundation"; in 1977 and 1982 Danish Arctic Contractors re-pillared the two stations by "jacking-up," which also moved each sideways; DYE-3's legs were eight steel beams whose attachments could be adjusted to realign the station; evacuated 1988, abandoned 1992 and "left to sink into the ice."

For DYE-2 (1980 study "Extending the useful life of DYE-2 to 1986"), a 210-ft sideways move was judged feasible, but backfilling the truss enclosure with ice "is expected to cost about $2.7 million less."

### 3.3 Summit Station, Greenland: the "Big House" (built 1989 to 1990)

- **SOURCED [ERDC/CRREL TR-11-10]:** elevation 3,230 m; "Summit Station typically receives 65 cm of annual snowfall. Accumulating snow combined with blowing and drifting can completely bury a structure in several years." The Big House (about 17 × 8 m: communications office, kitchen, scullery, a refrigerated room for perishable food, common room, washer and dryer, "a single flush toilet, and shower") stands on "10 steel columns, connected by steel trusses"; "the timber footings originally placed in the processed snow foundation are now approximately 18 m below grade."

Column height suffices for two lifts, so steel extensions are added every other lift.

## 4. Services inside ice

Additional source for this section: **[SR95-10]** Virgil J. Lunardini and John Rand, *Thermal Design of an Antarctic Water Well*, CRREL Special Report 95-10, March 1995, https://user-web.icecube.wisc.edu/~mnewcomb/radio/drilling/thermal_rodwell_crrel.pdf (a scanned PDF with no text layer; pages 1 to 26 were read by local OCR of the downloaded file).

### 4.1 Water from melted ice and snow

**The Rodriguez well (Rodwell): origin and principle.**
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 23]:** Henri Bader proposed in 1955 to melt "a shaft into the ice to a depth of approximately 100 feet, where the snow has a density of about 0.70 g/cm³ and is impermeable to water," then keep a pool liquid with steam jets and pump it up. The idea came from Site II, where water-borne sewage dumped in a pit "rapidly melted its way down to a depth of about 100 feet," then ponded in "a pool of ever increasing size which remained in the liquid state as long as the heat input was continued." Raul Rodriguez of the Army Engineer Research and Development Laboratories field-tested it in 1959; it became the "Rodriguez Well."
- **SOURCED [SR95-10, Introduction]:** "In situ water reservoirs were first designed and built by USACRREL in the early 1960s for Camp Tuto, Greenland"; on the northern Greenland ice cap "initial ponding of the meltwater occurs at depths between 36.5 to 48 m (120 to 150 ft)"; "with a large heat supply and small pumping rate the cavity can grow laterally rapidly, but if the pool is overpumped, the cavity tends to develop rapidly downward"; eventually the pool reaches an equilibrium size where all heat input is lost to the surrounding ice and air. Schmitt and Rodriguez observed a well supplied with "219,825 W (750,000 Btu/h)."

**Camp Century well (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 44 to 45]).** Diesel-fired standby steam generator "capable of producing 165 psi of saturated steam at 373°F and at a rate of about 800 pounds per hour"; primary heat was "the 10⁶ Btu/hr produced by the Nuclear Power Plant"; 5,000-gallon insulated, heated storage tank. The first well passed 500 ft deep, "nearing the maximum head for the type of deep well pump in use," and was moved in May 1962 (the gallons produced are garbled in the OCR: "3.5 x 10 gallons," exponent lost); "More than 5 x 10⁶ gallons have been pumped from the new well as of September 1964. The overall average monthly consumption rate has been slightly more than 230,000 gallons" (the OCR shows "230~00"; reading as 230,000 is DERIVED from context). Quality: "better in quality than water obtained by triple distillation in glass." **DERIVED:** 230,000 gal/month ÷ 30.4 = about 7,600 gal (about 28,700 L) per day; per person at the 100-person design figure, about 76 gal (287 L) per day (this includes the reactor's needs, which the text says the well also met).
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 51], the reactor's heat sink as a second water source:** the glycol heat sink, in about 2 years, produced "a subsurface cavity containing approximately 20,000,000 gallons of water," "entirely suitable for drinking," with no radioactivity above background; Clark: "the heat sink offers a most economical means of supplying water and cooling the plant with a single system."

**South Pole Rodwells.**
- **SOURCED [SR95-10]:** the first Pole well (1972 to 1973) ran in firn at "-51°C (-60°F), compared to a firn temperature of -28.9°C (-20°F) at Greenland sites," worked "without significant adverse freezing problems," and was stopped by "a frozen fuel line at the surface boiler" after "about 416,400 liters (110,000 gallons)." After 1,704 hours: 37,836 gal of useful water, 49,735 gal lost by leakage into the firn, average energy input 428,404 Btu/hr, 32.2 lb of water per lb of fuel.
- **SOURCED [SR95-10], design brief for the new station's well:** withdraw "2270 L (600 gal)/day of water during the winter (Feb-Sept) and 7570 L (2000 gal)/day during the summer (Oct-Jan)"; keep the well "for at least 5 years and preferably 10 years with a reservoir bottom depth not to exceed 152.5 m (500 ft)." Base case: reservoir 144 m (473 ft) deep after 10 years at an average 97,309 W (332,000 Btu/h), all from generator waste heat; 10-year totals 3,924,607 gal of water and 208,196 gal of fuel (if fuel-fired). A warning case: with too little heat, once summer withdrawal of 2,000 gal/day began, "the reservoir sank rapidly and collapsed after 421 days of operation." Chosen preliminary design (case 10): 62 days on fuel oil (11,385 L), then 87,930 W (300,000 Btu/h) of waste heat; after 303 days the pool is 84.4 m down and holds 1,018,276 L, having delivered 757,082 L. **DERIVED:** 3,924,607 / 208,196 = about 18.9 gal of water per gal of fuel over 10 years if all heat came from fuel.
- **SOURCED [SPS-RW]:** water surface 217 ft below the snow (first well), 295 ft (1995), 390 ft (2005); pools 39 ft across and 24 ft deep, about 80 ft across and 52 ft deep, and 72 ft deep, respectively; output 550 gal/day (first well, with 22 winterers), 1,450 gal/day (1995), about 2,450 gal/day (2005); "about 30 gallons of water per gallon of fuel" for the first two Pole wells versus about 55 at Camp Century; failures: frozen fuel line (1972 to 1973); March 1994 power outage, a heat-trace fire the next day and a freeze-up, after which "The snow melter ran for one more winter"; an ice lens over the water surface found in 1995 that a 2002 explosives attempt did not remove.
- **SOURCED [WP-RW]:** "The lifespan of a Rodriguez well is over seven years"; Rodwell-1 (1993) gave drinking water 1995 to 2003 and wastewater 2003 to 2012; Rodwell-2 (2002) drinking water 2003 to 2012, wastewater 2014 to 2025, "reached capacity as a wastewater outfall"; Rodwell-3 (2007) drinking water 2012 to now, wastewater projected 2030; Rodwell-4 projected 2030.
- **SOURCED [BRR]:** raw water leaves the well at about 36 °F; return water is heated to about 84 °F by "a glycol loop carrying generator waste heat"; heating a gallon from −60 °F to 50 °F is a 110 °F rise, plus the latent heat of fusion (about 350 Wh per gallon); a Rodwell is used "for several years" until it holds "millions of gallons"; two-minute showers are a rationing measure.

**Snow melters (the alternative).**
- **SOURCED [ERDC/CRREL TR-11-10]:** at Summit, water comes from snow "melted by waste heat in the garage shop," UV-treated, 1,514-L tank.
- **SOURCED [Wikipedia, Neumayer Station III]:** snowmelt "using 25 kW of waste heat from the diesel generators."
- **SOURCED [lswilson DEW page]:** DYE-2 scooped snow with "a 300 foot, variable height cable-way."

### 4.2 Sewage disposal into the ice

- **The method (SOURCED [TR174, p. 25]):** "dumping the waste into an unlined snow pit, from which it melted a vertical shaft down into the snow to an impermeable layer (approximately 100 feet), where it spread laterally and formed a bell-shaped cavity." Trash and other solid waste were buried in snow trenches "in the same manner as in normal sanitary fills."
- **Camp Century numbers (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 42 to 44]):** about 3.26 × 10⁶ gallons of liquid waste in the first two years, at 60 to 88 °F; in August 1962 the sump held 1.3 × 10⁶ gallons at a mean 33 °F, kept liquid by "the daily addition of 4650 gallons," adding "approximately 1.0 to 2.0 x 10⁶ Btu" a day, "365 to 730 x 10⁶ Btu/yr." That heat "warmed the surrounding snow mass several degrees and greatly accelerated the rate of deformation of the trenches"; the floor at the end of Trench 19 "settled about four feet, necessitating removal of the two T-5 buildings nearest the sump." The sump, unvented and only 150 ft from quarters, made "the odor of sewage ... almost unbearable in the nearest quarters by the following summer," with odor through Trenches 18 to 20; venting reduced but did not end it. Contamination spread "about 170 feet" sideways in less than two years. Clark's rule: a separate, sealed, vented sump trench at least 500 ft from buildings and 1,000 ft from the water well; keep records of discharge volume, frequency and temperature.
- **SOURCED [WP-CC] (2016 estimates, from Colgan et al. 2016):** the abandoned camp holds about "200,000 liters of diesel fuel" and "24 million liters of untreated sewage," buried about 36 m deep as of 2016; they could be exposed between 2135 and 2179 in one model; a 2021 assessment found meltwater "never penetrated more than 1.1 meters."
- **South Pole (SOURCED [SPS-UT1]):** the 1970s gravity outfall failed when drifts pushed down one end and snow over the sewage bulb settled; "the bulb filled and flooded the end of the utilidor" (1979 to 1980); fix: lift shaft, pumps, a new bulb, later grinder pumps. **SOURCED [SPS-RW]:** in winter 2005 the sewer bulb froze over and had to be melted through. **SOURCED [BRR]:** spent Rodwells become the "sewage outfall," with "millions of gallons" of raw wastewater stored beneath the station.
- **Summit (SOURCED [ERDC/CRREL TR-11-10]):** "All waste water is eliminated from the building through a gravity-driven, insulated sewer outfall pipe drilled roughly 9 m into the snow."
- **The rule in Antarctica today (SOURCED [Annex III to the Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty, text at https://www.biodic.go.jp/english/biolaw/nan_e/nanehu3.html]):** Article 2(2): sewage and domestic liquid wastes "shall, to the maximum extent practicable, be removed from the Antarctic Treaty area"; Article 4(2): not, as far as practicable, onto sea ice, ice shelves or the grounded ice sheet, but stations inland on ice shelves or the ice sheet "may be disposed of in deep ice pits where such disposal is the only practicable option," and such pits may not sit on ice-flow lines ending at ice-free areas or in high-ablation areas; Article 5(1)(b): coastal stations with "approximately 30 individuals or more" average weekly occupancy in summer must at least macerate sewage before sea discharge. **UNVERIFIED (search snippet of Australian regulations):** Australia's national rules are stricter and bar disposal onto the ice sheet.
- **Radioactive liquid waste (SOURCED [TR174, p. 50]):** the reactor's liquid waste went into the ice under the Danish-American limit of 50 mCi per year; solid radioactive waste had to leave Greenland.

### 4.3 Fuel storage in snow and under arches

- **South Pole (SOURCED [NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, https://gml.noaa.gov/obop/spo/dome.html]):** "forty-five 10,000 gallon fuel tanks which have a capacity of holding a total of 450,000 gallons of JP-8." **SOURCED [Jeffrey Donenfeld, first-hand blog, https://www.jeffreydonenfeld.com/2012/12/the-south-poles-fuel-supply/]:** fuel is flown in inside LC-130 tanks and drained into holding tanks; tanks sit in buried supply, service and fuel arches reached by sub-ice corridors at "a constant ambient temperature of -60F," so there is "almost no vapor pressure in the tanks"; an emergency escape hatch at the back of the fuel arch; an unverified hearsay cost of "around $35-$40" per gallon delivered. **UNVERIFIED (search snippet):** the arch "when originally constructed ... was on the surface as were these fuel tanks"; the station's first phase from 1997 was a "subsurface industrial complex" of fuel storage, garage and shops, and power plant.
- **Camp Century:** POL storage tanks were in the design list; fuel storage tanks budgeted at $200,000 (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 27, 31]).
- **Neumayer III:** 315,000 L of winterized diesel (SOURCED [Wikipedia]).
- **Old dome:** fuel bladders in the transverse arch (SOURCED [WP-SP]).

### 4.4 Ventilation and carbon monoxide

- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 32]:** at Camp Century the planned ventilation systems were not installed, but "Convection and the natural air permeability of snow provided adequate fresh air in the tunnels to prevent dangerous carbon monoxide levels from accumulating." Buildings drew fresh air "by a system of louvers and fans." The budget had "Alarm systems (fire & carbon monoxide)" at $10,000.
- **SOURCED [Wikipedia, Halley Research Station, §3.5]:** Halley III was abandoned because of "access and ventilation problems" once buried 12 to 15 m.
- **UNVERIFIED (search summary of a Museums Victoria article and a review; not fetched):** at Mawson's Cape Denison base (1912 to 1914) two men collapsed in a snow-dug shelter, "Aladdin's Cave," from carbon monoxide; Richard Byrd's 1934 solo winter at an interior weather station was marked by CO poisoning from loose, ice-clogged stove-pipe joints and a leaking generator exhaust.
- **NOT FOUND:** CO measurements inside any large under-snow facility (the 1959 *British Medical Journal* paper by L. G. C. E. Pugh, "Carbon Monoxide Hazard in Antarctica," is image-only at PubMed Central).

### 4.5 Fire safety and escape routes

- **Camp Century (SOURCED [TR174, pp. 27, 31 to 32]):** fire walls between buildings were specified but not installed "owing to shortage of time and funds"; plans to add them in 1961 were canceled "as it was considered that the installed sprinkler systems and other fire fighting equipment were more than adequate to isolate any fire"; the escape hatches had "spiral steel stairways" (listed by Clark among "bulky and unnecessarily heavy hardware"). The 1957 Fistclench layout drawing is labeled with a fire wall and an escape hatch (OCR of Fig. 8: reading UNVERIFIED).
- **South Pole:** a board at the tunnel entrance shows who is inside "in case of an emergency" (SOURCED [SPS-UT2]); escape hatches were built with the 1996 to 1997 sewer tunnel (SOURCED [SPS-UT1]); an emergency escape hatch at the back of the fuel arch (SOURCED [Donenfeld]); the 2014 tunnel-widening was driven partly by the difficulty of "pulling out an injured person" (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Fires at polar stations (SOURCED [Cool Antarctica fire history, https://www.coolantarctica.com/Antarctica%20fact%20file/science/antarctica_fire_history.php]):** Hope Bay 1948, base largely under snow ("a mound of snow with chimneys standing through it"), 2 killed, the survivor "lived alone in a tent for 16 days"; Mawson power house 1958 and 1959; Mirny 3 August 1960, 8 killed; Progress 5 October 2008, 1 killed, radio destroyed, station "unable to contact outside world for 4 days"; Comandante Ferraz 25 February 2012, generator hut, 2 killed. Why fires are worse there: "The cold temperatures make it very dry"; "unlikely to be very much liquid water around to fight it with"; "no possibility of rescue for weeks or months"; stores kept in a separate hut so supplies survive a station fire.
- **Vostok, 1982:** a short circuit caused a fire that destroyed a diesel generator and killed one man (SOURCED [MEDUZA]). **UNVERIFIED (search snippets):** 12 or 13 April 1982; in a 15-minute fire "all three diesel engines and their backups were destroyed"; the remaining 20 men wintered without electricity or heating because aircraft could not reach them for eight months.

### 4.6 Keeping the facility's own heat from melting the ice around it

- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 27]:** heated buildings stood on "timber cribs or piles, with approximately 2 feet of unobstructed clearance between the snow trench floor and building floor."
- **SOURCED [TR174, pp. 36 to 41]:** heat from buildings, reactor, vehicles, lights "and even people" raised trench temperatures; where heat concentrated, snow deformed "more rapidly than predicted by several fold"; convection barriers (omitted) and heat-expelling ventilation (not installed) were the planned defenses; air wells and open winter portals were the tested remedy (see §2.6). Sewage heat of 365 to 730 × 10⁶ Btu per year sank floors (see §4.2).
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 51]:** the reactor's waste heat was dumped into a pool in the ice (the glycol heat sink: glycol condenses turbine exhaust in a closed loop; water pumped from a pool in the ice cools the glycol in a heat exchanger), melting a 20-million-gallon cavity in about two years.
- **SOURCED [TR174, p. 52]:** "Subsurface camps on ice caps are feasible and practicable, but only in environments as cold or colder than Century."
- **SOURCED [SPS-UT2]:** the 1999 to 2002 South Pole tunnels were near ambient (about −60 °F) and about 20 degrees colder than the 1996 to 1997 tunnel, which the author suspects was warmed by nearby sewer bulbs.
- **SOURCED [ERDC/CRREL TR-11-10]:** the Big House's original timber footings are now about 18 m below grade (the building is lifted off them, not heated through them); heat loss through its envelope was the main deficiency found.

## 5. Scale and people

### 5.1 The largest enclosed volumes found that were built in ice or snow

- **Camp Century's trench network (1959 to 1960).** "21 tunnels with a total length of 9,800 feet (3.0 km)" (SOURCED [WP-CC]); "Tunnels were about 7 meters wide," the four reactor trenches "12 meters wide," "16 emergency escape hatches," two ramped entrances, three Peter millers "each removing more than 900 cubic meters of snow per hour" (SOURCED [Jay R. Thompson, "Benchmarks: October 1, 1960," *Earth*, 23 August 2016, https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-october-1-1960-camp-century-cold-war-ice-fortress-built/]). **DERIVED (rough):** 3,000 m of trench × about 7 m wide × about 7 m high ≈ 150,000 m³ of excavated and roofed space (assumes a rectangular section; the true arched section is smaller). The Atomic Heritage Foundation gives "26 tunnels, almost two miles in total" (SOURCED [https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/?p=31378], conflict logged).
- **Camp Century's reactor heat-sink cavity:** about 20,000,000 gallons of water melted into the ice in about two years (SOURCED [TR174, p. 51]). **DERIVED:** 20,000,000 gal × 3.785 L = about 75,700 m³.
- **Project Iceworm (planned, never built):** 4,000 km of tunnels, up to 600 missiles 4 miles apart (SOURCED [WP-IW]); "Altogether, the project would have required 11,000 soldiers to live full time under the ice," with "60 Launch Control Centers (LCCs)," and tunnels over "an additional 52,000 square miles," possibly 100,000 (SOURCED [AHF]; based on the 1960 Army study *Strategic Value of the Greenland Ice Cap*). Reasons given for cancellation (SOURCED [AHF]): the ice sheet was "simply too unstable to support the project," the Minuteman needed cold-weather modification, missiles under the ice were hard to locate and communicate with, and NATO nuclear sharing went to the Navy's Multilateral Force proposal instead.
- **The South Pole dome and arches:** dome 160 ft × 52 ft high with steel archways 46 × 79 ft (SOURCED [WP-SP]); fuel arch with 45 × 10,000-gal tanks (SOURCED [NOAA]); the 2024 report on the arches (SOURCED [Polar Journal guest article, 15 February 2024, https://polarjournal.net/problems-at-the-pole/]): the logistics arch holds nearly two years of station food and was built in the 1970s (paraphrase); the power plant and vehicle maintenance arches, added in the late 1990s and built below grade, had their life shortened by several decades (paraphrase); ice pressing in has reduced the gap between the arches and the buildings inside them from three feet to inches in places (paraphrase); "a failure in one or more of the arches could occur in the next five to seven years."
- **Into the Glacier, Langjökull, Iceland (opened 2015):** "the 500-meter (1,640-foot) ice tunnel inside Langjökull Glacier remains the largest of its kind in the world"; dug in "14 months of excavation"; visitors "descend 25 meters (82 feet)"; "5,500 cubic meters (194,000 cubic feet) of ice were removed"; over 320,000 visitors in ten years; closures for COVID-19 in 2020 and "glacier flooding in 2022" (SOURCED [Arctic Adventures press release, June 2025, https://mb.cision.com/Main/23464/4157937/3482278.pdf , read via text extraction]). Other sources give 300 m, 550 m, 800 m and 30 m depth (UNVERIFIED snippets; conflict logged).
- **Icehotel, Jukkasjärvi, Sweden (a building made of snow and ice, rebuilt every winter since 1990):** "a total floor area of some 5,500 square meters"; about 1,000 metric tons of ice and about 30,000 m³ of a snow-and-ice mix each year (SOURCED [Guinness World Records, "Largest ice structure," https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/largest-ice-structure]); construction takes six weeks; 15 to 20 ice rooms and 12 art suites; about −5 °C inside; ICEHOTEL 365 (opened November 2016) "uses solar-powered cooling to remain frozen year-round" (SOURCED [https://www.emeoutlookmag.com/construction/icehotel-frozen-icon]). Builder head count: UNVERIFIED (snippets: "about 100" visiting artists and designers, or "about a dozen").
- **Harbin Ice-Snow World, China (open-air ice and snow buildings, rebuilt yearly):** 2025 edition 1.2 million m², more than 400,000 m³ of ice and snow; "about 10,000 construction workers"; 200,000 m³ of river ice "harvested last winter has been preserved throughout the year" under multilayer insulation, "kept ... from melting even when temperatures exceeded 30 C in the peak of summer" (SOURCED [China Daily, 27 November 2025, https://govt.chinadaily.com.cn/s/202511/27/WS6937fb5e498e368550338a73/harbins-winter-wonderland-takes-shape-with-record-use-of-ice-and-snow.html]). **UNVERIFIED (snippet):** the 2024 edition used 300,000 m³, "More than 10,000 ice construction workers and nearly 1,000 pieces of mechanical operation equipment."
- **Svartisen Subglacial Laboratory, Norway (a laboratory reached through hydropower tunnels in rock, under the base of the Engabreen glacier):** "under 200 meters of glacier ice"; established 1992 during excavation for a hydroelectric plant; researchers melt "an opening in the ice" with "fire hoses with hot water," giving "a tunnel that is about 2 m high," which "shrinks to half its size each day"; "you can end up being trapped in the ice" if you do not watch the time; the opening is closed with steel beams when not in use (SOURCED [Science Norway, 2012, https://partner.sciencenorway.no/forskningno-glacial-geology-glaciers/worlds-most-claustrophobic-lab/1451112]). This is temperate (melting-point) ice, not cold polar ice.

### 5.2 Crews and who did what

- **Camp Century:** designed for 100 year-round (SOURCED [TR174]); "85 to 200 residents at a time" (SOURCED [Earth 2016]); "capable of housing 200 soldiers" (SOURCED [AHF]); reactor crew 18 (SOURCED [TR174]); a 20-man NCO crew shut down and winterized the reactor (SOURCED [USMA Class of 1955 compilation, https://www.west-point.org/class/usma1955/D/Hist/Century.htm]).
- **Camp Century construction:** Army Polar Research and Development Center troops, with Peter-miller operators trained in Michigan and a reactor crew trained at the ALCO factory (SOURCED [TR174, App. A]); materials 1,000 short tons (1959) and 4,600 short tons (1960) (SOURCED [TR174]).
- **South Pole:** first winter 1957, Paul Siple with "17 Navy and civilian specialists" (SOURCED [*Time*, "Science: Life in the Deep Freeze," 23 December 1957, https://time.com/archive/6869850/science-life-in-the-deep-freeze/]); 49 winterers in 2013 (SOURCED [The World, 14 August 2013, https://theworld.org/stories/2013/08/14/daily-life-south-pole]); about 150 in summer, capacity about 170 (SOURCED [Polar Journal 2024]); one winter in the dome era: "we had a crew of 50 in a station built to house about 20," over half construction workers; about 220 in summer with flights six days a week (SOURCED [first-hand account, "True Story: I Lived At The South Pole For A Year," 2014, https://www.yesandyes.org/2014/02/true-story-i-lived-at-south-pole-for.html]).
- **Vostok:** 30 to 35 summer, 12 to 13 winter; trades named: electricians, mechanic-drivers, cooks, radio operators, programmers, surgeons, anesthesiologists (SOURCED [MEDUZA]).
- **Halley VI:** about 13 winterers, including a chef, doctor, meteorologist, data manager, electricians and a station leader (SOURCED [Wikipedia, Halley]).
- **Neumayer III:** 60 summer, 9 winter (SOURCED [Wikipedia, Neumayer III]).
- **Summit:** a lift crew of 5 (two carpenters, trades helpers, an electrician) for about 3 weeks (SOURCED [ERDC/CRREL TR-11-10]); GISP2 camp designed for up to 40 in summer (same).
- **Camp Tuto (the support base at the ice edge):** 450 military personnel reported in 1958, two doctors and a dentist (SOURCED [WP-TUTO]).

### 5.3 Daily life, shifts and morale (first-hand and contemporary accounts)

- **Camp Century, plans (SOURCED [POPSCI60], February 1960, before completion):** "eight to 10 hours a day" of work; "rotating four-month tours of duty"; "Movies will be shown every night"; television relayed from Thule; a chaplain and chapel, laundry and barber shop, a library sharing quarters with the Base Exchange, "a 10-bed hospital with an operating room"; steak "in plentiful supply" with an extra half ration for the cold.
- **Camp Century, as lived (SOURCED [USMA 1955 compilation], emails written in 2013 by Leon McKinney, reactor operator on a "6-month tour from Sep 1962-Feb 63," and Joe Franklin, who ran the reactor removal):** outside temperatures "from 15 above to 65 below F" in "24-hour darkness and bad weather"; "about 3' of snow per month"; runway lights on drums had to be raised "every 4 weeks"; ice built up on tunnel ceilings and walls because "all of the air cooling was never built," and the "larger share of Camp EM ... spent much time with chain saws" cutting it back; the planned layout "was not built out," with no gym or theater (conflict logged); the commander's attempt to close the NCO club, "they were drinking over there," set off a near-revolt on New Year's Eve; a polar bear entered the main tunnel; an emergency appendectomy and a finger reattachment were done on site; the reactor removal compressed "the planned two-year schedule into less than one season." McKinney himself noted he was unsure some memories were accurate.
- **South Pole, first winter 1957 (SOURCED [*Time* 1957]):** huts joined by under-snow tunnels; a "snow mine" dug below the crust supplied water for weekly baths, each man spending about eight hours a month there at about −61 °F ("250 speedy steps down, 250 weary steps back"); movies three times a week; a weekly radiotelephone call home; Siple's rule, "work like hell most of the time; be pleasant to everyone all of the time"; the men refused a stateside psychologist's questionnaire about one another and the doctor tore it up.
- **South Pole, dome era (SOURCED [yesandyes first-hand account]):** "restricted to 4 minutes of running water per week for showering," one laundry load; general assistants shoveled "snow for 9 hours a day at 11,000 feet"; three meals a day plus a fourth, late-night meal in summer; about 4,000 calories a day; a 6 × 8 ft room with walls "more like cubicle dividers"; no flights "Feb 15-Oct 23"; "The best thing about wintering over was the people, and the worst thing about wintering over was the people"; "you have the same conversations over and over to the point where you want to scream"; most people come home "too fried (emotionally) to work right."
- **South Pole tunnels as a social space (SOURCED [IRIS]):** workers carve niches in the tunnel walls "to mark seasons, big events, and other occasions."
- **South Pole water (SOURCED [BRR]):** two-minute showers as rationing.
- **Vostok (SOURCED [MEDUZA]):** winterers spend about 1.5 years because of the overland route; "the living conditions at Vostok are still uncomfortable. It's old and obsolete."
- **UNVERIFIED (snippets):** isolation can bring "winter-over syndrome," with poor focus and disrupted sleep; "some people ... come back to work here for a second or even a third year."

### 5.4 What broke (as recorded in the sources above)

- **Structures:** Camp Century's Wonder Arches buckled within a year and were raised twice by about 6 ft; the reactor room ceiling dropped and was lifted 5 ft in mid-1962 (SOURCED [TR174], [WP-IW]); unsupported snow arches whose forms came off early "deformed rapidly" (SOURCED [TR174]); [OUT OF SCOPE (site history), not an input: ~~Old Byrd collapsed after about four years (SOURCED [WP-BY])~~]; the first South Pole station's roof caved in after abandonment (SOURCED [WP-SP]); Halley II lasted six years, Halley III was buried 12 to 15 m in ten (SOURCED [Wikipedia, Halley]); the South Pole dome's base-ring beams broke from overstress in 1988 (SOURCED [WP-SP]); DYE-3's frame developed "large secondary stresses" (UNVERIFIED snippet); the South Pole arches are being crushed and the elevated station has never been raised as designed (SOURCED [Polar Journal 2024]).
- **Utilities:** Camp Century's unvented sewage sump fouled the nearest quarters and its heat sank a trench floor 4 ft (SOURCED [TR174]); the South Pole sewer bulb flooded the utilidor (1979 to 1980) and froze over (2005) (SOURCED [SPS-UT1], [SPS-RW]); Rodwells froze after fuel-line and power failures (1972 to 1973, 1994) (SOURCED [SR95-10], [SPS-RW]); the current Pole well was described in 2024 as having about three years left with its replacement not yet built (SOURCED [Polar Journal 2024]).
- **Power:** PM-2A turbine faults in 1961 (about 2.5 months without electric output) and weeks offline in 1962 while the reactor trench roof was repaired (SOURCED [TR174]); Halley VI's coolant leak in July 2014 cut power and heat for 19 hours at −55 °C (SOURCED [Wikipedia, Halley]); the Vostok fire of 1982 (SOURCED [MEDUZA]).
- **Access:** portal ramps drifted shut within hours in storms; ramp floors disaggregated under tracked vehicles (SOURCED [TR174]); tunnel machines at the Pole broke down "every week or two" (SOURCED [SPS-UT2]).
- **Institutions and money:** year-round operation at Camp Century ended in 1963 "to reduce Greenland R&D support costs" (SOURCED [TR174]); at the Pole "Budget shortfalls suspended further efforts on the project in 2024" (berm clean-up), and a small, understaffed logistics crew cannot keep up with waste (paraphrase) (SOURCED [Polar Journal 2024]).
