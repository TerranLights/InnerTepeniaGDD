## Pick 1 — Aran Islands, Ireland (made soil on bare limestone)

**Claim to test:** "Islanders built fields from sand and seaweed on bare rock and walled them in stone (K14, K12)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and widened.** The field-building is attested in a primary eyewitness account (Synge, written 1898 to 1901, published 1907) and in an 1890 newspaper report. The same seaweed shore also supplied a cash product, kelp ash sold by iodine content, burnt in the islands until about 1948, so the shore fed both the made fields and the cash economy. Today the made-soil landscape is managed mostly as protected grassland, and farming is a part-time supplement to tourism.

### A. Snapshot
- Three inhabited islands at the mouth of Galway Bay, Co. Galway: Inis Mór / Árainn (largest), Inis Meáin (middle), Inis Oírr (smallest, east). About 10 to 13 km from the Galway coast; total area about 46 km². **SOURCED** — Clean Energy Transition Agenda for the Aran Islands, CFOAT / EU Clean Energy for EU Islands Secretariat, Oct 2019, p. 6 (https://clean-energy-islands.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2021-11/CETA_AranIslands_Oct2019_FULL.pdf); Wikipedia "Aran Islands" (read 2026-10-29).
- **Population, Census 2022: 1,347 total** — Inis Mór 820, Inis Meáin 184, Inis Oírr 343. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia "List of islands of Ireland", table citing CSO CNA17, read 2026-10-29; the three figures sum to 1,347, matching the total given in Wikipedia "Aran Islands"). Note: Údarás na Gaeltachta's planning page gives Inis Mór 762, Inis Oírr 281, Inis Meáin 183 (these appear to be 2016 figures; the page does not date them). **SOURCED** (https://udaras.ie/en/our-language-the-gaeltacht/language-planning-areas/galway/oileain-arann). Historical: 3,500 in 1841. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- Area by island: Inis Mór 31 km², Inis Meáin 9 km², Inis Oírr 8 km². **SOURCED** (Údarás page above).
- **Defining feature:** "The islands are made up of Carboniferous limestone and have no naturally occurring topsoil." Islanders made soil "mixing layers of sand and seaweed on top of rocks." Dry-stone walls enclosing the fields total "1,600 km or 1,000 mi in all." **SOURCED** (Wikipedia "Aran Islands"). ⚠ A tourism page puts "1,600 kilometers of dry stone walls on Inis Mór alone" (lovetovisitireland.com, undated) — the two sources disagree on whether the figure is for all three islands or Inis Mór only; **UNRESOLVED**.
- Karst limestone region "almost identical to that of the Burren"; cliffs on the exposed south side, some over 300 ft; very little tree cover because of wind and shallow soil; protected as Special Areas of Conservation (Natura 2000). **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, p. 6). Over 75% of the islands' land area is Natura 2000. **SOURCED** (Teagasc AranLIFE page, https://teagasc.ie/environment/biodiversity-countryside/research/completed-projects/aran-life/).

### How the made ground was made (primary and secondary sources) [K14, K12, P8, SM:competence]
- **Synge, eyewitness, Inis Meáin (c. 1898 to 1901):** "The other day the men of this house made a new field. There was a slight bank of earth under the wall of the yard, and another in the corner of the cabbage garden. The old man and his eldest son dug out the clay, with the care of men working in a gold-mine, and Michael packed it in panniers — there are no wheeled vehicles on this island — for transport to a flat rock in a sheltered corner of their holding, where it was mixed with sand and seaweed and spread out in a layer upon the stone." And: "Most of the potato-growing of the island is carried on in fields of this sort — for which the people pay a considerable rent — and if the season is at all dry, their hope of a fair crop is nearly always disappointed." **SOURCED** — J. M. Synge, *The Aran Islands* (1907), Project Gutenberg #4381 (https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4381/4381.txt), read in full text locally.
- **Rye grown for thatch, not grain:** "The land is so poor that a field hardly produces more grain than is needed for seed the following year, so the rye-growing is carried on merely for the straw, which is used for thatching." Rye threshed by beating sheaves on stones set on a bare rock, in autumn, by "men and boys." **SOURCED** (Synge, same).
- **Water scarcity alongside soil scarcity:** a few unreliable springs; drinking water carried up "in a water-barrel by one of the women"; washing done in tide pools with sea water. **SOURCED** (Synge, same). [K5]
- **1890 newspaper report** ("The Homes of Privation: Hard Struggle of the Impoverished Arran Islanders," *Darlington Herald*, S.C., 3 Dec 1890, p. 4): "The soil in which the potatoes are cultivated is all artificial. It is made of a queer compost of sand and seaweed. The sand is carried from the seashore on the backs of donkeys and Irish girls to the spots selected for a garden." "It is a common occurrence for a young woman of sixteen or eighteen years to carry basket loads of sand weighing 100 pounds from one to three miles all day long." Rent described as consuming the produce. **SOURCED** (https://historicnewspapers.sc.edu/lccn/sn93067808/1890-12-03/ed-1/seq-4/ocr; OCR page read). Care: a syndicated 19th-century newspaper piece written to evoke sympathy; figures are the reporter's.
- Modern secondary accounts add animal dung as a third component; seaweed (kelp and wrack cast after storms) carried up in baskets and creels; sand from the strand at low tide; "each spring, before planting, families spread fresh seaweed across their fields as fertilizer"; crops potatoes, rye, cabbage, also oats, barley, carrots, turnips. **SOURCED as stated on the page** but these are undated tourism and food-blog pages without cited sources (lovetovisitireland.com; irishfoodhub.com/?p=8204). Treat as low-grade.
- Walls: built from limestone cleared from the fields; no mortar; serve as windbreaks, hold thin soil, mark holdings, control livestock. **SOURCED** (michaelstephenwills.com, "From Limestone to Pasture," blog; and Wikipedia). Low-grade source for the functions; the clearing-for-walls origin is consistent across sources.

Then farming: "close to 200 small farms on the three islands. Farming is generally not sufficient to sustain families living on the islands, and many also have other sources of income." Also small-scale fishing boats, "a knit wear factory," hosting Irish-language students in homes, public-sector work in schools and the nursing home, and "small-scale food production facilities ... which sell locally and export." **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, p. 7). [K7, K8]
- **Tourist season** May to September; "thousands of tourists daily during the busy summer months, almost tripling the permanent resident population"; students return home in summer to work. **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, p. 7). Up to 3,000 visitors a day in the season (doctor quoted, *Irish Times*, 5 Aug 2014). **SOURCED**. Dún Aonghasa (OPW site): 144,153 visitors in 2023; 119,893 in 2022; 83,892 in 2021. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippet summarizing OPW / afloat.ie; pages not opened).

- **Comharchumann Forbartha Árann Teo** (Árainn development co-op), est. 1991 by islanders; voluntary board elected yearly by shareholders; three full-time staff. It set up the recycling center, fuel distribution center and home-heating service, runs the park and playground, childcare, the public bus service; was instrumental in Áras Rónáin (home for the elderly); secured funding for the €46 million Cill Rónáin harbor development; lobbies for island services. **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, p. 12). [K1, K8]

[K1]
- **Comharchumann Fuinnimh Oileáin Árann Teo (CFOAT)**, the Aran Islands Energy Co-op, est. 2012 aiming at carbon neutrality; helped homeowners retrofit via SEAI grants; promotes EVs. **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, p. 12). - **Schools:** each island has its own secondary school; four primary schools (one each on the smaller islands, two on Árainn); students leave for university. **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, pp. 7, 14).
- **Health:** in 2014 one GP covered all three islands on 24/7 duty, with a second GP appointed in 2003 for Inis Meáin and Inis Oírr; Inis Oírr nursing cover cut to three days a week after a 2013 retirement; helicopter evacuations by Irish Coast Guard (one case ~€7,000 vs ~€400 for a chartered plane). **SOURCED** (*Irish Times*, "Offshore and on call," 5 Aug 2014). It reached its 1,000th service launch, first RNLI lifeboat in Ireland to do so (RNLI news, Aug 2026). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet; RNLI page 403). Medical evacuations from the islands are a recurring call type. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of RNLI news titles). [K14, K1]
- **Electricity:** 3 MW subsea cable from Rossaveel to Árainn, branching to the other two islands; islands ran on diesel generators until the late 1990s; ~50% losses on the cable. **August 2016 cable cut:** days before temporary generators arrived, two months to reconnect; communications cut; visitors left; "frozen food melted and vital medicines had to be stored in a local public house fridge as this was one of the few places with a backup generator." **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, pp. 7, 11). [K5, K9, K10]

Heating fuels (kerosene, gasoil, coal, diesel, wood) all imported by ferry. Almost 100 private diesel cars on Árainn. **SOURCED** (CETA 2019, pp. 7, 9). Passenger ferries from Rossaveel (year-round) and Doolin (seasonal); Aer Arann Islands flies from Inverin. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). [K5]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Seaweed as soil and as cash.** Kelp (seaweed ash) burning was a cash income; Synge describes seaweed collected after autumn and winter storms, dried, ricked until June, burnt 12 to 24 hours in low shore kilns holding about two tons, broken with crowbars, taken by currach to Kilronan, "tested to determine the amount of iodine contained, and paid for accordingly. In former years good kelp would bring seven pounds a ton, now four pounds are not always reached." **SOURCED** (Synge). Kelp burning in Connemara and the Aran Islands "persisted until about 1948"; kelp ash was a reason some western landlords imposed rack rents on coastal tenants. **SOURCED** — Guiry & Morrison, *J. Appl. Phycol.* 25 (2013): 1823–1830 (seaweed.ie PDF, text extracted and read). [K8, K9, K14]
- **No single trade:** Synge records each man as fisherman, currach handler, farmer, kelp burner, net mender, house builder and thatcher, maker of rawhide shoes, cradles and coffins; "His work changes with the seasons." **SOURCED** (Synge). [K7, K14, SM:competence]
- **The protected landscape is a product of the made fields.** AranLIFE (EU LIFE+, €2.4 million, four years from 2014; partners incl. the Department of Arts, Heritage and the Gaeltacht, the Heritage Council, Teagasc, IT Sligo, and the islands' farmers) addressed "under-grazed pastures and overgrown fields" as farming declined; targeted limestone pavement and orchid-rich grassland. **SOURCED** (Teagasc page). Ran on about 70 farms; scrub removal, wall rebuilding, water for livestock, grazing plans on 1,000 ha. **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K1, K8, K9]
- **Fragility of supply:** the 2016 cable cut (above) shows the islands' dependence on single lines. [K5, K9]

## Pick 2 — Chinampas of Xochimilco, Mexico (Mexico City)

**Claim to test:** "Made ground built in a lake, with canals as streets and markets on the water (K10, K12)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in its first two parts, NARROWED in the third.** Chinampas are made ground built in shallow lake water from staked enclosures filled with canal-bottom mud and plant matter, and the canals are the circulation network (canoes historically carried produce to Mexico City). But "markets on the water" today is mainly mobile vendors in small boats selling food, drinks and flowers to tourist boats; the main produce and plant markets are on land beside the canals. Also: chinampas are not floating, despite the common "floating gardens" label; and most of the remaining chinampa area is not in active cultivation.

### A. Snapshot
- **Xochimilco borough (alcaldía)**, southern Mexico City: population 442,178 (2020 census: 226,726 women, 215,452 men); third-largest borough by area. **SOURCED** for population (Wikipedia "Xochimilco," read 2026-10-29); sex split **UNVERIFIED** (snippet citing INEGI). Area given as 122 km² (Wikipedia) and 113.50 km² (snippet citing INEGI) — **figures disagree**.
- **Chinampa zone:** total surface 2,215 ha; "El 80% de las chinampas (20,922) están inactivas y no se cultivan" (80% of chinampas, 20,922, are inactive and not cultivated). Annual output about 19,000 t of maize and vegetables, mainly lettuce, romerito, purslane (verdolaga) and collards (FAO 2019, cited). **SOURCED** — Guibrunet et al., *Modos de producción y comercialización en las chinampas de Xochimilco*, technical report, UNAM / Cocina Colaboratorio, Dec 2021, pp. 9–10 (https://iies.unam.mx/proyectos/SistemasSocioecologicos/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/ModosProduccionComercializacion_ChinanpasXochimilco.pdf; text extracted and read). ⚠ A different source: of ~2,215 ha, "1,000 hectares are abandoned and 300 hectares have been urbanized," and "Less than 3% of the existing farms are actually productive." **SOURCED** as stated (*Mexico News Daily*, B. Platanella, 19 Dec 2023), but its basis is not given. The two figures do not agree on what fraction is active; both say a large majority is not.
- **Historical extent:** about 500 years ago the lake extended 350 km² with 170 km² of chinampas and 750 km of canals; today about 25 km² of chinampas and 170 km of canals. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia "Xochimilco"; secondary).
- **Protections:** 1986 presidential decree creating the "Zona de Monumentos Históricos de Xochimilco, Tláhuac y Milpa Alta" (89.65 km² in Xochimilco, 83 historic monuments, about 18,000 chinampas, with the active area much reduced since); UNESCO World Heritage inscription 11 Dec 1987; Área Natural Protegida "Ejidos de Xochimilco y San Gregorio Atlapulco" created 1992; Ramsar site 2004; FAO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System (SIPAM/GIAHS) 2018, the only one in Mexico. **SOURCED** (UNAM report 2021, pp. 7–10).
- **Defining feature, how the ground is made:** "un método de agricultura lacustre, formado por una red de canales e islas compuestas por ramas, rocas, vegetación acuática en descomposición y sedimentos del lago" (lake agriculture formed by a network of canals and islands made of branches, rocks, decomposing aquatic vegetation and lake sediment); built by driving in ahuejote (native willow, *Salix bonplandiana*) stakes to form narrow, long islets covered with layers of silt and earth. **SOURCED** (UNAM report, p. 7, citing Coe 1964, Rojas Rabiela 1984, Rössler 2006). Not floating: kept in place by "an interwoven system of ahuejote roots (willow) and reeds beneath its surface which served as both an anchor and a source of moisture." **SOURCED** (*Mexico News Daily*, 19 Dec 2023). Rows of ahuejotes act as windbreaks in winter and bird habitat. **SOURCED** (UNAM report, p. 7). Built plot dimensions (8 to 100 m long, 2 to 25 m wide; raised ~50 cm above water, dried weeks before planting) — **UNVERIFIED** (worldhistory.org snippet).

### B. What else exists there
- **Who farms (small survey, 40 respondents, 2020–21, not representative):** 92% men; for 58% the chinampa is their main income; 52% work the land with family, others with hired workers, alone, friends or neighbors; 68% own the land (others rent, borrow, or are workers); plots mostly 500 to 6,500 m². **SOURCED** (UNAM report, pp. 15–17). [K7]
- **Methods:** seeds germinated in chapines — "pequeños cuadros de lodo donde se germinan las semillas" (small squares of mud where seeds are germinated); 90% open-air, 10% micro-tunnel; fertilizers: manure (cattle, horse) 25%, water hyacinth 22% (an invasive plant covering much of the canals, used as fertilizer), compost 17%, organic residues 14%, vermicompost 11%; no respondent held organic certification. **SOURCED** (UNAM report, pp. 25–27). [K14]
- **What is grown (survey):** 41 plant types incl. tomato, tomatillo, chiles, squash, nopal, quelites (huauzontle, romerito, quintonil, pápaloquelite, quelite cenizo), lettuce, cilantro, epazote, maize (incl. a local chinampa maize variety noted as once abundant), beans, cempasúchil (marigold), calendula. **SOURCED** (UNAM report, pp. 20–22). [K12]
- **Ornamentals:** remaining chinampas largely function as nurseries for ornamental plants. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia; secondary). Poinsettias 2025: 216 producers in Xochimilco and Tláhuac grew 1.8 million plants on 29.6 ha; 1,711,376 sold (91.3%); revenue 98,201,910 pesos; government support 5.4 million pesos; reported by Sedema / DGCORENADR. **SOURCED** (*Excélsior*, 26 Dec 2025). (Not stated how much of this is on chinampas versus greenhouses elsewhere in the borough.) [K8]
- **Selling:** modes (survey): intermediaries 33%, Xochimilco market 32%, direct local sale 11%, home delivery, family, cooperative, Central de Abasto, online sale, tourists (3–4% each or less). Biggest problem: "No tener un lugar fijo donde vender" (no fixed place to sell), 52%. Produce moved by canoe, tricycle, bicycle-taxi, motorcycle, wheelbarrow, pickup to the dock (muelle) and on. In COVID-19, sales often fell more than 70%; some harvests thrown away. **SOURCED** (UNAM report, pp. 31–37). [K5, K9]
- **Restoration enterprises:** Arca Tierra (brothers Lucio and Pablo Usobiaga), supplying 47 Mexico City restaurants and 300+ families by weekly baskets; "around five metric tons of food per hectare per month"; won the 2026 Champions of Change award (Latin America's 50 Best Restaurants). **SOURCED** (*Mexico News Daily*, 2 Oct 2026). Founded 2009, works with 35 farming families — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). Yolcan (2011, Nahuatl "land of origin") — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K8, K14]
- **Water:** canals are refilled with treated wastewater from the Cerro de la Estrella plant (Iztapalapa), which is insufficient; groundwater extraction for the city causes subsidence; some chinampa areas flood while higher ones no longer receive canal water; some canals have disappeared. **SOURCED** (*N+*, 9 Dec 2022, upd. 1 Feb 2023). Cerro de la Estrella supplying canals since 1971; Xochimilco springs channeled to supply Mexico City from the Porfiriato (early 1900s) — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). ## Pick 3 — Inle Lake floating gardens, Myanmar (Shan State)

**Care note honored:** current political situation reported neutrally and only where it bears on the place (tourism, festival interruption, displaced people).
**Claim to test:** "Whole villages farm on floating made ground and live on the lake (K10)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with a narrowing.** Villages of stilt houses stand in the lake and many families farm floating beds anchored with bamboo. But the floating gardens are overwhelmingly a commercial tomato monoculture (about 90% of beds, per a 2022 study), most of their growth came in recent decades (they account for most of the lake's loss of open water since 1935), and they carry heavy chemical inputs that pollute the lake. "Whole villages farm" is too broad: lake villages also live by fishing, crafts, boat transport and (until 2020–21) tourism.

### A. Snapshot

- **People:** Intha, with Shan, Taungyo, Pa-O (Taungthu), Danu, Kayah, Danaw and Bamar. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). About 70,000 Intha; 124 villages, 12 built directly over the water — **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets). On-lake population about 60,000 in 2012; wider area population rose from about 380,000 to about 570,000 (2007 to 2014 census basis, as the study states). **SOURCED** — "Spatial and temporal assessment of human-water interactions at the Inle Lake, Myanmar: a socio-hydrological DPSIR analysis," *Environmental Monitoring and Assessment* (2022), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10661-022-10730-4 (read in two parts; authors not captured by the fetch). The region has 446 villages in total (Burma News International, Sept 2024). **SOURCED**.
- **Defining feature:** floating beds made from lake vegetation and mud, anchored to the lake bottom with bamboo poles. Wikipedia: farmers "gather up lake-bottom weeds from the deeper parts of the lake, bring them back in boats and make them into floating beds." The 2022 study: beds consist of "water hyacinths, soil from the wetlands, and seaweed"; "constant addition of seaweed and mud increases the weight"; mean lifespan 8 to 15 years, falling to about 3 years under heavy exploitation; "floating gardens cover an area of about 32 km2"; "Tomatoes are grown on 90% of the floating gardens," two to three seasons a year, often from imported hybrid seed from China and Thailand. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia; DPSIR study 2022).

Between 1992 and 2009 floating-farm coverage grew 500% according to the Myanmar government. **SOURCED** (AFP via TerraDaily, 14 Nov 2023). Main growth before 1990 (DPSIR study 2022). **SOURCED**. ## Pick 4 — Uros floating reed islands, Lake Titicaca (Puno Bay, Peru)

**Care note honored:** tourism has reshaped it; recorded plainly below.
**Claim to test:** "Ground itself is built and constantly renewed from lake reeds (K14)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Each island is a platform of cut totora root-mass blocks tied together and topped with criss-crossed layers of cut reeds that must be renewed on a cycle of weeks to months, and the whole island is eventually abandoned and rebuilt. Two narrowings: (1) one scholarly line (Orlove 2002, via Tiner 2014) suggests the floating islands themselves date from the 1800s rather than from deep antiquity; (2) since the islands moved near Puno after storms in 1985–86, the main livelihood is tourism, and the renewal work now serves a tourist economy as much as a fishing one.

### A. Snapshot
- **Lake Titicaca:** 3,810 m above sea level on the Peru–Bolivia border; slightly brackish (~5 ppt); seasonal level change averages 70 cm (peak late April, minimum December); levels have fluctuated up to 6.5 m over the past 100 years. **SOURCED** — Ralph Tiner, "The Uros of Lake Titicaca and their Dependence on Totora," *Wetland Science & Practice*, June 2014, pp. 13–16 (PDF text extracted and read; citing Orlove 2002, Erickson 2000).
- **Islands and people (figures vary widely by source and date):** "about 1200 people live on somewhere around 60 floating islands," the number having increased in the decade before 2014. **SOURCED** (Tiner 2014). "As of 2011, about 1,200 Uru lived on an archipelago of 60 artificial islands"; elsewhere "about 120 self-fashioned floating islands"; total Uru population 5,343 (Bolivia 3,343; Peru 2,000). **SOURCED as stated** (Wikipedia "Uru people"). Uros Chulluni, reached after half an hour by launch from Puno: "100 islas"; "más de 3.500 familias" try to live off tourism there (BBC Mundo via Andina, 29 May 2018). **SOURCED as stated**; the 3,500-families figure does not square with the population figures above and is flagged as doubtful. Spanish-language secondary pages give 90 islands in two sectors or about 50, and 272 families — **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Defining feature, how the ground is made:** two layers — "one a layer of the natural rootmass about 1 meter thick and the second layer – one of harvested reeds laid in a crisscrossed pattern." Root mass "excavated from the marshes in large rectangular sections (5 m x 12 m x 1 m thick)," strung together with ropes through holes in the slabs, then topped with fresh reeds; islands anchored to prevent drifting; reeds added "every 3 months during the dry season and monthly during the wet season"; when the platform grows thick enough to rest on the lake bottom it is abandoned and a new one built; islands "last for about 15-18 years." **SOURCED** (Tiner 2014, citing Poon 2007). Wikipedia: blocks (khili) about 4 × 10 m, anchored with ropes to eucalyptus poles driven into the lake bottom, new layers "about every two weeks to three months depending on weather," islands last about 25 years. **SOURCED as stated**. BBC Mundo: "the khili doesn't last more than 50 years"; the reed layer "must be renewed every two to three months." **SOURCED** (Andina 2018). (Figures disagree; the renewal rhythm of weeks-to-months is consistent.)

### B. What else exists there
- **Totora as the whole material economy:** homes, boats, mattresses, furniture, handicrafts (fans, mats, toys); when the platform is refreshed, reed houses "are simply lifted off the ground and moved to the replenished section"; houses raised a little above the platform against damp. Reed boats (balsas) last 3–6 months in normal use, 8–12 months with plastic tarps inside. The white inner base of the young shoots ("high in iodine") is eaten raw; tea made from the flowers; dried totora used as fuel; medicinal use (wrapping a sore). Fishing, fowling and small-scale aquaculture on the islands. **SOURCED** (Tiner 2014, citing Heiser 1978, Banack et al. 2004). Fish named: ispi, carachi, catfish. **SOURCED as stated** (Wikipedia). [K12, K14, R]
- **Services:** "escuelas, una posta médica y hasta iglesias evangélicas" (schools, a medical post and even evangelical churches) at Uros Chulluni. **SOURCED** (Andina 2018). Shared solar panels, kindergarten and elementary schooling, an Uru-run FM radio station. **SOURCED as stated** (Wikipedia). Older students attend high school and college in Puno, studying hospitality and languages. **SOURCED** (Slate, 25 Feb 2011). Residents use reed boats or small motorboats to reach other islands and Puno "for shopping or school." **SOURCED** (Andean Textile Arts, V. Glenn, 24 Jul 2023). [K5]
- **Tourism economy:** Lake Titicaca gets "more than 750,000 visitors each year"; Uros Chulluni averaged 2,800 visitors a month (Jan–Aug 2016); only one in ten visitors staying in Puno province visits the floating islands; Uros Chulluni has "unos 12 alojamientos" (about 12 lodgings) and about six restaurants. **SOURCED** (Andina 2018). "Upward of 200,000 visitors a year" and "more than 80 percent of the population working in tourism" (2011). **SOURCED as stated** (Slate 2011). [K8]
- **Shared-problem rules [K1, K6]:** an entry charge for the settlement, "para repartir el dinero entre todas las familias" (to share the money among all families); its president said each family had received only US$618 in four years. A rotation rule — visits to north islands one day, south islands the next — was tried, but more successful operators pressed to lift it. **SOURCED** (Andina 2018). Islands rotate visitor access daily, with residents fishing and making crafts on non-tour days. **SOURCED as stated** (Slate 2011; dated).
- **Friction [K9]:** tour operators demand commissions from island families for landing visitors; fights between families and islands; impostors; when family members fall out, "cada uno corta su parte de isla y se la lleva" (each cuts off his part of the island and takes it away). Some families moved to Uros Titino to reduce competition; two dissident families from Uros Chulluni taught a neighboring group (named in the article as Chimu) to build islands. **SOURCED** (Andina 2018).

### C. Cultural locations (named)
- **Uros Chulluni** and **Uros Titino** island sectors (above). **SOURCED** (Andina 2018).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The ground requires continuous labor, so the islands are family units: "several thatched houses, typically belonging to members of a single extended family." **SOURCED** (Slate 2011). Because the ground can be cut, family quarrels can literally divide an island (Andina 2018). [K4, K9]

- Origins: historical accounts say the Uru fled to the marshes from the Inca and later the Spanish; Orlove (2002) suggests the first floating islands were built "in the 1800s when land-dwellers from Coata and Huata moved to Lake Titicaca's marshlands." **SOURCED** (Tiner 2014). Historical prejudice by the Inca, Spanish and local Aymara against the Uru is described. **SOURCED as stated** (Slate 2011).

### E. Structural features

Fodder demand may drive increased cultivation around the lake (Orlove 2002, via Tiner). **SOURCED**. [K1]

## Pick 5 — La Geria, Lanzarote (Canary Islands, Spain)

**Claim to test:** "Vines grown in hand-dug pits in volcanic ash behind stone half-walls, where no soil existed."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in method, CORRECTED on "where no soil existed."** The vines do grow in hand-dug pits in volcanic gravel (lapilli) behind semicircular dry-stone wind walls. But the pits were dug **down through** the 1730–1736 eruption's ash to reach the older soil buried beneath it (the paleosol); the ash layer then works as a moisture-trapping mulch. So La Geria is soil that was buried and re-reached, not soil made where none existed. (Local terms for the gravel and the wall are in the slang file, not here.)

### A. Snapshot
- **Where:** central-southern Lanzarote, bordering Timanfaya National Park to the west. **SOURCED as stated** (search-result summaries of portalfruticola/agrolanzarote; see below). The protected landscape (Paisaje Protegido de La Geria) covers **5,255 ha** across five municipalities: Tinajo, Yaiza, Tías, San Bartolomé and Teguise. **SOURCED** (*La Voz de Lanzarote*, Nov 2024; agreement signed 7 Nov 2024). Declared a natural park by Law 12/1987 (19 June 1987) and reclassified as a protected landscape by Law 12/1994. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of Spanish Wikipedia mirror). Marca Canaria: "Espacio Natural Protegido desde 1994," natural park 1987, also a Special Protection Area for Birds (ZEPA). **SOURCED** (marcacanaria.com).
- **Vineyard inside it:** "in La Geria there are 1,368 hectares" of vine cultivation, of which "237 hectares are abandoned" (15%), 145.7 ha of those in the core. **SOURCED** (*La Voz de Lanzarote*, Nov 2024).
- **The eruption:** Timanfaya erupted 1730–1736, 2,056 days, Europe's longest recorded eruption; it "covered 23 percent of Lanzarote with lava and ashes" and buried 26 villages; Lanzarote's population nonetheless doubled from about 5,000 in 1730 to 10,000 by 1768. **SOURCED** (FreshPlaza report of an article in *Geology Today* by F. Pérez, J. C. Carracedo, A. Rodríguez, V. R. Troll and Uppsala colleagues; fetch did not capture the date).
- **Defining feature, how the ground works:** "the largest wind dispersing mafic pyroclastic field in all of the Canary Islands." The farming technology uses the lapilli's "high surface / volume ratio, its low heat capacity, small thermal conductivity and high emissivity": at night the open surface cools and condensation forms, water is held in the pores and filters down quickly, and daytime evaporation affects only the surface layer; "semicircular stone walls are constructed to serve as protective barriers"; "grapevines and other fruit are grown in holes dug in the lapilli." **SOURCED** (Geoparque Lanzarote, geosite LZ36 La Geria, https://geoparquelanzarote.org/en/geositio/lz36-la-geria-2/).
- **Pit dimensions:** pits (hoyos) opened "1.25 meters deep, until reaching the paleosoil"; lapilli depth in La Geria 4 to 5 m; walls placed perpendicular to the prevailing wind; 250 to 300 plants per hectare; moisture retained up to 12 months after rain. **SOURCED** (portalfruticola.com, 9 Aug 2017, reproducing agrolanzarote.com, the Cabildo's agriculture service). Marca Canaria describes a conical pit "de varios metros de profundidad, aproximadamente dos metros y medio" (several meters deep, about two and a half meters), with "una media luna de rocas como protección contra el viento" (a half-moon of rocks against the wind). **SOURCED**. (Depth figures differ by source and site.)
- **Discovery story:** a bishop sent to assess damage found that "in the fields where the layer of cinder that covered the earth was thin, the plants not only survived, but grew with more vigor." **SOURCED** (FreshPlaza / *Geology Today*). Rainfall about 150 L/m² a year. **SOURCED** (same). [K14, SM:competence]
- **Related technique:** the artificial "enarenado" — a 10 to 15 cm layer of lapilli spread over cleaned soil — allows cropping on under 100 mm of rain a year; renewed every 15 to 20 years as the ash mixes into the soil; used for onions, melons, watermelons, sweet potatoes, legumes, cereals; the natural ash zones (about a third of the island) carry vines, figs and fruit trees. **SOURCED** (portalfruticola 2017).

## Pick 6 — Ifugao rice terraces, Philippines (Ifugao Province, Cordillera)

**Care note honored:** facts about the Philippines are reported as plain real-world facts, with no national characterization.
**Claim to test:** "Made ground on mountainsides, fed by shared water rules and harvest rituals (K1, K2)."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with one major correction and one nuance.** The terraces are built ground on mountain slopes (lower ones stone-walled, upper ones earth-walled), watered from family-owned forest woodlots above them through shared canals with assigned watchers and maintenance crews, and the agricultural calendar is set and marked by rituals led by a rice-culture leader and ritual specialists. **Correction:** the long-repeated "2,000 years old" age is contested by the Ifugao Archaeological Project, whose dating points to wet-rice terracing expanding within the last 400 to 500 years, around the period of Spanish colonization in the lowlands. **Nuance:** the water rules are tied to private-but-shared forest ownership (the muyong), not to a single communal water board; and much of the ritual apparatus has weakened, with many terraces now abandoned.

### A. Snapshot
- **Ifugao Province**, Cordillera Administrative Region, northern Luzon: population 207,498 (2020 census), about 11.54% of the region. **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of PSA page; PSA returned 403). Banaue population 20,652 (2020). **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **World Heritage:** "Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras," inscribed 1995, "the first-ever property to be included in the cultural landscape category"; criteria (iii)(iv)(v); five clusters: Batad and Bangaan (Banaue), Mayoyao Central (Mayoyao), Hungduan (Hungduan), Nagacadan (Kiangan); on the List in Danger 2001–2012. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, read 2026-10-29). (Peñafiel's FAO paper says 1996; Wikipedia and other sources say 1995.) Also a FAO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System. **SOURCED as stated** (Wikipedia).
- **Defining feature (FAO background paper):** "a series of benches of irrigated terraces carved out following the contour of the mountain slopes," above 1,000 m; terraced paddies in Banaue, Hingyon, Hungduan, Kiangan, Lagawe, Mayoyao and Tinoc "cover a surface area of approximately 20,000 hectares"; lower terraces "wider and supported by stone retaining walls," steeper ones "sometimes less than two meters wide" and "not supported by stonewalls"; vertical spacing from under 0.5 m to as much as 5 m. **SOURCED** — Samuel Peñafiel, "Ifugao Rice Terraces: Agricultural Heritage Systems dynamic conservation and practices," FAO background paper, pp. 125–128 (https://www.fao.org/4/a1122e/a1122e19.pdf; text extracted and read; publication year not printed on the extracted pages).
- **Age (contested):** Peñafiel repeats the conventional "over 2,000 years." The Ifugao Archaeological Project (director Stephen Acabado) finds the earliest rice remains in sediments "roughly 675 years ago," with marked increases "between 470 and 530 years ago"; no wet rice in precolonial cooking-pot residues at Old Kiyyangan (settled about 1,000 years ago), only taro and a sugarcane-like substance; Acabado interprets wet-rice expansion as linked to people taking "refuge in the mountains when faced with colonization." **SOURCED** (Karen Coates, *Archaeology* magazine, May/June 2018). Other reports of the same project give 300 to 400 years and wet rice "after 1650." **UNVERIFIED** (snippets). [K9]

### B. What else exists there
- **Water rules [K1, K6]:** irrigation water comes from streams fed by "family woodlots called Muyong and Pinugo (natural forests) that dominate the ridges"; water is diverted into small canals flowing terrace to terrace. "Members of the village are assigned to clean and maintain the irrigation canals and others are assigned to watch any diversion of water to other terraces." Terraces are kept flooded year-round, even after harvest, so soil does not crack and collapse the terrace, and so buried straw decomposes. A work group (named in the source) "helps in constructing and maintaining the terraced walls and canals"; others monitor the canals daily for clogging and diversion. Farm labor is by group effort among neighbors, families and clans; "Men do the site preparations including irrigation ditch and terrace maintenance while women help in the weeding, pest control and harvesting." **SOURCED** (Peñafiel).
- **Muyong (family forest) rules [K1, K6]:** woodlots 0.5 to 2.5 ha; "an entire area is to be inherited and cannot be subdivided"; owners cut only what they need (big trees for lumber, branches for fuel), leave twigs and leaves to rot; "Neighbors are allowed to collect fuelwood and other plant products provided they ask permission from the owners"; 264 plant species recorded (171 for fuelwood, 11 for construction and carving, 70 food, 10 rattans, 45 medicinal); coffee, cacao, betel palm and citrus integrated. **SOURCED** (Peñafiel, citing Rondolo). A second source: private ownership "usually open for use by kin and, in some cases, peripheral residents"; owner's approval needed for logging for building or carving, not for firewood and fruit; disputes heard by a village council that gives a verdict. **SOURCED** (N. Matsushima & Y. Tojo, Japan Wildlife Research Center, Satoyama Initiative case study, submitted 3 May 2010; fieldwork Dec 2008). In 1996 the Department of Environment and Natural Resources made a special cutting-permit policy for muyong trees. **SOURCED** (Peñafiel). [K14]
- **Crops and food [K12]:** about 9 indigenous rice varieties; most popular "tinawon, a fragrant native upland variety"; mostly one crop a year; seeding Nov–Dec, transplanting Jan–Feb; six months planting to harvest; no inorganic fertilizer or pesticide in the traditional system; native fish raised in pits dug in wide terraces; taro and vegetables on mud mounds. **SOURCED** (Peñafiel). Tinawon: "Eating a cup of tinawon is like eating two cups of the commercial rice" (farmer's saying as reported). **SOURCED** (Mongabay, K. Lapniten, 29 Aug 2021).
- **Abandonment and economy [K9, K8]:** in Banaue, 540 of 1,607 ha of terraces abandoned (2018 data), nearly a third; in Bangbang village (Hungduan) about half; causes: low profit, heavy labor, cost of schooling, youth preference for urban work. Tourism pre-COVID (2017–2019): 71,400 tourists a year, nearly half foreign; 2019 spending about US$18 million. **SOURCED** (Mongabay 2021). Peñafiel lists threats: vegetable cash crops that do not need standing water, leading to terrace collapse; large earthworms (*Polypheretima elongata*) tunneling when paddies dry; rats; outmigration; wood-carving demand thinning muyong canopy; evangelization reducing rituals; traditional work groups and ritual leaders losing roles to elected officials and state workers. **SOURCED**.
- **Institutions:** Save the Ifugao Terraces Movement (SITMo); Ifugao State University's Satoyama Meister training program (Japanese government-funded); an Ifugao Rice Terraces–GIAHS Center. **SOURCED** (Mongabay 2021). [K1, K14]

### C. Cultural locations and traditions (named)
- **Agricultural ritual calendar [K2]:** a rice-culture leader (the tomona) "determines the start of the planting season"; planting opens with a ritual at the main terrace "selected hundreds of years ago from among those belonging to the kadangyan (nobility)," where a seated rice-god figure (bulol) is touched with blood of a sacrificed chicken or pig; three months after planting the paad ritual at the main village granary "binds villagers not to eat fish and shellfish during the rice growing season" (which lets terrace fish and shellfish breed); a ritual specialist (mumbaki) performs a rest-day ritual in mid-April and post-planting rituals; "The planting calendar must be strictly observed" so pests are spread across all fields; harvest is completed quickly "to deprive rodents of food." **SOURCED** (Peñafiel). [K1, K6, SM:competence]
- **Punnuk / Huwah, Hungduan (post-harvest):** 2024 sequence: a ritual marking the start of harvest (12 June 2024), harvesting (13 June), household rituals led by the lead (wealthy, land-owning) family and the ritual specialist Daniel Bimuyag, culminating 19–20 July 2024 in the tug-of-war in the Hapao River with a rope (pakid) made from a thin tree trunk, usually attoba, and a straw figure (kina-ag); villages Hapao, Baang and Nungulunan; 261 community participants and 871 visitors in 2024; supported by Municipal Ordinance No. 80 (2018) and the National Commission for Culture and the Arts; purpose: thanksgiving and "to ensure a good one the following year." **SOURCED** (*Daily Tribune*, 3 Nov 2024). ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

## FINAL — Round 1 summary (2026-10-29)

**Claims tested (each place on its own terms):**
1. **Aran Islands** — CONFIRMED and widened: fields made of sand, seaweed and scraped clay on bare limestone (Synge eyewitness; 1890 report); the seaweed shore also produced kelp ash sold by iodine content until about 1948. Island services today run through development co-ops with no local government.
