## 1 · #10 Independence and St. Joseph, Missouri (the trail "jumping-off" towns)

**Round 2 focus:** who lived in the towns besides the emigrants (enslaved and free Black residents, New Mexican and other traders, Native nations, later Eastern European and Jewish packinghouse communities), their own spaces and rules, the towns' civic and festival life now, and the open threads from Round 1 (route-news institution, town ordinances, cholera counts, Independence's eclipse).

### A. Other settings (sub-areas, housing, workplaces, off-season)

- [K4, K5, P4] **Where the spring crowd stayed (Independence).** "Arriving in early spring, emigrants … had filled the hotels and boarded or camped on nearby farms." The Square was ringed by "stores, warehouses, hotels, saloons, medical and law offices, and banks" around the courthouse. SOURCED — NPS/OCTA/City poster *An Epicenter of International Commerce* (PDF, text extracted locally; created July 2011).
- [K4, K10] **The hotel in 1838.** Recollections of John McCoy (brother of the first mayor) for the *Jackson Examiner*: Uncle Wood Noland's two-story frame hotel on the NE corner of the Square, with a broad east porch and a stable on Main Street; a son received arriving guests; produce came from the family farm's orchard and garden; **circuit-court week** was the hotel's high season (rooms cleaned and refurnished; lawyers from the whole district lodged there; a law student sang to them on the porch in the evenings). Mail came weekly, seven days from St. Louis, at 25¢ a letter. Pack trains of mountain trappers announced arrival by firing guns at the Owens & Aull store, and "arrivals led to festivities that lasted many days." SOURCED — W. L. Webb, *The Centennial History of Independence, Mo.* (1927), ch. IX, vintagekansascity.com (a 1927 civic history quoting a recollection; treat as such).
- [K4, P9] **Camps outside town.** The **Rice-Tremonti Home** (8801 E. 66th St., Raytown; 1844 farmhouse with an associated 1830s-era slave cabin) is "mentioned by many Oregon and California-bound diarists as a popular campsite and a place where they could purchase food"; **Hart Grove** (Kansas City) and **Flat Rock Creek** (Lenexa, KS) were trail campgrounds; **Lone Elm Campground** (Olathe, KS) is "where many … travelers spent their first night west of the Missouri" (now a 160-acre city park). SOURCED — NPS *National Historic Trails Auto Tour Route Interpretive Guide: Western Missouri through Northeastern Kansas* (PDF, text extracted locally).
- [K4, K9, P9] **The Neck (Independence's largest Black neighborhood after the Civil War).** Settled in the early 1800s near the Square, populated largely by formerly enslaved people; by the mid-1900s integrated with a Black majority and working-class white residents; bounded roughly by U.S. 24 (N), Spring St. (E), McCoy St. (W), College St. (S). Life: neighbors walked through each other's yards, shared garden food, fruit-tree wine and pies; **weekly fish fries**; children played in the streets until dark and caught crawdads in a creek. Redlined "hazardous" in the late 1930s; no city sewer or running water to most homes (outhouses into the 1960s); the city's only hospital did not treat Black patients (residents went to General Hospital No. 2 in Kansas City); local businesses were allowed to dump waste there. SOURCED — Savannah Hawley-Bates, KCUR, 30 Jan 2023.
- [K4, P4, P9] **St. Joseph's Polish-German-Swedish neighborhood** (between Messanie and Lafayette Streets, 17th–22nd Streets; pre-WWI): residents raised geese, kept gardens, smokehouses and chickens — "the feel of an agricultural village"; the Polish main street was Warsaw Street; most men worked in the South St. Joseph packinghouses or at a large brickyard behind St. Patrick's Church, others in downtown garment factories; streetcars to the packing district were unaffordable for most, so some moved south and founded **St. Stanislaus Church**, after which intermarriage ended the enclave's homogeneity. A 1909 walk down Olive Street passed shoemakers, painters, blacksmiths, barbers, clothing merchants, drug stores, fuel and feed dealers, cigar makers, a sheet-metal shop, a grocery on nearly every block, several saloons, butchers, confectioners and an ice-cream-cone maker; shopkeepers lived above their shops. A neighborhood baseball team played 1896–1908. SOURCED — Kimberly Schutte, *The Eastern European Immigrant Community of St. Joseph, Missouri, c. 1850–1950* (City of St. Joseph Historic Preservation, accepted by SHPO 20 May 2021; PDF, text extracted locally); The Clio, entry 154292. (The neighborhood's name is a local coinage and is not recorded here.)
- [K4, K9, K6, P9] **Packinghouse boarding houses, South St. Joseph, 1908–09.** In fall 1908 an estimated **2,000–3,000 Eastern European men** lived in the South End and worked in the packinghouses, many single or with families left behind, saving to send money home. Boarding houses were cheap and "tended to be run by immigrant women" offering familiar food, music and sociability. Health inspectors found 20 people in a six-room house (117 Texas Ave., three simultaneous typhoid cases); twelve men in one 12×14 ft room (5206 King Hill Ave.); 27 men and one woman in four rooms (Michigan Ave.); in Jan 1909 police reported ~100 men renting a small King Hill Avenue house, some sleeping in a dugout cut into the hill behind it. One proprietor (326 Ohio St.) charged 17 boarders $1.50 a week. A grocers' committee (May 1908) found ">$1,200 is sent from the South End to foreign countries each week" and claimed the men lived on ~$1.20 a week. SOURCED — Schutte 2021 (citing *St. Joseph Gazette*, *News-Press*, *Observer*, 1908–09).
- [K4, P9] **The Jewish neighborhood** around Temple Shaare Sholem (725 S. 7th St.) and Temple B'nai Yaakov (615 S. 10th St.) was "the heart of the Jewish community in the first half of the 20th century," mixed blocks with a grocery on nearly every one. ~3,300 Jewish residents in 1919; fewer than 100 by 2018. SOURCED — Schutte 2021 (citing *Kansas City Jewish Chronicle*, 6 Sept 2018).
- [K4, K9] **Market Square, St. Joseph** (between Market Square and S. 3rd St., Edmond to Felix): wholesale food district c. 1850–1940 (also called the Old Farmers Market); lots sold by Robidoux 1846; fruit wholesalers (Sandusky & Co. c. 1867; Willman 1880; Central Fruit c. 1900; Waris 1930s), confectioners (Vegely & Kneer, 1862), a CB&Q track up the middle of the square bringing fruit and vegetables; **Dobler's Hall** (c. 1906) was a meeting and eating place where bakers' conventions lunched, leather workers met twice a month and a fraternal lodge met. NRHP-listed 17 March 1972, razed from **1 Dec 1973** under urban renewal; delisted 1999. SOURCED — Rick Drozd, *St. Joseph Memory Lane*, "Market Square" (local-history site; the author warns "circa" dates may be inaccurate). **Patee Market House** (S. 10th St., city-funded 1860 on land John Patee gave in 1855; new building 1906) later housed charities, the Tuberculosis Society, the Visiting Nurses and the Board of Health laboratory; renamed Patee Market Health Center 1995. UNVERIFIED (search snippets of hmdb and ldsgenealogy pages).

### B. Subcultures

- [P9, K9] **Free and enslaved Black residents (trail era).** Independence held public slave auctions on the courthouse steps and St. Joseph "regularly hosted public slave auctions"; travelers bought enslaved helpers there for the journey. The towns were "particularly inhospitable to Black Americans, even those that were not enslaved"; "merchants, outfitters, hotels, and restaurants frequently refused their services to Black emigrants"; Black emigrants often had to persuade wagon companies to accept them, at extra cost in money and time. SOURCED — NPS, "Captivity and Independence: Race and Enslavement in the Trailhead Towns along the Missouri" (summarizing Shirley Ann Wilson Moore, *Sweet Freedom's Plains*, 2012).
- [P9, K14, SM:power rising step by step] **Emily Fisher** (1808–1898): born enslaved, her father Adam Fisher was her enslaver; family moved to Missouri in 1836 (traveling the last 100 miles of the Santa Fe Trail, Franklin to Independence); freed by her father in the 1850s; as a free woman **managed the Independence Hotel**, known among trail travelers for "exceptional service and remarkable cleanliness"; when Civil War trade fell she made and sold a **healing salve**, carrying samples around the area; her marriage to Rowan (Robin) was registered at the Jackson County courthouse on **14 Aug 1865**. SOURCED — NPS "Emily Fisher" page. A Kansas City Public Library record says the hotel was owned by Adam Fisher (UNVERIFIED snippet; ownership unsettled).
- [P9] **A mixed trading crowd.** In 1848 J. Quinn Thornton described wagons at Independence driven by Spaniards, Black drivers, "Americans resembling Indians" and people of mixed ancestry, including an enslaved man driving a six-horse team. Traders included **Manuel Harmony** (Spanish-born, partner in a New York firm shipping English goods to Independence); **Doña Gertrudis Barceló** (New Mexican, invested $10,000 in the 1843 trade; a monte dealer and gambling entrepreneur); German Jewish merchants such as Charles Ilfeld; the Wyandot chief **William Walker** leased an Independence warehouse and his nation invested in the Santa Fe trade. SOURCED — NPS *Sweet Freedom's Plains* ch. 2, "Cosmopolitan Crossroads." Webb (1927, ch. VIII, SOURCED as his claim) lists Spanish, Italian, German, English, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Osage and Kaw as languages heard in the town.
- [K14, K7, P8] **Trail labor (occupational).** NPS lists the trail's jobs 1821–1880: freighter (*fletero*), teamster, wagon master (trains of up to 30 wagons), ox and mule drivers (*buyero*), scouts, ranch-stop operators, interpreters, soldiers, mail carriers, wagon makers; "sun-up to sun-down," with no legal limits on hours, no minimum pay, no injury protection. SOURCED — NPS "Laboring for a Living on the Santa Fe Trail" (updated 10 March 2022). Teamster pay $25–$50 a month with rations (UNVERIFIED snippets of Kansas Historical Society/Legends of America); one source says Independence employed "several thousand" teamsters and packers (UNVERIFIED).
- [P9, K9] **Native nations around the towns** (adds to R1): emigrant wagons passed the reservations of the relocated Shawnee, Delaware, Sauk and Fox, Kickapoo, Wyandot and Potawatomi, carved out of Kanza and Osage homelands (>10,000 people from >20 eastern tribes relocated west of the Missouri). Residents "returned lost livestock, sold fresh vegetables, carried mail, and pulled wagons from the mud and ferried them across streams," while cholera, smallpox and measles spread from emigrant camps into their communities. A Kanza chief, Is-ta-la-she, 1863: "White men tell us we will be driven out … but we like this place and want to stay." SOURCED — NPS Auto Tour guide. The 1844 flood: "the highest mortality rates included the Wyandot Indians," ~100 dead of disease near present Kansas City, Kansas. SOURCED — en.wikipedia "Great Flood of 1844."
- [P9, K2] **The Iowa (Báxoje) today.** The Iowa Tribe of Kansas and Nebraska is headquartered at **White Cloud, Kansas** (~12,038-acre reservation in Brown and Doniphan Counties, KS, and Richardson County, NE; ~6,000 acres controlled, mostly in trust; current boundaries from the Treaty of 6 March 1861; the 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act drew the territorial line across the reservation, hence the name; split in the 1870s into Kansas-Nebraska and Indian Territory groups). Enterprises: Casino White Cloud, a farm, bee farm, oil, sanitation, tires, a foreign-trade-zone firm; services: White Cloud Health Center, housing authority, food programs, burial assistance; annual **Báxoje Fall Encampment and Powwow** (date not given). SOURCED — iowatribeofkansasandnebraska.com history page. A July rodeo, August demolition derby and September powwow (UNVERIFIED, Wikipedia snippet).
- [P9, K9] **Eastern European and Jewish St. Joseph (1850–1950).** ~150 Ukrainians early in the 20th century, mostly from one Galician district, nearly all packinghouse workers; they founded a **Ukrainian Byzantine Catholic Church** (526 Virginia St., 1917; pastors at first from the Winnipeg archdiocese); the **Ukrainian American Society** hall (5801 Lake Ave., 1958–1982) ran a youth choir and Ukrainian classes and took a beer-by-the-drink license in Dec 1958. Poles first attended German-language Immaculate Conception, then founded SS. Peter and Paul (from 1883). **Jewish**: a Jewish Burial Society and Adath Joseph cemetery soon after 1849; Adath Joseph services from 1856 in a hall on Felix St. (German, relatively liberal); Eastern European Orthodox congregations from the late 1880s (Shaare Sholem, 65 members in 1899, "no rabbi is employed … the different members serving"; B'nai Yaakov from 1894; merged 1960 as B'nai Sholem); German and Eastern European Jews were buried in different cemeteries. The **Lollie Siegal Block Memorial House** (610 S. 10th St., given 1916 to the Federated Jewish Charities) taught new immigrants until sold in 1965. SOURCED — Schutte 2021.
- [K9, P9] **Anti-immigrant violence, 1908–09 (reported plainly).** Local youths stoned immigrant workers near the Livestock Exchange Building and the Hammond plant (April and Aug 1908; arrests followed); a sheriff's candidate (elected) promised the South End Democrats to drive them out; packinghouses were accused of firing resident laborers to hire immigrants; a vagrancy defendant said he stood in line every morning but lost out; in Aug 1909 three drunk men went house to house telling residents to leave, and **the neighborhood women banded together and chased them** into the stockyards, where police arrested one in a cattle alley. Neighbors on Harvard St. protested renting to immigrants; "some of the real estate dealers refuse to rent property to them." SOURCED — Schutte 2021 (the press of the time used an ethnic slur, recorded only in the slang file).
- [P9, K9, K1] **Black St. Joseph and the church as civic center.** First African Baptist Church formed **6 Dec 1863** (350 members by 1872); Francis Street Baptist Church (1889 building); demolished 1926 for what became City Center Park; rebuilt as **St. Francis Baptist Temple** (17th & Angelique; $80,000; opened 8 April 1928; debt paid 1946). It hosted the NACW president's lecture (Jan 1902), a 1911 meeting on an industrial home for Black girls, the **local NAACP's founding (Jan 1915)**, an Emancipation Day celebration (1 Jan 1915, 400 attended, sponsored by the Negro Business Men's League), a 1917 protest against a proposed Jim Crow law and a packed protest meeting after the East St. Louis riots, the Colored League of Women Voters (1919) and the Colored Women's Republican Club (1920). **Bartlett High School** was the only high school open to Black students; St. Joseph was the first Missouri district to comply with *Brown* (summer 1954: two Black elementary schools closed, Bartlett converted to an elementary). In 1957 theaters still kept Jim Crow while a few drugstore lunch counters served all races. SOURCED — Kimberly Schutte, *St. Francis Baptist Temple and the Civil Rights Movement in St. Joseph, Missouri* (City of St. Joseph, July 2024; PDF, text extracted locally). Black population 3,206 (1880), 5,847 (1900), 4,288 (1920) (UNVERIFIED, Missouri State Archives snippet).
- [P9, K6] **Restoration churches (Independence).** Independence is headquarters of the Community of Christ (Temple and Auditorium) and of the Church of Christ (Temple Lot), which has owned the original temple site since 1867; the RLDS and Temple Lot churches litigated over the lot in the 1890s; other Restoration groups are based in the city. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia snippets). The Auditorium (capacity ~6,000) hosts the triennial **World Conference**; >1,400 delegates in 2023; the 2025 conference (1 June 2025 legislative session) affirmed the church's first woman prophet-president. UNVERIFIED (snippets of Wikipedia, cofchrist.org listing, wheatandtares.org; the cofchrist.org page fetched held no content).

### C. Leisure industries (and what locals do)

- [K3, K8] **Chiefs training camp, St. Joseph.** Held at Missouri Western since 2010; players live in the dorms; most practices are free (parking is the main cost); a pizza restaurant's manager calls camp the busiest stretch of the year. In Dec 2025 the Chiefs announced a move to Kansas in 2031 with an Olathe practice facility "capable of hosting training camp"; no decision on future camps. SOURCED — KCTV5, 28 July 2026. The Convention & Visitors Bureau's $6.3 million impact estimate was a miscalculation, revised to $2.6 million for 2014 (UNVERIFIED, *The Pitch* snippet).
- [K13, K8] **Englewood Arts District, Independence.** A trolley-served shopping strip from the late 1800s that lost "energy over the last 50 years" (Englewood Theater 1949 – closed after ~60 years; a five-and-dime closed). In **2011** the city zoned it an arts district requiring businesses (galleries, coffee shops, salons) to display or sell art; a monthly **third-Friday art walk**; nonprofit **Englewood Arts** bought a vacant ~30,000 sq ft city-owned medical building (10901 E. Winner Rd.) in 2020 for an arts center (glass lab, culinary café, puppetry stage, neon studio, makerspace), had raised $2.1 million of $3 million by Nov 2021, and arranged bank and credit-union financing for artist housing; a sculptor said her rent was about the same as in midtown Kansas City but with more space. SOURCED — Laura Spencer, KCUR, 5 Nov 2021.
- [K2, K3] **Winter calendar, Independence (2026).** Square Lighting (20 Nov), **Living Windows on the Square** (4 Dec; businesses turn storefronts into live holiday scenes), Vaile Mansion Victorian Christmas (28 Nov–30 Dec, volunteer-decorated), Bingham-Waggoner holiday tours and gala, the **65th Holly and Mistletoe Homes Tour** (Independence Young Matrons), a Mayor's Christmas Concert with school students in the Community of Christ Auditorium, a 23rd Winter Solstice Torchlit Hike, Theater on Ice's *Nutcracker*, seniors' Thanksgiving and Christmas lunches at the Palmer Center, Wreaths Across America at Woodlawn Cemetery, and a Holiday Market run by the Square Association, Chamber and Visit Independence "to support vendors affected by the SantaCaliGon storm." SOURCED — City of Independence, holiday guide, 4 Nov 2026.

### E. Cultural activities and civic life

- [K2, K3] **Apple Blossom Parade and pageant, St. Joseph** — since **1924** ("St. Joe proud since 1924"; "the third oldest apple blossom festival in the United States"), run by the **Apple Blossom Association**; royalty divisions Queen (15–19), Junior Queen (11–14), Princess (7–10); downtown route (Noyes & Frederick → Felix & 6th); early May. SOURCED — appleblossomparade.com. 2023 parade Saturday 6 May, 9:30 a.m.; queen judged on poise, personality, appearance and an essay on how small businesses make St. Joseph (SOURCED — Missouri Western news, 12 April 2023). Created by James E. Hunt (UNVERIFIED, single snippet); first queen 1926 (UNVERIFIED); 1925 festival co-sponsored by Buchanan County, MO, and Doniphan County, KS, with an orchard auto tour from Market Square (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- [K2, K9] **SantaCaliGon Days 2026**: storms on the night of **6 Sept 2026** (60 mph winds, penny-size hail) collapsed vendor tents; the vendor portion was closed on Labor Day for safety while the carnival stayed open; out-of-town vendors who had signed "rain or shine" contracts lost stock. SOURCED — Brandon Azim, KCUR, 7 Sept 2026. The festival is the Chamber's biggest annual fundraiser (UNVERIFIED, KSHB/Axios snippets).
- [K2] **Pony Express Re-Ride** — run each June by the **National Pony Express Association** (volunteer nonprofit, HQ Pollock Pines, CA); ~10 days, around the clock, between St. Joseph and Sacramento; >750 riders; ~1,000 letters a year in a leather mochila with a GPS unit pinging every five minutes; radio hams relay trail reports. SOURCED — NPS "The Pony Express Re-Ride" (updated 13 May 2024). Annual since 1980, canceled 2020 (UNVERIFIED snippets).
- [K1, K9] **Civil-rights organizing (Independence).** Virginia Jacobs led CORE from 1964; 1965 petition for land for low-cost homes for displaced families; 1966 petition (23 residents) alleging below-value offers; on **4 July 1966** she led ~100 protesters during Truman's speech at the Library. Urban renewal (Land Clearance Redevelopment Authority, Northwest Parkway project) displaced ~179 families; offers ~$4,000 for a home and ~$7,000 for five lots against a 1965 average home price of ~$21,500; the neighborhood was gone by 1969 (McCoy Park). SOURCED — KCUR 2023. The city dedicated granite markers in McCoy Park in June 2024 (UNVERIFIED snippets).

### K6 — rules of everyday behavior (adds to R1)
- **Missouri law on enslaved and free Black people** (SOURCED — Missouri State Archives, "Laws concerning slavery"): **patrols** appointed by county courts from 1825 to visit quarters and suspected assemblies, at least 12 hours a month at 25¢ an hour, allowed (by 1845) to whip anyone "strolling … without a pass" (up to 10 lashes), not to stop Sabbath worship; **passes** required to leave an owner's property (1804); anyone >20 miles from home treated as a runaway; **liquor** could not be sold or given to an enslaved person (1830s; $300 fine to a permitting master); **1847**: gatherings of Black people, religious or otherwise, banned unless a sheriff, constable or police officer was present ($500 fine or six months), and no school could teach Black people to read or write; free Black non-citizens barred from settling (1825), with fines and lashes added 1844–45; carrying an enslaved person on a boat without permission cost $150. Dram-shop licenses were granted by county courts (e.g., St. Louis County applications of 1854–55 include a "vow not to sell liquor to slave without master's permission" — SOURCED, Missouri Historical Society archival listing); Missouri's first liquor law (Dec 1824) banned sales to Native people (UNVERIFIED snippet; page refused).
- **Town government:** Independence's first town trustees (five, one-year term, petition of two-thirds of taxable inhabitants) could appoint police; city charter by act of **8 March 1849**. SOURCED — Webb 1927, ch. VIII. **St. Joseph 1908 boarding-house rules**: a $10 annual license for every boarding-house proprietor; a Board of Health decree, "Large rooms shall not contain more than two beds, and not more than two persons shall sleep in a bed"; mattresses judged unsanitary were burned (38 in one day); later, more than four to a room or two to a bed meant arrest. SOURCED — Schutte 2021.
- **Hotel and public-accommodation refusals**: refusals of Black emigrants (SOURCED, NPS); Jim Crow in St. Joseph theaters still in 1957 (SOURCED, Schutte 2024).

## 2 · #56 Great St Bernard Hospice

**Round 2 focus:** the valley communities on both sides (Bourg-Saint-Pierre, Liddes, Orsières on the Valais side; Saint-Rhémy-en-Bosses and Étroubles on the Aosta side), the historic porter-guides, the people who work the pass now (dog keepers, tunnel staff, rescue), winter vs summer life, house rules, the valley's own festivals and off-season gatherings, and Round 1's open threads (Bertrand's unread pages, morgue, climate, pilgrim counts, rescue today).

### A. Other settings

- [K4, K10, K6] **The hospice's guest quarters (today).** Dormitories of 8, 9, 14, 16 or 17 beds and rooms of 2–4 beds; **"The allocation of dormitories is not optional"**; guests may be put in any dormitory or room except families with young children; dormitories provide pillow, fitted sheet and blanket and require a hygiene bag sold at reception; shared toilets and showers on each floor; bring your own towel; vegetarian but no vegan meals, gluten-free guests bring their own bread, dietary needs 24 hours ahead; **no animals anywhere in the hospice** (dogs allowed at the summer Auberge with a reservation); Wi-Fi reliable only around the second floor; winter access "from 15 October to the end of May by ski touring or snowshoes only." SOURCED — martigny.com tourism listing "Hospice of the Great Saint Bernard" (undated, read 2026-11-21). Meal and prayer times are in a separate winter 2025 timetable PDF (not reached).
- [K5, K7] **Winter at the pass.** Snow season roughly mid-October to early June; road (Swiss RN27 / Italian SS27) open about mid-June to mid-October; average snow depth ~11 m; in winter the hospice is reached on foot with snowshoes or skis, or by helicopter, or travelers use the year-round tunnel; it is "a meeting point for skiers and ski-mountaineers, especially on weekends and holiday periods." SOURCED — FFRandonnée, "Via Francigena : le col du Grand-Saint-Bernard en hiver," 24 Jan 2025.
- [K5] **Weather record (derived data).** Station "Grand St. Bernard" (WMO 06717, 2,479 m in this listing): mean annual temperature 1.8 °C (2022), 0.1 °C (2021), 1.0 °C (2020); precipitation ~1,732 (2022) and ~1,397 (2020) (units not stated, presumably mm); 60–83 snow days a year in 2020–22. SOURCED as aggregator data — tutiempo.net (NOAA-derived), not an official MeteoSwiss normal. Official 1991–2020 normals: NOT FOUND (opendata.swiss refused, 403).
- [K4, K10] **The summer dog kennel at the pass.** Since the canons handed the breeding to the Barry Foundation in **2005**, a **contract clause obliges the dogs to return to the hospice every summer**. In 2026, eleven dogs arrived just before the second weekend of June (three cars to carry dogs, food and gear); they live in boxes inside a stone house that holds a small museum plus three outdoor enclosures, and are brought in at night ("it was 3 degrees yesterday and the gates were frosted"). The keeper (deputy head of breeding, eleven years' experience) takes the first night shifts, sleeps at the Auberge and cooks "for the dogs, not the visitors"; in July "a guard of honor of 40 people taking photos"; ~85,000 vehicles use the pass road in the season (canton figures); the tarmac around the lake is the hardest part ("slaloming between cars and motorbikes"); the rule is to keep moving, "if you stop, a crowd forms," to avoid bites. SOURCED — Hugo Cousino, *24 heures*, 15 June 2026 (press clipping PDF on fondation-barry.ch, text extracted locally). 12 dogs spent summer 2023 at the hospice with keepers taking turns (UNVERIFIED snippet, Barry Foundation report 2023); guided ~2-hour walks with the dogs July–August (UNVERIFIED, loisirs.ch snippet).
- [K9, K7, P4] **The abandoned ski area above Bourg-Saint-Pierre.** Super Saint-Bernard opened **1963** (~1,900 m; three lifts, top 2,770 m; runs descending into Italy; no hotel); the commune, owner-operator, decided in **2002** to close it; a local hotelier ran it for about eight more years ("the only solution is to sell it to you for one franc"); lifts stopped for good in **2010** when CHF 25 million was needed for a new cable car; the wind was "enemy number one"; ski tourers still climb it on skins (~14 m of snow a winter). SOURCED — swissinfo FR, 23 March 2025 (translation of Tom Robbins, *Financial Times*).
- [K5] **The road tunnel and its people.** 2024: **1,029,431 vehicles** — a record since opening on **19 March 1964**; daily record 8,470 vehicles (Ascension, 12 May 2024), beating a 1992 record; 992,041 light vehicles, 7,132 coaches, 30,258 trucks; partly driven by Mont Blanc tunnel closures. Operated by **SISEX SA** (Italo-Swiss operating company; three part-time office staff) using ~70 staff, vehicles and equipment of the two concession companies; 223 unscheduled closures in 2024, 194 of them for rescue-team interventions; staff logged 10,705 hours of training; 167,214 maintenance checks. SOURCED — SISEX *Rapport de gestion 2024* (PDF, text extracted locally). Shareholders of the Swiss company: cantons of Vaud and Valais and the city of Lausanne (UNVERIFIED snippet).

### B. Subcultures

- [K14, K1, P8, SM:competence shown under pressure] **The porter-guides of Saint-Rhémy and Étroubles (historic).** From the 10th or 11th century (sources differ), residents of Saint-Rhémy and Étroubles held from the Counts of Savoy the **exclusive right to accompany travelers, pilgrims and goods** between the pass and Aosta (the privilege called the Viérie du Mont-Joux); the porter-guides were called **marronniers**; made official in **1273**; in return the villages maintained and cleaned the road and lodged the Savoy family and guests free; the wealthier used horses, mules or donkeys, the rest carried loads on their shoulders; equipment described as a felt-hooded head for the cold, fur-lined gloves, spiked boots and long poles for probing deep snow; services could last several days. SOURCED — viaggimedievali.com, 10 July 2022 (citing the Saint-Rhémy commune site); valledaosta-guidaturistica.it (undated). In **1627** Duke Charles Emmanuel I exempted the village's young men from military service in exchange for maintaining and marking the road and rescuing travelers — the **soldats de la neige**; this lasted until WWI (guidaturistica) or 1927 (viaggimedievali) — discrepancy noted. Marronniers "paid by the hospice to assist travelers" (UNVERIFIED snippet). Scholarly sources named but not read: Ezio-Emeric Gerbore (1989) on the Viérie and marronnage of Étroubles and Saint-Rhémy; Alberto Marcoz (2006) (see WANTED).
- [P9, K14] **The canons and their congregation.** In 1986 the congregation had **~80 members** in four priories (Valais; Champittet, Lausanne; Montfleury, Aosta; Hsincheng, Taiwan); activities: hospitality (Great St Bernard, Simplon), parish ministry (nine parishes in Valais), teaching (Champittet college; Montfleury agricultural school), mission (Taiwan). Canon **Gratien Volluz** (of Orsières; solemn vows 1955) launched **alpine pilgrimages** to the hospices as a renewed form of hospitality; the author asks whether hospitality "as practiced at the beginning of this century" is still credible "in the age of road tunnels." SOURCED — Georges Delavy, "La Congrégation du Grand-Saint-Bernard," *Échos de Saint-Maurice* 82 (1986), pp. 21–23; *Échos* 53 (1955) chronicle (PDFs, text extracted locally). The same 1986 text dates Pius XI's proclamation of St Bernard as patron of mountaineers to **21 Aug 1923** (R1 had a letter of 10 Aug 1923 — discrepancy noted). In 2001 the canons "help with avalanche rescue, which requires them to be skilled skiers"; and the hospice's **charnel house** "and its inhabitants remain, but its windows and doors were walled up decades ago." SOURCED — swissinfo, 14 Nov 2001.
- [P9, K9] **Who signed the register, 1812–1815 (completes Bertrand).** 521 signers between 19 July 1812 and 19 July 1815; ~398 (76%) French, Swiss or Italian; 123 others (23.6%): 28 Germans, 5 Dutch, 4 Belgians, 49 British (mostly Aug–Sept 1814 and May–June 1815), Austrian officers (4 in Jan 1814, 28 in May–June 1815), 3 Russians, 2 Americans; merchants 8–15 a year, moving mainly between Lyon, Dauphiné, Lake Geneva, Piedmont, Aosta, Milan and Genoa, some from southern Germany, Amsterdam, Odessa; conscript detachments (May 1813) and 700 men with the Austrians (June 1815) appear, but 40 Austrian soldiers (Jan 1814) and Prince Esterházy's 5,000 men (June 1815) do not. During the Revolution the canons kept no full list: "every day in winter as in summer people of every age and condition arrive," and they had "neither the time to take their names nor to note their number." The archive also holds a **journal by a peasant of Bourg-Saint-Pierre** counting Napoleon's troops in 1800. Bertrand reads the register as mostly a record of well-off travelers, officers and merchants; migrants going to work do not appear in it. SOURCED — Gilles Bertrand, *Le Grand Tour revisité*, ch. 2 (OpenEdition, books.openedition.org/efr/1998), characters 100,000–292,784 read in this round (R1 read the first 100,000).

**Round 2 focus:** how working people lived behind the inns and in the courts, the trades and crafts of the stone town, the licensing and public-order rules, the town's present-day third places, volunteer-run culture and festivals, and other Great North Road stage towns (Stilton, Boroughbridge) after the coaches; plus Round 1's open threads (ostler pay, Harper, closures after 1852).

### A. Other settings

- [K4, K9, P4] **Courts and yards (working-class housing).** Stamford had many courts and yards; at the end of WWI there were at least **400 substandard tenements** averaging over four occupants (≥1,600 people); with 9,882 people and 2,570 inhabited dwellings in 1921, "15% of the dwellings were of a slum type and housed over 16% of the population." Court life: Sunday mornings "a sharp smell of burning feathers"; kitchen walls hung with drying nets from "a successful co-operative poaching venture"; Sunday dinners cooked at an adjoining bakery for twopence; "Saturday afternoons were useless for doctors visiting" (men at football or on allotments, women bathing in zinc tubs by the kitchen fire). Population: ~3,000 (early 18th c.), 4,022 (1801), 7,760 (1841, incl. 1,375 in St Martin's after the 1832 Boundary Act), 7,332 (1851), 6,676 (1871), 9,646 (1911), 10,080 (1931). SOURCED — E. C. Till, *A Family Affair: Stamford and the Cecils 1650–1900* (1990), extract on Bournemouth University's "Talking About Technology" oral-history site.
- [K4, K9] **Where cottages could go.** Expansion beyond the medieval walls was "hardly possible, for the open fields remained unenclosed until 1875"; after the 1796 enclosure of St Martin's, Burghley Park "was immediately extended to reach the Great North Road," and Cecil land to the west "was not made available for building"; only after ~1810 were cottages built in numbers on the edge of town, mostly to the north, where "closely-built houses rapidly earned a dubious reputation"; a cottage group called New Town grew beyond Scotgate in the 1820s; most were later demolished, "removing a social problem." The Great North Road was diverted west of the town in **1961**. SOURCED — RCHME *Stamford*, Sectional Preface: Topographical History (British History Online node 108966).
- [K5, K1, K7] **Winter on the road: the snowstorm of December 1836.** The *Stamford Mercury* (30 Dec 1836): the heaviest storm in 23 years; the Edinburgh and Glasgow mails stuck between Wansford and Thornhaugh in drifts six to eight feet deep; guards reached Stamford on horseback; the Edinburgh coach was finally hauled in by **eight wagon horses**; mail went on horseback with bags "almost empty"; no coaches left Stamford for Cambridge, Leicester or Boston for three days; a passenger spent two days and nights on the roof of the Glasgow mail; a stranded coach party was taken in at a private house at Morborne; ~**50 extra laborers** cleared Casterton hill for four days, paid £25–£30 a day by the **turnpike surveyor**. SOURCED — Stamford Mercury Archive Trust blog, "Let it snow" (3 Jan 2023).
- [K4, K10] **Almshouses still in use.** The Hospital of William Browne, Merchant (charity 221428; founded by the wool merchant William Browne, d. 1489): **13 sheltered flats** for "poor people of good character," mostly elderly; two paid staff (clerk to the trustees; custodian/caretaker); a council 24-hour care line; a volunteer museum in the medieval buildings; Holy Communion in the original chapel monthly (first Wednesday, 10:00); income £148,352 (2024). SOURCED — opencharities.uk record (filed 28 Oct 2025). Residents accepted from age 60 (UNVERIFIED, 2018 directory snippet).

### B. Subcultures

- [K14, P8, R] **Collyweston stone slaters (the roofs of the Stamford area).** A local limestone that splits along its bedding; slates supplied to Rockingham Castle in 1375 and 1390 (14,000); pits and mines around Collyweston by 1633; 19th-century miners lay on their sides to undercut the seam, leaving waste columns as supports, and listened for a clicking that warned of collapse; quarried stone is kept damp and laid out so that **frost splits it** (in mild winters it went back underground, covered in wet sand, for the next winter) — "widely used" since the 16th century; nearly all work by hand, usually one person splitting, dressing and fixing; slates stacked in bundles of 840; laid in diminishing courses at ~47° (up to 65°) on oak rafters with riven oak or chestnut battens, oak pegs and a lime-and-hair mortar underside; slates last centuries and are reused; by the 1970s the craft "was in real danger of dying out"; the **Collyweston Stone Slaters' Trust** was set up in **1982** "to work towards sustaining the industry"; ~1,500 buildings in North Northamptonshire have such roofs. SOURCED — Collyweston Historical Society, "Collyweston Slate." Historic England (2015 case study, snippet): risk that splitting and laying skills "would be lost"; new slates becoming cost-competitive with reclaimed (UNVERIFIED). (The trade's own terms are in the slang file only.)
- [P9, K9] **Brewers, tied houses and magistrates.** All Saints' Steam Brewery (built 1825); Herbert Wells Melbourn took the lease in Oct 1869 aged 21; Melbourn Brothers formed April 1872; output ~3,500 barrels a year before re-equipping in 1876, 9,000 by 1900; tied houses 15 (1872; seven in Stamford), 31 (1884), 32 (1971); depots at Lincoln, Grantham, Leicester and Holbeach closed by the late 1920s as lorries took over; bought by the Earls of Liverpool and Gainsborough in 1971; brewing ceased **1974**. A pub on Water Street home-brewed until 1920/21. SOURCED — Chris Hunt, "Melbourn's Tied Houses 1869–1971," Stamford Local History Society (2024).
- [P9] **The George as the town's hall.** Lawrence Hoskins (d. 15 March 2023, aged 82) owned the George of Stamford for over 50 years with his wife; 45 bedrooms; two restaurants; a former manager (hired 1987) called it the "heart of the community," run on a principle of accommodating requests — "everyone in Stamford" held their special events there; "everything revolved around the kitchen." SOURCED — Caroline Baldwin, *The Caterer*, 16 March 2023.
- [P9] **Schools.** Stamford School founded 1532 by the will of Alderman William Radcliffe; re-endowed from Browne's Charity in 1873; Board of Governors 1872, scheme approved 1882; Stamford High School 1877; joint principal from 1993; governors voted unanimously (May 2022) to go co-educational at all stages, complete by Sept 2024; the old High School site is now a sixth-form campus. ### E. Cultural activities and civic life — other Great North Road stage towns

- [K3, K2, K9] **Stilton (Cambridgeshire):** at one point **14 public houses for ~500 people**; the Bell (recorded 1515, rebuilt 1642) and the Angel (early 17th c.; ceased as an inn; badly burned 1923); a 1.25-mile A1 bypass opened **21 July 1958**, "reducing passing trade through the village to almost nil," and a second bypass (A1(M)) in 1998; **cheese-rolling** begun **Easter Monday 1962** by the landlords of the Talbot and the Bell "to help revive the village after its decline," later held each May Day holiday; **canceled in 2018** for declining interest and "uncontrollable behavior," revived **2024**; 2,455 residents (2011, with Folksworth); one shop, three pubs, a primary school. SOURCED — en.wikipedia "Stilton, Cambridgeshire."
- [K9] **Boroughbridge (North Yorkshire):** 22 inns at its height serving drovers, road and river travelers; the Crown stabled 100 horses; ~2,000 cattle a day crossed the bridge in the droving season from Scotland; Barnaby Fair drew Romani travelers and horse dealers; the Ure navigation went insolvent and was sold to a railway company in 1847; "Stagecoach transport had disappeared by 1853 and the town had lost seven of its 22 inns"; former inns became flats, homes for the elderly, shops; bypassed **1963**, losing the Great North Road traffic "for the first time in 800 years." SOURCED — Boroughbridge town website, "The History of Boroughbridge." Ferrybridge's Angel handled 50 pairs of horses a day at its peak (UNVERIFIED snippet).

## 4 · #66 Post towns of the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō (e.g., Tsumago, Hakone)

**Round 2 focus:** how residents of Tsumago live and run the town today (inns, succession, the residents' organizations and their rules in practice), the walkers who now fill the old road, Hakone's workforce and residents, the shrine and civic festivals that locals run, crafts, and Round 1's open threads (charter rules, meshimori-onna terms, checkpoint staff, Hakone labor).

### A. Other settings

- [K4, K7, K9, P4] **Tsumago's inns: from out-migration to minshuku and back to scarcity.** Before preservation "most people living in Tsumago went away to work elsewhere" (seasonal migrant labor); with the 1970s tourism boom "many private houses began operating as lodgings — at most about 60," with only two or three souvenir shops; early on residents agreed to limit themselves so all could make a modest living: **lodgers up to 20 per house, meals up to 40, souvenir shops no wider than one bay (ken) of frontage**; visitors later approached ~1 million a year and ~400,000 recently with inbound tourism, and "the rules first decided are changing." By 2021 only **10** lodgings remained of ~40 businesses (10 lodgings, a dozen eateries, the rest souvenir shops), many closing with the owners' aging; one ryokan was a manor moved ~65 years earlier from 30–40 minutes away, now run with the owner's daughter and Russian-born son-in-law; "many other lodgings have no successor." Nagiso has no secondary industry and "runs on the taxes Tsumago earns from tourism"; forestry was the original main industry and still has a cooperative. SOURCED — Tsumago Tourism Association interview record (Tokyo Denki University, visit 26 March 2021, compiled April 2021; PDF, text extracted locally). Peak of 53 inns and minshuku vs 11 in 2016 (UNVERIFIED, Meijo University paper snippet).
- [K9, K4] **Empty houses and the "don't sell, don't rent, don't destroy" rule.** The same innkeeper argued for 30 years for renting to outsiders, against residents who fear "strange people" would damage the townscape; some won't rent because family return at Obon and New Year; two vacant houses given back to the town became a rest stop; "this rule has made it hard to create successors." SOURCED — same interview record.
- [K4, P4] **Edo-period building limits still visible:** buildings with usable second floors date from after Meiji; in the Edo period second-floor rooms were not allowed and the height of the main pillar was fixed. SOURCED — same.
- [K5, K10] **The walkers' road.** Hikers counted crossing Magome Pass (Nakasendō) April–December 2019: **~53,000**, the first time over 50,000, counted by the Tsumago wo Aisuru-kai. SOURCED — *Yamatogokoro*, 23 Jan 2020. ## 5 · #68 Jumak (Korean roadside taverns)

### E. Cultural activities and civic life

- [K2, K6] **The village drinking rite (hyangeumjurye).** Held yearly in the **10th lunar month** on an auspicious day at local Confucian schools and academies (and government offices in the capital and provinces), with local scholars, the host office and even commoners attending; the **guest of honor was "the most senior, virtuous, and well-conducted person,"** including filial sons; a banquet, an exchange of cups, and a pledge read aloud; purpose: honoring elders, caring for the aged, promoting filial piety, brotherhood, harmony and neighborliness; standardized through the 1474 *Gukjo oryeui*; in practice it came to resemble the village-pact (hyangyak) oath ceremony and the names were used interchangeably.
