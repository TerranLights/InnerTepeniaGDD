### 1b. How often do winter flights slip or fail? (the WinFly record, extended)

**The 2015 trial outcome (was NOT FOUND in Intake A; now found).**
- "Residents wintering at McMurdo Station saw a rare sight in June; A United States Air Force C-17 touching down at the Pegasus Airfield during Antarctica's darkest month." "The plane stayed for only a couple of hours before flying off into the night. It dropped off seven people and about 52,000 pounds of cargo and picked up eight people leaving the continent. A month later, another C-17 descended out of the blackness, rounding out a three-flight, proof-of-concept program." **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun story by Michael Lucibella, reprinted in the Old Antarctic Explorers Association *Explorer's Gazette*, vol. 15 no. 3 (Jul-Sep 2015), https://oaea.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Volume15Issue3.pdf (PDF read through a local text extraction).
- The Australian A319 "touched down on 18 April while the skies were still dimly lit in the Antarctic twilight. It brought in 13 people to the station and carried 39 off the Ice." It tested the A319 "to land late in the season" and "the effectiveness of the new LED landing lights at Pegasus Runway." **SOURCED**, same page.
- **One planned flight did not happen:** "A second plane, a Royal New Zealand Air Force C-130 cargo plane, was supposed to fly down as well, but delays and scheduling commitments prevented it from taking part." The June C-17's mail load "was particularly large because it carried the packages from the earlier cancelled April C-130 flight as well." **SOURCED**, same page. **DERIVED:** of four winter flights planned for April to July 2015 (A319, RNZAF C-130, two C-17), three were flown and one was canceled, for reasons the source gives as delay and scheduling, not weather alone.
- Planners "authorized another two winter flights for next year," "In the future, there may be as many as six flights per winter season." There had been "no planned winter flights for almost 50 years" since a scheduled Navy flight in June 1967. The June flight delivered "about 4,500 pounds of produce and 3,400 pounds of packages." **SOURCED**, same page.
- NSF's manager of transportation and logistics: "No longer do we have the mentality that the winter staff and the winter operation is a closed operation. We have the ability to fly in and out year round." **SOURCED**, same page. The NVG approach had been "built up over the course of a few years for emergency use," and the same C-17s are "channel flights" aircraft from a different Air Force unit than the summer McChord unit. **SOURCED**, same page.
- The 2 Sept 2015 Air & Space Forces Magazine piece says earlier landings "required the aircraft to turn on their landing lights to illuminate runway reflectors," which "forced straight-in approaches, which were impossible in crosswind conditions," and that a McChord C-17 made the first NVG landing of the 2015 WinFly on 23 Aug. **SOURCED**, https://www.airandspaceforces.com/year-round-antarctic-flights . (This confirms a stated crosswind limit on the older method; no figure given.)

## 2. Runway upkeep through a winter

### 2a. Pegasus (glacial ice runway, McMurdo; wheeled runway 1993-2016): the best primary account

Primary source: CRREL Monograph 98-1, *Construction, Maintenance, and Operation of a Glacial Runway, McMurdo Station, Antarctica* (1998), read in full text from the BTS copy (https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/14078/dot_14078_DS1.pdf; downloaded, text extracted). **SOURCED** for everything in this subsection unless marked.

- **Size:** "a surveyed 10,000- x 300-ft (3050- x 91-m) area." Snow cover was stripped, the ice laser-graded with "a laser-guided grader with a specially built chisel-tool blade," and the graded ice cleared with a snowblower.
- **Snow is the main upkeep problem:** "Snow management will likely be the most important maintenance issue associated with the majority of glacial ice runways. We see this to include both protection of the runway from melt problems and protection against snow accumulation and inundation." A naturally exposed blue-ice site "may require essentially no maintenance short of cleaning up any contaminants."
- **What winter leaves behind:** "A survey of the runway snow cover in late August 1993 indicated that the winter had provided an average of about 30 cm (12 in.) of snow. However, this snow was in no way uniformly distributed. The centerline was exposed along nearly the entire length of the runway. Along the edges, the runway had tapered drifts ... Many small drift pods (<10 m2) were randomly scattered ... the south half of the runway had notably more snow accumulation than the northern portion." Heavy wheeled aircraft could use the runway "from mid-January to near the end of October" in most years (about 15 November for the lighter aircraft the monograph names), ski aircraft all year; the winter snow is kept on it until melt risk passes, because a protective snow layer is needed between about 15 November and 10 January. **DERIVED:** 30 cm over 3050 x 91 m is about 83,000 m3 of snow cover if spread evenly (3050 x 91 x 0.30); the survey says it was uneven, so this is an upper bound for the average only.
- **Removal effort recorded:**
  - Overwinter snow removal in preparing the first season began in August 1992 ("Beginning in August of 1992, overwinter snow was removed") and grading and clearing "were completed at the end of October."
  - Putting the protective cover in place and spreading it took "about three weeks" in 1992-93 (bulldozers and a grader with skids, snowblower along the flanks); that was "about 10 days late."
  - Redistributing and smoothing the cover with a ripper, grader and bulldozers with skids, then snow planes and drags "took about two weeks to accomplish using a crew of four persons."
  - Protective cover thickness: "10-in. (25-cm) layer of snow" in place "by the end of November"; compaction with "heavy, rubber-tired rollers" (35-50 tons, tire pressure over 690 kPa) in thin lifts; at peak sun "the Pegasus runway snow surface needed treatment at least every other day, sometimes daily."
  - Drift-trapping: small windrows about 10 cm high "spaced a meter or so apart, perpendicular to the direction of the prevailing wind" trap on their lee side "an amount of snow at least equal to the volume of the windrow"; "on a day with steady winds carrying considerable snow building up over a half meter of snow was possible in a 24-hour period."
- **Crew sizes stated:** "construction of the runway required six people full time, and about the same size crew is required to maintain the snow cover during the November-January period"; "the 4 to 6 people working at the site" in the warm season; the monograph's authors state they "recommend two full-time maintenance personnel with intimate knowledge of the site," access to "one or two other operators who can be available when storms and drifting snow occur," and "a half-time mechanic/fabricator." Over the five-year development "approximately 17,000 hours of work were expended at the runway for a crew consisting of between two and five persons"; cost about $350,000 per year in the last two years. During the austral winter, when the runway was not in use, "all structures and navigational aids would be removed."
- **Drifting snow and the effect of storms:** the runway axis (15/33) was "selected to coincide with the direction of the strong or storm winds"; the prevailing wind, generally low speed, is a crosswind; "the ramifications of snow drifting and accumulation were not considered"; a gentle easterly "can transport a great quantity of snow through the Pegasus site over the course of a day"; "large amounts of snow are easily collected if north-south aligned berms, windrows, or obstacles are present, particularly, along the east side of the runway." Berms left from construction "must be significantly reduced in size." A 1998 paper by Lang and Blaisdell (ISSW) describes the concern that snow "adjacent to the berm area" might eventually inundate the runway, and a passive snow-removal experiment with a vortex fence; it reports "approximately 320 m3 of snow" scoured "during a five-month period" by one fence. **SOURCED**, https://arc.lib.montana.edu/snow-science/objects/issw-1998-190-196.pdf (text layer read).
- **What the monograph says about winter flying at Pegasus (written in 1998, before the 2015 trial):** "Winter flights to the Pegasus site would require lights and possibly additional navigation aids." "If winter flights are planned, we would recommend that they initially occur on a widely spaced regular schedule (e.g., one flight per month) to allow runway personnel to establish the procedures and techniques required for winter maintenance. Methods of using the wind to erode snow deposits on the runway will probably be needed in keeping the runway clear. Runway lighting should be provided with portable, self-contained (battery pack) type units ... only be emplaced for a few days prior to the flight and then retrieved after the aircraft is safely enroute." "The low temperatures at this time of year would make the snow road from McMurdo and Williams Field very sound with minimal grooming." The runway manager role: "In such a situation, a winter manager should also be assigned to the runway. This position could require minimal effort if the runway were unused during the winter, but, if winter flights are scheduled, the position will require all of the responsibilities and concerns of the summer manager."
- **Fuel for the operation:** not quantified. The monograph states a runway with "50-60 flights per month" "will require a dedicated fuels manager during the operational season" and describes a sled-mounted 19,000-L (5,000-gal) tanker on site during construction. **Fuel burned by the groomers, graders and blowers: NOT FOUND** in the monograph (searched the text for fuel, gallons, liters).
- **Secondary (southpolestation.com Pegasus history page):** "Between 57,000 and 250,000 cubic yards" of snow had to be cleared in some years to make Pegasus usable (**DERIVED** conversion: 43,600 to 191,200 m3, at 0.7646 m3 per yd3); Pegasus was a blue-ice runway "10,000 feet long and 300 feet wide," "about 18 miles from McMurdo by snow road."
- **2016 WinFly detail:** after weather cleared, the runway "still needed about a day of grooming" before the A319 could land (item 1b). **SOURCED** (blog).

## 4. Remote-site staffing, medical capability, arrival screening

### 4a. What an ice runway needs, by role (the monograph's list)

From the CRREL monograph (Pegasus; **SOURCED**, same file as item 2a), the roles it names for a glacial runway are: a **site manager** ("would function much as an airport manager at a moderate-sized rural runway"); in a program that operates year-round with different summer and winter workforces, a **winter manager**, a position that "could require minimal effort if the runway were unused during the winter, but, if winter flights are scheduled, the position will require all of the responsibilities and concerns of the summer manager"; a **facilities manager** (site and facilities roles "could be filled by one individual" if traffic is low); a **fuels manager** at a runway with 50 to 60 flights a month, with "specialized training for each aircraft type"; a **crash/fire chief and crew** ("It is critical to have on site a crash/fire crew and equipment any time air operations are possible. The size and specific skills ... will be dictated by the type of aircraft used, flight frequencies, and the specific requirements of the air support contractor"); a **meteorologist or weather observer**; **air traffic controllers** (with infrequent flights "it may be possible to dispense with an air traffic controller and have the facility manager act as a ground controller"); and **equipment operators and a mechanic**. It recommends staff attend airport training courses. No headcount for the whole is given; the maintenance-only figures are in item 2a.

## 5. Weather statistics: Mirny and Davis

### 5a. Method and caveats (read before using any number)

- **Source:** NOAA ISD global-hourly CSVs (public S3 bucket), station files `89592099999` (Mirny) and `89571099999` (Davis), downloaded for Mirny 2010-2025 (2025 partial, 911 reports, excluded from day counts) and Davis 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018-2024. Fields used: wind direction and speed (`WND`, speed in tenths of m/s), visibility (`VIS`, meters), ceiling (`CIG`, meters), present-weather code (`MW1`). **DERIVED** throughout.
- **Reporting times:** Mirny reports at 00, 06, 12, 18 UTC (22,480 reports over 16 years, about 4 a day); Davis at 3-hourly times (31,728 reports). So "percent of observations" is percent of instantaneous synoptic reports, not hours or days, and a day count below means "days on which at least one report met the test." Gusts are not in these reports (wind is the reported mean). Davis visibility is present in only 62 percent of reports (19,663 of 31,728), so its visibility percentages rest on those. At Mirny the ceiling is missing in 46 percent of reports (9,946 of 21,569), so ceiling is left out of the main Mirny tables.
- **These are station records** (40 m and 23 m elevation on the coast), not measurements at any airfield or at the Bunger Hills or Drygalski Island.
- **Blizzard definitions differ from mine.** The Australian Antarctic Division defines a blizzard as "a period of > one hour when the visibility is reduced below 100 m by blowing snow," with temperature below 0 C and wind above 33 knots. The "blizzard-like proxies" below are my combinations of the reports and are **not** that definition.

## FINAL: what the sources support, do not support, and NOT FOUND

**The sources support**
1. **Winter-season landings on Antarctic ice outside McMurdo are rare and are documented mainly as medevacs:** Byrd, April 1961 (ski Hercules, skiway readied by the station and Seabees); South Pole 2001, 2003, 2016 (Intake A); Davis, December 2020 (a ski landing area built by the station in 3 to 5 days, summer). A winter C-130 medevac to Marambio in 2017 and Rothera Dash 7 winter flights are seen only in unverified snippets. No winter landing at Troll, Novolazarevskaya, Progress, Mirny, Zhongshan, Halley or an Indian station was found.
2. **Winter flights slip.** Every WinFly or winter trial with a recorded outcome shows at least one delay (about a day to more than a week), and one of four flights in the 2015 trial was canceled outright. The weather-driven part is well documented (an 80-mph storm that delayed the 2014 flights by four days, two-day delays in 2009 and 2016, a runway needing a day of grooming after a storm); no source gives a rate.
3. **Ice runways need snow management more than anything else.** Pegasus: a four-person crew spent about two weeks redistributing snow; a six-person crew for construction and for the November-January cover; winter left an average of about 30 cm of snow, unevenly (exposed centerline, drifts at edges); a heavy year required 57,000 to 250,000 cubic yards cleared. The Wilkins start-up used four (2020) or eight (2025) people about 90 days, with a week-long blizzard and 90-knot winds in the second week. The monograph's authors say winter flights would need lights, wind erosion, a monthly schedule at first and a winter manager with the full summer duties.
4. **Cold limits are known best for fuel and engine starting, not airframes.** Jet A-1 freezes at or below -47 C; Jet A at -40 C; AN-8 is stated to be -58 C or lower; the Airbus warns at -45 C fuel temperature; Twin Otter engines list -40 C as the minimum start oil temperature; the Boeing 757 received Russian certification to -54 C; the AWI BT-67 aircraft are stated to operate to -54 C. C-17 operations at about -40 C use engines kept running, heaters at the gear and engines, and cycled flight controls.
5. **Airfield roles and small-site crews:** the Pegasus monograph lists the roles (site, winter and facilities managers, fuels manager, crash/fire crew, weather observer, controllers or a facility manager as ground controller, operators and a mechanic). Troll's winter team of six (unverified) cross-trains cooks and electricians into fire and tower duty. Australian stations carry one doctor, four lay surgical assistants and a Hobart telemedicine link, and state that evacuation is "impossible for at least part of the year."
6. **Arrival screening at the smallest ports is declaration-plus-medical-officer:** Pitcairn requires a health declaration emailed to its medical officer before arrival, a possible brief medical examination with temperature, immigration by the Immigration Officer for every passenger going ashore, and biosecurity inspection of fruit and seeds, citing the community's vulnerability to visitors' diseases.
7. **Mirny's wind is strongly directional and its winter is worse than its summer:** 87 to 94 percent of winds, and of strong winds, come from E to SSE; April to September has 8.5 percent of reports at 15 m/s or more (3.8 percent in October to March); visibility under 1 km in 13.5 percent of April-September reports and 70 days a year have at least one such report. Davis's strong winds come almost entirely from the northeast quarter; visibility under 1 km is 4.7 percent of April-September reports.

**The sources do not support**
- Any long-run WinFly or medevac delay or cancellation rate.
- Any stated airliner-manufacturer minimum ground-operating temperature for the A319/A320, 737, 767, C-17, Il-76 or Basler; the figures above are certification or operator statements, fuel specifications, or engine limits.
- A blizzard-day count for Mirny from a primary source (only my proxy counts from synoptic reports).
- Fuel burned per season for runway grooming at any field.

**NOT FOUND (each in the log, with where it died)**
- WinFly outcomes for 2011, 2013, 2017-2024; outcome of winter trial flights after 2015 beyond "two more authorized."
- The year and details of the first mid-winter C-17 medevac (PACAF and 446th pages refused).
