# Relung Panen soil research, slice E: the oasis's ground, water, light and climate

**Date:** 2026-10-25
**Scope:** This file covers only real-world physical facts about the Bunger Hills oasis (Knox Coast, Wilkes Land, East Antarctica, about 66°15′S, 100°45′E). It covers the ground, soils, water, light, climate, biology, any plant-growing trials at East Antarctic oases, and the ice-free-area figures. It contains no fiction, no recommendations and nothing invented.

## 1. Findings summary

### 1.1 Overview

- **Ground.** The bedrock is Precambrian granulite-facies gneiss. It includes a Neoarchean basement (protolith ages about 2800–2700 Ma), Palaeo–Mesoproterozoic orthogneiss and pelitic gneiss, Mesoproterozoic plutons (gabbro to granite) and many dolerite dyke swarms (Tucker et al. 2020; Sheraton et al. 1993, via Kulbe 1997). The rock is overlain by moraine and till.
  - The till matrix (<2 mm) is mostly medium sand, with a geometric mean of 0.33 mm (Gore & Leishman 2020).
  - The soils are gravelly Cryosols: Turbic, Skeletic-turbic and Histic Turbic. Only 18.7–32.7% of the soil is fine earth (<2 mm).
  - pH is alkaline (7.4–8.9) on bare and salty ground and acidic (5.1–6.1) under moss. Organic carbon is low (0.7–3.4% in Alekseev & Grek 2026; 0.11–3.33% in Nizamutdinov et al. 2021).
  - Soils with developed profiles cover **less than 0.1% of the oasis**. This is stated independently by two Russian teams (Dolgikh et al. 2023; Alekseev & Grek 2026).
  - A **"salt line"** divides the oasis. North of it, sea-spray halite and thenardite dominate and soils are strongly weathered with little vegetation. South of it, the salts are calcium carbonates and gypsum (Gore & Leishman 2020).
- **Permafrost.** Permafrost underlies the oasis. Measured depths to the permafrost table are 82–106 cm (2017/18). Locally, the ground thaws to 120–160 cm.
- **Water.** There are hundreds of lakes, about 36 km² in total (Simonov 1971). The largest freshwater lake, Figurnoye (Algae Lake), covers 14.3 km² and has a mean depth of 32 m. Its maximum depth is quoted as 137 m and as 145 m.
  - The Algae River system is about 25 km long and is fed by meltwater from the ice sheet, Apfel Glacier and snowbanks. Outflow from Algae Lake was estimated at more than 1 × 10⁷ m³ a year (1986/87).
  - Isolated lakes range from fresh to hypersaline (3.4–79‰).
  - The epishelf "lakes" are tidal marine inlets with a freshwater cap up to 88 m thick.
- **Climate.** All monthly figures here come from the only full-station record: Soviet Oasis station, 1956–58, about two years of data.
  - Mean annual air temperature is −9.0 °C. Monthly means are above 0 °C only in December (+1.7 °C) and January (+1.8 °C).
  - Precipitation is 204 mm a year from an uncorrected gauge, which the source says overstates the true amount. Potential evaporation is 450–600 mm a year.
  - Relative humidity averages 56%. Mean wind speed is 6.8 m/s; on 122 days a year the wind reaches 15 m/s or more, and the peak recorded speed is 56 m/s.
  - Global radiation is 3735 MJ/m² a year. December alone receives about 779 MJ/m², and the year's net radiation is positive.
  - The shortest day is about 2.5 h (June); there is no true polar night.
- **Biology.** There are mosses (at least 4 named species) and lichens: 64 species are recorded in total, which the 2020 survey calls the second-richest lichen flora in continental Antarctica (Andreev 2020). There are also algal and microbial mats.
  - Three flying birds breed: snow petrel (the most numerous), Wilson's storm petrel and south polar skua.
  - Weddell seals are present in the marine inlets. There are **no penguin colonies**, so nutrient input from birds is very small.
- **Plant-growing trials.** **No plant-growing trial of any kind is recorded at Bunger Hills.** Trials at other East Antarctic oases were all indoor or greenhouse work. The relevant ones are:
  - Maitri (Schirmacher), 1986: a germination test in local lake-shore soil.
  - Novolazarevskaya (Schirmacher), 1972/73: a greenhouse produced about 50 kg.
  - Davis (Vestfold Hills), 1969: an indoor garden and a glasshouse.
  - Syowa: hydroponic plant boxes.

## 2. Findings by question

### Q1. The ground: geology, surface cover and soils

#### 1a. Bedrock

**Tucker, Hand & Clark 2020**, "The Bunger Hills: 60 years of geological and geophysical research," *Antarctic Science* 32. Open access; full text read. SOURCED.
- The region is "primarily granulite facies rocks with igneous and sedimentary protoliths, voluminous weakly deformed gabbro–granite and mafic dykes."
- The rocks fall into six associations:
  1. a Neoarchean basement of tonalite–dolerite orthogneiss, dominant in the south-east (protolith and depositional ages about 2800–2700 Ma);
  2. and 3. Palaeo–Mesoproterozoic interlayered orthogneiss and pelitic gneiss;
  4. migmatitic garnet–cordierite pelitic gneiss in the central hills;
  5. Mesoproterozoic plutons, including the Paz Cove Batholith granite;
  6. mafic (dolerite) dyke suites intruded between about ≥1140 and 500 Ma.
- "Group 3 and 4 dykes are the most abundant ... dolerite and range up to 50 m in width."
- The moraine also carries foreign erratics: conglomerate, arkose sandstone and rapakivi granite.

**Kulbe 1997** (Berichte zur Polarforschung 254, AWI; summarizing Sheraton et al. 1992 and 1993). SOURCED.
- Three intrusive bodies (gabbro to quartz-monzogabbro to quartz-monzodiorite to granite) cover about 300 km² (Charnockite Peninsula), about 80 km² (Rybiy Khvost) and about 20 km² (Lake Figurnoye).
- Five mafic dyke swarms are about 1.14 billion years old. Minor alkali and trachyte basalts are about 0.50 billion years old.

**Relief.** SOURCED.
- Maximum elevation is about 165 m (AARI paleopolar page; Klokov et al. 1990) or 168 m (Nizamutdinov et al. 2021, citing Simonov 1971).
- Prevailing heights are 50–80 m (Nizamutdinov et al. 2021; Andreev 2020).
- The southern hills are a line of summits 130–150 m high (Alekseev & Abakumov 2020).
- The Russian term for the landscape is "структурно-денудационный мелкосопочник" (structural-denudational low hills) (AARI paleopolar page).

**Deglaciation.** SOURCED.
- The central hills were deglaciated about 30 ka BP. The southern, eastern and western ice margins were deglaciated during the Holocene, within the last 10,000 years (Gore & Leishman 2020, citing Gore et al. 2001 and Berg et al. 2020).
- Some hilltops were ice-free at the Last Glacial Maximum (Berg et al. 2020, abstract).

#### 1b. Surface cover and sediments

**Gore & Leishman 2020**, "Salt, sediments and weathering environments in Bunger Hills," *Antarctic Science* 32. Open access; full text read. SOURCED.
- 74 basal or ablation till samples were taken from pits **0.3–1.0 m deep**.
- The mean grain size of the <2 mm matrix ranges from 0.18 mm (fine sand) to 0.56 mm (coarse sand), with a geometric mean of **0.33 mm**. Sorting is "poorly sorted" (geometric mean 0.25 mm). The gravel (>2 mm) fraction was not analyzed.
- The central hills carry finer, better-sorted till. The ice margins carry coarser till that is negatively skewed.
- **Salt.** 122 salt samples contained 17 minerals.
  - Halite (NaCl) or thenardite (Na₂SO₄) appeared in 40 of 122 samples (33%), "dominantly in the north". These salts are marine-derived from sea spray blown west off the inlets.
  - Calcite appeared in 59 samples (48%) and aragonite in 29 (24%), mostly in the south and partly of subglacial origin.
  - Gypsum appeared in 13 samples (11%) and other weathering products in 20 (16%), including green atacamite on a copper-rich gneiss, traceable "for hundreds of metres".
- A **"salt line"** separates a salty, strongly weathered north (with tafoni, wind pits and sand accumulations, and few frost cracks) from a less weathered south and east (with glacial polish and frost cracks).
- In surface-sediment extracts, the geometric mean order of extractable ions is Na > Cl > Ca > K > Mg. Electrical conductivity and all ions are highest in the north, near Paz Cove and Zakrytaya Bay.
- The actual conductivity and ion values are in supplementary Tables SI–SV and the AAD dataset (doi 10.26179/5e1fa087f185c). **I could not retrieve them:** the file download requires a login (see Section 3).
- Land in the central and northern areas below about 10 m above sea level was likely saturated with seawater during the mid-Holocene highstand.

**Alekseev & Grek 2026** (*Geography, Environment, Sustainability* 19(1), doi 10.24057/2071-9388-2026-4107; full text read via reader proxy). SOURCED.
- "Moraines with high gravel content are very widespread, which leads to greater moisture deficit in the upper soil horizons."
- Fresh snow is "actively blown out" of the flat inter-ridge valleys.
- The parent material is mostly loamy.

**Dolgikh et al. 2023** (abstract in the conference volume *Мерзлотные почвы в антропоцене*, SPbU, 2023, p. 22). SOURCED.
- The loose deposits are "preimushchestvenno suglinistye" (mainly loamy) and dark in color, so hypolithic organic horizons are very rare.

#### 1c. Soils

**Classification.**
- Skeletic-turbic Cryosol (WRB) at two sites, with 75% and 72% coarse fraction (Alekseev & Abakumov 2020, *Czech Polar Reports* 10(1):7–22). SOURCED.
- Turbic Cryosols and Histic Turbic Cryosols (Alekseev & Grek 2026). SOURCED.
- Typic Haploturbel and Typic Haplorthel (US Soil Taxonomy) (Kudinova et al. 2021, *Microorganisms* 9:1728). SOURCED.
- Leptosols are "more often identified" in the inter-ridge valleys (Alekseev & Grek 2026). SOURCED.
- The most developed soils (in the Russian system, "литозёмы перегнойные" and "торфяно-литозёмы") occur in wind shelters under thick moss. They "occupy less than 0.1% of the territory of the oasis" (Dolgikh et al. 2023). SOURCED.

**Soil depth and organic horizons.**
- Organic horizons are up to 10 cm thick in wind-shelter soils. Their radiocarbon age is >1000 years, and the mid-profile horizons are >2500 years old.
- Organic horizons on surfaces newly exposed by glacier retreat are up to 3 cm thick and have a modern radiocarbon age (Dolgikh et al. 2023). SOURCED.
- Sampled profiles reached 15–23 cm (Kudinova 2021; Alekseev & Grek 2026), and Gore & Leishman dug till pits 0.3–1.0 m deep. **I found no published figure for typical total soil depth over bedrock** (see Section 3).

**Nizamutdinov, Andreev & Abakumov 2021** (*Eurasian Journal of Soil Science* 10(4):308–319). Full PDF read. SOURCED.
- 40 samples (21 ornithogenic, 19 non-ornithogenic) were taken at 5 sites south of Lake Figurnoye, at 19–125 m above sea level, in the 65th Russian Antarctic Expedition season (December 2019–March 2020). Sampling depth near nests was 7–10 cm.
- **pH (H₂O):** non-ornithogenic soils average 8.09 (range 6.55–9.71). Ornithogenic soils range from 4.4 to 8.33; site B61 is acidic (4.4–6.63).
- **Available P** (0.2 M HCl; GOST 54650-2011): 635–15,870 mg/kg.
- **Available K:** 115–1290 mg/kg.
- **NH₄-N:** 3.26–754.1 mg/kg. **NO₃-N:** 0.79–60.9 mg/kg.
- **Total C:** 0.11–3.33%. **Total N:** 0.01–1.49%.
- **Texture:** loamy sand, sandy loam and sand.
- The authors note: "Permafrost limits the migration of organic matter into the deeper horizons, which results in the horizontal transfer of organic matter."
- **Data-quality flags** (I am noting these, not resolving them):
  1. Many C/N ratios are 1.1–1.9. That is implausibly low for soil organic matter, and the N figures (up to 1.49%) look high against C of 1–3%.
  2. The Conclusion opens with a sentence copied from an unrelated agronomy paper ("A Lithovit treatment as foliage nutrition at a 6 g/L rate..."), which points to weak editorial control.
  3. The P extraction is an acid extraction, so it cannot be compared directly with Olsen or Mehlich values.

**Kudinova et al. 2021, Table 1** (soil sampled in 2018). SOURCED.
- Typic Haploturbel under fragmentary sodium bicarbonate crusts, central flat valley, 10–15 cm: C 0.13%, N 0.03%, moisture 4.17%.
- Typic Haplorthel in a southern wind shelter, AT horizon at 0–2 cm: C 6.62%, N 0.32%, moisture 10.02%. AB horizon at 10–15 cm: C 3.0%, N 0.40%, moisture 6.49%.
- The mineral grains are quartz, feldspars, pyroxenes and amphiboles from weathered gneiss. Clay content is low ("a weakly weathered environment of East Antarctica where clay content is low").
- **Data-quality flag:** Table 2 of the same paper gives native N for the AT horizon as 0.66%, against 0.32% in Table 1.

**Ornithogenic input.** SOURCED.
- "Very limited ornithogenic transfer, which is due to the absence of penguin rookeries and very rare nesting of flying birds" (Alekseev & Grek 2026).
- Dolgikh et al. 2023 likewise describe "крайне незначительный орнитогенный привнос" (extremely small bird-derived input), with bird material accumulating only locally in small shelters.
- Nizamutdinov et al. 2021 argue that a bird "fingerprint" is present across all their study soils. That is a different reading of different sites; both are reported here.

**Salinity of soils.**
- Alekseev & Abakumov 2020 attribute low topsoil resistivity to "high salt content with the most abundant halite and thenardite". No electrical-conductivity values are given. SOURCED.
- **Quantitative soil salinity values (EC in dS/m, or salt content in %) are NOT FOUND** in any source I could read. See Section 3.

**Permafrost and active layer.**
- **Permafrost table 82–106 cm** (Bunger 1 site), measured by vertical electrical resistivity sounding during the 63rd Russian Antarctic Expedition (December 2017–April 2018). The authors say "very little data on active layer and permafrost table depth are published for Bunger Hills" (Alekseev & Abakumov 2020). SOURCED.
- **Maximum seasonal thaw >120 cm** (Dolgikh et al. 2023). SOURCED.
- **Local thaw to 140–160 cm** (Andreev 2020, *Российские полярные исследования* No. 40, pp. 36–39). SOURCED.
- **Positive temperatures reach 2 m depth in summer** (Rusin 1961, via Gregorczuk 1980). SOURCED.
- **Daily ground-surface amplitude** is 25–30 °C in summer and ≤3 °C at 20 cm depth (Gregorczuk 1980). SOURCED.
- **Active layer 0.2–0.3 m near the ice margin, rising to 1.6 m away from it** (daold.ru). UNVERIFIED: a popular site, original source not named.
- **Through-taliks under lakes deeper than 20 m** (daold.ru). UNVERIFIED.
- **Russian permafrost boreholes exist.** The AARI note *Российские полярные исследования* No. 16 (2014) mentions boreholes for permafrost monitoring at the oasis. SOURCED as a fact; **no data from them found.**
- **Archaeal DNA in permafrost deposits:** Karaevskaya et al. 2014 (*Microbiology*). Abstract not retrievable; NOT READ.

**Comparison with the Dry Valleys** (only what the sources state).
- The McMurdo Dry Valleys have a mean annual temperature of −17.3 °C, against −11.2 °C at White Smoke Lake (Doran et al. 1996, via Kulbe 1997). SOURCED.
- Mean wind is 3.3 m/s in the Dry Valleys against 4.6 m/s at White Smoke Lake (Kulbe 1997). SOURCED.
- Summer thawing degree-days are similar: 71 at Bunger, "similar to" the Dry Valleys. Winter freezing degree-days are much lower at Bunger: 3987 (Doran et al. 1996). SOURCED.
- Soil CO₂ emissions at Bunger and Schirmacher "exceed those documented for the Dry Valleys" (Alekseev & Grek 2026). SOURCED.
- **Not obtained:** Campbell & Claridge 1987 and Bockheim 2015 (*The Soils of Antarctica*) were not available online in full and were not read. Beyer et al. 2000 (*Geoderma* 95) concerns the Casey/Windmill Islands area, not Bunger, and only a generic abstract was read.
- **No Antarctic Specially Protected Area management plan exists for Bunger Hills.** Searches found none. The oasis's Historic Sites are the Dobrowolski station pillar and the magnetic observatory (Wikipedia). There is **no ASPA soil data**.

### Q2. Water

**Inventory.** SOURCED.
- Lakes total 36 km², which is 9% of the land surface (Simonov 1971, via Filcek & Zieliński 1990). Klokov et al. 1990 give the same 36 km² as "6–7% of the oasis bedrock". COMPUTED check: 36 / 395 = 9.1%, so the 6–7% figure must use a larger denominator.
- "Hundreds of large and small lakes" (Klokov et al. 1990). "Hundreds of lakes" (Gore & Leishman 2020).
- "Almost all small and a greater part of larger lakes are without any outflow" (Filcek & Zieliński 1990).

**Lake Figurnoye (Algae Lake).**
- **Area** is variously given as 14.3 km² (Pryakhina et al. 2023), 14 km² (AARI page), 16 km² (Simonov 1971, via Filcek & Zieliński 1990) and 14.7 km² (Nawrot 2023). All SOURCED.
- **Mean depth** is 32 m (Pryakhina et al. 2023).
- **Maximum depth** is 137 m (BSE; Wikipedia) or 145 m (AARI; Laybourn-Parry & Wadham 2014, via plwiki).
- **Length** is 17 km (AARI) or 25 km (BSE).
- **Surface elevation** is 11.6 m above sea level (AARI, citing Verkulich et al. 2002).
- In summer the lake is "mostly ice-free". Water temperature is 1.0–3.0 °C, reaching 5 °C only by rocky shores.
- The lake is fed by meltwater from the ice sheet and snowfields and by streams from other lakes (AARI). SOURCED.
- **Volume ≈ 0.46 km³.** COMPUTED: 14.3 km² × 32 m.
- **Water quality:** mineralization is 17–18 mg/L (prevailing ions Na, Ca, Cl, SO₄), and "the effect of sea salts ... is not detectable". pH is about 6.9, rising to 7.2–7.4 after ice-out (Klokov et al. 1990). SOURCED.
- **Level rise** of 0.12 m was recorded in 1987 (Pryakhina et al. 2023, Fig. 2). SOURCED.

**Algae River.** Gibson, Gore & Kaup 2002, *Polar Record* 38:141–152 (abstract only). SOURCED.
- It is "25-km-long ... the third longest known in Antarctica".
- It receives meltwater from the Antarctic ice sheet, Apfel Glacier and snowbanks, passing through epiglacial lakes to Algae Lake.
- Algae Lake's outlet runs to Transkriptsii Gulf.
- "Total flow from Algae Lake was estimated to be greater than 1 × 10⁷ m³ a⁻¹ from data collected in the 1986/87 summer."
- Parts of the system flowed in 1946/47, 1985/86, 1986/87, 1994/95, 1995/96 and 1998/99 but **not** in 1999/2000, "emphasising that parts of the drainage network may become disconnected readily."
- **Discrepancy:** the AARI page says Figurnoye drains to Izvilistaya Bay, to the west. I have not reconciled this.

**Epishelf lakes** (tidal, fresh water over seawater).
- **Transkriptsii Gulf:** the upper layer has salinity 0.3–1.0‰ and is **88 m thick** at the center. Salinity is 11‰ at 93 m and 19‰ at 104 m (the bottom), and the bottom water contains H₂S (Klokov et al. 1990). SOURCED.
- Transkriptsii is perennially ice-covered, about 4 m thick, and its tides are synchronized with the ocean (Filcek & Zieliński 1990). SOURCED.
- **White Smoke Lake:** perennial ice **1.8–2.8 m** thick; "fresh and cold (<0.1 °C) throughout" (Doran et al. 2000, abstract; Gibson & Andersen 2002, abstract). SOURCED.
- **Lake Pol'anskogo** has two basins, one fresh and cold and one saline and warm. "Southern" Lake is cold and saline at depth. "Northern" Lake is relatively warm throughout (Gibson & Andersen 2002, abstract). SOURCED.
- **Rybiy Khvost Bay (Pass Cove)** has no fresh layer. Salinity runs from 9‰ under the ice to 18.6‰ at the bottom. It thaws in summer and supports marine fish and macroalgae (Klokov et al. 1990; Filcek & Zieliński 1990). SOURCED.

**Saline lakes.** SOURCED.
- Kaup, Haendel & Vaikmäe 1993 (*Antarctic Science* 5:41–50; abstract only) studied 12 saline lakes 5–35 m above sea level. Salinity was 3.4–79.0‰. The bottom of Lake Polest reached 17.7 °C under a thermohalocline. Phosphate was 3–10 µg P/L, total P 8–16 µg P/L, and nitrate and nitrite 1–4 µg N/L.
- **Unit note:** the OCR of Klokov et al. 1990 renders lake phosphate as "3–8 mg P l⁻¹". Kaup et al. 1993 give the same magnitudes in **µg**. The Klokov "mg" is very probably µg (OCR or typesetting). UNVERIFIED as written.
- Salt Lake reached 78‰ (Kaup, personal communication, via Filcek & Zieliński 1990).

**Freezing season** (1989 observations; Filcek & Zieliński 1990). SOURCED.
- Small ponds froze in late February. Large lakes began freezing in late February or early March and finished within about 10 days.
- "All the water reservoirs ... were frozen within a month."
- Bystraya Stream stayed open until early April.

**Precipitation.** SOURCED.
- **204.1 mm a year** (Tretyakov gauge, no corrections; Gregorczuk: "total precipitation values in Table III are over-estimated"), from Oasis station 1956–58.
- Monthly totals (mm): Jan 2.5, Feb 3.0, Mar 13.8, Apr 4.7, May 31.3, Jun 10.2, Jul 17.2, Aug 28.8, Sep 68.8, Oct 13.4, Nov 0.5, Dec 9.9. COMPUTED check: the months sum to 204.1.
- Precipitation (≥0.1 mm) falls on 93 days a year; "hard" precipitation (snow, granular snow, hail) on 156 days; blizzards on 53 days.
- **Text–table mismatch:** the text says 13% of the annual total falls in November–February. The table gives (0.5 + 9.9 + 2.5 + 3.0) / 204.1 = **7.8%** (COMPUTED). The two disagree.
- "The permanent snow cover does not form even in winter." Snow collects in sheltered landforms as snowfields covering "several tens of metres to 200–300 m²" (Smirnov, via Rusin 1961, via Gregorczuk 1980). SOURCED.
- Annual precipitation of about 200 mm against 400–600 mm of potential evaporation (AARI paleopolar page). Same figures in ROSHYDROMET 204 mm (Alekseev & Abakumov 2020; Alekseev & Grek 2026, "in liquid equivalent"). SOURCED.
- In 1989 the maximum snow depth was 40 cm (19 March 1989), and "a few windy days ... were enough to blow out nearly the whole snow" (Filcek & Zieliński 1990). SOURCED.
- Rain fell twice in January 2020, described as "небывалое явление" (an unprecedented event) (Andreev 2020). SOURCED.
- **Precipitation chemistry:** mean annual pH of precipitation at Mirny is 5.8 (Shcherbakov et al. 1984, via Klokov et al. 1990). Fresh snow at the oasis held 4–16 (µg?) per liter of total P (unit as above). SOURCED, with the unit caveat.

**Glacier melt contribution.** **NOT FOUND as numbers.** The bounding glaciers are named in the sources: Edisto (west), Apfel (south), the ice sheet (east), the Shackleton Ice Shelf (north), and Remenchus or Scott in some lists. The only quantified meltwater flux is the Algae River figure above. No measured seasonal water balance for any lake or catchment was found. Denman Glacier appears only in a geology context, as nunataks (Tucker et al. 2020).

**Wind and katabatics.** SOURCED.
- Two-thirds of winds are at most 5 m/s, yet one-third of days have wind above 15 m/s. The windiest periods are April and August–September, with monthly means of 8–10 m/s.
- Maximum winds come from the east, all in the ENE–ESE octant.
- The oasis sits in the slope-wind zone, but the main katabatic flow "miss[es] the elevated oasis or pass[es] above". Mean winter wind is 2–3 times lower than at nearby ice-sheet stations.
- Calms make up 39% of observations on average (45% in June; over 50% in some years), against 1% at Mirny.
- Local foehns raise the temperature by 4–6 °C and lower humidity by 20–30%.
- The lowest winter temperatures come with southern winds or calm, to −32 °C and below (all Gregorczuk 1980).
- At White Smoke Lake: mean 4.6 m/s, peak 32 m/s (Doran 1996, via Kulbe 1997).
- Present-day strong winds (>10 m/s) blow from **118 ± 21 °T**. Oriented tafoni measure 101 ± 18 °T (686 measurements at 28 sites), "indistinguishable" from modern winds (Gore & Leishman 2020, tafoni paper abstract).
- In the 1989 season: 35 of 65 days had wind ≤10 m/s, 12 days >20 m/s, and the maximum was **43 m/s** on 24 February 1989 (Filcek & Zieliński 1990).

**Wind chill** (Gregorczuk 1980, citing Gregorczuk 1978–79). SOURCED.
- June–September monthly mean wind chill is about 5600 kJ/m² per hour: "a great probability for freezing unprotected parts of body".
- In summer (December–February) it is 3600–4000 kJ/m² per hour, which the comfort scale rates "very cold".
- Clothing needed in winter: 6–7 clo at rest, 4–4.5 clo at moderate work, about 3 clo at hard work. In summer: 3.5–4, 2–2.5 and 1.6 clo respectively.
- April is the least favorable month.

**Humidity.** SOURCED.

- 33% was recorded at 16:00 on 29 January (Gregorczuk 1980).
- Lowest recorded 12% (Rusin, via Klokov et al. 1990).
- Fog is rare: 4 foggy days in 2 years, all in July 1958 (Gregorczuk 1980).

### Q4. Biology

**Lichens.** Andreev 2020, *Российские полярные исследования* No. 40. SOURCED.
- The 1989 survey (34th Soviet Antarctic Expedition) found 48 species. The list stood at 51 after later publications.
- The 2020 survey (400 specimens from 68 points in the south-east) found 38 species plus 5 unidentified ones. The updated list is **64 species in 30 genera and 16 families**, "the second richest in continental Antarctica after Schirmacher".
- 82% of finds were on stone, 34% of species on mosses and 21% on fine earth. "Slightly more than half" are Antarctic endemics.
- Large areas showed no plant life at all.
- **Other count:** 42 species (Andreev 1990, via Singh et al. 2015, *Polar Science*). UNVERIFIED (search snippet only; ScienceDirect blocked). It does not match Andreev's own figure of 48 for 1989.
- Where lichens occur in favorable places, they may cover **75% of the rock surface**. They are absent from large stretches, such as the south-west shore of Rybiy Khvost Bay (Filcek & Zieliński 1990). SOURCED.

**Mosses.**
- Recorded species are *Bryum algens*, *B. argenteum*, *Ceratodon purpureus* and *Schistidium antarcticum* (Alekseev & Grek 2026, citing Andreev 1990). SOURCED.
- Mosses were collected at 58 points in 2020 (Andreev 2020).
- Mosses and lichens are "most abundant around the ice margins where fresh snow and ice meltwater are abundant". They are reduced in the salty, wind-abraded center (Leishman, Gibson & Gore 2020, abstract). SOURCED.
- A complete moss species count is NOT FOUND (Leishman et al. 2020 full text was not accessible).

**Algae and microbes.** SOURCED.
- Figurnoye sediments contain microbial-mat laminae of blue-green and green algae and diatoms, plus moss layers (AARI Figurnoye page).
- Soil bacteria are dominated by Chloroflexi, Proteobacteria and Acidobacteria. Cyanobacteria are minor (Kudinova et al. 2021).
- The rotifer *Rhinoglena kutikovae* is a "probable relict species that survived Quaternary glaciations" (De Smet & Gibson 2007; title only).
- The copepod *Gladioferens antarcticus* (Bayly 1994; title only) and *Acanthocyclops mirny* (Klokov et al. 1990) are present.
- Terrestrial nematodes are recorded (Yeates 1979; title only).

**Birds.** SOURCED.
- **Snow petrel**, the most abundant: about 2000 pairs (V. Bulavintsev, personal communication, via Filcek & Zieliński 1990), or more than 1000 individuals (Bulavintsev et al. 1993, via Nizamutdinov et al. 2021). The two figures differ; both are reported.
- **South polar skua:** about 50 individuals (Gibson 2000, unpublished AAD report, via Nizamutdinov et al. 2021).
- **Wilson's storm petrel** also breeds.
- Snow petrels nest in bedrock crevices around the margins (Leishman et al. 2020, abstract).
- **No penguin rookeries** (Alekseev & Grek 2026; Dolgikh et al. 2023).

**Seals and marine life.** SOURCED.
- Weddell seals: two seen regularly in 1989, and "many mummified corpses" along Rybiy Khvost Bay (Filcek & Zieliński 1990).
- Rybiy Khvost Bay has sea urchins, sea stars, sponges, bivalves, red-algal meadows and fish (*Trematomus*, *Pagothenia*) (Klokov et al. 1990; Filcek & Zieliński 1990).

**Nutrient input from wildlife.** See Q1, Ornithogenic input. The sources describe it as very limited overall, but locally high around nests, with high available P.

### Q5. Plant-growing trials at or near East Antarctic oases

**Bunger Hills: NOT FOUND.** I searched in English, Russian and Polish and found no record of any agricultural, horticultural or greenhouse trial at Oazis, Dobrowolski, Oasis-2 or Edgeworth David. The Bamsey et al. 2015 review lists more than 46 Antarctic plant facilities and none at Bunger Hills.

**Other East Antarctic oases and coastal stations.** All from Bamsey et al. 2015, "Review of Antarctic Greenhouses and Plant Production Facilities," ICES-2015-060 (DLR elib; full PDF read). SOURCED.

- **Schirmacher Oasis, Maitri (India), 1986.** An 11-day germination test in **local soil collected from Lake Priyadarshini**. Radish, spinach, fenugreek, tomato and cucumber were grown in bags by a window. When moved to Dakshin Gangotri, "approximately half of the plants perished due to the freezing temperatures"; the survivors "continued to grow well".
  - The 1986 Indian experiments as a whole (local soils, Indian soil, snowmelt and lake water) "provided encouraging results", but "confirmed that neither sunlight nor the electric lighting systems were adequate for productive plant growth under the configurations used".
  - From 1990, Maitri had a 10.6 m × 2.66 m greenhouse attached to the station, using soil with a nutrient reservoir, NFT and rockwool. It was operational in 2001, and its later status is unknown.
- **Schirmacher Oasis, Novolazarevskaya (USSR).** A greenhouse "reported to have produced 50 kg of tomatoes, cucumbers, onions, chives and several types of flowers during the 1972-1973 season."
- **Schirmacher Oasis, Georg Forster (East Germany).** A year-round greenhouse from the late 1970s or 1980s used **soil brought from outside Antarctica**. All soil was removed at decommissioning in 1993.
- **Vestfold Hills, Davis (Australia), 1969.** An indoor garden in a spare donga (sand, gravel and vermiculite substrate under fluorescent light) and an external double-glazed glasshouse. 1984 saw a hydroponic container, and 2001/02–2014 a two-container hydroponic unit. Australia suspended all station plant growing in 1989 over non-native-species concerns and lifted the ban in 1991.
- **Syowa (Japan), East Ongul Island.**
  - 1966–68: plant boxes using a perlite and shale medium under fluorescent light gave "very satisfactory results".
  - From 2008: a 3 m² hydroponic chamber at about 1500 ppm CO₂ yielded 18.8 kg of lettuce between April 2008 and January 2009.
- **Mirny (USSR), 1958/59.** "A pilot grew onions under natural and artificial light." Greenhouses at Mirny, Molodezhnaya and Leningradskaya closed in the late 1980s.
- **Larsemann Hills (Zhongshan, Progress, Bharati):** no facility is listed. "There are presently no plant growth facilities operating at other Chinese stations" (other than Great Wall) (as of 2015).
- **Regulatory context:** the Madrid Protocol (in force 1998) means non-native soils are "no longer allowed in Antarctica" (Bamsey et al. 2015).
- **Earliest use of local Antarctic soil:** Discovery Expedition, McMurdo Sound, 1902. Cress and mustard were grown "in boxes containing Antarctic soil collected from the nearby hills" and harvested on 1 November 1902. This is not East Antarctica; it is included because it is the earliest documented use of local soil.
- **No outdoor, field-scale trial in natural East Antarctic oasis soil was found anywhere.**
