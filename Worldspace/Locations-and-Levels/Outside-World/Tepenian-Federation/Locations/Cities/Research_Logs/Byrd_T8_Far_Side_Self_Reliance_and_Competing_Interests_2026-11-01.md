**The 1933 vote.**
- **Date and form:** 8 April 1933, held the same day as the state election. **SOURCED** (WAEC page: "Polling Day: 8
  April 1933"). Question 1 (WAEC, verbatim): *"Are you in favor of the State of Western Australia withdrawing from
  the Federal Commonwealth established under the Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act (Imperial)?"* Question 2
  asked for a convention of equal numbers of representatives from each state to propose constitutional changes.
  **SOURCED.**
- **Result, Question 1 (withdraw):** Yes **138,653 (66.23%)**, No **70,706 (33.77%)**; valid 209,359; informal
  7,967; total 217,326; enrolled 237,198; turnout 91.62%. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, which cites Musgrave 2003, p. 107,
  for the counts). **Not confirmed against the WAEC itself:** the WAEC page carries the questions and date but no
  tally (died at the source). A 1933 New Zealand press report gave an early count of 69,930 No (Papers Past, search
  1 summary; an early count, UNVERIFIED as such).
- **DERIVED:** 138,653 ÷ 209,359 = 66.23% of valid votes (matches); 138,653 ÷ 237,198 = **58.5% of all enrolled
  voters** voted Yes.
- **Result, Question 2 (convention):** Yes **88,275 (42.58%)**, No **119,031 (57.42%)**. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- **Where the No vote was:** only **six of fifty** electoral districts voted No on Question 1, **five of them in the
  Goldfields and Kimberley** (Wikipedia, citing *The West Australian*, 3 May 1933). The State Library page: *"The
  goldfields were the only area to reject secession,"* backing a national convention instead. **SOURCED.**
- **Same-day government:** the pro-secession premier James Mitchell lost to Philip Collier's anti-secession
  Australian Labor Party; Mitchell lost his own seat. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia; State Library).
- **Not legally binding:** the Constitution has no provision for a state to leave; the only legal path would be a
  national referendum carried by a majority of voters in a majority of states. **SOURCED** (Parliamentary Education
  Office).
- **Why voters may have voted Yes:** the Parliamentary Education Office: the Depression sharpened complaints that the
  national system was unfair and that West Australians were *"ignored and neglected."* The State Library page
  calls the Dominion League *"a protest movement people were happy to support only because they suspected
  secession would never succeed,"* and says the League *"found the victory much harder to cope with than the
  campaign."* **SOURCED** (both pages, quotes as returned).
- **Grievances named (Wikipedia, "Secessionism in WA"; SOURCED to that page, not to primary documents):** protective
  tariffs from 1902 raised the cost of imported machinery for mining and farming; Canberra was seen as favoring the
  larger eastern population centers; WA called itself a *"Cinderella state"* that put more into federal funds than
  it got back; the 1934 case called federation *"hampering to its industries."*

**The Dominion League and the petition to Westminster.**
- **Dominion League of Western Australia:** formed **1930** (Wikipedia gives May 1930 in one article and a public
  launch at His Majesty's Theater, Perth, on **30 July 1930**, founded by **Keith Watson**, in the other). An earlier
  **Secession League** dates from **1926** (James MacCallum Smith, owner of *The Sunday Times*, which had run
  pro-secession articles since 1907). The **Federal League** ran the No campaign. The Dominion League disbanded in
  **1938**. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, two articles).
- **The delegation:** the Collier government, though anti-secession, passed the **Secession Act 1934** and sent a
  delegation led by **Hal Colebatch**; the petition was presented on **17 December 1934** (Wikipedia; Hansard 20 June
  1935 confirms the 17 December 1934 date). The delegation carried a printed **489-page "Case for Secession"**; the
  petition was written out in three copies by hand on **sheepskin rolls about 26 feet long, each in a jarrah
  casket**; a proposed "Dominion of Western Australia" flag was unfurled on the roof of the Savoy Hotel; the
  rallying cry was *"Westralia shall be free!"* **SOURCED** (The Conversation, 2024).
- **Why it failed:** a joint committee of both Houses was appointed *"to report whether the petition of the State of
  Western Australia is proper to be received."* It concluded *"that the petition was not proper to be received"*:
  such legislation would be within Parliament's *"strict legal competence"* but *"would not be within the
  constitutional competence"* of that Parliament without a request from the Commonwealth (the 1931 Statute of
  Westminster convention); the committee *"did not enter into the merits of the case."* The Attorney-General would not
  find time for a debate. **SOURCED** (UK Hansard, Commons, 20 June 1935). The Conversation calls the ruling that the
  petition *"could not even be received"* *"fatal to the secessionist cause"*; the British government was
  *"reluctant to involve itself in a stoush between Perth and Canberra."* **SOURCED.**
- **Date of the committee's report:** press reports of late **May 1935** (Papers Past, 25–31 May 1935; *Spectator*,
  31 May 1935, search 2 summary); the Parliamentary Education Office says *"In May 1935"*. Wikipedia's results summary
  read as November 1935: **conflict** (see Unresolved conflicts). Hansard of 20 June 1935 shows the report was then
  *"lying on the table,"* which fits May.
- **How it faded:** the campaign lost momentum after federal grants helped the state economy (Parliamentary
  Education Office). **SOURCED.**

**Later expressions (neutral).**
- **1974:** Lang Hancock founded the **Westralian Secession Movement** (taxes and tariffs); it stagnated within a few
  years. **2016:** the WAxit Party formed and won few votes. **2017:** a successful motion at the Liberal State
  Conference revived debate. An **October 2020** poll found **28%** of West Australians supported leaving the
  federation (pollster not named on the page). **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Secessionism in WA"); pollster **NOT FOUND**.
- **The GST share (the main modern dispute).** Australia's goods and services tax is pooled nationally and shared out
  by "relativities" that equalize states' fiscal capacity, so a state with high mining royalties receives less per
  head. WA's share fell to **about 30% of its population share (2016–17)**; the 2017–18 relativity table shows
  **0.34434**. The federal government announced a new model in **July 2018**; from 2019–20 every state has a
  **floor of 70% of its population share in 2022–23, rising to 75% from 2024–25**; a "no worse off" top-up for other
  states originally ran to 2026–27 and was extended in December 2023 to **2029–30**. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "GST
  distribution dispute"). Economists Saul Eslake and Chris Richardson (February 2024) suggested the cost could exceed
  **$50 billion by 2030** (same page; **SOURCED** to the page).
- **The WA government's own framing (SOURCED, WA government statement, 5 November 2025,
  https://www.wa.gov.au/government/media-statements/Cook%20Labor%20Government/Campaign-launched-to-ensure-Australia-keeps-the-GST-share-fair-20251105):**
  a national advertising campaign, *"WA's GST is working for Australia,"* plus a "People's Submission" for residents
  to sign; if the reforms were wound back WA would be *"around $6 billion per year worse off."* WA's fact page on the
  2026 review (https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/government-of-western-australia/gst-wa-the-facts-behind-the-productivity-commissions-interim-report):
  WA *"still provides around $2 billion a year to other states through the GST system,"* which without the reforms
  would be *"more than $8 billion"*; WA retains *"around 10% of mining revenue"* for iron ore and lithium. **SOURCED.**
- **The 2026 review:** the Productivity Commission's interim report was *"published on 14 August 2026"* (WA
  government). It said the 2018 changes *"have largely not achieved their goals and have cost more than four times as
  much as projected"* (deputy chair Alex Robson); Australian taxpayers pay *"more than $6 billion a year"* to top up
  WA; it recommends returning to the pre-2018 system with targeted changes for mining states. Every government except
  WA has called for the deal to be scrapped or strongly amended. **SOURCED** (AAP via Kyabram Free Press, 14 August
  2026, https://www.kyfreepress.com.au/national/wa-stares-down-east-coast-clowns-over-costly-gst-deal/). Final report
  due by the end of 2026 (same). The Prime Minister later pledged no change to WA's arrangements while he remained
  prime minister (Wikipedia, "GST distribution dispute"; **UNVERIFIED** against a primary source).
- **The 2020–22 hard border:** state borders closed **24 March 2020**, tightened to a full closure on **5 April
  2020**; a reopening for **5 February 2022** was announced on 13 December 2021 and delayed indefinitely on 20 January
  2022; on 18 February 2022 a **3 March 2022** reopening was announced for triple-vaccinated interstate travelers.
  **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "COVID-19 pandemic in Western Australia"). That the border actually opened on 3 March is
  **UNVERIFIED** on that page.

### 1.3 Sakha (Yakutia) and its diamonds in the 1990s (neighbor: a far region bargaining over its one great resource)

**Pages read:** A. Kasimov, "Yakutia: Autonomy and Diamonds," *Prism* (Jamestown Foundation), 12 January 1996,
https://jamestown.org/?p=40548 · "Alrosa Company Ltd. History," FundingUniverse,
https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/alrosa-company-ltd-history/ · Russian Wikipedia, "АЛРОСА,"
https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/АЛРОСА.

- **Steps toward control (SOURCED, Kasimov 1996):** declaration of sovereignty adopted **27 September 1990**; in early
  **August 1991** the Presidium declared the republic's enterprises its property and halted diamond and gold supplies
  to the USSR Finance Ministry and Goskhran (the state depository). President **Mikhail Nikolaev** was among the
  first regional leaders to back Yeltsin against the center.
- **Before:** a 1989 calculation (Kornilova) held that Yakutia got back only **about 4%** of the value of diamonds
  sent to Moscow (Kasimov doubts the figure). After: the republic *"officially"* had **about 20%** (the article does
  not define the base). In **1995** the diamond company paid **about $700 million** in taxes to the republic; the
  republic's 1995 budget was **about 7 trillion rubles**, including about 1 trillion in federal subsidies; 1995
  diamond output was worth **$1.3 billion**. **SOURCED** (Kasimov). The legal form of the "20%" (a share of rough
  output, of revenue, or of export quota) is **NOT FOUND**.
- **The company:** created by presidential decree **No. 158S of 19 February 1992** as Almazy Rossii–Sakha, built on
  the Yakutalmaz enterprises (Russian Wikipedia). At founding the federal government and the Republic of Sakha held
  **32% each**, the employee collective **23%**, **eight district (ulus) administrations 1% each**, and a state pension
  fund 5% (FundingUniverse; **SOURCED to that secondary page**). **DERIVED:** 32 + 32 + 23 + 8 + 5 = 100. Later split
  (Russian Wikipedia, as of about 2017): Russian Federation **33%**, Republic of Sakha **25%**, district (ulus)
  administrations **8%**, free float **34%**. **SOURCED to the page.**
- **The 2005–06 contest over control (SOURCED, *Kommersant*, 26 January 2006, https://www.kommersant.ru/doc/644020,
  translated):** shares then stood at Russian government **37%**, Republic of Sakha **32%**, work collective **23%**,
  eight diamond-district uluses **8%**. Moscow sought a controlling stake through a new share issue; the republic's
  parliament (Il Tumen) would agree only if Sakha kept 32% and the uluses 8%, with written guarantees of compensation for
  lost budget revenue *"in full and on a long-term basis,"* a say in company strategy, and objections to transferring
  Yakutalmaz property through the courts. A December 2005 protocol promised compensation *"in full"* (higher profit and
  property taxes, federal aid, and the federation taking over some social facilities from Sakha's books); the deputies
  judged it insufficient. In August 2006 the federal government said it would give Sakha **all** of the diamond extraction
  tax (then split **60% to regions, 40% federal**) as compensation for the state taking control (*"50 percent plus one
  share"*). **SOURCED** (Lenta.ru, 29 August 2006, https://lenta.ru/news/2006/08/29/nalog/, translated). (The 37/32/23/8
  split differs from the 1992 founding split above: see Unresolved conflicts.)
- **What failed (SOURCED, FundingUniverse):** De Beers cut off trade at the end of 1996 over an estimated $1 billion of
  diamonds leaking onto world markets; *"For the first three quarters of 1997, ARS did not export any diamonds"*; Moscow
  accused the company of tax fraud and raided its offices in early 1997.
- **Kept back for the region:** the company channels part of its social spending through the republic's **"Target
  Fund for Future Generations of the Republic of Sakha (Yakutia)"** (Tselevoy fond budushchikh pokoleniy), *"more than
  4.5 billion rubles"* invested since 2011; a PwC study put the company's social spending at **about 2.8% of revenue**
  against about 0.5% for other companies rated. **SOURCED** (Russian Wikipedia, translated).

**How goods arrive (SOURCED, city administration 2012, translated):**
- *"Year-round connection with Krasnoyarsk, as with other regions of Russia, is possible only by air, and during
  navigation by water along the Yenisei and the Northern Sea Route through the port of Dudinka."* Krasnoyarsk is
  **1,500 km by air and 2,000 km by river**.
- Three supply schemes: **river, June to early October, 40% of goods stocks**; **sea, Northern Sea Route from
  Murmansk to Dudinka, 50%**; **air, year-round, 10%, mostly perishables**.
- **May is the hardest month:** sea navigation is closed by ice and river navigation not yet open; *"the only way to
  deliver in this period is air transport."*
- River freight from Krasnoyarsk is **8 to 10 times cheaper per kilogram** than air, so summer orders are placed by
  river.
- **No more than 20 commercial firms** bring in large consignments and supply the retail trade; smaller shops buy on the
  local wholesale market. **SOURCED.**
- **(Note on the term *severny zavoz*, "northern delivery":** the federal and regional "northern delivery" program
  supplies remote settlements of Krasnoyarsk Krai, not Norilsk itself: the Yenisei River Shipping Company, *"main
  shareholder: Nornickel,"* delivered **about 70,000 metric tons** to Evenkia in 2019, serving districts where **about
  100,000 people** live (Mintrans, 2 July 2019). The 2012 city document describes Norilsk's own supply as done by
  private firms *"at their own expense, ensuring the food security of the territory."* **SOURCED.**)

**What the city makes for itself (SOURCED, city administration 2012, translated):**
- **32 businesses** make food: a bread plant and **15 mini-bakeries**, 7 confectionery shops, **a dairy**, a meat
  plant, 2 meat-processing shops, 3 ready-meal shops, **4 fish-smoking shops**, 4 soft-drink bottlers, **2 beer
  bottlers**, and a breakfast-cereal packer.
- Local producers supply **all the bread** eaten in the territory, **over 50% of milk and dairy**, **32% of sausage**,
  **over 60% of soft drinks**, and **about 8% of confectionery**.
- *"Because of the climate, there are no possibilities for agricultural production"* in the municipality; food and the
  raw materials for local food processing are brought in, **except reindeer meat and fish from local waters**.
- Nornickel opened its own grocery chain, **"Podsolnukh"** ("Sunflower"), described by the company as *"a social
  project aimed at the stable supply of quality food to the district"*; goods come mainly *"from Europe and Russia via
  the Northern Sea Route on Norilsk Nickel's own ships"* and by river; the second store (2011) had **more than 8,000
  product lines** on 600 m²; seven stores were planned. **SOURCED** (Retail.ru 2011). Measured effect on prices: **NOT
  FOUND**.

**What fails in self-supply (SOURCED, city administration 2012, translated):**
- In **2004** potato stocks were overbought and firms took heavy losses; in **2005** firms cut orders and the average
  retail price of potatoes rose from **16.6 to 26.2 rubles/kg**. **DERIVED:** (26.2 − 16.6) ÷ 16.6 = **+58%**.
- Egg shortages occur when stocks run out before the next delivery; eggs are then flown in *"in urgent order,"* and
  retail prices jump.
- The administration's diagnosis: **no one knows the total stock in the territory**; the law does not oblige firms to
  report stocks to local government; *"In the conditions of the Far North, such a state of affairs cannot be considered
  normal."*
- A regional decree (Krasnoyarsk Krai, **No. 281-p of 30 September 2003**) caps the markup on **14 food items** in
  Norilsk at **45%**, of which the supply-and-distribution markup is 15%; transport alone adds **5 to 150% or more**
  to purchase prices. Examples: salt bought in Krasnoyarsk at 6 rubles/kg costs 4 rubles/kg by river (**67%**) or 11.5
  rubles/kg by sea (**192%**) to deliver; sugar bought in Moscow at 30 rubles/kg costs 5.5 rubles/kg by river (**18%**)

### 2.3 What a self-reliant region keeps back for itself

**Western Australia's domestic gas reservation (main case).**
- **Origin (SOURCED, WA government media statement, 12 October 2006,
  https://www.wa.gov.au/government/media-statements/Carpenter%20Labor%20Government/State-Government's-domestic-gas-reservation-policy-released-20061012):**
  Premier Alan Carpenter set aside *"for domestic use the equivalent of 15 percent of gas available from any future
  offshore development."* Reason: *"contracted gas supplies from our North West Shelf are due to expire in 10 to 15
  years' time,"* and gas then fueled **60%** of the state's electricity. Applied *"as part of the negotiating process
  with export gas producers,"* case by case.
- **Current terms (SOURCED, WA Domestic Gas Policy page, updated 10 June 2026,
  https://www.wa.gov.au/government/wa-domestic-gas-policy):** *"Reserving gas equivalent to 15% of LNG exports for the
  WA market,"* *"over the life of the project or agreement not per annum"*; given effect through State Agreements,
  State Development Agreements and Domestic Gas Commitment Agreements struck before a project starts; applies only once
  gas comes onshore (so the floating Prelude and the Northern Territory–landed Ichthys are not covered); enforced by the
  Department of Energy and Economic Diversification. A **19 September 2024** update reserved a percentage of **onshore**
  gas production for domestic use **until 2030** (caption only; detail NOT FOUND). In **August 2020** the policy was
  amended *"to prevent the export of local gas"* (Natural Gas World, 17 August 2020; detail behind a sign-in wall).
- **Enforcement lever (SOURCED, K. Neill, UWA Business School Discussion Paper 15.08, March 2015,
  https://api.research-repository.uwa.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/96622067/15.08Neill%2CK.-WESTERNAUSTRALIASDOMESTICGASRESERVATIONPOLICY-THEELEMENTALECONOMICS.pdf):**
  *"Although the policy is not formalized in legislation, it is enforced by the Government via their right to deny
  access to land (or potentially state waters) for LNG plants and infrastructure."* Its aim is *"to maintain domestic
  gas prices below export parity."*

**Alaska Hire (neighbor: a local-hire law that failed in court).**
- Enacted **1972** as "Local Hire Under State Leases"; oil and gas leases, pipeline right-of-way permits and

### 3.1 Butte and the Anaconda Company: press, legislature and martial law

**Pages read:** Montana Historical Society, *Montana: Stories of the Land*, Chapter 20, Historical Document 1,
"Robert E. Miller, Duane Bowler, and Albert Gaskill Reminiscences on Working on Anaconda Company-owned Newspapers"
(source: Don Anderson Writing, 1959, Small Collection 1211, MHS Research Center; excerpted in *Not In Precious Metals
Alone*, 1976, pp. 217–19), https://mths.mt.gov/education/Textbook/Chapter20/Ch20-1_Miller.pdf (text extracted
locally) · Wikipedia, "The Montana Standard," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Montana_Standard · Wikipedia,
"Anaconda Copper," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaconda_Copper · Wikipedia, "1914 Butte, Montana, labor riots,"
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1914_Butte,_Montana,_labor_riots · Verdigris Project, "Butte, America's Story, Episode
54: Union Hall Destroyed" (transcript of a KBMF radio series hosted by Dick Gibson),
https://www.verdigrisproject.org/butte-americas-story-blog/butte-americas-story-episode-54-union-hall-destroyed.

**The press.**
- **Ownership and its end:** *"In 1959, the Anaconda Company sold its eight Montana newspapers, including the
  Standard, to the Lee Newspaper Group"* (renamed Lee Enterprises in 1960). By 1920 the company owned, among others,
  the *Butte Post*, *Butte Miner*, *Daily Missoulian*, *Helena Independent* and *Billings Gazette*. **SOURCED**
  (Wikipedia, "The Montana Standard"). Other sources in search 20 give seven dailies and two weeklies, held through a
  subsidiary, the Fairmont Corporation (**UNVERIFIED**; count conflict noted). A search summary's claim that the company
  owned *"nine of the state's fourteen major dailies"* by 1920 is **UNVERIFIED** (not on the pages read).
- **Handover date:** *"A new age in Montana journalism began June 1, 1959, with the Lee purchase"* (Albert Gaskill).
  **SOURCED** (MHS document).
- **How control worked, in the reporters' words (SOURCED, MHS document):**
  - Robert E. Miller (Helena, 1930s): after an editorial questioning a gold dredge, the editor was *"called to visit
    headquarters in Butte"* and told *"the rule was that no company paper ever questioned any aspect of the mining
    industry."* As editor at Livingston, his first task each morning was *"to read the Montana Standard very thoroughly
    and determine how many controversial matters had been handled,"* so he could follow company line *"without any
    day-to-day instruction."*
  - Duane Bowler (Helena, from 1941): an embezzlement story at the county courthouse *"was killed on orders from Butte"*
    because the official *"had performed a useful errand some years before and had been promised protection."* From
    about 1944: *"Union matters, industrial disasters, cost of living, consumerism . . . [were] given little or no
    attention. The legislature was played straight. No speculation, no interpretation. . . . Silicosis came under the
    euphemism of 'industrial hygiene.' Certain political figures' talks were give preminence [sic], others didn't exist."*
    Editors' lives were easier when *"the Sixth Floor — a name given to the company headquarters in Butte"* received
    the fewest calls from politicians and others; the owners *"didn't give a hoot whether the politicians like the
    newspapers or not. They did care how they voted."*
  - Albert Gaskill (Butte, from 1949): after the sale, *"Much news critical of the Anaconda Company became Page 1 news.
    We new employees of Lee did everything we could to show the public the Anaconda Company's copper collar was gone."*
    *"Many Butte persons were hard to convince that Anaconda still didn't control the press,"* and some called the new
    owners *"carpetbaggers from Iowa."* When the Montana Press Association met in Butte, *"the city's newspapers weren't
    represented at the sessions."*
- **"Copper collar":** *"a metaphor used to describe a person or company directly controlled by the Anaconda Company,"*
  coined in the late 1800s (Wikipedia, "Anaconda Copper"). **SOURCED to the page.**

**The legislature.** In **1903** the company shut down its operations, *"putting 15,000 men out of work until the
legislature enacted the regulations it demanded"* (Wikipedia, "Anaconda Copper"). **SOURCED to the page;** the law's
name is not on the page (NOT FOUND here).

**1914: the union hall, the city government and martial law (neutral account).**
- The Butte Miners' Union (founded 1878) enforced a **card system**: miners showed paid-up union cards before shifts,
  which was unpopular because dues were often in arrears. On **12 June 1914** two shifts at the Speculator mine refused
  to show cards. On **13 June** (Miners' Union Day) dissidents attacked union officers; the hall was ransacked; acting
  mayor Frank Curran was thrown from a second-story window. On **21 June** a meeting of an estimated **3,000 to 5,000**
  miners formed the **Butte Mine Workers' Union**. On **23 June**, while Western Federation of Miners president Charles
  Moyer addressed about 200 members, the hall was surrounded and **destroyed with dynamite** taken from a nearby mine.
  **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "1914 Butte labor riots"). The radio transcript: about **20 charges** over several hours; it
  calls the card system *"the companies' rustling card system,"* which the union had accepted and helped run. **SOURCED.**
  When the rustling card began: **NOT FOUND**.
- After a dynamite explosion at an Anaconda office on **30 August**, Governor Samuel Stewart declared **martial law** on
  **31 August**; about **500 National Guardsmen** arrived on **1 September**; the last left on **12 November 1914**. On
  **9 September** the mine owners announced they would recognize **neither union**; the mines *"recognized no union
  from 1914 until 1934."* **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). (A search summary placed the proclamation about 2 September: minor
  date conflict.)
- **City government:** Socialist mayor **Lewis Duncan**, who had kept police from interfering, was removed: on **6
  October 1914** the district court ordered Duncan and the county sheriff out of office, and the city council chose a
  new mayor. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).

**What failed later (SOURCED, Wikipedia, "Anaconda Copper"):** Chile's 1971 nationalization of Chuquicamata stripped
the company of *"two-thirds of its copper production"* (the post-1973 government paid $250 million); ARCO bought the
company on **12 January 1977** for **$700 million**; underground mining in Butte stopped in 1980 and *"mining ceased
completely in 1982"* when the Berkeley Pit pumps were shut off.

### 3.2 Broken Hill and the Barrier Industrial Council: a union body with power over who worked

**Pages read:** Wikipedia, "Barrier Industrial Council," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrier_Industrial_Council ·
Australian Women's Register, "Broken Hill union ban on married women working,"
https://womenaustralia.info/entries/broken-hill-union-ban-on-married-women-working · Australian Women's Register
exhibition, "Unions and politics," https://womenaustralia.info/exhib/bh/unionspol.html · Wikipedia, "Chapman
Commission," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapman_Commission.

- **What it is:** *"Formed in 1923 by 18 trade unions"*; the Amalgamated Miners' Association joined in 1923, and *"By
  the end of 1925 all the tradesmen's unions had affiliated."* Its *"control over the supply of labor"* let it dictate
  terms to mining companies and state politicians; under *"compulsory unionism,"* numbered badges showed a worker was a
  financial (paid-up) member. It owned the town's main newspaper, the ***Barrier Daily Truth***, from **1899 to 2024**.
  Its influence *"has declined to some extent"* with falling mining employment and more state and federal regulation.
  **SOURCED** (Wikipedia). (The newspaper date predates the council's 1923 founding; the page gives both: conflict
  noted.)
- **Who could work:** the council *"distributed the union tickets needed for employment in Broken Hill."* *"By 1930, the
  Barrier Industrial Council had decreed that married women be barred from employment altogether in order to save jobs
  for younger women"*; the bar was lifted for *"the six years during World War Two"*; *"Teachers and other professionally
  trained married women were allowed to continue working"* if no qualified single woman was available. In **1915**,
  Mrs Francis Egan *"refused to join a union and found herself barred from employment."* **SOURCED** (Australian
  Women's Register).
- **How it ended:** in **1981** Jeanine Whitehair, who lost her dental assistant job at the Town Dental Clinic after
  marrying in November 1980, won reinstatement with support from the **NSW Equal Opportunities Board**; the Register calls
  it *"the beginnings of the erosion of the power of the Barrier Industrial Council."* **SOURCED.**
- **The 1919–20 "Big Strike":** *"In May 1919 Broken Hill's mines closed down due to the Big Strike"*; the **18-month**
  strike was called off in **November 1920**. Demands: shorter hours, safer underground conditions, compensation for
  dust disease. The state appointed Professor **Henry Chapman** (University of Sydney) as commissioner in **December
  1919**, with union representatives, mine managers, government scientists and medical officials on the board. It found
  *"61 men were found to be suffering from the symptoms of lead poisoning"* and that *"pneumoconiosis and lead poisoning
  arises in Broken Hill miners as a result of the inhalation of dust."* The **Edmunds Award (September 1920)** set a
  **35-hour week for underground workers** and *"compulsory medical examinations before entering the workforce."*
  **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, "Chapman Commission").
- **The union body against the elected city council (1977; SOURCED, Wikipedia, "Barrier Industrial Council"):** *"In
  1977, the BIC engaged in industrial action directed at the City of Broken Hill"* which *"culminated in the resignation
  of the city council, including mayor Noel Hicks"* and *"the appointment of administrators by the state government."*
  It began when a council employee, Noel Latham, *"refused to pay a fine imposed by the BIC"*; other workers refused to
  work with him, *"resulting in a two-week pile-up of garbage"* (citing the *Canberra Times*); the BIC expelled him and
  asked the council to dismiss him; the council found no legal grounds; **97** council employees in garbage collection,
  sanitation and road works walked out.
- Search 43 summaries quote the *Australian Dictionary of Biography* (entry on BIC president W. S. "Shorty" O'Neil) as
  saying the council regulated job access and labor supply and also *"leisure activities, shopping hours, illegal two-up
  schools, off-course bookmaking, the local press, and beer prices"* (**UNVERIFIED**: the page returned HTTP 403).
- **NOT FOUND:** the council's control over shop and business hours; any residency or local-birth preference in hiring

### 3.3 Gary, Indiana, and U.S. Steel: the company built the town plan, not the whole town

  **12-hour, seven-day shifts** lasted until **1923**. **SOURCED** (Discover Indiana History).
- **1919 strike:** a riot on **4 October 1919** between steelworkers and strikebreakers; Governor James P. Goodrich
  declared martial law three days later; *"over 4,000 federal troops under the command of Major General Leonard Wood"*

- **What failed:** population peaked at **178,320 (1960)**; **69,093 (2020)**; **67,289 (2025 estimate)**. Gary Works

  **DERIVED:** 2,246 ÷ 30,000 = **7.5%** of the early-1970s workforce; 67,289 ÷ 178,320 = **38%** of the peak
  population.

### 4.1 Craft guilds versus merchant patricians (historical)

**Cologne, 1396.** Page read: German Wikipedia, "Kölner Rat bis 1796" (reached from "Verbundbrief"),
https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbundbrief.
- Until 1396 power lay with the patrician families (*Geschlechter*), a closed oligarchy. The **weavers' uprising of
  1370/71** weakened them; in **1391** they lost the right to appoint the mayor. After a bloodless coup, a provisional
  council sat in **June 1396**, and the **Verbundbrief** became the city's basic law, drafted by city clerk Gerlach van
  Hauwe. **SOURCED** (translated).
- **How power was shared:** voting ran through **22 *Gaffeln*** (guilds and merchant associations). The council had
  **36 elected members plus 13 co-opted members** (*Gebrechsherren*), **49** in all; one-year terms, with **half the
  council replaced every 24 June and 24 December**; a control body of **44 *Vertrauensleute*** ("trusted men") had to
  approve major decisions. **Four merchant Gaffeln** (Eisenmarkt, Windeck, Himmelreich, Schwarzenhaus) dominated; the
  weavers' guild (**Wollenamt**) was the largest Gaffel; most others were craft guilds. **SOURCED.**
- Search snippets add that every full citizen had to join one of the 22 Gaffeln and that the Verbundbrief took effect
  on **14 September 1396** (**UNVERIFIED**, snippet level).
- **Later contests:** revolts in **1481**, **1512/13** (followed by the *Transfixbrief*), **1609/10**, and **1683–85**
  (the Gülich revolt, ending with Nikolaus Gülich's execution). The council was dissolved on **28 May 1796** under
  French occupation. **SOURCED.**

**Florence, 1378.** Page read: Wikipedia, "Ciompi revolt," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciompi_revolt.
- Florence had **21 guilds: 7 major** (cloth merchants, bankers) and **14 minor** (artisans, craftsmen, skilled
  workers). Of roughly **14,000** people in wool manufacture, only about **200** qualified for guild membership; the rest
  (*sottoposti*) were subject to the wool guild (*Arte della Lana*) without a voice. **SOURCED.**
- **June–August 1378:** first violence **22 June**; the Ciompi seized the government on **21 July**. Demands, which the
  article calls *"fairly modest"*: a guild for wool workers; more wool production to cut unemployment; the right to
  elect three priors; less corporal punishment; tax reform. Gains: **three new guilds** with access to office (about
  **13,000** members combined); Michele di Lando as gonfaloniere of justice; a direct tax on household wealth (*estimo*,
  **29 October 1378**). On **31 August** di Lando's government defeated the radical "Eight Saints"; on **1 September**
  citizens approved dissolving the Ciompi guild. In **1382** elite families, citing fear of foreign incursion and a
  dispute between **wool merchants and dyers**, restored the old order and repealed the guild government's reforms.
  **SOURCED.**

### 4.2 Production versus freight

**Pittsburgh: steelmakers and shippers against the Pennsylvania Railroad.** Pages read: Wikipedia, "South Pennsylvania
Railroad," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pennsylvania_Railroad; Wikipedia, "Pittsburgh and Lake Erie Railroad,"
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_and_Lake_Erie_Railroad.
- The Pennsylvania Railroad's *"discriminatory rates"* were *"a hot issue in Pittsburgh."* The **Pittsburgh and Lake Erie
  Railroad** was formed by Pittsburgh businessmen (William McCreery, a merchant and railroad builder who *"had suffered
  at the PRR's hands"*; later James I. Bennett and David Hostetter); the article gives both **1870** (articles of
  association) and **1875** (formation): conflict. Nicknamed the **"Little Giant"**: about **0.1%** of the nation's rail
  mileage but *"around one percent of its tonnage,"* because it *"served the steel mills of the greater Pittsburgh
  area."* **SOURCED.** **DERIVED:** 1% ÷ 0.1% = about **10 times** its share of mileage.
- The **South Pennsylvania Railroad** (surveyed from **1881**, built into **1885**): backed by William H. Vanderbilt
  (New York Central) with Pittsburgh capitalists including **Andrew Carnegie and Henry Clay Frick**, who wanted to
  *"break the Pennsylvania Railroad's freight monopoly in Pittsburgh and western Pennsylvania."* J. P. Morgan brokered a
  settlement between the two railroads: construction stopped and the line sat idle for almost 20 years. The Pennsylvania
  Turnpike Commission bought it in **1937**, and the turnpike opened in **October 1940** through six of its nine tunnels.
  **SOURCED.** (The steel shippers lost their competing line through a deal between the two railroads.)

**"Pittsburgh Plus" (makers outside Pittsburgh and steel users against the basing-point price).** Pages read: *Time*,
"Business & Finance: Cease and Desist," 4 August 1924, https://time.com/archive/6652044/business-finance-cease-and-desist/
· NBER, *Price Research in the Steel and Petroleum Industries* (Committee on Price Research, 1939), chapter "Analysis of
Two Important Research Projects," https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c5800/c5800.pdf (text extracted locally).
- **How it worked:** U.S. Steel sold plates, shapes and bars delivered, priced as if made in Pittsburgh plus freight from
  Pittsburgh to the buyer, wherever the steel was actually made. The FTC called that freight *"imaginary."* **SOURCED**
  (*Time*).
- **Who complained:** the **Western Association of Rolled Steel Consumers**, backed by **32 states**. The FTC's order told
  U.S. Steel (which made *"nearly 50%"* of the nation's rolled steel) to stop selling at Pittsburgh Plus prices or at
  prices based on points other than where goods were made or shipped. Gary, Indiana, followed the fight *"with intense
  interest,"* since the order could shift the steel industry's center. **SOURCED** (*Time*, 1924).
- **What followed:** a multiple basing-point system replaced the single Pittsburgh base (search 29 summary of a law review,
  **UNVERIFIED**). *"Drastic changes in summer 1938 increased the number of basing points and substantially reduced the
  differential between Pittsburgh and other basing points,"* reducing *"'phantom freight'"* but leaving *"the essential
  character of the basing point method (i.e., delivered price quotations and freight absorption)"* unchanged. **SOURCED**
  (NBER 1939). A web page's FTC-attributed figure of **$7.60** phantom freight on a **$30** ton of Gary steel sold in
  Chicago is **UNVERIFIED** (not a primary source).

**Minneapolis, 1934: trucking as power.** Page read: Wikipedia, "Minneapolis general strike of 1934,"
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_general_strike_of_1934.
- Teamsters **Local 574** organized coal drivers through a strike in **February 1934**; the main strike began **16 May
  1934**; a second wave ran in July and August. Members were truck drivers, helpers and "inside workers" (warehouse
  staff, packers). The employers' **Citizens Alliance** *"had power and sway over the Minneapolis economy and thwarted union
  organizing."* **SOURCED.**
- **Why trucking gave leverage:** Minneapolis was *"the major distribution center for the Upper Midwest,"* and the strike
  shut down *"most commercial transport in the city."* **SOURCED.**
- **20 July ("Bloody Friday"):** police fired on picketers; **2 strikers killed, 67 wounded**. **26 July:** Governor
  Floyd Olson declared martial law with about **4,000** National Guardsmen; *"He banned picketing and offered permits only
  to trucks delivering essentials."* **Settlement:** on **21 August** a federal mediator secured acceptance of a proposal
  from the Citizens Alliance's A. W. Strong *"incorporat[ing] the union's major demands"*; formal end **22 August**.
  **SOURCED.**

**Port and warehouse workers versus the drivers' union: the West Coast "march inland."** Page read: ILWU Local 19, "The
ILWU Story: the warehouse industry," https://ilwu19.com/history/the_ilwu_story/the_warehouse_industry.
- After the 1934 waterfront strike, longshoremen carried organizing into warehouses; the "march inland" *"took on real
  organizational emphasis in early 1936"*; the first coastwise warehouse conference met in **June 1936**. In **1937** the
  AFL *"tried to strip the West Coast longshore union of its warehouse affiliates"*; the Pacific Coast district joined
  the CIO and became the ILWU. Teamster chief **Dave Beck** *"pulled a blockade and boycott of the port of San Francisco
  in the fall of 1937,"* which *"fizzled in the face of daily mobilizations."* By 1943 about **85%** of Sacramento
  warehouse workers were ILWU members. After James Hoffa succeeded Beck, limited cooperation began in **1958**; in
  **1960** the two unions *"agreed to present joint demands, pursue joint negotiations and strike jointly."* **SOURCED**
  (a union's own history; one side's account). A search summary credits Beck with stifling the march inland in Seattle,
  Tacoma, Portland and Los Angeles (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **NOT FOUND:** a documented contest between port or freight unions and **manufacturing** unions in one port-industrial
  city (the case found is port versus drivers over warehouse jurisdiction).

### 4.3 Mondragon: production, finance and retail inside one federation

**Pages read:** Wikipedia, "Mondragon Corporation," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondragon_Corporation · Mondragon
homepage, https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/ · NCEO (National Center for Employee Ownership) blog, "Two Co-ops
Leave Mondragon Corporation," https://www.nceo.org/employee-ownership-blog/two-coops-leave-mondragon-corporation.

- **Founding:** Father **José María Arizmendiarrieta** and students founded it in Mondragón (Arrasate), Gipuzkoa, in
  **1956**; the first company, **Talleres Ulgor** (later Fagor Electrodomésticos), started with five people selected in
  1955; first product, paraffin heaters. **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
- **Scale (2025):** **71,415** employees; revenue **€11.322 billion** (Wikipedia); the corporation's homepage gives
  *"70.000"* people and sales in more than 150 countries. **SOURCED.** Number of cooperatives now: NOT FOUND (257
  companies and organizations at end-2016, Wikipedia).
- **The 2025 Annual Report (SOURCED, MONDRAGON, *Annual Report 2025*,
  https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/people/site/assets/files/110675/mondragon-descargable-2025-eng.pdf, text extracted
  locally):** people **71,400**; sales **€11.322 billion**; investments **€406.3 million**; profits **€618.8 million**;
  more than **1,300** new jobs; *"the largest employer in the Basque Country, the second-largest employer in Navarre and the
  fifth-largest private-sector employer in Spain."* By area: **Industry** sales **€4.919 billion**, **26,975** jobs, €220
  million investment; **Distribution** sales **€6.403 billion**, **40,078** people, 1,508 outlets; **Finance** €33.913
  billion managed resources, LagunAro **30,135** active members and €7.818 billion equity; **Knowledge** €205 million in
  R&D, 2,133 full-time researchers. **DERIVED:** Distribution 40,078 ÷ 71,400 = **56%** of people and 6.403 ÷ 11.322 =
  **57%** of sales; Industry 26,975 ÷ 71,400 = **38%** of people and 4.919 ÷ 11.322 = **43%** of sales. (The retail and
  distribution side now outweighs the makers inside the federation that the makers founded.)
- **Structure:** operating rules approved by majority vote at **Cooperative Congresses** govern the **Standing
  Committee**, the **General Council**, the member cooperatives and four areas: **Finance, Industry, Retail, Knowledge**.
  **SOURCED** (Wikipedia).
  - **Finance:** **Caja Laboral** (founded **1959** as a labor savings bank; now **Laboral Kutxa**); **Lagun Aro** (social
    welfare, **1966**), which ended 2025 with a fund of **€7.818 billion** and **30,135** active members. **SOURCED.**
  - **Retail (distribution inside a producers' federation):** **Eroski**, formed **1969** from ten small consumer
    cooperatives; **both worker-members and consumer-members** sit in its decision-making bodies; in **2008** worker-members
    voted to turn subsidiaries into cooperatives; in 2025 it had more than **28,200 employees, 8,336 of them
    worker-members**. **SOURCED.** **DERIVED:** 8,336 ÷ 28,200 = **30%** worker-members; 28,200 ÷ 71,415 = **39%** of all
    Mondragon employees are in Eroski.
- **Pay ratio:** agreed ratios between executive pay and the lowest pay range from **3:1 to 9:1** across cooperatives,
  **averaging 5:1**; each cooperative sets its own by periodic democratic vote of its worker-owners. **SOURCED**
  (Wikipedia). The original ratio and its history: NOT FOUND on the page.
- **How contests are settled (documented mechanisms; SOURCED, Annual Report 2025):** *"A one-member, one-vote system is
  used to elect the cooperatives' governing and management bodies and to adopt the company's strategic decisions"*;
  **profit redistribution**: *"each cooperative earmarks a percentage of its profit (a minimum of 13%) to a divisional fund
  to achieve a more equitable sharing of the wealth generated or for partial offsetting of the cooperatives that have
  suffered losses"*; **"Redeployment of employees, enabling employment to be maintained within the cooperative group"**;
  financial intercooperation instruments for support *"in situations of difficulty."* LagunAro's **Employment Assistance
  Fund** rose to **€177.62 million** in 2025, and people in long-term unemployment under it fell from **563 to 410**.
  (This settles the 13% figure in NCEO's favor; Wikipedia's 10% is the outlier.)
- **What failed:**
  - **Fagor Electrodomésticos:** filed for bankruptcy on **16 October 2013** after heavy losses; about **5,600** jobs at

  - **Departures by vote:** **Ampo** and **Irizar** worker-owners voted to leave in **2008** (Wikipedia). **ULMA** and

    "interference." They keep insuring workers with Lagun Aro and keep working with Mondragon Unibertsitatea (Wikipedia).
    **SOURCED** (with the conflicts noted).

**Tangible effects of the reputation.**
- **Migration:** WA had a net gain of **630** people from interstate migration in the September 2020 quarter, *"the first
