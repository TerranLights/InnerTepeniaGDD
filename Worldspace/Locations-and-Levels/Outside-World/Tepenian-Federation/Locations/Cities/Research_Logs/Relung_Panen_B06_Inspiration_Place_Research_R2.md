## 1 · Inuvik Community Greenhouse, Inuvik, Northwest Territories, Canada

**R1 open threads chased:** the society's plot agreement and house rules; winter use of the building; the Arctic Market; Grollier Hall survivors' relationship to the site today. Plus R2 kinds: settings, subcultures, leisure, third places, festivals with origin and organizer.

### A. Other settings
- **The greenhouse across its year [K7] [P4]:** the building opens to members in early May (6 May in 2016, "Gardening season in Inuvik officially starts today with the opening of the Inuvik Greenhouse to its membership"), plant sales start later than the plots, and the doors "officially close mid-October" each year. SOURCED (greenthumbinuvik.blogspot.com, a member's blog, 2016-05-06; NWT News/North via yellowknifer.com, 2017-09-27). The 2016 member blog counts "125 member gardens and 15 community gardens" and calls the greenhouse "the hub of gardening in Inuvik" and "a huge part of summer time activities." SOURCED (blog, 2016-05-06; a member since 2005). Plot counts in R1 (88 in 2004, 188 in 2024) bracket this figure.
- **The greenhouse in winter [K10] [R]:** the main building does not host winter activity in any source found; the only winter growing is the heated hydroponic container (R1). In 2021 the executive director said winter is "too cold and dark" for traditional growing and was exploring modular LED/HVAC farms. UNVERIFIED (search snippet summarizing CBC/hortidaily; CBC was refused in R1 and not retried). Winter use of the building itself: NOT FOUND.
- **A café inside the greenhouse [K10, K12]:** the Inuvik Community Greenhouse Café opened the first week of July 2024; the menu changes weekly and uses greenhouse lettuce (Caesar salad) and seasonal peas and zucchini; board games and reading are available; workshops are held. The manager's stated reason: "There's a lack of these spaces in town and community spaces that are low-cost." A founding member: "The real benefit of the greenhouse is the social aspect." SOURCED (cabinradio.ca, 2024-07-09). The same article mentions Arctic Markets on Wednesdays at the greenhouse. SOURCED.
- **The Arctic Market's two homes [K10, K4]:** June to September in the covered wooden boardwalk of the Inuvik Welcome Centre (190 Mackenzie Road, inside Chief Jim Koe Park), Saturdays 11 am to 3 pm, "rain or shine, sleet or snow"; October to May in the Community Hall of the Midnight Sun Complex (95 Gwich'in Road), usually 11 am to 2 pm, "can vary depending on any related event's schedule." Run by the Town of Inuvik's tourism office. SOURCED (Town of Inuvik, *Arctic Market Agreement 2026*, PDF read via text extraction). [P4]
- **Residential-school geography [K9] [P6]:** two large residential halls stood in the town from the 1950s: Grollier Hall (Roman Catholic) and Stringer Hall (Anglican), both with electric heat, plumbing and utilidors. Families' seasonal round (fall moose hunts, winter trapping, spring muskrat camps, summer fish and whale camps) was cut off for students, who could "only join the fish camp when school was out." In June 1972 three Stringer Hall students fled toward Tuktoyaktuk; two died on the land. SOURCED (NiCHE, "The Land Kept Teaching," 2025-12-09). Grollier Hall opened 1959, run by the Oblates until 1985, closed 1997. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of Legacy of Hope / stamp listing).

### B. Subcultures
- **Indigenous groups and their halls [K4]:** Ingamo Hall Friendship Centre (Mackenzie Road), built 1977 from 850 logs rafted down the Mackenzie from Fort Simpson, "the largest log structure constructed north of the Arctic Circle," hosts "cultural events and community feasts geared towards helping Indigenous youth." SOURCED (inuvik.ca Ingamo Hall page, read 2026-10-29). Programs named elsewhere: drum dancing, beading, sewing, traditional cooking, Arctic sports, bingo. UNVERIFIED (snippets). The hall's name combines two group names (stated in a snippet; the town page read did not give it): UNVERIFIED.
- **Survivors and the greenhouse [K9] [P6]:** in 2021, after the Kamloops findings, a Gwich'in resident organized a walk to the greenhouse "as a kind of memorial" because it was part of Grollier Hall; walkers left children's shoes there and the greenhouse offered the space in front of it for a vigil. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of a CBC article; CBC refused in R1, not retried). In 2024 the town's volunteer naming committee (three residents and three councillors), after consulting elders, proposed renaming Ruyant Crescent, named for the priest who ran Grollier Hall for more than 20 years, to Jak Zheii Place (Gwich'in for "blueberry"); public hearing 14 May 2024; the mayor called it "long overdue." SOURCED (bishop-accountability.org reprint of a CBC story, 2024-05-08). Outcome of the hearing: NOT FOUND.
- **Gardeners as a group [K14]:** members include long-standing plot holders (the blog author since 2005), elders on free plots, high-school volunteers (R1, and snippet of "local high school students" planting and watering: UNVERIFIED). Home gardening also runs outside the greenhouse: flower beds, balcony gardens, potato boxes, and gardens at camps along the Mackenzie system. SOURCED (blog 2016).
- **Transient professionals [K4, K7]:** NWT legislative debates and press repeatedly name a shortage of staff housing for teachers, nurses and medical staff in the region, including Inuvik. UNVERIFIED for Inuvik specifics (search snippets of hansard.opennwt.ca; no Inuvik page opened). Turnover figures: NOT FOUND.

### C. Leisure industries
- **Nightlife and its rules [K6, K10]:** the Mad Trapper is described as the last dedicated bar in Inuvik; Shivers Restaurant and the Legion (McInnes Branch 220, members) have bar areas. UNVERIFIED (snippets). On 10 May 2017 council passed, 4 to 3, a bylaw letting the Mad Trapper open 16 more Sundays a year in summer on top of 10 already allowed; supporters argued people "will find alcohol on Sundays... whether it's from the bootleggers or they save it from the day before"; one opposing councillor called the result "totally disgusting." SOURCED (Inuvik Drum via yellowknifer.com, 2017). [K9]
- **Arctic Market as a sold leisure product [K8]:** vendor fee $10 per table per market, $10 for power; Midnight Sun Complex hall concession $30 per market (space, power, equipment); only crafted or handmade items and food; "mass produce" and non-handmade goods not allowed; food vendors must display a GNWT Food Establishment Permit; hair, tattoo, piercing and massage vendors need a Personal Service Establishment Permit. SOURCED (*Arctic Market Agreement 2026*). Handmade Inuvialuit and Gwich'in goods are its draw ("If it's grown, made, carved, sewn, cooked, baked or created"). SOURCED (spectacularnwt.com summer events story, undated).
- **Greenhouse produce sales as low-cost food [K1, K12]:** July 2020: 300+ kg harvested in about a month; new Thursday market; an "Odd Box" (3-5 items, $15-20) beside the regular veggie box (6 items, $25-35); Pay-What-You-Can Wednesday, with unsold produce going to the food bank; 40 more families bought produce than the year before during COVID-19; popular items rationed. SOURCED (cabinradio.ca, 2020-07-23). [K5]
- What locals do when visitors leave: winter markets in the Midnight Sun Complex hall (above), curling club and Legion (UNVERIFIED snippet), and the winter festivals below.

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Inuvik Sunrise Festival [K2]:** first held 1988 (UNVERIFIED, snippet); run by the Town of Inuvik's events coordinator. 2026 edition 9-11 January after ~30 days of polar night; first sunrise 6 January at 1:42 pm. Program: glow-in-the-dark skating at the Midnight Sun Complex and a free "Taste of Inuvik" (bannock, chili, donuts) on Friday; an Arctic market with 20+ makers, free hot drinks, craft workshops including traditional yo-yo making, and a bonfire and fireworks over Twin Lakes on Saturday; a pancake breakfast, an elder storytelling booth and a snowshoe race with cash and airline-ticket prizes on Sunday. All free; community sponsors. The coordinator: "This is the highlight of our winter." SOURCED (cabinradio.ca, January 2026). Other years add drum-dance performances in Inuvialuktun, a snow-carving competition at Twin Lakes, an ice village and snowmobile tours to reindeer herds; one source gives the dark period as 33 days. SOURCED (spectacularnwt.com, undated). [K3] [P4]
- **Muskrat Jamboree [K2]:** first held 1957, older than the town; ran every year without a break until the pandemic; dog and snowmobile races, jigging, log sawing, ice chiseling, drumming and dancing, muskrat skinning, community feast; organized by a volunteer committee that fundraises each year. UNVERIFIED (search snippets; the Yahoo/CBC reprint and yellowknifer pages returned 404; ykcf.ca 404). Died at the sources.
- **Great Northern Arts Festival [K13]:** founded 1989 by two local women as an art show at the Inuvialuit Corporate Centre with "some two dozen artists from different communities in the Beaufort Delta"; purpose: artists who "had been sending their work to the south for years... had never had the chance before to attend an exhibition where their own work was on display." SOURCED (greatnorthernartsfestival.org/history). The 2023 festival (planned 14-23 July) was cancelled: no executive director since late March, board members hard to retain, the town gradually reducing its annual donation. SOURCED (cabinradio.ca, 2023). [K8, K9]
- **Inuvialuit Day, 5 June** (marks 5 June 1984, the Inuvialuit land-claim settlement), with drum dancing, music and food; **National Indigenous Peoples Day, 21 June.** SOURCED (spectacularnwt.com summer story).
- **Regional greenhouse network [K1, K14]:** in summer 2017 ITI funded seven community garden coordinators (200 hours each) across Arctic communities, supervised from the Inuvik greenhouse. SOURCED (NWT News/North 2017-09-27).

## 2 · Hveragerði, South Iceland

**R1 open threads chased:** current greenhouse workforce (local or migrant); Blómstrandi dagar founding and organizer; heat tariff for growers; the Reykir open-day tradition and its date. Plus R2 kinds. (R1 pages refused/dead not retried: mbl.is / Iceland Monitor, south.is Blómstrandi dagar page, timarit.is, jowat.com, the FSu newsletter PDF.)

**Key source this round:** *Greinargerð með fjárhagsáætlun Hveragerðisbæjar fyrir árið 2025* (the town's 2025 budget statement, hveragerdi.is/static/files/2025/bladsida-8-greinargerd.pdf), read in Icelandic via PDF text extraction, my translation. Cited below as "Budget 2025." Also *Stefna Hveragerðisbæjar 2023-2028* (town strategy, May 2023, prepared with KPMG from meetings, a resident survey and interviews involving ~350 people), cited "Strategy 2023," and *Stefnumótun eldri borgara 2024* (older-residents policy), cited "Seniors 2024."

### A. Other settings
- **A fast-growing commuter town [K9] [P9]:** population 2,652 (1 Jan 2020), 2,737 (2021), 2,941 (2022), 3,139 (2023), 3,264 (2024), expected 3,386 (Jan 2025): +27% since 2020; +7.1% in 2023 alone; target "just under 4,000" by end of 2028. SOURCED (Budget 2025). Residents' own weakness list includes "a high share of residents chooses to work in other municipalities," "one-sided economy," "lack of housing options," "low education level," and "an industrial district in the middle of town"; strengths named include a "close-knit, peaceful and child-friendly community." Winter transport disruption over Hellisheiði (the mountain road to Reykjavík) is listed as an infrastructure issue. SOURCED (Strategy 2023). [K5]
- **Schools and childcare [P4]:** the primary school (Grunnskólinn í Hveragerði) is in its third construction phase (fourth designed by 2026), with a new kitchen; two kindergartens, Undraland and Óskaland (a three-department extension at Óskaland), and a new kindergarten in Kambaland designed in 2025; the town made three, then four, daily kindergarten hours free (fees down 43% over the term). A former dairy building (Mjólkurbú, Breiðumörk 26) is used as classrooms. SOURCED (Budget 2025). "Good schools but hard to attract professionals." SOURCED (Strategy 2023).
- **Sports grounds and their loss [K9]:** Hamarshöllin, the inflated sports hall, collapsed/blew down in **February 2022**; the budget says this caused "a major loss of service" for Hamar and other users; because "the safety conditions for a new inflated hall" were not met, the town chose instead a new artificial-turf football field (2025) and a new sports hall as an extension to the older hall at Skólamörk, overseen by a building committee formed September 2024: "the largest build-out of sports facilities in the history of Hveragerðisbær." Hamar's training in other municipalities is paid for (driving costs, rented halls). SOURCED (Budget 2025). The hall being ~5,000 m² and built 2012: UNVERIFIED (snippet of an Iceland Monitor story; mbl.is refused in R1, not retried).
- **Geothermal ground and its neighbors [K9] [R]:** the town heating system runs on two boreholes; some steam goes to a steam network "used in industry"; used water flows through a reservoir into the Varmá river. Veitur is replacing heat exchangers so that return water falls from ~80 °C to ~40 °C, cutting heat discharged to the Varmá from 13 MW to 5 MW; a new borehole was being drilled from late November 2024 into December 2025. Tariffs: not stated. SOURCED (veitur.is, read 2026-10-29). Residents list "power plants on Hellisheiði and falling temperature in the hot-spring area" as a threat. SOURCED (Strategy 2023). A 26 Oct 2026 opinion piece by a councillor and a geologist: 100% of the town's land lies inside the 15 km impact zone of Hellisheiðarvirkjun; steam plumes are visible; residents feel frequent earthquakes they attribute to the plant; air quality is monitored at a kindergarten; under a proposed property-tax model the town would get about 1% (~6.2 million ISK a year) of the plant's tax revenue. SOURCED as the authors' claims (dfs.is, 2026-10-26). [K8]
- **Entry house at the hot-spring park:** adult entry 500 ISK (2025); budgeted entry income 12.3 million ISK plus 4.3 million from goods and services. SOURCED (Budget 2025). [K8]
