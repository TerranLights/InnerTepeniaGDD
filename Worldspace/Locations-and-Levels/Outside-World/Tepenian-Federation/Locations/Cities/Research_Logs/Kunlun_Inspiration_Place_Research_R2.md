## Pick 1 · NOIRLab [PRIMARY] — "(under authority of the NSF)"

**Scope this round:** Round 1 covered the organization, the Tucson HQ, the AURA Recinto's facilities, the La Serena and Vicuña basics, the dark-sky laws and the work rhythm. This round goes into the **Chilean side** (life between the La Serena base and the mountain, the valley and its people, the region's festivals) because Pick 2 names Kitt Peak and Maunakea directly. **The Tucson and Tohono O'odham side and the Hilo and Waimea side are written under Pick 2** so they are not written twice.

### A. Other settings (the spaces daily life moves through)

- **The commute as a setting: the "carry-all."** Staff and visitors do not drive themselves up the mountain: "Use of rental cars to go up to the mountain is not allowed." A shuttle ("carry-all") runs on a fixed timetable. **Cerro Tololo:** Mon, Tue, Thu and Fri, leaves La Serena 08:45 and arrives 10:30, leaves Tololo 17:00 and arrives La Serena 18:45. On Wednesday there are two runs each way (up at 11:00 and 16:30; down at 08:30 and 13:15). **Cerro Pachón:** Mon and Thu, 08:45 up and 17:00 down, plus the same two Wednesday runs. "No carry-all service is available during weekends. A special transport can be reserved, if required." **SOURCED** — NOIRLab Science, "Visiting Astronomers Travel Guide," updated Sept. 15, 2026 (noirlab.edu/science/observing-noirlab/observing-ctio/cerro-tololo/visiting-astronomers-travel-guide, read 2026-10-26).
- **Mountain lodging and buildings named in the guide:** "houses and hotels at Cerro Tololo and Pachon"; dormitory rooms with Ethernet connections; a **Library** with computer terminals; the entrance to the 4-m telescope (terminals there have priority for scheduled observers and telescope operators); the **Round Office Building** on Tololo, where visitors settle their bills. QR codes in the houses and hotels give "dining room operating hours," the Wi-Fi key, a "night lunch request," mountain safety rules, and a "suggestions and/or congratulations" form. **SOURCED** — same guide. *This upgrades Round 1's UNVERIFIED "night lunch" and dormitory snippet: the night lunch is a real, billed meal.*
- **What a mountain day costs a visitor (current posted prices, Chilean pesos):** Tololo motel room 35,500; breakfast 11,000; lunch 15,500; dinner 22,000; **night lunch 22,000**; daily car rental 6,000. Cerro Pachón: room 35,500; breakfast 10,000; lunch 14,000; dinner 20,000; night lunch 20,000. La Serena compound motel room 55,000. Visitors are told to budget "around $135 per night." U.S. thesis students get lodging and meals at Tololo and two nights in the La Serena motel paid. **SOURCED** — same guide.
- **Rules that shape the mountain:** "it is strictly forbidden the consumption of alcoholic beverages while you are observing or working at the Observatory"; no more than two observers per telescope; observers are encouraged to arrive a day early "so they can look over-the-shoulder of the previous observer" and acclimatize, while taking care "to disturb the scheduled observers as little as possible." Water on the compound and both mountains "is carefully treated," but bottled water is recommended. **SOURCED** — same guide.
- **The La Serena base as a social setting:** visitors are invited to "Meet our staff at **science tea in the La Serena office library, Fridays @11am**, except on days where we have a colloquium scheduled," and asked to "consider giving a talk — CTIO staff are always interested to hear what others are doing." The guide's taxi directions say the offices are at "Colina El Pino sin numero, esquina de calle Huanhualí con Cisternas" (the hill district of **Colina El Pino**). **Casa 9** (four bedrooms, kitchen, living and dining area, a large patio) is the visitor motel; "in the longer term, we might fit 4 or 5 extra rooms in the back of the garden." **SOURCED** — NOIRLab "Planning, Executing, Wrapping up your run at CTIO" (updated Jan. 29, 2025) and the travel guide.
- **Getting to La Serena:** daily flights from Santiago (~50 minutes; LATAM and SKY); or sleeper buses (Libac, Pullman, Romani, Tur Bus) from Santiago's San Borja terminal, about seven hours, ~23,000 pesos, "two drivers, a stewardess, and bathroom." In the summer (December–March) Santiago immigration queues can make visitors miss the first La Serena flight; holders of "O.I. cédulas" (international-organization ID cards) can use the diplomat line. **SOURCED** — travel guide. *That staff of an international organization hold diplomatic-line ID in Chile is stated only in passing here; the legal basis was not researched.*
- **The mountain before the road (history that still names places):** in 1962 the summit "could only be reached by a horseback ride of about 10 hours, with an overnight stay halfway up." Two seven-meter eucalyptus flagpoles were carried up by a mule "specially trained to carry this cumbersome cargo," by way of **Los Placeres**. Water is pumped from two springs about a kilometer below the summit. The summit was cut down 14 m in early 1964 to make a platform. The 4-m telescope's parts arrived at the port of Coquimbo in June 1973 in 86 boxes weighing 500 tons. **SOURCED** — NOIRLab blog, "60 Years of Discovery from Cerro Tololo," Nov. 23, 2022 (read 2026-10-26). The same post says CTIO is now "home to over 40 telescopes, including several tenant observatories," and that "Tololo" is thought to be a Diaguita name meaning "at the edge of the abyss." CTIO administrative manager **Leonor Opazo**: "If you come to Chile and say 'Tololo,' everyone knows you're talking about a telescope in the north." **SOURCED.**
- **University of La Serena (the neighbor on Cisternas):** its astronomy degree started in **2000** as a physics degree with an astronomy track and became a Licenciatura in Astronomy in 2013. In 2025: **41 new undergraduates, 12 master's students, 24 doctoral students**; the doctorate is accredited for six years (Dec. 18, 2024 to 2030). The doctoral coordinator says one of the department's "obvious advantages" is "the very direct connection with the observatories' offices, with whom we have agreements and collaborate very closely"; a doctoral student says students get to "know and learn from professionals who come from other parts of the world." **SOURCED** — G5noticias, April 12, 2025 (read 2026-10-26). ULS has four campuses in La Serena, one of them **Campus Andrés Bello** — **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).

### B. Subcultures

- **Visiting observers and thesis students:** a transient population moving La Serena → mountain → La Serena on the carry-all, sleeping in mountain dormitories by day, paying in pesos by credit card at the Round Office Building. The guide's rules (two per telescope, over-the-shoulder handover, no alcohol, PI responsible for students' behavior) describe the etiquette. **SOURCED** — travel guide.
- **Chilean astronomers:** Chile's astronomers have guaranteed access to observing time "by agreement, **10 percent**" at the international observatories (René Méndez, University of Chile). Until the 1990s the University of Chile was the only university training astronomers; the first Chilean doctorate in astronomy began in **1999**, then at the Catholic University the next year. In 2013 "nine universities" trained astronomers, with "more than 160 researchers" (70 professors, 50 postdocs, 70 graduate students; figures as printed). A remembered founding-era custom at the old National Observatory under director **Federico Rutllant**: he "lived in the biggest house of the small Villa beside the Observatory, and there held parties and board games," and his long travel letters "were read at the Monday meetings" (astronomer Héctor Álvarez). **SOURCED** — Radio Universidad de Chile, "Caídos del cielo," Vivian Lavín, Sept. 1, 2013 (read 2026-10-26). *Round 1's NOIRLab page said "preferential access"; this source gives the 10% figure. Whether 10% is still the current rule at every AURA telescope was not checked.*
- **The Elqui Valley's spiritual seekers (a long-documented subculture beside the observatories):** a 1990 Chilean newspaper review (dated Wednesday, Aug. 8, 1990; newspaper name not legible in the scan) of Juan Guillermo Prado's book *Los iluminados del valle de Elqui* says that while popular devotion flows to shrines like Andacollo, "higher-income groups have turned the Elqui Valley into a zone of spiritual pilgrimage," fashionable among artists, intellectuals and TV personalities, valued for its mild climate and "the purity of its air — guaranteed by the presence of several astronomical observatories." Groups named: "El Cristo de Elqui" (Domingo Zárate Vega, a 1930s preacher near Vicuña); the "Hermandad del Pacífico" (claimed the valley was the spiritual center of the world; 400 original members, no more than 80 by 1990); the Gran Fraternidad Universal (an unbuilt ashram, one foundation stone and a sign); "madre Cecilia" and her **"Monasterio de Elqui" in Cochiguaz**, which claimed a scientific mission tied to a geomagnetic force in the valley, "a force categorically denied by the astronomers of El Tololo"; "hermana Gladys" and the Misión Rama (UFOs); and smaller circles. **SOURCED** — Biblioteca Nacional Digital scan RC0042746 (bibliotecanacionaldigital.gob.cl/colecciones/BND/00/RC/RC0042746.pdf, read 2026-10-26). Travel writing today still describes **Cochiguaz** as a hippie and spiritual village and **Pisco Elqui** as the place where artisans sell handmade goods on the plaza — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of minube and vakantio blogs).
- **Grape growers and pisco cooperatives:** **Cooperativa Agrícola Pisquera Elqui (CAPEL)** began in **1938** after the Great Depression, when Elqui growers joined to face collapsing exports; it started with 15 growers (another source says 25) and now has "over 1,200 members and associates." Together with the other big cooperative, **Pisco Control**, it makes about 90% of Chilean pisco. One Capel brand is "**Artesanos del Cochiguaz**." **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperativa_Capel (read 2026-10-26). About 1,300 members, 85% small producers; head office and main plant in Vicuña — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of cooperativacapel.cl, whose history page rendered empty, and bichosdecampo.com).
- **Astrotourism guides:** "most guides speak Spanish and English and bookings are made directly with the observatories." The region holds Starlight certifications for Mamalluca, Collowara and Cruz del Sur parks and Starlight Guide certificates for regional guides. **SOURCED** (first sentence) — SERNATUR Coquimbo, *Astroturismo* booklet (July 2018 edition, turismoregiondecoquimbo.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Astroturismo2019.pdf, read 2026-10-26). Starlight Guides certification — **UNVERIFIED** (search summary only).
- **The bailes chinos (religious dance brotherhoods):** "brotherhoods of musicians who express their faith through music, dance and singing," from the Norte Chico to central Chile, five styles named after valleys, "organized mainly by men from rural areas"; jumping, leg-flexing dances to drums and flutes "of pre-Columbian origin"; a leader sings memorized or improvised rhyming couplets; two symmetric columns; flag bearers and guards "usually women"; learned "through direct observation, imitation and transmission in the family"; "a tool for social participation providing prestige." Inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List in **2014**. **SOURCED** — ich.unesco.org, "Baile Chino" (read 2026-10-26).

### C. Leisure industries

- **Tourist observatories as an industry:** the region was "the first in Chile to host international astronomical observatories" and claims "the largest tourist-astronomy offer on the planet" (its own claim). It started with **Cerro Mamalluca** in Vicuña in **December 1998**, at 1,100 m, run by the **Municipality of Vicuña** (a 16-inch Meade in a dome, 12-, 16- and 17-inch Dobsonians on terraces, the **Museo de Astronomía Nicolás Copérnico**, a souvenir room and a café). Tours have seasonal times: October–March at 20:30, 22:30, 00:30 and 02:30; April–September at 18:30, 20:30, 22:30 and 00:30. The booklet lists more than 10 tourist observatories: El Pangue, Alfa Aldea, Astroelqui (Vicuña), **Cancana** (Cochiguaz; "in an area free of electric wiring," family groups of 6–20; astrophotography sessions starting at 02:00), Cielo Sur (Alcohuaz), Elqui Domos, Chakana (Pisco Elqui), **Cerro Mayu** (La Serena; "the first tourist observatory set up under the wing of a school," cash only), Collowara (Andacollo; three cross-shaped terraces), Hacienda Los Andes (Río Hurtado), Valle del Sol (Monte Patria), the Casino de Ovalle observatory and Cruz del Sur (Combarbalá). Side services: horseback rides, "country dinners under the stars," night walks, vineyard bike tours and themed hotels for "sleeping looking at the stars." The region's sky has been protected from light pollution by a standard "since 1999"; **CADIAS** (Centro de Apoyo a la Didáctica de la Astronomía) is named as one of the oldest education initiatives. **SOURCED** — SERNATUR Coquimbo booklet (2018 edition).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life

- **Fiesta de La Pampilla (Coquimbo, next to La Serena):** held **18–20 September** each year for the Fiestas Patrias. Legend says news of the 18 Sept. 1810 junta reached Coquimbo two days late, so **20 September is a regional holiday** and the closing day. Weeks before, "hundreds of families settle in tents and vehicles in the hills." The site has **fondas and ramadas** (stalls and dance halls selling traditional food and drink), traditional games, a vast market zone where stall holders pay the municipality for a permit, and a main stage (formerly a pirate-galleon stage, "**El Galeón**"). History: horse races in the late 19th century; the land was bought in **1968 by Coquimbo's Lions Club**, whose president Meli Elizondo Díaz organized the first music festival as a fundraiser, and passed to the municipality in 1978; the 1963 song and folk-ensemble contests were run with the firefighters' association and Radio Riquelme. The festival was **not held in 1973**, under the state of siege after the 11 September coup. Outside September the ground hosts amateur football and motorsport (a road course built in 2006). Attendance ">100,000." **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampilla_Festival (read 2026-10-26). **2026 edition:** first canceled after July storm damage, then held as a three-day "solidarity" edition raising money for affected families; entry **2,000 pesos**, free for people over 60, children under 10 and people with disabilities; acts included La Sonora de Tommy Rey, Inti-Illimani, Los Viking's 5, and the comic **Payahop** (traditional *payas* mixed with hip hop); televised by Mega for the first time; special "Pampilla" bus and *colectivo* routes. It "usually gathers more than 200,000 people each day" (photo caption). **SOURCED** — meteored.cl, "Vuelve 'la fiesta más grande de Chile'…" (2026, read 2026-10-26). *Attendance figures differ by source (100,000+; 200,000 a day; a snippet says 300,000) and are recorded, not reconciled.*
- **Fiesta Grande de la Virgen de Andacollo** (the mining town of Andacollo, southeast of La Serena; site of the Collowara tourist observatory): **23–27 December** (2025 program). About **150,000** pilgrims expected, **more than 130 bailes chinos**. The Virgin of the Rosary of Andacollo, called "**Chinita**," is patron of the Archdiocese of La Serena and "protector of miners." The origin story: after La Serena was burned in 1549, Spaniards hid an image in the hills; a local Indigenous man, Collo, found it after hearing "Anda, Collo, y dile a tu pueblo que estoy aquí" — the story given for the town's name. 2025 schedule: on the 23rd the image is brought down and carried to the Basilica; on the 24th a welcome Mass for the dance groups and Christmas Eve Mass; on the 25th a children's gathering of the dance groups in the parish gym, a meeting of dance-group leaders at the **Casa Cacical**, and **the election of the First and Second Cacique**, then an all-night vigil "Esperando el 26"; on the 26th the Archbishop's Mass and a procession through town; on the 27th a farewell Mass at the shrine door. Masses run hourly from 6:00 on the 25th and 26th, and the Basilica stays open all night on the 25th. **SOURCED** — meteored.cl, "Fiesta Grande de la Virgen de Andacollo 2025" (citing *Diario El Día* and *El Andacollino*, read 2026-10-26).
- **Rubin Observatory's working community (the newest NOIRLab program in Chile):** in May 2025 Rubin's operations director spent three weeks in Chile with staff and the commissioning team, and joined Base and Summit Data Facility teams **in La Serena** for the "Rubin NET meeting" on the **Long Haul Network** that carries images "from the summit through South America, up to Florida." Staff updates run through a **Slack workspace** (#commissioning-updates) and a community forum; there is a **staff-awards** nomination page; the annual **Rubin Community Workshop** was held in Tucson, July 28–Aug. 1, 2025; a Spanish-language media kit was produced with Chile's CATA. **SOURCED** — NOIRLab announcement rubinann25007, "Rubin Observatory Digest for 13 May 2025" (read 2026-10-26). ### Kitt Peak, Tucson and the Tohono O'odham Nation

#### A. Other settings
- **Tucson as "Optics Valley":** Tucson's nicknames include "The Old Pueblo," "**Optics Valley**" and "America's biggest small town." "Roughly 150 Tucson companies are involved in the design and manufacture of optics and optoelectronics systems," a cluster the article ties largely to the University of Arizona's Steward Observatory mirror casting. The University of Arizona is the city's largest employer; Davis-Monthan Air Force Base and Raytheon also employ many. The name Tucson comes from the O'odham *Cuk Ṣon*, "the base [of the hill] is black," referring to the basalt hill now called Sentinel Peak, where a giant "A" for the university has been whitewashed by freshmen since about 1915 (painted green for St. Patrick's Day since 1993). Neighborhoods named: the Presidio District, **Barrio Viejo**, the Congress Street arts district, the **Fourth Avenue** shopping district (in territorial times Barrio Tiburón), the Sam Hughes neighborhood east of campus, and **University Boulevard / Main Gate Square**, "most commonly frequented by the large student population." **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona (read 2026-10-26).
- **Kitt Peak's outreach center, run by a Tohono O'odham liaison:** **Jacelle Ramon-Sauberan** (PhD, American Indian Studies) is KPNO's **Tohono O'odham Nation education development liaison**; since 2020 she has developed the **NOIRLab Windows on the Universe Center for Astronomy Outreach**, building in the *Himdag* ("the culture, way of life, and values that are uniquely held and displayed by the Tohono O'odham"), O'odham astronomy words in student activities (for example *Maṣad*, moon) and a newsletter for tribal members. "I want people to come here knowing that they are on the O'odham Nation. They're on our ancestral land. They're on a sacred mountain." On engagement: "Now they're like wow, we have somebody, like a personal contact." **SOURCED** — Arizona Luminaria, Carolina Cuellar, "On a sacred mountain, Tohono O'odham history and astronomical phenomena," Nov. 29, 2024 (read via WebFetch extraction, 2026-10-26; direct curl returned 403). An earlier "Cultural Ambassador" role at the observatory is mentioned in search snippets — **UNVERIFIED**.

#### B. Subcultures
- **Tohono O'odham dance music — *waila* ("chicken scratch"):** dance music developed by the Tohono O'odham from acoustic fiddle bands playing European and Mexican polkas, schottisches and mazurkas; "at its root an interpretation of norteño music." The name *waila* is from Spanish *bailar*; "chicken scratch" is said to describe the heel-kicking dance. Bands today: alto sax, bass, guitar, drums and accordion (accordion and sax added in the 1950s). The dance is a smooth "walking two step or the walking polka," and "always performed counterclockwise." Named bands: the Joaquín Brothers, Los Papagos Molinas (Virgil Molina), Southern Scratch. The annual **Waila Festival** in Tucson and the Rock-A-Bye Music Fest in Casa Grande; labels Canyon Records and Rock-A-Bye Records; a "Best Waila" Native American Music Award since 2011. **SOURCED** — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicken_scratch (read 2026-10-26).
- **The saguaro harvest:** Tohono O'odham families harvest saguaro fruit with long poles of saguaro ribs, eat it raw or cook it to syrup, grind the seeds to flour, and ferment syrup into "a ceremonial wine used to herald in the monsoon rains"; "the saguaro is … regarded with the same respect given to people." **SOURCED** — Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum, "Desert People and the Saguaro" teaching sheet (desertmuseum.org/center/edu/docs/1-2_SaguaroTales_people.pdf, undated, read 2026-10-26). The harvest in late June, the traditional calendar beginning with the July monsoon, and the public "**Ha:san Bak**" saguaro harvest celebration — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
- **Tucson's amateur astronomers:** the **Tucson Amateur Astronomy Association** holds monthly general meetings in the **Steward Observatory Lecture Hall (room N210, 933 N. Cherry Ave.)**, hybrid on Zoom and YouTube; an online "Astronomy Fundamentals" meeting on the second Thursday of each month; free public star parties (for example, Saguaro National Park East sun-viewing; a northeast-Tucson star party that "fill[s] up fast," registration required). **SOURCED** — tucsonastronomy.org (read 2026-10-26). *Upgrades Round 1's snippet-only TAAA line for its meetings; the Chiricahua site was not re-checked.*

#### C. Leisure industries

## Pick 4 · Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) [SECONDARY] — "though Kunlun is not a 'university' setting"

### A. Other settings
- **The "East Side" dormitories (MIT's oldest undergraduate halls):** an East Campus resident's essay, published by *The Tech* (May 30, 2024; originally an email to undergraduates), describes the East Side as a cluster that "has notably attracted low-income students due to low room and boarding costs and artistic folk for the historic muraling policies," and as a center of queer student life since Senior House opened in **1916**. Its account: **Bexley Hall** (pseudo-anarchist self-government; an "anti-rush" meant to scare off freshmen who were not committed; demolished **2013** after the building separated from its foundation; a park replaced it); **Senior House** (official motto "A home for Wayward Beavers"; a "biker bar" in the basement; closed **2017**); **East Campus** (hall-by-hall cultures and rivalries; residents handed power tools at the build site in their first week; a 2020–21 negotiation over "clothing optionality" ending in a "toptionality" policy; murals, cats and customization under review in a renovation); **Random Hall** (murals, "partially covered"). **SOURCED as one resident's account** — *The Tech*, "MIT v. Eastside," May 30, 2024 (read 2026-10-26). *This is an opinion piece from inside one side of a dispute; its claims about administrators' motives are the author's and an alumnus's, not established fact.*
- **Senior House's closure as reported by a news outlet:** the house logo was a skull in American-flag colors borrowed from a Hunter S. Thompson cover, with the slogan "**Sport Death**" (a resident: "our take on carpe diem"); walls painted with "a museum's worth of murals" by residents and artists such as Shantell Martin; house rules allowed cats and indoor smoking. In summer 2016 it was placed on probation after data showed its undergraduates took longest to graduate ("only about 60 percent" in four years). After 2017 commencement Chancellor Cynthia Barnhart announced it would be emptied: "This was about a yearlong effort to eradicate parts of the culture that were very dangerous … And it didn't work." Residents said its culture of mutual care drew "some of the campuses' largest LGBT and minority populations (as a proportion)" and that "We can tell when somebody's not doing OK"; students and alumni occupied campus buildings to "keep Senior House weird." The space was to become a pilot dorm on career development and wellbeing, with cats and smoking banned. **SOURCED** — WBUR, Max Larkin, June 22, 2017 (read 2026-10-26).
- **MIT Lincoln Laboratory (Lexington) as a workplace setting:** the lab sits on **Hanscom Air Force Base** (mostly in Bedford, extending into Lincoln, Concord and Lexington — **UNVERIFIED**, snippet of Wikipedia Hanscom). The lab runs its own **Fitness Center** (Mon–Thu 6 a.m.–8 p.m., Fri 6 a.m.–7 p.m.; open to employees, spouses, dependents aged 18–24, subcontractors and retirees; key-fob entry; personal training and group classes; showers and towel service). **SOURCED** — llevents.ll.mit.edu/fitnesscenter (read 2026-10-26). Lab employees playing in base intramural softball and an MIT **Quarter Century Club** (25+ years' service) summer picnic — **UNVERIFIED** (snippets of 2001 MIT News).
- **Haystack Observatory (Westford) as a place:** a **1,300-acre** plot about 40 miles from Boston, "on an isolated hill" off Route 40, with the 37 m Haystack radio telescope under its white radome, two ionospheric antennas and the Westford 18 m telescope; about **50** research grants managed. Its first open house since COVID (May 23, 2024) had **6,500** people sign up for **150** places; director Phil Erickson spoke of "pent-up demand." A 71-year-old visitor from Billerica: "My head is spinning with all this knowledge." **SOURCED** — *Boston Globe*, Ava Berger, June 12, 2024 (read via WebFetch extraction). ### B. Subcultures
- **Graduate student workers (MIT GSU-UE):** eligible graduate students voted for a union on **April 4–5, 2022**; the first contract was ratified by union vote on **Sept. 23, 2023**. It brought a **20-hour workload** definition for bargaining-unit students and new portals to track appointments; MIT also raised most fellowship stipends to match. **SOURCED** — MIT Office of Graduate Education, "3 Questions: Implementing the MIT Graduate Student Union's collective bargaining agreement" (Ian Waitz interview, 2023; read 2026-10-26).
