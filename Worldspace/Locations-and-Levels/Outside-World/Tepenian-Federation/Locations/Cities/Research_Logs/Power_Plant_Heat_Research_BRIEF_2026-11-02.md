# Power-plant waste heat: the Pole heat dump, heat logistics, and agricultural/residential use (brief for four units, 2026-11-02)

**Why this exists.** The developer raised two ideas on 2026-11-02 evening: (1) the waste heat of the reactor or fusion plants that power the Amundsen Tower (about 324 GW electric net, continuous; at roughly one-third efficiency that is about 600 GW of heat, plus about 190 GW for the national grid) might be used rather than rejected, warming agricultural and residential areas; (2) the 10 to 20 km around each plant site could grow or raise food, first for people in orbit. The developer then asked for research on **the heat-dump question at the South Pole first**, then on **logistics for using plant heat to offset cold in agricultural and residential areas and to contribute to food production, in every Tepenian city where that makes reasonable sense, not only at the Pole.**

**Key setting facts (from the repo; do not re-research these):** Amundsen Station is at the geographic South Pole: about 2,835 m surface elevation, ice about 2,700 m thick (the Tower's foundation is bored about 2,700 m to bedrock), no sea within hundreds of kilometers. The setting is the year 2564 to 2812 of a fictional Antarctic nation; **real-world technology of the 2020s is the reference, with modest extrapolation.** Plant type (fission or fusion) is undecided, so give results **per gigawatt of heat (GWth)** and also for 1, 10, 100 and 600 GWth where that helps. A plant's reject heat is roughly 2 GW per GW of electricity (fission, about one-third efficient; state sources for any efficiency you use).

## Unit P1 · The heat dump at the Pole → `Heat_P1_Pole_Heat_Dump_Research_2026-11-02.md`
Read first: nothing from the repo is required.
1. **The ice and air at the Pole (SOURCED figures wanted):** annual mean and extreme air temperatures; surface inversion and wind; firn depth and density profile; ice thickness; ice temperature versus depth to the bed; thermal conductivity, specific heat and latent heat of ice and firn; ice flow speed and direction at the Pole; accumulation; basal condition (frozen or wet) under the Pole (SPICEcore, IceCube site surveys, radar).
2. **Precedents for putting heat or hot water into polar ice:** the South Pole station's Rodriguez well ("Rodwell") (power, melt rate, cavity growth, lifetime, how the cavity is managed); the IceCube hot-water drill (power, hole depth and diameter, melt rates, fuel); Camp Century and its PM-2A reactor (2 MWe, 1960s): where its heat went, what happened to the ice; hot-water drilling elsewhere; any thermal-pollution assessment of an Antarctic station.
3. **Published thermal models of heat sources in ice:** the 1970s studies of nuclear waste disposal in the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets (for example Philberth, Zeller and colleagues): temperature rise, melt radius and refreezing for a given power; any more recent modeling of buried heat sources in cold ice. Report the numbers per unit of power and per unit area.
4. **Derive (show the working):** steady heat flow that a plate of ice or firn (area A) can conduct away at the Pole's temperatures; how much ice a continuous 1, 10, 100 and 600 GWth would melt per year if dumped into ice; and how much surface area or air volume would be needed to reject that heat to air at the Pole's temperatures.
5. **Rejection to the air:** air-cooled condenser or dry-cooler performance at very low ambient temperature (design and operating limits, freezing risk, fan power); precedents in cold climates (Yakutsk, Fairbanks, Norilsk, Bilibino, Pevek, Arctic Canada); **ice fog** and plume effects from power plants and cities (Fairbanks and Yakutsk literature), including whether fog appears at what temperatures and how far it extends.
6. **Radiative rejection:** effective sky temperature over the polar plateau on clear, dry nights; published ideas or measurements of radiators at polar sites.
7. **Effect of a heat source on the local climate and on the ice:** heat-island effects of polar settlements, any measured effect at the Pole or at other Antarctic stations; surface-mass-balance effects of warm structures (snow drifting around buildings and heated cores).
NOT expected to be found: a study of reactor-scale heat dumped at the Pole itself. Report that plainly if so.

## Unit P2 · Cogeneration and long-distance heat transport → `Heat_P2_Cogeneration_and_Heat_Transport_Research_2026-11-02.md`
Read first: `Tepenia_Nuclear_Power_Logistics_Research_2026-11-02.md` §4.4 (district heat transport; what is already sourced).
1. **Nuclear district heating precedents in operation or demonstration:** Haiyang and Qinshan (China), Bilibino and Pevek (Russia), Beznau and Gösgen (Switzerland), Bohunice and Mochovce (Slovakia), Temelín (Czechia), Paks (Hungary), Loviisa (Finland), Ontario's Bruce Energy Centre and others. For each: thermal MW delivered, distance, supply and return temperatures, population or floor area served, years of operation, and what was lost in electricity.
2. **Long-distance hot-water transport:** the longest district-heat transmission lines in the world (the Prague–Mělník line and others), pipe diameters, supply temperatures, heat loss per km (percent), pumping power, cost per km, and the distance beyond which it stops making sense (published limits). Low-temperature and fifth-generation networks and heat pumps.
3. **The electricity penalty:** how much electric output is lost per unit of heat extracted (extraction steam, back-pressure) for light-water reactors and for combined-cycle plants; the "power-to-heat ratio" and the Z factor; or low-grade condenser heat with heat pumps. State supply temperatures required for district heating (high, standard, low-temperature networks) and what condenser temperatures nuclear plants reach.
4. **Polar and permafrost installation:** how district heating pipes are built and run in permafrost, Alaska, Siberia, Svalbard, Greenland, Nuuk and Antarctic stations (utilidors, insulated pipe, heat trace, freeze-up failure and outage experience, snow cover).
5. **Antarctic precedents for using plant and generator heat:** PM-3A at McMurdo, McMurdo and Amundsen–Scott waste-heat recovery, Concordia, Neumayer III, Princess Elisabeth, Halley VI and others; efficiency of heat recovery from engine or generator sets.
6. **Seasonal storage and demand matching:** published figures for seasonal thermal storage (borehole, pit, aquifer) and how heating demand varies across a year in a cold climate; what plants do with unusable heat in summer.
7. **Heat to water:** melting ice for water supply (energy per cubic meter of water from ice at −50 °C, −30 °C, −10 °C) and its use at Antarctic stations.

## Unit P3 · Food production and housing heat demand → `Heat_P3_Agriculture_Aquaculture_and_Housing_Heat_Research_2026-11-02.md`
Read first: `Relung_Panen_R1_Power_Research_2026-11-02.md` (power and heat in indoor farming; what is already sourced) and `Cities/Division_of_Industry/13_*.md` L440–L520 (indoor-farm power, the 10 / 40 / 256 kWh per kg cases).
1. **Heat budget of indoor farms:** published splits of vertical-farm and greenhouse energy between lighting, heating, cooling, dehumidification and pumping; how much heat the lamps themselves release into the room and when farms need extra heating or cooling; effect of very cold outside air on the building envelope load (published numbers for cold-climate greenhouses and vertical farms, Alaska, Iceland, Canada, Nordic and Siberian).
2. **Waste-heat greenhouse precedents:** Bruce Energy Centre (Ontario), the Netherlands (Westland CHP, OCAP), Iceland and Kamchatka geothermal greenhouses, Norwegian and Finnish industrial-heat greenhouses, Siberian examples: area, heat delivered per m², crops, yields, cost; any use of nuclear or fusion plant heat for greenhouses.
3. **Aquaculture with power-plant heat:** published cases (tilapia, shrimp, eels, sturgeon, salmon in recirculating systems at power stations in the US, China, Japan, Korea, Europe); heat needed per kg of fish; yields per m³; what limits it (water supply, biosecurity, regulators' stance on nuclear effluent).
4. **Livestock, poultry, insects and soil heating:** published heating loads of poultry and pig barns in cold climates; insect-farming heat needs; soil warming with pipes.
5. **Heating demand of housing and communities in extreme cold:** kWh per m² per year and per person for Yakutsk, Norilsk, Fairbanks, Nuuk, Longyearbyen, Iqaluit, Alert; Antarctic station figures per person; design temperatures. Heat pumps' performance at very low outdoor temperatures. Give per-person annual heat demand in kW average.
6. **Scale checks (DERIVED, show the working):** the thermal power needed to heat N square kilometers of greenhouse or stacked farm at the published loads; the heat that 1 GWth supports in people housed, in m² of greenhouse and in kg of fish a year; the share of a plant's reject heat that each use absorbs.
7. **Food for orbit:** published per-person food mass and calories per day (NASA, ESA, space-habitat studies) so that the repo's canon rate of 0.66 t per human per year can be compared; any published study of Earth-to-orbit food supply cost or mass.
