## 1 · #32 North Platte Canteen, Nebraska (1941–1946)

**Round 2 focus:** the canteen's own governance and rotation (Round 1's main NOT FOUND), the documents the volunteers left, the spaces it used, the groups around it (railroad workers, Japanese and Mexican Americans, Black residents and servicemen, farm women, teenagers), the canteen's afterlife as a re-openable institution (1949) and as present-day civic heritage, and present-day North Platte's civic and third-place life. The richest new source is the University of Nebraska–Lincoln's *Nebraska Stories of Humanity* digitized canteen documents (TEI transcriptions, 2026), which settle several Round 1 gaps.

### A. Other settings (the spaces that made up the canteen's daily life)
- **The depot lunchroom as a workplace** [K7, K14, R]: On 10 Jan 1944 UP president William Jeffers had the canteen redecorated, with new serving tables, a large dishwasher (donated by UP in 1944; "some believe" it was the first automatic dishwasher in Nebraska) and a battery of new coffee urns; magazine tables were replaced with racks. On 14 Jan 1944 he agreed to pay for two janitors; until then "the women had done their own janitor work." SOURCED: *Canteen: As It Happened* (North Platte Telegraph and Lincoln County Historical Museum, 2019) p. 72, citing *Telegraph* 10 Jan 1944 and *Daily Bulletin* 14 Jan 1944, https://online.flippingbook.com/view/109123939/72 (read 2026-11-21). UP depot dining-room and main-building plans of 1929 are held as digitized items (titles only seen). SOURCED (catalog): https://nestoriesofhumanity.unl.edu/the-north-platte-canteen
- **The cup loop** [K5, K6]: soldiers carried coffee mugs aboard; conductors returned boxes of dirty cups for washing (this is how the honor-system return recorded in Round 1 worked). SOURCED: Marie Bartlett, *Grit*, 17 Jun 2015, https://www.grit.com/community/travel/small-nebraska-town-serves-as-nations-largest-volunteer-effort-of-world-war-ii/
- **Night hours after V-J Day** [K7]: *Telegraph*, 15 Sep 1945: "officers of the day" opened at 5:30 a.m. and stayed until about midnight or 1 a.m. because many late trains carried troops returning from overseas who remembered the canteen from their outbound trip and "waited up" to visit again; secretary Jessie Hutchens: "We just can't let them down." *Daily Bulletin*, 14 Aug 1946: 10,000–12,000 service members a day over four days in one month; September 1945 visits exceeded the whole WWI canteen's total. SOURCED: *As It Happened* p. 109, https://online.flippingbook.com/view/109123939/109
- **The farms as the canteen's back kitchen** [K12, K5]: Signal Corps footage shot 4–14 Aug 1945 (four reels, silent, black-and-white) shows canteen workers offering fruit to African American soldiers at the station, farming scenes, women bringing food to the canteen, Gene Slattery auctioning his shirt, views of Elsie and North Platte, and V-J Day celebrations on Main Street. SOURCED: National Archives *Unwritten Record* blog, 12 Aug 2014 (updated 14 Jan 2022), https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/?p=3853 (record IDs 111-ADC-6284, -6285, -6286, -5123). A single farm couple could carry a whole day: on 1 Jul 1945 (*Telegraph*) Mr. and Mrs. E. G. Mattson, farmers near Holyoke, Colorado, supplied all food for the day; Mrs. Mattson baked 32 dozen cookies and 32 birthday cakes and gave 90 dozen eggs, "most of her own flock's supply." SOURCED: *As It Happened* p. 99, https://online.flippingbook.com/view/109123939/99
- **Weather as a cut-off** [K5]: 11 Feb 1944 (*Telegraph*): a storm and bad roads kept the Chappell group (Deuel County seat, nearly 100 miles west on the UP line), which was scheduled to work, from reaching North Platte. SOURCED: p. 72 (above).
- **The 1949 reopening (transit stranded)** [K1, K5, SM:competence shown under pressure]: in the blizzard of 2–5 Jan 1949 (about 15 in. of snow over three days, 75 mph winds, zero visibility; small towns and ranches cut off; the Army and Air Force dropped supplies), a troop train was stranded at the depot; railroad officials reopened the canteen room at the request of the Army Recruiting Office; the American Legion and its Auxiliary ran it; volunteer women served coffee and sandwiches; 20 merchants donated food and money; a jukebox was installed; about 300 stranded servicemen, recruits and veterans (some with wives) were housed by the Red Cross in hotels, on cots and in private homes; the recruiting office issued 421 meal tickets. SOURCED: *Telegraph-Bulletin*, 5 Jan 1949, reproduced in *As It Happened* p. 132, https://online.flippingbook.com/view/109123939/132 [R: cots, shared rest in a stranded-transit emergency]
- **WWI precedent's other settings** [K1, K9]: in the 1918 influenza epidemic (North Platte's first cases reported 4 Oct 1918) the Red Cross canteen distributed gauze masks (16 Oct 1918) and canteen workers brought soup to a temporary "detention hospital" opened about 31 Oct 1918 in the new fire station at Front and Vine streets. SOURCED: *As It Happened* p. 14, https://online.flippingbook.com/view/109123939/14

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **The governing board (town women)** [K1, K14]: minutes of a meeting "of the ladies working at the North Platte Canteen" (31 Jul 1942) record the election of officers who were to "compose the Board of directors to govern the work and business of the Canteen": Mrs. A. M. (Helen) Christ, chairman; Mrs. Chas. D. (Jessie) Hutchens, secretary; Mrs. I. J. (Edna) Neid, buyer; Mrs. Russell (Mayme) Wyman, kitchen chairman; Mrs. Vern (Opal) Smith, platform chairman. SOURCED: https://nestoriesofhumanity.unl.edu/item/soh.sto022.00013 . The same officers were re-elected "for the duration of the war," with Dave Fredrick elected treasurer separately (p. 72, SOURCED). The board's structure is thus a buyer, a kitchen head, a platform head and day officers. [K14]
- **How the outlying towns took turns (Round 1 NOT FOUND, now partly answered)** [K1, K4]: a contribution list for Wednesday 29 Nov (1944) is signed by Mrs. I. D. Brownfield, "November Canteen Chairman, Hershey, Nebraska," and lists: the Legion Auxiliary of Hershey (1 sheet cake); the Hershey community (80 pies); two women's clubs south of Hershey (24 pies, 24 roast chickens); plus 50 lb sandwich meat and 50 loaves of bread. SOURCED: https://nestoriesofhumanity.unl.edu/files/data_stories_humanity/source/tei/soh.sto022.00008.xml . This indicates each participating town named its own canteen chairman per month for its day. An undated Oshkosh list records one community's day: 283 doz cookies, 75 sheet cakes, 60 birthday cakes, 147½ doz cupcakes (two lines), 120 lb meat, 220 fried chickens, 338 doz eggs, 237 loaves bread, 94 cartons cigarettes, 509 packages of potato chips, 68 lb butter, 56 qt pickles, 12¼ gal cream, 8½ bu fresh tomatoes and more. SOURCED: soh.sto022.00007.xml. A handwritten list "Towns that worked at the North Platte Canteen" (undated) numbers about 116 Nebraska towns and 6 Colorado towns (as spelled in the source: Amhurst, Haxton, Holyoke, Julesburg, Ovid, Sedgwick); many entries carry a prefix "C of C," "PM" or "Corn Club" whose meaning the document does not explain (a reasonable reading is the sponsoring body in each town, but this is NOT CONFIRMED). SOURCED: soh.sto022.00005.xml. The UNL page itself says 136 Nebraska communities (a third figure beside 125 and about 122). In North Platte itself, in-town crews were organized by weekday: on the last day (1 Apr 1946) "regular Monday workers" were in charge together with Lutheran church women from North Platte and Gothenburg (UNVERIFIED, search snippet; source page not identified).
- **Farm women and girls** [P9]: oral accounts: Lorene (Runner) Huebner, a teenager from a Hershey farm, carried fried chicken in coffee cans with her mother; volunteers silently prayed the soldiers would come home; Bonnie Glo (Brown) Aubushon of Brady, a teen volunteer, collected soldiers' addresses to write to them; volunteers danced with soldiers, who sang around the piano ("Stardust," "String of Pearls" named); Sharron Hollen (later a *Telegraph* reporter for almost 50 years) played in a basket as a baby while her mother volunteered. SOURCED: Matthew Spencer, *Nebraska Life* (first printed Sep/Oct 2012), https://www.nebraskalife.com/blog/post/north-platte-canteen . Waneita May Schomer (née Anderson) of Maxwell, 18 in 1941: "Everyone around here stepped up to help." SOURCED: Grit 2015.
- **Teenage platform volunteers, and the one rule they broke** [K6]: the museum states the canteen leadership "did not approve" of girls putting their names and addresses in popcorn balls, "but it did not stop it from happening." SOURCED: https://lincolncountymuseum.org/4-exporting-morale (fetched again 2026-11-21 for this line). Charlotte Endorf, author of *Bless Your Hearts: The North Platte Canteen*, gives public talks for Humanities Nebraska on "the platform girls of the Canteen" (SOURCED as a listing: https://humanitiesnebraska.org/speakers/charlotte-endorf/ seen in search; book content NOT READ).
- **Black residents and Black servicemen** [K9, P9]: Round 1 recorded the 1929 expulsion. The Tougaloo College sundown-towns database classes North Platte as a "Probable" former sundown town (method: violent expulsion; ordinance and signs unknown); census Black population 59 in 1920, 35 in 1930 (of 13,061), the remainder "overwhelmingly male." SOURCED: https://justice.tougaloo.edu/sundowntown/north-platte-ne . The canteen's own appeal letter (Hutchens and treasurer R. L. Self, written after 1942) states service is extended to all "regardless of rank, color, or creed," and anyone who says it is his birthday receives a cake. SOURCED: soh.sto022.00004.xml. Signal Corps footage (Aug 1945) shows volunteers offering fruit to African American soldiers (SOURCED, above). The first Black locomotive engineer hired at North Platte was William A. Riley Sr., in 1968, who reportedly met racism there. SOURCED: Golden Spike Tower, https://goldenspiketower.com/?p=382807
- **Japanese Americans** [K9, P9]: Nisei families had ties around North Platte; within hours of Pearl Harbor North Platte police stopped and questioned the Rev. Hiram Kano (Episcopal priest and farmer), who was then interned for nearly three years. SOURCED: Blake Ursch, *Flatwater Free Press*, 7 Oct 2022, https://flatwaterfreepress.org/saving-the-story-japanese-immigrants-long-ago-made-nebraska-home-a-granddaughter-is-ensuring-we-remember . A Brown Harano photography studio operated in North Platte; Harano brothers released from Topaz (Utah) went to work in North Platte (Roy released 17 Jun 1944) and their brother John served in the 442nd (UNVERIFIED, snippets of 442sd.org and Hastings Museum). A Japanese-owned Palace Café (Richi Ugai, in North Platte from 1904) "was never threatened during war" (UNVERIFIED, snippet of a History Nebraska page; that site refuses fetches). The Lincoln County museum holds the Brown–Harano photograph collection (finding aids by surname; requests Tuesdays; volunteers have contributed "1,000's of hours"). SOURCED: https://lincolncountymuseum.org/brown-harano-photographs1/
- **Mexican and Mexican American railroad and packing workers**: the railroad bracero program let railroads hire Mexican section hands from 1943 (about 80,000 in the first phase to 1947, many in the Great Plains). SOURCED: Ralph H. Vigil, "Bracero Program," *Encyclopedia of the Great Plains*, https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.ha.005.html . North Platte-specific bracero or section-crew records: NOT FOUND. Today Latinos are about 10% of North Platte (SOURCED, Flatwater 2024, below).
- **Railroad employees today** [K7, K10]: Bailey Yard work groups run an annual "Cheer-Giving" drive: Adopt-A-Family with North Platte Public Schools (a school counselor identifies families), hot chocolate and free coats at the Cody Park Christmas Village (22 Dec 2021), cards and gifts to two care homes (140 residents). SOURCED: Union Pacific, 7 Jan 2022, https://www.up.com/news/people/sharing-the-spirit-220107
- **Speech (K11):** English; Spanish in the Latino community; North Platte schools now serve students in over 20 languages (Flatwater 2024). Nothing further recorded.

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Money and the civic machine behind the canteen** [K8, K1]: the canteen was a major beneficiary of Lincoln County's four scrap-metal drives (just under $7,000); on 7 Sep 1945 salvage chairman Glen Ritner announced $5,591.62 donated; a "financial emergency" in the canteen fund was declared at a joint meeting with the county scrap-fund committee. SOURCED: p. 109. The appeal letter asked people outside North Platte for money or supplies because the canteen "must reach beyond the limits of this community" and worked without USO support; "Everything dispensed from this good will builder is FREE." SOURCED: soh.sto022.00004.xml.
- **Rationing administered by local discretion** [K6, K1]: the North Platte OPA price-control board supervisor, J. C. Schlater (23 Jul 1946), wrote that there was no OPA regulation allowing sugar, meat or processed-food allotments to a canteen, but his office had obtained them anyway with verbal regional approval; he forwarded the Chamber of Commerce's request for extra sugar so local bottlers could make 1,000 cases of soft drinks for a "Canteen Celebration" on 14 Aug 1946. SOURCED: soh.sto022.00010.xml. Whether the celebration was held: NOT FOUND.
- **Closing the books** [K8]: the board's thank-you letter "To the people in your community" (25 Apr 1946) reported an audited balance of $3,033.00, which the board voted to spend on servicemen in veterans' hospitals; it asked that the letter be printed in each local paper. SOURCED: soh.sto022.00009.xml.
- **Recognition** [K9]: a 7 Dec 1943 letter from the Seventh Service Command (Omaha) says the canteen had been awarded a "Citation for Meritorious Wartime Service," to be presented by the commander of Fort Francis E. Warren, Wyoming (19 or 24 Dec proposed). SOURCED: soh.sto022.00011.xml. A history-book excerpt states there was never "official U.S. government recognition" (UNVERIFIED snippet); the two are recorded as they stand. In 2026 a Congressional Gold Medal bill had passed the Senate (more than 70 co-sponsors) and needed more than 200 House co-sponsors; the museum (curator-director Jim Griffin) marked its 50th anniversary around 4 Jul 2026 and urged residents to lobby their representative. SOURCED: Beatriz Reyna, KNOP/WOWT, 30 Jun 2026, https://www.wowt.com/2026/06/30/lincoln-county-historical-museum-marks-50th-anniversary-raises-awareness-congressional-honor-north-platte-canteen-volunteers/
- **Re-enactments and anniversaries** [K2, K3]: a canteen "reopening" re-enactment in May 1973 for the city's incorporation centennial (led by Doris Dotson, who began volunteering at 14; a 1940s-style troop train with several hundred servicemen; the WWII Salesmen's Club served cake and coffee; Mick McNeel's orchestra; a dozen birthday cakes) (SOURCED: p. 137, *Telegraph* 12 and 14 May 1973); a 40th-anniversary reunion with about 400 visitors (1986, UNVERIFIED snippet); the 2021 North Platte Canteen Festival for the 80th anniversary, with a vignette-style musical performance of volunteers' stories (Visitors Bureau; Nebraska Public Media, 17 Dec 2021, SOURCED: https://nebraskapublicmedia.org/es/news/news-articles/north-platte-canteen-festival-celebrates-80-years-since-canteens-open/); a 3rd annual festival in 2023 and a show "Sentimental Journey: North Platte Canteen" at the museum's Heritage Festival (2025) (UNVERIFIED, performer's site snippets). A homecoming parade for Gen. Miltonberger in June 1945, "the largest parade ever held" in the city, included canteen representatives; leftover barbecue beef went into canteen sandwiches (SOURCED: p. 99).
- **Present-day friction** [K9]: a rancher-owned meatpacker (Sustainable Beef; 556,000 sq ft; about 1,500 cattle a day; 800 jobs expected; single daytime shift) brought plans for about 2,000 housing units (the city had built 20–30 a year) and public fears about newcomers' language, religion and status voiced at council meetings 2021. SOURCED: Flatwater 2024 (above).
- **Everyday behavior rules (K6):** new findings: leadership's disapproved-but-tolerated address slips; uniform rule for WWI canteen officers ("compulsory for all Canteen Officers to wear the regulation Canteen uniform," National Red Cross HQ, *Telegraph* 4 Nov 1918; about $300 per officer; the station faced closing if uniforms could not be funded) (SOURCED: p. 14); service to all regardless of rank, color or creed (1940s appeal letter); OPA allotments granted by local discretion without a regulation. Rules on volunteer dating or chaperones: still NOT FOUND.

## 2 · #45 Gander, Newfoundland

**Round 2 focus:** life on the wartime base and in the old townsite (Round 1's blocked academic sources), the move to the new town, the town's clubs, churches and halls, its newcomers (wartime workers, Cold War asylum seekers, refugees of 1992, today's immigrants and students), the Mi'kmaq and the river country, the leisure year (Festival of Flight in full, SnoBreak, the *Come From Away* season, hockey, the river), and how the town keeps its two great events in memory. Round 1's Scanlon-based account of 9/11 is not repeated.

### A. Other settings
- **The wartime base as a closed place** [K6, K4]: residents "on the Gander" lived under base rules: visiting relatives needed a pass from the guardhouse before stepping off the train, tickets to Gander could not be bought freely, guards sent back anyone who left the train without clearance, and cameras were forbidden and confiscated (many were never returned because the storage building burned). Few civilian photographs exist; most are by RCAF photographers. SOURCED: Eileen Elms (aged 94) with Diane Vey-Morawski, "Life during the war in Gander," *Downhome* (undated), https://downhomelife.com/submission/life-during-the-war-in-gander/
- **The base school** [K4, P9]: one room, Grades 1–8, 14 pupils, one male teacher (male teachers lived in barracks during the war; a teachers' hostel for incoming women teachers was built after it); pupils were Newfoundlanders, Canadians and French-speaking children from Quebec who arrived without English; older siblings were sent away to Harbor Grace, Nova Scotia or St. John's for higher grades. Children used the RCAF drill hall and an Olympic-size pool when the military was not using them, and swam in Gander Lake from an RAF-built wharf. SOURCED: Downhome (above).
- **Theaters on each national side** [K4, K10, K13]: in wartime there were three cinemas, one on the Army side, one on the USAAF (American) side and one on the RCAF side near the Banting Memorial Hospital; a 1943 RCAF magazine printed the RCAF theater's film list (9 Nov–9 Dec 1943). After the war W. J. Lahey bought the Army and RCAF theaters (renamed the Star and the Globe; the Globe had about 200 seats and a gallery); the Globe was demolished when the new town was built and its equipment went into the Crescent Theater. Moviegoing on Sundays in the late 1940s–early 1950s was "dressy." SOURCED: https://www.bobsganderhistory.com/th.html (local history site, undated). The Crescent was the first cinema downtown, 1957–1972 (UNVERIFIED, cinematreasures snippet).
- **Wartime civilian labor** [K7, K9]: about 1,500 Newfoundlanders worked at Gander during the war as technicians, carpenters, electricians, meteorologists, mechanics, equipment operators and laborers (UNVERIFIED, snippet of a heritage page that fails to load). In 1941 a Gander truck driver, Malcolm Moss, wrote to the Commission of Government that Canadian drivers for Belmont Construction earned 15 cents an hour more than Newfoundlanders, who got 45 cents; board cost $1 a day; the government replied it had fixed no rate except for its own drivers. The US Army Corps of Engineers used the wages Atlas Construction paid at Gander to justify its own schedule. SOURCED: Steven High, "Working for Uncle Sam: The 'Comings' and 'Goings' of Newfoundland Base Construction Labor, 1940–1945," *Acadiensis* 32(2), 2003, https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/Acadiensis/article/download/10696/11391?inline=1
- **The move to the new town** [K4, K9]: after Confederation (1949) the federal Department of Transport inherited the airport settlement, whose residents lived there "at the pleasure of" the department; provincial and federal governments jointly built a new residential area a few kilometers from the airport, with CMHC-assisted housing, schools, churches, a hospital and shops; the old town was dismantled after residents moved. SOURCED (as a local blog's account, no sources cited): https://airportcoffeeshop.blogspot.com/2014/02/the-resettling-of-gander-airport.html (3 Feb 2014). CMHC agreed to draft the plan in 1951; by the end of 1957 the new town had 685 houses, 22 businesses including four department stores, two schools, a convent and five churches under construction (UNVERIFIED, snippets of a blog and a town guide). After the war an American-built log cabin at Deadman's Pond became a gathering place for weddings and parties (SOURCED: Downhome).
- **The town today: housing** [K9]: a September 2024 Housing Needs Assessment found most stock is single detached homes and projected over 700 new homes needed in ten years (UNVERIFIED, VOCM snippet); Gander received $4.35 million from the federal Housing Accelerator Fund (UNVERIFIED, snippet). A provincial report using Gander as a case study found about a third of renter households in the studied communities in unaffordable housing (up to 44%), a lack of options for seniors in family homes, and a nearly tenfold increase in shelter use outside the Avalon (2019–2023). SOURCED: Town of Gander summary of the Municipalities NL and Choices for Youth report, https://www.gander.ca/business-and-development/build-up-gander/pull-together-addressing-housing-insecurity
- **Clinics and hospital** [K5]: the James Paton Memorial Regional Health Center recruits emergency physicians repeatedly (postings in Sept 2024 and July 2026 cite rising ER demand and virtual support); CBC reported losses of specialists and an overburdened ER (UNVERIFIED: job-board and CBC snippets only).
- **Search and rescue base** [K14, SM:competence shown under pressure]: Round 1's open question is now confirmed: 103 Search and Rescue Squadron is based at 9 Wing Gander with three CH-149 Cormorant helicopters, providing 24-hour, year-round SAR to the Halifax Rescue Coordination Center over the lower Arctic, the Maritimes, Newfoundland and Labrador and offshore waters; most missions are marine. ## 3 · #46 Shannon and Foynes, Ireland

**Round 2 focus:** the new town's social engineering (housing, a community officer, clubs) and who came to live in it (Northern Irish families, Chilean refugees, Soviet airline crews, today's Polish and other newcomers); the long fight for a community venue; the leisure trade grown from transit (the Bunratty banquet as a workplace) and what locals do instead (GAA, festivals, sailing); Foynes as a working port village and its yacht club; the troop-transit economy and the protest against it; and Round 1's open item on the first printed evidence of Irish coffee.

## 4 · #117 Lisbon, 1940–1942

**Round 2 focus:** the settings beyond the Rossio cafés (the fixed-residence towns, Estoril's "forced tourism," the prison, the ships themselves), the groups (refugee women, children, writers, those who stayed, Lisbon's own young people, the locals of the fixed-residence and border towns), the city's leisure and the regime's moral rules, and Round 1's NOT FOUNDs (rationing for residents; Lisbon's 1940 population; Clipper fares). Reported neutrally; this is a trait of transit under pressure, not war damage.

### A. Other settings
- **Fixed-residence towns as a system** [K4, K6]: localities including Buçaco, Caldas da Rainha, Coimbra, Curia, Ericeira, Figueira da Foz and Luso had lost their tourists when the war began and filled again with foreigners (UNVERIFIED, abstract of a 2024 Coimbra study; estudogeral repository not requested, see F). Refugees there lived in rented rooms, pensions or hotels, could move only within a set perimeter, could not leave without PVDE permission and could not work; residence authorizations lasted 30 days, and from December 1940 only very short extensions were given, with the PVDE alone issuing them (Pimentel, as summarized in SOURCED: Joana Pires Vieira da Cruz, *Cascais e Estoril como centro de acolhimento de refugiados entre 1931 e 1945*, master's dissertation, ISCTE, Sept 2024, PDF read locally from https://repositorio.iscte-iul.pt/bitstream/10071/32708/1/maste_joana_vieira_cruz.pdf ).
- **Caldas da Rainha** [K1, K8]: in October 1941 all but five of the Jewish refugees imprisoned for overstaying or ignoring expulsion orders were freed and sent to forced residence at Caldas; about 160 were there, maintained by the JDC through the Portuguese Jewish relief committee; refugees dependent on relief had fallen from about 1,700 a year earlier to 501; Jewish refugees remaining in Portugal were "appreciably less than a thousand" (against about 10,000 a year before); more than 50,000 refugees had passed through since the fall of France. SOURCED: JTA Daily Bulletin, 28 Oct 1941, https://www.jta.org/archive/portugal-releases-refugees-from-prison-sends-them-to-forced-residence . The town's layered history of hosting: Boers (1901–02), German detainees of the First World War, Spanish Civil War refugees, then WWII refugees; Carolina Henriques Pereira's book on the town's WWII refugees was presented at the town's cultural center on Saturday 13 January (2018). SOURCED: *Gazeta das Caldas* front page (PDF read locally from https://gazetadascaldas.pt/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/JAN.pdf ; the file's internal date is January 2018).
- **Figueira da Foz** [K10]: Irene Vaquinhas (Coimbra) proposes a "places of memory" itinerary of sites where refugees lived or that they frequented in the beach resort (1943–45), as cultural tourism. SOURCED (abstract): *Pasado y Memoria* 23 (2021), pp. 362–383, https://pasadoymemoria.ua.es/article/view/17918 ; full text NOT READ (the repository PDF returned an empty page).
- **Estoril–Cascais: "forced tourism"** [K4, K10]: most foreigners and refugees lodged there stayed little more than a week, a stay Irene Pimentel calls "forced tourism": horse races, motor races, theater and cinema, fashion shows, sports, the casino and the beaches (above all Tamariz, which filled with refugees from summer 1940); a quieter refuge was the library of the Museu-Biblioteca Condes de Castro Guimarães in Cascais (directed from late 1941 by the writer Branquinho da Fonseca), whose reader registers show many foreigners, including refugees, enrolling for its books in several languages. On 15 Jun 1941 the local paper *O Estoril* reported a day for refugee children from the *O Século* children's beach colony: lunch at the Hotel do Parque, swimming demonstrations at the pool, the spa, the golf course, the Hotel Palácio, a film at the casino, play on Tamariz beach, a snack. SOURCED: Cruz 2024 (citing Pimentel; Cristina Pacheco; *O Estoril* no. 260).
- **The prison as a waiting room** [K6, K9]: a young German refugee (Heinz/Henry Silberman), whose visa was declared invalid on arrival by ship on 12 Feb 1940, was held at the Aljube political prison "for investigation": a dormitory of 25–30 men on thin pallets, bread at 10–11 a.m., a stew at 5 p.m., "coffee" at 8 p.m., queues for the tap and toilets; prisoners made dominoes from postcards; requests to be escorted to the US consulate (2–8 Apr 1940) went unanswered for a time; he was expelled by sea on 8 May 1940. SOURCED (family biography site citing the PVDE dossier and his memoir): https://sites.google.com/site/henryksilberman/biography/escape/lisbon
- **Ships as the last setting** [K5, K9]: the *Navemar* (sailed 18 Aug 1941) carried 1,180 refugees; a stall for four oxen on deck supplied meat because there was no refrigeration; sanitary, cooking and sleeping facilities were inadequate; JDC's Joseph Schwartz called it a "floating concentration camp"; several expectant mothers were taken off and sent to the Lisbon Jewish hospital; the US consulate reissued 250 expired visas; police restricted passengers' movement. SOURCED: JTA, https://www.jta.org/archive/refugee-ship-navemar-sails-from-lisbon-en-route-to-cuba-and-new-york . On the *Serpa Pinto* (boarded 10 Sep 1941), 56 children were confined to the bow in a former cargo hold, with stops at Casablanca and Bermuda; fresh Portuguese pineapples were served at every meal. SOURCED: Susan Warsinger, USHMM "Echoes of Memory," 2013, https://www.ushmm.org/en/remember/holocaust-reflections-testimonies/echoes-of-memory/the-pineapple-voyage . The *Serpa Pinto* made ten wartime voyages Lisbon–Rio and ten Lisbon–Philadelphia (SOURCED: Montreal Holocaust Museum, https://refairesavie.museeholocauste.ca/eng/voyages-serpa-pinto ).
- **The border town** [K4]: the Vilar Formoso memorial (opened 26 Aug 2017; two former warehouses at the station; six sections: People like us; The Beginning of the Nightmare; The Journey; Vilar Formoso / Frontier of Peace; In Portuguese Lands; The Departure; historian Margarida de Magalhães Ramalho; owned by the Municipality of Almeida) includes testimonies on the "friendliness of the people of Vilar Formoso," mainly in the last two weeks of June 1940. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilar_Formoso_Fronteira_da_Paz . During the November 1940 detention of the Luxembourg train, residents reportedly brought bread, water and coffee to the carriages (UNVERIFIED: Renascença snippet; page refused 403).

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Writers in transit** [K13, P9]: over 70 German-language writers passed through Portugal from June 1940 to December 1941 (Döblin, Feuchtwanger, Leonhard Frank, Habe, Koestler, Annette Kolb, Werfel, Golo and Heinrich Mann among them); a few stayed (Albert Vigoleis Thelen, Hermann Lewy, Ilse Lieblich Losa, Sybilla Blei, Sarah Halpern). Hannah Arendt and Heinrich Blücher arrived in January 1941 and left on 10 May 1941 aboard the *Guiné* (Companhia Colonial de Navegação); passages paid by the Emergency Rescue Committee (for them) and HICEM (for her mother), "but around the places there is a true battle" (Arendt, April 1941); they read Walter Benjamin's *Theses on the Concept of History* aloud to others in cafés. SOURCED: Christina Heine Teixeira, "Wartesaal Lissabon 1941: Hannah Arendt und Heinrich Blücher," *HannahArendt.net* 1(2), Sept 2006 (PDF read locally from https://hannaharendt.net/index.php/han/article/download/99/163/227 ).
- **Refugee women and Lisbon's young** [K4, K6]: the café terraces around the Rossio were full from early morning to late night with French, Belgian and Dutch émigrés and Jewish refugees, with spies of both sides among them; at the Pastelaria Suíça the customers were mainly émigré women, smoking and drinking coffee "to push down their fear"; Portuguese customers felt pushed out of their own cafés, and Portuguese men resented the women's influence on Portuguese women (SOURCED: Heine Teixeira 2006). Young Portuguese women began going out "without stockings, gloves and hat," sitting in cafés, smoking in public and going to the cinema alone (SOURCED: Cruz 2024, citing Pimentel). The singer Amália Rodrigues remembered Lisbon in 1943 as "a continuous party... restaurants, night clubs, places with music, all full of Spanish women, of refugees." SOURCED: Gabriela Cruz, "Lisboa, terra dos sorrisos (1938–45)," *Revista Portuguesa de Musicologia* n.s. 7/2 (2020), pp. 149–194 (PDF read locally from https://rpm-ns.pt/index.php/rpm/article/download/416/766/1496 ).
- **Children** [K1]: USCOM (founded June 1940) sailings from Lisbon in 1941: about 100–130 children on the *Mouzinho* in June, 45 on the *Mouzinho* in September, 57 (or 56) on the *Serpa Pinto* in September (figures differ by source: UNVERIFIED snippets of USHMM records; *New York Times* headlines of 21–22 Jun 1941 cite 132 due and 119 arrived, SOURCED via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Committee_for_the_Care_of_European_Children ). The *O Século* beach colony at São Pedro do Estoril housed many refugee children (SOURCED: Cruz 2024).
- **Aid workers** [K1]: Robert and Elizabeth Dexter (Unitarian Service Committee, active in Portugal 1940–50) lodged at the Hotel Atlântico, Estoril, and helped refugees with left-wing backgrounds, including Spanish Republican veterans who escaped Francoist camps (SOURCED: Cruz 2024, citing Pacheco). The Lisbon Jewish community's aid body provided medical care, lodging and food and helped with visas and passages; Hans Sahl wrote that HICEM paid for "the passage, the hotel and the barber, the cake..., the doctor and the pharmacy, the pills against fear, sleeplessness" (SOURCED: Heine Teixeira 2006). The Hospital Israelita, run by Zionist youth organizations, stood next to the soup kitchen (SOURCED: USC Shoah Foundation IWalk, https://iwalk-dev.usc.edu/jewish-refugees-in-lisbon/hospital-israelita ; its tour stops: Shaaré Tikvá synagogue, soup kitchen, hospital, a refugee home).
- **Spanish Republicans** [K9]: a separate group, detained and in some cases handed to Franco's authorities; the Caxias fort was a main detention site (UNVERIFIED for 1940–42 specifics; Fábio Faria's ISCTE thesis, not read).
- **Speech (K11):** Portuguese; among refugees German and French dominated, with Polish and others (Round 1); posted beach rules were in Portuguese only, which Cruz notes explains some foreigners' breaches. Nothing further.

### C. Leisure industries
- **Estoril's resort machine**: the casino (opened 1931 per Cruz), hotels, beaches, races and shows (above); the regime's main opera house, the Teatro de São Carlos, stayed nearly inactive 1934–1946 and "closed itself to the refugees," while other entertainment venues multiplied (SOURCED: G. Cruz 2020).
- **Selling to the waiting** [K7, K12]: barred from work, some refugees, especially Jews, sold homemade goods; Berliner doughnuts became a success in the cafés of Lisbon and Estoril (SOURCED: Cruz 2024, citing Pimentel 2006 and Kaplan 2022).
- **The passage trade** [K5, K8]: in February 1941 one Portuguese line's freighter space was sold out for nine months and American Export Lines' four small ships could carry 1,000–1,200 passengers in all (*Aufbau*, quoted); "the black market in passages flourished"; in March 1941 the international police ordered refugees to leave within 30 days under threat of arrest. SOURCED: Heine Teixeira 2006. Clipper fares for 1940–41: still NOT FOUND (Pan Am's own history lists passenger types, including "not a few refugees," and mail loads rising to thousands of pounds per flight by mid-1941: SOURCED, https://www.panam.org/war-years/when-passengers-were-news ).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life (and the city's own conditions)
- **Rationing (Round 1 NOT FOUND, now answered)** [K5, K12]: shortages led to rationing through a quarterly coupon system for codfish, olive oil, soap, oil and sugar; prices of milk, codfish and other staples were administratively controlled; urban residents were again allowed to keep poultry and rabbits in backyards, balconies and courtyards; from summer 1941 cars were barred from circulating three days a week; from March 1942 electricity and gas were rationed nationwide; olive oil production fell 58% in 1942 and the state bought the whole national stock at a fixed price in the last quarter of 1942; strikes in Lisbon public transport and industry and in Almada-Barreiro, late 1942–early 1943; a cyclone on 15 Feb 1941 caused hundreds of deaths and injuries, especially in the Tagus valley. SOURCED: Portuguese Business Cycle Dating Committee (CEPR/FFMS), "Recessions of 1939–1945" (PDF read locally from https://www.ffms.pt/sites/default/files/2023-07/4.%20Recessions%20of%201939-1945.pdf ). Heine Teixeira (2006) adds that Lisbon was struck by storms several times in January–February 1941, and that in February the extradition of Breitscheid and Hilferding by Vichy raised panic among refugees and "the suicide rate among the exiles rose" (SOURCED).

## 5 · #75 Seafarers' missions ("Flying Angel" clubs)

**Round 2 focus:** the settings of a seafarer's time ashore (the ship's mess when the chaplain boards, the drop-in center inside the terminal, the hostel for crew changes, the retirement home, the hiring street at home), the groups (Filipino Catholic ratings, Muslim seafarers, migrant fishers, retired seafarers, volunteers, seafarers' own clubs), the calendar (Sea Sunday, Day of the Seafarer, Christmas), money (levies, appeals, fundraising burden), and Round 1's NOT FOUNDs (current Mission to Seafarers port count, the Norwegian Seamen's Church, the Sailors' Society, the 2020 crew-change figure). Main new source: *"Being There": How Catholic chaplains support seafarers in the UK* (Montemaggi, Bullivant, Glackin; Benedict XVI Center, St Mary's University, June 2018; fieldwork Oct 2016–Feb 2017 in 11 ports; interviews with all 16 AoS chaplains; PDF read locally from https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/2397/1/Montemaggi-Bullivant-Glackin-Being-There.pdf ; cited below as "Being There").

### A. Other settings
- **The ship as the setting the visitor enters** [K7, K4]: most ships visited had a small gym, a mess room and a smoking room; very small vessels had only small cabins and a mess; ships carry no more than three nationalities as a rule; crews shrank from 40–50 to 20–30; arrival in port is the busiest work time, and chaplains reported crews working through the night to sail in the morning ("they lie on the books"); one seafarer had been at sea 48 days before Immingham and 72 days before that; "every day is Monday. There is no free time" (a chaplain). Visitors are shown to the mess room to wait while word passes through the crew; chaplains wear the same hi-vis and hard hat as any other visitor. SOURCED: Being There.
- **Port geography decides shore leave** [K5, R]: Immingham (largest UK port) runs a bus from outer berths to the seafarers' center, with Grimsby about 30 minutes by taxi, so many seafarers see only the center; Southampton's ships berth at the city's doorstep but it has no center; at Milford Haven, Fawley and Bristol seafarers need taxis, so free chaplain transport often decides whether they go ashore; at Teignmouth and Plymouth the AoS offers a code-locked room in the port, open 24 hours, with free Wi-Fi and tea and coffee, 5–10 minutes' walk from the ships. SOURCED: Being There.
- **Drop-in centers inside the terminal (Singapore)** [K10, K1]: the port authority runs four drop-in centers with seafarers' missions and maritime unions, inside the terminals, for seafarers without time to clear immigration: e.g., Seacare (Pasir Panjang Terminal Building 1; Mon–Fri 2–10 p.m.; on-demand shuttle; seats booked by phone or WhatsApp), the International Seafarers' Drop-in Center (Pasir Panjang Building 5; daily 10 a.m.–10 p.m. including holidays; pick-up from ship on request) and the Mission to Seafarers center at Jurong Port (Mon–Fri 10–10, Sat 4–10, closed Sundays); services include lounge, free Wi-Fi, computers, telephones, transport, counselling and a library. SOURCED: Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore, https://www.mpa.gov.sg/port-marine-ops/visiting-seafarers . MtS Singapore (founded 1926 per its 2026 appeal, funded at first by a harbor pilot's widow for one chaplain; closed in WWII) reaches about 20,000 seafarers a year against more than 140,000 vessel passages, needs about S$950,000 a year, and launched a centenary appeal in July 2026 with sponsor tiers from S$5,000 to S$50,000. SOURCED: *Maritime Executive*, 21 Jul 2026, https://maritime-executive.com/article/the-mission-to-seafarers-singapore-marks-100-years-of-service-with-centenary-appeal-2
- **A non-Western center's year (Colombo)** [K7]: 2025: 1,937 ship visits, 3,968 seafarers at the center, 3,756 pick-ups and drop-offs; December 2025: 160 ship visits, seafarers from about 50 vessels, a fourth "Christmas Away from Home" (22–24 Dec), about 30 goodie baskets to berthed ships; ship visiting every day the center was open; weather and fewer vessels lowered the totals. SOURCED: MtS Colombo monthly report, https://www.missiontoseafarers.org/colombo/?p=13031
- **Hostels for crew changes, and their decline** [K5, K9, R]: in Hamburg the German Seamen's Mission keeps a seamen's hotel in Altona (Große Elbstraße, a listed red-brick building near the fish market: shared rooms for two or three, meals for seafarers only, table football and billiards, a Filipino receptionist, interpreters in demand; many Filipino guests stay only a few days) (SOURCED: Luna Harms, *taz*, 3 Feb 2024, https://taz.de/die-ortsbegehung/!5987762/ ). Bookings are mostly made by shipping companies and agencies for crew changes, often for a few hours' rest before boarding; ill or injured crew left behind by ships are cared for in mission homes; during COVID the Bremerhaven "Portside" hotel served as two quarantine stations; staff accompany seafarers to doctors and with visa matters; in Douala the mission collects seafarers from ships to a secure hotel. The Portside (Bremerhaven) closed 31 Dec 2025: overnight stays fell from 22,500 guests (1976) to 5,240 (2023) and 3,670 (2024); the "Welcome" club at the North Lock continues. The mission runs 16 domestic stations and 17 more in 15 countries. SOURCED: Jörg Nielsen (epd), evangelisch.de, 30 Dec 2025, https://www.evangelisch.de/inhalte/251067/30-12-2025/kein-hotel-fuer-seemannsleute-ende-einer-aera-aus-fuer-seemannshotel-portside
- **Where seafarers retire** [K4]: more than 20 retired seafarers from Ghana, Indonesia, the Philippines, Germany, Austria, Switzerland and Cape Verde live at the Hamburg-Krayenkamp seamen's home; a staff member: it is not easy to found and keep a family when at sea nine to eleven months a year. SOURCED: evangelisch.de 2025 (above).
- **The hiring street at home** [K7, P9]: T. M. Kalaw Street in Manila's Ermita district concentrates manning agencies, training and assessment centers; job seekers crowded the street, and on 11 Jul 2024 the Overseas Workers Welfare Administration opened a first Seafarers Welfare Hub nearby (air-conditioned "safe space," free legal and medical consultations, Wi-Fi, refreshments). SOURCED (opening paragraphs; rest paywalled): Raine Cruz, *Manila Times*, 24 Jul 2024, https://manilatimes.net/2024/07/24/business/maritime/owwa-launches-seafarers-hub/1959352 . Seasonal crowds of April–May with agency signboards (UNVERIFIED, Rappler snippet; page refused 403).

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Filipino ratings and their faith** [P9, K2]: Filipinos were an estimated 27.8% of all seafarers and 36.7% of ratings (Ellis and Sampson 2003 data); in the ports visited, ratings were mostly Filipinos on 9–11-month contracts, often extending them; UK officer qualifications cost about £12,000, borne by seafarers; Filipino Catholicism is devotional (Black Nazarene of Quiapo; Our Mother of Perpetual Help at Baclaran, whose shrine receives thousands of letters a week from overseas workers and their families and runs a migrants' center; the Santo Niño of Cebu); a small Santo Niño icon in a bunk is common; Filipino ratings speak their own language in the mess to make it feel like family; one priest said a recent seafarers' Mass was the first several had attended in nine months. SOURCED: Being There. Christmas Eve feasting with shipmates in the home tradition (UNVERIFIED beyond the opening lines: Manila Times, 27 Dec 2023, paywalled).
- **Muslim seafarers** [P9, K6]: an estimate of about 18% of the world's merchant seafarers (about 214,000 of 1.19 million, 2005 data; largest groups from Turkey, Indonesia, Egypt, Pakistan, Iran); they pray in groups aboard, sometimes turning a cabin into a prayer space, and ask captains daily for the direction of Mecca; in Barcelona (2005) 17 of 759 residents of the Stella Maris residence were Muslim, mostly Moroccan; the center offered Qurans and contacts with mosques, which seafarers "always declined, though gratefully." SOURCED: Ricardo Rodríguez-Martos, *People on the Move* 101 (Suppl.), Aug 2006, https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_councils/migrants/pom2006_101-suppl/rc_pc_migrants_pom101-suppl_apostl-rodriguez.html
- **Who calls the helpline now** [P9, K9]: SeafarerHelp (ISWAN; free, confidential, multilingual, 24 hours, 365 days) in 2025: 2,741 new cases (−11.4%), 4,992 seafarers and family members assisted, 86 nationalities (80 in 2024), callers 92.3% men and 7.6% women (women's use up 35% year on year); where nationality was known, Indian 33.3% and Filipino 29.2% were the top groups; seafarers from Africa up 94.4%. SOURCED: *SeafarerHelp 2025 Annual Review* (PDF read locally from https://www.iswan.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/SeafarerHelp-2025-Annual-Review.pdf ).
- **Seafarers' own club (a migrant community)** [K4, K10, K12]: Malay seamen in Liverpool lodged in each other's houses (the Seamen's Mission at Canning Place often sent newcomers to them); telegrams to the lodging house called men to "the pool" for ships, sometimes three or four a day; a Malay Club opened in the mid-1950s on St James Road (bunk beds for visiting seamen, a games room with a dartboard, a prayer room), then from 1963 in a house at 7 Jermyn Street bought by a group of Malay seamen, whose first president was also its cook; spicy Malay food at gatherings; food was available in Ramadan ("between you and God"); several men married Englishwomen; by 1979 five or six in ten Malay men in the city were unemployed as dock work fell; the club closed by 2008. SOURCED: *BiblioAsia* 14(2), Jul–Sep 2018, https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-14/issue-2/jul-sep-2018/malay-seafarer-in-lp/
- **Migrant fishers and slavery cases** [K9, SM:man versus society]: in 2012 at Teignmouth four Filipino fishermen, unpaid, underfed and with passports taken, jumped ship; the captain attacked them; the chaplain called police, took them to a hotel and then to Buckfast Abbey as a safe haven; with the ITF, six or seven were rescued in all; this led AoS to work more closely with the fishing industry. SOURCED: Being There. The Fishermen's Mission (founded 1881 by Ebenezer Mather; mission ships from 1900 that also fished but not on Sundays, with a surgeon and four berths) now serves over 70 UK ports with showers, washing machines, accommodation, food, snooker and internet, and a 24/7 emergency service. SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishermen%27s_Mission
- **Norwegians abroad (Round 1 NOT CONFIRMED, now answered)** [K10, K12]: Sjømannskirken was founded in Bergen in 1864 to create safe havens for Norwegian seafarers; today it is commissioned by the Norwegian Parliament to serve all Norwegians abroad: 28 churches, chaplains covering about 80 countries, about 600 vessel visits a year (including North Sea oil installations), about 1,100 hospital visits, about 400 weddings and 1,200 services a year, a 24/7 emergency phone; budget 230 million NOK, half state and half private. SOURCED: https://sjomannskirken.no/english . The San Francisco church (2454 Hyde Street, opened 15 Oct 1951) has a chapel, a lounge, kitchen, a gift shop of Norwegian foods, resident pastor and host couples, Saturday porridge, coffee and waffles. SOURCED: https://www.rhnsf.org/norwegian-seamens-church
- **The Sailors' Society (Round 1 NOT CONFIRMED, now answered)**: founded 18 Mar 1818 at the City of London Tavern by George Charles Smith (a former sailor and Baptist pastor who had held shipboard prayer meetings under the Bethel flag); bought Dover's old post office in 1895 as a Bethel and Seamen's Rest; funded a sea ambulance in 1943; mobile center *Speedy III* (1986); "Wellness at Sea" from January 2015 and a first crisis response center in South Africa (2015); moved to 24/7 virtual support in 2023. SOURCED: https://sailors-society.org/history . Chaplains in about 90–91 ports (UNVERIFIED, snippets).
- **Volunteers** [K14]: ship visitors are often retired, need private transport and a mentoring period, and must be able to give a full day because ships' times are unpredictable; managing them is "bureaucratic"; teams debrief and share stories; at Tilbury the AoS, Mission to Seafarers and German Seamen's Mission chaplains work as one team ("We might disagree on theology, but we work together"). SOURCED: Being There. In Australia, centers depend on volunteers many of whom are in their 70s and 80s (SOURCED, MarineLink 2026, below).
- **Speech (K11):** English is the working language of visits; chaplains report that speaking some Russian or Polish, or a few words of a crew's language, opens doors; Filipino crews use their own language among themselves. Nothing further recorded.

### C. Leisure industries (time ashore as a product)
- **What a few hours buy** [K7, R]: on arrival the first preoccupation is Wi-Fi to call home; chaplains sell two or three kinds of SIM card (data vs. calling), often in euros or dollars, a job many chaplains find uncomfortable; "I don't want to go on shore. I want to call and sleep" (a seafarer at Milford Haven); lifts to shopping centers fill instantly once word spreads; leaflets with maps are passed around crews. SOURCED: Being There.
- **Sport** [K3]: Singapore's port authority has run futsal tournaments for visiting crews against local teams (eight seafarer and six local teams, 220 participants in one edition) (UNVERIFIED: MPA pages now 404); the North Sea Cup, a seafarers' football tournament at Hirtshals, Denmark, run by Norway's seafarers' sports service (UNVERIFIED: page 404).
- **Pay and status inside the crew** [K8]: a 65-year-old engineer earned $3,000 a month, his officer son $5,500 (2016–17 fieldwork). SOURCED: Being There.

### D. Third places
- **Recognition by name** [K10]: Filipinos recognize the name "Stella Maris"; most other seafarers use a generic label for any mission rather than its name (chaplains therefore introduce themselves as Stella Maris). SOURCED: Being There. Centers named Stella Maris after the patroness are described by the Church as "beacons and safe havens."
- **Rooms with no host**: the AoS code-locked 24-hour rooms (A).
- **Hotel common rooms**: table football and billiards in Hamburg-Altona; guests avoid sitting alone in their rooms; "What is your name?" matters to men called by their job titles for months (*taz* 2024).
- **Churches abroad as clubs**: the Norwegian churches (reading rooms, waffles, porridge).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Sea Sunday** [K2, K8]: second Sunday of July; established in 1975 through the Sailors' Society, the Mission to Seafarers and Stella Maris (ICMA; the Mission's own pages speak of over 160 years of Sea Sunday services, a conflict recorded as it stands); in England and Wales a mandatory Catholic second collection (one of twelve a year) providing about 20% of Stella Maris UK's income, though only about 10% of some 2,000 churches have active parish contacts; ICMA links 27 Christian charities in 850 locations in 77 countries. SOURCED: Jason Zuidema, ICMA, 9 Apr 2026, https://icma.as/?p=2409
- **Day of the Seafarer, 25 June** [K2]: established by a resolution of the 2010 Diplomatic Conference in Manila that adopted the revised STCW Convention; a UN observance; 2025 theme "My Harassment-Free Ship." SOURCED: https://www.imo.org/en/about/events/pages/day-of-the-seafarer-2025.aspx
- **Christmas** [K2, K12]: parishes and Catholic schools collect thousands of gift boxes and woolly hats filled with sweets (Immingham chaplains gave over 1,200 boxes one season); a crew put the presents under their tree and said they had used fake packets until then: "You're the first to give presents to us"; a carol evening at the Immingham center drew a full room of parishioners and seafarers. SOURCED: Being There. In Barcelona (2024) Stella Maris delivered hand-sized Nativity scenes and nougat to seven ships on Christmas Eve; in Marseille a deacon's wife hosts seafarers for Christmas meals at home; the cruise-terminal welcome center sees about 10,000 seafarers a year. SOURCED: AP via *Hellenic Shipping News*, 28 Dec 2024, https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/?p=1069674 . Hamburg's "Christmas in the Box" boxes are dropped at Krayenkamp 5, Duckdalben and the Altona mission (UNVERIFIED, Nordkurier snippet).
- **Money for welfare** [K1, K8]: New Zealand's Lyttelton chaplain (2020): the sector depended on overseas charities, churches, port companies and council grants; volunteers mostly in their 70s; the board proposed a levy of NZ$50 per trading vessel and NZ$100 per cruise ship at first port of call ("I don't think we should have to go cap in hand to little old ladies"). SOURCED: Stu Oldham, *Otago Daily Times*, 14 Aug 2020, https://www.odt.co.nz/canterbury/christchurch/seafarers-welfare-grossly-underfunded-as-pandemic-takes-toll-gg5z9wcc . New Zealand's levy system took effect from July 2024 (UNVERIFIED, snippet). Australia (survey of 14 locations, 2026): over 500,000 seafarers visit Australian ports a year; most centers limit hours (some open only weekday afternoons); 5–50% of staff and volunteer time goes to fundraising; services include medical advocacy, Wi-Fi and SIM cards, transport, faith services and currency exchange; advocates call for a national levy-funded model. SOURCED: Human Rights at Sea via MarineLink, 3 May 2026, https://www.marinelink.com/news/australian-seafarer-welfare-centers-538727
- **The 2020 crew-change crisis (Round 1 NOT FOUND, now answered)** [K9]: about 400,000 seafarers were stranded on ships by coronavirus travel bans; the Neptune Declaration asked that seafarers be recognized as key workers with priority vaccine access, for health protocols, operator–charterer collaboration and air links between maritime hubs; over 700 signatories by March 2021. SOURCED: Royal Museums Greenwich, https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/maritime-history/what-neptune-declaration
- **Everyday behavior rules (K6):** chaplains' unwritten rules: never act on a complaint without the seafarer's consent (agents would tell the company, and the seafarer could be sent home without pay); answer requests for prostitutes or drugs with "This is the wrong address"; tell crews ashore not to photograph girls in cafés or talk to children, to avoid misunderstandings; no proselytizing (Being There, SOURCED). Ship side: ISPS gangway control, visitor lists, temporary passes, bag searches (UNVERIFIED, P&I bulletin snippet). Missions' own rules on alcohol vary (Round 1).

### B. Subcultures and groups
