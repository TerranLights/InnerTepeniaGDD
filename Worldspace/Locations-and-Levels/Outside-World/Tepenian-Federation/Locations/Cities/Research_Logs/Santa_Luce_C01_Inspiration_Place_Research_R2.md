### A. Other settings

**Catalan Bay village (the one settlement on the Rock's east side)** [K4, K14, P3, P4, P9]
- **Origins as a seasonal camp:** Genoese seasonal fishermen used the cove in the 1700s; a 1777 census listed 54 Genoese fishermen, only one married to a local woman. The first permanent dwellings date to about 1787; an 1804 visitor recorded one house and several caves in which fishermen lived. After 1811, Genoese refugee families settled and the village became mainly Genoese: about 200 people by 1816, about 300 by the late 1800s; about 30 soldiers stationed there in 1846. SOURCED, Ministry for Heritage, "Our Lady of Sorrows Church," https://www.ministryforheritage.gi/heritage-and-antiquities/our-lady-of-sorrows-church-5 (undated; © 2021 footer). [K7, K14]
- **Residency was a fishing permit:** Governor George Don limited residence to fishermen and a few people they employed; property owners needed permits, sometimes for only two months at a time; the permit system ended in 1928. SOURCED (Ministry for Heritage). Wikipedia adds that a fishing permit from the Governor was required and that only a limited number were issued. SOURCED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_Bay. [K6, K1]
- **Hazards that shaped the village:** a 1811 rockfall killed 18 Spanish civilians sheltering in the cove during the Peninsular War; rockfalls again in 1903 and 1917; the 1875 flood destroyed much of the village and its chapel (rebuilt with a boundary wall to redirect floodwater); in 1937 a damaged Spanish Republican destroyer was beached in the bay and shells hit the village, wounding four. SOURCED (Ministry for Heritage). [K9, P3]
- **The chapel as school and hub:** a chapel built about 1811–1815 beside an anchovy store; a school attached to it opened in 1843 (ages 5–12, 30 pupils) and the church served as a school until World War II; Italian prisoners of war working nearby repaired and repainted the chapel and held their own services in it. SOURCED (Ministry for Heritage). [K10, P4]
- **Language:** Genoese was spoken in the village into the 19th century and died out in the early 20th. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Catalan Bay). [K11]
- **Today:** Gibraltar's second-largest sandy beach, crowded in summer; red flags flown when jellyfish (mauve stinger) arrive in numbers; a hotel, several seafood restaurants and the church; residents are mainly descendants of the Genoese fishermen (the residents' local name goes to the slang file, not here). The coast road south to Sandy Bay was closed from 2002 to late 2010 after a tunnel was closed for safety. SOURCED (Wikipedia, Catalan Bay). [P3, K9]
- **A trading rule written for the village:** shops in Catalan Bay Village (and one beach hotel) may open 12:00–18:00 on Saturdays and Sundays from 15 May to 30 September, but only to sell bathing and fishing articles, photographic requisites, souvenirs, newspapers and postcards, and bread and confectionery. SOURCED, Shops (Days and Times of Opening) Regulations 1990, reg. 5, version dated 1.8.2025, https://www.gibraltarlaws.gov.gi/uploads/legislations/shop-hours/1990s162/1990s162(01-08-25).pdf (read as PDF text; every page of this version is stamped "This version is out of date," so a later version exists; the amendments listed since 1996 touch only the Schedule). [K6, K2]

**The frontier as a daily setting** [K5, K7, K4]
- Persons entering by the land frontier in 2024: 8,925,184, which includes non-Gibraltarian frontier workers; visitors by land excluding frontier workers estimated at 5,853,984. Land arrivals run from about 609,000 (December 2023) to 875,000 (October 2023) a month, so the frontier crowd barely changes with the seasons. SOURCED, HM Government of Gibraltar Statistics Office, *Tourist Survey Report 2024*, Tables 4.01–4.02, https://www.gibraltar.gov.gi/uploads/statistics/2024/Reports/Tourist Survey Report 2024.pdf (read as PDF text). ### B. Subcultures (faith and origin communities)

**The Jewish community** [K4, K10, K12, P6, P9]
- **Size and institutions:** about 750 (with 700 given in the same article); four synagogues, one mikveh, a kosher coffeehouse on Casemates Square, separate religious high schools for boys and girls, a Jewish primary school with a record 140 pupils (expanded with government funding); Jewish-owned businesses on Main Street. Social life centers on the Sabbath. Membership rose about 25% in three years, mainly newcomers from England and Spain; the community offered £10,000 soft loans over 15 years to attract them. A vice president said the infrastructure "could cope with a community of 2,000." SOURCED, European Jewish Congress, "Outsized Jewish infrastructure in tiny Gibraltar," 12 December 2011, https://eurojewcong.org/news/communities-news/gibraltar/outsized-jewish-infrastructure-in-tiny-gibraltar/. [K8]
- **Living apart, living together:** the same article reports the community has become more observant and, some say, more insular; some families enroll children only in community-run activities; non-Jewish residents voiced concern about isolation; there are no non-Orthodox synagogues and rulings follow an Orthodox court in London; an Israeli population exists but generally does not affiliate. SOURCED (EJC 2011). [K4, K6, K9]
- **Generational change:** until the 1990s the community was largely Sephardic and Moroccan in character; since then it has integrated with British Jewry (Manchester, later London), children attend Orthodox Ashkenazi schools in England, Ashkenazi teachers have been recruited, and this has caused tension over education. SOURCED, Emily Paster, "People of the Rock: The Jewish Community of Gibraltar, Then and Now," Aish.com (undated; image paths suggest 2022), https://aish.com/people-of-the-rock-the-jewish-community-of-gibraltar-then-and-now/. [K9, P9]
- **History:** Jewish traders from northern Morocco supplied the garrison after 1713; permanent settlement allowed in 1749 (about 600 Jews then); peak of 1,500–2,000 in the mid-19th century. SOURCED (Aish). Note: the EJC article dates the border closure to 1967, against 1969 in Round 1's sources; recorded, not resolved.
- **Food (K12):** adafina, a chickpea, beef and egg stew cooked overnight from Friday for the Sabbath lunch; couscous on Shavuot; japonesas, custard-filled fried doughnuts, year-round and especially at Hanukkah; fried fish spiced in a Spanish-Moroccan-British style; shepherd's pie made with Moroccan spices; a Jewish-owned kosher bakery patronized by the whole town (Amar's Bakery); younger members less committed to traditional cooking because of work and family demands. SOURCED (Aish). (Dish names are recorded as dish names, like calentita in Round 1.) [K12, K10]

**The Hindu (Sindhi) community** [K4, K7, K9, P9]
- **Origin and trade:** Sindhi merchants, mostly men, came after the Suez Canal opened in 1869 as part of trading networks headquartered in Hyderabad and Shikarpur (Sindh); they traded jute, cotton, carpets and Asian goods; wives and kin stayed in Sindh to run finances and, the paper reports, to avoid a religious taboo on Hindus crossing open water for long periods. Before the British Nationality Act 1981, merchants could not legally settle for a set number of years, so a patronage system sent new Sindhis to fill places of those whose work permits expired. After the 1947 Partition, families followed. Census 2001: 491 (about 1.8% Hindu); estimated over 500 in 2015. SOURCED, James Peck, "'Hindus on a Rock': A socio-geographical analysis of the Sindhi community of Gibraltar," *Geoverse*, Oxford Brookes University (undergraduate journal; about 2015), https://www.brookes.ac.uk/getmedia/f7bce74e-20e2-4723-9d27-8518240c9076/HindusOnARock-PeckJ.pdf (read as PDF text; based on three focus groups, 16 people). [K6, K7]
- **How they live (focus-group findings):** Hindu children attend the same schools as others; members join general clubs (chess and karate named); they attend other faiths' festivals through an inter-faith group (Hindus invited to a Jewish Hanukkah ceremony); older members run Sindhi language and culture classes, often using internet material, which the young do not always attend; strong family pressure to do well in school; university in the UK, often a route out; jobs in law and accounting as well as shopkeeping; older members resist marriage outside the community, younger ones are more open. Focus groups were held in the evenings at the Hindu Temple. SOURCED (Peck). [K4, K10, K14]
- **The temple:** opened 1 March 2000 at Engineer Lane, near Main Street. UNVERIFIED (search summary of Wikipedia). More than 350 attended Diwali prayers at the temple in one reported year. UNVERIFIED (snippet, Gibraltar Chronicle).

Hanukkah candles had been lit at No. 6 the previous winter. SOURCED, HM Government of Gibraltar press release 300/2022, 27 April 2022, https://www.gibraltar.gov.gi/press-releases/government-celebrates-ramadan-iftar-at-no6-convent-place-3002022-7907. ### K6 · Rules of everyday behavior (Round 1 NOT FOUND, now found)
- **Shop hours (Shops (Days and Times of Opening) Regulations 1990):** every shop closed before 06:00, after 20:00, and on Sundays, Good Friday and Christmas Day, unless an exception applies; exceptions for bakeries and confectioners, restaurants (open any hour except 04:00–06:00), theater shops, airport terminal shops on flight days, market stalls, petrol stations, newsagents and a duty pharmacy; registered family shops (staffed only by the occupier's household) may sell bread, fritters, fish and chips and dressed food at any hour except 04:00–06:00; hairdressers and sweet shops must close by 15:00 one weekday a week (Saturday unless the shop has notified the police it will use Wednesday); a separate schedule for shops, "including a Jewish registered shop," in one sporting club's entertainment center. **Enforcement:** the Commissioner of Police registers family shops, grants 12-month permits to open outside hours and may revoke them; appeals go to the Governor. SOURCED (Regulations, version 1.8.2025, marked out of date; see caveat above). [K6, K1]
- **Alcohol hours (Licensing Rules 1961, under the Licensing and Fees Act):** taverns and beer shops may sell for drinking on the premises from 06:00 to midnight, extended on Friday and Saturday nights to 01:00; off-sales 08:00–21:00 daily; wine and beer merchants 08:00–21:00 (supermarkets with a wine license up to 22:00 if authorized); the licensing authority may extend to 01:00 (02:00 at weekends) for rooms serving table meals, and to 04:00 for rooms used for dancing, only if neighbors' comfort will not be substantially disturbed; 30 minutes' drinking-up time; Christmas Day and New Year's Day hours set by the Commissioner of Police in writing; casino gaming rooms may serve whenever gaming takes place; airport bars may be authorized around flights by the Minister for Tourism; no one under 16 may work serving liquor. SOURCED, Licensing Rules (consolidated download), https://www.gibraltarlaws.gov.gi/legislations/licensing-rules-725/download (read as PDF text; file created July 2023; currency after that not checked). [K6]
- **Origin:** the shop rules date in their current form from 1990 under a 1922 parent law; the alcohol rules from 1961, amended through the 1970s–2020s. Who enforces day to day: police (shops), a licensing authority (bars). SOURCED as above.

## 2 · Juneau Icefield Research Program (Alaska and British Columbia)

**Round 2 focus:** Round 1's open items (the Code of Conduct, Camp 17's setting, camp heights), the block-by-block shape of a season, the social rules of a closed camp, the people around the program (Indigenous partners, Atlin, Juneau), and how contact with home works.

### A. Other settings (the season as a sequence of places)
- **The block schedule (current program page):** 5–6 days of orientation in Atlin, BC; bus to Skagway, ferry to Juneau, 2–3 days in Juneau; then a hike up Blackerby Ridge to **Camp 17** (8–10 miles, about 5,000 ft gain, 6–12 hours; supplies by a 5–10 minute helicopter flight) for 10–14 days of safety training; a 2-day ski-and-hike traverse to **Camp 10** (about two weeks; mass-balance pits on the Taku Glacier, flow surveys, isotope geochemistry, geophysics, nunatak ecology); an 18-mile ski traverse, sometimes roped, to **Camp 18** (about two weeks; peak ascents, crevasse exploration, small-team student projects, science communication); a traverse to **Camp 26**, supplied by snowmobile to the Llewellyn snowline and then a 4–8 mile pack-in; one stated rest day at Camp 26; then a 12-hour-plus hike, a night on the shore of Atlin Lake without tents, a boat ride of several hours, and a five-day wrap-up in Atlin. Crossing to Canada on foot requires a valid passport. SOURCED, https://www.juneauicefield.org/field-season/block-schedule. [K7, K5, P5] [SM: competence shown under pressure]
- **Camp settings resolved (Round 1 open thread):** Camp 17 sits on a ridge between the Ptarmigan and Lemon Creek glaciers at a col between Vesper and Cairn peaks (bedrock not stated: still NOT FOUND whether a nunatak); Camp 10 is "on the icefield" with vegetated nunataks nearby (the page does not place it on a nunatak, against Round 1's snippet claim); Camp 18 is on a bedrock promontory called the Cleaver, rising 2,000 ft (600 m) above the Gilkey Trench; Camp 26 is on the Corona Peak nunatak above the Llewellyn Glacier's blue ice. - **Season dates and numbers:** 2026: arrival 10 June, departure 11 August; 32 students; staff and students stay the full eight weeks, faculty rotate in roughly two-week blocks. SOURCED, https://www.juneauicefield.org/field-season. 2024: 26 May–12 August; 32 students, 13 staff, 24 teaching faculty and about two dozen other researchers. ### E. Cultural activities, rituals and rules
- **Founding ethos:** education built on Emerson's triangle of Nature, Books and Action (from *The American Scholar*, 1837), with a Latin motto meaning "to learn from Nature, in Nature." SOURCED, https://www.juneauicefield.org/history. [P6, K14]

### K6 · Rules of everyday behavior (Round 1 NOT FOUND, now found)
From the Code of Conduct (revision May 2026), SOURCED as above:

## 6 · Los Alamos, New Mexico

### A. Other settings

### B. Subcultures
- **Volunteer-built recreation (the ski club):** formed by Manhattan Project scientists and staff; formally existing from 13 November 1943 as the Los Alamos Sawyer's Hill Ski Tow Association, with a nonprofit organized to pay for the first rope tow (powered by a 1932 Chrysler engine; a longer diesel tow in 1945); volunteers cleared trails with saws and explosives; about 130 paid dues by November 1943; a 1944 rescue exposed the lack of trained help, so a National Ski Patrol chapter was formed in 1946, plus a two-man ski school; in March 1957 13 members scouted Pajarito Mountain and the club moved there, with the county and club jointly funding $15,000 of road work; an Army surplus building moved up as the base lodge; 365 members skied in 1957–58; $60,000 raised in 1962 by selling 10-year season passes; membership peaked at about 4,000, then fell in the snowless 1990s; after installing snowmaking for 2010 and after forest fires the club ran out of money and the facilities passed to a public-private partnership between the county and a private management company; the club still owns the land. SOURCED, Seth Masia, "Manhattan Project Skiers: Los Alamos Ski Club Celebrates 75 Years," International Skiing History Association (undated; about November 2018), https://www.skiinghistory.org/news/manhattan-project-skiers-los-alamos-ski-club-celebrates-75-years.
