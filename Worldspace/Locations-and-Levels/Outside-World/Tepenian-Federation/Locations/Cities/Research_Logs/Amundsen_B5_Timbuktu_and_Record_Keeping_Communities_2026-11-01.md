## 1. THE TIMBUKTU MANUSCRIPT LIBRARIES (MALI)

Method note: WebSearch returns an AI summary, so every figure below was taken from a page opened with WebFetch unless marked otherwise. Several publisher pages (Cambridge Core, Smithsonian, Pulitzer Center) returned HTTP 403; their content is not used except as a lead and is marked UNVERIFIED.

### 1.1 What the libraries are (SOURCED)
- Private collections gathered by individuals or families for teaching or private reading, not libraries "in the modern sense" open to the public. SOURCED: Diadié Haidara and Taore, "The private libraries of Timbuktu," ch. 18 of *The Meanings of Timbuktu* (HSRC Press) https://publication.codesria.org/index.php/pub/catalog/download/141/1237/4155?inline=1 : "probably better referred to as private collections gathered by individuals or families who use them for educational purposes or their own private reading."
- Private collections are mentioned from the second half of the 16th century (Ahmad Baba and Moroccan and Spanish authors); the 17th-century chronicles mention scholars buying manuscripts and rulers (askiyas) commissioning copies for Timbuktu scholars; "Many of those opening their libraries today are descendants of these scholars." (same chapter)
- First collection opened to the public in the 20th century: the Moroccan bibliophile Ahmad Boularaf (b. about 1864, lived first in Shinqit), library opened about 1907, employed transcribers. His collection was 2,076 manuscripts in 1945 and 680 by 2002, many donated to the Ahmed Baba Institute. Owners of other collections saw the decline and decided to open theirs. (same chapter)
- Since 1996, 21 private collections inside Timbuktu opened to the public (chapter's Table 1); only five "easily accessible": Mamma Haidara (opened 1996, about 9,000), Fondo Ka'ti (1999, 7,026), al-Wangari (2003, about 3,000), Imam Soyuti (2004, about 800), Jingere-Ber (2004, about 500); "just over 20 000 manuscripts." By quarter: Sankore 8 libraries, Badjinde 7, Sareikeyna/Sareyk 4, Jingarey-Ber 2; concentrated in the old districts. Each entry in the table is a family custodian plus a library name (e.g., Imam al-Aqib / Sankore Mosque; Imam Hasey-Mukhtar / al-Wangari; Abdel Kader Haidara / Mamma Haidara; Ismael Diadie Haidara / Fondo Ka'ti; Imam Soyuti-Sane / Jingere-Ber Mosque).
- Library locations include mosques: three major mosques (Sankore, Djinguereber, Sidi Yahia) hold collections (HMML https://hmml.org/stories/a-decade-in-mali/ ; HMML Mali page https://hmml.org/about/global-operations/mali/ names Imam Ben Essayouti Library, Al-Wangara Library at Sidi Yahya Mosque, Al-Cady Al-Aqib Library at Sankore Mosque).
- Libraries outside the city exist (Arawan, Bujbeha in the Sahara; Diré on the river) (chapter, Intro). HMML also reached Djenne (2,567 manuscripts from more than 200 repositories) (HMML decade story).
- COUNT CONFLICT (library numbers): 21 libraries opened (chapter, written about 2008-09); 32 family libraries (Jean-Michel Djian in *The New Yorker* blog, 29 Jan 2013, Lila Azam Zanganeh, text extracted from PDF at lazanganeh.com); 35 families entrusted collections to SAVAMA-DCI (Google Arts & Culture https://artsandculture.google.com/story/cwXxzf6uEb1aJw ); "more than 30 family libraries" (HMML decade story). The figures count different things (opened to public; all family libraries; SAVAMA members). Not reconciled in any source.

### 1.2 Total holdings (SOURCED, conflicting)
- SAVAMA-DCI's own count (Google Arts & Culture): families entrusted 377,491 manuscripts; 161,628 inventoried (about 45%); 17 libraries fully inventoried in Arabic and French.
- HMML: about 302,000 manuscripts digitized in Mali (decade story, July 2024); 308,000 manuscripts and 4.35 million image files (HMML Mali page, project dates 2013-2026).
- CSMC Hamburg: "Approximately 300,000 manuscripts, large and small, were relocated from Timbuktu to Bamako" https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/timbuktu/about.html
- Wikipedia (secondary): "total number unknown"; estimates up to 700,000 appear in a Wikipedia-derived page; the Mamma Haidara Wikipedia article gives both about 22,000 items and "350,000 to 700,000" moved in 2012, which cannot describe one collection (the article does not reconcile them).
- Ahmed Baba Institute (state): 19,454 manuscripts recorded in December 2005 (Mail & Guardian, 11 Aug 2006, https://mg.co.za/friday/2006-08-11-on-the-paper-trail/ ); about 28,000 moved out in 2012 (AP, 17 Nov 2025 via https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2622904 ); more than 27,000 saved (AP, 12 Aug 2025 via PBS).
- Earlier 2013 estimates: 60,000-100,000 at Ahmed Baba and about 200,000 total in the region (*The New Yorker* blog, Jan 2013). Djian: about 50,000 in the 32 family libraries. These pre-date the later counts and are inconsistent with them.
- Dates of the manuscripts: 12th/13th to early 20th century; Arabic mainly, some Ajami (Fula, Songhay, Tamasheq, Bambara, Soninke) (Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu_Manuscripts ; Fondo Ka'ti chapter says 12th to 19th century). Contents: Qur'an and exegesis, law, theology and mysticism, history and genealogy, grammar, logic, poetry, astronomy, medicine and pharmacopoeia, mathematics; legal opinions and deeds on slave sale and manumission, marriage and divorce, coinage, trade in books, salt, gold, cloth, grain, spices and cola nuts; merchants' and rulers' letters (Fondo Ka'ti chapter).

### 1.3 How they were kept (SOURCED)
- Family custodianship by oath: "tradition calls for a family member to 'swear publicly that he will protect the library for as long as he lives'" (Wikipedia Timbuktu Manuscripts); The New Republic (Yochi Dreazen, 25 Apr 2013, https://newrepublic.com/article/112898/timbuktu-librarians-duped-al-qaeda-save-books ) says each generation has a member who publicly vows to protect the library, and that Abdel Kader Haidara took the family vow at age 17; families honor the manuscripts each year on Maouloud with readings from prayer books for the Prophet's birthday.
- Succession example, Fondo Ka'ti (chapter): begun when Ali b. Zyad al-Kunti left Toledo in 1469 with manuscripts and settled in Goumbou; enriched with the manuscripts of Askiya Muhammad and Alfa Kati Mahmud (d. 1593); moved with the guardian through Tindirma (to 1612), Bina (Mahmud Kati II, d. 1648), Goumdam and others; dispersed among family branches in villages along the Niger; brought together again in Timbuktu in 1999; 7,028 manuscripts. A line of custody that physically moves with the custodian.
- Mamma Haidara: core collection began in the 16th century with Mohamed El Mawlud; his Haidara descendants "kept the manuscripts at home after Moroccan soldiers sacked Timbuktu in 1591"; Mamma Haidara (judge, scholar, teacher, d. 1981) expanded it; Abdel Kader inherited the personal library in 1981, ran manuscript acquisition for the Ahmed Baba Institute 1984-1991, then focused on his own; opened the library in 2000 per Wikipedia (the chapter's Table 2 says 1996; the search summary quotes Haidara as saying he opened a private modern library in 1993; three dates, unreconciled) with an Andrew W. Mellon Foundation grant (Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamma_Haidara_Commemorative_Library ).
- Storage: "usually stored in tin trunks or on low tables called koma," which "crush the manuscripts, prevent air from circulating between them, and promote the development of micro-organisms" (chapter). The libraries are in rooms "exposed to the same environmental factors as any other building in Timbuktu: dust, light, highly variable climatic conditions and space constraints." Mud-brick houses (New Yorker blog). Google Arts: "often stored in poor conditions, sometimes loose in trunks. Rain, rodents, termites, and other insects have damaged some."
- Climate (chapter): winter 5-12 C, summer 45-52 C, "about 40 degrees" seasonal difference; hot dry air makes manuscripts brittle; the short rainy season brings humidity, mold and bacteria. Lighting change from oil to gas to electric, and to neon, discolors paper and ink.
- Material/damage factors: 16th-17th century paper worse than 13th-15th century linen/cotton paper; metal-gall (iron) ink eats paper; paper acidic and brittle. Human factors: library closures, dispersion through inheritance, mishandling, piling in chests, theft for the black market, breaking up manuscripts into sections, owners throwing away damaged items (chapter).
- Disaster: October 2008 flood in one household destroyed 700 manuscripts (Wikipedia Timbuktu Manuscripts, citation not opened; UNVERIFIED beyond Wikipedia).
- Belief in baraka: according to some conservation specialists, "these libraries bring 'baraka' ... dismantling them attracts misfortune"; margins contain family secrets, accounts, diaries; descendants are reluctant to reveal possible Jewish ancestry or illegitimate descendants (New Yorker blog 2013, a journalist's account quoting unnamed specialists; treat as UNVERIFIED color).
- Secrecy as protection: locals reported Islamists were loath to enter private houses (same blog; UNVERIFIED).

### 1.4 How they were copied (SOURCED)
- HMML, "A Scribble of Scribes" https://hmml.org/stories/series-scribes-a-scribble-of-scribes/ : three types of copyist. (1) Professional scribes, commissioned and paid; colophons often name both copyist and client. (2) Unpaid copyists who copied for friends, teachers, relatives or themselves ("I copied this for myself and for whoever comes after me"). (3) Students who copied foundational texts, often giving the copy to a teacher for baraka and to show mastery.
- Fees (examples with shelfmarks given in the story): five riyal for a copy of al-Jazuli's *Dala'il al-khayrat* (SAV ABS 01191); 500 cowries for a 25-page text on Ramadan (SAV BMH 29872); 5,000 cowries for an illuminated copy of al-Jazuli's poem (SAV BMH 17185). Payment "probably" in riyal (gold or silver measures) or cowrie shells.
- Women: "a significant minority" of named copyists and commissioners, no count given; examples: Hawa Sylla (owner, SAV BMH 17264); Khadijah al-'Awfi (copied a poetry collection with red-ink decoration, SAV BMH 21046). Students practiced on a wooden board (lawh); paper cheaper in the 20th century.
- Scribal families: Muhammad Gordo (1600s) appears in the genealogies of dozens of copyists; the Alfa Banya family, imams of Djinguereber Mosque, copied many manuscripts now in the Imam Essayouti library; Ahmad Bul'Arraf (1864-1955) commissioned copies (one completed 31 Oct 1943 by 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Sidi 'Uthman); family names Traore, Konate, Cisse, Toure, Sumare recur in 20th-century provenance notes.
- Some copyists refused to sign; colophons include self-deprecation ("full of sin").
- Wikipedia: scribes translated imported works and copied texts such as the 28-volume dictionary *al-Muhkam*; the article says nothing on paper or ink. The HMML search summary (not opened) reported a 1574 colophon recording who supplied the blank paper and the payment: UNVERIFIED.
- Paper: Mail & Guardian (2006): from the 14th century Islamic countries became net importers of European paper; European makers added Islamically acceptable watermarks such as crescents (attributed to conservator Mary Minicka); Leo Africanus 1526: manuscripts brought from Barbary sold for more than any other merchandise. Fondo Ka'ti chapter: "Many of the manuscripts contain watermarks which identify the origin of the paper." *New Yorker* blog: texts were copied on "camel shoulder blades, sheepskins, tree bark, and even papers from Italy" (journalist's list; UNVERIFIED). Chapter: one 12th-century Qur'an on vellum.
- Ink and pens: CSMC research project IRP 11 https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/research/irps/irp11.html states reed pens (qalams), inks, ink pots, leather covers and satchels are "neglected" topics, and that earlier studies concentrated on paper provenance; project runs 2026-2028 and has no findings yet. NOT FOUND: any opened page giving an ink recipe for Timbuktu (the search summary mentioned locally made inks in various shades and a ruling frame called mastara; not opened: UNVERIFIED). Chapter: metal-based (iron-gall type) ink "mainly from the sixteenth century."
- Scripts: Andalusi, Maghribi, Saharaoui, Suki, Sudani, Sharqi (chapter, Fondo Ka'ti section); formats 6-7 cm to 22-29 cm; tooled leather covers.
- Trade chain: the Moumouni 2007 article (sav.sk) on the manuscript-making chain surfaced in a search and was not opened: NOT READ.

### 1.5 The 2012-2013 hiding and evacuation (SOURCED, with conflicts)
Sequence from The New Republic (Dreazen, 25 Apr 2013; Pulitzer Center funded; text read via WebFetch):
- April 2012 Timbuktu falls to rebels. Early May 2012: evacuation begins; teams pack before sunrise, move lockers at night.
- Containers: metal lockers "roughly the size of large suitcases," up to 300 manuscripts each; "By July 2012, 1,700 lockers were hidden in basements and hideaways across the city." About 700 still in Timbuktu around January 2013.
- Organizers: Abdel Kader Haidara (library owner, founder of SAVAMA-DCI) and Stephanie Diakite (Seattle-raised bookbinder and conservator, organized finances and logistics). Couriers: dozens of young people recruited by phone through the libraries; truck drivers, housewives who fed couriers, villagers who distracted soldiers. Funds sent through Bamako businessmen for wages, vehicle rental and bribes; Haidara and Diakite drained personal savings.
- 18 October 2012: first 35 lockers left Timbuktu by pushcart and donkey carriage (the article's date); buses and trucks to Bamako for the first leg; in mid-to-late January 2013, after the French offensive began, lockers went down the Niger on 47 pirogues to Djenne, then by taxi to Bamako. Near Lake Debo gunmen stopped 20 of the 47 boats and demanded a ransom; couriers pooled cash and Haidara sent the rest; a French helicopter threatened to sink a pirogue until it saw paper. Haidara estimated about 95% of about 300,000 reached Bamako.
- Other counts: HMML says "about 1,400 metal boxes" held manuscripts from more than 30 family libraries, moved by boat and truck (decade story). A search summary of a Hammer-based source gave 2,500 footlockers (UNVERIFIED, not opened). Prince Claus Fund contract 17 Oct 2012 for evacuation of 200 lockers (from a search summary; the PCF page itself not opened: UNVERIFIED). German Foreign Office/CSMC: about 300,000, relocation roughly three months before March 2013.
- Date conflict: New Republic gives evacuation start May 2012 with first lockers out 18 Oct 2012; CSMC says "about three months before March 2013." These describe different phases (hiding in the city from May; main transfer later) but no single page states this.
- State collection: Ahmed Baba Institute. Abba Alhadi (elderly employee) hid manuscripts in rice and millet bags (search summary of Smithsonian; page returned 403: UNVERIFIED). AP (17 Nov 2025): Abdoulaye Cisse, general secretary of the institute, helped move about 28,000 manuscripts at night by donkey cart, river boat and bus to Bamako, "about 1,200 km," taking a month; AP 12 Aug 2025 gives 706 km (439 miles) Timbuktu-Bamako (a straight-line vs road distance difference is plausible but not stated). New Republic says about 400 miles by road; CSMC blog says about 1,000 km. Three distance figures; none reconciled.
- Haidara's own collection: Mamma Haidara Wikipedia: metal chests, mule carts, small vehicles, temporary safe-houses; a boat was seized and held for ransom. *New Yorker* blog (Djian): more than 15,000 of Mamma Haidara's manuscripts moved two months before the Jan 2013 date.
- Fondo Ka'ti: kept hidden in Timbuktu at first; family also sent manuscripts by boat hidden among clothes (Hawa Toure, manager) (search summary; UNVERIFIED).
- Destroyed: more than 4,000 manuscripts (UN expert mission, via AP); 4,203 (Wikipedia, Ahmed Baba collection). Jeppie (UCT, 18 Feb 2013, https://humanities.uct.ac.za/apc/articles/2013-02-18-timbuktu-manuscripts-safely-stored ): at the time, a Sky News claim of 25,000 burned was disputed; about 10,000 manuscripts were in trunks in the new building's vaults because the old building was full; local sources reported limited damage and looting of furnishings.
- Cisse, quoted in a search summary, said the final loss was under 5% of the Ahmed Baba collection (UNVERIFIED).

### 1.6 Institutions and programs (SOURCED)
- Ahmed Baba Institute (IHERI-AB): created 1973 (Mail & Guardian: with Kuwaiti funding on UNESCO's recommendation; Zanganeh blog: created by the Malian government); old building opened 1974; new building opened 2009, South Africa-Mali partnership, opposite Sankore mosque (Jeppie). Under Mali's Ministry of Higher Education (CSMC training page). Director in 2025: Mohamed Diagayete (AP, CSMC).
- South Africa-Mali project: President Mbeki visited Timbuktu in November 2001; South Africa helps conserve manuscripts and train Malian conservators; Apr-Jul 2005 four trainees spent two months at the National Archives (Pretoria) and National Library (Cape Town); Dec 2005 two-week follow-up in Timbuktu added five artisans (nine in all), who then made conservation boxes independently; about 70 attended a handling demonstration; Aug 2006 second phase of six weeks in South Africa (Mail & Guardian, Riason Naidoo, SA-Mali Project manager).
- Tombouctou Manuscripts Project (University of Cape Town, director Shamil Jeppie): research and translation; Wikipedia says founded 2003.
- SAVAMA-DCI ("Sauvegarde et valorisation des manuscrits pour la defense de la culture islamique"): a Malian NGO and association/cooperative of private library owners, founded by Haidara in 1996 (HMML decade story; Google Arts says 35 families). Working chain per Google Arts & Culture: dusting; boxing in a workshop of about 20 staff making made-to-measure acid-free boxes at three levels (paper wrap, cover for up to 10 manuscripts tied with ribbon, cardboard box); digitization with "a dozen professional scanners across several rooms" plus a Google-provided scanner; storage in Bamako facilities. Six Bamako storage locations being upgraded with air conditioning, shelving and dehumidification; family libraries being renovated "so collections can be returned." Eleven research programs (five in 2016); three catalogs; four unpublished critical editions. Mission: hold collections in safekeeping during crises with the aim of returning them to families.
- HMML: after a 2013 introduction by the Prince Claus Fund's Deborah Stolk, HMML visited Bamako in August 2013; two digitization studios by early 2014, growing to six in Bamako, plus six more contributed by the Juma Al-Majid Center (Dubai); more than 292,000 manuscripts digitized in Bamako studios; 7,279 from Timbuktu itself (three-year project; the ELIT project digitized 3,808 in three libraries per the search summary); funders Arcadia Fund (2011, 2015), Juma Al-Majid Center, Gerda Henkel Stiftung, German Foreign Ministry (Hamburg), British Library Endangered Archives Programme, Prince Claus Fund. Walid Mourad, HMML field director; Malian technicians trained. Reading Room vhmml.org: 130,000 manuscripts online, nearly 25,000 from Mali. Conservation problem: Bamako humidity vs Timbuktu desert; tightly packed boxes without air circulation.
- CSMC Hamburg: joined March 2013; German Foreign Office and Gerda Henkel funding; on-site conservation since 2014; "Timbuktu Manuscript Training Project": two-year DUT course at IHERI-ABT launched late 2019, about 30 students per cohort, funded by MINUSMA, UNESCO implementing; the UN Security Council on 30 June 2023 decided to withdraw MINUSMA by end of 2023, so courses had to end by end of July 2023 (CSMC blog, 30 Aug 2023). Bamako to Timbuktu "about 1,000 km," international guests travel by air only. Field manager Maria Luisa Russo (Bamako branch office).
- Other: Google digitization of about 40,000 pages/manuscripts (Wikipedia says over 40,000 pages for Mamma Haidara; the search summary said about 40,000 Malian manuscripts; unit differs; UNVERIFIED which); ALIPH-funded AMALIA project at Al-Aqib Library 2019-2021, about $75,000 (search summary; UNVERIFIED); Timbuktu Manuscripts Project (University of Oslo, 1999-2007, Luxembourg/NORAD/Ford/US Ambassadors Fund) (Wikipedia).
- Pre-2012 digitization share: before the smuggling only about 20% of Ahmed Baba's manuscripts were digitized, "nearly all" now (AP 17 Nov 2025).

### 1.8 Recent history, reported neutrally
- 2012: Tuareg separatist and Islamist groups took Timbuktu in April (Ansar Dine, later AQIM-allied groups, per the New Yorker blog); shrines demolished; manuscripts hidden and moved as above. 27 January 2013: Ansar Dine left, French and Malian forces entered (Jeppie).
- 2013-2016: manuscripts held in safe-houses and storage lockers in Bamako (Mamma Haidara Wikipedia: "as of 2016").
- 2023: MINUSMA withdrawal ended the funded training course early.
- 11 August 2025: Mali's government began returning Ahmed Baba Institute manuscripts by air: more than 200 crates, about 5.5 tons in the first shipment (AP via PBS). Timbuktu is under government control while "militants continue attacking its surroundings." AP 17 Nov 2025: Al-Qaeda-affiliated attack on Timbuktu in June 2025; a fuel blockade of Mali by JNIM; the institute's manuscripts held in aging mud-brick libraries, staff plan to install cameras; digital copies backed up on servers elsewhere; private libraries' estimated 377,000 manuscripts remain in Bamako (Taipei Times / AP wording as relayed by Arab News).
- Hold-over question: the Africanews summary says some manuscripts remain in family libraries in Timbuktu, while AP says all private ones were smuggled out and remain in Bamako. Unresolved.

## 2. CAIRO GENIZA, DUNHUANG LIBRARY CAVE, NAG HAMMADI, DEAD SEA SCROLLS

### 2.1 Cairo Geniza (Ben Ezra Synagogue, Fustat/Old Cairo)
Sources: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Geniza (opened); ScienceDaily https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/10/111018102711.htm (opened); search summaries for Britannica, Princeton Geniza Lab, JTS (those pages not opened, or 403: UNVERIFIED unless noted).
- What it was: a storeroom of about 400,000 fragments of Jewish manuscripts and Fatimid administrative documents, dating 6th-19th centuries, mainly Hebrew, Arabic, Aramaic. SOURCED (Wikipedia).
- Why kept: Hebrew script was considered sacred, so texts were kept rather than destroyed; genizot were normally emptied periodically and buried in a cemetery; this one was not (Wikipedia). Search summary: no one knows why this one was never emptied; use stretched to any Hebrew-letter writing including Arabic written in Hebrew letters (Britannica/Princeton via summary; UNVERIFIED). Start date conflicting in summaries: 9th century vs early 11th century (UNVERIFIED).
- The room: "no windows or doors," reached by climbing a ladder to an opening in the wall; papers thrown in over centuries; some stolen "probably by synagogue beadles" and sold to tourists (Wikipedia). So the "keepers" were the synagogue community and its beadles, a mix of custodian and leak. NOT FOUND: a source naming a keeper role or lineage for the geniza before the 1890s.
- Removal and dispersal: first European report about 1752-53 (Simon van Gelderen); Jacob Saphir explored 1864; Elkan Nathan Adler bought a sackful 1896; Solomon Schechter removed most of the contents in 1896-97 (funded by Charles Taylor, helped by the Chief Rabbi); Count Riamo d'Hulst sent 16 large sacks from the courtyard to the Bodleian by May 1898. (Wikipedia.)
- Holdings now (Wikipedia): Taylor-Schechter (Cambridge) nearly 193,000 fragments (137,000 shelfmarks); Jewish Theological Seminary 43,000; John Rylands over 11,000; Bodleian 25,000 folios; Lewis-Gibson 1,700 (bought by the Bodleian and Cambridge in 2013 after being listed at GBP 1.2 million). ScienceDaily: about 350,000 fragments held by roughly 70 institutions.
- Reconstruction: the work is joining pieces of the same original manuscript scattered across institutions. A search summary of a Sussman-catalog paper reports 5,400 fragments examined, 3,877 joined to 935 codices by manual work (UNVERIFIED; paper not opened). SOURCED (ScienceDaily, 18 Oct 2011): Tel Aviv University's Lior Wolf and Nachum Dershowitz, with the non-profit Friedberg Genizah Project, used software (based on face-recognition technology) comparing handwriting, physical page properties and line spacing; "some 1,000 confirmed joins" within a few months, "nearly as many as in 100 years of Genizah research"; a scholar verifies each proposed join. A TEI workshop slide cited in a summary put true-join precision for cross-collection candidates at about 30% (UNVERIFIED).
- Databases: Princeton Geniza Lab (since 1986) database over 30,000 records and 4,600 transcriptions (Wikipedia); Zooniverse "Scribes of the Cairo Geniza" volunteers sort fragments (Wikipedia). Goitein's index covers about 35,000 individuals (Wikipedia). NOT FOUND: staff counts for the Cambridge Genizah Research Unit or the Friedberg Project (dpul.princeton.edu page: certificate error; Cambridge pages not opened).

### 2.2 Dunhuang Library Cave (Mogao Cave 17)
Sources: Wikipedia Mogao Caves (first 100,000 characters read); Wikipedia International Dunhuang Project; China Daily 19 Mar 2024; Xinhua via chinaservicesinfo 4 Jun 2025 (opened); search summaries for staff counts (CGTN/China Daily/Sixth Tone not opened).
- Discovery: 25 June 1900 by Wang Yuanlu, "a Taoist who had appointed himself guardian of some temples." Cave was a memorial for the local monk Hongbian (d. 862), possibly his retreat. Contents 406-1002 CE: 1,100 bundles of scrolls, over 15,000 paper books and shorter texts, textiles, banners, damaged Buddhist figurines; total estimates up to 50,000 manuscripts. Xinhua: original yield "over 73,000 artifacts."
- Why sealed (the article lists five theories, not settled): waste repository for damaged or obsolete texts; a book store that filled over about 150 years; hiding before a Western Xia invasion (Pelliot); protecting the library after Khotan fell in 1006; preserving texts ahead of an expected Age of Decline. Sealing date disputed: 1002 (Rong 2000) vs early-to-mid 13th century (Huntington 1986).
- Keepers: earlier Buddhist monks of the cave-temple complex (the article describes no monastic custodian group); then Wang Yuanlu alone, who re-sealed the cave in 1904 after the Gansu governor objected to transport cost.
- Scattering: Stein 1907 (about 7,000 complete manuscripts and 6,000 fragments); Pelliot 1908 (almost 10,000 documents); Otani Kozui 1911 and Oldenburg 1914 (hundreds); remainder sent to Peking 1910, some stolen, a hidden cache found in the 1940s. Largest collections: Beijing, London, Paris, Berlin.
- Virtual reunification: International Dunhuang Project founded 1994 by the British Library (conference Oct 1993 at Sussex; "one staff member" at start; first director Susan Whitfield retired July 2017); seven founding partners; as of 1 Jan 2026, 154,919 catalogue entries of which 114,332 digitized; each centre responsible for conservation, cataloguing and digitization in its own country; renamed "Programme" in 2024. The Wikipedia article does not use the term "virtual reunification." Xinhua (4 Jun 2025): the Dunhuang Academy launched a "Digital Library Cave" database with 74,651 artifact records, over 9,900 manuscripts, more than 60,700 images and 8.4 million characters; the British Museum contributed digital copies; seven international partners (Harvard, Library of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, others) and nearly 20 Chinese institutions; "only 26,000 items remain in China." Features: AI text recognition, image stitching, knowledge mapping. Also a Dunhuang Academy-Tencent "Digital Library Cave" game-style recreation launched 18 April 2023 (search summary; UNVERIFIED).
- Custodial community today: Dunhuang Research Institute founded 1944 (became the Dunhuang Academy); headcount "about 10" in 1944 to "over 1,500" now (1,507 in one report), 12 departments, nearly 300 guides and visitor-service staff, one department with over 40 full-time specialists (archaeology to meteorology) (all from search summaries of CGTN, China Daily, APP, Sixth Tone, Buddhistdoor; UNVERIFIED, news/promotional sources). SOURCED (China Daily, 19 Mar 2024): the Academy's protection team grew from four members in 1992 to more than 200; director Su Bomin; 735 caves on a cliff 1,700 m long; over 45,000 m2 of murals and more than 2,000 painted sculptures. Summary: remote location reduces applicants and researchers work late for lack of staff (Sixth Tone, Chinadaily HK via summary; UNVERIFIED).

### 2.3 Nag Hammadi codices
Source: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nag_Hammadi_library (opened).
- Twelve leather-bound papyrus codices plus a tractate from a thirteenth, found late 1945 near Nag Hammadi, buried in a sealed jar; found by farmer Muhammed al-Samman and others. About 52 mostly Gnostic treatises, three Hermetic works, a partial Plato's Republic; written in Coptic.
- Who buried them, three hypotheses: (a) a nearby Pachomian monastery, buried after Athanasius's 367 Festal Letter condemned non-canonical books (Robinson; Lundhaug and Jenott 2015/2018; Linjamaa 2024 says a small monastic intellectual elite used the library); (b) a wealthy non-monastic person commissioned them and had them buried with him as funerary prestige items (Lewis and Blount 2014); (c) a grave-robbing find with a cover story. Not settled.
- Dispersal: Cypriot dealer Phokion Tanos acquired most; Department of Antiquities retained them; after 1952 to the Coptic Museum (Pahor Labib, director); the Jung Codex (Codex I) sold to a Belgian dealer, bought by the Jung Institute in Zurich in 1951 via Gilles Quispel, reached Cairo only in 1975. Over 1,000 pages in Cairo today.
- Reconstruction/translation: 1966 Messina Congress; James M. Robinson at the Institute for Antiquity and Christianity (Claremont); International Committee for the Nag Hammadi Codices formed 1970 by UNESCO and the Egyptian Ministry of Culture; 12-volume facsimile edition 1972-77 (Brill); English translation 1977, third edition 1988. NOT FOUND: team size and conservation work (page silent).

### 2.4 Dead Sea Scrolls
Sources: Wikipedia (read to character 200,000); Israel Antiquities Authority conservation page https://www.deadseascrolls.org.il/learn-about-the-scrolls/conservation (opened); SPIE/JPost via summary.
- Found 1946-1956 in caves near Ein Feshkha and Qumran; Bedouin shepherds first (Nov 1946-Feb 1947), first seven scrolls in jars; Cave 1 rediscovered 28 Jan 1949 (Lankester Harding and Roland de Vaux); full excavation of Qumran from Nov 1951. 981 manuscripts from 11 caves (the page's Qumran section says 972); Cave 4 gave about 90% (roughly 15,000 fragments of about 500 texts); Cave 12 found 2017, looted in the 1950s, no scroll recovered.
- Who kept/concealed: dominant theory Essenes at Qumran; others argue priests of Jerusalem (Sons of Zadok) or other groups; the page says worn-out sacred manuscripts were stored in earthenware jars and buried or put in caves, "in a practice related to the Jewish custom of genizah," with no specific reason given for concealment. Contested; no consensus.
- Reconstruction: the Cave 4 pieces had to be reassembled; Harding began and died 1979 unfinished; in 1952 the Jordanian Department of Antiquities assembled a scholar team; after 1953 scholars assembled and logged fragments in the "scrollery" of the Palestine Archaeological Museum (Rockefeller Museum); a small group led by John Strugnell controlled access for decades; the controversy ended in 1991 when the Biblical Archaeology Society published the Facsimile Edition. NOT FOUND: the original team's head count (not in the opened text).
- Conservation (IAA page): early scholars used adhesive tape and window-glass plates, causing irreversible damage; 1991 IAA began documenting and treating thousands of fragments from about 900 manuscripts; a temperature-controlled conservation laboratory and storage vault set up in 1991 with Getty Conservation Institute-sponsored advice; conservators "trained specifically for this project"; all treatments must be reversible; each fragment receives a condition description, photography, mapping and database entry; the climate system aims to match cave averages (caves 400 m below sea level; Jerusalem 800 m above); no figures given; about 80% leather/parchment and 20% papyrus; a Scroll displayed three months rests at least a year; March 2012 international committee reviewed procedures; unresolved issues in 2012: fragments in glass plates, ink corrosion, parchment delamination. Wikipedia: fragments kept on acid-free cardboard in solander boxes in climate-controlled storage; Shrine of the Book shows a reproduction of the Great Isaiah Scroll; original fragments rotated every few months. 2008: about 9,000 fragments (about 900 manuscripts) photographed, 4,000 pictures (search summary of a news report; UNVERIFIED). A Getty/IAA-adjacent site claiming "20 C / 50% RH" appeared in a search and was rejected as unreliable.
- Custody dispute: Jordan and the Palestinian Authority dispute Israel's custody (Wikipedia).

### 2.5 Pattern note (descriptive only)
Across the four cases the record survived because it was sealed, buried or walled in (jar, cave, unemptied storeroom), the sealing reasons are disputed in each case, and the dispersal and reassembly were done by outsiders decades to a century later. No source describes a standing keeper community for any of the four at the time of concealment except the monastic hypotheses (Nag Hammadi, Dunhuang's monks, Qumran's sect), all of them contested.

## 3. HAEINSA (TRIPITAKA KOREANA), MOUNT ATHOS, SAINT CATHERINE'S MONASTERY

### 3.1 Haeinsa and the Janggyeong Panjeon
Sources: Wikipedia Haeinsa and Tripitaka Koreana (opened; the latter read to character 100,000); Journal of Conservation Science 2022 abstract (koreascience page, opened); search summaries for ventilation studies.
- Collection: 81,258 woodblocks per Haeinsa article's body, 81,350 in its lead; 81,352 blocks per the Tripitaka Koreana article (which also gives 81,258 "pages"); counts not reconciled in any page. Each block about 24 cm x 70 cm, 2.6-4 cm thick, 3-4 kg, 23 lines of 14 characters per side.
- Making: second set carved from 1237, about 12 years; "a team of about 30 carvers is now estimated"; monks of Seon and Gyo schools took part; birch; logs soaked in seawater for three years, cut, boiled in salt water, dried three years in shade and wind; coated with lacquer against insects, metal frames against warping. Moved to Haeinsa in 1398. First set (1011-1087) burned 1232. Sugi collated against Northern Song, Khitan and Goryeo texts and published 30 volumes of *Additional Records* listing errors; a survey found missing characters and errors in the text (Tripitaka Koreana article), so "error-free" is a misconception.
- Storage halls (Haeinsa article): four halls in a rectangle (Beopbojeon north, Sudara-jang south, two small library halls east and west); each main hall about 60.44 m x 8.73 m x 7.8 m, fifteen rooms plus two adjoining; at about 655 m above sea level, the highest point of the temple, facing southwest away from damp southeasterly valley winds; mountain peaks block cold north wind; windows of different sizes on north and south sides for ventilation; clay floors with charcoal, calcium oxide, salt, lime and sand "absorb moisture in wet periods and release it in dry winter months"; clay roof and no direct sun; "animals, insects, and birds reportedly avoid the complex, but the page says the reason is unknown." Construction date uncertain; Sejo expanded or renovated in 1457; National Treasure 20 Dec 1962; UNESCO 1995.
- Evidence on the mechanism: the floor claim is from Wikipedia only; a search summary found "no peer-reviewed measurement study confirming the effect" (UNVERIFIED as a mechanism). Computational airflow studies (Hur, Lee and Yang, 2007; Jo et al. 2017; NAFEMS 2019 as reported in a search summary) found no stagnant air zones and that the current window shape performed best of those tested (UNVERIFIED, summaries). Hong et al. 2022 (Journal of Conservation Science, National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage, SOURCED abstract): airborne fungal concentration in Beopbojeon 1.44 times that of Sudarajang; 23 fungal genera in air; 11 fungal and 1 bacterial genera on block surfaces; only five types common to air and surface; 58.3% of surface fungi not detected in air. The abstract gives no temperature/humidity values; the summary quoted a 0.4 C average indoor-outdoor difference (UNVERIFIED).
- What failed: Haeinsa article: in 1970 a modern storage building was planned, but test blocks developed mildew, so the move was canceled; Tripitaka Koreana article: blocks moved to a new depository in the early 1970s "deteriorated quickly and were returned," a few were damaged, the building closed and is now a Zen Center; experts still debate whether the current storage is adequate. Temple main complex burned in 1817 and was rebuilt 1818; the halls were spared that fire, and the page says they survived seven serious fires and one near-bombing in 1951 (the Kim Young-hwan story is told in the article as "reportedly"; UNVERIFIED).
- Staff/custodial community: Haeinsa is head temple of the Jogye Order and an active Seon practice center; the master Seongcheol (d. 1993) started the Haein Chongnim (training monastery) in 1967 (search summary; UNVERIFIED). A travel site said "500 monks currently live there" (undated, non-official: UNVERIFIED, probably inclusive of trainees). Responsibility for management is split: Gyeongsangnam-do provides conservation funding, Hapcheon County handles operations (search summary of a tourism page; UNVERIFIED). Digitized files expected public by 2027 (Tripitaka Koreana article). NOT FOUND: the number of staff who care for the halls, their roles, daily cleaning or airing routines, any monk custodian ranks (searched in English; Korean-language sources not searched; died at the query language).

### 3.2 Mount Athos
Sources: Wikipedia Mount Athos (first 100,000 characters) and Monastic community of Mount Athos (first 100,000); Amen.gr via johnsanidopoulos.com (2011); athos.guide modern-era page; johnsanidopoulos 2012 pilgrim information (Pilgrims' Bureau); NBC/AP 22 Oct 2022 (Pantokrator).
- Geography/governance: peninsula about 50 km long, 335.6 km2, 7-12 km wide, peak 2,033 m; autonomous region of Greece governed by the monastic community of 20 monasteries ("Holy Community"), ecclesiastically under the Ecumenical Patriarch; each monastery run by an archimandrite elected by its monks for life, with the Gerontia as legislative body; Civil Administrator (appointed by presidential decree on Foreign Ministry recommendation) based in Karyes; 12 sketes; cells of 1-3 monks under monastery supervision that must earn income. 1913 council voted to keep autonomy; 1926 Charter of 188 articles drafted by five elected monks, ratified in the 1927 constitution (athos.guide). Holy Community income: 3% of each monastery's profits plus diamonitirion fees (athos.guide).
- Population: 1991 census 1,536; 2001 census 2,262; 2011 provisional 1,830 (Amen.gr via Sanidopoulos); Wikipedia infobox 1,746 (2021 estimate); a search summary gave 1,811 (2011) and 1,744 (2021) from citypopulation.de (UNVERIFIED). Monk counts: 9,900 (1910); 6,355 (1914); 3,817 (1935); 2,043 (1951); 1,649 (1961); 1,236 (1968); about 1,146 (1971-72); 1,610 (2000); nearly 2,000 (2009). The Governor said it is unclear whether censuses counted only monks or also restoration workers. Residents must be Orthodox men aged 18+ who are monks or workers (Wikipedia). A search summary of a Rough Guides page: laymen "including many Muslim Albanians of late," mostly agricultural or manual labourers; maintenance by hired resident lay workers "but otherwise by monks trained in the requisite specialties"; lumbering by outside syndicates employing local lay workers (UNVERIFIED, page not opened). A pilgrim-bureau summary: total of 1,811 residents plus administrative employees, police officers, business owners and seasonal civilian workers (UNVERIFIED, undated). NOT FOUND: a count of lay workers; clinic, post-office and police-force sizes (searched twice; died at the sources).
- Access rules: women barred by the avaton (formalized by the 1046 chrysobull of Constantine Monomachos, per Wikipedia), female domestic animals too except cats; quota 100 Orthodox and 10 non-Orthodox male visitors per day; general diamonitirion issued in Thessaloniki, 3 nights (Wikipedia) or a four-day visit (2012 Pilgrims' Bureau page) , applications up to six months ahead; special permit (idikon) from a monastery for 4 days to 1 year; clergy need an evlogia from the Ecumenical Patriarchate; fee 30 euros (10 for students) in 2012, 18/30 euros per a search summary of Wikivoyage; gates close at sunset; lodging free, donations accepted; video cameras forbidden, photography allowed; removing items from collections carries severe penalties (2012 page). Visits suspended March 2020-May 2021 (Wikipedia). Dafni main port, Karyes administrative center. Ferry from Ouranoupoli about 2 hours.
- Libraries: totals disagree: "about 14,500 Greek manuscripts" (search summary, source unnamed) vs "over 11,000" (Library of Congress summary) vs about 25,000 Ottoman-era works across Athonite libraries (AP 2022). Vatopedi's abbot says 2,074 manuscripts; Xeropotamou about 450 manuscripts and 3,500 printed books (search summaries; UNVERIFIED). The Library of Congress microfilmed the largest group of Athos manuscripts in 1952-53 (summary). Research: Foundation for Research and Culture (eie.gr) "Libraries of Mount Athos" cataloguing project (summary). Wikipedia: younger monks, many university-educated, catalogue and restore manuscripts, funded by UNESCO and the EU, "expected to take decades."
- Copying: monks copied texts (Dionysiou scriptorium from abbot Theonas; Xeropotamou 17th-century copy workshop) (search summary; UNVERIFIED). Wikipedia article says nothing on scriptoria. NOT FOUND: current copying practice.
- Access to libraries: in the 18th and early 19th century manuscripts were "practically inaccessible" to non-Orthodox scholars; Zograf restricts library access to digital copies only; research on-site requires clearance by the Holy Community; Ohio State's Hilandar microfilming had permission from the Greek civil authorities, the monastic government and Hilandar's brotherhood (all search summary; UNVERIFIED). AP 2022 (SOURCED): Pantokrator Monastery library, in a tower, researchers from the Free University of Berlin (Yiannis Niehoff-Panagiotidis; jurist Anastasios Nikopoulos) examine Ottoman-era documents (oldest 1371/1374) at a table beside a Pantokrator monk, Father Theophilos; the overwhelming majority are legal documents; not catalogued or digitized; rarer items kept in large wooden drawers.

### 3.3 Saint Catherine's Monastery (Sinai)
Sources: Wikipedia (first 100,000 characters); Pravmir reprint of TIME, 21-22 Jul 2013 (opened); Ligatus St Catherine's Project page (opened); search summaries of Saint Catherine Foundation newsletters.
- Founded 548-565 under Justinian; Church of Sinai headed by an archbishop who is also abbot; the library "founded sometime between 527 and 565," recognized by Guinness as the oldest continuously operating library; second-largest collection of early codices and manuscripts (after the Vatican); languages include Greek, Syriac, Georgian, Arabic, Latin, Armenian, Church Slavonic. Total count not given in Wikipedia; Time (2013): "1.8 million pages of script." A search-summary blog said "over 3000" manuscripts (UNVERIFIED); the Stainless Steel Boxing Project aims to house 2,187 parchment manuscripts in individual stainless steel boxes (Wikipedia), each with an oxygen-starvation system; stainless chosen over wood for insect, fire and off-gassing resistance. The Ligatus page: one of the most detailed surveys ever of such material; about 1,000 early printed books surveyed; main funder the Saint Catherine Foundation; boxing and renovation planning; a 2019 foundation bulletin says the conservation workshop designed by Ligatus "remains on hold, dependant on the availability of funds for Phase Two" (summary; UNVERIFIED).
- People: 25 monks (Time/Pravmir, July 2013); Father Justin, resident since 1996, serves as librarian; Wikipedia gives only historical figures (several hundred in the mid-14th century, several dozen a century later). The Jabaliya (Jebeliya) Bedouin live in the town of St. Catherine and are "believed to descend from the Byzantine soldiers who built the monastery"; during the 2011 revolution the monks moved the most valuable manuscripts into a storage room and Bedouin "allegedly armed themselves and guarded the perimeter"; a mosque (Fatimid-era, 1106) inside is still preserved; a 2013 foundation newsletter said close to 1,000 Bedouin families live in the vicinity, many near the poverty line (summary; UNVERIFIED). Security: 18 April 2017 attack at a checkpoint near the monastery killed one policeman and injured three; a lands dispute between the South Sinai Governorate and the Church of Sinai had a Court of Appeal ruling 28 May 2025 and was still ongoing in early 2026 (Wikipedia).

## 4. RECONCILING DIVERGENT COPIES; ALEXANDRIA; INTERNET ARCHIVE; LEGAL DEPOSIT; CLEARING HOUSES; STAFF

### 4.1 How textual scholars compare and reconcile copies
Source: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textual_criticism (first 100,000 of 218,005 characters read).
- Task: identify variants among manuscripts or printed editions and reconstruct the text closest to the original; weigh external evidence (age, origin, relationships of a witness) against internal evidence (what scribes or printers were likely to change).
- Methods: eclecticism (readings drawn from many witnesses on the principle that independent lines are unlikely to share errors; dominant for the Greek New Testament since the mid-19th century; canons like "harder reading," "shorter reading"); stemmatics (Karl Lachmann; family tree from shared errors, "community of error implies community of origin"); phylogenetics borrowed from biology (the Canterbury Tales Project used it on 84 surviving manuscripts and four early printed editions); best-text editing (Bedier: lightly correct one good witness); copy-text editing (McKerrow 1904; Greg 1950: copy-text governs spelling and punctuation, editorial judgment chooses substantive readings; Bowers, Tanselle).
- Scale example: New Testament: more than 5,800 Greek manuscripts, about 10,000 Latin, about 9,300 in other ancient languages, roughly 300,000 variants.
- Example of a text reconciled by a single scholar over many witnesses: Sugi's 30 volumes of *Additional Records* comparing editions of the Tripitaka (Tripitaka Koreana Wikipedia article, see 3.1).
- In the Timbuktu archive, the CSMC IRP 11 project treats copying as "a generative practice," not merely derivative (see 1.4); no source opened gives a collation of Timbuktu variants.
- NOT FOUND (page not read or silent): team sizes at textual-criticism institutes (the page omitted the Institut fur neutestamentliche Textforschung and the Coherence-Based Genealogical Method; the digital-scholarship section was truncated). The remaining 118,005 characters were not read.

### 4.2 Library of Alexandria: documented vs legendary
Source: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria (first 100,000 of 224,846 characters read).
- Foundation: documented as an institution under Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285-246 BC); Ptolemy I may have laid groundwork. The *Letter of Aristeas* (c. 180-145 BC) credits Ptolemy I and Demetrius of Phalerum; historians consider that source very late and inaccurate.
- Holdings: estimates range from 40,000 to 700,000 scrolls, perhaps about 100,000 books (Garland 2008); "true number is unknown."
- Copying from ships: Galen (a later source) reports that books found on arriving ships were taken to the Library and copied by official scribes; originals stayed, copies went to owners. Presented as his account, not independently confirmed.
- Staff: reportedly over 100 scholars at its height (Haughton 2011; MacLeod 2000); the Mouseion may have housed 30-50 learned men as early as 283 BC; Strabo says scholars received a large salary, free food and lodging, and tax exemption. Budget figures: NOT FOUND in the text read.
- Librarians and work: Zenodotus (first recorded head; Homeric texts; probably alphabetical arrangement per Casson), Callimachus (*Pinakes*, a 120-book catalogue, never head), Apollonius of Rhodes, Eratosthenes, Aristophanes of Byzantium (line division for poetry, Greek diacritics), Aristarchus of Samothrace (definitive Homeric texts and commentaries, forced out 145 BC). Legend: the Vitruvius story of Aristophanes chosen through a poetry competition; Archimedes' screw at the Library.
- Destruction accounts (the page's own assessments): Caesar 48 BC (Seneca quoting Livy says 40,000 scrolls lost; Cassius Dio suggests dockside warehouses; scholars conclude the Library was not fully destroyed; Strabo visited c. 20 BC); the Antony-Cleopatra 200,000 scroll gift (Plutarch flagged his source as unreliable); Aurelian 272 AD (Broucheion quarter destroyed); Diocletian 297; Theophilus 391 (Serapeum demolished; only Orosius mentions destroyed books); Arab conquest 642 (al-Qifti, before 1248, about 500 years after; historians skeptical).
- Reading for this unit: the legend and the documentation differ most on numbers and on the destruction story; the documented core is a state-funded scholarly institution that copied, compared and edited texts (Homer) and catalogued them.

### 4.5 Clearing houses and reconciliation mechanisms (only those found)
- OCLC WorldCat deduplication (search summaries from OCLC and Hanging Together; UNVERIFIED, pages not opened): rules-based Duplicate Detection and Resolution since the 1990s; reported averages of 11,000 records removed manually and 1 million through automation per month (undated); first machine-learning model August 2023, trained on feedback from over 300 users on about 34,000 duplicates, leading to removal of about 5.4 million duplicate printed-book records; extended to all formats, languages and scripts in February 2025; merges are reversible, and "WorldCat Metadata Quality staff" can recover merged records. NOT FOUND: OCLC headcount and total WorldCat records.
- LOCKSS (arXiv cs/0303026, Maniatis et al., 2003, via summary; the paper itself not opened, UNVERIFIED): peers vote in "opinion polls" by returning hashes of their replicas; more than 70% agreement means no action, under 30% means repair, in between raises an alarm; a damaged peer requests repair only from a peer that agreed with it in the past; polls require provable computational effort; rate-limited sampled voting. CLOCKSS-specific reconciliation NOT FOUND.
- Pattern note: the reconcilers named in these sources are people with narrow roles (collators, cataloguers, metadata quality staff, claims processors), plus hashing-based automatic comparison; the sources give no instance of a single reconciling "authority" beyond the legal-deposit claims agency.

## 5. ARCHIVE STAFFING REALITIES AND UNDERGROUND/DEEP-COLD STORAGE PRACTICE

### 5.1 Granite Mountain Records Vault (Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, Little Cottonwood Canyon, Utah)
Sources: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite_Mountain_Records_Vault (opened); Church newsroom page (opened); Deseret News 23 Jan 2013 (opened); search summaries.
- Location/rock: about one mile up Little Cottonwood Canyon in the Wasatch Range; "excavated about 600 feet into the canyon's north side"; rock is mainly quartz monzonite resembling granite (Wikipedia). Search summaries: "nearly 700 feet" / "180 metres" deep (UNVERIFIED, three depth figures); excavation began 1960, completed 1965, dedicated 22 June 1966 (summary, UNVERIFIED); Church newsroom says built 1965.
- Contents: over 2.4 million microfilm rolls and 1 million microfiche, roughly three billion pages (Wikipedia); Church newsroom: more than 3.5 billion images on microfilm, microfiche and digital media (two figures, unreconciled); microfilm library grows up to 40,000 rolls per year (Wikipedia). Images come under agreements with archives, libraries and churches in more than 100 countries; custodians get free copies, and extra copies sometimes replace records lost to disaster or fire (Church newsroom).
- Facilities: "administrative offices, shipping and receiving docks, a processing facility, and a microfilm restoration laboratory" (Wikipedia). Deseret News (a former evaluator hired in 1972): evaluators examine film at counters for nicks, cracks and exposure; flawed film is marked and sent back for re-filming and re-inspection. No staff count in any opened page.
- Access: no public access for security; digitization since 1999; records reached through Family History Centers and FamilySearch.
- NOT FOUND: staff count, temperature, humidity, fire-suppression design for the Church's vault (Wikipedia, Church and Deseret pages silent; three searches; died at the sources).
- Neighboring comparable (a different, commercial vault, NOT Granite Mountain): Perpetual Storage Inc., Little Cottonwood Canyon, AP 2003 via Deseret News https://www.deseret.com/2003/11/17/19796252/storage-vault-keeps-its-contents-safe : tunnel about 275 ft long and 35 ft high; opened 1968; excavation cost $750,000 (1960s); constant 60 F and about 28% humidity; air recirculated every six hours to filter dust; 6-ton door "designed to withstand a nuclear blast"; four power sources including canyon hydropower and diesel generator; seismic sensors to detect drilling; "about 10 employees, including guards and couriers"; storage policy bans anything that might burn; no fire-suppression system described; contents mostly business records on computer tapes and microfilm and backup copies of electronic records, plus 60 fossilized fish and four Remington bronzes on display; formerly gold and silver bullion for a Swiss bank, dropped because of the security burden. The LDS vaults (1964) are about a mile away; a BYU geologist concluded an earthquake was unlikely to damage the vault.

### 5.5 Climate-control and fire-suppression practice for underground and deep-cold storage
- National Archives guidance (search summary of archives.gov preservation page; page not retrievable): underground mine storage suits microfilm, motion picture film, audio/video tape and paper; advantages include physical protection, easy environmental maintenance; fire suppression options listed: wet and pre-action sprinklers, Halon, FM-200 (UNVERIFIED).
- Archive standards (search summaries): construction to NFPA 232, NFPA 909 and local code, strictest governing; a NARA conference slide set lists wet pipe, dry pipe and pre-action sprinklers, notes discharged water can carry rust or sediment, and describes water mist and dual gas-and-water systems; water mist suggested for cultural property because it releases less water; a 2008 NARA conference talk on low-oxygen suppression at the British Library (Julian Taylor); NFPA 909 points toward fire modeling (UNVERIFIED; the NARA PDF and the standards were not opened).
- British Library hypoxic practice (SOURCED, warehouseautomation.ca 30 Apr 2022): newspaper building void at 14 C and 55% humidity with oxygen held at 14% "compared with about 21%"; the Additional Storage Building "7% lower" oxygen; low oxygen "cited as preventing fires"; sealed doors and airlock; sprinklers not mentioned. Wikipedia: Boston Spa's storage is "temperature- and humidity-controlled."
- Library of Congress Packard Campus (SOURCED, Architect of the Capitol page https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/buildings-grounds/library-of-congress/packard-campus): 45 acres, 415,000 usable sq ft in four buildings (Collections Storage 135,000; Conservation 175,000; Nitrate Vaults 55,000; Central Plant 50,000); 124 nitrate vaults "largest in the western hemisphere" built to strict fire codes; "below-freezing conditions for film masters"; built primarily underground with sod roofs and a 173,000 sq ft green roof. Search summary: local code officials required a clean-agent suppression system with pre-action sprinklers as backup; 35 climate-controlled vaults for sound recording, safety film and videotape (UNVERIFIED; LoC "campus features" page returned 403). Setpoints (temperature/RH) NOT FOUND in any opened page.
- Dead Sea Scrolls (see 2.4) as a climate-matching case: storage tuned to cave averages, no figures published on the IAA page.
- Humidity in the relocated Timbuktu collections and Haeinsa's trial depository (see 1.6, 3.1) are the documented failure cases of climate mismatch.

## 6. RECORDS PROTECTED DEEP IN ROCK OR ICE; WHAT FAILED

### 6.1 Svalbard Global Seed Vault (Longyearbyen, Spitsbergen)
Sources: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svalbard_Global_Seed_Vault (first 100,000 of 130,591 chars); NordGen FAQ https://www.nordgen.org/our-work/svalbard-global-seed-vault/frequently-asked-questions/ ; Crop Trust blog "Svalbard: a day in the life" https://www.croptrust.org/blog/svalbard-a-day-in-the-life/ ; NordGen Annual Progress Report 2022 (Norwegian government site PDF, text extracted with pdftotext); Carrington, *Guardian* 19 May 2017 as republished by intercommunalworkshop.org (opened).
- Site/design: 130 m inside a sandstone mountain, elevation 130 m above sea level, one floor of about 1,000 m2; no tectonic activity, permafrost; storage at -18 C; "locally mined coal powers the refrigeration units"; if equipment failed it would take "at least several weeks" to reach bedrock temperature -3 C and about two centuries to reach 0 C (Wikipedia). Crop Trust: team walks 120 m into the mountain; 3 km drive from the airport. NordGen FAQ: chambers carved in rock in permafrost conditions "which lowers the energy needed for mechanical cooling"; annual running cost about EUR 1 million.
- Capacity/holdings: 4.5 million samples at about 500 seeds each; about 1.36 million accessions (June 2025; 1,378,238 in the yearly table; unreconciled); 127 depositors (March 2025). 2022: 31 genebanks deposited 69,825 samples; 102 deposit agreements signed, 93 active (NordGen report).
- Ownership/operation: Norway owns; NordGen operates; Crop Trust funds operations through an endowment and helps depositors; Norway funds upkeep; "black box": depositor retains ownership, only the depositor can access its seeds. Construction about 45 million kr (US$8.8 million, 2008); annual maintenance about 2.4 million kr (US$282,000) in 2019 (Wikipedia).
- Staffing, SOURCED: "no permanent staff on-site" (Wikipedia; NordGen FAQ). Statsbygg (public works agency), based in Longyearbyen, services the facility and "provides continuous surveillance," acts as keyholder and accompanies the team "all the way in" and "at all work inside the Seed Vault" (NordGen FAQ; Crop Trust; 2022 report). NordGen staff from Alnarp, Sweden travel for each opening; Crop Trust blog: 4-6 deposit occasions a year with 15-25 institutes (the blog; undated); the 2022 report: "Three Seed Vault openings" in 2022. Seed Vault Coordinator Asmund Asdal (NordGen) since 2015. Local logistics firm Pole Position Logistics picks up and stores boxes; Avinor and Avarn Security Aviation scan boxes at Longyearbyen airport (scanning must be booked a month ahead and is invoiced, new in 2022). Boxes are never opened; staff label each, shelve, then photograph and send photos to depositors. Nanofilm labels with accession data being attached to boxes: about 3,100 labels produced by 2021; 300-350 attached in 2022; "more labor demanding than first thought," requiring an extra trip of two and a half days (2022 report).
- Visitors: "No individuals other than authorized personnel may enter. Media and guests were allowed in the past, but a new risk assessment during the upgrade added measures" (NordGen FAQ). Polar bear paw prints seen near the vault (Crop Trust, anecdotal).
- 2017 meltwater event (SOURCED, Carrington 19 May 2017): after record warmth at the end of 2016 (Spitsbergen average temperatures more than 7 C above normal), heavy rain and meltwater flowed into the entrance tunnel and refroze "into a mass of ice that looked like a glacier"; the water did not reach the vault itself; crews chipped the ice out; seeds stayed at -18 C. Government's Hege Njaa Aschim: "It was not in our plans to think that the permafrost would not be there" and "now we are watching the seed vault 24 hours a day"; pumps installed inside the vault; waterproofing the 100 m tunnel, drainage trenches, removal of heat-producing electrical equipment. The vault had been built "to run without human help." Statsbygg (search summaries, UNVERIFIED): permafrost near the entrance thawed during construction a decade earlier had not refrozen as predicted. Wikipedia dates the water intrusion to October 2016, reaching 15 m into the 100 m tunnel; improvements completed 2019. NordGen FAQ: does not mention 2017; says after about 10 years of operation recurring minor leaks occurred during snowmelt and technical advice in 2018 led to an upgrade of about NOK 200 million (EUR 20 million) covering a watertight tunnel, security procedures and a new cooling system. Dating conflict: event in late 2016, reported May 2017.
- Other: first withdrawal 2015 by ICARDA (Syria), second larger one 2017 (Wikipedia). A 100-year seed-storage experiment in a coal mine: 35-year set tested February 2022 (NordGen report).

### 6.2 Arctic World Archive (Svalbard)
Sources: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_World_Archive ; Marketplace/BBC story 17 Feb 2025 (opened at the redirect URL); Blocks and Files 27 Sept 2021 (opened); AWA about page (opened).
- Site: a steel vault inside an abandoned coal mine (Store Norske Gruve 3) at Longyearbyen, "300m inside" the mine (Blocks and Files) / "about 300 meters below permafrost" (Marketplace); Wikipedia gives a range of 150-300 m and one figure of 250 m below the permafrost (three depth statements, unreconciled); opened 27 March 2017; reels in shipping containers behind a concrete perimeter wall and steel gate; "Permafrost keeps the interior below freezing"; mine closed "about three decades ago" (Marketplace).
- Operators: Piql (Norwegian data-storage company; offices in Drammen) and Store Norske Spitsbergen Kulkompani; SNSK handles operation, maintenance, security and access control (search summary). AWA about page: self-owned Norwegian foundation, board reports to the Norwegian Foundation Authority; named team of four staff (founder Rune Bjerkestrand, co-founder Katrine Loen, a heritage coordinator, a communications coordinator) at Gronland 56, Drammen (not on Svalbard). No on-site staff described anywhere.
- Medium/process: data printed as images on 35 mm film (piqlFilm); stated lifespan at least 500 years, "possibly up to 2,000" (Wikipedia) or "more than 1000 years" (Piql claim via Blocks and Files) or "no one knows" (Marketplace); retrieval by staff fetching the reel manually and loading onto a reader, data sent by fibre; Wikipedia: 20-30 minutes at fastest, up to 24 hours with subscription and up to 72 hours without; Blocks and Files: "could take just 30 minutes, but is likely to take longer"; deposit events held periodically, with write latency five to six months (Feb and Sept 2021). Marketplace (2025): more than 100 deposits from over 30 countries. GitHub: 21 TB snapshot on 186 reels (July 2020) (Wikipedia). Price not published. Depositors named: Vatican Library, National Museum of Norway, ESA, National Library of Hungary, Norwegian Armed Forces Museum, Norway's Natural History Museum.

### 6.3 Cheyenne Mountain Complex (Colorado)
Sources: Wikipedia Cheyenne Mountain Complex and Cheyenne Mountain Space Force Station (opened); search summary for staffing figures.
- Built: excavation began 18 May 1961, construction to 8 Feb 1966, operational February 1967; US$142.4 million initial funds; about 5.1 acres; three main tunnels 45 ft wide, 60.5 ft high, 588 ft long crossed by four cross tunnels; about 2,000 ft of granite above (the station page says 2,000 ft of granite; the complex page says 2,000 feet of "gravel" above, a conflict in the same family of articles); 25-ton north blast door; fifteen three-story buildings on over 1,000 springs limiting movement to about 1 inch; only underground DoD facility certified to sustain HEMP (Wikipedia).
- Services inside: own power plant, heating and cooling, water; 1.5-million-gallon reservoir as fire and water reserve and 4.5-million-gallon heat-sink reservoir; diesel reservoir, battery bank, redundant generators; most personnel sleep on cots, senior officers have suites; medical facility, market, cafeteria, fitness centers; blast valves with chemical/biological/radiological/nuclear filters maintained by Air National Guard personnel; a fire station run by the 721st Civil Engineer Squadron; 721st Security Forces Squadron expanded in 2011 (Wikipedia).
- Staff: Wikipedia pages give no headcount ("skeleton crewed," crew training center, alternate command center). Search summary: in 2007 a Space Command representative said 550 Space Command personnel remained assigned there (UNVERIFIED, from a summary of a news report; about 19 years old). A "1,450 personnel" figure in the same results was a projection for a relocated Space Command, not Cheyenne Mountain's staff. NOT FOUND: peak staffing and shift schedules.
- Operational changes: 2006 Space Control Center moved to the Joint Space Operations Center; 2008 NORAD and USNORTHCOM relocated and the site became the alternate main operations center (Peterson now manages it); 2011 day-to-day NORAD operations moved back after renovation (station page) - a contradiction with the complex page about direction; 2015 Raytheon $700 million contract to move systems in and shield them from EMP.
- What failed (the complex page; these are system failures, not storage failures): 427M computer system initially "wholly ineffective"; computer failures in 1979 and 1980 produced false missile warnings; the Cheyenne Mountain Upgrade of 1988 never installed after failing testing; Survivable Communications Integration System delayed and projected to rise from $142 million to $234 million; Granite Sentry's detonation data processing found inadequate; H6080 computers failed to meet timing requirements by 1981; SPADATS deactivated about 1980. Also September 2013 rockslide after heavy rain cleared by Fort Carson soldiers, "did not limit operations" (station page).

### 6.4 Pyhasalmi Mine (Pyhajarvi, Finland)
Sources: Wikipedia Pyhasalmi Mine and Callio (opened); Callio Lab slides, CELLAR Community Meeting 2022 (PDF text extracted with pdftotext).
- No records archive or document vault at Pyhasalmi was found in any opened source. What exists: a mine reused as an underground research and business site. Mine depth 1,444 m (Wikipedia) / 1,450 m (Callio page); opened 1962 by Outokumpu; underground mining ended August 2022 (extended from 2019); owner at the time First Quantum Minerals (slides); reuse coordinated by the town-owned Callio, scientific coordination by Callio Lab (University of Oulu); main level about 1,400 m, elevator about 3 minutes vs about 30 minutes by car on the 11 km decline.
- Underground conditions: about 15 C at 660 m and 28 C at 1,430 m (Callio Wikipedia); the Callio Extreme Run (May 2018) had temperatures above 23 C and 50-100% humidity (Pyhasalmi Wikipedia); four environmentally controlled labs at 75, 660, 990 and 1,430 m; ~4000 m.w.e. rock cover (slides). Services (slides): trucks and 20-ft containers through the incline, elevator for packages 1.5 x 2.0 x 1.5 m, optical internet line (1+ Gb), "leaky feeder" radio network, refuge bases (shelters), microseismic network, office space, local support team.
- Proposed uses: "a secure underground data center" is listed as proposed (Callio Wikipedia; an ARCEM data-center plan appears in a 2025 press-release title in search results, UNVERIFIED); pumped hydro storage (December 2019 agreement; phase two 75 MW; EU approved EUR 26.3 million for a 530 MWh project in late 2022); insect farm at 1,430 m from July 2019; hop growing at 660 m; world's deepest sauna at 1,410 m.
- What failed: LAGUNA neutrino-observatory proposal collapsed over funding; a 2012 explosives incident (pyrite-contaminated explosives) led to on-site destruction of hazardous waste from 2013; surface refinery fires in the 2000s; last fatality 1995 (Pyhasalmi Wikipedia). One Callio Lab presentation line (search summary) that "critical historical mining data has been put into safekeeping" could not be confirmed in the slides text opened (UNVERIFIED).
- NOT FOUND: staff numbers; water management; a records vault.

### 6.5 Cross-site facts on staffing of deep stores (descriptive)
- Svalbard seed vault and Arctic World Archive are both operated without resident staff; both use periodic scheduled openings with a government or company keyholder; the Seed Vault went to 24-hour surveillance after the water event.
- Cheyenne Mountain is a staffed, multi-service enclave; Pyhasalmi has a working-mine staff that no opened source counts.
