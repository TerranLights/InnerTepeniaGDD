# Relung Panen port research, slice D: precedents for landing cargo at an ice front, sea ice or ice edge, and Antarctic port logistics

**Date of research:** 2026-10-25
**Scope:** Real-world physical and logistical facts only. Nothing in this file describes the fictional setting. Nothing here is a recommendation.
**Why it was asked:** A fictional city of about 0.7 to 0.9 million people would sit at the real Bunger Hills oasis, about 50 to 85 km inland from the Shackleton Ice Shelf front. This slice collects what has actually been done elsewhere in Antarctica to land cargo at an ice front, on sea ice or across an ice edge, at what scale and with what equipment.

**Marking key**
- **SOURCED**: read in the cited source (page text, PDF text or document text). The date is the source's date.
- **COMPUTED**: arithmetic on SOURCED numbers. The working is shown, and any assumption is named.
- **UNVERIFIED**: seen only in a search-engine snippet, or in a source that could not be opened and read in full. Kept so the lead is not lost; not to be relied on.
- **NOT FOUND**: looked for and not found. Section 3 says where.
- **Spelling:** quoted source text has been put into American spelling (meters, liters, tons, Harbor) per the project rule. The search strings in section 4 are left exactly as typed.

**Real-world rules caveat (Q6):** the Madrid Protocol, MARPOL and Polar Code facts in section 2.6 are present-day real-world rules. They are recorded as facts only and may not apply to the fictional setting.

## 1. Findings summary

### 1.1 Five headline facts

1. **The largest single-season ice-edge delivery found in East Antarctica** was the Russian Vostok-rebuild sealift to Progress station (Larsemann Hills) in 2021/22: **6,780 t of cargo on two container ships plus 6,000 t of diesel on a tanker**, unloaded in Tala fjord from 25 November to 18 December 2021. The icebreaker *Kapitan Khlebnikov* needed **27 days** to cut a channel through fast ice that was **up to 90 km wide**, with hummocks 1.5 to 2 m high. (SOURCED: Russian Federation, ATCM XLIV BP 22, 2022.) That works out to about **530 t a day** over the 24-day unloading window (COMPUTED).
2. **The largest annual throughput is McMurdo**, which has one cargo ship and one tanker a year. The cargo ship brings **about 7 million lb (about 3,175 t)** in roughly 500 containers and pieces. The tanker brings **about 6 to 9 million US gallons (23 to 34 ML)** of fuel, and that fuel also supplies the wider US program. All of it came across a man-made **ice pier** from 1973, through a series of ice-pier failures from 2010/11 onward. A **328 ft × 100 ft (100 m × 30.5 m) steel barge pier** was installed in **March 2026** to replace it. (SOURCED: MSC/Navy, NZDF, Baird Maritime, Marine Log; see 2.2 and 2.3.)
3. **Ice-shelf-front berths are in use today at Atka Bay (Neumayer), on the Brunt Ice Shelf (Halley), at Novolazarevskaya, at the "Indian Barrier" (Maitri), at Breid Bay (Princess Elisabeth) and at 5°E (Troll).** At these sites the ship moors against a floating ice cliff low enough for its deck cranes, about **10 to 17 m**, and lands containers on sledges. Tractors then haul them **15 to 200 km** inland. A typical annual call is in the **hundreds of tons** and **0.2 to 0.5 ML of fuel**. (SOURCED: AWI, BAS, IPF, NCPOR, AARI; see 2.1.)
4. **Recorded ice-front failures:**
   - **Maitri, April 2012:** a **2 km × 1 km** piece of the Indian Barrier unloading site calved away. Indian tank containers and Russian cargo and fuel staged on it were lost and judged sunk. (SOURCED: NCPOR.)
   - **Breid Bay:** swell ripped the mooring pins out of the ice cliff and moved the ship **3 to 4 m** vertically. (SOURCED: IPF.)
   - **Atka Bay, 2007/08:** the freighter *Naja Arctica* was held off the berth by several meters of sea ice from 16 December 2007 until *Polarstern* broke a channel on 16 January 2008. (SOURCED: AWI.)
   - **Syowa, 2011/12 and 2012/13:** *Shirase* could not reach the station at all, and stopped about 20 km away. (SOURCED: NIPR *Antarctic Record*.)
5. **At this exact ice shelf:** in January 2022, *Akademik Fedorov* reached the Shackleton Ice Shelf at **65°40.3′S 100°11.6′E**, broke a 2 km channel and stopped **about 70 km from Bunger Hills**. Containers then went ashore **by helicopter over several days**. (SOURCED: University of Oslo *Titan* blog, 4 January 2022.) In **February 1912** the *Aurora* landed Frank Wild's Western Base party and stores **onto the Shackleton Ice Shelf itself**, up a cliff of about **100 ft (about 30 m)**. They used a flying fox, and the hut stood **640 yards (about 585 m)** inland. (SOURCED: Mawson, *Home of the Blizzard*, ch. 19, AAD transcription.)

## 2. Findings by question

### 2.1 Question 1: Atka Iceport, Halley and the Brunt, Kohnen and other ice-shelf-front operations

#### Atka Iceport, Neumayer Station

**What it is**
- Atka Iceport is "about 10 miles (16 km) long and wide", a more-or-less permanent indentation in the front of the Ekström Ice Shelf at 70°35′S 7°51′W. USS *Atka* named it in February 1955. (SOURCED: Wikipedia, *Atka Iceport*, citing USGS GNIS.)
- COMNAP's 2017 catalogue gives Neumayer III **"Ship landing facilities: Ice pier"**. It lists **2 ship visits a year in December, January and February**, peak population 20 staff plus 40 scientists, and 5 staff in winter. (SOURCED: COMNAP, *Germany Antarctic Station Catalogue*, Aug 2017.) "Ice pier" here is COMNAP's label for the natural ice-shelf-edge berth; no man-made pier is described in any AWI source read.

**How ships unload**
- 5 January 2017: *Polarstern* lay "alongside the ice shelf edge … north-eastern berth of Atka Sea Port". The shelf is about 150 m thick and free floating, with about 15 m above the sea surface, but "only 12 m" at the berth. (SOURCED: AWI weekly report.)
  - Landed: 24 containers, 1 Pistenbully and 320,000 L of fuel.
  - Taken back aboard: 32 t of return cargo. (SOURCED.)
  - Pistenbullys "tow sledge after sledge alongside the ship". (SOURCED: AWI weekly report text as returned by search.)
- 2 December 2005 (ANT-XXIII/2):
  - *Polarstern* reached the "Nordostanleger" (north-east berth). The station then lay 15 km inland; this was Neumayer II.
  - The first berth was rejected because a long crack meant a piece of shelf might break away under heavy loads.
  - A berth a few ship-lengths away was used instead: "a largely smooth, about 10 m high calving front without overhangs or deep crevasses", with an onshore wind holding the ship against the edge.
  - Pistenbullys, tank containers and container sledges had been staged on the shelf in advance. Unloading began two hours after arrival.
  - Landed: 30 containers, 200,000 L of fuel and 2 tracked vehicles, for the station and inland expeditions. (SOURCED: AWI *Wochenbericht Nr. 2*, ANT-XXIII/2, 2005, epic.awi.de 26906, German.)
- 18 December 2001 (*Polarstern* at the shelf edge): the shelf edge there was "ca. 40 m high", about 20 km east of Neumayer. Because of the height difference and environmental rules, fuel was pumped directly into tanks rather than craned. (SOURCED: perlentaucher.de "Die Polarstern legt an", 10 Feb 2002, German.)
- AWI IPY blog, 16 December 2007: sledge trains pulled by Pistenbullys carried containers "over 10 km of sea ice to the shelf ice edge". **Full tank containers could not go this way because they "would be too heavy and could be lost."** (SOURCED: IPY blog item 1176.)
- Royal Arctic Line, 23 November 2021: unloading "to the ice edge, which in some places can be up to 15 meters high". *Malik Arctica* delivered a crane of nearly 40 t for Neumayer III. RAL ships have served Neumayer III since 2007, starting with *Naja Arctica*. (SOURCED: ral.gl.)

**Distance from berth to station (as reported over the years)**
- 15 km (2005, Neumayer II).
- About 20 km (2001).
- 18 km "trail to northern shore" (AWI *Atka Xpress* blog, Feb 2021).
- 16 km and 19.5 km (search snippets only; UNVERIFIED).
- The station drifts toward the shelf edge with the ice. A snippet gives about 150 m a year (UNVERIFIED).

**Season and duration**
- For both 2024/25 and 2025/26, AWI's freight page says: "During about 3 days the containers are unloaded from the ship and fuel is bunkered."
- Ship arrival was planned for 15 January 2025 and for 3 January 2026, with cargo available at the station 5 to 7 days later. Both seasons used FS *Polarstern*. (SOURCED: AWI Spaces "Frachten / Freight" pages, updated 12 Dec 2024 and 7 May 2025, German.)

**Failure on record**
- *Naja Arctica* reached Atka Bay on 16 December 2007 carrying the Neumayer III construction cargo. "A sea ice barrier of several meters thickness" prevented her from tying up to the ice margin.
- *Polarstern* was diverted on 7 January and freed a channel to the ice edge on 16 January 2008, after more than a week of ramming.
- AWI had planned "roughly 30 percent extra time" into the schedule and was nearing the limit of it. (SOURCED: AWI press release, 16 Jan 2008.)

**Annual tonnage:** NOT FOUND as a single figure (see section 3).

**Kohnen (inland)**
- 757 km southeast of Neumayer III.
- Supplied by a convoy of 6 towing vehicles carrying up to 20 t each, with 17 sledges.
- Resupplied twice a year; the traverse takes 9 to 14 days. (SOURCED: Wikipedia, *Kohnen Station*.)
- In 2001/02: 7 Pistenbullys, each with 3 sledges, and 10 people; route about 758 km. (SOURCED: perlentaucher.de, 2002.)

#### Halley, Brunt Ice Shelf

**Relief method (BAS Final CEE for Halley VI, 2007)**
- Offloading takes place "onto seasonal sea ice formed within a small creek or bay in the Creek 2 area". Cargo goes onto sledges, is towed "across the sea ice, up a bulldozed snow ramp, and onto the ice shelf", then about 15 km to Halley V.
- If no creek or ramp is usable, a longer route from **N9, about 60 km** from Halley V, is used. There, cargo "can be offloaded from the ship directly onto the ice shelf".
- Cargo work runs around the clock in 24-hour daylight.
- Prefabricated module units were limited to about 9.5 t, "the expected bearing capacity of the sea ice for unloading".
- Plant listed:
  - a tracked mobile crane
  - 3 to 4 Nodwells
  - 4 Sno-cats
  - 2 Argo Challenger 765Bs
  - 2 John Deere 7820s with tracks
  - 4 bulldozers
  - telehandler, scissor lifts and cherry pickers
- The BAS resupply ship has a bulk fuel capacity of 150,000 L. Bulk fuel goes from ship to shelf in 5,000 L ski-mounted transit tanks into 24,000 L sledge tanks. The remaining 90,000 L arrives in drums.
- Annual fuel use is estimated at 240,000 L at Halley VI and 225,000 L at Halley V.
- *Ernest Shackleton* took about 12 days each way from Cape Town. During construction an estimated 72 ship-days were spent "tied up at the ice shelf".
- Construction cargo: 14,000 m³ in 2007/08 by a charter ship about twice the size of *Ernest Shackleton*, then 500 m³ in each of the next two seasons.
- Halley V population: 14 to 18 in winter, up to 70 during the two-month summer. Halley VI design: 16 in winter, 52 in summer. (All SOURCED: BAS, *Final CEE Halley VI*, NORA eprint 15413.)

**Other Halley facts**
- "Five potential relief sites … shelf heights between 12 and 25 meters … distance from station of between 15 and 20 km" (UNVERIFIED; snippet).
- A 50 km, four-hour haul by John Deere tractors and PistenBullys, and unloading of about 10 days in 24-hour shifts (Farmers Weekly; UNVERIFIED, page not opened).
- Ingenia: the shelf "protrudes 20 meters above sea level", so materials go onto "fragile sea ice with a maximum bearing capacity of only 9.5-metric tons" (UNVERIFIED; 403 on fetch, but it matches the CEE's 9.5 t).

**Recent change**
- 2023/24: *Malik Arctica* relieved Halley, "the first ship we've seen in 6 years". The work was completed in 9 days: 250 t of waste removed, 500 m³ of fuel bunkered, and food for 3 years. (SOURCED: Z-Fids Newsletter No. 55.)
- *Ernest Shackleton* left BAS charter on 30 April 2019. (SOURCED: Z-Fids No. 45.)
- From January 2026, BAS, the Norwegian Polar Institute and AWI share the chartered containership *Silver Mary* for Halley VI, Troll and Neumayer under a 10-year partnership. *Silver Mary* calls at Halley every other season. (SOURCED: Ocean News, 2 Dec 2025.)
- *Silver Mary*: built 2005 by Remontowa, Gdańsk; 6,365 dwt; 113 m (SOURCED: Maritime Executive snippet text, read only as a snippet; mark UNVERIFIED).
- Halley closed in winter 2017 and 2018 because of Chasm 1 and the Halloween Crack. Iceberg A-81 (about 1,550 km²) calved in January 2023 about 20 km from the station. (Search-result text from LiveScience and others; UNVERIFIED in detail.)

#### Other ice-shelf-front operations

- **Princess Elisabeth (Belgium), Breid Bay.**
  - The Belgian CEE says Breid Bay was used for unloading by Belgian (1958 to 1960), Belgian-Dutch (1964 to 1966) and Japanese (Asuka, 1986 to 1991) expeditions.
  - The CEE maps an access route of about 200 km. At "BEL-RAMP", access to the fast ice is through "a local recess … which forms a stable and gentle slope". An alternative at Polarhav Bay allows unloading "directly on the ice shelf (the ice cliff being less than 15 m high)", where "typically the fast ice completely disappears". (SOURCED: Belgian CEE text in env.go.jp PDF a-1_3.)
  - In one season the *Mary Arctica* lay against an ice shelf of **17 m** freeboard, "just the right height for the deck cranes".
    - Swell ripped out one mooring pin and snapped a second. The ship rose and fell "three or four meters with every swell" and held position on propeller and bow thrusters.
    - 27 containers, sledge-loads of timber and 2 vehicles were landed over about 2 days.
    - The station convoy was 4 tractors pulling 3 to 4 sledges each, a 20-hour drive.
    - In the same season the ship's primary stop, "Five Degrees East" for Troll, was "blocked by a massive pile-up of sea ice". (SOURCED: IPF, "Unloading at Breid Bay"; year not in retrieved text.)
  - Another season: *Vasily Golovnin* and *S.A. Agulhas II* unloaded, and three traverses moved about 85 + 150 + 80 = **about 315 t**. Prinoth tractors with Lehmann sledges travel at 10 to 12 km/h, and one traverse takes 22 to 25 h one way. (SOURCED: IPF, "BELARE team heads to coast to pick up sea cargo"; year not in retrieved text.)
- **Troll (Norway):**
  - 235 km from the coast. About 700 t was "hauled to the base from the ice shelf" for the 2005 expansion. (SOURCED: Wikipedia.)
  - *Mary Arctica* had supplied Troll "for the last 12 seasons". (SOURCED: RAL, 2021.)
- **SANAE IV (South Africa):**
  - 160 km from the ice-shelf edge. Summer population 110, winter 15 (2017). (SOURCED: Wikipedia.)
  - Ice-shelf operations at Penguin Bukta, with CAT-train traverses inland. (SOURCED: SANAP 2025 page, which gives no numbers.)
- **Novolazarevskaya (Russia):**
  - In March 2025, *Akademik Fedorov*, "following the classical scheme, moored at the ice barrier of Novolazarevskaya station and transferred diesel fuel to the barrier base". It also landed 50 drums of gasoline and 45 drums of Jet A-1.
  - Sledge-tractor traverse СГП 6-11 (8 people, 6 vehicles) went to the barrier base to haul cargo and support loading.
  - From 28 March, cargo work at the barrier was interrupted repeatedly by storm winds of up to 40 m/s. (SOURCED: AARI RAE operational summaries 04–10.03.2025 and 24–31.03.2025, Russian.)
- **Maitri (India):**
  - The "Indian Barrier" is the "cargo unloading site … on the shelf ice … about 100 km north" of Maitri.
  - Between 5 and 24 April 2012, a portion "nearly 2 km x 1 km" broke away with "some Indian tank containers and Russian cargo and fuel". It split into 10 small icebergs, and the cargo "in all probability has sunk". Seismic stations recorded five calving events between 20 and 28 April 2012.
  - In February and March 2012, neither the Russian icebreaker nor *Ivan Papanin* could reach the barriers. (SOURCED: NCPOR news 147.)
  - In 2025/26, *Vasily Golovnin* stopped about 6.5 miles from Maitri in 1.5 m consolidated ice and used helicopters (UNVERIFIED; snippet).

### 2.2 Question 2: Other precedents

**McMurdo and Scott Base**
- Until 1965, materials were offloaded directly onto sea ice. A thin-ice summer in 1964/65 prevented that, which led to the ice pier. (SOURCED: ASCE *Civil Engineering Source*, 26 Sep 2022, summarizing Wagner and Blaisdell, *J. Cold Regions Eng.*)
- The first ice pier dates from 1973. A steel dock was tried in 1972 and destroyed by a storm. (SOURCED: Wikipedia, *Ice pier*.)
- Annual cargo:
  - Feb 2019, *Ocean Giant*: 498 containers and breakbulk, "nearly 7 million pounds", plus 450 containers of retrograde.
  - Feb 2022: 503 pieces, "nearly 7 million pound". (SOURCED via the search-result text of MSC/Navy pages; the pages themselves returned 403. Treat as UNVERIFIED-strong.)
  - 2013: "nearly 7 million pounds" and "over 6 million gallons", 8-day offload, ice pier "500-foot". (SOURCED: Maritime Executive, 2013.)
  - 2023: *Ocean Giant* (19,382 dwt) offloaded 443 pieces onto a 65-ton Modular Causeway, because the ice pier was "severely damaged and unavailable". (SOURCED: Maritime Executive, 3 Feb 2023.)
  - 2026: *Plantijngracht* delivered 302 pieces from Port Hueneme plus 71 containers loaded in New Zealand, and took back 274 containers of retrograde. (SOURCED: DVIDS 562269, Apr 2026.)
- Cargo in pounds converted: 7,000,000 lb × 0.45359 kg/lb = **3,175 t** (COMPUTED).
- Fuel:
  - 2021/22, *Maersk Peary*: about 7.617 million USG over a 3-day, 24-hour operation with 40 people. Of this, 28.4 ML was AN8 diesel and 378,000 L was MOGAS.
  - Fuel was pumped through 1.6 km of lay-flat hose and travelled 2.5 km to 5 storage tanks of 7,560,000 L each.
  - The 8,324,000 USG "combined total" also counts fuel moved to and from *Polar Star* (439,700 USG) and HMNZS *Aotearoa* (271,000 USG). (SOURCED: NZDF, "Antarctic fuellies first in, last out", 20 May 2022.)
  - MSC called the same delivery "nearly 9 million gallons" (SOURCED: DVIDS 414272, 8 Feb 2022).
  - 2012: "approximately 6 million gallons" (SOURCED: gCaptain, 17 Apr 2012).
  - No MSC sealift ran in 2021 because of COVID-19. (SOURCED: DVIDS 414272.)
- *Maersk Peary*: 591 ft, 38,200 dwt, ice-class double-hull tanker, built in South Korea in 2004. (SOURCED: southpolestation.com 2011/12 shipping page.)
- Scott Base: the NZDF supplies about 45 personnel and a fleet of trucks for the ice-pier offload. In 2017 the ice channel was 35 km, and unloading took 4 days around the clock. (SOURCED: 1News, 19 Jan 2017.)
- McMurdo population: peak 800 staff plus 200 scientists; winter 150 staff plus 3 scientists; maximum 1,200 at once. (SOURCED: COMNAP, *USA Antarctic Station Catalogue*, Aug 2017.)

**Casey**
- January 2019: *Aurora Australis* anchored in Newcomb Bay, and a barge ran cargo to Casey wharf.
  - Over 550 t of cargo.
  - About 970,000 L of fuel, pumped in nearly 32 h through "nearly 2 km of hose".
  - 14 days in total. (SOURCED: AAD, This week at Casey, 11 Jan 2019.)
- 2021/22: *Nuyina* delivered "one million liters" of SAB in about 36 h, using ice lines and ship-to-shore hoses laid by barges. The general cargo ship *Happy Dragon* handled the cargo. (SOURCED: AAD, This week at Casey, 21 Jan 2022.) Another voyage delivered 1,030,400 L, "the most fuel to Casey Station in any one voyage" (UNVERIFIED; snippet).
- Failure: in 1998/99, *Aurora Australis* had an engine-room fire (22 July 1998) and the shipping program was rearranged, so Casey "did not receive its usual resupply". The replacement fuel was partly unsuitable below −38°C and only just enough with rationing. (SOURCED: AAD medal citation text, via search result; AAD SoE indicator 56 confirms "a year when … a resupply of fuel failed to take place".)

**Davis**
- Over-ice resupply: the ship breaks fast ice "up to 1.6 m thick" and stops about 1 km from Davis.
  - October and November 2025: 500 t of cargo, about 800,000 L of fuel and 140,000 L of water, in "just over a week". *Nuyina* spent six days at sea before reaching the fast ice. (SOURCED: AAD, "Voyage 1 update: resupplying Davis", 31 Oct 2025.)
  - An ice road is built from the station to the ship, a 2 km fuel hose is laid, and the ship's crane lands cargo onto vehicles at the shipside (SOURCED via AAD search-result text).
- In open water, cargo goes ship to barge to wharf (about 2 km each way) and the station crane lifts it onto trucks. Uniflote modular pontoons carry loads beyond the jet barge's capacity (SOURCED via AAD search-result text; pages not opened individually).
- Barge resupply, 25 to 28 January 2023, *Happy Diamond*: over 80 containers in and 52 out. The cumulative tally reached 366,217 kg = 67.85% complete on day 4. (SOURCED: AAD, This week at Davis, 3 Feb 2023.) Implied total: 366,217 / 0.6785 = **about 540 t** (COMPUTED).

**Mawson**
- The ship moors in Horseshoe Harbor and barges carry cargo ashore.

**Mirny**
- 1956: "the ships could approach close to shore, to a distance of 100 meters, no farther, so unloading … can be done straight onto the fast ice". The "conditions of unloading change as the fast ice breaks up. This lengthens unloading time." Cargo then went "from the unloading place to the settlement, a distance of 2.5 km." (SOURCED: AARI, *Russian Polar Research* no. 38, Senko diaries, Russian.)
- Fast ice near the station "reaches 30–40 km wide by the end of winter". Tanker delivery of fuel became "regular practice, about once every two years" after 1965.
  - Summer population was 160 to 200 and winter 40 to 50; from the 55th RAE, 15 to 20.
  - On 21 January 1956 a tractor with sledges broke through the ice and mechanic I. F. Khmara was killed.
  - Vostok traverses moved to Progress "in connection with the growth of the crack zone". (SOURCED: ru.wikipedia, *Мирный (антарктическая станция)*.)

### 2.3 Question 3: Engineering literature on ice piers, ramps, wharves and floating docks

**McMurdo ice pier**
- Method: seawater is flooded about 10 cm at a time over a bermed area and freezes within about 24 h. Two layers of steel cable are laid in. The pier is built from an initial 0.6 m of ice to about 6.7 m (22 ft) and topped with 15 to 20 cm of volcanic gravel.
- "Contemporary" size: about 240 m long × 90 m wide × 7 m thick (800 × 300 × 22 ft).
- Materials: about 6,300 m of steel cable. When a pier is disposed of, about 6,400 m of 25 mm cable and 200 m of 51 mm pipe sink.
- Lifespan: "three to five years".
- Chronology:
  - 1983: the *Southern Cross* incident, on a pier in its fourth season.
  - 1993: major damage from high winds and swell.
  - 2010: a "10-year storm washed pier out to sea", found again in summer 2011.
  - 1987: a Soviet ice pier at Mys Shmidta "more than 700 meters" long. (All SOURCED: Wikipedia, *Ice pier*.)
- First report: J. L. Barthelemy, *The Man-Made McMurdo Ice Wharf: History, Construction and Performance*, Civil Engineering Laboratory (Navy), Port Hueneme, February 1975 (DTIC ADA007527). It covers the wharf built in winter for Deep Freeze-73: "460-foot seaward face, a 635-foot backside face, a width of approximately 170 feet and a thickness approaching 29 feet." (UNVERIFIED: catalog abstract seen only in search results. DTIC returned 403 and openpolar.no has an anti-bot wall, so the report was not read.)
- A 1976 NCEL design and construction guide for a surface-flooded ice wharf exists (UNVERIFIED; snippet).
- The 2011/12 failure:
  - The 2010/11 pier "cracked, and was swept away in a powerful storm" and was found locked in sea ice.
  - The next pier was too thin: required about 20 ft, achieved 9 ft.
  - The US Army Modular Causeway System was flown and shipped in. 150 soldiers and 50 Navy sailors assembled the floating sections in 2.5 days and unloaded *Green Wave* over 8 days of 24-hour work. Disassembly took 36 h. (SOURCED: gCaptain, 17 Apr 2012; southpolestation.com 2011/12.)
  - The Modular Causeway was used again in 2019/20 and 2022/23 (SOURCED via the USAP page text in search results). It has 10 sections of 24 ft, weighs 65 tons, and draws 1 ft. (SOURCED: Maritime Executive, 2023.)
- Thermopile alternative: simulations put the time to create a bottomfast ice dock (40 m seaward × 100 m long) at **255 to 820 days**. The existing ice pier "requires deployment of a Bailey bridge", "a potential point for failure". (SOURCED: ASCE summary of Wagner and Blaisdell, "Expedient Sea Ice Infrastructure in a Cold Environment", *J. Cold Regions Eng.*, 2022.)

**The McMurdo steel barge pier ("NSF Discovery Pier")**
- 328 ft × 100 ft. Attached to shore by four stiff-leg columns, with two bridges for cargo.
- Ballast bins hold an iron-ore slurry of 300 lb/ft³. Hull steel is ABS grade.
- Gunderson contract: $43.5 million (November 2023). The NSF award to USACE was about $55 million. (SOURCED: southpolestation.com, barge pier page.)
- Towed by tug *Rachel* from 3 December 2025: 9,159 nmi in 69 days at an average 5.5 kn, via Honolulu, Pago Pago and Lyttelton. (SOURCED: Marine Log.) Check: 9,159 / 5.5 / 24 = 69.4 days (COMPUTED; consistent).
- Installed 28 March 2026, with piles 70 ft into the ice shelf. Design life is at least 20 years before shipyard maintenance.
- The raked hull "rises with forming ice, breaks through, and drops back" to avoid crushing.
- It removes about 1 week a season of causeway construction and the former 50-ton cargo maximum. The ice pier system "became unreliable starting in 2012 with multiple failures". (SOURCED: Baird Maritime vessel review.)

**Snow ramps, ice ramps and sea-ice bearing**
- Halley: bulldozed snow ramps in creeks, and a 9.5 t sea-ice limit per unit (see 2.1).
- Princess Elisabeth: a natural "stable and gentle slope" ramp to the fast ice (see 2.1).
- Gold's formula: bearing load P = A·h², with A recommended at 3.5 to 10 kg/cm² (lower bound for safety). Source: Gold, L. W. (1971), "Use of ice covers for transportation", *Can. Geotech. J.* 8(2):170–181. (SOURCED via Wikipedia, *Ice road*.)
  - For Davis fast ice of 1.6 m (160 cm) at A = 3.5 kg/cm²: P = 3.5 × 160² = 89,600 kg ≈ **90 t** (COMPUTED; illustrative only, no source applies this to Davis).
  - The ice thickness that gives Halley's 9.5 t at the same A: h = √(9,500 / 3.5) = **about 52 cm** (COMPUTED; illustrative only).

**Wharves**
- Rothera: a new 74 m wharf, £40 million, built over 18 months across two summers (November to May) by about 50 people.
  - 20 steel frames of 45 t each, over 1,000 t in all, backfilled with rock. 4,500 t of equipment was shipped from the UK (11,000 km).
  - Completed in April 2020 for RRS *Sir David Attenborough*. (SOURCED: Maritime Executive; Offshore Energy text in search results.)
  - It was the subject of a CEE (SOURCED via search-result text).
- Casey and Davis have wharves for barges (see 2.2). Halley, Neumayer and Mirny have no constructed wharf: COMNAP lists Mirny "Ship landing facilities: None" and Neumayer "Ice pier" (SOURCED: COMNAP 2017).

**Not found:** CRREL, *Antarctic Science* or *Cold Regions Science and Technology* papers specifically on ice-shelf-front ship berthing loads, cliff-edge stability under crane loads, or harbor design at a floating ice front (see section 3).

### 2.6 Question 6: Environmental and treaty constraints (real-world facts only; may not apply to the fiction)

- **Madrid Protocol** (Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty): adopted 4 October 1991, in force 14 January 1998 (SOURCED: AAD, *The Madrid Protocol*).
  - Article 7: "Any activity relating to mineral resources, other than scientific research, shall be prohibited." Modifying the Protocol requires unanimity until 2048 (SOURCED).
  - Annex I: environmental impact assessment, with a Comprehensive Environmental Evaluation (CEE) for activities with "more than a minor or transitory impact", circulated to the Committee for Environmental Protection (CEP) (SOURCED: AAD; ASOC text in search results).
  - Annex III: waste. Annex IV: marine pollution (discharge restrictions on oil, noxious substances, garbage and plastics). Annex V: protected areas, in force 24 May 2002. Annex VI: liability, not yet in force. (SOURCED: AAD.)
- **Real port and wharf works that went through EIA:**
  - Rothera Wharf reconstruction: a CEE, draft and final (SOURCED via search-result text).
  - Halley VI: a CEE in 2007 (SOURCED).
  - Jang Bogo station: a CEE, with a 1,100 L fuel spill reported to the ATCM (SOURCED).
  - Russian Vostok works: Initial Environmental Evaluations finding "no more than a minor or transitory impact" (SOURCED: ATCM BP 22).
- **Waste back-haul in practice:**
  - Halley 2023/24: 250 t of waste removed.
  - McMurdo 2019: 450 containers of retrograde. McMurdo 2026: 274 containers.
  - Princess Elisabeth sends all waste to South Africa "as the Madrid Protocol … requires".
  - Casey 2010/11: 1,005 t of contaminated soil removed. (SOURCED in the respective sources.)
- **Fuel handling:**
  - Neumayer: fuel is pumped rather than craned "for environmental reasons" (SOURCED, 2002).
  - Atka: AWI search text says fuel is not transferred over sea ice for ecological reasons (UNVERIFIED snippet). The 2007 blog says full tank containers are not taken over sea ice because they could be lost (SOURCED).
- **MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 43:** bans the use and carriage of heavy fuel oil in the Antarctic area from 1 August 2011. Only "vessels that are engaged in securing the safety of ships or in search-and-rescue operations are exempt" (SOURCED: Cruise Industry News / IAATO, Nov 2011). The density and viscosity thresholds (over 900 kg/m³ at 15°C; over 180 mm²/s at 50°C) and the "south of 60°S" area are UNVERIFIED (snippet).
  - This rule retired the old T-5 tankers and brought in *Maersk Peary* for McMurdo (SOURCED: southpolestation.com 2011/12).
- **Polar Code:** in force 1 January 2017 under SOLAS chapter XIV and MARPOL Annexes I, II, IV and V. Comminuted food waste (25 mm or less) may be discharged only at least 12 nmi from land, ice shelf or fast ice. (UNVERIFIED: search-result text from IMO and press pages, not read in full.)
- **Not found:** a CEP guideline specific to wharves, ice piers or ports.
