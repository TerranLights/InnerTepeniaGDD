## 1. Island and isolated immigration and quarantine stations

Each station is described on its own terms. Two kinds of station are covered, and the sources treat them differently. **Immigration stations** (Ellis Island, Angel Island) decided whether a person could enter, by legal and medical inspection. **Quarantine stations** (Grosse Île, Partridge Island and the Australian stations) held people until they were judged no longer a disease risk. A quarantine station did not decide whether a person could enter.

### 1a. Ellis Island (New York, 1892–1954)

**Transfer from ship.** "steerage passengers would be placed aboard a ferry or barge and brought to Ellis Island for their detailed inspection"; "First and second class passengers arriving in New York Harbor were not required to undergo the inspection process," receiving "a cursory inspection aboard the ship." **SOURCED** at the Statue of Liberty-Ellis Island Foundation, https://www.ellisisland.org/ellis-island/overview-history/ . A PHS medical officer, writing in 1912 and quoted by Unrau, describes the same split: "The boarding division had the responsibility for inspecting aliens in the first and second cabins on board incoming vessels." **SOURCED** at Unrau, *Historic Resource Study* vol. II, p. 610 (A. C. Reed, "The Medical Side of Immigration," *Popular Science Monthly*, April 1912), https://npshistory.com/publications/stli/hrs-v2.pdf . Before the 1921 reforms, arrivals could be held on the barges themselves while waiting for medical inspection. Commissioner Tod's 1921 reform list includes: "There will be no more waiting on the barge for medical inspection, but comfortable receiving rooms will be set aside for the new arrivals in the main immigration building." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 282, quoting *Survey* XLVI (1 August 1921).

**Medical inspection ("the line").** "incoming immigrants pass in single file down two lines. Each of these lines makes a right-angled turn midway in its course. At this turn stands a medical officer ... In case any positive or suspicious evidence of defect is observed, the immigrant receives a chalk mark indicating the nature of the suspicious circumstance. At the end of each line stands a second medical officer who does nothing but inspect eyes. He everts the eyelids of every person passing the line, looking for signs of trachoma ... All cases which have been marked on the line are separated from the others and sent to the medical examining rooms ... often 5,000 immigrants pass in a day." The hospital took "those acutely ill upon arrival, those taken sick during their stay on the island, and cases of acute sickness among aliens already landed who for some reason have been brought to the island for deportation." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 610-611, quoting Reed (1912).

**Legal inspection.** "Inspectors behind desks asked immigrants their name, home town, occupation, destination, and amount of money they carried"; "About one percent would be denied admission for violation of America laws"; immigrants "liable to become a public charge" were refused; women and children traveling alone "were detained until their safety was assured," usually needing "A telegram, letter, or prepaid ticket" from relatives; interpreters "often had to try several languages." **SOURCED** at NPS, https://www.nps.gov/places/000/historic-legal-inspection-2nd-floor.htm . The Foundation page gives "Only two percent of the arriving immigrants were excluded" and "the Ellis Island inspection process lasted 3 to 5 hours" for those whose papers and health were in order (**SOURCED**, Foundation page above). *The two exclusion figures (1% at NPS, 2% at the Foundation) differ; neither page gives the years or method.*

**Detention for special inquiry, and appeals.** In fiscal 1895 Commissioner Senner reported that "out of a total of 190,923 alien immigrants not less than 23,934 (12.53 per cent) were sent by the inspectors for a hearing before the board of special inquiry," under the law of 3 March 1893 requiring detention of "every person who may not appear to them to be clearly and beyond doubt entitled to admission." The monthly share "varies from 8.07 in September, 1894, to 29.9 in January, 1895," with "not less than 3,320 cases before the board of special inquiry in one month." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 209. In 1911, Commissioner Williams wrote: "In the cases of ships bringing a good class of immigrants, even 95 per cent may have passed inspection within three hours and less than 5 per cent been detained for special inquiry. On the other hand, where the class of immigrants is poor, it may under any correct execution of the law be necessary to detain as many as 30 per cent." He also described days "when we are compelled to receive 4,000 to 5,000 people." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 417 (Williams to Rep. Henry, June 1911). HIAS (Hebrew Immigrant Aid Society) handled 3,726 detained or excluded cases in 1913: "1,944 or 52.1 percent ... were admitted on rehearings"; on appeal, "461 were admitted, out of 736 appealed for by our Society, 146 ... out of 548 appealed for by others"; "6 died and one escaped; 1,199 Jewish immigrants of a total of 103,869 Jewish arrivals or 1.2 percent were deported." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 413 (HIAS annual report 1913). By 1921, some detainees "frequently ... have been held apart from the public for several weeks pending their examination by a special board of inquiry." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 280 (Husband memorandum, 16 November 1921).

**Who paid for detention and return.** 1902 bidding terms for the feeding concession: "The cost of maintenance of those detained was, generally speaking, paid for by the steamship companies (though in certain cases such companies are allowed to recover the amounts so paid from the immigrants)." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II, Appendix A, p. 300-301. Section 19 of the Immigration Act of 1907: "all aliens brought to this country in violation of law shall, if practicable, be immediately sent back ... to the country whence they respectively came, on the vessels bringing them. The cost of their maintenance while on land, as well as the expense of the return of such aliens, shall be borne by the owner or owners of the vessels on which they respectively came"; refusing to pay, charging the alien for return, or taking security from him was a misdemeanor. **SOURCED** at *United States v. Nord Deutscher Lloyd*, 223 U.S. 512 (1912), https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/223/223.US.512.611.html . *DERIVED: the carrier, not the state, bore the cost of a refused passenger's keep and return under the 1907 law.*

**Daily life in detention.** The 1902 feeding terms: "Detained immigrants (whose numbers varied greatly, there being sometimes as many as 1800 [OCR reads '1S00'] and sometimes not more than over 300), received three meals on each day of detention." The concession was paid "approximately the gross sum of $65,000" by the steamship companies in the year before 1902, at 10 cents for breakfast, 15 cents for dinner and 10 cents for supper. The menu was: breakfast "Bread and bowl of tea or coffee (with milk and sugar)"; dinner "Soup, boiled beef and potatoes ... with fish instead of beef on Fridays"; supper "Bread, bowl of tea or coffee (with milk and sugar) and stewed prunes." "the great majority left the Island within a few hours after their arrival"; about 482,900 immigrants were brought for inspection in the previous twelve months; a staff restaurant served "As many as 150 meals in one day." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II, Appendix A, p. 300-301 (1902 terms). The 1921 reforms added separate night quarters for women and young children, laundry for detainees, Sunday religious services, welfare workers "on duty at all hours," a system for passing allowed information between detainees and waiting friends, and an explanation of the reasons for exclusion given to those deported. **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 279-280. Commissioner Curran (1923–26) wrote of 1 July 1923, when about 2,000 "excess quota" arrivals were turned back: "In a week or two they all went back ... Day by day the barges took them from Ellis Island back to the ships again." On the food: "the impossibility of building a menu to suit the palates of sixty different national tastes. It was American food." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 287-288, quoting Curran, *Pillar to Post*.

**Staff.** Workforce on 1 April 1924: **493 employees**, of which the Boarding Division had 36 (29 inspectors), Deporting Division 31 (26 guards), Registry Division 37 (13 inspectors, 20 interpreters), Special Inquiry Division 27, Law Division 32, Information Division 27, Matron's Division 16, Night Division 52, Janitor's Division 59, Power Plant 52, Record Division 27, and Superintendent's Division including Marine 49 (with 4 pilots, 4 marine engineers, 8 marine firemen and 17 deckhands for the station's own boats). **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 295-298 ("List of Employees at Ellis Island by Divisions and Classes," 1 April 1924, RG 85). Public Health Service medical staff appear to be counted separately; Unrau's appendix E lists PHS employees on their own (**DERIVED** from the table of contents; not checked in detail). In March 1921 there were "about 780 employees at the station" (**SOURCED**, Unrau vol. II p. 292). In 1953 the District Director remarked that "there are only 237 detainees on the island, but there are 250 employees to handle them!" (**SOURCED**, NPS chronology).

**Throughput and change of role.** Opened 1 January 1892. On opening day of the new main building (17 December 1900), "2,251 immigrants were received" (**SOURCED**, Foundation page; the NPS chronology agrees). 1907 was the peak year: the Foundation says "over one million"; the NPS chronology says "1.2 million aliens are examined." *The two figures differ.* The single-day record of 11,747 (17 April 1907) is **UNVERIFIED** (secondary pages only). After the 1924 Act provided "for primary inspection of prospective immigrants at American consulates in the countries of origin, Ellis Island rapidly lost the basic function for which it had been created and became primarily a detention and deportation center." Thereafter "only those immigrants whose status in this country was questioned, whose papers were not in order, or who required medical treatment were sent to Ellis Island." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II p. 207 and p. 275. The NPS chronology says "Immigrants are now required to obtain visas at American consulates before embarking for America" (1924); about 1,000 enemy aliens were detained in May 1942; INS functions except detention moved to Manhattan in 1943; the last detainees were removed on 12 November 1954 (**SOURCED**).

### 1b. Angel Island (San Francisco Bay, 1910–1940)

**Purpose of the island site.** The 1910 station replaced "an old two-story shed at the wharf" and "would prevent Chinese immigrants from communicating with those in San Francisco, isolate immigrants with communicable diseases, and, like the prison on nearby Alcatraz Island, be escape proof." **SOURCED** at AIISF *Previsit Guide* 2018, https://www.aiisf.org/s/AIIS_Previsit_Guide-2018.pdf .

**Who went to the island.** "Most were detained on Angel Island from two weeks to six months, until their applications were approved. Many were denied entry. By contrast, European immigrants and first class passengers usually faced only an inspection on board the ship and were never detained on Angel Island." **SOURCED** at California State Parks brochure, https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/468/files/AngelIsland2007reprint.pdf . How arrivals were physically moved from ship to island is **NOT FOUND**.

**Processing.** "All entering Chinese had to prove their identity by matching details of their lives with the answers of their relatives in the United States. Inspectors had wide discretionary power"; "Processing and questioning of new arrivals took weeks and sometimes months and years." **SOURCED**, AIISF guide. The applicant went before "a Board of Special Inquiry, composed of two immigrant inspectors," with "a stenographer, and, if needed, a translator"; questioning could last "a few hours or days"; appeals resulted "in a prolonged stay at the detention center." **SOURCED at Wikipedia**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_Island_Immigration_Station .

**Numbers and length of stay.** AIISF figures, citing Lee and Yung (2010), NARA RG 85 and the Commissioner-General's annual reports: 550,469 arrivals (341,363 noncitizens) and 665,430 departures over 1910–1940; estimated detainees included Chinese 100,000 and Japanese 85,000; "Chinese immigrants represented one-third of all detained individuals"; Asians were detained at about 2:1 compared with non-Asians; "We believe 60% of all arrivals were held ... for at least three days"; Chinese detainees were held on average "three weeks to three months"; the longest known stays were 756 days and 600 days. Capacity was "between 700 to 1,000 individuals," with barracks for 230 to 350 men, a hospital for 60 to 80 patients, and women in the administration building (200 to 240). **SOURCED** at https://aiisf.org/vault/bythenumbers . Share refused: about 18% (**SOURCED at Wikipedia**, which cites a secondary source); Facing History's 33% figure is **UNVERIFIED** (snippet only). *The two figures differ.*

**Daily life.** The barracks were "cramped. Detainees slept, ate, and played in rooms that were unfamiliar to them" (**SOURCED**, AIISF guide). Detainees carved poems into the wooden walls (**SOURCED**, AIISF guide and State Parks brochure). Food, recreation rules and staff numbers: **NOT FOUND**.

**End.** A 1940 fire "destroyed the administration building and women's quarters"; "about 200" detainees were moved to a facility in San Francisco; the station operated "from January 21, 1910, to November 5, 1940." **SOURCED at Wikipedia**.

### 1c. Grosse Île (St. Lawrence River below Quebec, 1832–1937)

**Role and procedure.** "vessels and luggage were inspected and disinfected, while passengers were examined and vaccinated"; the station sought to "prevent sick immigrants from coming into contact with healthy travelers or people under observation"; operations from 1832 were "characterized by haste, improvisation, and trial and error." Medical superintendent F. Montizambert (1869–1899) relaunched operations. Late-19th to early-20th-century building included "modern first-, second- and third-class hotels" for travelers, plus "hospitals, travelers' accommodations, workers' houses, chapels." In later years it "treated almost exclusively minor childhood infections such as diphtheria, chicken pox and measles." It closed in 1937. **SOURCED** at Parks Canada, https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/qc/grosseile/culture/histoire-history/evolution .

**Volume.** "Around 1830, an average of 30 000 immigrants arrived annually in Québec"; "some 100 000 sailed for Québec in 1847"; "100 000 newcomers arrived in 1910, 170 000 in 1912 and 225 000 in 1914." **SOURCED**, same Parks Canada page. (These are arrivals at Quebec, all of which passed the station; how many were landed on the island is not stated.)

**1847.** The station was overwhelmed. Ships with fever "were required to fly a blue flag"; on 5 June "25,000 Irish immigrants were quarantined on Grosse Isle itself or waiting in the ships anchored nearby"; the medical superintendent required "healthy passengers to stay on ship for fifteen days once the sick had been removed," then dropped the rule as unenforceable; before 1847 "invalids were placed in hospitals while the healthy carried out their quarantine periods in sheds"; "3,452 died at the quarantine station"; four doctors died of typhus. **SOURCED at Wikipedia**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grosse_Isle (several of these sentences carry no citation or are tagged "citation needed" there, so treat them as weaker). The later disinfection building "features the original showers, waiting rooms and steam disinfection apparatus" (**SOURCED at Wikipedia**). *Closure date: Parks Canada gives 1937; Wikipedia gives 1932, tagged "citation needed". Parks Canada is the stronger source.*

### 1d. Partridge Island (Saint John harbor, New Brunswick)

About 1 km from the shore of west Saint John. It was designated a national historic site in 1974 for its "significant role ... in immigration to Canada ... in the 19th century." The station offered "treatment for immigrants and crewmembers who were ill" and "purification facilities for the healthy passengers aboard the ships." In 1847, "2000 Irish immigrants fleeing from the potato famine were quarantined here during a typhus epidemic"; "601 of them are buried in a mass grave on the island." **SOURCED** at Parks Canada DFHD, https://pc.gc.ca/apps/dfhd/page_nhs_eng.aspx?id=191 . Wikipedia gives first quarantine use in 1816, a hospital in 1830, "30,000 immigrants were processed by the island's visiting and resident physicians" in the 1840s, "1,196 dying at Partridge Island and the adjacent city of Saint John" in 1847, and "over 78,000 immigrants a year being examined or treated" in the 1890s (the last figure uncited). **SOURCED at Wikipedia**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partridge_Island_(Saint_John_County) . *The 2,000 (Parks Canada) and 30,000 (Wikipedia, citing the McCord Museum) figures measure different things (quarantined, versus processed by physicians) and are not necessarily in conflict.* Procedure, length of stay and staff: **NOT FOUND**. ### 1e. Australian quarantine stations

**Torrens Island (Port Adelaide, South Australia, 1855–1980 for people).** This is the most detailed source found on standard 20th-century Australian quarantine procedure. **SOURCED** throughout at SA Heritage Council, *Summary of State Heritage Place 26583* (provisionally entered 14 March 2024), https://cdn.environment.sa.gov.au/environment/docs/26583-Summary_Torrens-Island-QSC.pdf :
- Before land quarantine, "Quarantine in South Australia initially occurred on-board vessels, usually anchoring off the coast near Semaphore"; the 1850 Act allowed the sick to be "moved to another vessel and treated there." The land station "was established rapidly out of necessity in 1855 to accommodate passengers of the Taymouth Castle," which had smallpox and cholera aboard.
- **Access by water only.** The 1879 passenger jetty "was the only official point of access to the quarantine station until the construction of the causeway ... in 1962"; "In 1921-1922 a transverse ramp to allow safe passenger boarding was constructed." A tramway ran from the jetty to the luggage block.
- **The Commonwealth standard sequence (from 1909, under the Quarantine Act 1908).** Contacts waited in a 1915 waiting room "for their clothing to be sterilized before progressing to the bathing block," with men's and women's rooms and "separate external entrances to each room to prevent direct contact between attendants and contacts." The laundry sterilized and dried clothing; "A sterilized change of clothes would be tagged and placed in individual bathing cubicles for each person." The 1916 bathing block, built "from a standard plan drawn by the Australian Quarantine Service," was "Used for the personal disinfection of each contact entering quarantine," with "separate 'foul' and clean corridors, entrances, and exits"; "Contacts were disinfected using carbolic acid and hot water"; "Each cubicle had an undressing, disinfecting and clean dressing space"; it had "baths for the first-class passengers, and showers for the second and steerage classes, however in practice the baths were reserved for women and the showers for men." Luggage went to a 1915 block with a "steam autoclave and fumigation chamber"; "Attendants were required to shower, disinfect and change into sterilized clothes before re-entering" the clean bays. "After bathing and vaccination, contacts queued for linen ... before moving to their accommodation."
- **Separation.** Facilities were "separated by class, gender and marital status." A kitchen was designed so "kitchen staff could deliver food to a servery while having no direct contact with nursing staff."
- **Episodes.** On 30 December 1918 the troopship *Boonah* arrived with influenza, and "112 passengers were quarantined," followed by "hundreds of other returning South Australian soldiers." The last large group was the migrant ship *Stathaird* in 1954. "only six patients came through the Quarantine Station between 1960 and 1970." It closed to people in 1980, as "mass air travel" replaced "sea travel," and remained an animal station until 1995.
- **Detention of another kind on the same island.** A WWI internment camp opened on 9 October 1914, about 500 m from the jetties. By its closure in August 1915 there were "almost 400 internees ... of whom 350 were German subjects."

**North Head, Sydney (1832–1984).** "The complex operated as a quarantine station from 14 August 1832 to 29 February 1984"; "at least 580 vessels were quarantined," "more than 13,000 people were quarantined at the station," and "an estimated 572 died and were buried there"; the layout imposed "class distinctions in the landscape," including "the lateral and elevational separation of the Asian accommodation" and "barrier fences"; "the ship was fumigated and scoured for return to the owner with the minimum delay"; surviving equipment includes "The fumigation chamber, shower blocks and autoclaves"; after WWII it was used as a detention center for illegal immigrants. **SOURCED at Wikipedia** (quoting the NSW State Heritage Register H01003), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Head_Quarantine_Station . The quarantine period was "originally of 40 days" per a National Archives snippet (**UNVERIFIED**; the NAA page could not be opened).

**Point Nepean (Victoria, 1852–1980).** The 1852 *Ticonderoga* arrived with typhus and scarlet fever and passengers "set up tents on the shore." In the 1888–89 limestone buildings, two hospitals on the hill held the sick and three on the flats housed "healthy passengers"; from 1870, "hospitals 3 and 4 on the flats were for steerage class." In the early 1900s a tramway took luggage from the pier "into a sanitation chamber where they are heated." In the 1860s, after an inquiry, "staff numbers [were] reduced to just two people." Influenza huts "processed 300 ships between 1918 and 1919," mostly with returning soldiers. Later the site housed "Kosovar refugees"; it "ceased operation as a quarantine station in 1980." **SOURCED at Wikipedia**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_Nepean_Quarantine_Station .

**Woodman Point (near Fremantle, Western Australia, to 1979).** "approximately 350-400 vessels ... came under quarantine in Western Australia"; "Several staff and their families resided at the Station throughout its operation"; in 1918 the *Boonah*'s most unwell soldiers were sent there, and "A total of 27 soldiers, and 4 nurses died of influenza"; nurses volunteered in such numbers that names were drawn from a hat; it closed in 1979. **SOURCED** at the Friends of Woodman Point site, https://www.woodmanpointquarantinestation.com/history-of-the-quarantine-station (a volunteer site, not a government source; the Torrens Island heritage summary independently gives the same 27 soldiers and 4 nurses). ## 2. Present-day remote or transit-only border processing; pre-clearance; entry to Antarctica today

### 2a. US preclearance (US border inspection done abroad, before boarding)

- **What it is and what it does for the traveler.** "Generally, travelers who are inspected at a preclearance facility are permitted to arrive at a U.S. domestic facility and exit the U.S. domestic terminal upon arrival, or connect directly to a U.S. domestic flight, without further CBP processing. Preclearance facilities primarily serve to identify known and unknown threats to U.S. national security before arrival into the United States, thereby relieving congestion at federal inspection facilities in the United States." **SOURCED** at *Federal Register* 91 FR 16012 (31 March 2026), https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-03-31/pdf/2026-06142.pdf (the notice itself is FR Doc. 2026-06164 within that issue).
- **Volume.** "In Fiscal Year (FY) 2025, over 22 million aircraft travelers were processed at preclearance facilities. This figure represents more than 15 percent of all commercial aircraft travelers cleared by CBP in FY 2025." For the new Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport facility (opened 10 March 2026), CBP projected "300,000–350,000 travelers" in FY 2026 and "1 million travelers" annually in ten years. "Preclearance operations have been ongoing in Toronto, Ontario, at Toronto Pearson International Airport since 1952." The basis is the "2015 Agreement on Land, Rail, Marine, and Air Transport Preclearance" between the US and Canada, implemented locally by a CBP–Toronto Port Authority memorandum of understanding (21 November 2024). **SOURCED**, same notice.
- **Where and staffing.** CBP has "more than 600 officers and agriculture specialists stationed at 16 Preclearance locations in 6 countries": Ireland (Dublin, Shannon), Aruba, Bermuda, United Arab Emirates (Abu Dhabi), the Bahamas (Nassau), and ten in Canada. **SOURCED** at https://www.cbp.gov/border-security/ports-entry/operations/preclearance . *Five of the 16 locations are on islands (Aruba, Bermuda, Nassau, Dublin, Shannon). This is DERIVED from the list, not a statement by CBP.* Inspection covers immigration, customs and agriculture (search-result snippet: **UNVERIFIED** at the page; the CBP page read mentions "agriculture specialists").
- **The host country's terms (Canada).** Under Canada's *Preclearance Act, 2016*, s. 29, "every traveler bound for the United States may withdraw from preclearance" unless detained, and may then leave without going to the United States (s. 30 requires truthful answers to the questions put on withdrawal). Strip searches are requested of a Canadian border services officer (s. 22(2)). A preclearance officer who detains a traveler for a monitored bowel movement "must deliver the traveler into the custody of a border services officer as soon as feasible" (s. 23(2)), and may not transfer a traveler to a medical facility ("A preclearance officer is not permitted to do so," s. 24(3)). A false statement to a preclearance officer is an offense with a fine of up to $5,000 (s. 37). A refusal of preclearance or of entry to the US cannot be judicially reviewed in Canada (s. 40). **SOURCED** at https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-19.32/page-2.html and page-3.html . *DERIVED: the foreign border agency works on host-country soil under host-country law, and coercive steps beyond questioning pass to host-country officers.* What happens physically to a refused traveler (whether they simply leave the airport) is **NOT FOUND** at a primary source.

### 2b. Juxtaposed controls (two countries' checks in one place, before boarding)

Juxtaposed controls are a "reciprocal arrangement between Belgium, France, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom" under which border checks happen "before boarding the train or ferry, rather than upon arrival after disembarkation." Customs checks still generally happen on arrival, except on the Eurotunnel Shuttle. The legal sequence: the Sangatte Protocol (signed 1991, in force 1993, Eurotunnel); a tripartite agreement (15 December 1993, Eurostar London–Brussels); an Additional Protocol (29 May 2000, Eurostar stations); the Treaty of Le Touquet (4 February 2003, ferry ports); and a quadripartite arrangement adding the Netherlands (7 July 2020). On the Eurotunnel Shuttle, people refused UK entry are handed to the French Border Police, who "release them outside the terminal area"; at the Dover/Calais ferry ports they are released outside the port area. Refusals: "over 56,000 instances" in 2016 (citing a Home Office fact sheet, 2017); 2,232 at the Eurotunnel Calais terminal and 2,442 at Paris Gare du Nord and Lille-Europe in 2002–03 (citing Lords Hansard, 7 July 2003). Other examples listed: the Buquebus ferry (Uruguayan immigration and customs cleared in Buenos Aires before boarding); Hong Kong West Kowloon (a Mainland Port Area where mainland law applies); Shenzhen Bay; Woodlands (Singapore/Malaysia); and joint controls at Princess Juliana Airport, Saint Martin (France/Netherlands). **SOURCED at Wikipedia**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juxtaposed_controls (primary documents cited there not opened). *DERIVED: under these arrangements a refused traveler is never carried. The refusal happens on the departure side, so the carrier bears no return cost. This is the reverse of Ellis Island, where the carrier paid (item 1a).*

### 2d. How entry to Antarctica works today

(The "Australian Antarctic Territory is not considered part of Australia under the Migration Act 1958" statement, the international movement recorded at Hobart, and the absence of any border post at Wilkins, Phoenix, Union Glacier or Marsh are already in `Casey_Mirny_Airport_Operations_Research_2026-10-29.md` item 6 and are not repeated here.)

- **Treaty level: notice, not entry control.** Antarctic Treaty Article VII(5): "Each Contracting Party shall ... give them notice in advance, of (a) all expeditions to and within Antarctica, on the part of its ships or nationals, and all expeditions to Antarctica organized in or proceeding from its territory; (b) all stations in Antarctica occupied by its nationals; and (c) any military personnel or equipment." Article VII(2): observers "shall have complete freedom of access at any time to any or all areas of Antarctica." Article VIII(1): observers and exchanged scientists "shall be subject only to the jurisdiction of the Contracting Party of which they are nationals," without prejudice to positions on jurisdiction "over all other persons in Antarctica." Article IV freezes territorial claims. **SOURCED** at the Antarctic Treaty text, https://documents.ats.aq/recatt/att005_e.pdf .
- **United Kingdom: a permit to enter.** Antarctic Act 1994, s. 3(1): "No person who is on a British expedition may enter or remain in Antarctica" except in accordance with a permit. An expedition is British if it "was organized in the United Kingdom" or if "the place of final departure for Antarctica" was in the UK (s. 3(3)). Operators and masters of the vessel or aircraft are liable too (s. 3(7)). Exceptions: high-seas transit to a destination outside Antarctica, and commercial fishing (s. 3(2)). An expedition "organized in and authorized in writing by another Contracting Party" is not treated as British (s. 3(4)). **SOURCED** at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/15/section/3 .

### 3b. Historical shipboard inspection (New York, c. 1900–1950)

- **The sequence in New York harbor (1904 description).** Before sailing, "At the port of embarkation the immigrants' names are recorded upon lists or manifests, each list containing about thirty names," answering the questions set by section 12 of the 1903 Act; "The master or first officer and the ship's surgeon were required to make oath before an immigration officer at the port of arrival that the manifest lists were ... true"; each alien carried a card with list and line number. On arrival, "Upon entering New York Harbor the ocean liners were boarded by state quarantine officers, and the immigrants inspected for quarantinable disease such as cholera, smallpox, typhus fever, yellow fever, and plague. Then the immigrant inspectors and a medical officer of the Public Health and Marine Hospital Service boarded the vessel and examined the cabin passengers, paying particular attention to the second cabin ... [which] was the route most often employed by persons who found it necessary to evade the law. After the completion of the cabin inspection the ship's surgeon reports any cases of sickness among the aliens in the ship's hospital ... The immigrants are then taken from the ship upon barges to the immigrant station, Ellis Island." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II pp. 590-592 (quoting the *Annual Report of the Commissioner General of Immigration*, 1904, pp. 106-07) (two OCR errors in the text layer, "makr" and "typus," are corrected in the quotation above), https://npshistory.com/publications/stli/hrs-v2.pdf .
- **Inspection moved upstream to the port of embarkation.** In 1899 a Marine Hospital Service surgeon "had been detailed to Naples, Italy, where he inspected aliens before leaving for America ... his findings were merely advisory to the steamboat companies ... he had no legal right to forbid undesirable immigrants from embarking, [but] he informed the steamship companies that certain aliens would be subject to deportation if carried to United States ports. The steamship companies were subject to fines in such cases besides the expense of returning the immigrants." In two days he "had rejected on one vessel bound for New York 4 cases of trachoma, and on another, 19 cases." The experiment continued "at least through 1906 by which time 43,196 would-be immigrants were rejected at Naples." The 1904 report adds that "the imposition of one hundred dollars fine for bringing to our ports any case of a contagious character, have occasioned some improvement in the inspection made by ships' doctors at European ports." **SOURCED** at Unrau vol. II pp. 582-583 and 590 (citing the Commissioner-General's Annual Reports for 1899 and 1904, and the Surgeon-General's Annual Report for 1906, p. 60). In 1900, "448,572 immigrants were inspected by Marine Hospital Service officers at the Port of New York," a task for "the eight medical officials and one steward on duty" (**SOURCED**, Unrau vol. II p. 583).
- **Mid-century liners.** Customs officers, "along with Public Health and Immigration officers, would board a small US Customs cutter" and meet the ship "at the entrance to New York Harbor"; customs inspectors then had "about two hours to go over the passengers' customs declaration forms" before the pier; at the pier "even a straightforward inspection would take at least 10 minutes"; "The ideal ratio would be one inspector for every 10 passengers"; for the *Queen Elizabeth* on 11 May 1949, "159 inspectors were called" for "nearly 2,000 passengers and over 6,700 pieces of baggage"; passengers could wait "up to 2 hours in the pier building." **SOURCED** at South Street Seaport Museum, https://southstreetseaportmuseum.org/arriving-in-the-port-of-new-york/ .

## 4. Biosecurity and medical screening for remote arrivals

(Already known and not repeated: Annex II Art. 4; the CEP Non-native Species Manual 2011; AAD cargo biosecurity and the 2013 Hobart hub; the AAD aviation IEE mitigations; ALE gear checks with pressure washers; DAP and IAATO briefings; AAD and US medical standards (45 CFR 675); the AAD 2020 Hobart isolation; INACH's 2020 Punta Arenas protocol. ### 4a. A remote island with ship-only access: South Georgia (GSGSSI, 2026-27 season)

**SOURCED** throughout at GSGSSI, *Standard Operating Procedure: Visitors*, https://gov.gs/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/01_SOP_Visitors.pdf , and *Standard Operating Procedure: Vessels*, https://gov.gs/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/02_SOP_vessles.pdf :
- **Everyone is a visitor.** The territory "has no permanent human population. Everyone entering the Territory is therefore considered a visitor," including tourists, scientists, "Government staff" and "HM Armed Forces personnel."
- **Before arrival.** Every visitor receives a biosecurity briefing from the trip organizer and "must watch the official GSGSSI visitor briefing film before arrival"; a briefing on arrival by a Government officer happens "Only in exceptional circumstances." Fresh fruit, vegetables, meat, eggs and unpasteurized dairy "must not be brought"; "Poultry products are of particular concern." Packing advice: new clothing where possible, no Velcro or mesh, open-tread boots, bags stored "off the ground" away from rodents.
- **Pre-border check at sea.** "after leaving the last port outside the Territories, but before making their first landing, all visitors must thoroughly check all their clothing and equipment" for soil, seeds, animal material, invertebrates and "Animal damage (e.g. gnaw marks)"; masters and expedition leaders ensure that daypacks and camera bags "are brushed and vacuumed."
- **Each landing.** "Boot washing is mandatory before going ashore and again on return to the vessel for every landing"; boots are "scrubbed clean and dipped in an approved biocide (e.g. Virkon S)"; boats and tenders are inspected for rodents and organic material before embarking passengers and before leaving shore; "Government officers may inspect boot-washing facilities, procedures, vessels, staff and passengers prior to disembarkation."
- **Audit by sampling.** Every vessel gets "a comprehensive biosecurity audit on their first visit of the season." Passing earns "'Trusted Vessel' status" and smaller audits. Sample sizes come from the binomial model "n ≥ ln(1−D) / ln(1−p)" with an assumed 5% non-compliance: all passengers are checked on vessels of 1–12 (100% detection), 36 on vessels of 300+ (~84% detection). The regime targets "approximately 50–80% detection confidence." Officers also check "the clothing, boots and equipment of 3-5 expedition staff" before the shore team lands. Selection is random unless risk indicators direct otherwise. Each passenger is scored pass or fail as a unit. Corrective actions are "cleaning, re-inspection, education of passengers, or increased inspection effort."
- **Vessels and rodents.** "South Georgia & the South Sandwich Islands can only be accessed by ship." A "valid Ship's Sanitation Certificate must be in date upon entry"; rat guards are required alongside at all prior ports. Detector-dog searches in Stanley (Falklands) are risk-based and "take less than one hour," and are mandatory within 24 hours before departure for the government ship, the national research ship, and Royal Navy vessels using the King Edward Point jetty. Up to six non-toxic bait boxes and monitoring stations are carried and checked on entry, then on the 1st and 15th of each month for long stays. "Any vessel believed to have rodents on board will be required to leave the Territory"; re-entry follows only once GSGSSI is satisfied the vessel is free of rodents. Jetty use is restricted to listed vessels, and gangways are raised overnight where practicable.

### 4b. A remote island reached by air: Lord Howe Island (NSW, Australia)

Aviation requirements: visiting aircraft must not carry "live plants, mulch, soil or live animals" without written approval; on the day of departure, all cabin and hold spaces, wheel wells and "all luggage/cargo" are visually inspected for rodents, reptiles, amphibians and invertebrates, and "a period of darkness is not to pass between the completion of inspections and departure"; any soil or seeds found are removed and contacted items treated with "an 80% methylated spirits/water mixture"; cabin and hold are "sprayed with a commercially available insect knockdown spray or fogged with a pyrethroid (bifenthrin) insecticide"; "All arrivals to Lord Howe Island are required to provide applicable biosecurity information" on a declaration under the NSW Biosecurity Act 2015, listing "Ports visited in the last 14 days," handed to the "biosecurity inspection officer." **SOURCED** at LHIB, *Biosecurity Requirements: Aviation* (August 2020), https://www.lhib.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-09/Guidelines%20-%20Lord%20Howe%20Island%20Biosecurity%20-%20Aviation%20-%20Aug%202020.PDF . ### 4c. Remote Antarctic program practice (additions to what is already known)

- **AAD expeditioners (2024).** "Ensure all clothing, footwear and personal effects are free of Biosecurity Risk Material (BRM)," including new items; clean items are packed in clean or new boxes or bins with openings sealed; "Polystyrene beads, chips or similar packaging are prohibited"; in Antarctica and the sub-Antarctic, clothing and equipment are cleaned "between locations, such as different wildlife colonies or ice-free areas." **SOURCED** at https://www.antarctica.gov.au/antarctic-operations/expeditioner-hub/biosecurity/ (updated 28 May 2024). ### 4d. What isolation does to infection risk: isolated communities and wintering stations

- **An Antarctic wintering base (published 1971).** "Thirteen men wintering on an Antarctic base were isolated from other human contact for 10 months"; an administered Coxsackievirus A21 "apparently spread to uninoculated men," with "repeated re-infections" and persistence "for most of the period of isolation"; "An outbreak of respiratory illness occurred at the end of isolation and its origin was traced. No causative agent was detected." **SOURCED** at Holmes, Allen, Bradburne and Stott, *Journal of Hygiene* 69(2): 187-199 (1971), record at https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/526462 .
- **Tristan da Cunha (1971 epidemic).** "Respiratory disease on Tristan da Cunha has been observed since the islanders returned in 1962. An epidemic of unprecedented severity occurred in the winter of 1971 ... involved 273 (96%) of 284 islanders, 92 of whom had two attacks ... The epidemic was apparently introduced by the *Tristania* ... There were two deaths, both in elderly persons with chronic chest disease and heart failure ... influenza A2 of the Hong Kong serotype H3N2." **SOURCED** at Mantle and Tyrrell, "An epidemic of influenza on Tristan da Cunha," *J Hyg (Lond)* 71(1): 89-95 (1973), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2130434 . That ship visits "have virtually always been followed by a round of colds," and that islanders have fewer colds than less isolated communities (Shibli et al. 1971), are **UNVERIFIED** (snippets only).
- **Other station studies (UNVERIFIED, snippets only):** a cold outbreak at a British Antarctic base after 17 weeks of complete isolation, spreading to half the base within a week; at McMurdo (1977), when 125 newcomers mixed with 75 men who had wintered, 89% were susceptible to adenovirus 21 but only 15% were infected; at the South Pole (1976), outbreaks 8 and 20 weeks into isolation.
- *DERIVED, from the SOURCED items only: a fully isolated small population can still carry and pass a virus during isolation (1971 base study); the arrival of an outside vessel was followed by a near-total epidemic in an isolated island population (Tristan 1971).*

### 4e. Entry controls to protect isolated communities (Australia, 2020)

Under the Biosecurity Act 2015, a 2020 emergency determination required that "A person must remain outside a designated area" (remote communities in Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia and the Northern Territory), except for listed classes. For ordinary entrants, all of these had to apply: no symptoms immediately before entry; no unprotected exposure "in the 14 days immediately before the entry"; not "outside Australian territory" in those 14 days; and one of several conditions, of which the first was that "throughout the 14 days before entering, the person: (a) has been isolated from the general public; and (b) has not had any signs or symptoms." Other routes were entry for an "essential activity" (health care, policing, emergency services and others) "in a manner that is agreed with a human biosecurity officer so as to minimize the extent to which other persons in the area are exposed," permission from a "relevant decision-maker," and transit by "the most direct practicable route" without exposure. People entering on those routes "must take reasonable steps ... to minimize the extent to which anyone else in the area is exposed." **SOURCED** at *Biosecurity (Human Biosecurity Emergency) (Human Coronavirus with Pandemic Potential) (Emergency Requirements for Remote Communities) Determination 2020*, Compilation No. 1 (8 April 2020), ss 5-6, https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2020L00324/2020-04-08/2020-04-08/text/original/pdf .

## FINAL: what the precedents show about processing everyone at one isolated point or at sea

Facts only; no recommendation and no design. Each line rests on the items above.

**What the sources support**
1. **Isolated processing points are old and well documented, and they were chosen for isolation.** Angel Island was sited to stop detainees communicating with the mainland, to isolate the sick, and to "be escape proof" (1b). Quarantine stations were put on islands because "the surrounding water provid[ed] a natural barrier" (1e, Torrens Island). Access was by water only; Torrens Island had one official jetty until a causeway was built in 1962 (1e).
2. **At the big immigration island, most people passed quickly and a minority were held.** At Ellis Island, inspection took "3 to 5 hours" for those in order. Detention for special inquiry ran from 12.5% of arrivals (FY1895, monthly 8–30%) to "less than 5 per cent" or "as many as 30 per cent" depending on the ship (1911). Exclusion was about 1–2%. Daily volume of 4,000–5,000 was strained. A 1924 workforce was 493, with PHS medical staff counted separately (1a). Angel Island held 60% of arrivals for three days or more (1b).
3. **Detention capacity and daily life were a large part of the operation.** Ellis Island fed 300 to about 1,800 detainees three meals a day under a concession, paid by the steamship companies (1a). The 1921 reforms show what had been missing: night quarters for women and children, laundry, interpreters at the barge, information for relatives, and reasons given to those deported (1a).
4. **Refused arrivals went back with the carrier, at the carrier's cost.** This holds from 1907 law (1a) to present US law (3d). Moving the check upstream reduces refusals at the far end. Examples: the PHS surgeon at Naples (43,196 rejected by 1906, 3b), consular visas from 1924 (1a), preclearance, juxtaposed controls (where the refused are released on the departure side, 2b), and the Galápagos rule that all international flights must stop at Quito or Guayaquil for biosecurity inspection (2c).
5. **Processing at sea is a working present-day practice for immigration, not for biosecurity.** Australia (en-route clearance) and Hong Kong put officers aboard at the last foreign port, at the operator's expense, with a processing space, internet and passenger marshaling supplied by the ship. Australia still does customs and agriculture clearance on land at the port of disembarkation (3a). Schengen law permits checks "during crossings" by agreement (3a). Historically, health and cabin inspection was done on board in the harbor approach, and steerage was moved by barge to the island station (3b).
6. **Biosecurity for isolated places is layered and mostly upstream, with sampling at the point of arrival.** South Georgia relies on a pre-arrival film, a self-check at sea, boot washing at every landing, statistically sampled audits, ship rodent controls, and expulsion of vessels with rodents (4a). Lord Howe relies on a pre-departure aircraft inspection with no night between inspection and departure, insecticide, and a declaration (4b). Macquarie Island inspects baggage on arrival at the station (4c).
7. **Isolated populations are epidemiologically fragile on contact.** A single ship arrival preceded a 96% attack-rate influenza epidemic on Tristan da Cunha (1971). A sealed wintering base still sustained a virus for months (4d). During COVID-19, every Antarctic program used pre-departure isolation and testing at the gateway, and the one breach came through a gateway (4c). Australia's 2020 remote-community rule required 14 days' isolation before entry, or entry in a manner agreed with a biosecurity officer for essential work (4e).
8. **In Antarctica today there is no arrival control.** States control their own nationals and expeditions before departure (UK permit to "enter or remain"; treaty advance notice); Australia permits activities, not presence (2d).
