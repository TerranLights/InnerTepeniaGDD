## 1 · Airports

**Supports.**
- Transcription Gulf: two Il-18V airliners landed on 3 December 1963 on a strip prepared in advance "in one of the lagoons of Transcription Gulf", carrying 80 people (two Russian chronicles agree). A 1961–62 oasis runway also refueled an Il-18V and An-12 on the Christchurch leg, so the Il-18s flew McMurdo to the oasis as a single leg. The ice measured 2.95–3.20 m in 2019, and on every moving-load rule found (Gold, CRREL, Sharp 1947 with the 2025 table, the Pegasus tire-stress criteria) that is well above the thresholds for the heavy types listed (extrapolated beyond Sharp's range for the heaviest types; flagged in the source file).
- Australian Basler and Twin Otter flights to the Bunger Hills are routine in recent summers (a 1.5-hour leg from Casey, 39 t moved to Wilkins in one season, camps of 27–37 people).
- Blue ice, melt and temperature limit runways more than weight: Wilkins cannot be used above about −5 °C ground temperature, and so is closed in December–January, the oasis's own warmest months.

**Does not support.**
- That any aircraft has ever used the Apfel strip, or that the 2019 Russian airfield was commissioned.
- That any 767F, A330F or 777F has landed on ice. The pit-free length of the gulf is unknown (cryoconite pits in two of its three zones).

**Amendment proposed.** Cargo airport on the gulf stands. For the passenger airport, **Option A (original):** the Apfel strip, flagged unproven. **Option B (recommended):** the gulf too, with Apfel kept as a survey-only candidate. Reason: the gulf is the only oasis surface with a documented history of aircraft, and the passenger fleet (Basler, Twin Otter) is light. One surface for both is simpler and removes the unproven site.

## 2 · Winter intake airport

**Supports.**
- Drygalski: an ice island of about 205–220 km², dome 325–327 m, cliffs 23–46 m, 78–85 km from the mainland. An East German observer wintered there May–August 1960 at a temporary station ("Mir"). Sea-ice cover off it is about 75–82% in July–September and about 4–14% in February (1991–2020).
- Mill Island: **a CASA 212 landed there in January 2009** and the 2009/10 party spent three weeks drilling; summit 503 m; accumulation 1.35 m of ice per year; summer marine fog and rime; ice thickness assumed (not measured) at up to about 400 m.
- The only dome-top landing analogue found is Roosevelt Island: in one season a Basler flew 9 missions and an LC-130 landed once on the ungroomed surface, while Twin Otters failed.

**Does not support.**
- Any survey, ice-thickness, speed, crevasse or accumulation measurement for either dome, or any landing at all on Drygalski. The model (Bedmap3) says Drygalski is only 115–128 m thick, with a bed up to 208 m above sea level, which conflicts with the 1962 account of it resting on a sea bank; it has no survey behind it.
- A ship landing at either island. Drygalski's cliffs are 23–46 m high, so "the summer ship stocks it" is unproven there.
- No source describes a prepared runway on any island dome.

**Decision needed.**
- **A.** Keep Drygalski (nearest to Mirny at 92 km, but with no data and tall cliffs).
- **B.** Mill Island (a documented aircraft landing and a field party; but 367 km from Mirny, which a Twin Otter can fly but an airliner round-trip cannot, plus fog, rime, heavy snow accumulation and disputed summer melt).
- **C (recommended).** Rule the *properties* now (an ice-cap island on the Davis/Shackleton coast, isolated, reached only by air in winter, stocked by the summer ship) and name the island after one more look; list Drygalski and Mill as the two candidates with their gaps. The research says plainly that no island dome has been surveyed for a runway, so naming one now claims a certainty the data does not have.

## 3 · Summer sea gateway

**Supports.**
- Two-ship working is current practice: at Zhongshan in 2024 and 2025 one icebreaker (*Xuelong 2*) opened the channel and ferried cargo while the other (*Xuelong*) carried the bulk; in 2024, 280 t in 17 containers moved ship to ship in about 11 hours, and 101 people were moved to the station in about two weeks. McMurdo escorts each chartered cargo ship with an icebreaker.
- Helicopter flying from a ship on the Casey coast is small: October 2025, 14 people and cargo in seven two-hour flights at about 100 nautical miles.

**Corrections to what I proposed.**
- The IMO "pairing" text (MSC.1/Circ.1184) is an information-sharing measure among passenger ships tied to a five-day time to recover. **It does not say ships rescue each other.** I overstated it.
- **No clearance of arrivals at sea or at an ice edge was found anywhere.** My "reception ship" has no direct precedent (Australia and Hong Kong clearance at sea is an immigration-officer-aboard practice on ordinary voyages).
- Nothing read shows the *Nuyina* operating at the Bunger Hills; the Denman 2025 voyage was marine science and the Bunger Hills camp was supplied from Casey.

**Amendment.** Keep the pair of ships, with the roles as documented (an icebreaker opens the way and ferries; the other carries the bulk and the reception space) and **rule the processing function as invented, not copied**. Replace the "so each can rescue the other" reasoning with the information-sharing text. Keep numbers small: a ship-to-shore wave is a dozen people at a time.

## 4 · The port

**Supports.**
- Casey is not served by a deep-water berth: ships anchor in Newcomb Bay and cargo goes by a 45.5-t barge to a wharf; the wharf and slipway modernization is "postponed". Casey took 550 t in 2019 and about 1,200 t from one general cargo ship in 2021–22 (28 days).
- Casey's air link is Wilkins, 67 km inland, usable except December–January, by tractor and trailer.
- Real heavy-haul rates: Progress–Vostok 2022–23 delivered 2,486 t in 17 traverses of 1,480 km in a 102-day window with 45 vehicles; fuel is 0.12–0.24 liters per tonne-kilometer.

**The problem (my arithmetic).** About 105,535 workers feed a population of the order of 0.7 million. At the food model's own rate of about 0.66 t of food per human per year (10.37 Mt a year for the nation, `Division_of_Industry/13`), that is **about 480,000 t a year.** Casey's recorded cargo is about 1,200 t a season and the best polar traverse about 2,500 t a season, so the real-world logistics are about **a thousand times smaller** than the food export canon implies. Hauling 400,000 t over the 437–468 km between Casey and the oasis (a straight line is 437 km; a route around the head of Vincennes Bay is longer; I had earlier written 350–400 km in error) at 0.12–0.24 L per tonne-kilometer is about 25–54 million liters of fuel a year, on an ice-road window of about 100 days. This is not Relung's problem alone (Davis and Esperanza export food too); it is a national-scale assumption, and canon already has later-era transport (the rastra at 45 km/h) and a "Direct, seasonal" port for Casey.

**Decision needed.**
- **A.** Accept canon-scale transport as fiction: the road to Casey and Casey's port carry what the economy needs, and the real-world numbers are only a plausibility check. No new file edits beyond the wording.
- **B.** Rule that Casey gets a real wharf (as the "postponed" real-world modernization), so the port function exists.
- **C.** Split the export: perishables by air from the gulf, bulk and dry goods by road to Casey.

I recommend **A with C's air share for perishables**, because it needs no new infrastructure ruling and the research shows the sea route is the thin point.

## 6 · Power

**What the research found.**
- Lit vertical farming of leafy greens: 10–20 kWh/kg (range 7.8–23.8; lighting 46–75% of the load). Measured Antarctic indoor farms use far more: EDEN-ISS 205–256 kWh/kg, the South Pole chamber about 100 kWh/kg. Staples under lamps are 10 to 40 times more energy-intensive per kg than lettuce.
- For feeding N people **entirely** from lit indoor farming, the computed average power is **6 to 16 kW per person**: about 0.6–1.5 GW per 100,000, 4.5–11.6 GW per 750,000, 6–15.4 GW per 1,000,000 (and 1.2 kW per person, 1.2 GW per million, only if efficiency reached the 10% upper bound). The largest operating vertical farms draw an estimated 1–2.5 MW. **No city is fed this way anywhere.**
- Local: reanalysis gives about 7.0 m/s at 10 m at the oasis and a generic turbine capacity factor of about 31–37% (the oasis floor itself has more calms; indicative only, no measured wind study exists after 1958). Ross Island turbines measured 37–40%. Solar is strongly seasonal (two months of essentially zero sun, about 40% of the year's energy in December–January).
- Transmission: 1,100–1,400 km HVDC lines exist in subarctic Canada, 110 kV over about 490 km in Chukotka; icing, galloping and permafrost heave are documented failures; **no power line longer than a few kilometers exists on Antarctic ice.**
- Reactors: only early units (PM-3A 1.75 MW, PM-2A 2 MW at Camp Century) and a Russian floating plant (2 × 35 MW at Pevek); microreactors are in demonstration, none deployed at a polar site.

**CORRECTION ADDED 2026-11-02 (evening), after reading the canon food model.** The research's 6–16 kW per person is the *present-day staple-crop* range. The canon food model (`Division_of_Industry/13`, the EDEN-ISS section) already addresses exactly this: it assumes a "mature" 10 kWh/kg for all indoor food, finds the nation's indoor staple tier needs about 11.8 GW (11.6% of a national 101.7 GW), and calls the measured rate (944 GW) impossible, naming the 80-fold gap "the most important piece of technology in the setting". On that assumption Relung's export food (about 480,000 t a year) needs roughly **0.5 GW average**, not 6–15 GW. So the "gigawatts" finding below holds only if the present-day numbers govern; under canon's own model the load is of the order of half a gigawatt plus the city itself. **Which assumption governs Relung is the developer's call; I now lean to the canon model.**

**What this changes.** The grid line along the Casey road is technically precedented as a line, but a line carrying gigawatts would have to be supplied by a generating plant that does not exist at Casey either. The load depends on one number canon has not fixed: **how much of Relung's food is grown under electric light.** (The determination file notes an outdoor belt of about 400 km² sited at the Bunger Hills serving Relung Panen, Mirny and Casey; I have not checked how canon splits indoor and outdoor.)

**Decision needed.**
- **A.** Rule the lit-indoor share first (low share = megawatts to a few gigawatts; high = the numbers above); power is then sized from it.
- **B.** Rule power as a national-grid supply (the canonical Amundsen grid) with the Casey line, and accept that Relung is the grid's largest single load, with local wind as a fraction.
- **C.** Rule a local power park at the oasis (wind plus storage plus reactors), at fiction scale, with the Casey line as backup.
I recommend **A**, then B or C. The grid file (`Amundsen_Power_Supply.md`) is still empty, so B cannot be checked.

## 7 · Cold-chain share

**What the research found.** One published ratio: about **2.5 employees per 1,000 pallet positions** across Americold's 239 warehouses (13,755 employees, over 5.5 million pallet positions; all staff). Keeping times: head lettuce at most 4 weeks at 0 °C (a month overseas only in controlled atmosphere), greens about 2 weeks, mature-green tomatoes 6 weeks in controlled atmosphere, potatoes 2–12 months; frozen and dry food 12–18 months.

**The problem (my arithmetic).** 16,886 workers at 2.5 per 1,000 positions is about **6.8 million pallet positions, more than Americold's whole network.** Even if a year's supply were stored at once (about 400,000 pallets at one tonne each, my assumption), the benchmark gives about **1,000 workers.** A cold-chain depot of a few tens of thousands of pallet positions would need under 100.

**Amendment proposed: 1% of the distinctive tier (4,221 workers)**, a judgment call that allows for chilled handling and loading on a long seasonal route, with the 3% (12,664) as the alternative if the cold chain also covers the air-freight and road handling. Under 1%: mandated 39% of the distinctive tier (about 21.8% of the workforce), free about 34.2%. Note the benchmark is for storage only and the route needs buffer stock, so this share is generous, not tight.
