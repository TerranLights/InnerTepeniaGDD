- **Direction.** The Byrd Station Strain Network (52 near-square quadrilaterals, sides about 3 km, over 163 km) runs "from the ice divide to New Byrd Station" and "follows more or less the regional surface slope, it approximates the flow path" (search snippets of Whillans 1979; UNVERIFIED in full). (1998), read at https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19990078514, states: "The major flow into D originates south of Byrd Station" — Ice Stream D is the stream now called Bindschadler Ice Stream, draining to the Ross Ice Shelf (UNVERIFIED as a sourced statement: inferred from the 1996 Ice Stream D onset surveys being re-measured in 2002 under the Bindschadler name, Winberry et al. 2007). - **Speed rises downstream.** Surface velocity "shows a steady increase" along the network and increases almost linearly from the ice divide while slope, thickness and temperature profile change little; Whillans attributed the rise to ice-fabric development (snippets of Whillans 1979). Balance velocities along the network follow the same pattern but are "about 5% less than the true ice velocity because the ice sheet is thinning" (snippet of Whillans 1977, *Journal of Glaciology*; UNVERIFIED in full).
- **Is the ice sliding at the point?** The 1998 NASA abstract (read) says the authors derived flow parameters "assuming ice at Byrd is not sliding," and that the developing ice stream behaves differently from ice at Byrd. Against that, Gow and Williamson (1976, CRREL; abstract and later summaries via snippets, DTIC PDF HTTP 403) report strong horizontal shear in highly oriented fine-grained ice to 1,800 m depth, a rapid change of crystal fabric below 1,800 m, and that "some slipping along the bedrock seems likely" because the bed is at the pressure-melting point with liquid water. ## 3. Accumulation and firn

**Main source.** Gow, A. J. (1968), *Deep core studies of the accumulation and densification of snow at Byrd Station and Little America V, Antarctica*, CRREL Research Report 197, US Army Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory; full scanned PDF read through its OCR text layer (https://icedrill.org/sites/default/files/CRREL-Research-Report-197.pdf). ### 3.1 Accumulation rate

- **Long-term, older locus:** SOURCED: "Stratigraphic studies of cores to a depth of 88.56 m indicate that ice at this depth was originally deposited on the surface as snow about the year 1549. This record of snow deposition extending back more than 410 years represents an average rate of accumulation of 15.6 g cm−2 yr−1." Between 1892 and 1959 annual values ranged "from 24.4 to 9.3 g cm−2 yr−1"; accumulation declined somewhat after 1839, with dips in 1839–1879 and 1914–1954. "An accumulation rate of 15-16 g cm−2 yr−1 appears to be about average for West Antarctica, but it is more than double the accumulation observed in areas of the same latitude in East Antarctica."
  - DERIVED units: 15.6 g cm−2 yr−1 = 156 kg m−2 yr−1 = **0.156 m water equivalent per year** = 0.170 m of solid ice per year (÷ 0.917). The Talalay et al. (2020) preprint table gives "169.5" mm ice per year for Byrd, citing Gow (1968) — the same figure (read).
- **Stakes, older locus:** SOURCED: 41.7 cm of snow from 12 March 1957 to 28 January 1958, "equivalent to about 47 cm snow or 16 to 17 g cm−2 yr−1" per calendar year; about 18 g cm−2 yr−1 at 100 stakes in 1958–1959; 13.3 g cm−2 yr−1 for November 1960 – February 1962; "the average accumulation at all stakes at Byrd Station for the period 1957-63 has been 14.2 g cm−2 yr−1." Snow pits, 1956–1959: pit averages 17.1, 15.3 and 15.7 g cm−2 yr−1 (Table I).
- **Eastern locus (the point):** SOURCED: "a somewhat smaller rate of accumulation - 11 g cm−2 yr−1 over the past 3 years (Gow and Rowland, 1965)." DERIVED: 0.11 m water equivalent per year. This is a three-year figure only.
- **Lower estimates by radioactive dating (1960s–1970s).** Search snippets of a 1972 *Journal of Glaciology* paper on Byrd accumulation (Cambridge, full text HTTP 403; UNVERIFIED in full): stakes gave about 117 kg m−2 a−1 for 1962–70 "within a 10 km radius"; a fission-product method gave 67 ± 4 kg m−2 a−1 for 1955–68; a lead-210 method gave 110 ± 10 kg m−2 a−1 averaged over 60 years; conclusion: "the average accumulation at 'Byrd' station for the time interval 1910–68 is 110 ± 10 kg m−2 year−1." The same paper cites Windom (1969): "a mean accumulation rate of 98 kg m−2 year−1." And: "surface depressions are accumulating 30–50% more snow than the exposed crests" (local variability). These agree with Gow's 11 g cm−2 yr−1 (110 kg m−2 a−1) for the eastern locus and are lower than his 15.6 g cm−2 yr−1 for the older locus 1549–1959. See Unresolved conflicts.
- **Continental scale for context.** Arthern et al. (2006) (snippet): mean accumulation over the grounded Antarctic ice sheet "143 ± 4 kg m−2 a−1." The point's 110–156 kg m−2 a−1 is of that order.
- **Spatial pattern and stability.** SOURCED (Conway and Rasmussen 2009, read): accumulation "increases almost linearly across the divide"; ice-equivalent accumulation at the WAIS Divide core site 0.22 m per year (radar-derived) and 0.25 m per year at ITASE 00-1, 1651–2000. Neumann et al. (2008) (snippet): today's linear decrease of accumulation across the divide "has probably not changed through the Holocene"; Conway and Rasmussen (read): "the accumulation rate 3000 years ago was about 30% higher than present, its gradient across the divide has not changed for at least 5000 years." Siegert and Payne (2004, *Geophysical Research Letters* 31: L12403; snippets, full text HTTP 403): radar layers dated at the Byrd core site show "mean accumulation calculated for the last 3100 years matches well to present-day accumulation rates." A modern (post-2000) accumulation value at the point: NOT FOUND.
- **No melt.** SOURCED (Gow 1968, summary): "The total absence of melt layers would indicate that surface air temperatures in the vicinity of Byrd Station have not risen above freezing for the last 1900 years — the estimated age of the ice at 309-m depth." (Older locus; compare the January 2005 event in item 6.)

## 4. Ice-sheet dynamics of the interior

- **Marine ice-sheet instability (MISI), as defined in the source.** SOURCED (Bamber et al. 2009, "Reassessment of the potential sea-level rise from a collapse of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet," *Science* 324: 901–903, full PDF read from https://www.klimafakten.de/sites/default/files/science_bamber_etal_2009_wais_sealevel-3.pdf): the MISI hypothesis "has its basis in the idea that removal of fringing ice shelves will result in the rapid and irreversible inland migration of the grounding line" for ice grounded on beds that deepen inland. "There is compelling evidence that the WAIS has undergone partial collapse in the past, possibly as recently as 400 thousand years before the present." "Although much of the WAIS is grounded on bedrock below sea level (BSL), there are extensive areas around the Transantarctic and Ellsworth mountains and the Executive Committee Range that are not and/or that do not have negative bed slopes."
- **Sea-level size of the vulnerable ice.** SOURCED (same, read): instantaneous loss of the ice meeting the MISI conditions gives 2.46 ± 0.2 m eustatic sea-level rise; drawdown of the remaining margins adds 0.74 m and lithospheric response 0.06 m, total 3.26 m ("about 3.3 meters"); "a value of 4.8 m for the removal of the whole of the WAIS including the Peninsula." After collapse, the ice left would include "a fairly contiguous and sizable ice cap over the Executive Committee/Flood ranges [Marie Byrd Land Ice Cap] … about 600 km in length and 300 km in width" and an ice cap "centered over the Ellsworth, Whitmoor, and Thiel mountain ranges" (spelling "Whitmoor" as printed). The disintegration scenarios "are not meant to indicate a probable decay behavior, which is likely to be nonlinear and asymptotic." **Whether the point lies inside the area the paper removes: NOT FOUND** (stated only in the paper's map). The point's bed is about 634 m below sea level (item 1, DERIVED) and lies on the Byrd Subglacial Basin side, outside both named residual ice caps by their stated geography (the Executive Committee Range is 355 km away); that the point would be in the removed area is DERIVED and UNVERIFIED.

### 5.2 Subglacial volcanism near the point

### 6.4 Polar night and midnight sun (independent calculation)

- **Method.** DERIVED: NOAA solar-position algorithm (after Meeus), coded in the scratchpad (`sun.py`), evaluated every 4 minutes for every local mean-time day (UTC − 8 h) at 80.017°S, 119.517°W. Two definitions: (a) sunrise/sunset when the sun's center is 0.833° below the horizon (standard: upper limb plus 34′ refraction); (b) geometric center on the horizon, no refraction.
- **Polar night (no sunrise all day):** definition (a): **2026: 18 April to 24 August, 129 days** (2025: 18 April to 23 August, 128 days); definition (b): 15 April to 26 August, 134 days.
- **Midnight sun (no sunset all day):** definition (a): about **17 October to 23 February, 130 days** (DERIVED: 15 + 30 + 31 + 31 + 23 = 130); definition (b): 19 October to 21 February, 126 days.
- **Check by simple arithmetic.** DERIVED: the noon sun stays below the standard horizon when its declination exceeds 90° − 80.017° + 0.833° = 10.82° (north). The sun's declination passes 10.8° N in mid-April and again in late August, which agrees with the computed dates.
- **Comparison with the brief.** The brief's "~129 days, about 19 April to 25 August" and "midnight sun ~131 days" agree with this calculation to within one day at each end; the one-day differences come from the choice of horizon definition, time zone and year.
- **How dark the polar night is.** DERIVED (same script, 2026): the noon sun stays below −6° (no civil twilight even at noon) for 96 days, 4 May to 7 August; below −12° (no nautical twilight at noon) for 42 days, 31 May to 11 July; it never stays below −18°, so some astronomical twilight reaches the sky at noon even at midwinter. Noon sun altitude at the June solstice −13.45°; at the December solstice +33.4° (check: 90 − 80.017 ± 23.44).
- **A published table that disagrees, and why it is not used.** SOURCED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night (read), an uncited table: "79° South: about April 22 to August 22"; "80° South: about April 25 to August 19"; "81° South: about April 17 to August 24." The table is internally inconsistent (80°S is given a shorter polar night than 79°S, and 81°S starts before 80°S), which cannot be physically true, since polar night lengthens steadily toward the pole. It is rejected as a source and logged as a conflict. The fetch of timeanddate.com for the point returned HTTP 403 (**died at the SOURCE**).

### Unresolved conflicts

1. **Byrd Subglacial Basin depth.** Wikipedia: Bedmap2 showed the basin at 2,870 m below sea level. Bedmap2 paper (Fretwell et al. 2013): the −2,870 m point is near the grounding line of Byrd Glacier. Resolved in favor of the primary paper; the Wikipedia sentence is wrong. The basin's own deepest bed remains NOT FOUND.
2. **Surface elevation.** 1,530 m (four sources) vs 1,500 m and 1,515 m (older locus, 10 km west), 1,497 m (Gow et al. 2004 snippet), 1,542.9 m (AMRC snippet), 1,737 m (NASA blog snippet, rejected). Unresolved between 1,530 and 1,542.9 m for the station site.
3. **Ice thickness.** 2,164 m (drilled; also Talalay's pressure-melting extrapolation) vs 2,300 m (Wexler 1961 seismic, older locus) vs 2,500 m (Bentley 1964, older locus) vs 2,153 m vertical hole depth (resurvey snippet) vs 2,193 m drilled depth (Talalay Table 1). The drilled 2,164 m is taken as the thickness; the others are older estimates or hole lengths.
4. **Basal temperature.** −1.43 °C (Talalay preprint) vs −1.6 °C (seismic paper snippet) vs −1.8 °C (core paper snippet).
5. **Accumulation.** 15.6 g cm−2 yr−1 (1549–1959, older locus) vs 14.2 (stakes 1957–63) vs 11 (eastern locus, 3 years) vs 110 ± 10 kg m−2 yr−1 (1910–68) vs 98 (Windom 1969) vs 67 ± 4 (fission products 1955–68). Partly locus (10 km apart, with depressions taking 30–50 % more snow than crests) and partly period and method; not resolved.
6. **Velocity.** About 13 m/yr (Whillans 1979 via Bindschadler) vs "probably no greater than 10 m/yr" (Gow 1968 estimate). The later strain-network measurement is preferred, but it was read only at second hand.
7. **Sliding at the bed.** Bindschadler et al. (1998) assume no sliding at Byrd; Gow and Williamson (1976) consider some bedrock slip likely.
8. **Warming trend.** 2.44 ± 1.19 °C (1958–2010, original) vs a smaller corrected trend (corrigendum, value not reached) vs 2.02 °C (1957–2013, corrected record, Tamazian et al.); significance 99 % (original) vs 95.3 % (Tamazian et al.).
9. **Mount Casertz location.** Iverson et al. (2017): about 250 km from the WAIS Divide core. Talalay et al. (2020 preprint), citing Blankenship et al. (1993): a 23 km wide caldera about 100 km south of the WAIS Divide drill site. Whether these are the same edifice, and where it lies relative to the point, is unresolved.
10. **Polar night at 80°S.** This unit's calculation: about 18 April – 24 August (128–129 days). Wikipedia table: about 25 April – 19 August. The Wikipedia table is internally inconsistent and is rejected, but it is logged.
