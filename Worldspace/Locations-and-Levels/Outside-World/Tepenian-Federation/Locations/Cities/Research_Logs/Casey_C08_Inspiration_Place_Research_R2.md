## 1 · #12 Dawson Creek, British Columbia (Mile 0 of the Alaska Highway)

**Round 2 focus:** the town's other settings (housing, the shadow population, the shelter, the arenas and the ski hill), its groups (farm co-operators, oil-patch workers, Indigenous residents, Filipino newcomers, churches), what it does for itself when travelers are gone, and the history of its events. Round 1's NOT FOUND items are chased at the end.

### A. Other settings
- **Housing and the "shadow population" (K4, K7, K9) [P4, P9]:** The *Housing Needs Report, Dawson Creek* (Peace River Regional District, March 2021; PDF saved by WebFetch, read locally with pdftotext, SOURCED) states that "many families have lived in Dawson Creek for several generations," but "concerns exist within the community regarding the mobile nature of the 'shadow population' due to seasonal and project-oriented work." Its findings: 61% of households own and 39% rent; 64% of households are one or two people; 20% of households (36% of renters) spent 30% or more of income on shelter in 2016; work camps are used "to avoid impacting rental vacancy and rates on a large scale," but the report names the costs: more demand on health and social services, "crime and safety concerns," worry about "the maintenance of a community's social fabric when the permanent resident workforce is in the minority," larger male populations, companies renting units for employees, "illegal suites, campground stays, hotel stays." Stakeholders said homelessness had grown over five years, was largely hidden ("couch surfing"), that people using substances were turned away from shelters, that homeless youth aged 15–18 "allegedly live in forests near the community," and that women fleeing violence travel to Fort St. John for services. The Nawican Friendship Center told the study it is hard for Indigenous people on fixed incomes to find housing off-reserve. Survey: 173 respondents, July 15–August 13, 2020.
- **Boom conditions, 2017 (K7, K8) (BCBusiness, 2017-12-19, SOURCED):** "Our hotel rooms are full, the restaurants are full, rental accommodations are full" (Mayor Dale Bumstead). ~920 hotel rooms, mostly on Alaska Avenue; some energy workers lived in RVs; many industry workers earned $100,000–$150,000; employers named: ARC Resources (90 staff in town), Encana (~200), the Louisiana-Pacific OSB plant (~150; local aspen; ~30,000 panels a day; ~80% to the US).
- **Camps today (K4, K7):** camp hospitality staff in the region unionized with UNITE HERE Local 40 (~1,000 workers); in 2025 disputes at camps north of Fort St. John included a lockout reported by MINING.com (2025-04-30) (search summaries; UNVERIFIED; these camps are not in Dawson Creek). A remote-camp job near Dawson Creek was advertised at $40–45/hour on two weeks on, two weeks off (Indeed snippet; UNVERIFIED). Current camp counts near Dawson Creek: NOT FOUND (R1 gap stays open).
- **The downtown shelter (K4, K6, K9):** the **Nawican Emergency Shelter**, run by the Nawican Friendship Center with support from the Aboriginal Housing Management Association (AHMA), went before city council in August 2026 after an independent review prompted by downtown complaints of "public intoxication, trespassing, litter, noise, and disruptive behavior." Its four priorities: complaint management and accountability; supports for substance use and mental health; site management and a **Good Neighbor Policy**; coordination among partners. A move to the original Nawican building at 1320 102 Avenue was being explored (CJDC-TV, 2026-08-12, SOURCED). A supportive-housing building, **Nee Gin Naw Place**, opened in 2021 (BC Gov release, snippet; UNVERIFIED). A 2005 SPHS item lists Nawican's programs then as alcohol and drug programs, a soup kitchen, a women's group, culture and sharing circles and an elders' drop-in, and says a provincial body took over its governance after an executive director's dismissal (calverley.ca BN01 items, snippets; UNVERIFIED). [P4]
- **The ice buildings (K10):** **Memorial Arena** (first built 1952–53 as a memorial to Peace Country residents lost in two world wars; burned December 1956; rebuilt 1958; its roof fell in on 1997-01-08 at about −30 °C; reopened 1998 with a junior game watched by ~900 more people after the ceremony); a curling rink next door shares the city ice plant; **Kin Arena**; the **Ovintiv Events Center** (2008). The **Dawson Creek Speed Skating Club** began in fall 1956; its oval was moved behind the arena in the early 1960s (calverley.ca BN07-02 and BN07-36, snippets; UNVERIFIED). The junior team (Jr. Canucks from 1994, renamed Kodiaks for 2021–22) (Wikipedia snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **The ski hill (K13, K14, K1-style volunteer body) (SPHS BN07-09, SOURCED):** began in 1957–58 as the **International Ski Club** (about a dozen members; meetings in the president's living room at the NAR station; fees went to "a coffee fund"); volunteer work parties cleared runs; renamed **Dawson Creek Ski Club**; rope tow, then an A-frame chalet (begun 1961, opened 1963), a T-bar (1965); the chalet burned on 1979-01-23 and a $325,000 chalet opened 1979-11-24 with a $200,000 BC Lottery grant; snowmaking in 1987 ($350,000, half from lotteries). Potluck suppers were held in the first shack. Today volunteers are said to cover ~40% of operating costs and run snowmaking (CJDC snippet; UNVERIFIED); the **Bear Mountain Nordic Ski Association** runs the cross-country trails (federal grant record snippet; UNVERIFIED). [R: volunteer maintenance culture]

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Farm co-operators (K1, K14) (SPHS 05-012, "The Dawson Co-op's History," SOURCED):** the **Dawson Co-operative Union** was formed by settlers at a United Farmers meeting on 1921-04-09 (five directors), incorporated 1921-05-28; shares $25, unlimited in number, sold by canvassers touring homesteads; from 1923 new members could start with $5 down and pay the rest from dividends; profits to a reserve; **the store closed on Sundays** (adopted 1921-08-27); first dividend January 1923 (8% on shares, 2% on purchases). Context: sugar cost $20 and flour $10 per 100 lb. Later: in 2000 a plan to replace the Co-op mall with a supermarket leased from Federated Co-operatives drew **926 members** to the AGM; a $1.6M patronage refund from FCL turned a 2001 loss into net savings of $394,181 (SPHS snippets; UNVERIFIED). A longtime manager called the co-op "a formula between socialism and capitalism, which lets people help themselves" (SPHS 05-013 snippet; UNVERIFIED). [K6: Sunday closing] [P7]
- **Oil-patch workers (K7):** high wages and boom-bust (above); the spring thaw ("breakup") brings road weight restrictions from about mid-March that stall rig moves and cut contract workers' income (industry explainers, snippets; UNVERIFIED for Dawson Creek specifically). The **Spring Breakup Rodeo** (R1) is named for that season. A group calling itself the **South Peace Oilmen's** co-sponsors the Christmas tree light-up with Rotary and runs an annual **Lobsterfest** (Tourism Dawson Creek events list, SOURCED as names only); its founding and beneficiaries: NOT FOUND.
- **Indigenous residents (K4, K9, P9):** the 2016 census showed nearly 16% Métis or First Nations (R1). New in 2025: the **Cultural Gathering of Nations**, a four-day gathering at the Ovintiv Events Center (inaugural 2025; 2026 edition 17–20 September): powwow, hand games, cultural teachings, bingo, food trucks, vendors and children's activities; free (Saulteau First Nations post, 2026-06-02, SOURCED); described elsewhere as bringing together Treaty 8 nations and northern BC nations, "drug and alcohol-free," with a Tea Dance and traditional foods (events-center listing snippets; UNVERIFIED). Elite **Indian Relay Racing** entered the Exhibition in about 2021–22 as a new event (CJDC snippet; UNVERIFIED). The Dawson Creek Native Housing Society worked on elders' housing off 17th Street (SPHS snippet; UNVERIFIED). A Métis local in Dawson Creek: NOT FOUND (searched).
- **Filipino newcomers (K4) [P9]:** **KALIPI South Peace** (Kalipunan ng Lahing Pilipino) is "the Filipino association in Dawson Creek"; it helped host Philippine consular outreach missions in 2021 and 2022 (Philippine Consulate General Vancouver pages, snippets; UNVERIFIED) and organizes a **Philippine Independence Day flag raising at City Hall** in June, during Filipino Heritage Month (CJDC-TV, SOURCED); it also ran a "Dinner for a Cause" for typhoon victims (snippet). The 2016 census showed ~10% visible minorities, "primarily Filipino and South Asian" (Wikipedia via snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Churches (K4):** an SPHS list of November 2002 gives **18 congregations** in Dawson Creek: United, two Lutheran (Grace ELCC; St. Paul's), Presbyterian, Baptist, Pentecostal, Alliance, Roman Catholic (Notre Dame), Salvation Army, two Seventh-day Adventist, **Northgate Mennonite Brethren**, Apostolic, Nazarene, Anglican (St. Mark's), a community church, Grandview Chapel and Cornerstone Christian Assembly (calverley.ca BN09-22, SOURCED; the list states no language or ethnic affiliations). A Church of the New Jerusalem also meets weekly (snippet). No Hutterite colony or Dutch Reformed church found near Dawson Creek (searched).
- **Lodges and service clubs (K10):** Royal Canadian Legion **Branch 141** (Remembrance Day ceremony in Unchagah Hall, every seat filled in 2005, lunch after at the Legion Hall); the **Elks Hall** is the former Vogue movie theater; **Peace Lodge No. 126** (Masonic) constituted 1937, hall opened 1956-03-23; Rotary, Kiwanis, Kinsmen, Shriners (SPHS snippets; masonicmarkers; tourism list; UNVERIFIED except the tourism list names).
- **Rodeo youth (K14):** Northern Lights College fields what it calls the only college rodeo team in BC; it trains weekly at the Lakota Agriplex and members may live on a college farm a few minutes out; it won the Canadian Intercollegiate Rodeo Association championship at Brooks, Alberta, in 2025 (NLC rodeo pages, snippets; UNVERIFIED). BC High School Rodeo (since 1972) holds events in Dawson Creek; 4-H beef clubs (Pioneer, Boundary, Tate Creek) hold an Achievement Day (snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Pride:** the city's first gay pride festival in 2003 drew about 65 people to a drag show at the Kiwanis Performing Arts Center, followed by a dance (Peace River Block Daily News, July 2003, reprinted in SPHS BN05-88, SOURCED).

### C. Leisure industries and what locals do
- **The event calendar (K3) (Tourism Dawson Creek annual-events page, SOURCED as names):** 22 named annual events: DC Sportsman's Club Gun and Sportsman Show; Northern Handmade Market; South Peace Oilmen's Lobsterfest; Peace Arts Festival; Dawson Creek Triathlon; DEEP's Summer Solstice Run; Mile Zero Cruisers Summer Cruise and Show n' Shine; Multicultural Fusion Celebration; Pouce Coupe Canada Day Parade BBQ; Dare to Dream Music Festival; Rib Fest; South Peace Garden Club Garden Tour and Tea; the Exhibition; Rotary Fall Fair Parade; Shriners' Kiddies' Parade; Oktoberfest; NLC Intercollegiate Rodeo; Cultural Gathering of Nations; Pioneer Village Halloween Haunted Village; Mile Zero Figure Skating Club Totem Competition; Rotary and South Peace Oilmen's Christmas Tree Light-Up; Rotary Old-Fashioned Christmas. Nearly every one is run by a club, society or association.
- **Mile Zero Cruisers (K3, R1 extended):** in July 2003 the Summer Cruise had >240 cars in the Show and Shine (20 more than 2002) and ~120 cars registered on the Friday (club president Viril Anderson) (SPHS BN05-88, SOURCED). 2026 was billed "30 years," which would place the first cruise around 1997, but edition numbers in news items conflict (26th in both 2020 and 2022) (snippets; UNVERIFIED). Club founding year: still NOT FOUND. Weekly **Thursday night cruises** run in summer (events calendar snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Pubs (K10):** live music, karaoke and open mic on weekends; a guide says pubs are busy on Fridays with shift workers and quiet otherwise (travel-guide snippet; low-quality source; UNVERIFIED). Names listed: Browns Socialhouse (1100 Alaska Ave), Whiskey Pickle Pub, George Dawson Inn Thursday pub night, Rimrock Ridge Sports Lounge (snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **The 1922 stampede as a volunteer build (K2, K14) (SPHS 08-059, SOURCED):** first Dawson Creek stampede 2–3 July 1922, when the town had 300–400 people; grounds north of 108th Avenue at 20th Street (now Willowbrook); William S. Bullen laid out a racetrack on his land and volunteers built the track, fences, grandstand and corrals "over three Sundays, at no cost beyond meals." The Rolla stampede (1919) came first. The **Wilde & Lawless Stampede** at Taylor Flats (from July 1945) had a dance hall and lights and a wedding on horseback, and closed when the American Army and construction workers left and the government billed amusement taxes (same). The Exhibition Association says, "We started in 1922 and we've got a few blank years" (snippet; UNVERIFIED). The 100th edition was in 2022; the 104th ran in August 2026 (president Murray Pratt: attendance up on 2025; CJDC-TV 2026-08-10, SOURCED). Over 40,000 people over five days (2020 snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Older pattern of fun (K2, K10) (SPHS 07-028, SOURCED):** a 1914 sports day and dance at a homestead; Pouce Coupe community picnics from 1915-07-01 (tug-of-war, log-rolling, two nights of dancing); baseball trips of 2–3 days to Grande Prairie, Spirit River or Taylor "often" with a dance; Rolla's 1921 hockey team bought its own gear; games on natural ice, "often followed by a dance"; curling for the **Fynn Trophy**, "a wooden block with a tin cup nailed to it," challengeable by any team.
- **Music and drama festival (K13) (SPHS 07-009, SOURCED):** started about 1936 by the Rolla B.P.O. Elks (85 entries, ~175 participants, two days); moved among Rolla, Pouce Coupe and Dawson Creek; paused in the war; by 1949 run under School District 59 (eleventh annual). Participants included a school orchestra on homemade instruments and "the Sudeten children at Gundy." Peace Country Bluegrass Festival at Pioneer Village drew ~1,000 fans in 2003 (SPHS BN05-88, SOURCED).

## 2 · #13 Delta Junction, Alaska

**Round 2 focus:** the groups that make up the town (the Slavic Pentecostal and Baptist community and the 2022–25 Ukrainian arrivals, farm families, the post, the mine rotation), the services residents built for themselves (clinic, co-ops, a long-running farm forum), winter life, and Round 1's open threads (Fort Greely housing, Deltana Fair, the Delta Historical Society, tax and borough questions).

### A. Other settings
- **The post next door (K4, K7) [P4]:** Fort Greely is ~5 miles south of town (Wikipedia via snippet; UNVERIFIED). An Army article (2023-02-08, SOURCED) calls it "a small, remote enclave" ~90 miles SE of Fairbanks, with temperatures from ~85 °F and 21 hours of summer daylight to −50 °F and ~3 hours of winter sun; ~87 inches of snow had fallen by early February 2023; a "snow day" is "a normal day from October through April"; weapons and vehicles must work at −50, where standard lubricants gel. The **49th Missile Defense Battalion** (Alaska Army National Guard; some soldiers have served there over a decade) runs the interceptors, augmented then by the Mississippi Guard's 113th MPs. **Delta Junction High School serves 68 children** of soldiers and Army civilians, and the principal was credited with integrating them. Post housing is privatized (Lendlease / North Haven Communities) and has ranked near the top of Army resident-satisfaction surveys; residents raise concerns at quarterly town halls (army.mil snippets; UNVERIFIED). In 2004 the post had **325 housing units** (Delta CEDS 2004, SOURCED). R1's question "where do Fort Greely people live" is thus partly answered: on post in privatized housing and barracks, with some renting in the area. [P9]
- **Shared military-civil spaces (K10):** the post's **Aurora Community Activity Center** (an 8,900 sq ft indoor playground and family basketball addition announced 2025-11-24), a bowling center with weekly leagues (a Tuesday men's league, a youth league), and MWR events sometimes open to "the Delta Junction community"; the MWR has run winter ice-fishing and snowmachine outings at Quartz Lake (army.mil and greely.armymwr.com snippets; UNVERIFIED). The 2004 economic strategy says that "past dependence on the amenities of Fort Greely limited the private sector development of recreational facilities such as a movie theater or bowling alley, shopping, and health care" (CEDS 2004, SOURCED). [K9]
- **The mine camp (K7, K4):** Pogo is 38 miles NE of town by a 49-mile all-season road; in 2022 Northern Star (Pogo) LLC had 610 employees and >300 contractors; workers must stay in camp during rotations (dining, gym, sauna, Wi-Fi dorms); buses run from Fairbanks, Delta Junction and Tok; postings give "first consideration" to applicants from the Fairbanks North Star Borough, Delta and Tok regions; an older article cited 69% Alaska hire and a **Delta Mine Training Center** (DNR page and job-posting snippets; UNVERIFIED). [R: rotation and camp rest]
- **The homestead edge (K5, K7) (UAF, "The homesteader's tale," 2010-09-14, SOURCED):** one couple north of Delta cleared 45 acres by hand from the 1980s, farmed ~4 acres, fished and hunted moose, had no TV or computer, and took produce to the Fairbanks farmers' market twice a week by boat across the Tanana and then an hour-plus by truck. Local lore says not to plant outdoors before June 1; they started seedlings in cold frames during spring breakup mud. "Twenty five [homesteads] would have been allowed," the husband said; they were the only ones who settled there.
- **Health care built by residents (K1, K5) (KUAC 2013-11-27, SOURCED):** the **Interior Alaska Hospital Foundation**, formed by Delta residents in 2011 for the area's ~6,000 people, secured 13 acres of donated land for a 10-bed elder-care center and a $400,000 grant to open the **Interior Alaska Medical Clinic** (run by the faith-based Cross Road Medical Center of Glennallen) by March 2014. Before that the privately run **Family Medical Center** had been "Delta's only health facility for years" and had cut to weekday business hours in March 2013. "Delta has never had a pharmacy." The service area runs from the Canadian border to Paxson and north to Salcha. The new clinic was later listed with pharmacy, lab, radiology and behavioral health (directory snippets; UNVERIFIED). [SM: competence under pressure]

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **The Slavic community (K4, K9) [P9]:** 
  - *Origins:* "Pentecostal Russian and Ukrainian families fleeing religious persecution after the collapse of the Soviet Union first settled in Delta Junction in the early 1990s," coming from other states because land was affordable (ADN, 2025-05-16, SOURCED). Many were secondary migrants from California, Washington or Oregon who wanted land, houses of their own and animals (Alaskalink.US, undated feature, SOURCED). The 2004 city economic strategy recorded growth "from four families to 113 families" in ten years, 10–13 more families a year expected, average 3–4 children; Catholic Social Services had placed refugees in Delta since 1996 (32 in 1998, 12 in 2002) and opened a Delta office; the Job Center noted barriers of English, citizenship and job history (Delta CEDS, adopted by council 2004-11-16, PDF read locally, SOURCED). The newcomers petitioned lawmakers for language classes, job training and youth programs, which led to Catholic Social Services' Refugee Assistance and Immigration Services program (ADN 2025, SOURCED).
  - *Size:* one community member put the area's former-Soviet population at "more than 2,000," a third of Delta (Alaskalink, SOURCED as her claim); the city itself had 918 people in 2020 (R1), so the figure must cover the wider area. Not census-verified.
  - *Church life (K2, K6):* many are Pentecostal or Baptist, a small group Russian Orthodox; several churches stand off the Richardson Highway; **once a year young people are baptized in a lake on Delta's western edge** (nine teenagers the year of the article); in some groups women cover their hair at all times, in others only at church; church life takes up much of families' time, and the groups "tend to separate socially," though some churches join American churches for holiday events; children mix at school, and some reported being told by peers to "go home" (Alaskalink, SOURCED). ## 3 · #11 "Parting of the Ways," Wyoming (Oregon Trail)

**Round 2 focus:** the site has no residents, so Round 2 follows the people around it: the irrigation-farm and ranch community of Farson–Eden (the nearest settlement), the corridor's living commemorations (the Pony Express re-ride, the handcart treks, the fur-trade rendezvous), the Eastern Shoshone's own gatherings today, the energy boom in the county the cutoff crosses, and, for the historical decision point, the emigrants' own social customs (company property, the Fourth of July). Care note kept: a site, not a town; used for the decision-point idea.

### A. Other settings
- **Farson–Eden, the irrigation valley (K1, K8) [P8]:** the Bureau of Reclamation's **Eden Project** irrigates 17,010 acres near Farson and Eden, ~40 miles north of Rock Springs; settlers held permits to divert Big Sandy Creek water from 1886; features include Big Sandy Dam and Reservoir (39,700 acre-feet) and Eden Reservoir (7,500), and the Little Sandy, Means and Eden canals. A **1950 contract** made the **Eden Valley Irrigation and Drainage District** responsible for repaying construction at $75 an acre over 60 years; construction was substantially complete by December 1959; operation passed to the district in January 1970. At an October 1961 US Senate subcommittee hearing **held in Farson**, a Farson farmer on the district board called the project's economics "marginal at the very best." In 2018 the district sought a grant to line the Eden Canal, saving 1,554 acre-feet a year (USBR project pages and Senate hearing record via search summaries; UNVERIFIED). [SM: man versus society (the settler and the dry land)]
- **The school (K4):** Farson-Eden is a K-12 campus in Sweetwater County School District #1 (based in Rock Springs): ~45–47 elementary, ~39 middle and ~53 high-school students (school-directory snippets; UNVERIFIED; figures vary by year).
- **Big Sandy Reservoir (K10):** ~15 miles north of Farson; Reclamation-managed, free, primitive, "no on-site manager or law enforcement"; campgrounds close in winter (recreation.gov and campground snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Atlantic City, east of South Pass (K3, K10) (Wyoming Public Media, 2023-09-01, SOURCED):** an unincorporated former gold town (sign: population "about 57") on the Continental Divide Trail and the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route; where "only a few" hiked or biked twenty years earlier, "hundreds of hikers and a couple thousand cyclists" now pass each summer; a retired couple opened an RV park and added laundry, tent sites and public showers ("There's no place out there on the trail to take a shower"); a former historical-society president called the visitors "another boom"; businesses still struggle in winter. The **Atlantic City Mercantile** (1893; the town's economic and social center until the store closed in 1929; NRHP 1985) is now a restaurant, saloon and museum (same; Wikipedia snippet). [K9: five boom-and-bust cycles]
- **The rut itself as a setting (K9) (WyoFile, 2018-06-29, SOURCED):** ~500,000 migrants used the Oregon, Mormon and California trails from 1843; the last documented wagon passed in 1912; wind and sage regrowth are slowly erasing ruts; a Western Wyoming Community College researcher began digitally recording them at South Pass (ground-penetrating radar, magnetometer, resistivity); the BLM monitors annually a stretch of the Mormon trail used by church trekkers and found no significant erosion in 15+ years.
- **Regional land use (K9):** the BLM's 2023 plan amendment for the Rock Springs and Rawlins field offices removes wild horses from most of the checkerboard after the **Rock Springs Grazing Association** withdrew consent for horses on its private land in 2010; litigation has continued since (BLM and Wyoming Public Media snippets; UNVERIFIED in detail). R1 recorded the dispute; this is the background.

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **The cutoff's county in a gas boom (K4, K7, K9) [P9]:** Sublette County, which the Sublette Cutoff crosses, had ~10,000 people; producing wells rose from 26 in 1995 to 2,964 in 2008, in the Jonah Field and Pinedale Anticline. A citizen-guided Socioeconomic Task Group's 2006 report named "an increasing lack of affordable housing," "exponential increases in reported crimes and arrests, and a link between drug use and gas field activity," and "perceived negative impacts of the influx of temporary workers on community stability," and proposed that industry buy land for housing "to support reduction of 'man camps'"; the state, county and the towns of Big Piney, Marbleton and Pinedale formed a monitoring partnership (2006–2010) (Haggerty and McBride, Headwaters Economics, November 2014; PDF saved, read locally, SOURCED). Drilling fell back after a 2009 downturn (same). [K1]
- **Eastern Shoshone (K2, K9) [P9]:** **Eastern Shoshone Indian Days**, Fort Washakie, Wind River Reservation, usually the last weekend of June (55th edition in 2014, so begun ~1960): each day opens with a **grand entry**; dance contests run afternoon and night; a parade on the final morning is led by the American Legion color guard carrying the eagle staff; a **feast at Rocky Mountain Hall** is opened by an elder's prayer in Shoshone and English, and "out-of-state guests eat first"; host drums came from Saskatchewan and Oklahoma; 483 contest dancers registered in 2014 (over 1,000 in 2012) from at least 20 states and Canada; run by a powwow committee and the elected Eastern Shoshone Entertainment Council; the coordinator: "We have people who are sick, who are sad and mourning and who are confused," and the event exists to uplift them (WyoFile, 2014-07-01, SOURCED). The 64th edition was in 2024 (tribal news snippet; UNVERIFIED). A **Treaty Day Pageant** is held in late July (Lander Chamber listing snippet; UNVERIFIED). The tribe says it has lived in the Wind River range and environs for some 12,000 years and calls the Wind River Valley "Warm Valley" (easternshoshone.org snippet; UNVERIFIED). R1's treaty history stands; no source on a present-day Shoshone interpretation of the Parting site was found (searched).
- **Eden-Farson site (R1 thread):** a 2017 study refers to the site's people as Shoshone and counts at least 10 discrete households with at least 174 pronghorn (O'Brien and Walker, via tDAR snippet; UNVERIFIED); the SHPO dates occupation by a single radiocarbon date to "the cusp of the Protohistoric Period" (WY SHPO snippet; UNVERIFIED). This narrows but does not settle R1's NOT FOUND.
- **Living-history riders (K2, K14) (Cowboy State Daily, 2026-06-19, SOURCED):** the **National Pony Express Association re-ride** carries a **mochila** of letters ~2,000 miles from St. Joseph to Sacramento in ~10 days each June, east-to-west in 2026; riders had 59 hours to cover 489 miles of Wyoming; scheduled 2026 handoffs included Atlantic City (8 a.m. Saturday) and **Farson, at Highways 191 and 28 (2 p.m. Saturday)**; chapters are county-based (one had 11 riders); riders wear a red vest; families ride together; night legs are the hard ones ("Seeing, just kind of knowing where you are going"); the ride usually aims for a full moon. The association called Farson the halfway mark of the whole re-ride (NPEA 2025 schedule snippet; UNVERIFIED). [SM: competence under pressure]

### C. Leisure and commemoration industries
- **Handcart treks (K2, K3, K6) (Church of Jesus Christ, "Common questions about trekking at the Wyoming Mormon Trail Sites," read 2026-11-20, SOURCED):** treks run late May to mid-August (twelve weeks from the Tuesday after Memorial Day); reservations open in September; $15 per adult; up to 600 people per site, moving in subgroups of 150 an hour apart; up to 15 handcarts per 150 people, 6–10 per cart; itineraries from a 6-mile loop to Sixth Crossing–Rock Creek Hollow (~28–30 miles over two days); at least two missionary Trail Guides set the pace and can tell stories on request; two licensed medical professionals per 150 people; **rules:** no bikes, horses, dogs, firearms, fireworks, fishing gear, drones or ATVs; quiet hours 10 p.m.–6 a.m.; no air horns or bugles; campground water not for washing hair or feet; pack in, pack out; cart passengers only for medical reasons; pioneer clothing optional; square dancing allowed in campgrounds by reservation with the group's own caller; adult "ma" and "pa" leaders of each trek "family" attend training for Rocky Ridge. Martin's Cove draws more than 100,000 visitors a year (Deseret News 2026 snippet; UNVERIFIED). [R: shared rest rules]
- **Green River Rendezvous Days (K2, K3):** Sublette County residents have reenacted the fur-trade rendezvous at Pinedale since **1936**, on the second full weekend of July (2026: 9–12 July); the pageant is put on by the separate **Green River Rendezvous Pageant Association**; the **Museum of the Mountain Man** (opened 1990; Sublette County Historical Society with the county; ~12,000 visitors a season) hosts an art and wine auction, a fur-trade journal forum, a Mountain Man Hall of Fame, a trappers' camp and a Plains Indian encampment, with black-powder, sign-language, beaver-trapping and packing demonstrations; programs free, sponsored partly by a gas company (museum page, SOURCED; 1936 and pageant from snippets, UNVERIFIED). (Context: William Sublette, for whom the cutoff was misnamed, was a rendezvous-era trader; R1.)
- **Farson's stop (K10, K12):** the **Farson Mercantile**, "Home of the Big Cone," was leased in 2007 by a local couple (the husband also owns the Farson Feed Store, bought 2005); travelers stop mostly in spring and summer; owners still get handwritten letters with photos; staffing is hard because the work is seasonal ("many trained staff in spring and summer and few in winter") (SweetwaterNOW, 2019-05-25, SOURCED). It grew out of the Eden Valley irrigation project, sold flour, harness leather and ammunition, served as post office and boarding house, and still sells hunting licenses and groceries (WY State Historical Society page via snippet; that page was 404 in R1 and was not re-requested; UNVERIFIED).

### E. Civic life, rules and the decision unit

One source ties the 1996 installation to the 50th anniversary of Victory rather than the railway centenary; the regional tourism site's railway-centenary version (R1, SOURCED) is the primary one. Who commissioned it: still NOT FOUND.
- **Everyday city, by residents (K5, K9) (T—J, "6 problems of Vladivostok," 2025-07-23, reader comments, SOURCED):** "constant" traffic jams; many families own two cars; cars parked three deep on any free space; "built for cars rather than people"; living without a car "very difficult"; some buses stop running in the evening, so "life after 8 p.m. is difficult"; hot water cut off for months; frequent power cuts compared to the 1990s; streets dug up in spring and left open until autumn; a resident living since 1965 in a Khrushchev-era one-room flat shared with her adult son; local vegetables expensive; salaries "not comparable" to Moscow's; many young people leave (one former resident counted six classmates gone to St. Petersburg). (One-sided by design: a complaints compilation.)
- **Weather as a setting (K7):** June–July are the fog-and-humidity months, with the sea warming slowly; August and the first half of September are the bathing season and typhoon season; swimming is allowed only at beaches approved each season (travel-blog and tourism snippets; UNVERIFIED). Weekend traffic jams build on the routes to Shamora (Lazurnaya Bay, ~30 km out, the city's largest beach) and Russky Island (Sovcombank journal snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **The 2020 ice storm (K1, K5, K9) (Meduza, 2020-11-24, SOURCED):** on the night of 19 November 2020, ice up to 12 mm thick (unseen in 30 years) coated lines and trees; >90,000 Vladivostok residents lost power, water or heat the first evening (pumps stopped without power); ~53,000 were still without power five days later; >600 people treated for fractures; a homeless man froze into the ice by the Pokrovsky Cathedral. **How people coped:** gas-cylinder stoves (queues, prices doubled), water sold out, phones charged in cafes and at neighbors', neighbors with power offering tea, meals and beds, cafes feeding people, some burning fires in courtyards; ~2,500 volunteers; field kitchens; round-the-clock kindergarten groups. The Russky Island bridge closed for ice on its cables; a ferry discontinued in 2012 was revived (one trip every two hours, 20 cars, queues of hundreds); FEFU sheltered stranded people and students built a delivery bot. Residents said official help reached some districts only on the third day; fake voice messages predicting a citywide blackout spread panic. [SM: competence under pressure; man versus society]
- **The transit camp (K9) [P6] (Rambler/News Far East, "The Poet's Last Address: Barrack 11," 2018-12-27, SOURCED):** the Vladivostok transit point for prisoners bound for Kolyma (Dalstroy's gold mines) operated **1931–1941** in today's Morskoy Gorodok area, ~4 km from Vtoraya Rechka station (hence memoirs name the station), on ~7 hectares bounded by Dneprovskaya, Ilyicheva, Pechorskaya and Vostretsova streets, with separate zones for men, women, criminals and political prisoners; prisoners were marched to the Churkin cape and loaded onto ships for Nagaevo (Magadan); from the war years transports moved to Nakhodka and then Vanino. Prisoners who waited there included Shalamov, Korolev and the actor Zhzhenov. **Osip Mandelstam** arrived 1938-10-12 and died 1938-12-27 (typhus or heart failure is disputed); he was probably buried in a mass grave near an old fortress moat, now likely a pedestrian alley on Vostretsova Street beside a Pacific Fleet unit that occupies about a sixth of the former camp; the rest is housing. No plaque at the site was mentioned. A Mandelstam monument (sculptor Valery Nenazhivin, 1998, for the 60th anniversary) was vandalized and moved in 2004 to a square at the Vladivostok State University of Economics and Service. His 1938 case was overturned in 1956, the 1934 case in 1987. (A separate camp, Vladlag, operated at Vtoraya Rechka into the 1950s: ruwiki snippet; page 401, NOT READ; UNVERIFIED.)
- **The Pokrovsky Park and cathedral (K9):** the park began as the city cemetery on a hillside in the 1870s; the cemetery was later destroyed and a Soviet park laid out; the cathedral (early 20th c.), which once ran a school for children from the Korean quarter, was demolished in 1935 and its bricks used for a teachers' institute; it was rebuilt on the old foundation by 2006 and now houses a parish school and an alcohol-and-drug rehabilitation center (tonkosti/kupibilet snippets; UNVERIFIED). [deferred mortuary note: recorded as fact only]

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **Trawler crews (K7, K14) [R] (Vokrug Sveta, 2025-01-21, interview with a trawler captain-director, SOURCED):** voyages now up to six months (a year or more twenty years ago); the season runs herring (January) and pollock (February–March) in the Sea of Okhotsk, maintenance in Vladivostok in April when Okhotsk closes, then the Bering Sea (June–September); **six hours on, six off**, no weekends or holidays; crew of 70, four-berth cabins; a **sauna** where crew warm up after the hold or deck; women work as cooks, in the buffet and sorting caviar; pay good but unstable ("some crew buy apartments after a good voyage, while others spend everything within months"); opposing shifts compete over catch and processing; joint pain, hearing loss (engineers), eyesight loss (caviar sorters); calls home once cost ~$10/minute by satellite phone, now patchy internet; crew changes by helicopter when ice blocked the harbor (2008); "after long voyages his children had already grown up without him." The **Far Eastern State Technical Fisheries University** (founded 1930) supplies the fleet's officers (RG 2026 snippet; UNVERIFIED). The seafarers' union has pressed for shore periods to count toward pensions (Rambler snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Dockers (K1, K9) (Rambler/Primpress, 2020-10-11, SOURCED):** in October 2020 dockers of the **Vladivostok Commercial Sea Port** protested the dismissal of the general director, threw helmets at the FESCO president and the Moscow-appointed director, and ~1,000 port workers attended a meeting on 6 October under the banner "Dockers for Yusupov"; a docker of seven years said wages had risen from ~70–80k to ~140–150k rubles a month under the dismissed director; the workers feared cuts if port land was sold. Prosecutors ruled a strike organized by the union illegal because the port's work is tied to sustaining the population (EastRussia snippet; UNVERIFIED). [SM: man versus society]
- **The car trade as a way of life (K14, K8) (News.ru, 2019-12-07, interview with Vasily Avchenko, author of *Pravyi Rul*, SOURCED):** in the early 1990s used Japanese cars with automatic gearboxes, power steering and ABS were "a bright spot" in a region neglected by Moscow; duty rises in 2002 and 2008 produced, in turn, cars imported disassembled and re-registered against a rotted local car, and cars cut in half for import and rewelded locally; owners now keep cars longer; 70–80% of cars on Primorye's roads were still right-hand drive; older 1980s–90s Toyotas and Nissans are kept "well cared for"; drivers once carried weapons or traveled with police friends when driving cars west through dangerous cities; independent garage mechanics keep loyal customers and repair automatic transmissions by hand. "Nowadays it's considered that if you're really cool, you no longer drive a right-hand-drive car," but "Right-hand drive isn't giving up its position." In 1999 more than 70% of cars with Primorye plates were right-hand-drive Japanese imports (Za Rulyom 1999 snippet; UNVERIFIED). A local drift and JDM scene exists (video and blog snippets; UNVERIFIED). After 2023 sanctions Japan banned exports to Russia of hybrids, EVs and cars over 1.9 liters (auto.ru snippet; UNVERIFIED). [R: repair culture]
- **Uzbek and Central Asian workers (K4, K7) [P9] (Rambler/Primpress, 2021-05-13, SOURCED):** most Uzbek migrants to the Far East choose Primorye; the flow grew more than 5.5-fold since the early 2000s; ~800 Uzbek students in Primorye (~400 at FEFU); a **Consulate General of Uzbekistan** opened in Vladivostok on 2019-03-01; most migrants work in trade and services, markets, small fast-food outlets and construction for ≥30–35k rubles; undocumented workers risk withheld wages (the consulate recovered ~$500,000 in 2020); many earlier arrivals gained citizenship and brought relatives. Russian Wikipedia gives the city the largest Muslim community in Primorye (a 2013 mufti's estimate of 60,000) with Uzbek, Tajik and Kyrgyz organizations and a prayer house (ru.wikipedia, read at offset 200,000, SOURCED). A November 2025 regional-government discussion stressed that such workers are needed in utilities, services and construction (FedPress snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Koreans (K2, K9):** on 2014-09-20 the **Association of Korean Organizations of Primorye** and the krai administration held an evening in the **Andreevsky Hall of the Officers' House of the Fleet** for the 150th anniversary of Korean resettlement in Russia, with performers from the DPRK, South Korea, China and Russia, a week-long tour of the krai, and a planned memorial sign of Russian-Korean friendship at the old Korean quarter; the first Korean settlement is given as Tizinkhe in the Khasan district (EastRussia, 2014-09-20, SOURCED). - **Religious communities (K4) (ru.wikipedia, SOURCED):** Orthodox (Moscow Patriarchate; a ROCOR diocese; Old Believers; Armenian Apostolic); Catholic and Lutheran (St. Paul's) communities revived in the 1990s; five Baptist communities, Adventist, Methodist, Pentecostal, Presbyterian (a seminary moved to Vladivostok in 1998; 105 graduates 1998–2015); a synagogue (Beit Sima); a Buddhist (Karma Kagyu) center since 1995; Muslim organizations (above).

## 6 · #14 Coldfoot Camp, Dalton Highway

**Round 2 focus:** the people who make the "last services" work and pass through it (DOT crews, camp staff, truckers, aurora tourists, hunters, cyclists, Wiseman's residents), the rules of the road and the corridor, the history of the tourist trade, and the 2026 land transfer's effect on the nearest neighbors. Care note kept: very small; used for the "last services" role, not scale.

### A. Other settings
- **The DOT maintenance chain (K1, K7, K14) [R] (Alaska DOT&PF, Dalton maintenance page, read 2026-11-20, SOURCED):** seven year-round camps, each ~60–70 miles: Livengood (0–28, 3 operators), Seven Mile (MP 62; 28–100; 2), Jim River (MP 138; 100–156.2; 2), **Coldfoot (MP 175; 156.2–209.2; 2)**, **Chandalar (MP 239; 209.2–284.5, including Atigun Pass; 3)**, Sag River (MP 305; 284.5–356; 3), Deadhorse (356–414; 4, also maintaining the airport). Camps run 7 days, 6 a.m.–5:30 p.m.; crews **work week-on, week-off and live in camp during their shift; shift change usually Wednesdays**, disrupted by weather and canceled flights. ~$16.5M a year (SFY2019; $10.5M state, $6M federal); plowing, pothole repair and grading are state-funded only; budget cuts since 2015 reduced capacity; warmer winters bring icing, heavier rain makes gravel ungradable, and streams flood "in places where they never have before." No cell coverage; camps have only low-speed internet and phone lines, so "operators must return to camp to report conditions"; no 24/7 operations center; winter condition updates by 8 a.m. weekdays; three message boards (Fox and both sides of Atigun Pass). The 2015 flood closed the highway 28 days. (R1 had given "6 a.m.–6:30 p.m., two weeks on/off" from DOT's general page; this page differs: noted as a source conflict.) A state worker died in a fall from equipment at the Chandalar camp in 2014 (AP snippet; UNVERIFIED). In 2006 Coldfoot's maintenance station had "seven half-time residents" (Project Jukebox, below).
- **The visitor center's grounds (K10) (BLM AIVC activities page, SOURCED):** nightly 8 p.m. talks in the auditorium ("geology to bear safety to the history of the 'Haul Road'"); topics posted at the center and **at Coldfoot Camp**; self-guided trails: Dettmers Nature Trail (~½ mile, past the historic **Coldfoot cemetery**), a mining exhibit with a miner's-cabin replica, a pipeline viewing deck, and a ⅓-mile connector through boreal forest to Coldfoot Camp; all free; summer contact in Coldfoot, winter contact via the BLM Fairbanks office. Opened 2004 as a $5-million partnership of BLM, NPS and USFWS (search summary; UNVERIFIED). [deferred mortuary note: the cemetery recorded as a fact only]
- **BLM campgrounds on the road (K5) (BLM "Dalton Highway Campgrounds," PDF saved and read locally, SOURCED):** Five Mile (MP 60; artesian water; the road's only RV dump station, $5), Arctic Circle (MP 115; renovated 2021; 19 sites; no water), **Marion Creek (MP 180; 27 sites, potable water, bear lockers; $12)**, Galbraith Lake (MP 275; 12 sites; creek water to treat; $12); open about May–September, first come, first served; fees paid by cash, check or an app whose card charge goes through "once you are back in cell service."
- **Wiseman, 13 miles north (K4, K7) [P9] (Northern Alaska Environmental Center intern series, 2026-07-14, SOURCED):** few year-round residents (12 in 2022: Wikipedia via snippet); no store or restaurant, so food is hauled in; one German-born couple of ~40 years hosts visitors' breakfasts; a longtime resident leads tours, volunteers as the National Weather Service's daily observer, and warned downstream villages of a flood by social media; a younger couple built **Arctic Hive** (three guest cabins and two domes, materials hauled up the hill by hand), reachable on foot or in winter by dogsled, with sled dogs and winter retreats; firewood is cut from standing dead trees; "once the permafrost melts, it's gone." A 2006 interview contrasts Coldfoot ("service-oriented and industrial") with Wiseman ("residential, natural, historic") (Project Jukebox, SOURCED). Roughly 30 gold-rush cabins remain, used mostly by seasonal residents; aurora tourism is Wiseman's winter trade (alaska.org snippets; UNVERIFIED).

### B. Subcultures and groups
- **The tour trade and the winter turn (K3, K7) (UAF Project Jukebox, interview with Brett Carlson, part 1, 2006-11-17, SOURCED):** two tour companies began at Coldfoot in **1987**, Royal Highway (later Princess Tours) and **Northern Alaska Tour Company** (NATC, the first to run tours on the Dalton); Royal Highway had Dick Mackey renovate pipeline ATCO barracks into visitor rooms. **On the truck stop's first day in 1981 "more than 200 truckers were waiting in line"**; truckers helped build the larger facility in **1983**; falling oil prices in **1986** cut trucking and made Coldfoot costly to run; Mackey sold in **1990** to an Alaska corporation that subleased to a couple; **Sukakpak, Inc.** bought it in **1997** (R1 had May 2001 from a 2001 trade article: dates conflict). The interviewee's first winter brought "only five visitors"; aurora visitors grew by word of mouth, some returning yearly; one asked the guide "to stop the tour ... and allow him to just watch." Visitors were once separate streams (truckers, independent travelers, tour groups) and "now they mix." Winter costs: fuel hauled in, parts flown in, generators burning heavily at −50 °F, with backup generators because power must be restored within 30 minutes. The visitor center had ~six seasonal workers; a small air taxi served Coldfoot in summer. [R: shared power and heat rules]
- **Truckers (K14, K7) [SM: competence under pressure]:** a Fairbanks owner-operator hauls drill pipe, explosives and fuel; a typical winter load is a five-axle fuel tanker at 114,000 lb to Prudhoe; **in winter DOT lets Prudhoe-bound loads shift 4,000 lb from trailer to truck for traction** (42,000 lb on the drives); Alaska allows a 15-hour driving limit within a 20-hour duty window with no mandatory 30-minute break, because Fairbanks–Prudhoe cannot be done in the lower-48 11 hours; Atigun Pass grades are 12–14%; blizzards with drifting snow once closed the Beaver Slide for 12 hours; in such a blizzard he keeps moving rather than stopping, because a breakdown can mean hours before help; "Nobody plans to fail, but people fail to plan" (Western Star driver profile, undated, SOURCED). Another driver recalled waiting ~20 hours for a snowblower and being stuck days in Prudhoe, "two or three times in the winter" (Overdrive snippet; UNVERIFIED; that site's Coldfoot article was 403 in R1 and no Overdrive page was requested in R2). Drivers call winter's packed ice at −20s the best traction of the year (snippet; UNVERIFIED).
- **Bowhunters (K6, K7):** the five-mile corridor is **archery only for big game** (small game by falconry); the **Prudhoe Bay Closed Area** bans big-game hunting; the Central Arctic caribou herd (~35,000 in 2022, growing; the Board of Game raised the 26B bag limit in March 2024 to five caribou of either sex for residents, two bulls for nonresidents) moves unpredictably; "many hunters use the road, so you will likely not be alone"; hiking tussocks "is like hiking through a field of bowling balls," and many hunters suffer "an agonizing return trip -or trips-" packing meat across the five miles; some hunt in spring on skis or snowshoes; grizzlies are common north of the range; **stopping on the highway where you cannot get fully off it can bring a ticket of up to $1,000** if it obstructs traffic or pipeline access; wildlife troopers patrol; pack out human waste (ADF&G, "Hunting along the Dalton Highway on the North Slope — Unit 26B," 2025, PDF saved and read locally, SOURCED).
- **Cyclists (K3):** bikepackers ride Fairbanks–Deadhorse (~8 days in one account) through dust and mud, with friendly waves from some trucks; there is no public access to the Arctic Ocean from Deadhorse except by authorized tour (bikepacking/travel-blog and BLM snippets; UNVERIFIED).
- **Wiseman and the 2026 land transfer (K1, K9) [SM: man versus society] (R1 extended):** after the transfer, Wiseman hunters who could take one moose each winter on nearby federal land must go to federal land farther away or enter a state lottery open to all Alaskans and to guides with out-of-state clients; the exception that let them snowmobile within five miles of the road for hunting and firewood ended, with the state allowing travel on ten historic routes (High Country News, 2026-08-31, SOURCED). The transfer took effect 2026-05-05 (state) / 2026-05-06 (R1); 61,845 acres not top-filed by the state keep the federal subsistence priority; the state scheduled a public meeting in Wiseman on 2026-06-03; ten environmental groups sued (DNR, BLM, Alaska Public snippets; UNVERIFIED). Advocates say new antimony mines bring more year-round people and more hunting pressure on moose (northern.org snippet; UNVERIFIED; one side of a dispute).
- **Koyukon and Iñupiaq neighbors (P9):** Allakaket is predominantly Koyukon Athabascan and Alatna mostly Kobuk Iñupiaq; after the August 1994 flood, part of Allakaket moved two miles inland and Alatna was rebuilt a mile downriver (UAF ACCAP and Jukebox snippets; UNVERIFIED). At Anaktuvuk Pass the **Simon Paneak Memorial Museum** (opened 1986; the community voted the name) keeps Nunamiut history, including caribou-skin masks, the community's best-known craft (museum and NPS snippets; UNVERIFIED).
