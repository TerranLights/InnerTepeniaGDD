## 1 · #10 Independence and St. Joseph, Missouri (the trail "jumping-off" towns)

**Claim to test:** outfitting towns where emigrants bought supplies, joined companies and swapped route news before committing to the overland crossing (K5, K14).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with corrections.** (a) Both towns were outfitting and assembly points, but they were also *competitors* for that trade, and the trade was seasonal (spring), so "outfitting town" meant a town that filled, emptied and lived off a single annual surge. (b) Independence's role came first from the **Santa Fe trade** (a two-way international commercial road to Mexico, from 1821) and only later from Oregon/California emigrants; the outfitters were in place for the traders before the emigrants came. (c) Company organization was real but often happened at or just beyond the river, and companies governed themselves by written constitutions (K6). (d) "Swapping route news" is plausible and partly documented (newspaper boosterism, endorsements from men who had made the trip), but I found no institution dedicated to it (see F).

### A. Snapshot

**Independence (Jackson County, Missouri River's south side, east of today's Kansas City)**
- Platted and founded **1827**; the first Log Courthouse was "built by enslaved labor" (City of Independence timeline). SOURCED — independencemo.gov timeline page, read 2026-11-21.
- A "frontier village of only a few hundred people" that became the eastern terminus of the Santa Fe Trail, then an outfitting post for Oregon and California emigrants. SOURCED — independencemo.gov NFTM trails-history page.
- Steamboats passed or stopped daily, March–November, at the Independence landings of **Blue Mills** and **Wayne City**; "Independence dominated as the favored trail outfitting place until about 1850." Travelers bought supplies "for the five-month, 2,000-mile trek." SOURCED — NPS/OCTA/City poster *Three Trails from Independence* (PDF, text extracted locally). The poster gives the Santa Fe Trail as **800 miles**; the city page gives **900 miles**; the NPS New Mexican traders article says "nearly 800 miles one way." Discrepancy noted.
- City's own claim: "An estimated **400,000** migrants later follow the Santa Fe, California, and Oregon trails, all of which started in Independence" (1843 entry). SOURCED as the city's claim; the total is a city figure, not verified here.
- Today: **123,011** residents (2020 census). Largest industries 2023: health care and social assistance (8,143 people), retail (7,068), manufacturing (6,288). UNVERIFIED (search snippets of census.gov QuickFacts and Data USA). City lists major employers incl. Centerpoint Medical Center, City of Independence, Independence School District, Government Employees Hospital Association. UNVERIFIED (snippet).

**St. Joseph (Buchanan County, on the Missouri's east bank ~55 mi upriver)**
- Began **1826** as Joseph Robidoux's Blacksnake Hills trading post for the American Fur Company; Robidoux bought it **1830**; the land joined Missouri by the **Platte Purchase** (proclamation **28 March 1837**, via treaties with the Potawatomi [Treaty of Chicago 1835] and the Iowa and Sauk and Fox [1836]); town plat filed **26 July 1843**; incorporated **26 Feb 1845**. SOURCED — Missouri State Archives timeline 1821–1860; EBSCO Research Starters "St. Joseph, Missouri Historic Site"; gateway-octa.org trail history.
- About **1,000** residents in 1849 serving an estimated **50,000** gold seekers that year (EBSCO, SOURCED). Other figures circulating: "30,000 (1849) / 50,000 (1852)" (UNVERIFIED, snippet). gateway-octa.org states "the 1849 migration of about **500,000** emigrants" — this appears to be an error on that page (it exceeds most estimates for the whole trail era); recorded but treated as unreliable.
- Silas Newcomb (1850): St. Joseph had "some two thousand five hundred inhabitants," four good-sized hotels, ~20 stores, groceries and bakeries. SOURCED — NPS *Sweet Freedom's Plains* ch. 2.
- Today: **72,473** residents (2020 census), metro **121,467**. UNVERIFIED (snippet). Population "has stayed around 70,000–80,000 for the last century"; it was "about twice Kansas City's in 1860." SOURCED — KCUR "beginner's guide" 2024-07-20.

### B. What else exists there (economy, institutions, how goods arrived, who lived and worked there)

**The outfitting economy (historic)**
- [K5, K14, P8] Independence 1846 (Parkman): shops for emigrants and Santa Fe traders; "incessant hammering and banging" from **a dozen blacksmiths' sheds** where wagons were repaired and horses and oxen shod. SOURCED — NPS ch. 2.
- [K5] Josiah Gregg (*Commerce of the Prairies*, 1844): the "prairie adventurer" arrives "about the first of May… they purchase their provisions for the road, and many of their mules, oxen, and even some of their wagons." SOURCED — quoted on the NPS poster.
- [K5, P8] St. Joseph *Gazette*, 16 Feb 1849: **19 dry goods stores, 2 flour mills, 3 drug stores, 3 large hotels, 2 ferries**; local trades included saddlers, shoemakers, wagon makers, blacksmiths, tailors, gunsmiths. 2 April 1849 dispatch: every lodging full, people camping out. SOURCED — gateway-octa.org (St. Joseph Gateway Chapter, OCTA) trail history page.
- [K5] Costs (EBSCO, SOURCED): ferry over the Missouri **$5 per person, 50¢ per animal**; ~**$100** for a wagon; ~**$1,000** to outfit. NPS "Traveling the Emigrant Trails" (SOURCED): average wagon ~**$85**; complete outfit for a year in 1850 **$800–$1,200**; wagon ~10 ft × 4 ft carrying up to 2,500 lb, hardwood (maple, hickory, oak), wheels of osage orange or white oak. A yoke of oxen late 1840s ~$25–$65 (UNVERIFIED, snippet).
- [K5] Supply quantities: Joseph Ware's *The Emigrant's Guide* (St. Louis, 1849) list for three people included >1,000 lb flour and 600 lb bacon (NPS, SOURCED). Lansford Hastings: 200 lb flour, 150 lb bacon, 10 lb coffee per emigrant (UNVERIFIED, OCTA PDF snippet). Joel Palmer 1847: 200 lb flour per adult (UNVERIFIED, snippet).
- [K14, P8, SM:power rising step by step] **Hiram Young** (b. 1812 Tennessee, enslaved at birth; free **1847**; d. 1882): carpenter turned wagon maker; shop opened **1851** on North Liberty Street; **1860 census: 6,000 yokes and 300 wagons**; wagons built to haul ~6,000 lb; **50–60 employees** at peak; "one of the wealthiest men in Independence"; fled to Fort Leavenworth in 1861 because of border tensions; sued the U.S. for war losses and died before resolution; after the war started the first local school for African American children and was a founding member of Saint Paul's AME Church. SOURCED — NPS "Hiram Young" page (updated 2023-03-07). City timeline (SOURCED): "runs the largest factory in Independence" in 1860. Other figures in circulation ("800–900 wagons a year," UNVERIFIED snippet) conflict with the census figure; the census figure is the sourced one. Family lawsuit continued to 1907 and was lost (UNVERIFIED, snippet).
- [K5, K8] The Santa Fe trade: New Mexican merchant families (Chávez, Otero, Yrizarri, Perea) intermarried, formed joint ventures and dominated the trade; by the 1840s they bought goods in Baltimore, New York and Philadelphia; an 1854 Yrizarri invoice lists purchases from 19 suppliers; a July 1859 Perea caravan carried 35 tons, 14 wagons, 16 men, 162 cattle; teenage drovers were hired at $6–$8/month (José Liberado Gurulé, hired at 16 in 1867, an eleven-month round trip for $8 total). By Oct 1867 most goods went by rail to Hays, Kansas. SOURCED — NPS "New Mexican Traders on the Santa Fe Trail." Missouri goods were "one-third of the cost of goods shipped from Chihuahua." SOURCED, same.
- [K9, K5] **The competition between towns.** The *St. Joseph Gazette* ran editorials Feb–March 1849 urging emigrants to outfit there, claiming Independence's route was ~90 miles (six days) longer and that St. Joseph's route crossed no major rivers; it printed endorsements from men who had traveled the route in 1847–48 (Gilpin, Stockton, Kearny, Goodyear, Meek, Harris). The *Independence Expositor* (1847) had called the St. Joseph road "almost impassible." SOURCED — gateway-octa.org. The towns "competed fiercely"; emigrants were their main economic base. SOURCED — NPS ch. 2.
- [K9] **Independence's eclipse.** A flood in 1844 washed away the Wayne City landing (UNVERIFIED, Legends of America snippet); sandbars at the landing and the 1849 cholera reportedly turned emigrants toward Westport (UNVERIFIED, OCTA snippet; OCTA pages refuse fetches). NPS poster (SOURCED): dominance "until about 1850."

**Ferries (St. Joseph) [K1, K5, K6]**
- 1 April–15 June 1849: **1,508 wagons** crossed at St. Joseph (≈6,032 people at four per wagon); Duncan's ferry 4 miles above town carried **685**; ferries at Bontown, Savannah and up to the Bluffs ~**2,000** more. SOURCED — Kansas Historical Quarterly, "Ferries in Kansas, Part I, Missouri River, continued" (George A. Root), kansashistory.gov.
- Ebenezer Blackiston's flatboat replaced in **1852** by the steam ferry *Tidy Adala*; by 1858 a boat carrying 12–15 wagons per load in ~2 minutes. June 1859: ~40 teams/day, ~200 people/day. 1859 half-price fares: foot 5¢, horse/mule 15¢, yoke of oxen and wagon 40¢; trips every 15 minutes, sunrise to sunset. SOURCED — same.
- Licensing [K1, K6]: Missouri county courts licensed Missouri-side ferries (e.g., Buchanan County 1839); the Kansas Territorial Legislature (1855–60) chartered Kansas-side ferries, usually with **exclusive rights of one to two miles of river** for 15–25 years; Elwood's city council in late 1859 set ferry hours by ordinance (~6:30 a.m. to ~7 p.m.), which the owner did not strictly follow. SOURCED — same.
- The 1836 treaty with the Iowas, Sacs and Foxes promised the Indians "one ferryboat"; the Sac built a log crossing on the Wolf River and collected a toll. SOURCED — KHQ ferries article; gateway-octa.org.
- NPS (SOURCED, "St. Joseph Ferry Landing & Riverwalk"): ferries and riverboats used landings here **1849–1955** between St. Joseph and Elwood, Kansas; Missouri River ferries normally ran only in daylight; in 1852 John H. Clark's party joined the end of a "huge crowd of wagons" waiting.

**Transport after the trail years**
- Hannibal & St. Joseph Railroad completed **13 Feb 1859**, first railroad across Missouri; St. Joseph became the eastern terminus of the Central Overland, California & Pikes Peak Express stagecoach line. Pony Express from St. Joseph **3 April 1860**; end date given as **24 Oct 1861** (Missouri State Archives) or **20 Nov 1861** (EBSCO) — discrepancy noted (the first is the telegraph completion date, the second likely the last delivery). Run by Russell, Majors & Waddell from the Patee House; horses from Pikes Peak Stables. SOURCED — SOS timeline; EBSCO; NPS ferry landing page (first rider boarded the steamer *Ebenezer* 7:20 p.m.).
- St. Joseph's livestock and meatpacking began supplying travelers during the gold rush; Texas cattle came to the railhead late 1860s; Missouri River bridge 1873; St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. formed **1887**. SOURCED — EBSCO. By the early 20th c. meatpacking and livestock employed >4,500 (UNVERIFIED, snippet). 1870s onward: major wholesale supplier to the West; Victorian mansions (Harvey Ellis designs 1888–93). SOURCED — EBSCO.

### D. How the defining feature shaped these things
- [K5, K14] The spring surge produced a town economy of **trades that equip and repair** (blacksmiths, wagon makers, saddlers, gunsmiths, yoke makers), **overflow lodging** (full hotels, camping in the streets, Robidoux Row's temporary units), **river crossings run as licensed monopolies**, and **newspapers used as competitive advertising** for the town's route. All SOURCED above.
- [K7] Work rhythm was set by **grass**: emigrants gathered in early April and left mid-April to May "once the grass began to green," because livestock had to graze; too late risked mountain snow. SOURCED — NPS "Traveling the Emigrant Trails." Steamboat season March–November (SOURCED, poster); ferries ran sunrise to sunset.
- [K9] Crowding, cash-carrying strangers and spring disease made these towns places of theft and **cholera**: 1849 was Missouri's most serious cholera epidemic (>4,000 dead in St. Louis alone, SOS SOURCED); NPS (SOURCED) says cholera struck "each spring" in the early gold-rush years and spread through "unsanitary outfitting towns"; cholera reached the Iowa/Sac-Fox mission community in 1849 and smallpox killed more there in 1850 (gateway-octa.org, SOURCED).
- [K3, K8] Today the heritage is **re-staged as festivals and museums**, funded by Chamber and nonprofit booths (SantaCaliGon) or by an arts council (Trails West!, which failed on cost and attendance).

### E. Structural features (industry, economy, arts, work and life)

**Native nations and removal [K9, P9] — reported plainly**
- **Kanza (Kaw):** Treaty of **3 June 1825** at St. Louis (William Clark): ceded all Kanza lands in Missouri and more west of it; the land base fell from **18,314,240** to **712,513 acres** (K-State Chapman Center); reservation on the Kansas River; $3,500/yr for 20 years; 36 sections on the Big Blue River to be sold for Kanza schools; up to $3,000 to pay Missourians' claims. Historians and present-day Kaw citizens dispute the preamble's claim that the signers were authorized (leaders Shonganega and Wakanzare were absent). Rising Santa Fe Trail traffic was among the pressures. Further cessions 1846 and 1859; removal to Oklahoma after the **1872** Kaw Land Bill. SOURCED — K-State "Treaty of 1825" page. A separate Kansa treaty of **16 Aug 1825** at Sora Kansas Creek granted rights to survey and pass through for what became the Santa Fe Trail (UNVERIFIED, digitreaties snippet).
- **Osage:** Treaty of **2 June 1825** at St. Louis (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Iowa and Sac & Fox of the Missouri:** treaty at Fort Leavenworth (17 Sept 1836, UNVERIFIED for the day) ceded the land between Missouri and the Missouri River (the Platte Purchase country, where St. Joseph stands) for **$7,500** plus houses, fenced land, a farmer, blacksmith, teacher and livestock; both tribes were on reservations west of the river by fall **1837**. The Presbyterian **Iowa and Sac & Fox Mission** (founded Nov 1837; permanent building 1846, $8,000 of which $6,000 came from tribal annuities) printed a hymnal and grammars in the Iowa language from 1843; the 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act and new treaty reduced the lands; mission closed 1863. SOURCED — Kansas Historical Society Kansapedia. EBSCO (SOURCED) notes the land was annexed as slave territory.
- **Relocated Eastern nations:** the U.S. resettled Kickapoo, Delaware, Potawatomi and others west of the Missouri; these were among the first peoples emigrants met in NE Kansas; the Pawnee charged emigrants to cross their lands and use their resources and sometimes took belongings or livestock when refused; trail travel spread cholera, influenza, smallpox and measles and drove bison from the Platte Valley; by 1859 the Pawnee had signed away their remaining land. SOURCED — NPS "A Gathering Storm." Shawnee trader Charles Blue Jacket kept a trading post at the Wakarusa ford (UNVERIFIED, snippet).

**Border war and Civil War [K9]**
- Kansas-Nebraska Act (30 May 1854) set off the Missouri-Kansas border war. SOURCED — SOS. Battles of Independence **11 Aug 1862** (Quantrill captured the city) and **1864** (Price). SOURCED — city timeline.
- **General Order No. 11** (25 Aug 1863, Gen. Thomas Ewing, four days after the Lawrence raid) ordered rural residents of Jackson, Cass, Bates and northern Vernon counties living more than a mile from military posts to leave within 15 days; loyal residents could resettle at posts; Cass County fell from 9,794 to ~600 permitted residents; relaxed after Jan 1864. **K6 — rules of everyday behavior**
- **Company constitutions.** Overland companies "usually drafted constitutions, and sometimes bylaws, until about 1854"; they were brief frameworks to encourage cooperation, set procedures for judging unacceptable conduct, and some were signed as compacts; some created representative legislatures and authorized capital punishment; physical punishment was rare; a member could escape most sanctions (not death) by leaving the company; governance worked by mediation, and companies failed when "harmony" was lost. Beyond the Missouri, travelers were "without enforceable law, courts, or police." SOURCED — *Encyclopedia of the Great Plains* (UNL), "Overland Trail law." Companies typically elected a captain before departure; a wagonmaster called the start. SOURCED — NPS "Traveling." In May 1843 at Fitzhugh's Mill >900 emigrants divided into companies and elected wagon masters (Peter Burnett captain) (UNVERIFIED snippet). Company rules often restricted drinking and gambling; Sabbath observance varied widely (65 diaries of 1849–50 show "diverse patterns"); some companies rested Sunday for the animals (UNVERIFIED, snippets).
- **Town rules:** ferry licensing and hours by county court, territorial charter and city ordinance (SOURCED above). No Independence or St. Joseph town ordinance on emigrant camping, grazing, liquor or Sunday trade was found (two searches).

**K11 — speech (very loose):** English; Spanish among New Mexican traders and drovers on the Santa Fe trade; French among the fur-trade founders (Robidoux, Chouteau); the Iowa language in mission print. German and Polish immigrant communities in later St. Joseph (German Catholic Immaculate Conception parish 1870s; Polish packinghouse workers) (UNVERIFIED snippets of a City of St. Joseph document).

## 2 · #56 Great St Bernard Hospice

**Claim to test:** a pass hospice that sheltered and rescued travelers for centuries, hospitality and rescue as one institution (K1, K14).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with corrections.** (a) Shelter and rescue were one institution for most of its history (canons, servants/porters and dogs searching from the house), but **rescue has largely passed to helicopters and organized mountain rescue**; the dogs' last recorded rescue is given as 1955, and today the canons describe their work as a **"spiritual welcome"** with only occasional searches. (b) The rescue dogs' real job, per the Bern Natural History Museum, was mainly **finding the way back to the hospice in blizzards**, not avalanche search; the brandy keg is a 19th-century myth. (c) The hospice has always depended on **gifts** (historically including a yearly sack of salt from valley residents; today donations, plus paid stays), and its dogs were spun off in 2005 into a separately funded foundation. (d) Hospitality was **free** into the 20th century (Catholic Encyclopedia 1910: "harbors gratuitously 20,000–25,000 travelers every year"); today stays are charged.

### A. Snapshot
- **Location:** Col du Grand-Saint-Bernard, Pennine Alps, ~2,469–2,473 m (sources vary: 2,469 Wikipedia EN; 2,472 gsbernard.com; 2,473 saint-bernard.ch and fr.wikipedia); commune of **Bourg-Saint-Pierre**, canton of Valais, Switzerland; the Italian border (Aosta Valley, commune of Saint-Rhémy-en-Bosses) a few hundred meters south. SOURCED — en/fr Wikipedia; gsbernard.com; saint-bernard.ch.
- **Founding:** c. **1050** by Bernard (de Menthon / of Aosta / "de Montjoux"), archdeacon of Aosta, with companions from the Aosta clergy, originally dedicated to St Nicholas, "to end attacks on travelers"; a Carolingian monastery of St Peter at Bourg-Saint-Pierre is first mentioned c. 812–820 and was probably destroyed by Saracen raids c. 940; first written mention of the church 1125. SOURCED — gsbernard.com; fr/en Wikipedia. Other founding dates in circulation: 962 (Catholic Encyclopedia 1910, SOURCED as that source's claim), 1045/1049 (UNVERIFIED snippets). Bernard's birth "923" is legend; the congregation's prior (Simon Roduit, 2023) says he was born "at the dawn of the eleventh century." SOURCED — jubilee interview PDF (text extracted locally).
- **What it is now:** a year-round house of the **Canons Regular of the Great St Bernard** (Rule of St Augustine), with lodging and communal meals for walkers, ski tourers, pilgrims and passers-by; museum, baroque church ("the highest in Europe," per the hospice) and treasury; St Bernard dogs at the pass in summer. SOURCED — gsbernard.com; saint-bernard.ch.
- **Community size over time:** 18 monks (8 priests) in 1910 (Catholic Encyclopedia, SOURCED); 20 canons in 1964 (New Catholic Encyclopedia via encyclopedia.com, SOURCED); 5 canons + 1 oblate sister in 2014, congregation 45 members, 6 more at Simplon (swissinfo 21 Aug 2014, SOURCED); "4 canons and an oblate" (saint-bernard.ch, undated, SOURCED); 2024: congregation 30 canons, only **two** living permanently at the hospice plus a consecrated lay woman (Avvenire, 10 Sept 2024, SOURCED). Prior Jean-Michel Lonfat (2024, Avvenire SOURCED); Canon Simon Roduit prior since **Nov 2025** (fr.wikipedia, SOURCED).
- **Guests:** ~11,000 overnight stays and ~20,000 meals a year (swissinfo 2014, SOURCED); ~6,000 guests in summer and ~8,000 in winter, many on skis or snowshoes (Avvenire 2024, SOURCED) — i.e., **winter is the busier season for overnight guests**.

### B. What else exists there (institutions, economy, access, who lives and works there)
- [K1, P7] **The congregation** also serves the **Simplon Hospice** (entrusted by Napoleon; founded 1801/1805 per different sources; completed by the canons at their own expense 1831–35 — UNVERIFIED snippet) and several parishes in the region (nine in Valais per a 1986 source; priories in Valais, Aosta and Taiwan in 2004 — UNVERIFIED snippets). Its administrative seat, the **Maison Hospitalière du Grand-Saint-Bernard**, is in **Martigny** (gsbernard.ch heritage page footer, SOURCED). Medieval property: in **1137** Amadeus III of Savoy gave the canons Château-Verdun at Saint-Oyen (Aosta side) (fr.wikipedia, SOURCED). 1752: the hospice became a provostship independent of Aosta (encyclopedia.com, SOURCED); the Little St Bernard hospice has been run by Italian monks since 1752 (Catholic Encyclopedia, SOURCED).
- [K1, K8] **Funding:** historically "popes and emperors granted privileges and benefices in many lands" (encyclopedia.com, SOURCED); the hospice "has historically relied on public generosity, such as valley residents giving a sack of salt each year" (swissinfo 2014, SOURCED). In 2014 it sought ~CHF 4.7–5 million for renovation (pipes, wiring, treasury display, the inn, old stables converted into a "Maison du terroir," 120 windows), with a patronage committee chaired by Christophe Darbellay. SOURCED — swissinfo 2014. Medieval peak of attendance c. 1500, judged from offerings collected Sept–Jan (Bertrand, *Le Grand Tour revisité*, SOURCED).
- [K14, K1] **Volunteers:** offer "three weeks to a month of free service to travelers" (gsbernard.com, SOURCED). Paid seasonal staff also work there: one 2025 seasonal worker's tasks were welcoming pilgrims, serving meals, upkeep and **weather observations** "to help protect walkers"; "It is not man who imposes his rhythm, but the mountain that imposes it on man" (Aleteia FR, 17 Sept 2025, SOURCED). ~10 employees and volunteers support the canons (swissinfo 2014, SOURCED).
- **Auberge de l'Hospice** (summer inn, 30 rooms, restaurant with local products, souvenir shop), run by the Bonaud-Pierroz family (gsbernard.com, SOURCED); earlier run by Annick and Stéphane Boisseaux-Monod (2014, SOURCED). The inn opens early June to mid-October; the hospice all year. Capacity "80," dinner 19:15, breakfast 08:00, guests may attend vespers and morning office (refuges.info, UNVERIFIED snippet). Dormitory from CHF 32 with breakfast (2021 listing, UNVERIFIED).
- [K5, K7] **Access:** the pass road is open roughly June to October; 2026: snow clearing began 4 May, snowpack ~11 m, Swiss side open from 29 May, pass reopened 22 June 2026 (canton of Valais via search summary, UNVERIFIED); winter closure **Thursday 5 Nov 2026, 08:00**, reopening early June 2027 (gsbernard.com, SOURCED). In winter the hospice is reached only on skis or snowshoes, ~2.5 h from Bourg-Saint-Bernard; the road is closed above ~2,000 m (UNVERIFIED snippets of RTBF, myswitzerland). The pass is reachable by road "barely five months a year" (swissinfo 2014, SOURCED). Snow covers the surroundings "nine months of the year" (Catholic Encyclopedia 1910, SOURCED). **Great St Bernard road tunnel** opened **19 March 1964** (construction from 1958; 5,798 m; Bourg-Saint-Pierre–Saint-Rhémy-en-Bosses) (UNVERIFIED snippets); a highway 1903–05 and the tunnel reduced the rescue duties (encyclopedia.com, SOURCED); the tunnel "reduced the pass's importance for goods and most tourist traffic" (swissinfo 2001, SOURCED). A 1963 Swiss–Italian agreement set up juxtaposed national control offices at the tunnel (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Bourg-Saint-Pierre** (the commune): ~211 (2020) / 222 (end 2024) residents; its medieval community lived from pass traffic and guiding (UNVERIFIED — HLS snippets; HLS pages refuse fetches). Napoleon lunched at the Auberge de la Colonne militaire, now the Hôtel du déjeuner de Napoléon Ier (UNVERIFIED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped these things
- [K1, K14, P8, SM:competence shown under pressure] **Searching was built into the house.** In 1910: "Small huts on the neighboring mountains connect to the hospice by telephone or electric bells. The monks, accompanied by their famous dogs, search the mountains for travelers"; canons Contard and Glassey died on such a search on **19 Nov 1874**. SOURCED — Catholic Encyclopedia (New Advent). "Many canons died in their work" (encyclopedia.com, SOURCED). A 1793 archival entry shows a *marronnier* (hospice porter/servant) on his round raising the alarm about an avalanche victim; in 1595 porters of Saint-Rhémy and Étroubles complained about hospitality practice, being entitled under the constitutions only to a quarter loaf of bread. SOURCED — aasm.ch AGSB archive inventory.
- [K14, R] **Dogs:** bred at the hospice from dogs probably given by Valais families in the 1660s–70s; first definite mention 1709; first as guard dogs, then trained for rescue (en Wikipedia, SOURCED). Their main job was "finding the way back to the hospice in heavy blizzards," not avalanche search; over 200 years "the canons and their dogs saved over 2,000 people"; Barry (1800–1814) credited with ~40; legends (carrying a child, killed by a soldier) are not fact. SOURCED — Natural History Museum Bern. Last recorded dog rescue 1955 (en Wikipedia, SOURCED); a 1980 rescue where a dog located victims and avalanche-dog qualifications 1966/1972 are in the archives (aasm.ch, SOURCED). By 2004, 18 dogs remained; breeding transferred to the Barry Foundation (Jan/April 2005). Helicopters took over from the 1950s (UNVERIFIED).
- [K4, K9] **The register as a record of who crossed:** opened **19 July 1812** at the urging of French authorities, kept to 1970; 521 signers 1812–1815; early signers mostly Swiss, Italian, German, Dutch and Belgian; English, Scottish, Irish from Aug 1814; officers signed but ordinary soldiers were neither named nor counted. ~50,000 émigrés (mostly clergy and nobility) crossed late 1792–1793; 43,000 French soldiers 24 May–11 Nov 1798; Napoleon's Army of Reserve crossed in May 1800 (16–20 May; ~46,000 men per the Bern museum). SOURCED — Bertrand, *Le Grand Tour revisité* (OpenEdition, first 100,000 characters read; remainder NOT READ); nmbe.ch. From 1897 the registers used printed forms (date, names, status, domicile, persons, room number, departure) (aasm.ch, SOURCED).
- [K2] **Feast of St Bernard, 15 June**: pilgrimage on foot from Bourg-Saint-Bernard at 07:30, mass 10:30, aperitif and meal, vespers 15:00 at the pass. SOURCED — gsbernard.com agenda; saint-bernard.ch June 2026 calendar. **1923**: Pius XI (letter of 10 Aug 1923) named St Bernard patron of mountain dwellers and mountaineers; a jubilee year 15 June 2023–28 Aug 2024 marked that centenary and 900 years since his canonization ("guides' festivals, exhibitions, pilgrimages, shows, alpine events, colloquia"); a Holy Door stood in the church to 15 Sept 2024. SOURCED — jubilee PDF; Avvenire. Other house observances: Corpus Christi mass with procession; Christmas masses; Christmas youth camp (16+); New Year retreat. SOURCED — gsbernard.com.
- [K8] **The dogs became a separate economy.** Barry Foundation 2024 (annual report, SOURCED): ~30 breeding dogs (32 per Euronews 2026); **43 puppies from 5 litters**, 8 kept, 16 sold abroad; no state subsidy ("entirely dependent on donors"); **58,375 donors**; income CHF 8.25 million (individual donors 4.32 M; legacies 1.55 M; restaurant 0.85 M; shops 0.51 M; museum entries 0.46 M; Martigny city subsidy 0.18 M; dog sales 0.045 M); staff costs CHF 3.42 M; new Barryland under construction (CHF 8.97 M booked); partners include the **Tunnel du Grand-Saint-Bernard**, the communes of the Entremont district, Loterie Romande, the Laiterie d'Orsières, the Maison Hospitalière du Grand-St-Bernard. "Barry social": 578 animal-assisted interventions, 50 institutions, 1,887 beneficiaries; >1,200 people on ~185 dog walks.
- [K9] **Contraband and frontier:** from the late 15th to the 18th c. the pass became the Savoy–Valais border; under the ancien régime smuggling of cheese, livestock, wine, sugar, coffee and tobacco was common, and immigration of Lombards and Aosta Valley people through the pass was strong (UNVERIFIED — HLS snippets; HLS refuses fetches).

### E. Structural features
- [K7] **Year shape:** road open ~June to October/early November; the rest of the year the house is reached on foot, skis or snowshoes; overnight guests peak in winter (ski tourers, snowshoers) while day visitors, dogs and the museum peak in summer. SOURCED — Avvenire; gsbernard.com.
- [K10] **The house holds everyone in the off-season:** communal meals where strangers "exchange stories" (a couple walking the Via Francigena from Canterbury, a father and son finishing an alpine route, a couple who had walked across Switzerland); "all the hospitality revolves around the moments of prayer and not the reverse" (Anne Marie, consecrated lay associate). ## 3 · #57 Coaching inns on the Great North Road (e.g., Stamford)

**Claim to test:** inns that changed horses and fed travelers on a long road, turning towns into stage stops (K4, K10).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with additions.** (a) Stamford was "a major stage on the Great North Road which provided a vital boost to the local economy during the 17th–mid 19th centuries" (South Kesteven appraisal); one local estimate holds that ~75% of the population depended directly or indirectly on the coaching and carrying trade. (b) The inns were not only horse-changing stations: they were **posting houses** (the innkeeper often also the postmaster), **brewers and farmers**, **party political headquarters** at elections, and — at the George — places that **separated customers by class** (a separate tap for working-class drinkers). (c) A whole system sat behind them: **turnpike trusts** (K1) collecting tolls from 1663, **mail coaches** from 1784 (exempt from tolls, run with Post Office guards and contractor-supplied horses changed about every 10 miles), and **coach proprietors** with hundreds of horses. (d) The **railway's bypass** of Stamford (1852 GNR main line via Peterborough) "signalled the collapse of the coaching trade" and closed several inns; the motor age partly revived the survivors.

### A. Snapshot
- **Stamford:** market town in south-west Lincolnshire (South Kesteven district), on the River Welland, ~25 miles south of Grantham and 11 miles NW of Peterborough; A1 bypass built **1960** along its western edge; "primarily a dormitory town" for Peterborough and "a popular tourist and retirement destination." SOURCED — South Kesteven *Stamford Conservation Area Appraisal* (2011; PDF text extracted locally).
- **Population:** ~5,000 by 1801 (doubled in the 17th–18th c.); 5,837 in 1831 (Stamford Local History Society, SOURCED); 11,000 by 1851; "over 20,000" today (appraisal, SOURCED); 20,742 in 2021 census (parish) (UNVERIFIED snippet; other boundary figures 20,278–20,745).
- **Defining feature:** roughly **halfway between London and York** on the Great North Road (greatnorthroad.co.uk, SOURCED); at peak **40 coaches a day** passed through (greatnorthroad.co.uk "The George," SOURCED; single source). A 1796 survey counted **31 inns** in Stamford plus 5 south of the river (same, SOURCED; single source). London journey: ~2 days at first, ~9 hours after turnpike improvements (same).
- **First conservation area in England**: designated **1 October 1967** (Lincolnshire HER) / September 1967 (Historic England), two months after the Civic Amenities Act; at designation it held over half of Lincolnshire's listed buildings (UNVERIFIED — snippets; appraisal cover SOURCED: "Designated October 1967").

### B. What else exists there
**The inns (Stamford)**
- **George Hotel** (70 High Street St Martin's): an inn by **1568** (licensed to sell wine; Scarre family); east range c. 1600 "almost an entire inn of around 1600"; refronted 1724 by George Portwood; north range 1787–8, south range 1791–2, funded by the Earl of Exeter (1785–92); **1815: 38 bedrooms, 10 sitting rooms, a bar, stabling for 86 horses**; new carriage entry 1850 after Station Road was cut (1849); 19th-c. wooden sign spanning the road; Grade II*. SOURCED — British History Online (RCHME *Stamford*, High Street St Martin's); Lincolnshire HER MLI95383. Had its **own brewhouse and farm** and **a separate tap for working-class drinkers**; a 40-ft stone cockpit reportedly held 500 people (18th-c. cockfighting). SOURCED — greatnorthroad.co.uk. Visitors: Charles I (1641, 1645), William III (1696), King of Denmark (1768), Duke of Cumberland (1746) (same).
- **Bull and Swan** (No. 25; medieval open hall; Cecil crest; Grade II*), **Coach and Horses** (No. 19; leased 1797 by the Earl of Exeter to victualler George Betts on condition of alterations, with stone from the Earl's Wothorpe quarry), **Anchor** ("newly erected" 1821), **Ram**, **Marquis of Granby**; a **coachmaker** at No. 23A. SOURCED — BHO RCHME. **Red Lion** near All Saints' (appraisal, SOURCED).
- **Stamford Hotel** (16 St Mary's Street): begun **1810** on the site of the Black Bull Inn (first mentioned 1546) by **Sir Gerard Noel of Exton** "as part of a campaign to win political and electoral support against the Exeters"; front by J. L. Bond; work stopped after Noel's defeat in **1812**; empty until **1825**, when Thomas Standwell leased and reopened it; sold 1845; now several small shops; Grade II*, "one of the grandest of Regency hotels." SOURCED — Lincolnshire HER MLI95172. Noel's 1812 election petition alleged a moved poll, a partial returning officer and voters pressured "by threats" (Hansard, 11 Dec 1812, UNVERIFIED snippet).

**The road system [K1]**
- **Turnpikes:** the first turnpike Act (**1663**) authorized tolls on the Old North Road (gate at Wadesmill; intended gates at Caxton and Stilton not built then); tolls 1d per horse, 6d per coach, 1s per wagon. A **1776** Act let the **Wansford–Stamford–Bourne trust** widen the road through Stamford at its own expense ("an exception"). Trusts could claim statute labor, compulsorily purchase land, issue bonds; toll-collection rights were auctioned; **mail coaches did not pay**; riots against gates (Selby 1753; Duns 1791); drovers used the Bullock Road and Sewstern Lane to avoid tolls; acts not renewed from the 1850s; roads to councils 1888. SOURCED — greatnorthroad.co.uk "Turnpikes." From 1787 the road was gated the whole way London–Edinburgh (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Mail coaches:** John Palmer's trial run 2 Aug 1784 (Bristol–London in 16 h); contractors supplied horses at stages "usually every 10 miles"; the guard (the only Post Office employee aboard) sounded a horn to clear the road and warn toll-keepers (UNVERIFIED — Wikipedia/postal-history snippets; Postal Museum page refused, 403).
- **Coach proprietors (London end):** William Chaplin reportedly had **1,800 horses** horsing 14 of the 27 coaches leaving London each night; Edward Sherman's Bull and Mouth had underground stabling for 700 horses; the **Stamford Regent** left the George and Blue Boar, Holborn, ~12 hours to Stamford (an 1822 notice planned a switch from overnight to daytime). After the railways, Chaplin moved into railways (chairman of the London & South-Western); inn stables became railway goods offices. SOURCED — greatnorthroad.co.uk "London coaching inns."
- **The Hope post-coach** (1826–): Stamford–Doncaster daily, 08:00 from both ends, arriving ~18:30, from **Standwell's Hotel**, Stamford; proprietors Thos. Standwell & Co.; headline **"NO FEES TO COACHMEN"** (the company paid its coachmen and forbade them to ask passengers for money); a rival, *The Times*, launched on the same route in 1827. SOURCED — Stamford Mercury Archive Trust blog (May 1827 notice).

**Economy and institutions (historic and current)**
- 17th–18th c.: Welland Navigation reopened sea access (export of barley, malt, grain, stone; import of coal, timber, groceries); **by 1714 six major fairs and four large cattle markets a year**; brewing, iron founding, agricultural engineering and silk spinning at workshop scale. SOURCED — appraisal.
- The **Cecils** (Earls, later Marquesses of Exeter, of Burghley House) were the major landowners and "exercised a tight control over [the town's] affairs until the late 19th century which inhibited development and expansion"; they enclosed St Martin's common (1796) into Burghley Park. SOURCED — appraisal. Councilors "largely supported the Exeter interest"; tenants who voted against Exeter's candidates were reportedly evicted after 1830. SOURCED — Stamford Local History Society, 1831 election article.
- After the railway: J. C. Grant (agricultural implements), **Blackstone's** (agricultural engineering; roots 1837 as Smith & Ashby/Rutland Iron Works; Blackstone & Co 1889; Lister from 1936), Blashfield's terracotta (1858–1875). SOURCED — appraisal; Grace's Guide snippet UNVERIFIED for Blackstone dates. **All Saints Brewery** (est. 1825; Melbourn Brothers from 1870), the only Stamford brewery still brewing (UNVERIFIED snippet). Robert Newcomb, proprietor of the *Stamford Mercury*, built the **Cornstall Buildings** (1870) for his employees (appraisal, SOURCED).
- **Stamford Mercury**: began as the *Stamford Post* (1710), renamed *Stamford Mercury* in Aug 1712; the masthead long claimed 1695; "Britain's oldest continuously published newspaper title" (claim disputed by *Berrow's Worcester Journal*); archive holds a complete run from 1785 and much from 1714 (UNVERIFIED — Wikipedia and smarchive snippets).
- **Friday market**: organized by South Kesteven District Council; 75 traders; ~14,900 people on market streets on a Friday (UNVERIFIED, magazine snippet).
- **Burghley Horse Trials** (each September since **1961**, started by the 6th Marquess of Exeter): ~170,000–175,000 visitors over four days; ~£20 million to the local economy (estimate); Stamford businesses exhibit in its shopping village; a Best Dressed Window competition in town (27 businesses in 2025). UNVERIFIED (snippets).

### C. Cultural locations

SOURCED — Stamford Arts Center history page.
- **Stamford Shakespeare Company** — founded **1968** in the monastery gardens of the George Hotel; bought near-derelict **Tolethorpe Hall** in 1977; amateur company; three plays each summer in an open-air theater; >34,000 spectators a summer; one million tickets by 2018 (UNVERIFIED — Wikipedia/Visit Lincolnshire snippets).
- **Mid-Lent Fair** — street fair under a **charter of Richard III (1484)**; changing its days requires the Home Secretary; 2024: Stamford 11–16 March, then Grantham 17–20 March; streets and car parks closed for a week; South Kesteven District Council is paid site rent and recovers staff costs; the Showmen's Guild of Great Britain asked to extend Grantham from four to five days (refused). SOURCED — Stamford Local History Society "Mid-Lent fair in the past." Its medieval decline is linked to the Hundred Years' War keeping French merchants away (appraisal, SOURCED).

**Other Great North Road inns (the tradition in two more places)**

SOURCED — stilton.org "The Golden Age of the Stagecoach."

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- [K4, P9] **Class and party in the inn.** The George kept a separate tap for working-class drinkers (SOURCED). Inns were **election headquarters**: in 1831 the reformer Charles Tennyson ("Blue") used the George and Angel (renamed **Tennyson's Arms**, March 1831) and also met at Standwell's Hotel; the Exeter ("Red") candidates used Standwell's Hotel in the 1830s; eight named inns "plumped" for Tennyson (Black Horse, Horse and Jockey, Glaziers Arms, Chequers, Nags Head, Red Lion, Salutation, Stamford Arms); "**post boys and chairmen**" were given ale during the canvass. - [K14, R] **Competence of the yard:** posting houses stabling 86–100+ horses for fresh teams; ostlers, farriers, post boys, coachmen, chambermaids, cooks; larger inns "at least 100 horses" (UNVERIFIED general sources). ## 4 · #66 Post towns of the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō (e.g., Tsumago, Hakone)

**Claim to test:** regulated stage towns with inns, porters and checkpoints spaced along highways (K1, K4).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, and the regulation ran deeper than "inns and porters."** (a) Post towns (*shukuba*) were a **state-imposed duty system**: from **1601** the Tokugawa shogunate required each designated town to keep a fixed stock of horses and porters for official relay traffic (initially **36 horses** on the Tōkaidō), at official low rates, in exchange for exemption from the land tax on house plots; surrounding villages were conscripted (*sukegō*) when the town's stock ran short — a burden that provoked a mass protest in 1764. (b) Inns were **graded by who could use them** (*honjin* and *waki-honjin* for daimyo and officials; *hatago* with meals and *kichin-yado* without meals for commoners). (c) **Checkpoints** (*sekisho*) were separate from the post towns: at Hakone the focus was on women leaving Edo, inspected by female inspectors. (d) In the modern era, Tsumago turned its post-town fabric into a **residents' compact** (1971 charter: don't sell, don't rent, don't destroy) and became Japan's first preservation district; Hakone turned into a mass-tourism town now planning a lodging tax.

### A. Snapshot
**The system**
- The five highways (Tōkaidō, Nakasendō, Kōshū, Nikkō, Ōshū) were under direct shogunal control, administered by the *dōchū bugyō* (road commissioner). UNVERIFIED (search summary of Chiba city library / MLIT pages).
- **Tōkaidō**: 53 stations (imidas, SOURCED). **Nakasendō**: 69 stations over ~530 km; Tsumago was the **42nd** from Edo (MLIT tourism text PDF, extracted locally, SOURCED).
- **1601**: Tokugawa Ieyasu instituted the post-horse system on the Tōkaidō by red-seal documents (*tenma shuinjō*, five articles) requiring **36 horses** per station (MLIT Kantō Bureau Q&A page, SOURCED but partly mis-encoded); horses later rose to 100 (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **Bargain**: post towns on the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō were exempted from the land tax on house plots (*jishi*) and in return had to provide people and horses to holders of an official horse-use pass, at "low, fixed official fares"; spare capacity could be hired privately at negotiated fees; the shogunate gave subsidies and allowed *meshimori-onna* to sustain the system. SOURCED — imidas "shukuba" entry (Yamamoto Hirofumi, 2009). Official traffic paid either nothing or the fixed official rate, "a large burden" for those supplying horses (Yamamoto, *Hōsei shigaku* 21, UNVERIFIED snippet).

**Tsumago (Nagiso town, Kiso district, Nagano Prefecture)**
- Designated a post town in 1601; declined after the national road (1892) and railway (1909) opened; preservation began in 1968; selected in 1976; 34% of its buildings date from the Edo period to before WWII; ~200 buildings repaired and restored as of 2019. SOURCED — MLIT multilingual tourism text "Tsumago" (PDF, extracted locally).
- **Preservation district**: selected **4 Sept 1976** as Japan's first Important Preservation District for Groups of Traditional Buildings, **1,245.4 ha** — "about 30% of the total area of all such districts nationwide"; includes post-town, roadside, rural and natural-landscape zones ("borrowed scenery" from the Nakasendō); 197 repair projects and 8 restoration projects 1976–2025. SOURCED — Nagiso town / Agency for Cultural Affairs district sheet (dated 8 May 2026; PDF, extracted locally).
- Nagiso town population fell from ~11,000 (1955) to ~6,100 (1985); the district has 184 buildings; fires in 1825, 1868, 1921 and 1933 each destroyed 16–38 buildings. SOURCED — Siegenthaler, "Creation Myths for the Preservation of Tsumago Post-town," *Planning Forum* 9 (2003) (PDF, extracted locally).

**Hakone (Ashigarashimo district, Kanagawa Prefecture)**
- Hakone-juku was created on the pass by moving **50 households each from Odawara and Mishima** (100 total); 198 households by the Tenpō era; at 700+ m with mist and cold winds; residents lived mostly from travelers (MLIT Kantō Bureau Q&A, SOURCED but mis-encoded). Founding year: 1618 (several snippets, UNVERIFIED; the garbled MLIT page appears to say Keichō era).
- **Hakone checkpoint** established **1619** (Genna 5); abolished in early Meiji; fully rebuilt in **2007** with Edo-period techniques (hakonenavi, SOURCED). Reconstruction was based on a detailed 1865 repair report, *Sōshū Hakone o-sekisho go-shūfuku dekigata-chō*, found in the Egawa archive (Izunokuni) in 1983 (UNVERIFIED snippet of the official site). Admission ¥500 adults (hakonenavi, SOURCED).
- **Today**: 21.12 million visitors in 2025 (4.148 M overnight, 16.974 M day), 99.4% of 2018; foreign overnight guests +27.6%; November busiest. SOURCED — Hōnichi Lab, 10 June 2026. Population ~10,000–11,000 (UNVERIFIED snippets). **Lodging tax**: ¥350 per person per night from April 2028 (planned), a non-earmarked general tax — "the first of its kind in Japan" — ~¥1.4 billion a year, to cover waste disposal and other services strained by ~20 million visitors. ### B. What else exists there (institutions, logistics, workforce)
- [K1, K5, K7] **The relay office** (*toiyaba* / *jinba kaisho*): on the main street; head *toiya*, under him the *umasashi* (assigned horses and handlers) and *ninsokusashi* (assigned porters); the Kiso region had to keep **25 horses and 25 porters** ready at all times; in Tsumago the honjin and waki-honjin families **alternated the service every six months**. SOURCED — MLIT multilingual text "Tsumago Honjin / Jinba kaisho." In Tsumago the Shimazaki family served as toiya and honjin, the Hayashi family as toiya and waki-honjin (UNVERIFIED snippet, Nara National Research Institute report). At Ōiso (Tōkaidō) two toiyaba each took 10-day turns (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- [K1, K9] **Sukegō (assisting villages)**: when a station's standing stock was insufficient, labor and horses were levied on surrounding villages (UNVERIFIED snippets of municipal pages). The **1764 "Tenma sōdō"** — a mass protest from the twelfth month of Meiwa 1 against increased sukegō levies, centered on Musashi and Kōzuke — led to the punishment of 370+ people, 77 of them from 17 villages in Shinano (UNVERIFIED — snippet of a university paper citing Hōjō Takeshi). For Princess Kazu's 1861 procession, 12 standing and 29 temporary sukegō villages were mobilized for one Shinano station (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- [K4, P9] **Graded lodging**: *honjin* (daimyo on alternate attendance, imperial envoys, shogunal officials), *waki-honjin* (equivalent backup), *hatago* (meal-providing inns for ordinary travelers) — SOURCED, imidas. *Kichin-yado*: no meals; guests paid a firewood fee (*kichin*) to cook their own food; the word became a synonym for cheap lodging; in the Tenpō era hatago were ">5 times" kichin-yado (MLIT page, SOURCED but mis-encoded — the snippet reading is "5 times the price," so whether this means number or price is unclear). Price examples: hatago 120–200 mon, kichin-yado ≤50 mon on the Tōkaidō (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- [K9, K6, P9] **Meshimori-onna** — reported plainly: women at hatago who served meals and also worked as prostitutes. After post towns were barred from keeping courtesans in **1659**, meshimori-onna appeared as a substitute; **1662** flashy clothing banned; **1718** each hatago officially allowed **two**; in practice many inns kept more, serving local residents and horse drivers too; the four Edo-edge stations (Senju, Itabashi, Shinagawa, Naitō-Shinjuku) each held several hundred to over 1,000 such women. SOURCED — imidas "meshimori-onna." Recruitment terms: NOT FOUND.
- [K1] **Checkpoint staff (Hakone, 1686 establishment)**: 1 *bangashira* and 3 *hira-banshi* (samurai), 1 *kogashira*, 10 *ashigaru*, 2 *chūgen*; 3 *jōbannin* and **2 *hitomi-onna*** (female inspectors) hired from nearby farming families, with the shogunate paying their allowances; samurai rotated from Odawara castle monthly (on the 2nd), ashigaru on the 23rd; a *yokome* (inspector of the inspectors) added later. UNVERIFIED (Wikipedia mirrors and gltjp snippets). Separate latrines for officials and for ashigaru — travelers could not use them (official site snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Kiso forest law** [K6, K1, K8]: after ~100 years of heavy logging under the Owari Tokugawa, the domain closed *tomeyama* (reserved hinoki stands) and *sudoyama* (hawk-breeding mountains) to residents and banned cutting the **Five Trees of Kiso** (hinoki, sawara, asunaro, nezuko, kōyamaki); enforcement summed up as "**one tree, one head**"; Owari management ended 1869; imperial forests from 1889; Ise Shrine timber reserve from 1906. SOURCED — Forestry Agency Kiso office "history." Most Kiso forest was *akeyama* (open), where residents could take non-protected wood and wild plants (UNVERIFIED snippet, same agency). Woodcraft developed along the road (lacquerware, bentwood) (UNVERIFIED).
- **Infrastructure**: Hakone pass paved with stone in **1680** (before, a dirt path "muddy mire"); **ichirizuka** milestone mounds one pair per *ri* (~3.93 km), Hatajuku's marking 23 ri from Edo, with trees for shade. SOURCED — MLIT multilingual texts. Four teahouses opened in Ōidaira within a few years of 1619, also serving as courier bases and stables; only **Amazake-chaya** survives (rebuilt 2009 from old materials); others closed after National Route 1 opened in the late 19th c. SOURCED — MLIT.

**Tsumago**

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- [K1, K8] The duty system *made* the towns: Hakone-juku was populated by forced transfer from two neighboring stations; residents had no farmland and lived from travelers; the domain gave disaster relief and travel-expense aid to keep it running (MLIT Q&A, SOURCED/garbled).
- [K4] The inn hierarchy put rank into architecture (honjin vs hatago vs kichin-yado); the checkpoint separated officials' and soldiers' facilities from travelers'.
- [K9] **Decline by bypass**: Tsumago lost its function after the national road (1892) and railway (1909), and lost population in the postwar boom; preservation was explicitly an answer to depopulation — the late chair Kobayashi Toshihiko: "the original purpose, the fight against depopulation and escape from being a marginal village, succeeded" (Nagiso sheet, SOURCED). Visitors: ~18,000 (1967) → >500,000 (1972) → nearly 1 million/yr by the early 1990s (Siegenthaler 2003, SOURCED, citing municipal figures). Chartered tour buses at Tsumago fell from 6,560 (2013) to 3,931 (2018) (UNVERIFIED snippet, Nagano Prefecture). Foreign visitors returned strongly from 2023 (Nagiso sheet, SOURCED).
- [K13] The Kiso road's culture shaped literature (Tōson) and Hakone's road shaped a craft (yosegi as a souvenir sold at a honjin to daimyo and Dutch traders — UNVERIFIED snippet).

### E. Structural features
- [K6] **Rules of everyday behavior — many, and layered:**
  1. **Checkpoint rules**: "guns in, women out" (*iri-deppō ni de-onna*); women leaving Edo needed a **women's pass** issued by the shogunate's *rusui*; at Hakone, women were matched against physical descriptions on the pass and the *hitomi-onna* reportedly undid their hair to check; men and Edo-bound women passed by stating province and name; weapons checks were chiefly the Arai (Imagiri) checkpoint's job, not Hakone's (UNVERIFIED — snippets of imidas, gltjp, Kyushu University paper). Commoner men needed an *ōrai tegata* (travel permit), sometimes issued en route by inns (National Museum of Japanese History *Bulletin* 36, UNVERIFIED snippet). Checkpoint evasion (*sekisho-yaburi*) was a capital crime; penalties fixed in 1631 (UNVERIFIED). Local tradition holds that **Otama**, a farmer's daughter from Ōse village (Izu) who fled service in Edo, was caught on a bypass trail in **1702** and executed; the pond Otama-ga-ike is named for her — recorded as tradition, not documented (UNVERIFIED, tabi-mag). Women's parties often took side roads called *onna-michi* (UNVERIFIED, samurai-archives).
  2. **Inn rules**: two meshimori-onna per hatago (1718), dress restrictions (1662) (SOURCED, imidas).

## 5 · #68 Jumak (Korean roadside taverns)

**Claim to test:** roadside inns along historic routes where travelers ate, drank and exchanged news (K10, K12).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with corrections.** (a) The jumak was a **private, late-Joseon successor** to the state *won* (院) inns that served officials; it spread after the Imjin (1592–98) and Byeongja (1636–37) wars and with the growth of five-day markets. (b) It was a drink-and-food place first; **lodging was usually free for those who bought food or drink** (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture; *Korea Review* 1901), but at least one surviving jumak (Samgang) never offered beds. (c) News exchange is documented: jumak and market days were where rural people heard news, and **the state itself posted royal edicts at busy taverns**. (d) Jumak also carried **official and semi-official duties** (escort, reconnaissance, policing, carrying government goods and documents) in Gyeonggi. (e) Rank governed who slept where.

### A. Snapshot
- **Definition**: "a building that sold liquor and meals to travelers," today "a combined bar, restaurant and inn"; other names *jusa* (酒肆), *juga* (酒家), *jupo* (酒舖). Origin cannot be verified; one view dates jumak to 1097. SOURCED — Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (AKS), "Jumak" (E0053227). Also called *sulmak*, which by sound-play became *sutmak* (charcoal shed); in 1574 *tanmak* (charcoal sheds) were used as lodging. SOURCED — Kang Myeong-gwan, *Seoul Shinmun*, 2 June 2008; Hwang Gwang-hae, *Kyungbuk Maeil*, 16 Sept 2019.
- **Predecessor — the *won***: state lodgings for officials and official travelers, sited by post stations along main, middle and small roads; **1,310** were established in Joseon; managers (*wonju*) were monks, local clerks or officials, exempted from other labor; each *won* had farmland (*wonwijeon*: 1 gyeol 35 bu on main roads, 90 bu middle, 45 bu small); they also served as famine-relief sites and banquet venues; restricted use led to decline, and after the Imjin War abuses grew and *won* "changed into jumak or jujeom." Place names survive: Bojewon (outside Dongdaemun), Hongjewon (outside Seodaemun), Itaewon (outside Namdaemun). SOURCED — Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, "Won" (E0040567, Nam Do-yeong 1995/2024). Bojewon also fed the hungry, had doctors treating the poor free, and buried the kinless; herb merchants gathered nearby (today's Gyeongdong herb market). After the Imjin War the *pabal* courier system and *chamjeom* stations "one day's walk apart" replaced the *won* (SOURCED — Dongdaemun-gu page).
- **Scale of the market network**: 1,062 markets (late 18th c., *Dongguk munheon bigo*; 91.1% five-day) and 1,052 (early 19th c., *Imwon gyeongjeji*; 86% five-day); "every 20–30 li a five-day market, not a day without one" (Yi Ik). SOURCED — Kim Dae-gil, "Joseon hugi jangsi baldal-gwa sahoe-munhwa saenghwal byeonhwa," *Jeongsin munhwa yeongu* 35-4 (2012) (PDF, extracted locally).

### B. What else exists there (functions, people, logistics)
- **Where jumak stood**: market towns, the foot of large passes, ferry crossings, mining villages (Encyclopedia, SOURCED); in Gyeonggi at county seats, markets, post stations, passes, ferries, ports and bridge approaches, along the main roads and the Han and Imjin river routes (Gyeonggi study abstract, SOURCED — "Gyeonggi-do-ui jumak yeongu," *Munhwa yeoksa jiri* 32-3, 2020, DOI 10.29349/JCHG.2020.32.3.124). Under Jeongjo, 89 "store villages" (*jeomchon*) with several hundred households lay between Anbyeon and Pyeonggang; the 1872 district maps show, e.g., Gokseong with 3 markets and 16 jumak/taverns, Gwangyang 2 markets and 10 *jeommak*, Gurye 2 markets and 10 taverns, Nagan 3 markets and 12 *jeom* (Kim Dae-gil 2012, SOURCED).
- [K1, K14, P7] **Official functions**: beyond food and lodging, jumak keepers performed "official escort, reconnaissance, policing, transport of government goods, document delivery and commerce"; under Japanese colonial rule individual inns were pushed into restaurant or lodging licenses, "placed under licensing controls, and monitored as sources of colonial revenue," while some jumak clusters grew into railway and administrative towns (Gyeonggi study abstract, SOURCED).
- [K10] **News and notices**: market days and the jumak were where villagers heard rumors, received letters through acquaintances and news of relatives; under Jeongjo (1794) the court ordered notices in hanmun and hangul **posted at busy *jeommak*** in every township, and taverns were used to post royal proclamations; secret royal inspectors used jumak to learn local conditions. SOURCED — Kim Dae-gil 2012. Jumak were also places of "small-scale commerce" and of "various information" and a gathering and amusement place for locals, not just travelers (same).
- [K4, P9] **Customers**: travelers, peddlers and merchants on market circuits, officials; **exam-year scholars** filled jumak (Encyclopedia, SOURCED); unemployed yangban and commoners used jumak while officials and powerful yangban used government offices, post stations or temples (Kim Dae-gil 2012, SOURCED). A 17th-c. traveler from Yean (Kim Sun-ui, 1632 diary) slept at jumak where he had no personal connections (same). Seventeenth–eighteenth-c. yangban diarists often slept in private homes but ate at jumak (Kang 2008, SOURCED).
- [K14, K1] **Peddlers' guilds (*bobusang*)**: *bosang* carried light goods in cloth bundles (silk, leather, paper, ginseng); *busang* used A-frame carriers (fish, salt, beans, pottery, yeast, wood/handicrafts); married members' *Yojunghoe* (ranks *yeongwi*, *bansu*, *jeopjang*, *gongwon*, *jipsa*) and unmarried members' *Bibangcheong*; recognized under the Daewongun for grain transport and reconnaissance and granted selling monopolies; **Hyesang Gongguk** guild office **1883**; privileges abolished after the Russo-Japanese War. SOURCED — Encyclopedia, "Daebang" (E0014377). Guild relics (seals, tokens, rule books *jeolmok* 1851–1908, 43 petitions, appointment papers) designated Important Folk Cultural Property 1973–80; surviving members hold rites on the 1st and 15th of each month (Encyclopedia E0023369, SOURCED). The guild records show "self-regulation, mutual aid, and obligations to the state" (same).
- **Related establishments** (distinguished from jumak): *gibang* (courtesans for wealthy yangban), *saekjuga* (hostess drink-and-sex houses), *gaekju* (merchant inns: lodging, brokerage, consignment for peddlers), *yeogak* (lodging mainly for peddlers) (Encyclopedia, SOURCED). Gaekju and yeogak at ports also stored goods and lent money (UNVERIFIED snippets).

### C. Cultural locations (the tradition in three places)
**1. Samgang Jumak, Yecheon (Gyeongsangbuk-do)**
- At **Samgang Naru**, Pungyang-myeon, where the **Nakdong, Naeseong and Geumcheon** rivers meet. Built c. **1900**; two rooms, one kitchen, one veranda; no ceiling; three old trees and a wooden platform in front; survived many floods (wooden frame) incl. the Gapsul-year flood that washed away 27 houses and two taverns. **Samgang never offered lodging**; nearby **Dalji Jumak** (Yeongsun-myeon, Mungyeong) across the river had large guest rooms and horse hitching marks. Customers historically: salt boats (which sailed up to Andong's Hahoe ferry at high water), traders, people crossing to take exams, visitors to Jeomchon market; the ferry's large boat carried six cows; the ferry ended when **Samgang Bridge** opened (spring 2004). SOURCED — *Maeil Shinmun*, 13 Dec 2004.
- **Last jumo, Yu Ok-yeon**: born 20 Oct 1917; married at 15; bought the jumak at 19 (1936); widowed ~50 years earlier; raised five children; ran it ~70 years; "should have been designated about 20 years earlier." SOURCED — *Maeil* 2004. Death: 2005 at 89–90 (Robert Neff, *Korea Times* 2023, SOURCED; kbmaeil snippet) vs 2006 at 88 (*Maeil* 2024, SOURCED) — discrepancy noted. Neff gives her start as "early 1920s" (2023) and "1935" (2020) — also inconsistent.
- **Wall tally**: credit purchases scratched on the kitchen clay wall with chopsticks (makgeolli kettles counted by an illiterate jumo) (Yecheon County English page, SOURCED; NewsSeoul 2025, SOURCED).
- **Designations**: Gyeongsangbuk-do Folk Material No. 134 (20 Nov 2005, UNVERIFIED date); restored **2007** (~₩150 million, UNVERIFIED); **National Folk Cultural Heritage** announced **27 Nov 2025** as "Yecheon Samgang Naru Jumak" — thatched, 2×2 bays, 田-shaped plan, jumo's room and guest room each one bay, kitchen and *maru* behind, separate *ondol* flue for each room; a *seongju* jar (household guardian) in the attic over the kitchen; operated as a tavern c. 1900–2005. SOURCED — NewsSeoul, 27 Nov 2025.
- [K1] **Village institutions**: the **Samgang Ferry Association** (*Samgang doseon-gye*) records are preserved; the village rite (*dongje*) made offerings to the river god and a tavern guardian deity; the *Dongsin-gye chaek* records ~130 years of the rite; a village tree and phallic stone on the embankment. SOURCED — NewsSeoul 2025.
- **Today**: a newer tavern next door is run by the **village women's association**, profits going to village expenses (UNVERIFIED snippets); cabbage pancake (*baechu-jeon*), tofu, buckwheat jelly and makgeolli as a ₩12,000 set (early 2010s, UNVERIFIED); a makgeolli plant nearby planned 30 *mal*/day (2013, UNVERIFIED). Samgang Culture Complex (Yecheon County): Riverine Culture Exhibition, **Peddler Culture Experience Village**, camping, ecology park (SOURCED, ycg.kr).
- [K2, K3, K8] **Samgang Naru Jumak Festival** — 25–27 Sept 2026, run by the Yecheon Culture and Tourism Foundation, renamed after the national designation; reoriented from performances to history and hands-on activities around peddlers and ferrymen; "**Samgang Bobusang PASS**" (tour book and old coins); "Aeiyo Cheongdan," a modern take on Yecheon's folk play **Cheongdan Nolgeum**; electric shuttle; reusable food-truck containers. SOURCED — *Kyungbuk Maeil*, 27 Sept 2026. It was the 10th edition in 2019 (UNVERIFIED); >60,000 visitors over Chuseok 2023 incl. related events (UNVERIFIED).

**2. Mungyeong Saejae (pass on the Yeongnam Great Road to Seoul)**
- Mungyeong had 11 *won* (16th-c. *Sinjeung Dongguk yeoji seungnam*), four on the Saejae route (Joryeong-won, Yogwang-won, Gwaneum-won, Hwabong-won); Donghwawon and Sinhyewon also stood in the pass (UNVERIFIED snippets). The exam route from Yeongnam to Seoul; the Joryeong-won site lies within Historic Site No. 147 (UNVERIFIED). The Encyclopedia names Mungyeong Saejae among jumak locations, with state relay inns Joryeong-won and Donghwawon (SOURCED).
- [K6] Under Jeongjo (1788) the court **demolished all taverns on a shortcut** at Joryeong and banned passage because the shortcut weakened the pass's defenses; merchants kept using it anyway. SOURCED — Kim Dae-gil 2012 (citing *Seungjeongwon ilgi*).
- **Saejae Jumak rest stop** (Mungyeong City, weekends and holidays 19 Apr–end Oct 2025): exchange coins (1 *nyang* = ₩1,000) for one cup of omija drink, coffee or tea; free traditional games (jegichagi, tuho, gonggi, marbles); modeled on scholars resting on the way to the exams. SOURCED — *Kyungbuk Maeil*, 17 Apr 2025. Donghwawon villagers were moved to the present commercial area when the provincial park was developed (1996) (UNVERIFIED).

**3. Hwagae Market, Hadong (Gyeongsang–Jeolla border on the Seomjin River)**
- Gathered southern-coast seafood, salt, Honam grain, Jirisan wild greens and woodenware; flourished from mid-Joseon to Liberation via the Hadong river port; setting of Kim Dong-ri's story *Yeongma*; rebuilt (late 1999–spring 2001) with traditional market buildings, stalls, **jumak** and a smithy; now a permanent market (UNVERIFIED snippets; cost ₩1.6–1.7 billion conflicting).

**Other**: genre paintings of jumak by **Kim Hong-do** ("Jumak": jumo ladling from a jar, a child tugging her skirt, a man in a *paeraengi* hat finishing his rice) and **Shin Yun-bok** (Encyclopedia jumo entry; Kang 2008, SOURCED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Layout and signs**: large jumak had dozens of rooms, storerooms and stables (horses, mules, donkeys); small rural ones a few rooms and a serving area; roadside stalls operated by day only; kitchen attached to where the jumo sat; marked by a "酒" on the door, paper-panel lanterns, a *yongsu* strainer on a pole, or boiled cow/pig heads on trays; **no signboards** — customers referred to them by features (a tree, a well, the proprietor's appearance). SOURCED — Encyclopedia.
- [K12] **Food and drink**: *takju* (unrefined rice wine) the main drink, soju also, *bangmunju* for yangban; dawn vendors sold weak *moju* from lees; dry anju (dried beef, dried fish) and cooked anju (boiled meat, *neobiani*, *bindaetteok*, rice-cake skewers, grilled fish, *sulguk*); *jangguk-bap* (rice in beef broth) a staple; **each cup came with a free piece of anju**; a young male server (*jungnomi*) grilled anju and watched for people eating free anju. SOURCED — Encyclopedia. Gukbap shops are said to have started at jumak and markets in the 18th–19th c. (UNVERIFIED, press snippet). Western travelers (Bird, 1890s): rice, eggs, vegetables, soup, vermicelli, seaweed; no tea; well water often contaminated; chicken, pheasant or dog seasonally (Neff, *Korea Times*, 14 Oct 2023, SOURCED).
- [K8] **Payment**: "food and fodder were the only charged items; sleeping and stable rooms were free" (*Korea Review* 1901, via Neff 2023, SOURCED); Bird paid ~12–15 cents (or ~100 cash, ~18 cents) a night (Neff 2023/2020, SOURCED); jumo "often extended generous credit" (Encyclopedia jumo entry, SOURCED) — the Samgang wall tally.
- [K9, P9] **The jumo** — reported plainly: rural jumo worked hands-on; some jumo were former *gisaeng* (usually 50+), palace women expelled for offenses, or palace women dismissed when the court's staff was cut near the end of the Korean Empire; large Seoul jumak "often became hubs for covert prostitution," the jumo sometimes a concubine or backed by a patron, acting as madam. SOURCED — Encyclopedia "Jumo" (E0053235, Kim Yong-suk 1995/2023; AKS notes it reflects the author's views). Innkeeping was "a successful means of livelihood" for middle-class women (Bird/*Korea Review*, via Neff, SOURCED).
- [K9] **Danger**: bandits raided a jumak at Seonghwan (Yeongjo 32) (Kang 2008, SOURCED); a 1728 record during the Yi In-jwa rebellion says bandits slept at jumak at night and gathered at markets by day (Hwang 2019, SOURCED); travelers took refuge in jumak at night from bandits, tigers and feared spirits (Neff 2020, SOURCED).
- [K9] **Decline**: jumak faded from the late Korean Empire to early colonial years as new roads (*sinjangno*) and motor traffic arrived; Japanese-style *yeogwan* appeared under colonial rule (UNVERIFIED snippet); colonial licensing and taxation (Gyeonggi study, SOURCED). Modern hangover-soup restaurants and drink shops are considered successors; beer halls, cafés and karaoke are not (Encyclopedia, SOURCED).

### E. Structural features
- [K6] **Rules of everyday behavior**:
  1. **Rank over money in rooms**: first arrivals took the warm floor (*araetmok*); "rank determined the room — money alone did not get a good room," and lower-status guests were pushed to corners or halls (Encyclopedia, SOURCED).
  2. **Free bed with a purchase** (Encyclopedia; *Korea Review*, SOURCED); poor wanderers were given a place to sleep (Neff 2023, SOURCED).
  3. **Shared common room** used for eating and sleeping, wooden-block pillows; private rooms sometimes available (Neff 2020, SOURCED).
  4. **Alcohol prohibitions** (*geumjuryeong*): issued from 1392 in droughts and famines, usually spring–summer, lifted after harvest; **Yeongjo's strict ban (1758 per the Encyclopedia; 1755/1756 per other sources)** — tea replaced liquor even in royal rites; exceptions for state sacrifices, envoys, commoners' weddings and ancestral rites, medicinal use, and tacitly for **the poor brewing or selling liquor for a living**; obeyed in the provinces, largely ignored by Seoul officials (Encyclopedia E0007997, SOURCED). Reported penalties under Yeongjo (exile for brewers, enslavement for buyers; hanging liquor lanterns banned in Seoul) are UNVERIFIED (wiki/press snippets).
  5. **State demolition** of shortcut taverns at Joryeong (1788) (SOURCED).
  6. **Self-imposed rules**: scholar Yi Ok (1793) set himself a rule of no more than three cups at mountain jumak (Kang 2008, SOURCED). Peddler guilds' written *jeolmok* (rule books) existed (SOURCED); contents NOT FOUND.
- [K7] Five-day market rhythm (six market days a month) set traffic; exam years filled jumak (SOURCED, Kim Dae-gil; Encyclopedia).
- [K10] The jumak as a locals' gathering place when travelers were absent (Kim Dae-gil, SOURCED); today Samgang's tavern as a village-run gathering and income source (UNVERIFIED).
- [K11] Korean; hanmun and hangul notices both posted at taverns (Kim Dae-gil, SOURCED). No other detail sought.
- [K13] Genre painting (Kim Hong-do, Shin Yun-bok); literature (Kim Dong-ri's *Yeongma* at Hwagae; the 1956 film *Byeoraek Gamtu* shot the Samgang ferry and tavern — *Maeil* 2004, SOURCED).
- [R] Credit tallies and shared rooms as a low-cost rest stop with communal rules.

## 6 · #31 Barstow, California, and the Fred Harvey houses

**Claim to test:** a rail-and-road junction town; the railway hotel-restaurant chain staffed by young women on contract set a standard of hospitality for travelers (K4, K7).
**Verdict:** **Confirmed, with corrections and additions.** (a) Barstow is a junction town several times over — railroads (1880s), the National Old Trails Highway and **Route 66** (1926) with **US 91/466**, then **I-15 and I-40** — and its biggest employers today are **military** (Fort Irwin, Marine Corps Logistics Base) and **BNSF**, now planning one of the largest rail yards in the country. (b) The Harvey system did set a hospitality standard, and its waitresses worked under **written contracts with behavior rules** (no marriage during the term, curfew, dormitory housemother, dress code). (c) The Harvey Girl workforce was **almost always white** by company preference; Hispanic and Indigenous women were mostly kept in lower-paid back-of-house jobs until WWII labor shortages changed hiring. (d) The company also **marketed Native crafts and staged Native demonstrators**, shaping what was sold. (e) Barstow's Hispanic community grew largely through **chain migration from Belen and Valencia County, New Mexico**, from the late 1930s, meeting housing discrimination; earlier Mexican railroad labor at Barstow specifically was **not documented** in what I could read.

### A. Snapshot
- **Barstow**: San Bernardino County, Mojave Desert, on the Mojave River; at the junction of I-15 and I-40 between Los Angeles, Las Vegas and Flagstaff. Earlier names: Camp Sugarloaf, Grapevine, Fishpond, **Waterman Junction**; named for **William Barstow Strong**, Santa Fe president (renamed 15 Jan 1886 per one source; 1884 per digital-desert). Railroad chronology conflicts: the Southern Pacific built Mojave–Barstow–Needles in 1883 and the Santa Fe took over in 1884 (en.wikipedia, SOURCED); the Atlantic & Pacific completed its desert mainline in 1883 (digital-desert, SOURCED); 1885 California Southern connection (HMdb marker, UNVERIFIED); "Santa Fe reached the area in 1888" (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Population**: 1950: 6,135; 1970: 17,442; 1990: 21,472; 2010: 22,639; **2020: 25,415**; 2025 est. 24,804. 2020: Hispanic or Latino 44.44%, white non-Hispanic 26.15%, Black non-Hispanic 18.43%; median age 30.9. SOURCED — en.wikipedia "Barstow, California."
- **Top employers (2025 report)**: **Fort Irwin National Training Center 8,451**, **Marine Corps Logistics Base 1,935**, **BNSF 1,000**. SOURCED — en.wikipedia.
- **Casa del Desierto** (Barstow Harvey House): replaced a wooden depot/restaurant/hotel of 1885 that burned in 1908; built 1910–1913 / opened **22 Feb 1911** (Harvey Girls in place by that date — PBS SoCal); architect disputed — **Mary Colter** per NPS, PBS and digital-desert; **Francis W. Wilson** per HABS and Amtrak (Wikipedia), and SAH Archipedia argues Wilson (UNVERIFIED snippet; page refused). Spanish Renaissance/Classical Revival with Moorish elements; concrete frame, red tapestry brick, barrel tiles; restaurant, circular lunch counter, ballroom, lodging for travelers and employees. **Listed in the Green Book in the 1950s** as friendly to African-American motorists. Santa Fe closed the station in **1973** (Wikipedia, digital-desert) or **1971** (PBS); derelict, stripped by artifact hunters and squatters; Santa Fe planned demolition in the late 1980s; **city bought it in 1990**; the **1992 Landers earthquake (M7.3)** struck the day before a planned re-dedication; >$8 million restoration; reopened 1999; Route 66 "Mother Road" Museum opened 2000 (dedicated 4 July 2000); NRHP 3 April 1975; California Historical Landmark No. 892 (1976). SOURCED — en.wikipedia "Barstow Harvey House"; NPS "Harvey House Railroad Depot"; PBS SoCal, 2 Aug 2011; digital-desert.

Amtrak *Southwest Chief* stop, FY2025 ridership 3,300 (Wikipedia, SOURCED).

Lawsuit filed **2 July 2026** in San Bernardino County Superior Court (Sierra Club, East Yard Communities for Environmental Justice, NRDC; Center for Biological Diversity) alleging CEQA violations: >550 tons NOx/yr, >18 million gallons diesel/yr, +134,471 t CO2e/yr (41%), desert tortoise, Mojave fringe-toed lizard, burrowing owl. Wartime: Nebo Depot for ordnance and supplies; Daggett and Yermo facilities (digital-desert, SOURCED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
**The Fred Harvey system [K1, K7, K14]**
- Fred Harvey (b. London 27 June 1835; d. 9 Feb 1901); in winter 1875–76, under a gentleman's agreement with the Santa Fe's Charles F. Morse (rent-free space, free rail transport of food and staff), took over the Topeka depot lunchroom; planned a depot restaurant "about every 100 miles"; at peak the company ran **25 hotels, 40 dining rooms, 55 lunchrooms** and terminal restaurants in eight cities. SOURCED — HistoryNet (John Koster, *Wild West*, April 2011). At his death: 45 restaurants and 20 dining cars in 12 states; sold in **1968** to Amfac (now Xanterra). SOURCED — Kansas Historical Society, Kansapedia.
- **Meal service**: trains stopped three times a day; passengers had about **thirty minutes** (JSTOR Daily, SOURCED); before Harvey some restaurants reportedly bribed engineers to whistle early so food could be resold; a uniformed employee struck a **brass gong** at first sight of customers; Harvey Girls took orders for tables of 8–10 and poured drinks while a male manager delivered the main course; they assured passengers "the train would not leave without them" (HistoryNet, SOURCED). Crews counted diners and **wired the count ahead** (UNVERIFIED snippets). A **"cup code"** told the drink server what to pour (upright = coffee; upside down = hot tea; tilted = iced tea; away from saucer = milk — details differ, UNVERIFIED).
- Harvey made surprise inspections and fired managers who fell short (1881) (JSTOR Daily, SOURCED); pies cut in quarters, own dairies (HistoryNet, SOURCED).

**The Harvey Girls [K4, K6, K7, P9]**
- Women replaced male waiters from **1881** (Kansapedia) / **1883 at Raton**, on manager Tom Gable's proposal (HistoryNet) — dates differ. Recruits **18–30**, "good character," at least an **eighth-grade education**, interviewed in Kansas City; **$17.50/month plus room, board and tips**; agreed **not to marry for at least six months**; dormitories with a **housemother**; **10 p.m. curfew** unless a late train was expected; a **"courting parlor"** in each house; uniform: black shoes and stockings, long black skirt, high-collared blouse with black bow, starched white apron, white hair bow; **no makeup or chewing gum** on duty; after six months without marrying, a free round-trip ticket and a new contract. SOURCED — HistoryNet (dormitory/uniform details credited to Lucius Beebe). Other accounts: one-year contracts (Kansapedia, SOURCED); breaking a contract (most often by marrying) cost half pay (UNVERIFIED snippets); curfew 10:30/11:30 (UNVERIFIED); often >70 hours a week (UNVERIFIED).
- **Scale**: ~**100,000** women 1883–1950s, attributed to Lesley Poling-Kempes, *The Harvey Girls: Women Who Opened the West* (1989/1991) (UNVERIFIED secondhand); ~5,000 Western marriages (HistoryNet, SOURCED) vs ~20,000 brides (New Mexico History snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- [K9] **Race and hiring — plainly**: "Fred Harvey almost always hired white women as Harvey Girls. Women of color, including Hispanic and Indigenous women, were mostly hired for lower-paid 'back of house' jobs such as laundress and cleaner." In the 1930s an Arizona Harvey House kept a Hispanic Harvey Girl despite railroad workers' threats to leave. WWII labor shortages changed hiring: Hispanic women and Hopi, Zuni and Navajo women were hired (Lucy Delgadillo Moore; Marion Dale Lucero, Hopi, started as a dishwasher in 1949; Olive Monongye, Hopi, a Harvey Girl for 30 years; Hilda Velarde Salas, 1939–45, "the first one in the family that opened a bank account"). SOURCED — NPS "The Harvey Girls: Increasing Opportunity."

**The Indian Department and Native crafts [K8, K9] — reported plainly**
- Indian Department in **Albuquerque (1901/1902)** under **Herman Schweizer** and **John Huckel**; the **Alvarado Hotel's Indian Building**; **Hopi House** at the Grand Canyon (1905, Mary Colter) advertised as a replica Hopi dwelling; Navajo weavers and silversmiths hired as **demonstrators** before tourists; **Indian Detours** by motor coach from **15 May 1926** (some sources 1925) with college-educated women **"Couriers"** as guides (pay reportedly $150/month, $160 with a foreign language — UNVERIFIED). Exhibit text says the products "further exoticized and fetishized" Native people; souvenir sales provided "a profit" to Native artisans, though modest. UNVERIFIED (snippets of NAU exhibit; exhibit page 404). Reported Schweizer instruction "make it lighter, less expensive"; supplying sheet silver and pre-cut turquoise to smiths via trading posts; "Fred Harvey style" thin, stamped jewelry with motifs like arrows and thunderbirds (UNVERIFIED — dealer/gallery snippets; one TFAO page read was an **AI-generated report** and is not relied on).

**Coat rule [K6]**: men in Harvey dining rooms had to wear coats; dark alpaca coats were kept on hand so no one was turned away (Kansapedia, SOURCED); a man refusing was served at the lunch counter; early 1920s, the **Oklahoma Corporation Commission** threw out the rule and the company fought it; the Oklahoma Supreme Court reportedly upheld the company (UNVERIFIED snippets). Barstow locals recall the dining room requiring formal dress (PBS SoCal, SOURCED).

### E. Structural features
- [K9, P9] **Belen–Barstow migration**: families from Belen and Valencia County, New Mexico, came after the Santa Fe began hiring in Barstow in the late 1930s, then for **Fort Irwin and MCLB** civilian jobs; most planned to stay briefly but stayed for life; a painting of an abandoned house with a sign "Gone to Barstow"; by Sept 1947 at least **650 families** needed housing; many lived in leftover WWII **"Victory Houses"**; **Hispanic families faced discrimination** in buying and renting (Si Sisneros was told an owner wouldn't sell to "Mexicans"); families returned to Belen for fiestas, weddings, baptisms, funerals; businesses opened in Barstow by migrants (barbershop, filling station, dance hall, refrigeration, a horse "motel"). SOURCED — Richard Melzer, "Barstow or Bust, Part I," *Valencia County News-Bulletin*, 12 Nov 2026. Felix Gomez (first Hispanic supervisor at MCLB) said he started the migration by hiring Belen-area men; he estimated **50–60% of Barstow's Hispanic residents** trace roots to Belen; Barstow and Belen became **sister cities** (7 April 2007, "Belen Day"); Fiestas de Belen at Dana Park, ~3,000 attendees. SOURCED — Aaron Aupperlee, 9 April 2007 (reposted on Joe D. Gomez's blog; original outlet unconfirmed).
