## 3. Currents and water properties

**Naming note (SOURCED, Wikipedia "Davis Sea", citing USGS GNIS and the Great Soviet Encyclopedia):** the Davis Sea lies "between 82° and 96°E" (GNIS) or "from 87°E to 98°E" (GSE), up to 1,300 m deep; Mirny station is on its coast. The Mawson Sea (east of the Shackleton Ice Shelf, centered "65°00′S 105°00′E") was named only in a 2002 IHO draft "never approved" (Wikipedia "Mawson Sea"). The Russian reports below call the water just west of the shelf front the southeastern Davis Sea, "Tryoshnikov Gulf" (залив Трёшникова).

### 3.1 Current speeds and directions

**No current-meter record exists at the Shackleton front itself that I could find (NOT FOUND).** The nearest direct and regional measurements:

- **SOURCED (Peña-Molino, McCartney and Rintoul 2016, *JGR Oceans* 121:7390-7407, "Direct observations of the Antarctic Slope Current transport at 113°E"; PDF read through the UTAS figshare file).** Five moorings across the continental slope near **113°E** (about 600 km east of the Shackleton front), 1000-4200 m isobaths, deployed from *Aurora Australis* December 2009, recovered January 2012 (17-month record).
  - "The time-mean transport of the ASC is −29.2 Sv. Fluctuations in the transport are large, typically exceeding the mean by a factor of 2."
  - "The observed time-mean flow consists of a surface-intensified jet associated with the Antarctic Slope Front (ASF) and a broader bottom-intensified westward flow that extends out to approximately the 4000 m isobath."
  - Reconstructed "maximum surface velocity in the time-mean absolute velocity field … is −10 cm s⁻¹ and located at approximately 65°S in water depths of 2000 m" (westward). Record-mean speeds along the 2500 m isobath: −4 cm/s at the bottom and −2 cm/s at 1000 m.
  - The paper cites Bindoff et al. (2000) for the whole **80-150°E** slope: "the average surface drifter velocity they observed is −16 cm s⁻¹, in water depths of approximately 500 m," and SADCP maximum westward surface velocities over the upper slope of "−16 to −18 cm s⁻¹" across sections. (Quoted through Peña-Molino; Bindoff 2000 itself was not opened.)
  - "Seasonal changes in the wind also drive variations in the transport of the ASF and the flow in the upper slope." "Both mean and variability are largely barotropic."
  - DERIVED: 10-18 cm/s ≈ 8.6-15.6 km/day; 16 cm/s ≈ 0.31 knots.
- **SOURCED (Rintoul, van Wijk, Herraiz-Borreguero and Rosevear 2025, *Science Advances* 11:eadx1024, PMC12680056, read through WebFetch, first 100,000 of 109,336 characters).** An Argo float drifting under the Shackleton Ice Shelf at about 300 dbar: "The mean drift speed under the SIS was about 1 cm s⁻¹ from east to west." (Sub-ice-shelf flow, not open-water surface current.)
- **SOURCED (earlier port file A, not repeated in detail):** on the 2025 *Nuyina* voyage, near the shelf corner "where our model predicts the Antarctic Coastal Current flows, we saw large numbers of icebergs being carried rapidly westward." No speed was given.
- **UNVERIFIED (search summaries, not opened):** a review figure of "0.1 to 0.3 m s⁻¹" as typical ASC velocity; a 30-80°E ship survey reporting a narrow westward jet "up to 0.3 m s⁻¹" over the steepest slope; a 2025 *GRL* modeling study (doi 10.1029/2024GL113983) linked to the Shackleton/Denman area projecting ASC speed-up of about 14% over the past 25 years and 49% over the next 25. None is specific to the 95-105°E shelf.
- **Sea-ice drift as a proxy for surface flow near the coast: see item 4** (buoy-measured average westward drift 19-20 km/day).

### 3.2 Water temperature, salinity and water masses

- **SOURCED (AARI, *Russian Polar Research* no. 45 (2021), pp. 9-13, "Deep-water oceanographic research of the Southern Ocean from RV *Akademik Tryoshnikov* in the seasonal period of the 66th RAE", downloaded PDF, read in Russian).** A 205 km section with 21 CTD stations, 20-21 January 2021, "in the eastern part of the sea, between Drygalski Island and the Shackleton Ice Shelf"; station depths 257 m (shelf) to 2360 m (foot of slope). Findings, translated:
  - A deep basin in the southeastern Tryoshnikov Gulf exceeds 1,200 m; the bottom rises to 300-400 m at the shelf break, which is **330 m deep** here; the slope drops from 330 to 2500 m over 25 km.
  - The warm Circumpolar Deep Water core (potential temperature **0.5-1.5 °C** on the slope) lies at **600-650 m**, "considerably deeper than the shelf break," so warm CDW access to the shelf "is difficult."
  - Within about 30 km shoreward of the break, at **250-460 m**, relatively warm water of **−1.5 to −1.6 °C** (modified CDW entering over the sill).
  - South of a thermohaline front, Antarctic Shelf Water fills nearly everything deeper than 200 m: "temperature near the freezing point, i.e. below −1.8 °C," salinity **34.4 psu** near 200 m to **34.47 psu** at the bottom; the bottom layer is uniform at **−1.882 to −1.885 °C**. A 2017 measurement in the same basin gave 34.51 psu.
- **SOURCED (AARI, *Russian Polar Research* no. 3 (29) 2017, pp. 8-10, "Oceanographic research of the Southern Ocean on the 41st voyage of RV *Akademik Fedorov*", 62nd RAE, January-February 2017).** Three sections in Tryoshnikov Gulf, the first "approximately parallel to the front of the Shackleton Ice Shelf at a distance of about one mile from the barrier," the second along 95°E about 12 miles from the barrier.
  - Shelf water below −1.8 °C occupies "the main part of the total volume," salinity 34.4-34.55 ‰ (low-salinity shelf water).
  - Near the Shackleton Ice Shelf and the adjacent Roscoe Glacier: supercooled Ice Shelf Water "below −1.95 °C (salinity 34.31-34.32 ‰)," up to 60 m thick, at the unusually shallow depths of **100-200 m**.
  - Why the ship was there (relevant to item 7): see 7.3.
- **SOURCED (Rintoul et al. 2025)**, float and ship profiles under and beside the shelf:
  - "warm (up to −0.16°C) modified Circumpolar Deep Water (mCDW) is observed near the sea floor"; "Warm mCDW (Θ > −0.7°C) is only found in the Denman trough."
  - "ISW with temperatures as cold as −2.05°C forms a 50- to 100-dbar-thick layer beneath the ice shelves."
  - Near the ice front, "a pool of very fresh (SA < 33.5 g kg⁻¹) water"; end-members "warmest and saltiest mCDW (Θ = −0.2°C, SA = 34.78)" and "coldest, saltiest WW (Θ = −1.88°C, SA = 34.56)."
  - Summer surface: "stronger summer warming (to 0°C) in the Shackleton Polynya (profiles 214 to 234)"; but "no warming of surface waters is detected during summer 2021-2022 (profiles 140 to 160)" under ice cover.
- **SOURCED (Ribeiro, Herraiz-Borreguero, Rintoul, Williams, McMahon, Hindell and Guinet 2023, *JGR Oceans* 128(11), "Oceanic Regime Shift to a Warmer Continental Shelf Adjacent to the Shackleton Ice Shelf, East Antarctica"; abstract read through the HAL API; the full text is behind a bot wall, died at the source).** Sixty years of profiles west of the shelf (≈100°E): "Prior to 1996, cold mCDW water (θ < −1.6°C) was found below the surface mixed layer and cold Dense Shelf Water (DSW) with a salinity of >34.5 dominated the water column. After 2010, warm mCDW (≥−1.0°C) was widespread over the continental shelf and DSW with salinity over 34.5 was no longer present." "A shift occurred prior to 2011."

### 3.4 The Shackleton Polynya: size and seasonal behavior

- **SOURCED (Nihashi and Ohshima 2015, *J. Climate* 28:3650-3670, "Circumpolar Mapping of Antarctic Coastal Polynyas and Landfast Sea Ice: Relationship and Variability", PDF from the Hokkaido University repository, Table 1, 3, 5).** AMSR-E, thin ice defined as ≤ about 0.2 m:
  - Winter (May-August, 2003-2011) mean polynya area **7.5 ± 3.6 × 10³ km²** (± is the daily standard deviation); coefficient of variation 0.48.
  - Annual ice production (March-October, 2003-2010) **8.4 ± 0.8 × 10¹⁰ m³** (≈84 km³) by AMSR-E; 10.0 ± 0.9 × 10¹⁰ m³ by SSM/I.
  - Daily area is best correlated (r = 0.50 ERA-Interim; 0.47 NCEP CFSR) with the geostrophic-wind component from **133°** (128° in CFSR), that is, southeasterly, offshore-and-alongshore wind that drives ice away from the coast-plus-fast-ice boundary.
  - The polynya is one of those "formed adjacent to fast ice or a glacier tongue," where area tracks "the wind component that causes divergent ice motion away from a boundary comprising both coastline and fast ice."
- Already known from port file A (not repeated): Tamura et al. 2016 give 123 ± 12 km³/yr production and 11 ± 1.2 × 10³ km² winter thin-ice area for 1992-2013 (SSM/I, a different method and threshold; the two estimates are not interchangeable). Rintoul 2025: "large and persistent polynya to the west of the SIS."
- **Season:** polynyas are a freezing-season (March-October) feature; the mean annual maximum area of East Antarctic coastal polynyas falls "from June to October," and polynyas reach their smallest extent in February (**UNVERIFIED**, search-result summaries of Massom et al. 1998 *Ann. Glaciol.* and an MDPI *Remote Sensing* paper; neither PDF could be downloaded).
- **Years when the polynya shuts: NOT FOUND.** No source gives a year with no Shackleton Polynya. Tamura 2016 (port file A) and Nihashi 2015 both report interannual variability tied to wind; Massom et al. 1998 (summary, UNVERIFIED) found "no trends in total polynya areal extent over the period 1987-94." A satellite count of closed years was not attempted.

### 4.3 Fast ice (landfast ice)

- **No measured fast-ice thickness at the Shackleton front: NOT FOUND** (also not found by port file A).
- **SOURCED (AARI, *Russian Polar Research* no. 4 (18) 2014, pp. 12-16, read in Russian).** Mirny observatory (on the Davis Sea, ≈93°E, the nearest long fast-ice record to the front) holds "the longest series of observations of Antarctic fast ice." Over half a century its thickness "has decreased slightly, but the dates of breakout have moved later"; at the start of the 21st century the thinning versus the coldest 1960s "averaged about 10 cm, and the delay in breakout reached one month."
  - "In summer 2002, in Tryoshnikov Gulf, for the first time in the half-century history of Mirny observatory, a fast-ice belt thirty kilometers wide that formed in 2001 remained almost entirely unbroken," which stopped the chartered *Magdalena Oldendorff* from supplying fuel to the station in early May 2002. As a result the next year's tractor traverse could not deliver enough fuel to inland Vostok, and Vostok "was forced to be mothballed for the winter."
  - The same paper (other sectors, for scale): at Novolazarevskaya in 2000, a strip of first-year fast ice "150-180, in places more than 200 cm" thick with snow cover over 100 cm stayed along the ice-shelf berth; the ship approached to 20 m "with enormous difficulty by ramming."
- **SOURCED (Ivanov and Bezgreshnov 2014, *Problemy Arktiki i Antarktiki* no. 2 (100):33-40, read in Russian).** Sanefjord, Prydz Bay (≈77°E, far west of the site; given only as the nearest measured East Antarctic fast-ice profile found): mean fast-ice thickness 123-124 cm in 2012 (profile 105-160 cm), and 181-184 cm in 2011 (profile 120-245 cm), the 2011 ice judged "most likely multiyear (two-year)." The paper says standard fast-ice observations in East Antarctica are made at Mirny and Progress.
- **UNVERIFIED (search-result summaries):** Davis (≈78°E) maximum fast-ice thickness rose "from 130 cm to 180 cm" by decade (a review citing Heil et al. 1996, 2006); fast ice reaches maximum thickness "in October or November." Heil 2006 (*JGR* 111, abstract read at EarthRef, SOURCED) gives only trends: "dates of annual maximum ice thickness and final fast-ice breakout are delayed (each by +0.43 d yr⁻¹)" and "mean values of annual maximum ice thickness taken over subintervals have changed little."
- **UNVERIFIED (ru.wikipedia, through port file D):** Mirny's fast ice reaches 30-40 km wide by late winter.

### 4.4 Ice drift speed

- **SOURCED (Heil 1999, PhD thesis, University of Tasmania, "Sea-ice growth, drift and deformation off Eastern Antarctica"; abstract read through the figshare API).** From drifting buoys since the mid-1980s: "The average daily drift speed in the westward flow near the Antarctic coast is 0.22 m s⁻¹ (19.0 km d⁻¹) with considerable spatial and temporal variability. In the eastward flow north of the Antarctic Divergence the average is 0.17 m s⁻¹ (14.7 km d⁻¹)." There is "a net northward component (0.04 m s⁻¹ or 3.5 km d⁻¹)." Ocean heat flux to the ice base averaged 7.9 W m⁻² (range 5-12). "At sites close to the coast the annual maximum ice thicknesses are linked to the extent of the cooling of the oceanic mixed-layer occurring in upstream polynyas."
- **UNVERIFIED (search-result text of the AAD ASPeCt "Ice drift and deformation" page; the page now returns HTTP 410 Gone):** 39 buoys 1985-1997, mean drift 0.23 m/s (19.8 km/d) westward and 0.17 m/s (15.1 km/d) eastward.
- DERIVED: 0.22 m/s ≈ 0.43 knots. A floating structure held in place in drifting pack would see ice moving past it at roughly this speed on average; storm-driven peaks were not found (NOT FOUND for this sector).

### 4.5 Multiyear ice

- **SOURCED (Melsheimer, Spreen, Ye and Shokr 2023, *The Cryosphere* 17:105-126, "First results of Antarctic sea ice type retrieval from active and passive microwave remote sensing data", PDF read).** "Most of the Antarctic MYI is found in the Weddell Sea." "A substantial amount of the MYI along the East Antarctic coast is actually fast ice (Massom et al., 2010). This is often true MYI (older than 2 years) which is of great importance for the ecosystem and has the effect of buttressing ice shelves." On the AARI chart compared for 30 March 2017, young ice lay "mainly along the eastern Antarctic coast," and MYI "at the coast of Wilkes Land (130-170°E)," that is, well east of the Shackleton front.
- Already known (port file A): fast ice west of the Denman tongue holds the fractured R1/R2 zone in place; an open question whether part of the ADD's eastern "ice shelf" is multiyear fast ice.
- **NOT FOUND:** a multiyear-ice concentration figure for 95-105°E. The Bremen MYI dataset (PANGAEA doi:10.1594/PANGAEA.909054, 2013-2018 cold seasons) was identified but not extracted; its own caveat says "grounded icebergs can produce spurious multiyear ice."

### 5.4 What real offshore operators do about icebergs (all SOURCED from Eik 2008, *J. Navigation* 61:557-572, read in Eik's 2010 NTNU thesis PDF, unless marked)

- **Grand Banks (Newfoundland), oil production since 1997:** "On average 553 icebergs per year pass the 48°N parallel." Hibernia (80 m water, gravity base) is designed for a six-million-ton iceberg impact in the abnormal limit state. The floating production vessels Terra Nova (2002, ≈95 m) and White Rose (2005, ≈120 m) "are in need of some sort of ice management support."
  - Detection: "no single instruments will provide a 100% POD [probability of detection]" but a suite of satellites, aircraft, radar and visual watch is acceptable; in 2000-2004, "450 icebergs entered the tactical zones at Grand Banks and only two icebergs were undiscovered until they appeared within the tactical zone." "Most of the strategic and tactical iceberg detection … is performed from fixed wing aircraft." Visual detection is "severely limited by fog."
  - Deflection results from 1,500 recorded tows (Rudkin et al. 2005): "complete success" 8%, "successful" 27%, "acceptable" 42%, "poor" 23%. By method: single-vessel rope tow 77% successful (1,303 operations), two-vessel rope tow 56%, net tow 67%, propeller wash 74%, water cannon 53%. "No significant correlations between iceberg size and probability of success"; domed and wedge-shaped bergs are harder than tabular ones; no trend with wave height up to Hs 5 m, a drop at 5-5.5 m.
  - "After ten years with oil production at Grand Banks no severe accidents caused by impact with icebergs have been reported."
- **Terra Nova FPSO** (SOURCED, *Offshore* magazine, 1 April 1998, read through WebFetch): "Petro-Canada requires a planned disconnect for the approach of any iceberg in excess of 100,000 tons"; a planned disconnect "must be completed in about four hours from the decision to disconnect," with "the ability to conduct an emergency disconnect in only 15 minutes"; designers expected one disconnect a year, the operator "closer to one disconnect every three years." Water depth 312 ft (95 m); nine anchor legs of 5.75-in. chain; wellheads in "glory holes" 11.5 m deep against iceberg scour.
- **West Greenland, Fylla field, 2000 (Statoil, 1,150 m water, dynamically positioned drillship):** over ten weeks "228 iceberg targets were tracked … 64 had to be physically deflected"; "towing was successful 91% of the time"; bergs over one million tons were deflected; the drillship was forced off location once, by "one unstable iceberg in poor weather and high sea conditions"; total downtime 33 hours.
- **Icebergs in sea ice:** a 2005 attempt in the northeastern Barents Sea to tow a berg of "slightly less than 200 000 tons" frozen into 10/10 first-year ice about 0.5 m thick failed when a towline branch ruptured; lessons: "the difficulty in keeping the planned course and the risk of being trapped in the sea ice during towing." Eik's conclusion: "The possibility of handling icebergs when frozen into the sea ice is not documented."
- **NOT FOUND:** any iceberg-management operation in Antarctic waters, or any operator's procedure written for a fixed or moored structure near an Antarctic ice front.

### 6.5 Sea-spray icing

- **SOURCED (Guest, "Vessel Icing", *Mariners Weather Log* 49(3), December 2005, NOAA VOS page read):** icing conditions are wind "usually above 18 kts or 9 m/s but sometimes lower," air "below freezing (−1.7°C)," water "usually below 7°C"; icing "can occur everywhere in the Arctic Ocean and in the Southern Ocean surrounding Antarctica." Rates: light < 0.7 cm/h, moderate 0.7-2.0 cm/h, heavy 2.0-4.0 cm/h, "only a guide."
- **SOURCED (IMO Polar Code, chapter 4, para. 4.3.1.1, as reproduced on the PAME portal):** ships operating "in areas and during periods where ice accretion is likely to occur" must allow "30 kg/m² on exposed weather decks and gangways" and "7.5 kg/m² for the projected lateral area of each side of the ship above the water plane."
- **UNVERIFIED (search-result text, an Icelandic study):** below about −18 °C, spray freezes in the air before reaching the ship.
- **DERIVED (combining the sourced thresholds with the ERA5 table, not a forecast):** open-water months off the front (January-March) have air at about −2 to −9 °C mean and water near −1 to −1.5 °C (item 3.5), so the air-temperature and water-temperature conditions are met on most days; the wind condition (≥ 9 m/s) is met for a minority of hours (the January-March 90th-percentile wind is 12.7-15.5 m/s and the mean 6.5-7.9 m/s). **No measured icing rate for this sea area was found (NOT FOUND).**

### 7.3 What ice class is needed

- **SOURCED (Wikipedia "Polar Class", summarizing IACS UR I1 Table 1):** PC 1 year-round in all polar waters; PC 2 year-round in moderate multiyear ice (up to 3.0 m or more); PC 3 year-round in second-year ice with multiyear inclusions (up to 2.5 m); PC 4 year-round in thick first-year ice (over 120 cm); PC 5 year-round in medium first-year ice (70-120 cm); PC 6 summer/autumn in medium first-year ice; PC 7 summer/autumn in thin first-year ice (30-70 cm). "The IACS Polar Class rules apply for ships contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2007." Examples in the article: *Nuyina* and *Xue Long 2* PC 3; *S.A. Agulhas II* and RRS *Sir David Attenborough* PC 5.
- **SOURCED (UK MCA, MGN 637 (M), withdrawn 4 December 2025 and replaced by an amendment):** Polar Code "Category A covers ships designed for operation in polar waters at least in medium first-year ice" which "may include some old ice"; "Category B … in at least thin first-year ice"; "Category C … in open water or in ice conditions less severe."
- **UNVERIFIED (a Norwegian interpretation document seen only in search-result text):** Category A ships are built to IACS PC 1-5, Category B to PC 6-7.
- **What the sources show for this coast (facts only):** the sea ice met here includes thick first-year ice and heavy snow (2012), fast ice that did not break out in some summers (Mirny 2002), and in some years "no open water at all" (1948) or "too thick" ice 70 km from the oasis (2021-22, a vessel listed in port file D at 16,200 t displacement). The ships that reached the front or worked beside it in the sourced record are icebreakers or ice-strengthened research and supply ships (*Edisto* 1948, *Nella Dan* 1986, *Aurora Australis* 2000 and 2010, *Akademik Fedorov* 2017 and 2021-22, *Nuyina* 2025). **No source states a minimum ice class for this coast (NOT FOUND).**

### 8.2 Notes and additional facts per precedent

- **Grand Banks iceberg practice** (detection rates, tow success rates, Fylla drilling) is in item 5.4.
- **Kulluk alert system (SOURCED, Eik 2008):** two timers, Hazard Time (time until hazardous ice arrives) and Secure Time (time to secure and disconnect, "in the range of four to six hours"). Ice was watched "up to 15 km" upstream locally and to "100 km or more" regionally. "The effect of icebreaker support was, however, limited in situations with high ice pressure. Overly managed ice combined with high lateral ice pressure generated rafting and ridging and could cause even higher loads than in unmanaged ice."
- **Kulluk 2012 (SOURCED, heraldnet, Coast Guard report 2014):** while under tow to Seattle the unit met a "vicious storm," "multiple attempts to maintain tow lines failed," and it ran aground off Sitkalidak Island, Alaska, on 31 December 2012; the report cited "poor risk assessment and management"; Shell believed it "would have qualified as taxable property on Jan. 1, 2013" if it stayed in Alaska waters. Its later fate was not searched.
- **Molikpaq at Sakhalin (SOURCED, Sakhalin Energy page):** "In 1998, it was towed to South Korea," where a "15-meter steel 'spacer'" was fitted; installed 16 km off northeast Sakhalin; production first only "during the ice-free period"; a 2008 upgrade "allowed for the transition to year-round production." **UNVERIFIED (search-result text):** installed September 1998 in about 30 m of water; first oil July 1999; year-round production from December 2008.
- **McMurdo 2012 causeway (SOURCED, army.mil):** "an unusually warm winter prevented the pier from reaching the structural integrity required"; the Army's T-shaped floating causeway was "160 feet long (from ship to shore) and 144 feet wide," built "within 72 hours" by "a crew of 41 Soldiers," and "more than 8 million pounds of cargo" were unloaded over eight days; breakdown "took only 36 hours." *Conflict:* port file D cites gCaptain for 150 soldiers and 50 sailors and 2.5 days of assembly for the same operation; not resolved.
- **McMurdo 2023 (SOURCED, Maritime Executive, 3 February 2023):** "due to severe damage, and the weight of the cargo, the ice-pier was unavailable"; a "65-ton" modular causeway of "ten, 24-foot, pre-assembled pieces" with "a one-foot draft" was used; the soldiers also "had to break ice in the harbor."
- **Ice pier history (SOURCED, Wikipedia "Ice pier"):** a steel dock built in 1972 was destroyed by a storm, which led to the 1973 ice fender and then the ice pier; "Ice piers typically have a lifespan of three to five years"; principal threats are "onshore winds, high seas, and ocean swells"; the 2010 pier, washed out to sea, was found in 2011 and its equipment recovered by helicopter.
- **Very large floating structures generally (SOURCED, Wikipedia "Very large floating structure"):** horizontally large floating structures run "from 500 to 5,000 meters" long and 100-1,000 m wide with "typical thickness of 2 to 10 meters"; the pontoon type suits "calm water such as coves, lagoons, or harbors," while the semi-submersible type is "more suitable for deployment in high seas with large waves." The article has no polar or ice references.
- **Severny Polyus (SOURCED, AARI and seanews.ru):** built by Admiralty Shipyards (contract May 2018, launched 18 December 2020, delivered August 2022); its first expedition ("North Pole 41") lasted "20 months in the Arctic Ocean"; it "is capable of operating in ice conditions without the assistance of an icebreaker." **UNVERIFIED (search-result text):** the hull is shaped so that ice pressure pushes it up rather than crushing it, and fuel is sized for about two years of drift.

## FINAL: what the conditions and precedents imply, and what is not known

**Scope.** This section draws only on items 3-8 above and on port files A and D. Items 1 (sea state) and 2 (tides) are in the companion file, which was unfinished when this section was written, so wave and tide facts are not used here. **No recommendation is made on whether a floating gateway should be adopted; that is the developer's decision.**

### What the sources support

1. **Ice is present most of the year, and open water is short and unreliable.** The pack edge along 100°E lies a median ≈210-260 km north of the front in January-April and ≈540-740 km in July-October (item 7.2). Just off the front, open water averaged 14-29 days a season and was essentially absent in 6 of 9 recent seasons (port file A). Any structure kept on station off this front would sit in sea ice for most months in most years, and any ship arrival would have to cross a pack belt of the size shown in 7.2.
2. **The ice drifts, and it drifts westward along the coast.** Coastal pack drifts on average about 19-20 km/day westward (item 4.4); large icebergs cross the sector westward at a median ≈5.5 km/day along ≈64-65°S (item 5.1). The slope current near 113°E is westward with a mean transport of −29.2 Sv and surface speeds up to ≈10-18 cm/s (item 3.1). A moored structure here would face ice and bergs arriving mostly from the east.
3. **The ice includes thick and deformed ice.** Regional pack averages roughly 0.7-1.2 m including ridges, with ≈10-14% of the surface ridged (Worby 2008); first-year ice thick enough to trap an icebreaker for ≈10 days occurred east of the sector in 2012; fast ice can fail to break out for a whole summer (Mirny 2002) and multiyear fast ice occurs along this coast (item 4).
4. **Large icebergs are a recurring presence, and some stay for decades.** 2-10 tracked large bergs a year crossed or stood in the sector from 2003 on; several stayed nearly stationary for 8-21 years at ≈64-65°S (C-05, C-10, C-21B, C-24, C-30, C-31), consistent with grounding on banks ≈230-270 km from the oasis (item 5.1). Three large calvings ≈80-140 km from the oasis happened in March-April 2022 (C-39 from the Scott front, first listed ≈83 km away; C-38 from the Conger Ice Shelf, ≈113 km; C-37 from the Glenzer, ≈141 km; DERIVED distances from the NIC positions).
5. **The wind blows from the land toward the sea, and is strongest in winter.** ERA5 gives an east-southeasterly mean wind of ≈8-9 m/s over the water, gale-force ≈15-20% of hours in June-August, and hourly extremes of ≈35 m/s (item 6.1). Sea-spray-icing conditions (air and water temperature) are met in the open-water months; the wind condition is met for a minority of hours (item 6.5).
6. **The water is at or near freezing below the surface, with warmer water deeper.** Shelf water is below −1.8 °C; supercooled ice-shelf water (−1.95 to −2.05 °C) lies at 100-200 m near the front; warmer modified deep water (up to ≈−0.2 to −1.0 °C) reaches parts of the shelf, and has become more widespread since about 2010 (item 3.2).
7. **The real precedents that hold station in moving ice do so by brute strength, by leaving, or only seasonally.** Bottom-founded structures (Molikpaq, Hibernia, Prirazlomnaya) rest on the seabed in 19-80 m of water; the water off this front is ≈115-460 m deep (port file A), deeper than any bottom-founded precedent found. The floating precedents (Kulluk, Terra Nova, Sakhalin's floating storage) either disconnect and move off when ice or a large iceberg approaches, need two to four escort icebreakers, or work only in the open-water season. The one platform built to live in drifting pack (*Severny Polyus*) drifts with the ice instead of holding a position. Large pontoon platforms (Mega-Float) were built for sheltered water.
8. **The established iceberg-management methods are open-water methods.** Towing and deflection succeed in roughly 53-91% of attempts depending on method and site in open water (Grand Banks, Fylla); "the possibility of handling icebergs when frozen into the sea ice is not documented" (Eik 2008), which is the situation for most of the year off this front.
9. **Daylight is not a hard limit.** At 65.7°S the sun rises every day; midwinter has ≈3 hours of sun and ≈6 hours of civil light (item 6.6).

### Where the sources do not support a conclusion

- **Whether any existing class of floating structure could stay moored year-round off this front.** No precedent of a year-round moored floating structure in Antarctic pack, or in any drifting pack ice, was found; the absence is a gap in the record, not evidence either way.
- **Ice loads for this coast.** No design ice load or ISO 19906-type assessment exists for this sea; item 4.6's loads come from the Beaufort, Pechora and Grand Banks and are not transferable without analysis.
- **Fast-ice and pack thickness at the front itself:** not measured in any source found.
- **Current speed at the front itself:** the nearest moored current record is at 113°E; the only measurement under the shelf is ≈1 cm/s.
- **Fog and visibility over this water:** no percentages found.
- **Why the C-21B/C-24/C-30/C-31 bergs are stationary:** grounding is inferred from position only; depths there are gridded estimates.
- **How often ships fail to reach the front:** only case histories exist.

### NOT FOUND (collected from items 3-8)

Current-meter data at the front; years in which the Shackleton Polynya did not open; fast-ice thickness at the front or Mirny figures in centimeters; measured katabatic speed over the polynya; sea-fog frequency for 90-110°E; a measured icing rate for this sea; a measured calving-wave height at any Antarctic ice-shelf front; a minimum ice class stated for this coast; a failure rate for voyages to the front; any Chinese voyage to the Shackleton front; Mega-Float's mooring method and cost; a design ice load for any Antarctic structure; any year-round floating or moored structure in Antarctic waters.
