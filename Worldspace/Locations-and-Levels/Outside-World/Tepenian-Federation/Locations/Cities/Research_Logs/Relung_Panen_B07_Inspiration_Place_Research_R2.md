### A. Other settings (sub-areas, housing, schools, outdoor spaces)
- **Worker housing as a regulated category [K4, K6, P4]:** a 2018 municipal report (Jones Consulting Group) counted ~328 boarding units holding 3,300+ temporary agricultural workers, with illegal boarding houses and poor registration; a councillor warned the greenhouse industry could halt for lack of living quarters; Deputy Mayor (later Mayor) Hilda MacDonald: "there's no other community like ours facing these kinds of issues" — AM800 CKLW, 28–29 May 2018. **SOURCED.** Council policy of 29 Apr 2021 let existing boarding houses continue to 29 Apr 2022 and farm-worker dwellings to 31 Dec 2022 if retrofitted to the boarding-house provisions of the Building and Fire Codes; deadline later extended (11 Jan 2022 amendment); Official Plan Amendment No. 8 in force; Zoning By-law Amendment #17-21 appealed to the Ontario Land Tribunal by OGVG and therefore not in force — Municipality of Leamington, "Boarding Houses and Farm Worker Dwellings" page. **SOURCED.** May 2021: off-site farm-help dwellings in the agricultural area to sit within 800 m of the farm with a pedestrian path to it; boarding houses allowed to stay under a business license — search snippet (Southpoint Sun, 5 May 2021). **UNVERIFIED.** Employer-provided farm housing is exempt from Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act (snippet, The Narwhal). **UNVERIFIED.**

## 2 · Cameron Highlands, Malaysia — Round 2

**R1 open threads chased:** share of farmland under rain shelters · whether the 2023 lorry ban stood · festival calendar beyond Thaipusam/Ponggal · foreign workers' wages and housing. Founder-nation care note honored: structure only. Results in F.

### A. Other settings
- **Settlement pattern [P4, P5]:** three towns — Ringlet (1,100 m, first town after the Tapah turnoff; vegetables, passion fruit, strawberries), Tanah Rata (1,400 m, administrative center: police, government offices, hospital, schools, library, post office, bus station; jungle tracks to waterfalls and Orang Asli villages), Brinchang (1,450 m, second town; central market, nurseries, golf course, weekend night market) — plus five farm settlements: Bertam Valley, Kea Farm, Tringkap (terraced vegetables, bulk of output to Singapore), Kuala Terla (fruit, flowers, vegetables; ~4 km from Kampung Raja) and Kampung Raja (first town from the Simpang Pulai side) — Wikipedia, "Settlements in Cameron Highlands." **SOURCED.** Bandar Baru Brinchang developed in the early 1990s — The Edge (Feb 2010). **SOURCED.**
- **Farm settlements as postwar creations [P8, P9]:** development began after 1926; "three new vegetable farming settlements developed after World War II"; farms averaged one to two acres, worked by family labor; ~25 temperate crops above 1,000 m; trucks to the peninsula's cities with 25–30% exported to Singapore; heavy use of chicken dung as fertilizer; almost all seed imported from Japan — Shirasaka, "The Agricultural Development of Hill Stations in Tropical Asia: A Case Study in the Cameron Highlands," *Geographical Review of Japan* B 61(2), 1988 (abstract read on J-STAGE). **SOURCED.** A 1968 University of Chicago study, James D. Clarkson, *The Cultural Ecology of a Chinese Village, Cameron Highlands, Malaysia*, studies Bertam Valley's Chinese vegetable growers (174 pp.) — Penang Bookshelf listing. **SOURCED** (bibliographic only; text not read).
- **Housing stock [K4]:** 7,158 dwellings in 2003, 66.2% owner-occupied, 33.8% rented; shophouse prices doubled 1991–2010; a local environmental group (REACH) judged development at saturation — The Edge, Feb 2010. **SOURCED.** Mixed developments are supposed to be capped at seven storeys but special approvals have allowed up to 22 (a local organization head, quoted) — The Star, 28 Mar 2025. **SOURCED.**
- **Worker housing [K4, K6]:** foreign workers live in shared on-farm housing where they also eat and rest during breaks; employers must house them under Act 446 (workers' minimum housing standards) (snippets). **UNVERIFIED.** Sept 2021: the Human Resources Minister announced a Centralised Labour Quarters "pioneer project" for agricultural foreign workers in Cameron Highlands, site to be chosen by the Pahang government near workplaces; only documented workers to be housed; aim to end scattered housing and ease monitoring — Malay Mail, 9 Sep 2021. **SOURCED.** Whether it was built: **NOT FOUND.**
- **Indigenous resettlement [K4, K9]:** Kampung Sungai Ruil, Semai Orang Asli village on the edge of Tanah Rata (via Jalan Titiwangsa), ~45 houses and ~1,300 people (as stated); a landslide at 5:45 pm on 7 Aug 2011 killed seven and buried houses; the dead were buried at the Taman Sedia Muslim Cemetery the next day — Penang Travel Tips. **SOURCED** (as stated; the house count looks low against the population and is reported as given). A 152-house resettlement project followed the landslide (snippet). **UNVERIFIED.** Sungai Telimau's deputy headman (2014) recalls when the community could rely wholly on the forest, before the land "gradually turned into illegal farms" — The Star, 20 Nov 2014 (paywalled; opening read). **SOURCED.**

### B. Subcultures
- **Chinese smallholder farmers [K1, K7, P9]:** farming here driven by Chinese farmers since the 1920s, expanding in the 1980s; a 1990s licensing problem in which licences were issued for one lot while farmers occupied another, without surveys or boundary marking; since 2020 the Pahang Corporation has taken over agricultural land from the Land Office and is legalizing 5,526 ha by rental: rent up from ~RM1,000 to RM4,500 per acre, maximum three acres per person, MyGAP certification now mandatory, instalments allowed — The Rakyat Post, 6 Feb 2025. **SOURCED.** Bodies named: Cameron Highlands Vegetable Growers Association (secretary for decades also deputy chair of the Federation of Malaysian Vegetable Farmers' Associations) (snippet) **UNVERIFIED**; Cameron Highlands Chinese Farmers Association — The Malaysian Insight, 29 Jun 2023. **SOURCED.** [K1, K14]
- **Foreign workers [K4, K7]:** 5,000–7,000, mostly Indonesian, Bangladeshi and Nepalese; tea-estate pay RM1,300–1,500, service/food RM1,500, construction RM1,500–1,800 (the same rates offered locals, per an employer); employers say locals avoid manual work; a Sungai Ruil Orang Asli representative said farms prefer foreigners because they work longer and cannot refuse because of their permits, and payment delays put locals off — FMT, 17 Jan 2019. **SOURCED.** A farmer quoted: the land belongs to the Orang Asli and "we are the ones encroaching." **SOURCED** (same).

### A. Other settings (settlements, compounds, schools, clinics)
- **Workforce size and gender (R1 threads) [K4, K7, P9]:** an estimated 35,000 employees in about 55 flower farms in the wider Naivasha area (including the Kinangop) at the time of fieldwork, built from figures for 31 of 54 farms (24,695 employees; the three largest farms had 10,000 between them); a local union official's count gave 37,000 (Happ 2016, cited) — Kuiper 2017, pp. 118–119. **SOURCED.** About 30,000 workers in 30+ farms (Standard, 13 Aug 2018, via Business & Human Rights Resource Center) **SOURCED**; "over 40 flower farms" in Naivasha (The Star, 12 Mar 2024) **SOURCED**. Women's share: managers of three farms gave 40–60% female; Dolan, Opondo and Smith (2003) estimated 60% for the industry — Kuiper 2017, p. 112. **SOURCED.** A 2020 Star article gives women as 75%+ of the horticultural workforce (as stated) **SOURCED**; snippets give 65–75% **UNVERIFIED**. **The figures conflict; no Naivasha-wide count was found.** Residents were about evenly split by sex: Mirera sub-location (which holds Karagita) 19,554 men and 19,655 women in 2009; in 1962 Naivasha had only 85 women per 100 men — Kuiper 2017, pp. 112, 209–210 (citing the censuses). **SOURCED.**
- **Eight workers' settlements [K4, P4]:** Kihoto, Karagita, DCK, Kwa Muhia, Kamere, Kongoni, Tarabete sub-location (North Lake) and KCC — Kuiper 2017, p. 196 ff. **SOURCED.** What they are like: dense, dusty, pot-holed, little vegetation, no garbage system; quiet by day with toddlers and small livestock in the streets; busy in the late afternoon when workers come back and trade clusters at the bus drop-off points. Plots themselves are neat; the narrow central alleys of rental plots are where women do laundry. Rooms are divided by hung curtains or sheets; walls carry family photos, party calendars and European soccer posters. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, pp. 196, 219).
  - **Karagita** (south-east shore, ~10 km from town): a land-buying company of Africans from Nairobi bought a 1930s settler plot in 1966; first mud-and-stick houses 1972; early income from illicit liquor, fish and firewood; owners then built rows of one-room rentals for migrants; ~10,000 people by 1990; formally subdivided into 50 × 100 m plots in the early 1990s; with neighboring settlements (Mirera sub-location) 39,209 people in 2009. First borehole on record 1991; fluoride-free drinking water still a problem. Mirera Primary School opened c. 1980 (376 pupils sat final exams in 2011, the most of any school in Kenya that year). The dispensary opened 1991 in the old settler house, part government-funded, part raised by a residents' harambee (fundraising event). A 2002 court-ordered eviction on a disputed neighboring plot demolished housing; only a mosque was left standing. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, pp. 197–199).
  - **Kihoto** (in town): a former cooperative farm split among 30 Kikuyu shareholders into strips running down to the lake, which lengthened as the lake fell; owners live in older houses on one side of a plot and rent brick one-room units on the other. A flower farm on the strip north of it (opened 1996) closed in 2011 after a land dispute and was stripped by laid-off workers within two hours. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, p. 200).
  - **Tarabete sub-location** (north-west shore, ~30 km from town): the one settlement founded by government (1987), on 15 acres given to the Town Council when a 6,000-acre settler farm was split among 182 Kikuyu members; the plot had been fallow land used for drinking illicit brew and playing football; plots sold for ~KES 10,000, first house 1988; growth "suddenly" from about 1992 when farms switched to year-round roses; 8,699 people in 2009; chief and administrative police post since 1990; a KPAWU branch office; a 5-acre public cemetery; a mosque built in 1993 with a donor from Kuwait; one farm's on-site worker compound rebuilt from wood to brick two-room units to meet Fairtrade standards. Its common unofficial name is recorded in the slang file, not here. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, pp. 200–203, 214, 244).
  - **Kamere** (south-west shore): a 32-acre plot owned since at least 1983 by a former Attorney-General, subdivided and sold piecemeal with little infrastructure; on a steep hill with no drainage; ~24,000 people in 2015 (a planning officer said 40,000); named as a main source of lake pollution; beside an official fish landing beach and a KenGen staff compound; "a lively market along the Moi Lake Road." **Kwa Muhia**, next to it, was a settler farm sold to a Kikuyu buyer who subdivided it in the early 1990s; holds a KPAWU branch office; its only cemetery is the original owner's family plot. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, pp. 203–204, 221, 244).
  - **KCC** (Morendat, by the former Kenya Co-operative Creameries plant): the only settlement wholly on squatted land; corrugated-iron housing after two great fires (one in 2013 left 3,000+ families homeless, as reported); a row of iron-sheet churches at the entrance; two water tanks, a toilet block and a nursery school as its only formal infrastructure; illegal power connections; hippos a hazard. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, pp. 204–205).
  - **DCK** grew around the first flower farm (opened early 1970s) as a few dusty streets of shops, food kiosks and an ATM; most residents no longer know what the initials stood for. **Kongoni** (by Lake Oloiden) has had a police station since at least 1971 and stayed small because the largest farm houses its own workers. **SOURCED** (Kuiper 2017, pp. 199–200, 224).

### B. Subcultures
- **Migrant workers as permanent strangers [K4, K7]:** many treat their region of origin as home and all of Naivasha, dwellings included, as a workplace; they invest in a plot "at home," visit on annual leave, keep up home-village savings groups by mobile money; one single mother with no friends in Naivasha said she spends her day off washing clothes and sleeping, and sent her son back to his grandparents because she could not keep him in school — Kuiper 2017, pp. 20, 217–218, 243. **SOURCED.** About 40% of respondents used neither a Sacco nor a savings group. **SOURCED** (same, p. 215).
- **Landlords vs. tenants [K4, K9]:** settlement landowners are relatively often Kikuyu; migrants from western Kenya fear to invest locally after the 2008 post-election violence (a woman from Kakamega would not open a hair salon in Karagita); some move valuables home or leave family behind; no ethnic neighborhoods were found — every surveyed compound was mixed; interethnic marriage is common — Kuiper 2017, pp. 211–213. **SOURCED.**
- **Religious life [K4, K10]:** 95.5% of respondents Christian, 55.1% active members; 27 denominations named (Catholic the largest, with two parishes around the lake and a church in almost every settlement, led by volunteer catechists — the Tarabete catechist is a farm supervisor); many small local churches; a prophet-led church present in several settlements; few Muslims but several centrally placed mosques. Because farm work runs seven days, churches hold prayer meetings and counseling on weekday evenings for workers who cannot attend on Sunday; men pray together in the farm changing room before work — Kuiper 2017, pp. 185, 214. **SOURCED.** A worker attends Full Gospel Church every afternoon after work (Kuiper 2017, ch. 4). **SOURCED.**

**Most useful Round 2 finding per pick, one line each:**
1. Leamington and Kingsville — the migrant workforce has its own off-day civic calendar run through settled immigrant halls and churches (workers' soccer cup organized by farm, Festival of Guest Nations, Sunday outreach), while the towns regulate it through boarding-house licensing, light-bylaw prosecutions (88 counts, Feb 2023) and night rules at the waterfront.
2. Cameron Highlands — smallholders farm under yearly occupation licences now being converted to rental (RM4,500/acre, three-acre cap), and a weekend lorry ban (from June 2023) sets the trade's hours; 5,000–7,000 foreign workers fill farm labor.
3. Đà Lạt — the flower villages each trace to a named 1930s–1950s migration from the north, the Tết market sets the year, a music-café culture underlies the 2023 UNESCO music designation, and an official greenhouse rollback (to zero in inner wards by 2030) is resisted by growers.
4. Souss-Massa — Aït Amira has become a multinational worker town (Tachelhit, Darija, Wolof, Bambara, French) with an evening market timed to farm shifts and football as the shared ground; wage protests and arrests in Nov 2024.
5. Ica — the water comes partly from five Quechua-speaking herding communities across the Andes (transfer since 1950; a 2023 compensation law without regulations), and highland migrants settle in desert lots that wait years for titles and services.
6. Lake Naivasha — eight workers' settlements, each with its own land history, run their daily life through savings, welfare and burial groups, churches fitted around seven-day farm shifts, and a farm-built football tradition; the lake's riparian and abstraction rules are now confirmed in primary text (2013 Protection Order), and Kenya's bar-hours law was moved by Naivasha's own MP.
