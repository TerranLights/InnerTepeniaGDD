## 1. Real winter flights into Antarctica by jets or heavy aircraft

**Scope note.** "Winter" here is roughly April to October, as the brief sets it. The sources split winter flights into four kinds, and they are kept apart below because they are not the same thing: (a) **scheduled late-winter fly-ins** (McMurdo WINFLY, August), (b) **planned mid-winter proof-of-concept flights** (McMurdo 2015), (c) **emergency medical evacuations**, and (d) **airdrops without landing** (Australian stations). Outside McMurdo, no source was found of a jet or heavy aircraft **landing** in Antarctica between May and July except emergency medevacs.

### 1a. McMurdo WINFLY (winter fly-in), United States, every August
- **What it is.** "Winfly is the time between winter and the summer when additional support personnel, such as carpenters and cooks, arrive to prepare the station for the upcoming science field season." **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun, 17 Aug 2010 ("In the dark"), https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/2232/ (read through the Internet Archive; the live site failed DNS lookup).
- **How many flights, how many people, and how it went, by year** (all **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun pages read via the Internet Archive unless marked):
  - **2008:** WinFly was pushed into September and renamed SpringFly for budget reasons; five flights; the fifth, on 11 Sept 2008, was the first Antarctic C-17 landing on night-vision goggles; "one flight was delayed by about 24 hours"; ground temperature "about minus 30 [°F] or so; usually, we see minus 40 or 50." Before 2008 WinFly "took place a couple of weeks earlier, using a temporary runway on the sea ice near McMurdo Station." Round trip Christchurch-Pegasus-Christchurch: "12-hour." — Antarctic Sun, 26 Sep 2008, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/1544/
  - **2009:** five round trips planned, one of them an NVG training mission; first flight scheduled 20 Aug, "two straight days of poor weather conditions at McMurdo have delayed the arrival of the first flight"; it landed 22 Aug with 120 passengers; about 350 people for WinFly; 153 winter-over personnel at McMurdo. — Antarctic Sun, 21 Aug 2009, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/1865/
  - **2010:** "Seven flights are planned ... beginning on Aug. 13"; first three to be flown on NVGs; first sunrise at McMurdo 19 Aug; 198 people at the station over winter; "More than 400 people are expected to arrive in McMurdo in August." The first flight landed 15 Aug 2010 with "About 120 people," the first year passengers flew on an NVG mission. — Antarctic Sun, 30 Jul 2010 (https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/2217/) and 17 Aug 2010 (…/2232/).
  - **2012:** "six missions"; "More than 300 people"; first flights 20 Aug, last 28 Aug; "all of the missions will use night-vision goggles (NVG) for landing, a capability the Air Force established several years ago and retests each winter." — Antarctic Sun, 17 Aug 2012, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/2724/ . Outcome: six missions, "319 passengers and more than 230,000 pounds of cargo" in and "69 passengers and more than 35,000 pounds of cargo" out, with "multiple weather delays," completed nine days after starting 20 Aug. — Air & Space Forces Magazine, 13 Sep 2012, https://www.airandspaceforces.com/deepfreezeswinterflyingseasonconcludes
  - **2014:** "A storm packing 80-mile-per-hour winds slammed into McMurdo Station earlier this month, delaying the first flights ... by four days"; more than 200 people arrived from 24 Aug "after waiting in Christchurch ... for a break in the weather"; two passenger flights 24 Aug, two more 26 Aug; the last WinFly flight, an NVG cargo-only mission, 27 Aug, after which "another condition 1 storm moved into the region." Both the USAF C-17 and the Australian A319 used Pegasus, "a permanent, hard-ice runway located about 14 miles from McMurdo, on the glacial ice of the McMurdo Ice Shelf." "There haven't been any flights to McMurdo since March." Condition 1 "can involve wind speeds greater than 55 knots (about 63 mph), wind chills colder than minus 100 degrees Fahrenheit or visibility of less than 100 feet." — Antarctic Sun, 29 Aug 2014, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/4064/
- **Fuel practice on WinFly (C-17):** "The planes will carry enough fuel so they won't have to fill up at the airfield, meaning the engines will remain running while the military transport is on the ground for about an hour. The flight between New Zealand and Antarctica is about five hours each way." The squadron commander: "once the plane is going, everything works great. It's when you shut them down and try to start them back up again in the extreme cold that we increase the likelihood of a maintenance problem." August is "Historically ... the coldest month in McMurdo, with a mean temperature of minus 25 degrees Fahrenheit." — Antarctic Sun, 30 Jul 2010. **SOURCED.**
- **Pattern (DERIVED from the year-by-year list above, not stated by any source as a rate):** of the five WinFly seasons with an outcome found (2008, 2009, 2010, 2012, 2014), every one reported at least one weather delay at the start or during the operation (24 h in 2008; two days in 2009; "multiple" in 2012; four days in 2014), and 2010's first flight landed two days after its scheduled date (13 → 15 Aug; the reason is not stated). No source gives a WinFly cancellation count or a long-run delay rate. **NOT FOUND.**

### 1b. Planned mid-winter flights to McMurdo (2015 proof of concept)
- NSF scheduled flights to Pegasus "in April, June and July" 2015: two mid-April flights by the Royal New Zealand Air Force and the Australian Antarctic Division, and USAF C-17 flights on 1 June and 17 July, "primarily ... cargo the first year." NSF's manager: "We say we're accessible year-round. We just don't plan for it." "The challenges are going to be opening the runways and having a good weather period." Airfield manager: "The crew in the winter will activate the airfield according to the planned schedule." A new set of battery runway lights "upgraded with a glass mat battery for longer burn time in the cold" was bought for the A319 and RNZAF C-130, which do not land on NVGs as the C-17 does. A C-17 "flies weekly out of the Royal Australian Air Force Base Richmond near Sydney" and "could be diverted to Christchurch and return to its normal mission schedule within 24 hours." **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun, 31 Oct 2014, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/4086/
- The Australian A319's part: on 17 April 2015 the AAD announced it would fly "13 United States Antarctic Program (USAP) expeditioners" from Hobart direct to McMurdo "for the winter," the first time; "Australia's airbus usually undertakes 10–15 flights for the Australian Antarctic program each season, carrying about 400 passengers." **SOURCED**, AAD news 17 Apr 2015, https://www.antarctica.gov.au/news/2015/united-states-antarctic-program-trials-hobart-mcmurdo-flight/
- **Outcome:** an A319 landing on 18 April "during twilight" and two C-17 flights, "rounding out a three-flight, proof-of-concept program," with a further two winter flights authorized for the next year and "as many as six flights per winter season in the future." **UNVERIFIED** (search-result text of a SpaceRef reprint of an Antarctic Sun article; spaceref.com returned 403, died at the source). A USAF account of the 2015-16 season says night-vision missions ran "in June and July 2015," when "it normally would have been too dark to operate" (**SOURCED**, DVIDS, posted 14 Sep 2016, http://www.dvidshub.net/news/209599/night-vision-landings-shed-light-antarctic-airlift), which confirms flights in both months but not their loads or delays. CRREL (2019) refers to "the recent addition of monthly austral winter flights (April through July)" as part of NSF planning (**SOURCED**, item 4a). Whether monthly winter flights continued after 2016 was **NOT FOUND**.

### 1f. What item 1 adds up to (facts only)
- The only place in Antarctica with **recurring planned jet landings in late winter** is McMurdo (WINFLY, August, C-17 on NVGs since 2008-2012; A319 in some years). Mid-winter landings there (May-July) are emergency medevacs or the 2015 trial. All are from Christchurch or Hobart, 3,800-4,000 km, with round-trip fuel or hot refueling and no diversion field past the critical point. (**SOURCED** pieces as cited; summary **DERIVED**.)

## 2. Flying and landing in darkness and low light

### 2a. How dark it is (the light available)
- **At McMurdo (about 77.8°S):** "There is no sunlight in Antarctica for several months of the year, from around late March to mid-August"; winter-over crews have "complete darkness for four and a half months." **SOURCED**, US Air Force news (AFPN), 12 Sep 2008, read through the Internet Archive copy at globalsecurity.org (https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2008/09/mil-080912-afpn02.htm; the live page asked for payment, 402). First sunrise in 2010: 19 August. **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 30 Jul 2010.
- **At Mirny's latitude (66.55°S), month by month (DERIVED, my computation** from a standard solar-declination approximation, ±0.5° in declination, so about ±10-20 minutes; not an almanac; sun's center at −0.83° for sunrise, −6° civil twilight, −12° nautical twilight):

|---|---|---|---|---|

| 15 May | 4.7° | 5.6 h | 7.9 h | 10.2 h |

- **Moonlight matters for NVGs.** RNZAF flight commander, July 2021: "Night goggles' depth perception is pretty good," but "it's not entirely great down there because [we] rely on the moon"; that night was "overcast, the temperature as cold as -20C with a thick cloud layer at 3000ft" and "There was no starlight [getting] through a pretty solid cloud base." **SOURCED**, NZ Herald, July 2021, https://nzherald.co.nz/nz/antarctica-emergency-flight-into-winter-darkness/JE7E23D6WSPMXBSDZJIPNY55KI

### 2b. Methods actually used to land in the dark
1. **Burning fuel drums (before 2008).** "Until now, during this time the runway was used only in emergency situations, and in such cases 55-gallon drums of kerosene were lighted to illuminate the runway--a practice called 'barrel burning'." **SOURCED**, AFPN 2008. Airfield manager: "Electric and battery-powered lights don't do well in the cold, when temperatures can plunge to negative 60 degrees Fahrenheit." **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 26 Sep 2008. A Soviet pilot recalls asking for "a fire to be lit on the ground" to find a landing place. **SOURCED** (memoir), *Rossiyskaya Gazeta* 2006.
2. **Night-vision goggles plus unpowered retro-reflective markers (USAF C-17, since 2008).** "64 high-intensity retro-reflective cones placed on the runway edge ... When the aircraft turns to final [approach], the wingtip light reflects off the cones to the NVG's and we can see the entire runway"; "they light up like Christmas trees even though they're not powered ... you could see these things from two, three miles out"; the C-17's head-up display lets pilots fly on goggles while watching aircraft data. **SOURCED**, AFPN 2008. The cones "were screwed into the ice"; setting them out took "only about half a day." **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 2008. Current rule: "Due to the lack of runway edge lighting and the minimal contrast between the runway edge and the surrounding ice, High Intensity Retro-Reflective Runway Identification Markers (HIRRRIM) are utilized to aid in runway identification during C- 17 austral winter flight operations." **SOURCED**, *USAP Interagency Air Operations Manual*, revision 1 March 2026, §1.6.2.6, https://www.usap.gov/logistics/documents/Air_Operations_Manual.pdf (PDF downloaded and read).
3. **Temporary electric edge and threshold lights (non-military operators).** "The NSF Prime Contractor also manages a set of temporary runway edge and threshold lighting to be used by non-DOD aviation service providers as required outside of the austral summer." **SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §1.6.2.7. The policy line: "The utilization of Night Vision Goggles (NVG) by DoD and temporary runway edge/threshold lights (non- DoD service providers) provides year-round capability to McMurdo." **SOURCED**, §1.16.1. These are the battery lights "upgraded with a glass mat battery for longer burn time in the cold temperatures" for the A319 and RNZAF C-130. **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 31 Oct 2014. In July 2021 "The US crew at McMurdo Station placed lights on the ice runway to help the Kiwis land." **SOURCED**, NZ Herald 2021.
4. **Goggles for the approach, naked eye for touchdown (RNZAF C-130J, 2025).** The pilots flew the approach on NVGs and "took them off for the landing," saying "It was easier to see without the night-vision goggles on." **UNVERIFIED** (search-result text quoting Air Commodore Andy Scott; the NZDF release itself, read in full, says only that the crew "used Night Vision Goggles").
5. **Permanent lighting and visual aids at McMurdo's main fields (all seasons).** Approach lighting (SSALR) "may be installed" at Phoenix and Williams Field; runway end identifier lights (REILS), "synchronized flashing white lights," pilot-activated by "keying the transmitter 5 times within a 10 second window," and switched on for an arrival "when the ceiling and visibility is 3,000/5 or less"; a two-box APAPI glide-path light, which at Phoenix is "LED/IR" with "Visibility Mode for daytime operations and NVG Mode for NVG landings at night"; international orange windsocks at every approach end. **SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §1.6.2.
6. **No lights, no aids, daylight only.** Zenit (Progress) has no radio aid or lighting and runs "only in daytime with good visibility" (already known). Troll: "There is no instrument landing system (ILS); instead visual flight rules (VFR) apply" (**SOURCED at Wikipedia, uncited sentence**). Wolf's Fang: "absolutely no navigation aids" and visual contact needed from about 20 miles (already known).
7. **Wilkins (Casey):** the current Airservices Australia chart shows **runway lighting "RL: NIL"**, a PAPI on the left side of runway 09T only, and nothing on 27T. **SOURCED**, Airservices Australia DAP aerodrome chart WKSAD01-187, 9 Jul 2026 (copy at crc.id.au; PDF text extracted). *Reading:* Wilkins as published has no edge lighting for darkness; it is a summer daylight field. (DERIVED from the chart plus its October-March season.)

### 2c. Approach aids in use on Antarctic ice and snow runways
- **McMurdo (Phoenix, Williams Field):** "TLS, RNAV, and TACAN approaches"; RNAV (GPS) approaches also at the South Pole skiway; all "developed and certified annually," with FAA or equivalent flight checks "in mid to late October." **SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §§1.3.4.3, 1.7.2-1.7.5. (TLS = transponder landing system, a precision approach.)
- **Wilkins:** an RNP (satellite) approach to runway 09T only, with LNAV/VNAV usable in a procedure temperature range of **−30 °C to 0 °C**; the chart warns "READINGS FROM RADIO & RADAR ALTIMETERS MAY BE ERRATIC OVER ICE & SNOW SURFACES." An automatic weather broadcast (AWIS) is pilot-activated. **SOURCED**, Airservices DAP WKSGN01-187, 9 Jul 2026.
- **Ski landings in whiteout:** at McMurdo "A Whiteout Landing Area has been established on the Ross Ice Shelf, for ski-only landings, when landings are not possible on prepared surfaces due to weather," surveyed and certified each year, with up to two approach plates. **SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §1.5.4. "Currently there are no adverse weather landing sites available for wheeled aircraft in Antarctica. Aircrew must ensure sufficient reserve fuel is carried to allow diverting to an alternate airfield off continent." **SOURCED**, §1.5.2.
- **Whiteout defined (the low-light hazard that does not need darkness):** "an optical phenomenon that occurs in uniformly overcast sky conditions over a snow–covered surface ... diffuse (uniform), shadow less illumination that causes a complete lack of surface and horizon definitions"; "Whiteout conditions can occur while visibility (i.e. transparency of the air) remains good"; surface and horizon definition are reported as good, fair, poor or nil, "although there are no internationally agreed-upon definitions." **SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §2.5.4.

### 3b. Fuel

### 3c. Ground equipment and people at −20 to −40 °C (what real operations report)
- **Engines kept running instead of shut down.** USAF WinFly: engines "remain running while the military transport is on the ground for about an hour" because restart in extreme cold "increase[s] the likelihood of a maintenance problem" (**SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 2010). RNZAF 2021: refueled "with its engines running ... 'We keep the aircraft warm and stop any issues with shutting down the aircraft'" (**SOURCED**, NZ Herald 2021); RNZAF 2025 "hot refueling" (**SOURCED**, NZDF). At McMurdo, engine-running operations need the airfield manager's approval, otherwise aircraft "will be expected to shutdown to prevent damage to the airfield surfaces" (**SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §1.3.4.4).
- **External heaters on the aircraft.** C-17 at Pegasus in winter: five heating units used "to keep the aircraft from freezing up while it was being loaded" at −19 °F before wind chill. **UNVERIFIED** (search-result text of an af.mil photo caption; af.mil 403).
- **Lights and batteries.** "Electric and battery-powered lights don't do well in the cold, when temperatures can plunge to negative 60 degrees Fahrenheit" (**SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 2008); McMurdo's winter battery lights were "upgraded with a glass mat battery for longer burn time in the cold temperatures" (**SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun 2014).
- **Small engines and vehicles, Davis, August 2023:** at −20 to −25 °C side-by-sides "became very unhappy"; "pull starting [generators] at −25°C / −30°C impossible" unless warmed in a heated hut; unmodified Hilux utes started at −30 °C but were "put to the sword at −35°C ... with no plug in heating"; the writer got frostbite on two fingers in "slightly warmer temps." **SOURCED** (first-person station report, AAD 2023).
- **Aircraft frozen to the surface.** South Pole 2001: thawing the aircraft and freeing "frozen skis" from the snow delayed departure (**SOURCED**, item 3a).
- **Snow blowers.** The Wilkins snow blowers that clear winter snow both had mechanical failures in 2018; parts had to come from Germany and Norway by C-17 airdrop (**SOURCED**, AAD 2018; see item 4).

## 4. Keeping an ice, snow or sea-ice runway open through winter

**Already known, not repeated:** Wilkins' spring start-up (a crew of four, "two months," at least three surface passes, snow clearing, proof-rolling); Zenit's winter build and its slab hardening about 20% over its first winter; Phoenix's six-week mid-summer closure; the McMurdo sea-ice runway's October-December window and 2 m figure; Mario Zucchelli's groomed sea ice about 2 m thick; Pegasus's melt-and-dust failure.

### 4a. The one documented case of a runway kept usable year-round: McMurdo
- **Pegasus (glacial ice under a thin snow cap, 1993-2016).** "Prior to December of 2012, Pegasus could maintain operations year-round"; it was the field NSF relied on "for the months when the sea-ice airstrip was no longer operable (early December through September)." From 2012 it needed "more maintenance while providing fewer days of full operability," and in its last years "could not be used from December through early March." **SOURCED**, Haehnel, Blaisdell, Melendy, Shoop and Courville, *A Snow Runway for Supporting Wheeled Aircraft: Phoenix Airfield, McMurdo, Antarctica*, ERDC/CRREL TR-19-4, May 2019, pp. 1-2 (PDF read through the Internet Archive; the DTIC original returned 403). *Reading:* at McMurdo the year-round limit was summer melt, not winter. (**DERIVED** from the passage.)
- **How Pegasus was brought back after winter.** In its early years "the runway was allowed to naturally drift in and was ignored" over winter; "Depending on winter storms anywhere from 57,000 - 250,000 cubic yards of snow are removed to make Pegasus operational" (about 44,000-190,000 m³, **DERIVED** conversion). For a March 1996 medevac (canceled when the patient recovered) "The runway was completely ready to accept a flight in just a few days." **SOURCED**, John Hahn (ASA Operations), "Historical Background of WINFLY," July 1998, reproduced at southpolestation.com, https://southpolestation.com/trivia/90s/pegasushistory.html (secondary host). WINFLY flights "traditionally begin on the first or second day the sun rises near the end of the austral winter"; the first annual WINFLY, 1974, was three LC-130 ski flights. **SOURCED**, same.
- **Phoenix (deep compacted snow, from 2016-17).** Built partly through a winter: Table 15 of the report is titled "strength progression ... through the winter construction period into the spring." Method: compact the natural snow, bulldoze a 20 cm "Lift 2" from the runway edges, then compact every new snowfall into the pavement "on an interval determined by a combination of time since last compaction and environmental conditions." **SOURCED**, CRREL TR-19-4, §7. The design brief asked for "near-continuous wheeled aircraft between late August and late February" plus "the recent addition of monthly austral winter flights (April through July)," and "40 or more" C-17 missions a season. **SOURCED**, same, p. 2. On temperature: "unlike at Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station, only occasionally do austral winter temperatures dip below the safe operating temperature for typical USAP aircraft. Otherwise, the only concern related to temperature is for levels that are near or surpass the melting point." **SOURCED**, same, §2.5. Its first summer: not operated mid-November 2016 to end of January 2017 because of warmth, "with the weight cart being used twice a week to compact the runway"; flights resumed once it again carried "the fully loaded (73,000 kg, or 160,000 lb) weight cart" without rutting. **SOURCED**, same, summary. The ongoing rule: work "new snow accumulations into the pavement structure as quickly as possible by compaction to ensure that weak layers do not have a chance to form." **SOURCED**, same.
- **Current McMurdo maintenance standard (all seasons).** "Preparation and maintenance generally include grading, chaining, and rolling"; "Runways will be groomed frequently - often daily. Skiways are graded and groomed often daily and always immediately after new snow accumulation"; Phoenix may carry "up to one-inch depth of loose snow"; airfield checks "on a daily basis and after any event (heavy snowfall, numerous aircraft operations, high winds, etc.)." **SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §§1.15.2-1.15.5. In winter, the runway condition report is made "by the NSF's Prime Contractor Fleet Operations personnel at the end of the duty day prior to a scheduled flight," and redone if weather changes. **SOURCED**, §1.9.3. Before the 2025 medevac "The United States Antarctic Program Winter Team created the landing surface by grooming the ice." **SOURCED**, NZDF 2025. Phoenix is re-certified by the US Air Force "every November." **SOURCED**, §1.3.4.1.
- **Winter staff numbers at the airfield: NOT FOUND.** The total McMurdo winter population is about 150-200 (153 in 2009, 198 in 2010; **SOURCED**, Antarctic Sun), but no source gives how many of them keep the airfield.

### 4b. Staffing and equipment rules from the glacial-runway engineering record
- **Staffing (Pegasus, CRREL Monograph 98-1):** "For the Pegasus facility, we recommend two full-time maintenance personnel with intimate knowledge of the site. There should also be access to one or two other operators who can be available when storms and drifting snow occur. The maintenance staff should also include a half-time mechanic/fabricator," plus a site supervisor; "when the Pegasus runway required a large effort, as did many other facilities in the McMurdo area ... personnel who might normally be available to assist us were frequently occupied with other important tasks." **SOURCED**, Blaisdell et al., *Construction, Maintenance, and Operation of a Glacial Runway, McMurdo Station, Antarctica*, CRREL Monograph 98-1 (1998), ch. 5, p. 65 (read on coldregionsresearch.tpub.com, a reproduction of the public-domain report; two-column text re-ordered by me).
- **Snow management is the main job:** "Snow management will likely be the most important maintenance issue associated with the majority of glacial ice runways." To shed snow, "drag and plane the runway nearly continuously to break up drift pods as they formed"; to add snow cover, cut windrows across the wind, then level and roll with "a pneumatic-tire roller" (35-50 tons), compacting in layers of "no more than 10 cm"; "on a day with steady winds carrying considerable snow building up over a half meter of snow was possible in a 24-hour period." Runway markers and flag lines collect drifts and "should be kept to an absolute minimum"; a scarp can grow a drift "as much a 10 times the height of the scarp." **SOURCED**, same, pp. 65-69.
- **Equipment named:** graders, windrow drags, snow planes (small, medium, and a 12 m long-base plane), a 45-metric-ton pneumatic-tire roller, I-beam drags, hand shovels around markers. **SOURCED**, same, figures 68-73.
- **Surface rules:** a thin snow wearing surface "should never exceed a depth of 6 cm to avoid overstressing landing gear"; drag or plane it "once a day or after every aircraft operation"; friction of "at least 0.25 will be present on any glacial ice runway surface when the temperature is less than −10°C. Concern for low levels of friction should arise at temperatures warmer than −5°C"; patching of cracks "is best performed when the air and ice are cold (less than −10°C)." **SOURCED**, same, pp. 69-72. (Minus signs restored: the reproduction dropped them; the context, "warmer than," makes the sign clear. **DERIVED** correction.)
- **Surface definition (seeing the runway):** grader-cut ice was so well defined that "On clear days, pilots reported picking out the Pegasus runway from over 100 km away"; a roughened snow cover was "adequate for comfortable landings" "in all but the worst lighting conditions." **SOURCED**, same, p. 70.

### 4c. Russian ice and snow runways: winter versus summer
- **Novo (blue ice, 3,000 × 60 m, 550 m elevation, 7 km from the Schirmacher oasis):** "As a rule, this aerodrome is operated during the RAE's seasonal operations, from November to March. The main problem in preparing the runway after the winter season is partial clearing of the snow accumulated on the ice surface over the winter, and its partial compaction to create a dense protective snow layer on the ice surface. This work is done with a pneumatic-wheeled roller." In summer, sun on the low-albedo ice causes "melting of the snow-ice cover and the appearance of slush, and sometimes small lakes on the runway surface, and, as a consequence, the need to close the aerodrome in the warmest period of the year (January)." The ice is harder than 1.2 MPa "even at temperatures close to the phase-transition temperature." **SOURCED**, S.P. Polyakov, V.L. Mart'yanov, V.V. Lukin (AARI), "Snow-ice runways of the Russian Antarctic Expedition: features of preparation and development prospects," *Rossiyskie polyarnye issledovaniya* No. 2 (20), 2015, pp. 31-35, https://data.aari.ru/publicat/rpr/sources/20/RPI-20-2015-02-31-35.pdf (PDF downloaded and read; translations mine). In 2022 "The seasonal runway started working on October 26." **SOURCED**, Interfax, 11 Nov 2022. AARI's director said runway maintenance continues through winter to prepare for the next season. **UNVERIFIED** (search-result text of a 2023 report; not opened).
- **Novo's ice moves:** a 2024 study of the runway glacier over 826 days of monitoring (covering "not only the warm summer time but also the cold winter season") measured strain along the runway axis from −0.023 to +0.023 per year. **SOURCED**, *Led i Sneg* 2024 No. 3, pp. 387-402 ("New data on the structure and movement of the glacier in the area of the Novolazarevskaya landing site"), PDF via pureportal.spbu.ru (read in part; translation mine). Crevassing and changes to the runway's shape from uneven glacier flow are the subject of a 2022 *Led i Sneg* paper (**UNVERIFIED**, title and keywords only; ice-snow.igras.ru 403).
- **Progress, Vostok and Mirny (2015 status, before Zenit):** "On the airfields of Vostok, Progress and Mirny, special machinery only levels the snow surface of the runway and marks it"; all three were then ski-only. Vostok's runway (3,000 × 60 m) "is operated in the summer season from November to February." **SOURCED**, Polyakov, Mart'yanov and Lukin 2015.
- **Molodyozhnaya:** a skiway near the base was used for refueling stops from 2008 to 2014, but "given the absence of airfield machinery to maintain the runway, its surface gradually became uneven and unsafe," and it was moved in January 2015. **SOURCED**, same. *This is the sourced failure mode for an unmaintained snow strip.*

### 4f. What item 4 adds up to (facts only)
- On the record found, **winter does not damage ice and compacted-snow runways; summer does.** Pegasus ran year-round until 2012 and failed from summer melt; Phoenix was built through a winter and closes in midsummer; Novo closes in January; Zenit's slab got harder over its first winter. (**SOURCED** pieces; summary **DERIVED**.)
- **What winter does bring is snow.** Every source describes winter as the season snow accumulates on the runway (Pegasus, Novo, Wilkins), and the work needed to reopen ranges from "a few days" (Pegasus 1996, for one flight) to "two months" (Wilkins, full season start-up). Keeping a runway usable during winter means continuous drift control, which the engineering record says takes a small dedicated crew (two full-time plus storm help, at Pegasus) with graders, drags, planes and a heavy roller. (**SOURCED** pieces; summary **DERIVED**.)
- **Sea-ice runways are spring runways only** in every case found.

Savatyugin (AARI), "On the 60th anniversary of the First KAE," *Rossiyskie polyarnye issledovaniya* No. 1 (23), 2016, pp. 42-44, https://data.aari.ru/publicat/rpr/sources/23/RPI-23-2016-01-42-44.pdf, read; translation mine); later Il-14 (see 5c).
- **How the US handles crosswind on ski fields:** Williams Field (McMurdo) has a main skiway 25/07 and a "crosswind skiway" 33/15, each 220 × 10,000 ft (**SOURCED**, USAP AOM 2026 §1.3.3). Pegasus needed only one runway because "the prevailing winds rarely exceeded the allowable crosswind speed for the aircraft USAP operate" and strong winds came from one direction (**SOURCED**, CRREL TR-19-4, §2.4, siting).

### 5b. Mirny's airfields: history
**Already known, not repeated:** the 1956 ski operations; the Il-18 landings of 1961-62 and 1963-64 on prepared snow; air service ending around 1990-91 (Popov 2019) or closure in 1990 for cracks (Russian Wikipedia); the 2013-16 survey, the 10 Feb 2016 BT-67 landing, the 1.3 km length, slope, ice speed (6-80 m/yr) and crevasses; no old-runway location found.

New in this file:
- **The planned strip, 2015 (AARI):** after aerial photography from a Ka-32 helicopter and ground-penetrating radar to check for "internal cracks or cavities," the chosen strip was marked at **1,500 m**, centered at **66°34′08″S, 93°01′22″E**; it "is intended for medium-class aircraft on ski undercarriage" and "can be operated during the whole period of aircraft flights in the Antarctic, i.e. from October to March." The reason for the careful siting: "the glaciers in the area of Mirny station are extremely dangerous because of the considerable number of deep and not always visible crevasses, which has already led to tragic consequences." Monitoring duty: "the meteorologist's duties now include monthly measurements of the precise geodetic coordinates of all the stakes installed on the aerodrome territory, and its snow survey." **SOURCED**, Polyakov, Mart'yanov and Lukin 2015 (item 4c), pp. 33-34 (translations mine). *Note:* the 2019 paper reports the strip in use as ending at 1,300 m (already known); the two lengths are recorded as found.
- **Winter care of the strip, 2016:** "By the beginning of the 62nd RAE summer field season, the Mirny landing strip had been marked and rolled by the wintering crew. The completed signage included basic visual marking (runway centerline, runway shoulders, threshold), flight operator deck, and a wind cone." Through the 2016 winter, stakes were measured "once a quarter" with a handheld GPS; two 6 m stakes broke at the base in the wind and four lost their tops; four more "were lost in the zone of crevasses." **SOURCED**, Popov, Novikov, Belkov, Kashkevich, Tyurin, Mart'yanov and Lukin, *Earth's Cryosphere* XXIII(1), 2019, pp. 69-70 (English PDF, https://pureportal.spbu.ru/files/52258533/Popov19_Ice_sheet_dynamics_and_structure_in_the_area_of_ice_runway_at_Mirny_Station_East_Antarctica.pdf, read). The strip was then used "throughout the field season" for airborne geophysics (**SOURCED**, same).
- **Runway heading (orientation) of the present strip: NOT FOUND** in the 2015 article, the 2019 English paper or the 2019 Russian companion paper (*Kriosfera Zemli* XXIII(1): 80-90, also read for this). The 1956 airfield's location and heading: **NOT FOUND** (as before).
- **Soviet Il-14 accidents linked to Mirny** (from a tertiary list): 26 January 1989, an Avia 14 "burned out at [Mirny Ice Station] while being refueled," killing three technicians; **12 September 1990**, an Il-14 "belly landed on a glacier 221 mi from Mirny Ice Station" after an engine shutdown, no injuries. **SOURCED at Wikipedia** ("List of accidents and incidents involving the Ilyushin Il-14"); the primary records (aviation-safety.net) refused (403/DNS failure).

### 5c. Any record of winter flights at Mirny
- **1956, first Soviet expedition:** "In the Davis Sea and adjacent areas of the Indian Ocean, regular aerial reconnaissance was carried out year-round, which gave the first information on the ice regime of this sea and, in particular, on the extreme northern position of the sea-ice limit in winter between 80 and 100°E." **SOURCED**, Sokratova and Savatyugin 2016 (AARI), p. 44 (translation mine). *This is the only explicit statement found that aircraft flew from Mirny in winter.* Aircraft types, months and number of winter flights are not given (**NOT FOUND**).
- **September 1990:** the Il-14 belly landing 221 miles from Mirny on 12 September 1990 (5b) implies flying from Mirny in late winter that year. (**DERIVED** from a tertiary-sourced date; the flight's origin is not stated.)
- **After 1991:** no winter flight at Mirny found. The present strip's stated operating period is October to March (AARI 2015). The 2016 wintering crew prepared the strip but no winter landing is reported. (**SOURCED** absences in the papers read; "none found" is a search result, not proof.)

### 5d. Mirny's wind direction and crosswind exposure
- **Direction (already known, ocean file):** READER mean wind direction at Mirny, 1987-2026, 116-136° in every month (January 118°, June 129°): from the east-southeast to southeast. AARI's 2016 article on the first expedition describes "strong south-eastern winds" blowing snow off the Schirmacher oasis (a different station); for Mirny it gives no direction. A full wind rose (how often each direction blows) for Mirny: **NOT FOUND** (one more Russian search, logged).
- **Speed (already known):** monthly means 1991-2020 of 24.6-28.4 kt from March to September (READER, operations file); more than 200-220 days a year above 15 m/s (secondary).
- **Crosswind arithmetic (DERIVED, not a design):** a crosswind component is wind speed × sin(angle between wind and runway). At Mirny's winter monthly mean of about 26 kt, a wind 30° off a runway's line gives about 13 kt across it, 45° about 18 kt, 90° the full 26 kt. Set against the Twin Otter's demonstrated 20 kt (not a limit) and Skytraders' 5 kt jet landing criterion at Wilkins (2008), the sources do not let anyone say what share of winter days would be flyable at Mirny on any heading, because neither the strip's heading nor a direction-frequency table was found. **The sources do not support a crosswind-exposure figure for Mirny.**
- **How exposed the station is (already known):** AARI 2023: storms that confine staff indoors are "frequent" at Mirny; a news account records 90-120 km/h winds with visibility under 200 m in March.

## FINAL: what the sources support and do not support about winter air intake on this coast

**No recommendation.** Each line says what it rests on.

### Supported by the sources
1. **Jets and heavy aircraft have landed on Antarctic ice in every winter month,** but only at McMurdo, and outside August only as emergency medevacs or the 2015 trial: C-17 (on NVGs, since 2008), RNZAF C-130H/J (2021, 2025), A319 (April 2015, March 2020, July 2026). (Item 1.)
2. **Planned late-winter jet service exists in one place:** McMurdo's WinFly, five to seven C-17 flights each August, every season with recorded weather delays of one to four days, and with round-trip fuel or engines-running hot refueling. (Item 1.)
3. **Darkness is handled with known methods:** NVGs and unpowered retro-reflective markers (military), temporary battery edge and threshold lights (civil operators), permanent approach lights, REILs and an NVG-mode glide-path light at McMurdo, and satellite or TLS approaches with ordinary published minimums. On the Antarctic Circle itself (Mirny's latitude) the sun barely clears the horizon at midwinter but there are about six hours of civil twilight; the deep months-long darkness of McMurdo does not occur there. (Item 2; the light table is DERIVED.)
4. **The cold limits that bite in the sourced record are fuel and hydraulics,** not airframes as such: an A319 delays takeoff at a fuel temperature of −45 °C; Jet A-1 freezes at or below −47 °C; hydraulic fluid "starts to gel" around −58 °C (pilot statement); operators called −43 °C at McMurdo "right on the verge" of the A319's limits. Ground diesel needs an Antarctic blend (rated to −35 °C at Davis); small engines and unheated vehicles fail between −25 and −35 °C. (Item 3.)
5. **Winter itself does not ruin ice or compacted-snow runways; summer melt does.** Pegasus ran year-round until 2012; Phoenix was built through a winter; Novo and Wilkins close in midsummer. Winter's cost is snow: tens of thousands of cubic meters to clear after an unmaintained winter, or a standing crew grooming and rolling after every storm to keep one open. (Item 4.)
6. **Sea-ice runways are spring runways** (October to early December) in every case found. (Item 4.)
7. **Short-haul ski aircraft (Basler, Twin Otter) have the published range for every Mirny leg computed here,** carry 18-19 passengers in Australian configuration, and the Twin Otter has been flown to −69 °C in winter darkness. (Item 5.)
8. **Mirny has a ski strip (1.3-1.5 km) on a crevassed, fast-moving coastal glacier,** maintained by the wintering crew but stated to operate October to March. The only winter flying found from Mirny is 1956 year-round ice reconnaissance and a September 1990 Il-14 flight. (Item 5.)

### Not supported, or not found
- **No source found** of any aircraft landing in winter (May-September) at any station on this coast (Davis, Mirny, Progress, Casey/Wilkins), or at Novo or Troll.
- **No source found** of a scheduled winter passenger service anywhere in Antarctica; the McMurdo winter program is cargo-first, medevac-driven, and its continuation after 2016 was not established.
- **The sources do not support** any figure for how often a winter flight to this coast would be delayed or canceled (no long-run WinFly or medevac delay rate exists in what was read).
- **The sources do not support** a crosswind-exposure figure for Mirny (no runway heading; no direction-frequency table).
- **The sources do not support** a manufacturer's minimum ground-operating temperature for any jet type considered (A319/A320, C-17, Il-76).
