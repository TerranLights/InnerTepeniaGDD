### 1a. Population and origins, by census year

- **City census counts. SOURCED** (Wikipedia census table; 1911 Britannica): **1890: 10,723 · 1900: 30,470 · 1910:
  39,165 · 1920: 41,611 · 2020: 34,494** (the 2020 figure is the consolidated city-county, Butte-Silver Bow).
  *"the apparent jump in the 1980 census was due to the city's consolidation"*; *"In 1977, the city and county
  governments consolidated to form the sole entity of Butte-Silver Bow."* The population *"sank to a minimum around
  1990 and has stabilized since then."*
- **Silver Bow County. SOURCED** (Verdigris Project ep. 36, Dick Gibson, KBMF): *"In 1910, Silver Bow County counted
  56,848 people, and in 1920 the number was 60,313."*
- **The "100,000" peak. SOURCED as a claim, NOT as a count.** Wikipedia: *"Some sources say that Butte had a peak
  population of nearly 100,000 around 1920, but no documentation corroborates this."* Verdigris ep. 36: *"There also
  exists an official US Census estimate for 1917, which lists Butte (city) at 93,981"*; *"The 1917 city directory
  estimates 91,000 people in Butte, and for 1918 they say 93,000"*; the directories multiplied listed names by
  **2.25** because wives and children were not listed. Censuses *"typically undercounted some populations, such as
  the Chinese, prostitutes, and transients."* See Unresolved conflicts.
- **Foreign-born, 1900. SOURCED** (1911 Britannica, from the 1900 census): of 30,470 people, **10,210 foreign-born**,
  including **2,474 Irish, 1,518 English-Canadians, 1,505 English.** DERIVED: 10,210 / 30,470 = **33.5% foreign-born**;
  the three largest groups named = 5,497, about 54% of the foreign-born; the remaining **4,713** foreign-born
  (10,210 − 5,497) are not broken down in the source.
- **Foreign heritage, 1910. SOURCED (secondary)** (SAGE *Encyclopedia of Immigration*, ed. Bakken and Kindell, entry
  "Butte, Montana"; only the opening was readable): *"As of 1910, 70 percent of Butte's population claimed foreign
  heritage"*; between 1890 and 1930 Butte had *"the most ethnically diverse population in the intermountain West."*
  "Foreign heritage" here means foreign-born plus children of foreign-born (the usual census "foreign stock" sense);
  the entry does not define it, so the definition is UNVERIFIED.
- **Irish share. SOURCED (secondary, search summary of a Project MUSE excerpt and a second source; the MUSE PDF
  returned HTTP 403):** *"By 1900, 26 percent of the Butte population claimed Irish ancestry."* A third, popular
  source (IrishCentral via KPAX) says *"half"* — conflicting, not adopted. Today: Wikipedia, *"with over one-quarter
  of the city's population claiming Irish descent"* and *"the highest percentage of Irish Americans per capita of any
  city in the United States"* (stated "as of 2017"). The 1900 "26 percent" and the 2017 "one-quarter" are different
  measures (ancestry vs. self-reported descent) and should not be read as continuity without the underlying tables.
- **Where they came from. SOURCED** (Wikipedia): *"The mines attracted workers from Cornwall (England), Ireland, Wales,
  Lebanon, Canada, Finland, Austria,"* then *"Italy, China, Montenegro, Mexico, and more."* Serbians, Croatians and
  Lebanese are named as East Side residents by historicmt.org (below). **The "many nations" claim is supported as a
  list, not as counts:** a 1910 or 1920 country-of-birth table for Butte was **NOT FOUND** (search 1 died at the
  sources; the published 1910 census volume on foreign-born by city is the place to look).
- **Chinese. SOURCED** (Verdigris ep. 36): *"The census shows no more than 400 or so Chinese in Butte, but Rose Hum Lee
  estimates as many as 2,500 at the peak."* Wikipedia: Chinese immigration *"stopped in 1882 with the passage of the
  Chinese Exclusion Act"*; *"in 1895, the chamber of commerce and labor unions started a boycott of Chinese owned
  businesses"*; *"The history of the Chinese migrants in Butte is documented in the Mai Wah Museum."*

### 1b. Neighborhoods

- **SOURCED** (Wikipedia): *"the Irish of Dublin Gulch, the Eastern Europeans of the McQueen Addition, and the Italians
  of Meaderville"*; another passage names *"the Dublin Gulch (an enclave for Irish immigrants) and Corktown
  neighborhoods."* *"Historically, the St. Mary's section of Butte had a prominent population of Slavic and Finnish
  immigrants in addition to Irish before the mid-20th century."* Walkerville is a separate town on the hill, *"the
  one area excluded from the consolidation."*
- **The East Side. SOURCED** (historicmt.org East Side tour): *"Finntown is located along the upper streets, including
  East Broadway and East Park"*; *"Serbians and Lebanese (and a variety of other Southern European immigrants) resided
  on the lower East Side"*; *"Miners of other nationalities lived here in the 1890s."*
- **Finntown. SOURCED** (historicmt.org, Helsinki Bar and Steam Bath, 402 East Broadway): *"Twelve saloons, six
  grocery stores, eighteen boarding houses, a community hall, and three saunas"* served it in the 1920s; *"Finns were
  relative latecomers to Butte, arriving in numbers around 1910."* The building was a 1890s boarding house; *"By 1906,
  Hilda Nurmi managed the boarding house, the first of several Finnish proprietors"*; *"By 1911, the property boasted
  that treasured Finnish institution: the sauna"*; *"for twenty-five cents, Finns bathed, socialized, and renewed
  their connection to their homeland"*; *"In 1915, the baths were open from three to midnight, with Fridays reserved
  for ladies."* In the 1950s *"the Anaconda Company bought much of the Eastside, including Finn Town, for the
  Berkeley Pit and a buffer zone."* *"Today, the Helsinki Bar and Steam Bath stands alone."*
  **UNVERIFIED (search snippet of a Montana Standard article behind a paywall):** *"At its peak, Finn Town had a
  population of nearly 3,000"* and *"nearly 20 boarding houses."*
- **Finnish organizations. UNVERIFIED (search summary of the Butte-Silver Bow Public Archives' holdings):** the
  Finnish Worker's Club, the Finnish Worker's Drama Club, the Finnish American Athletics Club and the Finnish Hall
  Society.
- **Meaderville. SOURCED** (KXLF, 19 December 2018): *"largely Italian immigrants"*; a former resident: *"Everybody had
  vegetable gardens and gave one another stuff"* and *"The company bought the people out and they closed everything
  down"*; its volunteer firemen built a Christmas display in the 1940s–50s on the Butte–Helena highway, which ran
  through the neighborhood; a replica was rebuilt in 2018 by the Butte History Club with the Racetrack Volunteer Fire
  Department. **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** *"all of Meaderville was lost to the Pit by the early 1970s – at
  least 488 buildings were gone."*

### 1c. Boarding houses, saloons, halls, churches, mutual aid, press

- **Boarding houses. SOURCED** (Verdigris ep. 165, the Florence): *"three hundred rooms"*; its new manager *"bought
  six hundred new mattresses, putting two single beds in each room"*; a bake house with a 12-foot oven served *"as
  many as four hundred diners at each sitting"*; board and bed **$8 to $12 a week**; *"85 percent of the guests were
  Irish"* even though the Florence stood inside Finntown (Verdigris: *"The Florence sat squarely within Finntown"*,
  search 3 summary). Staff pay rose from *"$30/month for a twelve-hour shift"* to *"$35 a month for eight hours a
  day."* **UNVERIFIED (search summary of an oral-history page that did not load):** *"By 1916 Butte had 53 such
  houses. The boarding houses served an international clientele though Irish, Serbian and Finnish miners dominated
  the East side."* Beds shared by alternating shifts: **NOT FOUND** in the pages read.
- **Union halls as social buildings. SOURCED** (Verdigris "Life Underground" ep. 16): about **400** Walkerville miners
  formed the Butte Workingmen's Union at the Orphean Hall in 1878; **800 members (1881), 1,800 (1885)**; a new hall
  of 1882 *"collapsed before its opening ball"*; a Miners' Union hall on North Main was finished late 1885 and
  destroyed by about **25 dynamite blasts in June 1914**. Carpenters Hall (1906) held dances (a 1916 Harmony Dancing
  Club, a 1935 New Year's Eve frolic), a 1924 Woodmen of the World anniversary, a 1929 wrestling match, card parties
  for defense committees, speakers, and weekly meetings of the trades (Carpenters five a week; Machinists Thursdays;
  Ironworkers Mondays; Streetcar Men first and third Wednesdays); *"The Women's Protective Union kept offices in the
  Hall for decades"*; it was abandoned in the mid-1970s and saved from demolition by a lawsuit after 2012.
- **Miners' Union Day. SOURCED** (Verdigris ep. 40): the union was formed *"to further the goals of the strike on June
  13, 1878"*; June 13 became Miners' Union Day *"ever afterward"*; during the 1878 strike *"merchants helped establish
  a fund for out-of-work miners"* and extended credit. **UNVERIFIED (search summary of a 1911 socialist magazine
  account):** a parade of about ten thousand workers, then an open-air meeting at Columbia Gardens. Today the World
  Museum of Mining hosts the day (KPAX, search summary). Union sick, accident and death benefits: **NOT FOUND.**
- **Mass funerals. SOURCED** (History of Butte, Wikipedia): *"A funeral procession of 3,000 people took place for the
  deceased on January 18, 1895"*; which event the dead belonged to is not stated in the excerpt returned (UNVERIFIED;
  probably the January 1895 explosion, not checked). The same article: *"The BMU not only sent thousands of dollars to
  support the Idaho miners, they mortgaged their buildings to send more"* (solidarity outside the town).
- **Irish societies. SOURCED (thin):** the AOH archive lists *"the Ancient Order of Hibernians (Divisions 1, 2, & 3)"*
  in Butte, records 1882–1935 at the University of Montana's Mansfield Library. **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** three
  AOH divisions organized in 1880, membership limited to men born in Ireland and their sons; the Robert Emmet Literary
  Association was the Butte branch of Clan-na-Gael, founded September 1881 by 42 Butte Irishmen (one podcast gives
  1848: conflict, not adopted). Wikipedia: in 1923 *"protestors attempted to blow up the Hibernian Hall on Main
  Street with dynamite."*
- **Serbian lodges and church. SOURCED** (UM ScholarWorks, interview of 22 February 2003): *"the different Serbian
  lodges in Butte, how membership was determined by what part of Serbia you were originally from"*; *"the Serbian
  church in Butte, was the only one in the Northwest for much of early 20th century"*; customs *"slavas and kums."*
  Tagged lodges: Prosveta, Montenegro, Srpsko Jedinstvo, Serbian Benevolent Society, Bokelian Brotherhood; Circle of
  Serbian Sisters. **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** Holy Trinity consecrated 15 June 1905; first services 1897 for 35
  families. A Butte IWO lodge closed over fears it was Communist-run (search summary of a second interview).
- **Foreign-language press. UNVERIFIED (search summary of a Montana Historical Society page):** *"dozens of
  foreign-language and ethnically affiliated papers, including German, Swedish, Serbian, Croatian, Irish American and
  African American"* flourished in Montana in the early twentieth century; the Butte archives hold scattered copies of
  Croatian, Finnish and Serbian papers. Titles and circulations: **NOT FOUND.**

### 1d. Work and disaster: the Granite Mountain / Speculator fire, 8 June 1917

- **SOURCED** (Wikipedia, Speculator Mine disaster): a sprinkler-system cable's oil-soaked insulation was ignited by a
  foreman's carbide lamp; *"415 men were working, mostly as isolated pairs or clusters, in Granite Mountain and
  Speculator."* Of 29 men behind Manus Duggan's bulkhead, *"25 were rescued and survived"*; a bulkhead on the 2600
  level held 19 men, all found dead; six men of J.D. Moore's group on the 2200 level were rescued; some were found
  *"after as long as 55 hours."* *"many were freshly hired Eastern-European immigrants who could not read English."*
  The Metal Mine Workers Union formed *"less than two weeks after"* the fire.
- **Death toll. SOURCED with an internal conflict:** **168** (Wikipedia lead; Clio; Butte News; Butte Wikipedia) vs.
  *"150 of the 163 dead showed signs of carbon monoxide poisoning"* (same Wikipedia article, body). mtgenweb says
  *"more than 160."* Victims' countries of birth: **NOT FOUND.**
- **Memorial. SOURCED** (Clio; Butte News 2022): an open-air plaza at the top of North Main past St. Lawrence Church;
  *"opened in 1996 as a result of the work of Gerry Walter, a VISTA volunteer"*; dedicated **8 June 1996**;
  rededicated **2010**; funded by the EPA, ARCO, the Butte-Silver Bow government and residents. **UNVERIFIED (search
  summary):** the names of the 168 are etched in stone; J.D. Moore's note *"Tell her we done the best we could, but
  the cards were against us."*
- **Annual remembrance. SOURCED** (Butte News, 2022): the 105th anniversary service on **11 June 2022, 2:00 pm**,
  organized by *"The Granite Mountain Board"*; *"the original whistle from the Granite Mountain Mine"* is blown, with a
  flag raising and a short program. A Wikipedia reference title reads *"Hundreds attend Granite Mountain memorial
  service"* (attendance count UNVERIFIED).

### 1f. The open pit swallows neighborhoods: what happened to the people

- **SOURCED** (Berkeley Pit, Wikipedia): open-pit mining *"began in July 1955"*; Meaderville, McQueen, East Butte,
  Dublin Gulch and Finntown were *"consumed by land purchases to expand the pit eastward during the 1970s"*; homes were
  destroyed or moved to southern Butte; residents were compensated *"to some extent for their acquired property."*
  Butte Wikipedia: *"Thousands of homes were destroyed in the Meaderville suburb and surrounding areas, McQueen and
  East Butte"*; *"The expansion of the Anaconda Company in the 1960s and 1970s eradicated some of Butte's historic
  neighborhoods, including the East Side, Dublin Gulch, Meaderville, and Chinatown"*; the pit *"began encroaching on the
  Columbia Gardens"* (the company's amusement park). **UNVERIFIED (search summary of an hmdb marker):** many
  relocated residents *"didn't mind because it meant good-paying jobs would stay in the community."*
- **Count of households displaced: NOT FOUND.**

### 1g. Mining shrinks but continues

- **SOURCED** (History of Butte; Berkeley Pit, Wikipedia): the company moved away from underground mining from the
  1950s; *"In 1982, all mining in the Berkeley Pit was suspended"*; the Kelley Mine pumps *"3,800 ft (1,200 m) below the
  surface"* were turned off and groundwater began filling the pit; *"Anaconda ceased mining at the Continental Pit in
  1983"*; *"Montana Resources LLP bought the property and reopened the Continental Pit in 1986"* (a 1986 newspaper
  headline cited by Wikipedia: *"Butte Copper Pit Reopens, with Nonunion Miners"*); mining stopped in July 2000 for
  energy costs and resumed in November 2003. The pit was listed under Superfund (1983 in the infobox; *"1987"* in the
  body: conflict noted); a water-treatment plant has run since October 2019. Montana Resources' current workforce: see
  item 5d.

### 2a. Population and origins

- **SOURCED** (Greater Sudbury, Wikipedia): 2021 census, **166,004** (city), **170,605** (metropolitan area); **155,219**
  at the 2001 amalgamation. 2016 ethnic origins (multiple responses allowed): **French 37.7%, English 20.0%, Irish
  19.1%, Scottish 15.8%, Italian 8.5%, German 8.1%, First Nations 6.9%, Ukrainian 4.7%, Finnish 4.6%.** Visible
  minorities **6.6%** (national 26.5%); Indigenous **11%**; French mother tongue **22.6%**.
- **History of the mix. SOURCED** (Nangia 2009, citing Thomson 1993, Saarinen 1993 and 1999, Wallace 1993): town
  population **1,000 (1891) → 2,027 (1901) → 8,621 (1921) → 18,518 (1931) → 42,410 (1951) → 80,120 (1961) → 90,515
  (1971)**. 1901 census: *"55.2 percent of the Sudburians were of British and 35.6 percent French origin, 3.6 percent
  were Jewish, 2.4 percent Italians, and 1.9 percent Germans."* The early town had a *"'French Town' on the northeast
  and wealthier Anglo-Saxon community in the southeast."* *"Among the non-French/English ethnic groups in the town of
  Sudbury, Finns constituted the largest group in the 1930s, with about 7 percent population. By 1941, Ukrainians,
  who had started coming in 1896 ... emerged as the largest ethnic group, and by 1951, Italians reached the second
  place. In the 1960s, Germans moved to the third position surpassing the Finns."* By 1961 French and British were
  each about one-third; *"Italians 8 percent, Ukrainians 5 percent, and Germans and Finnish about 4 percent, each."*
  In 1951, *"of the 14,142 men in the labor force more than 8000 worked in mining and smelting."* DERIVED: 8,000 /
  14,142 = **at least 57%** of employed men.
- **Where groups lived. SOURCED** (Nangia, citing Wallace 1993b): *"the Flour Mill, Hanmer, Chelmsford, Belfour, and
  Rayside were predominantly French; Copper Cliff, Gatchell, and Robinson Lake were mainly Italian; and Capreol was
  largely English. Finns were concentrated in the west and northwest of Sudbury as well as in Copper Cliff, whereas
  British were spread throughout the region."*
- **Immigrants today. SOURCED** (Nangia, 2006 census): only **6.7%** of Greater Sudbury's population were immigrants
  (Ontario 28.5%, Toronto 46.4%); *"Nearly half of immigrants in Greater Sudbury came before 1961 and only one-third
  since 1971"*; immigrants from Europe fell from **90.5% (1961)** to **19.5% (2001)** of arrivals, from Asia rose from
  **3.2% to 58.2%**. (Many-origins mining-era stock now mostly locally born; new arrivals few.)
- **Sudbury's Finns. SOURCED** (WLU Press; Saarinen 1999): Finns in the area *"since 1883"*; they were agricultural
  settlers, labor reformers and builders of *"churches, halls, saunas, and athletic fields"*; the book's settlement
  areas: Copper Cliff, the company towns of Garson and Creighton, Sudbury's Downtown, Donovan and Lockerby, rural
  township enclaves, and the Lake Panache recreational zone; a part titled "The Great Divide" treats political
  divisions. **UNVERIFIED (search summary of a review):** *"bitter divisions between communists, socialists, and
  conservatives, and between Lutherans."* **UNVERIFIED (search summary of the Laurentian University archives finding
  list):** records of the Finnish National Society of Sudbury (*Sudburyn Suomalainen Kansallisseura*) 1930–1991, the
  Knights and Ladies of Kaleva lodges and the Sudbury Finnish Rest Home Society.

### 2b. Saunas, halls, clubs, churches

- **Saunas. SOURCED** (Lapalme 2010, a local blog): before the Second World War *"a slew of public saunas were in
  operation,"* named as the Copper Cliff Finnish Baths (Jaakkola's), the Sudbury Steam Bath (Sepällä's) and Alavo Steam
  Baths; Finntown businesses *"sought the business of generally young single males"*; the sauna *"remains the surefire
  symbol of Finnish culture in the Sudbury area,"* with saunas still on the Lake Panache shoreline. Count, dates and
  prices: **NOT FOUND.**
- **Copper Cliff, the company town. SOURCED** (Wikipedia): founded *"as a company town by the Canadian Copper Company
  in the 1890s"*; *"early residential development coincided with strict segregation by class and nationality"*; the
  immigrant area was called *"Shantytown"* for its shacks; *"Little Italy arose from the creation of a smelter by Inco
  leading to an influx of Italian immigrants"*, *"reinforced by an Italian church, and Italian stores and services"*;
  at incorporation in 1901 Copper Cliff was larger than the town of Sudbury; about **4,000** people at annexation; on
  **31 December 1972** residents held *"a ceremony ... to mourn the loss of the town"* (annexation). In the Second World
  War Italian Canadians in Sudbury were *"considered "enemy aliens" and put into work camps."* The Superstack (1972) is
  described as *"a symbol of the workers' importance and identity to the community."*
- **The Italian Club. SOURCED (thin)** (CTV, 22 October 2022): *"After nearly 90 years the Italian Club is closing it's
  doors"* (yard sale to clear the building). **UNVERIFIED (search summary):** it began as the Italian Ladies Society of
  Copper Cliff and was *"the core of the Italian community in Copper Cliff"*; a 1921 Methodist mission on Lombardy
  Street served Italians, later St. Stanislaus on Balsam Street. Founding year: DERIVED, about 1932–33 (2022 − "nearly
  90"), not stated.
- **Ukrainian halls. UNVERIFIED (search summary of Stacey Zembrzycki's oral history *According to Baba*; MUSE blocked
  the page):** Ukrainian miners spent free time in *"the parish halls, national homes, and labor temples that defined
  the ideologically factionalized Ukrainian community"*; by the late 1920s a pro-Soviet "progressive" faction met at the
  Ukrainian Labor-Farmer Temple Association hall and a conservative Catholic group stood apart; Inco relied on Catholic
  priests to influence its workforce.

### 2c. Employers: Inco and Falconbridge (people side; power side is T8)

- **SOURCED** (Greater Sudbury, Wikipedia): Inco employed about a quarter of the local workforce in the 1970s. As of
  November 2010, **Vale 3,999** staff and **Xstrata (Falconbridge's successor, now Glencore) 1,139**; mining about
  **6,000** jobs; the mining supply and service sector about **10,000** more; **Health Sciences North 3,700** and

### 5a. Institutions that bridged groups, and those that kept them apart

**Bridged (as the sources state):**
- **The union and its hall.** Butte: the union hall *"usually served as the camp's social center"*; unions *"set up funds
  that insured the care of the sick and the injured, paying to hire doctors or to build hospitals"* and *"paid for
  funerals for members killed in the mines and comforted their widows and children"* (Everett). Carpenters Hall hosted
  dances and every trade's weekly meeting (1c). Lead: the miners' union was chartered in 1880 as a *"benevolent and
  charitable organization"*; by 1890 it paid a weekly sickness benefit and **$75** funeral benefit on dues of **$1 a
  month** (Deadwood History). Iron Range: *"workplace organization"* was one of the shared experiences that *"created a
  new, interethnic identity"* (MNopedia). Calumet: the Christmas party at which 73 died was the union women's auxiliary's
  party for strikers' families of several origins (4g).
- **The mine itself, below ground.** Butte (a daughter's memoir, not a study): *"But 'below ground' there were no
  distinctions"*; *"Everyone knew that when trouble came, we were all family"* (Miller 2016).
- **Company welfare after strikes.** Iron Range: after the failed 1916 strike, mining companies' welfare programs are
  credited by MNopedia with breaking down ethnic distinctions.
- **Schools, libraries, sports.** Iron Range: *"Public schools and libraries were especially important to
  Americanization efforts"*; communities fielded *"excellent athletic teams"* (MNopedia). Genk: the Waterschei and
  Winterslag clubs merged into KRC Genk in 1988 (UNVERIFIED, 4e). Butte: a 2004 University of Montana thesis argues
  mining-league baseball worked as both assimilation and ethnic preservation (search 32 summary; PDF HTTP 403;
  UNVERIFIED).
- **A shared worker identity, from the second generation.** Limburg (Beyers 2004): the rise in standing of
  second- and third-generation Poles and Italians *"is vooral toe te schrijven aan de geleidelijke ontwikkeling van
  collectieve arbeidersidentiteiten tussen immigranten van de tweede generatie en gevestigden uit de arbeidersklasse"*
  (is mainly due to the gradual development of collective worker identities between second-generation immigrants and
  established working-class residents); the mine company gave migrants' sons *"ongeveer dezelfde promotiekansen als
  Belgische mijnwerkerszonen in de ondergrond, in de verenigingen en op het vlak van huisvesting"* (about the same
  promotion chances as Belgian miners' sons underground, in the associations and in housing); a latent shared company
  identity *"bevorderde ... vooral de vreedzame interetnische omgang in de buurt"* (fostered mainly peaceful interethnic
  relations in the neighborhood); and *"Herkenningspunten tussen vrouwen in verband met de opvoeding van de kinderen of
  het huishouden versoepelden de interetnische omgang"* (common ground among women over child-raising and housekeeping
  eased interethnic relations), without deeply eroding ethnic distinctions.
- **A Norilsk-wide "work community."** Norilsk (Nedoseka et al. 2026): residents form a *"сообщество работы"* (work
  community) in which *"профессиональная и корпоративная идентичности являются доминантными"* (professional and
  corporate identities are dominant).

**Kept apart (as the sources state):**
- **Lodges by origin, even within one nation.** Butte: Serbian lodges where *"membership was determined by what part of
  Serbia you were originally from"* (UM oral history). Butte AOH divisions limited to Irish-born men and their sons
  (UNVERIFIED).
- **Separate churches.** Iron Range: *"Each immigrant group had its own community life, and tensions existed between
  groups"*; separate Catholic churches for Irish, Slovenian and Croatian, and Italian congregations (MNopedia).
- **Company housing by class and nation.** Copper Cliff: *"strict segregation by class and nationality"* (Wikipedia).
  Limburg cités (Beyers): a person's job *"bepaalde in welke straat hij met zijn gezin kon gaan wonen, alsook welke
  verenigingen voor hem en zijn gezinsleden bestemd waren"* (determined which street he and his family could live in, and
  which associations were meant for them); immigrants were recruited only for underground work and lived in miners'
  streets, where *"een segregatie naar nationale herkomst, die evenwel fluctueerde"* (a segregation by national origin,
  which however fluctuated) appeared; the Zwartberg mine *"kwam deels tegemoet aan de Limburgse xenofobie door Belgische
  mijnwerkersgezinnen in de mijncité te scheiden van allochtone nieuwkomers"* (partly accommodated local xenophobia by
  separating Belgian miners' families from foreign newcomers in the cité); toward the first generation the established
  miners behaved *"ontwijkend of 'passief intolerant'"* (evasively or "passively intolerant"), while first-generation Poles
  and Italians *"hechtten zich in de eerste plaats aan hun eigen landgenoten"* (attached themselves first to their own
  compatriots); the Christian union and pillar saw immigrants until the late 1950s as labor-market competitors and a
  threat to morality and church life; company paternalism confirmed migrants' *"ondergeschikte positie"* (subordinate
  position): no foreman jobs, no surface jobs.
- **Each nationality its own saloon.** Rock Springs: *"Each nationality had its own saloon or meeting place"*; the
  company *"hired workers from all over the world to keep the unions weak"* (WyoHistory).
- **Politics inside one origin group.** Sudbury Finns divided between communists, socialists and conservatives, and among
  Lutherans (UNVERIFIED); Sudbury Ukrainians split into a pro-Soviet labor-temple faction and a Catholic faction
  (UNVERIFIED); Iron Range Finns faced *"No Finns or Indians allowed"* signs and were *"not church people as a rule"*
  (KAXE).
- **Ethnic lines inside the union.** Butte: *"Disputes along ethnic lines that pitted Cornish miners against Irish
  against Finns"* weakened solidarity; unskilled immigrants got the most dangerous tasks (Everett).
- **Job competition turned violent.** Rock Springs 1885 and Kalgoorlie-Boulder 1934 (4d, 3a): Pascoe and Bertola place
  the 1934 riot in a *"wider social context"* in which the rioters were *"descendants of English colonists"* and the
  targets Southern Europeans.
- **Residents versus rotation workers.** Norilsk (Nedoseka et al. 2026): the image of the *«вахтовик»* (rotation
  worker) is mostly negative; posts on it are the most-commented in the city group "Типичный Норильск" (Typical
  Norilsk); *"По мнению большинства представителей принимающего сообщества, вахтовикам не место в городе"* (in the
  view of most of the host community, rotation workers have no place in the city), though *"Норильск — это город
  приезжих"* (Norilsk is a city of newcomers); an interviewee: *"Вахтовик — это временный работник, он не вовлечен в
  коллектив"* (a rotation worker is a temporary worker, not part of the collective). Fort McMurray camps: about 10% of
  oil workers in five camps were recent immigrants or temporary foreign workers and about 5% women; camp staff, many
  women and people of color, are *"positioned differently"* (Dorow and Jean 2022, as summarized).
- **Intermarriage rates: NOT FOUND for any place.** Only qualitative statements: Iron Range immigrants *"married
  partners of the same nationality"* (MNopedia); a local paper said "mixed marriages" were once viewed with disgust (search
  33 summary, UNVERIFIED).

### 5b. Did a single local identity form, and when?

- **Iron Range. SOURCED** (MNopedia): *"As second and later generations gradually outnumbered their immigrant forbearers,
  the Iron Range shifted from an "immigrant" population to an "ethnic" one"*; *"Shared experiences created a new,
  interethnic identity."* Mass immigration *"ended at the close of the 1920s"* (the 1924 quota law; mechanization cut the
  labor force). **UNVERIFIED (search 33 summary of a *Star Tribune* article, HTTP 429):** the Range is *"a conglomerate
  of third- and fourth-generation immigrants from more than 40 countries"* lacking a single ethnic "glue."
- **Limburg mining cités. SOURCED** (Beyers 2004): Poles in the 1950s–60s and Italians in the 1970s–80s rose from
  "outsiders" to "established"; *"Polen en Italianen van de tweede en de derde generatie zijn in de mijncités
  ontegensprekelijk in aanzien gestegen"* (second- and third-generation Poles and Italians undeniably rose in standing).
  The turn came **in the second generation**, through a shared worker identity. Since 1958, schooling and associational
  life in the cités lagged other neighborhoods, which slowed the rise of part of the Italian second generation. VRT
  (2026): the Italian community *"groeide uit tot een vast onderdeel van de Limburgse identiteit"* (became a fixed part of
  Limburg identity).
- **Norilsk. SOURCED** (Nedoseka et al. 2026): residents describe the city as *«места не для всех»* ("a place not for
  everyone"); *«материк»* means everything outside Norilsk; a cited earlier study (Gonina 2015) found that *"Норильск —
  не родина, а рабочее место"* (Norilsk is not a homeland but a workplace); a post (37 likes): *"и у нас своя
  национальность (норильчанин)! Потому что слабаки уезжали на материк"* (and we have our own nationality,
  "Norilchanin"! Because the weak left for the mainland); average tenure at the plant *"более 15 лет"* (over 15 years)
  (*"приехал на 3 года, остался на 23"*, "came for 3 years, stayed 23"); the company offers subsidized mortgages *"на
  «материке»"* (on the mainland) after 10 years' work as retention "anchors"; *"Большая часть опрошенных нами
  информантов демонстрировала чёткие интенции на переезд по достижении пенсионного возраста"* (most informants clearly
  intended to move away on reaching pension age); average resident age about **35**. **So the local identity is strong
  and voiced as a "nationality," yet built on leaving at retirement.** The 1963 Soviet closure (entry by special
  invitation from the plant, party or city executive) and the 2001 re-closure are cited as roots of the city's apartness.
- **Sudbury: language shift. DERIVED** from Nangia's 2006 table: **12,325** residents had a non-official mother tongue
  (7.9%), but only **3,565** spoke a non-official language most often at home (2.3%) and **60** most often at work
  (0.1%). DERIVED ratio: home use / mother tongue = 3,565 / 12,325 = **29%**. Greater Sudbury reported **133** ethnic
  groups in 2006; *"For a large majority of people in Greater Sudbury ethnic and cultural identity is very important"*
  (Nangia citing Kauppi et al. 2004). French held: 25,500 speak it most at home (16.3%).
- **Kalgoorlie-Boulder.** Pascoe and Bertola's title names the rioters of 1934 *"second-generation 'Britishers'"*: a
  locally born identity that defined itself against newer Southern Europeans (abstract only; SOURCED as a title and
  abstract).
- **Butte.** St. Patrick's Day since 1882 and about 30,000 revelers; over one-quarter claiming Irish descent today
  (1a, 1h); *"Michigan was the most frequent state of birth among Butte's second-generation Irish"* (Michigan Tech),
  i.e., many second-generation Irish had already lived in another mining district. When "from Butte" outranked origin:
  **NOT FOUND.**
- **Rock Springs.** The "56 nationalities" slogan has been the town's identity for about a century (city site, search 29,
  UNVERIFIED); in 2025 a statue to the Chinese victims of 1885 *"joins a memorial to the other 55 nationalities"*
  (Michigan Public/NPR).
- **Fort McMurray. SOURCED** (RMWB 2025): 29.51% resident more than a decade, 23.39% more than 20 years; family is now
  the top reason to move there; permanent residents up 11.16% since 2021 while camp residents fell 22.15%. In 2010 *Grist*
  reported that few residents considered themselves permanent and the average age was 32; that the town had *fewer
  churches, social clubs, and veterans' groups* than most places its size; and that Suncor built the Community Leisure
  Center (rinks, curling, water park, library).
- **Generation at which the local name outranked the old-country origin, measured: NOT FOUND for any place.** The
  closest sourced statements are the Iron Range "second and later generations" line and Beyers's second-generation
  finding.

### 5d. When extraction shrinks to a secondary industry but continues

- **Butte. SOURCED:** Montana Resources: *"≈400"* employees, privately owned, open-pit copper and molybdenum, reserves
  *"projected to support mining through 2054"*; the company styles Butte *"the Mining City"*. In 2004 the mine had **351**
  employees (average pay and benefits about $42,800 plus profit sharing) after its 2000 shutdown had kept **5 of 327**;
  other employers then: NorthWestern Corp. about 500, Advanced Silicon Materials 280; the Touch America collapse
  (2002–03) cost several hundred jobs; a company vice president: *"we need to diversify"* (*Deseret News* 2004).
  Population fell from a claimed 100,000 era (unverified) to 34,494 (2020). Mining employment's share of today's
  Butte workforce: **NOT FOUND.**
- **Sudbury. SOURCED** (2c): mining about 6,000 jobs plus about 10,000 in mining supply and services; Health Sciences
  North 3,700; Laurentian 1,850; federal and provincial departments moved in after the 1980s crisis; the city serves a
  regional market of 550,000; SNOLAB (150 staff) sits inside a working mine; regreening became a long-running civic
  program employing 4,510 temporary workers to 2009.
- **Lead. SOURCED:** at the September 2000 announcement the workforce was to fall *"from the then current level of 366
  employees, to approximately 40"* (Homestake 10-Q); *"More than 330 employees will lose their jobs"*, after *"the 400
  laid off in 1998"*, in *"a community of 3,600"* (Minneapolis Fed, January 2001); town 2,982 in 2020; the lab employed
  about 130 (70 ex-Homestake) in 2013; the town passed an ordinance on vacant and abandoned homes; *"Lead learned that the
  era of being a company town was over."* DERIVED: 3,600 → 2,982 = −17%.
- **Mount Isa. SOURCED:** Glencore's underground copper closure (2025) was first said to cost about 1,200 jobs, revised
  to *"about 500-600 total job losses"*; *"about 90"* workers redeployed to George Fisher (zinc-lead) and *"a further
  70"* earmarked (*North West Weekly*, 26 February 2025); McKell Institute (May 2024): about **980** directly affected, a
  worst case of **2,000** jobs at risk, *"1 in 5 employed Mount Isa residents"*, population **19,047** (a later estimate
  than the 2021 census count of 18,317 in 4c; different measures). **UNVERIFIED
  (search 40 summaries):** the copper mine ceased on 31 July 2025; George Fisher's life of mine runs to 2036.
- **Rock Springs. SOURCED:** coal mines all closed in the mid-1950s when locomotives went diesel; trona mining opened
  near Green River; population **23,526** (2020), above its 1980 figure (Wikipedia; WyoHistory).
- **Iron Range. SOURCED:** taconite mining continues; *"an aging population, youth outmigration, and overall population
  decline"* (Wikipedia).
- **Limburg / Genk. SOURCED:** mines closed 1966 (Zwartberg), 1987 (Waterschei), 1988 (Winterslag), 1992 (Zolder, the
  last in Belgium); Ford Genk closed 18 December 2014; Genk now has 68,101 people, about 100 nationalities and about 54%
