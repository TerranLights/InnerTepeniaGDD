## 1. Casey site geology

### Location and landform
- Casey station is at 66°16′55″S, 110°31′36″E, "in an area of the low, rocky Windmill Islands and peninsulas," "perched on the edge of the massive Antarctic ice cap." The Windmill Islands contain "more than 50 islands." **SOURCED**, AAD Casey station page (antarctica.gov.au/antarctic-operations/stations/casey/).
- The station stands on the northern side of the Bailey Peninsula, overlooking Vincennes Bay, Budd Coast, Wilkes Land. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Casey Station" (secondary).
- Bailey Peninsula is "an area of rock exposures and permanent snow and ice fields lying between Newcomb Bay and O'Brien Bay, two kilometers south of Clark Peninsula." **SOURCED**, ASPA 135 Management Plan (North-east Bailey Peninsula), §6(i), PDF text extracted from env.go.jp copy.
- **Relief:** "Bailey Peninsula comprises low lying, rounded ice-free rocky outcrops (maximum altitude approximately 40 m), which rise from the coast to the Løken Moraines (altitude approximately 130 m). Intervening valleys are filled with permanent snow or ice, or glacial moraine and exfoliated debris, and contain water catchment areas." **SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan. *Reading:* the ground is a rounded rock-knob and valley landscape, not a plain. Lakes and ponds sit in bedrock hollows throughout the region (same source).
- The Windmill Islands group is about 31.5 km long along the coast and about 11.1 km wide. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Windmill Islands" (secondary, from USGS/GNIS-type gazetteer text).
- **Total ice-free area:** "The Windmill Islands and surrounding area constitute the largest ice-free area in Wilkes Land at 500 km²." **UNVERIFIED.** Search snippet only, attributed to a Springer chapter (rd.springer.com 10.1007/978-3-319-05497-1_6). The chapter page redirected to a Springer login (died at the source, not retried). The Wikipedia Windmill Islands article does not contain the figure. Treat the 500 km² as unconfirmed, and note that it would include islands and water-separated ground, not one contiguous block.
- **Distance to the ice sheet edge:** the ASPA plan places the Løken Moraines (the Law Dome ice margin) at the inland edge of the Bailey Peninsula and refers to "the over-snow access route to Law Dome, several kilometers to the south." **SOURCED** (wording only; no measured distance found). A Holocene study finds that "Law Dome has readvanced over part of the Windmill Islands since the culmination of the post-glacial retreat" (after c. 4000 yr BP). **SOURCED**, Goodwin abstract, Macquarie University research portal ("A mid to late Holocene readvance of the Law Dome ice margin, Budd Coast").
- **Map reading, AAD 1:50,000 "Windmill Islands" Maps 1 and 2 (Edition 6, August 2012; ice-free areas mapped from January 1994 aerial photography; 1 km grid).** **SOURCED** (PDFs downloaded from data.aad.gov.au and read as images; distances are read off the grid by eye, to about ±0.3 km):
  - **Bailey Peninsula** (station): ice-free ground is a patchwork of separate rock outcrops between permanent snowfields and lakes. It covers roughly 2.5–3 km east–west by about 1 km north–south. The largest single unbroken outcrop patches are a few hundred meters across.
  - **Clark Peninsula** (old Wilkes, to the north): a similar patchwork about 4 km east–west by 2 km, mostly inside ASPA 136.
  - **Mitchell Peninsula** (south of O'Brien Bay): mostly white (permanent snow or ice) with scattered rock patches; contours reach 60–120 m. It carries a marked "Mitchell Peninsula Field Landing Site," a line roughly 1.5 km long, drawn on snow.
  - **Ice-sheet edge:** the Løken Moraines lie about 3–4 km east of the station. Beyond them the ice-sheet contours climb from about 140 m to 200 m within about 2 km, roughly 3–4% by eye. An ice cliff and glacier tongue fill the head of O'Brien Bay.
  - **Result:** the maps show **no straight, contiguous ice-free line of 2.5–3 km** on the Bailey, Clark or Mitchell peninsulas. Every such line crosses snowfields, lakes, bays or moraines. This is a map reading, not a survey, and covers Maps 1 and 2 only. Maps 3–5 (the southern islands and Browning Peninsula) were not opened.
- **AAD map "Australian Antarctic Air Transport System, Map 2: Casey Runway location"** (February 2003, Landsat 1999 base). It shows exposed rock confined to the Windmill Islands coastal strip, with the runway site far to the southeast on the Law Dome flank, between Peterson Glacier and Vanderford Glacier. **SOURCED** (PDF read as an image).

### Bedrock: rock types and age
- Regional: the Windmill Islands are "one of the eastern-most outcrops of a Mesoproterozoic low-pressure granulite facies terrain," made of "migmatitic metapelites and metapsammites interlayered with mafic to ultramafic and felsic sequences ... undeformed granite, charnockite, gabbro, pegmatite, aplites and cut by easterly-trending late dolerite dykes." **SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan, Geology and soils.
- Metamorphic grade rises north to south: amphibolite facies at Clark Peninsula, through biotite-cordierite-almandine granulite, to hornblende-orthopyroxene granulite at Browning Peninsula. **SOURCED**, same.
- **Bailey Peninsula itself:** the main outcrop is a "leucocratic granite gneiss," specifically "garnet-bearing gneiss type 1 which is white, medium grained and foliated," with unmetamorphosed dolerite dykes and small outcrops of metapelite, metapsammite and leucogneiss. **SOURCED**, same.
- **Age:** two metamorphic phases, "c. 1400-1310 Ma" (upper amphibolite) and a granulite overprint "c. 1210-1180 Ma." **SOURCED**, same.
- **Weathering:** "The Ardery Charnockite of the south is prone to deep weathering and crumbles readily ... whereas the metamorphic sequences of the northerly parts of the region have a much more stable mineral assemblage and crystalline structure." **SOURCED**, same. *Reading for construction:* the station-area gneiss is the sound, stable rock; the charnockite to the south is the weak, crumbling one.
- Gravity modelling puts the dense intrusive Ardery Charnockite at 7–13 km depth. **UNVERIFIED** (search snippet of a Cambridge/Antarctic Science abstract; not opened).

### Glacial history, uplift, soils, permafrost
- Northern Windmill Islands, including Bailey Peninsula, deglaciated by 5500 corrected yr BP; southern islands by 8000. Isostatic uplift 0.5–0.6 m per 100 years; raised marine limit about 30 m on Bailey Peninsula. **SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan (citing Goodwin 1993). Goodwin's estimated total uplift of about 53 m: **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Soils and active layer:** soils derive from weathered gneiss, moraine and outwash gravels; "Soils are frozen much of the year during summer, the upper 30-60 cm thaws with the few top centimeters, refreezing at night." Soils are formed "by cryoturbation and cryoclastic weathering" and classed as cryosols. **SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan. *Reading:* permafrost under a 30–60 cm summer active layer, with frost churning in the soil. No permafrost depth figure was found.

### Climate, wind, snow
- Casey (altitude 32 m): warmest and coldest month means 2.2 and −11.4 °C; extremes 9.2 to −34 °C; mean annual snowfall 219 mm water equivalent; rain recorded in summer and in July 2008 and July 2009. **SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan.
- "Annual average wind speed of 25 km per hour. Gale winds are predominantly from the east, off the polar ice cap. Blizzards may occur very suddenly and are a frequent occurrence especially during winter ... the extremely strong winds scour the snow off exposed areas." **SOURCED**, same.
- **2001 statistics:** highest gust easterly 195 km/h (105 kn) on 11 September; wind above 22 kn on 178 days (49%); above 34 kn on 127 days (35%); blizzard on 61 days (17%); snowfall on 175 days (48%). **SOURCED**, AAD magazine issue 3 (Autumn 2002), "Australian Antarctic weather records: Casey." One year only.
- A 2023 gust of 114 kn (211 km/h): **UNVERIFIED** (snippet of an AAD station news page; not opened).

### Sea ice and fast ice
- Resupply is ship-to-shore across Newcomb Bay by barge to Casey wharf. In January 2018 "the first attempt was thwarted by thick fast ice stubbornly sitting in front of the wharf"; the ship left and came back after "a good blow which was strong enough to clear out much of the sea-ice." **SOURCED**, AAD "This week at Casey, 5 January 2018."
- No source read gives fast-ice width, thickness, season, or bearing capacity at Casey, and no source read reports a sea-ice runway or sea-ice skiway at Casey. *Gap.*
- Glacier fronts: the Vanderford Glacier lies immediately south of the Windmill Islands (Wikipedia "Windmill Islands," secondary). No calving or ice-front data were read.

### Station history and site choice
- **Wilkes Station** (US, January 1957, Clark Peninsula) was built in 16 days. By the mid-1960s "the constant drift snow buried structures for most of the year"; the buildings had become a fire hazard from fuel seepage. **SOURCED**, AAD history page "Wilkes." It "is now almost permanently frozen in ice and is only occasionally revealed every 4 or 5 years during a big thaw." **SOURCED**, same.
- The cause was the site: Wilkes stood in "a drift prone valley"; "By the end of winter the entire station was almost buried in drift snow, and with the summer melt, some 20 pumps were needed to prevent the station filling with water." **SOURCED**, AAD magazine issue 15 (2008), "From Wilkes to Casey."
- **The answer was design and exposure, not flat ground:** Repstat ("replacement station") was raised "two meters above the bedrock," with "a rounded profile facing the prevailing east wind to prevent snow build-up," buildings in a single line 7 m apart for fire safety. **SOURCED**, same. Repstat was begun in 1964 about 2 km south on the Bailey Peninsula and opened 19 February 1969 as Casey. **UNVERIFIED** for the "2 km" and exact date (search snippet; Wikipedia gives 1964 start and February 1969 completion, **SOURCED** secondary). The present station ("Red Shed") was first occupied in 1988. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia (secondary).
- *Reading:* the move to the Bailey Peninsula was made to escape a drift trap and put buildings on exposed, wind-scoured rock. Nothing read says the site was chosen for flat ground or for aviation.

## 2. Casey airfields

### Wilkins Aerodrome (Wilkins Runway, ICAO YWKS)
- **Location:** 66°41′27″S, 111°31′35″E. "Approximately 70 km southeast of Casey research station." **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins Aerodrome page. Other figures: "approximately 65 km" from Casey and 40 km from the coast (**SOURCED**, Wikipedia, secondary); "about 65 km south east of Casey" (**SOURCED**, AAD 2002–03 survey report record on researchdata.edu.au). Glacier name differs between sources: "Peterson Glacier" (AAD 2020 news), "Upper Peterson Glacier" (airport-technology), "Preston Heath" (Wikipedia), "Upper Vanderford Glacier region" for the A6 South site (AAD 2002–03 survey record, as summarized in search; the record page itself said only "about 65 km south east"). *Unresolved naming conflict.*
- **Elevation:** 720–780 m (**SOURCED**, AAD). Current aerodrome chart: aerodrome elevation 2522 ft (769 m); runway end elevations 2360 ft (719 m) at 09T and 2514 ft (766 m) at 27T. **SOURCED**, Airservices Australia DAP aerodrome chart "Wilkins Runway, Antarctica (YWKS)", 09 JUL 2026 (copy hosted at crc.id.au; text extracted locally).
- **Dimensions:** AAD page: strip 3,500 m × 100–150 m; runway 3,200 m × 30 m ("tested to 60 m width"); centerline slope 1.58% to the west, cross-slope under 1.0%. **SOURCED.** The 09 JUL 2026 DAP chart labels the runway **3000 × 30** (bearing 094°T/274°T), with a note "Changes: FROM SUP H28/26." **SOURCED.** So the published length was 3,000 m at July 2026; whether this is a temporary shortening or a permanent change is not stated. Earlier figures: 4,000 m × 100 m planned/graded (**SOURCED**, AAD 2006 SCAR-COMNAP news item and AAD magazine issue 6, 2004: "A 4000m by 100m area of blue ice was graded and tested"); "3.5 kilometers long" (**SOURCED**, AAD 2016 news).
- **Surface:** natural glacial ice, about 500 m thick, "approximately 70% exposed ice and 30% snow cover that is less than one meter deep." The base is "rolled with proof rollers to ensure that the surface ice has suitable bearing strength" and "tillered by a snowgroomer to manufacture higher friction levels prior to each flight." **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page. Construction (2006 account): bare blue ice has "a co-efficient of friction of 0.1"; fresh snow was spread over "the graded blue ice surface and compressed with a roller to approx 100mm," loaded up to 57 t, left 24 hours to bond, giving friction of about 0.3, "equivalent to a concrete runway on a wet day at Melbourne airport." **SOURCED**, AAD 2006 news. *So:* it is a blue-ice runway carrying a thin compacted-snow cap, not a bare blue-ice runway and not a deep compacted-snow runway. AAD's own pages call it both "blue ice" and "groomed glacial ice."
- **Ice movement:** "12 m/year to the southwest." **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page. The 2002–03 survey site moved "approximately 6 meters per year in an east-southeast direction." **SOURCED**, researchdata.edu.au record of the "Casey Blue Ice Runway Survey Report 2002–2003." "About 40 ft [12 m] each year": **SOURCED**, airport-technology (secondary).
- **Why it is there:** about 70 km inland for "low winter snow accumulation, a slope of less than 2%, and an altitude of 700m above sea level providing the best temperature conditions." **SOURCED**, AAD 2006. Placed at 700 m "to minimize the likelihood of melt, as the coast is relatively warm by Antarctic standards during the summer months." **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page.
- **Site search:** the 2001/02 study identified several blue-ice sites and investigated site "R3 in the upper Peterson Glacier (UPG) region approximately 50 km south east of Casey"; R3 was found unsuitable (reasons not given) and a site "about 65 km south east of Casey" was mapped instead. **SOURCED**, researchdata.edu.au survey record. In 2000 the plan was "a compressed snow runway near Casey," with a blue-ice alternate in the Bunger Hills, 440 km west. **SOURCED**, AAD news 2000 "Australia moves closer to Antarctic air link." In 2002 the chosen approach was "compacted snow runway built on glacial ice," with technical help from George Blaisdell. **SOURCED**, AAD news 2002 "Antarctic runway work to begin."
- **Rejected closer options:** **NOT FOUND.** No source read discusses a runway on the Bailey Peninsula, the Windmill Islands rock, or the sea ice near Casey. The air-link documents read never list those as options; they go straight to snow or glacier ice inland.
- **Season and temperature limit:** October to March, closing "for around six weeks at the height of summer, due to warmer temperatures causing sub-surface melt." **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page. "Ice temperatures need to be below minus 5 degrees Celsius for planes to land." **SOURCED**, AAD news 17 Feb 2020 ("Wilkins runway delayed opening"). That season the runway was shut 9 weeks against a usual 4–6, with one A319 flight cancelled and an A319 and a C-17A postponed. **SOURCED**, same. Published instrument approach (RNP RWY 09T) temperature range: −30 °C to 0 °C. **SOURCED**, DAP chart 09 JUL 2026.
- **Aircraft:** Airbus A319-115LR (Skytraders) and RAAF C-17A Globemaster III. **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page. The A319 carries typically up to 38 passengers, flies about 4 h 30 min each way from Hobart, with 2 hours on the ground, and needs no refueling in Antarctica; "Up to 20 flights may be conducted each season." **SOURCED**, AAD "A319 background information." RAAF C-130J also listed: **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Flights achieved:** planned 20 per season; 2 in 2010/11 and 4 the next season, "closed from December to February due to melt." **SOURCED**, phys.org 2012 (AFP report). In 2016: about 18 intercontinental flights (A319 and C-17A) planned plus about 20 intracontinental flights. **SOURCED**, AAD news 27 Sep 2016. "15 successful flights" in 2021/22: **SOURCED** via Wikipedia (secondary).
- **Weather and wind:** winter temperatures to −25 °C, winds up to 200 km/h, a week-long blizzard at the start of the 2020 season with gusts to 90 kn. **SOURCED**, AAD news 2020 "Herculean task waking up Wilkins Aerodrome." Mean temperature −14 °C. **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page. No crosswind limit or visibility statistics were found. *Gap.*
- **Maintenance effort:** each spring the crew must "clear the snow off the runway surface, proof-roll, survey, mark out," with at least three surface passes to set a friction baseline. **SOURCED**, AAD 2020. The 2020 start-up team was four (manager, two plant operators, a mechanic). **SOURCED**, same. Up to eight people live there in the flying season. **SOURCED**, AAD Wilkins page. Setup takes "two months to get the runway up and running after winter." **SOURCED**, createdigital.org.au (Engineers Australia), secondary.
- **Cost:** about A$46 million (A$46.3 m per airport-technology; "Aus$45 million" per phys.org/AFP). **SOURCED** (secondary press). First A319 landing: 9 December 2007 (Wikipedia "Casey Station") or 11 January 2008 (Wikipedia "Wilkins Runway"). **Conflicting secondary dates**; not resolved.
- **Future:** the Australian Government funded an "Aviation Facilities Renewal Project" covering Wilkins Aerodrome, the Casey ski landing area and the Davis ski landing area. **UNVERIFIED** (search summary of a contractor page, dcwc.com.au, not opened). The Davis paved runway, the only Australian alternative studied, was cancelled in November 2021 (see section 7).

For ski aircraft (Basler, Twin Otter and similar). Opened 30 December 2004; listed as 1,996 m (6,547 ft) on ice, at 66°17′17″S, 110°45′27″E. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia (secondary). ## 3. Mirny site geology

### Location and landform
- Mirny is at 66°33′S, 93°01′E on the Davis Sea coast of Queen Mary Land, Indian Ocean sector. **SOURCED**, AARI station page (aari.ru, "Mirnyy"). Elevation 35 m. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Mirny Station" (secondary); meteorological site 39.9 m per a Russian encyclopedia page (znanierussia.ru, secondary). **SOURCED** as read, secondary.
- Mabus Point, "just south of the Haswell Islands," marking "the eastern limit of McDonald Bay," "subsequently became the site of the Soviet scientific station, Mirny"; about 1 nautical mile northwest of Morennaya Hill; Ob' Passage separates it from Khmary Island. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Mabus Point" (from the USGS/ACAN gazetteer).
- **How much ice-free ground there is.** The strongest source read, from the Russian geological survey institute: "In the station area at the start of its operation there were more than two dozen islands and rock nunataks made of metamorphic and meta-intrusive rocks ... Later, the bedrock outcrops were to a large extent covered by glacial and snow cover, and **at present are preserved only directly on the area of the station.**" (translated from Russian). Bedrock outcrops "are located mainly on the hills (sopki), where the station's living and technical buildings stand." **SOURCED**, Maslov and Borovkov, "Results of seasonal continental specialized geological investigations of VNIIOkeangeologia in the 63rd RAE," *Russian Polar Research* (Rossiyskie polyarnye issledovaniya) No. 34, pp. 8–11 (AARI, data.aari.ru), on 2018 fieldwork.
- Named hills on the station: Sopka Radio and Sopka Komsomolskaya (**SOURCED**, same), plus Morennaya Hill near Mabus Point (**SOURCED**, Wikipedia Mabus Point). Komsomolskaya Hill is "just north of the main section of the station": **UNVERIFIED** (AAD gazetteer snippet; the gazetteer page timed out with a 504, so it died at the source).
- **"Four rock outcrops":** **NOT CONFIRMED.** No source read gives the number four. The 1956 accounts say a site "where outcropping rock had broken through the coastal ice" (**SOURCED**, Polar Journal, "Mirny: first Soviet research station," secondary) and "a piece of ice free land was spotted west of Depot Bay and south of Haswell Island, along a strip of coastline called Pravda Coast" (**SOURCED**, Gvozdetsky, IGY chapter, English translation hosted at southpolestation.com, text extracted locally). AARI describes the station as standing "on glacial ice and rocks" (**UNVERIFIED**, snippets of two Russian encyclopedia texts).
- **Area of ice-free ground: no number was found, but a map shows it.** ASPA 127 Management Plan (Haswell Island), Measure 5 (2016), Map 1, "Location of the Haswell Islands, Mirny Station, and logistic activity sites," with a scale bar of 0–800 m. On the mainland it shows a glacier-covered triangular promontory. "Soil, rocks" appear only as a band along the north shore at the station, roughly 300–400 m long and about 100 m wide, plus one smaller patch to the south. Everything else on the promontory is mapped as glacier. **SOURCED** (PDF downloaded via env.go.jp and read as an image; dimensions read off the scale bar by eye). The plan's text: Mirny stands "on Mirny Peninsula located in coastal nunataks." **SOURCED.** *Reading:* the mainland ice-free ground at Mirny is a few hectares.
- **Offshore islands:** Haswell Island, the largest of the archipelago, is "0,82 km²" in area and 93 m high, "at 2,5 km distance" from Mirny. **SOURCED**, ASPA 127 plan. The other islets on Map 1 (Tokarev, Gorev, Buromskiy, Poryadin, Zykov, Fulmar) are each roughly 100–300 m across (map reading). None is long enough for a runway.
- **Ice sheet at the station:** the ice surface right behind the station rises from 37 m in the north to 71 m in the southwest across the runway survey area, in "a crescent-shaped valley" bordered by crevasse zones on the south, east and west. **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2019, *Earth's Cryosphere* XXIII(1): 69–77 (see section 4). The ice sheet therefore begins at the edge of the station's rock hills. No ice-thickness figure for the station area was found; GPR there sounded only the upper 25 m.

### Bedrock: rock types and age
- "The geological structure of the Mirny area includes hypersthene-bearing plagiogneisses and crystalline schists, rheomorphic charnockitoids, metagabbroids, leucodiorites and plagiogranites. The history of geological development includes at least two tectonomagmatic events, corresponding to the Rayner (1000–900 Ma) and Pan-African (about 500 Ma) tectonothermal events." On Sopka Radio there is "a massive outcrop of orthopyroxene granitoids"; Sopka Komsomolskaya has a metamorphic complex of "mainly garnet-biotite gneisses" and Proterozoic "brown gneisses." **SOURCED**, Maslov and Borovkov, *Russian Polar Research* No. 34 (translated).
- Earlier Soviet description: "various gneisses and crystalline slates, including biotite gneisses, micaceous and hornblende slates, granulites ... Pre-Cambrian." **UNVERIFIED** (search snippet of a CIA FOIA translation; not opened). A 2009 *Doklady Earth Sciences* paper "New data on the age of rocks in the Mirnyi station area": title seen, abstract not read.
- No data were found on fracturing, weathering state, frost heave or active layer at Mirny. *Gap.*

### Wind and climate
- "On more than 200 days per year the wind is stronger than 15 meters per second, with occasional cyclones"; mean annual temperature −11.3 °C. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Mirny Station" (secondary). "220 days a year" over 15 m/s, extremes +6 °C and −40 °C: **SOURCED**, znanierussia.ru (Russian encyclopedia, secondary). AARI: average summer −3.2 °C, average winter −16.3 °C. **SOURCED**, AARI station page.
- Mean annual wind about 11 m/s; Mirny "fully exposed to katabatic flow": **UNVERIFIED** (search snippets of Hokkaido and Polar Record items, not opened; Britannica 403, Cambridge 403).
- First winter (1956): "A strong wind from the interior to the coast blew almost incessantly from the beginning of April on. The houses and other structures were soon buried in snow. The only way to get out was through holes in the roofs." **SOURCED**, Gvozdetsky (translated history). Two runway-area marker stakes (6 m aluminum pipes) were broken at the base by wind in winter 2016. **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2019.
- At Molodyozhnaya (context, not Mirny): katabatic gusts of 25–30 m/s on the morning of the first Il-18D arrival in 1980. **SOURCED**, Kornilov memoir chapter, *Russian Polar Research* No. 42, pp. 5–7.

### Sea ice, icebergs, glacier fronts
- In January 1956 the *Ob* "came up as far as the fast ice at the contemplated disembarkation point and stopped 100 m from shore. Unloading was a very complicated procedure." **SOURCED**, Gvozdetsky. "Unloading operations are initially hampered by unfavorable ice conditions." **SOURCED**, Polar Journal (secondary).
- Soviet fast-ice studies existed because loads had to cross "many kilometers of deformed fast ice" from ship to shore: **UNVERIFIED** (search snippet of a DTIC record, "Fast Ice Dynamics in the Mirny Area"; openpolar.no failed with a certificate error, so it died at the source).
- Icebergs calved from the Helen Glacier and coastal ice cliffs measured 8 to 50 m high: **UNVERIFIED** (snippet). A polynya west of the Shackleton Ice Shelf used by ships: **UNVERIFIED** (snippet).
- **Fast ice (primary figures):** "For the larger part of the year, the sea within the Area is covered with fast ice, whose width reaches 30-40 km by the end of winter. Fast ice breaks up between December 17 and March 9 (February 3, on average) and freezes between March 18 and May 5 (April 6, on average). The probability that the ice-free period off Mirny Station will last more than 1 month is 85%, more than 2 months 45%, and more than 3 months 25%. The Area is always full of icebergs. In summer, when fast ice disappears, icebergs drift westward along the coast." **SOURCED**, ASPA 127 Management Plan, "Ice conditions." Vehicles cross the fast ice to the islands once it "is safe for pedestrian and vehicle traffic." **SOURCED**, same.
- No fast-ice **thickness** or bearing-capacity figure was found for Mirny. *Gap.*

### Station history and site choice (1956)
- *Ob* left Kaliningrad 30 November 1955 and reached the coast in early January 1956; the plan for Depot Bay "proved unfeasible due to ice conditions," and "aerial reconnaissance identified an alternative location ... where outcropping rock had broken through the coastal ice." **SOURCED**, Polar Journal (secondary). Gvozdetsky gives the same sequence: Depot Bay, then "lengthy air reconnaissance," then ice-free land south of Haswell Island. **SOURCED.**
- Station opened 13 February 1956. **SOURCED**, AARI. 86 people wintered in 1956 under Somov (**SOURCED**, AARI); "about 90" (**SOURCED**, Gvozdetsky).
- The expedition brought "four airplanes and two helicopters," and aircraft logged "1500 flight hours." **SOURCED**, Polar Journal (secondary). Early flights: Cherevichny flew Somov over the geomagnetic pole region; Gvozdetsky says "an IL-14," other accounts say Il-12 (**UNVERIFIED**, snippets). In March 1956 "a light AN-2 ski-plane flew a distance of 400 km from Mirny and landed on the ice sheet at an elevation of 3,000 m." Gvozdetsky's chapter contains a photo captioned "Landing strip at Mirny," with no description. **SOURCED.** *Reading:* the 1956 operations were ski aircraft and ice-sheet landings. No source read describes a prepared wheeled strip in 1956.
- *Site-choice reading:* the sources say Mirny was chosen because exposed rock could be reached by ship where Depot Bay's ice blocked access. Nothing read says the site was chosen for aviation or for flat ground.

## 4. Mirny airfields

### Soviet era (1956–1991)
- Mirny was the main base for Vostok and the inland traverses for over fifty years, and "the snow runway, which used to exist there, has to be restored." **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2017, *Earth's Cryosphere* XXI(1): 67–77 ("The structure of the upper part of the glacier in the area of a snow runway of Mirny Station," PDF downloaded and text extracted). Air service at Mirny ran "from the earliest days" until the 1990/91 season, when it "ceased its operations due to the increasingly worsening economic situation in Russia." **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2019.
- In February 1980 an Il-14 was flown in from Mirny to test the new wheeled snow-ice runway at Molodyozhnaya. **SOURCED**, Kornilov memoir chapter, RPR No. 42. This shows Mirny was an Il-14 base in 1980. It does not show a wheeled heavy strip at Mirny.
- Il-14 accidents at or near Mirny (1979, 1986, 1990) appear in aviation-safety databases. **UNVERIFIED** (titles only; pages not opened).
- **Old airfield location, length and surface:** **NOT FOUND.** The 2016 *Ice and Snow* paper that probably covers it returned 403 (died at the source); its openpolar mirror failed on a certificate error.
- Li-2, Il-12 and An-2 use in 1956 is supported only by the Gvozdetsky An-2 passage and snippets. **No source read reports Il-76 or any heavy wheeled jet landing at Mirny.**

### Present landing strip (restored 2013–2016)
- **Purpose:** an intermediate airfield for intracontinental routes between field bases east of Casey and west of Davis, "about one and a half thousand kilometers" apart. **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2019.
- **History:** reconnaissance in 2013/14 (59th RAE), large-scale GPR and glaciological surveys in 2014–2016; "on February 10, 2016, the landing strip received a medium-range utility aircraft Basler Turbo 67 (DC-3T) by ALCI ... piloted by a Canadian crew from Kenn Borek Air Ltd." **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2019.
- **Length:** the strip ran to "the end (1300 m)," with crevasses in the section from 787 m to the end. **SOURCED**, Popov et al. 2019. *About 1.3 km.* No width given.
- **Surface:** a snow runway ("snow runway" in the 2017 paper title) on the coastal glacier; "marked and rolled by the wintering crew." **SOURCED.** Near-surface structure: 4.6–5 m of dense snow-firn over meteoric ice; snow-firn thickness varies "between zero ('blue ice') and approximately 40 m" across the survey area. **SOURCED**, Popov 2017 and 2019. The area is in the "infiltration zone": meltwater percolates. One core found water at 6.4 m. **SOURCED**, Popov 2017. A wet-ice layer at 5–5.4 m depth held about 15% water by volume. **SOURCED**, Popov 2019.
- **Elevation and slope:** 38 m at the start to 59 m at 873 m; the steepest segment (120–680 m) averages about 1.66° (29 m/km); runway parameters meet Soviet and Russian civil-aviation snow-runway norms "although they are close to the limit values." **SOURCED**, Popov 2019.
- **Ice movement:** "the glacier flow velocity varies from approximately 6 to 80 m/year" across the area (earlier estimate 18 to 65 m/yr). The strip "has been constructed on a poorly investigated dynamically active glacier." **SOURCED**, Popov 2019.
- **Crevasses:** 10 crevasses 10–20 cm wide opened in the 787–1300 m section; bridged by at least 1.5 m of snow-firn; crews "acquired good practical experience in healing small crevasses." Crevasse zones surround the valley on three sides. **SOURCED**, Popov 2019.
- **Season and limits:** the 2016/17 summer was "abnormally warm ... with no single snowfall"; the authors call for continued crevasse and stake monitoring "because of the rapidly changing coastal glaciers." **SOURCED**, Popov 2019. No season dates, crosswind limits or closure statistics were found. *Gap.*
- **Access:** a snow road connects the station and the strip. **SOURCED**, Popov 2019 (figure caption). Distance not stated, but the survey area was 3.5 × 3.5 km around the station (**SOURCED**, Popov 2017), so the strip is within a few kilometers.

## 5. What a passenger-jet runway needs

### Length for scheduled jets
- **Airbus A320-200 (CFM56), take-off weight limit at ISA, dry paved runway** (Airbus *Aircraft Characteristics – Airport and Maintenance Planning*, A320, rev. Jun 01/24, §3-3-1, figure). Read off the chart by eye for the sea-level curve: about 63 t at 1,200 m, about 76 t at 1,800 m, about 82 t at 2,400 m, flattening after that. A320-200 maximum take-off weight is in the 73.5–78 t class. **SOURCED** (PDF downloaded from aircraft.airbus.com and the chart read as an image; chart values approximate). *So:* a full-weight A320 needs roughly 2,000–2,400 m of dry pavement at sea level. Airbus notes the curves are "for information only." Ice or snow friction, contamination, and long-range fuel loads all push the figure up.
- **Aerodrome reference code:** the A320-200 is code 3C up to 73.5 t take-off weight and 4C above it. **SOURCED**, same document §3-3-3.
- **Australian planning figure:** the Davis paved runway was set at "2700 meters long, based on the length required by large commercial aircraft." **SOURCED**, AAD magazine issue 34 (June 2018).
- **Safety areas beyond the pavement** (ICAO Annex 14, as quoted in an ICAO APAC presentation, "RESA Phuket"): the runway strip extends 60 m beyond each runway end. "A runway end safety area shall extend from the end of a runway strip to a distance of at least 90 m where the code number is 3 or 4," and should extend at least 240 m. **SOURCED** (ICAO PDF, text extracted). *Arithmetic:* a 2,700 m runway needs a cleared, graded line of about 3,000 m at the 90 m minimum, or about 3,300 m with the recommended 240 m.
- **On ice, length goes up.** Bare blue ice has a friction coefficient of about 0.1 (**SOURCED**, AAD 2006), so aircraft rely on reverse thrust, and blue-ice runways are "often several miles long" (**SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Blue ice runway," secondary). The working Antarctic heavy wheeled strips are all about 3,000–3,400 m: Wilkins 3,000–3,200 m, Phoenix 3,353 m, Progress Zenit 3,000 m, Union Glacier 3,000 m, Novo about 3,300 m, Troll 3,300 m (figures and sources below).
- **Wide-body length: not researched.** No wide-body planning chart was read. A Boeing 767 has landed at Novo (Titan Airways), but Titan's page returned 403, so that stays **UNVERIFIED**.

### The surfaces that carry heavy wheeled aircraft in Antarctica
1. **Glacial blue ice, capped or bare** (Wilkins, Union Glacier, Novo, Troll). It exists only where there is "no net annual snow accumulation" (**SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Blue ice runway," secondary). At Wilkins the length was fixed by the site (3,000–3,200 m at a 1.58% slope). Bearing strength comes from about 500 m of glacier ice, proof-rolled before use (**SOURCED**, AAD). **Usable days:** October to March minus a mid-summer melt closure of about six weeks (four to nine weeks in practice). Landing needs ice colder than −5 °C (**SOURCED**, AAD). Siting rule from AAD: go high, about 700 m, "to minimize the likelihood of melt" (**SOURCED**). **Failure mode:** sub-surface melt. Pegasus, a glacial-ice runway at McMurdo, failed from "excessive melting from warmer temperatures combined with dust from nearby Black Island." **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Phoenix Airfield" (secondary).
   - Union Glacier: 3,000 × 50 m, 750 m elevation, blue ice, Il-76 operations, certified December 2008. **SOURCED**, Wikipedia (secondary). Boeing 757-200 operations by Icelandair/Loftleiðir: **UNVERIFIED** (snippets).
   - Novo: blue ice, about 3,300 m (Wikipedia list) or 3,000 × 60 m (snippet). **SOURCED**/**UNVERIFIED** respectively. Il-76TD-90VD flights from Cape Town (**UNVERIFIED**, snippets). Troll: blue ice, 3,300 m (**SOURCED**, Wikipedia list, secondary).
2. **Deep compacted snow on the ice sheet** (Phoenix; Progress "Zenit"; Molodyozhnaya 1980).
   - **Phoenix (US, McMurdo):** 3,353 m (11,000 ft) of compacted snow, about 60 wheeled flights a year; serious construction from 2015/16, opened in early 2017. Wheeled aircraft are banned for about six weeks in late December and January: "Conditions at the new Phoenix compacted-snow runway prevent the use of wheeled aircraft during the warm part of the austral summer." **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Phoenix Airfield" (secondary, quoting an NSF notice). The US Army Corps of Engineers engineering report (DTIC AD1073325) returned 403 and died at the source.
   - **Progress "Zenit" (Russia, 2022), the best-documented case.** **SOURCED** throughout, from Polyakov and Lunyov, "Results of two years of operation of the snow landing site 'Zenit', Progress station," *Russian Polar Research* No. 57, pp. 30–32 (AARI; PDF text extracted and translated):
     - *Siting:* "As a rule, the only possibility of organizing airfields near stations on the Antarctic continent is building them on the glacier." At Progress, "on the territory of the oasis itself the surface relief is absolutely unsuitable for organizing a ground [soil/gravel] airfield," and there were no blue-ice zones within reasonable distance. The site is **6 km** from the station, on glacier with **5–10 m** of snow cover. Mean annual accumulation is about **30 cm**, and **glacier movement does not exceed 2 m per year**. It lies in the infiltration zone, with occasional cracks under 0.5 m wide that can be filled.
     - *Method:* the Soviet norm VSN 37-76 (1976) needs at least two years, including one summer near melting point to ease compaction. Zenit was instead built in about six months of **winter** (March to August 2022) by layer-by-layer compaction of cold snow with a patented "compacting platform." The finished slab covers runway and apron, **350,000 m²**, **1.0–1.2 m thick**, with surface hardness 1–1.5 MPa. A **4 cm** layer of milled ice crumb raises wheel friction "to 50%."
     - *Bearing:* a 1 m slab with surface hardness of at least 0.9 MPa, resting on natural snow of at least 0.15 MPa, is calculated to carry an aircraft of up to **375 t** with Il-76-like gear, more than twice the Il-76's 150 t. The largest allowable crack or void under the slab is about 1 m. The test vehicle was a crane truck ballasted to 60 t with 0.8 MPa tire pressure.
     - *Season:* wheeled use from the start of October to the end of December and from the start of February to the end of April, "depending substantially on the weather." There is no radio navigation aid or lighting, so operations run "only in daytime with good visibility." 4 Il-76 flights in 2022/23 and 7 in 2023/24, with up to 90 people per flight. Rut depth after the first landing was no more than 3 cm. Slab hardness rose about 20% over the first winter.
     - *Dimensions:* 3,000 × 100 m. **SOURCED**, Interfax (7 Nov 2022 report).
   - **Molodyozhnaya (USSR, 1980):** "an airfield on a snow-ice base for receiving heavy aircraft on wheeled undercarriage of the Il-18D type," built near Mount Vechernyaya over the 24th SAE winter by a dedicated construction detachment. It was tested with a 75 t gear model, and the Il-18D landed on 13 February 1980. The memoir records the hazards: a blizzard on 6 February (gusts 30 m/s, mean 22 m/s, visibility 100 m) left drifts up to 60 cm on the runway that took two days to clear, and air temperature near −2 °C made the surface too weak until a cold spell arrived. **SOURCED**, Kornilov memoir chapter, *Russian Polar Research* No. 42, pp. 5–7. Length 2,559 m: **SOURCED**, Wikipedia list (secondary).
3. **Sea ice** (McMurdo Annual Sea-Ice Runway). Two 3,048 m runways; ice "over 2 meters ... thick to land an aircraft like the C-17"; "usually operated from October to December, after which time the sea ice would break up." **SOURCED**, Wikipedia "Ice runway" (secondary). *Failure mode:* break-up and warming; early-season only.
4. **Rock or gravel** (Rothera, Marambio, Frei, Boulder Clay; the cancelled Davis and Dumont d'Urville strips). All the built examples are short, at most about 2,200 m, and serve turboprops or the C-130J. **No rock or gravel runway in Antarctica carries scheduled jets.** Details are in section 7.

### What makes a runway unusable (from the sources above)
- **Summer warmth:** Wilkins needs ice below −5 °C; Wilkins, Phoenix and Pegasus all close in mid-summer; Molodyozhnaya's snow surface was too weak at −2 °C.
- **Drift and blizzard:** Wilkins start-up blizzards; 60 cm drifts at Molodyozhnaya.
- **Visibility and daylight:** Zenit is day-only VFR.
- **Ice movement and crevassing:** Mirny strip (section 4); Zenit's crack limit.
- **Sea-ice break-up:** McMurdo.
- **Dust darkening:** Pegasus.
- Crosswind limits were not found in any source read. *Gap.*

### Which surfaces are realistic beside a rock-outcrop coastal station (as the sources state it)
- Russia's own engineering statement: near coastal stations "the only possibility" is usually the glacier, and an oasis's rock relief can be "absolutely unsuitable" (Progress, above). Australia found the opposite in one place, the Vestfold Hills (410 km² of ice-free ground), where a 2,700 m rock runway was designed but cancelled (section 7).
- Blue ice needs a no-accumulation ablation zone; near the coast this was searched for at Casey and found only 50–70 km inland (section 2). The Progress team found none within reasonable distance.
- Deep compacted snow on slow, gently sloping glacier ice a few kilometers from the coast is the surface that has actually been made to work near a coastal oasis station (Zenit, 6 km).
- Sea ice works only early in the season, and only where the fast ice is thick and iceberg-free over 3+ km.

## 7. Buildability (dimensions, composition, ice option, precedents, construction scale)

### 7.1 Dimensions of the land

**Casey / Windmill Islands**
- Group extent about 31.5 × 11.1 km of islands and peninsulas (**SOURCED**, secondary). Total ice-free area "500 km²" (**UNVERIFIED**).
- Station peninsula (Bailey): rock patchwork about 2.5–3 × 1 km; outcrops to about 40 m, rising to the Løken Moraines at about 130 m. Clark Peninsula: about 4 × 2 km patchwork. Mitchell Peninsula: mostly snow-covered, contours to 120 m. (**SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan and AAD 1:50,000 maps read by eye.)
- **Longest straight contiguous ice-free line:** under 1 km for any single outcrop patch. **No 2.5–3 km straight ice-free line** appears on Maps 1–2 (map reading; Maps 3–5 not opened).
- **Fit:** a 2,700 m runway plus safety areas (about 3,000–3,300 m) **does not fit on existing ice-free rock** at the station peninsulas without crossing bays, lakes, snowfields or moraine. It would need either a line across several peninsulas and bays (reclaimed or filled ground) or a site on the ice sheet.

**Mirny / Mabus Point**
- Mainland ice-free ground: one band about 300–400 m × 100 m plus a small patch (ASPA 127 Map 1, read by eye). Haswell Island 0.82 km², 93 m high, 2.5 km offshore. Other islets about 100–300 m. (**SOURCED**.)
- The rock outcrops were once "more than two dozen islands and nunataks" but are now preserved "only directly on the area of the station." **SOURCED**, Maslov and Borovkov.
- The glacier immediately behind the station rises from 37 to 71 m across the runway survey area, and is crevassed on three sides. **SOURCED**, Popov 2019.
- **Fit:** a passenger-jet runway **cannot fit on rock** at Mirny; the rock is two orders of magnitude too small. The only ground available is glacier ice, fast sea ice, or reclaimed ground.

### 7.2 Composition of the ground

**Casey**
- Bedrock: leucocratic garnet-bearing granite gneiss with dolerite dykes; Mesoproterozoic, about 1.4–1.2 billion years old. Northern rocks are "much more stable"; the Ardery Charnockite to the south "crumbles readily." Surficial cover: moraine, exfoliated debris, outwash gravel; cryosols with cryoturbation; summer active layer 30–60 cm. (**SOURCED**, ASPA 135 plan.)
- Aggregate and blasting: no source discusses quarrying at Casey. *Inference, not sourced:* sound gneiss is the kind of rock Rothera and Davis planned to blast and crush. The Davis winter drilling found "hard metamorphic bedrock" alongside "sediment filled valleys with permafrost and extensive subsurface ice lenses," and the Davis ridge site was chosen for a "large rock outcrop with a thin veneer of soils which limits susceptibility to permafrost 'heave'" (**SOURCED**, NZGS article and AAD 2020). The same split (sound rock knobs, ice-rich valley fill) would apply to the Bailey Peninsula's snow- and moraine-filled valleys, but no Casey borehole data were found.

**Mirny**
- Bedrock: hypersthene-bearing plagiogneisses and crystalline schists, charnockitoids, metagabbroids, leucodiorites, plagiogranites; orthopyroxene granitoids on Sopka Radio; garnet-biotite gneiss on Sopka Komsomolskaya; events at 1000–900 Ma and about 500 Ma. **SOURCED**, Maslov and Borovkov.
- No data were found on fracturing, active layer, permafrost or frost heave. Any rock quarry would have to come out of the station's own hills or Haswell Island, which is a protected area (ASPA 127). *Inference.*
- **Glacier composition at the strip:** 4.6–5 m of dense snow-firn over meteoric ice, snow-firn thickness from zero (blue ice) to about 40 m, wet layers (water at 6.4 m depth; about 15% water in a thin layer at about 5 m). It is an infiltration (melt-percolation) zone. **SOURCED**, Popov 2017 and 2019.

### 7.3 The ice option (ice speed is the governing test)

**Casey**
- Wilkins (65–70 km inland, 720–780 m): ice about 500 m thick, moving **12 m/yr**; earlier survey site **6 m/yr**. Sub-surface melt closes it for 4–9 weeks each summer. **SOURCED**, AAD.
- Ice nearer the station: the ice sheet begins about 3–4 km east of Casey (Løken Moraines) and climbs about 3–4% by eye (AAD maps). **No ice-speed, thickness, accumulation or melt figure was found for the coastal Law Dome margin near Casey.** Law Dome summit flow about 2 m/yr: **UNVERIFIED** (snippet). AAD sited Wilkins high and far precisely because the coast is "relatively warm" in summer (**SOURCED**). The Casey ski landing area 10 km east and the Mitchell Peninsula field landing site show that ski-aircraft surfaces exist close in (**SOURCED**). Whether that ice is slow and flat enough for a 3 km wheeled snow runway is **not in any source read**.

**Mirny**
- Existing strip glacier: ice speed **about 6 to 80 m/yr** across the area, crevasses opening along the strip, slope 29 m/km at its steepest ("close to the limit values"), melt-percolation zone, abnormally warm 2016/17 season. **SOURCED**, Popov 2019.
- This is a dynamically active coastal glacier. On the developer's ice-speed rule, the fast parts (tens of m/yr) argue against a fixed long runway. The slow end (about 6 m/yr) is comparable to Wilkins's 6–12 m/yr. **No survey was found** mapping a 3 km × 100 m corridor of slow ice near Mirny. The strip actually built was 1.3 km.
- **Fast sea ice:** 30–40 km wide by late winter; breaks up 17 December to 9 March (mean 3 February); refreezes 18 March to 5 May; "always full of icebergs." **SOURCED**, ASPA 127. On the McMurdo precedent, a sea-ice runway would need ice over about 2 m thick and would run roughly October to December. **No thickness data for Mirny fast ice were found**, nor any evidence of an iceberg-free 3 km line.

### 7.5 Construction scale (order of magnitude, from the precedents only)
- **A rock runway of jet length at Casey:** the only real comparable is Davis: 2,700 m on a chosen rock ridge, >3 million m³ of drill-and-blast earthworks, 60 vertical meters leveled, multi-season, ship-supplied, about 250 people, cancelled partly for cost. Casey's peninsulas offer no single 3 km rock ridge (map reading), so any line would also cross water, lakes or snowfields. Rothera's 300,000 m³ of rock fill made only 915 m into 10–30 m deep water. *Scaling is not sourced:* the sources give no basis for a Casey volume, so none is stated here. Season: summer-only construction windows, as at Rothera and Davis. Supply is by sea. Fast ice at the Casey wharf can block or delay the ship (section 1).
- **A snow runway on ice near either station:** the Zenit precedent is 350,000 m² of slab, 1.0–1.2 m thick (*arithmetic:* about 350,000–420,000 m³ of compacted snow), built in about 6 months with one specialist compacting rig plus standard Antarctic plant, on a site with ice speed ≤2 m/yr. A comparable slab needs a site with comparable ice. **At Mirny, the only measured ice moves 6–80 m/yr. At Casey, no near-coast ice speed was found.**
- **A runway at Mirny on reclaimed ground:** no precedent exists at that length. Rothera (915 m) and Dumont d'Urville (about 1 km, destroyed by a glacier-driven surge, abandoned) are the only reclaimed or island-joining examples, and both are short. Mirny's sea is "always full of icebergs."

## 6. What the facts imply for an airport (implications; the only section that compares)

No recommendation. The developer decides. Each reading says what it rests on.

### Casey
- **Ground available near the city:** a patchwork of sound gneiss outcrops, each well under 1 km, about 2.5–3 km by 1 km in total on the station peninsula, broken by snowfields, lakes and moraine. A larger ice-free group of islands and peninsulas surrounds it ("500 km²," unverified).
- **Real airfield:** a 3,000–3,200 m × 30 m blue-ice runway with a snow cap, 65–70 km inland at 720–780 m, on ice about 500 m thick moving 12 m/yr. It is summer-only, needs ice below −5 °C, closes 4–9 weeks in mid-summer, and takes A319 and C-17 aircraft, up to about 20 flights a season. The distance from the station is the price of finding cold, slow, low-accumulation ice.
- **Main hazards:** easterly gales off the ice cap (49% of days over 22 kn and 17% blizzard days in 2001); summer melt at low elevation; fast ice that can block ship resupply at the wharf.
- **Feasibility reading (one per option):**
  - *Rock runway at the station:* **does not fit on existing ground.** Rests on the ASPA 135 description and AAD map reading: no 3 km contiguous ice-free line. It would need **major earthworks plus fill across water or valleys**, at Davis-or-greater scale (Davis: >3 M m³ for 2.7 km on a chosen ridge). No source has surveyed this.
  - *Snow or blue-ice runway close to the city (3–10 km):* **not supported or refuted by the sources.** The ice sheet starts 3–4 km east, but no ice speed, accumulation, slope survey or melt data were found for it, and AAD deliberately went 65–70 km inland to escape coastal warmth.
  - *Inland ice runway (the real one):* **works on existing ice, seasonally**, 65–70 km from the city.
- **What a designer would have to invent or adjust:** a near-city site (either a rock line across peninsulas and bays, or a cold, slow ice corridor near the coast), or accept a 65–70 km airport-to-city road. Something must also cover the mid-summer melt closure if service is to be "scheduled" year-round.

### Mirny
- **Ground available near the city:** a few hectares of rock at the station (a band about 300–400 × 100 m) plus 0.82 km² Haswell Island 2.5 km offshore. Everything else is glacier or sea.
- **Real airfield:** a 1.3 km snow strip on the coastal glacier behind the station, restored 2013–2016 for a BT-67. The ice there moves 6–80 m/yr, crevasses open along the strip, the slope is near the regulatory limit, and the firn is wet. A Soviet snow runway operated until 1990/91, details not found. No wheeled heavy-aircraft or jet operation at Mirny was found in any source.
- **Main hazards:** katabatic wind on more than 200 days a year over 15 m/s (secondary sources); fast, crevassed coastal ice; melt percolation; an iceberg-filled sea; fast ice 30–40 km wide that breaks up only between mid-December and early March.
- **Feasibility reading (one per option):**
  - *Rock runway:* **does not fit.** Rests on the ASPA 127 map and the AARI geology account.
  - *Snow runway on the glacier near the station:* **needs construction on ice, at a site not yet identified.** The only ice measured moves 6–80 m/yr and supported only 1.3 km. The Progress precedent (3,000 × 100 m, 6 km from the station, ice ≤2 m/yr) shows the method works where slow ice exists. The sources do not show slow ice of that extent near Mirny. On the developer's ice-speed rule, the deciding fact is missing.
  - *Fast-ice runway:* **seasonal only, unproven here.** Rests on ASPA 127 break-up dates and the McMurdo precedent (more than 2 m thick, October to December). No thickness or iceberg-free-length data were found.
  - *Reclaimed or island-joining runway:* **no precedent at jet length.** Dumont d'Urville, the closest analog (blasted and joined islands, about 1 km), was wrecked by a glacier-driven surge and abandoned.
- **What a designer would have to invent or adjust:** a slow-ice corridor of at least 3.0–3.3 km near the coast, or an inland site at a distance not specified by any source. If the developer wants a fixed runway close to the city, the real ice at Mirny would have to be ruled slower than measured.

### Where the sources do not support a conclusion
- Ice speed, thickness, accumulation and melt for the Law Dome margin within about 10 km of Casey.
- A slow-ice corridor of 3+ km anywhere near Mirny, and the location and length of the Soviet Mirny snow runway.
- Fast-ice thickness at either site; crosswind limits at any Antarctic runway.
- Permafrost, frost heave and quarry suitability at Mirny; borehole data at Casey.
- Earthworks volumes for any Casey or Mirny rock or reclaimed runway. None were estimated, because no source supports a number.
- Wide-body runway length; the "500 km²" Windmill Islands figure; the "four rock outcrops" at Mirny.
