### A. Other settings
- **Maintenance stations as homes [K7, K4] [P4]:** WYDOT job postings for snowplow operators at Arlington list state housing as part of the pay; an Arlington maintenance-specialist posting says state housing "may be available" (search snippets of 2026 postings, UNVERIFIED). - **Crews rooted in the place [K14] [SM:competence]:** WYDOT's newsletter *Interchange* (August 2025) profiles the Arlington and Elk Mountain crews that work the I-80 stretch near Elk Mountain, centered on a foreman born and raised in Elk Mountain who joined WYDOT at 19 and lives near where he grew up with his wife and daughter (search snippets only; the issuu page returned 404; UNVERIFIED).
- **Schools in the closure zone [K5, K6] [P4]:** Carbon County School District 2 (Hanna, Elk Mountain, Medicine Bow) has written storm guidelines with different steps for students in each community; the Elk Mountain and Medicine Bow school kitchens keep supplies to feed children lunch if travel becomes impossible (Saratoga Sun 2017-12-28 snippet; page 404; UNVERIFIED). In March 2019 the superintendent closed all CCSD2 schools under a blizzard warning covering Saratoga and Elk Mountain with forecast winds to 65 mph (Saratoga Sun 2019-03-13 snippet, UNVERIFIED). **Elk Mountain Elementary had no students enrolled for 2024-25**; the building stays available for use in bad weather and for community events; consolidation of northern Carbon County schools, most likely in Hanna, was raised (Bigfoot99 2024-06-03 snippet; page 404; UNVERIFIED). WYDOT's director said closures disrupt travel to schools and jobs (Cowboy State Daily 2020-03-04, SOURCED).
- **Wamsutter, a town on wheels on the corridor west of Rawlins [K4, K7, K9] [P4, P9]:** 153 homes; the latest census says 49% are mobile homes, longtime residents put it at 80-90%; a 2007 federal count found 20 single-family houses, 39 modular homes, 117 mobile homes, 49 RVs and 19 four-person dormitory units, with no rental units available. Population 139 (1970), 681 (1980), nearly 500 (2001), about 750 (2004). The town grew with pipeline (1952) and railroad (1957) jobs, declined when the railroad pulled out, and boomed with energy after 1973; in 2001 about 600 gas workers arrived and nearly every lot held trailers and motor homes; in 2004 about 20 drilling rigs and over 400 rig workers on 12-hour shifts, seven days on and seven off; booms around 2005, 2009 and 2015 brought waiting lists at the trailer court, whose vacancy reached about 72% in 2025; mobile homes on the assessor's roll fell from 181 (2010) to 115 (2026). Some workers lived in company trailers two to a bedroom. No grocery store (45 minutes to Rawlins); one church; the library was a single-wide trailer donated by BP America; most permanent residents are over 50. Residents say a dependable vehicle and knowing your neighbors matter (Cowboy State Daily, 2026-10-10, SOURCED).
- **Rawlins housing [K9]:** in early 2023 Cowboy State Daily described a housing crunch in Rawlins (about 5,000 people per the article) where affordable options other than hotel rooms were key to recruiting for the wind and oil-and-gas sectors (snippet, UNVERIFIED); a developer cited Rawlins' Title 16 ordinance as a reason not to build an RV park (Bigfoot99 snippet, UNVERIFIED). The Chokecherry and Sierra Madre wind project about 10 miles south of Rawlins (219,707 acres) was projected to need up to 1,000 construction workers at peak and at least 114 permanent operations jobs, mostly turbine technicians; the developer asked the state to reduce the projected cumulative workforce in the area; the 2% lodging tax stops applying after a 30-day stay (BLM release and Torrington Telegram snippets, UNVERIFIED; Torrington Telegram page refused 403).

### B. Subcultures
- **Long-haul truckers (majority of traffic) [K4] [P9]:** WYDOT's 2023 statistics to a legislative committee (SOURCED, read from saved PDF): weighted average daily traffic 13,828; semis (5+ axles) 46.4% eastbound and 46.5% westbound, nearly all non-resident (resident semis 1.1% and 0.8%); non-resident traffic overall 84.9% eastbound, 91.3% westbound; 2018-2022: 8,105 crashes (1,621 a year; 518 in work zones; about 12% of all crashes in the state), 4,106 commercial-vehicle crashes (54% weather-related, 78% on ice/frost/snow, 35% involving speeding, 173 involving wildlife, **about 90% involving an out-of-state driver's license**); 239 blowover crashes (122 heavy trucks, 64 pickups, 25 medium trucks, 28 other); I-80 spending averaged $68.39M of $756.92M total WYDOT spending (2016-2022, about 9%). [K9]
- **Sikh truckers and a Sikh-owned truck stop [K4, K10, K12] [P9]:** a Sikh owner-operator who came to the U.S. in 1999 bought the A&C Truck Stop off I-80 outside Laramie; he converted a former fireworks stand into a one-room Sikh temple that drivers use regularly, and runs an Indian restaurant with a clay oven; drivers keep uncut hair and turbans at work, which trucking allows; Sikhs PAC estimated an 18,000 rise in Sikh truckers in 2017; during a May snowstorm the temple sheltered about 20 motorcyclists bound for a funeral in Iowa overnight (Wyoming Public Radio, Maggie Mullen, 2019-01-18, SOURCED). The same owner's company (Akal) later ran the Akal Travel Center at Laramie exit 290 (28 acres, over 200 truck spaces after a remodel) plus stops on I-40 and I-80 elsewhere and a small tanker fleet; it also owns the truck stop and restaurant at Quealy Dome (Overdrive and Ten-4 snippets, UNVERIFIED; Overdrive not fetched, site refused in R1; Quealy Dome ownership SOURCED in Cowboy State Daily 2023-09-20). In September 2026 a Sikh truck driver was stabbed at the Bitter Creek rest area on I-80; police said the motive was unknown (AP-syndicated snippets, UNVERIFIED). [K9]
- **Truck-stop ministry [K10, K6] [P6]:** the Coffee Pot Bible Fellowship, a small chapel building in the Sapp Bros. truck stop lot in Cheyenne (I-80's eastern Wyoming city), run for five years (to 2025) by a former long-haul driver and his wife as a branch of Faith Baptist Church; its main rule is always to have free coffee ready; services Sunday morning and evening and Wednesday to Friday evenings, livestreamed; the chaplain calls drivers over CB on Sunday mornings and the cashier announces services over the PA; attendance ranges from none to about 20; drivers raise loneliness, pay-by-the-mile pressure, dispatchers, stimulant use and gambling; the chaplain once told a driver asking whether to keep going under a high-wind warning to park, which he did despite his dispatcher; at least 100 truck-stop chapels exist in the U.S. (Cowboy State Daily, 2025-05-03, SOURCED). - **Sinclair, a company town on the corridor [K4, K8] [P8]:** built 1924-25 by the Producers and Refiners Corporation (founder Frank Kistler) on a former sheep ranch, designed by Denver architects Fisher & Fisher in Spanish Colonial Revival style around the Parco Inn; sold to Consolidated Oil (later Sinclair) in 1934; renamed Sinclair in 1942; the refining company sold the townsite and buildings to residents in 1967; National Register historic district (search snippets of WyoSHPO, SAH Archipedia, Wikipedia, UNVERIFIED). A Rawlins official named Sinclair for its green chile (Cowboy State Daily 2026-04-18, SOURCED as a recommendation). [K12]
- **Corrections:** the old territorial/state prison outside Rawlins took its first prisoners on December 12, 1901 (moved from Laramie); it was described as a stabilizing influence on the railroad town; replaced by a new facility south of town in 1981; renamed the Wyoming Frontier Prison museum in 1988 (WyoSHPO and other snippets, UNVERIFIED). Life of corrections staff as a group: NOT FOUND.
- **Indigenous history [P9] [K9]:** in the 19th century the Laramie Plains were used by several nations, not held by one: Northern Arapaho, Northern Cheyenne, Oglala Lakota, Eastern Shoshone and White River Ute; the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 left the Northern Arapaho without a land base, and by 1878 they were placed with the Shoshone on the Wind River Reservation (search snippets of Wikipedia/Visit Laramie, UNVERIFIED). Fort Halleck was set up near Elk Mountain in fall 1862 to guard the Overland Trail (11th Ohio Cavalry). Attacks tied to hostilities with the Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho hit the trail's stations west of the plains from May 26 to June 22, 1865 (Bridger Pass, Sage Creek, Pine Grove, Sulphur Springs, Washakie), and from April 1 to August 15, 1867 the line reportedly lost 350 head of stock, 12 stations burned, three coaches destroyed and 13 employees killed; the 1867 Peace Commission treaties largely ended attacks by late 1868; at the peak three to five soldiers and an NCO were posted at exposed stations. Stages ran year-round; a February 1867 *New York Times* account told of a driver in a blizzard who drove onto a station barn's roof (WyoHistory, David Johnson, 2022-10-18, SOURCED).

### C. Leisure industries and what locals do
- **Hot springs as a free public soak [K10, K6] [P4]:** the Hobo Pool in Saratoga (Carbon County, about 20 miles south of I-80 at Walcott Junction) is free and open around the clock. The west-side springs were improved by the Civilian Conservation Corps in 1936; local legend says Depression-era transients riding the Saratoga-Encampment Railroad beside the pools jumped off to bathe; a bathhouse came after WWII; when the State sold 404 acres of the Hot Springs Reserve to private investors (1949), the remaining 16 acres, including the pools, went to the Town of Saratoga with a deed requiring them to stay "open and free to the public forever"; the pools run about 105-117°F and are used in midwinter (108°F water at -15°F air, January 24, 2026); locals greet newcomers by asking whether they fish, and then whether they have been to the hot springs; the Saratoga Museum opened an exhibit on the pools (WyoHistory, Justin Horn, Saratoga Museum director, 2026-05-17, SOURCED). A local essay gives posted rules: no alcohol, drugs, smoking or tobacco, and swimsuits required (Saratoga Sun 2019-11-06 snippet, UNVERIFIED). Other sources date the town's purchase to 1984 (Cowboy State Daily 2023 snippet, UNVERIFIED; conflicts with the 1949 deed account).
- **Rawlins tries to turn a pass-through into a stop [K3, K8]:** the Rawlins Downtown Development Authority runs a campaign with neighboring communities to convert passing I-80 travelers into overnight guests, built on the crossroads of the Union Pacific, the Lincoln Highway, the transcontinental airmail route and modern trails; the Continental Divide National Scenic Trail runs through downtown Rawlins (one of three towns where it does), about 10 miles from the trail's halfway point; the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route and TransAmerica Bicycle Trail pass nearby; hikers stop for meals and showers; attractions named: a downtown mural tour, the prison tour, the local museum, the Ferris Mansion, a trail on the Rawlins Uplift, rockhounding for jade, agate and petrified wood (Cowboy State Daily, 2026-04-18, SOURCED).
- **Highway seasons nearby [K2, K5]:** WYO 130 over the Snowy Range (Laramie-Saratoga) closes every fall when deep snow makes plowing impractical (closed November 10, 2022; November 11, 2021; November 14, 2020; October 29, 2019) and reopens with a target of Memorial Day weekend (May 26, 2022; May 21, 2021; May 18, 2020; May 25, 2019); Laramie and Saratoga crews clear from each side from late April with rotary plows and snowcats through drifts up to 15 ft (WYDOT release snippets; WYDOT pages fail with an expired certificate; UNVERIFIED). No public event tied to the opening was found.
- **Bars as long-hours places [K10]:** in Wamsutter the Desert Bar, owned by the same woman since 1980, opens at 4 a.m. every day (Cowboy State Daily 2026-10-10, SOURCED). In Laramie the Buckhorn Bar & Parlor (building on a site of an 1860s-70s tent hall; bar in place since after Prohibition; National Register; listed for sale in 2023) is known as a college and travelers' bar (snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- Hunting, fishing and outdoor recreation in the Sierra Madre, Ferris Mountains, North Platte River, Seminoe Reservoir and Red Desert (Cowboy State Daily 2026-04-18, SOURCED as a list).

### D. Third places
- **Jeffrey Community Center, Rawlins [K10, K5]:** used as the warming shelter in the May 18-19, 2026 storm and outage: over 45 people, mostly families and elderly residents, a mix of stranded motorists and townspeople without heat; Memorial Hospital of Carbon County stayed open for people needing oxygen or power for medical devices; Carbon County Search and Rescue was mobilized to transport them; the county's incident command post ran at the dispatch center from 7:30 a.m. Monday to 7 a.m. Tuesday (Cowboy State Daily 2026-05-19, SOURCED).
- **Rock Springs during a closure [K5, K4]:** when I-80 shuts for two or three days, the city tells truckers where to park (normally the Sweetwater Events Complex; when it is full, a recreation facility and other sites) and tells food vendors where to set up; emergency management, fire, police, city staff and events-complex staff coordinate; community members brought meals to drivers; the mayor said rolling closures spread trucks across towns so none is overwhelmed and bring the city sales-tax revenue; a 36-year driver wrote a public letter of thanks after being stuck nearly three days in December 2021 (Cowboy State Daily, 2021-12-22, SOURCED).
- **Truck parking as waiting room [K5]:** the Quealy Dome lot (about 20 miles west of Laramie, about 90 big rigs) and a Fort Steele lot were part of a $27M project with hill-climbing lanes, about 80% federally funded; before the lots, trucks waited out storms on shoulders and on-ramps, which the Highway Patrol said complicated a high-speed freeway; a driver described wind-blown snow when he "can't see past the hood" and using rumble strips to find the road edge (Cowboy State Daily, 2023-09-20, SOURCED). WYDOT's 2020 closure of 10 rest areas cost about 80 truck spaces (Land Line snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Other:** the A&C/Akal truck stop temple and the Cheyenne chapel (B above); the Hobo Pool (C above).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Halfway Days, Rawlins (K3, created on purpose) [P6]:** first held July 10-12, 2026; created by the Rawlins Downtown Development Authority as its first Continental Divide Trail event, growing out of the previous year's AdventureFest within its annual SummerFest; celebrates the town's place at the trail's midpoint; the Continental Divide Trail Coalition helped with trail work beforehand (debris removal, a new water station); over 50 volunteers and 40 sponsors; six nonprofits fundraised; over 4,000 attended; the 2027 event is being planned (Bigfoot99, 2026-08-06, SOURCED; volunteer and sponsor counts from snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Volunteer rescue as a standing institution [K1, K14] [SM:competence under pressure] [R]:** in the February 2023 storm, Rawlins Search and Rescue, Valley Search and Rescue, Baggs Fire Rescue, Classic Air, County Road and Bridge, the Highway Patrol, WYDOT, county emergency management and the sheriff's office worked Tuesday to Friday; Carbon County's Hagglund tracked vehicle (about $180,000, articulated, amphibious, at highway speed) was the primary rescue tool, and Laramie County lent a second one; one team took about four hours to travel 15 miles through six-foot drifts on the interstate; a 94-year-old woman was carried from an RV to a hotel; hospital staff were shuttled to work; the senior center got meals to vulnerable adults with volunteers; the county issued a "no unnecessary travel" advisory and commissioners declared an emergency so state resources could flow (Cowboy State Daily, Wendy Corr, 2023-02-26, SOURCED).
- **Citizen road reporters [K1, K14]:** ECAR began as a pilot in 2006; about 300 volunteers by 2012; trained first by WYDOT staff on what to report; they phone in real-time hazards, compared by WYDOT to National Weather Service storm spotters; WYDOT asked for more in 2012 to help prioritize plow and crew dispatch (K2 Radio, 2012-11-23, SOURCED).

### K6 · Everyday behavior rules
- **At the gate:** a trooper works the gate; to drive a closed road a driver needs clearance through WYDOT's Authorized Travel Program or the trooper's permission; WYDOT tries to accommodate local traffic, so drivers may see others pass the gate; fine up to $750 (Laramie Live, 2023-02-22, quoting WYDOT, SOURCED). Before gates, WYDOT staff stood on the highway at the closure point waving stop signs, which WYDOT called a waste of manpower and unsafe; hundreds of motorists were stranded on the road from 1970 to 1973 (Cowboy State Daily 2021-09-09, SOURCED).
- **Who decides:** WYDOT's director (2020): roads close only for visibility or crashes, as a last resort; WYDOT's operations chief: sensor data informs closures but field personnel make the final call (Cowboy State Daily 2020-03-04, SOURCED). This partly closes R1's gap; the physical routine of lowering a field gate remains NOT FOUND.
- **Stay with the vehicle:** tow operators on I-80 say stranded drivers often walk away for help and become disoriented, get hit in pileups or freeze; one suggested tying a rope between yourself and the car if you must leave; drivers may wait two or three hours for help in wind and blowing snow; impatience causes the worst wrecks; drivers are warned not to come up fast behind plows (Cowboy State Daily, 2024-12-29, SOURCED). A base safety article advises not leaving the vehicle unless help is visible within 100 yards (Warren AFB snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Preparation:** WYDOT's winter guide recommends a survival kit in every vehicle and checking 511/wyoroad.info before leaving (WYDOT snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Unwritten rules:** a reported local custom of greeting newcomers with questions about fishing and the hot springs (Saratoga, SOURCED above); a norm of strangers helping each other during closures appears in news accounts (R1; Rock Springs letter above) but no source states it as a rule. No other unwritten rule found.

### A. Other settings
- **The summit without services [K5, K9]:** the summit of Rogers Pass once had a 50-room hotel at affordable prices and a service station with a convenience store, both run under contract with Parks Canada; the station closed in 2009 and the lodge in 2012 after the owners' financial difficulties; after a long legal process Parks Canada took the buildings and announced demolition (site contaminated from its rail-yard past); backcountry users must drive about 45 minutes to Revelstoke or an hour to Golden for food, fuel or a bed. A skier-led "Revive Rogers" survey (November 2016) had 114 responses in its first week: 72% named lack of lodging as a barrier, 94% wanted lodging rebuilt; the park superintendent said a future facility might give travelers a place to wait out road closures (Backcountry Magazine, Amos Horn, 2017-01-10, SOURCED). - **Huts [K10] [P4]:** the Alpine Club of Canada's A. O. ### B. Subcultures
- **Forecasters and gunners at work [K14, K7] [SM:competence; dangerous system]:** Parks Canada's forecasting team combines telemetry weather stations with daily snow profiles dug by avalanche technicians and observations from driving the highway; it decides when to close the highway for control work and usually announces closures as early as possible, often the night before, on DriveBC; the senior avalanche officer described the aim as "more frequent and smaller avalanches" that stop short of the road. During a mission, gun teams set up at two of 17 permanent positions while traffic waits in holding areas; the gunner sights the target and **the lead forecaster gives the order to fire**; the forecaster then moves to the second gun team while the first relocates; forecasters also fire remote-controlled explosives at the east and west edges of the pass; Armed Forces members clear debris before reopening; a three-year project was adding 13 infrasound and radar arrays to detect avalanches in near real time (Mountain Sledder, Cassidy Randall, 2021-11-19, SOURCED). The guns are 105 mm C3 howitzers modified for precision firing from roadside platforms, brought west from CFB Shilo (1st Regiment, Royal Canadian Horse Artillery, augmented by Reserve units), firing at registered targets 3-5 km away; highway and railway close before each session and reopen after snow removal (Revelstoke Current, 2019-11-16, SOURCED). Living arrangements of the gunners: NOT FOUND beyond R1's snippet.
- **The national snow-safety network, run from Revelstoke [K1, K14] [R]:** the Canadian Avalanche Association's Industry Information Exchange (InfoEx) is a **confidential** daily exchange of snow, weather, avalanche and terrain observations among subscribing operations (backcountry guiding, ski areas, highway and railway avalanche programs, consultants, individual guides); it **began in 1991 on a BC Coroner's Service recommendation after a commercial avalanche fatality**; all subscribers are expected to contribute regularly during their operating season and must have a professional CAA member on staff so submissions meet the Observation Guidelines and Recording Standards (OGRS, 2007); about 130 operations contributed in peak winter months (2014); it began by fax, with a CAA technician compiling submissions nightly into a report faxed back to subscribers, moved to email in the late 1990s, a database (InfoEx 2.0) after 2003, and a geospatial system (InfoEx 3.0) from December 1, 2013; it is the data cornerstone for Avalanche Canada's public bulletins (Haegeli et al., ISSW 2014 proceedings, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). Avalanche Canada says each on-duty forecaster in its Revelstoke office reviews about 10,000 data points a day to produce forecasts for four regions, drawing mostly on InfoEx plus public Mountain Information Network reports, remote stations and the Meteorological Service of Canada (Avalanche Canada interview, snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Sledders [K14, K10] [P4]:** the Revelstoke Snowmobile Club (about five decades old in 2016) holds a trail management agreement with the province and maintains riding areas including Boulder Mountain and Frisby Ridge, each with a day cabin built and updated by members (the Frisby cabin is also an emergency shelter and search-and-rescue staging area); trail fee $20 a day; 2016-17 memberships $180 single, $270 family, $30 child, $75 senior, $15 non-rider, all including the BC Snowmobile Federation; annual events: an avalanche awareness day (January), a youth day (February), a vintage ride, snow drags and a ladies' ride (spring) (Sno Riders West, 2016-10-03, SOURCED). Snowmobile tourism was estimated at $31.2M a year to Revelstoke; the Frisby Ridge area was once shared with cross-country skiers until a voluntary closure was negotiated (snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Backcountry skiers under a permit regime [K6]:** see K6 below.
- **Italian community [P9]:** Italian names first appear on the Revelstoke school register in 1893; those of 1897-99 all worked for the CPR; the Italian population was about 400 by 1905; women's lives centered on home and church and many learned English only when their children started school (Stoke Histories, Leonie Heinz, undated student-history site, read; treated as UNVERIFIED because the page contains clear historical errors elsewhere). Italian immigrants later moved into shopkeeping, contracting, farming and trade; the first mayor of Italian descent served 1934-36 (Revelstoke Review snippet, UNVERIFIED). An Italian immigrant died building a railway tunnel near Revelstoke in 1915 (Vancouver Is Awesome snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Chinese community [K9] [P9]:** by 1900 Revelstoke's Chinese community was concentrated on two blocks around Front Street, Benson and King, with two grocery stores, a laundry and a temple (Revelstoke Mountaineer timeline snippet, UNVERIFIED); a merchant who came to build the CPR became a successful entrepreneur and was secretary of the Revelstoke chapter of the Chinese Freemasons in the 1910s, when about 100 Chinese lived in town (CBC snippets; page refused 403; UNVERIFIED). This partly closes R1's Chinatown gap.
- **Ukrainian internees, 1915 [K9] [P9]:** from September 6 to December 20, 1915 an internment camp halfway up Mount Revelstoke held 225 internees and 100 guards in log buildings; most internees were Ukrainian immigrants from Galicia and Bukovina (Austrian passports) who had come for railway, forestry or mining work, many interned because they were unemployed, lacked papers or had not reported to police; some Revelstoke citizens had asked that internees finish the summit road, and the parks commissioner agreed; weather and labor disputes meant they mostly cleared snow and cut firewood; the camp's winter water supply was inadequate; internees were moved to Yoho in December 1915; an interpretive station marks the site (Parks Canada, Mount Revelstoke internment camp page, SOURCED).
- **Japanese Canadian road camps, 1942-45 [K9] [P9]:** the Eagle Pass (Revelstoke-Sicamous) Highway Road Camp near Victor Lake was provincially recognized in 2017 and listed on the Canadian Register in 2021: Japanese Canadian men over 18 were sent by train from the Hastings Park collection center in Vancouver; those sent to Eagle Pass were mostly young or middle-aged Canadian-born and naturalized men; they cleared brush, burned slash, built culverts and hand-blasted rock to finish an undeveloped section of the Trans-Canada; the camps were guarded by armed RCMP and men needed permission to leave; they were separated from wives and children; food rations caused strikes and unrest; some men and boys died and others were injured; internees planted gardens, built bathhouses, bridges and baseball diamonds and played board games; from 1943 they could visit families in interior camps (Canadian Register of Historic Places, SOURCED). The men worked on 44.5 miles (71.6 km) of highway west of Revelstoke (marker snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **The 1910 dead and their memory [K9] [P6]:** the graves of the 32 Japanese victims of the March 4, 1910 avalanche were found at Mountain View Cemetery in **Vancouver** (not Revelstoke); researchers also traced 11 victims' families in Japan; a commemorative ceremony there on August 12, 2010 was supported by the National Nikkei Museum, the cemetery and Buddhist Temples of Canada, followed by events at Rogers Pass and Revelstoke on August 15, with four Japanese families expected; the research was led by a CAA professional member on the 1910 Rogers Pass Snow Slide Commemoration Committee and took over a year and a half (JCCA Bulletin, John Endo Greenaway, 2010-07-29, SOURCED). This corrects R1's implication that the Japanese graves were in Revelstoke.
- **Indigenous nations [P9] [K9]:** Revelstoke is described as the homeland of the Sinixt and within the territories of the Secwépemc, Syilx and Ktunaxa; the Sinixt territory runs from north of Revelstoke to Kettle Falls, Washington; the Hugh Keenleyside Dam (Castlegar, 1969) displaced about 2,000 settlers from the Columbia valley between Castlegar and Revelstoke; the Indigenous Friendship Society of Revelstoke formed in 2015 (Tourism Revelstoke, 2025-08-19, SOURCED). Canada declared the Arrow Lakes Band (Sinixt) extinct in 1956; in 2021 the Supreme Court of Canada (Desautel, 7-2) found that Sinixt people in the U.S. hold Aboriginal rights in Canada, though the extinction status under the Indian Act was not itself reversed (search snippets of CBC, Spokesman-Review, The Narwhal, UNVERIFIED). ## 3 · #20 Truckee and Donner Pass, California

**Round 2 focus:** the chain-installer trade (R1's main NOT FOUND), the Caltrans crew base at the summit, Truckee's historic and present neighborhoods, the workforce and its housing, the Washoe and the Italian community, volunteer search and rescue, the hospital district, a makerspace and repair clinics, schools and snow days, rules of everyday life (bears, rentals), and the festivals and third places R1 did not reach.

### A. Other settings
- **The summit crew base [K7, K14, K4] [SM:competence] [P4]:** Caltrans' Kingvale Maintenance Station (opened 1962) tends about 20 miles of I-80 between Truckee and Yuba Gap, where about 430 inches of snow fall a year; in a major storm **up to 100 workers take 12-hour shifts around the clock**, and the station's **82-room dormitory can house and feed 160 people**; two large repair and storage bays have heated floors (to melt ice off returning vehicles, keep brakes from freezing and moderate the temperature as vehicles come and go); covered sheds hold salt and sand; a communications center coordinates Caltrans radio, the changeable message signs and the public QuickMap feed; a pusher truck partnership with the California Trucking Association and CHP is stationed there; the station tests new equipment (a tow-plow clearing two lanes at once, a brine spreader); and a long-running training school there teaches Caltrans workers from around the state the latest snow-clearing techniques (Caltrans *Mile Marker*, March 2017, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). In 2019 the station hosted the third annual statewide equipment safety competition with 14 operators (Caltrans News snippet, UNVERIFIED). [R: a staffed base with bunks, galley and heated repair bays]
- **Historic Truckee's districts [K4, K9] [P3, P4]:** from the 1868 railroad on, Truckee was a lumber and ice boomtown; fires swept the residential and commercial districts in 1871, 1875, 1881 and 1883 (and the commercial district again in 1913 and the 1920s), so few early buildings survive; the largest homes belonged to lumber barons; the Truckee Lumber Company built "Brickelltown," a uniform block of employee houses with Italianate details along the west edge of town near its mill and box factory (named for a partner, E. J. Brickell); 15-20 dairies supplied summer pasture for valley herds and premium butter (the Varney-McIver dairy on Donner Pass Road is the only one left downtown); by 1878 the Chinese community was forced south of the river, and a decade later out altogether; **a large influx of Italian immigrants in the decline years after the 1880s filled the residential neighborhoods**; the Hilltop was bought from the railroad interests about 1910-1920 for skiing; its lodge (c. 1928-29) was built by members of the Truckee Ski Club, the Truckee Outing Club and the Sierra Dog Derby Association; a wooden ski jump (designed by a seven-time ski-jumping champion) stood there until the 1950s-60s; winter excursion trains ran until 1940; the Truckee Lumber Company closed after a fire and the last ice companies by the 1930s (Town of Truckee Historic Design Guidelines, Chapter 2 "Historic Overview," read from saved PDF, SOURCED). A Truckee Donner Historical Society historian wrote that much of Southeast River Street, where Chinatown had been moved, became an Italian area after the boycott (Sierra Sun, Gordon Richards, 2004-11-28, SOURCED; same source as R1, re-read only for this). This partly closes R1's gap on the Italian community; family origins and numbers remain NOT FOUND.
- **Tahoe Donner, a private town within the town [K4, K6]:** about 6,700 lots (about 86% built) on 7,300-7,700 acres; begun when land bought in the late 1960s was sold to investors in 1969 to become a homeowners' association; clubhouse 1971, its own downhill ski area 1972, tennis center 1972, campground 1973; some amenities are members-only (up to eight member IDs per property), others public for a fee; an amenity access rule adopted when under half the lots had homes was proposed for change in 2024 with a reservation system (Tahoe Donner association pages and snippets, UNVERIFIED). The association also publishes minimum standards for animal-resistant garbage enclosures (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **The summit settlements [P4]:** Soda Springs and Serene Lakes on Donner Summit (about 6,800 ft, outside town limits) get 400-500 inches of snow a year; about 40% of Soda Springs homes are said to be occupied year-round; some homes on dirt roads are walk-ins in winter; the county plows Serene Lakes' paved roads; a daily winter shuttle runs to the ski areas (real-estate pages, promotional, UNVERIFIED).
- **Schools in snow [K5, K6]:** the Tahoe Truckee Unified School District uses two-hour delays, minimum days and full snow days; full-district snow days were called in January 2023 and March 2024; **Truckee Elementary was closed on March 13, 2023 because snow load again exceeded the rating of older parts of the building**; after a run of snow closures in 2017 the superintendent proposed makeup classes during the February break the district calls Ski Skate Week (South Tahoe Now and Lake Tahoe News snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Hospital district [K1] [P7]:** the Tahoe Forest Hospital District was formed by local vote in April 1949, after a Rotary Club proposal and after a local man's wife died following childbirth complications that had forced a rush to Reno; he donated the land in memory of a son killed in WWII; a $400,000 bond built it; it opened April 14, 1952 with two doctors and 15 beds (replacing a small clinic in Brickelltown); a 42-bed wing followed in 1965; voters passed a $98.5M bond (Measure C) with 72% in 2007 (Sierra Booster, 75th-anniversary article, SOURCED). Today 25 acute and 36 long-term care beds (district snippet, UNVERIFIED).

### B. Subcultures
- **Chain installers [K14, K7, K8] [SM:competence] [R]:** independent contractors working under Caltrans, who keeps prices uniform across District 3: $5 to install in 1977, $20 in 2001-2003, $30 from 2013 to 2023, **$40 from the 2024-25 season** (removal $30; big rigs $40 per wheel to install and $30 to remove); a veteran who started in the 1977-78 season said the rise was the first in over a decade, driven by fuel and living costs, and that customers pay so they "don't have to get out" in the cold (KCRA, 2024-10-31, SOURCED). In a 2001 dry winter about a dozen installers worked a blizzard at Meyers (Highway 50); one, chaining since 1986 with a summer job as a youth counselor, said his income was down about 90% and the old thousand-dollar days were gone (a couple hundred dollars on a good day); another, a 22-year veteran and house painter, said chaining cannot be a sole income; SUVs and snow-rated tires had cut demand, though rental-car tourists still needed them (Tahoe Daily Tribune, Mary Thompson, 2001-12-19, SOURCED). In January 2017 an I-80 installer said he had lost count of cars chained in 48 hours; many customers pay to avoid being wet for hours (CBS Sacramento, 2017-01-04, SOURCED). Truck chains bought at the roadside can cost $600-700 (CBS snippet, UNVERIFIED). This closes R1's main gap on pay and status; the number of installers at Truckee remains NOT FOUND.
- **The workforce and its housing [K4, K9] [P9]:** Sierra Community House, formed about 2019 by the merger of four long-standing nonprofits, runs hunger relief, legal aid, family strengthening and domestic-violence support from five offices across North Tahoe and Truckee; in five years it gave nearly 100,000 services to over 7,000 households, $2M in rental assistance and 1.6 million meals; it is funded partly by a local fund ($400,000) and by the North Lake Tahoe tourism improvement district and Placer County hotel tax ($900,000 over three years); about **500 mobile homes housing local workers are at risk of conversion** to non-affordable housing, and the group urges mobile-home and rent-stabilization protections (Sierra Sun, Zoe Meyer, 2024-06-30, SOURCED). Census figures put Truckee's Hispanic or Latino share at 13.2% (2019-2023 ACS) and 10.1% foreign-born; the North Tahoe-Truckee region's Hispanic share was 20% in 2010 and 16% in 2020 (Census QuickFacts and Clear Impact snippets, UNVERIFIED; different products, not directly comparable). ## 4 · #24 CB radio trucker culture (1970s)

**Round 2 focus:** a tradition rather than a place, so Round 2 looks for its settings (home base stations, truck stops, patrol cars, clubs), its subcultures (women drivers, Black CB networks, emergency-monitor teams, owner-operator politics, hobbyists, a first lady), its music, its gatherings, and its rules. ### A. Other settings
- **The patrol car as a listening post [K1, K5] [P7]:** the Missouri State Highway Patrol began CB operation in August 1975; in its first three months (August-October 1975) officers logged **22,489 CB contacts with citizens**: 17,277 requests for assistance or reports of dangerous road conditions (including **3,859 reports of stranded motorists** and 2,980 of traffic accidents), 5,212 reports of traffic and criminal violations (leading to 1,307 arrests and 1,006 warnings) and 113 reports of felonies (52 arrests); examples include a CB user who followed a robbery car until troopers stopped it, a CB user who directed officers to two jail escapees, and truckers asked by CB to report hitchhikers near a stolen wreck (Missouri State Highway Patrol news release, reprinted in *ALERT 44* magazine, Vol. 6 No. 1, held in the Frances K. - **The home base station and the household [K10] [P4]:** in 1963 TIME described the Citizens Band as used by doctors, taxi fleets, contractors and farm families, with households using car-to-home calls and children talking for hours; over 350,000 licenses then, about 10,000 new applications a month, no technical test required, and as many as a dozen units per license; a complete set cost about $300; the FCC proposed in 1963 to cut talk to three minutes and reserve most channels for single-station use (TIME, "Hobbies: What Citizens Have Wrought," 1963-01-11, SOURCED; R1 cited the same article for the 150-mile limit). A 1974 Tampa Bay Times item described towns with 24-hour volunteer monitors on the emergency channel, in Pinellas County the REACT team; a 1977 Houston Chronicle item described a mother who monitored the emergency channel in the early morning while making her children's lunches; a 1977 North Carolina weekly described CB as a family hobby (children doing homework, mothers exchanging recipes, fathers doing business) (search snippets; pages not readable; UNVERIFIED).

### B. Subcultures
- **Volunteer emergency teams beyond REACT [K1, K14] [R]:** ALERT, Inc. was a national association "dedicated to the betterment of Citizen Two-Way Radio operation," headquartered in the National Press Building, Washington, D.C.; annual dues $9.50 including its quarterly magazine; it organized numbered local teams (examples: Durham, N.C.; Ellis County and El Paso, Texas; Des Moines, Iowa); team reports from about 1975-76 describe monitoring base control, getting an ambulance to a five-vehicle accident in front of a country store, assisting in three missing-person searches, directing traffic and guarding against looting after a tornado at a city police request, reporting 151 accidents with injuries and 63 disabled vehicles in a period, raising money at charity gatherings (one raised $843 for muscular dystrophy and Eastern Cherokee legal services) and for injured members; teams relayed a coast-to-coast model-airplane flight from town to town by CB in the 1975-76 Bicentennial (*ALERT 44*, Vol. 6 No. 1, Ford Library scan, SOURCED). In the same issue an FCC official told a citizens' radio council that the Commission was no longer monitoring communications for content but would concentrate on major violations (failure to identify, excess power, out-of-band operation, obscenity, malicious interference) and urged users to protect the emergency channel (same, SOURCED). A REACT president said teams had arrangements with local authorities to monitor the emergency channel when volunteers were off, and claimed an estimated sixty million emergency calls since 1962, about fourteen million of them highway accidents (Illinois News Index snippet, UNVERIFIED). NHTSA's National Emergency Aid Radio (NEAR) program trained volunteer and public-safety monitors on channel 9 and, from about 1977, let states use Section 402 highway-safety grants for it; a 1977 SAE paper reviewed Ohio REACT, a Detroit REACT program, the Missouri Highway Patrol experience and NEAR (snippets; the NHTSA/ROSA P and NCJRS documents were too large to read; UNVERIFIED).
- **Women drivers [K4, K9] [P9]:** in April 1976 TIME profiled a 33-year-old Los Angeles driver, a mother of three, who had just won a $6,000 Fair Employment Practices Commission settlement from a winery that refused to hire her as a driver because she was a woman; she led the 150-member Coalition of Women Truck Drivers, an offshoot of the Los Angeles chapter of the National Organization for Women, with cells in Dallas, Atlanta and central California; reported conditions: "men only" signs at some docks, many truck stops refusing women the showers, women losing jobs to younger men, and an alleged demand for sex from foremen or male drivers as a condition of hiring (a union local president said he had never heard of it); the coalition demanded separate sleeping rooms on overnight stops, relay driving (one driver per stretch), on-the-job training, no extra tests for state-licensed women, and adjustable seats and pedals; a grocery wholesaler's terminal manager cited heavy lifting as the reason it had been reluctant to hire women (TIME, "The Sexes: Women Truckers," 1976-04-26, SOURCED). The article does not mention CB. Her group's first campaign was the lack of women's restrooms at truck stops; she died in 2015 as a pedestrian struck by a vehicle (Women In Trucking and Overdrive snippets, UNVERIFIED). Women on CB specifically: still NOT FOUND beyond R1's line and the base-station items above.
- **Black CB networks [K4] [P9]:** in addition to R1: a keynote at the Rooster Channel Jumpers' convention urged Black CB users to form a national radio operators' organization for economic organizing, which Blake argues sat uneasily with members' regular disregard of FCC rules; members commonly sent signals by ionospheric skip far beyond the 150-mile limit (JSTOR Daily summary of Blake 2011, snippets, UNVERIFIED; the full article was not read).
- **A first lady on the air [K+: political use]:** in 1976, limited in campaigning by an injury, Betty Ford was given a CB by her daughter and licensed by the FCC (amid questions about how fast her license came through a backlogged FCC); in late April 1976, her motorcade passing through San Antonio, she talked on channel 12 with a nearby user; coverage during the Texas primary focused on jargon and jokes and some thought CB "beneath her"; Texas led the nation in CB licenses, followed by Pennsylvania, Ohio and Illinois; more than 10 million licenses had been issued by 1976 and one in three cars and three in four trucks had a set; a Ford Library curator called the craze blue-collar, "more a common-folk thing"; CB also spread word of counter-protests against school busing in the North, and in the mid-1960s Congress considered revoking licenses held by Ku Klux Klan members (The Bitter Southerner, Gabe Bullard, 2019-05-16, SOURCED).
- **Owner-operators and their politics [K8, K9] [SM:man vs society]:** under the Motor Carrier Act of 1935, trucks hauling unprocessed farm products were exempt from rate regulation, and independents worked in that niche on federally built highways; *Overdrive*'s publisher, a former driver, turned the magazine toward deregulation after the 1973-74 strike and founded the Independent Truckers Association in 1975, which tens of thousands joined (JSTOR Daily, Livia Gershon, 2017-12-01, SOURCED; partly in R1). In June 1979, with the Iranian Revolution deepening the fuel crisis, about 75,000 truckers struck over diesel prices and supply; the shutdown lasted two weeks to a month; contemporary reports in its third week told of shootings, truck burnings and booby-trapped roads; it ended with a 6% fuel surcharge and amnesty for participants (search snippets of Landline, Michigan Daily, Overdrive and CSM; pages refused; UNVERIFIED). The Motor Carrier Act of 1980 removed rate and entry oversight and ended the agricultural exemption; later research found driver earnings fell about 30% and workweeks exceeded 65 hours (LA Review of Books, Llewellyn Hinkes-Jones, 2014-11-25, SOURCED, citing Michael Belzer).

## 5 · #16 The Milepost (Alaska Highway travel guide)

**Round 2 focus:** how the guide is made today (R1's gap on field editors and mileage), the online community that now shares road knowledge beside it, the people along the road (lodge families and their turnover, maintenance camps, First Nations communities and what the highway did to them, Dawson Creek's wartime boom), the road's festivals created on purpose and tied to place (Whitehorse's Rendezvous, the Teslin Tlingit gathering, Tok's sled-dog race, Delta's fair), memorials to the Black regiments, and winter travel norms.

### A. Other settings
- **How the guide is made now [K14] [SM:competence]:** the managing editor (an Alaska resident since spring 2021) and her husband work as a husband-and-wife field team; the team logs about **15,000 miles a year** (the couple about 8,000 of them); editors carry each section as a document and check it mile by mile against the road (pull-offs, restaurants, attractions, campgrounds, prices), email visitor centers and towns each spring, and use the ferries for hard-to-reach places; every one of the 640 pages is reviewed each year; work runs a year ahead (one-third through the 2027 edition by August 2026), editing ends by Christmas, press in January, release in March; pages can be added only in multiples of eight; the 2026 edition was the 78th; a former field editor (a tour guide) reported a new pull-off near Whitehorse for the next edition (Truck Camper Magazine, Angela White, 2026-08-25, SOURCED). - **Maintenance camps [K7, K4] [P4]:** after construction, most construction camps became maintenance camps with fewer staff; camps averaged about 40 miles apart with 11 employees, plus about eight more for accommodation and fuel service (Yukon history snippets, UNVERIFIED). Canada took over the Yukon portion on April 1, 1946 under the army's Northwest Highway System, with camps at places such as Brooks Brook, Canyon and Koidern; by the late 1950s workers and their families were increasingly concentrated in centers such as Haines Junction and Whitehorse and finally a Whitehorse enclave (Cruikshank, below, SOURCED). - **Lodges and their turnover [K10, K9] [P4]:** an ongoing ExploreNorth register of lodges between historic Miles 496 and 872 (first posted 2016, updated to 2024) shows family operation (named husband-and-wife owners at nearly every lodge), frequent sales, fires (Liard River Lodge burned 2015; Rancheria main building 1969; Message Post 1986; Lower Post buildings 1986 after years abandoned; Teslin Inn 1956; Morley River 1952), and closures (Swift River closed 2009 and was demolished 2016; Morley River closed 2005; Fireside closed after 2015; Rancheria closed in fall 2018 after both operators died that year); some advertised 24-hour, year-round service in 1971 (Iron Creek, Lower Post, Transport Cafe), others were May-September only (Coal River in 2019); the lodge at the Liard hot springs was sold in 2003 to the Fort Nelson First Nation; one lodge was run by the same couple for 27 years from 1965 (ExploreNorth, "Alaska Highway lodges" part 2, read to 100,000 of 117,530 characters, SOURCED). The Northern Rockies Lodge at Muncho Lake is open year-round, run by a Swiss-born family in Canada since 1979, which added winter aurora tours in 2023 (syndicated feature and lodge pages, snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Dawson Creek's wartime boom [K9, K5] [P4]:** on March 9, 1942, 94 U.S. Army Quartermaster Corps members arrived by train, the first of about 3,000 engineers; the town of about 500 passed 3,000 quickly, with tent camps, a railhead camp, warehouses and a LORAN station; on **February 13, 1943 a fire and explosion in a livery barn holding explosives killed five, injured 150 and destroyed a business block**; the RCAF camp closed on December 15, 1946; military buildings were reused (a Catholic school in 1945, an Elks hall in a former U.S. mess hall, a "camp school" 1946-47); population 518 (1941), 3,539 (1951) (South Peace Historical Society, "Highlights of local history 1930-1990," SOURCED). This closes R1's NOT FOUND on the explosion.
- **Fort Nelson (mid-road) [K9]:** 2021 census 2,611, down 22.5% from 2016; contraction after low natural-gas prices and mill problems, with falling school enrollment and foreclosures; the municipality and Fort Nelson First Nation have tried to revive forestry; a regional official said the town still does "OK" as a transportation community (CBC, UNBC and other snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Delta Junction (the end) [P4]:** Tanana Athabascan people occupied the area in the 19th and early 20th centuries; bison were transplanted in the 1920s (the place was called Buffalo Center from 1927); Fort Greely was built about 5 miles south; a dairy began in 1946; the 1974-77 pipeline brought a boom; the state's Delta Agricultural Project I (1978) created 22 barley farms averaging 2,700 acres, sold by lottery with state loans, and Delta II (1982) 15 more; the 70,000-acre Delta Bison Range was created in 1980 to keep bison out of the barley (ExploreNorth "Deltana" history, from the Alaska community database, SOURCED). The Deltana Fair began in 1980 per one source (snippet, UNVERIFIED).

### B. Subcultures
- **First Nations and the highway [K9, K4] [P9]:** anthropologist Julie Cruikshank's paper (written about 1982 from church, government and military records and oral accounts recorded since 1974; revised from testimony to an inquiry of May 5, 1976) gives the fullest account found (Cruikshank, "The Gravel Magnet: Some Social Impacts of the Alaska Highway on Yukon Indians," PDF hosted by UAF Project Jukebox, read from saved PDF, SOURCED):
  - Before 1942 southern Yukon families moved over large areas in a yearly round (salmon in summer, meat in fall, less movement in winter, trapping in spring) within a matrilineal Wolf/Crow moiety system that governed marriage and obligations.
  - 34,637 men came in a year and a half; Whitehorse rose from 754 to about 20,000; line camps of 100-200 men stood every 10-15 miles. Indigenous guides determined much of the actual route of the pioneer road. Older people call it the second "rush."
  - In 1942, with fur prices low, many families at Teslin, Champagne and Burwash Landing did not trap and stayed near posts or went to Whitehorse for work; new permanent villages (Watson Lake, Haines Junction, Beaver Creek, Pelly Crossing) and the growth of others are attributed to the road; river villages emptied after riverboats stopped in the early 1950s.
  - U.S. soldiers and workers received resident hunting licenses; elders report moose shot for sport and left; a game sanctuary of about 10,000 square miles at Kluane (December 1942/1943) banned all hunting and trapping, which an Indian Affairs official at the time admitted made Indigenous hunters "bear the brunt" of depletion caused mainly by white and army hunting; a two-mile no-shooting zone along the highway was authorized; the Haines Road cut off fishing places at Dalton Post and Klukshu; registered traplines ignored matrilineal inheritance.
  - Women earned cash doing laundry, cleaning camps and sewing for soldiers (one earned $3,500 in laundry April-November 1942); the army bought sled dogs, leaving families without teams when they returned to trapping; cars bought in 1943 broke down and led to accidents.
  - **Epidemics:** in winter 1942-43 families waiting for jobs at Teslin were struck by measles, dysentery, jaundice, whooping cough, mumps, tonsillitis and meningitis, affecting only the Indigenous population; deaths at McDame, Telegraph Creek, Champagne and Ross River; a family leaving Whitehorse lost three children in one day; construction workers stopped work to build caskets for the children; a tuberculosis X-ray clinic toured the highway 1947-49.
  - Schools rose from two to eight and Indigenous students from 26 to 227 (1942-49); family allowances (1944-45) were tied to school registration; mothers had to stay in villages, changing trapping life; "the government became my wife's old man," one husband said.
  - Liquor charges against Indigenous people rose from 11 to 106 (1936-48); soldiers took items from grave houses and cabins as souvenirs; Indigenous offenders, including a 12-year-old, received year-long sentences; camps destroyed surplus food rather than give it away (to avoid competing with local business), remembered as "potholes filled with hams"; abuses included two soldiers court-martialled for night visits to the girls' dormitory of the Carcross residential school. Equal liquor rights for First Nations in Yukon came only in 1967.
- **Kluane First Nation [K9]:** Burwash Landing (founded 1904 after a gold find) became the Kluane First Nation's center in the 1940s after the highway; the 1943 sanctuary barred subsistence hunting; part became Kluane National Park (1972 or 1973; sources differ); harvesting rights there are now protected under the land claim; the Kluane First Nation's final and self-government agreements were signed in October 2003; 2016 population 72; the community relies on highway travelers and tourism; the Kluane Museum of Natural History is there (Parks Canada, Canadian Encyclopedia and KFN snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Kaska Dena at Watson Lake [P9]:** the Liard First Nation (about 1,256-1,400 members) is one of five Kaska Dena communities; its offices are in Watson Lake, and the community of Watson Lake includes the town and the First Nation's settlements of Upper Liard, Two-Mile Village and Two and a Half Mile Village; the language is Kaska (Dene Zāgé') (B.C. government, AFN Yukon and Yukon profile snippets, UNVERIFIED). Yukon University runs a Watson Lake campus that also serves as community meeting space (Yukon University page, SOURCED).
- **The Black regiments, remembered [K9, K2]:** a 1993 Alaska law named the Gerstle River bridge (milepost 1392, near Delta Junction) for the Black soldiers of the 93rd, 95th, 97th and 388th units (the act's list also names a 94th); a 1,885-ft replacement was planned in 2026, keeping two old spans as a tribute to the roughly 4,000 Black soldiers who built the first wooden bridge there; historian Lael Morgan founded an Alaska Highway Memorial Project (Alaska Legislature act and AP snippets, UNVERIFIED). In 2017 the governor signed SB 46 at an Anchorage Black church creating "African American Soldiers' Contribution to Building the Alaska Highway Day" (October 25); about 3,000 Black troops cleared roughly 1,500 miles in eight months; their record is credited with helping set the stage for the military's 1948 desegregation (KUAC, 2024-02-15, SOURCED).
- **The online travelers' community [K14, K5] [R]:** a Facebook group founded in 2016 or 2017 by the couple who later became the guide's field team; ten volunteer moderators; volunteers in Canada and Alaska post real-time updates, including a section on road closures; membership figures range from 14,000 to over 20,000 (40,000 across past groups); one summer rally a year (third in 2023) (Alaska tourism, podcast and Truck Camper snippets, UNVERIFIED; the rally sponsorship by the guide SOURCED in Truck Camper 2026).
- **Speech (K11):** English; Kaska, Southern Tutchone, Tlingit (Teslin) and Upper Tanana in First Nations communities (R1 and above); English became the dominant working language after the highway, opening a gap between young people and grandparents (Cruikshank, SOURCED).

### C. Leisure industries and what people do
- **Winter travel and winter locals [K5, K7]:** the highway is open year-round with big rigs, military convoys and local drivers; temperatures routinely about -30°C, lows near -44.5°C in the Yukon; plan 120-150 miles between year-round service; few places are open 24 hours; many seasonal businesses close (the guide marks those staying open, but travelers should phone ahead); most motels from Cache Creek north have vehicle plug-ins; leave at sunrise and be off the road by dark; carry long booster cables, tow rope, shovel, warm clothes, a block heater and winter tires; night traffic is so light that help after a breakdown may take long; long stretches have no cell service; a Whitehorse-area field editor called winter her favorite season (no bugs, light crowds); a winter soak at Liard Hot Springs is recommended (The Milepost, SOURCED).

### E. Cultural activities and civic life
- **Yukon Sourdough Rendezvous, Whitehorse (K3, created on purpose) [P6]:** winter carnivals in 1946-48 helped fund the Civic Center; six businessmen who had run them conceived the Rendezvous over coffee in fall 1961; on October 16, 1961 the Board of Trade called 30 local organizations together; the aim was to brighten the slow, dull stretch between New Year's and spring "socially, spiritually and financially," encourage Indigenous participation through skills and handicrafts, and bring money to the area; the first ran from February 16, 1962 (parade, beard contests, dog-sled races won by a Teslin musher, snowshoe races, a talent show, dances, a queen crowned at an ice palace built by the drama club and later the Jaycees); later events included ice fishing, flour packing, ice sculpture, a mock court, a one-dog pull and a pack race down Main Street; the army took full part through 1964 and RCAF cadets, the RCMP and the Dragoons supplied ceremony; about 1,500 outside visitors came in 1964 and hotels filled (Athol Retallack, history in the 1970 Rendezvous program, via ExploreNorth, SOURCED). ### A. Other settings
- **Who closes the road, and how the warning reaches them [K1, K14, K7] [SM:competence] [P7]:** the canton of Uri's 2010 government report on a winter-safe road between Realp and Hospental (answering a 2009 parliamentary motion by members from Erstfeld, Realp and Andermatt) describes the system: Uri's avalanche warning service links the cantonal road crews, the national-roads on-call service, the cantonal police, other carriers, building sites and municipalities; its operating core is **two avalanche centers, at Urnerboden and at Andermatt, the Andermatt one staffed by employees of the federal defense department (the army's mountain competence center)** and covering the canton from Seelisberg to Realp; using the national institute's (SLF) regional danger assessment, reports from local observers and automatic IMIS stations, it produces a danger level for each section of cantonal and national road and the railway, updated (if needed around the clock) and sent by SMS, e-mail or fax through the canton's MIKADO alert system, with continuous phone contact in difficult situations; **the closure decision itself is delegated to "the responsible road people who know the place": for cantonal roads, the crew foremen (Rottenchefs), their deputies or the duty staff and incident leaders**, who then inform their superiors, who may overrule; they are trained in SLF courses; an annual instruction updates the alarm procedures; closure length depends on blasting results and may need reconnaissance flights; a risk analysis of 48 avalanche paths found low risks on the Furka road Hospental-Realp and on the Gotthard road Gurtnellen-Göschenen, further cut by precautionary closures, warnings and artificial release (Regierungsrat Uri, report to the Landrat, 2010-08-17, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). ### B. Subcultures
- **Commoners and their calendar (historic) [K1, K6, K7] [P7]:** a University of Basel study using the valley archive shows that until about mid-20th century the corporation's ordinances and grazing maps (1935) divided the commons into zones with fixed dates: private hay land open to common grazing from September 29 to October 31; "free mountains" open to grazing until June 25 and from September 15, with wild-hay mowing in between; home-cow pastures (to about 1,900 m) open July 26-September 14, **with a cap of five cows per farm**; large-stock alps and small-stock alps open June 26-September 14; two named steep wild-hay slopes opened for only three or six days in August, other remote slopes from early August to mid-October; and **one day a year on which sedges and mat-grass could be gathered for stable litter**, since the valley had no straw; four alp wardens reported yearly on animals summered, compulsory communal labor and incidents; with almost no forest outside protective stands, people burned dwarf shrubs (heather, bilberry, alpine rose) when cheese-making and cut peat; after mid-century oil heating ended shrub and alder burning, wild-hay mowing stopped, use polarized (intensified near farms, abandoned elsewhere) and green alder spread (Rahel Wunderli, *Bauhinia* 22, 2010, read from saved PDF, SOURCED).
