## 1 · #39 Liverpool, the "Cunard Yanks" and the Cavern Club

**Claim to test (from the input):** a port where seamen brought home records from abroad, feeding a music scene and a famous cellar club (K13). **Verdict: PARTLY CONFIRMED, NARROWED.** The seamen ("Cunard Yanks") are real and well documented as carriers of American clothes, goods, some records and some instruments, 1940s to 1960s. The claim that their records *shaped Merseybeat* is **contested by most of the music historians who have examined it** (see the debate below). What is solid: the port made the city's population and its musical mix (Irish, West African and Caribbean, Chinese, American country), and the Cavern Club began as a jazz cellar in a dockside fruit warehouse. Records for the beat groups came mainly from city record shops (NEMS and others) carrying UK releases of American music.

### A. Snapshot
- **Liverpool:** city on the Mersey estuary, North West England. 2021 census population **486,100** (up 4.2% from 2011); 77% White British, 23% other ethnic groups; 57% Christian (71% in 2011). SOURCED: liverpool.gov.uk/council/key-statistics-and-data/census-2021 (accessed 2026-11-20). Liverpool City Region 2021 population about 1.55 million; the city provides about 279,000 jobs, 39% of the region's jobs. UNVERIFIED (search snippets from ONS/city-region pages). [P4]
- **Population history:** peak 846,302 (1931 census); 737,637 (1961); low 439,428 (2001), a 48% fall from the peak. Causes given: postwar suburban moves, slum clearance, about 160,000 people moved by council "overspill" estates (Kirkby, Skelmersdale, Runcorn, Winsford). SOURCED: Dave Mail, CityMonitor, 2023-09-25 (citymonitor.ai/politics/was-decline-liverpool-s-historic-population-really-unusual-3490). [K9, P9]
- **Defining feature (for this pick):** an Atlantic passenger-and-cargo port whose shipping lines tied it to New York, West Africa and China, and which sent tens of thousands of young men to sea. "Over 20,000 seamen sailed out of Liverpool" in the 1950s–60s era (SOURCED: Catalyst review of the film *Cunard Yanks*, catalystmedia.org.uk, comments 2007–2011); another figure, "almost 25,000", UNVERIFIED (snippet). Working on ships was "almost a rite of passage" for many young Liverpool men (same review, SOURCED).

### B. What else exists there (the people, the work, how they got there)
**The Cunard Yanks (who they were).** Young working-class British men, mostly Cunard Line "household" (hotel/catering) crew: waiters, stewards, cooks, on Liverpool–New York and Liverpool–Montreal routes, late 1940s to the 1960s. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunard_Yanks (stub; cites Guardian 2003, BBC Liverpool 2007). [K7, P9]
- They "sailed to New York and came back dressed like film stars, laden with exotic consumer goods and music unknown in ration-book Britain." SOURCED: BBC Radio 4 *Archive Hour*, "The Dream Boats" (58 min), as listed on History News Network, 2006-01-14 (historynewsnetwork.org/article/20495).
- **Money:** basic wages were low; tips from wealthy liner passengers were pooled and shared around the crew, and paid for the records, fashion and consumer goods brought home. Their usual New York hangout was the Market Diner at Pier 92, Manhattan. SOURCED: Catalyst review (above). A Merseyside Maritime Museum page (snippet only) adds that the best stewards could out-earn the captain through tips, that the New York run gave regular leave ("four or five trips on and one trip off"), and that crews were paid at the end of each crossing. UNVERIFIED (snippet; the museum page fetched blank, and the museum is closed for redevelopment from 2025-01-05 to about 2028, also UNVERIFIED). [K7, K8]
- **Hiring:** seamen waited for ships at "the Pool," a shipping employment office near the Pier Head; "hundreds would congregate every day waiting for ships" (veteran quoted). SOURCED: Catalyst review. Dock work was casual (men queued daily). Same source. [K1, K7]
- **Instruments:** Cunard Yank Ivan Hayward bought a Gretsch guitar in New York (not then available in the UK) and later sold it to George Harrison. SOURCED: Lashua & Owusu (2021), "Decentring Liverpool's popular music heritage: Routes Jukebox," in Mahoney & Schofield (eds.), *Music and Heritage*, Routledge, pp. 69–79 (UCL Discovery pre-print, read in full text). Another Cunard Yank, George Salmon, recalled bringing back "crates full of albums" of Perry Como, Tony Bennett, Coltrane, Ellington, Basie (jazz and crooners, not R&B). Same source, SOURCED. [K13, K14, R]
- **End of the route:** Cunard withdrew its last Liverpool liners (Sylvania, Carinthia) in December 1967 after heavy losses; final Liverpool–Canada sailing October 1967. Final Liverpool–New York sailing given variously as November 1966 or January 1968. UNVERIFIED (snippets; conflicting). The May 1966 national seamen's strike cost Cunard £4 million (SOURCED: Culture Liverpool *Cunard Learning Resource* PDF, 2024, read via text extraction). [K9]

**THE DEBATE (as the care note asks; recorded, not resolved).**
- *For:* the 2007 film *Liverpool's Cunard Yanks* (dir. Mike Morris, written by Dave Cotterill; premiered Liverpool Philharmonic Hall 2007-06-21) presented the seamen as "responsible for a music revolution" (narrator Paul Gambaccini). Pop historian Bob Stanley wrote that seamen "brought back jukeboxes as well as stacks of unknown records." Cavern Club director Dave Jones (in *Routes Jukebox*, 2016) said ship-borne music "did have some influence," above all **country and western**, going back to the 1930s. Ted "Kingsize" Taylor (Kingsize Taylor and the Dominoes) said he first acquired records by sea. SOURCED: Catalyst review; bobpitt.org.uk (below); Lashua & Owusu.
- *Against:* **Mike Brocken** (*The Twenty-First-Century Legacy of the Beatles*, 2015; chapter "'I'll Follow the Sun': The Cunard Yanks narrative and the beginnings of 1970s Beatles tourism") argues the story dates from the 1970s and grew with Beatles tourism; the 2007 film identified only one seaman who brought records in quantity, mainly jazz. **Bob Wooler** (Cavern compère; to Spencer Leigh, *Record Collector*, 2013): the seamen brought pre-war dance-band and 1950s country records; "there is no evidence that the beat groups were performing songs brought over from America by the Cunard Yanks." **Mark Lewisohn** (*All These Years*, vol. 1, 2013): the Beatles "got their American music from shops, not ships." **Alan Clayson and Spencer Leigh** (*The Walrus Was Ringo*, 2003) devote a chapter to refuting it. Spencer Leigh found about 350 originals of Liverpool groups' covers had UK releases (London American, Top Rank, Oriole, Stateside, Pye International). Bob Pitt traced 90+ Beatles R&B covers and found UK releases for all but three. SOURCED: Bob Pitt, "Not the 'Cunard Yanks': The real origins of the Beatles' R&B covers," bobpitt.org.uk, first published 2020, posted 2021-03-23, updated 2026-03-24. (Quotes are as Pitt reports them; the originals were not read. Secondhand.)
- *Alternative channels named:* NEMS record store (Whitechapel; Brian Epstein ran the record department and stocked at least one copy of every release), other city record shops and their listening booths; radio (Radio Luxembourg) and American servicemen at Burtonwood airbase are named by a different source. SOURCED: bobpitt.org.uk; livpost.co.uk (2024-03-30, below) for Burtonwood and Radio Luxembourg.
- *Middle position:* Lashua & Owusu (2021) call the Cunard Yanks story "mythical" in status yet record the clothes, records and instruments as real transatlantic flows. Pitt himself does not deny the seamen's broader cultural impact, only their centrality to Merseybeat's R&B. SOURCED.

**Who else the port brought (people and how they got there).** [P9]
- **Irish:** famine-era arrivals; a promotional source says 1.8 million Irish entered through Clarence Dock 1845–1853 (UNVERIFIED, festival listing). The Irish Center opened December 1964 at 127 Mount Pleasant (formal opening 1965-02-01, St Brigid's Day); 1,635 members within a year; the Liverpool Ceili Band won All-Ireland titles in 1963 and 1965; Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann Liverpool branch, Liverpool Pipe Band, John Mitchels GAA club based there; the building was lost in the 1990s and the center moved (1999) to St Michael's, renamed Liverpool Irish Center (2017), 6 Boundary Lane. SOURCED: liverpoolirishcentre.org/about-us. [K4, K10, K13]
- **Black Liverpool:** described by National Museums Liverpool as the oldest Black community in Europe; in the 1750s Black settlers included sailors, freed slaves and sons of African rulers; by the 1780s Liverpool was "the European capital of the transatlantic slave trade," whose profits built the city; after abolition (1807) the trade shifted to palm oil, timber, groundnuts and rubber; Kru sailors from Liberia and Sierra Leone and West African seamen on **Elder Dempster** ships settled; the old community was near Pitt Street by the docks; about 5,000 Black people lived in Liverpool after the First World War, when job competition led to riots (1919). SOURCED: National Museums Liverpool, *Liverpool Black Community trail* (PDF, read via text extraction). Elder Dempster ceased trading with containerization in 1989 and was wound up in 2000 (UNVERIFIED, snippet of LJMU/Nautilus PDF). [K9, P9]
- **Chinese:** first arrivals were seamen in the 1860s on Alfred Holt's **Blue Funnel Line** (Shanghai–Hong Kong–Liverpool: silk, cotton, tea); some married locally and opened boarding houses, groceries, laundries, restaurants. About 20,000 Chinese seamen were recruited into the British Merchant Navy in WWII, mostly based in Liverpool; they struck for equal pay in 1942; in October 1945 the Home Office carried out the "compulsory repatriation" of Chinese seamen, leaving wives and mixed-race children behind (the story surfaced in the 2002 BBC documentary *Shanghai'd*). SOURCED: LJMU news, 2023-01-19. [K9, P9]

**Seafarers' institutions.** Mersey Mission to Seafarers (Anglican, 1856) and Apostleship of the Sea Liverpool (Catholic, 1937) merged on 2008-10-01 as the Liverpool Seafarers Center (20 Crosby Road South), an ecumenical charity reporting 50,000+ seafarers helped a year. SOURCED: Liverpool Seafarers Center history page. A 2024 Church Times report says its crew-welfare hub served 55,000+ cruise crew in one summer (UNVERIFIED, snippet). The Liverpool Seamen's Friend Society (1820–1975) is an older body (UNVERIFIED, museum catalog snippet). [K1]

**Labor conflict (seafaring).** Two unofficial seamen's strikes in 1960 started in Liverpool and spread to other ports; militants formed the National Seamen's Reform Movement (Liverpool Museums holds its strike card, August 1960); a leader was jailed six months. The National Union of Seamen's first national strike since 1911 began 1966-05-16. UNVERIFIED (snippets: marxists.org ISJ 1973; liverpoolmuseums.org.uk artifact page; libcom). The Catalyst review (SOURCED) says 1950s seafarers fought both employers and a union it calls corrupt; that is the reviewer's characterization. [K1, K9, SM:man versus society]

**Dock labor and loss.** Liverpool dockers' dispute, 1995-09-25 to 1998-01-26 (about 850 days): five workers at the subcontractor Torside were sacked over overtime pay; Torside then dismissed its whole workforce (about 80); Mersey Docks and Harbor Company dismissed 300 of its 380 dockers for refusing to cross the picket; international solidarity actions in about 27 countries (1997-01-20); ended in a settlement without reinstatement. Some dockers bought a derelict Hope Street bar, renamed **The Casa**, funded partly by the £127,000 fee from the Channel 4 drama *Dockers* (1999, written with sacked dockers), opened Christmas Eve 2000 as a community hub giving free advice. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_dockers'_dispute_(1995–1998). [K9, K10, K1]

**Economy now (brief).** Music generates "more than £70 million" annual turnover (UNESCO UK, Liverpool City of Music page, SOURCED). Beatles heritage: £81.9m net additional value a year and 2,335 jobs, growing up to 15% a year (LJMU / University of Liverpool report for the city council, lead author Prof. Simeon Yates, 2016-02-08; SOURCED, news release). Press reports (AFP, 2023) give £120m and about 2,500 jobs, and the Cavern's own claim of 800,000 visitors a year (UNVERIFIED, snippets). [K8]

### C. Cultural locations (names)
**The Cavern Club** (10 Mathew Street). Opened 1957-01-16 by Alan Sytner as a jazz club modeled on Paris cellar clubs (Le Caveau de la Huchette); a cellar under a former fruit warehouse, used as an air-raid shelter in WWII; first act the Merseysippi Jazz Band. Rock 'n' roll was banned at first (skiffle tolerated); on 1957-08-07 Sytner sent a note to the Quarrymen to stop playing it. Ray McFall (an accountant, born Garston 1926) bought it on 1959-10-03 for £2,750, introduced all-nighters and "beat group cruises" on the ferry Royal Iris; first "Beat Night" 1960-05-25; Bob Wooler compère from late 1960/early 1961 and organizer of the **lunchtime sessions**, aimed at city-center office and shop workers. The Beatles played there 292 times (1961-02-09 to 1963-08-03), the first for a £5 fee shared among the band. Closed 1966-02-28, reopened 1966-07-23 (PM Harold Wilson present). Closed 1973-05-28 when British Rail compulsorily purchased the warehouses for a Merseyrail ventilation shaft that was never built; site became a car park. Rebuilt within Cavern Walks, reopened 1984-04-26 on about 70% of the original footprint using about 15,000 original bricks. Lost its license and closed December 1989 after a serious assault; reopened 1991-07-11 under Cavern City Tours (Bill Heckle, Dave Jones). Today about 40 live bands a week; front room noon to midnight. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cavern_Club; cavernclub.com "Ray McFall and the day the Cavern Club came alive"; bitaboutbritain.com (2024-03-26); beatlesbible (fee, snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K13, K10, K9]
- Conditions recorded: condensation dripping from the brick arches onto players and equipment; smells of sweat, scent, mold and cheese from the neighboring warehouse; overflowing toilets at the back. SOURCED: bitaboutbritain.com.

**Other Merseybeat-era places** (North West Sound Heritage, "Merseybeat Liverpool sound walk," 2021-06-29, SOURCED):
- **Jacaranda** (23 Slater Street): coffee bar opened 1958, basement members' bar with live music; murals by Lennon and Stuart Sutcliffe; now record shop, café and label.
- **Blue Angel** (106–108 Seel Street): former Wyvern Social Club, renamed 1960; auditions and signings.
- **NEMS** (12–14 Whitechapel): record and instrument shop (began as a furniture store); Epstein's record department; now a chain clothing store.
- **Kardomah Coffee House** (Whitechapel/Stanley Street): gathering place because it sat by NEMS, Hessy's and the Cavern.
- **Hessy's Music Stores** (62 Stanley Street): family music shop 1934–1996; **sold instruments on credit and gave free guitar lessons**; blue plaque 2017. [K14, R]
- **Iron Door Club** (13 Temple Street): capacity 1,600+, 1960–1964, in a former butter-packing factory.
- **Downbeat Club** (77 Victoria Street): jazz from 1960, then beat, later soul.
- **Rushworths** (Whitechapel, moved there 1960): large music house; in the 1970s–80s rented instruments to parents so children could learn. UNVERIFIED (snippet, University of Liverpool news).
- **Casbah Coffee Club** (8 Hayman's Green, West Derby): in the cellars of Mona Best's Victorian house; opened August 1959; closed 1962; Grade II listed with murals by the musicians. SOURCED: Kevin McManus, Historic England blog, 2023-05-11.
- **Eric's** (Mathew Street, 1976–1980): punk/post-punk basement opposite the old Cavern site. SOURCED: same, and Lashua & Owusu.
- *Mersey Beat* newspaper: Bill Harry, first issue 1961-07-06, 5,000 copies sold out; Harry and Wooler listed almost 300 local groups in 1961; Wikipedia estimates about 500 groups 1958–64, about 350 active at any time. UNVERIFIED (snippets).
- **Buddy Holly at the Philharmonic Hall** (March 1958): remembered by musicians as the moment local youths decided they could form groups; rock 'n' roll was otherwise "pretty well banned" from the classical hall (Spencer Leigh). SOURCED: Lashua & Owusu.

**Liverpool 8 (Granby/Toxteth) social clubs.** Over 20 clubs in the 1950s–60s, most set up by immigrant seamen from Commonwealth ships who were unwelcome in "white" clubs and dance halls; they also gave lodging advice, familiar food and music. Named: **Sierra Leone, Silver Sands, Federal** (Princes Avenue/Road); **Nigeria, Somali Club** (Parliament Street); later the **Ibo** and others. Not all were licensed; they opened and closed as owners chose. Decline: Commonwealth ships stopped coming, slum clearance moved residents out to Kirkby and Cantril Farm, the 1981 riots raised insurance costs, police raids on late openings and objections to licenses. By the early 1990s only the Nigerian, Ibo and Sierra Leone remained; in 2009 the **Caribbean Center** (Amberley Street) was the only licensed club in L8, dependent on council grants. SOURCED: Donna Palmer, "The Changing Club Scene of Liverpool 8," *Nerve* 14, Summer 2009 (catalystmedia.org.uk). A reader comment there disputes how early the scene ended. Lashua & Owusu (2021) record these clubs as a hidden music heritage outside the Beatles-centered story; L8 groups include the **Chants** (vocal harmony group, from 1962; the Beatles backed them at six shows in late 1962) and the **Real Thing** (UK No. 1, 1976). Derry Wilkie and the Seniors were the first Liverpool group to play Hamburg (1960). SOURCED. [K4, K10, K13, K9]

**Country music.** Liverpool had a large country-and-western scene in the late 1950s–60s. Hank Walters (a docker; led the Dusty Road Ramblers) opened the **Black Cat Club** (above a restaurant on London Road, about 200 capacity, open seven days, 1957). **The Musical Box** record shop (Tuebrook), described as the city's oldest record shop; a worker there from 1950 said sailors brought American country records by sea; suppliers claimed it sold more country records than anywhere in the UK. Other channels named: American servicemen at Burtonwood, Radio Luxembourg. Decline put down to the line-dancing boom of the 1990s and venues switching to solo acts with backing tapes. SOURCED: *The Post* (Liverpool), 2024-03-30 (livpost.co.uk), drawing on David Bedford, *The Country of Liverpool*. Lashua & Owusu record an album "Liverpool Goes Country" (1965). [K13]

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The port's ship routes brought the communities whose clubs and music made up the city's mix: Irish (ceili bands, Irish Center), West African and Caribbean seamen (L8 clubs, built because city-center venues barred them), Chinese seamen (boarding houses, restaurants), and American country records. SOURCED as above. Radio DJ Bernie Connor's view (in *Routes Jukebox*): "thousands and thousands of sailors... came ashore, and they wanted to hear music." [K13, K4]
- The Cavern itself exists because of the port's warehouse district: a cellar under a fruit warehouse near the docks. Its 1973 loss came from a transport project (rail ventilation shaft). [K9]
- The seamen's income system (low wages, pooled tips, pay at the end of each crossing, regular New York shore leave) is what let a large group of young men buy American goods. [K7, K8]
- The port's decline (containerization, the end of Cunard's Liverpool liners in 1966–68, end of Commonwealth ship visits) closed the seamen's clubs and shrank the dock workforce; the music heritage later became a tourism economy (Beatles heritage £81.9m a year). [K9, K8]

## 2 · #40 Hamburg, the Reeperbahn and the Star-Club

**Claim to test (from the input):** a port's entertainment district whose clubs served sailors and transients and became famous for live rock (K13, K10). **Verdict: CONFIRMED, with a correction on timing and audience.** The district grew from the 18th–19th centuries as the place outside the city gates where sailors and dockworkers spent shore time. The 1959–62 rock clubs (Kaiserkeller, Indra, Top Ten, Star-Club) at first drew "sailors, longshoremen, and prostitutes" and local youths; art students joined from late 1960. The musicians were themselves transients: young British bands on contract, many from Liverpool. A shift from jazz to rock in the clubs in the late 1950s is documented; that British sailors prompted the first booking of British musicians is reported only secondhand. The sex-trade side is outside the claimed parallel and is recorded below only where it bears on everyday rules (K6).

### B. What else exists there (the people, the work, how they got there)
**How the district came to serve sailors.** In the early 19th century Spielbudenplatz offered folk theater, curiosities, menageries and dance halls; dockworkers and sailors from ships at anchor filled the streets; port expansion from the 1860s brought ballrooms, music halls and beer halls; the Urania Theater (1841) is today's St. Pauli Theater; in the 1920s the Alkazar and Zillertal variety theaters were known beyond Hamburg. SOURCED: University of Hamburg, 2026-04-27. Steamships from 1816 had to moor at the harbor edge near the Hamburger Berg; the city gate closure (Torsperre) ended 1861, which boosted the district; a first police station opened 1840 and the **Davidwache** in 1868. SOURCED: planet-wissen.de (WDR), "Die Große Freiheit Nummer 7." [K5, K9, P3]

**The Große Freiheit.** The street belonged to Altona, under the Danish crown, and marked the old city boundary; its name refers to the freedoms of religion, trade and guild granted there in the 17th century that Hamburg's residents got much later. SOURCED: hamburg-tourism.de, "Große Freiheit – Where freedom began." (Founding year given as 1601 or 1610 by different sources, UNVERIFIED.) **St. Joseph**, Hamburg's oldest Catholic church, stands on the street; Große Freiheit 73 and 75 were Mennonite parish houses (UNVERIFIED, snippets). Around 1940 the street's legend formed: horse shows at the Hippodrome, loud music, sailors filling the bars. SOURCED: hamburg-tourism.de. [K4, P6]

**Chinese seamen (a port-arrival community).** From the late 19th century European lines hired Chinese seamen as stokers and coal trimmers, paid less than Europeans; before WWI non-European seamen were several thousand, about 10% of those employed in German merchant shipping. Groups of Chinese seamen were noticed in St. Pauli about 1900; after WWI men mostly from Guangdong settled and opened restaurants, laundries and small shops, closely tied to the port and to visiting countrymen; many partnered with or married German women. A small quarter of a few hundred people formed around the **Schmuckstraße** (on the Hamburg–Altona boundary) in the 1920s; Chinese restaurants operated on the Große Freiheit from the early 1920s. In 1925 the Hamburg Senate tightened the port law so seamen needed harbor-police permission (not only the captain's) to go ashore. On **13 May 1944** the Gestapo arrested the 130 Chinese nationals remaining in Hamburg ("Chinesenaktion"); they were mistreated for months, and in September 1944 60–80 men were sent to the "Arbeitserziehungslager" Langer Morgen in Wilhelmsburg. SOURCED: Lars Amenda, "Migration und Kriminalisierung: Das 'Chinesenviertel' in St. Pauli/Altona..." (akens.org PDF, read via text extraction). ## 3 · #44 St. John's, Newfoundland (George Street and kitchen parties)

**Claim to test (from the input):** a port city with a dense street of pubs and a strong home-and-pub music tradition shaped by seafaring (K13, K10). **Verdict: CONFIRMED, with refinements.** George Street is a two-block street of bars and restaurants with long licensed hours. The home music tradition is documented by folklorists as informal house (and fish-store and boat) gatherings in the fishing outports, centered on the kitchen, strongest in winter and at Christmas when fishing stopped, and kept alive today in homes and in pub sessions. "Shaped by seafaring" holds in the sense that the people, the work calendar (summer fishing, winter at home) and the Irish and West Country English origins all came through the migratory fishery. The public Irish-style pub session format is recent in St. John's (introduced in the 1980s by Irish musicians who moved there), and its musicians call the kitchen party private and invitation-only, not something visitors can buy.

### A. Snapshot
- **St. John's:** capital of Newfoundland and Labrador, on the northeast Avalon Peninsula. 2021 census: CMA population 212,579 (228.2 per km²); City of St. John's 110,525; CMA population grew 7.9% 2011–2021 while the province fell 0.8%. UNVERIFIED (snippets of IRCC economic profile and gov.nl.ca census profile; not opened). Health care, retail and public administration account for 43% of jobs in the region (same, UNVERIFIED). - **Province demography:** peak just over 580,000 in the early 1990s; decline 1993–2008; net out-migration peaked at about 12,000 in 1998 after the cod collapse, government restraint and Employment Insurance reform; 549,911 on 2025-07-01; median age 47.8 (2025), more than seven years above Canada's; deaths exceed births since 2012; net in-migration a record 10,987 in 2024. - **Defining feature (for this pick):** a harbor city that was the main landing place and supply center of the North Atlantic fishery, with a downtown packed with drinking and music venues.

### B. What else exists there (the people, the work, how they got there)
**Settlement through the fishery.** The Irish came to Newfoundland along the trade routes of the migratory fishery; most 17th–18th-century Irish immigrants came from around Waterford and Wexford, the provisioning ports for English West Country fishing ships; the main Irish immigrations were 1811–1816 and 1825–1833; the migrations were labor-related and came before the famine emigration of the 1840s, which largely bypassed Newfoundland. SOURCED: Evelyn Osborne, "The Most Irish Place in the World? 'Irishness' in the Recorded Folk Music of Newfoundland and Labrador," *MUSICultures* 42(2), 2015, pp. 79–102 (read via text extraction). Most Irish landed at St. John's or Harbor Grace; Irish Catholics were the city's largest group by 1850; English settlers came mainly from Devon and Dorset (UNVERIFIED, heritage.nf.ca snippets; that site failed to load, see log). [P9]

**Port labor.** The **Longshoremen's Protective Union** (LSPU): through militancy and frequent strikes established a permanent presence on the St. John's waterfront; a May 1903 strike won concessions; membership grew more than tenfold 1903–1914 and was open to all dockworkers including juveniles. UNVERIFIED (abstract snippet: Jessie Chisholm, "Organizing on the Waterfront: The St. John's LSPU, 1890–1914," *Labor/Le Travail* 26, 1990; the érudit page was behind a bot check). The union bought its hall on Victoria Street in **1912**, chosen for its harbor view so the secretary could see ships arriving; it held union meetings, speeches and bingo. SOURCED: Canada's Historic Places, "LSPU Hall" (historicplaces.ca). [K1, K7]

**Foreign fishing fleets in port.** On 27 May 1955 Portuguese fishermen of the White Fleet carried a statue of Our Lady of Fatima up to the Basilica of St. John the Baptist as a thank-you to the city (about 4,000 men, per a journal abstract); White Fleet crews shopped on Water Street and played soccer by the harbor; Canada stopped letting foreign fishing vessels use St. John's for supplies and repairs in 1986. UNVERIFIED (snippets: openparliament record 2010; CBC *Land & Sea*; an *NQ* online photo essay; the MDPI journal page refused 403 and the *NQ* page failed on its certificate). [K4, K5, P9]

**The fishery's collapse.** On **2 July 1992** the federal government closed the northern cod fishery (announced by minister John Crosbie as two years; made indefinite in 1993); nearly 30,000 people in the province lost work; income programs NCARP (August 1992, about 28,000 people) then TAGS (about $1.9 billion, ran out of money in 1997/98) followed. UNVERIFIED (snippets: Canadian Encyclopedia page loaded without its text; CBC refused 403; gov.nl.ca TAGS review snippet). The population effects are SOURCED (gov.nl.ca, above). [K9]

**Offshore oil.** Workers fly from St. John's by helicopter to the offshore platforms on three-week rotations (one worker's account); about 700 union members at Hibernia and Terra Nova in an older report. UNVERIFIED (snippets). [K7]

**Fire and rebuilding.** Great Fire of **8 July 1892**: began about 4:45 p.m. in a stable on Carter's Hill from a dropped pipe; dry weather, strong northwest wind and low water pressure from main work that day; destroyed nearly all the east end and much of the commercial district (Water and Duckworth Streets); at least 12,000 burnt out; losses about $13 million, under $5 million insured; aid from Britain, Canada and the US; afterward the city hired 22 paid firefighters by 1895 and rebuilt in a Second Empire style. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Fire_of_1892. The earlier Great Fire of 9 June 1846 began on George Street (a glue pot boiling over in a cabinetmaker's shop). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Street_(St._John's). [K9]

**Daily logistics.** About 50% of goods on the island arrive on trucks via the Marine Atlantic ferry; rough weather delays the ferries and empties store shelves within days; Oceanex container ships to St. John's supply the main grocery chains, and in 2020 the premier said the island had about five days of food. UNVERIFIED (CBC snippets, 2020 and 2024; CBC pages refuse fetching). Ferries "regularly get stuck" in ice and are delayed by summer winds; travel to the island is expensive (SOURCED: Everett 2016, below). [K5]

### C. Cultural locations (names)

Closed to traffic in the evenings and most of the day; open to vehicles in the mornings so bars can restock. Crowds build from about midnight and can last to 6 a.m.; hot-dog vendors and 24-hour restaurants nearby. **George Street Festival**: six nights in early August, ending the Tuesday before the Royal St. John's Regatta, rumored over 120,000 attendance over six days; a Mardi Gras in October. ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **The fishing calendar made the music season.** In the outport studied by folklorist Wilfred Wareham (Harbor Buffett, Placentia Bay), summer had almost no gatherings because it was the work season; informal gatherings ran in spring, fall and winter, most intensely in winter and above all at Christmas; formal hall dances were held in spring and fall when outside men came in for work, supplies or fish delivery; men with boats moored for winter hosted gatherings aboard. SOURCED: Wilfred W. Wareham (Memorial University), "Interior Forms – Interior Functions: Aspects of Socializing and Partying in Outport Newfoundland," *Material Culture Review* (UNB journals; year not shown on the page read). [K2, K7, K10]
- **The house as venue.** The kitchen was the central room of the house and the place of entertaining; stoves were sometimes moved outside and mats lifted to make room for dancing; fish stores were cleared of gear for dances. Informal gatherings: square dances, singing, recitation and yarning; open drinking (dark rum, incl. from St. Pierre). Formal hall dances (in the Society of United Fishermen's hall): a floor master called the dances, no singing and no open drinking, admission charged, Sunday clothes. Around WWII the button accordion replaced the fiddle as the main dance instrument. The community (Harbor Buffett) was abandoned in 1967 under the government resettlement program. SOURCED: Wareham. [K4, K10, K13, K12]
- **Mummering** (Christmas house-visiting): groups in disguise visit homes; hosts guess who they are; once identified, mummers unmask and are given food and drink (in many homes drink was withheld until the guess); fiddles, accordions, spoons and washboards; stomping step-dances. SOURCED: Memorial University Intangible Cultural Heritage inventory, mummering profile (mun.ca/ich/inventory/profiles/xmastrad/mummering.php). [K2]
- **From kitchen to stage:** Irish musicians who settled in the 1970s (the group Ryan's Fancy; CBC TV series 1974–77 that filmed in guests' home communities) saw themselves as models for moving the music "from the kitchen to the stage"; few local recordings existed in the early 1970s. In the 1980s and early 1990s Irish fiddler Séamus Creagh (arrived 1988, left 1993) and flutist Rob Murphy (a Cork doctor, early 1980s, about 15 years) introduced the international Irish instrumental session format to St. John's and encouraged a Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann branch. SOURCED: Osborne 2015 (citing Breslin 2011). [K13, K14]
- **Tourism and the kitchen party.** Tourism was about 8% of provincial employment and just over $1 billion a year (2015); just over 55% of "tourists" are residents of the province; non-resident visitors tend to be older and affluent; a record-store worker near the cruise terminal reported visitors expecting to "pay five dollars to get into a kitchen party," which he answered is "private, family, invite-only"; a label releases up to five traditional CDs every summer; good traditional musicians are in demand year-round, more so in summer. SOURCED: Holly Everett, "Do You Play Newfoundland Music? Tracking Traditional Music in the Tourist Imaginary," *MUSICultures* 43(1), 2016, pp. 112–131. [K8, K4, K7]

## 4 · #107 Mindelo, Cape Verde

**Claim to test (from the input):** a refueling port for passing ships whose bars and many-origin crowd produced a famous song tradition, carried to the wider world by the people passing through (K13, K10). **Verdict: CONFIRMED in outline, with corrections.** Mindelo grew from almost nothing because British companies made Porto Grande a coal-bunkering station from 1838; its population came from other islands (above all Santo Antão), from Portugal, the Azores and Madeira, and included a British business community and English-speaking Gibraltar Jewish traders. The morna was not born in Mindelo (sources place its origin on Boa Vista), but a distinct São Vicente form developed there in the 1930s–40s, and the coladeira is described as originating in Mindelo. Brazilian music arriving with ships is documented as an influence. The music was carried abroad above all by emigrants and sailors who left (Lisbon, Rotterdam, New England), and later by Cesária Évora, who began singing in Mindelo's bars. Drought, famine and contract-labor emigration are central to the story and are reported below plainly.

### A. Snapshot
- **Mindelo:** port city on Porto Grande Bay, island of São Vicente (about 227 km², hot desert climate, about 127 mm of rain a year); Cape Verde's second city. Population: 1858 about 1,400; 1879 3,717; 1980 36,746; 1990 47,109; 2000 62,497; 2010 70,468. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindelo; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/São_Vicente,_Cape_Verde (92.6% of the island's people live in Mindelo). National 2021 census: 491,233 residents; urbanization 73.9% (UNVERIFIED, RTP snippet; no 2021 Mindelo figure found).
- **Founding:** a settlement founded 1793 as Nossa Senhora da Luz; renamed Leopoldina about 1820 and Mindelo in 1838. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Mindelo. The island was uninhabited until the late 18th century; first settlers 1795, mostly from Fogo; a major influx from Santo Antão from about 1797. SOURCED: Dominika Swolkien, "Cape Verdean Creole of São Vicente," APiCS survey 32 (apics-online.info). [P9]
- **Defining feature:** a deep, sheltered harbor on the mid-Atlantic steamer routes, used as a coaling and cable station by British companies.

### B. What else exists there (the people, the work, how they got there)
**The coal port.** 1838: coal depot for British East India Company ships; 1850: Royal Mail Steam Packet Company. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Mindelo. Coal companies named by a historian of sport in Cabo Verde: Royal Mail (1850), Patent Fuel (1851), Visgent Miller and Miller's & Nephews (1873–1880), Cory Brothers & Co. (1875), and at the end of the century the "S. Vicente de Cabo Verde" company; their arrival brought a burst of building and changed the city's landscape. SOURCED: Victor Andrade de Melo, "Jogando no meio do Atlântico: o golfe, os trabalhadores e identidade em Cabo Verde," *Cadernos AEL* 16(28), 2010 (read via text extraction). 669 ships refueled in 1879 and 1,927 in 1889 (UNVERIFIED, low-authority snippet). An 1884 submarine cable made Mindelo a communications node linking Europe, Africa, India and the Americas (SOURCED: Wikipedia, Mindelo); the Western Telegraph station is dated 1874/1875 by other sources (UNVERIFIED). English-speaking Gibraltar Jews opened businesses and at one time outnumbered the Portuguese-born; English left traces in vocabulary of sport and port work; the last coal companies left in 1958, causing crisis and emigration. SOURCED: Swolkien, APiCS. [K7, P9]

**Decline.** From the early 20th century the port lost transatlantic importance: the switch from coal to oil, competition from Dakar and the Canary Islands, under-investment. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Mindelo. A University of Lisbon working paper lists competition from the Suez Canal, Santa Cruz de Tenerife, Las Palmas and Dakar, and notes that the port's prosperity built the telegraph, the lyceum, the hospital and the church (SOURCED: africanos.eu, "S. Vicente: Prosperidade e Decadência (1850–1918)," abstract and contents only). [K9]

**Labor and unrest.** The relationship between the coal companies and port workers was tense already under the Republic: in 1913 a state of siege was declared after a bomb exploded in the Government Palace gardens, and British warships were stationed in the port to protect British residents. Workers' bodies: Associação Operária 1º de Dezembro (São Vicente, 1913) and an Associação de Socorros Mútuos (1921). In the 1930s depression the port's traffic fell, unemployment rose, and São Vicente could no longer buy the farm produce of Santo Antão and São Nicolau, ruining farmers there; remittances from emigrants in the US also fell. In **1934** (dated 7 June elsewhere) unemployed residents led by a man known as "Nhô Ambrose" (Ambrósio) marched through Mindelo carrying a black cloth as a "flag of hunger," shouting hunger and misery, to the Câmara Municipal to demand a telegram to the governor; the crowd then broke into the customs warehouse and looted several firms; police and soldiers intervened; one demonstrator was killed and others wounded; leaders were jailed on Boa Vista and Sal and Ambrose was deported to Angola. SOURCED: Manuel António de Pina Souto Amado, *Instauração do Salazarismo em Cabo Verde (1926–1939)*, MA dissertation in contemporary history, May 2019 (memoriacomum.org PDF, read via text extraction); the 7 June date and one death also in Wikipedia, Mindelo (SOURCED). [K9, K1, SM:man versus society]

**Drought, famine and emigration (reported plainly).** Recorded famines across the archipelago since the 16th century, incl. 1773–75 (over 20,000 deaths), 1830–33 (about 30,000), 1854–56 (25% of the population), 1863–66 (20,000–30,000), and 1941–43 and 1947–48 (about 45,000 combined); causes given: semi-arid climate and erratic rains, erosion, overgrazing, maize favored over drought-tolerant crops, the morgado land system, colonial cash-crop priorities and an inadequate colonial response. About 80,000 Cape Verdeans were shipped to São Tomé and Príncipe between 1900 and 1970, many as contract laborers on cocoa plantations. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_in_Cape_Verde (citing Keese 2012, Moran 1982, Brooks 2006). Three droughts 1940–1958 caused famine; Mindelo kept growing as people came from Santo Antão. SOURCED: Wikipedia, Mindelo. Under the Estado Novo the word "fome" (hunger) could not be used publicly; people had to say weakness or debility instead (SOURCED: Amado 2019, citing Sérgio Neto). Most Cape Verdeans in São Tomé returned after 1975; about 3,000 remain (SOURCED: Wikipedia, Cape Verdean people in São Tomé and Príncipe). [K9, K6, P9]

**Sailors who left.** Porto Grande was the departure point of thousands of young sailors for Rotterdam; first arrivals in the 1950s, growth in the 1960s, women's migration in the 1970s; the community is estimated at 20,000–30,000 (Leiden) or 23,150 of Cape Verdean origin in the Netherlands (Statistics Netherlands, 2022). UNVERIFIED for the Porto Grande departure detail (snippet). ## 5 · #114 Piraeus and rembetiko

**Claim to test (from the input):** a port where a music of tavernas and back rooms took shape among sailors, workers and a large refugee arrival, a scene born of people in transit (K13, K10). **Verdict: CONFIRMED, with refinements.** Rembetiko (rebetiko) predates 1922: the national inventory dates its spread among the urban poor of the port cities to the first decades of the 20th century, and lists prisons, hashish dens, small taverns and beer shops as its first venues. Piraeus was the country's largest working-class and industrial city between the wars, and almost all of about 1.5 million refugees of 1922–23 passed through it; refugee musicians from Smyrna and Constantinople fused with the local bouzouki-based style, and a distinct "Piraeus" school is recognized. Sailors figure in the port's nightlife districts; the core social base named by the sources is the urban sub-proletariat and the refugees. "People in transit" fits the refugees (who stayed, often for decades in temporary housing), the musicians (most of the famous Piraeus quartet were not from Piraeus), and, later, emigrants and 2016 asylum seekers passing through the port.

### A. Snapshot
- **Piraeus:** municipality of 168,151 (2021 census; fifth largest in Greece), 10.865 km²; Greater Piraeus 448,051. Permanent habitation resumed 1829; municipality 1835. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus. Population: about 300 (1835); about 11,000 (c. 1870); about 75,000 (early 20th c.); 133,482 (1920); 251,659 (1928); 163,688 (2011). SOURCED: Evaggelia Bafounis (Director of Culture, Municipality of Piraeus), "Piraeus: a historical reflection," OT, 2022-06-07. [P9]
- **Industry:** Lucas Rallis's silk mill (1846), described as the first steam-powered factory in Greece; Vasileiadis machine works (1860); Retsina brothers' textile mill; by the early 20th century the country's leading industrial center (SOURCED: Bafounis 2022). Up to 1939, 35 large factories lined the road from the port to Tavros; 88 listed factory buildings today, most abandoned; the Retsina mill closed 1979 (SOURCED: Dimitra Triantafyllou, "To elliniko Mantsester," *Athens Voice* no. 267, 2010-09-17). The Drapetsona fertilizer works (statute 1909, operating October 1910; about 4,000 employees in 1936; company town with clinic, fire station, workshops, printing press; glassworks closed 1992; whole plant closed 1999; ruins listed from 2003) (SOURCED: skai.gr, 2021-02-03). [K7, K9]
- **The port today:** Greece's largest port. COSCO leased container piers II and III in 2009 (35 years); bought 51% of the Piraeus Port Authority in 2016 (€280.5m) and a further 16% in October 2021 (67%); container throughput 5.65 million TEU (2019), 4.64 million (2025); 19.15 million passengers (2025); 991 port authority employees (2020); the dockworkers' union represents about 350 (union claims of lower pay and smaller crane gangs, from nine to four; COSCO says it cut no one's pay). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Piraeus. Most Greek shipowners have offices on Akti Miaouli (SOURCED: Wikipedia, Piraeus). [K7, K9]
- **Defining feature:** the port of the capital and the country's chief industrial city, where refugee settlements, factories and the port's nightlife districts sat side by side.

### B. What else exists there (the people, the work, how they got there)
**The refugees (reported plainly).** After the Treaty of Lausanne (23 July 1923) about 1.5 million refugees came to Greece, nearly all passing through Piraeus; the interwar city was the largest workers' city in the country; shanty towns and refugee settlements formed where refugees and locals lived side by side. SOURCED: Alexandra Mourgou (NTUA), *Efimerida ton Syntakton*, 2018-07-15. Piraeus's population nearly doubled 1920–1928 (SOURCED: Wikipedia). Settlements named: Kokkinia (largest), Keratsini, Drapetsona, Nea Kallipoli, Tourkolimano, Rentis, Kaminia; the biggest shanty settlements at Drapetsona (UNVERIFIED, snippet). **Nea Kokkinia**: 33,201 refugees from Asia Minor, Pontus and Eastern Thrace in 1928, the largest organized settlement in Piraeus (UNVERIFIED, snippet); part of Piraeus until 1933; renamed Nikaia 1940; 88,077 residents (2021) (SOURCED: Wikipedia, Nikaia). [P9, K4]
- **Housing institutions (K1):** a Refugee Relief Fund (1922) and the Welfare Ministry built more than 22,000 houses in three years; the Fund began at the edge of the Athens–Piraeus planning zone, at Kaisariani, Nea Ionia, Vyronas and Kokkinia; first houses of wood, galvanized iron and tar paper on 20–25 m² plots, one room per family; in January 1924 the League of Nations' **Refugee Settlement Commission** (1923–1930, financed by loans of 1924 and 1928) took over 4,000 finished and 2,500 unfinished houses; sites were chosen near factories that could absorb refugees as cheap labor; in 1927 about 615,000 refugees remained in towns, most never helped, living in shacks, warehouses and barracks. In late 1924 about 1,000 refugees moved from the Faliro public baths into hospital tents beside the unfinished Kokkinia houses; one stormy night they occupied the houses themselves, and the commission threatened to stop work. SOURCED: Kapokakis, Kyramargiou, Lafazani, Tyrovolas, "The Urban Settlement of Refugees, 1923–1930," *The Historical Review* 20(1), 2025, pp. 31–58 (helios.eie.gr, read via text extraction). [K1, K9, K4]
- **Long life of temporary housing:** anthropologist Renée Hirschon (fieldwork 1969–1983 in Yerania, part of Kokkinia) found that the prefabricated refugee housing outlasted its intended life partly because a bride's dowry required a separate dwelling within her family's house; men and women both upheld family honor, expressed largely through a clean, tidy home; shared hardship and memories of their former status forged a refugee identity. SOURCED: HRAF summary of Hirschon, *Heirs of the Greek Catastrophe* (1989; 1998). [K4, K6]
- **War:** On 17 August 1944 German troops and collaborationist Security Battalions (about 2,000–2,500) rounded up Kokkinia: 315–350 executed, 6,000–8,000 taken hostage, about 1,200 sent to concentration camps, house blocks burned; no Security Battalion member was convicted by the Greek state. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executions_of_Kokkinia. An Allied bombing raid on 11 January 1944 damaged the city and its industry (SOURCED: Bafounis 2022). [K9]

**The musicians and their work.** Markos Vamvakaris (b. 1905, Ano Syros, Roman Catholic family) came to Piraeus at about 12 and worked as stevedore, coal worker, shoe-shiner; he learned the bouzouki, first played in clandestine hashish dens, recorded from 1932, and died in Nikaia in 1972; autobiography 1978. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markos_Vamvakaris. Greek press: dockworker, coal-store porter and hide-skinner at the slaughterhouses (SOURCED: *Ta Nea*, 2017-08-04). The "famous quartet of Piraeus" (photographed in a Drapetsona studio, summer 1934): Vamvakaris (Syros), Anestis Delias (Smyrna), Stratos Pagioumtzis (Ayvalik), Giorgos Batis (Methana); none was from Piraeus; they played at Sarantopoulos's place in Drapetsona, opposite the brothels, whose owner had good relations with the security police. SOURCED: Thanasis Niarchos, "I xakousti Tetras," *Ta Nea*, 2017-08-04. Batis's café (opened 1931) is described as where many songs were written (SOURCED: Destination Piraeus, municipal tourism site). Many musicians worked hard jobs in coal stores, slaughterhouses and foundries (UNVERIFIED, Greek snippets). [K7, K13, SM:competence]

**Refugee musicians and the recording industry.** Refugees brought Anatolian and European instruments, Ottoman café music, and an Italianate mandolin style; refugee musicians Spyros Peristeris and Panagiotis Toundas became studio directors for the big record companies; the bouzouki entered a Greek studio in October 1931 and became the emblem of the music in the later 1930s, displacing the Asia Minor instruments. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebetiko. Sources caution against a strict split between a "Smyrna" and a "Piraeus" school (UNVERIFIED snippet). [K13]

**Sailors and the port's nightlife districts.** **Trouba** (named after a large water pump installed in the 1860s to supply steamships, removed between the wars): bars, cabarets and brothels from the early 20th century, best known 1944–1967; brothels moved there after the Vourla district closed in 1937; prices doubled when the US Sixth Fleet was in port; on **12 September 1967** police and the municipality (junta-era mayor Aristeidis Skylitsis) closed the bars and brothels; by 1970 the port authority controlled the area and most venues were demolished, as shipping companies moved to Akti Miaouli. SOURCED: parapolitika.gr, "On this day," 2019-09-12 (citing books by Speranza Vrana and Vasilis Pisimisis). Bars and cabarets continued afterward (UNVERIFIED, interview snippet). [K9, K6, K10]

**Recent transit.** In spring 2016, after Balkan border closures, thousands of asylum seekers (nearly 6,000 at one point, mostly from Syria, Iraq and Afghanistan) camped in ferry terminals and warehouses at Piraeus; by 28 July 2016 the area had been emptied. UNVERIFIED (snippets: HRW, Reuters via Jordan Times, The New Humanitarian). Postwar emigrants to Germany traveled by bus to Piraeus and by ship to Italy (UNVERIFIED, Kathimerini snippet); the Agios Dionysios railway station (1904–2004) is remembered locally as the station of emigration (SOURCED: efsyn.gr, 2022-02-08). [K9, P9]

**Port labor (K1, K9).** On 25 October 2021 dockworker Dimitris Daglis, employed by a subcontractor, was killed near gantry crane 10 on Pier II of the COSCO container terminal; workers blamed missing safety measures, intensification, crews of four instead of six, split shifts, part-time and daily-wage contracts and the absence of collective agreements; the container-handling union ENEDEP and the port workers' federation OMYLE called a 48-hour strike; a joint health-and-safety committee was proposed. SOURCED: OT, 2021-11-02. A court declared the strike illegal on a notice technicality (UNVERIFIED, partisan sources). [SM:man versus society]

## 6 · #90 Veracruz

**Claim to test (from the input):** the historic arrival port where newcomers of many origins landed before the road climbed to the high plateau; its public square holds regular open-air dancing, and its music grew from that mixture (K13, K10). **Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Veracruz was New Spain's chief port and one of the largest slave-trading ports in the Americas around 1600 (most captives were sent inland); it is described as Mexico's principal entry port for transoceanic immigrants (Spaniards, Cubans, Italians, Lebanese, Spanish Republican exiles of 1939). Open-air danzón is held on the Zócalo on set evenings each week. The danzón came from Cuba (1870s–1890s) with musicians displaced by Cuba's independence wars; son jarocho, the region's own music, blends Indigenous, southern Spanish and West African elements. Slavery is reported plainly below.

### A. Snapshot
- **Veracruz:** port city on the Gulf of Mexico, state of Veracruz. 2020 census: city 537,952; municipality 607,209; metro area 968,070 (Wikipedia) or 939,046 (another metro definition, UNVERIFIED). SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veracruz_(city). Population history: about 11,000 (1860), 32,663 (1900), 53,115 (1910), driven by railway and port works that drew laborers' families from the hinterland and region. SOURCED: Arturo E. García Niño, "Reglamento de la Unión de Estibadores y Jornaleros del Puerto de Veracruz, México, 1939," *Travesía* 23(1), 2021, pp. 53–94 (read via text extraction). Climate: tropical savanna, wet season June–October, about 1,564 mm rain a year; Hurricane Karl flooded the city in 2010 (SOURCED, Wikipedia).
- **Founding:** Hernán Cortés founded Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz on 22 April 1519; the town moved in 1525 to the Antigua river mouth (cut off from its anchorage) and around 1600 back opposite the island of San Juan de Ulúa, to curb smuggling. Fort San Juan de Ulúa: begun 1582, enlarged 1635, finished 1707; the Baluarte de Santiago (1635) is the only remnant of the city walls. SOURCED: Wikipedia. [K9]
- **Port today:** third in Mexico by containers, 1,299,337 TEU in 2025 (−0.2%); 25.03 million tonnes of commercial cargo (20.03 import, 5 export). SOURCED: TheLogisticsWorld, 2026-09-10. First in Mexico for vehicles, 993,603 units in 2023 (UNVERIFIED, port administration via snippet). [K7]
- **Defining feature:** for centuries Mexico's main gate from the Atlantic, at the foot of the climb inland to Xalapa and Mexico City.

### B. What else exists there (the people, the work, how they got there)
**Slavery (reported plainly).** By 1599 more than 10,000 captives from West, West Central and Eastern Africa had landed at San Juan de Ulúa; tens of thousands more followed; at the start of the 1600s Veracruz was one of the largest slave-trading ports in the Americas; most captives were sent to inland plantations and mines; in the absence of free settlers the city relied increasingly on enslaved Africans' labor. UNVERIFIED (snippets of Joseph Clark, *Veracruz and the Caribbean in the Seventeenth Century*, Cambridge UP; the Cambridge excerpt refused, 403). By 1640 nearly 150,000 enslaved people had been imported to New Spain; around 1570 Gaspar Yanga, a man from Central Africa, led people who fled a sugar plantation to the highlands near Mt. Orizaba; the community (about 500) raided caravans on the Camino Real between Veracruz and Mexico City; after a 1609 Spanish campaign ended in stalemate a treaty (1618) recognized a free town, San Lorenzo de los Negros, established by 1630 near Córdoba, renamed Yanga in 1932. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspar_Yanga (dates inconsistent within the article). In the 2020 census about 2% of Mexicans identified as Afro-descendant (first time asked); Veracruz state ranks third with about 215,435 people (UNVERIFIED, Conapo/CNDH snippets). [K9, P9]

**Disease and the climate of arrival.** Yellow fever ("vómito prieto/negro") struck newcomers unused to the coast; risk was highest April–October below 1,000 m; in 1809 the viceroy ordered a lazaretto (never documented as built), military patrols to stop sick travelers fleeing the port, and weekly death reports from the hospitals; in 1807 a doctor "acclimatized" soldiers at Xalapa by bleeding and purging; muleteers and foot travelers were inspected. SOURCED: Archivo General de la Nación story on Google Arts & Culture, "Black Vomit in the Port of Veracruz." [K1, K6, K5]

**Arrivals of the 19th–20th centuries.** Italians and Cubans settled after 1821 (SOURCED, Wikipedia). In the last third of the 19th century the Cuban wars brought more than 3,000 Cubans of all conditions, from intellectuals and businessmen to cigar rollers and cane laborers; a 1864 register found most Cubans entered Mexico through Veracruz and often stayed (UNVERIFIED, snippets of UNAM studies; the UNAM journal host refused one download). Lebanese immigrants (mostly Catholic and Orthodox, during Ottoman rule) arrived at Veracruz; the Centro Libanés erected a monument to the Lebanese emigrant in 1970 (UNVERIFIED, México Desconocido snippet); statues on the Malecón honor Spanish, Jewish and Lebanese migrants (UNVERIFIED, travel guide). On **13 June 1939** the ship *Sinaia* from Sète landed 1,599 Spanish Republican refugees at Veracruz under President Lázaro Cárdenas's offer of asylum (organized by SERE and CTARE); intellectuals were only 10–15% of the exiles, the majority being workers, farmers, teachers, medical staff and engineers, who were dispersed to Morelos, the State of Mexico and the capital; a plaque in the port thanks Mexico. SOURCED: CNDH, "Buque Sinaia llega a México con refugiados españoles," 2025 (PDF, read via text extraction). About 26 ships brought over 25,000 Spanish refugees 1939–1942 (UNVERIFIED, Milenio snippet). Veracruz is called the principal port of entry for transoceanic immigrants in Mexican historiography (UNVERIFIED, INAH snippet). [P9, K4]

**Dock labor (K1, K7).** "Port works" inaugurated 1902; the Compañía Terminal de Veracruz (1906) joined rail and sea operations, run by foreign managers; a dock chief hired foremen from people he trusted, mostly not born in the city; crews were named each day at 6:00, with no fixed hours and 25 centavos an hour day or night; cargo in sacks and boxes over 100 kg moved on backs and handcarts. In July 1911 dockers stopped work; with the military commander and customs chief mediating, a commission of eight shipping firms, the Chamber of Commerce and a workers' representative agreed a 9-hour day from 6:00 (formerly 11+), $3.00 a day, 50 centavos per extra hour, $6.00 for holidays worked; the **Unión de Estibadores y Jornaleros del Puerto de Veracruz** was registered on 11 July 1911 and signed the first collective contract with all shipping agents on 17 July 1913; its 1939 work regulations are the only ones known for the trade. SOURCED: García Niño 2021. [K1, K7, K14, SM:man versus society]

**Rent strike, 1922 (K9, K1).** Tenants in cramped rented rooms (15 pesos a month when a worker earned about 2 pesos a day) formed the Sindicato Revolucionario de Inquilinos in February 1922 (led by Herón Proal); tenants stopped paying rent and posted signs that they were on strike; the movement spread to Mexico City, Xalapa, Orizaba, Guadalajara and San Luis Potosí; sex workers were among the tenants who led the July protests; on the night of 5–6 July 1922 soldiers attacked the union's headquarters, with an undetermined number of dead and wounded; the strike faded by October. SOURCED: Octavio Torres, *Expansión Política*, 2022-08-22. A stevedore, Rafael García Aulí, was mayor and on the first committee; the movement led to a tenancy law in April 1923 (UNVERIFIED, snippets). [K9, K4]

**Invasions.** French attack 1838 (Pastry War); US siege and occupation 1847; US occupation 21 April – 23 November 1914, resisted by naval cadets and townspeople, after which the city received its fourth "Heroic" title. SOURCED: Wikipedia (1838, 1847, 1914 dates); the cadets and fourth title UNVERIFIED (Mexican government and SEMAR snippets). [K9]

### C. Cultural locations (names)
- **Danzón on the Zócalo** (Plaza de Armas, at Lerdo de Tejada and Gutiérrez Zamora): Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays about 7 p.m.; anyone may join, no invitation needed; also Sundays at 6 p.m. at the Plazuela de la Campana, three blocks away. SOURCED: Zona Turística, "Tardes de danzón" (undated listing). Wikipedia says danzón is danced most evenings on the Zócalo, brought from Cuba in the 1870s (SOURCED). History: by the 1930s danzón was considered local; early orchestras included Cuban immigrants (Severiano Pacheco's, the Chinos Ramírez); from the 1940s–50s orchestras were made up of Veracruz and other Mexican musicians (Camerino Vásquez, Gildardo Hernández, Cipriano Camarero, **Alma de Sotavento**); the Veracruz style keeps closely to the beat with particular use of the musical pauses, unlike the syncopated Cuban style; in the 1980s promoter Natalia Pineda Burgos and dancer Sigfrido Alcántara won the opening of the Zócalo to danzón dancers, and the first dancers' circle, "Bailadores de Danzón Hoy y Siempre," was founded; Rosa Abdalá founded "Tres generaciones del danzón veracruzano" in 1989; the 1991 film *Danzón*, shot in the port, coincided with more dancers; orchestras Alma de Veracruz and Manzanita y su Son Cuatro Tropical play in squares and parks; Sunday dancing at Villa del Mar. SOURCED: PORTUS Online, "Puerto de vaivén acompasado: Veracruz y sus danzones durante la segunda mitad del siglo XX" (read via text extraction). [K13, K10, K3, K4]
- **Danzón's arrival and class:** danzón spread from Havana and Matanzas (in Black communities, before its "official" 1879 date) into white social clubs around 1870 and abroad with musicians exiled by Cuba's independence wars, reaching Mexico via Mérida and Veracruz; it was not a "fine" dance in the late 19th century; older Veracruz dancers told researchers they did not dance by counting; today's codified style and its revival public (largely retired middle-class people) differ from the past. SOURCED: María Cristina Tamariz Estrada, interview with Alejandro Madrid and Robin Moore, *Balajú* no. 19, 2023 (Universidad Veracruzana; read via text extraction). [K13, K6]
- **Son jarocho and the fandango:** a music blending Indigenous (mainly Huastec), southern Spanish Baroque (fandango) and West African elements, developed over about 250 years on the coast of Veracruz and southern Tamaulipas; instruments: jarana, requinto, arpa jarocha, leona; the **tarima** (wooden platform struck with the heels) acts as percussion; singers trade improvised décimas. SOURCED: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_jarocho. A revival ("movimiento jaranero") from the mid-1970s (group Mono Blanco) and 1979 onward, centered on the Encuentro de Jaraneros in Tlacotalpan (UNVERIFIED, snippets). [K13]
- **Carnaval de Veracruz:** held since 1866 (SOURCED, Wikipedia); eight days in February or March; on Carnival Tuesday night a figure representing "bad humor" is burned on the Zócalo after a street parade of comparsas, batucadas and baton troupes; a "Bando Solemne" of the carnival king is read aloud, a satirical decree in the tradition of colonial proclamations, written in the 1980s by local chronicler Francisco Rivera Ávila; fireworks follow. SOURCED: PORTUS Online, "El Carnaval de Veracruz: la quema del mal humor y los bandos solemnes" (read via text extraction). A first "ugly king" in 1926; a 2017 effigy of a US border wall; about a million visitors (UNVERIFIED, press snippets). [K2, K3]
- **Festival Internacional Afrocaribeño:** since 1994, run by the Instituto Veracruzano de la Cultura (IVEC) with the state government, usually July–August, to research and promote the state's Afro-mestizo heritage; 27th edition 2023; Cuban, Haitian, Colombian, Dominican and Venezuelan delegations; suspended in some years for lack of state money. UNVERIFIED (La Jornada and UV snippets; the scholarly article host returned 503). [K3, K8]
- **Gran Café de la Parroquia:** founded officially 1808 as a general store and café opposite the parish church (now cathedral), known by its present name from 1858; customers clink spoons on their glasses to call the waiter, who pours hot milk from a height into coffee (the "lechero"); the custom is said to date from 1890 when tram drivers rang their bells to get coffee brought out; regulars have included politicians and entertainers; Porfirio Díaz breakfasted there before leaving for exile. SOURCED: Rodrigo Osegueda, México Desconocido. The business later split in two over ownership (UNVERIFIED, Mexico News Daily snippet). [K10, K12, K6]
- **Landmarks:** Zócalo with its portales; the Malecón (seafront promenade); San Juan de Ulúa (fort, later prison); Baluarte de Santiago (museum); Veracruz Aquarium (1992). SOURCED: Wikipedia. Evening music (danzón, son jarocho, salsa and others) spills from cafés and cantinas under the portales (UNVERIFIED, Fodor's snippet).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- Being the gate meant receiving everyone: enslaved Africans in the 16th–17th centuries, Cuban exiles and workers in the 19th, Lebanese, Spanish and other immigrants and Republican refugees in the 20th. - The port works of 1902–1911 brought rail and dock labor, a doubling of population, organized stevedores, and crowded rented rooms whose tenants struck in 1922. SOURCED (García Niño; Expansión). [K7, K9]
- Public squares carry the music: the Zócalo was opened to danzón dancers by a citizens' initiative in the 1980s. [K10]
