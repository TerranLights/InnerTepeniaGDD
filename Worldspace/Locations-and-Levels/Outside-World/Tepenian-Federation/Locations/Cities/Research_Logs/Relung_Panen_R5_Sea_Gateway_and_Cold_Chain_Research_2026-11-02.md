### 1.3 Rules and guidance on pairing ships (IMO text read)

- **IMO MSC.1/Circ.1184 (31 May 2006), "Enhanced contingency planning guidance for passenger ships operating in areas
  remote from SAR facilities"** (PDF downloaded and converted, verbatim, SOURCED). Among enhancements to be considered:
  "**voyage 'pairing', i.e., mutual exchange of information that may be available to the SAR Authority or the vessel
  operator with reference to other passenger ships operating in the same area, so that, if two or more passenger ships
  are operating in the same general area at the same time, each can be used as a SAR facility in case of accident to
  another**." The same text uses a five-day "time to recover" parameter: a plan must "recover those who have taken to
  survival craft within the five day 'time to recover' parameter and/or within survival times." This is an
  exchange-of-information measure for passenger ships, not an instruction that two ships sail together; the circular
  does not say that a cargo or support ship may serve as a pair.
- Resolution A.999(25) (voyage planning for passenger ships in remote areas) exists and says ships in remote Arctic
  ice-covered waters should also refer to MSC/Circ.1056-MEPC/Circ.399 (snippet, UNVERIFIED). Its text was not read.
- **NOT FOUND:** any Polar Code or IMO text that requires, defines or regulates a two-ship pair (support ship plus
  icebreaker) for cargo or passenger work in ice; and any text giving convoy spacing.

## 2. Casey as the port, the Casey-Wilkins link, and heavy cargo over land

### 2.1 How Casey is actually resupplied by sea (SOURCED unless marked)

- **The ship does not tie up at a deep-water berth.** Records read: *Aurora Australis* "anchored in Newcomb Bay" (Jan 2019); "Containers and plant were craned onto a barge, moved across the bay, and lifted ashore at Casey wharf. Cargo then moved by truck and skid steer to the Greenstore warehouse"; fuel "ran from the ship via floats to a lower fuel farm at the wharf, then by hose over snow and ice to an upper fuel farm" (AAD "This week at Casey," 11 Jan 2019). The French inspection (2024) says the same for the *Nuyina*: "The SAB tanks are filled during the Nuyina's annual resupply visit by pumping fuel from the ship using floating hoses," and "There are no floating boats at Casey for the 2023/24 season. The boats used to push the barge and supervise operations on the water are on board the Nuyina and are used during its stopovers."
- **Season totals as recorded:**
  - Jan 2019: ">550 t cargo unloaded," "approximately 970,000 litres of fuel," "nearly 2 km of hose," "nearly 32 hours of pumping" on a four-hour-on, eight-hour-off roster; "Fourteen days in total"; the ship left the bay "more than once" when wind "exceed[ed] safe loading and barge operations limits" (no numeric limit given); boats kept the fuel line free of ice.
  - Jan 2022: *Nuyina* delivered "one million litres" of SAB diesel in about 36 hours; Newcomb Bay "glassy-smooth"; "ice lines" set before pumping; *Happy Dragon* then worked "28 days": "Casey received around 1,200 t of cargo and dispatched 570 t" of return cargo; a wharf crane lifted an excavator and a demountable accommodation unit onto a sled-mounted platform; the final load ashore was a traverse sled; goods bound for Australia were pressure-washed.
  - Dec 2025 (planned): Hobart loading 15-25 Nov "Casey resupply cargo, 2 jet barges, helicopters, Science Tender"; Casey 8-21 Dec "resupply and refuel."
  - A snippet of an AAD news item (2025 season, date uncertain) says the *Nuyina* "would take just over a week" to move about 500 t of cargo, 140,000 L of water and around 800,000 L of fuel; the matching Davis article (12 Dec 2025, SOURCED) says "Close to 490 tonnes of cargo was unloaded" at Davis; the 500 t / 140,000 L / 800,000 L set belongs to Davis (consistent with the Davis figures in the Intake D file), not Casey. Casey's own 2025-26 tonnage: **NOT FOUND**.
- **Ice at the wharf:** snippets of AAD Casey reports describe thick fast ice "stubbornly sitting in front of the wharf" (2018), ice blown into the bay "completely obstructing the wharf," an icebreaker (*AA2*) "dispatched into the fray to break up the ice and assist the barge getting to the wharf," a barge dodging "largish ice floes," and *Happy Dragon*'s crew describing ice filling Newcomb Bay "completely surrounding the ship and preventing us from being able to undertake operations" (all UNVERIFIED snippets; pages other than the 2019 and 2022 weekly pages were not opened).
- **The bay:** Wikipedia (snippet) gives Newcomb Bay as "a sheltered bay about 1 mile (1.6 km) in extent, between Clark Peninsula and Bailey Peninsula," with a 1957 USS *Glacier* sounding party (UNVERIFIED). A shipping-directory page (marinelink ports, fetched, secondary) says Casey "does not have conventional deep-water port facilities," gives the sea-access window as "November to March," and lists "cranes, cargo nets, and helicopters" and small landing craft; it gives no depth or draft.
- **Plans for the wharf:** the French inspection lists "Modernisation of the slipway and wharf" under **"Postponed"** (SOURCED). No study, drawing or specification of a Casey wharf upgrade was found.
- **Fuel storage at Casey (French inspection, SOURCED):** 1,362,000 L of SAB in total; 812,000 L at the Lower Fuel Farm near the wharf (6 bunded tanks + 8 double-skin tanks) and 450,000 L at the Upper Fuel Farm; 2 km of fuel pipe; 320,000 L of jet fuel in 1,600 200-litre drums at mid-season; "the arrival of the Nuyina on site in December can cause difficulties in managing reserves in the months prior" and "snow-clearing operations on the Wilkins aerodrome runway can lead to over-consumption."
- **Casey's population (French report, SOURCED):** capacity 120; 156 for one week in 2023-24 "due to the temporary unavailability of airborne resources"; about 100 average in summer; **30 in winter**; summer staff list of 87 includes Maintenance 23, Aviation 12, Helicopters 4, Traverse 8, Chefs 4, Supply officers 2; winter list of 30 includes Maintenance 11, Mechanical 3, Aviation 3, Traverse 3, Chef 1, Supply officer 1.
- **All waste goes out once a year by ship**; "Only some of the cardboard boxes can be evacuated by air through Wilkins... weighing around 1 tonne" (SOURCED).

### 2.2 Casey and the air link at Wilkins (SOURCED, French inspection and AAD schedule)

- **Wilkins Aerodrome:** 67 km southeast of Casey (the report says 67 km in one place and 69 km in another); about 750 m above sea level; "usable runway of 3 kilometres in length and approximately 300 meters in width"; "operational from late October to early March on average, with an inability to use the runway in the heart of summer, namely the months of December and January, due to surface deformation of the runway at ground temperatures above -5°C"; the site moves "approximately 12 meters per year," so "a general repositioning of the entire infrastructure every 4 years"; in winter the aerodrome is "completely evacuated."
- **2023-24 flights:** "23 intercontinental flights: either by military aircraft C-17 (7 flights)... or by Airbus A-319 (15 flights)" (7 + 15 = 22, not 23: the report's own totals disagree; flagged). A319 "maximum of 32 passengers from Hobart to Wilkins and a maximum of 38 ... from Wilkins to Hobart." The report elsewhere says the C-17 made "8 to 9 flights ... out of a total of around twenty intercontinental flights per year."
- **2025-26 listed flights:** A319 on 26 Nov, 2, 9, 15 Dec, 10, 27 Feb, 6 Mar (four more cancelled); C-17 on 15, 20, 29 Nov and 18, 21, 23 Feb (AAD schedule page). The page's own "Discrepancies" are noted in the log.
- **The land link between Wilkins and Casey:** "Transportation between Casey Station and Wilkins Aerodrome is provided by land: Incoming and outgoing expeditioners are transported by a bus (Terra bus with approximately 36 seats). Equipment and materials (such as cold containers) transported by the C17 military aircraft are delivered by tractors and trailers to Casey station" (SOURCED). The vehicle list names "a 40-seater bus to transport passengers between Wilkins aerodrome and Casey station." The Wilkins site "is fully supplied by Casey for its fuel and food needs." Casey keeps a traverse fleet (tractors, snow groomers, caravans) stored at the station and "in another area 25 km from the station."
- **2015 C-17 proof of concept (snippets, UNVERIFIED):** more than 109 t over five flights from Hobart; one flight unloaded 12,340 kg; a Hägglunds dual-cab vehicle was among the load.
- **Earlier file C (SOURCED there):** the Denman campaign's 39 t went by C-17 to Wilkins, then "by snow vehicles and track trailers 70 kilometers to Casey," then by Basler.

### 2.3 Heavy cargo over land from Casey

- **Casey-Wilkins** is the only road-like leg in the record that is used for bulk cargo: 67-70 km; tractors and trailers (above). No tonnage per trip, trip time or season total for it was found (**NOT FOUND**).
- **Casey to Law Dome:** earlier file C holds the 2021 trip (120 km inland, eight hours each way with two Hägglunds and a tractor; SOURCED there).
- **Million Year Ice Core traverse (the long Casey haul), 2025:** left Casey 1 Nov, "arrived at Dome C North on 18 November, 2025, after 17 days and 1168km of travel inland" with a 10-person team (AAD ice core diary, snippet, UNVERIFIED as to page text); equipment "62 tonnes" hauled 1,200 km (snippet of National Tribune, UNVERIFIED) **versus** "493 tonnes of gear" and 643 t gross in the earlier file C (SOURCED there from AAD "Long haul for science begins"). **Conflict, unresolved:** the two figures cannot both describe the same load; the difference may be gear for one trip versus a season's staged loads, but no page read resolves it. The French report adds that in 2023-24 a second traverse "test[ed] the rolling stock ... and transport[ed] 40 tonnes of materials."
- **DERIVED speed:** 1,168 km / 17 days = 68.7 km a day. At that rate 350 km takes 5.1 days and 400 km takes 5.8 days one way (arithmetic only; the sea-level to 3,200 m profile of the Dome C route is not the Casey-oasis terrain, and no traverse has been recorded over the Casey-oasis line; see file C).

## 5. Perishable export: how long produce keeps

All figures in this section are **SOURCED** from the USDA *Agriculture Handbook 66*, "The Commercial Storage of Fruits, Vegetables, and Florist and Nursery Stocks" (3.6 MB PDF, full text converted and read), unless marked. Temperatures are as printed.

### 5.1 Leafy greens

- **Head lettuce (chapter on lettuce):** "Lettuce should be quickly cooled and maintained as close to 0 °C (32 °F) as possible with 98 to 100% RH. Head types are better adapted to prolong storage than are the other types, but **none keep longer than 4 weeks, and about half that time at 5 °C (41 °F)**." Freezing point −0.2 °C. Respiration at 0 °C: head lettuce 6-17 and leaf lettuce 19-27 mg CO2 per kg per hour; 5 °C: 13-20 and 24-35. DERIVED heat of respiration: the handbook's factor is 61 kcal per tonne per day per mg/kg/h, so leaf lettuce at 0 °C is about 1,160-1,650 kcal per tonne per day.
- **Controlled atmosphere and overseas transit:** "Levels of 1 to 3% O2 at temperatures of 0 to 5 °C reduce russet spotting"; "**If lettuce needs to be in transit overseas for a month, an atmosphere of 2% CO2 and 3% O2 is recommended** because the reduction in decay achieved by 2% CO2 outweighs the danger of injury"; "Intact heads do not benefit from elevated CO2," and brown stain can develop above 2% CO2 when moved to air at 10 °C. Fresh-cut lettuce: browning controlled by packaging in "<1% O2 and 10% CO2." Russet spotting is caused by ethylene around 5 °C and "easily controlled" below 2 °C in an ethylene-free store; lettuce "should not be stored with ethylene-producing commodities such as apples, cantaloupes, pears, and peaches."
- **Other greens and spinach:** "Greens should be stored near 0 °C (32 °F) with 95 to 98% RH... When properly handled, **greens in general may be kept for about 2 weeks**... kale... up to 3 weeks." Spinach benefits slightly from 7-10% O2 with 5-10% CO2; "Neither CA nor modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) is commonly used commercially for leafy greens other than spinach." Spinach respiration at 0 °C is 19-22 and at 5 °C 32-58 mg CO2 per kg per hour (two to three times the lettuce rate at the same temperature).
- Snippets (UNVERIFIED) of the UC Davis romaine sheet (Sept 2025) say 0 °C and above 95% humidity support about 21 days; the UC Davis pages returned HTTP 403 and were not worked around.

### 5.2 Tomatoes

- "Mature-green fruit will ripen normally at 13 to 21 °C... ripe tomato fruit can be stored at 10 °C without visible symptoms of chilling injury, though flavor and aroma are negatively affected." Storage "<13 °C (55 °F) retards ripening and can lead to development of chilling injury, particularly in tomatoes at the mature-green stage. Red tomatoes can be stored at 7 °C (45 °F) for a couple of days." Chilling table: tomatoes ripe 7-10 °C (water soaking, softening, decay); mature-green 13 °C (poor color, alternaria rot). Ethylene: as little as 0.5 µL/L triggers ripening.
- **Controlled atmosphere:** "Storage in 3% O2 and 97% N2 extended postharvest life of mature-green tomatoes for **6 weeks at 13 °C** without off flavors (Parsons et al. 1970)." Recommended CA table: mature green 12-20 °C, 3-5% O2, 2-3% CO2, benefit "slight"; red 10-15 °C, 3-5% O2, 3-5% CO2, benefit "moderate." Packed tomatoes are "cooled to 12 °C (54 °F) for storage."
- Snippets (UNVERIFIED, UC Davis via search): mature-green fruit held "about 14 days at 12.5 °C before ripening" and ripe fruit 8-10 days at the optimum range.

### 5.3 Potatoes

- "Long-term storage of potato tubers—**up to 12 mo**—requires that they be cured" (about 20 °C, 80-100% RH, 1-2 weeks); "Quality tubers can be stored for **2 to 12 mo**, depending on quality at harvest, quality of storage facilities, variety, and whether or not sprout inhibitors are used." Holding temperatures: seed 4-5 °C; fresh consumption 7-10 °C; frying 10-15 °C; chipping 15-20 °C. "Respiration rate of potato tubers is lowest at 2 to 3 °C." Sprouting accelerates above 4 °C; freezing at about -1 °C; mahogany browning at 1-2 °C; "Maintaining 95 to 99% RH is required at all times." "The usefulness of CA storage is minimal, and economic justification doubtful." Darkness is essential to prevent greening and solanine.

### 5.4 Grain

- **FAO *Manual of the prevention of post-harvest grain losses* (fetched, SOURCED):** safe moisture contents for long-term storage "maize 13%, wheat 13%, paddy 14%, rice 13%," those that give equilibrium at 65-70 percent relative humidity, "the range where mould starts to develop." The page gives no temperature threshold or storage duration; it says higher temperature raises respiration and "Low, even temperatures and low relative humidity are favourable."
- **FAO table for one year at 27 degrees C and 70 percent RH (snippet, UNVERIFIED):** maximum moisture 13.5 percent for maize, wheat and sorghum, 15 percent for paddy, 13 percent for milled rice, 16 percent for millet (adapted from FAO 1970). FAO maize note (snippet): about 12 percent safe at 30 degrees C and about 14 percent at 10 degrees C; a two-year trial held maize at 14.5 percent moisture at 12 degrees C while samples at 25 degrees C deteriorated rapidly.
- **Hermetic storage (snippet, UNVERIFIED):** a 180-day maize trial held 12 percent grain well (1.2 percent weight loss against 35.8 percent in polypropylene bags); discolored grain rose several-fold at 14-18 percent.
- **NOT FOUND:** an FAO or IRRI table of storage duration at each moisture and temperature combination.

### 5.5 By sea and by air (what the sources give)

- **By sea:** the lettuce rule above (a month in transit possible with a 2 percent CO2, 3 percent O2 atmosphere and 0 degrees C), greens about 2 weeks, tomato mature-green about 6 weeks in CA at 13 degrees C, potato months in the dark at 7-10 degrees C. The Hobart-Casey supply line in 4.1 takes about 10-13 days from the end of loading to arrival, including bunkering and other stops.
- **By air:** the Wilkins flights run within a day (4.1). **NOT FOUND:** air-freight shelf-life data for leafy vegetables from Australia (4.2).

## FINAL: what the sources support, do not support, and NOT FOUND

### What the sources support

1. **Two-ship working in polar ice is documented in current practice, and the roles differ.** At Zhongshan in 2024 and 2025 one icebreaker (*Xuelong 2*) opened the way, ferried cargo to the unloading point and gave escort, and the other (*Xuelong*) carried the bulk; in the 2024 operation 280 t in 17 containers moved ship to ship in about 11 hours across an 18 km chaotic ice zone with ice up to about 4 m. At McMurdo one icebreaker escorts each chartered cargo ship through a channel of 7 to more than 20 miles.
2. **Ship-based helicopter flying on the Casey coast is limited by seats, hours and distance, and is small in scale.** The October 2025 Casey fly-off put 14 people and cargo ashore in seven two-hour flights at about 100 nmi; the 2026-27 voyage plans a stop 80 nmi off Casey; Zhongshan moved 101 people in about two weeks. No record of hundreds of people moved through ice by helicopter was found.
3. **Casey is not served by a deep-water berth.** Ships anchor in Newcomb Bay; cargo goes by 45.5 t barge to a wharf; fuel by floating hose; the wharf and slipway modernization is "postponed"; ice has repeatedly blocked the wharf approach; Casey took 550 t in 2019 and about 1,200 t from a general cargo ship in 2021-22 in 28 days.
4. **The air link is a hub 67 km inland.** Wilkins takes C-17 (7 in 2023-24) and A319 (15) flights, cannot be used in December and January because the surface deforms above -5 degrees C, and its cargo (including cold containers) reaches Casey by tractor and trailer, its passengers by bus.
5. **Heavy overland haul has measured rates.** Progress-Vostok delivered 2,486 t in 17 traverses of 1,480 km in a 102-day window with 45 vehicles; SPoT takes about 52 days for 2,060 miles with 8-10 people; Concordia tows 40-50 t at 6 L/km; fuel is 0.12-0.15 L per towed t-km (Concordia) to 0.24 L per delivered t-km (SPoT). All run in the summer window; none is year-round.
6. **IMO's only pairing text found is an information-sharing measure among passenger ships** (MSC.1/Circ.1184), tied to a five-day time to recover.
7. **Remote food supply runs on freezers, dry stores and air.** Davis's 490 t resupply held about 32 t of food of which 8.3 t was fresh; frozen and dry food last "12-18 months"; Casey grows winter vegetables in a hydroponic building run by one appointed person and volunteers; Svalbard and other remote places fly fresh produce.
8. **Keeping times (USDA handbook):** head lettuce at most 4 weeks at 0 degrees C (half that at 5 degrees C), a month overseas only in a 2 percent CO2, 3 percent O2 atmosphere; greens about 2 weeks; mature-green tomatoes 6 weeks in CA at 13 degrees C, with chilling injury below 13 degrees C; potatoes 2-12 months; grain safe at 13-14 percent moisture.
9. **One published staffing ratio exists in an industry filing:** about 2.5 employees per 1,000 pallet positions across Americold's 239 warehouses (all staff, DERIVED).
