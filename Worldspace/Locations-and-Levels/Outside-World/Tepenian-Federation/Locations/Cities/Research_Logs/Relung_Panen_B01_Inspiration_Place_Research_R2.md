## Pick 1 · Virgin Lands Campaign, northern Kazakhstan (Tselinograd)

**Round 2 focus:** the settlement stages and off-season shelter, the separate communities (deportees, Baltic specialists, Poles, Germans, Kazakhs), what free time was, the K6 question (rules, drinking, discipline and who enforced it), and how the campaign is marked today. Round 1 threads chased: everyday rules and alcohol; a Virgin Lands museum; deportee communities' own spaces; Kazakh pastoral land loss.

### A. Other settings
- **The settlement in stages [K4, K5, P3, P4]:** on arrival, half a year in tents, "from the end of April to the end of October"; single workers in shared twenty-place tents with double walls and roofs and windows of thick light-yellow cellophane; married couples in separate four-place tents without the wool lining that kept out cold; mattresses and pillows stuffed with dry, sharp feather grass; the state-farm office was itself a tent. Only toward winter did settlers build two barracks of adobe (clay mixed with straw) and several sod-roofed dugouts; in the cold, indoor temperature stayed below zero, water froze inside, and workers melted ice in metal mugs on small iron stoves (A. V. Belyaev memoir, quoted in A. M. Belov and A. A. Rybin, "Povsednevnaya zhizn' na tseline v 1954–1964 gg.," *Vestnik antropologii* 2019 no. 1, pp. 80–87, journals.iea.ras.ru, full text read, SOURCED).
- **A new settlement's first buildings, in order [P5]:** at the "Klyuchevskoy" state farm, Akmola oblast, Muscovites and settlers from Ivanovo began on 12 April 1954 by digging foundation pits for prefabricated houses and marking out streets; the first house was handed over by the end of April; then came a canteen, a bathhouse, a power station, and a school was laid down (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED). Adobe was an unfamiliar material: it cracked settlers' hands and badly laid walls washed away in rain; a Yaroslavl brigade was the first on its farm to master it and became the farm's leading construction brigade (SOURCED).
- **Clinics [K5, K9]:** first-aid posts were not built everywhere; built feldsher posts often stood empty for lack of staff; inexperienced newcomers were injured often: 110,800 injuries registered in the Kazakh SSR's new-land districts in 1954 and 154,300 in 1955; 13 fatal accidents in October 1954 alone; permanent clinics (polyclinics) appeared only by the early 1960s (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing GARF, SOURCED).
- **Nuclear testing nearby [K9]:** some Kazakh new-land farms lay within reach of the Semipalatinsk test site; a settler at the "Mirny" farm recalled seeing test flashes and feeling air waves at least five times; the article reports later cancers among the settlement's residents (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing L. A. Predybailo 2014, SOURCED as the article's account).
- **Schools [K4]:** about 750 schools and about 19 million m² of housing built during the campaign, over half the housing rural (A. Caukenov, zakon.kz, 7 Apr 2016, SOURCED). A former pupil of a school in Shamalgan recalled classmates who were Chechens, Meskhetians, Germans, Balkars, Uighurs, Ukrainians, Koreans and Russians, and school evenings each devoted to the culture of one people of the USSR (informant quoted in Mazhitova et al., "The Baltic Peoples in the Development of Virgin Lands in Kazakhstan," *Journal of Ecohumanism* 3(6), 2024, pp. 1960–1972, full text read, SOURCED).
- **A house that looked different [K4, P3]:** Baltic settlers built houses to their own pattern: Lithuanian houses painted in colors tied to the home region, with hedges instead of fences and gardens and lawns; Estonian houses with front gardens and full yards of livestock; Latvian houses with gable thatched roofs. A Kazakh informant recalled a Lithuanian neighbor explaining an unwritten rule among neighbors over who kept the more beautiful, cleaner house and plot (Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED).

### B. Subcultures
- **Arrival streams, more precisely [P9]:** more than 640,000 people arrived in Kazakhstan 1954 to 1956, 45.3% of the total rural population then living on the new lands (UNVERIFIED, search snippet of a Buketov University repository abstract); 640,000+ is also stated by zakon.kz 2016 (SOURCED). In 1954: 25,000 Komsomol volunteers in the first three weeks, over 75,000 by June, and 11,000 demobilized soldiers from September to December; agricultural workers in the new-land districts rose more than fivefold 1954 to 1959; oblast populations rose 41% (Akmola), 37% (Kokchetav), 56% (Kustanai), 44% (Pavlodar) (A. Mikhailov, informburo.kz, 17 Feb 2018, SOURCED). From the Ivanovo, Kostroma and Yaroslavl oblasts alone 23,161 people left for the new lands over the period, mostly aged 18 to 40 (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED).
- **A men's frontier, then a recruitment of women [K4, K9]:** the arrivals were overwhelmingly male from the start; *Pravda* printed an appeal from young women of the "Marinovsky" state farm calling on their peers to come; responses reported included 500 young women to North Kazakhstan oblast and 300 from Vinnytsia, Kirovohrad and other Ukrainian oblasts, plus women from the Baltic republics (informburo.kz 2018, SOURCED).
- **People already there, by compulsion [K9, P9]:** in 1954 Kokchetav oblast held 55,814 adult special settlers: 30,368 Germans, 8,981 Chechens and Ingush, 716 Tajiks, 15,749 Poles. Poles deported from Soviet Ukraine in 1936 lived compactly in Shortandy district (Akmola) and in the Krasnoarmeysky, Kellerovsky and Chkalovsky (now Tayynsha) districts; Tayynsha station was the transit and reception point; 27 labor settlements were set up in Krasnoarmeysky district. Many of these kolkhozes and labor settlements were turned into new-land state farms; in 1954, 15 Poles chaired kolkhozes and 35 were brigade leaders in Kokchetav oblast, and several deported Poles were later made Heroes of Socialist Labor as tractor and combine operators. Before the war special settlers could move only within their district; during the war they were restricted and attached to special commandant's offices; teachers came from German and Polish teacher-training colleges in Ukraine (A. S. Musagalieva and R. M. Musabekova, "Polyaki v osvoenii tselinnykh i zalezhnykh zemel' v Severnom Kazakhstane," *Vestnik Karagandinskogo universiteta*, History series, 2025, full text read, SOURCED).
- **Baltic specialists [K4, K9]:** Latvian and Lithuanian "construction and installation trains" (mobile construction units) were sent from 1961; from one Lithuanian train, 32 workers left without permission and 20 were sent home by decision of the train's general meeting for drunkenness and absenteeism, and two were prosecuted for hooliganism (State Archive of Astana, cited in Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED). Baltic informants describe speaking Lithuanian or Estonian at home and Russian in public, holding holidays quietly, living scattered, frequent mixed marriages (Estonians with Germans, Lithuanians with Ukrainians), and "keeping a low profile" (SOURCED). Today: an Estonian Cultural Center of Kazakhstan (Almaty), a Lithuanian Community of Karaganda, and a Belarusian-Baltic Association in Kostanai (SOURCED).
- **German religious communities [K4, K6, K2]:** in North Kazakhstan oblast in the late 1950s about 7,000 Germans lived in all 11 districts; the state's commissioner for religious affairs estimated 60 to 65% of adults were believers, most of them not openly. Lutherans (65 to 70% of believers) and Catholics were the main groups, with smaller Baptist, Mennonite, Adventist and Pentecostal groups, often unregistered. The largest unregistered Lutheran group, at Peterfeld (Petropavlovsk district), had 28 weekly attenders and a list of 90 confirmed Lutherans (22 men, 68 women) who asked to bring Pastor E. Bachmann from Akmolinsk for Easter; the commissioner recommended the Lutheran center be registered in Karaganda oblast instead, to keep it out of the oblast. A Catholic group at Asanovo, with 35 to 40 attenders, applied three times to register. Funeral and wedding rites, baptism, a harvest-thanks festival, Christmas and Easter were kept in almost all families; when a religious holiday fell on a working day, mass absences from work brought conflict with party bodies; younger people were drifting away (Zh. S. Mazhitova et al., "Everyday Religious Life of German Population in North Kazakhstan Region in the late 1950s," *Bulletin of L. N. Gumilyov ENU*, History series, 2023, full text read, SOURCED). Lutheran congregations met in private houses, gradually rebuilt as houses of prayer; services were in German until 1993, then Russian; heavy emigration in the 1990s; a church building in the capital completed in 2017 (Lutheran World Federation, 5 Jul 2019, SOURCED).
- **Kazakhs [K9, P9]:** a settler recalled conflicts with local Kazakh youth and elders because plowing took the pastures of their horses and camels; in one farm after the harvest all tractors were disabled with sledgehammer blows to the engine blocks; a KGB officer briefed visiting Kostroma students that metal rods had been found driven into fields to wreck combines, and told them to walk fields before harvesting (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing memoirs and the authors' own interviews, SOURCED as the article's account). Plowing reduced pastures and so cut livestock production (zakon.kz 2016, SOURCED); "the centuries-old nomadic economy was broken, and pastures suitable for animal husbandry irretrievably disappeared" (UNVERIFIED, search-summary wording). ### C. Leisure (how free time was organized)
- Free time on the farms went to the cinema, the library and the club, amateur art circles, and sports competitions between state farms (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing Yaroslavl archives, SOURCED). Club and house-of-culture openings were treated as farm events; houses of culture hosted dances, games and festivities as well as ideological work (UNVERIFIED, search-summary wording).
- **State "cultural trains" [K13, K8]:** touring companies were sent during harvest: a Lithuanian State Philharmonic team to North Kazakhstan oblast; Estonian Philharmonic teams and the Baltic Fleet sailors' club ensemble toured the new-land steppe; audiences demanded encores of Estonian songs and folk dances (archival reports cited in Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED). A Lithuanian amateur ensemble is photographed in 1955 (SOURCED).
- **Money with nothing to buy [K8]:** permanent workers earned on average 550 to 800 rubles a month against a mid-1950s farm average of 458; seasonal workers, mostly students, were often paid in labor-days (at least 3 kg of grain or 5 rubles per labor-day) and at least 12 rubles a day at farms and grain-receiving points; one Kostroma student earned 1,400 rubles in a harvest and bought a suit, coat and watch, plus 216 kg of grain by voucher at the nearest elevator; another recalled money kept in a suitcase because there was nothing to buy; one Ivanovo graduate was paid a cartload of oat straw for a year's labor-days; after the 1956 harvest the trips lost their appeal because pay was low (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED).

### D. Third places
- The farm canteen and the kolkhoz club were the shared rooms, and archival records show both as the scenes of fights (below). The bathhouse was among the first buildings (SOURCED, above).
- **Houses of prayer** were private homes (SOURCED, above).
- **The Shrine of Our Lady Queen of Peace, Ozernoye (North Kazakhstan oblast, between Petropavl and Kokshetau) [K2, K9, P6]:** a village formed in 1936 by Poles deported from Soviet Ukraine (more than 35,000 Poles deported to Kazakhstan on and after 28 April 1936; a second wave of over 60,000 in 1940 to 1941); a collective farm grew wheat in Soviet times; its closure brought unemployment and departures; fewer than 200 residents today; the school has closed; Polish is kept at home; a three-story pilgrim hotel is often full; Polish pilgrims recently restored the overgrown cemetery (RFE/RL Kazakh Service and P. Trotsenko, 28 Jul 2024, SOURCED). Village tradition holds that on 25 March 1941, in hunger, a lake formed from meltwater and filled with fish. Permission to build a church came 19 March 1990, building began May 1990, the church was blessed 9 August 1992 by Cardinal Józef Glemp, perpetual adoration began on the anniversary 25 March 1996, and the bishop consecrated the country to Our Lady there on 25 June 1996 (Vatican News, D. Castellano Lubov, 21 Sep 2022, SOURCED). Declared the national shrine of Our Lady Queen of Peace, patroness of Kazakhstan, in 2011; the "Star of Kazakhstan" altar installed 2013 (RFE/RL, SOURCED). A statue of the Madonna with a fishing net was set up at the lake in 1997 (RFE/RL, SOURCED). An annual youth gathering in August (UNVERIFIED, search summary; the date was not confirmed on a read page).

### E. Cultural activities, civic life, and the K6 question
- **K6, rules of everyday behavior: YES, there were rules, mostly enforced through the Komsomol and the collective. [K6, K1, P7]** What they were: labor discipline (not leaving posts), no drinking binges, no "hooliganism," attendance at Komsomol meetings, and production of brigade "combat leaflets" and flash bulletins naming the best workers and shaming violators; failures were treated as gross violations and the Komsomol organizers and district Komsomol secretaries were often punished for them (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing Kostroma party archive, SOURCED). How they worked: by "urgent request of the workers," violators faced a graded set of penalties up to expulsion from the party and Komsomol; under Khrushchev public (comrades') courts were given a role in "re-educating" those who committed antisocial acts; a general meeting of a work unit could send members home (Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED). Who enforced: brigade leaders, Komsomol organizers, general meetings, district Komsomol committees, the police and the security organs. Where it came from: the campaign's own breakdowns (below).
- **Where the rules came from: drinking, fights, desertion [K9, K6]:** in 1960 a Kostroma detachment harvesting in Aktobe oblast recorded negligence, drinking and fights; at the "Aktyubinsky" state farm, a wedding held without telling the farm management ended in mass drinking in which two young men died, and the brigade leader and the person responsible for the Kostroma group took part; at "Khobdinsky," a collective drinking bout ended in the beating of young workers, and one brigade left grain-loading two hours early; three workers from Buy drank on their first evening, insulted passersby at the kolkhoz club and fled that night (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED). In Kokchetav oblast courts registered 323 hooliganism cases in 1954, more than half at year's end; fights between special settlers and new-land workers in village canteens involved 10 to 25 people each time; a historian of Soviet mass disturbances counted 20 of 24 known openly ethnic clashes of the 1950s in the new lands, new construction sites and the North Caucasus; police mostly recorded incidents rather than prevented them (Mazhitova et al. 2024, citing archives and V. A. Kozlov 2009, SOURCED; reported neutrally as the article's archival record). A 1957 riot by trainees at Atbasar School No. 59 attacked instructors and police (SOURCED).
- **Food as a grievance [K12]:** 12 to 14-hour days starting at 7 a.m. and sometimes ending at 2 a.m.; meals outdoors or in improvised canteens; often no breakfast; cooking fat added to monotonous meals; a strike was organized because only macaroni was served; the basic foods were bread, grains, mutton, potatoes and vegetables (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED).
- **Baltic home foods and holidays [K12, K2]:** a Lithuanian informant recalled Christmas, the midsummer festival and the July day of King Mindaugas celebrated despite prohibitions, with cepelinai, kugelis, šakotis and a home kvass-like drink; an Estonian informant recalled a roasted-grain flour carried to the fields at harvest and mixed with milk or yogurt (Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED).
- **Commemoration today [K2, K3]:** in Akmola oblast the 60th anniversary was marked on 22 November 2014 at the "Kokshetau" cultural center together with the Day of Agricultural Workers: the oblast akim presided, first settlers and agricultural veterans attended, 154 people were honored, and a concert followed (zakon.kz, 22 Nov 2014, SOURCED). For the 50th anniversary the Kostanay Regional Museum of History and Local Lore opened a traveling photo exhibition, "Tselina: den' za dnem" (Virgin Lands: Day by Day), on 24 July 2004 in Uzunkol district, where the first settlers' train had arrived, touring the districts and including the life of the Indigenous population; a permanent Virgin Lands section was to follow in Kostanay (Caravan, 26 Jul 2004, SOURCED). The Kostanay museum was founded in 1915 (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **K11 (very loose):** Russian in public life; Lithuanian, Estonian, Polish and German kept mainly at home; services in German in Lutheran congregations until 1993 (sources above, SOURCED).
