## Pick 1 · Virgin Lands Campaign, northern Kazakhstan (Tselinograd)

**Round 2 focus:** the settlement stages and off-season shelter, the separate communities (deportees, Baltic specialists, Poles, Germans, Kazakhs), what free time was, the K6 question (rules, drinking, discipline and who enforced it), and how the campaign is marked today. Round 1 threads chased: everyday rules and alcohol; a Virgin Lands museum; deportee communities' own spaces; Kazakh pastoral land loss.

### A. Other settings
- **The settlement in stages [K4, K5, P3, P4]:** on arrival, half a year in tents, "from the end of April to the end of October"; single workers in shared twenty-place tents with double walls and roofs and windows of thick light-yellow cellophane; married couples in separate four-place tents without the wool lining that kept out cold; mattresses and pillows stuffed with dry, sharp feather grass; the state-farm office was itself a tent. Only toward winter did settlers build two barracks of adobe (clay mixed with straw) and several sod-roofed dugouts; in the cold, indoor temperature stayed below zero, water froze inside, and workers melted ice in metal mugs on small iron stoves (A. V. Belyaev memoir, quoted in A. M. Belov and A. A. Rybin, "Povsednevnaya zhizn' na tseline v 1954–1964 gg.," *Vestnik antropologii* 2019 no. 1, pp. 80–87, journals.iea.ras.ru, full text read, SOURCED).
- **A new settlement's first buildings, in order [P5]:** at the "Klyuchevskoy" state farm, Akmola oblast, Muscovites and settlers from Ivanovo began on 12 April 1954 by digging foundation pits for prefabricated houses and marking out streets; the first house was handed over by the end of April; then came a canteen, a bathhouse, a power station, and a school was laid down (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED). Adobe was an unfamiliar material: it cracked settlers' hands and badly laid walls washed away in rain; a Yaroslavl brigade was the first on its farm to master it and became the farm's leading construction brigade (SOURCED).
- **Clinics [K5, K9]:** first-aid posts were not built everywhere; built feldsher posts often stood empty for lack of staff; inexperienced newcomers were injured often: 110,800 injuries registered in the Kazakh SSR's new-land districts in 1954 and 154,300 in 1955; 13 fatal accidents in October 1954 alone; permanent clinics (polyclinics) appeared only by the early 1960s (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing GARF, SOURCED).
- **Nuclear testing nearby [K9]:** some Kazakh new-land farms lay within reach of the Semipalatinsk test site; a settler at the "Mirny" farm recalled seeing test flashes and feeling air waves at least five times; the article reports later cancers among the settlement's residents (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing L. A. Predybailo 2014, SOURCED as the article's account).
- **Schools [K4]:** about 750 schools and about 19 million m² of housing built during the campaign, over half the housing rural (A. Caukenov, zakon.kz, 7 Apr 2016, SOURCED). A former pupil of a school in Shamalgan recalled classmates who were Chechens, Meskhetians, Germans, Balkars, Uighurs, Ukrainians, Koreans and Russians, and school evenings each devoted to the culture of one people of the USSR (informant quoted in Mazhitova et al., "The Baltic Peoples in the Development of Virgin Lands in Kazakhstan," *Journal of Ecohumanism* 3(6), 2024, pp. 1960–1972, full text read, SOURCED).
- **A house that looked different [K4, P3]:** Baltic settlers built houses to their own pattern: Lithuanian houses painted in colors tied to the home region, with hedges instead of fences and gardens and lawns; Estonian houses with front gardens and full yards of livestock; Latvian houses with gable thatched roofs. A Kazakh informant recalled a Lithuanian neighbor explaining an unwritten rule among neighbors over who kept the more beautiful, cleaner house and plot (Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED).

### B. Subcultures
- **Arrival streams, more precisely [P9]:** more than 640,000 people arrived in Kazakhstan 1954 to 1956, 45.3% of the total rural population then living on the new lands (UNVERIFIED, search snippet of a Buketov University repository abstract); 640,000+ is also stated by zakon.kz 2016 (SOURCED). In 1954: 25,000 Komsomol volunteers in the first three weeks, over 75,000 by June, and 11,000 demobilized soldiers from September to December; agricultural workers in the new-land districts rose more than fivefold 1954 to 1959; oblast populations rose 41% (Akmola), 37% (Kokchetav), 56% (Kustanai), 44% (Pavlodar) (A. Mikhailov, informburo.kz, 17 Feb 2018, SOURCED). From the Ivanovo, Kostroma and Yaroslavl oblasts alone 23,161 people left for the new lands over the period, mostly aged 18 to 40 (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED).
- **A men's frontier, then a recruitment of women [K4, K9]:** the arrivals were overwhelmingly male from the start; *Pravda* printed an appeal from young women of the "Marinovsky" state farm calling on their peers to come; responses reported included 500 young women to North Kazakhstan oblast and 300 from Vinnytsia, Kirovohrad and other Ukrainian oblasts, plus women from the Baltic republics (informburo.kz 2018, SOURCED).
- **People already there, by compulsion [K9, P9]:** in 1954 Kokchetav oblast held 55,814 adult special settlers: 30,368 Germans, 8,981 Chechens and Ingush, 716 Tajiks, 15,749 Poles. Poles deported from Soviet Ukraine in 1936 lived compactly in Shortandy district (Akmola) and in the Krasnoarmeysky, Kellerovsky and Chkalovsky (now Tayynsha) districts; Tayynsha station was the transit and reception point; 27 labor settlements were set up in Krasnoarmeysky district. Many of these kolkhozes and labor settlements were turned into new-land state farms; in 1954, 15 Poles chaired kolkhozes and 35 were brigade leaders in Kokchetav oblast, and several deported Poles were later made Heroes of Socialist Labor as tractor and combine operators. Before the war special settlers could move only within their district; during the war they were restricted and attached to special commandant's offices; teachers came from German and Polish teacher-training colleges in Ukraine (A. S. Musagalieva and R. M. Musabekova, "Polyaki v osvoenii tselinnykh i zalezhnykh zemel' v Severnom Kazakhstane," *Vestnik Karagandinskogo universiteta*, History series, 2025, full text read, SOURCED).
- **Baltic specialists [K4, K9]:** Latvian and Lithuanian "construction and installation trains" (mobile construction units) were sent from 1961; from one Lithuanian train, 32 workers left without permission and 20 were sent home by decision of the train's general meeting for drunkenness and absenteeism, and two were prosecuted for hooliganism (State Archive of Astana, cited in Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED). Baltic informants describe speaking Lithuanian or Estonian at home and Russian in public, holding holidays quietly, living scattered, frequent mixed marriages (Estonians with Germans, Lithuanians with Ukrainians), and "keeping a low profile" (SOURCED). Today: an Estonian Cultural Center of Kazakhstan (Almaty), a Lithuanian Community of Karaganda, and a Belarusian-Baltic Association in Kostanai (SOURCED).
- **German religious communities [K4, K6, K2]:** in North Kazakhstan oblast in the late 1950s about 7,000 Germans lived in all 11 districts; the state's commissioner for religious affairs estimated 60 to 65% of adults were believers, most of them not openly. Lutherans (65 to 70% of believers) and Catholics were the main groups, with smaller Baptist, Mennonite, Adventist and Pentecostal groups, often unregistered. The largest unregistered Lutheran group, at Peterfeld (Petropavlovsk district), had 28 weekly attenders and a list of 90 confirmed Lutherans (22 men, 68 women) who asked to bring Pastor E. Bachmann from Akmolinsk for Easter; the commissioner recommended the Lutheran center be registered in Karaganda oblast instead, to keep it out of the oblast. A Catholic group at Asanovo, with 35 to 40 attenders, applied three times to register. Funeral and wedding rites, baptism, a harvest-thanks festival, Christmas and Easter were kept in almost all families; when a religious holiday fell on a working day, mass absences from work brought conflict with party bodies; younger people were drifting away (Zh. S. Mazhitova et al., "Everyday Religious Life of German Population in North Kazakhstan Region in the late 1950s," *Bulletin of L. N. Gumilyov ENU*, History series, 2023, full text read, SOURCED). Lutheran congregations met in private houses, gradually rebuilt as houses of prayer; services were in German until 1993, then Russian; heavy emigration in the 1990s; a church building in the capital completed in 2017 (Lutheran World Federation, 5 Jul 2019, SOURCED).
- **Kazakhs [K9, P9]:** a settler recalled conflicts with local Kazakh youth and elders because plowing took the pastures of their horses and camels; in one farm after the harvest all tractors were disabled with sledgehammer blows to the engine blocks; a KGB officer briefed visiting Kostroma students that metal rods had been found driven into fields to wreck combines, and told them to walk fields before harvesting (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing memoirs and the authors' own interviews, SOURCED as the article's account). Plowing reduced pastures and so cut livestock production (zakon.kz 2016, SOURCED); "the centuries-old nomadic economy was broken, and pastures suitable for animal husbandry irretrievably disappeared" (UNVERIFIED, search-summary wording). Specific aul mergers or school closures caused by the campaign: NOT FOUND.

### C. Leisure (how free time was organized)
- Free time on the farms went to the cinema, the library and the club, amateur art circles, and sports competitions between state farms (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing Yaroslavl archives, SOURCED). Club and house-of-culture openings were treated as farm events; houses of culture hosted dances, games and festivities as well as ideological work (UNVERIFIED, search-summary wording).
- **State "cultural trains" [K13, K8]:** touring companies were sent during harvest: a Lithuanian State Philharmonic team to North Kazakhstan oblast; Estonian Philharmonic teams and the Baltic Fleet sailors' club ensemble toured the new-land steppe; audiences demanded encores of Estonian songs and folk dances (archival reports cited in Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED). A Lithuanian amateur ensemble is photographed in 1955 (SOURCED).
- **Money with nothing to buy [K8]:** permanent workers earned on average 550 to 800 rubles a month against a mid-1950s farm average of 458; seasonal workers, mostly students, were often paid in labor-days (at least 3 kg of grain or 5 rubles per labor-day) and at least 12 rubles a day at farms and grain-receiving points; one Kostroma student earned 1,400 rubles in a harvest and bought a suit, coat and watch, plus 216 kg of grain by voucher at the nearest elevator; another recalled money kept in a suitcase because there was nothing to buy; one Ivanovo graduate was paid a cartload of oat straw for a year's labor-days; after the 1956 harvest the trips lost their appeal because pay was low (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED).

### D. Third places
- The farm canteen and the kolkhoz club were the shared rooms, and archival records show both as the scenes of fights (below). The bathhouse was among the first buildings (SOURCED, above).
- **Houses of prayer** were private homes (SOURCED, above).
- **The Shrine of Our Lady Queen of Peace, Ozernoye (North Kazakhstan oblast, between Petropavl and Kokshetau) [K2, K9, P6]:** a village formed in 1936 by Poles deported from Soviet Ukraine (more than 35,000 Poles deported to Kazakhstan on and after 28 April 1936; a second wave of over 60,000 in 1940 to 1941); a collective farm grew wheat in Soviet times; its closure brought unemployment and departures; fewer than 200 residents today; the school has closed; Polish is kept at home; a three-story pilgrim hotel is often full; Polish pilgrims recently restored the overgrown cemetery (RFE/RL Kazakh Service and P. Trotsenko, 28 Jul 2024, SOURCED). Village tradition holds that on 25 March 1941, in hunger, a lake formed from meltwater and filled with fish. Permission to build a church came 19 March 1990, building began May 1990, the church was blessed 9 August 1992 by Cardinal Józef Glemp, perpetual adoration began on the anniversary 25 March 1996, and the bishop consecrated the country to Our Lady there on 25 June 1996 (Vatican News, D. Castellano Lubov, 21 Sep 2022, SOURCED). Declared the national shrine of Our Lady Queen of Peace, patroness of Kazakhstan, in 2011; the "Star of Kazakhstan" altar installed 2013 (RFE/RL, SOURCED). A statue of the Madonna with a fishing net was set up at the lake in 1997 (RFE/RL, SOURCED). An annual youth gathering in August (UNVERIFIED, search summary; the date was not confirmed on a read page).

### E. Cultural activities, civic life, and the K6 question
- **K6, rules of everyday behavior: YES, there were rules, mostly enforced through the Komsomol and the collective. [K6, K1, P7]** What they were: labor discipline (not leaving posts), no drinking binges, no "hooliganism," attendance at Komsomol meetings, and production of brigade "combat leaflets" and flash bulletins naming the best workers and shaming violators; failures were treated as gross violations and the Komsomol organizers and district Komsomol secretaries were often punished for them (Belov and Rybin 2019, citing Kostroma party archive, SOURCED). How they worked: by "urgent request of the workers," violators faced a graded set of penalties up to expulsion from the party and Komsomol; under Khrushchev public (comrades') courts were given a role in "re-educating" those who committed antisocial acts; a general meeting of a work unit could send members home (Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED). Who enforced: brigade leaders, Komsomol organizers, general meetings, district Komsomol committees, the police and the security organs. Where it came from: the campaign's own breakdowns (below).
- **Where the rules came from: drinking, fights, desertion [K9, K6]:** in 1960 a Kostroma detachment harvesting in Aktobe oblast recorded negligence, drinking and fights; at the "Aktyubinsky" state farm, a wedding held without telling the farm management ended in mass drinking in which two young men died, and the brigade leader and the person responsible for the Kostroma group took part; at "Khobdinsky," a collective drinking bout ended in the beating of young workers, and one brigade left grain-loading two hours early; three workers from Buy drank on their first evening, insulted passersby at the kolkhoz club and fled that night (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED). In Kokchetav oblast courts registered 323 hooliganism cases in 1954, more than half at year's end; fights between special settlers and new-land workers in village canteens involved 10 to 25 people each time; a historian of Soviet mass disturbances counted 20 of 24 known openly ethnic clashes of the 1950s in the new lands, new construction sites and the North Caucasus; police mostly recorded incidents rather than prevented them (Mazhitova et al. 2024, citing archives and V. A. Kozlov 2009, SOURCED; reported neutrally as the article's archival record). A 1957 riot by trainees at Atbasar School No. 59 attacked instructors and police (SOURCED).
- **Food as a grievance [K12]:** 12 to 14-hour days starting at 7 a.m. and sometimes ending at 2 a.m.; meals outdoors or in improvised canteens; often no breakfast; cooking fat added to monotonous meals; a strike was organized because only macaroni was served; the basic foods were bread, grains, mutton, potatoes and vegetables (Belov and Rybin 2019, SOURCED).
- **Baltic home foods and holidays [K12, K2]:** a Lithuanian informant recalled Christmas, the midsummer festival and the July day of King Mindaugas celebrated despite prohibitions, with cepelinai, kugelis, šakotis and a home kvass-like drink; an Estonian informant recalled a roasted-grain flour carried to the fields at harvest and mixed with milk or yogurt (Mazhitova et al. 2024, SOURCED).
- **Commemoration today [K2, K3]:** in Akmola oblast the 60th anniversary was marked on 22 November 2014 at the "Kokshetau" cultural center together with the Day of Agricultural Workers: the oblast akim presided, first settlers and agricultural veterans attended, 154 people were honored, and a concert followed (zakon.kz, 22 Nov 2014, SOURCED). For the 50th anniversary the Kostanay Regional Museum of History and Local Lore opened a traveling photo exhibition, "Tselina: den' za dnem" (Virgin Lands: Day by Day), on 24 July 2004 in Uzunkol district, where the first settlers' train had arrived, touring the districts and including the life of the Indigenous population; a permanent Virgin Lands section was to follow in Kostanay (Caravan, 26 Jul 2004, SOURCED). The Kostanay museum was founded in 1915 (UNVERIFIED snippet).
- **K11 (very loose):** Russian in public life; Lithuanian, Estonian, Polish and German kept mainly at home; services in German in Lutheran congregations until 1993 (sources above, SOURCED).

## Pick 2 · Beidahuang state farms, Heilongjiang

**Round 2 focus:** company life in the Corps years (canteen, bunks, winter), the seasonal labor that now moves through the farms, the Hezhe fishing people and their gathering, the harvest festival and the memorial culture of the sent-down generation. Round 1 threads chased: year family farms began; season length and winter work; food culture; sent-down youth reunions and memorial culture; Hezhe fishing after reclamation.

### A. Other settings
- **A Corps company in the early 1970s [K4, P4]:** a memoir from the 3rd Division, 22nd Regiment of the Heilongjiang Production and Construction Corps (the Corps had six divisions): before housing was built, men and women slept in one large tent divided by a canvas sheet; afterward, two rows of heated brick platform-beds with six youths to each platform; winter below minus 40°C; building work (making mud bricks, hauling sand, felling timber) had to be finished in summer because snow came by the Mid-Autumn Festival; wheat harvest often ran 48 hours without a break; women carried 200-jin sacks; youths fell to their deaths while digging wells; the padded army clothing and quilt issued on arrival were deducted from wages and issued only once; platoon leaders and above were all veteran workers (Yezi, memoir, 18 Jun 2012, via aisixiang/Kdnet, reproduced at chinadigitaltimes.net/chinese/233851.html, SOURCED as the memoir's account).
- **The canteen as a ritual space [K6, K12]:** before entering the dining hall, workers shouted wishes of long life for Mao and health for Lin Biao three times; they ate standing, six to a table (three men, three women), sharing one plate of cabbage and ladling a thin shredded-radish soup from a large bucket into their own bowls; the memoir gives the soup as 2 radishes and half a jin of oil in three big buckets of water for over a hundred youths (Yezi 2012, SOURCED as the memoir's account).
- **Winter at minus 40 [K5, K7]:** on New Year's Day 1978 at the 16th Company, 68th Regiment, 6th Division, the canteen ran out of flour and firewood; every person was ordered to carry two loads of firewood, one for the dormitory and one for the canteen; flatbread was rationed at three per person; cadres borrowed from neighboring units and went about 70 km by horse-drawn sledge to division headquarters for supplies (Yang Liwei, "1978 nian wo zai Beidahuang ruhe guo yuandan," ifeng.com history, 23 Dec 2008, SOURCED as the memoir's account).
- **Today's farm year [K7, K5]:** in the Sanjiang Plain the best rice-transplanting window is 5 to 25 May; local hands are too few, so seasonal workers come every spring; harvest begins late September (Round 1). Snow by mid-autumn (memoir above).

### B. Subcultures
- **Seasonal transplanting workers [K4, K7, K5, P9]:** since 2000, China Railway Harbin Group has run chartered trains in transplanting season from Qiqihar and Suihua to the Sanjiang Plain farms; over 1.9 million workers carried in 25 years; a 2025 train from Qiqihar to Fuyuan (about 850 km) carried more than 600 workers and stopped at Jiansanjiang, Qianjin, Qianfeng and Honghe farms; workers come from rural Suihua and nearby and stay about three weeks; a 52-year-old transplanter operator, coming every spring since 2007, was promised 600 yuan a day; a husband-and-wife team can make about 30,000 yuan in a month (Zhou Huiying, China Daily, 28 May 2025, SOURCED). In 2022 a 32-year-old from Jianmin village, Qing'an county, coming every spring since 2015, expected over 20,000 yuan for the season; Jiangchuan Co. (Hongxinglong branch, 20,000 ha) employed 2,500 workers, down from 4,000 before 2018, because of 99% mechanization (China Daily, 26 Apr 2022, SOURCED).
- **Family farmers on state land [K8]:** a farmer at Qianjin with a 14-ha operation reported over 400,000 yuan in rice sales in a year (China Daily 2025, SOURCED).
- **Sent-down youth as a lifelong group [K9, K13]:** a writer and former Beidahuang youth divides the culture of the sent-down generation into three phases: exposure of suffering (late 1970s, with the return to the cities and "zhiqing literature"); "youth without regret" nostalgia (early 1990s, with works such as a Beidahuang memoir anthology); and a third, "warm, rational and constructive" phase oriented to education and public benefit. He led a volunteer group back to its old Beidahuang settlement, set up a library of 1,200 volumes and a computer station, and brought younger volunteers to teach (Yang Dongping, "Xin zhiqing wenhua," aisixiang.com, 6 Aug 2006, SOURCED). On 1 August 2006 hundreds of former sent-down youths from Shanghai, Beijing, Harbin and Ningbo gathered at Heihe for the foundation-laying of the Heilongjiang Sent-Down Youth Memorial Hall (SOURCED, same). The museum opened in 2009 and was funded by donations from former youths at home and abroad totaling over 30 million yuan (UNVERIFIED, search summary; the 90,000 m² size in the same summary is doubtful and not used).
- **The Hezhe [K9, K2, K14, P9]:** in the 20th century the Hezhe were organized into industrialized fishing brigades; very few speakers of the Hezhe/Nanai language remain (Cambridge MIASU "Naikhin and Tongjiang" project page, undated, SOURCED). Seasonal fishing bans: Heilong (Amur) and Ussuri rivers 11 June to 15 July and 1 to 20 October; Xingkai Lake 6 June to 20 July; a retired fishery expert says the Heilong River has had bans since the 1950s; border police collect boats and gear at the start of each ban and return them afterward; a 52-year-old fisherman said his family earned about 20,000 yuan a year from fishing, about double farm income (China.org via Eco-Business, 14 May 2013, SOURCED). Fishing was abandoned as the main source of Hezhe income decades ago; villagers now farm and run tourism (UNVERIFIED, search summary of China Daily). A direct documented link between state-farm reclamation and Hezhe fishing decline: NOT FOUND (the wetland-loss data in Round 1 are the only bridge, and no source joins them).

### E. Cultural activities, civic life, and the K6 question
- **K6, rules of everyday behavior: YES in the Corps years.** Documented: the collective slogan ritual before meals; standing meals at assigned mixed tables; military organization by division, regiment and company with veteran workers as platoon leaders and above; deductions from pay for issued kit (Yezi memoir, SOURCED as account); 1968 entry screening by family background (Round 1). Who enforced: company cadres (political instructors and vice instructors, as in the Yang Liwei memoir). Rules on romance, leave and home visits: NOT FOUND on a read page (searched in Chinese). - **Wurigong, the Hezhe gathering [K2, K3]:** the name means "joyful gathering" in Hezhe; traditionally on the 15th day of the fifth lunar month, Hezhe gathered on the Heilong River bank for sacrifices praying for good hunting and fishing, with songs and contests; now held every four years for two to three days, rotating among Hezhe areas; the 12th was held in June 2025 near Jiamusi, with birch-bark drums, wrestling, yimakan epic storytelling, fish-skin and birch-bark crafts, and dishes such as raw-fish salad and fish stew (Xinhua, Dai Jinrong and Xu Kaixin, 11 Jun 2025, SOURCED). ## Pick 3 · FELDA land settlement schemes, Malaysia

**Round 2 focus:** the scheme as a lived village (house, shop, eating shop, community center, mosque), the women's, youth and second-generation organizations, the K6 rules written into land law and into the block system, and Settlers' Day. Round 1 threads chased: women's and youth organizations; the community-hall role; a FELDA heritage gallery; text of the 1960 Act; everyday rules; food culture. Care note honored: structure only.

### A. Other settings
- **House and plot as a growth plan [K4, P3, P5]:** FELDA built its schemes in the style of the Malay village, with wooden houses on stilts surrounded by fruit trees; houses were deliberately given as one room, with FELDA-supplied plans for extending them as income rose, because the 4-ha plot was thought not to fill a family's year; settlers were encouraged to plant flowers around houses and vegetables on the house plot; in the Jengka Triangle 83% of Stage I settlers, 78% of Stage II and 42% of Stage III had enlarged or renovated, so "the age of the schemes can be determined immediately by looking at the size and development of the houses" (World Bank Operations Evaluation Department, *The Jengka Triangle Projects in Malaysia: Impact Evaluation Report*, 1987, ch. III, full text read, SOURCED). A community-funded replica of an early settler house at Felda Kerteh 1, Terengganu, is 3.5 m by 1.8 m with one bedroom, a small living area, a kitchen and an outside bathroom, built for nearly RM15,000 through communal work (The Star, 4 Sep 2025, SOURCED).
- **Services and their failures [K5, P4]:** settlers ranked the mosque first, then the primary school and kindergarten; kindergartens were not in the original plan and were added at settler demand, run by the scheme committee in the community centers; the FELDA shop sold on credit and its profits paid for welfare such as kindergartens; least satisfactory were farm roads (over two-thirds dissatisfied), water supply (45%) and environment (42%); taps ran dry for weeks in the dry season and FELDA trucked drinking water and distributed rations by household; in the absence of grid power, better-off settlers pooled with two or three neighbors to buy a generator, and others charged 12-volt car batteries weekly at shops (World Bank 1987, SOURCED).
- **A planned town that did not arrive [K9, P5]:** the 1967 master plan proposed three towns, the central one for about 50,000 people by 1975; by 1983 it was still at an initial stage and the other two existed only on paper, while unplanned commercial growth occurred at a roadside town on the Triangle's edge, about 90% of whose shops were owned by Chinese traders; a federal development corporation took over in 1983, about 1,000 houses were built and the central town reached about 8,000 people, mostly civil servants and timber-factory employees (World Bank 1987, SOURCED).
- **Heritage sites [K9, K13]:** the former FELDA office at Lurah Bilut is part of a Felda Lurah Bilut museum (UNVERIFIED, search summary); a dedicated national FELDA heritage gallery: NOT FOUND.

### B. Subcultures
- **Who the settlers were [P9]:** in Jengka, of 229 settlers surveyed, 72% were landless on entry; former rubber tappers 31%, rice farmers 25%, oil palm estate workers 6%; 75% came from states other than Pahang (unusually; elsewhere over 90% came from the scheme's own state); 99% Malay; for 90% this was their first move out of their birth state; only 2% had left or been expelled in 17 years; only a quarter went home four or more times in 1984 for visits and festivals; average household 6.7 (oil palm) and 5.9 (rubber); 96.5% of heads of household male, female heads mostly widows (World Bank 1987, SOURCED). Selection from 1961: citizens aged 21 to 50, married, landless or with under 1 ha; points for health, a farming, fishing or ex-service background, and more children; the age ceiling was later lowered so a 20-year loan could be repaid in working life, then raised again in 1984 when demand fell after the switch from land titles to shares (SOURCED).
- **Women [K4, K6, K9, K14]:** 75% of rubber-settler wives tapped, 5 to 6 hours a day, and 56% of oil palm wives harvested; extension advice went systematically to men only; latex payments went to husbands unless a wife had written permission to collect; social activities were divided by sex, with male officers handling the scheme committee and mosque committee and female officers the women's committees ("There is no mixing of social activities between men and women"); a later note says the Women's Institute (Gerakan Persatuan Wanita, GPW) gained seats on the scheme committee and 4 women sat on the settlers' National Consultative Council. Over half of settler wives owned a sewing machine, a major ambition, and sewed Muslim-style dresses for the local market. Entry required marriage but the loan title was in the husband's name only; on divorce or the husband taking another wife, the first wife generally had to leave with no land right; FELDA then began paying a divorced wife 50% of loan repayments already made, in monthly installments (World Bank 1987, SOURCED).
- **Youth and the second generation [K9]:** youth clubs met in the community centers (World Bank 1987, SOURCED). Once schooled, almost all children left; 40% of settlers wanted their children to stay, 51% wanted them in government service, 2% in farming (SOURCED). The Majlis Belia FELDA Malaysia (youth council) and Kami Anak FELDA are listed as FELDA community programs alongside GPW and the committees (UNVERIFIED, search summary). A second-generation association, Persatuan Anak Peneroka Felda Kebangsaan (Anak), led by Mazlan Aliman, campaigned against FELDA management; a 65-year-old first-generation settler from Felda Chini, Pekan, left GPW to join it (The Malaysian Insight, D. Ibrahim, 16 Dec 2017, SOURCED).

### C. Leisure industries
- **Breakfast at the eating shop [K10, K12, K7]:** rubber tapping starts at dawn and breakfast "will invariably be in the eating shops," privately owned and highly rated by settlers, selling hot and cold drinks, snacks and daily newspapers (World Bank 1987, SOURCED).
- **Football [K3, K8]:** FELDA founded a professional club on 19 January 2007, starting in the third tier; Malaysia Premier League champions 2010 and 2018, Super League runners-up 2016, FA Cup runners-up 2014; home ground the Tun Abdul Razak Stadium, Jengka, from 2016; withdrew from the league after 2020 for lack of financial support (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FELDA_United_F.C., SOURCED). In June 2014 the FELDA chairman changed the team's nickname because settlers now owned their land and the old name no longer fitted (The Star, E. Samuel, 4 Jun 2014, SOURCED; the nicknames themselves are not recorded here).
- **Settlers' Day as a mass event [K2, K3, K8]:** 2024: 12 to 14 July at MAEPS, Serdang, the first multi-day edition, with an entrepreneurs' exhibition and a motorcycle gathering aiming at a national record of 50,000 riders (The Sun, 2024, SOURCED). 2026, FELDA's 70th year: a four-day program, 4 to 7 July, with over 40 activities in technology, sport, education, innovation, culture and religion, ending on 7 July at the Tun Abdul Razak Stadium, Jengka, with awards for outstanding settler, settler woman, FELDA generation and youth (Bernama via Malaysiakini, 7 Jul 2026, SOURCED). 7 July was set by Tun Abdul Razak, FELDA's founder (The Sun 2024, SOURCED, which states it).

### E. Cultural activities, civic life, and the K6 question
- **K6, rules of everyday behavior: YES, and many are written into law or the work system. [K6, K1, P7]**
  - *Land law (Land (Group Settlement Areas) Act 1960, Act 530, reprint, jkptg.gov.my, full text read, SOURCED):* a rural holding may be held by one person or by not more than two; no joint ownership beyond that; land "may not at any time be subdivided or partitioned"; implied conditions in every title: the whole holding (except the house site) must be brought under the appropriate crop and kept "according to the rules of good husbandry"; no other crops; no building except the holder's own dwelling, placed where the Collector directs; occupation is limited to citizens who hold no other land beyond 2 acres of country land and a house plot; default in the annual charge is itself a breach; on breach, an authorized officer may re-enter the land; a right of way across neighbors' holdings extends only to bicycle, tricycle or handcart.
  - *Block system (World Bank 1987, SOURCED):* blocks of about 20 settlers; FELDA sets the program, the block leader drafts a monthly work plan discussed with members (endorsed by consensus in 76% of cases); if a member's work is unsatisfactory the block leader can order it redone, then impose a penalty and hire labor whose cost is deducted from that settler's income; FELDA staff inspect monthly; in some blocks proceeds are shared equally because soils vary.
  - *Social rules (World Bank 1987, SOURCED):* separation of men's and women's social activities; women's latex payments to husbands without written permission; marriage required for entry.
  - Who enforced: FELDA scheme managers and staff, block leaders, the scheme committee, the state land Collector. Where it came from: FELDA's view that conditionality made settlers "accept discipline, which is essential in the early settlement period" (World Bank 1987, SOURCED).
- **Committees [K1, P7]:** the scheme committee's real duties in Jengka were limited to calling tenders for some farm tasks, nurseries, road repairs, voicing settler views and organizing social activities; for major decisions over 50% of settlers preferred FELDA to decide, 12% the settlers (World Bank 1987, SOURCED).
- **Communal work [K14]:** the Kerteh 1 replica house was built through communal work (The Star 2025, SOURCED).

- **K11 (very loose):** Malay on almost all schemes (99% Malay settlers at Jengka, SOURCED); mixed early schemes as in Round 1.

## Pick 4 · Snowy Mountains Scheme and Cooma, Australia

**Round 2 focus:** the reception camp before the mountains, women and families, the recreation hut, the ski clubs that grew out of camps, the Ngarigo and Wolgalu peoples, today's one-season towns, and the anniversary reunions. Round 1 threads chased: alcohol and canteen rules in camps; women and families; Bonegilla pages; Jindabyne and Khancoban today.

### A. Other settings
- **The reception camp [K4, K6, K9, P5]:** Bonegilla, near Wodonga, Victoria, opened 1947 on an ex-army site and ran to 1971; about 309,000 migrants received (other counts 320,000); 130 ha, 24 blocks with their own kitchens, mess huts and bathrooms, over 800 buildings of timber and corrugated iron, rooms first holding 20 people; men and women were separated within blocks even when married, with luggage and blankets used as partitions; dance classes, a library, soccer, swimming in Lake Hume, films twice a week, a dance hall in Block 19. Displaced persons from camps in France, Austria and Germany came on two-year labor contracts; employment officers classed men as "laborers" and women as "domestics" and allocated jobs nationwide; refusing an offer could cost benefits. In 1949 thirteen children died of malnutrition and an inquiry criticized hospital staffing. In July 1952 Italian migrants without job allocations rioted and burned two or three huts; in 1961 Italian and German migrants protested long stays without work. Block 19 protected 1990; Victorian Heritage Register 2002; National Heritage List 2007; reopened as the Bonegilla Migrant Experience heritage park in 2010 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonegilla_Migrant_Reception_and_Training_Centre, SOURCED). The camp had its own churches, banks, sporting fields, cinema, hospital, police station and railway platform (UNVERIFIED, search summary).
- **The recreation hut [K10, K12]:** each of about a hundred camps had a recreation hut where men sang, drank beer, and played cards, darts and draughts; huts often had no floor coverings, little heating and no running hot water (UNVERIFIED, extended-search summary attributed to the National Museum of Australia's "Defining Moments" page, which returned 403).
- **Winter township medicine [K5]:** the doctor at Cabramurra treated everyone from mothers giving birth to men injured in tunneling accidents while the mountain roads were closed in winter; a bush nurse was based at Jindabyne (Snowy Hydro, "Our proud history: Women of the Snowy Scheme," fact sheet 2, 2025 PDF, read, SOURCED).
- **Today's one-season town [K4, K7, K9, P4]:** in the Snowy Mountains Special Activation Precinct study area (Jindabyne and surrounds), about 4,500 dwellings in 2016, of which 2,984 (68.8%) were occupied, the rest empty; tourist accommodation is "the dominant influence on the local housing market"; peak-season prices "present the biggest problem for seasonal workers"; residents are being priced out of Jindabyne; 2019 to 2020 medians: house $750,000, unit $377,500; weekly rent $660 a house and $425 a flat; population forecast 7,688 by 2026 and 10,668 by 2061; peak overnight visitors measured in August (Ethos Urban for NSW Department of Planning, *Summary of the Housing and Accommodation report*, Snowy Mountains SAP, 2021, read, SOURCED). Winter population nearly ten times summer's; a shortfall of 400 beds for seasonal workers; some seasonal workers camping on the lake foreshore (UNVERIFIED, search summaries of ABC and Snowsbest).

### B. Subcultures
- **Women [K4, K14]:** men outnumbered women ten to one at the height of the scheme, and by more in the regions (UNVERIFIED, search summary of Snowy Hydro 2021 fact sheet). Women worked as secretaries, telegraphists, caterers and cleaners; a woman was the first architect employed (1950); women led the drafting unit in the early days; one female engineer did hydrology, flood, sediment and evaporation studies, snow-melt forecasting and programming; scientists analyzed cement and counted dust (Snowy Hydro 2025 fact sheet, SOURCED). The Cabramurra accommodation officer is remembered in company lore for dressing formally for every occasion, after which engineers came to social functions in dinner jackets and gum boots and the township's social events appeared in magazines (same, SOURCED as company lore).
- **Learning English [K14, K4]:** English was the official working language; the Authority employed 38 English teachers who ran regular evening classes for workers (Snowy Hydro 2025 fact sheet, SOURCED).
- **Norwegians at Guthega [K4, K14, K13]:** the Norwegian contractor Selmer flew in 350 Norwegian workers between October 1951 and February 1952 and housed them in prefabricated huts imported from Norway; the dam was built December 1951 to April 1955; the workers formed the Selmer Norwegian Ski Club, built a jumping hill allowing jumps of about 50 m, and ran a rope tow (Australian Alpine Club heritage page, "Growth of skiing in the Snowy Mountains during the 1950s," read, SOURCED).
- **Traditional owners [K9, P9]:** archaeological salvage for Snowy 2.0 recovered over 38,500 stone tools and fragments over about 1,000 m² at Lobs Hole, Tantangara and Marica, plus a rare rock shelter at Tantangara; evidence of occupation of the mountains for more than 9,000 years and of the wider region for 20,000 years or more; the Wolgalu traditionally occupied the high country and the Ngarigo the surrounding tablelands, with distinct territories but shared cultural and linguistic ties, trade and seasonal movement; camps were placed on gentle slopes and flats near rivers; Snowy Hydro consulted First Nations people, many of whom joined the fieldwork, with findings presented in Tumut and Cooma (E. Mason, About Regional, 28 Sep 2025, SOURCED). Summer gatherings in the high country for the bogong moth season drew perhaps over 1,000 people in the 1840s and 1850s; the last moth hunt was recorded in 1865 (UNVERIFIED, search summary of kiandrahistory.net). ## Pick 6 · Imperial Valley, California

### A. Other settings

### B. Subcultures
- **The Japanese farming community, 1913 to 1942 [K9, K4, K12, P9]:** in 1941 Brawley's Japanese community had a Buddhist church and a Methodist church, two Japanese language schools (Buddhist and Methodist), about 14 restaurants, 2 pool halls, a dentist, a midwife, three hotels, a tofu shop, produce companies and stores (49 listed entries), and organizations including the Japanese Association of the Imperial Valley, the JACL and Buddhist and Methodist women's associations; "very little remains" (California Japantowns survey, Brawley page, read, SOURCED). - **Torres Martinez Desert Cahuilla and the Salton Sea [K9, K1]:** (the reservation lies north of the sea in the Coachella Valley, Riverside County, not in the Imperial Valley, and is recorded here because the loss came from the 1905 to 1907 flood and from farm drainage into the sea) the reservation was established 1876 (640 acres) and enlarged by about 12,000 acres in 1891; the 1905 to 1907 flood submerged about 2,000 acres; a 1909 order added 12,000 acres of which about 9,000 were already under water, on the expectation the sea would recede within 25 years, which did not happen; 11,800 acres remained submerged; in 1982 the United States sued the Imperial Irrigation District and the Coachella Valley Water District for trespass by drainage water, and a 25 August 1992 judgment awarded $3,008,602; a 1996 settlement of $14.2 million ($10.2 million federal, about $4 million from the two districts) let the tribe of "more than 500 members" acquire up to 11,800 acres in trust; Congress approved it in 2000 (U.S. Senate Report 106-474, 3 Oct 2000, govinfo.gov, SOURCED).

## FINAL · Round 2 close

**Finished:** 2026-10-29 (PDT). All six picks researched to the Round 2 brief (A to F) with search logs. No search-budget stop occurred.

### Most useful finding per pick (one line each)
1. **Virgin Lands:** a documented K6 regime: Komsomol meetings, combat leaflets, general meetings that sent drinkers home, and graded penalties up to expulsion, all created in response to recorded drinking deaths, canteen brawls and desertions (Belov and Rybin 2019; Mazhitova et al. 2024).
2. **Beidahuang:** the Corps canteen as a ritual space (slogans shouted three times, standing meals at mixed tables of six) and today's three-week chartered-train migration of transplanting workers at 600 yuan a day.
3. **FELDA:** the land law and the block system together are an explicit everyday-rule code (no subdivision, appropriate crops only, re-entry on breach; block leaders can order work redone, then fine and hire labor at the settler's cost), and houses were deliberately given as one room to be extended (World Bank 1987; Act 530).
4. **Snowy Mountains:** construction camps became ski villages through leases of vacated workers' cottages to clubs (1955) and a Norwegian workers' ski club with its own jump; the Bonegilla camp separated married couples and enforced two-year directed labor.
5. **MIA and Griffith:** the irrigation area was under prohibition until 1924 with a government-built "state hotel" left unlicensed; the Italian community built its own church in six months of volunteer labor (1940).
6. **Imperial Valley:** a farmworker pre-dawn civic ritual (the Calexico Farm Worker Appreciation Breakfast, 2:30 a.m., 3,500 workers in 2005) and an economy with 21% unemployment that peaks in summer, now selling fallowed-farm water to the coast.
