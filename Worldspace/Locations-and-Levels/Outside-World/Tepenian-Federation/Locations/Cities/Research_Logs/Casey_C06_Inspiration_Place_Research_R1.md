## 1 · #21 Interstate 80 across southern Wyoming (closure gates and ground blizzards)

**Claim to test:** a highway where wind-lifted snow under a clear sky closes the road, and closure gates and condition reports structure travel (K1, K5).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, and narrowed.** The core stretch is the 77 miles of I-80 between Laramie and Walcott Junction (via Arlington and Elk Mountain), opened October 3, 1970 and closed for the first time four days later. Closures are driven by wind, blowing and drifting snow and visibility, including on clear, sunny days, and a whole public system (gates since 1973, snow fences since 1971, variable speed limits since 2009, a 24-hour management center, a closure vocabulary written into statute, travel-authorization codes for residents) structures travel. Correction to the claim: the corridor's closures are not only ground blizzards; wind-only closures for light, high-profile vehicles are now the most common cause (WYDOT, 2023).

### A. Snapshot
- **What and where:** Interstate 80 crosses southern Wyoming for 403 miles from Evanston to Pine Bluffs (Cowboy State Daily, Leo Wolfson, 2023-05-31; SOURCED). The notorious stretch is the 77 miles (124 km) between Laramie and Walcott Junction, through Arlington and Elk Mountain, in Albany and Carbon counties (WyoHistory, John Waggener, 2025-09-01; FHWA Road Weather case study CS101; both SOURCED).
- **Opening and first closure:** construction began summer 1966; ribbon-cutting at the Arlington exit October 3, 1970 with over 400 attendees including Governor Stan Hathaway; first closure October 7, 1970 (Cowboy State Daily, Waggener, 2020-10-23, SOURCED). At the time it was the longest interstate section opened at once in the U.S. (same, SOURCED, the author's claim).
- **Route choice:** the Bureau of Public Roads planned the direct route through Arlington and Elk Mountain in 1956, 19 miles shorter than U.S. 30 (via Bosler, Rock River, Medicine Bow, Hanna). The Wyoming Highway Commission opposed it, with locals citing blowing and drifting snow near Arlington and wanting to protect the U.S. 30 towns; a Highway Department survey found nearly 90% of drivers between Rawlins and Laramie preferred the direct route and planned no stops in those towns. The dispute reached the U.S. Senate in 1959 (a bill by Senators O'Mahoney and McGee to let states decide routes). Federal officials said modern snow removal equipment could keep the road open; the state gave in; route approved summer 1959 (Waggener, 2020 and 2025, SOURCED). The story that Lady Bird Johnson chose the route is a myth: the route predates her role (WyoHistory 2025, SOURCED).
- **Elevation:** I-80's highest point is Sherman Summit between Cheyenne and Laramie, 8,640 ft (Wikipedia/Interstate Guide via search; UNVERIFIED; other sources give 8,835 ft for the Summit rest area). Laramie is about 7,200 ft (search snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Traffic:** 22,000-24,000 vehicles/day in places, about 8,000 in others; 80-85% out-of-state (WYDOT's Keith Fulton, quoted in Cowboy State Daily 2023-05-31, SOURCED). WYDOT data 2018-2022: semitractor-trailers about 46% of I-80 traffic; 84.9% of eastbound and 91.3% of westbound traffic nonresident (SweetwaterNow summary of WYDOT data, UNVERIFIED snippet). A 2021 book-talk report says a recent study found 60% of traffic is commercial trucks (Cowboy State Daily/Uinta County Herald, 2021-09, SOURCED as reported). Figures differ by segment and measure.
- **Defining feature:** wind-driven snow and wind alone. FHWA: within 3 months of opening, drifts 5 m (16 ft) deep buried the road at 27 places; the road closed for 10 days; gusts reached 161 km/h (100 mph). The road it replaced (U.S. 30) rarely closed from drifting snow because snow fences protected the parallel Union Pacific track (FHWA CS101, SOURCED).

### B. What else exists there (the system built around the road)
- **Closure gates [K1, K6] [P7]:** before gates, closures relied on workers waving stop signs. In 1973 WYDOT installed the first gates at Laramie and Walcott Junction; from 1991 drop-down gates and overhead information signs were installed (Cowboy State Daily 2021-09, Waggener book talk, SOURCED). Waggener states it was the first major U.S. highway to use road closure gates (Cowboy State Daily 2020-10-23, SOURCED as his claim; "believed to be" the first, per Saratoga Sun snippet, UNVERIFIED). Snippet: the first gates were swinging gates that caused drifting; the red-and-white "Wyoming Gate" was first used in 1991 (Saratoga Sun 2018-06-13 snippet, UNVERIFIED). TMC operators can now lower highway arms remotely and confirm with web cameras that the arm is down (Cowboy State Daily, Renée Jean, 2022-12-20, SOURCED). WYDOT says repairing damaged gates cost almost $180,000 over five years and that state statute makes closure signs, including overhead digital signs, sufficient notice before the gate (WYDOT release Dec. 2025, seen only in snippet; page refused with certificate error; UNVERIFIED).
- **Snow fences [K1, K14] [P8]:** WYDOT began building fences along I-80 in 1971: 18.3 km (11.4 mi) at the 27 drift sites, then about 64 km (40 mi) more along I-80; over 900 km (560 mi) now protect Wyoming roads. Results: accident rate down 70% in snowy, windy conditions; snow and ice removal costs down 50%; savings over 10 years paid for the fences; less blowing snow kept pavement warmer (FHWA CS101, SOURCED). Cowboy State Daily (Renée Jean, 2024-02-04, SOURCED) gives "more than 400 miles" of fences; the figures differ by source. The 12-foot "Wyoming fence" was developed in 1971 with the U.S. Forest Service using scale models (Waggener book talk, 2021, SOURCED). Ronald D. Tabler (U.S. Forest Service, later University of Wyoming) did the research behind the fences; his papers are at UW's American Heritage Center (Wyoming Public Media, Archives on the Air, 2023-12-26, SOURCED).
- **WYDOT Winter Science Team (successor to Tabler) [K14] [SM:competence]:** led by civil engineer Cliff Spoonemore with a geologist and a snowplow operator (Duard Dilday III, Kathy Ahlenius named in caption). Studies fence placement, vertical vs horizontal fences, ground gap, solar panels on fences, plow-light colors, drones for stockpile volume, and when to apply salt (sodium chloride works poorly below -6°F, best 16-28°F; magnesium chloride to -28°F, best 0-20°F). 2022-23 season: 234,564 tons of sand-salt mix, 6,044 tons bulk salt, just over 1 million gallons of liquid deicer (Cowboy State Daily 2024-02-04, SOURCED). Spoonemore is quoted that I-80 was closing "40 days out of 60" (same, SOURCED).
- **Transportation Management Center (TMC), Cheyenne [K1, K7] [P7]:** co-located with the Wyoming Highway Patrol; operators at stations with up to 10 monitors; in bad weather each highway district has its own operator, staffed around the clock. Operators keep DMS boards, variable speed limit (VSL) signs, highway advisory radios and the 511 app current; watch radar, 232 web cameras and 155 weather sensors; take a text feed of crashes from the Highway Patrol; talk by radio with districts, troopers and the National Weather Service. Crew and trooper condition reports are entered as numeric codes and one entry updates the map, 511 app and advisory radios together. Infrastructure: 20 advisory radios, 162 DMS boards, 212 VSL signs at 125 locations. DMS signs first appeared in 2003; first ITS project was Cheyenne-Laramie (Cowboy State Daily 2022-12-20, SOURCED).
- **Variable speed limits [K1, K6]:** first activated on the Elk Mountain corridor February 2009, expanded 2009-10; 52 miles between Laramie and Rawlins; limits 35-75 mph in 5 mph steps; a 2022 multi-state study estimated a 34% crash reduction (search snippets of ITS JPO and FHWA pages; ITS JPO page refused 403; UNVERIFIED). Rules from 2009-10 I-80 Coalition slides: legislation effective July 1, 2008 lets WYDOT set limits for "vehicle or weather emergency"; the TMC may lower speed when measured speeds vary more than 10 mph and no one is available to inspect; **only the Highway Patrol or Maintenance can raise a limit** (snippet of Kimley-Horn-hosted slides, UNVERIFIED). Variable limits added 2008 and expanded 2010 (Waggener book talk, SOURCED; dates differ slightly).
- **Who reports and who decides [K14, K1]:** WYDOT's 511 reports come "primarily from WYDOT maintenance crew members" plus public volunteers in the Enhanced Citizen-Assisted Report (ECAR) program (wyoroad.info 511 page, SOURCED). ECAR: started mid-2000s; over 400 citizen contributors by 2014; typically people who regularly drive the same stretch, including truckers; trained by WYDOT; phone in hazards (search snippets of FHWA 2014 report and ITS International; UNVERIFIED). Plow operators report by a tablet Road Condition Reporting app; in a same-storm comparison with radio reporting, road reports doubled and VSL change requests tripled (FHWA Innovator, Jan/Feb 2020, SOURCED). Closure decisions: recommendations come from maintenance crews and troopers to a supervisor who calls the open/close; closures are never scheduled on forecasts but reflect conditions at the time (visibility, severe icing, drifting) (Wyoming News Exchange article "Network of observers...", 2022, seen only in snippets; three copies refused 429/SSL/paywall; UNVERIFIED).
- **Connected Vehicle Pilot:** 402 miles of I-80, 76 roadside units; in-cab tablets gave trucks weather warnings, recommended speeds, closure notices and parking information; one evaluation estimated 46.5 gallons of diesel saved in idling per truck per closure (ITS JPO snippets; case study PDF refused 403; UNVERIFIED).
- **Plow crews [K7, K14] [SM:competence]:** WYDOT was about 70 maintenance workers short going into winter 2022-23; Rawlins normally has a 10-person crew and had 4-5; a Laramie crewman was transferred to Rawlins several times; drifts 6-7 ft; at the peak drivers were on call around the clock and rarely rested; crews fought drifts on clear days; diesel gelled despite additives; about 350 trucks, with decommissioned trucks kept as parts sources; snow budget $29.1M, spending $38.8M ($9.7M over); federal funds cannot pay for plowing; WYDOT trains people who lack a CDL (Cowboy State Daily, Mark Heinz, 2023-05-07, SOURCED). [R: parts-source trucks are a repair-culture fact]
- **Money [K8]:** I-80 is about 6% of Wyoming's roads but about 16% of WYDOT's maintenance budget and about 9% of its overall budget (Cowboy State Daily 2023-05-31, SOURCED). One trucking company estimated closures cost it $1-1.5M in lost revenue in one winter (same, SOURCED).
- **Truck parking [K5]:** WYDOT's I-80 Winter Freight project added nearly 100 spaces at the Quealy Dome truck parking area (about 20 miles west of Laramie) and the Fort Steele Rest Area; a federal event in Laramie in 2023 announced 200 new truck parking spaces (snippets; WYDOT and highways.dot.gov pages refused; UNVERIFIED). When Rock Springs lots fill, trucks are sent to the Sweetwater Events Complex (SweetwaterNow snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Towns on and near the stretch:**
  - **Laramie** (Albany County): 2020 census about 31,407; Union Pacific track reached it May 4, 1868; University of Wyoming founded there 1886 (search snippets of Wikipedia/WyoHistory, UNVERIFIED).
  - **Arlington:** began as the Rock Creek stage station on the Overland Trail (c. 1860-62), a home station with a blacksmith shop; renamed Arlington in the early 20th century; National Register Rock Creek Stage Station Historic District (1983); population 25 at the 2010 census (search snippets, UNVERIFIED).
  - **Elk Mountain** (Carbon County): Medicine Bow River crossing became a stage stop in 1862 when Ben Holladay moved the line south; declined after the Union Pacific was built in 1868; town incorporated 1909 (WyoHistory, Lori Van Pelt, 2023-02-07, SOURCED); 2020 population about 150 (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
  - **Rawlins** (Carbon County seat): 2020 population 8,221 (snippet, UNVERIFIED). Employers named by locals: railroad, Wyoming State Penitentiary, Sinclair refinery (Wyoming Public Radio, Melodie Edwards, 2018-09-14, SOURCED). The old penitentiary (1901-1981) is now the Wyoming Frontier Prison museum (snippet of corrections.wyo.gov, UNVERIFIED).
- **Rest area:** Wagonhound Rest Area at exit 267 near Elk Mountain; markers note teepee rings and the route around Elk Mountain used by early inhabitants, the Overland Trail and modern travelers (marker database snippets, UNVERIFIED).

### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- The wind problem produced a lineage of Wyoming-specific competence: the 1971 Wyoming snow fence, the 1973 gates, VSL, blowover research by UW, the Winter Science Team, the TMC. West of Laramie became "a major testing ground for winter road maintenance techniques" (Waggener 2020, SOURCED). [K14] [SM:competence; humanity and technology]
- **Closure vocabulary [K1, K6] [P7]:** WYDOT publishes fixed definitions (wyoroad.info definitions page, SOURCED):
  - *No Unnecessary Travel*: road open but travel advised against; oversize loads may go only to the nearest parking area or town unless the Highway Patrol approves.
  - *Extreme Blow Over Risk*: gusts of 60 mph or more where no closure signage exists, or lower gusts on very icy roads; aimed at RVs, moving vans, campers, small trailers, lightly loaded trucks.
  - *Weight-Based Wind Closure*: closure by gross vehicle weight, from UW research using wind speed, road orientation and geometry.
  - *Rolling Closure*: when a town on the route reaches capacity (parking, fuel, lodging), the closure moves back to the next town; residents can travel under the W-TAP program.
  - *Local Traffic Permitted*: residents and commuters may travel within the area without a WYDOT Authorized Travel code.
  - *Blowing Snow*: wind-driven snow at least one foot above the ground; *Reduced Visibility*: under 400 ft; *Drifted Snow*: drifts crossing the white line that cause a bump or pull on the wheel; *Strong Wind*: gusts of 40 mph.
- **Rolling closures and travel codes [K5, K4]:** WYDOT Director Luke Reiner (2021): trucks keep arriving after a closure, the closed town fills, so WYDOT closes the road at an unaffected population center to stack trucks there (e.g., Rawlins fills, close at Rock Springs). W-TAP lets registered Wyoming residents pass a gate with a code a trooper checks; Wyoming Authorized Travel issues permits milepost to milepost so people living just past a gate can get home (Cowboy State Daily, Wendy Corr, 2021-12-15, SOURCED).
- **Being stuck [K5, K9] [P4]:** on May 18, 2026 heavy wet snow (up to 30 in. in the Snowy Range) shut I-80 most of the day; hundreds were stuck more than 15 hours; a power outage in Rawlins and central Carbon County lasted over 24 hours and plow trucks in Rawlins could not refuel; truckers kept engines running (shutting down risks gelled fuel, frozen diesel exhaust fluid, locked brakes), chained drive tires and helped other drivers chain up; strangers shared food and helped dig out; the Highway Patrol refused a snowmobile supply drop (only tow trucks allowed on the road); a citizen Facebook page of road and weather reports had nearly a million hits that day; a Rawlins trucker said CB radio is little used day to day but still used in wrecks, delays and winter storms (Cowboy State Daily, Andrew Rossi, 2026-05-24, SOURCED). [K14] [SM:competence under pressure] [R: engine-idling and shared-fuel decisions]
- **Sheltering:** in a September 2000 closure, the Red Cross expected about 2,600 people at two shelters in Rawlins plus 200 in Rock Springs as hotels filled (Spokesman-Review snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Bypassed towns [K9]:** the direct route took through traffic away from Rock River, Medicine Bow and Hanna; Rock River has lost population with many buildings abandoned (Wyoming Tales and Trails snippet, UNVERIFIED); Hanna has fewer than 700 people and falling sales-tax revenue (Wyoming Public Media 2024 snippet, UNVERIFIED). Proposals to reroute I-80 onto the U.S. 30 corridor recur (WYDOT 2022 proposal; a $12.6B estimate in local media; snippets, UNVERIFIED).

### E. Structural features
- **Freight-dominated corridor:** roughly half of traffic is heavy trucks, mostly out-of-state drivers, so closures stack outsiders into small towns (Rawlins, Laramie, Sinclair, Rock Springs) whose motels, truck stops and parking set the limit on how far a closure can be held (Reiner 2021; definitions page; SOURCED). [K4, K5]
- **Crash history [K9]:** about 8,100 crashes on I-80 in five years, nearly 51% involving a commercial vehicle, 239 blowover crashes, 78% of commercial crashes on ice/frost/snow (Cowboy State Daily 2023-05-31, SOURCED). Major pileups listed by Bill Sniffin (Cowboy State Daily, 2025-02-22, SOURCED): Aug. 19, 2004 near Buford (7 dead, fog); Mar. 26, 2006 near Arlington (22 vehicles, 6 dead); Apr. 3, 2014 near Elk Mountain (54 vehicles); Apr. 2015 Quealy Dome (64 vehicles, 2 dead); Mar. 1, 2020 Rawlins-Wamsutter (nearly 100 vehicles, 3 dead); Feb. 14, 2025 Green River tunnel (26 vehicles, 3 dead, two of them Rawlins refinery workers). Jan. 28, 2023: two pileups near mileposts 261 and 266 (Wagonhound area), 44 vehicles, 1 dead (news snippets, UNVERIFIED). Charles Kuralt called the stretch the worst road in the U.S. in a 1972 CBS segment (Waggener book talk, SOURCED as reported). Troopers are regularly struck or nearly struck while working closures and crashes (news snippets 2022-2025, UNVERIFIED).
- **Workforce:** Rawlins has a long-settled Latino community (families from the 1890s in railroad and sheep work; later railroad, penitentiary and refinery jobs; south side historically the Mexican side; about 17 Latino-owned businesses in 2018) (Wyoming Public Radio 2018, SOURCED). [K4] [P9]
- **K6 everyday rules (statute-backed):** W.S. 24-1-109(a): willfully failing to observe a closure sign, marker or direction is a misdemeanor, up to $750 and/or 30 days. Enrolled Act 35 (SF0113, 2024), effective July 1, 2024, adds subsection (d) for closures to light and high-profile vehicles and weight-based closures: $1,000 and up to 30 days for a first offense; $2,500 and up to 30 days for a second within three years, deemed reckless driving for licensing; **the driver must pay the fine himself** (passed because some companies were paying drivers' citations) (enrolled act text, read from saved PDF, SOURCED; company-payment context Cowboy State Daily 2023-05-31, SOURCED). Chain law: Level 1 ("hazardous") and Level 2 ("extremely hazardous"); Level 2 requires chains on two drive wheels at opposite ends of one drive axle; trucks that cannot comply are turned back to the nearest community; $750 if a violation causes a closure, $250 otherwise (WYDOT chain law page snippets, UNVERIFIED; the live status page read only showed current status). Unwritten rules: none found.
- **Speech (K11):** English; Spanish also in daily use in Rawlins' long-settled Latino community (Wyoming Public Radio 2018, SOURCED). A nickname for the stretch exists (sent to the slang file, not recorded here).

## 2 · #22 Rogers Pass, British Columbia (avalanche control)

**Claim to test:** a mountain highway kept open by forecasters and controlled avalanche release, where reading the snow is a respected profession (K14).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED.** Parks Canada calls the Rogers Pass program the world's largest mobile avalanche control program. Avalanche forecasters (a section that has existed in some form since 1959) decide when and where to fire; Canadian Armed Forces gunners fire 105 mm howitzers from fixed roadside gun positions at about 270 targets on 135 paths; the Trans-Canada Highway is closed during missions (about 100 hours a year). The forecasting craft is institutionalized: a named line of lead forecasters since 1959, a national professional association (CAA, 1981) and the national public forecast center (Avalanche Canada) both based in nearby Revelstoke. **New fact (2026):** the Canadian Armed Forces is ending its part in the program when the agreement expires in August 2027.

### A. Snapshot
- **Where:** Rogers Pass, in the Selkirk Mountains inside Glacier National Park of Canada, on the Trans-Canada Highway between Revelstoke (west) and Golden (east). Elevation 1,330 m (4,364 ft), the third-highest point on the Trans-Canada; Rogers Pass National Historic Site since 1971 (Parks Canada avalanche "fact" page, modified 2024-10-31, SOURCED).
- **Traffic:** winter about 4,000 vehicles and 24-32 trains (10,000-15,000 ft long) a day; annual average daily traffic 6,000 (8,000 in summer) (same, SOURCED). Railway completed 1885; highway opened 1962 (same, SOURCED).
- **Snow:** about 155 snowfall days a year in the park; about 10 m of snow at treeline. Summit of Rogers Pass (1,315 m): average yearly snowfall 905 cm (1965-2024), record 1,553 cm (1966/67). Mt. Fidelity station (1,905 m): average 1,424 cm, record 2,151 cm (1966/67) (same, SOURCED).
- **Avalanches:** about 2,000 avalanche events a year recorded along the corridor; about 20 a year reach the highway with mass and about 80 with wind blast and snow dust; speeds up to 250 km/h (same, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** forecasters plus artillery plus static defenses keep a highway open through one of the most avalanche-exposed road corridors in the country.

### B. What else exists there
- **The control program [K1, K14] [SM:competence; dangerous system] [P7, P8]:** staff of 14 Parks Canada avalanche forecasters, technicians and visitor safety staff; 2 Parks Canada law enforcement officers; Parks Canada highway staff; two rotations of 17 Canadian Armed Forces personnel. 16 gun positions; 135 avalanche paths; about 270 artillery targets; about 40 km of highway; line-of-sight range up to 5 km with up to 1.8 km vertical rise; 105 mm C-3 howitzers firing live explosives. Average 20 control missions a year over the past 20 years (high 41 in 2011-12; low 8 in 2023-24) (Parks Canada fact page, SOURCED). DND gives 17 roadside positions (Western Sentinel, 2023-02-28, SOURCED); the counts differ by source.
- **Who decides [K14]:** Parks Canada staff decide which targets are fired on as the gunners work through the pass (DND Maple Leaf, Capt Lance Wade, 2023-03-06, SOURCED). Forecasters combine snow profiles (temperature, crystal form and size, moisture, density, hand hardness, stability tests) with weather and issue daily bulletins; technicians watch surface hoar and other surface changes (Parks Canada "Snow science history," SOURCED).
- **Operation PALACI (military) [K7] [SM:competence]:** November to April; two groups of 15-20 artillery members per season, each serving about half the season, drawn from Regular or Reserve artillery; 2023 was the first year all three Reserve brigades of 3rd Canadian Division shared the task (DND Western Sentinel 2023-02-28, SOURCED). Gunners fired over 1,000 rounds in 2021-22 (Maple Leaf 2023, SOURCED). The operation is described by Parks Canada as the longest-running Canadian Forces operation, domestic or expeditionary, and the only domestic CAF operation giving live fire support (fact page, SOURCED). A main mountain compound, apartment-style, provides food and lodging, holding up to 50 members at rotation change (search snippet of army.ca/DND, UNVERIFIED). ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **The railway gave up the pass [K9]:** in 30 years the CPR fought avalanches, snow, fires and mudslides; over 200 people were killed by avalanches at the pass in the 26 years after the line opened (Wikipedia, SOURCED as the page states). **March 4, 1910, about 11:30 p.m.:** a crew of 63 with a rotary plow was clearing a slide from Cheops Mountain when a second avalanche from Avalanche Mountain hit; 58 men died, one fireman survived (Billy Lachance); about 400 m of track buried; a relief train with 200 men, physicians and nurses came from Revelstoke and found no one to treat (Wikipedia, SOURCED; Parks Canada "End of the line," SOURCED for 58 dead and one survivor of the engine crew). **32 of the dead were Japanese workers**, contracted to the CPR through the Canada Nippon Supply Company (Nikka Yotatsu Kabushiki Kaisha); CPR paid $47.50 per person for funeral and burial; the Japanese remains were assumed sent to the company in Vancouver and graves went unrecorded and unmarked; one burial record (M. Yamaji, Mountain View Cemetery) was found in 2008 and the grave marked; full names and home prefectures come from the March 8, 1910 *Tairiku Nippo*, translated in 2008 (JCCA Bulletin, John Endo Greenaway, 2009-03-03, SOURCED). Snippet: at the 1910 Revelstoke service the Japanese names were read separately from the white men's names, with a line dividing the printed list (UNVERIFIED). The CPR began the Connaught Tunnel in 1913; it opened December 13, 1916; the surface route was abandoned 1916/1917 (sources differ) (Wikipedia; Parks Canada; SOURCED).
- **Highway made the profession [K14]:** the 1956 decision to route the Trans-Canada over the pass created the snow research section, artillery trials and static defenses; the highway opened 1962 "with static defenses and the avalanche control program keeping it open in winter" (Parks Canada timeline, SOURCED).
- **2003 deaths reshaped the field [K9, K1]:** January 20, 2003 La Traviata gully (about 30 km NE of Revelstoke), 7 dead; February 1, 2003 Connaught Creek near Rogers Pass, 7 students of Strathcona-Tweedsmuir School killed on a school trip; 29 avalanche deaths in Canada that year, about double the average. Parks Canada's June 2003 review made 36 recommendations; Grant Statham hired November 2003 as first avalanche and mountain risk specialist; public backcountry advisory to mainstream media late 2004; Canadian Avalanche Center formed November 2004 (later Avalanche Canada); the Avalanche Terrain Exposure Scale developed after 2003; B.C. had withdrawn its public-avalanche funding from the CAA in fall 2002 (The Tyee, Amanda Follett Hosgood, 2023-01-27, SOURCED). Connaught Creek was later rated Class 3 terrain, unsuitable for school groups (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Avalanche training [K14]:** CAA Industry Training Program: Avalanche Operations Level 1, 2, 3 for professionals (ski patrol, guiding, highways); Level 1 is prerequisite to Level 2; public training is separate (Avalanche Canada); over 12,000 people took basic avalanche training in a year (Tyee 2023, SOURCED for 12,000; course structure from snippets, UNVERIFIED).

## 3 · #20 Truckee and Donner Pass, California

**Claim to test:** the last town before a notorious mountain crossing, where chain controls and closures make road-condition knowledge a daily matter (K5, K14).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with a correction of framing.** Truckee (about 16,800 residents) sits on I-80 about 14 miles east of the Donner Pass summit; westbound it is the last town before the crossing, eastbound the first after it. Chain controls (R-1/R-2/R-3) are a codified routine with designated chain-up areas at Truckee, Donner Lake and Kingvale, licensed independent chain installers, a CHP commercial enforcement facility inside the town limits, and a town-wide winter street-parking ban from November 1 to April 30. Correction: today's Truckee is not mainly a road-service town; over half of its housing is second homes, and its economy is resort and recreation tourism. Its road-knowledge culture sits alongside a long history of snow-fighting on the railroad (snowsheds, rotary plows), a UC Berkeley snow laboratory, and a winter festival invented in 1895 to fill the slow season.

### A. Snapshot
- **Where:** Truckee, Nevada County, California, on the Truckee River and I-80, east of Donner Pass in the Sierra Nevada; Donner Summit on I-80 about 7,200 ft (Transport Topics/Sacramento Bee, Tony Bizjak, 2016-12-27, SOURCED).
- **Population and housing:** 16,778 (Town 2024 annual report); vacancy rate 50.6%, which the Town attributes to second-home ownership; 2.43 persons per household (snippet of Town of Truckee 2024 Community Development annual report, UNVERIFIED). About 60% of Tahoe-Truckee homes were second homes in 2019, up from 52% in 2000 (NNBW snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Freight:** about 3,500 commercial trucks cross Donner Summit daily, year-round, carrying nearly $5 million in goods (state estimate in Bizjak 2016, SOURCED). Union Pacific's Donner Pass route carries 10-20 long freight trains a day (Museum of Truckee History snippet, undated, UNVERIFIED); Truckee Amtrak station ridership 16,522 in FY 2025 (Great American Stations snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Snow:** Donner Summit averages about 34 ft of snow a year (Donner Summit Historical Society, SOURCED); Caltrans' Kingvale station averages 430 in. a year (Caltrans publication snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Defining feature:** a mountain crossing whose winter passability is managed day to day by Caltrans, CHP and a trade of chain installers, with a long, painful history (the Donner Party, the Chinese railroad crews, the snowsheds).

### B. What else exists there
- **Chain controls [K1, K6] [P7]:** Caltrans definitions (Caltrans "Chain Controls / Chain Installation," SOURCED):
  - **R-1:** chains required on all vehicles except passenger cars and light trucks under 6,000 lb with snow tires on at least two drive wheels (which must still carry chains); towing vehicles chain one drive axle; braked trailers chain one axle.
  - **R-2:** chains or traction devices on all vehicles except 4WD/AWD with snow-tread tires on all four wheels (which must carry devices).
  - **R-3:** chains on all vehicles, no exceptions; the highway is often closed before R-3.
  - Speed limit under chain control 25 or 30 mph; roughly one mile between "Chains Required" signs and the checkpoint; follow Caltrans or CHP at checkpoints even if broadcasts differ; Caltrans may refuse entry to any vehicle it judges will have difficulty; CHP can cite drivers who do not chain up.
- **Chain installers [K14, K7] [SM:competence] [R]:** independent business people, not Caltrans employees; customers should get a receipt and note the installer's badge number; installers may not sell or rent chains (Caltrans, SOURCED). ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things

Other sources give 41-42 dead and 47-49 survivors (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Two Indigenous guides sent by John Sutter (known as Luis and Salvador; identified as Miwok in most sources, possibly Nisenan) were shot and eaten by members of the snowshoe party; the shooter is generally believed to be William Foster (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Paiute leader Sarah Winnemucca later recalled her people would have helped "only my people were afraid of them" (NPS, SOURCED). - **The Chinese railroad crews, 1866-67 [K9, K14] [P9]:** Summit Tunnel (No. 6), 1,659 ft, above 7,000 ft, hand-carved; work began August 1866 and broke through August 1867 with the two headings two inches off; that winter crews at Tunnel 6 were almost entirely Chinese, in gangs of 30-40 with one white foreman, on three rotating 8-hour shifts; 6,000-10,000 men on the railroad that winter, up to 12,000; 44 storms; snowpack averaging 18 ft at the summit; the heaviest storm February 18-March 2, 1867; crews dug tunnels under the snow between camps and work sites; the railroad lost "uncounted" men to snow and an avalanche at Strong's Canyon buried two Chinese gangs and a culvert gang (one man not found until spring); nitroglycerin mixed on site from February 1867 sped progress to up to 2 ft a day per face (PBS American Experience, SOURCED). - **Ice and timber [K7, K8]:** ice reached Truckee in 1868; first company the Boca Mill and Ice Company; harvesters were summer lumberjacks given winter work, $3/day with room and board in the 1800s; Truckee ice cooled Nevada mines (up to 95 lb per miner per day), San Francisco hotels and bars, and Pacific Fruit Express cars; Boca Brewing stored over 5,000 tons of ice in 1884; industry peak 1880-1900; final Boca harvest winter 1926-27 (Sierra Sun, Judy DePuy, 2019-08-22, SOURCED). ## 4 · #24 CB radio trucker culture (1970s)

**Claim to test:** a road culture in which drivers passed condition reports and news to each other in real time, with its own etiquette and songs (K14, K6).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with two corrections.** (1) In the period sources the dominant uses were finding fuel during the 1973-74 shortages, warning of police radar and weigh stations, summoning help after crashes, relaying messages home and staying awake; weather and road reports were relayed too, partly by fixed home "base stations" along highways. (2) The culture ran *against* the formal rules: the GAO found in 1975 that the FCC called CB violations "rampant" and the situation "chaotic," that 42 of 45 states reported CB used to avoid law enforcement, and that station identification was the most-violated rule. Etiquette was largely self-enforced by users; songs and films (1975-78) turned it into a national craze. Not a single place: below are the tradition and four documented places where it was practiced (I-80 at Blakeslee, Pennsylvania; an Indiana Toll Road base station and a Pennsylvania I-80 truck stop; the Iowa 80 truck stop at Walcott; the Utah Highway Patrol).

### A. Snapshot (the tradition)
- **What:** Citizens Band (CB) radio: the FCC's Class D citizens service created September 11, 1958 on 27 MHz, originally 23 channels, expanded to 40 channels in 1977; 4 watts output (12 W peak on single sideband); a 150-mile/250 km distance limit on contacts (museum.tv Radio Encyclopedia, SOURCED; Time 1963, SOURCED for the 150-mile figure). First recorded licensee John M. Mulligan in 1958; 40 licensees by year's end (museum.tv, SOURCED).
- **Who:** long-haul truck drivers first, then the general public. Since 1972 thousands of drivers had bought sets, most often in the Midwest; a set cost about $150; the FCC required a $20 license per set, which few truckers bothered with (TIME, "Voices on the Road," December 3, 1973, SOURCED). In 1976 three out of five long-haul trucks were CB-equipped (1977 AP CB guide snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Scale:** licenses grew from 1.1 million to 1.5 million in the six months to June 30, 1975; the FCC estimated almost 6 million authorized transmitters; the license fee was cut from $20 to $4 in early 1975 (GAO report GGD-75-88, 1975, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). Applications ran at about 500,000-600,000 a month in 1976 (FCC commissioner's 1976 remarks and a 1976 CB guide, snippets, UNVERIFIED); about 12.25 million licensed users by end of 1977, about 10% more unlicensed (JSTOR Daily 2021 citing Blake, SOURCED); seven million units sold in one peak year (museum.tv, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** a shared, open, anonymous party-line among strangers on the road, run more by its users' customs than by federal rules.

### B. What else existed around it (institutions, rules, money)
- **FCC rules [K6] [P7]:** five-minute conversation limit followed by one minute of silence; substantive messages only (no "hobby or diversion" talk until rules changed); channel 9 reserved for emergencies; antenna height limits; station identification by call sign; no profanity (Time 1963, SOURCED; GAO 1975, SOURCED; 1976 rules booklet snippet, UNVERIFIED). Channel 9 was designated the emergency channel in 1967 (museum.tv, SOURCED; other sources give 1969, 1970 or 1972; snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **How the rules fared [K6, K9]:** GAO 1975 (SOURCED): the FCC received 31,000 CB complaints in FY1975 (55% of all radio-interference complaints it received), including 250 investigation requests from members of Congress; six rules accounted for about 70% of the 9,600 violations cited in FY1974; station identification was the most frequently violated rule, operators instead using pseudonyms; since 1966 CB users had been detected on 84 frequencies outside the band; 42 of 45 states reported CB used to avoid law enforcement and 41 reported truckers using it to avoid speed, weight and licensing checks; 7 reported other illegal uses; officials of 2 states said its benefits far outweigh its disadvantages; in 1973 the Pennsylvania State Police asked the FCC to identify truckers using CB to evade speed and weight limits, but the Communications Act's secrecy provision barred disclosure of monitored conversations; FCC enforcement in August and November 1974 led to Justice Department prosecution of 29 truckers, all of whom pleaded guilty; in February-March 1974 the FCC inspected radio-equipped trucks at California weigh stations and in June 1974 ran a nationwide truck inspection-education program; traveling enforcement teams had not deterred violators; FCC policy was to not monitor hobby conversations as such. A license survey found 37% of non-renewing users cited malicious interference, profanity and rude behavior on the air.
- **Licensing ended:** a license was not required after April 28, 1983 (RadioReference snippet, UNVERIFIED; museum.tv says a court made the fees illegal and the FCC issued a blanket authorization, SOURCED without date).
- **REACT [K1, K14] [SM:competence]:** Radio Emergency Associated Communication Teams, started January 1962 by Henry B. "Pete" Kreer with sponsorship from radio maker Hallicrafters after a Chicago snowstorm incident; three-member monitoring teams; 800 teams by 1964, when REACT proposed a national emergency channel (museum.tv, SOURCED). Volunteers watch channel 9 for motorists; later storm spotting and disaster communications (Wikipedia snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Federal and state monitoring [K1]:** NHTSA's National Emergency Aid Radio (NEAR) program trained and coordinated volunteer and public-safety monitors of channel 9; states could use federal Section 402 grants for it (1977 NHTSA release snippet, UNVERIFIED). Utah troopers bought their own CB sets for patrol cars and monitored channels 9 and 19; citizens reported crashes, drunk drivers and breakdowns; the 1978 legislature appropriated $32,000 for 40-channel Cobra radios able to scan channel 9 (Utah Highway Patrol history page, SOURCED).
- **Money and industry [K8]:** the 23-channel to 40-channel change (40 channels legal from January 1, 1977; sale of 23-channel sets ordered stopped January 1, 1978) collapsed prices; Hy-Gain bought back $12-14 million of 23-channel stock; Johnson Co. lost $4.4M on $10.3M sales in Q3 1977; two companies filed bankruptcy (Christian Science Monitor 1980, USITC, Time snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Owner-operators' organizations [K1] [SM:man vs society]:** the Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association was formed in 1973 amid the fuel crisis; its first office was a trailer chained to a light post in a truck-stop lot along I-70 in Missouri; Jim Johnston president 1975-2018; about 160,000 members today; all directors and officers current or past drivers (FreightWaves, Brielle Jaekel, 2023-09-14, SOURCED). Overdrive magazine publisher Mike Parkhurst formed the Independent Truckers Association in 1975 (JSTOR Daily, Livia Gershon, 2017, SOURCED).

### C. Cultural locations and expressions
- **Songs and films [K13]:** C.W. McCall's "Convoy" (1975) reached No. 1 on the U.S. pop and country charts and No. 1 in Canada (January 24, 1976); C.W. McCall was a character created by advertising man Bill Fries with Chip Davis (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Films: *Smokey and the Bandit* (1977; second-highest-grossing U.S. film of 1977, behind *Star Wars*), *Citizens Band / Handle with Care* (1977), *Convoy* (1978); TV *Movin' On* (snippets, UNVERIFIED; the films are also named in museum.tv, SOURCED).
- **Clubs and gatherings [K10, K3]:** local CB clubs held jamborees (a Long Island club's second annual jamboree at the Bethpage bowling alley, April 8, 1962, over 300 attendees the prior year; the Lake Compounce CB Jamboree in Bristol, Connecticut; charity jamborees in small-town parks, e.g., Warren, Arkansas, with members in matching vests) (Popular Electronics 1962 reprint and local recollections, snippets, UNVERIFIED). A reader recalled that local in-person meetups faded after the FCC dropped call signs (SWLing Post snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Black CB networks [K4] [P9]:** Black CB users were active by 1959 (the Rooster Channel Jumpers, a national network with formal governance and uniformed members); about 10,000 met in Dallas in 1978; the Deacons for Defense and Justice (Jonesboro, Louisiana) used CB to respond quickly to threats against Black residents and civil-rights workers; the Klan also used CB and Black users monitored those channels; mainstream media portrayed CB as a white trucker pastime (JSTOR Daily, Ashawnta Jackson, 2021-11-18, citing Art M. Blake, *American Quarterly* 63:3, 2011; SOURCED).
- **Scholarship:** W. Tyler Watts and Jared Barton, "'I Can't Drive 55': The Economics of the CB Radio Phenomenon," *The Independent Review* 15:3 (2011) (listing only; findings not read).

### D. How the defining feature shaped things: four places where it was practiced
1. **I-80 near Blakeslee, Pennsylvania (the 1973 shutdown) [K9] [SM:man vs society]:** on December 3, 1973 at 10 p.m., driver J.W. Edwards (37, of Overland Park, Kansas; a company driver hauling meat, earning $12,000 a year) stopped on I-80 after running out of fuel and broadcast his protest by CB; within an hour hundreds of rigs had stopped, jamming over 1,000 vehicles for about 12 miles each way. Demonstrations followed in more than 10 states on December 4; a two-day strike December 13-14; a coordinated strike from January 31, 1974; most rigs back on the road by February 11 after a federal threat to send troops. Grievances: high diesel prices, scarce fuel and the proposed 55 mph limit. Settlement February 7, 1974: owner-operators could add a temporary 6% fuel surcharge and truck stops were guaranteed more fuel. Violence: tire slashing, shooting at vehicles, a stabbing, bricks dropped from overpasses; Ohio's governor deployed the National Guard and tear gas; about 100,000 layoffs and "several deaths" (History.com, Joseph A. Williams, 2019/2025, SOURCED). Two drivers died in the February 1974 shutdown per the AP and NYT as cited (MSU and Michigan Daily snippets, UNVERIFIED); one South Carolina driver was reportedly shot in the chest near Harrington, Delaware (snippet, UNVERIFIED). Pennsylvania Gov. Milton Shapp promised more fuel for rigs (History.com, SOURCED).
2. **Indiana Toll Road base station and a Pennsylvania I-80 truck stop (1973) [K14, K5]:** Chet Haas, 64, monitored national weather and road reports from his home beside the Indiana toll road and relayed them to passing trucks; other base stations were in friends' homes along highways; the Quaker State Plaza truck stop on I-80 in Pennsylvania had employees who took customers' calls by CB and told low-fuel drivers to stop. Uses listed: avoiding inspectors and police, relaying messages home, calling for help after accidents, passing time, staying alert (drivers said CB was replacing stimulant pills); some solo drivers drove up to 14 hours a day despite limits; talk was generally clean, which TIME credited to the drivers themselves; a trucker said he could not make money if he did everything legally (TIME 1973-12-03, SOURCED). [R: relay stations as a shared-information service]
3. **Iowa 80 Truckstop, Walcott, Iowa [K10, K3]:** opened 1964 as a Standard station at the Walcott exit (now Exit 284) of a newly opened stretch of I-80, founded by Bill Moon; bought by Bill and Carolyn Moon from Amoco in 1984; 220 acres, parking for 900 tractor-trailers; a trucking museum from Moon's collection; the Walcott Truckers Jamboree each July (snippets, UNVERIFIED; official history page refused with certificate error). Walcott Radio, near the truck stop, describes itself as the nation's largest CB radio repair and sales store (snippet, UNVERIFIED). [K14] [R: radio repair shops]
4. **Utah Highway Patrol (late 1970s) [K1]:** see B above (SOURCED); a 1980 Parleys Canyon incident where a trooper used CB, lights and siren to warn traffic ahead of a runaway truck with burning axles.
- **CB repair trade [K14] [R]:** shops proliferated (C.B. World in Albany, Oregon, opened July 1976 as the town's ninth CB shop; technicians re-aligned radios with service manuals and factory schematics); an engineer's 1981 repair guide was written from late-night repair sessions with drivers at the Union 76 truck stop in Ontario, California, and noted many modifications were illegal without a commercial license (snippets, UNVERIFIED; cbradiomagazine.com unreachable).
- **Blizzard of 1978:** networks of CB operators helped dispatch emergency personnel in Indianapolis; stranded motorists in Illinois asked CB users for road conditions (Encyclopedia of Indianapolis and McHenry news-index snippets, UNVERIFIED).

### E. Structural features
- **The 55 mph limit [K6]:** Emergency Highway Energy Conservation Act signed January 2, 1974 (P.L. 93-239), effective 60 days later; states had to adopt 55 to keep federal highway funds; made permanent by the Federal-Aid Highway Amendments of 1974, signed January 4, 1975; states allowed 65 on rural interstates from 1987; repealed by the National Highway System Designation Act of 1995 (History.com and FHWA snippets, UNVERIFIED). A numeric code system and a large shared jargon existed; they are not recorded here (language caution).

## 5 · #16 The Milepost (Alaska Highway travel guide)

**Claim to test:** a long-running annual guide that turns accumulated traveler knowledge of a hard road into a shared reference (K5, K14).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with a correction about whose knowledge it holds.** *The Milepost* has been published every year since 1949 (77th edition in 2025): a mile-by-mile log, first of the Alaska Highway (opened to tourists in 1948) and now of 88 highways and roads across Alaska, Yukon and western Canada. Correction: the knowledge is gathered mainly by the publisher's own field editors, who live in Alaska and Canada and re-drive the routes every year; travelers contribute indirectly (reader comments are quoted, reader surveys are run), and the businesses listed pay for advertising. It is a commercial annual, not a crowdsourced one. Below: the guide itself, and the road and three places it serves (Dawson Creek, Watson Lake, Tok/Delta Junction), plus the lodges.

### A. Snapshot
- **What:** an annual travel guide/log. First edition 1949, by William A. "Bill" Wallace: 72 pages, a saddle-stitched booklet under 4 ounces, logging the Alaska Highway and seven other highways, sold mainly by mail and at northern newsstands (themilepost.com About Us, SOURCED; Morris Media Network history page, SOURCED). Named for the mileage posts that "filled such a vital need along the wilderness road" (Morris page, SOURCED). First edition imprint: Alaska Research Co., Anchorage (WorldCat snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- **Ownership:** Wallace sold the guide in 1962 to the owner of the publication that became *Alaska* magazine (Morris page, SOURCED); that magazine's owners were fur trader Robert A. Henning and journalist Robert N. DeArmond (Alaska Northwest Publishing Co., from 1958) (Wikipedia/Juneau library snippets, UNVERIFIED). 498 pages by 1975; bought with *Alaska* magazine by Morris Media Network in 1995 (Morris page, SOURCED; other sources give 1993 or 1997, UNVERIFIED). Main office 301 Arctic Slope Avenue, Anchorage (2019 media kit, read from saved PDF, SOURCED).
- **Today:** over 600-700 pages, about 3 lb; logs 88 highways and roads, from the 1,488-mile Alaska Highway to the 1.6-mile Anchor River Road; 500+ photos, 56 city/park maps, 42 strip maps; new edition each February; also digital and an app (themilepost.com About Us, SOURCED; My Itchy Travel Feet, Donna Hull, 2024 review updated 2025-05-27, SOURCED). Editor Kris Valencia for about 30 years (retired); current editor Serine Marie Reeves (Hull review, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** an annually re-verified, mile-referenced shared memory of a hard, thinly serviced road.

### B. What else exists around it

## 6 · #103 Andermatt, Switzerland

**Claim to test:** a village at the junction of several high pass roads, whose seasonal pass openings and closures are announced and awaited, so road-condition knowledge sets life there (K5, K7).
**Verdict:** **CONFIRMED, with additions.** Andermatt (Urseren valley, Canton Uri, about 1,410-1,440 m) sits where the Gotthard (south), Furka (west) and Oberalp (east) pass roads meet, with the Schöllenen gorge (north) as its only year-round road out. Gotthard, Furka and Oberalp close every winter; the canton announces closings and openings (Oberalp usually first in Uri, around late April-May; Furka late May; Gotthard around Ascension); in winter the valley's connections run by rail (Furka car-train, Oberalp car-train to Sedrun) and through the Schöllenen, which itself closes in avalanche danger, leaving Andermatt reachable only by train or not at all. Additions: (1) the valley's life is also structured by a medieval common-land body, the Korporation Ursern, which owns 93% of the valley; (2) the village was for about a century an army town (weapons range since 1885), and the army's withdrawal (2004) led to the resort development; (3) its best-known custom is tied to the protective forest above the village (Bannbrief of 1397).

### A. Snapshot
- **Where/what:** Andermatt, Urseren valley (Andermatt, Hospental, Realp), upper Reuss, Canton Uri; valley floor 1,430-1,540 m; first documented 1203; founded by Walser settlers ("an der Matte"); the Benedictine monastery of Disentis held basic rights from about 800 until bought out in 1649; municipality split into residential, citizens' and church communes only in 1897 (Gemeinde Andermatt history page, read from saved PDF, SOURCED).
- **Population:** 1,260 in 2009, about 1,410 by 2019-2021 (Blick, Sarah Frattaroli, 2021-08-19, reporting the HSLU study, SOURCED).
- **Defining feature:** a pass-junction village whose winter access shrinks to rail and one avalanche-exposed gorge road.

### B. What else exists there
- **Pass seasons [K5, K2] [P5]:** in autumn 2026 TCS listed Furka, Gotthard and Oberalp under winter closure for all vehicles; the Furka closed for the winter at 10:00 on October 28, 2026 (TCS and alpen-paesse snippets, UNVERIFIED). Oberalp is described as always the first Uri crossing to reopen (April 22, 2020; May 1, 2019); Furka reopened May 28, 2025 at 10:00 per the Uri works department; Gotthard opening targeted around Ascension (snippets, UNVERIFIED). Gotthard clearing (federal road, carried out by the canton): in 2024 clearing began in week 15 on the south side, higher sections waiting on snow depth and avalanche danger; cantonal staff assess avalanche conditions continuously and call in the federal avalanche institute (SLF) in critical situations; besides snow, crews patch the surface, restore drainage, reopen tunnels and remove loose rock; in a heavy-snow year 14 workers with five snow blowers; in 2024 snow at the start of clearing was about three times usual (ASTRA notice and Blick snippets, UNVERIFIED; luzern.com page refused 403). The Uri works department publishes weekly pass-opening reports (afbn.ch PDFs listed, not opened). [K14] [SM:competence]
- **Before the Gotthard opens [K6, K5]:** from April 4 to about mid-May 2025 Uri ran congestion rules on the A2: the Göschenen and Wassen southbound entrances close when the jam reaches 3 km (to stop traffic diverting through villages), the Göschenen entrance opens four times a day for 15 minutes so locals and workers can travel south; at 8 km the limit drops to 80 km/h between Altdorf and Amsteg; exits at Erstfeld and Amsteg are metered (Andermatt-Sedrun-Disentis tourism page, SOURCED). Village residents elsewhere in Uri have protested holiday detour traffic by blocking roads (Le Temps / bluewin snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Winter rail links [K5]:** the Matterhorn Gotthard Bahn runs a year-round car-train through the 15.4 km Furka base tunnel (Realp-Oberwald, about 15-20 minutes); in winter a car-train over the Oberalp to Sedrun (about 60 minutes in a 2021/22 timetable, cars to 3.5 t, booking at loading stations) (MGB and timetable snippets, UNVERIFIED). The Furka rail tunnel opened 1981, giving year-round connection to Valais (Gemeinde history, SOURCED).
- **When the gorge closes [K5, K9]:** reports describe the Göschenen-Hospental road closed for avalanche danger and Andermatt "reachable neither by car nor by train" when the railway also closed; in January 2021 Andermatt could be reached only by train; rockfall closed the Schöllenen in May 2015 and the cantonal police manned the Andermatt post (SRF and other snippets, UNVERIFIED; a resort news page returned 404). Late February 1999: lasting heavy snowfall interrupted all routes for an extended period; avalanches caused only property damage. Winter 2008/09: repeated interruptions from avalanche danger (Gemeinde history, SOURCED).
- **Korporation Ursern [K1, K6] [P7]:** a public-law corporation in its present form since 1888; 574 voting valley citizens (426 in Andermatt, 84 Hospental, 64 Realp); a 9-member valley council (Talrat); owns about 175 km², 93% of the valley (alps, forests, rock, glaciers, streams, lakes), of which 83 km² (about 48%) is summer grazing; tasks: farming and alp farming, forest and avalanche works, social affairs, culture, education, tourism, the valley archive; owns the Elektrizitätswerk Ursern; grants concessions (ski company, gravel firm); owns about 31 km of corporation roads plus about 12 km of military roads; members of foundations for the valley museum and the senior and health center; summer grazing about 1,750 cattle (about 650 wintered in the valley, 1,100 from outside) and about 6,500 sheep and goats (about 1,100 local, 5,400 outside), 15 small-livestock and 18 cattle alps; pays up to CHF 30 per animal for small livestock kept fenced at least 30 days in spring to fight scrub encroachment (Korporation Ursern presentation by Sandro Danioth, 2025-09-09, read from saved PDF, SOURCED). History: common land (alps and forests) reserved for valley people by a law recorded in 1363; the valley joined Uri by Landrecht in 1410 but kept its own customs, alps and commons; until 1976 the corporation kept some poor-relief and citizenship powers (HLS snippets, UNVERIFIED; HLS pages refused 403).
- **Protective forest [K1, K6]:** in 1397 the "Teilgenossen an der Matt" issued a forest ban letter (Bannbrief) reserving the forest above the village; in 1717 the village people were allotted the Gurschen forest (HLS snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Medieval mule transport [K1, K14] [P8]:** the Ursern muleteers held the monopoly on goods transport through the valley (muleteer ordinance 1393, renewed by Uri 1498 and 1701); goods moved stage by stage between warehouses (Sust) by part-time farmer-muleteers organized in cooperatives under a fixed rotation (Rod) set by the stage masters; direct through-haulage existed alongside; free muleteers paid a road fee to the cooperative (HLS "Säumerei" snippets, UNVERIFIED). The carriage road over the Gotthard was built 1818/1827-1830; Furka and Oberalp roads 1862-1866; the Gotthard railway tunnel (1882) was "an economic blow" to Andermatt (Gemeinde history, SOURCED; HLS snippet for road dates). Horse post: daily express coaches with ten seats and five horses from 1842 until 1882; first postbuses over the pass June 1922 (Flüelen municipal history PDF and Swiss Post snippets, UNVERIFIED; post.ch refused SSL).
- **Army [K7, K8, K9]:** the Andermatt weapons range has existed since 1885 (Gemeinde history, SOURCED). For decades the army employed about 200 residents; in 2004 it cut troops and closed many training centers including Andermatt's (swissinfo, Helen James, 2019-02-09, SOURCED). The army still runs the Mountain Competence Center (Kompetenzzentrum Gebirgsdienst der Armee) at Waffenplatz West, Andermatt, responsible for training and development of the army's mountain and avalanche service and administering the range (VTG pages, snippets, UNVERIFIED). An army sports base opened 1998 (Gemeinde history, SOURCED). The military emergency hospital served the Uri population for decades until replaced by a clinic in December 2016 (Canton Uri snippet, UNVERIFIED). [SM:competence]
- **Resort and residents [K9, K8]:** in 2005 investor Samih Sawiris (Orascom) was invited in; in 2006 the defense ministry sold former range land for CHF 10 million to the Korporation Ursern, which resold it to Sawiris at the same price; some villagers suspected the land was sold below value (swissinfo 2019, SOURCED). The Federal Council approved the sale despite the Lex Koller (September 2006); in May 2007 the municipal assembly cut the corporate tax rate from 2.4 to 0.1 per mille (a compilation chronology by a teacher, 2014, read from saved PDF, SOURCED as that compilation states, from press sources). The Chedi hotel: groundbreaking September 26, 2009, opened December 6, 2013 (Gemeinde history, SOURCED). HSLU study (2009-2020): overnight stays 73,000 (2009) to 129,000 (2019); single-family house prices up 270-500% (Switzerland 44%); rents up about 50%; municipal tax income nearly doubled in ten years; residents see more advantages than disadvantages, but many fear displacement and describe a spatial and social split between the old village and the new resort quarter, and "chaotic" weekends from ski visitors (Blick 2021, SOURCED). SRF (2022-03-27, SOURCED): 420 apartments built, nearly all sold (92 in the past year); Andermatt is the only place in Switzerland where new second homes still come on the market (approved before the 2012 second-home ban) and the resort is exempt from the Lex Koller until 2040; young families find it hard to buy; resort staff live in neighboring municipalities. Investment over CHF 1.2 billion since 2009 (Blick 2021, SOURCED). (Per the input's care note, the resort is recorded here only for its effect on residents.)

### C. Cultural locations
- **Woldmanndli procession [K2] [P6]:** end of October, on the Saturday of the village fair (Chilbi), about a hundred men, women and children in jute sacks with soot-blackened faces and cowbells come down from the Gurschen forest into the village at 1 p.m. and receive a hot soup in the village center (donated by a different sponsor each year); it is tied to the 1397 ban on the Gurschen forest, the participants representing the workers who tended the forest that protects the village from avalanches; given its fixed form (parade and shared soup) by Willi Bomatter-Furger after loose organization until the 1960s; kept up since 2005 by the local St. Nicholas organization; the evening continues in the pubs (Andermatt-Sedrun-Disentis tourism story, 2025-10-22, SOURCED). ### D. How the defining feature shaped those things
- **Avalanche disasters [K9]:** January 20, 1951: avalanches through the middle of Andermatt killed 13 residents, including women and children, after days of snowfall (snow up to 2 m); part of the population had been evacuated; civilians and army searched together; across the Alps more than 1,000 avalanches killed 75 people January 19-22 (SRF, Remi Bütler, 2019-01-15, SOURCED); the 1950/51 winter killed 98 in Switzerland with about 1,500 buildings destroyed (SLF snippet, UNVERIFIED). The municipality records the winters of 1951 and 1975 as when "mighty avalanches" reached homes and took lives (Gemeinde history, SOURCED). Later: avalanche barriers and hazard mapping expanded nationwide (SRF, SOURCED). A January 2017 off-piste avalanche at Gemsstock killed one (police release snippet, UNVERIFIED); a December 26, 2019 glide-snow avalanche hit a piste at the Oberalp (no deaths; prosecutor closed the case after two years; SLF had warned the operators) (snippets, UNVERIFIED).
- **Dams fought off [K9]:** reservoir projects that would have flooded the whole valley were defeated by the population in 1920 and 1946 (Gemeinde history, SOURCED).
- **Traffic gained and lost:** the 1830 road made Andermatt a holiday, spa and trade place; the 1882 rail tunnel hit it; the 1980 Gotthard road tunnel relieved it of through traffic (Gemeinde history, SOURCED); the valley had lobbied around 1960 for a road tunnel portal at Hospental (ETH thesis snippet, UNVERIFIED). The army then sustained the village for about a century until 2004 (swissinfo, SOURCED).
- **Flood [K9]:** August 24-25, 1987 flood disaster in the Urseren valley and much of Uri (Gemeinde history, SOURCED).
