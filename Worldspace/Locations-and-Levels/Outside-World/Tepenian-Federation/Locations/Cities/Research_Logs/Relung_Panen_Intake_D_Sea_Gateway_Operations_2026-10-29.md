- **Comparative risk (SOURCED, Anderson 2024, p. 10, citing DNV GL and Reflex Marine):** individual fatality
  risk per transfer **2.4×10⁻⁶ by helicopter** and **2.2×10⁻⁷ by crane-based capsule**; marine transfers have
  "potentially more incidents … but they are usually minor, as opposed to helicopter crashes, which more often
  result in fatalities."
- **Walk-to-work drawbacks (SOURCED, Anderson 2024, pp. 8-10):** expensive and mechanically complex; uses deck
  space; the supply vessel must be close enough to connect, which "increases the risk of vessel-vessel
  collisions"; "generally more attractive for smaller installations, in more benign environments"; not in use
  in Newfoundland and not common in the North Sea; "Operating wave heights for motion compensated systems are
  typically the same, or lower than crane-based systems." Helicopters there are limited by fog and by range
  "with a full complement of passengers."

### 1.2 Tenders and small boats: limits actually published

- **Stanley Harbor, Falklands (SOURCED, FIG Notice to Mariners 08/2024, issued 30 Sep 2024):** "When the wind
  conditions are sustained 30kts and gusting above 30kts, Stanley Harbor will be closed for fishing vessels
  and all passenger tenders and zodiacs"; it "may be closed at wind speeds below these limits"; "All tender
  operations will be suspended when visibility falls below 0.1nm"; "passenger movement to and from tenders
  should be limited to one tender at a time per pontoon." No wave-height limit is printed.
- **Dún Laoghaire / Dublin (UNVERIFIED, search-result text of the port notices, not opened):** tender permits
  must state their limiting wind, sea and swell, visibility, currents and tide, citing IMO MSC.1/Circ.1417;
  minimum visibility 1 nmi; 100 passengers per tender unless otherwise stated; daylight only.
- **Australian Antarctic Division IRB rules (SOURCED, AAD "Small boat (IRB/RIB) operations — safety
  principles" page):** "Except in emergencies, boat operations are only to be undertaken in fine and clear
  weather with calm or moderate wind" and a favorable forecast; "A boat is never taken out with a crew of less
  than two"; "No boat operation is to be undertaken that necessitates being at sea later than one hour before
  sunset"; icebergs: "you may not approach within a distance equal to twice the height of the iceberg"; in
  "calm conditions in pack ice around the Antarctic coast" a dry suit with flotation jacket or a flotation suit
  is required. The page gives no numeric wind limit.
- **AAD numeric IRB limit (SOURCED, AAD sitrep 20906, MPOV *Aiviq*, 28 Dec 2022, at 66°17'S 110°31'E,
  i.e. off Casey):** "The cut-off limit for our IRB's is 25 knots which is calculated not on gusts, but on the
  average wind speed taken over a 10 minute period." That day winds "averaged between 40-50 knots" and no boat
  work was done. **UNVERIFIED (search-result text):** another AAD sitrep near Mawson also gives 25 knots; others
  record barges recovered to deck as wind rose "above 30 knots" and operations suspended after gusts "above 25
  knots" with the ship leaving before cargo was finished.
- **US Antarctic Program, Palmer area (UNVERIFIED):** the archived USAP Field Manual chapter 8 (2008) is quoted
  in search-result text as "advised by radio if the wind speed reaches 20 knots" and "recalled if the wind
  reaches 25 knots or greater"; a UAB blog (2023) gives 20 knots for Zodiacs and 25 knots for the RHIB. Both
  pages failed to open (certificate rejected); not worked around.
- **IAATO (SOURCED, IAATO, "Guidelines for tourist operations in Antarctica", ATCM XXV IP-072, 2002):**
  Zodiacs carry "up to about 12 passengers … plus the driver"; "Each tour company has its own operating manual
  for Zodiac operations … and all are nearly identical in content"; boarding uses "an arm-to-arm grip" and a
  "securely fastened life vest"; the manual chapters include "Zodiacs around Wildlife and Icebergs." **No
  numeric wind or sea limit is given.** One company (Heritage Expeditions) ran "two amphibious all terrain
  vehicles for two Antarctic seasons in order to transport passengers from ship to shore across fast ice."
- **An expedition operator's description (SOURCED, Nautical Institute Iberia Branch, "Cruise Ship Expeditions
  in Antarctica — Ship Operation General Overview", 20 Mar 2023):** a "scout zodiac is deployed and the
  expedition team checks out the local conditions … If conditions look good, the green light is given";
  "Embarkation and disembarkation of passengers and crew, ashore and on top of ice, is challenging and requires
  well trained crew"; in the Drake Passage "Waves over 5 meters are common and anything below 3 meters is
  considered good weather." The example ship: Polar Category C, ice class 1B, 126 m, draft 4.75 m, 9,930 GT,
  180 passengers, 117 crew.

### 1.3 Transfers at an ice edge or alongside ice

- **Gangway onto fast ice (SOURCED, Lindblad/National Geographic *Endeavour* daily report, Weddell Sea, 30 Nov
  2005):** "Again and again Captain Lampe nosed the ship into fast ice until after lunch he found safe mooring.
  Able-bodied seamen lowered the gangway. The expedition staff then reconnoitered a safe route"; passengers
  spent "ninety minutes" on sea ice "no more than three feet thick"; at another site "the wind kicked up and
  forced us to abandon the landing."
- **Gangway, then crane cage when a lead opens (SOURCED, APECS MOSAiC Ambassador blog, R. McKay, "The bird
  cage", 10 Oct 2019):** "Usually, the gangway is pulled out so that expedition members can walk directly down
  to the ice"; when a lead opened between ship and floe "the gangway could no longer be safely used" and "the
  crane lifted people on and off the ice in the bird cage." People on the ice were covered by a bridge watch in
  two-hour shifts. Ship not named on the page; MOSAiC's ship was *Polarstern* (DERIVED from the program name).
  Arctic, not Antarctic.
- **A built-in covered gangway for ice (SOURCED, TRID abstract of *MacGregor-Navire News* 110, May 1987):** the
  *Akademik Fedorov* was given a MacGregor-Navire gangway crane on the forecastle: "In the extended position,
  its folding boom acts as a covered personnel gangway … the tip can be lowered to rest on ice." (Abstract
  only; whether it is still fitted or used: NOT FOUND.)
- **Davis over-ice changeover (SOURCED, AAD "This week at Davis", 24 Nov 2023):** *Nuyina* was in "thick pack
  ice" 3 km off on 10 November; "Using the surrounding fast ice as the wharf, the discharging of cargo and
  personnel began"; over 600 t of cargo went ashore; on day three "the sea ice at the back of the ship …
  [had] drifted off overnight" and the captain weighed leaving early until "the weather and ice conditions
  stabilized"; on 20 November "Both the incoming and outgoing teams walked from the station to meet Nuyina";
  71 expeditioners were split between ship and station. **UNVERIFIED (search-result text, Maritime Museum of
  Tasmania, *Maritime Times* 89):** the ship parked about 1 km out, "allowing … people to walk off the ship to
  the station."
- **Planned methods (SOURCED, AAD schedule, *Nuyina* 2026-27 V1):** Casey 27-29 Oct 2026 "Deploy cargo to Casey
  station via Helicopter"; Davis 4-13 Nov 2026 "Davis over ice resupply and personnel changeover. Deploy
  helicopters." **SOURCED (AAD news, 21 Sep 2016):** when sea ice retreated early (winter maximum "18.5 million
  square kilometers", record low), the fallback for the Davis over-ice resupply was "helicopter sling loads if
  the ice isn't solid enough."
- **A ship-to-ship passenger transfer in Antarctic pack ice (the *Akademik Shokalskiy* rescue, 2 Jan 2014):**
  - SOURCED (gCaptain, 2-3 Jan 2014): "Five flights were conducted to take the passengers from a makeshift
    helipad near the Shokalskiy"; *Aurora Australis* lay "about 14 nautical miles away"; "The helicopter landed
    on an ice floe adjacent to the Aurora Australis"; AMSA was told at "10:05 p.m. AEDT that all 52 passengers
    had been safely rescued"; the 22 crew stayed aboard; earlier icebreaker attempts failed because of "sea ice
    extent and blizzard conditions."
  - SOURCED (NBC News, 2 Jan 2014): the helicopter (from *Xue Long*) worked from a helipad passengers had
    "stomped out in the ice"; the plan was to fly to "an ice floe next to the Aurora Australis" and then be
    "taken by a small boat"; the rescue had been delayed several days by "high winds and fog"; ice was "up to
    12-feet thick."
  - UNVERIFIED (search-result text of AMSA updates and NBC): four flights of 12 and one of 4; the original plan
    was to fly to *Xue Long* first and move passengers by *Aurora Australis*'s barge, which "could carry up to
    22 people at a time"; helicopter operations ended about 22:45 AEDT. AMSA pages redirected to a login and
    were not read.

### 2.2 Weather and range limits actually published

- **The one complete published rule set found (SOURCED, AWI, *Polarstern*, 12 Oct 2022):**
  - "VFR (Visual Flight Rules) only." Day minima "600 ft" cloud base and "3 km" visibility. "There will be no
    flights under night conditions." "A visible horizon is mandatory for flying. Whiteout … flight condition is
    prohibited."
  - "Flights below -30°C are limited"; "Flights below -45°C are prohibited"; the battery must be warmed to at
    least −20 °C before start, so time on the ground away from the ship with engines off is limited.
  - Range: "the distance limit for the first helicopter is 80NM from the ship"; the second helicopter is the
    search-and-rescue aircraft and, if airborne, stays within "3NM from the ship with the ship in sight"; with
    only one helicopter serviceable, flights stay "within 10 NM"; in emergencies up to "150 NM" with refueling
    and a destination forecast, after a risk assessment.
  - Passengers: "limited to 5 passengers" so that a rescue with the other helicopter is always possible; "Only
    one team is allowed to be out by helicopter in a distance 3-80 NM and shuttling teams … is not allowed";
    "Rescue of all persons away from the vessel must always be possible with one single flight!"
  - Sling loads: from open water "the Helideck movement must be in the limitation"; the numbers are not given.
  - "The decision whether a flight can be conducted or not lies solely and entirely with the pilot."
- **Helideck motion limits (UNVERIFIED, search-result text of UK CAA CAP 437 material):** landing is permitted
  only when roll, pitch, inclination and heave rate are within limits, with "Motion Severity Index" and "Wind
  Severity Index" added for on-deck stability; the numeric limits were not found in the text read.
- **Treaty/IAATO environmental limits (SOURCED, IAATO 2002):** landing sites "no closer than 1,500 meters
  (5,000 ft) from concentrated wildlife"; no flight lower than 750 m laterally beside wildlife concentrations;
  landings on ice and snow; "No refueling operations will take place ashore at any time."
- **What stopped flights in the record (SOURCED unless marked):**
  - *Shokalskiy* rescue, Dec 2013-Jan 2014: delayed several days by "high winds and fog" (NBC). **UNVERIFIED
    (search-result text):** AMSA said the helicopter was "unable to fly in the current weather conditions";
    "rain and winds of 20 to 30 knots meant the helicopter could not fly"; the *Xue Long* helicopter could not
    land on *Aurora Australis* because of "load rating restrictions."
  - *Aurora Australis* at Syowa, 19 Jan 2009 (AAD sitrep 18163): after "23 flights to Syowa" the previous day,
    "only a small chance of flying today" because a cloud bank moved over (8/8 cloud, wind 6 knots).
  - Bunger Hills: the 2022 Polish party flew in "after several days of waiting for good weather"; in 1979 the
    exit flight went out in fog; in 1948 a Bell helicopter crashed in whiteout 7 miles from the ship (all in port
    file B).

### 3.3 Landing and passenger-number rules

- **ATCM Measure 15 (2009), adopted Baltimore 17 Apr 2009 (SOURCED, DFAT text, [2011] ATNIF 9):** operators
  are "to refrain from making any landings in Antarctica from vessels carrying more than 500 passengers"; for
  500 or fewer, "not more than one tourist vessel is at a landing site at any one time," "the number of
  passengers on shore at any one time to 100 or fewer," and "a 1:20 guide-to-passenger ratio." **Status:** "Not
  yet in force" in 2011 (SOURCED, DFAT); it enters into force only when all Consultative Parties approve
  (UNVERIFIED, search-result text); only Japan had approved by 2011 and Argentina approved on 12 Jul 2019
  (UNVERIFIED, search-result text of an Australian committee report and the Dutch treaty database). Its
  present status: NOT FOUND. The same limits are applied by IAATO's own "100 Passengers Ashore Bylaw", "20:1
  Guide / Passenger Ratio", "One ship at one site at one time" and "Cruise Only Bylaw" (SOURCED, IP-102 Fig.
  1.1); MercoPress 2026 says vessels of 201-500 passengers face stricter landing rules (SOURCED, rule not
  detailed).

### 3.4 Polar Code and evacuation rules for passenger ships

- **Polar Code chapter 8 (SOURCED, text as reproduced on the PAME Arctic Shipping Best Practice portal; paragraph
  numbers as given there):** "no lifeboat shall be of any type other than partially or totally enclosed type"
  (8.3.3.3.1); "for passenger ships, a proper sized immersion suit or a thermal protective aid shall be provided
  for each person on board" (8.3.3.1.1); group equipment and survival craft must give "effective protection
  against direct wind chill for all persons on board"; personal equipment must "prevent frostbite of all
  extremities"; where abandonment onto ice or land is possible, group survival equipment is carried and stowed
  for 110% of persons on board; "searchlights suitable for continuous use to facilitate identification of ice
  shall be provided for each lifeboat"; "ships shall have means to ensure safe evacuation of persons";
  "adequate emergency rations shall be provided, for the maximum expected time of rescue" (8.3.3.4). The
  chapter text quoted does not give a number of days; the "minimum of 5 days" appears in an ABS submission on
  the same page.
- **The five-day figure in IMO guidance (SOURCED, IMO MSC.1/Circ.1184, *Enhanced contingency planning guidance
  for passenger ships operating in areas remote from SAR facilities*, 31 May 2006):** remoteness is judged by
  "the total recovery capacity of SAR facilities available to reach the scene and recover those who have taken
  to survival craft within the five day 'time to recover' parameter," and survival "for a maximum of five days
  according to the 'time to recover' parameter"; measures to consider include "voyage 'pairing' … so that, if
  two or more passenger ships are operating in the same general area at the same time, each can be used as a
  SAR facility in case of accident to another," "enhanced life-saving appliances," and "additional life-saving
  resources." **UNVERIFIED (search-result text):** IMO Res. A.999(25), *Guidelines on voyage planning for
  passenger ships operating in remote areas*, applies to Arctic and Antarctic waters and asks for contingency
  plans "in the event of limited support being available."
- **SOLAS abandonment time (UNVERIFIED, secondary sources only):** survival craft on passenger ships must be
  launchable with their full complement within 30 minutes of the abandon-ship signal (SOLAS III/21.1.4, as
  cited); abandon-ship and fire drills weekly on passenger ships. The regulation text itself was not read.

## 4. Icebreaker escort and seasonal ice shipping

### 4.1 How escort and convoys work (a published procedure)

**SOURCED (Canadian Coast Guard, *Ice Navigation in Canadian Waters*, ch. 4, sec. 4.6 "Icebreakers",
amended 2025, 6th revision, 7th edition):**
- "For progress to be made, it is essential that escort operations be under the direction of the commanding
  officer" of the icebreaker; convoys are formed by the commanding officer after consulting the shore
  authority, and the order in convoy is "not necessarily on 'first come-first served' basis."
- Distance: "The minimum distance will be determined by the commanding officer of the icebreaker," based on the
  escorted ship's stopping distance; exceeding the maximum distance "creates the possibility of besetment";
  in convoy, ships keep "a suitable and safe distance" from each other. No number is given; it depends on ice.
- "While under escort, continuous and close communications must be maintained"; radio watch on 2182 kHz and
  VHF 16.
- The escorted ship must declare "any deficiencies or restrictions" beforehand and report at once if it stops
  or cannot keep up; if the icebreaker signals that it has stopped, the follower goes full astern with the rudder
  hard over.
- Risks named: besetment when the track closes behind the icebreaker, "especially at 9+/10 concentration under
  pressure"; collision "if the icebreaker suddenly slows"; overrunning the icebreaker.
- **UNVERIFIED (search-result text of research abstracts):** following distance is a trade-off; too far and
  the broken channel can close or refreeze, too close and collision risk rises (Chalmers 2024, IAHR 2026).
- **SOURCED (port file D, ATCM XLIV BP 22):** in Antarctica, convoy work has meant an
  icebreaker cutting a channel in fast ice for cargo ships and tankers: *Kapitan Khlebnikov* needed 27 days for
  fast ice "up to 90 km wide" to Progress in 2021 (port file D).

### 4.2 Baltic winter navigation (year-round shipping with assistance)

- **Season length and volume (SOURCED, Sixty Degrees North, 31 May 2024, summarizing the Finnish Transport
  Infrastructure Agency's season release):** the 2023-24 icebreaking season "started exceptionally early, on
  23.11.2023" and ran to 31 May 2024, **191 days**; in the Gulf of Finland, 4 January to 9 April, **97 days**;
  "a total of 2,234 vessels were assisted in the sea area, of which 428 involved towing," against "1,233
  assists, of which 77 were towing" in 2022-23; 1,142 icebreaker operating days against 684; ≈86,000 nmi
  steamed against 44,789; all of Finland's icebreakers took part (eight Arctia, two chartered tugs).
- **Ice that season (SOURCED, Finnish Meteorological Institute, "Ice season 2023/2024 was long"):** first ice
  at Tornio 22 Oct 2023; maximum ice area 135,000 km² on 12 Feb 2024; fast ice 40-80 cm in the Bay of Bothnia;
  "The first assistance restrictions came to force in Tornio, Kemi and Oulu on 22 November 2023"; "The last
  assistance restrictions were canceled from Kemi and Tornio on 30 May"; Baltic ice-free 4 June.
- **Mild-winter contrast (SOURCED, Arctia, 2024-25 season summary as reported):** the season ended 20 May 2025
  and "icebreaking assistance was not needed in the Gulf of Finland this season" (UNVERIFIED in detail;
  search-result text of the Arctia release). **UNVERIFIED (search-result text):** the agency says vessels are
  assisted "approximately 300-3,000 times per winter, depending on ice conditions."
- **Traffic restrictions (UNVERIFIED, search-result text of Finnish ice reports, Dec 2025 and Apr-May 2025):**
  ports set a minimum ice class and size for ships that will receive assistance, e.g. Tornio, Kemi and Oulu "II
  of more than 2000 tons" early in the season, tightened to "IA of more than 2000 tons" in hard ice, then relaxed

  ice-field" in April 2025. The Swedish Maritime Administration page with the normal start dates failed to open

- **Cost (SOURCED, Arctia "Icebreaking" page):** "Icebreaking services are free of charge to merchant vessels

## FINAL: what the sources support and do not support about a seasonal sea gateway on this coast

**Scope.** Drawn only from items 1-5 above and the "already known" files they point to. Facts only. **No design
and no recommendation; that is the developer's decision.**

### What the sources support

1. **Every passenger-transfer method has a published or practiced weather ceiling, and the ceilings are
   modest.** Small boats in Antarctic programs stop at about 25 knots mean wind (AAD, 10-minute average) and run
   only in daylight and "fine and clear weather"; Stanley Harbor closes tenders at sustained 30 knots and
   visibility under 0.1 nmi. Offshore motion-compensated gangways are rated to Hs 3-4.5 m and crane capsules to
   Hs 4.0 m, in open water, ship to platform. No published limit for transfer onto fast ice or a floe was found;
   it is a captain's and expedition staff's judgment (item 1).
2. **Ship-to-ice transfer is routine in practice, and its failure mode is the ice moving.** Gangways are lowered
   onto fast ice (Endeavour 2005; MOSAiC 2019), and a crane cage replaces the gangway when a lead opens; at Davis
   in 2023 people and cargo crossed fast ice ≈1 km and the ice behind the ship drifted off on day three (item 1).
3. **Ship-based helicopters are the standard way across ice that ships cannot cross, at real but limited rates.**
   Recorded rates: 23 flights to a station in one day; two helicopters each landing once an hour; five passenger
   flights of up to ≈12 people over ≈14 nmi in one evening. Seats are few (BK117: 6 passengers as configured; the
   *Polarstern* rule: 5 passengers so that a one-flight rescue stays possible); one published rule set limits
   the working helicopter to 80 nmi from the ship, daylight VFR only (600 ft / 3 km), no whiteout, limited
   below −30 °C and prohibited below −45 °C (item 2). On this coast ships have stood 50-100 km off the Bunger
   Hills and flown in (port file B).
4. **Antarctic passenger shipping is summer-only and small away from the Peninsula.** IAATO's season is October
   to April and 98% of voyages are in the Peninsula; the Ross Sea / Continental segment carried 1,251 passengers
   on 9 departures in 2023-24 (≈139 per departure, DERIVED). Hobart has a recurring East Antarctic tourist
   departure; Fremantle has none found (item 3).
5. **Passenger ships in polar water are planned around evacuation into a five-day wait.** IMO guidance uses a
   five-day "time to recover"; the Polar Code requires enclosed lifeboats, an immersion suit or thermal aid for
   every person on passenger ships, wind-chill protection, and rations for the maximum expected time of rescue;
   IMO suggests "pairing" passenger ships so each can rescue the other (item 3).
6. **Escorted ice shipping works where icebreakers are numerous and the route is short; long routes stay
   seasonal.** The Finnish Baltic runs year-round with free icebreaker assistance (2,234 assists in a 191-day
   2023-24 season); NSR transit ran ≈4 months in 2024 and ice ended it about a month early; McMurdo gets one
   escorted window a year. Escort is directed by the icebreaker's commander, spacing is set case by case, and
   besetment and collision are the named risks. A passenger expedition ship was beset near McMurdo in January
   2026 and freed by an icebreaker in six hours (item 4).
7. **One summer's ships stock a station for the year, and a missed ship is answered by air.** Station loads run
   from hundreds of tons (Mirny 2023: 65.9 t cargo, 34 t food, 350 t fuel in 10 days) to thousands (McMurdo,
   Progress; port file D). When ice blocked Mawson's resupply, RAAF C-17s with tanker support airdropped ≈9.5 t
   (2021) and ≈15 t (2024); a Davis drop onto sea ice needed ≥60 cm of ice for 9 t; when a ship could not reach
   Mirny in 2002, inland Vostok was mothballed the next winter (item 5 and the items3-8 file).

### Where the sources do not support a conclusion

- **Any precedent for a scheduled, seasonal passenger "gateway" that goes out to meet arriving ships in
  Antarctic pack ice.** None was found; transfers in ice in the record are expedition landings, station
  changeovers, and one rescue.
- **Transfer limits in ice.** No published wind, wave or ice-condition limit for gangway, crane-cage or
  helicopter transfer between ships in pack ice, or onto a floe, was found.
- **Throughput at scale.** The largest passenger movements in ice found are dozens per day (station
  changeovers, the 52-person rescue); nothing in the record moves hundreds or thousands of people through ice in
  a season.
- **Helideck motion limits and Antarctic ship-helicopter minima** beyond the one *Polarstern* document.
- **Convoy spacing** as a number; it is left to the icebreaker's commander.
- **Mirny's present annual tonnage and unloading method**, beyond January 2023.
