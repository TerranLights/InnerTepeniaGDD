### 3.1 · G2, physical and environmental constraint

**STRENGTH**

**S2C-4 · Most of what falls does not stay.** About 225 mm a year falls, about 86 mm lands (38.2%), and about 139 mm (61.8%) is "Lost to sublimation and wind transport"; "**KATABATIC MARGIN** — *snow flux, not snowfall*" (spec L103-106; 225.1 mm at L99). - →1 Accumulation runs at well under half of what falls.
- →2 Snow lost to "wind transport" is moved, not destroyed. The mechanism (clear-sky whiteout; drift from upwind) is admitted only at spec L109 part 2, which I know only through contract L174 (second-hand; REQ-32). The wind that sweeps open ground fills whatever makes a lee.
- →3 At Casey, what stands up decides where the snow ends up: every structure, vehicle and cutting is a snow trap. The clearing load therefore grows with what is built, not with the weather. A city that needs 155-221 km² (Extent L608) makes its own snow problem as it grows.
- *Both bodies:* clearing is moving work, which is the safer state for a robot (Frame L273), and exposure work for a human. The cost half is S2C-13.

**S2C-5 · The day never disappears; the night does.** "the sun rises every day of the year" (spec L112): about 2.5 hours at the solstice (L125), 2.7 h in June and 4.0 h in July (L137-138). The midnight sun runs "approximately December 11 → January 2 (~23 days)" (L113), because Casey sits in the asymmetric band where refraction pulls the two boundaries apart (L115-121).
- →1 No day of the year lacks a lit window for outdoor work and seeing.
- →2 Winter keeps a daily rhythm of short days. What goes missing is darkness, and only in summer.
- →3 Casey's timekeeping problem is never an absent day; it is always an absent night. Whatever has to be anchored to a daily stop is threatened in the light months, not the dark ones (S2C-9).
- *Both bodies:* both share the daily sunrise all year. Robots also keep a physiological night in every month, because they recharge overnight (Frame L276).

**S2C-6 · On the monthly averages, a working robot is never below her capacity line.** "Usable capacity falls to 50–60% by −20°C" (Frame L270). Casey's average lows run from −2.6 to −18.5 °C, above −20 in every month (spec L132-143). The −40 °C embrittlement line (Frame L271) has not been reached by the measured record (−37.5 °C in August, L139). Record lows below −20 occur in nine months, March to November (L134-142).
- →1 A robot moving outdoors is not capacity-limited on an ordinary day.
- →2 Her outdoor danger is a weather event (a record night, or the katabatic switch of L155), not the season.
- →3 Her nightly danger is rest, because recharging below freezing causes permanent loss (S2C-11). Casey therefore threatens a robot most when she stops, and a human most when she is outside. On Casey's own figures the two bodies' dangers fall at different times of day.
- *Humans:* average daily highs are below freezing in ten months (February −0.1 through November −2.5); only January (+2.2) and December (+1.4) are above (L132-143). "Cold is a matter of preparation, never a species gate" (Frame L265).

**S2C-7 · A sheltered anchorage and a navigable season (the founders' reach, counted here once).** "reasonably sheltered anchorage by Antarctic standards"; "a viable destination for incoming ships during the exile period" (spec L82). The sea is navigable "roughly November–March" (L154); January is "sea navigable" (L132) and December the "sea fully navigable season" (L143). Others routed ships to it in the exile period only (L82); nothing is assumed about later sea trade (PA-8, Frame L504).
- →1 Heavy arrival is possible for five months.
- →2 Every seaborne arrival comes in the light half of the year: the navigable months average 18.7 h of daylight (computed from L132-143).
- →3 A newcomer by sea meets Casey first in its open, light season, and meets the closed, dark season only by staying. The founders' ships came in season, with the first winter after (Frame L358).
- *Both bodies:* robots and humans arrive alike. A robot who came keeps firsthand memory of the arrival across both Acts (Frame L120).

**DEFICIT**

**S2C-8 · It cannot hold the sea open: about seven months ice-locked, and the light moves with the sea.** Sea ice forms in April (L135) and breaks up in October (L141); the sea is navigable roughly November to March, and "this directly determined Casey's supply windows" (L154). The characteristic failure (`02` L373) is that what the sea was supposed to prevent, being cut off, happens every year. The five open months average 18.7 h of daylight and the seven closed months 7.93 h (L132-143).
- →1 Isolation by sea and darkness arrive together.
- →2 Light returns long before the sea: 7.6 h in August, 11.3 in September and 14.9 in October, with the sea navigable from about November. Meanwhile the cold is a flat plateau from May to August (average lows −18.0 to −18.5; means −14.4 to −14.9; L136-139).
- →3 Casey's winter visibly ends before it actually ends. Its seams are April (ice forming, light failing, the last chance to leave by sea) and September to October (lit, warming, the ice breaking, still shut). - →2 The stop is either kept by something else or it lapses.
- →3 The months without a night are the months the sea is open (S2C-8). The season with the most arriving has no natural end to the working day. The characteristic failure, work running on through the night, has its best conditions exactly when ships can come (RF-23, Frame L463).
