## What Casey is made of

**P3A-2 · The ground: low rock, scoured bare, between bays; a city of this size spans water and ice.**
- *Canon and research.* The site is "an area of rock exposures and permanent snow and ice fields", with low rounded outcrops to about 40 m rising to moraines at about 130 m, and valleys filled with snow or ice, moraine and debris (S3-29, `03_Research` L280); an island-and-peninsula strip about 11.1 × 31.5 km; a full disc of 155-221 km² would take in bays, islands, peninsulas, moraine and ice (S3-29, `03_Research` L281). **The rock** (W1, snippet, from papers on the island group): the main country rock is a garnet-bearing granite gneiss; the older mapping describes "a layered sequence of schists, gneisses, and migmatites", intruded by a charnockite and a porphyritic granite.
- →1 Casey stands on old, layered crystalline rock in low knolls, not on soil or sediment: whatever is not ice is hard and bare.
- →2 Building on it means building on bedrock where the knolls are and on ice and snow-filled valleys where they are not, ice being buildable (Frame L95, citing Extent L530-532): the city's ground is two materials that behave differently under load and under thaw. Which parts of Casey stand on which is not in the material (RF-20; no layout, REQ-6) and is **Patterned, deferred** (`DR-4`).
- →3 Because a 155-221 km² extent at this site is archipelagic (S3-29 →1), the ground under Casey includes water that is open in one half of the year and frozen in the other (P3A-24). *A physical constraint on built form, not a district* (RF-9).
- *Divergence from source:* present-day geology and landforms; uplift at the site is an open thread at Step 3 (S3-29), and nothing here uses it.

**P3A-3 · The ice and the snow: the inland horizon is an ice dome; the snow on open ground goes past.**
- *Canon and research.* Casey is in the katabatic-margin regime: "**Snow flux, not snowfall.**" (precipitation L87). Cumulative snow transport "can be approximately 4 orders of magnitude higher than snow precipitation at coastal sites" (precipitation L56-57), so at a coastal katabatic city the snow moving horizontally past a person is on the order of ten thousand times the snow falling from the sky (precipitation L59-60). Casey's row: falls 225, lands 86, lost 140 mm (precipitation L158; the spec rounds the loss to ~139, spec L106). The snow that never lands "is overwhelmingly snow that already landed once and was picked back up" (precipitation L50). On the site: "the extremely strong winds scour the snow off exposed areas of the Peninsula", and snow gathers "in the lee of rock outcrops and in depressions in the substratum" (S3-20, `03_Research` L200). Inland: a large ice dome rises to about 1,395 m south of the coast, and the island coast lies with that dome's icecap to its east (W8, snippet, encyclopedia summaries). In one recorded year snow fell on 175 days, 48% of the year (W13, sourced; one year only).
- →1 Snow at Casey is frequent and thin: it fell on about half the days of the year it was counted in, and less than two fifths of what falls stays where it fell.
- →2 What Casey is visibly made of in snow is not a blanket but a pattern: bare crests and knolls, filled hollows and lees, drifts against anything standing (S3-20).
- →3 The snow a person sees at Casey is mostly snow in motion or snow parked in the wind's shadow; the landscape records the wind's direction more than the season's snowfall (an inference from the cited sources, labeled).
- *Figures, parked as the rows state them* (runbook L2695): falls ~225 / lands ~86 / lost ~139-140 mm (spec L104-106; precipitation L158). **Downstream uses:** P3A-3, P3A-8, P3A-11, P3A-15, P3A-24, P3A-28.

**P3A-4 · Living ground: moss, alga, birds and seals belong to this coast in the present-day record.** [present-day; c; P3A-DQ-2]
- *Research.* The peninsula on the site holds "some of the most extensive and best-developed plant communities in continental Antarctica", with closed moss turf; the islands are dominated by the green alga *Prasiola crispa*, with moss and lichen poorer there (W2, snippet). *P. crispa* grows mainly beside penguin colonies, on guano-rich ground, and penguin-derived ammonium moves in meltwater at the island group (W18, snippet); moss beds there grow on abandoned penguin colonies (W21, snippet). Present-day Adélie penguins in the island group number almost 200,000 breeding pairs, and one island of less than a square kilometre holds nearly 40,000 breeding penguins at peak (W14, sourced); snow petrels breed on the islands (W3, snippet). Southern elephant seal haulout sites and two Weddell seal pupping areas are mapped in the island group (W15, sourced); elephant seals come ashore to moult about January to April (W12, snippet).
- →1 The rock between the bays is not lifeless in the open half: green moss and alga, colonies of birds, seals hauled out.
- →2 A Band 5 city of 155-221 km² on a strip of about 350 km² of envelope (S3-29) would sit among these colonies and moss beds, not beside them.
- →3 What the city does to them, and they to its senses, is not in the material; the senses they supply are seasonal and biological, and they are the only outdoor smells at Casey (P3A-13). *Whether present-day ecology may stand for the site in 2564-2812 is P3A-DQ-2; until it is answered, every finding resting on it carries [present-day; c].*

**P3A-5 · The light: a sun that rises every day and, in summer, a night that never comes.**

## B · Sound

**P3A-11 · Humans: the wind is the voice of the place, and footsteps tell the season.**
- *Canon and research.* The wind (P3A-6). Snow underfoot: below about −10 °C snow squeaks, because boot pressure no longer melts the grains; warmer it is quieter, and near zero it "becomes a squelch" (W5, snippet of a university explainer and press pieces). At Casey the average low is below −10 °C from April to October and the average high from May to August; only December and January have an average high above freezing (spec L132-143; computed). Ice contracting under a fast temperature drop builds stress until it cracks, with booming and cracking sounds (W16, snippet; lake ice and sea ice).
- →1 *General population, outdoors:* a background of wind on about half the days of the counted year, gale on about a third (W13), with snow hissing past at ground level when it moves (P3A-3; an inference from snow in motion).
- →2 From May to August, on the averages, snow squeaks underfoot all day; in April, September and October at night and in the cold hours; in December and January it is wet and quiet. **At Casey a footstep is a thermometer:** the sound of walking says which half of the year it is (computed from spec L132-143 against W5's threshold; the threshold is a popular-science value).
- →3 The shut-half soundscape is dry and hard: wind, squeak, and cracking from the frozen bays when the temperature drops fast (an inference from W16 and from fast ice that forms on the near-coastal ocean each autumn, S3-1). The open-half one is wet and soft: squelch, meltwater in the valley catchments (S3-31) and the colonies [present-day; c]. The year changes key by sound before it changes by sight. *Indoors* nothing is written; the heated interior is where the wind is heard through walls (an inference only).
- *Figures parked as the rows state them:* the 2001 frequencies (W13) and spec L98's 7-10 m/s. **Downstream uses:** P3A-6, P3A-11, P3A-15, P3A-18, P3A-23, P3A-HF-10.

**P3A-12 · Robots: the same sounds, and possibly more of them; what lies outside human hearing at this site.** (c) [P3A-DQ-1]
- *Canon.* "robots perceive sound and vibration across a meaningfully wider range than humans do, structurally rather than through any add-on device" (physiology L187). The canon does not say in which direction the range is wider.
- *Research on what lies outside human hearing here.* Low: Antarctic infrasound arrays regularly record microbaroms from peri-Antarctic ocean swell at about a 5-second period, and winter impulsive signals of a few hertz attributed to sea-ice and glacier dynamics (W11, snippet, conference abstracts); thermal cracking in sea ice sends acoustic energy into the water and seismic waves through the ice (W16, snippet). High: Weddell seal trills often start above 20 kHz and drop to about 80 Hz and can carry more than 25 km under sea ice; ultrasonic calls reach 50 kHz and dominated in winter darkness in one study (W10, snippet); Weddell seal pupping areas are mapped in the island group (W15, sourced) [present-day; c]. These sounds travel in water and ice; whether they reach a listener in air at the shore is in no source read.
- →1 A robot at Casey hears everything a human hears: the wind, the squeak, the ice.
- →2 If her range extends low, she also perceives, as vibration, the swell of an ocean she cannot see behind the winter pack and the cracking of the ice in the bays; if it extends high, the parts of the wind and ice sounds above human hearing. Which, the canon does not say.
- →3 *For the majority body the shut half may not be the quiet season it is for humans* (c): the ice and the distant swell keep sounding through it. Conditional on P3A-DQ-1; nothing is asserted about seal calls in air.
- *Both bodies:* robots wear the full cold-weather garment register (physiology L47-51), so the squeak underfoot is the same for both.

## B · Smell

**P3A-13 · Humans: the cold air smells of little; the outdoor smells are living and seasonal, and they come from the sea side.** [present-day; c]
- *Research.* Low temperature slows the evaporation of volatile compounds and dry air carries odors less, so cold air smells of little; the felt "smell" of cold is partly a nerve's response to temperature (W7, snippet, popular science; the evaporation explanation is the most consistent across sources). Ammonia from an Adélie colony of about 60,000 rose more than 1,000 times above its baseline 8 km downwind when the wind blew from the colony, and is described as a pungent, fishy smell downwind of seabird colonies (W17, snippet; a colony on another coast). Elephant seal wallows have "a very strong odor made up of seal musk and feces" (W12, snippet, a fisheries agency), and the moult ashore runs about January to April (W12). The island group holds almost 200,000 breeding pairs of Adélie penguins (W14) and mapped elephant seal haulouts (W15). The gale wind comes "predominantly from the east, off the polar ice cap" (S3-18).
- →1 *General population, outdoors:* through the shut half, when the monthly means are below −10 °C (spec L135-141), Casey's outdoor air smells of almost nothing but cold.
- →2 In the open half, when the wind comes from the islands and the sea, it can carry the colonies' ammonia and the seals' musk; when it comes off the ice cap, it carries nothing (an inference from W17 and S3-18).
- →3 *A hypothesis (h):* at Casey a smell is a wind direction. The wind off the ice is odorless and the wind from the sea is not; and since a strong event at the site needs "the approach of a strong cyclonic system toward the coast" (S3-18), a summer smell from the sea side may arrive on the same air that comes before a blow (P3A-26). Indoors, heated air holds whatever the people inside produce; nothing more is written (food and drink are CST-stage, RF-6).

**P3A-14 · Robots: a chemical register exists in canon; a sense of smell at Casey is not written.** (a null, explained)
- *Canon.* Robots have "internal atmospheric management systems — not for respiration, but for **thermal regulation and sensory input processing**" that "move air or vapor-equivalent substances through internal chambers as part of managing heat and environmental awareness" (physiology L297); vapor compounds "interact with internal environmental sensors in ways that produce detectable signal changes" (physiology L300). No line says whether that register picks up ambient odors such as ammonia. The cold file leaves open whether coolant thickens and whether the normal cooling system becomes a liability in deep cold (cold L139-140).
- →1 A robot at Casey draws air below freezing, in every month on the averages, through chambers whose job is partly heat management; what that intake does to her heat budget and to her chemical register is not written.
- →2 *Robot smell at Casey is recorded as not in canon,* not as absent: the register exists, and its reach to a place's air is unwritten. The null says why (spine L148-152).
- →3 No finding rests on it; P3A-HF-8 hands it on.

## B · Feel

**P3A-15 · Humans: cold every month, wind on half the days, and ground that changes its grip with the season.**
- *Canon and research.* Every month's average low is below freezing (spec L132-143); only December and January have average highs above freezing (spec L132, L143); the glove trade-off between warmth and dexterity applies to any human-shaped hand (physiology L53-57); rain is on the site's record in summer and in July (S3-19); in the thaw weeks the low-sun hours are the firm hours (S3-15); exposed rock is scoured bare, and snow lies in lees and hollows (S3-20).
- →1 Underfoot, Casey is bare rock on the knolls and packed or drifted snow in the hollows, all year (S3-20).
- →2 In December and January surfaces are soft by day and refrozen at night (S3-15); after any rain on frozen ground there is glaze (S3-19 →2), in summer or in the dark months.
- →3 A human at Casey feels the year in her footing as much as on her skin: firm, dry, squeaking winter ground (P3A-11) and soft, wet, slippery summer ground. No human cold threshold is in the material (S2-69 (f)), so no figure is given for exposure.

**P3A-16 · Robots: what the place does to the body is a squeeze on capacity outdoors and a hard line at rest.**
- *Canon.* Ordinarily clothed, in coats and gloves; cold is a preparation problem, not a gate (physiology L38-57). Whether robots feel cold as discomfort is open: "whether robots feel cold as discomfort or only register it instrumentally" (physiology L59). What cold costs (cold L59-83): a reversible capacity reduction, "**−20–30% near freezing** · **down to 50–60% at −20 °C**" (cold L63); output sag under load (a cold robot cannot lift, sprint or strike as hard as a warm one at identical charge, cold L72); embrittlement below the ductile-to-brittle transition (the file's example: a knock that dents at −10 °C cracks at −50 °C, cold L81); lubricants failing below about −40 °C (cold L82); recharging below freezing causes permanent plating loss, and safe cold charging runs at 5-10% of capacity (cold L23-26). "A robot is safer moving than resting" (Frame L273; cold L87). Realistic skin sits at the hands, face and ears (physiology L35-36).
- *Casey's figures against them (computed from spec L132-143):* the December and January means are −1.2 and −0.3 °C, the near-freezing band; winter average lows are −18.0 to −18.5 °C, just above the −20 °C line; the measured record low is −37.5 °C (spec L139), above the −40 °C lubricant line.
- →1 Outdoors, a robot at Casey always works on a smaller tank than indoors: a modest squeeze in summer, approaching half on winter nights, returned when she warms (reversible, cold L61). Her outdoor strength sags under load in the same way.
- →2 What Casey does *not* do to her, on the measured record, is make her brittle or seize her joints: those lines lie below anything the site has recorded. The cold that matters here is the cold of rest, not of work.
- →3 So the robot's feel of Casey is a gradient outdoors and a line at the door: she can work outside in any month, dressed for it, with less in reserve and less strength; she cannot rest outside in any month without paying permanently (Frame L272). *Whether that registers as discomfort is open (physiology L59), and nothing here assumes it.* Her hands, where her skin is, meet the glove trade-off in every outdoor month.

## E · Seasonal variance

## F · Retroactive Mechanism

**P3A-25 · What is simply true here that nothing explains:** "a mild coast-facing day could be followed immediately by a katabatic event off the plateau" (spec L155). **P3A-26 · The physical system that would produce exactly that: the mild day and the blow are often one weather system, its front side and its back side.** [snippet; low until read at the primary]
- *Research.* At the site, the strong cross-slope flow comes with "the approach of a strong cyclonic system toward the coast and a blocking system downstream", and strong katabatic flow needs a supply of cold air inland (S3-18, `03_Research` L183, a model study). Across coastal Antarctica, extreme wind events are associated with "a synoptic‐scale transient dipole of pressure anomalies driving warm‐air advection", and katabatic forcing plays "a passive role" (W4, snippet of a 2024 study of coastal extremes; its record page died at the source, W20). Rain is on the site's record in July (S3-19), which also needs warm air from the sea.
- →1 The approaching cyclone first brings warm, moist sea air over the coast (the mild day, sometimes wet), and its pressure pattern then drives the strong wind and the blowing snow.
- →2 **The second-order consequence, for free:** at Casey a mild day does not merely fail to predict (S2-12); it is often the first half of the storm. **Mildness is the warning, not the respite.**
- →3 The felt season against the threshold (S2-67; S2-21 (b)) sharpens: the day a robot is likeliest to think that resting unheated is safe is the day before she most needs to be moving or indoors. For humans, a warm, wet day that ends in a blow leaves glaze (S3-19 →2). *A shadow candidate only, for Step 7 after REQ-34 (P3A-HF-9); never derived here.*
- *Divergence from source:* present-day meteorology and a model study; the 2024 abstract is snippet-level; nothing here dates or edits spec L155.
