### RT-9 with X-4 · The gulf airports' melt season (first, `DEC-586`)
**Asked:** the months in which lake- or sea-ice runways stop carrying wheeled aircraft, and what is known of Transcription Gulf's own surface. **Attaches to:** G2-C4, S2-21, S2-54, RF-34, RF-41, RF-57, Step 1 AS-5, DQ-23, REQ-32.
**Facts:**
- Wilkins aerodrome (glacier ice, 700 m asl; comparable): operates October–March; "closes for around six weeks at the height of summer ... due to warmer temperatures causing sub-surface melt" (AAD, Wilkins aerodrome page, https://www.antarctica.gov.au/antarctic-operations/stations/other-locations/wilkins-aerodrome/, #6); in 2019–20 it closed a week early and stayed shut ~10 weeks; flights resume "after ice temperatures dropped below the threshold temperature of minus 5 degrees Celsius" (AAD news "Ice runway reopens", https://www.antarctica.gov.au/news/2019/ice-runway-reopens/, #16). → **finding S3C-201; correction S3C-C-204.**
- Sea-ice runway practice (McMurdo, comparable): built in October, used "until early December, when the sea ice begins to soften and melt"; in 2012–13 the airfield was closed to wheeled aircraft from late December to 11 February (Antarctic Sun, https://antarcticsun.usap.gov/features/2816, #10, #139). → S3C-201.
- Sub-surface melt under blue ice "begins mid-December at depths of 40 cm or more and continues until late January", the cover thinning to 7 cm, a serious hazard; a chipped-ice layer prevented it (Paige 1968, J. Glaciology, https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A1729DF16653FFFDDBC82528B96C7A4D/S0022143000020700a.pdf/sub-surface-melt-pools-in-the-mcmurdo-ice-shelf-antarctica.pdf, #17); dust holes refreeze thinly and collapse under weight (Troll, Norwegian SciTech News 2018, https://norwegianscitechnews.com/2018/04/hole-ice-big-problem/, #11, #17). → S3C-201, S3C-202.
- The gulf itself: a 2019 GPR study chose "the central part of the epishelf Transcription Gulf, about 7 km from the base" for a runway "on the sea ice" (Sukhanova, Popov, Polyakov, Kashkevich, Martyanov 2019, *Arctic and Antarctic Research* 65(3):315–327, record https://openpolar.no/Record/ftjaaresearch:oai:oai.aari.elpub.ru:article%2F195, #1, #14; full text not reached, #4 and #7 tool deaths); the gulf is "a largely fresh-water, tidal epishelf lake connected to the ocean under the Edisto Ice Tongue" (Algae River paper, Polar Record, #5); epishelf lakes of the southern oasis carried ice 1.8–3.9 m (Gibson & Andersen 2002, #121). → corroborates `Airports.md` L21 (2.95–3.20 m); S3C-203.
- Light aircraft at this site are documented in early summer: a Basler 1.5 h from the Australian coast; a deployment mid-November to mid-December; a 2010 landing struck sastrugi (AAD intracontinental operations, https://www.antarctica.gov.au/living-and-working/travel-and-logistics/aviation/intracontinental-operations, #138). → S3C-201 (the documented window is November–December; the hazard named is a cold-season snow surface).
- Lake ice in the oasis: most central lakes become ice-free in summer; edge lakes in glacier contact keep cover; the site is marginal for thick lake ice (Doran et al. 1996, *Antarctic Science* 8(3):289–296, https://repository.lsu.edu/geo_pubs/692, #12, #13). → corroborates spec L205–207.
- Lake-ice runways in temperate lakes close by judgment, not a fixed temperature; snow insulates and slushes (Alton Bay, NHPR, https://www.nhpr.org/post/warm-weather-keeps-alton-bay-s-ice-runway-closed-season, #9). → ornament.

**S3C-201 (PROPOSED) · The comparables put the gulf's wheeled-aircraft gap at the height of summer, mid-December to late January, longer in a warm summer.** Every real ice runway found that carries wheeled aircraft through a summer closes in its warm core: glacier ice at 700 m for about six weeks (ten in a warm season), sea ice from early December, blue ice under sub-surface melt mid-December to late January; a runway reopens when its *ice* is back below about −5 °C. This place's own constructed air means for December (−0.0 °C), January (+0.6 °C) and February (−2.1 °C) (spec L179–180, L190) are warmer than the 700 m comparable's site, and its ice is sea-connected epishelf ice at sea level. **Months, from sources, as comparables: the gap would begin in December and end in late January at the earliest, possibly mid-February; the November–early December and late February–March shoulders are the documented flying windows of comparable surfaces.** No closure is written (`DEC-586`): the months go to the developer as S3C-DQ-202. *Rests on comparables and constructed means; not a ruling.*
**S3C-202 (PROPOSED) · What fails at the melt is the surface's inside, not its top.** The documented mechanism is sub-surface melt under a still-solid skin (Paige 1968) and collapsing refrozen holes (Troll), detected by measuring ice temperature, not by air temperature. For this place: any summer judgment about the gulf runways would be made by someone measuring the ice, which is a working role the air door implies (who is not written; `DR-137` item 11; spine REQ-48).
**S3C-203 (PROPOSED) · The gulf is sea-connected ice, and its sources call it sea ice.** The only runway study of the gulf calls its surface sea ice on an epishelf lake joined to the ocean under the Edisto Ice Tongue. Spec L13 ("a tidal epishelf lake connected to the ocean beneath the ice") stands; the refinement names which ice. Tidal working at the shore (Step 1 S1-62's tidal cracks) is the expected companion of that connection.

### X-5 · Animals at the height (`DEC-593`; with RT-13)
**Asked:** whether and how animals could be kept here. **Attaches to:** S2-27, DQ-30, REQ-50, RT-13; `12` L11–37.
**Facts:**
- Fully housed husbandry at high latitude is real: a farm in a Svalbard settlement kept poultry, cattle and pigs (tens each) in 1988; now only pigs remain (Agriculture in Svalbard, https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Agriculture_in_Svalbard, #23); Yakutia stables cattle ~9 months a year, winter diets short of protein, minerals and carotene (Biotech Asia 2015, https://www.biotech-asia.org/vol12no2/the-use-of-feed-additives-in-the-diet-of-cows-and-young-cattle-in-yakutia/, #25); Icelandic dairy cows housed ~8 months with ≥8 weeks' summer grazing required; Norway 2–4 months' grazing on indoor silage and concentrate (#148).
- Animals are partly their own heaters: animal heat ~25–40% of feed energy; the young need supplemental heat; ventilation cannot be sealed (Purdue AE-96, https://www.extension.purdue.edu/extmedia/AE/AE-96.html, #26).
- Tank fish: Arctic char tolerates high-density recirculating culture; risks are energy price, cold-chain, feed supply (#27). Insects on waste: black soldier fly larvae partly replace soymeal for laying hens (#103).
- A herd can be flown: 165 cows airlifted on 12 July 2017, ~4,000 claimed by air, more by sea (Baladna, https://baladna.com/qatari-company-flies-in-cows-to-meet-countrys-dairy-needs/, #101) → corroborates pick L157.
- Westland's made ground took ditch silt, pig manure and night soil, but its 1948 survey found drainage mattered more than manure (#102) → RT-4.
- The oasis's own animals: four bird species; ~1,000 breeding pairs of snow petrels in colonies of ≤50 pairs in bedrock crevices at the hill margins; south polar skuas nest on gravel and prey on them; Wilson's storm petrels rare; Adélie penguins only rarely (*Antarctic Science* 2020, "Spatial distribution of birds and terrestrial plants in Bunger Hills", https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antarctic-science/article/spatial-distribution-of-birds-and-terrestrial-plants-in-bunger-hills/78E336B6091E04D50FC4CDCE333D08F6, #140).
**S3C-204 (PROPOSED) · Keeping animals here is a housing question, and the real cases answer it with the smallest animals.** No pasture exists (spec L109); every real high-latitude case keeps animals indoors most of the year on bought or grown feed, and the one fully indoor polar settlement case shrank from poultry, cattle and pigs to pigs. Canon's own model already makes the species a feed-stream choice (`12` L29–31). The options for the developer are in S3C-DQ-203. Nothing here says animals are kept at the height.
**S3C-205 (PROPOSED) · The oasis already has animals, and they live where a settlement would build: at the rock margins.** Snow petrels nest in the crevices of the bedrock outcrops around the margins of the hills and the jointed cliffs; skuas nest on the gravels. A settled oasis meets them at its edges. *A physical fact of the coordinate; no culture is read from it.*
**Omitted:** Madrid Protocol Annex II on introduced animals (#24): real-world law, not this world's. **Open threads:** S3C-OT-206 (feed: what housed animals here would eat, against `12`'s feed streams).
