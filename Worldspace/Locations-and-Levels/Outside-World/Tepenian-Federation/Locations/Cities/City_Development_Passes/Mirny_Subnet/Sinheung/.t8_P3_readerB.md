### XB-102 · What the station's plant held (brief Q1), and whether the city took any of it up

  - **Buildings.** A three-storey office and living building for 50 people (25 in winter). It holds five laboratories: meteorological, "'wet' and dry oceanographic", satellite imagery, geophysical and hydrobiological. It also holds a radio hub, a medical unit, a galley and a gym. The 2014 management plan says the site is "suited to accommodating up to 100 personnel during summer". The 2017 facilities catalogue gives 50 beds and 1,500 m² under roof. The medical unit is 40 m², with capability "Basic, Dental, Surgery" and equipment for X-ray, ultrasound, endoscopy and telemedicine.
  - **Monitoring plant.** A unit that monitors satellite-navigation orbits and does "geodetic monitoring of the tectonic Earth's crust movements". A geomagnetic pavilion. A radar "for monitoring the state of coastal ice and icebergs and for air traffic control of helicopters and low-flying airplanes".
  - **Power, fuel, water and waste.** A 900 kW diesel power station. A garage for repair and maintenance of up to eight transporters, and repair shops. A boiler that burns waste lubricant. Fifteen 75 m³ double-walled fuel tanks and a fuel pipeline. Drinking water is drawn from a small lake in summer and another lake in winter. A reverse-osmosis unit desalinates brackish lake water. There is an incinerator and a biological sewage unit.
  - **Ground transport.** About 15 km of unsealed roads "formed from local material", including a 6.7 km road to the plateau. The site is "the major transportation base for supporting inland traverses". In 2022/23 that meant 45 over-snow vehicles and 90 drivers on a 1,480 km route, with 2,486 t of cargo delivered in the season.
  - **Air.** Two helipads, one lit and equipped with navigation support. A snow-and-ice runway of 3,000 m by 100 m took its first heavy wheeled cargo aircraft in November 2022. The 2017 catalogue lists a 1,500 m by 60 m strip, and a 2,000 m skiway was flown in 1986-89.
  - **Getting cargo ashore past the unusable shore.** The plan says: "Access from ships to the eastern shore of Broknes by small boat is difficult and sometimes impossible due to ice debris up to hundreds of metres off shore, blown by the prevailing north-easterly winds. Helicopters are therefore the only reliable means by which persons and supplies can be transported ashore quickly." Heavy cargo crosses the fast ice to a landing on the western peninsula (Stornes), then goes over-snow to the plateau and the station.
  - **Records and research.** A continuous synoptic weather record (index 89574) begins in April 1988, the month the station opened. Its GHCN-Daily series has no records for 1993-2002, matching a closure from 1993/94 to 1997/98. The plan's summary: "Progress is primarily intended as a support base for inland geological and glaciological operations. Meteorological, hydrological, geomagnetic observations and sea ice monitoring are also undertaken." The operator's own page adds summer work in biology, hydrobiology, limnology, glaciology and geomorphology, plus year-round oceanology, hydrology, meteorology and ionospheric observation.
