## B · Sound

**P3C-5 · The city's ambient sound is the wind, on about half its days.** *Present-day reference figures, parked "as the row states it".*
- *Sources.* In 2001 the site recorded wind above 22 knots on 178 days (49%), above 34 knots on 127 days (35%), blizzards on 61 days (17%), with a blizzard "a period of more than one hour when snow reduces visibility below 100m", below freezing and above 33 knots; the highest daily gust that year was "Easterly, 195km/h (105 knots) on 11 September" (**sourced**, web call 17). Snippets: "June, July and August are our windiest months and we can expect around one blizzard every five days during this period" (web call 12). The site's mean is about 25 km/h (S3-18). The katabatic season peaks in July (spec L138).
- *Parked figures and their downstream uses:* the 2001 counts (one year, present-day) are used at P3C-5, P3C-13, P3C-25 and P3C-29; a correction to them reaches those four entries.
- →1 On roughly every second day the wind is loud enough to be the sound of the place (above 22 knots); on about one day in six visibility falls below 100 m in blowing snow.
- →2 Speech outdoors on those days is short-range, and the wind's direction carries meaning: the gales come from the east off the ice cap (S3-18), so a rising easterly is heard before it is understood.
- →3 Casey's outdoor sound has two states, not a gradient: the blow and the calm (P3C-8). *Humans* hear the wind and feel it on the face; *robots* perceive "sound and vibration across a meaningfully wider range than humans do" (physiology L187), so a robot hears and feels more of the blow, in structures as well as in air (an inference from the canon line; its range is not specified). *Expression:* a building's hum in a gale is heard by everyone, felt by a robot in the frame of the wall.

**P3C-6 · The shut half squeaks underfoot, and exactly the shut half.** *The strongest finding of this phase.*
- *Sources.* Snow squeaks when the air is "below 14 degrees Fahrenheit" (about −10 °C), because pressure no longer melts a film between grains and "the ice crystals rub against each other, making a squeaking sound" (**sourced**, Montana State University acoustic atlas, web call 8; the same threshold in several snippets, web call 2). The threshold is approximate and is for air temperature, not snow temperature.
- *Casey's own figures* (spec L132-143): average lows below −10 °C in **exactly April to October** (−14.6, −18.5, −18.3, −18.4, −18.0, −17.1, −15.1 °C), and above it from November to March (−9.0, −3.7, −2.6, −5.0, −9.8 °C). Average highs are at or below −10 °C from May to August (−11.1, −10.4, −10.2, −10.2 °C) and above it in April, September and October (−7.5, −9.7, −8.0 °C).
- →1 On the averages, snow at Casey squeaks every night from April to October and through most of the day from May to August; in December and January (highs +1.4 and +2.2 °C) it never does.
- →2 The squeak months are the ice-locked months (spec L135-141, L154): the sound underfoot changes when the sea closes and changes back when it opens, on the averages. ## B · Smell

**P3C-10 · For seven months the outdoors smells of almost nothing; the city's smells live indoors.**
- *Sources.* Cold slows the evaporation of volatile compounds, so fewer odor molecules reach the air, and noses are less sensitive in cold air; part of the "smell of cold" is trigeminal sensation, not odor (**snippet**, web call 14, attributed to a chemical-senses researcher).
- *Casey's own figures:* every month's mean is below zero (spec L132-143), from −0.3 °C in January to −14.9 °C in August.
- →1 Outdoors, Casey's own smells (its buildings, works, vehicles and people) are suppressed all year, most of all from April to October.
- →2 *Inference:* in the shut half, smell is an indoor sense; heated air releases what the cold held (P3C-21).
- →3 Crossing into heat is crossing into smell. *Humans:* the cold registers on the nose as sting, not scent (trigeminal). *Robots:* see P3C-12. What the indoors smells of is everyday life, CST material (RF-6), and is not written here.

**P3C-11 · Summer smells of the sea birds, and the smell season is not the sea season.** (c)
- *Sources.* The breeding population of Adélie penguins in the Windmill Islands is "almost 200,000 breeding pairs"; one island under a square kilometer holds "nearly 40,000 breeding penguins" at the peak, at a density "higher than the human density of Manila" (**sourced**, web call 5). Breeding sites at 14 islands or sites in a 2011 survey (**snippet**, web call 1). Arrival is about late September, breeding runs October to February, and chicks leave in early to mid-February (**snippet**, web call 9). Downwind of a colony of about 60,000 Adélie penguins elsewhere, ammonia rose to 13.5 ppb, more than 1,000 times the baseline, about 8 km away when the wind came from the colony; after the birds left at the end of February, ammonia stayed about 100 times the baseline from the guano (**snippet**, popular coverage of a 2025 study, web call 19).
- →1 From about October to March the islands around the city hold a very large bird population, and a colony's ammonia carries kilometers downwind.
- →2 *Inference (c):* when the wind blows from the colonies toward the city, the city smells of them; the gales come from the east off the ice cap (S3-18), so the colony air arrives, if at all, with the sea-side winds, the "coastal westerly storm systems" (spec L98). Colony positions relative to the built area are not known (RF-20), so this is a mechanism, not a map.
- →3 The birds arrive about late September or October, while the sea is still shut and the shore ice still unbroken (S3-1: breakout between early October and 19 November): the smell of summer, like the light (S2-26), arrives before the sea opens; and it lingers after the birds leave, into March. **The nose and the calendar disagree in both seams.** *Expression:* the first sea-bird smell on a westerly in October, weeks before the first ship. *Humans* smell it; *robots*, see P3C-12. **Originated** in the sense of this place's physics; nothing is written about how anyone regards it. *Elephant seal wallows* are present (P3C-7) and are not given a smell here: not researched.

**P3C-12 · Whether a robot smells at all is written nowhere; canon gives her a chemical channel, not a nose.**
- *Canon.* Robots have "no respiratory system" (physiology L9); they do have "internal atmospheric management systems — not for respiration, but for **thermal regulation and sensory input processing**", which move air "as part of managing heat and environmental awareness", and vapor compounds "interact with internal environmental sensors in ways that produce detectable signal changes" (physiology L297-300).
- →1 A robot draws air for heat and sensing, and chemicals in it can register.
- →2 Whether ambient odors, such as colony ammonia, register as smell is not in canon (P3C-DQ-1 (b)).
- →3 At Casey, where outdoor smell is suppressed for seven months and arrives with the birds, this open question decides whether the robot majority shares the city's one strong seasonal smell. **Not assumed.** Written as open.
