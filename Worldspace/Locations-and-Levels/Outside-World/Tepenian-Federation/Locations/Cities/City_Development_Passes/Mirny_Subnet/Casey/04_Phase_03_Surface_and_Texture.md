## What Casey is made of

**P3-2 · The ground: low, old crystalline rock in rounded knolls, snow-filled hollows, bays and islands.** [present-day; physical] (P3A-2; P3B-2; P3C-2)
- *Canon and research.* The site is an area of rock exposures and permanent snow and ice fields; low rounded outcrops to about 40 m rise to moraines at about 130 m, with valleys filled with permanent snow or ice, moraine and debris (S3-29, `03_Research` L280); the island-and-peninsula strip is about 11.1 × 31.5 km, an envelope of about 350 km² with water included (S3-29) (A, B, C). **The rock** (snippet; P3A-W1, P3B-W1, P3C-W3): the island group's main country rock is a garnet-bearing granite gneiss, intruded by a charnockite and a granite; an older mapping describes a layered sequence of schists, gneisses and migmatites (A, B, C). No source classed the peninsula's own rock (died at the sources, all three). Lakes and ponds lie in bedrock depressions and are usually ice-free in January and February only (S3-31) (C). **No color is carried:** no source read states the rock's color (A; C's gray was an inference from gneiss in general, split 9).
- →1 Casey stands on hard, old, layered crystalline rock in low knolls, not on soil or sediment; whatever is not ice is hard and bare (A, C).
- →2 A built extent of 155-221 km² here must take in rock, moraine, ice and water together (S3-29 →1); ice is buildable (Frame L95), so the city's ground is two materials that behave differently under load and under thaw, rock that stays and bay ice that comes and goes. Which parts of Casey stand on which is not in the material (RF-20; no layout, REQ-6): **Patterned, deferred** (`DR-4`) (A, B).
- →3 The texture of being inside Casey changes with the ice even where nobody moves: in the shut half the bays are white ground continuous with the land; in the open half they are water (P3-35). A physical constraint on built form, never a district (RF-9) (A, B).

**P3-4 · The snow: what Casey shows of snow is a pattern the wind makes, not a blanket.** [Originated, physical] (P3A-3; P3B-6; P3C-2, P3C-28)
- *Canon and research.* Casey is in the katabatic-margin regime, snow flux rather than snowfall (precipitation L87; spec L103). Cumulative snow transport at coastal sites can be about four orders of magnitude higher than snow precipitation (precipitation L56-57), so the snow moving horizontally past a person is on the order of ten thousand times the snow falling (precipitation L59-60) (A, B). The snow that never lands is overwhelmingly snow that landed once and was picked back up (precipitation L50) (A). At the site: "the extremely strong winds scour the snow off exposed areas of the Peninsula" (`03_Research` L200, S3-20), and snow gathers in the lee of rock outcrops and in depressions, forming deeper drifts further down the slopes (S3-20) (A, B, C). ## B · Sound

*Ordinary condition first (spine L196-198): both bodies are ordinarily clothed and sheltered; robots are human-looking, with synthetic interiors (physiology L25-36), and wear coats and winter gloves (physiology L40, L45). Robots are 786,985 of 1,547,850 at Census I (spec L14; Frame L264). Robots perceive "sound and vibration across a meaningfully wider range than humans do, structurally rather than through any add-on device" (physiology L187); the line does not say in which direction the range is wider (DQ-14) (A, B, C).*

**P3-12 · The ambient sound of the outdoors is the wind, on about half the days; calm is the rare sound.** [present-day] (P3A-11 →1; P3B-7; P3C-5)
- *Basis.* P3-7: wind above 22 knots on 49% of the counted year's days, above 34 knots on 35%; gusts to 195 km/h from the east; blizzards that come on suddenly (S3-18). Moving snow is in the air on many ordinary days, hissing past at ground level (P3-4; an inference) (A, B, C).
- →1 On roughly every second day the wind is loud enough to be the sound of the place; outdoor speech on those days is short-range (A, B, C).
- →2 The wind's direction carries meaning: the gales come from the east off the ice cap (S3-18), so a rising easterly is heard before it is understood (C). Outdoors, a calm day is acoustically a different place (B).
- →3 **For both bodies the wind is heard; for robots it is also registered as vibration** in walls, frames and the ground, because physiology L187 names vibration explicitly (B, C). Whether that gives robots earlier warning of a wind change is not in canon and is not asserted (B). *Humans* hear its roar; whether its low parts are less audible to them than to robots turns on the direction of the robots' wider range and is not asserted (B withdrew the claim in Round 3; split 17).

**P3-13 · The shut half squeaks underfoot, and on the averages exactly the shut half.** *The phase's strongest sensory finding.* [Originated in the place's physics; physical, not a culture element] (P3C-6, heads; P3A-11 →2; P3B-8)
- *Source.* Snow squeaks when the air is below about −10 °C (14 °F), because pressure no longer melts a film between grains and the ice crystals rub against each other (**sourced**, an acoustic atlas, P3C-W8, confirmed P3A-R3-W3; also snippet, P3A-W5, P3B-W15, P3C-W2); warmer it is quieter, and near 0 °C it squelches (A, B, C). The threshold is approximate, for air temperature, and from temperate snow (C, B).
- *Casey's own figures* (spec L132-143): average lows below −10 °C in **exactly April to October** (−14.6, −18.5, −18.3, −18.4, −18.0, −17.1, −15.1 °C) and above it from November to March (−9.0, −3.7, −2.6, −5.0, −9.8 °C); average highs at or below −10 °C from May to August (−11.1, −10.4, −10.2, −10.2 °C); average highs above freezing only in December and January (+1.4, +2.2 °C). The monthly means give the same months (B; recomputed by all three in Round 3).
- →1 On the averages, snow at Casey squeaks every night from April to October and through most of the day from May to August; in December and January it is wet and quiet (A, B, C). **A footstep is a thermometer** (A).
- →2 The squeak months are the ice-locked months (spec L135-141, L154): the sound underfoot changes when the sea closes and changes back when it opens. The squeak half is also the dry-snow half, when snow moves at lower winds (dry 4-11 m/s against wet 7-14 m/s, S3-20), and the half in which 157.0 of the year's 225.1 mm falls (69.7%, the falls quantity, S2-14) (C).
- →3 **The sea's closure has a sound.** A person who cannot see the bay, in June's 2.7 hours of light or in a blizzard, can hear which half of the year she is in by walking; the first quiet footstep of spring is a sign of the opening and the first squeak of autumn a sign of the closing. Robots hear the change and its gradations at least as finely (physiology L187). A physical cue, never a custom (RF-6) (C; B).
- *Weak legs, recorded:* a general-physics threshold applied to monthly averages; individual days cross it both ways; Casey's wind-packed snow and crust (precipitation L87) may sound different (B, C).

**P3-14 · The frozen bays and the living coast have voices; the colonies' sound is in no source read.** [present-day; PA-10; (c); low] (P3A-11 →3; P3B-9; P3C-7)
- *Ice.* Sea ice cracks as wind, waves and other ice push against it and groans as it is slowly deformed; ice contracting under a fast temperature drop cracks with booming sounds; tidal movement flexes coastal ice (snippet, P3A-W16, P3B-W14, P3C-W18). At one East Antarctic coastal site, fast-ice cracking and seal calls overlapped by more than 90%, suggesting seals gather at tide cracks (snippet, P3C-W18) (A, B, C).
- *Animals.* The colonies and seals of P3-5 are present in the open half; **what they sound like is in no source read** (the colony page died at the source on sound, P3B-W4; recall withheld), so they are written as presence, not as sound (B, C).
- *Rare:* one December thunderstorm at the site was recorded as an extremely rare event (**sourced**, P3B-W11, confirmed P3A-R3-W2): thunder is effectively absent from Casey's sound [low] (B).
- →1 The shut-half soundscape is dry and hard: wind, squeak, and cracking and groaning from the frozen bays; the open-half one is wet and soft: squelch, meltwater in the valley catchments (S3-31), open water at the shore and the colonies' presence (A, B, C).
- →2 For seven months the city's bays are a surface that moves with the tide and the wind and is heard doing it; a crossing of the bay is made over a surface that can be heard working (C, an inference from the general mechanism; no site recording was found).
- →3 The year changes key by sound before it changes by sight (A). Whether those colonies lie within earshot of the built city is unknown (RF-20) (B).

**P3-15 · In the calm, sound may carry.** *General physics; a labeled inference for Casey; a Phase 4 question, not a finding.* (P3C-8)

**P3-16 · Robots and humans share one soundscape and not one sense of it.** [Determined; (c) on DQ-14] (P3A-12; P3B-7 →3; P3C-9)

## B · Smell

**P3-17 · Outdoors, Casey's cold air smells of little; crossing into heat is crossing into smell.** [snippet] (P3A-13 →1; P3B-10 →1; P3C-10)
- *Research.* Low temperature slows the evaporation of volatile compounds and dry air carries odors less, so cold air smells of little; noses are less sensitive in cold air, and part of the felt smell of cold is a nerve's response to temperature, not an odor (snippet, popular science, P3A-W7, P3C-W14) (A, C).
- *Casey's own figures:* every month's mean is below zero (spec L132-143), from −0.3 °C in January to −14.9 °C in August (C).
- →1 Outdoors, Casey's own smells (its buildings, works, vehicles and people) are suppressed all year, most of all from April to October, when the monthly means are below −10 °C (A, C).
- →2 *Inference:* in the shut half smell is an indoor sense; heated air releases what the cold held (C). *Humans:* the cold registers on the nose as sting, not scent (C).
- →3 What the indoors smells of is everyday life, CST material (RF-6), and is not written: heat, coolant, siligel and robot vapor products included (B, C). **A recorded null for indoor smell** (B).

**P3-18 · The outdoor smell has a season and a direction: the colonies, in summer, on the sea-side wind; and it arrives before the first ship.** [present-day; PA-10; (c); (h)] (P3A-13 →2-3; P3B-10; P3C-11)
- *Research.* Downwind of a large Adélie colony on another coast, ammonia rose more than 1,000 times its baseline about 8 km away when the wind came from the colony, and stayed about 100 times the baseline after the birds left at the end of February, because the guano kept releasing it; popular coverage calls the smell pungent and fishy (snippet, P3A-W17, P3B-W12, P3C-W19; mechanism taken, magnitudes not, and the baseline is not stated) (A, B, C). Elephant seal wallows have a very strong odor of musk and feces (snippet, a fisheries agency, P3A-W12) (A). The birds arrive about late September, breed October to February and leave the colony early to mid-February (snippet, P3C-W9; the colony page gives no adult dates, P3B-R3-W1) (C).
- *Casey's terms:* the gales come from the east off the ice cap (S3-18); colonies are on islands of the group (P3-5); their bearing from the built area is unknown (RF-20) (B, C).
- →1 *Humans:* in the breeding season and for some time after, a colony upwind of any part of the city is smelled as ammonia and guano; the wind off the ice carries nothing. The colony air arrives, if at all, on the sea-side winds, the coastal westerly storm systems of spec L98 (A, B, C). Which parts of the city smell of the colonies depends on layout against the wind: a mechanism, not a map, **Patterned, deferred** (RF-20) (B, C).
- →2 **(h) A smell is a wind direction** (A): the open half is the season with a smell and the shut half the season without one, the same two-state year again (S2-25) (B).
- →3 **The smell season is not the sea season** (C): the birds arrive while the sea is still shut and the shore ice unbroken (S3-1: breakout between early October and 19 November), so the smell of summer, like the light (S2-26), arrives before the sea opens, and the ammonia lingers after the birds leave, into March. The first sea-bird smell on a westerly in October, weeks before the first ship. Conditional on DQ-13 and on a snippet-level season (C).

**P3-19 · Robots: canon gives a chemical channel, not a nose; a robot's sense of smell at Casey is not written.** (a null, explained; DQ-14) (P3A-14; P3B-10; P3C-12)
- *Canon.* Robots have no respiratory system (physiology L9); they have "internal atmospheric management systems — not for respiration" that move air for thermal regulation and sensory input processing (physiology L297), and vapor compounds "interact with internal environmental sensors in ways that produce detectable signal changes" (physiology L300). No line says whether that register picks up ambient odors such as ammonia (spine L194-195: never infer from the word) (A, B, C). The cold file leaves open whether coolant thickens and whether the normal cooling system becomes a liability in deep cold (cold L139-140) (A).

## F · Retroactive Mechanism

**P3-37 · Simply true, unexplained: "a mild coast-facing day could be followed immediately by a katabatic event off the plateau" (spec L155). The physical system that would produce exactly that: the mild day and the blow are often one weather system.** [inference; snippet support; low until read at the primary] (P3A-26; P3B-20)
- *Simply true.* The spec states the switch as a fact of the weather and gives no mechanism; S2-12 read it as no promise in a mild morning (A, B).
- *The system, from the sources.* At the site, the very strong katabatic flow appears related to a supply of cold air inland, and the strong cross-slope flow comes with the approach of a strong cyclonic system toward the coast and a blocking system downstream (S3-18 (b), `03_Research` L183, a model study); strong katabatic flows usually stay on the escarpment and seldom reach the coast, probably held back by cold-air damming (S3-18 (c), snippet); the cold-air reservoir sits on the ice cap behind the coast (P3-3) (A, B). Across coastal Antarctica, extreme wind events are associated with a synoptic-scale dipole of pressure anomalies driving warm-air advection, katabatic forcing playing a passive role (snippet, P3A-W4, a 2024 study of coastal extremes; its record page died at the source, P3A-W20) (A). Rain is on the site's record in July (S3-19), which also needs warm air from the sea (A).
- *The mechanism, as an inference:* cold air pools on the ice slope behind the coast and is held there; the coast is sheltered and can be mild; an approaching cyclone first brings warm, moist sea air over the coast (the mild day, sometimes wet) and its pressure pattern then releases the pooled air and drives the strong wind and the blowing snow (A, B). **That the mildness itself is brought by the approaching cyclone is stated in no source read; it rests on B's labeled inference with A's snippet-level support** (split 19).
- →1 *The second-order consequence, for free:* at Casey a mild day does not merely fail to predict (S2-12); it is often the first half of the storm. **Mildness is the warning, not the respite** (A, an inference).
- →2 On Casey's own terms the warning sign is split between the inland sky and the sea's pressure (S3-18 →2): the sensory signature of danger is calm weather from the sea while cold builds unseen on the slope (B).
