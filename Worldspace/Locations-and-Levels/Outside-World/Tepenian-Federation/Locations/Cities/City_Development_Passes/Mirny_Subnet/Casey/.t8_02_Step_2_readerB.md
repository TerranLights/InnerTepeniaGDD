**S2B-3 · The year has two states, and its decisive date is the closing.**
1. *First order.* Casey's year is an open half (November to March: navigable sea, 13–24 h of light, means −0.3 to −7.2°C) and a shut half (April to October: ice-locked, 2.7–14.9 h, means −11.1 to −14.9°C) (L132-143, L154). The shut half averages −13.6°C and the open half −3.3°C.
2. *Second order.* Everything that must cross the sea is compressed into five months. Everything the shut half needs that the sea brings must already be at Casey when April comes (Frame L171).
3. *Third order.* What Casey is in winter is decided before April. The busiest open weeks are the ones that decide the winter, so the open half is lived toward the shut one.

**S2B-4 · Casey shuts at once and opens slowly, and its hardest stretch is a bright one. (The single strongest finding of this profile. It rests on G2 alone.)**
1. *First order.* The closing comes in a single row: April reads "Rapid darkening; sea ice forming" (L135). The opening is staggered. The light turns at the June solstice (L125; "Darkest period", L137). The cold floor runs flat from May to August, with means of −14.4 to −14.9°C, and August holds both the lowest mean (−14.9°C) and the record low (−37.5°C) (L136-139). The ice breaks up in October (L141), and the sea is navigable from about November (L154).
2. *Second order.* From late June to October the days lengthen from 2.7 to 14.9 hours while the cold stays at or near its floor and access does not change. Whatever was laid in before April is at its lowest in these same months, as far as the sea is the only route. The katabatic season peaks in July and only moderates in September (L138, L140), as far as the road is the route.
3. *Third order.* Casey's worst weeks are lit and lengthening. Hardship and visible hope fall in the same months, and the light promises relief months before the sea delivers it. For robots, the coldest nights of the year (near the −20°C capacity line on the averages, Frame L270; near the −40°C embrittlement line on the records, Frame L271) come after the light has turned. *A hypothesis for later phases, not a finding about culture:* a place where light is not access.

**S2B-5 · Heat for rest is the standing cost with the widest reach.**
1. *First order.* Every month's average low is below freezing (L132-143), so recharging in heated space is a year-round necessity on the averages for 786,985 robots (Frame L272, L275). Humans need warmth too, and the two bodies stop together: "Robots and humans stop at the same time, for different reasons." (Frame L276).
2. *Second order.* Heated rest is a load paid every night by everyone and named by no one. How many robots Casey can hold is bounded by how much warm rest it can keep running, every night.
3. *Third order.* The loss from a cold recharge is permanent (Frame L272), so a robot's nights outside the heat stay in her body. Anyone outside the heated stock (a traveler between stops, a crew held by the ice, a resident whose heat fails) pays in capacity, not comfort (Inherited S1-17). How much per night, and whether it accumulates, waits on `Robot_Cold_Physiology.md` (REQ-31).

**S2B-6 · Snow at Casey is a cost the city's own form makes.** *(Low weight on the mechanism, which sits on a line outside the spans.)*
1. *First order.* About 139 of the 225 mm that falls each year is sublimated or carried off (L104-106). 69.7% of it falls in the shut half, across the katabatic season ("katabatic winds increasing" in March, "katabatic season peak" in July, "katabatics moderating" in September; L134, L138, L140).
2. *Second order.* Snow that moves collects wherever the wind is interrupted ("drift from upwind", contract L174, citing spec L109 part 2, which lies outside the spans; REQ-32). So the snow burden is set less by how much falls than by what stands in the wind.
3. *Third order.* The more Casey builds across its 155–221 km², the more drift it makes for itself. Its snow work is generated by its own shape and peaks in the dark, shut months, where it reads as weather rather than as a price of building.

**S2B-7 · In the open months, ending the day is a decision, not a cue.**
1. *First order.* The navigable months (L132, L143, L154) have 13–24 h of light, 16.5 h or more from November to February, and include the ~23 days of midnight sun (L113).
2. *Second order.* The national shared night (Frame L276) has no darkness to mark it in the weeks when the sea brings the most work (RF-23). Its dark night falls only from March to October (Frame L191).
3. *Third order.* At Casey, stopping work in the open months is set by people. Nobody in the admitted material has a reason to name the cost of not stopping, because the hazard it would expose is "not yet ruled" (Frame L245). The deficit is diffuse and may be unspeakable (§6). *One property makes both S2B-7 and G2's daylight strength:* the refraction lift "*shortens* polar night and *lengthens* midnight sun" (L118). It gives winter its sun and takes summer's night (conflict C7).
