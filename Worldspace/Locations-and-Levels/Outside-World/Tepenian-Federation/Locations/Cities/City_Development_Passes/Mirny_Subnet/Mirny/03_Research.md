## 1 · The wind and the snow

- **Drift** *(Tabler, SHRP snow-fence guide and 2003 report; ASPA 127)*. A solid building fills with snow on its
  **windward** side first, then in its lee; at equilibrium both drifts run about 12–15 times its height. The snow a
  structure holds grows faster than the structure (height to the power 2.2). **Corners and ends scour** — the bare
  ground beside a building is where the wind accelerates, not behind it. A drift left alone stops growing; **a drift
  that is cleared refills** — so the cost is a choice remade after every storm. *(A, B, C)*
  → `[V]` **"Out of the wind is under the snow": CONFIRMED.** Drift geometry is fixed by height and wind direction,
  so **it is the half of the wind that can be written down.** *(A)*
- **Two different blindnesses** *(AMS, Australian Antarctic Division, WMO)*. Losing sight under a **clear sky** is a
  **ground blizzard** — wind-lifted snow; **whiteout** proper needs an **overcast** sky and unbroken snow (flat light,
  no wind needed). The spec's "whiteout under a clear sky" joins the two. *(B, C)*
- **At Mirny's ordinary wind, snow moves.** Blowing snow starts at roughly 5–10 m/s; Mirny's mean is 11–12 m/s. Most
  moving snow stays below the knee on an ordinary day and climbs into the face as the wind rises (88.5 % below 0.5 m
  at 10 m/s; 33.8 % at 30 m/s). Blowing-snow layers run 30–1,000 m deep (average about 120 m; about 150–230 m on
  katabatic coasts). Visibility falls steeply with wind speed and depends on how much loose snow lies upwind —
  fresh snow bonds, doubling in strength within a day, so **the same wind gives very different visibility on
  different days.** *(A, B, C)*
- **The cues are honest; they are unread** *(all three agree after Round 3)*. The sky clearing is the sign the wind
  is coming off the ice (the East Greenland piteraq sign); onset warms the air at the surface; and the strongest
  storms arrive **behind cyclones, as the sky clears** (Russian Antarctic institute, AARI). The spine's *"the sky
  lies"* reads more precisely as **"the sky tells the truth in a language nobody at the founding had learned."**
- **Can a storm be seen coming?** A visible "wall" of snow can form where the flow meets the coast in an otherwise
  clear sky — documented elsewhere, **not demonstrated at this coast.** Whether the wind reaches upslope ground first
  is **not established** (a dead end at the sources, three angles).
- **The wind's clock** *(Repina 2025; AARI; Australian Antarctic Division)*. In summer and the transition seasons the
  katabatic builds in the evening, **peaks about 05–08 local and eases by 15–18** — an afternoon lull. In winter it
  follows approaching cyclones. **Whether Mirny keeps a weak daily cycle in winter is UNKNOWN** — the source's "no
  daily cycle" is scoped to polar night, which Mirny does not have. Calm and weak wind together: about 3 % of
  observations. Storms last up to about nine days (220 h). Prevailing direction **east-southeast to southeast**; the
  record gust came from the east, with a cyclone.
- **Winter has no core.** Antarctic coastal winters are "coreless" — flat for months. *(B, C)* Deficit #4 is real
  physics.

## 2 · Light

*(USNO astronomical calculator; own computations; Wikipedia climate table)*
- **Solstice day: 2 h 10 min** of sun (21 June). June mean about 2.6 h.
- **Midnight sun: 29 days (about Dec 7 → Jan 4) today; about 27 days in the 2564–2812 frame** (Earth's tilt shrinks).
- **No darkness beyond civil twilight for about 80 days (Nov 11 → Jan 30).** The weeks with no dusk to end the
  working day are **mid-November to the end of January**, not just the 29.
- **June sunshine: 1.7 hours for the whole month.** The midwinter sun is barely above the horizon, due north;
  whether it is seen at all depends on refraction and on an open **northern** skyline (the Haswell Islands, up to
  93 m high, lie offshore to the north). `[V]`

## 3 · The sea

*(ASPA 127 management plan, primary)*
- Fast ice reaches **30–40 km wide** by the end of winter. It **breaks up between Dec 17 and Mar 9 (average Feb 3)**
  and **refreezes between Mar 18 and May 5 (average Apr 6)**. Open water lasts more than a month in 85 % of years,
  more than two months in 45 %, more than three in 25 %. A second, unverified figure gives an average break-up of
  Feb 13.
- **For about ten months the sea is a surface** — the plan speaks of fast ice "safe for pedestrian and vehicle
  traffic." The katabatic dies about 8–15 km offshore, so **the outer fast ice lies beyond the city's wind** —
  though not beyond the cyclones.
- **The window cannot be scheduled:** break-up varies over 83 days, freeze-up over 48.

## 4 · The relay, the ground

- **Storms attack a radio relay three ways** *(conditional — the relay's technology is not canon)*: **static from
  blowing snow** (noise raised 50 dB or more, radio data lost 10–30 % of the time in winter and spring at one
  inland station; wind-driven radio noise starts around 10 m/s and is worst on above-ground structures); **rime
  icing** when warm wet snow freezes (a cable snapped — possible here in the warm months); and **space weather**
  (high-latitude radio blackouts lasting hours to days, arriving under a calm clear sky). **The relay is noisiest
  exactly when the city most needs it.** *(A, B, C)*
- **The ground:** real rock is **coastal only** — charnockitic granite, schist and gneiss outcrops, and islands to
  the north. Inland is **moving ice (6–80 m/yr) with crevasses hidden under 1.5 m or more of snow**. Wind is strongest
  on the lower slope. **The surface hides its danger twice — in the air and underfoot.** No real rock was found to
  the south (see §7).

## 5 · The deficits, researched

- **Judgment of the wind, where it exists.** In **East Greenland**, the clearing sky means the piteraq is coming and
  a town alarm shuts everything; the wind is "well known to Greenlanders, but not well studied by scientists" —
  **Mirny's exact inverse: the study without the knowing.** **Inuit** hunters read hard snow ridges that always point
  along the prevailing wind — a compass for the feet when the eyes fail; younger hunters relying on GPS lose it
  (erosion — Mirny's case is absence). On the **Adriatic**, people name the bora by its sky (clear bora, dark bora);
  Mirny has at least three kinds of wind — clear katabatic, cloudy cyclonic, and the worst, the two combined.
- **Substitutes for judgment.** Norilsk's Storm HQ publishes one number at a named hour and "complaints are not
  reviewed" — and a crew keeps the plant running while the city stops. Norway runs road convoys on a published signal
  ("calm here, storm over the mountain"). Australian lifesavers set **flags each day** where a trained reader judges
  it safe — because only about a third of people who can spot a rip in a photograph can spot it on the real beach:
  **a record teaches the picture, not the place.**
- **No living teacher.** Fogbank (a U.S. weapons material): records survived, experts didn't, and an essential
  ingredient was never written down — the record set only a **maximum** where practice needed a **minimum**; the fix
  was checking outcomes, not the recipe. Nizhny Tagil's lacquer: the objects survived, the method did not.
- **One relay.** When Shetland's cable was cut (2022), the fallback was **people walking to fixed points**. At Mirny
  the storm that breaks the relay also stops people walking.
- **No year-round sea.** Road-less Arctic towns stake the year on the summer window. **Mirny has the road** — and the
  frozen sea is also a road.

## 6 · Borrowed Form — three founding stocks *(forms in the ancestral repertoire, never traits of the people)*

- **Russian:** the Pomor **lotsia** — a family book of the coast, amended by each generation that used it, whose key
  category is **places "where there is no escape"** from the weather. Wind names traveled with their speakers and
  were reattached to whatever local wind filled the same role (*shelonik*). "Sastrugi" is itself a Russian word.
- **Chinese:** the Hainan fishermen's **route books** (更路簿) — memorized first, written later, every copy amended by
  its keeper, entries marked **"此条准" ("this entry is verified")**, new books written when the boats changed. Both
  Russian and Chinese weather speech already split blowing snow **at head height** (白毛风, "white-hair wind").
- **Australian:** **a reader's judgment published as a marker, reset daily** (surf-patrol flags); judgment condensed
  into an instrument whose ceiling was set by the worst day its makers knew — and later exceeded (the McArthur fire
  meter).
- **What transfers — the amended copy.** Mirny's record came with no living layer writing back into it. The borrowed
  form is a record that **the survivors write in**, so a departure from the book becomes the next reader's
  instruction. **Divergence:** here the record precedes its practitioners, and the first marks would be the
  founders' own. *(A, B, C)* The other eleven stocks were not researched (not in the read set).
