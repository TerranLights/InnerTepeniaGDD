- →1 **The look of Casey is set by where snow is not.** Exposed rock crests are bare; hollows, lee faces and the downwind side of every structure carry drift. ## B · The four sensory sub-fields, separately

*Ordinary condition first (spine L196-198): both bodies are ordinarily clothed and sheltered; a robot is human-looking, with synthetic interiors (physiology L25-36), "a robot can (and nearly almost always does) wear clothing" (physiology L40), and "robots would also wear winter gloves" (physiology L45). Robots are 786,985 of 1,547,850 at Census I (spec L14; Frame L264). Robots have no respiratory system (physiology L9) and "perceive sound and vibration across a meaningfully wider range than humans do" (physiology L187).*

### B · Sound

**P3B-7 · The outdoors sounds of moving air, and calm is the rare sound.** [web, sourced; present-day]
- *For both bodies:* wind above 22 knots on 49% of days and above 34 knots on 35% (P3B-3); gusts recorded to 195 km/h from the east (web call 10); "Blizzards may occur very suddenly" (S3-18). Moving snow is itself in the air on many ordinary days (P3B-6).
- *Robots:* the wind's load on buildings and bodies falls in a range robots perceive more widely, "structurally rather than through any add-on device" (physiology L187), so for the majority body the wind is heard and also registered as vibration in walls, frames and the ground.
- *Humans:* the wind is heard; its low and structural parts are less audible to them than to robots (physiology L187, by its own comparison).
- →1 Outdoor speech is shaped by wind on half the days; a calm day is acoustically a different place.
- →2 For robots a building is never acoustically still in wind; the onset of a strong event would reach them through structure as well as air. *Whether that gives robots earlier warning of a wind change is not in canon* (open; not asserted).
- →3 The two bodies share the same weather and do not share the same soundscape: indoors, in heated rest, the robot majority still registers the storm through the walls, while humans may hear only its roar. A city whose majority body cannot fully shut the weather out by closing a door (inference from physiology L187; (h), **Phase 4**).

**P3B-8 · Underfoot, the year has two sounds: soft in the warm weeks, squeaking in the shut half.** [web, snippet; physics]
- *Source.* Snow underfoot shifts from a soft squelch near 0 °C to progressively crunchier toward about −10 °C, and squeaks below it (web call 15, snippet; several popular-science sources; mechanism disputed between sources and not carried).
- *Casey's figures:* December and January average highs +1.4 and +2.2 °C, lows −3.7 and −2.6 °C (spec L132, L143); April to October means −11.1 to −14.9 °C (spec L135-141).
- →1 From about April to October snow at Casey is cold enough on the averages to squeak underfoot; in December and January it is near its melting point and soft by day.
- →2 The season can be heard in a footstep, for both bodies; robots hear more of it (physiology L187).
- →3 The shut half has a sound the open half lacks, so the year's two states (S2-25) are audible as well as visible: the crunch of the closed sea's months against the slush of the open ones. A physical cue, never a custom (RF-6).
- *Divergence:* a threshold from temperate snow; Casey's wind-packed snow and crust (precipitation reference L87) may sound different; snippet level.

**P3B-9 · The ice and the living coast: sounds whose sources are sourced and whose sounds are not.** [web; present-day; (c)]
- *Ice:* sea ice cracks and groans as wind, waves and other ice push it (web call 14, general, snippet); the shore ice forms in autumn and breaks out between early October and mid-November (S3-1); locally generated waves break the ice at the polynya edge (S3-3).
- *Animals:* the island group's Adélie population is "almost 200,000 breeding pairs", "nearly half a million seabirds", and one island of "less than a square kilometre" holds "nearly 40,000 breeding penguins" at the season's peak (web call 4, sourced); snow petrels, Wilson's storm petrels, cape petrels and skuas nest in the island group (web call 13, snippet); southern elephant seals moult at wallows in the bay, December to about April (web call 6, snippet), their counts down 88% between 1989 and 2001.
- **What these sound like is in no source read** (web call 4 died at the source on sound; recall withheld). Written as presence, not as sound.
- →1 The open half carries sound sources the shut half does not: open water on the shore, a breeding bird population of the order of hundreds of thousands, seals hauled out to moult.
- →2 The shut half's sound sources are wind, snow and ice; its living sources are absent or few on the sources read.
- →3 The year's audible contrast is a population contrast: in summer Casey's sound includes a non-human, non-robot population larger than most of its own communities; in winter it does not. *Whether those colonies lie within earshot of the built city is unknown* (RF-20); present-day populations do not stand for the frame (P3B-DQ-1).
- *Rare:* one December thunderstorm was recorded as "an extremely rare event" (web call 11): thunder is effectively absent from Casey's sound.
- *Classification (Step 4 note):* none of these is a cultural element.

### B · Smell

**P3B-10 · Smell has a season and a direction: in summer, the colonies upwind; in winter, almost nothing outdoors.** [web, snippet; present-day; (c)]
- *Source.* Ammonia downwind of a large Adélie colony at another Antarctic site rose more than 1,000 times baseline when the wind came from the colony, about 8 km away, and stayed about 100 times baseline after the birds left at the end of February, because the guano kept releasing it; the smell is described in popular coverage as "pungent fishy" (web call 12, snippet; a single study at another site; mechanism taken, magnitudes not).
- *Casey's terms:* gales predominantly from the east (S3-18); colonies on islands of the group (web call 4); their bearing from the built area unknown (RF-20).
- *Humans:* in the breeding season, and for some time after, a colony upwind of any part of the city would be smelled as ammonia and guano; downwind of none, nothing.
- *Robots:* no respiratory system (physiology L9), but internal atmospheric systems move air for thermal regulation and for processing sensory input (physiology L297, paraphrased), and vapor compounds "interact with internal environmental sensors in ways that produce detectable signal changes" (physiology L297, L300). So a robot registers airborne chemistry as a signal change; **whether she experiences it as smell is not established in canon** (spine L194-195: never infer from the word). Written as detection, not as smell.
- →1 Outdoors, Casey's smell is mostly absent in the cold months and present in the warm ones, from a direction set by the wind.
- →2 Which parts of the city smell of the colonies in summer depends on layout against an east wind, a Patterned fact deferred (RF-20).
- →3 The open half is the season with a smell, the shut half the season without one: the same two-state year again (S2-25). Indoors: heat, coolant, siligel and robot vapor products are CST-stage and are not written (RF-6). A recorded null for indoor smell.

### B · Feel

**P3B-11 · Cold on the skin, and on the cells: the outdoors is priced differently for the two bodies, and both are dressed for it.**
- *Figures:* every month's average low below freezing, from −2.6 °C (January) to −18.5 °C (May) (spec L132-143); monthly record lows −31.2 to −37.5 °C from April to October (S2-22); "May is statistically our coldest month with an average maximum of −11.2 and minimum of −18.6 degrees" (web call 11, present-day, close to the spec).
- *Robots, clothed:* "Usable capacity falls to 50–60% by −20°C" (Frame L270); the record has not reached the −40 °C embrittlement line (Frame L271); "A robot is safer moving than resting" (Frame L273); impact damage changes kind as materials pass their brittle transition (cold file L81); the glove trade-off is "a GRADIENT across locations, never a gate" (physiology L57).
- *Humans, clothed:* exposure and wind; no human cold threshold is in the material (S2-69 (f)).
- →1 For a human, a Casey winter day outdoors is felt as cold and wind on the face and hands; for a robot it is felt (or registered: open, physiology L59-60) as capacity sliding toward a line it does not cross on the averages, and as the danger of stopping.
- →2 So the two bodies are uncomfortable in different postures: a human wants to shelter and be still; a robot wants to keep moving and must not rest unheated. The cold file's own words: "This is the exact opposite of human cold survival" (cold file L95).
- →3 The heated room is the one place both bodies' outdoor discomforts end, for different reasons. Heated rest is "a physical condition of robot life all year, before it is a custom" (Frame L275): the threshold the feel of Casey turns on is a door into heat. *Classification:* national (overnight recharge, physiology L334) inflected by Casey's cold in every month: Inflected.

**P3B-12 · Feel underfoot: rock, crust, drift, glaze, and a summer surface that is firm only in the low-sun hours.** [web; via contract]
- *Sources.* Bare scoured rock and lee drift (P3B-6); "Blue-ice and wind-crust surfaces" on the katabatic margin (precipitation reference L87); clear-sky whiteout and drift from upwind (spec L109 part 2 [via contract]); rain on the record in July 2008 and 2009, freezing as glaze (S3-19); in summer the top centimeters of thawed ground refreeze at night (S3-15).
- →1 Footing changes with the season and the hour: in winter, wind-crust, drift and occasional glaze; in December and January, soft by day and firm in the low-sun hours (S3-15).
- →2 Visibility can fail with a clear sky overhead, when snow moves at ground level [via contract]: the ground's surface can disappear while the sky stays blue.
- →3 To walk Casey is to read the wind's last work on the ground, and in summer to prefer the night's firmness over the day's warmth. For a robot, a fall on hard glaze in deep cold is a different damage type from a fall in the warm weeks (cold file L81); for a human, the risk is the fall itself.

## E · Seasonal variance

**P3B-19 · The worst point to the senses is June to August; the worst point to the store is September to October; they are not the same weeks, and Casey is not the same place in each.**
- *June to August:* 2.7 to 7.6 h of daylight (spec L137-139); "Darkest period" (L137); "katabatic season peak" (L138); August's mean −14.9 °C and record low −37.5 °C (L139); "around one blizzard every five days" (web call 11); bays frozen into white ground (P3B-2); colonies and seal wallows absent or empty on the sources' seasons (P3B-9); snow squeaking underfoot (P3B-8); little outdoor smell (P3B-10).
- *September to October:* the light lengthens to 11.3 and 14.9 h (spec L140-141) while the cold holds near its floor and the sea stays shut; the shore ice breaks out between early October and mid-November, before any ship can come (S3-1); this is the hardest stretch for the store (S2-26), "the worst weeks are the last ones, before the first ship" (S3-6).
- *December to January, the other pole:* 24 and 21 h of daylight (spec L143, L132), no civil darkness from about 13 November to 30 January (S3-14), average highs above zero, rain possible, melt, moss, open water, colonies, soft snow by day and firm at night (P3B-4, P3B-12).

- →2 **The constants are the rock, the wind and the heated night.** The wind is there on half the days in every season (P3B-3); for the robot majority, heated rest is owed every night of every month (S2-10). For the majority body, the single unchanging sensory fact of Casey's year is the walk from the moving cold into held heat.
- →3 So Casey is the same place to the robot half's nightly routine and two places to every other sense. Its worst point to the eye and skin (midwinter) comes before its worst point to the store (the lit late shut weeks), so the year's hardest feeling and its hardest fact are a season apart (S2-26).

## F · Retroactive Mechanism

**P3B-20 · "A mild coast-facing day could be followed immediately by a katabatic event off the plateau" (spec L155): what physical system produces exactly that.** [web; inference labeled]
- *Simply true:* the spec writes the switch and does not explain it.
- *The system, from Step 3's sources:* "The very strong katabatic flow appears to be related to the production of a supply of cold air inland from Casey"; the strong cross-slope flow comes with "the approach of a strong cyclonic system toward the coast" (S3-18 (b), `03_Research` L183); strong katabatic flows usually stay on the escarpment and seldom reach the coast, probably held back by cold-air damming (S3-18 (c), snippet). The cold-air reservoir sits on an ice cap about 200 km across and about 1,390 m high (P3B-2).
- *The mechanism:* cold air pools on the ice slope behind the coast and is held there; the coast is sheltered and can be mild; a cyclone approaching from the sea releases the pooled air, which arrives at once.
- *The second-order consequence, for free:* the mild day and the storm can share a cause, because the approaching sea cyclone that ends the mildness is also what releases the cold air. **An inference, labeled:** that the mildness itself is brought by the approaching cyclone is not stated in any source read.
- →3 On Casey's own terms the warning sign is split between the inland sky and the sea's pressure (S3-18 →2), and the sensory signature of danger is calm weather from the sea while cold builds unseen on the slope. *For Phase 5's decision point* (P3B-HF-3).

**P3B-21 · The green ground is old bird ground: why the most living surface on Casey's rock is where it is.** [web, snippet; (c)]
- *Simply true:* moss beds lie on some peninsulas and not others; the islands carry algae, not moss (P3B-4).
- *The system:* nutrients left by ancient penguin colonies plus meltwater flowing all summer produce the extensive moss beds (web call 2, snippet); a nine-thousand-year record of Adélie occupation in the island group exists by title (web call 3, a result title only, not read).
- *The second-order consequence, for free:* the vegetation map is a map of where birds once were and where water still runs, so the texture of the open half's green records a history the city did not make, and the city's own building and drift (P3B-6, P3B-18) decide which of it survives the frame. Present-day; conditional on layout (RF-20).
