## B. The four sensory sub-fields, separately

**B.0 The register that must not be collapsed: visually calm, tactilely and acoustically tense.** The admitted files give a place whose eye-cues and body-cues disagree: "Whiteout here routinely occurs under a clear sky" [ADM L82]; "even at relatively moderate temperatures, sustained katabatic events make outdoor exposure dangerous" [ADM L139]. A clear sky and a thermometer above freezing (December and January mean highs +0.4 and +1.1 °C, [ADM] L118, L107) are **not** signs of calm. The four sub-fields are therefore written apart.

### B.1 SOUND

**What the files give.** The only acoustic datum is the wind: "wind is the dominant environmental feature of this city" [ADM L71]; "average wind speeds 10–14 m/s" [ADM L71]; "mean wind 12.0 m/s" [ADM L82]; storms "exceeding 40 m/s (144 km/h)" [ADM L71]; "blizzard conditions can develop rapidly" [ADM L134]. `RES` §1 adds the wind's clock [CORR]: in summer and the transition seasons the katabatic builds in the evening, peaks about 05–08 local and eases by 15–18; in winter it follows approaching cyclones (whether Mirny keeps a weak daily cycle in winter is UNKNOWN); calm and weak wind together are about 3 % of observations; storms last up to about nine days (220 h). **No sound level, no source list, and no interior is given.**

**Human body — to the third order (P3-A2).**
- *1st:* outdoors the acoustic ground state is wind. That wind at these speeds is audible is physics [D]; no admitted file states a level.
- *2nd:* sound has a **shape in time**. Day-shaped in the warm seasons (evening build, early-morning peak, afternoon lull); storm-shaped in winter, with a single storm able to last about nine days. Quiet is rare: calm and weak wind together is about 3 % of observations [CORR]. In the warm season the quiet is a time of day (the afternoon lull); in winter it is luck.
- *3rd:* **in the lit weeks the wind, not the sun, is the day's clock** [D on CORR]. `SPN` §3 G2 records that no dusk ends the working day in the nightless season; the evening build is then what ends the outdoor day, and the afternoon lull is the part of the day when outdoor work and speech are easiest. And a nine-day storm is not an event but an ambient condition: a person can live a week inside one sound.

**Robot body.** `RP` L187: "robots perceive sound and vibration across a meaningfully wider range than humans do, structurally rather than through any add-on device" [IND]. *Not given:* which part of the wind's spectrum lies in the extra range; whether the extra range is a cue or a burden; any robot-specific sound the files name. *What follows without inventing a sense:* [D] the two bodies **do not hear the same wind** — so the wind clock above, which is a human-range statement, is not asserted for the robot body, and no claim is made that the wind is louder to a robot, only that it is a different band. The wind loads structures; vibration is part of what `RP` L187 widens; so the structure-borne part of the wind is a wider channel for the robot body. *Third order, stated as a question not a claim:* if wind-related cues lie in that range, the wind deficit is not uniform across the two bodies (RF-A4, R-A4); if they do not, it is. The admitted set cannot say.

**REQUESTED:** any acoustic level or source; interior sound; the robot band's relation to wind, ice and structure (R-A4).

### B.3 FEEL

**Feel-1 · The thermometer and the skin disagree (P3-A1).** [ADM] "effective wind-chill during katabatic events can drive apparent temperatures far below ambient" (L142); [ADM] "even at relatively moderate temperatures, sustained katabatic events make outdoor exposure dangerous" (L139); [ADM] danger persists "regardless of cold protection" (L131); [ADM/IND] December and January highs are above freezing (+0.4, +1.1 °C) with "brief rain possible" in January (L107). `RES` §1 [CORR, conditional on Mirny]: the sky clearing is the sign the wind is coming off the ice, and onset warms the air at the surface; the strongest storms arrive behind cyclones as the sky clears.
- *1st:* temperature, sky and ground can all read mild while the wind is dangerous.
- *2nd:* the **warm months are not the safe months in wind**; the hazard is wind, not cold (L139).
- *3rd:* [D on CORR] **the first sign of the dangerous wind can read as an improvement** — a clearing sky and a rising thermometer — so the eye and the thermometer say the opposite of what the skin will. The honest cues are present and unread (`RES` §1); a resident can learn them, a first-time arrival cannot (Phase 5).
- *Magnitude:* no wind-chill figure is in the admitted set; "far below ambient" is all (R-A5).

**Feel-2 · The body is the gauge (P3-A3).** [CORR, `RES` §1; conditional] Blowing snow starts at roughly 5–10 m/s; at 10 m/s 88.5 % of moving snow is below 0.5 m, at 30 m/s 33.8 % is; so on an ordinary day most moving snow runs below the knee and it climbs into the face as the wind rises; the same wind gives very different visibility on different days because fresh snow bonds, doubling in strength within a day. Mirny's mean is 12.0 m/s [ADM L82] — inside the band where snow moves.
- *1st:* on an ordinary day the legs and ankles stand in moving snow and the face does not.
- *2nd:* **how high up the body the moving snow runs is a reading of the wind**; both bodies share the same human-looking, clothed shape (`RP` L18–57), so the same gauge applies to both.
- *3rd:* [D] the gauge is calibrated only against the upwind snow supply — after fresh snow has bonded, the same wind lifts less — which is why "Whiteout here routinely occurs under a clear sky" [ADM L82] is not predictable from the anemometer alone. (`RES` §1 notes the spec's wording joins two blindnesses, a ground blizzard under a clear sky and a whiteout under overcast; the spec wording stands, SN-A5.)
