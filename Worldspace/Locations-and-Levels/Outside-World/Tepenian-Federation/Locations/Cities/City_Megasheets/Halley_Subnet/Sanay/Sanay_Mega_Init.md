## Who Lives Here, and Why

Sanay's founding population was South African, and the SANAE station's original infrastructure carried directly into the exile city — but the demographic story that followed broke the pattern every other Halley subnet city follows. Everywhere else in the subnet, American immigration eventually became Primary regardless of founding nation. At Sanay, Germany did instead — plausibly spillover from Neumayer's own substantial German community just up the coast. South Africa, the actual founding-operator nation, sits diluted at Notable tier, same shape as every other founding-nation story in this subnet, just a different demographic current responsible for it.

The bedrock foundation is the load-bearing fact of Sanay's whole existence — literally and narratively. Built on Vesleskarvet's rock rather than the moving, potentially-calving ice shelves that Halley and Neumayer sit on, Sanay took real damage in the Long Night War but wasn't erased by it. That distinction — endurance instead of exemption — is the entire shape of its civic self-image, and its own faction identity ("Competence Without Commentary") is built specifically around refusing to let that endurance be framed as luck or specialness worth talking about.

## What It Feels Like

Endless shipyards, a huge working port, industrialized and gritty, humans and robots working what can look like all hours of the day. Truckyards and warehouses occupy the lower city; the high rocky clifftops hold the major trucking/shipping depots and the import-export business district — and, woven into that same terrain rather than sitting apart in its own facility, the Halley subnet's actual Arcanet relay nexus. Along the cliffside and across the surrounding expanse, residential, commercial, and leisure districts give off-shift workers somewhere to be. Ship horns periodically pierce the atmosphere and echo across the city, tied to freighter arrivals running on a rotating, round-the-clock schedule rather than a fixed day/night rhythm.

This is not a huge city by Tepenian population standards — Lazar holds that title — but it is a genuinely major shipping and transportation hub by function, and its whole built environment and daily rhythm reflect that economic weight rather than any quieter, smaller-scale reading.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Rotterdam, Netherlands

By far the largest port in Europe and the world's largest outside Asia — grown to that scale through deliberate infrastructure investment (the Nieuwe Waterweg canal, opened 1870s, connecting the harbor directly to the North Sea and a booming German industrial hinterland) rather than any accident of geography alone. Rotterdam was devastated by wartime bombing, then rebuilt with spacious, functional, river-oriented architecture — planning that prioritized working function over nostalgic reconstruction of what had been lost.

**What this gives Sanay:** a real-world precedent for "function over sentiment" as a genuine, successful civic rebuilding philosophy, not just a coping mechanism — directly reinforcing Sanay's own established "Competence Without Commentary" faction identity. Worth naming the distinction precisely, though: Rotterdam was leveled and rebuilt essentially from scratch, while Sanay's bedrock meant it never needed that kind of total reconstruction — it endured rather than being erased and rebuilt. Two different paths (endurance vs. reconstruction) arriving at the same underlying value: keep the port working, don't romanticize the damage.

### Secondary: Guangzhou, China

A trade port with an unbroken commercial history stretching back to the Han dynasty and the Maritime Silk Road, later designated by the Qing dynasty as the sole authorized port for all foreign trade under the Canton System — foreign merchants confined to a small riverbank warehouse district, tightly regulated, permitted to trade but on strict, controlled terms. Modern Guangzhou has diversified into tech, finance, and logistics, and hosts the Canton Fair, China's oldest and largest trade fair.

**What this gives Sanay:** a concrete historical model for what a "trade yes, but on our terms" port actually looks like in practice — a specific, controlled zone where outside commercial partners are welcomed and dealt with, but within firm procedural boundaries rather than open access. This maps usefully onto Sanay's own relationship with South African freighter shipments: a plausible, earned detail is that Sanay's port enforces something functionally similar — a defined zone and clear procedures governing exactly how incoming Upper Earth-adjacent shipping interacts with the rest of the city, echoing Guangzhou's own centuries-old answer to the same underlying question (how does a working port control its exposure to an outside power while still profiting from it).

### Secondary: Silicon Valley, California

Before it was a tech hub, the region was known as "the Valley of Heart's Delight" — the largest fruit-producing and packing region in the world through the 1960s. The transformation into today's identity was gradual, built on a research base, capital, and defense spending rather than any single dramatic pivot. Its defining early cultural feature was informal, cross-company knowledge-sharing among engineers — competitors freely trading ideas after hours, an open culture credited as critical to the region's actual growth.

**What this gives Sanay:** direct structural confirmation of Sanay's own inherited-vs-current identity split — a founding-era purpose (Silicon Valley's orchards, Sanay's SANAE scientific research mission) quietly superseded by a completely different, now-dominant economic identity (tech, Sanay's industrial port economy), without the original identity needing to be erased to make room for the new one. It's still open, per the City Vision Notes, exactly how visible the SANAE research heritage remains today — Silicon Valley's own precedent suggests the honest answer is probably "present in institutional memory and place-names, essentially invisible in daily function," which is a concrete, earned way to close that open question. The informal cross-crew knowledge-sharing culture is also worth adapting directly: dockworkers and truckers from different backgrounds trading practical know-how off-shift, the working-port equivalent of engineers at a bar.

### Secondary: Durban, South Africa

The busiest port in sub-Saharan Africa, handling roughly 60% of South Africa's own trade revenue — a real, disproportionate share of a nation's economic throughput running through one city's harbor. Founded as a British colonial trading post, Durban's demographic story didn't stay British: a huge wave of later immigration (historically Indian) became a defining, dominant cultural presence, layered over rather than replacing the founding population's institutional legacy.

**What this gives Sanay:** the clearest structural parallel of all four picks — a founding population's institutional character persisting as heritage while a completely different, later immigration wave becomes the demographic majority. This is the same *shape* as Sanay's own established founding-legacy-vs-demographic-present dynamic — proof this split is a genuine, recurring real-world port-city pattern, not a coincidence unique to Sanay's own math.
