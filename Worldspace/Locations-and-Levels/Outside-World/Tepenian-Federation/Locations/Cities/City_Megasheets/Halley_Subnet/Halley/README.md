## What It Feels Like

The Brunt Ice Shelf moves roughly 400–700 meters a year, and Halley's entire architecture was built around that fact from the very beginning rather than treating it as an obstacle to overcome. Each ski-mounted module is fitted with tracks that dig into the ice and continuously, deliberately propel the structure toward the mainland — staying ahead of the shelf's own natural seaward drift, not waiting for a crisis relocation. Day-to-day movement around the city happens by snowmobile, humans and robots riding together across the municipal grounds. Halley has no fixed shipping docks and no airstrip by deliberate design — a city whose whole architecture exists because the ground itself moves would gain nothing from permanent maritime or aviation infrastructure the ice would simply carry away from any useful position over time. All resupply arrives overland, via whichever of Belgrano or Sanay currently has an open passage, trucked in along a connector road to Hwy 7. Older, abandoned positions — visible as structures left behind on the ice at various distances from the current city — form a literal, physical record of Halley's own movement history. The question of when to relocate and where to go next is a recurring civic conversation, not a crisis; the city understands itself as an ongoing project rather than a fixed inheritance.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Soviet/Russian North Pole Drifting Ice Stations (the NP series)

Scientific stations built directly on floating Arctic sea ice, inhabited for months or years at a time, evacuated urgently when the ice floe itself began breaking apart beneath them — NP-40 was evacuated in 2013 after only a few months when the floe started disintegrating.

**What this gives Halley:** the tightest structural match of all seven picks — a genuine precedent for human habitation built directly on ice that could give way, where evacuation-before-collapse is a known, practiced, real operational reality rather than a hypothetical. Halley's own established premise (survival depends entirely on staying ahead of the ice) has a real-world lineage in exactly this kind of station.

### Primary: Shishmaref, Alaska

An Inupiat village of roughly 600 that voted to relocate entirely due to active coastal erosion and permafrost thaw — three to five feet of shoreline lost per year, an "act or lose everything" precedent that took real, sustained community deliberation to actually decide.

**What this gives Halley:** a genuine precedent for relocation-by-collective-choice rather than sudden catastrophe — Shishmaref's own decades-long community deliberation process mirrors Halley's established civic culture of treating "when and where to move" as ongoing, deliberate business rather than a single dramatic crisis point.

### Primary: Kiruna, Sweden

An entire town center being physically relocated roughly two miles due to mining-caused ground subsidence, including moving a 113-year-old church intact on modular transporters.

**What this gives Halley:** a genuine whole-town relocation precedent at real civic scale — proof that moving entire structures, not just evacuating a small research crew, is something a real modern community has actually organized and executed, echoing Halley's own ski-mounted module architecture at a comparable scale of ambition.

### Secondary: Longyearbyen, Svalbard

An Arctic town forced to relocate or demolish buildings in high-risk zones after a fatal 2015 avalanche exposed how unstable permafrost-dependent structures had become.

**What this gives Halley:** a precedent for an Arctic community reactively discovering the limits of its own assumed stability — a useful contrast to Halley's own proactive, continuous relocation system, showing what happens to a comparable settlement that has to relocate under duress rather than by standing policy.

### Secondary: Cape Hatteras Lighthouse Relocation

The entire lighthouse was physically relocated inland in 1999 to escape coastal erosion — a single, massive, historically significant structure moved as one deliberate engineering project.

**What this gives Halley:** a tighter single-structure parallel to Halley's own ski-mounted module mechanic specifically — not a whole town, but a genuine precedent for moving one large, storied piece of infrastructure intact rather than rebuilding it from scratch at a new site.

### Supporting: Fairbourne, Wales

Officially designated by the UK government for eventual "decommissioning" due to sea-level rise, rather than active relocation — a place simply left to be lost once nobody keeps defending it.

**What this gives Halley:** the closest real-world match to Halley's own flagged, deliberately-deferred "permanent loss" path — a place whose worst-case future isn't sudden disaster but a quiet, administrative decision to stop maintaining what protects it.

### Supporting: Venice, Italy (MOSE Flood Barriers)

A city whose entire civic culture organizes around continuous engineering adaptation to unstable ground and water, rather than relocation itself.

**What this gives Halley:** a contrasting model worth naming directly — Venice chose to fight its unstable environment in place, while Halley chose to move with it instead; two entirely different civic answers to a structurally similar problem.

# Halley — Full Extrapolation

## I. Halley's Own Stake in the DLC 5 Central Conflict

**Proposed:** Troll Airfield's contested control is already established as the working candidate for DLC 5's overall central conflict, but Halley's own specific stake in it is different from Troll's or Belgrano's. Halley determines nothing about who wins that fight — it simply depends entirely on the outcome. Having deliberately forgone its own docks and airstrip, Halley's total dependency on overland resupply through Belgrano and Sanay means whoever controls the subnet's actual distribution logic (which the airfield fight decides) determines whether Halley eats this winter. Halley's DLC 5 role is being caught in the middle of a fight it cannot directly influence but cannot escape either.

---

## II. Current Mobility and Damage State: Newer Sections Mobile, Older Sections Stuck

**Proposed:** the most recently relocated modules, built with the most current propulsion-track technology, remain mobile and actively managed. The oldest sections — those that had less benefit from continuous relocation over their own operational lifetime — took the worst war damage to their ski-mounted platforms and are now genuinely stuck in place, sitting closest to real calving risk. This gives Halley's damage state a legible shape: newer, still-functioning parts of the city actively moving away from danger, older parts frozen in place and running out of time.

---

## III. Salagéa Aparast's Anchor: She Has None, By Design

**Proposed:** Salagéa doesn't have a single most-frequent port at all, and this isn't an unresolved gap so much as a structural fact of her established role. Her value as an itinerant datashard courier depends specifically on continuous movement between Atlantic subnet ports — settling into one place, even Halley, would undermine the exact function she serves. She is known to Halley's residents the same way she's known everywhere along her route, because staying mobile is the whole point.

---

## IV. Current Position vs. Historical Ones: Several Relocations Deep

**Proposed:** given the established continuous, non-crisis-triggered propulsion system and multiple relocations "within living memory," Halley has moved a genuinely significant cumulative distance from its original founding position — likely its fourth or fifth distinct site across the city's multi-century history. The oldest, founding-era position is now near or past the calving edge, lost or nearly lost to the sea entirely.

---

## V. Hwy 59's Current Condition: Physically Passable, Informationally Dead

**Proposed:** the physical road itself remains passable in sections, but its original dual purpose — highway and Arcanet cable corridor at once — has collapsed to just the first. With Amundsen Station destroyed, the highway's own southern terminus no longer functions as any kind of relay, meaning Hwy 59 today serves purely as a physical travel route with no remaining informational purpose, a genuinely different function than its founding design ever anticipated.

---

## VI. Weddell Sea Navigability: The Sea Isn't the Bottleneck Anymore, the Ports Are

**Proposed:** yes, the Weddell Sea remains seasonally navigable in principle — the ice conditions themselves haven't changed. What's actually degraded is the receiving infrastructure at Belgrano and Sanay, both damaged by the war. The sea was never Halley's real vulnerability; the ports it depends on are.

---

## VII. The Two Placeholder Holidays, Developed

- **The Crossing** — commemorating the founding generation's actual arrival onto the ice shelf itself, honoring the moment the city's whole "built to move" philosophy began, before there was anything here to move yet.
- **Position Day** — an annual civic ritual recording the city's current position relative to its founding site: a literal, practical act of surveying and record-keeping that's also inherently ceremonial, a yearly measure of exactly how far the city has come.

## IX. No Formal Designation for the Permanent-Loss Path — And That Absence Is Itself the Risk

**Proposed, directly answering the question surfaced in the Mega-Init:** no, unlike Fairbourne's actual administrative decommissioning status, Halley has no formal designation or bureaucratic contingency plan for what happens if nobody's left to keep the relocation program running. This isn't an oversight — it follows directly from Halley's own established civic identity, built entirely on the assumption that the collective choice to keep moving will always be made. The city has never planned for the scenario where that assumption stops holding, precisely because planning for it would contradict the confidence the whole culture is built on. The absence of a plan is itself worth naming as the real vulnerability, more dangerous than any single mechanical failure.

# Halley — Cross-Reference Synthesis: Gaps, Combinations, and Downstream Effects

**What this is:** a deliberate cross-referencing pass across every Halley document — `Specs/Halley.md`, `Local_Cultures/Halley_Subnet/Halley.md`, `City_Vision_Notes/Halley.md`, `City_Enneagram_Personalities/Halley_Subnet/Halley.md`, `Inspirational-Influences.md`'s entry, `Halley_Mega_Init.md`, and `Halley_Full_Extrapolation.md` — hunting for places where combining facts across files produces implications neither file states alone. Each finding is followed as far as its chain of implication honestly holds, in plain descriptive terms — no questline or dialogue-branch framing here; that mapping belongs to a later, separate quest-design pass, not this synthesis step. Written 2026-07-09.

---

## Finding 1: Halley has a consistent civic habit of trading concrete control for something less tangible — the nexus tradeoff, the missing infrastructure, and the missing contingency plan are one pattern, not three

**Combining:** the established Enneagram finding that Halley traded its own technical Arcanet nexus control for the subnet's naming honor, building Sanay's nexus by hand rather than keeping it + the established deliberate choice to forgo fixed docks and an airstrip entirely + the Full Extrapolation's proposed absence of any formal contingency plan for permanent loss.

**2nd-order effect:** these three facts, established across entirely separate sessions, describe the same underlying civic instinct — Halley repeatedly gives up concrete, defensive forms of control in favor of something more symbolic, confident, or forward-looking.

**3rd-order effect:** the same instinct that traded real technical power for naming credit is the same instinct that never built a formal decommissioning plan — planning for failure would contradict the same confident self-image that let the city give away its own nexus without apparent regret.

**4th-order effect:** worth naming explicitly as a unifying character trait for Halley as a whole, rather than three unconnected facts — a civic identity built around trading tangible control for something less measurable, consistently, across its whole history.

---

## Finding 2: Position Day's archive may be the only trace of Halley's history that actually survives, since the city's own past sites don't

**Combining:** the Full Extrapolation's proposed Position Day civic ritual (an annual survey recording the city's exact position) + the proposed multi-relocation history (Halley's fourth or fifth distinct site, its oldest position lost to the calving edge) + the established fact that a Tepenian city's ruins normally remain visitable even after abandonment, unlike Halley's own former sites, which are physically carried out to sea.

**2nd-order effect:** if Halley's own past positions are lost to the ocean rather than simply abandoned in place, Position Day's accumulated record may be the only surviving trace of the city's full geographic history — no ruin to visit, no site to excavate.

**3rd-order effect:** this makes the Position Day archive uniquely irreplaceable in a way most Tepenian cities' historical records aren't — other cities' history can, in principle, be reconstructed from visitable ruins even if written records are lost; Halley's oldest history can only be reconstructed through this one specific record.

**4th-order effect:** worth flagging as a genuine stakes-raiser for future design — losing the Position Day archive would cost Halley something no other Tepenian city could lose in quite the same way, since every other city's own ruins remain a fallback source of history even when the records fail.

---

## Finding 3: Halley's damage pattern is the inverse of Port Lockroy's and Fort McMurdo's own "old core survives" findings — and the reason why is instructive

**Combining:** the Full Extrapolation's proposed Halley damage pattern (newer, recently-relocated modules mobile and functioning; older modules stuck and damaged) + Port Lockroy's own established Cross-Reference finding (its older, wartime-built core survived better than newer peacetime additions) + Fort McMurdo's own parallel finding (its older, heavier-engineered core also outlasted its newer additions).

**2nd-order effect:** Halley's own pattern runs the opposite direction — here, newer sections fare better, because its core survival mechanism (continuous, active relocation) inherently rewards whichever modules have had the most recent engineering attention, unlike Port Lockroy's or Fort McMurdo's passive, built-in-the-past durability.

**3rd-order effect:** this is a genuinely distinct third variant of the "which era of construction survived better" question already surfacing across multiple Megasheets — the answer isn't a universal rule, it depends entirely on whether a city's defining resilience mechanism is active (favoring recency) or passive (favoring age).

**4th-order effect:** worth citing directly alongside the Port Lockroy and Fort McMurdo findings whenever this pattern comes up again — Halley is the clearest example of the active-mechanism case, a useful contrast rather than a contradiction.

---

## Synthesis: The Pattern Across All Three Findings

All three findings point toward the same underlying idea: Halley's whole existence is organized around continuous, active choice rather than passive endurance. Its civic identity consistently trades concrete control for something less tangible (Finding 1); its own history survives only through an ongoing act of deliberate record-keeping rather than any physical site remaining behind (Finding 2); and even its physical resilience depends on active, recent engineering rather than durability built once and left alone (Finding 3). Halley reads, across all three findings, as a city that has never once been able to simply persist — everything about it, from its politics to its memory to its architecture, has to be actively, continuously chosen or it stops existing.

## Worth Your Attention

Finding 1 is the most significant of the three — it ties together facts established in three completely separate sessions (the Sanay nexus tradeoff, the missing dock/airstrip infrastructure, the missing contingency plan) into one coherent civic character trait, giving Halley a genuine, unifying psychological throughline rather than three isolated, unrelated design choices.
