## II. What Survived the Strike — the Formal Archive vs. One Archivist's Own Backup

**Proposed:** Upper Earth's strike succeeded exactly as intended against the formal archive and its Arcanet link — both were fully destroyed, consistent with the strike's own established purpose (denying Upper Earth's adversaries any surviving record of the intelligence Juan Carlos held). What survived, and what "The Archivist's Trail" is actually built around, is something outside that formal system entirely: one specific archivist's personal, off-network notes or audio log, kept for reasons that had nothing to do with official recordkeeping and therefore weren't targeted or destroyed alongside the system that was. This resolves the tension between "the strike was total and deliberate" and "something clearly survived for the questline to find" without requiring the strike to have failed at its own stated purpose.

---

## III. When the Archive Relocated to Amundsen Station

**Proposed:** sometime reasonably close to the Arcanet's own original construction (per `Timeline.md`, beginning around 2614) — a unified national communications network and a unified national archive are a natural pairing, and it's plausible the same wave of institutional capability-building that produced the Arcanet also produced the decision to consolidate Tepenia's scattered early records into one central, better-protected archive. This gives the relocation a concrete anchor point rather than leaving it floating somewhere across roughly two and a half centuries of Second Interwar Period history.

## IV. Demonym: Carlense

Following standard Spanish place-name demonym patterns (the "-ense" suffix, as in "bonaerense" for Buenos Aires) rather than an awkward literal rendering of the full name "Juan Carlos" — **Carlense** (singular and collective plural).

## VI. A Hay-on-Wye-Style Self-Mythologized Civic Identity

**Proposed:** Juan Carlos's residents, even well after the practical archive function relocated to Amundsen Station, retained a specific, quietly proud self-image as "the people who remember" — not literally book-keepers, but carrying forward the same underlying impulse toward preserved, spoken, and argued knowledge as a civic good, expressed through the tertulia tradition rather than through physical archives. A plausible informal civic nickname, in keeping with Hay-on-Wye's own playful self-mythologizing register: residents half-jokingly referring to their own city as **"the Federation's memory,"** a title with real historical truth behind it (Juan Carlos genuinely was that, once) worn with the same wry, self-aware pride Richard Booth's "Kingdom of Books" carried, rather than solemn civic reverence.
