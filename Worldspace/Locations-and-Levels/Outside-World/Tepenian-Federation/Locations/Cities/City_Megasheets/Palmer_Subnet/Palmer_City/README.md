## Who Lives Here, and Why

Three groups arrived together on day one and built the city as equals: robots exiled under the treaty's blanket provision, humans in committed relationships with specific robots who chose exile rather than separation, and human ideological supporters who refused to accept the exile as legitimate. Nationality was never the organizing principle — relationship to robots was. This is the only Tepenian city with no founding-nation-vs-majority story at all, because the founding population was never sorted by nationality to begin with.

That founding fact metastasized into something structural: because *everyone's* ancestors walked the same ice paths first, no faction or nation could claim precedence, and neutrality became an inheritance rather than a policy. Combined with a deliberate 2026-07-03 expansion that added all 43 master-list nations in Notable-tier presence, Palmer City became the one place in Tepenia where literally every available exile nation is represented — not as a token gesture, but as the direct, logical extension of "the place everyone passed through."

## What It Feels Like

A web of interconnecting streets full of cultural buildings — dance halls with live bands playing jazz, rockabilly, and electroswing; casinos loud with chips and fortunes won and lost; concerts spanning a genuine range of genres. Streets lush with rain, sometimes delicate, sometimes pounding, with sun breaking through unpredictably — the only Tepenian city where above-freezing temperatures and actual rain are a normal, expected part of daily life.

Jazz is the founding touchstone (from records found in the original station buildings), but the *living* scene is broader — rockabilly and electroswing sit alongside it as part of an ongoing, present-tense nightlife identity, electroswing in particular resonating as a genre that's itself a hybrid of old and new, fitting a society built partly on found records from a vanished era. The economy runs substantially on Upper Earth tourism revenue — casinos, nightlife, and entertainment drawing money from an Upper Earth that officially disdains Tepenia but privately, and as a genuine open secret on their own side, indulges in it. Visitors arrive via Machu Picchu Base on King George Island (the customs/immigration gateway, distinct from Sejong, the actual Tepenian city on the same island), then cross calmer waters to reach Palmer City itself — anyone able to make the journey was welcome, on the understanding that Tepenian law applied to them while present, a real and pointed assertion of sovereignty over Upper Earth visitors on Tepenian soil.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Las Vegas

Spectacle, gambling, and reinvention as the whole point of the place — a city that exists because it decided to, not because geography or resources demanded it. Already directly cited in Palmer City's own established lore ("the spectacle and excess of Las Vegas").

**What this confirms:** the sheer scale and unapologetic centrality of entertainment as Palmer City's actual economic engine, not a side character to some other "real" industry — Vegas's own identity is proof that an entertainment economy can be a city's whole reason for existing, not a supplement to one.

### Secondary: Atlantic City

The real-world "Vegas of the East Coast," but distinct — beachfront boardwalk energy layered onto casino culture, generally read as a more broadly accessible, less totalizing experience than Vegas itself. Already cited directly in Palmer City's lore ("the boardwalk energy of Atlantic City").

**What this confirms:** the City Vision Notes session's own resolution that Palmer City is "broadly accessible, not exclusively an ultra-rich playground" — Atlantic City's real-world reputation as the more democratic, working-class-friendly counterpart to Vegas is exactly the register Palmer City's tourism economy was already established to occupy.

### Secondary: New Orleans

Deep musical soul, French Quarter intimacy, jazz as the actual living culture rather than a museum piece, Creole cuisine as identity. Already cited directly in Palmer City's lore ("the cultural depth and musical soul of New Orleans").

**What this confirms:** the difference between entertainment as spectacle (Vegas/Atlantic City) and entertainment as *soul* (New Orleans) — Palmer City's founding jazz-records story only works because jazz is treated as genuinely meaningful heritage, not just another attraction, the same distinction New Orleans itself draws against more purely commercial gambling destinations.

### Secondary: Montreal — newly researched, not yet incorporated into any existing lore

Montreal was a genuine nerve center of jazz in North America for nearly fifty years (1920–1970), its legendary nightlife and abundant work drawing musicians from across the whole continent — a reputation seeded specifically during American Prohibition, when Montreal became known as one of North America's "sin cities" with unmatched nightlife, owing partly to permissive local laws (late last call, lower drinking age) that American visitors couldn't get at home. That jazz history is rooted specifically in the Little Burgundy neighborhood, home to Montreal's historic Black community, drawn there originally because the city's two major train stations were built nearby and railway portering was one of the few jobs available to Black men at the time. Montreal is also genuinely bilingual (56.4% English-French bilingualism) and hosts, today, the Guinness-recognized largest jazz festival in the world.

**What this gives Palmer City, freshly:** two details that neither Vegas, Atlantic City, nor New Orleans supply on their own. First, Montreal's Prohibition-era role is a real-world precedent for exactly the dynamic City Vision Notes already established for Palmer City — a nearby, more restrictive jurisdiction (the USA during Prohibition; Upper Earth's own official disdain for Tepenia today) driving visitors toward a place with different rules, which then builds a genuine, lasting identity around having been that place. Second, Montreal's specific jazz history — a music scene built by a marginalized community using one of the only economic footholds available to them, becoming, in time, a defining civic export — is a strikingly close real-world echo of Palmer City's own founding jazz story (records left behind by a Black-originated art form, adopted immediately by an exile population building a life from nothing). Montreal is proof this pattern happened for real, not just a thematically nice-sounding parallel.

### Secondary: St. Petersburg, Russia

Founded in 1703 by Peter the Great's sheer will, on captured, marshy, geographically unpromising land, specifically to be a "window to the West" — a deliberately Western-facing capital meant to modernize Russia and pull it out of isolation, built at devastating human cost (an estimated 30,000+ deaths in construction) and declared imperial capital within less than a decade of founding. Already cited directly in Palmer City's lore ("the grand ambition of St. Petersburg").

**What this deepens:** the "window" framing is a precise structural match for Palmer City's actual role, not just a vibe comparison — St. Petersburg was built specifically to face outward toward a foreign power and mediate contact with it on the new nation's own terms; Palmer City, via its Upper Earth tourism economy and the Machu Picchu Base gateway, occupies exactly the same structural position for Tepenia — the deliberately-outward-facing point where a nation that otherwise turned its back on the world lets a controlled amount of the old world back in, on its own terms. St. Petersburg's founding cost (tens of thousands of forced laborers) doesn't map onto Palmer City's own founding (a population that arrived by choice, not compulsion) — worth naming as a genuine point of difference, not a parallel to force.

## I. The Tourism Mechanism, Formalized

Adopting the Montreal Prohibition parallel from the Mega-Init directly, rather than leaving it a loose resonance: Palmer City's tourism economy runs on the same structural logic that made 1920s Montreal a "sin city" for thirsty Americans — a nearby jurisdiction's own restrictiveness manufactures the demand, and the more permissive place next door simply receives it. Upper Earth's official personhood/robot-rights posture (recognized in law since the 2318 Jeju-do ruling, but clearly not always respected in practice, per the inciting incident itself) makes certain forms of entertainment, certain kinds of open cohabitation between humans and robots, and certain simple freedoms genuinely harder to access at home than they are in a place that never had Upper Earth's hangups to begin with. Palmer City isn't offering anything exotic by Tepenian standards — it's offering ordinary Tepenian life, dressed up as spectacle, to visitors for whom that ordinary life is itself the forbidden thing.

This reframes the "open secret" status City Vision Notes already established: Upper Earth doesn't just quietly tolerate its own citizens visiting Palmer City for casinos and nightlife — on some level, everyone involved understands that part of what's being purchased is proximity to a society that simply doesn't carry Upper Earth's baggage about robots, exile, and personhood. That's a genuinely uncomfortable thing to admit officially, which is exactly why it stays an open secret rather than a stated one.

## II. Named Streets and Landmarks (proposed)

- **Rue de la Réception** *("Street of Reception")* — a bilingual-flavored name (a deliberate nod to Montreal's own bilingual civic character) for the boulevard that grew up around 100 Miles Davis Boulevard as the city expanded — the formal, ceremonial approach street used for visiting dignitaries and Upper Earth tourism arrivals, distinct from Miles Davis Boulevard's own historic, lived-in character.
- **The Petrograd Room** — Palmer City's grandest casino/entertainment hall, named half-jokingly, half-reverently after St. Petersburg's own imperial ambition (Petrograd being one of that city's own historical names) — the closest thing Palmer City has to a St. Petersburg-style declaration of "we willed this into existence and it worked."
- **Little Burgundy Quarter** — a direct, openly-acknowledged homage to Montreal's own historic jazz neighborhood, the district around the original station buildings where the found jazz records first got played and where live music venues remain most densely packed today.
- **The Boardwalk** *(placeholder, Atlantic-City-inspired)* — a stretch of elevated walkway built specifically to let visitors experience Palmer City's rare above-freezing, rainy weather without direct exposure to the worst of the storm-window hazards, doubling as prime nightlife real estate.

## III. The Long Night War's Inciting Incident — proposed names and fate

**The gynoid: proposed name, "Solenne Vaskarre."** Working in Palmer City's entertainment industry (hostessing or performing in one of the city's grand venues — plausibly the Petrograd Room itself, given the diplomat's own high status would put him in the grandest available setting) explains the closed-quarters circumstance directly: she wasn't a private citizen who stumbled into contact with a visiting diplomat, she was working, in a role Palmer City's whole tourism economy depends on, when he denied her personhood and made his attempt. This detail sharpens the tragedy rather than softening it — the same tourism economy that makes Palmer City who it is also produced the exact circumstance that got her killed by history, even though she was the one who survived the room.

**Her fate, proposed:** Solenne was quietly relocated out of Palmer City in the aftermath — not punished (Tepenian law never treated her as anything but a person defending herself), but protected, given how quickly the incident escalated into international significance. She was moved to Concordia specifically, well before Palmer City's eventual destruction, making her one of the rare Palmer City-connected residents to actually reach the capital rather than one of its rare surviving refugees fleeing the war itself. This lands with real weight against the established fact that Palmer City evacuees in Concordia are unusually scarce: the single most consequential person the city ever produced is also one of the very few who ever successfully left it, years before it fell. She lived out the rest of her existence in Concordia — likely for a genuinely long time, given established robot lifespans — carrying the full weight of being the reason a war started, in a country whose own official story about her (unlike Upper Earth's) never doubted her innocence for a moment.

**The diplomat: proposed name and nationality, "Aldric Whitmore Sennett."** A senior political figure from a nation whose own real-world stance on personhood/rights questions runs conservative and hierarchical enough to make his denial of her personhood plausible as personal conviction rather than an isolated lapse — his specific nationality is left flexible in this proposal, since it's the one detail most likely to have real downstream implications for exactly which Upper Earth nation's relationship with Tepenia curdled hardest afterward, and that call is better made once (or if) the war's broader Upper Earth-side politics get more fully developed.

## IV. The Antarctica Flag — resolved

The flag was quietly moved to Concordia alongside Solenne, in the same general window of relocations prompted by the inciting incident's escalating significance — not because anyone connected the two events at the time, but because Palmer City's cultural custodians, sensing (correctly) that international attention was about to fall hard on their city, made a point of securing what they considered irreplaceable before anything worse happened. It survives today in Concordia, in whatever cultural institution holds Palmer City's preserved legacy — worn, tattered, genuinely the original object, not a reproduction. Its condition hasn't meaningfully changed since it was found; Antarctic cold is an excellent preservative once the initial decades of exposure are already baked into an object's condition.

## V. Municipal Holidays — named

- **Founding Day** → **"The Day We Didn't Wait"** — commemorating June 21, 2564, explicitly naming the detail that mattered most about the founding: that the exiles arrived the same day the treaty was signed, without hesitation.
- **The Records** → **"Needle Down"** — a musical observance celebrating the discovery of the jazz records, structured (proposed) as a citywide, dusk-to-dawn live-music event drawing on the full breadth of Palmer City's genre range (jazz, rockabilly, electroswing) rather than jazz alone — Montreal's own modern-day jazz festival, the largest in the world, is worth taking directly as a structural model for what "Needle Down" could look like at civic scale.

---

## VI. DLC 3 Ruins — accessibility resolved

Given the established gameplay notes (rain, flooding, fog, no polar night, wet-cold hazard mechanics), Palmer City's ruins are genuinely accessible but genuinely dangerous in a way distinct from any other Tepenian ruin site — not buried under snow and stabilized by permafrost the way an interior city's ruins would be, but actively decaying: flooded lower levels, structurally compromised buildings weakened by decades of freeze-thaw cycling (itself only possible because Palmer City is the one Tepenian location that ever gets above freezing), and fog that hides genuine structural hazards rather than just limiting sightlines. DLC 3's "archaeology of Tepenia at its most vibrant" framing gains real mechanical teeth from this: exploring Palmer City means racing weather windows and structural decay simultaneously, distinct from the more stable, preserved-by-cold ruin logic everywhere else in the game.

## VII. Historical Beats Between 2564 and the War (proposed)

- **The Byrd Expedition contribution** (already established) sits early in this window — Palmer City as an established, functioning settlement lending its own historical records to help find an even earlier lost site, a nice structural echo of Palmer City's own founding-first identity being useful to others.
- **Proposed addition — the first "Needle Down":** the moment the found jazz records were first played publicly for the whole young settlement, likely within the first year or two of founding, functioning as the informal origin of the holiday later formalized as such.
- **Proposed addition — the Machu Picchu Base agreement:** at some point after Palmer City's tourism economy had grown large enough to matter, a formal (if quiet, given the "open secret" framing) understanding was reached between Tepenia and whatever Upper Earth authority oversees Machu Picchu Base, establishing the visa-on-arrival, tracked-stay system already described in City Vision Notes. This would have been a genuinely significant piece of early Tepenian diplomacy — the first real, sustained point of negotiated contact between the new nation and the world that exiled it — and could be worth developing further as its own historical beat if Tepenia's broader Upper Earth relationship ever gets more attention.

## Finding 1: Palmer City's cosmopolitanism is doubly justified — it doesn't depend on any one piece of its history staying true

**Combining:** the Specs-established founding population (three groups defined by relationship to robots — exile decree, partnership, ideology — explicitly *not* by nationality) + Local_Cultures' 2026-07-03 expansion adding all 43 master-list nations, justified separately as "everyone passed through eventually."

**2nd-order effect:** Palmer City's universal openness rests on two genuinely independent historical processes that happen to point the same direction — the founding population's relationship-based (not national) organizing principle, and the much later demographic reality that every available exile nation eventually showed up here in some number.

**3rd-order effect:** this makes Palmer City's cosmopolitan identity unusually structurally sound compared to most Tepenian cities' civic myths, which typically rest on one single founding fact. Even if either process were altered or reconsidered later, the other would independently produce the same "everyone belongs here" conclusion — a rare case where a city's self-image is over-determined rather than fragile.

**4th-order effect:** an identity this hard to discredit makes Palmer City's legacy an unusually credible reference point for factions elsewhere to appeal to. If a dispute in present-day Concordia ever needs a genuine, hard-to-attack example of universal Tepenian belonging, Palmer City's is the least assailable available, precisely because no single faction's grievance about who "really" belongs can undermine it — which in turn makes the Question of the Unbroken Name faction's own grief (see Finding 6) more credible and less merely sentimental than a typical loss narrative, since what it's mourning is unusually well-founded, not just emotionally significant.

## Finding 2: the city's "everyone's ancestors arrived here first" birthright myth is more generous than its own literal founding facts

**Combining:** Local_Cultures Section 6a's stated social contract ("everyone belongs here, because everyone's people arrived here first") + Specs' own founding population data (a narrow Primary/Significant tier — USA, Germany, France, UK, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, plus founding-wave Argentina/Chile/Uruguay) + the fact that the other 33 nations were added later, in smaller Notable-tier shares, with no ancestors present at the actual 2564 founding.

**2nd-order effect:** the "everyone's ancestors arrived here first" framing is, taken literally, not quite true — only a specific narrow founding wave actually has that claim. The other 33 nations' residents belong here by the city's own current-day welcome, not by literal descent from day one.

**3rd-order effect:** rather than a continuity error, this is worth reading as a genuine and realistic piece of civic mythology — a gap between founding fact and founding *story* that shows up in real nations' own self-narratives constantly. Palmer City's civic religion is more egalitarian than its own history, precisely because "the place everyone passed through" is a story the city needs to be more generous than the facts strictly support, in order to keep functioning as the unifying myth it's supposed to be.

**4th-order effect:** if the myth is aspirational rather than fully lived, it's plausible that some residents descended from the 33 later-added nations quietly experience themselves as less "founding," even while the city's official self-image insists no such distinction exists — a private, rarely-voiced awareness of being a later arrival dressed in a myth of universal belonging. That quiet gap is worth keeping in reserve as a possible subtle tension within Palmer City's own Concordia diaspora community, distinct from (and potentially compounding) the grief the Question of the Unbroken Name faction already carries.

## Finding 3: Palmer City's hospitality is inseparable from a quiet, ongoing act of political sovereignty

**Combining:** the Enneagram read's "Assertive... actively wants to be seen and experienced" + City Vision Notes' Machu Picchu Base visa/tracking system + the established understanding that Upper Earth visitors "obey Tepenian law while present... a real assertion of Tepenian sovereignty on Tepenian soil."

**2nd-order effect:** Palmer City's Assertive energy isn't only cultural magnetism — it's also literal political assertion, disguised as hospitality. Every visa-tracked Upper Earth tourist is a small, repeated demonstration that Tepenia is a functioning, law-having nation a hostile foreign power's own citizens must submit to.

**3rd-order effect:** this makes Palmer City, without anyone designing it this way, a genuine site of ongoing sovereignty theater — Upper Earth officially disdains Tepenia's legitimacy (see the inciting incident itself), yet its own citizens quietly consent to Tepenian jurisdiction every time they cross into Palmer City for entertainment. The city's whole tourist economy is, structurally, a repeated small proof-of-concept that Tepenia's law actually applies to people who don't believe it should.

**4th-order effect:** repeated over decades or centuries, that lived experience — actually submitting to Tepenian law, actually seeing it function, regardless of what one's home government officially says about Tepenia's legitimacy — plausibly softened at least some individual Upper Earth visitors' private views over time, even while their nations' official policy stayed disdainful. This is a plausible origin point for at least some of the still-undeveloped "Upper Earth Defector" character archetype (already flagged open in `TODO.md`) — people whose sympathy for Tepenia didn't come from ideology or crisis, but from years of ordinary, repeated firsthand contact with a place their own government insisted was illegitimate.

## Finding 4: the inciting incident is the exact place the sovereignty-assertion system was always going to fail

**Combining:** Finding 3's sovereignty-theater reading + the established fact that the inciting incident happened in a "closed-quarters setting with no witnesses."

**2nd-order effect:** the diplomat was structurally exactly the kind of visitor Palmer City's whole legal/tourism framework existed to constrain — and the system caught up with him only *after* the fact, vindicating Solenne's personhood in Tepenian law and memory, but not in time to prevent what happened.

**3rd-order effect:** this reframes why the war's spark happened here specifically, beyond simple narrative convenience. Any system built on "the law applies to everyone, even visitors who don't believe it should" has an obvious structural blind spot: the private spaces its own hospitality economy creates — VIP suites, closed meetings, exactly the settings tourism and entertainment industries generate constantly. Palmer City wasn't an arbitrary backdrop for the incident; it was, by its own founding logic, the single place in Tepenia most likely to eventually produce this exact collision, given enough time and enough visitors.

**4th-order effect:** if that structural blind spot is real, Solenne's incident almost certainly wasn't the first of its *kind* — only the first (and only) one to escalate this catastrophically. Smaller, less consequential incidents of the same basic shape (an Upper Earth visitor mistreating a robot in a private setting, without it becoming an international crisis) plausibly happened over Palmer City's centuries of operation and simply never left the room. That opens room for genuinely smaller-stakes quest content — a player investigating an earlier, lesser-known incident of the same type, discovered secondhand, that foreshadows or quietly parallels the one that mattered.

## Finding 5: Solenne's proposed occupation makes her a living embodiment of Palmer City's core tension, not an unlucky bystander

**Combining:** the Full Extrapolation's proposal that Solenne worked in Palmer City's entertainment industry + the established fact that this industry is Palmer City's dominant economic sector (45% of Division of Industry, per the same Extrapolation) + the founding population's own defining trait (people who left Upper Earth specifically because they wouldn't accept robots being treated as property).

**2nd-order effect:** Solenne's daily existence sat exactly at the intersection of everything Palmer City is — a city founded by people who refused to accept robot personhood being denied, built almost entirely on an economy that exists to host the very civilization whose own citizens sometimes still deny it in practice.

**3rd-order effect:** this makes her less a random victim of circumstance and more a structurally inevitable collision — not because anyone chose it, but because a city this specifically shaped, hosting this specific kind of visitor, at this specific economic scale, was always eventually going to produce someone standing exactly where she stood. It gives "why here, why her" a real answer beyond narrative convenience: given Palmer City's own founding logic taken to its natural conclusion, something very like this incident was close to statistically inevitable, somewhere, eventually.

**4th-order effect:** if Solenne (assuming she survived, per Finding 7) is still around in present-day Concordia, her personal story carries direct, current political weight, not just historical weight — she becomes a natural, ready-made cautionary reference point any time Concordia's own factions debate rebuilding any kind of Upper-Earth-facing tourism or economic relationship. Her presence alone would be an implicit argument in that debate, whether she wants it to be or not.

## Finding 6: Solenne's proposed survival makes her a live, unresolved figure inside an already-established grief faction

**Combining:** the Full Extrapolation's proposal that Solenne was relocated to Concordia before Palmer City's destruction + Local_Cultures' established "Question of the Unbroken Name" faction, in which Palmer City's Concordia refugee community is described as "perhaps most stuck" — since letting go of the founding-site identity feels like abandoning an unclaimed claim.

**2nd-order effect:** if she genuinely reached Concordia and survived, Solenne isn't a piece of history the Unbroken Name faction merely reveres from a distance — she's a living person inside their own community, embodying the exact "unclaimed claim" the faction is described as stuck defending.

**3rd-order effect:** this produces a real, usable character-design fork rather than just a nice detail: the faction may want to elevate her as a living symbol (the war began because of what was done to her; she *is* the founding trauma, in person), while she herself — given the sheer weight of being permanently known as "the reason the war started" — might resist or actively avoid that role. That tension (a community's need for a symbol vs. an individual's right not to be reduced to one) is a genuinely strong seed for a companion or major NPC, distinct from simply referencing her as legend.

**4th-order effect:** her own personal resistance to being flattened into a symbol could, if written as an active arc rather than a static fact, become the actual mechanism by which the wider Unbroken Name faction eventually "unsticks" itself. Watching one person model what it looks like to hold real grief without being permanently paralyzed by it is a plausible, earned resolution path for a faction currently described as stuck — turning Solenne from a passive symbol the faction argues over into the person whose own choices give the faction somewhere to go.

## Finding 7: given robot lifespans, Solenne may still be alive in the present-day game era — a significant, unresolved design fork

**Combining:** the fixed Long Night War date (2812) + Palmer City's 2564 founding (leaving up to ~248 years during which the incident could have occurred) + the project-wide established fact that robots can live for centuries + the Full Extrapolation's proposal that Solenne survived and relocated.

**2nd-order effect:** depending on when within that roughly 250-year window the incident actually took place, Solenne could plausibly still be alive and present in Concordia during the game's own current-day setting (≈2822–2827).

**3rd-order effect, flagged as the single biggest open design question in this whole document:** this raises a genuine fork the developer hasn't resolved — is Solenne meant to remain a purely historical, legendary figure (referenced, mourned, mythologized, but not present), or could she be a meetable, living character — someone the player could actually encounter, carrying in person the exact weight of having personally started the war that shaped everyone else's world? Both are legitimate, dramatically strong choices, and they lead to very different places (a name in Tepenia's collective memory vs. an active character with her own arc). This is worth flagging explicitly as a decision point rather than assumed either way in any future writing.

**4th-order effect:** if she's alive and meetable, she's also the single most valuable living witness in all of Tepenia — not just to the incident itself, but to nearly 250 years of Tepenian history she could have lived through firsthand. That makes her a plausible target for multiple different actors with competing interests: historians and journalists (Tepenian or Upper Earth) wanting her firsthand account, factions wanting to use her testimony for their own present-day political purposes, and — most dramatically — Upper Earth interests wanting to silence, discredit, or extract a different story from the one witness who could contradict their own official version of events. That turns her from a historical curiosity into a live, ongoing protection-or-extraction quest hook, generations after the event that made her famous.

## Finding 8: Palmer City has a faint, currently one-directional historical bond with Byrd that neither city's own culture references

**Combining:** the established founding-era fact that Palmer City (alongside Rothera) contributed historical records and maps that helped the Belgrano-led expedition locate Byrd Station + this session's own earlier work establishing Byrd's current City Vision Notes identity (vast icefields, underground fabrication, a genuine sense of shared community) with no reference back to how Byrd was originally found.

**2nd-order effect:** Byrd's present-day identity is, in a small but real way, downstream of Palmer City's own founding-era generosity — Byrd would not have been rediscovered when it was, or the way it was, without Palmer City's contribution — yet nothing in Byrd's own established culture currently reflects this debt.

**3rd-order effect:** this is a minor but genuine missing cross-reference, not a contradiction — worth surfacing as a small available addition rather than a problem needing urgent fixing. Byrd's own culture, built as it is around endurance, community, and being found via expedition (paralleling Dome Fuji's own rediscovery structure), could plausibly hold some faint institutional memory or gratitude toward Palmer City specifically, if that thread is ever worth pulling on — two founding-era neighbors, one destroyed early and thoroughly, one still standing and isolated, connected by a debt only one side's history currently remembers.

**4th-order effect:** since Palmer City was thoroughly destroyed and Byrd wasn't, it's plausible Byrd's own archives still hold copies of exactly the historical records, manifests, and maps Palmer City sent to help find it centuries earlier — meaning Byrd could unexpectedly be one of the only places in present-day Tepenia holding real primary-source material about pre-war Palmer City, material that no longer exists at its own point of origin. That's a genuinely poetic, usable design hook: DLC 2 (Byrd) as a place where players stumble onto real documentary history about DLC 3 (Palmer City) that Palmer City's own destruction erased everywhere else.

## Worth Your Attention

If only three of these eight findings get carried forward into future design work, make it these: **Finding 4** (the inciting incident happening in exactly the one setting the city's own sovereignty-assertion system couldn't reach — the real, structural answer to "why here"), **Finding 7** (the genuinely open fork on whether Solenne is legendary-only or a living, meetable character — the single biggest unresolved decision to come out of this whole pass, since it changes what kind of story this becomes), and **Finding 8** (Byrd possibly holding surviving primary-source material on Palmer City that Palmer City's own destruction erased — a small, concrete, ready-to-use cross-DLC hook, not just an interesting observation).

## Closing Synthesis

Two patterns worth naming once, across all eight findings:

- **Palmer City keeps turning out to be structurally, not just narratively, tied to its own destruction.** Findings 3, 4, and 5 all independently arrive at the same underlying shape: the city's sovereignty-assertion tourism economy, its private-space hospitality infrastructure, and its founding population's own core convictions about robot personhood all point toward the same conclusion — that something like the inciting incident wasn't an accident that happened to occur in Palmer City, but close to the single most statistically likely place in Tepenia for exactly that collision to eventually occur, given enough time. That's a stronger, sadder, and more coherent answer to "why did the war start here" than coincidence alone would give.
- **The city's civic myths are consistently more generous than its literal facts, and that gap is itself a real, usable feature.** Finding 2's founding-birthright myth outrunning the actual founding-wave demographics, and Finding 1's doubly-justified cosmopolitanism, both show a city whose self-image works hard to be more inclusive than its history strictly requires — which reads not as sloppiness in the source material, but as an accurate description of how founding myths actually function in real cultures, and is worth preserving rather than "fixing" into stricter consistency.
