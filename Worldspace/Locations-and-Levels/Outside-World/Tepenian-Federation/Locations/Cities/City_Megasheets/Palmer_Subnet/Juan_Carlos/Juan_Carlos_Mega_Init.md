## Who Lived Here, and Why

Juan Carlos developed differently from its South Shetland Islands neighbor Sejong precisely because it lacked Sejong's density of immediate national competitors. Livingston Island's relative quiet gave the exile community genuine room to develop its own culture — language use, food, social temperament — with real coherence, independently and on its own terms, rather than being constantly negotiated and re-defined against a dozen adjacent communities the way Sejong's Korean identity was. Spain itself held on at Significant tier rather than being diluted to Notable, an unusually strong founder retention for the Palmer subnet, while USA became demographic Primary through the same long-run pattern seen across the rest of the subnet — a comparatively gentle founding tension, in keeping with the city's whole quiet, uncontested character.

But Juan Carlos's real historical weight had nothing to do with its size or its culture. It was Tepenia's first bureaucratic archive — where the young Federation kept its very first real records: who entered, who left, who applied for Palmer City visas or permanent immigration, and specifically, a watch on anyone who had once held a position in their home country's Upper Earth government. This connected directly to the Machu Picchu Border & Customs Authority, Tepenia's own customs institution. As the Federation grew more capable, this archive's contents were eventually copied and relocated deeper into Antarctica — becoming, in time, the very same unified archive that Amundsen Station's pre-Split-Brain lore, and DLC 1 (Echoes of Amundsen) itself, is built on. A quiet island city, easy to overlook, turns out to be the literal origin point of the game's deepest lore stakes.

## What It Feels Like

Settled and coherent — Juan Carlos never had to constantly define itself against immediate neighbors the way Sejong did. Its defining social institution is the **tertulia**: not an open gathering but a hosted one, with a guest list, an implied topic or sensibility, and real intellectual expectation — argument pursued as its own worthwhile, even joyful civic practice, not something to be won or avoided. The city's whole rhythm follows the practical demands of maritime access and Livingston Island's genuinely rugged, glaciated terrain rather than any light cycle, since the island sits far enough north to have neither true polar night nor midnight sun.

Beneath that quiet, coherent surface sat an outsized, almost invisible national responsibility — a founding-era archive function that most residents likely thought of as ordinary administrative work, not as the seed of Tepenia's entire institutional memory.

Managing that Federation-wide bureaucratic record was handled with methodical, unemotional precision, and even the city's eventual destruction is framed in Head-center terms: a calculated act of war against a city whose whole significance was informational, not emotional or symbolic.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: St. Gallen, Switzerland

Began as a genuinely modest, secluded hermitage — an Irish monk withdrawing into an untamed mountain valley in 612 — and grew, centuries later, into one of the most important cultural and intellectual centers in Europe, anchored by the Abbey Library of St. Gallen, the oldest library in Switzerland and one of the oldest monastery libraries in the world, still holding 170,000 books today.

**What this gives Juan Carlos:** a genuine real-world precedent for the exact shape of its own story — a quiet, out-of-the-way founding (Livingston Island, deliberately less contested and less negotiated than Sejong) whose archival function nonetheless grew into something of disproportionate, lasting significance far beyond what its own modest origins would suggest. St. Gallen's abbey didn't need to be a major population center to become one of Europe's most important intellectual institutions; Juan Carlos didn't need to be one of Tepenia's largest or most storied cities to become the literal origin point of the Federation's entire institutional memory.

### Secondary: Hay-on-Wye, Wales

A declining market town saved entirely by one resident's specific, eccentric vision: Richard Booth's 1960s idea to fill the town with secondhand bookshops, which by the 1970s had made Hay the world's first "book town." Booth then declared Hay an independent kingdom in 1977, crowning himself "Richard Cœur de Livre" — a deliberate, self-mythologizing civic identity built entirely around books, now home to one of the world's largest literary festivals, "the Woodstock of the Mind."

**What this gives Juan Carlos:** a concrete model for how a town's whole civic self-image can crystallize around textual/intellectual preservation as an identity, not just a function — and a genuine question worth asking of Juan Carlos's own culture: does its archive heritage, even after the practical function relocated to Amundsen Station, leave behind a similar self-mythologizing civic identity, where residents still think of themselves as fundamentally "record-keeping," "memory," or "argument" people long after the actual bureaucratic work moved elsewhere? The already-established tertulia tradition — argument and intellectual exchange treated as genuine civic practice — reads as exactly this kind of residue: not literal book-keeping, but the same underlying impulse toward valuing preserved, spoken, and argued knowledge as a communal good.

### Secondary: Wolfenbüttel, Germany

A planned Renaissance town (Germany's first, under Duke Julius's grid-pattern design) whose defining legacy became one ruler's obsessive personal collecting project: Duke August the Younger sent agents across Europe to gather books, driven by what's described as "a truly universal, almost utopian ambition: to leave posterity the entire knowledge of his time in book form." The resulting Herzog August Library became one of Europe's largest collections, hosting figures like Leibniz and Lessing, and outlasted its founder by centuries.

**What this gives Juan Carlos:** a model for how a practical, administrative undertaking (in Duke August's case, personal collecting; in Juan Carlos's case, visa and customs record-keeping) can be driven, at some point in its history, by one specific figure's disproportionate ambition — someone who pushed the archive to be more thorough, more complete, more genuinely comprehensive than the job strictly required, in the same spirit as Duke August's own "entire knowledge of his time" ambition. This gives Juan Carlos's still-open "Notable Figures: TBD" line a concrete, earned direction: not a faceless bureaucratic institution that simply grew on its own, but one with a real founding archivist whose personal ambition shaped what it became.

## Worth Your Attention

St. Gallen is the standout of this pass — not for introducing anything new, but for giving Juan Carlos's already-established story real historical weight. A quiet, secluded founding producing an institution of disproportionate, lasting significance isn't a convenient narrative invention; it's a documented pattern real intellectual institutions have followed before. That should make Juan Carlos's whole "quiet city, outsized invisible responsibility" identity read as more earned, not less, once this Megasheet's findings are folded back into the main files.
