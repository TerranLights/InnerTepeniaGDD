## Who Lives Here, and Why

France founded, named, and shaped Dumont d'Urville's entire cultural template — but by Census II, the demographic majority belongs to a China/USA pairing that arrived generations later, largely drawn by the practical opportunities of a coastal Janbogo subnet post rather than any attachment to St. Jules or Adélie Land specifically. Unlike Fort McMurdo's eventual exact-parity resolution, the founding nation here never caught back up demographically — French cultural inheritance simply persisted as the civic default anyway, the way Janbogo's Korean template did, because nothing ever displaced it. This plays out at a genuinely small, intimate scale: a city of ~312,000 rather than Fort McMurdo's industrial sprawl, an island rather than a peninsula, and — notably — a population where robots meaningfully outnumber humans, since Dumont d'Urville was never a place many humans chose to live if they had another option, given the wind.

The city's other defining, unavoidable co-resident is the Adélie penguin — the Géologie Archipelago holds one of the largest Adélie breeding grounds in Antarctica, and no arriving population could ever have displaced colonies of that scale even if they'd wanted to. Dumont d'Urville didn't found itself in opposition to its environment. It founded itself in the middle of one it would never fully own.

## What It Feels Like

A tight, bustling, spatially stratified city. The downtown core, on Petrel Island proper, is dense, expensive, and genuinely alive — heating units run between buildings, not just inside them, keeping the spaces themselves livable against wind that's more severe here than anywhere else documented in Tepenia. Live music inside almost every eatery is an ordinary, expected feature of downtown life — "New Orleans scaled down to 1/20th the size," many small, packed, informal venues rather than one large performance industry. The bridge to the mainland (still genuinely undecided as permanent structure vs. seasonal ice/boat crossing) is the island's most prominent feature outside downtown itself, and the city's spatial economics run outward from it: expensive downtown, cheaper residential territory past the bridge, the highway network beyond that.

Everything else here is built around accommodation, not resistance — the wind, the penguins, the sea are permanent co-residents whose terms have to be lived with, never conquered. This isn't Mirny's grim stoicism; it's closer to an ongoing, generations-deep negotiation.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Quebec City, Canada

The only remaining walled city in North America north of Mexico — founded 1608, its fortifications still substantially intact, its narrow cobblestone streets and cliff-top position making it among the most physically distinctive small cities on the continent. Despite conquest, subsequent British rule, and 400 years of being surrounded by an overwhelmingly anglophone continent, French remains the city's living, functional default — spoken by 94% of residents today, not a preserved museum-piece but an actual working civic language.

**What this gives Dumont d'Urville:** direct, real-world confirmation that a small, physically bounded city can maintain a minority founding culture as its genuine civic default for centuries against real demographic pressure — exactly the *shape* of Dumont d'Urville's own established tension, a founding culture persisting as civic default beneath a later demographic majority. Quebec's walls were built for military defense rather than wind, but the underlying pattern — a small, defensively-built city preserving a specific cultural identity against constant outside pressure — maps cleanly onto Dumont d'Urville's own low, anchored, wind-first architecture and its own founding civic character surviving well past the point demographics alone would predict.

### Secondary: San Miguel de Allende, Mexico

Flourished on colonial silver-mining wealth, then genuinely nearly became a ghost town by the early 1900s — saved first by a 1926 national historic-monument designation, then decisively transformed when an American artist, Stirling Dickinson, arrived in 1937 and helped establish a genuine art colony that turned a declining town into a globally recognized haven for visual artists.

**What this gives Dumont d'Urville:** a concrete real-world precedent for a small, physically demanding town avoiding decline specifically through a culture built around music, art, and creative life — worth reading Dumont d'Urville's own "New Orleans at 1/20th scale" downtown culture through this lens directly: not just an ordinary civic amenity, but plausibly the actual mechanism holding a small, exposed, war-damaged population together against the very real temptation to simply leave a place this demanding. San Miguel de Allende shows that this kind of cultural anchor is a genuine, historically real way a small town avoids emptying out — not just a pleasant detail layered on top of survival, but load-bearing to it.

### Secondary: San Miguel de Tucumán, Argentina

Chosen as the site of Argentina's own 1816 Congress of Independence specifically because of its practical, strategic geographic position — an intermediate point between the Río de la Plata and Upper Peru — not because of its own size or population. The city's entire national significance traces to one specific date and one specific building (the Casa Histórica), and it carries that founding-moment identity as "the Cradle of Independence" centuries later, alongside an entirely separate, affectionate civic nickname ("El Jardín de la República") for its natural beauty.

**What this gives Dumont d'Urville:** two direct structural parallels at once. First, Tucumán's selection for practical geographic reasons rather than ambition mirrors Dumont d'Urville's own founding logic exactly — Petrel Island was chosen because its rock was buildable, not because anyone expected it to become significant. Second, and more strikingly: a city whose entire civic identity anchors to one specific historical date and moment (Tucumán's 1816 Congress; Dumont d'Urville's own St. Jules Day, commemorating Jules Dumont d'Urville's January 21, 1840 landing) is a genuine, real pattern — proof that a small city's whole civic identity can legitimately orbit a single founding moment rather than ongoing scale or achievement, exactly what St. Jules Day already does for Dumont d'Urville.

## Finding 1: Dumont d'Urville's French identity persists because it's actively used, not passively preserved

**Combining:** Quebec City's real-world precedent of a minority founding culture surviving centuries through genuine, functional daily use rather than ceremonial preservation + the established fact that "even residents with no French ancestry tend to pick up functional French simply through years of civic life conducted in it."

**2nd-order effect:** Dumont d'Urville's French persistence isn't a fragile, ceremonial holdover kept alive mainly through names and holidays — it's a self-sustaining, functional system, since ordinary civic participation itself is what transmits the language to each new generation of arrivals, regardless of their own ancestry.

**3rd-order effect:** this makes the city's founding tension (a demographically thin founding nation whose civic template nonetheless persists) structurally more durable than a city where the founding language survived only ceremonially — new arrivals here become carriers of the founding culture through ordinary daily life, not through any deliberate act of cultural preservation.

**4th-order effect:** this distinction — functional daily transmission versus ceremonial-only preservation — is a real, checkable difference worth applying to other Tepenian cities carrying a similar founding-nation-demographic-minority pattern (Fort McMurdo, Janbogo, Zukelli among them), to determine which of those cities' founding cultures rest on the same durable mechanism Dumont d'Urville's does, and which are closer to ceremonial preservation that could plausibly erode over further generations.

## Finding 2: the downtown music culture may be load-bearing to the city's actual post-war survival, not just a surviving amenity

**Combining:** San Miguel de Allende's real precedent of an art colony rescuing a town headed toward ghost-town status + Dumont d'Urville's own established damaged post-war condition (some sections abandoned, penguins colonizing the empty spaces) + the already-established "New Orleans at 1/20th scale" downtown eatery-and-music culture.

**2nd-order effect:** the downtown music culture and the city's post-war survival may not be two separate, coincidentally-surviving facts — the culture plausibly functions as a genuine, load-bearing reason people stayed rather than evacuated a place this physically demanding even before the war added damage on top of it.

**3rd-order effect:** if that's true, the actual fragile point in Dumont d'Urville's post-war existence isn't simply its total population figure — it's specifically the downtown core where that culture lives. The already-damaged outlying sections have already been absorbed into the city's ongoing existence without threatening its survival; a comparable level of damage to downtown itself would threaten something the outlying damage doesn't.

**4th-order effect:** this reframes what "Dumont d'Urville's survival" actually depends on for any future story content — not the city's overall footprint or population count, but specifically whether its small, dense downtown core remains intact enough to keep sustaining the cultural life that appears to be holding the whole population's will to stay together in the first place.

---

## Finding 3: Dumont d'Urville's whole significance rests on modest, disconnected, unglamorous facts rather than any single claim to importance

**Combining:** San Miguel de Tucumán's real precedent of a city chosen for practical geographic position rather than ambition, carrying its entire national significance from one specific historical date + Dumont d'Urville's own founding logic (Petrel Island chosen because its rock was buildable, not because of any expectation of significance) + St. Jules Day's established civic centrality, itself anchored to one specific 1840 date.

**2nd-order effect:** Dumont d'Urville's importance comes from two entirely separate, unglamorous sources that have nothing to do with each other — a practical, accidental reason for its physical location, and a specific, commemorative reason for its civic identity — neither of which involves any claim to scale, strategic vitality, or ambition.

**3rd-order effect:** combined with Finding 1's durable-but-quiet linguistic transmission and Finding 2's unglamorous, load-bearing music culture, a consistent pattern emerges across all three findings: Dumont d'Urville matters through accumulated, modest, practical persistence, never through any single grand or dramatic claim to significance.

**4th-order effect:** this "modest but durable" quality is worth naming as a real throughline distinct from, though fully compatible with, the city's already-established "Negotiated Ground" identity — Negotiated Ground describes *how* the city relates to its hostile environment; this pattern describes *why* the city itself continues to matter at all, despite never having anything dramatic to point to as the reason.

## Synthesis: The Pattern Across All Three Findings

All three findings converge on the same underlying observation: everything genuinely significant about Dumont d'Urville is quiet, functional, and accumulated rather than dramatic or singular. Its language survives through ordinary use, not ceremony. Its culture may be quietly load-bearing to its own survival, not merely decorative. Its whole reason for existing traces to two unrelated, unglamorous facts rather than one grand founding claim. This is a city whose importance is easy to overlook precisely because nothing about it was ever built to be impressive at a glance.
