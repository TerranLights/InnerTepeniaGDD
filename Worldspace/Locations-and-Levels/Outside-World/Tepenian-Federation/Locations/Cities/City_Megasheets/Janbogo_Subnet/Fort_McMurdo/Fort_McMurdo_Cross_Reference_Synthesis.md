## Finding 1: The capital's formalization may trace to more than one concurrent mega-project, not the Amundsen Tower alone

**Combining:** the Full Extrapolation's proposed timing for Fort McMurdo's formal capital designation (aftermath of the Amundsen Tower's construction, ~2629–2639) + the established fact that Fort McMurdo's industrial output built "a significant share of Concordia's own infrastructure" — its dome structures, corridor systems, and equipment + the Tower's own established role as the era's single largest national infrastructure undertaking.

**2nd-order effect:** if Concordia's own growth as a hub required a similarly massive, concurrent supply of materials during roughly the same broader era of national construction, Fort McMurdo's formalization may not trace to the Tower alone — it may mark the moment the city became simultaneously indispensable to more than one of Tepenia's largest-ever infrastructure projects at once.

**3rd-order effect:** this reframes the capital's formalization as a response to a broader era of unprecedented, concurrent national construction, rather than one isolated crisis — Fort McMurdo's industrial capacity became critical to multiple different projects at the same time, compounding the pressure toward formal recognition.

**4th-order effect:** worth flagging as a richer causal account for whenever a future Timeline document pins down the exact designation date — not one triggering event, but a convergence of several, all tracing back to the same underlying industrial capacity.

## Finding 2: Fort McMurdo and Port Lockroy share an identical damage pattern for two entirely different reasons

**Combining:** Fort McMurdo's proposed Section VIII finding (the original McMurdo Station-era core survived the war better than the later Tepenian-era residential expansion) + Port Lockroy's own established Cross-Reference finding (its 1944 wartime-built core survived better than its later peacetime additions) + the different underlying mechanisms behind each: Port Lockroy's core was deliberately hardened for wartime military use; Fort McMurdo's core was simply built heavier, to the older American Antarctic Program's extreme-environment engineering standard, for reasons that had nothing to do with war.

**2nd-order effect:** these are the same visible pattern — an older core outlasting newer additions — produced by two unrelated causes in two different cities, not one universal Tepenian rule about age and resilience.

**3rd-order effect:** this is worth naming precisely so "old core survived" doesn't become a lazy, self-explanatory shorthand applied identically everywhere — the actual reason behind each city's own resilience should shape what its ruins look like and what remains inside them.

**4th-order effect:** worth flagging as a genuinely useful distinction for level design — Port Lockroy's surviving core would read as hardened military infrastructure; Fort McMurdo's would read as heavy industrial engineering built for an extreme environment, a different visual and material language for what is, on the surface, "the same" kind of ruin.

## Finding 3: Fort McMurdo's own "presence, not population" identity may be exactly what made its informal status intolerable

**Combining:** Fort McMurdo's established "presence, not population" identity — real significance that isn't legible through any obviously countable metric + the newly-proposed capital-formalization timing, driven by growing, undeniable national dependency + the established "McMurdo Problem" faction naming real resentment about the city's outsized, informal influence.

**2nd-order effect:** an invisible, hard-to-quantify kind of power is precisely the kind of power that provokes the deepest resentment when left informal — there's no countable way for outsiders to confirm how much influence Fort McMurdo actually holds, only a persistent, uneasy sense that it's substantial.

**3rd-order effect:** formalizing Fort McMurdo's status may have been less about legitimizing power that already had a clear, measurable shape, and more about making visible and accountable a kind of influence that was, by its own established nature, deliberately difficult to see or measure.

**4th-order effect:** this deepens why the "McMurdo Problem" faction specifically existed and specifically drove formalization — not simple resource jealousy, but a genuine structural frustration with a kind of power that couldn't even be properly named until the government finally did so.

## Synthesis: The Pattern Across All Three Findings

All three findings converge on the same underlying idea: Fort McMurdo's significance has always been harder to see than it is to feel. Its formalization as capital likely traced to more than one project depending on it at once, rather than a single visible crisis (Finding 1); its own physical resilience shares a visible pattern with Port Lockroy's for reasons that have nothing to do with each other (Finding 2); and its whole civic identity — presence over population, felt rather than counted — may be exactly what made an unaccountable, invisible kind of power impossible to leave informal any longer (Finding 3). Fort McMurdo reads, across all three findings, as a city whose real weight consistently exceeds what any single, countable fact about it can capture.

## Worth Your Attention

Finding 3 is the most conceptually significant of the three — it ties Fort McMurdo's own signature "presence, not population" identity directly to the specific historical mechanism (the "McMurdo Problem" resentment) that eventually forced its capital status into the open, giving the city's abstract civic-gravity concept a concrete political consequence rather than leaving it as pure atmosphere.
