## Who Lives Here, and Why

France founded, named, and set Dumont d'Urville's earliest civic institutions in place — but by Census II, the demographic majority belongs to a China/USA pairing that arrived generations later, largely drawn by the practical opportunities of a coastal Janbogo subnet post rather than any attachment to St. Jules or Adélie Land specifically. Unlike Fort McMurdo's eventual exact-parity resolution, the founding nation here never caught back up demographically — the early civic template, independently grown on-site, simply persisted as the civic default anyway, the way Janbogo's own founding-era template did, because nothing ever displaced it. This plays out at a genuinely small, intimate scale: a city of ~312,000 rather than Fort McMurdo's industrial sprawl, an island rather than a peninsula, and — notably — a population where robots meaningfully outnumber humans, since Dumont d'Urville was never a place many humans chose to live if they had another option, given the wind.

The city's other defining, unavoidable co-resident is the Adélie penguin — the Géologie Archipelago holds one of the largest Adélie breeding grounds in Antarctica, and no arriving population could ever have displaced colonies of that scale even if they'd wanted to. Dumont d'Urville didn't found itself in opposition to its environment. It founded itself in the middle of one it would never fully own.

## What It Feels Like

A tight, bustling, spatially stratified city. The downtown core, on Petrel Island proper, is dense, expensive, and genuinely alive — heating units run between buildings, not just inside them, keeping the spaces themselves livable against wind that's more severe here than anywhere else documented in Tepenia. Live music inside almost every eatery is an ordinary, expected feature of downtown life — "New Orleans scaled down to 1/20th the size," many small, packed, informal venues rather than one large performance industry. The bridge to the mainland (still genuinely undecided as permanent structure vs. seasonal ice/boat crossing) is the island's most prominent feature outside downtown itself, and the city's spatial economics run outward from it: expensive downtown, cheaper residential territory past the bridge, the highway network beyond that.

Everything else here is built around accommodation, not resistance — the wind, the penguins, the sea are permanent co-residents whose terms have to be lived with, never conquered. This isn't Mirny's grim stoicism; it's closer to an ongoing, generations-deep negotiation.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Singapore

Founded as a British trading post in 1819, Singapore's population became overwhelmingly ethnic Chinese as waves of migration arrived through the 19th and 20th centuries — yet English, the founding administrative language, remains the country's primary language of government, law, business, and inter-ethnic communication today, not a preserved relic but a fully living civic default used by residents of every background.

**What this gives Dumont d'Urville:** direct, real-world confirmation that a founding institutional language can remain a genuine, living civic default for generations after the population that first spoke it natively becomes a demographic minority — exactly the *shape* of Dumont d'Urville's own established tension, a founding culture persisting as civic default beneath a later demographic majority that arrived for entirely practical, unrelated reasons. Singapore's multi-ethnic population organizing daily civic life around a founding-era administrative language, rather than the language of whichever group happens to be numerically largest, maps cleanly onto Dumont d'Urville's own founding civic character surviving well past the point demographics alone would predict.

### Secondary: San Miguel de Allende, Mexico

Flourished on colonial silver-mining wealth, then genuinely nearly became a ghost town by the early 1900s — saved first by a 1926 national historic-monument designation, then decisively transformed when an American artist, Stirling Dickinson, arrived in 1937 and helped establish a genuine art colony that turned a declining town into a globally recognized haven for visual artists.

**What this gives Dumont d'Urville:** a concrete real-world precedent for a small, physically demanding town avoiding decline specifically through a culture built around music, art, and creative life — worth reading Dumont d'Urville's own "New Orleans at 1/20th scale" downtown culture through this lens directly: not just an ordinary civic amenity, but plausibly the actual mechanism holding a small, exposed, war-damaged population together against the very real temptation to simply leave a place this demanding. San Miguel de Allende shows that this kind of cultural anchor is a genuine, historically real way a small town avoids emptying out — not just a pleasant detail layered on top of survival, but load-bearing to it.

### Secondary: San Miguel de Tucumán, Argentina

Chosen as the site of Argentina's own 1816 Congress of Independence specifically because of its practical, strategic geographic position — an intermediate point between the Río de la Plata and Upper Peru — not because of its own size or population. The city's entire national significance traces to one specific date and one specific building (the Casa Histórica), and it carries that founding-moment identity as "the Cradle of Independence" centuries later, alongside an entirely separate, affectionate civic nickname ("El Jardín de la República") for its natural beauty.

**What this gives Dumont d'Urville:** two direct structural parallels at once. First, Tucumán's selection for practical geographic reasons rather than ambition mirrors Dumont d'Urville's own founding logic exactly — Petrel Island was chosen because its rock was buildable, not because anyone expected it to become significant. Second, and more strikingly: a city whose entire civic identity anchors to one specific historical date and moment (Tucumán's 1816 Congress; Dumont d'Urville's own St. Jules Day, commemorating Jules Dumont d'Urville's January 21, 1840 landing) is a genuine, real pattern — proof that a small city's whole civic identity can legitimately orbit a single founding moment rather than ongoing scale or achievement, exactly what St. Jules Day already does for Dumont d'Urville.
