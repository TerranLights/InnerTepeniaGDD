## What It Feels Like

The wind arrives first, immediately and physically — not an occasional hazard but the permanent condition of outdoor life, severe enough that even moderate ambient temperatures become genuinely dangerous under sustained katabatic loading. Mirny answers this by building itself as its own windbreak: dense buildings arranged in a scattered, concentric formation two or three layers deep, a heavily fortified, smooth-shaped outer ring built specifically to shed the wind rather than catch it. Inside that shielded core sit huge industrial yards, machinery running more often than not, with residential space folded directly into the same inner buildings rather than kept in a separate layer. Entertainment is minimal — mostly rough, unpolished bars — and Mirny is, by its own residents' reckoning, a rough city. But that roughness sits alongside a genuine, unforced sense of pride and kinship, not despite the hardship but simply alongside it, unremarked. A few days each year, near each solstice, the sun grazes the horizon rather than rising or setting fully — a quiet, otherworldly civic moment that residents anticipate and newcomers find startling, the literal, visible proof that Mirny sits exactly on the line between the polar world and everywhere else.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Yakutsk, Russia

The coldest city of its size on Earth, built on continuous permafrost, where average January temperatures sit near −37°C and the city has never recorded an above-freezing reading between mid-November and mid-March. Founded in 1632 as an insignificant Russian fort, Yakutsk remained minor for centuries until 1880s gold and diamond discoveries transformed it into one of Russia's most rapidly growing regional cities. Despite the extreme cold, life continues largely as normal — schools stay open unless temperatures drop below −55°C, and adults rarely take a day off for weather alone.

**What this gives Mirny:** a direct real-world precedent for managing genuinely severe conditions as routine rather than spectacle — schools staying open past −50°C, life continuing largely as normal — the same practical, unromanticized relationship to hardship that Mirny's own wind-first engineering culture already models. Yakutsk's own transformation from an obscure fort into a major city, driven entirely by resource extraction rather than any founding glory, mirrors Mirny's own economic identity as a genuine industrial and construction engine for its subnet, not a symbolic capital trading on its founding name.

### Secondary: Nizhny Tagil, Russia

Home to Uralvagonzavod, the world's largest tank manufacturer — a single industrial complex so dominant it effectively defines the entire city's economy, employing some 30,000 people across railway cars, tanks, and heavy machinery production.

**What this gives Mirny:** a concrete precedent for a city whose *entire* civic and economic identity organizes around one dominant heavy-industrial complex, at a scale matching Mirny's own established "huge industrial yards, machinery running more often than not."

### Secondary: Wolfsburg, Germany

Built entirely around a single Volkswagen plant — the world's largest single car-manufacturing complex — so thoroughly that the factory has given Wolfsburg the highest GDP per capita in Germany.

**What this gives Mirny:** a structural precedent for a city physically organized as an enclosure around its own industrial core, echoing Mirny's own established "windbreak" architecture, where the entire outer ring exists specifically to shelter the industrial yards inside rather than the reverse.

### Secondary: Chongqing, China

A genuinely massive heavy-industrial center — nearly 80% of its gross industrial output comes from heavy industry, anchored by one of China's largest integrated iron-and-steel complexes.

**What this gives Mirny:** less a direct proportional match than a useful scale reference — Chongqing's ~80% heavy-industry dominance is considerably higher than Mirny's own established, more balanced economy (industrial/manufacturing at roughly 20%, on par with communications/Arcanet and technical/scientific sectors rather than eclipsing them). Chongqing usefully illustrates the far end of what heavy-industry dominance *can* look like, throwing Mirny's own comparatively balanced, multi-sector economy into sharper relief by contrast rather than by direct resemblance.
