## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Tyumen

Founded in 1586 as Russia's first Siberian settlement, Tyumen isn't itself the site of Western Siberia's oil and gas fields — it's the service and logistics base that makes working those fields possible, its advantageous position at the crossing of road, rail, water, and air routes turning it into the essential support city for extraction happening elsewhere. Despite this fundamentally unglamorous, service-oriented role, Tyumen has grown into a genuine center of industry, science, culture, education, and even contemporary art, with a cosmopolitan character and a notably bright civic outlook.

**What this gives Davis:** a real-world precedent for exactly the "enabler, not the explorer" identity Davis's own namesake already embodies — Tyumen isn't the resource, it's the reason the resource can be worked at all, the same structural role John King Davis played for other men's expeditions. Tyumen's own real transformation — unglamorous service town growing genuine cultural depth (arts, education, culture) without ever stopping being fundamentally a working city — gives grounded precedent for Davis's own established layering of serious agricultural and research labor with a small but genuine arts/music community.

### Secondary: Vladivostok

A major Russian Pacific port and naval headquarters, named "Lord of the East" — an assertive, glory-claiming name that sits in direct, productive contrast to Davis's own deliberately humble namesake. Vladivostok's commercial identity leans heavily on fishing and shipping (fishing alone accounts for roughly four-fifths of its commercial production), and it spent decades as a closed city, off-limits to outsiders.

**What this gives Davis:** less a direct parallel than a useful foil — Vladivostok shows what a maritime Antarctic-adjacent city looks like when it leans into strategic/military prestige framing, which throws Davis's own explicitly anti-glory civic character into sharper relief. Its real fishing/shipping-dominant economy also gives concrete grounding for Davis's own established Prydz Bay maritime trade and harbor economy.

### Secondary: Guatapé

A Colombian town whose entire character is shaped by a man-made reservoir — the town was physically relocated and rebuilt a few miles from its original site after the valley was flooded to create the lake. Its distinguishing visual tradition, brightly painted murals called *zócalos* depicting local life and belief, emerged directly from a place otherwise defined by its dramatic lake geography, transforming what was once a quiet agricultural village into one of Colombia's most visited destinations.

**What this gives Davis:** a concrete real-world example of a settlement whose whole identity — including its visual arts tradition — grows directly out of its defining lake geography, exactly matching Davis's own established naturalist visual-arts tradition documenting the Vestfold Hills. It also surfaces a genuine, unresolved tension worth naming: Guatapé embraced tourism built around its lake; Davis's own established ethos explicitly resists performing importance or courting visitors, even though its terrain is, by any objective measure, remarkable enough to invite exactly that kind of attention.
