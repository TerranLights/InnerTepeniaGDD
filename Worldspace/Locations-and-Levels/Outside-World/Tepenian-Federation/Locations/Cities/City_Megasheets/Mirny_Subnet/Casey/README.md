## Who Lives Here, and Why

China and USA sit co-Primary, but the real story is the width of the Significant tier underneath them — Japan, France, South Korea, Russia, Indonesia, and an Australian founding wave, six nations close enough in share that none dominates. That breadth isn't accidental: a junction city that received "a wider, more mixed early wave of settlers than more geographically isolated founding sites" (per its own founding story) never had the demographic conditions to develop a singular dominant identity the way Zhongshan or Janbogo did. Casey's people are, structurally, as diffuse as its function — a city that serves everyone equally because it was never built around any one group in particular.

---

## What It Feels Like

The junction registers first, immediately legible even to a stranger — two roads meeting, a clear decision point. Then the specific, efficient warmth of a city that has hosted an enormous number of strangers and long since perfected doing it well. And then, for anyone who stays past their first layover, **Splinters** — a full city block on the old peninsula, Tepenia's largest and most famous bar, hosting a genre-spanning Classic Rock/Jazz/Blues/Acoustic Folk scene famous along the entire Dumont d'Urville Sea and Indian Ocean coastline. The name itself is rediscovered history — residents found records of the real, historical "Splinters" social bar at the pre-exile Casey Station and revived it for their own, far grander establishment. Casey was never a city that celebrated itself the way Palmer City did. It served a function, and it happened to be extraordinarily good at one specific, unglamorous part of that function: giving travelers somewhere worth staying an extra night for.

## Personality (Enneagram Read)

**Feeling (Heart) / Assertive / Positive Outlook** — a civic identity organized entirely around Splinters as felt, shared cultural experience, actively projected outward (famous along an entire coastline, not just locally beloved) with warmth and genuine festivity rather than strain. Casey shares this exact Enneagram profile with Dumont d'Urville and Palmer City — the distinguishing axis is scale and reach: Dumont d'Urville is dense-and-local, Palmer City is national-and-historic, and Casey sits in between as **singular-and-regional** — one landmark, famous across one whole coastline, neither a citywide texture nor a national legend.

## Real-World Inspirations, Researched

### Primary: Flagstaff, Arizona

A genuine highway crossroads by both history and design — historically the meeting point of US Route 66 (the nation's major east-west artery) and US Route 89 (the major north-south route west of the Rockies), and today the junction of Interstate 17 (from Phoenix) and Interstate 40 (east-west), with Highway 180 branching off toward the Grand Canyon. Modern sources describe it plainly as "a bustling city situated at the great crossroads of the vast West" — crossroads function and civic identity are treated as the same fact, not two separate things.

**What this gives Casey:** direct, load-bearing precedent for a literal highway-junction identity rather than an incidental one — Flagstaff isn't merely located near a crossroads, its whole civic character and tourist/service economy are organized around being one, exactly mirroring Casey's own Hwy 110/Hwy 2 junction as "the defining geographic and civic fact of Casey" (per its own Local Cultures file).

### Secondary: Memphis, Tennessee

A genuinely multi-modal crossroads — two interstates (I-40/I-55), five converging freight rail lines, and the Mississippi River all meeting in one place — with Beale Street's live-music scene sitting directly at that same junction, drawing visitors from far beyond the city itself.

**What this gives Casey:** reinforces the junction identity at a larger structural scale (multiple transport modes converging, not just two highways), and gives real grounding to Splinters specifically — Beale Street is proof that a junction city's nightlife district can become the city's actual defining civic landmark, recognized regionally, rather than staying a mere local amenity.

### Secondary: Reno, Nevada

A historic transcontinental-railroad stopover that pivoted in 1931 — legalized gambling, a six-week divorce residency — from "just a stopover" into a genuine destination with its own full civic identity. A recent local piece is literally titled "Reno doesn't have an identity crisis: it has a love story."

**What this gives Casey:** the single closest real-world precedent for Casey's own established founding tension — being a *destination* versus being a *waypoint* — already named directly in its Post-Culture Identity section ("residents live full lives in a city whose external reputation reduces it to a highway junction"). Reno's real 1931 turn from waypoint to destination gives that tension a genuine historical case study, not just an invented parallel.

## I. When Casey Was Destroyed, and Why

**Proposed:** Casey was an early casualty, deliberately and specifically targeted rather than caught incidentally in broader war damage. The reasoning traces directly from an already-established fact: Hwy 2/DCH runs *directly through* Casey rather than merely connecting to it, meaning a single strike here achieves two strategic outcomes at once — severing Concordia's coastal supply lifeline along Hwy 110, and cutting the Dumont d'Urville coast off from the rest of the highway network entirely, with no possible reroute. For an attacking force choosing where to spend limited early strikes for maximum effect, a junction that collapses two supply corridors simultaneously is a uniquely efficient target — more valuable, strike-for-strike, than either Zukelli's symbolic-deterrent destruction or a straightforward population-center strike.

**Formerly proposed consequence for Pink Lucy's Route B, now moot:** this section previously argued that an early Casey destruction would narrow, not close, the possibility her migration passed through Casey's ruins. **Retired 2026-07-12** — her route was resolved as Dumont d'Urville → Janbogo instead; Route B is no longer under consideration, so Casey's own destruction timing has no bearing on her backstory *(corrected 2026-07-13)*. Casey's own destruction-timing reasoning above stands independently of this.

## II. Casey's Economy Beyond Transit

**Proposed:** the underspecified "technical/scientific" and "industrial/manufacturing" sectors (15% and 10% of Casey's economy per its own Division of Industry) trace directly to the inherited Australian Antarctic Division infrastructure — specifically, meteorological and katabatic-monitoring equipment, repurposed post-exile into the technical backbone of Casey's own established wind-warning and travel-safety coordination culture ("be useful, be quick, be honest about the route" is already Casey's cardinal social value). Sitting precisely on the Antarctic Circle threshold, Casey is a genuinely useful observation point for the transition between full polar conditions and milder coastal ones — giving its inherited scientific capacity a real, functional reason to exist beyond simple institutional carryover, and feeding directly into the same subnet-wide wind-safety network already established as central to Casey's Arcanet culture.

---

## III. Wilkes Station Ruins — Likely Status

**Proposed:** by the time of the Long Night War, Wilkes Station's own ruins are almost certainly fully buried and inaccessible — a site already abandoned and unmaintained since roughly 1969 in the real-world record, meaning it had over 250 additional years of snow accumulation on top of decades of pre-exile abandonment even before Tepenia's own war damage entered the picture. Casey's own ruins, by contrast, are comparatively recent and would retain far more visible structure. This gives Wilkes a clean, earned reason to remain "a local curiosity" rather than a DLC-relevant explorable site — it was already fading from relevance before the war, and the war didn't change that trajectory so much as make it permanent.

---

## IV. Which DLC Covers Casey's Ruins

**Proposed:** whichever DLC ultimately covers the broader Mirny subnet also covers Casey, rather than treating Casey as special main-game-accessible content. Casey sits geographically and administratively inside the same subnet as Mirny, Davis, Zhongshan, Vostok, and Kunlun — all of which are already established as DLC territory, distinct from the main game's Concordia-only setting. Treating Casey as an exception would require a specific justification that doesn't currently exist anywhere in its own files; folding it into the same DLC as its subnet neighbors is the simpler, more consistent outcome.

## V. Did Casey's Own Residents Feel They'd "Arrived" at Something?

**Proposed, directly answering the question surfaced in the Mega-Init:** yes, at least partially, and the answer is already implicit in an established fact that hasn't been read for its full weight — Splinters' name wasn't invented by the exile generation, it was *rediscovered*, deliberately, from old pre-exile records of the real historical bar at Casey Station. That act of choosing to revive a specific piece of their own city's buried history, rather than building something generic or simply letting the highway define them, is itself Casey's own version of Reno's 1931 turn — quieter, gradual, and continuous rather than a single dramatic legal change, but a genuine act of self-definition all the same. Splinters' regional fame is therefore not purely an export felt by outsiders passing through; it's something Casey's own people chose, on purpose, to be known for.

# Casey — Cross-Reference Synthesis: Gaps, Combinations, and Downstream Effects

**What this is:** a deliberate cross-referencing pass across every Casey document — `Specs/Casey.md`, `Local_Cultures/Mirny_Subnet/Casey.md`, `City_Vision_Notes/Casey.md`, `City_Enneagram_Personalities/Mirny_Subnet/Casey.md`, `Inspirational-Influences.md`'s entry, `Casey_Mega_Init.md`, and `Casey_Full_Extrapolation.md` — hunting for places where combining facts across files produces implications neither file states alone. Each finding is followed as far as its chain of implication honestly holds, in plain descriptive terms — no questline or dialogue-branch framing here; that mapping belongs to a later, separate quest-design pass, not this synthesis step. Written 2026-07-08.

---

## Finding 1: Casey held the best information and the least civic instinct to recognize its own strategic danger

**Combining:** Hwy 2 running directly *through*, not merely to, Casey + Hwy 110 as Concordia's supply lifeline + Casey's own established political character ("comparatively muted... civic energy absorbed by its logistics function rather than by grievance politics," Local Cultures Section 21-22) + the Full Extrapolation's proposed early-strategic-target destruction timing.

**2nd-order effect:** Casey was simultaneously the single highest-value strategic chokepoint in its own subnet and the city least civically oriented toward treating that vulnerability as a political priority — its whole culture was organized around *serving* the junction's function, never defending it.

**3rd-order effect:** the residents who, by daily occupation, held the clearest possible view of exactly how strategically vital the junction was (dispatchers, road-condition reporters, logistics coordinators) belonged to a civic culture that explicitly deprioritized "ideological or grievance politics" in favor of simply keeping the junction running — meaning the best-informed people in Tepenia about Casey's own vulnerability had the least established civic mechanism for acting on that knowledge politically.

**4th-order effect:** this produces a genuine, specific irony worth preserving for future design — Casey's destruction likely arrived with less internal warning or civic preparation than a more politically self-aware city might have mustered, not for lack of information, but because the culture itself never developed the instinct to treat operational knowledge as strategic knowledge.

---

## Finding 2: Casey has two genuinely different identities depending on who's describing it

**Combining:** Local Cultures Section 7 ("Casey's identity is real but diffuse, organized around function rather than a strong unifying aesthetic or principle") + the Full Extrapolation's proposed reading of Splinters' revival as a deliberate act of civic self-definition + the Enneagram profile's "singular-and-regional" distinguishing trait (one landmark, famous across an entire coastline).

**2nd-order effect:** Splinters becoming Casey's singular, externally famous landmark sits in direct tension with Casey's own established self-description as a city with no strong singular identity — the city tells itself it has no defining thing, while its regional reputation says otherwise.

**3rd-order effect:** this isn't necessarily a contradiction requiring resolution so much as a genuine split in vantage point — a visitor arriving specifically *for* Splinters experiences a city with one clear, famous identity; a longtime resident living the actual day-to-day junction function experiences the diffuse, function-first city the Local Cultures file describes. Both descriptions are simultaneously true.

**4th-order effect:** "what Casey actually is" depends structurally on who's answering the question — a genuinely useful fact to keep in mind for any future writing, since a visitor-facing scene and a resident-facing scene would reasonably describe the same city in two different, equally valid registers.

---

## Finding 3: The three real-world picks map a trajectory Casey's own history was moving along when the war cut it off

**Combining:** Flagstaff's pure-function crossroads identity + Memphis's crossroads-plus-defining-landmark identity + Reno's stopover-transformed-into-full-destination identity + Casey's own founding-to-destruction timeline (post-Falkland Treaty founding → Splinters' gradual growth "over generations," implied by records old enough to need rediscovery → early Long Night War destruction, per Section I of the Full Extrapolation).

**2nd-order effect:** the three research picks aren't interchangeable variations on "crossroads city" — they represent three distinct stages a junction city can occupy, and Casey's own established history shows it moving through exactly this sequence: founding-era Casey would have read as pure Flagstaff-style function, then gradually, as Splinters grew into regional fame, shifted toward the Memphis model of function-plus-defining-landmark.

**3rd-order effect:** given the early-destruction timing already proposed, Casey was very plausibly cut down mid-transition — somewhere between the Memphis stage and the fuller Reno-style transformation into a place its own residents would claim with unambiguous pride, rather than a place merely famous for what happened to pass through it.

**4th-order effect:** this gives Casey's destruction a specific, earned tragic shape beyond generic wartime loss — not the end of a settled identity, but the interruption of a transformation that was visibly, gradually happening and never got to finish. Whatever Casey might have become, had the war not intervened, is left permanently as an open question rather than a foreclosed one.

---

## Synthesis: The Pattern Across All Three Findings

All three findings converge on the same underlying shape: Casey was a city in genuine motion, not a settled one, at the exact moment it was destroyed. Its strategic vulnerability existed precisely because its culture hadn't yet caught up to what its own function had made it (Finding 1); its identity was actively splitting between an old diffuse self-description and a new singular external reputation (Finding 2); and its whole civic arc was mid-transition between two recognizable real-world models when it ended (Finding 3). Casey reads, across all three findings, as a city interrupted rather than a city completed — which is a meaningfully different kind of loss than most other destroyed Tepenian cities carry.

---

## Worth Your Attention

Finding 3 is the most structurally valuable — it reframes Casey's destruction from a static historical fact into an interrupted arc, giving any future writing about the city's ruins or its diaspora's grief a specific, earned shape to draw from: not simply "a city that was lost," but "a transformation that never got to finish," which is a meaningfully different emotional register.

## Additions (pre-war, now ruins)

- **Splinters** — Tepenia's largest, most famous bar, a full city block on the old peninsula, with its own stage, dance floor, and rows of seating along the side-flanks and rear.
- **The Junction Dispatch Office** — where road-condition dispatchers like "Long Odds" Okonkwo-Hale coordinated wind-window and travel-safety information subnet-wide.
- **Waystation Row** — the string of resupply depots and transit lodging strung along the Hwy 110/Hwy 2 junction itself.
- **The Wilkes Curiosity Walk** — an informal path out to the older, already-buried Wilkes Station ruins, a curiosity even before Casey's own destruction.

## Small offices for educational training (pre-war)

- road-condition dispatch and katabatic/wind-window forecasting
- resupply logistics and rapid siligel/goods turnover
- hospitality-for-strangers (fast information exchange, efficient service)

## Social cohesion mechanisms (pre-war)

- **Waystation Suppers** — shared meals at resupply points doubling as information exchange between travelers and locals.
- **Splinters Set Nights** — recurring live-music nights spanning Classic Rock, Jazz, Blues, and Acoustic Folk, the city's actual social heart.
- **Junction Toasts** — informal recognition for a dispatcher or driver who got someone through a dangerous wind window safely.
- **Road Story Circles** — the oral tradition of accumulated travelers' tales, swapped informally at waystations.

## Diaspora and survivor relevance, present-day

Casey's diaspora in Concordia carries its quiet, non-demonstrative reticence even into loss (Local Cultures Section 32) — references to Splinters, the junction, and the waystation culture above are the kind of thing a Casey-descended Concordia resident would mention in passing, not perform as grief. Any surviving Casey-community members the player meets in the DLC itself would hold this same material as lived memory rather than historical trivia — a dispatcher character, for instance, would plausibly still use junction vocabulary ("at the junction," per Local Cultures Section 8) unprompted, regardless of where they ended up after the city's destruction.
